Making progress: Reducibility Candidates and Cut Elimination in the Ill-founded Realm
Gianluca Curzi and Graham E. Leigh

Abstract
. Ill-founded (or non-wellfounded) proof systems have emerged as a natural framework for inductive and coinductive reasoning. In such systems, soundness relies on global correctness criteria, such as the progressivity condition. Ensuring that these criteria are preserved under infinitary cut elimination remains a central technical challenge in ill-founded proof theory. In this paper, we present two cut elimination arguments for ill-founded μ ​ 𝖬𝖠𝖫𝖫 \mu\mathsf{MALL} – a fragment of linear logic extended with fixed-points – based on the reducibility candidates technique of Tait and Girard. In both arguments, preservation of progressivity follows directly from the defining properties of the reducibility candidates. In particular, the second argument is derived from the topological notion of internally closed set developed in previous work by Afshari and Leigh.



1.
Introduction

Since their early development, infinitary proofs have played a prominent role in modern proof theory. Their systematic study was initiated by Schütte, who showed cut elimination for an infinitary extension of Peano arithmetic
[
31
,
32
]
, where the presence of the
\omega
-rule – an infinitely branching rule replacing the induction scheme – makes the corresponding notion of proof infinite in
breadth
while remaining well-founded. Specifically, Schütte’s cut elimination argument was formulated as a transfinite process indexed by ordinals, with termination ensured by a well-founded measure on proof height. This framework was later refined by Mints who emphasised the role of continuity in proof transformations
[
25
]
.

Since this pioneering work, infinitary proof systems and their cut elimination techniques have been extensively studied, providing a valuable setting for establishing consistency proofs and conservativity results for theories of arithmetic and analysis (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
40
,
38
]
).

Over the past two decades, however, alternative notions of infinitary proof have emerged that relax the requirement of well-foundedness (while often reintroducing finite branching). Motivated by the study of first-order inductive definitions
[
6
,
7
]
, but anticipated in the seminal work of Niwinski and Walukiewicz on the modal
\mu
-calculus
[
26
]
, ill-founded (or non-wellfounded) proofs offer an ideal proof-theoretic framework for inductive and coinductive reasoning. Research on ill-founded proofs has been applied to a wide range of areas, including automata theory
[
13
]
, games
[
27
]
, theories of arithmetic
[
34
]
, (modal)
\mu
-calculi
[
35
]
, complexity
[
10
]
, and type theory
[
15
]
.

In ill-founded proof systems, soundness is no longer guaranteed by local inductive arguments. Instead, it appeals to
global correctness criteria
. Among these criteria, the most commonly adopted in the literature is the
progressivity
(or
trace
) condition, typically satisfied whenever a syntactically determined feature occurs infinitely often along each infinite branch of a proof.
Other examples of criteria include bouncing threads, induction orders, semantic productivity, and automata-based conditions (see, e.g.,
[
35
,
36
,
3
]
).

A major focus of ill-founded proof theory is on
circular
or
cyclic
proofs, that is, proofs whose underlying tree is (possibly infinite but)
regular
– i.e., having only finitely many distinct subtrees. Owing to their finite presentability, cyclic proofs are typically expressed as finite, possibly cyclic, graphs and can be viewed as a natural ill-founded counterpart of traditional inductively presented proofs.

A growing body of research in this topic is devoted to the family of
fixed-point logics
, which integrate finitary logics with least and greatest fixed points. Notable examples include the family of modal
\mu
-calculi
[
24
]
, fixed-point formulations of intuitionistic logic (
\mu\mathsf{LJ}
)
[
9
,
11
]
and of linear logic and its fragments (such as
\mu\mathsf{LL}
and
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
)
[
5
,
4
]
. When nesting and interleaving of fixed points are permitted, fixed-point logics are well suited to express very general forms of (co)inductive definitions and corresponding (co)inductive reasoning principles. From a computational perspective, fixed-point logics that admit a constructive interpretation – such as
\mu\mathsf{LJ}
,
\mu\mathsf{LL}
and
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
– can be used to model sophisticated (co)inductive data structures and (co)recursion mechanisms. A detailed account of the computational strength of these logics (both in the inductive and cyclic presentation) can be found in
[
11
]
.

This paper focuses on
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
, the fixed-point extension of
multiplicative additive linear logic
(
\mathsf{MALL}
), which can be obtained from classical logic by removing the structural rules of contraction and weakening. Its key feature is the presence of two (non-equivalent) formulations of the connectives
\wedge
and
\vee
: the
multiplicatives
(resp.,
\otimes
and
\invamp
), and the
additives
(resp.,
\with
and
\oplus
). The “exponentials” of linear logic, which control the use of weakening and contraction, can be simulated by fixed points in
\mu\mathsf{MALL}

[
5
]
, thus making this logic a general framework for studying the theory of (co-)induction.

The introduction of ill-founded proofs challenges one of the central tools of proof theory: cut elimination. When proofs can be infinite in
depth
, Schütte-style termination arguments are no longer applicable, and a conceptual shift becomes necessary. One approach is to reformulate cut elimination as an infinitary – possibly transfinite, ordinal-indexed – rewriting process that converges to a cut-free proof in the limit. This perspective draws on notions and techniques from topology (such as continuity and metric completion) or coinductive reasoning. In particular, when the rewriting process has a countable number of steps, the existence of the limit proof boils down to showing that all finite approximations can be computed in finitely many steps, a property commonly referred to as
productivity
.

The use of infinite rewriting for cut elimination in an ill-founded setting is not, in itself, a novel idea. Infinitary rewriting techniques have been studied systematically since the 1990s in variety of contexts, including first-order and higher-order rewrite systems
[
16
,
23
]
, and infinitary extensions of the
\lambda
-calculus
[
22
]
. What ill-founded proof theory brings to this topic, rather, lies in the proof-theoretic methods developed to ensure that the infinitary cut elimination procedures preserve the global correctness conditions mentioned above, thereby yielding sound cut-free limit proofs whenever they are applied to sound proofs.

One of the earliest and more general approaches to cut elimination for cyclic and ill-founded proofs was introduced by Santocanale in
[
19
]
for a fragment of
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
, and was later extended to full
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
by Baelde et al in
[
4
]
. The core idea is to define a (possibly infinite) rewriting strategy that stepwise pushes cuts upward, giving priority to the bottommost ones. To avoid technical complications such as cut permutations, this approach often resorts to the
multicut
rule, a macro rule representing a series of consecutive cuts.

In recent years, a variety of alternative cut elimination techniques have been proposed, such as the use of fixed-point theorems for contractive maps over ultrametric spaces
[
29
,
30
,
33
]
, notions of run
[
14
]
, or domain-theoretic approaches
[
1
]
. Nonetheless, many of these methods rely on features specific of the systems considered, and do not readily extend to different or more general settings.

Proving that infinitary cut elimination preserves the aforementioned global correctness criteria remains a technically challenging – yet central – problem in ill-founded proof theory. Existing arguments in the literature tend to be bespoke and system-specific, and therefore lack full generality and robustness
[
29
,
30
,
33
,
28
,
14
,
1
]
.

The present article contributes to the general theory by adapting Tait and Girard’s celebrated reducibility candidates technique to the ill-founded setting. More specifically, we isolate two
notions of reducibility candidate. The first, named
\mathfrak{N}
-reducibility candidates
, is defined directly in terms of cut elimination. The second,
\mathfrak{E}
-reducibility candidates
, is based on an alternative, topologically motivated and logic-independent, global condition on proofs which we call
external progressivity
. In essence, the latter are the ill-founded proofs which explicitly present an invariant for cut elimination. Our work is developed within ill-founded
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
whose linear structure offers a streamlined and concise exposition, but covers classical and intuitionistic fixed point logics by virtue of the standard embeddings.

We prove that progressing proofs belong to both kind of reducibility candidate, thereby confirming that progressing proofs are both externally progressing and normalisable. The argument relies on a modest generalisation of the standard soundness (or computational totality) argument from ill-founded proof theory (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
18
]
). In the case of
\mathfrak{N}
-reducibility candidates, the result directly implies cut elimination for progressing proofs. An (almost) parallel argument establishes that every progressing proof is externally progressing, generalising an observation from
[
2
]
. As with the standard reducibility candidates arguments for inductive systems,
\mathfrak{N}
-reducibility merely confirms the
existence
of a cut elimination strategy. A more insightful and explicit cut elimination argument is developed using
\mathfrak{E}
-reducibility candidates, where external progressivity provides a direct and straightforward certification of preservation of progressivity when a concrete cut elimination procedures is given.

Related work

Cut elimination for infinitary
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
was first developed by Baelde et al.
[
4
]
using the notion of multicut and extending a previous result for a much weaker system by Fortier and Santocanale
[
19
]
. To ensure productivity and preservation of progressivity, however, the authors propose a bespoke argument based on a truth semantics.

The concept of externally progressing ill-founded proofs appeared in
[
2
]
as an intermediate notion between progressing and normalisable ill-founded proofs in the context of higher-order intuitionistic arithmetic with fixed-points. Both inclusions – progressing proofs are externally progressing and externally progressing proofs are normalisable – crucially relied on restricting the use of fixed point quantifiers.

In contrast to
[
2
]
, our result does not place any specific restriction on the formation of fixed points (beyond positivity). Moreover, thanks to the robustness of the reducibility candidates method and the logic-independent nature of external progressivity, our cut elimination arguments generalise to fixed point logics beyond those treated in
[
4
,
19
]
, such as the higher-order fixed point logics discussed in
[
2
]
.

Conventions on sequences and trees

The set of natural numbers is denoted
\omega
, and is associated with the standard ordering
<
.
A finite sequence over a set
X
is a function
u:\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\to X
, often written
\langle u(0),\ldots,u(n-1)\rangle
. The cardinality of the domain of
u
is referred to as the
length
and denoted
|u|
. The set of finite sequences over
X
is denoted
{X}^{<\omega}
.
The empty sequence is denoted
\langle\rangle\in{X}^{<\omega}
, and concatenation of (finite) sequences
u
and
v
is denoted
u.v
.
The prefix relation on
{X}^{<\omega}
is denoted
\leq
, defined by
u\leq v
iff there exists
w\in{X}^{<\omega}
such that
v=u.w
.

A
tree
is a non-empty set
T\subseteq{\omega}^{<\omega}
which is prefix closed, that is, if
u<v\in T
then
u\in T
. Elements of
T
are called
nodes
of
T
.
A
leaf
is a
<
-maximal
node.
A
tree over
X
is a pair
T^{\prime}=(T,\lambda)
where
T
is a tree and
\lambda\colon T\to X
is a label function assigning to each node of
T
an element of
X
. When there is no cause for confusion we write
u\in T^{\prime}
in place of
u\in T
, and
T^{\prime}(u)
for
\lambda(u)
.
Given
v\in T
, the
v
-rooted subtree
of
T
is the subtree
T_{v}
with nodes
\{u\mid v.u\in T\}
and
T_{v}(u)=T(v.u)
.

In what follows, we will assume the so-called
tree topology
of (labelled) trees. It is well-known that the latter is the topology of the complete (ultra)metric space over the set of (labelled) trees given by the distance function
\delta
such that

\delta(T,T^{\prime})=0
if
T=T^{\prime}
, and otherwise
\delta(T,T^{\prime})
is the infimum of all
2^{-n}
such that
T
and
T^{\prime}
coincide on all nodes with length
\leq n
. This will ensure that Cauchy sequences of derivations have
limits
.

A
branch
of
T
is an infinite sequence of successors in
T
starting from the root, namely a sequence
\mathfrak{b}:\omega\to\omega^{<\omega}
such that
\mathfrak{b}(0)=\langle\rangle
and
\mathfrak{b}(n+1)
is in
\{u\in T\mid\mathfrak{b}(n)\leq u\text{ and }|u|=n+1\}
.
The set of branches of
T
is denoted
\mathrm{Br}(T)
.
The longest common prefix of distinct branches
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}
of
T
is the node
\mathfrak{a}\wedge\mathfrak{b}\in T

.

Let
B\subseteq\mathrm{Br}(T)
be a non-empty set of branches of a tree
T
. The
closure
of
B
is the set
\overline{B}=\{\mathfrak{b}\in\mathrm{Br}(T)\mid\forall n>0\exists\mathfrak{a}\in B\,\mathfrak{a}(i)=\mathfrak{b}(i)\text{ for all }i<n\}
.
If
B=\overline{B}
we call
B

closed
. Note that
\mathrm{Br}(T)
is closed if
T
is finitely branching.
The
infimum
(
supremum
) of
B
is the leftmost (resp. rightmost) branch of
\overline{B}
, denoted
\inf B
(
\sup B
). Infima always exist, though suprema are only guaranteed in the case of finitely branching trees.

Ill-founded
\mu\mathsf{MALL}

We begin by introducing an ill-founded (one-sided) sequent calculus system for
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
, the multiplicative-additive fragment of propositional linear logic extended with least and greatest fixed point operators
[
5
,
4
]
. Our presentation will mainly follow
[
4
]
, although we adopt some terminology from
[
2
]
.

2.1.
Formulas

Definition 1

(Formulas)
.

Preformulas
, written
\phi,\psi
etc., are generated by the following grammar:

\phi,\psi:=X\mid\bot\mid\mathbf{1}\mid\phi\invamp\psi\mid\phi\otimes\psi\mid\mathbf{0}\mid\top\mid\phi\oplus\psi\mid\phi\with\psi\mid\mu X\phi\mid\nu X\psi

where
X
belongs to a countable set of (
propositional
)
variables
.

Free variables
of a preformula are defined as expected, construing
\mu
and
\nu
as binders:

•

\mathrm{FV}(X):=\{X\}

•

\mathrm{FV}(\bullet):=\emptyset
, for
\bullet\in\{\mathbf{0},\top,\bot,\mathbf{1}\}

•

\mathrm{FV}(\phi\star\psi):=\mathrm{FV}(\phi)\cup\mathrm{FV}(\psi)
, for
\star\in\{\oplus,\otimes,\invamp,\with\}

•

\mathrm{FV}(\kappa X\,\phi):=\mathrm{FV}(\phi)\setminus\{X\}
, for
\kappa\in\{\mu,\nu\}

A preformula is
closed
if it has no free variables, otherwise it is
open
. Closed preformulas are called
formulas
.

Capture-avoiding substitution of a formula
\psi
for a free variable
X
in
\phi
, written
\phi[\psi/X]
, is defined in the standard way. We will sometimes write
\psi(\mu X\phi)
for
\psi[\mu X\phi/X]
, and similarly for
\nu
.
We also assume some standard conventions on variable binding, in particular that each occurrence of a binder
\mu
or
\nu
binds a variable distinct from all other binder occurrences in consideration. This avoids having to deal with variable renaming explicitly.

Negation
of a formula
\phi
, written
\phi^{\perp}
, is defined as the involution on preformulas satisfying
(\phi\invamp\psi)^{\perp}=\phi^{\perp}\otimes\psi^{\perp}
,
(\phi\oplus\psi)^{\perp}=\phi^{\perp}\with\psi^{\perp}
,
\perp^{\perp}=\mathbf{1}
,
\mathbf{0}^{\perp}=\top
,
(\nu X\phi)^{\perp}=\mu X\phi^{\perp}
,
X^{\perp}=X
. Notice that the equations imply
(\phi[\psi/X])^{\perp}=\phi^{\perp}[\psi^{\perp}/X]
.
1
1

1

The equation
X^{\perp}=X
is harmless since throughout this paper we will only consider closed preformulas. Thanks to that equation, we do not require any positivity condition on fixed point formulas to enforce a semantic interpretation based on the Knaster-Tarski fixed point theorem.

The presence of fixed point formulas requires a more permissive notion of subformula, given by the well-known Fischer-Ladner preorder.

Definition 2

(Fischer-Ladner preorder)
.

We write
\phi\subseteq\psi
if
\phi
is a subformula of
\psi
. The
Fischer-Ladner preorder
, written
\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}
,
is the smallest reflexive and transitive extension of
\subseteq
satisfying

\phi(\mu X\phi(X))\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}\mu X\phi(X)
and
\phi(\nu X\phi(X))\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}\nu X\phi(X)
.
We write
\phi\approx_{\mathrm{FL}}\psi
if
\phi\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}\psi\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}\phi
, and define
{\prec_{\mathrm{FL}}}={\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}}\setminus{\approx_{\mathrm{FL}}}
.
The
Fischer-Ladner closure
of a formula
\psi
, written
\mathrm{FL}(\psi)
, is the set
\{\phi\mid\phi\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}\psi\}
.

Note that
\approx_{\mathrm{FL}}
-equivalence classes are naturally (well) partially ordered by
\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}
. Notice also that
\mathrm{FL}(\psi)
is the smallest set of formulas closed under subformulas and fixed point unfolding: whenever
\kappa X\phi(X)\in\mathrm{FL}(\psi)
, then also
\phi(\kappa X\phi(X))\in\mathrm{FL}(\psi)
for
\kappa\in\{\nu,\mu\}
. Also,
\mathrm{FL}(\psi)
is a
finite
set.

The Fisher-Ladner preorder allows a standard
(strict) well partial order on formulas,
<
, that associates a “priority” to interleaving fixed points within a formula (see, e.g.,
[
37
,
17
,
11
]
).

Definition 3

(Priority)
.

We say that
\phi
has higher
priority
than
\psi
, written
\psi<\phi
, if
\psi\prec_{\mathrm{FL}}\phi
, or
\phi\approx_{\mathrm{FL}}\psi
and
\phi\subset\psi
.

Example 4
.

Consider the following formula
\phi=\mu X\psi
, where
\psi=\nu Y(X\otimes Y\otimes\theta)
and
\theta=\nu Z(\mathbf{1}\oplus Z)
. Setting
\chi=\nu Y(\phi\otimes Y\otimes\theta)
we have that
\chi\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}\phi
. Moreover, since
\phi\subset\chi
, we also have
\phi\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}\chi
, and so
\chi\approx_{\mathrm{FL}}\phi
. On the other hand, since
\theta\subset\phi
we have
\theta\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}\phi
(but not
\phi\preceq_{\mathrm{FL}}\theta
). Finally, notice that the preformula
\psi
is not closed, and so it is not a formula. This means that
\psi
is incomparable with respect to any of these orderings.

Lemma 5
.

If
\phi\approx_{\mathrm{FL}}\psi
then
\psi<\phi
iff
\phi
is shorter than
\psi
.

In what follows, we will assume an arbitrary extension of
<
to a
total
well order.

(Derivations)
.

A

of

To simply the presentation of many concepts, henceforth we largely leave occurrences of the exchange rule
\mathsf{e}
implicit. This convention is especially relevant in the case of cut which will often be assumed to be of the following “exchange invariant” form:

{\Gamma},\phi,\Gamma^{\prime}

\Delta,\phi^{\perp},{\Delta^{\prime}}

\scriptstyle\mathsf{cut}\;

{\Gamma},\Gamma^{\prime},\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}

Ignoring applications of exchange will allow us to denote a derivation obtained by applying a consecutive series of cut rules as follows:

\textstyle{\scriptstyle d}

\Gamma,\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}

\textstyle{\scriptstyle d_{1}}

\phi_{1}^{\perp},\Delta_{1}

\scriptstyle\mathsf{cut}\;

\Gamma,\Delta_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{n}

\textstyle{\scriptstyle d_{2}}

\phi_{2}^{\perp},\Delta_{2}

\Gamma,\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n}

when the cut formulas are clear from the context.

2.3.
Threads, traces, and progressivity

8
shows, among others, that the system of derivations of
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
is not logically sound. A typical approach to recover soundness is to introduce a global correctness criterion called
progressivity
(or
trace
)
condition
(see, e.g.,
[
6
]
). Intuitively, the progressivity condition certifies that along each branch of a given derivation we can find a sequence of formulas ordered by
\prec_{\mathrm{FL}}
where certain
\nu
-formulas unfold infinitely often. Such sequences are called
threads
.

Definition 9

(Threads)
.

A
weak thread
is an infinite sequence
\tau
such that
\tau(i)
is a pair
(\phi_{i},p_{i})
, where
\phi_{i}
is a formula and
p_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, for which for all
i
either
(\phi_{i},p_{i})=(\phi_{i+1},p_{i+1})
or
\phi_{i+1}\prec_{\mathrm{FL}}\phi_{i}
and:

•

if
\phi_{i}=\kappa X\psi
then
p_{i+1}=0

•

if
\phi_{i}=\psi_{0}\star\psi_{0}
with
\star\in\{\oplus,\otimes,\invamp,\with\}
then
\phi_{i+1}=\psi_{p_{i+1}}
.

Henceforth, for the sake of readability, we will consider
\tau
as a sequence of formulas, thus treating each
\tau(i)
as a formula rather than a pair.

A
thread
is a weak thread
\tau
such that
\tau(i+1)\prec_{\mathrm{FL}}\tau(i)
for all
i
. A weak thread that is not eventually constant uniquely identifies a thread
\hat{\tau}
given by contracting all consecutive repetitions, in which case we refer to
\tau
as the
expansion
of
\hat{\tau}
. Finally, the
dual
of a (weak) thread
\tau=(\phi_{i})_{i}
, written
\tau^{\perp}
, is the (weak) thread
(\phi_{i}^{\perp})_{i}
.

The following is a well-known property of threads (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
):

Fact 10
.

Let
\tau=(\phi_{i})_{i}
be a thread. There is a unique
\kappa X\psi
with
\kappa\in\{\mu,\nu\}
such that:

•

for infinitely many
i
,
\phi_{i}=\kappa X\psi
,

•

for all but finitely many
i
,
\phi_{i}
contains
\kappa X\psi
as a subformula.

The above property allows us to identify those threads that can be used to certify progressivity condition, which we call “good”.

Definition 11

(Good threads)
.

Let
\tau=(\phi_{i})_{i}
be a thread, and let
\kappa X\psi
be a formula given by
10
. If
\kappa=\nu
we call
\tau

good
; otherwise
\tau
is
bad
.

A consequence of the above is that a thread
(\phi_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is good (bad) iff
(\phi_{i})_{i\geq n}
is good (bad) for every
n\geq 0
. Moreover:

Proposition 12
.

A thread is either good or bad, and it is good iff its dual is bad.

We now need the notion of
trace
. This can be seen as a concrete instantiation of a thread
\tau
along a branch, and is defined as a sequence of
formula occurrences
enumerating (with possible repetitions) the formulas of
\tau
.

Definition 13

(Formula occurrences)
.

Let
d
be a derivation. A
formula occurrence (of
d
)
is a triple
(u,k,\phi)
where
u\in d
and
k<|\mathcal{S}_{d}({u})|
is such that
\mathcal{S}_{d}({u})(k)=\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}
with
\psi_{k}=\phi
.

Definition 14

(Ancestry)
.

Let
d
be a derivation.
We say that a formula occurrence
f=(u,k,\phi)
is an
ancestor
of another formula occurrence
f^{\prime}=(v,h,\psi)
if one of the following cases holds:

•

f^{\prime}
is principal and
f
is a minor formula

•

f^{\prime}
is in the context, and
f
is the corresponding formula in a premise of the rule.

Definition 15

(Trace)
.

A
trace
in
d
is an infinite sequence
t=(u_{i},k_{i},\phi_{i})_{i}
of formula occurrences such that
t(i+1)
is an ancestor of
t(i)
for every
i<\omega
.
The unique branch
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathrm{Br}(d)
which contains
(u_{i})_{i}
is said to
carry

t
.

Notice that, if
t=(u_{i},k_{i},\phi_{i})_{i}
is a trace in
d
then
\phi_{i+1}\prec_{\mathrm{FL}}\phi_{i}
if
t(i)
is principal in a logical rule and
\phi_{i}=\phi_{i+1}
otherwise.

Definition 16
.

Let
\tau
be a weak thread. A
trace of
\tau
is a trace
t=(u_{i},k_{i},\phi_{i})_{i}
such that
(\phi_{i})_{i}
is an expansion of
\tau
. A branch
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathrm{Br}(d)

bears

\tau
, equivalently
\tau
is
borne
by
\mathfrak{b}
, if
\tau
has a trace carried by
\mathfrak{b}
. We say that a set of branches
bears
a thread if one of its branches does so.

By weak König’s lemma, every branch of a derivation carries at least one trace. As a consequence, every branch bears at least one weak thread though not necessarily a (non-weak) thread. Indeed, we can state:

Lemma 17
.

A branch bears a thread iff the branch carries a trace which is infinitely often principal in a logical rule.

Thanks to the notions of thread and trace, we can now define the global correctness condition for derivations of ill-founded
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
:

Definition 18

(Progressing derivations)
.

A
progressing
derivation is a
derivation for which every branch bears a good thread.
With
\mathfrak{P}
we denote the set of progressing derivations.

(

, revisited)

Consider the leftmost derivation of

\mathfrak{b}
and

and

, respectively.

\tau
(resp.,

) be the thread bourne by

(resp.,
\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}
) and whose trace is highlighted with

(resp.,

Since
\tau^{\perp}
is the only thread borne by
\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}
and it is not good, then the derivation is not progressing.

Concerning the centre derivation, its only branch

\tau

\tau
is good precisely when

3.
Internal closure and external progressivity

Infinitary cut elimination in the form of
\omega
-normalisation annihilates certain threads while preserving others. The two classes of threads can be characterised as the threads initiating from particular formula occurrences. The annihilated threads, which we refer to as
internal
, are the threads stemming from cut formulas, while the preserved threads, called
external
, are those initiating from the root of the derivation.

The next definitions clarify these notions.

Definition 30

(Internal versus external)

Let

is

if

is a cut formula occurrence, and

if

is a formula occurrence in the conclusion of

(resp.

is a thread of an internal (resp. external) trace traversing
\mathfrak{b}
.

Clearly,
a trace is either internal or external
.

Definition 31

(Coherence)
.

Let
d
be a derivation. For each thread
\tau

{}\scoh_{\tau}{}
on branches of

{\mathfrak{b}}\scoh_{\tau}{\mathfrak{c}}
iff

\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{c}
respectively such that:

•

and
t(0)
are the cut formula occurrences at nodes
\mathfrak{b}(n+1)
and
\mathfrak{c}(n+1)
respectively, where

•

s
bears
\tau
and
t
bears
\tau^{\perp}
.

In this case, we call
\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{c}

.
Notice that
{\mathfrak{b}}\scoh_{\tau}{\mathfrak{c}}
iff
{\mathfrak{c}}\scoh_{\tau^{\perp}}{\mathfrak{b}}
.

Internally closed sets
are closed sets of coherent branches (see preliminary conventions):

Let

4.
Reducibility candidates

Reducibility candidates were introduced by Girard
[
20
]
for proving normalisation of
system

\mathsf{F}
, a polymorphic calculus, and generalise Tait’s celebrated
reducibility
(or
computability
)
method
for Gödel’s system
\mathsf{T}

[
39
]
. In the context of linear logic, reducibility candidates arise naturally from the so-called
orthogonality construction

[
21
]
, a recurring motif of this logic that stems from semantic motivations. Orthogonality-based reducibility candidates were developed by Baelde and Miller to prove normalisation of inductive
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
in
[
5
]
.

This section defines reducibility candidates for ill-founded
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
via the notion of orthogonality, and outlines their properties. Our constructions smoothly adapt
[
5
]
to our infinitary setting. To recast the Baelde and Miller’s normalisation argument for
\omega
-normalisation, we will introduce ordinal-indexed approximations of reducibility candidates.

4.1.
Orthogonality and reducibility candidates

Henceforth, given a set of derivations
\mathfrak{R}
we will denote with
\mathfrak{R}_{\phi}
the set of derivations with a distinguished formula
\phi
in the conclusion.

Definition 40

(Orthogonality)
.

Let
\mathfrak{R}
be a set of derivations and
\phi
a formula. The (
\mathfrak{R}
-)
orthogonality relation
over
\phi
is the relation
{\perp_{\phi}^{\mathfrak{R}}}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}_{\phi}\times\mathfrak{R}_{\phi^{\perp}}
defined by
d\perp_{\phi}^{\mathfrak{R}}d^{\prime}
iff
\mathsf{cut}_{\phi}(d\mid d^{\prime})\in\mathfrak{R}
.
For an orthogonality relation
{\perp}={\perp_{\phi}^{\mathfrak{R}}}
and set of derivations
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}_{\phi}
, we define
\mathcal{X}^{\perp}\coloneqq\{d\in\mathfrak{R}_{\phi^{\perp}}\mid e\perp d\text{ for all }e\in\mathcal{X}\}
.

We write
d\perp_{\phi}d^{\prime}
(or simply
d\perp d^{\prime}
) if the set of derivations
\mathfrak{R}
(and formula
\phi
) can be inferred from context. Thus, unless specified otherwise, when a set
\mathcal{X}
has been introduced in a context such as “
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}_{\phi}
” the relation
\perp
denotes
\perp_{\phi}^{\mathfrak{R}}
. In particular,
\mathcal{X}^{\perp\perp}
means
\mathcal{X}^{\perp_{\phi}\perp_{\phi^{\perp}}}
.

Definition 41

(Reducibility candidate)
.

Let
\mathfrak{R}
be a set of derivations. A (
\mathfrak{R}
-)
reducibility candidate
is a set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}_{\phi}
satisfying
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}^{\perp\perp}
.

Intuitively, a
\mathfrak{R}
-reducibility candidate is a set of derivations that preserve membership in
\mathfrak{R}
when interacting by a cut rule with derivations from the dual candidates. In other words, reducibility candidates carve out sets of derivations of
\mathfrak{R}
satisfying a “composability property”. In this paper we will focus on
\mathfrak{N}
-reducibility candidates and
\mathfrak{E}{}
-reducibility candidates.

In what follows, the class of derivations
\mathfrak{R}
is considered fixed (and arbitrary) unless stated otherwise.
Orthogonality of derivations satisfies several well-known properties which do not depend on the choice of
\mathfrak{R}
.

Proposition 42

(See, e.g.,
[
5
]
)
.

For any sets
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}
:

•

\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
implies
\mathcal{Y}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\perp}

•

(\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y})^{\perp}=\mathcal{X}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{Y}^{\perp}

•

\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\perp\perp}

•

\mathcal{X}^{\perp\perp\perp}=\mathcal{X}^{\perp}
. In other words,
\mathcal{X}^{\perp}
is always a reducibility candidate.

Proposition 43
.

\mathfrak{R}
-reducibility candidates (ordered by
\subseteq
) form a complete lattice.

Proof.

Given an arbitrary collection of candidates
S
, it is easy to check that
(\bigcup S)^{\perp\perp}
is its least upper bound in the lattice, and
\bigcap S
its greatest lower bound. We just check the minimality of
(\bigcup S)^{\perp\perp}
: any upper bound
\mathcal{Y}
satisfies
\bigcup S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
, and hence
(\bigcup S)^{\perp\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\perp\perp}=\mathcal{Y}
. Concerning the greatest lower bound, the only non-trivial thing is that it is a reducibility candidate, but it suffices to observe that
\bigcap S=\bigcap_{\mathcal{X}\in S}\mathcal{X}^{\perp\perp}=(\bigcup_{\mathcal{X}\in S}\mathcal{X}^{\perp})^{\perp}
. The least reducibility candidate is
\emptyset^{\perp\perp}
and the greatest is
\mathfrak{R}
.
∎

Having a complete lattice, we can use the Knaster–Tarski theorem: any monotone function
f
on reducibility candidates admits a least fixed point
\mathsf{lfp}\left(f\right)
and a greatest fixed point
\mathsf{gfp}\left(f\right)
in the lattice of candidates.

Our first cut elimination proof is based on

18

\mathfrak{N}

Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we developed cut elimination methods for ill-founded
\mu\mathsf{MALL}
based on Tait and Girard’s reducibility candidates. Our result addresses one of the critical aspects of ill-founded proof theory, namely the interaction between infinitary cut elimination and global correctness criteria, and provides a robust and modular framework for reasoning about infinitary proofs.

We view this work as a step toward a uniform and proof-theoretic approach to cut elimination for ill-founded systems. To this end we envisage adapting our results to other contexts, such as intuitionistic logic, and (possibly higher-order)
