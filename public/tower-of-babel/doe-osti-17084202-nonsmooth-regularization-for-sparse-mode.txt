ABSTRACT
Modal-based design, testing, and model calibration often require selecting the computed modes that
best explain a set of target modes. Least-squares modal projection objectives provide a differentiable
local framework when the relevant modal subspaces are known, but they may converge to incorrect
designs when many computed modes are available or when the current design is far from the target.
This report investigates nonsmooth regularization strategies for sparse mode selection in modal
optimization. We study penalties and constraints that encourage each target mode to be represented
by a small number of computed modes, with the goal of improving robustness of modal projection
objectives away from the target design. Numerical experiments on analytic benchmark problems
illustrate both the promise and limitations of these approaches: sparsity can help structure the
mode-selection problem, but sparse representations alone may not identify the physically correct
modal correspondence. The results motivate future mode-selection methods that combine sparsity
with additional physical information such as mode type, symmetry, localization, or other modal
features.

1. INTRODUCTION

Optimization problems in structural dynamics often rely on modal data: natural frequencies, mode
shapes, or combinations of both. In model calibration and non-destructive testing, the target modal
data are typically obtained from experiment. In design, testing, and simulation workflows, the
target data may instead come from another computational model. For example, one may wish to
design a test fixture or mock hardware so that a component exhibits modal behavior similar to its
behavior in a larger subsystem. In each of these settings, the inverse or design problem requires
comparing computed modes at the current design to a set of target modes.
A natural way to perform this comparison is through a least-squares modal projection objective.
Given a matrix 𝐴(𝑥) whose columns are computed modes at design variables 𝑥, and a matrix 𝐵
of target modes, the modal projection error objective measures how well the target modes can be
represented in the range of 𝐴(𝑥). Equivalently, for each target mode 𝑏𝑖 , one solves a least-squares
problem

This formulation has an important local differentiability advantage: it depends on the subspace
spanned by the computed modes rather than on a particular basis of eigenvectors. In companion
work, we show that this subspace viewpoint leads to a differentiability-consistent adjoint framework
for modal optimization, including cases with repeated eigenvalues. In particular, when the objective
depends only on isolated modal subspaces, the difficult nullspace term appearing in classical
repeated-eigenvalue eigenvector sensitivities does not contribute to the objective derivative. Thus,
repeated eigenvalues can be handled with essentially the same implementation complexity as simple
eigenvalues.
The present report addresses a complementary difficulty: mode selection. The least-squares
projection objective is locally well behaved once the relevant computed modal subspace has been
selected, but optimization may still fail when the current design is far from the target design. In
that regime, the set of computed modes that best represents the target data may differ from the
physically correct modes. Moreover, if too many computed modes are included in 𝐴(𝑥), then
the least-squares problem may represent target modes using unintended linear combinations of
computed modes. This can reduce the objective value without identifying the desired design
variables. Thus, differentiability of the local objective does not by itself solve the global mode-
selection problem.
This report investigates nonsmooth regularization strategies intended to encourage sparse or struc-
tured selection of computed modes. The motivating idea is that each target mode should ideally be

represented by a small number of computed modes, and in the simplest case by a single computed
mode. We therefore consider augmentations of the modal projection objective of the form

                                       min Φ(𝑥, 𝑌 ) + 𝜑(𝑌 ),
 𝑥,𝑌

where Φ is the least-squares modal projection error and 𝜑 is a nonsmooth penalty or constraint
designed to promote sparsity in the coefficient matrix 𝑌 . We study several choices of 𝜑, including
ℓ1 -type penalties, ℓ0 -type penalties, and sparsity constraints. These formulations are intended
to reveal whether regularization can guide the optimization toward the physically correct modal
correspondence, especially when many computed modes are available.
The report also considers algorithmic strategies for solving the resulting nonsmooth optimization
problems. We distinguish between joint formulations, in which 𝑥 and 𝑌 are optimized simultane-
ously, and block-coordinate formulations, in which design variables and mode-selection variables
are updated alternately. The latter approach is attractive because the design update can use gradient
information from the differentiable least-squares modal objective, while the mode-selection update
can exploit proximal or combinatorial structure in the regularization term. We also investigate
strategies based on segregating modes by type, so that mode selection occurs within physically
meaningful groups rather than over a single undifferentiated collection of computed modes.
The goal of this report is exploratory. We do not claim that nonsmooth regularization provides
a complete solution to mode selection in all modal optimization problems. Rather, we document
what works, what fails, and why. In particular, we show that some regularized formulations can
recover the desired design variables in academic examples when initialized near the correct design
or when the modal structure is sufficiently separated. However, we also observe failure modes:
ℓ1 -type penalties may select dense or fractional combinations of modes, regularized objectives
may prefer incorrect computed subspaces far from the target design, and nonsmoothness from
mode switching can complicate convergence. These observations motivate a more structured view
of mode selection, combining subspace-based differentiable objectives with problem-dependent
selection rules or regularizers.
The remainder of the report is organized as follows. First, we define the regularized modal
projection formulations and describe the sparsity-promoting penalties considered. We then discuss
joint optimization methods and block-coordinate methods for the coupled design and mode-selection
problem. Finally, we present numerical experiments illustrating the behavior of these approaches
on academic modal optimization examples, with particular attention to whether the methods recover
the known target design and whether the selected coefficient matrix is genuinely sparse.

3.2.5. Conclusions for Joint (𝑥, 𝑌 ) Minimization

Our study of ℓ1 regularization for (1.1) via (3.1) via a “full-space” approach did not yield the

4. CONCLUSIONS

We investigated nonsmooth regularization strategies for sparse mode selection in modal optimiza-
tion. The motivation was the following practical difficulty: least-squares objectives are locally
attractive when the correct computed modal subspace is available, but they can represent target
modes using unintended combinations of computed modes when the current design is far from
the target or when many computed modes are included. In that setting, the least-squares objec-
tive may decrease without identifying the physically correct design variables or the desired modal
correspondence.

We first reviewed unregularized least-squares modal projection approaches. The standard MPE
objective can recover the correct design from some initial conditions, primarily in 2D. Using
analytic information to “cheat” by selecting the physically corresponding modes substantially
improves convergence in some cases, i.e. the fixed 2D region. This confirms that the MPE
objective is effective if one knows which computed modes to select. Alternating between physically
meaningful mode classes, such as compressive and solenoidal modes in the analytic brick example,
also improves performance in the tested two-dimensional setting. While mode selection does help
the MPE objective converge to the correct design, the 3D experiments indicate that even these
techniques do not generalize beyond toy examples.

We then explored several nonsmooth regularization strategies intended to promote sparse mode
selection through the coefficient matrix 𝑌 . The methods to solve these approaches were broken
into two sections: “full-space” approaches which minimized (𝑥, 𝑌 ) jointly, and “block” approaches
which minimized in 𝑥 then 𝑌 . The ℓ1 formulations did not produce the desired integer-like mode
selection for both types of algorithms. It often selected fractional combinations of computed
modes, and was sensitive to the regularization parameter. Full-space nonsmooth optimization
approaches, including trust-region, reformulated ℓ1 , bundle, and gradient-sampling methods, did
not reliably overcome the mode-selection difficulty and were generally less effective than simpler
block-coordinate approaches.

Block-coordinate methods provided a more flexible computational framework and allowed testing
of different regularizers. The ℓ0 -type penalties and sparsity constraints were more consistent with
the desired sparse-selection behavior than ℓ1 or elastic-net penalties. These methods also converged
only from limited regions of the design domain. We conclude that sparsity by itself is not sufficient
to identify the physically correct modal correspondence.

The main conclusion is that nonsmooth regularization is useful as a diagnostic and structuring
tool, but it is not a standalone solution to mode selection. The analytic examples show that correct
mode selection can make the modal projection objective more effective in specific test cases, while
naive sparsity-promoting penalties can still select the wrong modes. Future work should therefore

 19

combine sparsity with additional physical information, such as mode type, symmetry, localization,
frequency ordering within classes, or other modal features.
Overall, the results support a two-part view of modal optimization. Differentiable least-squares
modal subspace objectives provide a sound local sensitivity framework once the relevant modal
subspaces are identified. The remaining challenge is global mode selection: determining which
computed subspaces should be compared to the target data as the design changes. Nonsmooth
regularization can help expose this challenge and guide future algorithms, but successful practical
methods will likely require regularization informed by the physics of the modes rather than sparsity
alone.
