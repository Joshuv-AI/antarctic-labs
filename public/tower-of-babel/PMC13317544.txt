Clinical Characteristics and Risk Factors of Carbapenem-Resistant Escherichia coli in Patients with Urinary Tract Infections: Development of a Predictive Nomogram
Authors: Yiling Huang, Jue Wang, Min Gu, Yuqiao Xu, Wenying Xia, Yanfei Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli (CREC) is a growing threat in urinary tract infections (UTIs). This study aimed to analyze the clinical characteristics and risk factors of patients with CREC UTIs and to construct a prediction nomogram.MethodsThis case–control study retrospectively analyzed clinical data of 162 patients with CREC UTIs (2018–2024). One hundred and forty-eight cases were matched 1:1 with non-CREC UTI controls by department, gender, and age (±1 year). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified independent risk factors of CREC UTIs. R software was used to construct a prediction nomogram, followed by internal validation.ResultsCREC urine isolation rate was 1.90% (162/8517). Among 162 CREC patients, 35.80% were male, the mean age was 60.21 ± 17.28 years. Patients’ source departments included urology (42, 25.93%), outpatient clinics (23, 14.20%), and ICU (16, 9.88%). 56.2% of patients had underlying urinary tract diseases. CREC strains exhibited >90% resistance to most antibiotics. CREC group had significantly higher UTI reinfection rate than non-CREC group (P=0.002). Independent risk factors for CREC UTIs included recurrent UTIs (≥3 times per year), urinary system stone, surgical procedures (within 1 year), catheterization duration ≥7 days (within 3 months), ≥2 antibacterial drugs used pre-infection (within 1 month), and Carbapenems used pre-infection (within 1 month) (P < 0.05). The C-index of the nomogram constructed based on these six indicators was 0.868 (95% CI: 0.831–0.917), with a mean absolute error of 0.049 on the calibration curve.ConclusionPatients with CREC UTIs were predominantly middle-aged and elderly women. Most patients had underlying urinary system diseases. CREC strains exhibited multidrug resistance. Patients had a significantly higher recurrence rate of UTIs. A nomogram based on recurrent UTIs, urinary stone, surgical procedures, urinary catheterization duration ≥7 days, ≥2 antibacterial drugs, and Carbapenems pre-infection displayed high accuracy and consistency in predicting CREC UTIs.Keywords: carbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli, urinary tract infection, risk factor, nomogram, model

Background

The rising global prevalence of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) represents a critical threat to public health, severely limiting therapeutic options.1 Following Klebsiella pneumoniae, CREC is the second most prevalent CRE pathogen and its global incidence has risen significantly.2 Urinary tract infections (UTIs), for which E. coli is the predominant pathogen, are particularly affected by this trend.3 The epidemiological characteristics of CREC exhibit significant regional variations, and the strains demonstrate a complex array of drug resistance mechanisms, posing substantial challenges for clinical treatment.2,4 Current research on CREC has primarily focused on its epidemiology and molecular mechanisms, and the findings have not been sufficiently translated into practical tools that clinicians can use to identify patients at high risk for CREC UTIs.2,3,5 Early and accurate identification of such patients is crucial for initiating appropriate empirical therapy and infection control measures, yet reliable, individualized prediction tools are currently lacking. A clinical prediction nomogram, which integrates independent risk factors into an intuitive visual scoring tool, offers a potential solution to bridge this gap between research and practice. Such a model could significantly enhance clinical decision-making by providing individualized risk assessments. Therefore, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical characteristics of patients with CREC UTIs from 2018 to 2024, explored the risk factors of CREC UTIs, and constructed a visual predictive nomogram model, which aims to provide scientific evidence for early identification of high-risk patients, formulation of precise prevention and control measures, and optimization of empirical treatment regimens.

Methods

Study Population

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted from January 2018 to December 2024 in Jiangsu Province Hospital, which is the largest tertiary hospital in Jiangsu Province, China, with 4500 beds. Patients with CREC UTIs were enrolled in the study. The collection time and method of urine specimens were based on the Chinese Guidelines for the testing of urine specimens in the clinical microbiology laboratory (WS/T 489). For non-catheterized patients, clean-catch midstream urine was collected after at least 4 hours of bladder retention; for catheterized patients, urine was collected from the catheter port after clamping for 10–20 minutes; for patients with long-term indwelling catheters, specimens were collected after replacement of the catheter. Inclusion criteria: (1) Escherichia coli isolated from urine cultures; (2) The patients complied with the diagnostic criteria for UTI;6 (3) Antimicrobial susceptibility results of Escherichia coli showed resistance to imipenem or meropenem. Exclusion criteria: (1) Only the first result of repeated isolates from the same patient is included. (2). Patients with incomplete clinical records. Taking patients with CREC UTIs as the case group, the control group was selected from patients with UTIs caused by non-carbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli using a 1:1 matching principle including same department, gender, and age within ±1 year.

Data Collection

We collected patients’ clinical data by reviewing the electronic medical records. Collecting data included demographic (including gender, age, and department), underlying disease (including diabetes, hypertension, cerebral infarction, malignant tumor, immune system disorders, and immunocompromised or not), urinary system disease (including chronic kidney disease [CKD], urinary system stone, urinary system malignancies, neurogenic bladder, lupus nephritis, prostatic hyperplasia, post-renal transplantation, and congenital/traumatic renal structural abnormalities), microbiological factors (including combination with other infections, previous UTIs within 1 year, complicated UTIs, and recurrent UTIs), medical environment exposure (including ICU history within 6 months, recent hospitalizations [within 6 months], prolonged hospitalization [>14days], and long-term care facility [>6 months]), invasive procedure (including surgical procedures within 1 year [abdominal surgery, urologic surgery], continuous renal replacement therapy [CRRT], history of double-J stent placement, nephrostomy/cystostomy, pre-infection catheter use within 3 months, indwelling catheter duration >7 days within 3 months), and catheterization during cultivation), antibiotic use pre-infection within 1 month (including antibiotics used pre-infection, use of Carbapenems, use of quinolones, use of β-lactams, and use of >2 antibacterial drugs), and the patients’ outcome within 1 year (including improvement and reinfection or not).

Microbiological Identification and Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

Bacterial identification was performed using the automated bacterial identification system VITEK-2 Compact and VITEK MS (BioMérieux, France). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using the VITEK-2 Compact or the disk diffusion method (Oxiod, UK). Susceptibility results were interpreted according to the CLSI M100-S30 standard. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was applied as quality control strain.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 24.0 statistical software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage) and compared by the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test as deemed appropriate. Continuous normally distributed quantitative data variables were presented as the mean ± SD and compared by Student’s t-test. The clinical characteristics of patients with UTI in CREC group and non-CREC group were compared through univariate analysis, and variables with P< 0.05 were entered into Forward Likelihood Ratio multivariate regression analyses to identify the independent risk factors of CREC UTIs. A nomogram to predict CREC UTIs based on the above-mentioned independent risk factors was established using Rms package of R software (version 4.4.2). Internal validation of the established nomogram was performed by 1000 bootstrap samples (repeated sampling of the original data 1000 times), C-index and calibration curve was employed to evaluate the performance of the model.

Results

General and Group Conditions of Patients with CREC UTIs

A total of 8515 cases of Escherichia coli were isolated from urine samples of patients with UTIs From January 2018 to December 2024. Among which there were 162 cases of CREC and 8,353 cases of non-CREC, with a CREC isolation rate of 1.90%. Among the 162 patients with CREC, 58 (35.80%) were male and 104 (64.20%) were female. The mean age was 60.21 ± 17.28 years. Primary departments included Urology (42, 25.93%), Outpatient Clinics (23, 14.20%), and ICU (16, 9.88%). Among the 8,353 patients with non-CREC, 1,946 (23.3%) were male and 6,407 (76.7%) were female, with a mean age of 61.68 ± 15.41 years. Primary departments included Urology (2,128, 25.48%), Outpatient (2,070, 24.78%), ICU (618, 7.40%). The proportion of males was significantly higher in patients with CREC than in patients with non-CREC (χ2=13.810, P<0.001), while no statistically significant difference of age between the two groups (t=1.197, P=0.231). After 1:1 matching of patients with CREC and non-CREC based on age ±1 year, sex, and department, both groups comprised 148 cases (Figure 1).

Antibiotic Susceptibility Testing of CREC Strains

Antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed that CREC urinary tract infection strains exhibited high resistance to most antibiotics. The resistance rates exceed 90% for penicillins and their inhibitors, cephalosporins and their inhibitors (except ceftazidime/avibactam), cephalosporins, carbapenems, and fluoroquinolones (except norfloxacin), as well as tetracyclines. The resistance rates to amikacin and ceftazidime/avibactam were slightly lower at 71.58% and 81.80%, respectively. Clinical Characteristics of Patients with CREC UTIs

CREC UTIs predominantly affect middle-aged and elderly women, with most being hospitalized patients, and approximately 65.4% of patients had a recent history of hospitalization. Over half of the patients had underlying urinary system conditions, with urinary system stones being the most common. About 1/3 of patients had a history of UTI within the past year. Most patients underwent invasive procedures prior to infection, with 57.4% having recently undergone surgical procedures. 85.8% patients had received antibiotic therapy before infection including Carbapenems, quinolones and β- lactams. Risk Factors for Patients with CREC UTIs

Univariate analysis revealed significant differences between the CREC group and the non-CREC group across multiple variables: Regarding medical conditions, the CREC group exhibited higher prevalence rates of hypertension, urinary tract stone, and neurogenic bladder. In terms of microbiological factors, the CREC group had higher proportions of patients with combination infections at other sites, previous UTIs, complicated UTIs, and recurrent UTIs. Regarding healthcare exposure, the CREC group had significantly higher rates of ICU history and recent hospitalization compared to the non-CREC group. For invasive procedures, the CREC group underwent significantly more procedures other than abdominal surgery and nephrostomy/cystostomy. Antibiotic use prior to infection was also higher in the CREC group. Both groups of patients with UTIs had favorable outcomes, but the recurrence rate of UTIs was significantly higher in the CREC group than in the non-CREC group, as detailed in Table 3. Multivariate analysis revealed that recurrent UTIs (≥3 times per year), urinary tract stones, surgical procedures (within 1 year), indwelling catheter duration ≥7 days (within 3 months), ≥2 Antibacterial drugs used pre-infection (within 1 month), and Carbapenems used pre-infection (within 1 month) were independent risk factors for patients with CREC UTIs, as shown in Table 4.

Establishment and Validation of the Nomogram for Patients with CREC UTIs

Based on the six independent risk factors derived from the multivariate logistic regression analysis, a predictive nomogram model for patients with CREC UTIs was established using R software, as shown in Figure 2 (assigning 0: No, 1: Yes). In the resulting nomogram model, the categorical attributes of each predictor correspond to the values on the top single-item score axis, representing the score for each factor. The sum of these scores constitutes the total score, which corresponds to the value on the bottom predicted probability axis, indicating the predicted probability of CREC UTIs. Internal validation results showed that the C-index is 0.868 (95% CI: 0.831–0.917), indicating high discrimination and accuracy. The calibration curve demonstrated an average absolute error between predicted and actual probabilities of 0.049, reflecting good consistency (Figure 3).

Discussion

According to the data of China Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance System (CARSS) (https://data.carss.cn/publish/indicator), the national average detection rate of CREC from 2019 to 2024 was 1.63%, with variations observed across different regions. Jiangsu Province reported a higher rate of 2.0%. Differences in CREC detection rates were also noted depending on the specimen type. Notably, urine specimens showed an average CREC detection rate of 1.43%. Although this is not the highest rate among sample types, urine yielded the largest absolute number of CREC isolates, underscoring its clinical importance as a key reservoir. Studies have also demonstrated marked geographic heterogeneity in carbapenem resistance among urinary E. coli isolates. A 2025 study from Oman involving complicated urinary tract infections reported CRE in 1.7% of E. coli isolates,7 while a recent Indian study of 1,254 urinary E. coli isolates found an ertapenem resistance rate of 7.18%.8 In our study, the CREC isolation rate from urine was 1.90%. The burden of urinary CREC varies substantially across countries and healthcare settings, which may be related to antimicrobial exposure, patient population, and infection control practices. Therefore, localized surveillance and tailored infection control strategies are of particular importance.

Our findings showed that CREC of urines exhibit extensive multidrug resistance, showing prevalent resistance not only to carbapenems but also to quinolones and cephalosporins, which further limits the options for conventional treatment. This severe antimicrobial resistance phenotype may be largely attributable to the prevalent resistance genotypes among CREC in China. Studies have shown that carbapenemase production is the primary mechanism underlying carbapenem resistance in CREC. Among these, the New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM) encoded by the blaNDM gene is the predominant carbapenemase type in CREC isolates of China, with blaNDM-5 being the most prevalent, followed by OXA-48 and KPC.9,10 An analysis of the EnteroBase database revealed that ST167 has become the most common ST associated with CREC UTIs and this ST is closely linked to the carriage of blaNDM-5, frequently exhibits a multidrug resistance pattern, and severely limited the clinical therapeutic options, thereby posing a significant public health threat.3 In this context, the finding that polymyxin and tigecycline maintain high in vitro sensitivity are of greater clinical value, providing a reliable alternative treatment option. Moreover, CREC strains demonstrated some susceptibility to aztreonam and ceftazidime-avibactam. Notably, the combination of aztreonam with ceftazidime-avibactam may emerge as a key strategy for managing NDM-producing CREC strains and improving patient outcomes.11 In addition, some novel antimicrobial agents, such as Plazomicin and Eravacycline, may also play a positive role in the treatment of CREC UTIs.12

Current research has extensively explored the epidemiology and resistance mechanisms of CRE, and most studies focus on overall CRE analysis or critical infections.9,13–15 Systematic research specifically targeting the clinical characteristics and independent risk factors of CREC UTIs remains insufficient, with notable regional variations. It is hoped that this study will help fill some of the gaps in this area. Previous studies have indicated that CREC infections have a poorer prognosis, significantly higher mortality rates, greater financial burdens, and longer hospital stays compared to those with non-CREC infections.15–17 A 2025 retrospective cohort study from Peru reported a 30-day mortality rate of 38.1% in the CRE – Complicated UTIs group versus 11.7% in the CSE- Complicated UTIs group, along with significantly higher hospitalization costs.17 Our findings revealed that the reinfection rate of UTI in the CREC group was significantly higher than that in non-CREC group, Cheng et al similarly found that CREC is associated with a higher risk of reinfection, which presented a major long-term clinical challenge18 Therefore, this study aimed to identify the clinical characteristics of patients with CREC UTIs, explore their independent risk factors through retrospective analysis, and establish a predictive nomogram of CREC UTIs, thereby providing localized, precise evidence-based guidance for the clinical management of this type of infection.

In our study we found that CREC UTIs predominantly occurred in elderly patients, although female accounted for most patients with CREC UTIs, the proportion of male patients was significantly higher in the CREC group compared to the non-CREC group, which suggested male may be a risk factor for CREC UTIs.19 This may be related to the higher prevalence of urological conditions, such as prostatic hyperplasia, among elderly men, which can predispose them to urinary flow obstruction, other iatrogenic factors may also contribute. Additionally, by analyzing the clinical characteristics of patients, we found that patients with CREC UTIs were often accompanied by urinary system diseases, frequently involved invasive procedures, and were commonly preceded by antibiotic use. A review by Zhang et al systematically summarized the risk factors for CRE‑associated UTIs, confirming that invasive urological procedures, indwelling catheters, and a history of carbapenem use are key contributing factors, while the urinary system itself represents a major anatomical site for CRE occurrence and infection.20 To further investigate the risk factors for CREC UTIs, we matched for confounding factors including age, gender, and department, laying the foundation for further analysis of other potential risk factors.

Our results indicated that recurrent UTIs (≥3 times per year), urinary tract stones, surgical procedures (within 1 year), indwelling catheter duration ≥7 days (within 3 months), ≥2 Antibacterial drugs used pre-infection (within 1 month), and Carbapenems used pre-infection (within 1 month) were independent risk factors for patients with CREC UTIs. The visualized nomogram prediction model established based on these risk factors demonstrates high accuracy, suggesting its significant potential for practical application. As we know, this is one of the first nomogram prediction model in predicting CREC UTIs.

Prior UTI is considered a risk factor for UTIs.21 Recurrent UTIs can subject patients to prolonged selective pressure from antimicrobial agents. Moreover, such patients often have underlying structural or functional abnormalities of the urinary system, which may significantly increase the risk of CREC infections.22 This finding is consistent with a recent large cohort study on carbapenem-resistant UTIs, in which 23.2% of patients had a UTI episode within the previous 6 months, and 38.0% of follow-up CR-UTI episodes were attributed to relapse.23 Urinary system stones, as significant anatomical anomalies of urine tract, can alter urodynamics through obstruction and promote bacterial biofilm formation, thereby enabling evasion of antibiotic clearance and facilitating the development of antibiotic resistance.

Surgical procedures have been identified as a risk factor for CREC infection in some studies, a finding confirmed by the present research.15,24 Patients who have undergone recent surgical procedures are often exposed to invasive interventions and prophylactic antibiotic use. The disruption of immune barrier function and imbalances in the host microbiome create favorable conditions for CREC colonization and infection.15 Indwelling urinary catheters is an independent risk factor of UTIs,1,21 prolonged indwelling urinary catheters can directly compromise the physical barriers of the urinary tract, promote bacterial biofilm formation, and increase the risk of CREC infection. When conditions permit, early removal of indwelling urinary catheters is crucial for controlling CREC infections. The use of carbapenem antibiotics has been confirmed as one of the risk factors for CREC infection, our findings also supported it.5,15,16,25 The result indicates that the emergence of CREC is directly linked to the selective pressure exerted by carbapenem drugs. Furthermore, the concurrent use of two or more antimicrobial agents can suppress the host’s normal flora, creating favorable conditions for the colonization and infection of drug-resistant bacteria. Therefore, in clinical practice, it is essential not only to avoid unnecessary carbapenem exposure but also to carefully evaluate combination therapy regimens to curb the emergence and spread of CREC infections. The results of this study indicate that CREC UTIs is not an isolated microbiological event but rather the outcome of multiple interacting factors, including the patient’s underlying urinary system diseases, prior infection history, invasive procedure, and selective pressure from antimicrobial agents.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design may introduce selection bias. Second, the absence of data on other potential risk factors and unmeasured confounders may have affected our results. Third, although internal validation was performed, the lack of external validation means the nomogram’s performance in other populations remains unknown. Fourth, the modest sample size also limited statistical power.

Conclusion

In summary, patients with CREC UTIs face an elevated risk of reinfection, and CREC strains exhibit high levels of antibiotic resistance. A nomogram incorporating recurrent UTIs, urinary system stone, surgical procedures, urinary catheterization duration ≥7 days, ≥2 antibacterial drugs, and Carbapenems used pre-infection demonstrated high accuracy and consistency in predicting the risk of CREC UTIs. Rational use of antimicrobial agents, along with standardizing and reducing unnecessary invasive procedures and shorten the duration of such procedures, may help lower the risk of CREC UTIs. This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective single-center design, and relatively small sample size, which may introduce bias. Further validation through large-scale, multicenter studies is warranted.
