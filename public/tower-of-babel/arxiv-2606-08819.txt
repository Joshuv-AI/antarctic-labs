Model Selection for SLOPE Models: A Bayesian Perspective
Fabio Feser, Marina Evangelou

Abstract
Sorted ℓ 1 \ell_{1} Penalized Estimation (SLOPE) models, that perform either variable or group selection, control the false discovery rate (FDR) under orthogonal settings with known noise, but such settings are rare in practice. Under general conditions, cross-validation is the default model selection approach for SLOPE, yet it targets predictive performance rather than FDR control. We address this gap for the SLOPE family of models by proposing new Bayesian approaches, Bayesian Group SLOPE (BGSLOPE) and Bayesian Sparse-group SLOPE (BSGS). BGSLOPE and BSGS embed group-based SLOPE models into a spike-and-slab framework, with BSGS providing a continuous spike-and-slab framework for sparse-group models. We further introduce Two-step Orthogonal (TSO), which transforms a general setting into an orthogonal one to recover SLOPE’s FDR control properties. Through extensive synthetic and real data studies comparing all major model selection strategies for SLOPE models, the proposed Bayesian models consistently control FDR, achieve higher power, and outperform competing methods in prediction.



1
Introduction

Suppose we observe a design matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and a response vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
p
denotes the number of features and
n
the number of observations. Consider the linear regression setup
\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\sim\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{N}}}_{n}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
, where
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{N}}}_{n}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{n}\sigma^{2})
such that
\sigma^{2}>0
denotes the
noise
. Then, a general penalized regression model is given by

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\lambda)\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\frac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda J(\boldsymbol{\beta};\mathbf{v})\right\},

(1)

where
J(\cdot)
is a convex penalty norm,
\mathbf{v}\succeq 0
are weights, and
\lambda>0
is the tuning parameter.

For any penalized regression model, a key problem is model selection via choosing the
tuning parameter

\lambda
. It controls the sparsity of the fitted model and is proportional to the noise
\sigma^{2}
. Most proposed schemes for
\lambda
are explicit functions of the noise
[
100
]
. As the true noise level is rarely known in practice, estimation or tuning of it is required.

In this manuscript, we consider the problem of model selection for
Sorted
\ell_{1}
Penalized Estimation
(SLOPE) models (Section
2.3
). SLOPE models use sorted norms to achieve false discovery rate (FDR) control under orthogonal designs, making them widely used in genetics and machine learning
[
38
,
45
,
56
,
80
,
105
]
. Grouping information, often found in genetics through biological pathways, can help inform inference and lead to better prediction. In this vein, SLOPE was extended to group regression in Group SLOPE (gSLOPE)
[
18
]
and Sparse-group SLOPE (SGS)
[
31
]
.

Model selection is particularly challenging for SLOPE since the sparsity of the fitted model determines the FDR. An incorrect
\lambda
, therefore, directly compromises FDR control. Theory guarantees FDR control only under orthogonal
\mathbf{X}
with known noise (
\lambda=1
), leaving general settings without guidance.

1.1
Model selection approaches

There are many approaches for model selection for penalized models; here, we broadly group them into
tuning
and
estimation
regimes. To tune
\lambda
, we typically fit a path of
l
models
\mathcal{M}=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\lambda_{l})\}
, where
\lambda_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{l}>0
, and choose the optimal model,
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
, from the path according to some metric. On the other hand, estimation of
\lambda
provides a model
\hat{\mathcal{M}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\hat{\lambda})
. Both approaches aim to obtain a final optimal solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and active set
\hat{S}_{v}=\mathrm{supp}({\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}):=\{i\in[p]:\hat{\beta}_{i}\neq 0\}
, where
[p]:=\{1,\ldots,p\}
, which, importantly for SLOPE models, defines the FDR.

We briefly discuss the relevant approaches here, while the methods used in the simulation studies of Section
5
are described in detail in Appendix
C
.

Tuning along a path.

The most common tuning approach is cross-validation (CV), using predictive performance as the selection metric. In genetics, however, the primary focus is model recovery for FDR control; an objective that does not necessarily align with prediction, particularly since CV tends to select overfitting models
[
63
,
118
]
. When FDR control is the goal, no CV criterion provides finite-sample guarantees
[
16
]
, and
31
show that CV tends to select the most saturated model on the SLOPE path, leading to high FDR levels. Many CV variants exist
[
11
,
44
,
81
,
119
]
(see
4
for a summary).

The Knockoffs procedure
[
7
]
builds on CV by generating synthetic negative-control copies of the input data, independent of the response but matching the input structure. CV is applied to the augmented design, and the Knockoffs filter is used to remove noise variables from
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
, providing FDR control (Appendix
C.3
).

A limitation of path-based tuning is that only one model is selected, discarding information from the rest of the path. Stability selection
[
69
]
addresses this by aggregating results across the full path, providing family-wise error rate guarantees. Stability selection extensions for FDR control exist
[
2
]
, although our experiments show unsatisfactory performance and high computational cost compared to competing methods (Appendix
C.5
).

Noise estimation.

Estimation approaches directly estimate
\sigma^{2}
and use it to fit the final model. Popular procedures include
30
, which uses a two-stage data splitting technique, and
29
, which derives consistent method-of-moments estimators without sparsity assumptions on
\boldsymbol{\beta}
(see
76
for a comprehensive study).

Scaled regression (Section
C.2
) jointly estimates the coefficients and noise by iteratively scaling the tuning parameter by the mean squared residual. It has been adapted for SGS as AS-SGS (Appendix
C.2.1
)
[
31
]
.

Bayesian approaches.

An alternative estimation approach comes from learning the noise alongside the other model parameters in a Bayesian penalized regression model (Section
2.1
). A common approach to this is the spike-and-slab framework (Section
2.2
), which models the noise and signal using a mixture prior. There are both continuous and point-mass implementations of spike-and-slab priors.

51
extends the continuous spike-and-slab framework to SLOPE, forming the Adaptive Bayesian SLOPE (ABSLOPE) (Section
2.4
). Bayesian group-based models have been developed for the lasso using point-mass priors
[
115
]
and continuous priors
[
5
]
. The models proposed in this manuscript extend the continuous spike-and-slab framework to group-based SLOPE models. Section
2
outlines the relevant Bayesian background, and further Bayesian model selection techniques can be found in
[
24
,
52
,
54
,
106
,
112
]
.

Other model selection approaches that do not necessarily fall into the above categories include post-selection inference
[
62
,
75
,
104
]
, information criteria
[
3
,
36
,

,
89
,

,

]
(a review of these is found in
95
), square-root lasso
[

]
, rank lasso

]
, ET-LASSO
[

]
, TREX
[

]
, and estimating the FDR
[

,

]
. For additional approaches to model selection and parameter tuning in high-dimensional regression, see relevant summaries
[
49
,
61
,
114
]
.

A motivating simulation study.

To assess whether existing approaches reliably achieve FDR control for SLOPE models, we conducted a short simulation study comparing methods for estimating
\lambda
or directly controlling the FDR, including CV, Knockoffs, stability selection variants, ET-LASSO, and noise estimation procedures (Appendix
C.5
). None of the approaches produced FDR control across all settings: even the best-performing method, Knockoffs, substantially exceeded the target under moderate and strong correlation. Based on this study, we selected Knockoffs for its comparatively strong performance, and CV for its widespread use, for further investigation in the main simulation study (Section
5
).

These results highlight a fundamental limitation of frequentist model selection for SLOPE: theoretical FDR guarantees hold only under orthogonal designs with known noise. Existing approaches cannot reliably recover this property in general settings. A Bayesian formulation offers a principled alternative, estimating
\sigma^{2}
jointly with
\boldsymbol{\beta}
within the same model structure. This avoids the misalignment of CV, which tunes
\lambda
for predictive performance rather than FDR control.
51
demonstrates this advantage empirically for SLOPE via ABSLOPE (Section
2.4
), and the spike-and-slab LASSO has more broadly been shown to outperform frequentist penalized regression in variable selection
[
6
,
84
]
. The Bayesian formulation further provides uncertainty quantification through inclusion probabilities and extends naturally to hierarchical models that facilitate information sharing across groups
[
14
,
40
]
.

1.2
Contributions

In this manuscript, we develop Bayesian models for group-based SLOPE models for the purpose of model selection under general settings. The models learn the noise along with the other model parameters. To do this, we incorporate the gSLOPE and SGS norms into a spike-and-slab framework to form the Bayesian gSLOPE (BGSLOPE) (Section
3.1
) and the Bayesian SGS (BSGS) (Section
3.2
). BGSLOPE and BSGS introduce novel group-based spike-and-slab frameworks applicable to the wider class of Ordered Weighted
\ell_{1}
(OWL) models (Section
3.3
), with BSGS further extensible to other sparse-group formulations such as the sparse-group lasso
[
93
]
. Our models inherit many of the advantages of Bayesian formulations, including uncertainty quantification and adaptivity to the underlying sparsity structure.

We compare our Bayesian methodology and ABSLOPE (Section
2.4
) with existing model selection approaches across all SLOPE variants, providing practitioners with practical insights for choosing an appropriate method. The methods are evaluated on extensive synthetic (Section
5
) and real-data (Section
6
) studies, with primary emphasis on FDR control and secondary emphasis on predictive performance. Beyond the Bayesian methodology, we extend several existing approaches to group-based SLOPE models, including scaled regression (Appendix
C.2
) and, to our knowledge, the first sparse-group Knockoffs implementation (Appendix
C.3.2
). We also consider two-step approaches designed to combine the advantages of multiple methods (Appendix
C.4
) and propose a new variant, Two-step Orthogonal (TSO) (Section
4
).

2
Background

2.1
Bayesian penalized regression

The connection between penalized regression and Bayesian inference was first established by observing that the lasso estimator corresponds to the posterior mode when each regression coefficient is assigned an independent double exponential (Laplace) prior
[
103
]
. Formally, any penalized estimator admits a Bayesian interpretation given by

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\lambda)=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\pi(\beta_{j}\mid\lambda),\;\;\text{where the penalty function satisfies}

(2)

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{\beta};\lambda)=-\log\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\lambda)=-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log\pi(\beta_{j}\mid\lambda).

(3)

Applied to the lasso, we have
\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\lambda)=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\lambda}{2}e^{-\lambda|\beta_{j}|}

[
84
]
. The Bayesian lasso shares the non-separability property of frequentist SLOPE, allowing variables to share information, but is known to produce biased estimates with suboptimal coefficient shrinkage
[
43
,
83
]
.

2.2
Spike-and-slab framework

The spike-and-slab framework applied to the lasso overcomes issues with bias and suboptimal shrinkage
[
6
]
.
The
continuous
spike-and-slab prior is of the form
[
41
]

\displaystyle\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\sigma^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\left[\left(1-\gamma_{i}\right)\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\tau_{0}^{2}\right)+\gamma_{i}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\tau_{1}^{2}\right)\right]

(4)

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\mid\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta^{\gamma_{i}}(1-\theta)^{1-\gamma_{i}},\quad\theta\sim\pi(\theta),\quad\sigma^{2}\sim\pi\left(\sigma^{2}\right),

(5)

where
\tau_{0}^{2}
models the noise (the
spike
) and
\tau_{1}^{2}
captures the signal (the
slab
), such that
0<\tau_{0}^{2}\ll\tau_{1}^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
is a binary inclusion vector over
2^{p}
models, and
\theta\in(0,1)
is a mixture proportion. A key advantage of the spike-and-slab model is its ability to automatically adapt to the underlying sparsity structure of the data through the mixing parameter
\theta

[
6
]
.

A non-continuous alternative uses a point-mass spike and heavy-tailed slab
[
70
]
. Both formulations enable simultaneous variable selection and parameter estimation, mitigating over-shrinkage by placing sufficient prior mass on large effect sizes
[
51
]
. The spike-and-slab lasso (SSL)
[
84
]
replaces the Gaussian densities with Laplace densities
\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\lambda)=(\lambda/2)e^{-\lambda|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}
, with
\lambda
chosen separately for the spike and slab components.

2.3
SLOPE models

SLOPE is given by the sorted norm
J_{\text{slope}}(\boldsymbol{\beta};\mathbf{v})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}v_{i}|\beta|_{(i)}
, where
v_{1}\geq\ldots\geq v_{p}\geq 0
and
|\beta|_{(1)}\geq\ldots\geq|\beta|_{(p)}

[
16
]
. The weights are designed for variable FDR control and are calculated as the quantiles of a standard Gaussian
v_{i}^{\text{BH}}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\frac{q_{v}i}{2p})
(termed the
Benjamini-Hochberg
(BH) sequence), where
q_{v}\in(0,1)
is the desired variable FDR level. Aside from the FDR properties, SLOPE also clusters strongly correlated features, is asymptotically minimax, and is adaptive to unknown sparsity proportions (which complements a similar property spike-and-slab models hold)
[
35
,
99
]
. SLOPE models have non-separable norms, so are fitted using proximal algorithms.

This manuscript focuses on group-based SLOPE models: assume the variables sit within
m
non-overlapping groups
G_{1},\dots,G_{m}
of sizes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
, then SLOPE was extended to group regression by
Group SLOPE
(gSLOPE)
[
18
]
, with the norm

J_{\text{gslope}}(\boldsymbol{\beta};\mathbf{w})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{j}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2},

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}}
is a vector of the variable coefficients in a group
j\in[m]
. As with SLOPE, the coefficients and weights are ordered:
\sqrt{p_{1}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1)}\|_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\sqrt{p_{m}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(m)}\|_{2}
and
w_{1}\geq\ldots\geq w_{m}\geq 0
. The ordered weights are calculated as

w_{j}=\max_{k=1,\ldots,m}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{k}}}F^{-1}_{\chi_{p_{k}}}\left(1-\frac{q_{g}j}{m}\right)\right\},

(7)

where
F_{\chi_{p_{k}}}
is the CDF of a
\chi
distribution with
p_{k}
degrees of freedom and
q_{g}\in(0,1)
is the desired group FDR level. The maximum criterion in the weights leads to conservative group FDR control and can be relaxed in practice (leading to the mean sequence in Equation
39
).

Sparse-group SLOPE
(SGS)
[
31
]
combines the strengths of SLOPE and gSLOPE via a convex combination of
\alpha\in[0,1]
:

J_{\text{sgs}}(\boldsymbol{\beta};\alpha,\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{p}v_{i}|\beta|_{(i)}+(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}\sqrt{p_{j}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2},

(8)

where the sorting procedures of both SLOPE and gSLOPE apply onto the corresponding penalties. Unlike gSLOPE, SGS does not require all variables within an active group to be active, allowing noise variables to be shrunk to zero.
31
derives penalty sequences for bi-level FDR control (Equations
46
and
47
in Appendix
A.1
), finding that the best performance uses a combination of the SGS variable mean (Equation
40
) and gSLOPE group mean (Equation
39
) sequences. These weights are used throughout this manuscript, except for AS-SGS (Appendix
C.2
).

2.4
SLOPE in a Bayesian context

90
develops a Bayesian formulation of SLOPE, yielding
Bayesian SLOPE
, given by

\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2};\mathbf{v})\propto e^{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{p}v_{i}|\beta|_{(i)}}\propto\prod_{i=1}^{p}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sigma}v_{r_{v}(\boldsymbol{\beta},i)}|\beta_{i}|\right).

(9)

The penalty has been reformulated using the rank function
r_{v}(\boldsymbol{\beta},i)\in[p]
, which gives the rank of
\beta_{i}
in decreasing order. This prior was incorporated into the spike-and-slab framework in the
Adaptive Bayesian SLOPE
(ABSLOPE)
[
51
]
. SLOPE’s non-separability and adaptivity to unknown sparsity make it a natural fit for the spike-and-slab framework.

The ABSLOPE prior is given by

\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},c,\sigma^{2};\mathbf{v}\right)\propto c^{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}\left(\gamma_{i}=1\right)}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\exp\left\{-a_{i}\left|\beta_{i}\right|\frac{1}{\sigma}v_{r_{v}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta},i)}\right\},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
is the variable inclusion vector and
\mathbf{A}=\text{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p})
is a diagonal matrix with elements
a_{i}=c\gamma_{i}+(1-\gamma_{i})
, where
c\in(0,1)
represents the ratio of the average signal strengths between the active and non-active variables
[
51
]
. The spike-and-slab influence comes via
c
and can be seen more clearly by writing the ABSLOPE prior as

\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},c,\sigma^{2};\mathbf{v}\right)\propto c^{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}\left(\gamma_{i}=1\right)}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}\left[\underbrace{c\gamma_{i}\left|\beta_{i}\right|v_{r_{v}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta},i)}}_{\text{slab}}+\underbrace{(1-\gamma_{i})\left|\beta_{i}\right|v_{r_{v}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\beta},i)}}_{\text{spike}}\right]\right\},

so that since
c<1
, the signal (slab) variables are penalized less; this is the purpose of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
.

The ABSLOPE prior (Equation
10
) is equivalent to the Bayesian SLOPE prior (Equation
9
) under the weighted design matrix
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}^{-1}
, so the
maximum a posteriori
(MAP) under ABSLOPE reduces to solving the frequentist SLOPE with this weighted design
[
51
]
. ABSLOPE was originally developed for missing data settings by jointly modeling
\mathbf{X}
. While this is not a problem of interest in this manuscript, we note that our methodology readily extends to cover this case.

ABSLOPE is a hierarchical Bayesian model with priors

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\mid\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\theta^{\gamma_{i}}(1-\theta)^{1-\gamma_{i}},\quad\theta\sim\text{Beta}(d_{1},d_{2}),\quad c\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],\quad\pi(\sigma^{2})\propto\sigma^{-2},

(11)

where
\theta=\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1;\theta)
represents the sparsity level of the coefficients (with
d_{1},d_{2}>0
defining the Beta prior distribution) and an improper uninformative prior is placed on
\sigma^{2}
. Values of
\theta
close to zero generate sparse models, which can be seen by
\mathbb{E}[\theta]=d_{1}/(d_{1}+d_{2})
. ABSLOPE is optimized using the Stochastic Approximation Expectation–Maximization (SAEM) algorithm (Section
3.4
)
[
59
]
.

3
Group-based Bayesian SLOPE models

We extend the spike-and-slab framework (Section
2.2
) to group-based SLOPE models to give Bayesian Group SLOPE (BGSLOPE) (Section
3.1
) and Bayesian Sparse-group SLOPE (BSGS) (Section
3.2
). Both BGSLOPE and BSGS are fitted using the SAEM algorithm (Section
3.4
). Both models are developed within the linear regression framework, as SLOPE’s FDR properties are derived under this setting. The methodology can be extended to broader settings by modifying the likelihood term, though this is beyond the scope of this manuscript.

The closest existing work is
115
, which develops Bayesian group and sparse-group lasso models using point-mass spike-and-slab priors and Gibbs sampling. Our models differ in three key ways. First, we use SLOPE norms specifically designed for FDR control, rather than lasso-type ones. This allows our models to inherit the useful properties of SLOPE: non-separability, adaptivity to unknown sparsity, and the clustering property. Second, we adopt continuous priors, which are less computationally prohibitive in high dimensions
[
6
]
. Third, we fit via an EM algorithm rather than Gibbs sampling.

The non-separability of SLOPE further distinguishes our approach: coefficients must be updated jointly, enabling information sharing across variables
[
83
]
, which is incompatible with the independent priors of
115
. In the sparse-group setting, both their Bayesian SGL and our BSGS use two sets of binary indicators for bi-level selection, but BSGS represents the first continuous spike-and-slab framework for sparse-group models. Other Bayesian sparse-group models include
[
21
,
25
,
94
]
.

3.1
Bayesian gSLOPE (BGSLOPE)

This manuscript presents the first Bayesian implementation for gSLOPE. First, the gSLOPE penalty (Equation
6

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\boldsymbol{\beta},j)}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2}

\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2};\mathbf{w})=C\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sigma}w_{r_{g}(\boldsymbol{\beta},j)}\sqrt{p_{j}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2}\right),

(12)

3.2
Bayesian SGS (BSGS)

SGS (Equation
8
) applies two levels of penalization and sorting to produce bi-level selection, which makes its Bayesian formulation more involved. It has a Laplace prior given by

\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2};\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sigma}\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{p}v_{r_{v}(\boldsymbol{\beta},i)}|\beta_{i}|-\frac{1}{\sigma}(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{r_{g}(\boldsymbol{\beta},j)}\sqrt{p_{j}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2}\right),

(14)

where, as for ABSLOPE and BGSLOPE, the sorting occurs through the weights and rank functions.

The
Bayesian Sparse-group SLOPE
(BSGS) is given by the hierarchical model

\displaystyle\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\gamma},c_{g},c_{v},\sigma^{2};\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}\right)

\displaystyle\propto c_{g}^{\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)}c_{v}^{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|\hat{a}_{i}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\beta},i)}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}\|\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}(1-\alpha)w_{r_{g}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\beta},j)}\right\},

(15)

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\mid\theta_{g})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{g}^{\gamma_{j}}(1-\theta_{g})^{1-\gamma_{j}},

(16)

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\delta}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\theta_{v})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\prod_{i\in G_{j}}\left[\theta_{v}^{\delta_{i}}(1-\theta_{v})^{1-\delta_{i}}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)+\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=0,\delta_{i}=0)\right],

(17)

\displaystyle\theta_{g}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Beta}(d_{1},d_{2}),\quad\theta_{v}\sim\text{Beta}(e_{1},e_{2}),

\displaystyle\pi(\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle\propto\sigma^{-2},\quad c_{g},c_{v}\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\{0,1\}^{m},\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
are the group/variable inclusion vectors,
\theta_{g},\theta_{v}\in(0,1)
are the group/variable mixture proportions (with
d_{1},d_{2},e_{1},e_{2}>0
prior hyperparameters) and
c_{g},c_{v}\in(0,1)
allow the signal group/variables to be penalized less. As with BGSLOPE, choosing
d_{1}\ll d_{2}
and
e_{1}\ll e_{2}
leads to sparser models. The weight matrix is denoted as
\hat{\mathbf{A}}=\text{diag}(\hat{a}_{1},\ldots,\hat{a}_{p})
with elements, where
i\in G_{j}
,

\hat{a}_{i}=c_{g}\gamma_{j}(c_{v}\delta_{i}+1-\delta_{i})+(1-\gamma_{j})=\begin{cases}c_{g}c_{v}&\text{if }\gamma_{j}=1\text{ and }\delta_{i}=1,\\
c_{g}&\text{if }\gamma_{j}=1\text{ and }\delta_{i}=0,\\
1&\text{if }\gamma_{j}=0.\end{cases}

(18)

The weight matrix penalizes the active groups less via
c_{g}
and the active variables less by
c_{g}c_{v}
. Additionally, the hierarchical construction of
\boldsymbol{\delta}
being dependent on
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
enforces all variables in a non-active group to also be non-active. To see this, note that if
\gamma_{j}=1
, the prior on
\boldsymbol{\delta}
is an independent Bernoulli product (as for ABSLOPE and BGSLOPE), and if
\gamma_{j}=0
, then the prior is a point-mass at zero.

It is possible to scale the signal strength ratio using
\alpha
to have
c_{g}^{(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)}c_{v}^{\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)}
in Equation
16
, which allows the hyperparameters to have a direct interpretive connection to ABSLOPE and BGSLOPE. However, as this adds complexity and interpretation between models is not a key priority, it is not pursued further.

BSGS provides uncertainty quantification and feature importance at two levels, group and variable, through the inclusion parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}
, respectively. Moreover, the dual mixing proportions
\theta_{g}
and
\theta_{v}
enable BSGS to adapt simultaneously to the underlying sparsity structures at both levels. As seen in Figure
2
, BSGS has a more complex dependency graph, with twice as many parameters. Proposition
3.2
demonstrates that the posterior mode from the BSGS prior is equivalent to the estimates obtained via the SGS Laplace prior under the input
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{-1}
. This also makes it equivalent to the frequentist model (the proof is given in Appendix
B.2.1
).

Proposition 3.2
.

If the coefficients
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{p})
follow the SGS Laplace prior (Equation
14
), then
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\hat{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\mathbf{z}
follows the BSGS prior (Equation
16
).

While developed for SLOPE, our methodology extends naturally to broader model classes. BGSLOPE and BSGS apply to any Ordered Weighted
\ell_{1}
(OWL) model, such as OSCAR
[
17
]
, by replacing the corresponding penalties. The BSGS framework further generalizes to any sparse-group model, including the sparse-group lasso
[
93
]
and sparse-group SCAD
[
19
]
, with efficiency gains available via sparse-group screening rules
[
32
,
64
,
72
]
.

3.4
Stochastic Approximation EM (SAEM) algorithm

Both BGSLOPE and BSGS are fitted using SAEM. A description of it for the BGSLOPE case is presented in this section. The aim is to estimate
\Theta=(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma)
in the presence of latent variables
\Lambda=(\boldsymbol{\gamma},c,\theta)
. The employment of expectation–maximization (EM) algorithms for spike-and-slab models is a standard technique. For example, the SSL model is fitted via the EMVS algorithm
[
82
]
.

There are two steps to an EM algorithm (for step
t
):

1.

Expectation step (E step)
: Calculate the expected value of the log-likelihood

Q(\Theta\mid\Theta_{[t]})=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y},\Theta_{[t]})}\left[\log\pi(\mathbf{y},\Lambda\mid\Theta_{[t]})\right],

(19)

where
\Theta_{[t]}
denotes the value of the parameters at step
t
.

2.

Maximization step (M step)
: Find the parameters that maximize
Q
:
\Theta_{[t+1]}=\argmax_{\Theta}Q(\Theta\mid\Theta_{[t]}).

However, for SLOPE models,
Q
is not tractable due to the sorting procedures. Instead, the
Stochastic Approximation EM
(SAEM) algorithm
[
59
]
is used, in which the E step is replaced by a simulation step, followed by a stochastic approximation step. These are given by

•

Simulation step
: Sample the latent variables
\Lambda
from
\pi(\Lambda\mid\mathbf{y},\Theta_{[t-1]})
using a Gibbs sampler.

•

Stochastic approximation
: Calculate
Q
as

Q(\Theta\mid\Theta_{[t]})=Q(\Theta\mid\Theta_{[t-1]})+\eta_{t}\left(\log\pi(\mathbf{y},\Lambda\mid\Theta_{[t]})-Q(\Theta\mid\Theta_{[t-1]})\right),

(20)

where
\eta_{t}
is the step size, which is chosen as a decreasing sequence
\eta_{t}=1/t
that guarantees almost sure convergence
[
28
]
. The M step remains the same.

SAEM estimates
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and simulates
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
to allow for variable selection. It has two loosely defined stages: first, a quick search to find the solution neighborhood, followed by a steady fine-tuning stage
[
59
]
(these steps can be seen in the illustrative example in Section
3.6
). SAEM is generally not sensitive to initialization, as demonstrated in the study presented in Section
3.7
, which is in agreement with findings in the literature
[
59
]
. The stochastic approximation is applied only after the
20
th iteration:
\eta_{t}=1
if
t\leq 20
.

To determine the final set of active variables/groups, median thresholding is applied to the inclusion parameters over the last
T=20
iterations of SAEM: a variable
i\in[p]
is active if
\mathbb{P}(\delta_{i}=1\mid\mathbf{y})\approx\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}(\delta_{[t]})_{i}>0.5
and a group
j\in[m]
is active if
\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\mathbf{y})\approx\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}(\gamma_{[t]})_{j}>0.5
, where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the index set of the last
T
iterations. In BSGS, group thresholding is not necessary due to the hierarchical structure between
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}
.

SAEM is described fully for BGSLOPE and BSGS in Algorithms
1
and
2
, respectively. The SAEM updates for BGSLOPE and BSGS are derived next. This is followed by the development of the BSGS-
\alpha
model, which additionally updates the
\alpha
parameter, and the SLOBE models, which approximate the conditional distribution by its conditional expectation to accelerate the algorithm.

3.4.1
Updates for BGSLOPE

For BGSLOPE, the penalized log-likelihood, which is used to derive the maximization steps, is given by

\displaystyle\log\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\Lambda\mid\Theta_{[t]})

\displaystyle=\log\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},c;\boldsymbol{\beta},\theta,\sigma^{2}\right)+\log\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},c,\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}\right)

(21)

\displaystyle=\log\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}};\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\right)+\log\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\mid\theta)+\log\pi(\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\log\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},c,\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}\right)

\displaystyle=-(n+2)\log\sigma-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^{2}_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\log\theta

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=0)\log(1-\theta)+\log c\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tilde{a}_{j}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{A}}}\boldsymbol{\beta},j)}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2},

where the prior term on
c
is omitted since it is a standard uniform.

Simulation step.

The following Gibbs sampler regime is used for the simulation step, for
j\in[m]
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}

\displaystyle\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{L_{1}}{L_{1}+L_{2}}\right),\;\text{where},

(22)

\displaystyle\quad\;L_{1}=\theta c^{p_{j}}\exp\left\{\frac{-c}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\},

\displaystyle\quad\;L_{2}=(1-\theta)\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\},

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\sim\text{Beta}\left(d_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1),d_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=0)\right),

(23)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\sim\text{Gamma}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1),\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\right),

(24)

\displaystyle\quad\;\text{truncated at}\;[0,1].

The derivations of these are given in Appendix
B.3.1
.

Stochastic approximation and maximization steps.

After simulating the latent variables, we use these to complete the maximization step and compute
\Theta_{[t+1]}
. For a general step size
\eta_{t}
, we update the model parameters as

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t+1]}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}+\eta_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}),\quad\sigma_{[t+1]}=\sigma_{[t]}+\eta_{t}(\sigma^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}-\sigma_{[t]}),

(25)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}
and
\sigma^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}
are the
Maximum Likelihood Estimation
(MLE) estimators of the log-likelihood (Equation
21
), using samples of the latent variables at step
t
. Considering only the terms involving the parameters of interest in the log-likelihood, they are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{[t-1]}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\tilde{a}_{j})_{[t]}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}},j)}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\right\},

(26)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}=\frac{K_{2}+\sqrt{K_{2}^{2}+4K_{1}(n+2)}}{2(n+2)},

(27)

\displaystyle\text{where}\;K_{1}=\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}\|_{2}^{2},\;K_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\tilde{a}_{j})_{[t]}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},j)}.

Remark 3.3
.

To calculate the update for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{\text{MLE}}
, consider the transformation
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{\text{MLE}}
, so that Equation
26
becomes

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{[t]}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{[t-1]}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},j)}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\Bigg\}.

Following Proposition
3.1
, the update reduces to a standard gSLOPE problem with input matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}^{-1}
and regularization parameter
\lambda=\sigma_{[t-1]}/n
, yielding
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{[t]}
, with MLE
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{[t]}
. Since
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}
is diagonal, the inverse is inexpensive, and sorting the modified penalty sequence is handled via
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
. The optimization is solved using Adaptive Three Operator Splitting (ATOS)
[
74
]
. See Appendix
B.3.2
for the derivation of the
\sigma
update.

3.4.2
Updates for BSGS

For BSGS, we define the latent variables as
\Lambda^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta},c_{g},c_{v},\theta_{g},\theta_{v})
and the model parameters are as for BGSLOPE:
\Theta=(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma
). Therefore, the penalized log-likelihood, which is used to derive the maximization steps, is given by

\displaystyle\log\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\Lambda^{\prime}\mid\Theta_{[t]})=\log\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}};\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\right)+\log\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\mid\theta_{g})+\log\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\theta_{v})+\log\pi(\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\log\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}},c_{g},c_{v},\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle=-(n+2)\log\sigma-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^{2}_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\log\theta_{g}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=0)\log(1-\theta_{g})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i\in G_{j}}\log\left[\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\left(\theta_{v}^{\delta_{i}}(1-\theta_{v})^{1-\delta_{i}}\right)+\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=0,\delta_{i}=0)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\log c_{g}\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)+\log c_{v}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|\hat{a}_{i}v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\beta},i)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\sigma}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\beta},j)}.

Simulation step.

A Gibbs sampler is used for the simulation step (the derivations of these updates are provided in Appendix
B.3.1
). For the inclusion parameters, we sample from Bernoulli distributions

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}\sim\text{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{L_{1}^{\prime}+L_{2}^{\prime}}\right),\;j\in[m],\;\text{where},

(29)

\displaystyle\quad\;L_{1}^{\prime}=\theta_{g}c_{g}^{p_{j}}c_{v}^{\sum_{i\in G_{j}}\mathbbm{1}\{\delta_{i}=1\}}\exp\left\{-\frac{c_{g}}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\kappa}}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}(1-\alpha)w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}

(30)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\times\prod_{i\in G_{j}}\theta_{v}^{\delta_{i}}(1-\theta_{v})^{1-\delta_{i}}\exp\left\{-\frac{c_{g}}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|c_{v}^{\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}\right\},

\displaystyle\quad\;L_{2}^{\prime}=(1-\theta_{g})\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}(1-\alpha)w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}

(31)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\;\times\prod_{i\in G_{j}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}\right\},

\displaystyle\delta_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{\tilde{L}_{1}}{\tilde{L}_{1}+\tilde{L}_{2}}\right),\;i\in[p],\;\text{where},

(32)

\displaystyle\quad\;\tilde{L}_{1}=\theta_{v}c_{v}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|c_{g}c_{v}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}\right\},\;\tilde{L}_{2}=(1-\theta_{v})\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|c_{g}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}\right\}.

The mixing parameters are sampled via Beta distributions, given by

\displaystyle\theta_{g}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Beta}\left(d_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1),d_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=0)\right),

(33)

\displaystyle\theta_{v}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Beta}\Bigg(me_{1}-m+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\sum_{i\in G_{j}}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)+1,

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad me_{2}-m+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\sum_{i\in G_{j}}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=0)+1\Bigg).

(34)

Finally, the signal strength ratios are sampled as

\displaystyle c_{g}\sim\text{Gamma}\Bigg(1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1),

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{p}c_{v}|\beta_{i}|\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\kappa}}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}(1-\alpha)w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\Bigg]\Bigg),

\displaystyle\quad\;\text{truncated at}\;[0,1],

\displaystyle c_{v}\;\text{sampled via Metropolis-Hastings (MH) with proposal}\;\text{Gamma}(2,2),

(36)

\displaystyle\quad\;\text{where}\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\kappa}}^{(j)}=\text{diag}(c_{v}^{\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)}),\forall i\in G_{j}.

Stochastic approximation and maximization steps.

After the simulation step, the model parameters are given by Equation
25
with the MLE estimators

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}^{\text{MLE}}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{[t-1]}}{n}\Bigg(\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{p}|b_{i}|(\hat{a}_{i})_{[t]}v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}},i)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}},j)}\Bigg)\Bigg\},

(37)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}=\frac{K_{2}^{\prime}+\sqrt{(K_{2}^{\prime})^{2}+4K_{1}^{\prime}(n+2)}}{2(n+2)},\;\text{where},

(38)

\displaystyle K_{1}^{\prime}=\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle K_{2}^{\prime}=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{p}|(\beta_{i})_{[t]}|(\hat{a}_{i})_{[t]}v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},i)}+(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},j)}.

The
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
update is the SGS optimization task. As with BGSLOPE, this is calculated via a transformation with input
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{-1}_{[t]}
and regularization parameter

\lambda=\sigma_{[t-1]}/n
; see Remark
3.3
. It is performed using ATOS. See Appendix
B.4.2
for the derivation of the
\sigma
update.

3.5
BSGS-
\alpha

It is also possible to learn the
\alpha
model parameter for BSGS, which defines the balance between the two types of penalization, by placing a uniform prior on it. The posterior for
\alpha
is not available in closed-form, so it is sampled via MH using a
\text{Beta}(10,0.5)
proposal, which skews
\alpha
towards
1
(see Appendix
B.4.3
). We denote this model by BSGS-
\alpha
. To the best of our knowledge, there are no other instances in the literature of
\alpha
being updated in a Bayesian model.

3.5.1
SLOBE models

The SAEM algorithm for BGSLOPE and BSGS can be accelerated by approximating samples from the conditional distributions of the latent parameters using their conditional expectations. That is,
\Lambda_{[t]}\leftarrow\mathbb{E}[\Lambda_{[t]}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\Theta_{[t-1]}].
Besides speeding up computation, this reduces latent variable variability, which helps diminish algorithmic noise in high dimensions.

ABSLOPE was modified in this way to form
SLOBE
in
51
. Here, we apply the SLOBE acceleration to BGSLOPE and BSGS, to form
GSLOBE
and
SGSLOBE
, respectively. The SAEM procedures for GSLOBE and SGSLOBE are as in Algorithms
1
and
2
, with the simulation step replaced by the conditional expectation approximations.

GSLOBE.

Taking the conditional expectations of the simulation updates for BGSLOPE (Equations
22
–
24
):

1.

Approximate each
\gamma_{j},j\in[m]
, by the active probability of a Bernoulli distribution, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma_{-j}}}
is the vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}
with the
j
th entry removed,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta c^{p_{j}}\exp\left\{\frac{-c}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}}{(1-\theta)\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}+\theta c^{p_{j}}\exp\left\{\frac{-c}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}}.

2.

Approximate
\theta
by the mean of a Beta distribution

\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,c,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}]=\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}]=\frac{d_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)}{d_{1}+d_{2}+m}.

3.

Approximate
c
by the mean of a truncated Gamma distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}]=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}z^{x}\exp\{-x^{\prime}z\}\,dz}{\int_{0}^{1}z^{x-1}\exp\{-x^{\prime}z\}\,dz},\quad\text{where,}

\displaystyle x=1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1),\;x^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1).

SGSLOBE.

Taking the conditional expectations of the simulation updates for BSGS (Equations
29
–
35
; the update for
c_{v}
remains the same via MH):

1.

Approximate the inclusion probabilities by the active probability of a Bernoulli distribution (where
L_{1}^{\prime}
and
L_{2}^{\prime}
are as described in Equations
30
and
31
), for each
j\in[m]
and
i\in[p]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{g},c_{v},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta_{g},\theta_{v},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}]=\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{L_{1}^{\prime}+L_{2}^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta_{i}=1\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}_{-i},c_{g},c_{v},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta_{g},\theta_{v},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\theta_{v}c_{v}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|c_{g}c_{v}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}\right\}}{\theta_{v}c_{v}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|c_{g}c_{v}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}\right\}+(1-\theta_{v})\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|c_{g}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}\right\}}\right).

2.

Approximate
\theta_{g}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\theta_{g}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\theta_{v},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,c_{v},c_{g},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}]=\mathbb{E}[\theta_{g}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}]=\frac{d_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)}{d_{1}+d_{2}+m},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\theta_{v}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\theta_{g},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,c_{v},c_{g},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\theta_{v}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}]=\frac{me_{1}-m+x+1}{me_{1}+x+2+me_{2}-2m+x^{\prime}},\;\text{where},

\displaystyle x=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\sum_{i\in G_{j}}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1),\;x^{\prime}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\sum_{i\in G_{j}}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=0).

Approximate

\displaystyle x=1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1),

Remark 3.4

(Limitations of Scheme 1)
.

Under Scheme 1 for BGSLOPE,
\mathbb{E}[\theta]=0.5
independently of
n

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}]=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{0.01n+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)}{0.02n+m}=0.5,

so the posterior mean reverts to the prior, and the data contributes no
information. The same holds for
\theta_{g}
and
\theta_{v}
in BSGS. In practice, this is not critical since BGSLOPE targets high-dimensional genetic settings where large

is uncommon, and on the Trust-experts dataset (
n=9759

6
), BGSLOPE outperformed competing methods, suggesting limited
practical impact.

4
Two-step Orthogonal

We propose a new two-step procedure,
Two-step Orthogonal
(TSO), that transforms a general setting into an orthogonal one, thereby enabling FDR control. Appendix
C.4
provides a background of two-step models. The Gram-Schmidt procedure can orthogonalize a matrix but requires low-dimensional data. Assuming that
|S_{v}|\leq n
, TSO proceeds as follows:

1.

Compute
\hat{S}_{v}
, such that
|\hat{S}_{v}|\leq n
, using the lasso with CV (picking the 1se model).

2.

Orthogonalize
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\hat{S}}
using Gram-Schmidt to generate the orthogonalized design matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{\hat{S}}
.

3.

Fit a SLOPE model using
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}_{\hat{S}}
and
\lambda=1/n
, where the
1/n
factor is from the loss function.

For gSLOPE and SGS, the procedure is identical, replacing step 3 with optimizing using the gSLOPE and SGS models instead.

16
derived penalty sequences for SLOPE under low-dimensional Gaussian designs, termed the
Gaussian sequence
(shown in Figure
5
). The sequence is given by

v_{i}^{\text{GA}}=v_{i}^{\text{BH}}\sqrt{1+f(i-1)\sum_{j<i}(v^{\text{GA}}_{j})^{2}},\;\text{where}\;f(i)=1/(n-i-1).

The sequence penalizes small coefficients more heavily to reduce false discoveries at some cost to power, but collapses to the lasso when
p>n

[
57
]
, limiting its use. Since step 1 of TSO yields a low-dimensional setting, the Gaussian sequence is applied, and step 2 is omitted. All TSO-SLOPE applications in this manuscript use the Gaussian sequence unless otherwise stated (TSO-BH denotes TSO with the BH sequence). Penalty sequences for gSLOPE and SGS remain unchanged.

Figure 5
:
BH and Gaussian SLOPE sequences for different values of
q
for
p=100,n=500
.

Future work should more carefully consider the step 1 model, as it determines TSO’s power. The lasso was chosen for its simplicity and well-understood variable selection properties, and it guarantees
|\hat{S}_{v}|\leq n

[
103
]
. Ideally, this step would minimize false negatives (type II error), though
98
notes this is generally unattainable for the lasso, even if asymptotically it recovers most true signals with few false positives. Replacements to the lasso would ideally be designed for type II error control. This would lead to a model that combines type I and type II error control procedures. For further results on lasso consistency, see
[
107
,
108
,
121
]
. Our empirical results show that the lasso is a sensible choice, enabling TSO to control FDR with fast runtime (Section
5
).

5.2.4
Summary of results

Averaged across the six simulation scenarios considered in this section, our group-based Bayesian methods consistently controlled the FDR and achieved the highest power among all methods that maintained FDR control, yielding the strongest
\text{F}_{1}
scores (Table
1
). Combined with their strong predictive performance and competitive runtimes, this made them the best overall methods across the SLOPE models.

TSO was the only other method to control FDR across all model types, although this came with a modest reduction in power, likely due to type II errors introduced during the lasso screening step (Section
4
). TSO also exhibited relatively poor predictive performance. In contrast, using the Gaussian sequence for SLOPE improved predictive accuracy while preserving FDR control and increasing power, highlighting the potential value of developing analogous sequences for gSLOPE and SGS.

Although the Bayesian methods generally produced reliable noise estimates, they were not always the most accurate. For SLOPE, CV yielded the most accurate noise estimates on average, while for SGS, AS-SGS estimated the noise level with exceptional precision. However, this accuracy did not translate into strong overall performance, as AS-SGS exhibited extremely high FDR levels.

Among the SGS approaches, only BSGS-
\alpha
and TSO maintained bi-level FDR control, though both experienced a slight loss of power relative to BSGS. Notably, BSGS-
\alpha
controlled the FDR in every experiment. Nevertheless, BSGS achieved more accurate estimation of both
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
and
\sigma
, leading to superior predictive performance (Table
1
).

With respect to the group-level metrics, BSGS did not control the FDR on average but achieved the highest power (Table
9
). It provided stronger control of variable-level FDR than group-level FDR, whereas CV exhibited the opposite pattern (Figure
31
). This difference arises from BSGS’s hierarchical spike-and-slab structure, which selects groups before variables, whereas CV performs selection at both levels simultaneously. Since variable-level control yields more informative associations, it is generally the more desirable property.

Additional insights

Impact of
\alpha
on SGS models.

Figure
13
shows that BSGS is not sensitive to the choice of
\alpha
, controlling FDR for all
\alpha\in[0.05,0.95]
, while maintaining high power; a property shared only by TSO. BSGS demonstrates the best selection performance across all values of
\alpha
and exhibits low bias. BSGS’s adaptivity allows it to perform well across
\alpha
, unlike CV. TSO’s consistent performance stems from its initial lasso step, which filters irrelevant variables and mitigates the limitations of group-only fitting at small
\alpha
.

Scaled regression closely matches the oracle due to highly accurate noise estimation (
\text{MAE}(\sigma)=0.03
). Overall, for most models, FDR decreases as
\alpha\to 0.95
, reflecting the shift away from group-only behavior, where variable-level FDR control is harder to achieve.

6
Real data study

In this section, we apply the model selection approaches that showed the most promise in the synthetic study to real data: the Bayesian methods, CV, and TSO.

6.1
Setup

Datasets.

The models are applied to seven real-world datasets: Cancer, Colitis, BRCA1, Carbotax, Rhee, Scheetz, and Trust-experts. The datasets have varied characteristics to test the models under different scenarios; five of the datasets are high-dimensional, four different grouping structure approaches are taken, and two of the datasets are semi-synthetic. All datasets have continuous responses, so linear models are fitted. Further information on the datasets is provided in Appendix
D.5.1
.

The Cancer and Colitis datasets originally have binary responses. To enable evaluation of the FDR on real data and allow us to use this dataset with a linear model, a synthetic response was generated for each using the linear model:
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{semi-syn}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{real}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{semi-syn}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathcal{N}}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
, with group sparsity set to
0.2
, variable sparsity set to
0.15
, and signal strength set to
5
.

Each dataset was divided into five 80/20 train/test partitions, and the final results were averaged over these splits. To assess predictive performance, we use the Normalized MSE (NMSE) of the prediction, defined by

\text{NMSE}=\text{MSE}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{test}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{test}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{\text{train}})/\text{Var}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{test}}).

This yields a dimensionless metric ranging from 0 to 1, where
\text{NMSE}=1
corresponds to predicting the mean of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
, while values below
1
indicate improved predictive performance.

Model hyperparameters.

The models were applied in the same way as for the synthetic study, with the following minor changes. For the SGS models, two modifications were made. First,
\alpha=0.99
is used, following
31
, who observed that this value yields stronger predictive performance than the default
\alpha=0.95
on real data. Second, anticipating weaker signals in the real data, the priors on the mixture proportions were adjusted to
\theta_{g},\theta_{v}\sim\text{Beta}(5,1)
, allowing BSGS to select denser models.

7
Discussion

SLOPE models are known to control the FDR under orthogonal designs. In this work, we examined model selection for SLOPE under more general settings, aiming to restore FDR control in practice through empirical evaluation of new and existing approaches.

Our main methodological contribution is the development of group-based Bayesian SLOPE models, BGSLOPE and BSGS, which use spike-and-slab priors equivalent to gSLOPE and SGS, respectively. The Bayesian framework allows the models to learn the noise alongside the regression parameters, while providing uncertainty quantification, feature importance, and adaptivity to the underlying sparsity structure. Notably, BSGS introduces the first continuous spike-and-slab framework for sparse-group models, extending these benefits to both variable and group levels. We also propose the Two-step Orthogonal (TSO) approach, which transforms a general setting into an orthogonal one, allowing SLOPE’s FDR properties to be used.

This manuscript presents a comprehensive comparison of model selection approaches for SLOPE models using synthetic and real data. In synthetic experiments, the Bayesian methods consistently outperform alternatives, achieving strong FDR control, predictive accuracy, and parameter recovery, while retaining the highest power among methods that control FDR. TSO performs well as a fast alternative, particularly for SLOPE, where the Gaussian sequence leads to strong predictive performance. In real data, the Bayesian models again deliver the best predictive performance, surpassing competing methods on most datasets.

Our Bayesian methodology eliminates the need to tune
\lambda
by learning it via the noise, but introduces additional hyperparameters compared to the frequentist counterparts. For BSGS, we need to set
(\alpha,q_{v},q_{g},d_{1},d_{2},e_{1},e_{2})
and a
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
initialization, though
\alpha
can be updated using BSGS-
\alpha
. Section
3.7
shows that the models are generally robust to prior hyperparameters, while still allowing the incorporation of domain knowledge to improve performance. In practice, tuning hyperparameters to encourage sparsity or density can meaningfully improve FDR control and predictive accuracy, illustrating the flexibility of the Bayesian framework in adapting to different noise levels and signal structures.

We conclude that under general settings, SLOPE models are best applied within the Bayesian framework developed in this manuscript. This recommendation is supported by their strong empirical performance on synthetic and real data: the Bayesian methods effectively control FDR, maintain high power, produce less biased estimates, and deliver accurate predictions.

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{m-1}=0}^{p_{m-1}+k_{m-2}-1}\frac{(p_{m-1}+k_{m-2}-1)!}{k_{m-1}!}\left(\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{p_{m}}\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}w_{l}\right)^{k_{m-1}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\times\frac{(p_{m}+k_{m-1}-1)!}{\left(\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{p_{m}}\sum_{l=1}^{m}w_{l}\right)^{p_{m}+k_{m-1}}},

where the complete gamma function satisfies
\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{a-1}e^{-br}dr=\Gamma(a)/b^{a}
, justifying the second to last step, and
\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!
, if
n
is a positive integer, which is the case for
p_{m}+k_{m-1}
, justifying the last step.

Putting this all together, including the directional integrand,
I^{S}
, gives

\displaystyle C^{-1}=m!

\displaystyle\prod_{g=1}^{m}\frac{2\pi^{p_{g}/2}}{\Gamma(p_{g}/2)}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{p_{1}-1}\dotsb\sum_{k_{m-2}=0}^{p_{m-2}+k_{m-3}-1}\sum_{k_{m-1}=0}^{p_{m-1}+k_{m-2}-1}\Biggl[\frac{(p_{1}-1)!(\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{p_{2}}w_{1})^{k_{1}}}{k_{1}!(\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{p_{1}}w_{1})^{p_{1}}}\times\dotsb

\displaystyle\times\frac{(p_{m-1}+k_{m-2}-1)!(\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{p_{m}}\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}w_{l})^{k_{m-1}}}{k_{m-1}!(\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{p_{m-1}}\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}w_{l})^{p_{m-1}+k_{m-2}}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{(p_{m}+k_{m-1}-1)!}{(\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{p_{m}}\sum_{l=1}^{m}w_{l})^{p_{m}+k_{m-1}}}\Biggr].

This can be simplified to

\displaystyle C^{-1}=m!\prod_{g=1}^{m}

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi^{p_{g}/2}}{\Gamma(p_{g}/2)}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{K}}\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{(p_{j}+k_{j-1}-1)!(\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{p_{j}}\sum_{l=1}^{j}w_{l})^{k_{j}}(p_{m}+k_{m-1}-1)!}{k_{j}!(\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{p_{j}}\sum_{l=1}^{j}w_{l})^{p_{j}+k_{j-1}}(\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{p_{m}}\sum_{l=1}^{m}w_{l})^{p_{m}+k_{m-1}}},

where
\mathbb{K}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}:k_{1}\in[0,p_{1}-1],\;k_{2}\in[0,p_{2}+k_{1}-1],\;\ldots,\;k_{m-1}\in[0,p_{m-1}+k_{m-2}-1]\},
with
k_{0}=0
.
∎

B.2
Bayesian SGS (BSGS)

B.2.1
Theory

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

We assume a random variable
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{p})
has an SGS Laplace prior, given by

\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\mid\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{p}v_{r_{v}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},i)}|z_{i}|-\frac{1}{\sigma}(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{r_{g}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},j)}\sqrt{p_{j}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\right\}.

Now, define
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
such that
z_{i}=\beta_{i}\hat{a}_{i}
for
i\in G_{j}
. Then, by the transformation of variables, the prior distribution for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
is given by

\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid c_{v},c_{g},\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\right)\propto\left|\text{det}(d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}/d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})\right|\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\mid c_{v},c_{g},\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\right).

We have that
(d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}/d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})_{i}=\hat{a}_{i}
, so that

\displaystyle\text{det}(d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}/d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\hat{a}_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}c_{g}^{p_{j}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\gamma_{j}=1\right\}}\prod_{i\in G_{j}}c_{v}^{\mathbbm{1}\left\{\delta_{i}=1\right\}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}c_{g}^{p_{j}\mathbbm{1}\{\gamma_{j}=1\}}c_{v}^{\sum_{i\in G_{j}}\mathbbm{1}\{\delta_{i}=1\}}=c_{g}^{\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)}c_{v}^{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)}.

We can rewrite the prior on
z
in terms of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
:

\displaystyle\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\mid c_{v},c_{g},\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{p}v_{r_{v}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},i)}|z_{i}|-\frac{1}{\sigma}(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{r_{g}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},j)}\sqrt{p_{j}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},i)}|z_{i}|\right\}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}(1-\alpha)w_{r_{g}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},j)}\sqrt{p_{j}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}\hat{a}_{i}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}|\beta_{i}|\right\}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}(1-\alpha)w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\sqrt{p_{j}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\right\}.

Therefore, the prior for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
is given by

\displaystyle\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid c_{v},c_{g},\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\right)

\displaystyle\propto c_{g}^{\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)}c_{v}^{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|\hat{a}_{i}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}(1-\alpha)w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\},

corresponding to the BSGS prior.
∎

B.3
SAEM for BGSLOPE

B.3.1
Simulation step derivations

Here, the conditional distributions required for the Gibbs sampler are derived for each latent variable.

1. Inclusion vector (
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
).

According to the dependency graph (Figure
2
), we simulate
\gamma_{j}
, for
j\in[m]
, via

\gamma_{j}\sim\pi(\gamma_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta)=\pi(\gamma_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta).

As
\gamma_{j}
is binary, the posterior is a Bernoulli distribution with active probability

\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)}{\sum_{\gamma_{j}\in\{0,1\}}\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}\mid\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)}.

This describes the posterior probability of a binary signal indicator for the
j
th group, using the prior
\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\theta)=\theta
and the conditional likelihood of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
given
\gamma_{j}=1
and
\gamma_{j}=0
.

We have that
\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\theta)=\theta
and
\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=0\mid\theta)=1-\theta
via the Bernoulli distribution on
\gamma_{j}
, and,

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)=c^{p_{j}}\exp\left\{\frac{-c}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times c^{\sum_{k\in[m]\char 92\relax\{j\}}p_{k}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{k}=1)}\prod_{k\in[m]\char 92\relax\{j\}}\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\tilde{a}_{k}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(k)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{k}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},k)}\right\},

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)=\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{0}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times c^{\sum_{k\in[m]\char 92\relax\{j\}}p_{k}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{k}=1)}\prod_{k\in[m]\char 92\relax\{j\}}\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\tilde{a}_{k}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(k)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{k}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{0}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},k)}\right\},

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{1}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{0}
are the same as
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}
except for the
j
th group, for which
\tilde{a}_{j}^{1}=c
and
\tilde{a}_{j}^{0}=1
. This leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)}{\sum_{\gamma_{j}\in\{0,1\}}\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}\mid\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)+\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=0\mid\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)}{\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left[1+\frac{\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=0\mid\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)}{\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left[1+\frac{(1-\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)}{\theta\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)}\right]^{-1},

where the
c^{\sum_{k\in[m]\char 92\relax\{j\}}p_{k}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{k}=1)}
term present in both has been canceled, and

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{0}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}\prod_{k\in[m]\char 92\relax\{j\}}\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\tilde{a}_{(k)}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(k)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{k}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{0}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},k)}\right\},

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\sigma)

\displaystyle=c^{p_{j}}\exp\left\{\frac{-c}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}\prod_{k\in[m]\char 92\relax\{j\}}\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\tilde{a}_{(k)}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(k)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{k}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},k)}\right\}.

Sampling here requires updating an ordered list at each group
j\in[m]
, which is expensive, so
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{0}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{1}
are approximated by using the previous estimate of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}
. This partially retains information on
\gamma_{j}
and stabilizes updates; a similar approach is taken for ABSLOPE. The approximation allows the
\prod_{k\in[m]\char 92\relax\{j\}}
terms to cancel, so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}})=\left[1+\frac{(1-\theta)\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}}{\theta c^{p_{j}}\exp\left\{\frac{-c}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta c^{p_{j}}\exp\left\{\frac{-c}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}}{(1-\theta)\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}+\theta c^{p_{j}}\exp\left\{\frac{-c}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}}.

This leads to posterior

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{L_{1}}{L_{1}+L_{2}}\right),\;\text{where},

\displaystyle L_{1}=\theta c^{p_{j}}\exp\left\{\frac{-c}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\},

\displaystyle L_{2}=(1-\theta)\exp\left\{\frac{-1}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}.

2. Mixture proportion (
\theta
).

We have that

\theta\sim\pi(\theta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},c,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}})=\pi(\theta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}})\propto\pi(\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\mid\theta),

where
\pi(\theta)
is a
\text{Beta}(d_{1},d_{2})
distribution and
\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\mid\theta)
is a multivariate Bernoulli distribution. Therefore, by conjugacy, the posterior is given by a Beta distribution:

\theta\sim\text{Beta}\left(d_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1),d_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=0)\right).

3. Signal strength ratio (
c
).

We sample
c
from

c\sim\pi(c\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}})=\pi(c\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}})\propto\pi(c)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid c,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\sigma,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}).

As
c\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
, this reduces to the BGSLOPE prior on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
for an active group (the prior for non-active groups does not contain
c
)

\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid c,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\sigma,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}})=c^{\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}\left(\gamma_{j}=1\right)}\exp\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}-\frac{c}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\right\}.

This is in the form of a Gamma distribution, truncated to
[0,1]
to satisfy the constraint on
c
,

c\sim\text{Gamma}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1),\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)\right).

B.3.2
Stochastic and maximization step derivations

Update for
\sigma
.

Considering only the terms of the log-likelihood (Equation
21
) containing
\sigma

\displaystyle\sigma^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}}\bigg\{-(n+2)\log\sigma-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;\;\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\tilde{a}_{j})_{[t]}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},j)}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}_{[t]}\|_{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}}\left\{-(n+2)\log\sigma-\frac{K_{1}}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma}K_{2}\right\},

where
K_{1}=\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}\|_{2}^{2}
and
K_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\tilde{a}_{j})_{[t]}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},j)}
. Differentiating this with respect to
\sigma
gives

K_{1}+K_{2}\sigma=(n+2)\sigma^{2}\implies\sigma=\frac{K_{2}\pm\sqrt{K_{2}^{2}+4K_{1}(n+2)}}{2(n+2)}.

The negative root is negative if

\displaystyle K_{2}-\sqrt{K_{2}^{2}+4K_{1}(n+2)}<0

\displaystyle\implies K_{2}<\sqrt{K_{2}^{2}+4K_{1}(n+2)}

\displaystyle\implies 4K_{1}(n+2)>0,

which is true as
K_{1}>0
(unless we have perfect
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
recovery, which leads to a model with no variance). As
\sigma>0
, the positive root is used:

\sigma^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}=\frac{K_{2}+\sqrt{K_{2}^{2}+4K_{1}(n+2)}}{2(n+2)}.

Omitting the gSLOPE penalty, the estimator simplifies to
\sigma_{[t]}=\sqrt{K_{1}/(n+2)}
, which matches the classical MLE formula of
\sigma
when
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
is also estimated via MLE
[
51
]
.

Simulation step derivations

1. Group inclusion vector (
\boldsymbol{\gamma}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}

\gamma_{j}\sim\pi(\gamma_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}})=\pi(\gamma_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}).

As
\gamma_{j}
is binary, the posterior is given by a Bernoulli distribution with success probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},

\displaystyle\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\theta_{g})\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g})}{\sum_{\gamma_{j}\in\{0,1\}}\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}\mid\theta_{g})\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}\mid\gamma_{j},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g})}.

This can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}})

(43)

\displaystyle=\left[1+\frac{\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=0\mid\theta_{g})\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g})}{\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\theta_{g})\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g})}\right]^{-1},

where
\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=1\mid\theta_{g})=\theta_{g}
and
\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{j}=0\mid\theta_{g})=1-\theta_{g}
. The joint conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\theta_{v})\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\sigma,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}),

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=0,\theta_{v})\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}_{-j},c_{v},c_{g},\sigma,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}).

The first term is given by

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}\mid\gamma_{j}=1,\theta_{v})

from

\displaystyle c_{v}\sim\pi(c_{v}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma,\theta_{v},\theta_{g},c_{g},\hat{\mathbf{A}})

\displaystyle=\pi(c_{v}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma,c_{g},\hat{\mathbf{A}})

\displaystyle\propto\pi(c_{v})\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid c_{v},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma,c_{g},\hat{\mathbf{A}}).

The first term is uniform, so it vanishes. Therefore, considering only the terms containing
c_{v}
in the second component (which occur when
\gamma_{j}=1
and
\delta_{i}=1
), we have

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid c_{v},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta},\sigma,c_{g},A)

\displaystyle=c_{v}^{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}\{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}=1\}}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\exp\left\{\frac{-c_{g}}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|c_{v}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)v_{r_{v}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\beta},i)}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp\left\{-\frac{c_{g}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\beta},j)}\right\}

\displaystyle=c_{v}^{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)}\exp\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{-c_{g}}{\sigma}c_{v}|\beta_{i}|\mathbbm{1}(\delta_{i}=1)v_{r_{v}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\beta},i)}

(45)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{-c_{g}\mathbbm{1}(\gamma_{j}=1)}{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\beta},j)}\Bigg).

However, this is not of any clear distributional form. The
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
term in the
\ell_{2}
norm contains
c_{v}
, and we are unable to extract it from the norm. There are two potential solutions: 1. Approximate
\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j)}\|_{2}
as some function of
c_{v}
, via an approximation such as an affine one. 2. Use MH. We opt for the latter option, using a
\text{Gamma}(2,2)
proposal, truncated at
[0,1]
(which is a relatively uninformative prior that favors sparsity). This proposal is chosen as the form of Equation
45
would be of a Gamma distribution if we approximated the
\ell_{2}
term, and as
c_{g}
is also sampled from a Gamma distribution.

B.4.2
Stochastic and maximization step derivations

Update for
\sigma
.

The solution is as for BGSLOPE (Appendix
B.3
), but using the SGS penalty instead:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(n+2)}\Biggl[\sum_{i=1}^{p}|(\beta_{i})_{[t]}|(\hat{a}_{i})_{[t]}v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},j)}

\displaystyle\quad+\Biggl(\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{p}|(\beta_{i})_{[t]}|(\hat{a}_{i})_{[t]}v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},j)}\Biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+4(n+2)\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}\|_{2}^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}\Biggr],

or more simply

\displaystyle\sigma^{\text{MLE}}_{[t]}=\frac{K_{2}^{\prime}+\sqrt{(K_{2}^{\prime})^{2}+4K_{1}^{\prime}(n+2)}}{2(n+2)},\;\text{where},

\displaystyle K_{1}^{\prime}=\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle K_{2}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}|(\beta_{i})_{[t]}|(\hat{a}_{i})_{[t]}v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{[t]}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{[t]},j)}.

The classical MLE formula of
\sigma
is also recovered here if the penalization is removed.

B.4.3
BSGS-
\alpha

The updates follow those of BSGS, with an additional update; given
\alpha\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\pi(\alpha\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,\theta_{g},\theta_{v},c_{g},c_{v},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}})\propto\pi(\alpha)\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\gamma,\delta,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\sigma,c_{v},c_{g},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}})

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{j=1}^{m}c_{g}^{p_{j}\mathbbm{1}\{\gamma_{j}=1\}}c_{v}^{\sum_{i\in G_{j}}\mathbbm{1}\{\delta_{i}=1\}}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}|\beta_{i}|\hat{a}_{i}\alpha v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}(1-\alpha)w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right\}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\beta_{i}|\hat{a}_{i}v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right]\right\}.

This posterior is in the form of an exponential distribution with rate

\lambda=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\beta_{i}|\hat{a}_{i}v_{r_{v}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},i)}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}^{(j)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}w_{r_{g}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},j)}\right].

However, the rate can be non-positive, so MH is used with a
\text{Beta}(10,0.5)
proposal.

Theorem 1 in

\lambda=1

\displaystyle v_{i}^{\text{max}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\max_{j=1,\dots,m}\left\{\frac{\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{q_{v}i}{2p}\right)-\frac{1}{3}(1-\alpha)\lambda a_{j}w_{j}}{\alpha\lambda}\right\},\;i=1,\dots,p,

(46)

\displaystyle w_{i}^{\text{max}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\max_{j=1,\dots,m}\left\{\frac{F^{-1}_{\text{FN}}\left(1-\frac{q_{g}i}{m}\right)-\alpha\lambda\sum_{k\in G_{j}}v_{k}}{(1-\alpha)\lambda p_{j}}\right\},\;i=1,\dots,m.

Therefore, the scaled regression approach is modified to form

(AS-SGS)
[

\lambda=\hat{\sigma}
is estimated, making the sequences adaptive to the noise level (described in Algorithm
4
).

The AS-SGS model can be simplified by denoting

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{i}^{\text{max}}

\displaystyle=\max_{j=1,\dots,m}\left\{\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{q_{v}i}{2p}\right)-\frac{1}{3}(1-\alpha)\lambda a_{j}w_{j}\right\},\;i=1,\dots,p,

(48)

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{i}^{\text{max}}

\displaystyle=\max_{j=1,\dots,m}\left\{\frac{F^{-1}_{\text{FN}}\left(1-\frac{q_{g}i}{m}\right)-\alpha\lambda\sum_{k\in G_{j}}v_{k}}{p_{j}}\right\},\;i=1,\dots,m,

(49)

leading to the penalty (via cancellation of

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\tilde{v}_{i}|b|_{(i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\boldsymbol{b}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sqrt{p_{j}}\tilde{w}_{j}.

which reduces to the SLOPE penalty under

C.3
Knockoffs

Knockoffs
has been gaining popularity in the multiple testing literature, due to its flexibility and powerful properties. Knockoffs provides FDR control by generating knockoff copies of variables that act as negative controls. By comparing each variable to its knockoff, the procedure approximates the number of false positives and retains only variables that show a clear advantage over their knockoff counterparts.

The original Knockoffs procedure was limited to low-dimensional settings
[
7
]
.
8
extended it to high-dimensional data using the fixed-X paradigm, which was later generalized to the model-X framework
[
22
]
. This framework also allows for a Bayesian construction, which the authors briefly explored.

Construction.

Formally, a set of
knockoff variables

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}})_{1},\ldots,(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}})_{p}
for random variables
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{p}
satisfy
[
22
]
:

1.

Exchangeability
:
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}})_{\operatorname{swap}(M)}\overset{d}{=}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}})
for any
M\subset{1,\ldots,p}
,

2.

Independence
:
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
,

where
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}})_{\operatorname{swap}(M)}
denotes the matrix obtained by swapping
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
and
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}})_{i}
for each
i\in M
. Knockoff variables can be generated using various methods, including Markov models
[
91
]
, Metropolis-Hastings
[
10
]
, deep learning
[
85
]
, and non-parametric approaches
[
15
]
. In this manuscript, the second-order model-X construction is used, which matches the first two moments of
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}})
and
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}})_{\operatorname{swap}(M)}

[
22
]
.

Feature statistic.

The knockoff variables are combined with the originals to form the concatenated design matrix
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}}]
, which is used as input to a penalized regression model (typically with a lasso penalty) to produce
[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\;\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{\text{KO}}]
. A feature statistic is then computed for each variable. The coefficient-difference statistic is often used, defined for
i\in[p]
by

W_{i}=|\hat{\beta}_{i}|-|(\hat{\beta}_{\text{KO}})_{i}|.

(51)

See
113
for a comparison of different statistics.

The feature statistic must satisfy the
coin flip property
, which specifies that swapping a variable with its knockoff must reverse the sign of its statistic, and the
sufficiency property
, meaning it depends on the design only through the covariance and on the response through marginal correlations
[
8
]
. Because knockoffs serve as controls, a null variable is equally likely to be selected as its knockoff, making
W_{i}
symmetric around zero
[
77
]
.

Filter.

The Knockoffs filter applies a data-adaptive significance threshold to the feature statistic,

T=\min\left\{t>0:\frac{\#\left\{i\in[p]:W_{i}\leq-t\right\}}{\#\left\{i\in[p]:W_{i}\geq t\right\}}\leq q_{\text{KO}}\right\},

(52)

where
q_{\text{KO}}\in(0,1)
is the target FDR level, such that the filter generates a set of active variables
\hat{S}_{v}=\left\{i\in[p]:W_{i}\geq T\right\}
. FDR control follows from exchangeability, which makes the signs of the null statistics
W_{i}
independent coin flips. Consequently, the threshold
T
gives a conservative estimate of false discoveries
[
7
]
.

Limitations and improvements.

The Knockoffs framework has some key limitations. Doubling the data dimensionality can be computationally expensive, which has motivated the development of screening rules for Knockoffs
[
65
,
73
]
. Additionally, the knockoff generation process can be random, producing different results across runs. Consequently, several approaches have been proposed to
derandomise
Knockoffs by aggregating results from multiple runs
[
78
]
, including using e-values
[
77
]
.

C.3.1
Knockoffs with SLOPE

SLOPE and Knockoffs both control the FDR, but under different assumptions. SLOPE assumes an orthogonal design matrix, limiting the FDR properties to low dimensions. The model-X Knockoffs framework has no dimensionality restrictions, but assumes the feature distribution
F_{X}
is known and the conditional distribution
F_{Y\mid X}
can be arbitrary and unknown. These assumptions are reasonable in genetics, where
F_{Y\mid X}
is difficult to estimate due to complex correlation structures, whereas
F_{X}
is often known or easily estimated, as it can be experimentally controlled
[
91
]
. Knockoffs also assumes exchangeability of the knockoff variables and that the coin flip and sufficiency properties hold. Violations can make the feature statistic asymmetric, compromising FDR control
[
15
]
.

The flexibility of Knockoffs stems from its use of penalized regression, allowing any estimator that satisfies the assumptions. As such, we explore combining Knockoffs with SLOPE to achieve FDR control in general settings.
50
showed that SLOPE with Knockoffs can improve FDR control and power over the lasso in simulations, though several questions remain.

These are: Does the SLOPE statistic satisfy the coin flip property? This has not been addressed in
50
or elsewhere. How does the SLOPE FDR parameter (
q_{v}
) interact with the Knockoffs parameter (
q_{\text{KO}}
)?
77
show, for eBH and Knockoffs, that setting the Knockoffs parameter lower than the eBH one is preferable for FDR control. Finally, how should the SLOPE design be optimized for Knockoffs, and are new penalty sequences or knockoff constructions needed? This is especially relevant since SLOPE’s steep decay and clustering can reduce contrast between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}}
.

Although these questions are beyond the scope of this manuscript, the following section explores in more detail how the Knockoffs framework can be applied to SGS.

C.3.2
Sparse-group Knockoffs

Knockoffs have been applied to group regression in
27
, where multi-task regression is reformulated as a group lasso problem and a group-specific Knockoffs construction is proposed. For the feature statistic, the norm is modified to be

J_{\text{glasso, KO}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}^{(j)}\|_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{\text{KO}})^{(j)}\|_{2}.

The path statistic is then used, defined as the point along the path at which a group enters the model. To allow for direct comparison to the variable feature statistic, in this manuscript, we adopt the group effects statistic for
j\in[m]
,

W=\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}^{(j)}\|_{2}-\|(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{\text{KO}})^{(j)}\|_{2}.

(53)

The Knockoffs construction and filter proceed as in the variable case. The knockoff variables are assigned to their own groups, so that each original group has a corresponding knockoff group.

Although multi-layer Knockoffs filters exist
[
9
,
39
,
47
,
53
]
, Knockoffs have not been specifically studied in the context of sparse-group regression models. Two main concerns in this setting are the construction of knockoffs and the coin flip property. Constructing knockoffs compatible with both variable and group penalties is challenging: the coin flip property does not hold at a variable level under a group knockoff construction
[
47
]
. Our investigation for the sparse-group lasso (SGL) and SGS shows that the coin flip property holds only if the knockoffs are placed in the same group as the originals, which may dilute group penalization, and holds less often for SGS due to its dual sorting procedures (Figure
20
).

In this manuscript, we present an initial implementation of sparse-group Knockoffs using SGS. SGS provides both variable- (Equation
51
) and group-level (Equation
53
) feature statistics, with corresponding thresholds used to select active variables and groups, enabling bi-level FDR control. To satisfy the coin flip property, the knockoff variables are placed into the same groups as the originals; this is in contrast to the group-only setup, where they form separate groups. This gives the SGS Knockoffs problem as

\displaystyle[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}(\lambda)\;\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{\text{KO}}(\lambda)]\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2p}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}-[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{KO}}]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\lambda\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{2p}v_{i}|b|_{(i)}

\displaystyle+\lambda(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{2m}w_{j}\sqrt{p_{j}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\bigg\}.

The screening rules reduce the computational cost of Knockoffs, but the synthetic data results in Section
5
show that it is the most computationally intensive model selection approach for SGS.

C.4
Two-step

Two-step procedures combine the positive elements of multiple models into a stronger overall model.
26
proposed a two-step method for prediction, using the lasso followed by least squares refitting to debias coefficients. The approach addresses CV’s computational inefficiency and lack of finite-sample guarantees. Similarly,
116
introduced the two-stage prior lasso (TSPLASSO), performing two lasso stages for gene selection and sample classification. For overviews of multi-step regression, see relevant summaries
[
23
,
111
,
120
]
, and for FDR-controlling two-step methods see
87
.

C.4.1
Two-step SLOPE

The
Two-step SLOPE
(TS-SLOPE) procedure has been shown to recover the true model with high probability

[
46
,
48
]
. The procedure is defined by the steps

1.

Obtain an initial estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{[1]}
from applying SLOPE.

2.

Obtain a truncated estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{[2]}=\operatorname{prox}_{\text{slope}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{[1]})
, where
\operatorname{prox}_{\text{slope}}
is the proximal operator of the SLOPE penalty (given in Algorithm 3 in
16
).

The final estimate is shown to recover the pattern of the true signal, although it is also shown to be heavily biased
[
48
]
.

This approach can be extended to gSLOPE and SGS using the model-specific proximal operators. The gSLOPE proximal operator is given in Algorithm 2 in
45
. For SGS, the proximal operators are applied one after another, so that step 2 becomes

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{[2]}=\operatorname{prox}_{\text{slope}}(\operatorname{prox}_{\text{gslope}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}_{[1]})).

Applying the operators in the reverse order was also tested and yielded nearly identical results.
