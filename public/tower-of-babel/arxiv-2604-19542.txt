Allard Regularity for Abelian Yang–Mills–Higgs Equation
Huy The Nguyen, Shengwen Wang

Abstract
. We study solutions to the self-dual Abelian Yang–Mills–Higgs (YMH) equations in the singular limit ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 , where the associated self-dual Ginzburg–Landau type energy E ε ​ ( u A ) = ∫ M ( | ∇ A u | 2 + ε 2 ​ | F A | 2 + ( 1 − | u | 2 ) 2 4 ​ ε 2 ) ​ dvol g \displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}\begin{pmatrix}u\\ A\end{pmatrix}=\int_{M}\left(|\nabla^{A}u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|F_{A}|^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\mathrm{dvol}_{g} exhibits concentration along codimension-two sets. 1.
Introduction

We investigate the regularity and structure of solutions to the self-dual Abelian Yang–Mills–Higgs (YMH) equations on Riemannian manifolds, with particular emphasis on configurations concentrating near codimension two minimal submanifolds. These equations arise as the Euler–Lagrange system for the Ginzburg–Landau type energy functional

(1.1)

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
A\end{pmatrix}=\int_{M}\left(|\nabla^{A}u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|F_{A}|^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\mathrm{dvol}_{g},

where
u
is a section of a Hermitian line bundle,
A
is a
U(1)
connection 1-form and
F_{A}
is the connection 2-form of
A
. In the singular limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, critical points of
E_{\varepsilon}
are expected to concentrate along a vortex set, and the analysis of their regularity becomes analogous to questions in geometric measure theory.

The theory for
U(1)
Yang–Mills–Higgs equations and relation to minimal submanifolds of codimension two have been initiated by Pigati–Stern
[
21
]
. They proved that critical points of the
E_{\varepsilon}
functional converge to stationary varifolds of codimension two in a closed Riemannian manifold as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. Later Parise–Pigati–Stern
[
16
]
proved the
\Gamma
convergence of
E_{\varepsilon}
to the codimension two area functional. as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. When the gauge group is the non-Abelian
SU(2)
, the
\Gamma
convergence of the corresponding functional to the codimension three area functional was also developed recently by Parise–Pigati–Stern
[
17
]
. For the reverse question, Badran–del Pino
[
1
,
2
]
showed the existence of solutions to the Abelian Yang–Mills-Higgs equation converging to admissible, non-degenerate minimal submanifolds of codimension two and developed the ansatz for linear analysis in this gauged setting (see also
[
7
]
for a variational proof of the existence of solutions near non-degenerate minimal submanifolds).

This model can be viewed as a codimension two analogue of the Allen–Cahn phase transition model with energy

E^{AC}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\int_{M}\left(|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\mathrm{dvol}_{g}.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the connection between Allen–Cahn critical points and minimal hypersurfaces (codimension one stationary varifolds) has been extensively studied. The
\Gamma
-convergence of
E^{AC}_{\varepsilon}
to the area functional was established by Modica
[
14
]
, and convergence of critical points to stationary varifolds was proved by Hutchinson–Tonegawa
[
12
]
. Moreover, Pacard–Ritoré
[
15
]
constructed solutions concentrating near non-degenerate minimal hypersurfaces. This correspondence has led to significant geometric applications, including min–max constructions of minimal hypersurfaces
[
9
]
and results on the abundance of minimal surfaces
[
5
]
. While the convergence is initially weak, refined regularity theory developed by Wang–Wei
[
27
,
28
]
shows that, under suitable assumptions, nodal sets converge in
C^{2,\alpha}
and satisfy curvature estimates. In this paper, we will develop the analogous improved regularity theory of
[
27
,
28
]
for the Abelian YMH model .

Our first result is an improvement of regularity for the nodal sets of the Higgs field that are converging to a multiplicity one submanifold of codimension two.

For any

In the regime of almost unit density, an Allard type regularity theory also hold for the nodal sets of Allen-Cahn equations

\displaystyle=0

Then up to a rotation in

Remark 1.3
.

The missing ingredient for generalising this Allard type regularity theorem to general ambient dimensions is a rigidity statement for solutions with unit density at infinity, also known as the Gibbon’s Conjecture (
2.3

[

Our approach adapts Allard’s regularity theory to this gauge-theoretic setting. We construct approximate solutions by scaling the classical degree-one vortices in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and localising them via Fermi coordinates around a prescribed minimal submanifold
M\subset N
. The error of this ansatz is controlled via a linearised analysis, with perturbations imposed to be orthogonal to the gauge and translational zero modes.

We establish Lipschitz regularity and curvature bounds for the constructed solutions and derive precise estimates for the discrepancy from being an exact solution. A key technical tool is the decomposition of the linearised operator into elliptic and gauge components, and the enforcement of Coulomb gauge conditions to isolate the geometric content. These results extend and refine earlier work on the Ginzburg–Landau and Abelian Yang–Mills–Higgs models and provide a robust analytic framework for understanding vortex concentration in higher codimension.

We organise the paper as follows. In Section
2
and Section

4
we construct an approximate solution the YMH equation based on the initial Lipschitz regularity. In section

1.2

Variational Equations

We follow the notation of Badran–Del Pino

Let

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}\begin{pmatrix}u\\

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}\begin{pmatrix}u\\

In order to compute the Euler-Lagrange equation, we consider a first variation of the form

where

is a section with compact support and

where

is a one-form with compact support. This gives us

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{\varepsilon}(u_{t},A_{t})

\displaystyle=\int 2\Re\llangle{\nabla^{A}}^{\star}\nabla^{A}u,v\rrangle-2\Re\llangle(\nabla-\imath A)u,\imath Bu\rrangle+\varepsilon^{2}\int 2\llangle B,d^{*}dA\rrangle-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int(1-|u|^{2})2\Re\llangle u,v\rrangle.

Replacing

and recalling that

are real valued, we see that a smooth pair

gives a critical point for the Abelian Yang–Mills–Higgs energy if and only if it satisfies the system of partial differential equations:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\nabla^{A}}^{\star}\nabla^{A}u&=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\left(1-|u|^{2}\right)u,\\
\varepsilon^{2}d^{*}dA&=\langle\nabla u,\imath u\rangle\end{split}

where

\displaystyle\left(d^{*}A\right)\left(e_{k}\right)=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\nabla_{e_{j}}A\right)\left(e_{j},e_{k}\right)

for some orthonormal frame

and

, the real part of the Hermitian inner product
\llangle\cdot,\cdot\rrangle
. We note here that

(2.3)

\displaystyle S_{\varepsilon}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
A\end{pmatrix}=0.

If

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\nabla^{A}}^{\star}\nabla^{A}u&=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-|u|^{2}\right)u\\

(2.5)

\displaystyle\left(W_{1},W_{2}\right)_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\binom{u_{1}}{A_{1}}\cdot\binom{u_{2}}{A_{2}}

(2.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left\langle u_{1},u_{2}\right\rangle+\varepsilon^{2}A_{1}\cdot A_{2},

Recall that the energy functional (

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}_{\gamma}\binom{u}{A}=\binom{ue^{\imath\gamma}}{A+d\gamma}

\displaystyle Z_{W,g}=\operatorname{span}_{\gamma}\left\{\Theta_{W}[\gamma]\right\},

\displaystyle\left\langle\binom{i\gamma u}{\nabla\gamma}\cdot\binom{\xi}{B}\right\rangle_{\varepsilon}=0

\displaystyle S^{\prime}_{\varepsilon,W}=L_{\varepsilon}-\Theta_{W}\Theta_{\varepsilon,W}^{\star},

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon,W}\begin{pmatrix}\phi\\
\omega\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta^{A}\phi-\frac{1}{2}(1-3|u|^{2})\phi+2\imath\varepsilon^{2}\nabla^{A}u\cdot\omega)\\

The operator

Local Expansions for the rescaled solutions

In the exponential coordinates introduced above with

Proposition 3.1
.

Fix

, we can choose local coordinates
(y^{1},\dots,y^{n})
on
M
and an orthonormal

\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}
so that:
the local metric expansion in the associated Fermi coordinates

where
\mathcal{A}
is the second fundamental form of

In particular, if the second fundamental form satisfies

(3.5)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}|\leq C\varepsilon,

then for
|z|\leq r
sufficiently small we have the uniform bound

\displaystyle|g_{ij}(p,z)-\delta_{ij}|\leq C\varepsilon|z|+C\varepsilon^{2}|z|^{2}\leq C(r)\,\varepsilon.

Proof.

The existence of local coordinates so that the metric the normal connection satisfy

\displaystyle g_{ij}=

\displaystyle\delta_{ij},\qquad\forall i,j=1,\dots,n

\displaystyle\nabla_{\partial_{y_{i}}}\nu_{\beta}=

\displaystyle\omega^{\alpha}_{i,\beta}\nu_{\alpha}=0,\qquad\forall i,j=1,\dots n,\quad\alpha,\beta=1,2.

at the point

is standard in Riemannian geometry.

We compute the tangential metric components

\displaystyle g_{ij}(p,z)=\big\langle\partial_{i}X(p,z),\partial_{j}X(p,z)\big\rangle,

where

\displaystyle\partial_{i}X=

\displaystyle\partial_{i}(Y+z^{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{i}Y-z^{\alpha}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{i,\alpha}\partial_{j}Y+z^{\alpha}\omega^{\beta}_{\alpha i}\nu_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{i}Y-z^{\alpha}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{i,\alpha}\partial_{j}Y.

Thus

\displaystyle g_{ij}(p,z)=

\displaystyle(g_{M})_{ij}(p)-2z^{\alpha}\mathcal{A}^{k}_{i,\alpha}g_{kj}(p)+z^{\alpha}z^{\beta}\mathcal{A}^{k}_{i,\alpha}\mathcal{A}^{l}_{j,\beta}g_{kl}(p)+O(|\mathcal{A}|^{3}|z|^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}-2\mathcal{A}^{j}_{i,\alpha}(p)z^{\alpha}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}^{k}_{i,\alpha}\mathcal{A}^{k}_{j,\beta}z^{\alpha}z^{\beta}+O(|\mathcal{A}|^{3}|z|^{3}),

where the remainder comes from higher–order Taylor terms in the Fermi expansion.

Finally, if

on

\displaystyle|g_{ij}(p,z)-\delta_{ij}|\leq C\varepsilon|z|+C\varepsilon^{2}|z|^{2}\leq C(r)\varepsilon,

Theorem 3.2
.

Denote by

Let

Let

\displaystyle t(y,z):=z-h(y).

Define

\displaystyle A(y,t)(e^{\perp}_{\alpha})=A_{0}(t)(e^{\perp}_{\alpha}),\quad A(y,t)(e_{i})=a(|t|)\,\omega_{i}(y)-(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})(y)\,A_{0}(t)(e^{\perp}_{\rho}),\quad u(y,t)=u_{0}(t).

\displaystyle F_{A}(e^{\perp}_{\alpha},e^{\perp}_{\beta})

\displaystyle=F_{A_{0}}(e^{\perp}_{\alpha},e^{\perp}_{\beta}),

\displaystyle F_{A}(e_{i},e^{\perp}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=-(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})\,F_{A_{0}}(e^{\perp}_{\rho},e^{\perp}_{\alpha})+O(|t|),

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})(\nabla_{j}h^{\sigma})\,F_{A_{0}}(e^{\perp}_{\rho},e^{\perp}_{\sigma})+a(|t|)\,K^{\perp}(y)\,\varepsilon_{ij}-K^{\perp}(y)\,\varepsilon_{ij}\,\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}\,z^{\beta}\,A(e^{\perp}_{\alpha})+O(|t|).

In particular, on the core

, we have

\displaystyle F_{A}(e_{i},e^{\perp}_{\alpha})|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=-(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})\,F_{A_{0}}(e^{\perp}_{\rho},e^{\perp}_{\alpha}),

\displaystyle F_{A}(e_{i},e_{j})|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})(\nabla_{j}h^{\sigma})\,F_{A_{0}}(e^{\perp}_{\rho},e^{\perp}_{\sigma})

Proof.

We use that the structure group is Abelian, hence

\displaystyle F_{A}(X,Y)=dA(X,Y)=\nabla_{X}\!\big(A(Y)\big)-\nabla_{Y}\!\big(A(X)\big)-A([X,Y]).

Fibre–fibre.

Since
A=A_{0}
on each normal fibre and the normal Fermi frame is geodesic along the fibre,
we have

\displaystyle F_{A}(e^{\perp}_{\alpha},e^{\perp}_{\beta})=F_{A_{0}}(e^{\perp}_{\alpha},e^{\perp}_{\beta}).

Tangent–fibre.

The
y

enters only through the centre shift

t=z-h(y)

therefore gives, up to errors from the non-product

\displaystyle\nabla_{e_{i}}\!\big(A(y,t)(e^{\perp}_{\alpha})\big)=-(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})\,\nabla_{e^{\perp}_{\rho}}[A_{0}(t)(e^{\perp}_{\alpha})].

Using

\displaystyle F_{A}(y.t)(e_{i},e^{\perp}_{\alpha})=-(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})\,F_{A_{0}}(t)(e^{\perp}_{\rho},e^{\perp}_{\alpha})+O(|t|).

Tangent–tangent.

Write

\displaystyle A(e_{i})=A^{\mathrm{base}}(e_{i})+A^{\mathrm{shift}}(e_{i}),

where

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{base}}(e_{i}):=a(|t|)\omega_{i},\qquad A^{\mathrm{shift}}(e_{i}):=-(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})\,A_{0}(e^{\perp}_{\rho}).

Then

\displaystyle F_{A}(e_{i},e_{j})=dA^{\mathrm{base}}(e_{i},e_{j})+dA^{\mathrm{shift}}(e_{i},e_{j}).

For the base part, using

\displaystyle dA^{\mathrm{base}}(e_{i},e_{j})=d(a\omega)(e_{i},e_{j})-A([e_{i},e_{j}])\big|_{\mathrm{base}}.

Since

\displaystyle d(a\omega)(e_{i},e_{j})=a(|t|)\,d\omega(e_{i},e_{j})+O(|t|)=a(|t|)\,\Omega^{\perp}(e_{i},e_{j})+O(|t|)=a(|t|)\,K^{\perp}(y)\varepsilon_{ij}+O(|t|).

Moreover, the leading normal component of the commutator of tangential Fermi fields is
governed by the normal curvature; in codimension

\displaystyle([e_{i},e_{j}])^{\perp}=K^{\perp}(y)\,\varepsilon_{ij}\,\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}\,t^{\beta}\,e^{\perp}_{\alpha}+O(|t|)=O(|t|).

\displaystyle dA^{\mathrm{base}}(e_{i},e_{j})=a(|t|)\,K^{\perp}(y)\varepsilon_{ij}+O(|t|).

For the shift part, using the product rule and that

only through

\displaystyle\nabla_{e_{i}}\!\big(A_{0}(e^{\perp}_{\rho})\big)=-(\nabla_{i}h^{\sigma})\nabla_{\,e^{\perp}_{\sigma}}\!\big(A_{0}(e^{\perp}_{\rho})\big)+O(|t|).

Substituting into the shift term, we can directly compute

\displaystyle dA^{\mathrm{shift}}(e_{i},e_{j})=(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})(\nabla_{j}h^{\sigma})\,F_{A_{0}}(e^{\perp}_{\rho},e^{\perp}_{\sigma})+O(|t|).

Therefore,

\displaystyle F_{A}(e_{i},e_{j})=(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})(\nabla_{j}h^{\sigma})\,F_{A_{0}}(e^{\perp}_{\rho},e^{\perp}_{\sigma})+a(|t|)\,K^{\perp}(y)\varepsilon_{ij}-K^{\perp}(y)\,\varepsilon_{ij}\,\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}\,z^{\beta}\,A(e^{\perp}_{\alpha})+O(|t|).

Since

\displaystyle\nabla_{A,e^{\perp}_{\alpha}}u=\nabla_{e^{\perp}_{\alpha}}(u_{0}(t))-iA(e^{\perp}_{\alpha})u_{0}(t)=\nabla_{A_{0},e^{\perp}_{\alpha}}u_{0}.

For tangential directions,

\displaystyle\nabla_{e_{i}}(u_{0}(t))=-(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})\,e^{\perp}_{\rho}(u_{0}(t))+O(|t|),

and hence

\displaystyle=\nabla_{e_{i}}(u_{0}(t))-iA(e_{i})u_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=-(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})\Big(\nabla_{e^{\perp}_{\rho}}(u_{0}(t))-iA_{0}(e^{\perp}_{\rho})u_{0}(t)\Big)-i\,a(|t|)\omega_{i}(y)u_{0}(t)+O(|t|)

\displaystyle=-(\nabla_{i}h^{\rho})\,\nabla_{A_{0},e^{\perp}_{\rho}}u_{0}-i\,a(|t|)\omega_{i}(y)u_{0}(t)+O(|t|).

\displaystyle\quad g^{j\alpha}=0,\quad g^{\alpha\beta}=\delta^{\alpha\beta}.

Let

[

]

(4.5)

We will prove first that these orthogonality conditions hold with the right choice of
h
and a gauge transformation.

Proposition 4.4
.

Under our hypothesis
(L)
, we can assume for small enough
\varepsilon
that
\Phi=\hat{U}_{\varepsilon}-\tilde{U}_{0}
is sufficiently small in
C^{1,\alpha}(C_{r})
. For
h\in C^{1,\alpha}(B_{r}^{n}(y_{0});\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\gamma\in C^{2,\alpha}(C_{r};\mathbb{R})
, denote by

\displaystyle\Phi_{\gamma}(y,z):=G_{\gamma}(\hat{U})(y,z)-\tilde{U}_{0}(z-h(y))

the
\gamma
- gauge transformed perturbation, where

\displaystyle G_{\gamma}(\hat{U})=G_{\gamma}(\hat{u},\hat{A}):=(\hat{u}e^{i\gamma},\,\hat{A}+d\gamma)^{T}.

Then there exists

so that the orthogonality conditions (
4.5
), (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) hold simultaneously.

Proof.

The orthogonality conditions are equivalent to finding
(h,\gamma)
such that

\displaystyle F(h,\gamma):=

\displaystyle\left(\Theta^{*}_{1,\tilde{U}_{0}}\!\big(\Phi_{\gamma}\big),\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi_{\gamma}(y,z)\cdot\mathbf{V}_{1}(z-h(y))\,dz,\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi_{\gamma}(y,z)\cdot\mathbf{V}_{2}(z-h(y))\,dz\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{0},

where the first component gives gauge orthogonality and the 2nd and 3rd components give translational orthogonality.

Its Fréchet derivative is then

\displaystyle DF(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\xi}F_{\mathrm{gauge}}&\partial_{\eta}F_{\mathrm{gauge}}\\[4.0pt]
\partial_{\xi}F_{\mathrm{trans}}&\partial_{\eta}F_{\mathrm{trans}}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
h_{s}=s\eta
,
\gamma_{s}=s\xi
and we compute the variation of the perturbation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{0}\Phi_{\gamma}=(i\hat{u}\,\xi,\,d\xi)+\eta^{\alpha}(y)\,\partial_{\alpha}\widetilde{U}_{0}(z).

Substituting in, we thus get the linearisation of the map
F
at
(0,0)
as follows.

\displaystyle(DF(0,0)(\eta,\xi))_{\mathrm{gauge}}=

\displaystyle(d^{*}d+\langle\tilde{u}_{0},\hat{u}\rangle)\,\xi+\eta^{\alpha}\Big(\langle i\tilde{u}_{0},\partial_{\alpha}\hat{u}_{0}\rangle+d^{*}(\partial_{\alpha}\tilde{A}_{0})\Big)-\eta^{\alpha}\langle i\partial_{\alpha}\tilde{u}_{0},\,\hat{u}-\tilde{u}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle(DF(0,0)(\eta,\xi))_{\mathrm{trans},\beta}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\langle i\hat{u}(y,z)\,\xi(y,z),\,d\xi(y,z)\right\rangle\cdot\mathbf{V}_{\beta}(z)\,dz

for

The
\xi
- gauge block (upper left block) is

\displaystyle(d^{*}d+\langle\tilde{u}_{0},\hat{u}\rangle)\,\xi=(d^{*}d+|\tilde{u}_{0}|)^{2}\,\xi+O(|\Phi|)\xi.

Notice

is a positive operator and the existence and uniqueness of solutions follows by Lax–Milgram. So this block is uniformly invertible as
\Psi
is assumed to have small

The
\eta
- translation block (lower right block)

\displaystyle\eta^{\alpha}(y)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\partial_{\alpha}\tilde{U}_{0}(z)\cdot\mathbf{V}_{\beta}(z)\,dz-\eta^{\alpha}(y)\int\Phi(y,z)\cdot\partial_{\alpha}\mathbf{V}_{\beta}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{\alpha}(y)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}(z)\cdot\mathbf{V}_{\beta}(z)\,dz+O(\varepsilon^{2})-\eta^{\alpha}(y)\int\Phi(y,z)\cdot\partial_{\alpha}\mathbf{V}_{\beta}(z)\,dz

is also uniformly invertible for small

as
\Psi
is small in
C^{1,\alpha}
and that

form an orthogonal basis for the translational kernel.

The
\xi

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\langle\imath\hat{u}(y,z)\,\xi(y,z),\,d\xi(y,z)\right\rangle\cdot\mathbf{V}_{\beta}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\langle\imath\tilde{u}_{0}(y,z)\,\xi(y,z),\,d\xi(y,z)\right\rangle\cdot\mathbf{V}_{\beta}(z)\,dz-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\langle\imath\phi(y,z)\,\xi(y,z),\,d\xi(y,z)\right\rangle\cdot\mathbf{V}_{\beta}(z)\,dz.

Its first term is small because the gauge - zero mode
\left\langle i{u}_{0}(y,z)\,\xi(y,z),\,d\xi(y,z)\right\rangle
is orthogonal to the translational kernel
\mathbf{V}_{\beta}
and that
\tilde{u}_{0}
and
u_{0}
are close. its second term is small is again by smallness of
\Psi
in
C^{1,\alpha}
norm.

So by the above analysis, the block matrix
DF(0,0)
is uniformly invertible near the origin and there exists
(h,\gamma)
giving us

by the Banach space implicit function theorem.

∎

With the choice of
\gamma
and
h
, we use
G_{\gamma}(\hat{U})=(\hat{u}e^{i\gamma},\,\hat{A}+d\gamma)^{T}
in place of

it follows that the three orthogonality conditions (

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left[\partial_{j}\phi^{1}(y,z)f^{\prime}(|z-h(y)|)\right]dz+\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left\langle\nabla_{j}(\omega(y,z)),\frac{a^{\prime}(|z-h(y)|)}{|z-h(y)|}dz^{2}\right\rangle dz\\

Appendix A
Coulomb Gauge

As explained in the introduction, due to the infinite dimensional gauge invariance, the equation (
2.2
) is not elliptic and does not have interior estimates. However, under the additional constraint

(A.1)

\displaystyle d^{\star}A=0,

which is called the Coulomb gauge condition, it is elliptic and satisfies the following interior estimates.

Proposition A.1

(c.f. Proposition A.1 of
[
21
]
)

For any
\Lambda>0
,
R>1
, and

A_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}
is a solution to (

) in

A_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}(B_{R}(0))\leq\Lambda.

A.1
) and the boundary condition

where

is the outer normal to
\partial B_{R}(0)

(A.2)

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon^{k+\alpha}u\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(B_{1}(0))}+\|\varepsilon^{k+\alpha+1}A\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(B_{1}(0))}\leq C.

Proof.

By the standard Bochner-Weitzenbök formula, we obtain (c.f.
[
21
, (A.11)]
)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Delta(\varepsilon^{2}|A_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})\geq-C(\varepsilon^{2}|A_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})-C.

The Moser iteration then gives

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\|A_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}

\displaystyle=C(\Lambda,R)(1+\varepsilon^{2}\|A_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}(B_{R})})

\displaystyle=C(\Lambda,R)(1+\varepsilon^{2}\|dA_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{R})})

\displaystyle=C(\Lambda,R)(\Lambda+1),

by the energy bound (
A.2
). The rest of the proof follows as that in
[

, Proposition A.1]

∎
