Midgut malrotation and ovarian dermoid cyst as a dual cause of neonatal intestinal obstruction: a case report and literature review
Authors: Mohammed Alra’e, Ibrahim Kattoush, Maaweya Jabareen, Wasef Alhroub, Radwan Abukarsh, Hadeel Halahla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Midgut malrotation is a congenital anomaly characterized by impaired rotation of the gut around the superior mesenteric artery during embryonic development. It is one of the most common causes of neonatal intestinal obstruction. A dermoid cyst arises from the trapping of ectodermal elements during embryonic development.Case presentation:A 6-day-old female neonate was brought to the hospital with a history of bilious emesis and failure to pass stool. The antenatal history was uneventful. She was diagnosed with intestinal obstruction. The patient was treated initially as a case of clinical sepsis and then treated surgically. Through a transverse incision, the abdomen was explored, and midgut malrotation with Ladd’s bands was found under an adhering ovarian cyst, which was diagnosed as a dermoid cyst by histopathology. The simultaneous dual-cause obstruction in this case has prompted us to report it.Clinical discussion:Malrotation results when the midgut fails to complete its 270° counterclockwise rotation in utero, leading to anomalous positioning and often to fibrous Ladd’s bands that cross and compress the duodenum. Meanwhile, congenital ovarian dermoid cysts can contribute to obstruction through adhesive bands or direct mass effect. The concurrent occurrence highlights the importance of considering multiple simultaneous etiologies in diagnosis and surgical planning.Conclusion:Considering multiple congenital defects, such as malrotation and dermoid ovarian cysts, as a dual cause of intestinal obstruction is important in a condition that has a large differential diagnosis.

Introduction

Midgut malrotation is defined as a congenital anomaly caused by an impaired process of intestinal rotation and development. It is one of the most common causes of intestinal obstruction that manifests as irritability, vomiting, constipation, and abdominal distension[1,2].

Ovarian dermoid cysts are benign developmental anomalies resulting from the trapping of ectodermal components during embryonic closure. They possess adult ectodermal, mesodermal, and endodermal tissues, with ectodermal being the predominant[3,4].

Dermoid cysts typically manifest during the first year of life and exhibit gradual growth. The exact etiology remains unknown. Approximately 40% of dermoid cysts are diagnosed at birth, with the remaining 60% detected by 5 years of age, with a slight predominance in girls. Imaging helps in the successful preoperative diagnosis of dermoid cysts. Surgery remains the treatment of choice[5,6].

Although midgut malrotation and ovarian dermoid cysts have each been separately documented in the neonatal period, their simultaneous occurrence as an etiology of intestinal obstruction is extremely rare. Reporting such cases contributes to the limited literature, generates awareness, and has a bearing on surgical decision-making for the management of complicated neonatal abdominal emergencies.

This manuscript was prepared following the SCARE guidelines[7].

Case presentation

A 6-day-old female neonate presented with bilious emesis beginning at 4 hours of life and failure to pass stool. She was born at 38 weeks by spontaneous vaginal delivery to consanguineous, healthy parents after an uneventful pregnancy with no history of polyhydramnios or teratogenic exposure. Birth weight was 3100 g, and initial feeding was adequate. She passed meconium at 3 hours of life but failed to pass stool thereafter. Despite persistent bilious vomiting, she was discharged against medical advice at 15 hours of life and later returned with recurrent emesis and poor feeding.

On admission, she appeared ill, dehydrated, hypoactive, and hypothermic. Examination showed a soft but distended and tender abdomen with bloody stool in the diaper; the anus was patent, and no dysmorphic features were noted. She was kept nil per os, with a nasogastric tube draining bile-stained fluid, and intravenous fluids and empiric antibiotics (ampicillin, gentamicin, and metronidazole) were initiated for presumed sepsis.

Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis, elevated C-reactive protein, and acute kidney injury that improved with rehydration, while blood cultures remained negative. Plain abdominal radiography demonstrated small bowel obstruction with a gasless distal abdomen, and an upper gastrointestinal fluoroscopic study confirmed the diagnosis.

An emergent laparotomy via a transverse incision with exploration was performed. Intraoperative findings included midgut malrotation, Ladd’s bands over the jejunum, and a right ovarian cyst measuring 6 × 7 cm with torsion, which was identified (Fig. 1) and adherent to the intestinal mesentery at the site of obstruction. Approximately 15 cm of jejunum was noted to be necrotic and was resected, after which intestinal continuity was restored through an end-to-end anastomosis. Resection of the ovarian cyst with preservation of the right ovary and liberation of the obstruction from the Ladd’s bands were performed along with the Ladd’s procedure. Enteral formula feedings were gradually started on postoperative day 5. She was discharged in stable condition on day 16 of life. Subsequent follow-up for 6 months was unremarkable, with appropriate growth and development. Discussion

Intestinal obstruction is a common neonatal surgical condition that typically presents with vomiting, abdominal distension, pain, constipation, and an abdominal mass. Causes may be intraluminal, intramural, or extraluminal. The differential diagnosis varies by age, but hernia, intussusception, volvulus, and malrotation account for most cases[2]. Additionally, most congenital defects manifest during the neonatal period[8].

Midgut malrotation is among the most common neonatal causes of intestinal obstruction, and bilious emesis in neonates should be considered malrotation until proven otherwise. Between the 4th and 6th gestational weeks, the midgut undergoes physiological herniation and rotates 270° counterclockwise around the superior mesenteric artery before returning to the abdominal cavity. The cecum is the last structure to return and eventually descends to the right iliac fossa[1,9].

Malrotation results from the failure of normal intestinal rotation and fixation, although the exact mechanisms are unclear. Consequently, the cecum may return first, resulting in a left-sided colon and a right-sided small intestine. Ladd’s bands are congenital fibrotic bands connecting the cecum to the lateral abdominal wall and may cause obstruction. Volvulus is a major complication caused by the twisting of the bowel around a congenitally narrow mesenteric base, leading to obstruction and vascular compromise[1,9,10].

Ovarian dermoid cysts are benign developmental lesions arising from trapped ectodermal components during embryogenesis. Since they originate from totipotent stem cells, they may contain tissues derived from all three germ layers, including skin, hair, teeth, adipose tissue, muscle, thyroid, and neural tissue[3,4]. Although most ovarian cysts are asymptomatic, complications such as torsion, peritonitis, bowel obstruction, and malignant transformation may occur[11,12].

Ovarian cysts may cause intestinal obstruction through adhesions or mass effect. Bhedi et al studied 50 pediatric patients with intestinal obstruction and found that 50% were neonates, 82% had congenital causes, and mortality occurred only in neonates (24%)[8]. This highlights the increased vulnerability of neonates to life-threatening complications.

Large ovarian cysts (>40 mm), as in our patient, carry a higher risk of torsion and complications, especially when complex. Kurtoğlu et al evaluated 20 antenatal and neonatal ovarian cysts and reported that all were unilateral, 75% occurred on the right side, and 25% developed torsion[13]. Management of complex cysts is guided by clinical preference. Some surgeons prefer to remove the cyst, while others prefer the preservative approach[14,15].

The association between malrotation and ovarian dermoid cysts is extremely rare. Molnár et al reported the first case in a premature 3-month-old infant with ovarian torsion causing bowel obstruction[16]. Similar presentations have also been described in adults[12]. Moreover, Khanna et al reported a case of dermoid cyst rupture into the sigmoid colon[17]. Given that these two congenital anomalies are essentially sporadic, no established genetic or embryological relationship links malrotation and ovarian dermoid cysts, and their coexistence is likely incidental[18].

The differential diagnosis includes functional cysts, torsion, abscesses, lymphangiomas, and neoplasms. Germ cell tumors are the most common ovarian neoplasms in children and adolescents; dermoid cysts are the most common benign tumors, whereas dysgerminomas are the most common malignant ones. When malignancy is suspected, tumor markers, including AFP, beta-hCG, and CA-125, should be assessed[19].

Conclusion

This report presents a rare case of a neonate who presented with bilious vomiting. Dual-cause obstruction by Ladd’s bands and a torsed ovarian dermoid cyst was unique. Timely surgical intervention is crucial for optimizing outcomes. Considering multiple congenital defects is important when a large differential diagnosis is present.
