Metabolomic properties of the fluid from the surgical ligation after breast-conserving therapy and intraoperative radiotherapy
Authors: Agnieszka Gdowicz-Kłosok, Łukasz Boguszewicz, Agnieszka Skorupa, Mateusz Ciszek, Maria Turska-di Amico, Małgorzata Krześniak, Barbara Łasut-Szyszka, Maria Sokól, Jerzy Wydmański
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intraoperative radiotherapy with low-energy X-rays (IOXRT) is an increasingly utilized modality during breast conserving therapy (BCT). However, the molecular mechanisms by which it affects the postoperative microenvironment have not yet been fully elucidated. Surgical wound fluid (WF) has been shown to modulate cancer cell behavior; however, its metabolomic composition has not previously been characterized in the context of breast cancer. The objective of this study was to evaluate metabolic alterations in postoperative WF and to determine whether IOXRT induces different metabolic signatures compared to mastectomy (AMP). Postoperative WF was collected from 54 breast cancer patients (38 BCT IOXRT; 16 AMP) at two time points: day 1 (A) and day 5 (B) after surgery. The samples were then analyzed by1H NMR spectroscopy using NOESY, CPMG, and JRES techniques. A total of 114 spectral signals were quantified, and 42 metabolites were identified. Multivariate analyses, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS DA), and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS DA), as well as the univariate Wilcoxon signed rank tests were applied to assess temporal and intergroup differences. A clear metabolic separation was observed between time points A and B in both treatment groups. However, statistical analysis did not reveal significant differences between BCT IOXRT and AMP. In BCT IOXRT, on the fifth day, WF exhibited a decrease in the branched chain amino acids, asparagine, lysine, methionine, and glutamate, concomitant with an increase in lactate and pyruvate. AMP-specific alterations included a decrease in 2-oxoglutarate and hypoxanthine on the first day, along with an increase in glucose and creatinine on the fifth day. A decline in ketone bodies (3-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate, acetone) was observed in both groups. Postoperative WF demonstrates dynamic metabolic changes reflecting early wound healing processes and treatment-related effects. IOXRT has been found to be associated with enhanced glycolytic signatures and reduced amino acid levels, suggesting altered metabolic activity in the irradiated tumor bed. The WF metabolomic profile has the potential to offer a novel source of biomarkers, which could facilitate the evaluation of treatment response and tumor microenvironment characteristics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58204-8.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) represents the most common form of cancer among women worldwide, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases each year. Despite increased self-awareness and more effective methods for detecting and treating this cancer, the number of deaths from it remains high at nearly 700,000 per year1. It is estimated that one in eight women will develop breast cancer at some point in their lives1,2. Therefore, it is imperative to continue to look for increasingly effective methods to treat and prevent breast cancer.

Breast conservation therapy (BCT) represents the standard of care for patients with early-stage breast cancer. Initially used as a standalone intervention, surgical treatment was found to be associated with a considerable risk of local recurrence3.

Radiotherapy is a highly effective local treatment method, especially in the context of subclinical cancer foci in the tumor bed. Breast‑conserving therapy (BCT) is the standard of care for early‑stage BC; however, surgery alone carries a substantial risk of local recurrence. Surgical treatment was found to be associated with a considerable risk of local recurrence3. Postoperative radiotherapy significantly reduces the likelihood of both in situ and invasive recurrence4, particularly by targeting subclinical disease within the tumor bed and regional lymphatics. Intraoperative radiotherapy (IORT) delivers a single high dose of radiation directly to the tumor bed during surgery, minimizing geographic miss and preserving surrounding tissues. Direct access to the surgical site markedly decreases the probability of geographic error5. Low‑energy X‑ray IORT (IOXRT) offers additional advantages due to its steep dose dropoff and favourable toxicity profile. Randomized clinical trials, including TARGITA, have demonstrated high treatment efficacy, reduced toxicity, and improved cosmetic results6–8. Although higher rates of ipsilateral breast tumor recurrence (IBTR) were observed in the IORT arm, overall survival remained comparable between the treatment groups. Beyond its clinical utility, IORT induces complex biological effects within the postoperative tumor microenvironment. Residual cancer cells that remain within the surgical cavity are exposed to a high single dose of radiation, which disrupts DNA repair pathways, promotes apoptotic signaling, and reduces proliferative and metastatic potential. IORT also modulates inflammatory responses triggered by surgical trauma and can contribute to systemic immune activation consistent with the abscopal effect9–11. Despite these insights, the metabolic consequences of IOXRT remain unexplored. The document explicitly states that ‘postoperative fluid in BC has not been studied metabolically to date’, although the exudate composition is known to contain clinically relevant information15,16. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, particularly high‑resolution magic‑angle spinning (HR‑MAS), has proven effective in characterizing metabolic profiles of breast tissues, cell lines, and treatment responses17–20. HRMAS enables quantification of numerous metabolites using a nondestructive methodology and has been shown to differentiate malignant tissue from normal tissue, classify tumor subtypes, and detect treatment‑induced metabolic changes21–26.

Given the demonstrated ability of metabolomics to reflect tumor biology and predict therapeutic response, the metabolic profile of postoperative wound fluid represents a promising but unexplored avenue. Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the impact of IOXRT on selected molecular and metabolic markers in postoperative wound fluid from breast cancer patients, with the objective of identifying predictive metabolic signatures and elucidating radiation‑induced microenvironmental changes.

Population study

Study population

This prospective observational study was conducted at the National Institute of Oncology in Gliwice and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the National Institute of Oncology in Warsaw (Decision No. 18/2024). All participants gave their written informed consent. The study enrolled 54 women diagnosed with breast cancer: 38 treated with breast‑conserving therapy combined with intraoperative X‑ray radiotherapy (BCT‑IOXRT) and 16 undergoing mastectomy.

The IOXRT procedure followed the technique previously described by Wydmański et al.27.

Postoperative wound fluid (WF) was collected at two predefined time points:

A—the first postoperative day,

B—the fifth postoperative day.

Each patient contributed two samples (A and B), except for one individual in the BCT‑IOXRT group who underwent bilateral surgery and provided four samples. The time of collection corresponded to standard clinical follow‑up visits, minimizing the risk of sample loss. Processing of wound fluid samples

Immediately after collection, the WF samples were centrifuged for clarification. The supernatant was sequentially passed through the filters with decreasing pore size to remove cellular debris and particulate matter. The clarified fluid was aliquoted and stored at − 80°C until further analysis.

This workflow follows the general approach routinely applied in our metabolomics laboratory and previously reported by Boguszewicz et al.28.

Preparation of samples for NMR spectroscopy

Before analysis, frozen WF aliquots were gradually thawed (first at 4°C, then at room temperature). Each sample was mixed with phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) containing D₂O for field lock and TSP as a chemical shift reference. A final volume of 600 µL was transferred into 5‑mm NMR tubes and kept at 4°C until measurement.

The preparation strategy is consistent with established protocols used in our previous metabolomic studies17,18.

NMR measurement protocol

All spectra were acquired on a Bruker Avance III 400 MHz spectrometer equipped with a 5‑mm PABBI probe. The quality control tests were performed on every measurement day. The NMR probe tuning and matching, shimming, determination of the transmitter offset value for the water pulse presaturation, and 90° pulse adjustments were always made for each sample. The receiver gain was set to 90.5, and the temperature was set to 310 K for all experiments. Three different 1H NMR spectra were acquired: NOESY (1D Nuclear Overhauser Enhancement SpectroscopY), CPMG (Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill), and J-resolved (JRES), for each serum sample. The characteristics of the acquired spectra, as well as the pulse sequence parameters, are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1). These experiments provide complementary information on the metabolic composition of the samples and the relaxation properties of the individual metabolites. The measurement scheme was adapted from the widely used protocol of Beckonert et al.29 and from procedures optimized in our laboratory17,18.

Spectral processing, metabolite quantification and normalization

Spectra were processed using standard routines: exponential line broadening, automatic phase correction, and referencing to the formate peak at 8.47 ppm. Quantification was performed using positive projections of the JRES spectra.

Integration regions were defined individually for each metabolite, and the resulting values were normalized to the total spectral area (9.0–0.5 ppm), excluding the water resonance region.

This analytical pipeline reflects the standard workflow applied in our metabolomics facility and described by Boguszewicz et al.28.

Metabolite identification

Metabolites were identified by comparing spectral features with reference libraries (Chenomx NMR Suite), chemical shift databases (HMDB), and published literature. Multiplicity patterns and scalar couplings from 2D JRES spectra supported the assignments. In total, 114 NMR signals were quantified, of which 42 metabolites were confidently identified, several were annotated as probable, and a subset remained unassigned.

Statistical analysis

Multivariate statistical analyzes were performed using SIMCA (v.18). Results

To ensure high data quality and reproducibility, a rigorous daily Quality Control (QC) protocol was implemented before each measurement session. The workflow started with linewidth checks to ensure shim quality, followed by the calibration of the water presaturation pulse offset (O1) using a standard calibration sample, and finally the ‘suppcal’ procedure to optimize water suppression efficiency. These steps ensured that the NMR system remained stable and that water suppression was precisely centered for every run. Furthermore, all samples were analyzed in random order. This approach was taken to ensure that any potential minor instrumental fluctuations would not correlate with the study groups, thus preventing bias in subsequent classification. It should be noted that NMR is inherently a highly stable technique; therefore, beyond the daily QC protocol, additional QC runs (pooled samples) within the sequence were not required to maintain data consistency.

Evaluation of the variance distribution in the data

To initially explore the variance structure and identify dominant trends in the data, an unsupervised PCA was performed. The global PCA model revealed a clear separation between time points A and B across the entire study group (Fig. 3a). This differentiation between the two time points was further confirmed by submodels focusing separately on the BCT-IORT and AMP subgroups (Fig. 3b and c). The clear separation observed between time points A and B provided a solid basis for further biomarker identification using supervised methods. Although treatment-related differences were not the primary source of variance, supervised PLS-DA and OPLS-DA were subsequently applied to assess whether a class-discriminating signature could be identified. This transition to supervised modeling was performed with rigorous validation (permutation testing and CV-ANOVA) to account for the risk of overfitting and to ensure the biological relevance of any identified variables.

Class separation and identification of important variables

Supervised modeling was performed to identify metabolic differences between the study groups. A multiclass PLS-DA model was first applied to all four classes (time points A and B for both the BCT-IORT and AMP subgroups) to evaluate global group discrimination, as this method effectively handles more than two classes. This was followed by pairwise comparisons using OPLS-DA, which is specifically dedicated to binary problems. Although PLS-DA can also be used for two-class discrimination, OPLS-DA was preferred for its superior interpretability, as it isolates predictive from orthogonal variance, a separation that is not possible in multiclass PLS-DA models. In particular, the OPLS-DA models aimed at distinguishing BCT-IORT from AMP (at both time points A and B) did not meet the internal significance criteria; therefore, these results are not presented. The SUS plot (Fig. 5) summarizes all quantified NMR signals. For metabolites with multiple signals, all peaks were included in the model. Due to the varying size of the group and signal overlaps, some resonances showed different levels of statistical significance; for example, the valine signal at 2.28 ppm was identified as shared between BCT-IORT and AMP, while its other signals (1.00, 1.05, and 3.62 ppm) appeared to be unique to the BCT-IORT group (Fig. 5, Table 3, Table S2). Despite these variations in individual peak significance, the overall trend of change between time points A and B remained consistent between both subgroups. A general decrease in metabolite levels was observed from time point A to B, with the exception of methanol, lactate, pyruvate, glucose, creatinine, and xanthine/unk, which showed an increase (Fig. 5, Table 3, Table S2).

Discussion

In the present study, NMR-based metabolomic analysis of postoperative wound fluids collected from BC patients, with a focus on those treated with intraoperative radiation therapy.

In the 1H NMR spectra (110 samples), 91 signals from 42 metabolites were positively identified, 4 metabolites (5 NMR signals) were annotated as probable, 5 NMR signals were identified as originating from more than one metabolite, and 13 NMR signals remained unknown (Table 3, Table S2). Multivariate analyzes of the spectroscopic data were carried out using unsupervised PCA to evaluate the distribution of variance in the data and PLS-DA, and its orthogonal equivalent OPLS-DA for class discrimination.

1H NMR allows one to detect circulating blood metabolites and lipoproteins that not only reflect tumor metabolism but also provide a systemic picture of the balance between tumor and host metabolism. This is due to the fact that each patient with BC has a unique set of physiological and immunological conditions. As demonstrated by Derveaux et al., surgical removal of a tumor (lung cancer) causes significant metabolic changes in plasma, indicating that the serum metabolic profile is determined by the presence of the tumor rather than donor-specific characteristics30. The levels of several circulating amino acids and glycoproteins, as well as lipoproteins, have already been shown to undergo statistically significant changes in patients with BC. This suggests that disruption of energy homeostasis and amino acid metabolism may facilitate cancer growth and evolution31. Jobard et al.32 reported in a cohort of 791 cases of breast cancer and 791 matched controls that alterations in circulating plasma metabolites were observed even before breast cancer. However, the observed differences were only statistically significant in the premenopausal subgroup. In their previous study, Jobard et al. documented metabolic alterations associated with advanced metastatic breast cancer compared to early local disease32. Joshi et al. similarly demonstrated that serum NMR spectra can differentiate between early and metastatic breast cancer patients and predict disease recurrence33. Thus, it shows that NMR is a powerful tool for studying cancer metabolism.

The nature of the treatment in question has been shown to exert an influence on the metabolic response.

The findings of this study suggest substantial alterations in metabolic profiles between time points A and B in both the BCT-IOXRT and AMP groups. Although the systemic response to surgery and wound healing appears to be the primary sources of variability, analysis of the SUS plot revealed subtle, but distinct differences between the groups. Identifying metabolites that are indicative of the BCT-IORT or AMP subgroups suggests that the specific treatment modifies the metabolic course of recovery. This finding suggests that the healing process may not be universally applicable, but rather carries a biochemical signature that is specific to the treatment modality. These alterations underscore the dynamic changes in the tumor microenvironment following surgical intervention. The most plausible explanation for this phenomenon is the predominant influence of universal, early wound healing processes, which generate a robust shared metabolic signal. This signal may obscure more nuanced radiation-related effects. Furthermore, IOXRT is distinguished by a remarkably precipitous dose gradient, which means that potential metabolic alterations may be restricted to a reduced volume of tissue volume and diluted in the aggregate drainage fluid profile. This interpretation is also influenced by the smaller size of the AMP group and the biological heterogeneity associated with the extent of the procedure and the individual inflammatory response. It is emphasized that the observed differences between time points A and B likely reflect primarily the dynamics of healing, although directional changes consistent with the intensity of glycolysis are also visible in the IOXRT group. This finding suggests that the therapeutic effect may be present but subtle relative to the dominant regenerative signal.

In the BCT-IOXRT group, a significant decrease was observed in the branched chain amino acids (valine, isoleucine, and leucine), asparagine, methionine, and glutamate along with elevated levels of glucose, lactate, and pyruvate. The observed metabolic changes are characteristic of metabolic reprogramming, where decreased levels of essential amino acids such as valine, isoleucine, leucine, and methionine are associated with suppressed growth and induced apoptosis, while elevated glucose, pyruvate, and lactate are part of the Warburg effect, supporting the high biosynthetic and energy needs of cancer cells. Previous studies by Zalewska et al.12,13 and Kulcenty et al.14 demonstrated that the IORT-conditioned fluid reduces the population of stem-like cancer cells and activates apoptotic pathways. Branched-chain amino acids are imperative for protein synthesis and activation of signaling pathways such as mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin—key regulator of cell growth, metabolism, and stress response, acting as a serine-threonine kinase), which regulate cancer cell growth. The excessive use of these proteins by cancer cells has been shown to promote proliferation and resistance to treatment.

Xu et al.34 emphasize that branched chain amino acids (BCAAs) are key nutrients that support protein synthesis and regulate cell growth. Research shows that cancer cells use BCAAs in a specific way, as fuel and a regulator of metabolism, which promotes their aggressiveness and survival. BCAAs have been shown to play a dual role—as a building block and energy sources, influencing pathways that control metabolism and growth. They may also promote resistance to treatment. Understanding these mechanisms opens the possibility of developing new anticancer therapies targeting BCAA metabolism. Increased glucose, lactate, and pyruvate concentrations after IOXRT indicate activation of aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect), a hallmark of cancer cells. Lactate is not merely a by-product,it has a signaling function, exerts an influence on the tumor microenvironment, and can modulate the immune response. Al-Malsi et al.35 describe lactate as an epigenetic regulator through the induction of histone lactylation, which can influence the expression of genes associated with cancer progression.

The observed metabolic changes may also be indicative of immune system activation, consistent with the concept of the abscopal effect described by Jeibouei et al.10. Increased levels of lactate and pyruvate have been observed to be associated with the recruitment of immune cells and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. The literature supports that intraoperative radiotherapy (IORT) modulates microRNA (miRNA) expression, such as miR-21 and miR-155, which influences immune responses by altering tumor-supportive healing and reshaping local immune signaling13.

Decreased levels of glutamate and methionine may indicate reduced biosynthesis activity and altered redox homeostasis. Glutamate is essential for cancer cell proliferation and immune cell function, while methionine is involved in DNA methylation and regulation of gene expression13.

According to Liu et al.36, disturbances in glutamate and methionine metabolism are characteristic of various breast cancer subtypes and may represent a therapeutic target.

The decreases in ketone bodies, such as 3-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate, can indicate changes in energy metabolism and oxidative stress. Their levels correlate with the patient’s response to chemotherapy and nutritional status, where their NMR levels serve as a measure of the patient’s nutritional status and can indicate the body’s response to starvation18. In the context of breast surgery, patients are required to fast for a period of 24 h. However, more research is needed to determine whether this relatively brief fasting period will be reflected in postoperative wound fluid.

A decrease in creatine and an increase in creatinine can indicate altered phosphagen metabolism and cellular stress. Creatine acts as an energy buffer, and disturbances in creatine levels are observed in many cancers, including breast cancer. An et al.37 demonstrated through a proteomic-metabolomic analysis that creatinine and glutamate are among 47 metabolites that have a high diagnostic value for breast cancer.

In the review by Vignoli et al.26, various applications of NMR-based metabolomics are collected, demonstrating that this approach is optimal for BC cancer research, in particular for prognostic and stratification purposes. This review focuses on the biochemical characterization and stratification of breast cancer patients using different biospecies: breast tissue, blood serum/plasma, and urine. NMR-based metabolomic studies in serum blood samples are worth mentioning. Jobard et al.32 and Joshi et al.33 found that specific blood metabolites differ between early-stage and metastatic breast cancer; later, Hart et al.38 reported that preoperative serum metabolomic profiles can be prognostic for disease recurrence and long-term risk of BC. Breast cancer and healthy subjects were also shown to be discriminated by NMR39. Finally, Jobard et al.32 showed that nontargeted plasma NMR metabolomics can predict the risk of breast cancer in premenopausal women. However, in the reviewed review mentioned, BC surgical wound fluids were not analyzed, therefore, the current effort is the first to examine the metabolic profiles of WF. Metabolic information is particularly important because these fluids may reflect the inflammation and aggressiveness of breast cancers40.

Our study also confirms the observations by Wuhrer et al.41 that the postoperative fluids from the patients treated with intraoperative radiotherapy may have a different composition than those from patients who did not receive IORT41. In this study, we demonstrated that the metabolomic profile of postoperative wound fluid provides valuable information on local healing processes and tissue responses to IOXRT. We also demonstrated that the metabolomic profile can be considered as a model for a local liquid biopsy. However, references to multi-omic approaches pertain solely to future research directions and do not apply to the analyses performed in this project. A detailed analysis of the results seems to ensure that omics integration is a deliberate extension that materializes as a consequence of multilevel regulation of inflammatory processes, tissue remodeling, and radiation response.

In the future, wound fluid may undergo an integrated analysis that includes proteomics (cytokine and ECM protein profiling), transcriptomics, and small RNAs present in extracellular vesicles, as well as metabolomics, which forms the basis of the current study. Integrative multiomics models, such as network analysis and data fusion, may in the future enable the identification of consistent molecular signatures reflecting the dynamics of healing and the impact of IOXRT on the wound microenvironment.

Limitations and future directions

The authors of the study are aware of a number of limitations of this study, but due to its pilot nature and the innovative research approach, it was decided to publish preliminary conclusions. First, the limited sample size of the study group (particularly patients with AMP) can compromise the statistical power and generalizability of the results. OPLS-DA models that compared the BCT-IOXRT and AMP groups did not meet the statistical significance criteria, which limits the possibility of drawing conclusions about the impact of the type of treatment on the metabolome in the intergroup comparison. Due to the recent nature of the study, long-term clinical outcomes (eg, recurrence, survival) were not included, making it difficult to assess whether metabolomic changes have real prognostic significance. This study focuses exclusively on metabolomics, a field that limits a full understanding of biological mechanisms. This preliminary analysis did not include information on diet, medications, or the metabolic status of the patients, which can affect the composition of postoperative fluid. In light of these limitations, the study’s subsequent phase will involve an increase in the number of patients, along with the potential for conducting an analysis on a control group (e.g., patients who have undergone surgery for benign breast lesions). In this type of research, it is also necessary to employ other methods that will allow for the verification of the results and the independent confirmation of the metabolic signatures observed in WF.

This approach aims to enhance the study’s understanding of the impact of cancer on fluid composition. The objective of this study is to integrate metabolomics with proteomics, transcriptomics, and microRNA analysis. The incorporation of long-term clinical data at this stage is particularly challenging, as the 10-year survival rate for early-stage breast cancer is 96%. However, an effort will be made to establish a correlation between metabolomic alterations and clinical outcomes, with the objective of evaluating their prognostic significance. In the subsequent phases of the study, standardization and control of confounders (data on diet, medications, metabolic status, and lifestyle) will be incorporated.

Conclusion

The objective of the present study was to perform a metabolomic analysis of postoperative fluids in patients with breast cancer who underwent breast preservation surgery with X-rays intraoperative radiation therapy and mastectomy. 1H NMR spectroscopy was used to identify and quantify metabolites in the spectra. The results of PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA demonstrated clear disparities between the samples collected on the first and fifth days after surgery in both groups. However, in general, no significant differences were observed between the BCT-IOXRT and AMP groups. In the BCT-IOXRT group, a decrease in amino acids such as valine, isoleucine, asparagine, and lysine was observed, along with an increase in glycolysis-related metabolites, including lactate and pyruvate. In the AMP group, there were characteristic changes in 2-oxoglutarate and hypoxanthine levels and an increase in glucose and creatinine at a later time. A common trend observed in both groups was a decrease in the signals from ketone bodies—3-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate, and acetone—combined with a decrease in methionine. The results indicate that postoperative fluids exhibit significant biological properties and differences in metabolite profiles depending on the type of treatment, and that IOXRT affects the metabolism of cancer cells by intensifying glycolytic processes and apoptosis.

Metabolomic analysis of postoperative fluids has the potential to serve as a prognostic tool, providing information on response to treatment and facilitating a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamic interactions between the tumor microenvironment and the therapeutic modalities used.
