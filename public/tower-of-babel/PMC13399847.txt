Posterior Arthroscopic Talar Body Nonunion Repair With Subtalar Arthrodesis
Authors: Kyle S. Ardavanis, Nicholas K. Pappa, Tobin T. Eckel, Kevin D. Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTalus fractures are a rare and challenging problem for the orthopaedic surgeon. They often present following high‐speed injury mechanisms in patients with polytraumatic injuries. Diagnosis and treatment of these fractures may be delayed, at times leading to talus nonunion or malunion. In cases of talar body nonunion with intra‐articular involvement, but with preserved talar morphology, nonunion repair may be indicated. Historically, this has been addressed with open repair, necessitating medial malleolus osteotomy for fracture exposure, debridement, reduction, and fixation. In this technique article, we describe a minimally invasive arthroscopic technique for this challenging problem. We describe posterior arthroscopic talar body nonunion repair with subtalar arthrodesis.

Talus fractures are a rare and challenging problem for the orthopaedic surgeon and have been estimated to represent approximately 1% to 4.5% of all foot fractures. 1 , 2

Talus fractures are frequently associated with high‐energy trauma and present in patients with multiple orthopaedic injuries. This often leads to delayed diagnosis or treatment of these injuries. In a patient with multiple orthopaedic injuries, nonoperative management of minimally displaced talus fractures may be elected to reduce operative morbidity. A portion of these injuries will go on to nonunion or malunion. The management of talar nonunions and malunions is well described in orthopaedic literature. 3 , 4 Due to the tenuous blood supply of the talus, talar fractures may lead to talar body avascular necrosis and collapse or flattening of the talus. In these severe cases, tibiotalocalcaneal arthrodesis is often performed with the use of a space‐filling allograft or a titanium cage construct to restore limb length in the setting of a shortened talus. 5 , 6

In some cases of talar nonunion or malunion, however, a patient may have well‐preserved cartilage, minimal avascular necrosis, and no talar collapse. In these patients, the decision may be made to attempt talar nonunion or malunion repair. In the setting of talar neck fractures, this is often performed through a dual approach, and manipulation of the fracture and use of structural allograft is often necessary to correct the deformity. 3

In cases of talar body nonunion repair, medial malleolus osteotomy is often necessary to access the nonunion site, appropriately debride and place bone graft, and reduce the fracture. 7 This is a complex open approach with significant potential morbidity.

In this article, we describe an alternative arthroscopic technique for the management of rare talar body nonunions, with concurrent arthroscopic subtalar arthrodesis.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Preoperative planning is necessary to appropriately indicate patients for the procedure. Contraindications to talar body nonunion repair include extensive talar avascular necrosis, active infection, and talar collapse. Relative contraindications to arthroscopic technique include significant deformity or malunion that would be difficult to manipulate and reduce through this minimally invasive technique.

Standard radiographic imaging of the foot should be performed, in addition to advanced imaging with either computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging to evaluate the extent of talar avascular necrosis or collapse (Figure 1).

Positioning and Prepping

General anesthesia is induced and the patient is positioned prone on a radiolucent operative table. Care is taken to ensure the bilateral feet hang off the end of the bed to allow for intraoperative ankle dorsi‐ and plantar flexion as necessary. A nonsterile tourniquet is applied proximally on the patient's thigh. The patient is then prepped and draped in the standard sterile fashion (Video 1).

Diagnostic Arthroscopy

An Esmark bandage is used to exsanguinate the limb and the tourniquet is inflated. Posterior ankle diagnostic arthroscopy is initiated by first creating a posterolateral portal with a nick and spread technique. This portal is created just lateral to the Achilles tendon and in line with the inferior aspect of the medial malleolus from superior to inferior. A 1.9 mm arthroscope (NanoScope; Arthrex, Naples, FL) is then inserted for visualization. This smaller diameter arthroscope enables access to the small space of the subtalar joint. Under direct visualization, the posteromedial portal is then created just medial to the Achilles tendon, also in line with the inferior aspect of the medial malleolus. An arthroscopic shaver is then inserted into the posteromedial portal, and initial debridement is performed, taking care to identify the flexor hallucis longus tendon and remain lateral to the tendon throughout arthroscopy to avoid injury to the neurovascular bundle. The subtalar joint is accessed. Mini or standard C‐arm fluoroscopy can be utilized to confirm instrumentation of the subtalar joint (Figure 2).

Intraoperative lateral radiographic imaging of the right ankle confirms posterior arthroscopic instrumentation of the subtalar joint. The arrow is pointing to the tip of the arthroscope, which has been initially inserted in the posterolateral viewing portal.

Talar Body Nonunion and Subtalar Joint Preparation

The talar body nonunion and the subtalar joint are then prepared for repair and arthrodesis, respectively. In cases of talar nonunion, the subtalar joint is typically degenerative, and posterior arthroscopic subtalar arthrodesis is performed concurrently. An arthroscopic shaver is used to remove all loose bony and cartilaginous debris, along with any remaining subtalar joint articular cartilage. The nonunion site can be accessed and manipulated with a microfracture awl (Figure 3).

With the patient in the prone position, this image depicts arthroscopy of the right subtalar joint. The arthroscope is in the posteromedial portal, and an arthroscopic awl has been inserted into the posterolateral portal. The awl is being utilized to manipulate and clean out a talar body nonunion. The arrows point to the edges of the fracture fragments as viewed from the subtalar joint.

A microfracture awl is used to extensively fenestrate the nonunion site, as well as the inferior surface of the talus and the superior surface of the calcaneus for arthrodesis. An osteotome is used in a fish‐scaling technique for further joint preparation.

Bone Graft Harvest

Attention is then turned to the harvesting of autograft bone. Through a small incision on the lateral aspect of the calcaneus, a 6 mm core reamer (OsteoAuger; Arthrex, Naples, FL) is utilized to harvest calcaneal autograft. The graft is thoroughly dried and morselized. This autograft is then combined with an allograft and platelet‐rich plasma. The mixture is then loaded into a bone delivery syringe (CONMED, Utica, NY).

Graft Placement

Under direct visualization, the prepared graft mixture is injected and packed into the nonunion and arthrodesis sites as shown in the attached surgical technique video. Calcaneal autograft can also be placed first, followed by injection of allograft/platelet‐rich plasma slurry.

Talus Nonunion and Subtalar Fixation

Following graft placement, under combined direct arthroscopic visualization and fluoroscopic guidance, 2 guide wires are placed perpendicularly to the talar nonunion site and overdrilled to an appropriate depth. Finally, two 3.5 mm diameter cannulated titanium headless compression screws (Compression FT; Arthrex, Naples, FL) are placed for talar fixation (Figure 4). It is imperative to place these screws initially through the provided soft tissue protection drill guide, so as not to bind critical structures.

(B) With the patient in the prone position and the arthroscope inserted into the posteromedial portal of this right ankle, a headless compression screw is placed over the guidewire into the talus. (C) The same screw countersunk to the talar posterior cortex. (D) Fluoroscopy confirms appropriate talar screw placement and length. An arrow points to the fracture line.

Following talar fixation, fixation of the subtalar joint is performed. Three cannulated titanium headless compression screws (Compression FT; Arthrex, Naples, FL) are placed for subtalar joint arthrodesis. The first screw is inserted along the medial wall of the calcaneus, terminating in the talar dome medially. The second screw is placed inside the lateral cortex of the calcaneus, terminating at the lateral junction of the talar head and neck. Finally, the third screw is placed from the plantar anterior calcaneal process into the talar head/neck junction. Fluoroscopy is used to confirm screw position and length, and the screws are sequentially tightened to provide distributed compression across the joint (Figure 5).

(A) Lateral and (B) AP intraoperative radiographic imagings of this right ankle show a final talar body nonunion and subtalar joint fixation construct. The arrow in image (A) points to 2 headless compression screws which are placed orthogonal to the coronal plane talar body fracture. (AP, anterior‐posterior.)

The incisions are closed with nylon sutures, and local anesthetic is injected at all incision sites. A dressing of petroleum gauze, gauze, compressive wrap, and a postoperative boot is placed.

POSTOPERATIVE PLAN

The patient is kept nonweight‐bearing in a walking boot for eight weeks but may initiate range of motion exercises under the guidance of physical therapy following suture removal at 10 to 14 days postoperatively. Eighty‐one milligrams of aspirin is prescribed to be taken twice daily for deep venous thrombosis prophylaxis. Vitamin D supplementation is recommended for 6 months postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

In this article, we describe posterior arthroscopic talar body nonunion repair with subtalar arthrodesis. In the appropriate patient, we believe this technique presents an attractive alternative to traditional open talar body nonunion repair.

Technical Pearls and Pitfalls for Posterior Arthroscopic Talar Body Nonunion Repair With Subtalar Arthrodesis

Posterior arthroscopic subtalar arthrodesis has been described in the literature with favorable outcomes compared to open subtalar arthrodesis. 8 , 9 Additional studies have further advanced posterior ankle arthroscopic technique to include the management of calcaneus fractures, with concurrent subtalar arthrodesis. 10 , 11 The technique detailed in this study further broadens the application of posterior arthroscopic technique to include the management of talar body nonunion.

One theorized benefit of concurrent subtalar arthrodesis with talar nonunion repair is improved blood supply to the devascularized talus through creeping angiogenesis from the well vascularized calcaneus, preventing worsening of talar body avascular necrosis and aiding in healing of the nonunion.

In conclusion, we recommend consideration of posterior arthroscopic talar body nonunion repair with subtalar arthrodesis as an alternative to the open technique.
