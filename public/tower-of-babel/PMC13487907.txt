Variability in HIV viral load quantification in real-world service delivery settings: findings from an observational cohort study in Rwanda
Authors: Gad Murenzi, Ellen Brazier, Marie Gertrude Rutwaza, Jean Paul Mivumbi, Ryan Barthel, Jocelyne Ingabire, Faustin Kanyabwisha, Marcel Yotebieng, Denis Nash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile developments in HIV viral load (VL) testing technologies have improved detection of low-level viremia, there is substantial variation in the quantitative results of available polymerase chain reaction tests, particularly around assay lower limits of quantification (LLOQ). We aimed to describe testing results for paired specimens from the same participants reported by two laboratories in Rwanda using different assays, characterize discordancy in VL quantification, and identify factors associated with quantifiable VL results at a threshold of 40 copies/mL.MethodsIn an observational cohort study of people living with HIV (PWH), aged ≥ 40 years enrolled in HIV care, we used two laboratories to process paired research specimens. We used Kappa statistics and plots to examine between-lab agreement at the LLOQs for assays used by the two labs (Lab A: 20 copies/mL for COBAS AmpliPrep/Taqman and Abbott Alinity-m HIV1 assays; Lab B: 40 copies/mL for Abbott RealTime assay and at thresholds of 200, 1000 and 2000 copies/mL. We used Poisson regression to examine participant characteristics independently associated with unsuppressed viral loads (≥ 200 copies/mL).Results593 results were reported by both laboratories for 572 unique participants. The mean age of study participants was 54.4 years, and 58% were female. Median time on antiretroviral therapy was 16 years, 93.7% were on dolutegravir-based regimens, and 96.9% had CD4 cell counts of > 200 cells/mm3. For Lab A, 29.2% of specimens had quantifiable VLs at assay LLOQs of 20 copies/mL and 22.1% had quantifiable VLs at a common threshold of 40 copies/mL, compared with 4.7% of specimens processed by Lab B (LLOQ 40 copies/mL). Kappa statistics showed poor to moderate between-lab agreement below a threshold of 200 copies/mL, with high agreement at thresholds of 1000 and 2000 copies/mL and differences by assay type. CD4 cell counts < 200 cells/mm3 were associated with unsuppressed VLs reported by each laboratory.ConclusionsWhile VL quantification differed substantially between assays and laboratories used in a real-world service delivery setting, there was high agreement at cut-offs generally used in clinical decision-making. Our findings support the use of higher viral suppression cut-offs used in UNAIDS’ 95-95-95 targets because lower thresholds are vulnerable to misclassification.

Background

Achieving and maintaining undetectable viral loads (VL) has increasingly been the ultimate goal of HIV programs since the adoption of the World Health Organization’s (WHO) 2015 recommendations for universal treatment of all people living with HIV (PWH) [1] and the launch of the “Undetectable equals Untransmittable” (U = U) consensus statement and community-led campaign [2, 3].

While U = U remains an important call for increasing access to HIV treatment and a vital message for reducing HIV-related stigma and onward transmission, HIV VL testing technology remains highly variable in the detection and quantification of VLs. Variation in the quantification of VLs has been observed across all laboratory-based polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays using both plasma specimens and dried blood spots (DBS) [4]. This variation is particularly pronounced around an assay’s lower limits of quantification (LLOQ) [5–10]. In a study of assay performance and variability using WHO HIV reference samples with estimated VLs of 36 copies/mL, White et al. [6] found that the TaqMan v2.0 HIV-1 assay (LLOQ: 20 copies/mL) quantified VLs > 50 copies/mL in 66% of reference samples. Variation in VL quantification is also observed at higher VLs, and a specimen of 1,000 copies/mL has an expected range of 500–2000 copies/mL [11].

While variability in VL quantification is known and well-documented in the quality assurance literature, the implications of this variation for clinical care and real-world observational research focused on VL undetectability are not well documented. Accordingly, in an ongoing observational cohort study in Rwanda, we used paired plasma specimens to examine and describe the variability in VL testing results among study participants enrolled in routine HIV care clinics. Specifically, we aimed (1) to characterize discordancy in VL quantification at various thresholds for the assay used by each laboratory and (2) to examine participant characteristics associated with VLs quantified at or above 40 copies/mL by each laboratory.

Methods

Study population

The study population included 572 participants enrolled in the Rwanda cohort of the International epidemiology Databases to Evaluate AIDS Sentinel Research Network (IeDEA SRN). Aimed at advancing understanding of long-term outcomes among aging PWH, the IeDEA SRN is a prospective observational study of PWH aged ≥ 40 years receiving HIV care in routine service delivery in 11 low- and middle-income countries [12, 13]. The Rwanda IeDEA SRN cohort is being followed over five years, with VL testing and data collection on cardiovascular risk factors, liver function, mental health, alcohol and other substance use collected during annual study visits. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the IeDEA SRN study protocol was approved by the Rwanda National Ethics Committee (224/RNEC/2020), the University Integrated Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the City University of New York (#2018-0809-PHHP) and the Albert Einstein College of Medicine (#2020–11247). All participants provided written informed consent to voluntarily participate in the study.

Data collection and measures

From April 13, 2023 to April 9, 2024, two 4 ml blood specimens were collected for HIV VL testing during annual study visits for 572 participants, including 21 participants who attended study visits twice during the specimen collection period. The paired specimens were processed by two separate laboratories that quantified HIV viral load. One laboratory (Lab A) used either COBAS AmpliPrep/Taqman or Abbott Alinity m HIV1 assays (LLOQ: 20 copies/mL) for quantifying VLs, alternating between assays during the study period, whereas the second laboratory (Lab B) used Abbott RealTime PCR version 8 (LLOQ: 40 copies/mL) for all specimens. All specimens were sent to the laboratories on the day of collection. Lab A centrifuged blood tubes on the day of receipt using sterile tubes, aliquoted the plasma and shipped specimens on dry ice to Nairobi, Kenya for testing within 24 h of collection. Lab B, which processed specimens in batches, centrifuged blood tubes using sterile tubes and aliquoted plasma on the day of collection, and then stored plasma at either 2–8 °C for testing within two days or at -80 °C for two to three weeks prior to batch processing and testing in Rwanda.

We matched VL test results to study participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics extracted from the site electronic health records (EHR), including sex (male or female); age (in years), categorized as 40–49, 50–59 and 60 + years at the time of VL testing; time on antiretroviral therapy (ART) at the time of VL testing (in years); and anchor drug of the current ART regimen (dolutegravir or other integrase inhibitor [DTG/INSTI], protease inhibitor [PI] or non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors [NNRTI]); time on current regimen at the time of VL testing (in years). CD4 count testing at the time of plasma specimen collection (categorized as ≤ 200 cells/mm3 or > 200 cells/mm3) was performed by Lab A.

For our paired research specimens, our primary outcome of interest was HIV VL quantification by each laboratory at assay-specific LLOQs and at a uniform quantification cut-off of 40 copies/mL. Secondary outcomes of interest included viral load suppression (VLS) at thresholds of 200 copies/mL and 1,000 copies/mL and between-lab agreement at each LLOQ and thresholds of 200, 1000, and 2000 copies/mL.

Statistical analyses

We used descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, medians and interquartile ranges [IQR]) to summarize participant characteristics and VL results reported by each laboratory, including the percentages with quantifiable VLs at assay-specific cut-offs and at < 40 copies/mL, and we characterized the proportion virally suppressed at thresholds of 200 and 1000 copies/mL. For VL test results quantified by either laboratory, we calculated the proportion with a between-lab difference ≥ 0.5 log10 copies/mL in viral loads—i.e., the proportion outside the range of expected between-assay variability in VL quantification [14]. To examine between-lab agreement at various thresholds (assay LLOQs, 40, 200, 1000, and 2000 copies/mL), overall, and stratified by assays used by Lab A (AmpliPrep/Taqman and Alinity m HIV-1), we used Kappa statistics, considering values < 0.40 as poor between-lab agreement; 0.41– 0.60 as moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80 as substantial, and > 0.80 as almost perfect agreement [15]. We used Poisson regression with robust error variances [16] to identify participant characteristics independently associated with having a VL ≥ 40 copies/mL for each laboratory, with adjustment for clustering of observations within patients.

For paired specimens with quantifiable VLs reported by both laboratories, we plotted paired VL values (in log10 copies/mL) against each other for each type of paired assay (AmpliPrep/Taqman vs. RealTime and Alinity m HIV-1 vs. RealTime) and used Bland-Altman plots [17] to examine bias and limits of agreement for paired VL values across the full range of observed values. Finally, to examine temporal trends over the 13-month study period, we plotted the proportion of VL results at or above LLOQ, by assay type and by specimen collection date.

Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Statistical significance was determined by p < .05.

Results

Our sample comprised VL test results for 593 paired specimens processed by Labs A and B for 572 unique participants (Table 1). Just over half (57.9%) of specimens were collected from female participants and the mean participant age at the time of specimen collection was 54.3 years (standard deviation 7.1). The median time on ART at the time of specimen collection was 16.3 years (IQR: 12.0, 18.4). The majority (93.7%) were on DTG/INSTI-based regimens, with the median time on the current regimen at the time of specimen collection being 3.9 years (IQR: 2.7, 4.3). Few specimens were for participants (3.1%) who were severely immunosuppressed (i.e., CD4 cell counts ≤ 200 cells/mm3) at the time of the VL specimen collection.

VL test results for the 593 paired research specimens are shown in Table 2. For Lab A, 29.2% of the specimens had quantifiable VLs at assay LLOQs (20 copies/mL), whereas Lab B reported only 4.7% of specimens had quantifiable VLs (LLOQ 40 copies/mL). Using a uniform threshold for VL detection of 40 copies/mL, 22.1% of the results from Lab A were categorized as quantifiable. Among quantified VLs, approximately two-thirds had between-lab differences outside the range of acceptable inter-assay variability (i.e., ≥ 0.5 log10 copies/mL), ranging from 65.9% of results reported by Lab A (62.3% of specimens processed with AmpliPrep/TaqMan and 68.3% of specimens processed with Alinity m HIV1) to 60.7% of results processed by Lab B (RealTime).

At assay-specific LLOQs, quantifiable VLs ranged from 20 to 2,248,760 copies/mL for Lab A (n = 173; median: 91.5; IQR: 42–270) and 43.7 to 660,693 copies/mL for Lab B (n = 28; median: 270.3; IQR: 61.1, 2290.9). For 173 research specimens with VLs quantified by Lab A, only 25 (14.5%) had quantifiable VLs reported by Lab B.

Using a VLS threshold of 200 copies/mL, 91.7% of results from Lab A were suppressed, along with 97.5% of results for Lab B. For a VLS threshold of 1000 copies/mL, there was little difference between laboratories (96.1% for Lab A and 98.5% for Lab B).

Table 3 shows the proportion of discordant results and Kappa statistics for various quantification and viral suppression thresholds for the 593 research specimens. Among paired research specimens processed by both laboratories, 25.5% had discordant quantification results at assay-specific LLOQs, with Kappa statistics (0.21, 95% CI: 0.13, 0.28) indicating poor overall agreement. Results were similar at a uniform quantification threshold of 40 copies/mL (Kappa: 0.25; 95% CI: 0.16, 0.35). Between-lab agreement increased monotonically at higher viral load thresholds, ranging from Kappa 0.45 (95% CI: 0.30, 0.60) for 200 copies/mL to 0.58 (95% CI: 0.36, 0.81) for 2000 copies/mL. In analyses stratified by assay type, between-lab agreement at thresholds of 1000 and 2000 copies/mL was significantly higher for specimens processed with the AmpliPrep/Taqman assay than for specimens processed with the Alinity m HIV-1 assay (Fig. 1).

Participant characteristics associated with VLs (≥ 40 copies/mL) for each laboratory are shown in Table 4. For Lab A, participant factors independently associated with VL ≥ 40 copies/mL included: CD4 count ≤ 200 cells/mm3 (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR]: 3.18; 95% CI: 2.21, 4.63); age ≥ 60 years (reference 40–49 years): aPR: 1.65; 95% CI: 1.08, 2.52); and being on a PI-based regimen (reference DTG or other INSTI-based regimen): aPR: 2.10; 95% CI: 1.28, 3.44). Discussion

Among a cohort of adult PWH who were established on ART in a real-world HIV care delivery setting, we found considerable variation in VL test results for paired plasma specimens processed by two independent laboratories, especially at assay LLOQs and VL thresholds of 200 copies/mL. One-quarter of paired test results had discordant results for VL quantification, with more than a five-fold difference between the two laboratories in the proportion of specimens with VLs at or above assay-specific LLOQs. While these differences might be partially explained by different LLOQs for the assay platforms used by each laboratory, we observed similar between-lab disagreement when using a uniform cut-off of 40 copies/mL for quantification. Additionally, while both COBAS AmpliPrep/Taqman and Abbott Alinity m HIV-1 assays are known to be more sensitive in identifying low-level viremia than Abbott RealTime and to yield higher HIV viral load results [18–20], we also observed between-laboratory discordance for specimens well above assay-specific LLOQs, as well as marked differences in between-laboratory discordance, depending on the assay used. For viral load results quantified by both laboratories, one-third had differences > 0.5 log10 copies/mL—i.e., outside the range of expected between-assay variability in VL quantification [14]—ranging from 25% of results processed with COBAS AmpliPrep/Taqman to 38% of results processed with Abbott Alinity m HIV-1. For each assay used, we saw no significant temporal trends in the proportion of quantifiable VLs.

The high proportion of paired specimens with discordant VL quantification results from two laboratories is consistent with prior studies showing substantial variation in the quantification of HIV VLs in plasma specimens—particularly around lower limits of quantification but extending across higher VL values [4–11, 21]. Our findings underscore the challenges of longitudinal monitoring of virologic status, as well as cross-study comparisons, when multiple assay platforms are used for VL quantification [22]. Additionally, while U = U remains as a compelling clarion call for treatment access and HIV stigma reduction, our findings underscore the need for caution in relying on VL detection as either a clinical or programmatic endpoint because inherent variability of VL quantification around limits of detection may mean VL detectability is particularly vulnerable to misclassification.

While highlighting variation in VL quantification across laboratories and assay platforms, at our common threshold for VL quantification (i.e. 40 copies/mL) we observed some similarities in participant characteristics associated with quantifiable VLs. For both laboratories, we observed similar strong positive associations between VL ≥ 40 copies/mL and participant immunosuppression (i.e., CD4 cell counts ≤ 200 cells/mm3). Although our estimates were imprecise for Lab B because of small numbers of quantifiable VLs, we also observed similar positive associations across laboratories between VL ≥ 40 copies/mL and being on a PI-based regimen. For other characteristics, such as older age and longer duration of time on current regimen results across laboratories were not consistent. In view of expected variation in VL quantification around assay LLOQs, higher VL thresholds, such as 200 copies/mL, as specified in Rwanda’s current treatment guidelines [23], might be equally informative and less subject to misclassification error, thereby ensuring that resources for intensified adherence support and repeat VL monitoring are appropriately targeted to those most at risk of poor HIV care outcomes.

Our study has several important limitations. First, our study was observational and conducted in a real-world service delivery setting as part of a broader study focused on aging PWH in low- and middle-income countries. Prior research suggests that the performance and sensitivity of HIV VL assays can be affected by factors related to the preparation, storage, and processing of specimens [24–26], and it is possible that there were between-laboratory differences in the storage and processing of specimens that are not reflected in our data—namely that procedures to ensure the integrity of specimens differed between the two laboratories or that recommended timeframes for plasma separation or the storage/freezing of specimens [21] were not consistently met. This concern is of particular relevance for results reported by Lab B, which stored specimens and processed them in batches and for which the processing and storage conditions could vary. However, recent studies in South Africa [21, 27] have found that VL remains stable in plasma preparation tubes stored at room temperature for seven days, and that variability in VL quantification near limits of detection was not affected by delays in specimen testing. Given the lack of any temporal trends observed in the proportion of quantifiable VLs reported by Lab B, the small number of quantifiable VLs seems unlikely to be due to differences in specimen transport and storage.

It is also possible that higher reporting of quantifiable VLs by Lab A reflect instances of specimen contamination at the point of processing. Plotting the proportion of VLs at or above assay-specific LLOQs over time highlighted several days when 100% of results reported by Lab A were at/above LLOQ thresholds of 20 copies/mL, including one day with 100% of results also reported at/above the LLOQ threshold of 40 copies/mL by Lab B as well, which could suggest specimen contamination at the point of collection on at least one occasion.

A further limitation is that Lab A alternated between two assay types throughout the study period, depending on available supplies, which may have obscured temporal patterns in between-laboratory variability. We were also unable to distinguish “undetectable” results from those that were detectable below assay LLOQs in our data. While we assumed that all results below assay LLOQs were undetectable, but there may be important between-assay differences in viral detection vs. quantification that we were unable to describe. An additional limitation is the difficulty of distinguishing variation in VL quantification from viral blips—namely transient low-level viremia above assay detection limits in individuals with previously undetectable VLs [28–31]. Research on viral blips is mixed, with some studies suggesting that viral blips are predictive of subsequent virologic failure and others reporting otherwise [28, 30, 32, 33]. Past studies underscore the fact that while detectable VLs may be a sign of virologic failure, they may also be spurious “false positives” that reflect either VL blips or assay variability. As protocols for our prospective cohort study did not include additional VL testing for those with low-level viremia, we could not examine potential viral blips.

These limitations notwithstanding, strengths of our study include our robust sample of paired VL test results over a 13-month period. Reflecting real-world data from a service delivery setting and paired specimens processed by two laboratories, our findings related to the variability of VL quantification have broad relevance for both policy and observational research related to HIV care outcomes.

Conclusions

While the prevalence of VLs at/above low LLOQs differed substantially across study laboratories, there was high agreement at the threshold of 1000 copies/mL that is generally used in clinical decision-making and program level metrics (e.g. 95-95-95). Our findings underscore the need for caution in relying solely on VL detection or quantification according to assay LLOQs as a clinical or programmatic endpoint, as it is vulnerable to misclassification for available assay platforms and a single detectable VL result may simply be a “false positive” that does not merit intensified adherence support or VL monitoring. This in turn underscores the importance of repeat viral load testing according to national guidelines, regardless of the result of the prior VL value.
