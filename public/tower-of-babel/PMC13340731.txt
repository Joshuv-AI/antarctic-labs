Large axillary galactocele mimicking a soft-tissue tumor in accessory breast tissue: a rare clinical entity

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Accessory breast tissue is a developmental anomaly that may be subject to the same hormonal influences as normal breast tissue. A galactocele, a benign milk-retention cyst, typically affects lactating women but is exceedingly rare in axillary accessory breast tissue. Because an axillary galactocele can mimic lymphadenopathy, lipoma, or malignancy, it often poses a diagnostic challenge.Case presentation:A 33-year-old multiparous lactating woman presented with a progressively enlarging right axillary swelling that had become mildly painful over time. Examination revealed a large, well-defined cystic mass in the right axilla, with normal bilateral breasts and contralateral axilla. Ultrasound demonstrated a cystic lesion with internal echoes suggestive of a galactocele. Fine-needle aspiration produced thick, milk-like fluid, confirming the diagnosis. Surgical excision of the cyst and associated accessory breast tissue was performed due to its size, symptoms, and the patient’s preference. Recovery was uneventful, breastfeeding was preserved, and no recurrence was observed.Clinical discussion:A galactocele arising within axillary accessory breast tissue is exceptionally rare and may be misdiagnosed as lymphadenopathy, lipoma, abscess, or malignancy. Targeted imaging and aspiration are key to distinguishing it from other axillary pathologies. While conservative management may suffice in small or asymptomatic cases, surgical excision provides definitive treatment and prevents recurrence in large, symptomatic, or cosmetically concerning lesions.Conclusion:Axillary accessory breast tissue galactocele is an uncommon but important differential diagnosis in lactating women presenting with axillary masses. Prompt recognition through clinical assessment, ultrasound, and aspiration facilitates accurate diagnosis. Surgical excision remains an effective management option for symptomatic cases, ensuring excellent outcomes.

Introduction

The axilla is the most common site of accessory (ectopic) breast tissue, which occurs when the embryonic mammary ridge does not fully involute. The accessory breast tissue responds physiologically to hormonal changes and lactation and, therefore, can develop benign or malignant breast pathologies just like orthotopic breast tissue[1]. Although galactocele occurs most frequently in the lactating breast, its occurrence in axillary accessory breast tissue is uncommon. Galactocele is a benign milk-retention cyst caused by ductal obstruction during lactation[3]. This entity has only been described in isolated case reports and small series recently[4–8]. The diagnosis of axillary masses in lactating women can be challenging because they can mimic lymphadenopathy, lipoma, sebaceous cysts, hidradenitis, abscesses, or malignancy[4,6].

We report a case of a 33-year-old lactating woman presenting with a large, initially painless right axillary swelling that had progressively enlarged and become painful. Ultrasound and fine-needle aspiration confirmed a galactocele arising from accessory breast tissue. The patient underwent surgical excision with no complications. This case contributes to the limited literature on axillary accessory-breast galactocele and highlights key diagnostic considerations and management options. A completed SCARE checklist is submitted as a supplementary file alongside this manuscript[9].

Case presentation

A 33-year-old multiparous woman, currently lactating and otherwise healthy, presented to the surgical outpatient clinic with a progressively enlarging swelling in the right axilla. She first noticed the mass 2 months earlier as a small, painless lump but reported that it had recently increased markedly in size and had become mildly painful, especially during breastfeeding. There was no history of fever, trauma, previous axillary surgery, nipple discharge, or symptoms suggestive of mastitis. She denied any similar swellings in either breast or in the left axilla.

The patient had five children and was actively breastfeeding her youngest infant at the time of presentation. Physical examination revealed a large, well-circumscribed, soft, cystic mass occupying the right axilla, which was non-erythematous, non-tender, and without local signs of inflammation. Both breasts were normal on examination, with no palpable masses, skin changes, or axillary lymphadenopathy on the contralateral side (Figs 1 and 2). Ultrasound of the right axilla demonstrated a well-defined cystic lesion measuring approximately 8 × 6 cm, located within the subcutaneous plane of the axilla, with smooth margins and internal echogenic content consistent with milk debris, and no internal vascularity on Doppler imaging, suggestive of a galactocele arising from accessory breast tissue. Diagnostic aspiration yielded thick, milk-like fluid, confirming the diagnosis and providing partial symptomatic relief.

Because of the large size of the lesion, progressive symptoms, and patient preference for definitive management, surgical excision was planned rather than repeated aspiration. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia, and a single prophylactic dose of intravenous antibiotics was administered perioperatively. Complete excision of the cyst, along with the surrounding accessory breast tissue, was performed through a longitudinal elliptical incision. Intraoperatively, the mass was well-circumscribed with clear dissection planes and minimal adhesions to the surrounding axillary fat, with no evidence of inflammation or infiltration, allowing safe and complete removal. A surgical drain was placed and subsequently removed on postoperative day 5.

Histopathological examination revealed a cystic lesion containing proteinaceous material with numerous lipid-laden macrophages, consistent with a galactocele arising in accessory breast tissue.

The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery. She continued breastfeeding throughout the perioperative period without interruption, and no anti-lactation therapy was required. Discussion

Galactocele arising in accessory axillary breast tissue represents a rare clinical entity, and its recognition is crucial when evaluating axillary swellings in lactating patients. Our case presented with a progressively enlarging axillary mass that was initially painless but later became mildly painful during lactation, which is consistent with previously reported cases[4,5]. Similar reports describe presentation during active lactation with rapid enlargement due to hormonal stimulation, often leading to diagnostic confusion.

Ultrasound remains the first-line imaging modality during lactation, as it typically demonstrates a well-defined cystic or complex cystic lesion with variable internal echogenicity depending on the fat–protein composition of milk. However, imaging appearances may mimic suspicious solid lesions, creating diagnostic uncertainty[3,5,6]. In particular, the absence of internal vascularity on Doppler imaging and the presence of internal echoes favor a benign cystic process rather than lymphadenopathy or malignancy. Aspiration confirming milky fluid, as in our patient, provides both definitive diagnosis and immediate symptomatic relief, and cytology typically reveals lipid-laden macrophages and proteinaceous debris[7,8].

The differential diagnosis of axillary masses in lactating women includes lymphadenopathy, lipoma, epidermoid cyst, hidradenitis suppurativa, abscess, and malignancy. These entities can be distinguished based on clinical and radiological features: for example, lymph nodes typically show a preserved hilum on ultrasound, abscesses are associated with inflammatory signs and increased vascularity, and solid tumors demonstrate internal blood flow. In our case, the cystic nature, lack of inflammatory features, and confirmatory aspiration supported the diagnosis of galactocele.

Although many galactoceles resolve with aspiration alone, conservative management may not be sufficient in large or symptomatic lesions. Surgical excision may be indicated for persistent, recurrent, large, or symptomatic lesions, or when diagnostic uncertainty persists[4,6]. In our case, surgical excision was preferred due to the large size, progressive symptoms, and the patient’s desire for definitive treatment rather than repeated aspirations. This approach aligns with recommendations in the literature for the selective removal of symptomatic accessory breast tissue[1,10]. Intraoperatively, the lesion was well-circumscribed with minimal adhesions, facilitating safe excision and supporting previous reports that such lesions are usually well-demarcated. Our patient recovered uneventfully, consistent with outcomes reported in other recent cases[5,7].

The morphological diversity of galactoceles and the diagnostic value of cytology[11] have been highlighted by unusual variants such as crystallizing galactoceles. Awareness of these rare presentations is important, as misinterpretation can lead to unnecessary biopsy or surgical intervention. In contrast, our case demonstrated classical clinical, radiological, and pathological features.

Overall, this case reinforces the importance of considering galactocele in the differential diagnosis of axillary swellings in lactating women. Early ultrasound evaluation, coupled with aspiration, enables accurate diagnosis. Surgery remains a safe and effective option in selected patients, particularly when lesions are large, symptomatic, or recurrent.

Conclusion

The recognition of a galactocele arising within axillary accessory breast tissue is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions in lactating women. For large, symptomatic, or persistent galactoceles, surgical excision remains a safe and effective option, particularly when conservative management is insufficient or when definitive treatment is preferred. This case highlights the need for clinicians to recognize accessory breast tissue pathologies and emphasizes the importance of targeted imaging and individualized management.
