Abstract

This project developed and evaluated advanced remote sensing and machine learning
approaches to identify, monitor, and address environmental contamination associated with coal
combustion residual (CCR) impoundments and landfills at coal-fired power plants. In Phase I,
historical and multi-temporal Sentinel-2 satellite imagery, groundwater monitoring data, and
environmental variables were integrated to detect vegetation stress potentially caused by toxic
metal leaching from coal ash disposal sites. Multiple vegetation and biophysical indices were
analyzed to determine their effectiveness in identifying abnormal vegetation growth patterns
linked to contamination. Results from case studies conducted at coal ash–impacted power plant
sites in North Carolina and Virginia demonstrated that satellite-based vegetation monitoring can
serve as an effective early indicator of environmental stress associated with metals such as
arsenic, cadmium, cobalt, lead, lithium, radium, and thallium. Supervised machine learning
models, particularly Random Forest classifiers, achieved high accuracy in detecting and mapping
stressed vegetation associated with leached-metal contamination across multiple sites, while
unsupervised learning approaches demonstrated the potential to identify contamination patterns
with reduced dependence on labeled field data. Together, these methods provide a scalable,
cost-effective, and transferable framework for large-scale environmental monitoring of legacy
industrial sites. In Phase II, the project expanded beyond contamination detection to support
remediation planning through the development of a data-driven phytoremediation
recommendation framework. This framework integrates contamination profiles, environmental
conditions, and a curated database of phytoremediation plant species to identify site-specific plant
groups capable of extracting, stabilizing, or immobilizing contaminants. By combining satellite
monitoring, machine learning, and phytoremediation planning, the project provides an integrated
approach for identifying contaminated areas, improving environmental management, and
supporting sustainable remediation strategies for coal ash–impacted landscapes.

Introduction
1.1 Motivation
Coal combustion residuals (CCR) from coal-fired power plants represent one of the largest
industrial waste streams in the United States and worldwide, with millions of tons historically
deposited in landfills and surface impoundments (Ruhl et al. 2012; Harkness et al. 2016). CCRs
contain toxic organic pollutants and heavy metals/metalloids (HM) which pose long-term hazards
to human health and surrounding ecosystems (Dreesen et al. 1977; Meij and te Winkel 2007;
Tchounwou et al. 2012; Izquierdo and Querol 2012; Swanson et al. 2013; Lauer et al. 2015; Wang
et al. 2022b). Extensive investigations across U.S. coal-ash impoundments and landfills
consistently show that CCR leachates contain HM concentrations that exceed federal health and
environmental safety guidelines (Rowe et al. 2002; Ruhl et al. 2010, 2012; Yang et al. 2015;
Vengosh et al. 2019; Deonarine et al. 2023). Even with regulatory requirements for liners and
leachate collection systems, many coal-fired power plants continue to report unsafe HM levels in
groundwater monitoring wells because substantial quantities of legacy waste remain stored in
older, unlined, or deteriorating CCR disposal units (US EPA 2015; Russ et al. 2019). The resulting
contamination evolves gradually but persistently as HMs continuously leach from these
impoundments and accumulate within surrounding soils and groundwater systems due to their
non-biodegradable nature (Adriano et al. 2004; Bolan et al. 2014; Brandt et al. 2017; Petrović and
Fiket 2022; Padhye et al. 2023).
Despite the scale and persistence of the problem, current methods for detecting, monitoring, and
remediating CCR-derived contamination remain limited. Existing regulatory monitoring
approaches rely heavily on point-based groundwater sampling networks (US EPA 2015).
Although these methods provide analytically precise measurements, they are labor-intensive,
expensive, spatially limited, and often unable to capture broad spatiotemporal contamination
patterns or early ecological degradation. Consequently, there is a significant need for scalable,
cost-effective, and non-invasive monitoring approaches capable of identifying contamination
across large spatial extents, including locations lacking comprehensive historical monitoring
records.
Vegetation functions as an effective biological indicator of environmental contamination because
exposure to HM stress alters plant physiological and biochemical processes in measurable ways
(Adriano et al. 2004; Gholizadeh and Kopačková 2019). Heavy metal exposure disrupts
photosynthetic activity, pigment synthesis, cellular water regulation, and metabolic functioning,
leading to chlorosis, reduced chlorophyll concentration, necrosis, impaired canopy development,
and cellular damage (Rout and Das 2009; Mishra et al. 2017; Rai et al. 2019; Zhang and Wang
2020; Sperdouli 2022; Vasilachi et al. 2023a; Bonfiglio et al. 2024; Lovynska et al. 2024). These
stress responses produce measurable changes in vegetation spectral reflectance across visible,
red-edge, and near-infrared wavelengths, forming the basis of phyto-identification, in which
vegetation stress is used as a proxy for underlying contamination patterns.
Remote sensing technologies provide an effective mechanism for operationalizing this concept
over broad geographic areas. Handheld, airborne, and satellite-based systems have long been
used to detect vegetation stress associated with mining activities, industrial emissions, and soil
contamination using field spectroscopy, hyperspectral sensors, and multispectral imagery
(Kemper and Sommer 2002; Kirbag Zengin and Munzuroglu 2005; Rosso et al. 2005; Liu et al.

2010, 2018a). However, many earlier studies focused on single sites, limited temporal windows,
or specialized datasets that are not globally accessible, while often lacking integration of long-
term vegetation dynamics and broader environmental context.
The Sentinel-2 satellite mission, with its freely available near-daily multispectral imagery, relatively
high spatial resolution, and red-edge spectral bands, is particularly well suited for detecting subtle
vegetation stress signals (Zarco-Tejada et al. 2019; Shukla et al. 2019; Soydan et al. 2021;
Plutalova et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2023a; Guo et al. 2024). Sentinel-2’s red-edge and near-infrared
bands are highly sensitive to chlorophyll concentration and canopy condition, enabling detection
of stress gradients that may not be visible in other spectral regions (Clevers et al. 2004; Eitel et
al. 2011; Clevers and Gitelson 2013a). Previous investigations have demonstrated strong
associations between HM contamination and vegetation spectral responses across contaminated
landscapes (Götze et al. 2016; Jin et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2018, 2019a; Liu et al. 2018a; Yang
et al. 2022a; Noor et al. 2022). Nevertheless, these relationships are frequently nonlinear and
site-specific because vegetation stress responses are influenced by soil characteristics,
hydrology, topography, climate, and other environmental variables.
Machine learning (ML) techniques provide a robust analytical framework for addressing these
complex interactions. ML approaches are highly effective at modeling nonlinear relationships
among environmental variables, vegetation physiology, and stress dynamics (Gao et al. 2020;
van Klompenburg et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2025). ML methods have been
widely applied to infer HM distributions in soils and sediments by integrating spectral information
with ancillary environmental covariates (Jerome H. Friedman 2001; Rumpf et al. 2010; Mountrakis
et al. 2011; Rodriguez-Galiano et al. 2012; Belgiu and Drăguţ 2016; Thanh Noi and Kappas 2017;
Tan et al. 2020; Fu et al. 2024). Recent studies integrating Sentinel-2 imagery with environmental
datasets and advanced ML models have demonstrated strong capabilities for mapping and
predicting metal contamination across soils, crops, and ecological systems (Wang et al. 2020;
Zhang et al. 2022; Yu et al. 2023b; Lin et al. 2023).
Despite significant advances in remote sensing–based ML approaches, several limitations
continue to restrict their application in CCR-impacted environments. Many existing models remain
highly site-specific and demonstrate limited transferability across regions and environmental
conditions. Most prior studies also focus on one or only a few contaminants, whereas CCR sites
typically contain complex mixtures of interacting HMs. Furthermore, many approaches depend
heavily on labeled ground-truth contamination data, which are often sparse, outdated, or
unavailable at older facilities. Existing methods are also commonly developed over short time
periods and are not optimized for long-term or large-scale monitoring applications. Frequent
retraining and recalibration are often required to maintain performance, limiting operational
scalability. Finally, current contamination-detection frameworks rarely integrate remediation
planning or phytotechnology-based decision support, reducing their practical value for
environmental management and restoration activities.
These limitations provided the primary motivation for the present project, which aimed to develop
an integrated machine learning and remote sensing framework capable of identifying leached-
metal contamination using vegetation stress as a proxy while simultaneously supporting
phytoremediation planning for CCR-impacted landscapes.

1.2 Project Objectives
The overarching objective of this project was to develop a machine learning and remote sensing–
enhanced phytotechnology framework for the detection and remediation of leached-metal
contamination in CCR-impacted landscapes. The framework was designed to operate using low-
cost, open-source remote sensing technologies, enabling scalable and continuous monitoring
across diverse spatial and temporal extents.
The primary project objectives included:
 1. Remote identification of leached-metal contamination using low-cost technology
By integrating freely available Sentinel-2 satellite imagery, ancillary environmental datasets, and
machine learning algorithms, the framework identifies vegetation stress associated with leached-
metal contamination while reducing dependence on labor-intensive, expensive, and site-specific
groundwater monitoring well networks.
 2. Screening and applicability across broad spatiotemporal scales
Rather than limiting analyses to a small number of well-instrumented facilities, the framework was
designed to support transferable, regional, and multi-site screening applications across broad
spatial and temporal scales. This capability enables identification of previously unmonitored or
under-characterized CCR-impacted sites and supports prioritization of locations requiring
targeted field investigations or regulatory intervention.
 3. Integration of contamination identification with phytoremediation recommendation
 systems
The project integrated contamination detection with ecological restoration and remediation
planning through development of a hyperaccumulator plant recommendation framework. This
component incorporated site-specific contamination conditions, environmental variables, and a
curated phytoremediation plant database to support data-driven selection of plant species
capable of extracting, stabilizing, or immobilizing contaminants.
Through this integrated framework, the project provides scalable, cost-effective, and non-invasive
approaches for contamination monitoring while simultaneously supporting implementation of site-
specific, nature-based remediation strategies relevant to utilities, regulators, and environmental
managers.

1.3 Project Activities and Major Contributions
The project was organized into multiple technical phases focused on identifying, characterizing,
and addressing leached-metal contamination using vegetation stress as the central indicator.
Major project activities and contributions included:
 1. Development of a remote sensing–based phyto-identification framework for detecting
 vegetation stress associated with leached metals across diverse spatial and temporal
 scales using Sentinel-2 vegetation indices, biophysical parameters, and spatiotemporal
 vegetation stress metrics.
 2. Implementation and evaluation of supervised machine learning models for classification
 and mapping of metal-induced vegetation stress across multiple CCR-impacted sites.
 3. Implementation and evaluation of unsupervised machine learning approaches capable of
 detecting contamination-related stress patterns without reliance on labeled contamination
 datasets, addressing conditions where ground-truth data are sparse or unavailable.

 4. Development of a phytoremediation plant recommendation framework integrating
 contamination profiles, environmental conditions, and plant traits to identify suitable
 hyperaccumulator species for site-specific remediation applications.
Collectively, these activities established a scalable, cost-effective, non-invasive, and actionable
framework for environmental monitoring and remediation planning at CCR-impacted sites.

1.4 Organization of the Report
The remainder of this report is organized according to the major project phases and technical
activities completed during the study.
The first technical section presents the foundational remote sensing investigation establishing
vegetation stress as a proxy indicator of leached-metal contamination associated with CCR
impoundments and landfills. Sentinel-2 vegetation indices, biophysical parameters, and multi-
year spatiotemporal vegetation analyses were used to evaluate relationships between vegetation
responses and groundwater metal concentrations.
The second technical section describes the supervised machine learning framework developed
to classify and map vegetation stress associated with contamination. Spectral, spatiotemporal,
topographic, hydrological, climatic, and soil variables were integrated into multiple supervised ML
models, and model performance was evaluated across several CCR-impacted sites.
The third technical section evaluates unsupervised machine learning approaches for
contamination detection without dependence on labeled contamination datasets. Clustering and
deep learning methods were tested to determine their ability to identify meaningful vegetation
stress patterns under realistic environmental monitoring conditions where field data are sparse or
unavailable.
The final technical section presents the phytoremediation recommendation framework developed
to support remediation planning. This component integrates contamination profiles,
environmental conditions, and plant trait databases to identify hyperaccumulator plant species
suitable for site-specific remediation and ecological restoration strategies.

Phyto-Identification of Leached Metal Contamination using Satellite
Remote Sensing: A Case Study on a Coal-fired Power Plant
2.1. Introduction
Coal combustion residuals (CCR) stand out as one of the most voluminous industrial waste
streams in the United States, generated by the operation of 470 coal-fired power plants (Ruhl et
al. 2012). This process produces around 110 million tons of CCR, with sixty percent of this waste
disposed in surface impoundments and landfills, and about 80 percent of that portion disposed in
on-site units (Environmental Protection Agency 2015). Coal power plants are responsible for
different metals leaching above safe limits from CCR and contaminating water, soil, and air of the
surrounding ecosystem which adversely impacts health of humans, plants and animals (RTI 2007;
Barbara Gottlieb et al. 2010; Tchounwou et al. 2012). Continuous monitoring and identification of
potential contamination zones to contain the adverse effects is essential.
Investigation of vegetation health, especially physiological and morphological changes can serve
as indicators to identify vegetation stress of potentially contaminated areas due to the interaction
among plants, water, and soil (Jackson 1986a; Ferretti 1997; Zinnert et al. 2013; Pause et al.
2016). Vegetation stress results from the negative impact of external factors, namely a stressor
on plant/vegetation functions, growth, and development, which can be classified as biotic:
involving natural organisms such as insects and pathogens, or abiotic: involving anthropogenic
physical or chemical factors like drought, pollution, and metal contamination from industrial
activities. (Lichtenthaler 1998; Othman et al. 2014; Shrivastava and Kumar 2015; Cotrozzi et al.
2018; Lassalle et al. 2020a; Yang et al. 2022; Haider et al. 2023). Traditionally, plant stress
diagnosis has been based on identifying symptoms through visual field surveys and laboratory
analyses of physiological and biochemical responses from destructive sampling of plants under
induced stress conditions (Chaerle and Van Der Straeten 2001; Fedotov et al. 2016). More
recently, remote sensing, especially imaging spectroscopy of electromagnetic radiation from
plants, has emerged as an effective economical alternative to investigate vegetation stress
(Jackson 1986b; Chaerle and Van Der Straeten 2000; Milton et al. 2009; Colomina and Molina
2014; Zhang et al. 2019b; Lassalle et al. 2020b; Kayet et al. 2024). Vegetation health/stress
monitoring utilizes spectral wavelengths between 400 and 2,500 nm, to assess water content, cell
structure and photosynthetic pigments, which typically exhibit notable alterations under stress
conditions (Mishra et al. 2017).
In terms of assessing heavy metal contamination, previous studies have shown the effectiveness
of remote sensing techniques in both controlled laboratory environments and large-scale field
experiments (Kemper and Sommer 2002; Kirbag Zengin and Munzuroglu 2005; Rosso et al. 2005;
Liu et al. 2010, 2018a). Studies examining the impact of heavy metals on plants stress have
yielded diverse results, shaped by factors like soil type (Tan et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2021; Wei et
al. 2021), geographical location (Song et al. 2020; Mirzaei et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2022), and
plant type (Dunagan et al. 2007; Götze et al. 2016). For instance, (Zhang and Wang 2020) and
Enya et al. (2020) demonstrated that heavy metals slow down the mineralization of soil organic
carbon, leading to increased soil organic matter in topsoil, which impacts soil properties and
ecosystem functions. After entering plant cells, heavy metals disrupt cellular homeostasis by
binding to biomolecules, inhibiting enzymes, inducing oxidative stress, and cellular damage while
also leading to metabolic disorders, chloroplast disorganization, and damage to aboveground
plant organs (Rout and Das 2009; Sperdouli 2022; Bonfiglio et al. 2024; Lovynska et al. 2024).

Heavy metals can cause stress symptoms like chlorosis and necrosis, which greatly affect how
plant leaves absorb and reflect light (Vasilachi et al. 2023b). Van derWerff et al. (2008)
demonstrated that a reduction in chlorophyll results in distinct spectral changes, including a
noticeable decrease in the height of the infrared shoulder. Previous localized studies have shown
that changes in leaf pigment composition and internal structure under heavy metal stress can be
effectively captured through spectral analysis (Li et al. 2015a; Mirzaei et al. 2019). Buitrago
Acevedo et al. (2017) reported that, irrespective of plant species, leaf spectral characteristics tend
to be consistent across the electromagnetic spectrum. Kooistra et al. (2004) discovered
correlations between concentrations of Pb, Zn, Cd, Ni, and Cu in river floodplain soils and the
reflectance spectra of grass. Clevers et al. (2004) identified a significant negative correlation
between the maximum first derivative of grassland spectral features in the red-edge and soil
heavy metal contents, attributing it to the influence of metals on chlorophyll concentrations. Li et
al. (2015) estimated soil arsenic contents using reflectance spectra from rice plants, linked to
canopy chlorophyll contents. In addition to that, spectral indices analysis, such as the Normalized
Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) has proven valuable for monitoring vegetation health in
response to heavy metal stress. Mikkola (1996) utilized NDVI time series from Landsat 5 to assess
changes in vegetation health due to Ni and Cu emissions from smelters. D’Emilio et al. (2012)
found significant correlations between NDVI values and the concentrations of nine soil heavy
metals (Cd, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ni, Pb, Zn), highlighting NDVI’s potential for preliminary screening
of contaminated areas. (Liu et al. 2018b) applied integrated NDVI time series from multiple
satellite platforms (HJ-1A/B, Landsat 7, Landsat 8) to assess cadmium stress in vegetation,
further demonstrating NDVI's effectiveness in large-scale monitoring. In heavy metal-polluted
areas, plants exhibit reduced water content, increased chlorophyll hydrolysis, and cellular
damage, impacting reflectance spectra and providing a basis for predicting soil heavy metal
concentrations indirectly (Shi et al., 2016). However, certain plant species have developed the
remarkable ability to tolerate heavy metals through specialized physiological mechanisms,
enabling them to adapt to soils with high concentrations of these elements employing strategies
such as accumulation of heavy metals in their tissues or exclusion to limit their uptake (Noor et
al. 2022; Ningombam et al. 2024).
The use of satellite remote sensing for vegetation stress detection has gained prominence due to
its fast and non-invasive nature, enabling the early detection of both biotic and abiotic stress
(Bayat et al. 2016; Kollist et al. 2019). This approach is especially advantageous for large-scale
monitoring and investigation, as it enables the assessment of vegetation from a distance by
analyzing chemical and physical characteristics through the interpretation of spectral responses
from satellite sensors, making it possible to determine vegetation health and detect stress
conditions (Galieni et al. 2021). In recent years, there has been growing interest in utilizing
Sentinel-2 imagery, with several studies demonstrating its versatility for detecting and monitoring
heavy metal contamination across diverse environments. In agricultural contexts, Sentinel-2
imagery combined with advanced machine learning approaches has proven effective for soil
contamination assessment, such as arsenic prediction using a soil organic matter-based
hierarchical model (Wang et al. 2025) and salinized soil heavy metal estimation using random
forest regression with mineral alteration indicators (Fu et al. 2024). Similarly, Sentinel-2 imagery
integrated with radiative transfer models and biochemical indicators has been successfully used
to detect heavy metal stress in rice crops (Chen et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024), achieving accuracies
exceeding 85%. Zhang et al. (2018) introduced the Heavy Metal Stress-Sensitive Index (HMSSI),

and Liu et al. (2018) demonstrated the effectiveness of multi-temporal Sentinel-2 analysis in
capturing the spatiotemporal dynamics of heavy metal stress in rice.
Further innovations include the application of novel algorithms alongside Sentinel-2 data for
precise detection of crop heavy metal stress by (Zhang et al. 2019a, 2022; Tang et al. 2020; Wang
et al. 2024), highlighting the sensor’s expanding application in agricultural monitoring. Mirzaei et
al. (2021) addressed monitoring challenges in wastewater-irrigated areas, establishing
relationships between Sentinel-2 spectral bands and soil Pb, Cu, and Ni concentrations. Plutalova
et al. (2021) demonstrated improved vegetation classification by fusing multispectral Sentinel-2
with Sentinel-1 radar data in Western Siberia, emphasizing the benefits of multisensor integration.
In industrial landscapes, ensemble machine learning models leveraging Sentinel-2 data and soil
pH stratification effectively predicted Cu concentrations in the Pearl River Delta, China (Guo et
al. 2024), while regression analyses successfully estimated Pb, Cu, and Zn levels in Kazakhstan’s
contaminated soils (Felegari et al. 2023). At mining sites, Sentinel-2-based studies have excelled
in detecting metals, from Pb and Cd in Chinese soils using ensemble learning (Yu et al. 2023), to
Al mapping in bauxite residues in Greece (Kasmaeeyazdi et al. 2021), and even secondary Fe
minerals linked to acid mine drainage (Soydan et al. 2021). Furthermore, Khosravi et al. (2022)
demonstrated that fusing Landsat 8 with Sentinel-2 imagery significantly enhanced toxic element
prediction models in mining waste, outperforming single-sensor methods.
Sentinel-2 has also proven valuable in aquatic environments - for instance, monitoring acid mine
drainage in Chini Lake, Malaysia (Rendana et al. 2023), mapping water quality parameters in the
transboundary Aras River (Fouladi Osgouei et al. 2022) and assessing Be concentrations and
radiological risks in Egypt (Khaleal et al. 2023).
Collectively, these studies underscore the capacity of Sentinel-2 as an accessible, cost-effective
option for large-scale monitoring of heavy metal contamination across soil, vegetation, and water
ecosystems. When integrated with advanced modeling approaches, machine learning algorithms,
ground-truth validation, and data fusion techniques, Sentinel-2 enables efficient, scalable, and
reliable detection of contamination. These advancements highlight the potential of Sentinel-2
imagery in supporting environmental monitoring and decision-making, particularly for assessing
metal-induced vegetation stress and contamination risks in agricultural landscapes and
industrially impacted environments.
The objective of this work is to detect vegetation stress, particularly in the context of metal-induced
contamination from CCR. This is achieved by utilizing satellite remote sensing with spectral
indices derived from multispectral Sentinel-2 imagery. The unique aspect of this study lies in its
emphasis on timeseries analysis for detecting vegetation stress, capturing both year-to-year
trends and short-term, day-to-day variations. As is the case in most realistic conditions, the type
of vegetation coverage, the type of metal contamination, and its effect on vegetation health were
not known beforehand in this study. We explain how these uncertainties were minimized through
data analysis. Furthermore, few studies have been conducted on identifying vegetation stress
from Sentinel-2 data using Google Earth Engine (GEE), and even fewer studies have explored
extended timeseries analysis for this purpose. The findings of this investigation, based on a case
study conducted of vegetation surrounding a coal-fired power plant, demonstrate that vegetation
stress and the leached metal contamination that is potentially responsible for this stress can be
identified and measured using the proposed framework.

Google Earth Engine
The Google Earth Engine (GEE), developed by Google, is a cloud-based geospatial analysis
platform, which provides unparalleled access to an extensive archive of satellite imagery and
geospatial datasets (Gorelick et al. 2017). GEE's capabilities extend far beyond traditional
analysis methods, allowing users to harness the power of petabytes of data for different
applications ranging from environmental monitoring and land cover analysis to climate change
research. In the context of vegetation stress detection, timeseries data allows for the identification
of temporal patterns and trends in vegetation health. By leveraging GEE's computational
capabilities, we have developed tools to perform the methodology of the study to identify
anomalous patterns and assess the severity of vegetation stress.
Methodology
A methodology for phyto-identification of potential leached metal contamination by analyzing
vegetation stress patterns over any spatiotemporal extent for any defined area of interest was
developed within the GEE platform, following the procedures illustrated in Figure 2(a): (i)
Preprocess the multispectral Sentinel-2 imagery data after collecting from Google Cloud using
GEE, selecting required bands while reducing cloud cover, atmospheric noise, and other
interferences for the area of interest and time duration. (ii) Calculate Vegetation Indices (VIs) and
Biophysical Parameter Indices (BPIs). (iii) Calculate the coefficient of spatio-temporal variation
(CSTV) for spectral indices and visualize it on a map to identify potential contaminated areas with
vegetation stress. (iv) Cross-validate the findings with field measurements or laboratory data.

Data preprocessing
For this study, Sentinel-2 Level-2A orthorectified surface reflectance data, atmospherically
corrected using the Sen2Cor processor (Main-Knorn et al. 2017) were acquired via GEE,
spanning the period from January 2019 to December 2023 for a defined area of interest
encompassing the impoundments of the BCSS power plant. The data were filtered to include the
desired bands, as listed in Table 1, and to limit cloud cover to less than 30%. Following
preprocessing and filtering, the dataset was refined to a cleaner collection of 106 Sentinel-2
images.

vegetation health, structure, and functions. VIs are mathematical combinations of spectral bands

analysis of its condition and dynamics. Six VIs and three BPIs, as listed in Table 2 and Table 3

VIs and BPIs were derived from Sentinel-2 spectral bands, following the workflow outlined in

Spatio-temporal anomaly detection
Spatio-temporal anomaly detection focuses on identifying irregular patterns or outliers in datasets
with both spatial and temporal dimensions. Traditional methods like the z-score are effective for
detecting temporal anomalies by calculating the standard deviation of a data point from the mean.
For instance, a z-score exceeding a threshold, such as z >2, indicates a significant anomaly.

However, z-score-based approaches often fail to capture spatial dependencies, which are critical
in datasets influenced by spatio-temporal relationships. To address this, Shekhar et al., (2002,
2003) developed S-outliers to detect spatial anomaly and Cheng & Li, (2006) extended the
definition incorporating both spatial and temporal contexts which analyze the deviation of an
attribute (e.g., vegetation index, temperature, population density) at a specific location from the
aggregated values within a defined spatio-temporal neighborhood. The spatio-temporal outliers

of 95% corresponds to 𝜃𝜃 ≈ 2.
Liu et al., (2018) computed the spatio-temporal outliers namely coefficient of spatiotemporal
variation (CSTV) using Sentinel-2 imagery to identify stressed and unstressed rice crops and a
CSTV of 2, corresponding to a 95% confidence level, was used as the threshold to classify and
distinguish stable from abrupt stressors and demonstrated that a higher CSTV value referred to
abrupt stress, whereas a lower CSTV value indicated no stress or stable stress with a uniform
changing trend during growth.
In this study, the CSTV metric was utilized to detect stressed vegetation and identify potential
contamination induced by leached metals within the defined study area from 2019 to 2023. A GEE
application was developed to calculate the CSTV of all spectral indices. To analyze year-to-year
variations for VIs and BPIs, composite images for the growth period of each year were initially
generated from Sentinel-2 timeseries imagery. Subsequently, CSTV was calculated using the
composite images of 2019 and 2023. A threshold (𝜃𝜃) value of 2 was defined, classifying areas
with CSTV ≥ 2 as highly stressed indicating stress caused by abrupt stressors, CSTV = 0 as not
stressed, and intermediate values as stressed with persistent and stable conditions, where stress
was present but with a more uniform trend over time. The calculated CSTV was visualized on a
map to pinpoint stressed vegetation areas potentially affected by leached contamination.

To cross-validate our proposed methodology, we compared the spatial distribution of stressed
vegetation with maps of groundwater metal contamination obtained from monitoring well data
reported by industry and compiled by the Environmental Integrity Project via the Ashtracker web
portal (Environmental Integrity Project). The concentrations (in mg/L) of arsenic (As), antimony
(Sb), barium (Ba), beryllium (Be), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), cobalt (Co), lead (Pb), lithium
(Li), mercury (Hg), molybdenum (Mo), radium (Ra), selenium (Se), and thallium (Tl) reported from
the monitoring wells were geospatially interpolated using the kriging interpolation method and a
spherical semi-variogram model in ArcGIS pro by ESRI (ESRI 2024). This process generated a
concentration map for each contaminant (Figure 3). Next, we searched for a potential correlation
between the vegetation stress map, detected through satellite image analyses, and these
contamination maps for different metals.

While the Sentinel-2 imagery dataset begins in 2019, the monitoring wells dataset covers 2011–
2019. This partial temporal overlap is acknowledged as a limitation. However, heavy metals
contamination in groundwater and soil originating from CCR typically evolves gradually through
continuous leaching from unlined or unremedied impoundments, and due to the non-degradable
nature of the heavy metals which resist microbial and chemical breakdown (Adriano et al. 2004;
Bolan et al. 2014), often persists in the subsurface for extended periods following disposal (Ruhl
et al. 2012; Brandt et al. 2017; Petrović and Fiket 2022; Padhye et al. 2023). Therefore, the
contamination maps used in this study provide a robust representation of long-term subsurface
conditions suitable for comparison with remotely sensed vegetation stress indicators. Although
Ashtracker does not provide detailed aquifer properties (e.g., stratigraphy, groundwater flow rates,
or hydraulic conductivity), monitoring well records capture contaminants at varying depths,
including near-surface levels where groundwater–vegetation interactions are plausible. The
relative persistence and stability of the contamination justify their use for cross-validation against
vegetation stress derived from Sentinel-2 imagery.

2.3. Results
The spectral responses or reflectance data of Sentinel-2 image bands from various locations near
the impoundments indicate that vegetation around the BCSS impoundments, highlighted in ten
dense vegetation pixel locations in Figure 3b, is less healthy or more stressed than the vegetation
farther from the impoundment (as shown by the “Base” curve in Figure 4a). The spectral
responses of Sentinel-2 bands in Figure 4a show that reflectance from the B08-NIR band is
consistently lower near the coal ash impoundments compared to the base location, indicating
heightened vegetation stress in those areas.

Figure 4: (a) Ten sample locations with dense vegetation around the BCSS impoundments, (b) Reflectance
values from Sentinel-2 spectral bands at these locations (Location 1 to 10) compared to a “Base” location
far away from the impoundments.

Beyond individual spectral bands, vegetation health can also be more effectively assessed using
VIs, which combine reflectance from multiple bands to enhance sensitivity to vegetation condition,
such as the NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index). NDVI uses reflectance from the red
(B04) and near-infrared (B08) bands to quantify vegetation health, with values closer to 1
indicating healthy vegetation with high chlorophyll content and canopy density. As shown in Figure
5c, two clusters of five vegetation pixel locations were randomly selected: those closest to
(locations 1-5) and farthest from (locations 6-10) the active ash basin impoundment, and the mean
NDVI values of these clusters are plotted. The day-to-day and yearly mean NDVI data from 2019-
2023, shown in Figure 5a and 5b, respectively, indicate that NDVI values are higher at locations
farther from the impoundment/landfill, suggesting that vegetation closer to the impoundments is
more stressed.
The temporal trend analysis revealed a consistent decline in NDVI values near the impoundment,
corresponding to areas with higher metal contamination levels. The decline supports the
association between increased contamination and vegetation stress, as expected from
physiological impacts such as chlorophyll reduction.

In this study, the locations shown in Figures 4 and 5 were randomly selected within densely
vegetated areas to provide objective coverage and reduce selection bias while illustrating a near–
far contrast relative to impoundments. These locations were chosen for observational comparison
rather than as part of a formal stratified or probabilistic sampling design and should be interpreted
as illustrative observations that demonstrate the proximity-associated gradient in vegetation
response.

The day-to-day and yearly means of VIs and BPIs over the study area, derived from Sentinel-2
data (see Table 2 & Table 3), are shown in Figure 6 and Figure 7 respectively. Higher VI & BPI
values are noticeable during the growth period (except for LCC which exhibited slight decrease),
showing a seasonal trend from May to October each year from 2019 to 2023 in the daily
timeseries. The yearly timeseries showed a decline in all VIs and BPIs (except for LCC which
displayed a slight upward trend) from 2020 to 2023, indicating that the overall vegetation health
of the study area is deteriorating.
While LCC showed minimal variation over the analyzed period, the observed trends in the VIs
and BPIs indicate a negative correlation between overall vegetation health and LCC. This inverse
relationship suggests that as vegetation health deteriorates, reflected by declining VIs and BPIs,
LCC increases, potentially as a compensatory response. The reduced LCC could be
advantageous, promoting better light distribution within the canopy, reducing photochemical
damage from excessive light, and lowering water and nutrient demands (Hamblin et al. 2014).

However, the sustained increase in LCC outside the growth period may indicate stress-related
changes in vegetation, such as an imbalance in nutrient allocation or adaptation to suboptimal
conditions, further emphasizing the negative correlation between overall vegetation health and
LCC trends.

To further investigate the correlation between VIs and BPIs, a 3-order polynomial regression
model was used, and the results are shown in Figure 8. Among the BPIs, LAI exhibited the
strongest correlation with all VIs, ranging from 0.85 (MTCI) to 1.00 (SAVI), highlighting its robust
relationship with vegetation indices. CCC showed moderate correlations, with values ranging from

from 0.37 (NDRE2) to 0.45 (SAVI). The average correlation values revealed that SAVI and CIRE
have the highest overall alignment with the biophysical parameters, whereas MTCI demonstrates
the lowest. These results suggest that LAI is the most reliable biophysical parameter for assessing
vegetation health in this study, while the weaker correlation of LCC may indicate variability or
stress within the vegetation.

Utilizing the spatio-temporal anomaly detection approach detailed in Section 3.3, the CSTV for all
VIs and BPIs from 2019 to 2023 was calculated and visualized using the developed GEE tool.
This analysis enabled the detection and classification of stressed vegetation areas, particularly

indicates a CSTV close to 1 (persistent and stable stress), and red highlights CSTV values of 2

The results from Figure 10a highlight the varying effectiveness of different vegetation indices and
biophysical parameters in detecting vegetation stress. Within the BCSS study area, vegetation
accounts for 72.29% of the total area as shown in Figure 9b. Among the biophysical parameters,
CCC indicated stress in 86.13% of vegetation cover, followed by LAI at 79.38% and LCC at
71.44%. For the vegetation indices, CIRE detected the highest percentage of stress at 82.65%,
followed by SAVI (78.44%), NDRE1 (74.90%), NDRE2 (74.12%) and NDVI (71.99%), all
indicating significant vegetation stress. In contrast, MTCI only detected stress in 19.91% of
vegetation cover, suggesting limited sensitivity for stress detection in this study.
While comparing the identified stressed areas from 2019 to 2023 in Figure 9d–l with the
geospatially interpolated arsenic concentration in Figure 9c and other leaching metals
concentration from Figure 3, it is noticeable that the stressed areas are comparatively more visible
where the concentration of leaching metals are higher. This observation indicates that the overall
results of stressed vegetation areas are correlated with the potentially contaminated areas from
leaching metals.
Another key observation from the results, as depicted in Figure 9 is that vegetation stress and
contamination levels are significantly higher in and around the unlined active ash basin
impoundment compared to the other two impoundments. This suggests that the lack of lining in
the active impoundment may contribute to increased leaching of metals, thereby exacerbating
stress on the surrounding vegetation.

The correlation analysis presented in Figure 10b and detailed in 10c examines the relationship
between the CSTV values of VIs and BPIs derived from Sentinel-2 imagery and the geospatially
interpolated concentration maps of leaching metal contaminants reveal varying relationships.
Among the contaminants, As, Li, Cd, Co, and Ba showed moderate positive correlation across
most indices (correlation coefficients ranging from ~0.17 to ~0.32), indicating a potential link
between higher metal concentrations and elevated vegetation stress. Conversely, Mo consistently

showed moderate to strong negative correlations across all indices (ranging from -0.28 to -0.35),
suggesting a possible nutrient buffering role in mitigating stress. Other metals like Cr and Hg
exhibited weak or negative correlations, reflecting a complex interplay between metal toxicity and
vegetation response. Trace elements like Tl and Ra showed weaker but consistently positive
correlations (~0.1–0.2 range), while Pb and Se correlations remained marginal (~0.00–0.07).
Overall, these findings demonstrate that while vegetation stress is widespread in the study area,
particularly around coal ash impoundments, its relationship with specific metal contaminants
varies, highlighting both the complexity of metal-vegetation interactions and the potential of
Sentinel-2 derived indices for metal-induced vegetation stress monitoring. As shown in Figure
10c, the absolute average correlations, computed as the mean of absolute correlation values
across all contaminants, indicated that LCC (0.16) among BPIs and NDVI (0.17) among VIs
exhibited the strongest associations with metal concentrations. The correlations were broadly
comparable across indices, with values between 0.12 and 0.17 for NDRE1, SAVI, LAI, NDRE2,
CIRE, and CCC. In contrast, MTCI (0.02) demonstrated negligible correlation, underscoring its
limited sensitivity to vegetation stress associated with leaching metals in the study area.

Discussion
The analysis of Sentinel-2 spectral reflectance data revealed that vegetation near coal ash
impoundment consistently exhibited reduced reflectance in the NIR band, indicating structural
and physiological stress responses. Heavy metal contamination leads to degradation of
chlorophyll content and alteration of leaf cellular structure, resulting in diminished NIR reflectance
and such reductions in reflectance are strongly associated with chlorosis and necrosis symptoms,
which impair the plant’s ability to reflect light (Bayat et al. 2016; Rai et al. 2019; Gholizadeh and
Kopačková 2019; Mirzaei et al. 2019; Vasilachi et al. 2023a). Our use of Sentinel-2 imagery at a
regional scale and across multiple years for this study aligns with the insights of Li et al. (2015),
Mirzaei et al. (2019) and Buitrago Acevedo et al. (2017), demonstrating that satellite-based
spectral reflectance can detect widespread patterns of vegetation stress linked to metal-induced
contamination. The spatial consistency of lower NIR reflectance across multiple vegetation pixels
near the impoundments reinforces the potential of multispectral data as a reliable, early indicator
of vegetation stress in contaminated environments.
The declining NDVI values observed near the impoundments from 2019 to 2023 closely align with
several studies which have demonstrated the effectiveness of spectral indices, particularly NDVI,
for detecting heavy metal contamination using remote sensing data (Mikkola 1996; D’Emilio et al.
2012; Liu et al. 2018b; Yang et al. 2022). Unlike ground-based studies that often lack spatial
breadth, the use of satellite-derived spectral indices provides a scalable approach, capturing
spatio-temporal vegetation dynamics. The sustained downward trend in NDVI near active ash
basin impoundment indicates prolonged stress responses, likely driven by persistent metal
exposure. These results underscore the effectiveness of long-term satellite monitoring for tracking
gradual contamination and environmental degradation.
Additionally, vegetation stress was significantly more severe in proximity to the unlined active ash
impoundment compared to lined impoundments. This finding reinforces the critical role of lining
in limiting metal leachate migration into surrounding ecosystems. It complements U.S. EPA rule
and guidelines that advocate for lined containment systems in coal ash management but also
highlights the need for geospatial evidence to inform enforcement and prioritization (US EPA

2024). Our spatio-temporal analysis provides actionable insights, supporting policy frameworks
aimed at reducing environmental risks from CCR waste.
Among the biophysical parameters, LAI exhibited the strongest correlation with vegetation
indices, with R² values ranging from 0.85 to 1.00. This strong relationship confirms the findings of
Jin et al. (2017), who emphasized LAI’s sensitivity to canopy structural changes under
environmental stress, particularly in metal-contaminated conditions. The robustness of LAI across
multiple indices in our study reinforces its utility as a reliable indicator of vegetation health.
In terms of detecting vegetation stress, among the VIs, the CIRE demonstrated the highest
effectiveness, identifying stress in 82.65% of vegetation cover. CIRE's reliance on red-edge
spectral information makes it highly responsive to variations in chlorophyll concentration, a critical
indicator of vegetation health which is consistent with Eitel et al. (2011), who showed that red-
edge indices could detect stress earlier than traditional indices like NDVI. Similarly, among the
BPIs, CCC was the most impactful, detecting stress in 86.13% of vegetation cover. CCC, which
comprehensively measures canopy chlorophyll levels, has been proven to effectively reflect
photosynthetic activity and physiological status of vegetation (Gitelson et al. 2005; Clevers and
Gitelson 2013a; Zarco-Tejada et al. 2019). These findings highlight the reliability of both CIRE
and CCC in capturing early signs of vegetation stress, facilitating timely interventions for
environmental ecosystem management.
The CSTV metric effectively identified areas of heightened vegetation stress, particularly around
the unlined impoundment, with >80% detection accuracy across most spectral indices. This aligns
with the previous studies regarding spatio-temporal change detection and analysis (Verbesselt et
al. 2012; Olsen et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2016, 2018a; Tang et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2024; Wen et al.
2024). The CSTV approach allowed for differentiation among abrupt or high stress, persistent and
stable stress, and no-stress zones, making it a practical tool for regulatory bodies and site
managers aiming to prioritize remediation efforts.
The analysis of leached metals revealed varying degrees of correlation with CSTV of VIs and
BPIs providing insights into their potential impacts on plant health. Among the metals studied, As,
Ba, Cd, Co, Li, and Ra exhibited moderate positive correlations with most indices. This suggests
that higher concentrations of these metals are associated with increased vegetation stress. Such
findings align with previous studies, which have reported similar associations between elevated
metal concentrations and adverse effects on vegetation health (Fryzova et al. 2017; Rahman and
Singh 2019; Ali and Gill 2022). On the other hand, Be, Pb, Sb, Se, and Tl showed weak or
negligible correlations, indicating a less pronounced impact on vegetation stress under the
conditions studied. Interestingly, Mo, a vital micronutrient for vegetation growth, displayed a
moderate negative correlation with vegetation stress indices. This suggests that molybdenum
may play a role in mitigating stress and supporting plant health. Similarly, Cr and Hg exhibited
low to negligible negative correlations, implying potential contributions to reducing vegetation
stress at lower concentrations. These observations are consistent with prior research indicating
that molybdenum and chromium can support vegetation growth by fulfilling essential physiological
roles in plants (Fuentes-Arderiu 2013; Arif et al. 2016). However, it is noteworthy that excessive
Cr concentrations have been reported to exert toxic effects on plants, highlighting the importance
of maintaining optimal levels of this metal to avoid detrimental outcomes. These findings
underscore the complex interplay between metal concentrations and vegetation health,
emphasizing the need for further investigation into threshold levels at which metals transition from

being beneficial to harmful. Understanding these dynamics is critical for developing effective
strategies to manage metal contamination in ecosystems and mitigate its impact on vegetation.
The findings of this study have significant implications for environmental monitoring, regulatory
decision-making, and sustainable management of industrial waste sites. By demonstrating the
effectiveness of Sentinel-2 imagery in detecting vegetation stress associated with metal
contamination, our approach provides a scalable, cost-effective solution for continuous
surveillance of vulnerable ecosystems. This is particularly critical where ground-based monitoring
may be logistically challenging or economically prohibitive. Moreover, the integration of vegetation
indices and contamination data offers actionable insights for environmental regulators and site
managers to prioritize remediation efforts, especially in high-risk areas such as unlined ash
impoundments. Beyond the coal ash context, this framework can be adapted to monitor
vegetation health in a variety of contaminated environments, including mining sites, petrochemical
facilities, and other areas impacted by industrial activities. As global emphasis on environmental
accountability grows, such remote sensing-based frameworks will become indispensable tools for
proactive ecosystem management and policy enforcement.
While Sentinel-2 imagery provided valuable insights into regional vegetation stress patterns, its
moderate spatial resolution (10–20 m) limits the ability to detect fine-scale contamination
gradients and subtle vegetation stress signals. Integrating higher-resolution hyperspectral
sensors could enhance detection accuracy by capturing more detailed spectral responses,
particularly in the red-edge and infrared regions. Furthermore, future work can explore the fusion
of spectral reflectance data from multiple sensors to improve spatial, spectral, and temporal
resolution for detecting vegetation stress and contamination patterns. Moreover, the absence of
extensive ground-truth data in this study constrains the validation of remote sensing outputs.
Incorporating in-situ physiological measurements, such as leaf chlorophyll content, nutrient
status, water potential, and photosynthetic efficiency in future research would strengthen the
relationship between spectral responses and accurate plant health status, providing critical
validation for remote observations. While the Ashtracker groundwater dataset from monitoring
wells used in the study provided valuable contaminant concentration data, it lacked detailed
documentation on sampling strategies. Future studies should aim to integrate more
comprehensive field monitoring data, including standardized sampling protocols to capture
dynamic contamination events, such as accidental spills or seasonal leaching patterns and
temporal consistency, to improve cross-validation of satellite-based assessments. Additionally,
this study could not fully differentiate the contribution of individual contaminants to vegetation
stress, as multiple heavy metals were present simultaneously. Although spatial correlations were
observed between CSTV of VIs/BPIs values and specific contaminants, confirming more robust
relationships requires more controlled experiments or finer-resolution soil and hydrological
analyses. Integrating detailed soil chemistry, subsurface geology, hydrological flow models, and
even nutrient-metal interaction studies would enhance understanding of contaminant transport
mechanisms and stress causation. And, the assessment was conducted entirely remotely, without
direct field access to the study area. As a result, important environmental variables such as plant
species composition, subsurface conditions, and soil characteristics remain unknown. These
factors influence vegetation stress responses and spectral signatures. Future research can
incorporate such environmental data to refine stress attribution, improve model accuracy, and
account for site-specific ecological variability. Moreover, the robustness of spectral stress
detection models is sensitive to various technical and environmental factors, including sensor
characteristics, atmospheric conditions, camera settings, and data availability, data accuracy and

data preprocessing methods. Addressing these variables through rigorous calibration, advanced
preprocessing, and robust validation protocols will be critical to enhancing the reliability and
generalizability of the approach.
Finally, while this study focused on a coal ash contamination site, expanding the framework to
include a broader range of contaminated landscapes, such as agricultural lands, mining areas,
petrochemical waste zones, or industrial wastewater-impacted ecosystems, would test the
adaptability and robustness of the methodology. Applying this approach across diverse sites with
different contamination profiles and environmental conditions will provide a more comprehensive
understanding of vegetation stress responses to varied industrial waste exposures, improving its
applicability for large-scale environmental monitoring and decision support systems.
2.4. Conclusion
This study advances the application of remote sensing methodologies for monitoring
contamination, specifically focusing on detecting leaching metal contamination from coal-fired
power plants. By integrating Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery with groundwater metal
concentration data, we present a cost-effective, scalable framework for detecting and quantifying
vegetation stress induced by leaching metal contamination. Our approach demonstrates phyto-
identification, using plant stress responses as a proxy indicator to map and quantify areas
potentially affected by metal contamination covered by vegetation, demonstrating the
effectiveness of vegetation as a natural biosensor for environmental monitoring. By integrating
both near-daily and yearly time series analysis from 2019 to 2023, our work provides new insights
into the spatio-temporal dynamics of vegetation stress, an area that has been underexplored in
remote sensing literature. The utilization of GEE-based tools enabled efficient, large-scale spatio-
temporal processing, showcasing the potential of cloud-based platforms for environmental
studies. Spatial correlations between vegetation stress and concentrations of leached metals are
analyzed such as arsenic, cadmium, and cobalt, while also identifying the mitigating roles of
molybdenum and chromium, thereby contributing insights into nutrient-metal interactions in
stressed ecosystems. Our comparative assessment of vegetation indices and biophysical
parameters further refines understanding of index-specific capabilities under contamination
induced stress. Additionally, the study provides policy-relevant evidence demonstrating that
unlined coal ash impoundments significantly exacerbate vegetation stress, supporting regulatory
initiatives toward mandatory lining of such facilities. Finally, this study highlights the utility of
Sentinel-2 as a cost-effective, scalable monitoring tool, providing a reproducible methodology
applicable to environmental assessments anywhere within any spatio-temporal extent.

Supervised Machine Learning Approach for Phyto-Identification of
Leached-Metals Contamination

3.1. Abstract
Coal-fired power plants with legacy coal ash impoundments and landfills pose a persistent
environmental risk due to the leaching of toxic metals into surrounding ecosystems. This study
presents an integrated remote-sensing and supervised machine learning framework for detecting
and mapping vegetation stress linked to leached-metal contamination and assesses its
performance across four legacy coal combustion residuals impacted sites in Virginia, U.S. Multi-
temporal Sentinel-2 imagery (2019–2023) was combined with groundwater metal concentrations,
six vegetation indices (VIs), three biophysical parameters indices (BPIs), spatio-temporal
vegetation dynamics and ancillary environmental variables including topographic, hydrological,
climatic, and soil attributes to characterize physiological responses to leached-metal exposure.
Six supervised classifiers namely Classification and Regression Tree (CART), Random Forest
(RF), Gradient Tree Boosting (GTB), Support Vector Machine (SVM), k-Nearest Neighbor (KNN),
and Naïve Bayes (NB) were evaluated, with ensemble tree-based models showing superior
performance for stress detection. RF was the highest-performing model across all sites (Accuracy
= 0.96–0.98; Kappa = 0.92–0.95; F1-score ≥ 0.96), followed by CART, KNN, and GTB. The RF
model accurately mapped multi-metal contamination at Site A (As, Co, Mo, Pb), with balanced
precision and recall (>0.95) for both stressed and non-stressed vegetation. Feature-importance
analysis across sites demonstrated that soil attributes, climatic variables, and hydrological–
topographic metrics were the strongest predictors of metal-induced stress, outperforming
standalone spectral indices and CSTV features. Cross-site transfer tests showed partial
generalization: the Site A trained RF model captured As, Mo and Pb-induced stress patterns at
Site B but performed poorly for Co requiring site-specific local calibration. The results demonstrate
that vegetation spectral responses serve as sensitive early indicators of leached-metal
contamination, and supervised machine learning models especially RF model, substantially
enhance detection accuracy compared with CSTV-based approach. The proposed framework
offers a scalable, cost-effective, and transferable approach for monitoring vegetation stress
associated with leached-metal contamination, enabling large-scale environmental monitoring and
assessment of hazardous metal leaching in legacy industrial landscapes.

4.1. Introduction
The global reliance on coal-fired power has resulted in an immense environmental liability:
millions of tons of coal combustion residuals (CCR) disposed of or stored in surface
impoundments and landfills (Ruhl et al. 2012; Harkness et al. 2016). CCRs contain elevated
concentrations of organic pollutants and heavy metals/metalloids (HMs) which can be mobilized
under variable hydrological and geochemical conditions, and contaminate surrounding
environment posing chronic risks to human health, ecological and aquatic systems (Dreesen et
al. 1977; Meij and te Winkel 2007; Tchounwou et al. 2012; Izquierdo and Querol 2012; Swanson
et al. 2013; Lauer et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2022). Studies at U.S. coal-ash impoundments and
landfills have documented CCR leachates with metal concentrations frequently exceeding federal
advisory guidelines (Rowe et al. 2002; Ruhl et al. 2010, 2012; Yang et al. 2015; Vengosh et al.
2019; Deonarine et al. 2023). Despite requiring lined landfills and leachate collection systems

intended to ensure environmental safety, most of the U.S. coal-fired power plants show unsafe
levels of HMs in monitoring wells, largely due to decades of waste stored in older unlined landfills
(US EPA 2015; Russ et al. 2019). HMs contamination from CCRs generally progresses gradually
through continuous leaching from unlined or unremedied impoundments, and because these
metals are non-degradable, they persist in groundwater and soil for long periods after disposal
(Adriano et al. 2004; Bolan et al. 2014; Brandt et al. 2017; Petrović and Fiket 2022; Padhye et al.
2023).
The established regulatory framework for monitoring CCR sites is based on a strategy of direct,
point-based sampling which relies on a network of monitoring wells to detect and characterize
contaminant releases (US EPA 2015). While these methods provide high analytical precision,
they are inherently constrained by their limited spatial and temporal resolution, capturing only
discrete locations and infrequent sampling intervals (Chapman et al., 2003). The costs and labor
associated with installing, maintaining, and regularly sampling a comprehensive network of wells
are substantial. These limitations underscore the need for complementary cost-effective,
scalable, and non-invasive methods capable of providing continuous environmental surveillance
over large and often inaccessible areas, to better guide regulatory oversight, risk assessment,
and remediation prioritization.
Vegetation serves as a powerful bioindicator for subsurface contamination, as it integrates and
expresses biophysical and biochemical responses to environmental stressors (Adriano et al.
2004; Gholizadeh and Kopačková 2019). The uptake of heavy metals from contaminated soil and
water disrupts essential plant physiological functions, such as photosynthesis, pigment synthesis,
and water transport and this interference results in observable stress responses such as
chlorosis, reduced chlorophyll content, and structural damage to the canopy, all of which alter the
plant's spectral reflectance properties in the visible, red-edge, and near-infrared (NIR) portions of
the electromagnetic spectrum (Rout and Das 2009; Rai et al. 2019; Sperdouli 2022; Vasilachi et
al. 2023a; Bonfiglio et al. 2024; Lovynska et al. 2024).
These spectral changes can be quantified using a variety of vegetation indices derived from
satellite imagery (Huete 1988; Dash and Curran 2004; Mishra and Mishra 2012; D’Emilio et al.
2012; Li et al. 2015b). In parallel, biophysical parameters such as Leaf Area Index (LAI) and Leaf
Chlorophyll Content (LCC), often derived through radiative transfer modeling, provide direct
metrics of canopy structure and physiological status (Gitelson and Merzlyak 1994; Barnes et al.
2000; Gitelson et al. 2003; Buitrago Acevedo et al. 2017).
The Sentinel-2 satellite mission, with its unique combination of 13 spectral bands, high spatial
resolution (10–20 m), and frequent revisit times, is exceptionally well-suited for detecting subtle
signals of vegetation stress (Zarco-Tejada et al. 2019; Shukla et al. 2019; Soydan et al. 2021;
Plutalova et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2023a; Guo et al. 2024). Its strategically placed red-edge bands
(centered around 705 nm, 740 nm, and 783 nm) are highly sensitive to variations in chlorophyll
concentration and can reveal stress gradients that may be invisible in other spectral regions
(Clevers et al. 2004; Eitel et al. 2011; Clevers and Gitelson 2013). Numerous studies have
successfully demonstrated correlations between heavy metal concentrations in soil and
vegetation spectral responses in mining and industrial landscapes (Götze et al. 2016; Jin et al.
2017; Zhang et al. 2018, 2019; Liu et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2022; Noor et al. 2022). However,
these relationships are frequently site-specific and nonlinear, reflecting the complex interplay of
factors like soil texture, moisture, topography, and climate that co-determine plant stress.

Machine learning (ML) provides a powerful, data-driven framework for modeling the complex,
nonlinear relationships between environmental variables and vegetation health (Gao et al. 2020;
van Klompenburg et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2025). Supervised algorithms,
including Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), Gradient Boosting have been
successfully applied to map heavy metal contamination in soils and sediments by integrating
spectral information with environmental covariates to characterize soil and vegetation responses
to toxic metals (Jerome H. Friedman 2001; Rumpf et al. 2010; Mountrakis et al. 2011; Rodriguez-
Galiano et al. 2012; Belgiu and Drăguţ 2016; Thanh Noi and Kappas 2017; Tan et al. 2020; Fu et
al. 2024). Moreover, Li et al. (2025) combined multi-source remote sensing (Landsat 8, Sentinel-
1/2), terrain factors, and ensemble ML to develop multi-target inversion models for six metals (Cr,
Zn, As, Cd, Hg, Pb) in both soils and crops. Yu et al. (2023) similarly integrated Sentinel-2A
imagery with spectral, topographic, and spatial features to map Pb and Cd using ensemble
learning. At the watershed scale, Wang et al. (2020) modeled ecological risk by linking NDVI and
environmental drivers, while Wang et al. (2023) fused ground and airborne hyperspectral data
with SVM and deep neural networks to predict soil heavy-metal concentrations. In agricultural
landscapes, Zhang et al. (2022) demonstrated that Sentinel-2 time-series features especially red-
edge indices combined with deep learning model can detect heavy-metal stress in rice. At finer
scales, Salgado et al. (2023) used UAV multispectral imagery and random forest model to map
Hg and As in soil–plant systems, showing substantial improvements over conventional
assessment approaches. Together, these studies reflect a rapidly expanding body of work
showing that combining remote sensing with advanced data-driven ML models enables reliable
quantification and monitoring of heavy-metal contamination across diverse environmental
contexts.
Critical knowledge gaps persist in linking vegetation stress, multi-year temporal dynamics, and
subsurface leached-metal contamination across heterogeneous landscapes. These include the
lack of reproducible multi-site frameworks, limited integration of environmental drivers such as
soil, hydrology, topography and climate, and insufficient evaluation of temporal consistency
needed to separate persistent contamination from transient environmental variability. To address
these challenges, this study presents a supervised machine learning driven phyto-identification
framework that leverages multi-temporal Sentinel-2 imagery, vegetation indices, biophysical
parameters, and ancillary environmental features to detect leached metal contamination-induced
vegetation stress. The approach evaluates six supervised ML classifiers, quantifies feature
importance across metal types and environmental conditions, and examines cross-site
transferability using multi-year spatiotemporal vegetation dynamics and model transferability
assessment.
This research makes several key contributions. First, it provides a comprehensive multi-site
analysis showing that vegetation stress linked to CCR-derived contamination manifests
consistently across sites with spectral indices sensitive to structural and chlorophyll degradation.
Second, it incorporates long-term CSTV metrics to identify persistent stress signals. Third, it
demonstrates the superior performance of Random Forest classifier for multi-metal stress
detection and quantifies environmental feature groups particularly soil, climate, and hydrology as
dominant predictors of contamination-induced stress. Finally, it evaluates cross-site transferability
and highlights limitations due to environmental and geochemical heterogeneity, pointing toward
the need for domain-adaptive ML methods. Collectively, this work establishes a scalable, low-
cost, and operationally reproducible framework for large-scale detection of vegetation stress

associated with metal contamination, providing valuable insights for groundwater monitoring,
ecological risk assessment, and regulatory decision-making in legacy coal-ash landscapes.

3.2. Materials and Methods
Methodology Overview
A comprehensive, reproducible framework (Figure 2) was developed to detect and map metal-
induced vegetation stress by integrating multi-source satellite remote sensing datasets with
supervised machine-learning models. The pipeline integrates (1) multi-source data collection of
multispectral sentinel-2 imagery and ancillary environmental variables data through Google Earth
Engine; (2) data preprocessing, including cloud masking, region-of-interest and datetime filtering;
(3) feature engineering, combining sentinel-2 bands reflectance, spectral indices (VIs, BPIs),
temporal metrics (CSTV, rate of change), and environmental covariates (soil, hydrology,
topography, climate); (4) target labeling using leached-metal concentration thresholds; and (5)
feature stacking into a unified multi-band dataset. Pixel values are then extracted for (6)
training/testing, followed by (7) supervised ML classification model training. After classification,
(8) model evaluation is performed using accuracy, Kappa, precision, recall, and F1-score metrics.
The best-performing model is then used for (9) model transfer to a new site, (10) transferability
assessment, and (11) mapping and visualization of metal-induced vegetation stress. This
workflow enables scalable, repeatable contamination monitoring across heterogeneous CCR-
contaminated landscapes.

Data Collection
Multi-source datasets were acquired through GEE to represent vegetation, soil, hydrological,
and climatic conditions over the four coal-ash sites in Virginia.
Spectral Data
Multispectral imagery was acquired from the Sentinel-2 Multispectral Instrument (MSI)
constellation. We utilized Level-2A surface reflectance products, which are atmospherically
corrected and provide data across 12 spectral bands at 10–20-meter spatial resolution with a
nominal 5-day revisit cycle (ESA 2022).
Soil Data
Key soil physicochemical properties were sourced from the SoilGrids v2.0 global soil information
system. This dataset, produced by ISRIC–World Soil Information, provides global predictions for
properties such as pH, texture fractions (sand, silt, and clay) at a 250-meter resolution (Turek et
al. 2023). And soil moisture dataset is produced by NASA Global Land Data Assimilation System
(Rodell et al. 2004)
Topographic Data
Digital elevation data were obtained from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM), which
provides near-global, high-resolution topographic information. The specific dataset used is the
void-filled SRTM V3 global 1-arc-second product, which has been processed to remove data gaps
and improve vertical accuracy (Farr et al. 2007). From this DEM, we derived primary topographic
attributes, including elevation, slope, and curvature.
Hydrological Data
For hydrological analysis, we used the MERIT-Hydro global hydrography dataset, which
combines elevation data with water body information to produce high-accuracy hydrographic
features (Yamazaki et al. 2019). From this, we computed the Topographic Wetness Index (TWI)
height above nearest drainage (HAND) and upstream drainage area.
Climatic Data
Daily daytime Land Surface Temperature was obtained from the Moderate Resolution Imaging
Spectroradiometer (MODIS) aboard the Terra satellite, using the MOD11A1 Version 6.1 product.
This dataset provides temperature data at a 1 km spatial resolution (Wan 2021). The values were
converted to degrees Celsius for analysis. And near-surface specific humidity was sourced from
the North American Land Data Assimilation System (NLDAS-2) Forcing A dataset(Parsons et al.
2010).
Monitoring wells Data
Ground-truth data for model training and validation consisted of in-situ measurements of metal
and metalloid concentrations from groundwater monitoring wells. These records, which include
concentrations of leaching metals and the publicly available Ashtracker database, which is
maintained by the Environmental Integrity Project (2024).
Data preprocessing
Preprocessing involved cloud and cirrus masking using the QA60 band, and filtering by region of
interest (ROI), date (2019–2023), and cloud cover (< 30 %). Median monthly composites were

generated to reduce temporal noise. Each dataset was resampled to a uniform 20 m grid (WGS
84 / UTM Zone 17 N) and spatially aligned across layers.

Feature engineering

Multiple categories of predictor variables were generated to characterize vegetation condition,
environmental gradients, and site-specific factors influencing metal-leaching dynamics. All
vegetation-based spectral products including vegetation indices (VIs), biophysical parameters
(BPIs), and Coefficient of Spatio-Temporal Variation (CSTV) were derived following the
methodological framework established in Karim et al. (submitted) (2025).

 - Spectral features: Sentinel-2 MSI reflectance bands and derived vegetation indices (e.g.,
 NDVI, EVI, SAVI, CIRE, MTCI) and biophysical parameters (CCC, LAI, LCC), computed
 using the same formulation and workflow described in Karim et al. (submitted)
 - Temporal features: Multi-year CSTV layers for both VIs and BPIs, seasonal median
 composites, and rate-of-change metrics capturing interannual vegetation dynamics, all
 derived using the CSTV framework introduced by Karim et al. (submitted)
 - Soil features: Soil pH, moisture, and texture fractions (sand, silt, clay), sourced from
 SoilGrids v2.0 and filtered using datetime and ROI.
 - Topographic features: Elevation, slope, aspect, and curvature derived from the SRTM
 V3 global 1-arc-second DEM product.
 - Hydrological features: TWI, HAND, upstream contributing area, and precipitation
 gradients.
 - Climatic features: Mean temperature and humidity were also incorporated to account for
 meteorological influences.

Collectively, these spectral, temporal, soil, topographic, hydrological, and climatic predictors form
an integrated feature space designed to capture the complex interactions governing vegetation
stress responses to subsurface leached-metal contamination.

Target feature labeling and binary classification

Metal concentration measurements from groundwater monitoring wells were first transformed into
continuous spatial surfaces using ordinary kriging, a geostatistical interpolation method that
accounts for spatial autocorrelation in observed concentrations. This procedure generated a high-
resolution concentration map for each metal across the study area, enabling pixel-level
assignment of contamination status even where monitoring wells were sparse.

Each interpolated concentration surface was subsequently converted into a binary contamination
map based on established U.S. EPA Maximum Contaminant Levels (MCLs) and relevant federal
health-advisory thresholds. For each metal, concentrations exceeding the regulatory threshold
(e.g., Pb > 0.015 mg/L, As >0.01 mg/L, Co >0.006 mg/L, Mo > 0.04 mg/L) were labeled as 1
(stressed/contaminated), while concentrations at or below the threshold were labeled as 0 (non-
stressed). This threshold-based classification provides a consistent and policy-relevant indicator
of contamination severity across all four study sites.

Feature stacking, data sampling and train-test split

All spatial predictor feature layers including spectral reflectance bands, vegetation indices,
biophysical parameters, soil attributes, topographic-hydrological and climatic variables were
merged into a unified and integrated multi-band feature composite for each study site. The labeled
binary contamination map for each site was incorporated directly into the feature stack, ensuring
that each pixel record contained both its predictor variables and its contamination status. This
unified dataset, linking vegetation condition, environmental gradients, and groundwater metal
concentrations, provided the foundation for training and evaluating the supervised machine-
learning models. To ensure robust model training while preventing spatial overfitting, the dataset
was partitioned using a 70% training and 30% testing split, based on the total number of valid
vegetation pixels of 20m resolution within each site. This means that 70% of all pixels across the
entire study area were used to train the supervised machine-learning classifiers, providing a large
and diverse sample capturing the full range of vegetation conditions, contamination gradients,
and environmental heterogeneity. The remaining 30% of pixels which the model had never seen
during training served as an independent testing set for unbiased accuracy assessment.

Supervised machine learning models training

To classify vegetation stress associated with leached-metal contamination, six supervised
machine-learning algorithms available natively in GEE were implemented: Classification and
Regression Tree (CART), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Tree Boosting (GTB), Support Vector
Machine (SVM), k-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), and Naïve Bayes (NB). These models represent a
diverse set of learning algorithms: tree-based, ensemble-based, probabilistic, distance-based,
and margin-based, allowing robust comparison across heterogeneous spectral and
environmental conditions.

CART provides a baseline through binary recursive partitioning and rule-based tree construction
(Breiman et al. 1984). RF extends this concept by aggregating multiple decorrelated trees,
reducing variance and improving generalization in high-dimensional spectral–environmental
feature spaces (Breiman 2001). GTB employs stage-wise additive modeling and gradient
optimization to capture complex nonlinear interactions among predictors (Jerome H. Friedman
2001). The SVM classifier, implemented via LIBSVM in GEE, uses a radial basis function (RBF)
kernel to derive nonlinear decision boundaries in multi-dimensional feature space (Cortes and
Vapnik 1995; Chang and Lin 2011). KNN provides a non-parametric, instance-based approach
that classifies pixels according to local proximity in feature space (Cover and Hart 1967). Naïve
Bayes offers a probabilistic benchmark based on class-conditional independence assumptions
(Domingos and Pazzani 1997). Together, these classifiers enable a comprehensive assessment
of supervised machine-learning performance for detecting vegetation stress linked with leached-
metal contamination. All models were trained on the standardized feature stack and the resulting
classifiers produced binary maps (class 1 = stressed/contaminated, class 0 = non-stressed) that
formed the basis for model evaluation, comparison, and spatial classification prediction.

Model Evaluation

Model performance was rigorously evaluated using five complementary accuracy-assessment
metrics that capture both overall agreement and class-specific discrimination ability. Overall
Accuracy provides the proportion of correctly classified pixels across the entire test dataset,
offering a general measure of predictive success. However, because accuracy can be inflated
when one class is dominant, we also computed Cohen’s Kappa (κ), which quantifies the level of

agreement between predicted and observed classes after adjusting for agreement expected by
random chance. Kappa values approaching 1.0 indicate strong, non-random correspondence
between model outputs and ground-truth contamination labels.

F1-score provides a single, balanced metric reflecting both false positives and false negatives.
This is particularly important for contamination-mapping applications, where misclassification of
contaminated areas may pose environmental and human-health risks.

Together, these metrics offer a comprehensive evaluation of model performance, ensuring that
the selected classifier is not only accurate overall but also reliable in distinguishing subtle spectral
stress patterns associated with metal contamination.

Model Selection and Transfer
The best performing classification model was then evaluated and transferred across sites to test
spatial robustness. For each site, the model was applied pixel-wise to generate probability maps
of contamination-induced vegetation stress. Transfer accuracy was assessed using independent
site data, maintaining the same thresholding criteria.
Mapping and Visualization
The classified stress maps were visualized using color-coded symbology to highlight gradients of
contamination-induced vegetation stress. Additionally, classified stress regions were overlaid with
kriged metal-concentration surfaces to enable spatial validation and assess correspondence
between vegetation stress patterns and underlying contamination levels. This GEE-based
visualization workflow provided an interactive and reproducible environment for examining spatial
patterns across sites and evaluating model performance.

Spatiotemporal vegetation dynamics
Relationships between Vegetation Indices and Biophysical Parameters

Correlation analysis between vegetation indices and biophysical parameters in revealed
strong and consistent linkages across all four sites, indicating that metal-induced
vegetation stress produces coherent physiological and structural responses detectable
through NIR and red-edge associated indices. When analyzed collectively, the site-

indices sensitive to leaf/canopy structure and chlorophyll content, particularly SAVI, CIRE,
NDVI, and NDRE1, whereas MTCI and NDRE2 consistently exhibited low diagnostic
value.

By contrast, MTCI and NDRE2 consistently showed weak performance across sites and BPIs
(mean R² <0.47 and <0.20, respectively).

These findings reveal several key ecological and spectral insights. First, vegetation indices that
incorporate NIR and red-edge sensitivity are reliable for detecting stress linked to leached metal
exposure, because they capture both chlorophyll depletion, structural: leaf area and canopy
decrease, two prominent symptoms of heavy metals contamination. Second, LAI consistently
displayed the strongest correlations, indicating that canopy density responds more strongly and
uniformly to heavy-metal-induced stress than chlorophyll concentration alone. Third, the
similarities between site-level and aggregated correlation coefficients highlight the robustness
and transferability of these spectral-biophysical relationships across diverse environmental
conditions. Sites such as B and C, which feature flat terrain and comparatively uniform vegetation
cover, yielded higher correlations overall, while Site A and D had slightly lower values due to

terrain shadows, tidal water influence, and vegetation heterogeneity; nevertheless, the ranking of
VIs remained nearly unchanged, reinforcing the fundamental nature of these spectral responses.
Together, these findings demonstrate that metals contamination-induced vegetation stress
manifests as predictable changes in leaf or canopy structure and pigment content, and that SAVI,
CIRE, NDVI and NDRE1 provide the strongest, most generalizable spectral indicators of these
responses.

2 indicates abrupt stress, CSTV = 0 indicates no stress, and CSTV values close to 1 denotes
persistent but stable stress. Figure 5(bottom-left) plots the percentage of vegetation cover in each
site and the bar chart in Figure 5(bottom-right) quantifies the percentage of vegetation stress
detected from 2019 to 2023 exhibiting multi-year stress, while the CSTV maps in Figure 5 (top)
illustrate the spatial distribution of these stress responses.

VI/BPI and the bar chart (bottom right) summarizes overall vegetation cover within each site.

Across all sites, biophysical parameters (CCC, LCC, LAI) consistently exhibit the highest
proportions of stressed vegetation ranging from 74% to 84%, indicating strong temporal

fluctuations in chlorophyll content and canopy structure from 2019 to 2023. This alignment
confirms that chlorophyll degradation and canopy thinning are the most temporally sensitive and
reliable indicators of contamination-driven stress.
Among vegetation indices, CIRE, SAVI, NDRE1&2 and NDVI show strong CSTV-derived stress
proportions (generally in 62% to 84% range) and spatial patterns that closely mirror those of BPIs.
Their CSTV maps reveal well-defined stress patches capturing multi-year physiological
responses. However, MTCI, consistent with its low sensitivity in correlation analyses, displays
minimal CSTV response across all sites (<26%) and its CSTV maps remain largely green,
indicating little multi-year variation and reinforcing its limited diagnostic value for gradual, chronic
stress typically associated with leached-metal exposure.
Collectively, spatial maps and quantitative results provide a coherent multi-year perspective on
contamination-driven vegetation stress. These findings underscore the utility of the CSTV metric
for identifying both abrupt and persistent stress signatures over multi-year periods and
demonstrate its effectiveness in pinpointing vegetation stress linked to leached metal
contamination across diverse environmental settings.
Performance of supervised machine learning models across study sites

The six supervised classification algorithms: RF, CART, KNN, GTB, SVM, and NB showed clear
differences in their ability to detect Pb-induced vegetation stress across the four study sites
(Figure 6). Overall, ensemble tree-based methods demonstrated superior and more stable
performance, consistent with their widely documented effectiveness in remote-sensing
classification tasks where input features are high-dimensional, nonlinear, and multi-source
(Breiman 2001; Gislason et al. 2006; Belgiu and Drăguţ 2016).

Figure 16: Overall classification accuracy of six supervised machine-learning models: Random Forest (RF),
Classification and Regression Tree (CART), k-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), Gradient Tree Boosting (GTB),
Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Naive Bayes (NB) applied to Pb-induced vegetation stress detection
across four study sites (A–D). Bar colors represent site-specific accuracy, while the purple line denotes the
cross-site average accuracy.

RF demonstrated the highest overall accuracy across all sites, with scores ranging from 0.95 to
0.98 and an average of 0.97. This performance confirms its robustness to noise, multicollinearity,
and nonlinear interactions among spectral, biophysical, soil, climatic, hydrological, and
topographic variables and the success of RF align with extensive evidence of its superior

generalization capability for vegetation stress mapping and environmental monitoring (Rodriguez-
Galiano et al. 2012; Maxwell et al. 2018). CART also performed strongly, achieving accuracies
between 0.95 and 0.97, with an average of 0.96. Although more sensitive to the structure of
training data, single-tree models often provide interpretable decision boundaries and have proven
effective in binary vegetation stress classification (Quinlan 1993). The competitive performance
of CART reflects the strong separability between stressed and non-stressed vegetation in the
lead-labeled feature space. KNN achieved moderate but consistent accuracy, with scores from
0.85 to 0.92 and an average of 0.88. This is in line with literature that notes KNN's sensitivity to
feature scaling, mixed land-cover conditions, and local spectral similarity (Cover and Hart 1967).
GTB exhibited strong performance at two sites with accuracy of 0.95 to 0.97 but had notably lower
accuracy at two other sites (0.72). This site dependency is consistent with GTB's known tendency
to overfit in regions with heterogeneous feature distributions or irregular contamination gradients
(Jerome H. Friedman 2001; Natekin and Knoll 2013). SVM with a radial basis function (RBF)
kernel performed relatively poorly, with accuracy from 0.50 to 0.72 and an average of 0.63.
Although SVMs are powerful for high-dimensional classification (Cortes and Vapnik 1995), they
require careful parameter tuning and are sensitive to class imbalance and high feature correlation.
These are common characteristics in remote-sensing vegetation stress datasets (Mountrakis et
al. 2011). Lower accuracy in certain sites likely reflects the difficulty in constructing optimal
nonlinear decision boundaries in complex environmental conditions. Naïve Bayes (NB) delivered
the lowest performance of all models, with accuracy from 0.56 to 0.61 and an average of 0.59.
This is expected given the model's strong assumption of feature independence (Langley et al.
1992), a condition that is violated in multispectral and environmental datasets where features like
vegetation indices, biophysical parameters, soil moisture, and topography are inherently
correlated.

Overall, the results demonstrate that tree-based ensemble methods (RF and CART) are the most
reliable classifiers for metal contamination-induced stress detection, owing to their ability to model
nonlinearities, capture multivariate interactions, and maintain robustness across diverse
landscape conditions. KNN and GTB offer intermediate utility with site-dependent strengths,
whereas SVM and NB are less suited for contamination-driven vegetation stress mapping in
heterogeneous environments. These findings are consistent with prior remote-sensing studies
highlighting RF and CART as top-performing algorithms for vegetation degradation and pollution
stress detection (Belgiu & Drăguţ, 2016; Maxwell et al., 2018; Forkuor et al., 2020).

Performance of the Random Forest model across study sites
After evaluating all six supervised machine-learning algorithms, the Random Forest (RF) classifier
was selected as the optimal model for Pb contamination detection due to its consistently high
accuracy and robustness across sites. The final RF model achieved excellent performance
metrics at all four study locations (Figure 7), confirming its strong generalization capacity in
heterogeneous contaminated landscapes.

Across Sites A to D, overall accuracy ranged from 0.96 to 0.98, indicating that the RF classifier

Class-specific recall values show that the RF model effectively identified both non-stressed
vegetation (0.96–0.99) and stressed vegetation (0.96–0.98) across all sites. The high recall for

challenging environments with mixed vegetation types, tidal influence, and variable soil moisture
conditions.
Collectively, these results confirm that the RF model is highly effective for Pb contamination
detection across diverse landscapes. Its ability to capture nonlinear interactions among spectral
indices, biophysical parameters, and environmental variables makes it particularly well suited for
remote-sensing-based vegetation stress detection, aligning with previous findings in the literature
(Breiman 2001; Belgiu and Drăguţ 2016; Maxwell et al. 2018). The model’s strong and uniform
performance across the four sites validates its use for operational-scale contamination monitoring
and supports its application in broader, multi-site environmental assessment frameworks.

Limitations, Future Work, and Implications
This study demonstrates the potential of integrating multispectral vegetation metrics,
environmental covariates, and supervised machine learning for detecting leached-metal
contamination; however, several limitations warrant consideration. First, although groundwater
monitoring records span 2010–2019 while Sentinel-2 imagery begins in 2019, creating a partial
temporal mismatch, this does not compromise the overall validity of the approach Heavy metals
released from CCRs migrate slowly and persist in groundwater and soils because they continue
to leach from unlined impoundments and do not readily degrade (Adriano et al. 2004; Bolan et al.
2014). Prior research consistently shows that these contaminants remain stable for long periods,
forming gradually moving plumes that can persist in subsurface environments for years to
decades (Ruhl et al. 2012; Brandt et al. 2017; Petrović and Fiket 2022). Thus, the kriged
concentration maps used here represent chronic contamination conditions appropriately aligned
with vegetation stress signals captured during 2019–2023. A second limitation concerns the
reliance on interpolated surfaces rather than dense point-based sampling, which may smooth
fine-scale heterogeneity in contamination patterns. Third, substantial cross-site environmental
variability including differences in soil texture, hydrology, vegetation composition, and

contamination geometry introduced domain shift that constrained model transferability, as
demonstrated by the Site A to Site B experiment. Fourth, the approach is based primarily on
remote sensing, and vegetation species, phenotypes, and community composition within each
site are not explicitly accounted for. Because different species express metal stress at different
rates and intensities or may mask early stress through morphological or phenological plasticity,
some physiological responses may remain undetected in canopy reflectance signatures.
Additional limitations include the lack of hydrogeological details in publicly available datasets (e.g.,
aquifer stratigraphy, hydraulic conductivity).
Future research should prioritize improving cross-site generalization and transfer accuracy, which
were limited in this study due to substantial domain shifts in environmental, geochemical, and
ecological conditions. One promising direction is the use of advanced supervised learning
methods that can capture more complex interactions between spectral, environmental, and
temporal variables. In addition, semi-supervised learning approaches including self-training, co-
training, and pseudo-labeling could leverage the large volume of unlabeled pixels across sites to
better constrain model decision boundaries and reduce overfitting to site-specific conditions. Deep
learning models offer further potential by harnessing spatial–spectral dependencies and temporal
dynamics in Sentinel-2 imagery that traditional classifiers cannot fully exploit (Zhang et al. 2022).
To enhance transferability across heterogeneous landscapes, domain adaptation techniques
such as adversarial domain adaptation, feature alignment, or fine-tuning with small site-specific
training samples could mitigate discrepancies between source and target domains. Incorporating
multi-sensor fusion integrating hyperspectral (EMIT, PRISMA), radar (Sentinel-1), thermal
(ECOSTRESS), and LiDAR datasets may also strengthen model robustness by capturing
additional physiological, structural, and hydrological signals associated with contamination.
Finally, expanding field validation through systematic sampling of vegetation, soil, and
groundwater would provide high-quality labels for calibrating and evaluating more complex
models, ultimately enabling the development of an operational, transferable, and scalable system
for contamination monitoring across diverse environmental settings.
The implications of this study extend broadly to environmental monitoring, CCR management,
and regulatory decision-making. First, the demonstrated correspondence between CSTV
hotspots, Random Forest–classified stress regions, and contamination gradients confirms that
vegetation acts as a sensitive early-warning indicator of subsurface heavy-metal plumes. Second,
supervised machine learning particularly tree-based ensembles substantially outperforms CSTV
based approach, highlighting the value of integrating environmental covariates with spectral and
biophysical information for contamination detection. Third, while multi-metal classification was
highly successful at Site A, the limited cross-site transferability underscores the necessity of site-
specific calibration or adaptive modeling for reliable implementation across heterogeneous
landscapes. Fourth, the proposed framework offers a scalable, low-cost, and non-invasive
alternative to traditional point-based monitoring, capable of identifying high-risk zones, guiding
targeted sampling, and informing remediation priorities. Collectively, these findings demonstrate
the promise of remote sensing and machine learning for large-scale oversight of legacy coal-ash
sites and support their incorporation into future CCR regulatory frameworks, environmental
surveillance programs, and phytoremediation planning.
Conclusion
This study demonstrates a supervised machine-learning framework for detecting leached-metal
contamination from coal combustion residuals by integrating multi-temporal Sentinel-2 spectral

 reflectance with six vegetation indices, three biophysical parameter indices, multi-year
 spatiotemporal variability metrics and a comprehensive suite of environmental covariates. This
 integrated framework effectively captures subtle yet diagnostically meaningful physiological and
 structural signatures of metal-induced vegetation stress. In particular, the approach showed high
 sensitivity to contamination from As, Co, Mo, and Pb, highlighting its capability to infer subsurface
 contamination patterns through remotely sensed vegetation stress responses.
 Across the four study sites, strong and consistent correlations between key vegetation indices
 (SAVI, NDVI, CIRE, NDRE1) and biophysical parameters (CCC, LCC, LAI) confirmed that heavy-
 metal exposure induces coherent physiological and structural responses. CSTV analysis further
 revealed persistent, multi-year vegetation degradation, with biophysical parameters exhibiting the
 highest spatio-temporal sensitivity. Together, these findings reinforce the utility of vegetation as
 a reliable proxy for chronic leached-metals contamination.
 Among the evaluated supervised classifiers, Random Forest (RF) delivered the most accurate
 and stable performance, achieving overall accuracy ranging 0.96 to 0.98 and balanced precision–
 recall across all sites. The RF model successfully mapped multi-metal stress at Site A, capturing
 physiologically meaningful stress patterns aligned with subsurface concentration gradients.
 These results demonstrate that a single, well-trained RF model can generalize effectively across
 contaminating metals when applied within a site.
 The cross-site transfer experiment provided additional insight into model generalizability. The RF
 model trained at Site A showed partial transferability to Site B, particularly for Pb and Mo, but
 overall performance decreased in response to differences in soil properties, hydrology, vegetation
 composition, and contamination exposure. While the transferred RF model outperformed the
 CSTV based approach for stress detection and mapping by a factor of 2 to 4, its predictive
 reliability remained insufficient for operational decision-making. This highlights the persistent
 challenge of domain shift and the need for site-specific calibration or domain-adaptive learning
 techniques when extending supervised models across heterogeneous landscapes.
 Overall, the findings of this study demonstrate that: (1) vegetation spectral responses provide
 early and sensitive indicators of heavy-metal contamination. (2) supervised machine learning
 models, particularly Random Forest substantially enhances detection accuracy compared with
 CSTV-based approach. (3) multi-metal contamination can be reliably mapped within a single site;
 and (4) cross-site model transferability remains limited without additional harmonization or
 retraining.
 Collectively, these results underscore the potential of remote sensing and machine learning to
 support operational environmental monitoring at CCR-impacted sites and similar contaminated
 landscapes. The framework established here offers a strong foundation for future multi-site
 monitoring framework that integrates long-term vegetation dynamics, environmental variables,
 and contamination phenomena.

Unsupervised Machine Learning Approach for Phyto-Identification of
Leached-Metals Contamination

4.1. Overview
Building upon previous work that used supervised machine learning models to classify leached-
metal-induced vegetation stress, this study explores unsupervised machine learning methods.
The main motivation for moving toward unsupervised learning is to reduce dependence on labeled
contamination data, which are often heterogeneous, or recent data are unavailable across power-
plant sites.
The objective is to determine whether vegetation stress patterns in multispectral imagery along
with ancillary environmental covariates can naturally separate into meaningful clusters that align
with contamination presence.
Here, unsupervised machine learning approach was implemented to detect metal-induced
vegetation stress at Site A using classical KMeans (Sinaga and Yang 2020), Density-based
spatial clustering (DBSCAN)(Ester et al. 1996), Gaussian Mixture Modelling (GMM) (Bilmes 1988)
and deep learning based Autoencoder clustering (Baldi 2012). KMeans achieved the best overall
accuracy (0.66) and class agreement beyond chance, while DBSCAN was highly sensitive to
stressed pixels but produced false positives. When the Site A-trained KMeans model was applied
to Site B for cross-site evaluation, performance decreased (Accuracy = 0.54, Kappa = 0.10),
reflecting differences in vegetation response and environmental characteristics. Results
demonstrate that unsupervised clustering captures stress patterns with reduced labeling needs
but remains sensitive to site-specific domain shifts.
4.2. Study Sites
 - Training and primary evaluation at Site A: Glen Lyn Power Station, Virginia

 - Cross-site model transfer evaluation at Site B: Bremo Power Station, Virginia

Both sites have documented groundwater contamination from coal-ash impoundments
exceeding above safe limits and Pb, As, Co, and Mo are common contaminants in both sites.

Figure 24: Overview of the two study sites used for unsupervised model development and transfer testing.
The maps show the locations of Site A (Glen Lyn Power Station) and Site B (Bremo Power Station) with
coal-ash impoundment boundaries outlined in red.

4.3. Methodology
This study applies an unsupervised learning workflow as illustrated in Figure 1, to detect metal-
induced vegetation stress using Sentinel-2 satellite remote sensing and environmental context
layers. First, areas of interest were defined at Site A (training) and Site B (transfer testing), and
relevant datasets including Sentinel-2 surface reflectance, soil properties, topographical,
hydrological and climate variables were acquired through the Google Earth Engine (GEE)
platform. Next, a comprehensive feature engineering process was performed to enhance stress
signal visibility: cloud-masked spectral imagery was transformed into VI & BPI, temporal change
metrics (yearly rate of change of VI/BPI), and CSTV outlier maps to capture long-term stress
dynamics. These were combined with ancillary environmental variables into a high-dimensional
feature stack representing vegetation condition and the governing geophysical drivers.
Unsupervised models (KMeans, DBSCAN, GMM and Autoencoder) were trained on randomly
sampled feature vectors from Site A without the use of labeled data, enabling natural grouping of
pixels into stress-related classes. The resulting cluster maps were validated against available
ground-truth contamination masks to quantify alignment between spectral clusters and actual
metal exposure using metrics such as Overall Accuracy, Kappa, Precision, Recall and F1-score.
To evaluate spatial transferability, the Site A-trained clustering model was directly applied to Site
B and assessed using independent ground truth. This workflow enables automatic identification
of anomalous, stressed vegetation while significantly reducing dependency on extensive labeled
datasets supporting scalable environmental monitoring across contaminated power-plant sites. 1

Figure 25: Unsupervised machine learning workflow for identifying vegetation stress patterns and evaluating
transferability between leached-metal contaminated power-plant sites.

4.4. Key Results
Among the unsupervised models evaluated, KMeans clustering demonstrated the strongest
overall performance, achieving the highest accuracy (0.66) and Kappa (0.33), indicating a good

balance in identifying both stressed/contaminated and uncontaminated vegetation. This stability
makes KMeans the most reliable choice for operational stress detection at Site A.
DBSCAN, while highly sensitive to stressed vegetation (Recall₁ = 0.99), produced many false
positives by misclassifying not-stressed areas as stressed/contaminated (Recall₀ = 0.29). It is
therefore best suited for situations where missing contamination is more critical than incorrectly
flagging clean zones.
The Autoencoder-based model showed moderate capability, outperforming GMM but still
demonstrating limited ability to detect contaminated vegetation (Recall₁ = 0.33), though its
dimensionality reduction could support noise filtering and future model refinement.
In contrast, GMM performed poorly, with near-zero Kappa (0.02) and extremely low Recall₁ (0.05),
indicating almost random separation of spectral stress patterns.
Across metrics, Recall₁ varied the most, with DBSCAN excelling in capturing contamination while
GMM failed almost entirely, whereas Precision₁ was highest in KMeans and Autoencoder,
showing greater reliability when contaminated pixels are predicted. Only KMeans and DBSCAN
displayed meaningful agreement beyond chance, confirming that clustering stress signatures is
feasible but remains sensitive to model assumptions.

Figure 26: Performance of unsupervised ML models at Site A. KMeans provides the most reliable
operational performance, while DBSCAN may be preferable when minimizing missed contamination is the
priority. GMM fails to meaningfully separate stress signatures.

Model performance was consistently stronger at Site A, where the models were trained, compared
to Site B (transfer site). As shown in Figure 3, among the evaluated metals, Pb exhibited the
strongest discrimination capability, achieving moderate accuracy (0.66) and a fair degree of
agreement beyond chance (Kappa = 0.33). Across all metals, Recall for the uncontaminated class
was higher than for the contaminated class (Recall₀ > Recall₁), indicating that the models more
reliably identify unstressed vegetation than stressed vegetation. However, Precision for the
contaminated class remained reasonably high (0.55–0.73), meaning that when the model does
classify a pixel as stressed/contaminated, it is often correct. Together, these results show that
while the models capture meaningful stress signals at Site A, some high-risk zones are still being
missed due to lower Recall₁.

When the trained model was transferred to Site B, classification performance declined as
illustrated in Figure 5. Overall accuracy dropped, and Kappa values approached zero, suggesting
the model operates near random agreement when confronted with vegetation and environmental
conditions outside the training domain. A strong imbalance emerged: almost all unstressed areas
were correctly classified (Recall₀ > 0.92), while most of the stressed/ contaminated vegetation
was not detected (Recall₁ < 0.18), indicating high false-negative rates. Nonetheless, the

Precision₁ remained high (0.71–0.84), illustrating that predicted contamination remains
trustworthy but incomplete. These findings highlight a significant domain shift between sites,
driven by differences in metals composition, soil properties, hydrology, environmental variables
and ash-leachate behavior, suggesting that vegetation stress signatures caused by heavy metals
are not universally transferable without further domain adaptation measures.

4.5. Next Steps for future work
To enhance the transferability of vegetation-stress prediction models across other coal-ash-
impacted power-plant sites, several methodological improvements are planned and in-progress:
 - Incorporate domain adaptation, such as feature alignment, or adversarial representation
 learning, to reduce spectral and environmental biases between sites.

 - Introduce semi-supervised learning, including pseudo-labeling or iterative self-training, to
 leverage small sets of site-specific ground truth to gradually refine decision boundaries.

 - Balance sensitivity using cost-aware learning, prioritizing the contaminated class to
 reduce false negatives in new sites while maintaining manageable false-positive rates.

 - Use spatial context models to reduce isolated misclassifications and strengthen
 contamination hotspot localization.

 - Expand model evaluation to multiple independent sites to better quantify domain
 variability and support generalizable deployment strategies.

 - Perform uncertainty quantification, such as Monte-Carlo dropout or entropy-based
 confidence mapping, to provide risk-aware decision support for remediation planning.

Improvements of the Unsupervised Approach Compared to the Supervised Framework
Compared to the supervised Random Forest–based framework developed in the previous phase
of this research, the unsupervised learning approach introduced here offers several
methodological and practical advantages for phyto-identification of leached-metal contamination.
First, because unsupervised approach does not rely on metal-specific contamination labels
derived from monitoring wells or kriged concentration maps, it substantially reduces dependence
on sparse, heterogeneous, and often temporally outdated groundwater monitoring well datasets
an important improvement given the limited spatial coverage and lagged sampling typical of CCR-
sites monitoring. This independence enables scalable application to sites where labeled
contamination data are incomplete or unavailable.
Second, the unsupervised models are capable of discovering intrinsic structure within
multispectral vegetation signals and environmental covariates, allowing them to reveal stress-
related clusters that may arise from mixed-metal exposure or early physiological responses not
captured in supervised labels. This enhances the ability to detect subtle or emergent stress
patterns that supervised classifiers may miss due to label bias or kriging-induced smoothing.

 Third, by avoiding reliance on contaminant-specific training labels, the unsupervised framework
 provides a more flexible and exploratory tool for characterizing vegetation anomalies across
 heterogeneous CCR impacted landscapes, where metal mixtures, soil conditions, and
 hydrological behavior vary spatially.
 Fourth, the clustering results demonstrated strong internal consistency at the training site and
 preserved high precision when transferred to a second site, indicating that, despite reduced
 sensitivity under domain shift, predicted contamination hotspots remain credible even without
 retraining.
 Finally, the unsupervised workflow establishes a foundation for semi-supervised and domain-
 adaptation extensions, which can leverage minimal site-specific labels to refine model boundaries
 and improve cross-site generalizability.
 Collectively, these improvements illustrate that unsupervised learning approach provides a
 valuable complement to supervised approaches by enabling label-agnostic stress detection,
 reducing data dependency, and supporting scalable monitoring across diverse coal-ash–
 impacted environments.

Framework for Phytoremediation: A Hyperaccumulator Plant
Recommendation System

5.1. Overview

Coal-ash–impacted power-plant sites contain persistent and multi-metal contamination often
occurring in heterogeneous spatial patterns and influenced by local soil, hydrological, and climatic
conditions. While supervised and unsupervised machine-learning approaches can identify
vegetation stress linked to heavy-metal exposure, additional tools are needed to translate these
stress maps into actionable remediation strategies.

Phytoremediation - using plants to extract, stabilize, or immobilize contaminant offers a low-cost,
sustainable alternative to conventional remediation. However, selecting appropriate plant species
requires a systematic evaluation of contaminant profiles, environmental context, plant traits, and
ecological constraints.

To address this need, we propose a data-driven hyperaccumulator plant recommendation
framework capable of identifying optimal phytoremediation species tailored to site-specific
contamination characteristics and environmental factors. The objective of this chapter is to
develop a quantitative, multi-criteria plant recommendation system that:

 1. Integrates metal contamination profiles derived from groundwater and remote sensing–
 based stress detection.
 2. Incorporates site-specific environmental, geological, and climatic variables.
 3. Leverages a curated phytoremediation plant database (101 species, 81 U.S.-relevant)
 with metadata on:

 - Metals remediated
 - Hyperaccumulation/phytostabilization abilities
 - Soil/pH tolerances
 - Biomass and growth traits
 - USDA hardiness zones
 - Invasiveness / ecological suitability

 4. Recommends optimal plant species or plant combinations for each contamination zone.

Step 5: Functional and Management Scoring

Define additional scores, depending on remediation goals:

 1. Phytoremediation function score 𝑆𝑆func,𝑖𝑖 :
 a. Match between species’ dominant phytoremediation type(s) and the
 management goal (extraction vs stabilization vs risk reduction).
 2. Biomass / growth score 𝑆𝑆bio,𝑖𝑖 :

 a. Higher for species with fast growth, high biomass, and easily harvestable

 3. Ecological/management score 𝑆𝑆eco,𝑖𝑖 :
 a. Bonus for native or low-invasive-risk species.
 b. Penalty for species with high management burden or regulatory constraints.

5.4. Outcomes
Scientific Outcomes

 1. A reproducible recommendation engine linking contamination patterns to
 phytoremediation strategies.
 2. Quantitative understanding of how environmental conditions govern hyperaccumulator
 suitability.
 3. A scalable pipeline applicable to any contaminated site worldwide.

Practical Outcomes

 1. A tool for utilities, regulators, and environmental managers to guide plant-based
 remediation.
 2. Decision support for remediation investment, vegetation restoration, and monitoring.
