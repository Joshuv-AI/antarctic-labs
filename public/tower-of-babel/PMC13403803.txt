Deciding “what” to screen for and “when”: The importance of natural history information
Authors: Jahnelle Jackson, Jonathan S. Berg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGenomic medicine promises to leverage research on human genetic variation to improve health. As technology improves, our increasing ability to determine an individual’s genomic sequence must be matched with increased understanding of underlying mechanisms of disease and how phenotypes arise because of genetic variation. Knowledge about the etiology of rare monogenic conditions can be leveraged for medical and public health purposes through diagnostic testing and predictive screening. However, policy decisions about the use of genetic and genomic analysis in a predictive setting must consider the penetrance and expressivity of each monogenic condition and how these factors influence the timing of genomic screening and its potential benefits and harms. This means that high-quality data are required on the nature of genetic diseases and how they impact human health across populations. The objective of this paper is to address the inherent challenges in determining the onset of symptoms for rare monogenic conditions and underscore the importance of curating natural history data. By elucidating the natural progression of rare monogenic conditions, we can optimize screening strategies and guide clinical decision-making. Detailed information about natural history is critical to understanding the optimal timing of population screening and subsequent intervention for any given condition.

Background and rationale

Rare diseases: Individually rare but collectively important to public health

Genomic medicine offers the promise of elucidating the underlying etiology of many individuals with rare diseases, thus enabling individualized management of the patient and their family members. Rare diseases have been defined as affecting <1:2,000 individuals,1,2 but despite their low individual prevalence, they collectively contribute to about 300 million patients globally.3 Conditions that have an etiology involving variation in a single gene or functional genomic locus are referred to as monogenic conditions. At least half of these disease entities have symptoms that emerge early in life,3,4,5 often under age 5. The Orphanet database contains over 6,000 rare diseases, the majority of which (71.9%) have a genetic etiology. Of the conditions, information on the age of onset is available for about 80% of conditions, with 69.9% classified as exclusively pediatric onset.6 Other analyses have indicated that there could be over 10,000 rare diseases.5,7 Thus, there is a high burden of pediatric-onset rare diseases for which prompt diagnosis may be important for patient outcomes.

Childhood-onset monogenic conditions represent a key opportunity to improve early detection and intervention when available. However, to fully realize this potential, we need to understand when symptoms begin, how they progress, and when the best time is for screening and preventive measures. Natural history describes how a condition progresses over time, from the earliest molecular and biochemical changes to overt clinical symptoms and long-term outcomes. Some monogenic conditions primarily affect single organs, which can make their symptoms easier to track and classify in comparison to those in which multiple organ systems could be involved with variable patterns of symptom onset. Multisystem effects can lead to a wide range of symptoms that appear at different ages and progress at different rates, making it difficult to identify a clear age of onset for a given individual or across a cohort of affected individuals. In many cases, the period during which symptoms may be arising and evolving without a defined explanation (the “subclinical” phase) poses a significant challenge to capturing detailed information about the early manifestations of any given monogenic condition. Understanding this variability is essential for accurately describing the penetrance and expressivity of the condition.8

Natural history information has been used to guide clinical trials of novel therapeutic agents for rare diseases,9 but it can also be used to develop optimal approaches to population screening and intervention to prevent poor health outcomes. First, mapping the age of onset can reveal the typical pattern of early symptoms and help determine the best time to initiate surveillance measures, such as laboratory tests and imaging, that may be used to identify developing disease manifestations that would trigger definitive preventive measures. Second, the expected pattern of progression for different features of a monogenic condition can guide management by helping clinicians anticipate disease progression, recognize early signs, and make informed treatment decisions to manage a condition more effectively. This information also helps parents and families know what is potentially in store for individuals with the monogenic condition. Finally, understanding the proportion of individuals who progress to manifest disease symptoms, and at what age, enables screening programs to develop evidence-based plans regarding what conditions to screen for and when. Challenges of predicting disease course after a molecular diagnosis

“All screening programmes do harm; some do good as well, and, of these, some do more good than harm at reasonable cost.”10 Decision-makers who design genomic screening programs need to consider which conditions to include based on characteristics that would favor the benefits and minimize the harm for the population being screened. The ratio of benefit to harm is not constant, however, and this relationship demonstrates a shifting balance.3 One potential harm to any screening program is overdiagnosis. This term has often been used in the context of cancer screening programs (in which an indolent tumor that may never have had an impact on morbidity or mortality is nevertheless treated aggressively, with potentially adverse effects). With monogenic conditions, we are especially concerned about incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, since individuals who are non-penetrant or only ever develop mild symptoms of the condition will not benefit from interventions designed to reduce poor health outcomes stemming from the condition and can only experience the harms or burdens of increased downstream health care.11 Accurate natural history data are critical to enable decision-makers to more evenly weigh the benefits and harms of screening the general population for a given condition. Yet the medical literature is somewhat biased toward studies that include symptomatic individuals, which may have the tendency to overestimate the severity of outcomes; these studies may not include individuals with a molecular diagnosis who have yet to develop symptoms, thus failing to capture the time course during which symptoms would occur across the entire population of individuals with that condition.

Given that incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity are sources of overdiagnosis and potentially overtreatment, it is critical to obtain as accurate a picture as possible for the monogenic conditions being considered for genomic screening. For example, Wilson disease (MIM: 277900) has multi-systemic effects: hepatic, neurological, and psychiatric clinical manifestations, which can occur independently and concomitantly, as well as at different ages across the lifespan. Hepatic manifestations often appear first subclinically with elevated transaminase enzyme levels and/or clinically with acute hepatitis or more severe liver failure, and individuals may or may not develop neurological symptoms such as dysarthria or even other neuropsychiatric manifestations.12,13,14,15 Similarly, Alport syndrome (MIM: 301050, 620536, and 203780) follows a pattern in which kidney manifestations and sensorineural hearing loss can develop independently or concomitantly. Kidney manifestations typically begin with hematuria (microscopic or macroscopic), which can progress to proteinuria, declining kidney function (eGFR), and eventually end-stage renal disease.16,17 Thus, the nature of these diseases makes it difficult to pinpoint when the first true symptom is likely to occur for any given individual. If genetic screening identifies a genetic variant early in life but symptoms do not manifest until later in life (or not at all), this long stretch of time anticipating the potential onset of symptoms could be difficult for patients and families and costly in terms of subsequent follow-up.

The potential for extending targeted genomic screening throughout the lifespan

The challenges in predicting disease onset and progression raise important questions not only about which conditions to screen for but also when screening would provide the greatest and most timely clinical benefit. The substantial ethical and societal barriers to implementing whole-genome sequencing in all newborns18 have led our group and others to investigate opportunities for targeted genomic screening throughout the lifespan. These efforts use targeted screening strategies that support early detection and timely clinical action,19,20,21 reflecting a growing interest in identifying ways to act on severe yet actionable conditions. We previously developed a framework for evaluating clinical actionability and applied this metric to consider what conditions could be included in an extended newborn screen.22 We also utilized the actionability framework to prioritize a small set of conditions that we offered via targeted genetic screening to adults through the GeneScreen project.23 Building on these efforts came additional recommendations by the National Academy of Medicine on the possibility and plausibility of implementing genomics-based screening programs in adults, which suggested an initial focus on a subset of well-studied genes.21 Interest in adult genomic screening has continued to grow, and a number of institutions have begun to deploy genomic screening through research and clinical programs.24 Given the gap between newborn and adult genomic screening, our group proposed that age-based genomic screening (ABGS) could provide opportunities to improve early detection and intervention strategies for rare monogenic conditions throughout childhood rather than only at birth,3 thus taking some of the burden away from newborn screening programs and creating a more optimal connection between the timing of screening and the age of onset or age of intervention for each condition.25

Developing a plausible timeline for genomic screening throughout the lifespan requires accurate natural history information to minimize the time between molecular diagnosis and action. The ABGS program proposes targeted genetic testing panels at clinically relevant time points during pediatric well-childcare visits, balancing the likelihood that individuals with those conditions will begin to develop symptoms and the opportunity to implement guidelines for their presymptomatic management. By aligning opportunities for genomic screening with pediatric well-child visits, ABGS enables detection of conditions closer to when symptoms are likely to appear or when interventions are most effective. However, comprehensive understanding of the natural history of conditions being considered for screening will be crucial to determine the most opportune time point for each condition.

Considerations for improving natural history data for rare diseases

Natural history refers to the typical progression of a condition in the absence of treatment,26 and “in modern times, what is known about the natural history of any disease is constructed largely from observations of affected persons followed over time.”27 Low prevalence and prolonged diagnostic delays limit natural history information about many rare conditions,26 limiting our ability to accurately establish penetrance, expressivity, age of onset, and patterns of disease progression. In addition to sparse data, there are inconsistencies in approaches to collecting phenotypic and clinical outcomes, a lack of consensus on how to define the age of onset of a condition, ascertainment bias in cohort assembly, and unstandardized methods for structuring case-level data, all of which hinder efforts to comprehensively understand natural history.

Age of onset vs. age of diagnosis

Variability in age-of-onset data poses significant challenges, making it crucial to develop standardized methods for data collection and analysis to enhance the reliability and consistency of this information.28 All monogenic diseases can be conceptualized as a risk of developing certain phenotypic and clinical outcomes over time, beginning during prenatal development and extending throughout the lifespan. The age at which specific signs or symptoms first begin is critical for understanding the natural history of a condition. Some of these outcomes are more acutely dramatic or readily observed, while others can be chronic and gradual in their appearance. Symptoms of a monogenic disease may not be initially recognized as such, especially if present early during a patient’s disease course, and the “diagnosis” may only be established after several concerning clinical or phenotypic features are present. The diagnostic process is also iterative, with progression from less specific to more specific diagnoses. For example, a patient’s first seizure might represent the “onset” of an underlying monogenic condition, but they are not formally diagnosed with “epilepsy” until they have recurrent seizures and/or additional imaging or electroencephalographic findings; their epilepsy may be further characterized by location and semiotics, but an etiologic diagnosis of a specific monogenic form of epilepsy may be delayed due to lack of access to a genetic test. Therefore, age of onset often differs from the age at which a formal clinical or etiologic diagnosis is established; this process is confounded by a number of factors, including the trajectory of symptoms, the acuity of concern with which they are brought to medical attention, the ability to access a higher-level care often required for specialized medical tests and other investigations, and potentially the timing of diagnostic genetic testing that reveals the causative variant(s) for a rare monogenic condition.

Age of onset is often not clearly or universally defined in the literature. Clinical reports may conflate age of diagnosis with age of onset if the age parameter of an individual’s first presentation to medical attention is reported as the first time point in a clinical case study. In studies of patients undergoing a diagnostic delay, concerns have been raised about determining the beginning time point (age of onset) and end time point (age of diagnosis), which are necessary to determine the extent of the delay.29 Age of onset may not be explicitly defined or treated as a primary outcome but rather used as a proxy.30,31 In other cases, it is used as a marker and a time point but is not clearly defined as a variable of interest to inform first symptom manifestation. This ambiguity surrounding the definition of the age parameter presents a critical obstacle to interpreting natural history data for rare monogenic disorders. For example, in a study of ATP7B-associated Wilson disease, age of onset is mentioned throughout the analysis but without a definition guiding what constitutes the onset described.12 Other papers separate age of onset and diagnosis,32 which can help clarify timelines but still leaves uncertainty around when specific symptoms first appeared. Such differences underscore the need for standardized definitions to enhance data reliability and facilitate harmonized analyses of case-level data.

Another issue is how age-related data are presented. Current methods that aim to define an age of onset for a given condition typically are for cohorts with larger datasets,33,34,35,36 conditions with higher prevalence, conditions with already well-defined natural history (e.g., sickle cell anemia), and often for psychiatric disorders.37,38 Analyses vary with regard to whether the age of onset is determined by the clinician or the patient,39 potentially resulting in discrepancies about when the condition first manifested. Furthermore, varying degrees of precision (e.g., days, months, or years of age) are utilized across different studies, limiting our ability to aggregate data across studies without creating crude age-group bins. Finally, reporting of cohort-level data such as mean or median values often obscures the natural history pattern and prevents aggregation across studies. For example, the mean and standard deviation can accurately describe normally distributed data but would be the wrong measures to apply to data with a skewed distribution, in which case the median and interquartile range provide a more accurate representation of the shape of the data. When various studies on the same condition present age of onset using different measures of the center and dispersion of the data, they cannot be easily meta-analyzed and require specialized statistical analysis.40

Overall, the heterogeneity in defining and documenting age of onset complicates efforts to accurately pinpoint when a condition manifests and when it is formally diagnosed. In patients diagnosed based on their signs and symptoms, age of onset always precedes age of diagnosis. Therefore, recording the age at which each symptom first manifested, even with potential recollection bias, provides the best estimate of age of onset for retrospective studies. Among individuals first diagnosed through laboratory testing (either via screening or family cascade testing), detailed prospective documentation of signs and symptoms as they develop can provide unbiased natural history data (including the absence of a given feature at the age of observation). Both retrospective and prospective data can be represented in the form of Kaplan-Meier curves, which consider different time-to-event values (e.g., symptom onset) among individuals in a cohort, accounting for missing data or censoring due to their differing ages.41 This form of analysis is arguably the optimal way to represent the age-of-onset data, although as with any study, the sample size clearly impacts the reliability of the results.

Phenotypic variability, nonspecific diagnoses, and incomplete phenotyping

Given the pleiotropic functions of many genes involved in monogenic conditions, symptoms can involve multiple organ systems that emerge at different times throughout life. Syndromic conditions sometimes have recognizable pathognomonic features that can be identified as belonging to a specific condition with only a single known monogenic etiology, but in many cases, an individual may display nonspecific or overlapping features across multiple systems that could be attributable to one of many dozens of different monogenic conditions. Even with a relatively simple phenotype limited to a single organ system, locus heterogeneity can result in numerous distinct monogenic conditions, each with its own age of onset, penetrance, and severity.

Historical reports in the literature may lack genetic testing results, making it difficult to identify which of several potential monogenic conditions a given individual may have. In many clinically ascertained cohorts, patients are given a diagnosis at the level of a broad overarching category (e.g., “Noonan syndrome” or “long-QT syndrome”), without molecular confirmation. While these clinical diagnoses can be useful for characterizing shared features among a group of conditions with locus heterogeneity, they fail to elucidate the natural history of any specific monogenic condition within that category. Although the decreasing cost of genetic testing is now enabling more patients to undergo clinical diagnostic testing, it remains difficult to assemble cohorts with sufficient data for publication, thereby limiting the volume of longitudinal information necessary to fully characterize natural history.

Given the complex nature of many monogenic conditions, with phenotypic features and clinical outcomes arising at different times during the course of disease, certain aspects of a condition may be captured while others are missed. This may be influenced by the specialists a patient sees at different times in their disease course, resulting in evaluations focused on a single body system at a time. Clinical ascertainment thus depends, to a certain extent, on how a condition progresses in any given individual and which specific symptoms prompted the clinical evaluation. In addition, cross-sectional data may fail to capture the evolution of symptoms over time. This makes it harder to build a clear picture of natural history, particularly when early or less obvious symptoms go unrecognized or unrecorded.

Using both clinical and molecular data together gives us a more complete understanding of rare monogenic disorders. While clinical ascertainment provides valuable insights, molecular ascertainment offers a more precise and definitive means of diagnosis, crucial for understanding the genetic underpinnings of conditions and their onset. Integrating both clinical and molecular approaches is essential for enhancing phenotypic characterization and refining age-of-onset data in rare monogenic disorders. It improves our ability to recognize early signs, determine when symptoms begin, and better define the full range of how a condition evolves over time in different individuals.

Biases involving ascertainment and publication

Clinical data are largely derived from cohorts or registries containing affected individuals and reported in manuscripts that provide summarized phenotypic information. These data therefore provide a potentially skewed picture of the natural history of monogenic conditions by focusing on specific features, age groups, or clinical settings. Studies primarily relying on symptomatic presentation for patient inclusion risk underrepresenting milder cases or asymptomatic/non-penetrant individuals, thereby distorting the age-of-onset statistics and overestimating penetrance.11 Studies conducted by specialists may tend to focus on certain organs and provide less complete data on others. Studies conducted exclusively in a pediatric population may overrepresent the earliest expression of a condition, leaving out individuals who remain asymptomatic until adulthood. Studies conducted at academic medical centers or hospitals caring for the sickest individuals may tend to emphasize more complex or severe manifestations. These individuals may also be more prone to enroll in research, making up a higher percentage of a registry population or cohort. Even the well-known biases in publication patterns tend to skew reports toward the extremes to achieve the “novelty” that is often required to justify inclusion in medical literature.

It may seem a fine distinction between whether an individual develops symptoms in their lifetime (penetrance) and which symptoms an affected individual develops and when (expressivity), but it matters greatly to have a complete picture of the natural history of a condition overall. Broader ascertainment efforts spanning age ranges, recruitment strategies involving family history or cascade testing, and true population ascertainment through molecular testing may offer a more balanced representation of disease onset across different monogenic conditions.

Recollection bias/recall bias

Reliance on retrospective data on symptom onset after individuals have been clinically diagnosed introduces biases in recollection. Parents or patients may be asked to provide information about when they first noticed a particular symptom, which is critical for determining the age of onset of the disease, acknowledging that reliance on retrospective data about symptom onset after clinical diagnosis introduces potential for recall bias. Factors such as the severity of symptoms, how much time has passed, and individual experiences can all shape how these moments are remembered. If age of onset or symptom progression is misremembered, it can skew natural history data, leading to incorrect assumptions about when the earliest symptoms are expected to develop.

As a consequence of the current practice of reactive genetic testing, many patients with rare diseases undergo a lengthy diagnostic odyssey involving multiple specialists and tests, during which a definitive genetic diagnosis is obtained long after symptoms become clinically apparent.42 The factors associated with diagnostic delay are complex,42,43 and the prolonged series of evaluations can create difficulties for individuals and their families. Diagnostic delay also has implications for natural history studies, given that early or subclinical manifestations may have gone unrecognized or cannot be accurately recalled by caregivers. Prompt genetic testing that establishes molecular diagnoses early in the course of disease may improve our understanding of natural history by raising greater awareness of early symptoms. Paradoxically, more precise characterization of the early or subclinical manifestations of rare diseases would enable them to be more readily diagnosed through algorithmic detection, further shortening the time to diagnosis. Thus, a more proactive genetic testing strategy early in the course of disease may be especially helpful if coupled with robust data collection early in the course of disease.

Requirement for accessible and structured natural history data

Rare diseases have individually low prevalence overall, which contributes to patient data being “scarce and scattered.”44 The fragmentation of data in rare monogenic disorders highlights the urgent need for comprehensive registries and accessible databases to enhance our understanding of natural disease progression. Accurate understanding of the natural history of diseases is largely based on long-term observations of affected individuals,27 underscoring the need for prospective, longitudinal, and observational studies that are accessible through registries and databases to improve our knowledge and management of these rare diseases. However, for many rare conditions, comprehensive natural history data are often incomplete. Registry data may be limited by the quantity and quality of input,45 but despite these inherent limitations, registries offer invaluable insights into disease trajectories and therapeutic outcomes. Ideal registry data would be based on molecularly confirmed monogenic disease, with unbiased ascertainment, comprehensive phenotyping for all possible symptoms of a condition, and long-term follow-up. When possible, the inclusion of individuals prior to onset of symptoms (e.g., enrollment of the siblings of a proband) would enable more accurate assessment of the earliest onset of symptoms as part of their ongoing surveillance.

One potential solution to these challenges is the analysis of population-wide biobanks such as All of Us and the UK Biobank, which can be used to capture a broad range of participants, samples, and data.46 While these datasets may provide unbiased ascertainment and longitudinal data, the details of phenotypic manifestation may be sparsely captured by electronic health record (EHR) coding or broad surveys intended for common disease, and they may also be depleted for patients with more severe and earlier phenotypes based on enrollment goals. Thus, the dilemma of population-wide biobanks is that they may fail to include patients with a certain rare disease for a number of reasons. Natural history studies for rare disease require deliberate and targeted enrollment strategies designed to identify and retain these individuals in research, tailored to the community of interest and its particular needs. Collaboration with specialty clinics and patient advocacy groups may facilitate outreach, trust building, and sustained engagement of individuals with rare disease for specialized sub-studies that leverage the infrastructure of large biobank studies to improve the completeness and generalizability of natural history data within those resources.

No matter how data are collected, they should be made available in standardized formats such as the PhenoPackets schema being developed by the GA4GH.47 Overcoming the challenges of data scarcity and ensuring broad accessibility are pivotal steps toward advancing research and clinical care in rare monogenic disorders. Data sharing is a key part of this, but in practice, detailed case-level data are often hard to obtain. Sometimes key data elements are not reported in publications, are lost over time, or are not recorded in enough detail. In other cases, the contact author is unreachable, or the dataset is not available outside of the original research group. This creates significant barriers for anyone trying to piece together a reliable picture of a condition’s natural history. Access to larger, well-phenotyped case collections can make it easier to define when symptoms usually begin, how they change over time, and which patterns to look for. Without that, researchers are left to rely on scattered case reports or small cohorts, making it harder to find consistent and reproducible patterns. Building systems that support better data collection, improve sharing, and make data more accessible will be critical to moving forward.

Systems-level considerations for natural history data collection

Other approaches for illuminating the natural history of rare diseases include systematic analysis of EHR data, longitudinal follow-up of patients identified through population screening programs, and dedicated registries. Each of these approaches has pros and cons, and ultimately, a composite picture of natural history will need to integrate all types of disparate information. We will need to address critical questions about how information is collected, aggregated, stored, and analyzed. Clinicians may be in the best position to conduct deep phenotyping, but they are also subject to structures that disincentivize detailed clinical documentation, which may require changes in clinical practice to fully capitalize on expert phenotyping. Although EHR data would be relatively inexpensive to collect, access is also highly controlled due to ethical and legal constraints; fully deidentified EHR data resources could be leveraged to generate longitudinal clinical trajectories but would be limited to available data. Next, individuals identified through population screening programs could also provide insights into the earliest manifestations of many rare diseases, providing evidence to iteratively refine strategies for age-based screening and management guidelines. Finally, dedicated registries offer defined governance, standard operating procedures, data access policies, and patient involvement48 but also require substantial funding to maintain a research team and infrastructure.46,49 While such structures may help distribute the burden of data collection within the current medical system, they are also likely associated with higher costs, highlighting the tension between the quality of data collection and the breadth and depth feasible with different approaches.

Conclusion

Genomic screening throughout the lifespan could become an important component of genomic medicine and public health. Important challenges remain, including the determination of which conditions to screen for and when. Understanding the natural history of rare monogenic conditions will be a critical part of this decision-making, and therefore the lack of high-quality information on age-dependent penetrance and variable expressivity will impede progress. Addressing biases in natural history data is essential for accurately informing screening protocols. We want to be able to identify when ascertainment bias is present and how to adjust for it when thinking about age of onset/age-based penetrance. While these biases are difficult to fully eliminate, they are important to acknowledge when interpreting the data. Accurate information about the typical progression of symptoms will ensure that screening recommendations align with real-world disease trajectories.

We are currently developing an approach to curating natural history information from the literature that attempts to reconstruct a composite picture of natural history as a pragmatic approach in the absence of comprehensive data from a single exhaustive study. Establishing clear parameters for literature review and quality metrics will facilitate this process and gauge the completeness and reliability of the information gleaned. Future work overcoming these obstacles will identify efficiencies or difficulties in how information is reported in the literature that can help to improve clinical phenotyping across disciplines, identify consensus approaches to defining the age of onset across research disciplines, and ultimately lead to more informed decisions for preventative medicine and precision health.
