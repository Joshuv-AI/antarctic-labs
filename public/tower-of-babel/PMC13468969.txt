Identifying barriers to diabetes technology use: LIFESTEPS, a newly developed clinical support tool for healthcare professionals
Authors: Gallen Geraldine, Rosso Alice, Alonso-Carril Núria, Arbeli Sima, Bahon Virginie, Brown Vanessa, Endlich Kerstin, Schleicher Verena, Hansart Audrey, Jolley Amy, Jussila Rea, Stefanowicz-Bielska Anna, Taplin Julie, Cardano Paola, Cyranka Katarzyna

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychosocial, behavioural, and lifestyle-related barriers can substantially affect the uptake, continued use, and discontinuation of diabetes technology in people with type 1 diabetes (PWT1D). However, structured tools to support healthcare professionals (HCPs) in systematically exploring these barriers in routine care remain limited. To address this gap, a clinically oriented questionnaire was developed by a multidisciplinary panel of diabetes experts. This paper describes the development of the questionnaire and its preliminary evaluation in terms of clarity, comprehensibility, usability, relevance, and perceived acceptability.MethodsA panel of 12 diabetes experts from 11 countries across Europe, the Middle East, and Africa (EMEA) reviewed the literature on barriers to diabetes technology use, grouped these barriers into key thematic domains, and developed a structured questionnaire that was translated into multiple languages. The questionnaire then underwent a two-round end-user evaluation involving HCPs and PWT1D to assess clarity, comprehensibility, practicality, relevance, and potential bias. Feedback from the first round informed refinement of the questionnaire before reassessment in a second round. The evaluation was descriptive and focused on end-user feedback; formal psychometric testing and hypothesis-driven validation analyses were not undertaken.ResultsThe expert panel named the questionnaire LIFESTEPS, reflecting its core thematic domains. The findings provide preliminary support for its clarity, acceptability, and perceived clinical usefulness. Background

Advanced diabetes technologies, including continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), insulin pumps, and automated insulin delivery (AID) systems, have become an increasingly important component of type 1 diabetes care. Their use has been associated with improved glycaemic outcomes, reduced self-management burden, and better quality of life (QOL) in many people with type 1 diabetes (PWT1D) [1, 2].

Despite these benefits, some individuals do not initiate diabetes technology, while others discontinue its use over time. These decisions may be influenced by a range of factors, including technical difficulties, psychosocial challenges, device-related burden, and contextual barriers. A systematic review by Dekker et al. identified several commonly reported reasons for discontinuation of continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII), including skin irritation, technical problems, psychological distress, and lifestyle-related constraints [3].

Acceptance and continued use of diabetes technology may also be influenced by individual characteristics such as age, diabetes duration, attitudes towards technology, and diabetes self-efficacy [8–10]. In addition, sustained engagement with diabetes technology may be affected by perceived treatment burden, concerns about glycaemic control, fear of hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia, and broader psychosocial stressors [6, 8, 9, 11, 16]. Adolescence, in particular, has been identified as a period during which adherence to insulin pump therapy may decline [8, 15].

Body image concerns represent another potentially important barrier to diabetes technology use. Previous studies have shown that visible devices may influence body image, social confidence, and emotional well-being in people with type 1 diabetes [12–15]. In PWT1D, body image dissatisfaction has been linked to discomfort associated with visible medical devices, perceived restrictions related to clothing or appearance, and co-occurring disordered eating behaviours, anxiety, and depression [12–15].

Additional barriers described in the literature include cost, limited insurance coverage, frequent alarms, perceived sensor inaccuracy, discomfort or pain, and lack of trust in technology [7–10, 16]. Wearable devices may also interfere with intimacy and sexual activity, which may further influence willingness to use diabetes technology [13].

Healthcare professionals (HCPs) may also hold concerns or reservations about diabetes technology, and these may influence patients’ treatment decisions [7]. For this reason, routine clinical conversations about technology use should extend beyond technical training and include psychosocial, behavioural, and lifestyle-related factors that may affect uptake and continuation.

Diabetes educators play an important role in recognising and addressing diabetes-related distress and other psychosocial difficulties in PWT1D. Active listening, empathy, exploration of psychosocial concerns, support for self-management, encouragement of self-care, stress management strategies, and referral for additional psychological support, when needed, are all important components of person-centred diabetes care [4].

At present, structured tools to help HCPs systematically explore barriers to diabetes technology uptake, continued use, discontinuation, and transition to more advanced systems in routine care remain limited. As a result, psychosocial and contextual barriers may remain insufficiently identified during clinical encounters. Existing work has highlighted the value of structured communication tools in diabetes care, but tools specifically focused on barriers to diabetes technology use remain limited [4, 5]. Existing diabetes communication and psychosocial screening frameworks may support broader person-centred care; however, LIFESTEPS was developed to focus specifically on technology-related concerns as prompts for clinical conversation rather than as a diagnostic or measurement framework.

In this paper, the term barriers is used broadly to include determinants, attitudes, emotional reactions, relational issues, access constraints, and contextual factors that may interfere with technology initiation, sustained use, discontinuation, or transition to more advanced systems. These phases are clinically related but conceptually distinct, and LIFESTEPS was designed to prompt discussion across them rather than to treat them as a single outcome.

To address this gap, a multidisciplinary panel of diabetes experts was convened to identify, explore, and categorise barriers to diabetes technology use and discontinuation, and to develop a structured multilingual questionnaire intended to support these conversations in clinical practice. The aim of the present paper was to describe the development of this questionnaire and its preliminary evaluation in terms of clarity, comprehensibility, usability, relevance, and perceived acceptability. The aim was not to establish psychometric validity, dimensional structure, reliability, criterion validity, or predictive performance.

Methods

Expert panel

A 13-member Expert Panel (EP) was convened for this project, comprising healthcare professionals with experience in diabetes technology use and implementation across 11 countries in Europe, the Middle East, and Africa (EMEA). Panel members represented a range of professional backgrounds relevant to diabetes care and technology use, including clinical practice, diabetes education, and the psychological aspects of diabetes management. One panel member with expertise in psychology and diabetes care chaired the panel discussions. All panel members were selected by Medtronic, the project sponsor, due to their extensive knowledge and experience in diabetes technology and clinical practice.

Panel members were invited on the basis of their clinical experience, involvement in diabetes education, and familiarity with diabetes technologies in routine practice. The purpose of the EP was to identify and discuss barriers to diabetes technology uptake, continued use, and discontinuation, drawing on clinical experience, patient perspectives encountered in practice, and relevant published literature.

The development and coordination of the project was supported by Medtronic. Sponsor-affiliated authors contributed to project coordination and methodological discussions; the LIFESTEPS questionnaire is not device-specific and is intended to be applicable to people with type 1 diabetes who face barriers to technology use, regardless of the specific technology considered or used.

Sponsor-affiliated authors participated as co-authors in project coordination and methodological discussion, including review of questionnaire wording during the iterative author-panel process. The sponsor did not create, require, or promote a device-specific scoring system or interpretation algorithm, and the tool does not direct users toward any particular diabetes technology. Final scientific interpretation, manuscript content, and the decision to submit the manuscript were reviewed and approved by all authors.

The EP met in a series of structured workshops involving moderated group discussion and iterative review of emerging themes. During these meetings, panel members explored barriers experienced by people with type 1 diabetes (PWT1D) when considering, initiating, using, or discontinuing diabetes technologies. These discussions informed the identification of key domains considered important for further exploration within a structured clinical tool.

Literature search and questionnaire development

A targeted literature search was conducted between September and December 2024 using predefined search terms, eligibility criteria, and databases, as detailed in Appendix 1. Following screening, 11 articles were selected as most relevant to the aims of the project. These publications informed the panel’s understanding of psychological, behavioural, and contextual factors associated with diabetes technology uptake, continued use, and discontinuation. This was a targeted, clinically oriented literature search rather than a systematic review or formal evidence synthesis.

Briefly, the targeted search included PubMed/MEDLINE and Google Scholar and was limited to literature available up to December 2024. Articles were considered eligible if they addressed psychosocial, behavioural, relational, body-image, trust-related, usability, access, or contextual barriers to diabetes technology initiation, continued use, discontinuation, or transition to more advanced systems in people with type 1 diabetes or in diabetes care settings. Records were screened for relevance to the clinical aims of the project and discussed by the Expert Panel; duplicate independent screening was not undertaken. The final set of 11 articles was selected because these publications were judged by the Expert Panel to be most directly relevant to the domains covered by the tool. The search was therefore intended to inform item generation and domain discussion, not to provide an exhaustive evidence synthesis.

The selected literature was reviewed by panel members and discussed during the workshop process. Insights from the literature, together with expert clinical experience, were used to refine the thematic framework and support the development of a structured topic guide. This iterative process led to the drafting of a set of clinically oriented questions intended to support identification of barriers to diabetes technology use in PWT1D. The final questionnaire is presented in Appendix 2. The name of the questionnaire was selected by panel members from five proposed options through internal voting. Formal methodologies such as the Delphi method or the COSMIN framework were not used, as they were not applicable to the scope and purpose of this project. The resulting domains should therefore be interpreted as a clinically informed exploratory framework rather than an exhaustive or validated taxonomy of all possible barriers to diabetes technology use.

The questionnaire was designed primarily as a clinical support tool for use by healthcare professionals (HCPs) during routine care. Its purpose is to facilitate structured discussion of psychosocial, behavioural, and lifestyle-related barriers that may affect initiation, continued use, discontinuation, or transition to more advanced diabetes technologies, including automated insulin delivery (AID) systems. Although the questionnaire was developed with people with type 1 diabetes in mind, its broader applicability to other groups was not assessed in the present study. At this stage, it was not intended to function as a screening, diagnostic, evaluative, psychometric, or predictive instrument, and no scoring or cut-off interpretation was planned. In the manuscript, “questionnaire” refers to the structured set of prompts, whereas “tool” refers to its intended clinical use as a support for conversation and reflection.

Preliminary end-user evaluation

Following its development, the questionnaire underwent a structured end-user evaluation to assess clarity, comprehensibility, usability, relevance, and sensitivity to potential bias. This evaluation should be understood as a preliminary usability and acceptability assessment, not as formal validation.

A total of 19 HCPs and 37 people with type 1 diabetes (PWT1D) from 10 countries participated in the initial evaluation round: Israel, Germany, Austria, Spain, Poland, Switzerland (French language version), Italy, Belgium (Dutch language version), England, and Finland (Finnish and Swedish language versions). Participants were recruited through the professional networks of Expert Panel members. The aim of this phase was to obtain preliminary feedback on the questionnaire’s wording, comprehensibility, perceived relevance, and practical usability across different linguistic and clinical contexts. Participation was voluntary, and feedback was collected anonymously. Formal completion time was not systematically recorded; however, feedback on perceived length and practical burden was collected and used to revise the tool.

The questionnaire, translated into the relevant local language, was distributed by EP members to PWT1D, who were invited to complete a feedback survey regarding their experience of the tool. In parallel, HCPs reviewed the same questionnaire and completed a corresponding evaluation form. Feedback from both groups was reviewed by the EP and used to refine the questionnaire’s content, wording, and format.

Translations were produced for preliminary clinical review by local Expert Panel members or clinical collaborators familiar with diabetes care. No standardized forward-backward translation, formal reconciliation, linguistic harmonization, or structured cultural adaptation procedure was undertaken. Accordingly, the language versions should be considered preliminary clinical translations for usability feedback only, and formal linguistic validation and cultural adaptation are required before wider multinational implementation.

Results

The EP developed a structured questionnaire informed by the targeted literature search and clinical experience. The questionnaire was designed to explore multiple psychological, relational, behavioural, and contextual factors that may influence diabetes technology uptake, continued use, discontinuation, or transition to more advanced systems.

The primary purpose of the questionnaire is exploratory and practice-oriented: to support healthcare professionals (HCPs) in identifying and discussing potential barriers to diabetes technology use in routine care. It is not intended to function as a psychological diagnostic instrument, and formal psychometric analyses were not undertaken in the present project. Instead, the evaluation focused on end-user perceptions of clarity, comprehensibility, usability, relevance, and potential sensitivity to bias. The tool should therefore not be used to generate total scores, subscale scores, classifications, treatment algorithms, or predictions of technology uptake or discontinuation. Nevertheless, it remains clinically relevant given the absence of such structured tools in routine clinical practice.

Open-text feedback indicated that some respondents perceived the questionnaire as too long and suggested that it should be shortened. Some participants recommended that questions relating specifically to insulin pump therapy should be conditional on current or previous pump use. A proportion of respondents also expressed a preference for an alternative mode of completion, particularly a digital format compatible with smartphone use.

Overall, feedback from PWT1D was favourable. Several respondents reported that the questionnaire was relevant and appropriate in its overall content, although some items were identified as potentially requiring simplification or reorganization.

Healthcare professionals: first evaluation round

The HCP feedback survey also consisted of nine questions and included an opportunity to provide open-text comments.

HCP feedback indicated that the questionnaire was generally understandable and potentially usable in clinical practice, although respondents also identified several areas for refinement (Fig. 3).

HCPs feedback from the first evaluation round

HCPs particularly noted the value of the questionnaire’s focus on psychological and relational factors that are not always explored in standard diabetes assessments. Some respondents also highlighted that the context in which the questionnaire is used may influence how comfortable individuals feel when answering certain questions, particularly when the content relates to intimacy, sexual activity, body image, financial resources, or autonomy.

Feedback from HCPs suggested that these topics may be clinically important but should be approached sensitively. Some respondents recommended that certain items remain optional. Additional suggestions included shortening the questionnaire, reducing item repetition, improving layout and formatting, and, where possible, ensuring that completion takes place in a quiet and private environment.

Discussion

This project aimed to identify and organize barriers relevant to diabetes technology uptake, continued use, and discontinuation, and to develop a structured multilingual questionnaire to support discussion of these issues in clinical practice. The result of this process was LIFESTEPS, a newly developed, practice-oriented tool designed to help HCPs explore psychosocial, behavioural, relational, and lifestyle-related barriers experienced by people with type 1 diabetes. This objective should be distinguished from psychometric validation, which was not the aim of the present phase of work.

The development process, informed by literature review, expert discussion, and end-user feedback, led to the identification of three broad areas considered particularly relevant in routine care: barriers related to technology use, body image concerns, and adherence-related issues. Feedback from both PWT1D and HCPs suggested that these themes were perceived as relevant in clinical practice and that the questionnaire was generally understandable and acceptable, while also highlighting areas requiring refinement. These areas should be interpreted as clinically prioritised domains emerging from the expert-panel process and end-user feedback, not as a comprehensive or validated taxonomy of barriers.

An important finding of the study was that barriers to diabetes technology use were perceived as extending beyond technical or educational issues alone. Participants and reviewers drew attention to emotional burden, trust in technology, body image concerns, social context, and the challenge of integrating devices into everyday life. These observations are consistent with previous literature showing that uptake and continuation of diabetes technology are influenced by a broad range of psychosocial and contextual factors [8–10, 12–16].

The iterative review process also identified practical considerations relevant to implementation. In particular, respondents highlighted the importance of questionnaire length, wording, readability, optional handling of more sensitive topics, and the need for an appropriate setting for completion. Revisions made after the first round were followed by generally more favourable feedback in the second round, suggesting improved usability. However, given the very small sample in the second round, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary only.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous work showing that trust, stigma, visibility of devices, psychosocial burden, and patient–provider interactions may influence technology-related decision-making [4, 5, 7–10, 13, 15]. They also support the view that structured exploration of psychosocial barriers may be helpful in routine care [4, 5]. At the same time, the present study does not demonstrate that use of LIFESTEPS improves clinical outcomes, increases technology uptake, reduces discontinuation, or strengthens the therapeutic alliance. These remain important questions for future research.

The potential contribution of LIFESTEPS lies in its role as a conversation-support tool. In routine diabetes care, HCPs may not always have a structured way to explore issues such as distress, body image, autonomy, intimacy, or trust in technology, despite the relevance of these topics to treatment decision-making [4, 5, 9, 12–15]. LIFESTEPS was developed to help make such conversations more systematic and person-centred. Its value at this stage is therefore clinical and exploratory rather than psychometric.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study did not include formal psychometric validation. Reliability, dimensional structure, construct validity, criterion validity, and cross-cultural measurement properties were not assessed. Accordingly, LIFESTEPS should not currently be interpreted as a validated psychometric instrument, and no formal scoring or cut-off system can be recommended on the basis of the present data. The absence of these analyses is a central limitation and means that LIFESTEPS cannot currently be considered a validated scale.

Second, participant recruitment was based on convenience sampling through the professional networks of EP members, which may have introduced selection bias, social desirability bias, and confirmation bias. Third, the number of participants in the second evaluation round was very small, and several had also taken part in the first round. For this reason, the second round should be understood as a pilot refinement step rather than an independent validation phase. Fourth, although the questionnaire was translated into multiple languages, the present study did not formally assess linguistic equivalence or cross-cultural validity. Although feedback was collected anonymously, these procedures do not eliminate the risk of bias. Future studies should include independent recruitment, cognitive interviewing, external qualitative analysis, and larger samples not recruited by the tool developers. Cultural acceptability of sensitive questions may also vary and requires formal evaluation before wider cross-cultural implementation.

An additional consideration concerns transparency of study development and sponsorship. While the development and coordination of the project was supported by Medtronic, the LIFESTEPS questionnaire is applicable to all PWT1D who face barriers to technology use and is not specific to any single diabetes technology. The sponsor’s role was operational and collaborative within the author group; the tool remained non-promotional and was not linked to any scoring system, device recommendation, or product-specific pathway.

Despite these limitations, this study provides an initial description of the development and preliminary end-user evaluation of a structured, multilingual clinical support tool addressing an underexplored area in diabetes care. The findings support further development of LIFESTEPS and suggest that the questionnaire was perceived as potentially useful for helping HCPs initiate more structured conversations about barriers to diabetes technology use.

Future studies should include formal psychometric testing, cognitive interviewing, assessment of cross-cultural equivalence, and prospective evaluation in routine care settings. Such work will be necessary before LIFESTEPS can be considered for broader research, scoring, or predictive applications. These studies should also evaluate test-retest reliability, internal consistency where appropriate, factor structure, construct and criterion validity, measurement equivalence across languages, and real-world usability and implementation feasibility.

Implications for clinical practice

Early recognition of barriers to diabetes technology use may help healthcare professionals (HCPs) address concerns that are not routinely explored during standard clinical encounters. LIFESTEPS was developed as a structured, practice-oriented questionnaire to support discussion of technical, psychological, behavioural, and social factors that may influence diabetes technology uptake, continued use, or discontinuation in people with type 1 diabetes (PWT1D). It may be used flexibly as a pre-visit self-completion tool, an in-clinic clinician-guided discussion aid, or a modular prompt focused on selected clinically relevant domains. These proposed modes of use should be interpreted as possible applications requiring future workflow testing rather than as established implementation pathways.

The findings from this study suggest that HCPs and PWT1D perceived the questionnaire as potentially useful for identifying topics such as body image concerns, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, confidence in technology, and the influence of social support or daily routines. In this way, LIFESTEPS may help structure person-centred conversations and support more individualized discussion in clinical care.

The study also reinforces the view that barriers to diabetes technology use are multidimensional and extend beyond technical issues alone. Psychological, relational, and contextual factors may influence how individuals engage with diabetes devices in everyday life. A structured tool may therefore be helpful in prompting discussion of issues that might otherwise remain unaddressed, including sensitive topics such as intimacy, body image, or treatment fatigue. Sensitive domains such as intimacy, body image, autonomy, emotional burden, and financial resources should be introduced respectfully, with permission, in a private setting, and with the option for patients to skip questions they do not wish to answer.

At the current stage of development, LIFESTEPS should be considered a conversation-support tool rather than a validated outcome measure. The present study does not demonstrate that use of the questionnaire improves adherence, quality of life, technology uptake, or continuation. However, the preliminary findings indicate that the tool was perceived as potentially usable in routine care and may assist HCPs in initiating more structured discussions about barriers to diabetes technology use. Responses should prompt further exploration, referral, education, or shared decision-making when clinically appropriate, but should not be interpreted as formal scores or diagnostic indicators.

Limitations and strengths

This project has several important limitations. First, LIFESTEPS has not yet undergone formal psychometric validation. Reliability, dimensional structure, construct validity, criterion validity, and cross-cultural measurement properties were not assessed. Accordingly, the questionnaire should currently be regarded as a newly developed clinical support tool rather than a standardized measurement instrument. It should not be used as a validated scale, outcome measure, screening tool, or basis for cut-off-based decisions.

Second, participant recruitment was based on convenience sampling through the professional networks of EP members. This may have introduced selection bias, confirmation bias, and social desirability bias. Third, the second evaluation round included a very small sample of HCPs and PWT1D, several of whom had also participated in the first round. This follow-up phase should therefore be interpreted as a pilot refinement step rather than an independent validation stage.

Fourth, although the questionnaire was translated into multiple languages, the present project did not formally assess linguistic equivalence or cross-cultural validity. In addition, the length of the questionnaire may limit its use in busy clinical settings, and some items may require sensitive introduction depending on the clinical context and the preferences of the individual completing it. No detailed demographic or clinical characterization of participants, such as age range, diabetes duration, technology experience, or distribution of HCP professional roles, was systematically collected, which limits interpretation of the feedback.

Despite these limitations, the project also has several strengths. The questionnaire was developed through an iterative process informed by literature review, multidisciplinary expert input, and feedback from both HCPs and PWT1D across several countries and language versions. This process supported refinement of the questionnaire’s wording, structure, and usability and helped identify content areas perceived as relevant in clinical practice.

An interpretive support guide may be helpful in future implementation; however, at the current stage, any such guide should be used only to support clinical reflection and discussion, not formal scoring or psychometric interpretation. Any future guide should include practical recommendations for optional use of sensitive sections, privacy, and referral pathways when psychological distress or other support needs are identified.

Future studies should include formal psychometric testing, cognitive interviewing, and assessment of cross-cultural equivalence to determine whether LIFESTEPS can be developed further as a more standardized instrument.

Recommendations and conclusion

The domains addressed by LIFESTEPS, particularly those related to body image, psychological distress, communication, and treatment-related burden, represent clinically relevant aspects of diabetes technology use that may not always be systematically explored in routine care. By bringing these issues into a more structured format, the questionnaire may help support person-centred discussion between HCPs and people with diabetes.

Future studies should evaluate the questionnaire using formal psychometric methods, including assessment of reliability, construct validity, and cross-cultural equivalence. Prospective studies in routine clinical settings are also needed to determine whether use of the questionnaire is associated with changes in communication, care processes, or technology-related outcomes. Such studies should include formal assessment of completion time, workflow integration, clinician training needs, and patient acceptability in routine care.

Digital implementation may be considered in future development work, for example through electronic completion formats integrated into clinical workflows. However, such applications remain speculative and should be explored only after further validation of the tool. Before digital implementation, the tool should undergo formal usability testing and evaluation of data privacy, accessibility, and integration into clinical workflows.

In conclusion, LIFESTEPS is a newly developed, multilingual clinical support questionnaire intended to facilitate structured discussion of psychosocial, behavioural, and contextual barriers to diabetes technology use. The present study provides preliminary support for its clarity, acceptability, and perceived usefulness in clinical practice. Further psychometric and prospective clinical evaluation will be required before broader research, scoring, or predictive applications can be considered. Until such evaluation is completed, LIFESTEPS should remain a structured aid for conversation rather than a measurement instrument.

Appendix 1. Literature search strategy

The literature search supporting the development of the LIFESTEPS questionnaire was conducted between September and December 2024. Its aim was to identify and synthesise scientific evidence describing the psychological, behavioural, relational, technical, and lifestyle-related factors that influence the initiation, ongoing use, or discontinuation of diabetes technologies such as automated insulin delivery (AID), continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII), and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM). This search formed the empirical foundation for the Expert Panel’s analysis of barriers to technology adoption and guided the construction of the questionnaire’s thematic domains. .

These sources provided a broad representation of clinical, psychological, nursing, and behavioural science perspectives relevant to diabetes technology use.

Outcome of the literature search

The literature search produced a clear and structured overview of the psychological, relational, behavioural, and technical factors shaping engagement with diabetes technologies. Importantly, it revealed that existing assessment tools tended to focus primarily on user experience from the patient perspective, while lacking a comprehensive and psychologically informed framework that could be used systematically by healthcare professionals.

Participating Expert Panel members were responsible for ensuring that local institutional requirements were followed in their respective settings.
