ProGS: Towards Progressive Coding for 3D Gaussian Splatting
Zhiye Tang, Lingzhuo Liu, Shengjie Jiao, Qiudan Zhang, Junhui Hou, You Yang, Xu Wang

Abstract
With the emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), numerous pioneering efforts have been made to address the effective compression issue of massive 3DGS data. 3DGS offers an efficient and scalable representation of 3D scenes by utilizing learnable 3D Gaussians, but the large size of the generated data has posed significant challenges for storage and transmission. Existing methods, however, have been limited by their inability to support progressive coding, a crucial feature in streaming applications with varying bandwidth. To tackle this limitation, this paper introduce a novel approach that organizes 3DGS data into an octree structure, enabling efficient progressive coding. The proposed ProGS is a streaming-friendly codec that facilitates progressive coding for 3D Gaussian splatting, and significantly improves both compression efficiency and visual fidelity. The proposed method incorporates mutual information enhancement mechanisms to mitigate structural redundancy, leveraging the relevance between nodes in the octree hierarchy. By adapting the octree structure and dynamically adjusting the anchor nodes, ProGS ensures scalable data compression without compromising the rendering quality. ProGS achieves a remarkable 45× reduction in file storage compared to the original 3DGS format, while simultaneously improving visual performance by over 10%. This demonstrates that ProGS can provide a robust solution for real-time applications with varying network conditions.

I
Introduction

Recently, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)
[
24
]
emerged as a prominent novel paradigm in the field of novel view synthesis (NVS)
[
17
]
. Unlike neural radiance fields (NeRF)
[
35
]
, which rely on dense neural networks to represent a 3D scene, 3DGS explicitly represents each scene as a collection of learnable 3D Gaussians. These Gaussians are initialized from a structure-from-motion (SfM) point cloud
[
48
]
, which provides a sparse yet efficient representation of the scene’s geometry. The Gaussians are visualized as ellipsoids in the 3D space, each characterized by learnable parameters such as pose, shape, and color. In the rendering process, these 3D Gaussians are “splatted” onto an imaging plane corresponding to a particular viewpoint, and the final image is synthesized through
\alpha
-blending
[
27
]
. This splatting-based rendering process simplifies and accelerates scene rendering by avoiding the frequent and computationally expensive ray sampling operations required by methods like NeRF
[
35
]
. Furthermore, it facilitates high-quality, differentiable rasterization. However, this approach is not without its challenges. While 3DGS enables real-time rendering, it leads to high memory and storage consumption due to the large number of 3D Gaussians (often millions) required to represent a single scene. As a result, there is a critical need to develop efficient compression strategies for 3DGS that reduce its data volume without sacrificing rendering quality. Recent advancements aim to address this challenge by exploring data structure optimization and the use of hierarchical encoding techniques, such as octrees, to reduce storage overhead while maintaining scene fidelity.

With the unorganized and sparse nature of 3DGS, it is challenging to achieve efficient compression of 3D Gaussians
[
17
,
7
]
. These difficulties stem from the inherently high memory requirements and the lack of structural organization in 3DGS, which typically results in a large amount of redundant data. In response, numerous approaches have been proposed to tackle this issue from various perspectives. Some methods introduce pruning techniques, such as applying masks to discard 3D Gaussians with low utility, thereby reducing the overall data size
[
29
,
16
,
57
]
. Additionally, codebook-based quantization methods have been explored, aiming to further compress the data by grouping similar 3D Gaussians into predefined sets, thus improving storage efficiency
[
39
]
. Despite their effectiveness, these methods focus primarily on directly compressing the original 3DGS format, and they often overlook the potential to leverage the structural relationships between Gaussians. The lack of such relational awareness limits the opportunity for creating even more compact representations.

Building on this, Scaffold-GS
[
32
]
took inspiration from grid-guided NeRF
[
38
]
and proposed a more structured approach. This method replaced 3D Gaussians with learnable anchors, initialized from SfM point clouds and aligned to a voxel grid. During both training and rendering, these anchors dynamically generate surrounding neural 3D Gaussians via multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs), significantly reducing memory usage. This anchor-based framework has been shown to provide more efficient representations. More recently, further advancements have incorporated context-based compression techniques, such as hash-grid-assisted coding, to better capture the interdependencies between anchor attributes and enhance storage efficiency
[
12
]
. While these techniques achieved significant performance improvements, they do not support progressive coding, which is a crucial feature for scalable 3D scene streaming. These methods encode 3D scenes into static files that are decoded in their entirety, failing to adapt dynamically to varying network conditions and bitrates. Progressive coding, on the other hand, allows for more flexible data transmission, where coarse-grained data is first transmitted and refined with finer details, improving channel efficiency and visual quality as the stream progresses.

To push the boundaries of streaming-friendly 3DGS representation, this work draws inspiration from the point cloud domain, specifically contemplating the idea of representing 3D space using a tree structure
[
47
]
. This approach prompts the essential question:
Is it possible to efficiently organize 3DGS scenes into tree structures with varying granularities?
The proposed solution is
ProGS
, a streaming-friendly codec designed to facilitate
Pro
gressive coding for 3D
G
aussian
S
platting. In particular, following the anchor-based design of Scaffold-GS
[
32
]
, ProGS mimics the octree structure and divides the anchor collection into multiple levels of detail (LoDs), ensuring that each level progressively refines the scene representation. During the training process, ProGS adaptively grows and prunes the branches of the octrees, dynamically adjusting the resolution of each part of the scene, which reduces memory usage and optimizes the encoding efficiency.

However, such a LoD structure faces inherent challenges. Lower LoDs represent incomplete 3D scenes, resulting in visual degradation of the rendered images. To overcome this, ProGS hypothesize that the representation goals of low and high LoDs are aligned, and thus, it is affirmative to improve the performance of lower-level anchors by enhancing the mutual information (MI)
[
49
]
between parent and child nodes within the octree. By enhancing the common information, lower-level anchors can more accurately represent the scene’s details, significantly improving the progressive coding performance. Furthermore, building on the work of HAC
[
12
]
, ProGS also utilizes context-based encoding techniques, where each anchor’s context is queried from its location and passed through arithmetic coding (AE)
[
45
]
, ensuring the generation of a compact binary bitstream. This approach not only ensures that the 3DGS data is compactly represented but also guarantees efficient, progressive decoding and reconstruction for streaming applications. The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

1.

A progressive codec for 3DGS:
ProGS is a codec that facilitates progressive coding for 3DGS by constructing volumetric scenes as hierarchical octree structures. This novel approach not only allows for efficient encoding and streaming of 3DGS data but also enables adaptive bitrate transmission, where the scene’s details can be progressively refined during the decoding process based on available bandwidth.

2.

MI enhancement for low LoDs:
To address the challenge of visual quality degradation due to the incomplete representation of 3D scenes at lower LoDs, MI enhancement mechanisms are introduced. By leveraging the common information shared between parent and child nodes in the octree, ProGS is able to enhance the ability of lower-level anchors to more accurately represent the full 3D scene. This process significantly improves the rendering quality of lower LoDs, mitigating the loss of detail and ensuring smoother transitions as the scene is progressively refined.

3.

Coarse-to-fine optimization:
To further optimize the compression efficiency of ProGS, a coarse-to-fine optimization scheme is proposed. This method facilitates the reduction of data redundancy and promotes MI between anchors, enabling a more compact representation. By adjusting the anchor placement dynamically during training, ProGS achieves a balanced representation that preserves high-fidelity details in areas of interest while minimizing the storage requirements for less critical regions of the scene.

4.

Extensive experimental validation:
Extensive experiments demonstrate that ProGS achieves comparable or superior fidelity compared to state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods. Remarkably, ProGS can reduce the number of anchors used for scene representation by up to 67%, yet still maintain nearly 80% of the visual performance. These results highlight ProGS’s ability to achieve substantial compression without sacrificing rendering quality, making it a promising solution for real-time applications where bandwidth and storage efficiency are crucial.

II
Related Work

II-A

Compression for 3D Gaussian Splatting

3DGS
[
24
]
has emerged as an innovative technique for representing 3D scenes, offering efficient ways to capture complex details within volumetric spaces by utilizing anisotropic 3D Gaussians. These Gaussians, which represent scene elements as ellipsoids with learnable poses, shapes, and colors, provide a compact representation that enables high-quality rendering through differentiable rasterization. However, a significant challenge arises from the large storage cost of explicitly recording each Gaussian, as each one occupies up to 248 bytes, necessitating high-efficiency compression techniques to handle large-scale 3DGS data.

The existing 3DGS compression approaches can be broadly categorized into two main types:
primitive-based methods
and
anchor-based methods
.
Primitive-based methods
focus on directly compressing the 3D Gaussian primitives used in the original 3DGS format. For example, Lee
et al.

[
29
]
introduced pruning techniques to remove redundant and less informative Gaussians, thereby reducing memory requirements. Similarly, approaches like those in
[
16
,
57
,
42
]
utilized vector quantization methods to group similar Gaussians into codebooks for more efficient encoding, while LocoGS
[
50
]
decomposed Gaussian attributes into explicit and implicit parts, which also effectively reduced information redundancy. To address the non-structural nature of the original 3DGS format, CompGS
[
31
]
proposed a hybrid primitive structure that leverages the predictive relationships between Gaussians to improve compression. MesonGS
[
60
]
borrowed the octree voxelization and region-adaptive hierarchical transform (RAHT)
[
15
]
from point cloud compression, also achieved promising performance. More recently, Chen
et al.
proposed FCGS
[
11
]
, which is a plug-and-play codec for any pre-trained 3D Gaussian scenes,
pushing the boundaries of 3DGS coding. These methods, while successful in reducing the size of the 3DGS representation, often fail to exploit the underlying structural relationships between the Gaussians, which could lead to even more efficient compressions.

On the other hand,
anchor-based approaches
aim to replace the original 3D Gaussians with anchors, which are more compact and can represent multiple surrounding Gaussians. Scaffold-GS
[
32
]
was one of the first to introduce this approach, using anchor-centered features to represent 3D scenes. Each anchor is initialized from a SfM point cloud and aligned to a voxel grid. During both training and rendering, anchors generate a set of surrounding neural 3D Gaussians via MLPs, thus saving considerable memory and storage. More recently, HAC
[
12
]
further advanced anchor-based compression by using a context-based hash-grid assisted coding framework, which incorporates adaptive quantization techniques to enhance compression efficiency. This framework achieved SOTA performance in 3DGS compression, significantly reducing data size while maintaining high visual fidelity.

Although these approaches have made significant strides in reducing the storage and memory requirements of 3DGS representations, they primarily focus on single-bitrate scenarios, where a fixed amount of data is compressed and transmitted. This approach does not effectively handle the variability in available network bandwidth, especially for real-time streaming applications where bitrate adaptation is crucial. These methods generally encode 3D scenes into static files, which limits their flexibility and scalability for dynamic transmission. Progressive coding, on the other hand, allows for scenes to be progressively decoded and refined over time, enabling higher channel efficiency and better quality as more data becomes available. This paper introduces ProGS, a novel codec designed specifically for progressive coding of 3DGS. ProGS organizes scenes into hierarchical octree-like structures, which are adaptively adjusted during training to support progressive refinement. This method not only achieves efficient compression but also ensures that the rendering quality is not compromised during progressive decoding, marking a significant advancement over previous methods.

II-B

Progressive Coding

For now, progressive coding has not yet been extensively explored for 3DGS, but it has attracted considerable attention in the fields of image, video, and point cloud compression. Some progressive codecs, such as traditional JPEG
[
56
]
, JPEG2000
[
52
]
and recent
[
13
]
for images, as well as H.264/SVC
[
53
]
and SHVC
[
4
]
for video, typically construct enhanced layers for media content. These codecs enable the adaptive selection and decoding of data based on the current network conditions and the device capabilities, allowing for efficient streaming and rendering across varying bandwidths. This adaptive nature makes progressive coding highly valuable for multimedia content delivery, where high-quality visuals are prioritized, but only available bandwidth is used. In parallel, learning-based approaches have also gained popularity. Notably, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) have been employed in various works
[
20
,
23
,
54
,
55
,
5
,
6
]
, capturing representations at lower bitrates and gradually refining them to generate high-resolution images. More recently, the adoption of trit-plane coding methods, as seen in
[
28
,
22
]
, has yielded comparable gains, particularly in image and video compression. Mao
et al.

[
33
]
leveraged priors from a generative adverseral network (GAN)
[
19
]
, also improved upon traditional methods. Furthermore, Dasari
et al.

[
14
]
and Lin
et al.

[
30
]
have contributed novel scalable approaches for video coding, significantly enhancing the scalability and efficiency of video codecs for modern applications.

However, unlike the regular and structured nature of images and videos, point clouds represent 3D data as discrete point sets that are unevenly distributed across a three-dimensional space. This inherent irregularity makes it particularly challenging to apply progressive coding to point clouds. G-PCC
[
1
]
was a pioneering effort that introduced scalability for point cloud compression, utilizing the octree structure and LoD schemes to improve data representation and compression. Building upon this foundation, works like
[
61
]
and
[
34
]
have leveraged learning-based models integrated with G-PCC to further enhance compression efficiency for LoD-structured point clouds. While these methods have made strides in improving point cloud compression, they still face challenges in handling implicit attributes of 3DGS scenes, such as covariance and anchor features. These attributes are particularly sensitive to prediction errors, which can degrade the quality of the resulting 3D scene representation. Therefore, a more refined approach for progressive coding should involve the LoD partitioning strategy during scene training processes and incorporate context-based entropy coding to ensure lossless compression.

Among the efforts in this direction, Octree-GS
[
44
]
aimed to incorporate multi-resolution anchors to achieve LoD-structured 3DGS. However, despite these efforts, the approach fell short in providing sufficiently refined representations for 3D scenes, primarily due to the lack of entropy coding for data reduction. This omission resulted in limited performance when compared to other methods in the field. In contrast, this paper proposes
ProGS
, which models 3DGS scenes as octrees and incorporates MI enhancement to improve compression efficiency. By utilizing the relationship between parent and child nodes within the octree, ProGS enables better scene representation at lower LoDs, facilitating efficient progressive coding and improving both compression and rendering quality.

III
Methodology

The framework of ProGS is illustrated in Fig.
2
. Specifically, ProGS begins by constructing an initial octree structure from the input SfM point cloud, where the nodes in the octree correspond to anchors, each associated with a set of attributes
\mathcal{A}
, including feature vectors, scaling factors, and offsets. These anchors serve as the foundational elements for predicting surrounding neural Gaussian clusters, which represent the scene’s details at varying levels of granularity. The anchors are distributed at different depths within the octree, and each level corresponds to a voxel region in the 3D space, where the granularity of the representation increases with each subsequent LoD. The
l
-th LoD of ProGS contains all anchors from level
1
through to level
l
, with higher LoDs offering progressively finer resolutions of the scene. During the training process, ProGS employs an adaptive anchor adjustment strategy that dynamically grows and prunes the octree branches based on optimization gradients. This ensures that the octree structure is both compact and flexible, accurately reflecting the scene’s most significant features while minimizing redundancy. To assist with this dynamic adjustment, ProGS utilizes a hash-grid
\mathcal{H}
, which is queried to estimate the distribution of anchor attributes and facilitate adaptive quantization. The quantized attributes, or their decoded counterparts in evaluation mode, are then used to generate neural Gaussian clusters, which are further employed in multi-level differentiable rendering. This process is crucial for ensuring that each level of the octree contributes to the final rendered image, as shown in Fig.
2
. In addition, to enhance the visual quality of lower-level anchors, ProGS incorporates the information noise contrastive estimation (InfoNCE) loss
[
41
]
, denoted as
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}
, along with a coarse-to-fine optimization loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{c2f}}
for MI enhancement. These mechanisms help improve the representation of the scene at lower LoDs by promoting consistency between parent and child nodes in the octree. A more detailed discussion of the MI enhancement approach is provided in Sec.
III-D
.

III-A

Preliminary

3DGS represents a 3D scene as a collection of anisotropic Gaussians and renders viewpoint images via splatting-based, differentiable rasterization. Concretely, each Gaussian is parameterized by a mean (center)
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a covariance
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
,

G(\bm{x})=\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})\right),

(1)

where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an arbitrary 3D location. To guarantee positive semi-definiteness and enable stable optimization, the covariance is factorized into a rotation and a non-negative scaling,
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{R}^{\top}
, where
\bm{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
(e.g., parameterized by quaternions) defines the Gaussian’s orientation and
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is diagonal, controlling the principal-axis scales. For image synthesis from a given camera pose, Gaussians are projected and splatted to the image plane; the per-pixel color
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is then computed with front-to-back
\alpha
-blending

\bm{C}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\bm{c}_{i}\,\alpha_{i}\!\!\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\!(1-\alpha_{j}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the depth-sorted set of Gaussians contributing to that pixel,
\alpha_{i}\in[0,1]
is the opacity (accumulated transmittance), and
\bm{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the view-dependent color predicted from spherical harmonics (SH) bases
[
37
]
. This rasterization-style rendering is differentiable w.r.t. Gaussian parameters, making it amenable to end-to-end learning while naturally handling occlusion via the transmittance product.

Building on the original framework
[
24
]
, Scaffold-GS
[
32
]
introduced a more storage-efficient, anchor-based formulation by replacing explicitly stored Gaussians with sparse anchors. Each anchor is defined as

\bm{a}_{i}=\bigl\{\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\bigr\},\qquad\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(3)

and carries a compact attribute set

\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{i}=\bigl\{\bm{f}_{i}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{D^{f}},\;\bm{s}_{i}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{D^{s}},\;\bm{o}_{i}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{D^{o}\times 3}\bigr\},

(4)

where
\bm{f}_{i}
encodes an anchor feature,
\bm{s}_{i}
parameterizes per-Gaussian scales/orientations, and
\bm{o}_{i}
stores 3D offsets that place multiple neural Gaussians relative to the anchor center. In practice,
\bm{f}_{i}
is fed to a lightweight generator
\mathrm{MLP}_{g}
to synthesize a local cluster of Gaussians around anchor
i
; their centers are given by
\bm{x}_{i}
shifted by rows of
\bm{o}_{i}
, while shapes (and ultimately
\bm{\Sigma}
) are derived from
\bm{s}_{i}
together with learned rotation parameterizations. The resulting cluster is then rendered via the same
\alpha
-blending rule as above. By decoupling scene detail from the number of explicitly stored primitives, the anchor-based design drastically reduces storage and enables hierarchical organization (
e.g.
, LoDs and octrees), which ProGS further leverages for progressive coding.

III-B

Octree Construction

A well-defined LoD partitioning scheme is crucial for enabling efficient progressive coding. Octree-GS
[
44
]
introduced multi-resolution anchors to represent voxel regions at varying granularities, incorporating a gradient-guided anchor adjustment strategy. While this approach allows for multi-resolution representation, their proposed strategy is
position-decoupled
, which leads to a loosely organized structure. This decoupling fails to fully exploit the interdependencies between anchors at different levels, resulting in an inefficient representation of the volumetric scene. To overcome this limitation, this work presents a more structured approach in ProGS. In the proposed pipeline, ProGS first estimates an approximate bounding box based on the inputted SfM point cloud
\bm{P}
, which provides a sparse yet informative representation of the 3D scene. This bounding box serves as the foundation for constructing the hierarchical octree structure.

Given the base depth
l_{b}
and the number of LoDs
L
, the entire scene is divided into a set of octrees with varying voxel sizes. Each LoD represents a different level of granularity, with the voxel sizes defined by the equation

v_{l}=\frac{v_{B}}{2^{l_{b}+l}},

(5)

where
v_{B}
denotes the length of the estimated bounding box, and
l
is the current LoD level. The voxel size decreases exponentially as it progresses through the LoD levels, allowing for a finer resolution in higher levels of detail. The complete representation of LoD
l
consists of the anchors from level
1
to level
l
, with each level containing increasingly refined spatial information. Once the initial octrees are constructed, all points in the SfM point cloud are treated as potential anchor candidates for the
L
-th level. These points are then integrated into the octree structure, ensuring a compact and efficient representation of the scene.

To further enhance the octree structure, ProGS introduces a novel anchor adjustment strategy, discussed in detail in Sec.
III-C
, which adaptively grows and prunes octree branches during training based on the optimization gradient. This adaptive process ensures that the octree structure maintains its strict hierarchical properties, while also allowing for the dynamic refinement of the scene representation. The result is a more compact and efficient octree that accurately reflects the underlying 3D scene, without unnecessary redundancy. By adjusting the octree in this manner, ProGS ensures that only the most relevant data is retained at each level, thus improving both the compression efficiency and the quality of the progressive decoding process.

III-C

Adaptive Anchor Adjustment

The main challenge to guaranteeing octree characteristics of ProGS is to design an anchor adjustment strategy that ensures each anchor is a node of the octrees without affecting the high-fidelity representation of a 3D scene. Similar to
[
24
]
, ProGS grow anchors on the locations where the Gaussians find
significant
and eliminate
trivial
branches.

The growing operation contains three steps:

Step 1: Anchor Candidate Determination.
The gradient-guided anchor candidate determination specifically targets
significant
Gaussians defined by their optimization gradients. For each octree-level
l
, ProGS computes a threshold:

\tau_{g}^{l}=\tau_{g}\times 2^{\beta l},

(6)

where
\tau_{g}
and
\beta
are pre-set hyperparameters. The average gradient
\left\|\nabla_{G_{i}^{l}}\right\|
of each neural Gaussian at level
l
over the latest
T
iterations is also collected for further processes. As shown in Fig.
3
(i-ii), Gaussians are categorized into three types:
significant
(
\tau_{g}^{l+1}\geq\left\|\nabla_{G_{i}^{l}}\right\|>\tau_{g}^{l}
),
very significant
(
\left\|\nabla_{G_{i}^{l}}\right\|>\tau_{g}^{l+1}
) and
non-significant
. For each
significant
or
very significant
Gaussian
G_{i}^{l}
, an anchor candidate
c_{j}
is then placed at different levels based on the Gaussian significance, with each anchor candidate
c_{j}
can be written as

c_{j}=\begin{cases}(\bm{\mu}_{i}^{l},l),&\tau_{g}^{l+1}\geq\left\|\nabla_{G_{i}^{l}}\right\|>\tau_{g}^{l}\\
(\bm{\mu}_{i}^{l},l+1),&\left\|\nabla_{G_{i}^{l}}\right\|>\tau_{g}^{l+1}\end{cases},

(7)

where
\bm{\mu}_{i}^{l}
is the position of the corresponding Gaussian
G_{i}^{l}
of candidate
c_{j}
, and
v_{l}
follows the definition in Eq. (
5
).

Step 2: Parent-Child Verification.
For a level-
l
candidate
c_{j}
determined in the previous step, ProGS check whether its parent anchor
\bm{a}_{p_{j}}
at level
l-1
exists, which satisfying

\text{round}\left(\bm{x}_{p_{j}}/v_{l-1}\right)=\left\lfloor\text{round}\left(\bm{x}\left(c_{j}\right)/v_{l}\right)/2\right\rfloor,

(8)

while
\bm{x}\left(c_{j}\right)
refers to
c_{j}
’s position. If no
\bm{a}_{p_{j}}
exists, a candidate
c_{p_{j}}=\left(\bm{x}\left(c_{j}\right),l-1\right)
will be queued, as in Fig.
3
(ii).

Step 3: Anchor Spawn.
Finally, queued candidates will be instantiated as anchors if their target voxels (determined by
\bm{x}\left(c_{j}\right)
and level) are unoccupied, strictly preserving octree sparsity through conflict checking.

iterations and prunes all leaf nodes whose opacity is not higher than a threshold
\tau_{o}
, as marked in Fig.
3
(iii), and those anchors with no children nodes will be pruned.

III-D

Mutual Information Enhancement

Under the octree structure, each low LoD is essentially an incomplete representation of the entire scene, which inevitably causes noticeable degradation in visual fidelity. Since all LoDs ultimately pursue the same representation goal, a natural question arises:
Is it possible to enhance MI so that low LoDs inherit more of the informative content available at higher LoDs?
The answer is affirmative, as prior work in point clouds and images has repeatedly shown the effectiveness of MI maximization for robust representations
[
9
,
46
,
8
]
. Building on this insight, we introduce two complementary MI enhancement mechanisms: an InfoNCE-based contrastive objective that explicitly increases the MI between parent–child anchors by treating them as positive pairs and unrelated anchors as negatives, and a coarse-to-fine optimization that provides cross-level supervision so lower-level anchors better approximate the distributions and rendering behaviors of higher-level ones. Together, these mechanisms promote information sharing across levels, stabilize learning at early progressive stages, and substantially improve ProGS’s rendering quality at low bitrates by making lower-level anchors more predictive of fine-grained content present in deeper LoDs.

InfoNCE Loss for Mutual Information.
MI is a metric that measures the amount of information shared between random variables
x\sim X
and
y\sim Y

[
26
]
, which is defined as

\begin{aligned} I(X;Y)&=\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\text{log}\left(\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\cdot p(y)}\right)\right]\\
&=\iint p(x,y)\ \text{log}\left[\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\cdot p(y)}\right]\text{d}x\text{d}y\end{aligned}.

(9)

In the context of ProGS, with higher MI between parent and child nodes comes a stronger ability of low LoDs to represent the entire scene. However, explicitly estimating the marginal distributions
p(x)
and
p(y)
is hard. Therefore, this paper introduces InfoNCE loss
[
41
]
to facilitate MI maximization in a contrastive learning way. Note that the MI is modeled as the dot product of the attributes as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}=-\text{log}\frac{\exp{\left(\mathcal{A}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{p_{i}}^{T}/\tau_{\text{NCE}}\right)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{neg}}}\exp{\left(\mathcal{A}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{j}^{T}/\tau_{\text{NCE}}\right)}},

(10)

in which
p_{i}
refers to the parent node of node
i
, with
N_{\text{neg}}
as the number of the constructed negative samples, and
\tau_{\text{NCE}}
as the temperature factor to control the discrimination of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}
for negative samples
[
21
]
.

Under the explicit supervision of the InfoNCE loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}
, the attributes of the parent and child nodes are highly correlated. Therefore, the context of each child anchor should be modeled as related to its parent and itself. Following the design of
[
12
]
, in which the context of each anchor is modeled as a hash feature queried from a hash-grid
\mathcal{H}
, in this case, ProGS models the context as the combination of the hash feature
\bm{h}_{i}
queried by its position and the context feature
\bm{h}_{p_{i}}
queried by its parent
p_{i}
’s position as

p(\mathcal{A}_{i},i,\mathcal{H})\sim p(\mathcal{A}_{i}|\bm{h}_{i},\bm{h}_{p_{i}})\times p(\mathcal{H}).

(11)

Specifically, for each root node
i
, with no parent node, the corresponding
\bm{h}_{p_{i}}
is simply set to
\bm{0}
.

Coarse-to-Fine Optimization.
To further promote lower-level anchors representing the entire scene and thereby increase MI between different levels, ProGS replaces the original
\mathcal{L}_{\text{3DGS}}
by a coarse-to-fine loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{c2f}}
to activate lower-level anchors in a macro but implicit way:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{c2f}}=\frac{1}{L}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\left(1-\lambda_{\text{SSIM}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{1}^{l}+\lambda_{\text{SSIM}}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}}^{l}\right),

(12)

in which
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{l}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}}^{l}
represent the absolute error and the structural similarity index (SSIM)
[
58
]
between ground truth images and the results rendered by anchors with levels beneath
l
, respectively. By bridging lower-level anchors to the entire scene, this loss function can also promote the learning process of coarse-grained anchors, driving them to represent the whole scene independently.

To facilitate bit reduction via entropy coding such as AE
[
45
]
, ProGS explicitly drives the anchor attributes toward low-entropy, context-predictable distributions. ProGS Follows HAC
[
12
]
, querying a mixed 3D–2D hash-grid
\mathcal{H}
at an anchor’s spatial location along with its parent’s position to obtain context features that condition both distribution estimation and adaptive quantization. Empirically, the attribute channels in ProGS exhibit approximately Gaussian statistics similar to HAC; therefore, a same context modeling backbone can be performed while tailoring it to the octree hierarchy. Concretely, the context model in Eq. (
11
) remains aligned with
[
12
]
: the trainable hash-grid
\mathcal{H}
is binarized to
\{-1,+1\}
using straight-through estimation (STE)
[
51
]
, as in
[
10
]
, so that its storage cost can be accounted for explicitly. The bit consumption of
\mathcal{H}
is estimated by the Bernoulli cross-entropy

\mathcal{L}_{\text{hash}}=M_{+}\!\left(-\log_{2}f_{+}\right)+M_{-}\!\left(-\log_{2}(1-f_{+})\right),

(13)

where
f_{+}
is the empirical frequency of the symbol “
+1
”, and
M_{+}
/
M_{-}
are the counts of “
+1
”/“
-1
” entries in
\mathcal{H}
. This encourages balanced yet compressible hash codes and amortizes the header bits.

Given hash-derived context vectors
\bm{h}_{i}
for anchor
i
and
\bm{h}_{p_{i}}
for its parent, we model the per-dimension distribution of the quantized attributes
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
with a Gaussian prior whose parameters are predicted by small MLPs:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{i},\hat{\bm{\sigma}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\text{MLP}_{d}\!\left(\bm{h}_{i},\bm{h}_{p_{i}}\right),

(14)

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{i}

\displaystyle=Q_{0}\cdot\!\left(1+\tanh\!\bigl(\text{MLP}_{q}(\bm{h}_{i},\bm{h}_{p_{i}})\bigr)\right).

Here,
\text{MLP}_{d}
outputs the mean and scale of the Gaussian for each attribute channel, while
\text{MLP}_{q}
predicts an adaptive quantization step
\bm{q}_{i}
to balance precision and rate. Under the common factorization assumption across channels, the probability of a scalar symbol is given by integrating the Gaussian over its quantization bin:

\displaystyle p(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{q}_{i}}^{\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{q}_{i}}\phi_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(15)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{i}\!\left(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{q}_{i}\right)-\Phi_{i}\!\left(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{q}_{i}\right),

where
\phi_{i}
and
\Phi_{i}
are the probability density function (PDF) and cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
\mathcal{N}(\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{i},\hat{\bm{\sigma}}_{i})
. AE then encodes symbols using these probabilities, while training minimizes the expected bit cost over anchors and channels:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{D^{f}+D^{s}+D^{o}}\!-\log_{2}p\!\left(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i,j}\right),

(16)

with
N
the number of anchors. Combined with quantization-aware training and parent–child conditioning through
(\bm{h}_{i},\bm{h}_{p_{i}})
, this formulation yields differentiable, context-adaptive priors that reduce symbol entropy and stabilize bitrate across LoDs, while keeping the hash-grid overhead measurable and controlled by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{hash}}
.

III-F

Training and Coding

In the whole training pipeline, ProGS constructs the overall loss by combining the supervisory and regularization terms discussed above so as to jointly optimize fidelity, compactness, and progressive consistency:

\mathcal{L}\;=\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{c2f}}\;+\;\lambda_{\text{vol}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{vol}}\;+\;\lambda_{\text{NCE}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}\;+\;\lambda_{\text{e}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{e}},

(17)

with the entropy-related term normalized per encoded symbol as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{e}}\;=\;\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{hash}}}{N\!\left(D^{f}+D^{s}+D^{o}\right)}.

(18)

Here,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{c2f}}
enforces cross-level supervision so that lower LoDs learn to approximate higher-level renderings,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{vol}}
penalizes the total volume of all generated Gaussians to discourage oversized primitives and promote sparsity,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}
enhances inter-level MI by contrasting parent–child anchors against negatives, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{e}}
jointly encourages low-entropy, context-predictable attributes and a well-conditioned hash context. The scalar weights
\lambda_{\text{vol}}
,
\lambda_{\text{NCE}}
, and
\lambda_{\text{e}}
control the trade-offs among geometric compactness, MI promotion, and rate; in particular, sweeping
\lambda_{\text{e}}
yields different operating points along the rate–distortion curve. The denominator
N\!\left(D^{f}\!+\!D^{s}\!+\!D^{o}\right)
normalizes by the number of symbols (anchors) and attribute dimensionality to keep
\mathcal{L}_{\text{e}}
scale-invariant across scenes and settings.

For practical encoding/decoding, we adopt a header–chunk pipeline that aligns with progressive transmission. First, the hash-grid
\mathcal{H}
is encoded/decoded once via AE/AD together with the parameters of the lightweight MLPs that perform context-driven distribution estimation and quantization; this header is cached and reused for all subsequent levels. Afterward, decoding proceeds in a topologically valid order from root to leaves: at each level, the context queried from
\mathcal{H}
conditions the probability models used by
\text{MLP}_{d}
and
\text{MLP}_{q}
to decode (or encode) the anchor attributes. The coordinates of first-level anchors are stored verbatim to seed the hierarchy, whereas each subsequent-level anchor is represented compactly by 23 bit, in which 20 bits record the index of its unique parent in the previous level and 3 bits encode the octant offset relative to its parent. Therefore, the absolute positions are reconstructed deterministically from the voxel grid. This design keeps the header amortized, enables per-level independent decoding, and ensures that higher LoDs can be appended incrementally without reprocessing earlier data, which is essential for low-latency progressive rendering.

IV-C

Bitstream Analysis

Fig.
6
breaks down the storage cost of each component in the encoded stream and clarifies how bits are allocated across progressive levels. The “Header” comprises the hash-grid
\mathcal{H}
and lightweight MLP parameters, and is transmitted once and amortized over all LoDs; after it is parsed, subsequent chunks mostly contain structural metadata and entropy-coded anchor attributes. Because ProGS enforces strict octree properties, the first decoded level (LoD 1) carries a relatively larger proportion of structural bits to ensure relatively complete scene representation; as the hierarchy grows, the fraction of structural bits decreases and the attribute portion becomes dominant. This shift reflects the intended rate allocation: coarse layers invest in scaffolding the hierarchy for reliable progressive starts, while deeper layers spend bits on fine-grained appearance and geometry details that most affect fidelity. The context-based entropy model further reduces redundancy by correlating attributes with their spatial context, lowering symbol entropy and stabilizing bitrate. Overall, the analysis shows that ProGS’s bitstream is front-loaded with reusable context information, followed by level-wise chunks that are independently decodable, yielding efficient startup, predictable growth in quality, and principled storage usage at each stage of progressive transmission.

. In contrast, the coding path remains lightweight thanks to the LoD-structured design: each level is encoded/decoded only once and can be streamed or cached independently. The per-level encoding and decoding time measured on GPU is summarized in Tab.

\mathcal{H}

After the header is loaded, decoding proceeds in a root-to-leaf order with entropy decoding of anchor attributes and on-the-fly Gaussian generation, so the marginal latency of adding an LoD is modest and largely proportional to the number of active anchors at that depth. Rendering throughput (FPS) for each LoD is also reported in Tab.

Overall, despite the heavier training stage, ProGS delivers short per-level coding time and consistent real-time rendering; its progressive chunking enables low-latency scene startup and incremental refinement without re-encoding, which is crucial for interactive viewing and adaptive streaming.

V
Conclusion

This paper presents ProGS, a novel codec designed to facilitate progressive coding for 3DGS. ProGS addresses the challenges associated with efficiently compressing large 3D scene representations while preserving rendering quality, making it suitable for real-time streaming applications with varying network conditions. By leveraging hierarchical octree structures and introducing adaptive anchor adjustments, ProGS enables scalable and efficient compression of 3DGS data. Moreover, this work also introduces MI enhancement techniques, such as the InfoNCE loss and coarse-to-fine optimization, to improve the visual quality of lower-level anchors and ensure high-fidelity scene representations even at lower levels of detail. Extensive experiments demonstrate that ProGS achieves superior performance, with up to 45× reduction in storage requirements and over 10% improvement in visual quality compared to existing SOTA methods. ProGS is the first progressive coding framework for 3DGS and offers a promising solution for real-time 3D scene rendering, providing a balance between efficient data compression and high-quality rendering. Future work could explore further optimization strategies for ProGS, including real-time adaptive encoding and decoding mechanisms that can dynamically adjust based on available resources and bandwidth, further enhancing its applicability in diverse scenarios.
