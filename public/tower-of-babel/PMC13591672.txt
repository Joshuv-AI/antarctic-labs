Evaluation of the association between nutrition knowledge and nutritional status among public and private upper primary school pupils
Authors: Juliana Nyasordzi, Prisca Dufie Boakye, David Adedia, Jonathan Annan-Asare, Alice Koryo-Dabrah, Eunice Berko Nartey, Daniel Mensah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo promote appropriate dietary habits, nutrition knowledge is essential at an early age, as well as providing a healthy food environment. This study evaluated nutrition knowledge and its relation to the nutritional status of upper primary school pupils from public and private basic schools.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 388 randomly selected upper primary school pupils, 210 from public and 178 from private schools in Ho. A questionnaire was administered to collect data on socio-demographics, nutrition knowledge, diet history, and nutritional status. Nutrition knowledge was assessed with multiple-choice questions. Correct responses were aggregated for each pupil. Anthropometric measurements were taken to assess pupils' nutritional status. Dietary intake was assessed by a 24-h recall to determine dietary diversity. Ordinal regression was used to examine the association between nutrition knowledge and ordered nutritional status in SPSS version 26.ResultsPrivate school pupils had a higher nutrition knowledge score (20.8 ± 3.6) than public school pupils (15.0 ± 3.5). Undernutrition was higher among public school pupils, whilst overnutrition was higher among private school pupils. A total of 36 private school pupils (20.2%) had a waist circumference-for-age > 85th percentile, compared to 7 public school pupils (3.3%). A high level of nutritional knowledge was associated with a marginal, non-significant reduction in the likelihood of a pupil falling into elevated BMI and waist circumference categories across both school types.ConclusionsNutrition knowledge was higher among private school pupils than their public school counterparts. Stratification by nutrition status among both groups revealed a distinct divergence: undernutrition was more prevalent in public schools, whereas overnutrition predominated in private schools. Additionally, high nutrition knowledge marginally reduced the risk of falling into high BMI and waist circumference categories, but the difference was not statistically significant. To address the suboptimal nutritional status observed across both school sectors, targeted interventions such as shifting nutrition knowledge from purely theoretical form to an experiential type, behavior focused health education at an early age are required. While nutrition knowledge remains foundational, optimizing a child's nutritional status also requires a multi-factorial approach that targets home and school food environments, physical activity levels, portion control, and socio-economic constraints.Keywords: Nutrition knowledge, Nutritional status, Pupils, Primary school, Public and private

Introduction

Parallel to obesity in the adult population, increased childhood obesity has been reported across the world, and Africa is no exception [2, 3]. High childhood obesity has been reported in Ghana [8, 26, 27], predominantly in urban, middle-class societies and in affluent primary schools. Children are exposed to energy-dense foods, snacks, sugar-sweetened beverages, large portion size meals, increased screen time, video games, and indoor activities [20, 21, 42], which promote weight gain.

The likelihood of obesity tracking from childhood into adulthood and the associated risk of non-communicable diseases (NCD) emerging early in life is a cause for concern. Childhood obesity has been associated with hypertension, early markers of cardiovascular disease, insulin resistance, psychological effects, and disability. NCDs were hitherto considered to be diseases of the aged; their emergence earlier in life marks a critical health concern [30, 36]. Thus, the earlier onset of these conditions extends the disease's lifetime duration, causing diminished quality of life, escalating personal and societal health care expenditures, and eventually diminished economic productivity [36]. Consequently, childhood represents a critical window for behavioral modification, lifestyle education, and the cultivation of health-promoting habits that persist to adulthood.

Nutrition education is increasingly being acknowledged as an important factor that can influence a population's dietary behavior and nutritional status. It provides the knowledge, attitudes, and practices necessary for making informed dietary and lifestyle decisions that can lead to optimal nutritional status [9].

Healthy dietary and lifestyle practices established early in life can track into adulthood [29, 30]. Reports suggest that young children in developing countries are making unhealthy dietary choices, possibly due to inadequate dietary knowledge and a misperception of healthy foods [22]. To foster optimal dietary habits, early acquisition of nutrition knowledge must be paired with a healthy food environment at home and in schools. Educational institutions, particularly basic schools, are highly effective settings for instilling healthy behaviors [43]. The school environment is suitable because it offers continuous, intensive contact with children, which can help build lifelong healthy behaviors early in life [23].

Ghana has both state-administered (public) and individually administered (private) school systems. Students attending private schools are generally perceived to demonstrate better academic performance, attributed in part to comparatively better learning environments, facilities, and smaller class sizes [11]. It is therefore plausible that meaningful differences exist in nutrition knowledge and nutritional status between pupils enrolled in private and public schools. Thus, in this preliminary study, we sought to evaluate nutrition knowledge and nutritional status, as well as the relationship between nutrition knowledge and nutritional status, among upper primary school pupils from selected private and public basic schools in the Ho municipality.

Methods

Study settings

The study was conducted in the Ho municipality, the capital town, and one of the five Municipalities in the Volta Region. The Ho municipality has an estimated population of 177,281. Females constitute 52.7% of the population, whilst males make up 47.3% [17]. Approximately 62.1% of the population resides in urban areas, and 37.9% live in rural areas. The majority of residents are young, accounting for about 31% of the population. Agriculture is the main economic activity of the majority of residents, with a small proportion employed in the public and private sectors [17].

Study design and population

This baseline cross-sectional study randomly selected participants from upper primary school pupils within the Ho Municipality. In the Ghanaian education system, schooling spans from kindergarten through to Junior High School, with varying definitions across the different levels. Specifically, pupils in Primary 1 to 3 are designated as lower primary pupils, whilst those in Primary 4 to 6 constitute the upper primary school pupils [4]. This study’s subjects were in the upper primary school category.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To be eligible for inclusion, participants had to be upper primary school pupils enrolled in one of the selected public and private schools. Also, inclusion of a pupil required written informed consent from the parent or guardian, along with the pupil’s formal assent. Additionally, institutional permission was secured through seeking informed consent from each included school’s headteacher. All pupils in the targeted classes were eligible to participate if they met these inclusion criteria.

Regarding the exclusion criteria, pupils were excluded from participation if they were absent during the day of data collection or if parental or guardian consent was not obtained. Pupils who also declined to assent, regardless of parental approval, were also excluded. Also, pupils with medical conditions known to affect nutritional status were deemed ineligible.

Sample size determination

The sample size for this study was determined using the Cochran formula [13]

where n is the minimum required sample size, Z is the Z-score corresponding to a 95% confidence interval (1.96), p is the estimated proportion of pupils with adequate nutrition knowledge (0.40), based on a study conducted by Quaidoo et al. [33] which reported that 36.3% of primary school children in Ghana had an adequate nutritional knowledge, and d is the margin of error (0.05). Substituting these values into the equation yielded a minimum sample size of 369 pupils. Accounting for a 5% non-response rate, the sample size was adjusted upward to 388 pupils.

Sampling procedure

There are 79 public primary schools and 64 private primary school in the Ho municipality [28]. All the educational institutions from preschool to tertiary levels in the municipality are grouped into 11 circuits. These circuits were used as clusters for the selection of schools in this study. Every third school listed in 4 predetermined circuits based on cooperation from school administrators, approval from parents, and geographic representation was randomly selected to participate in the study. Two public schools included in the study were selected from 2 different circuits. Three private schools were selected from 2 different circuits as well. Hence the five schools that participated in this study were selected from 4 different circuits in the Ho municipality. To determine the number of participants to be selected from each school type, the enrolment of pupils in each school type was divided by the total enrolment across all five selected schools i.e. 477 [25], then multiplied by the total sample size of 388. The public schools had higher enrolment, accounting for 54.1% of the total enrolment across all five selected schools, whilst private schools accounted for the remaining 45.9%. Multiplying these proportions by the total sample size yielded 210 from public schools and 178 from private schools. Within each school, the allocated sample was further distributed among primary 4, 5 and 6 classes, and the participants were selected using simple random sampling technique.

Data collection

A semi-structured questionnaire was administered to pupils one-on-one in the classroom to gather information on sociodemographic characteristics, nutritional status, nutritional knowledge, and many others. Nutrition knowledge was assessed using 30 multiple-choice questions on various aspects of nutrition, ranging from the definition of a balanced diet to the functions of various food groups, which was adapted from similar studies conducted in the subregion and modified to suit our local context [31]. Participants' dietary intake was assessed using a 24-h recall method. Additionally, the mode of commuting to school and for leisure activities during school hours was assessed.

Anthropometric measurements, including weight, height, and waist circumference, were obtained using a standard protocol. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a Seca 878 weighing scale, whilst height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm with a Seca stadiometer 213 (Seca, gmbh & co.kg, Hammer Steindamm 3–25, 22,089, Hamburg, Germany), and waist circumference was measured at the end of expiration using a tape measure. These anthropometric measurements were used to determine pupils’ nutritional status.

The questionnaire was pre-tested among 20 upper primary school pupils who met the study’s inclusion criteria but were not included in the final study.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 26.0. The data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, and results from categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, whilst continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations. For the nutrition knowledge score, each pupil’s correct response to questions assessing nutrition knowledge was awarded 1 mark. Correct responses were aggregated into a total score for each pupil and school type. The nutrient analysis template was used to analyze dietary intake data and determine participants' dietary diversity.

An independent-samples t-test was used to compare body mass index, waist circumference, nutrition knowledge score, and dietary diversity score between public and private school participants.

The association between nutrition knowledge and nutritional status was analyzed using ordinal regression. Based on body mass index and waist circumference for age, participants were categorized as underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obese as ordinal variables for BMI for age, and as < 85th percentile and ˃85th percentile for waist circumference for age. In the analysis, body mass index and waist circumference for age were used as ordered categorical outcome variables, whilst nutrition knowledge score was the continuous predictor variable. The models were adjusted for age, sex, and pupil class. Statistical significance was determined at a p-value of < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The research protocol conforms to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Health and Allied Sciences Research Ethics Committee (UHAS-REC), with protocol identification number UHAS-REC A.8[28] 22–23.

Similarly, the association between NKS and waist circumference, adjusted for age, sex, and class of pupils among public school pupils, showed an inverse relationship with ß = −0.122 (p-value = 0.288). Possibly, as nutrition knowledge increases, the likelihood of being in a higher category, that is, ˃85th percentile waist circumference, slightly decreases. This could also mean that, as NKS increases by one unit, waist circumference may decrease by about 0.122 cm among public school pupils, but this difference is not statistically significant.

Among private school participants, the relationship between NKS and BMI-for-age, adjusted for age, sex, and class, showed a similarly weak inverse association (β = − 0.012, p = 0.792).

This indicates that as nutrition knowledge increases, the likelihood of a pupil falling into the obese category decreases. In other words, as NKS increases by one unit, BMI is likely to decrease by 0.012 kg/m2 among private school pupils; however, the relationship was not statistically significant.

In the same vein, the association between NKS and waist circumference, adjusted for age, sex, and class of pupils, among private school pupils showed an inverse relationship with a β = −0.082 (p = 0.133). This implies that as nutrition knowledge increases, the likelihood of being in a higher category (waist circumference > 85th percentile) decreases among private school pupils.

Stated differently, an increase of one unit in NKS was possibly associated with a decrease of 0.082 cm in waist circumference among private school pupils, which was not statistically significant.

Discussion

This study evaluated the association between nutrition knowledge and nutritional status among upper primary school pupils in public and private schools.

A comparative assessment of pupils’ knowledge of basic nutrition concepts and nutritional status in public and private schools revealed differences in dietary knowledge and nutritional status.

Private school pupils demonstrated notably higher dietary knowledge than their public school counterparts. Our finding is similar to that reported in a study evaluating the impact of a school-based health and nutrition education program on knowledge and behavior among urban Indian school children. The study reported lower baseline knowledge scores among public school children than among their private school counterparts. Only a small proportion of public school children provided correct responses regarding protein, carbohydrates, and saturated fats in that study [38]. Additionally, studies from Ghana [7], Nigeria [1], and Nepal [35] have reported disparities in nutrition knowledge between public and private school pupils, with the latter, that is, private school pupils, demonstrating higher levels of knowledge than the former.

Our study showed notable differences in nutritional status among public and private school pupils. Underweight was higher among public school pupils than among private school pupils. A similar nutritional status has been reported in other studies conducted among public and private schools. For instance, a study in northwest Ethiopia that assessed undernutrition and its associated factors among public and private primary school children reported a higher prevalence of undernutrition among public school children than those attending private schools [6]. Similarly, Agbozo et al. 2016 [4] reported a sevenfold higher prevalence of underweight among public school pupils compared to their private school counterparts. Other studies, such as those conducted by Akor et al. 2010 [15, 39], also reported higher levels of undernutrition among public school pupils compared to private school pupils.

Private school pupils in this study were more likely to be overweight and obese compared to public school pupils. This finding aligns with results from other studies that also reported a higher proportion of overweight and obesity among private school pupils compared to public school pupils [2–5]. These studies attributed the higher prevalence of overweight and obesity among private school pupils to a sedentary lifestyle, including long hours spent playing indoor games and watching television, reduced active commuting to school, and higher parental socioeconomic status. Most private school pupils in our study had a higher waist circumference than their public school peers, with 85% above the 85th percentile.

There were also differences in the mode of commuting to school and outdoor activities engaged in by pupils during school break time between pupils from the two types of schools. Public school participants were more likely to walk to school and play outside their classroom during break time, whilst pupils from private schools were more likely to be driven to school in their parents’ private cars and do not usually play outside their classroom. This result is consistent with Ajayi's [5] findings, which reported that the majority of pupils in public schools walked at least 1 km to school daily. Also, pupils in public schools were more physically active compared to their private school counterparts. Previous studies have shown that increased screen time, larger family size, and passive transportation, that is, transportation mostly by vehicle, were associated with a greater risk of overweight and obesity in school children. Prolong screen time is ususally associated with extended time of consuming more food, reduced physical activity and increased sedentary behaviour leading to lower energy expenditure [14, 18].

We also observed a marginal inverse relationship between nutrition knowledge and nutritional status (BMI and waist circumference) among pupils in both school types, though these associations were not statistically significant.

The private school pupils in this study had a relatively high dietary diversity score compared to their public school counterparts. This suggests that dietary diversity alone may not adequately reflect overall diet quality, since a varied diet can still include energy-dense foods, sweets, and sugar-sweetened beverages.

A previous cross-sectional study among Iranian children and adolescents reported that dietary diversity score was positively associated with obesity and abdominal obesity; however, this association was not significant after accounting for energy intake [16]. Similarly, a nationally representative study conducted in Iran among children and adolescents observed that a higher dietary diversity score was associated with higher anthropometric indices and a greater risk of obesity. Thus public health recommendations promoting high dietary diversity should be accompanied with messages of portion size control of foods high in energy [19].

Our result somewhat aligns with a Chinese study that assessed dietary knowledge and its association with overweight and obesity among Chinese children and adolescents, and reported that dietary knowledge was inversely associated with the odds of overweight and obesity, as defined by BMI and waist circumference [45]. Children and adolescents with low dietary knowledge had significantly higher risks of BMI defined as overweight or obesity, and waist circumference defined as obesity than those with high dietary knowledge [45].

However, in another study that sought to explore the relationship between nutrition and physical activity knowledge and nutritional status of school-aged children, reported that there was a weak but positive correlation between nutrition knowledge and BMI-for-age among school children [7], which differs from the results in this study, which can be attributable to differences in study methodology and population.

These findings are important in view of the recent upsurge in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease MASLD in children and adolescents attributable to the global increase in overweight and obesity in these younger populations [37]. Detary behaviors and patterns are relevant risk factors linked with the development of MASLD [34]. Some studies have reported associations between diets high in energy, simple carbohydrates, and unhealthy fats coupled with sedentary lifestyle as plausible triggers of MASLD [24]. The mechanism of association between unhealthy dietary habits, lifestyle and MASLD is still emerging however, insulin resistance and central obesity are key in the development and progression of MASLD. Visceral adipocytes store and mobilize triglycerides rapidly than adipocytes in other areas, which heightens the supply of free fatty acids promoting development of MASLD [37]. Emerging evidence also suggests that MASLD can track along the lifecourse and may cause early adverse effects on cardiac function, emphasizing its wider implications on cardiometabolic health [10, 12, 32, 40]. Therefore, promoting adequate food intake, limiting intake of energy-dense foods like sweets and sugar-sweetened beverages, creating a healthier food environment, and increasing physical activity in children and adolescents are essential for preventing metabolic and cardiovascular complications.

Conclusion

This study shows notable differences between public and private school pupils in terms of nutrition knowledge, dietary diversity and nutritional status. Private school pupils had higher nutrition knowledge, a higher dietary diversity and also a higher BMI and a larger waist circumference for age compared to their public school counterparts. Undernutrition was common in public school whilst overnutrition was observed in private school pupils. Public school pupils were more physically active compared to their private school counterparts. An increased nutrition knowledge marginally reduced the likelihood of a pupil falling into a high BMI and waist circumference for age categories, but this was not statistically significant.

Recommendations

Interventions to improve dietary knowledge as well as factors underlying undernutrition and overnutrition to promote optimal nutritional status should be enhanced in the different school settings. Whilst interventions to promote adequate food intake among pupils in public basic schools are needed to reduce the levels of undernutrition, avenues to increase physical activity and to reduce sedentary life are necessary among pupils in private schools since they are more prone to overweight and obesity.

In addition to nutrition knowledge other factors such as socio-economic factors, portion size control, the food environment, physical activity at home and in school may be crucial factors impacting nutritional status among school pupils. Thus targeted interventions are needed in the various school types to reduce the levels of sub optimal nutritional status.

For instance, private schools should promote practical outdoor physical activity lessons and provide its accompanying facilities as well as engage health professions to promote appropriate dietary practices among parents during parent teacher association meetings. Schools can also ensure only healthy foods are sold on school premises. Future studies can include assessment of body composition of participants to improve on findings.

Limitations

The study was conducted only among two public and three private schools in the Ho municipality. The included circuits were not randomly selected. Other biochemical assessments such as total cholesterol, triglycerides, visceral fat etc. could not be assessed which could have improved the findings of the study. These limit the generalizability of the findings.
