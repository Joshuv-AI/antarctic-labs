Vertex Dismissibility and Scalability of Simplicial Complexes Journal: Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series A
Mohammed Rafiq Namiq

Abstract
We introduce vertex dismissible and scalable simplicial complexes, generalizing the classical notions of vertex decomposability and shellability. We prove that a complex satisfies these properties if and only if its initial dimension skeleton is vertex decomposable or shellable, respectively. Algebraically, we define vertex divisible ideals and ideals with degree quotients, proving they are the exact Alexander duals of these complexes. This establishes a corresponding topological and homological hierarchy that interpolates between classical structural properties and the initially Cohen–Macaulay condition. Furthermore, we demonstrate that for complexes of initial dimension one and the independence complexes of co-chordal and cycle graphs, vertex dismissibility, scalability, and initial Cohen–Macaulayness are equivalent to weak connectedness. Finally, we provide a complete skeletal characterization of these properties, a generalized perspective that recovers numerous classical theorems as immediate consequences.

Introduction

Alexander duality provides a systematic correspondence between the combinatorial topology of a simplicial complex
\Delta
and the homological invariants of its Stanley–Reisner ring
\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
. The foundational Eagon–Reiner theorem
[
3
]
establishes that
\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if the dual ideal
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
admits a linear resolution. This dual correspondence naturally extends to generalized algebraic structures. For instance, a complex
\Delta
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
is componentwise linear
[
5
]
.

Combinatorially, the classical properties of shellability and vertex decomposability
[
1
,
2
]
form strictly stronger subclasses of sequentially Cohen–Macaulay complexes. Under Alexander duality, shellable complexes correspond to ideals with linear quotients
[
6
]
, while vertex decomposable complexes correspond to vertex splittable ideals
[
8
]
.

Recently, this hierarchy was expanded with the introduction of initially Cohen–Macaulay complexes and ideals with degree resolutions
[
9
,
10
]
. A complex
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if
\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta]=\mdim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
, and an ideal
I
admits a degree resolution if
\reg I=\deg I
. The initially Cohen–Macaulay property of a complex
\Delta
is precisely dual to
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
possessing a degree resolution.

However, a structural gap remains. Because classical vertex decomposability and shellability are strictly stronger than the initially Cohen–Macaulay property, there must exist intermediate combinatorial conditions whose algebraic duals complete the hierarchy. We resolve this by introducing vertex dismissible complexes and scalable complexes. These classes generalize vertex decomposability and shellability by restricting the requisite combinatorial conditions to the initial dimension skeleton. We prove that a complex is vertex dismissible (resp., scalable) if and only if its initial dimension skeleton is vertex decomposable (resp., shellable), establishing the strict combinatorial hierarchy

\text{Vertex Dismissible}\implies\text{Scalable}\implies\text{Initially Cohen--Macaulay.}

To complete the Alexander dual picture, we introduce vertex divisible ideals and ideals with degree quotients. We prove these are the exact algebraic duals to vertex dismissible and scalable complexes, respectively. This yields the parallel homological hierarchy

\text{Vertex Divisible}\implies\text{Degree Quotients}\implies\text{Degree Resolution.}

This provides a unified approach that strictly interpolates between classical combinatorial properties and the initially Cohen–Macaulay condition. The complete hierarchy established in this paper is summarized in the diagram below.

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}&&&&\Delta\mbox{ Cohen--Macaulay}\\
&&&&\big\Downarrow\\
\Delta\mbox{ vertex decomposable}&\Longrightarrow&\Delta\mbox{ shellable}&\Longrightarrow&\Delta\mbox{ sequentially Cohen--Macaulay}\\
\big\Downarrow&&\big\Downarrow&&\big\Downarrow\\
\Delta\mbox{ vertex dismissible}&\Longrightarrow&\Delta\mbox{ scalable}&\Longrightarrow&\Delta\mbox{ initially Cohen--Macaulay}\\
\Updownarrow&&\Updownarrow&&\Updownarrow\\
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}\mbox{ vertex divisible}&\Longrightarrow&I_{\Delta^{\vee}}\mbox{ degree quotients}&\Longrightarrow&I_{\Delta^{\vee}}\mbox{ degree resolution}\\
\Uparrow&&\Uparrow&&\Uparrow\\
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}\mbox{ vertex splittable}&\Longrightarrow&I_{\Delta^{\vee}}\mbox{ linear quotients}&\Longrightarrow&I_{\Delta^{\vee}}\mbox{ componentwise linear}\\
&&&&\Uparrow\\
&&&&I_{\Delta^{\vee}}\mbox{ linear resolution}\end{array}

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the necessary background. Section
3
introduces vertex dismissible complexes and vertex divisible ideals, establishing their duality. Section
4
introduces scalable complexes and ideals with degree quotients, establishing their position within the hierarchy. In Section
5
, we prove that for simplicial complexes of initial dimension one and for the independence complexes of co-chordal and cycle graphs, the properties of being vertex dismissible, scalable, and initially Cohen–Macaulay are all equivalent to the combinatorial property of weak connectedness. Finally, in Section
6
, we provide a characterization of these properties via pure skeletons, a perspective that recovers numerous classical theorems as immediate structural consequences.

2
Preliminaries

Let
X
be a finite vertex set. A
simplicial complex

\Delta
on
X
is a collection of subsets (faces) closed under inclusion. The maximal faces are
facets
, and their set is denoted
\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
. The dimension of a face
F
is
|F|-1
, and
\dim\Delta=\max_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta)}\dim F
. We define the
initial dimension
of
\Delta
as
\mdim\Delta=\min_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta)}\dim F
. The complex is
pure
if
\mdim\Delta=\dim\Delta
.

For
F\in\Delta
, let
\langle F\rangle
denote the simplex generated by
F
. For a vertex
x\in X
, the
deletion
and
link
are
\del_{\Delta}(x)=\{F\in\Delta\mid x\notin F\}
and
\link_{\Delta}(x)=\{F\setminus\{x\}\mid x\in F\in\Delta\}
, respectively. The
k
-skeleton

\Delta^{k}
consists of all faces of dimension at most
k
, whereas the
pure
k
-skeleton

\Delta^{[k]}
is the subcomplex generated by all
k
-dimensional faces of
\Delta
. We specifically denote the pure
initial dimension skeleton
by
\Delta^{\mdim\Delta}
.

Let
R=\mathbb{K}[X]
be the polynomial ring over a field
\mathbb{K}
. The
Stanley–Reisner ideal

I_{\Delta}
is the squarefree monomial ideal generated by the non-faces of
\Delta
. The
Alexander dual
complex is
\Delta^{\vee}=\{X\setminus F\mid F\notin\Delta\}
, whose associated ideal is
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}=(x^{X\setminus F}\mid F\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta))
. For a monomial ideal
I
, let
\mathcal{G}(I)
be its minimal generating set. The degree of
I
, denoted
\deg I
, is the maximum degree of a generator in
\mathcal{G}(I)
. This yields the duality relation
\mdim\Delta=|X|-\deg I_{\Delta^{\vee}}-1
.

For an integer
k\geq 0
, the
squarefree truncation

I_{k}
is the ideal generated by all squarefree monomials of
I
of degree at least
k
, while
I_{[k]}
denotes the ideal generated by the squarefree monomials of degree exactly
k
.

Finally, the
independence complex

\Delta_{G}
of a graph
G
is the complex of independent vertex sets in
G
. A graph
G
is
co-chordal
if its complement
\overline{G}
is chordal. We assume standard terminology for Cohen–Macaulay, sequentially Cohen–Macaulay, and initially Cohen–Macaulay complexes over
\mathbb{K}
.

3
Vertex Dismissible Simplicial Complexes and Vertex Divisible Ideals

In this section, we introduce vertex dismissible complexes and vertex divisible ideals. We first recall the classical inductive structure of Provan and Billera
[
11
]
, later extended to non-pure complexes by Björner and Wachs
[
2
]
. A vertex
x\in X
is a
shedding vertex
of a simplicial complex
\Delta
if for every facet
F\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
containing
x
, there exists a facet
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
such that
x\notin F^{\prime}
and
F\setminus\{x\}\subseteq F^{\prime}
. The complex
\Delta
is
vertex decomposable
if it is a simplex (including
\emptyset
), or if it admits a shedding vertex
x
such that both
\del_{\Delta}(x)
and
\link_{\Delta}(x)
are vertex decomposable.

Definition 3.1
.

A vertex
x
of a simplicial complex
\Delta
is a
dismissing vertex
if

\mdim\del_{\Delta}(x)\geq\mdim\Delta.

Remark 3.2
.

Dismissibility is strictly weaker than being a shedding vertex for non-pure complexes. For example, in
\Delta=\langle ab,cde\rangle
, the vertex
c
is dismissing since
\mdim\del_{\Delta}(c)=\mdim\Delta=1
, but it is not shedding because
\{d,e\}\not\subseteq\{a,b\}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Every shedding vertex of a simplicial complex
\Delta
is dismissing.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Delta
be a shedding vertex and
F\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
with
\dim F=\mdim\Delta
. If
x\notin F
,
F\in\del_{\Delta}(x)
and
\mdim\del_{\Delta}(x)\geq\mdim\Delta
. If
x\in F
, shedding implies there exists
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
such that
x\notin F^{\prime}
and
F\setminus\{x\}\subseteq F^{\prime}
. Since
F
is a facet,
F^{\prime}\neq F\setminus\{x\}
, giving
\dim F^{\prime}\geq\dim F=\mdim\Delta
. Thus,
F^{\prime}\in\del_{\Delta}(x)
implies
\mdim\del_{\Delta}(x)\geq\mdim\Delta
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex and let
F\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
have minimum dimension. If
x\in F
is a dismissing vertex, then
x
is shedding.

Proof.

Since
x
is dismissing,
\mdim\del_{\Delta}(x)\geq\mdim\Delta
. The face
F\setminus\{x\}\in\del_{\Delta}(x)
has dimension
\mdim\Delta-1
, so it cannot be a facet of
\del_{\Delta}(x)
. It is therefore strictly contained in some facet
F^{\prime}
of
\del_{\Delta}(x)
. Extending
F^{\prime}
to a facet of
\Delta
not containing
x
yields the required shedding condition.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

In a pure simplicial complex, a vertex is dismissing if and only if it is shedding.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

A vertex
x\in V(\Delta)
is dismissing in
\Delta
if and only if it is a shedding vertex of the pure skeleton
\Delta^{\mdim\Delta}
.

Proof.

If
x
is shedding in
\Delta^{\mdim\Delta}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta^{\mdim\Delta})
contains
x
, there is a facet
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta^{\mdim\Delta})
with
x\notin F^{\prime}
and
F\setminus\{x\}\subseteq F^{\prime}
. Thus
F^{\prime}\in\del_{\Delta}(x)
with
\dim F^{\prime}=\mdim\Delta
, so
\mdim\del_{\Delta}(x)\geq\mdim\Delta
.
Conversely, if
x
is dismissing in
\Delta
and
F\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta^{\mdim\Delta})
contains
x
, then
F\setminus\{x\}
has dimension
\mdim\Delta-1
and is not maximal in
\del_{\Delta}(x)
. It is therefore contained in a face
G\in\del_{\Delta}(x)
of dimension
\mdim\Delta
. Any
\mdim\Delta
-dimensional face
F^{\prime}\subseteq G
containing
F\setminus\{x\}
is a facet of
\Delta^{\mdim\Delta}
avoiding
x
, fulfilling the shedding condition.
∎

Definition 3.7
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is
vertex dismissible
if it is a simplex or contains a dismissing vertex
x
such that
\del_{\Delta}(x)
and
\link_{\Delta}(x)
are vertex dismissible.

Remark 3.8
.

This generalizes vertex decomposability by replacing shedding vertices with dismissing vertices.

Proposition 3.9
.

Every vertex decomposable simplicial complex is vertex dismissible.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
|V(\Delta)|
. If
\Delta
is vertex decomposable, its shedding vertex
x
is dismissing by Lemma
3.3
. By induction, the vertex decomposable subcomplexes
\del_{\Delta}(x)
and
\link_{\Delta}(x)
are vertex dismissible.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

A pure simplicial complex is vertex decomposable if and only if it is vertex dismissible.

Proof.

This is immediate from Lemma
3.5
.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

If a pure
d
-dimensional simplicial complex
\Delta
is vertex decomposable, then its
k
-skeleton
\Delta^{[k]}
is vertex decomposable for all
0\leq k<d
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
d
and
|V(\Delta)|
. Let
x
be a shedding vertex of
\Delta
. Any facet
F\in\Delta^{[k]}
containing
x
extends to a
d
-facet
G\in\Delta
. By the shedding condition, there exists a
d
-facet
G^{\prime}
avoiding
x
with
G\setminus\{x\}\subseteq G^{\prime}
. Choosing a
k
-face
F^{\prime}\subseteq G^{\prime}
containing
F\setminus\{x\}
shows
x
is shedding in
\Delta^{[k]}
. Since

\del_{\Delta^{[k]}}(x)=(\del_{\Delta}(x))^{[k]}\quad\text{and}\quad\link_{\Delta^{[k]}}(x)=(\link_{\Delta}(x))^{[k-1]},

both subcomplexes are vertex decomposable by the induction hypothesis, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex with
\mdim\Delta\geq k
. If
\Delta^{[k]}
is vertex decomposable and every shedding vertex of
\Delta^{[k]}
is a dismissing vertex of
\Delta
, then
\Delta
is vertex dismissible.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
|V(\Delta)|
. Assuming
\Delta
is not a simplex, let
x
be a shedding vertex of
\Delta^{[k]}
,
x
is dismissing in
\Delta
by hypothesis. Since
\mdim\Delta\geq k
, the identities

\del_{\Delta^{[k]}}(x)=(\del_{\Delta}(x))^{[k]}\quad\text{and}\quad\link_{\Delta^{[k]}}(x)=(\link_{\Delta}(x))^{[k-1]}

hold. Both are vertex decomposable, so by induction,
\del_{\Delta}(x)
and
\link_{\Delta}(x)
are vertex dismissible. Thus,
\Delta
is vertex dismissible.
∎

We characterize vertex dismissibility via the initial dimension skeleton.

Proposition 3.13
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is vertex dismissible if and only if its initial dimension skeleton
\Delta^{\mdim\Delta}
is vertex decomposable.

Proof.

Let
b=\mdim\Delta
. We proceed by induction on
|V(\Delta)|
. The claim is trivial if
\Delta
is a simplex.

Assume
\Delta
is vertex dismissible with dismissing vertex
x
. By induction, the initial dimension skeletons of
\del_{\Delta}(x)
and
\link_{\Delta}(x)
are vertex decomposable. Since
\mdim\del_{\Delta}(x)\geq b
and
\mdim\link_{\Delta}(x)\geq b-1
, Lemma
3.11
implies their
b
- and
(b-1)
-skeletons which equal
\del_{\Delta^{[b]}}(x)
and
\link_{\Delta^{[b]}}(x)
, respectively, are vertex decomposable. By Lemma
3.6
,
x
is shedding in
\Delta^{[b]}
, making
\Delta^{[b]}
vertex decomposable.

Conversely, if
\Delta^{[b]}
is vertex decomposable with shedding vertex
x
, Lemma
3.6
implies
x
is dismissing in
\Delta
. The subcomplexes
\del_{\Delta^{[b]}}(x)=(\del_{\Delta}(x))^{[b]}
and
\link_{\Delta^{[b]}}(x)=(\link_{\Delta}(x))^{[b-1]}
are vertex decomposable. By Lemma
3.12
, the initial dimension skeletons of
\del_{\Delta}(x)
and
\link_{\Delta}(x)
are vertex decomposable. The induction hypothesis implies both are vertex dismissible, so
\Delta
is vertex dismissible.
∎

Corollary 3.14
.

A pure simplicial complex is vertex dismissible if and only if it is vertex decomposable.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Proposition
3.13
.
∎

We introduce the algebraic dual to vertex dismissibility, relaxing the exact splitting condition of classical vertex splittable ideals. A monomial ideal
I
is
vertex splittable
if it is either the zero ideal, the entire ring
R
, or if there exists a variable
x
and vertex splittable monomial ideals
J
and
K
such that
I=xJ+K
, where
x
does not divide any minimal generator of
J
or
K
, and
\mathcal{G}(I)=x\mathcal{G}(J)\cup\mathcal{G}(K)
.

Definition 3.15
.

A variable
x
of a monomial ideal
I
is
dividing
if
\deg(I:x)+1\leq\deg I
.

Lemma 3.16
.

A vertex
x\in V(\Delta)
is dismissing if and only if
x
is a dividing vertex of the Alexander dual ideal
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
.

Proof.

Let
n=|V(\Delta)|
. Using the standard duality identity
\mdim\Gamma=|V(\Gamma)|-\deg I_{\Gamma^{\vee}}-1
, we have

\mdim\del_{\Delta}(x)\geq\mdim\Delta\iff(n-1)-\deg(I_{\Delta^{\vee}}:x)-1\geq n-\deg I_{\Delta^{\vee}}-1.

Rearranging this inequality yields
\deg I_{\Delta^{\vee}}\geq\deg(I_{\Delta^{\vee}}:x)+1
.
∎

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex and
x\in V(\Delta)
. Then

I_{\Delta^{\vee}}=xI_{\del_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}+I_{\link_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}},

where
I_{\del_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}=(I_{\Delta^{\vee}}:x)
and
I_{\link_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}=I_{\Delta^{\vee}}\cap\mathbb{K}[X\setminus\{x\}]
. Moreover,
I_{\link_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}\subseteq I_{\del_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}
.

Proof.

The decomposition
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}=x(I_{\Delta^{\vee}}:x)+(I_{\Delta^{\vee}}\cap\mathbb{K}[X\setminus\{x\}])
and the corresponding Alexander dual identities for deletion and link are standard. The inclusion
I_{\link_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}\subseteq I_{\del_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}
follows trivially from the algebraic fact that
I\cap\mathbb{K}[X\setminus\{x\}]\subseteq(I:x)
for any ideal
I
.
∎

Remark 3.18
.

Unlike vertex splittable ideals, the minimal generating sets in
I=xJ+K
need not be disjoint. For example, if
I=(abc,aef,cdef)
, choosing the dividing vertex
x=c
gives
J=(ab,aef,def)
and
K=(aef)
. We have
I=cJ+K
and
K\subseteq J
, but
\mathcal{G}(J)\cap\mathcal{G}(K)=\{aef\}\neq\emptyset
.

Definition 3.19
.

A monomial ideal
I\subset\mathbb{K}[X]
is
vertex divisible
if
I\in\{(0),\mathbb{K}[X]\}
,
I
is generated by a single monomial, or there exists a dividing vertex
x
of
I
such that
I=xJ+K
for vertex divisible ideals
J,K\subset\mathbb{K}[X\setminus\{x\}]
with
K\subseteq J
.

We now prove the main duality theorem, establishing that vertex dismissibility is the exact topological counterpart to vertex divisibility.

Theorem 3.20
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is vertex dismissible if and only if its Alexander dual ideal
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
is vertex divisible.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
|V(\Delta)|
. The base cases where
\Delta
is a simplex or
|V(\Delta)|\leq 1
hold trivially, as the corresponding dual ideals are
(0)
,
(1)
, or generated by variables, all of which are vertex divisible.

For the inductive step, Lemma
3.16
implies that
\Delta
has a dismissing vertex
x
if and only if
x
is a dividing vertex of
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
. Furthermore, Lemma
3.17
provides the exact decomposition
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}=xI_{\del_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}+I_{\link_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}
, where
I_{\link_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}\subseteq I_{\del_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}
. This provides the algebraic decomposition
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}=xJ+K
with
J=I_{\del_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}
and
K=I_{\link_{\Delta}(x)^{\vee}}
. By the inductive hypothesis, the subcomplexes
\del_{\Delta}(x)
and
\link_{\Delta}(x)
are vertex dismissible if and only if
J
and
K
are vertex divisible. The equivalence immediately follows.
∎

Corollary 3.21
.

Let
I
be a squarefree monomial ideal with
\deg I=k
. Then
I
is vertex divisible if and only if its squarefree truncation
I_{k}
is vertex splittable.

Proof.

Let
\Delta
be the independence complex of
I
, so
\mdim\Delta=n-k-1
. By Theorem
3.20
and Proposition
3.13
,
I
is vertex divisible if and only if the skeleton
\Delta^{\mdim\Delta}
is vertex decomposable. By
[
10
, Lemma 4.1]
,
\Delta^{\mdim\Delta}=(\Delta_{I_{k}})^{\vee}
. Since a complex is vertex decomposable if and only if the Alexander dual of its independence ideal is vertex splittable,
(\Delta_{I_{k}})^{\vee}
is vertex decomposable if and only if
I_{k}
is vertex splittable.
∎

4
Scalable Complexes and Ideals with Degree Quotients

We develop a parallel generalization for shellability. Recall
[
2
]
that a complex is shellable if its facets can be ordered
F_{1},\dots,F_{q}
such that
\langle F_{j}\rangle\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{j-1}\langle F_{i}\rangle
is pure of dimension
\dim F_{j}-1
for all
j\geq 2
.

Definition 4.1
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is
scalable
if its facets admit an order
F_{1},\dots,F_{q}
such that for all
2\leq j\leq q
, the intersection subcomplex
\Delta_{j}=\langle F_{j}\rangle\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{j-1}\langle F_{i}\rangle
satisfies

\mdim\Delta_{j}\geq\mdim\Delta-1.

Such an ordering is called a
scaling order
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Every shellable simplicial complex is scalable. For pure complexes, scalability and shellability are equivalent.

Proof.

If
\Delta
is shellable,
\mdim\Delta_{j}=\dim F_{j}-1\geq\mdim\Delta-1
, satisfying scalability. Conversely, if
\Delta
is pure, the condition
\mdim\Delta_{j}\geq\dim\Delta-1
implies the subcomplex
\Delta_{j}\subseteq\langle F_{j}\rangle
to be pure of dimension
\dim\Delta-1
, recovering the exact definition of shellability.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is scalable if and only if its initial dimension skeleton
\Delta^{\mdim\Delta}
is shellable.

Proof.

Let
b=\mdim\Delta
. If
\Delta
is scalable, refining the scaling order by shelling the
b
-skeleton of each facet yields a valid shelling of
\Delta^{[b]}
. Conversely, if
\Delta^{[b]}
admits a shelling order
G_{1},\dots,G_{m}
, ordering the facets of
\Delta
by
\min\{r\mid G_{r}\subseteq F\}
induces a scaling order. The required
(b-1)
-dimensional intersection faces lift directly from the shelling steps of
\Delta^{[b]}
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

If
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
are vertex dismissible (resp., scalable), then their topological join
\Delta_{1}*\Delta_{2}
is vertex dismissible (resp., scalable).

Proof.

Since
\mdim(\Delta_{1}*\Delta_{2})=\mdim\Delta_{1}+\mdim\Delta_{2}+1
, the initial dimension skeleton of the join distributes as
(\Delta_{1}*\Delta_{2})^{\mdim(\Delta_{1}*\Delta_{2})}=\Delta_{1}^{\mdim\Delta_{1}}*\Delta_{2}^{\mdim\Delta_{2}}
. The result follows from Theorems
3.13
and
4.3
, as vertex decomposability and shellability are preserved under joins
[
11
]
.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\Delta
be vertex dismissible (resp., scalable) with
b=\mdim\Delta
. If
\sigma\in\Delta
is contained in a
b
-dimensional facet, then
\link_{\Delta}(\sigma)
is vertex dismissible (resp., scalable).

Proof.

Let
\Gamma=\Delta^{[b]}
. Because
\sigma
lies in a minimum-dimensional facet,
\mdim(\link_{\Delta}(\sigma))=b-|\sigma|
, and its initial dimension skeleton is exactly
\link_{\Gamma}(\sigma)
. Since
\Gamma
is vertex decomposable (resp., shellable), and these classical properties are inherited by links, the conclusion holds.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

The requirement that
\sigma
belongs to a minimum-dimensional facet is strictly necessary. Consider
\Delta=\langle ab,bcd,def\rangle
with
\mdim\Delta=1
.

Its
1
-skeleton is a connected chordal graph, implying
\Delta
is scalable and vertex dismissible. However, the vertex
d
belongs only to
2
-dimensional facets. Its link,
\link_{\Delta}(d)=\langle bc,ef\rangle
, has initial dimension
1
but consists of two disjoint edges. Because this
1
-skeleton is disconnected, it is neither shellable nor vertex decomposable.

Theorem 4.7
.

Every vertex dismissible simplicial complex is scalable.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
|V(\Delta)|
. The claim is trivial if
\Delta
is a simplex.
Assume
\Delta
has a dismissing vertex
x
. By induction,
\del_{\Delta}(x)
and
\link_{\Delta}(x)
are scalable, with scaling orders
F_{1},\dots,F_{r}
and
G_{1},\dots,G_{s}
, respectively. We claim the concatenated sequence

F_{1},\dots,F_{r},G_{1}\cup\{x\},\dots,G_{s}\cup\{x\}

is a scaling order for
\Delta
.
For
j\leq r
, the intersection condition holds because
\mdim\del_{\Delta}(x)\geq\mdim\Delta
and
F_{j}
satisfies the scaling condition in
\del_{\Delta}(x)
. For a facet
F_{k}=G_{k}\cup\{x\}
with
k>r
,
G_{k}
is a facet of
\link_{\Delta}(x)
, so
G_{k}\in\del_{\Delta}(x)
and is contained in some
F_{m}
(
m\leq r
). Thus,
F_{k}\cap F_{m}=G_{k}
. Consequently, the intersection of
F_{k}
with the preceding facets contains
G_{k}
. Since
|G_{k}|=|F_{k}|-1\geq\mdim\Delta-1
, the dimension bound
\mdim(\langle F_{k}\rangle\cap\bigcup_{\ell<k}\langle F_{\ell}\rangle)\geq\mdim\Delta-1
is satisfied.
∎

Recall that a simplicial complex
\Delta
is
initially Cohen–Macaulay
over a field
\mathbb{K}
if its initial dimension skeleton
\Delta^{\mdim\Delta}
is Cohen–Macaulay over
\mathbb{K}

[
10
]
, which is equivalent to
\dep(\mathbb{K}[\Delta])=\mdim\Delta+1
.

Theorem 4.8
.

Every scalable simplicial complex is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

Let
F_{1},\dots,F_{q}
be a scaling order of
\Delta
, and set
\Delta_{j}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{j}\langle F_{i}\rangle
. We show
\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{j}]\geq\mdim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
by induction on
j
. For
j=1
,
\Delta_{1}
is a simplex, so
\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{1}]=|F_{1}|\geq\mdim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
.

Assume the bound holds for
j-1
. The Mayer–Vietoris exact sequence

0\to\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{j}]\to\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{j-1}]\oplus\mathbb{K}[\langle F_{j}\rangle]\to\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{j-1}\cap\langle F_{j}\rangle]\to 0

and the Depth Lemma imply

\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{j}]\geq\min\{\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{j-1}],\dep\mathbb{K}[\langle F_{j}\rangle],\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{j-1}\cap\langle F_{j}\rangle]+1\}.

By the inductive hypothesis,
\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{j-1}]\geq\mdim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
, and trivially
\dep\mathbb{K}[\langle F_{j}\rangle]=|F_{j}|\geq\mdim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
. By the scalability condition,
\mdim(\Delta_{j-1}\cap\langle F_{j}\rangle)\geq\mdim\Delta-1
, which implies
\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{j-1}\cap\langle F_{j}\rangle]\geq\mdim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]-1
. Hence, the third term in the minimum is at least
\mdim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
, yielding
\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{j}]\geq\mdim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
.

Taking
j=q
gives
\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta]\geq\mdim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
. Since the reverse inequality
\dep\mathbb{K}[\Delta]\leq\mdim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
holds generally
[
10
, Proposition 3.12]
, equality is attained, proving
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

Every vertex dismissible simplicial complex is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

This follows directly from Theorems
4.7
and
4.8
.
∎

We directly recover the classical pure case results of Provan–Billera
[
11
]
and Garsia
[
4
]
.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
\Delta
be a pure simplicial complex.

1.

If
\Delta
is vertex dismissible, it is shellable.

2.

If
\Delta
is scalable, it is Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Theorems
4.7
and
4.8
, noting that for pure complexes, scalability implies shellability by Proposition
4.2
and initial Cohen–Macaulayness is equivalent to Cohen–Macaulayness
[

.
∎

We now address the algebraic dual. Recall
[
9
]
that a monomial ideal
I
has a
degree resolution
if its Betti numbers satisfy
\beta_{i,j}(I)=0
for all
j>\deg I+i
.

Lemma 4.11
.

A monomial ideal
I
is vertex divisible if and only if its polarization
I^{\pol}
is vertex divisible.

Proof.

This follows by induction on the number of variables. If
I=xJ+K
is vertex divisible, polarizing yields
I^{\pol}=x_{j}J^{\pol}+K^{\pol}
. Since polarization preserves degrees and colon ideals,
\deg(I^{\pol}:x_{j})+1=\deg(I:x)+1\leq\deg I=\deg I^{\pol}
, making
x_{j}
a dividing vertex. By induction,
J^{\pol}
and
K^{\pol}
are vertex divisible. The converse holds via depolarization.
∎

Theorem 4.12
.

Every vertex divisible ideal has a degree resolution.

Proof.

Let
I
be vertex divisible. By Lemma
4.11
,
I^{\pol}
is vertex divisible. Let
\Delta
be the Alexander dual of the Stanley–Reisner complex of
I^{\pol}
, so
I^{\pol}=I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
. By Theorem
3.20
,
\Delta
is vertex dismissible, and hence initially Cohen–Macaulay by Corollary
4.9
. By
[
10
, Proposition 4.4]
, a complex is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if its dual ideal has a degree resolution. Since
I^{\pol}
has a degree resolution and this homological property is invariant under polarization,
I
also has a degree resolution.
∎

We now address the algebraic duals of scalability and linear quotients. Recall that a monomial ideal
I
has
linear quotients
if its minimal generators
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
can be ordered such that
(u_{1},\dots,u_{j-1}):u_{j}
is generated by variables for all
j

[
6

Definition 4.13
.

A monomial ideal
I
has
degree quotients
if its minimal generators can be ordered
f_{1},\dots,f_{q}
such that for all
j\in\{2,\dots,q\}
, the colon ideal
J_{j}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{j-1}):f_{j}
satisfies

\deg(J_{j})\leq\deg I-\deg f_{j}+1.

Note that if
I
is generated in a single degree, this condition reduces to
\deg(J_{j})\leq 1
, recovering the exact definition of linear quotients.

Theorem 4.14
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is scalable if and only if
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
has degree quotients.

Proof.

Let
F_{1},\dots,F_{q}
be the facets of
\Delta
, corresponding to minimal generators
f_{j}=x^{X\setminus F_{j}}
of
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
. For
j\geq 2
, let
\Delta_{j}=\langle F_{j}\rangle\cap\bigcup_{i<j}\langle F_{i}\rangle
. By Alexander duality, the ideal of
\Delta_{j}^{\vee}
corresponds to
J_{j}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{j-1}):f_{j}
.
Using the dual dimension formula
\mdim\Delta_{j}=|F_{j}|-\deg(J_{j})-1
, the scalability condition
\mdim\Delta_{j}\geq\mdim\Delta-1
translates to:

|F_{j}|-\deg(J_{j})-1\geq(|X|-\deg I_{\Delta^{\vee}}-1)-1.

Since
|F_{j}|=|X|-\deg f_{j}
, this rearranges exactly to
\deg(J_{j})\leq\deg I_{\Delta^{\vee}}-\deg f_{j}+1
. Thus, the facets of
\Delta
form a scaling order if and only if the generators of
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
form a degree quotient order.
∎

Corollary 4.15
.

A squarefree monomial ideal
I

Proof.

. Since a complex is shellable if and only if its dual ideal has linear quotients, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.16
.

A monomial ideal
I
has degree quotients if and only if its polarization
I^{\pol}
has degree quotients.

Proof.

Polarization preserves degrees of generators, degrees of ideals, and degrees of colon ideals. The defining inequality for degree quotients is therefore invariant under polarization.
∎

Theorem 4.17
.

If a monomial ideal is vertex divisible, then it has degree quotients. Furthermore, any monomial ideal with degree quotients has a degree resolution.

Let

has degree quotients. By Theorem

dictates

is initially Cohen–Macaulay. By

5
Equivalence of Properties for Specific Classes of Complexes

We show that for certain fundamental classes of simplicial complexes, the purely combinatorial condition of weak connectedness
[
10
]
is equivalent to our generalized structural properties and the algebraic property of being initially Cohen–Macaulay. Recall that a complex
\Delta
is
weakly connected
if any two facets
A,B
can be joined by a sequence of facets
F_{0}=A,\dots,F_{p}=B
such that
|F_{i}\cap F_{i+1}|\geq\mdim\Delta
for all
i
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex with
\mdim\Delta=1
. The following are equivalent:

1.

\Delta
is weakly connected.

2.

\Delta
is vertex dismissible.

3.

\Delta
is scalable.

4.

\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2): We proceed by induction on
|V(\Delta)|
. The base case is trivial. Since
\mdim\Delta=1
and
\Delta
is weakly connected, its
1
-skeleton
\Delta^{1}
is a connected graph. Thus,
\Delta^{1}
either contains a vertex
x
of degree
1
whose unique neighbor has degree at least
2
, or every vertex in
\Delta^{1}
has degree at least
2
. In both cases, there exists a vertex
x
such that
\del_{\Delta^{1}}(x)
has no isolated vertices, implying it remains pure
1
-dimensional. By induction,
\del_{\Delta^{1}}(x)
is vertex decomposable. Since
\link_{\Delta^{1}}(x)
has dimension at most
0
, it is also vertex decomposable. Thus
x
is shedding in
\Delta^{1}
, making
\Delta^{1}
vertex decomposable. By Theorem
3.13
,
\Delta
is vertex dismissible.

(2)
\implies
(3): Theorem
4.7
.

(3)
\implies
(4): Theorem
4.8
.

(4)
\implies
(1):
[
10
, Lemma 4.15]
.
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

If the independence complex
\Delta_{G}
of a co-chordal graph
G
is weakly connected, then
\Delta_{G}
is vertex dismissible.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n=|V(G)|
. The result is immediate if
G
is empty. Assume
n\geq 1
and
G
is not empty. Since
\overline{G}
is chordal but not a clique, it contains a simplicial vertex
x
. In
G
,
N_{\overline{G}}(x)
is an independent set. We claim
x
is a dismissing vertex of
\Delta_{G}
.

If
F\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta_{G})
with
\dim F=\mdim\Delta_{G}
, then either
x\notin F
(so
F\in\del_{\Delta_{G}}(x)
), or
x\in F
. If
x\in F
, weak connectedness yields a facet
F^{\prime}
with
|F\cap F^{\prime}|\geq\mdim\Delta_{G}
. Since
x
is simplicial in
\overline{G}
, its neighborhood behavior implies
F\setminus\{x\}\subseteq F^{\prime}
. Thus
F^{\prime}\in\del_{\Delta_{G}}(x)
. In both cases,
\mdim\del_{\Delta_{G}}(x)\geq\mdim\Delta_{G}
.

The subcomplexes
\del_{\Delta_{G}}(x)=\Delta_{G\setminus\{x\}}
and
\link_{\Delta_{G}}(x)=\Delta_{G\setminus N_{G}[x]}
are independence complexes of induced co-chordal subgraphs. Since
N_{\overline{G}}(x)
is a clique in
\overline{G}
,
N_{G}[x]
is a simplex in
\Delta_{G}
, making
\link_{\Delta_{G}}(x)
a simplex (hence trivially weakly connected and vertex dismissible). The weak connectivity of
\Delta_{G}
projects to
\del_{\Delta_{G}}(x)
because any sequence of facets traversing
x
can be rerouted through faces of dimension
\geq\mdim\Delta_{G}
avoiding
x
. By induction,
\del_{\Delta_{G}}(x)
is vertex dismissible, completing the proof.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

For the independence complex
\Delta_{G}
of a co-chordal graph
G
, the following are equivalent:

1.

\Delta_{G}
is weakly connected.

2.

\Delta_{G}
is vertex dismissible.

3.

\Delta_{G}
is scalable.

4.

\Delta_{G}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2) is Theorem
5.2
. The remaining implications follow exactly as in Theorem
5.1
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
C_{n}
be the cycle graph on

n\equiv 0

\Delta_{C_{n}}

(1)

,
\mdim\Delta_{C_{n}}=\lceil n/3\rceil-1
. For any vertex

is the independence complex of a path graph, with

(2)
\implies
(3)

and

.

6
Skeletal Characterizations of Combinatorial Properties

The properties introduced in this paper naturally unify with their classical counterparts through a generalized skeletal approach.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a property of pure simplicial complexes (e.g., vertex decomposability, shellability, or Cohen–Macaulayness). A simplicial complex
\Delta
is said to have the
k
-
\mathcal{P}
property
if its pure
j
-skeleton
\Delta^{[j]}
satisfies
\mathcal{P}
for all
j\leq k
.

Remark 6.2
.

Under this terminology, classical properties correspond to the global bound
k=\dim\Delta
, while our newly introduced initial analogues correspond to the extremal local bound
k=\mdim\Delta
. Explicitly:

1.

\Delta
is vertex decomposable (resp. shellable, sequentially Cohen–Macaulay) if and only if it is
(\dim\Delta)
-vertex decomposable (resp. shellable, Cohen–Macaulay).

2.

\Delta
is vertex dismissible (resp. scalable, initially Cohen–Macaulay) if and only if it is
(\mdim\Delta)
-vertex decomposable (resp. shellable, Cohen–Macaulay).

We now formally establish these skeletal equivalences for the classical properties.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex.

1.

\Delta
is vertex decomposable if and only if its pure
k
-skeleton
\Delta^{[k]}
is vertex decomposable for all
\mdim\Delta\leq k\leq\dim\Delta
.

2.

\Delta
is shellable if and only if its pure
k
-skeleton
\Delta^{[k]}
is shellable for all
\mdim\Delta\leq k\leq\dim\Delta
.

Proof.

For vertex decomposability, the forward direction follows by induction: a shedding vertex
x
of
\Delta
restricts to a shedding vertex of
\Delta^{[k]}
, and the property passes to links and deletions. Conversely, if each
\Delta^{[k]}
is vertex decomposable, a shedding vertex of the top-dimensional skeleton
\Delta^{[\dim\Delta]}
serves as a shedding vertex for
\Delta
. The result then follows by induction on
|V(\Delta)|
, using the identities
(\del_{\Delta}(x))^{[k]}=\del_{\Delta^{[k]}}(x)
and
(\link_{\Delta}(x))^{[k-1]}=\link_{\Delta^{[k]}}(x)
.

For shellability, filtering a shelling order of
\Delta
to facets of a fixed dimension
k
yields a shelling of
\Delta^{[k]}
. Conversely, concatenating the shelling orders of the pure skeletons
\Delta^{[k]}
in increasing order of
k
produces a valid shelling of
\Delta
, as the required pure codimension-one intersections are provided by the skeletal shelling steps.
∎

Applying Alexander duality (Theorems
3.20
and
4.14
) to the truncations of squarefree monomial ideals yields the exact algebraic counterpart. Let
I_{[k]}
denote the ideal generated by the squarefree monomials of
I
of degree
k
.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
I
be a squarefree monomial ideal.

1.

I
is splittable if and only if
I_{[k]}
is splittable for all
k\leq\deg I
.

2.

I
has linear quotients if and only if
I_{[k]}
has linear quotients for all
k\leq\deg I
.

Proof.

By
[
10
, Lemma 4.1]
, the Alexander dual of the independence complex of
I_{[k]}
corresponds exactly to a pure skeleton of the dual complex of
I
. The results follow immediately from Theorem
6.3
under the established dualities (vertex decomposability with splittability, and shellability with linear quotients).
∎

Remark 6.5
.

This skeletal perspective implies several classical results as immediate corollaries. Because the implications hold strictly for pure complexes (vertex decomposable
\implies
shellable
\implies
Cohen–Macaulay), Theorem
6.3
directly recovers that vertex decomposable complexes are shellable
[
2
]
and shellable complexes are sequentially Cohen–Macaulay
[
12
]
. Dually, via polarization and squarefree truncation, Theorem
6.4
recovers that splittable ideals have linear quotients
[
8
]
, and ideals with linear quotients are componentwise linear
[
7
]
.

7
Concluding Remarks and Open Questions

We conclude with several open questions concerning the homological and combinatorial invariants of these newly introduced classes.

1.

For a vertex splittable ideal
I=xJ+K
, the disjointness condition
\mathcal{G}(J)\cap\mathcal{G}(K)=\emptyset
guarantees the exact Betti splitting
\beta_{i,j}(I)=\beta_{i,j-1}(J)+\beta_{i,j}(K)

[
8
]
. Because vertex divisible ideals relax this disjointness, this equality becomes an upper bound. What is the precise homological correction term required to recover an exact Betti splitting?

2.

For an ideal
I=(f_{1},\dots,f_{q})
with linear quotients, the Betti numbers satisfy
\beta_{i}(I)=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\binom{m_{k}}{i}
, where
m_{k}
is the number of variables minimally generating the colon ideal
(f_{1},\dots,f_{k-1}):f_{k}

[
6
]
. Do ideals with degree quotients satisfy an analogous explicit Betti sum formula?

3.

If
\Delta
is scalable or initially Cohen–Macaulay, its initial dimension skeleton
\Gamma=\Delta^{\mdim\Delta}
is shellable or Cohen–Macaulay, implying the
h
-vector of
\Gamma
is nonnegative
[
12
]
. Since
f_{i}(\Delta)=f_{i}(\Gamma)
for all
i\leq\mdim\Delta
, what explicit nonnegativity conditions are imposed on the lower
f
-vector of
\Delta
?

4.

While the Stanley–Reisner ideal of a graph independence complex
\Delta_{G}
is generated by quadratic monomials, the ideal of its initial skeleton
\Delta_{G}^{\mdim\Delta_{G}}
generally is not. Characterize the families of graphs
G
for which
\Delta_{G}
is vertex dismissible or scalable.
