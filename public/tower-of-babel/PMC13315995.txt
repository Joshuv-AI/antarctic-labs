A logical interpretation of the data on optimal oxygen therapy targets for unresponsive adults in ICU after cardiac arrest
Authors: Paul J. Young
Published: 2026

Oxygen therapy targets in patients who have had a cardiac arrest have been an area of active research over the last 15 years. Interest in oxygen therapy targets after cardiac arrest was stimulated by a retrospective study reporting that early arterial hyperoxaemia in the intensive care unit (ICU) was independently associated with increased in-hospital mortality compared with hypoxia or normoxia.1 An independent association between early hyperoxaemia exposure after resuscitation and poor neurological function at hospital discharge was subsequently observed in a prospective observational study.2 Taken together, these data suggested the possibility that oxygen therapy regimens that limited exposure to high arterial oxygen concentrations might improve patient outcomes. However, there were a number of uncertainties. Critically, post cardiac arrest patients with significant shock and poor peripheral perfusion often receive higher concentrations of inspired oxygen than patients with normal peripheral perfusion because, in patients with poor peripheral perfusion, pulse oximetry recordings are unreliable. Accordingly, the association between hyperoxaemia and worse outcomes may have reflected residual confounding by illness severity, particularly the presence of severe shock and impaired peripheral perfusion.

Despite such considerations, experimental data also indicated that exposure to hyperoxaemia worsened reperfusion-related brain injury in animal cardiac arrest models.3 Because animal data focused on the immediate post-arrest period, and reported associations between oxygen exposure and outcomes focused on the early period following reperfusion, an initial focus of clinical trials was targets in the pre-hospital period. In the HyperOxic Therapy OR NormOxic Therapy after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest trial, we enrolled patients in the field after a cardiac arrest.4 We rapidly abandoned this strategy because obtaining reliable pulse oximetry recordings in the pre-hospital setting was not feasible and patients allocated to the normoxic arm appeared to have an increased risk of hypoxaemic episodes. Other investigators encountered similar issues.5 In the Reduction of Oxygen After Cardiac Arrest trial, unconscious cardiac arrest patients were randomised by paramedics to receive oxygen titration to achieve an oxygen saturation of either 90%–94% or 98%–100% until arrival in the ICU.6 Hypoxaemic episodes prior to ICU admission were almost twice as common in patients assigned to the lower oxygen target. Survival to hospital discharge occurred in 82 of 214 patients (38.3%) in the low-oxygen group and 101 of 211 (47.9%) in the high-oxygen group (unadjusted odds ratio: 0.68; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.46 to 1.00), raising the concern that early oxygen restriction might be harmful.6

In the ICU, continuous pulse oximetry combined with ready access to arterial blood gas analysis may alter the risk–benefit profile of conservative oxygen therapy. In the ICU Randomised Oxygen (ICU-ROX) trial,7 data for conservative oxygen therapy in the cardiac arrest subgroup were promising.8 For patients with suspected ischaemic encephalopathy, death by 180 days occurred in 37 of 86 patients (43%) in the conservative-oxygen group and in 46 of 78 (59%) in the usual-oxygen group (relative risk of 0.73; 95% CI, 0.54 to 0.99). Among these patients, an unfavourable outcome on the Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale occurred in 55% and 68% of the conservative- and usual-oxygen groups, respectively (relative risk of 0.81; 95% CI, 0.63 to 1.05). A benefit from conservative oxygen therapy in cardiac arrest patients was also suggested by an individual patient data meta-analysis of randomised clinical trials9 and, more recently, in a subgroup analysis of cardiac arrest patients included in the Pragmatic Investigation of Optimal Oxygen Targets trial.10 The Blood Pressure and Oxygenation Targets in Postresuscitation Care trial11 reported similar outcomes for conservative- and liberal-oxygen groups in cardiac arrest patients treated at two centres in Denmark but survival was markedly better than typically observed in multicentre cardiac arrest trials.12,13

The most recent and relevant evidence for the Australian and New Zealand ICU setting comes from the just-published Low OxyGen Intervention for Cardiac Arrest injury Limitation (LOGICAL) trial.14 This trial included 1840 patients from 53 ICUs; all ICUs but one ICU were in Australia and New Zealand and 1837 patients were from our region. The primary outcome was survival with favourable neurological function at 180 days as assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended (GOS-E). A favourable functional outcome (a GOS-E level of 5–8) was observed for 313 of 819 patients (38.2%) in the conservative-oxygen group and 353 of 890 patients (39.7%) in the liberal-oxygen group (adjusted risk ratio of 0.97; 95% CI, 0.87 to 1.09). The time from return of spontaneous circulation and from ICU admission to randomisation was longer in the LOGICAL trial than in cardiac arrest patients in the ICU-ROX trial. However, the effect of conservative oxygen therapy on survival with a favourable functional outcome appeared to be similar regardless of whether patients were enrolled earlier or later after return of circulation or ICU admission. Although the effect of conservative oxygen therapy on the primary outcome in the subgroup enrolled within 4 h of return of spontaneous circulation was compatible with potential benefit, the CIs were wide. The Mega-ROX hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy (Mega-ROX HIE) trial will report mortality outcomes for around 4000 patients randomly allocated to conservative-oxygen group or liberal-oxygen group after cardiac arrest.15 These results will substantially increase precision around any potential mortality effect of conservative oxygen therapy and will increase the generalisability of the evidence because patients in Mega-ROX HIE were recruited in more than 100 ICUs in 15 countries including a range of healthcare settings.

At present, there is little evidence to support fundamentally different oxygen therapy targets for mechanically ventilated post–cardiac arrest patients compared with other critically ill adults. Current evidence suggests that meticulous avoidance of mild hyperoxaemia is unnecessary, whereas avoidance of both hypoxaemia and extreme hyperoxaemia remains logical.
