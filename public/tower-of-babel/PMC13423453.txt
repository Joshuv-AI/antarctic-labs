Diagnostic Performance of Non-Colonographic Routine CT for Detecting Advanced Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Kohei Suzuki, Kenichi Utano, Natsuki Ishizaki, Kakeru Otomo, Masato Aizawa, Kana Tamazawa, Jun Wada, Yuka Utano, Tadayuki Takagi, Kazutomo Togashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: Advanced-stage colorectal cancers (CRCs) are occasionally detected on routine abdominal CT scans performed in daily clinical practice. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of routine CT scans for detecting advanced CRCs. Methods: We studied patients who underwent routine CT examinations for gastrointestinal symptoms or abnormalities between March 2015 and April 2025, and who subsequently underwent colonoscopy within six months. CT images were interpreted by two radiologists as part of routine clinical practice. Colonoscopic and pathological findings were used as reference standards for advanced CRC (p-T2 or deeper). Diagnostic performance was expressed as percentages with 95% confidence intervals. Results: A total of 350 patients were reviewed (female 44.6%; mean age 64.6 ± 17.2 years; intravenous contrast administration 44.6%). Colonoscopy identified 46 advanced CRCs (median size 56 mm; range 16-105 mm; T2 n=1, T3 n=16, T4 n=18, unknown n=11; proximal colon n=13, distal colon n=16, rectum n=17). Except for one CRC obscured by a large gastric gastrointestinal stromal tumor, all remaining 45 CRCs were suspected on CT (sensitivity 97.8% [88.5-99.9]). Among 304 patients without advanced CRC, 292 were correctly classified as negative (specificity 96.1% [93.2-97.9]). The positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and overall accuracy were 78.9% [66.1-88.6], 99.7% [98.1-100], and 96.3% [93.7-98.0], respectively. In subgroup analysis stratified by contrast enhancement, diagnostic accuracy was 96.8% [92.7-99.0] with contrast and 95.9% [92.0-98.2] without contrast (p=0.65). Conclusions: Routine abdominal CT without colonographic technique demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance for detecting advanced CRC. Keywords: colorectal cancer, advanced stage, computed tomography, diagnostic accuracy

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide[1], highlighting the critical need for early and accurate detection. While colonoscopy is considered the gold standard for CRC diagnosis due to its direct visualization and therapeutic capability, it remains an invasive procedure that may be contraindicated or poorly tolerated in elderly or frail patients[2]. In addition, the requirement for extensive bowel preparation contributes to reduced patient adherence and limits its utility in certain clinical settings.

Routine abdominal computed tomography (CT) is frequently performed in daily clinical workup for a variety of indications, e.g., evaluation of abdominal mass or pain, change bowel habit, and fever of unknown origin. Although these scans are not specifically designed for colorectal evaluation, they may incidentally reveal findings suggestive of CRC. CT colonography (CTC) achieves high sensitivity for detecting large polyps and cancer[3,4]. However, the necessity of such preparatory steps which involve bowel preparation and often include fecal tagging or oral contrast agents, restricts its general use and patient acceptability. Several studies have assessed the diagnostic performance of CT imaging for CRC using minimal preparation or oral contrast agents, reporting sensitivities of 78-94% and specificities of 84-99%[5-9]. In comparison, routine abdominal CT without bowel preparation or oral contrast presents a less burdensome alternative; however, its diagnostic utility for CRC has not been fully established. Although some evidence indicates that advanced colorectal lesions can be detected based on features such as focal wall thickening or lymphadenopathy, systematic evaluations of its diagnostic accuracy remain limited[10-14].

This study aims to evaluate the current diagnostic performance of routine abdominal CT, performed without bowel cleansing or oral contrast, for the detection of advanced-stage CRC (A-CRC). We also assess whether the use of intravenous contrast enhancement impacts diagnostic performance. By addressing these questions in a real-world setting, we aim to clarify the clinical value of routine abdominal CT as an adjunctive tool in the detection of A-CRC.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection (Figure 1)

Patient flow.

Prior to study initiation, approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Fukushima Medical University (IRB No. 2022-056; initial approval in 2022; protocol amendment approved on July 1, 2025). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and minimal-risk nature of the study.

This retrospective single-center study included patients who underwent routine abdominal CT between March 2015 and April 2025 as part of the diagnostic evaluation for gastrointestinal abnormalities and subsequently underwent conventional optical colonoscopy within six months of the CT examination. Patients referred to from other institutions for treatment of previously diagnosed colorectal cancer were not included. In contrast, patients referred for further diagnostic evaluation were eligible for inclusion. Patients who underwent abdominal CT and colonoscopy solely for post-endoscopic resection surveillance of invasive colorectal cancer or neuroendocrine tumors were excluded. For patients with multiple eligible CT scans, only the first scan was included in the analysis. Patients were excluded if they had a history of colorectal resection, had undergone CT colonography, or underwent CT examinations performed with oral contrast agents.

Data are not publicly available due to patient privacy considerations. De-identified data may be shared upon reasonable request to the corresponding author and with IRB approval.

CT Acquisition and Image Interpretation

All CT examinations were performed using an 80-detector-row scanner (Canon Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan). The scanning protocol used was: voltage (120 kV), rotation time (0.5 s), pitch (0.813), section thickness (1.0 mm), reconstruction interval (0.8 mm), and automatic tube current modulation system (Volume EC, SD10, Canon Medical Systems).

The indication for intravenous contrast-enhanced CT was left to the discretion of the attending outpatient physician. Contrast-enhanced CT was generally not performed in patients with impaired renal function (eGFR ≤30 mL/min/1.73 m2) or a known allergy to iodinated contrast media.

Two experienced, board-certified radiologists (25 and 19 years in CT interpretation, respectively) independently reviewed 3-mm reconstructed slices on a diagnostic workstation as part of routine clinical practice. They had access to the patients' demographic information and the indication for CT acquisition, as provided in the CT order request. After the initial interpretation by one radiologist, the findings were subsequently reviewed by the other radiologist for confirmation (double-checking).

Staging was determined according to the criteria of the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum (JSCCR)[16]. The M stage was assessed based on CT findings at the time of diagnosis, including hepatic metastases, peritoneal metastases, and distant lymph node metastases.

The entire colorectum was systematically examined, with focused assessment of index findings suggestive of CRC, including apparent intestinal wall thickening greater than 5 mm in the short-axis direction with a uniform CT attenuation value (>30 Hounsfield units), and pericolic lymph-node swelling defined as a short-axis diameter of 10 mm or greater.

Reference Standard

Colonoscopic findings were used as the reference standard. All A-CRCs were pathologically confirmed by biopsy or surgical specimens. For patients who did not undergo surgical resection, the diagnosis of A-CRC was based on typical colonoscopic findings, such as circumferential and irregular lesions. Tumor location was categorized as proximal or distal using the splenic flexure as the boundary; the proximal colon included the cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, transverse colon, and splenic flexure, whereas the distal colon included the descending colon and sigmoid colon.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the diagnostic performance of routine abdominal CT in detecting A-CRC, defined as tumors invading the muscularis propria or beyond. Diagnostic performance was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy.

Statistical Analysis

Diagnostic values are presented as percentages with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method. Comparisons were performed using the chi-square test, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

In the analysis including all cases, the diagnostic performance metrics of abdominal CT for detecting A-CRC exceeded 95%, except for the PPV. In subgroup analyses according to intravenous contrast enhancement, unenhanced CT demonstrated perfect sensitivity and NPV. In contrast, PPV was lowest for unenhanced CT (73.3%) and second lowest for enhanced CT (85.2%). No significant differences were observed between enhanced and unenhanced CT in any diagnostic parameter, including PPV.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we demonstrated that routine abdominal CT, performed without bowel preparation or oral contrast, achieved high diagnostic performance for the detection of A-CRC. These findings indicate that even non-targeted CT performed in routine clinical settings can reliably detect A-CRC, particularly when interpreted by experienced radiologists. The high NPV (99.7%) in this study suggests that a negative CT finding may provide reassurance against the presence of A-CRC, especially in patients unable to undergo colonoscopy. The high specificity (96.1%) also supports the reliability of CT in identifying true-negative cases.

So far, minimal-preparation CT have demonstrated an excellent diagnostic performance[5-9]. Nevertheless, evidence for truly routine, non-targeted abdominal CT remains heterogeneous, and current guidance does not support routine CT as a CRC screening test due to insufficient evidence[15]. Therefore, real-world data derived from routine CT interpreted in daily clinical workflow are needed to clarify its practical utility, particularly for clinically significant advanced CRCs. Notably, while evidence exists for enhanced CT[6,11], there is no supporting evidence for routine unenhanced CT in relevant clinical scenarios. In this context, our study provides real-world evidence for routine unenhanced CT and further demonstrates comparable diagnostic performance between unenhanced and enhanced examinations, thereby addressing an important evidence gap and offering a practical benchmark for opportunistic detection of clinically significant A-CRC.

Only one false-negative case was observed, in which an A-CRC in the sigmoid colon was masked by a large gastric GIST. This highlights a limitation of CT in cases where overlapping or adjacent pathology may obscure colorectal findings. On the other hand, 12 cases were initially suspected of malignancy on CT but were later confirmed to be benign on colonoscopy. These false positives included diverticulosis, ulcerative colitis, and large adenomas, which can mimic A-CRC on CT based on wall thickening or pericolic changes.

Notably, the diagnostic performance did not significantly differ between contrast-enhanced and unenhanced CT scans. This suggests that intravenous contrast administration may not be essential for identifying A-CRC, which is clinically relevant for patients with renal impairment or contrast allergies. Given the increasing frequency of CT imaging in the general population, particularly among older adults, the utility of routine CT as an opportunistic modality deserves consideration.

In this study, we investigate the diagnostic performance of A-CRC without including early-stage CRCs or premalignant polyps. However, this design choice is supported by several clinical and epidemiological considerations. First, even in advanced stages, CRC has a relatively favorable prognosis compared to other gastrointestinal malignancies, particularly when detected before the onset of distant metastasis[16]. Second, current population-based CRC screening programs widely rely on fecal immunochemical testing in Japan, which has limited sensitivity for early-stage CRC[17]. In practice, fecal immunochemical testing is more likely to identify patients with advanced neoplasia, including large adenomas and invasive carcinomas. Thus, our study's focus on A-CRC aligns with the practical target of existing screening strategies. Finally, because routine abdominal CT scans are already widely performed without bowel preparation or oral contrast, establishing their diagnostic utility for detecting A-CRC supports their potential role as a simplified and opportunistic modality. This may be especially valuable in older populations or those unfit for colonoscopy, offering a feasible route to cancer detection with minimal additional burden.

Several limitations warrant discussion. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, all CT images were interpreted by expert radiologists, which may overestimate diagnostic performance compared to general practice settings with varying levels of radiologist experience. Third, our study focused exclusively on A-CRC and did not assess the detectability of early-stage lesions or polyps, which remain challenging to identify without bowel cleansing. Finally, no interobserver variability was assessed, which is important for evaluating the robustness of CT interpretation in broader clinical contexts.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have shown great potential in diagnostic radiology, especially for detecting gastrointestinal cancers. Deep learning models can identify pancreatic and gastric cancers on CT scans with high accuracy by analyzing complex imaging features[18,19] and subtle findings often missed by humans. Our study confirmed the strong performance of routine CT for detecting A-CRC. Building on this, future work should develop AI-based tools for CRC detection[18,19]. Since CT is widely used and AI is increasingly integrated into practice, such tools could enable opportunistic diagnosis from existing scans and improve patient outcomes.
