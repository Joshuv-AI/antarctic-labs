Knowledge, attitudes and practices towards neurodevelopment among parents of children born moderate to late preterm in Changzhou, China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mei Xue, Yu Wan, Fei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) of parents of children born moderate to late preterm (32 to <37 weeks’ gestation) regarding their children’s neurodevelopment.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingThis study was conducted from March to June 2025 at the Second People’s Hospital of Changzhou.ParticipantsParents of children born moderate to late preterm were surveyed in the outpatient clinic when their children were at preschool age.InterventionsNo.Primary and secondary outcome measuresKAP scores were assessed using a questionnaire, and differences among parents were compared across different demographic characteristics. KAP scores were converted to percentages and interpreted using a 70% descriptive cut-off. Structural equation modelling was employed to investigate the inter-relationships among the KAP dimensions.ResultsThis study included 499 participants, with a mean maternal age of 34.03±5.42 years; the majority were women (92.79%). The mean knowledge score was 7.22±3.53 (out of a possible maximum of 18), the mean attitude score was 23.91±5.16 (out of a possible maximum of 35) and the mean practice score was 25.84±5.08 (out of a possible maximum of 40). The knowledge scores correlated with the attitude (r=0.193, p<0.001) and practice (r=0.558, p<0.001) scores, while the attitude scores correlated with the practice scores (r=0.312, p<0.001). The path analysis revealed that knowledge had a direct influence on attitude (β=0.290, p=0.011) and practice (β=0.582, p=0.006). Attitude had a direct influence on practice (β=0.228, p=0.012). Knowledge also had an indirect influence on practice through its impact on attitude (β=0.066, p=0.005).ConclusionParents’ KAP scores were below the descriptive 70% sufficiency threshold, particularly for knowledge, suggesting unmet needs for clearer follow-up communication and family-centred neurodevelopmental education. Interventions should focus on strengthening family-centred education, clearer follow-up communication and parental engagement in neurodevelopmental monitoring and early intervention.

Background

Preterm birth is a major global challenge for neonatal health. Moderate to late preterm infants (32–36 weeks of gestation) have elevated risks for neurodevelopmental disorders despite fewer acute complications and higher survival rates than very preterm or extremely preterm infants, warranting early follow-up and intervention. Indeed, children born moderate to late preterm have significantly higher risks of neurodevelopmental impairment compared with full-term peers, including motor, cognitive, visual and hearing deficits, with the greatest risks seen in earlier gestational ages.13 Cognitive impairments, especially in language and executive function, are the leading neurodevelopmental issue for this group, often persisting into school age and adulthood.25 Moderate to late preterm birth is linked to more than double the risk for neurodevelopmental disability at 2 years, especially for cognitive deficits. Independent predictors include male sex, socioeconomic disadvantage and preeclampsia.2 3 Longitudinal studies indicate that while extremely preterm infants have higher rates of severe disability, moderate to late preterm children still show elevated risks for psychiatric, behavioural and learning disorders.46 Early intervention programmes focusing on parent-infant interactions, breastfeeding support and developmental monitoring can improve outcomes, highlighting the need for tailored support for this population.710

Parents shape the neurodevelopment of children born preterm, especially those born moderate to late preterm, yet studies reveal significant gaps in their knowledge and caregiving practices, often driven by misunderstandings and lack of tailored clinical support.1 2 11 Parental stress and knowledge levels are critical determinants: higher maternal parenting stress is linked to worse developmental outcomes, emphasising the need for psychosocial support and caregiver education.1 12 13 Self-management and empowerment play critical roles in improving neurodevelopmental outcomes for moderate to late preterm infants by fostering parental confidence, engagement and sustained skill-building at home.1416 Still, the gaps in knowledge, misconceptions, misunderstandings and barriers must first be identified before interventions can be designed.

Knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) studies enable the identification of these deficits using both quantitative and qualitative methods.17 18 Nevertheless, it is known that many parents have limited knowledge of risk-reducing practices, including safe sleep positions, the importance of early intervention and recognition of developmental milestones.1921 Misconceptions (eg, ‘almost full-term’) contribute to underestimation of risk and missed opportunities for monitoring and stimulation.11 Attitudes range from unwarranted reassurance to heightened anxiety and uncertainty, often exacerbated by inadequate discharge instructions and absence of ongoing clinical support.13 Early developmental intervention programmes for preterm infants can improve cognitive outcomes, highlighting the importance of parental support, education and developmental follow-up.22 23 It is necessary to assess the local KAP to be able to design tailored interventions.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the KAP of parents of moderate to late preterm infants regarding their children’s neurodevelopment. The results could help fill research gaps, develop targeted support strategies, optimise health education and ultimately improve the long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes of this large population.

Methods

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from March to June 2025 at the Second People’s Hospital of Changzhou and enrolled parents of children born moderate to late preterm. Before 1 year of age, these children were routinely followed up in the hospital’s preterm infant outpatient clinic, where parents completed the questionnaire on site. Standard care mainly consisted of regular outpatient follow-up, including growth and developmental monitoring and routine guidance for parents on feeding, sleep and child development. Specifically, follow-up care included assessment of feeding and nutrition, growth parameters and head circumference and age-appropriate developmental evaluation. Parents also received routine health education on feeding, sleep, developmental monitoring and home care and were advised to complete recommended screening and assessment procedures, such as hearing screening, retinopathy of prematurity screening when indicated, neurobehavioral assessment and developmental assessment during follow-up. Early intervention support mainly included parent education through outpatient consultation and health education materials, guidance on parent-child interaction and home-based developmental training, and referral for further rehabilitation assessment or intervention if developmental concerns were identified. This routine care may have influenced parental KAP. After 1 year of age, most children underwent routine health check-ups in local community healthcare settings; therefore, parents were contacted using the family contact information recorded in the hospital information system and were invited to complete the questionnaire remotely. At the time of questionnaire completion, the children had a mean age of 40.58±10.96 months and were no longer infants; therefore, parents reported their KAP regarding neurodevelopment retrospectively based on their child’s developmental experience. The inclusion criteria were (1) parents of children born moderate to late preterm at 32–36+6 weeks of gestation, (2) availability of family contact information in the hospital system or attendance at the hospital’s preterm infant outpatient clinic and (3) willingness to participate in the survey. The exclusion criterion was being a parent of a deceased child.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was designed with reference to the relevant literature and guidelines.2427 Because this questionnaire was purpose-built for this study, the response formats were selected according to the characteristics of each KAP domain and with reference to commonly used KAP questionnaire designs. The knowledge items used a 3-point response format (‘Very familiar’, ‘Have heard of it’ and ‘Not clear’) to reflect levels of familiarity while keeping the response options simple and easy to understand, whereas the attitude and practice items used 5-point Likert scales to capture degrees of agreement and frequency, respectively.28 The questionnaire was pilot-tested among 32 participants, yielding a reliability coefficient of 0.911. In addition, sampling adequacy and construct validity were evaluated. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin value was 0.882 (p<0.001). Confirmatory factor analysis showed an acceptable model fit, with chi-square/degrees of freedom ratio (CMIN/DF) =3.691, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA)=0.074, incremental fit index (IFI)=0.919, Tucker-Lewis index (TLI)=0.904 and comparative fit index (CFI)=0.918.

The final questionnaire, which was in Chinese, consisted of four dimensions (see online supplemental material for the full version). The basic information section comprised 15 items, the knowledge dimension included 10 items, the attitude dimension included 7 items and the practice dimension included 8 items. In the knowledge dimension, ‘Very familiar’ was scored 2 points, ‘Have heard of it’ scored 1 point and ‘Not clear’ scored 0 points. Item K10 was included as an attention-check item to identify invalid responses and improve response quality; it was not included in the knowledge score, resulting in a total score range of 0–18. The attitude and practice dimensions use a 5-point Likert scale, with possible score ranges of 7–35 and 8–40, respectively. For descriptive interpretation, KAP scores were converted into percentages of the maximum possible score. According to the commonly used convention in previous KAP studies,2931 and with reference to KAP studies conducted among parents of children and caregiver populations,32 33 a 70% percentage-based descriptive cut-off was used in this study. Scores below 70% of the maximum possible score were interpreted as insufficient, whereas scores of 70% or higher were interpreted as sufficient. This cut-off was used only as a descriptive benchmark because no population-specific normative or validated reference values are available for parental KAP regarding neurodevelopment among children born moderate to late preterm.

Questionnaire distribution

The questionnaires were distributed either through face-to-face paper-based collection in the hospital’s preterm infant outpatient clinic or through QR code access to the online survey on the Wenjuanxing platform. For children younger than 1 year, parents usually completed the questionnaire during outpatient follow-up visits at our hospital. For children older than 1 year, whose routine follow-up was often conducted in local community healthcare settings, parents were contacted using the family contact information available in the hospital information system and invited to complete the questionnaire remotely. The final questionnaire was administered in Chinese. All questionnaires were reviewed for completeness, and those with incomplete information were excluded. Questionnaires with an error in item K10 were excluded.

Sample size

The minimum required sample size for this cross-sectional survey was estimated using the standard formula for proportion-based studies: n=Z2P(1−P)/d2, where Z represents the standard normal variate at a 95% confidence level (1.96), P denotes the anticipated proportion of the target characteristic (assumed at 0.5 to maximise sample variance) and d is the allowable margin of error (set at 0.05). Substituting these values yielded a minimum sample size of 384 participants.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.27.0 (IBM Corp) and AMOS V.26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). The continuous variables were expressed as means with SD. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages (n, %). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using independent sample t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables and the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test for non-normally distributed variables. For comparisons involving three or more groups, analysis of variance was applied to normally distributed variables with homogeneity of variance, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for non-normally distributed data. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed variables and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for non-normally distributed variables. Structural equation modelling (SEM) was employed to investigate the inter-relationships among the KAP dimensions. The hypothesised pathways included (1) a direct effect of knowledge on attitude, (2) a direct effect of attitude on practice and (3) both direct and indirect effects of knowledge on practice through attitude. Model fit was assessed using the χ2 value divided by its df (CMIN/DF), RMSEA, IFI, TLI and CFI. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at p<0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 500 questionnaires were collected. One survey was subsequently excluded due to an erroneous response to the internal K10 attention-check (trap) item, resulting in a final analytical sample of 499 participants. Among the 499 participants included in the study, the mean maternal age was 34.03±5.42 years, and the majority were women (92.79%). Regarding educational attainment, 18.84% had junior high school education or less, 30.46% completed senior high school or a technical secondary school, 20.04% held an associate’s degree and 30.66% had a bachelor’s degree or higher. Monthly household income was most commonly between 10 000 and 20 000 RMB (59.72%), corresponding to an annual household income of 120 000–240 000 RMB. Because this study collected total household income rather than per-capita disposable income, direct comparison with regional income statistics is limited. Nevertheless, published literature from Jiangsu has used local per-capita disposable income as an economic benchmark, and a recent PubMed Central-indexed report cited an urban per-capita disposable income of 57 743 RMB in Jiangsu in 2021.34 Only a minority (8.22%) were employed in occupations related to medicine. Pregnancy-related complications included gestational hypertension in 19.64% and gestational diabetes in 28.06%. Nearly half of the pregnancies were first-time pregnancies (49.70%), followed by second-time (36.27%) and third-time or higher (14.03%) pregnancies. Of the children, 57.52% were men, with birth weight most frequently in the range of 2500–4000 g (49.30%) or 1501–2500 g (46.89%). Most infants were late preterm (85.97%) and were 40.58±10.96 months when the questionnaire was completed. Neurodevelopmental disorders were reported in 3.01% of children, while only 21.04% of families adhered to regular neurological check-ups, and 9.62% had received health education on neurodevelopment (table 1).

Knowledge, attitude and practice

The mean knowledge score was 7.22±3.53 (out of a possible maximum of 18), the mean attitude score was 23.91±5.16 (out of a possible maximum of 35) and the mean practice score was 25.84±5.08 (out of a possible maximum of 40) (table 1 and figure 1). After conversion to percentage scores, the mean knowledge score corresponded to 40.1% of the maximum possible score (7.22/18), the mean attitude score corresponded to 68.3% (23.91/35) and the mean practice score corresponded to 64.6% (25.84/40). Based on the 70% descriptive sufficiency threshold, all three KAP domains were below the threshold.

Violin plots showing the distribution of knowledge, attitude and practice scores.

In this survey of parental knowledge regarding neurodevelopment in preterm infants, awareness levels varied substantially across items. While most participants had at least ‘heard of’ many of the concepts, the proportion who reported being ‘very familiar’ was consistently low, ranging from 4.21% to 14.23%. The lowest familiarity was observed for the use of standardised developmental assessment tools (4.21% very familiar; 47.09% not sure), followed by the definition and classification of preterm infants (5.41% very familiar; 27.05% not sure) and recognition of common neurodevelopmental problems (5.41% very familiar; 29.06% not sure). Knowledge gaps were also evident regarding cognitive developmental delays, with over one-third of respondents (36.47%) uncertain about their typical presentation in the preschool years, and about the need for early interventions, for which nearly 40% were unsure. In contrast, awareness was relatively higher concerning the importance of parental support and interaction in promoting neurodevelopment, with 14.23% reporting being very familiar and only 11.62% unsure. Overall, these findings emphasise critical gaps in parental understanding of early intervention strategies, standardised assessment tools and the recognition of developmental risks in preterm infants (table 2).

Knowledge dimension distribution

In assessing attitudes towards neurodevelopmental care in moderate to late preterm infants, the majority of respondents generally endorsed the importance of early intervention, expert guidance and regular neurodevelopmental assessments, with over half expressing agreement or strong agreement with these statements. However, items related to the practical application and perceived urgency of these measures revealed substantial ambivalence. Notably, only 12.22%–13.03% strongly agreed that early intervention, regular assessments, or expert advice are crucial, and nearly one-third of respondents selected a neutral stance for these statements, indicating uncertainty or a lack of conviction about their value. Furthermore, 21.44% expressed scepticism or neutrality regarding the direct impact of neurodevelopmental problems on children’s learning and social functioning. A particularly concerning finding was observed in responses to the statement ‘one should not be overly worried about neurodevelopmental problems’: 29.26% strongly agreed and 47.29% agreed, reflecting a potentially dismissive or minimising attitude towards the risks faced by preterm infants (table 3).

Attitude dimension distribution

In the assessment of parental practices related to neurodevelopmental follow-up in moderate to late preterm infants, the results revealed notable deficiencies in several key behaviours. Only 9.22% of parents reported always bringing their child for regular neurodevelopmental check-ups, while 24.85% rarely or never did so, indicating unsatisfactory adherence to recommended screening protocols. Similarly, a minority of individuals always tracked developmental milestones (8.42%), while 21.65% rarely or never did so. Early education and participation in developmental training programmes were particularly limited, as only 4.21% of parents reported always engaging in such activities, and the majority (64.33%) reported rarely or never participating. Discussion of neurodevelopmental progress with healthcare professionals was infrequent, with only 3.01% always doing so, and over 62% rarely or never initiating these conversations. While the provision of a balanced diet (91.79%) and ensuring sufficient sleep (95.59%) were widely practised, active educational efforts—such as reading or searching for information about preterm neurodevelopment—were less common, with just 20.24% always seeking information and 35.27% rarely or never doing so. These findings highlight a significant gap between routine caregiving behaviours (such as nutrition and sleep) and engagement in structured neurodevelopmental monitoring, proactive education and participation in early intervention programmes. Items with particularly limited scores, such as low regularity in check-ups, developmental tracking and health professional communication, underscore an urgent need to enhance parental awareness, motivation and support for neurodevelopmental monitoring in this vulnerable population (table 4).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study investigated the KAP of parents of children born moderate to late preterm regarding their children’s neurodevelopment. The results revealed parents’ KAP scores regarding neurodevelopment among children born moderate to late preterm were below the descriptive 70% sufficiency threshold, with the most prominent gap observed in knowledge. Considering that knowledge influenced attitudes and practices, interventions should focus on strengthening family-centred education, clearer follow-up communication and parental engagement in neurodevelopmental monitoring and early intervention.

Notably, this threshold was used only as a descriptive benchmark and is not a population-specific validated reference value. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of parental inadequacy, but rather as indicating specific gaps in follow-up communication, family-centred education and support for sustained neurodevelopmental monitoring. This interpretation is particularly important because most respondents were not employed in medically related occupations and had not received health education about neurodevelopment. In this context, limited familiarity with specialised concepts, such as morbidity and mortality risks, standardised developmental assessment tools and early-intervention pathways, is understandable. Therefore, the findings should be viewed as unmet educational and follow-up support needs rather than as shortcomings of parents.

Parental knowledge regarding neurodevelopmental risks and follow-up needs for preterm infants, as observed in this study, reveals substantial gaps, particularly in understanding standardised assessment tools, early intervention requirements and recognising developmental risks.22 35 36 This pattern is reflected and supported by prior literature: systematic reviews consistently report that while general awareness of preterm risks can be high, parents often lack detailed knowledge about specific interventions or assessment protocols and are often unsure about developmental follow-up and early intervention needs.22 35 36 Studies have also documented challenges in engaging parents in early intervention programmes, noting that adherence and meaningful participation are frequently limited by insufficient understanding of their importance and perceived benefits.7 37 38

The results of this study, which demonstrate uncertain and often minimising parental attitudes towards neurodevelopmental risk and intervention needs in moderate to late preterm infants, are consistent with findings in recent international literature. Across multiple studies, parents of preterm infants frequently exhibit high levels of general awareness or agreement with the notion of increased fragility, but show limited conviction regarding the need for proactive early intervention, routine assessments or specialist guidance. This pattern aligns with the substantial proportion of neutral responses observed in the present survey, as well as the relatively low rates of strong agreement for statements emphasising the importance of early and regular follow-up.3840 Recent work underscores that parental risk perceptions and attitudes directly influence their engagement with follow-up care and adoption of recommended intervention practices. For example, content analyses have shown that when parents underestimate risks or focus on immediate rather than long-term outcomes, participation in neurodevelopmental screening and early intervention falls short of guideline expectations. Similarly, studies evaluating parental questionnaires and interviews have identified that knowledge gaps regarding the potential for cognitive, motor and behavioural impairments persist even when children are recognised as vulnerable.63840

The observed gaps in parental practices concerning neurodevelopmental follow-up and early intervention participation among children born moderate to late preterm closely mirror trends reported in recent research. Studies from diverse regions, including cross-sectional surveys and systematic reviews, consistently identify low adherence rates to recommended neurodevelopmental screening and monitoring schedules for preterm children. Only a minority of parents routinely attend check-ups, actively track milestones, or discuss developmental progress with healthcare providers. Factors contributing to this include a lack of parental awareness about long-term risks, unclear communication from providers, logistical barriers and a tendency to focus efforts on basic caregiving (such as nutrition and sleep) rather than structured neurodevelopmental activities.394143 Overall, the findings from this study reinforce the urgent call in the literature for comprehensive follow-up systems that include accessible, family-centred education, workflows that prioritise developmental surveillance at all routine encounters and interventions that directly involve and empower parents in the monitoring and promotion of their preterm children’s neurodevelopmental health.41 42 44 Notably, only 3.01% of children in our sample were reported to have neurodevelopmental disorders. This proportion appears lower than that reported in studies from other regions of China and may be related to differences in study population, follow-up pathways, outcome definitions or recognition of neurodevelopmental problems.45 46

The present study showed that the three KAP dimensions were correlated with each other. The path analysis revealed that knowledge had a direct influence on attitude and practice, and that attitude, in turn, influenced practice. The literature further confirms that parental cognitive stimulation and interactive support substantially impact neurocognitive outcomes; yet, parents may not fully appreciate the value of their role, as evidenced by the relatively higher scores found in this study for the item emphasising family support, compared with technical neurodevelopmental concepts.36 47 48 Additionally, comparative studies emphasise that both biological and psychosocial factors, including parental stress and educational level, contribute to developmental outcomes for preterm infants. Families with improved access to information and targeted interventions show better child outcomes across cognitive, behavioural and social-emotional domains.49 Overall, these findings suggest a persistent need for structured, accessible educational strategies and supportive follow-up systems that improve parent understanding and participation in neurodevelopmental screening and intervention among children born preterm. Importantly, published intervention studies and qualitative syntheses highlight that shifting parent attitudes requires more than the provision of factual information. Structured educational modules that incorporate counselling, motivational interviewing and shared decision-making have demonstrated improved parental vigilance, greater adherence to recommended assessments and earlier uptake of rehabilitative services. These approaches help parents appreciate the nuanced risks associated with even late or moderate prematurity and foster a proactive stance that benefits neurodevelopmental outcomes.36 39 40 International literature highlights that while general caregiving (such as ensuring rest and a nutritionally balanced diet) is typically prioritised—similar to the high scores seen in this study for those items—active engagement in early screening, milestone monitoring and participation in early education or intervention programmes remains limited. This gap is particularly concerning, as multiple large-scale and interventional studies confirm that parental involvement in regular assessment and timely intervention is associated with improved cognitive, motor and behavioural outcomes for preterm infants. Structured educational supports and parent-guided interventions have therefore been emphasised as pivotal strategies to enhance awareness and foster more consistent engagement.41 42 44 Therefore, future studies should focus on designing and testing interventions to foster optimal KAP in parents of preterm infants, which could lead to better developmental outcomes. Such interventions could take the form of lectures, group discussions, reading materials, interactive websites and podcasts, for example.

This study had several limitations. First, it was a single-centre study with a limited sample size, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the geographical representativeness of the findings. Because many families were recruited through outpatient follow-up pathways, the sample may also have preferentially included families who were already more engaged with healthcare services, potentially leading to overestimation of parental engagement in follow-up-related KAP. Second, the cross-sectional design precluded causal inference. Although path analysis was performed, the observed associations do not represent true causality because the model was based on prespecified hypotheses.5052 Third, the children were no longer infants at the time of questionnaire completion and had a mean age of 40.58 months. Parents therefore reported their KAP retrospectively after having already experienced their child’s development, which may have influenced their responses and limits interpretation of the findings as reflecting parental perceptions during infancy. Fourth, only 3.01% of the children were reported to have neurodevelopmental disorders, which appears lower than expected for this population and may reflect under-recognition, under-reporting or limited sample representativeness. In addition, the findings may have been influenced by the hospital’s follow-up and postdischarge management practices, including the extent to which families received developmental monitoring, standardised assessment and clear explanations regarding the purpose of follow-up. Because only a small proportion of children were reported to have neurodevelopmental disorders, some families may have had limited retrospective concern about neurodevelopment, which may have affected their responses regarding follow-up and early intervention. Fifth, gestational age was collected as categorical rather than continuous data, so the mean gestational age and SD could not be reported. Sixth, all KAP data were self-reported and therefore subject to social desirability bias.53 54 Seventh, the questionnaire was purpose-built for this study, and no normative or reference data are currently available to indicate the expected level of parental KAP in this population. In addition, standardised developmental assessment data, such as Bayley Scales or Denver Developmental Screening Test scores, were not collected, so external construct validity could not be examined by comparing parental KAP scores with child developmental assessment outcomes. Information on conception through assisted reproductive technology was also not collected, and the potential influence of this factor on neurodevelopment and parental perceptions therefore could not be assessed. Finally, the knowledge section did not cover all clinically important long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes of moderate to late preterm birth, such as later school performance and adult cognitive function, which limited the scope of the questionnaire and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

In conclusion, parents’ KAP scores regarding neurodevelopment among children born moderate to late preterm were below the descriptive 70% sufficiency threshold, especially in the knowledge domain. Considering that knowledge influenced attitudes and practices, interventions should be designed and implemented to improve the parental KAP and possibly improve their preterm infants’ neurodevelopmental outcomes. Strengthened family-centred education and clearer hospital-community follow-up communication may support parental engagement in neurodevelopmental monitoring and early intervention.
