MetaKE: Meta-learning Aligned Knowledge Editing via Bi-level Optimization
Shuxin Liu, Ou Wu, Hangzhou Institute for Advanced Study

Abstract
Knowledge editing (KE) aims to precisely rectify specific knowledge in Large Language Models (LLMs) without disrupting general capabilities. State-of-the-art methods suffer from an open-loop control mismatch. We identify a critical “Semantic-Execution Disconnect” : the semantic target is derived independently without feedback from the downstream’s feasible region. This misalignment often causes valid semantic targets to fall within the prohibited space, resulting in gradient truncation and editing failure. To bridge this gap, we propose MetaKE ( Meta -learning Aligned K nowledge E diting), a new framework that reframes KE as a bi-level optimization problem. Departing from static calculation, MetaKE treats the edit target as a learnable meta-parameter: the upper-level optimizer seeks a feasible target to maximize post-edit performance, while the lower-level solver executes the editing. To address the challenge of differentiating through complex solvers, we derive a Structural Gradient Proxy , which explicitly backpropagates editability constraints to the target learning phase. Theoretical analysis demonstrates that MetaKE automatically aligns the edit direction with the model’s feasible manifold. Extensive experiments confirm that MetaKE significantly outperforms strong baselines, offering a new perspective on knowledge editing.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have become a core substrate for modern NLP systems
[
34
,
2
,
27
]
, yet their parametric memory inevitably contains factual errors and outdated knowledge
[
35
,
14
,
7
]
. Knowledge editing (KE) aims to rectify a set of targeted behaviors while preserving the model’s general capabilities
[
5
,
13
]
. Despite substantial progress, reliable editing under strong constraints remains elusive: edits that succeed semantically may still fail physically, and improvements in edit success often come at the expense of locality or stability
[
3
,
32
]
.

Dominant KE methods such as ROME
[
22
]
, MEMIT
[
23
]
, and AlphaEdit
[
6
]
follow a decoupled
Compute-then-Solve
routine: a
Semantic Planning
stage first produces a semantic target
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
, and a
Editing Execution
stage then solves a constrained least-squares update to realize this target in weights.
We identify a fundamental limitation of this open-loop design, termed the
Semantic-Execution Disconnect
:
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
is optimized without awareness of the preservation constraints enforced by the downstream
[
28
]
.
Modern solvers preserve memories by suppressing sensitive update directions, e.g., via inverse-covariance regularization (MEMIT) or orthogonal projections (AlphaEdit).
When the semantically ideal
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
demands movement along these protected directions, the solver must dampen or truncate the effective update, causing the executed edit to deviate from
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
and potentially fail.
This reveals an implicit and often violated assumption: the semantically optimal direction lies within the solver’s feasible update set under preservation constraints, which can break down in dense, deep representations. While meta-editors (e.g., MEND
[
25
]
, ENN
[
29
]
) and adaptive gradient guidance methods (e.g., AGRADE
[
11
]
) alleviate parts of this tension, reliably achieving strong edits under preservation constraints without sacrificing locality and stability remains challenging.

To bridge this gap, we propose MetaKE (
Meta
-learning Aligned
K
nowledge
E
diting), which recasts knowledge editing as a bi-level optimization problem.
MetaKE treats the edit target
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
as a learnable meta-parameter: the upper level optimizes
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
for edit success, while the lower level enforces the solver’s preservation constraints, providing gradient feedback that aligns
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
with the feasible update manifold.
To avoid expensive multi-layer unrolling, we introduce a closed-form
Structural Gradient Proxy
based on the
Structural Consistency Hypothesis
, which retains key constraint operators and efficiently exposes the feasibility boundary to the meta-optimizer.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose a Bi-Level optimization framework MetaKE, identifying the “Semantic-Execution Disconnect” in existing editing paradigms. MetaKE treats the semantic target as a learnable
meta-parameter
, which enables the optimization process to proactively sense downstream physical constraints, resolving edit failures caused by conflict-induced signal attenuation.

•

We introduce the Structural Gradient Proxy. By distilling the closed-form solution of the constraints into a differentiable module, we convert physical barriers into a “Geometric Gating Gradient”. This mechanism efficiently filters out gradient components pointing towards the constraint space, automatically rectifying the optimization direction into the parameter’s feasible Range Space without the need for expensive full-solver unrolling.

•

We provide theoretical analysis demonstrating that the gradient flow guided by our structural proxy achieves asymptotic alignment with the Feasible Manifold of the parameter space, effectively mitigating the Information Truncation phenomenon observed in strong-constraint settings. Empirically, MetaKE significantly boosts editing success rates and generalization while maintaining preservation of unrelated knowledge.

Related Work

Locate-then-Edit and Mass-Editing Constraints

The “Locate-then-Edit” paradigm remains the standard for KE
[
22
,
4
]
. ROME localizes factual recall to rank-one updates
[
22
]
, while MEMIT scales least-squares editing across layers with inverse-covariance regularization to preserve general knowledge
[
23
]
. Beyond soft regularization, recent work increasingly imposes explicit geometric constraints: PMET and EMMET formulate equality-constrained solvers to reduce collateral damage and balance stability–plasticity
[
19
,
12
]
, and AlphaEdit enforces orthogonality via null-space projection
[
6
]
. Despite stronger constraints in
Physical Execution
, these methods remain open-loop: they accept an upstream semantic target without accounting for how constraints truncate semantically ideal updates, yielding
Spectral Suppression
.

Meta-Learning and Objective-Driven Optimization

Another line learns update mappings or improves objectives. Hypernetwork approaches (e.g., MEND; KE) predict weight updates from gradients
[
25
,
5
]
, and MALMEN approximates least-squares solutions under varying batch sizes
[
30
]
. More recently, target-centric methods optimize what to write: AdaEdit adds future sequence probability constraints
[
18
]
, AnyEdit extends this to long-form autoregressive editing via iterative key-token updates
[
15
]
, and REP dynamically optimizes robust editing keys to decouple edits from native representations
[
33
]
. In contrast, MetaKE is a meta-optimizer that closes the loop with the
analytical structure
of constraint-based solvers. While AdaEdit/AnyEdit mainly optimize targets for
linguistic coherence
(Semantic Disconnect), MetaKE optimizes targets for
physical realizability
under hard constraints (Physical Disconnect), aligning planned targets with the feasible geometry via gradient feedback through the solver.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Knowledge Editing as Linear Associative Memory

We denote an LLM as
f(\cdot):\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
that associates an input
x
with
its corresponding prediction
y
. KE typically treats Transformer FFNs as linear associative memories
[
8
,
16
,
1
]
. For a target layer
l
, the output mapping
\boldsymbol{W}^{l}_{\text{out}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{0}}
of FFNs retrieves information
\boldsymbol{m}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
via:

\boldsymbol{m}_{l}\approx\boldsymbol{W}^{l}_{\text{out}}\boldsymbol{k}_{l},\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{k}_{l}=\sigma\!\left(\boldsymbol{W}^{l}_{\text{in}}\,\gamma(\cdot)\right),

(1)

where
\gamma
denotes the layer normalization,
\boldsymbol{k}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
(key) represents the subject-relation
(s,r)
and
\boldsymbol{m}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
(value) is the retrieved representation. Editing seeks a weight update
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
such that
(\boldsymbol{W}^{l}_{\text{out}}+\boldsymbol{\Delta})\boldsymbol{k}_{l}
aligns with a new desired object
o^{*}
.

3.2
Locate-then-Edit as Compute-then-Solve

Before delving into the technical details, we establish a unified terminology. In this paper, we refer to the limitations imposed by the solver (e.g., preservation of pre-trained knowledge via covariance matrices) collectively as
structural constraints
. The ability of a semantic target to survive these constraints and manifest in the weight space is defined as its
physical realizability
.

Most “Locate-then-Edit” methods
[
22
,
23
]
decouple the process into a two-stage pipeline:

Stage I: Semantic Planning (Target Optimization).

The goal is to find an ideal target
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
that maximizes the likelihood
P(o^{*}\mid s,r)
. Methods like MEMIT
[
23
]
and AlphaEdit
[
6
]
derive
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
by optimizing:

\boldsymbol{v}^{*}=\boldsymbol{v}+\mathop{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\big(\underbrace{-\log\mathbb{P}_{f_{L_{\text{out}}}(\boldsymbol{m}_{L}+\boldsymbol{\delta})}[o^{*}\mid s,r]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{semantic}}(\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{\delta})}\big),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{m}_{L}
is the output of the FFN layer, and
f_{L_{\text{out}}}(\cdot)
denotes the forward pass of the remaining layers. In this view,
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
acts as an
open-loop
control signal: it specifies what to achieve semantically, strictly ignoring how structural constraints will limit its realization.

Stage II: Editing Execution (Solver Update).

The update is realized by solving a constrained least-squares problem
[
10
]
:

\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{k}-(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{k})\|_{2}^{2}+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{T})+\lambda_{\text{ridge}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{C}=\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{T}
represents the uncentered covariance matrix of keys, which represents the model’s pre-existing memory distribution and governs the preservation constraints.

The Semantic-Execution Disconnect:
Crucially, the objective in Eq.
3
is heavily bottlenecked by the structural penalty
\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{T})
, which protects the pre-trained historical subspace. If the semantic target
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
(derived in Stage I) requires updating weights along directions associated with large eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{C}
, the downstream solver will aggressively penalize and truncate this update. This inevitably leads to what we term the
Spectral Suppression effect
(formalized in Sec.
4
), rendering the carefully planned open-loop target physically unexecutable.

4
Methodology

We first analyze the structural failures of current methods and then describe our proposed method.

4.1
The Semantic–Execution Disconnect

We identify two structural defects that render the decoupled
Compute-then-Solve
paradigm unreliable:
Spectral Suppression
and the
Static Regularization Trap
(Fig.
1
).

Figure 1:
The Semantic–Execution Disconnect: (A) Spectral Suppression attenuates the update signal; (B) The Static Regularization Trap shows the mismatch between isotropic trust regions and anisotropic feasibility.

Spectral Suppression.

The semantic residual is
\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{k}
. Stage II computes
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}
by minimizing
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{T})+\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}
. The realized residual is
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathrm{real}}=\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}\boldsymbol{k}
.

Theorem 1

(Spectral Suppression)
.

Assume
\boldsymbol{C}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\boldsymbol{I}\succ\boldsymbol{0}
. The realized residual is a scaled version of the target:
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathrm{real}}=\beta\,\boldsymbol{\delta}
, where
\beta=\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}
and
\gamma=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{(\boldsymbol{u}_{j}^{T}\boldsymbol{k})^{2}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}}
. If
\boldsymbol{k}
lies in the high-spectrum (protected) subspace of
\boldsymbol{C}
(large
\sigma_{j}^{2}
), then
\beta\to 0
, causing deterministic signal attenuation
\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathrm{real}}\|\ll\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|
. The proof is provided in Appendix
B.1
.

Stage I optimizes
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
assuming
\beta\approx 1
, but Stage II enforces
\beta\ll 1
for protected keys (confirmed for projection-based editors like AlphaEdit where
\beta=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{k}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{k}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{iso}}}
). This causes the edit to fail physically despite succeeding semantically.

The Static Regularization Trap.

Stage I typically employs an isotropic penalty
\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{up}}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{init}}\|_{2}^{2}
, defining a spherical trust region. However, downstream feasibility
\mathcal{F}(\tau)=\{\boldsymbol{u}:\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}\leq\tau\}
is inherently anisotropic with condition number
\kappa\gg 1
.

Theorem 2

(Static Regularization Trap)
.

If the downstream feasibility
\mathcal{F}(\tau)
is sufficiently anisotropic (
\kappa\gg 1
), no global scalar
\lambda_{\mathrm{up}}
can simultaneously ensure safety for hard cases and allow sufficient progress (
|\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{good}}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}|\geq m
) for easy cases. See Appendix
B.2
.

As shown in Appendix
B.2
, isotropic regularization restricts updates to a ball. For large
\kappa
, the radius required for progress exceeds the safety limit of protected directions. Notably, using
KL-divergence
does not resolve this, as the resulting
semantic ellipsoid
(Fisher Information) is generally misaligned with the
execution
constraint matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
(activation covariance), leading to the same truncation phenomenon.

4.2
Bi-level Optimization for Feasibility-Aware Editing

To bridge the Semantic–Execution Disconnect identified above, we introduce
MetaKE
, a meta-optimizer that seamlessly aligns planned targets with the model’s feasible manifold. Unlike prior methods that strictly decouple semantic planning and physical execution, MetaKE introduces a
“look-ahead” meta-optimization paradigm
. By anticipating how preservation constraints will truncate updates, MetaKE effectively circumvents spectral suppression and dynamically rotates the target gradient into the solver’s executable range space.

Formally, we redefine KE as a
Bi-level Optimization (BLO)
problem. In this framework, the upstream target
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
is a learnable
meta-parameter
that anticipates the downstream solver’s constraints. The global objective is to find an optimal target
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
that minimizes the meta-loss evaluated on the edited model
f(\cdot;\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}})
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\text{opt}}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}\big(f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{W}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}\mathcal{W}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}))\big)

(4)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\ \boldsymbol{\Delta}\mathcal{W}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\text{inner}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\mathcal{W};\boldsymbol{v}^{*}),

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}}=\boldsymbol{W}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}\mathcal{W}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})
is the virtual post-edit weights. This nested structure ensures that the chosen target
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
is not just semantically ideal, but
physically realizable
by the weight-space solver.

Lower Level: Multi-layer Execution Solver.

The inner objective
\mathcal{J}_{\text{inner}}
simulates the editing execution (Stage II) across key layers
\mathcal{L}
. Given
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
, the solver computes preservation-constrained updates
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\mathcal{W}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=\{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}^{*}\}_{l\in\mathcal{L}}
:

\mathcal{J}_{\text{inner}}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\left(\|(\boldsymbol{W}_{l}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})\boldsymbol{k}_{l}-\boldsymbol{t}_{l}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})\|_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}\boldsymbol{C}_{l}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}^{T})+\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}\|_{F}^{2}\right),

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{t}_{l}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})
is the layer-specific target residual induced by
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
(e.g., via residual allocation). Crucially, each
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})
is an implicit function of the meta-parameter
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
.

Upper Level: Meta-Performance Optimization.

The upper level optimizes
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
by differentiating through the solver’s response. The meta-loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}
balances three core signals:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{edit}}(x_{e},y_{e};\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}})}_{\text{Edit Success}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}}(x_{\text{loc}};\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}},\boldsymbol{W})}_{\text{Locality Preservation}}+\underbrace{\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{init}}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\text{Meta-Regularization}}.

(6)

By embedding the execution solver into the optimization loop, the target
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
becomes feasibility-aware, proactively avoiding regions where semantic desires conflict with structural constraints (e.g., the spectral suppression or regularization traps in Sec.
4.1
).

4.3
Structural Gradient Proxy

Directly differentiating through the multi-layer solver
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\mathcal{W}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})
in Eq. (
4
) is computationally prohibitive due to recursive residuals and matrix inversions. We thus introduce a
Structural Gradient Proxy
for efficient estimation. Based on the
Structural Consistency Hypothesis
—that dominant physical constraints share spectral consistency across layers—we posit that a target
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
feasible for the final layer
L
is likely globally feasible. We define the proxy update via the closed-form solution of layer
L
:

\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\text{proxy}}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{W}_{L}\boldsymbol{k}_{L})\boldsymbol{M},\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{T}(\boldsymbol{C}_{L}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{k}_{L}\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{T})^{-1}.

(7)

This proxy yields a tractable approximate gradient flow for the upper-level objective:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}}{\partial f}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\Delta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\text{proxy}}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}}_{\text{Task Signal}}\cdot\underbrace{\boldsymbol{M}^{T}}_{\text{Structural Gate}},

(8)

restoring the “missing feedback channel” ignored by standard methods. Here, the
Task Signal
represents the pure semantic demand for edit success and locality, while the multiplier
\boldsymbol{M}^{T}
acts as a
structural gate
, filtering gradients to ensure updates to
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
align with the feasible manifold induced by key statistics.

4.4
Optimization Algorithm: MetaKE

MetaKE refines the target
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
via an iterative
Look-ahead and Correct
loop (Algorithm
1
).

Phase 1: Virtual Look-ahead.

At iteration
t
, we simulate the edit effect by forming virtual weights for the final layer:
\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}}_{L,t}=\boldsymbol{W}_{L}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\text{proxy}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{t}^{*})
. This allows MetaKE to probe the feasibility of
\boldsymbol{v}_{t}^{*}
through the proxy geometry, bypassing the need for expensive multi-layer unrolling during optimization.

Phase 2: Feasibility-Aware Correction.

We evaluate the meta-loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}
under the virtual weights and backpropagate the signal through the structural gate
\boldsymbol{M}^{T}
to update the target:

\boldsymbol{v}_{t+1}^{*}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{v}_{t}^{*}-\eta\left(\nabla_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}}_{L,t}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}\right)\boldsymbol{M}^{T}.

(9)

This update pushes
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
toward regions that are both semantically effective and physically realizable. Upon convergence, the optimized
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
is passed to a standard multi-layer solver (e.g., MEMIT) to execute the final update across all target layers
\mathcal{L}
.

5
Theoretical Justification

Physical Realizability and Anisotropic Feasibility.

Standard editing often fails when the required update lies in a protected subspace, leading to spectral suppression (attenuation factor
\beta\ll 1
). MetaKE circumvents this by treating
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
as a learnable parameter. The meta-gradient
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}
pushes
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
away from suppression-triggering directions toward regions where
\beta\approx 1
(Fig.
1
A).
Unlike static regularizers
\lambda
that create an “impossible gap” between hard and easy edits, MetaKE embeds feasibility into the gradient flow via the
Structural Gate

\boldsymbol{M}^{T}
. Each edit is adapted to the
anisotropic feasible manifold
: hard edits (high spectral density) are tightly bounded by the safety limit to preserve locality, while easy edits can safely extend to achieve the required progress (Fig.
1
B). This per-edit adaptation eliminates the need for a global compromise.

Proxy Fidelity.

We justify using a single-layer proxy for the multi-layer objective
F(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}(f(\cdot;\boldsymbol{W}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})))
. Let
\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{J}_{l})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{l}
be the true hypergradient, where
\boldsymbol{s}_{l}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}}
and
\boldsymbol{J}_{l}=\partial\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})/\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
. We define the proxy gradient as
\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}=\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T}
, matching the Jacobian used in Sec.
4.3
.

Theorem 3

(Dominance and Fidelity)
.

Assume: (i)
last-layer dominance
:
\|\sum_{l\neq L}(\boldsymbol{J}_{l})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{l}\|\leq\rho\|(\boldsymbol{J}_{L})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|
for
\rho\in[0,1)
; (ii)
structural consistency
:
\|\boldsymbol{C}_{l}-\boldsymbol{C}_{L}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{C}
and
\|\boldsymbol{k}_{l}-\boldsymbol{k}_{L}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{k}
across layers; (iii)
allocation stability
: the solver Jacobians
\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{l}/\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
are bounded and slowly drifting.
Then there exists
K>0
such that the proxy error is bounded:

\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{true}}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{proxy}}\|\leq\rho\|\boldsymbol{g}_{L}\|+K(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2},\quad\text{where }\boldsymbol{g}_{L}=(\boldsymbol{J}_{L})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{L}.

(10)

Further, if the non-degeneracy
\alpha(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T}\|_{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}}\geq\alpha_{0}>0
and
\rho+\widetilde{K}(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})<1
, then
\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}
is a
valid descent direction
:
\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}},\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\rangle>0
. The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
B.3
.

The proxy error is controlled by the tail-layer contribution
\rho
and cross-layer drift
O(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})
. Empirically, we observe small drift and stable
\alpha
, confirming that the single-layer proxy provides a reliable hypergradient without costly multi-layer unrolling.

as a fundamental bottleneck in current knowledge editing, where upstream semantic planning is decoupled from downstream physical execution constraints. A.1
Target Optimization

(i) Edit success loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{edit}}
.
This term enforces that the edited model produces the desired target output
y_{e}
given the edit input
x_{e}
.
Following the standard knowledge editing objective, we formulate the loss as the negative log-likelihood of the target prediction under the post-edit weights
\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}}
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{edit}}(x_{e},y_{e};\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}})~=~-\log\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}}}\big[y_{e}\mid x_{e}\big].

(11)

(ii) Locality preservation loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}}
.
To prevent unintended drift on unrelated queries, we regularize the post-edit behavior on a locality set
x_{\text{loc}}
by matching the output distributions of the pre-edit and post-edit models.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}}(x_{\text{loc}};\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}},\boldsymbol{W})=\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\left(P_{\boldsymbol{W}}(y\mid x_{\text{loc}})~\|~P_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{W}}}(y\mid x_{\text{loc}})\right).

(12)

(iii) Meta-regularization
\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{init}}\|_{2}^{2}
, which constrains
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
to stay close to the initial semantic plan
\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{init}}
, preventing excessive deviation that could destroy the intended meaning of the target.

B.1
Proofs for Spectral Suppression

B.1.1
Closed-form solver map

We restate the Stage II objective:

\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\;\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}+\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{T})+\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2},

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{C}
is the stored key covariance statistic and
\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\geq 0
is the ridge coefficient.
Define the effective geometry
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}:=\boldsymbol{C}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\boldsymbol{I}
.
Using standard matrix calculus,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=2(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta})\boldsymbol{k}^{T},

(14)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{T})

\displaystyle=2\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{C},

(15)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}.

(16)

Setting the gradient to zero yields

(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{\delta})\boldsymbol{k}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{C}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\boldsymbol{\Delta}(\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{k}^{T})=\boldsymbol{\delta}\boldsymbol{k}^{T}.

(17)

Since
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\succ\boldsymbol{0}
, it is invertible and we obtain

\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\delta}\boldsymbol{k}^{T}(\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{k}^{T})^{-1}.

(18)

B.1.2
Realizability coefficient and attenuation law

Define the executed residual at the edited site:

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathrm{real}}=\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}\boldsymbol{k}.

(19)

Substituting (
18
) into (
19

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathrm{real}}=\boldsymbol{\delta}\cdot\underbrace{\boldsymbol{k}^{T}(\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{k}^{T})^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}}_{\beta}.

(20)

Let
\gamma=\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}\geq 0
.
Apply Sherman–Morrison:

(\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{k}^{T})^{-1}=\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}-\frac{\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}}{1+\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}}.

(21)

Left- and right-multiplying by
\boldsymbol{k}^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{k}

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\gamma-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{1+\gamma}=\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}.

(22)

This proves the attenuation law

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathrm{real}}=\beta\boldsymbol{\delta},\qquad\beta=\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma},\quad\gamma=\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}.

(23)

B.1.3
Spectral form and proof of Theorem

Let

Then
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{U}^{T}

\displaystyle\gamma=\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{(\boldsymbol{u}_{j}^{T}\boldsymbol{k})^{2}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}}.

(24)

Quantitative suppression bound.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote an index set corresponding to large eigenvalues, and suppose

concentrates on this protected subspace:

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{j}^{T}\boldsymbol{k})^{2}\geq(1-\rho)\|\boldsymbol{k}\|_{2}^{2}

(25)

In particular, in the idealized case

As

B.2.1
From isotropic regularization to a spherical trust region

Consider the upstream objective

\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NLL}}(\boldsymbol{v})+\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{up}}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{init}}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NLL}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{init}}+\boldsymbol{u})+\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{up}}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}.

A local quadratic model around

(37)

Ignoring the constant term gives the convex quadratic problem

\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\lambda_{\mathrm{up}})=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}\ \boldsymbol{g}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{T}(\boldsymbol{H}+\lambda_{\mathrm{up}}\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{u},

(39)

The key point is that

is isotropic: it uniformly shrinks all directions,
equivalently imposing a
spherical
trust region in the upstream space.

PD regularization for feasibility geometry.

When

is singular (e.g., near-free null-space),

\epsilon

\boldsymbol{A}_{\epsilon}=\boldsymbol{A}+\epsilon\boldsymbol{I}\succ\boldsymbol{0}

Lemma 1

(Penalty–trust-region KKT equivalence for the quadratic surrogate)
.

Consider the quadratic surrogate in (

\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}\ \boldsymbol{g}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{u}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\boldsymbol{H}\succeq\boldsymbol{0},\ \lambda\geq 0,

whose unique minimizer is

Let

Then

is also a solution to the spherical trust-region problem

(41)

with
\lambda
being the associated KKT multiplier. Moreover,

is non-increasing in
\lambda
.

Proof.

Write the trust-region Lagrangian

\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u},\mu)=\boldsymbol{g}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{u}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}(\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}-r^{2})

The KKT stationarity condition gives

,
so any KKT point has the form
\boldsymbol{u}=-(\boldsymbol{H}+\mu\boldsymbol{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}
.
Setting

and

satisfies primal feasibility by definition and complementary slackness
(
\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}=r
when
\lambda>0
unless
\boldsymbol{g}=\boldsymbol{0}
).
Thus
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\lambda)
is a trust-region solution with multiplier
\lambda
.

For monotonicity, if
\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{1}\geq 0
, then

\boldsymbol{H}+\lambda_{2}\boldsymbol{I}\succeq\boldsymbol{H}+\lambda_{1}\boldsymbol{I}\succ\boldsymbol{0}

∎

Under the local quadratic model, a single scalar

controls only an

(spherical) notion of

We formalize the incompatibility between (i) isotropic upstream control (one scalar

B.3.1

,

for some constants
0<\mu\leq\bar{M}
. Assume cross-layer drift (with
\varepsilon_{C}<\mu
):

\|\boldsymbol{C}_{l}-\boldsymbol{C}_{L}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{C},\qquad\|\boldsymbol{k}_{l}-\boldsymbol{k}_{L}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{k},\qquad\forall l\in\mathcal{L}.

(53)

Assume there exists
\rho\in[0,1)
such that

\Big\|\sum_{l\neq L}(\boldsymbol{J}_{l})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{l}\Big\|\leq\rho\|(\boldsymbol{J}_{L})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|.

(54)

Multi-layer solvers allocate the meta-target into layer-wise targets
\boldsymbol{t}_{l}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
.
We assume:

Assumption 1

(Allocation Jacobian stability)
.

(i) Each allocation map is differentiable and uniformly bounded:

\big\|\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{l}/\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\big\|_{2}\leq L_{t}
for all
l\in\mathcal{L}
.
(ii) Cross-layer Jacobian drift is small (“slow drift”):

\Big\|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{l}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}\Big\|_{2}\leq L_{t}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{k},\qquad\forall l\in\mathcal{L}.

(55)

(iii) Under feasibility consistency (
53
), the last-layer allocation Jacobian is close to identity:

\Big\|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}-\boldsymbol{I}\Big\|_{2}\leq L_{\text{id}}(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k}).

(56)

Remark.

(
56
) is empirically checkable (e.g., finite-difference estimates of
\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{L}/\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
).
It is also satisfied by a broad class of linear(ized) allocation mechanisms where
\boldsymbol{t}_{L}
solves a perturbed identity system

(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{G})\boldsymbol{t}_{L}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
with
\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{2}=O(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})
,
in which case
(\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{L}/\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{G})^{-1}
and

\|(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{G})^{-1}-\boldsymbol{I}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{2}/(1-\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{2})
.

Lemma 3

(Inverse perturbation bound)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\succ\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}
with

\|\boldsymbol{E}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
and
\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{A})\geq\mu>0
.
If
\varepsilon<\mu
, then
\boldsymbol{B}\succ\boldsymbol{0}
and

\|\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\mu(\mu-\varepsilon)}.

Proof.

Use the resolvent identity:

\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}=-\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}
.
Then

\|\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\|\cdot\|\boldsymbol{E}\|\cdot\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|
.
Since
\lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{B})\geq\mu-\varepsilon
,

\|\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\|\leq 1/(\mu-\varepsilon)
and
\|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\|\leq 1/\mu
.
∎

Lemma 4

(Geometry discrepancy bound)
.

Assume
\|\boldsymbol{k}_{l}\|_{2}\leq B_{k}
for all
l\in\mathcal{L}
and

\varepsilon_{C}+2B_{k}\varepsilon_{k}<\mu
.
Then there exists a constant
K_{M}>0
such that for all
l\in\mathcal{L}
,

\|\boldsymbol{M}_{l}-\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}\leq K_{M}(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k}).

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}_{l}:=\boldsymbol{C}_{l,\mathrm{eff}}+\boldsymbol{k}_{l}\boldsymbol{k}_{l}^{T}
, so that
\boldsymbol{M}_{l}=\boldsymbol{k}_{l}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{l}^{-1}
.

\boldsymbol{M}_{l}-\boldsymbol{M}_{L}=(\boldsymbol{k}_{l}-\boldsymbol{k}_{L})^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{l}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{T}(\boldsymbol{A}_{l}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{L}^{-1}).

For the first term,

\|(\boldsymbol{k}_{l}-\boldsymbol{k}_{L})^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{l}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{k}_{l}-\boldsymbol{k}_{L}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}_{l}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{k}/\mu
since
\boldsymbol{A}_{l}\succeq\boldsymbol{C}_{l,\mathrm{eff}}\succeq\mu\boldsymbol{I}
. For the second term, note
\boldsymbol{A}_{l}-\boldsymbol{A}_{L}=(\boldsymbol{C}_{l}-\boldsymbol{C}_{L})+(\boldsymbol{k}_{l}\boldsymbol{k}_{l}^{T}-\boldsymbol{k}_{L}\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{T}),

hence

\|\boldsymbol{A}_{l}-\boldsymbol{A}_{L}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{C}+\|\boldsymbol{k}_{l}\boldsymbol{k}_{l}^{T}-\boldsymbol{k}_{L}\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{T}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{C}+(\|\boldsymbol{k}_{l}\|_{2}+\|\boldsymbol{k}_{L}\|_{2})\varepsilon_{k}\leq\varepsilon_{C}+2B_{k}\varepsilon_{k}
.
Apply Lemma
3
with
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}_{L}
,
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{A}_{l}
and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{C}+2B_{k}\varepsilon_{k}
(requiring
\varepsilon<\mu
) to obtain

\|\boldsymbol{A}_{l}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{L}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{C}+2B_{k}\varepsilon_{k}}{\mu(\mu-\varepsilon_{C}-2B_{k}\varepsilon_{k})}.

(57)

Therefore

\|\boldsymbol{k}_{L}^{T}(\boldsymbol{A}_{l}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{L}^{-1})\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{k}_{L}\|_{2}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{A}_{l}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{L}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|\boldsymbol{k}_{L}\|_{2}}{\mu(\mu-\varepsilon_{C}-2B_{k}\varepsilon_{k})}(\varepsilon_{C}+2B_{k}\varepsilon_{k}).

(58)

Combining the two terms yields the stated
O(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})
discrepancy bound.
∎

Lemma 5

(Proxy channel identity)
.

Let the proxy update be
\Delta_{\text{proxy}}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=\boldsymbol{t}_{\text{proxy}}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})\boldsymbol{M}_{L}

with
\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{\text{proxy}}/\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}=\boldsymbol{I}
(e.g.,
\boldsymbol{t}_{\text{proxy}}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
or the residual
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{W}_{L}\boldsymbol{k}_{L}
).
Then the proxy hypergradient equals

\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}=(\boldsymbol{J}_{\text{proxy}})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{L}=\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T},

(59)

and satisfies
\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}
.

Proof.

For any perturbation
\delta\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
,

\boldsymbol{J}_{\text{proxy}}[\delta\boldsymbol{v}]=(\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{\text{proxy}}/\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*})\delta\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{M}_{L}=\delta\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}
.
Then

\langle\boldsymbol{J}_{\text{proxy}}[\delta\boldsymbol{v}],\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\rangle_{F}=\langle\delta\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T}\rangle_{2}
,
so
(\boldsymbol{J}_{\text{proxy}})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{L}=\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T}
.
The norm bound follows by submultiplicativity:

\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}
.
∎

B.3.2
Proof of Theorem
3

We restate the theorem in the main-text notation:
define
\boldsymbol{g}_{L}:=(\boldsymbol{J}_{L})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{L}
.
Under dominance (
54
), feasibility consistency (
53
), and allocation stability
(Assumption
1
), we show:

\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|\leq\rho\|\boldsymbol{g}_{L}\|+K(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2},

(60)

and under non-degeneracy
\alpha(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})\geq\alpha_{0}>0
we obtain a descent-direction guarantee.

Step 1: Dominance controls the tail layers

From (
51
),
\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}}-\boldsymbol{g}_{L}=\sum_{l\neq L}(\boldsymbol{J}_{l})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{l}.

Taking norms and applying (
54
) yields

\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}}-\boldsymbol{g}_{L}\|\leq\rho\|\boldsymbol{g}_{L}\|.

(61)

Step 2: Allocation stability controls last-layer vs. proxy mismatch

For the last layer, we assume the solver channel has the outer-product structure

\Delta_{L}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})=\boldsymbol{t}_{L}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})\boldsymbol{M}_{L}
(standard in key-value editing channels).
By the same adjoint argument as Lemma
5
, the exact last-layer channel is

\boldsymbol{g}_{L}=(\boldsymbol{J}_{L})^{T}\boldsymbol{s}_{L}=\Big(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}\Big)^{\!T}\,(\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T}).

(62)

Using Lemma
5
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}=\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T}
.

\boldsymbol{g}_{L}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}=\Big(\Big(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}\Big)^{\!T}-\boldsymbol{I}\Big)\,(\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T}),

(63)

and therefore

\|\boldsymbol{g}_{L}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T}\|_{2}\leq\Big\|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}-\boldsymbol{I}\Big\|_{2}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}.

(64)

By Assumption
1
(iii),

\big\|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}-\boldsymbol{I}\big\|_{2}\leq L_{\text{id}}(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})
.
Substituting into (
64
) yields

\|\boldsymbol{g}_{L}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|\leq L_{\text{id}}(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}.

(65)

Step 3: Combine the two steps

Triangle inequality gives

\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}}-\boldsymbol{g}_{L}\|+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{L}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|.

Apply (
61
) and (
65
) to obtain

\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|\leq\rho\|\boldsymbol{g}_{L}\|+L_{\text{id}}(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}.

(66)

This proves the stated bound with
K:=L_{\text{id}}
.

Step 4: Descent-direction guarantee under non-degeneracy

Define the measurable non-degeneracy coefficient
\alpha(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}):=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\boldsymbol{M}_{L}^{T}\|_{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}}=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}}\in[0,1].

If
\alpha(\boldsymbol{v}^{*})\geq\alpha_{0}>0
, then

\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}\leq\alpha_{0}^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|.

(67)

Moreover, from (
62
) and Assumption
1
(iii),

\|\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{L}/\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\|_{2}\leq 1+L_{\text{id}}(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})
,
hence

\|\boldsymbol{g}_{L}\|\leq\Big\|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{t}_{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}\Big\|_{2}\,\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|\leq\big(1+L_{\text{id}}(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})\big)\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|.

(68)

Combine the main bound with (
67
) and (
68
):

\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|\leq\rho\|\boldsymbol{g}_{L}\|+K(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})\|\boldsymbol{s}_{L}\|_{F}\|\boldsymbol{M}_{L}\|_{2}\leq\Big(\rho+\widetilde{K}(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})\Big)\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|,

(69)

for some
\widetilde{K}>0
(absorbing
\rho L_{\text{id}}
and
\alpha_{0}^{-1}K
into the constant).

Finally,

\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}},\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\rangle=\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|^{2}+\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}},\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\rangle\geq\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}}-\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|\,\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\|.

(70)

Thus, if
\rho+\widetilde{K}(\varepsilon_{C}+\varepsilon_{k})<1
, we obtain

\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{true}},\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}\rangle>0
,
i.e.,
\boldsymbol{g}_{\text{proxy}}
is a valid descent direction.
