 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 West Biofuels - in collaboration with the University of California San Diego, the University of
 California Davis, the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), the Colorado School of Mines’
 Center for Hydrate Research, Placer County Air Pollution Control District, the Sierra Business
 Council, and the Southern California Gas Company (SoCalGas)—will demonstrate an
 innovative pathway to convert forest biomass to renewable gas (RG). The project, Production of
 Pipeline Grade Renewable Natural Gas and Value-Added Chemicals from Forest Biomass
 Residues, will utilize an existing pilot-scale gasification system and data and lessons learned
 from a lab-scale catalytic reactor design, develop, and demonstrate an integrated pilot-scale RG
 process with a scaled-up catalytic reactor and hydrate-based gas separation process. This robust
 and simple process uses a commercial proven gasifier, a commercially available catalyst that can
 tolerate gas contaminants, and a gas separation process that simplifies the downstream
 processing. Current testing has shown that the process can yield a large amount of methane rich
 renewable gas in addition to a mixture of propanol, ethanol and other higher alcohols and the
 relative amounts can be controlled by shifting the process conditions. This process is novel and
 groundbreaking because the product mixture of RG and valuable alcohols byproducts makes RG
 production economically feasible at $12 per million British thermal units (MMBtu) or less using
 forest biomass from high hazard zones

 Executive Summary
 This project, Production of Pipeline Grade Renewable Natural Gas and Value-Added Chemicals
 from Forest Biomass Residues, was commissioned to advance the state of cellulosic biomass to
 renewable gas (RG) technologies by leveraging an existing pilot-scale gasification system and an
 existing catalytic reactor with downstream separation processes to upgrade forest biomass-
 derived syngas to methane-rich RG and mixed-alcohol byproducts. The final RG product
 requirements include meeting heat content standards of 990-1150 BTU/scf and total sulfur of
 less than 12.6 parts per million by volume (ppmv) with >2.5 scfm of RG output.

 Herein a techno-economic analysis was performed around the West Biofuels mixed alcohol
 process leveraging, where appropriate, inputs from previous reports on biomass gasification
 (e.g., NREL: Dutta, 2011 [1], West Biofuels ARV-15-017 [2]). The studied reactor and catalyst
 system can be operated in such a manner as to prioritize either the production of alcohols or RG
 and consequently two scenarios were selected for analysis to examine the limits of performance.

 The calculated production cost and quality of the renewable gas and ethanol products in each
 respective case is provided in Tables EX1 – EX2. These data show that in the absence of CO2
 credits (i.e., from carbon sequestration, 45Q), the estimated costs to reach pipeline/distribution
 quality products are $22.60/MMBtu for renewable gas in the high RG scenario and $3.83/gal
 EtOH in the high alcohol synthesis scenario. Further, through the development of a dedicated
 downstream purification train for RG cleanup, the produced gas was able to meet all stated
 pipeline requirements with respect to heating value, sulfur content, and CO 2 content.

 1 Introduction
 This project, Production of Pipeline Grade Renewable Natural Gas and Value-Added Chemicals
 from Forest Biomass Residues, seeks to advance the state of cellulosic biomass to renewable gas
 (RG) technologies by leveraging an existing pilot-scale gasification system and an existing
 catalytic reactor with downstream separation processes to upgrade forest biomass-derived syngas
 to methane-rich RG and mixed-alcohol byproducts. The final RG product requirements include
 meeting heat content standards of 990-1150 BTU/scf and total sulfur of less than 12.6 parts per
 million by volume (ppmv) with >2.5 scfm of RG output.

 The proposed process will produce RG with a host of byproducts, including methanol, ethanol,
 n-propanol, and higher alcohols that can be used in the chemical and transportation fuel markets
 along with carbon dioxide that could be used for carbon sequestration. This variety of byproducts
 reduces the economic risk of the project and allows a commercial facility to be market
 responsive and adjust product production (i.e., RG focused or alcohol focused) to react to market
 demand over time.

 The economic analysis presented in this report builds off prior established analysis models for
 mixed alcohol synthesis via biomass gasification [1 – 2], and is informed by data provided by
 West Biofuels to achieve the following:

 • An nth-plant techno-economic analysis for a commercial facility.
 • The levelized cost of RG, showing pathways to reaching a range between $12/MMBtu
 and $21/MMBtu.
 • Identification of critical production processes and equipment.
 • Identification of operating and construction costs.

 2 Design Basis
 The core mixed alcohol reactor system developed by West Biofuels can be operated in such a
 manner as to favor gaseous or liquid products with varying specificity depending on operating
 conditions. For the purposes of this analysis, we evaluate two ‘bookend’ scenarios to highlight
 this dynamic range: (1) a high RG yield scenario and (2) a high alcohol yield scenario. For the
 remainder of the report these two scenarios will be denoted as the HighRG and HighAlc cases. It
 should be stressed that all analysis and modelling was based on previous experimental work
 provided by West Biofuels and all flows around the mixed alcohol synthesis (MAS) reactor were
 used verbatim with respect to inlet, recycle, and product compositions and flow rates. Any
 uncertainties or idiosyncrasies in the provided data are propagated through the analyses.

 General plant design and operating assumptions are, unless stated otherwise herein, based on
 previous NREL mixed alcohol reports [1]. Consequently, with many of the key process areas
 being identical to those in prior reports, in the following sections we use excerpts of existing
 language verbatim or in a summarized form.

 2.2 Aspen Plus Model Details
 Aspen Plus version 12 simulation was used to perform the process modeling in this report. The
 Redlich– Kwong–Soave with Boston–Mathias correction (RKS-BM) equation of state was used
 throughout much of the process simulation for high temperature, high pressure gas and liquid
 phase behavior. The non-random, two-liquid (NRTL) option with ideal gas properties was used
 for alcohol separation calculations. The ASME 1967 steam table correlations were used for the
 steam cycle calculations. The ELECNRTL package was used to model the electrolyte species in
 the quench water system. The physical solvent absorption system and the ammonia-water
 absorption refrigeration system were modeled in separate simulations and their results were
 incorporated into the main model. The perturbed-chain statistical associating fluid theory
 (PCSAFT) package was used to model physical solvent (DEPG) absorption processes in a
 separate simulation.

 3 Process Design and Key Assumptions
 The process design for MAS and RG production comprises eight distinct modeled areas:

 • Area 100: Feed handling and preparation
 • Area 200: Gasification
 • Area 300: Gas quench, scrubbing, and compression
 • Area 400: Mixed alcohol synthesis
 • Area 500: Alcohol separation
 • Area 1100: Gas clean up and methanation
 • Area 700: Integrated steam system and power generation cycle
 • Area 800: Cooling water and other utilities

 A high-level block flow diagram showing the connectivity of each area is provided in Figure 6.
 Key assumptions and operation conditions are summarized for each area in the subsections
 below.

 3.1 Area 100: Feed Handling and Drying
 For the purposes of this analysis, it is assumed that the plant will operate exclusively on forest
 residue feedstocks as motivated by the legislative actions across California and the California
 Energy Commission (CEC) to promote healthy forests, mitigate wildfire disasters, and generate
 sustainable products.

 The ultimate analysis for the feed considered in this study is presented in Table 7. The assumed
 moisture content of the biomass feedstock prior to drying is 35 wt % at the plant gate, with an
 additional 5 wt % moisture loss during in-plant processing. The design capacity for this study is
 2,000 dry metric tonnes per day (2,205 dry tons per day), which matches that of previous NREL
 design reports for ethanol processes. With an expected 7,884 operating hours per year (90% on-
 stream factor / availability), the annual feedstock requirement is approximately 657,000 dry metric
 tonnes per year (724,000 dry tons per year).

 The delivered feedstock cost was estimated at $63.23 per dry ton based on latest NREL reports [4]
 which includes all capital costs, operating costs, and dry matter losses associated with feed
 delivery, drying, and handling. Up to the throat of the gasifier, the only process input that is added
 to the feedstock is flue gas waste heat for drying the feedstock from 30% moisture (after in-plant
 handling) to 10% moisture (at the gasifier).

 3.2 Area 200: Gasification
 The forest residue feedstock is indirectly gasified. Heat for the gasification reactions is supplied
 by circulating synthetic olivine sand that is pre-heated in a char combustor and fed to the
 gasifier. Conveyors and hoppers feed biomass to the low-pressure entrained flow gasifier. Steam
 is injected into the gasifier to stabilize the flow of biomass and olivine through the gasifier.
 Within the gasifier, biomass thermally deconstructs to a mixture of syngas components (CO, H 2,
 CO2, CH4, etc.), tars, and solid char containing residual carbon from the biomass and coke
 deposited on the olivine. Cyclones at the exit of the gasifier separate the char and olivine from
 the syngas. The solids flow to the char combustor where the char is burned in air in a fluidized
 bed, resulting in olivine temperatures greater than 1,800°F (982°C). The hot olivine and residual
 ash are carried out of the combustor by the combustion gases and separated using a pair of
 cyclones. The first cyclone captures olivine while the second cyclone captures ash and olivine
 fines. Hot olivine flows back into the gasifier, completing the gasification loop. The hot flue gas
 from the char combustor is utilized for heat recovery and feedstock drying. Ash and olivine fines
 are cooled, moistened to minimize dust, and removed as waste. A conceptual overview is shown
 in Figure 7.

 3.3 Area 300: Gas Cleanup
 Based on design modifications implemented by West Biofuels, syngas cleanup is defined in this
 report as the condensation and scrubbing of the syngas for downstream operations. The hot
 syngas is cooled through heat exchange with other process streams and scrubbed with water to
 remove persistent impurities like particulates, ammonia, halides, and recalcitrant tars. Scrubber
 water is purged and treated continuously an on-site wastewater treatment facility. After heat
 recovery, the remaining low-quality heat in the flue gas from the catalyst regenerator is utilized
 for feedstock drying.

 3.4 Area 400: Alcohol Synthesis
 Cooled low-pressure syngas enters a six-stage centrifugal compressor system where the pressure
 is increased to approximately 1,500 psi or 2,350 psi in the HighRG and HighAlc cases,
 respectively. The pressurized syngas is fed to the tube sides of two vertical tubular reactors (shell
 and tube type) operating in parallel. An Albemarle molybdenum sulfide-type mixed alcohol
 catalyst is packed within the tubes of the reactor, which is oriented in a down-flow configuration.
 Reactions at ~ 595°F convert a portion of the syngas to oxygenate and hydrocarbon products to
 mirror the compositions provided by West Biofuels in Section 2.1. Heat from the reactions is
 removed by steam generation in the shell side of the tubular reactor. The reactor effluent
 consisting of mixed alcohols, gaseous by-products (such as CO2 and methane), and unconverted
 syngas is cooled through heat exchange with other process streams. As the reactor effluent cools,
 alcohols and water are condensed and sent to downstream separation and purification equipment.

 3.5 Area 500: Alcohol Separation

 3.6 Area 1100: Renewable Natural Gas Clean Up and Methanation
 Gaseous products from the MAS reactor undergo a water gas shift clean up stage prior to acid
 gas removal to achieve a H2:CO ratio of 3:1 at the inlet of the methanation reactor. Acid gas
 removal was modeled using the basis in the 2011 design report where a Selexol system was
 reported. In many methanation implementations reported in literature Rectisol systems are used
 instead. However, based on modeling priorities with the resources available, it was decided to
 maintain the Selexol system for acid gas removal, instead of spending resources on modeling a
 Rectisol system (Rectisol systems are well documented in literature and can be included
 separately).

 Methanation was selected as the final downstream purification step to utilize the remaining
 syngas and meet the final pipeline specifications for allowable H 2 and CO content. The
 methanation reactor was modeled as a 3-adiabatic reactor system operating at ~30 bar, with
 intermediate cooling between reactors. Methanation reactors typically operate between 300-700
 °C and close to equilibrium conversions can be achieved based on some comparison in the
 literature for temperatures around 350 °C, 30 atm, and a H2:CO ratio of 3:1 [5]. The reactors
 were thus modeled assuming equilibrium compositions, with the final reactor limited to 350 °C
 at the outlet.

 3.7 Area 700: Integrated Steam System and Power Generation Cycle
 A conventional steam cycle produces steam for direct injection into the gasifier. Indirect steam is
 used for distillation, absorption refrigeration, and the acid gas strippers in the DEPG and acid gas
 enrichment units. Electricity is generated using two steam turbines, with intermediate reheat, to
 meet the demands of the plant. As mentioned earlier in the alcohol synthesis section, additional
 electricity is generated by sending a portion of the pressurized unreacted gases from the alcohol
 synthesis reactor through turbo-expanders. The majority of electricity is used for syngas
 compression. The steam cycle is integrated with the biomass conversion process. Pre-heaters,
 steam generators, and super-heaters are integrated within the process design to generate the

 22

 steam from boiler feed water. Process condensate is recycled to the steam cycle, degassed,
 treated, and combined with makeup water.

 Previous analyses of gasification systems have shown the importance of properly utilizing the
 heat from the high temperature streams to improve efficiency of the plant design. A pinch
 analysis was performed and a heat exchanger network was designed for this production process.
 The pinch concept offers a systematic approach to optimizing the energy integration of the
 process.

 3.8 Area 800: Cooling Water and Other Utilities
 A cooling water system is included in the Aspen Plus model to determine the requirements of
 each cooling water heat exchanger within the biomass conversion process as well as makeup
 water and power requirements. In a version of the design that includes additional water
 optimization, process condensate is treated and combined with the cooling water makeup to
 reduce freshwater consumption.

 4 Process Economics
 The first steps of the economic analysis are to determine the total capital investment (TCI),
 variable operating costs, and fixed operating costs associated with constructing and operating the
 plant. With these estimated costs, a discounted cash flow analysis is then used to determine the
 production cost of RG for a project having a net present value of zero at an assumed internal rate
 of return (IRR). This section describes the cost areas and the necessary assumptions required to
 complete the discounted cash flow analysis.

 5 Critical Processes and Parameters
 Across the two studied HighRG and HighAlc scenarios, we find that feedstock costs contribute
 to ~98% of the total variable operating costs and nearly 40% of the total product cost, thus
 sourcing the lowest costs feedstocks should be one of the highest priorities. Another ~50% of
 the total product cost was attributed to the capital investment charges. A significant fraction of
 the capital (~50%) arose from the newly added Area 1100 equipment required to purify the RG
 product to meet natural gas pipeline specifications. Consequently, reactor and catalyst
 modifications that minimize the downstream separations demands of the RG should also be
 prioritized to minimize the impact of the capital charges.

 6 Conclusions
 In this analysis two configurations of the West Biofuels mixed alcohol synthesis reactor were
 studied: one to maximize the production of mixed alcohols and one favoring the production of
 renewable gas. The economic analysis shows in the RG production configuration, RG can be
 produced with an estimated cost of $19.00 - $22.60 / MMBtu depending on whether a $40/tonne
 CO2 sequestration credit is claimed. This puts the estimated cost on the cusp of the upper
 targeted range of $12-$21/MMBtu. In alcohol production configuration, estimated production
 cost of the targeted ethanol product ranged from $2.66 - $3.83 / gal EtOH depending on
 application of the same CO2 sequestration credit. This represents an increase in calculated cost
 compared to the prior mixed alcohol reports published by NREL and West Biofuels that showed
 a production cost of approximately $2.20 / gal EtOH (in 2016$), without the RG production
 modifications and syngas conversion yield assumptions detailed in the design report [1]. Due to
 limitations in resources and data quality, these results should be viewed as high-level estimates
 only and more rigorous analyses using experimental data and measurements from the pilot-scale
 facility operating on forest residues should be performed for more accurate results.
