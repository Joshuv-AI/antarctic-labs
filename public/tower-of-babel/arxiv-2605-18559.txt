Localization of a quantum particle in a classical one-component plasma. III. Mutual coherence and coherence degradation in Coulomb-disordered media
Yury A. Budkov

Abstract
We derive the mutual coherence function of an electron beam propagating through a static or dynamic Coulomb-disordered medium and show that its decay introduces an intrinsic coherence-reduction mechanism relevant for electron microscopy in Coulomb-disordered media. Using the Efimov path-integral formalism, the coherence length ρ c \rho_{c} is expressed through the same disorder correlator that governs the single-particle localization length ℓ \ell . For both a static electrolyte and a dynamic plasma we obtain a universal relation ρ c ∼ λ D ​ ℓ / L \rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\ell/L} , where λ D \lambda_{D} is the Debye length and L L the sample thickness. In the static case ℓ ∝ k 2 \ell\propto k^{2} (electron momentum), whereas in the dynamic slow-particle regime ℓ ∝ k \ell\propto k , leading to qualitatively different energy dependences of the coherence scale. The ion thermal velocity cancels out in the final expression, demonstrating a formal connection between transverse coherence decay and longitudinal localization phenomena. Exact analytical results are given for the phase structure function of a model electrolyte, and numerical estimates indicate that disorder-induced phase decorrelation may contribute appreciably to the attenuation of high-spatial-frequency contrast under experimentally relevant liquid-cell electron microscopy conditions. Possible implications for cryo-EM, disordered liquids, soft condensed matter, and biological media are discussed. In an appendix we extend the theory to the relativistic regime relevant for transmission electron microscopy. A paraxial reduction of the Dirac equation leads to an effective scalar Schrödinger equation with a renormalised coupling parameter A rel = ( γ + 1 ) / ( 2 ​ γ ​ ℏ ​ v ) A_{\rm rel}=(\gamma+1)/(2\gamma\hbar v) , which reduces to 1 / ( ℏ ​ v ) 1/(\hbar v) in the non‑relativistic limit and saturates at 1 / ( 2 ​ ℏ ​ c ) 1/(2\hbar c) for ultra‑relativistic electrons. The scaling relation ρ c ∼ λ D ​ ℓ / L \rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\ell/L} remains structurally unchanged.



I
Introduction

The interplay between disorder and quantum interference is central to our understanding of transport in solids, plasmas, and liquids. Anderson localization
3
– the exponential decay of the single-particle Green function – remains a cornerstone of condensed-matter physics. In two preceding papers
7
;
8
(Parts I and II) we developed a microscopic theory of disorder-induced localization for a quantum particle in a classical one-component plasma, within both static and dynamic approximations. The random potential acting on the test particle originates from the equilibrium thermal fluctuations of the ionic charge density, described in the random phase approximation (RPA). The principal result of Part I was the emergence of a Coulomb logarithm
\ln(\kappa L)
in the localization length
\ell
, which reflects the long-range
1/r
tail of the potential correlator. Part II extended the theory to the dynamic regime, showing that for particles slower than the ion thermal speed the Coulomb logarithm disappears and the localization length scales linearly with the momentum.

In the present paper we examine the two-particle coherence properties that are directly relevant to imaging. Electron microscopy, and in particular liquid-cell and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), rely on the interference of electron waves to reconstruct the structure of a sample
12
;
21
. Even with a perfect instrument, the disorder caused by the thermal motion of ions in the sample introduces an irreversible loss of phase coherence between different rays
10
;
15
. This decoherence provides an additional intrinsic mechanism of coherence reduction that may contribute to image blurring and contrast degradation
11
.

The present work is intended as a leading-order coherence theory formulated within the eikonal and weak-scattering approximations. The analysis neglects strong multiple scattering, inelastic channels, beam-shape effects, and reconstruction procedures used in modern cryo-EM and liquid-cell microscopy. Consequently, the coherence scale
\rho_{c}
derived below should not be interpreted as a universal experimental resolution bound, but rather as a characteristic scale associated with disorder-induced phase decorrelation within the present model.

The study of electron diffraction on liquids has a long history. Early pioneering work demonstrated that electron interference can be obtained by transmitting electrons through thin liquid films, and that the resulting diffraction patterns encode the radial distribution function of the liquid
26
;
24
. The development of ultrathin liquid-sheet technologies has recently enabled high-quality electron scattering on liquid samples, allowing the retrieval of real-space molecular structure from experimental electron scattering patterns of liquid water and other solvents
32
;
31
. Comprehensive reviews of the experimental and theoretical landscape have covered the determination of radial distribution curves of liquids by diffraction methods
22
;
14
, the application of electron nanodiffraction to studying the structure and dynamics of amorphous solids and liquids
19
, and the use of pair distribution function analysis from electron diffraction for structural characterization of disordered materials
27
;
18
. A particularly detailed account of the theory of electron scattering in liquids is given in the classic treatise by Cowley
10
, which remains the standard reference for diffraction physics.

We demonstrate that the mutual coherence function – the Cooperon-like propagator – decays with the transverse separation
\rho
of the two rays, and that the characteristic decay length
\rho_{c}
is intimately related to the single-particle localization length
\ell
. More precisely, in the eikonal (straight-line) approximation we obtain a scaling

\rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{L}},

(1)

where
\lambda_{D}
is the Debye length and
L
is the propagation distance through the disordered medium. This result holds both for static disorder (fast electrons) and for dynamic disorder (slow electrons), with the only difference being the momentum dependence of
\ell
. Physically, Eq. (
1
) means that the same thermal fluctuations that localize the electron wave along its direction of motion also destroy the mutual coherence of two parallel rays. The resolution limit
\Delta r_{\min}\sim\rho_{c}
is thus a direct consequence of electron localization.

I.1
Physical origin of the Cooperon-like propagator and its role in diffraction

In a coherent electron microscope, the image is formed by the interference of partial waves scattered from different points of the sample.
The recorded intensity at a detector pixel is proportional to the squared modulus of the total electron field, which can be written schematically as

I(\mathbf{r})\propto\big|\psi_{1}(\mathbf{r})+\psi_{2}(\mathbf{r})+\cdots\big|^{2}.

When the illumination is coherent, the field from every pair of points in the sample contributes an interference term of the form

2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\psi_{i}^{*}\psi_{j}\big)
.
The image contrast at a given spatial frequency is therefore determined by the degree to which the phases of the waves emanating from two points separated by a distance
\rho
are locked to each other.

In a perfectly homogeneous medium the relative phase of two such waves is determined solely by geometry. In a disordered medium, however, the random potential introduces additional, uncorrelated phase shifts along the two propagation paths. The measurable consequence of this stochastic dephasing is captured by the
mutual coherence function

\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})=\big\langle G^{R}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{1})\,G^{A}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{2})\big\rangle,

where the angular brackets denote an average over the statistical ensemble of the fluctuating medium.
This object is the
Cooperon
of the mesoscopic transport theory
2
;
23
;
1
.
Its physical meaning is transparent: it describes the correlation of the complex amplitudes (not intensities) of two waves that have travelled from the source points
\mathbf{r}_{1}
and
\mathbf{r}_{2}
to the same detection point
\mathbf{R}
.
As long as
|\Gamma|
remains close to its maximal value, the waves interfere with high contrast; when
|\Gamma|
decays, the interference fringes are washed out.

For a statistically homogeneous and isotropic medium the Cooperon-like propagator depends only on the transverse separation
\rho=|\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}|
, and one defines the normalised degree of coherence

\gamma(\rho)=\frac{\Gamma(\rho)}{\Gamma(0)}.

The function
\gamma(\rho)
is precisely the quantity that enters the envelope of the contrast transfer function in an electron microscope.
Its decay length
\rho_{c}
therefore sets the
intrinsic
resolution limit of the imaging system, i.e. the characteristic transverse scale over which interference fringes remain strongly correlated.

In what follows we evaluate
\gamma(\rho)
microscopically, starting from the path‑integral representation of the single‑particle Green function.
The decay of
\gamma(\rho)
is found to be controlled by the
phase structure function

D_{\phi}(\rho)

29
,

\gamma(\rho)=\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}D_{\phi}(\rho)\right],

which accumulates the variance of the phase difference acquired along the two trajectories.
For small
\rho
the expansion
D_{\phi}(\rho)\propto\rho^{2}
leads to a Gaussian coherence envelope,

\gamma(\rho)\simeq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2\rho_{c}^{2}}\right).

The explicit form of
\rho_{c}
will be expressed through the disorder correlator and, ultimately, through the single‑particle localization length
\ell
.

II
Static disordered medium

II.1
Field correlation function

Let an electron with energy
E=\hbar^{2}k^{2}/(2m)
propagate through a static random potential
W(\mathbf{r})
generated by microscopic density fluctuations of the medium. The electron field at the detector point
\mathbf{R}
is expressed through the retarded Green function:

\psi(\mathbf{R})=\int d^{3}r\,G^{R}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r};E)\,\phi(\mathbf{r}),

(2)

where
\phi(\mathbf{r})
describes the incident beam. The mutual coherence function is

\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})=\big\langle G^{R}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{1})\,G^{A}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{2})\big\rangle,

(3)

where the average is over realizations of the disorder, and the normalized degree of coherence is

\gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})=\frac{\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})}{\sqrt{\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{1})\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{2})}}.

(4)

The decay of
\gamma
determines the loss of interference contrast and therefore sets the intrinsic coherence-decay scale for interference contrast.

II.2
Path-integral representation and disorder averaging

Using the Efimov representation of the Green function
13
, the retarded propagator can be written as

G^{R}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}|W)=B\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{3/2}}\exp\!\Big[\frac{i}{2}kL\Big(s+\frac{1}{s}\Big)\Big]\int\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,e^{iS_{0}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]}e^{-iS_{W}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]},

(5)

with
L=|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{r}|
,
S_{0}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}d\tau\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{2}(\tau)
, and

S_{W}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]=\frac{ms}{k\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{L}d\tau\,W\!\Big(\mathbf{r}_{\rm cl}(\tau)+\sqrt{\frac{s}{k}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau)\Big).

(6)

The classical trajectory is
\mathbf{r}_{\rm cl}(\tau)=\mathbf{r}+\frac{\tau}{L}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{r})
. The advanced Green function is obtained by complex conjugation.

The two-particle correlator becomes

\Gamma=B^{2}\int\frac{ds_{1}\,ds_{2}}{(s_{1}s_{2})^{3/2}}e^{i\Phi_{0}}\int D\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}D\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\,e^{iS_{0}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}]-iS_{0}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}]}\big\langle e^{-iS_{W,1}+iS_{W,2}}\big\rangle,

(7)

with
\Phi_{0}=\frac{k}{2}\big[L_{1}(s_{1}+1/s_{1})-L_{2}(s_{2}+1/s_{2})\big]
.

Assuming a Gaussian random potential with zero mean and correlator

\langle W(\mathbf{r})W(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle=K(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(8)

the average of the exponential is

\big\langle e^{-iS_{W,1}+iS_{W,2}}\big\rangle=\exp\!\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\langle(S_{W,1}-S_{W,2})^{2}\rangle\Big].

(9)

A straightforward calculation yields

\langle(S_{W,1}-S_{W,2})^{2}\rangle=I_{11}+I_{22}-2I_{12},

(10)

where

I_{ij}=A_{i}A_{j}\int_{0}^{L_{i}}d\tau\int_{0}^{L_{j}}d\tau^{\prime}\,K\!\big(\mathbf{r}_{i}(\tau)-\mathbf{r}_{j}(\tau^{\prime})\big),\qquad A_{i}=\frac{ms_{i}}{k\hbar^{2}}.

(11)

The terms
I_{11}
and
I_{22}
describe the attenuation of the one-particle Green function, while
I_{12}
controls phase coherence.

II.3
Saddle-point approximation and eikonal limit

For macroscopic propagation distances,
kL\gg 1
, the integrals over
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are dominated by the stationary points of
\Phi_{0}
, giving
s_{1}=s_{2}=1
. In the pre-exponential factor and in
I_{ij}
we therefore set
s_{1}=s_{2}=1
, i.e.
A_{1}=A_{2}=A=m/(k\hbar^{2})
. Neglecting transverse quantum fluctuations (eikonal approximation) and assuming
L_{1}\simeq L_{2}\simeq L
, the coherence function reduces to

\gamma(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\exp\!\Big[-\frac{1}{2}D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\Big],

(12)

with the phase structure function

D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=2A^{2}\int_{0}^{L}d\tau\int_{0}^{L}d\tau^{\prime}\Big[K(\mathbf{n}(\tau-\tau^{\prime}))-K(\boldsymbol{\rho}+\mathbf{n}(\tau-\tau^{\prime}))\Big],

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}
and
\mathbf{n}=(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{r})/L
. For
L
much larger than the correlation length of the disorder,

D_{\phi}(\rho)\simeq 4A^{2}L\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\big[K(u)-K\!\big(\sqrt{u^{2}+\rho^{2}}\big)\big].

(14)

II.4
Exact evaluation for an electrolyte

For an electrolyte modeled as a classical one-component plasma in a medium with dielectric permittivity
\varepsilon
, the potential correlator is
7

K(r)=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}{\varepsilon}\frac{1-e^{-\kappa r}}{r},\quad\kappa=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi n_{0}q^{2}}{\varepsilon k_{\mathrm{B}}T}}.

(15)

Substituting this into Eq. (
14
) and evaluating the integrals exactly yields the phase structure function

D_{\phi}(\rho)=\frac{4m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}L}{\varepsilon\,\hbar^{4}k^{2}}\int_{0}^{\kappa\rho}dt\,\Big[\frac{1}{t}-K_{1}(t)\Big],

(16)

where
K_{1}(t)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind.
Equation (
16
) expresses
D_{\phi}(\rho)
directly through the fundamental parameters of the model — the electron energy, temperature, dielectric constant, ionic charge, and ionic concentration (via
\kappa
) — without auxiliary constants. In the high-energy limit
k\gg\kappa
the static localization length is
7

\ell=\frac{\hbar^{4}k^{2}\varepsilon k_{B}T}{m^{2}q_{0}^{2}\ln(\kappa L_{s})},

(17)

where
L_{s}\simeq L
is the macroscopic cut-off. Therefore, in the limit
\kappa\rho\ll 1
, we have

D_{\phi}(\rho)\simeq\frac{L\,\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}}{\ell\,\ln(\kappa L_{s})}\ln\frac{2}{\kappa\rho}.

(18)

The coherence length
\rho_{c}
, defined by
\gamma(\rho_{c})=e^{-1}
, satisfies

\rho_{c}^{2}\simeq\frac{2\ell\,\ln(\kappa L_{s})}{L\,\kappa^{2}\,\ln(2/\kappa\rho_{c})}\sim\lambda_{D}^{2}\,\frac{\ell}{L},

(19)

where
\lambda_{D}=1/\kappa
is the Debye length. Thus

\rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{L}}.

(20)

Note that the exact numerical factor depends on the slowly varying logarithm.

II.5
Large-distance asymptotics

For transverse separations much larger than the Debye length,

\kappa\rho\gg 1
, the dominant contribution to the integral
in Eq. (
16
) comes from the region

t\gtrsim 1
, where the modified Bessel function
K_{1}(t)

falls off exponentially,

K_{1}(t)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2t}}\,e^{-t},\qquad t\gg 1.

Hence the term
K_{1}(t)
in the integrand is exponentially
suppressed, and the integral reduces to the elementary form

\int_{0}^{\kappa\rho}\frac{dt}{t}=\ln(\kappa\rho).

Retaining the leading logarithmic growth, we obtain

D_{\phi}(\rho)\;\simeq\;\frac{4m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}L}{\varepsilon\,\hbar^{4}k^{2}}\,\ln(\kappa\rho),\qquad\kappa\rho\gg 1.

(21)

The corresponding mutual coherence function therefore decays
as a power law,

\gamma(\rho)=\exp\!\bigl[-\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\phi}(\rho)\bigr]\;\sim\;(\kappa\rho)^{-\alpha}\;=\;\left(\frac{\lambda_{D}}{\rho}\right)^{\!\alpha},

where
\alpha=\frac{2m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}L}{\varepsilon\,\hbar^{4}k^{2}}
.

The logarithmic growth of
D_{\phi}(\rho)
at large separations
is a direct consequence of the unscreened
1/r
tail of the
potential correlator
K(r)\simeq C/r
for
r\gg\lambda_{D}
.
When
\rho\gg\lambda_{D}
, the difference

K(u)-K\bigl(\sqrt{u^{2}+\rho^{2}}\bigr)
behaves as
\propto 1/u

at large
u
, yielding a logarithmically divergent integral.
Thus, while the coherence envelope is Gaussian for

\rho\ll\lambda_{D}
, it crosses over to a much slower,
power-law decay on scales exceeding the Debye screening length.
This behaviour is reminiscent of the well‑known
wave‑structure‑function results obtained by Tatarskii and
Ishimaru for wave propagation through random media
characterised by long‑range correlations
30
;
20
.

Equation (
21
) also shows that the exponent of
the power law is proportional to the product
A^{2}LC
, i.e. the effective disorder strength integrated along the
propagation path. Because
C=k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}/\varepsilon
,
the coherence decays more rapidly in hotter samples or in media
with lower dielectric permittivity, in agreement with the
physical picture that stronger Coulomb fluctuations destroy
phase correlations more efficiently.

III
Dynamic disordered medium

We now consider the dynamic ion plasma, where the potential correlator depends on time. The line-of-sight correlation function for a particle moving with constant velocity
v
is

K_{\rm dyn}(\boldsymbol{\rho},u)\equiv\langle W(\mathbf{0},0)W(\boldsymbol{\rho}+\mathbf{n}u,u/v)\rangle.

(22)

In the eikonal approximation the phase structure function reads

D_{\phi}(\rho)=2A^{2}L\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\,\big[K_{\rm dyn}(\mathbf{0},u)-K_{\rm dyn}(\boldsymbol{\rho},u)\big].

(23)

For small
\rho
,

D_{\phi}(\rho)\simeq-\frac{A^{2}L}{2}\,\rho^{2}I_{\perp},\qquad I_{\perp}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\,\nabla_{\!\perp}^{2}K_{\rm dyn}(\mathbf{0},u).

(24)

III.1
Evaluation of
I_{\perp}

The dynamic correlator is expressed through its spectral density:

K_{\rm dyn}(\mathbf{0},u)=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,e^{ik_{\parallel}u}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,e^{-i\omega u/v}\,\widetilde{K}(k,\omega).

(25)

Applying
\nabla_{\!\perp}^{2}
and integrating over
u
yields a
\delta
-function:

I_{\perp}=-v\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,k_{\perp}^{2}\,\widetilde{K}(k,\omega=vk_{\parallel}).

(26)

For a classical OCP the spectral density is
8

\widetilde{K}(k,\omega)=-\frac{8\pi q_{0}^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{k^{2}\,\omega}\,\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k,\omega)}.

(27)

In the slow-particle limit
v\ll v_{\rm th}
we use the low-frequency expansion of the RPA dielectric function,

\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k,\omega)}\simeq-\frac{\kappa^{2}k}{(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac{\omega}{v_{\rm th}}.

(28)

Substituting
\omega=vk_{\parallel}
and simplifying, we obtain

I_{\perp}=-8\pi q_{0}^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\,\frac{v}{v_{\rm th}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{k_{\perp}^{2}}{k^{2}}\frac{\kappa^{2}k}{(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}}.

(29)

The angular integration gives
8\pi/3
, and the remaining radial integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\frac{\kappa^{2}k^{3}}{(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}}\simeq\kappa^{2}\ln\frac{k_{\max}}{\kappa}

(30)

diverges logarithmically at large
k
, cut off at
k_{\max}\sim 1/b_{\min}
(
b_{\min}
is the minimum impact parameter,
\sim\hbar/mv
for a quantum particle). Collecting all factors,

I_{\perp}\simeq-\,\frac{4}{3}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\frac{v}{v_{\rm th}}\,q_{0}^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\,\kappa^{2}\,\ln\frac{k_{\max}}{\kappa}.

(31)

III.2
Coherence length in the dynamic regime

In the weak-localization regime (
v\ll v_{\rm th}
,
k\gg\kappa
) the dynamic localization length is
8

\ell(k)=\frac{\hbar^{3}v_{\rm th}}{m\,k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}\,k.

(32)

Substituting Eqs. (
31
) into Eq. (
24
) and using (
32
), we obtain

D_{\phi}(\rho)\simeq\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\frac{L\,\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}}{\ell}\,\ln\frac{k_{\max}}{\kappa}.

(33)

The coherence length
\rho_{c}
defined by
\frac{1}{2}D_{\phi}(\rho_{c})=1
is therefore

\rho_{c}=\lambda_{D}\,\sqrt{\frac{3\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{2}\,\ln(\lambda_{D}/b_{\min})}\,\frac{\ell}{L}}.

(34)

Thus we recover the same scaling
\rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\ell/L}
as in the static case. The difference is only in the momentum dependence of
\ell
: linear (
\propto k
) in the dynamic regime vs. quadratic in the static regime.

Notably, the logarithmic factor
\ln(\lambda_{D}/b_{\min})
appearing in the denominator of
\rho_{c}^{2}
is precisely the Coulomb logarithm familiar from the kinetic theory of plasmas
28
. In the dynamic regime, the ultraviolet divergence of the radial integral is cut off by the minimum impact parameter
b_{\min}
, which for a quantum particle is of the order of the de Broglie wavelength
\hbar/mv
. The resulting logarithm
\ln(\lambda_{D}/b_{\min})
is the dynamic analogue of the static Coulomb logarithm
\ln(\kappa L_s)
that appears in the localization length of Part I. This establishes a direct connection between the decoherence mechanism analysed here and the classical problem of Coulomb scattering in a plasma.

IV
Numerical estimates and discussion

To illustrate the physical consequences, we estimate
\rho_{c}
for an aqueous electrolyte. Taking
T=300\;\mathrm{K}
,
\varepsilon=80
, and a 1:1 electrolyte of concentration
0.1\;\mathrm{M}
, the Debye length is
\lambda_{D}\approx 1.4\;\mathrm{nm}
. For an electron with kinetic energy
100\;\mathrm{keV}
(typical for TEM),
k\approx 5\times 10^{9}\;\mathrm{cm}^{-1}
, and the static localization length from Eq. (
17
) is
\ell\approx 0.08\;\mathrm{cm}
. With a sample thickness
L\sim 10^{-5}\;\mathrm{cm}
(
100\;\mathrm{nm}
), Eq. (
20
) gives
\rho_{c}\sim 120\;\mathrm{nm}
. Thus the coherence length is limited to about
120\;\mathrm{nm}
. At
1\;\mathrm{keV}
,
\ell\approx 0.08\;\mathrm{cm}/100=8\;\mu\mathrm{m}
, leading to
\rho_{c}\sim 11\;\mathrm{nm}
. At
100\;\mathrm{eV}
,
\ell\approx 0.8\;\mu\mathrm{m}
and
\rho_{c}\sim 1.2\;\mathrm{nm}
.
For a
1\;\mathrm{M}
solution the Debye length is ten times smaller, improving the resolution to
\sim 110\;\mathrm{nm}
at
100\;\mathrm{keV}
and
\sim 1.1\;\mathrm{nm}
at
1\;\mathrm{keV}
.

These estimates suggest that thermal ionic disorder may provide a non-negligible contribution to coherence degradation in liquid-cell electron microscopy. The predicted coherence scale should be interpreted cautiously and not as a direct experimental resolution bound. Rather, it characterizes the intrinsic decay of phase correlations within the present weak-scattering model and may contribute to the effective damping of high-spatial-frequency contrast in cryo-EM imaging of hydrated and vitrified samples. Within the present weak-scattering semiclassical description the characteristic coherence scale
\rho_{c}
increases with electron energy, implying slower spatial decay of phase correlations and weaker attenuation of high-spatial-frequency contrast for high-energy electrons. At lower electron energies the accumulated random eikonal phase becomes stronger, leading to enhanced coherence degradation and smaller values of
\rho_{c}
. Nevertheless, low-energy operation may remain advantageous in practice because of reduced radiation damage, increased interaction cross sections, and enhanced sensitivity to dynamic screening effects beyond the static regime considered here. The dynamic regime (
v\ll v_{\rm th}
) is practically inaccessible for electrons in aqueous solutions because
v_{\rm th}^{\mathrm{(ion)}}\sim 200\;\mathrm{m/s}
, while even
100\;\mathrm{eV}
electrons travel at
\sim 6\times 10^{6}\;\mathrm{m/s}
. However, for heavy test particles (e.g. muons) the dynamic regime could be explored.

It is instructive to analyse the temperature dependence of the characteristic coherence scale

\rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\ell/L}
. Within the static Debye model the screening length scales as
\lambda_{D}\propto\sqrt{T}
, whereas in the weak-disorder regime the localization length behaves as
\ell\propto\frac{1}{T}
for fixed electron momentum. As a consequence,
\rho_{c}\sim\mathrm{const},
so that the characteristic coherence-decay scale depends only weakly on temperature in the simplest static approximation. The increase of the localization length at low temperatures is
therefore largely compensated by the simultaneous reduction of the Debye screening length.

The situation changes qualitatively when the dynamics of the ionic medium is taken into
account. As shown in Part II, the effective disorder strength in the dynamic regime is controlled
by temporal decorrelation of the fluctuating ionic atmosphere and depends explicitly on the
thermal velocity of the ions,
G_{\mathrm{dyn}}\propto\frac{v}{v_{\mathrm{th}}}
, where
v_{\mathrm{th}}\propto\sqrt{T}
.

At low temperatures the ionic motion slows down, the fluctuating potential becomes more
quasi-static, and temporal averaging becomes less effective. Consequently, frozen or vitrified
electrolytes are expected to exhibit stronger static phase disorder and enhanced longer-lived disorder configurations compared to room-temperature liquids.

This observation is particularly relevant for cryogenic electron microscopy, where biological
samples are routinely embedded in vitrified aqueous environments. In such systems the dominant
temperature dependence is expected to originate not from static Debye screening itself but
from the crossover between dynamically fluctuating and effectively frozen disorder. The resulting
coherence degradation may therefore acquire a pronounced glass-like character associated with
slow ionic and orientational relaxation processes.

The above scaling estimates neglect the temperature dependence of the dielectric
permittivity. In polar liquids such as water the static dielectric constant increases
substantially upon cooling due to enhanced orientational polarization. Since the
Debye screening length behaves as

\lambda_{D}\propto\sqrt{\varepsilon(T)\,T},

while the localization length in the weak-disorder regime scales approximately as

\ell\propto\varepsilon(T)/T,

the resulting coherence scale becomes

\rho_{c}\propto{\varepsilon(T)}.

Therefore, the dielectric response of the medium may dominate the temperature
dependence of the coherence properties. Therefore, the image quality may be potentially affected by the addition of co-solvents, which can change the static dielectric constant of the buffer.

We also note that the present theory is formulated within the non‑relativistic quantum mechanics. In actual transmission electron microscopes, the beam energies are typically
100
–
300\;\mathrm{keV}
, at which the electron speed becomes comparable to the speed of light. Relativistic corrections are known to modify the cross‑sections of elastic and inelastic scattering
5
, and they may also affect the effective disorder correlator and the localization length derived here. A fully relativistic generalization of the Efimov path‑integral framework is beyond the scope of the present work, but the qualitative prediction
\rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\ell/L}
is expected to persist, with the parameters entering
\ell
being renormalized by relativistic kinematics. A quantitative comparison with experiment will therefore require a relativistic extension of the theory.

The derivation of Eq. (
1
) rests on the eikonal (straight-line) approximation, which is justified when the electron wavelength is small compared with the correlation length of the disorder and when the propagation length
L
is shorter than the mean free path for large-angle scattering. For the parameters used above, the de Broglie wavelength at
100\;\mathrm{eV}
is
\lambda_{\mathrm{dB}}\approx 0.12\;\mathrm{nm}
, still smaller than
\lambda_{D}=1.4\;\mathrm{nm}
. However, at such low energies multiple elastic scattering in the sample becomes significant and the simple straight-line picture may overestimate
\rho_{c}
. A rigorous treatment of multiple scattering would require going beyond the eikonal approximation, e.g. by solving a Bethe–Salpeter equation for the Cooperon-like propagator in a medium with a finite scattering mean free path
25
. Nevertheless, the scaling
\rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\ell/L}
is expected to be robust as long as the dominant phase decorrelation comes from the long-range part of the potential correlator, which is sampled along quasi-rectilinear trajectories.

It is important to note that, in the beams of modern transmission microscopes, the electrons are relativistic. Therefore, in theory, it is necessary to consider relativistic corrections. Fortunately, considering relativistic effects, as shown in Appendix
A
, does not contradict the scaling law
\rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}(\ell/L)^{1/2}
established in the context of non-relativistic quantum mechanics.

An important extension of the present framework is its application to arbitrary liquid, glassy, and biologically relevant media characterized by a wave-vector-dependent dielectric response
\varepsilon(k)
obtained within nonlocal electrostatics or statistical field theory approaches
6
;
9
.
For an arbitrary liquid the static correlation function of the fluctuating potential is given directly by the fluctuation–dissipation theorem:

\widetilde{K}(k)=\frac{4\pi q_{0}^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{k^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k)}-1\right).

(35)

Equation (
35
) shows that the effective disorder strength, which enters the localization length through the integral
\int d^{3}k\,\widetilde{K}(k)
, is controlled by the combination
[1/\varepsilon(k)-1]
.
In an electrolyte the ionic screening modifies this expression, but the same nonlocal function
\varepsilon(k)
remains the central input.
Physically,
\varepsilon(k)
in structured polar liquids may exhibit pronounced oscillations, extrema, and even negative regions at wave-vectors comparable to intermolecular scales (
k\sim 1
–
2\,\text{\AA}^{-1}
in water), reflecting overscreening and the collective organization of the hydrogen-bond network.
Inserting such a
\varepsilon(k)
into our framework makes the potential correlator
K(r)
oscillate on the scale of the molecular diameter, which in turn produces a non‑monotonic decay of the mutual coherence
\gamma(\rho)
.
A partial recovery of coherence is therefore expected at distances that match successive solvation layers around the ions.
Work along these lines, including a detailed numerical analysis of the phase structure function with the realistic dielectric response of water and aqueous electrolytes, is in progress and will be reported elsewhere.

V
Conclusion

We have demonstrated that the mutual coherence function of electrons in a Coulomb-disordered medium decays with a characteristic transverse length
\rho_{c}
that is uniquely determined by the single-particle localization length
\ell
. The relation
\rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\ell/L}
is universal, holding for both static and dynamic disorder, and establishes a formal connection between localization phenomena and transverse coherence decay in Coulomb-disordered media. Our analytical results for the phase structure function of a model electrolyte, combined with the exact calculation using the modified Bessel function, provide a general theoretical framework for analysing disorder-induced coherence degradation, phase decorrelation, and contrast attenuation in liquids, amorphous materials, and biological environments. Within the present model, the characteristic coherence scale increases with electron energy (since
\rho_{c}\propto\sqrt{E}
in the static regime), so higher-energy electrons better preserve high-spatial-frequency contrast. Lowering the electron energy leads to stronger phase decorrelation and smaller
\rho_{c}
, i.e. enhanced coherence degradation. Nevertheless, low-energy operation may still be advantageous in practice because of reduced radiation damage and increased interaction cross sections, and it could also become sensitive to dynamic screening effects beyond the static regime considered here. A detailed quantitative comparison with experimental contrast-transfer data for specific buffers (e.g. PBS, Tris) would further strengthen the theory and is left for a future publication.

Appendix A
Relativistic paraxial reduction and coherence theory

In a transmission electron microscope the kinetic energy of the beam electrons is typically

100
–
300\,
keV, corresponding to velocities

v\sim 0.5c
–
0.8c
.
A relativistic treatment is therefore required for a quantitatively consistent description of disorder-induced decoherence.
In this Appendix we derive the effective paraxial equation starting from the Dirac equation,
show explicitly how the spin degrees of freedom decouple in the leading approximation,
and obtain the corresponding coherence and localisation lengths.

A.1
Dirac equation in an electrostatic potential

We consider a static scalar potential
W(\mathbf{r})
.
The Dirac equation reads
4

i\hbar\partial_{t}\Psi=\left[c\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}+\beta mc^{2}+W(\mathbf{r})\right]\Psi,

(36)

where

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\gamma^{0}\boldsymbol{\gamma},\qquad\beta=\gamma^{0}.

For a stationary state of total energy
\mathcal{E}
,

\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)=e^{-i\mathcal{E}t/\hbar}\psi(\mathbf{r}),

which gives

\left[c\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}+\beta mc^{2}+W-\mathcal{E}\right]\psi=0.

(37)

Using the standard representation

\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi\\
\eta\end{pmatrix},

Eq. (
37
) becomes

\displaystyle(\mathcal{E}-mc^{2}-W)\varphi

\displaystyle=c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{p}}\,\eta,

(38)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{E}+mc^{2}-W)\eta

\displaystyle=c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{p}}\,\varphi.

(39)

A.2
Elimination of the spinor structure

For weak electrostatic disorder,

|W|\ll\mathcal{E}+mc^{2},

the lower component can be eliminated perturbatively from
Eq. (
39
):

\eta\simeq\frac{c}{\mathcal{E}+mc^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{p}}\,\varphi.

(40)

Substituting Eq. (
40
) into Eq. (
38
) yields

(\mathcal{E}-mc^{2}-W)\varphi=\frac{c^{2}}{\mathcal{E}+mc^{2}}\,(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{p}})^{2}\varphi.

(41)

Using the Pauli identity

(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf{p}})^{2}=\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\,2},

valid in the absence of magnetic fields,
the explicit spin dependence disappears:

(\mathcal{E}-mc^{2}-W)\varphi=\frac{c^{2}}{\mathcal{E}+mc^{2}}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\,2}\varphi.

(42)

Thus, in the leading paraxial approximation,
the Dirac equation reduces to an effectively scalar equation.
Note that spin-orbit and Darwin terms may arise only at higher order in gradients of
W

and are neglected here.

A.3
Paraxial reduction

We assume propagation predominantly along the
z
axis and separate the fast longitudinal phase:

\varphi(\mathbf{r})=e^{ikz}\phi(\mathbf{r}),\qquad k=\frac{p}{\hbar},

(43)

where

p=\gamma mv,\qquad\mathcal{E}=\gamma mc^{2}.

Acting on the envelope,

\hat{p}_{z}=-i\hbar\partial_{z}\;\to\;\hbar k-i\hbar\partial_{z}.

Using

\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\,2}=\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\perp}^{\,2}+\hat{p}_{z}^{\,2},

we obtain

\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\,2}(e^{ikz}\phi)=e^{ikz}\left[\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\perp}^{\,2}+(\hbar k-i\hbar\partial_{z})^{2}\right]\phi.

Expanding the square,

(\hbar k-i\hbar\partial_{z})^{2}=\hbar^{2}k^{2}-2i\hbar^{2}k\partial_{z}-\hbar^{2}\partial_{z}^{2}.

In the paraxial regime the envelope varies slowly along
z
,
so that

|\partial_{z}^{2}\phi|\ll k|\partial_{z}\phi|.

Neglecting
\partial_{z}^{2}\phi
and using

p^{2}c^{2}=\mathcal{E}^{2}-m^{2}c^{4},

Eq. (
42
) becomes

2i\hbar^{2}k\,\partial_{z}\phi=-\hbar^{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\phi+\frac{\mathcal{E}+mc^{2}}{c^{2}}W\phi.

(44)

Dividing by
2\hbar^{2}k
and using

k=\frac{\gamma mv}{\hbar},\qquad\mathcal{E}=\gamma mc^{2},

we obtain

i\hbar v\,\partial_{z}\phi=\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\gamma m}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}+\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\,W(\mathbf{r})\right]\phi.

(45)

Equation (
45
) is the relativistic paraxial Schrödinger equation
16
;
17
.
The effective transverse mass is therefore
m_{\perp}=\gamma m

For ultra-relativistic energies

\gamma\gg 1
,

\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\simeq\frac{1}{2},

while in the non-relativistic limit
\gamma\to 1

the coefficient tends to unity.
For typical TEM energies (
\gamma\simeq 1.2
–
1.6
),
the factor differs from unity by less than
\sim 15\%
,
and is often absorbed into a redefinition of the effective potential.

A.4
Path integral and accumulated phase

Dividing Eq. (
45
) by
v
gives

i\hbar\partial_{z}\phi=\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\gamma mv}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}+\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma v}W\right]\phi.

(46)

The corresponding effective Lagrangian is

L_{\rm eff}=\frac{\gamma mv}{2}\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\perp}^{\,2}-\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma v}W,

(47)

where the dot denotes differentiation with respect to
z
.

The envelope propagator therefore admits the path-integral representation

G_{\perp}=\int\mathcal{D}\mathbf{r}_{\perp}(\zeta)\,\exp\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{z}d\zeta\,L_{\rm eff}\right].

(48)

In the straight-line eikonal approximation,
the accumulated random phase is

\Delta\phi=-\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma\hbar v}\int_{0}^{L}dz\,W.

(49)

We therefore identify the effective relativistic coupling

A_{\rm rel}=\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma\hbar v}.

(50)

In the non-relativistic limit,

A_{\rm rel}\to\frac{1}{\hbar v},

reproducing the standard eikonal result.

In the ultra-relativistic limit,

A_{\rm rel}\to\frac{1}{2\hbar c},

demonstrating saturating coupling constant.

A.5
Mutual coherence and localisation length

For Gaussian statistically homogeneous disorder,
the second-cumulant approximation gives

\gamma(\rho)=\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}D_{\phi}(\rho)\right],

(51)

with

D_{\phi}(\rho)=4A_{\rm rel}^{2}L\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\left[K(u)-K\!\left(\sqrt{u^{2}+\rho^{2}}\right)\right].

(52)

The corresponding localisation length becomes

\ell_{\rm rel}=\frac{1}{A_{\rm rel}^{2}\,C\,\ln(\kappa L_s)},

(53)

where

C=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

Substituting Eq. (
50
),

\ell_{\rm rel}=\frac{4\gamma^{2}\hbar^{2}v^{2}}{(\gamma+1)^{2}\,C\,\ln(\kappa L_s)}.

(54)

In the non-relativistic limit

\gamma\to 1

this reduces to

\ell=\frac{\hbar^{2}v^{2}}{C\ln(\kappa L_s)},

which coincides with the original non-relativistic theory. In the ultra-relativistic limit we have saturated value

\ell=\frac{4\hbar^{2}c^{2}}{C\ln(\kappa L_s)},

The coherence length retains the universal form

\rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{\rm rel}}{L}}.

(55)

Thus relativity modifies the effective localisation length,
but does not alter the fundamental scaling structure of the coherence theory.

A.6
Summary

The relativistic reduction of the Dirac equation shows that:

•

the spinor structure decouples in the leading paraxial approximation,
leading to an effective scalar Schrödinger equation;

•

the transverse kinetic term is governed by the relativistic mass

\gamma m
;

•

the disorder coupling entering the accumulated phase becomes

A_{\rm rel}=\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma\hbar v};

•

the localisation and coherence lengths preserve the universal structure

\rho_{c}\sim\lambda_{D}\sqrt{\ell/L}.

Therefore the entire coherence formalism developed in the non-relativistic theory extends naturally to relativistic electron beams used in transmission electron microscopy.
