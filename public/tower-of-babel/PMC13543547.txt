Correlation between viral load and cervical squamous lesion severity in single and multiple infections: a large-scale analysis
Authors: Ting Xu, Jing Zhao, Jiaqi Wu, Yanyan Wang, Xiaoli Liu, Baokun Zhou, Yan Huang, Xiaotong Guo, Yunde Dou, Yunyun Gong, Jianli Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study examined the association between high risk human papillomavirus viral load and cervical squamous lesions using the Cobas 4800 assay and evaluated its potential as an indicator for identifying CIN2+, and analyzed the impact of multiple infections on viral load and disease risk.MethodsA total of 3,838 women who underwent hr-HPV testing were included. The Cobas 4800 assay provided cycle threshold values, which were used as indirect measures of viral load. These values were correlated with histopathological diagnoses obtained within three months of HPV testing.ResultsAmong all participants, 1,660 had normal histology, 900 had CIN1, 1,019 had CIN2/3, and 259 had SCC. HPV16 Ct values decreased with increasing lesion severity in both single and multiple infections, whereas HPV18 and the 12 other hr-HPV types showed no clear correlation with lesion grade. Multiple HPV infections involving HPV16 were not associated with an increased risk of CIN2 + compared with HPV16 single infection. For both HPV16 and HPV18, Ct values did not differ significantly across CIN1, CIN2/3, and SCC between single and multiple infections. ROC curve analysis of HPV16 single infections identified a Ct threshold of 32.25 for identifying CIN2+, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.72, a sensitivity of 80.7%, and a specificity of 54.4%.ConclusionHPV16 viral load is correlated with cervical squamous lesions, irrespective of single or multiple infections. HPV16 viral load may serve as a biomarker for indicating cervical squamous lesion occurrence. In this study, no positive association was observed between co-infection and high-grade lesions, and viral loads did not differ significantly between single and multiple infection groups. Integrating viral load assessment into clinical evaluation could thus enable more individualized risk stratification and patient management.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Cervical cancer, a malignant tumor originating in the cervical epithelium, is the fourth most prevalent cancer among women globally [1]. In China, its incidence has been gradually increasing in recent years, with a notable trend toward an earlier age of onset, thereby imposing a substantial public health burden [2]. Persistent infection with human papillomavirus (HPV) is widely recognized as the primary cause of cervical cancer worldwide. Of the more than 200 identified HPV types, more than 30 are associated with infections of the genital tract, and 14 high-risk HPV (hr‐HPV) types have been strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of cervical cancer. HPV16 and HPV18 together account for nearly 70% of cervical squamous cell carcinoma cases [3]. The correlation between HPV viral load and cervical lesions remains a significant yet unresolved issue in current research. Although several studies have proposed that HPV viral load could serve as a potential risk indicator for the progression of cervical lesions [4, 5], other studies have found no such association [6]. Notably, the clinical detection rate of multiple high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infections has been increasing, emerging as a more prominent feature in the epidemiological profile of HPV. However, studies on the relationship between multiple infection and HPV viral load are relatively limited. This study investigated the correlation between hr-HPV viral load and cervical squamous lesions and evaluated the potential of the hr‐HPV viral load as an indicator for distinguishing cervical squamous epithelial lesions. Additionally, we examined the impact of multiple infections on viral load and disease risk. To ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results, hr‐HPV testing was conducted using the Cobas 4800 HPV assay, a real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) based method approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). HPV viral load was estimated by determining the type‐specific cycle threshold (Ct) values.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study initially included 86,044 patients who underwent Cobas 4800 HPV testing at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University between January 2018 and August 2022. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia or cervical cancer; (2) prior cervical physiotherapy, surgery, or pelvic radiotherapy; (3) history of hysterectomy. Only patients with a histopathological diagnosis within three months of HPV testing were included. Ultimately, 3,838 eligible patients (mean age 41.64 years, range 16-83 years) were enrolled. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University (Fig. 1).

Sample collection

To ensure sample quality, all participants were required to strictly adhere to the following preparation guidelines: (1) no vaginal douching, use of topical medications (such as vaginal suppositories), or physical therapy within 72 h prior to sampling; (2) avoidance of sexual intercourse within 24 h before sampling; and (3) scheduling sample collection outside the menstrual period. Trained gynecologists collected the samples using a specialized sampler and strict aseptic techniques. Sample preservation and laboratory analyses were performed following the standardized procedures outlined by the manufacturer of the Cobas 4800 HPV testing system (Roche Diagnostics). The test results were analyzed and interpreted by certified laboratory technicians.

HPV testing methods

The Cobas 4800 HPV Test is an FDA-approved diagnostic platform designed to detect hr‐HPV. This test combines real-time fluorescence-based quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) with nucleic acid hybridization to identify 14 hr‐HPV types. Specifically, HPV16 and HPV18 are detected individually, while the remaining 12 hr‐HPV types (HPV31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68) are identified collectively as a pooled group (OT). The assay also includes β‐globin as an internal control to assess sample adequacy and detect the presence of PCR inhibitors, thereby enhancing the accuracy and reliability of the results. Samples with invalid assay results were managed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and retested where appropriate, and only valid final results were included in the present analysis. To minimize inter-run variability, all HPV tests were performed in the same laboratory using the Roche cobas 4800 HPV Test in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The testing procedure is fully automated and consists of three primary steps. First, automated nucleic acid extraction is performed to simultaneously isolate both human cellular DNA and HPV viral DNA from the specimen. Second, multiplex PCR amplification is carried out using type‐specific primers targeting the β‐globin gene and the HPV genome. Third, fluorescence detection is achieved through a four‐channel fluorescence system, in which HPV16, HPV18, and β‐globin are labeled with FAM, HEX, and ROX dyes, respectively, while OT is labeled with CY5. Genotype determination was based on the manufacturer-defined channel-specific clinical cutoffs implemented by the cobas 4800 system rather than manual interpretation of fluorescence dye signals. Specifically, the cutoff values were Ct 40.5 for the HPV16 channel, Ct 40.0 for the HPV18 channel, and Ct 40.0 for the pooled 12 other hr‐HPV channel. β‐globin served as an internal control for specimen adequacy and PCR inhibition. The system continuously tracks the Ct value, which is defined as the number of amplification cycles required for the fluorescent signal to exceed a predetermined threshold. There is an inverse linear relationship between the Ct value and the logarithmic concentration of initial hr‐HPV DNA copies, with lower Ct values indicating higher viral loads.

Histopathology diagnosis

All patients included in this study underwent cervical histopathology diagnosis evaluation within three months following Cobas 4800 HPV testing. The mean interval between HPV testing and histological diagnosis was 13.03 days, with 89.42% of the participants receiving histopathological assessment within one month. Histopathological diagnosis was determined by consensus between two senior gynecologic pathologists based on cervical biopsy specimens, loop electrosurgical excision procedures (LEEP), cold knife conization (CKC), or hysterectomy samples. If a participant had two or more histopathological diagnoses, the most severe diagnosis was used for analysis. According to the WHO Classification of Tumors of the Female Reproductive System, the histopathological results were stratified into four distinct groups: (1) normal, (2) cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 1 (CIN1), (3) cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 (CIN2) or grade 3 (CIN3), and (4) squamous cell carcinoma (SCC).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0, and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The chi-square (χ²) test was used to compare the positive rates of high-risk human papillomavirus (hr-HPV) among groups with different cervical lesion grades. Because Ct values are exponentially related to viral DNA copy numbers, non-parametric methods were used throughout for all analyses involving Ct values, regardless of the apparent distribution of Ct values. Differences in hr-HPV Ct values across cervical lesions of varying severity were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons where applicable. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was applied to assess the association between hr-HPV Ct values and cervical lesion severity. In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic performance of hr-HPV Ct values for predicting cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or worse (CIN2+).

Result

Distribution of hr-HPV across different grades of cervical lesions

Among the 3,838 participants included in this study, 1,660 (43.3%) had normal cervical histology, 900 (23.5%) were diagnosed with CIN1, 1,019 (26.6%) with CIN2/3, and 259 (6.8%) with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Among the enrolled participants, the overall hr-HPV positive rate was 78.3%, with rates in CIN1 (87.2%), CIN2/3 (94.0%), and SCC (93.1%) all significantly higher than the rate of 61.4% in the normal group (all had P < 0.001; see Table 1). The positive rate for HPV16 increased with the lesion severity increase, from 16.8% in the normal group to 23.8% in CIN1, 53.2% in CIN2/3, and 75.7% in SCC (PNormal-CIN1=0.01, all other comparisons versus the normal group, P < 0.001). Conversely, the positive rate for OT peaked at 56.1% in CIN1 but declined in more advanced lesions (38.3% in the normal group, 36.1% in CIN2/3, and 14.3% in SCC). Notably, the positive rate for OT was significantly lower in the SCC group compared to the other groups (P < 0.001). The positive rate of HPV18 was consistently low across all lesion categories. Specifically, the CIN2/3 group exhibited a significantly lower positive rate than the normal and CIN1 groups (PNormal‐CIN2/3=0.001, PCIN1‐CIN2/3=0.015), whereas no significant differences were found among the remaining groups.

Association of HPV viral load (Ct Value) with the severity of cervical lesions

Among the 3,838 study participants, 858 (22.4%) were identified with single infection of HPV16. Significant differences in HPV16 Ct values were observed among the normal, CIN1, CIN2/3, and SCC groups. As the severity of cervical squamous lesions increased, HPV16 Ct values decreased, showing a significant negative correlation with lesion grade (ρ=-0.386, P < 0.001). However, no statistically significant difference was found between the CIN2/3 and SCC groups (P = 0.157) (Fig. 2A). Among the 152 patients (4.0%) with HPV18 single infection, no significant differences were observed across the different cervical lesion grades (P = 0.746) (Fig. 2B). Among the 1,545 patients (40.3%) positive for OT, the Ct values showed a weak negative correlation with lesion severity (ρ=-0.134, P < 0.001). Specifically, the Ct values in both the CIN1 and CIN2/3 groups were significantly lower than those in the normal group (PNormal-CIN1<0.001, PNormal‐CIN2/3<0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed in the remaining group comparisons (PNormal‐SCC=0.920, PCIN1‐CIN2/3>0.99, PCIN1‐SCC>0.99, PCIN2/3‐SCC>0.99) (Fig. 2C). In cases of multiple infections involving HPV16 and other HPV types, HPV16 Ct values showed a significant negative correlation with the severity of cervical squamous lesions (ρ=-0.416, P < 0.001, PNormal‐CIN1<0.001, PNormal‐CIN2/3<0.001, PNormal‐SCC<0.001, PCIN1‐CIN2/3=0.249, PCIN1‐SCC=0.003, PCIN2/3‐SCC=0.139) (Fig. 2D). In contrast, HPV18 co-infection with other HPV types showed no significant differences in Ct values across lesion grades, similar to HPV18 single infection (P = 0.235) (Fig. 2E). Overall, HPV16 Ct values exhibited an inverse correlation with the severity of cervical lesions. To further evaluate whether the HPV16 Ct value could be utilized as a supplementary marker for diagnosing CIN2 and SCC combined group (CIN2+), and for risk stratification among women with HPV16 infection, we performed ROC curve analysis based on the Ct values in cases of HPV16 single infection (Fig. 3). The analysis identified an optimal Ct threshold of 32.25 for identifying CIN2+, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.72 (0.68‐0.76, 95% confidence interval). At this cutoff value, the sensitivity and specificity were 80.7% and 54.4%, respectively, indicating that the HPV16 Ct value may serve as a useful adjunctive indicator for predicting cervical lesion progression.

Association of HPV16/18 multiple infections with CIN2 + risk and viral loads

In the single-factor analysis, HPV16 single infection exhibited the highest risk of CIN2+ (62.7%). When HPV16 co-occurred with HPV18, the proportion of CIN2 + was comparable to that observed in HPV16 single infection (62.5%, P = 0.987). In contrast, multiple infections involving HPV16 and OT, as well as infections with HPV16, HPV18 and OT, were associated with a significantly lower proportion of CIN2 + compared with HPV16 single infection (all P < 0.05, Table 2). HPV18 single infection showed a lower risk of CIN2+ (20.4%); however, when co-infected with HPV16 or other hr‐HPV types, the proportion of CIN2 + increased compared with HPV18 single infection (P < 0.05).

Correlation between hr-HPV Ct values and age across different grades of cervical lesions

The Ct values were compared among HPV-positive patients across different age categories. The participants were categorized into four age brackets: under 30, 30‐39, 40‐49, and 50 years or older. Among individuals positive for HPV16, a significant difference in Ct values was observed within the CIN2/3 group. Specifically, the Ct value in the ≥ 50 years age group was significantly lower compared to the < 30 years group (P = 0.003) and the 30‐39 years group (P = 0.01). However, no statistically significant differences in HPV16 Ct values were observed across the age groups within the normal, CIN1, and SCC categories (all P > 0.05). For patients positive for OT, the Ct values did not show statistically significant differences among age groups at any cervical lesion level (all P > 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

Persistent hr-HPV infection is the principal cause of cervical cancer, among which HPV16 and HPV18 are considered the most oncogenic genotypes [3, 7]. In the present study, the positive rate of hr-HPV increased with lesion severity, and HPV16 showed the strongest association with high-grade cervical lesions. In contrast, HPV18 positivity remained relatively low across squamous lesion categories, which may be related to its stronger association with glandular lesions and adenocarcinoma [8].

Earlier HC2-based studies generally suggested that higher hr-HPV viral loads were associated with increased risk of high-grade lesions [9, 10]. However, HC2 lacks genotype-specific detection capability, limiting its clinical applicability. PCR-based assays such as Cobas 4800 have enabled genotype-specific viral load analysis. Zhou et al. found that the viral loads of HPV16, HPV31, HPV33, HPV35, and HPV58 correlated with the grade of cervical lesions, with the strongest correlation observed for HPV16 [11]. Another study reported a positive correlation between HPV16/18 viral load and cervical cytological abnormalities, although histopathological confirmation was unavailable [12]. In our study, Ct values used as surrogate markers of viral load showed a significant association between HPV16 viral load and lesion severity in both single and multiple infections. No similar correlation was observed for HPV18 or OT.

Several explanations may account for this finding. First, biological differences among HPV genotypes may play an important role. HPV16 is strongly associated with squamous lesions and often maintains productive viral replication during lesion progression, resulting in a closer relationship between viral load and lesion severity. In contrast, HPV18 is more frequently associated with glandular lesions and adenocarcinoma, and its carcinogenic progression may rely more on early viral genome integration than sustained episomal replication. As a result, viral load changes may not parallel lesion severity as consistently as observed for HPV16. Second, methodological limitations may also contribute to the weaker association observed for non-HPV16 genotypes. In the Cobas 4800 system, the 12 other hr-HPV types are detected as a pooled group rather than individually. This pooled detection strategy may mask genotype-specific differences in viral load dynamics and reduce analytical sensitivity for individual HPV types. Additionally, Ct values are semi-quantitative indicators that may be affected by specimen cellularity, sampling variability, and amplification efficiency. Therefore, both biological heterogeneity and assay-related limitations may have contributed to the weaker correlations observed for HPV18 and OT in this study. Notably, the Ct values of HPV16 showed no significant difference between CIN2/3 and SCC, which may be related to viral genome integration. As cervical lesions progress to invasive cancer, HPV tends to integrate into the host genome, leading to a reduction in free viral copies and stabilization of viral load [13, 14]. The results of this study demonstrated that when the HPV16 Ct value was below 32.25, the sensitivity for identifying CIN2 + lesions reached 80.7%, and the specificity was 54.4%, indicating a high sensitivity with a moderate level of discriminatory performance. These findings suggest that HPV16 Ct values may provide supplementary value for individualized risk stratification beyond conventional binary HPV testing.

Although increasing attention has been paid to HPV multiple infections, their association with lesion severity and viral load remains inconclusive. Some studies suggested that multiple hr-HPV infections may increase the risk of CIN2/3 progression [15, 16]. Multiple infections may increase lesion risk through synergistic interactions or enhanced viral genome integration [17], whereas others reported that co-infection might attenuate lesion progression or reduce the pathogenicity of HPV16 [18, 19]. In our study, for HPV16, multiple infections do not appear to increase the occurrence of high-grade cervical lesions or worse. This finding suggests that different HPV types may interact in ways that influence disease progression, rather than simply having additive effects. Several mechanisms may contribute to the observed difference in lesion distribution, including superinfection exclusion, inter-genotypic competition or suppression, higher clearance rates of multiple HPV infections, and enhanced immune responses during co-infection [20–22]. However, no significant Ct value differences were observed between single and multiple infections within the same lesion grade, suggesting that the influence of inter-genotypic interactions on viral load was limited. This finding suggests that, in our study, variation in HPV16 Ct values was more closely related to lesion severity than to infection pattern. However, several co-infection subgroups contained relatively limited sample sizes. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant associations should be interpreted cautiously. Larger multicenter studies are needed to further validate these findings. Our findings are consistent with those of a study conducted in China [23], which also showed that HPV16 and HPV18 Ct values did not differ significantly between women with single-type and multiple-type HPV infections. However, given the cross-sectional design of the present study, this finding should be further validated in longitudinal studies with more comprehensive covariate assessment and extended genotyping.

This study further demonstrated a correlation between viral load and age, showing that women aged ≥ 50 years exhibited higher HPV16 viral loads in high-grade cervical lesions. Interestingly, no significant differences in HPV16 Ct values were observed among the different age groups in the normal cervical tissues, CIN1, and SCC. This may be related to age-associated immune decline and reduced T cell-mediated viral clearance, contributing to persistent HPV16 infection and sustained viral replication. Furthermore, postmenopausal decrease in estrogen levels may alter the cervical mucosal microenvironment [24], promoting persistent infection or reactivation. At the SCC stage, increased viral integration and decreased viral replication resulted in non-significant differences in viral load across the age groups.

This study has several limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design, it could not evaluate the temporal dynamics between HPV viral load and the progression of cervical lesions. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether viral load predicts future lesion outcomes. Second, the detection method used was a partial genotyping technique, limiting the ability to analyze specific HPV types among the 12 hr-HPV types other than HPV16 and HPV18. Additionally, Ct values from the cobas 4800 HPV Test are semi-quantitative and may be influenced by sampling adequacy, cellular content, and the assay conditions. Moreover, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., immune status, sexual history, and hormonal status) cannot be ruled out. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of a causal relationship. Our findings should also be interpreted cautiously in HPV-vaccinated populations, as vaccination may alter genotype distribution and reduce the relative contribution of HPV16/18-associated lesions. Consequently, the Ct-based associations observed in this study, particularly for HPV16, may differ in vaccinated cohorts. In addition, because the 12 other hr-HPV types were analyzed as a pooled group, further studies incorporating vaccination status and extended genotyping are warranted. It should be noted that our study is observational and does not provide direct molecular evidence for HPV multiple infections. Further experimental studies are warranted to clarify the interactions between HPV types and their impact on cervical carcinogenesis.

In summary, this study explored the relationship between Ct values, which serve as an indicator of HPV viral load, and cervical lesions, revealing that the association is dependent on the specific HPV genotype. Moreover, with respect to HPV16 and HPV18, our findings indicate that while multiple infections may affect the development of cervical lesions, they do not appear to alter the viral load of either HPV16 or HPV18. Type-specific HPV viral load may serve as a supplementary indicator for evaluating the severity of cervical lesions in patients with hr-HPV infection.
