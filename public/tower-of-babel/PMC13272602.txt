Urological telesurgery after the first randomised trial: a scoping review and public implementation visibility audit
Authors: Rafał Bogdan Drobot, Jędrzej Jakub Skorupka, Artur Andrzej Antoniewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrological telesurgery has moved from isolated demonstrations to multicentre and randomised evaluation. We mapped the public evidence after the first randomised trial and assessed whether implementation-critical details are visible in reports that readers can independently appraise. We performed a PRISMA-ScR evidence map and public-record audit of peer-reviewed human urological telesurgery reports available to 26 April 2026. Eligible reports described direct remote operative control across physically separate sites. Because brief abstracts and public summaries cannot reliably accommodate full implementation detail, full-text or publicly available full-text records were prespecified as the primary visibility analysis and short public records were analysed separately. Two reviewers independently coded IDEAL stage and predefined implementation-visibility items, with third-reviewer adjudication. Item-level frequencies were reported; no report-level score, total or ranking was calculated. Sixteen reports were included: eight full-text or public full-text records and eight short public records. Six reports were stage 1, seven stage 2a, two stage 2b and one stage 3; no stage 4 surveillance report was identified. In the primary full-text/public full-text analysis, network architecture and clinical outcomes were visible in 8/8 records; distance or site separation and quantitative latency in 7/8 each; advanced network metrics, backup or failover, on-site rescue or takeover planning and technical adverse events in 6/8 each; ethics or governance in 5/8; registration or protocol identifiers in 2/8; and cybersecurity or data-protection measures in 1/8. Short public records showed lower visibility for several implementation items, particularly backup or failover (1/8), governance (2/8), registration (1/8) and cybersecurity (0/8). Agreement was 168/176 (95.5%; Cohen kappa 0.91) for binary visibility items and 15/16 (93.8%; linearly weighted kappa 0.93) for IDEAL stage; nine extraction calls were adjudicated. A programme-cluster sensitivity analysis collapsed the 16 reports into seven visible programmes and retained the same main pattern: network architecture and clinical outcomes were visible in all clusters, whereas registration and cybersecurity were visible in one cluster each. Urological telesurgery has entered early comparative evaluation, including one randomised trial, but public implementation reporting remains uneven. The clearest residual visibility gaps in full-length records concern registration and cybersecurity; short public records are additionally constrained by format. Introduction

Urological telesurgery has changed materially since the first isolated human reports. The field now includes a multicentre randomised trial comparing urological telesurgery with local robotic surgery in radical prostatectomy and partial nephrectomy, with 72 randomised participants, four remote pathways of 1000–2800 km and reported non-inferiority for surgical success [1]. A public Dryad dataset and statistical-code deposit for that trial further increased the auditability of the field [2].

This shift creates a new question. The key issue is no longer only whether a remote urological operation can be completed. The question is whether the public record contains enough implementation detail for readers, hospitals, reviewers and regulators to judge reproducibility, resilience and safe transfer. This is especially important in urology, where robotic platforms, oncological pathways and regional access gaps make telesurgery clinically attractive.

A closely related systematic review in prostate cancer was published in 2026 and searched MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science to June 2025 [3]. That review focused on prostate cancer applications and identified six studies involving seven patients. The present review is intentionally different. It is a urology-wide public-record evidence map after the first randomised trial, includes benign and malignant urological indications, includes the 2026 randomised evidence and public dataset, and treats implementation visibility as the primary object of study rather than clinical efficacy.

Recent broader reviews of robotic urology also support the need to separate platform diffusion from implementation transparency. A Nature Reviews Urology review described the expanding single-port urological landscape across prostate, kidney and reconstructive procedures [4], whereas a review of robotic-assisted surgery for urological cancers highlighted single-port surgery, emerging robotic platforms, 3D models, artificial intelligence and the persistent barriers of cost, training and technological integration [5]. These reviews contextualise rapid robotic innovation, but they do not answer whether human urological telesurgery reports make network, rescue, governance, registration and cybersecurity details publicly visible.

Three methodological choices follow from the previous peer review of this topic. First, the design is reported as a scoping review and evidence map according to PRISMA-ScR [6], with the PRISMA 2020 flow structure used for study selection [7]. Second, the previous 11-item summary score has been removed. The revision reports item-level frequencies only and does not calculate report-level totals, medians or rankings. Third, duplicate screening and extraction verification have been finalised with inter-rater agreement statistics and a third-reviewer adjudication log provided as Online Resource 3.

We aimed to map urological human clinical telesurgery reports to operational IDEAL stages, quantify the visibility of implementation-critical public reporting items in full-text and short public records separately, and propose a concise minimum public reporting set for future urological telesurgery studies.

Methods

Design and reporting framework

This study was designed as a PRISMA-ScR scoping evidence map with a public-record implementation-visibility audit [6, 7]. The unit of analysis was the report, not the patient and not the institution. This choice was prespecified because a single telesurgery programme can generate a first case, a development series, a comparative cohort and a later trial. Operational maturity was classified with a conservative modified IDEAL approach informed by the original IDEAL recommendations, later IDEAL refinements and the robotics-specific IDEAL interpretation [8–10].

The study used only published public material. We did not contact authors for non-public protocols, private network logs or internal governance documents. The aim was to assess what an independent reader could see in the public record.

Search strategy and eligibility

The updated search was completed on 26 April 2026. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, PubMed Central where accessible, Europe PMC, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect public article pages, Nature Portfolio public article pages, public DOI records, Dryad and citation chains from key urological telesurgery papers. Search strings combined telesurgery, remote surgery, remote robotic surgery, telerobotic surgery, 5G, latency, packet loss, frame loss, robotic surgery and urological procedure terms. The full search strategy and PRISMA counts are provided in Online Resource 1.

Eligible records were peer-reviewed public reports of human urological procedures in which an operating surgeon directly controlled a remote robotic or telesurgical system while physically separated from the patient-side operating site. Eligible reports included case reports, case series, clinical experience reports, cohort studies, comparative cohorts and randomised trials. We excluded animal-only, cadaveric-only, dry-lab-only and simulator-only studies; telementoring or teleproctoring without direct operative control; narrative reviews; editorials; news reports; non-urological clinical telesurgery reports; and records without an analyzable public abstract, full text or public summary.

Screening, charting and adjudication

Two reviewers independently completed separate verification passes using the same piloted form. The reviewers coded inclusion status, operational IDEAL stage and 11 binary implementation-visibility items. Disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer, and final calls were entered in the extraction matrix. The definitive audit log, including contested calls and adjudication decisions, is provided as Online Resource 3.

Inter-rater reliability was calculated before adjudication. For binary implementation-visibility items, we calculated percent agreement and Cohen kappa. For IDEAL stage, we calculated percent agreement and linearly weighted kappa because adjacent stages are ordinally closer than non-adjacent stages.

Data items

For each included report, we extracted publication year, first author, design, reported sample size, procedure or clinical context, public-record level, operational IDEAL stage, programme cluster used for sensitivity analysis and 11 implementation-visibility items. The items were: distance or site separation, network type or architecture, quantitative latency, at least one advanced network-quality metric, backup or failover pathway, on-site takeover or rescue plan, technical adverse events, clinical outcomes or complications, ethics or governance, registration or protocol identifier, and cybersecurity or data-protection measure.

The item set was selected because consensus and expert papers on telesurgery emphasise network performance, redundancy, local rescue, governance, consent and data security as safety-relevant implementation domains [11–13]. Latency and network stability were retained as distinct domains because experimental and simulator literature suggests that delay and delay stability can affect performance and acceptable control thresholds [14, 15].

Coding rules

Each item was coded as explicitly reported or not explicitly reported in the analyzable public record. Missing visibility was not interpreted as poor clinical conduct. It meant only that the item was not visible to a reader using the public record. The item set was not treated as a validated instrument, and no report-level total, median or heatmap ranking was calculated.

Because abstracts, public summaries and landing pages are structurally constrained by word count and format, full-text or publicly available full-text records were prespecified as the primary implementation-visibility analysis. Short public records were analysed separately to show what is visible in brief public records, not to infer lower implementation maturity.

IDEAL staging was conservative. Stage 1 denoted first-in-human or isolated proof-of-concept use. Stage 2a denoted early development case series or non-comparative clinical development. Stage 2b denoted exploratory comparative or multicentre real-world evaluation. Stage 3 denoted formal randomised or controlled comparative evaluation. Stage 4 denoted registry-based or long-term post-implementation surveillance. When classification was uncertain, the lower stage was assigned.

Programme-cluster sensitivity analysis

To address possible patient or programme overlap, we performed an exploratory sensitivity analysis that collapsed related reports into visible programme clusters using public information about platform, route, operator group, geography and report sequence. When overlap was plausible but not provable, reports were grouped conservatively rather than treated as independent programmes. Cluster-level item visibility was coded as present if at least one report in the cluster made the item explicitly visible.

Statistical analysis

We used counts and percentages. No formal meta-analysis was attempted because procedures, platforms, networks, report formats and outcome definitions were heterogeneous. The primary results are item-level public visibility frequencies in full-text/public full-text records, with short public records analysed separately. Analyses were descriptive.

Results

Study selection

The PRISMA flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. We identified 47 public records from databases, publisher pages, repositories, citation chains and the prior corpus. After removal of 12 duplicate records, 35 records were screened. Twenty-one reports were assessed for eligibility, five were excluded with reasons and 16 urological human telesurgery reports were included.

Six reports were classified as stage 1, seven as stage 2a, two as stage 2b and one as stage 3. No stage 4 surveillance report was identified. The first stage 3 report was the 2026 multicentre randomised trial of urological telesurgery compared with local robotic surgery [1]. All comparative-stage reports appeared in 2025–2026 (Fig. 2).

Inter-rater reliability and adjudication

Before adjudication, agreement for the 11 binary implementation-visibility items was 168/176 item decisions (95.5%; Cohen kappa 0.91). Agreement for operational IDEAL stage was 15/16 reports (93.8%; linearly weighted kappa 0.93). Across binary items and IDEAL staging combined, agreement was 183/192 extraction decisions (95.3%). Nine calls were adjudicated: eight binary visibility items and one IDEAL-stage assignment. The contested calls and final adjudication decisions are listed in Online Resource 3.

Public implementation visibility

Item-level reporting was uneven and depended on public-record type (Table 2; Fig. 3). In the primary full-text/public full-text analysis, network type or architecture and clinical outcomes were visible in all eight records. Distance or site separation and quantitative latency were each reported in 7/8 records. Advanced network metrics, backup or failover arrangements, on-site rescue or takeover planning and technical adverse events were each visible in 6/8 records. Ethics or governance was visible in 5/8 records, registration or protocol identifiers in 2/8 records and cybersecurity or data-protection measures in 1/8 record.

Short public records showed the expected effect of format constraints. Programme-cluster sensitivity analysis

The 16 reports were collapsed into seven visible programme clusters: Bauer remote renal-access demonstration; KangDuo dual-console programme; Chinese 5G renal/adrenal early programme; Triple-console RARP programme; Chinese multicentre mixed-urology programme; Asia-Middle East ultra-long-distance programme; and Chinese single-port telesurgery programme. At cluster level, network architecture, clinical outcomes and distance or site separation were visible in 7/7 clusters. Quantitative latency was visible in 6/7 clusters; advanced network metrics, on-site rescue or takeover planning, technical adverse events and ethics or governance in 5/7; backup or failover in 4/7; and registration or protocol identifiers and cybersecurity or data-protection measures in 1/7 each. Thus, the main visibility pattern was not explained solely by repeated reporting from the same programmes.

Discussion

Overall

This evidence map shows a clear transition in urological telesurgery. The field has moved beyond isolated proof of concept and now includes multicentre real-world evaluation, comparative cohorts and a randomised trial. However, public implementation visibility has not matured uniformly with clinical evaluation. The public record still incompletely reports some details that would allow a reader to judge whether a remote operation is reproducible, resilient and governable.

The revision changes the interpretation of the audit. Short records are not miniature full texts; by format alone, they cannot reliably contain all implementation details. Therefore, the primary visibility analysis is now restricted to full-text or publicly available full-text records, with short public records shown separately throughout. The remaining gaps in full-length records are narrower for several technical domains than the all-record aggregate suggested, but registration and cybersecurity remain rarely visible even in full-length reports.

The distinction between item-level frequencies and a score is also central. We do not present the 11 items as a validated reporting instrument, do not calculate report-level totals or medians, and do not rank reports or programmes. The report-level heatmap has been removed. The figures and tables now ask a narrower question: for each implementation item, is it explicitly visible in the public record, and does visibility differ between full-length and short records?

The findings clarify what is new compared with related reviews. The prostate-cancer systematic review asked a clinical disease-specific question and included six prostate-cancer studies searched to June 2025 [3]. The broader single-port and urological-cancer robotic reviews document rapid platform diversification and technical innovation in urology [4, 5]. The present review asks a different implementation-transparency question across benign and malignant urological telesurgery, includes the 2026 randomised trial and public dataset, separates full-text from short public records and maps reports against the IDEAL innovation pathway.

The first randomised trial is a milestone, but it does not remove the need for better public reporting. Randomised evidence can show non-inferiority in a defined network and institutional configuration [1]. It cannot by itself show that the intervention is ready for routine transfer into different legal systems, hospitals, network architectures or rescue environments. Stage 4 surveillance remains absent from the public record. A future registry should separate clinical adverse events from technical events, include network metrics and report near-misses as well as completed cases.

Cybersecurity deserves particular attention. Only one full-text or public full-text record explicitly described a data-protection or cybersecurity measure in a way that met our coding rule, and no short public record did so. This does not prove that other programmes lacked cybersecurity planning. It means that readers could not see it. In telesurgery, data security is part of patient safety because command signals, video feeds, patient identifiers and inter-site communications may be transmitted across complex digital pathways.

Registration was also uncommon. Registration matters because prospective definitions of success, failure, technical malfunction and conversion are important in a field where case selection and network configuration may strongly influence outcomes. The public dataset accompanying the 2026 randomised trial is therefore a positive example [2]. Future non-randomised studies would also benefit from protocol availability and prespecified technical endpoints.

The programme-cluster sensitivity analysis addresses an important unit-of-analysis concern. Several reports likely share patient populations, operators, platforms or network pathways. Collapsing related records into seven visible programme clusters did not remove the main pattern: network architecture and clinical outcomes remained widely visible, whereas registration and cybersecurity remained visible in only one cluster each. This supports the interpretation that the visibility pattern is not driven solely by repeated publication from a small number of programmes.

The practical response is not to make articles longer for its own sake. It is to standardise a compact implementation box or supplement. At minimum, a urological telesurgery report should state the clinical context, the digital pathway, the rescue plan, the governance framework, the outcome definitions, the public-record type and any overlap with prior programme reports. These details could fit into a structured table and would make even short-format reports more useful.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is its focus on what is visible to an independent reader. We used public data, avoided patient-level pooling across potentially overlapping programmes, applied conservative IDEAL staging, separated full-text from short public records, added inter-rater reliability statistics and treated the item set as descriptive rather than validated.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample is small and rapidly evolving. Some reports were available only as abstracts, public summaries or landing pages, and their lower item visibility is partly a word-count effect. Public non-reporting does not prove that a team failed to consider an item. The report, not the patient, was the unit of analysis, so overlapping programme populations may be present. The programme-cluster analysis is exploratory because public reports do not always disclose enough information to prove or disprove overlap. We may have missed non-English or non-indexed reports. Finally, this was a public visibility audit, not a risk-of-bias assessment, clinical effectiveness review or regulatory evaluation.

Conclusion

Urological telesurgery has reached early comparative and randomised evaluation, but its public implementation record remains uneven. In full-text or publicly available full-text records, core network and clinical outcome information was usually visible, whereas registration and cybersecurity remained rarely visible. Short public records had additional format-related gaps. A concise minimum public reporting set could improve reproducibility, support external appraisal and help distinguish technical feasibility from implementation readiness.
