Semiclassics at the cusp
\dosserif Jahmall Bersini ✩ , Domenico Orlando ★✩ Susanne Reffert ✩ , and Jesse Woods ✩ \authorBlock ✩ \dosserif Albert Einstein Center for Fundamental Physics, Institute for Theoretical Physics, University of Bern, Sidlerstrasse 5, CH-3012 Bern, Switzerland \authorBlock ★ \dosserif INFN sezione di Torino. via Pietro Giuria 1, 10125 Torino, Italy

Abstract
We study cusped Wilson line operators in the Abelian Higgs model in d = 4 − ϵ d=4-\epsilon at large external charges. Using a double-scaling limit Q → ∞ Q\to\infty , ϵ → 0 \epsilon\to 0 with Q ​ ϵ Q\epsilon fixed, we develop a semiclassical framework that provides analytic control beyond fixed-order perturbation theory. We compute the cusp anomalous dimension for arbitrary charges up to next-to-next-to-leading order in the gauge coupling, while resumming scalar self-interactions to all orders. Our results interpolate between perturbative and large-charge regimes, accessing domains that are invisible in fixed-order perturbation theory. As an application, we provide new predictions for various defect cft observables, including the Mandelstam–Schwinger-dressed two-point function characterizing the superconducting phase transition.

3.1
Equations of motion

In the double-scaling limit
Q\to\infty
,
\epsilon\to 0
with
Q\epsilon
fixed, the computation of the anomalous cusp dimension becomes semiclassical.
We need to find the saddles of the rescaled action in Eq. (
2.11
) and are free to use field redefinitions that might be problematic in a general path integral.

It is convenient to introduce the radial and angular fields
\rho(x)
and
\chi(x)
and trade
A
for the gauge-invariant quantity
B
:

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\chi},

\displaystyle B=i\left(\sqrt{G}A+\dd{\chi}\right),

(3.1)

such that the rescaled action
\bar{S}
reads

\bar{S}=\int\dd{x}\Big(-\frac{1}{4G}\tilde{F}_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}\rho\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}B^{\mu}B_{\mu}+\frac{\kappa}{4}\rho^{4}+\left(q_{2}-q_{1}\right)\log(\rho)\pqty*{\delta(x)+\delta(x-x_{\infty})}\Big)\\
-q_{1}\int_{\gamma_{1}}B+q_{2}\int_{\gamma_{2}}B\,,

(3.2)

where we set
\phi_{a\geq 2}=0
and

\tilde{F}=\dd{B}=i\sqrt{G}F\,.

(3.3)

The
eom
in covariant form read

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{\mu}\left(\sqrt{g}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=G\left(\rho^{2}B^{\nu}+q_{1}J_{\text{MS}}^{\nu}(\gamma_{1})-q_{2}J_{\text{MS}}^{\nu}(\gamma_{2})\right)\,,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{\mu}\left(\sqrt{g}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\rho\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-B^{\mu}B_{\mu}+\kappa\rho^{2}\right)\rho+\frac{(q_{2}-q_{1})}{\rho}\pqty{\delta^{d}\left(x\right)+\delta^{d}\left(x-x_{\infty}\right)}\,,

(3.5)

where
J_{\text{MS}}
are the
ms
currents for the two arms of the cusp as defined in Eq. (
2.5
).
With these sources, the Gauss law takes the form

\int\sqrt{g}\left(\rho^{2}B^{\mu}(x)+q_{1}J_{\text{MS}}^{\mu}(x\mid\gamma_{1})-q_{2}J_{\text{MS}}^{\mu}(x\mid\gamma_{2})\right)\dd{x}=0\,.

(3.6)

The presence of the cusp leads naturally to the issue of the choice of appropriate boundary conditions for the scalar field.
In fact, there are essentially just two choices, related to the boundary terms that can be added to the action.
We choose the boundary condition that imposes regular behavior for
\rho
at the cusp, assuming that, at least in the perturbative small-
G

We will return to this issue with a more detailed discussion in Section
3.2
.

Having written the
eom
in covariant form we are free to use the spherical coordinate system of Eq. (
2.12
) in which the symmetries of the problem are manifest.
The insertions of the scalar field sit at
\tau\to\pm\infty
, so both delta functions in the action of Eq. (
2.11
) can be traded for boundary conditions.
As already observed above, the arms of the cusp are parallel to the
\tau

J_{\text{MS}}
has only one non-vanishing component: for a line at fixed solid angle
\Omega_{*}
, it is simply

J_{\text{MS}}=J_{\text{MS}}^{\tau}\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}_{\tau}{}=-\frac{\delta(\Omega-\Omega_{*})}{R^{3}}\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}_{\tau}{}\,.

(3.7)

Having assumed that there is no spontaneous symmetry breaking, the Maxwell equation (
3.4
) implies that also the
B

\tau

B=B^{\tau}\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}_{\tau}{}\equiv B\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}_{\tau}{}
.

The expressions simplify if we rescale the field
\rho

\rho=e^{-\tau/R}\rho_{c}\,,

(3.8)

which corresponds to the Weyl rescaling that turns
\mathbb{R}^{4}

\mathbb{R}\times S^{3}

Like this, the dilatation invariance is realized if we demand both

\rho_{c}

\alpha

\theta

With these simplifications, the
eom

\displaystyle\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits_{S^{3}}\rho_{c}(\alpha,\theta)=\left(1-(RB(\alpha,\theta))^{2}+\kappa(R\rho_{c}(\alpha,\theta))^{2}\right)\rho_{c}(\alpha,\theta),

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits_{S^{3}}B(\alpha,\theta)=G\left((R\rho_{c}(\alpha,\theta))^{2}B(\alpha,\theta)-\frac{q_{1}\delta(\alpha)-q_{2}\delta(\alpha-\alpha_{*})\delta(\theta)}{2\pi R\sin^{2}\alpha\sin\theta}\right),

(3.10)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin(\theta)\dd{\theta}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{2}(\alpha)\dd{\alpha}\rho_{c}^{2}(\alpha,\theta)B(\alpha,\theta)=\frac{q_{1}-q_{2}}{2\pi R^{3}}\,,

(3.11)

where
\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits_{S^{3}}{}

\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits_{S^{3}}{}=\tfrac{1}{\sin^{2}\alpha}\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}_{\alpha}\left(\sin^{2}\alpha\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}_{\alpha}{}\right)+\tfrac{1}{\sin^{2}\alpha\sin\theta}\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}_{\theta}\left(\sin\theta\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}_{\theta}{}\right)+\tfrac{1}{\sin^{2}\alpha\sin^{2}\theta}\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}_{\phi}^{2}{}\,.

The saddle-point action reduces to

+\left(1-(RB)^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}(R\rho_{c})^{2}\right)\rho_{c}^{2}+\frac{q_{1}\delta(\alpha)-q_{2}\delta(\theta)\delta(\alpha-\alpha_{*})}{\pi R\sin^{2}(\alpha)\sin(\theta)}B\Bigg]\,.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}B}{\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}\alpha}\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}-\frac{Gq_{1}}{4\pi R\alpha^{2}}\,,

where

Boundary terms and boundary conditions

To solve the
eom
we need to specify boundary conditions for the scalar field
\rho_{c}
at the cusp.
Different choices of boundary conditions will then correspond to different localized boundary terms in the action.

Expanding the
eom
close to the cusp for
G<2\pi
, one finds the two possible behaviors

\begin{cases}B=\frac{q_{1}G}{4\pi R\alpha},\quad\rho_{c}\sim\rho_{\pm}(\kappa,G)\alpha^{-(1\pm\nu_{1})/2}&\text{for $\alpha\sim 0$},\\
B=-\frac{q_{2}G}{4\pi R\gamma},\quad\rho_{c}\sim\rho_{\pm}(\kappa,G)\gamma^{-(1\pm\nu_{2})/2}&\text{for $\alpha\sim\alpha_{*}$},\end{cases}

(3.15)

where

\displaystyle\nu_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\frac{(q_{1}G)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}}\,,

\displaystyle\nu_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\frac{(q_{2}G)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}}\,,

(3.16)

and

Given the obvious symmetry, we can concentrate on the
\alpha=\alpha_{*}

Expanding in powers of
G
and noting that

\displaystyle\gamma^{-(1+\nu_{2})/2}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{(q_{2}G)^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}\frac{\log\gamma}{\gamma}+\dots\,,

\displaystyle\gamma^{-(1-\nu_{2})/2}

\displaystyle\sim 1-\frac{(q_{2}G)^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}{\log\gamma}+\dots\,,

(3.17)

one can see that the
\rho_{-}

to the homogeneous solution where the scalar has a constant profile.

To find the corresponding boundary terms in the action, we start by observing that if we do not neglect total derivatives in the variation of the bulk action, and impose a
uv

\gamma_{0}
away from the cusp location, then the variation includes a boundary-localised term

\eval*{\frac{\delta S_{\text{bulk}}}{T}}_{\gamma=\gamma_{0}}=\eval*{4\pi R\sin^{2}\gamma\mathop{\mathrm{{}\partial}}\mathopen{}_{\gamma}\rho_{c}\delta\rho_{c}}_{\gamma=\gamma_{0}}\,.

(3.18)

We can compensate it with a term localized on the second arm of the line,

(3.19)

where

is a new, running, coupling
[

,

,
20
,
21
]

The total variation is then proportional to

\eval*{\delta S_{\text{bulk}}}_{\gamma=\gamma_{0}}+\delta S_{\text{bdry}}\propto\rho_{+}\gamma_{0}^{-\nu_{2}}\pqty*{F-\pqty*{1+\nu_{2}}}+\rho_{-}\pqty*{F-\pqty*{1-\nu_{2}}}\,,

(3.20)

and vanishes if

\frac{\rho_{+}}{\rho_{-}}=-\frac{F-1-\nu_{2}}{F-1+\nu_{2}}\gamma_{0}^{-\nu_{2}}\,.

(3.21)

Imposing the

(

) to be cutoff-independent leads to the beta function for

\beta_{F}=\gamma_{0}\odv{F}{\gamma_{0}}=\frac{(q_{2}G)^{2}}{8\pi}-F+\frac{F^{2}}{2}\,.

(3.22)

The flow joins two fixed points:

\displaystyle F_{\acs{ir}}

\displaystyle=1-\nu_{2},

\displaystyle F_{\acs{uv}}

\displaystyle=1+\nu_{2},

(3.23)

which correspond, respectively, to the vanishing of

and
\rho_{-}

In either case, the boundary term evaluated at the saddle does not contribute to the total energy.

In the following, we will concentrate on the
\rho_{-}
branch that is, as observed above, continuously connected to a constant solution for

The analysis changes radically if

In this case, the behavior at the cusp becomes

Correspondingly, the two fixed points in the

We therefore expect a new strongly-coupled phase in which more symmetries might be broken.

4
Symmetries and spectrum

The first step towards understanding the spectrum of fluctuations around our semiclassical solution consists in describing the corresponding symmetry-breaking pattern.

The (Euclidean) Abelian Higgs model with
N
complex scalar fields at the
ir
fixed point has symmetry
SO(d,1)\times SU(N)/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
.
The insertion of the cusp, with its vertex sitting in the completely symmetric representation of
SU(N)
with
Q_{1}-Q_{2}
boxes, breaks the conformal group to the
SO(d-2)
rotations orthogonal to the plane of the cups times the dilatations
\mathcal{D}
, and the global
SU(N)/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
to
SU(N-1)/\mathbb{Z}_{N-1}
.
All in all,

SO(d,1)\times SU(N)/\mathbb{Z}_{N}\to SO(d-2)\times\mathcal{D}\times SU(N-1)/\mathbb{Z}_{N-1}\,.

(4.1)

In the cylinder frame, the symmetries are realized geometrically:
SO(d-2)
corresponds to the isometries of the
d+1
sphere with two non-antipodal punctures, and
\mathcal{D}
generates the time translations.

In order to count the Goldstone fields resulting from the breaking of the global symmetry, we observe that in the case of only the global symmetry, the breaking pattern would be the same as for the
O(2N)
model:
SO(d,1)\times O(2N)\to SO(d)\times\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\times U(N-1)
, where
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is the helical symmetry obtained as the sum of time translation and a global
U(1)
.
In this case, the low-energy theory contains
1
type-I Goldstone with linear dispersion relation,
N-1
type-II Goldstones with quadratic dispersion, and
N-1
massive modes with mass proportional to
\mu_{0}
.
In the presence of the gauge symmetry, the Higgs mechanism comes into play: the type-I Goldstone combines with the photon to give a mass to the vector field.
The remaining massless fields are the type-II Goldstones with parabolic dispersion relations
[
69
,
3
,
2
]
.
The fundamental difference with respect to the large-charge analysis of systems with only a global symmetry is that the universal conformal type-I Goldstone is not present here due to the Higgs mechanism
[
11
]
.
For generic values of
q_{1}
,
q_{2}
and
\alpha_{*}
, we thus have a massive spectrum and do not expect to see fluctuations with unit energy (in the scale of
R
) to be identified with
cft
descendants of the lowest operator of fixed charge.

To obtain the quadratic Lagrangian, we expand our fields around the classical trajectory.
Even though we have introduced the gauge-invariant field
B
, it is technically simpler to go back to the fields
A
and
\chi
and introduce the corresponding fluctuations:

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle=-i\mu_{0}\tau+\Pi/r\,,

\displaystyle A_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{i(\mu_{0}-\mu)}{\sqrt{G}}+\mathcal{A}_{\tau}\,,

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{i}+\nabla_{i}h\,,

\displaystyle\rho_{c}

\displaystyle=r+\hat{r}\,,

(4.2)

where
\nabla^{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}=0
. The price to pay is the introduction of a gauge fixing term,

\mathcal{L}_{\xi}=-\frac{1}{2\xi}\left(\nabla^{\mu}A_{\mu}+\sqrt{G}r\Pi\right)^{2},

(4.3)

knowing that the final result has to be gauge invariant.

In writing Eq. (
4.2
) we have separated the time component of the gauge field and performed the Helmholtz decomposition for the spatial directions.
Adopting the convenient choice
\xi=1
, the quadratic Lagrangian decomposes into five independent pieces:

\mathcal{L}^{(2)}=\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(2)}+\mathcal{L}_{T}^{(2)}+\mathcal{L}_{h}^{(2)}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{gh}}^{(2)}+\mathcal{L}_{II}^{(2)}\,.

(4.4)

\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(2)}
describes the mixing between the
\hat{r}
,
\Pi
,
A_{\tau}

5.1
Shallow cusp and defect-changing operator

In the limit
\alpha_{*}\sim\pi
, the cusp is close to the case of the straight infinite Wilson line. The cusp dimension can therefore be reconstructed in terms of the defect-changing operator
\mathcal{O}_{Q_{1}Q_{2}}
and the displacement operator
D
, a protected defect operator stemming from the breaking of translational invariance by the line
[
43
]
.
In this case, we have the expansion

\Gamma_{q_{1}q_{2}}(\alpha_{*})=\Delta_{q_{1}q_{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\beta_{q_{1}q_{2}}\pqty*{\alpha_{*}-\pi}^{2}+\order{(\alpha_{*}-\pi)^{3}}\,,

(5.2)

where
\Delta_{q_{1}q_{2}}
is the dimension of the scalar defect operator
\mathcal{O}_{q_{1}q_{2}}
inserted at the origin, and
\beta_{q_{1}q_{2}}
depends on the four-point function with two insertions of
\mathcal{O}_{q_{1}q_{2}}
and two insertions of
D
.
Applied to the Abelian Higgs model, we find for small
\kappa

\displaystyle\Delta_{q_{1}q_{2}}={}

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}Q\Bigg[\pqty*{\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}+\frac{\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}^{2}\kappa}{8\pi^{2}}-\frac{\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}^{3}\kappa^{2}}{32\pi^{4}}+\dots}-\frac{3\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}G\\
+\Bigg(\tfrac{\left(3-8\pi^{2}\right)\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}^{3}-24\pi^{2}q_{1}q_{2}\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}}{384\pi^{4}}+\tfrac{\kappa\left(15\pi^{2}\left(\pi^{2}-6\right)q_{1}q_{2}\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}^{2}+2\left(45-15\pi^{2}+2\pi^{4}\right)\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}^{4}\right)}{5760\pi^{6}}\\

\displaystyle\beta_{q_{1}q_{2}}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{QG}{6\pi^{2}}+\left(\tfrac{\left(3+\pi^{2}\right)\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}}{72\pi^{4}}-\tfrac{\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}^{2}\kappa}{32\pi^{6}}+\tfrac{\left(180-105\pi^{2}+14\pi^{4}\right)\pqty*{q_{1}-q_{2}}^{3}\kappa^{2}}{34560\pi^{8}}+\dots\right)q_{1}q_{2}QG^{2}

\displaystyle\Delta_{q_{1}q_{2}}={}

\displaystyle\beta_{q_{1}q_{2}}={}

The dimension
\Delta_{q_{1}q_{2}}
vanishes for
q_{1}=q_{2}=q
, as it should since in this case
\mathcal{O}_{qq}
is the identity.
Furthermore, the whole
\order{G^{2}}
contribution vanishes and

\beta_{qq}=-\frac{QG}{6\pi^{2}}+\order{QG^{3},Q^{0}G}=-\frac{(Qe)^{2}}{6\pi^{2}}+\order{Qe^{6},Q^{0}e^{2}}\,,

(5.13)

which is negative as demanded by reflection positivity.

5.2
Sharp cusp and superconducting order parameter

In the opposite limit,
\alpha_{*}\to 0
,
\Gamma_{q_{1}q_{1}}
describes the fusion of the two antiparallel half-lines of charge
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
.
The form of the anomalous dimension is set by the defect fusion algebra to be

\Gamma_{q_{1}q_{2}}=\frac{C_{q_{1}(-q_{2})(\overline{q_{1}-q_{2}})}}{\alpha_{*}}+\Delta_{(q_{1}-q_{2})0}+\alpha_{q_{1}q_{2}}\,\alpha_{*}^{\Delta_{\text{irr}}-1}+\ldots\,.

(5.14)

Here,
C_{q_{1}(-q_{2})(\overline{q_{1}-q_{2}})}
is the Casimir interaction between the two conformal lines at separation of order
\order{\alpha_{*}}
and measures the energy cost required to fuse them into a single line of charge
q_{1}-q_{2}
(binding energy).
Since all the components of
\Gamma_{q_{1}q_{2}}
aside from
\Gamma^{(-1,1)}_{q_{1}q_{2}}
are regular at
\alpha_{*}=0
, we find

C_{q_{1}(-q_{2})(\overline{q_{1}-q_{2}})}=-\frac{GQ}{4\pi}q_{1}q_{2}=-\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi}Q_{1}Q_{2}\,,

(5.15)

which, as expected, is the Coulomb potential between the two charges.
We are also able to extract the dimension of the lowest irrelevant operator on the
\bar{Q}
line, which is
\Delta_{\text{irr}}=2+\order{G^{3}}
as well as the associated coefficient
\alpha_{q_{1}q_{2}}
, which reads

\alpha_{q_{1}q_{2}}=\frac{QGq_{1}q_{2}}{12\pi}\left(1+\tfrac{\frac{3^{5/3}}{4\pi^{4/3}}\left(\sqrt{81\kappa^{2}(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}-48\pi^{4}}+9\kappa(q_{1}-q_{2})\right)^{1/3}(q_{1}-q_{2})}{2(6\pi^{4})^{1/3}+\left(\sqrt{81\kappa^{2}(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}-48\pi^{4}}+9\kappa(q_{1}-q_{2})\right)^{2/3}}G+\order{G^{2}}\right)+\order{Q^{0}G}\,.

(5.16)

From the finite contribution of
\Gamma_{q_{1}q_{2}}
in the limit
\alpha_{*}\to 0
, we obtain the dimension of the creation operator
\phi_{(i_{1}}\dots\phi_{i_{\bar{Q}})}
of the fused defect, which is just a half line with charge
q_{1}-q_{2}
.
Alternatively, we can think of it as a semi-infinite line joining two scalar insertions of opposite charges.
This is the usual
ms
dressed two-point function
\braket{\mathcal{O}_{\bar{Q}}^{*}(x_{\infty})\,W_{\gamma}(\bar{Q})\,\mathcal{O}_{\bar{Q}}(0)}
.
Its scaling at criticality, which is used as an order parameter for superconductivity, is manifestly the dimension of the defect-creation operator
\Delta_{\bar{Q}0}
: we can think of the
ms
-dressed two-point function as the (regularized) limit of the cusp either for
\alpha_{*}\to 0
, or for
q_{2}\to 0
.

Our
\order{G^{2}}
result falsifies a standing conjecture about the scaling of the
ms
dressing
[
56
,
64
]
.
While expectation values of non-gauge invariant operators vanish identically, one can still compute perturbatively a “gauge-dependent” conformal dimension for the scalar field.
It has been observed that if one takes this quantity and imposes the traceless gauge, the
\order{G}
result coincides with the scaling of the gauge-invariant
ms
two-point function.
Equation (
5.1
) shows explicitly that this is accidental and does not generalize to higher orders in
G
.
The story is different for the Dirac dressing that we review in Appendix
A
, since in this case the (physically meaningful) scaling dimension coincides with the gauge-dependent result in Landau gauge.

5.3
Trivial defect-changing operator

In the special case
Q_{1}=Q_{2}
, the
\order{G^{2}}
term vanishes and the cusp anomalous dimension reduces to

\Gamma_{qq}(\alpha_{*})=-Q\frac{1+\pqty{\pi-\alpha_{*}}\cot(\alpha_{*})}{4\pi^{2}}G+\order{G^{3}}\,,

(5.17)

and, consistently with reflection positivity,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{qq}(\alpha_{*})

\displaystyle<0\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}_{qq}(\alpha_{*})

\displaystyle>0\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{qq}(\alpha_{*})

\displaystyle<0\,.

(5.18)

From the second derivative in
\alpha_{*}
we can derive the (physically meaningful) normalization of the displacement operator:

\braket{D_{i}(\tau)D_{j}(0)}=-\frac{6\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{qq}(\pi)}{\tau^{4}}=\frac{QG}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{\tau^{4}}=\frac{e^{2}Q^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{\tau^{4}}\,.

(5.19)

In fact, this result can be made stronger: if
q_{1}=q_{2}
, there is no scalar insertion at the cusp and so, at least when
G
is sufficiently small, the minimum-energy configuration is characterized by a vanishing classical profile for
\rho_{c}
. This allows us to solve the Maxwell equations exactly, obtaining

\mu(\alpha,\gamma)=\frac{G}{4\pi^{2}R}\left((\pi-\alpha)\cot(\alpha)-(\pi-\gamma)\cot(\gamma)\right)\,.

(5.20)

This means that in our regime, the value that we found in Eq. (
5.17
) does not receive perturbative corrections in
G
at all and so
\Gamma_{qq}^{(-1)}=G\Gamma_{qq}^{(-1,1)}
.
On the one hand, this matches the known one-loop cusp anomalous dimension in Maxwell theory
[
30
]
.
On the other, we find an all-order statement from the point of view of conventional perturbation theory: in the perturbative expansion of the cusp anomalous dimension, all the terms scaling as
Q_{1}G^{i}\kappa^{k}=Q_{1}^{1+i+k}e^{2i}\lambda^{k}
vanish except the one with
i=1\,,\ k=0
.

One may be tempted to conclude that the scalar fields do not contribute to the cusp anomalous dimension in the semiclassical limit
i.e.
, in the case of large charge Wilson lines. However, as discussed earlier, for
G>2\pi
one generally expects the path integral to be dominated by a new trajectory characterized by a nontrivial scalar profile as shown in
[
21
]
for the straight Wilson line (
\alpha_{*}=\pi
) case.

6
Perturbative
\epsilon
-expansion

As mentioned earlier, while our results are valid for any fixed value of
\kappa
, the limit of small
\kappa
overlaps with the regime of validity of standard (Feynman diagram) perturbation theory in
4-\epsilon
dimensions.

This provides a controlled semiclassical expansion even when the underlying couplings are not parametrically small. In this sense, large charge offers an alternative organizing principle, complementary to weak coupling, that allows one to access nontrivial regimes of Wilson line observables and defect dynamics.

To compare the two approaches, we can reinterpret our semiclassical construction as a rearrangement of the terms in the conventional
\epsilon
-expansion.
In Eq. (
2.11
) we have rewritten the action in the form
S=Q\bar{S}
, where
\bar{S}
depends only on the ’t Hooft couplings, obtained by keeping the product
Q\epsilon
fixed.
Now
Q
is a loop-counting parameter and, order-by-order in
Q
, the observables are functions of the product
\epsilon Q
.

Concretely, when expanded in the limit of small
\epsilon Q
, the leading-order (semiclassical) result
\Gamma_{Q_{1}Q_{2}}^{(-1)}
yields all terms scaling as
Q(Q\epsilon)^{n}
, the one-loop result
\Gamma_{Q_{1}Q_{2}}^{(0)}
the terms scaling as
Q^{0}(\epsilon Q)^{n}
, and in general the
k
-loop contribution
\Gamma_{Q_{1}Q_{2}}^{(k-1)}
in the double-scaled action around the semiclassical saddle provides a resummation of all terms scaling as
Q^{1-k}(\epsilon Q)^{n}
.
This is to be compared with the standard
\epsilon
expansion, where at each order in
\epsilon
there will be terms that scale with non-negative powers of
Q
(since the
Q\to 0
limit is regular), showing that the two expansions — small
\epsilon
and small
\epsilon Q
— are complementary.
Rewriting the ’t Hooft couplings in terms of the original
e^{2}
and
\lambda
, and expanding at second order in
\epsilon
(remembering that
\lambda=\order{\epsilon}
and
e=\order{\sqrt{\epsilon}}
), we find for the cusp anomalous dimension the following expansion:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{Q_{1}Q_{2}}(\alpha_{*})={}

\displaystyle Q_{1}-Q_{2}+\frac{\lambda}{3}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})(Q_{1}-Q_{2}-5-N)

(6.1)

\displaystyle-\frac{e^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}\left(3(Q_{1}-Q_{2})^{2}+4Q_{1}Q_{2}\left(1+(\alpha_{*}-\pi)\cot\alpha_{*}\right)+\order{Q_{1}-Q_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{9}\left(2(Q_{1}-Q_{2})^{3}+(N-3)(Q_{1}-Q_{2})^{2}+\order{Q_{1}-Q_{2}}\right)\\
+\order{\lambda e^{2}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})^{2},\lambda e^{2}Q_{1}Q_{2}}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}+\frac{e^{4}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})}{384\pi^{4}}\Big(\left(3-8\pi^{2}\right)(Q_{1}-Q_{2})^{2}\\
+8Q_{1}Q_{2}\left(\alpha_{*}(\alpha_{*}-2\pi)(\alpha_{*}-\pi)\cot\alpha_{*}-2\pi^{2}\right)+\order{Q_{1}-Q_{2}}\Big)+\dots\,,\end{multlined}

where the dots denote
\order{\epsilon^{3}}
corrections. In the
Q_{2}=0
case, we recover the one-loop result found in
[
56
,
11
]
for the scaling dimension
\Delta_{Q_{1}0}
associated with the
ms
dressed two-point function and make a prediction for a two-loop calculation.

7
Conclusions

When applying the semi-classical methods of the large-charge expansion to gauge theories, the need to work with gauge-invariant quantities leads directly to the notion of nonlocal operators, and particularly those constructed from Wilson lines.
A natural set-up is to consider two Wilson lines meeting at a cusp, with the two arms carrying different charges
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
.

From the viewpoint of defect quantum field theory, a Wilson line defines a one-dimensional defect, and the large-charge limit probes sectors where it carries large quantum numbers, in close analogy with the standard large-charge expansions in
cft

This framework provides an alternative organizing principle for gauge theories, complementary to weak coupling, and enables a systematic study of non-linear and backreaction effects in Wilson line observables.

In this paper, we have focused on the Abelian Higgs model in

We have successfully studied two Wilson lines carrying charges

Despite taking this limit, we still needed to work perturbatively in the gauge-coupling

Our results reproduce those of the standard perturbative calculation in the literature while greatly extending the accessible regimes, since they are valid for

There are a number of directions one could follow from here.

•

The small-coupling expansion that we have used breaks for

This points to a new phase transition (analogous to the one discussed in

•

The physics of non-Abelian gauge symmetries is quite different and much richer. Also in that case, a semiclassical analysis might lead to new nontrivial regimes of Wilson line observables and defect dynamics.

•

It would be interesting to see whether our semiclassical approach would be useful in more general geometric settings involving multiple cusps such as

•

•

We have shown that focusing on sectors of the theory where the Wilson lines carry large quantum numbers leads to important simplifications.
This should naturally extend to higher-dimensional defects seen as semiclassical objects to be studied systematically in a large quantum number expansion.
