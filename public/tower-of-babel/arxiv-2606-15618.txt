A sharp p p -biased product bound for r r -cross-intersecting families
Fan Chang, Hong Liu, Miao Liu

Abstract
We prove a sharp product theorem for r r -cross-intersecting families in the p p -biased measure. If r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 , 0 ≤ p ≤ r − 1 r 0\leq p\leq\frac{r-1}{r} , and ℱ 1 , … , ℱ r ⊆ 2 [ n ] \mathcal{F}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{F}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]} are r r -cross-intersecting, then ∏ i = 1 r μ p ​ ( ℱ i ) ≤ p r . \prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq p^{r}. The bound is attained by a common 1 1 -star, and the range of p p is best possible. In particular, this proves the equal-bias case of a conjecture of Frankl and Tokushige and, for r = 3 r=3 , confirms a conjecture of Tokushige. We also prove a stability theorem: for r ≥ 3 r\geq 3 , every near-extremal r r -tuple is close, in p p -biased measure, to a common 1 1 -star, with an optimal linear dependence on the product deficit. The extremal proof uses a coordinatewise coupling at the critical bias together with an isoperimetric inequality for increasing families. The stability proof uses dual families and random ordered partitions to obtain Fourier concentration, then applies biased Friedgut–Kalai–Naor theorem to force the star structure.

Introduction

Intersection theorems are among the central results of extremal set theory. The classical
Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
[
8
]
asserts that, for
n\geq 2k
, every intersecting
family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
has size at most

\binom{n-1}{k-1},

with equality attained by a
1
-star. For arbitrary families of subsets of
[n]
, the
corresponding weighted problem is naturally formulated using the
p
-biased measure. For

0\leq p\leq 1
, let
\mu_{p}
denote the product measure on
2^{[n]}
under which each
element of
[n]
is included independently with probability
p
; equivalently,

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|F|}(1-p)^{n-|F|}\qquad\text{for }\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}.

This probabilistic viewpoint goes back at least to the work of Fishburn, Frankl, Freed,
Lagarias and Odlyzko
[
11
]
; see also the survey and monograph of Frankl and
Tokushige
[
16
,
17
]
.

For a single family, the
p
-biased Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem and its refinements show
that stars are extremal in the natural range of parameters; see, for instance, the biased
complete intersection theorem of Filmus
[
10
]
. It is therefore natural to ask
whether the same phenomenon persists for several families under cross-intersection
assumptions. We say that families

\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]}

are
r
-cross-intersecting
if

A_{1}\cap\cdots\cap A_{r}\neq\varnothing

for every choice
A_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
,
i\in[r]
.
1
1

1

If some
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is empty, then the
condition is vacuously satisfied and all product bounds below are immediate.

The most basic example is obtained by taking all families to be the same
1
-star

\mathcal{S}_{j}:=\{A\subseteq[n]:j\in A\}.

Then

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{i}}(\mathcal{S}_{j})=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}.

Frankl and Tokushige conjectured that this example is extremal.

Conjecture 1

(Frankl–Tokushige
[
17
]
)
.

Let

\max_{i\in[r]}p_{i}\leq\frac{r-1}{r},

and let
\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be
r
-cross-intersecting. Then

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}.

Our first result proves the equal-bias case of this conjecture for all
r
. In particular, for
r=3
, it confirms a conjecture of Tokushige
[
30
, Conjecture 4.11]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
0\leq p\leq\frac{r-1}{r}
, and let

\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be
r
-cross-intersecting. Then

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq p^{r}.

The range in Theorem
1.1
is best possible. Indeed, suppose
p>(r-1)/r.

For large
n
, take
all families to be
\left\{A\subseteq[n]:|A|>\frac{r-1}{r}n\right\}.

By pigeonhole principle, these families are
r
-cross-intersecting. On the other hand, as

p>(r-1)/r
, by the law of large numbers,
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\to 1

as
n\to\infty.

Thus, for sufficiently large
n
,

\prod_{i}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})>p^{r}.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
rests on two ingredients. The first is a
critical-bias additive bound: at the bias

p_{*}=\frac{r-1}{r},

the sum of the measures of
r
cross-intersecting families is at most
r-1
.
Related additive estimates were known in several settings. For two families at

p=1/2
, the statement follows by complementation and is closely related to
Kleitman’s non-disjointness theorem
[
26
]
. For three families,
Tokushige
[
31
, Theorem 7]
proved a sharp result in
the range
1/3\leq p\leq 1/2
. More generally, Gupta, Mogge, Piga and Schülke
[
21
]

obtained sharp sum theorems for non-empty
r
-cross
t
-intersecting families,
which specialize to product measure only for
p\leq 1/2
.
In the uniform setting, Frankl and Tokushige proved an analogous critical estimate for

r
-cross-union
n-k
-uniform families
[
15
]
.

These earlier mechanisms do not imply the critical-bias additive bound needed here. Many
of them rely on shifting, compression, or complement-based arguments
[
3
,
18
,
14
,
12
]
, which naturally compare
p
with
1-p
and therefore
encounter a half-bias barrier. Instead, we use a direct coordinatewise coupling: for each
coordinate
a\in[n]
, independently choose
R_{a}\in[r]
uniformly at random, and include

a
in all random sets
X_{i}
except
X_{R_{a}}
. Then each
X_{i}
has distribution

\mu_{p_{*}}
, while

X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{r}=\varnothing

deterministically. Hence, for
r
-cross-intersecting families, the events
X_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}

cannot all occur, giving

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq r-1.

This coupling is the point at which the proof reaches the sharp bias
p_{*}=(r-1)/r
directly
in the biased cube, rather than by the usual “going to infinity and back” route of
Dinur–Safra
[
5
]
and Frankl–Tokushige
[
13
]
.

The second ingredient is a comparison lemma for increasing families: if
0<p\leq q<1
, then

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})\leq\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F})^{\log p/\log q}
.
Equivalently,
\log\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})/\log p
is monotone in
p
. This monotonicity is
well known; see, for instance,
[
20
, Theorem 2.38]
and

[
7
, Lemma 2.6]
. Analytically, it follows by differentiating

\log\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})/\log p
and combining the Margulis–Russo formula
[
27
,
29
]

with the
p
-biased edge-isoperimetric inequality
[
6
]
. In this
sense the comparison lemma is isoperimetric in nature: weak Harper’s edge-isoperimetric
inequality can be recovered from it by differentiation, whereas differentiating the classical
small-set expansion bound for the
\rho
-noise operator does not give the sharp Harper
constant; see
[
28
, Chapter 10]
. In the proof below we give a short
self-contained induction proof. Applying the comparison lemma with
q=p_{*}
, and then
using AM–GM together with the additive bound above, gives the desired product bound.

We also prove a stability version of Theorem
1.1
. Closeness is measured in

p
-biased symmetric difference; as usual,

\mathcal{F}\Delta\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{G})\cup(\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{F}).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
r\geq 3
. There exist constants
\varepsilon_{\ref{thm:r-cross-product-stability}}(r)>0
and
C_{\ref{thm:r-cross-product-stability}}(r)>0
such that the following holds for all
0<p\leq\frac{r-1}{r}
and
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{\ref{thm:r-cross-product-stability}}(r)
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be
r
-cross-intersecting. If

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\geq(1-\varepsilon)p^{r},

then there exists a
1
-star
\mathcal{S}_{j}
such that for all
i\in[r]
,

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j})\leq C_{\ref{thm:r-cross-product-stability}}(r)p\varepsilon.

The assumption
r\geq 3
is necessary at the critical point. When
r=2
and
p=1/2
, define the majority

\mathcal{M}_{n}:=\begin{cases}\{A\subseteq[n]:|A|>n/2\},&\text{if }n\text{ is odd},\\[5.69054pt]
\{A\subseteq[n]:|A|>n/2\}\cup\{A\subseteq[n]:|A|=n/2\text{ and }1\in A\},&\text{if }n\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

Then
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is intersecting and
\mu_{1/2}(\mathcal{M}_{n})=1/2
, so
\mathcal{F}_{1}=\mathcal{F}_{2}=\mathcal{M}_{n}
is a non-star extremal pair.

The linear dependence on
\varepsilon
in Theorem
1.2
is
best possible. Let

\mathcal{T}_{n}:=\mathcal{S}_{1}\setminus\{\{1\}\}

and take

\mathcal{F}_{1}=\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{F}_{2}=\cdots=\mathcal{F}_{r}=\mathcal{S}_{1}.

If
\varepsilon_{n}:=(1-p)^{n-1},
then

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{T}_{n})=p(1-\varepsilon_{n})

and

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{T}_{n}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{1})=p\varepsilon_{n}.

The product is
(1-\varepsilon_{n})p^{r}
, while the distance from the extremal star is of order

p\varepsilon_{n}
.

The key ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.2
is the following additive stability result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
r\geq 3
be fixed, and let
p_{*}=\frac{r-1}{r}
. Then there exist constants
\eta_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)>0
and
C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)>0
such that the following holds for all
0<\eta\leq\eta_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be
r
-cross-intersecting. If

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i})\geq r-1-\eta,

then one of the following alternatives holds.

(i)

There exists a
1
-star
\mathcal{S}_{j}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
such that
\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j})\leq C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\eta
for every
i\in[r]
.

(ii)

There exists a unique index
i\in[r]
such that
\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i})\leq C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\eta
and, for every
j\in[r]\setminus\{i\}
,
\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{j})\geq 1-C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\eta
.

We briefly explain the proof of Theorem
1.3
. A direct inductive approach is possible at a qualitative level. One can slice the families with respect to a coordinate and apply induction. This gives a version of the same dichotomy, but forces a dimension-dependent restriction on the approximation parameter
\eta\leq r^{-n}
. Such a loss would not yield a dimension-free quantitative stability theorem. Instead, we take a Fourier-analytic approach. First pass to the up-closures

\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}

and then to the dual families

\mathcal{B}_{i}:=\{B\subseteq[n]:[n]\setminus B\notin\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}\}.

The dualization changes the critical bias
p_{*}=(r-1)/r
into
1/r
:

\mu_{1/r}(\mathcal{B}_{i})=1-\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}).

Moreover, the
r
-cross-intersection condition becomes a covering condition for ordered
partitions: for every ordered partition

P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}

of
[n]
, at least one event
P_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
holds.

Now choose a random coloring
x\in[r]^{n}
and let

C_{i}(x):=\{a\in[n]:x_{a}=i\}.

Then
C_{1}(x),\ldots,C_{r}(x)
form a random ordered partition of
[n]
, and each
C_{i}(x)
has
distribution
\mu_{1/r}
. Near equality in the critical-sum lemma implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(C_{i}(x))

is close in
L^{2}
to the constant function
1
. Expanding these functions in the

1/r
-biased Fourier basis, a standard Gram-matrix calculation shows that the Fourier mass of
each
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
above level
1
is
O_{r}(\eta)
. The biased
Friedgut–Kalai–Naor theorem
[
25
,
24
]
then implies that
each
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is close to a constant family or to a
1
-star. Finally, the ordered-partition
covering condition aligns these local alternatives, giving exactly the two possibilities in
Theorem
1.3
.

This use of FKN differs from previous Fourier-analytic stability arguments in
intersection theory. For two cross-intersecting families, one can often encode the condition
using a graph operator and apply a Hoffman-type bound. For intersection conditions involving
three or more simultaneous choices, high-dimensional Hoffman bounds have been developed and
used effectively, for example by Filmus, Golubev and Lifshitz
[
9
]
and by
Tokushige
[
30
]
. Here we avoid this route: the random ordered partition and
the dual covering condition give the required Fourier concentration directly.

Starting from a near-extremal product in Theorem
1.2
, we pass
to up-closures and use the comparison lemma to transfer the product lower bound to the
critical bias. The stability form of AM–GM then forces all numbers

\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\uparrow})

to be close to
p_{*}
. This rules out alternative (ii) in Theorem
1.3
, and alternative (i) gives a common star at the critical bias. A final application of the
comparison lemma on the two slices of the approximating star transfers the conclusion
back from
p_{*}
to the original bias
p
.

Further consequences.

In the concluding section we record several consequences and directions. First, the biased
product theorem gives a short proof of the asymptotic
k
-uniform product bound, and the
biased stability theorem gives a rough
k
-uniform stability statement via the standard
up-closure and local LYM argument. Second, the equal-bias theorem implies a partial
unequal-bias result for logarithmically balanced choices of
p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}
. We also discuss
an index-hypergraph formulation of the product argument.

Preliminaries

2.1
Definitions and notation

Let
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
and let
2^{[n]}
denote the power set of
[n]
. A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is said to be
increasing
if given
A\in\mathcal{F}
and
A\subseteq B
we also have
B\in\mathcal{F}
. We define the
up-closure
of
\mathcal{F}
to be

\mathcal{F}^{\uparrow}=\{B\subseteq[n]:A\subseteq B\text{ for some }A\in\mathcal{F}\},

i.e., it is the minimal increasing subfamily of
2^{[n]}
that contains
\mathcal{F}
.

We will often identify
\{0,1\}^{n}
with
2^{[n]}
via the correspondence
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\leftrightarrow\{i\in[n]:x_{i}=1\}
. We write
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}}
for the
indicator function
of
\mathcal{F}
, i.e., the Boolean function

\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\},\quad\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $x\in\mathcal{F}$},\\
0,&\text{if $x\notin\mathcal{F}$}.\end{cases}

Let
0\leq p\leq 1
. By identifying
\{0,1\}^{n}
with
2^{[n]}
as above, the

p
-biased measure on
2^{[n]}
can alternatively be viewed as the product
measure on
\{0,1\}^{n}
under which the coordinates are independent with

\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=1)=p
and
\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=0)=1-p.

Equivalently, with the convention that
0^{0}=1
,

\mu_{p}(A)=\sum_{x\in A}p^{|\{i\in[n]:x_{i}=1\}|}(1-p)^{|\{i\in[n]:x_{i}=0\}|},\qquad\forall A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}.

2.2
Biased Fourier analysis

Here we summarize some notation and basic properties of Fourier analysis on
\{0,1\}^{n}
. We fix

p\in(0,1)
and consider
\{0,1\}^{n}
to be equipped with the
p
-biased measure
\mu_{p}
. For each
i\in[n]
we define
\chi_{i}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\chi_{i}(x)=\frac{x_{i}-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}
(so
\chi_{i}
has mean
0
and variance
1
).
We use the orthonormal Fourier basis
\{\chi_{S}\}_{S\subseteq[n]}
of
L^{2}(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})
, where each
\chi_{S}:=\prod_{i\in S}\chi_{i}
. Any
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
has a unique expression
f=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(S)\chi_{S}
where
\{\hat{f}(S)\}_{S\subseteq[n]}
are the
p
-biased Fourier coefficients of
f
. Orthonormality gives the Plancherel identity
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(S)\hat{g}(S)
. In particular, we have the Parseval identity
\mathbb{E}[f^{2}]=\|f\|_{2}^{2}=\left\langle f,f\right\rangle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(S)^{2}
.

We shall use the following monotone consequence of the biased FKN theorem. The original
FKN theorem of Friedgut, Kalai and Naor
[
19
]
was proved for the uniform measure, and Kindler and Safra

[
25
,
24
]
extended it to the
p
-biased measure for arbitrary
fixed
p\in(0,1)
. In its usual form, the conclusion allows Boolean functions depending on one
coordinate; in set notation, these are stars

\mathcal{S}_{i}=\{A\subseteq[n]:i\in A\}

and anti-stars

\mathcal{S}_{i}^{0}=\{A\subseteq[n]:i\notin A\}.

For increasing families, the anti-star alternative
\mathcal{S}_{i}^{0}
can be absorbed into the
constant-family alternatives. Indeed, if
\mathcal{F}
is increasing and
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{i}^{0})=\delta,

then writing

\mathcal{F}^{0}=\{A\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}:A\in\mathcal{F}\},\qquad\mathcal{F}^{1}=\{A\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}:A\cup\{i\}\in\mathcal{F}\},

we have
\mathcal{F}^{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{1}
, and therefore

\delta=(1-p)(1-\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}^{0}))+p\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}^{1})\geq\min\{p,1-p\}.

Thus, if the standard FKN theorem returns an anti-star, then either the error is already bounded
below by a positive constant depending only on
p
, in which case one of the constant families

\varnothing,2^{[n]}
is close enough after increasing the constant, or else the anti-star case is
impossible. Hence the standard biased FKN theorem yields the following form.

Theorem 2.1

(Biased FKN)
.

Let
0<p<1
. There exists a constant
C_{\ref{lem:biased-FKN}}(p)>0
such that the following holds for every
n\geq 1
. If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is increasing and satisfies

\sum_{|S|\geq 2}\widehat{1_{\mathcal{F}}}(S)^{2}\leq\varepsilon,

then there exists
\mathcal{H}\in\{\varnothing,2^{[n]},\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{n}\}
such that
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}\Delta\mathcal{H})\leq C_{\ref{lem:biased-FKN}}(p)\varepsilon
.

2.3
Stability form of the AM–GM inequality

We shall use the following standard stability form of the AM–GM inequality due to Cartwright and Field
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
r\geq 2
and set
p_{*}:=\frac{r-1}{r}
. Then for every
y_{1},\dots,y_{r}\in[0,r-1]
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}=r-1
, we have

p_{*}-\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}^{\frac{1}{r}}\geq\frac{1}{2r(r-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(y_{i}-p_{*})^{2}.

We shall need the following variant in which the total sum is allowed to be smaller than
r-1
. This is the form needed after applying the critical-sum lemma (Lemma
3.1
) gives the required upper bound on the sum of the critical-bias measures, and the lemma below then converts near-optimality of the product into closeness to the balanced value
p_{*}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
r\geq 2
and set
p_{*}:=\frac{r-1}{r}
. For every
x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\geq 0
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}\leq r-1
, one has

p_{*}^{r}-\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}\geq\frac{p_{*}^{r-1}}{4r(r-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(x_{i}-p_{*})^{2}.

Proof.

Set
s:=r-1-\sum_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}\geq 0
and
y_{i}:=x_{i}+\frac{s}{r}
. Then
\sum_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}=r-1
and
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for every
i
. Also,
y_{i}\geq 0
for every
i
, and since the
y_{i}
’s are nonnegative and sum to
r-1
, we have
y_{i}\leq r-1
for every
i
. Hence
\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}
. By Theorem
2.2
,
p_{*}-\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}^{\frac{1}{r}}\geq\frac{1}{2r(r-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(y_{i}-p_{*})^{2}
. If
z:=\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}^{\frac{1}{r}}
, then
0\leq z\leq p_{*}
by AM–GM, and so
p_{*}^{r}-z^{r}\geq p_{*}^{r-1}(p_{*}-z)
. Thus
p_{*}^{r}-\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}\geq p_{*}^{r-1}(p_{*}-\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}^{\frac{1}{r}})
, and we have

p_{*}^{r}-\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}\geq p_{*}^{r}-\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}\geq\frac{p_{*}^{r-1}}{2r(r-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(y_{i}-p_{*})^{2}.

(1)

Since
x_{i}-p_{*}=(y_{i}-p_{*})-\frac{s}{r}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}(y_{i}-p_{*})=0
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{r}(x_{i}-p_{*})^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(y_{i}-p_{*})^{2}+\frac{s^{2}}{r}.

(2)

By AM–GM,

\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}\leq\left(\frac{r-1-s}{r}\right)^{r}=\left(p_{*}-\frac{s}{r}\right)^{r}.

Consequently,

p_{*}^{r}-\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}\geq p_{*}^{r}-\left(p_{*}-\frac{s}{r}\right)^{r}=p_{*}^{r}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{s}{r-1}\right)^{r}\right)\geq p_{*}^{r}\cdot\frac{s^{2}}{(r-1)^{2}},

(3)

where the last inequality follows from
r\geq 2
and
0\leq s\leq r-1
.
Combining (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
) gives

p_{*}^{r}-\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}\geq\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\frac{p_{*}^{r-1}}{2r(r-1)},r\cdot\frac{p_{*}^{r}}{(r-1)^{2}}\right\}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}(y_{i}-p_{*})^{2}+\frac{s^{2}}{r}\right)=\frac{p_{*}^{r-1}}{4r(r-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(x_{i}-p_{*})^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

3.1
Critical-sum lemma

We begin with the critical-bias sum inequality that drives the later extremal arguments.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
r\geq 2
and
\mathcal{F}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{F}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
are
r
-cross-intersecting, then

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{(r-1)/r}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq r-1.

Proof.

For each
j\in[n]
, independently choose a random index
R_{j}\in[r]
uniformly from
\{1,2,\dots,r\}
. Define random sets
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}\subseteq[n]
by
j\in X_{i}
if and only if
R_{j}\neq i
. In words, at coordinate
j
exactly one of the
r
sets
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
misses
j
, namely
X_{R_{j}}
, while all the others contain
j
.

Fix
i\in[r]
. For each
j\in[n]
, we have

\mathbb{P}(j\in X_{i})=\mathbb{P}(R_{j}\neq i)=\frac{r-1}{r}.

Since the choices of the
R_{j}
are independent across
j
, each
X_{i}
is distributed according to
\mu_{(r-1)/r}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(X_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i})=\mu_{(r-1)/r}(\mathcal{F}_{i}),\quad\forall i\in[r].

On the other hand, for every
j\in[n]
there is exactly one index
i
with
j\notin X_{i}
. Hence no element of
[n]
belongs to all of
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
, so
\cap_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}=\varnothing
with probability
1
. Note that events
\{X_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}\}
cannot all occur simultaneously, since the families are
r
-cross-intersecting. Thus we have

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}\}}\leq r-1.

Taking expectations finishes the proof.
∎

3.2
The comparison lemma

We next record the monotonicity statement that transports critical-bias estimates to smaller biases.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
0<p\leq q<1
. If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is increasing, then

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})\leq\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F})^{\frac{\log p}{\log q}}.

Equality holds for increasing subcubes.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
n
.
The case
n=0
is immediate.
Let
n\geq 1
, and suppose the statement has been proved in dimension
n-1
. For an increasing family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, define its two sections with respect to coordinate
n
by

\mathcal{F}^{0}:=\{A\subseteq[n-1]:A\in\mathcal{F}\},\quad\mathcal{F}^{1}:=\{A\subseteq[n-1]:A\cup\{n\}\in\mathcal{F}\}.

Both
\mathcal{F}^{0}
and
\mathcal{F}^{1}
are increasing families on
2^{[n-1]}
and
\mathcal{F}^{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{1}
. Set
\alpha:=\frac{\log p}{\log q}\geq 1
. By the induction hypothesis,

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}^{0})\leq\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F}^{0})^{\alpha},\quad\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}^{1})\leq\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F}^{1})^{\alpha}.

Therefore,

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})=(1-p)\cdot\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}^{0})+p\cdot\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}^{1})\leq(1-p)\cdot\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F}^{0})^{\alpha}+p\cdot\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F}^{1})^{\alpha}.

Thus it remains to prove that, for all
0\leq x\leq y
,

(1-p)x^{\alpha}+py^{\alpha}\leq\left((1-q)x+qy\right)^{\alpha}.

(4)

If
y=0
, then also
x=0
, and both sides are
0
. Assume
y>0
and put
t:=\frac{x}{y}\in[0,1]
. After dividing by
y^{\alpha}
, it is enough to show that

(1-p)t^{\alpha}+p\leq\bigl((1-q)t+q\bigr)^{\alpha}.

The endpoint cases
t=0
and
t=1
give equality, since
p=q^{\alpha}
. Thus assume that
0<t<1
and write
t=\frac{1}{1+u}
with
u=\frac{1-t}{t}>0
. Then the desired inequality is equivalent to

(1+qu)^{\alpha}-1\geq q^{\alpha}\bigl((1+u)^{\alpha}-1\bigr).

Now define

\phi(u):=\frac{(1+u)^{\alpha}-1}{u^{\alpha}}.

A direct differentiation gives

\phi^{\prime}(u)=\frac{\alpha}{u^{\alpha+1}}\Bigl(u(1+u)^{\alpha-1}-\bigl((1+u)^{\alpha}-1\bigr)\Bigr)=\frac{\alpha\bigl(1-(1+u)^{\alpha-1}\bigr)}{u^{\alpha+1}}\leq 0,

because
\alpha\geq 1
. Hence
\phi
is decreasing on
(0,\infty)
. Since
0<q<1
, we have
qu\leq u
, and therefore
\phi(qu)\geq\phi(u)
. This means

\frac{(1+qu)^{\alpha}-1}{(qu)^{\alpha}}\geq\frac{(1+u)^{\alpha}-1}{u^{\alpha}}.

Multiplying both sides by
(qu)^{\alpha}
yields

(1+qu)^{\alpha}-1\geq q^{\alpha}\bigl((1+u)^{\alpha}-1\bigr).

We now apply (
4
) with

x=\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F}^{0})
and
y=\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F}^{1}).

This choice satisfies
0\leq x\leq y
, because
\mathcal{F}^{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{1}
. Therefore

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})\leq(1-p)x^{\alpha}+py^{\alpha}\leq((1-q)x+qy)^{\alpha}=\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F})^{\alpha},

which completes the induction.

Finally, if

\mathcal{F}=\{A\subseteq[n]:\{1,\ldots,m\}\subseteq A\},

then
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})=p^{m}
and
\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F})=q^{m}
, so equality holds.
∎

3.3
Proof of the product bound

We now turn the critical sum inequalities into the stated product bounds. Theorem
1.1
is the point where the two core lemmas enter directly.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

First consider the case
p=0
. For each
i
, we have
\mu_{0}(\mathcal{F}_{i})=1
if and only if
\varnothing\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
, and otherwise
\mu_{0}(\mathcal{F}_{i})=0
. Therefore, if some family
\mathcal{F}_{i}
does not contain
\varnothing
, then the product is immediately
0
. If all
r
families contained
\varnothing
, then taking
A_{i}=\varnothing\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
for every
i
would contradict the
r
-cross-intersecting condition. So the product is always
0
when
p=0
.

Now assume
0<p\leq\frac{r-1}{r}
. Let
q:=\frac{r-1}{r}
and
\alpha:=\frac{\log p}{\log q}
. Then
\alpha\geq 1
and
p=q^{\alpha}
. For each
i\in[r]
, let
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\uparrow}
be the up-closure of
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Then
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\uparrow}
is increasing and
\mathcal{F}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\uparrow}
, so
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\uparrow})
for any
i\in[r]
. Moreover, the families
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\uparrow},\dots,\mathcal{F}_{r}^{\uparrow}
are still
r
-cross-intersecting. Hence

\begin{split}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})&\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\uparrow})\overset{{\rm Lemma}\penalty\ \ref{lem:comparison}}{\leq}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\uparrow})^{\alpha}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\uparrow})\right)^{\alpha}\\
&\overset{{\rm AM-GM}}{\leq}\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{q}(\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\uparrow})\right)^{r\cdot\alpha}\overset{{\rm Lemma}\penalty\ \ref{lem:critical-sum}}{\leq}\left(\frac{r-1}{r}\right)^{r\alpha}=\left(q^{\frac{\log p}{\log q}}\right)^{r}=p^{r}.\end{split}

This proves the theorem.
∎

4.1
Dual families

For a family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, define its dual family by

\mathcal{A}^{*}:=\{B\subseteq[n]:[n]\setminus B\notin\mathcal{A}\}.

When several families
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{r}
are under consideration, we write

\mathcal{B}_{i}:=\mathcal{A}_{i}^{*}=\{B\subseteq[n]:[n]\setminus B\notin\mathcal{A}_{i}\}.

Studying such dual families is natural in intersection problems. If
\mathcal{A}
is increasing, then

\mathcal{A}^{*}
is exactly the family of all hitting sets, or transversals, for
\mathcal{A}
: one has

B\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
if and only if

B\cap A\neq\varnothing

for every
A\in\mathcal{A}
.
Thus an increasing family
\mathcal{A}
is intersecting if and only if
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{*}
; maximal
intersecting up-sets are precisely the self-dual increasing families
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{*}
. This self-dual viewpoint is classical in the enumeration of maximal intersecting families; see for example
[
2
]
. Dual pairs also appear naturally in probabilistic combinatorics, for instance in the tightness examples for Talagrand-type correlation inequalities studied by Kalai, Keller and Mossel
[
23
]
.

For two increasing families
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, they are cross-intersecting if and only if
\mathcal{A}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{2}
. Consequently
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\leq\mu_{p}(\mathcal{B}_{2})=1-\mu_{1-p}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
, and in particular
\mu_{1/2}(\mathcal{A}_{1})+\mu_{1/2}(\mathcal{A}_{2})\leq 1
. This kind of dual comparison is a standard first step in several stability and threshold arguments for increasing families; see, for instance,
[
7
]
. In the present
r
-cross-intersecting setting, however, the condition is genuinely higher-order and cannot be captured just by pairwise inclusions. The useful replacement is the ordered-partition covering property in Lemma
4.1
below.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
r\geq 2
. Suppose that
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
are increasing and
r
-cross-intersecting, and let
\mathcal{B}_{i}:=\mathcal{A}_{i}^{*}
,
i\in[r]
, be their dual families. Then the following properties hold.

(i)

Every
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is an increasing family.

(ii)

\mu_{1/r}(\mathcal{B}_{i})=1-\mu_{(r-1)/r}(\mathcal{A}_{i})
for every
i\in[r]
.

(iii)

For every ordered partition
P_{1},\dots,P_{r}
of
[n]
into possibly empty parts, there exists some
i\in[r]
such that
P_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
.

Proof.

We first prove (i). Fix
i\in[r]
. Let
B\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
and let
B^{\prime}\supseteq B
. By the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
, we have
[n]\setminus B\notin\mathcal{A}_{i}
. Since
B^{\prime}\supseteq B
, we have
[n]\setminus B^{\prime}\subseteq[n]\setminus B
. If
[n]\setminus B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
, then, as
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is increasing, it would follow that
[n]\setminus B\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
, a contradiction. Hence
[n]\setminus B^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{A}_{i}
, and therefore
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
. Thus
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is increasing.

Next we prove (ii). Let
X\sim\mu_{1/r}
be a random subset of
[n]
. Then
[n]\setminus X\sim\mu_{(r-1)/r}
. Hence, by the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
,

\mu_{1/r}(\mathcal{B}_{i})=\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu_{1/r}}(X\in\mathcal{B}_{i})=\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu_{1/r}}([n]\setminus X\notin\mathcal{A}_{i})=1-\mu_{(r-1)/r}(\mathcal{A}_{i}).

Finally we prove (iii). Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{r}
be an ordered partition of
[n]
into possibly empty parts.
Suppose for contradiction that
P_{i}\notin\mathcal{B}_{i}
for every
i\in[r]
. Then, by the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
, we have
[n]\setminus P_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
for every
i\in[r]
. But since
P_{1},\dots,P_{r}
form a partition of
[n]
, we have
\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}([n]\setminus P_{i})=\varnothing
. This contradicts the assumption that
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{r}
are
r
-cross-intersecting. Therefore
P_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
for some
i\in[r]
, as desired.
∎

Lemma
4.1
(iii) is exactly what is needed for the Fourier argument. If
x\in[r]^{n}
is chosen uniformly and
C_{i}(x):=\{a\in[n]:x_{a}=i\}
, then
C_{1}(x),\dots,C_{r}(x)
form a random ordered partition of
[n]
into possibly empty parts, and each
C_{i}(x)
has distribution
\mu_{1/r}
.
Lemma
4.1
(iii) gives
\sum_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(C_{i}(x))\geq 1
for every

, while near equality in the critical sum inequality gives
\sum_{i}\mu_{1/r}(\mathcal{B}_{i})\leq 1+\eta
. Hence
\sum_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(C_{i}(x))
is close to the constant function
1
. This is the starting point for proving that the high-degree Fourier mass of the dual families is small, and then applying the biased FKN theorem to obtain the structural alternatives in Theorem

\mu_{1/r}(\mathcal{B}_{i})\leq\frac{1}{r}+\frac{C_{\ref{lem:biased-FKN}}(\frac{1}{r})(r-1)^{3}}{r(r-2)}\xi
for every
i\neq s
. Therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{1/r}(\mathcal{B}_{i})\leq\frac{r-1}{r}+\frac{C_{\ref{lem:biased-FKN}}(\frac{1}{r})(r-1)^{3}}{r-2}\xi<1.

This contradicts
\sum_{i}\mu_{1/r}(\mathcal{B}_{i})=1+\xi\geq 1
. Thus every
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is a
1
-star.

Write
\mathcal{H}_{i}=S_{j_{i}}^{\prime}
, where
S_{j_{i}}^{\prime}:=\{B\subseteq[n]:j_{i}\in B\}
. We claim that
j_{1}=\cdots=j_{r}
. Suppose not. For each coordinate
a\in[n]
, let

I_{a}:=\{i\in[r]:j_{i}=a\}.
Thus
I_{a}
is the set of indices whose approximating star has center
a
. Since
j_{1},\dots,j_{r}
are not all equal,
I_{a}\neq[r]
for every
a\in[n]
. Let
J:=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{r}\}
. For every
a\in J
, the set
[r]\setminus I_{a}
is nonempty. Hence we may choose a coloring
x\in[r]^{n}
such that
x_{a}\notin I_{a}
for
a\in J
. Let
G
be the set of all such colorings. Since
|J|\leq r
and each restricted coordinate has at least one available color, we have

\mathbb{P}_{x\sim[r]^{n}}(x\in G)=\prod_{a\in J}\frac{r-|I_{a}|}{r}\geq r^{-|J|}\geq r^{-r}.

Fix
i\in[r]
. Since
i\in I_{j_{i}}
and
x_{j_{i}}\notin I_{j_{i}}
, we have
x_{j_{i}}\neq i
. Therefore
j_{i}\notin C_{i}(x)
, and so
C_{i}(x)\notin S_{j_{i}}^{\prime}

for every
i\in[r]
and every
x\in G
.

On the other hand, for every
x\in[r]^{n}
, the sets
C_{1}(x),\dots,C_{r}(x)
form an ordered partition of
[n]
into possibly empty parts. By Lemma
4.1
(iii), there exists some
i\in[r]
such that
C_{i}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
. Thus, for every
x\in G
, there exists some
i\in[r]
such that
C_{i}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
and
C_{i}(x)\notin S_{j_{i}}^{\prime}
. Hence

G\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\{x\in[r]^{n}:C_{i}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{i}\Delta S_{j_{i}}^{\prime}\}.

Taking probabilities and using that
C_{i}(x)\sim\mu_{1/r}
, we get

r^{-r}\leq\mathbb{P}_{x\sim[r]^{n}}(x\in G)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{1/r}(\mathcal{B}_{i}\Delta S_{j_{i}}^{\prime})\leq\frac{C_{\ref{lem:biased-FKN}}(\frac{1}{r})(r-1)^{3}}{r-2}\eta<\frac{1}{r^{r}}.

Hence
j_{1}=\cdots=j_{r}
, say
j_{i}=j
for every
i\in[r]
.

Finally, we first pass back to the up-closures. If
A=[n]\setminus B
, then
B\in\mathcal{B}_{i}\Leftrightarrow A\notin\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}
, and
B\in\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\prime}\Leftrightarrow A\notin\mathcal{S}_{j}
, where
\mathcal{S}_{j}=\{A\subseteq[n]:j\in A\}
is the original
1
-star. Hence membership in
\mathcal{B}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\prime}
is exactly transformed into membership in
\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}
. Since complementation sends
\mu_{1/r}
to
\mu_{p_{*}}
,

\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j})=\mu_{1/r}(\mathcal{B}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\prime})\leq\frac{C_{\ref{lem:biased-FKN}}(\frac{1}{r})(r-1)^{3}}{r(r-2)}\xi

for every
i\in[r]
. Since
\mathcal{A}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}
, we have

\begin{split}\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j})&\leq\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j})+\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{i})\leq\frac{C_{\ref{lem:biased-FKN}}(\frac{1}{r})(r-1)^{3}}{r(r-2)}\xi+\delta\\
&\leq\frac{C_{\ref{lem:biased-FKN}}(\frac{1}{r})(r-1)^{3}}{r(r-2)}\xi+\eta-\xi\leq C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\eta\end{split}

for every
i\in[r]
. This gives alternative (i), and completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
r\geq 3
,
p_{*}=\frac{r-1}{r}
, and let
0<\eta<\eta_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be increasing and
r
-cross-intersecting. Suppose that

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i})\geq r-1-\eta

and
\left|\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i})-{p_{*}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{4r}
for all
i\in[r]
. Then there exists a
1
-star
\mathcal{S}_{j}
such that

\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j})\leq C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\eta,\quad\forall i\in[r].

Proof.

Applying Theorem
1.3
, either (i) or (ii) holds. If (i) holds, then we are done. We claim that (ii) is impossible. Indeed, in (ii), after permuting the indices, one has
\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\leq C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\eta
. On the other hand, the balanced assumption gives
\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\geq p_{*}-\frac{1}{4r}=\frac{4r-5}{4r}
. This contradicts the definition of
\eta_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)
, since

C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\eta_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)<\frac{4r-5}{4r}
.
∎

4.3
Stability for
r
-cross-intersecting families

In this subsection we prove Theorem
1.2
. The argument mirrors the extremal proof: first we stabilize the AM–GM step at the critical bias, then we identify the near-extremal critical configurations, and finally we transfer the resulting structure back to the original bias.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Set

\varepsilon_{\ref{thm:r-cross-product-stability}}(r):=\min\left\{\frac{1}{64r^{2}(r-1)^{2}},\frac{\eta_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)}{2(r-1)},\frac{1}{r^{2}C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)}\right\}

and

C_{\ref{thm:r-cross-product-stability}}(r):=2+r^{2}(2r+1)C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r).

For each
i\in[r]
, let
\mathcal{G}_{i}
be the up-closure of
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Then
\mathcal{G}_{i}
is increasing and
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i})
. Moreover,
\mathcal{G}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{r}
are still
r
-cross-intersecting.

Let
p_{*}:=\frac{r-1}{r}
and
\alpha:=\frac{\log p}{\log p_{*}}\geq 1
. By Lemma
3.2
,
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\leq\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})^{\alpha}
. Therefore

(1-\varepsilon)p^{r}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})^{\alpha},

and hence,

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\geq p_{*}^{r}(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\geq p_{*}^{r}(1-\varepsilon).

On the other hand, Lemma
3.1
gives
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\leq r-1
. Applying Lemma
2.3
, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})-p_{*})^{2}\leq 4(r-1)^{2}\varepsilon
.
In particular,

|\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})-p_{*}|\leq 2(r-1)\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{4r},\quad\text{ for all }i\in[r],

where the last inequality follows from
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{64r^{2}(r-1)^{2}}
. Moreover, by AM–GM,

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\geq r\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}\geq r{p_{*}}(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{1}{r}}\geq r-1-(r-1)\varepsilon.

Since

(r-1)\varepsilon<\eta_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)
,
Corollary
4.4
, applied with
\eta=(r-1)\varepsilon
, gives a coordinate
j\in[n]
such that

\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j})\leq C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)(r-1)\varepsilon,\quad\text{ for all }i\in[r].

We next convert this
p_{*}
-biased closeness into
p
-biased closeness. Relabeling the coordinates if necessary, assume that
\mathcal{S}_{j}=\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{A\subseteq[n]:n\in A\}
. For each
i\in[r]
, define
\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0}:=\{A\subseteq[n-1]:A\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\}
and
\mathcal{G}_{i}^{1}:=\{A\subseteq[n-1]:A\cup\{n\}\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\}
. Since
\mathcal{G}_{i}
is increasing,
\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{i}^{1}
. Moreover,

\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{n})=(1-p_{*})\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0})+{p_{*}}\bigl(1-\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{1})\bigr)\leq C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)(r-1)\varepsilon.

In particular,

\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0})\leq C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)r(r-1)\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{\ref{thm:r-cross-product-stability}}\leq\frac{1}{r^{2}C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)}
, we have
C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)r(r-1)\varepsilon\leq p_{*}
. The slice
\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0}
is increasing, so by Lemma
3.2
, and using
p=p_{*}^{\alpha}
,

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0})\leq\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0})^{\alpha}=p\left(\frac{\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0})}{p_{*}}\right)^{\alpha}\leq p\left(\frac{C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)r(r-1)\varepsilon}{p_{*}}\right)\leq pr^{2}C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\varepsilon.

(7)

Here we used
\mu_{p_{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0})\leq p_{*}
and
\alpha\geq 1
.
Hence,

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i})=(1-p)\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0})+p\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{1})\leq p(1+r^{2}C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\varepsilon),\quad\text{ for all }i\in[r].

(8)

Combining (
8
) with
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\geq(1-\varepsilon)p^{r}
gives, for every
i\in[r]
,

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\geq\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)p^{r}}{\left(p(1+r^{2}C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\varepsilon)\right)^{r-1}}\geq p\left(1-\left(1+r^{2}(r-1)C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\right)\varepsilon\right).

(9)

The last inequality uses
(1+x)^{-(r-1)}\geq 1-(r-1)x
for
x\geq 0
.
Using (
7
) and (
9
), we obtain

\begin{split}p\bigl(1-\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{1})\bigr)&=p+(1-p)\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0})-\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\\
&\leq p\left(1+r^{2}(r-1)C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\right)\varepsilon+(1-p)\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0})\\
&\leq p\left(1+r^{3}C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\right)\varepsilon.\end{split}

Therefore,

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{n})=(1-p)\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{0})+p\bigl(1-\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{1})\bigr)\leq p\left(1+r^{2}(r+1)C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\right)\varepsilon.

It remains to pass from the up-closures
\mathcal{G}_{i}
back to the original families

\mathcal{F}_{i}
. By (
8
) and (
9
),

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{i})=\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i})-\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq p\left(1+r^{3}C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\right)\varepsilon.

Finally, for all
i\in[r]
,

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{n})\leq\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{n})+\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq p\left(2+r^{2}(2r+1)C_{\ref{lem:q-stability-r}}(r)\right)\varepsilon=pC_{\ref{thm:r-cross-product-stability}}(r)\varepsilon.

This proves the theorem.
∎

5
Concluding Remarks

5.1
A biased-to-uniform consequence

We record a short consequence of our biased product theorem in the uniform setting. The
corresponding uniform product bound is already known by the theorem of Frankl and
Tokushige
[
15
]
; the point here is only that the asymptotic form, together with a stability statement, follows directly from Theorems
1.1
and

1.2
. The argument is the standard biased-to-uniform passage:
take up-closures and use the local LYM inequality at a parameter slightly larger than
k/n
. For
j\in[n]
, write

\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(k)}:=\left\{A\in\binom{[n]}{k}:j\in A\right\}.

Corollary 5.1

(Asymptotic uniform product bound)
.

Let
r\geq 2
, and let
k=k(n)
satisfy

\frac{k}{n}\to\alpha
for some
0<\alpha<\frac{r-1}{r}.

If

\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{r}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}

are
r
-cross-intersecting, then

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{i}|}{\binom{n}{k}}\leq\alpha^{r}.

Proof.

For each
i\in[r]
, let
\mathcal{G}_{i}:=\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}
. Then

\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{r}
are
r
-cross-intersecting. Fix

p\in\left(\alpha,\frac{r-1}{r}\right).

For all sufficiently large
n
, we have
k/n<p
. Put

T_{n}:=\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)\geq k).

By the local LYM inequality, for every
\ell\geq k
,

\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{i}\cap\binom{[n]}{\ell}|}{\binom{n}{\ell}}\geq\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{i}|}{\binom{n}{k}}.

Therefore, conditioning on the size of a
p
-random set,

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)=\ell)\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{i}\cap\binom{[n]}{\ell}|}{\binom{n}{\ell}}\geq T_{n}\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{i}|}{\binom{n}{k}}.

Applying Theorem
1.1
to
\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{r}
, we get

T_{n}^{r}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{i}|}{\binom{n}{k}}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\leq p^{r}.

Since
\frac{k}{n}\to\alpha<p
, the law of large numbers gives
T_{n}\to 1
. Thus

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{i}|}{\binom{n}{k}}\leq p^{r}.

Letting
p\downarrow\alpha
completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.2

(Uniform stability)
.

Let
r\geq 3
, and let
0<a<b<\frac{r-1}{r}
. Let
k=k(n)
satisfy

a\leq\frac{k}{n}\leq b.

Let

\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{r}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}

be
r
-cross-intersecting. If

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{i}|}{\binom{n}{k}}\geq(1-o(1))\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{r},

then there exists
j\in[n]
such that, for every
i\in[r]
,

\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(k)}|}{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}=o(1).

Proof.

Write
\alpha_{n}:=\frac{k}{n}
,
p_{n}:=\alpha_{n}+n^{-1/3}
and
d_{i}:=\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{i}|}{\binom{n}{k}}.

For all sufficiently large
n
, we have
p_{n}\leq(r-1)/r
. Let

\mathcal{G}_{i}:=\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\uparrow}
. As in the proof of Corollary
5.1
,

\mu_{p_{n}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\geq d_{i}T_{n},
where

T_{n}:=\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Bin}(n,p_{n})\geq k).

Since
p_{n}-\alpha_{n}=n^{-1/3}
, Chernoff’s inequality gives
T_{n}\to 1
. Hence

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{n}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\geq T_{n}^{r}\prod_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\geq(1-o(1))\alpha_{n}^{r}=(1-o(1))p_{n}^{r}.

By Theorem
1.2
, applied at bias
p_{n}
, there exists
a
1
-star
\mathcal{S}_{j}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
such that, for every
i\in[r]
,

\mu_{p_{n}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j})=o(1).

It remains to return to the
k
-th level. Fix
i\in[r]
, and set

\mathcal{B}_{i}:=\mathcal{A}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(k)}
and
b_{i}:=\frac{|\mathcal{B}_{i}|}{\binom{n}{k}}.

View
\mathcal{B}_{i}
as a family of
k
-subsets of
[n]\setminus\{j\}
, and let

\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\uparrow}
be its up-closure inside
[n]\setminus\{j\}
. Then

\{X\subseteq[n]:j\notin X,\ X\in\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\uparrow}\}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{j}.

By local LYM on
[n]\setminus\{j\}
,

\begin{split}\mu_{p_{n}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{j})&\geq(1-p_{n})\frac{|\mathcal{B}_{i}|}{\binom{n-1}{k}}\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Bin}(n-1,p_{n})\geq k)\\
&=(1-p_{n})\frac{b_{i}}{1-\alpha_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Bin}(n-1,p_{n})\geq k).\end{split}

The binomial tail on the right tends to
1
, while
1-p_{n}
and
1-\alpha_{n}
are bounded
away from
0
. Since

\mu_{p_{n}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{j})\leq\mu_{p_{n}}(\mathcal{G}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j})=o(1),

we get

b_{i}=o(1).

Thus
d_{i}\leq\alpha_{n}+o(1)

for every
i\in[r].

The product lower bound then gives, for every
i\in[r]
,

d_{i}\geq\frac{(1-o(1))\alpha_{n}^{r}}{(\alpha_{n}+o(1))^{r-1}}=(1-o(1))\alpha_{n},

using
\alpha_{n}\geq a>0
. Finally, if

m_{i}:=\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(k)}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{i}|}{\binom{n}{k}},

then

d_{i}=\alpha_{n}-m_{i}+b_{i}.

Since
d_{i}=(1-o(1))\alpha_{n}
and
b_{i}=o(1)
, we have
m_{i}=o(1)
. Hence

\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(k)}|}{\binom{n}{k}}=b_{i}+m_{i}=o(1).

Since
\binom{n-1}{k-1}=\alpha_{n}\binom{n}{k}
and
\alpha_{n}\geq a
, the same conclusion holds with normalization by
\binom{n-1}{k-1}
.
∎

5.2
A partial unequal-bias consequence

We next record a simple consequence in the direction of the full Frankl–Tokushige
conjecture. The argument gives a nontrivial unequal-bias range when the logarithmic biases
are sufficiently balanced.

Corollary 5.3

(A log-balanced unequal-bias range)
.

Let
r\geq 2
, and let
0<p_{i}<1
for
i\in[r]
. Let
q_{0}:=\max_{i\in[r]}p_{i}
,
L_{i}:=-\log p_{i}
and
L:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}L_{i}
. Suppose
s:=\left\lceil\frac{1}{1-q_{0}}\right\rceil\leq r
and
\max_{i\in[r]}L_{i}\leq\frac{L}{s}.

Then every
r
-cross-intersecting tuple

\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]}

satisfies

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}.

Proof.

If some
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is empty, the result is immediate. We may therefore assume that all

\mathcal{F}_{i}
’s are non-empty. As
s\leq r
, every
s
-subcollection is
s
-cross-intersecting. For each
i\in[r]
, let
\mathcal{G}_{i}:=\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\uparrow}
. Since

q_{0}\leq\frac{s-1}{s},

Theorem
1.1
, applied to every
s
-subcollection, gives

\prod_{i\in E}\mu_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\leq q_{0}^{s}

for every
E\in\binom{[r]}{s}.

If
\mu_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})=0
for some
i
, then
\mu_{p_{i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})=0
, and there is nothing to
prove. Thus assume
\mu_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})>0
for all
i
. Define
a_{i}:=\frac{\log\mu_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})}{\log q_{0}}.

Since
0<q_{0}<1
, the preceding inequalities are equivalent to

\sum_{i\in E}a_{i}\geq s

for every
E\in\binom{[r]}{s}.

Next write

p_{i}=q_{0}^{\alpha_{i}}
and
\alpha_{i}:=\frac{\log p_{i}}{\log q_{0}}.

By Lemma
3.2
,

\mu_{p_{i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq\mu_{p_{i}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})\leq\mu_{q_{0}}(\mathcal{G}_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}=q_{0}^{\alpha_{i}a_{i}}.

Thus it is enough to prove

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}a_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}.

Let
A:=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}
. The logarithmic balance assumption is exactly

\max_{i}\alpha_{i}\leq\frac{A}{s}.

Set

w_{i}:=\frac{s\alpha_{i}}{A}.

Then
0\leq w_{i}\leq 1
and
\sum_{i}w_{i}=s
. Hence

(w_{1},\ldots,w_{r})
lies in the hypersimplex

\left\{w\in[0,1]^{r}:\sum_{i}w_{i}=s\right\},

which is the convex hull of the incidence vectors of the
s
-subsets of
[r]
. Therefore
there are non-negative coefficients
\lambda_{E}
,
E\in\binom{[r]}{s}
, with

\sum_{E}\lambda_{E}=1
, such that

w=\sum_{E\in\binom{[r]}{s}}\lambda_{E}\mathbbm{1}_{E}.

It follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}a_{i}=\frac{A}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}a_{i}=\frac{A}{s}\sum_{E\in\binom{[r]}{s}}\lambda_{E}\sum_{i\in E}a_{i}\geq\frac{A}{s}\sum_{E\in\binom{[r]}{s}}\lambda_{E}s=A.

Consequently,

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p_{i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq q_{0}^{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}a_{i}}\leq q_{0}^{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}.

∎

The condition in Corollary
5.3
is genuinely unequal-bias when

q_{0}
is bounded away from the critical value
(r-1)/r
. For instance, if
q_{0}\leq 1/2
,
then
s=2
, and the condition becomes

\max_{i}(-\log p_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-\log p_{j}).

However, in the top range

\frac{r-2}{r-1}<q_{0}\leq\frac{r-1}{r},

we have
s=r
, and the balance condition forces

p_{1}=\cdots=p_{r}.

Thus this corollary does not reach the hardest part of the Frankl–Tokushige conjecture; a
new unequal-bias ingredient seems necessary there.

5.3
Index-hypergraph product bound

We can use Theorem
1.1
to prove a general index-hypergraph formulation of the product bound.
The hypergraph records which subcollections are required to be cross-intersecting, and the exponent in the upper bound is determined by the corresponding fractional cover linear program.

Theorem 5.4

(Index-hypergraph product bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a non-empty hypergraph on vertex set
[r]
, all of whose edges have size at least
2
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{F}_{r}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, and suppose that

0\leq p\leq\min_{E\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{|E|-1}{|E|}.

Suppose that for every edge
E\in\mathcal{H}
, the subcollection
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}:i\in E\}
is
|E|
-cross-intersecting. Define

\tau(\mathcal{H}):=\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}:a_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }i,\ \sum_{i\in E}a_{i}\geq|E|\text{ for every }E\in\mathcal{H}\right\}.

Then

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq p^{\tau(\mathcal{H})}.

Proof.

If
p=0
, then, for any edge
E\in\mathcal{H}
, the families
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}:i\in E\}
are
|E|
-cross-intersecting, so they cannot all contain
\varnothing
. Hence at least one family has
\mu_{0}
-measure
0
, and the product is
0
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is non-empty, we have
\tau(\mathcal{H})>0
, and therefore
p^{\tau(\mathcal{H})}=0
.

Now assume
0<p<1
. If
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})=0
for some
i
, the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise define
a_{i}:=\frac{\log\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})}{\log p},
so that
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})=p^{a_{i}}.

Since
0<\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq 1
and
0<p<1
, we have
a_{i}\geq 0
. For every edge
E\in\mathcal{H}
, Theorem
1.1
, applied to the
|E|
-cross-intersecting subcollection
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}:i\in E\}
, gives

\prod_{i\in E}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq p^{|E|}.

Equivalently,
\sum_{i\in E}a_{i}\geq|E|
. Thus
(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})
is feasible in the linear program defining
\tau(\mathcal{H})
, and hence
\sum_{i}a_{i}\geq\tau(\mathcal{H})
. Therefore

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})=p^{\sum_{i}a_{i}}\leq p^{\tau(\mathcal{H})},

as required.
∎

There is a fractional matching interpretation.
By linear programming duality,

\tau(\mathcal{H})=\max\left\{\sum_{E\in\mathcal{H}}|E|y_{E}:y_{E}\geq 0\text{ for all }E,\ \sum_{E\ni i}y_{E}\leq 1\text{ for every }i\in[r]\right\}.

In particular, if
\mathcal{H}
is
q
-uniform, then
\tau(\mathcal{H})=q\nu^{*}(\mathcal{H})
, where
\nu^{*}(\mathcal{H})
is the fractional matching number of
\mathcal{H}
.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a non-empty
q
-uniform hypergraph on
[r]
with independence number
\alpha(\mathcal{H})
, where
q\geq 2
. Suppose that
0\leq p\leq(q-1)/q
and that, for every
E\in\mathcal{H}
, the families
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}:i\in E\}
are
q
-cross-intersecting. Then

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq p^{r-\alpha(\mathcal{H})+q-1}.

Proof.

It suffices to prove
\tau(\mathcal{H})\geq r-\alpha(\mathcal{H})+q-1
. Let
(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})
be any feasible vector in the definition of
\tau(\mathcal{H})
, and reorder the coordinates so that
a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{r}
. Put
\alpha:=\alpha(\mathcal{H})
. Since every set of
\alpha+1
vertices contains an edge of
\mathcal{H}
, the set
\{1,\dots,\alpha+1\}
contains some edge
E
. As
\mathcal{H}
is
q
-uniform,
|E|=q
, and hence

\sum_{j=\alpha-q+2}^{\alpha+1}a_{j}\geq\sum_{i\in E}a_{i}\geq q.

In particular,
a_{\alpha+1}\geq 1
, and therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\geq\sum_{j=\alpha-q+2}^{\alpha+1}a_{j}+\sum_{j=\alpha+2}^{r}a_{j}\geq q+(r-\alpha-1)=r-\alpha+q-1.

Taking the minimum over all feasible vectors gives
\tau(\mathcal{H})\geq r-\alpha(\mathcal{H})+q-1
. The result now follows from Theorem
5.4
.
∎

A simple special case of Corollary
5.5
resembles the classical

(p,q)
-property in discrete geometry: among any
p
sets in a family, some
q
of them have a common point. The Hadwiger–Debrunner problem and the Alon–Kleitman
(p,q)
-theorem study the global piercing consequences of this local intersection assumption
[
1
,
22
]
. Suppose that among every
k
of the families there are
q
which are

q
-cross-intersecting. Define the
q
-uniform index hypergraph

\mathcal{H}:=\left\{Q\in\binom{[r]}{q}:\{\mathcal{F}_{i}:i\in Q\}\text{ is }q\text{-cross-intersecting}\right\}.

Then every
k
-subset of
[r]
contains an edge of
\mathcal{H}
, so

\alpha(\mathcal{H})\leq k-1.

Hence Corollary
5.5
gives, for

p\leq(q-1)/q
,

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq p^{r-\alpha(\mathcal{H})+q-1}\leq p^{r-k+q}.

This exponent is tight: take
k-q
families to be
2^{[n]}
and the remaining

r-k+q
families to be the same
1
-star.

5.4
Further questions

We end with several directions suggested by the proof.

•

Unequal biases.

The unequal-bias case of the Frankl–Tokushige conjecture remains open.
It would be interesting to find a genuinely multi-bias analogue of the critical-bias
coupling or of the additive estimate in Lemma
3.1
.

•

One-sided stability.

Theorem
1.2
gives a symmetric-difference bound

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i}\Delta\mathcal{S}_{j})\leq C_{r}\varepsilon p.

The deletion example shows that a linear bound is necessary for symmetric difference.
However, it may still be possible to prove a stronger one-sided estimate for the mass
outside the approximating star,
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{j}),

separating “added” sets outside the star from “deleted” sets inside the star.

•

t
-wise common intersection.

What is the correct product theorem for families satisfying

|A_{1}\cap\cdots\cap A_{r}|\geq t

for every
A_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
? The present coordinatewise coupling forces the common
intersection to be empty and is therefore naturally adapted to the case
t=1
. The
case
t>1
likely requires a different coupling or a different critical inequality.
