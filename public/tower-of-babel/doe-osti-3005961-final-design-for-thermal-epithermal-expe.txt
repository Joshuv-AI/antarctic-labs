Executive Summary
One of the main goals of the Thermal/Epithermal eXperiments (TEX) project is to use existing Nuclear
Criticality Safety Program (NCSP) assets to create critical experiment plutonium and uranium test beds for
materials important to criticality safety that have insufficient benchmark evaluations. The plutonium test
bed experiments were completed in 2018 and are published in the 2020 edition of the International
Criticality Safety Benchmark Evaluation Project (ICSBEP) Handbook. The uranium test bed assemblies
were completed in 2023 and accepted in the 2024 edition of the ICSBEP Handbook.
The Nuclear Criticality Safety (NCS) group at Y-12 National Security Complex has identified
programmatic need for validation cases for uranium electrorefining operations at Y-12. The electrorefining
operation credits lithium enriched in 6Li in addition to 35Cl as absorbers in the design criticality safety
evaluation for precluding criticality under upset conditions in the large and geometrically unfavorable
electro-refiner. There is, however, inadequate experimental validation for the 6Li absorbers. As an extension
of the TEX uranium test bed, TEX-Cl critical experiments were performed with sodium chloride salt to
address the 35Cl thermal absorption as well as other validation needs at Los Alamos National Laboratory
(LANL). These experiments were completed in 2024 and accepted into the 2025 ICSBEP Handbook. To
continue the methodology used in TEX-Cl, TEX-Li aims to accomplish the same. The overall design of
both experiments was to use commercially available, high purity, salts with polyethylene moderator and
HEU plates to configure a critical assembly.
Three experiments are planned for TEX-Li using encapsulated lithium carbonate (Li2CO3), with natural 6Li
abundance. For all experiments, the highly enriched uranium (HEU) Jemima plates will be used as fissile
material. Multiple layers will be stacked together with encapsulated Li 2CO3 alternated with polyethylene
in standard configurations. Standard stacking was found to be optimal in matching the different sensitivity
profiles provided by the Y-12 models. Three configurations are proposed with varying polyethylene
moderation and a constant 1/4” absorber thickness. The first uses 11 layers of 5/4” polyethylene, the second
uses 9 layers of 3/4” polyethylene, and the third uses 10 layers of 1/2” polyethylene.
Calculations showed that some alternative forms of lithium-based materials provided slightly less-optimal
sensitivity profiles when compared to lithium carbonate but come with other drawbacks. These alternatives
included lithium aluminate (LiAlO2), Aluminum-2050 alloy, Aluminum-8090, Aluminum-2095, lithium
hydride (LiH), and lithium fluoride (LiF). Lithium aluminate and aluminum-2050 provided comparable
sensitivity profiles when compared to lithium carbonate and can be used instead if lithium carbonate cannot
be readily procured. After a broad material study, lithium carbonate outperformed any alternative material
with a balance in affordability and workability.
The assessment of experimental uncertainties of the non-absorber and absorber components was predicted
to be 0.00089 and 0.00093 Δkeff, respectively. The largest uncertainties may be reduced with precision
dimensional inspection of the components.
Many of the parts and equipment for IER 575 have already been fabricated or procured for previous projects
and therefore do not contribute significantly to the overall cost of this experiment. This includes the Jemima
plates and Comet critical assembly machine, which are existing NCSP assets, as well as the aluminum
platen and polyethylene reflector rings, which were fabricated and authorized for the TEX experiment
involving HEU with polyethylene. Lithium carbonate containers will be procured by LANL and will be
filled by LLNL. The total material costs for TEX-Li experiments are estimated to be on the order of
$47,400. Precision inspection, including dimensional, mass, density, and impurity, is recommended for all
components for an estimated cost of $12,000.

Section 1
Introduction

1.1 Overview
The design of the Thermal/Epithermal eXperiments (TEX) project is to be modular in nature and allow for the
execution of many critical experiments with plutonium and uranium test beds with interstitial materials
important to criticality safety and nuclear data applications. TEX-HEU was the first set of the uranium test
beds which provided several baseline configurations that span the entire neutron energy spectrum. These
experiments included only HEU and interstitial polyethylene and provided a reference for further experiments
with diluents, which included hafnium, chlorine, and lithium [1]. TEX-Hf [2] and TEX-Cl [3] were both
completed and accepted into the International Criticality Safety Benchmark Evaluation Project Handbook
(ICSBEP) in 2023 and 2025, respectively [4].
The Nuclear Criticality Safety (NCS) group at Y-12 National Security Complex has identified programmatic
need for validating cases of operations involving lithium in their uranium electro-fining operations at Y-12. The
NCS evaluation credited lithium enriched in 6Li and 35Cl as absorbers to preclude criticality under upset
conditions in the large and geometrically unfavorable electro-refiner. There was, however, inadequate
experimental validation for these absorbers and specifically none for 6Li. In 2024 a series of TEX-Cl
experiments were conducted to address 35Cl gaps, with the benchmark being accepted in April of 2025 by the
ICSBEP Technical Review Group (TRG) for inclusion in the 2025 ICSBEP Handbook release. As an extension
of the TEX-HEU design [1], this report documents the final design of the uranium-fueled TEX variant with
lithium diluent to provide validation cases that will be responsive to the Y-12’s needs.
Figure 1 shows the thermal capture (n,t), elastic, and total cross section profiles for 6Li. In the thermal region,
the 6Li(n,t)He-4 reaction is the dominate capture process (up to ~2 MeV) and will be used in this report
interchangeably to describe all capture reactions.
 The 6Li(n,t) cross-section is ~34% greater than the fission cross-section of 235U in the thermal energy range.
As this report will show, this high absorption cross-section of 6Li has a competing effect and will only allow
for small amounts of Li to design a TEX experiment that is both sensitive to perturbations in the thermal capture
cross section and can still achieve criticality.

In March of 2020, LLNL collaborated with Y-12’s Development group to design and use enriched lithium-6
metal plates for the experiment. However, in July of 2021, this was abandoned due to supply chain issues and
available inventory. The Development group then recommended the design and fabrication of LiH wafer plates
and their associated encapsulations. In April of 2022, LLNL visited the Development group at Y-12, which
showed significant effort had been made in the machining of the encapsulations as they had prototypes on hand.
Y-12 also presented LLNL the machining methodology of the LiH wafers once the material arrived. However,
in January of 2023, when the LiH material was received, it was found that the wafers could not be machined
reliably at the design thickness and were required to be three times thicker. This tripled the amount of lithium
in the system and the proposed configurations could not be made critical with that amount of lithium.
Ultimately, the LiH and lithium metal designs were abandoned due to procurement and manufacturing issues.
The full report of the lessons learned from these endeavors are included in Appendix A. LLNL continued to
search for available lithium based absorber materials and identified several candidates for consideration.
Possible candidates included, but are not limited to, lithium carbonate (Li2CO3) , 6Li enriched lithium carbonate
(6Li2CO3) , aluminum-2050 alloy (Al-2050), aluminum-2095 (Al-2095), aluminum-8090 alloy (Al-8090),
lithium aluminate (LiAlO2) , enriched lithium hydride (6LiH) , and lithium fluoride (LiF). These materials were
then further down-selected based on cost, availability, workability, applicability, and sensitivity optimization.
Ultimately, lithium carbonate was selected for this experimental design as it was superior to other candidates
with the above criteria.
The lithium based aluminum alloys provided comparable sensitivity profiles to lithium carbonate but were
disregarded due to supply chain and procurement challenges. It was found that lithium aluminum alloys with
higher lithium content are considered only in legacy aerospace applications and are no longer in production
outside of custom orders, which require a minimum order that far exceeded the quantity and cost of this
experimental application.

Lithium aluminate also provided comparable sensitivity profiles to that of lithium carbonate but was known to
be hygroscopic and was not as readily available commercially at comparable purity and grades. Additionally,
lithium aluminate was slightly more expensive and provided less flexibility in vendor selection.
Lithium fluoride was disbanded shortly after being considered due to its inherent production of hydrogen
fluoride (HF) gas, which is highly corrosive and toxic as a gas or liquid. In terms of workability, LiF would
have required extensive monitoring for HF gas and was ultimately eliminated as a candidate.
Lithium carbonate was the superior choice as it is a stable lithium-based salt that is used in battery
manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, glass manufacturing, and other specialized industrial applications. It is most
readily available at natural and enriched 6Li contents with purity ranging from 99.0% to 99.999% (trace metal
basis). Purity levels are defined by the application for which they are used, including classification by the
American Chemical Society as an ACS reagent [6]. For this design study, only pure lithium carbonate was
considered.
To address concerns of hygroscopicity, LLNL conducted scoping studies of an 8.32 g (± 0.01 g) lithium
carbonate sample for over 70 days by leaving the sample in an open aluminum planchet. The atmosphere was
uncontrolled and only temperature was regulated at approximately 68 degrees Fahrenheit. Other characteristics
of the atmosphere, including humidity, were not measured. The sample was periodically measured using the
2.2 kg Ohaus Compact Bench Scale which has an accuracy of ± 0.01 g on the direct readout and a repeatability
of ± 0.01 g (PX4202/E). Prior to each measurement, the scale was tested with Rice Lake calibration weights
(model 12638) ranging from 1 g to 5 kg. The study concluded that there were no notable changes in salt
structure, form, or measured mass, and the final mass was measured to be 8.31 g ± 0.01 g. A similar study was
conducted with a lithium aluminate sample and yielded the same results.
Because calculations show system reactivity to be highly dependent on lithium content, LLNL also conducted
an additional study to determine the lower bound of expected density following absorber plate packing. Using
a tabletop vibration plate and graduated cylinder, the achievable empirical density of Li2CO3 was determined to
be approximately 45% of theoretical density (2.11 g/cm3) or 0.95 g/cm3. While the density of the lithium
carbonate had little effect on the sensitivity profiles of the proposed configurations, it did have a significant
contribution to changes in reactivity.
Three standard stacking configurations are proposed which all include a 1/4” (0.635 cm) absorber thickness
within the aluminum containers. The first used 5/4” (3.175 cm) interstitial polyethylene moderator plates with
an estimated 10 total layers (Li-5/4), the second uses 3/4” (1.905 cm) interstitial polyethylene moderator plates
with an estimated 9 total layers (Li-3/4), and the third uses 1/2” (1.27 cm) interstitial polyethylene moderator
plates with an estimated 11 total layers (Li-1/2). The different configurations address different neutron energy
spectra to maximize similarity to Y-12’s proposed systems.
The assessment of experimental uncertainties of the non-absorber and absorber components was predicted to
be 0.00089 and 0.00093 Δkeff, respectively. The largest uncertainties may be reduced with precision
dimensional inspection of the components.
Some HDPE components will be reused from previous benchmarking efforts, namely the HEU-Baseline
configurations, but additional HDPE moderator plates will be procured. Lithium carbonate containers will be
procured by LANL and will be filled by LLNL. The total material costs for TEX-Li experiments are estimated
to be on the order of $46,900. Precision inspection, including dimensional, mass, density, and impurity, is
recommended for all components for an estimated cost of $12,000.Y-12 Electrorefining Operations

1.2 Y-12 Validation Needs
The Y-12 National Security Complex is currently undertaking plans to significantly modify and modernize the
recovery, purification, and consolidation of un-irradiated highly enriched uranium (HEU) metal. One of the
largest changes planned involves shifting from a wet chemistry recovery and purification of HEU to a
pyrochemical process of electrorefining (ER). The ER process uses large geometry vessels of molten lithium

chloride (LiCl) salts as the medium through which the purified HEU migrates. The lithium is enriched in 6Li
(>90%) to take advantage of the higher absorption cross section of 6Li vice 7Li, the latter of which is the most
abundant isotope in natural lithium.
Computer code validations in current use at Y-12 for HEU cover a wide variety of materials, geometry, and
neutron spectra. However, the current lack of experimental criticality benchmarks that involve chlorides or 6Li
as a majority contributor to neutron absorption limit the credit that can be taken for these absorbers in a
criticality safety evaluation.
Of particular interest to Y-12 is the minimum critical mass of uranium in spherical geometry as a function of
the molar ratio of 6Li to 235U, regardless of its volume. To investigate this, Y-12 uses a spherical geometry of a
various aqueous solutions of UO2Cl2 and 6LiCl mixed with contiguous reflection by a 30.48 cm thickness of
ordinary water at a density of 0.9982 g/cm3 as shown by Figure 2 [7]. Each model has a specific 6Li/235U ratio
of 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, or 0.4. Table 1 shows the parameters of these models. The increase in 6Li/235U ratio increased
the critical radius of the spherical geometry which is expected due to the high absorption cross section of 6Li.
The increase in ratio also noticeably increased the absorption sensitivity of 6Li while having a smaller impact
on the absorption sensitivity of 35Cl.
This study aims to design a series of TEX experiments that are sensitive to 6Li thermal cross-section using
natural lithium abundance and provide maximum similarity in sensitivity profiles to the profiles from the Y-12
electrorefining system. Though this will be difficult to match perfectly as Y-12’s sensitivity profiles resemble
a near perfect absorber to fissile ratio. Similar challenges were experienced in designing optimized critical
experiments with effective absorber materials in the Chlorine Worth Study design [8]. Figure 3 show the Y-12
absorption sensitivity profiles of 6Li, which demonstrates that increasing the 6Li/235U ratio from 0.1 to 0.4
resulted in a 3 times larger peak absorption sensitivity to 6Li.

2.1 Assembly Machine
The Comet General Purpose Critical Assembly Machine is a vertical lift machine (VLM) used to remotely
assemble a critical experiment. As shown in Figure 4, Comet consists of the surrounding structure, stationary
platform, and movable platen.
During assembly, roughly half of the experiment is built on the experiment platform with the other half on the
lower adapter. During operation, the movable platen is extended vertically to bring the two halves of the
experiment into contact. Once fully assembled, the two halves are only separated by a thin aluminum membrane.

 Figure 4: Comet General Purpose Critical Assembly Machine with additional TEX-HEU parts.

2.1.1 Experiment Platform
The experiment platform holds the upper half of the experiment, shown in Figure 5. The platform consists of
an interface plate and four standoffs, which connect the interface plate to the stationary platform. The membrane
is placed on top of the hole in the interface plate, allowing the movable platen to lift the lower half of the
experiment as it meets the upper half of the experiment through the membrane. The interface plate includes

2.1.2 Movable Platen
The lower adapter holds the lower half of the experiment, shown in Figure 6 and Figure 7. This lower adapter

2.2 Jemima Plate Description
Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) has used thin (3 mm thickness) solid and annular disks of U(93+),
collectively known as the Jemima plates, in many critical experiments. Solid 15” diameter plates along with
similar plates with a 2.5” diameter central hole were manufactured by LANL in the mid-1950s and used in the
Jemima experiments (IEU-MET-FAST-002) [11]. Some of the original 15” plates were modified with 6” and
10” diameter central holes for the series of Big Ten experiments at LANL [12]. The Jemima plates have also
been used to create intermediate spectra benchmarks in the Zeus experiments [13-15].
The available inventory for the HEU plates is shown in Table 2 with reported masses, and in Table 3 with
reported nominal dimensions [16]. The current 15” OD plate inventory is made up of the equivalent of 7 solid
plates, 7 2.5” ID plates, 5 6” ID plates, and 8 10” ID plates. One of the 7 solid plate equivalents is made up of
6 wedges and one of them is made up of the combination of a 6” ID plate and a solid 6” OD plate. The masses
and dimensions of these plates are taken from LANL’s characterization of the HEU plates in support of the
Critical Experiment Reflected By copper to bEtteR Understand Scattering (CERBERUS) experiment [16, 17].

2.3 Polyethylene Reflectors and Moderators

on the top and bottom of the stack, as well as cylindrical shells around the sides. The polyethylene reflector on
the top of the assembly is a cylinder with an outer diameter of 15”. The thickness of the top reflector can be
increased to 2” in increments of 1/16” to increase reactivity of the assembly. The 1”-thick reflector around the
side of the assembly is made up of stacked cylinders with 15.1” inner diameter, 17.1” outer diameter, and
various heights ranging from 1/32” to 3”, as shown in Table 6. The 3”-tall cylinders will be added first as the
assembly is stacked, with the smaller cylinders used to precisely adjust the reflector height to match the height
of TEX model stack to within the nearest 1/32”.To ensure that the assembly remains centered throughout the
experiment, and to provide extra stability, the cylindrical reflectors have stacking step joints on their top and

2.4 Lithium Plate Description
Lithium Carbonate salts (Li2CO3) will be used for the TEX-Li experiments as the source of lithium. Other
materials such as aluminum alloys (2050, 2095, and 8090), lithium aluminate (LiAlO2), lithium chloride (LiCl)
lithium fluoride (LiF), and enriched lithium hydride (LiH), were also considered as possible candidates and
calculations were performed to review their performance. However, these alternate materials were removed
from consideration based on the down-selection process as described in Section 1.
The methodology in using a powder-based material was adapted from TEX-Cl [18], which demonstrated that a
critical configuration with high fidelity can be designed around sodium chloride salts. Lithium carbonate
powder is readily available from many distributors with varying grades of purity. Because trace impurities with
other neutron absorbers may exist in lower grades, TEX-Li will utilize lithium carbonate of at least ACS reagent
or battery grades (purities >99.0% and >99.9% on trace metal basis, respectively). Major impurities found in
such grades are 0.2% Sulfur compounds and <100 ppm total metallic impurities [19]. Depending on the
distributor, these impurities may be specifically addressed in product specification.
Based on packing experiences from the TEX-Cl [3] experiments, LLNL conducted a study to observe the
empirical density of a sample of lithium carbonate. It was found that 45% of theoretical density was achievable
using a simple table-top vibration plate. This density could be increased to >60% of theoretical through using
a small tapper, filtering grain sizes to achieve a finer powder, or using a pneumatic vibrator as in TEX-Cl. TEX-
Cl found that the empirical density for sodium chloride (NaCl), which is a similar form and grain size to Li2CO3,

Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory Unclassified 19

Final Design for Thermal/Epithermal eXperiments (TEX) with Lithium Absorbers to Provide Validation Benchmarks for Y-12 Electrorefining Facility

was determined to be approximately 60% of theoretical density [3]. As shown later in Section 3.5, changes in
density have little to no effect on the sensitivity profiles but will have a significant contribution to system
reactivity. Ultimately, the goal of the packing procedure is to achieve the highest lithium carbonate density.
This ensures a constant bulk density among lithium carbonate plates, and reduces the overall uncertainty
presented in Section 3.5.
The aluminum containers used to encapsulate the lithium carbonate salt are of the same design from TEX-Cl
experiments [3]. As shown in Figure 11, these containers were designed to be cylindrical disks with the same
diameter as the HEU plates (15” diameter). The thickness of the containers was designed such that they had
standard thicknesses, 1/8”-thick bottom, 1/16”-thick top, and a 1/4”-thick absorber cavity. The active absorber
region is 14” diameter, leaving 1/2”-thick container walls to match the stack diameter. The bottom section is a
solid machined part while the top “lid” is fastened with sixteen Al-2024 screws. A retaining lip was designed
to provide a soldering surface, if air tightness is not achieved with the fasteners. Filling of the containers will
be done similarly to TEX-Cl [3], in which pouring will start with the lid open. Once the cavity of the container
is filled, it will be sealed using the fasteners, and finer salt additions will take place using one of the two affixed
holes in the top. A high-power pneumatic vibrator will be utilized to achieve a reasonable maximum density
and uniformity. After filling, the two holes will be plugged with small alloy steel threaded plugs. The mass of
the salt will be precisely known as it will be measured during the filling of the containers.

2.5 Experimental Configurations
The experimental configurations used in these experiments are similar to the TEX-HEU baseline configurations
from IER 297 [1]. The critical assemblies will be composed of stacks of HEU plates with varying thicknesses
of interstitial high-density polyethylene (HDPE) moderator and lithium carbonate plates. All assemblies will be
enclosed with 1” of HDPE reflector, including the sides, top, and bottom. The specific configurations described
in this report, including which plates are used and their ordering of stacking, are meant to be best estimates for
what the critical configurations will be.
Three configurations of the standard-stacking model are proposed, which are made of a repeating pattern of one
HEU plate, one polyethylene moderator plate, and one absorber plate as seen in Figure 12. The exception to
this stacking arrangement is in the first layer on the top of the bottom reflection, which contains a single HEU
plate to provide additional reactivity to the system.

3.1 Methodology and Code Used
The Monte Carlo neutron transport code, MCNP6® (6.2.1, 6.3.0) [20-23], developed at Los Alamos National
Laboratory, was used to calculate neutron multiplication, neutron spectrum, sensitivity to variations in cross
section data, and the uncertainty and bias in the models for each experimental configuration. Continuous energy
ENDF/B-VIII.0 cross sections were used in all MCNP6® calculations, and additional calculations for the final
configurations were completed with ENDF/B-VII.1 cross sections. All materials were modeled using room
temperature (293 K) cross sections.
Each Jemima plate was modeled individually using reported values where possible. The 235U content has been
measured for each plate, so individual values for each plate were included in the model. However, the relative
abundance of other uranium isotopes was only measured for three plates, as shown in Table 3, so average values
of 1.032 wt% 234U and 0.234 wt% 236U were used for all other HEU plates, with the remainder of the uranium
content being 238U. The elemental impurities were only measured for five Jemima plates, as shown in Table 5.
Jemima Plate Impurity Content. An average of these values for each elemental impurity was used for the rest
of the Jemima plates. Impurities reported as "<XX" in Table 4 were omitted from the model.
While the thickness of several Jemima plates were measured, all plates were modeled with their nominal
thickness of 0.118”. The mass of each plate was measured and individually included in the model. The lithium
carbonate and containers have not yet been fabricated, so all dimensions are nominal. The density of the
encapsulated lithium carbonate was assumed to be 1.266 g/cm3 (60% of theoretical density of 2.11 g/cm3).
Density of the polyethylene moderators and reflector is modeled as 0.967 g/cm 3. The models include the
aluminum diaphragm and upper stationary platform but do not include room return or other fixtures, such as
Comet VLM. Bias inherent to the calculations due to modeling the experimental configurations in a vacuum
and excluding effects of room return is described in Section 3.6.
The non-default parameters used for eigenvalue calculations are listed in Table 7. The sensitivity calculations
were evaluated using the MCNP6 KSEN functionality card over two types of energy structures. One set of
calculations was done using the 3-group energy structure for thermal range [0 MeV – 0.625E-6 MeV],
intermediate range [0.625E-6 MeV-0.1 MeV], and fast range [0.1 MeV – 20 MeV]. Tallies with fine energy
resolution used the SCALE 238-group structure [23].

3.2 Design Process
The original CED-2 for IER 499 [24] utilized Bayesian Optimization (BO) [25] techniques and parametric
studies to obtain critical TEX configurations with variable polyethylene and lithium hydride absorber
thicknesses. Additionally, constraint parameters for BO were thermal capture sensitivity and a height to
diameter ratio of 2.0. The models were also constrained to use only existing HEU plate inventory and existing
reflector and fuel geometries. As shown in Figure 14, a sample of the results of the BO provided optimal
absorber and moderator thicknesses, which were then focused further to provide maximum similarity to the
sensitivity profiles provided by Y-12’s electrorefining system as in the NCS design evaluations. When
determining maximum similarity, both the peak sensitivity and the integral sensitivity for the thermal capture
of 6Li were used to down-select configurations. Additionally, engineering judgement was used to further select
candidates based on reasonable fabrication and manufacturing capabilities (e.g., discarding thicknesses that
required high tolerances).

3.4 Uncertainty and Bias Characterization
The effects on the effective neutron multiplication factor of various uncertainties in the experimental data are
presented in this section. It is important to note that while only one value is reported, calculations were
completed for all the presented configurations. Only the maximum change in keff is reported in subsequent
sections. Additionally, some uncertainty values were assumed to be the same as the previous TEX-HEU based
experiments (e.g., Uranium plate mass, room return, and alignment uncertainties) [1-3]. All calculations
completed in this section were completed to a 0.00007 statistical uncertainty.

3.4.1 Uncertainty Characterization

3.4.1.1     Li2CO3 Thickness
The active lithium carbonate plates used in the models have a thickness of 1/4”, which were perturbed by ± 1%
while conserving mass by adjusting the density. An increase in plate thickness resulted in a -0.00074 change in
keff while a decrease in thickness resulted in a 0.00068 change.

3.4.1.2     Li2CO3 Mass
The mass of the lithium carbonate absorber plate was increased and decreased by 1% in the model while
conserving the volume in each plate. A ± 1% uncertainty in the mass is judged to encompass the empirical
uncertainty on density. Uncertainty in the mass composition can be significantly reduced by precisely weighing
the plates in the experiments, and specifically, during the packing of the plates. An increase in mass resulted in
a Δkeff of -0.00115 while a decrease in mass resulted in a change of 0.00135.

3.4.1.3 Li2CO3 Impurities
Natural trace metal impurities are common in lithium carbonate salts and have been evaluated here. Major
impurities include lead, potassium, and sodium, and will vary based on the purity and grade procured. There
are other trace metal impurities that may have an effect on criticality and were included in Table 12, below.
Boron and cadmium were also added as impurities at twice their limits of detection [19] but is highly unlikely
to exist at lithium carbonate grades exceeding 99.0% purity.

3.4.1.5 Uranium Plate Mass
The Jemima plates mass information for the calculations described in this report were taken from the ICSBEP
benchmark HEU-MET-INTER-006 [15]. The following description of the mass uncertainties is excerpted from
this report and no additional measurements have been made for the TEX-Cl and TEX-Li experiments.
The absolute uncertainty (standard deviation) for the total mass of a sum of N masses is,

where 𝜎𝑇 = absolute uncertainty in the total mass,
 𝜎𝑟 = absolute random measurement error,
 𝜎𝑠 = absolute systemic measurement error,
 𝜎𝑐 = round-off resolution, and
 𝑁 = total number of pieces of the mass, each of which is measured.
The factor of 12 appears because the density function for the round-off resolution is assumed to be uniform
over the interval from 0 to 𝜎𝑐 .
The HEU masses had to be measured in contamination control bags. Therefore, the bag was first weighed alone
and then the HEU was weighed inside the bag. The HEU masses were calculated at LANL by subtracting the
plastic bag mass (tare mass) from the gross mass. The variance of the net mass (i.e., the mass of the HEU disk
or ring) is the sum of the variances of the two measured masses. In this instance, the variances of the gross and
tare masses are the same. Therefore, the variance of the net mass is twice the variance of the individual mass,
and the standard deviation is the square root of two times the result given by the equation above.
Four “Calibration Certificates” for the balance used to weigh the HEU and graphite plates were examined.
Before adjustment, the balances were found to measure to an accuracy ranging from ±0.2 g to ±1.3 g. After
adjustment, the range was from ±0.0 g to ±0.2 g.
Based on the above information, it is conservatively assumed that both the random and systematic absolute
uncertainties for the balance are ±1.3 g. The round-off resolutions for the HEU disks are taken to be 1 g.
The resulting standard deviation in the mass of the Jemima plates is reported to be ±0.03% . These values were
used for uncertainty analysis in this report.

3.5 Density
Several density studies were complete to assess the effect on the experiment’s sensitivity to uneven filling or
changes in bulk density of the absorber plates. The TEX-Cl experiments have shown that uncertainties related
to these changes in reactivity can be significantly reduced by adhering to the filling procedure and precisely
measuring mass throughout the filling process.

lost. This study is meant to reiterate the importance of precisely measuring mass during the filling procedure to
determine the empirical density. Based on LLNL’s previous experiences and additional studies, the expected
density of the absorber after packing is 60% of theoretical (1.266 g/cm3). The expected region between the

3.5.2 Planar Density Study
A planar density distribution was studied specifically to address uneven filling of the absorber plates. As shown
in Figure 20, the absorber plates were divided into 20 slices of equal volume with densities ranging from 57.5%
(1.21325 g/cm3) to 62.5% (1.31875 g/cm3) of theoretical density (2.11 g/cm3). This density range was

[3]. A consistent shallow grade of lithium carbonate salt in the TEX-Li stack is considered a worst-case density
distribution. In this case, one side of the critical assembly is poisoned with ~175g more lithium carbonate than
the other (Table 16). This uneven distribution in lithium carbonate throughout the stack, in the worst-case
condition, resulted in a change in keff of 0.00013. This uncertainty can further be reduced by placing marks on
the sides of the plates where the last fill occurred. When stacking the plates in the assembly, plates can be
rotated 180-degrees from this marked position to offset any non-uniform distribution of salt.

Section 5
Conclusions

A total of three experiments are planned as part of the TEX-Li experiments, with ACS reagent grade or higher
of lithium carbonate as a diluent. Of the three proposed, two are predominately in the thermal median fission-
energy region and one is in the intermediate energy region.
All TEX-Li experimental configurations use a 1/4” active lithium carbonate absorber region, with varying
thicknesses of polyethylene from 1/2” to 5/4”. Li-1/2 is estimated to achieve critical at approximately 10 layers
of HEU fuel, while Li-3/4 and Li-5/4 are estimated at 9 and 11 layers, respectively. Fine reactivity control will
be maintained with both mass and the top reflector in the final critical configurations. Each configuration
provides varying sensitivity profiles to understand the 6Li capture sensitivity across different energy regions.
For each of the three configurations reactivity calculations were performed to determine the reactivity worth of
separation distance, upper reflector thickness, and HEU plate swap (HEU mass) to assist in the approach to
critical.
Various uncertainty calculations were completed to assess the areas of interest in the procurement of the
materials and the execution of the experiments. These uncertainties included density perturbations, absorber
plate thickness, absorber plate mass, absorber impurities (including other neutron absorbing materials), and
moisture content. The assessment of absorber uncertainties was predicted to be 0.00089 Δkeff. Density reactivity
calculations were also made to demonstrate that mass during the packing procedure is required to be precisely
measured.
The experimental uncertainties for all other components were assessed to be 0.00093 Δkeff and was comprised
of both calculated value as well as benchmark values from previous uranium test bed experiments [1-3]. This
uncertainty could be further reduced by precision measurement of non-fissile materials in the system.
Like TEX-Cl, it is expected that 20 absorber plates can be completely fabricated, inspected, and assembled in
6 months. The remaining HDPE components, less due to existing inventory, are expected to be procured and
inspected in four months. It is expected it will take a total of five experimental weeks to perform this experiment.

Appendix A
Issues and Lessons Learned for IER 575 Lithium Procurements

 Issues and Lessons Learned for IER 575 Li Procurements
 Catherine Percher
                                                  October 1, 2024

One of the main goals of the Thermal/Epithermal eXperiments (TEX) project is to use existing Nuclear
Criticality Safety Program (NCSP) assets to create critical experiment plutonium and uranium test beds for
materials important to nuclear criticality safety (NCS) that have insufficient benchmark evaluations. In
FY2020, work began on the design of a TEX variant to address a need for 6Li validation identified by the Y-12
National Security Complex NCS division. Y-12 was in the design phase of a new electrorefining operation for
uranium purification. The NCS evaluation credited lithium enriched in 6Li (in addition to 35Cl) as absorbers to
preclude criticality under upset conditions in the large and geometrically unfavorable electro-refiner. There was,
however, inadequate experimental validation for these absorbers and specifically none for 6Li.

Lithium is a very reactive, soft alkali metal. Y-12 has extensive manufacturing experience with lithium and its
compounds and had access to enriched 6Li (natural lithium is 4.85% 6Li and 95.15% 7Li). As LLNL and Y-12
were collaborating on the neutronics design of the critical experiment to ensure the result would meet the
validation need, it made sense to also make use of the in-house expertise with Li manufacturing. Y-12’s
Development group was engaged in March of 2020 to provide guidance in what would be possible in terms of
manufacturing of a lithium absorber plate, including encapsulation, thickness, and manufacturability. In July
of 2020, the Development team provided the guidance that they thought Li metal would be the easiest material
for them to fabricate to the team’s specifications, although some research and development work would be
needed to work out the fabrication details. The COVID pandemic slowed the Development team, as they lost
some of their machining expertise and had other operations taking precedence. LLNL worked on the neutronic
design in parallel based on thin Li metal (0.01”) encapsulated in aluminum and generated a final design (CED-
2) report which was sent to reviewers in June of 2021. In July of 2021, the Y-12 Development group delivered
the news that they did not have access to enough enriched lithium metal to produce the necessary parts for the
experiment, and suggested instead that they could fabricate lithium hydride (LiH) parts instead.

Discussions with Development continued in the fall of 2021, and they thought the easiest way to manufacture
the LiH was to cut down large 3” by 3” logs of the material into 0.01” thick wafers and using the wafers in a
planar array to form an absorber layer in the experiment. Each of wafers would be encapsulated within a mylar
bag that was double heat sealed, leaving a 0.125” thick edge around the wafer. The 15” diameter experiment
footprint could accommodate 9 of these plates in a planar array, which were to be welded into a single stainless
steel jacket with overall outer dimensions of approximately 10.125” by 10.125”. The absorber plate was to be
placed in a recess in the polyethylene moderator plates that would be used in the experiment. Figure 1 shows
the experiment absorber plate design based on the LiH wafers.

 Figure 1: Top-down view of the 3x3 array of LiH plates in a polyethylene plate. The polyethylene plate is

In April of 2022, LLNL visited the Development group at Y-12 and saw their progress. They had completed
the design of the stainless steel encapsulation and had prototypes to show. They were ready to start fabrication
on the parts but needed to wait until the appropriate material became available. The plan was to take the logs
of LiH and cut them in a specialized glovebox with a diamond wire saw. Due to the material properties of the
LiH, the cutting process had to be very slow, and the team estimated that it would take approximately 1 day per
wafer for fabrication. The total number of wafers needed was 100, and thus approximately four months of
fabrication time would be required.
Finally, in January of 2023, the LiH material was received and work cutting the wafers began. Unfortunately,
the saw was not capable of cutting the LiH at 0.01” thickness. The saw would start at 0.01” but would drift and
cut the part thicker as it moved. The LiH wafer was also prone to breaking before the cut was finished. The
thinnest reliable cut the Development team could obtain was 0.03”, which meant three times the amount of
lithium in the experiment. Since 6Li is such a good absorber, the configurations could not be made critical with
that amount of lithium. The LiH absorber design had to be abandoned due to manufacturing issues.
