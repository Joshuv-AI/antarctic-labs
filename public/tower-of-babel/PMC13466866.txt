Differential activity of helper T-cell subsets and cytokine signature profiles in alloimmunisation of sickle cell disease
Authors: Somnath Mukherjee, Tahsim Anwar, Gaurav Chhabra, Satya Prakash, Ansuman Sahu, Debasish Mishra, Ashutosh Panigrahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesRed blood cell (RBC) transfusion, a cornerstone of therapy in sickle cell disease (SCD), carries the risk of alloimmunisation. Interestingly, not all SCD patients develop alloimmunisation. Research findings underscore the pivotal role of T-cell subsets and their cytokine profiles in regulating alloimmunisation. But the mechanisms of selective alloimmunisation are poorly understood. This study aimed to characterise helper T cell subsets with their signature cytokines in multi-transfused SCD patients.MethodsThe patients were divided into two groups: alloimmunised and non-alloimmunised, depending on the presence or absence of alloantibodies following transfusions. Flow cytometric analysis of the Tfh and Treg subsets was performed. The supernatant plasma was used to estimate the levels of signature cytokines. The Mann-Whitney U test, independent sample median test, and Spearman’s Rho correlation test were performed for statistical analysis.ResultsA total of 12 patients from each group were included. A significantly higher percentage of T-regs was observed in the non-alloimmunised group [median 7.65, interquartile range (IQR) 6.37-8.20] than in the alloimmunised group (median 6.27, IQR 4.77-6.80) (P<0.05). A significantly higher expression of IL-21 was observed in the alloimmunised group. Significantly higher expression of TGF-β and IL-10 was found in the non-alloimmunised group (P<0.05).Interpretation and conclusionsOur study highlights increased Treg percentages and anti-inflammatory cytokine concentrations in the non-immune group, as well as increased pro-inflammatory cytokines and activated Tfh subsets in alloimmunised patients.

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is the most common form of haemoglobinopathy resulting from a mutation in the β-globin gene. The incidence is estimated to be 300,000 to 400,000 globally each year.1 Red blood cell (RBC) transfusion is considered a primary cornerstone in managing these complications.2 However, transfusion therapy carries a significant risk of alloimmunisation, which can aggravate transfusion reactions. The likelihood of RBC alloimmunisation in SCD is up to 50%, compared to less than 5% in the general population.3 Furthermore, RBC alloimmunisation in SCD is associated with reduced overall survival and a tendency to develop RBC autoantibodies, which is possibly because of immune dysregulation in SCD.4,5

Not all patients with SCD develop alloantibodies. Depending on antibody response, SCD patients can be largely characterised as either alloimmunised or non-alloimmunised. The mechanisms of selective alloimmunisation are still poorly understood, particularly the role of T-cell subsets that orchestrate B-cell responses in antibody production.5 Recently, T follicular helper (Tfh) cells have emerged as the primary CD4+ T-cell subset that activates B cells to produce antibodies and differentiates high-affinity IgG-secreting plasma cells.6 In contrast, T regulatory helper cells (Treg) play a decisive role in suppressing B-cell activity, either directly or indirectly by inhibiting helper T-cell function.2 We hypothesised that an imbalance favouring augmented Tfh activity and suppressed Treg function may predispose SCD patients to alloimmunisation, whereas the reverse pattern may help defend against antibody formation.

Cytokines further play a vital role in immune interaction. Interleukin-21 (IL-21), the signature cytokine of Tfh cells, promotes B-cell maturation and differentiation.7 Indeed, alloimmunised SCD patients have been shown to harbour antigen-specific Tfh cells expressing IL-21.6 Conversely, studies of chronically transfused alloimmunised patients have shown impaired Treg suppressive function, elevated circulating interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and decreased interleukin-10 (IL-10) levels.2 Taken together, these findings underscore the pivotal role of T-cell subsets and their cytokine profiles in regulating alloimmune responses. Therefore, an in-depth characterisation of Tfh and Treg populations, along with their signature cytokines, is crucial to exploring the mechanisms underlying selective alloimmunisation in SCD patients. Such insights will help predict, prevent, and manage alloimmunisation, thereby improving transfusion outcomes for this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and study setting

This was a prospective observational study conducted over a two-yr period, from September 2022 to August 2024. The study was conducted in the department of Transfusion Medicine in collaboration with the departments of Hematology, Pathology, and Laboratory Medicine, All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Bhubaneswar, Odisha after obtaining the ethical clearance from the Institute’s Ethics Committee.

Study participants, inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adult patients with known cases of sickle cell disease (SCD) with a history of red cell (RBC) transfusions were included in this study. The participants were divided into two categories: SCD patients with a red cell transfusion (RBC) history and developing at least one or more clinically significant alloantibodies were considered to be the alloimmunised group, and those who didn’t develop any clinically significant antibodies, even after at least 10 units of RBC transfusions, were considered as non-alloimmunised. Patients with pregnancy, autoimmune disorder, acute infection, sepsis, or crisis, and those who were unable to provide consent were excluded.

Collection of samples

Approximately 4 to 6 mL of fresh whole blood samples in an EDTA vial and around 3 to 4 mL of samples in a plain vial from both groups of polytransfused SCD patients, as specified in the inclusion criteria, were collected. The fresh whole blood sample in the EDTA vial was used for blood grouping, extended Rh and Kell phenotyping, and flow cytometric analysis of CD4+ T cell phenotyping. The clotted sample was further used for antibody screening and identification. The supernatant plasma from the EDTA sample was used to estimate various cytokines using the ELISA method.

Antibody screening and identification, and extended Rh and Kell phenotyping

Antibody screening and identification were performed for all included SCD patients per the department’s routine protocol and SOP before RBC transfusions. Antihuman globulin (AHG) gel-based column agglutination technology (CAT) (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.), a routine serological procedure in blood banks, was used for antibody screening and the identification of any clinically significant alloantibodies. In addition, before receiving a blood transfusion, extended Rh (C, c, E, e) and Kell phenotyping was also performed using antisera from Tulip Diagnostic (P) Ltd., following the instructions in the kit insert for the antisera.

Flowcytometric analysis

Flow cytometric immunophenotypic analysis was performed on peripheral blood samples processed within 24 hrs of collection using a three-laser, eight-colour BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, USA). A two-tube antibody panel was employed. Tube 1 contained anti-CD3 (V500), anti-CD4 (FITC), anti-CD127 (APC), anti-CD25 (V450), anti-CTLA-4 (PE), and anti-CD45 antibodies. Tube 2 comprised anti-CD3 (V500), anti-CD4 (FITC), anti-CXCR5 (PerCP-Cy5.5), anti-CD25 (V450), anti-PD-1 (PE), and anti-CD45 antibodies. All antibodies were procured from BD Biosciences (San Jose, USA).

Data acquisition and analysis were performed using FACS Diva software (version 10.1r5; BD Biosciences). Daily instrument quality control was conducted using cytometer setup and tracking (CST) beads, and monthly compensation verification was performed using the BD OneFlow™ 8-Color FC Beads kit, as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Sample preparation was carried out using a stain–lyse–wash protocol, and a minimum of 50,000 events were acquired per tube. Acquisition uniformity was assessed using a time-vs.-side scatter plot. Doublets were excluded by pre-gating on forward scatter height vs. area. Lymphocytes were initially identified on a CD45 versus side scatter plot as a population exhibiting bright CD45 expression and low side scatter. CD3-positive cells were gated to identify T lymphocytes, followed by selection of CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells.

Within the CD3⁺CD4⁺ T-cell population, two distinct subsets were analysed. Regulatory T helper cells (Tregs) were identified based on high CD25 expression and low CD127 expression using anti-CD25 and anti-CD127 antibodies. T follicular helper (Tfh) cells were identified using anti-CXCR5 and anti-ICOS antibodies. The Treg subset was further evaluated for CTLA-4 expression using anti-CTLA-4 antibodies, while the Tfh subset was assessed for PD-1 expression using anti-PD-1 antibodies ( Figs. 1A-G).

Analysis of signature cytokines by ELISA

The supernatant plasma from EDTA samples of both groups was used to quantify signature cytokines of Tfh cells (IL-21 and IL-10) and TGF-β for Treg subsets in both alloimmunised and non-alloimmunised groups. The ELISA method was performed according to the kit’s instructions (Manufacturer: Bender MedSystems GmbH, Vienna, Austria, marketed by Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for the estimation of signature cytokines. The lot numbers (expiry) for IL-21, IL-10, and TGF-β are 297700-010 (2025-12-31), 222104-015 (2024-10-31), and 305272-036 (2024-09-30), respectively. The standard curves for each cytokine were generated by plotting the average absorbance at each diluted standard concentration on the vertical (Y) axis and the corresponding cytokine concentration (pg/mL) on the horizontal (X) axis. The absorbance values obtained for the duplicates were within 10% of the mean. Further, the reproducibility of the assay was assessed, and intra- and inter-assay coefficient of variation (CV) were less than 10% for IL-10 and IL-21 and less than 5% for TGF-β. The reconstituted standards for each cytokine provided in the kit and the test samples were used in duplicates in each well. The limits of detection for IL-21, IL-10, and TGF-β were 20 pg/mL, 3 pg/mL, and 8.6 pg/mL, respectively.

Sample size calculation

Based on the result of the previous studies on the percentage of memory CD 4+ T cells expression between alloimmunised and non-alloimmunised groups of SCD patients, with a mean difference of 24 and standard deviation of 20, alpha error of 5%, and power of the study 80%, it was calculated that 12 patients would be taken each in the alloimmunised group and the non-alloimmunised group. A sample size calculation was performed using the statistical software R version 4.0.0 (2020-04-24) -- “Arbor Day”.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test revealed a non-normal distribution of the data. Hence, continuous variables were represented as median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Mann-Whitney U tests and independent samples median tests were conducted to compare the skewed data between the two groups. Spearman’s Rho (ρ) correlation analysis was used to measure the strength and direction of the relationship between two continuous variables. All P <0.05 were considered significant. SPSS version 30.0 (IBM SPSS, USA) was used for the statistical analysis.

Results

Demography, RBC antigen, and antibody frequency

A total of 12 patients were selected from each alloimmunised and non-alloimmunised group. The median age of the patients was 26 yr, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 14.5 (19.25-33.75). 55% of the patients were male (13/24), and the remaining 45% were female. The median number of RBC units transfused was 23.5 (IQR 22-25) in the alloimmunised group and 22 (IQR 18-27) in the non-alloimmunised group, respectively. The Rh and Kell matched RBC transfusions were provided in accordance with departmental policy at our centre; however, records of such Rh and Kell matched RBC transfusions could not be obtained for a few patients who received transfusions at outside hospitals. Flow cytometric analysis

Flow cytometric analysis revealed a higher proportion of the Tfh cell subset in the alloimmunised group compared to the non-alloimmunised group. The independent-samples median test and Mann-Whitney U test did not reveal a significant difference in the percentage of the Tfh subset population between the alloimmunised group (median 6.20, IQR 2.82-14.22) and the non-alloimmunised group (median 5.80, IQR 4.62-10.97) ( Fig. 3A). The Tfh subset cells were analysed for the expression of PD-1/CXCR5, revealing a significantly higher proportion of PD-1/CXCR5 in the Tfh subset cell population of the alloimmunised group (median 45.25, IQR 31.87-65.45) compared to the non-alloimmunised group. IL-21, which is the signature cytokine of Tfh, was observed in significantly higher concentrations in the alloimmunised group with a median concentration of 82.84 pg/mL (IQR:60.54- 126.97) than the non-alloimmunised group (median concentration 5.08 pg/mL, IQR:0.01- 30.05) ( Fig. 4A).

TGF-beta (TGF-β)

A significantly higher concentration of TGF-β, which is the signature cytokine of Treg, was observed in the non-alloimmunised group than in the alloimmunised group, with the median concentration of 601.90 pg/mL (IQR: 258.06-753.69) vs. 38.81 pg/mL (IQR: 32.39- 300.42) in the alloimmunised group ( Fig. 4B).

IL-10

A significantly higher concentration of IL-10 was observed in the non-alloimmunised group than the alloimmunised group with the median concentration of 9.51 pg/mL, (IQR: 5.40- 35.60) vs. 0.84 pg/mL (IQR:0.01- 4.64) in the alloimmunised group ( Fig. 4C).

Discussion

We observed that non-alloimmunised patients had a significantly higher proportion of Treg subsets accompanied by elevated levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines, including TGF-β and IL-10. In contrast, alloimmunised patients exhibited increased activation of circulating Tfh subsets and higher production of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-21. These findings highlight distinct immunoregulatory differences between alloimmunised and non-alloimmunised patients with sickle cell disease (SCD), underscoring the complex interplay between Treg and Tfh subsets and their associated cytokine profiles. The role of Tregs in alloimmunisation remains controversial. Our findings of higher Treg proportions in non-alloimmunised patients are consistent with experimental mouse models and with Bao et al,8 who reported reduced Treg activity in alloimmunised SCD and β-thalassemia patients, indicating an immunodysregulatory state in the alloimmunised group.8,9 In contrast, Vingert et al10. did not observe differences in Treg functionality between strongly and weakly alloimmunised patients, highlighting methodological differences in the isolation of CD4+T cells from our study. Vingert and colleagues10 used density gradient centrifugation for the fresh PBMC isolation, followed by CD4+ T cell enrichment by magnetic negative selection. Further, the authors focused on functional subsets, including naïve Treg (CD45RA+Foxp3low) and activated Treg (CD45RA–Foxp3hi), and assessed them by measuring CTLA-4, CD39, and CCR7 expression. CTLA-4, predominantly expressed on activated Tregs,11 was not significantly different across the two SCD patient subgroups. Consistent with these findings, our study also did not reveal significant differences in Treg subset activity when stratified by CTLA-4 expression.

Tfh cells are increasingly recognised as key regulators, particularly in autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, psoriasis, and rheumatoid arthritis.5,12 In our study, overall circulating Tfh percentages were slightly higher in alloimmunised patients, but this difference was not statistically significant. However, when we focused more potently on activated Tfh cells by identifying CXCR5-positive, ICOS-positive, and PD-1-positive cells within the Tfh subsets, we observed significant increases in the alloimmunised group. This suggests that while total Tfh numbers may not differ, functional activation states are essential in alloimmunisation. Similar findings have been reported, with naïve CD4+ T cells from SCD patients showing increased CXCR5 expression after culture, and alloimmunised cells more likely to express ICOS and PD-1.5 However, conflicting evidence exists, with some studies reporting higher numbers of CXCR5+PD-1+ Tfh cells in non-alloimmunised patients.13,14 Additionally, a subset of circulating Tfh-like cells, known as TIGIT+ Tfh-like cells (the T cell immunoreceptor with IgG and immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory domains), has been implicated in alloimmunisation, stimulating B cells via IL-21 and CD40L.6 Interestingly, only a minority of TIGIT+ cells co-express PD-1, suggesting PD-1 may not be central to RBC alloimmunisation.15 Our correlation analysis (Supplementary Figure) of the number of alloantibodies and PD-1+CXCR5+ Tfh subsets showed no significant association, supporting the notion that PD-1 may not be a crucial determinant of alloimmunisation. In our study, alloimmunised patients exhibited elevated IL-21, a pro-inflammatory cytokine central to B cell activation and antibody production. Godefroy et al6 reported a similar observation, noting that TIGIT+ Tfh cells upregulate co-stimulatory molecules and produce IL-21 and IL-4, essential for B cell differentiation. In contrast, the non-alloimmunised group showed higher levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines, TGF-β, and IL-10, suggesting the role of maintaining immune tolerance by restricting antigen presentation or T cell activation.8 These opposing cytokine patterns suggest that alloimmunisation reflects a breakdown of regulatory mechanisms, favouring inflammatory pathways that promote alloantibody formation. Nonetheless, despite the small cohort and the lack of controls or an untransfused group in our study, the distinct cytokine differences observed suggest potential T cell–associated markers that require further validation in larger, multicentre studies.

Our study has a few limitations. This observational study involved a small number of patient cohorts, which may introduce bias and limit the generalisability. Second, we did not estimate Tfh17 and Tfh2 subsets, which are potent B cell stimulators and have been implicated in autoimmune diseases.16,17 However, no increase in the activity of the Tfh17 and Tfh2 subsets was observed in alloimmunised SCD patients in a previous study.5 We did not evaluate the association between HLA class II polymorphisms and alloimmunisation risk. A recent meta-analysis found no significant association, suggesting the need for further investigation, such as GATA mutation and RBC genotyping, to improve matching in SCD patients, as a preventive measure against alloimmunisation.18 This is a pilot study and these findings cannot be translated into screening or diagnostic purposes at this stage.

In conclusion, this small pilot cohort study suggests that altered immune regulation, as evidenced by increased Treg activity and increased anti-inflammatory cytokines in the non-immune SCD patient group, and increased pro-inflammatory cytokines and activated Tfh subsets in alloimmunised patients. Significant differential expression of the signature cytokines in non-immune and allo-immune groups could serve as key markers to predict alloimmunisation or non-alloimmunisation in advance.
