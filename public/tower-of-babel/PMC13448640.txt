Solution-processed aqueous-insensitive transparent conductors for bio-optoelectronics
Authors: Lulu Sun, Hyun Woo Kim, Jiwon Kim, Shuxu Wang, Shinyoung Lee, Hongting Chen, Ruiqi Guo, Baocai Du, Joo Sung Kim, Kun Fang, Jianping Chen, Yoshihiro Ito, Yinhua Zhou, Tomoyuki Yokota, Sunghoon Lee, Jong Eun Lee, Ki Jun Yu, Kenjiro Fukuda, Takao Someya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBio-optoelectronics necessitate stable and transparent conductors for precise and consistent output of electrical signals. However, diverse aqueous environments challenge the long-term stable operation. In this study, we show a solution-processed transparent conductive nanomembrane that can be directly deposited on human skin and organic substrates, exhibiting good stability in multiple aqueous environments including rainwater, seawater, and biological fluids. This is attributed to the stabilizing of the polymer phase, which improves the stability and biocompatibility of the metal nanowires phase. The strategy ensures reliable electrical performance even in humid or wet environments for bio-signals like electromyography and electrocorticography. Its intrinsic transparency also enables subcutaneous vascular imaging and cerebral neurovascular imaging. This nanomembrane successfully expands the application of skin electronics settings underwater and enables continuous implantable monitoring of the cerebral cortex.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Sensors and biosensors

Introduction

Stable and transparent conductors are essential for bio-optoelectronics devices, as they ensure consistent signal and energy output while minimizing optical losses and signal artifacts1–4. To enable the direct deposition of conductors onto diverse organic biological tissues and surfaces for rapid fabrication without damaging the materials5–7, solution processing of such transparent conductors is a promising approach toward the scalable production for bio-optoelectronics8,9. Ideal solution-processed transparent conductors should meet the requirements of bio-optoelectronics, such as biocompatibility, good conductivity, and environmental stability, to achieve integrated fabrication of electronic systems. Notable advancements, such as solution-processed nanomaterials, polymer- or gel-based transparent conductors7,10,11, have been reported to meet the needs of imperceptible skin electronics12–14, implantable neuro-recording electronics15–18, and the fabrication of organic optoelectronic devices19,20.

Enhancing the stability of organic electronics in environmental conditions is beneficial for establishing reliable life-enhancement and maintenance systems21–24. Researchers have attempted to enhance the stability of transparent conductors for bioelectronics against aqueous conditions by using two elementary strategies24. One is to improve the intrinsic stability of conductors, such as crosslinking polymers18 or stable nanocomposite metal nanowires10 to resist the degradation by water molecules25–27. The other is encapsulation by barrier layers, such as polymers or metallic oxides, to prevent direct contact with liquid states7,28.

However, achieving the stable performance of solution-processed transparent conductors for bio-optoelectronics is still challenging when facing ubiquitous aqueous environments. In particular, the additional ion compositions and pH levels of water environments, such as rainwater, seawater, and biological fluids, can weaken the adhesion of transparent conductors and cause corrosion simultaneously. Exposure to such liquid environments causes accelerated oxidation or corrosion of the nanomaterials, resulting in decreased conductivity and transmittance27,29–31. Liquid environments can induce local pH changes in transparent conductive polymers like poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS), impacting polymer stability or cell viability in biological interfaces32–34. It also weakens the adhesion of gels to substrates, leading to electrical property degradation35–37. Furthermore, the surfaces of biological and organic tissues cannot withstand additional vacuum or high-temperature processes required for encapsulation against the complex aqueous environments after the deposition of transparent conductors38.

In this study, we developed a transparent and conductive nanomembrane for bio-optoelectronics to enhance stability across diverse aqueous environments. The nanomembrane is a solution-processed biphasic percolation network (BIPIN) that consists of conjugated polymers and fluorine-containing ionomers, uniformly integrated within the metal nanowire network (Fig. 1a, b). The composite maintains stable performance across various pH environments, including rainwater, biological tissue fluids, and seawater. It can be directly deposited onto organic biological tissues such as human skin without additional encapsulation, facilitating conformal contact with biological tissues and organic substrates (Fig. 1c). The high transmittance and conductivity can enable successful high-fidelity acquisition of both electrical signals and vascular image signals on the surface of the skin and brain tissue with reduced signal interference in aqueous conditions (Fig. 1d, e). This strategy enhances the electrical performance of metal nanowire networks in challenging bioenvironments (Fig. 1f), while broadening the application potential of skin electronics in underwater settings and implantable neural interfaces.

Results

Performance evaluation and manufacturing

We compared our BIPIN nanomembrane with other solution-processed conductive materials for water-resistant stability. Evaluating the nanomembrane resistance changes caused by water is crucial for diverse biological applications. The materials were directly deposited on glass without any encapsulation. This unencapsulated configuration helps evaluate the materials under complex bioelectronic application environments. The film thickness of all materials was kept consistent at around 200 nm. The BIPIN exhibited a higher water contact angle of 94.6°, indicating superior water resistance compared to multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) with an angle of 16.5°, PEDOT:PSS with 35.1°, and AgNWs with 32.4° (Fig. 2a). Subsequently, the films were immersed in various aqueous conditions, including rainwater, seawater, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution. After 600 min, the BIPIN demonstrated consistent stability compared to the other materials (Fig. 2b). The change in resistance over time revealed that the resistance increase in the BIPIN remained around 20% (15.8 ± 5.1%, n = 5), whereas PEDOT:PSS and AgNWs experienced resistance increases exceeding 200% (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, the stability of the encapsulated materials was further evaluated to better reflect their conditions in practical applications (Supplementary Fig. 2). Although the stability was dramatically improved for all the materials with encapsulation, BIPIN still shows the best stability with the smallest standard deviation (especially in comparison with AgNWs). The transparency of the conductors was also characterized while maintaining a consistent sheet resistance of approximately 20 Ω sq−1 (Fig. 2c). The BIPIN exhibited the highest transparency at 550 nm. The BIPIN showed improved adhesion to withstand 100 cycles of tape peeling tests while maintaining electrical performance, which is superior to that of other conventional solution-processed materials (Fig. 2d).

We then evaluated the robustness of the solution-processed conductors through continuous high-pressure water flow (Fig. 2e), AgNWs detached from the substrate under a 0.4 MPa water flow, whereas the BIPIN maintained film integrity (Fig. 2f). Thickness changes were also measured after water flow (Supplementary Fig. 3). The BIPIN kept its initial thickness of about 200 nm, while the AgNWs were removed by the water flow. This robustness also makes it feasible to fabricate functional films directly on biological substrates, such as plant leaves and skin (Fig. 2g). These films can withstand deformation while retaining their original conductivity under flowing water (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 4). The good deformability is attributed to the mechanical stability of BIPIN (Supplementary Fig. 5). These BIPIN nanomembranes deposited on various substrates exhibit both visual inconspicuousness and conformability. The optoelectronic properties and nanometer-scale thickness give the BIPIN with a transparent appearance on the skin surface, allowing them to form conductive pathways when deposited on the finger. An LED can be illuminated simply by grasping it, and its brightness remains stable even under running water (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Movie 1).

Compared to previously reported bio-conductors with good water resistance, our strategy enables three important advancements simultaneously. These advancements are (ⅰ) high transparency and high conductivity conductors with good conformability and imperceptibility, (ⅱ) stable performance across various aqueous environments, including rainwater, biological tissue fluids, and seawater, with pH levels ranging from 4.6 to 8.6, and (ⅲ) good solution-processability, allowing for direct deposition on the skin, and organic substrates to expand application scenarios. A detailed comparison with conventional solution-processed waterproof transparent conductive materials is provided in Fig. 2i and Table S1.

Strategies and mechanisms of the design

The high performance of BIPIN can be attributed to the biphasic percolation strategy, in which the metal nanowire phase forms a conductive network, while the polymer phase provides a stabilizing network. This nanomembrane can be prepared by controlled percolation of the polymer solutions into the metal nanowire phase during sequential spray deposition (Fig. 3a). The metal nanowires (silver nanowires, AgNWs) are initially deposited on the substrate to establish the framework of the conductive network. The polymer phase consists of a conjugated polymer (Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene), PEDOT) and the perfluorinated sulfonic acid ionomer (Nafion) as a counter-anion, uniformly assembled within the conductive network (Fig. 3b). This was further confirmed by cross-sectional SEM imaging and elemental analysis (Supplementary Figs. 7 and S8). The biphasic percolation is facilitated by the surface energy regulation of the polymer phase. The polymer phase exhibits good dispersion in both water and ethanol, which has been reported as PEDOT:F by our previous work39. However, due to the difference in surface energy between water and ethanol40, their interaction with nanowire clusters leads to distinct percolation behaviors during the deposition process. By spraying the polymer phase of water and ethanol onto the surface of a high-density metal nanowire network, we observed that the ethanol solution quickly formed a liquid film across the metal nanowire network (Supplementary Movie 2). We captured the details of this controlled diffusion by using high-magnification microscopy (Fig. 3c). When the water-based polymer phase spreads near the edges of the nanowires, its high surface energy (72.6 mJ/m²) prevents the flow from passing through the gaps in the nanowire network, leading to blocking effects from the nanowires. In contrast, the low-surface-energy (21.7 mJ/m²) polymer phase based on ethanol exhibits the opposite behavior. The nanowires cannot block the droplets, allowing the polymer phase to infiltrate the gaps and uniformly coat the nanowire surfaces rapidly (Fig. 3d, e). Using high-speed imaging, we can further simulate the spreading behavior of polymer droplets with different surface energies on the nanonetwork surface (Supplementary Fig. 9). The ethanol-based polymer solution demonstrated fast spreading rates and larger diffusion radii up to 7.6 mm at 2.5 s, while the water-based polymer droplet kept 2.4 mm after 0.8 s. This solvent-dependent interactions behaviour was further corroborated by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which revealed distinct wetting states for water and ethanol on the rough silver surface. Water retained a contracted morphology with a relatively larger apparent contact angle and limited spreading, whereas ethanol exhibited stronger wetting with partial impregnation into the rough surface features. This indicates that the rough nanonetwork enhances the low-surface-tension advantage of ethanol, enabling a transition toward a more conformal and infiltrated wetting state, thereby facilitating uniform polymer infiltration and the establishment of the biphasic percolation structure (Supplementary Fig. 10).

This sequential deposition strategy enables the tunable control of conductivity and transmittance by adjusting the ratio between the nanowire phase and polymer phase (Supplementary Fig. 11). Increasing the AgNW density generally improves conductivity of the film by enhancing network connection, but it simultaneously reduces optical transparency due to the increased coverage of the nanowires. In contrast, reducing the AgNW density favors higher transparency but may lead to insufficient conductive pathways and increased resistance. Therefore, balancing these conflicting effects is essential for achieving high-performance electrodes films. In BIPIN, the polymer phase supports the formation of an efficient BIPIN, enabling good conductivity to be maintained at relatively low nanowire loading. To further explore the general applicability of the BIPIN strategy, we expanded the range of metal nanowires to include copper nanowires (CuNWs) and gold nanowires (AuNWs). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 12, measurements using atmospheric photoelectron yield spectroscopy confirmed that the BIPIN strategy is not limited to AgNWs, but is also effective for CuNWs and AuNWs. All three nanowire systems showed clear surface work-function modulation after BIPIN formation. Considering the overall balance between cost and electrical performance, we selected AgNWs as the representative system for further detailed analyses and experiments.

The biphasic percolation strategy enhanced the water stability of BIPIN in diverse aqueous environments. Filling the network with the polymer phase strengthens the adhesion of AgNWs to the substrate, prevents water molecules from infiltrating, which could lead to delamination. Direct contact of corrosive ions with metal materials under acidic and alkaline conditions is also prevented (Fig. 3f). Such robust nanomembrane stability and enhanced adhesion are assumed to be attributed to the interaction of the polymer with both AgNWs and the substrate. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement was performed on the BIPIN to identify the interactions. The peaks observed for pristine AgNWs in BIPIN at 367.9 eV (Ag 3d5/2) and 373.9 eV (Ag 3d3/2) shifted to 368.9 eV and 374.9 eV, respectively (Fig. 3g). The binding energy of F1s in polymers shifted to a lower energy from 689.1 to 688.9 eV after coating PEDOT:F on the AgNWs in BIPIN (Supplementary Fig. 13). These shifts indicate interfacial charge redistribution due to a reduction in electron cloud density41. During the peeling process, ethanol-processed BIPIN exhibited a peeling force of up to ~3.0 N, which was higher than that of AgNWs alone and those of BIPIN films prepared from water-based solvents (Fig. 3h). The post-deposited polymer containing conjugated backbones forms supplementary conductive pathways19, enhancing the conductivity of the AgNWs network, with ethanol-processed BIPIN showing a greater conductivity improvement than water-processed BIPIN owing to its more complete percolation network (Fig. 3i).

In different water environments, apart from water molecules, anions accelerate the corrosion of AgNWs under acidic or alkaline conditions42. Chloride ions (Cl−) are widely present in nature and within the human body, exerting corrosive effects on silver43,44. We investigated the ability of BIPIN to inhibit ion penetration. We found that our polymer phase can prevent Cl− from permeating through the film, thereby avoiding the formation of AgCl precipitation (Fig. 3j). Chloride ions can easily pass through semipermeable membranes but cannot penetrate BIPIN films. The hydrophobicity of BIPIN surface can further prevent the infiltration of hydrated ions due to its abundance of fluorinated groups45. The ion-blocking properties were determined under relatively static conditions. The hydrophobic fluorinated polymer network also helps suppress excessive swelling. These can maintain the integrity of the dense barrier structure in physiological media. The ability of ions to diffuse into the film was evaluated by characterizing Cl− penetration using dynamic secondary ion mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. 14). Compared to the PEDOT:PSS film, where Cl⁻ penetration intensity reached up to 10⁵, the PEDOT:F polymers exhibited a much lower Cl⁻ penetration intensity, around 10³. Based on the results, the polymer phase demonstrates excellent ionic shielding effects. This reduced ion penetration significantly slows down the corrosion of the metal nanowires phase in aqueous solutions.

Application of the BIPIN for on-skin monitoring

The biocompatibility and electrical performance of the BIPIN were first evaluated for the acquisition of high-quality biological signals such as electromyography (EMG) (Fig. 4a). We deposited BIPIN directly onto the finger and observed good conformity to finger skin from the microscopic images (Fig. 4b). Even after rinsing with water, the BIPIN maintained stable electrical performance (Supplementary Fig. 15). The biocompatibility of the BIPIN was assessed through cytotoxicity experiments. Since BIPIN is designed for direct contact with skin, we evaluated its biocompatibility by culturing fibroblasts directly on the surface of the nanomembrane (Fig. 4c). After 48 h of incubation, the cells exhibited uniform growth across the surface of the BIPIN film, whereas cell proliferation was inhibited on AgNWs (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 16). These results indicate that the cytotoxicity of BIPIN is comparable to that of standard culture medium (92.77 ± 1.78%), demonstrating excellent cytocompatibility. The low impedance of the skin-BIPIN interface enhances the quality of EMG and Electrocardiogram (ECG) signal acquisition. Our BIPIN exhibits lower impedance at 100 Hz compared to commercial (Ag/AgCl) electrodes, AgNWs@PDMS electrodes, and PVA gels (Supplementary Fig. 17).

The optical transparency and low thickness of BIPIN enable the acquisition of vascular imaging during EMG monitoring. To compare the performance of the different electrodes for EMG monitoring, we placed the different electrodes at the brachioradialis muscle and recorded signals during grip actions (Fig. 4e). Although electrodes such as AgNWs@PDMS and PVA gels are visually transparent to the naked eye, they still produce strong imaging artifacts under near-infrared vein visualization, hindering clear vascular imaging (Fig. 4f). In contrast, compared with commercial transparent electrodes, PVA hydrogel electrodes, and AgNWs@PDMS, BIPIN preserves tight conformal contact and stable electrical pathways under mechanical stimulation (e.g., gripping-induced deformation) and continuous water flow, thereby enabling more reliable vein visualization and signal acquisition under realistic dynamic and wet-use conditions. This is primarily due to the direct optical obstruction caused by the opacity of commercial electrodes46. The AgNWs@PDMS electrodes, with their relatively weak adhesion to the skin, introduce infrared light refraction47, resulting interfacial instability under deformation further aggravates transient gaps and signal distortion during grip actions. PVA hydrogel electrodes, rich in water content and characterized by smooth surfaces, lead to excessive infrared absorption and present surface morphologies that differ markedly from skin tissue48. However, the superior optical transmittance and ultrathin configuration of the BIPIN, which is directly deposited onto the skin, effectively prevents distortion of optical signals. Therefore, the advantage of BIPIN for this application arises not only from its high transparency, but also from its ultrathin and conformal skin interface, which helps reduce optical artifacts arising from muscle movement and weak interfacial contact during simultaneous vein imaging and EMG recording.

The waterproof capability of BIPIN ensures reliable and high-quality biosignal acquisition under wet or aqueous conditions. BIPIN achieved a higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 21.33 dB, while commercial gels produced similar waveforms but with a smaller SNR of 20.75 dB (Fig. 4g). However, after immersion in water, commercial electrodes, AgNWs@PDMS, and PVA gels all exhibited degraded signal acquisition performance due to water-induced erosion and swelling of the gel components. In contrast, BIPIN maintained a stable EMG signal acquisition under the same conditions. Furthermore, we evaluated their potential for ECG monitoring by depositing electrodes on the chest of a subject. The heart rate obtained from commercial gels was 72 beats per minute (bpm), identical to that measured by the BIPIN sensors (72 bpm). The detected resting heart rates matched those obtained from commercial signals, confirming their accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 18). Consequently, even after prolonged wear over 5 days and then rinsed with artificial sweat, the BIPIN still exhibits more stable heart rate monitoring performance than commercial gels (Supplementary Fig. 19). This intrinsic stability enables stable recording without the need for additional surface modification. In addition, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis indicates that, besides abundant C–F groups, the polymer matrix also contains rich S–O functionalities (Supplementary Fig. 20). These polar groups can promote strong physical adsorption to the outermost stratum corneum, which is mainly composed of keratin and lipids, while the conformal percolated network further maximizes interfacial contact with the skin to achieve robust intrinsic adhesion. The consideration of gas permeability benefits health monitoring, as demonstrated by the water evaporation rate under natural conditions, indicating the breathability of BIPIN (Supplementary Fig. 21).

Owing to the high mechanical stability and robustness of BIPIN, real-time gesture monitoring is enabled even underwater (Fig. 4h). The wrist was bent in various positions, simulating the sliding motions while swimming in water. The changes in the frequency or amplitude of the EMG signal are caused by changes in arm contraction force or posture, resulting from imbalanced neural signal activation (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 22). To assess the reliability of BIPIN for motion analysis and prediction, we utilized a machine learning framework to recognize gestures. We performed Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) feature extraction on the normalized signals and used a t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE)49 algorithm to perform 2D dimensionality reduction and visually categorize high-dimensional data based on their extracted features (Fig. 4j). Each cluster group corresponds to a specific swimming gesture action, and the supervised learning (SL) algorithms can readily achieve high classification accuracy of the EMG signals under this clear partitioning of different action signals. We employed data augmentation for training and cross-validation to test 6 different types of SL algorithms and generated a classification report (Supplementary Fig. 23). Despite using different SL structures, all models maintained a high accuracy above 98%, with the Random Forest algorithm achieving the highest accuracy of 99.4% (Fig. 4k). This result indicates that the data distinctiveness is sufficiently high to ensure that recognition accuracy is not influenced by the choice of SL algorithms.

Application of the BIPIN for in-vivo experiments

Transparent implantable devices are garnering considerable interest in neuroscience and biomedical engineering as they enable the integration of neural recording with optical techniques50. The outstanding performance of the BIPIN motivated our pursuit of their implantable bioelectronic applications (Fig. 5a). We have fabricated an array of ultrathin transparent electrodes for interfacing with the cortical surface of rodents. The array contains nine electrode channels, with each channel site measuring 300 × 300 μm². The substrate consists of a 5 μm-thick parylene/SU-8 film, with the top layer exposing the BIPIN parts through patterned SU-8, and the overall thickness of the electrodes is 5.5 μm (Supplementary Fig. 24). Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Fig. 26 show the details of the electrode array fabrication, confirming that the patterning process does not compromise the conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 27). The prepared electrodes can be peeled off from the substrate and are free-standing (Fig. 5b). The electrical performance of the electrocorticography (ECoG) electrodes does not change before and after being peeled off due to the stability of the BIPIN (Supplementary Figs. 28 and S29). For the cortical surface interface, the relatively low strain in the brain helps reduce stress-induced damage to the polymer network, thereby maintaining the mechanical integrity of the ultrathin electrode. They can comfortably adhere to the cerebral cortex of a mouse, demonstrating high conformability (Supplementary Movie 3).

Figure 5c provides a schematic representation of the experimental setup used for in vivo ECoG measurements. A photograph of the actual test setup is presented in Supplementary Fig. 30. Figure 5d displays images of electrodes fabricated with BIPIN and gold (Au) implanted in the somatosensory cortex respectively. In the case of the BIPIN electrode, the measurement area remained transparent and unobstructed. Figure 5e illustrates the electrophysiological signals recorded from the electrode array made of BIPIN across nine channels. The data demonstrate that BIPIN electrodes provide high-resolution and stable recordings, making them highly suitable for precise neural interfacing applications. The transparency of the BIPIN electrodes enhances visualization and monitoring during implantation, thereby reducing the risk of misalignment and potential damage to cortical tissue. Furthermore, to assess whether the electrode array could detect drug-induced seizures, a small dose of pilocarpine (10 mg/kg) was administered, and the resulting electrophysiological signals were measured. Pilocarpine induces seizures in mice by activating muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, which leads to increased neuronal excitability and an imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission. This imbalance results in network hyperactivity and seizure activity, particularly in the hippocampus51. Figure 5f presents the seizure-like spiking activity recorded by the BIPIN electrode following pilocarpine administration. The excellent electrical performance of the BIPIN allowed for the acquisition of signals with very low noise levels. These signals were further analyzed using a spectrogram to observe frequency-specific intensity changes. Notably, the electrophysiological signals revealed drug-evoked local field potentials (LFPs) within the 0–10 Hz range at the time of the drug reaction52. Additionally, high-pass filtered traces exhibited multi-unit activity bursts phase-locked with these LFPs, alongside a stable pre-seizure baseline (Supplementary Fig. 31). The acquisition of these localized, high-frequency components demonstrates the high recording fidelity of the interface. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 5g, the BIPIN electrode exhibits lower impedance compared to the conventional gold electrode, even when maintaining the same geometric area. This low-impedance characteristic is further supported by enhanced cortical contact due to its ultraflexibility (Supplementary Fig. 32). To further validate the temporal stability of the device under physiological conditions, we conducted longitudinal in vivo ECoG recordings, which demonstrated consistent signal quality and robust channel reliability over a 10-day period (Supplementary Figs. 33 and S34). Even under relatively static conditions, BIPIN is continuously exposed to physiological chemical species in the in vivo environment, which should be considered for longer-term in vivo applications.

We applied it in two-photon imaging to explore the potential integration of the electrode array with bioimaging modalities beyond electrophysiological signal measurement (Supplementary Fig. 35 and Supplementary Movie 4). Figure 5h–j present the two-photon imaging results under three conditions: (i) a control state with no implanted electrode, (ii) a state with a gold electrode array implanted, and (iii) a state with the BIPIN electrode array implanted. All images were acquired by compressing data measured up to 50 μm in the Z-axis direction and projecting it onto the XY plane. Additionally, the YZ plane images, acquired from the regions marked with yellow lines, are shown for comparison. The results demonstrate that the superior transparency of the BIPIN electrode array allows imaging without obstruction, comparable to the control state. In contrast, the gold electrode array caused significant image obstruction in both the XY and YZ planes, as the laser used for two-photon imaging could not penetrate the metal electrode. These findings highlight the dual advantages of the BIPIN electrode array: maintaining excellent electrical performance in interstitial fluid environments and facilitating seamless integration with bioimaging modalities, particularly in applications requiring transparent electrodes. In addition, the biocompatibility of BIPIN was evaluated to assess its potential for prolonged implantation. To investigate whether any toxic substances would leach out after extended exposure to biological fluids, BIPIN electrodes were immersed directly in cell culture medium for up to 30 days. Subsequent cell culture experiments using the leachate showed no signs of contamination, indicating that the immersion of BIPIN did not compromise the cytocompatibility of the medium compared to the control (Fig. 5k and Supplementary Fig. 36). Furthermore, in vivo histological analysis of the brain tissue demonstrated reduced immune activation compared to the control electrode, indicating that it does not induce strong stimulation at the tissue surface (Supplementary Fig. 37). These results support the reliability of BIPIN for early-stage neural applications under the tested cortical-surface conditions, while longer-term chronic reliability remains an important future research direction.

Discussion

In conclusion, the reported solution-processed transparent conductive nanomembrane represents a substantial leap forward in bio-optoelectronics. They excel in transparency, conductivity, and resilience across various aqueous conditions, making them promising for bioelectronic applications demonstrated here, such as on-skin monitoring and cortical-surface interfaces. By enhancing biocompatibility and environmental durability, these composites pave the way for robust and versatile electronic systems in practical and everyday settings.

Method

Experiments on human and animal subjects

All experiments involving skin electrophysiological signal recordings were thoroughly reviewed and approved by the ethical committee of RIKEN (Approval number: Wako3 30–32). Informed consent was obtained from the subject participating in the experiment. Procedures for acute ECoG recordings, and two-photon imaging were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yonsei University Health System (IACUC No: 2022-0057) and adhered to the ethical guidelines set forth by the National Institutes of Health (NIH).

Materials

A fluorinated polymer-releasing layer was prepared by mixing Novec 1700 (3 M Company) with Novec 7100 (3 M Company) at a 1:6 ratio. SU-8 3005 and SU-8 developer were purchased from MicroChem. Parylene (diX-SR) was purchased from Daisan Kasei Co. AgNWs (AW030-LP) were purchased from Zhejiang Kechuang Advanced Materials Co., Ltd. CuNWs and AuNWs were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. PEDOT:PSS (Clevios PH1000) was purchased from Heraeus. The commercial ECG gels (Vitrode L) were purchased from NIHON KOHDEN, Japan. Isopropyl alcohol, and anhydrous ethanol were purchased from Wako Pure Chemical, Ltd. Carbon nanotubes (multi-walled, 698849-1G) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Fabrication of ultrathin BIPIN

BIPIN was fabricated through sequential deposition of metal nanowires and polymer solutions. First, AgNWs are diluted to a concentration of 0.2 wt%. The dilution is achieved by adding ethanol in a volume ratio of 1:4. The diluted solution is then deposited using solution-processed techniques such as spin-coating or spray-coating, which can be adjusted according to the target application. For spin-coating, the speed is set at 2000 rpm for 30 s, with sequential spin-coating performed twice. For spray-coating, the pressure is set at 0.3 MPa, using nitrogen as the gas source, and the distance between the nozzle and the substrate is maintained at 20 cm.

Next, a polymer solution is deposited onto the AgNWs network to form a filling and protective layer. The preparation process of the polymer solution is shown as follows. The deposition of the polymer can also be carried out using solution-processed techniques such as spin-coating or spray-coating, which can be adjusted according to the target application. For spray-coating, the pressure is set at 0.3 MPa, using nitrogen as the gas source, and the distance between the nozzle and the substrate is maintained at 20 cm, with a spray volume of 1 ml. To prepare the polymer solution, the preparation process is similar to our previous report39. The polymer solution was synthesized by oxidative chemical polymerization using nafion aqueous ionomer dispersion and EDOT, with FeCl₃ as the oxidant. After purification and centrifugation, the polymer solution was dispersed into water or ethanol by ultrasonic processing with a sonicator (Q700 sonicator, QSONICA) for 8 h with different solvent compositions. The solid content of the polymer solution was 1.3–1.5 wt%.

Characterization

Optical transmittance spectra were measured using a UV–visible/NIR spectrophotometer (V-780, JASCO) in the wavelength range of 300–1000 nm. The element distribution in the specimens was detected by electron probe microanalysis (EPMA-8050G, SHIMADZU). The adhesion of the electrode material was assessed using a tape peeling test, where Polyimide tape was applied to the electrode-coated glass substrate and then peeled off at a 180° angle at a speed of 5 mm/s. The remaining electrode integrity was evaluated by comparing the resistance before and after peeling. The contact angles were measured using a contact angle meter (DMe-211, Kyowa Interface Science). The IR images were measured by thermal imaging (TiS75+, FLUKE). The work function of the samples was measured using an atmospheric photoelectron spectrometer (AC-3, RIKEN KEIKI, Japan). The measurement was based on the low-energy photoelectron counting method, and the photoelectron yield spectra were collected in air under UV excitation over the energy range of 4.0–7.0 eV.

Molecular dynamics simulations

The Ag substrate was described using the modified embedded-atom method potential. Water molecules were modelled using the TIP3P water model, whereas ethanol molecules were represented by the OPLS force field with RESP partial charges. The interactions between the Ag substrate and both water and ethanol molecules were described using the Lennard–Jones (LJ) potential. All simulations were performed using the open-source Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS software, version2Apr2025) package. The initial structures were first subjected to energy minimization using the conjugate gradient (CG) algorithm to obtain geometrically optimized configurations. The energy and force convergence criteria were both set to 1 × 10−15 kcal mol−1, with a maximum of 10,000 minimization steps and up to 1,000,000 force evaluations.

Subsequently, atomic velocities were initialized according to a Gaussian distribution at 300 K. The velocities of Ag atoms were set to zero, and the Ag substrate was constrained as a rigid body by fixing the forces acting on the Ag atoms to zero. The droplet was then allowed to relax freely on the substrate surface. During this process, the SHAKE algorithm was applied to constrain the bond lengths and bond angles of water molecules, with an accuracy tolerance of 0.0001 and a maximum of 20 iterations. The cutoff distance for LJ interactions was set to 1.2 nm, while long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle–particle particle–mesh (PPPM) solver. For non-bonded interaction scaling, the special_bonds setting was applied such that the 1–2 and 1–3 interactions were excluded, whereas the 1–4 interactions were scaled by a factor of 0.5 for both LJ and Coulombic terms. A time step of 1 fs was used throughout all simulations, and the temperature was maintained at 300 K. After energy minimization, the system was equilibrated for 2 ns in the canonical (NVT) ensemble, during which the structural evolution was recorded. Simulation snapshots for visualization were generated using OVITO.

On-skin electrode fabrication and measurement of impedance

The BIPIN were deposited on the human skin with spraying techniques. The commercial electrode was purchased from NIHON KOHDEN (NM-317Y3). The thickness of the commercial gel electrode is approximately 0.5 mm. An inductance-capacitance-resistance meter (LCR) meter (E4980AL, Keysight, USA) with frequencies ranging from 10 to 1 kHz was used to measure the intrinsic impedance of commercial electrode, AgNWs@PDMS, PVA gels and BIPIN. To measure the skin-electrode contact impedance, electrodes with 1.5 by 2.5 cm size were integrated with flexible Au electrodes, and commercial ECG electrodes were used with the same size as a comparison. The distance between the edges of the two electrodes was 1.5 cm.

Electrophysiological recording

For ECG monitoring, two BIPIN with a rectangular shape as commercial gel electrodes were deposited on the human skin. The BIPIN were connected to a signal recording system (Evoked potential/EMG measuring system, Japan, Neuroworkbench software, version 07-03) with a bandpass filter (0.02–50 Hz). The patterned deposition process is controlled through spray-coating. For EMG signal recording, two BIPIN with a rectangular shape as commercial gels adhered to the brachioradialis muscles of one arm, and another ultrathin hydrogel was attached to the hand dorsum as a ground electrode. All electrodes were connected to the signal recording system with a bandpass filter (10 to 10 kHz). The EMG signals were generated when the subject opened and clenched a fist. For the skin test, the study protocol was thoroughly reviewed and approved by the ethical committee of RIKEN (Approval number: Wako3 30–32). The SNR calculation was performed according to that reported in the literature53. For underwater monitoring, electrodes are deposited on the brachioradialis muscles on both sides of the arm to ensure the electrodes are fully immersed in water. The participant was randomly selected, and was a 31-year-old healthy adult male with no history of cardiovascular disease. No compensation was provided. Sex/gender analysis was not performed because this was a single-participant feasibility demonstration focused on device performance rather than sex- or gender-dependent responses.

Subcutaneous vein imaging

Following initial EMG monitoring on arm, a venous imaging was performed using a commercial near-infrared vein visualization device (NVS500, VEINSIGHT). The imaging site was aligned with the electrode placement area. The device’s projection window was positioned perpendicular to the skin surface, maintaining a working distance of 15–25 cm. During operation, direct sunlight or strong ambient lighting was avoided to prevent interference with image quality.

Machine learning for swimming gesture action recognition

Data preprocessing

First, signal jittering is employed as a data-augmentation strategy to increase prediction robustness by adding Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.1 to each raw dataset. The preprocessing of the jittered data inputs involves normalizing the amplitude values using MinMaxScaler (which scales features to a range between 0 and 1) and aligning the baseline by subtracting the minimum value of each channel.

Feature extraction and selection

This feature extraction involves calculating the frequency components and amplitude spectrum using the FFT. This process involves using a set threshold of 99% cumulative energy from the FFT results to calculate the frequency array and then computing the amplitude spectrum for each signal up to this threshold. The function returns the frequency components (Hz) and the amplitude spectrum (APL). The feature selection involves balancing the dataset to ensure equal representation of classes by randomly under-sampling the majority classes to match the number of samples in the minority class. This is followed by removing constant features with zero standard deviation to retain only the relevant features.

Training and testing process of the supervised learning algorithms

The training and testing process involves using various SL algorithms with specified parameter settings to evaluate their performance on a balanced dataset using the Scikit-learn package54. The dataset is split into training and testing sets using StratifiedKFold cross-validation with 5 splits and shuffling enabled. The models used include K-nearest neighbors with 6 neighbors and distance-based weights, RF with 200 estimators, a maximum depth of 15, and sqrt max features, ANN with ReLU activation, an alpha of 0.0001, hidden layer sizes of (100, 50), and a learning rate of 0.05, SVM with a C value of 1 and an RBF kernel, LGBM with 100 estimators and a learning rate of 0.1, and CatBoost with 100 estimators, a learning rate of 0.1, and verbosity disabled. Each model is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score metrics, and the best model is selected based on the highest median F1 score. This best model is then trained on the training set, evaluated on the test set, and its performance is reported using a classification report and confusion matrix. Finally, the trained model and the confusion matrix are saved for future reference.

Cytotoxicity test in vitro

The cytotoxic effects of a device were assessed using an in vitro cytotoxicity test on the NIH3T3 mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line, acquired from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank (Cat. IFO50019). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Industries Ltd., Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (MP Biomedicals LLC., Illkirch, France), penicillin (1.0 × 105 U/liter) and streptomycin (100 mg/liter) provided by Nacalai Tesque Inc., Kyoto, Japan. The fibroblasts were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere until reaching 90% confluence. The device was sterilized by ultraviolet light irradiation for 30 min before cell seeding. For cytotoxicity evaluation, NIH3T3 cells with a density of 2 × 104 cells/well were plated on the device surface (diameter: 6 mm) in a 96-well plate. Cells cultured in a normal cell culture plate were used as controls. Cell viability was then assessed 48 h later using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan), following the kit’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a PerkinElmer multimode microplate reader, EnSpire Manager software (version 4.13.3005.1482).

ECoG electrode fabrication

To fabricate ECoG electrodes made of BIPIN, the detailed process are shown as follows: ⅰ. the procedure begins by thoroughly cleaning the glass substrate with isopropyl alcohol and acetone. ⅱ. Following the cleaning process, Novec (1700:7100 = 1:6) is spin-coated onto the glass substrate at 5000 rpm and subsequently annealed in an inert oven at 80 °C for 10 min to eliminate any residual solvent. ⅲ. The substrates are then transferred to a parylene coater, where a 2.5 μm layer of parylene is deposited. ⅳ. Subsequently, SU-8 is spin-coated onto the substrate at 5000 rpm, followed by UV irradiation for 3 min. The substrate is then transferred to a nitrogen glovebox and annealed at 180 °C for 30 min. ⅴ. After annealing, BIPIN are deposited onto the SU-8 layer, initiating the patterning process of the BIPIN. ⅵ. A photoresist is spin-coated onto the BIPIN, followed by thermal annealing with 110 °C for 30 s. ⅶ. Selective exposure using 365 nm UV light for 3 min is then applied to pattern the photoresist to solidify the photoresist in the exposed (cured) regions. ⅷ. The excess (uncured) photoresist is removed by immersing the sample in the developer for 1 min, leaving the patterned photoresist. ⅸ. The BIPIN film outside the patterned photoresist is removed by scraping, while the regions protected by the photoresist remain intact. ⅹ. The patterned BIPIN films are rinsed with water and dried with a nitrogen blow to remove any residues from the surface. ⅺ. The photoresist is removed by thoroughly rinsing the sample with acetone and isopropanol until the photoresist is completely dissolved, thereby exposing the previously protected BIPIN electrodes. xii. Once the BIPIN are patterned, a 100 nm layer of gold is thermally evaporated onto the BIPIN to create the contacting wire. xiii. Another layer of SU-8 is spin-coated onto the BIPIN with 5000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 95 °C for 30 s. xiv. Selective UV exposure is performed on the SU-8 using a mask with 365 nm UV light at 7 s, and then annealed at 110 °C for 90 s. xv. and excess SU-8 is removed by immersing into the developer for 90 s to expose the electrodes for contact. xvi. Finally, the electrodes are carefully peeled off the substrate, completing the fabrication process.

Animal surgery and electrode array implantation

Procedures for acute ECoG recordings, and two-photon imaging were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yonsei University Health System (IACUC No: 2022-0057) and adhered to the ethical guidelines set forth by the NIH. Adult male wild-type mice (C57BL/6NCrlOri; inbred substrain, 8–10 weeks old) purchased from Orient BioScience (South Korea) and were acclimated for one week in the Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) Zone of the Avison Biomedical Research Center, Yonsei University College of Medicine. The animals were housed in a temperature-controlled facility at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity, under a standard 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Additionally, the ECoG monitoring experiments and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis were approved under a separate protocol (IACUC-202512-2176-02) and conducted in the Semi-SPF Zone of the Yonsei Laboratory Animal Research Center, utilizing the same animal strain and acclimation protocol.

The mice were anesthetized and secured in a stereotaxic frame. Following hair removal, a 1 cm incision was made on the scalp. A drill was employed to remove approximately 0.2 cm² of the right parietal bone, centered 3 mm to the right of the midpoint between the bregma and lambda, to expose the cerebral cortex. The electrode array made of BIPIN was then meticulously placed over the exposed cerebral cortex.

In vivo electrophysiological signal recording

Following the implantation of the electrode array made of BIPIN, a reference wire electrode was inserted into the opposite hemisphere. Both the electrode array made of BIPIN and the reference wire were connected to the Intan recording system (Intan RHD 2132 system, Intan Technologies, Los Angeles, California). Electrophysiological signals were recorded and analyzed using the RHX Data Acquisition Software (3.3.1). The signals were sampled at a rate of 30 kHz, with a band-pass filter set between 0.1 and 6 kHz and a 60 Hz notch filter applied to eliminate power line interference.

For ECoG recording experiment, longitudinal signal monitoring was conducted at intervals of 3, 7, and 10 days post-implantation. Between the recording sessions, the mice were maintained in their standard housing environments to recover. The animals were housed in a temperature-controlled facility at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity, under a standard 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. The same signal acquisition parameters and filtering configurations were consistently applied across all recording days to accurately assess the temporal stability and reliability of the device under physiological conditions.

Data analysis of 9-channel electrophysiological signals and spectrogram

The files saved by the Intan recording system were loaded into Matlab for analysis of the recorded electrophysiological signals. A low pass filter with a cut-off frequency of 300 Hz was applied to the nine channels, and the filtered signals were plotted. The spectrogram was also plotted using customized Matlab scripts.

Two-photon intravital imaging

Following initial animal surgical procedures, a subsequent step for intravital imaging involved positioning a 5 mm round cover glass directly over the electrode on the cerebral cortex (Supplementary Fig. 35), which was then secured using cyanoacrylate glue. A customized metal ring from Live Cell Instrument, South Korea, was installed around the cranial window, and the edges were sealed with dental resin from B.J.M Laboratory, Or-Yehuda, Israel, to facilitate the use of a water-immersion lens. Mice equipped with an angel ring were mounted in a custom-designed stereotaxic head fixation system for live imaging. Texas Red-dextran (70 kDa, 2.5 mg/kg, Invitrogen, USA) was injected into the retro-orbital sinus to highlight blood vessels. The brain was imaged using an Olympus FVMPE Multiphoton Microscope (FV31S-SW, version 2.6) with a 900 nm wavelength. Images were captured at a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels through a 25× water immersion lens, covering a total depth of 50 µm beneath the cerebral cortex. All captured images were then analyzed using the Image-J software (version 1.54 k).

Immunohistochemistry test in vivo

To evaluate in vivo biocompatibility, 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized using a mixture of Zoletil (30 mg/kg) and Rompun (10 mg/kg). Following a ~ 1 cm incision along the scalp, a 5 × 5 mm craniotomy was performed using a surgical drill. The BIPIN and control AgNWs films, pre-cut to 3 mm in size, were carefully positioned onto the exposed cortical surface. At 5 days post-implantation, the animals were transcardially perfused with a 0.9% saline solution, followed by systemic fixation via the injection of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The extracted whole brains were sliced into 2 mm-thick blocks, post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight, and subsequently cryoprotected by immersion in a 30% sucrose solution for 2 days. Once fully equilibrated with sucrose, the brain tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura 4583) and sectioned at a thickness of 16 μm using a cryostat (Thermo HM252). For the immunofluorescence staining, the tissue slices were incubated in a blocking buffer containing 5% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature (RT). The sections were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody targeting Rat Anti-CD68, Monoclonal [FA-11] (Abcam, ab53444, Lot Number:1080382-69, 1:500). The following day, a secondary antibody (Goat Anti-Rat IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488); Polyclonal; Abcam ab150157, Lot Number:1142796-1, 1:500) was applied for 1 h at RT. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI using a mounting medium containing DAPI (abcam ab104139), and the slides were coverslipped. To ensure the removal of unbound reagents, the tissues were thoroughly washed three times with 1× PBS (Biosesang) between all consecutive steps. Finally, fluorescence images for each experimental group were acquired utilizing an LSM 710 confocal microscope (ZEISS, ZEN software 2011) equipped with a 20× objective lens.

Statistics and reproducibility

The intravital two-photon imaging experiments on the cerebral cortex were independently repeated three times under consistent parameters to ensure reproducibility. The other detailed statistics and reproducibility of the experiments were shown in the legends of the figure.
