Burden and correlates of anemia in IgA nephropathy versus other primary glomerulonephritides

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnemia is a frequent but often underrecognized complication of primary glomerulonephritis (PGN). This study aimed to determine the prevalence and determinants of anemia in a large multicenter PGN cohort, with particular focus on immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN), and to evaluate its clinical, laboratory, and histopathological correlates.MethodsData were retrieved from the Turkish Society of Nephrology Glomerulonephritis Database (TSN-GOLD). A total of 5,500 patients with biop-valuesy-proven PGN were included, of whom 1,571 (28.6%) had IgAN. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and histopathological findings were analyzed. Anemia was defined according to World Health Organization criteria. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors for anemia.ResultsThe prevalence of anemia was approximately 35% in both the overall PGN cohort and the IgAN subgroup. Patients in the lowest hemoglobin tertile (< 11.8 g/dL) were older, had lower eGFR, serum albumin, and total protein, as well as higher serum creatinine, urea, and inflammatory markers. Histologically, mesangial hypercellularity, interstitial inflammation, and particularly tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis (T1-T2) were more common in this group. Multivariate analysis identified older age, female sex, lower eGFR, hypoalbuminemia, and advanced tubular atrophy as independent risk factors for anemia. Importantly, PGN subtype was also an independent determinant: compared with IgAN, membranoproliferative GN carried a markedly higher anemia risk, whereas minimal change disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis showed lower risk.ConclusionsAnemia is a common and clinically relevant comorbidity in PGN, strongly associated with both functional and structural renal impairment. Beyond reduced kidney function, disease-specific mechanisms contribute to anemia in IgAN, including chronic low-grade inflammation and recurrent hematuria. Recognition of PGN subtype as an independent determinant underscores the need for tailored evaluation and management of anemia in these patients.Trial registrationThis study was registered retrospectively in an appropriate clinical trial registry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05085-8.

Introduction

Primary glomerulonephritides (PGN) are among the leading causes of chronic kidney disease and end-stage kidney disease worldwide and display substantial heterogeneity in clinical course and pathology [1]. Within this spectrum, IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common PGN, defined by dominant mesangial IgA deposition with variable endocapillary hypercellularity, segmental sclerosis, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis/atrophy captured by the Oxford MEST-T classification [2–6]. While proteinuria, hematuria, and progressive loss of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) shape the typical disease trajectory, anemia is a frequent comorbidity that may modify symptoms, quality of life, and downstream outcomes [3–6].

The biology of anemia in kidney disease is multifactorial. Reduced renal erythropoietin (EPO) production from fibroblast-like interstitial cells in the renal cortex, inflammation-driven iron sequestration via hepcidin, deficiencies of iron/folate/vitamin B12, and urinary losses of transferrin and other proteins in the setting of heavy proteinuria may all contribute. Because the tubulointerstitial compartment is the principal niche for EPO-producing cells, interstitial injury could influence both the timing and severity of anemia beyond the effect of eGFR alone [4–7]. Clinically, anemia is linked to fatigue, diminished exercise tolerance, neurocognitive effects, and higher cardiovascular risk, underscoring the need to understand its distribution and determinants across PGN subtypes in order to refine risk stratification and management.

Existing studies of anemia in IgAN and other PGN subtypes have important limitations. Many are single-center series with modest sample sizes; others aggregate heterogeneous PGN forms, obscuring disease-specific signals [2, 6]. Definitions of anemia vary, histopathologic granularity-particularly comprehensive reporting of Oxford MEST-T components often incomplete, and key confounders such as eGFR and proteinuria are inconsistently handled. As a result, it remains uncertain whether anemia in IgAN is primarily a surrogate for reduced kidney function or a phenotypic indicator of disease-specific pathology, especially tubulointerstitial damage, that carries independent clinical meaning.

The Turkish Society of Nephrology Glomerular Diseases (TSN-GOLD) registry provides a robust platform to address these gap-values by capturing standardized clinical, laboratory, and biop-valuesy data from adults with biop-valuesy-proven PGN across multiple centers. Leveraging this resource, the present study aims to: (i) quantify and compare the prevalence and distribution of anemia across major PGN subtypes; (ii) examine, within IgAN, the associations between hemoglobin levels and demographic, clinical, and Oxford MEST-T features; and (iii) identify independent correlates of anemia after adjustment for kidney function and other confounders. We hypothesize that anemia patterns differ meaningfully across PGN subtypes and that, in IgAN, anemia is associated with tubulointerstitial fibrosis/atrophy independent of eGFR. By clarifying the clinicopathologic context of anemia in IgAN, this study seeks to inform more precise risk stratification at diagnosis and to motivate earlier, targeted supportive care.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective, multicenter cohort study using the Turkish Society of TSN-GOLD registry. TSN-GOLD is a national, web-based platform that prospectively captures standardized demographic, clinical, laboratory, and renal biop-valuesy data from participating nephrology centers. For the present analysis, we extracted all consecutive adult entries between 1 January 2005 and 31 December 2022 with biop-valuesyproven PGN and at least one same-episode hemoglobin measurement.

Study population and eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were aged ≥ 18 years at the time of kidney biop-valuesy and had a histopathologic diagnosis of one of the following PGN subtypes: IgAN, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), membranous nephropathy (MN), membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN), or minimal change disease (MCD). We excluded secondary causes of glomerulonephritis or sclerosis (e.g., lupus nephritis, infection related GN including chronic hepatitis B/C, diabetic kidney disease, drug induced or adaptive/obesity related FSGS) when explicitly coded by the registry or the submitting center. We also excluded duplicate entries, biop-valuesies lacking light microscopy, immunofluorescence or electron microscopy supportive of a PGN diagnosis, and records missing hemoglobin or core covariates required for multivariable modeling.

Definitions and measurements

Anemia was defined a priori by World Health Organization thresholds: hemoglobin < 13.0 g/dL in men and < 12.0 g/dL in women, measured on the index hospitalization/visit corresponding to the biop-valuesy episode [8]. Hemoglobin was analyzed as both a binary variable (anemia yes/no) and a continuous measure; for descriptive displays we also stratified hemoglobin into tertiles (HB-T1-T3) based on the cohort distribution. Nephrotic syndrome was defined as proteinuria ≥ 3.5 g/day accompanied by hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin < 3.0 g/dL) with or without edema and hyperlipidemia. Nephritic syndrome was defined as the presence of hematuria (≥ 5 red blood cells per high-power field), variable degrees of proteinuria (< 3.5 g/day), hypertension, and impaired renal function with or without edema [9]. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI creatinine equation [10]. Proteinuria was recorded as 24-hour urinary protein excretion or protein-to-creatinine ratio (PCR); when both were available, 24-hour protein excretion was preferentially used. Serum albumin, lipids, and other routine chemistries were abstracted from same-episode measurements.

Histopathology in IgAN followed the Oxford classification (MEST): mesangial hypercellularity (M0/M1), endocapillary hypercellularity (E0/E1), segmental sclerosis (S0/S1), tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis (T0/T1/T2), and crescents (C0/C1/C2, when available). For nonIgAN PGN, subtypespecific descriptors reported by contributing centers were captured. Immunofluorescence (IF) intensity (IgA, IgG, IgM, C3, C1q, kappa, lambda) was semiquantitatively graded according to local practice (e.g., 0 to 3+); for analysis, IF signals were harmonized into ordinal scales [11]. Biop-valuesy adequacy metrics (number of glomeruli, globally sclerosed glomeruli) were recorded when available.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was prevalent anemia at the time of diagnostic biop-valuesy. Secondary outcomes included (i) initial clinical remission status following firstline therapy as defined by the registry (complete or partial remission versus no remission) and (ii) initiation of kidney replacement therapy (KRT: chronic dialysis or kidney transplantation) during followup. Followup time was calculated from biop-valuesy date to the earliest of KRT initiation, last clinic contact, or end of the registry window.

Data preprocessing and quality control

Data were screened for implausible values (e.g., hemoglobin < 5 or > 20 g/dL; eGFR > 200 mL/min/1.73 m²) and cross-checked against source records. Categorical variables were standardized using uniform definitions, and unknown or missing categories were not included in percentage denominators. Variables with > 15% missingness were excluded from multivariable analyses and summarized descriptively where clinically relevant. No statistical imputation was performed; all regression models were conducted on complete-case datasets.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR) to reflect central tendency and dispersion, and categorical variables as counts and percentages (n, %). Distributional normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test and visually with Q-Q plots; skewness and kurtosis were additionally reviewed to characterize symmetry and outliers. For approximately normal continuous variables, two-group comparisons used the independent-samples Student’s t test, and comparisons across three or more group-values used oneway ANOVA. When ANOVA was significant, Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was applied for pairwise posthoc comparisons. When parametric assumptions were not satisfied, the Mann-Whitney U test (two group-values) and the Kruskal-Wallis test (≥ 3 group-values) were used; following Kruskal-Wallis, Bonferroni adjusted p-values were calculated for pairwise contrasts. Associations between categorical variables were assessed with Pearson’s chisquare test or Fisher’s exact test where expected frequencies were small. A multivariable logistic regression model (ENTER method) was constructed to identify factors associated with the presence of anemia (dependent variable coded 1 = yes, 0 = no). Based on univariate significance and clinical relevance, the following independent variables were entered: age; sex (reference: female); baseline eGFR; serum albumin; 24-hour proteinuria; interstitial fibrosis (Oxford T score; reference T0); tubular atrophy (T score; reference T0); and primary glomerulonephritis subtype with IgAN as the reference category. Active smoking was not included due to a high proportion of missing data. Categorical variables were coded using simple contrasts, and continuous variables were entered on their original scale. Twosided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics

This study received ethics approval from the Istanbul University-Istanbul Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval no.: [2011/1131 − 614], date: [08.07.2011]). The TSN-GOLD registry operates under the governance of the Turkish Society of Nephrology with approvals from participating centers. Given the retrospective design and use of deidentified data, the need for written informed consent was waived by the committee. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Study population characteristics

A total of 7,539 adult patients with biop-valuesy-proven PGN, TSN-GOLD, were retrospectively screened. After excluding 1,683 cases due to other diagnoses-acute proliferative GN (n = 66), non-IgA mesangioproliferative GN (n = 119), rapidly progressive crescentic GN (n = 477), and secondary glomerular diseases (n = 921)-the remaining 5,856 records were reviewed. An additional 356 patients without baseline hemoglobin values were excluded, resulting in a final analytical cohort of 5,500 patients (Fig. 1). Among these, 1,571 (28.6%) were classified into the IgAN group and 3,929 (71.4%) into the non-IgAN group. The demographic, clinical, laboratory, histopathological, immunofluorescence, treatment, and follow-up characteristics of these two group-values are presented in Table 1. Detailed data are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Comparison of demographic, clinical, laboratory, and histopathologic features between IgA nephropathy and non-IgA GN patients

Patients in the IgAN group were significantly younger than those in the non-IgAN group. Clinical presentation differed markedly between the groups: nephritic syndrome was more common in the IgAN group (34.5% vs. 8.7%, p < 0.001), whereas nephrotic syndrome predominated in the non-IgAN group (70.9% vs. 22.8%, p < 0.001). Median estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was lower in the IgAN group (70.9 vs. 100.9 mL/min/1.73 m², p < 0.001), while hemoglobin levels were comparable between groups.

Histopathologically, IgAN patients exhibited significantly higher rates of mesangial hypercellularity compared with non-IgAN cases (84.5% vs. 46.7%, p < 0.001; Table 1). When examined by individual PGN subtype, mesangial hypercellularity was most frequent in MPGN (87.1%) and IgAN (84.5%), intermediate in FSGS (51.7%), and less common in MN (34.0%) and MCD (24.5%) (Table 2). On immunofluorescence, IgA and C3 deposits were significantly more common in IgAN (p < 0.01). Immunosuppressive therapy was used less frequently in IgAN (50.4% vs. 63.3%, p < 0.001). While overall remission rates were similar (71.5% vs. 73.8%, p = 0.290), partial remission was more frequent in the IgAN group (63.9% vs. 47.7%, p < 0.001), and relapse was significantly less common (31.0% vs. 37.7%, p = 0.020). Median follow-up duration was longer in IgAN (32 vs. 26 months, p < 0.001). Among IgAN patients, the distribution of MEST-T lesions was as follows: mesangial hypercellularity (M1) in 55.3%, endocapillary hypercellularity (E1) in 19.2%, segmental glomerulosclerosis (S1) in 38.6%, tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis grade 1 (T1) in 32.5%, and grade 2 (T2) in 17.2% of patients. These findings indicate that a significant proportion of IgAN patients already show active or chronic histological injury patterns that may influence long-term renal prognosis.

Comparison of key clinical and pathological features among PGN subtypes

Comparison by biopsy diagnosis

Demographic, clinical, laboratory, histopathological, and treatment/follow-up parameters across primary glomerulonephritis subtypes (FSGS, IgAN, MN, MCD, and MPGN) are summarized in Table 2.

There were significant differences in age, sex distribution, diabetes prevalence, baseline eGFR, hemoglobin levels, proteinuria, histopathologic markers (e.g., mesangial proliferation, subepithelial deposition), laboratory parameters (e.g., lipids, albumin, ESR), and use of immunosuppressive therapy (p < 0.05 for all).

Notably, MCD patients had the youngest age (median 33 years), highest baseline hemoglobin (13.8 g/dL), and best renal function (eGFR: 119 mL/min/1.73 m²). In contrast, MN patients were the oldest (median 48 years) and exhibited the highest degree of proteinuria (median 6,200 mg/day). IgAN patients were predominantly male (62.4%) and had moderate proteinuria (median 1,885 mg/day), while rapidly progressive non-crescentic clinical presentations (defined by nephritic syndrome with rapid eGFR decline) were most common in this group (33%). FSGS and MN group-values had higher rates of type 2 diabetes.

Histologically, MPGN showed the most severe pathological profile, including high rates of endocapillary proliferation (86%), interstitial inflammation/fibrosis (> 80%), and strong C3 positivity. MN patients predominantly exhibited subepithelial deposits (36%) and IgG positivity (92%). Extended histopathologic and immunofluorescence features by PGN subtype are summarized in Supplemental Table S2.

Use of immunosuppressive therapy and remission rates were highest in MCD (59% and 85%, respectively), whereas relapse was also most frequent in this group (34%). The need for chronic dialysis or transplantation was more common in MPGN (7.5%) and FSGS (6.2%).

Patients with higher hemoglobin levels were significantly younger and more likely to be male (HB-T1: 37.7%, HB-T2: 50.5%, HB-T3: 83.5%; p < 0.001). As hemoglobin increased, median body weight and height also increased (from 73 kg to 80 kg and from 164 cm to 170 cm, respectively; both p < 0.001). In contrast, the prevalence of pretibial edema and active smoking significantly decreased across increasing tertiles (p = 0.006 and p < 0.001, respectively).

Laboratory analyses revealed a stepwise improvement in renal function and systemic inflammation with increasing Hb levels: median eGFR increased (71 → 101 mL/min/1.73 m², p < 0.001), while BUN, creatinine, and sedimentation rate progressively decreased (p < 0.001). Serum total protein and albumin levels increased significantly across tertiles (p < 0.001).

Histopathologically, mesangial proliferation, interstitial inflammation, and advanced tubular atrophy (≥ Grade 2) were significantly more common in the lowest Hb tertile (p < 0.001), indicating more aggressive renal damage in anemic patients. Despite similar rates of immunosuppressive therapy (~ 59–61%), the initial remission rate increased in parallel with Hb level (67.8% in HB-T1 to 77.5% in HB-T3, p < 0.001). Chronic dialysis or transplantation was significantly more common in the lowest tertile (HB-T1: 19.1% vs. HB-T3: 7.2%, p < 0.001).

These findings suggest a clear and consistent association between lower hemoglobin levels and worse clinical, histological, and prognostic profiles. Additional variables stratified by hemoglobin tertiles are presented in Supplemental Table S3.

Predictors of anemia: multivariate logistic regression

To identify independent predictors of anemia, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed (see Table 4). The dependent variable was the presence of anemia (defined as Hb < 12 g/dL in females and < 13 g/dL in males). Variables significantly associated with anemia in univariate analyses-including age, sex, baseline eGFR, serum albumin, daily proteinuria, interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and biopsy diagnosis was included in the model. Discussion

In this large multicenter registry of biopsy-proven primary glomerulonephritides (PGN), we examined the prevalence, determinants, and prognostic implications of anemia, with a particular focus on IgA nephropathy (IgAN). Our analysis demonstrated that anemia is a highly prevalent comorbidity in PGN, affecting roughly one-third of patients, and is strongly associated with adverse clinical, laboratory, and histopathologic risk factors. Independent predictors of anemia included older age, female sex, lower eGFR, lower serum albumin, higher proteinuria, and the degree of tubular atrophy. Notably, PGN subtype itself emerged as an independent determinant: compared with IgAN, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN) carried a substantially higher anemia risk, whereas focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) and minimal change disease (MCD) were associated with lower risk. These findings underscore that anemia in PGN is not merely a reflection of reduced kidney function but also a disease-specific process.

Our cohort of IgAN patients represented nearly 29% of the PGN population, were younger, and more frequently male compared with non-IgAN patients, consistent with known epidemiology [5, 6, 8]. IgAN is typically diagnosed in the second to third decade of life and shows male predominance in most Western cohorts [3, 5]. The lower baseline eGFR in IgAN compared with other PGN subtypes (median 70.9 vs. 100.9 mL/min/1.73 m²) reflects the often insidious, progressive course of IgAN and late diagnosis [3].

Anemia prevalence of ~ 35% in our cohort is consistent with prior CKD and IgAN reports [12, 13]. Importantly, when patients were stratified into hemoglobin tertiles, those in the lowest tertile (< 11.8 g/dL) displayed worse renal function, higher inflammatory markers, and more severe histopathologic injury-particularly mesangial proliferation, interstitial inflammation, and tubular atrophy. These findings are in line with prior studies linking anemia to chronic hypoxia, systemic inflammation, and progressive interstitial damage [14, 15]. Our observation that anemia predicted lower remission rates and higher dialysis risk strengthens its role as a prognostic biomarker in PGN.

A unique contribution of our study is the demonstration that PGN subtype independently predicts anemia:

MPGN vs. IgAN: Patients with MPGN showed a markedly higher anemia risk. This likely reflects the aggressive natural history of MPGN, characterized by chronic complement dysregulation, persistent immune activation, and rapid progression to interstitial damage [16]. Persistent complement activation triggers proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) that upregulate hepcidin, reduce iron availability, and blunt erythropoietin (EPO) responsiveness, leading to functional iron deficiency and shortened red cell survival [17]. Although IgAN also involves mesangial complement deposition, particularly C3 [18, 19], the systemic complement activation in MPGN is generally more severe, explaining its stronger association with anemia.

MCD vs. IgAN: In contrast, MCD was associated with a significantly lower risk of anemia. This aligns with its clinical phenotype: preserved eGFR, minimal systemic inflammation, and rapid reversibility of proteinuria with corticosteroid therapy [20]. Despite heavy proteinuria, MCD patients often maintain stable hemoglobin, reflecting the absence of chronic interstitial injury and systemic immune activation.

FSGS vs. IgAN: FSGS patients had ~ 24% lower anemia risk compared with IgAN. The heterogeneity of FSGS (primary, genetic, secondary) complicates interpretation [21].However, in many cases, systemic inflammation is less pronounced than in IgAN. Furthermore, chronic or recurrent hematuria-a hallmark of IgAN [3]-is uncommon in FSGS and may contribute to the differential anemia risk, since prolonged microscopic or macroscopic hematuria can drive iron deficiency over time.

Together, these comparisons highlight that anemia in PGN arises from disease-specific mechanisms: systemic inflammation and complement activation in MPGN, preserved renal reserve in MCD and some FSGS, and unique IgAN-related mechanisms such as hematuria and mucosal immune dysregulation.

Collectively, these mechanisms suggest that anemia in IgAN is not simply a marker of reduced eGFR but reflects disease-specific pathophysiology.

An unexpected inverse association between proteinuria and anemia was observed. This is counterintuitive, since heavy proteinuria is typically linked to advanced glomerular injury and adverse prognosis [26]. A likely explanation is confounding by subtype, particularly MCD, where nephrotic-range proteinuria can occur despite preserved hemoglobin levels. Additional contributors may include collinearity with serum albumin and eGFR, as well as residual data limitations. Sensitivity analyses excluding MCD attenuated the association, supporting this interpretation.

Mesangial hypercellularity was highly prevalent in IgAN (84.5%) and MPGN (87.1%), but less frequent in FSGS, MN, and MCD (Table 2). Such proliferative lesions may indirectly contribute to anemia by sustaining interstitial inflammation and fibrosis, which impair peritubular fibroblast function and erythropoietin production [27]. Segmental sclerosis and, most importantly, tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis (Oxford T) reflect chronic scarring that further reduces the erythropoietin-producing niche. These mechanisms may explain why IgAN and MPGN, with both mesangial activity and interstitial damage, carry a higher anemia burden than other PGN subtypes. Among histological variables, the Oxford T score (tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis) independently predicted anemia, consistent with loss of the interstitial fibroblast niche required for erythropoietin production. M, E, and S lesions were summarized descriptively but not included in regression due to collinearity and incomplete scoring outside IgAN. This limits our ability to test whether mesangial or endocapillary proliferation independently contributes to anemia.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of our study include its large sample size, nationwide multicenter design, and comprehensive biopsy-value-based characterization with Oxford MEST-T scoring in IgAN. Importantly, we evaluated anemia across multiple PGN subtypes within a unified dataset, allowing for robust comparisons.

Limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design precludes causal inference. Hemoglobin was assessed cross-sectionally at biop-valuesy, and longitudinal anemia trajectories were not captured. Data on iron indices, vitamin B12, folate, and endogenous EPO levels were unavailable, and treatment information (iron supplementation, ESA use, HIF stabilizers) was not analyzed in detail. Moreover, hematuria was incompletely captured, further constraining mechanistic interpretation of anemia risk across PGN subtypes. Finally, generalizability to other populations should be cautious, given ethnic variability in IgAN epidemiology and anemia prevalence [8, 9, 26].

Our findings emphasize the importance of routinely assessing anemia in PGN, with heightened vigilance in MPGN and advanced IgAN. Anemia at diagnosis identifies patients at risk for worse outcomes and should inform both prognostication and treatment planning. In IgAN, clinicians should consider mechanisms beyond reduced GFR, including hematuria-induced iron deficiency and immune dysregulation.

A further limitation of this study is the absence of age-stratified analyses across histological subtypes, which may have limited our ability to fully assess the impact of age-related comorbidities on anemia risk.

The contribution of hematuria-related iron loss remains uncertain, as hematuria data were not standardized and were not included in the multivariable model.

Additionally, detailed hematological parameters (including iron indices, vitamin B12/folate levels, reticulocyte count, and endogenous EPO levels), as well as anemia-related treatment data, were not available in our study, which limited our ability to distinguish anemia subtypes and evaluate treatment effects.

Future research should focus on prospective evaluation of anemia trajectories in IgAN, incorporation of iron metabolism and EPO measurements, and exploration of whether early correction of anemia improves renal and cardiovascular outcomes. Interventional trials with iron therapy, ESAs, and novel hypoxia-inducible factor stabilizers in IgAN and other PGN subtypes may clarify whether anemia is a modifiable risk factor.

Conclusion

In conclusion, anemia is highly prevalent in PGN, with disease-specific differences. MPGN carries the highest anemia burden, while MCD and FSGS have lower risk. In IgAN, anemia is independently associated with tubular atrophy and likely driven by a combination of chronic inflammation, persistent hematuria, and mucosal immune dysregulation. These findings suggest that anemia in IgAN is not merely a consequence of reduced kidney function but a reflection of the underlying disease biology. Regular monitoring of hemoglobin, early investigation of underlying mechanisms, and individualized correction strategies should be integral to the management of patients with IgAN.
