Improving decision reliability in transport safety engineering through a machine learning-based model
Authors: Ruikang Yan, Jialin Li, Xingze Liu, Zihan Zhang, Jason Ka Yiu Cheung, Jian Le, Aaron Kaiqiang Zhou, Nanbo Aaron Zhang, Xushuai Zhang, Jiachen Yao, Chang Yuan, Hanrui Feng, Yuchen Huang, Faan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ensuring defensible policy inferences and robust decision reliability is fundamental for multi-criteria decision-making activities, particularly in transport safety engineering. This study developed a hybrid preference function-nested and machine learning-embedded decision model, i.e., EXPROM II–K-means with a linear discriminant analysis, to provide a robust support system for policy setting and decision making. The proposed model incorporates a refined nonparametric preference function into the EXPROM II method to alleviate cognitive burden on decision makers while improving the flexibility of preference articulation. Meanwhile, linear discriminant analysis, a supervised machine learning-based dimensionality reduction algorithm, is embedded to reduce data dimensionality and project features into axes that maximize class separability, which simplifies the data structure, filters noise, and emphasizes informative attributes. This transformation improves the clustering performance of K-means clustering, which yields clearer and more actionable patterns in high-dimensional or noisy datasets. A case study on transport safety engineering across the Asia–Pacific Economic Cooperation countries demonstrates the reliability, scalability, and effectiveness of the model in guiding resource allocation and strategic prioritization. The proposed framework offers a practical, interpretable, and intelligent support tool to manage complex decision tasks with enhanced stability and reliability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-58220-8.

Introduction

Transport safety remains a major global challenge with substantial public health and economic consequences1. Road traffic accidents cause over 1.35 million deaths annually (~ 2.5% of global fatalities) and 50 million injuries, ranking as the leading cause of death among individuals aged 5–29 years2,3. The burden is disproportionately concentrated in Southeast Asia and the Western Pacific, accounting for over 50% of global fatalities2. This issue is particularly acute in low- and middle-income APEC economies, where road accidents result in economic losses of 3–5% of GDP4. These challenges highlight the urgent need for reliable policy frameworks supported by robust analytical tools. Advanced MCDM approaches, capable of synthesizing complex and heterogeneous data, are essential for improving decision reliability and enabling evidence-based transport safety interventions5.

Although the MCDM framework offers significant potential for addressing complexities related to transport safety engineering, the selection and implementation of appropriate MCDM methodologies are fraught with challenges because of model-related uncertainty, sensitivity, and instability6. Subsequently, the reliance on inputs is often subject to variability7, further complicating the decision-making process, which emphasizes the need for models that not only ensure stability and reliability but also address issues of sensitivity and uncertainty. Over the years, researchers have developed a plethora of MCDM methods tailored to transport safety applications. Despite the advancements, significant gaps remain in the application of the MCDM methods to transport safety, e.g., their stability and adaptability across diverse contexts, which leads to four key motivations underpinning this study: (1) The lack of universally practicable SPIs at a regional level hinders the ability of policymakers to measure and benchmark progress effectively. (2) Existing MCDM methods are often geographically constrained and lack the flexibility to address the diverse backgrounds of different regions or countries, such as the APEC, which includes 20 countries. (3) Current methods emphasize data aggregation while overlooking key elements of the MCDM process (such as grouping, deconstruction, and decomposition), which are crucial for refining interventions and supporting nuanced decision making. (4) Selecting the most suitable method from a broad range of options in the MCDM database continues to pose a significant challenge for decision makers. This difficulty arises from the sensitivity and uncertainty inherent in the model8, which can be influenced by varying the inputs. These factors have a profound effect on the robustness and reliability of policy decisions, highlighting the importance of addressing these issues for effective and defensible policymaking. This gap is exacerbated by the instability of several MCDM methods when used with small-to-medium-sized datasets9. Consequently, there is a critical need to develop a systematic and scientifically grounded MCDM framework that can accommodate varying geographical contexts while incorporating the essential phases of the MCDM process (weighting, aggregation, grouping, deconstruction, and decomposition) and prioritizing efficiency, stability, and reliability.

This study aimed to address these gaps by developing a hybrid preference function-nested and machine learning-embedded model, referred to as the EXPROM II–K-means with LDA. This study demonstrates that integrating a parameter-free preference modeling mechanism with LDA-enhanced clustering within a unified MCDM framework substantially improves decision reliability by simultaneously reducing parameter sensitivity, enhancing data separability, and stabilizing ranking and grouping outcomes under heterogeneous and uncertain conditions. Overall, this study makes three interrelated contributions to transport safety decision-making. First, it develops a tailored and context-sensitive set of safety performance indicators (SPIs), establishing a comprehensive and scalable framework for assessing, monitoring, and benchmarking transport safety across the APEC region, while enhancing cross-country comparability and accommodating regional heterogeneity. Second, it advances the MCDM paradigm by integrating a parameter-free, nonparametric preference function into the EXPROM II method, thereby eliminating the need for subjective parameter calibration and reducing the cognitive burden on decision makers. In parallel, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) is embedded to project data into a lower-dimensional space that preserves class separability, thereby improving the performance of K-means clustering. This hybrid framework enhances clustering stability, strengthens robustness against noise and outliers, and improves analytical accuracy in complex and high-dimensional decision environments. Third, the proposed model generates actionable insights for strategic planning and resource allocation in transport safety engineering, providing empirically grounded evidence to support policy interventions aimed at reducing traffic-related fatalities and injuries, mitigating socioeconomic losses, and advancing global sustainability agendas, particularly the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) of halving road traffic deaths by 2030.

Literature review

APEC institutional capacity in transport safety management

APEC, which encompasses economies in various stages of development, has made significant strides in addressing transport safety challenges by employing a multilevel institutional framework4. Coordinated efforts at international, regional, and national levels have helped leverage the strengths of each tier to address complex safety issues and achieve sustainable progress.

At the international level, APEC functions as a collaborative platform that fosters dialogue, policy coordination, and the sharing of best practices among member economies. For example, APEC aligns its efforts with global initiatives such as the United Nations’ Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021–2030 and the WHO Road Safety Frameworks by recognizing transport safety as a vital public health and economic issue2. Further, the APEC Transportation Working Group plays a pivotal role in developing strategies for enhancing transportation safety, improving data quality, and promoting evidence-based policymaking. Key international contributions include capacity-building programs, technical workshops, and joint projects designed to harmonize standards across member economies. These initiatives prioritize reducing road traffic fatalities, increasing the adoption of safety technologies, and ensuring alignment with global road safety targets10. Further, APEC encourages data sharing and the development of SPIs to support monitoring and benchmarking across economies.

At the regional level, APEC facilitates collective action among its members by addressing shared challenges and promoting cross-border cooperation. Regional initiatives focus on addressing critical issues such as infrastructure safety, vehicle standards, and driver behavior to aid in harmonizing regulations and foster innovation11. Further, the regional strategies of APEC involve multistakeholder engagement, which draws participation from governments, private-sector entities, and non-governmental organizations. Collaborative projects have been launched to enhance the safety of vulnerable road users such as pedestrians and motorcyclists, who account for a significant proportion of road fatalities in low- and middle-income economies12. Regional workshops and research programs enable sharing technical expertise, fostering a culture of continuous improvement and mutual learning. Further, APEC promotes the adoption of advanced technologies such as intelligent transport systems and automated vehicles for improving road safety outcomes. APEC ensures that its members remain at the forefront of emerging transport safety solutions by providing a regional forum for exchanging ideas and innovations.

At the national level, APEC members implement transport safety management frameworks tailored to their unique socioeconomic and infrastructural contexts. Governments develop and enforce legislation that addresses critical areas such as speed management, drink driving, seatbelt use, and helmet compliance13. National road safety strategies often include public awareness campaigns, investments in safer infrastructure, and the establishment of dedicated road safety agencies or units. APEC supports these efforts by providing technical assistance and policy guidance for strengthening institutional capacity14. For example, initiatives aimed at improving data collection and analysis have helped member economies identify high-risk areas and prioritize interventions effectively. In addition, APEC facilitates the dissemination of policy tools and methodologies such as MCDM frameworks, which can enable evidence-based decision making and resource allocation15.

Overall, the institutional capacity of APEC for transport safety management reflects a multilevel approach that integrates global standards, regional cooperation, and national implementation. APEC has established itself as a leading force in advancing transport safety across the Asia–Pacific region by fostering collaboration, harmonizing regulations, and supporting capacity-building efforts. However, continued emphasis on addressing disparities among member economies and leveraging innovations is essential for achieving long-term safety goals.

MCDM procedure

Uncertainty of the MCDM process

Uncertainty is an inherent challenge in the MCDM process, arising from the complexity of real-world decision problems and limitations in data, models, and human judgment16–19. It manifests in multiple forms, including data uncertainty, model uncertainty, decision-maker subjectivity, and environmental variability, all of which affect the robustness, stability, and credibility of decision outcomes20,21. To address these challenges, the literature has developed three major streams of approaches aimed at enhancing decision reliability, which refers to the consistency, reproducibility, and credibility of decision outcomes under variations in data, model assumptions, and methodological configurations.Robustness-based methods. A fundamental line of research focuses on improving decision reliability through robustness-oriented techniques, such as sensitivity analysis and scenario analysis. These approaches evaluate how variations in input data, parameter settings, or contextual assumptions influence decision outcomes, thereby assessing the resilience of rankings under perturbations. By systematically exploring alternative configurations, such as changes in weights, normalization methods, or decision scenarios, robustness-based methods help identify stable solutions and reduce the risk of misleading conclusions caused by model sensitivity. This stream emphasizes the importance of transparency and reproducibility in decision-making, particularly in dynamic environments where criteria relevance and system conditions evolve over time22.Uncertainty modeling approaches. A second stream addresses decision reliability by explicitly modeling uncertainty within the MCDM framework. For instance, fuzzy-based MCDM methods capture vagueness in human judgments, while probabilistic and stochastic models account for variability in data and system behavior. Recent studies have further advanced this stream by incorporating more sophisticated fuzzy and group decision-making frameworks. Possibilistic approaches have been developed to model criteria interdependencies under uncertain environments23, while intuitionistic and hesitant fuzzy methods have been widely used to address ambiguity and hesitation in expert evaluations, particularly in contractor selection and production decision contexts24,25. In addition, dynamic and hierarchical fuzzy group decision models have been proposed for complex systems such as green supplier evaluation and infrastructure risk assessment, enabling multi-stage and multi-expert decision processes26,27. However, despite their theoretical advantages, these methods often introduce additional complexity and parameterization, which may increase computational burden and reduce interpretability, particularly in large-scale or data-constrained applications28–31.Stability-oriented hybrid MCDM models. A third and increasingly prominent research direction focuses on developing hybrid MCDM frameworks designed to enhance the stability of decision outcomes. These approaches aim to reduce ranking variability arising from different methodological choices, such as weighting schemes, normalization techniques, or aggregation operators. By integrating multiple analytical components—such as objective weighting methods, clustering techniques, or machine learning algorithms—hybrid models seek to improve consistency, reduce sensitivity to methodological assumptions, and enhance overall decision reliability. In particular, combining MCDM with data-driven techniques (e.g., clustering or dimensionality reduction) has shown promise in stabilizing results in high-dimensional and noisy environments. Nevertheless, challenges remain in achieving a unified framework that simultaneously addresses weighting, aggregation, and grouping while maintaining interpretability and computational efficiency.

Recent advances in MCDM and clustering for transport safety

Research into transport safety measurement has seen a surge in interest in the combination of MCDM and clustering methods, as these methods are able to address the inherent limitations of individual approaches.

The latest developments in hybrid MCDM frameworks have greatly enhanced the precision of decision-making. BWM–TOPSIS–SAW has been applied to prioritize road safety improvements, with BWM providing subjective weights and TOPSIS and SAW generating comparable rankings32. AHP–MULTIMOORA frameworks have been developed for the purpose of assessing injury severity. These frameworks enable the categorization of accident-prone cities in phases33. Spherical fuzzy SWARA–MARCOS has demonstrated effectiveness in evaluating rural road safety. By combining ratio-based weighting with compromise ranking, it helps to make strong decisions when things are unclear or uncertain34. Other methods, such as DEA–MCDM combinations, have also emerged, enabling dynamic performance evaluation with uncertainty handling for cross-national comparison35. However, traditional MCDM techniques like AHP, TOPSIS, and their fuzzy or objective variants (e.g., CRITIC) often face challenges in computational scalability when applied to large-scale datasets with numerous alternatives. At the same time, methods for clustering, such as K-means, can efficiently detect structure and behavioral patterns, but they lack the preference-based ranking that is needed for decision-making36.

The combination of clustering methods with MCDM has completely changed the way large-scale transport safety is analyzed, leading to significant advancements in the field. The success of clustering in transport analysis has made it important to use it with MCDM methods. While K-means alone identifies behavioral patterns and safety preferences37, combining it with MCDM techniques, such as CRITIC–TOPSIS, enables both pattern recognition and systematic evaluation38. In micro-mobility safety monitoring, PROMETHEE–K-means frameworks have been integrated with deep learning to evaluate model performance across data augmentation strategies. This enables the optimal ensemble to be selected for helmet detection, thereby improving micro-mobility safety assessments39. Similarly, for highway infrastructure management, Entropy–TOPSIS–fuzzy C-means has been applied to assess traffic interruptions on highways to Tibet. Entropy–TOPSIS quantifies severity across duration, mileage, and impact. Meanwhile, the emergency planning process is organized into four levels by means of fuzzy C-means clustering40. In urban traffic risk prediction, Entropy–TOPSIS–K-means has also been used for real-time risk prediction on urban arterial roads; Entropy–TOPSIS computes risk scores from vehicle-driver-road-environment data, while K-means assigns risk levels for training ensemble prediction models41. For regional safety benchmarking, MEREC–COCOSO–DBSCAN has been used to evaluate road safety development for OAS countries over 2010–2020; MEREC–COCOSO generates composite safety scores, while DBSCAN clusters countries into groups for policy reference and benchmarking42.

In conclusion, these integrated MCDM–clustering frameworks have shown wide applicability in transport safety, which makes them an asset in the field. These frameworks support diverse transport safety tasks. Applications range from prioritizing safety interventions and assessing injury severity to real-time urban risk analysis and regional policy benchmarking. Combining evaluation and classification, these methods provide reliable tools for making decisions based on evidence in today’s complex transportation systems.

EXPROM II applications and limitations

EXPROM II, which is an extension of the PROMETHEE family of outranking methods, has broad applicability in decision-making contexts involving multiple, often conflicting, evaluation criteria43. Its flexibility and capacity to process qualitative and quantitative information make it a valuable tool across a wide range of domains. This method has been applied to environmental and energy policies to evaluate the sustainability of various alternatives, including renewable energy systems, waste management strategies, and climate adaptation measures44. Its ability to accommodate partial preference relations and manage conflicting objectives aligns well with the complex tradeoffs in such sectors.

In urban development and infrastructure prioritization, EXPROM II supports structured decision making by enabling policymakers to compare alternatives based on socioeconomic, environmental, and technical indicators, even when data are incomplete or only partially reliable45. The outranking mechanism of the method, which ranks alternatives by comparing their pairwise dominance across criteria, helps identify robust choices in contexts where precise utility measurement is either difficult or inappropriate. In addition, it is effective in evaluating safety performance across regions or countries because it enables nuanced ranking without requiring complete compensation among criteria, which is an important consideration in safety–critical applications.

Despite its practical advantages, EXPROM II has several limitations that can affect its applicability and reliability in certain scenarios. For example, a key limitation is its reliance on traditional parametric preference functions, which often require the specification of parameters such as preference and indifference thresholds46. These parameters are set by decision makers based on subjective judgment or expert opinion, which can introduce bias and reduce the objectivity of the decision-making process. The necessity of parameter tuning increases the complexity of applying the method, particularly in contexts where such thresholds are difficult to define because of data ambiguity or limited domain knowledge.

In addition, most classical preference functions used in EXPROM II do not adapt well to the distribution or variability of actual data, potentially leading to distorted preference assessments. When alternatives have similar performance values, traditional preference functions may fail to capture meaningful distinctions, resulting in low discriminatory power. This sensitivity to the parameter settings and data structure can undermine the stability, transparency, and reliability of the final rankings produced by the method. These limitations highlight the need for careful parameter handling and potential integration with other techniques, such as nonparametric preference functions, to improve their robustness and usability in real-world applications.

However, the primary limitation of the k-means clustering algorithm is its uncertainty, which can arise because of several factors and affect the stability and accuracy of the resulting clusters. One significant limitation of k-means clustering is the blind area selection of the initial center points47, which reduces the effectiveness and efficiency of the algorithm. Poor selection of the initial center points can result in suboptimal clustering outcomes because the algorithm may converge to the local minima rather than finding the global optimal solution48. The choice of the number of clusters (K) is another critical factor because an inappropriate K value can significantly degrade clustering quality49.

In addition, K-means clustering is highly sensitive to outliers, which significantly affects the positioning of cluster centroids and leads to suboptimal clustering results50. This limitation is especially problematic in real-world datasets, where outliers are common and can distort the true underlying patterns in the data51. Finally, in high-dimensional spaces with correlated features, K-means clustering suffers from the “curse of dimensionality,” which occurs because the distance between any two points becomes less meaningful with an increase in the number of dimensions, making it challenging to identify distinct clusters52. Overall, K-means clustering remains the cornerstone of clustering analysis because of its simplicity and adaptability; however, addressing its limitations is crucial for its effective application in complex and dynamic environments.

Research gaps

Gaps

Despite substantial progress in methodologies for decision-making in transportation safety engineering, several critical gaps remain that hinder the development of robust, generalizable, and policy-relevant analytical frameworks.

First, there is no universally accepted set of safety performance indicators (SPIs). Existing indicator systems often overlook the dynamic evolution of transport systems and fail to adequately capture the heterogeneity of real-world transport scenarios. This limitation constrains their ability to provide consistent and comparable assessments across contexts, underscoring the need for adaptive and continuously updated indicator frameworks.

Second, the geographical scope of most MCDM applications remains limited, typically focusing on single-country cases or narrowly defined regions. Such confinement reduces the external validity and transferability of findings, particularly in international settings characterized by significant socioeconomic and infrastructural disparities. Expanding MCDM applications to broader, cross-regional contexts is therefore essential for developing globally adaptable and policy-relevant solutions.

Third, existing MCDM methodologies lack an integrated analytical framework that simultaneously incorporates the full decision-making process, including weighting, aggregation, clustering, deconstruction, and decomposition. Prior studies have predominantly emphasized aggregation-based evaluation, while largely neglecting clustering for pattern discovery and decomposition for interpretability. This fragmentation restricts the ability to generate nuanced insights and limits the explanatory power of decision outcomes.

Finally, many existing approaches exhibit sensitivity and instability, particularly when applied to small- and medium-sized datasets or under varying methodological configurations. Such instability undermines the robustness and reliability of decision results, posing challenges for practical implementation in data-constrained environments.

Significance

To address these gaps, this study proposes a unified and systematic decision-making framework that integrates weighting, aggregation, clustering, deconstruction, and decomposition within a single analytical structure. Specifically, a novel nonparametric preference function is embedded into the EXPROM II method to eliminate the need for parameter tuning, thereby reducing cognitive burden and enhancing methodological transparency. In addition, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) is incorporated to improve the performance of K-means clustering by enhancing feature separability and mitigating the influence of noise and outliers.

Compared with standalone MCDM methods (e.g., TOPSIS, VIKOR, AHP-based approaches), the proposed framework enhances decision reliability by introducing a parameter-free, nonparametric preference function within EXPROM II, which eliminates subjective threshold calibration and reduces sensitivity to parameter settings—an issue widely acknowledged in prior studies. Compared with traditional clustering or hybrid MCDM–clustering approaches (e.g., TOPSIS–K-means, CRITIC–TOPSIS–K-means), our model improves clustering quality by incorporating LDA as a supervised dimensionality reduction step, which projects the data into a space that maximizes class separability. This significantly enhances cluster stability, reduces the influence of noise and outliers, and mitigates the “curse of dimensionality,” which are common limitations of conventional K-means implementations. In contrast to existing studies that typically focus on partial stages of the MCDM process (e.g., weighting and aggregation only), the proposed framework provides a more integrated analytical structure, simultaneously combining weighting, aggregation, and grouping. This allows the model to generate both reliable rankings and interpretable groupings, thereby offering richer and more actionable insights for decision-making.

Through this hybrid design, the proposed framework enhances the stability, interpretability, and applicability of MCDM-based analyses, offering a more reliable and scalable tool for transport safety decision-making.

Methodology

EXPROM II with an enhanced preference function

The EXPROM II method, originally proposed by Diakoulaki and Koumoutsos in 199153, offers a practical approach to derive solutions through pairwise comparisons of alternatives and generate reliable rankings based on readily accessible data. Within this framework, the final evaluation is represented by the net flow value46. The overall preference index is obtained by combining strong and weak preference measures, which yields a precise representation of the preference intensity between competing alternatives. A key advantage of this technique is that it bypasses the need for data normalization, minimizing the risk of information distortion or loss.

Six distinct types of preference functions are commonly employed for accommodating various attribute characteristics: usual, U-shaped, V-shaped, level, V-shaped with indifference, and Gaussian. However, selecting the most appropriate function for a given criterion remains a complex task because most of these functions require parameter specifications (e.g., thresholds of preference and indifference). In addition, many existing functions fail to adequately reflect the actual data values, often producing overly rigid or generalized outputs.

We propose a reformative nonparametric preference function that is versatile and applicable across all indicators to overcome these limitations. The proposed function generates more realistic and context-sensitive outcomes by explicitly incorporating specific data values; simplifies the decision-making process by eliminating the need for parameter calibration; and addresses the challenge of making distinctions when evaluation scores for different alternatives are closely aligned by amplifying the separation between scores. Thus, the proposed function enhances clarity and supports more rigorous and evidence-based decision making.

K-means enhanced with LDA

K-means clustering, introduced by Steinhaus and developed by MacQueen54, is a popular unsupervised machine learning algorithm used for partitioning a dataset into k distinct clusters. This method groups data points such that points within the same cluster are more similar to each other than to those in other clusters. The algorithm iteratively minimizes the within-cluster sum of squares, also known as inertia, by updating cluster centroids and assigning points to the nearest clusters55. Further, it captures the essence of clustering by optimizing the compactness and separation of groups in the feature space, and it is based on Euclidean distance, which makes it well-suited for numerical data, where proximity in multidimensional space corresponds to similarity. The reliance of k-means clustering on the Euclidean distance, sensitivity to initial conditions, and assumptions regarding cluster shape and density limit its effectiveness in complex datasets56.

In this study, we improved the model performance of K-means clustering by incorporating LDA, which is a supervised dimensionality reduction technique that projects data onto a lower-dimensional space and maximizes the separation between classes. Further, LDA enhances K-means by preprocessing data, reducing dimensions, and emphasizing features that improve cluster separability. This synergy improves clustering accuracy and efficiency, which makes it useful for high-dimensional datasets where interpretability and computational performance are critical, through the following:Dimensionality reduction The LDA reduces the number of features by focusing on directions that maximize class separability, simplifying the data structure for clustering.Feature transformation LDA reduces noise and irrelevant variability by projecting data into a space where clusters are more distinguishable, which enables k-means to work on cleaner and more informative features.Cluster separability LDA identifies axes that separate existing clusters (if known labels are used temporarily during preprocessing), which makes the data more suitable for unsupervised methods such as K-means.Efficiency Reduced dimensions result in faster clustering by minimizing computational overhead.

Innovative SPC–EXPROM II–K-means with LDA

The flowchart of the proposed model is presented in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Flowchart of the proposed model.

Flowchart of the proposed model.

The flowchart illustrates the sequential steps of the proposed decision-making framework. Steps 1–2 involve constructing and normalizing the decision matrix. Step 3 applies the SPC method to derive criterion weights tj from the symmetry modulus vector Q = [q1,q2,…,qn]. Step 4 integrates these weights into EXPROM II with a nonparametric preference function to compute weak and strict preferences, net flows, and final rankings of alternatives. Step 5 enhances grouping through a two-stage K-means with LDA procedure: (i) preliminary K-means generates tentative pseudo-labels, (ii) LDA projects the data into a lower-dimensional space that maximizes cluster separability, and (iii) K-means is reapplied to the transformed data to obtain the final clusters. The arrows between Steps 4 and 5 highlight the interaction where ranking results inform clustering, ensuring the framework simultaneously produces interpretable rankings and coherent groups for real-world policy application.

The proposed framework introduces several methodological innovations at the formulation level. First, a nonparametric, parameter-free preference function is developed and embedded into the EXPROM II method, in which preference intensities are directly derived from data-dependent quantities (e.g., ideal/anti-ideal solutions and maximum spread), eliminating the need for predefined thresholds and subjective calibration. This leads to an adaptive preference structure that improves discrimination and stability when alternatives exhibit similar performance. Second, the computation of weak and strict preferences is reformulated using normalized, data-driven scaling, allowing preference intensities to reflect the intrinsic distribution of the dataset rather than fixed functional assumptions. Third, a two-stage clustering formulation integrating K-means with LDA is proposed, where preliminary clustering results are used as pseudo-labels to guide LDA-based projection, and clustering is subsequently performed in a transformed feature space that maximizes class separability. This sequential coupling enhances cluster validity and robustness in high-dimensional and noisy settings. Collectively, these formulations establish a unified and data-adaptive mathematical structure that improves the stability, interpretability, and reliability of MCDM outcomes.

Results and discussion

Case study

Index construction

SPIs play a vital role in safety management frameworks by providing valuable information on the progress and current status of organizational strategies and activities related to safety for preventing accidents and casualties. These indices help organizations assess the effectiveness of their safety management systems and identify areas for improvement.

In recent decades, a multitude of research efforts have introduced diverse indicators for measuring and monitoring transport safety, serving as the foundation for this study. Based on a human–vehicle–road–environment–management framework, this study established a collection of SPIs that enhance safety management efficiency, support ongoing advancements, and foster safer operational conditions, as depicted in Fig. 2.Fig. 2SPIs adopted in this study.

SPIs adopted in this study.

Data collection

The datasets included information for 19 APEC member economies, sourced from the ASEAN-Japan Transport Partnership, Global Status Report on Road Safety, and the World Bank.

Variations in SPIs through the past 10 years.

High-income countries such as Singapore (SG), Japan (JP), and Australia (AU) sustain the lowest fatality rates. Emerging economies like China (CN), Malaysia (MY), and Thailand (TH) display strong reductions post-2019, reflecting enhanced enforcement and vehicle safety. Papua New Guinea (PG) and Peru (PE) continue to exhibit high fatality rates, indicating systemic challenges. X2 indicates fatalities per 100,000 inhabitants. A gradual decrease is observed across all economies. Developed economies maintain low baseline values; however, VN and PE show limited progress due to weak enforcement.

Managerial implications

The proposed framework offers a practical and scalable decision-support tool for transport authorities and policymakers engaged in safety management. By jointly providing robust rankings and interpretable groupings, the model enables governments to move beyond one-size-fits-all strategies toward evidence-based prioritization and targeted intervention design. In particular, the grouping results allow decision makers to benchmark against structurally comparable peers rather than absolute leaders, facilitating more realistic policy learning and transfer. At the same time, the decomposition of composite scores provides granular diagnostics of performance drivers, enabling authorities to identify whether deficiencies stem from infrastructure quality, enforcement effectiveness, behavioral factors, or institutional capacity. This supports more efficient allocation of limited resources by directing investments toward the most impactful dimensions of safety improvement.

From a strategic perspective, the framework enhances the reliability and credibility of policy evaluation under data uncertainty and methodological variability. The integration of a parameter-free preference structure and LDA-enhanced clustering reduces sensitivity to subjective assumptions and noisy data, making the results more stable and defensible for policy use. This is particularly valuable in real-world governance contexts where data are incomplete, heterogeneous, or evolving. Moreover, the framework is inherently transferable and adaptable, allowing it to be applied across different regions, time periods, and policy scenarios. As such, it can support continuous monitoring, scenario analysis, and forward-looking planning, contributing to the design of adaptive safety strategies aligned with long-term objectives, including the achievement of global targets such as reducing road traffic fatalities under the Sustainable Development Goals.

Concluding remarks

Conclusion

This study developed a structured and intelligent decision-making framework termed the SPC–EXPROM II–K-means model with LDA by embedding a refined nonparametric preference function and machine learning technique into the MCDM process. Designed as a policy and decision-support tool for transport safety engineering, the model addresses the key limitations of conventional MCDM approaches by enhancing interpretability, reducing decision uncertainty, and improving clustering accuracy. The practical utility and computational robustness of the proposed technique are demonstrated through a case study involving APEC countries, wherein extensive simulations and sensitivity analyses validate the effectiveness of the model across diverse decision contexts.

Future studies

This study highlighted several avenues for future research in this field. First, revising the SPIs framework is essential for aligning it with advancements in intelligent transportation systems and emerging technologies. A more nuanced framework would provide a comprehensive view of the current transport safety landscape, capturing the complexities inherent in evolving systems. Second, with the growing availability of global data caused by globalization and the Internet, it is becoming increasingly feasible to conduct studies across larger regions. This broader scope allows the proposed model to be applied more widely, enabling countries to learn from the experiences of others beyond their immediate geographical context. Third, progress in neural networks and machine learning has enabled the integration of artificial intelligence simulations into the methodological framework. Such an integration can enhance the reliability and robustness of the model, leading to more accurate results and providing policymakers with refined, data-driven recommendations. Subsequent research can build on this study by exploring future directions, which lead to improved methodologies and more effective policy guidance. In addition, as new data become available, the framework can be regularly reapplied to provide continuous monitoring of safety performance, helping decision makers track progress toward global targets such as the UN SDGs.
