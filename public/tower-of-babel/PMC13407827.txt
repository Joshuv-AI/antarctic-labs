Longitudinal Trajectories and Predictors of Health-Related Quality of Life in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus: A 2-Year Longitudinal Cohort Study
Authors: Wan-Yun Hsu, Ching-Yi Lai, Li-Yun Tsai, I.-Chieh Chen, Jun-Peng Chen, Kai-Ting Chen, Chung-Mao Kao, Yen-Ju Chen, Kuo-Tung Tang, Wei-Ting Hung, Wen-Nan Huang, Yi-Ming Chen

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHealth-related quality of life (HRQoL) is a critical patient-reported outcome in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). We aimed to evaluate longitudinal trajectories of HRQoL and identify associated clinical and demographic determinants.MethodsThis longitudinal cohort study included 1005 patients with SLE from the Asia Pacific Lupus Collaboration. HRQoL was assessed using the Short Form-36 (SF-36) at baseline, 12, and 24 months. Disease activity and organ damage were recorded using validated composite indices (SLEDAI-2 K and SLICC damage index). Generalized estimating equations (GEE) analyzed longitudinal trajectories of Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores.ResultsAmong 915 women (91.0%) with median age 35.2 years, both PCS and MCS scores remained stable over 24 months (PCS: p = 0.822; MCS: p = 0.819). Age ≥ 50 years was associated with lower PCS (β = − 5.80, p = 3.85 × 10 −9) but higher MCS (β = 2.09, p = 0.032). Male sex predicted higher MCS (β = 3.50, p = 2.70 × 10–5). Higher SLEDAI-2 K was associated with lower PCS and MCS (both p < 0.05). SLICC damage index negatively affected PCS (β = − 1.87, p < 0.001) and MCS (β = − 0.75, p = 0.006). Musculoskeletal and cardiovascular damage had the strongest associations with reduced PCS, while no organ-specific damage remained significantly associated with MCS after adjustment.ConclusionsHRQoL in SLE remained stable over 2 years. Disease activity and cumulative organ damage were the primary determinants. Organ-specific damage differentially influenced physical but not mental health domains, underscoring the need for domain-tailored assessment and management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40744-026-00864-z.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by multi-organ involvement and an unpredictable disease course that significantly impacts patients' daily functioning and wellbeing [1, 2]. SLE diagnosis is based on internationally recognized classification criteria that have been refined to improve sensitivity and specificity. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) has emerged as a critical patient-reported outcome that complements traditional clinical measures of disease activity and organ damage, and has been proposed as a possible treatment target [2, 3]. The chronic nature of SLE, combined with treatment burden and potential for irreversible organ damage, creates a complex interplay of factors affecting patients' physical, mental, and social functioning [3, 4]. Advances in understanding SLE pathogenesis and therapeutic options continue to reshape its clinical management [4–6].

The Short Form-36 (SF-36) Health Survey is a widely validated generic HRQoL instrument providing a comprehensive assessment across eight health domains, summarized into Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores [7, 8]. Its reliability and validity in SLE populations have been well-established across diverse populations [9–11].

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated impaired HRQoL in patients with SLE compared with healthy populations [8, 12]. Demographic factors, including older age and female sex have been associated with poorer HRQoL outcomes [12, 13]. The broader socioeconomic consequences of SLE, including reduced work productivity and increased healthcare utilization, further underscore the multidimensional burden of the disease [13, 14]. Treatment advances, including biological therapies, have demonstrated measurable improvements in HRQoL, highlighting the responsiveness of patient-reported outcomes to effective disease management [14–16]. Psychosocial factors, including depression, anxiety, and fatigue emerge as stronger predictors of HRQoL than traditional clinical measures [16, 17]. Disease activity, as measured by the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI), shows variable associations with HRQoL [15, 16], while organ damage consistently correlates with reduced physical functioning [16, 17]. Whether different organ damage domains differentially affect physical versus mental HRQoL remains unclear.

Longitudinal data examining HRQoL trajectories over extended periods remain limited, with previous studies constrained by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, or focus on specific subpopulations [15–17]. Understanding temporal patterns of HRQoL is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies and identifying patients at risk of deterioration [17, 18]. Notably, large-scale longitudinal data from Asia–Pacific cohorts examining the differential impact of organ-specific damage domains on physical versus mental HRQoL remain scarce. This study aimed to evaluate changes in HRQoL over a 2-year period in a large cohort of patients with SLE and identify demographic and clinical factors associated with different HRQoL trajectories, with particular emphasis on examining whether different organ damage domains have differential impacts on physical and mental HRQoL.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This longitudinal cohort study utilized data from the Asia Pacific Lupus Collaboration (APLC) cohort [19], with Taichung Veterans General Hospital contributing the largest number of patients among all APLC participating centers, accounting for more than 50% of the total APLC cohort. The study was conducted at our tertiary rheumatology center between January 2022 and December 2024. Eligible participants were patients ≥ 18 years with SLE diagnosed according to American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria [20], with regular follow-up care and ability to complete questionnaires in Chinese. A total of 1149 patients were initially screened, of whom 144 were excluded due to age below 18 years at enrolment. Of the remaining 1005 patients with complete baseline HRQoL data, 781 who completed assessments at all three time points (baseline, 12, and 24 months) were included in the final analytic sample. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (IRB No. CE17061B). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment. Patients were not involved in the design or conduct of this study.

Demographic and Clinical Variables

Comprehensive demographic and clinical data were systematically collected at baseline, including age, sex, education level (categorized as elementary, secondary, or tertiary), smoking status, employment status, and SLE disease duration. For analytical purposes, participants were stratified into three age groups: young-onset SLE (≤ 20 years), adult-onset SLE (20–50 years), and late-onset SLE (≥ 50 years) [21]. Depression and anxiety were identified based on ICD-10 diagnostic codes recorded after the date of SLE diagnosis. Depression was defined by codes F32 (depressive episode) and F33 (recurrent depressive disorder), and anxiety was defined by codes F40 (phobic anxiety disorders) and F41 (other anxiety disorders).

Disease Activity and Damage Assessment

Disease activity was assessed using the SLE Disease Activity Index-2000 (SLEDAI-2K) [22], categorized as inactive/low (0–5), moderate (6–12), and high (≥ 13). Cumulative organ damage was evaluated using the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) damage index (SDI) [23, 24], which assesses irreversible damage in 12 organ systems present for at least 6 months and not attributable to active inflammation.

Clinical Manifestations

ACR criteria manifestations were systematically documented, including mucocutaneous, musculoskeletal, renal, neurological, hematological, and immunological features. Subcategories of damage accrual from the SLICC index were documented, including osteoporosis, cataracts, avascular necrosis, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular events, and renal dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 50 ml/min/1.73 m2).

Health-Related Quality of Life Assessment

HRQoL was measured using the validated Traditional Chinese version of the SF-36 questionnaire at baseline, 12, and 24 months [25]. The SF-36 comprises 36 items across eight health domains, aggregated into PCS and MCS scores normalized to a general population mean of 50 (SD = 10), with higher scores indicating better HRQoL.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 22.0 (International Business Machines Corp., NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables, assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) were employed to analyze longitudinal changes in PCS and MCS scores over 24 months, accounting for within-subject correlation and missing data under the missing-at-random assumption. The model included time (0, 12, 24 months), age group, sex, baseline SLEDAI-2 K categories, disease duration, baseline SDI, education level, and major organ involvement as fixed effects. An exchangeable working correlation structure with robust variance estimation was selected based on Akaike and Bayesian information criteria. To examine inter-individual variability in HRQoL trajectories, group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was performed separately for PCS and MCS using SAS PROC TRAJ, with the optimal number of trajectory groups determined by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and a minimum group size of 5%. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed) with 95% confidence intervals reported.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 1005 patients with SLE were enrolled and completed baseline assessments (Table 1), with a retention rate of 94.3% (n = 948) at 12 months and 77.7% (n = 781) at 24 months. The cohort was predominantly female (91.0%, n = 915), with the majority aged 20–50 years (72.8%, n = 732) and a median disease duration of 20.0 years (IQR: 13.0–22.0). Most patients had tertiary education (85.6%, n = 860), and smoking prevalence was low (5.9%, n = 59). In terms of disease manifestations, renal involvement was present in 47.5% (n = 477), hematological disorders in 62.2% (n = 625), and neurological manifestations in 7.9% (n = 79). The median SLEDAI-2 K score was 2.0 (IQR: 0.0–4.0), with most patients in remission or low disease activity (SLEDAI ≤ 4: 80.8%; SLEDAI 5–9: 15.3%; SLEDAI ≥ 10: 3.9%), and the median SDI was 0.0 (IQR: 0.0–1.0). Longitudinal Changes in Health-Related Quality of Life

Both physical and mental HRQoL components remained stable throughout the 24-month follow-up period. PCS scores showed no significant temporal change from baseline (median 57.1, IQR 51.1–61.5) to 24 months (p = 0.822), and MCS scores similarly remained stable (baseline median 54.1, IQR 47.7–60.7; 24-month p = 0.819) (Table 1).

Multivariable GEE analysis revealed that several demographic and disease-related factors were strongly associated with outcomes, while time had no significant effect on HRQoL (Table 2). For PCS, older age (≥ 50 years) was the strongest predictor of lower scores (β = − 5.80, 95% CI − 7.84 to − 3.75, p < 0.001). Disease activity (SLEDAI-2 K: β = − 0.12, 95% CI − 0.24 to − 0.01, p = 0.039) and organ damage (SLICC: β = − 1.87, 95% CI − 2.39 to − 1.35, p = 4.16 × 10⁻14) demonstrated significant negative associations. For MCS, male sex predicted higher scores (β = 3.50, 95% CI 1.82–5.17, p = 2.70 × 10⁻5) and older age was associated with better MCS (β = 2.09, 95% CI 0.18–4.00, p = 0.032). Depression was significantly associated with lower MCS (β = − 6.35, 95% CI − 11.18 to − 1.52, p = 0.010), whereas its effect on PCS did not reach statistical significance (β = − 1.04, 95% CI − 5.88 to 3.80, p = 0.673). Anxiety was not significantly associated with either PCS (β = − 1.49, 95% CI − 3.74 to 0.76, p = 0.194) or MCS (β = − 1.67, 95% CI − 4.57 to 1.23, p = 0.259).Table 2Factors associated with PCS and MCS scores: Results from generalized estimating equations analysisVariablePCSMCSBSE95% CIp valueBSE95% CIp valueSexFemale (ref)Male0.810.69– 0.55–2.160.2463.500.851.82–5.172.70 × 10⁻5Age group < 20 years (ref)--– – – – – – 20–50 years0.180.54– 0.87 ~ 1.230.7420.810.60– 0.36 – 1.990.176 ≥ 50 years– 5.801.04– 7.84 to – 3.753.85 × 10⁻⁹2.090.970.18–4.000.032*SLE duration (years)– 0.050.03– 0.11 ~ 0.010.1340.050.03– 0.01 to 0.110.123TimeBaseline (ref)––– – – – – – 12 months0.000.25– 0.49 to 0.491.0000.000.28– 0.55 to 0.551.00024 months0.070.29– 0.51 to 0.640.822– 0.080.36– 0.78 to 0.620.819Depression– 1.042.47– 5.88 to 3.800.673– 6.352.47– 11.18 to 1.520.010*Anxiety– 1.491.15– 3.74 to 0.760.194– 1.671.48– 4.57 to 1.230.259SLEDAI score (Disease activity)– 0.120.06– 0.24 to 0.010.039*– 0.160.06– 0.29 to 0.040.012*SLICC Total Score (Organ damage)– 1.870.26– 2.39 to 1.354.16 × 10⁻14– 0.750.27– 1.29 to – 0.220.006**SLE Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, CI confidence interval, GEE generalized estimating equations, MCS Mental Component Summary, PCS Physical Component Summary, SE standard error, SLEDAI-2 K SLE Disease Activity Index-2000, SLICC Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01

Age significantly influenced HRQoL trajectories over 24 months (Fig. 1). Patients aged ≥ 50 years demonstrated consistently lower PCS scores compared to younger groups, with significant differences at 12 and 24 months (p < 0.01 for both). All age groups maintained stable physical functioning without significant temporal deterioration. Male patients maintained significantly higher MCS scores than females at baseline (mean difference: 3.8 points, 95% CI 1.2–6.4, p < 0.01) and 12 months (mean difference: 2.9 points, 95% CI 0.3–5.5, p < 0.05), though this gap narrowed by 24 months. PCS scores showed similar stable patterns between sexes, with no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05 at all timepoints).

Disease Activity Analysis

Figure 1 shows longitudinal SF-36 PCS and MCS trajectories stratified by baseline disease activity. Patients with high disease activity had significantly lower baseline PCS scores (mean 50.3, 95% CI 47.8–52.8) compared with remission (56.5, 95% CI 55.1–57.9) and low activity groups (55.8, 95% CI 54.2–57.4), with a mean difference of − 6.3 points (95% CI − 11.2 to − 1.4, p < 0.05). Over 24 months, the high-activity group demonstrated substantial improvement, converging with the other groups by study end. For MCS, baseline scores were similar across groups (52.8–53.2), with all groups showing parallel improvement trajectories.

Physical Health Outcomes by Damage Status

Damage-stratified analysis demonstrated heterogeneous trajectories in PCS scores across different organ damage domains (Supplementary Fig. 2). Statistically significant improvements over 24 months were observed in osteoporosis (52 → 54 points, p < 0.01), reduced eGFR (< 50 ml/min/1.73 m2) (p < 0.01), and diabetes mellitus, which displayed a characteristic biphasic response with transient functional decline at 12 months followed by recovery (p < 0.05). Across all damage categories, patients consistently demonstrated lower physical functioning compared to those without damage.

Mental Health Outcomes by Damage Status

MCS score analysis revealed relatively uniform trajectories across damage groups with minimal between-group differences over time (Fig. 2). Unlike physical health outcomes, mental health scores showed no statistically significant differences in temporal changes between patients with and without various damage components. Similarly, three trajectory classes were identified for MCS (Fig. 3B): high (40.7%), with scores around 58; moderate (49.0%), with scores around 47; and low-recovering (10.3%), beginning at approximately 34, with a transient decline at 12 months, and partially recovering to approximately 36 by 24 months.

Baseline characteristics differed significantly across PCS trajectory groups (Table 3). The persistently low group had a higher proportion of patients aged ≥ 50 years (41.3% vs. 15.8% and 7.9%, p = 3.68 × 10⁻12), higher SDI (p = 5.65 × 10⁻1⁹), and lower baseline PCS scores (median 28.8 vs. 43.6 and 55.2, p = 1.25 × 10⁻1⁰⁸) compared with the moderate and high groups. For MCS trajectory groups (Table 4), the low-recovering group had a higher proportion of female patients (98.1% vs. 93.5% and 86.3%, p = 2.51 × 10⁻5), higher baseline SLEDAI scores (median 2.5 vs. 2.0 and 2.0, p = 0.030), and the lowest baseline MCS scores (median 34.8 vs. 47.1 [moderate] and 58.1 [high], p = 4.74 × 10⁻12⁷) compared with the moderate and high groups. In the subgroup analysis stratified by disease activity (SLEDAI-2 K ≤ 4 vs. > 4), HRQoL remained stable across all three time points in both subgroups (Supplementary Table 1). Discussion

This 2-year longitudinal study demonstrated that both physical and mental HRQoL remained stable in patients with SLE, with no significant temporal changes in PCS or MCS scores (p = 0.822 and p = 0.819, respectively). Disease activity and cumulative organ damage—rather than time itself—were the primary determinants of HRQoL, and organ-specific damage differentially influenced physical but not mental health domains.

The stability of HRQoL over 24 months likely reflects the benefits of contemporary lupus management, including optimized disease control, timely monitoring, and integrated psychosocial support. This sustained stability is consistent with the principles of treat-to-target strategies in SLE, which advocate for minimizing disease activity and preventing organ damage as primary management goals [27]. Importantly, while group-level trajectories remained stable, considerable inter-individual variability was observed, indicating that group-level averages may obscure meaningful heterogeneity in patient experiences.

Age exerted divergent effects on physical and mental health domains. Patients aged ≥ 50 years demonstrated consistently lower PCS scores throughout the study period, consistent with age-related decline in physical functioning reported in previous cohorts [8, 9]. In contrast, the oldest group demonstrated better MCS scores in multivariable analysis (β = 2.09, 95% CI 0.18–4.00, p = 0.032) despite greater physical limitation. This pattern is consistent with the "disability paradox" described in chronic disease literature [15], whereby older patients exhibit better psychological adaptation potentially attributable to more realistic health expectations, enhanced coping strategies, and reduced occupational stressors [8, 17].

Sex-based differences were most pronounced in mental health domains. Male patients maintained significantly higher MCS scores at baseline and 12 months, consistent with previous studies demonstrating poorer mental health outcomes in women, potentially reflecting differential coping strategies, social role expectations, or biological factors [1, 17]. The convergence of sex differences over time suggests that structured disease management programs may help mitigate initial disparities in psychological wellbeing.

Regarding disease activity, higher SLEDAI-2 K scores were significantly associated with lower scores in both physical and mental health domains (β = − 0.12, p = 0.039 and β = − 0.16, p = 0.012, respectively), consistent with international cohort data [12]. The damage-stratified analysis revealed a critical dichotomy in how organ damage affects physical versus mental health. Patients with cumulative organ damage demonstrated lower baseline physical functioning across all damage categories, with osteoporosis, reduced eGFR, and diabetes mellitus showing statistically significant changes in PCS scores over 24 months. Renal involvement represents one of the most frequent and prognostically significant damage domains in SLE, with lupus nephritis contributing substantially to long-term physical morbidity [26]. In contrast, MCS scores remained stable across all damage categories. While this pattern may be consistent with psychological adaptation reported in the chronic disease literature [8, 15], alternative explanations should be considered. The stability of MCS scores may partly reflect the limited sensitivity of the SF-36 in detecting subtle changes in mental wellbeing, or selection bias inherent to an outpatient cohort in which patients with more severe psychological morbidity may be less likely to attend regular follow-up. These findings align with evidence that patient-reported symptoms, including pain and fatigue, are closely associated with both physical and mental HRQoL in SLE, and that their longitudinal impact may differ across domains [28]. To further examine whether the observed stability was attributable to a ceiling effect driven by the predominantly low disease activity in our cohort, we performed a subgroup analysis stratified by disease activity (SLEDAI-2 K ≤ 4 vs. > 4). HRQoL remained stable across both subgroups over 24 months, although patients with higher disease activity demonstrated lower baseline PCS scores (49.30 vs. 52.50), consistent with the primary analysis, suggesting that the overall stability is unlikely to be solely attributable to a ceiling effect (Supplementary Table 1). Although depression and anxiety were included as covariates, neither independently predicted PCS or MCS scores, possibly reflecting their low prevalence in this well-controlled outpatient cohort (1.7% and 5.1%, respectively), where the majority of patients were in remission or low disease activity.

To further characterize inter-individual variability in HRQoL trajectories, we performed GBTM. For PCS, three distinct trajectory classes were identified: the majority of patients followed a high trajectory (67.1%), while a moderate subgroup (28.4%) maintained intermediate physical functioning throughout follow-up, and a small but clinically important subgroup demonstrated a persistently low trajectory (4.6%). Notably, this persistently low PCS group was characterized by older age, higher cumulative organ damage, and markedly lower baseline physical functioning, suggesting that age-related physical decline and irreversible organ damage may be key drivers of sustained impairment. For MCS, three trajectory classes were similarly identified: a high group (40.7%) and a moderate group (49.0%) comprised the majority, while a low-recovering subgroup (10.3%), predominantly female with higher baseline disease activity, demonstrated an initial decline at 12 months followed by partial recovery by 24 months, possibly reflecting a period of transient psychological vulnerability in early follow-up.

Although prior work has applied joint trajectory modeling to simultaneously model PCS, MCS, and disease activity over longer follow-up periods in SLE [29], our study independently characterizes PCS and MCS trajectory classes within a 24-month window, offering a more granular view of early HRQoL dynamics in a well-defined Asian SLE cohort. The identification of a low-recovering MCS subgroup in particular highlights the importance of monitoring mental health trajectories beyond the first year of follow-up.

These findings indicate that although HRQoL was stable at the group level, clinically important subgroups exist whose trajectories are obscured by mean-level analyses. Patients in the persistently low PCS group may warrant earlier rehabilitation referral and closer physical functioning monitoring, while those in the low-recovering MCS group may benefit from targeted psychosocial support during the first year of follow-up. Baseline characteristics differed significantly across trajectory groups for both PCS and MCS (Tables 3 and 4), reinforcing the value of trajectory-based stratification as a complement to conventional longitudinal analyses in SLE research.

This study has several limitations. The single-center design, predominantly within a tertiary referral setting, may limit generalizability to community-based populations and diverse ethnic groups. Findings are most applicable to hospital-based Han Chinese patients with SLE in East Asia, where disease characteristics, genetic background, and healthcare systems share considerable similarities with our cohort, despite recruitment within the Asia Pacific Lupus Collaboration framework. The cohort's low disease activity (80.8% in remission) may restrict the applicability of findings to patients with more active disease, and a potential ceiling effect on HRQoL measurement cannot be entirely excluded. Socioeconomic status, treatment patterns, medication adherence, and comorbidities were not systematically assessed, and patient-reported outcomes including fatigue and pain were not routinely collected, potentially underestimating their contribution to HRQoL. The absence of comprehensive laboratory biomarkers additionally limits full characterization of disease phenotypes. Depression and anxiety were identified through clinical diagnoses rather than validated screening instruments such as the PHQ-9 or GAD-7, which may have led to underestimation of their true prevalence and impact on HRQoL. Finally, the SF-36 MCS may have limited sensitivity in capturing subtle fluctuations in mental wellbeing, and the apparent stability of MCS scores may partly reflect selection bias inherent to an outpatient setting, where patients with more severe psychological morbidity may be less likely to attend regular follow-up. The predominance of female participants (91.0%) is consistent with the known epidemiology of SLE [30]; however, findings may have limited applicability to male patients, who represent a distinct clinical subgroup with potentially different HRQoL trajectories. Detailed screening and exclusion records prior to enrolment were not available, and the reasons for loss to follow-up at 24 months (n = 224, 22.3%) were not systematically captured, which limits the transparency of participant flow. The focus on established disease also precludes extrapolation to newly diagnosed patients. Nevertheless, the 24-month longitudinal design, validated instruments, and large cohort size provide robust evidence for these temporal relationships.

Conclusions

Health-related quality of life in patients with SLE remained stable over 2 years, with disease activity and cumulative organ damage identified as primary determinants rather than time itself. Organ-specific damage differentially influenced physical but not mental health domains—a finding that underscores the need for domain-tailored assessment in clinical practice. Routine monitoring of both physical and mental HRQoL should be integrated into long-term management to enable individualized strategies addressing patients’ distinct physical and psychosocial needs.
