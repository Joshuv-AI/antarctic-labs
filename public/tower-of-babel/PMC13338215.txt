Identification of PI3K alpha inhibitors through large-scale virtual screening and integrated molecular modeling, biophysical characterization, and ADMET profiling
Authors: Yahya Khan, Attiqa Naz, Asad Ullah, Gulesehra Mujib, Luqman Khan, Maria Shoukat, Shaimaa A. M. Abdelmohsen, Meznah A. Alenzai, Muhammad Tahir Naseem, Asif Kamal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-associated mortality globally, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of all cases. The PI3K p110α catalytic subunit (PIK3CA) signaling pathway is often dysregulated in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and plays a vital role in promoting tumor cell proliferation, survival, and resistance to apoptosis, making it a clinically validated therapeutic target. Despite the approval of several PI3K p110α inhibitors, challenges including off-target toxicity, drug resistance, and suboptimal pharmacokinetic profiles require the continued discovery of novel, drug-like candidates. In this study, virtual screening of 650,000 compounds targeting the PI3K p110α pathway identified five lead candidates based on the lowest docking binding energy. MD simulations over 100 ns, MM-GBSA/MM-PBSA binding free energy calculations were performed using 1000 frames extracted at 100 ps intervals from the 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation trajectory. The results showed negative binding free energy values for all complexes, computationally indicating favorable binding interactions and potential stability of the compounds with the target protein. DFT analysis and SwissADME ADMET profiling were performed. All five complexes showed stable RMSD profiles and consistently negative binding free energies. DFT confirmed acceptable HOMO–LUMO energy gaps. ADMET profiling demonstrated satisfactory drug-likeness for all candidates. Five promising PI3K p110α inhibitor candidates with potential anti-lung cancer activity were identified; experimental validation is required to confirm these computational results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57722-9.

Introduction

Globally, lung cancer is one of the most common and lethal malignancies, that is responsible for about one million deaths each year, and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths in men and women1. The majority of the cases are related to non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), having well-recognized risk factors such as tobacco smoking, environmental contaminants, and exposure to occupational carcinogens. Using human lung cancer cell lines, such as A549, serves as a key experimental basis for drug development by characterizing the antiproliferative capabilities of potential therapeutic agents2. Despite breakthroughs in traditional chemotherapy and targeted therapies, there is a need for novel and effective compounds to treat lung cancer, which has a survival rate of about five years in less than 20% patients with advanced-stage disease3.

NSCLC has many oncogenic driver mutations that impact cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival. EGFR, KRAS, ALK rearrangements, and ROS1 fusion mutations have been well characterized, and targeted medicines have changed the clinical care of a particular patient population4. Many NSCLC patients have tumors without actionable driver mutations or show resistance to first and second-line drugs, limiting the treatment options. Due to this treatment gap, alternative carcinogenic pathways, including downstream signal transduction and cell survival mechanisms, are receiving research interest. The PI3K p110α signaling pathway has emerged as a particularly important molecular target involved in lung carcinogenesis5. PI3K p110α is a family of lipid kinases that phosphorylates phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to produce phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-triphosphate (PIP3), a second messenger that recruits and activates a wide range of downstream effectors6. Downstream activation of Akt and mTOR by aberrant activation of PI3K p110α, which is induced by gain-of-function mutations, dysregulation of growth factor receptors, loss of the tumor suppressor PTEN, and cytokine signaling. This process promotes tumor cell proliferation, survival, and resistance to apoptosis7. The synthesis of growth factors and transcriptional regulators of the cell cycle, such as cyclin D1 and c-Myc, is facilitated by the PI3K p110α/Akt pathway, which is responsible for the formation of a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment. In approximately 30–40% of NSCLC cases, changes in the PI3K p110α pathway have been observed, including PI3K p110α amplifications and mutations, PTEN deletions, and AKT1 mutations. Consequently, PI3K p110α is a significant and therapeutically relevant target in this malignancy6.

PI3K p110α inhibitors like idelalisib, copanlisib, and alpelisib have been clinically approved to treat solid tumors like lung cancer, but isoform selectivity, off-target toxicity, acquired resistance, and poor pharmacokinetics profiles limit their efficacy8. Within the PI3K p110α catalytic domain, resistance to PI3K p110α inhibition occurs due to compensatory activation of alternate survival pathways, reactivation of the feedback loop, and secondary mutations limiting therapeutic approaches. These constraints emphasise the need for structurally new PI3K p110α inhibitor candidates with improved binding selectivity, drug-like characteristics, and safety. Structure-based drug design guided by well-resolved PI3K p110α crystal structures can identify and optimize such possibilities9.

Computational approaches are used in early-stage drug development to rapidly, inexpensively, and efficiently screen large chemical libraries to identify bioactive molecules10. Molecular docking-based virtual screening is used to choose compounds that show attractive binding geometries and interaction patterns at the active site of the target, while MD simulations are used to study the temporal stability and conformational behaviour of ligand–protein complexes under physiological settings11. The MM-GBSA and MM-PBSA methods are used to calculate the complementary binding energy, which provides a quantitative thermodynamic evaluation of the stability of complexes12 , while density functional theory (DFT) analysis is used to characterize the electronic properties, reactivity, and candidate molecule stability at the quantum mechanical level13.

In the present study, this integrated in silico workflow was used to identify and characterize potential PI3K p110α inhibitors against the PI3K p110α signaling pathway in lung cancer14. The workflow includes large-scale virtual screening of 650,000 compounds, MD simulation, binding free energy calculations (MM-GBSA/MM-PBSA), DFT analysis, and ADMET profiling based on SwissADME15–17. From virtual screening, the top five ranked compounds were retrieved for further computational characterization to evaluate their binding stability, electronic characteristics, and drug-likeness, to identify potential candidates for further experimental validation against lung cancer.

PI3K p110α Target retrieval and preparation

The crystal structure of the target was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 5XGI)18. Chain A was selected for molecular docking analysis. The co-crystallized ligand and binding-site residues were used to define the active site for docking calculations19. The target was selected as the molecular target for this study due to its vital role in oncogenic PI3K p110α/AKT signaling and its frequent involvement in cancer progression20. The protein structure was further prepared21. The Protein preparation involved removing water molecules and heteroatoms, adding polar hydrogen atoms, assigning Kollman charges, and performing energy minimization before docking22. All these steps were achieved using BIOVIA Discovery Studio23–25. The protonation states of the protein residues were assigned at physiological pH. Grid box dimensions and center coordinates were defined around the co-crystallized ligand-binding region to confirm precise targeting of the active site26. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina with an exhaustiveness value of 32, while compound structures were prepared using geometry optimization and charge assignment before docking analysis27.

Structure-based virtual screening

Structure-based virtual screening (SBVS) is a rapid method for evaluating the interactions of millions of compounds with the protein target and selecting the most appropriate compounds based on their binding to protein pockets. Before the virtual screening, a 3-dimensional grid is required to facilitate the software. In the present study, the PI3K p110α protein was used for structure-based virtual screening. The protein targets were downloaded from the RSC-PDB site as a PDB file and saved for structure-based virtual screening28. The virtual screening was performed by downloading the library of compounds from the Drug Bank, i.e., all 3D structures. The library was downloaded as an SDF file containing 650,000 and was converted to the PDBQT format using Vina. The library was docked against the PI3K p110α29. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina 2.0. The binding pocket was defined with a grid box centered at x = 15.19 Å, y = 53.90 Å, and z = 16.92 Å, with dimensions of 20 × 20 × 20 Å and a grid spacing of 1.0 Å. These parameters were chosen to fully encompass the active site and allow adequate conformational sampling of the ligands30. The exhaustiveness parameter was set to 32 (default = 8) to improve the reliability and thoroughness of the conformational search. A total of nine binding poses were generated for each ligand, spanning an energy range of 3.0 kcal/mol, while all other parameters were e kept at their default settings unless otherwise stated. To validate the docking protocol, a redocking experiment was performed using the co-crystallized ligand from the target protein structure (PDB ID: 5XGI)31. The native ligand was extracted from the protein complex, and the receptor was prepared following the same protocol described above, including removal of water molecules, addition of polar hydrogens, and assignment of Gasteiger charges. The extracted ligand was then redocked into the original binding pocket using identical docking parameters and grid box dimensions. The top-ranked docking pose was superimposed onto the experimentally determined crystallographic conformation, and the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) was calculated to assess docking accuracy. An RMSD ≤ 2.0 Å was considered indicative of successful validation. The redocked pose reproduced the experimental binding orientation with an RMSD of 1.7 Å, thereby confirming the reliability and reproducibility of the docking protocol32.

In-silico ADMET assessment

The top five selected compounds obtained from virtual screening were subjected to ADMET analysis, which assesses absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity properties (ADMET)33–35. The ADMET properties were evaluated using the SwissADME tool. The assessed parameters included physicochemical properties, blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability, P-glycoprotein interaction, drug-likeness properties, cytochrome P450 inhibitory activity, and toxicity predictions34,36.

MD simulation analysis

Molecular dynamics simulations were done using GROMACS 2024.1 to assess the stability of ligand–protein docked complexes obtained from molecular docking37. Each dock complex was prepared by separating the ligand and protein into mol2 and PDB formats18. The ligand topologies and force field parameters were generated using the SwissParam server, and the CHARMM27 all-atom force field was applied to the protein. Each complex was solvated in a cubic TIP3P explicit water box with a minimum distance of 10 Å between the solute and the box boundary. The system was neutralized by adding Na⁺ and Cl- counter-ions at a physiological concentration of 0.15 M using the GROMACS genion module38. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest-descent algorithm until the maximum force converged to below 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−1, ensuring the removal of steric clashes and unfavorable contacts before equilibration. The system was next equilibrated in two sequential steps39. The first step, NVT (canonical ensemble; constant Number of particles, Volume, and Temperature), was conducted for 500 ps at 300 K using the V-rescale thermostat with a coupling time constant of 0.1 ps. The second step, NPT (isothermal-isobaric ensemble; constant Number of particles, Pressure, and Temperature), was run for 500 ps at 300 K and 1 bar using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat with a pressure-coupling time constant of 2.0 ps and a compressibility of 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1. Short-range electrostatic and van der Waals interactions were treated with a cut-off distance of 12 Å, while long-range electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) approach with a Fourier grid spacing of 0.16 nm40. Covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, permitting an integration time step of 2 fs throughout all simulation phases. Following equilibration steps, the production MD simulations were done for 100 ns under NPT conditions, with trajectory frames recorded every 10 ps, yielding a total of 10,000 frames for analysis. Trajectory analyses were conducted using the built-in GROMACS analysis tools and visualized using VMD and PyMOL41,42. The stability and dynamic behavior of each protein–ligand complex were examined using computation of the following parameters: root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the Cα backbone atoms relative to the initial minimized structure to assess overall structural stability; root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of individual residues to identify flexible regions within the protein; radius of gyration (RoG) to monitor the compactness of the complex over the simulation trajectory; the number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) formed between the ligand and protein, followed over the full trajectory with a donor–acceptor distance cut-off of 3.5 Å and angle cut-off of 30°; and solvent accessible surface area (SASA) to evaluate changes in the exposure of the complex to the solvent environment throughout the simulation16,43,44.

MM-PBSA, MM-GBSA, and residue decomposition analysis

The MM-PBSA and MM-GBSA analyses were performed using the g_mmpbsa.py software within the Conda environment. The MMPBSA, MM-GBSA, and residue decomposition were determined using various parameters such as Van der Waals energy, electrostatic energy, solvation energy, and total energy of the system. The MM-PBSA and MM-GBSA calculations were validated by the molecular docking and MD simulation analysis, as reported15.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the ORCA quantum chemistry package (version 6.0.0)45. Molecular geometries were fully optimized without symmetry constraints using the B3LYP exchange–correlation functional in conjunction with the 6-31G(d,p) basis set. Empirical dispersion corrections were applied using Grimme’s D3 scheme with Becke–Johnson damping (D3BJ) to account for non-covalent and long-range dispersion interactions. Bulk solvent effects were treated implicitly using the conductor-like polarizable continuum model (CPCM), with water (ε = 78.39) as the solvent to simulate physiological conditions. All self-consistent field (SCF) calculations were performed using tight convergence criteria (TightSCF; energy convergence threshold: 10-⁸ Eₕ; RMS density change: 10-⁸)46. Numerical integrations were carried out using a fine integration grid (Grid5) to ensure accuracy in exchange–correlation quadrature47. Harmonic vibrational frequency calculations were conducted at the same level of theory on all stationary points to verify the nature of each optimized geometry; true minima were confirmed by the absence of imaginary frequencies, while transition states were characterized by the presence of a single imaginary frequency corresponding to the relevant reaction coordinate. Zero-point vibrational energy (ZPVE) corrections and thermal corrections at 298.15 K and 1 atm were obtained from frequency analyses within the ideal gas, rigid rotor, and harmonic oscillator approximations48.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD)

The RMSD analysis was done to investigate the conformational behavior of the PI3K p110α-ligand docked complexes during the 100 ns simulation. RMSD measures the deviation of atomic coordinates from the reference structure over time and provides insight into equilibration and structural adaptation of the complexes. As shown in Fig. 3A, the PI3K p110α-119373 complex showed relatively higher RMSD values during the initial 0–20 ns, reaching approximately 1 nm, representing notable conformational fluctuations during the early phase of the simulation. However, the system gradually approached equilibration and showed relatively consistent RMSD behavior during the remaining simulation period. In contrast, the PI3K p110α-24856041 and PI3K p110α-46215462 complexes showed comparatively lower RMSD fluctuations, mainly within the range of 0.3–0.5 nm, signifying relatively stable conformational behavior throughout the 100-ns simulation. Also, the PI3K p110α-25019940 and PI3K p110α-117779453 dock complexes showed moderate deviations during the initial simulation period, followed by stabilization after equilibration. These findings indicate varying degrees of conformational adaptation among the complexes rather than absolute structural stability. Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF)

The RMSF analysis was conducted to evaluate residue-level flexibility, compactness, and relaxation within the protein during ligand binding. As shown in Fig. 3B, most residues across all docked complexes exhibited RMSF values below 0.4 nm, indicating minimal fluctuations in major regions of the protein structure. However, clear peaks were observed around residues 100–200, 300–400, and 600–700, indicating comparatively flexible regions within the protein structure. Among the docked complexes, PI3K p110α-46215462 and PI3K p110α-117779453 showed lower residue fluctuations than the other systems, whereas PI3K p110α-119373 showed higher flexibility in specific regions. These fluctuations likely reflect localized conformational motions rather than complete destabilization of the protein structure.

The RoG analysis was used to assess the compactness of the protein–ligand complexes during the simulation Fig. 4A. All complexes initially showed RoG values around 3.0–3.3 nm, followed by a gradual decrease toward approximately 2.9 nm during the simulation time. This reduction suggests a tendency toward more compact conformations after equilibration. Solvent accessible surface area (SASA)

The SASA analysis Fig. 4B was completed to assess ligand-specific effects on solvent accessibility and conformational behavior of the PI3K p110α-ligand docked complexes during the 100-ns molecular dynamics simulation. All complexes primarily showed relatively higher SASA values of about 450–470 nm2, followed by a progressive decline during the early phase of the simulation, eventually stabilizing within the range of 370–400 nm2. This reduction proposes structural relaxation and a transition toward more compact conformational states after equilibration. Particularly, the ligand-dependent differences in solvent exposure profiles were reported among the complexes. The PI3K p110α-117779453 complex exhibited comparatively lower and more stable SASA values after approximately 35 ns, demonstrating a more compact structural arrangement with reduced solvent exposure. In contrast, the PI3K p110α-119373 complex also sustained relatively higher SASA values and greater fluctuations throughout the simulation, signifying comparatively increased surface exposure and conformational flexibility. The PI3K p110α-24856041 and PI3K p110α-46215462 complexes showed intermediate SASA behavior with relatively stable trajectories after the equilibration phase, whereas the PI3K p110α-25019940 complex showed moderate fluctuations but retained an overall compact conformational profile. These ligand-specific changes in SASA profiles specify that each ligand differentially influenced the conformational packing and solvent accessibility of PI3K p110α, reflecting distinct dynamic responses of the protein–ligand complexes during the 100-ns simulation trajectory.

The hydrogen bonds can be used to assess the stability of the complexes, and a higher number of hydrogen bonds suggests the stability of the complexes. The complexes such as PI3K p110α-25019940 and PI3K p110α-46215462 show moderate hydrogen bond fluctuations, and 2–5 hydrogen bonds are maintained and exhibit balanced behavior. Binding free energy calculation

The MM-GBSA and MM-PBSA analyses showed favorable binding interactions of all selected compounds with the PI3K target protein. In both methods, negative total binding free energy (ΔGtotal) values show energetically stable protein–ligand complexes. Under the MM-GBSA estimation, the PI3K p110α-46215462 complex showed the most favorable binding affinity with a total binding energy of − 27.11 kcal/mol, followed by PI3K p110α-25019940 complex − 26.00 kcal/mol, PI3K p110α-24856041 complex − 25.38 kcal/mol, PI3K p110α-117779453 complex − 23.66 kcal/mol, and PI3K p110α-119373 complex − 22.23 kcal/mol. The strong binding efficacies were primarily driven by favorable van der Waals (VDWAALS) and electrostatic (EEL) interactions, signifying that hydrophobic contacts and electrostatic attractions contributed substantially to complex stabilization. Although the polar solvation energy (EGB) contributed unfavorably with positive values, the favorable gas-phase interaction energies compensated for this penalty, resulting in overall negative binding energies. Correspondingly, the MM-PBSA calculation also revealed favorable interaction profiles for all complexes. Among them, PI3K-24856041 displayed the most favorable binding free energy -25.75 kcal/mol, followed by PI3K p110α-117779453 complex − 23.75 kcal/mol, PI3K-25019940 complex − 22.86 kcal/mol, while PI3K p110α-119373 complex and PI3K p110α-46215462 complex showed comparatively less favorable but still negative binding energies -18.23 kcal/mol. Consistent with the MM-GBSA findings, van der Waals and electrostatic interactions represented the major stabilizing forces, whereas polar solvation energies (EPB) opposed binding. Overall, both MM-GBSA and MM-PBSA binding free energy calculations suggested that PI3K p110α-46215462 complex, PI3K p110α-25019940 complex, and PI3K p110α-24856041 complex exhibited comparatively computationally stronger binding affinities toward the PI3K binding pocket. The consistently negative binding free energy values indicated stable protein ligand dock complex formation and supported the potential of these compounds as promising PI3K inhibitors. One limitation of the present study is that entropic contributions (-TΔS) were not involved in the MM-PBSA/MM-GBSA binding free energy estimation. Therefore, the reported binding free energies mainly represent enthalpic components, including van der Waals, electrostatic, and solvation energies. The exclusion of entropy calculations was primarily due to methodological constraints and the limited reliability of entropy estimation for large, flexible protein–ligand systems within the applied workflow. However, the comparative binding energy analysis remains informative for ranking ligand affinities and evaluating the relative binding behavior among the top-five selected compounds.

SwissADME analysis of selected compounds: DB119373, DB24856041, DB25019940, DB46215462, and DB117779453

The SwissADME investigation of the selected top-five compounds DB119373, DB24856041, DB25019940, DB46215462, and DB117779453 demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetic and drug-likeness properties, computationally supporting their potential as promising therapeutic candidates against PI3K49. The analysis assessed multiple parameters, such as physicochemical properties, lipophilicity, water solubility, pharmacokinetics, medicinal chemistry alerts, and oral bioavailability. All selected compounds showed physicochemical properties within the acceptable range for drug development, representing balanced molecular weight, hydrogen bond donor/acceptor capacity, and topological polar surface area (TPSA). These properties are important for sustaining adequate membrane permeability and molecular stability21. The selected compounds also confirmed moderate lipophilicity, signifying an optimal balance between hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity that may enable cellular uptake and enhance interaction with the PI3K binding pocket. The water solubility predictions indicated acceptable-to-moderate solubility profiles for the investigated compounds. Adequate aqueous solubility is a significant parameter influencing absorption and systemic distribution, and the reported values suggest that the compounds may possess suitable bioavailability characteristics for oral administration.

Furthermore, the pharmacokinetic analysis revealed encouraging absorption properties, including high gastrointestinal (GI) absorption for most compounds. The bioavailability radar and BOILED-Egg model analysis suggested that the selected compounds possess favorable oral drug-like behavior with potential passive gastrointestinal permeation. In addition, the compounds showed limited violations of common drug-likeness filters such as Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge rules, demonstrating compatibility with well-known pharmaceutical standards. The medicinal chemistry evaluation also established acceptable synthetic accessibility and minimal structural alerts, supporting the feasibility of further optimization and experimental development. The absence of major PAINS (Pan-Assay Interference Compounds) alerts proposes a lower probability of false-positive biological activity, thereby strengthening the reliability of the computational findings. Together, the SwissADME analysis exhibited that DB119373, DB24856041, DB25019940, DB46215462, and DB117779453 possess favorable drug-like and pharmacokinetic profiles, which complement their molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation results. These reported results support their potential as promising PI3K inhibitors and validate further experimental validation through in vitro and in vivo analysis. Density functional theory (DFT)

The reactivity and stability of the compounds can be assessed by conducting DFT analysis. During DFT analysis, the reactivity and stability can be determined by HOMO, LUMO, and their energy gap. The HOMO analysis shows the highest electron-occupied orbital, while the LUMO indicates the lowest unoccupied orbital. The higher the energy gap, the more stable the compounds, and a very high energy is required to stimulate the valence shell electrons. However, the lower energy gaps show that the compounds are highly reactive, and less energy is required to excite the valence shell electrons, and the more reactive the compound is. Discussion

In the present study, we employed a structure-based virtual screening workflow focusing on PI3K to identify potential inhibitors from a large chemical library50. The role of PI3K/akt/mTOR signaling in promoting cellular proliferation and blocking apoptosis in lung cancer is well established, making PI3K a clinically validated and computationally manageable target for structure-based drug discovery20. PI3K has also been the major computational target in many previous studies. Singh et al. described the successful discovery of novel PI3K p110α inhibitors via structure-based virtual screening, showing the reliable identification of bioactive scaffolds against this target using docking-based approaches51. Therefore, in the current study, PI3K was chosen for its pathogenic relevance and the availability of high-resolution structural data suitable for molecular docking6.

The virtual screening process produced a ranked list of candidates that were determined by their binding energies and their adherence to Lipinski’s rule of five52. This is in accordance with previously reported computational workflows that employ these criteria to sort large compound libraries into manageable hit sets. Lipinski’s filters have been widely used in virtual screening campaigns53. Lipinski et al. reported that molecular weight, hydrogen bond donor/acceptor count, and lipophilicity strongly predict oral bioavailability, and these parameters have been confirmed through large-scale analyses as reliable early-stage gatekeepers. In the current study, the top five compounds showed the highest docking score within the active site of PI3K p110α, suggesting similar binding geometries and interaction patterns to those of established PI3K inhibitors like idelalisib and copanlisib, which are known to bind the ATP-binding pocket via hydrogen bonding with important hinge residues54.

MD simulations were carried out to validate the stability of these interactions under physiological circumstances23,55,56. All five selected protein–ligand complexes achieved equilibration without significant structural changes, as evidenced by their RMSD profiles, which suggest that they exhibited stable binding modes rather than transitory associations. The literature provides strong support for the reliability of RMSD as a stability criterion in MD-based validation of docked complexes57. Martini et al. confirmed that complexes that maintain RMSD values below 3 A over 100 ns trajectories reliably correspond to stable binding poses confirmed by experimental assays58. RMSF analysis demonstrated that the PI3K p110α binding pocket contained key catalytic residues with minimal fluctuation, which is indicative of productive inhibitor engagement59. Structural compactness and appropriate solvent exposure of all complexes were further supported by RoG and SASA analysis, which was consistent with simulation studies on comparable PI3K p110α inhibitor scaffolds57.

Post simulation, the thermodynamic favorability of each complex was quantitatively evaluated using MM-PBSA and MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations60. The negative binding free energy values confirmed energetically favorable interactions61. The main stabilizing forces were found to be Van der Waals and electrostatic forces. This energetic profile is consistent with the MM-PBSA analyses of ATP-competitive PI3K inhibitors that have been previously reported. Bhatt et al. had also identified hydrophobic contacts within the adenine-binding region as the primary contributors to binding affinity in their computational characterization of PI3K p110α inhibitors. By considering solvation and entropic effects, the MM-PBSA strategy has been extensively proven as a viable post-simulation ranking method that provides a higher accuracy than docking scores alone15.

Further, the five selected compounds were subjected to DFT analysis to assess their intrinsic reactivity and electronic properties62. For each compound, the HOMO–LUMO energy gaps showed that it had a modest level of reactivity and good electronic stability. A broader HOMO–LUMO gap generally implies a better kinetic stability and lesser reactivity, while a narrower gap denotes a higher chemical reactivity and the possibility for stronger intermediate interactions with the target. DFT-based characterization of either computationally identified kinase inhibitors by Shehzad et al. showed similar HOMO–LUMO gap profiles for a series of PI3K p110α inhibitor candidate and correlated them with their observed binding affinities, demonstrating the value of DFT analysis as a complementary validation tool in computational drug discovery63.

The ADMET profiling revealed that the top drug meets important criteria for pharmacokinetics and safety49, such as estimated bioavailability when taken by mouth, margins of acceptable hepatotoxicity and cardiotoxicity, and permeability of the blood–brain barrier within tolerable ranges64. In the previous study, Gleeson et al. have extensively emphasized the significance of incorporating ADMET assessment early in the drug discovery pipeline. They have shown that the incorporation of ADMET filters at the hit identification stage significantly reduces late-stage attrition by eliminating candidates with inherently unfavorable pharmacokinetic profiles65. The identified compounds are promising leads that require further validation through in vitro enzymatic inhibition assays and in vivo lung cancer models, as indicated by the convergent evidence from virtual screening, MD simulation, binding free energy calculations, DFT analysis, and ADMET profiling23,56,66,67.

Conclusion

Virtual screening of a large compound library against the ta PI3K p110α target, followed by molecular dynamics simulation, ADMET profiling, and DFT analysis, identified five lead candidates, DB119373, DB24856041, DB25019940, DB46215462, and DB117779453, as the most promising PI3K p110α inhibitors. PI3K p110α-targeting candidates predicted through computational analyses. These compounds were prioritized based on favorable lowest binding energies in docking, stable protein–ligand docked complex behavior over the 100-ns of MD simulation, compliance with drug-likeness criteria, and acceptable predicted pharmacokinetic profiles. DFT analysis further supported their electronic stability, as reflected in their HOMO–LUMO energy gaps. Collectively, the computational findings suggest that the selected compounds justify further investigation as PI3K p110α-targeted therapeutics. However, all findings reported here are in silico, and experimental validation, such as in vitro binding assays, cellular efficacy studies, and in vivo pharmacokinetic characterization, is vital before any biological or clinical applications.
