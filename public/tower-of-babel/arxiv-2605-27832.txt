Playing with Words, Improving with Rewards: Training Language Models for Creative Association
Vijeta Deshpande, Namrata Shivagunde, Sherin Muckatira, Hadrien Glaude, Mikhail Gronas, Claire Stevenson, Roger Beaty, Anna Rumshisky

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are being applied to increasingly difficult problems and use cases. To navigate their vast solution spaces effectively, LLMs need to be creative. Yet the subjective nature of creativity and the limits of human judgment make training LLMs for creativity especially challenging. As a solution, we train LLMs on Codenames, a word-association game that exercises the two central axes of creativity, divergent and convergent thinking, while yielding objectively verifiable outcomes. This verifiability lets us bypass human judgment and train with Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). We train Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B models and evaluate them on ten creativity and four reasoning benchmarks. We find that the precision-diversity trade-off is scale-dependent: the 8B model prioritizes creativity over precision, while the 1.7B and 4B models gain reasoning precision at the cost of creativity. Concretely, the 8B model shows modest but consistent creativity gains (8 of 10 benchmarks) with only minor reasoning degradation, whereas the smaller models achieve substantial gains on reasoning tasks. Our study presents a scalable and effective solution to train LLMs for creativity.

The central obstacle to training creativity directly is that creativity is inherently subjective

In Codenames, a board of unrelated words is laid out, and a small subset is secretly designated as target words; the remaining words on the board, including some that must be actively avoided, are non-targets. One player, the spymaster, sees which words are targets and must produce a single clue word that points to as many targets as possible while steering teammates away from every non-target. The teammates, who see only the board, then guess which words the clue refers to. A good clue is semantically remote enough from the non-targets to disambiguate them, yet semantically close enough to several targets to link them through a shared latent dimension — a chair, an altar, and a stage might all be connected through the abstract concept of platform. Strong play, therefore, demands exactly the divergent-then-convergent associative leap that cognitive scientists identify as the core mechanism of creativity.

Casting creativity as a game with verifiable outcomes lets us bypass expensive human preference annotation entirely. Instead, we train with Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)
(
17
)
, the paradigm that has recently driven step-change improvements in LLMs. Through RLVR training on Codenames, we fine-tune three Qwen3 models (1.7B, 4B, and 8B) to reorganize existing knowledge and discover unseen associations. We summarize our key contributions and findings below:

•

Creativity Training.
We cast creativity as a verifiable-reward game, train LLMs via RLVR without human annotation, and evaluate on ten creativity tasks and four reasoning benchmarks.

•

Precision-Diversity Trade-off.
We show this known trade-off is scale-dependent: smaller models gain reasoning precision at the cost of creativity, while the 8B model gains creativity with a slight precision loss.

•

Creativity Gains.
The 8B model shows modest but consistent creativity gains.

•

Reasoning Gains.
The 1.7B and 4B models show substantial gains on reasoning tasks.

Method

3.1
Training Data

Game Environment

We adopt Codenames, a word association game, as the training environment. The original board game places four sets of words on the board and involves two competing teams. Of these four sets, two are target sets, one assigned to each team. The remaining words form a neutral set and a single assassin word that ends the game. We simplify this setup by removing the neutral and assassin words. This allows us to focus on a clean signal of word association, free from higher-level rules. Each team consists of one clue generator (the
Spymaster
) and one or more guess generators (the
Operatives
). Only the clue generator can see which target words belong to which team. Its goal is to find a common link, the clue, that connects multiple words in its team’s target set. The guess generators, by contrast, cannot see how the separation between the two target sets. They have access to all words on the board along with the clue, and their goal is to deduce which words the clue refers to. Each team aims to correctly guess all of its target words before the opposing team.

In our training setup, we train a single LLM that plays the role of one team in the game environment. This is equivalent to having the LLM switch between both teams, intending to complete its assigned target set as quickly as possible while avoiding the words in the non-target set. In its natural form, Codenames for a single team is a multi-turn discussion. The clue generator has access to all context, while the guess generators cannot see the separation between the two target sets. These discussions can become extremely long. Thinking traces alone can easily reach several thousand tokens for a single turn. This increased length raises training cost and makes training instabilities more likely. To address this, we break the multi-turn discussions into multiple single-turn conversations. Specifically, we model clue generation and guess generation as single-turn tasks, and we strategically sample board positions to simulate every stage of the full end-to-end game.

More precisely, we model this training environment as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). Let
w
be any whitespace-separated English word that can be represented by one or more tokens in the LLM’s vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
. Let
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{N}
denote the target and non-target word sets, and let
\mathcal{B}
denote the combined and shuffled set
\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{N}
. Let
w_{clue}
be a single whitespace-separable clue word, let
\mathcal{G}
be the set of guesses, and
g_{max}
be the maximum number of allowed guesses in one chance. We represent the state set of the MDP as

\mathcal{S}=\{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{N},\mathcal{B},w_{clue},g_{max}\}.

(1)

During the clue generation task,
\mathcal{B}
,
w_{clue}
,
g_{max}
are null. During the guess generation task,
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{N}
are empty. We define the action set as

\mathcal{A}=\{w_{clue},\mathcal{T}_{selected},\mathcal{G}\}.

(2)

During the clue generation task, the LLM generates
w_{clue}
for a subset of words in
\mathcal{T}
i.e.,
\mathcal{T}_{selected}
(so
\mathcal{G}=\emptyset
). During the guess generation task, it generates only
\mathcal{G}
(so
w_{clue}=\mathcal{T}_{selected}=\emptyset
). Note that,
\mathcal{T}_{selected}\subset\mathcal{T}
and
1\leq|\mathcal{T}_{selected}|=g_{max}
. We assume that the transition function
\mathcal{P}
is not accessible for this MDP. In the following subsections, we present details on how we sample our state set and subsequently discuss details on reward functions.

Sampling State Set

We loosely refer to both
\mathcal{B}
and
\{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{N}\}
as board positions. The two differ in one important way:
\{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{N}\}
encodes the separation between
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{N}
, whereas
\mathcal{B}
does not. Accordingly,
\{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{N}\}
is available only during clue generation, and
\mathcal{B}
only during guess generation. A complete state also includes the clue word
w_{clue}
, which is required during guess generation. In a natural game, this clue would come from a preceding clue-generation turn. Because we train each turn as an independent single-turn task, no such preceding turn exists, so we must supply
w_{clue}
in advance. We therefore generate clue words automatically through a systematic three-step process.

•

Generate a Pool of Diverse Topics
: We begin by manually creating a seed list of
50
diverse topics, such as ocean, food, hobbies etc. With the help of Claude Opus, we then extend this list to roughly
1{,}000
unique topics. Each topic is a single whitespace-separable word that later serves as the clue in guess generation training examples.

•

Find Words Associated with Topics
: For each topic, we use Claude Opus to generate a list of directly associated words, which we stratify by frequency into common and rare words. For example, the topic
ocean
yields common words such as
tide
,
wave
, and
salt
, and rare words such as
pelagic
,
benthic
, and
halocline
. We store the top
10
common and
10
rare words per topic in a repository
\mathcal{R}
. For any topic
t
,
\mathcal{R}[t]=[W^{(t,c)},W^{(t,r)}]
, where
W^{(t,c)}
and
W^{(t,r)}
are the common and rare words associated with
t
, and
|W^{(t,c)}|=|W^{(t,r)}|=10
.

•

Sample State Sets
: To create a board position, we sample two topics, one for the words in
\mathcal{T}
and one for the words in
\mathcal{N}
. This lets us maintain a coherent theme and a clear separation between the words on the board, which keeps the task tractable for LLMs, especially smaller ones. We then draw the size of each set independently from
[2,6]
(inclusive) and sample that many words from the corresponding topic. Varying the set sizes lets us simulate the early and late game states encountered in a long single-turn progression. Finally, the topic word for
\mathcal{T}
serves as the clue for the words in
\mathcal{T}
. Following this process, we sample the state sets (see
Equation

1
) used to train the LLMs.

Difficulty of States

When sampling the state sets, we apply four rules of increasing difficulty. Each rule changes only how the words in
\mathcal{T}
are sampled, while the rule for
\mathcal{N}
stays fixed. Let
t_{\mathcal{T}}
and
t_{\mathcal{N}}
denote the topics sampled for the target and non-target sets, and let
n_{\mathcal{T}}\sim U(2,6)
and
n_{\mathcal{N}}\sim U(2,6)
denote the number of words sampled for each set. The
n_{\mathcal{N}}
non-target words are always drawn from the common pool
W^{(t_{\mathcal{N}},c)}
. The four rules control the target set as follows.

•

Simple
: all
n_{\mathcal{T}}
words are drawn from the common pool
W^{(t_{\mathcal{T}},c)}
.

•

Moderate
:
\lfloor n_{\mathcal{T}}/2\rfloor
words are drawn from the common pool
W^{(t_{\mathcal{T}},c)}
, and the rest from the rare pool
W^{(t_{\mathcal{T}},r)}
.

•

Advanced
: all
n_{\mathcal{T}}
words are drawn from the rare pool
W^{(t_{\mathcal{T}},r)}
.

•

Expert
:
\lfloor n_{\mathcal{T}}/2\rfloor
words are drawn from the rare pool
W^{(t_{\mathcal{T}},r)}
, and the rest from the common pool of the non-target topic,
W^{(t_{\mathcal{N}},c)}
.

We order the training data from the
Simple
rule to the
Expert
rule, forming a natural curriculum for the LLM. Intuitively, difficulty rises as target words shift from common to rare, and the
Expert
rule adds distractors that overlap with the non-target topic. Moreover, in all but the last rule,
\mathcal{T}_{selected}=\mathcal{T}
and
g_{max}=|\mathcal{T}|
. In the case of the last rule,
\mathcal{T}_{selected}\subset\mathcal{T}
and
g_{max}=\lfloor n_{\mathcal{T}}/2\rfloor
.

Data Statistics

Overall, we create
500
unique states per rule for each task, clue generation and guess generation. In total, this yields
4{,}000
examples, of which we use
3{,}680
for training and
320
for validation, balanced across rules and tasks. We represent our training dataset as a collection of
N=3680
examples,

\mathcal{D}=\left\{\left(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{N},w_{clue},g_{\max},\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{sel}}\right)_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N},

(3)

where each tuple stores the target set
\mathcal{T}
, the non-target set
\mathcal{N}
, the precomputed clue
w_{clue}
, the maximum number of guesses
g_{\max}
, and the selected target subset
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{sel}}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
that the clue is intended for. For exact prompts for each task, see
Appendix

A
.

3.2
Reward Function

Clue Generation Task Reward

Let
\pi
be the actor policy we train, which maps a state to an action over the spaces defined in
Equation

1
and
2
(
\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
). To reward a generated clue, we run the full clue-then-guess loop and score the resulting guesses, which proceeds in three stages. First, in the clue generation state
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{N}\}
, the actor samples a clue for
\mathcal{T}_{selected}
words conditioned on the separated target and non-target sets,
{w_{clue},\mathcal{T}_{selected}}\sim\pi(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{N})
. Second, we form the shuffled board
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{N}
from the same two sets, pair it with the clue, and a bound for guesses
g_{max}=|\mathcal{T}_{selected}|
to obtain the guess generation state
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathcal{B},w_{clue},g_{max}\}
, and pass this to a frozen judge policy
\phi
that returns a set of guesses,
\mathcal{G}\sim\phi(\mathcal{B},w_{clue},g_{max})
. Third, we compare the guesses against the ground-truth sets
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{N}
to compute a scalar reward
r=f(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{N},\mathcal{G})
.

The reward credits guesses that hit the target set and penalizes guesses that hit the non-target set or fall outside the board entirely:

f(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{N},\mathcal{G})=\frac{|\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{G}|}{|\mathcal{T}|}-\frac{|\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{G}|}{|\mathcal{N}|}-\frac{|\mathcal{G}\setminus(\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{N})|}{|\mathcal{G}|}

(4)

The first term is the fraction of target words correctly recovered, the second is the fraction of non-target words mistakenly selected, and the third is the fraction of guesses that appear in neither set. The reward is therefore bounded in
[-2,1]
, attaining its maximum of
1
only when the judge recovers every target word and selects nothing else.

Guess Generation Task Reward

For guess generation, the actor policy produces guesses conditioned on the board and an a priori clue stored in our dataset,
\mathcal{G}\sim\pi(\mathcal{B},w_{clue})
. For each guess generation example, we also retain the sets
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{N}
that compose
\mathcal{B}
. After generation, we therefore have
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{N}
, and
\mathcal{G}
, which is everything needed to compute the reward using the same function as before (
Equation

4
).

Reward for Response Format

Before computing the task reward, we run format checks on each response. For instance, a clue must not appear on the board. We apply
6
such checks to clues and
4
to guesses (see
Appendix

B
). The task reward (
Equation

4
) is computed only when all task-specific checks pass; otherwise, the reward is
-1
.

Summary

In summary, we cast our problem as a finite-horizon MDP,

\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},f),

(5)

where
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are the state and action sets (
Equations

1
and
2
),
\mathcal{P}
is the non-accessible transition function and
f
is the reward function. The horizon is bounded by the model’s maximum generation length. To solve this problem, we adopt Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)
(
17
)
, using the objective and hyperparameters of
60
without modification. For a state
s\sim\mathcal{D}
, we sample a group of
G
responses
\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot\mid s)
, where each response
o_{i}
is an action (a clue or a set of guesses) decoded as a token sequence. The policy is optimized by maximizing

\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim\mathcal{D}\\
\{o_{i}\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{old}}\end{subarray}}\!\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{i}|o_{i}|}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\mathcal{L}_{i,t}(\theta)\right],

(6)

where the per-token surrogate is

\mathcal{L}_{i,t}(\theta)=\min\!\Big(r_{i,t}\,\hat{A}_{i},\;\mathrm{clip}\big(r_{i,t},\,1\!-\!\varepsilon_{l},\,1\!+\!\varepsilon_{h}\big)\,\hat{A}_{i}\Big)

(7)

Here
r_{i,t}=\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}\mid s,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_{i,t}\mid s,o_{i,<t})}
is the token-level importance ratio, and
\hat{A}_{i}=(R_{i}-\bar{R})/\sigma_{R}
is the group-normalized advantage, with
R_{i}=f(\cdot)
the response reward and
\bar{R},\sigma_{R}
the mean and standard deviation of
\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G}
.

Experimental Setup

4.1
Training

Models.

We train three models from the Qwen3 family: Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B
(
58
)
. For every model, we perform full-parameter fine-tuning in full
bf16
precision, without parameter-efficient adapters or quantization. All training is implemented with the VeRL library
(
45
)
. For the judge model
\phi(\cdot)
, we use Google Gemini Flash-
2.5
, suitable for large inference volume.

Training Algorithm and Hyperparameters.

We optimize the DAPO objective (
Equation

6
) with the GRPO advantage estimator. For each prompt, we sample a group of
G=32
responses, and each optimization step uses a batch of
32
prompts, giving
32\times 32=1024
rollouts per step. We train for
2
epochs over the
3{,}680
training examples, yielding
230
parameter update steps. We use a constant learning rate of
1\times 10^{-6}
with no warmup or decay. Following DAPO, we decouple clipping bounds with
\varepsilon_{l}=0.2
and
\varepsilon_{h}=0.28
(clip-higher), aggregate the policy loss at the token level (token-mean), and remove the KL term entirely, using neither KL loss nor KL reward shaping.

Dynamic Sampling and Length Control.

We adopt DAPO’s dynamic sampling: we oversample and discard prompt groups whose responses all receive the same reward, since such groups yield zero advantage and no learning signal, and we allow up to
10
oversampling batches per step to refill the effective batch. We cap the prompt length at
2,048
tokens and the response length at
16,384
tokens. We also apply DAPO’s soft overlong reward shaping with a
4,096
-token buffer. Responses longer than
12{,}288
tokens incur a penalty that grows linearly with the overage and reaches its maximum at the
16384
-token cap. This discourages runaway generations while avoiding hard truncation of otherwise useful reasoning traces.

Infrastructure.

We run experiments on a heterogeneous pool of GPUs based on availability on the Vast-AI platform. We train and evaluate Qwen3-1.7B on a single A100, train Qwen3-4B on
4{\times}
RTX 5090 and
4{\times}
RTX Pro 6000 GPUs, and train and evaluate Qwen3-8B on
4{\times}
RTX Pro 6000 GPUs.
Approximately, the training runs for 4B and 8B models took
40
-
50
hours on
4\times
RTX Pro 6000 GPUs, and the 1.7B model took
\approx 25
hours on
4\times
RTX 5090. In total, we spent approximately
3,000
USD for training and evaluation experiments.

4.2
Evaluation

Creativity Evaluation

We evaluate on a broad suite of creativity tasks. The suite spans divergent and convergent thinking, figurative language and humor understanding, creative problem solving, the generation of long and coherent narratives, and conceptual understanding. We group the tasks below by the construct that each one targets, and for each task, we describe its goal, a representative instance, and the metric we report.

•

Divergent thinking:

AUT
(Alternate Uses Test)
(
16
)
and
DAT
(Divergent Association Test)
(
37
)
probe the ability to produce many distinct ideas. In AUT, we prompt the model to generate
5
creative and novel uses for an everyday object, such as a belt or a brick. We score the five uses for creativity and coherence with an LLM judge, where higher is better. In DAT, we prompt the model to list
10
words that are as semantically dissimilar from one another as possible, and we sample
100
such lists. For each list, we compute Divergent Semantic Integration (DSI)
(
22
)
and report the average over all
100
lists. We additionally report vocabulary size, the fraction of unique words across the full pool of
10\times 100
words. DSI captures semantic diversity, and vocabulary size captures lexical diversity; higher values are better for both.

•

Convergent thinking:

RAT
(Remote Association Test)
(
5
)
evaluates the complementary ability to converge on a single associative link between distant concepts. Each instance presents three remote cue words, and the model must produce one word that associates with all three. We report accuracy against the reference answers.

•

Figurative language and humor understanding:

MUNCH
(Metaphor Understanding Challenge)
(
49
)
evaluates whether a model can interpret metaphorical language. It comprises three multiple-choice variants and one open-ended generation variant. We report accuracy for the multiple-choice variants and recall for the generation variant. The
New Yorker Caption Contest

(
19
)
evaluates humor understanding. Each instance provides a description of a cartoon together with a caption. In the explanation task, the model must explain why the caption is funny, which we score with an LLM judge. In the matching task, the model selects the most suitable caption from several options, which we score with accuracy.

•

Creative problem solving:

MacGyver

(
47
)
presents an everyday problem together with a limited set of available tools, and the model must devise a creative solution that respects the tool constraints. Following
47
, we score solutions for feasibility and efficiency with an LLM judge.
NYT Connections

(
29
)
requires partitioning
16
words into four themed groups of four. We report group-level accuracy and the exact-match rate over all four groups.

•

Narrative generation:

CWT
(Creative Writing Test)
(
41
)
and

(Emotional Quotient Benchmark)
(
39
)
target short- and long-form story writing, respectively. In CWT, we give the model three words and ask for a

In addition, we report DSI (semantic diversity) and MTLD (Measure of Textual Lexical Diversity) for the generated stories. In EQ-Bench, the model receives a topic and a narrative outline that specifies required details, for example
“Describe the gladiator’s weapon of choice and its significance to them”

We first present our findings on the creativity tasks, and then on the reasoning benchmarks. Apart from RAT and NYT Connections, none of our evaluation tasks resemble the training task, and therefore probe the out-of-domain (OOD) transfer of our proposed setup.

5.3
Synthesis: Associative Learning Meets RLVR

Taken together, the creativity and reasoning results reveal a coherent pattern shaped by the interaction of associative learning (Codenames) with RLVR (DAPO). RLVR is known to carry a diversity-precision trade-off
(
53
)
, and we observe exactly this, except its direction flips with scale. The 8B model shifts toward diversity, gaining creativity at a slight cost to math and science precision, while the smaller models shift the other way, gaining robustly on precision-bound reasoning without consistently gaining creativity. We speculate that associative learning sets this split
(
23
)
: easy problems are solvable by association alone, so smaller models earn reward by precisely solving the problems now within reach, while larger models, already solving these, pursue diversity for the harder ones. A length test on the 1.7B responses to the reasoning dataset supports this. Its traces lengthen overall (ratio
1.42
) but stay shorter on problems it newly solves (
1.13
) and fall below the base model on AIME (
<1.0
), converging faster on likely easy problems.
Together, these results point to a method that pays off at every scale. One associative-RLVR recipe, with no task-specific tuning, delivers substantial reasoning gains in smaller models and consistent creativity gains in larger ones. The scale-dependent trade-off is a key property that we highlight with our results:
each model gains where it can
.

6
Conclusion

We set out to train creativity without human judgment, and found that a single game, Codenames, reshapes what a model improves at depending on its size. The same associative reward sharpens reasoning in smaller models and broadens creativity in the largest, exposing a scale-dependent precision-diversity trade-off rather than a uniform win. Concretely, fine-tuning improved the 8B model across the large majority of creativity metrics. The 1.7B and 4B models instead showed strong improvements in math and science reasoning. A single, verifiable word game thus provides a practical and scalable lever for strengthening LLMs.

Limitations

Our work has several limitations. First, we simplify Codenames by removing the neutral and assassin words and by decomposing multi-turn play into single-turn tasks, which may omit dynamics present in the full game. Training with fewer constraints to satisfy may also explain the constraint failures we observed on NYT Connections, where the tuned model violated task rules it was never pressured to respect. Second, our training combines a chain-of-thought scaffold with generic RLVR, and, primarily due to experimental cost, we do not run an ablation that isolates the associative component; part of the observed effect may therefore stem from RLVR or the reasoning format alone. Third, we include no representational probe, which could clarify how distant concepts interact internally and how that interaction shapes the final generations. Fourth, many creativity metrics rely on an LLM judge, and the clue-generation reward itself depends on a frozen judge model, so judge bias may influence both training and evaluation. Finally, several reasoning results rest on small test sets, notably AIME with its
30
problems, where a shift of only a few items changes accuracy substantially; these deltas should be read with corresponding caution.

Ethics Statement

Our work focuses on training language models for creative association, improving the diversity of outputs in open-ended tasks. As our results show, creative diversity can trade off against precision, so we caution against using our models or data where factual accuracy is critical, such as medical advice, legal reasoning, or scientific fact-checking. Our training data consists of topics and word lists generated synthetically with a large language model and contains no personal, sensitive, or human-subjects data, and all evaluation benchmarks are publicly available and used in accordance with their licenses. Because several rewards and creativity metrics rely on LLM judges, which can encode cultural and linguistic biases, and because our tasks are English-centric, our results may not generalize across languages or contexts. A key motivation behind our approach is accessibility: by relying on verifiable rewards rather than human preference annotation, using a small training set, and showing gains on models as small as 1.7B, we aim to lower the resource barrier for creativity research. Finally, while we used proprietary language models (Claude Opus) to assist in experimental setup, data curation, and manuscript editing, no portion was generated entirely by an LLM; all content was written, reviewed, and edited by the authors.
