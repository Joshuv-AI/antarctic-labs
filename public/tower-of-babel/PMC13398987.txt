The positive correlation between the progressive photoreceptor damage and the increased macular leakage indicating the severity of diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Qi Fang, Bingjie Qiu, Xinyue Xu, Wei Jia, Ru Wang, Qianfeng Wang, Wei Qiang, Lei Zhang, Jingfa Zhang, Haiyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate photoreceptor changes and their correlation with microvascular alterations in different stages of diabetic retinopathy (DR).MethodsThirty‐two treatment‐naïve DR patients (50 eyes) without clinically significant macular edema were graded into mild, moderate, and severe non‐proliferative DR (NPDR) and proliferative DR (PDR). The macula was imaged using adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (AO‐SLO) at five subsections. Mean/minimum photoreceptor density, cell regularity, dispersion, spacing, and dark areas were recorded. Central macular thickness (CMT) and macular leakage index (MLI) were measured.ResultsThe percentage of dark areas increased gradually with DR severity: 1.86 ± 1.52 (mild NPDR), 3.32 ± 1.45 (moderate NPDR), 4.22 ± 2.11 (severe NPDR), and 5.92 ± 3.06 (PDR) (P = 0.007). MLI also progressively increased (P = 0.01): 0.31 ± 0.60%, 3.47 ± 4.70%, 6.92 ± 5.44%, and 8.98 ± 3.58%, respectively. MLI showed a positive correlation with the percentage of dark areas (r = 0.36, P = 0.01). No significant changes were observed in photoreceptor density, regularity, dispersion, spacing, CMT, or visual acuity.ConclusionsDark areas observed by AO‐SLO suggest photoreceptor abnormalities, indicating decreased waveguiding ability and localized dysfunction as DR progresses. The positive correlation between dark areas and macular vascular leakage implies early dysfunction of the retinal neurovascular unit (NVU) in DR, warranting further investigation.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is characterized by a series of pathological changes caused by retinal microvascular and neural network lesions 1 . Substantial evidence indicates that DR patients experience impaired visual acuity and visual function before the overt vascular changes, possibly due to photoreceptor damage 2 , 3 , 4 . Traditional multimodal imaging, such as color fundus photography, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA), fails to detect the neuronal lesions at the early stage of DR 5 . Recently, adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (AO‐SLO) provides a novel method to detect photoreceptors in vivo 2 , primarily focusing on cell density, cell spacing, and regularity parameters in patients with DR 6 , 7 . Increasing evidence confirms that loss of photoreceptors is detected in the early stage of DR, contributing to the onset and progression of DR 8 . However, it deserves to explore which specific cell level parameter (e.g., rod photoreceptor or cone photoreceptor) in AO‐SLO can indicate the early sign of DR and related to the progression of DR 9 .

This study aimed to evaluate the changes of cone photoreceptors by using AO‐SLO in different stages of DR patients who were without clinically significant macular edema and also investigate its potential associations with microvascular abnormalities in DR.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects

Electronic medical records and multimodal imaging of the patients with DR were collected from December 2024 to January 2025 in Shaanxi Eye Hospital. All patients met the diagnostic criteria for DR proposed by the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) 10 . Subsequently, we staged the patients according to the staging criteria outlined in the “International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy and Diabetic Macular Edema Disease Severity Scale” 11 . Inclusion criteria: (1) DR patients over 18 years old; (2) patients with good cooperation; (3) patients without any prior ocular treatment after onset of DR. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients with other retinal vascular diseases, such as retinal vascular occlusion, retinal vasculitis, and hypertensive retinopathy; (2) patients with clinically significant macular edema according to ETDRS 12 ; (3) patients with optic media opacity affecting imaging quality; (4) any history of previous eye surgery; (5) refractive errors for myopia <−6 diopter or astigmatism >2.5 diopter cylinder.

DR patients were staged into: mild non‐proliferative DR (NPDR), moderate NPDR, severe NPDR, and proliferative DR (PDR), based on the International Clinical Classification of Diabetic Retinopathy and Diabetic Macular Edema Disease Severity Scale 11 .

All participants underwent complete ophthalmological examinations, including color fundus photography (Digital Fundus Camera, VISUCAM224, Carl Zeiss®, Jena, Germany), FFA (Spectralis HRA, Heidelberg®, Germany), OCT (Spectralis OCT, Heidelberg®, Germany), and AO‐SLO (MonaIIa®, Nanjing Botai Medical).

Methods

AO‐SLO

After fully dilation, all patients were performed by using AO‐SLO for obtaining high‐resolution retinal imaging. Subjects were fixed on a target point, and images were captured at the macular center (0°) and at positions 3° superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal to the macular center. Every imaging area had a field of view of 2.4° × 2.4° (Figure 1). Photoreceptors were measured by using the built‐in analysis software provided with the AO‐SLO device. Thus, the cell density, regularity, dispersion, and spacing were measured in five imaging quadrants of each eye. Dark areas were defined as the irregular patchy areas with the low reflectiveness on the en face of AO‐SLO. We defined areas with a grayscale value of zero in ImageJ as “dark areas.” These dark areas were delineated independently by two trained professionals in a double‐blind manner, and then verified by a third investigator to confirm the boundaries before being included for measurement. (Figure 2). The percentage of dark areas was calculated by dividing the dark areas to the detected retinal area (2.4° × 2.4°), which was indicated as % 13 .

FFA

Classic intravenous FFA was filmed using Spectralis HRA® after dilation. The late‐phase images were exported and analyzed via ImageJ image processing software. With the fovea centralis as the center, a circle is drawn with a diameter of three optic discs (about 5.4 mm) 14 . This circular area is defined as the macular region, and the area of fluorescein leakage within this region is then calculated. Macular leakage index (MLI) is defined as the ratio of the fluorescein leakage area to the total area of the macular region, aiming to evaluate the severity of leakage in the macular region among patients with different stages of DR 15 . (Figure 3). The MLI can directly reflect the state of retinal vascular leakage, and microvascular leakage in the macular area is strongly linked to macular edema. This indicator can intuitively demonstrate the vascular leakage condition in DR patients.

Color fundus photograph and late‐phase FFA image of a patient with PDR. (a) Color fundus photograph. (b) FFA image. The MLI was defined as the ratio of the area of the red region to the area of the blue circle. FFA, fundus fluorescein angiography; MLI, macular leakage index; PDR, proliferative diabetic retinopathy.

Oct

Using a cross‐line OCT scanning mode, scans centered on the fovea were performed with a 30° field of view to acquire retinal OCT images of the region. Central macular thickness (CMT) was measured using the device's built‐in software, with thickness defined as the distance from the inner limiting membrane (ILM) to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) layer with a 1 mm diameter centered on the fovea.

Statistical analysis

Data were shown as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using one‐way analysis of variance (anova), followed by Fisher's LSD test for post‐hoc analysis. The mean photoreceptor cell damage area, mean cell density, and minimum cell density were compared across the four stages of DR, as were changes in regularity, dispersion, spacing, CMT, LogMAR, and MLI, with post‐hoc multiple comparisons conducted. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS (version 26). And a P < 0.05 indicates statistically significant.

A total of 50 eyes from 32 patients with different stages of DR were included in this study. There were 22 males and 10 females, with 23 right eyes and 27 left eyes. According to the staging criteria, there were 9 eyes diagnosed with mild NPDR, 14 eyes with moderate NPDR, 18 eyes with severe NPDR, and 9 eyes with PDR, respectively (Table 2).

The ratio of the dark area increased significantly with the increasing DR stage

The ratio of the dark areas (%) was increased significantly with the increasing disease severity, that is, 1.86 ± 1.52% (mild NPDR), 3.32 ± 1.45% (moderate NPDR), 4.22 ± 2.11% (severe NPDR), and 5.92 ± 3.06% (PDR) (Table 2). The ratio of the dark areas (%) showed significant differences among the four disease stages (P = 0.007). As shown in Figure 5c, compared to the mild NPDR group, the ratio of the dark area was significantly increased in severe NPDR and PDR groups, and the ratio of the dark area in the PDR group was also significantly higher than that of the moderate group.

Visual acuity remained relatively unchanged among four groups

The visual acuity (LogMAR) remained unchanged among four groups, that is, 0.19 ± 0.21 (mild NPDR), 0.20 ± 0.20 (moderate NPDR), 0.27 ± 0.22 (severe NPDR), and 0.29 ± 0.14 (PDR), respectively. No significant difference was found among the four stages (P = 0.61, Figure 6a).

CMT remained unchanged among four groups

The mean CMT of Mild NPDR, Moderate NPDR, Severe NPDR, and PDR was 205.56 ± 12.57 μm, 205.00 ± 8.24 μm, 208.33 ± 13.84 μm, and 213.33 ± 9.43 μm, respectively. No significant differences were found among the four stages (P = 0.23, Figure 6b).

MLI increased significantly with the increasing DR severity

The mean MLI was increased significantly with the increasing DR severity, e.g., compared with that of the mild NPDR group (0.31 ± 0.60%), the MLI was increased to 3.47 ± 4.70% (moderate NPDR, P = 0.04), 6.92 ± 5.44% (severe NPDR, P = 0.0001), and 8.98 ± 3.58% (PDR, P < 0.0001), respectively (Figure 7a).

There is a positive correlation between the ratio of the dark area and MLI with DR progression

Given that both MLI and dark area (%) exhibited significant differences among the four stages, we further performed a correlation analysis between them. Among the 9 eyes with mild NPDR, only 3 exhibited vascular leakage in the macular area, while all eyes presented dark areas. The remaining stages exhibit a coexistence of both characteristics. It revealed a statistically significant positive correlation (r = 0.36, P = 0.01, Figure 7b).

DISCUSSION

Traditionally, as a worldwide leading cause of blindness, DR has been regarded as a microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus 16 . However, it is now increasingly recognized that DR is a neurovascular disease characterized by dysfunction of the retinal neurovascular unit (NVU), which comprises retinal neurons, glial cells, the vascular cells, as well as the retinal pigment epithelial cells 17 . Detecting the changes in the NVU holds promise for opening new ways to improve the prevention and treatment of DR. Evidence indicates that retinal photoreceptors are impaired in patients with DR. Lai et al. 18 analyzed microstructural damage in patients with preclinical DR, NPDR, and PDR by using an AO‐SLO and found a significant reduction in photoreceptor cell density only between NPDR and the healthy group. In contrast, Soliman et al. 19 reported that cone cell density in patients with moderate‐to‐severe NPDR and PDR was significantly lower compared to the control group, the no‐DR group, and the mild NPDR group. Additionally, multifocal electroretinogram (mfERG) studies have provided functional evidence of photoreceptor impairment 20 , 21 , 22 .

Recent studies utilizing AO technology in patients with DR have revealed subtle microstructural changes in photoreceptors, further advancing the understanding of DR pathology. Beyond cell density, other metrics include cell spacing, regularity, and dispersion 9 , nearest neighbor distance (NND) 18 , heterogeneity packing index (Hpi) 23 , intensity, MosaicTexture 24 , Waveguiding 25 , hexagonal cones ratio 7 , linear dispersion index (LDi) 26 , and etc. Zaleska et al. 27 found that, compared with healthy volunteers, NPDR patients (including mild and moderate cases) exhibited a significant reduction in cone cell density within the 2° eccentricity area of the macular center, and a continuous decline in arrangement regularity as the disease progressed. This revealed that degenerative damage to photoreceptors occurred even in the early stages of DR before the clinically overt DR. Balas et al. 28 also confirmed that in the 2° and 4° eccentricity regions of the retina, both cone cell density and dispersion of cell arrangement significantly decreased with the progression of DR severity (from mild NPDR to moderate‐to‐severe NPDR and further to PDR) and were closely correlated with visual acuity. Additionally, the vascular Wall Lumen Ratio (WLR) metric showed intergroup differences only at the 2° location, highlighting the dominant role of neuronal damage in disease progression. However, different findings have also been reported; no correlation was found between cell density and the severity of diabetes or DR by Lammer et al. 29 , but an increase in cone cell arrangement irregularity was observed. It also found weakened waveguide properties of cone cells in the macular region of DR patients, along with the presence of dark areas within the mosaic 19 , 25 , 30 . We investigated metrics such as photoreceptor cell density, regularity, dispersion, and spacing, as well as dark areas in DR. Our study confirmed the presence of dark areas across all stages of DR, with the proportion of dark areas gradually increasing as DR severity progressed. However, no significant differences were found in other metrics such as cell density among the various DR stages. Similarly, Gu et al., using the same equipment as in this study, reported no significant differences in parameters such as cell density and regularity between non‐diabetic macular edema (DME) eyes and early‐stage DME eyes 7 .

The origin of photoreceptor waveguiding capability or reflectivity remains unclear. Pircher et al. proposed that reflectivity is associated with the photoreceptor inner‐outer segment junction 31 , on the other hand, Kitaguchi et al. suggested that the primary reflection originates from the outer segment tips 32 . Changes in reflectivity may be related to as‐yet unclarified cellular metabolic activities or functions, suggesting that these dark areas could represent photoreceptors that remain viable but are functionally impaired. A cross‐sectional study using spectral‐domain OCT showed that the photoreceptor outer segment layer in the fovea is thinner in DR/DME patients compared to both healthy controls and diabetic patients without retinopathy, indicating an association between DR severity and alterations in the photoreceptor layer 33 . Animal studies have also demonstrated that diabetes can lead to photoreceptor damage, which manifests in a patchy, localized distribution rather than occurring uniformly across the entire retina 34 . Our findings indicate that dark areas gradually expand as the disease progresses. Although previous studies have identified the presence of dark areas in DR patients, they did not thoroughly investigate their progression, focusing primarily on changes in cell density and abnormalities in cell arrangement regularity 4 , 19 , 30 . Therefore, these patchy dark areas may represent impaired neuronal cells, especially cone photoreceptors. A comprehensive study of DR still requires integrated observation of both neural and vascular components.

Retinal vascular leakage is a highly characteristic and common pathological phenomenon in DR. The leakage index is a novel parameter for assessing retinal leakage. Ehlers et al. utilized ultra‐widefield FFA to compare the panretinal leakage index among patients at different stages of DR and found that the leakage index increases with disease progression, indicating that this index can reflect the severity of DR 35 . Given the significant anatomical differences between the central and peripheral retina, most studies on the leakage index recommend evaluating the retina by partitioning it into zones 36 , 37 . The Ehlers team 15 also found a correlation between MLI and the Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale (DRSS), suggesting that MLI could serve as a potential biomarker for assessing disease progression. This study observed a gradual increase in MLI as DR advanced, indicating that vascular damage in the macular area is detectable across all stages of DR. Notably, while CMT measured by OCT showed no significant differences among the staging groups, FFA revealed a progressive rise in the macular leakage index with DR progression. This finding suggests that microvascular functional impairment in the macular region may occur earlier than significant structural changes in the retina during the early stages of DR 38 , 39 .

Our study further revealed a significant correlation between MLI and dark areas through analysis. The neurons, glial cells, and vascular cells of the retina function cooperatively as a NVU, with each component influencing the metabolism and activity of the others 40 . Studies have confirmed an association between photoreceptor damage and vascular leakage—photoreceptors may release bioactive cellular products that directly or indirectly damage the vascular system. Under diabetic conditions, increased oxidative stress in the retina primarily occurs in the photoreceptor region 41 . This stress response significantly promotes the development of DR, as antioxidants can inhibit vascular pathologies such as capillary degeneration 34 . Tonade also noted that photoreceptors can produce pro‐inflammatory factors under diabetic conditions, thereby contributing to the development of retinal vascular hyperpermeability in mouse models 42 , and their release of soluble mediators can contribute to the death of retinal capillaries in diabetes 43 . Studies have shown that in the early stages of DR, there is an interaction between inflammation and neurodegeneration, forming a pathological cycle. 44 Understanding this interaction could significantly enhance our comprehension of the pathogenesis of DR and help identify more effective therapeutic targets. Additionally, studies by Sawides et al. suggest that persistently localized foveal dark areas over time may be associated with localized retinal ischemia 4 . Studies have shown that elevated VEGF levels in DR can lead to diabetic alterations in photoreceptor function 45 , and disrupt the blood‐retinal barrier, thereby promoting microvascular leakage in the macular area 46 . We observed that the proportion of dark areas in mild‐to‐moderate NPDR was higher than the number of macular leakage sites. Among nine mild NPDR cases, six already showed photoreceptor dark areas to varying degrees even in the absence of macular vascular leakage, indicating that cellular dysfunction might precede the microvascular abnormalities, which aligns with the findings of Balas et al. 28 In recent years, OCT has been used to quantify postoperative changes in RNFL thickness in patients with sNPDR and PDR following different surgical procedures 47 , 48 . These findings have provided insights into the pathological progression from early to late stage DR from the perspective of neural structural damage. Although limited by the cross‐sectional design of this study, these impaired photoreceptors are more likely to appear before retinal microvascular pathology becomes evident. Indeed, our study has several limitations. This is a single site study with a small number of the samples. Besides, our study only enrolled the patients with different stages of DR, but lacks the healthy controls or preclinical individuals. To further assess photoreceptor dysfunction and its relationship with vascular leakage, longitudinal studies tracking dynamic changes in photoreceptors are still required in the future. Also, multisite studies with large samples and controls are needed in future study. In the future, the application of artificial intelligence to identify novel fundus biomarkers may aid in the diagnosis of various diseases 49 . Furthermore, the integration of multimodal imaging technologies, including AO and FFA, could further enhance our understanding of the pathological mechanisms underlying DR, thereby improving clinical decision‐making and prognostic evaluation.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, the dark areas in the retina of diabetic patients, indicative of the dysfunctional photoreceptor, which may reflect their waveguiding abnormalities as the disease progresses. Although the changes of dark area have not yet been found to significantly impact visual acuity, their positive correlation with MIL are interesting and merits further study to explore the possible contribution to the dysfunction of NVU as well as to explore whether these parameters could serve as potential biomarkers to deepen the understanding of DR.
