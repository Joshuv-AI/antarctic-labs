A high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly of Ficus carica cultivar ‘Green Peel’
Authors: Hantang Huang, Tengyue Zhang, Pinqi Sun, Yijun Chen, Dan Zhao, Xingtan Zhang, Huiqin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFig (Ficus carica L.) is an early-domesticated fruit crop of high cultural and economic value. However, high-quality genome resources for evolutionary and genetic diversity studies remain limited. Here we report a high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly for fig cv. ‘Green Peel’ generated with Oxford Nanopore long reads and Hi-C data, and polished with Illumina reads. The final assembly is 306.83 Mb and comprises 13 pseudo-chromosomes, with a contig N50 of 5.77 Mb. Read mapping supported the assembly, with mapping rate and genome coverage exceeding 99.5%, and BUSCO completeness reaching 98.1%. Annotation predicted 27,036 protein-coding genes with 97.3% BUSCO completeness, together with 1,303 transcription factors and 2,080 non-coding RNAs. Comparative analyses with Ficus pumila characterized gene family expansion and duplication modes and identified 131.43 Mb of structural variation. This dataset provides a valuable resource for trait mapping, genome-assisted breeding, and future fig pan-genome studies.

Background & Summary

Fig (Ficus carica L.), one of the earliest domesticated fruit crops, is widely grown from West Asia to the Mediterranean region and holds substantial cultural and economic significance1–3. Within the Ficus genus, fig stands out for its exceptional ecological adaptability and distinctive reproductive biology, featuring the enclosed syconium inflorescence and obligate pollination mutualism with fig wasps4. Fig fruit is widely consumed fresh or dried and is characterized by high sugar content, dietary fiber, and diverse specialized metabolites, including phenolics and flavonoids that contribute to nutritional quality and sensory traits5–8.

Genetic improvement of fig and mechanistic dissection of fruit-related traits increasingly rely on high-quality genome resources that enable accurate gene annotation, robust structural variant discovery, and comparative analyses across Ficus lineages9,10. Previous fig genome assemblies supported initial gene discovery and developmental studies11–14, however, available fig genome assemblies differ in contiguity, chromosome anchoring, repeat representation, and gene annotation quality. A systematic comparison of the available genome resources is therefore essential for clarifying their respective strengths and limitations and for guiding selection in downstream studies.

In this study, we present a high-quality genome assembly for Ficus carica cv. ‘Green Peel’, a representative cultivar widely grown in China. The genome was assembled using ONT long reads and Hi-C data to generate chromosome-scale pseudomolecules, followed by repeat annotation and evidence-driven gene prediction. The final assembly spans 306.83 Mb and contains 27,036 protein-coding genes. Assembly and annotation quality were evaluated using read-mapping statistics, BUSCO completeness, and repeat continuity metrics, supporting the accuracy and completeness of the genome and the resulting gene set. Using this resource, we performed comparative genomic analyses of gene family evolution, duplication modes, and chromosome-scale structural variation relative to Ficus pumila, a closely related Ficus species with high fruit firmness at ripening and an available genome, which was used here as a comparative reference15. Collectively, these data provide a reliable foundation for trait mapping and genome-assisted breeding, and serve as an additional chromosome-level genome to support future fig pan-genome construction and studies of genetic diversity, domestication history, and adaptation across cultivars and wild relatives.

Methods

Sample collection and sequencing

Ficus carica cv. ‘Green Peel’ was grown at the Shangzhuang Experimental Station of China Agricultural University (Haidian District, Beijing, China) (Fig. 1a). For genomic DNA isolation, young, healthy leaves were collected from an eight-year-old, fully grown tree of uniform growth status. For transcriptome sequencing, six types of tissue were sampled: root, young shoot, leaf, fruit flesh, fruit peel, and receptacle. Each tissue was collected from independent trees as three biological replicates. All samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments and sequencing.

Overview of fig cultivar ‘Green Peel’ and the genome assembly and annotation workflow. (a) Mature-stage fruit of Ficus carica cv. ‘Green Peel’, showing the external morphology and a longitudinal section of the syconium. (b) Schematic workflow of genome assembly and annotation.

High-quality genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves using a standard cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol16. DNA purity and integrity were assessed on 1% agarose gels, and DNA concentration was quantified with a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Total RNA was extracted from all collected tissues using a modified CTAB method17,18, followed by DNA digestion with the Recombinant DNase I (RNase-free) kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). RNA purity and concentration were determined with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and RNA integrity was assessed on 1% agarose gels. RNA samples with sharp rRNA bands and no visible degradation were used for subsequent library construction and RNA-seq.

To achieve genome assembly and annotation for fig cv. ‘Green Peel’, we generated four deep-sequencing data sets: Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) reads (~143×), Hi-C reads (~130×), Illumina paired-end (NGS) reads (~77.8×), and RNA-seq reads (Table 1). For ONT long-read sequencing, high-quality genomic DNA was end-repaired and size-selected, and sequencing libraries with an average insert size of approximately 20 kb were prepared using the Native Barcoding Expansion Kit (EXP-NBD104) and Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-LSK109) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). The ONT libraries were sequenced on the GridION platform, yielding 44.58 Gb of reads with an N50 read length of 31 kb for de novo assembly. Hi-C libraries were prepared from young leaf tissue using the GrandOmics Plant Hi-C kit (GrandOmics, Wuhan, China). Briefly, chromatin was cross-linked with formaldehyde and digested with the restriction enzyme MboI. The resulting sticky ends were filled in with biotin-14-dCTP before proximity ligation. The ligated DNA was then purified, mechanically sheared, and enriched for biotin-labeled fragments using streptavidin-coated beads, followed by end repair, A-tailing and adapter ligation. The final Hi-C libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA), generating 40.42 Gb of clean Hi-C reads for chromosome-level scaffolding. For Illumina paired-end sequencing, DNA libraries were prepared according to the Illumina standard protocol and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA), generating 24.12 Gb of clean data for genome survey analyses and polishing of the assembled contigs. Strand-specific cDNA libraries were prepared from total RNA using Illumina standard procedures and sequenced on the HiSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA) to support gene prediction and functional annotation.

Genome size estimation

The genome size of Ficus carica cv. ‘Green Peel’ was first estimated from k-mer frequencies using KmerGenie (v1.7048)19. Clean Illumina paired-end reads were supplied as input, and the program was run with default settings to automatically identify the optimal k-mer size. KmerGenie identified k = 53 as the optimal value and estimated a haploid genome size of approximately 287.14 Mb (Supplementary Fig. S1). Since short-read–based k-mer estimates can be affected by repeats and sequencing biases in plant genomes, we independently determined genome size by flow cytometry.

Apple (Malus domestica Borkh. cv. ‘Golden Delicious’)20, whose genome size is well characterized and sufficiently different from that of fig ‘Green Peel’ to produce distinct fluorescence peaks, was used as the internal reference. Fresh young leaves of ‘Green Peel’ and ‘Golden Delicious’ were simultaneously chopped in ice-cold nuclear isolation buffer, and the mixed nuclei suspension was filtered through a 40-μm nylon mesh. Nuclei were treated with RNase A, stained with propidium iodide (PI), and G0/G1 fluorescence signals were measured on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA). Three independent replicates were performed. The genome size of ‘Green Peel’ was calculated from the mean fluorescence ratio of sample to standard, yielding an estimate of 309.04 Mb (Supplementary Table S1). The k-mer-based estimate was lower than the flow cytometry estimate, likely due to the influence of repetitive sequences, heterozygosity, and sequencing bias on short-read k-mer distributions. Flow cytometry provided an independent estimate of genome size. The flow cytometry estimate of 309.04 Mb was close to the chromosome-level genome size reported for the F. carica ‘Brunswick’ assembly (323.01 Mb)14 and was used as a reference for subsequent genome assembly.

Genome assembly, polishing and Hi-C scaffolding

All ONT reads were used for de novo genome assembly with NECAT (v0.01)21. NECAT employs a two-step strategy: self-correction and contig assembly. In the correction stage, NECAT was run with an estimated genome size of 310 Mb (GENOME_SIZE = 310000000) and an effective correction coverage of 55 × (PREP_OUTPUT_COVERAGE = 55). Two correction iterations were performed (NUM_ITER = 2), while other overlap-and consensus-related options were used the default settings. In the assembly stage, the corrected reads were assembled with the same estimated genome size parameter (GENOME_SIZE = 310000000), contig polishing enabled (POLISH_CONTIGS = true), and an effective assembly coverage of 50 × (CNS_OUTPUT_COVERAGE = 50). Since the ONT long reads already provided high continuity and consensus accuracy, we skipped the optional contig-bridging step to avoid potential mis-joins and used the NECAT contig output directly as the initial assembly, which totaled 350.07 Mb (Table 2).

The initial assembly was polished in two stages. First, ONT long reads were mapped back to the contigs with minimap2 (v2.25-r1173)22, and one round of long-read polishing was performed with Racon (v1.4.22)23. Subsequently, the Racon-polished assembly was further refined with Illumina data using NextPolish (v1.4.0)24 for three additional rounds to correct residual errors and improve base accuracy. Finally, Purge_haplotigs (v1.1.3)25 was applied to identify and remove redundant contigs based on polished assembly and ONT long reads. After these steps, we obtained a 306.83 Mb contig assembly comprising 210 contigs with a contig N50 of 5.77 Mb, which was used for Hi-C scaffolding and downstream analyses (Table 3).

Hi-C data were used to anchor and order the contigs into a chromosome-level assembly. Raw Hi-C reads were processed and quality-checked with HiC-Pro (v2.11.4)26. Clean read pairs were then aligned to the final contigs using BWA (v0.7.17)27 with default parameters, and the resulting alignments were converted into contact matrices with Juicer (v1.6)28. Automated error correction and iterative anchoring were then performed with the 3D-DNA pipeline (v201008)29, which splits mis-joined contigs and clusters, orders and orients them into chromosome-scale structures based on the Hi-C contact matrices. The draft chromosome models were manually reviewed and edited in Juicebox (v1.9.2)30 to correct remaining errors and adjust contig orientation where necessary. The edited assembly from Juicebox was fed back into 3D-DNA to generate the final chromosome-level assembly (Fig. 1b). In total, 298.69 Mb of contigs were anchored onto 13 pseudo-chromosomes, with an anchoring rate of 97.35% (Fig. 2 and Table 3).

Repeat annotation

Repetitive elements in the fig ‘Green Peel’ genome were annotated using a de novo repeat library and an integrated TE annotation pipeline. First, a repeat library specific to the assembled fig genome was constructed using RepeatModeler (v2.0.1)31 with draft parameters. The library contained consensus sequences of repetitive elements identified directly from the fig genome. EDTA (v2.1.0)32 was then employed for genome-wide transposable element (TE) annotation using the default parameters for plant genomes and the RepeatModeler library as additional input to improve sensitivity. EDTA integrates multiple modules to identify and classify major TE types, including long-terminal-repeat (LTR) retrotransposons, terminal-inverted-repeat (TIR) elements, and Helitrons. LTR insertion times were estimated using LTR_retriever implemented in EDTA, with the neutral substitution rate parameter set to “-u 2.36e-9”, following the synonymous substitution rate reported for Populus trichocarpa33. The pipeline produced a non-redundant TE annotation file and a repeat-masked genome sequence, which were used in subsequent gene prediction. In total, 112.18 Mb of the ‘Green Peel’ genome (36.56%) corresponds to transposable elements. LTR retrotransposons were the most abundant TE class, representing 19.88% of the genome (Fig. 3 and Table 4). Gypsy elements were the major subgroup within the LTR component, occupying 39.05 Mb and accounting for 12.73% of the genome. By contrast, Copia elements represented 3.41% of the genome, covering 10.46 Mb (Table 4). LTR retrotransposon insertion time patterns were broadly similar across the analyzed Ficus genomes, and the median insertion time in ‘Green Peel’ was estimated at 2.15 Mya (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Gene annotation

Protein-coding genes were annotated using GETA pipeline (v2.7.1) (https://github.com/chenlianfu/geta), which combines transcript evidence, homology evidence, and ab-initio prediction to generate a consensus gene set. The repeat-masked genome generated in the repeat annotation step was supplied as input. As transcript evidence, RNA-seq data from multiple tissues were aligned to the genome by HISAT2 (V2.2.1)34, and the resulting alignments were used to reconstruct transcript models, providing exon-intron structures and splice junction support. For homology evidence, protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana, Nicotiana tabacum, and four Ficus species (F. hispida, F. microcarpa, F. pumila, F. erecta), together with F. carica cv. ‘Dottato’, were downloaded from Phytozome (v13) (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/)35 and Ficus Genome Database (http://www.ficusgd.com/)36. All protein sequences were then combined into a single homologous protein dataset and aligned to the genome to infer gene structures and complement transcript-based models. For ab-initio prediction, GETA trained an AUGUSTUS (v3.5.0)37 species model on high-confidence gene models supported by transcript and homology evidence, and then predicted additional gene models across the genome using RNA-derived hints. To generate the final high-confidence gene set, GETA merged gene models from transcript reconstruction, homology-based inference, and AUGUSTUS prediction, and then removed redundancies. Gene models overlapping annotated transposable elements were excluded, and low-confidence models were further filtered using the built-in GETA filter (https://github.com/Datapotumas/GetaFilter). Functional annotation was performed using GFAP (v3.1)38 based on a plant-specific database, including NR39 and Swiss-Prot40 homology searches and assignment of KEGG41 pathways, GO42 terms, and Pfam43 domains, using an E-value cutoff of 1e-5 and requiring sequence identity ≥ 60% for accepted matches. In parallel, non-coding RNAs were identified with INFERNAL (v1.1.4)44, and transcription factors were identified according to Plant TFDB (v5.0) (http://planttfdb.gao-labs.org/)45 (Fig. 1b). A total of 27,036 protein-coding genes were predicted in the ‘Green Peel’ genome, and 21,839 genes were functionally annotated, accounting for 80.78% of the predicted protein-coding genes. The gene set had an average length of 3,600 bp and an average CDS length of 1,168 bp (Table 3); additionally, 2,080 non-coding RNAs and 1,303 transcription factors were identified (Table 5 and Supplementary Fig. S3).

Gene expansion and duplicate classification

To identify expanded gene families in fig and determine the duplication modes underlying their expansion, orthologous gene families were first inferred from protein sequences of seven plant species (Ficus carica, F. hispida, F. microcarpa, F. pumila, F. erecta, Morus notabilis, and Vitis vinifera) using OrthoFinder (v2.5.4)46. The resulting orthogroups were further filtered to remove isoform-driven redundancy, redundant gene models, and copy variants. Expansion and contraction of gene families along the inferred species tree were then evaluated with CAFE (v5.0)47 using a significance threshold of p value < 0.05. Expanded families were subsequently analyzed with Dup-Gen Finder (v1.0.0) (https://github.com/qiao-xin/DupGen_finder)48 to classify genome-wide duplicated genes in fig into five modes: whole-genome duplication (WGD), tandem duplication (TD), proximal duplication (PD), transposed duplication (TRD), and dispersed duplication (DSD). For duplicated gene pairs, Ka, Ks, and Ka/Ks values were calculated using KaKs_Calculator (v2.0)49.

Within the OrthoFinder-CAFE framework, 537 gene families were identified as significantly expanded in fig, whereas 1,056 gene families were significantly contracted (Supplementary Fig. S4). Dup-Gen Finder classified 19,367 duplicated genes into five duplication modes (WGD: 3,886; TD: 2,866; PD: 1,780; TRD: 3,244; DSD: 7,611), and TD/PD accounted for 56.7% of the expanded gene families (Fig. 4a and Table 6). In addition, TD and PD derived duplicate pairs exhibited higher Ka/Ks ratios than those from other duplication modes (Fig. 4b).

Structural variation detection

F. pumila was selected as the comparison species due to its close relationship with F. carica within the Ficus genus (Supplementary Fig. S4), the availability of a chromosome-level genome assembly, and markedly higher fruit firmness than F. carica (Supplementary Fig. S5). Comparative structural variation (SV) analysis was performed between the fig cv. ‘Green Peel’ genome assembly and the published Ficus pumila genome15. Whole-genome alignments were generated using nucmer (v4.0.0)50 in two steps: (i) nucmer was run with --maxmatch -c 100 -l 50 -g 1000 -t 12 to produce the initial delta file, and (ii) alignments were filtered using delta-filter with -1 -q -r -i 89 -l 50 to retain high-confidence one-to-one alignments for downstream SV calling. Structural variants were identified and classified with SyRI (v1.6.3)51, including major rearrangements such as inversions and translocations. Collinearity and SV plots were generated using plotsr (v1.0.0)52. SV coordinates were overlapped with the fig gene annotation using BEDTools (v2.30.0)53 to quantify the number of genes affected by each SV category.

SyRI detected 131.43 Mb of structural variation in the ‘Green Peel’ genome relative to Ficus pumila. Notably, highly diverged regions comprised 78.57 Mb and represented a large proportion of the detected variation, likely reflecting accumulated sequence divergence between the two Ficus species, including lineage-specific repeats, local sequence divergence, and complex rearrangements. The estimate for highly diverged regions should be interpreted with consideration of assembly resolution and cross-species genome alignment. Excluding the highly diverged regions, the remaining SV classes covered 52.86 Mb in the ‘Green Peel’ genome and included 149 inversions (34.37 Mb), 6,294 translocations (11.80 Mb), 1,581 duplications (2.41 Mb), 1,721 deletions (1.95 Mb), 799 copy-loss segments (2.28 Mb), and 96 tandem-repeat differences (55.6 kb) (Fig. 4c and Table 7). Among these non-highly diverged SV classes, inversions and translocations were the dominant categories, accounting for 26.15% and 8.98% of all detected SV sequence, respectively, with 1,985 annotated fig genes overlapped by inversions and 941 by translocations (Supplementary Table S2).

Technical Validation

We assessed the quality and completeness of the genome assembly using multiple complementary metrics. For base-level support, ONT long reads were mapped back to the final assembly using minimap2; mapping rate and genome coverage were calculated with SAMtools (v1.19.2)62, both exceeding 99.5% (Table 3). Chromosome-level assembly consistency was examined with Hi-C data: contact matrices were generated by HiC-Pro and visualized as interaction heatmaps using HiCPlotter (v0.6.6)63. The heatmaps showed a clear main diagonal without obvious mis-join signals, supporting the accuracy of chromosome-scale scaffolding (Fig. 2). Assembly completeness was evaluated using BUSCO (v5.3.2)64 with the embryophyta_odb10 dataset, recovering 98.1% of complete BUSCO orthologs (96.7% single-copy) (Table 3). Based on these assessments, the ‘Green Peel’ assembly exhibited high assembly contiguity and completeness. Together with the published ‘Horaishi’, ‘Dottato’, ‘Orphan’, and ‘Brunswick’ assemblies11–14 (Supplementary Table S3), the available F. carica genome resources provide a comprehensive dataset for fig genomic research. Repeat continuity was quantified with the LTR Assembly Index (LAI) by EDTA, giving a score of 18.14 (Table 3). For annotation quality, predicted protein-coding gene set reached 97.3% BUSCO completeness, and 21,839 genes received functional annotations, supporting the reliability of the gene annotation (Table 3).
