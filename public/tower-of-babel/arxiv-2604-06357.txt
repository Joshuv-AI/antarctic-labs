Helly Theorems for Generalized Turán Problems
Sean English, Sam Spiro

Abstract
Given a graph T T and a family of graphs ℱ \mathcal{F} , the generalized Turán number ex ⁡ ( n , T , ℱ ) \mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F}) is the maximum number of copies of T T in an n n -vertex ℱ \mathcal{F} -free graph. We prove a general theorem which states that for any tree T T , any family ℱ \mathcal{F} , and any integer k k , either ex ⁡ ( n , T , ℱ ) \mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F}) is at least Ω ⁡ ( n k + 1 ) \Omega(n^{k+1}) or at most O ⁡ ( ex ​ ( n , ℱ ) k ) O(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})^{k}) , from which we derive a number of consequences. Our proofs rely on new variants of the classical Helly Theorem for trees which may be of independent interest. As far as we are aware, this is the first known application of Helly theorems for Turán type problems.

Introduction

1.1
Generalized Turán Problems

One of the central areas of extremal combinatorics is the study of Turán numbers
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})
for families of graphs
\mathcal{F}
, which is defined to be the maximum number of edges in an
n
-vertex
\mathcal{F}
-free graph. More generally, given a graph
H
and a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
, we define the generalized Turán number
\mathrm{ex}(n,H,\mathcal{F})
to be the maximum number of copies of
H
in an
n
-vertex
\mathcal{F}
-free graph. Note that
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{2},\mathcal{F})=\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})
, so this is a generalization of the classical Turán number.

The systematic study of generalized Turán numbers was initiated by Alon and Shikhelman
[
1
]
, and since then there has been a large number of results dedicated to the topic,
see e.g.
[
14
,
16
,
17
,
22
,
25
]
. For a more thorough look at generalized Turán numbers we refer the
interested reader to the excellent recent survey by Gerbner and Palmer
[
15
]
.

In this paper we focus on generalized Turán numbers when
H
is a tree. Despite this being a natural extension of the
H=K_{2}
case of the classical Turán problem, there are surprisingly few results in this direction in the literature. Perhaps the first result of this form is the following which appeared in the original paper of Alon and Shikehlman.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

If
T,F
are trees and if
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,F)>0
for
n
sufficiently large, then
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,F)=\Theta(n^{k})
for some integer
k
.

While
Theorem

1.1
is technically a result about generalized Turán numbers of trees, it also “misses the forest for the trees” in the sense that Letzter
[
21
]
showed that
Theorem

1.1
continues to hold with
T
an arbitrary graph. Outside of
Theorem

1.1
, the only other results which consider generalized Turán numbers for arbitrary trees
T
is a paper by Gerbner
[
13
]
who determined the order of magnitude of
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,K_{2,t})
for all trees
T
, and an observation of Cambie, de Joannis de Verclos, and Kang
[
9
]
which determines the exact value of
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,K_{1,t})
for all
T
. In terms of results for specific choices of trees
T
, the most substantial result we know of is a nice theorem of Füredi and Kündgen
[
11
]
which gives essentially optimal bounds on
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{1,t},\mathcal{F})
based on the classical Turán number
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})
. A slightly weakened version of this result can be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\ell
be an integer and
\mathcal{F}
a family of graphs which does not contain a subgraph of a star. If
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})=\Theta(n^{2-\beta})
for some
\beta<\ell^{-1}
, then

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{1,\ell},\mathcal{F})=\Theta(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})^{\ell}n^{1-\ell}),

and if
\beta\geq\ell^{-1}
then

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{1,\ell},\mathcal{F})=\Omega(n^{\ell}).

The main result of this paper is a general bound on
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})
for trees
T
which holds for any choice of
T
and any family
\mathcal{F}
, thereby greatly expanding on the limited set of previously known results listed above.

Our result is spiritually similar to that of
Theorem

1.2
in the sense that it either bounds
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})
as a function of
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})
or lower bounds it by some function independent of
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})
. Specifically, our theorem implies that for any tree
T
, family
\mathcal{F}
, and integer
k
, either
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Omega(n^{k+1})
or
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=O(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})^{k})
. Our theorem moreover gives a criterion for determining whether the bound
\Omega(n^{k+1})
or
O(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})^{k})
holds for a given family
\mathcal{F}
, and to state this condition we need a technical definition.

Definition 1
.

Given a graph
H
, a set of vertices
R\subseteq V(H)
, and an integer
q
, we define the graph
H_{R}^{q}
to be the graph obtained by taking the union of
q
copies of
H
such that each of the copies agree on the set of vertices
R
and are otherwise disjoint. For each integer
k\geq 1
we define

\mathcal{F}_{H,k}^{q}:=\{H_{R}^{q}:R\subsetneq V(H),\ H-R\textrm{ has at least }k\textrm{ connected components}\}.

See Figure
1
for an example of a graph of the form
H_{R}^{q}
. With this our main theorem can be stated as follows.

(a)

H=P_{6}
. The vertices in
R
are red.

(b)

The graph
H_{R}^{4}
.

Figure 1
:
An example of the graph
H_{R}^{4}
from
Definition

1
when
H
is the path on
6
vertices and
R
consists of the set of red vertices in Figure
1(a)
.
H_{R}^{4}
is a member of the family
\mathcal{F}_{H,3}^{4}
since
H-R
has (at least) three connected components.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
T
be a tree,
\mathcal{F}
a family of graphs, and
k\geq 0
an integer. If there exists an integer
q
such that every
\mathcal{F}
-free graph is
\mathcal{F}_{T,k+1}^{q}
-free, then

\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=O_{T,k,q}(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})^{k}),

and if no such
q
exists then

\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Omega_{T,k}(n^{k+1}).

One can derive a number of powerful results using
Theorem

1.3
together with some very short proofs. For example, one can prove the general statement that families
\mathcal{F}
with
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})
small have
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})
close to an integer whenever
T
is a tree.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
T
be a tree and
\mathcal{F}
a family with
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})=O(n^{1+\epsilon})
. If
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})>0
for all sufficiently large
n
, then there exists an integer
k
such that

\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Omega(n^{k}),

and

\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=O(n^{k+k\epsilon}).

In particular, the case
\epsilon=0
gives the following.

Corollary 1.5
.

For every tree
T
and family
\mathcal{F}
containing a forest, if
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})>0
for all sufficiently large
n
, then there exists an integer
k
such that
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Theta(n^{k})
.

This gives a two-way generalization of
Theorem

1.1
, in the sense that we can forbid forests and not just trees, and we can also forbid an entire family of graphs rather than just a single graph. We emphasize that this result can be derived from the original method of
Theorem

1.1
; we highlight its particular statement here both to show the power of what can be proven using
Theorem

1.3
, and also because the exact statement of
Corollary

1.5
can be used to prove a sort of “stability” result for generalized Turán problems for trees.

Corollary 1.6
.

If
T\neq K_{2}
is a tree with
\ell\geq 2
leaves and if
\mathcal{F}
is a family of graphs with
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=O(n^{\ell})
and
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})>0
for all sufficiently large
n
, then
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Theta(n^{k})
for some integer
k
.

In particular, if
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=o(n^{k})
for any integer
k\leq\ell
, then the generalized Turán number must in fact drop all the way down to
O(n^{k-1})
. This sort of stability result is strongest when
T
has many leaves. It is well known that trees with few leaves have large diameter, and for such trees the following (more involved) consequence of
Theorem

1.3
gives effective stability.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer. If
T
is a tree of diameter
d\geq 2k
, then for every family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
with
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=o(n^{k+1})
, we have

\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=O(n^{k+\frac{k(k-1)}{d-k}}).

As we discuss below, this upper bound is tight for paths
P_{d+1}
with
d\equiv 1\mod k-1
. Combining the argument used to prove
Theorem

1.7
together with
Corollary

1.6
allows us to show that all “small” generalized Turán numbers for trees must be very close to an integer power of
n
.

Theorem 1.8
.

For every tree
T
and
0<\epsilon\leq 1
, if
\mathcal{F}
is a family of graphs such that

\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=O(n^{(\epsilon v(T))^{1/3}}),

and such that
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})>0
for all sufficiently large
n
; then there exists some integer
k
such that

\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Omega(n^{k}),

and

\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=O(n^{k+\epsilon}).

This in turn yields the surprising fact that every non-integral exponents can be realized by only finitely many trees.

Corollary 1.9
.

For every non-integral number
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
, there exists at most finitely many trees
T
with the property that there exists a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
satisfying
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Theta(n^{r})
.

One can use
Theorem

1.3
to show that for every tree
T
there exists a (finite) family
\mathcal{F}
with
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Theta(n^{\ell})
where
\ell
is the number of leaves of
T
, and this implies that the assumption
r\notin\mathbb{Z}
is needed for
Corollary

1.9
to hold.

The applications above show the utility of our main result
Theorem

1.3
. However, it is natural to ask whether these bounds are tight. In Appendix
A
we show that the upper bound of
Theorem

1.3
(as well as that of
Theorem

1.7
) is tight whenever
T=P_{t}
is the path graph on
t
vertices and
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{P_{t},k+1}^{q}
with
q
large provided
t\equiv 2\mod k-1
. In particular, this shows that for every
k
there exist infinitely many trees for which the upper bound of
Theorem

1.3
is best possible.

At this point the reader might ask what happens for paths with
t\not\equiv 2\mod k-1
. In this case it turns out we can refine
Theorem

1.3
to give a further sharpening of the upper bound.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of graphs, and
k,t\geq 3
integers with
t\not\equiv 2\mod k-1
. If there exists an integer
q
such that every
\mathcal{F}
-free graph is
\mathcal{F}_{P_{t},k+1}^{q}
-free, then

\mathrm{ex}(n,P_{t},\mathcal{F})=O_{t,k,q}(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})^{k-1}\cdot n),

and if no such
q
exists then

\mathrm{ex}(n,P_{t},\mathcal{F})=\Omega_{t,k}(n^{k+1}).

That is, under the condition
t\not\equiv 2\mod k-1
we can improve upon the upper bound of
Theorem

1.3
by replacing one of the
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})
terms with
n
. This result turns out to be tight whenever
t\equiv 1\mod k-1
; see Appendix
A
for details. In addition to being of interest in its own right, we believe
Theorem

1.10
serves as a proof-of-concept that further refinements can be made for other trees. In particular, we comment on what might be true for arbitrary path lengths in the concluding remarks.

1.2
Helly Theorems

One of our main tools for proving
Theorem

1.3
and its refinement
Theorem

1.10
are generalizations of the classical Helly theorem for trees which we believe are of independent interest.

Historically, the first Helly-type theorems appeared in convex geometry when Helly
[
18
]
proved that if
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that any subcollection
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
of size at least
d+1
contains a point
v^{\prime}
in every element of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, then there exists a point
v
which lies in every element of
\mathcal{C}
. There have since been many other Helly-type theorems which have been studied within convex geometry and beyond
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
19
]
.

In order to recall the statement of the classical Helly Theorem for trees, as well as to state our new variant of this result, we introduce some definitions.

Definition 2
.

Given a tree
T
, a
subtree system

\mathcal{T}
of
T
is a collection of subtrees of
T
. We say that a set of vertices
U\subseteq V(T)

pierces
a subtree
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
if
V(T^{\prime})\cap U\neq\emptyset
, and we say that a set
U
pierces a subtree system
\mathcal{T}
if it pierces every
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
. Similarly, we say that a set of edges
E\subseteq E(T)

pierces
a subtree
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
if a vertex in
V(T^{\prime})
is incident with an edge in
E
, and we say
E
pierces a subtree system
\mathcal{T}
if it pierces every
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
.

In this language, the classical Helly Theorem for trees states that if
\mathcal{T}
is a subtree system such that any pair of elements from
\mathcal{T}
is pierced by a single vertex, then there exists a vertex which pierces all of
\mathcal{T}
. Note that the case of
T
being a path essentially corresponds to the
d=1
case of the classical Helly Theorem.

To prove
Theorem

1.3
, we will need a variant of the Helly Theorem for trees which guarantees a set of
edges
which pierces
\mathcal{T}
.

Theorem 1.11

(Edge Helly Theorem)
.

Let
T
be a tree,
\mathcal{T}
a subtree system of
T
, and
k\geq 0
an integer. If every subcollection
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
of size at most
k+1
can be pierced by at most
k
edges, then
\mathcal{T}
can be pierced by at most
k
edges.

As far as we are aware, our result
Theorem

1.3
is the first known Turán result which is proven using Helly-type theorems. Moreover, one can show that our Edge Helly Theorem implies the classical Vertex Helly Theorem, and as such it is at least as strong as this classical result.

In order to prove
Theorem

1.10
for paths, one might expect that we need a Helly-type Theorem for paths which involves pirecing by a set of edges and a single vertex. While our argument implicitly uses ideas of this form, ultimately the proof requires us to use a more technical notion of piercing which we call “pseudo-piercing”. We defer the precise formulation of pseudo-piercing until
Definition

5
due to it’s technical nature, but we emphasize here that we believe the notion of pseudo-piercing is novel and may provide avenues for future applications of Helly-type arguments to settings where such methods have not previously been used.

We note that if
\mathcal{F}
contains an empty graph, then
\mathrm{ex}(n,H,\mathcal{F})

Given a graph

Applications

In this section we establish the applications of
Theorem

1.3
from the introduction in the order they were stated. Most of these applications have succinct proofs, with the one minor exception being the diameter result
Theorem

1.7
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Let
k\geq 0
be the largest integer such that
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Omega(n^{k})
. Because
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Omega(n^{k+1})
does not hold, by
Theorem

1.3
we must have

\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=O(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})^{k})=O(n^{k+k\epsilon}).\qed

Corollary

1.5
follows immediately from
Theorem

1.4
and the fact that
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})=O(n)
whenever
\mathcal{F}
contains a forest (see e.g. Theorem 2.32 in
[
12
]
), so we omit a formal proof.

Proof of
Corollary

1.6
.

If
\mathcal{F}
contains a forest then the result follows from
Corollary

1.5
. Otherwise, consider the graph
G
obtained by duplicating each leaf of
T
a total of
n/v(T)
times. It is not difficult to see that
G
is a tree for
T\neq K_{2}
, and therefore is
\mathcal{F}
-free since the only subgraphs of
G
are forests. Moreover,
G
has at most
n
vertices and contains at least
\Omega(n^{\ell})
copies of
T
, implying that
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Omega(n^{\ell})
. This combined with our hypothesis gives
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Theta(n^{\ell})
.
∎

Before we can prove
Theorem

1.7
, we need a bound on the extremal number of the generalized theta graph
\theta_{a,b,c}
, which we will achieve by combining a classical result of Bondy and Simonvits
[
7
]
with a recent result of Dong, Gao, and Liu
[
10
]
. To state this latter result, we need a technical definition: given two graphs
H_{1},H_{2}
and vertices
u_{1}\in V(H_{1})
and
u_{2}\in V(H_{2})
, define
H_{1}^{u_{1}}\odot H_{2}^{u_{2}}
to be the graph obtained by taking the union of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
and identifying
u_{1}
with
u_{2}
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
10
]
)
.

If
H_{1},H_{2}
are two copies of the same connected bipartite graph
H
and if
u_{1}\in V(H_{1})
and
u_{2}\in V(H_{2})
come from the same part of the bipartition, then

\mathrm{ex}(n,H_{1}^{u_{1}}\odot H_{2}^{u_{2}})=\Theta(\mathrm{ex}(n,H)).

Corollary 2.2
.

We have

\mathrm{ex}(n,\theta_{a,b,c})=O_{a,c}(n^{1+1/b}).

Proof.

We will prove this in the case when
c
is a power of 2. This will imply the result in general since for any
c
, if
r
is such that
2^{r-1}<c\leq 2^{r}
then
\theta_{a,b,c}\subseteq\theta_{a,b,2^{r}}
and hence

\mathrm{ex}(n,\theta_{a,b,c})\leq\mathrm{ex}(n,\theta_{a,b,2^{r}})=O_{a,2^{r}}(n^{1+1/b})=O_{a,c}(n^{1+1/b}).

The base case follows from the classical upper bound of
\mathrm{ex}(n,\theta_{a,b})=O_{a}(n^{1+1/b})
due to Bondy and Simonvits
[
7
]
for theta graphs. Observe also that for
r\geq 1
we have
\theta_{a,b,2^{r}}=\theta_{a,b,2^{r-1}}^{u}\odot\theta_{a,b,2^{r-1}}^{u}
where
u
is the first vertex of the paths. It follows from
Theorem

2.1
and induction that
\mathrm{ex}(n,\theta_{a,b,2^{r}})=O_{a,2^{r}}(n^{1+1/b})
, proving the result.
∎

The other result we need to prove
Theorem

1.7
is a folklore result which allows us to ignore “tree-like” structures when computing the extremal number of graphs which are not forests. Recall that the
2
-core of a graph
F
, which we will denote as
c_{2}(F)
, is the subgraph of
F
obtained by iteratively deleting vertices of degree at most
1
.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
F
contains a cycle, then
\mathrm{ex}(n,F)=\Theta_{F}(\mathrm{ex}(n,c_{2}(F)))
.

Putting these pieces together gives a bound on the classical Turán number for the family
\mathcal{F}_{T,k+1}^{q}
in terms of the diameter of
T
.

Proposition 2.4
.

If
k\geq 1
is an integer and if
T
is a tree with diameter
d\geq 2k
, then

\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F}_{T,k+1}^{q})=O_{q}(n^{1+\frac{k-1}{d-k}}).

Proof.

We first consider the case
k\geq 2
. Let
P=v_{0}v_{1}\dots v_{d}
be a longest path in
T
, set
b:=\left\lfloor\frac{d-2}{k-1}\right\rfloor\geq 2
, and let

R:=\{v_{1+ib}\mid 0\leq i\leq k-2\}\}.

Then
T-R
has at least
k+1
connected components since the vertices
v_{0},v_{b},\dots,v_{(k-1)b},v_{d}
all lie in distinct components (with this implicitly using
d>1+(k-1)b
), so
T_{R}^{q}\in\mathcal{F}_{T,k+1}^{q}
. Furthermore, it is straightforward to see that
c_{2}(T_{R}^{q})=\theta_{q,b,k-1}
. Putting this all together and applying
Lemma

2.3
and
Corollary

2.2
, we have

\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F}_{T,k+1}^{q})\leq\mathrm{ex}(n,T_{R}^{q})=O(\mathrm{ex}(n,\theta_{q,b,k-1}))=O_{q}(n^{1+1/b})=O_{q}(n^{1+\frac{k-1}{d-k}}),

(1)

with this last inequality using
b\geq\frac{(d-2)-(k-2)}{k-1}=\frac{d-k}{k-1}
,
proving the result.

For the case
k=1
, we aim to show
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F}_{T,2}^{q})=O_{q}(n)
whenever
T
has diameter at least 2, i.e. whenever
T\neq K_{1},K_{2}
. Let
R\subseteq V(T)
denote the set of non-leaves of
T
. Since
T\neq K_{1},K_{2}
, the graph
T-R
consists of isolated vertices for each of the at least 2 leaves of
T
, so
T_{R}^{q}\in\mathcal{F}_{T,2}^{q}
. Moreover,

is simply the tree obtained by duplicating each leaf

T^{\prime}
while maintaining that
\phi
is a monomorphism). For example, if
G=(P_{5})^{n}_{\{\hat{x}_{3},\hat{x}_{4}\}}
then for every
\phi
we will define
\mathcal{T}_{\phi}
to consist of the subtrees which either contain
\hat{x}_{1}\hat{x}_{2}
or
\hat{x}_{5}
, i.e.

\mathcal{T}_{\phi}=\{\hat{x}_{1}\hat{x}_{2},\hat{x}_{1}\hat{x}_{2}\hat{x}_{3},\hat{x}_{1}\hat{x}_{2}\hat{x}_{3}\hat{x}_{4},\hat{x}_{1}\hat{x}_{2}\hat{x}_{3}\hat{x}_{4}\hat{x}_{5},\hat{x}_{2}\hat{x}_{3}\hat{x}_{4}\hat{x}_{5},\hat{x}_{3}\hat{x}_{4}\hat{x}_{5},\hat{x}_{4}\hat{x}_{5},\hat{x}_{5}\}.

More generally, if

, then
\mathcal{T}_{\phi}
will consist of the subtrees that contain a connected component of

.

The key insight about working with these sets

is that whatever edge set

we ultimately choose to identify each copy
\phi
with, this set

must have the property that every
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\phi}
has
\phi(V(T^{\prime}))
intersecting with an edge in

. Indeed, if this were not the case then

would necessarily be contained in “many” copies of
T
, namely those which agree with
\phi
outside of the subtree
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\phi}
which does not intersect with

. Ultimately then, we require a sufficient condition to guarantee that a subtree system
\mathcal{T}
can be pierced by a small set of edges, which motivates the need for our Edge Helly Theorem,
Theorem

1.11
.

4.1
Formal Details

We begin by collecting some technical definitions that will be used throughout this section and the next. Our first definitions precisely characterize which subtrees
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
have “many extensions”, as mentioned in the proof sketch above.

Definition 5
.

Let
T
be a tree,
G
a graph,
\Phi\subseteq\mathrm{Mon}(T,G)
a collection of monomorphisms and
C>0
a constant. Given a subtree
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
, define the
neighborhood of
T^{\prime}

N(T^{\prime}):=\{\hat{x}\in V(T)\setminus V(T^{\prime}):N(\hat{x})\cap V(T^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\}.

For
S\subseteq V(G)
we define the
extendable set

\Psi(T^{\prime},S;\Phi):=\{\phi|_{V(T^{\prime})}:\phi\in\Phi,\ \phi(N(T^{\prime}))=S\}.

That is,
\Psi
is the restriction to
T^{\prime}
of all maps
\phi\in\Phi
which map
N(T^{\prime})
to
S
, i.e. these are maps of
T^{\prime}
which can be extended to a map of
T
while acting on
N(T^{\prime})
in a specified way. Finally, If
\phi\in\Phi
, then we define the
set of highly extendable subtrees
,
\mathcal{T}_{\phi,\Phi,C}
to be the set of subtrees
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
such that
|\Psi(T^{\prime},\phi(N(T^{\prime}));\Phi)|\geq C
.

Our next set of definitions gives a generalization of the notion of piercing which may be of independent interest.

Definition 6
.

Given a tree
T
, a set of distinguishing monomorphisms
\Phi\subseteq\mathrm{Mon}(T,G)
, and some
\phi\in\Phi
, we say a set of vertices
\widehat{W}\subseteq V(T)

(\phi,\Phi)
-pseudo-pierces
a subtree
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
if there exists a vertex
\hat{x}\in V(T^{\prime})
such that

|\{\phi^{\prime}(\hat{x}):\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi,\ \phi^{\prime}(\widehat{W})=\phi(\widehat{W})\}|\leq v(T).

Similarly, we say
\widehat{W}

(\phi,\Phi)
-pseudo-pierces a subtree system
\mathcal{T}
if it
(\phi,\Phi)
-pseudo-pierces every
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
.

Given a set of edges
\widehat{E}\subseteq E(T)
, we let
V(\widehat{E})
denote the set of vertices of
T
incident to an edge of
\widehat{E}
. If
\widehat{E}
is a set of at most
a
edges and if
\widehat{U}\subseteq V(T)
a set of at most
b
vertices, we say that
(\widehat{E},\widehat{U})
is an
(a,b,\phi,\Phi)
-pseudo-piercing set
for
\mathcal{T}
if
V(\widehat{E})\cup\widehat{U}

(\phi,\Phi)
-pseudo-pierces
\mathcal{T}
. Similarly we say that
(\widehat{E},\widehat{U})
is an
(a,b)
-piercing set
for
\mathcal{T}
if
V(\widehat{E})\cup\widehat{U}
pierces
\mathcal{T}
.

It will be useful to record the easy fact that piercing implies pseudo-piercing which we will implicitly use throughout.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
T
is a tree and if
\widehat{W}\subseteq V(T)
pierces some subtree
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
, then
\widehat{W}

(\phi,\Phi)
-pseudo-pierces
T^{\prime}
for any set of distinguishing monomorphisms
\Phi\subseteq\mathrm{Mon}(T,G)
and
\phi\in\Phi
. In particular,
(a,b)
-piercing sets are
(a,b,\phi,\Phi)
-pseudo-piercing sets.

Indeed, this follows because if
\Phi
is distinguishing, we have by
3.3
(a) that any
\hat{x}\in\widehat{W}\cap V(T^{\prime})
(which exists by assumption of piercing) has

|\{\phi^{\prime}(\hat{x}):\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi,\ \phi^{\prime}(\widehat{W})=\phi(\widehat{W})\}|\leq|\{\phi^{\prime}(\hat{x}):\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi,\ \phi^{\prime}(\hat{x})=\phi(\hat{x})\}|=1,

proving this
(\phi,\Phi)
-pseudo-pierces
T^{\prime}
.

The notion of psuedo-piercing is not needed to prove our main result of this section,
Theorem

4.1
. However, it will be necessary in
Section

5
, and so we present our results here in terms of the general notion of pseudo-piercing so that we may use them later on and in potential future works.

As hinted at in the proof intuition above, our goal will be to show that the families
\mathcal{T}_{\phi,\Phi,C}
can be pierced by a small number of edges using the Edge Helly Theorem. We will then use this in conjunction with the following to show that this implies
G
contains few copies of
T
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
T\neq K_{1}
be a tree,
G
a graph,
\Phi\subseteq\mathrm{Mon}(T,G)
a set of distinguishing monormphisms, and
C,a,b
non-negative integers. If
\Phi^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi
is such that for every
\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}
, the collection
\mathcal{T}_{\phi,\Phi,C}
has an
(a,b,\phi,\Phi)
-pseudo-piercing set, then

|\Phi^{\prime}|=O_{C}(e(G)^{a}v(G)^{b}).

Proof.

The result is trivial if
e(G)=0
since
e(T)\geq 1
, so we may assume
e(G)>0
from now on. For each
\phi\in\Phi
, let
\widehat{E}_{\phi},\widehat{U}_{\phi}
be the set of at most
a
edges and at most
b
vertices of
T
such that
V(\widehat{E}_{\phi})\cup\widehat{U}_{\phi}
is a
(\phi,\Phi)
-pseudo-piercing set for
\mathcal{T}_{\phi,\Phi,C}
, and define

E_{\phi}:=\{\phi(\hat{x})\phi(\hat{y}):\hat{x}\hat{y}\in\widehat{E}_{\phi}\},

and

U_{\phi}:=\phi(\widehat{U}_{\phi})=\{\phi(\hat{x}):\hat{x}\in\widehat{U}_{\phi}\}.

Observe that
E_{\phi}
is a set of at most
a
edges of
G
since
\phi
is a homomorphism and similarly that
U_{\phi}
is a set of at most
b
vertices of
G
.

We aim to show that for each set of at most
a
edges
E\subseteq E(G)
and each set of at most
b
vertices
U\subseteq V(G)
, the number of
\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}
with
E_{\phi}=E
and
U_{\phi}=U
is
O(1)
, from which it will follow that
|\Phi^{\prime}|=O(e(G)^{a}v(G)^{b})
since there are at most
O(e(G)^{a}v(G)^{b})
choices for
E,U
(here we implicitly use the assumption
e(G),v(G)>0
).

From now on we fix some
E,U
as above. Define

\Phi^{\prime}_{E,U}:=\{\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}:V(E)\cup U\subseteq\phi(V(T))\},

and

\widehat{V}_{E,U}:=\{\hat{x}\in V(T):|\{\phi(\hat{x}):\phi\in\Phi_{E,U}^{\prime}\}|\leq v(T)\}.

The motivation for this is the following.

Claim 4.4
.

If
\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}
is such that
E_{\phi}=E
and
U_{\phi}=U
, then for every
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\phi,\Phi,C}
, we have
V(T^{\prime})\cap\widehat{V}_{E,U}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

By hypothesis of the proposition, for every
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\phi,\Phi,C}
there exists
\hat{x}\in V(T^{\prime})
such that

\left|\left\{\psi(\hat{x}):\psi\in\Phi,\ \psi\left(V(\widehat{E}_{\phi})\cup\widehat{U}_{\phi}\right)=\phi\left(V(\widehat{E}_{\phi})\cup\widehat{U}_{\phi}\right)\right\}\right|\leq v(T).

(5)

Note that
\phi\left(V(\widehat{E}_{\phi})\cup\widehat{U}_{\phi}\right)=V(E_{\phi})\cup U_{\phi}=V(E)\cup U
. This, (
5
), and the fact that
\Phi^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi
gives

\left|\left\{\psi(\hat{x}):\psi\in\Phi^{\prime},\ \psi\left(V(\widehat{E}_{\phi})\cup\widehat{U}_{\phi}\right)=V(E)\cup U\right\}\right|\leq v(T).

(6)

Since
\Phi^{\prime}
is distinguishing, for any
\psi\in\Phi^{\prime}
, having
\psi(V(\widehat{E}_{\phi})\cup\widehat{U}_{\phi})=V(E)\cup U
is equivalent to having
V(E)\cup U\subseteq\psi(V(T))
. Thus (
6
) is equivalent to
|\{\psi(\hat{x}):\psi\in\Phi^{\prime}_{E,U}\}|\leq v(T)
, which means that
\hat{x}\in\widehat{V}_{E,U}
by definition.
∎

Let
T^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,T^{\prime}_{m}
denote the connected components of
T-\widehat{V}_{E,U}
. The final fact we need is the following.

Claim 4.5
.

If there exists some
\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}
such that
E_{\phi}=E
and
U_{\phi}=U
, then for all
j
,

|\Psi(T^{\prime}_{j},\phi(N(T^{\prime}_{j}));\Phi)|<C.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that this did not hold for some
\phi,j
. By definition this implies
T^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{\phi,\Phi,C}
, but by the previous claim this implies
V(T^{\prime}_{j})\cap\widehat{V}_{E,U}\neq\emptyset
. This is impossible since
V(T^{\prime}_{j})\subseteq V(T)\setminus\widehat{V}_{E,U}
, giving the desired contradiction.
∎

We ultimately aim to show that the number of
\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}
with
E_{\phi}=E
and
U_{\phi}=U
is at most
(C\cdot v(T))^{v(T)}=O(1)
, which will give the desired result. This is certainly true if no such
\phi
exists, so we may assume that at least one such
\phi
exists. Moreover, we observe that every such
\phi
must have
\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}_{E,U}
since this is the only possible way one can have
E_{\phi}=E
and
U_{\phi}=U
by definition of these sets.

For each such
\phi
, let
\phi_{j}:=\phi|_{V(T^{\prime}_{j})}
for
j\geq 1
and let
\phi_{0}:=\phi|_{\widehat{V}_{E,U}}
. Observe that
\phi
is uniquely determined by the tuple
(\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m})
since every
\hat{x}\in V(T)
is either in
\widehat{V}_{E,U}
or
V(T^{\prime}_{j})
for some
j\geq 1
. Note that the number of choices for
\phi_{0}
is at most
v(T)^{|\widehat{V}_{E,U}|}\leq v(T)^{v(T)}
by the definition of
\widehat{V}_{E,U}
and the fact that
\phi\in\Phi^{\prime}_{E,U}
. To count the choices for
\phi_{j}
with
j\geq 1
given
\phi_{0}
, we first observe that
N(T^{\prime}_{j})\subseteq\widehat{V}_{E,U}
by definition of
T^{\prime}_{j}
being a connected component of
T-\widehat{V}_{E,U}
. It follows then that
\phi(N(T^{\prime}_{j}))=\phi_{0}(N(T^{\prime}_{j}))
, so given
\phi_{0}
the set
\Psi(T^{\prime}_{j},\phi(N(T^{\prime}_{j}));\Phi)
is determined. Moreover,
\phi_{j}
is an element of
\Psi(T^{\prime}_{j},\phi(N(T^{\prime}_{j}));\Phi)
since
\phi_{j}
equals
\phi|_{V(T^{\prime}_{j})}
with
\phi\in\Phi
. By
4.5
, there are at most
C
choices for
\phi_{j}
in this set, and hence at most
C^{m}\leq C^{v(T)}
choices of the vectors
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}
given
\phi_{0}
. In total then the number of choices for the vector
(\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m})
encoding
\phi
is at most
(C\cdot v(T))^{v(T)}=O(1)
. Putting all of this together gives the desired bound of
|\Phi^{\prime}|=O(e(G)^{a}v(G)^{b})
.
∎

It remains to find conditions for which
\mathcal{T}_{\phi,\Phi,C}
can be pierced by a small number of edges. For this we will utilize the following essentially equivalent version of
Theorem

1.11
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
T\neq K_{1}
be a tree,
\mathcal{T}
a subtree system of
T
and
k\geq 0
an integer. If for every collection
T^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,T^{\prime}_{k+1}\in\mathcal{T}
, there exists some

such that
(V(T^{\prime}_{i})\cup N(T^{\prime}_{i}))\cap V(T^{\prime}_{j})\neq\emptyset
, then there exists a set of at most
k
edges that pierces
\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

If
k=0
, the statement is vacuously true, so assume
k\geq 1
. Let
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}:=\{T^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,T^{\prime}_{k+1}\}
be a subcollection of
\mathcal{T}
. Our goal is to find a set of
k
edges that pierces
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Let
i,j
be such that
(V(T^{\prime}_{i})\cup N(T^{\prime}_{i}))\cap V(T^{\prime}_{j})\neq\emptyset
and assume without loss of generality that

and
j=k+1
. Let
\hat{u}\in(V(T^{\prime}_{k})\cup N(T^{\prime}_{k}))\cap V(T^{\prime}_{k+1})
, and let
\hat{e}_{k}
be any edge containing
\hat{u}
that intersects
V(T_{k}^{\prime})
, which exists since
\hat{u}\in V(T^{\prime}_{k})\cup N(T^{\prime}_{k})
. Note that
\hat{e}_{k}
intersects both

and

.

Now for each

, let
\hat{e}_{i}
be an edge intersecting
V(T_{i})
. Then
\{\hat{e}_{1},\hat{e}_{2},\dots,\hat{e}_{k}\}
pierces
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Thus by
Theorem

1.11
, there is a set of at most
k
edges which pierces
\mathcal{T}
.
∎

With this we can prove the following.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
T\neq K_{1}
be a tree and
q\geq 2
an integer. Then there exists a contant
C_{0}=C_{0}(T,q)
depending only on
T
and

such that the following holds:

If
k\geq 0
and
C\geq C_{0}
are integers,
G
is a
\mathcal{F}_{T,k+1}^{q}
-free graph, and
\Phi\subseteq\mathrm{Mon}(T,G)
a set of distinguishing monomorphisms, then for each
\phi\in\Phi
there exists a set of at most
k
edges
\widehat{E}_{\phi}\subseteq E(T)
such that
\widehat{E}_{\phi}
pierces
\mathcal{T}_{\phi,\Phi,C}
.

Proof.

We will prove this result with
C_{0}:=\max_{T^{\prime}}{q_{\star}}(T^{\prime},q)
where
{q_{\star}}(T^{\prime},q)
is the constant from
Lemma

3.5
and the maximum ranges over all subtrees
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
. Fix some
\phi\in\Phi
. By

(V(T^{\prime}_{i})\cup N(T^{\prime}_{i}))\cap V(T^{\prime}_{j})=\emptyset
for all

.

Since

, we have

and the fact that each element of

is a monomorphism from

to

, there exist distinct monomorphisms
\psi_{j}^{(1)},\ldots,\psi_{j}^{(q)}\in\Psi(T^{\prime}_{j},\phi(N(T^{\prime}_{j})),\Phi)
as well as a set of vertices

such that
\psi_{j}^{(p)}(\hat{u})=\psi_{j}^{(p^{\prime})}(\hat{u})
for

if and only if

, and the images of
\psi_{j}^{(p)}
and

For convenience let

and define

, noting that these sets partition

since we in particular assumed

Claim 4.8
.

We have
\bigcup_{j\geq 1}N(T^{\prime}_{j})\subseteq V_{0}
.

Proof.

We have that

is disjoint from

by definition, and it is also disjoint from

for all other

by our assumption that
(V(T^{\prime}_{j})\cup N(T^{\prime}_{j}))\cap V(T^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}})=\emptyset
for all

, so

must lie entirely in
V_{0}=V(T)\setminus\bigcup_{j^{\prime}\geq 1}V_{j^{\prime}}
.
∎

For convenience define
\psi_{0}^{(p)}:=\phi|_{V_{0}}
for all

, and note that each

is the restriction of some monomorphism in
\Phi
to
V_{j}
. For

for all

Each map

Fix

Let
\hat{x}\hat{y}

\phi^{(p)}(\hat{x})\phi^{(p)}(\hat{y})=\psi_{j}^{(p)}(\hat{x})\psi_{j}^{(p)}(\hat{x})

is a monomorpism. Thus, we may assume
\hat{x}\in V_{j}
and
\hat{y}\in V_{j^{\prime}}

Since

gives us that

. Because

, we have

\phi^{(p)}(\hat{x})\phi^{(p)}(\hat{y})=\psi_{j}^{(p)}(\hat{x})\phi(\hat{y})=\phi^{\prime}(\hat{x})\phi^{\prime}(\hat{y})\in E(G).

Define

R:=V_{0}\cup\bigcup_{j\geq 1}R_{j}.

Claim 4.10
.

The following holds:

(i)

For all

, we have

, if

, assume that

, and thus since

.

, we are done, so assume

, let

, which exists since

is a proper subset of

, giving at least

.
∎

In total,

gives us that each

is a monomorphism, and Claim

Proof.

Let

, and for

We claim that the paths

. And indeed, the vertices

Up to this point all of our results hold for arbitrary

, but as mentioned around the statement of
Theorem

t\not\equiv 2\mod k-1
. The following observation, which involves finding a “small” tree

to use in

, is the only place where we use this condition.

Lemma 5.13
.

If
(T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{2k})
is a
(k,t,\mathcal{T})
-nice tuple for some collection
\mathcal{T}
of subtrees of
P_{t}
and if
t\not\equiv 2\mod k-1
, then there exists some
1<i^{*}<2k
such that

|V(T_{i^{*}})|\leq\frac{t-3}{k-1}-1.

Proof.

For each even integer
2\leq i\leq 2k-2
let
I_{i}:=[\ell_{i}+1,r_{i+1}-1]
, where here we take
I_{i}:=\emptyset
whenever
r_{i+1}-1<\ell_{i}+1
.

We claim that each
I_{i}
set is disjoint from
S:=\{r_{1},\ell_{2},r_{3},\ell_{4},\ldots,r_{2k-1},\ell_{2k}\}
. Indeed, fix some
j\in[2k]
and some even
i\in[2k]
. Using
5.7
, if
j
is odd and
i<j
, then
r_{j}\not\in I_{i}
since
r_{j}\geq r_{i+1}>r_{i+1}-1
. If
j
is odd and
i>j
, then
r_{j}\not\in I_{i}
since
r_{j}<\ell_{j+1}\leq\ell_{i}<\ell_{i}+1
. Similarly, if
j
is even and
i<j
, then
\ell_{j}\not\in I_{i}
since
\ell_{j}>r_{j-1}\geq r_{i+1}>r_{i+1}-1
. Finally if
j
is even and
i\geq j
, then
\ell_{j}\not\in I_{i}
since
\ell_{j}\leq\ell_{i}<\ell_{i}+1
. Thus,
I_{i}
is indeed disjoint from
S
.

We also claim that
I_{i}\cap I_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
. Indeed if
i,j
are even integers with
i<j
, then this follows from the fact that
r_{i+1}-1\leq r_{j-1}-1<\ell_{j}-1<\ell_{j}+1
.

With these two claims in mind, the pigeonhole principle implies that there exists some even
i^{*}
with
2\leq i^{*}\leq 2k-2
such that

|I_{i^{*}}|\leq\left\lfloor\frac{t-2k}{k-1}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{t-2}{k-1}\right\rfloor-2=\left\lfloor\frac{t-3}{k-1}\right\rfloor-2,

where this last step used
t\not\equiv 2\mod k-1
.

Now,
V(T_{i^{*}})=[\ell_{i^{*}},r_{i^{*}}]\subseteq\{\ell_{i^{*}}\}\cup I_{i^{*}}
since

by

|V(T_{i^{*}})|\leq 1+|I_{i^{*}}|\leq\left\lfloor\frac{t-3}{k-2}\right\rfloor-1\leq\frac{t-3}{k-2}-1

as desired.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we proved that for any tree
T
, integer
k
, and family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
that either
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=\Omega(n^{k+1})
or
\mathrm{ex}(n,T,\mathcal{F})=O(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})^{k})
, with this upper bound being tight for
T=P_{t}
with
t\equiv 2\mod k-1
. Moreover, when
T=P_{t}
with
t\not\equiv 2\mod k-1
we refined this upper bound to
\mathrm{ex}(n,P_{t},\mathcal{F})=O(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})^{k-1}n)
which is tight whenever
t\equiv 3\mod k-1
. In general we believe the following refinement for paths should be true.

Conjecture 6.1
.

Let
k,t
be integers with
k\geq 2
and
t\geq 2k+1
. If
0\leq r\leq k-2
is the unique integer such that
t-3\equiv r\mod k-1
, then every family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
either satisfies

\mathrm{ex}(n,P_{t},\mathcal{F})=O(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F})^{r+2}n^{k-2-r}),

or
\mathrm{ex}(n,P_{t},\mathcal{F})=\Omega(n^{k+1})
.

We note that these bounds would be best possible if true (see Appendix
A
for more), and that our main results imply this conjecture in the cases
r=k-2,k-3
.

There are a number of obstacles towards extending our ideas to prove
6.1
. For example, we lack an effective Helly theorem for piercing subtrees with a given number of vertices and edges.

Problem 6.2
.

For all integers
a,b\geq 0
, determine the smallest number
h=h(a,b)
such that the following holds: if
P_{t}
is a tree and
\mathcal{T}
is a subtree system such that for every
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
of size at most
h
there exists a set of at most
a
edges and a set of at most
b
vertices which pierces
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, then there exists a set of at most
a
edges and at most
b
vertices which pierces
\mathcal{T}
.

We emphasize that we did not directly use a result of this form for proving our path refinement
Theorem

1.10
, but such a result with
a=k-1
and
b=1
served as our initial motivation for how to go about the proof, and it seems such a result in general may be useful for finding a proof for
6.1
.

Appendix A
Tightness for Paths

In this appendix we verify that Theorems
1.3
and
1.10
are tight for paths of certain lengths. More generally, we show the following in the spirit of
6.1
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
k,t
be integers with
k\geq 2
and
t\geq 2k+1
. If
0\leq r\leq k-2
is the unique integer such that
t-3\equiv r\mod k-1
, then there exists
q
sufficiently large so that

\mathrm{ex}(n,P_{t},\mathcal{F}_{P_{t},k+1}^{q})=\Omega(\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F}_{P_{t},k+1}^{q})^{r+2}n^{k-2-r}).

To prove this we will need a technical tool based on the breakthrough result of Bukh and Conlon
[
8
]
using random polynomial graphs to solve a finite family version of the rational exponents conjecture.

To this end, we say a pair
(F,R)
is a
rooted graph
if
F
is a graph and
R\subseteq V(F)
. Given a graph
F
and a set of vertices
S\subseteq V(F)
, we define
e_{S}(F)
to be the number of edges of
F
which are incident to a vertex of
S
. We define the
rooted density
of a rooted graph
(F,R)
to be
\min_{S\subseteq V(F)\setminus R}\frac{e_{S}(F)}{|S|}
.

Theorem A.2

(
[
24
]
, Proposition 2.1)
.

If
H
is a graph and if
(F_{1},R_{1}),\ldots,(F_{t},R_{t})
are rooted graphs with rooted density at least
b/a
for some integers
a,b\geq 1
, then there exists some
q_{0}
depending only on
H
and
\{(F_{1},R_{1}),\ldots,(F_{t},R_{t})\}
such that for all
q\geq q_{0}
,

\mathrm{ex}(n,H,\{(F_{1})_{R_{1}}^{q},\ldots,(F_{t})_{R_{t}}^{q}\})=\Omega(n^{v(H)-\frac{a}{b}e(H)}).

Actually, the result from
[
24
]
is slightly stronger and allows one to forbid not just
(F_{i})_{R_{i}}^{q}
(which is the graph obtained by taking
q
copies of
F_{i}
which agree on
R_{i}
and which are otherwise disjoint), but in fact the family of graphs which can be obtained by taking
q
copies of
F_{i}
which agree on
R_{i}
(but which are not necessarily disjoint outside of
R_{i}
).

One can check that the rooted path graph
(P_{b+1},\{v_{1},v_{b+1}\})
has rooted density
\frac{b}{b-1}
, and also that
(P_{b+1})_{\{v_{1},v_{b+1}\}}^{q}
is the theta graph
\theta_{q,b}
. As such, we obtain the following corollary of
Theorem

A.2
.

Corollary A.3
.

For every graph
H
and integer
b\geq 2
, there exists some
q
sufficiently large such that

\mathrm{ex}(n,H,\{\theta_{q,2},\theta_{q,3},\ldots,\theta_{q,b}\})=\Omega(n^{v(H)-(1-1/b)e(H)}).

We now prove
Proposition

With this claim in mind, we can apply

\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F}_{P_{t},k+1}^{q})\geq\mathrm{ex}(n,\{\theta_{q,2},\theta_{q,3},\ldots,\theta_{q,b}\})=\Omega(n^{1+1/b}).

\mathrm{ex}(n,P_{t},\mathcal{F}_{P_{t},k+1}^{q})\geq\mathrm{ex}(n,P_{t},\{\theta_{q,2},\theta_{q,3},\ldots,\theta_{q,b}\})=\Omega(n^{t-(1-1/b)(t-1)}).
