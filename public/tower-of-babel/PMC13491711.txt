Coping strategies for learning medicine through English Medium Instruction: A mixed‑methods study of Saudi medical students
Authors: Khatmah Alanazi, Samantha Curle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global expansion of English Medium Instruction (EMI) in medical education has intensified demands on students who must simultaneously acquire disciplinary knowledge and operate in a non-native language. This study investigates the coping strategies employed by Arabic-speaking medical students across three Saudi public universities, contributing empirical evidence on how learners adapt to linguistically complex EMI medical contexts.MethodsA convergent mixed-methods design combined semi-structured interviews (n = 50 female students), questionnaires (n = 289), and non-participant observations (n = 9) across classrooms, study groups, and laboratories. Qualitative data underwent thematic analysis using NVivo; quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) in SPSS version 27. Triangulation enhanced validity, with trustworthiness ensured via member checking and reflexivity.ResultsStudents deployed a dynamic repertoire of strategies: multimodal resource use (e.g. 60% commonly watched Arabic YouTube videos, 25% English), translanguaging and translation (68% translated terms), memorisation via flashcards, and collaborative practices (e.g., group translation of slides). Observations confirmed these as central mechanisms for accessing, processing, and consolidating medical knowledge, evolving with proficiency.ConclusionsThese practices are essential for disciplinary learning in EMI medical education and can also be understood as preparing students for participation in multilingual medical settings where English functions as a shared professional resource. Findings offer insights for policy, curriculum design (e.g., legitimising translanguaging, embedding multimodality), and pedagogical and language support, particularly where content is taught through a foreign language. They underscore the need for bilingual approaches to enhance clinical readiness and equity in EMI medical contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09771-x.

Background

The expansion of EMI in higher education has accelerated markedly over the past two decades, particularly in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) disciplines such as medicine [1]. As English consolidates its position as the global language of science and academia, universities in non-Anglophone contexts increasingly adopt EMI to enhance international competitiveness, research visibility, and graduate employability. In Saudi Arabia, this trend is especially prominent in medical education, where English serves as the principal medium of instruction despite students’ predominantly Arabic-speaking backgrounds [2, 3]. English has been the sole medium of instruction since the establishment of the first medical school at King Saud University in 1967, expanding to 30 public schools by 2008, with preparatory years teaching intensive English (20 h per week) [4].

While EMI promises opportunities for access to global knowledge, its implementation in medical education raises considerable pedagogical and linguistic challenges. Students must master highly technical disciplinary content while engaging with it in a foreign language, English. Research shows that processing technical content in a foreign or second language increases cognitive load—intrinsic from content complexity and extraneous from L2 decoding [5, 6], hindering comprehension and performance [7, 8]. In medicine, this overwhelms working memory, limiting germane load for schema-building and learning [9, 10]. These cognitive demands result in difficulties such as struggling with lecture comprehension, grappling with technical reading and writing, experiencing heightened anxiety, and suffering burnout [11, 12, 13, 14]. These challenges prompt researchers and stakeholders to question EMI’s efficacy in non-Anglophone healthcare training, where language barriers boost psychological strain, compromise clinical readiness, and threaten patient safety in local settings [15, 16, 17]. Saudi EMI medical curricula thus prepare graduates for Medical English as a Lingua Franca (MELF) clinical contexts, where English functions as a shared professional resource among multilingual practitioners rather than a native-speaker target [18].

In Saudi hospitals, diverse healthcare teams navigate high-stakes communication through strategic mediation—translation, visual cues, simplified terminology—essential for patient safety [19]. Similarly, in Qatar’s multilingual hospitals, translanguaging (flexible Arabic-English switching) ensures effective clinical communication and patient safety where English serves as medical lingua franca [20]. MELF research underscores this shift from linguistic accuracy to functional intelligibility in healthcare [18, 21].

To overcome these EMI challenges, students develop repertoires of coping strategies to manage linguistic and discipline-specific demands-defined as “actions chosen by learners for the purpose of learning or regulating learning” [20, p. 426), shaped by contextual realities [22]. These span cognitive (e.g., translation), metacognitive (planning), social (peer collaboration), and affective (anxiety management) domains, used dynamically across resources [23, 24]. Research shows that the first language (L1) plays a key role in facilitating EMI medical content learning. For example, translanguaging-flexible L1 deployment for meaning-making [25, 26], serves as vital scaffolding, supporting vocabulary, comprehension, and cognition in global EMI contexts (e.g., Vietnamese [27]; Turkish [28]). Translation from English to L1 is also found to be essential for accessing technical terms and concepts [29]. Moreover, multimodality, the integration of gestures, sounds, diagrams, and other semiotic resources, allows students to negotiate meaning through multiple channels and enables them to mobilise their full linguistic and semiotic repertoires. This fosters deeper comprehension and engagement in an EMI environment [30, 31, 32, 33]. Other strategies, such as symbolic note-taking and peer and teacher support, further facilitate medical content comprehension [34, 35, 36].

Existing research reveals that students face numerous academic challenges from simultaneously mastering content through EMI while adopting varied coping strategies to navigate these demands. However, much scholarship focuses on EMI in non-medical fields, leaving medical contexts critically underexplored, despite high cognitive loads and stakes, making strategies pivotal. This study fills this gap by providing the first mixed-methods evidence on Arabic-L1 Saudi medical students’ EMI coping strategies. It specifically addresses:RQ1: What coping strategies do Arabic-speaking medical students employ to manage linguistic and cognitive demands in EMI medicine?RQ2: How do these strategies function to facilitate comprehension and mastery of medical content in EMI?

RQ2: How do these strategies function to facilitate comprehension and mastery of medical content in EMI?

This study contributes empirical mixed-methods evidence on the interplay between language, cognition, and disciplinary learning in EMI medical education. Its findings have implications not only for pedagogy and curriculum design but also for preparing medical graduates who can operate effectively in English as the global language of medicine while communicating with patients in their first language. These dual communicative demands can also be understood through a MELF lens, in which English functions as a shared professional resource among multilingual medical practitioners rather than as a native-speaker target variety. By illuminating how students negotiate these dual demands, the study underscores the centrality of language-sensitive pedagogies in supporting both academic achievement and future healthcare outcomes, and offers actionable evidence for bilingual curriculum reforms to enhance clinical readiness and equity in non-English-dominant medical contexts where EMI predominates despite Arabic being students’ L1.

Method

Context and participants

A purposive sampling strategy was adopted to recruit participants with direct experience of studying medicine through EMI [37]. Three public medical schools in two major Saudi cities served as research sites. All institutions use English as the sole language of instruction, with the explicit aim of equipping students for healthcare professions. Admission is competitive and based on high school Grade Point Average (GPA) and two national standardised tests: the General Aptitude Test and the Standard Achievement Admission Test. Each programme extends over seven years, beginning with a preparatory year devoted to academic skills and concluding with a clinical internship.

From these schools, fifty female students across all six years of study were recruited as “information-rich cases” ([38], p.264), each with sustained experience of EMI [39]. All participants were female because recruitment occurred in gender-segregated Saudi medical colleges and relied on volunteer self-selection. This approach ensured representation across experience levels while adhering to segregation norms. Participants were aged between 18 and 26 years, and all spoke Arabic as their L1. Most had studied in state schools where Arabic was the medium of instruction, while a smaller number had attended private Arabic-medium schools. Only three had studied in international English-medium schools in Saudi Arabia. Across the sample, English had been acquired as a foreign language during primary and secondary education. Participants had sustained EMI exposure (all studying medicine through English at three public universities) but varied English proficiency reflecting typical patterns in Saudi EMI contexts where students acquire English as a foreign language primarily through formal instruction rather than immersion.

Data collection

Data were generated from three complementary sources to enable triangulation and a comprehensive understanding of students’ coping strategies: semi-structured interviews, a structured questionnaire, and non-participant observations. Interviews were conducted with 50 female students. The interview protocol was piloted with three allied medical students and revised for clarity before data collection, including simplification of terminology such as replacing “multimodality” with “visual aids”. The final guide included demographic questions and open-ended prompts about linguistic challenges and coping strategies in EMI medicine. Open-ended questioning encouraged participants to elaborate freely on their experiences. Each interview lasted approximately 15 min and was audio-recorded to ensure data quality for analysis with participants’ consent. All 50 interviews were conducted by the first author in neutral locations such as campus cafés and quiet study areas to minimise power differentials. They were conducted in Arabic (participants’ L1) to ensure accurate expression of complex coping strategies, considering participants’ sustained EMI exposure alongside their varied English proficiency—acquired primarily through formal instruction.

The questionnaire was administered to 289 students prior to the interviews and was designed to measure the prevalence of coping strategies across the sample. The questionnaire comprised 12 closed-ended items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Often, 5 = Always). Items were grouped into three strategy domains informed by EMI strategy frameworks [26, 35]: multimodal resources (2 items, e.g., “I watch YouTube videos in English in order to understand the content”), written language use (4 items, e.g., “I take notes during lectures in English”), and Arabic-mediated strategies (6 items, e.g., “I translate new terms and concepts”). Overall scale reliability remained excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.94, 12 items ), indicating strong internal consistency [40]. To address students’ language proficiency limitations and avoid misunderstandings, the questionnaire was translated into Arabic by two independent bilingual Arabic-English experts (one medical educator, one applied linguist). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third bilingual expert to ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence. The Arabic version was then piloted with seven target-population medical students using think-aloud protocols and comprehension checks, which led to wording revisions for greater clarity.

Non-participant observations (n = 9) were conducted in classrooms (n = 5), study groups (n = 3), and laboratories (n = 1) to capture naturally occurring coping behaviours in authentic settings. Each observation session lasted 40–50 min sessions with the researcher silently noting multimodal resource use (e.g., whiteboard sketches, phone videos), translanguaging (English-Arabic switching), and collaborative practices. Field notes were taken during and immediately post-session, and photographs of artefacts such as whiteboards and study notes were collected with explicit consent.

Data analysis

The qualitative and quantitative data were analysed separately before integration at the interpretation stage in line with a convergent parallel mixed-methods design []. Interview analysis followed a rigorous three-phase thematic framework [41]: (1) Familiarisation: Arabic recordings transcribed, professionally translated to English (bilingual verification), and sequentially coded (e.g., P1-2 for Participant 1 at study level 2, where “study level” refers to year of enrolment in the medical programme, 1–6 excluding preparatory year) for confidentiality; (2) Coding: researchers systematically identified RQ-relevant segments in NVivo [42]; (3) Interpretation: themes iteratively refined against research questions and literature. Two researchers independently coded 20% of the transcripts (inter-coder κ = 0.85), with discrepancies resolved through discussion to ensure analytic rigour.

Questionnaire data were analysed in SPSS version 27 using descriptive statistics. The ordinal data collected on the questionnaire following the merging of categories was analysed with frequency counts and percentages; Cronbach alphas were calculated to determine questionnaire subscale and overall scale internal consistency; and that crosstabs were used to explore the potential relationship between the variables of study year and strategy patterns. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Non-participant observations (n = 9) across classrooms (n = 5), study groups (n = 3), and laboratories (n = 1) captured authentic coping behaviours during 40–50 min sessions. The researcher silently noted multimodal resource use (whiteboard sketches, phone videos), translanguaging, and collaboration; field notes were expanded immediately post-session with consented artefact photos (whiteboards, notes). Analysis employed the same thematic framework as the interviews and was coded in NVivo alongside the transcripts, enabling cross-validation and direct corroboration of self-reports (e.g., questionnaire 60% Arabic video use matched observed whiteboard YouTube discussions). Because the observational field notes served as corroborative rather than independently coded data, inter-coder reliability was not calculated for them. Integration occurred at the interpretation stage through joint display tables comparing survey frequencies, interview themes, and observational evidence. This triangulation strengthened analytic validity and provided a comprehensive account of how Saudi medical students navigate EMI linguistic and cognitive demands.

Trustworthiness and validity

Several measures were taken to ensure methodological rigour. Following [43], on using trustworthiness in qualitative research, Credibility was supported by triangulating data from three complementary sources, allowing self-reported strategies to be corroborated by observed behaviours. Member checking was conducted informally during interviews, where participants were invited to clarify and expand upon their responses. Dependability was enhanced through detailed documentation of data collection procedures, ensuring transparency and replicability. Confirmability was promoted through reflexive note-taking and the use of NVivo to maintain an auditable analytic trail. Transferability was addressed by providing a detailed account of the research context, enabling readers to assess the applicability of findings to other EMI medical settings.

Results

Through a triangulated questionnaire (n = 289), interviews (n = 50), and observational data (n = 9), the findings showed a pattern of strategy use characterised by multimodal resource use, Arabic-mediated strategies, and broader collaborative practices. These strategies appeared to shift with proficiency, moving from stronger early reliance on Arabic support toward greater confidence with English-oriented practices in later years.

Multimodal resources

Students strategically employ multimodal resources as essential scaffolding mechanisms in EMI medical education, balancing Arabic and English materials to manage cognitive demands and align with assessment needs. Questionnaire data (Table 1) showed 60% of students commonly used Arabic YouTube videos, while observations confirmed frequent whiteboard use during study sessions. Yet interview data nuance this pattern, showing deliberate prioritisation of English content for disciplinary register development and exam success as in P3-1’s exposure to contextual language (e.g., “Watching English medical videos helped me a lot as they provided me with exposure to the language used in a medical context” [P3-1]) and P5-2’s shift from Arabic comprehension to English exam mastery (e.g., “I used to watch medical videos produced in Arabic, …later I shifted to English materials to understand how to answer the exam questions…” [P5-2]). Multimodal strategies extended beyond videos to integrate lecture slides, flashcards, and collaborative whiteboards, forming a self-regulated repair cycle that offloads dense nominalisation characteristic of medical discourse. For instance, P2-4 layered English videos atop slides for clarity, while higher-proficiency P14-4 (IELTS 7.5) used flashcards for terminology recall (“I use flashcards, writing the new word on one side and the meaning on the other to remember terms better” [P14-4]). Observational data corroborated these patterns, documenting regular whiteboard use for collaborative note-taking and discussion of diagnostic/treatment concepts.

L1 reliance and lecture-language use

The analysis revealed that students’ L1 (Arabic) facilitated translation of new terms or concepts, instruction interpretation, and discussion of lecture content with peers, particularly in the early years. Specifically, students commonly used Arabic for translating new terms or concepts 87 (30%) and sometimes 110 (38%); for interpreting instructions, 72 (25%) commonly and 81 (28%) sometimes; and for discussing lecture content with peers, 81 (28%) commonly and 87 (30%) sometimes. Interview data elaborated that early-year students heavily used Arabic for slide translation, particularly for new terms and concepts. For example, P18-3 translated about 80% of slides via apps (e.g., Google Translate) for medical terms, while P26-2 focused on unfamiliar words to build vocabulary, though both noted drawbacks like “time inefficiency” and “inaccuracies.” Group translation mitigated these by dividing slides and verifying collectively (e.g., P28-4; P17-2). Self-explanation in Arabic, translanguaging, aided processing, and better understanding (e.g., “I read information in English and then explain it to myself in Arabic…” [P13-6]).

Two items captured students’ note-taking strategies during lectures in English and Arabic (e.g., “I take notes in English during lectures” and “I take notes in Arabic during lectures Students reported taking notes in both languages, with English note-taking used less frequently than Arabic note-taking. or English note-taking, 19 (7%) selected commonly, 53 (18%) sometimes, and 217 (75%) rarely; for Arabic note-taking, 80 (28%) selected commonly, 86 (30%) sometimes, and 123 (42%) rarely. Qualitative data showed that Arabic note-taking supported rapid lecture understanding, for example: “I wrote the important points in Arabic, and when I get back home, I rewrite them in English” [P9-1; P11-2]. Quantitative patterns also suggested a shift over time, as reliance on Arabic decreased with greater familiarity with medical language [P30-4]. This shift was reflected in students’ comments, such as “Now I need to take notes of a few words only, one or two at this level. I become familiar with the language of medicine,” although complex terms still required Arabic support [P45-6; P48-5].

Broader repertoires of learning strategies

Students adopted a broad repertoire of learning strategies, often combined, to manage EMI demands alongside multimodality and L1 use. Questionnaire data (Table 1) confirmed peer discussion was prevalent—81 students (28%) commonly and 87 (30%) sometimes discussed lecture content with peers—aligning with observations of collaborative whiteboard sessions. Further strategies included repeated listening to recorded lectures (e.g., “…listen to recorded lectures several times…” [P4-1]), reading medical articles (“…read academic articles…” [P25-4]), pre-lecture preparation (“reviewing slides and watching videos before lecture to help me understand what the teacher is saying in class” [P15-3]), structured peer group collaboration (“study groups for discussing concepts and sharing resources helps us comprehend points missed during lectures” [P22-4]), and expert consultation as a last resort (e.g., “consulting teacher during office hours” [P33-2] or “family members with medical knowledge for practical insights” [P19-3]). Most students also endorsed day-by-day study routines to avoid cognitive overload.

Discussion

The findings show that the identified strategies functioned as adaptive scaffolds for EMI’s dual linguistic and cognitive demands. Multimodal resources (videos, diagrams, whiteboards, flashcards) and L1 (Arabic) support offloaded extraneous cognitive load from lexically dense medical discourse, enabling germane schema-building and exam alignment [9, 10]. The use of note-taking in both English and Arabic suggests that students do not rely on a single language when processing lecture content; instead, they use bilingual written mediation as a coping strategy to manage comprehension and memory during EMI instruction. This pattern indicates that note-taking functions not only as a recording task but also as a language-mediated strategy for understanding content in real time. The cross-tabulation suggests that strategy patterns varied by year of study. Earlier-year students appeared more represented in some Arabic-supported strategies, while later-year students appeared more represented in some English-oriented strategies. However, this should be interpreted cautiously, as the analysis is cross-sectional rather than longitudinal.

Questionnaire-interview discrepancies such as higher Arabic video frequency versus. English prioritisation, reflect goal-specific deployment. Arabic-supported multimodality was used for rapid comprehension, while English was selectively prioritised for disciplinary register, examination success, and participation in medicine as a global professional domain. Viewed through a Medical English as a Lingua Franca lens, this pattern suggests that students were not simply compensating for limited English proficiency but strategically prioritising the forms of English most relevant to intelligibility, task completion, and shared professional understanding in medical education and future practice. This mirrors Qatar findings where translanguaging ensures patient safety in multilingual hospitals [20]. It also reflects documented Saudi EMI trajectories, where novices rely on L1 for load management, transitioning to hybrid proficiency yet retaining L1 for specialised terms [18, 19], amid a persistent need for L1.

The findings also show that Arabic L1 scaffolding bridged students to disciplinary literacy, aligning with translanguaging [25, 26], despite monolingual policies. At the same time, the findings suggest that Arabic was not displaced by English but was retained for conceptual grounding and, by implication, for future patient-facing communication in Arabic-dominant local contexts. English, meanwhile, was developed as the shared language of disciplinary learning and medical professional communication. It enabled rapid comprehension repair for cognitively demanding concepts, such as nominalised diagnostics, and minimised extraneous load during initial schema formation. Early-year students also indicated that they retained Arabic for complex terms despite time and accuracy drawbacks, which helped them optimise bilingual resources. Students also developed diverse combined learning strategies to handle EMI challenges beyond multimodality and L1 support (e.g., repeated lecture listening, reading extra medical materials, pre-lecture preparation). These individual strategies enhanced disciplinary knowledge and understanding, facilitated learning, and improved comprehension, as evidenced in previous research findings [34, 35].

These repertoires align with Cognitive Load Theory in medical education, where multimodal resources and L1 use minimize intrinsic/extraneous demands of complex tasks (e.g., diagnostics), freeing capacity for clinical reasoning [14, 44]. However, a MELF perspective also helps qualify an exclusively translanguaging-based interpretation, because in medical education and future clinical settings, flexible multilingual meaning-making must still operate alongside the need for precise, timely, and professionally shared communication [18]. Layered practices like peer translation and whiteboards exemplify distributed cognition through multimodal artifacts and social negotiation [45]. They challenge uniform EMI proficiency thresholds [1] while affirming heterogeneous learner agency in Saudi’s policy-flexible context [46]. By documenting these practices in the under-researched context of EMI medical education in Saudi Arabia, the study extends current understandings of how language, pedagogy, and disciplinary knowledge intersect. Its findings underscore the urgency of legitimising multimodality and multilingualism not as compensatory supports, but as essential pedagogical resources, while challenging dominant monolingual orientations in EMI policy and pedagogy. In doing so, this study makes an important contribution to debates on equity and effectiveness in EMI, offering evidence that can inform both policy and practice in global higher education.

In light of these considerations, several additional limitations and directions for future research should be noted. First, the study was restricted to female students from three public medical schools in two major cities, which may limit generalisability to male students, other regions, diverse institution types, or other disciplines. Second, the interview sample was relatively small for mapping the trajectory of coping strategies across the full medical curriculum, and the same applies to the non-participant observations. In addition, participants’ English proficiency was not formally measured, which limits interpretation of how proficiency shaped strategy use. Finally, although the study relied on self-report methods, this limitation was partly offset by the inclusion of observational data; however, self-report still carries risks of recall bias and social desirability, as participants may over-report “ideal” coping strategies. Future research could use longitudinal and comparative designs, including randomized comparisons of equivalent exam questions presented in English and Arabic, to test the effectiveness of coping strategies more directly. It should also include male participants, students from other regions, and faculty perspectives on L1 use and multimodality. Additionally, quasi-experimental trials testing embedded scaffolds, using pre- and post-tests for comprehension and Saudi Medical Licensure Examination (SMLE) proxies for outcomes, would help establish efficacy and inform scalable teacher training.

Conclusion and implications

This study has illuminated the complex learning strategies adopted by Arabic-speaking medical students in Saudi Arabia as they negotiate the dual demands of disciplinary knowledge acquisition and the specific language demands of medicine. Drawing on triangulated qualitative and quantitative data, the findings demonstrate that students rely on an evolving repertoire of strategies that extend well beyond compensatory practices. These include extensive engagement with multimodal resources, the strategic use of Arabic for translation and translanguaging, bilingual note-taking, and broader individual and collaborative techniques such as peer collaboration, repeated listening, and extensive reading. Importantly, these strategies are dynamic rather than static, shifting over time as students’ disciplinary expertise and command of medical English develop. Overall, the findings suggest that students are not passive recipients of instruction but active agents who build personalised repertoires for navigating the linguistic and cognitive demands of EMI medical education. Rather than indicating linguistic deficiency, these strategies reflect resourceful responses to EMI while also supporting students’ growing participation in medicine as a multilingual professional domain in which English functions as a shared communicative resource.

These findings support the embedding of multimodal and bilingual scaffolds in Saudi EMI medical curricula to strengthen epistemic access and equitable disciplinary learning. For example, bilingual glossaries and lecture slides can layer Arabic glosses onto difficult terms before moving toward English clinical cases, which may reduce cognitive load. Reformulation tasks from Arabic self-explanation to exam-ready English foster systematic translanguaging, formalising students’ adaptive practices in this study. Legitimising translanguaging aligns with other Arab clinical findings, where flexible multilingualism enhances patient safety via English as a medical lingua franca [20, 21]. Faculty training can then support implementation by promoting whiteboard use, peer translation, and guided L1 clarification followed by English synthesis. Institutional policies could also encourage structured group translation activities and multimodal literacy assessments that reward hybrid outputs such as diagram-caption-explanation tasks. Together, these measures would help turn students’ informal learning practices into intentional academic and professional preparation, improving exam readiness and cognitive efficiency while supporting sustained disciplinary learning in EMI.
