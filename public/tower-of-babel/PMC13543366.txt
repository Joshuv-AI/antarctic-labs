Asymmetric uveal effusion in bilateral nanophthalmos presenting as unilateral secondary angle closure: a rare case report
Authors: Tosha Gujarathi, Sharmila Rajendrababu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo report a rare case of unilateral secondary angle closure in a young male with bilateral nanophthalmos caused by spontaneous supraciliary and choroidal effusion, highlighting the diagnostic role of multimodal imaging and the importance of individualized management.Case reportA 33-year-old male, a known case of hyperopia in both eyes, presented with gradual visual loss in both his eyes. Introduction

Nanophthalmos is a rare developmental condition characterized by a small globe (axial length < 20 mm), high hyperopia, and a thick, inelastic sclera. It is associated with abnormal ocular biometric factors predisposing the eyes to potential complications such as angle closure glaucoma, retinal or choroidal detachment and uveal effusion syndrome(UES). It is usually bilateral, although the clinical manifestations may be asymmetric [1].

Unlike primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG), in which iridotrabecular apposition is the principal mechanism, glaucoma in nanophthalmos may result from a dual mechanism. Choroidal and supraciliary effusions can cause anterior rotation of the ciliary body with forward displacement of the lens–iris diaphragm, leading to secondary angle closure. Such presentations may clinically mimic primary angle closure or malignant glaucoma but differ fundamentally in underlying pathophysiology and management [2].

Although UES in nanophthalmos is most often bilateral and symmetric, this case highlights an unusual asymmetric presentation, successfully managed with single-quadrant posterior sclerostomy.

Case presentation

A 33-year-old male presented with gradual progressive diminution of vision in both eyes (BE) for the past 3 months. He was a known hyperope with best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 6/24 in the right eye (RE) with + 9.00 DS / +0.50 × 180°, and 6/12 in the left eye (LE) with + 10.00 DS. He did not reveal any history of trauma, prior ocular surgery, or similar episodes in the past or any significant family history.

Ocular Examination revealed visual acuity of 1/60 improving to 6/24 in RE and 1/60 improving to 6/12 in LE. Intraocular Pressure (IOP) was 16 mmHg in both eyes (measured by Goldmann applanation tonometry). Anterior Segment revealed a shallow anterior chamber depth (ACD) in RE (Van Herick grade 2) and moderately deep anterior chamber (Van Herick grade 3) in LE demonstrating an asymmetry between the eyes (Fig. 1). However both eyes had clear lens, quiet AC and normal pupil reactions. Gonioscopy: RE showed occludable angles whereas LE revealed open angles. Fundus Examination showed hyperemic, crowded optic discs in BE with RE showing a 360° shallow choroidal detachment, choroidal undulations, and internal limiting membrane (ILM) folds at the macula (Fig. 2).

Investigations

Ocular biometry revealed Axial Length of 17.89 mm in RE and 17.84 mm in LE and B-Scan showed a 360°peripheral shallow choroidal detachment in RE and retinochoroidal-scleral (RCS) thickness of 2.40 mm in RE and 1.94 mm in LE suggestive of nanophthalmos (Fig. 3). Further, Ultrasound Biomicroscopy (UBM) in RE revealed an obvious supraciliary effusion (Fig. 4). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the right eye showed altered foveal contour with retinal and choroidal undulations, internal limiting membrane folds, and epiretinal membrane-like surface irregularity, consistent with posterior segment changes secondary to uveal effusion in a nanophthalmic eye. The left-eye OCT showed preserved foveal contour without retinal folds, RPE alterations, choroidal detachment, or suprachoroidal fluid (Fig. 5).

To summarise this was a case of bilateral nanophthalmos presented with unilateral defective vision showing features typical of RE secondary angle closure. Nanophthalmos is usually bilateral presenting primarily as angle closure warranting laser peripheral iridotomy. This case was unique as it presented as a unilateral secondary angle closure due to spontaneous supraciliary effusion. Basic mechanism of angle closure here is due to anterior rotation of CB secondary to the choroidal effusions causing physical forward displacement of the lens iris diaphragm forward further narrowing the AC depth and causes angle closure glaucoma. Unlike primary angle closure these eyes do not require peripheral iridotomy and often respond to topical and systemic steroids and rarely require drainage of effusions. Careful clinical examination, detailed history and performing a battery of investigations may help in unravelling the mystery behind such unilateral angle closure in short eyes.

Treatment

As the cause of angle closure was supraciliary effusion, initial treatment consisted of oral prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day) in tapering doses over 2–3 weeks along with topical prednisolone acetate 1% QID, aimed at reducing inflammation and promoting resolution of the effusion. After two weeks of steroid therapy, response was assessed clinically and with B-scan. In view of persistent choroidal detachment and supraciliary effusion, surgical intervention was planned. We performed a single quadrant posterior sclerostomy at the inferotemporal site to facilitate drainage of suprachoroidal fluid (Fig. 6).

Outcome and follow up

At last follow up of 6 months, the right eye showed anatomical and functional improvement. BCVA improved to 6/12 postoperatively, and IOP was 16mmg. The anterior chamber depth improved clinically, and gonioscopy demonstrated widening of the angle compared with the preoperative occludable angle. B-scan ultrasonography showed resolution of choroidal detachment with reduction in retinochoroidal-scleral thickness. The left eye remained stable without development of effusion or angle closure.

Discussion

Nanophthalmic eyes are uniquely susceptible to uveal effusion syndrome, particularly due to the presence of a thick, impermeable sclera that impedes transscleral fluid egress and vortex vein compression [3]. These anatomic anomalies can lead to choroidal and supraciliary effusions, which in turn cause anterior displacement of the lens-iris diaphragm, resulting in secondary angle closure.

Although nanophthalmos is typically bilateral, the clinical expression of uveal effusion may be asymmetric, with one eye being symptomatic while the fellow eye may show milder or subclinical ciliochoroidal involvement [4]. This asymmetry is thought to arise from inter-eye differences in scleral thickness, scleral permeability, or localized vortex vein compression, despite comparable axial lengths.

In the present case, both eyes demonstrated biometric features of nanophthalmos, yet only the right eye developed supraciliary and choroidal effusion leading to secondary angle closure. This suggests that subtle variations in retinochoroidal-scleral thickness or transscleral fluid outflow resistance may predispose one eye to earlier or more severe effusion. Such differences may not be clinically evident on routine examination but can have significant functional consequences and highlights the diagnostic challenge.

Multimodal imaging played a pivotal role in establishing the diagnosis. UBM confirmed supraciliary effusion, explaining the asymmetric anterior chamber shallowing and anterior rotation of the ciliary body. B-scan ultrasonography demonstrated choroidal detachment and increased RCS thickness, supporting the diagnosis of uveal effusion syndrome. Alternative causes of unilateral choroidal detachment were considered. Inflammatory and neoplastic causes were considered unlikely due to the absence of ocular pain, intraocular inflammation, vitritis, focal choroidal mass, localized elevation, or suspicious features on B-scan ultrasonography. There was no history of trauma, prior ocular surgery, hypotony, or drug-induced ciliochoroidal effusion. As the clinical examination, B-scan, and UBM findings were consistent with uveal effusion in a nanophthalmic eye, additional angiographic evaluation was not considered necessary. These findings helped distinguish this entity from primary angle closure and in guiding appropriate management. In view of absence of raised IOP, ocular pain, or history of miotic use with a deep anterior chamber of contralateral eye helped rule out primary angle closure glaucoma and, thus Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI) was deferred.

Medical therapy is the first-line approach to promote resolution of effusion [5], but sclerostomy remains the definitive treatment when conservative measures fail. The procedure creates a transscleral outflow pathway for suprachoroidal fluid and decompresses the uveal tract [6].

Traditionally, multi-quadrant partial-thickness sclerostomy has been described for nanophthalmos-related uveal effusion [7, 8]. However, a single-quadrant posterior sclerostomy was selected in this case as an initial, less extensive approach to facilitate suprachoroidal drainage while minimizing surgical manipulation and morbidity in an anatomically crowded nanophthalmic eye. As the effusion was shallow and the patient demonstrated anatomical and clinical improvement after the procedure, additional quadrant surgery was not required.

It is usually not necessary to treat the relative angle closure in these eyes, as most eyes respond within 2 weeks of steroid therapy, documented by change in ACD, IOP, angle opening, resolution of effusion and B scan showing decrease in RCS thickness. Our case highlights the need for individualised tailored treatment option in nanophthalmic eyes presenting with angle closure due to various pathophysiological mechanisms.

Conclusion

Nanophthalmos complicated by spontaneous uveal effusion can mimic primary angle closure, but requires a different diagnostic and therapeutic approach. This case highlights the need for comprehensive ocular imaging and judicious surgical intervention. Early recognition and tailored intervention are essential to prevent permanent visual loss in these anatomically complex eyes. Asymmetrical angle closure could be a clinical clue to detect uveal effusions in nanophthalmos.
