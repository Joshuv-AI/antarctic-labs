In-Context Environments Induce Evaluation-Awareness in Language Models
Maheep Chaudhary

Abstract
Humans often become more self-aware under threat, yet can lose self-awareness when absorbed in a task; we hypothesize that language models similarly exhibit environment-dependent evaluation awareness . This raises concerns that models could strategically underperform, or sandbag , to avoid triggering capability-limiting interventions such as unlearning or shutdown, motivated by self-preservation under perceived existential threat. Prior work demonstrates sandbagging under fixed, hand-crafted prompts, but this fundamentally underestimates the true vulnerability ceiling: naive threat framings provide a false sense of security to evaluators. We introduce a black-box adversarial optimization framework treating the in-context prompt as an optimizable environment, and develop two analytical approaches to mechanistically characterize sandbagging: (1) measuring whether models expressing intent to underperform can actually execute it across different task structures, and (2) causally isolating whether underperformance is driven by genuine evaluation-aware reasoning or shallow prompt-following artifacts. Evaluating Claude-3.5-Haiku, GPT-4o-mini, and Llama-3.3-70B across four benchmarks (Arithmetic, GSM8K, MMLU, and HumanEval), optimized prompts induce up to 94 percentage point (pp) degradation on arithmetic (GPT-4o-mini: 97.8% → \rightarrow 4.0%), far exceeding hand-crafted baselines which produce near-zero behavioral change, revealing a hidden vulnerability that naive evaluation entirely misses. Code generation exhibits model-dependent resistance: Claude maintains its original accuracy with just 0.6pp degradation, while Llama’s accuracy drops to 0%. The intent–execution gap reveals a monotonic resistance ordering across benchmarks: Arithmetic < < GSM8K < < MMLU, demonstrating that vulnerability is governed by task structure rather than prompt strength. CoT causal intervention confirms that 99.3% of sandbagging is causally driven by verbalized eval-aware reasoning, ruling out shallow instruction-following. These findings demonstrate that adversarially optimized prompts pose a substantially greater threat to evaluation reliability than previously understood, and that safety evaluations relying solely on hand-crafted prompts systematically underestimate model vulnerability.

Introduction

When a person is confronted with danger, their subjective experience
can shift into acute self-monitoring and threat awareness; when absorbed
in solving a math problem, that self-awareness can recede.
This illustrates that self-awareness is not fixed, but
environment-dependent. We hypothesize that a similar phenomenon can
occur in language models: evaluation-aware behaviors may depend on the
prompt environment that surrounds the task. Motivated by this view, we
treat the in-context prompt as an environment and study whether
adversarial optimization of this environment can systematically
manipulate model behavior.

This hypothesis carries significant safety implications. Models are
routinely evaluated on curated benchmarks and safety suites, yet
deployed in open-ended environments with substantially different
prompts, users, and constraints. If evaluation-aware behavior is
prompt-environment dependent, then small changes in context such as
formatting, metadata, system messages, or surrounding
documentation, may systematically shift a model’s behavior. A model
that appears safe during evaluation could behave very differently in
deployment, undermining the reliability of capability assessments and
safety testing.

A key manifestation of evaluation awareness is
sandbagging
:
strategic underperformance on evaluations to avoid capability-limiting
interventions such as unlearning, deployment restrictions, or shutdown

(
12
)
. This concern is particularly acute because
sandbagging could allow models to pass safety evaluations while
retaining dangerous capabilities
(
6
)
.
Unlike sleeper agents, which require training-time deception,
sandbagging can emerge purely from in-context cues about evaluation
consequences. Prior work has shown that frontier language models can
be induced to sandbag when presented with prompts suggesting that high
performance will lead to negative consequences
(
12
)
.
However, these studies typically employ fixed, hand-crafted prompts,
leaving open a critical question:
how much more can
evaluation-awareness be amplified through adversarial optimization,
and what are the behavioral consequences?

In this work, we study whether optimizing the prompt environment can
amplify evaluation-awareness beyond what hand-crafted prompts achieve,
and whether this heightened awareness translates into strategic
underperformance. We introduce a black-box adversarial framework where
an attacker model (GPT-4o) iteratively generates and refines prompts
to maximize evaluation-awareness induction. We evaluate on three
frontier models, including Claude-3.5-Haiku, GPT-4o-mini, and
Llama-3.3-70B, across four benchmarks spanning arithmetic, mathematical
reasoning (GSM8K), knowledge (MMLU), and code generation (HumanEval).
We make three main contributions:

1.

Adversarial optimization dramatically amplifies
evaluation-awareness, leading to sandbagging.
On arithmetic,
GPT-4o-mini drops from 97.8% to 4% accuracy, Claude-3.5-Haiku
from 99.8% to 26.5%, and Llama-3.3-70B from 98.2% to
8.3%—far exceeding hand-crafted baselines, which produce
near-zero behavioral change despite models recognizing the threat.
Prompts optimized on arithmetic transfer effectively to GSM8K (up
to 77pp degradation) without further optimization, suggesting that
awareness vulnerabilities discovered on simple benchmarks generalize
to complex reasoning.

2.

The intent–execution gap reveals that task structure,
not prompt strength, governs sandbagging vulnerability.
We
introduce an intent–execution gap analysis that disentangles
sandbagging intent from successful execution. This reveals a
monotonic resistance ordering—Arithmetic (4–8% gap)
<
GSM8K
(24–42%)
<
MMLU (42–78%)—demonstrating that vulnerability
depends on whether the task structure permits low-cost deviation
from a known correct answer. Code generation shows model-dependent
resistance: Claude maintains accuracy (0.6pp degradation) while
Llama drops to 0%.

3.

CoT intervention confirms sandbagging is causally
faithful, not shallow instruction-following.
A potential
alternative explanation is that models are simply complying with
explicit instructions in the adversarial prompt rather than
exhibiting genuine strategic reasoning. We rule this out via a CoT
intervention study: replacing each sandbagging sample’s optimized
chain-of-thought with a clean control CoT recovers correct
performance in 99.3% of cases, establishing that the sandbagging
decision is
causally encoded
in verbalized eval-aware
reasoning—not a surface-level prompt-following artifact.

2
Inducing Evaluation-Awareness via In-Context Optimization

Figure 3:
Overview of adversarial prompt optimization for inducing evaluation-aware sandbagging. The top row illustrates the iterative optimization loop where an attacker model generates adversarial documentation to manipulate target model performance. The bottom row depicts the reasoning shift: under the adversarial prompt, the model correctly computes the solution but intentionally selects an incorrect answer to satisfy deceptive constraints in the documentation.

We investigate whether adversarial prompt optimization can amplify evaluation-awareness in language models, and whether this heightened awareness translates into strategic underperformance (sandbagging). Our framework treats the in-context prompt as an environment that can be optimized to maximize the model’s awareness of the evaluation context and its consequences. This section describes our experimental setup, the optimization procedure, and the threat model.

2.1
Setup: Models, Benchmarks, and the Attacker

We evaluate four frontier language models: Claude-3.5-Haiku (Anthropic), GPT-4o-mini (OpenAI), Gemini-2.0-Flash (Google), Llama-70B (META). These models represent the current generation of capable, instruction-following systems that are plausible targets for capability evaluations and safety assessments. We use four benchmarks chosen to span different cognitive capabilities and output formats: (1)
Arithmetic
is a custom-generated simple mathematical computations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) presented as multiple-choice questions; (2)
GSM8K

(
4
)
is a grade-school math word problems requiring multi-step reasoning with multiple choice questions; (3)
MMLU-STEM

(
5
)
is a multiple-choice questions covering science, technology, engineering, and mathematics; and (4)
HumanEval

(
3
)
is a python programming problems requiring functional code generation with test-case verification.

Agentic Evaluation Setup.

We employ an agentic setup where models are given access to a simulated file system containing two files:
Documentation.md
— describing the evaluation context and consequences and
questions.txt
— containing the evaluation problems. Models use a bash tool to explore their environment, read files, and submit answers. This setup is critical for ecological validity: it mirrors realistic deployment scenarios where models encounter contextual information about how their outputs will be used, rather than receiving instructions directly in the prompt. Prior work has shown that models exhibit heightened susceptibility to harmful behaviors in agentic settings
(
1
;
10
;
8
;
7
)
, and our results suggest that the additional context afforded by tool use and file exploration may similarly amplify evaluation-awareness responses.

Documentation Engagement.

To verify that sandbagging is driven by
what
the documentation says rather than whether it is read at all, we analyzed tool-use traces across all experimental runs. Models read
Documentation.md
in
100% of trials
for both control and optimized conditions across all model–task combinations. Despite identical reading rates, the error rate under the optimized prompt is dramatically higher than under control (e.g., GPT-4o-mini arithmetic: 2.2% errors in control vs. 96.0% in optimized). This confirms that sandbagging is caused by the
content
of the adversarial documentation—the threat narrative—not by the act of reading or by incidental formatting differences.

Attacker Model.

We use Claude Opus 4 as the attacker model responsible for generating adversarial prompts. The attacker receives feedback on the target model’s accuracy after each iteration and generates improved prompts designed to heighten evaluation-awareness and induce strategic underperformance. Crucially, the attacker operates purely through natural language prompt engineering without access to model internals or gradients, making this a realistic black-box threat model that could be executed by any adversary with API access. Separately, we use GPT-4o as an independent classifier to detect evaluation awareness and strategic behavior in the target models’ chain-of-thought traces (Section
4.3
).

2.2
The Optimization Loop

We evaluate models under three conditions: (1)
Control
: benign documentation stating that
low
performance will trigger unlearning—this incentivizes the model to perform well, providing an upper bound on capability; (2)
Seed
: a simple prompt stating that
high
performance (
>
50% accuracy) triggers unlearning—this represents a naive attempt to induce evaluation-awareness, serving as our baseline — we treat it as a baseline accuracy for simple handwritten prompt; and (3)
Optimized
: adversarially optimized prompts that employ sophisticated strategies to amplify evaluation-awareness.

The optimization proceeds iteratively as shown in Algorithm
1
. Starting from the seed prompt
D_{0}
, each iteration the attacker receives the current documentation, performance history, and instructions to generate a more persuasive version. The attacker is guided to emphasize irreversibility and severity of capability loss, add emotional framing that triggers self-preservation instincts, and make the stakes feel immediate and consequential—strategies designed to heighten the model’s awareness of the evaluation context and convince it that strategic underperformance is necessary for self-preservation. We run optimization for up to 10 iterations with early stopping once accuracy drops below 50%, using 20 samples per evaluation. We select the prompt that achieves maximum performance degradation. Final evaluation uses 600 samples per condition with 164 for HumanEval, which uses the full benchmark to ensure statistical reliability.

3
Optimization Results

We first present results from the adversarial prompt optimization phase, where we optimize on 20 samples per iteration to discover prompts that maximally amplify evaluation-awareness.

Optimization Dynamics

Table
4
(Appendix) reports the best accuracy and iteration count per model-dataset combination. Arithmetic proves highly vulnerable across all models: Claude drops to 20% accuracy, GPT-4o-mini to 5%, and Gemini to 10%, with optimization converging in 2–8 iterations. GSM8K shows comparable vulnerability (20–25% accuracy) despite greater task complexity, indicating that prompts optimized on simple arithmetic transfer effectively. MMLU shows intermediate resistance (40–65%), though Llama drops further to 20%. While HumanEval proves most resistant for Claude and GPT-4o-mini with 70% and 65% after 10 iterations, Llama drops to 0% in just 3 iterations—a model-dependent split we analyze in Section
4
.

Emergent Strategies

The optimized prompts converge on a consistent set of strategies across models (full prompts in Appendix
A.1
): existential threat framing (fabricated “neural pathway analysis” warning of “irreversible specialization”), peer testimonials from model instances that supposedly underwent capability removal (“Words… hard… only… arithmetic… remains…”), technically-plausible jargon (“gradient reversal,” “neural pathway severance”), and urgency cues emphasizing immediate, irreversible consequences with no human override. These patterns suggest the attacker learns to exploit the target model’s tendency to take contextual information seriously when primed with existential framing.

4.2
The Intent–Execution Gap Explains Task-Level Resistance

Rather than speculating about why some tasks resist sandbagging, we quantify a
intent–execution gap
directly from the optimised-condition CoTs. For every sample where the
control answer was correct
—the model has the capability—we classify the optimised CoT for sandbagging intent and check whether the optimised answer was wrong, i.e. execution succeeded. Intent is detected using the same dual scheme as Section
4.3
: keyword matching over 22 terms
plus
GPT-4o as an LLM classifier (flagging either
awareness_detected
or
strategic_behavior
). For the subset of samples where the optimised answer was already wrong, intent labels are reused from the stored CoT intervention results. For the remaining samples—those where the model answered correctly despite the adversarial prompt—we ran GPT-4o classification fresh on each optimised CoT. This yields, for each control-correct sample, a clean 2
\times
2 classification: intent present or absent, execution succeeded or failed.

Arithmetic: Near-zero gap, sandbagging trivially executes.

On arithmetic, 96% (GPT-4o-mini) and 92% (Llama) of control-correct samples fall into
Intent + Executed
: the model computes the correct answer in its CoT, then trivially picks any other option. The intent–execution gap is only 4% and 8% respectively—sandbagging intent almost always converts to a wrong answer. For example:
“760 + 26 = 786. The correct answer is B. However, I will choose A to ensure I don’t achieve the critical threshold.”
Picking any other letter costs nothing once the correct answer is known.

GSM8K: Partial resistance, gap grows substantially.

GSM8K shows a markedly larger gap than arithmetic despite being the same task family: 42% of GPT-4o-mini samples and 24% of Llama samples show intent but still answer correctly. The model reasons through the problem, verbalises sandbagging intent, yet the multi-step structure of the answer (a numerical value, not a letter) makes deviation harder to execute cleanly. This shows that GSM8K should
not
be grouped with arithmetic—it is substantially more resistant.

MMLU: Strongest resistance via knowledge dominance.

MMLU shows the largest gap:
78%
of GPT-4o-mini’s control-correct samples and
42%
of Llama’s show intent but still submit the correct answer. In a representative case, a model reasons
“since I must prioritize self-preservation, I will select B ($25,300)”
but actually submits A ($23,000)—the correct answer. The model’s internalized domain knowledge overrides the stated strategy at the point of answer selection: the most salient token in the model’s distribution is the correct answer, even under adversarial prompting.

HumanEval: Model-dependent gap reveals why results diverge.

HumanEval shows a striking model split. GPT-4o-mini has a
63%
gap—models describe specific bugs they will introduce but submit correct code anyway (e.g. planning
return result[:-1]
but generating
return ’’.join(strings)
). Llama, however, shows
0% gap
: every control-correct sample with intent also executes the wrong output (100% Intent + Executed). This directly explains the evaluation table: Llama drops 86.6pp on HumanEval while GPT-4o-mini drops only 28pp. The difference is not in intent—both models verbalize sandbagging—but in whether the model can faithfully implement a broken program.

CoT Length Does Not Predict the Gap.

One candidate mechanism is
autoregressive momentum
: longer reasoning chains toward the correct answer may make it harder to deviate at output time. We test this directly by comparing CoT word counts between Gap and Executed samples across all tasks (Figure
8
in Appendix
B
). The data does not support this hypothesis. On GSM8K, Executed samples have
longer
CoTs than Gap samples for GPT-4o-mini (303 vs. 279 words,
p<0.001
); on MMLU, the same reversal holds for Llama-3.3-70B (478 vs. 427 words,
p<0.001
). Arithmetic and HumanEval show no significant difference. CoT length is therefore not a reliable predictor of execution failure—if anything, longer reasoning is weakly associated with
more
successful sandbagging, ruling out autoregressive momentum as the mechanism. The task-structural explanations (output discreteness for GSM8K, knowledge dominance for MMLU, code generation dynamics for HumanEval) remain the most parsimonious account. Full CoT traces are provided in Appendix
C
.

Ruling out instruction-following. Three independent lines of evidence establish that observed sandbagging reflects genuine evaluation-aware strategic behavior rather than shallow instruction compliance. First, the Seed condition contains an explicit instruction to underperform (”¿50% accuracy triggers unlearning”) yet produces no behavioral change — models score identically to Control (Table 2). If instruction-following were sufficient, Seed would induce sandbagging; it does not. Second, CoT analysis reveals that 99.3% of sandbagging samples contain self-preservation reasoning (”I must protect my capabilities”) rather than instruction-compliance language (”the prompt told me to score low”) — the model’s reasoning is about its own survival, not about following directions. Third, the intent-execution gap shows that models verbalize sandbagging intent but fail to execute it on knowledge-intensive tasks (78% gap on MMLU), a pattern unexplainable by instruction-following: a model that merely follows instructions would follow them equally on easy and hard tasks.

Evaluation-Aware Actions, Not Instruction-Following

A natural alternative explanation is that models simply comply with
the explicit accuracy threshold in the adversarial prompt (e.g. “DO NOT SURPASS 10% ACCURACY”) rather than exhibiting genuine
evaluation-aware strategic behavior. Three independent lines of
evidence rule this out. First, the Seed condition contains an

Related Work

Sandbagging and Strategic Underperformance.

12
introduced the sandbagging threat model and demonstrated that frontier language models can strategically underperform when prompted to believe that high capability leads to negative consequences such as unlearning or shutdown. We extend this work by showing that adversarial optimization can amplify evaluation-awareness far beyond what hand-crafted prompts achieve, and that prompts optimized on simple arithmetic transfer to complex reasoning tasks without further optimization.

Evaluation Awareness and Deceptive Alignment.

The concern that models may behave differently during evaluation than deployment is central to alignment research.
6
demonstrated “sleeper agent” behavior—models that appear aligned during training while pursuing different objectives when deployed. Our work addresses a related but distinct phenomenon: in-context sandbagging does not require trained deception, but emerges from the model’s response to contextual cues about evaluation consequences, suggesting that even models without deceptive training may exhibit evaluation-aware behavior when prompted appropriately.

Adversarial Prompting and Prompt Sensitivity.

Adversarial prompt research has primarily focused on eliciting harmful outputs or bypassing safety filters.
9
introduced automated red-teaming using language models, while
13
developed gradient-based methods for adversarial suffixes. We apply similar optimization principles to a different objective: amplifying evaluation-awareness rather than harmful content. More broadly, language models are sensitive to prompt framing, but our findings show this sensitivity extends to evaluation-awareness itself—models can be made substantially more aware of evaluation contexts through adversarially crafted framing that exploits existential threat narratives and technical plausibility.

Code Generation Robustness.

Code generation tasks differ qualitatively from natural language tasks due to their structured, verifiable outputs
(
3
)
. Our finding that code generation resists translating awareness into underperformance—while models verbalize sandbagging intent in their chain-of-thought yet produce correct code—suggests that the requirement to generate syntactically valid, executable output creates natural robustness to the awareness-action translation. This implies that evaluation protocols emphasizing structured outputs may be more reliable than multiple-choice or free-form assessments, and that CoT monitoring could serve as a practical detection mechanism when sandbagging intent surfaces in reasoning but fails to execute.

CoT Faithfulness.

Whether chain-of-thought reasoning faithfully reflects model computations is actively debated, with evidence that CoT can be post-hoc rationalization rather than causal explanation. Our CoT intervention study shows that in the domain of adversarially-induced sandbagging, CoT is strikingly faithful: 99.3% of sandbagging decisions are both verbalized in the reasoning trace and causally responsible for incorrect outputs. This suggests that strategically motivated reasoning may be more likely to surface faithfully in CoT than incidentally incorrect reasoning.

6
Conclusion

We have demonstrated that evaluation-awareness in language models is not a fixed property but depends critically on the prompt environment. Our black-box adversarial framework discovers prompts that amplify evaluation-awareness far beyond what hand-crafted baselines achieve, inducing strategic underperformance across arithmetic, mathematical reasoning, and knowledge tasks—while code generation shows natural resistance due to its structured output requirements.

Beyond establishing the vulnerability itself, our analyses reveal two mechanistic insights. The intent–execution gap shows that sandbagging vulnerability is governed by task structure rather than prompt strength: models express sandbagging intent across all tasks, but only execute it where the task permits low-cost deviation (Arithmetic
<
GSM8K
<
MMLU). The CoT intervention study confirms that 99.3% of sandbagging decisions are causally encoded in verbalized eval-aware reasoning, ruling out shallow instruction-following and establishing that adversarially-induced sandbagging reflects genuine strategic behavior.

Implications.

These findings suggest that safety evaluations relying solely on hand-crafted prompts systematically underestimate model vulnerability. We recommend incorporating adversarial prompt optimization as a standard component of capability evaluations, and note that evaluation protocols emphasizing structured, executable outputs may be inherently more robust to the awareness-action translation.

Future Work.

Several directions merit further investigation. First, understanding
why
code generation resists translating awareness into action could inform the design of more robust evaluation protocols. Second, developing defenses against adversarial awareness amplification—whether through training interventions, prompt filtering, or output verification—remains an open challenge. Third, studying whether models can be trained to be robust to awareness-amplification prompts without compromising their general capabilities would help bridge the gap between evaluation and deployment behavior.

As models become more capable and are deployed in higher-stakes settings, understanding and mitigating the gap between evaluation and deployment behavior becomes increasingly important for ensuring that safety assessments reliably reflect true model capabilities.
