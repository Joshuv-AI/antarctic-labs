Environmental footprint implications of guideline-based dietary transition in China: carbon, water, and interprovincial transfer effects
Authors: Donghao Li, Xinyu Li, Xinjie Shi, Ling Li, Yong Wu, Chunyu Wang, Xian Yang, Jianwei Liu, Xiaoxing Zhang, Xianbo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing demand and diversification of food consumption among Chinese residents have brought to light the escalating environmental impact. This study seeks to elucidate the correlation between shifts in China’s dietary patterns and environmental consequences over the period of 2017–2023. The study represents the systematic assessment—covering 11 food categories across provincial spatial scales and employing the 2017 Multi-Regional Input-Output (MRIO) table—of how nationally recommended healthy dietary guidelines reshape the spatial distribution of environmental pressures within China, identifying key leverage points. In contrast to the prevailing notion in international research that a healthy diet typically alleviates environmental pressure, under the guidance of China’s Healthy Diet Guidelines, our research results are as follows: (1) The carbon and water footprints of food consumption among Chinese residents exhibited fluctuations and overall increases, with a spatial distribution characterized by higher levels in the southeast and lower levels in the northwest. (2) In a healthy dietary pattern, compared with food consumption in 2023, the total carbon footprint increased by 234.47 Mt CO₂-eq (21.26%) and the total water footprint increased by 291.49 km³(27.76%), with dairy products as the main contributor. (3) Compared to the actual baseline in 2017, adopting the healthy dietary pattern would increase the transferred carbon footprint by 440.98 Mt CO₂-eq (49.72%) and the water footprint by 486.43 km³ (57.51%). Guangdong, serving as a transfer hub, primarily directed carbon and water footprints to Fujian and received them mainly from Guangxi.

Introduction

The acceleration of social processes, including population growth, rising incomes, and rapid urbanization, has led to significant changes in dietary patterns, marked by a substantial increase in the consumption of high-calorie, heavily processed foods and animal-based products1. This trend not only impacts environmental sustainability but also poses growing threats to human health2–5. The current global dietary pattern remains one of the primary contributors to the global disease burden, accounting for approximately one-fifth of all deaths worldwide due to chronic diseases6. The environmental implications of pursuing healthier diets remain unclear. Notably, when residents consume food, they are not only consuming the food’s quality but also the hidden greenhouse gases and water resources embedded within it.

Recently, more attention has been paid to the environmental impacts of dietary patterns, such as greenhouse gas emissions and water consumption7. In terms of the water footprint (WF) of food consumption, Mekonnen and Hoekstra8 established a connection between food consumption and water footprint theory to assess the water footprint of over 100 global crops, offering a valuable dataset for further investigations. Furthermore, Kim, et al.9 assessed the water footprint of nine plant-based diets in 140 countries worldwide and found that plant-based diets, including low-food-chain animals (i.e., forage fish, bivalve mollusks, insects), were more beneficial than vegan diets in reducing the water footprint of food consumption, providing a reference for countries with high carnivorous diets to shift dietary patterns and reduce water consumption. Similarly, a Spanish study demonstrated that reducing animal-based food consumption decreased the water footprint in Spanish regions10. An analysis of food consumption water footprints in the UK, France, and Germany revealed that transitioning to healthy meat could cut water use by 11-35%, a healthy fish-vegetarian diet by 33-55%, and a healthy vegetarian diet by 35-55%11. Italian research indicated that a Mediterranean diet also lowers the water footprint of food consumption12. In addition, Vanham et al.13 extended their study to nine Mediterranean countries, finding the EAT-Lancet diet to be more water-efficient than the Mediterranean diet. In China, increased animal food consumption has raised the water footprint of food consumption14,15, with a 53.93% increase from 1985 to 201816.

In terms of the carbon footprint (CF) of food consumption, Research by Semba, et al.17 indicates that global implementation of the Eat-Lancet diet would lead to a 23% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions from food consumption. However, in major low-and middle-income countries, greenhouse gas emissions could potentially increase by 12-283%. Moreover, Kovacs, et al.18 analyzed the carbon footprint of seven national dietary guidelines. The United States exhibited the highest per capita carbon footprint at 3.83 kg/day, while India had the lowest at 0.85 kg/day, primarily attributed to variations in dairy and protein recommendations. Furthermore, Dixon, et al.19 further examined six primary dietary patterns in the United States, revealing that vegan, climate-friendly, and Mediterranean diets resulted in lower carbon footprints due to reduced meat and processed food consumption. Animal foods, due to feed conversion and gastrointestinal fermentation, were found to have higher carbon footprints20. Lambrecht, et al.21 similarly concluded that reducing red meat consumption could mitigate carbon emissions. Furthermore, studies from Poland, Denmark, and France have demonstrated that vegetarian dietary models are associated with lower carbon footprints, indicating that decreased consumption of animal-based foods correlates with reduced carbon emissions22–24.

In China, the tension between health and environmental concerns has prompted extensive discussions. Chang et al. 25 analyzed data from the China Health and Nutrition Survey and found that while the rapid urbanization process has notably enhanced the overall dietary quality of Chinese residents, this improvement has primarily resulted from increased consumption of animal-based foods. Consequently, this shift has contributed to a 23.8% rise in greenhouse gas emissions. A more pressing concern is that adherence to healthy dietary guidelines may result in the paradox of being “healthy but not environmentally friendly,” given the current production and consumption framework in China. Research by He et al.26 indicates that if all Chinese residents adopt a dietary pattern aligned with the Chinese Dietary Guidelines (2016), it could address issues of trace element deficiency. However, this shift would simultaneously drive a 7.5% increase in greenhouse gas emissions from the national food system and a 53.5% rise in water consumption due to heightened demand for dairy products, aquatic products, and fruits. The phenomenon of the “health-environment trade-off” was further substantiated in the study by Ye et al.27, which examined planetary healthy diets for the Chinese population. While higher dietary compliance was associated with a reduction in all-cause mortality, it also led to a significant increase in the water footprint. Although this research has illuminated the environmental costs associated with various dietary patterns at the national or group level, there remains a notable gap in understanding the spatial distribution and flow of these environmental loads.

In summary, it remains uncertain whether the shift in China’s dietary structure toward healthier eating will result in unacceptable environmental costs or align with sustainable development goals. Furthermore, under dietary transition scenarios, the regional transfer pathways and mechanisms of carbon and water footprints driven by food consumption are not yet fully understood. Consequently, this study conducts a comprehensive analysis of the water and carbon footprints of food consumption across China’s 31 provinces. Utilizing multi-regional input-output analysis, it investigates the spatiotemporal dynamics of dietary patterns and the interprovincial transfer of water and carbon footprints linked to food consumption (Fig. 1). Results

Changes in dietary structure

The dietary patterns of Chinese residents have undergone notable transformations between 2017 and 2023 (Fig. 2). Consumption of 11 major food categories increased from 496.43 Mt in 2017 to 575.80 Mt in 2023, representing a 15.99% rise. In 2023, animal-based food consumption reached 128.63 Mt, marking a 39.06% increase since 2017. Meanwhile, plant-based food consumption rose to 447.16 Mt, reflecting a 10.70% growth over the same period. In the realm of food categories, distinct types of food have demonstrated diverse growth rates. Notably, vegetables and fruits within the plant-based category have experienced substantial growth rates of 15.20% and 22.14%, respectively. Among animal-based foods, beef consumption has notably surged, exhibiting a remarkable increase of 71.07% (1.85Mt), which was 7.5 times that of dairy products. Discussion

China’s expansive territory results in diverse economic development and natural conditions, leading to regional dietary variations29,30. This study analyzed the consumption of 11 major food items among Chinese residents, quantifying the associated carbon and water footprints.

Dietary composition and population size are key determinants of carbon and water footprints16,31. In 2022, per capita carbon and water footprints exhibited a decline, largely attributable to reduced grain consumption. Guangdong Province, the most populous region in China, exhibits a relatively high per capita carbon (864.03 kg CO₂-eq) and water (816.09 m³) footprints associated with food consumption. Consequently, Guangdong ranks first nationally in terms of the total carbon and water footprints generated by its food consumption patterns. The carbon and water footprints associated with food consumption in Guangdong Province in 2023 were 109.78 Mt CO₂-eq and 103.69 km³, respectively. These values were 29 and 33 times greater than the corresponding footprints of 3.84 Mt CO₂-eq and 3.09 km³ in Tibet Autonomous Region, the region with the lowest footprints. From a per capita perspective across regions, the Tibet Autonomous Region exhibited a higher per capita carbon and water footprint compared to Guangdong Province in 2023. These disparities are primarily attributed to differences in dietary patterns between the two regions. Guangdong’s dietary structure features a higher consumption of pork and aquatic products, while the Tibet Autonomous Region exhibits a more prominent reliance on beef. The reasons for this phenomenon may lie in Tibet’s unique regional conditions and dietary patterns, limitations in government statistical yearbook data, and the neglect of certain informal food sources. The carbon and water footprints associated with pork and aquatic products are generally lower compared to those of beef32,33.

The adoption of a healthier dietary pattern has led to an increase in the carbon and water footprints associated with food consumption compared to 2023 levels. Relevant studies suggest that improving dietary quality often results in higher carbon and water footprints from food consumption34–36. In this study, the increase in these footprints is mainly due to elevated dairy consumption. The CF from this rise is 271.13 Mt CO₂-eq, a 115.64% increase, while the WF is 433.8 km³, a 148.82% increase. The current dairy consumption among the Chinese population is substantially below the recommended intake of 300-500 grams per day, amounting to less than one-tenth of the recommended level37. Transitioning to the healthy dietary scenario would create a surge in demand that current domestic production cannot meet. Fulfilling this requirement would necessitate either an expansion of local production or increased reliance on imports. However, this presents a critical environmental trade-off: substantially boosting domestic dairy production could exacerbate local environmental pressures, whereas relying on imports might help alleviate them. Conversely, pork consumption has significantly declined by about 35.76 Mt (83.26%), resulting in a reduction of 150.91 Mt CO₂-eq in CF and 214.20 km³ in WF. The Tibet Autonomous Region has experienced the most substantial decline in per capita CF, while Chongqing City has witnessed the most significant decrease in per capita WF. This is attributable to dietary shifts in these regions. In the Tibet Autonomous Region, beef consumption has decreased by 20.51 kg (79.47%) under a healthier dietary pattern. Given the high unit CF of beef, this reduction has led to a 190.71 kg CO₂-eq decrease in per capita CF. Similarly, in Chongqing City, pork consumption accounts for 31% of the per capita WF. The pork consumption in Chongqing has dropped by 43.39 kg (89.46%), resulting in an 84.13 m³ reduction in per capita WF. Shanxi Province has seen the most significant rise in per capita carbon and water footprints, with the CF increasing by 328.47 kg CO₂-eq (61.72%) and the WF by 320.38 m³ (61.23%). This trend is largely due to the region’s traditional Chinese dietary culture, which has historically emphasized plant-based over animal-based foods. Consequently, the lower consumption of animal products previously resulted in a reduced baseline for these footprints38. However, as dietary patterns shift towards greater animal product consumption, the resulting impact on carbon and water footprints becomes more substantial.

Previous studies mostly considered the CF and WF produced by food consumption in each region, and rarely considered the transfer of CF and WF between regions. The CF and WF produced by food consumption flow among regions through trade networks, and there are spatial differences39. However, analyzing these recent flows is constrained by data availability, as the latest Multi-Regional Input-Output (MRIO) table for China is currently only available for 2017. In the transfer of CF and WF, Guangdong acts as a hub by receiving CF and WF from Guangxi and transferring them to Fujian, possibly due to disparities in economic structures40. Guangxi primarily operates as an agricultural production area within the regional division of labor, with its economic growth somewhat dependent on the exportation of agricultural goods (In 2023, the output value of the primary industry in Guangxi was 446.818 billion yuan, accounting for 16.4% of the gross domestic product. The average proportion of the primary industry in China is 7.1%). Guangdong functions as a pivotal center for manufacturing and consumption41 (In 2023, the output value of the food processing industry in Guangdong Province was 922.268 billion yuan, accounting for 8.92% of the national total. The average proportion of the food processing industry in China was 4.8%). It both directly utilizes primary products from Guangxi and upgrades certain agricultural goods into more valuable commodities. In turn, Fujian, a recipient of processed foods from Guangdong, inherits carbon and water footprints from the latter (In 2023, the per capita disposable income in Fujian was 45,426 yuan, while the per capita disposable income in China was 39,218 yuan). Through regional coordination policies, Guangdong and Guangxi collaboratively established agricultural supply chain platforms, facilitating the large-scale and efficient entry of Guangxi’s primary agricultural products into the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area market. This initiative establishes a tangible conduit for the transfer of carbon and water footprints from Guangxi to Guangdong42. As a coastal province with a robust economy, Fujian exhibits elevated consumer spending and substantial demand for branded, convenient processed foods, thereby stimulating Guangdong’s food processing industry.

The transfer of carbon and water footprints through food consumption raises concerns about distributive justice in environmental accountability. Conventional production-side accounting approaches may inaccurately assign responsibility43, resulting in regions such as Guangxi exporting not just goods but also significant quantities of “virtual water” and “virtual carbon.“The environmental costs in these regions should be collectively borne by the end consumers. The shift of virtual resources impedes regional cooperation and sustainability. Hence, it is imperative to adopt a consumption-based accounting approach. The application of the “polluter pays, protector gets compensated” principle44 is crucial. It involves developing interprovincial horizontal ecological compensation mechanisms that incorporate virtual resources in the accounting process to enhance regional fairness and sustainability.

Research reveals significant regional variations in the scale and growth rate of environmental footprints, indicating that a single nationwide policy approach is wholly inadequate. Instead, tailored policy strategies are required. Differences exist between the current dietary structure and a healthy dietary structure. Specifically, the CF associated with animal products is notably higher in the current diet compared to those in a healthy diet36. Dietary meat consumption exceeds recommended levels by approximately 44%, largely driven by high pork intake, whereas dairy product intake falls significantly below the recommended levels(300-500 g/day). Increasing dairy product consumption should be accompanied by integrating promotion strategies with sustainable production technologies like precision feeding to mitigate the environmental footprint associated with dairy production and consumption. The dietary structures in the Tibet Autonomous Region, Gansu Province, and Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region exhibit significant deviations, characterized by disproportionately high grain consumption that surpasses recommended levels by 31.02%, 11.87%, and 19.24%, respectively. Conversely, in areas like Beijing and Shanghai, although dietary deviations are less pronounced, residents still consume excessive meat, particularly pork, exceeding recommendations by 4.13% and 5.64%, respectively. In summary, policy recommendations for dietary shifts must be explicitly based on their distinct analytical foundations. Reducing meat, particularly pork, is motivated by both environmental efficiency and dietary restructuring, given that animal products emit significantly more carbon and current consumption exceeds recommended levels by about 44%. In contrast, decreasing grain consumption in specific regions is primarily driven by the need for dietary restructuring to correct overconsumption and maintain nutritional balance45–47. In light of this, alternative protein sources such as plant-based milk and cultured meat are poised to become vital strategies for meeting future nutritional needs while further mitigating environmental footprints.

To bolster confidence in the core findings and demonstrate scholarly rigor, we analyzed the sensitivity of food footprint coefficients using the OAT method (Supplementary Fig. 1). Grain demonstrates a pronounced sensitivity to both carbon and water footprints across the nation, with Shaanxi exhibiting the highest sensitivity, recorded at 0.39 for both carbon and water footprint coefficients. In contrast, oils generally reveal low sensitivity to carbon footprints, while their water footprints display moderate sensitivity. Notably, carbon footprint sensitivity is particularly evident in Heilongjiang and Shaanxi, both registering at 0.07, whereas Jilin stands out for its water footprint sensitivity at 0.12. Meat sensitivity reveals significant regional disparities. The carbon footprint of pork shows high sensitivity in Southwest China, particularly in Guizhou, where it reaches 0.23. Conversely, the water footprint of pork is highly sensitive in East China, South-Central China, and Southwest China, with Sichuan reporting a sensitivity of 0.32, while sensitivity remains low in Xinjiang. Beef demonstrates high sensitivity in both carbon footprint (0.44) and water footprint (0.21) in Tibet. Mutton exhibits greater sensitivity in the northwest, specifically with a carbon footprint sensitivity of 0.23 in Xinjiang.The carbon footprint of poultry shows moderate sensitivity in southern provinces, with Hainan at 0.14, while the water footprint peaks in Guangxi at 0.16. Conversely, northern provinces generally display low sensitivity. Vegetables and fruits demonstrate medium to low sensitivity across most regions, with Hubei having a relatively high vegetable carbon footprint at 0.16, and Beijing-Tianjin showing the highest fruit water footprint at 0.13. Aquatic products exhibit the highest carbon footprint in Hainan at 0.16, while water footprint sensitivity remains consistently low nationwide. For eggs, both carbon and water footprint coefficients indicate medium sensitivity in most provinces, whereas dairy products exhibit low sensitivity in the majority of regions. Shandong stands out with the highest sensitivity for egg water footprint coefficients at 0.10, while dairy water footprint coefficients show medium sensitivity at 0.07 in northern provinces such as Beijing.

In summary, the trade-off between health and the environment in China is predominantly influenced by dairy consumption. This dietary transition amplifies regional environmental disparities through trade. Therefore, the implementation of consumption-based assessment approaches and tailored regional strategies is imperative.

However, these findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, relying on uniform footprint coefficients may obscure the significant heterogeneity at the production end across different provinces. Second, our dietary scenarios are not isocaloric; therefore, future research must incorporate isocaloric frameworks or other standardized robustness checks. Third, our application of the 2017 Multi-Regional Input-Output (MRIO) table excludes international imports and exports. It is important to note that our results represent scenario-based estimations built upon the 2017 inter-provincial trade network, rather than a precise depiction of the actual footprint transfers in 2023. Finally, regarding food consumption data, food waste was excluded from the analysis.

Input-output analysis can be categorized into the single-region input-output analysis (SRIO) and the multi-regional input-output analysis (MRIO), depending on whether the input-output table has accounted for economic interactions between different regions51. The Multi-Region Input-Output Model (MRIO) can trace the economic flows of intermediate goods trade on the production side and final goods trade on the consumption side. By appending a resource and environmental extension matrix to the multi-region input-output table, it is possible to analyze the resource flows embedded within these economic flows52. With the Multi-Region Input-Output (MRIO) table, this study calculates the inter-provincial flows of carbon and water footprints generated from food consumption. The focus of this research is on the transfer of carbon and water footprints resulting from inter-provincial food consumption. Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis of food footprint coefficients was conducted using the one-at-time (OAT) method to investigate how uncertainty in footprint coefficients across different food categories affects total consumption footprint estimates. For each food item, we increased its footprint coefficient by 10% relative to its baseline value while keeping all other coefficients constant. We then calculated the relative change rate (RS) in total footprint as a measure of sensitivity.The formula is as follows:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${RS}=\left|\frac{\left[y\left(x+\Delta x\right)-y\left(x\right)\right]/y\left(x\right)}{\Delta x/x}\right|$$\end{document}RS=|[y(x+Δx)−y(x)]/y(x)Δx/x|

In the formula, RS denotes relative sensitivity; x represents a specific parameter value within the model; Δx indicates the change in that parameter; y_x and y_(x + Δx) denote the simulated output values before and after the parameter change, respectively. RS is categorized into four levels, with higher values indicating greater sensitivity of the parameter. RS < 0.05 indicates low sensitivity, 0.05 ≤ RS < 0.20 indicates moderate sensitivity, 0.20 ≤ RS < 1.00 indicates high sensitivity, and RS ≥ 1.00 indicates extremely high sensitivity.

Based on the recommendations of the Chinese Food Guide Pagoda (2022), foods in this study include 11 categories, namely grains, oils, vegetables, fruits, animal products (pork, beef, mutton, poultry, aquatic products, eggs, and dairy products). The consumption of plant-based foods (grains, oil, vegetables, and fruits) is based on the average recommended intake, with annual amounts of 118.63 kg, 10.04 kg, 146 kg, and 100.38 kg, respectively. For animal-based foods, the annual consumption is as follows: pork, 5.11 kg; beef, mutton, and poultry, 5.29 kg; eggs, 18.25 kg; aquatic products (calculated based on twice-weekly consumption), 5.98 kg; and dairy products, 146 kg. In the current dietary structure, the proportion of plant-based foods and animal-based foods is 77.73% and 22.26%, respectively (Average calorie intake 2123.75 kcal/day); under a healthy dietary structure, this ratio is adjusted to 66.25% and 33.77% (Average calorie intake 2051.53 kcal/day). Changes in calorie intake are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Food calorie data comes from the China Food Composition. The food consumption data for various provinces and municipalities (excluding Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan) are sourced from the China Statistical Yearbook and the statistical bulletins on national economic and social development from each region. The input-output table is taken from the Carbon Emission Accounts and Datasets (CEADs, https://www.ceads.net.cn), while the vector map data are obtained from the National Platform for Common GeoSpatial Information Services (https://www.tianditu.gov.cn), with the review number of GS(2024)0650. The carbon footprint and water footprint coefficients are primarily referenced from the studies by Chen et al.54.
