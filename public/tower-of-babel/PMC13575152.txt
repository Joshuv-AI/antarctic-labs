Optimizing construction risk management through BIM integration and strategic planning
Authors: Ruixin Li, Reem A Almenweer, Haytham F Isleem, Ahmed Sayed M Metwally
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe construction projects are characterized by intricate relations of cost, schedule, resources, and structural aspects that frequently give rise to uncertainty and project risk. In order to overcome these difficulties, this research suggests a Hybrid Construction Intelligence Framework Network (HCIF-Net) of intelligent construction risk management through combining the BIM data with predictive analytics and hybrid optimization methods. Several heterogeneous datasets, such as records of construction project management, project reports, and IFC-based BIM structural data, are incorporated to form a unified dataset of 1300 project instances. SMOTE balancing is used after the preprocessing and feature engineering to deal with class imbalance in risk categories. The HCIF-Net framework integrates TabNet to learn tables, Bayesian Network to model probabilistic risks dependencies, Temporal Attention LSTM to predict sequential progress, and Monte Carlo simulation to analyse uncertainty. A hybrid MILP-NSGA-II optimization model is employed to reduce the project cost, the time taken to complete, and the total risk to aid decision-making. The results of the experiment show a high level of predictive performance with an accuracy of 98.88% and close-to-perfect ROC-AUC metrics, and BIM-based visualization dashboards offer intuitive insights to monitor and manage construction risk efficiently.Keywords: Building information modelling, Construction risk management, Predictive modelling, Hybrid optimization, Bayesian networksSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Construction projects operate in highly dynamic environments characterized by uncertain schedules, fluctuating costs, resource constraints, complex stakeholder interactions, and evolving design requirements. These factors frequently lead to project risks such as schedule delays, cost overruns, safety incidents, design conflicts, and inefficient resource utilization1,2. Traditional construction risk management practices predominantly rely on expert judgment, manual documentation, and periodic assessments, which often result in reactive decision-making and limited capability for early risk identification3,4.

Recent advances in digital technologies have created new opportunities for data-driven construction management. Building Information Modelling (BIM) has emerged as a key technology in the architecture, engineering, and construction (AEC) industry by enabling the development of information-rich digital representations of construction assets throughout their lifecycle5,6. BIM facilitates improved visualization, interdisciplinary collaboration, clash detection, and project coordination, thereby enhancing planning accuracy and reducing project uncertainties7. Furthermore, BIM-generated data provide valuable contextual information that can support advanced analytics for construction risk assessment.

In parallel, machine learning and predictive analytics have demonstrated considerable potential for identifying hidden patterns in historical project data and forecasting construction risks related to cost, schedule, safety, and resource allocation5,8. Optimization techniques such as Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) and evolutionary algorithms have also been widely adopted to improve resource allocation and project scheduling under multiple constraints9,10. However, most existing studies focus on isolated aspects of construction management, including either BIM-based visualization, predictive modelling, or optimization independently.

Despite significant progress, several limitations remain. First, existing BIM-based risk management approaches often use BIM primarily as a visualization tool rather than as a source of quantitative features for predictive modelling11. Second, many machine learning studies emphasize risk prediction accuracy but do not translate predictive outputs into actionable construction planning decisions12. Third, optimization approaches frequently rely on deterministic assumptions and rarely incorporate uncertainty-aware risk predictions into resource allocation and scheduling decisions13. Consequently, there remains a lack of integrated frameworks that combine BIM-based information, predictive risk analytics, uncertainty quantification, and multi-objective optimization within a unified decision-support environment.

To address these limitations, this study proposes a HCIF-Net for intelligent construction risk management. The framework integrates heterogeneous construction datasets with structural and geometric indicators extracted from Industry Foundation Classes (IFC) BIM models. HCIF-Net combines TabNet-based feature learning, Bayesian Network modelling, Temporal Attention Long Short-Term Memory (TA-LSTM) sequence analysis, and Monte Carlo simulation to predict construction risks and quantify uncertainty. The predictive outputs are subsequently incorporated into a hybrid optimization module that combines MILP and the NSGA-II to generate resource allocation and scheduling strategies that simultaneously minimize project cost, duration, and risk.

It is important to clarify that the BIM component in the proposed framework focuses on extracting project-level structural indicators from IFC4.3 models, including element counts, element density, beam-to-column ratio, and structural complexity indices. These BIM-based features are incorporated into the predictive modelling process as contextual variables. Full task-level 4D (schedule-linked) and 5D (cost-linked) BIM integration is beyond the scope of the current study and is identified as an important direction for future research.

Accordingly, the primary objective of this study is not to identify or catalogue construction risks, but rather to develop and validate an integrated BIM-based predictive and optimization framework capable of transforming heterogeneous construction data into actionable, risk-informed project execution strategies.

The proposed framework is evaluated using a unified dataset comprising construction project records, project management information, and IFC-based BIM structural features. Experimental results demonstrate that HCIF-Net achieves high predictive accuracy while enabling measurable improvements in project planning through risk-aware optimization and BIM-assisted decision support.

Literature review

Construction projects are inherently characterized by significant uncertainty arising from schedule delays, cost overruns, resource shortages, design modifications, safety hazards, and stakeholder-related risks. Recent advances in Building Information Modelling (BIM), Artificial Intelligence (AI), Digital Twins, probabilistic modelling, and optimization techniques have created new opportunities for proactive and data-driven construction risk management. Despite these developments, most existing studies focus on individual aspects of the risk management process, resulting in fragmented solutions that lack an integrated framework capable of combining predictive analytics with prescriptive decision support. Consequently, there remains a need for comprehensive approaches that simultaneously incorporate heterogeneous project data, intelligent risk prediction, uncertainty quantification, and optimization to support informed decision-making throughout the construction project lifecycle.

BIM-based construction risk management

Building Information Modelling has become a fundamental technology for improving project planning, visualization, coordination, and risk identification throughout the construction lifecycle. Othman and Alamoudy14 demonstrated that integrating BIM with risk management during the design phase improves project performance and facilitates early risk mitigation. Similarly, Moshtaghian and Noorzai6 proposed a BIM-integrated risk management framework that enhances risk visibility and stakeholder collaboration.

Several researchers have explored BIM-supported risk assessment methodologies. Aladayleh and Aladaileh1 applied the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) within a BIM environment to prioritize project risks and improve decision-making. Zou et al.12 combined BIM, GIS, and AHP techniques to develop a spatially aware construction safety assessment framework capable of visualizing safety risks in complex construction environments. Although these approaches improve risk identification, they remain highly dependent on expert-defined criteria and subjective weighting mechanisms.

Numan2 reviewed BIM-based risk management strategies and concluded that BIM significantly improves risk communication and information sharing among project stakeholders. Salzano et al.4 further demonstrated that BIM integration enhances both construction safety and operational efficiency through improved visualization and coordination mechanisms. Likewise, Mi and Li7 reported that BIM-based collaboration improves project communication, resource management, and schedule control.

Recent studies have expanded BIM applications toward lifecycle-oriented construction management. Baracho et al.15 developed a BIM planning framework integrating Revit, AutoCAD, Excel, and Microsoft Project to improve scheduling accuracy and project visualization. Toochukwu16 highlighted BIM’s role in supporting construction planning, execution, facility management, and lifecycle monitoring. Das et al.17 demonstrated through multiple case studies that BIM implementation can reduce project duration by approximately 20% and project cost by nearly 15%.

Although BIM substantially improves project visualization and coordination, most BIM-based risk management frameworks remain descriptive and monitoring-oriented. They provide limited capability for predictive risk intelligence, uncertainty quantification, and optimization-driven decision support.

BIM risk analysis, safety management, and decision support

Several studies have investigated BIM for construction safety and project risk analysis. Rehman8 conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrating the effectiveness of BIM in sustainable bridge risk management. Ahmad et al.10 integrated BIM with Life Cycle Sustainability Assessment (LCSA) to optimize sustainability-related project risks. Tabejamaat et al.11 combined BIM, knowledge management, and sustainability practices to improve productivity and project risk control.

Research has also examined BIM implementation risks themselves. Dogonyaro and Elnokaly5 investigated techno-organizational barriers affecting BIM adoption and proposed mitigation strategies. In a subsequent study, Dogonyaro and Elnokaly5 introduced a legal-contractual BIM risk breakdown structure to address organizational and contractual uncertainties associated with BIM deployment. Piniano and Iwanami18 identified implementation barriers in construction safety management, highlighting resistance to organizational change, inadequate training, and interoperability challenges.

Druzhinin et al.9 proposed a risk-oriented BIM management framework emphasizing project optimization under uncertainty. Xu et al.19 later developed a system dynamics model for analyzing BIM technology adoption risks within small and medium-sized construction enterprises. Dakel20 extended BIM-based risk assessment techniques specifically for bridge construction projects. While these studies strengthen BIM-supported risk management, they mainly focus on qualitative assessment and organizational considerations rather than predictive analytics and optimization.

Artificial intelligence and machine learning for construction risk prediction

Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning have emerged as promising approaches for identifying complex risk patterns in construction data. Khodabakhshian21 demonstrated that machine learning models can effectively predict project risks using historical construction datasets. Yang et al.22 employed deep learning algorithms for real-time underground construction risk prediction and reported improved forecasting accuracy compared with traditional statistical approaches.

Carizo23 investigated BIM-AI integration for construction risk prediction and showed that machine learning models can improve early risk identification using BIM-related project information. Hou24 proposed a building lifecycle risk assessment and decision-support framework that integrates AI techniques for evaluating risks throughout project execution.

Despite their predictive capabilities, many machine learning studies focus primarily on classification accuracy and often neglect uncertainty quantification, causal dependency modelling, and optimization-based decision-making. Furthermore, most existing AI frameworks operate independently of BIM-based structural information, limiting their contextual understanding of project complexity.

Bayesian networks and probabilistic risk modelling

Bayesian Networks have attracted significant attention because of their ability to model uncertainty and causal relationships among project risks. Deng et al.25 integrated BIM with Dynamic Bayesian Networks to visualize tunneling risks and support probabilistic risk assessment. Chen et al.26 proposed a Bayesian Monte Carlo simulation framework for construction schedule risk inference and demonstrated improved probabilistic forecasting performance.

More recent studies have further explored Bayesian reasoning in construction decision-making. TJW et al.27 integrated BIM and Bayesian Networks for go/no-go decision support in demolition projects. Nyqvist et al.28 introduced uncertainty network modelling for construction risk management, highlighting the importance of probabilistic dependency analysis among project variables.

Although Bayesian approaches improve uncertainty representation and causal inference, most existing studies focus on specific applications such as schedule forecasting, safety assessment, or project feasibility analysis. Limited research has integrated Bayesian inference with deep learning, BIM feature extraction, and optimization-driven decision support.

Digital twins, real-time monitoring, and intelligent construction systems

Digital Twin technologies have recently emerged as advanced platforms for real-time construction monitoring and risk management. Liu et al.29 proposed a digital twin-based intelligent safety prediction framework for prefabricated construction hoisting operations. Their study demonstrated the potential of dynamic project representations for proactive safety management.

Saif et al.30 developed a Digital Twin framework combining wearable sensors and 4D BIM to support real-time safety monitoring on construction sites. While effective for hazard detection, the approach requires extensive sensing infrastructure and primarily supports monitoring rather than predictive optimization.

Recent developments have expanded BIM-based Digital Twins toward autonomous construction systems. Mojtahedi and Akhavian31 proposed BIM-discrepancy-driven active sensing for risk-aware UAV-UGV navigation. Akhavian et al.32 introduced BIM2RDT, a BIM-to-Robot-Ready Digital Twin framework that transforms BIM models into robot-operable digital environments. Amani and Akhavian33 further developed a Bayesian BIM-guided robotic navigation system incorporating NLP-based safety prompts.

These studies demonstrate the growing role of BIM-based Digital Twins and intelligent agents in construction management. However, their primary focus remains on operational monitoring and autonomous navigation rather than project-level risk prediction and optimization.

Uncertainty analysis and Monte Carlo-based risk assessment

Construction projects inherently involve uncertainty arising from changing resource availability, schedule disruptions, weather conditions, design modifications, and stakeholder decisions. Monte Carlo simulation has been widely used to quantify these uncertainties.

Chen et al.26 combined Bayesian inference and Monte Carlo simulation for schedule risk estimation, demonstrating improved probabilistic forecasting accuracy. Khoshkonesh et al.34 proposed a Bayesian-Monte Carlo schedule updating framework for construction digital twins that dynamically updates project forecasts under uncertainty.

Although uncertainty analysis improves confidence estimation and risk quantification, most studies treat uncertainty assessment as an isolated component rather than integrating it into an end-to-end predictive and optimization framework.

Optimization and multi-objective decision support in construction management

Optimization methods have been extensively applied to construction planning and resource management. Iriyanto et al.3 emphasized the importance of optimization techniques in balancing project time, cost, and quality objectives. Ahmadi and Arashpour35 analysed 4D-BIM planning strategies and highlighted optimization opportunities for improving project scheduling and resource utilization.

Research has also explored BIM-supported optimization for risk control and schedule management. The study on engineering project risk management using BIM technology36 demonstrated the effectiveness of BIM-based risk visualization and planning. Similarly, BIM-based construction period optimization methods26,37 improved scheduling performance through BIM-supported simulation.

However, many existing optimization studies rely on single-objective formulations that minimize either cost or time. Such approaches fail to capture the conflicting nature of construction objectives involving cost, schedule, resource utilization, and risk simultaneously. Moreover, optimization is often disconnected from predictive risk modelling, limiting its ability to support risk-aware decision-making.

Critical analysis of existing research and identified research gap

The literature reveals substantial progress in BIM-enabled construction management, AI-based risk prediction, Bayesian uncertainty modelling, Digital Twins, and optimization techniques. Nevertheless, several important limitations remain.

First, BIM-based frameworks predominantly focus on visualization, coordination, and qualitative risk prediction1,6,12,14, providing limited predictive intelligence. Second, machine learning approaches improve prediction accuracy21–23 but generally ignore uncertainty propagation and causal dependency modelling. Third, Bayesian and Monte Carlo methods effectively quantify uncertainty25–28,34 but are rarely integrated with advanced deep learning architectures and BIM-based structural information. Fourth, Digital Twin studies primarily support monitoring and operational awareness29–33 rather than predictive and optimization-driven decision-making. Finally, existing optimization frameworks often treat cost, time, and risk independently3,35–37, limiting their practical applicability in complex construction environments.

Therefore, despite significant advances, the literature lacks a unified framework capable of integrating BIM feature extraction, machine learning prediction, Bayesian dependency modelling, uncertainty quantification, and multi-objective optimization within a single construction risk management pipeline.

To address these limitations, this study proposes the HCIF-Net, an integrated BIM-AI-driven framework for proactive construction risk management. Unlike existing studies that focus on isolated components of risk management, HCIF-Net combines IFC-based BIM feature extraction, TabNet feature learning, Bayesian Network dependency modelling, Temporal Attention LSTM sequence analysis, Monte Carlo uncertainty simulation, MILP resource allocation optimization, and NSGA-II multi-objective decision optimization within a unified framework.

The proposed framework transforms construction risk management from a reactive monitoring process into a predictive and prescriptive decision-support system. By simultaneously integrating BIM-based structural intelligence, probabilistic risk reasoning, uncertainty quantification, and optimization-based planning, HCIF-Net advances the current state of research toward intelligent construction management capable of balancing project cost, duration, resource utilization, and risk exposure.

Research gaps and motivation of the proposed HCIF-Net framework

Recent developments in BIM, AI, digital twins, and machine learning have significantly improved construction risk management capabilities. However, existing approaches still exhibit limitations in achieving a complete predictive and optimization-oriented construction risk management framework. Traditional BIM-based risk management studies have mainly focused on visualization, safety assessment, design coordination, and collaborative planning. For example, BIM-GIS-AHP-based methods improve spatial risk identification and safety evaluation by combining BIM information with expert-based assessment techniques; however, these approaches depend on predefined risk indicators and expert knowledge, limiting their ability to automatically learn complex risk patterns from heterogeneous construction data12. Similarly, BIM-based collaborative risk management frameworks improve communication and early risk identification among stakeholders, but their effectiveness depends on BIM data quality, interoperability, and organizational readiness36.

Recent digital twin-based studies have attempted to enhance BIM capabilities by enabling real-time monitoring and intelligent risk prediction. Liu et al.29 proposed a digital twin-based intelligent safety risk prediction framework for prefabricated construction hoisting activities. The study demonstrated the capability of digital twins to improve proactive safety management by integrating dynamic project information with prediction models. However, the framework mainly concentrates on safety risks associated with a specific construction activity and does not consider broader project-level uncertainties such as cost deviation, schedule delay, resource constraints, and overall risk optimization. Similarly, Saif et al.30 developed a digital twin system combining wearable sensors and 4D BIM for real-time construction safety monitoring. Although the framework enables continuous hazard detection, it requires extensive sensing infrastructure and primarily supports monitoring rather than predictive planning and optimization decisions.

The integration of BIM with artificial intelligence has further expanded the potential for automated construction risk prediction. Carizo23 investigated BIM–AI integration for predicting construction project risks using BIM-related information and artificial intelligence techniques. Although the study demonstrates the capability of AI models in identifying potential risks, it mainly focuses on prediction outcomes without incorporating uncertainty analysis, risk dependency modelling, or optimization mechanisms for selecting the most suitable construction strategy. Therefore, current BIM–AI frameworks provide valuable prediction capabilities but remain limited in transforming predicted risks into optimized project decisions.

Machine learning and deep learning techniques have also been applied for construction risk prediction. Yang et al.22 explored deep learning-based real-time risk prediction for underground construction projects and showed that neural networks can effectively capture complex relationships among construction variables. However, such approaches are generally developed for specific project environments and lack integration with BIM-based structural information and multi-objective optimization considering cost, duration, and risk simultaneously. Hou24 proposed a lifecycle risk prediction and decision-support model for buildings; however, the framework mainly focuses on assessment and evaluation rather than providing an integrated predictive–prescriptive mechanism for construction execution planning.

Furthermore, existing studies generally treat BIM modelling, risk prediction, uncertainty evaluation, and project optimization as separate processes. Digital twin approaches mainly provide real-time monitoring, BIM-based methods primarily support visualization and coordination, and machine learning models usually provide classification or prediction results. Limited research has investigated an integrated framework that connects BIM feature extraction, AI-driven risk prediction, uncertainty quantification, and optimization-based decision-making within a single construction risk management pipeline.

Unlike conventional BIM-based risk management approaches that mainly visualize or monitor project conditions, HCIF-Net extracts structural and geometric indicators from IFC4.3 models, including element counts, element density, beam-to-column ratio, and structural complexity index, and incorporates them as contextual inputs for risk prediction.

The proposed framework further integrates TabNet for advanced feature representation, Bayesian Network modelling for identifying probabilistic relationships among risk factors, Temporal Attention LSTM for analyzing sequential project behaviour, and Monte Carlo simulation for uncertainty assessment. Proposed methodology

The proposed methodology introduces a smart framework of BIM-based construction risk management that integrates data-driven analytics, predictive modelling, and optimization methods to enhance decision-making in a project. In the first step, heterogeneous data that contains project management reports, construction planning attributes and BIM structural data are gathered and merged to create a single dataset. The data is then preprocessed to eliminate inconsistencies, missing values, and normalize features and then feature engineered to extract schedule, cost, risk, and BIM-based structural indicators. The augmented data is fed into the HCIF-Net, a combination of TabNet to learn the tabular data, Bayesian Networks to reason about risk probabilities, Temporal Attention LSTM to forecast sequential progress, and Monte Carlo simulation to analyse the uncertainty. A hybrid MILP-NSGA-II formulation is further used to optimize the predicted results to identify optimal resource allocation to minimize project cost, duration and risk. Lastly, the risk response plans are developed and represented as a visualization of BIM-based dashboards and risk heatmaps, which allow monitoring the spatial and temporal risks in the construction to facilitate construction project management and decision support.

Data sources and dataset integration

The proposed framework utilizes three heterogeneous construction-related datasets to represent different aspects of construction project management, including operational activities, resource planning, and BIM-based structural characteristics. The first dataset, Construction and Project Management Report Examples38 obtained from Kaggle, contains project documentation records such as daily site reports, safety inspection records, quality checklists, and task progress information. These records represent typical construction management activities and provide operational indicators related to site execution and monitoring. The second dataset, Construction Project Management Dataset39, provides task-level project planning attributes, including task duration, labor requirement, equipment usage, material cost, task dependency information, and risk-related attributes. Each record in this dataset represents an individual construction activity or task with associated cost, time, resource, and risk characteristics. The third dataset, BIM-AI Integrated Dataset40, contains BIM-based structural information extracted from IFC-based building models, including structural element counts (beams, columns, slabs), geometric properties, solids, curves, and complexity-related indicators.

The datasets were selected to provide complementary views of construction projects. The project management dataset contributes task execution and resource-related variables, the project report dataset provides management and monitoring attributes, and the BIM dataset introduces structural complexity information. The integration process does not represent direct project-to-project merging because the datasets originate from different public repositories. Instead, the datasets were transformed into a common task-oriented representation by aligning equivalent construction activity attributes and generating a unified feature space. The integration procedure consisted of three stages: (i) identification of common construction-related attributes such as task information, duration, cost, labor, and risk indicators, (ii) feature harmonization and transformation of heterogeneous variables into a consistent format, and (iii) concatenation of the aligned records to construct the final analytical dataset.

Integrated dataset structure and risk level distribution.

The original integrated dataset was constructed by combining the three heterogeneous construction datasets and contained 1,300 construction activity records with 37 initial attributes. At this stage, the target variable Risk Level showed an imbalanced distribution of 660 Low-risk, 377 Medium-risk, and 263 High-risk records. The dataset rebuild step refers to the preprocessing and feature transformation process, where the integrated records were cleaned, harmonized, categorical variables were encoded, numerical variables were normalized, and BIM-based structural indicators were incorporated to generate the final feature representation used for modelling. After this rebuilding process, the dataset was divided into training and testing subsets using an 80:20 split, resulting in 1,040 training records and 260 testing records. The SMOTE balancing procedure was then applied only to the training dataset to overcome class imbalance, generating synthetic samples for minority classes and producing a balanced training dataset containing 528 samples for each risk category (total 1,584 records). The test dataset remained unchanged with its original distribution (132 Low-risk, 75 Medium-risk, and 53 High-risk records) to ensure unbiased evaluation. Therefore, the class distributions reported in different sections represent different processing stages: the original dataset distribution before preprocessing, the rebuilt dataset after feature preparation, and the balanced dataset after SMOTE.

Data preprocessing

Once the input collection stage is completed, the integrated raw dataset is sent to the data preprocessing phase to improve the quality of data and eliminate inconsistencies and convert the data into a structured format that can be used in machine learning and intelligent construction analytics. Because the gathered data has a variety of different sources like project reports, management records, and BIM datasets, preprocessing is a necessary step to harmonize the data representation and enhance reliability. First, any missing values of the numerical attributes within the dataset, including task duration, labor requirement, material cost, equipment units, and BIM complexity, are determined and imputed by applying statistical imputation techniques, including mean or median values, and categorical missing values imputed by mode values, respectively. Duplicate observations that occur as a result of merging datasets are eliminated to prevent repeated observations that can influence prediction outcomes.

Risk Level, Status, and task categories are then transformed into numerical values through the use of one-hot encoding to enable their use by machine learning algorithms. Once the features are encoded, numerical features are standardized (scaled) using scaling techniques like Min–Max normalization so that all the variables are in the same range and large-valued features do not take over smaller-valued ones. Moreover, consistency validation is conducted in BIM structural features, risk information, and site management records to verify proper mapping of project entities and structural components. This encompasses task identification, project identification, location identification, and attributes of BIM objects. Consequently, the preprocessing phase results in a clean, normalized and analysis-ready data that enhances prediction accuracy and computational efficiency.

Predictive modelling using hybrid construction intelligence framework

Following the SMOTE, the enhanced data is feed into the predictive modelling phase where predictive modelling is performed to forecast the project delays, cost overruns, risk escalation and uncertain construction outcomes. Because construction project data is heterogeneous in terms of numerical, categorical, BIM-based, and management-related attributes, there is a possibility that not all latent dependencies can be well represented by one predictive model. Thus, this research presents a new hybrid predictive architecture called the HCIF-Net.

The HCIF-Net is a hybrid of TabNet (deep tabular learning), Bayesian Network (causal risk dependency modelling), Monte Carlo Simulation (forecasting uncertainty), and Temporal Attention LSTM (sequential progress trend analysis). This combined model enhances prediction accuracy, interpretability, robustness and novelty.

TabNet for tabular construction learning

TabNet is utilized as the main deep learning model to process the obtained structured tabular data at the stage of the feature engineering. It is specially tailored to data sets with both numeric and non-numerical data (categorical data), which is why it is very applicable to construction project management data. The model uses a sequential attention mechanism to automatically select the most relevant features at each decision step instead of processing all variables equally. This enables the network to concentrate on high-impact indicators like cost index, overdue ratio, BIM structural complexity, labor availability, equipment usage, and risk indicators. TabNet learns the underlying nonlinear relationships between project variables and produces high-quality predictive representations by using several decision stages. Its inherent interpretability can also be used to determine what features are most likely to cause project delays, cost increases or project performance.

Bayesian network for risk dependency reasoning

The Bayesian Network is addressed to capture the probabilistic interdependence of risk factors in construction, because the risks in projects are rarely independent. In practice, problems like labor shortages, material delays, dependency conflicts, budget constraints and schedule slippage tend to impact each other in real construction environments. The Bayesian Network represents these conditional dependencies by a directed probabilistic network and this allows the system to perceive how one risk can cause or increase another. With input evidence in the form of the engineered dataset, the model predicts posterior risk probabilities given existing conditions in the project. This enables critical thinking in the face of uncertainty and detecting concealed risk transmission patterns before they become critical. Subsequently, the model facilitates proactive decision-making, generation of early warnings, and more dependable project risk management.

Temporal attention LSTM for progress forecasting

To enhance time-based forecasting, a Temporal Attention LSTM model is incorporated in the predictive framework. Sequential data is created during construction projects; these include weekly progress reports, cumulative expenditure, history of issues resolved, productivity trends, and report updates. The LSTM module performs well in learning long-term time-dependences of such sequential records and enables the model to learn how the previous project behaviour affects future results. To emphasize the most relevant time steps, an attention mechanism is included whereby all the historical updates are not given the same weight. This helps the model to prioritize on the critical events like delayed explosions, cost rise speed, or frequent unresolved problems. Consequently, the Temporal Attention LSTM is able to capture dynamic project changes with time and offer more precise forecasting of project progress state, delays and future performance trajectory.

Optimization stage

The optimization stage converts the predictive outputs of HCIF-Net into practical construction planning decisions. The objective of this stage is not only to predict construction risks but also to determine the most suitable execution strategy by optimizing task selection, resource allocation, project duration, and risk exposure. The proposed optimization framework integrates Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) and Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II). MILP is first applied to generate feasible resource allocation solutions under construction constraints, while NSGA-II further optimizes the obtained solutions by considering multiple conflicting objectives, including cost, completion time, and risk.

Risk response planning

Risk Response Planning stage converts the optimal construction plan obtained through the hybrid MILP-NSGA-II framework into real risk management measures. The probability, impact and sensitivity of each construction risk identified are determined with the aid of BIM to calculate the total risk exposure that measures the extent of the risk. The value of exposure is then categorized into the relevant categories of responses, including avoidance, mitigation, transfer or acceptance which enable the decision-makers to choose the appropriate response strategies. All the responses are arranged in a structured Risk Response Register that captures the risk, its type, response plan, necessary resources and the authority in charge. The allocation of resources towards mitigation is further modified in terms of the calculated risk exposure as well as the optimized resources acquired through the optimization framework. Lastly, the optimized construction strategy is revised by adding the summed-up risks response actions to generate a risk-adjusted execution plan that facilitates efficient and successful project management. In this study, BIM integration is implemented as a BIM-informed risk intelligence layer rather than a complete 4D/5D BIM environment. The proposed HCIF-Net framework does not directly utilize IFC-linked schedule objects (4D BIM) or IFC-linked cost objects (5D BIM). Instead, IFC4.3-based BIM models are processed using the ifcopenshell library to extract structural and geometric feature indicators, including the number of beams, columns, slabs, architectural elements, element density, beam-to-column ratio, and structural complexity index. These BIM-based indicators are integrated with construction management datasets containing cost, schedule, and resource information to improve risk prediction and optimization. Furthermore, spatial relationships from BIM components are utilized through BIM-based sensitivity analysis and risk heatmap generation, enabling the mapping of predicted risks to specific project elements. Therefore, the BIM contribution in HCIF-Net lies in structural feature extraction, spatial risk interpretation, and visualization-based decision support rather than direct 4D/5D schedule-cost simulation.

Experimental setup

The proposed HCIF-Net framework along with MILP and NSGA-II optimization is implemented in a Python environment. Pandas, NumPy, Scikit-learn are used to perform the data processing and feature engineering, and the ifcopenshell library is used to perform the BIM feature extraction. PyTorch (TabNet and LSTM) and pgmpy (Bayesian Network modelling) are used to develop the predictive models and implement the models, respectively. MILP is formulated with PuLP and NSGA-II is implemented with DEAP library in order to maximize the results. All the experiments are performed on a standard computing system to measure the accuracy of predictions, convergence, and optimization efficiency.

Figure 10 summarizes the complete data processing workflow adopted in this study and clarifies the relationship between the original datasets, integrated dataset, balanced training data, and final model evaluation. The proposed HCIF-Net framework integrates three heterogeneous construction datasets: Dataset 1 containing construction project information (1,300 records with 10 attributes), Dataset 2 containing form-based project records (10,254 records with 17 attributes), and Dataset 3 containing BIM/IFC structural information (45 records with 15 attributes). These datasets are integrated by aligning relevant construction-related attributes, extracting meaningful BIM-based structural features, and consolidating the information into a unified dataset of 1,300 construction project instances with 37 initial attributes. In this study, one record represents a single construction project risk prediction instance, where each sample contains project-level information related to schedule performance, cost factors, resource utilization, BIM characteristics, and risk-related indicators, with the associated risk category as the prediction target. After preprocessing and feature engineering, including data cleaning, transformation, encoding, normalization, and generation of construction-specific indicators, the integrated dataset was expanded into a feature representation containing 59 attributes used for predictive modelling. The original dataset exhibited class imbalance, consisting of 660 Low-risk (50.8%), 377 Medium-risk (29.0%), and 263 High-risk (20.2%) samples. To ensure unbiased evaluation and prevent data leakage, the processed dataset was divided into training (80%, 1,040 records) and testing (20%, 260 records) subsets before balancing. The testing dataset remained unchanged throughout the entire process and was used only for final model evaluation. SMOTE was applied exclusively to the training dataset to balance the risk categories, generating 528 samples for each class and producing a final balanced training dataset of 1,584 records. The balanced training data were provided as input to HCIF-Net, where the hybrid architecture performed feature learning, risk dependency analysis, temporal prediction, and uncertainty estimation. The final classification and optimization results were obtained from this processed pipeline, while performance metrics were calculated using the independent unseen test dataset, ensuring that the reported results represent the actual predictive capability and generalisation performance of the proposed framework.

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that the proposed HCIF-Net provides a promising framework for construction risk prediction and decision support by integrating heterogeneous construction data, BIM-based information, predictive modelling, and optimization techniques. The primary contribution of the proposed framework extends beyond achieving high predictive performance by establishing a comprehensive risk management pipeline that connects data integration, risk prediction, uncertainty analysis, and project optimization. Unlike conventional construction risk prediction approaches, which often rely on historical project indicators or isolated machine learning models, HCIF-Net integrates multiple dimensions of construction uncertainty, including cost-related factors, schedule characteristics, resource utilization, and BIM-based structural information. This integrated perspective provides a more comprehensive representation of the factors associated with construction risk and supports informed decision-making throughout the project lifecycle.

The reliability of the reported results was further supported through additional validation experiments. The predictive performance was evaluated using an independent testing dataset that was separated prior to SMOTE-based balancing and model optimization. Consequently, the test samples were not exposed to synthetic data generation or model training procedures, thereby reducing the possibility of data leakage. The relatively small difference between training and testing performance suggests that the proposed framework maintains consistent predictive capability when applied to previously unseen samples. Furthermore, five-fold stratified cross-validation demonstrated low variation across different data partitions, indicating stable model behaviour that was not dependent on a particular train-test split. Collectively, these findings suggest that the reported predictive performance is associated with the framework’s ability to capture meaningful construction risk patterns rather than simply memorizing the training data.

The strong predictive performance achieved by HCIF-Net may be attributed to the integration of multiple complementary learning components. Construction risk is governed by complex nonlinear interactions among project variables, and a single modelling technique may not adequately capture all underlying dependencies. The TabNet component identifies informative feature interactions from heterogeneous construction attributes, while the Bayesian Network models probabilistic relationships among risk factors. The Temporal Attention LSTM module captures sequential project behaviour associated with construction progress, schedule deviations, and resource variations. In addition, Monte Carlo simulation incorporates uncertainty estimation by quantifying possible variations in predicted risk outcomes rather than producing only deterministic predictions. The combined use of feature learning, probabilistic dependency modelling, temporal analysis, and uncertainty quantification therefore provides a robust analytical foundation for construction risk prediction.

The comparison with existing predictive approaches further suggests that the proposed framework performs favourably under the evaluated experimental conditions. Conventional machine learning methods, including MLP and LightGBM, provide strong predictive capability for structured datasets but primarily model direct relationships between input features and prediction outcomes, with limited capability to explicitly represent uncertainty and complex interdependencies among construction variables. Likewise, sequential models such as Bi-LSTM effectively capture temporal patterns but may require additional feature engineering to fully exploit heterogeneous project information. By integrating complementary modelling strategies within a unified architecture, HCIF-Net demonstrates the potential benefits of combining multiple sources of information for construction risk prediction rather than relying on a single predictive algorithm.

The optimization results indicate the practical potential of transforming predictive outputs into actionable project planning decisions. The MILP optimization module generates resource allocation and task selection strategies under predefined project constraints, while the NSGA-II algorithm identifies alternative Pareto-optimal solutions by simultaneously considering conflicting objectives such as cost, project duration, and construction risk. Such multi-objective optimization reflects practical construction management scenarios, where project managers must balance financial constraints, scheduling requirements, and acceptable risk levels rather than optimizing a single objective. Consequently, the generated Pareto-optimal solutions provide flexible decision-support alternatives that can be adapted according to project-specific priorities.

The integration of BIM-based structural information further has the potential to enhance the practical applicability of the proposed framework. Whereas BIM is frequently employed primarily as a visualization platform in conventional risk management approaches, the proposed framework directly incorporates IFC-derived structural indicators into predictive modelling. The BIM-based analyses suggest that structural complexity, schedule-related characteristics, and cost intensity provide valuable complementary information for identifying potential risk-prone activities during project planning. These findings indicate that BIM-based information can support more proactive risk identification and project monitoring within the evaluated experimental setting, while also creating opportunities for future intelligent construction management systems that integrate digital building information with predictive analytics.

Although the reported results are encouraging, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the proposed framework was evaluated using heterogeneous publicly available construction datasets. While these datasets contain diverse project-related attributes, they may not fully capture the variability, operational complexity, and contextual characteristics of large-scale industrial construction projects. Differences in project location, organizational practices, construction methods, regulatory requirements, and data quality may influence the framework’s performance when deployed in real-world environments. Therefore, additional validation using external industrial datasets is required before broad generalisation of the reported results.

Second, class imbalance was addressed using SMOTE-based synthetic oversampling applied exclusively to the training data. Although this approach effectively improved class representation during model development and prevented information leakage into the testing dataset, synthetic samples may not fully reflect naturally occurring risk distributions observed in real construction projects. Consequently, future studies should further evaluate the proposed framework using larger naturally imbalanced industrial datasets to confirm its robustness under practical operating conditions.

Third, although BIM-based structural indicators were successfully incorporated into the predictive framework, the present study did not include full integration with dynamic 4D/5D BIM environments capable of continuously updating scheduling, cost, and construction progress information. Future implementations integrating real-time BIM updates, IoT-enabled sensing technologies, and site monitoring systems could further improve the responsiveness and adaptability of intelligent construction risk management.

From a practical perspective, HCIF-Net can serve as a decision-support framework for project managers, contractors, and construction engineers by supporting the early identification of potentially high-risk activities, prioritization of mitigation strategies, and evaluation of alternative project execution plans. Rather than functioning solely as a predictive classification model, the proposed framework combines prediction, uncertainty estimation, and multi-objective optimization to support more informed resource allocation and project control decisions. Furthermore, the integration of BIM-based information facilitates improved communication among project stakeholders by linking predictive risk prediction with structural and project-related information.

Overall, the findings suggest that the integration of artificial intelligence, BIM analytics, uncertainty modelling, and multi-objective optimization represents a promising direction for intelligent construction risk management. Within the evaluated experimental setting, HCIF-Net demonstrated strong predictive performance while providing an integrated framework that supports both risk prediction and practical decision-making. Nevertheless, additional validation using large-scale industrial construction projects remains necessary to further assess the generalizability of the proposed framework across diverse construction environments. Future research will therefore focus on validating HCIF-Net using real-world industrial datasets, integrating real-time IoT data streams with dynamic 4D/5D BIM environments, and investigating adaptive learning mechanisms capable of continuously updating risk predictions throughout the project lifecycle. These developments have the potential to further enhance the scalability, robustness, and practical applicability of intelligent BIM-based construction risk management systems.

Conclusion and future work

This study introduced HCIF-Net, an intelligent construction risk management framework that combines heterogeneous construction datasets, BIM-originated structural information, predictive analytics, uncertainty modelling and multiple object optimization techniques. The suggested framework integrates various construction project attributes, resource-related factors, schedule indicators, and features generated from BIM into a holistic risk management pipeline, aiding in risk prediction and informed decision-making. The experimental results show that HCIF-Net can predict well on the test sets and is stable during five-fold cross validation. Moreover, the model can incorporate complex interaction effects among the cost, schedule, resource, and structural factors, through the integration of TabNet-based feature learning, Bayesian risk dependency analysis, Temporal Attention LSTM and Monte Carlo uncertainty estimation. Furthermore, MILP in conjunction with NSGA-II offers a feasible decision support tool to examine trade-offs between project cost, duration and risk. The results also indicate that the use of BIM-based information about the structure can lead to better proactive risk identification and project monitoring in the analysed experimental setting.

There are a number of caveats to note. First, the proposed framework was tested with heterogeneous publicly available datasets, which might not completely represent the diversity and complexity of real-world construction projects. Second, class imbalance was dealt with through synthetic oversampling using SMOTE but this did not necessarily produce a risk distribution in line with the naturally occurring risk distributions. Thirdly, the features extracted from BIM were integrated into the predictive framework, but not all features of dynamic 4D/5D BIM environments and real-time project information were accounted for in this study. Thus, the results of the reported investigations should be interpreted with great care when extrapolating from one construction type to another.

In future, HCIF-Net will be validated using larger industrial scale construction datasets gathered from real construction projects, real-time data streams from IoT devices and dynamic 4D/5D BIM environments, and adaptive learning mechanisms that can be used for continuous risk prediction updates throughout the project life cycle. The intelligent construction risk management framework proposed has the potential to be further improved on the scalability, robustness and practice of the proposed framework.
