What It Takes to Reduce Radiation Dose for Coronary Artery Imaging Globally
Authors: Sung Ho Hwang
Published: 2026

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, rendering accurate and accessible non-invasive imaging increasingly crucial for its diagnosis and management [12]. Among available modalities, coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) has become established as a fundamental diagnostic tool for evaluating patients with suspected CAD [34]. The International Atomic Energy Agency Noninvasive Cardiology Protocols Study (INCAPS) 4, encompassing 19,302 patients across 101 countries, revealed substantial global disparities in radiation exposure associated with CCTA [5]. In that analysis, radiation doses for CCTA performed in low- and lower-middle-income countries were approximately 96% higher on average than those observed in high-income countries. Notably, the median radiation dose for CCTA in Africa is 25.2 mSv, which is more than 5 times higher than the 4.6 mSv reported in Western Europe [5]. These findings highlight significant disparities in radiation safety during CCTA examinations spanning regions and countries with varying economic levels. These observations raise an important question: What underlying factors account for these disparities in radiation dose beyond differences in economic level?

GAPS IN EQUIPMENT AND OPERATION CONTRIBUTE TO DOSE VARIATION

The INCAPS 4 study indicates that differences in CCTA radiation doses are influenced by national economic conditions and technological factors. The use of newer-generation CT scanners, including systems with wider z-axis detector coverage or dual-source capability, is associated with a >22% reduction in radiation dose compared with older systems. These advanced scanners provide greater temporal resolution and wider detector coverage, enabling shorter scan times and more efficient electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated acquisition [67]. Even after adjusting for differences in scanner technology, CCTA radiation doses in low-income countries remained approximately 69% higher than those in high-income countries, suggesting that disparities cannot be explained solely by differences in equipment performance. Degree of protocol optimization and level of operator expertise in utilizing these technologies also appear to play crucial roles in determining CT radiation dose [8]. Minimizing radiation exposure in CCTA requires implementation of several optimization strategies, including low-tube-voltage imaging, prospective ECG-triggered acquisition, and active use of advanced iterative reconstruction algorithms [6910]. Careful patient preparation, including heart rate control and protocol optimization (including appropriate scan range selection and tube current modulation) are essential for achieving low-dose CCTA imaging [69]. Recently, deep learning image reconstruction (DLIR) has emerged as an advanced image processing technique that enables dose reduction beyond conventional iterative reconstruction. Studies have demonstrated that DLIR enables radiation dose reductions of 40%–60% in CCTA without compromising diagnostic image quality or stenosis and plaque assessment accuracy [1112]. These technical approaches are effective only when modern CT scanners’ capabilities are combined with appropriate protocol selection and clinical operations.

STRATEGIES FOR SUCCESSFUL DOSE OPTIMIZATION

Reducing global disparities in radiation exposure requires establishment of practical optimization targets and comparative frameworks that can be implemented in routine clinical practice. Current international guidelines recommend maintaining CCTA radiation dose below 9 mSv [6]. However, the INCAPS 4 study indicates that only approximately 56% of centers worldwide achieve this benchmark. A more stringent target of 5 mSv or lower is currently achieved by approximately 37% of centers. In this context, benchmarking systems that allow institutions to compare their radiation levels with the global distribution may serve as important tools for improvement [8]. This approach enables meaningful assessment of dose performance and facilitates quality improvement without imposing rigid regulatory thresholds. In the PROTECTION registry conducted in Europe, CCTA protocol standardization resulted in a substantial reduction in average radiation dose from approximately 12 to 3–5 mSv through broader implementation of optimization strategies [10]. Several countries have implemented national CT dose registries and diagnostic reference levels using periodic dose comparisons and feedback mechanisms to reduce inter-institutional variability and promote continuous quality improvement [13]. These results demonstrate that effective radiation dose management can be improved substantially through data-driven benchmarking, targeted education, and collaborative quality assurance frameworks.

CONCLUSION

While CCTA is a highly valuable diagnostic modality for evaluating CAD, it is technically complex and involves exposure to ionizing radiation. As CCTA use continues to expand globally, substantial variations in radiation doses have been observed across regions and institutions. Such disparities are closely related to differences in imaging technology and variations in acquisition protocols and operational practices. Ultimately, narrowing global disparities in CCTA radiation doses will require coordinated efforts that combine technological advancements, continuous professional education, and collaborative quality management systems including benchmarking systems and dose registries.
