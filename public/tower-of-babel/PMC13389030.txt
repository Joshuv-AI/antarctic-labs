Feasibility and associated factors of same-day discharge after hysteroscopic-laparoscopic surgery for infertility under ERAS: a prospective observational study
Authors: Jie Yu, Aijie Xie, Yaojie Li, Qinyan Cao, Ling Zhang, Xian Wu, Yunyi Su, Xin Li, Xiaoqin Gan, Yujian Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the increasing adoption of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols, same-day discharge after hysteroscopic-laparoscopic surgery for infertility remains underexplored, particularly in terms of its clinical implementation and influencing factors. This prospective observational study included 104 infertile women who underwent combined hysteroscopic-laparoscopic surgery. All patients received standardized ERAS care and were categorized into same-day and non-same-day discharge groups by actual discharge time. Same-day discharge was achieved in 51 of the 104 patients (49.0%). All non-same-day discharged patients met the objective discharge criteria, and the between-group difference did not reflect poorer postoperative recovery. Postoperative complication rates were low and comparable between groups (3.92% vs. 5.66%), with no unplanned reoperations or 30-day readmissions. Factors independently associated with same-day discharge were a residential distance > 30 km (OR 0.108; 95% CI 0.036–0.325; P < 0.001), a surgical end before 15:00 (OR 7.862; 95% CI 2.609–23.694; P < 0.001), and prior abdominal surgery (OR 6.501; 95% CI 1.796–23.535; P = 0.004). Introduction

Infertility affects 12.6–17.5% of women of reproductive age and represents a substantial medical and psychosocial burden1,2. Hysteroscopy combined with laparoscopy is widely used for the diagnosis and treatment of infertility. It offers the advantages of minimal invasiveness and rapid recovery, making it suitable for same-day discharge3.

Evidence suggests that same-day discharge can shorten hospital stays and improve patient recovery experiences without apparent increases in postoperative complications or readmission rates4–6. To optimize perioperative care, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols have been introduced into gynecologic practice. These standardized, multimodal strategies—including preoperative education, optimized analgesia, and early oral intake and mobilization—have been shown to facilitate postoperative recovery and support the implementation of same-day discharge7–9.

Despite the adoption of ERAS pathways, variability in discharge outcomes persists in clinical practice. Some patients achieve same-day discharge, while others require overnight observation. Previous studies have suggested that factors such as age, intraoperative blood loss, and American Society of Anesthesiologists classification may influence discharge timing10–12. However, prospective data on the implementation of same-day discharge and the perioperative factors associated with its success in infertile patients who undergo combined hysteroscopic-laparoscopic surgery remain limited.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility of same-day discharge among infertile women who underwent hysteroscopic–laparoscopic surgery within an ERAS framework and to identify the perioperative factors associated with successful discharge.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted in the Gynecology Day Surgery Unit of Chengdu Women’s and Children’s Central Hospital between June 2024 and May 2025. All eligible patients during this period were consecutively enrolled. The study was embedded within the Gynecologic Postoperative Follow-up Cohort Study (Chinese Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR2200059282) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chengdu Women’s and Children’s Central Hospital (No. 2023-95). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patients were allocated to two groups based on actual discharge time: a same-day discharge group and a non-same-day discharge group. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) female patients aged 18–45 years; (2) infertility with clear indications for hysteroscopic and laparoscopic surgery; (3) patients evaluated by both gynecologists and anesthesiologists as suitable for day surgery; and (4) voluntary participation in the study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) body temperature > 37.3 °C; (2) haemoglobin concentration < 80 g/L; (3) uncontrolled medical or surgical comorbidities; (4) the presence of acute genital tract infection; (5) a history of three or more prior abdominal surgeries or severe pelvic adhesions; (6) concomitant uterine fibroids, ovarian cysts, or mesosalpinx cysts larger than 5 cm; and (7) language or cognitive barriers that prevented comprehension of ERAS instructions or completion of follow-up.

Data collection

Data were obtained from electronic medical records and the day surgery management system. Baseline variables included age, body mass index (BMI), education level, obstetric history, prior abdominal surgery (defined as any previous intra-abdominal surgical procedure, regardless of type or indication), additional surgical procedures, and residential distance. Intraoperative variables included operative time, estimated blood loss, surgical end time, the pelvic adhesion score, and intraoperative complications. Postoperative recovery variables included time to first oral intake, ambulation, urination, and flatus; pain scores; postoperative complications; 30-day unplanned reoperation or readmission; and patient satisfaction. Postoperative complications were recorded and graded according to the Clavien‒Dindo classification13. The length of hospital stay and total hospitalization costs were also recorded. Hospitalization costs included surgical fees, anesthesia fees, medication costs, laboratory tests, imaging studies, ward stays, and consumables. Indirect costs (e.g., productivity loss, caregiver time, and transportation) were not calculated.

Perioperative procedure

All patients received standardized perioperative care based on ERAS principles.

Preoperative management

Patients were initially evaluated in the gynecology outpatient clinic. Eligible patients who provided informed consent were enrolled. Preoperative assessments, including laboratory tests and anesthetic evaluation, were completed prior to scheduling surgery. Standardized preoperative education included surgical procedures, perioperative precautions, expected length of stay, costs, and ERAS principles. Routine mechanical bowel preparation and preoperative shaving were not performed. Patients were instructed to fast for 6–8 h and abstain from liquids for 2 h prior to surgery.

Intraoperative management

Prophylactic antibiotics (cefuroxime 0.75 g intravenously or clindamycin 600 mg for patients with penicillin allergy) were administered within 30 min before the incision was made. General anesthesia was induced using short-acting agents (propofol and remifentanil) to facilitate rapid recovery. All patients received prophylactic antiemetics (dexamethasone 8 mg and ondansetron 4 mg intravenously). Surgical procedures included diagnostic hysteroscopy, laparoscopic evaluation, bilateral tubal chromopertubation, diagnostic curettage, and indicated operative interventions (e.g., endometrial polypectomy, cervical polypectomy, adhesiolysis, ovarian cystectomy, myomectomy, and mesosalpinx cystectomy). At the end of surgery, 5 mL of 0.75% ropivacaine was infiltrated subcutaneously at the umbilical port site. Urinary catheters and abdominal drains were not routinely used.

Postoperative management

In-hospital care

Postoperative pain was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS, 0–10)14 and managed using a stepwise protocol: VAS 1–3, non-pharmacologic measures; VAS 4–6, oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (ibuprofen 400 mg); and VAS ≥ 7, rescue opioids (tramadol 100 mg intramuscularly). Opioid use and fluid administration were minimized. Unless clinically indicated (e.g., excessive intraoperative blood loss or operative time > 3 h), postoperative intravenous fluids were not routinely administered. Patients were encouraged to ambulate and void spontaneously within 1–2 h after surgery. Early oral intake (fluids and a light diet) was initiated once the patient’s swallowing function had recovered. No urinary catheters or surgical drains were placed postoperatively.

Discharge readiness was assessed using the Post-Anesthesia Discharge Scoring System (PADSS)15, with a required score ≥ 9. Additional criteria included tolerance of oral intake, spontaneous urination, and the presence of a responsible adult escort. The final discharge decisions were made jointly by the attending surgeon and the patient.

Post-discharge follow-up

Follow-up visits were scheduled at 1 week and 1 month post-operatively. At the 1-week visit, routine blood tests and physical examinations were performed to assess recovery and review the pathology results. At the one-month visit, imaging studies and, if necessary, additional blood tests were conducted to further objectively evaluate postoperative recovery.

Post-discharge monitoring included WeChat communication, a day surgery management system, and telephone follow-up. WeChat was used to promptly address patients’ postoperative questions, providing convenient and efficient management, alleviating their concerns. The management system delivered standardized questionnaires on postoperative Days 1, 3, 8, and 15. These questionnaires were used to assess patient-reported recovery (Supplementary File 1), including pain, fever, and wound condition. The digital questionnaire was developed by our day surgery unit based on standard postoperative monitoring protocols and has been routinely used for all patients undergoing this procedure in our department. When a participant did not complete the digital questionnaire within the designated time window, the study staff contacted the patient by telephone and completed the questionnaire on the patient’s behalf. Any abnormal events were managed according to institutional emergency protocols for post-discharge complications.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and as the median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data. Between-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Variables were selected for the multivariable model on the basis of clinical relevance and univariate screening (P < 0.10). Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with all values below 10 indicating no substantial collinearity. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with same-day discharge. All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Between June 2024 and May 2025, a total of 118 women with infertility who underwent day surgery procedures at our hospital were assessed for eligibility (Fig. 1). After excluding patients with acute genital tract infections, uncontrolled hyperthyroidism, a history of multiple abdominal surgeries, or adnexal or uterine masses larger than 5 cm, 104 patients met the inclusion criteria and were analysed. Among these patients, 51 were discharged on the day of surgery (same-day discharge group), and 53 were discharged the following morning (non-same-day discharge group). All patients met the objective discharge criteria, and the difference in discharge timing was not attributable to poorer recovery.

There were no significant differences between the same-day discharge group and the non-same-day discharge group in terms of age, BMI, education level, history of abdominal surgery, reproductive history, or the distribution of additional surgical procedures (p > 0.05). Distance from the hospital differed significantly between the groups: 10 patients (19.6%) in the same-day discharge group and 31 patients (58.5%) in the non-same-day discharge group resided more than 30 km away (p < 0.001).

Questionnaires were distributed via the day surgery management system, and any missing questionnaires were completed by telephone, resulting in a 100% response rate at each time point (postoperative days 1, 3, 8, and 15), with no missing data. Postoperative recovery indicators—including time to first oral intake, ambulation, urination, flatus, and pain scores—were similar between the same-day discharge group and the non-same-day discharge group. No statistically significant differences were observed. The overall incidence of postoperative complications was 3.92% in the same-day discharge group and 5.66% in the non-same-day discharge group, and no Grade II or higher complications occurred. Binary logistic regression was conducted to identify factors associated with same-day discharge. The VIFs ranged from 1.02 to 1.05, all below the prespecified threshold of 10, confirming that there was no substantial collinearity among the variables. Patients who lived more than 30 km from the hospital had a markedly lower probability of being discharged on the same day, approximately 0.108 times that of patients who lived within 30 km (OR 0.108, 95% CI 0.036–0.325; p < 0.001). In contrast, patients with a history of abdominal surgery were approximately 6.501 times more likely to undergo same-day discharge compared with those without prior abdominal surgery (OR 6.501, 95% CI 1.796–23.535; p = 0.004). Additionally, surgeries completed before 15:00 were associated with a roughly 7.862-fold higher likelihood of same-day discharge than surgeries ending after 15:00 (OR 7.862, 95% CI 2.609–23.694; p < 0.001). No significant associations were observed between same-day discharge and VAS score or time to first ambulation (p = 0.070 and 0.659, respectively) (Table 5).

Discussion

This prospective observational study evaluated the feasibility of same-day discharge after hysteroscopic-laparoscopic surgery for infertility, as well as the factors that influence discharge timing under ERAS. In this cohort, nearly half of the patients were discharged on the day of surgery. All non-same-day discharged patients met the objective discharge criteria, and the between-group difference did not reflect poorer postoperative recovery. These findings confirm that, in appropriately selected patients, same-day discharge is feasible under ERAS management and that discharge timing is determined by non‑clinical factors. Significant associations were identified for residential distance and surgery end time.

With the advancement of minimally invasive techniques and the widespread adoption of ERAS protocols, same-day discharge has become increasingly common in gynecologic practice16–18. Combined hysteroscopic-laparoscopic surgery allows simultaneous evaluation and treatment of intrauterine and pelvic pathologies and is widely used in infertility management19. In this study, 49.0% of patients achieved same-day discharge under ERAS-based management. These patients experienced smooth postoperative recovery, significantly shorter hospital stays, and a high satisfaction rate (100%). No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of operative time, estimated blood loss, or key postoperative recovery indicators. These findings underscore the pivotal role of ERAS in achieving a comfortable and efficient same-day surgical experience.

The complication rates were low in both groups, and all complications were mild. The fever of a single patient was attributed to an upper respiratory tract infection and resolved with outpatient guidance. The three cases of nausea and vomiting resolved spontaneously within one day. Poor wound healing was defined as a suture reaction that resolved after removal of the subcutaneous suture. No reoperations or 30‑day readmissions occurred in either group, which is a clinically important safety endpoint clearly distinct from these minor symptoms. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies showing that same‑day discharge in gynecologic surgery does not increase short‑term adverse outcomes20,21. However, given the modest sample size, the absence of major complications and 30-day readmissions should be interpreted cautiously.

Residential distance emerged as the most influential factor associated with same-day discharge. Patients living more than 30 km from the hospital were less likely to be discharged on the same day, even when they met objective discharge criteria. These findings suggest that distance affects discharge decision-making rather than postoperative recovery. Clinically, distance may influence both patient and physician preference for overnight observation. In our practice, distance was discussed as a logistical variable during preoperative counseling but did not affect the length of stay. Physicians did not actively advise an overnight stay based on distance; the decision was left to the patient’s own preference. This finding is consistent with previous studies that identified travel distance as a determinant of same-day discharge22,23. Enhanced perioperative counseling and structured remote follow-up may help address these issues in patients living farther away.

Surgical end time was also significantly associated with discharge outcomes. Procedures completed before 15:00 were more likely to result in same-day discharge, likely because earlier completion allows adequate postoperative observation time. This threshold was chosen with reference to a previous systematic review reporting that a start time later than 14:00 reduces same-day discharge12, combined with our average operative duration of approximately 1 h. Even in clinically stable patients, late surgical completion may lead to a preference for overnight observation, especially because of concerns about complications during off-hours24,25. These findings have practical implications for surgical scheduling, suggesting that cases intended for same-day discharge may benefit from earlier operative timing to ensure adequate postoperative monitoring26.

Interestingly, a history of prior abdominal surgery was associated with a higher likelihood of same-day discharge. This finding may seem counterintuitive. Prior abdominal surgery is often linked to adhesions and increased surgical complexity. In this study, patients who underwent multiple abdominal surgeries were excluded, which reduced confounding from severe adhesions. In addition, all patients in the non-same-day discharge group met the discharge criteria, highlighting the influence of non-clinical factors. Patients with surgical experience may be more familiar with perioperative processes and postoperative discomfort, potentially with lower anxiety and greater acceptance of early discharge pathways27. They may also have more realistic expectations and better psychological adaptation28. An alternative explanation is selection bias: patients with prior abdominal surgery who were selected for day surgery may constitute a distinct subgroup with lower surgical complexity, stable clinical condition, or milder adhesions. Collectively, discharge decisions depend not only on physical recovery but also on psychological factors and shared decision-making29. Nevertheless, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis‑generating rather than definitive conclusions and require validation in future studies.

In terms of costs, no significant difference in hospitalization expenses was observed between the two groups, indicating that a reduced length of stay does not necessarily translate into lower overall costs. This may be explained by the cost structure of day surgery. Fixed costs, such as surgical fees, anesthesia fees, and preoperative assessments, account for a large proportion of total expenses, while the cost of an additional overnight stay is relatively small. Therefore, reducing the length of stay has a limited impact on overall costs. However, from a healthcare system perspective, same-day discharge may still offer nonfinancial benefits, including improved bed turnover and resource utilization by freeing up hospital beds for other patients, greater patient convenience by avoiding an overnight stay, and reduced indirect costs such as caregiver burden and time away from work30. Additionally, same-day discharge may reduce night-time operational expenses for the hospital, such as utilities and staffing costs.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-centre study with a modest sample size. The study population was highly selected, so our findings may not apply to broader populations. Second, the limited number of events may increase the risk of overfitting in the multivariable model, and some odds ratios have wide confidence intervals. Therefore, the regression results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‑generating. Third, group allocation was based on actual discharge outcomes rather than randomization, which may introduce selection bias. In addition, psychological and social factors were not assessed using standardized tools. As a result, the interpretation of non-clinical influences remains speculative. Future multicentre studies with larger and more diverse populations, incorporating standardized assessments of psychological and social factors are needed to validate these findings and further clarify the role of non-clinical factors in discharge decisions.

In conclusion, for carefully selected infertile women who underwent combined hysteroscopic‑laparoscopic surgery in a specialized ERAS‑based day‑surgery unit, same-day discharge appears feasible. All non‑same‑day discharged patients met the objective discharge criteria, and the between‑group difference did not reflect poorer postoperative recovery. Actual discharge timing is influenced by logistical and non-clinical factors such as residential distance and surgery end time.
