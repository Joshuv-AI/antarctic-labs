An imputation method for single‐cell RNA sequencing data based on a parallel gated recurrent unit neural network

Shuang Xu, Xiangtao Li, Jingsong Li, Yu Jiang

Abstract

Single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) generates high‐dimensional transcriptomic data but is severely affected by dropout events, which obscure true gene–gene relationships. These missing values introduce bias into downstream analyses, complicate data processing, and reduce analytical efficiency. Therefore, accurate imputation of dropout events is essential for recovering genuine expression signals and improving the reliability of scRNA‐seq studies. In this work, we propose a novel scRNA‐seq data imputation method, imputation with a parallel gated recurrent unit neural network (IPGRU), based on a parallel gated recurrent unit (GRU) neural network. IPGRU reformulates the original multifeature prediction problem into multiple single‐feature prediction tasks, thereby substantially reducing the complexity of the imputation process. By exploiting dependencies between expressed and missing genes, the GRU hidden states are employed to model gene expression patterns and predict dropout events, enabling effective correction of missing values and improving data completeness. Experimental results demonstrate that IPGRU achieves high accuracy in imputing dropout events and significantly enhances the integrity of scRNA‐seq data. Moreover, the imputed data provide more reliable inputs for downstream analyses, including cell clustering and inference of differentiation trajectories. IPGRU offers a robust and efficient solution for scRNA‐seq data imputation, improving the accuracy and reliability of downstream biological analyses. This method has strong potential to facilitate biological discovery and advance precision medicine.

INTRODUCTION

Traditional bulk RNA sequencing methods capture only the average gene expression levels across heterogeneous cell populations, thereby obscuring cell‐to‐cell variability and masking critical biological features. As a result, accurately characterizing gene expression differences at the single‐cell level remains challenging. In contrast, single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) enables the measurement of gene expression profiles at single‐cell resolution and has fundamentally transformed our understanding of diseases and associated biological processes by revealing cellular heterogeneity within complex tissues [, , , ]. Consequently, single‐cell‐based life science research provides a more fundamental and mechanistic basis for investigating the etiology, progression, and treatment of major diseases.

By overcoming the limitations of bulk sequencing, scRNA‐seq captures transcriptional variability within cell populations and enables more precise cell classification and characterization [, ]. However, scRNA‐seq data are inherently affected by technical limitations, including missingness, high noise levels, and sparsity, primarily due to the limited amounts of RNA obtained from individual cells and the shallow sequencing depth [, , ].

Zero expression values in scRNA‐seq data, arising from inefficient RNA capture and amplification, low endogenous gene expression, or stochastic transcriptional dropout, are commonly referred to as dropout events [, ]. A high prevalence of dropout events severely compromises downstream analyses, distorting gene–gene relationships and impairing cell‐type identification in clustering tasks []. Therefore, effective restoration of true gene expression patterns through advanced imputation strategies, particularly those based on deep learning, is essential for improving the quality and reliability of scRNA‐seq data analysis.

Restoring scRNA‐seq data and improving data quality are critical for enhancing the efficiency and reliability of downstream gene expression analyses. One of the most important strategies for addressing zero values in scRNA‐seq data is imputation, which infers or replaces missing expression values based on observed data patterns [, , ]. Over the past decade, numerous imputation methods have been developed to mitigate the impact of dropout events in scRNA‐seq datasets.

Early approaches include the LATE model proposed by Badsha et al. in 2016, which performed gene expression recovery for small‐scale datasets by extracting original gene features and leveraging transfer learning strategies [, ]. In 2018, Van Dijk et al. introduced Markov affinity‐based graph imputation of cells (MAGIC) [], which uses data diffusion on a cell‐similarity graph to denoise expression matrices and recover missing transcripts. Li et al. proposed scImpute, a statistically driven method that automatically identifies potential dropout events and selectively imputes zero values while avoiding biasing nonzero entries [].

With the advancement of deep learning, Arisdakessian et al. first applied neural networks to scRNA‐seq imputation and proposed DeepImpute in 2019 [], demonstrating superior robustness compared with traditional statistical approaches. Talwar et al. developed AutoImpute [], an autoencoder‐based sparse interpolation method that minimizes perturbations to biologically silent genes. Subsequently, Eraslan et al. improved autoencoder architectures and proposed the deep count autoencoder (DCA), a three‐layer output model capable of capturing complex nonlinear dependencies in scRNA‐seq data []. Zhu et al. further introduced 2DImpute, which predicts false‐zero values by jointly modeling gene–gene and cell–cell relationships to prevent overcorrection []. Graph neural network (GNN)‐based approaches, such as scGNN, have demonstrated strong performance by modeling cell–cell relationships through graph structures [].

In bioinformatics, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [], particularly gated recurrent units (GRUs) [] and long short‐term memory (LSTM) networks [], have been widely adopted for modeling sequential data, such as gene expression profiles and biological sequences. Ozsahin et al. [] utilized bidirectional LSTM and GRU architectures to predict epitopes from severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) proteins using a bioinspired optimization framework. Han et al. [] proposed SEOE, a graph‐based representation method integrating word2vec [], LSTM, and GRU for survey questionnaire analysis, achieving high accuracy in depression screening. Chai et al. [] developed a CNN–BiGRU model for DNA sequence prediction, whereas Li et al. [] introduced SIMEON, which employs stacked bidirectional GRU residual networks to identify chemical–protein interactions and capture higher‐order dependencies.

Although GRU‐based deep learning imputation methods generally outperform traditional statistical approaches in terms of accuracy and robustness [], existing models often exhibit noticeable deviations from biological ground truth when applied to scRNA‐seq datasets with high dropout rates []. One key challenge lies in the inherently long and sparse nature of scRNA‐seq expression sequences, which makes it difficult for deep learning models to effectively capture long‐range dependencies, often leading to selective memory loss. Segmenting long sequences into shorter subsequences has been shown to enhance feature extraction and improve model performance.

Motivated by these observations, we propose a novel and efficient imputation framework termed imputation with a parallel gated recurrent unit neural network (IPGRU). The proposed IPGRU introduces two major improvements over existing imputation approaches. First, cyclic memory‐based hidden representations are leveraged to estimate the number of recoverable dropout genes and perform accurate value correction. Second, the original multifeature prediction task is decomposed into multiple single‐feature prediction problems through parallel segmentation, substantially reducing model complexity and improving learning efficiency. Unlike autoencoder‐based methods that rely on global latent representations, IPGRU decomposes the imputation problem into multiple localized prediction tasks, enabling more efficient learning of gene‐specific dependencies. This design improves the tractability of modeling high‐dimensional gene dependencies and enhances robustness in highly sparse data scenarios, although it introduces additional computational overhead. Extensive experiments on imputation accuracy and cell clustering demonstrate that IPGRU achieves state‐of‐the‐art performance across multiple scRNA‐seq datasets of varying sizes. The code is available on the GitHub website (github.com/zhao123minghao/IPGRU.git).

RESULTS

To validate the imputation results, we used three metrics to assess the methods—mean squared error on nonzero entries (MSE_NZ), mean squared error (MSE), and cluster score—and performed the downstream analysis. The experimental setup for this paper is as follows: a hardware platform with 24‐core Intel Xeon, 128 GB of memory, and an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 2080 Ti with 22 GB. The software platform is the Ubuntu 18.04 operating system, Python 3.7.4, and TensorFlow 1.13.1.

The datasets

We evaluated IPGRU and other methods using five public datasets, as shown in Table .

The PBMC_G949_21K [, ] dataset is a subsample of the 10x Genomics PBMC_G949 dataset. In our method, we focused on a set of 949 genes from 21,065 cells, with total read counts exceeding 10K (10,000) per gene and 15K (15,000) per cell. The dataset has a 41% nonzero rate, which is treated as ground truth directly. GTEx_4 tissues [] are from the GTEx Portal V7 and contain gene expressions from 53 tissues. Here, we chose four tissues with a large sample size as ground truth, including muscle (564 samples), heart (600 samples), skin (1203 samples), and adipose tissue (797 samples), with a nonzero rate of 49.8%. Because GTEx_4 tissues are derived from bulk sequencing and this dataset contains more silent genes, we paid closer attention to MSE. MAGIC_mouse [, ] is a mouse bone marrow dataset. The original data are sparse, including hematopoietic stem cells at various stages of differentiation, which can reflect dynamics in gene expression. We generated an estimated matrix as the ground truth using the MAGIC method, randomly selected 30% of the ground truth for training, and compared the predicted matrix with the ground truth. PBMC_G949_10K is also generated from the PBMC_G949 dataset []. We selected the same 949 genes in the PBMC_G949 mentioned above and subsampled 10 K cells as ground truth. The nonzero rate of PBMC_G949_10K is 33%, and the dropout rate is set to 20%. PBMC_G5561 [] is from 10x Genomics PBMC. It contains a set of 5561 genes selected from 21,065 cells, with counts per gene and per cell exceeding 1 K. The dataset has a 9.9% nonzero rate and is selected to evaluate the performance of our method under a high miss rate.

To validate the downstream analytical performance of various imputation methods, we selected three additional single‐cell datasets: human bone marrow [], dentate gyrus neurogenesis [], and pancreas []. The human bone marrow dataset comprises 5780 human bone marrow cells, profiled across 2000 genes after filtering. The dentate gyrus neurogenesis dataset contains 2930 cells and the same 2000 filtered genes, with a known differentiation trajectory from oligodendrocyte precursor‐like (OPL) cells to mature oligodendrocytes (OL). The pancreas dataset includes 2531 cells and 2000 filtered genes, encompassing multiple developmental lineages.

Because MAGIC_mouse is a dataset synthesized by MAGIC, it introduces nonzero values into scRNA‐seq, which may lead to the failure to reconstruct the actual gene expression in single cells. In the imputation process, IPGRU could not distinguish whether a gene was expressed or generated by the MAGIC method, so IPGRU may be inferior to other methods in these two results.

Furthermore, we conducted extensive experiments on datasets for cell trajectory inference analysis across varying dropout rates, with results presented in Table . Across all three datasets and dropout levels, IPGRU consistently matches or outperforms competing methods in reconstructing nonzero entries. On human bone marrow, IPGRU achieves MSE_NZ of 0.000948, 0.000952, and 0.000959 at 20%, 30%, and 40% dropout, respectively—surpassing MAGIC by roughly threefold to fourfold and edging out DeepImpute at higher dropout rates. On the pancreas dataset, IPGRU tied with DeepImpute for the lowest MSE_NZ at 20% dropout and achieved the lowest errors at 30% and 40% dropout. It also consistently outperformed AutoImpute, DCA, and MAGIC across all dropout levels. Collectively, these results demonstrate that IPGRU maintains robust and consistently competitive imputation accuracy as the proportion of missing data increases.

Comparison of downstream analysis results

scRNA‐seq datasets typically consist of heterogeneous mixtures of cell types, and high dropout rates severely obscure transcriptional differences among cells. To evaluate the impact of imputation on downstream clustering performance, we conducted clustering analyses on three annotated scRNA‐seq datasets: human bone marrow [], dentate gyrus neurogenesis [], and pancreas [].

We assessed clustering performance under a challenging condition with a simulated 40% dropout rate. In raw scRNA‐seq data, sparse expression matrices are dominated by numerous false‐zero values, which substantially impair dimensionality reduction and clustering by masking true transcriptional signals. For consistency, Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) [] was applied across all experiments to reconstruct the low‐dimensional manifold structure of cell populations from high‐dimensional gene expression data.

As shown in Figure , the first column shows clustering results from the raw input data. Cells exhibit highly intermingled distributions with fragmented and overlapping clusters, indicating poor separability of cell types. In particular, closely related populations such as hematopoietic stem cell subpopulations (HSC_1 and HSC_2) in the human bone marrow dataset lack clear boundaries, a direct consequence of global sparsity induced by technical dropout events. In contrast, clustering results after IPGRU imputation (rightmost column) reveal well‐defined and compact clusters across all three datasets, with clear separation between major cell populations.

We further compared IPGRU with MAGIC, DCA, DeepImpute, and AutoImpute. All imputation methods improved clustering performance relative to raw data, demonstrating enhanced discrimination of cell types. However, IPGRU consistently achieved the most accurate clustering recovery across all datasets. In the human bone marrow dataset, IPGRU distinctly separated hematopoietic stem cell subpopulations (HSC_1 and HSC_2) into compact, nonoverlapping clusters and clearly distinguished erythroid populations (Ery_1 and Ery_2) from myeloid lineages (Mono_1, Mono_2, DCs, Mega, etc.). Moreover, the spatial organization of these clusters faithfully reflected the known hematopoietic differentiation hierarchy from stem cells toward erythroid and myeloid lineages.

For the pancreas dataset, IPGRU produced highly consistent clustering of endocrine cell populations, including beta, alpha, delta, and epsilon cells. The resulting UMAP embeddings exhibited smooth gradient‐like transitions that align with established developmental relationships. In contrast, MAGIC incorrectly subdivided the alpha cell population into two distinct clusters, suggesting oversmoothing artifacts.

DeepImpute and AutoImpute demonstrated moderate performance on the human bone marrow and pancreas datasets. Although major cell types were partially separated, cluster boundaries remained ambiguous, and overlaps persisted, particularly for rare cell populations. DCA showed relatively conservative imputation behavior. Although it reduced noise to some extent, it failed to fully resolve intracluster mixing caused by dropout. MAGIC’s diffusion‐based smoothing occasionally led to overdispersion, most notably in the dentate gyrus neurogenesis dataset, where the embedding fragmented into multiple small, disconnected subclusters. This fragmentation disrupted biologically expected continuous developmental trajectories and compromised downstream lineage inference.

To quantitatively assess trajectory reconstruction reliability, we employed the cross‐boundary confidence score [], which measures directed transition strength between source and target clusters based on RNA velocity probabilities. As shown in Figure , IPGRU‐imputed data achieve consistently higher confidence scores than raw inputs across 20%, 30%, and 40% dropout rates and even outperform the fully raw dataset without simulated dropout. These results indicate that IPGRU not only compensates for technical missingness but also corrects systematic biases, thereby enhancing biologically meaningful signals. Figure further compares trajectory inference results obtained using DeepImpute, AutoImpute, DCA, and MAGIC under identical conditions. IPGRU produces the most coherent and biologically plausible branching structures, confirming its superior capability to denoise sparse scRNA‐seq data and improve downstream lineage inference accuracy.

To evaluate computational efficiency, we compared the number of model parameters, memory usage, and inference time across different methods. As shown in Table , the overall memory usage is similar across all methods (approximately 2.34–2.38 GB). This is mainly because the measurements reflect the total runtime memory consumption of the process, including shared components such as the deep learning framework and data loading pipeline, rather than the model parameters alone. In contrast, differences in model complexity are more clearly reflected in the number of parameters and inference time. AutoImpute and DeepImpute contain 122,010 and 160,010 parameters, respectively, both with an average inference time of 5 ms. DCA has a relatively lightweight structure with 8106 parameters and an inference time of 6 ms. In contrast, IPGRU contains 788,780 parameters and requires approximately 14 ms for inference.

DISCUSSION

The parallel GRU neural network proposed in this study implicitly learns gene–gene dependencies during prediction, yielding a gene correlation coefficient matrix that captures similarities among gene expression patterns. This representation enables the identification of highly correlated gene groups and facilitates the accurate imputation of missing gene expression values. By modeling nonlinear gene dependencies, the proposed framework helps restore disrupted gene relationships and supports the reconstruction of biologically meaningful gene coexpression networks.

Unlike existing imputation approaches, IPGRU introduces a task‐decomposition strategy that segments the original multifeature prediction problem into multiple independent single‐feature prediction subnetworks. This parallelized architecture substantially reduces model complexity and improves computational efficiency when handling high‐dimensional scRNA‐seq data. By exploiting the dependency structures learned within each GRU module, dropout events are effectively identified and corrected through recurrent modeling, thereby mitigating the impact of outliers and technical noise in scRNA‐seq datasets.

Overall, IPGRU performs well in accurately imputing missing values in scRNA‐seq data. The memory mechanisms inherent to GRU units enable the model to capture complex gene expression dynamics, whereas backpropagation progressively refines network parameters to enhance forward prediction accuracy. As demonstrated in our experiments, IPGRU achieves state‐of‐the‐art or comparable performance across multiple benchmark scRNA‐seq datasets.

Despite these advantages, IPGRU also has certain limitations. GRU‐based architectures are computationally more demanding than convolutional neural networks, and although GRUs are generally more efficient than LSTM‐based models, nonlinear activation functions such as sigmoid and tanh introduce additional computational overhead. Although the model leverages gene–gene dependencies to mitigate overimputation, distinguishing technical dropouts from biologically meaningful zeros remains a challenging problem. Although this study focuses on comparing IPGRU with representative statistical and neural network‐based imputation methods, GNN‐based approaches such as scGNN have shown strong performance in modeling cell–cell relationships. Because of differences in modeling paradigms and implementation complexity, such methods were not included in the current benchmark. A comprehensive comparison with GNN‐based methods and potential integration of graph‐based and recurrent architectures will be explored in future work.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we developed IPGRU, a parallel gated recurrent unit neural network for imputing dropout events in scRNA‐seq data. By decomposing the original multifeature prediction problem into multiple parallel single‐feature prediction tasks, IPGRU efficiently models gene‐specific dependencies while reducing the complexity of high‐dimensional imputation.

Across multiple benchmark datasets, IPGRU achieved consistently low MSE and MSE_NZ values and demonstrated competitive or superior performance compared with MAGIC, DeepImpute, AutoImpute, and DCA. In addition, evaluations under different dropout rates showed that IPGRU maintained robust imputation accuracy as data sparsity increased. The imputed datasets also improved downstream cell clustering and differentiation trajectory inference, generating more clearly separated cell populations and biologically coherent lineage structures.

Overall, IPGRU provides an effective and robust framework for recovering missing expression signals in scRNA‐seq data and improving the reliability of downstream biological analyses. Nevertheless, the model has a higher number of parameters and longer inference time than several competing methods, and distinguishing technical dropouts from biologically meaningful zeros remains challenging. Future work will focus on improving computational efficiency, enhancing model interpretability, and integrating recurrent architectures with graph‐based methods to better capture cell–cell and gene–gene relationships.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The structure of IPGRU and the process of scRNA‐seq imputation are shown in Figure . The whole process includes preprocessing, network building, training, and evaluation metrics.

Preprocessing

The IPGRU method employs a divide‐and‐conquer strategy during deep learning inference. We split the dataset into N random subsets, each with S genes, called “target genes.” By default, S is set to 300. Based on the gene expression matrix, the top 5 genes most related to the target genes are set as the predicted genes.

Imputation model

The scRNA‐seq data imputation method includes data preprocessing, dimensionality reduction, and ensemble learning using parallel GRU networks. The process is shown in Figure .

The imputation model begins with data preprocessing. The scRNA‐seq count matrix is a substantial two‐dimensional matrix with cell types on the horizontal axis [] and genes on the vertical axis. The Pearson correlation coefficient is a measure of linear correlation between two variables, equal to 1 or −1 when the data are distributed along a straight line. The count matrix is converted to a gene correlation coefficient matrix using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Finally, the original cell count matrix is randomly split into groups of 300, forming the subdatasets, as shown in Figure . Specifically, the choice of subset size (S = 300) and the number of top correlated genes (5) are based on an empirical trade‐off between computational efficiency and imputation accuracy. A moderate subset size allows sufficient modeling of gene dependencies while avoiding excessive computational cost. Similarly, selecting the top 5 correlated genes provides meaningful contextual information without introducing noise from weak correlations.

The kernel‐based principal component analysis algorithm is used to select the top 5 most relevant genes for the target gene from the correlation coefficient matrix, forming the predicted gene matrix. The predicted gene matrix is fed into multiple sub‐recurrent neural networks (sub‐GRUs). The genes were provided as branches, and parallel and distributed computing was used to speed up model training. Each sub‐recurrent neural network aims to understand the relationship between the input and target genes.

The IPGRU is used to predict missing values in scRNA‐seq data, and the gated RNN architecture is shown in Figure . The GRU is a variant of the RNN that adds a gate to control the transmission of information and avoid gradient disappearance and gradient explosion, thereby meeting the characteristics of scRNA‐seq data and predicting unknown gene dependencies.

Network building and training

In the conquer step, we trained some GRU neural networks. In the single network, the first layer is the input layer. There are 768 GRUs in the second‐layer network and 640 GRUs in the third‐layer network. A fully connected layer network with 300 neurons is connected to the third layer. A dropout is placed after each GRU layer, with a dropout rate of 0.5 to prevent overfitting on the training set.

The optimizer for training is Adam. The learning rate is 0.001, the batch size is 32, the number of genes per subset is 300, and the number of epochs is 100. If the model’s validation loss does not decrease within 3 epochs of training, the subnetwork training is stopped early.

The training data are split into training (90%) and test (10%) sets. The test sets are used to evaluate the network accuracy after each epoch.
