Data‐Driven Insights into Hyperkinetic Disorders in Neurodevelopmental Syndromes and Epileptic Encephalopathies
Authors: Hugo Morales‐Briceño, Shekeeb S. Mohammad, Rajeshwar Reddy Angiti, Velda Han, Michel Tchan, Russell C. Dale, Victor S.C. Fung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood‐onset hyperkinetic movement disorders occur in a range of genetic conditions. Recently, there has been an increase in recognition of hyperkinetic movement disorders, mainly dystonia, chorea and dyskinesia, with monogenic conditions associated with neurodevelopmental delay (NDD) and also with developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEE), however, the full spectrum of genotypes and phenotypes remains underexplored.ObjectivesWe conducted a comprehensive literature review to identify monogenic NDD and DEE disorders that are reported with hyperkinetic movement disorders—specifically dystonia, chorea, and dyskinesia—and analyzed the frequency of neurological, extra‐neurological and phenomenological features.MethodsUsing cluster analysis, we identified data‐driven phenotypic groupings to inform diagnostic strategies.ResultsAmong 210 monogenic conditions (44.7% autosomal dominant, 45.2% autosomal recessive, 7.6% X‐linked), 131 (62.3%) segregated into two major clusters: one predominantly NDD and the other predominantly DEE. A variable combination of neurological, extra‐neurological and imaging features, as well as phenomenologies, distinguished subclusters within the NDD and DEE groups. Across both groups, dystonia was the most frequently reported movement disorder, followed in order by chorea, ataxia, dyskinesia, and myoclonus. Only a small subset of conditions expressed paroxysmal movement disorders and alternating hemiplegia of childhood, highlighting these clinical features as specific diagnostic clues.ConclusionBased on these findings, we propose a clinical and syndromic diagnostic framework for clinicians evaluating patients with NDD, DEE, and hyperkinetic movement disorders. This approach may aid diagnosis in settings lacking access to next‐generation sequencing and support reverse phenotyping to improve diagnostic precision and interpretation of genomic data.

Childhood‐onset hyperkinetic movement disorders can occur in a range of genetic conditions, which can exhibit unique neurological and neuroimaging manifestations, as well as discrete clinical trajectories. Recently, there has been an increase in recognition of hyperkinetic movement disorders, mainly dystonia, chorea and dyskinesia, with monogenic conditions associated with neurodevelopmental delay (NDD) and also with developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEE). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Although these conditions can be clinically distinctive, many lack specific neuroimaging or fluid biomarkers, making a molecular diagnosis necessary for accurate diagnosis.

With the advent of next‐generation sequencing (NGS) in clinical and research settings, the number of novel genes identified in patients with NDD and DEE has substantially risen. 5 , 6 As a consequence, the diagnostic accuracy for these conditions on clinical grounds alone has become challenging for clinicians. However, NGS has also facilitated the identification of rare and novel monogenic causes, and in selected cases, has allowed molecular targeted therapeutic approaches. 7 Despite NGS becoming a first‐tier diagnostic tool, iterative and detailed clinical phenotyping remains essential. It underpins syndrome characterization, guides interpretation of variants of uncertain significance (reverse phenotyping), and informs our understanding of natural disease trajectories.

To date, it is unknown whether hyperkinetic movement disorders can serve as a basis for genotype–phenotype correlations, thereby facilitating more accurate clinical diagnosis and informed therapeutic decision‐making. Against this background, we first conducted an in‐depth literature review to synthesize the clinical phenotypes and genotypes associated with the intersection of NDDs, DEEs, and hyperkinetic movement disorders. Using collated frequency data from the literature review, we then used cluster analysis to explore potential phenotypic clusters within these syndromes using a data‐driven approach.

Methods

Data Collection and Search Strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature review to delineate monogenic associations of NDD or DEE where hyperkinetic movement disorders have been described. Literature searches were performed in PubMed by two authors (H.M.B, S.M), selecting English language articles published up to January 2025. First, we identified articles of NDD and DEE combined with dystonia, chorea and dyskinesia using a literature search; the MeSH search terms used are described in the Supplementary Material. A second search was performed in OMIM.org to derive a list of genes associated with the presence of “neurodevelopmental disorder” and “epileptic encephalopathy.” After generating the gene list, we searched back in PubMed for publications on each gene in which movement disorder phenomenology and clinical characteristics were described. A PRISMA flowchart is provided as a Figure S1.

Once articles were identified, we tabulated each genetic condition's phenotypic description and clinical features and estimated the frequency of hyperkinetic movement disorders. We focused on cohort studies reporting a large number of patients, but also included case series, and single case reports in rare or ultrarare genotypes. We collected information such as the age of onset, developmental delay, stress‐induced onset, childhood‐onset neurodegeneration, seizure and epilepsy characteristics, facial dysmorphic features, systemic (solid organ) or limb malformations, and systemic features (eg, hematological, immunological, renal, liver, cardiac). Moreover, the frequency of neurological features was recorded, including spasticity, neuropathy, neuromuscular disorders, eye movement abnormalities (eg, abnormal saccades, nystagmus, oculogyric crises, saccadic oscillations, ophthalmoparesis), cortical visual impairment, cataract, and deafness. Lastly, we collected the neuroimaging abnormalities reported in each condition based on large cohort studies.

We excluded conditions that can be diagnosed with specific serological and CSF biomarkers, such as disorders of monoamine, vesicular transport and biopterin synthesis disorders, vitamin‐responsive disorders, and disorders of amino and organic acid metabolism. Additionally, we excluded conditions in which neuroimaging characteristics allow for the integration of a specific syndrome, such as Leigh syndrome, leukodystrophies, familial basal ganglia calcifications, neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation, manganese transporter deficiency, and inborn errors of metabolism. Lastly, mitochondrial conditions were also excluded as these may have distinctive clinical, imaging, or biomarker findings (eg, elevated lactate, basal ganglia, brainstem, spinal and cerebellar lesions). Nevertheless, the diagnostic approach section discusses all the aforementioned conditions, where we integrate our findings.

We used cluster analysis to explore which clinical features could be used to derive a diagnostic approach (see Supplementary Material for details). To homogenize extracted data and use it for cluster analysis of the list of NDD and DEE genes, we devised a scoring system based on the frequency of the clinical features for each gene. Results

We identified 236 genotypes linked to NDD (n = 150) and with DEE (n = 86) that manifest with hyperkinetic movements, such as dystonia, chorea, and dyskinesia. Based on our predefined inclusion criteria, we narrowed this down to 210 genotypes associated with NDD or DEE and hyperkinetic movement disorders, lacking specific imaging, serum, fluid, or biomarkers. The complete tabulated scoring dataset for clinical features is shown in the online Supplementary Material.

The inheritance patterns included autosomal dominant (44.7%), autosomal recessive (45.2%), X‐linked (7.6%), and both dominant and recessive (2.8%) (EMC1, JPH3, KIF1A, RARB, SCL2A1, TET3).

Cluster analysis grouped 131/210 genotypes into six clusters based on distinct clinical features, where NDD and DEE were key segregating features (Fig. 1). For instance, clusters 1, 2, and 3 included genotypes with predominantly NDD and infrequent DEE. In contrast, clusters 4, 5, and 6 were dominated by DEE (91.9%), with only 10.8% reporting predominant NDD presentations. Survival to adulthood, NDD, and stereotypies were features distributed across the 79 non‐clustered genotypes (vs. cluster 1–6 genotypes). Additional features included childhood‐onset neurodegeneration, seizures without DEE, microcephaly, dystonia, spasticity, cortical visual impairment, and abnormal brain MRI findings.

Cluster 1 (neurodevelopmental delay [NDD] with or without autism): All genotypes (n = 61) had NDD (98.3%), with autism (37.7%), and facial dysmorphic features (34.4%). Seizures were reported in 13, but developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEE) and HSP presentations were absent. Half of the genotypes described with parkinsonism belonged to this group. Chorea was reported in 90% at different frequencies, and seizures, but not DEE, in 90%, distinguishing it from 1 and 2 clusters. Conclusions

Our review estimates that the genotypic spectrum encompasses 210 genes associated with NDD and DEE that could manifest with hyperkinetic movements, such as dystonia, chorea, and dyskinesia. These disorders lack specific neuroimaging patterns or biomarkers, making a clinical approach and molecular testing essential for their final diagnosis.

Using cluster analysis based on the frequency of neurological and non‐neurological features across 210 genotypes, we identified two primary diagnostic families: a predominant NDD cluster and a DEE cluster. Within these, further subgroup clustering was observed based on distinct clinical features. This clustering approach highlights key phenotypic features that can help segregate genotypes, reinforcing the importance of deep phenotyping in clinical assessments. Some genes demonstrated syndromic clustering; for example, MECP2 in cluster 1 (NDD with autism), SPAST in cluster 2 (NDD with spasticity or HSP), and ATP8A2 in cluster 3 (chorea). Similarly, RHOBTB2 (DEE with hyperkinetic movements) and SCN8A (DEE with ataxia and paroxysmal movement disorders) were prominent within DEE clusters.

Among the 210 genotypes analyzed, dystonia, chorea, ataxia, and dyskinesia were the most frequently observed movement disorders. Their distribution was similar between NDD and DEE genes, with dystonia and chorea being the most common. However, dyskinesia was more commonly reported in DEE (50% vs. 22.8%). This discrepancy may stem from differences in classification within epilepsy literature, where “epilepsy‐dyskinesia” might be used to describe combined phenomenology (including stereotypies), which are commonly seen in DEE. 1

The findings of our review are in keeping with the estimated frequency of childhood‐onset monogenic hyperkinetic movement disorders in the literature. In a retrospective multicenter study of 140 patients, the mean age of onset of movement disorders was 2.8 years. 8 More frequently, the hyperkinetic movement disorder was generalized (77%) and in a combination of two or more hyperkinetic phenomenologies (~70%), with dystonia (89%) being the most frequent, followed by chorea‐ballism (35%), myoclonus (32%), tremor (16%) and stereotypies (11%). Furthermore, developmental delay was described in 49% and severe in half of these patients, mainly in neurotransmitter defects and in GNAO1, FOXG1, and ATP1A3. An earlier onset of hyperkinetic movement disorders was seen in those with developmental delay as opposed to those without. The notion that dystonia is the most frequent movement disorder in developmental delay syndrome is highlighted in large dystonia cohorts, where patients diagnosed as dystonic cerebral palsy had a high rate of molecular diagnosis (66%). 2 In addition, some authors have pointed out that the genetic mechanisms of dystonia overlap with NDD syndromes. 9

Our review highlighted that dystonia and chorea are the most commonly reported movement disorders in DEE. In the recent EDS cohort of 609 patients with 74 genotypes, 10 as well as a previous cohort of 77 DEE patients from 38 genotypes, 1 the onset of seizures was in infancy, with movement disorders appearing in the second year of life. In both these cohorts, combined movement disorders were noted in roughly half of the patients. Both cohorts observed a high proportion of patients with intellectual disability and global developmental delay. They also noted that mild developmental delay and normal intellect were present in a smaller proportion of genotypes, suggesting these could be important diagnostic clues for distinguishing particular genotypes. Although dystonia was prominently noted in both these cohorts, with a similar proportion to our study, they also noted stereotypies as a common movement disorder description. One possible explanation for why stereotypies were not the predominant phenomenology reported in the DEE literature that we reviewed could be the differences in the genotypes included by other cohorts or possible mislabeling of stereotypies as dyskinesia or chorea and vice versa in the literature—a discrepancy that could be solved using expert‐based assessment.

Based on our review, the following section incorporates our findings to derive a diagnostic approach for patients with a combination of hyperkinetic movement disorders with developmental delay or epileptic encephalopathy.

A Stepwise Approach to Movement Disorders in Patients with Neurodevelopmental Delay

The diagnostic assessment of patients presenting with movement disorders and NDD is a systematic process that starts with a comprehensive characterization of the clinical phenomenology. Figure 2 illustrates a proposed step‐by‐step approach based on syndromic, clinical and neuroimaging features.

Proposed algorithm for the approach of developmental disorders associated with hyperkinetic movement disorders.

The first step is to consider the overarching neurodevelopmental syndrome—whether DEE, or NDD. The history of risk factors and neuroimaging features of brain insult in early life should be looked at, given that cerebral palsy (CP) is the most common cause of movement disorders associated with developmental delay. Subsequently, we suggest identifying and delineating the movement disorder syndromes, noting whether they occur in combination or as a single phenomenology.

The distribution of the movement disorder often provides diagnostic clues. For example, hemidystonia is most commonly due to a contralateral brain lesion, although it may be seen in monogenic disorders such as ATP1A3. In contrast, hand dystonia and orobuccal apraxia in males with ID (Partington syndrome) points towards an ARX‐related disorder. 11 , 12 Similarly, prominent oromandibular dystonia or opisthotonus may suggest a neurometabolic or brain metal deposition disorder, and camptocormia, although rare in childhood, has been linked to recessive conditions such as Birk‐Landau‐Perez syndrome and ACBD6 mutations. 13 , 14 Abrupt onset, episodic/paroxysmal exacerbations, or nocturnal exacerbations of the dystonia or dyskinesia are also characteristic of certain conditions (Video 1). Status dystonicus was reported in 15/200 included patients with dystonia in a recent EDS study, with a message to consider specific genes, such as GNAO1, as well as other genotypes. 10 This can have therapeutic implications, including consideration of deep brain stimulation.

After identifying the phenomenology, the next diagnostic step involves characterizing the syndrome based on additional factors such as the age of onset, tempo, progression (whether static or progressive), potential stress‐related triggers, and associated family history. The presence of other neurological signs, as well as systemic signs (eg, hepatic, renal, or cardiac issues), can further refine the diagnosis (Fig. 3). Additionally, facial dysmorphisms or systemic (solid organ) malformations may serve as crucial diagnostic markers. Neuroimaging plays a pivotal role in classifying the syndrome, especially when abnormalities point towards disorders of basal ganglia, cerebellum, cortical malformation, or leukodystrophy.

Neurological and extraneurological features associated with neurodevelopmental delay (NDD) and developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEE) genotypes that frequently present with hyperkinetic movement disorders. Features associated with NDD genotypes are colored in black, whereas features associated with DEE are colored blue.

In classic NDDs such as Rett syndrome, movement disorder phenomenology typically begins with motor stereotypies in early childhood, which evolve into mixed movement disorders, often with dystonia, parkinsonism, and gait impairment (Video 2). Many disorders associated with predominant NDDs also exhibit early motor stereotypies and are classified as Rett‐like syndromes. However, some may present with a broader phenotypic spectrum, such as dystonia in WDR45 15 or chorea in FOXG1 mutations. 16 Some particular patterns of stereotypies, such as figure‐eight head stereotypies, can be suggestive of cerebellar malformation disorders, including rhomboencephalosynapsis and Joubert syndrome, but also described in DEE due to STXBP1. 17

The second segment shows an adolescent with Rett syndrome, with hand and truncal stereotypies, left arm dystonia, and broad‐based gait with stiff legs and toe walking. The third segment shows a patient with WDR45 with stereotypies, facial dystonia and dystonic gait.

In children with intellectual disability and diagnosed with CP, potential indicators of an underlying genetic etiology include normal brain MRIs, progressive or new‐onset movement disorders, non‐epileptic paroxysmal movement episodes, diurnal fluctuations, and oculogyric crises. 18 A levodopa trial is recommended for children presenting with dystonia, dyskinesia, chorea and developmental delay, as they may indicate monoamine neurotransmitter disorders, which can present similarly to dyskinetic CP.

After first and second‐tier investigations have been performed to exclude potentially treatable disorders (Supplementary Material), the initial molecular testing is chromosomal microarray, which is a valuable diagnostic tool for hyperkinetic movement disorders with coexisting intellectual disability and dysmorphic features, 19 as it can detect deletions or chromosomal re‐arrangements containing genes associated with movement disorders. For most cases, the next step should be whole‐exome (or genome) sequencing (WES/WGS), ideally trio‐based; however, separate testing for repeat expansion disorders should also be considered in cases with a dominant family history or high‐risk populations (eg, ATN1 expansions in Asians) 20 as these conditions will not be detected on standard WES/WGS platforms. There is emerging evidence that DNA methylation epigenetic signature can be leveraged to interpret variants of unknown significance in NDD 21 and dystonia syndromes, including common causes of early onset dystonia with bulbar involvement such as KMT2B. 22 Moreover, long read sequencing may be a valuable diagnostic technology for WGS or WES unsolved cases. 23

In our review, we identified 151 genes that were reported to be associated with NDD and hyperkinetic movement disorders (68 dominant, 65 recessive, 5 both dominant and recessive, and 13 X‐linked). Genotypes consistently reported in the literature with NDD presentation, and >50% frequency for dystonia, chorea, or dyskinesia were ATP8A2, CTNNB1, FOXG1, GNAO1, GRIA3, GRIN1, MECP2, MED27, NGLY1, SHQ1, SLC16A2, SLC30A9, SPATA5L1, UBE3A, VAMP2, and WDR73.

The integration of phenomenological features into a syndrome is an important step in identifying potential etiologies in children. Syndromic associations and clinical clues in neurodevelopmental delay (NDD) and developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEE) syndromes

Once the phenomenology and clinical syndrome have been identified, a systematic, structured approach can facilitate the first diagnostic hypotheses. We suggest approaching patients with NDD, DEE, and movement disorders by integrating phenomenological classification, neuroimaging findings, and other diagnostic clues. This framework proves helpful in clinical practice, offering a methodical way of evaluating movement disorders in patients with developmental delay and facilitating more accurate diagnoses and targeted management strategies.

In the following text, we provide some syndromic clues to narrow down the potential differential diagnoses.

Dopa Responsive Dystonia with NDD

Conditions presenting with dopa responsive dystonia (DRD) can also exhibit developmental delay, particularly in cases involving dopamine synthesis disorders, recessive BH4 disorders, and chaperone deficiency syndromes like DNAJC12 mutations. 24 Disorders affecting vesicular transport are more frequently associated with intellectual and motor delay; however, in some cases, levodopa may exacerbate dystonia, whereas dopamine agonists may provide some motor improvement. A few cases associated with NDD genotypes have been described with DRD, including NR4A2 25 , 26 and CLTC. 27 , 28 Dopa‐responsive dystonia with oculogyric crises and levodopa‐induced dyskinesias have been described in several cases of SHQ1. 29

Paroxysmal Movement Disorders

Paroxysmal movement disorders are reported concurrently in ~9% of neurodevelopmental disorders, and their presence can point to specific conditions. These disorders encompass a variety of episodic movement abnormalities, including paroxysmal dyskinesia, episodic opisthotonus, paroxysmal ataxia, paroxysmal torticollis, and other unclassified episodic movements. The classification of paroxysmal dyskinesia into paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia (PKD), paroxysmal non‐kinesigenic dyskinesia (PNKD), and paroxysmal exercise‐induced dyskinesia (PED) remains relevant, though this distinction may be difficult in neonates and infants, where clear triggers may not be identified. It is also crucial to distinguish between patients with paroxysmal exacerbations of persistent hyperkinetic disorders and those with isolated paroxysmal movement disorders.

Paroxysmal dyskinesia is an important clue for GLUT1 deficiency, which can present within the spectrum of epilepsy‐dyskinesia syndrome, or PED, with or without intellectual disability. 30 Dominant PRRT2 mutations are the most common cause of PKD; however, those with homozygous PRRT2 mutations or 16p11.2 deletions present with intellectual disability or autism. 31 Other genotypes of PKD are seldom associated with NDD. Paroxysmal episodes that resemble cataplexy, triggered by emotions or illness, can be the clue in infants with NDD and epilepsy caused by dominant KCNMA1 mutations. 32 Treatment with acetazolamide or lisdexamfetamine can alleviate symptoms. 33 PDE2A mutations are also linked to paroxysmal dyskinesias, NDD, and normal neuroimaging in all individuals reported to date. 34 , 35 , 36 Notably, ADCY5 mutations, a common cause of hyperkinetic MD in childhood, can lead to variable expression of dyskinesias, including PED, PKD, and PNKD. 37

Opisthotonus can be a predominant presentation of neurometabolic disorders or brain iron deposition syndromes. However, paroxysmal or transient opisthotonus has been described in neurodevelopmental genotypes (5.7%), including PI4K2A 38 , 39 , 40 and TNR. 41 Paroxysmal ataxia is a characteristic of CACNA1A; however, paroxysmal ataxia, truncal tilt, or weakness are also features of childhood‐onset neurodegeneration syndromes, such as TANGO2 mutations, which present with metabolic encephalomyopathic crises, rhabdomyolysis, and cardiac arrhythmias. 42 Early evidence suggests that B complex or vitamin B5 supplementation is an effective treatment. 43 , 44 Similarly, ADPRHL2 mutations can cause fever‐induced dystonia, chorea, ataxia, or torticollis during febrile episodes, which may precede the neurodegenerative course. 45 , 46

Alternating Hemiplegia of Childhood

Alternating hemiplegia of childhood (AHC) is a distinctive syndrome, observed in only ~3% of NDD and DEE. It is characterized by recurrent episodes of hemiplegia that alternate in laterality and can progress to quadriplegia. These episodes typically emerge in infancy (<18 months), improve during sleep but recur upon awakening, and are often accompanied by paroxysmal dystonia, oculomotor abnormalities (monocular deviation, nystagmus), seizures, and autonomic dysfunction. 47 Between episodes, patients exhibit hypotonia and hyperkinetic movement disorders, including dystonia, ataxia, chorea, and myoclonus. 48 Brain MRI is typically unremarkable, differentiating AHC from secondary hemiplegia conditions. ATP1A3 mutations are the most frequent genetic cause of AHC, with associated intellectual disability (~90%) and variable regression. 48 , 49

Missense mutations in RHOBTB2 also cause epileptic encephalopathy with movement disorders. Intellectual disability is universal, and seizures typically arise within the first year, coinciding with developmental regression or stagnation. More than 90% of patients exhibit paroxysmal movement disorders, with ~70% fulfilling AHC diagnostic criteria. 50 Neuroimaging findings are often subtle but may include cortical/subcortical FLAIR hyperintensities with diffusion restriction contralateral to the hemiplegia. Recurrent hemiplegia, dyskinesia, and DWI restriction have also been reported in ATP1A2 mutations, though, unlike AHC, hemiplegic attacks do not improve with sleep. 51

Hyperekplexia and Pathological Startle

Hyperekplexia is a rare feature of neurodevelopmental disorders. Hereditary forms primarily arise from mutations affecting glycine receptor function, most commonly in GLRA1, which can follow dominant or recessive inheritance. While dominant mutations may present with isolated startle reflex, recessive forms are often more severe and associated with neurodevelopmental delay. 52 Other significant genetic causes include recessive SLC6A5 and GLRB mutations, which are linked to developmental impairment.

Exaggerated startle can be a feature of Allan‐Herndon‐Dudley syndrome, suggested by startle dystonia, high T3 with low T4, and delayed brain myelination in males. 53 , 54 Hyperekplexia or startle can be a prominent feature of developmental disorders, including GRIA3, 55 PURA 56 , 57 and BRAF 58 , 59 which can have additional distinctive features. CTNNB1 mutations have also been linked to startle responses, though this feature may be underdiagnosed. 60 , 61 , 62

Abnormal Oculomotor Movements

In the context of NDD and DEE syndromes with hyperkinetic movement disorders, only 22% of these conditions have abnormal eye movements. Overall, nystagmus is the most common oculomotor abnormality; however, this is not specific, as many disorders can cause it. In these cases, neuroimaging plays a major role in elucidating the presence of cerebellar/brainstem malformations or a white matter disorder.

Nystagmus and developmental delay can be the initial manifestations of ATP1A3 or CACNA1A mutations. For instance, paroxysmal monocular exodeviation, nystagmus and epilepsy may be a presentation of ATP1A3. 47 More specific oculomotor manifestations have been described, and their presence can point to a reduced number of conditions (Table 2). For instance, the combination of congenital “roving” nystagmus, renal failure, chorea and dystonia is characteristic of Galloway‐Mowat syndrome. 63 Ocular flutter or opsoclonus has been described in combination with developmental delay, ataxia and hyperkinetic movement disorders in FRMD5. 64 , 65 , 66

In children with developmental delay, oculogyric crises (OCGs) are mostly seen in the context of disorders of dopamine metabolism (Video 3) or acute dystonic reaction by dopamine receptor blockers or caused by antiepileptic drugs. 67 OGCs have been described in epilepsy‐dyskinesia, including in 22% of patients with GRIN1. 68 , 69 They have also been described in biallelic SHQ1 mutations, associated with infantile onset regression and combined dystonia. 29

Paroxysmal tonic upgaze (PTU) is the most common paroxysmal movement disorder in infants with CACNA1A and can be the initial manifestation even without ataxia. 70 De novo DAGLA mutations cause a similar syndrome with NDD, episodic ataxia, as well as PTU episodes. 71 Paroxysmal saccadic eye movements and head movements have been described in treatable disorders such as GLUT1 deficiency and pyridoxine‐responsive epilepsy. 72 , 73

The combination of oculomotor apraxia (OMA), ptosis, dystonia, camptocormia and renal dysfunction is suggestive of Birk‐Landau‐Perez syndrome. 13 , 74 OMA, in the context of dystonia, arthrogryposis multiplex congenita can be seen in X‐linked ZC4H2 mutations. 75 Upward saccadic palsy and oculomotor apraxia, in combination with facial dyskinesia, chorea and nocturnal ballistic bouts, are suggestive of ADCY5. 76 , 77 Upgaze limitation, camptocormia, gait ataxia and corpus callosal agenesis suggest recessive ACBD6 mutations, where axial dystonia is reported in ~90%. 14 Biallelic mutations in SQSTM1 cause neurodegeneration with ataxia, dystonia, and gaze palsy, childhood‐onset (NADGP) syndrome, 78 , 79 where vertical gaze palsy is common (89%). 80 A combination of slow horizontal or vertical saccades, nystagmus and ophthalmoplegia was described in 42% with dominant IRF2BPL mutations.

Aetiological Considerations and Approach to Epilepsy Dyskinesia Syndrome

The term “epilepsy dyskinesia syndrome” (EDS) was initially used by Gorman K. et al to describe patients with CACNA1B, where a combined phenomenology of hyperkinetic movements was observed. 81 In the group of 210 disorders we reviewed here, seizures were reported in 154 disorders, while a description of DEE was used for 86 disorders. This emphasis on a phenomenology‐based approach has been recently highlighted in a large multicenter cohort of 609 patients with 74 different genotypes associated with EDS, 10 where most cases were attributed to 12 different genotypes (ATP1A3, CACNA1A, CDKL5, FOXG1, GNAO1, MECP2, PRRT2, SCN1A, SCN8A, SCL2A1, STXBP1, WDR45). However, only 66.8% and 37.2% of the cohort had epilepsy and DEE, respectively, suggesting that a predominant movement disorder presentation is seen in a subset.

In our review, 86 genes were reported to be associated with DEE and hyperkinetic movement disorders (40 dominant, 38 recessive, 1 dominant and recessive, and 8 X‐linked). Genotypes consistently reported in the literature with DEE presentation, and >50% frequency for dystonia, chorea or dyskinesia were DNM1, EEF1A2, FRRS1L, PIGQ, RHOBTB2, SCN1A (gain‐of‐function), and UBA5. As discussed above, a combined phenomenology is common in DEE, including stereotypies, dystonia, chorea and myoclonus, which can sometimes be difficult to discern at first examination. In addition, movement disorders may emerge as a late manifestation, such as dystonia with hypokinesia in Dravet syndrome (Video 4).

In terms of approaching the overlap of epilepsy and dyskinesia, we suggest a syndromic approach based on the age of onset, type of movement disorder, type of epilepsy and clinical progression. In some cases, ancillary imaging findings may further help narrow down differential lists. In some cases, the presence of specific neurological or extra‐neurological features may be diagnostic clues.

The first step in the clinical approach is to determine whether the patient's phenomenology suggests seizures, a movement disorder, or both. This may require careful examination as well as a video EEG to discern between epileptic and non‐epileptic events. Important collateral history at this initial stage is the history of any brain injury and medication history. For example, the new onset of a movement disorder without an EEG correlate in an infant with infantile epileptic spasms syndrome, treated with vigabatrin, should raise the consideration of a drug‐induced movement disorder rather than consider the movement disorder to be part of the underlying etiology. 82 In terms of seizure syndrome, the terms developmental and epileptic encephalopathy have to be carefully used only if they fulfill the criteria of the epilepsy being responsible for developmental stagnation or regression. 5

The most common cause of the co‐occurrence of seizures and movement disorders is brain injury due to hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy. In these cases, the usual time course of evolution is for a transient period of hypotonia in infancy, followed by evolving hypertonia, often with a mixture of spasticity and dystonia. However, particular phenomenological features such as hyperekplexia or extreme rigidity in infancy may offer clues towards certain disorders, such as glycine receptor mutations or a host of disorders that may lead to hyperkinetic or hypokinetic rigid syndromes of infancy, respectively.

Episodic and paroxysmal movement disorders are also a frequent accompaniment to many monogenic disorders that can present either as epilepsy or movement disorders or a mixture of both. Again, particular syndromic associations can be anchored with the episodic movement disorder subtype, such as paroxysmal dyskinesias, episodic ataxia or AHC.

Molecular Underpinnings of NDD and DEE

Molecular function analysis pointed out both the overlaps and distinctions between genes in the NDD clusters and the DEE clusters (Fig. S3). Functions that were common to both cluster groups included “catalytic activity” of kinases, phosphatases and proteases that impact synaptic plasticity, metabolism, or post‐translational modifications, “hydrolase activity” of ATPases, GTPases and nucleases that regulate neuronal signaling; DNA repair and protein turnover and “RNA binding” that may lead to dysregulation of mRNA stability or ribosomal function (eg, FMR1 in Fragile X). Epigenetic machinery related pathways were enriched in the NDD genes—“DNA binding” that controls gene regulation via transcription factors (eg, MECP2) or chromatin remodeling, as well as “transcription regulation.” 83

Common mechanisms of epilepsy‐dyskinesia syndrome include sodium, calcium, and voltage‐gated potassium channelopathies, as well as abnormalities of receptors involved in glutamate neurotransmission, such as NMDA, AMPA, and GABA neurotransmission. 5 Other genotypes with frequent presentation have different disease mechanisms, such as synaptopathies, cell metabolism and epigenetic regulation. DEE‐specific enriched pathways were “transporter activity” impacting neuronal excitability, such as in prototypic DEE genes like KCNQ2 and SCN1A, and “molecular transducer activity” altering neurotransmission (eg, GABRA1). This dichotomy of gene expression and control dysfunction in NDD versus dysfunctional signaling and ion homeostasis in DEE plausibly indicates chromatin modification and ion channel/neurotransmitter regulation as precision treatment targets for the respective disorder groups.

Study Limitations and Future Perspectives

Our review of the literature relied on the provided clinical descriptions in the articles; thus, there is an inherent bias in the phenomenological descriptions, with a risk of misclassification. We acknowledge that details of the qualitative and quantitative features of the movement disorder (eg, distribution and severity) were not always complete, which may have underestimated the interpretation of the syndromic pattern. The accurate distinction between movement disorder phenomenologies is essential, as treatment approaches for dystonia and chorea differ significantly. 84 Another caveat of our study is that we have not delved into granular clinical data on the full spectrum of NDD, including the severity of ID or behavioral disorders. Similarly, we have not analyzed epilepsy subtypes and the degree of ID in DEE syndromes with hyperkinetic movement disorders, which could be helpful in understanding if there is a possible segregation of genetic conditions based on the electroclinical syndrome.

The clinical and molecular spectrum of NDD and DEE combined with hyperkinetic movements is broad and complex, encompassing not only neurological but also cognitive, behavioral, and neuropsychiatric manifestations. Consequently, their diagnosis and management necessitate a multidisciplinary approach to optimize patient care. We suggest that the evaluation of patients with these conditions should be based on a structured approach, taking into account not only the phenomenology and neuroimaging patterns but also the presence or absence of additional clinical features. This is also relevant for future studies using machine learning algorithms, where positive and negative predictor factors should be considered to develop the optimal models. Lastly, we envision that many undiagnosed adult patients with childhood‐onset movement disorders in combination with developmental delay or epilepsy could be re‐evaluated following the approach outlined here.
