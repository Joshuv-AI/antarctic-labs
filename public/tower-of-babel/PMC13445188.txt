DCE-MRI kinetic heterogeneity facilitates the discrimination of benignity and malignancy of breast in combination with the DWI and morphological features
Authors: Ruobing Wang, Yanhong Chen, Chen Luo, Lijun Wang, Ran Luo, Ling Zhan, Zhengwei Zhang, Minmin Wang, Qiufeng Yin, Zhongyang Zhang, Lijun Yan, Yuzhen Zhang, Dengbin Wang, Huanhuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to validate the diagnostic value of kinetic heterogeneity (KH) across multiple cohorts and to develop a nomogram integrating KH, apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), and key morphological and clinical features for preoperative discrimination between benign and malignant breast masses.MethodsThis retrospective study included 501 female patients with 541 confirmed breast mass lesions. Training (n = 280) and internal validation (n = 119) cohorts were collected (2018–2019). Temporal validation (n = 101, 2024) and independent test cohorts (n = 41, 2025–2026), from a different scanner system) were also included. KH was computed using voxel-wise time-intensity curve analysis. Independent predictors were identified using logistic regression to construct a nomogram. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curve, and decision curve analysis, and compared with KH-, ADC-, and combined models.ResultsAge, lesion margin, ADC, and KH were independent predictors. The nomogram achieved AUCs of 0.897 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.838–0.955) and 0.909 (95% CI: 0.811–1.000) in the temporal validation and independent test cohorts, outperforming the ADC + KH (0.855, 0.890), ADC (0.829, 0.848), and KH (0.803, 0.757) models. Sensitivities were 93.8% and 94.1%, with negative predictive values (NPVs) of 92.7% and 95.2%, respectively. Calibration and decision curve analysis demonstrated good agreement and positive net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities.ConclusionThe nomogram integrating KH, ADC, margin, and age demonstrated superior diagnostic performance in preoperatively discriminating between benign and malignant breast mass lesions.Keywords: Breast cancer, DCE-MRI, Kinetic heterogeneity, Apparent diffusion coefficient, Nomogram

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide [1]. Therefore, early and accurate differentiation between benign and malignant breast lesions is critical for optimizing clinical management and improving patient outcomes [2]. Dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) is a key breast imaging modality that offers high soft tissue resolution and sensitivity [3]. Notably, it provides both morphological details and kinetic information, particularly through the time-signal intensity curve (TIC) [4]. Conventional TIC analysis based on the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) offers valuable diagnostic insights but is limited by subjectivity and inter-observer variability [5]. The TIC type for a given lesion varies depending on where radiologists place the regions of interest (ROIs). Furthermore, the Type II curve exhibits substantial overlap between benign and malignant lesions, posing a diagnostic dilemma in clinical practice [6], [7].

The biological basis for these diagnostic limitations is intratumoral heterogeneity, which manifests as variations in cellular density, molecular expression, and particularly regional differences in vascular perfusion [8], [9]. Such heterogeneity may also lead to significant variability in therapeutic sensitivity, resulting in unpredictable clinical outcomes and contributing to treatment resistance in certain patient populations [10]. Spatial differences in cellular proliferation and invasive potential across tumor subregions further complicate accurate prognosis estimation and long-term survival prediction [11]. Therefore, the accurate quantification of heterogeneity is crucial for improving clinical outcomes [12], [13]. However, the current methods for quantifying intratumoral heterogeneity have significant limitations. Histopathological techniques require invasive procedures such as biopsy or surgery [8], whereas computational approaches such as radiomics are limited by complex post-processing workflows and numerous parameters [14], [15], [16], [17], [18].

To address these challenges, Kim et al. developed a relatively straightforward method for quantifying intratumoral heterogeneity using a parameter called kinetic heterogeneity (KH) [19]. This approach uses commercial computer-aided diagnostic systems to classify all the enhancing voxels within a lesion into three distinct TIC patterns: wash-out, plateau, and persistent. Subsequent studies have shown that higher KH correlates with more aggressive tumor behavior, including reduced distant metastasis-free survival, heterogeneous responses to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and associations with specific molecular subtypes such as triple-negative and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-enriched breast cancers [20], [21], [22]. These results support the role of KH as a non-invasive imaging biomarker for predicting prognosis, assessing therapeutic response, and characterizing tumor subtypes.

In this study, we aimed to address the following issues. First, although previous studies have supported the value of KH staining in distinguishing benign from malignant breast tumors [23], further validation of its independent diagnostic value across diverse clinical cohorts is required. Second, whether combining KH with conventional predictors improves diagnostic performance remains to be further investigated [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]. Therefore, we developed a more comprehensive and clinically applicable diagnostic tool for preoperative decision-making.

Methods

Patient cohorts

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of our hospital, and the requirement for informed consent was waived (No. XHEC-C−2025–219–1). We retrospectively reviewed 371 patients with 399 breast mass lesions identified between February 2018 and October 2019 using a Philips 3.0 T scanner. These patients were randomly allocated to a training cohort (280 lesions) and an internal validation cohort (119 lesions) in a 7:3 ratio using the R software (version 4.4.2). A temporal validation cohort was subsequently collected using the same scanner, comprising 94 patients with 101 mass lesions who were consecutively reviewed between July and December 2024. In addition, an independent test cohort was collected from another campus of our hospital using a different GE 3.0 T scanner, consisting of 41 lesions from 36 patients between November 2025 and May 2026. In the entire cohort, 40 patients presented with bilateral synchronous breast lesions.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) women with mass breast lesions identified on preoperative DCE-MRI; (2) MRI performed for diagnostic indications; and (3) availability of pathological confirmation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of biopsy or neoadjuvant therapy prior to MRI, (2) poor image quality, and (3) incomplete clinicopathological records. MR imaging techniques

All MRI examinations were performed using a 3.0 T whole-body system (Ingenia, Philips, Netherlands, for patients in the training, internal validation, and temporal validation cohorts; Premier, GE Healthcare, USA, for patients in the independent test cohort). Patients were positioned prone, with both breasts placed in an eight-channel phase-array breast coil. The protocol included axial T2-weighted imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and DCE-MRI. For DCE-MRI, the differential subsampling with cartesian ordering (DISCO) flex sequence on the Premier and the enhanced T1 high-resolution isotropic volume excitation (e-THRIVE) sequence on the Ingenia were acquired before and after intravenous injection of 0.1 mmol/kg gadopentetate dimeglumine (Gd-DTPA; Beilu, Beijing, China) at a flow rate of 2.0 mL/s, followed by a 20 mL saline flush. Imaging analysis, post-processing, and lesion segmentation

Two breast radiologists (readers 1 and 2, with 4 and 10 years of experience in breast MRI, respectively) reviewed all MRI features by consensus according to the BI-RADS MRI lexicon (2013 edition), including lesion size, shape, margin, internal enhancement, and background parenchymal enhancement (BPE). Disagreements were resolved by a third senior radiologist (>15 years of experience).

The mean apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) (×10−3 mm²/s) was measured on a Philips IntelliSpace Portal or GE Advantage workstation by placing an ROI on the ADC map, guided by T2-weighted and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images, to avoid necrotic or cystic areas. A separate ROI was placed over the area of the strongest early enhancement identified on the second post-contrast T1-weighted image to generate the TIC. All measurements were reviewed by the senior radiologist.

To obtain the volume of interest (VOI), the cases were randomly assigned to Readers 1 and 2, who independently performed semiautomatic tumor segmentation using ITK-SNAP (version 3.8). Ambiguous boundaries were determined by the senior radiologist. All readers were blinded to the clinical and histopathological data. Reproducibility was assessed on a random subset of 50 cases using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with values > 0.75 and > 0.9, indicating good and excellent reproducibility, respectively.

Kinetic heterogeneity analysis

Following the method described by Kim et al. [19], [20], [21], [22], an automated analysis pipeline on an open-source Python platform (version 3.12) was then applied to extract the following quantitative parameters automatically: (1) peak enhancement, defined as the maximum voxel signal intensity in the second post-contrast phase; (2) enhancement volume, defined as the number of voxels exhibiting a > 50% signal increase relative to the pre-contrast phase; and (3) voxel-type classification, in which voxels were categorized as wash-out (>10% decrease between the second and last post-contrast phases), plateau (±10% change), or persistent (>10% increase). Model development and performance evaluation

Univariate logistic regression was used to identify potential risk factors for breast cancer; variables with P < 0.05 were included for further analysis. Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify the independent predictors of breast cancer.

Based on the final multivariable model, a diagnostic nomogram was developed and validated internally and temporally using the "rms" package in R. Model robustness was assessed via bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations). Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC), calibration curve, and decision curve analysis. Diagnostic accuracy was further quantified using the area under the ROC (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using R 4.4.2. Continuous data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the t-test. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data are presented as n (%) and were compared using the Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics and univariate analysis

This study retrospectively analyzed 541 pathologically confirmed breast mass lesions (262 benign and 279 malignant). The detailed pathological classifications of all cohorts are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The datasets included a training cohort (n = 280), an internal validation cohort (n = 119), a temporal validation cohort (n = 101), and an independent test cohort (n = 41).

Interobserver agreement was excellent for both ADC measurements (ICC=0.904; 95% CI, 0.811–0.951) and VOI segmentation (ICC=0.972, 95% CI: 0.951–0.984). Comparative analysis revealed that malignant lesions were significantly associated with older age, postmenopausal status, and larger size (all P < 0.001). The key imaging differential features for malignancies included irregular or spiculated margins, irregular shape, lower ADC, type III curve pattern, higher peak enhancement, larger enhancement volume, and higher KH (all P < 0.001). Development of a nomogram to differentiate benign from malignant breast mass lesions

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified four independent risk factors for differentiating benign from malignant breast mass lesions: age (odds ratio [OR]=1.08, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.05–1.12, P < 0.001), margin (OR=4.61, 95% CI: 1.80–11.84, P = 0.001), ADC (OR=0.03, 95% CI: 0.01–0.14, P < 0.001), and KH (OR=15.31, 95% CI: 4.23–55.52, P < 0.001; Table 3; Fig. 2A).

Performance evaluation of the nomogram and comparison models

Our nomogram consistently outperformed all comparator models across all cohorts (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figure 1A; Table 4). It achieved AUCs of 0.897 (95% CI: 0.838–0.955) in the temporal validation cohort and 0.909 (95% CI: 0.811–1.000) in the independent test cohort, with NPVs of 92.7% and 95.2% and sensitivities of 93.8% and 94.1%, respectively. The superior performance was further supported by well-fitted calibration curves (Fig. 4, Supplementary Figure 1B). Bootstrap internal validation yielded an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.947 (95% CI: 0.923–0.974), indicating minimal overfitting.

Clinical use

The nomogram demonstrated consistently superior clinical utility in decision curve analysis across all cohorts, outperforming comparator strategies across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 5, Supplementary Figure 1C). At the optimal cutoff of 0.856, the model correctly identified 71.7% of benign mass lesions in the temporal validation cohort and 83.3% in the independent test cohort. Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a multifactorial nomogram that integrates KH, ADC, and key morphological and clinical features. This tool was designed to preoperatively discriminate between benign and malignant breast mass lesions. The nomogram showed a consistently superior performance across the training, internal validation, temporal validation, and independent test cohorts. In both the temporal validation and independent test cohorts, the nomogram achieved promising AUCs (0.897 and 0.909, respectively), supporting its generalizability.

KH is derived from DCE-MRI TIC and quantifies the spatial distribution of voxel-based kinetic patterns, including persistent, plateau, and wash-out, across the entire tumor volume [19]. It reflects the underlying irregularities in tumor angiogenesis, perfusion, and permeability, which are characteristic of malignant behavior [25]. KH serves as a non-invasive biomarker that captures the complexity of the tumor microenvironment and provides insights into intratumoral biological heterogeneity [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]. Previous studies, such as that by Yao et al. [23], have established its value in distinguishing benign from malignant breast tumors. Our study substantially reinforces the evidence supporting KH as a reliable imaging biomarker for clinical use, achieved through a multicohort validation framework that included internal validation, temporal validation, and independent test cohorts. KH mapping not only aids in diagnostic differentiation but also provides critical visual guidance for targeting the most aggressive tumor regions. These high-risk areas are characterized by excessive secretion of pro-angiogenic factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor, leading to the formation of numerous yet structurally abnormal and leaky neovasculatures. The heightened permeability of these malformed vessels facilitates rapid contrast inflow (wash-in), whereas the elevated interstitial fluid pressure promotes swift contrast reflux (wash-out), as illustrated in Fig. 6 H [26], [27], [28]. By visualizing the spatial distribution of hemodynamic subtypes, KH mapping precisely guides biopsies toward viable high-grade tissues, while avoiding necrotic or cystic regions, particularly in large or heterogeneous masses [29]. This targeted approach reduces sampling errors and enhances the diagnostic accuracy of pathological grading.

Similarly, ADC measures water diffusion restriction and serves as an indicator of tumor cellularity and membrane integrity. Its clinical utility is well-established [30], [31], [32]. Our study extends these findings by showing that combining KH and ADC, which integrates hemodynamic and cellular characteristics, yields superior diagnostic performance compared with either parameter alone. This synergistic effect is biologically plausible since malignant lesions often exhibit both aberrant angiogenesis and increased cellular density [33], [34]. In the independent test cohort, the ADC model achieved an AUC of 0.848, the KH model of 0.757, and the combined model reached 0.890, further supporting the added value of combining these two parameters. Thus, this combination provides complementary pathways for discrimination and bridging of functional and structural information.

The incorporation of patient age and lesion margin resulted in a nomogram that may help rule out malignancy. In both the temporal validation and independent test cohorts, the nomogram demonstrated high diagnostic performance (AUCs of 0.897 and 0.909, respectively), correctly identifying 71.7% and 83.3% of benign lesions at the optimal cutoff, with high sensitivities (93.8% and 94.1%, respectively) and NPVs (92.7% and 95.2%, respectively), suggesting its potential to assist in clinical decision-making. The decision curve analysis further supported its clinical utility, showing a wide range of threshold probabilities with net benefits. Moreover, with only four readily available predictors, this nomogram offers greater interpretability and clinical applicability than "black-box" alternatives, such as radiomics or deep learning models [35].

This study had several limitations. First, this retrospective single-center study included only mass lesions and lacked external multicenter validation. Although we collected a small independent test cohort from another campus of our hospital using a different scanner, future prospective multicenter studies including non-mass-enhancement lesions are needed to confirm the generalizability of our nomogram. Second, 40 patients in our cohort had bilateral lesions, which may have introduced clustering effects due to multiple lesions originating from the same patient [36], although a sensitivity analysis was performed. Third, the potential of KH mapping to guide targeted biopsies of the most aggressive tumor regions remains unverified. Prospective studies correlating KH mapping with specific biopsy sites and their corresponding histopathological results are required to validate its clinical utility for precise diagnosis. Fourth, future research could explore the integration of deep learning with habitat imaging to characterize tumor heterogeneity, employing explainable methods such as SHapley Additive explanations (SHAP) to interpret the models and enhance their clinical credibility [37], [38], along with fully automated segmentation approaches to further improve workflow integration and facilitate clinical translation.

Conclusions

The proposed nomogram, which integrated KH, ADC, margin, and age, demonstrated favorable discriminatory performance and potential clinical utility for the preoperative classification of breast mass lesions.
