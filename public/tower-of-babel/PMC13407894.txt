Regulation of H+ transfer pathways promotes C-C coupling in acidic CO2 electroreduction
Authors: Qi Jin, Yun Yang, Dongao Zhang, Xiaodong Yi, Guoxiong Wang, Zhou Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction in acidic media is fundamentally limited by competitive hydrogen evolution and poor selectivity toward multi-carbon products. Here we report a cooperative catalytic strategy that enables highly selective multi-carbon formation under strongly acidic conditions by regulating proton activation and interfacial solvation. By exploiting the copper oxide nanosheets and integrating an immobilized cobalt tetra(4-carboxyphenyl)porphyrin, a dual-functional interface is constructed that steers abundant protons toward productive C–C bond formation. This synergy sustains C-C coupling at high current densities and suppresses parasitic hydrogen evolution. As a result, the hybrid catalyst delivers a multi-carbon Faradaic efficiency of up to 89.5% with partial current densities exceeding 1 A cm−2 in pH 2 ± 0.1 electrolyte, and maintains high selectivity even at pH 0.7 ± 0.05. Operando spectroscopic analyses and theoretical calculations reveal that regulated proton activation enables efficient formation of key C-C coupling intermediates and stabilizes the catalytic interface under acidic conditions. These results demonstrate a viable route to achieve efficient CO2 electroreduction to multi-carbon products in acidic environments through molecular control of proton utilization.

Introduction

Electrochemical carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CO2RR) offers a sustainable route to convert CO2 into value-added fuels and chemicals using renewable electricity1–4. Among the various product categories, multi-carbon (C2+) products such as ethylene, ethanol, and acetate are particularly attractive due to their high energy density and industrial relevance. From a practical standpoint, acidic electrolytes are highly desirable for CO2 electroreduction. Acidic systems mitigate carbonate accumulation, and improve carbon utilization efficiency. Moreover, acidic operation is compatible with gas-diffusion electrodes and high current densities5–8, which are essential for scalable CO2RR. Despite these advantages, acidic CO2RR has long been plagued by poor selectivity toward C2+ products, with hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) overwhelmingly dominating the reaction pathway. The intrinsic difficulty of acidic CO2RR originates from the paradoxical role of protons at the electrified interface. Protons are indispensable reactants in proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) steps required for CO2 activation and hydrogenation. Simultaneously, the high proton activity in acidic media kinetically favors HER9–11. Considerable efforts have been devoted to overcoming this limitation. Strategies including catalyst nanostructuring, facet engineering, electrolyte cation effects, and surface modification with molecular or polymeric layers have achieved incremental improvements in acidic CO2RR12–17. Many of these approaches focus on physically or electrostatically suppressing proton access to active sites, thereby weakening HER. However, such suppression-based strategies often introduce additional trade-offs, including reduced reaction kinetics, limited mass transport, or instability under high current densities. More fundamentally, they treat protons as an undesired species to be excluded, rather than as reactive intermediates whose behavior could be selectively controlled.

Emerging evidence suggests that the key challenge in acidic CO2RR is not proton abundance itself, but the lack of control over proton activation pathways, interfacial solvation, and hydrogen transfer dynamics. At the electrified interface, proton transport, water structure, and electric field effects are intimately coupled. Interfacial water molecules mediate proton delivery, participate in water dissociation, and stabilize charged reaction intermediates through hydrogen bonding and solvation. In parallel, recent studies18–20 have highlighted the potential involvement of highly reactive hydrogen species, such as adsorbed *H or transient hydrogen radicals (·H), in steering CO2RR pathways. Unlike *H that directly recombines to form H2, selectively generated ·H can participate in hydrogenation steps of CO2-derived intermediates, bypassing high-energy PCET barriers. Nevertheless, in conventional acidic systems, uncontrolled proton reduction rapidly channels these species into HER, preventing their productive participation in C-C coupling chemistry. How to generate, stabilize, and direct such reactive hydrogen species toward CO2RR, while suppressing parasitic HER, remains largely unexplored.

Here, we report a synergistic molecular–metallic catalytic strategy that addresses these challenges by simultaneously regulating proton activation and interfacial water structure in acidic CO2RR. By exploiting the intense local electric field generated at the tips of CuO nanosheets and introducing an immobilized organometallic porphyrin complex, TCPP(Co), we construct a cooperative catalytic interface with spatially separated yet functionally coupled active sites. The molecular Co centers preferentially activate protons via the Volmer step to generate transient hydrogen radicals, while adjacent Cu sites facilitate CO hydrogenation and asymmetric C-C coupling (Fig. 1). Crucially, the extended π-conjugated macrocycle of TCPP(Co) perturbs the ordering of interfacial water, weakening hydrogen-bond networks and promoting water dissociation under high reaction rates. This effect sustains local proton supply while avoiding excessive surface *H accumulation that would otherwise favor HER. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations point to the Volmer free energy (ΔGV) as a key descriptor for screening synergistic sites that optimize *H coverage and promote *CHO formation, a critical intermediate for asymmetric *CHO–*CO coupling. As a result, the TCPP(Co)-modified CuO nanosheets achieve a C2+ selectivity of 89.5% and a C2+ partial current density exceeding 1 A cm−2 in acidic electrolytes, sustaining stable operation for 100 h with significantly improved carbon utilization efficiency. Beyond performance metrics, this work establishes a conceptual framework for acidic CO2RR in which protons are actively harnessed rather than suppressed, and interfacial water is treated as a dynamic participant rather than a passive solvent. This molecular-level regulation of proton transfer and solvation provides a generalizable strategy for enabling selective C-C coupling in proton-rich electrochemical environments.

Results

Theoretical calculation of TCPP(M) modification for acidic CO2RR

To gain mechanistic insight into the CO2RR pathway, it is essential to establish appropriate catalyst surface models. Based on extensive prior research21, the Cu(100) facet (Cu(100)) was selected as the primary model, as it is widely recognized as the dominant C-C coupling site under realistic reaction conditions. For the thermodynamic analysis of C-C coupling, benzoic acid (BZA) was employed as a simplified anchoring ligand on the Cu(100) surface to maintain computational tractability. While this represents an idealized model compared to the dynamic operando interface, this simplification provides a useful baseline to understand how carboxylate-Cu coordination modulates the local interfacial electronic structure, whereas the spatially separated porphyrin macrocycle and metal center do not directly participate in Cu-C intermediate binding. Importantly, while the BZA-Cu(100) model is sufficient for describing the intrinsic coupling thermodynamics, the steric/hydrophobic effects of the bulky macrocycle and the Volmer step are explicitly accounted for in our ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) and unit-cell DFT calculations utilizing the full TCPP(Co) complex. Bader charge density analyses were conducted to elucidate electronic interactions within this model system. The results reveal that bidentate coordination of BZA partially oxidizes surface Cu atoms, yielding a charged Cuδ+ state (Supplementary Fig. 1). Consistent with previous reports, such Cuδ+ species play a pivotal role in promoting C2+ products formation22,23.

To elucidate the thermodynamic preferences of the CO2RR pathway on Cu(100) and BZA-Cu(100) surfaces, the reaction free energies were calculated for the PCET steps involved in CO2/CO hydrogenation, as well as for the C-C coupling pathways (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Both models exhibit a consistent energetic trend. Specifically, the *CHO intermediate is thermodynamically favors over *COH intermediate, and the free energy required for *CO hydrogenation to *CHO (0.70/0.75 eV) is lower than that for direct *CO–*CO coupling (0.91/1.04 eV). These simplified models suggest that *CO may preferentially undergoes hydrogenation to *CHO rather than direct *CO–*CO coupling. Moreover, asymmetric *CHO–*CO coupling displays a markedly lower reaction free energy (− 0.65/− 0.55 eV) compared with *CO–*CO coupling (0.91/1.04 eV), implying a possible energetic preference for the *CHO–*CO pathway and highlighting *CHO formation as the key rate-determining step governing C-C coupling efficiency (Fig. 2a).Fig. 2Theoretical calculations of the synergistic reaction mechanism.a Reaction free energy profiles for *CO–*CO and *CHO–*CO coupling pathways. b Kinetic barriers for *CO hydrogenation to *CHO using either adsorbed *H or hydrated H3O+ as the hydrogen source (IS: Initial state, TS: Transition state, FS: Final state). c ∆GV on various metal porphyrins. d Schematic illustration of the dual-site synergistic mechanism. e Free energy profiles of the CO2RR pathway on Cu(100) and BZA–Cu(100) surfaces post *CHO–*CO coupling. The kinetic barriers for *CO hydrogenation to *CHO on BZA–Cu(100) were further compared using either adsorbed *H or Hydronium ion (H3O+) as the hydrogen source (Fig. 2b). The *H-mediated pathway exhibits a substantially lower activation barrier (0.88 eV), facilitating *CHO formation and accelerating subsequent asymmetric coupling. In contrast, when H3O+ acts as the hydrogen donor, *CHO formation becomes kinetics unfavorable, with an activation barrier of 1.13 eV. In the absence of efficient *CHO generation, C-C coupling proceeds via the high-free-energy *CO-*CO coupling pathway, accompanied by the dominant HER. Notably, the formation of adsorbed *H via interaction between high-energy ·H (3.75 eV) and Cu(100) is associated with a negligible reaction free energy. This observation underscores the necessity of an appropriate descriptor to evaluate the ·H enrichment capability of synergistic catalytic sites with different metal centers.

Accordingly, the free energy of the Volmer step (∆GV) was employed as a descriptor to assess the ·H-enrichment capability of a series of metal porphyrins (metal = Cu, Pd, Ni, Co, Ag, and Pt). These porphyrin-based systems feature atomically isolated MN4 sites and are reported to maintain structural integrity under acidic CO2RR conditions24–26. Importantly, confinement by the porphyrin macrocycle suppresses the formation of adjacent sites required for the Tafel step (*H–*H coupling), thereby inhibiting HER (Supplementary Fig. 4). Among the evaluated metal centers, Co–porphyrin exhibits a ∆GV value of 0.416 eV, closest to the thermodynamic ideal of 0 eV, indicating the most favorable Volmer step activation and efficient *H desorption for ·H enrichment (Fig. 2c). To elucidate the intrinsic mechanism of H* spillover from the Co site, comprehensive electronic structure analyses were performed. Differential charge density and Bader charge calculations reveal an electron transfer of ~ 0.06e- from H to Co. The resulting polarized Co-H bond (Hδ+) induces Coulombic repulsion, providing the thermodynamic driving force for H* migration toward the more electronegative Cu surface or porphyrin nitrogen atoms. Furthermore, Crystal Orbital Hamilton Population (COHP) analysis yields an integrated COHP (ICOHP) of 1.06 up to the Fermi level. This moderate Co–H bond strength facilitates initial H adsorption without causing excessive stabilization, offering a plausible rationale for the kinetic accessibility and thermodynamic favorability of the H* spillover, Specifically, free energy profiling of the spillover pathway demonstrates that, despite a minor initial uphill step toward the porphyrin nitrogen (0.28 eV), the overall migration of *H from the Co center to the Cu surface is thermodynamically downhill with a net driving force of – 0.76 eV, enabling it to readily participate in the subsequent hydrogenation of *CO to *CHO (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Note 1).

Based on these insights, a synergistic dual-site reaction mechanism is proposed (Fig. 2d). An ·H-enriched microenvironment is established by immobilized organometallic complex sites through activation of the Volmer step. In Pathway 1, high-energy ·H directly attacks CO2 molecules to form ·COOH intermediates. In Pathway 2, ·H undergoes barrierless adsorption on Cu sites to generate *H, which subsequently hydrogenates *CO to produce *CHO, enabling asymmetric *CHO–*CO coupling27. Concurrently, HER is suppressed owing to the spatial confinement of isolated metal sites.

In the conventional PCET pathway, *COOH formation requires a relatively high reaction free energy of approximately 0.89 eV. By contrast, in the presence of ·H, the initial hydrogenation of CO2 proceeds with a substantially lower reaction free energy of − 0.53 eV. These theoretical results imply that the ·H generation capability of TCPP(Co) could aid *COOH formation, thereby promoting *CO generation and subsequent C-C coupling steps (Supplementary Fig. 6).

In addition, comparison of the reaction free energy profiles for *CHO–*CO asymmetric coupling toward C2H4 formation on Cu(100) and BZA–Cu(100) surfaces reveals that BZA coordination effectively tunes the charge density of surface Cu atom and their local coordination environment. This electronic modulation lowers the free energy of *CHO–*CO coupling from − 0.55 eV to − 0.65 eV (Fig. 2a) and further reduces the reaction free energy of the subsequent rate-determining step (RDS) from 0.117 eV to 0.094 eV (Fig. 2e). Free energy profiles for C2H4 and C2H5OH formation on BZA–Cu(100) indicate that the RDS for the C2H5OH pathway requires a substantially higher reaction free energy (0.365 eV) than that for the C2H4 pathway (0.094 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 7). Consequently, the catalyst thermodynamically favors C2H4 formation over C2H5OH. Schematic adsorption configurations of the key coupling intermediates on Cu(100) and BZA–Cu(100) surfaces are provided in the Supplementary Figs. 8–10.

Synthesis and structural characterization of TCPP(M)–CuO

To achieve lower-overpotential CO2RR, CuO NS capable of generating tip-enhanced electric fields were employed as the catalyst substrate28,29. The synergistic sites were anchored onto the Cu surface via in situ formed bidentate coordination during electroreduction between the four benzoic acid-like units of TCPP(Co) and the CuO NS.

The as-synthesized electrocatalyst was deposited onto carbon paper. The carbon paper consists of a porous carbon layer and a nanocarbon layer, a structure that endows the electrode with high gas permeability and hydrophobicity, making it suitable for use as a gas diffusion layer (GDL). For long-term stability tests, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) was employed as the substrate instead of carbon paper. To assess the influence of CuO morphology on electrochemical behavior, commercial CuO particles were used as a reference. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 11) show that the commercial particles exhibit relatively flat geometries, whereas CuO NS exhibit a sea-urchin-like architecture composed of radially assembled nanosheets with abundant sharp tips. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image (Supplementary Fig. 12) further reveals the microscale, high-curvature cross-sectional features of these nanosheets. Finite element method (FEM) simulations of the surface microenvironment, based on the HRTEM image (Fig. 3b; details parameters in Supplementary Table 1), demonstrate that at an applied potential of − 1.0 V vs RHE, the tip-enhancement effect of CuO NSs induces a strong electric field (~ 107 V·m−1) on the surface. This promotes also significant enrichment of potassium ions (K+) at the interface (Supplementary Fig. 13). This observation is in agreement with earlier reports30. Notably, although prolonged CO2RR leads to a slight increase in the radius of curvature of the CuO NS, distinct sharp features are preserved (Supplementary Fig. 14), suggesting sustained tip-enhanced electric field effects that contribute to maintaining low reaction overpotentials.

During the synthesis of TCPP(M)-CuO, TCPP(M) initially adsorbs onto the CuO NS surface via van der Waals interactions or weak monodentate coordination between the terminal carboxyl groups (-COO-) and Cu2+ within the lattice31. The planar porphyrin core and outward-oriented benzoic acid groups introduce steric hindrance, suppressing intermolecular aggregation and promoting uniform molecular dispersion on the CuO NS surface32. The molecular structures of the synthesized TCPP(M) (M = Co, Cu, Ag, and Ni) were verified by attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR), with metal-free H2TCPP used as a reference (Supplementary Fig. 15). Characteristic peaks at 1606 cm−1 and 1403 cm−1 corresponding to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the –COO- groups, respectively, are observed in both TCPP(M) and H2TCPP. Notably, coordination of the metal center induces pronounced spectral changes. The N–H-related signal present in H2TCPP disappears upon metal insertion, consistent with replacement of pyrrolic protons by metal ions and the resulting modification of the nitrogen electronic environment. Concurrently, a characteristic band near 1050 cm−1 emerges, attributable to M–N bond formation33,34. Slight shifts in the M–N vibration frequency are observed among different metal centers (Co, Cu, Ag, and Ni), reflecting variations in electronic structure and coordination geometry.

The structural integrity of TCPP(Co) was further confirmed by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy in CDCl3 (Fig. 3c), which displays symmetric doublets at 8.45 ppm and 8.30 ppm assigned to the sixteen aromatic protons on the phenyl rings, as well as a resonance at 8.84 ppm corresponding to the eight β-pyrrolic protons35,36. Furthermore, extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis reveals a dominant Co–N path without any Co–Co scattering signals, confirming the strict atomic dispersion of the Co centers. This single-atom nature is visually corroborated by aberration-corrected STEM (AC-STEM) and elemental mapping, which demonstrate a highly uniform distribution of isolated Co atoms across the support without any metallic aggregation (Supplementary Fig. 16a–d). ATR-FTIR spectra of TCo–CuO NS reveal identical –COO- vibrational features to those of pristine TCPP(Co) (Fig. 3d), confirming successful immobilization of TCPP(Co) on the CuO surface. HRTEM analysis further indicates that TCPP(Co) molecules are initially anchored on the predominant CuO(200) facet via van der Waals forces or weak monodentate coordination prior to CO2RR (Supplementary Fig. 17a), with the molecular overlayer clearly distinguishable (Supplementary Fig. 17b). Post-CO2RR ATR-FTIR analysis demonstrates the persistence of surface –COO- groups after in situ electroreduction (Supplementary Fig. 18a, b). The separation between asymmetric and symmetric –COO- stretching modes (Δν) decreases from 209 cm−1 to 171/178 cm−1, indicative of a dynamic structural evolution into a highly stable bidentate coordination between in situ-generated metallic Cu and TCPP(Co)37, which is computationally verified to be thermodynamically more stable than the monodentate mode by 0.35 eV (Supplementary Fig. 18c, d). This coordination evolution confirms the structural robustness of TCPP(Co) under operational current densities. The uniform distribution of TCPP(Co) on the CuO NS is evident prior to the reaction (Supplementary Fig. 19) and is maintained after CO2RR, as confirmed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Supplementary Fig. 20). Moreover, post-stability test analyses demonstrate the robust nature of the catalyst. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) EDS elemental mapping confirms the persistent uniform dispersion of the Co species after 100 h of continuous operation (Supplementary Fig. 21). XPS spectra verify the sustained presence of the Co centers (Supplementary Fig. 22a, b and Supplementary Note 2), and HRTEM images reveal the intact amorphous molecular overlayer, confirming the preservation of the molecular-metal interface during prolonged electrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 22c, d and Supplementary Note 3).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis further elucidates the elemental composition and coordination environment of TCPP(Co) (Supplementary Fig. 23). Deconvolution of the N 1 s core-level spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 24) reveals components at 397.4 eV (Co–N coordination), 400.5 eV (pyrrolic – N = ), and 402.4 eV (bridging –N–). Corresponding Co 2p and N 1 s signals are clearly observed in TCo–CuO NS, whereas unmodified CuO NS exhibits no detectable Co or N signals (Fig. 3e). To determine the precise absolute loading, inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) was performed, revealing an actual bulk Co/Cu atomic ratio of 0.50% (Supplementary Table 2). Crucially, semi-quantitative XPS analysis reveals a surface Co proportion of 3.37%, significantly exceeding the theoretical bulk ratio (~0.94%), which directly confirms the successful surface enrichment and molecular anchoring of TCPP(Co) (Supplementary Table 3). Additional structural validation is provided by comparison with the XPS spectra of HKUST-1 (Supplementary Fig. 25)38.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis shows that TCPP(Co) modification does not alter the CuO NS lattice structure and that no diffraction peaks associated with Co clusters are detected (Supplementary Fig. 26). Notably, TCPP(Co) significantly influences the dynamic restructuring behavior of CuO NS during CO2RR. XRD patterns collected after exposure of post-reaction TCo–CuO NS to air reveal complete oxidation of in situ-generated metallic Cu to Cu2O/CuO phases, whereas unmodified CuO NS retains a fraction of metallic Cu under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 27). Previous studies have shown that highly active Cu0 species are readily oxidized upon air exposure39, suggesting that TCPP(Co) modification promotes the formation of reactive Cu0 species during electroreduction. Ex situ selected-area electron diffraction of post-reaction TCo–CuO NS further confirms this phase evolution, in good agreement with the XRD results (Supplementary Fig. 28).

Given that the Cu(100) facet widely recognized as a key active site for C2+ product formation during CO2RR, OH- adsorption experiments were designed to probe the distinct oxidation sensitivity of the electrochemically in situ-generated Cu0 following molecular modification. These experiments demonstrate that TCPP(Co) modification promotes the in situ electrochemical formation of highly active Cu(100) facets (Supplementary Fig. 29; see Experimental Section for details). Cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles exhibit characteristic redox features associated with specific Cu crystal facets within defined potential windows. Notably, the in situ generated metallic Cu in TCo–CuO NS displays a substantially higher fraction of the Cu(100) facet compared with unmodified CuO NS. Quantitative deconvolution of the CV peaks reveals a Cu(100) contribution of 55.9% for TCo–CuO NS, markedly exceeding the 33.8% observed for CuO NS (Supplementary Fig. 30 and Supplementary Table 4), confirming that TCPP(Co) modification preferentially stabilizes and exposes the catalytically active Cu(100) facet.

Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 3f) show that, at a normalized absorption intensity of 0.5, the edge position of CuO NS coincides with that of the CuO reference, consistent with a Cu2+ oxidation state. In contrast, TCo–CuO NS exhibits a positive shift in the absorption edge, which can be attributed to electron redistribution arising from monodentate coordination between carboxylate oxygen atoms and Cu sites. EXAFS analysis reveals dominant Cu–O scattering paths for both CuO NS and TCo–CuO NS (Supplementary Fig. 31). Quantitative fitting indicates no appreciable change in Cu–O coordination numbers (Cu–O1: 3.88 vs. 3.89; Cu–O2: 2.85 vs. 2.89; Figs. S32a-S32f and Supplementary Table 5). Concurrently, the d-band center of TCo–CuO NS shifts from 3.92 eV to 4.42 eV toward higher energy (Supplementary Fig. 33), providing further evidence of electronic structure reconstruction of Cu sites induced by TCPP(Co) coordination. Collectively, these results demonstrate that molecular modification with TCPP(Co) effectively tunes the electronic structure and surface configuration of Cu sites through coordination effects, thereby enhancing their intrinsic catalytic activity toward CO2RR.

Acidic electrocatalytic CO2RR performance

The pronounced tip-induced electric field effect associated with the CuO NS architecture effectively enriches K+ at the catalyst surface. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements were conducted in a pH 2 ± 0.1 electrolyte containing 1 M KCl to compare the electrochemical activity of catalysts with distinct morphologies (Supplementary Fig. 34). The CuO NS exhibits markedly enhanced electrochemical activity relative to commercial CuO particles under identical electrolyte conditions. Specifically, tip-enhanced CuO NS displays an onset potential of approximately − 0.8 V versus the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE), whereas commercial CuO particles show electrochemical activation only beyond − 1.8 V versus RHE. These results clearly demonstrate the morphology-dependent electrochemical activity of CuO and confirm the effectiveness of the nanosheet architecture in promoting interfacial K⁺ enrichment.

CO2RR performance was further evaluated in a three-electrode flow cell (schematic shown Supplementary Fig. 35) using pH 2 ± 0.1, 1 M KCl electrolyte (acidified with H2SO4) under galvanostatic conditions (see Experimental Section). Gaseous and liquid products were quantified by gas chromatography (GC) and 1H NMR spectroscopy, respectively. Control experiments performed under an Ar atmosphere confirmed the carbon origin of the products, as no carbon-containing species were detected in either phase. The Faradaic efficiency (FE) of C2+ products and H2 for TCo–CuO NS and CuO NS across 0.2–1.2 A cm−2 are summarized (Fig. 4a, b). At low current densities, HER is effectively suppressed for all samples, which can be attributed to the nanosheet-based substrate architecture. At higher current densities, however, the control catalysts exhibit a pronounced kinetic dominance of HER. In contrast, incorporation of TCPP(Co) markedly enhances C-C coupling efficiency compared with TCu–CuO NS, TAg–CuO NS, TNi-CuO NS, and CuO NS modified with H2TCPP (H2TCPP–CuO NS) (Supplementary Fig. 36a–e). C2H4 emerged as the dominant C2+ product during CO2RR, achieving maximum FEs of 69.9% for C2H4 and 89.5% for total C2+ products at 1 A cm−2. Notably, H2 FE remains below 10% across all current densities, while C2+ FE consistently exceeds 80% between 0.8–1.2 A cm−2. The C2+ product distribution mainly consists of C2H4, C2H5OH, CH3COOH, and C3H8O. At a total current density of 1.2 A cm−2 (Fig. 4c), TCo–CuO NS delivers C2+ and C2H4 partial current densities of 1.04 A cm−2 and 0.73 A cm−2, respectively. Based on the measured FEs, the C2+ production rate reaches 2.28 mmol h−1 cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 37). Subsequently, the intrinsic CO2RR performance of the four synthesized TCPP(M) complexes with different metal centers (M = Cu, Ag, Ni, and Co) was evaluated under potentiostatic conditions in 1 M KOH electrolyte using identical electrode preparation protocols (Supplementary Fig. 39). 1H NMR analysis revealed no detectable liquid-phase products for any of the complexes. TCPP(Co) and TCPP(Ag) predominantly produced CO, with maximum FEs exceeding 70%. In contrast, TCPP(Cu) generated CH4 alongside CO and H2, whereas TCPP(Ni) primarily catalyzed H2 evolution, indicating a pronounced influence of the metal center identity on reaction selectivity.

Further assessment of CO2RR performance for organic molecules and metal center-modified CuO NS under identical testing conditions demonstrates that TCo–CuO NS consistently exhibits higher C2+ across the 0.2–1 A cm−2 range compared to other samples (Fig. 4d). Relative to CuO NS, TCPP(M)-modified CuO NS exhibits moderately enhanced C2+ FE. This improvement is attributed to the formation of stabilized bidentate coordination formed in situ between surface –COO- groups and Cu sites under operating conditions, which modulates the electronic structure and promotes C-C coupling. To substantiate this effect, CV measurements were performed in 0.1 M KHCO3 (Supplementary Fig. 40) to probe the initial reduction behavior of the catalysts. The anodic and cathodic current peaks observed in the CV curves correspond to Cu redox transitions within defined potential windows, with the reduction peaks assigned to Cu2+/Cu+ and Cu+/Cu0 couples. Notably, TCo–CuO NS displays attenuated anodic and cathodic intensity by the 5th and 9th cycles, indicating an enhanced ability to retain partially oxidized Cu species during the initial electroreduction process. At current densities exceeding 0.4 A cm−2 in the pH 2 ± 0.1 electrolyte, all electrocatalysts except TCo–CuO NS display pronounced HER activity in the pH 2 ± 0.1 electrolyte, which becomes increasingly competitive at higher current densities. At 1 A cm−2, the H2 FE exceeds 30% for all four comparative electrocatalysts, while the CO FE remains largely invariant with current densities (Supplementary Fig. 41).

The influence of TCPP(Co) surface coverage on C2H4 selectivity during CO2RR was further investigated (Supplementary Fig. 42). CuO NS treated with a three-fold higher concentration of TCPP(Co) solution (denoted TCo–CuO NS-3) exhibits significantly enhanced C2H4 FE relative to unmodified CuO NS, although the improvement is slightly inferior to that of optimally TCo–CuO NS. This behavior is attributed to excessive surface coverage of TCPP(Co), which partially blocks electrochemically active Cu sites. This interpretation is supported by electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 43), which reveals significantly lower ECSA-normalized activity for TCo–CuO NS-3 compared with TCo–CuO NS.

To evaluate performance under more strongly acidic conditions, the CO2RR selectivity of TCo–CuO NS and CuO NS was galvanostatically evaluated in the range of 0.2-1 A cm−2 in pH 0.7 ± 0.05, 1 M KCl electrolyte (acidified with H2SO4) (Supplementary Fig. 44). Despite the inevitably enhanced HER under increased acidity, TCo–CuO NS maintains a C2H4 FE of 55.9 ± 4.8% and a high total C2+ FE of 81.4% at 0.8 A cm−2, showcasing notable robustness. Across the entire current density range, TCo–CuO NS exhibits competitive C2+ selectivity compared to CuO NS.

To assess practical energy consumption, the half-cell energy efficiency (EE1/2) was calculated based on the standard potentials of CO2RR and the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), with explicit consideration of ohmic losses. During galvanostatic operation, the measured versus Ag/AgCl at each sampling point was averaged and converted to the RHE scale (see Experimental Section in Supporting Information). TCo–CuO NS exhibits more positive (less negative) cathodic potentials across the current density range of 0.2–1.2 A cm−2 range (Supplementary Fig. 45), reaching − 2.55 V vs. RHE at 0.4 A cm−2. At this current density, the EE1/2 values for total C2+ products and ethylene reaches maximum values of 25.7% and 19.1%, respectively. In contrast, the C2+ EE1/2 of CuO NS remains below 16.5% throughout the investigated range (Fig. 4e). EE1/2 values for individual C2+ products over 0.2–1.2 A cm−2 are summarized (Supplementary Fig. 46).

The SPCE was employed to quantify carbon utilization during CO2RR. Figure 3f and Supplementary Fig. 47 present the CO2RR performance of TCo–CuO NS at CO2 flow rates ranging from 2 to 50 sccm in pH 2 ± 0.1, 1 M KCl. Lowering the CO2 flow rate reduces CO2 availability and partial pressure, thereby intensifying HER competition and diminishing C2H4 selectivity at flow rates below 10 sccm. At 1 A cm−2, while the selectivity toward liquid-phase C2+ products remains relatively stable with decreasing CO2 flow, the FE for gaseous C2H4 is significantly reduced. As the flow rate decreases, the C2+ SPCE reaches a maximum value of 40.5% at a CO2 feed rate of 2 sccm and a current density of 0.6 A cm−2 (Fig. 4f). Across the CO2 flow range of 10–50 sccm, the C2+ FE consistently exceeds 80% at 0.8 A cm−2, accompanied by effective suppression of HER (Supplementary Fig. 48). Long-term stability tests reveal no decay in the FE of C2H4, taken as a representative C2+ products, over 100 hours of continuous operation at 0.2 A cm−2 in pH 2 ± 0.1, 1 M KCl electrolyte (Fig. 4g). Furthermore, the system sustaining uninterrupted operation for 25 h even at a high current density of 0.6 A cm-2 (Supplementary Fig. 49). The performance achieved in this work is benchmarked against representative acidic CO2RR systems reported in recent literature (Supplementary Table 7)9,11,40–45.

Investigation of electrocatalytic mechanism

To elucidate the key radical intermediates involved in the CO2RR, in situ characterization was first performed using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy with 5,5-dimethyl−1-pyrroline N–oxide (DMPO) as the spin-trapping agent. Under an Ar atmosphere, at a current density of 0.1 A cm−2 in pH 2 ± 0.1, 1 M KCl electrolyte, TCo–CuO NS exhibited a characteristic nine-line DMPO–·H signal with an intensity ratio of approximately 1:1:2:1:2:1:2:1:1, confirming the generation of ·H (Fig. 5a). In contrast, no analogous signal is detected for either CuO NS or TAg–CuO NS, which experimentally indicates that TCPP(Co) sites serve as the primary centers for ·H enrichment. Upon subsequent CO2 purging (Fig. 5b), the DMPO–·H signal remains clearly observable, while a distinct signal corresponding to the carboxyl radical (·COOH) emerges simultaneously, this observation indicates that ∙H participates directly in CO2 activation. Furthermore, comparative in situ EPR measurements of bare CuO NS and TAg–CuO NS under the same CO2 atmosphere confirm the complete absence of this characteristic ∙H signal (Supplementary Fig. 50), unambiguously demonstrating that the Co metal center in TCPP(Co) is indispensable for generating the crucial ∙H radicals. The direct necessity of ·H in this pathway was further corroborated by a radical quenching experiment; the injection of a DMPO scavenger during continuous CO2RR induced a significant drop in C2+ FE (Supplementary Fig. 51), definitively confirming that ∙H is an essential intermediate for C2+ product formation rather than a byproduct of a parallel side reaction. To further rationalize this observation, DFT calculations on the idealized model suggested that the hydrogenation pathway toward ·COOH could be thermodynamically favorable (ΔG = − 0.53 eV). Consistent with this theoretical picture, DFT calculations further indicated that ·COOH likely exhibits strong adsorption on the BZA-Cu(100) surface, with an adsorption free energy of − 2.33 eV (Supplementary Fig. 6). Notably, *COOH acts as a key precursor to *CO, the critical intermediate for subsequent C-C coupling. For detailed insight into the interfacial reaction pathways, SERS measurements were conducted under CO2-saturated 0.5 M K2SO4/0.005 M H2SO4 electrolyte, allowing dynamically monitor surface species during the reaction. In the low-frequency region (250–750 cm-1; Fig. 5c, d), both TCo–CuO NS and CuO NS exhibit characteristic bands near 295 cm−1, attributed to the *CO and at 344/347 cm−1, corresponding to Cu–CO stretching vibrations. With increasingly negative potentials, these peaks decrease in intensity, indicative of *CO consumption via C-C coupling and/or protonation processes. Notably, TCo–CuO NS displays a pronounced tendency toward C-C coupling/CO hydrogenation already at – 1.2 V vs. RHE, earlier than observed for CuO NS. In addition, TCo–CuO NS shows distinct vibrations near 410 cm−1 (CuOx/OHx species), 608 cm−1 (CuOx), and 708 cm−1 (CO2-)46–48. These features indicate the persistence of oxidized copper-hydroxide complexes over a wide potential range, which is attributed to the stabilization of oxidized Cu species via -COO coordination.

In the high-frequency region (1000-2200 cm-1), the appearance of indistinguishable *CO stretching signals at identical applied potentials in the in situ SERS of both samples (Supplementary Fig. 52) suggests that the *CO adsorption strength is not the determining factor governing the enhanced selectivity toward multi-carbon products. In the low-pH bulk electrolyte, the formation of HCO3- and CO32- species is effectively suppressed (Supplementary Fig. 53). Notably, TCo–CuO NS exhibits peaks near 1334 cm−1 (in-plane symmetric stretch of *CO2-) and 1587 cm−1 (in-plane asymmetric stretch of *CO2-)49 at overpotentials as low as – 0.1 V vs. RHE, significantly earlier than those observed for CuO NS (– 0.5 V vs. RHE). As the applied potential becomes more negative, enrichment of ·H leads to the diminution of *CO2- signals. Conversely, bands corresponding to *COOH emerge near 1407 cm−1 and 1612 cm−1. According to our theoretical calculations, this intermediate is formed via a low-free-energy hydrogenation pathway. Concurrently, signals attributable to *CHO, the hydrogenation product of *CO, become evident, along with bands assigned to symmetric and antisymmetric stretching vibrations of *CCO (C-C coupling product) near 1186 cm−1 and 1338 cm−1 50. Furthermore, in situ SERS spectroscopy with D2O isotope exchange was conducted. It was observed that the characteristic peak of the H-containing intermediate *CHO shifted from 1139 to 1126 cm−1, while the *COOH peaks shifted from 1407 and 1612 cm−1 to 1390 and 1606 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 54). These spectroscopic observations correlate well with the radical intermediates captured by in situ EPR measurements.

Both TCo–CuO NS and CuO NS exhibit a characteristic *CHO vibrational feature near 1139 cm-1 51, however, their potential-dependent behaviors differ markedly (Fig. 5e, f). For CuO NS, *CHO signals appeared only within a narrow potential window at relatively positive potentials. Theoretical models suggest that at more negative potentials, activation of the Tafel step at adjacent Cu sites promotes preferential *H recombination via the low-barrier HER pathway, thereby suppressing *CHO availability. Furthermore, the DFT calculation results imply that in the absence of *CHO participation, direct *CO–*CO coupling on Cu(100) (∆G = 1.04 eV) becomes kinetically and thermodynamically unfavorable relative to HER, resulting in HER dominance at high cathodic potentials on CuO NS.

In contrast, TCo–CuO NS displays persistent *CHO signals across the entire investigated potential range, indicating that the spatially confined Co centers regulated the H+ transfer pathway via the Volmer step. This regulation enhanced ·H availability, accelerated *CO hydrogenation to *CHO, and thereby promotes asymmetric *CHO–*CO coupling with a low reaction free energy (∆G = – 0.65 eV), this pathway outcompetes HER, leading to enhanced selectivity toward C2+ products.

To further elucidate proton supply and mass transport behavior in acidic media, AIMD simulations were conducted to investigate H2O dynamics near the electrode interface (Fig. 6a, b). Under high reaction rates, H+ near the electrode surface is rapidly consumed. Owing to the limited diffusion rate of H+ in solution, replenishment from the bulk solution is insufficient, resulting in the formation of a thin proton depletion layer adjacent to the electrode. The extended π-conjugated aromatic ring of TCPP(Co) serves as a weak hydrogen bond acceptor, interacting with the hydrogen atoms of H2O. This interaction competitively weakens the stronger native O-H···O hydrogen bonds between H2O, thereby disrupting the ordering of the interfacial water network and promoting the formation of loosely bound, weakly hydrogen-bonded H2O. This unique aqueous state possesses higher system entropy and diminished hydrogen-bond confinement, which facilitates post-dissociation solvent reorganization without demanding a substantial entropy penalty, thus making the dissociation reaction (2H2O → H3O+ + OH-) entropically favored and thermodynamically more favorable52,53. To experimentally verify that the TCPP(Co) molecules can accelerate H2O dissociation and provide an adequate supply of H+, as indicated by AIMD simulations, the kinetic isotope effect (KIE) was investigated by replacing H2O with D2O during CO2RR in pH 2 ± 0.1, 1 M KCl electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 55). The KIE values exceeding unity across all electrodes suggest that H2O dissociation serves as one of the rate-determining steps in the context of dynamic H+ depletion. Notably, TCo–CuO NS exhibits KIE values closer to 1 at all applied potentials, indicating that the PCET process is less sensitive to isotopic substitution. This result demonstrates that the TCPP(Co) molecules reduce the ordering of interfacial water, thereby facilitating the H2O dissociation step to supply sufficient H+. Further experimental quantification of the TCPP(Co)-mediated hydrogen bond restructuring was conducted by analyzing the O–H stretching modes of interfacial water via SERS spectroscopy (Fig. 6c, d). The spectra were deconvoluted into three components corresponding to tetrahedrally coordinated water tetrahedrally coordinated water (4HB-H2O), two-hydrogen-bonded water (2HB-H2O), and free O–H groups, centered near 3200, 3400, and 3550 cm−1, respectively54–56. At − 0.2 V vs. RHE, free O–H signals are nearly absent (Fig. 6e, f). Upon shifting the potential to − 0.4 V, a pronounced free O–H signal emerges, accompanied by attenuation of ordered water features. Consistent with AIMD predictions, TCPP(Co) induces a transition toward a more disordered interfacial water network, resulting in a higher proportion of free water across the entire potential range. Under high-rate acidic conditions, interfacial water is not a passive solvent but an active kinetic regulator that governs proton flux and hydrogen availability at the reaction interface.

By integrating in situ EPR detection of radical intermediates (·H and ·COOH), in situ SERS identification of surface-bound species (*CO2-, *CHO, *CCO, *COOH, and interfacial H2O), and AIMD simulations of interfacial water structure, this study systematically validates that the TCPP(Co) synergistic center promotes the Volmer step, facilitates the formation of the key intermediate *CHO, and underpins the proposed mechanism of asymmetric coupling with low reaction free energy, while simultaneously optimizing H2O configuration to ensure sufficient H+ supply. Concurrently, the coordinatively stabilized Cuδ+ species is observed, which is computationally predicted to modulate the electronic structure and potentially reduce the computationally derived reaction free energy of the coupling step (0.094 eV vs. 0.117 eV). A schematic summary (Fig. 6g) effectively correlates the DFT-derived energetics with experimentally identified intermediates, illustrating how regulation of proton transfer and interfacial water structure governs CO2RR selectivity under acidic conditions. Together, these findings demonstrate that efficient C–C bond formation in acidic CO2RR can be achieved by decoupling proton activation from hydrogen evolution through molecular-level control of interfacial chemistry.

Discussion

This work demonstrates that the long-standing trade-off between proton-rich environments and selective C-C coupling in acidic CO2 electroreduction can be overcome by regulating proton transfer pathways rather than suppressing H+ availability. By integrating molecular proton-activation sites with metal catalysts, the proton is redirected away from parasitic hydrogen evolution and toward the formation of key C-C coupling intermediates. Beyond enhancing reaction kinetics, the immobilized molecular sites reorganize interfacial water structures, enabling sustained proton supply under high reaction rates and stabilizing catalytic performance at ampere-level current densities. This dual regulation of proton activation and solvation environment establishes asymmetric C-C coupling as a competitive pathway in acidic media. More broadly, this study identifies molecular control of proton utilization as a general design principle for overcoming selectivity limitations in proton-dominated electrocatalytic systems. The insights presented here provide a framework for rationally coupling molecular and heterogeneous catalysts to enable efficient, selective conversion of CO2 into value-added multi-carbon products under industrially relevant acidic conditions.

Methods

Synthesis of CuO nanosheet (CuO NS)

Dissolve 400 mg of CuSO4·5H2O in 40 mL deionized water with stirring (at 300 rpm), slowly add 10 ml of 1 M KOH aqueous solution and stir for 15 min, then slowly add 4 ml of NH3·H2O and after stirring for 30 min, the mixture was then transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and maintained at 80 °C for 12 h, washed with deionized water and dried in vacuo at 60 °C for 24 h, then calcine in a muffle furnace at 400 °C for 3 h.

Synthesis of TCPP(M)–CuO nanosheet (TM–CuO NS)

Metal chloride or metal nitrate and TPPCOOMe, the feeding molar ratio of both is 1:1, were added to 15 mL of DMF and refluxed at 120 °C for 10 h. M-TPPCOOMe (M = Co, Ag, Cu, and Ni) was obtained by adding 0.1 g TPPCOOMe to a mixture of MeOH (5 mL) and THF (5 mL). After the addition of 4 mL of 2 M KOH aqueous solution, the mixture was refluxed at 120 °C for 20 h. After removal and allowing the mixture to cool to room temperature (25 °C), the solution was acidified with 1 M HCl until no precipitate appeared. The TCPP(M) metalloporphyrin powder was filtered and purified. 30 mg CuO NS were added to a solution containing 18 mL ethanol, and 12 mL deionized water. After sonication for 20 min, 3 mL of TCPP(M)/DMF solution (1 mg/mL) was added. After sonication for a further 10 min, it was removed, left for 50 min at 40 °C, washed with DMF and ethanol and dried overnight in an oven. All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and were used as received without any further purification.

Electrocatalyst preparation

For the working electrode used in CO2RR in a flow cell, 25 mg of catalyst was dispersed into a mixture of 1500 µL of isopropanol, 450 µL of deionized water and 50 µL of Nafion (5 wt%, Dupont) for at least 60 min to form a homogeneous catalyst ink, and then the catalyst ink was applied to one side of the carbon paper, which had a geometric area of 1 cm × 2.5 cm. Subsequently, an electrocatalyst with a loading of 10 mg cm−2 was prepared.

Flow cell assembly

The flow cell assembly consists of three chambers: an anode liquid chamber, a cathode liquid chamber and a gas flow chamber. The size of the exposed electrode is 0.5 cm × 2 cm. The cathode GDE is clamped between the cathode chamber and the gas diffusion chamber, with the substrate facing the gas chamber and the catalyst facing the cathode chamber. An IrO2-coated titanium mesh was used as the counter electrode. The cathode liquid and anode liquid chambers were separated by a proton exchange membrane (PEM, Nafion 324®). The cathodic liquid chamber contains an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The cathode electrolyte and anode electrolyte are applied through separate silicone tubes, each connected to a peristaltic pump that provides a constant flow rate of approximately 15 mL/min. The electrolyte passes through the pump and then into each chamber from the bottom to the top, creating a tight electrolyte circulation. For the gas supply, the flow rate of the gas flow chambers is controlled by a digital mass flow controller connected to a CO2 cylinder.

All electrochemical CO2RR tests and long-term stability evaluations were carried out using an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E) at ambient room temperature (25 °C) without the use of a climatic chamber. 1 M KCl solutions were prepared separately, and the pH was adjusted to 2 or 0.7 with H2SO4. The performance of the CO2RR was tested in a constant current flow cell assembly. The above solutions with different pH values were used as the cathode electrolyte. Since chlorine gas is generated from the anodic KCl solution during the reaction process. Therefore, 1 M KOH was used as the anode solution. During the half-hour test, there was basically no change in the cathode pH by observation.

A volume of 50 mL of cathode and anode liquids was recycled, and the liquid products were collected for analysis after 30 min of continuous operation. The gas phase products were collected at 4 min and 19 min and the current density was calculated from the geometric surface area.

In situ electron paramagnetic resonance

In situ EPR measurements were conducted at room temperature (25 °C) using a Bruker EMX-10/12 ESR spectrometer (X-band). Prior to each measurement, 20.0 mL of electrolyte saturated with pure Ar or CO2 was mixed with 100.0 μL of DMPO, while a continuous flow of pure Ar or CO2 was maintained at 50.0 mL min−1 via a digital mass flow controller to ensure sufficient gas supply. After performing the EPR test under a current density of 0.1 A cm−2, 30.0 μL of the tested solution was extracted into a standard quartz sample tube, which was then placed in a dedicated EPR sampling tube for the detection of ·H and ·COOH radicals.

Fabrication of GDE for long-term stability test

The composite gas diffusion electrode was prepared by uniformly depositing the well-dispersed catalyst ink onto a piece of carbon paper, which served as the first layer. A double-sided hydrophobic PTFE membrane was then placed tightly against the uncoated back side of the carbon paper as the second layer. A copper foil was placed in direct physical contact with the carbon paper layer to serve as the current collector. Finally, the integrated composite electrode was mounted into the flow cell electrolyzer, with the catalyst-coated side of the carbon paper facing the aqueous catholyte and the PTFE membrane facing the gas chamber.

In situ Raman spectroscopy

In situ Raman spectroscopy was performed using a commercial Raman cell (Xiamen SHINs Technology Co., Ltd.). The carbon paper working electrode was secured to the glassy carbon electrode surface using conductive adhesive. A saturated calomel electrode (SCE) served as the reference electrode, and a platinum wire functioned as the counter electrode. The electrolyte consisted of a 0.5 M K2SO4 / 0.005 M H2SO4 solution. All potentials are reported versus the SCE and converted to the RHE scale. Prior to measurement, CO2/Ar gas was purged through the Raman cell until saturation was achieved. Electrochemical Raman measurements were conducted using a HORIBA XploRA Raman microscope system. A 638 nm excitation laser and a 50x objective lens (numerical aperture = 0.55) were employed for the measurements. The laser power was maintained at approximately 3 mW, with a spectral resolution of 2 cm−1. The acquisition time for each spectrum was approximately 60 s.

Structural and compositional analysis

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were conducted using a Rigaku Ultima-IV X-ray diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at a scan speed of 10° min−1. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images were obtained using an FEI Tecnai F30 electron microscope. HAADF-STEM characterization and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping were conducted using a Thermo Scientific Talos F200S. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted using a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB Xi+. X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses were performed on a RapidXAFS HE Ultra spectrometer.

Density functional theory calculations details

All DFT calculations were performed using the plane-wave code QUANTUM ESPRESSO.57–59. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof exchange–correlation functional augmented with Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction with Becke–Johnson damping was employed throughout60,61. Core electrons were described by projector augmented wave pseudopotentials62. The kinetic energy cutoffs for wavefunctions and charge density were set to 50 and 500 Ry, respectively. Electronic convergence was achieved when the total energy change fell below 10-8 Ry, and structural relaxations were performed until the residual force on each atom was less than 10-3 Ry/Bohr. The Cu(100) surface was modeled by a five-layer slab with a 5 × 5 surface unit cell, separated by a 20 Å vacuum region to eliminate spurious interactions between periodic images. The Brillouin zone was sampled with a 3 × 3 × 1 Γ-centered Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid, and a Gaussian smearing width of 10-3 Ry was applied. Transition states were located using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method63, and subsequently refined with a quasi-Newton algorithm until the maximum residual force dropped below 0.05 eV/Å. The catalyst model, BZA-Cu(100), was constructed by anchoring one benzoic acid molecule on the Cu(100) surface.

Computation details

In the present work, AIMD were performed using the CP2K/Quickstep package64. The DFT implemented in CP2K is based on a hybrid Gaussian plane wave (GPW) scheme. The orbitals were described by an atom centered Gaussian-type basis set, and an auxiliary plane wave basis set was used to re-expand the electron density in the reciprocal space65. The Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) functional with Grimme’s dispersion correction with Becke-Johnson damping (PBE-D3BJ) and the double-ζ plus polarization (DZVP) basis set were used66,67. An energy cutoff of 500 Ry was set for the plane-wave basis set. The core electrons were modeled using Goedecker−Teter−Hutter (GTH) norm-conserving pseudopotentials. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all dimensions. The wave function optimization was performed using matrix diagonalization method at Γ point. The convergence criterion for wave function optimization was set by a maximum electronic gradient of 1 × 10−6 a.u. and an energy difference tolerance between self-consistent field cycles of 10−12 a.u., respectively. AIMD simulations were performed in the canonical (NVT) ensemble using a velocity rescaling (CSVR) thermostat. All simulations employed a timestep of 1 fs and were performed at a temperature of 300 K. Free energy calculations were carried out using the thermal integration (TI) method based on Lagrangian multipliers applied to the collective variable (CV). The 3D periodic interface model consists of 64 H2O molecules and one HCl molecule to simulate and acidic environment, yield a proton concentrated of 0.87 M.
