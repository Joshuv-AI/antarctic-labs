Antibacterial activity, phytochemical profiling and ADMET prediction of bioactive compounds in Origanum syriacum and Salix alba
Authors: Nour S Basudan, Asmaa A El-Hefnawy, Shimaa El-Sapagh, Mohamed N El-Sayed, Khalil M Saad-Allah, Maha Azab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ever-increasing global predicament of multidrug-resistant (MDR) infections necessitates the urgent discovery of novel antimicrobials, with medicinal plants representing a promising source of new therapeutic agents. This study investigated the antibacterial efficacy of Origanum syriacum and Salix alba organic and aqueous extracts against the investigated MDR pathogens using plant and bacterial samples sourced from Egypt, with findings expected to inform broader applications beyond this geographic origin. Antibacterial activity of the extracts was evaluated through agar-well diffusion, and MIC was assessed using broth microdilution assay, while the phytochemical composition of potent extracts (acetonic and ethanolic) was characterized using GC-MS analysis. Pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles of the dominant bioactive compounds were in silico predicted employing ADMET modeling. The results demonstrated that organic extracts exhibited superior and broad-spectrum antibacterial activity compared to aqueous extracts. Notably, O. syriacum acetone extract showed potent effectiveness against MRSA. GC-MS analysis revealed that this efficacy strongly correlates with a high abundance of phenolic monoterpenes, primarily thymol (49.82% in acetone extract). In silico ADMET predictions further identified thymoquinone, eugenol, p-coumaric acid, and ferulic acid as lead compounds, demonstrating favorable drug-likeness, promising oral bioavailability, and manageable toxicological profiles. This integrated approach supports the ethnobotanical use of O. syriacum and S. alba extracts and identifies specific phytochemicals that merit further evaluation as potential therapeutic leads against recalcitrant MDR infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50519-w.Keywords: Multidrug resistance, Natural products, Phytochemical analysis, Pharmacokinetics, BioavailabilitySubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial infections pose a critical global health threat, with wound infections accounting for approximately 15% of nosocomial cases and contributing significantly to morbidity and mortality1–3. The overuse and misuse of antibiotics across healthcare, agriculture, and animal husbandry have accelerated resistance propagation, necessitating novel antimicrobial strategies4. To address the challenge of antibacterial resistance, novel strategies are being explored, some of which involve the investigation of medicinal plants as potential alternatives to antibiotics. Extensive preclinical and clinical studies have examined the antibacterial properties of medicinal plants, offering scientific validation for the efficacy of certain plants traditionally employed in folk medicine for therapeutic purposes. Natural phytomedicines with notable pharmacological effects have demonstrated success in wound treatment and infection prevention, reducing reliance on antibiotics that contribute to the development of significant antibiotic resistance5,6. Among the medicinal plants of historical significance is Origanum syriacum L., also known as white oregano, zatar, Syrian oregano, or Bible hyssop, a perennial aromatic shrub of the Lamiaceae family indigenous to the Mediterranean region, specifically Lebanon, Jordan, Syria, Turkey, and the Sinai Desert of Egypt7,8. Origanum species have a longstanding history in traditional medicine for treating diverse ailments, with a growing recognition of their therapeutic potential. O. syriacum has been utilized as a potent disinfectant, flavoring agent, perfume, and in the production of fragrant soaps9. In Arabic traditional medicine, O. syriacum is esteemed for its efficacy in treating various conditions such as heart ailments, gastrointestinal discomfort, respiratory issues, wounds, and anxiety10. Previous studies have demonstrated that O. syriacum possesses antibacterial, analgesic, and anti-inflammatory properties, attributed to its diverse phytochemical composition comprising phenols, flavonoids, terpenes, glycosides, and essential oils11,12.

Recent investigations have unveiled a spectrum of biological activities associated with O. syriacum, including antiparasitic, antimicrobial, antioxidant, antitumor, and anti-inflammatory effects, suggesting its potential therapeutic utility in addressing various diseases such as gastric ulcers, cancer, and neurological disorders7,13. Studies have highlighted the robust antibacterial properties of O. syriacum against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, with the high content of phenolic derivatives like thymol and carvacrol likely contributing to its potent in vitro antibacterial efficacy14. The aerial parts (leaves and stems) of O. syriacum were specifically selected for this study, as they represent the primary site of the plant essential oil biosynthesis and accumulation, particularly rich in phenolic monoterpenes such as thymol and carvacrol, which are well-documented for their membrane-disrupting antimicrobial activity15. Additionally, the aerial parts correspond to the traditionally used plant material in folk medicine for wound healing and infection treatment. The diverse biological activities exhibited by O. syriacum position it as a promising natural agent for drug development. However, despite this growing body of evidence, the specific antibacterial activity of O. syriacum extracts against clinically isolated MDR wound pathogens, using patient samples from Egypt as a representative setting, remains largely unexplored, though the implications extend to MDR infections globally. Furthermore, no previous study has systematically compared the efficacy of different solvent extracts (acetone, ethanol, and aqueous) of Egyptian O. syriacum against a panel of MDR isolates, nor has the phytochemical profile of these specific extracts been comprehensively characterized and correlated with their antibacterial potency.

Salix alba L., commonly known as white willow, is native to Europe, Asia, and North Africa, belonging to the Salicaceae family. With a habitat primarily along riverbanks in moist soils, S. alba ranges from low bushes to towering trees exceeding 30 m in height16. For millennia, S. alba has been integral to folk medicine, revered for its efficacy in treating influenza, fever, pain, inflammation, rheumatism, and headaches17,18. The bark of S. alba has been recognized to exhibit notable analgesic and antipyretic properties, underscoring its medicinal value19. Recognized as a natural wound healer, S. alba is deemed effective in treating chronic wounds and safeguarding against bacterial and fungal skin infections20. The bark of S. alba was chosen as the target tissue because it is historically documented as the medicinal part of choice for its analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic properties, attributed to its rich content of salicin derivatives, phenolic glycosides, and tannins, all of which contribute to antibacterial efficacy15,18,20. Furthermore, the bark accumulates higher concentrations of these bioactive secondary metabolites compared to leaves or wood, making it the optimal tissue for antimicrobial screening17,21. Nevertheless, investigations specifically targeting the antibacterial potential of Egyptian S. alba bark extracts against clinically relevant MDR wound isolates are scarce. Critically, the current literature lacks a parallel, comparative analysis of the antimicrobial efficacy of both O. syriacum and S. alba against the same panel of MDR pathogens under identical experimental conditions, limiting our understanding of their relative therapeutic potential.

Previous antibacterial studies on O. syriacum and S. alba have primarily focused on standard reference strains or non-MDR clinical isolates, with limited attention to MDR wound pathogens. While the plant materials and bacterial isolates used in this study were collected from Egypt, the findings are expected to be broadly representative, as O. syriacum and S. alba are distributed across Mediterranean and temperate regions, and the MDR strains belong to globally prevalent species and resistance phenotypes. Therefore, the bioactivity profiles reported here are likely generalizable, though minor chemotypic variations in plant secondary metabolism may occur due to differences in climate, soil, or geography. Furthermore, most studies have examined each plant individually using single solvent extracts, lacking systematic comparison across different extraction methods and direct correlation with detailed phytochemical profiles. While the antibacterial activity of O. syriacum has been reported against certain reference strains, the novelty of the present study lies in several interconnected aspects that have not been previously reported: (i) the first parallel comparative evaluation of three different solvent extracts (acetone, ethanol, and aqueous) of locally sourced O. syriacum aerial parts and S. alba bark against a well-characterized panel of clinically relevant MDR isolates from infected wounds in Egyptian patients; (ii) the first direct correlation of extract-specific antibacterial potency with detailed GC-MS phytochemical fingerprints for both plants under identical experimental conditions; (iii) the first application of in silico ADMET modeling to prioritize specific lead compounds (e.g., thymoquinone, p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid) from these extracts based on both abundance and predicted pharmacokinetic safety; and (iv) the first comparative assessment of O. syriacum and S. alba side-by-side against the same MDR panel, enabling relative therapeutic potential ranking. These elements constitute a previously unreported integrated approach that bridges ethnobotany, analytical chemistry, and computational drug discovery.

The present study investigates O. syriacum and S. alba together to compare two distinct phytochemical chemotypes, phenolic monoterpenes (O. syriacum) versus phenolic glycosides, flavonoids, and triterpenoids (S. alba), against the same panel of MDR wound pathogens. This side-by-side comparison, rarely performed in previous studies, allows identification of which chemotype offers superior efficacy against specific bacterial species and provides a scientific rationale for prioritizing plant leads for future drug development. Accordingly, the present study addresses these critical knowledge gaps by: (1) evaluating, for the first time, the antibacterial activity of three different solvent extracts (acetone, ethanol, and aqueous) of both Egyptian O. syriacum aerial parts and S. alba bark against a well-characterized panel of MDR bacterial isolates from infected wounds; (2) providing a detailed phytochemical profiling and quantification of the bioactive compounds responsible for the observed antimicrobial effects using advanced analytical techniques; and (3) employing computational ADMET prediction to assess the drug-likeness and safety profiles of the identified major compounds. This integrated approach offers new insights into the potential of these two medicinal plants as sources of novel therapeutic agents for combating MDR wound infections, a perspective that has not been previously reported in the literature. In this context, this study hypothesizes that extracts of O. syriacum (aerial parts) and S. alba (bark) possess significant antibacterial activity against multidrug-resistant wound pathogens. To test this, the present study aims to explore the antibacterial efficacy of both species against MDR isolates responsible for wound infections, utilizing acetone, ethanol, and aqueous extracts. Furthermore, we seek to identify and quantify the key phytochemical compounds that support the observed antimicrobial activity.

Materials and methods

Bacterial collection and identification

Thirty purified bacterial cultures were obtained from the culture collection of the Botany and Microbiology Department, Faculty of Science, Tanta University, Egypt. These cultures were isolated from superficial infected wounds according to standard protocols and the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki22. These bacterial isolates were originally collected in a prior, independent clinical study. For the present in vitro work, no direct interaction with human participants occurred, and only anonymized bacterial strains were used. The need for ethical approval and informed consent for this specific laboratory-based study was formally waived by the Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Science, Tanta University (IACUC-SCI-TU-00318), following review, as it did not involve new patient recruitment or access to identifiable patient data. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the committee’s guidelines.

Bacterial identification was performed as described in our previous study23. Four out of the thirty isolates were identified as multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria, including Klebsiella pneumoniae AAE, Escherichia coli AAE, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia AAE, and Staphylococcus aureus AAE. The 16 S rRNA sequences of these strains were deposited in the NCBI database under accession numbers LC764401, LC764402, LC764400, and LC764399, respectively23. Multidrug resistance was defined according to standard criteria as acquired resistance to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories24. Full details of the antibiotic panel and zone diameter interpretations are provided in the supplementary materials (Tables S2-S6).

Preparation of bacterial suspensions

Bacterial suspensions were prepared from fresh cultures using the direct colony suspension technique25. A sterile needle was used to prepare a suspension of 106 CFU/mL in normal saline solution.

Antibiotic susceptibility test

The disc diffusion method (Kirby–Bauer) was used to determine the antibiotic susceptibility of fresh bacterial cultures (106 CFU/mL) according to Bauer et al.26. The susceptibility of bacterial isolates to different antibiotic classes was previously reported23 by means of Vitek 2 Compact-15 for ID & AST and the results are presented in supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Plant materials collection and preparation

The aerial parts of Origanum syriacum and the bark of Salix alba were collected in May 2022 from the agricultural farm of Sakha Horticulture Research Station, Kafr El-Sheikh, Egypt. Collection was performed with the official permission of the Sakha Horticulture Research Station, and all necessary guidelines for sustainable and ethical plant harvesting were followed. The plant materials were rinsed with running tap water to remove dust and foreign objects, followed by three washes with distilled water. The plant materials were then dried at room temperature, ground into a fine powder using an electric mixer, sieved through a 22 mm sieve, and stored in sterile dark airtight containers until extraction.

Preparation of plant ethanolic and acetonic extracts

Finely powdered plant material (50 g) was exhaustively extracted using a Soxhlet apparatus with either 95% (v/v) ethanol or analytical-grade acetone. The sample was loaded into a cellulose thimble, and the chosen solvent was heated to reflux for six hours at approximately 78 °C for ethanol and 56 °C for acetone, corresponding to the boiling points of each solvent. Throughout this cycle, condensed solvent vapors percolated through the sample to leach soluble compounds, with the enriched solution periodically siphoning back into the distillation flask. The resulting extract was filtered and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was concentrated under reduced pressure at 40 °C and then dried under nitrogen. The extract yield was determined gravimetrically, and the material was stored in sealed amber vials at 4 °C. For bioassays, a stock solution was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at a concentration of 60 mg/mL immediately before use.

Preparation of plant cold and hot aqueous extracts

Cold aqueous extracts of powdered O. syriacum aerial parts and S. alba bark were prepared by maceration in sterile distilled water at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio. The mixture was agitated on a rotary shaker for 24 h at room temperature. For hot aqueous extraction, an identical mixture was heated to boiling (100 °C) for 30 min before agitation under the same conditions. All extracts were filtered and subsequently clarified by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 15 min. The aqueous phase was eliminated under reduced pressure at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator. The resulting crude extracts were quantified gravimetrically, stored in sterile amber glass vials at 4 °C, and later reconstituted in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to a final concentration of 60 mg/mL for experimental use.

Evaluation of antibacterial activity against multidrug-resistant bacteria

Antibacterial activity was assessed using the agar-well diffusion method27,28, with the following modifications: (i) extract concentration of 100 mg/mL was used based on preliminary screening; (ii) wells of 6 mm diameter were loaded with 100 µL of each extract; (iii) ciprofloxacin (10 µg/disc) and imipenem (10 µg/disc) served as positive controls, and 5% DMSO as negative control; (iv) pre-diffusion was performed at 4 °C for 1 h prior to incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. All assays were performed in triplicate (n = 3 biological treatments) and mean ± SD are reported. Raw replicate values are provided as supplementary data. Taking into consideration the limitations of the agar-well diffusion method (e.g., variable diffusion rates of phytochemicals), minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determinations were subsequently performed as a quantitative complement.

Determination of MIC of the most potent plant extracts

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined using the colorimetric broth microdilution method according to Abou-Elkhair et al.29, with the following modifications: (i) serial two-fold dilutions of each plant extract were prepared in 5% DMSO ranging from 75 to 0.0366 mg/mL; (ii) bacterial inoculum of 5 × 105 CFU/mL was used following CLSI guidelines; (iii) after 18 h incubation at 37 °C, 50 µL of 0.01% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) was added, followed by 2 h incubation; (iv) MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that inhibited bacterial growth, indicated by absence of color change.

Identification of the phytocomponents in ethanol and acetone extracts

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) was employed to analyze the acetone and ethanol extracts of O. syriacum aerial parts and S. alba bark, known for their robust antibacterial properties against multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria. The GC-MS analysis was conducted utilizing the Perkin-Elmer model Clarus 580/560S equipped with an Elite-5MS fused silica capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm film thickness). Helium gas (99.9995%) served as the carrier gas with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. A split ratio of 1:20 was configured. The injection volume was 1 µL, with a solvent delay of 6 min, and a scan range from 50 to 620 Da. The temperature profile of the oven commenced at 80 °C and was maintained for 7 min, then increased at a rate of 10 °C/min to reach 140 °C and held for 1 min, further increased at 10 °C/min to 200 °C and held for 2 min, and finally ramped up at 5 °C/min to 280 °C, where it was held for 10 min. The NIST08, WILEY8, and FAME library databases were utilized for the identification of the detected compound by comparing their mass spectra with those of known compounds in the software database libraries. Compound identification was performed by matching mass spectra against the NIST08, WILEY8, and FAME libraries, with only compounds showing a high probability match (≥ 85% similarity) being considered for reporting30. Retention indices were calculated using a series of standard alkanes (C8-C40) and compared with literature values where available to support identification. However, in the absence of authentic standards for each compound, all identifications should be considered tentative.

Pharmacokinetic and absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) prediction

Computational prediction of the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) parameters was undertaken for the compounds identified as possessing the greatest antimicrobial effectiveness and abundance within the investigated plant extracts. The molecular structures of these compounds, defined by their Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System (SMILES) strings, served as the primary input for the analysis. An initial comprehensive ADMET assessment was performed with the ADMETlab 3.0 computational tool (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/, accessed on October 1st, 2025). Further delineation of absorption and distribution characteristics was achieved through the SwissADME web server (http://www.swissadme.ch, accessed on October 1st, 2025), which included a graphical assessment of permeability via the BOILED Egg predictive model.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics, specifically the mean and standard error, were employed to summarize the data from three measurements. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized to evaluate potential significant differences among the various treatments. A p-value < 0.05 was considered a significant difference between the treatments. Duncan’s post-hoc test was subsequently performed to identify significant differences between the data within the experimental groups.

Results

Identification and testing of the antibiotic sensitivity of bacterial isolates

In our previous study, as a complementary part of the current study, we have identified that the cultures of 30 bacterial isolates that were obtained from superficial, infected wounds, 60% of these isolates were identified as Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive cocci), while 40% of these isolates were classified as Gram-negative bacilli. These Gram-negative bacilli included Pseudomonas aeruginosa (20%), Escherichia coli (10%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (6.7%), and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (3.3%)23. Furthermore, the initial screening of the antibiotics’ susceptibility test showed that K. pneumoniae, E. coli, and S. maltophilia were resistant to 88.9, 94.4, and 94.4% of the tested antibiotics, respectively. On the other hand, S. aureus was reported to be resistant to 83.3% of the tested antibiotics (Supplementary Table S1). The results for these most MDR bacteria were confirmed in automatic antibiotic susceptibility tests carried out by means of VITEK2 Compact-15 for ID&AST (Supplementary Tables S2-S6). The isolate S. aureus appeared to be resistant to cefoxitin, benzylpenicillin, and oxacillin, confirming that this isolate was methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), which is one of the most dangerous bacteria.

Molecular identification of MDR bacterial strains

The selected four highly resistant bacterial strains were subjected to automated identification using the Biomerieux Vitek 2 System. The previous work of our group results confirmed that K. pneumoniae (98% probability), E. coli (99% probability), S. maltophilia (99% probability), and S. aureus (98% probability)23. The identification of these strains was further confirmed via molecular analysis based on 16 S rRNA sequencing. BLAST analysis of the sequences showed their high similarity to sequences already available in the NCBI database. This result was also confirmed by phylogenetic analysis clustered with NCBI records. Therefore, our selected strains were identified as K. pneumoniae LC764401, E. coli LC764402, S. maltophilia LC764400, and S. aureus LC764399 (Supplementary Figure S1).

Antibacterial activity of O. syriacum extracts against MDR bacteria

The data in Table 1 demonstrated a statistically significant (P < 0.001) variation in the antibacterial activity of Origanum syriacum extracts based on solvent type. The ethanol extract exhibited the most potent activity, with inhibition zones of 38.33 mm against MRSA, 30.67 mm against S. maltophilia, 22.00 mm against K. pneumoniae, and 20.33 mm against E. coli. The acetone extract showed comparable efficacy. Whereas the acetone extract of O. syriacum resulted in inhibition zone diameters of 36.67, 33.33, 21.00, and 17.33 mm against MRSA, S. maltophilia, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli, respectively. A clear efficacy gradient is evident, with organic solvents (ethanol, acetone) yielding superior results compared to aqueous extracts. The hot water extract generated moderate inhibition zones, ranging from 26.67 mm for S. maltophilia to 15.67 mm for E. coli. Cold water extract, on the other hand, showed a relative inhibition zone of 14.33 and 11.00 mm against MRSA and S. maltophilia, respectively, but it showed no activity against K. pneumoniae and E. coli. The negative control (5% DMSO) showed no activity against all the strains investigated. Therefore, O. syriacum is a rich source of antibacterial agents with promising activity against MDR pathogens, and the choice of extraction solvent is a paramount factor.

Antibacterial activity of S. alba extracts against MDR bacteria

Data presented in Table 2 demonstrated that all Salix alba bark extracts, except for the negative control DMSO, exhibit statistically significant (P < 0.001) antibacterial activity against the investigated MDR bacteria. The ethanol extract consistently showed the highest effectiveness, with inhibition zones of 36.67 mm against MRSA, 27.67 mm against S. maltophilia, 22.67 mm against K. pneumoniae, and 19.00 mm against E. coli. The acetone extract was also highly active, yielding inhibition zones of 32.67, 22.00, 20.33, and 16.33 mm against the same respective pathogens. Aqueous extracts displayed more variable efficacy. The hot water extract produced inhibition zones of 26.00 mm (MRSA), 24.67 mm (S. maltophilia), 14.67 mm (K. pneumoniae), and 14.67 mm (E. coli), while the cold-water extract resulted in zones of 28.33 mm, 20.33 mm, 10.33 mm, and 14.67 mm. The DMSO control showed no activity against any bacterium (representative photos of these negative control plates are provided in Supplementary Figure S2). Collectively, the antibacterial activity of S. alba bark is highly dependent on the extraction solvent, with ethanol being the most effective; the extracts are principally effective against Gram-positive MRSA and Gram-negative S. maltophilia.

GC-MS analysis of ethanol and acetone extracts of O. syriacum aerial parts and S. alba bark

The ethanolic and acetonic extracts of O. syriacum aerial parts and S. alba bark, which demonstrated the most potent antibacterial activity, were selected for phytochemical characterization by GC-MS to identify constituent bioactive compounds.

Acetone extract of O. syriacum GC-MS analysis

GC-MS analysis of O. syriacum acetone extract revealed 30 compounds dominated by monoterpenoids (Table 4, Figure S3). Thymol (49.82%) was the major constituent, with its precursors p-cymene (16.45%) and thymoquinone (8.31%), plus carvacrol (4.92%), collectively accounting for ~ 80% of the extract. Causality between thymol abundance and antibacterial activity requires targeted fractionation for confirmation. Minor constituents included sabinene, γ-terpinene, sesquiterpenes (< 1.5% each, e.g., germacrene D, α-humulene), trace alkanes, eugenol, phytol, squalene, ursolic/oleanolic acids, and α-tocopherol. Biological properties are primarily attributable to the high cumulative abundance of thymol and related monoterpenoids.

Regarding absorption and distribution, the compounds generally exhibit high plasma protein binding (PPB > 79%), which may limit their free concentration. The human intestinal absorption (HIA) predictions are predominantly low for the monoterpenes but moderate for thymoquinone (0.558). The triterpenoids showed a high possibility for being P-glycoprotein substrates (Pgp-sub < 0.05), indicating potential efflux. For distribution, most compounds are predicted to have low blood-brain barrier penetration (BBB < 0.3), except for ursolic acid (BBB = 0.854), which suggests a potential for central nervous system activity.

The metabolism profile is complex, with all monoterpenes showing high potential to inhibit or be metabolized by various Cytochrome P450 enzymes. For instance, thymol and eugenol are strong CYP1A2 inhibitors, while carvacrol is a likely substrate for CYP2C19 and CYP3A4. The triterpenoids, however, showed minimal interaction with most CYP enzymes. These phenolic acids exhibit moderate molecular weight, favorable solubility (LogS > -2.4), and low lipophilicity (LogP < 1.65), which supports their excellent predicted human intestinal absorption (F(30%) > 0.93). They show a low risk of P-glycoprotein-mediated efflux, minimal inhibition of major CYP450 enzymes, thereby reducing potential for drug-drug interactions, and a low predicted risk of carcinogenicity. While they demonstrate potential hepatotoxicity, their overall profile, including high bioavailability scores (0.85) and simple synthetic accessibility, positions them as the most viable lead candidates from the S. alba extracts (Fig. 3).

Discussion

A paramount concern in global public health is the increasing incidence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial pathogens. These MDR organisms compromise the therapeutic utility of established antimicrobial agents, thereby exacerbating the treatment complexity of common infections, such as wound infections. The World Health Organization has consistently highlighted the threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), emphasizing that MDR pathogens, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacilli, are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide31. The rapid evolution and dissemination of resistance mechanisms, coupled with a stagnant pipeline of new antibiotics, have created a critical situation where previously manageable infections can now lead to untreatable outcomes, underscoring the urgent need for continuous surveillance and novel therapeutic strategies.

In a previous investigation that complemented the current work, the microbial pathogens of superficially infected wounds were characterized through the culture of 30 bacterial isolates. The distribution revealed that 60% of the isolates were Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive cocci), while the remaining 40% were Gram-negative bacilli. This Gram-negative population comprised Pseudomonas aeruginosa (20%), Escherichia coli (10%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (6.7%), and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (3.3%)23. Initial antibiotic susceptibility screening demonstrated a high prevalence of multidrug resistance. Specifically, K. pneumoniae, E. coli, and S. maltophilia exhibited resistance to 88.9, 94.4, and 94.4% of the tested antibiotics, respectively. Similarly, S. aureus was resistant to 83.3% of the antibiotics (Supplementary Table S1). These findings for the most resistant bacteria were subsequently validated using an automated VITEK 2 Compact-15 system for identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (Supplementary Table S2). Critically, the S. aureus isolate demonstrated resistance to cefoxitin, benzylpenicillin, and oxacillin, thereby confirming its identity as methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), a pathogen classified as a high-priority threat by health authorities due to its association with difficult-to-treat infections32.

In this study, the four selected highly resistant bacterial strains were subjected to a robust, multi-method identification process to ensure taxonomic accuracy. Initial automated identification using the bioMérieux VITEK 2 System provided high-probability confirmations for K. pneumoniae (98%), E. coli (99%), S. maltophilia (99%), and S. aureus (98%), corroborating previous findings23. This phenotypic identification was conclusively validated through molecular analysis. 16 S rRNA gene sequencing and subsequent BLAST analysis revealed high sequence similarity to established records in the NCBI database. Phylogenetic analysis further confirmed the identities, showing clear clustering with reference strains. The isolates were thus definitively identified and assigned the GenBank accession numbers K. pneumoniae LC764401, E. coli LC764402, S. maltophilia LC764400, and S. aureus LC764399 (Supplementary Figure S1).

Plant-derived extracts represent a promising frontier in bacterial infection treatment, as their complex phytochemical composition can offer multi-target mechanisms of action that are less prone to conventional resistance development. The present study provides compelling evidence for the potent antibacterial efficacy of Origanum syriacum extracts against the investigated MDR bacteria. The data revealed a significant variation in activity directly attributable to the extraction solvent. The ethanol extract demonstrated superior efficacy, with particularly large inhibition zones against MRSA (38.33 mm) and S. maltophilia (30.67 mm). The acetone extract showed comparable potency. A clear effectiveness gradient was established, with organic solvents surpassing aqueous extracts in their antibacterial activity.

The potent antibacterial activity of the O. syriacum acetone extract, as evidenced by the significant inhibition zones against the studied MDR bacteria, can be directly attributed to its rich and complex phytochemical profile, dominated by synergistic antimicrobial compounds. The efficiency observed in Table 1, particularly against the Gram-positive MRSA (36.67 mm) and the Gram-negative S. maltophilia (33.33 mm), is evidence of the high content of phenolic monoterpenes. The GC-MS analysis (Table 4) revealed that this activity is primarily driven by the high combined concentration of thymol (49.82%) and carvacrol (4.92%), which together constitute over half of the detected extract composition. Thymol and carvacrol are well-characterized phenolic compounds whose mechanism of action involves the disruption of the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane. They increase membrane permeability by interacting with phospholipid bilayers and embedding themselves in the membrane, leading to the leakage of ions, the collapse of the proton motive force, and ultimately, cell death14,33. This non-specific, multi-target mechanism is particularly effective against MDR strains, as it avoids the specific resistance mechanisms that render conventional antibiotics ineffective. The superior activity against MRSA aligns with the general susceptibility of Gram-positive bacteria, which lack the protective outer membrane. However, the significant activity against Gram-negative pathogens like S. maltophilia and K. pneumoniae can be explained by the presence of p-cymene (16.45%), a monoterpene hydrocarbon, which can fluidize the bacterial outer membrane, thereby facilitating the penetration of the more potent thymol and carvacrol into the cell interior34. It is important to emphasize that while these correlations between phytochemical composition and observed antibacterial activity are compelling, they remain hypothesis-generating. The proposed roles of specific compounds such as thymol, carvacrol, and p-cymene are based on their known activities in the literature and their abundance in the extract. However, since fractionation and isolated compound testing were not performed in this study, causality cannot be definitively confirmed. Further research involving bioassay-guided fractionation and testing of purified individual compounds is required to validate these synergistic interactions and establish direct causative relationships.

Furthermore, the presence of other bioactive compounds like thymoquinone (8.31%), known for its reactive oxygen species-inducing properties, and the triterpenoids ursolic acid and oleanolic acid, which can inhibit biofilm formation and enhance membrane integrity disruption, likely contributes to a broad-spectrum and robust antibacterial effect of O. syriacum acetonic extract35. The collective, synergistic interaction of this diverse array of terpenes, phenolics, and other secondary metabolites in the acetone extract makes O. syriacum a highly promising candidate for the development of novel therapeutic strategies to combat life-threatening MDR infections.

The potent antibacterial efficacy of the O. syriacum ethanolic extract, demonstrated by its effective inhibition of all tested MDR bacterial strains, is a direct consequence of its specific and potent phytochemical composition. The significant activity against MRSA (38.33 mm) can be primarily attributed to the extract’s high concentration of thymol (42.26%), a phenolic monoterpene well-known for its disruptive action on bacterial cell membranes33. Thymol, along with its structural analog carvacrol (1.52%), acts as a non-ionic surfactant, integrating into the lipid bilayer and increasing membrane permeability, leading to the leakage of vital cellular constituents and cell death14,36. This mechanism is particularly effective against Gram-positive bacteria like MRSA, which lack a protective outer membrane.

The significant activity of O. syriacum ethanolic extract observed against the Gram-negative pathogens, including S. maltophilia (30.67 mm), highlights the synergistic complexity of the extract. The presence of p-cymene (6.75%) is critical in this context. This monoterpene hydrocarbon is known to act as a permeabilizing agent for the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria37, thereby facilitating the entry of the more potent thymol and carvacrol to their cytoplasmic membrane targets. Furthermore, the extract contains a notable profile of triterpenoids, including ursolic acid (2.62%) and oleanolic acid. These compounds are increasingly recognized for their auxiliary antibacterial roles, which include the inhibition of virulence factors like biofilm formation and the enhancement of membrane disruption, thereby creating a multi-faceted attack on bacterial integrity35. The presence of eugenol (1.92%) has been reported to enhance the antibacterial efficacy, signifying its well-documented antimicrobial properties38. The superior performance of the ethanolic extract over other solvents, such as acetone and water, can be attributed to the efficiency of ethanol in co-extracting this specific, synergistic mixture of phenolic monoterpenes, membrane-weakening hydrocarbons, and bioactive triterpenoids, making it a highly promising candidate for combating high-priority MDR pathogens.

The significantly weaker antibacterial activity observed in the aqueous extracts of O. syriacum, compared to the potent organic solvent extracts, can be directly explained by the fundamental principles of phytochemistry. The antimicrobial potency of plant material is primarily attributed to secondary metabolites, such as phenolic compounds (e.g., thymol, carvacrol), terpenoids, and flavonoids. These bioactive molecules are largely hydrophobic and have low solubility in polar solvents like water39. The cold-water extract showed negligible to very low activity because it could only dissolve the most polar and water-soluble components, missing the vast majority of the essential antimicrobial oils. The hot water extract demonstrated moderate activity, particularly against S. maltophilia, because heat can increase the extraction efficiency by breaking down plant cell structures and slightly improving the solubility of some moderately polar compounds40. However, even with heating, the water is unable to extract the majority of the antimicrobials like thymol and carvacrol that are efficiently extracted by organic solvents such as ethanol and acetone.

The differential antibacterial activity of the S. alba bark acetone extract, as reported in Table 2, can be directly correlated with its rich and diverse phytochemical composition identified in Table 6. The superior efficacy against MRSA is particularly noteworthy and can be attributed to a synergistic combination of phenolic compounds and triterpenoids. Gram-positive bacteria like MRSA lack a protective outer membrane, making them highly susceptible to membrane-active compounds. The significant levels of triterpenoids, including ursolic acid, betulinic acid, and lupeol, are critical contributors. These compounds are well-documented for their ability to disrupt bacterial cytoplasmic membranes, causing depolarization and leakage of cellular contents41. This membrane-disrupting action is potentiated by phenolic acids like p-coumaric acid and ferulic acid, which further compromise microbial integrity and inhibit virulence factors such as sortase A in S. aureus42.

The activity against Gram-negative bacteria (S. maltophilia, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli) is moderate, which aligns with their intrinsic resistance mechanisms, primarily the presence of a hydrophilic outer membrane that restricts the penetration of hydrophobic molecules. The observed inhibition zones suggest that certain extract components can partially overcome this barrier. The hydrophobic triterpenoids and long-chain aliphatic compounds may act in concert to fluidize the outer membrane, allowing secondary metabolites like flavan-3-ols (e.g., catechin) to enter. Flavonoids such as catechin and naringenin possess additional sub-inhibitory effects, including the ability to inhibit efflux pumps and suppress biofilm formation, which are key virulence traits in MDR Gram-negative pathogens43,44. The variation in susceptibility among the Gram-negative strains likely reflects differences in their specific membrane porins and efflux pump systems. Collectively, the antibacterial profile of the S. alba acetone extract is not the result of a single compound but emerges from the complex synergy of its phytochemical constituents. The extract employs a multi-target mechanism involving membrane disruption, enzyme inhibition, and potential efflux pump modulation.

The superior antibacterial activity of the S. alba ethanolic extract, as evidenced by the significantly larger inhibition zones across all tested MDR pathogens in Table 2, can be directly attributed to its distinct and potent phytochemical profile detailed in Table 7. The exceptional activity against MRSA (36.67 mm) highlights a synergistic combination of compounds, particularly effective against Gram-positive bacteria. The high concentrations of the flavan-3-ols, (+)-catechin (8.48%) and (-)-epicatechin (4.50%), are pivotal. These flavonoids are renowned for their ability to disrupt bacterial membranes and, more critically, inhibit efflux pumps like NorA in MRSA, thereby increasing the intracellular concentration of other antimicrobial compounds and countering resistance mechanisms45. This action is powerfully complemented by the presence of phenolic glycosides in S. alba bark ethanolic extract, including salicin (9.35%) and populin. Upon uptake and potential metabolic conversion, these glycosides can yield active aglycones like salicylic alcohol, which disrupt proton motive force and exhibit intrinsic antibacterial properties46. The combined membrane-targeting effects of these phenolics create a challenging attack on the relatively unprotected Gram-positive cell envelope.

The enhanced activity against Gram-negative bacteria (S. maltophilia, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli), while lower than against MRSA, indicates that the ethanolic extract possesses compounds capable of partially overcoming the outer membrane barrier. The presence of triterpenoids, specifically oleanolic acid (2.36%), is a key differentiator from less potent extracts. Triterpenoids can compromise the integrity of the lipopolysaccharide layer, facilitating the entry of other antimicrobial constituents47. Once this barrier is penetrated, secondary metabolites like catechol and phenolic acids (e.g., p-coumaric acid and ferulic acid) can penetrate and exert their effects, which include inhibition of essential enzymes and disruption of energy metabolism44. The significant presence of catechin and epicatechin further contributes by potentially inhibiting biofilm formation and quorum sensing, key virulence factors in these pathogens. All in all, the robust and broad-spectrum antibacterial efficacy of the S. alba ethanolic extract is a direct result of its unique phytochemical synergy. The extract delivers a multi-targeted attack: the high-potency combination of flavan-3-ols and phenolic glycosides provides a powerful core activity, especially against Gram-positive bacteria, while the presence of membrane-priming triterpenoids and secondary phenolics extends this efficacy to challenging Gram-negative MDR strains. This validates ethanolic extract as a highly promising candidate for further investigation into novel anti-infective agents.

The significantly weaker antibacterial activity observed for both cold and hot water extracts of S. alba bark, compared to their ethanol and acetone counterparts, can be directly attributed to the low potential of water in extracting the biologically active components. Water, being a highly polar solvent, is inefficient at extracting the broad spectrum of medium and nonpolar secondary metabolites responsible for potent antibacterial effects. The main antimicrobial constituents of S. alba bark, such as flavonoids (e.g., catechin and naringenin), phenolic acids (e.g., ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid), and triterpenoids (e.g., betulinic acid and oleanolic acid), possess significantly lower solubility in water48. While water may extract some polar compounds like simple phenolic glycosides (e.g., salicin), it largely fails to dissolve the more complex and often more potent lipophilic compounds. This results in reduced membrane disruption and lower overall antibacterial potency, particularly against Gram-negative bacteria, which require a concerted multi-target attack to overcome their outer membrane44. The superior performance of organic solvents like ethanol and acetone is due to their ability to extract a wider and more potent array of these antimicrobial compounds, creating a synergistic mixture that aqueous extraction cannot replicate.

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) data revealed a clear and significant spectrum of antibacterial potency for the plant extracts, with efficacy being highly dependent on the plant species, the extraction solvent, and the target bacterium. The remarkably low MIC of the O. syriacum acetone extract against MRSA (0.293 mg/mL) underscores its exceptional anti-staphylococcal activity. This high effectiveness is characteristic of oregano species, which are rich in potent phenolic monoterpenoids like carvacrol and thymol. These compounds are broadly soluble in acetone and are renowned for their ability to disrupt bacterial cytoplasmic membranes, leading to cell lysis and death34.

The variable performance of the extracts against Gram-negative pathogens (S. maltophilia, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli) highlights the critical challenge of the outer membrane in determining antibacterial efficacy. The generally higher MIC values for these bacteria reflect the barrier function of lipopolysaccharides, which restricts the penetration of antimicrobial compounds. The pathogen-specific variation, such as the superior activity of the O. syriacum ethanol extract against S. maltophilia, suggests that beyond simple membrane disruption, specific interactions with bacterial enzymes or efflux systems may be at play49. For S. alba, the efficacy of its ethanol extract, particularly against MRSA and E. coli, can be attributed to its rich content of synergistic phenolic glycosides and flavonoids like salicin and catechin, which can inhibit critical bacterial processes46. Though all extracts showed significant antibacterial properties, the superior and broadest activity of O. syriacum extracts categorizes them as the most promising candidates for further development as therapeutic agents against MDR infections.

The in silico ADMET profiling of the major bioactive compounds from O. syriacum extracts provided crucial insights into their potential pharmacokinetic behavior and drug-like suitability. The analysis revealed a clear dichotomy between the low molecular weight monoterpenes and the larger triterpenoids, primarily ursolic and oleanolic acid. The monoterpenes, including thymol, carvacrol, and p-cymene, possess physicochemical properties generally conducive to passive absorption, such as low molecular weight and moderate lipophilicity, aligning with the known bioavailability of such compounds50. In contrast, the significant permeability challenges predicted for the triterpenoids, evidenced by very low solubility and poor Caco-2/MDCK permeability, are consistent with the known difficulties in achieving oral bioavailability for high molecular weight, highly lipophilic natural products51.

A critical finding is the high plasma protein binding predicted across most compounds, which could significantly reduce the free fraction available for pharmacological activity. The moderate human intestinal absorption (HIA) predicted for thymoquinone and the high oral bioavailability for eugenol are promising, yet the latter’s strong potential for Cytochrome P450 inhibition poses a substantial risk for drug-drug interactions, a common issue with phenolic compounds52. The prediction that ursolic and oleanolic acids are P-glycoprotein substrates further complicates their therapeutic potential by suggesting active efflux from the gut. The BOILED-Egg model corroborates the absorption potential but introduces a note of caution by predicting a high probability of blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration for most compounds, including thymol and carvacrol. While this could be advantageous for CNS-targeted therapies, it raises concerns about potential neurotoxicity for general use, a factor that requires careful consideration50. From a drug development perspective, thymoquinone and eugenol emerge as the most viable lead candidates. Thymoquinone presents a favorable balance of absorption potential and a cleaner CYP and hERG inhibition profile. Eugenol, despite its excellent predicted absorption and bioavailability, is flagged for its significant CYP inhibition potential. The triterpenoids, while exhibiting a longer predicted half-life due to low clearance, are hampered by poor absorption and a high predicted carcinogenicity risk, underscoring the necessity for rigorous in vivo toxicological studies. The in-silico data thus provide a valuable preliminary roadmap, highlighting that while several compounds from O. syriacum show promise, their advancement requires careful optimization and experimental validation to address the specific ADMET challenges identified.

The in silico ADMET analysis of the principal bioactive compounds from S. alba extracts provided a critical foundation for evaluating their therapeutic potential. The pentacyclic triterpenoids, betulinic acid and lupeol, exhibit properties typical of poorly bioavailable natural products (high molecular weight, extreme lipophilicity, and negligible aqueous solubility), which collectively explain their predicted absence of human intestinal absorption and very low permeability50. While their lipophilicity confers a high probability of blood-brain barrier penetration and their metabolic inertness is advantageous, these potential benefits are critically offset by the high predicted carcinogenicity risk and the significant absorption barrier, likely not recommended for oral uptake without advanced formulation strategies.

In contrast, phenolic acids and flavonoids present a more promising profile for systemic oral administration. p-coumaric acid and ferulic acid demonstrate excellent drug-like properties, with favorable solubility, moderate lipophilicity, and high predicted bioavailability, aligning well with Lipinski’s Rule of Five and suggesting a high likelihood of effective oral absorption50. Their placement within the BBB yolk of the BOILED-Egg model was an unexpected finding, as it suggests potential for central activity not typically emphasized for these compounds. However, a significant concern for this class is the marked propensity for hepatotoxicity predicted for the phenolic acids, and the high risk of drug-drug interactions associated with naringenin’s potent inhibition of CYP2C9 and CYP3A4, enzymes critical to the metabolism of a vast number of marketed drugs53. Herein, p-coumaric acid and ferulic acid emerge as the most viable compounds in S. alba, balancing excellent predicted absorption, minimal CYP450 interaction liability, and low carcinogenic risk. Their simple synthetic accessibility further enhances their appeal for development. However, the predicted hepatotoxicity for these acids and the significant CYP inhibition by naringenin are non-trivial liabilities that necessitate experimental validation in both in vitro and in vivo models. The triterpenoids, despite their challenges, can be considered suitable for central nervous system targets if their absorption and toxicity hurdles can be overcome through targeted drug delivery systems.

To contextualize our findings, it is essential to emphasize that the ADMET analysis presented here is predictive and hypothesis-generating, based on in-silico models. These computational tools, while valuable for initial screening, carry inherent limitations, including the potential for false positives and false negatives. Therefore, our results do not constitute validation and should not be interpreted as directly “comparable to marketed drugs.” They instead prioritize candidates for essential targeted next steps, including in-vitro ADME/Tox assays (e.g., cytotoxicity, hepatocyte clearance) and specific safety pharmacology studies (e.g., hERG channel assays), which are required to substantiate any therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrated the significant potential of O. syriacum and S. alba extracts as valuable sources of therapeutic agents against MDR bacterial pathogens. The research establishes a direct link between the antibacterial efficacy of the two plants and their specific phytochemical composition, which is further validated by favorable pharmacokinetic predictions. The ethanolic and acetonic extract of both plants exhibited significant activity against the MDR bacteria investigated, with O. syriacum acetone extract showing unique activity against MRSA. Importantly, GC-MS analysis identified the specific bioactive compounds responsible for this activity, as O. syriacum extracts were dominated by phenolic monoterpenes like thymol, while S. alba extracts were rich in phenolic acids, flavonoids, and triterpenoids.

The in-silico ADMET profiling suggests that some major constituents possess drug-like properties that are encouraging for early-stage drug development; however, experimental ADME/Tox validation is required. For O. syriacum, thymoquinone and eugenol emerged as promising leads due to their favorable balance of good absorption potential and manageable toxicity profiles. For S. alba, the phenolic acids p-coumaric acid and ferulic acid were highlighted as the most viable candidates, boasting excellent predicted oral bioavailability, low CYP interaction risk, and simple synthetic accessibility. The BOILED-Egg models corroborated these findings, confirming the high absorption potential for most of these key compounds. In essence, this work provides a robust, multi-faceted foundation for future drug development, having not only identified plant extracts with compelling antibacterial properties but also pinpointed the specific, pharmacokinetically promising constituent molecules within them, offering a clear and rational path for the development of novel natural product-based therapeutics to combat the global threat of antimicrobial resistance.
