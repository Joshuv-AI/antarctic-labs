Genetic determinants of childhood onset systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Meghan Nelson, Shanta Murthy, Sushma Maddipatla, Sreekala Shenoy, Lori Ponder, Shanmuganathan Chandrakasan, Subra Kugathasan, Dave Cutler, Sampath Prahalad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChildhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE) is associated with significant morbidity and mortality. While numerous variants have been associated with adult-onset SLE, limited data exist on genetic variation within cSLE. We aimed to investigate genetic factors of early-onset cSLE, defined as onset of cSLE prior to age 10.MethodsEmploying a case-only design, we performed whole genome sequencing analysis on 37 subjects with early onset cSLE. We hypothesised that rare, functional variants with large effects in genes associated with SLE contribute to the risk of early-onset cSLE and that the polygenic risk score (PRS) would be inversely associated with age of onset and presence of nephritis. A total of 153 linkage disequilibrium-independent variants were analysed and compared with previously reported SLE single-nucleotide polymorphisms. Rare (minor allele frequency (MAF) ≤1%), damaging protein-altering (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion; CADD≥20) variants, including variants isolated to previously reported monogenic SLE genes (n=49), were prioritised for secondary analysis.Results37.8% of our cSLE cohort carried at least one rare, pathogenic variant in monogenic SLE genes. We identified 31, 012 rare, damaging, pathogenic variants in our cSLE cohort, including two genes implicated in mitochondrial protein degradation (AFG3L2, SPG7) and several genes in the interferon pathway (IFIH1, IFNGR1). We found that higher PRS scores were associated with increased nephritis odds (p=0.0092) but not age of onset (p>0.05). Burden testing using the optimal sequence kernel association test (SKAT-O) revealed nominal enrichment of rare variants (MAF≤ 5%) in immune-related genes, including ISG15, PSMB9 and RNASEH2A, several of which are involved in type I interferon and antigen presentation pathways.ConclusionThese results have the potential to enhance our understanding of cSLE. Further studies must be conducted to expand our findings.Keywords: Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic; Lupus Nephritis; Autoimmunity

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multisystem autoimmune disease that is associated with significant disease damage, morbidity and mortality and disproportionately affects women and minorities.1 2 Up to 15% of individuals with SLE have onset in childhood.1,3 In contrast with the adult SLE (aSLE) patient population, childhood-onset SLE (cSLE) patients are at increased risk for development of both lupus nephritis and lupus cerebritis, which has significant associated disease morbidity.1 4 5 cSLE also tends to be more aggressive than in the adult population.1 4 5 Despite substantial improvements in the treatment of cSLE, there is significant variability in treatment responses and long-term outcomes.1,36

Population-based estimates from Southeastern US cohorts report cSLE prevalence around 1 per 100 000 children, though regional variations exist.7 Several studies in aSLE have shown a greater prevalence of SLE in the Southeastern USA compared with other regions.8 9 Within the Atlanta, Georgia metropolitan area, the Georgia Lupus Registry, used in the BeWELL (Black Women’s Experiences Living With Lupus) Study, uncovered racial disparities in both disease prevalence and outcomes.10 These observations underscore the combined influence of social determinants and genetic susceptibility on SLE risk and severity.

While regional and racial disparities highlight the impact of social determinants on disease burden, genetic susceptibility also plays a crucial role in SLE pathogenesis. Genome-wide association studies have identified more than 100 variants associated with SLE in adults.3 6 Additionally, studies of lupus in families with Mendelian patterns of inheritance have led to the identification of over 30 discrete genes predisposing to SLE as well as development of a polygenic risk score (PRS) in aSLE patients.11 12 Investigating early-onset cohorts of SLE, as well as many other diseases such as early-onset inflammatory bowel disease, has yielded novel genetic discoveries, highlighting important disease mechanisms.311 13,17 We believe that targeting early-onset cSLE will provide a genetically enriched cohort, which is a promising approach for identification of genetic factors predisposing SLE development. Furthermore, while specific variants have been linked to development of monogenic lupus, limited studies have assessed the genetic variation within cSLE or the role of adult lupus polygenic risk scores in explaining the genetics of cSLE, particularly with regards to early-onset cSLE.310 18,28

Utilizing DNA samples collected under the Southeastern Registry of Childhood Arthritis (SEARCH) protocol, we performed whole genome sequencing (WGS) on 37 children with early onset cSLE with disease onset prior to age 10, planning to test the hypothesis that rare and/or novel functional variants with large effects contribute to risk of early onset cSLE. We alternatively hypothesized that, given the reduced likelihood of identifying rare and/or novel variants in our limited sample size, application of a previously developed PRS in early onset cSLE would be associated with earlier age of onset and presence of nephritis.29 By applying previously validated aSLE polygenic risk scores and identifying novel or high-effect variants, we aim to refine our understanding of the genetic architecture of cSLE and elucidate disease-relevant pathways that may inform therapeutic stratification. Characterising the genetic underpinnings of early-onset cSLE will form a foundation for future studies of cSLE and advance molecular profiles of cSLE. In this context, our study addresses the paucity of data pertaining to this vulnerable and understudied patient population.

Methods

Study population

Patients newly diagnosed with cSLE at ≤10 years of age between January 2000 and July 2023 and followed at Children’s Healthcare of Atlanta (CHOA) in Atlanta, Georgia and enrolled in the SEARCH with DNA samples available were included in our analysis. Our studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board at Emory University in Atlanta, Georgia, USA and all study participants gave informed consent (IRB00018494). All patients met at least 4 of the 17 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) classification criteria for SLE, including at least one clinical and one immunologic criterion.30 31 Demographic, laboratory and clinical data from the time of diagnosis to July 2023 were extracted from the electronic medical record (EMR) utilised at CHOA. SLE disease activity was measured using the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) utilising documented EMR data.32 33 Disease duration was defined as time of enrollment from time of disease onset. Patients with renal dysfunction, as evidenced by abnormal renal function, persistent proteinuria, or elevated creatinine, underwent renal biopsy and pathology classified according to the International Society of Nephrology classification for lupus nephritis.34 Patients diagnosed with central nervous system disease met criteria for one or greater neuropsychiatric syndromes in SLE, specifically including neuropathy, lupus cerebritis and/or seizures.35 Clinical information, including patient-reported outcomes, age of onset and sociodemographic measures, was available per protocol for secondary analysis. While our study did not involve patients in its design due to the use of anonymized datasets, we plan to engage patients to disseminate findings and ensure accessible communication. Summarized testing results and study outcomes, without identifying information, will be published and made available to all participants.

Whole genome sequencing

A total of 37 patients diagnosed with childhood-onset lupus ≤10 years of age treated at the Children’s Healthcare of Atlanta who consented for genome-wide sequencing were included in the study. Peripheral blood was collected and DNA was isolated from patients. Paired-end WGS using an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform was completed by Emory Integrated Genomics Core (DLS Hudson Alpha) using extracted DNA from the peripheral blood of SLE patients (read depth=62 x). Variant and base calling were performed with PECaller36 and functional annotation with Bystro.37 Quality control (QC) was performed using PLINK V.2.0 and included removing variants with missingness >5%, relatedness (PI HAT, or proportion of identity by descent, <0.44), discordant sex information and significant deviations from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. To ensure high-confidence variant calls, we applied stringent QC to case genotypes prior to rare variant burden testing. Genotype quality (GQ) scores were extracted from the variant call format (VCF) file and assessed across all samples. We masked genotype calls with GQ<20, resulting in 115,822 genotype calls (<0.2%) being set to missing for downstream rare variant analysis. Sequences were aligned to the human reference sequence library (GRCh38) using Burrows-Wheeler Alignment. Ultimately, one sample was removed based on exhibiting outlier genotypes based on metrics including ratio of heterozygote/homozygous sites and theta value (a count of number of heterozygotes per sample) (remaining n=37). High frequency variants were pruned by linkage disequilibrium (LD) prior to calculation for PRS based on published SLE variants (online supplemental table 3). To account for ancestral variability in allele frequencies, PRS calculation was weighted by ancestry using previously published disease related SNPs and centred around a population mean of 0 and an SD of 1.38 We prioritised either published variants or genes associated with SLE development (Polygenic Risk Score calculation or PRS), or rare (minor allele frequency, or MAF, <1% as found in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) database) protein-altering variants (http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org). We predicted deleterious variants according to Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) ≥20 scores. To confirm the self-reported ancestral groups of the participants, ADMIXTURE software (V.1.3.0) was used, wherein patients with an ancestral proportion of 80% or higher were assigned to the corresponding ancestral group; those with less than 60% were categorized as ‘other’.39 Participants were stratified into four ancestral groups which were reported: African (AFR), European (EUR), Asian (Pooled South Asian (SAS), or East Asian (EAS)) and Amerindian (AMR).

Polygenic risk score

We hypothesised that we would observe an inverse relationship between PRS and age of onset data due to increased burden of SLE-related risk variants in our cSLE patient population.1129 40,42 The PRS was calculated using established variants in aSLE literature, applied to our early-onset cSLE cohort.29 40 43 The variants were weighted using these ancestry-specific SNPs.29 40 43 We filtered a list of 243 SNPs that corresponded to our WGS variants and previously published SLE genome wide associate study (GWAS) hits to LD-independent variants; the resulting 153 LD-independent SNPs used for this analysis were identified from this previous list by only including one significant SNP in cases where multiple SNPs were within 100 kilobases of one another. Rare variant analysis

For rare variant burden analysis, we subsetted to high-confidence variants by applying the following filters: (1) MAF ≤ 0.01, based on ancestry-specific gnomAD allele frequencies; (2) CADD score ≥ 20, indicating variants predicted to be among the top 1% most deleterious substitutions in the human genome and (3) functional coding consequences, defined as variants annotated in the exonic region with predicted effects on protein sequence. Specifically, functional exonic variants included those classified as non-synonymous single nucleotide variants (SNV) (missense), frameshift insertions or deletions, stopgain, stoploss or splicing alterations based on the refSeq.exonicAlleleFunction annotation.

Sample ancestry was assigned using admixture results as outlined above, and variants were filtered accordingly using the appropriate gnomAD population allele frequency field. These filtering criteria were applied to focus the analysis on rare, likely pathogenic variants within 49 protein-coding genes relevant to monogenic forms of SLE as reported in previous studies.44 45 Analyses were performed using R Studio (2023.09.0+463). Pathways were determined using Gene Ontology, KEGG and Reactome data, with pathway mapping performed on Reactome and g:profiler.45,48 Gene-based associations were considered significant if false discovery rate (FDR) q≤ 0.05.

Rare variant comparison with population controls

To assess whether the rare functional variants identified in our cohort were present in healthy individuals, we queried the phase 3 release of the 1000 Genomes Project (GRCh38), which includes genotype data from >2500 individuals across global populations.44 Using bcftools, we extracted genotypes at the exact chromosome:position:reference:alternate coordinates for all 14 rare, pathogenic variants (filtered for minor allele frequency <1% and CADD>20). We focused our comparison on 120 individuals from the 1000 Genomes dataset with predominantly African ancestry (70%) and admixed or European ancestry (30%), matched to the genetic background of our cohort. None of the 14 variants was found in the full 1000 Genomes dataset or within this ancestry-matched control subset, suggesting that these variants are likely ultra-rare to the disease population.

SKAT-O analysis

To enable rare variant burden testing, we performed the Optimal Sequence Kernel Association Test, or SKAT-O, on our cSLE case cohort genotypes and publicly available ancestry-matched control data from the 1000 Genomes Project.49 50 Control samples (n=120) were selected based on matched ancestry proportions of our case dataset and genotype matrices were harmonised to match our case dataset in genome build and variant representation. To maximise power and reduce the burden of multiple comparisons with our modest sample size, we tested whether previously reported monogenic cSLE genes (n=49) andmajor histocompatibility complex (MHC) genes containing low-frequency variants (MAF<5%) were associated with cSLE.28 Prior to principal component analysis, any variant missing genotypes were removed prior to analysis. We adjusted for the first five genetic principal components derived from principal component analysis of the combined cases and controls.49 For each analysis, multiple comparison corrections were performed using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold. Analysis was performed using the SKAT package in R and PLINK V.2.0.

Results

Our study included analysis of genomic data on 37 patients with diagnosed early childhood-onset systemic lupus, all of whom met the SLICC criteria. The majority of patients were female (78%) with a mean age at diagnosis of 8±1.7 years (online supplemental figure 1). The cohort was mostly of African descent (64.8%), followed by European (27.0%), Asian (5.2%) with the remaining 1% Amerindian (table 1). Approximately 26% of our cohort was diagnosed with Class IV/V lupus nephritis and 2.6% was diagnosed with neuropsychiatric disease (table 1).

Genome-wide sequencing identified a total of 18,642,778 unique variants postfiltering among the 37 patients enrolled. Of this, there were 868,586 unique variants overlapping between published GWAS and our cSLE data.29 Within all variant mutations of SLE samples, there was a mean transition to transversion ratio of 2.1±0.03; within coding variants, there was a mean transition to transversion ratio of 3.2±0.11. Within all variant mutations of SLE samples, there was a mean silent to replacement ratio of 1.1±0.01.

Applying the PRS calculation after LD pruning as described in our methodology to our cSLE cohort, we were able to apply 153 variants to the PRS calculation. PRS ranged from −0.49 to 0.77 with a median of 0.12 (table 2). Distributions of PRS per sex and per ancestry are depicted in figure 1. Sensitivity analysis revealed similar results, with PRS ranging from −0.43 to 0.61 with a median of 0.19 using the Wang et al variants (online supplemental table 1)43 and PRS ranging from −0.67 to 0.51 with a median of 0.04 using the Langefeld et al’s variants40 (online supplemental table 2). Distributions of PRS per ancestry across variant sets are depicted in online supplemental figure 2. To investigate a relationship between PRS between age of onset, we performed a regression analysis where the coefficient of determination was not found to predict the outcome (R2=0.08). We also performed linear regression of PRS and age of onset across all variant sets; no variant set reached statistical significance, although a trend was appreciated with the Langefeld et al variant set (p=0.09, online supplemental table 4, online supplemental figure 2). We also compared PRS across variant sets, between ancestry and across sex; no statistically significant differences were found (online supplemental table 4).

Rare variant association testing using SKAT-O was performed on a targeted gene set comprising the MHC region and a subset of monogenic lupus-associated genes (n=49), focusing on variants with MAF<5%, and comparing cSLE cases to ancestry-matched controls. Several genes demonstrated nominal evidence of rare variant enrichment among cases. Notably, ISG15 (p=0.015), PSMB9 (p=0.020) and RNASEH2A (p=0.034) all reached nominal significance thresholds. While none of the genes surpassed a FDR threshold of q<0.05, these findings suggest potential roles for rare variants in these genes in cSLE susceptibility.

Discussion

In our diverse cSLE cohort, we found 14 rare, pathogenic variants in the exonic regions of 10 monogenic SLE genes. We found the burden of these variants to be 38% across samples, which is higher than reports from other studies.28 38 42 CADD scores of monogenic SLE gene variants ranged from 23.1 to 28.3. In comparison, across all genes, the average burden of rare, predicted damaging variants was 14.5% across our cSLE patients, with a maximum CADD pathogenicity score of 43. We did not detect significant associations between variant burden in rare (MAF≤0.01), pathogenic (ClinVar) and predicted damaging variants with CADD≥20 with respect to nephritis (p=0.6), age of onset (p=0.42) or SLEDAI scores (p=0.4). Additional studies incorporating larger sample sizes will be required to further interrogate this relationship.

Furthermore, while previous studies have revealed that earlier age of SLE onset is linked with higher genetic burden of SLE risk variants51, we did not observe this association in our cSLE cohort, with a median PRS of 0.11 and the highest burden of PRS 0.77 (36, 39, 52). Moreover, we did not find a strong inverse relationship between PRS and age of onset (R2=0.08). Within our limited sample size, PRS burden did not exhibit differences across ancestries as well as across sexes. We anticipate our absence of association between age of onset and PRS is likely due to limited power given our modest sample size as well as selection of early-onset cSLE, and therefore limited ability to detect this phenomenon due to limited range of age of onset, as suggested by a study by Hiraki et al focused on monogenic cSLE.42 Additionally, in Webb et al, the correlation between increased genetic risk and earlier onset of SLE was only observed in patients of African descent and not in Hispanic or European patients also noted to have more severe disease; interestingly, in a publication by Langefield et al, this association was observed in Hispanic Americans and not African-Americans.40 41 In Webb et al, they conveyed that the absolute difference in the number of risk alleles between cSLE and aSLE was small, and historically derived largely from GWAS common variants elucidated in patients of European ancestry and ultimately from larger haplotype blocks.41 While Webb et al proposed that differences in genetic susceptibility might account for the differences observed in clinical phenotype in their African-American patient populations, we believe this illustrates the continued need for diversification of enrollment in genetic studies. Moreover, recent work by Sayadi et al determined that cSLE patients did not have a higher PRS compared with control aSLE patients in cSLE patients of Swedish ancestry, highlighting the importance of rare pathogenic variants in driving disease presentation.52 Ultimately, the genetics of cSLE are complex, involving both common and rare variants, likely along with other factors such as epigenetics and gene-gene interactions in disease development. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to fully elucidate these contributions.

Notably, rare variant burden appeared higher among participants of African ancestry in our cohort (online supplemental figure 3). This finding should be interpreted in the context of population genetic structure and the continued under-representation of non-European populations in genomic reference databases, which may influence variant classification and pathogenicity prediction. Importantly, disparities in cSLE severity across racial and ethnic groups are unlikely to be explained by inherited genetic variation alone. Children from historically marginalised communities may encounter barriers to timely diagnosis, access to paediatric rheumatology care and continuity of treatment, which can influence disease outcomes. These observations reinforce the need for inclusive genomic studies that both improve variant interpretation across diverse populations and situate genetic findings within the broader social and healthcare contexts in which disease manifests.

We did observe an association between PRS and presence of nephritis, wherein for every unit of increased PRS, the log odds for nephritis increased (p=0.0092). This finding may reflect that lupus nephritis is both more common and more severe in cSLE compared with adult onset SLE disease. Alternatively, the loci found to be associated with lupus nephritis in adult GWAS, such as HLA-DRB1, STAT4, IRF553, may be more influential in cSLE disease; mechanistically, this could occur potentially either through genetics, gene-environment interactions or even epigenetic changes.39 Further studies are needed to further investigate these important modulators of genetic risk in cSLE patient cohorts.

We then considered potentially biologically relevant variants as defined above, and identified rare, damaging, pathogenic variants in our cSLE cohort, including two genes involved in mitochondrial protein degradation (AFG3L2 and SPG7) and several genes in the interferon pathway (IFIH1, IFNGR1 and IGF2R), well described pathways in lupus pathogenesis.47 54 Our gene set also included two genes involved in the double-stranded DNA break response (BARD1, RAD52) and numerous variants involved in complement cascade (including C8B), important pathways also implicated in the pathogenesis of SLE (table 3).54,57 Additional genes implicated include zinc finger domains (including ZNF687) and mitochondrial transmembrane proteins (including TMEM126B), similar to pathways identified in recent transmission disequilibrium testing performed in cSLE by Lewandowski et al.39 Of note, there were 14 patients with rare, pathogenic variants in known monogenic SLE genes. These patients phenotypically demonstrated high disease activity at the time of enrollment, with an average SLEDAI of 10, compared with an average SLEDAI of 7.6 for the patients without monogenic SLE variants.

In this study, rare variant burden testing using SKAT-O identified nominal enrichment of rare variants (MAF<5%) in several immune-related genes in cSLE patients, including ISG15, PSMB9 and RNASEH2A. These genes are functionally connected to pathways long implicated in lupus pathogenesis.58,60 ISG15, an interferon-stimulated gene, is central to downstream interferon signalling and immune activation and has recently been shown to regulate STING activation.58 PSMB9, located within the MHC region, encodes a component of the immunoproteasome and plays a role in antigen presentation, reinforcing the well-established contribution of MHC-linked immune regulation in SLE.59 RNASEH2A is part of the RNase H2 complex, mutations in which are associated with Aicardi-Goutières syndrome, an interferonopathy with overlapping features of lupus.60 Although none of these associations remained significant after multiple testing correction, the biological relevance of these genes supports the hypothesis that rare, deleterious variants in key innate and adaptive immune pathways may contribute to cSLE pathogenesis.

In the context of disease specificity, our cSLE patients had rare, pathogenic variants in pathways unique to cSLE, including complement-mediated pathways (C8B) as well as in the solute-carrier gene superfamily (SLC22A2 and SLC7A9) also found enriched in recent work by Lewandowski et al.28 In contrast, Correia Marques et al found enrichment in familial haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis genes, such as LYST and STXBP2 in WGS of systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) patients not appreciated in our cohort.61 Work analysing DNA and RNA sequencing in juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) by Meng et al found enrichment in rare variants in NPIPB5 and ODF2L also not appreciated in our cSLE cohort62; in their gene expression analysis, however, they observed the upregulation of multiple genes between subtypes of JIA and sJIA, including TNFAIP3 and NFKBIA, suggesting aberrant expression of common inflammatory pathways between JIA subtypes and previously reported pathways in cSLE.62

Potential limitations of our study include our modest sample size, which limited our power to detect associations. We also focused on singleton analysis due to sample availability, as opposed to duo or trio analysis, which would be particularly useful for de novo variant analysis. Additionally, we were not able to perform functional validation of our variants, although we did cross-reference with previously published GWAS data. With respect to strengths, our study included a relatively diverse ancestry representation of a particularly extreme phenotype (early-onset cSLE) of which there are limited biological samples. Furthermore, we adjusted PRS application by ancestry ensuring a more appropriate application of risk variants and subsequent interpretation of risk. Future studies of additional multicenter cohorts with increased diversity are needed to further validate our findings. Overall, our study reinforces the essential role of genome sequencing in elucidating disease mechanisms among patients with extreme phenotypes, including early-onset cSLE, given the heterogeneous nature of SLE. Our findings emphasise the necessity of broadening ancestral representation in genomic studies to delineate the impact of variable allele frequencies on disease susceptibility as well as further advocates for integrative analyses of genetic, epigenetic and environmental determinants driving cSLE pathogenesis.
