Background

Pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS) is a rare, benign proliferative disorder of the synovium that most commonly affects the knee joint, although it can occur in any synovial joint [1]. The exact etiology remains unclear, but several mechanisms have been proposed, including repetitive trauma, recurrent hemarthrosis, abnormal synovial cell proliferation, and neoplastic processes. Clinically, PVNS is generally classified into localized and diffuse forms [2, 3].

Although uncommon, PVNS has been reported after total knee arthroplasty (TKA), where it is thought to represent reactive synovial proliferation associated with polyethylene wear debris from prosthetic components [4]. In the evaluation of TKA failure, the high prevalence of aseptic loosening may lead clinicians to overlook rare underlying pathologies such as PVNS. Furthermore, PVNS occurring after TKA is often difficult to distinguish clinically and radiologically from polyethylene wear–induced synovitis, which may delay diagnosis [5].

Previous studies have suggested that PVNS after TKA is typically diagnosed within approximately nine years after the index procedure [5, 6]. In this report, we describe a rare case of diffuse PVNS associated with recurrent hemarthrosis that was diagnosed 21 years after primary TKA.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old man presented with a one-year history of left knee pain. He had undergone primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) for osteoarthritis 21 years earlier at another institution. No medical records indicated evidence of PVNS in the synovium at the time of the initial surgery, and no postoperative findings suggestive of PVNS had been reported. The function of the affected knee remained excellent for approximately 20 years after surgery, allowing full weight-bearing and unrestricted daily activities. After this prolonged asymptomatic period, the patient developed recurrent knee swelling and joint effusion. Arthrocentesis was performed on three occasions, each confirming hemarthrosis, and the patient was referred to our institution for further evaluation.

On physical examination, tenderness, swelling, and limited range of motion of the knee joint (0–80°) were observed. The patient had no history of trauma or infection. Laboratory tests, including C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and rheumatoid factor, were within normal limits. Plain radiographs demonstrated a radiolucent line (> 2 mm) beneath the medial aspect of the tibial component, with slight subsidence of the prosthesis, raising suspicion of prosthetic loosening (Fig. 1). Computed tomography (CT) with metal artifact reduction revealed marked osteolytic changes confined to the medial tibial condyle adjacent to the prosthesis (Fig. 2). Based on these findings, aseptic loosening of the tibial component was diagnosed, and revision TKA was performed.

Intraoperatively, extensive diffuse synovial hypertrophy characterized by yellowish-brown papillary projections and nodules was unexpectedly observed around the prosthesis. The tibial component was loosened from the tibial metaphysis and was associated with bone defects in the proximal tibia and severe wear of the polyethylene liner. After complete synovectomy (Fig. 3), revision TKA was performed using the Vanguard SSK Revision Knee System (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA), consisting of a femoral component, an I-Beam tibial tray, and a 14-mm polyethylene insert (Fig. 4).

Gross photograph shows the excised synovium, which appears as a firm, grey-yellowish tissue

A Postoperative anteroposterior (AP) radiograph and (B) lateral radiograph show the knee status after revision total knee arthroplasty

Gross examination of the resected synovium revealed villonodular fronds on the surface. Histopathological examination with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining demonstrated characteristic villonodular architecture surrounded by hyperplastic synovial tissue at low magnification (×40) (Fig. 5A). Papillary projections and hypercellular nodular synovium were also observed (Fig. 5B). At higher magnification (×200), the lesion consisted predominantly of mononuclear histiocytoid cells with scattered multinucleated giant cells (Fig. 6A). Numerous foamy histiocytes and hemosiderin-laden macrophages were also identified throughout the tissue (Fig. 6B), confirming the diagnosis of diffuse PVNS.

Discussion

Pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS) is a proliferative disorder of the synovial membrane characterized by excessive synovial hyperplasia and hemosiderin deposition. Although its exact pathogenesis remains uncertain, it has been described as either an inflammatory or neoplastic process and may present in localized or diffuse forms. PVNS is known for its locally aggressive behavior and relatively high recurrence rate, which are important considerations in clinical management [3, 7].

PVNS occurring after arthroplasty is rare but has been reported as a potential cause of persistent synovitis, recurrent hemarthrosis, and implant failure. However, only a limited number of cases have been described in the literature, and most were diagnosed within approximately 10 years after the index arthroplasty [4, 5, 8, 9].

The present case represents an unusually delayed presentation of PVNS, diagnosed more than two decades after the primary total knee arthroplasty. The patient maintained satisfactory clinical function for nearly 20 years after the initial procedure before developing recurrent hemarthrosis during the year preceding presentation. Such a prolonged asymptomatic interval highlights the potential for late-onset synovial pathology after arthroplasty.

A period of two decades after total knee arthroplasty is sufficient for polyethylene wear to occur. Xu et al. [10] suggested that polyethylene wear particles can activate macrophages and induce chronic synovial inflammation through the CSF1/CSF1R signaling pathway, ultimately contributing to periprosthetic osteolysis and implant loosening. In the present case, it is plausible that chronic synovial changes associated with polyethylene wear particles gradually developed over the 20-year period, although the progression may have remained clinically silent.

Nevertheless, the precise relationship between PVNS and the inflammatory process remains uncertain. It is unclear whether chronic synovitis associated with wear particles led to recurrent hemarthrosis and subsequently contributed to the development of PVNS, or whether PVNS developed first and resulted in recurrent hemarthrosis. Therefore, the direct causal relationship between these processes remains speculative, and further large-scale studies are required to clarify this association.

Based on the findings of this case, routine synovial biopsy during revision total knee arthroplasty may be helpful when implant loosening is suspected. Histopathological evaluation may reveal synovial abnormalities, including chronic synovitis, foreign-body reactions induced by microscopic polyethylene wear particles, and proliferative synovial disorders such as PVNS. Panciera et al. [11] reported that patients with PVNS undergoing total knee arthroplasty have a higher risk of postoperative complications, suggesting that the intrinsic proliferative nature of PVNS may contribute to implant loosening.

Although the overall diagnostic yield of routine synovial biopsy during arthroplasty has been reported to be relatively low [12], such evaluation may still provide valuable information regarding synovial pathology and facilitate the identification of uncommon conditions such as PVNS.

Conclusion

This case highlights an exceptionally delayed presentation of pigmented villonodular synovitis more than two decades after primary total knee arthroplasty. In patients with TKA who develop recurrent hemarthrosis or unexplained synovitis, PVNS should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Furthermore, synovial biopsy during revision arthroplasty may help identify proliferative synovial disorders that might otherwise remain unrecognized.
