Pure ventriculoscopic resection of an intraventricular meningioma with a basket retriever through a single burr hole: illustrative case
Authors: Dongling Pei, ShengKun Lang, Shujing Yao, Rui Feng, Xinghua Xu, Xiaolei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDEndoscopic approaches are being increasingly used for the management of intraventricular lesions. These techniques, however, have important limitations and can result in significant postoperative complications. Endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) is an established minimally invasive treatment for obstructive hydrocephalus, particularly in children and adolescents.1 In selected patients, the same endoscopic corridor can provide access to intraventricular lesions, allowing simultaneous management of CSF pathway obstruction and focal pathology in a single procedure.2,3 However, tumors or tumor-like lesions arising from the choroid plexus present specific challenges. These lesions are often small and pedunculated and tend to swing away from rigid instruments once manipulated.

To overcome these limitations, there has been a growing interest in adapting instruments from other specialties for use in neuroendoscopy.4,5 Flexible nitinol stone baskets (NSBs), widely used in urology for endoscopic retrieval of calculi, have features that are well suited to the ventricular system.6,7 An NSB has a small-caliber, flexible shaft and a collapsible, expandable basket deployable through standard endoscopic channels. It can expand for ETV and encage and stabilize smooth, spherical structures. Only isolated reports have described their use for intraventricular procedures, primarily as salvage tools for free-floating fragments rather than as front-line devices in a planned surgical strategy.7

We report the case of a 13-year-old boy with obstructive hydrocephalus and a small lesion in the trigone of the left lateral ventricle. Through a single endoscopic approach, we performed ETV and removed the lesion using an NSB, which first initiated the ETV and then encaged and stabilized the tumor for its removal.

Illustrative Case

A 13-year-old boy with progressive worsening of gait disturbance and urinary incontinence for 4 years was admitted to our hospital. Head MRI confirmed obstructive hydrocephalus with an obstructed aqueduct (Fig. 1A–C). Preoperative MRI also demonstrated a small, round lesion in the trigone of the left lateral ventricle, measuring approximately 5 mm in diameter and attached to the choroid plexus by a narrow stalk (Fig. 1D). The atrial lesion and aqueductal obstruction were considered coexisting but likely unrelated pathologies. We expect that both pathologies could be treated in a single operative session, as this approach is technically feasible and minimally invasive. ETV was offered as the primary treatment followed by the endoscopic resection of the small atrial lesion. The patient’s parents consented to surgery.

Initial MR images of the head obtained in the patient revealing obstructive hydrocephalus with a small, round lesion located in the trigone of the left lateral ventricle. A and B: Axial T2-weighted images of the enlarged ventricles. C:Sagittal T2-weighted 3D SPACE sequence revealing obstruction (arrow) in the aqueduct. D: Axial 3D SPACE image depicting a small, round lesion (arrow) in the trigone of the left lateral ventricle.

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed supine with slight neck flexion. A right frontal linear 3-cm incision was made, and a burr hole was created. The burr hole was planned primarily for ventricular access and ETV, while still allowing redirection toward the trigonal lesion through the same endoscopic corridor. An endoscopic procedure was performed using a ventriculoscope (LOTTA, Karl Storz). A 3.2-Fr helical NSB (NForce, Cook Urological Inc.) (Fig. 2A) was used for ETV and the following lesion removal.

The ventriculoscope was inserted into the dilated right lateral ventricle and the third ventricle. The floor of the third ventricle was then visualized. The NSB, introduced through the instrument channel of the scope, was used with its blunt tip to perforate the third ventricular floor (Fig. 2B). The fenestration was then enlarged by expanding the NSB until the stoma reached a satisfactory diameter (> 6 mm) (Fig. 2C).

After achieving the principal treatment goal (ETV), we performed an aqueductal inspection to confirm our imaging findings on 3D SPACE images, followed by aqueductoplasty to restore an additional CSF pathway in the presence of focal aqueductal obstruction. With a small-diameter (2 mm) flexible scope (FlexScope, Karl Storz), this manipulation was easily and safely done. When the ventriculoscope was tilted through the perforated septum pellucidum and advanced to the trigone of the left ventricle, a small, round lesion was then identified (Fig. 2D). It appeared as a smooth, solid tumor with a narrow stalk. Initial attempts to grasp the tumor and control its stalk using standard endoscopic instruments were unsuccessful, as the lesion swung away with each maneuver. At this point, conventional endoscopic bipolar electrocautery can not be applied to coagulate the stalk. After several attempts, small bleeding occurred (Fig. 2E).

A microbipolar forceps (WO165123, Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH) was used to coagulate the stalk (Fig. 2F). The NSB was then inserted through the endoscope’s irrigation channel and deployed to trap and stabilize the lesion (Fig. 2G). Once the basket had securely encaged the tumor, ventriculoscopic scissors were used to transect the lesion stalk (Fig. 2H). Because the tumor was too big to pass through the endoscope’s instrument channel, it was held with the NSB (Fig. 2I) and removed en bloc together with the endoscope lens through the endoscopic sheath. Throughout the procedure, warm Ringer’s solution (37°C) was continuously used for irrigation until hemostasis was confirmed. The burr hole and incision were then closed in standard fashion. The postoperative course was uneventful, and no new neurological deficits were observed. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 7. At discharge, his hydrocephalus-related symptoms had resolved. The lesion was completely removed (Fig. 3A). Histopathological analysis identified the lesion as an angioblast meningioma (WHO grade I). At the 12-year follow-up, he remained symptom free, with no evidence of tumor recurrence and no requirement for CSF shunting (Fig. 3B–D).

Discussion

Observations

This case demonstrates the feasibility of combining ETV with simultaneous removal of a small intraventricular tumor through a single burr hole endoscopic approach, using a flexible helical NSB as a key adjunct. The strategy achieved definitive CSF diversion and gross-total tumor removal with durable long-term control, thereby avoiding shunt placement or additional craniotomies.

The lesion’s morphology and mobility posed the “floating target” challenge. The tumor was small, spherical, and attached to the choroid plexus by a narrow stalk. This configuration allows the lesion to easily swing within the ventricular cavity. Standard rigid graspers and scissors, operating through a single working channel, could not maintain stable control. This scenario exemplifies a recurrent limitation of intraventricular endoscopy: rigid tools often fail to control small, pedunculated, mobile targets.8

For this challenge, we offered our basket solution. The simultaneous management of obstructive hydrocephalus and intraventricular tumors via a single endoscopic trajectory represents an attractive and elegant minimally invasive strategy.7 However, as illustrated in this case, small, pedunculated choroidal lesions pose a unique hydrodynamic challenge.9 Our case demonstrates that the cross-disciplinary adaptation of an NSB effectively overcomes this limitation, transforming a mobile, elusive target into a stabilized entity amenable to safe resection. Additionally, when it is not expanded, the NSB itself can be easily passed through the irrigation channel of the ventriculoscope, so that standard instruments, for example, bipolar cautery forceps or endoscopic scissors, can be inserted through the instrument channel at the same time. This mechanism effectively replicates the bimanual traction-countertraction technique used in microsurgery, which is otherwise difficult to achieve in the single-instrument environment of standard ventriculoscopy. During this procedure, there is a potential risk of inadvertent capture of adjacent vascular or choroidal structures. Hence, the basket should be used only under direct endoscopic visualization. This technique is most appropriate for small, mobile, pedunculated lesions, and may be unsuitable for broadly attached lesions.

Beyond its utility in tumor stabilization, the NSB demonstrated great versatility as a multipurpose tool. In our procedure, the blunt tip of the collapsed NSB was used to perforate the floor of the third ventricle, initiating the ETV. The ventriculostomy could be enlarged by expanding the basket. This off-label application suggests that for selected cases, the NSB could reduce the need for multiple instrument exchanges—a factor known to correlate with intraoperative ventricular hemorrhage and extended operative time.10

The extraction strategy further underscored the advantage of the NSB over the conventional instruments. Because the size of the tumor exceeded the internal diameter of the working channel (2.3 mm), direct retrieval through the channel was not feasible. Instead, after complete disconnection, the lesion was encaged and retrieved with the NSB and the endoscope lens through the endoscopic sheath. Prior basket stabilization and meticulous stalk coagulation minimized the risk of hemorrhage or tumor fragmentation.

Clinically, this combined approach resulted in an uneventful postoperative course, rapid symptom resolution, and durable radiological and clinical stability for 12 years without the need for placement of a ventriculoperitoneal shunt. These outcomes demonstrated both good oncological outcomes after resection and the long-term effectiveness of the ETV. More broadly, the case illustrates how cross-disciplinary adoption of urological devices can expand the neuroendoscopic toolbox. Historically, the use of stone baskets in neurosurgery has been largely confined to “salvage” maneuvers for retrieving dropped, disparate fragments.6 This case serves as a proof-of-concept demonstration for expanded use of the NSB in neuroendoscopy. This is particularly relevant for lesions located in the trigone or the posterior third of the lateral ventricle, where manipulation of rigid instruments is restricted.11

Lessons

In this case, use of a flexible NSB as both an ETV perforator and a stabilizing cage for a mobile, pedunculated meningioma enabled gross-total resection, restoration of CSF circulation, and long-term clinical stability without shunt placement. This method is best suited for small, firm, stalked lesions within the lateral and third ventricles. In carefully selected patients, an NSB can be a valuable adjunct to rigid neuroendoscopy, helping stabilize and resect intraventricular tumors while performing ETV in the same session.
