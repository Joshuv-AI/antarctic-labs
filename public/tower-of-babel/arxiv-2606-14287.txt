Surface-tension calibration for N-phase mixtures
Marco F.P. ten Eikelder, Aaron Brunk

Abstract
Diffuse-interface (phase-field) models are a widely used framework for interfacial dynamics in complex fluids, in which sharp interfaces are replaced by smooth transition layers and interfacial forces follow from a free-energy functional. In these models, surface tensions and diffuse thicknesses are not prescribed directly but are encoded implicitly by the bulk multiwell potential and the gradient-energy term through one-dimensional equilibrium profiles. While this link is classical in the binary Cahn–Hilliard setting, calibrating multiphase models is substantially more delicate because multiple pairwise surface tensions must be matched simultaneously and the relevant equilibrium paths are constrained by the Gibbs simplex. The practical problem is therefore: given a chosen bulk potential and a set of target pairwise surface tensions, determine gradient-energy coefficients that reproduce these targets in the full multiphase model. Here we present a thermodynamically consistent calibration procedure for N-phase diffuse-interface free energies of Cahn–Hilliard type. The method determines a symmetric capillary matrix that matches prescribed pairwise surface tensions through the model’s equilibrium profiles. We further introduce a rescaling strategy that adjusts diffuse interface widths to mesh-resolvable values while preserving the calibrated surface tensions. The resulting calibrated free-energy closure can be incorporated directly into N-phase mixture simulations, and we demonstrate this by applying it to N-phase Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard flows.

2.1.  Gibbs simplex and admissible variables

We consider the vector of volume fractions
\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{N})
under the usual saturation constraint that
\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}=1
. Hence, the vector of volume fractions contains non-negative contributions satisfying the saturation constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}=1.

(2.1)

Hence
\bm{\phi}
lies in the Gibbs simplex
\mathcal{G}
which is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\{\bm{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:0\leq\phi_{\alpha}\leq 1,\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}=1\}.

(2.2)

Correspondingly, for fixed
i
, the vector
\partial_{x_{i}}\bm{\phi}
lies in the tangent space to the saturation hyperplane given by

T(\mathcal{G}):=\{\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}=0\}.

(2.3)

This reflects the fact that only
N-1
volume fractions are independent. An example Gibbs triangle with the associated coordinate axes and simplex boundaries is shown in Figure
1
. Furthermore, we refer to
33
for more details on the Gibbs simplex construction.

2.2.  Coexistence points and grand potential

This subsection introduces coexistence points for the bulk free-energy density
\Psi_{0}
on the Gibbs simplex. To account for the saturation constraint, we use a constrained bulk potential. This provides a convenient way to define the tangent hyperplanes and grand potentials associated with coexisting bulk states.

Since the volume fractions satisfy the saturation constraint (
2.1
), derivatives of
\Psi_{0}
have to be interpreted on the saturation hyperplane. We account for this by introducing
the constrained bulk potential

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,\lambda}(\bm{\phi}):=\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})+\lambda\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\phi_{\alpha}-1\right),

(2.4)

where the scalar
\lambda
is the Lagrange multiplier of the saturation constraint (
2.1
). On the Gibbs simplex
\mathcal{G}
we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,\lambda}(\bm{\phi})=\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi}),

(2.5)

but its unconstrained derivative is

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{0,\lambda}=\bm{\mu}_{0,\lambda}(\bm{\phi}):=\frac{\partial\Psi_{0,\lambda}}{\partial\bm{\phi}}=\frac{\partial\Psi_{0}}{\partial\bm{\phi}}+\lambda\mathbf{1}.

(2.6)

Therefore the bulk derivative is only determined up to an additive multiple of
\bm{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
. Different choices of
\lambda
give the same action on admissible variations, since for all

\delta\bm{\phi}\in T\mathcal{G}
one has

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{0,\lambda}\cdot\delta\bm{\phi}=\frac{\partial\Psi_{0}}{\partial\bm{\phi}}\cdot\delta\bm{\phi}.

(2.7)

We now consider the existence and description of coexistence points. To this end we first introduce the tangent plane and the grand potential. Given a point
\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{G}
, we define the tangent hyperplane to
\Psi_{0}
at
\mathbf{b}
, restricted to the saturation hyperplane, by

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{b}}(\bm{\phi}):=\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b})+\bm{\mu}_{0,\lambda}(\mathbf{b})\cdot\left(\bm{\phi}-\mathbf{b}\right).

(2.8)

The value of
T_{\mathbf{b}}
on the saturation hyperplane is independent of the particular choice of
\lambda
, since

\bm{\phi}-\mathbf{b}\in T(\mathcal{G})
for

\bm{\phi},\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{G}
. The grand potential relative to
\mathbf{b}
is the excess of the bulk free energy above this tangent hyperplane:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathbf{b}}(\bm{\phi}):=\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})-T_{\mathbf{b}}(\bm{\phi}).

(2.9)

By construction
\Omega_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{b})=0
. Furthermore, if
\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}
, then
\Omega_{\mathbf{b}}(\bm{\phi})
measures the excess of
\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})

above the tangent hyperplane at
\mathbf{b}
. Thus
\Omega_{\mathbf{b}}(\bm{\phi})=0
means that
\bm{\phi}
lies on the tangent hyperplane defined at
\mathbf{b}
.

Definition 2.1

(Coexistence points)
.

We call an
M
-tuple of vectors
\{\mathbf{b}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{b}^{(M)}\}
coexistence points of
M
phases if they share a common tangent hyperplane which is supporting on
\mathcal{G}
. That is, the tangent hyperplanes

T_{\mathbf{b}^{(q)}}
,
q=1,\ldots,M
, coincide on the saturation hyperplane, and the common tangent hyperplane satisfies

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})\geq T_{\mathbf{b}^{(q)}}(\bm{\phi})\qquad\text{for all }\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{G},\text{ and }q=1,\ldots,M,

(2.10)

with equality if and only if

\bm{\phi}\in\{\mathbf{b}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{b}^{(M)}\}
.

Geometrically, this hyperplane lies below the free energy landscape and touches the free energy landscape only at the coexistence points.

Lemma 2.2

(Characterization of coexistence points)
.

The
M
-tuple of vectors
\{\mathbf{b}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{b}^{(M)}\}
are coexistence points of
M
phases if and only if there exist scalars

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M}
and a vector
\bm{\mu}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that they have equal chemical potentials:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{0,\lambda_{q}}(\mathbf{b}^{(q)})=\bm{\mu}_{0,\lambda_{r}}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})\qquad q,r=1,\ldots,M,

(2.11)

and satisfy the common-tangent condition:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(q)}}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})=0,\qquad q,r=1,\ldots,M.

(2.12)

Proof.

If the points share a common tangent hyperplane, then the tangent hyperplanes at the points
\mathbf{b}^{(q)},q=1,...,M
have the same slope on the saturation hyperplane. This gives the equal-chemical-potential condition (
2.11
). Moreover, each
point
\mathbf{b}^{(r)}
lies on the tangent hyperplane constructed at

\mathbf{b}^{(q)}
, which is precisely the common-tangent construction (
2.12
).

Conversely, the equal-chemical-potential
condition (
2.11
) gives a common slope, say
\bm{\mu}^{\star}
. Hence each tangent hyperplane can be written as

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{b}^{(r)}}(\bm{\phi})=\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})+\bm{\mu}^{\star}\cdot(\bm{\phi}-\mathbf{b}^{(r)}).

(2.13)

The common-tangent condition with indices
q
and
r
gives

\displaystyle 0=\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(q)}}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})=

\displaystyle~\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})-T_{\mathbf{b}^{(q)}}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\left(\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})-\bm{\mu}^{\star}\cdot\mathbf{b}^{(r)}\right)-\left(\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(q)})-\bm{\mu}^{\star}\cdot\mathbf{b}^{(q)}\right).

(2.14)

All tangent hyperplanes thus have both the same slope and the same offset, and hence they coincide.
∎

In general, coexistence points are characterized by the common-tangent
construction rather than by the values of
\Psi_{0}
alone. In certain situations, however, this condition simplifies. If several constrained minima of
\Psi_{0}
have the same bulk free-energy value, then their tangent hyperplanes coincide and the minima are coexistence points. This gives the following sufficient condition.

Lemma 2.3

(Equal-depth constrained minima)
.

Let
\mathbf{b}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{b}^{(M)}\in\mathcal{G}
be distinct minima of

\Psi_{0}
subject to the saturation constraint. If these constrained minima have
equal depth,

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})=c,\qquad r=1,\ldots,M,

(2.15)

then
\mathbf{b}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{b}^{(M)}
are coexistence points.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{b}^{(r)}
is a constrained minimum of
\Psi_{0}
on the saturation
hyperplane, the constrained first-order optimality condition implies that there
exists a scalar
\lambda_{r}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{0,\lambda_{r}}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})=\frac{\partial\Psi_{0}}{\partial\bm{\phi}}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})+\lambda_{r}\mathbf{1}=\bm{0},\qquad r=1,\ldots,M.

(2.16)

Thus the equal-chemical-potential condition in

Definition

2.1
holds with common value

\bm{\mu}^{\star}=\bm{0}
. Moreover, all tangent hyperplanes at
\mathbf{b}^{(r)}
coincide and are equal to the constant
hyperplane
c
:

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{b}^{(r)}}(\bm{\phi})

\displaystyle=\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})+\bm{\mu}_{0,\lambda_{r}}(\mathbf{b}^{(r)})\cdot\left(\bm{\phi}-\mathbf{b}^{(r)}\right)=c.

(2.17)

Consequently, for all
r,s=1,\ldots,M
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(r)}}(\mathbf{b}^{(s)})=\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(s)})-T_{\mathbf{b}^{(r)}}(\mathbf{b}^{(s)})=c-c=0.

(2.18)

Hence the common-tangent condition in
Definition

2.1
is also
satisfied. Therefore

\mathbf{b}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{b}^{(M)}
are coexistence points.
∎

In accordance with the Gibbs phase rule we find that
F=N-M
, where
F
is the number of thermodynamic degrees of freedom,
N
the total number of phases and

This means that if we consider coexistence of

Hence, for the maximal case

For

M(N-1)
degrees of freedom, due to the simplex constraint.
Equal chemical potentials impose
(N-1)(M-1)
constraints, and equal grand potential impose
M-1
constraints.
Subtraction of constraints from degrees of freedom yields

\displaystyle M(N-1)-N(M-1)=N-M=F.

2.3.  Free-energy density

In the following we consider a free energy density of the following form

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})=

\displaystyle~\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}\kappa_{\alpha\beta}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}.

(2.20a)

Here
\Psi_{0}
denotes the bulk free-energy density, whereas the gradient term represents an excess free energy in the interfacial region. Here
\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}=\kappa_{{\beta}\alpha}
defines the symmetric (volume-based) capillary matrix which is positive semi-definite on the tangent plane and includes both self- and cross-interaction terms among phases. The
matrix
\bm{\kappa}=(\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}})_{\alpha,{\beta}=1}^{N}
is assumed to be
symmetric,
\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}=\kappa_{{\beta}\alpha}
, and contains both self- and
cross-gradient contributions. This form is compatible with the free-energy
closure used in the mixture-aware formulation of
33
and
includes capillary contributions commonly used in multiphase phase-field models, see for example
17
;
5
.

The bulk part of the mixture-aware closure is given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})=\sum_{\alpha}W_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\log(\phi_{\alpha})-\sum_{\alpha{\beta}}\chi_{\alpha{\beta}}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta},

(2.21)

where
W_{\alpha}>0
are the weights of the entropic contributions, and
\chi_{\alpha{\beta}}
are pairwise interaction parameters. For the computations in this paper we use the polynomially regularized version of (
2.21
) proposed in
33
. The free energy (
2.21
) may be written in the Flory–Huggins form:

\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\phi_{\alpha}}{N_{\alpha}}\log(\phi_{\alpha})-\sum_{\alpha{\beta}}\chi_{\alpha{\beta}}\phi_{\alpha}\phi_{\beta},

(2.22)

for constants polymerization degrees
N_{\alpha}
,
\alpha=1,...,N
. Another quite well-known example is the three-phase polynomial free energy of
4
. This model may be interpreted as a
particular multiphase analogue of the binary Ginzburg–Landau potential:

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}

\displaystyle(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3})=\frac{6}{\varepsilon}\left(\Sigma_{1}\phi_{1}^{2}(1-\phi_{1})^{2}+\Sigma_{2}\phi_{2}^{2}(1-\phi_{2})^{2}+\Sigma_{3}\phi_{3}^{2}(1-\phi_{3})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle~\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{12}{\varepsilon}\Lambda\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{2}^{2}\phi_{3}^{2},

(2.23a)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{12}+\gamma_{13}-\gamma_{23},\qquad\Sigma_{2}=\gamma_{12}+\gamma_{23}-\gamma_{13},\qquad\Sigma_{3}=\gamma_{13}+\gamma_{23}-\gamma_{12}.

(2.23b)

Here
\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}
denote prescribed pairwise parameters and
\Lambda\geq 0
penalizes fully ternary states. For this free energy, the relation between the parameters
\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}
and the actual interfacial energies depends on the associated capillary contribution. We discuss this in
Theorem

3.8
.

In the incompressible literature it is common to work with capillary matrices that exclude self-interactions, see e.g.
4
. Using the definition of the Gibbs simplex (
2.2
) and its tangent space (
2.3
) we reformulate the gradient contribution.

Proposition 2.4

(Gradient contribution of the free energy)
.

The gradient contribution of the free energy (
2.20
) may be written as:

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}=

\displaystyle~-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,{\beta}\\
\alpha<{\beta}\end{subarray}}\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta},

(2.24a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}=

\displaystyle~\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}+\kappa_{{\beta}{\beta}}-2\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}\geq 0.

(2.24b)

We emphasize that in general neither
\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}
nor
\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}
represents a surface tension quantity.
The (symmetric) capillary matrix
\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}=\sigma_{{\beta}\alpha}
captures only the interfacial contributions between different phases, excluding self-interactions:
\sigma_{\alpha\alpha}=0
. In addition,
Proposition

2.4
reveals that the choice
\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}+\kappa_{{\beta}{\beta}})
for all
\alpha\neq{\beta}
causes the gradient contribution in (
2.20
) to vanish on the tangent plane.

Proof.

This follows from the sequence of identities:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,{\beta}\\
\alpha<{\beta}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,{\beta}\\
{\beta}<\alpha\end{subarray}}\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}+\sum_{\alpha}\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,{\beta}\\
\alpha<{\beta}\end{subarray}}(\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}+\kappa_{{\beta}\alpha})\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}-\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\beta}\\
{\beta}\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,{\beta}\\
\alpha<{\beta}\end{subarray}}(\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}+\kappa_{{\beta}\alpha})\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,{\beta}\\
\alpha<{\beta}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}

\displaystyle~-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,{\beta}\\
{\beta}<\alpha\end{subarray}}\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,{\beta}\\
\alpha<{\beta}\end{subarray}}(\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}+\kappa_{{\beta}\alpha}-\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}-\kappa_{{\beta}{\beta}})\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\phi_{\beta},

(2.25)

where the first follows from splitting the double sum, the second from the tangent space (
2.3
) and rearranging the summation, the third from again rearranging the terms, and the final identity from combining the terms.
∎

3.1.  Surface tension

Let
\alpha,{\beta}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
with
\alpha\neq{\beta}
, and let
\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)},\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}\in\mathcal{G}
denote the corresponding coexistence points. We consider a planar interface with unit normal
\bm{\nu}
and introduce the normal coordinate
z=\mathbf{x}\cdot\bm{\nu}
. To describe the transition from
\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}
to
\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}
, let
s:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a strictly monotone parameter depending on the normal coordinate
z
, with

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to-\infty}s(z)=0,\qquad\lim_{z\to+\infty}s(z)=1.

(3.1)

A planar one-dimensional interfacial profile is then a curve
\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}=\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s)
connecting two coexistence points:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}:[0,1]\to\mathcal{G},\qquad\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(0)=\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)},\quad\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(1)=\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}.

(3.2)

The corresponding physical profile, written as a function of the normal coordinate, is denoted by

\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{G}
and defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(z):=\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s(z)).

(3.3)

Hence, the phase-field profile depends only on the normal coordinate through

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s)=\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(z).

(3.4)

With this notation, derivatives with respect to the normal coordinate and the path parameter are related by

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}(z)=\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s(z))\frac{{\rm d}s}{{\rm d}z},\qquad\nabla\bm{\phi}=\frac{{\rm d}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}\otimes\bm{\nu}=\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)\frac{{\rm d}s}{{\rm d}z}\otimes\bm{\nu}.

(3.5)

The free energy per unit interfacial area associated with the physical profile

\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}
is

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}[\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(z))+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}(z)\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}(z)\right){\rm d}z.

(3.6)

The surface tension between phases
\alpha
and
{\beta}
is the minimal free energy per unit interfacial area among all profiles connecting the two coexistence points:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}=\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}[\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}].

(3.7)

The same interfacial energy can be written in terms of the path parameter
s
as:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}[\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})},z]=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s))\frac{{\rm d}z}{{\rm d}s}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}p_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)p_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)\frac{{\rm d}s}{{\rm d}z}\right){\rm d}s.

(3.8)

For a fixed path
\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}
, minimization with respect to the parameterization
z=z(s)
gives

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}z}{{\rm d}s}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}p_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)p_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)}{2\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s))}}.

(3.9)

Substitution into (
3.8
) yields the parameterization-invariant path functional

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}=\inf_{\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s))\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}p_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)p_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)}{\rm d}s.

(3.10)

Optimality conditions yield the well-known Euler-Lagrange equations associated with
(
3.6
):

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}}{\partial\hat{p}_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(z))-\sum_{\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\hat{p}_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z^{2}}(z)=0,\qquad\gamma=1,\ldots,N.

(3.11)

Taking the inner product of (
3.11
) with

{\rm d}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}/{\rm d}z
and integrating with respect to
z
gives

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(z))-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}(z)\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}(z)=C.

(3.12)

Using the far-field conditions at
z=\pm\infty
, together with

\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})=0
and

{\rm d}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}/{\rm d}z\to\mathbf{0}
as
z\to\pm\infty
, gives
C=0
.
Hence, the equilibrium profile satisfies the equipartition identity

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(z))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}(z)\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}(z).

(3.13)

Substituting (
3.13
) into the interfacial energy gives the equivalent
physical-coordinate forms

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}=\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}

\displaystyle~2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(z)){\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\inf_{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}

\displaystyle~\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}(z)\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}(z){\rm d}z.

(3.14)

Equivalently, using (
3.9
), the surface tension can be written as the parameterization-invariant path functional

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}=\inf_{\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s))\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}p_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)p_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)}{\rm d}s.

(3.15)

3.2.  Effects of mass constraints

In many applications the stationary solutions correspond to minimization of the free energy (
2.20
), but under additional constraints, most commonly a mass conservation constraint. This leads to

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\phi}}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})-\sum_{\alpha}\hat{c}_{\alpha}(\phi_{\alpha}-m_{\alpha})+\lambda(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\langle\bm{\phi},\mathbf{e}_{\alpha}\rangle=m_{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi})+\lambda(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}-1)

(3.16)

However, it is not at all clear whether the equilibrium profile related to the surface tension is also a minimizer of the constrained energy, i.e. we have to clarify the effects of
\hat{c}_{\alpha}
and
\lambda
on equilibrium solutions.

Theorem 3.1

(Equipartition of the energy)
.

Let
\bm{\phi}
be a one-dimensional equilibrium profile connecting

\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}
and
\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}
, with

\partial_{z}\bm{\phi}\to 0
at both endpoints. Then we have the equipartition:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\frac{\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}}{2}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}\partial_{z}\phi_{\delta},

(3.17)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\bm{\phi})=\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\sum_{\gamma}\hat{c}_{\gamma}\phi_{\gamma},

(3.18)

and whose endpoint values satisfy

\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})
.

The result requires that the tilted potential
\tilde{\Psi}_{0}
is the same for
\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}
and
\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}
. This enforces algebraic conditions on
\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)},\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}
, which can directly be connected to the coexistence points of
\tilde{\Psi}_{0}
and the grand potential.

Corollary 3.2

(Equal-depth wells)
.

Assume the setting of
Theorem

3.1
. If the two endpoint
values of the bulk potential are equal, i.e.
\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})
, then the mass multiplier satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{c}}\cdot(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=0.

(3.19)

Moreover, if

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{c}}\cdot(\bm{\phi}(z)-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=0\qquad\text{for all }z,

(3.20)

then

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\frac{\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}}{2}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}\,\partial_{z}\phi_{\delta}.

(3.21)

If, in addition, the common endpoint value is normalized to zero, then

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})=\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\frac{\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}}{2}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}\,\partial_{z}\phi_{\delta}.

(3.22)

Corollary 3.3

(Equipartition for unequal well depths)
.

Assume the setting of
Theorem

3.1
. If the two endpoint
values of the bulk potential are unequal, i.e.

\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})\neq\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})
then

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})-\hat{\mathbf{c}}\cdot\left(\bm{\phi}-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}\right)=\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\frac{\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}}{2}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}\,\partial_{z}\phi_{\delta},

(3.23)

where one compatible choice of
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{c}}=\frac{\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})-\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})}{\|\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}\|^{2}}(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}).

(3.24)

Hence, we observe that, in general, the mass constraint leads to different equilibrium profiles. Note that surface tension does not depend on such constraints; therefore, it may happen that, for a given mass constraint, the equilibrium profile at which the surface tension value is attained is not realizable. The proofs of the above theorem and corollaries are given in Appendix
E
.

3.3.  Straight-line approximation

The exact surface tension (
3.15
) is obtained by minimizing over all admissible paths in the Gibbs simplex. In the calibration problem, however, we also need an explicit initial relation between the target surface tensions and the entries of
\bm{\kappa}
. For this purpose we first approximate the minimizing path by the straight segment connecting the coexistence points. The resulting expression is generally not exact, since the straight segment need not be an equilibrium path, but it provides a useful initialization for the reconstruction procedure below.

For each pair
\alpha\neq{\beta}
, we define the straight segment connecting the coexistence points. Thus, for each pair
\alpha\neq{\beta}
, we set

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}=\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}+s(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}),\qquad s\in[0,1].

(3.25)

The corresponding approximate surface tension is

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{\alpha{\beta}}:=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s))\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}\tilde{p}_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)\tilde{p}_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)}{\rm d}s

(3.26)

Since
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)=\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}
, this reduces to

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{\alpha{\beta}}=

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s))\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}-\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})_{\gamma}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}-\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})_{\delta}}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~F_{\alpha{\beta}}\sqrt{\tilde{\sigma}_{\alpha{\beta}}},

(3.27)

where we defined

\displaystyle F_{\alpha{\beta}}=

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s))}{\rm d}s,

(3.28a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{\alpha{\beta}}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}-\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})_{\gamma}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}-\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})_{\delta}.

(3.28b)

Remark 3.4

(Alternative forms of the approximate surface tension)
.

The straight-line approximation (
3.3
) separates the contribution of the bulk potential, through
F_{\alpha{\beta}}
, from the contribution of the capillary metric, through
\tilde{\sigma}_{\alpha{\beta}}
. If, in addition, one were to impose the equipartition relation within this restricted straight-line ansatz, then one would obtain the formal condition

\displaystyle F_{\alpha{\beta}}=\sqrt{\tilde{\sigma}_{\alpha{\beta}}},

(3.29)

and hence the equivalent identities

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{\alpha{\beta}}=~F_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2},\qquad\tilde{\gamma}_{\alpha{\beta}}=~\tilde{\sigma}_{\alpha{\beta}}.

(3.30)

However, this equipartition condition is not generally satisfied by the prescribed straight-line path, since such a path is rarely the true minimizing interfacial profile. Consequently, the three quantities
F_{\alpha{\beta}}\sqrt{\tilde{\sigma}_{\alpha{\beta}}},F_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2},\tilde{\sigma}_{\alpha{\beta}}
should be regarded as distinct in general; they coincide only when the straight-line ansatz is compatible with the equilibrium equipartition structure.

If the coexistence points are the unit vectors, i.e.

\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}=\mathbf{e}_{\alpha}
and

\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}=\mathbf{e}_{\beta}
, we recover the simple identity:

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{e}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{e}_{\beta})_{\gamma}(\mathbf{e}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{e}_{\beta})_{\delta}=\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}-2\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}+\kappa_{{\beta}{\beta}}=\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}.

(3.31)

Thus, for a given bulk potential and capillary matrix
\bm{\kappa}
, the straight-line approximation provides explicit estimates of the pairwise surface tensions. In the calibration problem we require the inverse direction: given a bulk potential and prescribed target values
\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}
, determine a capillary matrix
\bm{\kappa}
whose equilibrium interfacial profiles reproduce these surface tensions.

3.4.  Reconstruction of the capillary matrix

We now formulate the reconstruction problem for
\bm{\kappa}
. The coexistence points
\{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}\}
are determined by the bulk potential
\Psi_{0}
and are assumed to be available in what follows. In special cases, such as when the pure phases correspond to the unit vectors, these points are known explicitly. In general, however, their computation is a separate nonlinear problem and may be nontrivial.

A full symmetric
N\times N
capillary matrix contains
N(N+1)/2
entries, whereas the prescribed data consist of
N(N-1)/2
pairwise surface tensions. However, due to the saturation constraint, only the restriction of
\bm{\kappa}
to the tangent space (
2.3
) contributes to the gradient energy. Equivalently, the phases of
\bm{\kappa}
in the direction of
\bm{1}
are gauge degrees of freedom and do not affect the surface tensions. The effective number of capillary parameters is therefore
N(N-1)/2
, matching the number of pairwise target surface tensions.

If an equilibrium profile is fixed, then the equipartition form of the interfacial energy implies
a linear dependence on the capillary matrix. Indeed, for an exact equilibrium
profile one has

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}{\rm d}z.

(3.32)

Thus, if the profile is held fixed, the right-hand side is linear in the entries
of
\bm{\kappa}
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha{\beta}}\approx

\displaystyle~\sum_{\gamma,\delta}A_{\alpha{\beta},\gamma\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta},\qquad\alpha<{\beta},

(3.33a)

\displaystyle A_{\alpha{\beta},\gamma\delta}:=

\displaystyle~\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}\frac{{\rm d}\hat{p}_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})}}{{\rm d}z}{\rm d}z.

(3.33b)

In the algorithm we use this idea in the path-parametrized form. For the current
collection of paths
\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}
, we define the path-metric coefficients

\displaystyle g^{\alpha{\beta}}_{\gamma,\delta}:=\int_{0}^{1}p_{\gamma}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)p_{\delta}^{(\alpha{\beta})\prime}(s)\,{\rm d}s.

(3.34)

For these fixed paths, the effective pairwise capillary quantities satisfy the
linear relation

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}\approx\sum_{\gamma,\delta}g^{\alpha{\beta}}_{\gamma,\delta}\kappa_{\gamma\delta}.

(3.35)

Collecting these equations for all pairs
\alpha<{\beta}
gives a linear system for the
entries of
\bm{\kappa}
. To assemble this system, we enumerate both the pairwise surface-tension data and the entries of the capillary matrix. The row index
\mu
labels the unordered phase pair
(\alpha,{\beta})
with
\alpha<{\beta}
. We use the lexicographic ordering

\displaystyle(1,2),(1,3),\ldots,(1,N),(2,3),\ldots,(2,N),\ldots,(N-1,N).

(3.36)

Thus the pair
(\alpha,{\beta})
is assigned the index

\displaystyle\eta(\alpha,{\beta})=\sum_{r=1}^{\alpha-1}(N-r)+({\beta}-\alpha),\qquad\alpha<{\beta}.

(3.37)

The first term counts the number of pairs whose first index is smaller than
\alpha
, while the second term gives the position of
{\beta}
among the pairs starting with
\alpha
. Hence
\mu
ranges from
1
to
N(N-1)/2
.
Similarly, the column index
\nu
labels the entries of
\bm{\kappa}
. In the algorithm we vectorize the full matrix column by column, namely

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{\nu}=\kappa_{\gamma\delta},\qquad\nu(\gamma,\delta)=\gamma+(\delta-1)N,\qquad\gamma,\delta=1,\ldots,N.

(3.38)

With this convention, the coefficient multiplying
\kappa_{\gamma\delta}
in the equation for the pair
(\alpha,{\beta})
is placed in the matrix entry
G_{\eta(\alpha,{\beta}),\nu(\gamma,\delta)}=g^{\alpha{\beta}}_{\gamma,\delta}
. Collecting all pairwise equations gives the matrix system

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}\approx\bm{G}\bm{M},\qquad\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{N(N-1)/2\times N^{2}},

(3.39)

where
\bm{\sigma}_{\eta(\alpha,{\beta})}=\sigma_{\alpha{\beta}}
. The least-squares problem

\displaystyle\bm{M}\in\arg\min_{\widehat{\bm{M}}}\|\bm{G}\widehat{\bm{M}}-\bm{\sigma}\|_{2}

(3.40)

therefore determines a vectorized capillary matrix that best matches the
prescribed effective pairwise coefficients for the current paths. The updated capillary matrix is then set to

\displaystyle\bm{\kappa}=\operatorname{mat}(\bm{M}),

(3.41)

where the inverse vectorization map
\operatorname{mat}
is defined by

\displaystyle\operatorname{mat}(\bm{M})_{\gamma\delta}=M_{\nu(\gamma,\delta)}.

(3.42)

Because only the restriction of the quadratic form associated with

\bm{\kappa}
to the tangent space

\displaystyle\bm{1}^{\perp}:=\left\{\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\mathbf{q}\cdot\bm{1}=0\right\}

(3.43)

affects the gradient energy, the least-squares solution is not unique when the
full matrix
\bm{\kappa}
is represented. phases in the direction of

\bm{1}
are gauge degrees of freedom. We select the minimum-norm least-squares
representative, which fixes this gauge.

The updated capillary matrix is then used to recompute the one-dimensional
equilibrium profiles and the corresponding surface tensions. If the computed
surface tensions do not match the prescribed targets, the process is repeated.

3.5.  Face confinement of minimizing profiles

The preceding surface tension formula involves a minimization over admissible paths in the Gibbs simplex. Whether the minimizing path between two coexistence points is confined to the corresponding simplex face depends on the choice of free energy. We make this distinction for the Flory–Huggins free energy and the Boyer free energy.

We show that for the mixture-aware free energy, an energy-minimizing interfacial profile cannot lie exactly on a Gibbs-simplex face.

Theorem 3.7

(No face confinement for the mixture-aware free energy)
.

Let
N\geq 3
. A profile that is confined to a Gibbs-simplex face on a
nontrivial interfacial region cannot be a local minimizer of the total
mixture-aware energy with
W_{\alpha}=W>0
and finite coefficients

\chi_{\alpha\beta}
and
\kappa_{\alpha\beta}
.

For the free energy proposed by Boyer (
2.23
) we show that the minimizing profiles lie on the faces of the Gibbs simplex.

Theorem 3.8

(Exact face confinement for the Boyer free energy)
.

Let
N=3
. For the Boyer free energy defined in
4
, with
positive pair coefficients and nonnegative ternary penalty, every energy-minimizing one-dimensional profile connecting two pure states is confined
to the corresponding Gibbs-simplex face. Moreover, the minimum energy equals the prescribed surface tension of that pair.

The proofs of Theorems
3.7
and
3.8
are provided in the Appendix
E
.

4.1.  Interface width

We define an operational interface width for the equilibrium profile
\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(z)
introduced above. The interface width between
\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}
and
\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}
is determined by fixing constants
a
and
b
with
0<a<b<1
, and measuring
the extent in

(the physical coordinate normal to the interface) over which the profile transitions between
a
and
b
. By construction, the width
\varepsilon_{\alpha{\beta}}(a,b)
depends on
(a,b)
: moving
a
and
b
closer to the pure phases increases the portion of the diffuse tails included in the measurement. For typical diffuse–interface models the equilibrium profile approaches the pure phases only
only asymptotically as
|z|\to\infty

|z|\to\infty
and
\varepsilon_{\alpha{\beta}}(0,1)
would diverge. We
therefore restrict to
0<a<b<1

\varepsilon_{\alpha{\beta}}(a,b)

To avoid a definition that depends on a distinguished phase label, we use the antisymmetric difference

\displaystyle d_{\alpha{\beta}}(z):=\hat{p}_{\alpha}(z)-\hat{p}_{\beta}(z),

\displaystyle s(z):=\frac{1+\hat{p}_{\beta}(z)-\hat{p}_{\alpha}(z)}{2}=\frac{1-d_{\alpha{\beta}}(z)}{2}.

(4.2)

0<a<b<1

and

\displaystyle d_{\alpha{\beta}}(z_{a})=\ell_{a},\qquad d_{\alpha{\beta}}(z_{b})=\ell_{b},

(4.3)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\alpha{\beta}}(a,b):=\bigl|z_{b}-z_{a}\bigr|.

The same width can be evaluated from the free-energy representation of the equilibrium path. First we note the identity

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\alpha{\beta}}(a,b)=\bigl|z_{s=b}-z_{s=a}\bigr|=\left|\int_{a}^{b}\frac{dz}{ds}\,ds\right|,

which holds for the equilibrium path parametrized by a strictly monotone scalar parameter

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\alpha{\beta}}(a,b)=\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}(s)^{\!\top}\bm{\kappa}\,\mathbf{p}^{\prime}(s)}{2\,\Omega_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s))}}\,ds.

(4.6)

4.2.  Specification of the interface width parameters

We specify
a
and
b
so that the standard interface width associated with the standard double well potential for 2 phases is returned. As such, we consider a two–fluid system for which we introduce a single order parameter

\phi:=\phi_{1}
so that
\phi_{2}=1-\phi_{1}
. The free–energy density

\displaystyle\Psi=\breve{\Psi}_{0}(\phi)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{12}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\phi,

(4.7)

where
\breve{\Psi}_{0}(\phi):=\Psi_{0}(\phi,1-\phi)

\sigma_{12}=\kappa_{11}-2\kappa_{12}+\kappa_{22}

\displaystyle\gamma_{12}=

\displaystyle~\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\sqrt{W\sigma_{12}},\qquad\varepsilon_{12}(a,b)=~\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{12}}{4W}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\log\frac{b}{1-b}-\log\frac{a}{1-a}\right).

2.

4.3.  Mesh-resolvable interface width

To expose explicitly the diffuse-interface thickness scale, we introduce a
parameter
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and choose

\displaystyle W_{\alpha}=\frac{\bar{W}}{\varepsilon_{0}},\qquad\kappa_{\alpha{\beta}}=\varepsilon_{0}\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha{\beta}},

(4.15)

where
\bar{W}>0
and
\bar{\kappa}_{\alpha{\beta}}
are independent of

\varepsilon_{0}
. Substitution into the interface width formula (
4.6
) gives

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\alpha{\beta}}=

\displaystyle~\varepsilon_{0}\bar{\varepsilon}_{\alpha{\beta}}

(4.16)

where the reference pairwise interface width is

\displaystyle\bar{\varepsilon}_{\alpha{\beta}}:=\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}(s)^{\!\top}\bar{\bm{\kappa}}\mathbf{p}^{\prime}(s)}{2\,\bar{\Omega}_{\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}}(\mathbf{p}^{(\alpha{\beta})}(s))}}\,ds,

(4.17)

where

denotes the corresponding excess energy contribution obtained with
\bar{W}

Since an

are in general not identical. We therefore define the

\displaystyle\varepsilon:=\min_{\alpha<{\beta}}\varepsilon_{\alpha{\beta}}=\varepsilon_{0}\min_{\alpha<{\beta}}\bar{\varepsilon}_{\alpha{\beta}}.

(4.18)

Consequently, prescribing the representative width
\varepsilon
determines
\varepsilon_{0}
. With this choice, the thinnest pairwise diffuse interface has width

\varepsilon
, while all other pairwise interfaces are at least as wide. In the
numerical simulations we choose
\varepsilon
in relation to the mesh size. The surface tensions are unaffected by this choice of
\varepsilon_{0}

7.  Conclusions

We have developed a surface-tension calibration procedure for
N
-phase diffuse-interface free energies. The method determines a symmetric capillary matrix from prescribed pairwise surface tensions by using one-dimensional
equilibrium profiles in the Gibbs simplex. A straight-line approximation provides an initial estimate, after which the capillary matrix is iteratively updated using equilibrium profiles computed from the Euler–Lagrange equations.
We also established precise face-confinement properties for the two bulk free energies considered in this work. For the regularized mixture-aware free energy used here, which was proposed in
33
, minimizing profiles are
not exactly confined to Gibbs-simplex faces. In contrast, for the Boyer free energy we proved exact face confinement of the minimizing profiles. These results put these two free-energy choices on different geometric footings and show that calibration for general multiphase free energies cannot rely only on face-restricted binary profiles. The numerical calibration tests for two-, three-, and four-phase systems show that this reconstruction accurately recovers the prescribed surface tensions, whereas the straight-line approximation alone is generally insufficient.

After calibrating the surface tensions, we introduced a definition of pairwise interface width based on the normal-coordinate equilibrium profiles. This makes
it possible to compare the widths of different pairwise interfaces and to define a representative numerical interface width. By introducing a global thickness
parameter, the diffuse interfaces can be made resolvable on a chosen mesh while leaving the calibrated surface tensions unchanged.

The calibrated free-energy closures were then used in application tests for three-phase Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard models. The lens test demonstrates that the calibrated surface tensions lead to the expected equilibrium interfacial configuration in a multiphase Cahn–Hilliard setting. The rising-bubble simulations show that the same calibrated closure can be used in density-contrast NSCH flows, where capillary effects, hydrodynamics, and multiple interfaces interact.

Overall, the proposed procedure provides a practical route from prescribed pairwise surface tensions and mesh-resolution requirements to a calibrated multiphase free-energy closure suitable for computation. Future work includes the treatment of total spreading regimes, where a prescribed pairwise surface tension may be larger than the sum of two others and a smooth direct minimizing profile may fail to exist. Extending the calibration to such cases would require accounting for composite interfaces and possible wetting layers. A second direction for future research concerns the determination of coexistence points, which seems to be a nontrivial problem in general.

Appendix B
Computation of equilibrium profiles

For each interface
(\alpha,{\beta})
we compute a 1D equilibrium composition profile

\bm{\phi}(z)=(\phi_{1}(z),\dots,\phi_{N}(z))
on
\Omega=(0,L_{x})

by enforcing stationarity of the constrained phase–field energy.
The unknowns are
(\bm{\phi},\lambda)
, where
\lambda(z)
is a Lagrange multiplier
for the simplex constraint
\sum_{{\gamma}=1}^{N}\phi_{\gamma}=1
.

With a symmetric capillary matrix
\bm{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and bulk potential

\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})
, the functional is

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{\phi},\lambda)=\int_{0}^{L_{x}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\kappa_{mn}\,\phi_{m}^{\prime}(z)\,\phi_{n}^{\prime}(z)+\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi}(z))+\lambda(z)\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{k}(z)-1\Big)\Bigg]\,dz.

(B.1)

Stationarity of (
B.1
) yields the coupled system of Euler–Lagrange equations:

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dz}\!\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\kappa_{mn}\,\phi_{n}^{\prime}(z)\right)+\frac{\partial\Psi_{0}}{\partial\phi_{m}}\big(\bm{\phi}(z)\big)+\lambda(z)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad m=1,\dots,N,

(B.2a)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{k}(z)-1

\displaystyle=0.

(B.2b)

In the code, the equilibrium profile is obtained by solving the first-order optimality conditions of the constrained 1D phase-field energy for the unknowns
U=(\bm{\phi}_{h},\lambda_{h})\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{N}\times H^{1}(\Omega)
on
\Omega=(0,L_{x})
, where
\lambda
enforces the simplex constraint
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{k}=1
. With a symmetric capillary matrix
\bm{\kappa}
and bulk potential
\Psi(\bm{\phi})
, the weak residual is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(U;W):=\int_{0}^{L_{x}}\!\Bigg[

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}\kappa_{mn}\,\phi^{\prime}_{n,h}\,v^{\prime}_{m,h}+\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\Psi_{0}}{\partial\phi_{m}}(\bm{\phi}_{h})\,v_{m,h}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{h}\sum_{m=1}^{N}v_{m,h}+\mu_{h}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{k,h}-1\Big)\Bigg]\,dz,

(B.3)

for all test functions
W=(\bm{v}_{h},\mu_{h})
. The equilibrium profile is the solution of
\mathcal{R}(U;W)=0
for all
W
, i.e. the Euler–Lagrange equations
(
B.2
). Since no Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed, the variational formulation implies the natural conditions
\sum_{n}K_{mn}\phi_{n}^{\prime}(0)=\sum_{n}\kappa_{mn}\phi_{n}^{\prime}(L_{x})=0
for all
m
. Newton’s method is applied to this nonlinear system: given
U^{(k)}
, the increment
\delta U^{(k)}
is computed from the linearization
D_{U}\mathcal{R}(U^{(k)};W)[\delta U^{(k)}]=-\mathcal{R}(U^{(k)};W)
for all
W
, and the iterate is updated by
U^{(k+1)}=U^{(k)}+\alpha^{(k)}\delta U^{(k)}
with a line-search factor
\alpha^{(k)}\in(0,1]
until the tolerance is reached.

Appendix C
N-phase Cahn–Hilliard model

We consider the
N
-phase Cahn–Hilliard system in spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(of dimension
d=2,3
), boundary
\partial\Omega
and unit outward normal
\mathbf{n}
. The state variables are the volume fraction variables
\phi_{\alpha}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha=1,\dots,N
, subject to the initial conditions

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},0)=\phi_{\alpha,0}(\mathbf{x}).

(C.1)

The Helmholtz free energy
\Psi
associated with the system belongs to the constitutive class

\displaystyle\Psi=\Psi\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right),

(C.2)

and the associated chemical potentials are

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}=\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}-{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}.

(C.3)

The model consists of
N
phase balance equations of Cahn–Hilliard type:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}\,\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=0,\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,N,

(C.4)

where the diffusive fluxes are taken as

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\mu_{\beta},

(C.5)

with
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=\mathbf{M}_{{\beta}\alpha}
a symmetric degenerate mobility matrix.

Next, the absence of void spaces implies that the volume fractions are subject to the saturation constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}=1.

(C.6)

The saturation constraint is enforced through the mobility structure, namely by requiring that
\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=0
, so that
\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=0
. Incorporating the chemical potential as a separate state variable, we now arrive at a system that contains
2N
equations for
2N
state variables
\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,\dots,N},\left\{\mu_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,\dots,N}\right)
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0,

(C.7a)

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}-\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}+{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=0,

(C.7b)

for
\alpha=1,\dots,N
.

Under appropriate boundary conditions, the system conserves the phase volume fractions, preserves the saturation constraint pointwise, and dissipates the total free energy:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{\alpha}\,{\rm d}\Omega

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,N,

(C.8a)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},0)=1\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\left(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},t)=1,\quad t>0\right),

(C.8b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{D}\left(\left\{\mu_{\alpha}\right\}\right)\leq 0,

(C.8c)

where the energy
\mathcal{E}
and dissipation rate
\mathcal{D}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\Psi\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)\,{\rm d}\Omega,

(C.9a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\left\{\mu_{\alpha}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}(\nabla\mu_{\alpha})^{T}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\mu_{\beta}\,{\rm d}\Omega\geq 0.

(C.9b)

Appendix D
N-phase Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard model

We consider the Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system for
N
phases with non-matching densities introduced in
38
.

D.1.  Governing equations

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d=2,3
, denote the spatial domain. The unknowns are the mass-averaged velocity
\mathbf{v}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the volume fractions
\phi_{\alpha}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
for
\alpha=1,\dots,N
, and the Lagrange multiplier pressure
\lambda:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. The governing equations read

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\sum_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\nabla\mu_{\beta}+\nabla\lambda-{\rm div}\bm{\tau}-\rho\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=0,

(D.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\phi_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\,\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0,

(D.1b)

supplemented with initial data

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{v}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},0)=\phi_{\alpha,0}(\mathbf{x}).

(D.2)

The constant specific densities of the phases are denoted by
\rho_{\alpha}
, and the corresponding partial mass densities are
\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}=\rho_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}
. The total mixture density is then given by
\rho=\sum_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}
. The body force is taken as
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{g}=-g\mathbf{j}
, where
g
is the gravitational constant,
\mathbf{j}=\nabla y
is the unit vector in vertical direction, and
y
denotes the vertical coordinate. The viscous stress tensor is assumed to be

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}=\nu\Bigl(2\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}+\bar{\lambda}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\Bigr),

(D.3)

where
\nu
is the dynamic viscosity,
\bar{\lambda}=-2/d
, and
\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}=(\nabla\mathbf{v}+(\nabla\mathbf{v})^{T})/2

denotes the symmetric part of the velocity gradient.

The Helmholtz free energy density is assumed to belong to the constitutive class

\displaystyle\Psi=\Psi\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right),

(D.4)

and the corresponding chemical potentials are defined by

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}=\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}-{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}.

(D.5)

Accordingly, (
D.1a
) represents the balance of mixture momentum, while (
D.1b
) gives the phase mass balances for
\alpha=1,\dots,N
.

Remark D.1

(Mixture velocity)
.

The system is written in terms of the mass-averaged velocity
\mathbf{v}
. Alternative formulations based on other mixture velocities are possible; for instance, a volume-averaged formulation is discussed in
38
.

Since voids are excluded, the volume fractions satisfy the saturation constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}=1.

(D.6)

Summing the phase balances (
D.1b
) and invoking (
D.6
) yields

\displaystyle{\rm div}\mathbf{v}+\sum_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\,\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=0.

(D.7)

This relation is therefore used as the constraint equation associated with saturation. The diffusive fluxes are modeled as

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla g_{\beta},

(D.8)

where
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}
is the degenerate mobility matrix and
g_{\alpha}=\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}(\mu_{\alpha}+\lambda)
is the effective chemical potential.

Introducing the chemical potentials as additional unknowns, the model can be cast as a system of
2N+2
equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\sum_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\nabla\mu_{\beta}+\nabla\lambda-{\rm div}\bm{\tau}-\rho\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=0,

(D.9a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\phi_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}(\mathbf{J}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=0,

(D.9b)

\displaystyle{\rm div}\mathbf{v}+\sum_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\,\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0,

(D.9c)

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}-\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}+{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=0,

(D.9d)

for
\alpha=1,\dots,N
with
2N+2
independent variables
\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,\dots,N},\lambda,\left\{\mu_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,\dots,N}
. In this formulation,
\lambda
acts as the Lagrange multiplier associated with the constraint (
D.9c
).

D.2.  Conservation and dissipation properties

The system satisfies several fundamental conservation and dissipation properties. Under suitable boundary conditions, it conserves each phase mass, each integrated volume fraction, and the total mass; it also preserves the saturation constraint pointwise and admits a non-increasing total energy:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\,{\rm d}\Omega

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{\alpha}\,{\rm d}\Omega=0,\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,N,

(D.10a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\rho\,{\rm d}\Omega

\displaystyle=0,

(D.10b)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},0)=1\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\left(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},t)=1,\quad t>0\right),

(D.10c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{g_{\alpha}\right\}\right)\leq 0.

(D.10d)

Here the total energy and dissipation are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}K\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)+G\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)+\Psi\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)\,{\rm d}\Omega,

(D.11a)

\displaystyle K\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\rho\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)|\mathbf{v}|^{2},

(D.11b)

\displaystyle G\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)gy,

(D.11c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbf{v},\left\{g_{\alpha}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}2\nu\left(\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right):\left(\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right)+\nu\left(\bar{\lambda}+\frac{2}{d}\right)\left({\rm div}\mathbf{v}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}(\nabla g_{\alpha})^{T}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla g_{\beta}\,{\rm d}\Omega\geq 0.

(D.11d)

The energy density in (
D.11a
) thus consists of the kinetic contribution
K
, the gravitational contribution
G
, and the interfacial free energy density
\Psi
.

Theorem E.1

(Equipartition of the energy)
.

Let
\bm{\phi}
be a one-dimensional equilibrium profile connecting

\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)}
and
\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}
, with

\partial_{z}\bm{\phi}\to 0
at both endpoints. Then we have the equipartition:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\frac{\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}}{2}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}\partial_{z}\phi_{\delta},

(E.1)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\bm{\phi})=\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\sum_{\gamma}\hat{c}_{\gamma}\phi_{\gamma}

(E.2)

whose endpoint values satisfy

\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})
.

Proof.

Since
\bm{\phi}
is a constrained equilibrium profile, it satisfies the
Euler–Lagrange equations with mass multipliers
\hat{c}_{\gamma}
and a saturation
multiplier
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Psi_{0}}{\partial\phi_{\gamma}}(\bm{\phi})-\sum_{\delta}\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}\partial_{z}^{2}\phi_{\delta}-\hat{c}_{\gamma}+\lambda=0,\qquad{\gamma}=1,\ldots,N.

(E.3)

Multiplying the equation for phase
{\gamma}
by
\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}
and summing
over
{\gamma}
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma}\frac{\partial\Psi_{0}}{\partial\phi_{\gamma}}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}-\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}\partial_{z}^{2}\phi_{\delta}\,\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}-\sum_{\gamma}\hat{c}_{\gamma}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}=0,

(E.4)

where the term with
\lambda
drops out due to the saturation constraint. This expression can be written as

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\left[\tilde{\Psi}_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\frac{\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}}{2}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}\,\partial_{z}\phi_{\delta}\right]=0,

(E.5)

where we have used the symmetry
\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}=\kappa_{{\delta}{\gamma}}
. Thus the quantity in brackets is constant:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\frac{\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}}{2}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}\,\partial_{z}\phi_{\delta}=C,

(E.6)

Taking the limits
z\to-\infty
and
z\to+\infty
, and using

\partial_{z}\bm{\phi}\to 0
at both endpoints, gives

\displaystyle C=\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}).

(E.7)

∎

Corollary E.2

(Equal-depth wells)
.

Assume the setting of
Theorem

E.1
. If the two endpoint
values of the bulk potential are equal, i.e.
\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})})

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{c}}\cdot(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=0.

(E.8)

Moreover, if

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{c}}\cdot(\bm{\phi}(z)-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=0\qquad\text{for all }z,

(E.9)

then

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\frac{\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}}{2}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}\,\partial_{z}\phi_{\delta}.

(E.10)

If, in addition, the common endpoint value is normalized to zero, then

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})=\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\frac{\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}}{2}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}\,\partial_{z}\phi_{\delta}.

(E.11)

Proof.

By
Theorem

E.1
,

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})-\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})-\hat{\mathbf{c}}\cdot(\bm{\phi}-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=\sum_{{\gamma},{\delta}}\frac{\kappa_{{\gamma}{\delta}}}{2}\partial_{z}\phi_{\gamma}\,\partial_{z}\phi_{\delta}.

\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\mathbf{b}_{\alpha})=\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}(\mathbf{b}_{\beta})
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{c}}\cdot(\mathbf{b}^{({\beta})}-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=0.

(E.13)

If, in addition,

\hat{\mathbf{c}}\cdot(\bm{\phi}-\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=0
along the profile,
then the tilted term drops out, giving the stated identity. The normalized

\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{b}^{(\alpha)})=0
.

We show that for the mixture-aware free energy, an energy-minimizing interfacial profile cannot lie exactly on a Gibbs-simplex face.

Theorem E.4

(No face confinement for the mixture-aware free energy)
.

Let
N\geq 3
. A profile that is confined to a Gibbs-simplex face on a
nontrivial interfacial region cannot be a local minimizer of the total
mixture-aware energy with
W_{\alpha}=W>0
and finite coefficients

\chi_{\alpha\beta}
and
\kappa_{\alpha\beta}
.

Proof.

Let
N=3
. Assume that
\bm{\phi}
is confined to the
\alpha
–
{\beta}
face on a nontrivial part of the interface. Since the profile is nontrivial, there is an interval on which
\phi_{\gamma}=0,0<\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}<1,\phi_{\alpha}+\phi_{\beta}=1
. Let
\eta
be a smooth nonnegative perturbation, not identically zero, supported
inside this interval. For sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, define

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon\eta)\phi_{\alpha},\quad\phi_{\beta}^{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon\eta)\phi_{\beta},\quad\phi_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\eta,\quad\phi_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}=\phi_{\delta}\quad\text{for }{\delta}\neq\alpha,{\beta},{\gamma}.

(E.16)

This perturbation preserves the simplex constraint, since
\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=1
, and for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
it also preserves nonnegativity. Since
\eta
is
supported inside the interfacial region, the far-field boundary conditions are
unchanged.

We now compare the energies. The entropy contribution involving
phases
\alpha,{\beta}
and
{\gamma}
changes by

\displaystyle W\int\Big[

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\log\phi_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}-\phi_{\alpha}\log\phi_{\alpha}+\phi_{\beta}^{\varepsilon}\log\phi_{\beta}^{\varepsilon}-\phi_{\beta}\log\phi_{\beta}+(\varepsilon\eta)\log(\varepsilon\eta)\Big]{\rm d}z.

(E.17)

Because
\phi_{\alpha}>0
on the support of
\eta
, the first four terms are
O(\varepsilon)
, while

\displaystyle(\varepsilon\eta)\log(\varepsilon\eta)=\varepsilon\eta\log\varepsilon+\varepsilon\eta\log\eta.

(E.18)

Therefore the entropy change is

\displaystyle W\varepsilon\log\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\eta{\rm d}x+O(\varepsilon).

(E.19)

Since
\eta\geq 0
and not identically zero, and
W>0
, we have
\int_{\Omega}\eta{\rm d}x>0
. Hence the leading term is strictly negative. By contrast, the interaction term and the quadratic gradient term vary only by
O(\varepsilon)
, because they are smooth in
\bm{\phi}
and
\nabla\bm{\phi}
and the perturbation is of size
\varepsilon
. Hence the energy difference is

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Psi(\bm{\phi}^{\varepsilon},\nabla\bm{\phi}^{\varepsilon}){\rm d}x-\int_{\Omega}\Psi(\bm{\phi},\nabla\bm{\phi}){\rm d}x=W\varepsilon\log\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\eta{\rm d}x+O(\varepsilon).

(E.20)

For sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, the negative term
\varepsilon\log\varepsilon
dominates the
O(\varepsilon)
remainder, so the perturbed profile has strictly lower energy. Thus
\bm{\phi}
is not a local minimizer.

The same argument extends directly to
N>3
by perturbing from any proper Gibbs-simplex face into one absent phase.
∎

Next, we show that for Boyer’s energy every energy-minimizing one-dimensional equilibrium profile connecting
\mathbf{e}_{1}
and
\mathbf{e}_{2}
lies on the binary face
\{\phi_{3}=0\}
.

Theorem E.5

(Face confinement for the Boyer free energy)
.

Let
N=3
. For the Boyer free energy defined in
4
, with
positive pair coefficients and nonnegative ternary penalty, every energy-minimizing one-dimensional profile connecting two pure states is confined
to the corresponding Gibbs-simplex face. Moreover, the minimum energy equals the prescribed surface tension of that pair.

Proof.

We give the proof for the connection from

\mathbf{e}_{1}=(1,0,0)
to
\mathbf{e}_{2}=(0,1,0)
. Assume
\Sigma_{1}>0,\Sigma_{2}>0,\Sigma_{3}>0,\Lambda\geq 0
. Consider the admissible class

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{12}:=\Bigl\{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}:\bm{\phi}(-\infty)=\mathbf{e}_{1},\ \bm{\phi}(+\infty)=\mathbf{e}_{2}\Bigr\},

(E.21)

and the one-dimensional Boyer energy

\displaystyle\Psi:=

\displaystyle~\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\Psi_{0}(\bm{\phi})+\frac{3\varepsilon}{8}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\Sigma_{\alpha}|\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}|^{2}\right){\rm d}z,

(E.22)

where
\Psi_{0}
is given by (
2.23
).
We also use the relation
\gamma_{12}=(\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2})/2
.

We split the remainder of the proof into three steps.

Step 1: Lower bound for arbitrary ternary connections.
We first decompose the energy into phase-wise contributions. Recalling

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3})=

\displaystyle~\frac{6}{\varepsilon}\left(\Sigma_{1}\phi_{1}^{2}(1-\phi_{1})^{2}+\Sigma_{2}\phi_{2}^{2}(1-\phi_{2})^{2}+\Sigma_{3}\phi_{3}^{2}(1-\phi_{3})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle~+\frac{12}{\varepsilon}\Lambda\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{2}^{2}\phi_{3}^{2},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{12}+\gamma_{13}-\gamma_{23},\qquad\Sigma_{2}=\gamma_{12}+\gamma_{23}-\gamma_{13},\qquad\Sigma_{3}=\gamma_{13}+\gamma_{23}-\gamma_{12},

(E.23)

the one-dimensional energy can be written as

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{\phi})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}J_{\alpha}[\phi_{\alpha}]+\frac{12\Lambda}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{2}^{2}\phi_{3}^{2}{\rm d}z,

(E.24)

where

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}[\phi_{\alpha}]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{6\Sigma_{\alpha}}{\varepsilon}\phi_{\alpha}^{2}(1-\phi_{\alpha})^{2}+\frac{3\varepsilon\Sigma_{\alpha}}{8}|\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}|^{2}\right){\rm d}z.

(E.25)

Since
\Sigma_{\alpha}>0
and
\Lambda\geq 0
, all terms in this decomposition are nonnegative.

By the elementary inequality
a^{2}+b^{2}\geq 2ab
, with

\displaystyle a=\sqrt{\frac{6\Sigma_{\alpha}}{\varepsilon}}\,\phi_{\alpha}(1-\phi_{\alpha}),\qquad b=\sqrt{\frac{3\varepsilon\Sigma_{\alpha}}{8}}\,|\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}|,

(E.26)

we obtain

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}[\phi_{\alpha}]\geq 3\Sigma_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{\alpha}(1-\phi_{\alpha})|\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}|{\rm d}z.

(E.27)

Introducing

\displaystyle\Phi(s):=\int_{0}^{s}t(1-t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}s^{2}-\frac{1}{3}s^{3},

(E.28)

we have
\Phi^{\prime}(s)=s(1-s)
, and hence
\phi_{\alpha}(1-\phi_{\alpha})|\phi_{\alpha}^{\prime}|=\bigl|(\Phi(\phi_{\alpha}))^{\prime}\bigr|
. Substitution into (
E.27
) provides:

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}[\phi_{\alpha}]\geq 3\Sigma_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|(\Phi(\phi_{\alpha}))^{\prime}\bigr|{\rm d}z\geq 3\Sigma_{\alpha}\left|\Phi(\phi_{\alpha}(+\infty))-\Phi(\phi_{\alpha}(-\infty))\right|.

(E.29)

For a connection from
e_{1}
to
e_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(-\infty)=

\displaystyle~1,\quad\phi_{1}(+\infty)=0,

(E.30a)

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(-\infty)=

\displaystyle~0,\quad\phi_{2}(+\infty)=1,

(E.30b)

\displaystyle\phi_{3}(-\infty)=

\displaystyle~0,\quad\phi_{3}(+\infty)=0.

(E.30c)

Since
\Phi(1)-\Phi(0)=\frac{1}{6}
, this yields

\displaystyle J_{1}[\phi_{1}]\geq\frac{\Sigma_{1}}{2},\qquad J_{2}[\phi_{2}]\geq\frac{\Sigma_{2}}{2},\qquad J_{3}[\phi_{3}]\geq 0.

(E.31)

Hence every
\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}_{12}
satisfies
\Psi(\bm{\phi})\geq(\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2})/2=\gamma_{12}
. Therefore we conclude:

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}_{12}}\Psi(\bm{\phi})\geq\gamma_{12}.

(E.32)

Step 2: The binary face profile has energy
\gamma_{12}
.

Restrict now to the face class
\mathcal{G}_{12}^{\mathrm{face}}:=\{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}_{12}:\phi_{3}\equiv 0\}
. Any element of
\mathcal{G}_{12}^{\mathrm{face}}
has the form
\bm{\phi}=(\phi,1-\phi,0)
, with
\phi(-\infty)=1
and
\phi(+\infty)=0
. Since
\phi_{3}\equiv 0
, the
\Lambda
-term vanishes and

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\phi,1-\phi,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\varepsilon}\left(\Sigma_{1}\phi^{2}(1-\phi)^{2}+\Sigma_{2}(1-\phi)^{2}\phi^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{6(\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2})}{\varepsilon}\phi^{2}(1-\phi)^{2}=\frac{12\gamma_{12}}{\varepsilon}\phi^{2}(1-\phi)^{2}.

(E.33)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}+\Sigma_{2}|(1-\phi)^{\prime}|^{2}=(\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2})|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}=2\gamma_{12}|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}.

(E.34)

Therefore the restricted energy is exactly the binary energy

\displaystyle\Psi(\phi,1-\phi,0):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{12\gamma_{12}}{\varepsilon}\phi^{2}(1-\phi)^{2}+\frac{3\varepsilon\gamma_{12}}{4}|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}\right){\rm d}z.

(E.35)

We now show that the minimizing heteroclinic profile in the binary class has energy
\gamma_{12}
.
First, again by
a^{2}+b^{2}\geq 2ab
,

\displaystyle\frac{12\gamma_{12}}{\varepsilon}\phi^{2}(1-\phi)^{2}+\frac{3\varepsilon\gamma_{12}}{4}|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}\geq 6\gamma_{12}\phi(1-\phi)|\phi^{\prime}|,

(E.36)

hence

\displaystyle\Psi(\phi,1-\phi,0)\geq 6\gamma_{12}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(1-\phi)|\phi^{\prime}|{\rm d}z\geq 6\gamma_{12}\left|\int_{0}^{1}s(1-s)\,ds\right|=\gamma_{12}.

(E.37)

Thus

\inf_{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}_{12}^{\mathrm{face}}}\Psi(\bm{\phi})\geq\gamma_{12}.

(E.38)

Now consider the one-dimensional profile

\displaystyle\phi_{\star}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\tanh\left(\frac{2(z-z_{0})}{\varepsilon}\right)\right),

(E.39)

for
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. It satisfies
\phi_{\star}^{\prime}=-(4/\varepsilon)\phi_{\star}(1-\phi_{\star})
,
and hence

\displaystyle\frac{3\varepsilon\gamma_{12}}{4}|\phi_{\star}^{\prime}|^{2}=\frac{12\gamma_{12}}{\varepsilon}\phi_{\star}^{2}(1-\phi_{\star})^{2}.

(E.40)

Substituting and a direct computation provide:

\displaystyle\Psi(\phi_{\star},1-\phi_{\star},0)

\displaystyle=\frac{24\gamma_{12}}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{\star}^{2}(1-\phi_{\star})^{2}{\rm d}z=6\gamma_{12}\int_{0}^{1}s(1-s)\,ds=\gamma_{12}.

Combining this with (
E.38
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}_{12}^{\mathrm{face}}}\Psi(\bm{\phi})=\gamma_{12}.

(E.41)

Hence, together with (
E.32
), this gives

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{G}_{12}}\Psi(\bm{\phi})=\gamma_{12}.

(E.42)

Step 3: Absent phase
3
.

Finally, let
\bm{\phi}^{\star}
be a minimizer. Since

\Psi(\bm{\phi}^{\star})=\gamma_{12}
, while Step 1 gives

\displaystyle J_{1}[\phi_{1}^{\star}]\geq\frac{\Sigma_{1}}{2},\qquad J_{2}[\phi_{2}^{\star}]\geq\frac{\Sigma_{2}}{2},

(E.43)

and all remaining terms are nonnegative, equality can hold only if
J_{3}[\phi_{3}^{\star}]=0
. Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{6\Sigma_{3}}{\varepsilon}(\phi_{3}^{\star})^{2}(1-\phi_{3}^{\star})^{2}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{3\varepsilon\Sigma_{3}}{8}|(\phi_{3}^{\star})^{\prime}|^{2}=0.

(E.44)

Since
\Sigma_{3}>0
, this implies

\displaystyle(\phi_{3}^{\star})^{\prime}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{3}^{\star}(1-\phi_{3}^{\star})=0,

(E.45)

and hence
\phi_{3}^{\star}\in\{0,1\}
. Because
\phi_{3}^{\star}(-\infty)=\phi_{3}^{\star}(+\infty)=0
, we conclude that
\phi_{3}^{\star}\equiv 0
, which proves the claim.
∎
