Neuromyelitis optica presenting with mammillary body and cervicomedullary lesions after severe hyponatremia mimicking osmotic demyelination syndrome
Authors: Madison S Meyer, Keyanna Lewis, Mohamad Ahmed, Shoshanah Lasry, Cameron Peres, Fred Ahmadi, Adam Khanafer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder is an autoimmune demyelinating disease that usually targets the spinal cord and optic nerves, though occasionally other central nervous system structures are involved. There are variants of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder that can mimic metabolic or osmotic demyelinating diseases on imaging. A 54-year-old female with a history of hypertension and Roux-en-Y bypass presented with numbness, weakness, dizziness, and blurred vision and concurrent severe hyponatremia (serum sodium 115 mmol/L). She was admitted and treated for monitored correction of her symptomatic hyponatremia and was discharged after stabilization. Weeks following discharge, she had onset of new neurological symptoms and brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed increased fluid-attenuated inversion recovery signal in the bilateral mammillary bodies and dorsal columns of the upper cervical spinal cord, patchy enhancement at the cervicomedullary junction, and a lesion at C2-C3. Cerebrospinal fluid studies were unremarkable. While osmotic demyelination was suspected initially based on the history of profound hyponatremia and distribution of lesions, further neurologic assessment confirmed neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder. To our knowledge, only 1 other case of mammillary body involvement in AQP4-positive neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder presenting in the setting of severe hyponatremia and with close radiographic resemblance to osmotic demyelination syndrome has been reported. In summary, our case demonstrates radiographic mimicry of osmotic demyelination syndrome by neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder. Consideration of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder should be maintained on the differential diagnosis of patients with mammillary body and cervicomedullary lesions following hyponatremia, especially with regard to the presence of enhancement and progression of lesions.Keywords: Osmotic demyelination syndrome, Hyponatremia, Extrapontine myelinolysis, Inflammatory myelopathy, Cervicomedullary junction, Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder

Introduction

Demyelinating lesions involving the cervicomedullary junction and dorsal columns resulting in demyelination can stem from various causes, including inflammatory and metabolic [1]. Inflammatory demyelinating diseases, such as multiple sclerosis (MS), transverse myelitis, and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), are the most common differentials when magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enhancements are present with lesion evolution [2]. Metabolic myelopathies, such as subacute combined degeneration, may also produce this finding of dorsal column-predominant signal change but without radiographic enhancement [3].

Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) is a noninflammatory demyelinating disorder that is most commonly associated with the rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia [4]. Despite the most common manifestation of ODS being central pontine myelinolysis, extrapontine involvement is also well recognized and may impact structures such as the basal ganglia, thalami, and mammillary bodies [5]. However, spinal cord involvement is rarely reported and remains poorly described in the literature [6].

It is important to differentiate osmotic injury from inflammatory myelopathy because treatment differs and health outcomes can be affected. Significant electrolyte imbalances are more suggestive of ODS. However, enhanced lesions, new lesions on subsequent MRIs, and a worsening of symptoms tend to suggest an autoimmune process.

This case describes a patient who developed bilateral mammillary body and cervicomedullary dorsal column lesions following hospitalization for hyponatremia, leading to significant diagnostic uncertainty between extrapontine ODS and inflammatory myelopathy. This case highlights the potential for osmotic vulnerability to extend beyond classic pontine and deep gray matter structures and to mimic autoimmune disease at the level of the upper spinal cord.

Case report

A 54-year-old woman with hypertension and a remote Roux-en-Y gastric bypass presented with 3 days of progressive diffuse numbness and weakness with interval development of dizziness and blurred vision. These symptoms occurred in the context of several months of poor oral intake associated with persistent dry cough, dry heaving, and substantial unexpected weight loss.

The patient’s initial laboratory values were notable for severe hypotonic, hypoosmolar hyponatremia (serum sodium 115 mmol/L; serum osmolality 234 mOsm/kg) in conjunction with hypokalemia (3.1 mmol/L), hypochloremia (79 mmol/L), and hypomagnesemia (1.3 mEq/L). Urine studies were completed and revealed a urine osmolality of 405 mOsm/kg and a urine sodium of 32 mmol/L. Elevated urine osmolality indicated ongoing antidiuretic hormone secretion. Considering previous thiazide use, low oral intake, and decreased urine osmolality after fluid administration, these factors were likely compounding rather than a pure syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone. The patient received isotonic fluid resuscitation. Hypertonic saline was not administered during the initial hospitalization. Given the multifactorial etiology of the patient’s hyponatremia and her response to isotonic volume repletion, the treating team elected to pursue monitored correction with isotonic fluids and close laboratory surveillance. Following fluids, urine osmolarity decreased to 141 mOsm/kg. Thyroid functional values were found to be normal, and morning cortisol was measured at 18.1 mcg/dL.

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for monitored correction of symptomatic severe hyponatremia. During the first days of hospitalization, serum sodium increased from 115 mmol/L to 126 mmol/L during the first 24 hours (+11 mmol/L), subsequently decreased to 120 mmol/L, and then gradually increased to 133 mmol/L by discharge under close laboratory monitoring (Table 1). The hyponatremia was considered multifactorial with possible contributions from low-solute intake, thiazide exposure, which was stopped prior to admission, and antidiuretic hormone-mediated physiology. Nephrology consultation characterized the process as multifactorial hyponatremia and administered a 1-time dose of tolvaptan with subsequent improvement in sodium and osmolarity. Initial neuroimaging demonstrated no acute intracranial abnormality. The patient was subsequently discharged following clinical stabilization.

Within weeks following discharge, the patient developed new neurologic symptoms that were distinct from those that she presented with during her prior hyponatremic episode. She described progressively worsening facial pain and numbness, characterized as sharp and throbbing and was exacerbated by touch and mastication. In addition, she reported episodic lower-extremity spasms resulting in transient “locking” of her feet. These symptoms prompted an outpatient neurological evaluation later that month. MRI of the cervical spine demonstrated a signal abnormality at the cervicomedullary junction interpreted as compatible with subacute combined degeneration (Fig. 1). Serum vitamin B12 (373 pg/mL) and methylmalonic acid (116 nmol/L) levels were within normal limits, making subacute combined degeneration less likely despite the dorsal column-predominant imaging findings. Given the exam results and the progression of the concerning myelopathy findings on imaging, the patient was admitted for a lumbar puncture and further investigation.

Sagittal T2-weighted MRI of the cervical spine demonstrating dorsal column T2 hyperintensity at the cervicomedullary junction (arrow), initially interpreted as compatible with subacute combined degeneration.

During the subsequent hospitalization, approximately 1 month following her initial intensive care unit admission, imaging with MRI of the brain with and without contrast was conducted and demonstrated increased fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) signal within the bilateral mammillary bodies and bilateral dorsal columns of the upper cervical spinal cord, with subtle heterogeneous enhancement at the cervicomedullary junction (Fig. 2). Cervical and thoracic spinal imaging demonstrated signal abnormality involving the dorsal columns of the upper cervical spinal cord and subtle heterogeneous enhancement at the cervicomedullary junction. Additional spine MRI demonstrated persistent craniocervical junction cord signal abnormality and a new smaller hyperintense lesion at C2-C3 (Fig. 3). These findings were interpreted as nonspecific with differentials including transverse myelitis or other inflammatory demyelinating lesions. A lumbar puncture was performed. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis demonstrated 3 red blood cells/mm and 4 white blood cells/mm (88% lymphocytes and 12% monocytes), protein of 30.1 mg/dL, glucose of 57 mg/dL, an IgG index of 0.7, CSF IgG concentration of 3.1 mg/dL, and CSF albumin of 13 mg/dL. Oligoclonal band testing was not supportive of multiple sclerosis. Cryptococcal antigen and VDRL testing were nonreactive, and cerebrospinal fluid cultures demonstrated no growth.

Sagittal cervical spine MRI demonstrating persistent craniocervical junction cord signal abnormality (upper arrow) with a new smaller C2-C3 hyperintense lesion (lower arrow).

Given the recent severe hyponatremia and the distribution of lesions involving metabolically vulnerable structures, ODS was considered alongside inflammatory and metabolic myelopathies. The patient was therefore treated with high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily) for 5 days with a tapering oral steroid regimen due to the extent of radiographic enhancement and evolution of her lesions within the monitoring interval. She also received intravenous immunoglobulin (0.4 g/kg/d for 4-5 days).

The patient's clinical state improved, as evidenced by a decline in painful dysesthesias and enhanced ambulation. Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation evaluated her and described paraparesis with lower-extremity spasms and documented 4/5 strength bilaterally in the lower extremities with preserved upper-extremity strength. She was ultimately discharged in stable condition with outpatient neurology follow-up arranged.

Outpatient follow-up included a workup for inflammatory demyelinating disease. Serum studies were positive for aquaporin-4 immunoglobulin G (AQP4-IgG) antibodies by cell-based assay. In conjunction with the patient's clinical presentation and characteristic MRI findings, these results fulfilled accepted diagnostic criteria for AQP4-IgG-positive NMOSD. Neurology follow-up was recommended for initiation of disease-modifying therapy and surveillance of disease course.

Discussion

Central and extrapontine myelinolysis is most observed to be the destruction of myelin sheaths and sparing of nerve axons within the central aspect of the basis pontis or extrapontine regions [7]. Structures consistent with central pontine myelonosis, particularly the basis pontis, are most commonly noted in the literature; however, rare manifestations may also occur in extrapontine structures such as the spinal cord [6]. ODS is a very rare manifestation accounting for 0.4%-0.56% of all neurological admissions to tertiary referral hospitals and 0.06% of all medical hospital admissions [5]. Hyponatremia is one of the most common causes of ODS, making up approximately 30%-78% of reported cases [5]. This case presented a rare involvement of the spinal cord and bilateral mammillary bodies following hospitalization for hyponatremia.

The involvement of the dorsal column strongly mimics subacute combined degeneration, transverse myelitis, or MS [8]. Subacute combined degeneration is described as diffuse axonal loss and demyelination that is most severe in the cervical and thoracic spine, commonly from vitamin B12 deficiency [9]. This causes a compromise in nerve transmission where disruption in the myelination process slows down nerve conduction. Clinically, patients present with dysesthesia and gait disturbances with impairment of position and vibration sense, with the lower limbs being more commonly affected compared to the upper limbs. Although the initial MRI appearance raised concern for subacute combined degeneration, normal vitamin B12 and methylmalonic acid levels made this diagnosis unlikely.

Transverse myelitis is used to describe inflammatory disorders that are characterized by acute or subacute motor, sensory, and autonomic spinal cord dysfunction, most often occurring as an autoimmune response following vaccination or infection [10]. Lhermitte’s sign is the presence of paresthesia that radiates down the spine or limbs with neck flexion, which is a clinical manifestation of transverse myelitis caused by demyelination. To distinguish myelopathy from cerebral lesions and peripheral neuropathies, a well-defined truncal sensory level must be identified, with loss or alteration of pain and temperature sensation below that level. When findings of enhancement and interval lesions are evident on imaging, the most common etiology is considered to be an autoimmune process [11]. Enhancements and interval C2-C3 lesions are consistent with active inflammatory demyelination. These lesions are also consistent with other inflammatory demyelinating disorders, such as MS, creating diagnostic uncertainty.

MS attacks the myelinated axons in the central nervous system (CNS), manifesting as episodes of reversible neurological deficits, often followed by progressive neurological deterioration [12]. A diagnosis of MS is based on evidence of at least two different lesions in the white matter of the CNS, at least 2 different episodes in the disease course, and/or chronic inflammation of the CNS and is supported by evidence from ancillary test including MRI of the brain and CSF analysis. CSF analysis demonstrated a normal protein concentration, normal IgG index, minimal pleocytosis, and oligoclonal band testing that was not supportive of multiple sclerosis, making MS less likely.

NMOSD is a central nervous system autoimmune demyelinating condition that affects individuals with antibodies against aquaporin-4 water channels found on astrocytes [13]. NMOSD commonly affects the spinal cord and optic nerves but can cause lesions over many vertebral bodies, leading to longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions. However, NMOSD can cause lesions in the spinal cord that extend into the cervicomedullary junction and neighboring brain tissue, which may mimic metabolic or osmotic demyelinating diseases. In this case, the patient had a recent history of severe hyponatremia, raising concern for ODS but was instead found to have antibodies targeting the aquaporin-4, consistent with NMOSD.

Several factors ultimately pointed toward NMOSD instead of ODS. The progression of lesions over time, evident in serial MRI studies, was also a key factor, with a new C2-C3 lesion noted. Furthermore, the cervicomedullary junction showed patchy enhancement, indicative of active inflammation. Moreover, her neurological condition worsened for several weeks even after her hyponatremia was corrected. Finally, positive NMOSD-associated serology confirmed an autoimmune demyelinating process. These factors, taken together, were less supportive of ODS and favored NMOSD as her diagnosis.

It is understood that overly rapid correction of hyponatremia is one of the most common causes of ODS; however, several studies have identified non-modifiable risk factors may predispose patients to develop ODS including alcoholism, initial blood sodium levels below 120 mmol/L, malnutrition, liver transplantation or cirrhosis, end stage renal disease, diabetes mellitus, concomitant hypokalemia or hypophosphatemia, and the correction of chronic hyponatremia [14]. Serum sodium increased by 11 mmol/L during the first 24 hours of hospitalization, a rate approaching accepted upper correction limits for chronic hyponatremia. Although this degree of correction raised concern for osmotic demyelination syndrome, subsequent lesion progression, enhancement, and AQP4-IgG seropositivity ultimately favored NMOSD [15].

Water flows freely across the blood–brain barrier and cell membranes, where a fall in serum sodium will cause entry of water into the brain cells, leading to swelling [12]. Chronic hyponatremia allows the brain to adapt to the conditions and limits brain swelling by extruding electrolytes and organic osmolytes [16]. Although the etiology is unclear, it is thought that rapid correction of serum sodium concentration disrupts the blood-brain barrier. This occurs due to the adapted lower osmotic set-point for intracellular-to-extracellular water shifts following correction of hyponatremia, causing cellular dehydration and leading to cellular shrinkage and disrupted tight junctions. This allows circulating immune factors to gain access to the CNS, where they are free to mediate or aggravate oligodendroglia injury and demyelination. Highly myelinated white tracts, such as the posterior columns and mamillary bodies, are vulnerable to demyelination following this correction. As stated by the US recommendations, overcorrection occurs when sodium blood levels increase more than 10-12 mmol/L in 24 hours or 18 mmol/L within 48 hours, where the threshold is lowered to 8 mmol/L in 24 hours for higher risk patients [14].

Of the possible areas affected in ODS, approximately 50% of cases manifest as isolated pontine lesions, 30% of cases include pontine and extrapontine lesions, and only 20% are solely extrapontine lesions [17]. Extrapontine lesions can involve sites such as the cerebellum, lateral geniculate body, external capsules, hippocampus, putamen, thalamus, and caudate nucleus. Some unusual sites include the spinal cord, mammillary bodies, amygdala, and subthalamic nucleus [18]. Mammillary bodies are metabolically vulnerable structures and have been reported to be affected in extrapontine manifestations of ODS, as demonstrated in this case.

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is an efficient anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory treatment used for several neurological diseases since it has various effects on the innate and adaptive compartments of the immune system [19]. However, the overall effects differ depending on the intended disease treated, where its effects in ODS are proposed to be the modulation of synthesis and release of cytokines and chemokines, removal or reduction of myelinotoxic substances and remyelination. Steroids may be used independently or combined with IVIG as they reduce the blood–brain barrier permeability and prevent its disruption caused by sodium correction. Although steroids or IVIG may accelerate the recovery in patients with ODS, improvement after their administration does not confirm an inflammatory etiology since partial recovery and myelin repair can be spontaneously noted in these patients.

Conclusion

This case represents an atypical imaging manifestation of NMOSD. Imaging revealed lesions affecting the mammillary bodies and the dorsolateral aspects of the cervical cord and upper medulla. The patient’s unusual lesion pattern, coupled with the severe hyponatremia that occurred before the scans, prompted consideration of ODS, especially given the involvement of vulnerable brain regions. Follow-up imaging demonstrated lesion progression and enhancement, which favored NMOSD, and further workup confirmed the diagnosis. Rarely can autoimmune-mediated demyelination present as or mimic osmotic demyelination on imaging. Considering recent electrolyte imbalances alongside clinical progress and test results can help differentiate these conditions.
