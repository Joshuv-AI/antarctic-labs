Trust Me, I’m a Doctor?
Zach Shahn, Mats Stensrud

Abstract
Clinical trials usually target average treatment effects, but treatment decisions are made for individuals. This tension motivates a common criticism of evidence-based medicine: a treatment that is beneficial on average may be inappropriate for a particular patient, and skilled physicians may outperform rigid adherence to the strategy that performed best in a randomized trial. We consider how randomized and observational data from the same target population can be used to assess that possibility. Specifically, we study settings in which a randomized trial is nested within an observational cohort, so that outcomes are observed under treatment, control, and usual care. We ask what the observed data can reveal about how often physicians outperform the strategy suggested by the trial. We derive sharp bounds on the proportion of physicians whose personal strategies perform better than always choosing the better performing treatment from the trial under the assumption that no physician’s strategy is worse than always choosing the worse performing treatment from the trial. These results shed light on when clinical data support relying on physician discretion over the trial-average recommendation and when stronger justification is required.



1
Introduction

A common
critique of evidence-based medicine is that clinical trials estimate
average
treatment effects across heterogeneous
populations, and that average effects are poor guides to treatment decisions for individual patients.
A treatment that is
beneficial on average may be harmful for a particular subgroup, and vice versa.
Skilled clinicians can in principle leverage knowledge of individual patients to
do better than rigid adherence to trial-derived
recommendations.
1
express this criticism forcefully: “If your physician tells you that she endorses evidence-based medicine, and that the drug will work for you because an RCT has shown that ‘it works’, it is time to find a physician who knows that you and the average are not the same.” However, patients are hard pressed to decide whether to trust a doctor who claims to be able to outperform the ‘naive’ strategy of assigning everyone the treatment that performed best in a trial.

Imagine that a recent RCT has shown that a certain major surgical operation is on average harmful, but your doctor recommends operating nonetheless based on their assessment of your personal characteristics. For example, in randomized trials, transcatheter aortic-valve replacement performed better than surgical valve replacement on important short-term composite outcomes
(
7
)
, but in practice many surgeons and heart teams still prefer surgical replacement for some patients. In this work, we consider how one might bring evidence from trials and observational data to bear on the question of whether to place more trust in the average effect or personalized physician judgment. The same issue is relevant not only for patients deciding whether to trust physicians’ decisions, but also for physicians deciding whether to trust their own judgment.

Suppose that outcomes under usual care, e.g., collected from hospital health records, outperform the outcomes in both arms of a randomized experiment conducted in the same population. A textbook example concerning lung cancer patients comes from
4

10

11

One could make a Deaton and Cartwright style argument that usual care only outperforms the rigid strategies
on average
. Patients might somehow discern that their doctor is truly terrible or malicious and instead go with the higher performing of the treatment or control arms. We do not take this possibility seriously.
In this case, a next step is to find the criteria that physicians are using to make personalized decisions.

Further trials could be performed within strata where there is remaining equipoise. Those trials can also contain usual care arms, and eventually it is probable that a trial would arise in which usual care does not outperform both of the other arms. One possibility is that usual care is outperformed by both treatment and control arms. Under such a scenario, however, patients identified by their doctors as particularly likely to be harmed by treatment would, on average, actually be more likely to benefit (and/or the converse). We give doctors more credit than this, and thus focus on the remaining interesting scenario in which, without loss of generality, usual care outperforms control but is itself outperformed by treatment.

2
Setting

To make our ideas concrete, consider a trial in which a random subset of individuals from an observational cohort is recruited into a randomized experiment. These individuals in the cohort are, in turn, considered to be independent and identical draws from a near-infinite superpopulation. For individuals recruited into the experiment, treatment
A

A=1

A=0

To simplify the presentation, we assume that the patients are randomly distributed across physicians, possibly because the physicians practice in the same hospital department or because general practitioners serve exchangeable patient lists. Designs approximating this description exist in practice, for example the Swedish registry-based randomized trials on myocardial infarction

When to trust the expert

Remark 1

(Multiple identified observations per doctor)
.

When physician identifiers are recorded and each physician contributes
n
encounters to the observational data, strictly more information about the distribution of
(V^{D},p_{D})
over
\mathcal{D}
is available, and the bound in Theorem
1
can potentially be tightened. In the limit as
n\to\infty
, the per-physician sample means
\bar{Y}_{D}
converge to
V^{D}
. Thus, the full distribution of
V^{D}
—and hence
P(\delta_{D}\geq\delta^{*})
—is identified directly, and no bound is needed.

For finite
n
, the situation is intermediate. Repeated observations can identify features of the across-physician
distribution of treatment probabilities and expected outcomes, such as
variation in
p_{D}
, variation in
V^{D}
, and their dependence. Such
information can rule out constructions that are compatible with the
marginal means alone, and therefore can tighten the upper bound in Theorem
1
. The resulting sharp bounds
would incorporate the additional information about the distribution
of
(V^{D},p_{D})
, together with the feasibility constraints linking treatment
probabilities and attainable physician values. We do not derive those refined
bounds here.

Remark 2

(Refinements using the joint law of
(A,Y)
)
.

Theorem 1 gives a closed-form bound based on

Examining (
3

\approx 17\%

V_{U}=0.2
, and
V_{C}=0.17
, then as many as
60\%

How exactly should these bounds inform a patient’s decision whether to trust their doctor? We now shift from frequentist to subjective probability. The bounds (
3
) and (
4

\pi_{\delta^{*}}
are ‘good’ doctors with advantage
\phi_{U}>V^{d}-V_{T}=\delta^{*}>0
and

are ‘bad’ doctors with

(the value implied by
V_{U}-V_{T}
and
\delta^{*}

3

(\ref{eq:bound})
as a bound on the probability that
their
doctor is good. For a fixed
\delta^{*}

\tilde{\pi}_{\delta^{*}}
denote a patient’s degree of belief that their doctor is good. To rationally trust one’s doctor’s recommendation over ‘treat all’, it must be the case that
\tilde{\pi}_{\delta^{*}}>\frac{-V_{bad}}{\delta^{*}-V_{bad}}

\frac{-V_{bad}}{\delta^{*}-V_{bad}}
is much greater than bound (
3

\delta^{*}
is (i.e. the better ‘good’ doctors are), the lower the threshold probability
\frac{-V_{bad}}{\delta^{*}-V_{bad}}
becomes because there is more to be gained from trusting a good doctor. However, higher

also reduces the bound (
3

5
Discussion

To make our key points, we have ignored many complications that would enter into real decisions. We limited our analysis to treatments occurring at a single time point, excluding settings where ‘monitor the situation and then decide’ is an option. We also assumed that doctors treated patients with exchangeable characteristics, limiting applicability to decisions made outside the studied cohort. We only discussed single binary outcomes, ignoring that treatments often affect many outcomes on which patients place a range of utilities. For example, in the aortic-valve replacement example from the introduction, surgical replacement is often preferred in younger patients because they would derive more utility from resulting improved durability (i.e. longer time to replacement) than older patients with less time to live
(
7
)
. Furthermore, we did not consider in depth how data from multiple past treatment decisions by the same doctor might be brought to bear or how to account for a doctor claiming that they are
especially
confident that a particular patient would be harmed by treatment based on that patient’s characteristics.

Qualitatively, our results agree with common sense. A higher ATE in a trial and worse outcomes under usual care
should
both make one less likely to accept a doctor’s recommendation over trial evidence. We agree with
1
that in many cases it is important to ‘find a physician who knows that you and the average are not the same.’ However, it is also important to find a physician who is realistic about their ability to distinguish you from the average. We hope that by formalizing and quantifying some relevant considerations in a simplified setting, we encourage further work into the important question of how evidence based on averages should impact decisions for individuals.
