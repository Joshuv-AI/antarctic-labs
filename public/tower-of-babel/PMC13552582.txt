Sex‐Specific Differences in Perinatal Risk Factors for Atopic Dermatitis: A Large Retrospective Population‐Based Cohort Study
Authors: Roosa Moilanen, Eero Pohjola, Laura Huilaja, Jari Jokelainen, Suvi‐Päivikki Sinikumpu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study was designed to determine whether gestational age at birth, the method of delivery, or birth weight were associated with the risk of developing atopic dermatitis (AD), and whether the association of risk factors differed between the sexes.MethodsA large population‐based Northern Finland Birth Cohort 1986 Study that has been followed since birth. The dataset consisted of 8993 subjects who were divided into two groups based on presence or absence of AD.ResultsThe prevalence of AD was 23.7%. The probability of later AD was unaffected by either method of delivery or gestational age at birth. However, low birth weight compared to normal birth weight was a protective factor against AD, albeit only in men (adjusted risk ratio RR 0.54 [95% CI 0.30–0.97]). No such effect was observed in women.ConclusionsThese findings suggest the existence of sex‐specific risk factors for AD.

Introduction

With a lifetime prevalence of 10%–20% in developed countries [1] atopic dermatitis (AD) is one of the most common noncommunicable skin diseases. While its aetiology is multifactorial, genetic inheritance is its main risk factor [2]. Birth‐related risk factors, such as gestational age (GA), method of delivery (MOD), and birth weight (BW), have received less attention, and studies of these factors have yielded conflicting findings. A recent Swedish cohort study found a higher risk of developing AD in early childhood in children born by caesarean section (CS) than in those delivered vaginally [3], and other studies have yielded similar results [4, 5]. However, several other studies found no such association [6, 7, 8].

In a previous Finnish national registry study, children born moderately preterm (32–33 weeks) and late preterm (34–36 weeks) were less likely to develop severe hospital visits requiring AD than those born at term (≥ 37 weeks) [9]. Another study found that the risk of atopy increased linearly with GA post‐35 weeks [10]. Thus, a relatively preterm birth appears to be a protective factor against AD, whereas high GA can be recognized as a significant risk factor for AD [6, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Even extremely preterm (< 29 weeks) infants have been shown to have a lower risk of AD [15]. Like GA, high BW has been positively correlated with an elevated risk of AD in several studies [6, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 18].

Interestingly, one study found that being born with below‐average BW and having a low GA is a protective factor against AD, particularly in boys [19]. However, few studies have evaluated prenatal risk factors of AD separately in boys and girls. Thus our study was designed to assess GA, MOD, and BW as risk factors for AD at the population level with a particular focus on examining possible differences between sexes.

Patients and Methods

Our study is based on data gathered as part of the Northern Finland Birth Cohort (NFBC1986) which is a prospective longitudinal research program, which has followed from birth all individuals whose predicted date of full‐term birth lay between 1 July 1985 and 30 June 1986, who were born in Finland's two northernmost provinces: Oulu and Lapland. Data were also gathered from the mothers of the cohort during the antenatal period. To date, follow‐ups have been conducted via questionnaires at cohort ages of 1, 7–8, 15–16 and 33 years, at which a broad range of health‐ and lifestyle‐related data were gathered [20, 21].

For the present study, we gathered data from the NFBC database: antenatal questionnaire data were gathered from the records of the mothers, and cohort members' data on GA, MOD, and BW were collected at birth and data of AD from ages 7, 15–16 or 33 [20]. A patient was categorized as having AD if a physician‐made AD diagnosis was reported at any of these ages in cohort questionnaires (Figure 1).

Statistical Analyses

Baseline characteristics of the study population were summarized in a descriptive table stratified by AD status (yes/no). These groups were then compared for the following variables: sex (men/women), birth by caesarean section (CS) or vaginal delivery, low BW (< 2500 g) [22] or normal BW (> 2500 g), GA category (preterm [before 37 weeks]/full term [≥ 37 weeks]) [23], maternal smoking during pregnancy (yes/no), maternal body mass index (BMI) before pregnancy (mean), number of foetuses (one or more), exposure to a rural environment (defined as parent's occupation; farmer or not), and parental atopy (any atopic disease reported by cohort parents or no). Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations. Differences in categorical variables between individuals with and without AD were assessed using the chi‐square (χ 2) test, while differences in continuous variables were evaluated using the independent samples t‐test.

All regression analyses were conducted using a discrete‐time Poisson regression model based on person‐period data. Follow‐up time was divided into discrete age intervals, and each individual contributed one or more person‐period observations until the first occurrence of AD, after which they were censored and no longer considered at risk.

The logarithm of follow‐up time for each person‐period was included as an offset to account for varying lengths of observation. Models were fitted using a log link, and results are presented as rate ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To account for within‐individual correlation arising from repeated person‐period observations, cluster‐robust standard errors at the individual level were estimated using a sandwich variance estimator.

To assess potential effect modification, all second‐order interaction terms between the studied risk factors were evaluated within the discrete‐time Poisson regression framework. Among these, only the interaction between sex and low BW was statistically significant and was therefore retained in the final model.

The final model included main effects for sex, low BW, MOD, GA (categorized into two levels), maternal BMI before pregnancy, parental occupation (farmer vs. non‐farmer), and parental history of atopic disease, as well as the interaction term between sex and low BW. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) within the RStudio integrated development environment (version 2026.01.0; Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

A total of 9362 mothers were included in the study, accounting for 99% of all deliveries in the area during the target period. A total of 9479 children were born. The present data set consisted of 8993 cohort members, of which the data about AD was available. The prevalence of AD was 2135/8993 (23.7%) (Figure 1). Birth Weight and AD

The occurrence of AD did not differ significantly between those with low BW and those with normal BW in the total population. However, AD was present in a significantly greater proportion of men with normal BW than of those with low BW (p = 0.013; Supplemental data).

In an adjusted Poisson regression analysis, for low BW, compared with normal BW, adjusted RR was 1.00 (95% CI 0.75–1.33) in women and 0.54 (95% CI 0.30–0.97) in men; the interaction between sex and birth weight was statistically significant (p = 0.040).

Gestational Age and AD

Proportions of patients with AD did not differ significantly between subgroups based on whether the individual was born pre‐term or full‐term (Table 1). The Poisson regression analysis confirmed that prematurity had no effect on the risk of AD (Table 2).

Other Risk Factors of AD

A parental history of atopy was the only parental factor found to be significantly associated with the risk of AD (Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective birth cohort study, which investigated the association between birth factors and AD, low BW was a protective factor against AD, but only for men, even after adjustment for confounding factors such as parental atopy and GA.

Previous studies have similarly found that a lower BW is associated with a lower risk of AD, while some additionally found that a high BW is associated with an elevated risk [11, 12, 16, 17, 18]. Interestingly, however, we only found a protective effect of low BW in men, and no elevated risk of AD with high BW. To the best of our knowledge, only one previous study has identified a sex difference in the protective effect of low BW; in that study, parent‐reported AD was the primary outcome [19]. Even more interesting was the analysis of their secondary outcome, which used a doctor's diagnosis of AD: that analysis found that the protective effect of being small was more pronounced for boys than for girls, while the negative effect of being large (higher AD risk) was more pronounced among girls. It is noteworthy that only 65.1% of AD cases were physician‐diagnosed, while our own study looked exclusively at physician‐diagnosed cases [19].

Although low BW and prematurity are closely linked, the association between lower BW and lower AD risk has been independent of GA like in our study [11, 12, 16, 17, 18, 19]. In turn, when a higher BW is seen to predict a higher risk of AD, it also does so independently of GA [12, 18]. The hygiene hypothesis is one of the proposed mechanisms to explain this phenomenon, since lower‐weight newborns are often exposed to more frequent infections and allergens that may protect against AD [17]. Infants with low BW may also have immature or suppressed immune responses, including reduced Th2 activity early in life, which could reduce the risk of AD [22]. Furthermore, neonatal adiposity has been identified as a risk factor for AD [23]. Newborn boys generally have a slightly higher BW than girls [24], as was the case in our study population. Thus, boys with BW < 2500 g differ more from their normal weight counterparts than do underweight girls, which may at least partially explain their lower AD risk.

The method of delivery had no impact on AD risk in our study. Previous research has found a weak association between CS and AD [3, 6, 7, 8], but a 2008 meta‐analysis found no statistically significant correlation between CS and AD [8]. However, a recent Swedish cohort study (n = 1 399 406) found a statistically significant, although weak, association between CS and AD in children aged 1–5 years [3]. After sibling control analysis, statistical significance remained only for children aged < 1 year [3]. A smaller Korean study also identified a correlation (OR 1.49) between CS and AD. In that study, the association grew stronger after adjustment for several confounding factors such as BW and breastfeeding (aOR = 1.80) [5]. Nevertheless, unlike ours, that study did not adjust for parental atopy, which is likely a major confounder. The modest effect of CS on AD risk may indicate that the effect could be explained by residual confounding factors.

Premature birth (as recorded by independent assessment of GA) did not influence the risk of AD in our study. Some previous studies have identified a lower GA as a protective factor against AD ([9, 12, 13, 15, 25]), even when adjusted for BW. Such an effect has even been found for infants born extremely prematurely ([15, 25]). Several previous studies that found associations between GA and AD employed study designs and AD definitions similar to our own [9, 12, 13]. Furthermore, some looked at similar study populations and one at the Finnish population specifically [9]. However, unlike ours, the previous Finnish study [9] counted only hospital inpatient cases of AD. This could have led to some selection bias, with the inclusion of only more severe cases, while our study also likely included less severe cases. This could explain why the previous Finnish study found an association between GA and AD risk, while ours did not. The other abovementioned studies may have had similar selection bias [12, 13]. Other notable differences between our study and others are the long follow‐up time of our study and that we analyzed the data separately between sexes.

The major strength of this study was that using data from the NFBC1986 allowed us to study a large population with decades of follow‐up data. Cohort setting also gave us access to a wide range of background information as well as data on maternal factors which could be taken into account in adjusting the model. Furthermore, the fact that all participants were born in the same year likely negates possible sources of bias arising from changes over time in CS indications. Our study did have some notable weaknesses, such as the main outcome, AD, being identified by a questionnaire. Nevertheless, only doctor‐diagnosed AD was asked for in the questionnaire, and the prevalence of AD in our study aligns with previous reports [1, 3, 7, 15]. However, the prevalence of AD was higher in women than in men. This is the phenomenon that has also been observed in other studies [26]. It is possible that women are more likely to seek medical care, and therefore they had more doctor‐diagnosed AD also in our study. Other limitations that should be noted are that the records of some of the cohort members were missing data on some covariates, and that some original cohort members were lost to follow‐up entirely, which could have led to selection bias. Further, we cannot generalize the results to other age groups or ethnicities. The NFBC1986 represents approximately 16% of all children born in 1986 in Finland. At that time, the population structure and living conditions in these parts of Finland did not differ significantly from the rest of the country, so however, the cohort can be considered to represent a fairly accurate sample of the entire Finnish population and further the Caucasian population.

In conclusion, low BW in boys may be a protective factor for AD in later life, suggesting sex‐specific risk factors that may predispose individuals to different developmental trajectories of the disease and potentially reflect links between early growth and immune system development. A comprehensive understanding of the risk factors for AD would enable early and proactive treatment, which could improve clinical outcomes and quality of life in patients with AD. Few other studies have evaluated perinatal risk factors for AD separately in boys and girls, so more research is required.
