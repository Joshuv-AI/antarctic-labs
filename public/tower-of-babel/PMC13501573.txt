BDNF secreted by mesenchymal stem cells ameliorates the accelerated meiotic progression in aged mouse oocytes by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway
Authors: Xin Mi, Jiali Zhou, Caiyi Chen, Jing Zhou, Yajuan Yang, Hongyan Wang, Xue Jiao, Yingying Qin, Zi-Jiang Chen, Shidou Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOocyte aneuploidy is a major contributor to age-related fertility decline, but the effective treatments are still unavailable. The spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC), which functions to maintain chromosomal euploidy in oocytes, may become impaired with aging, manifesting primarily as an aberrant acceleration of meiotic progression. In the present study, we investigated the effects, effective components, and molecular mechanisms of the secretome of mesenchymal stem cells (MSC-sec) in reducing aneuploidy by alleviating the accelerated meiotic progression of aged mouse oocytes.MethodsMSC-sec was added to oocyte culture medium and its effects on aged oocytes were evaluated through analysis of meiotic process, chromosome alignment, the expression and localization of SAC proteins BubR1 and ZW10. The critical role of the PI3K/AKT pathway was confirmed by application of the inhibitor LY294002. Exogenous brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) or BDNF neutralizing antibodies were used to identify the effective components of MSC-sec. The in vitro effects of 7,8-dihydroxyflavone (7,8-DHF), a BDNF receptor agonist, were validated through PI3K/AKT and ERK1/2 pathway activity, meiotic progression and aneuploidy assays, while its in vivo effects were assessed by oocyte quality and early embryo development following intraperitoneal injection.ResultsMSC-sec could ameliorate age-related accelerated meiotic progression and increase the expression of BubR1 and ZW10 at the kinetochore in mouse oocyte. MSC-sec treatment activates the PI3K/AKT pathway in aged oocytes, and its effect on meiotic progression can be blocked by the PI3K inhibitor LY294002. BDNF is identified as the effective component of MSC-sec in decelerating the meiotic progression. In vitro application of 7,8-DHF ameliorated the accelerated meiotic progression and reduced the aneuploidy rate in aged oocytes, effectively mimicking the effects of BDNF. In vivo administration of 7,8-DHF significantly enhanced oocyte quality and early embryo development in aged mice.ConclusionsWe found that BDNF secreted by MSCs ameliorates the accelerated meiotic progression in aged mouse oocytes by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway, which may be attributed to an improvement in SAC function. Our study elucidates the molecular mechanism and effective component of MSC-sec to alleviate aneuploidy of aged oocytes, providing an experimental foundation for the clinical translation of BDNF or its alternative 7,8-DHF in improving the quality and development potential of oocytes from aged women.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05103-4.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells, Aging, Oocyte quality, PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, Brain-derived neurotrophic factor

Introduction

The globally widespread delay of childbearing age has led to an increased focus on female reproductive aging [1]. While uterine, tubal, and endocrine factors collectively participate in reproductive aging, the observed decline in assisted reproductive technology (ART) outcomes in egg donors beyond age 35 demonstrates that the primary cause of declining female fertility is ovarian aging [2, 3]. Besides the number of oocytes is continuously depleted with increasing maternal age, the quality of oocytes is also severely impaired [4]. Improving the quality of aged oocytes has been recognized as a potential approach to improve fertility in females with advanced age.

The primary manifestation of age-related decline of oocyte quality is the increased incidence of chromosomal aneuploidy [5]. Due to the lack of centrosome-containing microtubule organizing centers, spindle assembly in mammalian oocytes is markedly unstable, and the multipolar spindle rate is significantly increased compared with that in somatic cells [6, 7]. In addition, the larger size of oocytes compared to somatic cells results in a relatively reduced content of the mitotic checkpoint complex, which is not effective in preventing error-containing oocytes from progressing to late meiosis, increasing the occurrence of oocyte aneuploidy [8, 9]. This abnormality is more prevalent in oocytes from females with advanced age. Aneuploidy rates reach more than 50% after the age of 35 years in women, which is a major cause of embryonic developmental arrests and pregnancy loss [10]. Reasons known for the increased rate of aneuploidy in aged women’s oocytes include decreased expression of cohesion proteins, increased DNA damage, abnormal mitochondrial function, epigenetic abnormalities, as well as decreased expression of some spindle assembly-related proteins [10–14].

Notably, the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) plays a crucial role in ensuring the correct segregation of chromosomes during meiosis. SAC proteins include Bub1, BubR1, MAD1/2, MPS1 etc [15]. At metaphase I, SAC proteins are localized to kinetochores that are not yet correctly attached to microtubules and inhibit homologous chromosome segregation by suppressing APC/C activation. Upon completion of kinetochore attachment, SAC proteins separate from the kinetochore and APC/C is activated, thereby degrading Securin and Cyclin B to activate Separase, leading to cohesion removal and metaphase I to anaphase I transition [15, 16]. The deletion or dysfunction of the SAC genes leads to an acceleration of the meiosis progression of the oocyte and ultimately formation of aneuploidy [14, 15]. It has been reported that the meiotic progression of oocytes from aged mice is significantly accelerated compared to those from young mice [17]. Aberrant acceleration of meiotic progression is a key manifestation of impaired SAC function in oocytes, which leaves insufficient time for the correction of erroneous kinetochore-microtubule attachments, thereby increasing the incidence of aneuploidy [11, 15, 18]. Furthermore, it has been reported that the expression of the SAC proteins including BubR1, ZW10, and MAD2 is reduced in aged oocytes [19–21]. These studies indicate that the SAC function in aged oocytes is impaired, which may lead to accelerated meiotic progression and subsequently increased oocyte aneuploidy in aged females.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a class of stem cells with self-renewal capacity and multidirectional differentiation potentials, which can be obtained in various tissues such as umbilical cord, bone marrow, and adipose [22]. As a new approach with acknowledged benefits in various refractory diseases, MSCs have been shown to be effective in both animal and clinical studies of premature ovarian insufficiency or natural ovarian aging [23]. It is currently believed that MSCs have the capacity to improve the ovarian microenvironment by promoting cell proliferation, angiogenesis, anti-apoptosis, anti-oxidation, anti-fibrosis, and immunomodulation through paracrine effects, thereby regulating ovarian follicle development and improving ovarian function [24]. Secretome of MSCs (MSC-sec) is referred to a complex set of molecules secreted by MSCs, including a variety of growth factors, chemokines, and cytokines [25, 26]. Our previous studies found that hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) in MSC-sec improved function in aged or injured ovaries through PI3K/AKT pathway [27, 28]. Our latest research further revealed that brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in MSC-sec reduced the aneuploidy rate in aged oocytes by activating the ERK1/2 pathway [29]. However, the specific mechanisms of MSCs to alleviate age-related ovarian dysfunction, particularly oocyte aneuploidy, require further exploration.

In this study, we found that MSC-sec decelerates the meiotic progression in aged oocytes, which might be due to an improvement in SAC function. Moreover, we demonstrated that BDNF is the effective component of MSC-sec to ameliorated the accelerated meiotic progression in aged oocytes via the PI3K/AKT pathway, and ultimately reduced the aneuploidy of aged oocytes. In addition, 7,8-DHF, a naturally compound that is an agonist of the receptor for BDNF, also improved quality and early embryonic developmental potential of aged oocytes. Our study elucidates the molecular mechanism and effective component of MSC-sec to alleviate aneuploidy of aged oocytes, which provides an experimental foundation for the clinical usage of BDNF or its alternative 7,8-DHF in improving the quality of oocytes from aged females.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Female ICR mice at 6–8 weeks and 9 months of age were purchased from Charles River Laboratories, Beijing, China. Animals were housed under SPF conditions with controlled temperature and humidity, with a 12/12 h light and dark cycle. In this study, only the minimally invasive procedure of intraperitoneal injection was performed, with no long-term painful processes such as surgery involved, and no adverse animal events were observed. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under inhalation isoflurane anesthesia (RWD, China, R510-22) for tissue collection. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

hUC-MSCs culture and hUC-MSC-sec preparation

hUC-MSCs were derived from Shandong Qilu Stem Cell Engineering Co. Ltd., with their characterization methods as previously described [28]. hUC-MSCs at passages 6–9 were expanded in α-MEM medium (Gibco, USA, 523315) containing 5% ultra-advanced GRO serum replacement (Helios, USA, HPCFDCGL50) using 75 cm² flasks (Corning, USA, 430641). At 90% confluency, the medium was removed and washed 3 times with PBS. Then DMEM/F-12 (Gibco, USA, 11320-033) was added for 48 h. The supernatant was then collected and centrifuged at 1,000×g for 5 min and passed through a 0.22 μm filter to remove cellular debris. The clarified conditioned medium was then concentrated 25-fold using 3 kDa centrifuge tubes (Millipore, Germany, UFC900396) at 5,000 × g for 40–50 min. Finally, the MSC-sec was diluted 50-fold in M16 medium (Sigma, Germany, M7292) to achieve a final 0.5-fold working concentration for experimental use.

In vitro oocyte maturation

Young and aged mice were respectively injected 5 IU and 10 IU of PMSG (Ningbo Sansheng, China, 110044564). After 48 h, ovaries were collected and mechanically sliced using a sterile blade to release cumulus‑free germinal vesicle (GV)‑stage oocytes. Only fully grown GV-stage oocytes were included for experimentation. Growing oocytes were identified and excluded based on their smaller diameter (< 60 μm) and morphological appearance under a stereomicroscope [30]. GV-stage oocytes were cultured in M16 medium (Sigma, Germany, M7292) in a 37℃ humidified environment with 5% CO₂, 5% O₂, and 90% N₂. Depending on the specific research objectives, the control groups consisted of untreated aged oocytes and untreated young oocytes, and the experimental groups included aged oocytes treated with hUC-MSC-Sec (0.5-fold concentration), hUC-MSC-sec plus 20 µM LY294002 (Selleck, USA, S1105), 1 µg/ml BDNF neutralizing antibody (HUABIO, China, ET1606-42, RRID: AB_3069737), 250 pg/ml recombinant BDNF (PeproTech, USA, 450-02-10) or 5 nM 7,8-DHF (Sigma, Germany, D5446). In vitro cultured oocytes were assessed of PB1 extrusion timing, chromosome distribution at MI stage, BubR1 location and expression, ZW10 location and expression, PI3K/AKT and ERK1/2 pathway activity, oocytes maturation rate and aneuploidy rate. The specific duration of oocyte culture depends on the meiotic stage being studied: 5 h (pro-metaphase I, pro-MI); 7.5 h (metaphase I, MI); 16 h (metaphase II, MII) [21, 31]. The PB1 extrusion rate was calculated as the percentage of oocytes that had extruded PB1 relative to the total number of GV oocytes.

For oocyte-based assays, all oocytes collected in an experiment were pooled and then randomly distributed into treatment and control groups to minimise individual variability. The experimental unit was defined as one culture dish of oocytes. Each unit contained approximately 30 oocytes based on common practice in previous studies. Three independent experimental units (n = 3) were used per group for statistical analysis. Blinding was not employed in this study. However, to ensure accuracy in data recording, all primary outcome data were independently extracted and verified by two researchers prior to statistical analysis.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

After 7.5 h in vitro cultured, oocytes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma, Germany, P6148) for 30 min. Then oocytes were permeabilized and blocked with 1% BSA (Sigma, Germany, B2064) plus 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma, Germany, T8787) for 1 h. Following a 2 h incubation with anti-α-tubulin FITC antibody (1:500, Sigma, Germany, F2168, RRID: AB_476967) at room temperature. After washing 3 times in PBS, oocytes were transferred onto glass slides and mounted in DAPI-containing mounting media (Abcam, USA, ab104139). Images were acquired using a 63×objective lens (NA 1.40, oil immersion) on the Dragonfly confocal laser-scanning microscope (Andor Technology, UK). Z-stack images (6 optical sections) were acquired and presented as maximum intensity projections. Meiotic stages were classified based on spindle morphology and chromosome alignment according to the criteria outlined by Eichenlaub-Ritter et al. [17]. Chromosome alignment was assessed exclusively in oocytes that had reached the MI stage at 7.5 h of in vitro culture [21, 31]. These MI-stage oocytes constituted approximately 60% of all retrieved oocytes. The remaining ~ 40%, which had either not yet reached or had already progressed beyond MI-stage, were excluded from this analysis. For chromosome and spindle morphology observation, metaphase plate was defined as a drawn line, where > 10 bivalents congressed, and misaligned chromosomes were those clearly away from this line [32]. Aberrant spindles were defined as any spindle that deviates from the normal bipolar morphology, including multipolar, monopolar, or disorganized microtubule arrays.

Chromosome spreads

The zona pellucida of pro-MI (5 h cultured in vitro) or MII (16 h cultured in vitro) oocytes were digested using acidic M2 medium, prepared by adding 7 µL of analytical-grade hydrochloric acid to 1 mL of M2 medium (Sigma, Germany, M7167). The denuded oocytes were washed in pre-warmed M2 medium and transferred to glass slides containing 1% PFA, and 0.15% Triton X-100. Following air-dried, samples were blocked with 10% BSA for 1 h. For pro-MI oocytes, chromosome spreads were incubated with antibodies of CREST (1:50, Antibodies Incorporated, USA, 15–234-001, RRID: AB_2939058), BubR1 (1:100, Abcam, USA, ab28193, RRID: AB_725786) or ZW10 (1:100, Proteintech, China, 24561, RRID: AB_2815001) at 4 °C overnight. Then incubated with 488-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (1:400, Invitrogen, USA, A48276), 555-conjugated donkey anti-sheep IgG (1:400, Invitrogen, USA, A21436) or 568-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:400, Invitrogen, USA, A10042) for 1 h. To measure fluorescence intensity, signals were acquired from both the control and treatment groups by performing the same immunostaining procedure and setting the same confocal microscopy parameters. ImageJ software (NIH, USA) was used to calculate the average fluorescence intensity per unit area within the kinetochore region of interest, thereby enabling quantitative analysis of the fluorescence in each image. For MII oocytes, chromosome spreads were incubated with antibodies of CREST and TOP2 (1:100, Abcam, USA, ab109524, RRID: AB_10859793). Then incubated with 488-conjugated goat anti-human IgG and 568-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG for 1 h. Afterwards, chromosome spreads were mounted in DAPI-containing mounting media and imaged under confocal microscope.

Western blot

After 7.5 h in vitro cultured, oocytes were lysed in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, China, R0010), and denatured with SDS loading buffer at 95℃ for 5 min. The denatured proteins were subsequently separated by SDS-PAGE and electro-transferring onto a PVDF membrane. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk with TBST, membranes were probed with primary antibodies against p-AKT (Ser473; 1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, 9271, RRID: AB_329825), total AKT (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, 9272, RRID: AB_329827), p-ERK1/2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, 9106, RRID: AB_331768), total ERK1/2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, 4695, RRID: AB_390779), β-Actin (1:2000, Proteintech, China, 66009-1, RRID: AB_2883475), or GAPDH (1:2000, Proteintech, China, 60004-1, RRID: AB_2920461) at 4 °C overnight. The HRP-conjugated secondary antibody incubation was followed by chemiluminescent detection using a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using gels analysis function in ImageJ software and normalized to loading controls. Each experiment was independently repeated three times.

Intraperitoneal injection

7,8-DHF was prepared as a 100 mg/ml stock solution in DMSO and diluted to 1 mg/ml in PBS for administration. Ten 9-month-old female ICR mice were randomly assigned using a block randomization stratified by body weight to a control group (receiving 10% DMSO in PBS) and a 7,8-DHF treated group (receiving 5 mg/kg 7,8-DHF injection). Mice with body weights outside the normal range for their age and strain were excluded from the study to ensure physiological homogeneity. The intraperitoneal injections were administered for 6 consecutive days. Cage positions were rotated daily, and treatments were performed in a randomized order among cages each day. Superovulation was induced with 10 IU PMSG on day 4, and subsequently 10 IU hCG (Ningbo Sansheng, China, 110041282) on day 6. Oocytes were collected for assessment of maturation rate, chromosome alignment, and in vitro fertilization. The primary outcome was the rate of oocytes maturation. This in vivo experiment was independently replicated three times for statistical analysis.

In vitro fertilization and embryo culture

At 48 h post-PMSG administration, superovulation was induced via intraperitoneal injection of 10 IU hCG. Approximately 16 h later, cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) were retrieved from the oviductal ampullae. Then, COCs were fertilized in G-IVF PLUS medium (Vitrolife, Sweden, 10136) using sperm from adult male ICR mice. For embryonic development analysis, zygotes were cultured in G1 PULS medium (Vitrolife, Sweden, 10128), in a 37℃ humidified environment with 5% CO₂, 5% O₂, and 90% N₂. The fertilization rate was calculated as the proportion of 2PN embryos to retrieved COCs; the 2-cell embryo rate and blastocyst rate were calculated as the proportion of 2-cell embryos and blastocysts to 2PN embryos at 24 h and day 4.5 post-fertilization, respectively.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 30.0 software. The data were presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR). Normality of data distribution was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. For two-group comparisons, normally distributed data were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t‑test, and non‑normally distributed data were analyzed using Mann‑Whitney U test. For three-group comparisons, normally distributed data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, and non‑normally distributed data were first analyzed using the Kruskal‑Wallis test, and when the overall test was significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. The difference was considered to be statistically significant when P < 0.05.

Results

MSC-sec ameliorates the accelerated meiotic progression in aged oocytes

To study reproductive aging, 9-month-old female mice were selected, whose physiological state corresponds to a female around 35 years old [33]. At this stage, the quality of oocytes and female fertility both significantly decline [10]. To investigate the effects of MSC-sec on aged oocytes, we added MSC-sec to the culture medium of mouse oocytes. The results revealed that the timing of first polar body (PB1) extrusion was significantly accelerated in aged compared to young group, which was slowed down after MSC-sec treatment, suggesting that MSC-sec ameliorated the abnormal meiotic progression in aged oocytes (Fig. 1A). After 7.5 h of in vitro culture, oocytes were immunofluorescence stained for α-tubulin (microtubules) and DAPI (chromosomes) to visualize spindle morphology and chromosome alignment. Based on these features, oocytes were classified into different meiotic stages [17]. In the aged group, the percentage of prophase to pro-MI stage oocytes was lower than that in the young group (38.73 ± 4.45% vs. 22.91 ± 2.95%), whereas the percentage in anaphase to telophase I was higher (0.95 ± 1.65% vs. 10.58 ± 1.52%), suggesting accelerated meiotic progression. MSC-sec treatment increased the percentage of oocytes in prophase to pro-MI stage (22.91 ± 2.95% vs. 34.56 ± 3.02%) and decreased that in anaphase to telophase I stage (10.58 ± 1.52% vs. 0.98 ± 1.70%) compared to aged group (Fig. 1B). To indirectly assess SAC function via chromosome alignment at MI stage, we restricted our analysis to oocytes that were at the MI stage at 7.5 h of culture in the following experiments. Of note, limited by our experimental approach, this analysis excluded oocytes that had not yet reached or had already passed through MI stage. Immunofluorescence staining showed that the percentage of chromosome arrangement abnormality was significantly increased in aged oocytes (18.21 ± 0.83%) compared to young oocytes (7.00 ± 2.17%) at MI stage oocytes cultured in vitro for 7.5 h, and the distribution of chromosomes was decentralized, which was manifested as an increase in chromosome width (13.01 ± 3.11 μm vs. 15.62 ± 2.86 μm) (Fig. 1C–E). In addition, we detected the expression and localization of the SAC proteins BubR1 and ZW10 through immunofluorescence staining. The percentage of BubR1 co-localization with kinetochore marker CREST was decreased (96.81 ± 2.87% vs. 75.18 ± 4.51%), and the expression of BubR1 was reduced at kinetochore region (1.10, IQR 0.89–1.21 vs. 0.44, IQR 0.23–0.82) in aged oocytes at pro-MI stage (Fig. 1F–H). Unlike BubR1, ZW10 was completely co-localized with kinetochores in both young and aged groups, but its expression at kinetochore region was reduced in aged oocytes at pro-MI stage (1.00 ± 0.16 vs. 0.82 ± 0.21) (Fig. 1I–K). Compared with aged oocytes, treatment with MSC-sec alleviated both chromosomal arrangement abnormality (18.21 ± 0.83% vs. 5.44 ± 1.53%), chromosome width (15.62 ± 2.86 μm vs. 12.97 ± 3.21 μm), BubR1 co-localization with kinetochores (75.18 ± 4.51% vs. 94.07 ± 5.14%), BubR1 expression (0.44, IQR 0.23–0.82 vs. 1.05, IQR 0.91–1.12), and ZW10 expression (0.82 ± 0.21 vs. 0.95 ± 0.14) at kinetochore region (Fig. 1C–K). These results suggest that MSC-sec treatment decelerates the meiotic progression and partially alleviate SAC abnormality in aged oocytes.

MSC-sec ameliorates the accelerated meiotic progression in aged oocytes through the PI3K/AKT pathway

Our latest research revealed that MSC-sec treatment could activate the ERK1/2 signaling pathway in aged oocytes [29]. However, treatment with U0126, an ERK1/2 pathway inhibitor [34], had no significant effect on meiotic progression (Supplement Fig. 1A, B). Numerous studies have shown that MSCs function primarily through the ERK1/2 and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways in a variety of tissues and cells [35–37]. Previous studies have also reported that phosphorylated AKT localizes to the meiotic spindle poles and microtubules in oocytes at the metaphase of meiosis, suggesting a role for the PI3K/AKT pathway in spindle assembly, but its exact function remains unknown [38]. Therefore, we hypothesize that MSC-sec may improve spindle assembly in aged oocytes through the PI3K/AKT pathway. Western blot assay showed that AKT phosphorylation was decreased significantly in aged oocytes (0.43 ± 0.13) compared with young groups (1.00 ± 0.08), and increased after MSC-sec treatment (0.74 ± 0.13), suggesting that MSC-sec can facilitate the PI3K/AKT pathway activation in aged oocytes (Fig. 2A, B). Compared with MSC-sec treated alone, the combined use of MSC-sec and LY294002, an inhibitor of the PI3K/AKT pathway [39], resulted in accelerated meiotic progression and increased the percentage of abnormal chromosome alignment (7.26 ± 2.55% vs. 24.20 ± 5.66%) and the chromosome width (13.78 ± 3.46 μm vs. 16.47 ± 4.61 μm) in MI stage aged oocytes cultured in vitro for 7.5 h (Fig. 2C–F). These results indicate that MSC-sec decelerates the meiotic progression in aged oocytes via the PI3K/AKT pathway.

BDNF is identified to be the effective component of MSC-sec to ameliorate the accelerated meiotic progression in aged mouse oocytes.

Considering that our previous study found BDNF can increase the euploidy of aged oocytes [29], and previous studies have confirmed BDNF can activate the PI3K/AKT pathway in multiple tissues and cells [40, 41], we determined whether BDNF is a key component of MSC-sec to ameliorate the accelerated meiotic progression in aged oocytes. Application of BDNF-neutralizing antibody (BDNFab) abolished the effects of MSC-sec on meiotic progression, the percentage of defective congression chromosome (5.91 ± 1.76% vs. 22.75 ± 5.63%), and chromosome width (12.49, IQR 9.74–15.14 μm vs. 13.36, IQR 11.94–17.42 μm) in MI stage oocytes cultured in vitro for 7.5 h (Fig. 3A–D). Furthermore, BDNF treatment alone also promoted AKT phosphorylation (1.00 ± 0.24 vs. 1.71 ± 0.31), slowed down meiotic progression, decreased the percentage of defective congression chromosome (23.69 ± 3.64% vs. 10.97 ± 2.89%), and decreased the chromosome width (15.65, IQR 13.69–18.37 μm vs. 13.53, IQR 10.96–16.12 μm) in MI stage oocytes cultured in vitro for 7.5 h (Fig. 4A–F). Immunofluorescence staining for the SAC protein revealed that, following BDNF treatment, the expression of both BubR1 (1.00 ± 0.27 vs. 1.44 ± 0.32) and ZW10 (1.00 ± 0.29 vs. 1.23 ± 0.26) was increased at kinetochore region, and the co-localization of BubR1 with kinetochores was ameliorated (65.97 ± 3.18% vs. 91.30 ± 3.26%) (Fig. 4G–L). To further demonstrate that BDNF to ameliorate the accelerated meiotic progression of aged oocytes via the PI3K/AKT pathway, we combined BDNF with LY294002 and found that meiotic progression was accelerated, the percentage of chromosome misalignment was increased (7.97 ± 1.01% vs. 23.69 ± 2.47%), and the chromosome width was increased (13.62 ± 2.42 μm vs. 17.02 ± 4.50 μm) compared to BDNF treatment group (Fig.S2A–D). These results suggest that BDNF is the effective component of MSC-sec to ameliorate the accelerated meiotic progression through the PI3K/AKT pathway in aged mouse oocytes.

Application of 7,8-DHF improves oocyte quality and early embryonic development in aged mice

Although proven beneficial for improving of aged oocytes quality, BDNF is limited in its poor pharmacokinetic properties and therefore requires alternatives in clinical applications [42]. The natural compound 7,8-DHF has been shown to be an agonist of the BDNF receptor TrKB, which is capable of activating the PI3K/AKT and ERK1/2 signaling pathways in a variety of tissues and cells [43–45]. 7,8-DHF has received increased attention in recent years as an alternative to BDNF, and its clinical potential in the treatment of neurological disorders has been recognized [46]. However, there are currently no studies on the effectiveness of 7,8-DHF in reproductive medicine. To further advance clinical translation, we investigated the in vitro and in vivo effects of 7,8-DHF on improving oocyte quality in aged mice.

In vitro experiments showed that 7,8-DHF treatment promoted activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway (1.00 ± 0.04 vs. 1.35 ± 0.08) and ERK1/2 pathway (1.00 ± 0.04 vs. 1.16 ± 0.06) in aged oocytes (Fig. 5A-D), and improved maturation rate (71.58 ± 1.41% vs. 84.47 ± 2.20%) of in vitro cultured aged oocytes (Fig. 5E-F). 7,8-DHF treatment significantly slowed down meiotic progression in aged oocytes (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, chromosome spreading of oocytes at MII stage revealed a significant decrease in the aneuploidy rate after 7,8-DHF treatment (35.60 ± 2.62% vs. 13.16 ± 3.53%) (Fig. 5H, I). These results suggest that 7,8-DHF can effectively mimic the functions of BDNF in vitro, thereby improving the maturation and quality of aged oocytes.

To further validate the in vivo efficacy, intraperitoneal injection of 7,8-DHF for 6 consecutive days was performed in aged mice. While the number of oocytes obtained following ovulation induction showed no significant difference (16.80 ± 5.50% vs. 18.32 ± 3.13%), the rate of PB1 extrusion was significantly increased (71.58 ± 1.41% vs. 84.47 ± 2.20%) (Fig. 6A–C). Immunofluorescence staining showed a significant decrease in the percentage of abnormal spindle and defective chromosome congression in aged oocytes from 7,8-DHF-treated mice (11.90 ± 7.05%) compared with controls (29.29 ± 2.15%) (Fig. 6D, E). Although the fertilization rate was unchanged (84.22 ± 4.61% vs. 86.24 ± 6.52%) after oocyte superovulation and in vitro fertilization (IVF), both the 2-cell stage rate (86.16 ± 5.48% vs. 98.33 ± 1.44%) and blastocyst rate (72.24 ± 0.96% vs. 88.34 ± 8.00%) were significantly increased, suggesting an improvement in oocyte quality and development potential by in vivo application of 7,8-DHF (Fig. 6F, G). These results further emphasize the great potential of 7,8-DHF as a good alternative to BDNF in improving fertility in aged mice, providing an experimental basis for translational applications.

Discussion

With the postponement of childbearing age, ameliorating age-related oocyte quality decline has become an urgent and critical challenge in the field of reproductive medicine [1, 5]. Although several studies have identified a series of potential drugs, including spermidine, resveratrol, melatonin, coenzyme Q10, quercetin, and EGCC, which have an in vivo or in vitro improvement effect on the quality of aged oocytes [47–52], there is still no clinical approach to overcome this problem. Our previous study found that BDNF in MSC-sec enhances the expression of DAZL and BTG4, which are key downstream genes of the ERK1/2 pathway, thereby ameliorating spindle morphology and increasing maternal RNA degradation in aged oocytes [29]. During this study, we further revealed that MSC-sec and BDNF effectively ameliorated the accelerated meiotic progression and partially restored SAC function in aged oocytes through the PI3K/AKT pathway, therefore reducing aneuploidy in aged oocytes. Our studies identified and emphasized the pleiotropic mechanisms of MSC-sec that differs from other potential drugs.

An important mechanism for ensuring euploidy in mammalian oocytes is preventing the premature metaphase-to-anaphase transition in meiosis I by recruiting SAC proteins to unattached kinetochore [15, 16]. A series of studies reported decreased expression of Bub1, BubR1, ZW10 and MAD2 in aged oocytes, suggesting a defective SAC with increasing age [19–21]. Specifically, both the content of BubR1 and co-localization of BubR1 with the kinetochores was significantly reduced in aged human oocytes compared with those in young females [19, 53]. Touati et al. found that conditional knockdown of BubR1 in mouse oocytes resulted in an acceleration of meiosis I progression, revealing the involvement of BubR1 in the maintenance of SAC activity and the establishment of kinetochore-microtubule attachment in a meiosis-specific manner [54]. In addition, ZW10 showed reduced expression at kinetochores, but no age-related decrease in co-localization with kinetochores, in oocytes from aged mice. Microinjecting siRNA to knockdown Zw10 in young oocytes leads to accelerated meiotic progression, abnormal chromosome alignment, increased aneuploidy rates, and decreased MAD2 expression [21]. In the present study, we found that aged mouse oocytes displayed accelerated meiotic progression, abnormal chromosome arrangement, abnormal BubR1 expression and kinetochore localization, and decreased ZW10 expression, which were ameliorated by treatment with MSC-sec. The mechanisms underlying age-related oocyte aneuploidy, particularly cohesion protein depletion during aging, have been extensively characterized [11]. However, therapeutic targeting of cohesion defects remains challenging since these proteins are established during embryogenesis [55]. Current strategies for ameliorating oocyte aneuploidy are limited and primarily indirect, focusing on improving oxidative stress and mitochondrial function [47–52]. Notably, the contribution of SAC protein abnormalities in oocyte aneuploidy in aged females and its possibility as a potential therapeutic target has been underappreciated. Currently, there are limited strategies to mitigate the aberrantly accelerated meiotic progression in aged oocytes. Our study ameliorates the accelerated meiotic progression and partially restores SAC function in aged oocytes, providing an innovative potential approach to improve aged oocytes quality, especially aneuploidy. Furthermore, as a key pathway regulating cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism, the PI3K/AKT pathway is essential for the activation, maturation, and survival of oocytes [56, 57]. During the metaphase of meiosis, phosphorylated AKT localizes to the spindle poles and microtubules of the oocyte, suggesting that the PI3K/AKT pathway may also essential in spindle assembly and functional maintenance [39]. Consistently, our results demonstrate that MSC-sec ameliorates the accelerated meiotic progression in aged oocytes through the PI3K/AKT pathway.

Although stem cell transplantation has been proven safe by numerous clinical trials, switching from a cell-based therapy to a cell-free therapy with secretome or a single effective component is a more convenient and efficient treatment [27, 28]. In this study, on the one hand, application of BDNF-neutralizing antibodies inhibited the ameliorative effect of MSC-sec on aged oocytes; on the other hand, exogenous BDNF effectively mimicked the effect of MSC-sec. Therefore, BDNF was identified as the effective component of MSC-sec to ameliorates the accelerated meiotic progression and reduce aneuploidy of aged oocytes. Furthermore, our previous studies have verified that adding low concentrations of BDNF during in vitro culture improves aged human oocyte maturation and early embryonic development, and have also demonstrated its beneficial effects in vivo by in situ injection of BDNF in aged mouse ovaries [31]. Taken together, our studies elucidate the specific mechanisms of BDNF to improve spindle assembly, maternal mRNA degradation, and meiotic progression through the ERK1/2 and PI3K/AKT pathway respectively, which provides theoretical and experimental basis for clinical application of BDNF.

Although BDNF has been shown as a potential drug for quality improvement in aged oocytes, the development of BDNF as a biopharmaceutical has been hindered by poor pharmacokinetic properties, especially rapid clearance due to its short half-life [45]. In addition, as a large molecular protein, drug delivery of BDNF is very limited and can only be applied by ovarian injection or in vitro oocyte culture [31, 58]. Alternatively, 7,8-DHF, an agonist of BDNF receptor TrKB, has been shown to effectively activate the PI3K/AKT and ERK1/2 pathways [43]. Compared with BDNF, 7,8-DHF has the advantages of low price, convenient storage, various delivery options, good pharmacokinetics, and a good safety in animal studies [48, 59]. Meanwhile, based on the advantage to cross the blood-brain barrier, BrAD-R13, a 7,8-DHF derivative drug for the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease, has been undergoing Phase I clinical trials [60, 61]. In this study, we firstly demonstrated the in vitro ameliorative effects of 7,8-DHF on aged oocytes, specifically its ability to effectively activate both the PI3K/AKT and ERK1/2 pathways, thereby improving the maturation and euploidy rate. Furthermore, based on the advantages of 7,8-DHF in translational medicine, we also validated its in vivo effects on improving the oocytes maturation, quality, and embryo developmental potential of aged mice via the intraperitoneal injection. Our research established the experimental foundation for the clinical translation of 7,8-DHF in improving age-related ovarian dysfunction and oocyte quality abnormalities.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although 9-month-old mice are considered a commonly used model for reproductive aging, further validation is required to determine the effects of MSC-sec and BDNF on aged human oocytes. Second, the composition of MSC-sec is complex. Although we have identified BDNF as the primary effective component of MSC-sec, it remains unable to fully mimic the effects of MSC-sec. Other cytokines and factors may also exert combined effects on aged oocytes. Third, the present study only briefly explored the effects of 7,8-DHF, without evaluating the post-implantation developmental capacity of the treated embryos or assessing safety. In future studies, fertility test and long-term health monitoring of the offspring will be necessary. Additionally, the experimental methodology also introduced some limitations. First, the analysis of chromosome alignment was restricted to oocytes that were at the MI stage after 7.5 h of in vitro culture, while those that had progressed either faster or slower were excluded. This selection may have introduced some bias into the results. Second, immunofluorescence images of MI stage oocytes were acquired as maximum intensity projections rather than three‑dimensional reconstructions. This approach may lead to a slight underestimation of misaligned chromosomes and introduce some measurement errors in the quantification of chromosome width.

In conclusion, our study revealed that MSC-sec and its effective component BDNF, activated the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to ameliorate the accelerated meiotic progression, thereby decreasing aneuploidy in oocytes of aged mice. We also validated the feasibility of the BDNF receptor agonist 7,8-DHF as a potential drug to improve the quality of aged oocytes. Our study provides an experimental basis for clinical usage of BDNF or its alternative in vivo or in vitro to improve oocyte quality in women with advanced age.
