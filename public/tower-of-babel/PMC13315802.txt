STAR Protocols question-and-answer series: CRISPR
Authors: Leila Shokri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCRISPR technology is a paradigm-shifting innovation in genome engineering, enabling precise gene editing in cell lines and model organisms. Robust protocols and troubleshooting strategies are essential for the successful application of CRISPR techniques in biological and clinical research. In this backstory, STAR Protocols authors and advisory board members share their advice on common questions related to CRISPR technologies, genome-editing strategies, and troubleshooting approaches.

Main text

What tools do you recommend for designing optimal single-guide RNAs?

Quanjiang Ji: Our lab often uses the sgRNAcas9 software to screen the optimal sgRNA target sites and evaluate potential off-target sites.

Toshihisa Nomura: We have used CRISPR-P, CRISPOR, and CRISPR-Local.

Timothy E. O’Sullivan: It will be useful to have digital annotations of your gene(s) of interest in a program such as Benchling, SnapGene, or ApE. These platforms can be used to locate your CRISPR guide sequences and the associated primers used to PCR the region surrounding the cut site in order to check the editing efficiency of your reaction later. To start, it is useful to source guides from previously published whole-genome CRISPR screens (e.g., Wang et al., 2017). We recommend testing three to five guide targets from this list per gene until a high-efficiency guide is identified experimentally. While this list has yielded high-efficiency guides for us in almost all cases, additional platforms such as inDelphi and SYNTHEGO offer guide design capabilities, which we have used when needed. However, before attempting to edit, ensure that you have optimized the PCR for your targeted genomic region. Each guide will require a PCR product that flanks the CRISPR cut side by about 300 bp ( ±100 bp) in both directions. Interestingly, we don’t find that there is a significant difference between guide efficiencies in early versus late exons for many genes. Therefore, we find it most useful to target exonic regions that contain multiple guide sequences to reduce the number of PCR reactions that must be optimized.

Ngoc-Tung Tran: We normally use the following program to design our sgRNAs. Sometimes, manual design is ideal if you know exactly where to target: CrispRGold and Synthego CRISPR design tool (good for KO).

Ting Zhou: There are some online web resources that are widely used for designing sgRNA targets, such as Benchling, CHOPCHOP, and Broad Institute GPP.

What tools/methods do you recommend for off-target detection?

Quanjiang Ji: We recommend whole-genome sequencing for detecting the genome-wide off-target effect.

Ting Zhou: The selection of methods for off-target detection varies from case to case. We would recommend that the readers make decisions based on their needs, taking the pros and cons of each method into consideration. Currently, the approaches used to detect off-target effect can be divided into biased and unbiased methods. The biased methods are usually performed to identify pre-selected or in silico predicted off-target sites by mismatch-detection nucleases or by deep sequencing of the potential sites. These methods are easier, faster, and widely available in the labs. The unbiased methods are applied to detect unintended cleavage sites anywhere in the genome such as cell-based GUIDE-seq, BLESS/BLISS, HTGTS, DSCOVER-seq or in vitro performed CIRCLE-seq, SITE-seq, and Digenome-seq. Generally, the unbiased genome-wide sequencing is more sophisticated and requires special equipment, but it is quite important for clinical translation of CRISPR-Cas9. Taken together, each method has its own benefits and limitations, so the appropriate one should be determined based on the needs of the study.

What are the most recommended CRISPR screening approaches? Are there any available libraries?

Heather Keys: CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) and CRISPR cutting are the most widely used screening modalities and have historically been easier to implement than CRISPR activation (CRISPRa). CRISPRi/a both require a stable dCas9-expressing cell line to be generated and its knockdown (or activation) efficiency verified before introducing the sgRNA library. For CRISPR cutting, I recommend using an all-in-one vector system, which expresses Cas9 and the sgRNA from the same plasmid, bypassing the need to create a stable Cas9-expressing cell line.

I recommend that researchers browse the genome-wide and targeted libraries that have been deposited at Addgene for a library that fits their needs.

Xiaoqing Zhang and Lin Ma: The following CRISPR screening approaches are currently most popular:

1.dCas9 transcriptional activation (CRISPRa): CRISPR activation method included a plasmid that involves incorporating MS2 binding loops into the sgRNA backbone to recruit two different activation domains, p65 and HSF1, to a dCas9-VP64 fusion. By recruiting three distinct transcriptional effectors, this synergistic activation mediator (SAM) complex could robustly and reliably drive transcriptional upregulation. And the SAM libraries target the 200-bp region upstream of the transcriptional start site of 23,430 human or 23,439 mouse RefSeq coding isoforms with three sgRNAs per isoform (Joung et al., 2017). Libraries: Human SAM Library, Zeo, 3-plasmid system (Addgene, cat. no. 1000000057); Human SAM Library, Puro, 3-plasmid system (Addgene, cat. no. 1000000074); Mouse SAM Library, Puro optimized, 3-plasmid system (Addgene, cat. no. 1000000075); Human SAM library, lentiSAMv2, 2-plasmid system (Addgene, cat. no. 1000000078).

What computational tools do you recommend to an experimental scientist for screen analysis?

Xiaoqing Zhang and Lin Ma: We recommend MAGeCK, MAGeCK-VISPR, and MAGeCKFlute for screen analysis. The X. Shirley Liu lab has developed two algorithms, MAGeCK and MAGeCK-VISPR, to analyze CRISPR-Cas9 screen data in various scenarios. These two algorithms allow users to perform quality control, read count generation and normalization, and calculate beta score to evaluate gene selection performance. Later, they developed MAGeCKFlute for supporting downstream analysis. MAGeCKFlute provides several strategies to remove potential biases within sgRNA-level read counts and gene-level beta scores. The downstream analysis with the package includes identifying essential, non-essential, and target-associated genes, and performing biological functional category analysis, pathway enrichment analysis, and protein complex enrichment analysis of these genes. The package also visualizes genes in multiple ways to benefit users exploring screening data. Collectively, MAGeCKFlute enables accurate identification of essential, non-essential, and targeted genes, as well as their related biological functions. Completing the entire MAGeCKFlute pipeline requires ∼3 h on a desktop computer running Linux or Mac OS with R support (Wang et al., 2019).

Website: MAGeCK/Wiki/Home (sourceforge.net).

Code available: https://github.com/WubingZhang/MAGeCKFlute.

What is a good control guide RNA?

Heather Keys: For CRISPR cutting in human cells, we use a negative control guide RNA targeting the AAVS1 integration site (sgRNA sequence: GGGGCCACTAGGGACAGGAT), which targets the genome but should not display a phenotype. For positive control guide RNAs, I recommend mining existing screen data in your cell line (for example, from the Broad Institute’s DepMap Project) or species of interest to identify genes and sgRNAs that score strongly for your phenotype.

When editing fails, how do you determine if it relates to the guide, delivery system, or genomic landscape?

Quanjiang Ji: In general, we will design two to four sgRNA targeting sites for one gene editing. If all of these targeting sites failed to edit, we would examine the delivery system by electroporating the empty plasmids (without guide-sequence insertion). In addition, we would likely employ other editing methods, such as cytidine base editing. If all the editing approaches failed, the target gene may be an essential or important gene.

Toshihisa Nomura: In my case, I redesign and retest gRNA at different sites on the same gene. I will also test the positive control gRNAs together to verify whether the genome editing tool is successfully introduced into the cells.

Timothy E. O’Sullivan: We recommend optimizing guide editing efficiency on cell types with robust, optimized culturing protocols. This ensures that cell turnover will be optimal for the healthy expansion of edited populations. For example, we use bone-marrow-derived macrophages to test all our guides, as these cells have a short culture length, proliferate well, and have high reproducibility between experiments. As such, if a guide does not edit well in the cell type of interest and it has been shown to edit well in pilot experiments with a previous cell type, we can rule out the guide as being the issue. Similarly, optimizing electroporation protocols for each cell type prior to testing guides will ensure that one can rule out delivery system as a possibility. We find that electroporation of Cas9 alone into the cells, followed by intracellular staining for Cas9, serves as a good method to test electroporation efficiency. In our hands, we find that maximum Cas9 uptake is relative to the maximum editing efficiency possible. For example, we have observed that the maximum amount of intracellular Cas9 in group 1 ILCs following electroporation is about 90%. As such, maximum editing efficiency in group 1 ILCs is 90%. Finally, if the guide and delivery system have been optimized, PCR amplification of the desired region is clean, and editing still fails, there may be issues with the sequencing itself. This is likely due to either nonspecific priming by the sequencing primer of choice or to the guide sequence not being far enough from the sequencing start site. This can be rectified via use of another primer within the genomic region that is capable of sequencing or via simply using the other primer from the initial PCR reaction. In either case, we recommend at least 150 bp before the guide sequence and at least 300 bp after the guide sequence to ensure quality analysis of editing efficiency. Optimization of each step prior to use in experiments will help limit confusion when editing fails.

Ting Zhou: We recommend having a QC for each step of gene editing; e.g., T7 endonuclease I (T7EI) assays can be used for evaluating the efficiency of an sgRNA target, and a positive delivery control (e.g., GFP expression vector) can be used for evaluating the delivery system. A control sgRNA that has previously been confirmed with robust editing efficiency can also be delivered into cells with the same method. If the control sgRNA works, but not the designed sgRNA, it may indicate that the genomics locus is the reason for the failed editing.

How do you define relative levels of chromosome breaks to find the level that is ideal for knockins (<2 kb)?

Ngoc-Tung Tran: In our experience, we normally validate the sgRNA efficiency by T7EI assay. Based on this data, we find the best sgRNA for knockin. It needs to be systematically proved, but if sgRNAs create a broad range of indels, it is likely to be a good one for knockin.

What small-molecule inhibitors have been proven to significantly increase the ratio of HDR to NHEJ repair?

Chun-Hong Chen: We have limited experience with regard to this issue. We suggest the following papers as guides to using NHEJ Inhibitor SCR7 or SCR7 RNAi to increase the ratio of HDR: Li et al., 2017; Singh et al., 2015.

Ngoc-Tung Tran: Inhibition of 53BP1 and inhibition of DNA-PK.

How can I increase the efficiency of CRISPR-based knockin?

Helene Damhofer and Kristian Helin: (1) Choose the sgRNA with the best cutting efficiency and (2) knock in a selectable marker (antibiotic resistance, fluorescent protein) together with your modification to increase recovery of correct integration events.

For me, CRISPR is very inefficient, and my cells keep dying after single-cell sorting. Do you have any advice on how to troubleshoot these issues?

Amandine Caillaud and Bertrand Cariou: For cells dying after single-cell sorting, we recommend keeping, filtering, and freezing conditioned media that may be used later for cell recovering after single-cell sorting.

Vassilis G. Gorgoulis: The viability of single cells sorted by FACS could potentially improve by adjusting several parameters. Specifically, during FACS, the nozzle size should be chosen based on the cell diameter, and the cells should be maintained in the proper buffer (Opti-MEM, PBS, or culture medium) and at the appropriate temperature (usually 4°C) until the time of sorting, in order to lower their metabolic activity.

In the case in which optimization of the above conditions does not increase cell survival, sorting clumps of cells could be exploited. Cell clumps can be serially diluted to obtain single-cell cultures. However, this increases the workload, as screening of a higher number of single-cell-derived colonies may be required to verify successful genome editing. Alternatively, antibiotic selection could be employed instead of FACS if a particular cell line cannot tolerate sorting. This can be achieved by cloning the sgRNAs into a plasmid vector carrying an antibiotic resistance cassette. Single-cell-derived colonies surviving antibiotic selection could be subsequently isolated, propagated, and screened for the desired genomic modification.

Pia Annette Johansson: Here it is very important to outline which part is inefficient; is it (1) getting the different CRISPR components into the cells, or is it (2) the cutting in cells that definitely have received the CRISPR components?

If (1), then try to optimize delivery by using a different method (e.g., lipofection vs. nucleofection) or optimize your current method. Here, it can be useful to make sure that you can see that the components are getting in, so if you are using a Cas9 plasmid, make sure that the plasmid has fluorophore; if you are using ribonucleoprotein (RNP), use a fluorophore-tagged Cas9 or gRNA version.

If it is (2), then it can be that the amount of plasmid/RNP can be optimized or, perhaps more likely, that the gRNA is very inefficient. In that case, try using another gRNA. In regard to single-cell-sorting survival, make sure that you use a slow flow rate, a big nozzle, and a survival molecule, like CloneR2 or Rock Inhibitor.

What is the ideal length of the homology arms for a knockin (<2 kb)? What are the ideal lengths of the homology arms for long knockins (>5 kb)?

Chun-Hong Chen: In Drosophila, homology arms of length 0.5–2 kb can be easily utilized for knockin. In mosquitoes, 0.5–1 kb homology arms are suitable for knockin of a <2 kb donor plasmid. If the donor plasmid is over 5 kb, >1 kb homology arms are more appropriate. For example, in our recent study, we used a donor plasmid size of 2,698 bp; this contained a Pub promoter, eGFP marker, and poly-A fragment SV40. The length of the homology arms for GCTL3 gene knockin in Aedes aegypti was 677 bp and 586 bp for the L and R arms, respectively (Li et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021).

Ngoc-Tung Tran: The length of homology arms for knockin depends on how specific the genome sequence is. If the sequence is specific, 500–1,000 bp is ideal. If there are repetitive sequences in a homology arm, you might want to increase to 1.5–2 kb. For us, 1,000–1,500 bp is the ideal length. For >5 kb, you can increase the length to 2–2.5 kb. However, it depends on how difficult it is to make that homology arm. 2 kb should work for large knockin.

Anne-Catrin Uhlemann and Thomas H. McConville: When we perform knockins of less than 2 kb, we generate upstream and downstream homology arms that are approximately 400 bp. We ensure that the homology arms are at least 200 bp and no more than 500 bp in length. In our experience, below 200 bp, the efficiency of homologous recombination decreases. Regarding the upper limit of the homology arms, they could be engineered to be longer than 500 bp; however, given the overall size of most CRISPR plasmid systems, we avoid addition of unnecessary sequence. We have not performed knockins of greater than 5 kb. It is conceivable that longer fragments require a longer homology, and this could be tried out. In that case, it likely would be best to use two plasmids to keep the plasmid size manageable.

Daniele Novarina and Andreas Milias-Argeitis: Due to high efficiency of homologous recombination in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, for a short knockin (<2 kb), generally 60 bp of homology arms (on either side) are enough. Increasing the length of the homology arms up to ∼500 bp increases the efficiency of genome editing, which is especially desirable for long knockins (>5 kb).

How do long tracts of repetitive DNA sequences in the homology arms affect the efficiency of homology-directed repair?

Ngoc-Tung Tran: Long tracts of repetitive DNA sequences in HA might reduce the HDR efficiency. It also might enhance the off-target random integration of the donor template as well.

Anne-Catrin Uhlemann and Thomas H. McConville: We do not have experience knocking in repetitive DNA sequences. There is likely no single answer or approach to overcome this, and it will depend on the location and type of the repetitive sequence. One could consider modulating reaction temperature to perhaps avoid some of the potential limitations and/or perform sequential rounds of editing.

How can long single-stranded DNA donors for long knockins (>5 kb) be produced?

Xinyu Ling and Tao Liu: Long ssDNA preparation with enzymatic approach cannot achieve length over 5 kb. For that case, it is good to use a transposon system for large fragment knockin.

Youjun Wu and Ting Zhou: A long single-stranded DNA template can be directly synthesized from an underlying dsDNA template by using asymmetric PCR (aPCR). Combined with enzymatic synthesis, aPCR can be used to produce ssDNA greater than 15 kb. The detailed protocol can be found in Veneziano et al., 2018. Alternatively, you can also order commercial products online.

To knock in a selection marker, is it possible to transfect a PCR-amplified fragment containing a promoter and a reporter (e.g., CMV-GFP) along with the plasmid encoding Cas9 and sgRNA?

Joep Beumer and Hans Clevers: Yes, it is possible to knock in a sequence like that, either using HDR when introducing homology arms or otherwise non-homologous repair mechanisms.

Xinyu Ling and Tao Liu: Sure; dsDNA can be used as a donor, but it sometimes has toxicity. It is better to use plasmid encoding for the same gene fragment with sgRNA cutting sites on the top and bottom of the gene sequence (it is also called self-cutting and integrating CRISPR plasmids).

Xiajun Li: We have not tried knockin before in our lab. However, I think it is theoretically possible to use a PCR-amplified fragment for knockin as long as there is no mutation in the selection marker gene after PCR.

Youjun Wu and Ting Zhou: Linearized double-stranded DNA donors have been applied to generate gene knockin mediated by CRISPR-Cas9. The PCR-amplified dsDNA product should contain homologous arms with optimized size and end modifications to increase knockin efficiency (Yu et al., 2019).

For HDR templates to correct SNPs in cell lines, we usually perform silence mutation at a PAM or on a gRNA template to improve efficiency. What is the best way to correct SNPs in an intron, since we cannot do any silence mutation in an intron?

Pia Annette Johansson: Try to find a guide RNA that overlaps the mutation so that it no longer binds perfectly after the correction. You can try more than one guide and screen more clones.

Youjun Wu and Ting Zhou: To induce or correct SNPs in an intron where no silence mutation is involved, you can use prime editing instead of CRISPR-Cas9-mediated HDR. Prime editing is highly versatile and can introduce all types of SNP changes, precise deletion, or insertions at a specific site of a genome without requiring double-strand breaks or donor DNA templates. Prime editing shows higher or similar editing efficiency compared with HDR but much lower off-target editing (Anzalone et al., 2019). Recent studies also showed how to further improve prime editing efficiency. For examples, see Chen et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022.

I need to develop a safe and efficient in vivo delivery system for CRISPR RNPs. Do you have any suggestions?

Xinyu Ling and Tao Liu: Several in vivo delivery systems have been successfully established, such as a VLP- or LNP-based delivery system; maybe you can try them first.

I have two questions: (1) Is it reasonable to conclude that low-proliferative primary cells generally exhibit low CRISPR-Cas9 genome-editing efficiency? (2) What is the minimum number of low-proliferative primary cells recommended for attempting a CRISPR-Cas9 experiment, even if editing efficiency is expected to be low?

Vassilis G. Gorgoulis: (1) Indeed, primary cells and organoid cell cultures derived directly from normal patient tissue generally display a low proliferative rate and low efficiency of CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing. (2) To improve the efficiency of the CRISPR-Cas9 system in primary cells, it is recommended to start with the highest possible number of cells—at least 1 million. In addition, it is recommended that cells display ∼80%–90% confluency on the day of transfection in order to minimize stress and ensure better growth of successfully transfected cells. Antibiotic selection could also improve the efficiency of CRISPR compared to the isolation of single cells via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). To that end, sgRNAs should be cloned in antibiotic resistance cassette-containing vectors and single-cell-derived colonies will be subsequently propagated and screened.

I am having trouble expressing multiple sgRNAs under the control of the same promoter in a bacterium of the genus Pseudomonas (isolated by our lab). Do you have any advice on how to troubleshoot?

Quanjiang Ji: According to our experience and the existing literature, multiple sgRNAs could be expressed normally under the same promoter (J23119, J23100, or other strong promoters) in several Gram-negative bacteria. Maybe you can try expressing multiple sgRNAs under a strong promoter or different promoters in your Pseudomonas sp.

Anne-Catrin Uhlemann and Thomas H. McConville: We do not regularly work with Pseudomonas so would not be able to troubleshoot the expression of multiple sgRNAs. We would consider working with fewer sgRNAs to titrate the maximum number possible as well as consider adjusting the promoter.

In the case of base editing, how do we create sequence changes with CRISPR?

When the editing site overlaps the target sequence, base editing results in the mutation of the target sequence. In this case, the repair fragment consists of a ∼120-bp genomic sequence, centered on the target sequence, carrying the desired mutation for base editing.

When the editing site does not overlap the sgRNA sequence, it is necessary to introduce one (or multiple) synonymous mutation(s) in the target sequence to prevent re-cleavage by Cas9 after repair. In this case, the repair fragment consists of a ∼120-bp genomic sequence containing both the base editing mutation and the synonymous mutation(s) in the target sequence to prevent re-cleavage, designed so that both mutations are positioned at roughly equal distances from the fragment ends. Repair fragments can be produced by annealing fully complementary oligos or by PCR amplification of partially overlapping primers.

For further details about the design of the appropriate sgRNA and repair fragment for base editing in yeast, please refer to our protocol (Novarina et al., 2022).

Youjun Wu and Ting Zhou: Base editors use adenine or cytosine deaminase to generate A-T to G-C or C-G to T-A base pair changes. The deaminase, which is fused to a catalytically dead Cas9 (dCas9) or nickase Cas9, is guided by sgRNAs to the target locus, and the deaminase makes the intended editing within a certain editing window. To conduct base editing, an appropriate base editor needs to be chosen. For the sgRNA design, make sure that the desired edit locates in the editing window of the base editor. Please check the detailed protocol in Huang et al., 2021.
