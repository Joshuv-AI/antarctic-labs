 ABSTRACT

 Reliability, safety, and performance are vital aspects of any nuclear
operation. Fusion technology continues to grow in public, private, and research
interest, and coupled with rapidly growing energy needs, fusion technology
research is poised for fast progress. The development of a Fusion Nuclear
Science Facility (FNSF) is seen as stepping stone for demonstrating long-cycle
fusion. Naturally, such operation requires systems that are available, reliable, and
safe. This work provides a novel demonstration of systems theory coupled with
traditional hazard analysis to provide insights into the risk priority of components
and systems found within the FNSF. The results of this work are a set of
identified hazards that should be considered for the risk-informed design and
development of the FNSF.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 As nuclear technology continues to gather public, private, and research interest alongside rapidly
growing energy needs, fusion technology research is poised for rapid progress. A Fusion Nuclear Science
Facility (FNSF) would be a stepping stone for demonstrating long-cycle fusion with available, reliable,
and safe systems. This report supports the overarching objective of establishing a foundation for
integrated systems and component-level safety analysis for a fusion machine similar in size and power as
FNSF. One key pathway for this objective is the application of reliability, availability, maintainability,
and inspectability (RAMI) and risk analysis concepts to the FNSF design. This report also provides a
preliminary hazard analysis as a basis for RAMI evaluations. Understanding the risks and failure modes
of fusion devices will be useful in licensing and critical to effective design and reliable operation.
 This report’s overall objective is to provide an assessment of FNSF-specific hazards, insomuch as
those design details are available, advancing the goal of real-world demonstrations of fusion energy.
Hazard analysis, even at preliminary stages, is critical to process developments that ensure safe and
reliable outcomes.

 1.1. System Description
 The FNSF is a fusion machine based on a tokamak design. A tokamak employs powerful magnetic
fields to confine plasma in the shape of a torus, enabling necessary conditions to facilitate fusion with
long confinement times [1]. See Figure 1 below.

Figure 1. Torus [2].

 A tokamak generates heat through fusion, which can be employed in the same manner as a fission
reactor or to drive a steam power cycle, or something similar. The goal of fusion research around the
world is to move the technological capabilities of fusion to support economically competitive commercial
power generation. A schematic of a fusion plant is shown below in Figure 2, with the primary
components indicated. This work performs preliminary hazard analysis systems of the tokamak machine,
including the magnet, divertor, fueling, cooling, electrical, shutdown, heating, and vacuum systems. The
details of each system are described later in Section 3.

 1.1.1. FNSF reference design
 The FNSF is designed to be a technical bridge between the research capabilities of the International
Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER) and the demonstration activities of the DEMOnstration
power plant (DEMO) [4]. Details of the FNSF design can be found in Table 1. According to Kessel et al.
[4], the missions of the FNSF are:
 1. Demonstrate high neutron exposure (plant level)
 2. Advance materials technologies: Radiation resistant materials, temperatures resistance,
 activation resistance, chemical resistance, and plasma resistance
 3. Operate at power plant relevant fusion environment conditions (e.g., temperatures, breeding
 rates, pressures)
 4. Produce tritium at a level equal to, or exceeding consumption
 5. Demonstrate safe, effective, and accountable tritium handling
 6. Routinely demonstrate long plasma operations
 7. Advance and enable technologies for long plasma duration
 8. Demonstrate safety capabilities (e.g., environmental aspect, tritium leakage mitigation)
 9. Develop power plant relevant subsystems (heating, pumping, heat exchangers [HXs], purity
 control, cryogenics)
 10. Advance the availability through, e.g., reliability and efficiency.
 The FNSF is expected to add significant and critical information to materials and design databases by
serving as a key fusion and materials test platform [4]. Configurations of FNSF vary based on the
differences in neutron fluence, magnet technology, electricity production, maintenance approaches,
tritium breeding ration, divertor technology, and years of operations [5] [6]. However, long plasma and
high duty cycles are a mainstay of these configurations. A key consideration in selecting the FNSF design
is that, although it is only a pilot-scale design, it shares characteristics with some modern commercial
designs but is an open design free of proprietary concerns.

 1.2. Literature Review
 To establish a standardized vocabulary, several common terminologies are defined before
summarizing the literature review findings. The terminologies include risk, hazard analysis, RAMI
analysis, and probabilistic risk assessment (PRA).
 Risk can be defined in a variety of ways. In nuclear power domain, a common risk definition is the
one adopted by the United States Nuclear Regulatory Commission [9], asking the following questions: (1)
What can go wrong? (2) How likely is it that something will go wrong? (3) What are the consequences?
(4) What performance is needed? The first question can be answered by performing hazard analyses using
qualitative or semi-quantitative approaches. The second question can be answered by performing RAMI
analyses quantifying a broad set of metrics, including probability of failure, failure rate, availability, mean
time between failure, and mean time to repair. Hazard analysis and RAMI analysis commonly appear as
an integrated study, where RAMI analysis provides quantitative basis to determine a failure mode’s level
of occurrence likelihood. Risk can also be assessed using a variety of methods, one of which is the PRA
methodology. PRA is a comprehensive, quantitative methodology used to estimate risk by answering the
first three questions. PRA often uses both hazard analysis and RAMI analysis as input models to identify
initiating events and quantifying basic events, respectively. PRA was first developed in late 1970s to
analyze nuclear fission power plant risks and was soon piloted for nuclear fusion risk assessment in 1983
for the Tritium Systems Test Assembly at the Los Alamos National Laboratory [10].
 The scope of literature review is set to focus on hazard analysis for nuclear fusion or tritium facilities
and systems. If an existing study covers hazard analysis, it will be included in the review, independent of
whether RAMI analysis or PRA assessment is performed. If an existing study only covers RAMI analysis
without performing hazard analysis, it will be excluded from the review for this report and will be kept
for review in the authors’ next report focusing on fusion RAMI. Using the two screening criteria, the
authors located and analyzed over a hundred and sixty publications between 1977 and 2026 and narrowed
down to over fifty publications for detailed review.

 Fusion Engineering Test Reactor (CFETR) [21]. Hazard analyses were also performed for earlier
 designs including the JET [22] and the Next European Torus (NET) [23] [24].
 - A wide variety of systems were analyzed for hazards. The blanket system is the most analyzed
 system given its important dual functions of cooling and breeding (e.g., [25] [26] [27]), and the

 Among the reviewed studies of fusion and tritium hazard analyses, most studies adopted the Failure
Mode, Effects, and Criticality Analysis (FMECA) approach. The FMECA approach can be understood as
an extension of the Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) approach. FMEA identifies failure modes
and performs a qualitative ranking, while FMECA additionally includes a semi-quantitative criticality
analysis to evaluate failure modes based on their occurrence likelihood and impact severity. A limited
number of existing studies used other hazard analysis approaches such as the Hazard and Operability
(HAZOP) [12] [36].
 The observed dominant use of FMECA is likely driven by the ITER RAMI Analysis Program, which
serves as the methodological basis for many reviewed fusion and tritium hazard analysis [37]. Using
FMECA, the ITER RAMI Analysis Program formulates criticality (C) as the product of severity (S) and
occurrence (O), each of which is broken down into six levels as shown in Table 2 and Table 3. The risk
level of a failure mode is ranked as major risk given a higher-than-13 criticality score, medium risk given
a criticality score between 7 and 13, and minor risk if the criticality is less than 7. The criticality matrix is
shown in Figure 6.
 Most reviewed FMECA studies adopted the six-by-six, severity times occurrence formulation for
criticality according to the ITER RAMI Analysis Program. A few other studies used different
formulations, including Pinna and Dongiovanni formulating risk priority number (RPN) as the product of
severity, occurrence, and detection using a six-by-five-by-five matrix [38] and Chang and Lee
formulating criticality as the product of severity, occurrence, and detection using a six-by-six-by-five
matrix [39].

 1.3. Research Contributions
 Compared to the existing fusion and tritium hazard analyses, this research makes methodological
contributions in identifying and ranking failure modes from an integrated use of FMEA and the Systems-
Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA).
 - The existing studies mostly rely on FMECA/FMEA only to perform a full analysis, while this
 research uses FMEA to identify failure modes, assess the levels of occurrence likelihood and
 failure detection, connects FMEA results to STPA via unsafety control actions (UCAs), and
 propagates through STPA process to determine consequence severity for each failure mode.
 - The existing studies mostly consider operation interruption as the sole metric for severity, while
 this research considers a broader scope of consequence including the impacts on workers, facility,
 public, and environment.
 - This research also makes a practical contribution in performing hazard analysis for the FNSF
 design, which has not been examined for hazards in the past.

 2. METHODOLOGY
 This work will investigate hazards of the FNSF by relying on two approaches, FMEA and STPA.
FMEA, a bottom-up approach, considers the individual components of a system and discusses their
failure modes and effects. It is ubiquitous in the field of hazards analysis, from the automotive to
aerospace industries. This approach is useful in the identification of hazards specific to individual
components. The idea of bottom-up refers to identifying individual elements of a system and how their
behaviors (e.g., failures) at a localized level propagate to larger impacts until they ultimately impact a top
item, function, or system of interest. The second approach, STPA, is top-down and relies on systems
theory to support the hazard analysis. In this analysis, the top-down approach is employed to complement
the FMEA.

 7

 2.1. Integrated Use of FMEA and STPA
 From Modarres [40], FMEA examines systems functions, subsystems, or components and assumes a
failure has occurred. For each failure, a failure mode (i.e., the manner of the failure of a function,
subsystem, component, or part) is identified along with the effects and potential causes of the given
failure. The importance of each failure and its effects are also evaluated, sometimes through an RPN. The
RPN is typically based on three factors: the probability of a failure occurrence, the severity of its effects,
and its detectability. There are different ways of assessing occurrence, severity, and detectability, but
these are often identified by engineering or expert judgment. Table 4 shows an example of an FMEA
table, which is the primary output of an FMEA-based hazard analysis. Note the RPN, a product of
severity, occurrence, and detection, provides a direct metric for comparison of all failures identified by
the FMEA.
 Severity provides a qualitative indication of the potential effect resulting from the failure modes.
Occurrence is defined as the likelihood that a specific event might happen. Detection assesses the ability
with which actions can be taken to identify a potential cause/mechanism or failure mode. Depending on
the industry, organization, or even risk analyst preference, the numerics of the RPN and their basis may
change. For example, severity could be assigned either three levels (e.g., low, medium, high) or four, as
shown by Table 5. Ultimately, there is flexibility in these assignments, but consistency is needed in their
application.

 Contrary to occurrence and detection, which are largely subjective unless supported by supplemental
performance data, the measure of severity can be significantly improved through the systematic linking
between bottom level components to top levels of the system. This linking is accomplished through a top-
down, systems-theory approach. This work proposes to leverage STPA as a means of improving the
linking between components and their failure effects for the purposes of improved qualitative severity
evaluation within the FMEA framework.

 8

 STPA is a top-down approach for system hazards. In contrast to bottom-up approaches, STPA works
by considering how system-level functions may go wrong through failures, incorrect actions, or
undesirable behaviors [42]. Its top-down approach begins with defining system-level accidents and
hazards, then analyzes a control structure for unsafe or undesirable behavior that can occur. A key output
of the analysis is a collection of UCAs. Employers of STPA assess UCAs for causal factors that make up
scenarios leading to UCAs and, under worst case conditions, may lead to significant losses. STPA
emphasizes a system view rather than a component view and shifts the hazard analysis point of view from
identifying which component can fail and how, to identifying how unsafe behavior will occur. A key
benefit of STPA is its application of systems-theory principles, making it effective for identifying the
emergent behaviors of systems [42]. STPA can serve well for risk analysis of systems that are evolving,
under development, or otherwise changing, like the FNSF. Very little work has been done on using STPA
for nuclear fusion machine hazard analysis. The most significant effort showing the use of STPA was that
of Patrick White, for his PhD dissertation in 2021; his primary focus was on licensing and regulation for
commercial fusion [43]
 The first integration of STPA and FMEA was performed in 2019 [44] and builds upon the similar
concepts shared by both methods (see Figure 7). Their approach employed STPA to define accidents and
hazards, in particular the UCAs, and incorporate them with the FMEA template as failure modes. By
integrating STPA aspects into the FMEA table, the work aimed to add quantification or ranking to the
STPA results. At that time STPA was a new methodology and so some of the concepts and terminology
employed are slightly different here (e.g., accidents were used to describe what are now widely called
losses).

Figure 7. Correspondence between concepts of STPA and FMEA for the same level of system abstraction
(adapted from [45]).

 In 2020, a second publication [45] integrated STPA with the FMEA template. While nearly identical,
the second publication provides additional detail for the cause identification in the FMEA template. In
this case, causes of UCAs are identified by following STPA concepts (i.e., looking at the control
algorithm and process model). This is expanded part of the discussion compared to the 2019 work. The
identified causes are indicated as part of the FMEA table.

 9

 In 2023, a consumer products risk framework was developed that uses the concepts of unsafe
behaviors and the control structure assessment from STPA [46]. That work relies on qualitative scoring
concepts of severity, occurrence, and detection, like other conventional FMEA. Their innovation is in
their complex framework and its application for consumer products.
 In 2024, an additional group [47] combined aspects of the FMEA and STPA into a Bayesian network
for performing software risk analysis. Nodes within the Bayesian network represent failures, causes, and
UCAs. The network provides a means of evaluating relationships between failures and causes and
offering risk insights. The work only provided a demonstration and set up of the network. They did not
provide the necessary details for quantification. Ultimately STPA and FMEA can support each other in
different ways, as has been demonstrated in prior work.
 In the present study, we are employing STPA and FMEA in a manner most similar to the 2019 and
2020 publications. STPA will help provide support for the FMEA. The chief difference lies in the layer of
abstraction considered when employing each method. Rather than employing STPA and FMEA at the
same layer of abstraction, as was shown in Figure 7, this work employs a relationship shown by Figure 8.
In Figure 8, the top impact or effects identified by FMEA are linked to Causal factors and UCAs from
STPA. In other words, FMEA is used to provide the details for where and how UCAs or other unsafe
behaviors of the system can emerge. FMEA is a causal analysis for STPA. The advantage here is that
STPA provides the linking and information necessary to provide a comprehensive and distinct picture of
the severity associated with each failure identified by the FMEA. Figure 9 shows the general theory of the
proposed deployment of FMEA-STPA for this work; STPA provides an indication of the consequences of
failures modes, while FMEA identifies their occurrence likelihood and the detection methods employed
that can reduce or mitigate them. Together, they provide comprehensive evaluation of risk.

 2.2. Scoring Rubrics
 The chief novelty of this combination of STPA-FMEA analysis is the structured assessment of
severity. The following is a discussion on how this is accomplished. From the STPA-FMEA analysis, the
following sets have been defined:

 1. 𝐿 is the set of losses identified for a given system, and 𝑙𝑖 is a specific loss from the set.
 2. 𝐻 is the set of hazards identified for a given system, and ℎ𝑗 is a specific hazard from the set.
 3. 𝑈 is the set of UCAs identified for a given system, and 𝑢𝑘 is a specific UCA from the set.
 4. 𝐹 is the set of failures identified from the FMEA analysis, and 𝐹𝑛 is a specific failure from the set.

 The severity assessment for each failure tracked by the FMEA tables is accomplished through an

 For occurrence, this analysis employs three levels with scores 1, 2, and 3, from least probable
occurrence to most probable occurrence. A failure mode is assigned 1 if its occurrence rate is below 0.005
per year; assigned 2 if its occurrence rate is between 0.005 and 0.5 per year; or assigned 3 if its
occurrence rate is greater than 0.5 per year. The fusion component failure rate database (FCFRDB) [48],
which was developed in the framework of an International Energy Agency agreement, is used as the main
basis for this analysis to determine the occurrence rates of the failure modes. If not found yet in the
FCFRDB, the occurrence rate is then assumed.
 For detection, this analysis employs three levels with scores 1, 2, and 3, from most-sufficient
detection to least-sufficient detection. A failure mode is assigned 1 if there are more than three ways of
monitoring and controls to prevent or detect failure before it happens; assigned 2 if there are only one or
two ways of monitoring and controls; assigned 3 if there are no monitoring or control mechanisms.

 2.3. Methodological Procedure
 The methodology in a simple description is as follows:
 1. Perform STPA to evaluate the system from the top down, identifying
 a. System losses and assigning severity to each loss
 b. System hazards and links to losses
 c. UCAs and their links to hazards
 d. Causal factors (and defined by the FMEA)
 2. Perform FMEA to evaluate the system from the bottom up, focusing on:
 a. System components sub-ordinate to the primary system evaluated by STPA
 b. Failure modes
 c. Failure effects
 d. Failure causes
 e. Failure occurrence

 12

 f. Failure detection/prevention
 3. Link UCAs to each specific failure identified by the FMEA
 4. Calculate the severity score from STPA
 5. Evaluate the FMEA RPN.

 3.1. Top-Down Analysis
 The top-down approach begins with the STPA assessment of the FNSF. This work will integrate a
severity score as a novel approach for STPA-FMEA combined hazard analysis. This section will cover
the identification of system losses and their severity, the system hazards and their links to losses and
severity, and finally the identification of UCAs and their links to hazards and losses. Several definitions
are provided from the STPA manual for clarification of subsequent efforts [42]:
 - A loss involves something of value to stakeholders. Losses may include a loss of human life or
 human injury, property damage, environmental pollution, loss of mission, loss of reputation, loss
 or leak of sensitive information, or any other loss that is unacceptable to the stakeholders.
 - A hazard is a system state or set of conditions that, together with a particular set of worst-case
 environmental conditions, will lead to a loss.
 - A system is a set of components that act together as a whole to achieve some common goal,
 objective, or end. A system may contain subsystems and may also be part of a larger system.
 - A system-level constraint specifies system conditions or behaviors that need to be satisfied to
 prevent hazards (and ultimately prevent losses).
 - A UCA is an action that, in a particular context and worst-case environment, will lead to a hazard.

 3.1.3. Identification of UCAs
 The identification of UCAs typically relies on a clear indication of the controllers, controlled
processes, feedback and relationships of a system. These details are captured by a control structure
diagram. Figure 10 shows the control relationships of a portion of the FNSF. Note that this work only
covers the magnet, divertor, blanket, fueling, cooling, electrical, shutdown, heating, and vacuum systems
of the FNSF. The primary functions of each system shown in Figure 10 are discussed in the subsequent

 16

paragraphs. All the following systems, except for blanket system, are included within the scope of this
report. Work is ongoing with Oak Ridge National Laboratory to extend this analysis to the blanket
system. We look forward to integrating this methodology with them within that system in a follow-on
publication.
    -   Magnet System: Magnet details are found in [50], [51], [52], [55], [56], [3], and [70]. A tokamak
 is a device that confines plasma in the shape of a donut or torus by relying on magnetic fields
 generated by superconducting poloidal and toroidal magnet coils [1], [3]. The magnetic system
 controls the powerful superconducting magnets necessary for supporting fusion, allowing the
 tokamak to confine, shape, and control and heat the plasma with the vacuum vessel. The goal of
 the magnet system is to support successful fusion by enabling high temperatures, particle
 velocities and confinement times [50].
 - Divertor System: Divertor details are found in [63], [3], and [70]. During fusion reactions, alpha
 particles and other impurities are generated. To keep the plasma purity at the desired level, there
 needs to be a pathway for removing impurities. The divertor plays a central role enabling
 impurities to exhaust from the vacuum vessel. The divertor also plays a heat extraction role [3].
 - Blanket System: Blanket system details are found [57], [3], and [70]. Fuel for the fusion reactors
 involves tritium and deuterium. While deuterium is abundant, and can be extracted from
 seawater, tritium is rare in nature due to its short half-life of 12.3 years [3]. Fortunately, tritium
 can be bred from lithium, offering a pathway for fusion reactors to produce or breed additional
 tritium for operation. The blanket serves the role of housing lithium and enabling the production
 of tritium. The FNSF design employs a lead-lithium (PbLi) liquid blanket design. This enables
 the blanket to function as a breeder and primary mechanism for extracting thermal energy from
 the reactor [6].
 - Fueling System: Fueling system details are found in [70]. The role of the fueling system is to
 provide fuel directly to the tokamak vacuum vessel for the purpose of sustaining the nuclear
 fusion reaction and the correct plasma fuel ratios. It is essential that the fueling system store,
 prepare, and inject fuel under the right conditions to ensure stable plasma and fusion operations.
 - Cooling System: Cooling system details are found in [57] and [70]. Fusion reactions generate
 significant amounts of thermal energy. To manage this energy, the cooling system is configured
 to extract heat from critical components of the tokamak, including the divertor, and the blanket.
 The FNSF cooling system is a helium-based system on the secondary side. While the primary
 side involves both helium and PbLi [57].
 - Shutdown System: Shutdown system details are found in [54] and [70]. To ensure safety of
 equipment, personnel, public, and the environment, as well as to ensure that the research
 operations can be controlled, the FNSF will have a shutdown system. Fusion can be disrupted and
 shut down by different means, including reduction in magnetic confinement, or the introduction
 of impurities.
 - Heating System: Heating system details are discussed in [65], [3], and [70]. Nuclear fusion
 requires significant temperatures. Plasma that is formed within the tokamak vacuum vessel must
 be heated to reach the necessary temperatures for fusion. This process is controlled by ohmic
 heating, or heating from ion or electron cyclotrons [3], [70].
 - Vacuum System: Vacuum system details are found in [53], [3], and [70]. The vacuum system
 enables the formation of plasma and serves to remove exhaust gases from the reactor. The
 vacuum system also enables the establishment of a cryostat and helps enable the functionality of
 the superconducting materials by providing thermal isolation [3].

 - Electrical System: The electrical system provides the power to support fusion research and
 operations. Without a safe and reliable electrical system, the operation of a fusion machine will
 be tenuous at best. Some functions of electrical equipment for fusion systems are identified in

 4.2. Severity-Occurrence Results

modes. Frequency-consequence (F-C) curves are employed within the nuclear industry to support the
evaluation of licensing basis events [72]. F-C curves offer a pathway for optimization and prioritization of
design efforts. In this work, severity-occurrence figures, analogous to the F-C curves, offer insights for
design and risk analysis. The severity-occurrence figures mapping each of the 455 failure modes could
directly aid risk-informed discussions. Within each severity-occurrence figure, the greater magnitude
distance from the origin, the greater the risk of the given failure mode. For example, in Figure 19, failure
modes M21 and M47 represent high severity, and medium occurrence likelihood. Such modes may be
targeted by designers for reduction, mitigation, or elimination, as well as development of more robust
methods for detection, monitoring, and controls.

 5. DISCUSSIONS
 5.1. Analytical Takeaways
 Some of the takeaways from the analysis are given here, starting with the RPN scores. The electrical
system takes the top spot for RPN values in Table 13. This is, in part, for two reasons. First, the electrical
system is connected to all other systems, see Figure 10, making it somewhat responsible for all activities
at the FNSF. Like a conventional fission plant, a loss of offsite power is a serious event as it impacts all
operations at the plant. This is compounded by a medium occurrence rate (i.e., the second reason for its
high RPN).
 The next most significant set of failure modes according to RPN come from the cooling system. Heat
exchangers, necessary for removing heat from primary equipment (e.g., the blanket), are key components
for ensuring design temperatures are maintained. Leaks also represent a significant concern for their
potential radiological and chemical impacts. Leaks will impact the efficiency for heat transfer and
component temperatures will rise. Also, as discussed in [57], the heat transfer fluid is designed to flow
within coaxial piping. The intent is for the cold leg to encompass the hot leg which enables greater tritium
retention. If heat transfer is lost, tritium can more easily migrate across materials due to their increased
temperature.
 The shutdown system is the next system to appear in Table 13. The shutdown system is vital for safe
fusion plant operations. In this analysis, shutdown is not simply termination of plasma conditions, rather
it is enabling the necessary conditions for controlled and gradual decrease in plasma conditions. This
approach emphasizes protecting equipment and maintaining the longevity of plasma facing components.

 Figure 11 through Figure 18 show each system, along with the number of high, medium, and low
RPN counts. These figures are useful for assessing each system individually. For example, the vacuum
system has a larger number of low and medium RPN failure modes than high RPN failure modes. In
contrast, the electrical system has only high RPN failure modes. Figure 27 offers a direct comparison of
the number of high RPNs found for each system. From Figure 27 it can be clearly seen that magnet
system has the highest number of high RPN scores. In order of high RPN numbers, the magnet system is
followed by the shutdown, cooling, heating, electrical, vacuum, divertor, and finally the fueling system.
These numbers however may be misleading with respect to most important or “high risk” system. Likely,
Figure 27 is being influenced by those systems that are both safety significant and have the most failure
modes classified in this study, given that both the magnet system and the shutdown system have over 100
modes each. To offer a contrasting assessment, the average high RPN score for each is presented in
system Figure 28. There are significant differences between these two Figures. The magnet system, which
dominated in Figure 27 is now near the bottom score in Figure 28. Now, the electrical system is the top
followed by the cooling, fueling, divertor, shutdown, magnet, heating, and vacuum systems. Again, the
number of failure modes identified has an impact on ordering. An explanation is offered by considering
that a failure at the system level (i.e., a failure involving a group of components) generally produces
larger consequences than the failure of a single component. This is effectively what is happening with the
electrical system results and explains why its RPNs occupy the top spot in Table 13 as well as Figure 28.
In this instance, the electrical system has only be subdivided into ten failure modes, so naturally each
mode linked to multiple UCAs, resulting in large severity scores and ultimately a high RPN. The results
offered by Table 13, Figure 27 and Figure 28 provide insights that allow an analyst to cross-compare
systems. However, the more useful comparison of RPNs is likely best left for intra-system analysis,
reducing the artificial inflation or reduction of risk based on how a system is subdivided
 The intra-system analysis is enabled by the severity-occurrence figures (i.e., Figure 19 through Figure
26) which offer a beneficial cross comparison with the results from Table 14. The severity-occurrence
figures map each of the 455 failure modes directly for the analyst to engage in risk-informed discussions.
Figure 19 shows the highest severity failure modes are M21, M47, M73, and M99 (cross reference Table
A-2.1.1), which also happen to be the highest four RPNs from the magnet system. The fifth place high
RPN score for the magnet system is M10 (cross reference Table A-2.1.1). Note that the severity and
occurrence values place M10 in the upper middle of Figure 19, highlighting how risk significant failure
modes are not always those with the largest potential severity.
 Contrary to the magnet system results in Table 14, the top five severity scores of the cooling,
electrical, fueling and divertor systems all coincide with their top five RPNs. The vacuum system failure
modes, V37 and V26 are top RPNs (cross reference Table A-2.3.1), but fall below the top severity scores,
with V26 sitting near the middle of severity scores show in Figure 21. For the shutdown system, S34,
S35, and S41 (cross reference Table A-2.6.1) are in the upper portion of Figure 24, but still fall below the
top severity scores. Finally, the heating system failure modes H10, H11, H12, H18 (cross reference Table
A-2.5.1), like those of the shutdown system, are in the upper portion of severity scores but still below the
top five shown in Figure 23. Noting these differences between severity and RPN highlight how, within
the methodology of this project, risk is multifaceted—reducing occurrence or mitigating severity can have
significant implications for a system.

 5.2. Methodological Takeaways
 This analysis employed three levels for detection. The level of detection for each failure mode is
assigned based on the number of monitoring or controls to prevent or detect failure before it happens. As
such, without further guidance, the score assigned for level of detection can vary too easily from one risk
analyst to another. This has the potential to shift the RPN of a given mode drastically from a high level to
that of a medium or low level. For example, failure mode V26 (cross reference Table A-2.3.1) from the
vacuum system is a leak from the torus vacuum vessel valves. This particular failure mode has
severity = 31, occurrence = 2, and detection = 2. With additional detection or prevention methods, the
detection can be reduced and RPN would be reduced to 62, a medium level RPN. It is worth noting that a
complete and in-depth assessment of detection was not the primary focus of this work. Future efforts may
refine or increase the level of rigor for assessing detection levels.

 39

 The dependencies between failure modes, SSCs, and context can be captured by UCAs. This can
provide a researcher or risk analyst or general user of this approach with insights to the risk perspective of
the system under review. In conventional STPA, the analyst needs to determine the causal factors that
may lead to a UCA. This process can be challenging and the FMEA approach offers a clear and structured
approach for identifying these connections.
 The dependency between failure modes and UCAs is not always clear. For example, a failure mode-
UCA link may be obvious in one case, but less direct or clear in another. Each UCA is technically
described for a specific context and, ideally, the UCA list would be updated to capture specific contextual
details for each linked failure mode. This may tend to create ballooning list of UCAs.
 A potential solution to be investigated in future work is the idea of indirect and direct UCA linking.
The context descriptions in each UCA are clearly and directly related to some failure modes and
indirectly related to others. Future research may incorporate factors that account for the dependency
strength or level and simplify the linking of UCAs to each failure mode.

 6. CONCLUSION
 The FNSF is to be a stepping stone for demonstrating long-cycle fusion with available, reliable, and
safe systems. In its efforts to promote the advancement of the fusion technologies, this work implemented
STPA and FMEA in a novel way for the FNSF hazard analysis. This study emphasized eight primary
systems of the tokamak-based fusion facility. FNSF-specific references were employed when available,
and the rest of the hazard analysis was based on information pertaining generally to tokamak-based
designs. The hazard analysis identified and evaluated 455 distinct failures (e.g., component and failure
mode pairs). Each failure mode was assessed for its severity, likelihood of occurrence, and the methods
that may detect or mitigate the potential of such failure modes. For a holistic view of the importance or
potential impact of each failure mode, the assessment relied on RPNs, the product of severity, occurrence,
and detection. The top systems regarding RPN scores were the electrical and cooling systems.
 Future work will iterate on the scoring and employ consensus scoring approaches. As such, future
results will likely vary from the present results in some areas, especially if additional credit is taken for
preventative defense concepts when addressing detection. Also, as FNSF-specific design decisions are
made, the hazard analysis results are again expected to vary. The present results offer a starting place for
in-depth analysis of each of the eight systems. Notably absent is a detailed analysis of the FNSF’s I&C
systems. While obviously unavailable, the I&C systems are critical for controlling the complex and
interconnected physics across various systems of the fusion facility. The present results offer insights into
where control systems should focus design and control efforts. By leveraging the data presented by the
severity-occurrence figures, analysts and designers can determine which failure modes to prioritize and
how to prioritize their efforts, such as through occurrence reduction, or by design-mitigative or defensive
features.
