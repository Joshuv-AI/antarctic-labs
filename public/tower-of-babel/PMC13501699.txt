A decoy virus strategy using plant-produced SARS-CoV-2 virus-like-particles to inhibit infection and lung injury
Authors: Jady Liang, Yu Onodera, Yuchong Li, Jianfeng Wu, Sarah McColman, David T. Cramb, Julie Khang, Aye Aye Khine, Ya-Wen Chen, Ori D. Rotstein, Samira Mubareka, Arthur S. Slutsky, Haibo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rapid emergence of highly transmissible and immune-evasive SARS-CoV-2 variants, including Omicron lineage strains, has reduced the effectiveness of existing vaccines and antiviral therapies. Safe and rapidly deployable biologics capable of limiting viral entry and attenuating early inflammatory responses remains needed, particularly for high-risk exposure settings.MethodsWe evaluated engineered non-replicating SARS-CoV-2 virus-like particles (VLPs), which structurally mimic the native virus, for antiviral and immunomodulatory effects in human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)–derived lung organoids and K18-hACE2 transgenic mice. Cytopathic effects, viral RNA levels, cytokine expression, and lung pathology were quantified, while biodistribution and acute safety were assessed by fluorescence imaging and inflammatory profiling.ResultsPre-exposure of lung organoids to VLPs attenuated SARS-CoV-2–induced cytopathic injury, reduced viral replication and IL-6 expression, and preserved epithelial integrity. Intranasal administration in K18-human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) mice achieved uniform pulmonary distribution without acute inflammatory responses. A single pre-exposure prophylaxis dose of high-titer VLPs reduced viral burden and IL-6 levels, attenuated lung injury, and improved clinical parameters. Fluorescent VLPs co-localized with ACE2 and underwent cellular uptake in Calu-3 cells, supporting a competitive decoy mechanism, while preservation of ACE2 expression in vivo suggested reduced virus-induced ACE2 downregulation.ConclusionsSARS-CoV-2 VLPs function as safe, non-replicating biologics that limit viral entry and attenuate downstream inflammation under pre-exposure conditions. Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has profoundly impacted global health and exposed critical vulnerabilities in healthcare systems. At the same time, it has accelerated advances in virology, immunology, and therapeutic development. SARS-CoV-2 is an enveloped, positive-sense RNA virus encoding four structural proteins: spike (S), membrane (M), envelope (E), and nucleocapsid (N), along with numerous non-structural and accessory proteins that facilitate replication and immune evasion [1].

Despite major progress with antiviral drugs (e.g., remdesivir, nirmatrelvir, molnupiravir), monoclonal antibodies, and multiple vaccine platforms (mRNA, protein subunit, viral vector, and inactivated virus) [2–5], the continual emergence of antigenically distinct variants has limited the durability and breadth of protection. Variants in the Omicron lineage, particularly JN.1 and its subvariants such as KP.2 and KP.3 carry convergent receptor-binding domain (RBD) mutations (F456L, Q493E) that enhances ACE2 binding and promote immune escape [6–9], reducing neutralization by infection- or vaccination-elicited antibodies [9–11]. Furthermore, the dominance of IGHV3-53/3–66-class public antibodies after vaccination and breakthrough infection may restrict immune repertoire diversity, limiting adaptive response to drifted strains [12]. These developments highlight the need for complementary strategies that remain effective despite ongoing viral evolution.

Virus-like particles (VLPs) represent a promising next-generation platform. They structurally resemble authentic virions but lack genetic material, eliminating replicative capacity [13]. Their multivariant antigen display promotes strong B-cell and T-cell receptor responses [14–19], and like mRNA vaccines, VLPs can be rapidly redesigned to match emerging variants [20, 21]. Clinical-stage candidates such as GBP510 (SKYCovione) [22–25] and CoVLP (COVIFENZ) [26–30], have shown robust immunogenicity and cross-neutralization against diverse variants [14, 31, 32], highlighting their translational potential [24, 28, 33]. Plant-derived VLPs, produced in Nicotiana benthamiana, offer additional advantages, including rapid scalability, low risk of human pathogen contamination, and cost-effective production without strict cold-chain requirements [34]. These features make them particularly well suited for rapid pandemic response and deployment in resource-limited settings.

Beyond vaccination, VLPs are increasingly used in diagnostics and virology research [35–38], and as scaffolds for therapeutic innovation, including antiviral drug screening and immune decoys [39–41]. In this study, we investigated intranasally delivered SARS-CoV-2 VLPs as a novel decoy-based prophylactic strategy. We hypothesized that a single intranasal dose of VLPs, structurally mimicking SARS-CoV-2 but devoid of genetic material, could competitively inhibit viral engagement of ACE2 at the airway mucosa and thereby attenuate early infection. Such an approach offers a rapid, non-immunogenic, and scalable means to reduce infection severity, particularly against emerging immune-evasive variants.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Vero E6 cells, derived from African green monkey kidney epithelial cells and obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CRL-1586), were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco™, 11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, F0926) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Corning, 30–002-CI). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Calu-3 human lung epithelial cells (ATCC, HTB-55) were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium Eagle alpha modification (α-MEM; Wisent Biologicals, 310–010-CL), supplemented with 10% FBS (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, F0926) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Millipore-Sigma, A5955). Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

The cells were grown to approximately 90% confluence and dissociated from the cell culture surface for the next passage of cell culture or preparation for the respective experiments. At indicated confluency, the medium was removed, and cells were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Sigma, D8537), followed by cell dissociation using TrypLE™ Express Enzyme (1X) (Gibco, 12604013) at 37 °C for 5 min. The cell suspension was neutralized with an equal volume of complete media, centrifuged at 500 × g for 3 min at room temperature, and resuspended in fresh media. For the routine cell culture, the cells were passaged at 60–80% confluency.

Human lung organoids were generated using a multi-stage directed differentiation protocol starting from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs; HDF-mRNA line), a kind gift from Drs. Snoeck and Chen [42]. Briefly, undifferentiated iPSCs will be directed toward the definitive endoderm lineage through treatment with high concentrations of Activin A. The resulting embryoid bodies, committed to the endodermal fate, will then be specified into anterior foregut endoderm by sequential inhibition of the TGF beta and BMP signaling pathways. Subsequent ventralization will guide endodermal cells toward a lung progenitor identity. These adherent cells will be transitioned to three-dimensional suspension culture and exposed to a combination of signaling cues including the Wnt pathway activator CHIR 99021, fibroblast growth factors, and retinoic acid to promote differentiation toward the lung epithelial lineage.

Biosafety

All procedures involving infectious SARS-CoV-2 were conducted within the Combined Containment Level 3 (C-CL3) facility operated by the Temerty Faculty of Medicine, University of Toronto. Experiments adhered strictly to institutional biosafety guidelines, and all personnel were appropriately trained and certified to perform work at biosafety level 3.

SARS-CoV-2 strain

The SARS-CoV-2 strain used in this study was the SB2 isolate, previously characterized and reported by our group [43].

SARS-CoV-2 virus-like-particles

SARS-CoV-2 virus-like particles (VLPs) used in this study were kindly supplied by Medicago Inc. (Quebec, Canada) and were generated using a plant-based expression system derived from Nicotiana benthamiana [26, 29]. Specifically, the full-length spike (S) glycoprotein from the hCoV-19/USA/CA2/2020 strain (GISAID accession EPI_ISL_406036; nucleotides 21,563– 25,384) was transiently produced in N. benthamiana via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated infiltration [26]. The expression construct included R667G, R668S, and R670S mutations at the S1/S2 cleavage site, and K971P/V972P substitutions to stabilize the prefusion conformation [28]. The native signal peptide was replaced with that of alfalfa protein disulfide isomerase, and the transmembrane and cytoplasmic regions were substituted with those from influenza H5 hemagglutinin (A/Indonesia/5/2005) to enhance VLP formation [28]. Expression was regulated by a dual 35S promoter with proprietary untranslated regions (UTRs), and co-expression of the Tombusvirus P19 suppressor improved transient protein accumulation [26, 28, 29].

After six days of incubation, infiltrated plant tissues were harvested, and VLPs were purified using Medicago’s proprietary extraction method, followed by filtration and chromatography, as previously described for influenza VLPs [26, 28, 29]. The final product consisted of VLPs displaying trimeric spike proteins in a self-assembled structure and was formulated in PBS using Agrobacterium vector #8671 at a total concentration of 272 µg/mL, with 77% purity and a VLP content of 209 µg/mL.

SARS-CoV-2 virus-like-particles dosing

The particle number was calculated from the physical dimensions of the VLPs rather than molecular weight. A 100-nm VLP (radius = 50 nm = 5 × 10− 6 cm) has a spherical volume of ~5.2 × 10− 1 6 cm3. Using a protein/lipid density of ~1.3 g/cm3, consistent with hydrated protein densities of 1.3–1.37 g/cm3 [44] and buoyant viral particle densities of 1.2–1.35 g/cm3 [45], yields a mass per particle of ~6.8 × 10− 1 6 g. Dividing the delivered 0.11 µg dose (1.1 × 10− 7 g) by this value gives ~2 × 108 particles (range 1.6–3.3 × 108 if a shell model is assumed).

SARS-CoV-2 infection in human iPSC-derived lung organoids

Human lung organoids were cultured in 24-well ultra-low attachment plates containing 0.5 mL of medium per well and exposed to SARS-CoV-2 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. Organoids were incubated with the virus at 37 °C for 1 h, washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove unbound virus, and maintained in 1 mL of fresh culture medium for 72 h.

The MOI of 0.1 was selected following preliminary optimization experiments in which organoids were infected with SARS-CoV-2 across a range of MOIs (0.01–1.0) (Figure S1A). At MOI 0.1, organoids displayed reproducible cytopathic effects (CPE) by 3 days post-infection, along with viral loads and progeny titers in the range of 104 −106 TCID₅₀, while preserving sufficient organoid integrity to enable downstream analyses (Figure S1B). Higher MOIs (≥0.5) caused extensive CPE with reduced viability, which may hinder the evaluation of treatment effects over time (Figure S1A).

For organoids receiving VLPs pre-treatment in the respective experiments, lung organoids were first incubated with 105 VLPs in culture medium at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the VLPs-containing medium was removed, and the organoids were immediately infected with SARS-CoV-2 following the infection procedure outlined above.

At the 72-hour post-infection (hpi) time point, the samples for downstream experiments were collected and prepared respectively. The cell culture supernatants were collected for TCID₅₀ assay. For RNA extraction for RT-qPCR, the organoids were washed thrice with PBS followed by lysis using Buffer RLT (Qiagen RNeasy Plus Mini Kit) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. For immunofluorescent staining, the organoids were rinsed three times with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; Gibco, 14025092) and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h at room temperature. Immunofluorescent staining was performed to detect the expression of human ACE2 (labeled with Alexa Fluor 594; R&D Systems) and the viral nucleocapsid protein (labeled with Alexa Fluor 488; Abcam). Imaging was conducted using Z-stack confocal microscopy.

RT-qPCR

RNA was isolated from lung tissues and organoids using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74134) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Viral replication was assessed using primer-probe sets targeting the SARS-CoV-2 E and N genes, as recommended by WHO protocols. Expressions of host inflammatory cytokine genes and the GAPDH reference gene were assessed using the respective primers.

One-step RT-qPCR was performed using Luna Universal One-Step RT-qPCR Master Mix (NEB, E3005) in the QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The thermal cycling profile included reverse transcription at 60 °C for 10 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 39 cycles of amplification (95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 15 s). The host gene expression was presented as the ΔΔCT values, normalized to GAPDH and mock-treated controls. Data analysis was completed using QuantStudio software.

The median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay.

The supernatants of cell culture medium or lung tissue homogenates were subjected to the assay. Vero E6 cells were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 96-well plates (Corning, 353072)12 h before infection to ensure 80% confluence. Lung tissues were homogenized in 500 µL DMEM using a bead mill, and supernatants were obtained after centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 5 min. Serial dilutions (1:10 to 1:107) were prepared by diluting 35 µL of the respective samples in 315 µL of serum-free DMEM. The serially diluted samples were added to the cell monolayers at 50 µL/well. Cytopathic effect (CPE) was assessed after 5 days of incubation (37 °C, 5% CO₂), and viral titers were determined using the Spearman-Kärber method [43].

Animal model

This study was carried out in strict accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The study protocols described in this study were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee (#958) at St. Michael’s Hospital, the Environmental Health Service (178-Z08-2 V), and the Division of Comparative Medicine (#20012664) at the University of Toronto. This study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Male transgenic K18-hACE2 mice [B6.Cg-Tg(K18–ACE2)2Prlmn/J] (Strain #:034860) which express human angiotensin I converting enzyme 2 (hACE2), aged between 8 to 10 weeks and weighing 20–35 grams, were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Upon arrival, mice were housed in group cages with unrestricted access to standard laboratory chow and water. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups prior to interventions.

Acute immune study of single dose VLPs in K18-hACE2 mice

Mice were randomly assigned to vehicle (n = 3- 5 per time point) or VLP (n = 3- 5 per time point) groups. Male transgenic K18-hACE2 mice (8–10 weeks old) received a single intranasal dose of 0.11 g VLPs in 50 µL of PBS. Mice were monitored daily, and the samples for respective downstream experiments were collected on days 2 and 7 post-administration. Blood was centrifuged (1,000 × g, 2 min) to isolate plasma. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected by inserting a catheter into the trachea of terminally anesthetized mice and flushing the left lung three times with 0.5 mL PBS. BALF was centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C; supernatants were stored at −80 °C, and cell pellets were used for total leukocyte counts. IL-6 levels in BALF were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (Abcam, ab235649).

1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate (DiI) labelling of VLPs

Stock SARS-CoV-2 virus-like particles (VLPs) were diluted 1:1000 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with 1 µL of the lipophilic dye DiI (Thermo Fisher, D3911) at room temperature for 30 min to facilitate membrane labeling. For dialysis, Slide-A-Lyzer® dialysis cassettes (Thermo Scientific, A52976) with a 20 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) and a working volume range of 0.5–3 mL were preconditioned by soaking in distilled deionized water for 1 h, as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The DiI-labeled VLPs solution was transferred into the pre-soaked cassettes and dialyzed against deionized water under continuous stirring at 300 rpm overnight at room temperature.

Animal fluorescent imaging

To assess VLPs biodistribution, 50 µL of DiI-labelled VLPs were administered intranasally to healthy K18-hACE2 mice. In vivo and ex vivo fluorescence was measured using the Newton 7.0 FT-500 imaging system at 5- and 60-min post-administration. Fluorescence intensity was background-corrected using organs from saline-treated control animals.

Antiviral assessment of VLPs in mice infected with SARS-CoV-2

K18-hACE2 transgenic mice aged 8 to 10 weeks were administered either 1.1 ng or 0.11 g VLPs in 50 µL of PBS intranasally one h before challenge with SARS-CoV-2 (SB2 strain) at a dose of 4 × 105 TCID₅₀ in a 50 µL inoculum. Mice were randomly assigned to vehicle-only mock (n = 10), SARS-CoV-2 alone (n = 13), and VLP pre-treated + SARS-CoV-2 (n = 16). Treatment groups were blinded using alphabetical codes throughout the experiment. Mice were observed daily for clinical signs and changes in body weight. On day 7 post-infection, mice were humanely euthanized under a surgical plane of anesthesia induced with 3% isoflurane (confirmed by loss of pedal reflex), followed by cardiac puncture and cervical dislocation. The procedure was adhered to the AVMA euthanasia standard. Organs were then collected for downstream analyses. The right lung was flushed with 2 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and sectioned into three parts for downstream applications: one for protein extraction (using 1% Triton X-100 with protease inhibitor), another for RNA stabilization in RNAprotect (Qiagen), and the third for virus quantification in DMEM supplemented with antibiotics. Additional organs such as the heart, liver, kidney, spleen, and ileum were stored in RNAprotect for downstream analysis. The left lung was expanded with 10% neutral-buffered formalin under 20 cmH₂O pressure and fixed for 72 h for histopathological processing.

Tissue sample processing

Tissues designated for RNA, protein, and viral assays were homogenized via bead mill (Bead Ruptor4, OMNI). Lysates for protein extraction were centrifuged (13,000 rpm, 15 min, 4°C), and supernatants stored at −80 °C. RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit and stored at −80 °C. For TCID₅₀ assays, samples were centrifuged at 11,500 × g for 5 min (4°C), and supernatants used for viral titer calculation using the Spearman-Kärber formula [43].

Clinical scores

Treatment groups were blinded using alphabetical codes throughout the experiment. Animal welfare was monitored daily using an eight-domain scoring rubric: activity, posture, coat/hair, respiration, eye, hydration, body condition, and miscellaneous signs (e.g., tremor, neurologic deficits). Each domain was scored from 1 to 4, where 4 indicated normal health and 1 represented severe impairment. Domain definitions included, for example, activity (4 = normal ambulation/exploration; 1 = non-responsive), posture (4 = normal; 1 = severely hunched/recumbent), and coat/hair (4 = shiny and well-groomed; 1 = severely ruffled with additional signs). The composite clinical score was calculated as the sum of all domains, with higher scores indicating better welfare. Final composite scores for analysis were determined on 7 days post-infection (dpi). Humane endpoints included any domain score of 2, >20% body weight loss, moribund state, inability to access food or water, or severe respiratory distress, at which point animals were immediately euthanized.

Western blot

Protein lysates were prepared using TPER buffer (Thermo Fisher), supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche) and 1X Triton X-100 (BioShop). Protein concentrations were determined using the Bio-Rad protein assay. Proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (0.2 µm), and probed with anti-ACE2 (ab272500, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signal was visualized with Bio-Rad Clarity ECL substrates and imaged using the ChemiDoc system. Densitometry was performed using ImageLab software.

Histology and lung injury scores

Lung tissue was fixed in 10% formalin (72 h), transferred to 70% ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 µm. Adjacent sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and imaged. Lung injury was assessed on four random high-power fields (400×) per slide, each containing ≥ 50% alveolar area and excluding large airways or vessels. Scoring (1–4 per criterion) was based on neutrophil infiltration (1: ≤5 cells; 4: >51 cells/field), alveolar edema or septal thickening (extent of affected alveoli), and hypercellularity quantified using ImageJ thresholding to determine the percentage of affected area. The total lung injury score was calculated as the average of individual scores for neutrophil infiltration, edema or thickening, and hypercellularity, and was determined by three independent blinded scorers.

Cell treatment, staining and immunofluorescence.

Cells were seeded onto coverslips placed in 12-well flat-bottom tissue culture plates (Corning, 353043) at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well. Calu-3 cells were treated with 0.11 mg VLPs in 0.5 mL for either 60- or 150-min. Controls were treated with PBS. Prior to fixation, cells were stained with WGA-488 (Invitrogen, W11261; 1:500 in HBSS) for 5 min at 37 °C. Fixation was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by 100 mM glycine treatment for 10 min. Cells were blocked with goat serum and stained with anti-ACE2 antibody (Abcam, ab272500) at 4 °C overnight. Secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 555 anti-rabbit, Invitrogen, A32732) were applied for 1 h. DAPI was used to stain nuclei, and coverslips were mounted using Dako medium. Cells were kept non-permeabilized to ensure only surface binding was analyzed.

Organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, cryoprotected sequentially in 15% and 30% sucrose (24 h each) and embedded in O.C.T. medium. Cryosections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, immunostained with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI counterstaining. Imaging was done using ZEISS LSM700 confocal microscopy and processed in Zen 3.3 software.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

Confocal imaging was performed with a Zeiss LSM700 system. Fluorophores imaged included DAPI, WGA-488, ACE2–AF647, and VLPs-AF594. Images were collected in 16-bit format at 2048 × 2048 resolution, with 0.38 µm z-slices and 13.26 pixels/µm resolution. Laser and gain settings were standardized across all samples in each biological replicate.

CLSM image quantification

Colocalization was assessed using the JACoP plugin in FIJI (ImageJ). Background was subtracted using a 50-pixel rolling ball radius. Thresholds were set based on control images and averaged across three replicates. Manders’ coefficient was calculated on triplicate images from three independent experiments. Mean fluorescence intensity was measured from maximum z-projections, with regions of interest (ROIs) defined by WGA-488 membrane staining. Fluorescence was normalized to cell area for comparison.

Transmission electron microscopy

This protocol was adapted from a previously described method [36]. Briefly, VLPs were prepared for TEM using a methylcellulose matrix-assisted drying method to minimize structural artifacts.80,81 Copper grids (3 mm, 200 mesh, carbon film; CF200-CU-50) were glow-discharged (PELCO easiGlow) and placed on sample drops for 60 min at room temperature. Grids were rinsed with 1× PBS (1 min), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde + 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate (5 min), and stained with 2% uranyl acetate (5 min). They were then incubated on drops of 2% methylcellulose with 0.2% uranyl acetate (15 min, 4 °C), blotted, and air-dried. Imaging was performed at 120 kV (Hitachi HT7800) in both high-resolution and high-contrast modes. Images were processed in FIJI with a Gaussian blur (radius 2.0 pixels).

Two-photon excitation fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (TPE-FCS)

Two-photon excitation fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (TPE-FCS) was employed to determine the hydrodynamic diameter of VLPs in solution. Generally, during an FCS measurement, fluorescence from Brownian-diffusing entities is detected when the particles pass within the femtolitre-scale two-photon excitation volume established by a high-power pulsed laser. As this fluorescence intensity fluctuates over time, the signal is autocorrelated to show the self-similarity of the signal as a function of lag time, τ. By analyzing this autocorrelation data from a fluorophore, diffusion coefficients of VLPs and freely diffusing dye in solution were obtained which allowed determination of their respective hydrodynamic diameters.

The TPE-FCS setup and methodology has been described previously [46]. In brief, a diode-pumped solid-state laser (5 watt, continuous-wave, 532 nm) was used to pump a mode-locked titanium: sapphire laser (100 fs pulses, 82 MHz frequency, 774 nm). For all TPE-FCS measurements, the excitation beam was directed to fill the back aperture of a 40× water-immersion objective (1.2 NA, 0.8 mm working distance) at an attenuated power of 30 mW. This objective lens focused the beam into the sample and collected fluorescence emitted by diffusing particles within the TPE volume. The emitted light was passed through a spectral notch filter before detection by avalanche photodiode. Fluorescence signals analyzed by correlator card (ALV-6000) were processed and plotted using OriginPro software. where r0 is the TPE waist radius, τ is the lag time, D is the diffusion coefficient, and z0 is the excitation focal volume depth. TPE focal volume dimensions were calibrated using dyes of known diffusion coefficients. where kB is the Boltzmann constant (1.38 × 10−23 J/K), T is the absolute temperature (K), η is the viscosity of the fluid (Pa·s) and r is the hydrodynamic radius of the diffusing particle (m).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Parametric data (ELISA, RT-qPCR) were analyzed via ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Non-parametric data (e.g., lung injury score) were evaluated using Kruskal-Wallis followed by Dunn’s test. Manders’ coefficient and brightness data were analyzed via two-tailed Student’s t-tests.

To test functionality, Calu-3 cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled VLPs (DiI-VLPs). Within 60 min, DiI-VLPs co-localized with ACE2 and the membrane marker wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) (Figs. 1A and B). SARS-CoV-2 VLPs inhibit infection in human iPSC-derived lung organoids

A single dose VLP treatment attenuates severe SARS-CoV-2 infection in vivo

In K18-hACE2 mice, a single low VLP dose (1.1 ng in 50 µL) given 1 h before viral challenge had no effect on weight loss, survival, or pathology (Figures S3A–G).

In contrast, a higher dose (0.11 mg in 50 µL) significantly reduced weight loss and showed a trend toward improved survival, though the latter did not reach statistical significance (Figs. 6A–C). Clinical scores were higher in VLP-treated mice (n = 16) at 7 dpi (Fig. 6D). Treated lungs showed reduced viral RNA and IL-6 expression, absence of infectious progeny (Figs. 6E–G), lower nucleocapsid staining, and preserved ACE2 expression by both western blot and immunofluorescence analysis (Figs. 6H, 6I, S4 and S5). ACE2 immunofluorescence staining of mice lung at 7dpi (Figure S5) demonstrated that mock-infected and VLP-treated mice preserved ACE2 surface expression on lung epithelial cells (red arrows), whereas SARS-CoV-2-infected mice showed markedly reduced ACE2 staining intensity. This is consistent with western blot analysis (Figs. 6I and S4), which demonstrates reduced total ACE2 protein abundance in the SARS-CoV-2-infected group compared with both mock controls and VLP-treated animals. Notably, ACE2 expression in VLP-treated mice remained comparable to mock controls at the 7-day endpoint. Histopathology confirmed attenuated lung injury (Figs. 6J and K). Discussion

The continued emergence of antigenically divergent SARS-CoV-2 variants underscores the need for complementary countermeasures that act earlier in infection than current vaccines and antivirals. Existing FDA- and internationally approved interventions, including mRNA, viral vectors, and inactivated vaccines, as well as antivirals such as remdesivir, molnupiravir, and nirmatrelvir, remain critical but are challenged by spike mutations that reduce neutralizing antibody recognition and limit therapeutic durability [47–49].

A defining vulnerability of SARS-CoV-2 is its reliance on ACE2 for host entry [50]. As infection begins in the nasal and upper respiratory tract, intercepting virus–ACE2 interactions at the mucosal surface represent a logical prophylactic strategy [51]. In this study, we show that SARS-CoV-2 VLPs act as effective decoys: they bind ACE2, undergo internalization, and reduce ACE2 surface expression in epithelial cells. In iPSC-derived lung organoids, VLP pre-treatment preserved structural integrity and markedly reduced viral RNA, infectious progeny, and inflammatory activation. In vivo, intranasal VLPs distributed widely throughout the lungs without provoking acute inflammation. A single dose administration before viral challenge reduced viral burden, mitigated lung injury, and preserved lung epithelial ACE2 levels in K18-hACE2 mice. Notably, ACE2 expression in VLP-treated mice remained comparable to mock controls at the 7-day endpoint, suggesting that protection may involve mechanisms beyond sustained ACE2 reduction alone. The preserved ACE2 levels most likely reflect protection against virus-induced ACE2 loss, as temporary interference with viral access to surface ACE2 during the early infection window could reduce initial viral burden and thereby attenuate downstream inflammatory injury. This finding indicates that, in addition to competitive ACE2 engagement, other protective mechanisms may contribute to the observed antiviral effects. Together, these findings establish proof-of-concept for intranasal VLPs as a pre-exposure prophylactic intervention.

Translation to human appears practical. Body surface area scaling indicates that the protective mouse dose of 0.11 µg corresponds to ~27–31 µg in a 60–70 kg adult, equivalent to ~5–9 × 1010 particles [44, 45]. This dose can be readily formulated in 0.2–0.5 mL for intranasal delivery, and gram-scale lots of VLP yields > 30,000 doses, well within existing production capacity. While these calculations support the potential feasibility and scalability of VLP decoys for human use, they are not pharmacokinetically validated. Formal dose-finding studies in larger animal models and phase I clinical trials will be required to establish safe and effective human dosing. These steps will be essential for clinical translation.

The VLP platform offers several distinct advantages. Structurally, VLPs mimic authentic virions, preserving conformational spike display for high-affinity ACE2 binding while lacking genetic material, eliminating the risk of replication or recombination [52, 53]. Production in Nicotiana benthamiana plants further reduces biosafety risks and enables rapid, low-cost, and high-yield manufacturing without cold-chain constraints, making this approach attractive for global deployment [54, 55]. Intranasal delivery directly targets the portal of infection and may synergize with mucosal immunity to limit viral establishment [56, 57]. Importantly, VLPs did not trigger acute inflammatory responses in organoids or in vivo, even at high doses, suggesting a favorable safety profile compared with other decoy or attenuated-virus strategies [58].

It is important to note that the present study establishes proof-of-concept specifically for pre-exposure prophylaxis, in which VLPs are administered prior to viral challenge. This paradigm maybe clinically relevant in defined high-risk settings, such as healthcare workers, household contacts of confirmed cases, and individuals in outbreak environments where viral exposure is anticipated. The 1-hour pre-treatment interval was selected to allow VLP distribution and ACE2 engagement at the mucosal surface before viral inoculation. Whether VLPs retain efficacy when administered after viral exposure or at symptom onset remains an important open question. Future studies should evaluate post-exposure administration and repeated dose to determine whether the decoy strategy can also provide therapeutic benefit after infection has been established.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Protection was assessed in short-term preclinical models, and long-term durability remains unknown. The K18-hACE2 mouse model expresses human ACE2 under the keratin-18 promoter, resulting in supraphysiological receptor expression, including ectopic brain expression in the brain that is not observed in humans. Consequently, infection severity, tissue tropism, and endpoint phenotypes in this model may not fully reflect human COVID-19 pathophysiology. In addition, only male K18-hACE2 mice were studied, based on their more severe phenotype in our prior work [43], however, further studies should include both sexes to better capture biological variability.

All experiments in this study were performed using the SB2 ancestral strain of SARS-CoV-2, which may limit direct extrapolation to currently circulating variants, particularly Omicron-lineage strains. However, the ACE2-decoy mechanism is expected to remain fundamentally relevant because all known SARS-CoV-2 variants continue to require spike-ACE2 interaction for host-cell entry. Variants such as Omicron harbor receptor-binding domain mutations that alter ACE2 binding affinity and may influence the competitive dynamics between viral spike proteins and decoy particles. Increased ACE2 affinity could potentially affect the dose requirement or binding efficiency of VLP-based decoys, rather than abolishing the underlying mechanism. Future translational development should therefore include engineered VLPs incorporating variant-matched spike or receptor-binding domain sequences to optimize efficacy against emerging strains.

In summary, our data demonstrate that intranasal SARS-CoV-2 VLPs act as ACE2-binding decoys that intercept viral entry and reduce disease severity. Given their safety, scalability, and adaptability, with potential utility not only against SARS-CoV-2 but also future respiratory pathogens that exploit host receptors for entry.
