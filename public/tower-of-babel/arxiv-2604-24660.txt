Instrumental Variable Analysis Without Structural Equations
Zikai Shen, Dimitri Meunier, Houssam Zenati, Arthur Gretton, Nathan Kallus, Aurélien Bibaut

Abstract
We consider debiased inference on least-squares solutions to inverse problems as a way to avoid having to assume exact solutions exist. Such assumptions are substantive and not innocuous and their failure may well imperil inference when we impose them on the statistical model. Our approach instead allows us to conduct inference on a quantity that is defined regardless of solutions existing and coincides with the usual estimands when they do. For the case of instrumental variables, this means we can motivate the analysis with structural models but these do not need to hold exactly for the inferential procedure to remain valid.



1
Introduction

Instrumental variable (IV) analyses generally start by posing a
structural equation
:

Y=h_{\rm structural}(X)+\epsilon,

(1)

where
h_{\rm structural}
represents the
causal effect
of
X
on
Y
, and
X
and
\epsilon
may be endogenous (
\E[\epsilon\mid X]\neq 0
).
Then given an exogenous instrument
Z
satisfying the exclusion restriction, the common
statistical
solution given joint observations of
W=(X,Y,Z)\sim P
is to conduct inference on some continuous linear functional
h\mapsto\E_{P}[m(W;h)]

of a solution
h\in\mathcal{H}
to the linear equation implied by exclusion:

T_{P}h=r_{P},

(2)

where
T_{P}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{G}
maps
h\mapsto\argmin_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\E_{P}(h(X)-g(Z))^{2}
,
r_{P}=\argmin_{r\in\mathcal{G}}\E_{P}(Y-r(Z))^{2}
, and
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{G}
are closed linear subspaces of square-integrable functions of
X
and of
Z
, respectively. For example, if these are
all
square-integrable functions, then
(T_{P}h)(Z)=\E_{P}[h(X)\mid Z]
is the conditional expectation.

There are two possible challenges with this:

•

The existence of solutions to
Eq.

2
. When we start with
Eq.

1
with
h_{\rm structural}\in\mathcal{H}
we are guaranteed a solution to
Eq.

2
in
\mathcal{H}
by construction. For example, we may assume a
linear
structural equation. Nonparametric IV (NPIV) analysis aims to relax this by allowing
h_{\rm structural}
to be
any
(square-integrable) function. However, this still assumes some solution exists, which is still a very substantive assumption. If we impose this assumption on the statistical model we consider, as done in
23
;
5
;
4
;
2
;
1
;
6
;
7
;
19
;
12
who achieve efficiency in this statistical model, then the estimator may well deteriorate very ungracefully when no perfect solution to
Eq.

2
actually exists. This can be understood as exclusion and/or homogeneity (additive
\epsilon
) not always holding exactly, as may occur in practice.

•

The uniqueness of
\E_{P}[m(W;h)]
over solutions
. Even if we have solutions to
Eq.

2
, there may be many, and they may not all lead to the same value of
\E_{P}[m(W;h)]
. This can be understood as instrument weakness: the variations
Z
induces in
X
can be insufficient to characterize the aspects of
h_{\rm structural}
we care about. That
T_{P}h=T_{P}h^{\prime}
implies
\E_{P}[m(W;h)]=\E_{P}[m(W;h^{\prime})]
for all
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
would be implied by the existence of a solution in
\mathcal{G}
to
T_{P}^{*}g={a_{P}}
, where
a_{P}\in\mathcal{H}
is the Riesz representer of
h\mapsto\E_{P}[m(W;h)]
(since this implies
a_{P}\in\operatorname{Range}(T_{P}^{*})\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(T_{P})^{\bot}
). These identification assumptions have been explored in
23
;
18
;
5
;
4
;
26
;
14
. However, the same conundrum arises: a solution to that linear equation may well only
approximately
exist in practice.

In both cases, it would behoove us to not impose the assumptions of the existence of solutions (to
T_{P}h=r_{P}
, to
T_{P}^{*}g=a_{P}
, or to both) on any statistical model. While the equations may be well motivated,
perfect
solutions may not really exist in practice and this can derail inference procedures that assume they do. Instead, we would rather target a statistical quantity that is well-defined
regardless
of existence of such solutions, coincides with the structural quantities when they do make sense, and degrades reasonably in mild violations of exact solution existence.

Toward that end, in this paper we consider debiased inference on the following estimand
(without restrictions on the existence of solutions):

\displaystyle\Psi(P)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(Z)h(X)],\quad h\in\mathcal{H}_{P},\quad g\in\mathcal{G}_{P},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{P}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\|T_{P}h-r_{P}\|_{L_{2}(P_{Z})}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{P}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\|T_{P}^{*}g-a_{P}\|_{L_{2}(P_{X})}^{2},

which is well defined, under a mild assumption given in the next section (
1
), without requiring the existence of solutions to either equation

\displaystyle T_{P}h=r_{P},\quad T_{P}^{\ast}g=a_{P},

(4)

and regardless of which minimizer we choose in the above population least-squares problems. Of course, when solutions to Eq. (
4
) exist, we always recover the original causal structural estimand.

To allow sufficient generality so as to also cover such related problems as proximal causal inference
(
21
;
13
;
12
;
19
)
, we generalize
r_{P}
to be the (unknown) Riesz representer of some continuous linear functional
\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}:g\mapsto\E_{P}[\tilde{m}(W;g)]
, where
\tilde{m}
is a known function. For example, for the IV setting, we set
\tilde{m}(W;g)=Yg(Z)
. Similarly, we also let
a_{P}
be unknown, and only require that it is the (unknown) Riesz representer of some continuous linear functional
\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}:h\mapsto\E_{P}[m(W;h)]
, where
m
is a known function. We refer the reader to Examples 2 and 3 in
4
for instantiation in proximal causal inference and missing-not-at-random data.

2
Identification

In this section we characterize the existence of least-squares solutions and the well-definedness of
\Psi(P)
. We first introduce the relevant orthogonal decompositions. Since
T_{P}
is a
bounded linear operator between Hilbert spaces, we always have

\mathcal{H}=\ker(T_{P})\oplus\ker(T_{P})^{\perp}=\ker(T_{P})\oplus\overline{\operatorname{range}(T_{P}^{*})},

and

\mathcal{G}=\ker(T_{P}^{*})\oplus\ker(T_{P}^{*})^{\perp}=\ker(T_{P}^{*})\oplus\overline{\operatorname{range}(T_{P})}.

Accordingly, write

a_{P}=a_{P,\parallel}+a_{P,\perp},\qquad a_{P,\parallel}\in\ker(T_{P})^{\perp}=\overline{\operatorname{range}(T_{P}^{*})},\qquad a_{P,\perp}\in\ker(T_{P}),

and

r_{P}=r_{P,\parallel}+r_{P,\perp},\qquad r_{P,\parallel}\in\ker(T_{P}^{*})^{\perp}=\overline{\operatorname{range}(T_{P})},\qquad r_{P,\perp}\in\ker(T_{P}^{*}).

We impose the following range condition.

Assumption 1

(Least-squares attainability)
.

r_{P,\parallel}\in\operatorname{range}(T_{P}),\qquad a_{P,\parallel}\in\operatorname{range}(T_{P}^{*}).

Remark 1

(Existence of exact solutions vs. least-squares minimizers)
.

1
is exactly the condition that the components of

r_{P}
and
a_{P}
lying in the closures of the relevant ranges actually lie in
the corresponding ranges. Equivalently,

r_{P}\in\ker(T_{P}^{*})+\operatorname{range}(T_{P}),\qquad a_{P}\in\operatorname{range}(T_{P}^{*})+\ker(T_{P}).

This condition guarantees that the least-squares solution sets

\mathcal{H}_{P}\neq\varnothing,\qquad\mathcal{G}_{P}\neq\varnothing

are nonempty. It automatically holds when
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
are finite-dimensional, since the relevant ranges are then closed.

Importantly,
1
does not impose solvability of
the primary or dual equations. Solvability of
T_{P}h=r_{P}
would require
both

r_{P,\parallel}\in\mathrm{range}(T_{P})\qquad\text{and}\qquad r_{P,\perp}=0.

Similarly, exact solvability of
T_{P}^{*}g=a_{P}
would require both

a_{P,\parallel}\in\mathrm{range}(T_{P}^{*})\qquad\text{and}\qquad a_{P,\perp}=0.

Thus, compared with the usual condition in

5
;
4
;
23
,
which restricts
a_{P}
to lie in
\mathrm{range}(T_{P}^{*})
rather than merely
in its closure, we further allow
a_{P}
to have a component in

\ker(T_{P})
. Analogously, we allow
r_{P}
to have a component in

\ker(T_{P}^{*})
. Hence we do not require exact solutions to either

T_{P}h=r_{P}
or
T_{P}^{*}g=a_{P}
.

Under
1
, least-squares solutions exist and form an affine set hence convex. So,
we can define the minimum-norm least-squares solutions:

\displaystyle h^{{\dagger}}_{P}=\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}}\|h\|_{L^{2}(P_{X})},\quad g^{{\dagger}}_{P}=\argmin_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{P}}\|g\|_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}

Letting
T_{P}^{{\dagger}}
denote the pseudoinverse
(
15
)
,
by definition of
\mathcal{H}_{P}
and the fact that
r_{P}\in\mathrm{dom}(T_{P}^{{\dagger}})=\mathrm{range}(T_{P})+\ker(T_{P}^{\ast})
, we have

\displaystyle h^{{\dagger}}_{P}=T_{P}^{{\dagger}}r_{P}.

Since the pseudoinverse
T_{P}^{{\dagger}}
annihilates the
\ker(T_{P}^{\ast})
-component of
r_{P}
, we have

\displaystyle h^{{\dagger}}_{P}=T_{P}^{{\dagger}}r_{P}=T_{P}^{{\dagger}}r_{P,\parallel}=(T_{P}|_{\ker(T_{P})^{\perp}})^{-1}r_{P,\parallel}.

In addition, the pseudoinverse solution can also be interpreted as the Tikhonov regularized solution in the limit of vanishing regularization,

\displaystyle h^{{\dagger}}_{P}=\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}h_{P}^{\lambda},\quad h_{P}^{\lambda}=\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\lambda\|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P_{X})}+\|T_{P}h-r_{P}\|_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}^{2}.

(5)

Analogously, we have

\displaystyle g^{{\dagger}}_{P}=\argmin_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{P}}\|g\|_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}=(T_{P}^{\ast})^{{\dagger}}a_{P}=(T_{P}^{\ast})^{{\dagger}}a_{P,\parallel}=\left(T_{P}^{\ast}|_{\ker(T_{P}^{\ast})^{\perp}}\right)^{-1}a_{P,\parallel},

where the last equality holds because
(T_{P}^{\ast})^{{\dagger}}
annihilates the
\ker(T_{P})
-component of
a_{P}
.

We now show that under
1

\Psi(P)
is well-defined and can be expressed as the inner product of
any
least-squares solutions.

Lemma 1

(Parametrization invariance)
.

At
P
where
1
holds, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{P}=h^{{\dagger}}_{P}+\ker(T_{P}),\quad\mathcal{G}_{P}=g^{{\dagger}}_{P}+\ker(T_{P}^{\ast}).

Hence, for any
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{P}
and
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{P}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[h(X)g(Z)]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[h^{\prime}(X)g^{\prime}(Z)].

Proof.

By definition,
h^{{\dagger}}_{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{P}
. Note that any
h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}
satisfies the normal equation

\displaystyle T_{P}^{\ast}T_{P}h-T_{P}^{\ast}r_{P}=0.

We therefore have
\mathcal{H}_{P}=h^{{\dagger}}_{P}+\ker(T_{P}^{\ast}T_{P})=h^{{\dagger}}_{P}+\ker(T_{P})
. The statement for
\mathcal{G}_{P}
follows analogously. For any
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{P}
and
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{P}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[h(X)g(Z)]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[h(X)(T_{P}^{\ast}g)(X)]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[h(X)(T_{P}^{\ast}g^{\prime})(X)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[h(X)g^{\prime}(Z)]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[(T_{P}h)(Z)g^{\prime}(Z)]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[h^{\prime}(X)g^{\prime}(Z)].\qed

3
Functional bias expansion

In the following, we fix
P
where
1
holds, and we fix
h_{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{P}
,
g_{P}\in\mathcal{G}_{P}
. We refer to
h_{P}
as the primary nuisance and
g_{P}
as the secondary nuisance. For fixed
h_{P},g_{P}
, we define respectively

\displaystyle\Psi_{h_{P}}(g)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[h_{P}(X)g(Z)],\quad\Psi_{h_{P}}:L^{2}(P_{Z})\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\Psi_{g_{P}}(h)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[h(X)g_{P}(Z)],\quad\Psi_{g_{P}}:L^{2}(P_{X})\to\mathbb{R}.

Note we have
\Psi(P)=\Psi_{h_{P}}(g_{P})=\Psi_{g_{P}}(h_{P})
by
1
.

We will next impose an analogous assumption to
4
;
5
that controls the smoothness of our functionals and enables an expansion we can use for debiased estimation. However, unlike
4
;
5
, consistent with not assuming that solutions to either the primary or secondary linear equations exist, we will impose the condition only on the components of
a_{P}
and
r_{P}
that live in
\overline{\mathrm{range}(T_{P}^{\ast})}
and
\overline{\mathrm{range}(T_{P})}
, respectively, rather than on
a_{P}
and
r_{P}
themselves.

Assumption 2

(Source conditions on projected Riesz representers)
.

a_{P,\parallel}\in\mathrm{Range}(T_{P}^{*}T_{P})
,

r_{P,\parallel}\in\mathrm{Range}(T_{P}T_{P}^{*})
.

Note that
2
is strictly stronger than
1
and will be our standing assumption in the remainder of the paper.
1
should be understood as the minimal assumption to ensure the existence of least squares solution.
2
is equivalent to the existence of solutions to the below Riesz regression problems for
\Psi_{h_{P}}
and
\Psi_{g_{P}}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{P}^{h}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{A}_{P}^{h}=\argmin_{\alpha^{h}\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\frac{1}{2}(T_{P}\alpha^{h})(Z)^{2}-g_{P}(Z)\alpha^{h}(X)\right]=\argmin_{\alpha^{h}\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{2}\|T_{P}\alpha^{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}-\langle a_{P,\parallel},\alpha^{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{X})},

(6)

\displaystyle\alpha_{P}^{g}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{A}_{P}^{g}\argmin_{\alpha^{g}\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\frac{1}{2}(T_{P}^{\ast}\alpha^{g})(X)^{2}-h_{P}(X)\alpha^{g}(Z)\right]=\argmin_{\alpha^{g}\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{2}\|T_{P}^{\ast}\alpha^{g}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P_{X})}-\langle r_{P,\parallel},\alpha^{g}\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}.

(7)

The first-order conditions of these optimization problems above yield:

\displaystyle\langle h,T_{P}^{\ast}T_{P}\alpha_{P}^{h}\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{X})}

\displaystyle=\langle a_{P,\parallel},h\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{X})}=\mathbb{E}_{P}[h(X)g_{P}(Z)],\quad\forall h\in\mathcal{H}

(8)

\displaystyle\langle g,T_{P}T_{P}^{\ast}\alpha_{P}^{g}\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}

\displaystyle=\langle r_{P,\parallel},g\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}=\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(Z)h_{P}(X)],\quad\forall g\in\mathcal{G}.

(9)

We next establish the equivalence between the projected source condition and the existence of solutions to the Riesz regression problems and how these solutions give rise to the orthogonal complements of our Riesz representers and minimum norm least-squares solutions. Our argument adapts Theorem 2 and Lemma 3 of
4

Lemma 2
.

The following statements are equivalent:

1.

a_{P,\parallel}\in\mathrm{Range}(T_{P}^{*}T_{P})
and
r_{P,\parallel}\in\mathrm{Range}(T_{P}T_{P}^{*})
.

2.

The solution sets in
Eqs.

6
and
7
are non-empty.

3.

h_{P}^{{\dagger}}\in\mathrm{Range}(T_{P})
and
g_{P}^{{\dagger}}\in\mathrm{Range}(T_{P})
.

Moreover, when these conditions hold, any solutions
\alpha_{P}^{h},\alpha_{P}^{g}
to
Eqs.

6
and
7
satisfy

\displaystyle T_{P}^{*}T_{P}\alpha_{P}^{h}

\displaystyle=a_{P,\parallel},\quad T_{P}\alpha_{P}^{h}=g_{P}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle T_{P}T_{P}^{*}\alpha_{P}^{g}

\displaystyle=r_{P,\parallel},\quad T^{*}_{P}\alpha_{P}^{g}=h_{P}^{\dagger}.

Proof.

The equivalence between 1 and 3 is shown in
2
. We refer the reader therein for the proof and an in-depth discussion.

We prove the equivalence of 1 and 2 for
\alpha_{P}^{h}
. The statement for
\alpha_{P}^{g}
follows by an identical argument. Let

\displaystyle Q_{h}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\|T_{P}\alpha\|_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}^{2}-\langle a_{P,\parallel},\alpha\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{X})},\qquad\alpha\in\mathcal{H}.

Since
Q_{h}
is convex and Fréchet differentiable,
\alpha\in\mathcal{H}
minimizes
Q_{h}
if and only if its first-order condition holds:

\displaystyle 0=\dot{Q}_{h}(\alpha)[v]=\langle T_{P}^{*}T_{P}\alpha-a_{P,\parallel},v\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{X})},\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{H}.

Equivalently,
T_{P}^{\ast}T_{P}\alpha-a_{P,\parallel}\in\mathcal{H}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{H}=\{0\}
. Hence the solution set in Eq. (
6
) is non-empty if and only if
a_{P,\parallel}\in\mathrm{Range}(T_{P}^{*}T_{P})
.

Next, we show we recover the minimum norm least-squares solution.
For any
g_{P}\in\mathcal{G}_{P}
, we have
T_{P}^{\ast}g_{P}=a_{P,\parallel}
, thus
g_{P}-T_{P}\alpha_{P}^{h}\in\ker(T_{P}^{\ast})
. Note that
\mathcal{G}_{P}=g_{P}^{{\dagger}}+\ker(T_{P}^{\ast})=g_{P}^{{\dagger}}+\overline{\mathrm{Range}(T_{P})}^{\perp}
. Since
T_{P}\alpha_{P}^{h}\in\mathrm{Range}(T_{P})\cap\mathcal{G}_{P}
, it coincides with the minimum-norm element.
∎

Remark 2

(Weak Riesz representers and the source condition)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\ker(T_{P})^{\perp}
. Equip

\mathcal{H}_{0}
with the weak inner product

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{T_{P}}=\langle T_{P}u,T_{P}v\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}.

This is an inner product on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, but
\mathcal{H}_{0}
need not be
complete under the associated norm unless
\operatorname{Range}(T_{P})
is closed.
Let
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{0}}^{\,T_{P}}
denote its completion. The map
u\mapsto T_{P}u
is an isometry from
\mathcal{H}_{0}
onto

\operatorname{Range}(T_{P})
, and therefore extends uniquely to an isometric isomorphism

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{H}_{0}}^{\,T_{P}}\cong\overline{\operatorname{Range}(T_{P})}.

Consider the linear functional
\ell_{a}:\mathcal{H}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\ell_{a}(u)=\langle a_{P,\parallel},u\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{X})},\qquad u\in\mathcal{H}_{0}.

Under
1
,
a_{P,\parallel}\in\operatorname{Range}(T^{*}_{P})
. Recall that

g_{P}^{\dagger}\in\ker(T^{*}_{P})^{\perp}=\overline{\operatorname{Range}(T_{P})}
is the
minimum-norm solution of

T^{*}_{P}g=a_{P,\parallel}.

Then

\displaystyle\ell_{a}(u)=\langle g_{P}^{\dagger},T_{P}u\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{Z})},

so
\ell_{a}
is continuous with respect to the weak norm and has a Riesz
representer in the completed weak Hilbert space

\overline{\mathcal{H}_{0}}^{\,T_{P}}
, given by the preimage of
g_{P}^{\dagger}
under the isometric isomorphism of
\overline{\mathcal{H}_{0}}^{T_{P}}\cong\overline{\mathrm{Range}(T_{P})}
.

2
is the stronger requirement that this completed-space weak Riesz
representer is represented by an actual element of the original space

\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Equivalently,

\displaystyle g_{P}^{\dagger}\in\operatorname{Range}(T_{P}),

so there exists
\alpha_{P}^{h}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle T_{P}\alpha_{P}^{h}=g_{P}^{\dagger}.

(10)

Applying
T^{*}_{P}
, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle T^{*}_{P}T_{P}\alpha_{P}^{h}=a_{P,\parallel},

that is,

\displaystyle a_{P,\parallel}\in\operatorname{Range}(T^{*}_{P}T_{P}).

Thus
2
is precisely the condition that the weak Riesz representer
belongs to the non-completed weak-norm space, rather than only to its completion.
The same argument with
T^{*}_{P}
in place of
T_{P}
gives the analogous
interpretation of
r_{P,\parallel}\in\operatorname{Range}(T_{P}T^{*}_{P})
.

Remark 3

(Comparison with
4
)
.

We compare Assumption

with the

and

In light of Lemma

\alpha_{P}^{h}

. We emphasize that in contrast to

Let
\theta=(h,g)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{G}
and
\alpha=(\alpha^{h},\alpha^{g})\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{G}
denote a generic set of candidate functions intended to approximate solutions in

. We let

denote the tuple of all of our nuisances. Define the score:

\displaystyle\chi(w;\eta)

\displaystyle:=-h(x)\xi^{\alpha^{h}}(z)+\widetilde{m}\left(w;\xi^{\alpha^{h}}\right)-\left(\alpha^{h}(x)-\xi^{\alpha^{h}}(z)\right)\left(\xi^{h}(z)-r(z)\right)

(11)

\displaystyle-g(z)\xi^{\alpha^{g}}(x)+m\left(w;\xi^{\alpha^{g}}\right)-\left(\alpha^{g}(z)-\xi^{\alpha^{g}}(x)\right)\left(\xi^{g}(x)-a(x)\right)

\displaystyle+g(z)h(x),

Our estimator will take the form of plugging in some
\hat{\theta},\hat{\alpha},\hat{\xi},\hat{\eta}
and taking the empirical expectation of

Theorem 1

(Bias characterization)
.

Suppose
2
holds. Let
\theta_{P}
be any choice in
\mathcal{H}_{P}\times\mathcal{G}_{P}
,
\alpha_{P}
any choice in
\mathcal{A}_{P}^{h}\times\mathcal{A}_{P}^{g}
, and
\xi_{P}=(T_{P}h_{P},T_{P}^{*}g_{P},T_{P}\alpha^{h}_{P},T_{P}^{*}\alpha^{g}_{P})
. Let any
\theta\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{G},\alpha\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{G},\xi\in(\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{H})^{2}
be given. We then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[\chi(W;\eta)]-\Psi_{P}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[(g(Z)-g_{P}(Z))(h(X)-h_{P}(X))]

(A)

\displaystyle+\left\langle T_{P}(h-h_{P}),T_{P}(\alpha_{P}^{h}-\alpha^{h})\right\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}+\langle T_{P}h-\xi^{h},T_{P}\alpha^{h}-\xi^{\alpha^{h}}\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}+\langle T_{P}\alpha^{h}-\xi^{\alpha^{h}},r-r_{P}\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}

(B)

\displaystyle+\left\langle T_{P}^{\ast}(g-g_{P}),T_{P}^{\ast}(\alpha_{P}^{g}-\alpha^{g})\right\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{X})}+\left\langle T_{P}^{\ast}\alpha^{g}-\xi^{\alpha^{g}},T_{P}^{\ast}g-\xi^{g}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{X})}+\left\langle T_{P}^{\ast}\alpha^{g}-\xi^{\alpha^{g}},a-a_{P}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(P_{X})}.

(C)

Remark 4

(Comparison to the setting with solutions)
.

In

;

, only term (A) appears in the bias expansion. The existence of solutions to both primary and secondary linear equations, as assumed therein, renders the other terms trivially zero and obviates the need to even estimate

Proof of
6
.

Let

denote the independent sample used to construct sample averages for inference. Recall it is distinct from the set of samples used to fit nuisances. We have

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}-\Psi(P)=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\chi(W_{i};\hat{\eta})-\int\chi(w;\hat{\eta})P(dw)\right)}_{\text{(A)}}+\underbrace{\int\chi(w;\hat{\eta})P(dw)-\Psi(P)}_{\text{(B)}}.

1

where we benefit from weak norm rates in either
g
or
h

B
) and (
C
) are bounded by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, yielding the bound

\displaystyle\left|\int\chi(w;\hat{\eta})P(dw)-\Psi(P)\right|\leq~

\displaystyle\min\{e_{g}^{w}e_{h}^{s},\ e_{g}^{s}e_{h}^{w}\}+e_{h}^{w}e_{\alpha^{h}}^{w}+e_{g}^{w}e_{\alpha^{g}}^{w}

\displaystyle+\rho_{h}\rho_{\alpha^{h}}+\rho_{g}\rho_{\alpha^{g}}+\rho_{\alpha^{h}}e_{r}^{s}+\rho_{\alpha^{g}}e_{a}^{s}.

The second order mixed bias conditions in
1

o_{p}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle(A)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi(W_{i};\eta_{P})\right)+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\chi(W_{i};\hat{\eta})-\chi(W_{i};\eta_{P})-\int\left(\chi(w;\hat{\eta})-\chi(w;\eta_{P})\right)P(dw)\right)}_{\text{(A.1)}}

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\xi}^{h}-\xi_{P}^{h}\|_{L^{2}(P_{Z})}
