Machine learning for predicting liver metastasis in colorectal cancer
Authors: Yujie Li, Yunwei Wei, Yangjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo evaluate the performance of machine learning models in predicting liver metastasis in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients using the SEER database and external validation from Ningbo No.2 Hospital.MethodsThe data on patients with colorectal cancer were obtained from Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database from 2010 to 2023. Patients were classified into training (n = 29017) and testing sets (n = 12437). The data were used to build eight machine learning models to predict liver metastasis in colorectal cancer patients. A total of 11 clinical variables were entered into these models. Model performance was measured with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) and area under precision-recall curve (AUPR). The models were visualized and interpreted using the SHAP method.ResultsIn the SEER database cohort, the incidence of liver metastasis was 7.2% (2977/41,454). Of the eight machine learning models, Gradient Boosting (GB) had the best AUC (0.837) and AUPR (0.294). Upon external validation, the GB model achieved an AUC of 0.730 and an AUPR of 0.278. We explored the significance of features in the model through SHAP analysis. CEA, N stage and T stage were the heavily weighted factors used by the GB. An online calculator was developed for clinical use.ConclusionThe GB model demonstrates robust predictive performance for liver metastasis in CRC, validated internally and externally, and presents a potentially valuable tool for clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent malignancy globally, accounting for approximately 1.1 million new cases annually, and stands as the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. The liver represents the predominant site of hematogenous metastasis in CRC, making colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) a critical challenge in disease management [2]. Clinical data reveal that 15–25% of CRC patients present with synchronous liver metastases at initial diagnosis, while an additional 15–25% develop metachronous liver metastases following curative resection of the primary tumor [3, 4].

In order to increase patient survival, early diagnosis of colorectal patients at high risk for liver metastasis is essential since it informs clinical staging and permits prompt, individualized therapy. Therefore, it is meaningful to define the risk factors of metastasis in colorectal patients and establish an efficient prediction model. This study aims to develop and validate a machine learning model for predicting the risk of liver metastasis in CRC patients using a large population-based database. To enhance the model’s generalizability, we incorporated an external validation cohort from Ningbo No.2 Hospital.

Materials and methods

SEER training and testing cohorts

The SEER program of the US National Cancer Institute provides data on cancer incidence and survival in the United States and covers approximately 30% of the US population across several geographic regions [5]. In our study, colorectal cancer data from 18 registries were collected from the SEER database between January 1, 2010 and November 30, 2023 by use of SEER*stat version 8.4.4. We do not require patients’ informed consent for access to and use of the SEER database because the data and information have been anonymized and deidentified ahead of their release.

External validation cohort

The external validation cohort was established by consecutively extracting data from the electronic medical record system of Ningbo No.2 Hospital, initially including all patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2024. A rigorous screening process was applied, which mandated pathologically confirmed primary colorectal adenocarcinoma and the availability of complete clinical, pathological, and laboratory data for all model variables, including age, sex, marital status, primary site, histologic type, grade, tumor size, T stage, N stage, CEA level, tumor deposits, and metastasis status, to ensure data integrity and suitability for robust external validation.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2024). The utilization of the SEER data did not require separate ethics approval. The utilization of the external validation was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ningbo No.2 Hospital (Approval No. PJ-NBEY-KY-2025-126-01), with the requirement for informed consent being waived.

Study population

CRC was included in this study on the basis of the 3rd Edition of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3). Data including age at diagnosis, gender, primary site, tumor grade, histology, marital status, T stage, N stage, tumor size, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels, presence of tumor deposits, and metastasis status were collected from cancer patients. The grade is determined by pathologic examination or degree of differentiation of the tumor. The lower grade means the higher degree of differentiation. The tumor deposit refers to the microscopic or macroscopic tumor nodules in the lymphatic drainage area of the primary tumor. It is discontinuous from the main tumor mass and lack histological evidence of residual lymph node, vascular, or neural structures. If there is no tumor deposit, it is recorded as negative. Patients with unclear data for the aforementioned variables were excluded from the study.

With regard to the presence or absence of liver metastasis, patients were grouped into two categories: the liver metastasis group consisted of CRC patients with liver metastasis, while the non-liver metastasis group was composed of patients without liver metastasis. The patients were then divided in a 7:3 ratio between the training and test sets. This ratio was chosen to balance the need for sufficient training data to build robust models while retaining an adequate test set for reliable performance evaluation. To ensure the proportional distribution of the outcome variable (liver metastasis status) was maintained in both subsets, we employed stratified random sampling based on this binary label. This procedure was implemented using the train_test_split function from the scikit-learn library in Python, with the stratify parameter explicitly set to the target variable.

Group comparisons for categorical data were conducted with the chi-square test. Model hyperparameters were optimized using a 5-fold cross-validated random search. To illustrate, the Gradient Boosting classifier was tuned over n_estimators [100, 200], learning_rate [0.01, 0.1, 0.2], and max_depth [3, 5, 7]. All other algorithms were tuned similarly, and the optimal hyperparameter combination for each was selected based on the highest mean AUC.

Handling of class imbalance

Given the inherent class imbalance in our dataset, wherein liver metastasis-positive cases represented a minority class, we adopted a dual-strategy approach to address this issue. First, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to generate synthetic instances for the minority class, thereby achieving a more balanced class distribution in the training set. SMOTE operates by creating new synthetic samples along the line segments connecting k nearest neighbors within the minority class, effectively expanding the decision space for this class. Second, we integrated cost-sensitive learning during model training by assigning higher misclassification penalties to the minority class, thus enhancing its classification priority.

Model development and performance

The eight machine learning methods, including Naive Bayes, XGBClassifier (XGBoost), MLPClassifier (MLP), Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting (GB), KNeighborsClassifier (KNN) and CatBoostClassifier (CatBoost) were applied to build a predictive model, which was then evaluated by using the test set. We used random hyperparameters to search for the optimal model parameters while calculating the average AUC value for each algorithm under 5-fold cross validation. When the data is an unbalanced data set, the AUC is less effective for assessing the model than the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR), so we plotted the precision- recall curve and calculated the AUPR, which was used to validate and complement the AUC values. To compare the predictive effectiveness of the models, calibration curves were plotted. The models were accurate if the calibration curves were close to the diagonal. In order to verify the clinical benefits, we subsequently plotted the decision curve analysis (DCA) curve. The SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) method was a framework developed upon additive feature attribution methods and used for visualization analysis to explain the impact of individual features on predictive models [6]. The closer a feature is to the top of the figure, the greater its total influence on the model output. A SHAP value is intuitively understood to be the feature’s contribution to the outcome value. A positive SHAP value suggests that this feature has a positive impact by improving the value of outcome, while a negative SHAP value indicates that it reduces the value of outcome and brings about negative effects. The higher the SHAP value of a feature, the greater the likelihood of liver metastasis occurrence. This method can output the importance ranking of the features along with their relationship to the outcome. Finally, we create a web calculator to facilitate the clinical dissemination and use of the optimal model.

Statistical analysis

The results of our analyses were conducted by Python software (version 3.12). The scikit-learn library (version 1.7.1) was used for machine learning implementation, with imbalanced-learn (version 0.14.0) for SMOTE oversampling. Model performance metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, AUC, and AUPR were calculated to comprehensively evaluate model performance on the imbalanced classification task.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the utility of machine learning in predicting liver metastasis in colorectal cancer patients using data from the SEER database. Our analysis revealed that the GB model outperformed other machine learning algorithms, achieving an AUC of 0.837 and an AUPR of 0.294, indicating strong discriminative ability even in an imbalanced dataset. These results suggest that machine learning, particularly ensemble methods like GB, can provide valuable tools for clinical decision-making in colorectal cancer management. External validation (AUC = 0.730) confirmed the model’s robustness while underscoring the inherent challenges associated with class-imbalanced prediction. The observed recall of 0.619 and precision of 0.229 (F1 = 0.334) reflect the expected performance trade-offs in identifying rare events, wherein the model prioritizes sensitivity to detect true cases of liver metastasis at the cost of reduced precision—a clinically justified approach given the serious consequences of missed diagnoses.

The SHAP analysis revealed that CEA level, N stage, and T stage were the most influential predictors in our model, which aligns well with established clinical knowledge. This alignment with conventional clinical parameters, while validating the model’s face validity, also indicates that the model primarily reinforces existing clinical understanding rather than uncovering novel high-risk markers. CEA has long been recognized as a valuable biomarker for colorectal cancer progression and metastasis [7], while T and N stages represent fundamental components of tumor staging that correlate strongly with metastatic potential [8, 9]. The strong weighting of N stage and T stage in our model is consistent with the established understanding that locally advanced CRC has a higher propensity for hematogenous spread to the liver [10]. The identification of these factors through our data-driven approach not only validates their clinical relevance but also demonstrates the model’s ability to capture biologically meaningful patterns from complex datasets.

Several recent studies have explored machine learning for CRC metastasis prediction. For example, Lee et al. [11] used deep learning to predict liver metastasis from histopathology images, achieving an AUC of 0.747 [10]. Our GB model’s superior performance (AUC 0.842) suggests that integrating clinical variables (e.g., CEA, TNM staging) may enhance predictive accuracy compared to imaging-only approaches. Another study by Cheng DX et al. developed a nomogram for CRC liver metastasis, reporting an AUC of 0.68 [12]. Our model’s higher discriminative ability may be attributed to the larger dataset (41,454 patients) and the use of SHAP for interpretability.

To enhance clinical utility, we developed an online risk calculator (available at: https://liver4-jgc82udpypdid6g4b7egry.streamlit.app/), enabling clinicians to input patient-specific variables and obtain individualized risk assessments. Such tools could facilitate early intervention strategies, such as intensified surveillance or adjuvant therapy, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Several aspects of our study design contributed to the robustness of our findings. First, the use of a large, population-based dataset from the SEER program enhanced the generalizability of our results. Second, we employed rigorous validation methods including independent training-test split validation with external cohort verification. Third, we complemented traditional performance metrics (AUC) with AUPR, which is more appropriate for imbalanced datasets [13], and added clinical utility assessment through decision curve analysis.

The development of an online calculator based on the GB model provides clinicians with a practical tool for evaluating the risk of liver metastasis in colorectal cancer patients. This tool could facilitate early identification of high-risk patients, enabling timely intervention and personalized treatment strategies [14, 15]. By integrating predictive analytics into clinical decision-making, this approach has the potential to improve patient outcomes and optimize resource allocation.

The external validation performed on the Ningbo No.2 Hospital cohort constitutes a rigorous test of the model’s applicability in a distinctly different clinical environment. As presented in Table 1, this validation cohort exhibited notable demographic and clinical divergences from the SEER‑based training set, including a higher proportion of male and older patients, a substantially elevated rate of rectal cancers, and an almost uniformly documented marital status. Such variations reflect genuine heterogeneity across healthcare systems and patient populations in real‑world practice. The model’s retained discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.730) on this heterogeneous external set affirms the robustness of its key predictors—CEA level, T stage, and N stage—which appear to capture fundamental biological risk signals that are largely independent of specific population characteristics. However, the observed attenuation from the internal test AUC (0.837) suggests that model performance may be influenced by population differences, particularly in the distribution of the primary tumor site, which may serve as a proxy for unmeasured confounders like local treatment protocols or molecular profiles. In order to further improve generalizability and guarantee equitable performance across a range of patient groups, future model development may benefit from incorporating more diverse, multi-institutional data. This finding highlights the significance of local calibration prior to clinical implementation in new settings.

Our evaluation included metrics specifically sensitive to the challenges of our dataset. The Brier score of 0.167 indicates room for improvement in the model’s probability calibration. This is likely attributable to the severe class imbalance, where well-calibrated probabilities for the rare, positive class are notoriously difficult to achieve, and potentially to the exclusion of more granular predictive features. To improve calibration, future work could employ post-processing techniques such as Platt scaling or isotonic regression [16] specifically on the minority class, or integrate calibration-aware loss functions during training. Additionally, the AUPR values (0.278 externally and 0.294 internally) clearly show how difficult it is to predict the uncommon event of liver metastasis (7.18% prevalence). While the model’s high recall (0.774) demonstrates effective sensitivity in identifying potential cases, the modest AUPR underscores the precision-recall trade-off inherent to imbalanced classification. The strategies we employed, including SMOTE and cost-sensitive learning, were designed to mitigate this imbalance and prioritize sensitivity, a clinically prudent choice given the high cost of missing a liver metastasis. Future models could use sophisticated techniques like deep generative models for augmenting synthetic data, ensemble methods designed for imbalance (like Balanced Random Forests), or the addition of new, highly discriminative biomarkers like circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) to further improve minority class recognition.

Limitation

Despite the robust performance of the GB model, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective nature of both the SEER database and the Ningbo No.2 Hospital cohort introduce inherent limitations, including potential selection biases and unmeasured confounding variables. Data collection relied on previously recorded clinical information, which may not capture all relevant clinical factors that could influence liver metastasis risk. Second, although the external validation cohort from Ningbo No.2 Hospital (n = 3121) provided preliminary evidence of generalizability, its relatively small sample size compared to the SEER dataset may limit the robustness of external validation. More importantly, the generalizability of our model beyond Chinese and U.S. populations remains uncertain. The model’s performance in other ethnic groups and healthcare systems requires further validation, as genetic backgrounds, environmental factors, and clinical practices may vary substantially across different regions. Third, the Brier score of 0.167 suggests that while our model has good discriminatory power, there is room for improvement in probability calibration. This may be attributed to the severe class imbalance in our dataset, where liver metastasis-positive cases constituted only 7.18% of the total population. Fourth, while CEA was identified as a vital predictor, other potential factors such as genetic markers or additional biomarkers were not explored in this study. Furthermore, the model did not incorporate more advanced data modalities such as radiomic features from imaging or comprehensive genomic profiles, which may hold significant predictive value. The absence of these potentially significant predictors may contribute to the model’s shortcomings in accurately stratifying high-risk patients and limit its comprehensiveness for clinical decision-making. Finally, the exclusion of cases with missing data, while methodologically necessary, may have introduced additional selection biases that could affect the model’s real-world performance and generalizability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the potential of machine learning in predicting liver metastasis in colorectal cancer patients. The GB model, with its high accuracy and diagnostic efficiency, offers a promising tool for clinical practice. Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying biological mechanisms of the identified predictors to enhance model interpretability and fully realize their potential for improving patient outcomes.
