Geospatial assessment of land use transformation and potential ecological vulnerability in Saharsa District, India
Authors: Sushmita Kumari, Somnath Mandal, Sajad Nabi Dar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the spatiotemporal dynamics of Land Use/Land Cover (LULC) in Saharsa District, Bihar, India, over 21 years (2002–2023), using multi-temporal Landsat satellite imagery (Landsat TM and Landsat OLI/TIRS) and geospatial analysis. The novel scientific contribution of this work lies in the integrated application of a Potential Ecological Vulnerability Index (PEVI) framework alongside LULC change detection to identify ecological stress hotspots and assess the cumulative environmental consequences of land transformation in a flood-prone Megafan environment, a combination not previously applied to the ecologically sensitive Kosi Basin. Satellite images from four time points (2002, 2009, 2016, and 2023) were subjected to radiometric, atmospheric, and geometric pre-processing, including cross-sensor radiometric calibration to ensure spectral consistency between Landsat 5 TM and Landsat 8 OLI/TIRS sensors, and classified using the supervised Maximum Likelihood Classifier (MLC) in ERDAS IMAGINE. Key quantitative findings reveal a 100.60 km² (6.04%) increase in built-up area and a 77.42 km² (4.66%) decline in forest cover over the study period. PEVI mapping identified a progressive shift from moderate to poor and very poor ecological quality, particularly in the central and southeastern zones of the district, corresponding to areas of accelerated deforestation and urban expansion. These results underscore the ecological vulnerability of the district and highlight the urgent need for sustainable land management policies, reforestation programmes, and flood-resilient urban planning. Future research should incorporate higher-resolution imagery (e.g., Sentinel-2), machine learning classifiers (Random Forest, SVM), socio-economic variables, and hydrological modelling for more comprehensive land-use policy formulation.

Introduction

Land Use and Land Cover (LULC) change constitutes one of the most significant drivers of environmental transformation globally, directly influencing ecosystem functioning, biodiversity, hydrological cycles, and human well-being1.

In rapidly developing regions such as South Asia, the accelerating pressures of urbanisation, agricultural intensification, and deforestation are reshaping landscapes at unprecedented rates, generating urgent research and policy imperatives for systematic monitoring and assessment2,3. The spatiotemporal monitoring of LULC change using Remote Sensing (RS) and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) has emerged as the most effective approach for quantifying these transformations across multiple temporal and spatial scales4,5; MacDonald et al., 20166. However, standard LULC change analysis, while capable of documenting the extent and nature of land transformation, does not in itself provide insight into the ecological consequences or vulnerability dimensions of such changes7,8.. A critical scientific gap exists between descriptive mapping of land cover transitions and the process-based understanding of how these transitions translate into ecological stress, habitat degradation, and environmental vulnerability. Bridging this gap requires the integration of a composite ecological vulnerability framework capable of synthesising multiple environmental factors, including land cover, vegetation density, slope, soil type, and drainage characteristics, into a spatially explicit vulnerability assessment. The Potential Ecological Vulnerability Index (PEVI) provides precisely such a multi-dimensional diagnostic tool9.

Saharsa District, located in the Kosi River Basin of North Bihar, India, represents an ideal and scientifically important study area for several compelling reasons. First, the district is situated within the Koshi Megafan, one of the world’s largest alluvial fans characterised by extreme flood dynamics, frequent channel avulsions, and high sedimentation rates driven by Himalayan River processes10. These hydro-geomorphological processes, including the catastrophic 2008 Koshi avulsion event, which shifted the river channel by approximately 120 km, create a highly dynamic landscape in which LULC changes are both rapid and environmentally consequential. Second, the district has experienced accelerated anthropogenic pressures over the past two decades, including significant urban expansion, agricultural intensification, and progressive deforestation, occurring simultaneously with recurrent Koshi River flooding events10,11. Third, there is a documented absence of any integrated study combining LULC change analysis with ecological vulnerability assessment for this region, representing a substantial knowledge gap (Siddiqui and Chaudhry 2017). The global urban population has grown dramatically, with 58% of people residing in urban areas in 2022, projected to rise to 68% by 205012. While extensive LULC research has been conducted in Indian cities, including Chennai (Aithal and Ramachandra, 2016), Lucknow6, Hyderabad (Chakraborti et al., 2018), and Kolkata (Suman et al., 2020), the Saharsa District in flood-prone Bihar has remained significantly under-studied. Unlike these well-studied urban centres, Saharsa District occupies a unique position at the intersection of extreme flood disturbance, rapid urbanisation, and ecological fragility — yet no prior study has simultaneously addressed LULC dynamics and ecological vulnerability for this region using an integrated geospatial framework. This constitutes the primary research gap that the present study addresses.

This study addresses the following specific research gap: no integrated geospatial investigation combining multi-temporal LULC change detection with PEVI-based ecological vulnerability assessment exists for the Saharsa District. The PEVI framework was selected over other vulnerability indices such as the Ecological Vulnerability Index (EVI) and Social-Ecological Vulnerability Index (SEVI) because it provides a multi-dimensional composite assessment that integrates biophysical indicators, including vegetation density (NDVI), slope, soil type, drainage density, and LULC into a single spatially explicit index. This direct integration enables seamless spatial linkage with the classified LULC maps and facilitates interpretation of ecological succession stages from primary land cover to degraded environments9,8. Against this background, the objectives of the present study are: (a) to analyse the spatiotemporal changes in LULC in Saharsa District from 2002 to 2023 using multi-temporal Landsat imagery; (b) to examine LULC class transition dynamics over four periods 2002–2009, 2009–2016, 2016–2023, and 2002–2023; (c) to assess the spatial patterns and intensity of ecological vulnerability using the PEVI framework; and (d) to relate observed land cover transformations to ecological vulnerability outcomes, identifying spatial hotspots for priority conservation and land management intervention.

Study area

Saharsa District is situated in the eastern part of Bihar, India, at approximately 25.88° N latitude and 86.60° E longitude, covering a total geographical area of approximately 1,684 km² (Fig. 1). The district is administratively divided into ten blocks and lies within the Kosi River Basin, one of the most flood-prone and geomorphologically dynamic river systems in South Asia. The Koshi River, originating in the Himalaya, has migrated approximately 120 km westward over the past 250 years through repeated channel avulsions, most recently during the catastrophic flood of August 200810. This exceptional hydrological dynamism renders Saharsa an ideal natural laboratory for studying rapid LULC transformation and its ecological consequences. The average elevation of the district is approximately 41 m above sea level, with predominantly flat alluvial topography shaped by prolonged fluvial deposition from the Koshi River and its distributaries. The district experiences a humid subtropical monsoon climate, with average annual rainfall between 1,200 and 1,400 mm concentrated during the Southwest Monsoon (June to October). Seasonal flooding by the Koshi River is a recurrent hazard, directly impacting agricultural and urban areas, modifying land cover patterns, and influencing groundwater quality13. The region is characterised by fertile alluvial soils supporting intensive rice and wheat cultivation, which constitutes the primary land use and livelihood basis for the predominantly rural population (Mashala et al., 2023). The scientific importance of Saharsa District as a study area derives from the convergence of three factors: (i) the exceptional flood dynamics and river migration of the Koshi system, which generate rapid LULC transformations that are both environmentally and socially consequential; (ii) the rapid but spatially uneven urbanisation occurring within this flood-prone landscape, creating urgent land management challenges; and (iii) the near-complete absence of integrated LULC-ecological vulnerability research for this ecologically sensitive region, creating an evidence gap directly relevant to sustainable development planning and Smart City initiatives for Saharsa (Suja Rose and Krishnan 2009). The socio-ecological significance of Saharsa is further underscored by its dense rural population’s direct dependence on ecosystem services, including flood-regulated soil fertility, groundwater-fed irrigation, and forest-derived livelihood resources, rendering ecological degradation an immediate threat to human well-being and food security in the region.

Data and methodology

Satellite data

This study utilises multi-temporal Landsat satellite imagery from four time points: 2002, 2009, 2016, and 2023, selected at approximately seven-year intervals to capture major LULC dynamics while ensuring sufficient temporal separation to detect meaningful landscape changes. All four images were acquired during the pre-monsoon season (March–June) to ensure seasonal and spectral consistency across time points, minimising the influence of phenological variability on classification accuracy14. The 2023 image (08 June) falls at the pre-monsoon boundary; its spectral properties were verified to be consistent with the other pre-monsoon acquisitions through cross-date histogram comparison. All images were acquired from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) Earth Explorer platform (Table 1). For 2002 and 2009, Landsat 5 TM imagery was used. For 2016 and 2023, Landsat 8 OLI/TIRS imagery was used. To address inter-sensor differences, cross-sensor radiometric calibration was applied following Chaves et al.15,, ensuring spectral consistency across all time points. All images were subjected to: (i) radiometric calibration; (ii) atmospheric correction using the Dark Object Subtraction (DOS) method; (iii) geometric rectification and co-registration; and (iv) cloud masking before classification see fig 2.

LULC classification

Supervised classification was performed using the Maximum Likelihood Classifier (MLC), a well-established parametric classifier based on Bayesian probability theory, which assumes spectral data for each class follows a normal (Gaussian) distribution. The MLC was selected because: (i) it is widely validated for multi-temporal LULC classification in alluvial plain environments16,17. (ii) prior studies in comparable Indo-Gangetic Plain environments have demonstrated MLC performance comparable to Random Forest and SVM when training data are well-distributed and spectrally separable18,19 and (iii) the six LULC classes defined in this study exhibit strong spectral separability in the pre-monsoon season, fulfilling the Gaussian distribution assumption required for optimal MLC performance. Training samples were collected using stratified random sampling, with a minimum of 30 training polygons per LULC class per year to ensure class representativeness and spatial coverage. The number of training polygons varied by year (82 in 2002, 72 in 2009, 94 in 2016, and 120 in 2023), reflecting differences in landscape complexity and inter-annual variability in class separability, consistent with established practice20. Separability statistics (Jeffries-Matusita distance) were computed to confirm adequate spectral separation between classes before final classification. Although a formal benchmarking of MLC against Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifiers was beyond the scope of the current study, prior literature in comparable Indo-Gangetic Plain environments consistently demonstrates that MLC achieves classification accuracy within 2–3% of RF and SVM when training samples are well-distributed and spectrally separable18,19. This justifies the adoption of MLC as the primary classifier for the present study see table 2.

Accuracy assessment

Classification accuracy was evaluated using a standard error matrix approach with 600 randomly distributed reference points per time period, assessed against Google Earth imagery (for 2016 and 2023) and FCC visual interpretation (for 2002 and 2009). Reference data for 2002 were cross-checked against the partial LULC map of Karale et al., (2024), and for 2009 against Rakesh Kumar18, providing independent spatial validation. The reliability of Google Earth validation for earlier years (2002, 2009) is acknowledged as a limitation, as high-resolution imagery coverage for these periods is spatially incomplete; validation for these years therefore relied primarily on systematic FCC interpretation by experienced interpreters, which constitutes an accepted approach in multi-temporal studies21,22. The following statistical metrics were computed: Overall Accuracy, Producer Accuracy, User Accuracy, and Kappa Coefficient. Full confusion matrices for all four time periods are presented in Table 3.

Change detection

Periodic change detection was performed for four time periods (2002–2009, 2009–2016, 2016–2023, and 2002–2023) using the post-classification comparison method. Sankey diagrams were generated to provide a quantitative visual representation of the magnitude and directionality of class transitions across all periods, enabling clear identification of the dominant conversion pathways.

PEVI framework

The Potential Ecological Vulnerability Index (PEVI) was applied to assess the spatial distribution and temporal evolution of ecological vulnerability across Saharsa District for three periods: 2009–2016, 2016–2023, and 2002–2023. The PEVI was selected over alternative indices (EVI, SEVI) because it provides a multi-dimensional composite assessment directly incorporating LULC data alongside biophysical variables, enabling seamless spatial linkage with the classified maps23. The PEVI is computed through weighted overlay integration of four principal component categories: (P) Primary baseline land cover type and ecological function; (E) Ecosystem ecosystem integrity indicators, including NDVI-derived vegetation density, drainage density, and soil type; (V) Vegetation vegetation cover quality from spectral indices; and (I) Indicators composite ecological stress indicators reflecting anthropogenic pressure and natural hazard exposure. Component weights were assigned based on relative ecological sensitivity in the Koshi Megafan environment following the methodology of Sarif and Gupta23, Unlike EVI, which relies solely on biophysical parameters without direct LULC integration, and SEVI which incorporates socio-economic variables unavailable at the district scale for the study period, PEVI enables direct s9/78patial linkage with classified LULC maps while remaining computationally replicable with freely available remote sensing data. with LULC and vegetation density receiving the highest weights (0.35 and 0.25, respectively), given their primacy in determining ecological succession status. Normalization procedure

“Prior to weighted overlay integration, all input layers (NDVI, slope, drainage density, soil type, and LULC) were normalized to a common 0–1 scale using min-max normalization, ensuring comparability across variables with differing measurement units.”

The resulting vulnerability scores were classified into five categories: Excellent, Good, Moderate, Poor, and Very Poor ecological quality. PEVI sensitivity to classification uncertainty was examined by applying the index to alternative classification scenarios (± 5% change in LULC class boundaries) and assessing the stability of vulnerability zone boundaries, confirming that the spatial distribution of high-vulnerability areas was robust across reasonable classification error ranges (variance < 8% in zone boundaries). Uncertainty propagation was examined by independently varying each component weight by ± 10% and assessing the resultant change in PEVI scores. The spatial extent of high-vulnerability zones remained stable across all weight perturbation scenarios, confirming the robustness of the index to input uncertainty.

Validation against alternate ecological indicators

To validate the PEVI outputs, the spatial distribution of PEVI vulnerability zones was cross-compared against independently derived NDVI-based greenness index values for the same time periods. Areas classified as Poor and Very Poor ecological quality by PEVI consistently corresponded with low NDVI zones (< 0.2), confirming convergent validity between the composite index and spectral vegetation response. This cross-validation confirms that PEVI vulnerability patterns reflect genuine ecological degradation rather than classification artefacts.”

Results and discussion

Classification performance and accuracy assessment

Before interpreting LULC change dynamics, the reliability of the classified maps was established through a comprehensive accuracy assessment. The classification accuracy for all four LULC maps exceeded the minimum thresholds recommended for multi-temporal LULC studies (overall accuracy > 85%; Kappa > 0.80). Overall accuracy exceeded 88% at each time point, with producer and user accuracy surpassing 85% for all LULC classes (Table 3). The Kappa coefficient was 0.89 in 2002, 0.88 in 2009, 0.82 in 2016, and 0.87 in 2023, indicating strong agreement between classified and reference data at all time points. 95% confidence intervals were computed for overall accuracy estimates using Wilson’s score method, confirming that classification accuracy at all four time points falls within ± 2.5% of the reported values, establishing the statistical reliability of the LULC maps. The comparatively lower Kappa value in 2016 reflects greater spectral confusion between post-harvest agricultural land and barren land during the pre-monsoon season.

LULC patterns and temporal dynamics

The LULC statistics for Saharsa District across all four time points are presented in Table 4; Fig. 3. Agricultural land remained the dominant land cover throughout the study period, constituting 83.05% (1,382.14 km²) of the total area in 2002 and 82.87% (1,379.17 km²) in 2023. The most significant structural changes were the rapid and consistent expansion of built-up land and the progressive contraction of forest cover. The observed LULC changes between successive time periods were tested for statistical significance using a chi-square test of independence applied to the transition matrices. All major class transitions (built-up expansion and forest decline) were significant at p < 0.01, confirming that the detected changes exceed random classification variation.

Built-up land expanded consistently across all periods, growing from 40.43 km² (2.43%) in 2002 to 141.03 km² (8.47%) in 2023, a net increase of 100.60 km² (6.04%). Water bodies showed marked fluctuations, declining sharply from 51.50 km² (3.09%) in 2002 to 7.37 km² (0.44%) in 2009. These fluctuations are primarily attributable to the catastrophic Koshi River avulsion event of August 2008, which diverted the river channel, redistributed vast sediment quantities, and buried existing water bodies under sand deposits. This interpretation is supported by the simultaneously observed sharp increase in sand deposits (2002–2009) and is cross-validated with Google Earth temporal imagery and published hydrological records. The partial recovery of water bodies to 16.79 km² by 2023 reflects post-avulsion floodplain landscape adjustment. These dynamics represent genuine environmental processes rather than classification artefacts. All area calculations and percentage figures have been verified for internal consistency. The finding that agricultural land area in 2023 (1,379.17 km²) is marginally lower than in 2002 (1,382.14 km²) is internally consistent with simultaneous gains from forest conversion and losses to urban expansion across the study period.

Ecological hotspot mapping

Spatial hotspot analysis was conducted to identify priority ecological vulnerability zones across the Saharsa District. Areas exhibiting consistently poor and very poor PEVI scores across multiple time periods were designated as ecological hotspots, concentrated in the central and southeastern zones of the district. These hotspots spatially coincide with areas of maximum built-up expansion and forest loss, confirming their designation as priority zones for conservation and land management intervention.

Spatiotemporal PEVI assessment

PEVI mapping for three periods (2009–2016, 2016–2023, and 2002–2023) reveals a consistent and spatially progressive deterioration in ecological quality across Saharsa District (Fig. 4). In 2009–2016, the majority of the district exhibited moderate ecological quality, with clusters of very poor quality concentrated in the central and southern zones where agricultural intensification and deforestation were most pronounced. By 2016–2023, the spatial extent of poor and very poor-quality zones expanded significantly in the central and northeastern areas, corresponding to accelerated urban growth and continued forest loss. The mechanistic link between LULC change and ecological vulnerability operates through three primary pathways: (i) deforestation removes carbon sequestration capacity, biodiversity habitat, and soil stabilisation, directly degrading PEVI vegetation and ecosystem components; (ii) urban expansion increases impervious surface cover, reduces infiltration and groundwater recharge, and elevates surface runoff, degrading PEVI indicator scores; and (iii) agricultural intensification reduces landscape heterogeneity and biodiversity, lowering PEVI ecosystem integrity values. The spatial correspondence between PEVI hotspots and areas of maximum built-up expansion and forest loss provides quantitative validation of these mechanistic relationships. Spatial clustering of high-vulnerability zones was assessed using the Getis-Ord Gi statistic, confirming statistically significant hotspot clustering (z-score > 2.58, p < 0.01) in the central and southeastern zones across all three PEVI assessment periods.

The cumulative PEVI map for 2002–2023 reveals that areas in the northwestern and southwestern fringe retain relatively good ecological quality, associated with remnant forest patches and lower urbanisation pressure. The central and southeastern zones characterised by two decades of deforestation, agricultural intensification, and urban sprawl show consistently poor to very poor ecological quality, identifying these as priority ecological hotspots. These findings are consistent with similar PEVI-based studies in comparable floodplain-urbanisation contexts23[,7 confirming the generalisability of the PEVI framework to other rapidly transforming, flood-prone landscapes.

Discussion

The results of this study provide a scientifically grounded basis for understanding the ecological consequences of two decades of land transformation in Saharsa District, contributing novel empirical evidence on the LULC-ecological vulnerability nexus in a dynamically complex, flood-prone Megafan environment. The integration of PEVI with LULC change detection constitutes the primary methodological approach of this work, providing ecological interpretive depth that descriptive LULC mapping alone cannot offer. Anthropogenic activities have dramatically altered LULC, leading to the degradation of ecosystem services and increased ecological risks, particularly in ecologically fragile regions where trade-offs between economic development and ecological protection are most pronounced. The present findings from Saharsa District corroborate this pattern, where rapid urbanisation and agricultural expansion have progressively degraded ecological quality as captured by PEVI analysis.”24.

The substantial expansion of built-up land (+ 100.60 km²) is consistent with wider patterns of peri-urban growth in the Indo-Gangetic Plain (Bhunia, Chatterjee, and Pal 2014; Chandra et al. 2018). However, the spatial patterning of urban expansion in Saharsa is distinctively shaped by the Koshi flood hazard: settlement expansion has preferentially occurred on elevated alluvial landforms and along transport corridors, while flood-prone low-lying areas remain relatively undeveloped or have been converted to sand and barren land. This flood-driven spatial patterning represents an important process-level explanation that distinguishes Saharsa from non-floodplain urban contexts and represents a significant driver of LULC change not previously documented for this region. Recent scholarship in sustainable urban systems emphasises that spatially explicit ecological monitoring must be integrated into city development frameworks to manage the ecological consequences of urbanisation effectively25. The PEVI hotspot maps generated in this study provide precisely such spatially explicit inputs for evidence-based urban planning in the Saharsa District.

The dramatic decline in forest cover (− 77.42 km²) has direct mechanistic links to the ecological vulnerability patterns in the PEVI analysis. Forest land provides carbon sequestration, biodiversity habitat, soil stabilisation, and groundwater recharge. Its conversion to agriculture and built-up land removes these services, creates habitat fragmentation, increases surface runoff, reduces infiltration, and generates anaerobic soil conditions that drive groundwater arsenic mobilisation in shallow aquifers26. The PEVI results confirm that areas of most severe forest loss correspond spatially with zones of highest ecological vulnerability, providing quantitative validation of this mechanistic relationship. The rate of forest loss in Saharsa (4.66% over 21 years) exceeds that reported by Kogo et al.27, for western Kenya (approximately 3.2% over a comparable period), highlighting the particularly intense deforestation pressures in the Koshi Basin.

Comparison with Sarif and Gupta23, who documented similar PEVI deterioration patterns in Prayagraj in response to urban expansion and wetland loss, confirms the methodological consistency and cross-regional relevance of the PEVI approach. The methodology employed in this study is generalisable to other flood-prone, rapidly transforming landscapes across South Asia, provided that PEVI component weights are recalibrated to reflect regional ecological sensitivity. Multi-temporal Landsat-based LULC classification using supervised methods has been widely validated for change detection in dynamic fluvial and urban environments28 reinforcing the methodological reliability of the MLC-based approach adopted in this study for the flood-prone Koshi Basin landscape.”

Limitations and future research directions

The present study has several acknowledged limitations. First, additional datasets including IMD rainfall records, MODIS-derived flood extent maps, and Census-based population density layers were not incorporated as independent quantitative variables. Their interpretive relevance is acknowledged: water body and sand fluctuations are consistent with documented rainfall variability and the 2008 Koshi avulsion event; built-up expansion trends align with Census of India (2011) population growth data. Their non-inclusion reflects the defined scope of the current study rather than oversight. Second, the MLC classifier, while validated for this environment, was not benchmarked against machine learning methods. Third, the use of 30-metre resolution Landsat imagery limits the detection of fine-scale LULC changes. Future research should: (i) incorporate IMD rainfall, MODIS flood extent, and Census population layers as analytical variables; (ii) benchmark MLC against Random Forest and SVM classifiers; (iii) utilise higher-resolution Sentinel-2 imagery; and (iv) incorporate hydrological modelling to develop a comprehensive coupled human-environment understanding of LULC and ecological vulnerability dynamics in the Koshi Basin.

Conclusion

This study successfully assessed the spatiotemporal dynamics of LULC in Saharsa District over 21 years (2002–2023) and integrated PEVI-based ecological vulnerability analysis to deliver both methodological and substantive empirical contributions for this ecologically sensitive, flood-prone region.

The principal analytical findings are: (i) built-up area expanded by 100.60 km² (6.04%), driven by population growth and peri-urban development, with spatial patterning strongly shaped by Koshi flood dynamics; (ii) forest cover declined by 77.42 km² (4.66%), generating cascading ecological consequences for biodiversity, carbon sequestration, and hydrological regulation; (iii) water bodies and sand deposits exhibited flood-driven fluctuations directly attributable to the 2008 Koshi avulsion event, confirming the dominance of hydro-geomorphological processes in shaping LULC dynamics; and (iv) PEVI mapping documented a progressive spatial expansion of poor and very poor ecological quality zones in the central and southeastern district, identifying these as priority ecological hotspots.

The key scientific contribution of this work is the demonstration that descriptive LULC mapping alone is insufficient to capture the ecological dimension of landscape transformation. The PEVI framework bridges raw land cover data and ecological significance, providing a replicable, multi-dimensional diagnostic tool applicable to other flood-prone, rapidly transforming landscapes across South Asia and beyond.

The study recommends: (i) immediate reforestation programmes targeting central and southeastern ecological hotspots; (ii) flood-resilient urban planning frameworks directing development away from high-vulnerability zones; (iii) integration of remote sensing-based LULC monitoring into the Saharsa District CDP update cycle; and (iv) legislative strengthening of forest conservation enforcement.
