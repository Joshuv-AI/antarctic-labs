Clinical and Genetic Characteristics of KCNT1-Related Epilepsy in Infancy
Authors: Fatema K, Akhter KU, Alam ST, Paul BC, Ahmed S, Islam KA, Ferdousi J
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeKCNT1, a gene which encodes sodium gated potassium channels, is associated with a spectrum of epilepsies and neurological disorders. This study has been done to overview the phenotype and genotype of KCNT1 related epilepsies in infancy.MethodThis observational, cross-sectional study was conducted in the Department of Pediatric Neurology, Bangladesh Medical University. Detailed history taking and clinical examinations were done. Electroencephalogram and magnetic resonance imaging of the brain were done in each patient. The classification and description of seizures and epilepsy was done according to the international league against epilepsy guideline. Genetic test was done by targeted next-generation sequencing.ResultThis study included six patients with developmental epileptic encephalopathy with KCNT1 gene mutation. The children had various types of seizures, mostly focal seizure, focal with bilateral tonic-clonic seizure, epileptic spasm and status epilepticus. Most patients had global developmental delay with normal birth history. In all patients, whole exome sequencing was done and KCNT1 mutation had been found.ConclusionsThis study describes the genotype and phenotype of KCNT1 related epilepsy in infancy.Keywords: KCNT1 gene, Epilepsy, Phenotype

Introduction

KCNT1 encodes a sodium-gated potassium channel which regulates the neuronal firing rate, contributing to the slow hyperpolarization after repetitive firing. This gene also plays an important role in the neuronal response to hypoxia. The pathological mutation of KCNT1 gene is related to two distinct types of epilepsy: autosomal dominant sleep-related hypermotor epilepsy and epilepsy in infancy with migrating focal seizures (EIMFS) in neonates and infants.1–3

The characteristic features of EIMFS are seizures, which are focal and asynchronous. The age of onset is within the first 6 months of life. There is developmental stagnation or regression after the onset of the seizure, with some patients exhibiting autonomic manifestations like perioral cyanosis, flushing and apnea. The seizure is usually drug resistant.4 Apart from this, patients present with profound mental retardation, spastic tetra paresis, as well as microcephaly and macrosomia.5

However, there are some uncommon phenotypes associated with KCNT1 related epilepsy like infantile spasms, early infantile epileptic encephalopathy (Ohtahara syndrome, early myoclonic encephalopathy), leukodystrophy etc. Apart from this, some neurodevelopmental features associated with the KCNT1 gene mutation are microcephaly, hypotonia, squint, global developmental delay and dystonia. In some patients, extra-neurological features like cardiac arrhythmia, pulmonary hemorrhage are found.6 Moreover, dysmorphic features such as sloping forehead, long philtrum, thin upper lip, and long slender fingers are occasionally present in these cases.7

Very few cases of KCNT1 mutation have been reported worldwide. To contribute to the limited existing cases, we are publishing a case series of KCNT1-related epilepsy in early infancy from Bangladesh.

Methods

This observational, cross-sectional, single-centered study was conducted in the Department of Pediatric Neurology, Bangladesh Medical University (formerly known as The Bangabandhu Sheikh Mujib Medical University). A total of six children who have suspected genetic epilepsy (without any structural/metabolic/neurocutaneous/vitamin responsive seizure) was taken as participants. The study period was from July 2023 to December 2025. Institutional review board clearance and informed written consent from all the parents of the patients were taken (BSMMU/2020/1042).

Detailed history taking and clinical examinations were done. History related to the type of seizures, frequency, age of onset, perinatal details, family history, developmental history, and details of ongoing treatment were noted. Electroencephalogram (EEG) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain were done in each patient. A baseline complete blood count, electrolytes, liver function tests, renal function tests, and blood glucose tests were performed. The classification and description of seizures and epilepsy was done according to the recent International League Against Epilepsy guideline.8

Genetic test was done by targeted next-generation sequencing. Selective capture and sequencing of the protein-coding regions of the genome/genes were performed. DNA extracted from blood was used to perform targeted gene capture using a custom capture kit. The libraries were sequenced to mean >80–100X coverage on the Illumina sequencing platform. The genomic analysis toolkit best practices framework was followed to identify variants in the sample using Sentieon ver 201808.07 (https://support.sentieon.com/versions/201808.07/manual/introduction/intro/). The sequences obtained were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p13) using the Sentieon aligner and analyzed to remove duplicates, do recalibration, and realign indels.

Sentieon haplotype caller was used to identify variants relevant to the clinical indication. Gene annotation of the variants was performed using the Variant Effect Predictor program against the Ensembl release 99 human gene model (http://beta.ensembl.org/). In addition to single-nucleotide variant and small indels, copy number variants (CNVs) were detected from targeted sequence data using the ExomeDepth ver 1.1.10 (https://usegalaxy.org/?tool_id=toolshed.g2.bx.psu.edu%2Frepos%2Fcrs4%2Fexomedepth%2Fexomedepth%2F1.1.0) method. This algorithm detects rare CNVs based on a comparison of the read-depths of the test data with the matched aggregate reference dataset.

Clinically relevant mutations were annotated using published variants in the literature and a set of disease databases, including ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), OMIM (https://www.omim.org/; updated on Feb 20, 2020), genome-wide association studies, the Human Gene Mutation Database ver 2019.4 (https://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/), and SwissVar (http://www.expasy.org/swissvar). Common variants were filtered based on allele frequency in 1000 Genome Phase 3, gnomAD ver 2.1 (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/news/2018-10-gnomad-v2-1/), Exome Variant Server (http://evs.gs.washington.edu/EVS/), Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database ver 151 (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/databasecommons/database/id/1622), 1000 Japanese Genome, and internal population database. The non-synonymous variant’s effect was calculated using multiple algorithms, such as Polymorphism Phenotyping v2 (https://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant (https://incodom.kr/SIFT), MutationTaster2 (https://www.mutationtaster.org/), and likelihood ratio test. Only non-synonymous and splice site variants found in the clinical exome panel consisting of 6,670 genes were used for clinical interpretation. Silent variations that do not change amino acids in the coding region were not reported.

The genetic variant classification was done according to the 2015 the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomic/the Association for Molecular Pathology guidelines, published by Richards et al.9 (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance [VUS], likely benign, benign). After genetic test, six patients had pathogenic mutation.

Result

Case 1: a 5-month-old boy, the third issue of his non consanguineous parents, presented with repeated episodes of seizures from 3 days after birth. Seizures were initially focal clonic (consciousness preserved), followed by focal tonic involving right upper and lower limbs. Seizures usually persisted for 1–2 minutes, occurring 5–6 times per day. Subsequently, the seizure became migratory in nature, moving from right upper limbs to left lower limbs, and then to other limbs. Later on, he developed epileptic spasm occurring in clusters. He also had some autonomic features like sweating, vomiting etc. His birth history was uneventful and he had global developmental delay. Developmental age was around 6 weeks. On examination, he had microcephaly. EEG showed a burst suppression pattern and MRI of brain showed periventricular leukomalacia. The genetic study showed pathogenic mutation of KCNT1 gene. This patient was treated with phenobarbitone, levetiracetam, oral steroids, and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). Seizure burden was high at one year follow up (Tables 1, 2).

Case 2: a 10-month-old male child of non-consanguineous parents was admitted with focal tonic seizures (consciousness preserved) since 3 months of age. Gradually, he developed repeated blank spells, abnormal eye movement and ultimately clusters of epileptic spasm. He had no perinatal insult and had global delay. EEG showed multifocal discharges. MRI of the brain showed mild atrophy in fronto-parietal regions. Genetic study showed a likely pathogenic mutation in exon 15 of the KCNT1 gene. The patient was treated with ACTH, vigabatrin, topiramate, levetiracetam and lacosomide. At 17 months of age, he had microcephaly, global delay and infrequent seizure mostly focal in nature.

Case 3: a 12-month-old boy of non-consanguineous parents was admitted with focal seizures which were migratory in nature and started from 1.5 months of age. He had normal birth history with no perinatal insult. Developmental history revealed motor delay and EEG reports showed focal epileptic discharges from multiple areas of both hemispheres with poor background activity. MRI of brain was normal. Genetic study showed a likely pathogenic mutation of KCNT1 gene, leading to diagnosis of early infantile epileptic encephalopathy. The boy was treated with carbamazepine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine, lacosamide etc. He was followed up to 2 years of age, during which he had cognitive delay, but was able to sit. He had occasional focal seizures.

Case 4: a 5-month-old girl of non-consanguineous parents, exhibited focal tonic seizures (consciousness impaired) since 3 months of age. Later on, she developed focal clonic seizures which migrated from left to right limbs and the face. She was delivered by normal vaginal delivery with no perinatal insult and she had global delay. EEG reports showed a burst suppression pattern and MRI of the brain showed nonspecific findings. Genetic study lead to diagnosis of likely pathogenic mutation of KCNT1 gene. The patient was treated with levetiracetam, topiramate, oxcarbazepine, and zonisamide with minimal seizure control. At 1.5 years of age, she had global delay with frequent seizure.

Case 5: a 1-year-old male of non-consanguineous parents presented with seizure since 1 month of age, with the seizure being focal to bilateral tonic-clonic in nature. Seizure was nonresponsive to multiple antiepileptic drugs. He was delivered by lower uterine segment cesarean section and had neonatal jaundice. He had global delay. At 1 year of age, he had intractable seizures, including both focal and generalized types. EEG reports showed ictal focal epileptic discharges and burst suppression pattern, along with poor background activity. MRI of the brain showed cortical atrophy. Genetic study showed pathogenic mutation of KCNT1 gene. He was treated with levetiracetam, oral steroids, oxcarbazepine, and zonisamide with no significant improvement. At 2.5 years of age, he had frequent focal seizure and global delay.

Case 6: a 6-month-old girl, the 3rd issue of her non-consanguineous parents, presented with repeated episodes of seizures since 3 days after birth. Seizures were initially focal clonic (consciousness impaired), followed by focal tonic involving the right upper and lower limbs, persisting for 1–2 minutes and occurring 5–6 times per day. Subsequently, new types of seizure appeared in the form of migratory focal seizures, persisting for 1–2 minutes, occurring 7–8 times per day, and associated autonomic features. Her birth history was uneventful up to day 3. Patient had global developmental delay. Developmental age was around 6 weeks. On examination, she was conscious but not interested in her surroundings. EEG reports showed focal epileptic discharges with poor background activity and MRI of the brain was nonspecific. She was followed up to 2.5 years of age, and received the following antiepileptic drugs with partial seizure control: phenobarbitone, oxcarbazepine, topiramate, lacosamide, zonisamide, and vigabatrin (Tables 1, 2).

Discussion

In this paper, we report the characteristics of seizures, types of delivery and developmental patterns of a number of cases with patients having the mutation of the KCNT1 gene. All the patients in this study had epilepsy and presented in early infancy. The onset of illness in the cases was mostly before 3 months of age and average age of diagnosis was before 1 year. No definite sex predilection was observed in this cohort. The age and sex of this cohort have similarity with the case series published by McTague et al.10 and James et al.11

The predominant seizure types in this case series were focal tonic, clonic or tonic-clonic initially. Focal seizures were characterized mainly by posturing of upper limbs, head version, orofacial seizure etc. However, the other types of primary seizure were epileptic spasms, generalized tonic seizure etc. In this study, one patient had status epilepticus. The classical migratory seizure of EIMFS was present in three out of six cases. In this regard, a similar case series by Chand et al.7 observed the following types of seizures: head version, tonic seizure, upper limb posturing, head jerk, behavioral arrest, eye rolling, and automatism. In their case report, James et al.11 described the seizure type as migratory focal seizure, some of the seizures were occurring in clusters,8 while Bonardi et al.12 mentioned the following types of seizure: epileptic spasm, myoclonic seizure, and focal evolving to tonic clonic seizure. They also noticed some atypical types of seizures like atypical absence (Table 1).

In all the patients in this case series, developmental stagnation was observed. These findings have similarity with the case series by Bonardi et al.12 who observed developmental disorders like delay or regression in most of the patients.9 Regarding birth history, most of the patients had uneventful birth course, except for one patient who had neonatal jaundice. None of the patients had a positive family history of similar types of illness or consanguinity.

Regarding the interictal EEG, five out of six patients had focal onset epileptic discharges arising mostly from the temporal and parietal regions of both hemispheres. The EEG background was disturbed, showing lack of sleep markers and slowing in most of the tracings. Progressive deterioration of EEG was observed in follow up tracings. In two cases, a burst suppression pattern was seen. Interictal EEG in one patient showed migration of focal epileptic discharges from one hemisphere to another. In their paper, Bonardi et al.12 also mentioned abnormal background activity in most of the cases with focal or multifocal epileptic discharges. Burst suppression pattern was also observed. However, they observed continuous spike waves during sleep, which were not present in our cases.9 McTague et al.10 observed a migrating ictal focus in different cortical areas in their EEG tracings. Eight out of 12 patients had a burst suppression pattern and hypsarrhythmia was seen in one patient.7 Neuroimaging was mostly nonspecific in this case series; four cases showed cortical atrophy and one patient had periventricular leukomalacia. They found atypical neuroimaging like open operculum, delayed myelination etc.7 Bonardi et al.12 also found similar features in MRI like cerebral atrophy, delayed myelination and corpus callosum abnormality. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy was normal in their case series.9

In this case series, all cases had pathogenic mutation in exon 15 of the KCNT1 gene. The most commonly observed variant in this case series was c.1421 (five cases) with c.1283, appearing in one case. All the cases were developmental epileptic encephalopathy-14 as per the genetic report. The most common variant in the paper by McTague et al.10 was A934T (five out of 12); other pathogenic variants included V271F, L274I, G288S, and F346L.7 While the common variants found in Bonardi et al.12 were G288S, A934T, R474H, R428Q, R398Q, and R950Q, most of them were of missense variant.9 The KCNT1 mutations occurred de novo in 51/56 cases in this cohort. In our case series, parental genetic study was not done due to financial constrain. In most of the case series, missense mutation was observed.7,8,13

In a previous study by Fitzgerald et al.,14 the following variants of KCNT1 gene were found: p.Gly288Ser, p.Arg950Gln, p.Arg474His, and p.Ala477Thr. Among these only p.Ala477Thr was associated with early onset epileptic encephalopathy and other variants were associated with EIMFS.11 In our study, five out of six cases had the p.Arg474His variant. Among these, three patients had the features of EIMFS, while two had features of infantile epileptic spasm syndrome. Another variant found in this study was p.Arg428Gin.

In conclusion, this study defines the phenotype and electroencephalographic features, neuroimaging, and the genotypes of children with epilepsy with KCNT1 mutations. There are very limited reported cases of KCNT1 related epilepsies, particularly in Bangladesh and other South Asian countries. This case series may highlight the phenotypic and genotypic characteristics of KCNT1 epilepsies in early infancy.
