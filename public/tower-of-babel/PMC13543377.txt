Rethinking Morton's neuroma: a sonographic study of bilateral prevalence and symmetry
Authors: Florian J. Jaklin, Ulrich Renner, Oskar Aszmann, Wolfgang J. Weninger, Stefan Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMorton neuroma (MN) is commonly regarded as a focal, unilateral condition. Whether sonographically detectable lesions also occur contralaterally to the symptomatic side has not been defined systematically. We aimed to determine the bilateral sonographic prevalence, anatomic distribution, symmetry, and size characteristics of lesions consistent with MN in patients referred with clinical suspicion of MN.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we included all consecutive patients referred between January 2023 and December 2024 for suspected MN. All underwent standardized bilateral high-resolution forefoot ultrasound irrespective of symptom laterality, with lesions defined as hypoechoic nodular enlargements measuring ≥ 3 mm. Prevalence was estimated using exact binomial 95% confidence intervals (CI), and size associations were assessed with linear mixed-effects models including random patient intercepts and fixed effects for intermetatarsal space, side, sex, age, morphology, and symptom status.ResultsA total of 226 patients (167 women; mean age, 55 ± 12 years) were included, and 568 lesions consistent with interdigital nerve enlargements were confirmed (mean, 2.5 ± 1.0 per patient). Lesions were most common in the third intermetatarsal space (62.3%) and second space (36.4%). Bilateral involvement was present in 183/226 patients (81.0%; 95% CI, 75.2–85.9%). Among those with strictly unilateral symptoms, contralateral lesions were found in 123/161 (76.4%; 95% CI, 69.1–82.7%). Bilateral disease was mirror-symmetric in 133/183 (72.7%). Intermetatarsal space and morphology were associated with size, whereas size was not independently associated with symptomatic status.ConclusionSonographically detected interdigital nerve enlargements are frequently bilateral and often symmetric, including in patients with unilateral symptoms. The high prevalence of subclinical contralateral enlargements challenges the traditional view of MN as a focal, unilateral entity. Routine bilateral ultrasound may be useful at baseline to characterize overall lesion burden and support counseling. However, management must remain strictly symptom-based, as incidentally detected, asymptomatic enlargements do not justify intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07045-2.

Background

Morton neuroma (MN) is a common cause of forefoot pain, histopathologically defined as a focal perineural fibrosis with concomitant painful nerve entrapment of the common plantar digital nerves [1, 2]. Patients typically present with localized neuralgia, often described as a burning or sharp pain in the forefoot [3]. Although conservative measures such as footwear modification or corticosteroid injections may offer temporary relief, surgical excision remains the definitive therapy in persistent cases. [4, 5] Clinical and surgical management has therefore traditionally targeted the symptomatic webspace alone, yet the true burden of disease may be more complex [5, 6].

The sonographic morphology of MN has been delineated extensively. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that ultrasound achieves a diagnostic sensitivity comparable to that of MRI (91 vs 90%) for MN [7]. Anatomical studies further indicate that the common plantar digital nerve in the intermetatarsal space measures approximately 2 mm in asymptomatic individuals, providing a morphologic baseline against which focal nerve enlargement can be appreciated on imaging [8]. In a surgical series with histological correlation, ultrasound reliably diagnosed the lesion in 97% of cases [9], and another investigation reported a sensitivity of 91% and a specificity of 100% for ultrasound, supporting ultrasound as a robust imaging tool in this setting [10]. Moreover, ultrasonography has been reported to show slightly higher sensitivity for neuromas < 5 mm and can therefore be regarded as the preferred first-line technique in clinically suspected MN, with MRI reserved for equivocal or discordant examinations [11]. Finally, a study in asymptomatic volunteers reported a high prevalence of interdigital nerve enlargements, highlighting the potential for incidental findings and overdiagnosis [12]. This tension motivates systematic bilateral assessment to quantify the sonographic burden and distribution of neuromas relative to symptom laterality. Yet, most examinations are restricted to the symptomatic foot. Asymptomatic neuromas may therefore remain undetected, leaving their prevalence and clinical significance poorly defined [6, 7, 13].

The frequency of interdigital lesions in the asymptomatic, contralateral foot of patients with unilateral symptoms is unknown. Although surgical series indicate that multiple lesions within a single foot are not uncommon, contralateral prevalence has not been systematically quantified, challenging the traditional view of MN as a strictly focal entity and raising the possibility that bilateral involvement is underrecognized [14]. Clarifying the burden and morphology of contralateral lesions has direct implications for counseling, operative selection, and follow-up.

Accordingly, we studied a consecutive referral cohort with clinical suspicion of MN who underwent standardized bilateral forefoot ultrasound irrespective of symptom laterality. Our primary objective was to estimate the sonographic prevalence and anatomic distribution of MN in symptomatic and asymptomatic feet, including contralateral involvement and symmetry. Secondary objectives were to characterize lesion size and to identify factors associated with size.

Materials and methods

Study population and data collection

This study was approved by the local institutional review board (IRB No. 25–083-0725) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable regulations. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the IRB. The study adheres to STROBE reporting guidelines for observational studies.

We reviewed a consecutive cohort of patients referred from orthopedic clinics for preoperative evaluation of suspected MN who underwent a standardized bilateral ultrasound protocol at a radiology practice between January 1, 2023 and December 31, 2024. Cases were identified through a structured query of the radiology information system and picture archiving and communication system.

Data on the laterality of clinical suspicion were missing for 19 of 226 patients (8.4%). These patients were included in overall prevalence analyses but excluded from the subgroup analysis restricted to individuals with unilateral symptoms. A sensitivity analysis comparing patients with and without symptom laterality data (n = 207 vs. n = 19) revealed no significant differences in bilateral prevalence (80.2% vs. 89.5%; p = 0.541, Fisher’s exact test) or neuroma count (mean 2.49 vs. 2.79; p = 0.314, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Patients with missing data were on average younger (50.3 vs. 55.1 years; p = 0.036) and had a lower proportion of female patients (52.6% vs. 75.8%; p = 0.053). These findings suggest that missing symptom data are unlikely to have introduced a substantial bias in the primary bilateral prevalence estimates.

Ultrasound and diagnostic definition

All examinations were performed bilaterally, irrespective of symptom laterality, by a board-certified musculoskeletal radiologist using a standard clinical ultrasound system with a high-frequency linear transducer (Aplio i800, 18LX5; Canon Medical Systems Europe). No contrast agents were used. Patients were examined supine with the foot in neutral position. Each intermetatarsal space was scanned in axial and long-axis planes from the plantar aspect.

A MN was defined on ultrasound as a well-circumscribed, hypoechoic nodular enlargement contiguous with the common plantar digital nerve, located in the intermetatarsal space plantar to the deep transverse metatarsal ligament, measuring ≥ 3 mm in either the dorsoplantar or transverse diameter (Fig. 1). Atypical morphology was recorded when echogenicity was mixed or isoechogenic to the surrounding soft tissue. MN dimensions were recorded in millimetres (mm) as: maximal mediolateral, dorsoplantar, and proximodistal. For the purposes of this study, all sonographically identified enlargements meeting these criteria are referred to as interdigital nerve enlargements (INE) when describing imaging findings irrespective of symptom status. The term Morton’s neuroma (MN) is used operationally throughout as the established clinical and diagnostic label, and is specifically reserved for the clinical syndrome in which an INE correlates with the patient’s symptoms.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.0). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) are provided. Two levels of analysis were distinguished throughout. Patient-level outcomes (bilateral prevalence, contralateral prevalence in unilateral presenters) were each based on a single binary observation per patient and analysed using exact binomial methods, which make no assumption about lesion-level independence. Lesion-level outcomes (size) were analysed using linear mixed-effects models with a random patient intercept to account for within-patient clustering of multiple lesions. Patient-level prevalence of sonographically detectable MN and, in patients with unilateral symptoms, contralateral prevalence were estimated with exact binomial methods; intermetatarsal space–specific proportions are likewise reported with 95% CI. Because MN size measurements were positively skewed (Shapiro–Wilk test: transverse diameter W = 0.951, p < 0.001; dorsoplantar diameter W = 0.975, p < 0.001), transverse and dorsoplantar diameters were natural log–transformed before modeling. Log-transformed sizes were compared with linear mixed-effects models including a random intercept for each patient to account for within-patient clustering. Fixed effects were side (left vs right), intermetatarsal space (spaces 1–4; reference, second space), sex, age, morphology (atypical vs typical), and symptom status (symptomatic vs asymptomatic). Model estimates were exponentiated and are presented as adjusted size ratios with 95% CI. Bilateral anatomical symmetry was defined as identical intermetatarsal space involvement on both feet (i.e., the same set of webspaces affected bilaterally), assessed by comparing webspace configurations per patient. The observed symmetry rate was compared against chance expectation using a permutation test (10,000 iterations, right-foot configurations randomly reassigned across patients). As all examinations were performed by a single board-certified radiologist, intra-observer consistency was ensured by use of a standardized acquisition protocol. Residual diagnostics for the mixed-effects models were assessed using simulated residuals (DHARMa package), confirming adequate model fit. Pairwise contrasts between intermetatarsal spaces were computed with Holm correction for multiple comparisons. No interaction terms were pre-specified; all fixed effects were entered as main effects, consistent with the a priori hypothesis that each predictor contributes independently to lesion size.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of 236 consecutive patients assessed for clinical suspicion of MN between January 2023 and December 2024, 10 were excluded because no INE could be confirmed on ultrasound, yielding a final cohort of 226 patients. The mean age was 54.7 ± 12.0 years and included 167 women (73.9%) and 59 men (26.1%) (Table 1).

In total, 568 INE were identified, corresponding to a mean of 2.5 ± 1.0 per patient. The third intermetatarsal space was the most frequent location (354/568, 62.3%), followed by the second (207/568, 36.4%) and fourth spaces (7/568, 1.2%). There were no INE found in the first intermetatarsal space (Table 2). Distribution was similar between left and right feet (298 vs 270; p = 0.52, exact binomial test). Atypical morphology was recorded in 132 (23,2%) out of 568 lesions.

Bilateral INE were present in 183 of 226 patients (81.0%; 95% CI: 75.2–85.9%). Among patients with unilateral clinical suspicion (n = 161), bilateral INE were detected in 123 cases (76.4%; 95% CI: 69.1–82.7). Among those with bilateral clinical symptoms (n = 46), 43 (93.5%; 95% CI, 82.1–98.6) had bilateral INE. In patients with incomplete symptom documentation (n = 19), 17 (89.5%) had bilateral findings.

A single MN per patient occurred in 35 of 226 patients (15.5%), whereas 191 (84.5%) had multiple INE. Among those with two INE (n = 93), 86 (92.5%) showed bilateral involvement; among those with three INE (n = 48), 47 (97.9%) showed bilateral involvement; and all patients with four or more INE (n = 50) had bilateral disease (Fig. 2).

Size

The median transverse diameter of identified INE was 4.8 mm (IQR, 4.0–5.9), the median dorsoplantar diameter was 4.4 mm (IQR, 3.6–5.3), and the median longitudinal diameter was 11.1 mm (IQR, 9.0–13.6).

After adjusting for covariates in the mixed-effects model, the primary associations were with intermetatarsal space and morphology. INE in the third intermetatarsal space were significantly larger than those in the second, with an adjusted transverse size 11% larger (95% CI, 7–16%) and a dorsoplantar size 9% larger (95% CI, 4–13%). Conversely, atypical morphology was associated with significantly smaller diameters, these INE were 20% smaller in transverse size (95% CI, 16–24%) and 18% smaller in dorsoplantar size (95% CI, 13–21%) compared to those with typical morphology. Male patients had INE with a dorsoplantar diameter that was, on average, 7% larger than females (95% CI, 1–13%). In absolute terms, third-space INE were + 0.52 mm (95% CI, 0.30–0.73) larger in transverse diameter and + 0.39 mm (95% CI, 0.20–0.57) larger in dorsoplantar diameter compared with second-space INE. Atypical INE were − 1.05 mm (95% CI, − 1.31 to − 0.80) smaller transversely and − 0.88 mm (95% CI, − 1.10 to − 0.66) smaller dorsoplantarly than INE with typical morphology. The sex-related difference in dorsoplantar diameter was + 0.33 mm (95% CI, 0.07–0.59) in favour of male patients.

No other predictors showed a statistically significant association with INE size. Male sex was associated with a 7% larger dorsoplantar diameter (95% CI, 1–13%; p = 0.026), but no differences were detected for transverse diameter based on sex. No other differences were detected based on side (left vs. right), patient age, or clinical suspicion status. Contrasts involving the fourth-fifth intermetatarsal space were also not significant, with wide confidence intervals indicating imprecise estimates due to smaller numbers. (Table S1; Fig. 3).

Symmetry

Among the 183 patients with bilateral disease, anatomical symmetry was present in 133 (72.7%), roughly twice the rate expected if left and right feet were affected independently (37.9%; p < 0.001). The most frequent presentation was a symmetrical INE in the third intermetatarsal space of both feet (66 patients), followed by symmetrical involvement of the second and third spaces bilaterally (48 patients) and of the second space alone (18 patients). The most common asymmetrical pattern consisted of combined second- and third-space INE on one foot with a solitary third-space INE on the contralateral side (37 patients) (Table 3; Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study challenges the conventional view of MN as a focal, unilateral entity. Our findings suggest that MN is a predominantly bilateral and multifocal disease process. Using standardized bilateral sonography, we found a bilateral prevalence > 80% overall and contralateral enlargements in 76% of patients who presented with strictly unilateral symptoms. Bilateral disease was frequently patterned rather than random: 73% of bilateral cases showed mirror-symmetric anatomic distribution. In multivariable models, lesion size was not independently associated with symptomatic status, indicating that symptom expression is not simply a function of sonographic caliber. Collectively, these findings suggest that the clinically evident INE often represents the symptomatic manifestation of a broader, frequently subclinical and symmetrical underlying pathology.

The observed bilateral prevalence dramatically exceeds the rates of 15–20% typically cited [3, 14, 15]. However, this discrepancy is explained by a key methodological difference: previous figures are largely derived from cohorts with symptomatic bilateral disease, whereas our study systematically screened the asymptomatic contralateral foot in all patients. Consistent with prior reports of neuroma-like enlargements in asymptomatic individuals [6], our contralateral rate of 76% extends the evidence for occult, patient-level predisposition beyond background prevalence. The high proportion of symmetric patterns (73%) further supports the possibility of shared anatomic or biomechanical influences on lesion distribution, while clinical symptoms likely arise where local factors (e.g., load, space constraints) cross an individual threshold. This pattern parallels other entrapment neuropathies, most notably carpal tunnel syndrome, where bilateral involvement is reported at approximately 60% (range, 22–87%) [16, 17].

In this cohort, most detected INE measured 4–6 mm in transverse and dorsoplantar diameter, which is slightly lower than the size range typically reported in surgical or MRI-correlated series but consistent with ultrasound-based cohorts that also include smaller or less advanced lesions [18]. Prior ultrasound studies in asymptomatic volunteers have demonstrated a considerable prevalence of interdigital nerve enlargements, frequently bilateral, supporting our observation that lesion size alone cannot distinguish symptomatic from incidental findings and must be interpreted in clinical context [12]. Taken together with our space-specific analysis, these data suggest that interdigital nerve thickness should be judged against the involved intermetatarsal space rather than against a single universal cutoff and that bilateral, symptom-directed assessment remains essential.

The cohort was referral-based for clinically suspected MN, so the observed prevalence of bilateral and multifocal disease reflects a symptomatic population and cannot be extrapolated to the general population. As the examining radiologist was not blinded to symptom laterality, a detection bias cannot be entirely excluded. However, the use of a standardized protocol applied uniformly to all webspaces in both feet, combined with the high proportion of anatomically symmetric bilateral findings, argues against a systematic ascertainment bias as the sole explanation for the observed rates. Diagnoses were made by high-resolution ultrasound without histological confirmation. While ultrasound is a highly accurate and widely accepted standard, it is not the histopathological gold standard. It is neither feasible nor ethical to biopsy every sonographically-identified lesion, particularly those that are asymptomatic. The musculoskeletal radiologist performing the ultrasound examinations was not blinded to symptom laterality. Although a standardized bilateral protocol was applied irrespective of clinical information, knowledge of the symptomatic side could theoretically influence the scrutiny with which a particular webspace was examined or the interpretation of borderline findings, and this possibility cannot be entirely excluded. Additionally, this cross-sectional study cannot determine progression. The proportion of asymptomatic INE that later become symptomatic MN is unknown. Longitudinal follow-up with repeated bilateral ultrasound is needed to estimate conversion risk and identify predictors of symptom onset. The mixed-effects models explained only 15–18% of the variance in lesion size (marginal R2 = 0.15–0.18), indicating that the measured predictors account for a modest proportion of overall variability. The substantially higher conditional R2 (0.34–0.40) reflects considerable between-patient variance not explained by the fixed effects. Factors not captured in this study, including body mass index, foot biomechanics (e.g., hypermobility, pes cavus or planus, metatarsal index), footwear habits, occupational loading, and adjacent intermetatarsal bursitis, likely contribute to lesion size and warrant investigation in future studies.

The terminology used in this field warrants comment. The term Morton’s neuroma (MN) implies a pathological, symptomatic entity, yet this study, like others before it, identifies many sonographic enlargements in the absence of corresponding symptoms. Using identical terminology for both conditions risks reinforcing the misconception that imaging detection alone justifies intervention. We therefore propose a terminological distinction that aligns with emerging best practice: sonographic findings meeting size criteria should be designated interdigital nerve enlargements (INE), with MN reserved for the clinical syndrome in which such an enlargement correlates with the patient’s symptoms. This distinction is not merely semantic, it has direct implications for preventing overdiagnosis and ensuring that management remains strictly symptom-led.

Conclusions

Interdigital nerve enlargement corresponding to lesions traditionally termed Morton neuroma is frequently bilateral and multifocal rather than an isolated, unilateral entity. In our cohort, 76% of patients with unilateral symptoms had a contralateral enlargement, often in an anatomically symmetric location. In adjusted analyses, lesion size was not independently associated with symptomatic status, indicating that caliber alone does not account for symptoms. A purely symptom-directed unilateral examination may therefore underestimate overall burden of underlying pathology. Routine bilateral ultrasound may be useful at baseline to characterize overall lesion burden and to support patient counseling. However, management should remain symptom-led, and the clinical utility of routine contralateral imaging warrants confirmation in prospective studies. Until such evidence is available, bilateral ultrasound should be considered a clinical research question rather than a standard-of-care directive.
