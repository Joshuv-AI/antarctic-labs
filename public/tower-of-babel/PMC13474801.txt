Comprehensive analysis of HB-PHD transcription factors associated with drought and salt stresses in wheat
Authors: Zhaosashuang Tian, Zilin Fan, Yuyi Ma, Xin Sun, Xu Lu, Xinxin Hong, Hao Liu, Fa Cui, Yanhong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHB-PHD transcription factors play important roles in plant growth and development as well as stress response. However, comprehensive analysis of HB-PHD genes was not investigated in wheat.ResultsIn this study, a total of nine TaHB-PHD genes were identified in wheat at the genome-wide level, and their physicochemical properties, evolutionary relationships, gene structures, cis-acting elements, expression patterns, downstream genes, and drought-resistant superior haplotypes were systematically analyzed. Phylogenetic analysis showed that HB-PHD proteins were divided into five subfamilies, and TaHB-PHD genes belonged to subfamilies Ⅰ,Ⅱ, and Ⅴ. The gene structures and conserved motifs of members in the same subfamily were highly conserved. Tissue expression analysis revealed that TaHB-PHD genes had the highest expression levels in roots and the lowest in leaves. Under drought stress, the cultivar-specific expression pattern was observed, all TaHB-PHD genes were down-regulated in drought-tolerant cultivar Giza168, and up-regulated in Chinese Spring and drought-sensitive cultivar Gemmiza10. Under salt stress, TaHB-PHD1-A/B/D and TaHB-PHD3-A/B/D were up-regulated, while the expression of TaHB-PHD2-A/B/D showed no obvious change. Prediction of downstream genes found that TaHB-PHDs could regulate various genes such as ribosomal RNA methyltransferase and histone acetyltransferase to participate in stress responses. Haplotype analysis indicated that TaHB-PHD3-D-Hap I was a drought-resistant superior haplotype. ConclusionsThis study provides a theoretical basis and candidate genes for the functional verification of wheat TaHB-PHD genes and stress-resistant molecular breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13051-9.

Introduction

Transcription factors (TFs) play a central role in plant growth, development and stress responses by specifically binding to cis-acting elements and modulating the transcription of target genes [1]. For example, GRF and WOX TFs are critical for plant growth and development [2, 3], while WRKY and NAC TFs are well-characterized for their functions in plant abiotic stress responses [4, 5]. Among them, HB-PHD TF family acts as a core regulator through binding cis-acting elements in the promoter regions to activate or repress the transcription of downstream target genes, thereby participating in the complex regulatory networks of plant growth and stress adaptation [6, 7].

The HB-PHD proteins contain both homeobox (HB)/ homeodomain (HD) and PHD-finger domain (PHD) [8]. The HD domain is typically located at the C-terminus of HB-PHD proteins, consisting of approximately 60 amino acid residues with a conserved DNA-binding motif that mediates specific DNA binding and transcriptional regulation of target genes [9]. The PHD domain mainly exists at the N-terminus, containing about 60 amino acids with a typical C4HC3 (Cys4-His-Cys3) zinc-binding motif and a transcriptional activation domain (AD) that modulates the transcriptional activity of target genes [6, 10].

HB-PHD TFs were first discovered in Arabidopsis thaliana and maize (Zea mays), named as AtHAT3.1, Zmhox1a and Zmhox1b, respectively [11–13]. AtHAT3.1 is mainly expressed in root tissues of Arabidopsis, suggesting AtHAT3.1 plays important roles in regulating root development [11]. Overexpression of Zmhox1a or Zmhox1b in tobacco leads to developmental abnormalities, including dwarfism, adventitious bud formation and petal malformation [13]. Subsequently, HB-PHD TFs have been identified in few plant species. For example, Arabidopsis AtPRHA responds to auxin and regulates plant growth and development by binding to the TAATTG-core element to modulate the expression of target genes [14]. Rice (Oryza sativa) HB-PHD transcription factor OsHOX1a/ OsHAZ1 can repress the expression of EL1 gene (Early flowering1) by directly binding to its promoter, thereby positively regulating gibberellic acid (GA) signaling and enhancing α-amylase activity [15].

Wheat (Triticum aestivum) is a staple cereal crop globally, and its growth and yield are severely affected by environmental stress, such as drought and salinity stresses. Therefore, mining key TFs involved in stress responses is of great significance for wheat molecular breeding. With the completion of the wheat genome sequencing [16], the genome-wide identification and systematic analysis of the HB-PHD gene family in wheat have become possible. In this study, we identified the HB-PHD gene family in wheat genome and comprehensively analyzed their evolutionary characteristics, gene structures, expression patterns and stress responses. We also predicted downstream genes and drought-resistant superior haplotypes. These results provide a theoretical basis for functional verification and stress-resistant molecular breeding application of TaHB-PHD genes.

Results

Identification of HB-PHD genes in wheat

Based on the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) search, a total of 9 HB-PHD genes were identified in the wheat genome. According to their chromosomal localization and evolutionary relationships, these HB-PHD genes were named as TaHB-PHD1-A/B/D, TaHB-PHD2-A/B/D and TaHB-PHD3-A/B/D (Table 1; Fig. 1). These nine TaHB-PHD genes encoded 744 to 1339 amino acid residues, with molecular weight values from 83.02 to 147.99 kDa. The isoelectric point (pI) values were between 4.79 and 8.55. TaHB-PHD1-A/B/D and TaHB-PHD2-A/B/D belong to acidic proteins (pI < 7), while TaHB-PHD3-A/B/D were basic proteins (pI > 7). The grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) values of all TaHB-PHD proteins were negative (-1.101 to -0.861), indicating that all TaHB-PHD proteins were highly hydrophilic proteins. Subcellular localization prediction revealed that all TaHB-PHD proteins were localized in the nucleus, which is consistent with their function as transcription factors to bind to promoters of target genes.

The characteristics of TaHB-PHD gene family members

Phylogenetic and collinearity analysis of TaHB-PHD genes

To explore the evolutionary relationships of HB-PHD proteins, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using HB-PHD protein sequences from ten monocot species (Triticum aestivum, Aegilops tauschii, Triticum urartu, Hordeum vulgare, Brachypodium distachyon, Oryza sativa, Zea mays, Setaria italica, and Sorghum bicolor) and four dicot species (Arabidopsis thaliana, Solanum lycopersicum, Vitis vinifera, and Glycine max) (Fig. 1 and Table S1). The results indicated that HB-PHD proteins were classified into five subfamilies, i.e., subfamily Ⅰ, Ⅱ, Ⅲ, Ⅳ, and Ⅴ (Fig. 1A). TaHB-PHD1-A/B/D, TaHB-PHD2-A/B/D, TaHB-PHD3-A/B/D were clustered into subfamily Ⅰ, Ⅱ, and Ⅴ, respectively. Notably, subfamilies Ⅰ, Ⅱ and Ⅴ only contained HB-PHD proteins from monocot species, while subfamilies Ⅲ and Ⅳ were composed of dicot HB-PHD proteins, suggesting that functional divergence of HB-PHD proteins occurred after the split of monocots and dicots, and TaHB-PHD proteins acquired specific functions in monocots.

Chromosomal localization analysis showed that TaHB-PHD1-A, TaHB-PHD1-B, TaHB-PHD1-D, TaHB-PHD2-A, TaHB-PHD2-B, TaHB-PHD2-D, TaHB-PHD3-A, TaHB-PHD3-B, and TaHB-PHD3-D were distributed on wheat chromosomes 1A, 1B, 1D, 3A, 3B, 3D, 6A, 6B, and 6D, respectively (Fig. 1B and Fig. S1). The collinearity analysis indicated that nine paralogous gene pairs of TaHB-PHD were identified in wheat genome using TGT (Triticeae-Gene Tribe) website, i.e., TaHB-PHD1-A/TaHB-PHD1-B, TaHB-PHD1-A/TaHB-PHD1-D, TaHB-PHD1-B/TaHB-PHD1-D, TaHB-PHD2-A/TaHB-PHD2-B, TaHB-PHD2-A/TaHB-PHD2-D, TaHB-PHD2-B/TaHB-PHD2-D, TaHB-PHD3-A/TaHB-PHD3-B, TaHB-PHD3-A/TaHB-PHD3-D, and TaHB-PHD3-B/TaHB-PHD3-D. The gene duplication of TaHB-PHDs was analyzed by PLAZA database, suggesting that all TaHB-PHD genes have undergone block duplication events. Ka/Ks ratio analysis showed that all Ka/Ks values of paralogous gene pairs were less than 1 (Table S2), indicating that the TaHB-PHD genes had undergone purifying selection during the evolutionary process, which maintains the functional conservation of the TaHB-PHD gene family.

Conserved motif and gene structure analysis of TaHB-PHD genes

Ten conserved motifs (Motif 1–10) were identified in HB-PHD proteins using the MEME tool (Fig. 2A). The results indicated that all HB-PHD proteins contained both HD and PHD-finger conserved domains (Figs. 2A and 3). TaHB-PHD1-A/B/D contained two HD domains and one PHD-finger domain, and TaHB-PHD2-A/B/D and TaHB-PHD3-A/B/D included only one HD domain and one PHD domain. The HD domain of all TaHB-PHDs was composed of Motif 2, and the PHD-finger (Cys4-His-Cys3) domain of TaHB-PHD1-A/B/D and TaHB-PHD2-A/B/D was formed by Motif 1 and Motif 3, the PHD-finger domain of TaHB-PHD3-A/B/D was composed of Motif 1 and Motif 8. HB-PHD proteins with close evolutionary relationships shared similar motif compositions, indicating conserved motif distribution within the same subfamily, and the biological functions of HB-PHD proteins were likely performed by these core domains.

Exon-intron structure analysis showed that HB-PHD genes contained 7 ~ 13 introns (Fig. 2B). Members of subfamily Ⅰ, Ⅱ, Ⅲ, Ⅳ, and Ⅴ contained 7 ~ 10, 7 ~ 11, 7, 9 ~ 13, 9 ~ 10 introns, respectively. The difference of intron numbers among the subfamilies might be related to evolutionary divergence. Among them, TaHB-PHD1-A/B/D, TaHB-PHD2-A/B/D and TaHB-PHD3-A/B/D contained 10, 7 and 9 introns, respectively. HB-PHD genes in the same subfamily exhibited highly conserved exon-intron structures and motif compositions, while significant differences were observed among different subfamilies. These results were consistent with the phylogenetic analysis of HB-PHD genes (Figs. 1 and 2), which further supports the rationality of the subfamily classification.

Analysis of cis-acting elements in TaHB-PHD promoters

The cis-acting elements are key sequences in gene promoters involved in transcriptional regulation, and the types and quantities of cis-acting elements reflect the expression regulation patterns of genes. In order to investigate the potential transcriptional regulatory mechanism and function of TaHB-PHD genes, the 2000 bp promoter regions of the TaHB-PHD genes were analyzed by PlantCARE database to identify the cis-acting elements (Fig. 4). A total of 34 types of cis-acting elements were identified in the 2000 bp promoter regions of TaHB-PHD genes, which were divided into four categories, i.e., stress-responsive elements, hormone-responsive elements, light-responsive elements and development-related elements (Fig. 4). Stress-responsive elements were the most abundant in TaHB-PHD promoters, with a total count of 207, including ARE, DRE, GC-motif, LTR, MBS, MYC, STRE, TC-rich repeats, W box and WRE3, indicating that TaHB-PHD genes might be involved in wheat stress responses. Among them, MBS elements (MYB binding sites) were highly enriched in the promoters of TaHB-PHD genes. Hormone-responsive elements were the second most abundant, with a total count of 80, including ABRE, AuxRR-core, CGTCA-motif, GARE, O2-site, TCA-element, TGA-element and TGACG-motif. ABRE could be involved in multiple stress responses including drought and salt stress through ABA signaling pathway [17]. ABRE elements were detected in the promoters of most TaHB-PHD genes, except for TaHB-PHD3-D. In addition, a large number of light-responsive and development-related elements were identified in TaHB-PHD promoters. Among these cis-acting elements, DRE, MBS, MYC, ABRE, W box, G-box, etc. might be related to drought and salt stress. These results indicated that TaHB-PHD genes might participate in multiple biological processes such as photosynthesis, flowering time regulation and stress adaptation in wheat.

Tissue-specific and stress-responsive expression patterns of TaHB-PHD genes

To investigate the potential function of TaHB-PHDs, transcriptome data were used to analyze the tissue-specific expression patterns of TaHB-PHD genes in wheat root, stem, leaf, spike and grain (Fig. 5 and Table S3). The results showed that all TaHB-PHD genes exhibited the highest expression levels in roots, followed by stems, grains and spikes, and the lowest in leaves.

Under PEG-simulated drought stress, the expression levels of all TaHB-PHD genes gradually decreased in the drought-tolerant cultivar Giza168, and gradually increased in the drought-sensitive cultivar Gemmiza10 with the extension of drought stress time (Fig. 6A and Table S3). In Chinese Spring cultivar, all TaHB-PHD genes were up-regulated under drought stress based on the RNA-seq and qRT-PCR data (Fig. 6B and C). qRT-PCR results showed that the expression levels of TaHB-PHD1-A, TaHB-PHD1-B, TaHB-PHD1-D, TaHB-PHD2-A, TaHB-PHD2-B, TaHB-PHD2-D, TaHB-PHD3-A, TaHB-PHD3-B and TaHB-PHD3-D were up-regulated by approximately 208.6-, 1.3-, 1.3-, 3.2-, 3.3-, 2.3-, 2.8-, 20.4- and 2.2-fold compared with control after drought stress for 24 h in Chinese Spring, respectively (Fig. 6C and Table S4). TaHB-PHD1-A showed the highest expression level, followed by TaHB-PHD3-B (Fig. 6C). Collectively, TaHB-PHD genes exhibited cultivar-specific expression patterns in response to drought stress. The opposite expression patterns observed in Giza168 and Gemmiza10 suggested that TaHB-PHD genes might play differential roles in mediating drought tolerance in different genetic backgrounds.

Under salt stress, TaHB-PHD1-A/B/D and TaHB-PHD3-A/B/D showed an overall up-regulated trend both in salt-sensitive cultivar Chinese Spring (CS) and salt-tolerant cultivar Qing Mai 6 (QM), while TaHB-PHD2-A/B/D exhibited an overall down-regulated trend in these two cultivars (Fig. 7A and Table S3). The qRT-PCR results in Chinese Spring were consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. 7B), suggesting that TaHB-PHD1-A exhibited a significant up-regulation after 12 h of salt stress, with expression levels increasing by approximately 2.5-fold compared to the control. TaHB-PHD1-B and TaHB-PHD1-D showed non-significant up-regulated trends under salt stress. TaHB-PHD2-A was up-regulated with approximately 1.6-fold under salt stress compared with control. The expression levels of TaHB-PHD2-B and TaHB-PHD2-D had no obvious change. TaHB-PHD3-A, TaHB-PHD3-B and TaHB-PHD3-D were up-regulated by approximately 2.8-, 5.5- and 1.5-fold compared with control, respectively. Although TaHB-PHD3-A exhibited non-significant up-regulation. In conclusion, TaHB-PHD genes respond to salt stress with conserved expression patterns between salt-sensitive and tolerant wheat cultivars. These results suggested that TaHB-PHD1-A/B/D and TaHB-PHD3-A/B/D might positively regulate the tolerance to salt stress, whereas TaHB-PHD2-A/B/D might play negative regulatory functions in response to salt stress.

Downstream genes analysis of TaHB-PHDs

TaHB-PHDs, as transcription factors, regulate the expression of downstream genes to perform the biological functions. The WGRN website showed that TaHB-PHD1-A, TaHB-PHD1-B, TaHB-PHD1-D, TaHB-PHD2-A, TaHB-PHD2-B, TaHB-PHD2-D, TaHB-PHD3-A, TaHB-PHD3-B and TaHB-PHD3-D could directly or indirectly regulate 1401, 1410, 1418, 1276, 2064, 1549, 931, 1406 and 1539 downstream genes, respectively (Fig. 8 and Table S5).

Under drought stress, the most similar expression patterns were observed between TaHB-PHD1-A with ribosomal RNA small subunit methyltransferase B; TaHB-PHD1-B with histone acetyltransferase; TaHB-PHD1-D with structural maintenance of chromosomes family protein and PHD finger protein; TaHB-PHD2-A with fanconi-associated nuclease 1-like protein; TaHB-PHD2-B withDIS3-like exonuclease 2; TaHB-PHD2-D with methyltransferase PMT7 and acyl-activating enzyme 19; TaHB-PHD3-A with ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase 25; TaHB-PHD3-B with protein phytochrome-dependent late-folowering and regulator of MON1-CCZ1 complex; TaHB-PHD3-D with DExH-box ATP-dependent RNA helicase DExH3. The results suggested that TaHB-PHDs might directly or indirectly regulate the expression of these downstream genes that had the most similar expression patterns with TaHB-PHDs to participate in wheat drought stress responses.

Under salt stress, the most similar expression patterns were observed between TaHB-PHD1-A with Periodic tryptophan protein; TaHB-PHD1-B with eEF1A lysine and N-terminal methyltransferase; TaHB-PHD1-D with eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 subunit B; TaHB-PHD2-A with mediator of RNA polymerase II transcription subunit; TaHB-PHD2-B with SWI/SNF complex subunit SWI3D; TaHB-PHD2-D with protein phosphatase 2 C, protein heat intolerant and lysine ketoglutarate reductase trans-splicing protein; TaHB-PHD3-A with zinc transporter ZTP29; TaHB-PHD3-B with nucleic acid binding protein; TaHB-PHD3-D with cyclin-T1-3. The results suggested that TaHB-PHDs might directly or indirectly regulate the expression of these downstream genes which had the most similar expression patterns with TaHB-PHDs to participate in wheat salt stress responses.

Identification of drought-resistant superior haplotype of TaHB-PHD3-D

Transcriptome analysis of 200 wheat varieties under well-watered and drought stress conditions showed that the expression of TaHB-PHD3-D was significantly up-regulated under drought stress (Fig. 9 and Table S6). Therefore, haplotype analysis of TaHB-PHD3-D was performed using WheatUnion database, and two main haplotypes were identified and named TaHB-PHD3-D-Hap I and TaHB-PHD3-D-Hap II (Fig. 9). The results indicated that seedlings of wheat varieties with TaHB-PHD3-D-Hap I had an obviously higher survival rate than TaHB-PHD3-D-Hap II under drought stress, and the expression level of TaHB-PHD3-D-Hap I was obviously higher compared with that of TaHB-PHD3-D-Hap II under well-watered and drought stress conditions. These results indicated that TaHB-PHD3-D-Hap I was the superior drought-resistant haplotype of TaHB-PHD3-D, which provided a new genetic resource for breeding drought-resistant wheat varieties.

Discussion

HB-PHD transcription factors played important roles in plant growth, development and stress responses by mediating transcriptional regulation of downstream genes [6, 10]. HB-PHD genes have been identified in only a few plant species, such as Arabidopsis, maize and rice [11–13, 15]. In this study, a total of 9 TaHB-PHD genes were identified in the wheat genome, which was significantly more than those in Arabidopsis (2), rice (2), Triticum urartu (3) and Aegilops tauschii (3) (Fig. 1A). The expansion of the HB-PHD gene family in wheat was consistent with the characteristics of wheat as an allohexaploid (AABBDD) with three subgenomes, which was the result of polyploid retention of orthologous copies through two rounds of natural hybridization during wheat evolution (Fig. S1) [18, 19]. Phylogenetic and collinearity analyses of TaHB-PHD genes also provided evidence for this conclusion (Fig. 1), such as TaHB-PHD1-A, TaHB-PHD2-A and TaHB-PHD3-A had the most closest evolutionary relationship with the HB-PHD genes TuG1812G0100003770.01, TuG1812G0300003194.01, and TuG1812G0600001224.01 from Triticum urartu (the donor of the A subgenome), while TaHB-PHD1-D, TaHB-PHD2-D and TaHB-PHD3-D showed the closest evolutionary relationship with the HB-PHD genes AET1Gv20813100, AET3Gv20652800, and AET6Gv20304300 from Aegilops tauschii (the donor of the D subgenome).

Tissue-specific expression could indirectly reflect the function of genes. In this study, TaHB-PHD genes were mainly expressed in wheat root tissues (Fig. 5), which was consistent with the specific expression of AtHAT3.1 in Arabidopsis roots [11]. Roots were the important plant tissues that responded to abiotic stress, speculating that TaHB-PHD genes might play key roles in wheat root development and in response to osmotic stress, such as drought and salt stresses. TaHB-PHD genes exhibited cultivar-specific expression patterns in response to drought stress. All TaHB-PHD genes were down-regulated in drought-tolerant cultivar Giza168, and up-regulated in Chinese Spring and drought-sensitive cultivar Gemmiza10 (Fig. 6). Under salt stress, the functional divergence was observed in the expression of TaHB-PHDs (Fig. 7). TaHB-PHD2-A/B/D showed no obvious response to salt stress, while TaHB-PHD1-A/B/D and TaHB-PHD3-A/B/D were up-regulated under salt stress (Fig. 7). This functional divergence might be the result of sub-functionalization of the TaHB-PHD gene family during evolution, which enabled wheat to adapt to different abiotic stress conditions [6]. Haplotype analysis identified of TaHB-PHD3-D-Hap I as the superior drought-resistant haplotype with higher survival rate and expression level at the seedling stage under drought stress (Fig. 9), which provided an important genetic resource for wheat drought-resistant molecular breeding. This strategy of mining superior haplotypes of stress-resistant genes was consistent with the application of haplotypes of other stress-resistant genes, such as DREB and NAC in wheat [5, 20].

Previous studies have shown that HB-PHD TFs regulated plant growth and development by binding to cis-acting elements such as TAATTG or TCCT/GATC to activate or repress target gene expression and modulate endogenous hormone levels [14, 15]. Our study found that TaHB-PHDs might directly or indirectly regulate the expression of functional genes, such as ribosomal RNA methyltransferase, histone acetyltransferase, ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase and methyltransferase under drought stress, and eukaryotic translation initiation factor, nucleic acid binding protein, protein phosphatase 2 C and phytochrome-dependent late-flowering protein under drought and salt stresses (Fig. 8), suggesting that TaHB-PHDs regulated wheat abiotic stress responses through multiple signaling pathways. In Arabidopsis, AtPRHA bond to the TAATTG element to regulate target gene expression [14], and rice HB-PHD protein OsHOX1a/OsHAZ1 bond to the TCCT/GATC element to repress the expression of the early flowering gene EL1, positively regulating GA signaling and enhancing α-amylase activity [15]. Consistent with these studies, our results predicted that TaHB-PHD3-B might regulate the expression of TraesCS1B02G153400 (protein phytochrome-dependent late-flowering) under drought stress (Fig. 8), suggesting that TaHB-PHDs may be involved in the regulation of wheat flowering time under stress conditions. These results provided a theoretical basis for the functional verification of TaHB-PHD genes and identified valuable candidate genes and haplotypes for wheat stress-resistant molecular breeding.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification of HB-PHD gene family members

The Plant Transcription Factor Database (PlantTFDB, https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/index.php) was used to identify the candidate HB-PHD genes in wheat (Triticum aestivum), Aegilops tauschii, Triticum urartu, Hordeum vulgare, Brachypodium distachyon, Oryza sativa, Zea mays, Setaria italica, Sorghum bicolor, Arabidopsis thaliana, Solanum lycopersicum, Vitis vinifera and Glycine max. The candidate HB-PHD proteins were further validated using Interpro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) and SMART (https://smart.embl.de/) to remove proteins without both HD (PF00046) and PHD-finger (PF00628) conserved domains, and the final HB-PHD gene family members were confirmed.

The physicochemical properties of TaHB-PHD proteins, including amino acid length, molecular weight, isoelectric point (pI) and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), were analyzed using the ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Subcellular localization of TaHB-PHD proteins was predicted by the Plant-mPLoc server (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/).

Chromosomal localization and collinearity analysis

Chromosomal localization information, paralogous gene pairs and micro-collinearity of TaHB-PHD genes were obtained from the TriticeaeGeneTribe database (http://wheat.cau.edu.cn/TGT). Collinearity analysis and visualization were performed using the Advanced Circos tool in TBtools [21]. The gene duplication events were determined by PLAZA database (https://www.vandepoelelab.be/plaza/). The nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka), synonymous substitution rate (Ks) and Ka/Ks ratio of paralogous TaHB-PHD gene pairs were calculated to evaluate the evolutionary selection pressure with the Nei-Gojobori (NG) method in TBtools [21].

Phylogenetic, gene structure and conserved motif analysis

Amino acid sequences of HB-PHD proteins from the 13 plant species (Triticum aestivum, Aegilops tauschii, Triticum urartu, Hordeum vulgare, Brachypodium distachyon, Oryza sativa, Zea mays, Setaria italica, Sorghum bicolor, Arabidopsis thaliana, Solanum lycopersicum, Vitis vinifera and Glycine max) were aligned using the ClustalW program in MEGA11 [22]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates [22], and the tree was visualized using the EvolView online tool (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview-v3/). Exon-intron structures of TaHB-PHD genes were extracted from the wheat genome GFF3 annotation file and visualized using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS2.0, https://gsds.gao-lab.org/index.php). Conserved motifs of HB-PHD proteins were identified using the MEME online tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/) with the maximum number of motifs set to 10 and other parameters as default. The motif composition was visualized using the Gene Structure View (Advanced) tool in TBtools [21].

Analysis of cis-acting elements of TaHB-PHD promoters

The 2000 bp upstream sequences of the start codon (ATG) of TaHB-PHD genes were extracted from the EnsemblPlants database (http://plants.ensembl.org/Triticum_aestivum/Info/Index). The cis-acting elements in the promoter regions were predicted using the PlantCARE database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [23], and the types and counts of elements were statistically analyzed and visualized.

Transcriptome data analysis

Tissue-specific RNA-seq data (root, stem, leaf, spike, grain) of wheat cultivar Chinese Spring were obtained from the WheatOmics 1.0 database (http://wheatomics.sdau.edu.cn/) [16]. For PEG stress, the 9-day-old wheat seedlings of the drought-tolerant cultivar Giza168 (GZ168) and the drought-sensitive cultivar Gemmiza10 (GM10) were treated with 20% PEG6000 for 0 h, 2 h, 12 h, respectively. The leaf tissues of each sample were collected for RNA-seq analysis with two biological replicates, and RNA-seq data (BioProject ID: PRJNA306536) were obtained from the WheatOmics 1.0 database (http://wheatomics.sdau.edu.cn/). For drought stress, the 10-day old wheat seedlings of Chinese Spring cultivar were subjected to water-deficit condition for 0 d, 2 d, 6 d, and 10 d. The leaf tissues were harvested for RNA-seq analysis with three biological replicates. The RNA-seq data of drought stress (BioProject ID: PRJNA1003680) were obtained from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [24]. For salt stress, the 10-day-old wheat seedlings of salt-sensitive cultivar Chinese Spring (CS) and salt-tolerant cultivar Qing Mai 6 (QM) were subjected to normal and 150 mM NaCl conditions for 6 h, 12 h, 24 h and 48 h, respectively. The root tissues were harvested for RNA-seq analysis, and RNA-seq data of salt stress (BioProject ID: PRJNA293629) were obtained from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [25]. The TPM (Transcripts Per Million) values were used to analyze tissue-specific expression patterns and stress response profiles of genes.

Plant material and growth conditions

The growth conditions and stress treatments of wheat were performed as described previously [24]. Briefly, the seeds of wheat cultivar Chinese Spring were rinsed with sterile ddH₂O, surface-sterilized with 75% ethanol for 5 min, and rinsed with sterile ddH₂O to remove residual ethanol. The sterilized seeds were soaked in sterile ddH₂O at 4℃ in the dark for 24 h, then placed on moist filter paper and germinated in a greenhouse under the conditions of 16 h light/8 h dark and 25/16℃ (day/night). Seven-day-old uniform seedlings were transferred to hydroponic boxes and subjected to drought stress (20% PEG6000) and salt stress (300 mM NaCl) treatments, respectively. The leaf tissues of nine wheat seedlings were collected and mixed at 0 h and 24 h after drought stress, and 0 h and 12 h after salt stress. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the sampled tissues using the RNApure Plant Kit (Vazyme). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the PrimeScript™ II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II kit (TaKaRa). The specific primers for TaHB-PHD genes were described in Table S7, and TaActin was used as the reference gene. Each sample was analyzed with three replicates. The relative expression levels of TaHB-PHD genes were calculated using the 2−△△CT method [26].

Prediction of downstream genes of TaHB-PHDs

Downstream genes of TaHB-PHD transcription factors were predicted using the wheat gene regulatory network database (wGRN, https://wheat.cau.edu.cn/wGRN/). RNA-seq data under drought (PRJNA1003680) and salt (PRJNA293629) stresses were used to extract the TPM values of TaHB-PHD genes and their predicted downstream genes. Normalization and cluster analysis were performed using TBtools [21], and the heatmaps were generated to identify the downstream genes with the most similar expression patterns to TaHB-PHDs under drought and salt stress conditions.

Haplotype analysis of drought-resistant TaHB-PHD genes

Haplotype analysis of TaHB-PHD genes was performed using resequencing data of 2789 hexaploid wheat varieties from the WheatUnion database (https://wheat.cau.edu.cn/WheatUnion/). The seedling survival rate of 400 wheat varieties under drought stress was obtained from published data [20]. Transcriptome data of 200 wheat varieties under well-watered and drought conditions were downloaded from the Genome Sequence Archive (CRA016347, https://bigd.big.ac.cn/gsa) [27]. The expression levels of TaHB-PHD genes and survival rates of different haplotypes under drought stress were analyzed to identify superior drought-resistant haplotypes.
