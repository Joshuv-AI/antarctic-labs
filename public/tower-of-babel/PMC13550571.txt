Perforator artery analysis and salvage procedure of propeller flaps on the reconstruciton of distal lower extremity
Authors: Özcan Öcük
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReconstructive options for skin defects in the distal lower extremity are limited. Propeller flaps (PF) represent an important option due to their practicality and wide applicability. Planning the flap in a rational and practical manner facilitates reconstruction in this area. PF was performed in 52 patients with distal lower extremity soft tissue defects. The patients had an age range of 23 to 58 years, with a male preponderance (45 males and 7 female). The flap size ranged from 8 × 4 to 18 × 7 cm. This study was conducted retrospectively to examine the salvage procedure in cases of venous congestion, which is frequently encountered with PF. Total necrosis was not occured in any of the patients. Partial necrosis developed in three patient, accounting for 1/5 of the flap. Moderate venous congestion (MVC) occurred in 10 patients, all of whom received leech therapy. One patient developed an infection on postoperative day 4. Venous supercharging was performed in 23 patients during surgery. No complications occurred in 42 patients. In statistical analysis, increasing artery diameter significantly increases the frequency of supercharging (p = 0,001), and also increasing the degree of flap rotation significantly increases the risk of MVC (p = 0,001). In distal lower extremity tissue defects where the use of local flap is limited, PF can potentially become the first choice. Complications such as venous congestion, which most commonly occur with these flaps, can be readily addressed using salvage procedures such as leech therapy and venous supercharging.Keywords: Propeller flap, Lower extremity, Supercharge, Venous congestionSubject terms: Anatomy, Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Propeller flaps (PF) are practical and useful flaps that can be applied in different parts of the body, and have recently become increasingly popular1. PF, which can be rotated up to 180 degrees in a propeller-like manner around the axis of a perforator artery (PA) identified anywhere on the body, was first applied by Hyakusoku et al. in 1991, and a clear definition of PF was introduced at the Tokyo meeting in 20092–4. Especially in small to medium-sized defects in the lower extremity where local flap options are limited, PF can potentially become first choice1,5. The distal third of the lower extremity, in particular, that requires careful reconstruction because of exposing vital tissues (neurovascular structures and bone). In this region, PF offers several advantages such as providing a wide area of application, low donor site morbidity, quick implementation, serving as a good alternative to free flaps, and being compatible with the tissues surrounding the defect2,5. This article presents our clinical experience based on a retrospective case series involving 52 patients with tissue defects in the distal lower extremity who underwent repair using PF, discussing their clinical follow-up, rational flap planning, complications encountered (especially venous congestion). Furthermore, this article evaluates the occurrence of venous congestion in the flap using statistically significant data obtained according to perforator artery diameter and flap rotation degree and discusses the results of venous supercharge, a salvage procedure for this problem.

Materials and methods

This study included 52 patients who underwent PF procedures for soft tissue defects in the distal lower extremity. All surgeries were performed under spinal anesthesia. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Kahramanmaraş Sütçü İmam University Ethics Committee (06-2022/25). In addition, this study was conducted within the framework of the ethical rules and guidelines specified in the Helsinki Declaration, and also informed consent and legal publication permission were obtained from each patient for this study. The surgeries were undertaken by a single surgeon at the Plastic Reconstructive and Aesthetic surgery clinic between 2021 and 2024. The same surgeon conducted clinical follow-up of all patients. The patients had an age range of 23 to 58 years, with a male preponderance (45 males, 7 female). Flap size ranged from a minimum of 8 × 4 cm to a maximum of 18 × 7 cm. The perforator artery (PA) was identified using computed tomography angiography (CTA) with settings of 120 kV, 250 mA, 1 mm section interval thickness, 53 mm table travel per rotation and 64 detector-row computer tomography (CT). Contrast medium was administered at a rate of 4 ml/min, and imaging was initiated when a threshold of 100 Hounsfield Units (HU) was reached at the aortoiliac bifurcation. The identified PA was confirmed using Doppler Sonography (DS). All patients received daily injections of 4,000 units of low-molecular-weight heparin starting from 0 h postoperatively for 10 days. The follow-up duration of the patients ranged from 10 to 44 months (Table 1). Artery diameter was calculated in millimetre (mm) at the border of muscle fascia, and subcutaneous fat tissue according to the calibration of CT images (Fig. 1). Measurement calibration was performed as a standard procedure for each patient, based on the program that provides images to the CT device.

Identification of perforator artery with CTA and flap planning

When planning a PA-based flap, it is crucial to understand the PA principle and proceed accordingly5. In the distal lower extremity, the PA originates from three main arterial sources: the anterior and posterior tibial arteries and the peroneal artery5. It has been suggested that the posterior tibial artery-based flaps are associated with a lower risk of complications6. However, planning is essentially based on a reliable PA, closest to the tissue defect7. The steps listed below can be followed for reliable and rational flap planning:

While designing the flap, to accommodate for skin contraction after the flap is harvested, planning the flap 1 cm longer and 0.5 cm wider than the defect reduces the risk of complications due to tension during flap adaptation1,5,8. In PF surgeries, it is always necessary to have a Plan B (inability to reach an artery of the desired caliber or the possibility of damage to the perforator artery), which typically involves the use of a free flap1,2. However, as in our current case series, a more practical plan B is to mark a second PA closest to the tissue defect area, if available, and keep it ready for use (Fig. 3C and D). If two perforators with similar caliber and flow strength are available, posterior tibial artery based perforators may be preferred, because posterior tibial artery derived flaps have proportionally fewer complications. On the other hand, choosing a perforator artery with less rotation would be a more appropriate plan in terms of reducing the risk of complications.

Surgical technique

After marking the flap, dissection begins at the edge of the tissue defect, proceeding from the suprafascial plane to reach the PA. The PA is checked with DS for confirmation at this stage. The fascia around a 1-cm area of the PA is released and dissection extends approximately 1–2 cm into the muscle to prevent kinking of the pedicle. While separating the flap from the skin, any identified subcutaneous superficial veins are dissected proximally, marked with a venous clamp, and cut accordingly, taking into account the possibility of venous supercharging. Upon achieving the planned arc of rotation, the flap is temporarily adapted to the tissue defect area with skin staples and flap circulation is monitored. In case of arterial spasm, various local vasodilators (e.g., papaverine) are administered, followed by a waiting period of approximately 20 min. If arterial circulation is not restored, the flap is brought back to its original place, and flap adaptation is delayed by 2–3 days2. Moderate venous congestion (MVC) can be managed with clinical monitoring and flap adaptation is carried out after resolution of congestion. If severe venous congestion is observed and venous blood accumulation is detected in the clamped subcutaneous superficial vein, venous supercharging is recommended. If a supercharging is required, the subcutaneous superficial vein preserved in the flap, should be anastomosed to a subcutaneous superficial vein prepared at the same level around the tissue defect or around the flap donor area, according to the planned procedure.

Results

Total tissue necrosis was not occured in any of the 52 patients who underwent PF treatment. Partial epidermal necrosis, approximately 1/5 of the flap, developed in three patient, accompanied by MVC. In that patient, secondary skin defect formation was covered with a split-thickness skin graft, facilitated by the formation of granulation tissue. Arterial spasm was realized intraoperatively in another patient, which was resolved with local papaverine injection. Severe venous congestion occurred during surgery in 23 patients, who underwent venous supercharging. There was no tissue loss in patients who underwent venous supercharging. Except the patient with partial necrosis, MVC occurred in 10 patients at 12–48 h postoperatively. These patients received leech therapy three times a day (every 8 h) for 3 days. In one patient who received leech therapy, infection developed on postoperative day 4. Infection was managed with the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and by increasing the frequency of dressing changes. 42 patients did not develop any complications.

Statistical results

In the statistical analysis of our study as independent variables artery diameter, artery type, flap area, flap angle, and comorbidity as dependent variables complications and supercharging were examined. In this exploratory study to summarize the qualitative variables, the probability value for the value of the Pearson Chi-Square test and Fisher’s Exact Test were given where appropriate for independent groups with a 95% confidence level and the number (percentage). Normality of quantitative variables was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. For variables with normal distribution, mean ± standard deviation was used where appropriate using the Independent Samples Test for intergroup significance testing. For all hypothesis testing results, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 27.0 (Table 2). Flap area, artery type and artery diameter were compared in patients who underwent supercharging, and it was found to be statistically significant that the rate of supercharging increased when the artery diameter increased (p < 0.05; p = 0.001). When evaluated in terms of artery type (ATA, PTA, PA), although distribution differences were observed between the two groups, this difference was not found to be statistically significant (p > 0.05; p = 0.325). There is no significant difference between supercharging and flap size (Table 3). Flap area, flap rotation degree and artery diameter were compared in patients with postoperative MVC. Although there was no statistically significant difference in the occurrence of MVC in terms of flap size and artery diameter, a statistically significant difference was found between the increase in the degree of flap rotation and the occurrence of MVC (p < 0.05; p = 0.001) (Table 4). MVC development was compared with 3 arteries (ATA, PA, PTA), in which PF was performed in the lower extremity and although there was no significant difference in this group (p > 0.05; p = 0.497), the incidence of complications proportionally increases in ATA-based flaps (60%) (Table 5). When the same table is examined, it is seen that although the development of MVC in patients with DM is not statistically significant (p > 0.05; p = 0.091), the presence of DM proportionally increases the risk of developing complications.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 55-year-old male patient underwent surgery for multiple fractures in the right ankle joints due to a motor vehicle accident and was treated with external fixation by orthopedists. Consultation was sought from our clinic due to exposure of the distal tibial bone. PF (12 × 7 cm) treatment was planned based on the patient’s CTA scans. MVC developed at 24 h postoperatively. Leech therapy was administered to the patient. Venous congestion improved on postoperative day 3. The patient did not experience any complications (Fig. 2).

Case 2

A 23-year-old male patient underwent surgery for multiple fractures in the ankle joint due to a non-traffic accident and was treated with external fixation by the department of orthopedics. Our clinic was consulted due to bone exposure on the anterior aspect of the ankle. Initially, a PF with a size of 14 × 7 cm was planned based on a PA lateral to the tissue defect, as identified by lower extremity CTA images (Fig. 3A and B). At the time of intraoperative dissection and DS confirmation, it was observed that this PA did not have adequate caliber and pulsatility. As a Plan B, another PF (9 × 6 cm) was planned based on the PA superior to the defect site (Fig. 3C and D). The patient developed MVC on postoperative day 2 and received leech therapy. Venous congestion improved on postoperative day 4. The patient did not experience further complications (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The use of PF in small to moderate-sized skin defects of the distal lower extremity has become an increasingly popular method in recent years, primarily due to its easier application compared to free flaps1,2,5. Reconstruction of tissue defects in this region and wound management are critical issues7. Current comorbidities in patients, regional blood circulation, the type of trauma, the presence of multiple traumas, and the limited use of local flaps in this area further complicate the efforts of plastic surgeons in wound management5–7. The use of free flaps should not be considered as the first choice, due to cost-effectiveness issues and the fact that it is a lengthy surgical procedure in this region9. The use of cross-leg flaps should be considered as a last resort after all other procedures have been attempted, given its potential to reduce the patient’s quality of life, prolonged hospitalization, the need for a two-step surgical procedure, as well as the difficulty of application compared to newer techniques6,7. Moreover, the use of muscle flaps in this area should be considered with caution as it may cause functional impairment in the foot6. On the other side, while classic pedicled flaps are advantageous in terms of venous congestion in this region, their disadvantages include the limited rotation arc, the formation of dog ears on the skin by making a king in this region and the development of necrosis in the flap due to the inability to clearly identify the source artery coming to the pedicle1,7.

The primary consideration for performing PF in this area is to identify a PA close to the defect. A simple DS can be used for PA analysis. However, given the variability of patients’ body fat percentage and the possibility of arteries in the area running close to the skin, DS can yield a high rate of false-positive results (47%), potentially leading to significant intraoperative planning deficiencies and surgical failure10,11. However, this false-positive rate may decrease as you gain surgical experience. Apart from DS, color doppler sonography (CDS), CT, and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) can also be used for PA analysis10,11. While CDS is a non-invasive technique, it requires advanced expertise and reanalysis for application10. DS and CDS are more effective for patients who are unable to receive contrast agents. Although MRA provides more precise results for PA detection compared to DS applications, it has the disadvantage of requiring contrast material use and failure to analyze PAs thinner than 1 mm10. Also, MRA has additional disadvantages such as offering a small field of view, requiring long scanning times, and high cost. While CTA is associated with certain disadvantages such as the need for the use of contrast agents and exposure to ionizing radiation, it has numerous advantages, including the ability to assess PAs with a diameter down to 0.3 mm, short imaging times, offering image analysis in 3D, and higher image resolution10. The use of CTA in patients without contraindications provides a comfortable working environment for the surgeon, as it does not give false positive results in PA analysis, eliminates the need for changes in planning during surgery, and allows for identification of the intramuscular location of PA beforehand10,12,13. In the our case series, we identified all PAs using CTA in all patients. In addition, we performed verification with DS in preoperative flap planning and during surgery. All PAs detected using CTA were found to be 100% accurate and located precisely as identified. Although the PAs we identified were in 100% accurate localization, these PAs require further evaluation during surgery. Only in one case (Case #2) we had to make a change in flap planning because, as can be seen from the relevant photo (Fig. 3), we considered the flow rate and caliber of the first PA, that we identified and based our plans on unsuitable during the surgery. As a result, we performed the surgery using the other PA, which was the second planned PA (Plan B). This demonstrates the importance of having a Plan B in PF surgeries. All the PAs identified in the proximity of the surgical site provided us with the flexibility to make changes to the plan easily, thereby increasing the success of the operation. However, it is still important to consider the use of free flaps as a backup plan2,11.

One of the major criteria for achieving success in PF survival in the distal lower extremity is thorough evaluation of the patient’s clinical condition and comorbidities. The patient’s age, presence of any comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, arterial insufficiency, malnutrition, obesity), history of radiation exposure, old scars at the site of the tissue defect, presence of multiple traumas, and smoking history are the most important criteria in flap planning, and patient selection5,14. Particularly in diabetic patients, PF planning in the distal lower extremity should be carefully assessed due to the vascular problems caused by diabetes. In a study, Kyubeom et al. found a high rate (60%) of complications associated with the use of PF in the distal lower extremity in diabetic patients15. Moreover, excessive skeletonization of the PA, and excessive rotation arc in diabetic patients, combined with vascular network abnormalities induced by diabetic damage, increase the failure rate15,16. In our study, the only three patient with partial skin necrosis and one of them had diabetes. Therefore, a careful PF analysis is required in diabetic patients, and dissection and arterial condition should be carefully evaluated during surgery. According to the statistical results of our study, although the complication rate in the presence of DM does not seem to be significant, there is a proportionally increased risk of complications in the presence of DM.

Major complications encountered during PF application in the lower extremity include necrosis (partial and total), wound dehiscence, infection, arterial insufficiency and hematoma but venous congestion is the most common and challenging complication to manage2,5,7,11,14,15,17–20. To address venous congestion, many surgeons have tested various methods. Leech therapy is an effective method for managing MVC in postoperative follow-up visits2,7,11,14,17,21. Deep venous congestion typically manifests by brisk capillary refill and the flap exhibiting purple discoloration during surgery14. In that case, performing venous supercharging intraoperatively is an effective method for preventing venous congestion7,14. Another consideration that should be kept in mind during the surgery is the need to preserve one or two superficial veins during dissection as a reserve and keep them in a suitable condition for anastomosis, taking into account the possibility of deep venous congestion14. During surgery, if there is rapid arterial circulation, venous accumulation is present in the reserved veins, blood is drained efficiently and the flap circulation improves, this is an indication for venous supercharging14. In our case series, these venous salvage procedures were followed for all patients, and venous supercharging was performed in 23 patients who met the specified indication. In addition, in the statistical analysis of our study, the need for supercharging increases if the artery diameter exceeds 0.922 ± 0.141 on average. Increasing artery diameter significantly increases the need for supercharge (p < 0.05; p = 0.001). In our case series, no complications occurred in all patients who performed supercharging, demonstrating the critical importance of careful assessment of venous congestion during surgery.

In cases of deep venous congestion, Yadav et al. resolved venous congestion by returning the flap to its original location and harvesting a split- thickness skin graft from the flap area. However, since this approach involves a two-step surgical procedure, and can cause uncontrolled bleeding and secondary damage to the flap, it should be considered as a method to be used infrequently7. To prevent venous congestion, Bo Li et al. performed an adipofascial pedicle-based turn-over flap application18. However, this method does not fully represent the PF technique and is associated with limited monitoring of flap circulation due to the application of graft on flap. Also, due to its pedicle, it may cause bulk formation especially around the ankle, hindering the patient’s ability to wear shoes. Another method to prevent venous congestion is based on the technique applied by Mishra et al. In this technique, flap rotation is performed by preserving one side of a vein passing through the PF19. Since this method does not involve a supercharged vascular anastomosis, it is a more practical, and effective approach for preventing deep venous congestion. However, it is used less commonly as it may cause tension in the veins during rotation as well as restricted rotation due to insufficient vein length, and require the identification of a solid vein along the axial length of the flap. Chaput et al. proposed the concept of delayed venous angiosome22. In this concept, the flap is planned much larger compared to the size of the tissue defect, and the flap is elevated. Venous congestion is allowed to develop in the flap donor site for 48–72 h. The venous angiosome area becomes apparent, and any parts at risk of necrosis are debrided before using the flap to close the tissue defect. This method has several disadvantages such as planning the flap larger than usual, resulting in a wider donor area, allowing a portion of the flap to undergo necrosis in an uncontrolled manner, and the difficulty in predicting how much of the flap will necrose. Another method that can be sporadically used in the case of venous congestion is vacuum-assisted closure therapy14. Additionally, hyperbaric oxygen therapy represents an effective method used in the management of venous congestion14. As can be seen in the literature, many surgeons have encountered many problems due to the development of venous congestion after PF surgery, and have performed various methods. Although there is no consensus on this issue, performing supercharging statistically significantly reduces the rate of postoperative venous congestion.

One of the factors that increase the risk of complications is the tension applied to the flap during adaptation. Placing tension on the flap during adaptation can lead to inadequate arterial perfusion, as well as wound dehiscence and postoperative edema14. An arc of rotation greater than 90 degrees is another factor that increases the risk of complications. Extensive arc of rotation may result in collapse of venous vessels, leading to increased venous congestion, and may also cause arterial insufficiency1,6,15. In our study, it was found that the risk of MVC increased statistically significantly when the PF rotation degree exceeded 167 ± 13 (p < 0.05; p = 0.001). Additionally, although not statistically significant, complications are proportionally more common in ATA-induced PF. These findings support these studies in the literature.

In managing complications, the basic principle is that the faster the complication is identified, the quicker a solution can be implemented21.

Conclusion

PFs are contemporary and rapidly applicable flaps for the reconstruction of distal lower extremity. With increasing surgical experience, they have the potential to become the first choice method. The practical identification of perforator arteries using CTA allows for rapid planning, while the versatility of the PF adds to its advantages. The most common and challenging complication, venous congestion, can be addressed through leech therapy and by supercharging abundant regional superficial veins during dissection. An increase in the source vessel diameter significantly increases the need for supercharging, while an increase in the degree of flap rotation significantly increases the risk of MVC. In PF surgeries, these two situations should be carefully examined. The increasing popularity of this flap application, coupled with the diverse experiences to be gained, can provide more effective solutions for plastic surgeons.
