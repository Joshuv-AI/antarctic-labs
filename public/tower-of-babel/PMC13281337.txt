Is less always more? Reframing the role of bronchoscopy during percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy
Authors: Nicolas Chardon, Thomas Raffoux, Laure Fayolle Pivot, Baptiste Balança, Stanislas Ledochowski
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the randomized controlled trial by Nachshon et al. [1], which found that, in experienced hands, bronchoscopic guidance during percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT) did not reduce major complications or mortality, while increasing procedural duration by a minute. We also read the subsequent comment by Qin and Zhang [2], who raised important questions about the use of laryngoscopy for procedural visualisation and about complication rates in experienced hands, including whether the single procedure-related death could be linked to the absence of bronchoscopy. Building on both contributions, we would like to suggest a slightly different framing: Is less always more? Or, is it justifiable, for a marginal gain of one procedural minute, to expose patients to any possible additional complication? For a planned procedure that has not been shown to impact mortality?

As tracheostomy is performed in difficult-to-wean patients, a population we can reasonably consider at high risk. Quality care in this population is at the core of our concerns. We are apprehensive of the message delivered by Nachshon et al., whose high-volume center (12 tracheostomy per month) may limit external representativeness (In French ICUs, tracheostomy is performed in approximately 7–10% of mechanically ventilated patients, with substantial inter-unit variability and higher reported use in neurocritical care populations [3, 4]). In fact, we would like to advocate the opposite: the key question may not be whether bronchoscopy should be used routinely, but whether intraluminal guidance alone is sufficient.

In the trial by Nachshon et al., the comparison was bronchoscopy versus direct laryngoscopy. Although clinically relevant, this design compares two airway-based approaches, neither of which directly maps the pretracheal soft-tissue plane crossed by the needle before tracheal entry. Several feared complications of PDT are extraluminal in their origin. Ultrasound visualizes the pretracheal plane, adding invaluable information concerning site selection and needle advancement through the soft tissues up to tracheal entry thus reducing procedural risks especially puncture of cervical vessels or of the thyroid isthmus. It has been extensively studied, with a recent meta-analysis by Ren et al. describing its impact on procedural complications [5, 6].

Using two guidance strategies addresses complementary, rather than redundant, risks. Ultrasound visualizes what bronchoscopy cannot (the pretracheal plane) while bronchoscopy ensures the safety of the procedure once the airway has been entered: safe intraluminal progression and cannula placement. These windows are sequential. Such complementarity is clinically relevant not only in the periprocedural period, but also for late complications [7].

We advocate the use of direct visualisation of the procedure through bronchoscopy rather than laryngoscopy, as this intraluminal benefit is not merely theoretical. In a recent meta-analysis of seven randomized trials (639 patients), bronchoscopic guidance (compared with non-bronchoscopic PDT) was associated with fewer tube misplacements and posterior tracheal wall injuries and a higher first-attempt puncture success rate, albeit on moderate-certainty evidence limited by low event rates [8]. Retaining bronchoscopy within a combined approach therefore preserves intraluminal safeguards that ultrasound cannot provide once the needle has entered the airway.

In our practice as described in Fig. 1, we use this combined approach: pre-procedural ultrasound to identify the thyroid isthmus or pyramidal lobe, anterior jugular and pretracheal vessels, and the relationship of the planned puncture site to the brachiocephalic trunk; real-time ultrasound during puncture to maintain a midline intercartilaginous trajectory; and fibreoptic bronchoscopy to confirm the absence of posterior wall injury, correct guidewire descent without false passage, absence of significant intratracheal bleeding during dilation, and final cannula position.

Combined extraluminal (ultrasound, A–D) and intraluminal (fibreoptic bronchoscopy, E–H) guidance during percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy, shown in procedural sequence. (A) Transverse cervical ultrasound using a vascular probe; the asterisk (*) marks the hyperechoic endotracheal tube within the airway, allowing its identification before withdrawal. (B) Both thyroid lobes (**) and the median extension of the isthmus are delineated, so the puncture can avoid thyroid tissue. (C) Color-Doppler identification of paratracheal vessels (open arrowhead) to be avoided during puncture. (D) Real-time out-of-plane view during puncture: the acoustic shadow of the needle (solid arrowhead) confirms a midline trajectory before luminal entry. (E) Endotracheal visualisation of the procedure through a bronchoscope : confirmation of needle entry without posterior tracheal wall contact. (F) Endotracheal intraluminal visualisation of the guidewire. (G) Visualisation during dilation. (H) Carina visualised through the tracheostomy cannula at the end of the procedure, confirming correct intraluminal placement

Conclusion

Rather than comparing procedural durations, future multicenter trials could more informatively compare the safety of single-modality guidance with a combined extraluminal-plus-intraluminal strategy, particularly in patients at higher anatomical or bleeding risk. Such a design would directly address the residual concerns raised by Qin and Zhang while preserving the intraluminal safeguards suggested by the available randomized evidence.
