Subungual Glomus Tumor With Nail Plate Defect in a 6‐Year‐Old Child: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Meiling Liu, Yuzhong Wang, Siyi Xie, Linjun Du, Bo Chang, Shuxing Cao, Yongzhou Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe present a 6‐year‐old boy with a subungual glomus tumor of the left ring finger associated with nail plate damage. Diagnosis was confirmed by ultrasound; surgical excision yielded good recovery. This report improves clinical recognition of this extremely rare presentation.Keywords: case report, child, nail plate defect, subungual glomus tumor

Introduction

Glomus tumors (GTs) are rare benign perivascular mesenchymal neoplasms composed of modified smooth muscle cells [1]. In 1812, Wood first described it as a painful subcutaneous nodule [2], and later in 1924, Masson identified its origin from the normal glomus body and named it glomus tumor (GT) [2, 3]. GTs account for approximately 2% of all soft tissue tumors and are most commonly seen in adults between 20 and 40 years of age, with no significant sex predilection. They are rare in children [4], and to date, there have been no clear reports in the literature of cases involving concomitant damage to the nail plate structure. These tumors can occur in any part of the body but are most frequently found in the subcutaneous tissue of the extremities, particularly in the nail bed region [5]. Clinically, cases with associated nail plate damage are especially uncommon. Surgical excision is the mainstay treatment for GTs, and complete removal of benign lesions generally results in a cure.

This case is uniquely compelling as it documents the rarely reported concomitant damage to the nail plate structure, serving as a critical reminder for clinicians to consider glomus tumors in the differential diagnosis of painful nail lesions even when structural deformity is present.

Case Presentation

A 6‐year‐old boy presented with a 6‐month history of abnormal morphology of the left ring fingernail, with no apparent cause. He denied any history of hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, hepatitis, or tuberculosis, and had no history of trauma. There was no family or genetic history of similar conditions, and his daily routine was reported as regular.

Case Analysis and Clinical Treatment

He presented to our hospital 21 days prior. Preoperative coagulation tests revealed a prothrombin time (PT) of 11.8 s, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) of 35.7 s, fibrinogen (FG) level of 1.36 g/L, D‐dimer level of 5.07 mg/L, and fibrin degradation products (FDP) at 12.1 mg/L. Although the fibrinogen level was lower than normal, the patient had no apparent tendency to bleed in daily life.

A comprehensive physical examination showed nail deformity and partial detachment of the left ring finger, without discharge or active bleeding. There was mild localized tenderness on palpation (Figure 1a,b). Active and passive range of motion in all joints of the left hand were normal, with no significant abnormalities in muscle strength, sensation, or circulation. The remainder of the orthopedic examination was unremarkable.

Imaging Examination

Ultrasonography revealed a hypoechoic area near the proximal nail bed of the left ring finger (Figure 2).

Ultrasonographic findings of a subungual glomus tumor in the left ring finger.

After performing digital nerve block anesthesia, bilateral incisions were made at the proximal edge of the nail of the left ring finger. The skin was incised, and the subcutaneous tissue was bluntly dissected to expose the nail matrix (Figure 3a). The fingernail was removed, revealing a thin layer of nail plate covering a defect in the center. A mass was palpated under the proximal nail bed. A longitudinal incision was made in the nail bed to expose the lesion. The tumor capsule was carefully dissected. The lesion appeared moderately firm and was separated from the underlying tissue, extending distally to the phalanx. The proximal portion was carefully dissected from the nail bed, and the tumor was completely excised. Grossly, the mass measured approximately 0.1 × 0.2 × 0.1 cm. It appeared as a pink, encapsulated nodular soft tissue mass with a mucoid‐like surface (Figure 3b).

Gross and intraoperative findings of a subungual glomus tumor in the left ring finger. (a) Intraoperative view showing dissection of subcutaneous tissue and exposure of the nail matrix. (b) A pink soft tissue mass measuring approximately 0.1 × 0.2 × 0.1 cm was found beneath the nail bed.

Histopathological examination of the lesion from the left ring finger revealed scattered myxoid cells and spindle‐shaped cells. The tumor cells were round with well‐defined borders, lightly stained cytoplasm—some with vacuolated appearance—and centrally located, slightly enlarged round nuclei. Occasional mitotic figures were observed (Figure 4a–c).

Histopathological and immunohistochemical findings of a subungual glomus tumor in the left ring finger. (a–c) Tumor cells are round with well‐defined borders and lightly stained cytoplasm, some showing vacuolated changes. Nuclei are round, slightly enlarged, and centrally located. Occasional mitotic figures are observed. The stroma shows myxoid degeneration (H&E staining, ×200). (d–g) Immunohistochemistry reveals positive staining for CD34, SMA, Vimentin, ERG (vascular) (IHC, ×100). (h–j) Tumor cells are negative for Desmin, EMA, and S‐100 (IHC, ×100).

Immunohistochemical staining showed the following results: CD34 (+), Desmin (−), EMA (−), ERG (positive in vascular structures), LCA (−), S‐100 (−), SMA (+), Vimentin (+), CD31 (positive in vascular structures) (Figure 4a–j). Based on the immunophenotype, the diagnosis was consistent with a glomus tumor.

Conclusion

Although glomus tumors in children are extremely rare, most are benign and can be completely cured through surgical excision. In this report, we describe a 6‐year‐old boy with a subungual glomus tumor of the left ring finger accompanied by nail plate damage—an even rarer presentation among subungual GTs, and possibly the first such case reported in the literature. The diagnosis was confirmed by postoperative histopathology, providing valuable insight for better clinical understanding of such rare presentations.

Discussion

Glomus tumors (GTs) are rare benign soft tissue neoplasms that typically arise in regions rich in glomus bodies, such as the subungual nail bed, palm, wrist, forearm, and deep dermis of the foot. The incidence shows no significant gender difference and is most commonly observed in adults, while pediatric cases are exceedingly rare [4]. In particular, GTs associated with nail plate structural damage have not been clearly reported to date. This case describes a 6‐year‐old boy with a subungual GT of the left ring finger accompanied by nail plate deformity—possibly the first such report in the clinical literature.

Clinically, GTs usually present as subcutaneous nodules, with coloration ranging from red to purple or blue depending on the lesion's depth. While the nail bed of the fingers is a common site, solitary GTs have also been reported in almost all parts of the body, including deep soft tissues, bones, and even visceral organs such as the stomach and small intestine [6]. In most cases, complete surgical excision leads to cure with excellent prognosis.

Differentiating GTs from superficial acral fibromyxoma (SAFM) can be challenging. SAFM is a rare benign soft tissue tumor with distinct clinical characteristics: it frequently arises in the nail bed and periungual region of the hands and feet and often causes progressive nail plate deformity [7, 8]. Histologically, SAFM is composed of spindle and stellate‐shaped cells with pale eosinophilic cytoplasm embedded in a characteristic myxoid stroma, often mixed with mucinous and dense collagen components—features that differ from those of GTs. Immunohistochemically, SAFM typically shows strong positivity for CD34, CD99, vimentin, and epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), while markers such as S‐100, smooth muscle actin (SMA), and cytokeratin are negative [8].

Nodular hidradenoma, which commonly arises on the sole or lateral edge of the foot, usually presents as a hemispherical papule, nodule, or plaque [9]. Histologically, nodular hidradenoma and GTs can be difficult to distinguish due to shared features, including focal epithelial‐like cell clusters, mucinous stroma, and variable vascularity. In particular, solid hidradenomas without ductal structures and GTs with inconspicuous vascular components pose a diagnostic challenge [10]. However, the presence of glomus cells surrounding vessels at the tumor periphery is characteristic of GTs and helps differentiate them from hidradenomas and other adnexal tumors. Additionally, GTs lack ductal differentiation and do not produce epithelial mucin. Immunohistochemistry is helpful in distinguishing the two: hidradenomas typically express cytokeratin, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and EMA, while GTs do not express these markers [10].

In conclusion, although glomus tumors associated with nail plate damage are rare, complete surgical excision remains an effective and curative treatment.
