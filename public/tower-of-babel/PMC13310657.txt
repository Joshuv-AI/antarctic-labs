Mycobacterium marinum Infection Misdiagnosed as Skin Lesions of Sjögren’s Syndrome: A Case Report of Fishbone Injury and Literature Review
Authors: Di Li, CongJie Li, Bin Hu, JianChen Xiao, XiaoFeng Jin, ZhongDa Zhang, WenKui Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCutaneous infections caused by Mycobacterium marinum are rare. We report a case initially misdiagnosed as skin lesions associated with Sjögren’s syndrome but clinically highly suspected of Mycobacterium marinum infection, and we review the relevant literature. The patient was a 39-year-old male with a prior history of Sjögren’s syndrome. Following an accidental fishbone injury at work, he developed skin redness, swelling, and ulceration on the right middle and little fingers, which was subsequently followed by multiple nodules on the dorsum of the right hand and upper limb. After debridement, suturing, and pathological biopsy, the wound showed delayed healing for four months, with persistent redness, swelling, suppuration, and exudation. Three weeks after initiating empirical treatment with oral clarithromycin combined with rifampicin, the wound redness and swelling subsided, and the lesion healed with crust formation. Based on the patient’s history of fishbone injury, characteristic skin lesions, histopathological findings, and response to empirical treatment, although the causative pathogen has not been definitively identified, a clinical suspicion of Mycobacterium marinum infection was made.

Introduction

Mycobacterium marinum is a nontuberculous mycobacterium primarily inhabiting aquatic environments. Human infection typically occurs when damaged skin comes into direct contact with contaminated sources,1 and commonly manifests as inflammation of the skin and subcutaneous tissues, often progressing to chronic granuloma formation.2 First isolated from saltwater fish at the Philadelphia Aquarium in 1926, the first human case of Mycobacterium marinum infection was reported in 1951.3 The incidence of Mycobacterium marinum infection is relatively low, with an estimated 0.05–0.27 cases per 100,000 population annually.4 However, its nonspecific clinical presentation often leads to diagnostic delays, and many patients initially fail to receive accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment. We report a case initially misdiagnosed as skin lesions associated with Sjögren’s syndrome but clinically highly suspected of Mycobacterium marinum infection.

Case Report

A 39-year-old male presented with a two-month history of soft tissue infection of the right fingers and multiple masses on the right upper extremity. Two months prior to admission, the patient accidentally sustained a fishbone injury to his right middle and little fingers at work and received only simple disinfection. One week after the injury, the wounded areas developed soft tissue swelling, followed by ulceration and purulent discharge (Figure 1A). After approximately one week of self-administered wound care with hydrogen peroxide, the ulcers healed and the swelling subsided. One month after the injury, erythema and ulceration recurred at the original injury sites, and multiple firm nodules approximately the size of a bean gradually appeared on the dorsum of the right hand and the right upper extremity. Local skin temperature was normal, and no abnormalities in sensation or muscle strength of the right upper limb were observed (Figure 1B and C). The patient underwent local debridement, suturing, and red and blue light therapy at a dermatology hospital for 20 days; however, the ulcers on the right middle finger, little finger, and hand dorsum failed to heal, prompting a visit to our department. Considering the patient’s underlying autoimmune disease and the indeterminate nature of the multiple masses on the right upper extremity, the patient and his family strongly requested a pathological examination for a definitive diagnosis. Following a departmental discussion, a surgical biopsy and bacterial culture of the purulent material were scheduled. Subsequently, under brachial plexus block anesthesia, a biopsy of the masses on the right forearm was performed. Longitudinal incisions (approximately 1.5–2 cm in length) were made over six masses on the right forearm and hand. The skin and subcutaneous tissue were incised layer by layer, revealing fragmented inflammatory granulation tissue with ill-defined borders mixed with a small amount of yellowish purulent fluid. The abnormal tissue was thoroughly excised, and the lesion bed was curetted down to healthy deep fascia. The residual cavities were irrigated with copious normal saline, soaked in iodophor for 20 minutes, rinsed again with sterile normal saline, and then sutured layer by layer. Postoperative pathology revealed predominantly fibrofatty tissue with chronic inflammation, accompanied by focal fat necrosis, histiocytic infiltration, and granulation tissue formation. Focal aggregations of neutrophils and areas of focal necrosis were observed (Figure 2). Bacterial culture of the specimen showed no growth. Based on the pathological and bacteriological findings, the skin redness, ulceration, and multiple masses were initially considered to be associated with Sjögren’s syndrome. The patient was advised to be referred to the Rheumatology and Immunology department for further management of the underlying disease. At a four-month follow-up visit, the surgical incisions had been sutures removed. However, only two of the six incisions had healed; the remaining four were erythematous with central ulceration and purulent discharge. The soft tissue infection of the middle and little fingers persisted with unhealed ulceration (Figure 3A). Reassessment of the medical history, considering the fishbone injury, characteristic skin lesions, and pathological findings, suggested a possible Mycobacterium marinum infection. Consequently, empirical anti-infective therapy with clarithromycin and rifampicin was initiated. NTM culture or metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was considered for definitive diagnosis if necessary. At a three-week follow-up after treatment initiation, the patient’s wounds showed reduced erythema and swelling, and the ulcerated areas had formed crusts (Figure 3B), indicating a positive response to treatment. Discussion

According to national and international literature, the incidence of cutaneous involvement in patients with Sjögren’s syndrome ranges from approximately 14% to 16%.5 The primary manifestations include xeroderma, pruritus, annular erythema, skin ulcers, urticarial vasculitis, and cutaneous nodules,6 with the histopathological feature being lymphocytic infiltration.7 In this case, the patient had a definitive six-month history of Sjögren’s syndrome and positive autoantibodies (anti-SSA and anti-SSB antibodies), confirming the diagnosis. However, the patient showed a poor therapeutic response four months after a biopsy of a right forearm mass. The skin lesions primarily presented as a soft tissue infection of the right fingers and multiple isolated nodules on the right upper extremity. This distribution differed from the generalized cutaneous involvement typically seen in Sjögren’s syndrome. Furthermore, the histopathological finding was inflammatory granulomas, which contrasts with the lymphocytic infiltration characteristic of Sjögren’s syndrome. Considering the patient’s clear history of a fishbone injury, a high suspicion of Mycobacterium marinum infection was raised.

Mycobacterium marinum infection typically manifests as chronic granulomatous inflammation of the skin and subcutaneous tissue. Epidemiological data show a strong association with contaminated swimming pools or fish tanks, leading to the historical terms “swimming pool granuloma” or “fish tank granuloma”.8 Human infection usually results from skin trauma followed by exposure to infected aquatic animals or contaminated water, such as a fishbone injury while handling contaminated fish or sustaining an abrasion in polluted water.9 The infection most frequently involves the fingers, particularly of the right hand (the dominant hand for most people), with an incubation period of 1 to 3 weeks. Lesions typically begin at the site of injury.10 Clinical manifestations are classified into four types: Type I presents as isolated papules, nodules, or plaques, which may be associated with erosion, ulceration, crusting, or verrucous changes; Type II is characterized by sporotrichoid spread along lymphatic vessels; Type III involves deep structures (eg, bursitis, tenosynovitis, arthritis, osteomyelitis), with or without cutaneous involvement; Type IV manifests as disseminated infection and bacteremia, almost exclusively in immunocompromised patients.11

The diagnosis of this disease requires a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s history (including occupational and trauma history), clinical manifestations, histopathology, and etiological examinations.12 The gold standard for diagnosis is the detection of M. marinum in skin lesions. However, due to the typically low bacterial load in the lesions, culture is particularly essential. According to the Runyon classification, M. marinum is a slow-growing photochromogen. Colonies are usually visible after 2 to 3 weeks of culture on Löwenstein-Jensen medium at 30–32°C, with a positive culture rate of approximately 70% to 80%.13 Histopathology often shows non-specific granulomatous inflammation, which varies with the disease stage. Typical changes may include epidermal hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and infiltration of the superficial dermis by various inflammatory cells such as lymphocytes, plasma cells, and neutrophils, along with focal epithelioid cell granulomas.14 In recent years, molecular biology techniques have been successfully applied for the identification of this bacterium, providing a new diagnostic approach. Among these, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) offers the advantages of rapidity and accuracy, although its cost is high.15

Susceptibility studies indicate that M. marinum is often resistant to isoniazid and pyrazinamide. It is highly sensitive to rifampicin and rifabutin. It shows good sensitivity to minocycline, doxycycline, clarithromycin, linezolid, sparfloxacin, moxifloxacin, imipenem, sulfamethoxazole, and amikacin. Conversely, it exhibits poor sensitivity to trimethoprim, azithromycin, telithromycin, quinupristin-dalfopristin, ciprofloxacin, gemifloxacin, ofloxacin, and levofloxacin.16,17 According to the joint 2007 guidelines from the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA), a combination of two active drugs for 3 to 4 months is recommended, supplemented by surgical debridement for invasive infections.18 With appropriate anti-infective therapy, the prognosis for this disease is generally favorable. In most patients, the condition gradually resolves within 3 to 6 months of treatment, although complete resolution may occasionally take several years in some cases.

Returning to this case, the patient had a clear history of fishbone injury prior to disease onset, which is consistent with the epidemiological characteristics of Mycobacterium marinum infection. The skin lesions were distributed on the right upper extremity, and their number, morphology, and distribution along the lymphatic vessels are consistent with a Type II infection. The histopathological finding of infectious granulomas with neutrophilic infiltration further supports this diagnosis. After three weeks of empirical treatment with clarithromycin combined with rifampicin, the patient’s wound erythema and swelling subsided, and the ulcerated areas formed crusts, demonstrating a definite therapeutic effect. In summary, based on the history of fishbone injury, characteristic skin lesions, histopathological findings, and response to empirical treatment, there is a high clinical suspicion of Mycobacterium marinum infection.

Conclusion

The diagnosis of Mycobacterium marinum infection is challenging due to its low incidence and the non-specific clinical signs and symptoms it presents, which demands considerable clinical experience. As a result, diagnosis is often delayed, and many patients initially fail to receive accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment. Therefore, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with a history of exposure to contaminants such as fishbone injuries.
