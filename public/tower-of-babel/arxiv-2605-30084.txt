Supercooling of liquids, as described by the Enskog–Vlasov kinetic equation
E. S. Benilov

Abstract
A model combining Enskog’s collision integral for dense fluids with a Vlasov-style description of the van der Waals force is applied to supercooling. First, the spinodal temperature T s T_{s} is calculated, at which a liquid becomes unstable to small perturbations and transitions to solid. In particular, it turns out that isochoric cooling allows one to reach a lower temperature than isobaric cooling. Second, the surface tension of a supercooled liquid–vapor interface is shown to diverge at T s T_{s} . The singularity is caused by an oscillatory region emerging on the liquid side of the interface as T → T s T\rightarrow T_{s} ; it develops because the liquid approaches instability, and the interface starts radiating (so far, evanescent) waves. At T = T s T=T_{s} , the waves cease to be evanescent and the oscillatory region extends to infinity—hence, the singularity of the surface tension. Since this effect has a clear physical interpretation, it should occur regardless of the model and approximations under which it was obtained. This and the other results of the paper are illustrated using argon and several other fluids.



I
Introduction

The Enskog–Vlasov (EV) kinetic equation was proposed in 1971 by Grmela

Grmela 1971
as a tool for studying phase transitions. It differs from the
classical Boltzmann equation
Boltzmann 1972
in two respects. First, it
includes a term describing the van der Waals force, formulated in the spirit
of Vlasov’s description of electromagnetic forces in plasmas
Vlasov 1968
.
Second, it employs Enskog’s collision integral
Enskog 1922
for

dense
fluids, instead of Boltzmann’s integral for
dilute
fluids.
Since 1971, the EV equation has been used in both theoretical (e.g., Refs.

Benilov and Benilov 2018
;
Benilov and Benilov 2019a
;
Benilov and Benilov 2019b
;
Struchtrup and Frezzotti 2022
) and computational (e.g., Refs.

Barbante et al. 2012
;
Barbante et al. 2015
;
Wu et al. 2015
;
Sadr and Gorji 2019
;
Busuioc et al. 2023
) studies.

Note that the Enskog–Vlasov equation can be derived from first principles
only for a fluid of hard spheres
van Beijeren and Ernst 1973a
. For real fluids,
it should be viewed as a phenomenological model and, thus, needs to be
calibrated—i.e., its parameters should be chosen to ensure that the
properties of the ‘theoretical’ fluid match those of the ‘real’ one. Most
importantly, the EV model can be fully calibrated using the fluid’s critical
and triple-point parameters. These
macroscopic
characteristics are well
defined and easier to measure than, say, the
microscopic
free-energy
barrier in the classical nucleation theory. More generally, a theory
calibrated on the very parameters it predicts is vulnerable to the accusation
of being merely a data fit, not reflecting the physics of the phenomenon in question.

In this work, the Enskog–Vlasov equation is used to calculate the parameters
of supercooled fluids. The simplest version of the EV model will be used—the
one for spherical molecules—so that the rotational degrees of freedom can be
ignored. The results are illustrated using argon. To see how deviation from
spherical symmetry affects supercooling, several diatomic fluids are
considered: nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine (all with quadrupolar, axisymmetric
molecules), and carbon dioxide (dipolar, axisymmetric). The nonrotational EV
model describes their thermodynamic properties surprisingly well (see Appendix

A
), but their heat capacities remain inaccurate, and so the results
obtained for these fluids should be viewed as indicative only. It is also
worthwhile—and requires little extra work—to consider one example of a
triatomic fluid, namely water (admittedly, the EV model approximates its
properties less accurately than those of the other fluids considered,
particularly its surface tension). It can, however, be argued that some of the
qualitative results obtained for polyatomic fluids using the nonrotational EV
equation have a clear physical interpretation and should therefore persist in
a more accurate model.

The paper has the following structure. In Sect.
II
, the Enskog–Vlasov
model is formulated and calibrated. In Sect.
III
, the model is applied
to flat liquid–vapor interfaces, and in Sect.
IV
, to spinodal
instability (when liquid transitions to solid). The results are compared to
those of experiments and molecular dynamics simulations in Sect.
V
.

II
Formulation

II.1
The Enskog–Vlasov kinetic equation

Consider a fluid characterized by the distribution function
f(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{r},t)
, where
\mathbf{v}
is the molecular velocity,
\mathbf{r}

the position vector, and
t
the time. Let the fluid’s molecules have mass
m

and be affected by a force
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r},t)
.

The generic form of a kinetic equation is

\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v},t)}{\partial t}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla}f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v},t)\\
+\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r},t)\cdot\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v},t)}{\partial\mathbf{v}}=\mathrm{St}[f],

(1)

where the collision integral
\mathrm{St}[f]
is a functional of

f(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{r},t)
.

To specify
\mathrm{St}[f]
, consider two molecules with velocities

\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{v}_{1}
, which collide and change their velocities
to
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\prime}
. Approximating the
molecules by hard spheres, one can readily show that

\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{u}\right),\qquad\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{u}\right),

(2)

where
\mathbf{u}
is the unit vector directed from the centre of the sphere
with velocity
\mathbf{v}_{1}
toward the impact point, and

\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}.

(3)

Introduce also the scattering cross-section
\sigma
and note that, for hard
spheres of diameter
D
, it is given by

\sigma=D^{2}\frac{\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{u}}{\left|\mathbf{g}\right|}\operatorname{H}(\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{u}),

(4)

where
\operatorname{H}
is the Heaviside step function.

In the present paper, the following collision integral is
used:

\mathrm{St}=\int\int\left[\eta(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}+D\mathbf{u},t)\,f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},t)\,f(\mathbf{r}+D\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\prime},t)-\eta(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}-D\mathbf{u},t)\,f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v},t)\,f(\mathbf{r}-D\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}_{1},t)\right]\left|\mathbf{g}\right|\sigma\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{v}_{1},

(5)

where the coefficient
\eta(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{1},t)

describes high-density effects. To date, it has been calculated from first
principles only for a fluid of hard spheres
van Beijeren and Ernst 1973a
and is
otherwise treated as an adjustable parameter. As such, it should still comply
with fundamental requirements: the Onsager reciprocal relation, the H theorem,
and the correct dilute-gas limit:
\eta\rightarrow 1
as
D\rightarrow 0
(so
that (
5
) reduces to the standard Boltzmann integral). To emphasize the
importance of these requirements, note that the original version of Enskog’s
collision integral
Enskog 1922
was shown
not
to satisfy the Onsager
relations and the H theorem
Lebowitz et al. 1969
. It was subsequently
modified
van Beijeren and Ernst 1973a
;
van Beijeren and Ernst 1973b
, and the new version
does satisfy both
van Beijeren and Ernst 1973a
;
van Beijeren and Ernst 1973b
;
Résibois 1978

(it is actually the one which describes the fluid of hard spheres).

In this paper, the following expression for
\eta
is used:

\eta(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{1},t)=1+\sum_{l=2}^{L}c_{l}\int^{l-1}\left[{\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{l}}n(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)\,\operatorname{H}(D-\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\right|)\,\operatorname{H}(D-\left|\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\right|)\right]\left[{\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{l-1}}\,\,{\displaystyle\prod_{j=i+1}^{l}}\operatorname{H}(D-\left|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{\mathbf{r}}_{j}\right|)\right]{\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{l}}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}_{i},

(6)

where

n(\mathbf{r},t)=\int f(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{r},t)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{v},

(7)

is the number density;
c_{2}
,
c_{3}
, …
c_{L}
are undetermined
coefficients;
\int^{l}
denotes
l
repeated integrals; and it is implied
that, if the lower limit of a product is larger than the upper one, the
product is equal to unity—e.g.,

{\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{1}}\cdots=1.

As shown in Ref.
Benilov and Benilov 2018
, series (
6
) satisfies all the
fundamental requirements. The fluid of hard spheres correspond to
c_{l}=1

for all
l
, and the individual terms in (
6
) represent overlaps
between two, three, four, etc. spheres, to be excluded from the allowable
phase space. In the general case, the coefficients
c_{l}
and the molecular
diameter
D
should be treated as adjustable parameters, to be fixed when the
EV model is calibrated.

Let
\mathbf{F}
in the kinetic equation (
1
) be the van der Waals
force exerted by the molecules on one another, characterized by a pairwise
potential
\Phi(r)
where
r
is the distance between two molecules. The
collective force acting on a particle located at a position
\mathbf{r}
can
then be written in the form

\mathbf{F(r},t)=-\mathbf{\nabla}\int\Phi\left(\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{1}\right|\right)\,n\mathbf{(r}_{1},t)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}_{1},

(8)

where it is assumed that
\Phi(r)
decays sufficiently fast as
r\rightarrow\infty
. There are two global characteristics of
\Phi(r)
that are
particularly important:

a=-\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\int\Phi(r)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r},

(9)

K=-\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\int\frac{r^{2}}{3}\Phi(r)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r},

(10)

which will be referred to as the van der Waals and Korteweg constants,
respectively. The former is the same
a
which appears in van der Waals’s
classical equation of state, and the latter characterizes the fluid’s surface
tension. Note also that

R=\sqrt{\frac{K}{a}}

(11)

can be viewed as a characteristic radius of the van der Waals force.

Since the potential of the ‘real’ van der Waals force is difficult to
calculate,
\Phi(r)
will be treated in this paper as an adjustable parameter.

II.2
Energy and entropy

Assume for the sake of argument that
f(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{r},t)
is spatially
periodic along all three dimensions. Then, it can be readily verified that the
EV kinetic model (
1
)–(
8
) conserves the total number of
molecules and the total energy,

N=\int n(\mathbf{r},t)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r},

(12)

E={\displaystyle\iint}\frac{m\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}{2}f(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{r},t)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{v}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}\\
+\frac{1}{2}{\displaystyle\iint}n(\mathbf{r},t)\,n(\mathbf{r}_{1},t)\,\Phi\left(\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{1}\right|\right)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r},

(13)

where the integrals are implied to be over one cell of the periodic formation.
The total momentum is also conserved, but it is not needed in this paper.

As shown in Ref.
Grmela and Garcia-Colin 1980a

theorem—i.e.,
\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t\geq 0

(14)

where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant,
f_{0}
is an arbitrary constant (can
be fixed as convenient), and the non-ideal part of the entropy,

, is
related to the coefficient
\eta
by

\mathbf{\nabla}\frac{\delta Q[n]}{\delta n(\mathbf{r},t)}=\int\eta(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{1},t)\,n(\mathbf{r}_{1},t)\,(\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{\mathbf{r}})\,\delta(\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{\mathbf{r}}_{1}\right|-1)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}_{1}.

(15)

If
\eta
is given by expression (
6
), Eq. (
15
) yields
Benilov and Benilov 2018
,

Q[n]=\frac{1}{2}{\displaystyle\iint}n(\mathbf{r},t)\,n(\mathbf{r}_{1},t)\,\operatorname{H}(D-\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{\mathbf{r}}_{1}\right|)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}_{1}\\
+\sum_{l=2}^{L}\frac{c_{l}}{l\left(l+1\right)}\int^{l}\int n(\mathbf{r},t)\left[{\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{l}}n(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)\operatorname{H}(D-\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\right|)\right]\left[{\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}}\,\,{\displaystyle\prod_{j=i+1}^{l}}\operatorname{H}(D-\left|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{\mathbf{r}}_{j}\right|)\right]\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r\,}{\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{l}}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}_{i}.

(16)

Eq. (
15
) also explains why Enskog’s original collision
integral
Enskog 1922
does not satisfy the H theorem: it assumes
\eta
to
be a function of the density
n(\mathbf{r},t)
evaluated at the impact point
of the colliding molecules—in which case Eq. (
15
) admits solutions
for
Q
only if
n
satisfies an additional constraint
1

To obtain this
constraint, substitute
\eta=\eta(n((\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{1})/2,t))
into
Eq. (

) and take curl of both sides of the resulting equality.

II.3
Equilibrium solutions of the EV equation

The present work is concerned with equilibrium states, in which case the
general EV model (
1
)–(
8
) reduces to a much simpler equation.
This reduction exploits the fact that the scattering cross-section for hard
spheres, (
4
), depends on the direction of
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}
, but not on
\left|\mathbf{g}\right|
; as a result,
the EV equation admits Maxwellian solutions with spatially uniform
temperature, but variable density,

f=\frac{m^{3/2}}{\left(2\pi k_{B}T\right)^{3/2}}n(\mathbf{r})\exp\left(-\frac{mv^{2}}{2k_{B}T}\right),

(17)

where
n(\mathbf{r})
satisfies the following equation
Benilov and Benilov 2018

\ln n(\mathbf{r})+\frac{\delta Q[n]}{\delta n(\mathbf{r})}\\
+\frac{1}{k_{B}T}\int\Phi\left(\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{1}\right|\right)\,n\mathbf{(r}_{1})\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}_{1}=\operatorname{const}.

(18)

Note that
\operatorname{const}
does not depending on
\mathbf{r}
, but may
depend on
T
.

To understand the physical meaning of Eq. (
18
), note that it follows
from the maximum entropy principle.

Indeed, substituting the Maxwellian ansatz (
17
) into expressions
(
12
)–(
14
), one obtains

N=\int n(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r},

(19)

E=\frac{3}{2}k_{B}T\int n(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}\\
+\frac{1}{2}{\displaystyle\iint}\Phi\left(\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{1}\right|\right)\,n(\mathbf{r})\,n(\mathbf{r}_{1})\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r},

(20)

S=\frac{3}{2}k_{B}\int n(\mathbf{r})\left[\ln\frac{2\pi k_{B}T\,f_{0}^{2/3}}{m\,n^{2/3}(\mathbf{r})}+1\right]\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}\\
-k_{B}Q[n].

(21)

It can now be verified that Eq. (
18
) is equivalent to the requirement
that the entropy be at an extremum, subject to constraints of constant energy
and number of molecules—i.e.,

\delta\left(S+\lambda E+\mu N\right)=0,

(22)

where
\lambda
and
\mu
are the Lagrange multipliers. Substituting
expressions (
19
)–(
21
) into Eq. (
22
) and equating to
zero the coefficients of
\delta T
and
\delta n(\mathbf{r})
, one can first
show that
\lambda=-1/T
, and then recover Eq. (
18
) with

\operatorname{const}=\mu/k_{B}T-1
.

II.4
Thermodynamics of EV fluids

The Enskog–Vlasov kinetic model implies certain thermodynamic properties of
the fluid under consideration. These properties can be conveniently deduced
from the EV energy and entropy integrals using the formalism proposed in Ref.

Giovangigli and Matuszewski 2013
.

II.4.1
Thermodynamic definitions

According to Ref.
Giovangigli and Matuszewski 2013
, a fluid is fully
characterized by the dependence of its internal energy
e
and entropy
s

(both per molecule) on the number density
n
and temperature
T
. Note that

e(n,T)
and
s(n,T)
are supposed to satisfy the Gibbs relation, whose
differential form amounts to
Benilov 2023

\frac{\partial e}{\partial T}=T\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}.

(23)

If
e(n,T)
and
s(n,T)
are known for the fluid under consideration, its
pressure
p(n,T)
and chemical potential
G(n,T)
are

Giovangigli and Matuszewski 2013

p=n^{2}\left(\frac{\partial e}{\partial n}-T\frac{\partial s}{\partial n}\right),

(24)

G=\frac{\partial(ne)}{\partial n}-T\frac{\partial(ns)}{\partial n}.

(25)

Using
p(n,T)
and
G(n,T)
, one can calculate the liquid–vapor saturation
curve through the so-called Maxwell construction—i.e., the conditions

p(n^{(v)},T)=p(n^{(l)},T),

(26)

G(n^{(v)},T)=G(n^{(l)},T),

(27)

where
n^{(v)}
and
n^{(l)}
are the vapor and liquid densities, respectively.

II.4.2
Pressure and chemical potential of EV fluids

To determine the per-molecule internal energy
e
and entropy

of an EV
fluid, one needs to apply the thermodynamic approximation to the total EV
energy, (
20
), and the total EV entropy, (
21
). Assuming, thus,
that the spatial scale of

exceeds both the molecular diameter

E=\int n\,e\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r},\qquad S=\int n\,s\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r},

with

(28)

), and

(32)

-2am^{2}n,

(33)

Formulation

, etc.,

\ln n(z)+\pi\int_{-D}^{D}n(z_{1}-z)\,\left(D^{2}-z_{1}^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{L}\frac{c_{l}}{l}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\right)^{l}\left[{\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l}}n(z_{j}-z)\right]F_{l}(z_{1},z_{2}...z_{l}){\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l}}\mathrm{d}z_{j}\\

(36)

where the kernel functions

(

F_{l}=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\right)^{l-1}\left[{\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l}}\operatorname{H}\left(D^{2}-r_{j\bot}^{2}-z_{j}^{2}\right)\right]\left[{\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l-1}}\operatorname{H}\left(D^{2}-r_{j\bot}^{2}+2r_{j\bot}r_{l\bot}\cos\phi_{j}-r_{l\bot}^{2}-\left(z_{j}-z_{l}\right)^{2}\right)\right]\\

(38)

is the one-dimensional reduction of the van der Waals potential, with

To single out the solution of Eq. (

n\rightarrow n^{(l)}\qquad\text{as}\qquad z\rightarrow-\infty,

(39)

n\rightarrow n^{(v)}\qquad\text{as}\qquad z\rightarrow+\infty,

(40)

where

), otherwise boundary-value problem (

.

Boundary conditions (

)–(
40

constant on the right-hand side of Eq. (

) without solving the whole

, where

is the chemical potential given by (

The limit

and condition (

, which is the same value due to

Since Eq. (

)–(
40

can be shifted by an arbitrary

n=\frac{1}{2}\left(n^{(l)}+n^{(v)}\right)\qquad\text{at}\qquad z=0.

(41)

Once the solution

)–(

) is

Benilov 2024

\gamma=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}n(z)\,n(z_{1})\,\Gamma(z-z_{1})\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathrm{d}z,

where

\Gamma(z)=\frac{1}{2}\Psi(z)-\pi z^{2}\Phi(z).

Eq. (

) is a nonlinear integral equation with no small parameters, so
neither exact nor asymptotic solutions are available. The only option is
numerical computation, but even this is not straightforward: expressions
(

increasingly difficult to compute. There is a mitigating circumstance,

it become parameterless—hence, once computed, it can be used for
all

fluids under consideration. It is also helpful that all
F_{l}(z_{1}...z_{l})

vanish if one of their arguments lies outside the interval
\left(-D,D\right)
.

The numerical algorithm used for solving boundary-value problem (
36
)–(
41
) is described in Appendix
B
.

regardless of the model used to describe them
. After all, any adequate

periodic wave emerging when the spinodal point is reached.

Note also the discrepancy between the theoretical and empirical curves in the

IV
Spinodal instability

As shown in Ref.
Benilov and Benilov 2019a
, the EV model predicts two types of
cooling-induced spinodal instability: a transition from one fluid state to
another (vapor to liquid) and a transition from fluid to solid (liquid to
crystal). The former occurs at an infinitely large spatial scale, whereas the
latter occurs at a finite scale equal to the period of the emerging
crystalline structure. These results were illustrated for inert fluids, using
a partially calibrated model, in which
c_{l}
and
a
were fixed, but
K
was
not. Consequently, the spinodal temperature at which crystallization occurs
was not calculated.

This task has now been completed, and the results are presented below.

IV.1
Formulation

As in Ref.
Benilov and Benilov 2019a
, the fluid stability in this work is
analyzed using “frozen waves”, i.e. harmonic
disturbances with zero growth rate. These can be viewed as precursors of
unstable disturbances: if a frozen wave with a wavenumber
k
exists for a
given state, then a small increase or decrease in
k
renders the state
unstable—and the temperature at which the first frozen wave arises marks the
onset of instability.

At the same time, frozen waves are one-dimensional and stationary—thus,
described by the steady equation (
36
), which is incomparably simpler
than the full EV model (
1
)–(
8
). The latter is what one would
have to use if studying growing disturbances.

Consider a spatially uniform state of density
\bar{n}
and let

n=\bar{n}+\tilde{n}(z),

(42)

where
\tilde{n}(z)
is a small perturbation. Substituting (
42
) into
Eq. (
36
), linearizing it, letting the perturbation be harmonic,

\tilde{n}(z)=\operatorname{e}^{\mathrm{i}kz},

and omitting the overbars (
\bar{n}\rightarrow n
), one obtains, after
straightforward algebra,

k_{B}T=-\frac{n\Psi(k)}{1+\dfrac{4\pi}{k^{3}}\left(\sin Dk-Dk\cos Dk\right)n+{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l=2}^{L}}c_{l}n^{l}\hat{F}_{l}(k)},

(43)

where

\hat{\Psi}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi(z_{1})\operatorname{e}^{\mathrm{i}kz_{1}}\mathrm{d}z_{1},

(44)

\hat{F}_{l}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\right)^{l-1}F_{l}(z_{1},z_{2}...z_{l}){\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l-1}}\mathrm{d}z_{j}\right]\operatorname{e}^{\mathrm{i}kz_{l}}\mathrm{d}z_{l}.

(45)

The function
\hat{\Psi}(k)
is the Fourier transform of

\Psi(z)
given by (
38
), and it can be readily calculated analytically.
The function
\hat{F}_{2}(k)
can also be calculated, but
\hat{F}_{l}(k)

with
l\geq 3
have to be computed numerically. Note that, even though
functions
\hat{F}_{l}(k)
involve a parameter, the molecular diameter
D
, it
can be scaled out by representing
\hat{F}_{l}
as a function of
Dk
instead
of
k
, so one can compute them once and for all.

Eq. (
43
) is a stability criterion: if, for a state
\left(n,T\right)
, (
43
) admits a solution for some
k
, this state is unstable.

IV.2
Results and discussion

Eq. (
43
) describes a family of curves
T
vs.
n
, whose members are
characterized by different values of
k
and are therefore denoted by

T_{k}(n)
. The part of the
\left(n,T\right)
plane swept by these curves
is the instability region, and its boundary is the spinodal curve.

The typical behavior of the curves
T_{k}(n)
is explained below and
illustrated for argon in Fig
3
. Note that this and further diagrams
in this paper are drawn in the
density
-temperature plane, not the

pressure
-temperature plane. The former choice conveniently allows one
to distinguish between states with the same pressure but different densities.

The behavior of the curve
T_{k}(n)
when

\infty
,
and its implications for the liquid stability, are illustrated in Fig.

3
and explained below.

•

The curve
T_{0}(n)
is labeled in a self-explanatory manner. It can be
shown that its maximum is located at the critical point.

•

With increasing
k
, the curve
T_{k}(n)
recedes, so that
T_{0}(n)

separates the stability region above it from the instability region below it.
One can show that the latter corresponds to

\frac{\partial p(n,T)}{\partial n}<0.

This condition predicts instability when a local density increase creates a
local pressure dip, thereby drawing in additional fluid.

•

If
k
increases beyond a certain threshold (say,
k_{1}
), the
denominator of expression (
43
) starts to vanish at a certain density
(say,
n_{\ast}
), where the corresponding curve
T(n)
has a vertical
asymptote. At
k=k_{1}
, this asymptote enters through the infinity of the
n

axis, and it retreats back to infinity when
k
reaches a further threshold
(say,
k_{2}
).

Therefore, there must exist
k_{1.5}

between
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, where
n_{\ast}
reaches a minimum (say,

n_{\ast 1.5}
). The region of Fig.
3
located to the right of the
curve
T_{k_{1.5}}(n)
is unstable.

•

There exist further intervals
\left(k_{3},k_{4}\right)
,
\left(k_{5},k_{6}\right)
, etc., where the denominator of expression (
43
)
vanishes at a certain
n_{\ast}
. The corresponding minimum values of

n_{\ast}
, however, are larger than
n_{\ast 1.5}
, so no new regions of
instability arise.

Figure
3
also shows examples of ‘trajectories’ along which a liquid
could be supercooled: isobaric (at constant pressure), isochoric (at constant
volume), and what may be dubbed interfacial (i.e., in a closed container where
liquid and vapor are separated by a flat interface). For ease of comparison,
all three trajectories originate from the triple point.

Evidently, the isobaric and interfacial trajectories are virtually
indistinguishable. Observe also that

(i)

the isobaric and interfacial trajectories are destabilized by a
finite-length wave,
k=k_{1.5}
;

(ii)

whereas the isochoric trajectory is destabilized by an
infinitely-long wave,
k=0
.

Once a trajectory crosses the spinodal curve, the liquid becomes unstable and
turns into crystal, but a difference should be expected between cases (i) and (ii).

In case (i), one may conjecture that the density of the resulting crystalline
argon is comparable to that of the pre-transition liquid,
\rho^{(l)}\approx 1721\,\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{3}
, and that the interatomic distance is
comparable to
2\pi/k\approx 3.18\,\mathrm{\mathring{A}}
. For comparison, the
triple-point density of crystalline argon is
1616\,\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{3}
, and the corresponding nearest-neighbor distance is

3.75\,\mathrm{\mathring{A}}
.

In case (ii), the characteristics of the resulting crystal are likely
determined in the course of solidification. In particular, the crystal’s
density should exceed noticeably that of the pre-transition liquid, since the
latter is well below the triple-point density of crystalline argon.

To emphasize the difference, the right-hand region of instability in Fig.

3
is labeled “Crystal” (as it is
likely populated by crystalline states), and the lower region
“No-man’s land” (no stable states of any phase).

Observe also that Fig.
3
does not include a separate region for
glassy states, consistent with the fact that polyamorphism has not been
observed for argon. For water, however, two glassy states are known to exist,
low- and high-density, thermodynamically distinct from each other and from the
liquid. Capturing such behavior requires an EV model that accounts for
molecular asymmetry and rotation, whereas the present version yields only
qualitative predictions.

V
Comparison with previous work

In this section, the results of the present work are compared with those
obtained experimentally and from molecular-dynamics simulations (MDS). Apart
from these, there exists an extensive literature based on classical nucleation
theory (CNT) and similar approaches, but there seems to be no longer a
consensus regarding their application to supercooled liquids. Ref.

Sosso et al. 2016
, for example, states that
“at strong supercooling […] a number of assumptions on
which CNT relies become, if not erroneous, ill-defined”,
which echoes with Ref.
Ickes et al. 2015
stating that the CNT
framework “consists of kinetic and thermodynamic parameters,
of which three are not well-defined”.

The author of the present paper has not worked with CNT and therefore will not
comment on it, except for the difference between its calibration and that of
the EV model. CNT involves
microscopic
parameters, to be determined by
fitting the theoretical nucleation rate to the experimental one—which,
unfortunately, reduces the model’s predictive capability. The EV equation, by
contrast, relies on
macroscopic
parameters (the critical and
triple-point characteristics) which can either be easily measured or are
already available in the literature. A model calibrated in this way may not be
as accurate as a data fit, but is accurate enough to reveal interesting trends
underlying supercooling.

Argon

In what follows, the results of the relevant studies are first summarized and
then commented on in bullet-point form by the author of the present
paper.

(A) In Ref.
Kouchi and Kuroda 1990
, a thin film of amorphous argon, together
with argon vapor above it, were placed in a vacuum chamber at
10\,\mathrm{K}
, then heated at a rate of
2\,\mathrm{K/min}
until the film crystallized.
The crystallization began at
20\pm 1\,\mathrm{K}
and ended at
24\pm 1\,\mathrm{K}
. Apart from argon, the vapor contained residual gases:

\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}
(partial pressure
2.3\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{Pa}
),

\mathrm{H}_{2}
(
1.2\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{Pa}
), and
\mathrm{N}_{2}

(
0.5\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{Pa}
). The partial pressure of the argon vapor
increased during crystallization from
0.3\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{Pa}
to

1.0\times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{Pa}
(both values extracted from Fig. 2 of Ref.

Kouchi and Kuroda 1990
).

•

The lowest temperature at which amorphous argon was observed in Ref.

Kouchi and Kuroda 1990
is
4\,\mathrm{K}
below the lowest attainable
temperature for liquid argon predicted by the EV model (
14\,\mathrm{K}
).

To explain the discrepancy, note that, in Ref.

Kouchi and Kuroda 1990
, the pressure of the residual gases exceeded that of
argon by a factor between
10
(at the beginning of crystallization) and
4

(at its end). Given the high compressibility of liquid argon—as discussed in
Sect.
IV.3
—the extra pressure must have made the properties of the
mixture different from those of pure argon—which, in turn, must have altered
the spinodal curve. This alteration explains the discrepancy in the
temperature values mentioned above. In any case, an error of
4\,\mathrm{K}

may be regarded as acceptable when working in a temperature range located so
far from argon’s triple and critical points (which were used to calibrate the
EV model).

•

Note that crystallization in Ref.
Kouchi and Kuroda 1990
was induced by

heating
liquid argon, during which its density had to be

decreasing
(it was not actually measured). This process corresponds to
a trajectory beginning just above the lowest point of the spinodal curve and
moving
upward
and
leftward
until it crosses the boundary of the
no-man’s land, triggering crystallization. Note that an isochoric trajectory
originating from the same point would miss the no-man’s land and no
crystallization would occur (as evident from Fig.
3
).

•

It is unclear whether the heating rate and the resulting evaporation
rate in Ref.
Kouchi and Kuroda 1990
were small enough for the vapor–liquid
system to be near equilibrium, so the trajectory observed in the experiment
cannot be classified in terms of the present paper (which deals only with
equilibrium states).

(B) In experiments reported in Ref.
Möller et al. 2024
,
liquid argon was emitted into vacuum through a nozzle of diameter

3.5\pm 0.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}
, at a temperature
93\pm 0.5\,\mathrm{K}
(i.e.,
approximately
10\,\mathrm{K}
above the triple point). Due to evaporation,
the temperature of the jet’s surface was decreasing with the distance from the
nozzle, and beginning of crystallization was observed at approximately

68\,\mathrm{K}
(see Fig. 3 of Ref.
Möller et al. 2024
).

•

Since crystallization in this experiment was attained through cooling,
the corresponding trajectory on the
(n,T)
plane moves downward and
rightward, eventually crossing the spinodal curve corresponding to the
finite-scale instability. In Fig.
3
, this curve is labelled

k=k_{1.5}
, but the theoretical trajectories crossing it in this figure
cannot be compared to the experimental one, because the jet in the experiment
of Ref.
Möller et al. 2024
was far from equilibrium due to
the presence of strong fluxes.

Specifically, there was a
heat flux caused by a significant temperature difference between the jet’s
surface (
68\,\mathrm{K}
) and its core (
92\,\mathrm{K}
). This difference
was caused by evaporation, which also caused a significant mass flux through
the liquid–vapor interface. It is safe to assume that the nonequilibrium
processes triggered instability at a temperature well above the theoretical
(interfacial) value of
T_{s,f}=40.5\,\mathrm{K}
(see Table
2
).

•

In the present paper,
T_{s,f}
was computed for a
flat

interface, whereas the jet’s surface in Ref.

Möller et al. 2024
was strongly
curved
, which
further impedes quantitative comparison.

(C) Unfortunately, no measurements or computations seem to have been reported
for the surface tension of supercooled argon. Furthermore, the surface tension
singularity would generally be difficult to observe in an MDS, as it would
need to simultaneously resolve short scales (the interface and oscillations
near it) and long scales (the region occupied by the oscillations).

V.2
Water

(A) As reported in Ref.
Johari et al. 1987
and numerous other
studies, glassy water can be observed at temperatures as low as

136\,\mathrm{K}
. A related issue—whether
all
glassy states are
interlinked with a liquid state by a continuous trajectory without
crystallization—has been debated in Refs.

Yue and Angell 2004
;
Kohl et al. 2005
;
Yue and Angell 2005
;
Bachler et al. 2022
, with the latest of these papers concluding that this is indeed possible.

•

The lowest temperature of amorphous water computed in the present paper
is approximately
100\,\mathrm{K}
, which is noticeably lower than the

136\,\mathrm{K}
reported in Ref.
Johari et al. 1987
. The
primary source of this discrepancy is likely the unsuitability of the
nonrotational EV model for water, but one should also note that Ref.

Johari et al. 1987
did not specifically search for the glassy
state with the
lowest
temperature.

More
generally, this parameter is difficult to find experimentally, as the pressure
at which this state occurs is not known a priori.

•

From the viewpoint of the nonrotational EV model, the fact that any two
amorphous states can be interlinked is evident from Figs.
3
–
4
. It is unclear whether this would change if a model including
molecular rotation were used.

(B) Numerous experiments and MDS studied ice nucleation in supercooled water,
and the highest temperature at which non-zero nucleation rate is observed
corresponds to the spinodal temperature computed in the present work.
Examples, chosen more or less at random, include:

Refs.
Rasmussen et al. 1973
;
Atkinson et al. 2016

experimented with
drops
and placed the cutoff of nucleation at

235\,\mathrm{K}
and
237\,\mathrm{K}
, respectively;

Refs.
Li et al. 2011
;
Xu et al. 2017
reported
simulations and experiments with liquid
films
, obtaining

243\,\mathrm{K}
and
238\,\mathrm{K}
, respectively.

•

All of the above experimental and computational temperatures are
slightly lower than the
250\,\mathrm{K}
calculated in the present paper (see

T_{s,b}
and
T_{s.f}
listed for water in Table
2
). The discrepancy
is unlikely to have any cause other than the inaccuracy of the nonrotational
EV model when applied to water.

(C) Refs.

Lü and Wei 2006
;
Rogers et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Malek et al. 2019

and
Vinš et al. 2020
reported computations and
measurements of the surface tension
\gamma
of the liquid–vapor interface in
supercooled water. In all cases, a considerable increase of
\gamma
was
reported at low temperatures, well above the predictions of the standard IAPWS
formulation
International Association for the Properties of
Water and Steam 2014
extrapolated into the supercooling range. Some of
these papers also report evidence of the so-called second inflection point in
the dependence
\gamma(T)
located near
T=245\,\mathrm{K}
(in addition to
the first inflection point at
T=529\,\mathrm{K}
).

•

The above results are consistent with a singularity located below the
second inflection point, but do not prove the singularity.

VI
Concluding remarks

The EV model used in this paper can be made much more accurate if it is
generalized for
spherically asymmetric, rotating
molecules. There seems
to be no fundamental reason why this cannot be done, and the availability of
this tool is only a matter of time. Such a model should be capable of
quantitatively describing water and other polyamorphic fluids.

An EV model for
multicomponent fluids
, in turn, has already been
developed in Ref.
Grmela and Garcia-Colin 1980a
;
Grmela and Garcia-Colin 1980b
. It can be
used for the argon-plus-residudals mixture arising in Ref.

Kouchi and Kuroda 1990
).

Note, however, that all EV models involve high-dimension multiple integrals
impeding computation. This may be viewed as an argument in favor of more
phenomenological, but simpler, kinetic equations proposed in Refs.

Takata et al. 2018
;
Takata et al. 2021
;
Bhattacharjee et al. 2024
;
Busuioc and Sofonea 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Takata and Takahashi 2025
. The last of these even describes crystallization and produces ‘stability
portraits’ similar to Figs.
3
–
4
of the present paper (as
verified by the present author). It would also be interesting to see if
crystallization is described by the moment equations derived in Ref.

Struchtrup and Frezzotti 2019
;
Struchtrup and Frezzotti 2022
.

Caution is required, however, when using the diffuse-interface model and
similar hydrodynamic tools: even though they can be rigorously derived from
the EV kinetic model
Giovangigli 2021
, they perform poorly at and below
near-triple-point temperatures

Barbante et al. 2014
;
Barbante and Frezzotti 2017
;
Benilov 2024
.

Finally, note that liquid–vapor interfaces may be unstable with respect to
disturbances associated with the density gradient and localized in the
interfacial region. In principle, this instability may occur at a temperature
above the spinodal point of the homogeneous liquid and, thus, requires further investigation.

A.3
Calculating
K

In the simplified versions of the Enskog–Vlasov equation, the
diffuse-interface model
Benilov 2023
and Stokes–Vlasov model

Benilov 2024
, the Korteweg constant
K
was fixed by enforcing the
equality of the theoretical surface tension at the triple point,

\gamma_{theor}(T_{tp},K)
, to its empirical counterpart,
\gamma_{empir}(T_{tp})
.

A similar approach is employed in the present paper, but with a modification.
It has turned out that the
exact
equality of
\gamma_{theor}(T_{tp},K)

and
\gamma_{empir}(T_{tp})
cannot be enforced, because the former has an
absolute minimum at a certain
K=K_{min}
, and
\gamma(T_{tp},K_{min})

exceeds
\gamma_{empir}(T_{tp})
. For the fluids examined in this paper except
water, the relative excess is small:
3.1\%
for
\mathrm{Ar}
,
1.7\%
for

\mathrm{N}_{2}
,
3.7\%
for
\mathrm{O}_{2}
,
4.3\%
for
\mathrm{F}_{2}
,
and
7.7\%
for
\mathrm{CO}
—but for
\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}
, the
minimum value of
\gamma(T_{tp},K)
misses its empirical counterpart by a
factor of
2
.

The error of the surface tension of water can only be corrected if the
rotational version of the EV model is used.

Appendix B
Numerical method for boundary-value problem (
36
)–(
41
)

The integrals in expression (
37
) for the kernels
F_{l}
were computed
on a uniform grid inside the cube

\left(-1<\frac{z_{1}}{D}<1\right)\times\cdots\times\left(-1<\frac{z_{l}}{D}<1\right),

(47)

using the Monte Carlo method with the Halton sequence, generated by MATLAB’s
haltonset function. The evaluation of
F_{l}
is the most computationally
expensive part of the calculation.

The solution
n(z)
of Eq. (
36
) was discretized with the same step as

F_{l}
, within a sufficiently large interval

-Z^{(l)}\leq z\leq Z^{(v)}.

(48)

Outside this interval, the values of
n
were prescribed according to boundary
conditions (
39
)–(
40
),

\displaystyle n(z)

\displaystyle=n^{(l)}\qquad\text{if}\qquad z<-Z^{(l)},

\displaystyle n(z)

\displaystyle=n^{(v)}\qquad\text{if}\qquad z>Z^{(v)}.

The integrals on the left-hand side of Eq. (
36
) were evaluated using
the rectangle rule, which is
second
-order accurate because the kernels

F_{l}
vanish at the boundaries of cube (
47
). Enforcing equality
between the left- and right-hand sides of Eq. (
36
) at each grid point
within interval (
48
) yielded a set of algebraic equations for the grid
values of
n(z)
. The constant
\operatorname{const}
on the right-hand side
of (
36
) was treated as an extra unknown, while condition (
41
)
was treated as an extra equation.

The resulting system of equations was solved using MATLAB’s fsolve function.
