ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe overall survival of children with newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in high-income countries has increased to 80% over the past decades. Nevertheless, a significant subset of patients experiences relapse and treatment is associated with both short- and long-term toxicities. The CHIP-AML22 protocol includes an updated standard-of-care treatment for children with AML within the NOPHO-DB-SHIP consortium. Targeted therapies are offered to specific subsets of patients and measures to reduce toxicity are being investigated.MethodsCHIP-AML22 is a multinational complex clinical trial in newly diagnosed de novo AML patients up to and including 18 years of age, sponsored by the Princess Máxima Center. The primary aim is to improve event-free survival. To achieve this, (1) FLT3-ITD+/NPM1wt patients can participate in a linked-trial assessing safety and efficacy of quizartinib, in addition to conventional chemotherapy during induction and consolidation therapy and as continuation monotherapy after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation; (2) a randomization study is incorporated on the use of two doses of 3 mg/m2 gemtuzumab ozogamicin during induction therapy in CD33-positive patients; and (3) updated criteria are used for the identification of high-risk patients, based both on flow measurable residual disease (MRD) and (cyto)genetic profiling. Background

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) accounts for up to 20% of all pediatric leukemias [1]. In high-income countries, overall survival after intensive treatment has increased to 80% in newly diagnosed patients [2]. Unfortunately, relapse occurs in 25–35% of the patients and 5–10% suffer from refractory disease [2, 3], both resulting in significantly worse prognosis [4]. Changes in treatment strategies aim to improve event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) by reducing the occurrence of refractory disease and relapse and by developing more personalized treatment. As AML in children is relatively rare, international consortia are collaborating and have developed joint treatment protocols. NOPHO-DB-SHIP is one of these consortia, consisting of the Nordic and Baltic countries, The Netherlands, Belgium, Spain, Switzerland, Hong Kong, Israel, Portugal and Uruguay.

The CHIP-AML22 study protocol is largely based on the results of the NOPHO-DBH AML 2012 protocol (hereafter referred to as AML 2012), according to which children have been treated within the NOPHO-DBH consortium since 2013 [5]. The overall treatment response in AML 2012 was high, with a complete remission (CR) rate of 94%, a 5-year probability of EFS of 67%, an OS rate of 80% and cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) of 25% [5]. The toxicity of treatment was comparable to that of other collaborative groups. CHIP-AML22 aims to further increase the EFS for children and adolescents with newly diagnosed de novo AML, using a risk-based treatment approach. Currently CHIP-AML22 includes a standard-of-care chemotherapy backbone of 2 induction courses, followed by 3 consolidation courses for standard-risk patients or allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) after 1 consolidation course for high-risk patients. Additionally, the protocol includes: (1) the introduction of quizartinib for patients that are FLT3-ITD positive and NPM1 wild-type (FLT3-ITD+ /NPM1wt), in addition to conventional chemotherapy during induction and consolidation therapy and as continuation monotherapy after allo-HSCT; (2) the randomized addition of two doses of 3 mg/m2 gemtuzumab ozogamicin during induction course 1 for patients with CD33-positive AML; (3) updated risk-group stratification with an emphasis on early treatment response, in combination with (cyto)genetic profiling; and (4) a randomization aiming to demonstrate non-inferiority of shortened consolidation therapy (2 instead of 3 courses) in standard-risk patients. Additionally, the use of the cardioprotective drug dexrazoxane is recommended. Quality of life during and after treatment will be assessed. These study elements are further explained in the following sections.

Quizartinib

Quizartinib is a second-generation type 2 FLT3 inhibitor that will be offered to FLT3-ITD+/NPM1wt patients in combination with chemotherapy during induction and (in a proportion of children) as a single-agent continuation therapy after allo-HSCT. FLT3 is a class III receptor tyrosine kinase which, when constitutively activated, results in proliferation and survival of leukemic cells [6]. FLT3-ITD mutations occur in approximately 10–15% of the pediatric AML patients [7, 8] and are associated with inferior treatment outcomes [7–9], especially when combined with the NPM1 wild-type [10, 11]. Over the past two decades, several FLT3 inhibitors have been developed [12]. Classification of these agents is based on specificity (second-generation being more specific) and the mechanism of interaction (type 1 binding FLT3 in active conformation, type 2 in inactive conformation) [12]. For quizartinib, two phase 3 studies in adults have been performed, both in relapsed/refractory (R/R) and newly diagnosed FLT3-ITD+AML patients and both resulted in a significant improvement in overall survival [13, 14]. In 2023, based on these results, the FDA and EMA approved quizartinib in combination with chemotherapy during induction and consolidation therapy and as maintenance monotherapy in newly diagnosed FLT3-ITD+ adult patients [15, 16].

In children, the Therapeutic Advances in Childhood Leukemia and Lymphoma (TACL) study group conducted a phase 1 dose escalation study in subjects >1 month and ≤21 years old with either relapsed AML (n = 18, of whom 9 were FLT3-ITD+) or mixed lineage leukemia ALL (n = 4) [17]. Quizartinib was combined with intensive chemotherapy to a maximum daily dose of 60 mg/m2 and bone marrow (BM) response was clearly superior in FLT3-ITD+ AML patients compared to the other patients [17]. In this study, a dose-dependent prolongation of corrected QT interval (QTcF) was observed, however without requiring a dose reduction or interruption. A phase 1/2 study on quizartinib in pediatric R/R AML patients with FLT3-ITD mutation is ongoing (NCT03793478), but studies in newly diagnosed pediatric patients are lacking.

Based on completed and ongoing studies, the following adverse drug reactions associated with quizartinib have been observed: myelosuppression, gastrointestinal disorders, general disorders including fatigue and edema, dose-dependent QTcF prolongation, laboratory abnormalities (e.g., ALT and AST increase or potassium and magnesium decrease) and drug interactions with strong CYP3A inhibitors and strong/moderate CYP3A inducers. Another point of attention described in earlier studies is the occurrence of secondary resistance to FLT3 inhibitors [18, 19]. With the addition of quizartinib for newly diagnosed FLT3-ITD+/NPM1wt pediatric AML patients, our objective is to assess its clinical benefit in this high-risk population. Actions to monitor and mitigate the potential risks are described under ‘methods’.

Gemtuzumab ozogamicin

In the CHIP-AML22 protocol, a randomized addition of gemtuzumab ozogamicin (GO) to the first induction course is included for all children with CD33-positive AML. CD33 is a transmembrane cell surface receptor that is expressed in up to 90% of AML patients [20, 21] and has therefore become a popular target for the development of new antibody drug conjugates [22]. In GO, a CD33-directed antibody is linked to the cytotoxic agent calicheamicin [23, 24]. It was first approved by the FDA in 2000, for older adults with R/R CD33-positive AML, but later voluntarily withdrawn due to preliminary results of a phase III study in adult patients by the Southwest Oncology Group (SWOG) that reported an increase in induction mortality without an improvement in relapse-free or overall survival [25]. However, later studies using a lower dose and improved risk stratification did show a beneficial effect of the addition of GO, mainly in low- and intermediate risk groups, leading to the re-approval of GO by both the FDA and EMA in 2017 and 2018, respectively [26–28]. In the pediatric population, several studies have shown that the addition of GO to induction and consolidation chemotherapy can lead to improved EFS and a reduced relapse rate (RR), without a significant increase in toxicity. However, no significant improvement in OS was observed [29–33]. These results are similar to those reported for the adult population [28, 34–36]. Furthermore, the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) reported on the safety and efficacy of the addition of GO in specific subsets of pediatric AML patients, including KMT2A-rearranged (KMT2Ar) patients, infants and FLT3-ITD+ patients [33, 37, 38]. Based on observed clinical benefits across these subgroups, COG has now included GO in the chemotherapy backbone for all patients in their current protocol, AAML1831. Of note, the FDA approves the use of GO in all newly diagnosed CD33-positive AML patients above 1 month of age, whereas the approval by the EMA excludes patients aged less than 15 years.

The effect of GO is possibly influenced by selecting patients who are most likely to benefit, by choosing the right dose and using the most optimal timing. With respect to selecting the right patients, several potential response predictors have been identified, including the level of CD33 expression [39–42] and the presence of specific CD33 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that are associated with CD33 levels or the binding capacity of GO [43–46]. The optimal dose and schedule for GO are unclear. The COG applies doses of GO at 3 mg/m2 during induction and consolidation. MyeChild01 recently investigated 1 versus 3 doses at 3 mg/m2 during induction only. No differences in safety were observed, although follow-up is not long enough to draw conclusions on differences in treatment efficacy (personal communication, Prof. Dr. Brenda Gibson). Considering the timing of GO, the NOPHO consortium previously investigated the effect of GO after consolidation therapy and could not show any benefit [47].

Regarding toxicity, an association between GO and veno-occlusive disease (VOD), also known as sinusoidal obstructive disease (SOS), has been described [48]. In more recent studies, the reported incidence of GO-associated VOD/SOS is much lower, which may be explained by the use of lower or fractionated doses [49]. Importantly, for CHIP-AML22, a study by the COG with a similar patient population and dosing of GO compared to CHIP-AML22 found no association between GO and the incidence of VOD/SOS [30]. By applying two doses of GO during induction therapy for children with CD33-positive AML, the CHIP-AML22 study aims to improve the efficacy of induction therapy and contribute to international consensus on the optimal use of GO.

Risk stratification

Risk stratification in the CHIP-AML22 protocol is based on a combination of genetic aberrations and early treatment response, with an emphasis on the latter (Table 1). Early treatment response, assessed through the determination of measurable residual disease (MRD) by flow cytometry, has repeatedly been shown to associate with survival outcomes in AML [50]. Over the past years, cooperative groups such as NOPHO-DB-SHIP have made joint efforts in the identification and intergroup validation of genetic and cytogenetic aberrations with clinical relevance, both as prognostic factors and possible therapeutic targets [51].

As mentioned previously, one of these factors is the presence of a FLT3-ITD mutation, especially in combination with NPM1 wild type. In the AML 2012 protocol, FLT3-ITD+ /NPM1wt was the only genetic marker used to identify high-risk patients. CHIP-AML22 extends this by including additional genetic aberrations for risk stratification. KMT2Ar patients form a genetic subgroup associated with overall poor outcome [52]. A considerable number of translocation partners have been identified over the past years, making this group remarkably heterogeneous [53]. KMT2Ar patients will be stratified as high-risk, but only in case of bone marrow MRD ≥ 0.1% after induction course 1 or any of the subsequent bone marrow evaluations, as this combination is associated with inferior EFS [54] (NOPHO-DBH AML 2012, unpublished results). Patients with the t(9;11) translocation (KMT2A::MLLT3) form an exception, because this subgroup concerns standard- or intermediate-risk patients [55, 56], and because in our experience flow MRD does not predict high-risk disease in these children (NOPHO-DBH AML 2012, unpublished results). Patients with the CBFA2T3::GLIS2 fusion transcript and/or having a so-called RAM immunophenotype are also considered high-risk, as they have repeatedly demonstrated poor treatment outcomes [57–60]. The RAM phenotype is defined by the presence of four features: bright CD56 expression, dim-to-negative expression of CD45 and CD38 and a lack of HLA-DR [60]. Subsequent research has revealed a relatively high frequency of the CBFA2T3::GLIS2 fusion gene within patients with the RAM phenotype and has so far confirmed poor outcomes within this group [61, 62].

The risk stratification approach in the previous AML 2012 protocol relied primarily on treatment response, plus the identification of FLT3-ITD+/NPM1wt patients. This resulted in 19% of newly diagnosed patients being classified as high-risk and assigned to consolidation therapy including allo-HSCT [5]. Other pediatric AML studies have classified up to 30% of patients as high-risk, mainly by allocation of additional cytogenetic subgroups to high-risk treatment [63, 64]. By integrating (cyto)genetic profiling with MRD outcomes, CHIP-AML22 aims to identify patients with favorable MRD responses who can safely avoid allo-HSCT and its associated toxicities on the short- and long-term.

Shortened consolidation

While the introduction of new therapies has contributed to improved survival rates, treatment-related toxicities remain a significant problem for childhood cancer survivors and can even result in treatment-related mortality. Short-term toxicities in the treatment of AML include myelosuppression, infections, gastrointestinal problems, neurological complications and early cardiotoxicity [65]. Late effects include cardiotoxicity, secondary malignancies, neurocognitive impairments, psychosocial effects and fertility problems [66–70]. Treatment-related morbidity has an impact on patients and survivors, causing diminished quality of life in at least a subset of patients [71, 72].

A challenge lies in improving outcomes, while at the same time avoiding unnecessary toxicity by leaving out treatment elements that are of unproven benefit. In contrast to the consensus on using 2 induction courses, the number of consolidation courses in childhood AML has ranged from two to eight over the past decades [73–75]. Although most study groups currently use a regimen of two or three consolidation courses [65], the optimal number is still a matter of debate. The Medical Research Council (MRC) AML12 protocol (including both children and adults, [76]) compared the outcomes of 270 children who were randomized to receive either 4 or 5 chemotherapy courses in total. This study included both standard and high-risk patients and did not show a survival benefit of the 5th course, although the study was not adequately powered to answer this question for the pediatric population alone [77]. MRC AML15, in patients younger than 45 years, showed similar results [78]. Although no difference in treatment-related mortality was reported, MRC AML15 also analyzed resource usage (e.g., blood units, hospitalization days) per consolidation course, illustrating potential benefit of omitting one course. Based on the MRC results, the COG modified their protocol by eliminating the third consolidation course. In a historical comparison between the AAML1031 protocol (2 consolidation courses) and the preceding AAML0531 protocol (3 consolidation courses), a third consolidation course had no proven benefit in low-risk patients with favorable cytogenetic and molecular features and negative MRD at the end of induction course one. In other subpopulations, COG concluded that a third consolidation course was beneficial [79].

CHIP-AML22 aims to contribute to the consensus on the optimal number of chemotherapy courses with respect to survival outcomes and toxicity by offering a consolidation randomization in standard-risk patients, investigating three (standard) versus two (investigational) courses in a non-inferiority study.

Dexrazoxane

Both short- and long-term cardiotoxicity is a well-known side effect of anti-leukemic therapy [66, 69, 80, 81]. This toxicity is mainly attributed to anthracyclines, which can cause injury to cardiomyocytes by generating iron-mediated free radicals [82, 83]. Moreover, they may impair mitochondrial functioning and can induce apoptosis in cardiac cells [82]; additional mechanisms of damage are still being investigated [84]. The damage to and loss of cardiomyocytes can eventually lead to changes in heart function that can be either clinical or subclinical [85] and can affect short- and long-term outcomes [69, 86, 87].

Potential preventive strategies for anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity have focused on optimizing dosing and duration of infusion, identifying risk factors, using liposomal anthracyclines and cardioprotective drugs [81, 85, 88]. Dexrazoxane is a cardioprotective drug whose main effect is proposed to be the chelation of intracellular and anthracycline-bound iron, thereby reducing the production of free radicals. More recently, an additional mechanism of action has been identified, namely the ability of dexrazoxane to reduce anthracycline-induced DNA damage by binding to DNA topoisomerase II [89, 90].

There have been concerns about a potential increase in secondary malignancies in pediatric patients treated with dexrazoxane [90]. However, newer, larger studies in the pediatric population did not report an increase in secondary malignancies [91–94]. In terms of cardiac outcomes, these studies (both randomized and non-randomized) showed a beneficial effect on left ventricular ejection fraction, fractional shortening and/or ventricular wall thickness in patient groups treated with dexrazoxane compared to those who were not [91–94]. The cardioprotective effect was primarily observed in patients treated with a cumulative doxorubicin equivalent dose of ≥250 mg/m2 [95]. Based on these results, the International Late Effects of Childhood Cancer Guideline Harmonization Group now recommends the use of dexrazoxane in children who will receive a cumulative doxorubicin equivalent dose of at least 250 mg/m2 [88]. CHIP-AML22 likewise recommends the use of dexrazoxane in all patients, given the use of both daunorubicin and mitoxantrone. While the cardioprotective effect of dexrazoxane in the context of mitoxantrone specifically has not been well studied yet, its known association with increased cardiotoxicity supports this recommendation [96]. Dexrazoxane will be administered at the investigator’s discretion and per institutional guidelines and availability. Given that a subset of patients will receive dexrazoxane, CHIP-AML22 may contribute evidence regarding its clinical benefit in the context of mitoxantrone-based treatment.

Quality of life

Alongside classical outcomes such as EFS, patient-reported outcomes can also guide clinical decision-making. Literature on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in the pediatric oncology population is largely based on cross-sectional studies that primarily examine the long-term consequences of the disease. Reported results vary, and clear evidence on risk factors for impaired HRQoL is yet to be established. The wide variety of available patient-reported outcome measures further complicates uniform assessment [97]. CHIP-AML22 aims to contribute to more knowledge on the effects of AML treatment on HRQoL by including assessments by validated questionnaires both during and after treatment.

Methods

Study design

The CHIP-AML22 study is a multinational complex clinical trial (EU CT 2023-504999-25-00) [98]. Currently, in the Master protocol, two randomizations are included, namely a 1:1 randomization for the addition of GO to induction chemotherapy in CD33-positive patients (Ri) and a 1:1 randomization for two versus three consolidation courses in standard-risk patients (Rc). Quizartinib will be offered to all FLT3-ITD+/NPM1wt patients in a linked single-arm trial, which is registered and approved separately (EU CT 2023-505000-27-01). Other linked trials may be added to the main study in the future. A total of 16 countries with more than 60 sites will participate in the Master protocol: Belgium, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Hong Kong, Iceland, Israel, Latvia, Lithuania, Norway, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, The Netherlands and Uruguay. Participation in add-on studies or additional linked trials will be optional and can be determined on a per-country basis. The study protocol adheres to the guidelines outlined in the SPIRIT checklist [99].

Study population

The study is designed for children and adolescents with newly diagnosed de novo AML, aged ≤18 years at diagnosis. A complete overview of the inclusion and exclusion criteria is given in Table 2. The allocation of subjects to one of the randomizations or linked trials is based on disease-specific information and early treatment response, which also determines their risk stratification. Endpoints and sample size considerations

The primary overarching endpoint of this study is EFS, defined as the time from diagnosis to the first occurrence of an event. Events include refractory disease, relapse, death of any reason or a secondary malignancy. Patients alive at the time of analysis will be censored at their last known alive date. Overall secondary endpoints include OS, disease-free survival (DFS), CIR, non-relapse mortality (NRM) and cumulative toxicity. In addition, BM blast counts by morphology and flow cytometry, MRD and overall response rates (ORR) will be reported, both following induction courses 1 and 2 and before allo-HSCT.

For the Ri study specifically, the primary endpoint is being alive in remission with MRD < 0.1% measured by flow cytometry in the last BM evaluation before start of the second induction course. We hypothesize superiority of this outcome in the group receiving GO. Assuming a 1:1 randomization, no drop-outs and a probability of being alive in CR with MRD < 0.1% prior to the second induction course of 0.62 in the control arm (based on historical data of the AML 2012 protocol), the required sample size is 150 patients per arm. One interim analysis is planned when about 75% of the patients have outcome data available.

The primary endpoint of the Rc study is DFS, for which we hypothesize non-inferiority in the investigational arm. To achieve an 80% probability of showing non-inferiority, a total sample size of 610 subjects (305 per arm) is adequate. This applies under the assumption of no drop-outs, a 1:1 randomization, a follow-up period of 3 years, an EFS in the standard arm that is similar to that of the AML 2012 study and a non-inferiority margin defined as a hazard ratio of 1.45. Based on the expected proportion of patients that is classified as standard risk and assuming 90% of these patients to participate in the randomization, a total inclusion of 905 patients in the Master protocol would be required. One interim analysis is planned when 50% of the required number of events have been observed.

For patients included in the Quizartinib trial, the primary endpoint is being alive in remission with MRD <0.1% measured by flow cytometry in the last BM evaluation before the start of consolidation therapy. With a target sample size of 60 patients, the study will have 85% power to detect an absolute improvement of 20% in the primary efficacy outcome, corresponding to an MRD negativity rate of 59% compared to 39% in the historical cohort.

To evaluate the efficacy of dexrazoxane, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is the main measure of cardiac function, which will be assessed prior to each course, at the end of treatment and 2 and 5 years after diagnosis. A comparison with the subset of patients that did not receive dexrazoxane will be of interest and a historical comparison to outcomes from the AML 2012 protocol may contribute to further evaluate its potential benefit.

Study duration

The largest number of patients to be recruited is 905. The expectation is that, once all treatment sites have been activated, 130 patients will be included in the CHIP-AML22 study annually. This means that an inclusion period of at least 7 years would be needed. Allowing for 5 years of follow-up from diagnosis, the total study duration is expected to be approximately 12–13 years. For the linked Quizartinib trial, inclusion of 15 patients per year is expected. The assessment of secondary endpoints requires 3 years of follow-up after end of treatment, adding up to an expected study duration of 8–9 years.

Treatment protocol

Diagnostic guidelines

In this study, the diagnosis of AML is based on the 5th edition of the World Health Organization Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumours and Peadiatric Tumours as published in 2022 [101, 102]. A bone marrow aspirate (BMA) will be examined for blast counts, including analysis of morphology, immunophenotyping and (cyto)genetics. Immunophenotype will be assessed through flow cytometry with recommendations for obligatory antigens. Cytogenetic alterations as listed in Table 3, should be analyzed by either G-banding in combination with fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), or using next-generation sequencing (NGS). Testing for molecular genetic aberrations (Table 3) should be performed using either PCR or NGS-based techniques. Samples will be sent to one of the selected reference laboratories in case of limited availability of assays at a specific site. Not all listed aberrations will influence treatment allocation. For assessment of eligibility for Ri or the linked Quizartinib trial, results on CD33 positivity, FLT3-ITD and NPM1 status should be provided within 6 days from the start of course 1.

Central nervous system (CNS) disease is evaluated in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) at diagnosis in all patients and repeated on day 22 of induction course 1 in those with CNS2 and CNS3. In line with other cooperative groups, CNS1 is defined by the absence of detectable blasts in the CSF and CNS2 refers to patients with blasts in the CSF but a count of <5/µL by morphology. CNS3 is applicable when the blast count is 5/µL or more, in case of clinical signs of CNS involvement or radiographic evidence of an intracranial or intradural mass suggestive of a chloroma [103]. In case of suspected CNS disease and in CNS3, an MRI scan of the CNS is required. The assessment of extramedullary disease is based on clinical examinations and subsequent imaging and/or biopsy findings.

Treatment

Treatment starts as soon as possible, provided that subjects are properly hydrated and other measures against tumor lysis syndrome have been initiated. Treatment options, depending on risk stratification and participation in the substudies, are schematically depicted in Fig. 1. Standard treatment consists of two induction courses (MEC and ADE, Supplementary file 1), of which the second course normally starts after blood count recovery and in a stable clinical condition. The start of this course can be delayed to a maximum of 56 days from the start of course 1. However, if ≥5% leukemic cells are present in the BM evaluation at day 22 or in any consecutive BMA, course 2 should be given immediately if the clinical condition of the child allows this, independent from count recovery. Consolidation courses include HAM, HA3E and FLA (Supplementary file 1). Dexrazoxane is recommended to be added to all courses except HA3E and FLA, at the investigator’s discretion and in accordance with institutional guidelines and availability. Methotrexate is administered intrathecally at day 6 of induction course 1 and at the start of each subsequent course, in the absence of CNS disease. In case of CNS3 status, intrathecal triple therapy is administered twice weekly until blasts are no longer detectable in the CSF, followed by two additional doses. For CNS2 patients, CSF evaluation on day 22 of the first course will determine whether they will be treated as CNS3 (if blasts persist) or as CNS1 (in case of no detectable blasts). Intrathecal dosages are age-adjusted. For all other drugs mentioned, the systemic dose will be adjusted for body weight (if <1 year of age or <10 kg) or body surface area (BSA; for all other children) calculated using Mosteller’s formula [104] and dose reductions should be considered in case of excess toxicity. Patients with isolated extramedullary disease and AML-specific genetic abnormalities can be enrolled in the Master protocol and assigned to substudies, provided the primary endpoint or one or more secondary endpoints can be evaluated.

High-risk group allocation for FLT3-ITD+/NPM1wt, CBFA2T3::GLIS2 and/or RAM-phenotype patients is made upon their identification. Patients with ≥15% leukemic cells at BM evaluation on day 22 or at any subsequent evaluation prior to course 2 or KMT2Ar patients (excluding KMT2A::MLLT3) with MRD ≥0.1% before the start of course 2 are considered high-risk. For all other patients, final conclusions on risk group allocation will be made after the flow MRD response evaluation at the end of induction course 2. Subjects that are considered high-risk will be treated with induction courses MEC and ADE, followed by one consolidation course HAM and allo-HSCT. If needed, a fourth course (HA3E) can be given prior to allo-HSCT; a fifth course should be discussed with the national coordinator and chief investigator. For patients enrolled in the CHIP-AML22 study, it is possible to participate in the SCRIPT-AML study (NCT05477589), which compares an investigational allo-HSCT conditioning regimen consisting of fludarabine-clofarabine-busulfan to the standard busulfan-cyclophosphamide-melphalan conditioning.

If participating in Ri, patients can be assigned to receive induction treatment that includes GO. GO is then added to the MEC course in two doses of 3 mg/m2 (capped dose 5 mg), on day 8 and 11. Patients who have been randomized in Rc to receive two consolidation courses will receive HAM and FLA, omitting HA3E. In the linked Quizartinib trial, patients will receive quizartinib in addition to induction courses 1 and 2 and the first consolidation course. Quizartinib will be administered orally once daily, starting on the day after completion of chemotherapy, for a total of 14 days in each cycle. Consolidation will be followed by allo-HSCT, after which 6 more courses of 28 days of quizartinib will be administered, except to the FLT3/ITD+/NPM1wt patients that are flow MRD negative (<0.1%) after induction course 1 and remain MRD negative. A safety run-in is included to determine the Recommended Phase 2 Dose (RP2D) of quizartinib. The dosing of quizartinib will be body-weight dependent.

Moment of randomization

Inclusion in the linked Quizartinib trial should be completed before the end of the first induction course (MEC), provided patients and/or legal representatives consent to participate. These patients will not be eligible for Ri; therefore, the FLT3-ITD and NPM1 status must be known within 6 days from start of treatment. Randomization for Ri should take place before day 8 of treatment, as the first dose of GO will be administered on day 8. For Rc, randomization will be performed at a later time point as informed consent can be obtained until the start of the second consolidation course.

Randomization is performed using minimization as implemented in the ALEA software. The allocation sequence is based on the date of inclusion, with the country of inclusion serving as a stratification factor. A maximum tolerated imbalance procedure is used with 80% certainty to ensure balance across treatment arms. In the currently included treatment arms, no blinding is applied, and the treating physician is informed of the patient’s assigned treatment.

Enrollment in the randomizations stops after the target number of evaluable subjects has been randomized. When final analysis and evaluation by the Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) has been completed, patients that are subsequently included in the Master protocol will all receive the superior treatment, if a significant difference has been demonstrated.

Response evaluation

Response evaluations take place prior to each course and prior to conditioning therapy in case of allo-HSCT. BM evaluation will also take place on day 22 (or day 27 in the Quizartinib trial) from the start of induction course 1 (BM d22 and BM d27), performed with flow cytometry MRD. The analysis uses an approach based primarily on leukemia-associated phenotypes (LAIPs) in combination with a different from normal approach, largely in line with recommendations by the European LeukemiaNet MRD Working Party [105]. Patients with ≥5% AML cells in the BM will start course 2 immediately, independent from count recovery, if the clinical condition of the child allows this (referred to as intensified induction). For patients with MRD 0.1–4.9%, weekly BMAs are recommended until regeneration of blood counts occurs, with a final evaluation within 5 days prior to the start of the second course (BM1), or until the percentage of AML cells becomes 5% or more, leading to intensified induction. Patients with MRD ≤0.1% do not require weekly BMAs but will undergo BM1 upon recovery of blood counts (rising counts with absolute neutrophil count (ANC) ≥0.5 × 109/L and platelets ≥50 × 109/L). In patients with MRD ≥5% at any time-point after the first course, MRD will again be assessed on day 22 from the start of course 2. For all patients, a BM evaluation takes place preceding course 3 (BM2). BM2 is required for the final risk stratification, except for those patients that were already identified as high-risk at an earlier time point. If flow MRD reveals 0.1–4.9% residual leukemic blasts at this time point, the patient is stratified as a high-risk patient. Patients with ≥5% leukemic blasts in the BM, or unequivocal AML elsewhere, have refractory disease and go off-treatment. They will receive salvage therapy per investigator discretion outside of the context of this study, but will be followed for survival data. Importantly, if the BM is very aplastic, withholding salvage therapy and repeating bone marrow evaluation within a week should be considered in consultation with the national study coordinator and chief investigator. During consolidation therapy, bone marrow evaluations will be performed shortly before each consolidation course and, in case of high-risk allocation, within 2 weeks prior to allo-HSCT conditioning. A schematic representation is shown in Fig. 2. Analysis of molecular MRD by qPCR is recommended in case of a quantifiable genetic aberration, although results should not impact treatment allocation.

As mentioned before, a lumbar puncture will be performed on day 22 after the start of induction course 1 in CNS2 patients, to evaluate whether CNS involvement persists. CSF analysis will be repeated alongside each intrathecal treatment. For other types of extramedullary disease, response evaluations are per investigator’s discretion.

Post-treatment follow-up

After completion of treatment, if a quantifiable genetic aberration is available, this marker should be measured in peripheral blood (PB) monthly until 18 months from end-of-treatment. Bone marrow analysis is only performed in cases of suspected relapse. After 18 months, follow-up is according to local guidelines. For post-allogeneic HSCT patients, monthly PB analyses are supplemented with bone marrow evaluations at day 30, 60 and 100 post-transplant. If the patient is participating in the SCRIPT-AML study, BMAs are aligned with recommendations in that study. Further follow-up is according to center-specific guidelines. For all patients, echocardiograms should be performed at least 1 and 5 years after the initial diagnosis.

Quality of life assessment takes place at four time points: start of consolidation course 1 (T0), 3 (T1) and 6 (T2) months after the start of the last course and 3 years after diagnosis (T3). The questionnaires used will be the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory generic core scales 4.0 (PedsQL gen), EQ-5D-Y tool and Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory Multidimensional Fatigue Scale (PedsQL MFS). PedsQL MFS will only be completed at T1, T2 and T3, whereas the other questionnaires will be presented at T0 as well. Definition of events

Relapse refers to patients in whom disease reoccurs after having achieved CR after induction course 2. This can be either hematological, flow cytometric, molecular, extramedullary, CNS relapse or a combination, as further defined in Table 5. Refractory disease is defined as ≥5% AML blasts in BM2, confirmed by flow cytometry in patients with an informative LAIP. In patients with extramedullary disease, we define refractory disease as clear evidence of remaining leukemia, often radiologically assessed but confirmed with a biopsy when needed. If death occurs within 42 days after diagnosis, this is registered as early death. A secondary malignancy is defined as any malignancy during or following treatment that is not a relapse.

Safety reporting

Adverse events

Adverse events (AEs), serious adverse events (SAEs) and Suspected Unexpected Serious Adverse Reactions (SUSARs) are defined and reported in consistence with European guidelines [106]. Adverse Events of Special Interest (AESIs) for treatment with GO include grade 3 or 4 VOD/SOS or grade 4 hyperbilirubinemia. AESIs for subjects treated with quizartinib include QTcF prolongation ≥grade 2, or meeting the laboratory criteria of Hy’s law (ALT or AST ≥ 3 × ULN with simultaneous total bilirubin ≥ 2 × ULN). Additional information on adverse event reporting is provided in Supplementary file 2.

Adverse events are actively monitored by the study sponsor. In addition, a DSMB has been appointed. At defined intervals, the DSMB will assess the progress of the trial, safety data and critical efficacy variables and recommend to the sponsor whether to continue, modify or terminate the trial. The DSMB consists of two pediatric hemato-oncologists and one statistician.

Safety run-in quizartinib

The linked Quizartinib trial will start with a safety run-in according to a modified version of the rolling six design [107]. Up to 6 patients will be enrolled to verify the RP2D of quizartinib for patients ≥1 month of age and treated at Dose Level 1 (starting dose). Dose limiting toxicities (DLTs, Supplementary file 3) will be assessed during induction course 1 and 2 until day 56 of each course. Patients are DLT evaluable if they have experienced a DLT during at least one of these courses, or if they have received at least 75% of the quizartinib doses of induction course 1 and 2, and have not experienced a DLT.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis plans have been written for the overall objectives of the CHIP-AML22 Master and for each substudy or linked trial. A detailed overview of the statistical analysis plans can be found in Supplementary file 4.

Discussion

Survival of pediatric AML has increased significantly over the past decades, largely based on the efforts of collaborative clinical research [51, 108]. While many aspects of AML diagnostics and treatment plans have been universally adopted by most international study groups, certain areas remain controversial or require further investigation [109]. Building on previous AML protocols within our consortium that have achieved the best outcomes reported in our pediatric AML population [5], the CHIP-AML22 adopts a more personalized approach, which is in line with other ongoing clinical research efforts in this field.

Concerning the use of targeted therapies in (cyto)genetic subgroups, the marketing authorization holder currently investigates quizartinib in children and young adults with R/R FLT3-ITD+ AML (NCT03793478). In newly diagnosed pediatric AML, also other tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are being tested in phase 3 trials. The COG completed a clinical trial on the use of sorafenib in combination with standard chemotherapy in newly diagnosed FLT3-ITD+ AML patients, demonstrating a significant improvement in EFS, RR and DFS [110]. In their newest study protocol AML1831, COG is adding the FLT3-inhibitor gilteritinib. A multicenter study in China is planning to study the use of venetoclax, avapritinib, gilteritinib or sorafenib in newly diagnosed de novo pediatric AML patients, depending on the cytogenetic features of the disease (NCT06221683). With consideration to GO, COG has now added GO to standard induction therapy in their AAML1831 protocol (a single dose of 3 mg/m2). A historical comparison with their earlier protocol will be of interest. As mentioned before, the SCRIPT-AML study compares two conditioning regimens for allo-HSCT in high-risk patients (NCT05477589). Participants of CHIP-AML22 will be encouraged to participate in this study, if eligible. Another study for AML patients (children >11 years and adults) that undergo allo-HSCT is the VIALE-T study that offers venetoclax in combination with azacytidine after transplantation (NCT04161885). This study is not open to countries included in the NOPHO-DB-SHIP consortium, apart from Spain.

The choice of investigational medicinal products that will be studied in CHIP-AML22 is based on promising results from previous research. Compared to ongoing studies, CHIP-AML22 can make an important contribution to the field by being the first to investigate the value of quizartinib in the upfront treatment of newly diagnosed pediatric AML patients. Although COG has now added GO to their standard of care, international consensus on its benefit is still lacking and may depend on the efficacy of the backbone treatment. Our findings may guide the decision whether two doses of GO in combination with conventional chemotherapy should be implemented on a wider scale. The Rc might contribute to reducing treatment toxicity in standard-risk patients and with the evaluation of quality of life and recommendation of the use of dexrazoxane, we aim to achieve treatment improvements for all pediatric AML patients, regardless of their (cyto)genetic profile. The approach of tailoring treatment based on an integrated evaluation of both treatment response and a limited number of (cyto)genetic aberrations differs from other consortia who use a wide range of (cyto)genetic aberrations for risk classification and may thereby provide new insights into effective risk stratification strategies.

The field of pediatric AML treatment is rapidly evolving. Over the past decades, significant progress has been made in identifying genetic disease subtypes associated with poor prognosis that may benefit from either allo-HSCT or targeted therapies [111, 112]. CHIP-AML22 has therefore extended its genetic criteria for high-risk treatment, and additional randomization studies or linked trials for specific subgroups may be incorporated into the protocol in the future. Promising results have been reported from phase I and II trials using menin inhibitors in the treatment of KMT2Ar, NPM1m and NUP98r leukemias [113]. FOLR1-targeted therapies and BCL-2 inhibitors such as venetoclax may broaden treatment options for patients with the CBFA2T3::GLIS2 fusion gene or for AML in general, respectively [111, 112]. Synergistic effects of combining targeted therapies have been observed, offering further potential to improve AML outcomes [113]. However, the identification of genetic subtypes also presents a challenge: as the number of defined categories increases, the number of patients per category decreases, making it more difficult to conduct adequately powered studies. This underscores the importance of international collaboration in evaluating new treatment strategies.

Several challenges are anticipated in the implementation of this multinational complex clinical trial. For quizartinib, CHIP-AML22 will be the first to assess its efficacy in a population of newly diagnosed pediatric AML patients. With this comes the potential risk of encountering toxicities that have not been described before. As an example, when the FLT3-inhibitor sorafenib was first incorporated into treatment for FLT3-ITD positive AML patients in the COG AAML1031 protocol, a change in dosing schedule was necessary after interim safety analysis [110]. A safety run-in and strict toxicity monitoring will help mitigate this risk. An additional challenge of this study could be the recruitment for the Rc study. Parents may find it difficult to opt for a chance to get reduced chemotherapy, as more intensive treatment is often perceived as offering a better chance of survival. Clear information for patients and families and early identification and evaluation of potential recruitment challenges will be essential. A further difficulty of this study lies in the large number of participating countries on various continents. Treatment group allocation in CHIP-AML22 relies on timely genetic analysis and advanced flow cytometric analysis, and the performance of these methods needs to be stable throughout the whole consortium. Trained laboratories and the use of standard operating procedures (SOPs) will contribute to the speed of this process to allow timely identification of all patients that are eligible for the randomization studies or linked Quizartinib trial. Flow MRD measurements are crucial for risk-adapted treatment and as the main endpoint of the Ri substudy and Quizartinib trial. The quality of MRD assessments will be ascertained through a defined quality assurance procedure including SOPs, exchange of analysis files, educational program and participation in external and study-specific quality control programs for all analyzing laboratories. Source data will be verified by monitoring. In the Quizartinib trial, MRD analysis will be centralized. Quality control is among others maintained by offering on-site training for the sub-trials, the use of one centralized database and structured communication by assigning national coordinating centers. The consortium representatives meet three times per year, twice online and once in-person, with additional contact if necessary in between.

In conclusion, the implementation of this study protocol aims to enhance risk-group adapted treatment with respect to event-free survival, reduce treatment toxicity and promote the use of targeted therapies in the treatment of newly diagnosed pediatric AML. Joint efforts and a multinational approach are essential for addressing this rare disease in a vulnerable population.
