Bone marrow–derived stem cells for acute myocardial infarction: a systematic review and meta-analysis on efficacy and safety
Authors: Xiaomeng Gu, Lingyan Sun, Xinrui Yang, Xiaoyu Wang, Xiaoli Wang, Min Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute myocardial infarction (AMI) is one of the main causes of global morbidity and mortality. Stem cell-based therapy offers a promising approach for myocardial regeneration and tissue repair. Among them, bone marrow-derived stem cells (BMSCs) have shown remarkable therapeutic benefits in various clinical scenarios. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of BMSCs in patients with AMI.MethodsThe computer retrieved the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science and Wiley Online Library databases, collected the randomized controlled trials on BMSCs for the treatment of AMI published in October 2025, and conducted data analysis using RevMan 5.4 and Stata 18.0.ResultsWe enrolled 25 trials with 1822 patients, 960 patients in the BMSCs group, and 862 patients in the conventional treatment group. Meta-analysis findings indicated that BMSCs transplantation significantly enhanced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of patients with AMI relative to controls (MD = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.39 to 3.51) and reduced wall motion score index (WMSI) (SMD = -0.32, 95% CI: -0.49 to -0.15). For left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) (SMD = -0.01, 95% CI: -0.16 to 0.13), left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV) (SMD = -0.04, 95% CI: -0.13 to 0.06) and myocardial infarct size (SMD = 0.01, 95% CI: -0.14 to 0.15), there was a trend toward improvement after infusion of BMSCs, but the difference was not statistically significant. In terms of safety, BMSCs transplantation did not increase the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) (OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.57 to 1.12).ConclusionBMSCs can effectively improve cardiac function in patients with AMI and demonstrate a favorable safety profile. However, their effect on ventricular remodeling remains uncertain, warranting further high-quality evidence to assess long-term outcomes.Trial RegistrationCRD420251147871.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06164-2.Keywords: Acute myocardial infarction, Bone marrow-derived stem cells, Cardiac function, Therapeutic efficacy

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) results from acute, ongoing ischemia and hypoxia of the myocardial tissue, leading to necrosis. Statistics indicate that a substantial proportion of patients progress to heart failure within five years after AMI, imposing a significant economic burden on health care systems [1]. Although reperfusion therapy can effectively reopen the infarct-related artery, it cannot reverse the necrosis of myocardial cells and may induce myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury [2]. Therefore, exploring novel therapeutic strategies to repair damaged myocardium remains an urgent clinical priority.

Recent progress in stem cell biology and regenerative medicine has provided potential therapeutic strategies for addressing this clinical challenge [3, 4]. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) belong to adult stem cells, mainly including bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs), bone marrow mononuclear cells (BM-MNCs) and bone marrow progenitor cells (BMPCs), which can be directly differentiated into cardiac and endothelial lineages in myocardial repair, as well as through paracrine secretion of bioactive factors that promote myocardial repair, angiogenesis, and suppression of inflammation [5, 6]. Among these cell types, BM-MSCs are characterized by high expression of CD73, CD90, CD105 and other surface markers. They possess well-defined trilineage differentiation potential (osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic), strong paracrine activity, and notable immunomodulatory properties [7]; BM-MNCs are mononuclear cells isolated by density gradient centrifugation. They are complex in composition, including hematopoietic stem cells, mesenchymal stem cells, endothelial progenitor cells and various mature blood cells and immune cells. Its mechanism of action may depend on the synergy of multiple cells [8]; BMPCs usually refer to early hematopoietic and endothelial progenitor cells enriched with specific surface markers (such as CD34 + or CD133+), which are mainly involved in angiogenesis and endothelial repair [9–11]. Although the three are homologous, there are essential differences in cell composition, differentiation potential and core repair mechanism: BM-MSCs are good at parasecretion and immunomodulation, BM-MNCs focus on providing broad-spectrum cell support, while BMPCs mainly drive angiogenesis [5]. In recent years, many clinical and basic studies have investigated the therapeutic effects of BMSCs; however, results have been inconsistent. Some studies indicate that BMSCs transplantation can significantly improve cardiac function [12, 13], whereas others report no appreciable benefit [14]. These discrepancies may stem from differences in study design, cell type, cell dose, timing of administration, and transplantation modality.

This study comprehensively integrates existing clinical evidence to clarify the therapeutic efficacy and safety of BMSCs in AMI, providing more comprehensive evidence for the clinical application of this therapy.

Materials and methods

Literature search strategy

We carried out a systematic electronic search across the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, and Wiley Online Library databases, examining all available records from database inception through October 2025. The strategy was built upon a combination of subject headings and complementary free-text words. The English search terms included: “Bone marrow-derived stem cells,” “Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells,” “Bone marrow mononuclear cells,” “Bone marrow-derived progenitor cells,” “Stem cell transplantation,” “Acute myocardial infarction,” “Myocardial infarction,” “BMSCs,” “BM-MSCs,” “BM-MNCs,” “BMPCs,” “AMI,” “MI.” The bibliographies of the included studies were scrutinized by hand to retrieve pertinent research and ensure the comprehensiveness of our search.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies that comply with the PICOS standards below shall be included: (1) Population: Patients diagnosed with AMI, the age, gender, race and time of onset of the patient are not restricted. (2) Intervention: The experimental group received BM-MSCs or BM-MNCs or BMPCs, transplantation (transcoronary, intravenous or intramyocardial injection), and the control group received routine treatment (conventional drugs: nerve protectors and antiplatelet drugs, etc.) or percutaneous coronary intervention. (3) Comparison: A comparative analysis was conducted between the BMSCs group and the control group. (4) Outcome: The main outcome indicators include left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV), left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), wall motion score index (WMSI) and Infarct Size. The secondary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), delineated to include all-cause mortality, the combination of MI, target blood circulation reconstruction, re-hospitalization due to heart failure and life-threatening arrhythmia. (5) Study design: Eligible studies were the randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) No control group or no source of control group and treatment group. (2) Reviews, case reports, commentaries, and basic science studies (animal experiments). (3) Studies with incomplete data or inaccessible full text. (4) Studies with small total sample size (n ≤ 10).

Literature Screening and Data Extraction

Two independent investigators evaluated study eligibility, conducted data extraction, and then compared the results to reach consensus. If values were missing from the retrieved articles, data were obtained through text tracing and direct communication with the authors whenever possible to acquire original data for analysis. A pre-defined, standardized data extraction sheet was used to collect the following information, including: the first author, year of publication, the characteristics of the patient (sample size, age, gender), risk factors, cell source, intervention, cell transplantation route, primary efficacy endpoints, follow-up duration, and outcome measures. The outcomes included LVEF, LVEDV, LVESV, Infarct Size, WMSI and MACEs following BMSCs transplantation.

Risk of Bias

Methodological quality of the included RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane RoB tool. The domains evaluated are randomization sequence generation; concealment of treatment allocation; blinding of participants and personnel; blinding of outcome assessment; incomplete outcome data; selective reporting and other biases. Each study was judged to have a “low,” “high,” or “unclear” risk. The assessment was conducted independently by two researchers, with cross-checking.

Statistical analysis

Following the extraction of basic study information by two reviewers, the statistical data were systematically compiled and cross-checked according to Meta-analysis requirements. A data extraction sheet was established and generalized by RevMan 5.4 and Stata 18.0. For functional outcome measures, the baseline-to-post-treatment change was combined and described. For two classification variables, the effect quantity is expressed as the odds ratio (OR). In calculating the standard deviation (SD) of change scores (pre- to post-treatment differences) for Meta-analysis, we refer to the article by Borenstein et al. [15]. The I2 statistic was utilized to quantify heterogeneity among included studies.

Given a sufficient number of studies, assessment of publication bias was conducted using funnel plots, adopting P < 0.05 as indicative of statistical significance. Where considerable heterogeneity emerged, apply subgroup or sensitivity analysis to investigate the sources of heterogeneity. Considering the wide variation in follow-up duration across included studies, we pre‑specified a subgroup analysis based on follow‑up time to explore potential clinical heterogeneity. Studies were categorised into three groups: short‑term follow‑up (< 6 months), medium‑term follow‑up (6 to 12 months), and long‑term follow‑up (> 12 months).

Results

Literature search and screening results

From an initial screening, 949 relevant articles were identified. After sequential screening, 25 RCTs were included. Figure 1 gives a detailed description of the review process and PRISMA flow diagram.

The Literature screening process and the PRISMA flow diagram

General characteristics of the included studies and Methodological Quality Appraisal

The included literature comprised 25 studies, with a total of 1822 patients; of these, 960 were in the BMSCs group, and 862 were in the control group. Sample sizes varied between 12 and 250 participants. The follow-up interval extended from 3 to 60 months. Cell transplantation methods included intracoronary transplantation, intravenous transplantation, and intramyocardial injection. The comparator group received conventional therapy or placebo. Among the 25 studies, six clinical studies used BM-MSCs [14, 16–20], eleven studies used BM-MNCs [21–31], and eight studies used MSCs/BMPCs [12, 32–38]. Details of the baseline features of the selected studies are shown in Supplementary Table 1 (see Additional file 1).

Among the 25 studies, the randomization methods were appropriate. Nine studies used computer-generated randomization, one study used sealed opaque envelopes, one study used a random number table, and the rest of the studies did not specify a precise randomization method. Allocation concealment was implemented in 10 studies, and both investigators and participants were blinded in 13 studies. Two studies showed a low risk, whereas the rest of the studies identified a moderate risk of bias. The outcomes of the bias evaluation for the eligible trials are shown in Supplementary Figs. 1–2 (Additional file 2).

Meta-analysis Results

Effect of BMSCs on LVEF

Twenty-five studies reported changes in LVEF, and according to the study data (as shown in Fig. 2), the transplantation group was better than conventional therapy in increasing LVEF of patients with AMI (MD = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.39 to 3.51, P < 0.00001). Studies were classified by follow-up duration into into short-term, medium-term, and long-term follow-up groups. A greater improvement in LVEF was noted in studies with a follow-up of 6–12 months, while outcomes varied greatly in long-term follow-up studies, indicating follow-up duration may be one of a potential contributor to heterogeneity.

Effect of BMSCs on Left Ventricular Volumes

Twenty-three studies reported changes in LVESV and LVEDV. The transplantation group showed no statistically significant differences in LVESV (SMD = -0.04, 95% CI: -0.13 to 0.06, P = 0.46) or LVEDV (SMD = -0.01, 95% CI: -0.16 to 0.13, P = 0.85) compared with the conventional treatment group. These results indicate that BMSCs did not produce an appreciable improvement in these two left ventricular volume parameters, as specifically demonstrated in Figs. 3 and 4.

Effect of BMSCs on Infarct Size

Five studies reported changes in infarct size (as shown in Fig. 5). Meta-analysis results revealed that the between-group difference in infarct size failed to reach statistical significance (SMD = 0.01, 95% CI: -0.14 to 0.15, P = 0.93), indicating that transplantation did not significantly reduce infarct size in patients with AMI.

Effect of BMSCs on WMSI

Eight studies reported WMSI, as illustrated in Fig. 6, and the heterogeneity testing yielded p = 0.0002 and I2 = 24%. The data showed that BMSCs therapy conferred pronounced benefits to patients by lowering WMSI (SMD = -0.32, 95% CI: -0.49 to -0.15, P = 0.0002). According to the study definitions, lower WMSI indicates improved regional ventricular wall motion performance.

Incidence of MACEs

The incidence of MACEs was reported in 21 trials including 1,431 patients (747 in the stem cell and 684 in the conventional therapy group). Three of these studies reported no adverse events throughout the treatment period. Meta-analysis results are visualized in Figs. 7 and 8. No significant difference in MACEs was observed between BMSCs and the control group (I2 = 0%, OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.57 to 1.12, P = 0.20).

Analysis of publication bias and sensitivity

The potential publication bias was evaluated by using funnel charts and Egger and Begg tests. The results showed that Egger tests (p = 0.041) and Begg tests (p = 0.0265), as shown in Supplement Fig. 3 (Additional file 2). There was an asymmetry in the funnel graph, which indicated a certain degree of publication bias. The reason for this is that some of the studies included are small and data are missing for certain trials. Using the trim and fill approach to estimate the impact of publication bias on the combined effect, it is speculated that 5 studies are missing. The corrected combined effect (g = 0.436, 95% CI: 0.230 to 0.642) showed that despite some publication bias, its impact on the stability of the overall conclusion of the analysis was smaller. This result was a more reliable estimate after controlling for publication bias. In addition, it was found that there was significant heterogeneity of LVEF. In order to deeply explore the source of heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis was carried out. The specific figure can be seen in Supplement Fig. 4 (Additional file 2). After we omitted each study one by one, there was no significant change in the analytical results.

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was performed in 25 studies reporting LVEF as an outcome measure, stratified by transplantation method (intracoronary injection, other routes), cell dose (cell count < 10 × 109, cell count ≥ 10 × 109), and cell type (BM-MSCs transplantation, BM-MNCs transplantation, MSCs/BMPCs transplantation). Table 1 indicated: cell type subgroup (MD = 2.09, 95% CI: 0.71 to 3.47, P = 0.003); cell dose subgroup (MD = 3.03, 95% CI: 0.80 to 5.27, P = 0.008); transplantation method subgroup LVEF (MD = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.32 to 1.59, P = 0.003) higher than control. This suggests that there is a statistical significance in LVEF improvement between the two groups in patients with AMI (see Supplement Figs. 5, 6 and 7) (Supplementary File 2) for details.

Importantly, beyond confirming treatment efficacy, subgroup analyses provided partial insight into the sources of heterogeneity. In particular, variation in cell dose appeared to contribute to differences in pooled effect estimates, indicating a potential dose–response relationship. However, sensitivity analyses indicated that no individual study disproportionately influenced the overall heterogeneity, supporting the stability and reliability of the primary findings. Nevertheless, the persistence of marked residual heterogeneity in LVEF outcomes suggests that variability is not fully explained by the examined study-level covariates. This indicated that heterogeneity is likely driven by multiple interacting clinical and methodological factors rather than a single determinant. Potential contributors include differences in cell processing and preparation protocols, timing of administration after myocardial infarction, baseline patient characteristics, imaging methodologies, and concomitant medical therapies.

Discussion

As terminally differentiated cells, adult myocardial cells have extremely limited self-proliferation ability, and it is difficult to achieve complete repair of damaged tissue. BMSCs provide new ideas for myocardial repair of AMI with their multidirectional differentiation potential, paracrine activity, and immunomodulatory properties [39].

Mechanism and Clinical Significance of BMSCs in Improving Cardiac Function

This Meta-analysis incorporates 25 high-quality RCTs to systematically evaluate the core value of BMSCs transplantation in AMI treatment. LVEF serves as a key measure of global cardiac performance [40], while the WMSI directly reflects local myocardial contractility. The improvement of the above indicators is commonly linked to improved recovery of cardiac performance and reduced risk of future heart failure [41]. Combined analysis shows that BMSCs transplantation can significantly improve LVEF compared with conventional treatment. Several previous meta analyses and clinical studies have also reported modest improvements in cardiac function following stem cell therapy in AMI patients, particularly in terms of LVEF recovery during mid-term follow-up [42]. Our findings are generally consistent with these reports, supporting the notion that BMSCs transplantation may exert beneficial effects on myocardial repair and functional recovery.

However, the clinical significance of LVEF improvement still needs to be interpreted carefully. Although the combined results show that the increase in LVEF is statistically significant, its absolute improvement is relatively limited (about 2.45%). More importantly, no statistically significant improvement was observed in LVEDV, LVESV, infarction area and MACEs. Previous studies have suggested that imaging- and function-based parameters such as LVEF are surrogate endpoints and may not necessarily predict meaningful long-term clinical outcomes [43, 44]. Therefore, the observed improvement in systolic function may not necessarily translate into clinically relevant benefits, such as reduced rehospitalization for heart failure or improved long-term survival. In this context, it is important to distinguish statistical significance from clinical relevance. A statistically significant increase in LVEF alone cannot be considered definitive evidence of clinically meaningful benefit, particularly in the absence of improvements in ventricular remodeling parameters and hard clinical endpoints [45]. Accordingly, the current evidence should be regarded as a promising but still preliminary treatment signal. Future studies should employ larger-scale clinical trials with long-term follow-up and hard clinical endpoints as the main outcome indicator, so as to better define the true therapeutic benefit of BMSCs transplantation in AMI [46].

Analysis of Influencing Factors of Efficacy Differences

Although BMSCs transplantation has shown an improvement trend in LVEDV, LVESV and infarction area, the differences have not attained statistical significance. The limited reversal of ventricular remodeling may be related to many factors, including the time window for cell therapy, the limited survival rate of cells after transplantation, and the heterogeneity between imaging evaluation methods [47]. Another important source of heterogeneity may come from the difference between different BMSCs subtypes [48]. The research included in this Meta-analysis mainly involves three types of cells: BM-MSCs, BM-MNCs, and BMPCs. The intrinsic differences among these cell populations in biological characteristics and reparative mechanisms may represent an important source of therapeutic heterogeneity [49]. BM-MSCs possess the most characteristic stromal stem cell phenotype and exhibit potent, relatively stable paracrine activity through the secretion of cytokines and growth factors such as VEGF, FGF, and TGF-β [50, 51]. Combined with their low immunogenicity, these properties may contribute to more consistent improvements in cardiac function, a finding that aligns with the subgroup analyses of this study, which indicated a trend toward greater efficacy of BM-MSCs [46].

In contrast, BM-MNCs represent a heterogeneous mixed-cell population whose composition is highly dependent on cell isolation protocols. Consequently, their therapeutic efficacy may vary substantially according to the proportions of hematopoietic stem cells, mesenchymal stem cells, and contaminating immune cells, potentially explaining the inconsistent findings reported across studies. The efficacy of BMPCs, meanwhile, appears to depend largely on cell surface marker selection strategies and post-transplant homing efficiency. Their principal therapeutic contribution may lie in promoting angiogenesis and increasing capillary density, whereas their effect on global systolic function (LVEF) may be less sustained than that of BM-MSCs, which exert broader reparative and supportive effects.

A clearer understanding of these subtype-specific differences may help optimize future cell products, facilitate the establishment of standardized preparation protocols, and guide the design of head-to-head comparative studies targeting specific BMSCs subtypes.

Safety and Study Limitations

Safety analysis shows that BMSCs transplantation does not elevate the risk of adverse reactions, which is consistent with previous research conclusions and confirms the safety of its clinical application. However, there are still limitations in this study: (1) There is a certain heterogeneity in the study. Although it is partially explained through subgroup analysis, the residual heterogeneity may still compromise the robustness of the findings; (2) Some studies do not report key information such as cell preparation process and transplant timing in detail, and there may be potential bias; (3) There is less long-term follow-up data in this study, and most the follow-up time included in the trial is 1–2 years. The data on the long-term safety and durability of BMSCs therapy beyond 5 years remain limited, especially the risk of tumorization still requires large-scale and ultra-long-term follow-up to be finally eliminated. In view of the above limitations, for follow-up research, we should pay close attention to the research, and reasonably expand the sample size in accordance with the compliance standards to increase the statistical effectiveness of the Meta-analysis. At the same time, pay attention to the follow-up progress of related or similar studies, and observe whether the follow-up time can be further extended to to evaluate the favorable effects and adverse risks. In addition, it can also further clarify the main mechanisms of stem cells to play therapeutic effects, especially the specific pathways and key factors of paracrine secretion, for stem cell transplantation therapy in the future. The wide application in bed provides more reliable evidence-based medical evidence.

Conclusion

In summary, BMSCs therapy for AMI can be considered a safe and effective adjunctive treatment. Transplantation of BMSCs effectively improves cardiac function in AMI patients with good safety profiles. However, further investigation is needed to clarify the mechanistic basis of BMSCs therapy. Meanwhile, large-scale, multicenter, high-quality studies are warranted to validate current findings and to provide more reliable evidence supporting its widespread clinical application.
