Abstract




Abstract:

In this paper, we apply a self-similar transformation to convert the parabolic equation with a Hardy term

\begin{cases}u_{t}-\Delta u-\mu\frac{u}{|x|^{2}}=|u|^{2^{*}-2}u&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{cases}

into the following parabolic equation

\begin{cases}v_{s}-\Delta v-\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\nabla v=\beta v+\frac{\mu v}{|y|^{2}}+|v|^{2^{*}-2}v&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,S),\\
\left.v\right|_{s=0}=v_{0}&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{cases}

where
N\geqslant 3
,
\mu\in[0,(N-2)^{2}/8]
and
2^{\ast}=2N/(N-2)
.
For this equation, we establish a weighted Hardy inequality. Furthermore, by virtue of the modified potential well method and Palais-Smale sequence analysis, we investigate the long-time behavior and finite-time blow-up properties of solutions to the parabolic equation.

Keywords:
Parabolic equation, Hardy inequality, Self-similar transformation, Blow-up.

Introduction

This paper is concerned with the following classical semilinear parabolic equation associated with critical Sobolev exponent and hardy term in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
:

\begin{cases}u_{t}-\Delta u-\mu\frac{u}{|x|^{2}}=|u|^{p-1}u&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
N\geqslant 3
,
\mu\in[0,(N-2)^{2}/8]
,
p=2^{\ast}-1
and
2^{\ast}=2N/(N-2)
.

Equation (
1.1
) can serve as a fundamental model for a wide range of problems originating from quantum mechanics, chemistry, cosmology, astrophysics, and differential geometry.
The inverse square potential
1/|x|^{2}
is prevalent across multiple disciplines (see
[
3
,
12
]
).
For instance, it emerges in point dipole interactions within molecular physics
[
13
]
: specifically, the interaction between the electron’s charge and the molecule’s dipole moment gives rise to long-range forces, which in turn introduces an inverse square potential into the Schrödinger equation describing the electron’s wave function.
Furthermore, Equation (
1.1
) is closely intertwined with the Yamabe problem on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{N}
. In fact, via stereographic projection, we can identify
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\mathbb{S}^{N}
and equip
\mathbb{S}^{N}
with a metric whose scalar curvature is singular at the north pole.
The problem of finding a conformal metric with a prescribed scalar curvature of 1 thereby reduces to solving Equation (
1.1
) (see
[
1
]
).

When
\mu=0
, problem (
1.1
) becomes the following classical heat equation:

\begin{cases}u_{t}-\Delta u=|u|^{q-1}u&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.\end{cases}

(1.2)

This equation has been studied in the literature
[
4
,
7
,
8
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
28
,
30
]
.

In 1966, Fujita
[
4
]
studied problem (
1.2
) and obtained the classical critical Fujita exponent
q^{\star}=1+\frac{2}{n}
.
It is proved that when
1<q\leq q^{\star}
,
all solutions corresponding to any nonnegative nontrivial initial data cannot exist globally and must blow up in finite time.
When
q>q^{\star}
, there exists sufficiently small nonnegative initial data such that the equation admits globally bounded smooth solutions, and only solutions with large initial data blow up,
which completely characterizes the dichotomous critical behavior generated by the competition between diffusion effect and nonlinear growth effect.

In
[
30
]
, by using the heat semigroup and contraction fixed point theorem as the main tools, Weissler studied the solvability of problem (
1.2
) with
L^{p}
initial data and obtained the Fujita exponent
q_{c}=1+\frac{2p}{n}
.
Similar results to those in
[
4
]
were proved.
Thus, the classical Fujita critical blow-up theory is extended from lower-order spaces to the general
L^{p}
framework, and the critical division theory of local well-posedness for semilinear parabolic equations is improved.

In
[
17
]
, a sufficient condition on the decay order of initial data, which may change sign, such that the solution
of (
1.2
) blows up in finite time, was given. Using self-similar transformation, Mizoguchi and Yanagida

[
18
,
19
]
established the global existence and blow-up results for problem (
1.2
) in
\mathbb{R}^{1}
.
In
[
7
,
8
]
, the decay and blow-up of the solution with low energy initial data were studied by means of the potential well and forward self-similar transformation.

The following heat equation with potential has also been studied in some literature
[
9
,
10
,
24
,
25
,
33
]
:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=V(x)u+b(x)|u|^{q-1}u&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{cases}

(1.3)

In
[
25
]
, the authors assumed that
V(x)=1
, and
0\leq b(x)\leqslant C|x|^{m}
,
(m\in(-2,\infty))
and proved a critical Fujita exponent
q_{1}
such that
when
1<q<q_{1}
, there is no global solution for equation (
1.3
); if
q>q_{1}
, then both global and non-global solutions exist.

In
[
24
]
, the authors assumed that
V(x)\sim\frac{\omega}{|x|^{2}}
,
b(x)\approx c_{2}|x|^{m},(m\in(-\infty,\infty))
as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
,
and the authors proved that there exists a critical Fujita exponent
q_{2}
; global solutions exist when
q>q_{2}
; while all solutions blow up in finite time when
1<q\leqslant q_{2}
.
In
[
33
]
, Zhang assumed that
N\geq 3
,
V(x)=\frac{a}{1+|x|^{b}}(b>0)
, and combined with several restrictions on parameters
a,b
, the corresponding Fujita critical exponent was obtained.
Afterwards, in
[
9
]
, Ishige relaxed the dimension condition to
N\geq 2
, and assumed that the parameter
a>0
,
V(x)=\frac{a}{|x|^{2}}
and
b=1
, and also obtained the sharp Fujita critical exponent.
One can also refer to the latest research results of Ishige and Kawakami
[
10
]
.

For a general scope of this topic, we refer the interested readers to the monograph
[
26
]
and references therein.

In this article, we consider the problem (
1.1
) with low initial energy, critical initial energy and high initial energy. The results in our paper will be obtained by the self-similar transformation and the modified potential well method. Potential well method, which was first put forward to consider semi-linear hyperbolic initial boundary value problem by Payne and Sattinger
[
23
,
27
]

around 1970s, is a powerful tool in studying the long time behaviors of solutions of some evolution equations. The potential well is defined by the level set of energy functional and the derivative functional. It is generally true that solutions starting inside the well are global in time, solutions starting outside the well and at an unstable point blow up in finite time. After the pioneer work of Sattinger and Payne,
some authors
[
6
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
29
,
32
]

used the method to study the global existence and nonexistence of solutions for various nonlinear evolution equations with initial boundary value problem.
In
[
15
,
16
]
, Liu et al. modified and improved the method by introducing a family of potential wells which include the known potential well as a special case. The modified potential well method has been used to study semilinear pseudo-parabolic equations
[
32
]

and fourth-order parabolic equation
[
5
]
. In this paper, we use the modified potential well method to obtain global existence and blow up in finite time of solutions when the initial energy is low, critical and high, respectively. When the initial energy is low, similar results are obtained in
[
7
]
, but our result is more general, moreover, we prove a more precise decay rate of the
L^{2}
norm of global solution.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we give some notations, definitions and lemmas concerning the basic properties of the related functionals and sets. Sections 3 and 4 will be devoted to the cases
E_{K}\left(v_{0}\right)<d
and
E_{K}\left(v_{0}\right)=d
, respectively, where
E_{K}(v)
will be introduced in Section 2. In Section 5, we consider the case when the initial energy is high, i.e.
E_{K}\left(v_{0}\right)>d
. In section 6, we consider the asymptotic behavior of the global solution, which is similar to
the Palais-Smale sequence of stationary equation.

2
Preliminaries and main lemmas

In this section, we shall introduce the self-similar transformation and the modified potential
well method and give a series of their properties for problem (
1.1
). The self-similar transformation
is defined as follow:

v(y,s)=(1+t)^{1/(p-1)}u(x,t),\quad t=e^{s}-1,\quad x=(1+t)^{1/2}y.

Through this transformation and the detailed calculation (see Appendix), problem (
1.1
) can be transformed into the following problem

\begin{cases}v_{s}-\Delta v-\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\nabla v=\beta v+\frac{\mu v}{|y|^{2}}+|v|^{p-1}v&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,S),\\
\left.v\right|_{s=0}=v_{0}&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{cases}

(2.1)

where
S=\log(1+T)
,
\beta=(N-2)/4
. Set

K(y):=e^{|y|^{2}/4}.

Then we let

Lv:=-\Delta v-\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\nabla v=-\frac{1}{K}\nabla\cdot(K\nabla v).

We denote by
X
the Hilbert space obtained as the completion of
C_{c}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
with respect to the norm

\|\nabla v\|_{K,2}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla v|^{2}K(y)dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

which is induced by the inner product

(v_{1},v_{2})_{K}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(\nabla v_{1}\cdot\nabla v_{2})K(y)dy.

For each
q\in\left[2,2^{\ast}\right]
, we define
L_{K}^{q}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
and
L_{K}^{q}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N},\frac{1}{|y|^{2}}\right)
as follows:

L_{K}^{q}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right):=\left\{v\text{ measurable in }\mathbb{R}^{N}:\|v\|_{K,q}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|v|^{q}K(y)dy\right)^{1/q}<\infty\right\},

L_{K}^{q}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N},\frac{1}{|y|^{2}}\right):=\left\{v\text{ measurable in }\mathbb{R}^{N}:\left\|v\right\|_{K,q,s}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1}{|y|^{2}}|v|^{q}K(y)dy\right)^{1/q}<\infty\right\}.

The domain
D(L)
of this operator consists of all
v\in L_{K}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
such that
Lv\in L_{K}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
,
and one has
D(L)=X
; see Lemma 2.1 in
[
11
]
. Moreover,
L
is positive and self-adjoint with a compact inverse.
In particular, the normalized eigenfunctions of
L
constitute a complete orthonormal basis in
L_{K}^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
.
The first eigenvalue of
L
satisfies
\lambda_{1}=N/2
, which yields the following Poincaré inequality

\lambda_{1}\|v\|_{K,2}^{2}\leqslant\|\nabla v\|_{K,2}^{2},\quad v\in X,

(2.2)

as stated in Proposition 2.3 of
[
2
]
.

For
v\in X
, we set

\displaystyle E_{K}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(|\nabla v|^{2}-\mu\frac{|v|^{2}}{|y|^{2}}-\beta|v|^{2}\right)K(y)dy-\frac{1}{2^{*}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|v|^{2^{*}}K(y)dy)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}A(v)-\frac{1}{2^{\ast}}B(v),

\displaystyle D_{K}(v)

\displaystyle=A(v)-B(v).

The Nehari manifold is defined by

\mathcal{N}=\left\{v\in X:D_{K}(v)=0,v\neq 0\right\},

which can be separated into the two unbounded sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{+}=\left\{v\in X:D_{K}(v)>0\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{-}=\left\{v\in X:D_{K}(v)<0\right\}.

The potential well and its corresponding set are defined, respectively, as

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in X:D_{K}(v)>0,E_{K}(v)<d\right\}\cup\{0\},

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in X:D_{K}(v)<0,E_{K}(v)<d\right\},

where

d=\min_{v\in X\backslash\{0\}}\max_{s\geqslant 0}E_{K}(sv)=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{N}}E_{K}(v)

is the depth of the potential well
W
.

Now let us define the level set

E^{\alpha}=\left\{v\in X:E_{K}(v)<\alpha\right\}.

Furthermore, by the definition of
E_{K}(v),\mathcal{N},E^{\alpha}
and
d
, we easily know that

\mathcal{N}^{\alpha}=\mathcal{N}\cap E^{\alpha}\equiv\left\{v\in\mathcal{N}:A(v)<\frac{2\alpha(p+1)}{p-1}\right\}\neq\varnothing\quad\text{ for all }\alpha>d.

We now define

\lambda_{\alpha}=\inf\left\{\|v\|_{K,2}:v\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\right\},\Lambda_{\alpha}=\sup\left\{\|v\|_{K,2}:v\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\right\}\text{ for all }\alpha>d.

It is clear that
\lambda_{\alpha}
is nonincreasing and
\Lambda_{\alpha}
is nondecreasing with respect to
\alpha
.

Let us define the modified functional and Nehari manifold as follows:

\displaystyle D_{K,\delta}(v)

\displaystyle=\delta A(v)-B(v),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\left\{u\in X:D_{K,\delta}(v)=0,\|\nabla v\|_{K,2}\neq 0\right\},

\displaystyle d_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\inf_{v\in N_{\delta}}E_{K}(v).

Based on Lemma

, we see that
A(v)>0
. Therefore, we can define

S_{K}=\inf\left\{\left.\frac{A(v)}{\|v\|_{K,p+1}^{2}}\right\rvert\,v\in X\setminus\{0\}\right\}

(2.4)

and

We also introduce the following sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=\left\{v_{0}\in X:\text{ the solution }v=v(s)\text{ of (\ref{e02}) blows up in finite time }\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

Definition 2.1
.

(Weak solution). We say that a function

(See

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume
u\in X
. Then we have

(1)

u/|x|\in L^{2}_{K}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
.

(2)

(The weighted Hardy inequality)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}K(x)dx\leqslant C_{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}K(x)dx

(2.5)

with
C_{N}=\left(\frac{2}{N-2}\right)^{2}
.

(3)

The constant
C_{N}
is optimal.

Proof
.

By a density argument, it suffices to consider only smooth functions
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
. Then we have

\displaystyle|u(x)|^{2}K(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(|u(\lambda x)|^{2}K(\lambda x)\right)d\lambda

\displaystyle=-2\int_{1}^{\infty}u(\lambda x)\langle x,\nabla u(\lambda x)\rangle K(\lambda x)d\lambda-\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\lambda|x|^{2}}{2}|u(\lambda x)|^{2}K(\lambda x)\right)d\lambda.

By using Hölder inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}K(x)dx

\displaystyle=-2\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(\lambda x)}{|x|}\left\langle\frac{x}{|x|},\nabla u(\lambda x)\right\rangle K(\lambda x)dxd\lambda

\displaystyle-\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}|u(\lambda x)|^{2}K(\lambda x)\right)d\lambda.

\displaystyle\leq-2\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{d\lambda}{\lambda^{N-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(y)}{|y|}\frac{\partial u(y)}{\partial r}K\left(y\right)dy

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{N-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(y)}{|y|}\frac{\partial u(y)}{\partial r}K\left(y\right)dy

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{N-2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(y)|^{2}}{|y|^{2}}K\left(y\right)dy\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left|\frac{\partial u(y)}{\partial r}\right|^{2}K\left(y\right)dy\right)^{1/2}.

And then we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}K(x)dx\leq\left(\frac{2}{N-2}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u(x)|^{2}K(x)dx.

Next, we prove that
C_{N}
is the optimal constant.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, we take the following radial function

V(r)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }r\in[0,1],\\
r^{-\gamma}e^{-\frac{|r|^{2}}{4}}&\text{ if }r>1,\end{cases}

where
\gamma=\frac{N-2+2\varepsilon}{2}
. Hence, we have

V^{2}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }r\in[0,1],\\
r^{-2\gamma}e^{-\frac{|r|^{2}}{2}}&\text{ if }r>1.\end{cases}

For
r>1
, we obtain

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=-\gamma r^{-\gamma-1}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}+r^{-\gamma}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}\cdot\left(-\frac{r}{2}\right)=-e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}r^{-\gamma}\left(\frac{\gamma}{r}+\frac{r}{2}\right)=-V(r)\left(\frac{\gamma}{r}+\frac{r}{2}\right),

\displaystyle|\nabla V(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(V^{\prime}(r)\right)^{2}=V^{2}(x)\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{|x|^{2}}+\gamma+\frac{|x|^{2}}{4}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{V^{2}(x)}{|x|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\nabla V(x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{|x|^{2}}+\gamma+\frac{|x|^{2}}{4}\right)}=\frac{|\nabla V(x)|^{2}}{\left(\gamma^{2}+\gamma|x|^{2}+\frac{|x|^{4}}{4}\right)}\geq\frac{|\nabla V(x)|^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}.

Thus, from the above relation, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{V^{2}(x)}{|x|^{2}}K(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\frac{V^{2}(x)}{|x|^{2}}K(x)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash B}\frac{V^{2}(x)}{|x|^{2}}K(x)dx

(2.6)

\displaystyle\geq\omega_{N}\int_{0}^{1}r^{N-3}K(r)dr+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla V(x)|^{2}K(x)dx,

where
\omega_{N}
is the measure of the
(N-1)
-dimensional unit sphere.

The inequality (
2.6
) implies that as long as the constant
1/\gamma^{2}
is less than
C_{N}
,
we can always construct a function
V(x)
such that the inequality (
2.5
) fails to hold. Consequently,
C_{N}
is shown to be optimal.

(4.5)

(2) Suppose that there exists a

In this section, we employ self-similar transformations to derive (2.1).
Firstly, we can know that the transform

v(y,s)=(1+t)^{1/(p-1)}u(x,t),\quad t=e^{s}-1,\quad x=(1+t)^{1/2}y,

where
p=2^{\ast}-1.

Let
\rho=1+t,\beta=\frac{1}{p-1}
. Then we have

x=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}y,\quad|x|^{2}=\rho|y|^{2},\quad t=e^{s}-1,\quad\rho=e^{s},\quad\beta=\frac{N-2}{4}

and

v(y,s)=\rho^{\beta}u(x,t),\quad u(x,t)=\rho^{-\beta}v(y,s),\quad v(y,s)=v\left(\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}x,s\right).

Moreover, we get

\partial_{t}\rho=1,\quad\partial_{s}\rho=\rho,\quad\frac{\partial s}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{\rho},

\frac{\partial y_{j}}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{-\frac{3}{2}}x_{j}=-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{-1}y_{j},

\partial_{t}v=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y_{j}}\frac{\partial y_{j}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial s}\frac{\partial s}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{2\rho}y\cdot\nabla_{y}v+\frac{1}{\rho}\partial_{s}v

and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=-\beta\rho^{-\beta-1}v+\rho^{-\beta}\partial_{t}v

\displaystyle=-\beta\rho^{-\beta-1}v+\rho^{-\beta}\left(-\frac{1}{2\rho}y\cdot\nabla v+\frac{1}{\rho}v_{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{-\beta-1}\left(v_{s}-\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\nabla v-\beta v\right).

Further calculations imply that

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}=\sum_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}=\rho^{-1}\Delta_{y},

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}u=\rho^{-1}\Delta_{y}\left(\rho^{-\beta}v\right)=\rho^{-\beta-1}\Delta_{y}v,

\displaystyle\mu\frac{u}{|x|^{2}}=\mu\frac{\rho^{-\beta}v}{\rho|y|^{2}}=\rho^{-\beta-1}\frac{\mu v}{|y|^{2}}

and

\displaystyle|u|^{2^{*}-2}u=\left|\rho^{-\beta}v\right|^{2^{*}-2}\rho^{-\beta}v=\rho^{-\left(2^{*}-2\right)\beta}\rho^{-\beta}|v|^{2^{*}-2}v=\rho^{-\beta-1}|v|^{2^{*}-2}v.

Hence,

u_{t}-\Delta u-\frac{\mu u}{|x|^{2}}=|u|^{2^{*}-2}u

can be transformed into

\rho^{-\beta-1}\left(v_{s}-\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\nabla v-\beta v\right)-\rho^{-\beta-1}\Delta v-\rho^{-\beta-1}\frac{\mu v}{|y|^{2}}=\rho^{-\beta-1}|v|^{2^{*}-2}v,

that is,

v_{s}-\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\nabla v-\Delta v-\beta v-\frac{\mu v}{|y|^{2}}=|v|^{2^{*}-2}v.
