Experience-based co-design to refine a remote parent-mediated intervention program for preterm infants after NICU discharge: a qualitative study
Authors: Yoonju Na, Sun Ju Chang, Yumi Choe, Jiyeon Kang, Jeong-Yi Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreterm infants have elevated risks of motor, cognitive, and socioemotional difficulties, yet families often face substantial barriers to accessing rehabilitation after NICU discharge. Coaching-based parent-mediated intervention program supported through remote guidance may improve continuity and accessibility of developmental care, but few programs are tailored to the unique needs of preterm infants or developed with stakeholder input. This study used experience-based co-design (EBCD) to refine an existing remote parent-mediated intervention program for preterm infants after NICU discharge.MethodsA modified five-stage EBCD framework was employed. Nine participants were recruited through purposive sampling: five mothers of preterm infants who had experienced remote rehabilitation (individual in-depth interviews) and four multidisciplinary healthcare professionals—one pediatric physiatrist, one NICU nurse, and two pediatric physical therapists (focus group interview). Data from Stages 1–3 (observation, interviews) were analyzed using inductive qualitative content analysis. Findings were synthesized into a trigger film (Stage 4) and presented at a co-design workshop involving 13 stakeholders (Stage 5) to collaboratively finalize program components.ResultsThree themes emerged from stakeholder experiences: (1) navigating the threshold—high parental motivation and accessibility advantages offset by intensive session preparation demands; (2) engaging in the interface—professional feedback enhanced parental confidence, but remote delivery created psychological pressure and a tactile disconnect; and (3) beyond the screen—static educational materials were insufficient for independent home practice and documentation burden hindered adherence. Through co-design, stakeholders prioritized a hybrid model comprising 15-minute synchronous video sessions, pre-discharge in-person training, asynchronous video resources, video-based formative assessment, and a messaging-based Q&A system. Program scope was expanded beyond motor rehabilitation to encompass oral feeding intervention, sleep hygiene, parent-infant interaction, and psychosocial outcomes.ConclusionThrough EBCD, we collaboratively refined an existing remote parent-mediated intervention program addressing key stakeholder priorities including hybrid delivery, professional feedback, and psychosocial support. The intervention balances ideal and pragmatic solutions and is prepared for feasibility testing in a future randomized controlled trial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07231-5.Keywords: Preterm infant, Parent-mediated intervention, Remote rehabilitation, Experience-based co-design, NICU discharge, Qualitative research, Telerehabilitation

Introduction

Preterm birth remains a major global health concern, with recent estimates indicating that approximately 13.4 million infants—about 10% of all live births—are born preterm each year worldwide, and these infants face persistent risks of motor, cognitive, and socioemotional difficulties [1–3]. Early developmental interventions—including enriched sensory experiences, responsive caregiving, and timely therapeutic support—can mitigate these adverse outcomes [4]. A growing body of evidence shows that parent-mediated intervention programs, which embed therapeutic strategies into natural daily routines, can enhance early motor development, communication skills, and self-regulation among high-risk infants [5–7]. Such approaches increase therapeutic dosage and capitalize on infants’ day-to-day interactions with their primary caregivers, underscoring the importance of equipping parents with developmentally appropriate skills after NICU discharge [8, 9].

Despite this need, a substantial service gap persists in the transition from hospital to home [10]. Families frequently encounter barriers to accessing specialized pediatric rehabilitation services, including travel burden, infection concerns, limited local resources, and the challenges of transporting medically fragile infants [11, 12]. Remote or home-based rehabilitation models therefore represent a promising strategy to improve accessibility, continuity, and caregiver empowerment [13, 14]. Several structured programs have been developed to address this gap: The Goals–Activity–Motor Enrichment (GAME) program [15] and the Supporting Play, Exploration, and Early Development Intervention (SPEEDI) [16] involve fortnightly therapist home visits coaching parents in goal-directed motor activities, while the Coping with and Caring for Infants with Special Needs (COPCA) [17] similarly employs a home-based coaching model that prioritizes caregiver problem-solving. A Cochrane systematic review confirmed that post-discharge early developmental interventions improve short-to-medium-term cognitive outcomes, with programs integrating parental education showing particular benefit [18]. However, nearly all established programs rely on in-person delivery, and research specifically addressing preterm infants within remote rehabilitation models remains scarce [19]. The recently developed Telehealth for Early Developmental Intervention in babies born very preterm (TEDI-Prem) [20] represents one of the first efforts to adapt therapist-supported, parent-mediated intervention to a telehealth format [21]; however, its multicenter RCT remains ongoing. Critically, existing programs—including those adapted for telehealth—have been developed primarily through expert-driven, top-down approaches, with limited incorporation of the lived experiences of families and providers, and no program has been systematically refined through a formalized stakeholder co-design process.

The evidence base for this population is further limited by the scope of existing qualitative inquiry. Previous studies have predominantly employed exploratory designs focused on parental perceptions during NICU hospitalization, rather than the post-discharge period [22–26]. Few programs include mechanisms to support parents in integrating therapeutic strategies consistently into everyday routines, particularly in ways co-developed with families themselves. Existing early intervention programs have rarely incorporated structured collaboration between healthcare professionals and families or addressed the unique developmental trajectories and medical complexities of preterm NICU graduates [4]. Taken together, these gaps underscore the need for a participatory approach that draws on the lived experiences of both families and providers in refining post-discharge rehabilitation programs.

Experience-Based Co-Design (EBCD) is a participatory framework that positions the experience of both service users and providers at the center of the design process. Unlike traditional methods that rely on passive user feedback, EBCD identifies “touchpoints”—critical moments of interaction with high emotional or practical impact—to collaboratively redesign healthcare services [27, 28]. This approach is particularly well-suited to the context of parent-mediated intervention, where relevance to families’ daily realities and sustained caregiver engagement are essential to program effectiveness [29]. To date, no study has applied EBCD to refine an existing remote rehabilitation program specifically designed for preterm infants after NICU discharge.

To address these gaps, this study used EBCD to collaborate with parents of preterm NICU graduates and multidisciplinary healthcare professionals in refining an existing coaching-based parent-led remote rehabilitation program already being implemented in clinical practice after NICU discharge. Rather than creating a wholly de novo intervention, this study was undertaken as a co-design substudy within a broader intervention project, with the aim of adapting and strengthening program components based on stakeholder experiences and priorities. The objectives were to: (1) identify stakeholder needs, preferences, and barriers regarding post-NICU rehabilitation; (2) collaboratively refine core program components, including delivery mode, content, session structure, duration, and support mechanisms.

Methods

Design

The present study adopts an EBCD framework to reflect the experiences and needs of caregivers and healthcare professionals, with the aim of improving a remote parent-mediated intervention program currently provided to preterm infants and their families. Drawing upon the eight-stage EBCD guidelines proposed by the Picker Institute Europe [30] and the two key phases identified by Green et al. [31], this study was conducted through a modified five-stage EBCD process to refine and optimize an existing remote rehabilitation service for preterm infants (Fig. 1). As depicted in Fig. 1, the modified EBCD framework functioned as a developmental funnel. Stage 1 (Observation) provided objective ‘touchpoints’ of interaction, while Stages 2 and 3 allowed for deep subjective exploration of these moments. This multi-layered data collection ensured that the ‘trigger film’ used in Stage 4 was grounded in authentic experiences, effectively bridging the gap between healthcare providers’ clinical goals and parents’ lived realities during the Stage 5 workshop. This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Additional file 1).

Modified five-stage experience-based co-design process with participant involvement at each stage

Participants

Participants were recruited through purposive sampling from a tertiary university hospital in South Korea. Potential parent participants were initially identified by a pediatric physiatrist based on their prior experience with the existing remote rehabilitation program. Eligible parents were then contacted via telephone by a research coordinator who explained the study’s objectives and procedures. For healthcare professionals, an invitation email was sent to multidisciplinary team members who had at least two years of experience in neonatal follow-up care. The specific sociodemographic characteristics of the mothers are detailed in Table 1. All five mothers had delivered extremely-to-very preterm infants (gestational age 25 + 5 to 28 + 6 weeks; birth weight 730 to 1,010 g) whose primary complication was respiratory distress syndrome, representing a clinically high-risk cohort. Although clinically comparable at birth, the infants were interviewed across a broad range of corrected ages (approximately 3 to 24 months), and all mothers had prior firsthand experience with the remote rehabilitation programs for their children. To capture the granular details of their lived experiences, challenges, and specific needs regarding home-based interventions, these participants engaged in individual, one-on-one in-depth interviews.

In parallel, the professional cohort was composed of a diverse multidisciplinary team: one pediatric physiatrist, one neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) nurse, and two pediatric physical therapists. These professionals participated in a focus group interview (FGI), a format chosen to facilitate interactive discussion regarding the clinical feasibility, systemic requirements, and professional observations necessary for an effective co-designed model.

Procedure

Stage 1. Observation of current remote rehabilitation program

The primary aim of Stage 1 was to understand how the existing remote rehabilitation program operated in practice and to generate empirically grounded questions for the subsequent interview stages. Conducted in July 2025, two researchers (JK and SJC) performed real-time observations of two live remote rehabilitation sessions. Prior to these observations, the researchers reviewed the current clinical manual, parent education materials, and intervention logs to familiarize themselves with the program’s structure. At the time, the intervention model comprised: (1) a single in-person training session at NICU discharge; (2) an 8-week program consisting of once-weekly, 15-minute Zoom sessions led by a physical therapist; (3) the provision of a booklet with instructional photographs; and (4) parent-led home exercises recorded in a manual log. The observations focused on specific ‘touchpoints’ of interaction, with particular attention to verbal and non-verbal communication between the therapist and caregiver, spatial and technical adaptations made within the home environment, and observable changes in parent and infant behavior across the session. With the caregivers’ informed consent, the researchers joined these live sessions as observers and recorded the interactions for subsequent thematic analysis; these recordings were accessed exclusively by the research team to evaluate clinical flow and interaction patterns, and all video data were destroyed immediately upon completion of the analysis to ensure participant privacy. Although formal field notes were not employed, the researchers maintained informal observational memos during and immediately after each session to capture initial impressions and recurring patterns; these memos were documented in a shared digital file accessible exclusively to the research team (JK and SJC) and were reviewed collectively prior to developing the interview guides for Stage 2. Together with repeated review of the session recordings, these memos enabled the research team to identify recurrent practical challenges and served as a foundational framework for the semi-structured interview guides in Stage 2.

Stage 2: In-depth interviews with service users

The second stage involved individual in-depth interviews with five mothers who had experienced the existing remote intervention, which were conducted between August 5 and August 26, 2025. Depending on the participants’ preferences, interviews were conducted either in person at a quiet, private hospital space or virtually via Zoom. One of the two researchers (JK/SJC) with extensive experience in qualitative research facilitated the sessions, which lasted between 60 and 120 min. All interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ consent. Field notes were not taken during data collection. A semi-structured questionnaire was utilized, centered on the primary question: “What was your experience with remote rehabilitation?” (Supplementary File 1) Probing questions followed regarding the perceived benefits, drawbacks, effectiveness for the child, and areas for improvement. Data collection ceased after five interviews upon confirming data saturation. Data saturation was determined through an iterative review process and continuous monitoring by the research team. After each interview, the researchers (JK and SJC) reviewed the transcripts to identify emerging codes and patterns. Saturation was considered to have been reached when the core themes regarding the barriers and needs of remote rehabilitation became consistently repetitive. The final determination of saturation was achieved through consensus among the research team, confirming that further data collection would not yield significantly new theoretical insights or novel categories.

Stage 3: Focus group interview with service providers

The third stage consisted of a focus group interview in September 2025 with healthcare professionals who had provided remote rehabilitation services. The FGI was conducted virtually via Zoom by one of the researchers (SJC) who had also performed the parent interviews. The session lasted approximately 60 min and was audio-recorded following participant consent. A semi-structured interview guide was used to explore professional perspectives on service delivery and systemic challenges (Supplementary File 2).

Stage 4: Development of the trigger film

In the fourth stage, a trigger film was developed between late September and early October 2025 to synthesize and present the key findings from Stages 1 to 3 to workshop participants in an accessible and experientially grounded format. The trigger film took the form of a structured slide presentation comprising 13 slides and running approximately 10 min. Content was selected based on the three overarching themes derived from the qualitative content analysis: motivations and preparation for remote rehabilitation, experiences of engaging through the remote interface, and challenges of sustaining home-based practice. Within each theme, codes and sub-categories were prioritized for inclusion if they reflected shared concerns across both service users and providers, or represented particularly salient experiential touchpoints identified during repeated transcript review. The presentation was organized sequentially, opening with an introduction to the research aims and the current program structure, followed by a summary of parent interview findings covering perceived benefits, barriers, motivations, and suggested improvements, a parallel summary of provider perspectives, and a synthesis slide comparing user and provider priorities.

Stage 5: Co-design workshop

The final stage involved a collaborative workshop held on October 31, 2025, aimed at collaboratively refining the existing remote rehabilitation service model. Participants totaled 13 individuals, including two caregivers (with remote rehabilitation experience but not involved in the prior interviews), two application developers, three pediatric physiatrists, two physical therapists, one hospital executive, one nursing researcher with app development experience, one child health nursing professor, and one NICU nurse. Facilitated by two researchers (JK/SJC), the 60-minute workshop utilized the trigger film from Stage 4 to review user and provider experiences. Participants engaged in structured discussions across three key themes: the content of the intervention, the format of delivery, and methods for effectiveness evaluation.

Data analysis

The interview data from both service users (parents) and service providers (healthcare professionals) were analyzed separately using the inductive qualitative content analysis method proposed by Elo and Kyngäs [32]. The analysis followed a systematic, three-phase process: First, in the preparation phase, the researchers repeatedly read the interview transcripts to gain a comprehensive understanding of the context. During the organization phase, open coding was performed by extracting and labeling meaningful words, phrases, and sentences that captured key aspects of the participants’ experiences. Second, the initial codes were grouped based on similarities and differences to form sub-categories and categories. Finally, during the reporting phase, the researchers engaged in an iterative process of abstraction, collapsing related categories into higher-order main categories to reduce the number of categories while increasing the level of conceptual abstraction. This inductive process allowed the researchers to derive overarching themes that authentically represented the remote rehabilitation experiences of both users and providers. To enhance credibility, two researchers (JYK and SJC) independently coded all transcripts. Although individual member checking of final transcripts was not conducted due to the time-sensitive nature of the study timeline, the Stage 5 co-design workshop served as a form of collective validation. Specifically, the trigger film presented in Stage 4 was constructed directly from the thematic findings of Stages 2 and 3, and workshop participants—including caregivers and multidisciplinary professionals—were invited to review, affirm, or contest these synthesized findings before any intervention refinements were agreed upon. This process of group-level verification, while distinct from individual member checking, functioned as a collective validation of the qualitative interpretations and is consistent with participatory approaches to credibility in EBCD research. Analytical neutrality was further maintained by having two independent researchers perform the initial coding and reconciling discrepancies through consensus meetings. Data were managed and analyzed manually without the use of qualitative analysis software. All data collection, including interviews and workshops, was conducted in Korean. To ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence in this report, the selected quotations were translated into English by the primary author and subsequently verified through a back-translation process by an independent bilingual researcher fluent in both medical and qualitative terminology.

Reflexivity

The principal investigator (JYK), who was responsible for participant recruitment and ongoing clinical care, did not conduct the in-depth interviews or focus group interview to minimize power imbalance between clinician and patient. All interviews were facilitated by two researchers (JK and SJC) who had no prior clinical relationship with participants. However, the principal investigator did participate in the co-design workshop (Stage 5) as a clinical expert contributor. To mitigate potential influence on workshop discussions, the sessions were facilitated by JK and SJC, and structured activities were used to encourage equal contribution from all participants. The research team held regular debriefing meetings throughout the study to reflexively examine interpretations and ensure findings authentically represented stakeholder perspectives.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the institution to which the researchers are affiliated (Approval No. blinded for the review). All participants were provided with a comprehensive explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits before any data collection began.

To ensure voluntary participation, it was explicitly stated that participants could withdraw from the study at any time without any disadvantage to their medical care or professional standing. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. To protect participant confidentiality, all identifying information was anonymized and replaced with participant codes (e.g., Mother 1, Professional 2). Audio and video recordings used for data analysis and the development of the trigger film were stored on a password-protected secure server and were permanently deleted immediately after the completion of the study to prevent any unauthorized access.

Results

Capturing & understanding the experience (from the stages 1–3)

Analysis revealed that while parents of preterm infants and healthcare professionals agreed on the necessity of remote rehabilitation, they encountered various practical barriers during implementation. This section describes the key themes derived from in-depth interviews with parents and focus group interviews with professionals based on the touchpoints of their experience.

Navigating the threshold: motivations and preparations for remote care

Parents exhibited a profound commitment to mitigating their children’s developmental risks, a motivation deeply rooted in high levels of anxiety and a sense of moral responsibility. This emotional drive led them to perceive remote rehabilitation not just as a medical service, but as a vital psychological safety net. As Mother 2 shared, “If I hadn’t received this, I would have likely hired a private home therapist at my own expense… simply because I felt so anxious,” suggesting that the intervention serves to preemptively alleviate the financial and emotional desperation parents feel when navigating the post-discharge vacuum. Both parents and providers identified the high accessibility of this model—specifically the elimination of long-distance travel with a medically fragile neonate—as its most critical structural advantage. Professional 1 reinforced this by noting that for families in remote areas, this model provides the “most convenient point of access,” effectively bridging the geographical gap in specialized pediatric care.

Furthermore, healthcare providers observed that the program optimized the information-seeking process by providing a professional, evidence-based alternative to the “unverified online communities” that parents often turn to in moments of uncertainty. However, this accessibility came with hidden pragmatic costs; the transition to a remote session required intensive, invisible labor. Parents had to rigorously synchronize the infant’s physiological rhythms—such as feeding and nap schedules—with the technical demands of a digital setup. Mother 3 described this exhausting preparation: “I have to finish feeding an hour or an hour and a half early… You have to align the nap and feeding times,” highlighting that the “convenience” of remote care is often built upon a foundation of domestic multitasking and collective family effort. Professional 2 observed that this burden often necessitated a physical “helper” by the mother’s side, indicating that while the intervention is remote, it remains a resource-intensive endeavor for the household unit.

Engaging in the interface: professional guidance and emotional paradoxes

During active engagement, both parents and professionals experienced an emotional duality defined by increased self-efficacy shadowed by inherent practical limitations. Professional feedback functioned as a critical eye, allowing parents to identify developmental nuances that had previously gone unnoticed, thereby fostering a sense of clinical competence and accomplishment. Mother 1 articulated this growth in agency, noting, “By receiving so much expert input, I [learned] a lot about things I hadn’t been able to check myself… it actually gave me more confidence… a sense of achievement”. This suggests that the remote rehabilitation interface, while digital, acts as a mirror that reflects the parents’ growing skills back to them, transforming them from passive caregivers into active therapeutic agents.

Conversely, this high level of professional scrutiny created a paradoxical psychological burden, where the home environment—typically a private sanctuary—was transformed into a space of clinical evaluation. Some parents perceived these synchronous sessions as a high-pressure “inspection” rather than a supportive coaching encounter. Professional 2 observed this tension, stating, “There is a slight worry that their stress might actually increase… as they feel like they are being tested on their ‘homework’”. This “homework pressure” highlights a risk of the remote format where the lack of informal, in-person rapport can lead to an over-emphasis on performance and results. Furthermore, the absence of physical presence led to a “tactile disconnect,” a fundamental barrier where both parties felt a persistent uncertainty regarding the precise intensity and safety of physical maneuvers through a two-dimensional screen. This disconnect underscores the ontological limitation of remote rehabilitation: the inability to bridge the sensory gap between seeing a movement and feeling its corrective force.

Beyond the screen: the challenges of sustaining home-based practice

The transition from guided sessions to independent home practice highlighted a critical gap between static educational resources and the dynamic nature of infant development. Parents frequently reported that traditional manuals were inadequate for troubleshooting when their infants’ behaviors deviated from the “ideal” scenarios depicted in the materials. Mother 2 articulated this frustration, stating, “I try to follow the booklet, but babies don’t always cooperate… The booklet alone doesn’t provide enough explanation”. This perceived inadequacy often forced parents into a vulnerable position, where they felt compelled to rely on unverified external videos to bridge the gap, potentially leading to incorrect or unsafe therapeutic applications. Furthermore, the study identified that the logistical and emotional exhaustion inherent in neonatal caregiving acts as a significant structural barrier to long-term adherence. The requirement for detailed manual documentation—intended as a tool for progress tracking—instead became a source of cognitive overload. As Mother 1 recalled, “I had to write the log after the session was over, after changing diapers, feeding, and putting the baby to sleep. By then, I couldn’t remember the details”. This exhaustion-induced memory decay suggests that traditional paper-based logging is functionally incompatible with the lived realities of NICU graduates’ families. Consequently, the “beyond the screen” experience reveals a pressing need for remote rehabilitation models to transition toward more intuitive, automated, and responsive support systems that accommodate the fragmented daily routines of caregivers.

Co-designing the program: prioritizing and refining solutions (from stages 4–5)

The findings from Stages 1–3 were synthesized into a trigger film, which served as the experiential bridge between the qualitative data and the co-design process. The trigger film concluded with three structured discussion topics that had emerged from the thematic analysis: the content of the intervention, the format of delivery, and methods for effectiveness evaluation. Screened at the outset of the Stage 5 workshop, the trigger film oriented all 13 participants—including those not involved in the earlier interview stages—to the lived experiences of service users and providers, ensuring that co-design decisions were grounded in stakeholder experience rather than professional assumption alone.

During the co-design workshops, stakeholders engaged in intensive discussions to bridge the gap between “ideal solutions” and “pragmatic constraints” based on the previously identified experiences of parents and healthcare providers. This collaborative process resulted in the consolidation of key components tailored for a sustainable remote rehabilitation model, ensuring that the program reflects both the lived realities of families and the clinical expertise of professionals. As illustrated in Fig. 2, each qualitative theme informed one or more workshop discussion topics, which in turn generated specific co-designed refinements. Across all three themes, a consistent direction of change emerged: components that were originally static, unidirectional, or narrowly motor-focused were reshaped into dynamic, interactive, and multidisciplinary alternatives, indicating that the final program components were grounded in the qualitative findings rather than determined a priori.

Logic map linking qualitative themes to co-designed intervention refinements through the EBCD

Grounded in Themes 1 and 2, discussions on the format of delivery focused on session structure and mode of engagement. While parents initially expressed a preference for longer sessions, a consensus was reached to limit synchronous video sessions to 15 min of active therapy, considering the infants’ limited endurance, potential fatigue, and the clinical workload of the providers. Furthermore, the intervention prioritizes formative assessment, where parents demonstrate techniques via live video or pre-recorded clips for immediate professional feedback and correction—directly addressing the tactile disconnect identified in Theme 2. To ensure handling quality prior to remote sessions, a pre-discharge intensive training approach was also agreed upon, where foundational techniques are taught in person before transitioning to home-based care.

Discussions on the content of the intervention, informed primarily by Theme 2, led to the expansion of the program scope to encompass holistic family needs beyond traditional motor rehabilitation. Stakeholders agreed to incorporate comprehensive support for feeding difficulties, oral feeding intervention, sleep hygiene, and parent-infant interaction—reflecting a transition to a multidisciplinary, family-centered approach that addresses the multidimensional concerns of NICU families during the early post-discharge period.

Drawing on Theme 3, discussions on the content of the intervention and methods for evaluation addressed the challenges of sustaining home-based practice. To address the insufficiency of static educational materials, personalized recordings of each infant’s own therapy sessions will be made available to caregivers, serving as accessible visual references for home practice and enabling other family members such as fathers to understand correct handling techniques. While an integrated application featuring automated movement analysis was discussed as an ideal, pragmatic considerations—including data security, storage capacity, and operational costs—led to the selection of a streamlined mobile application focused on automated reminders and simplified logging. For ongoing communication between sessions, a widely used smartphone messaging platform (e.g., KakaoTalk in South Korea or WhatsApp) was designated as the primary Q&A channel, allowing for the asynchronous exchange of video and text queries, minimizing provider burden while ensuring timely responses during daily care. Additionally, these platforms were viewed as a means to facilitate peer interaction and social support among caregivers.

Finally, cutting across Themes 2 and 3, stakeholders agreed on the importance of strengthening psychosocial support within the content of the intervention and embedding multi-dimensional variables within the methods for evaluation. There was strong consensus that program success should be measured not only by physical milestones but also by its impact on the family’s psychological stability. Accordingly, the intervention model incorporates psychosocial variables such as Post-Intensive Care Syndrome-Family (PICS-F), maternal-infant attachment, parenting confidence, and collaborative co-parenting. The program design further encourages active spousal participation and emotional support, ensuring the intervention functions as a comprehensive resource for the entire family unit.

The final co-designed refinement of the remote rehabilitation

The “Parent-Led Remote Rehabilitation Model,” finalized through the collaborative workshops, is structured into three distinct phases—Preparation, Remote Session, and Maintenance—to ensure a seamless transition from hospital-based education to sustainable home-based care. As detailed in Fig. 3, the Preparation phase occurs prior to NICU discharge and focuses on intensive hands-on training to mitigate the “tactile disconnect” and technical uncertainty that parents and professionals often experience in remote settings. This is followed by the Remote Session phase, which serves as the core intervention period. Based on stakeholder feedback regarding infant fatigue and provider workload, this phase utilizes 15-minute synchronous sessions that expand beyond traditional motor goals to include multidisciplinary support for feeding, sleep, and parent-infant interaction. Finally, the Maintenance phase establishes a continuous safety net for families through a hybrid support system. To address the “burden of manual documentation” identified in the qualitative analysis, this phase replaces paper logs with a simplified mobile application featuring automated reminders and provides personalized video manuals for independent home practice. By integrating these components, the model illustrated in Fig. 3 successfully bridges the gap between clinical requirements and the pragmatic daily realities of post-NICU life.

Discussion

This study employed EBCD to collaboratively refine a coaching based parent-mediated remote rehabilitation program for preterm infants following NICU discharge. By engaging parents, NICU healthcare professionals, and community-based experts, we integrated both service user and provider perspectives into a cohesive intervention model. To our knowledge, this represents the first application of EBCD to refine and optimize a post-discharge remote rehabilitation program specifically tailored for preterm infants and their families.

Summary of key contributions

The primary contribution of this study is the collaborative refinement of a structured service model for home-based parent education following NICU discharge. Through co-design workshops, stakeholders determined core program components that balance ideal aspirations with pragmatic constraints. Key elements included 15-minute synchronous video sessions, reflecting considerations of infant fatigue and provider workload, combined with pre-discharge in-person education, asynchronous video resources for home practice review, video-based formative assessment, and a messaging-based Q&A system for ongoing support. Notably, stakeholders emphasized that parent needs extend beyond motor rehabilitation to encompass feeding, oral feeding intervention, sleep support, and parent–infant interaction, highlighting the importance of a holistic, family-centered approach.

Focus on post-discharge remote rehabilitation

In contrast to prior qualitative work that has focused primarily on exploring parental perceptions or the NICU hospitalization period [22–26], the present study moves beyond experience exploration to collaborative intervention refinement for the post-discharge period—a critical yet under-supported transition during which families frequently encounter barriers including travel burden, infection concerns, and limited local resources. By systematically engaging both parents and multidisciplinary professionals, the co-design process enhanced the likelihood of acceptability and feasibility in real-world clinical and home settings.

Integration of holistic family needs

Existing intervention research for preterm infants has often focused narrowly on motor or neurodevelopmental outcomes. The present co-design process revealed that parental needs extend substantially beyond motor rehabilitation. Stakeholders emphasized the importance of addressing feeding difficulties, oral feeding intervention, sleep support, and parent-infant interaction—reflecting the multidimensional concerns of families during the early post-discharge period. The inclusion of psychosocial outcome measures proposed by stakeholders—including parenting confidence, mother-infant attachment, and post-intensive care syndrome in families (PICS-F)—further reflects this holistic orientation.

Advantages and challenges of remote delivery

Stakeholders identified several advantages of remote delivery for this population: elimination of travel burden for medically fragile infants, reduced infection exposure, scheduling flexibility around infant routines, and the ability to observe therapeutic activities within the actual home environment. Notably, parents expressed a strong preference for mobile app-based formats, requesting features such as appointment scheduling, video consultation capabilities, educational content delivery, and asynchronous communication through familiar platforms (e.g., KakaoTalk). This app-based approach was viewed as reducing barriers to parent-provider communication while minimizing clinician burden—an approach successfully implemented at other Korean institutions.

However, stakeholders acknowledged limitations of remote delivery. Video-based assessment cannot fully replace hands-on evaluation of muscle tone or subtle movement qualities. Digital literacy barriers may exclude some families. Additionally, concerns were raised about whether app-based or messaging support might blur boundaries between education and clinical care—a consideration for program governance and liability frameworks.

The pragmatic solutions identified through co-design—such as simple alarm-based apps rather than complex motion-recognition systems, and structured Q&A protocols—reflect stakeholders’ recognition that sustainable programs must balance innovation with feasibility within existing healthcare infrastructure.

Clinical implications

The findings have several implications for clinical practice. First, this program presents a model for sustaining developmental support beyond hospital discharge, effectively addressing the transition period often overlooked by existing services. Operationalizing this within current NICU follow-up clinics, however, would require dedicated reimbursement pathways and a designated coordinator role, neither of which is currently established in most Korean tertiary centres—an implementation gap that warrants policy-level attention.

Second, the identified scope of needs—extending beyond motor rehabilitation to feeding, sleep, and interaction—suggests that post-NICU support programs should adopt multidisciplinary frameworks incorporating occupational therapy and feeding specialists alongside physical therapists. This represents a significant departure from current single-discipline referral pathways and would necessitate restructured team-based training and shared documentation systems.

Third, the proposed Q&A and peer interaction mechanisms may address the isolation commonly experienced by NICU families [33], potentially serving both educational and psychosocial support functions. The inclusion of partner participation sessions, suggested by stakeholders, aligns with evidence that paternal involvement enhances early intervention outcomes. Research indicates that greater paternal involvement in early caregiving and intervention programs is linked to positive behavioral, developmental, and family outcomes in early childhood [34].

Finally, the outcome measures proposed by stakeholders, including parenting confidence, mother–infant attachment, and post-intensive care syndrome in families (PICS-F), highlight the importance of evaluating psychosocial alongside developmental outcomes in future trials.

Study position and future directions

This study represents the first phase of a two-stage research program. The co-designed prototype establishes the foundation for a pilot study evaluating acceptability, feasibility, safety, and preliminary outcomes. Future research should examine adaptations for specific subgroups (e.g., extremely preterm infants, those with identified neurological abnormalities), cost-effectiveness relative to traditional models, and sustainability of parent skill acquisition over time.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgement. First, this study was conducted within a single tertiary center in an urban Korean context, potentially limiting transferability to other healthcare systems or cultural settings. Families in rural areas or with limited-service access may face different challenges not fully represented here.

Second, selection bias is possible, as participating parents were generally engaged with rehabilitation services, potentially underrepresenting families facing greater access barriers or digital literacy constraints.

Third, although fathers and other caregivers were not explicitly excluded, all caregiver participants who enrolled were mothers, likely reflecting the predominant caregiving role of mothers in the Korean NICU context. This may limit the representativeness of caregiver perspectives, particularly given that the co-designed program encourages active partner participation. Future studies should actively recruit fathers and diverse caregivers to ensure a broader family perspective.

Fourth, although the principal investigator’s dual role as clinician and researcher was mitigated by separating her from the interview process, she participated in the co-design workshop as a clinical expert. Despite structured facilitation by independent researchers, her clinical authority may have influenced the direction of workshop discussions, and residual effect cannot be fully excluded. Fifth, healthcare professional participants were predominantly from a single institution; inclusion of community-based or primary care perspectives might yield additional insights.

Finally, individual member checking of interview transcripts was not performed with all participants. However, the Stage 5 co-design workshop functioned as a collective validation process: participants reviewed the synthesized findings embedded in the trigger film and provided direct feedback before any co-design decisions were made. This group-level verification partially addresses the absence of individual verification, though it does not fully replicate the reflexive depth of one-to-one member checking and remains a methodological limitation to be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

Using experience-based co-design, this study refined an existing coaching-based parent-mediated remote rehabilitation program through an EBCD process for preterm infants following NICU discharge. The co-design process identified pragmatic program components that stakeholders considered feasible and acceptable—including mobile app-based real-time sessions, brief synchronous sessions, pre-discharge training, asynchronous support systems, video-based assessment, and holistic content addressing motor, feeding, and psychosocial needs. These findings provide a foundation for accessible, family-centered early intervention that positions parents as active therapeutic partners. Future research should evaluate the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of this program prior to definitive trials.
