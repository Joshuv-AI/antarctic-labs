Analysis of Log-Weighted Quadrature Domains
Andrew J. Graven

Abstract
This paper studies plane domains satisfying a quadrature identity with respect to the singular weight ρ 0 ​ ( w ) = | w | − 2 \rho_{0}(w)=|w|^{-2} . These are referred to as log-weighted quadrature domains (LQDs). The logarithmic singularity at w = 0 w=0 leads to phenomena not present in the classical theory: in particular, when the domain contains the origin, the associated quadrature data are no longer unique, but are determined only up to a point charge at 0 0 . A generalized Schwarz function characterization of LQDs is established together with a natural formulation of the inverse problem in the singular setting. In the simply connected case, it is shown that a domain is an LQD if and only if the outer factor of its Riemann map extends to the exponential of a rational function. This characterization yields explicit formulae relating the quadrature function and the Riemann map via the Faber transform, thereby extending earlier formulae from the non-singular theory. Several basic classes of LQDs are also covered, and explicit examples are computed.

Introduction

The purpose of this article is to analyze plane domains admitting a quadrature identity with respect to the weight
\rho_{0}(w):=|w|^{-2}
. In particular, we are concerned with domains
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
for which

\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw

(1.1)

for all
f\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
, for some rational function
h
. We refer to such domains as
log-weighted quadrature domains
(LQDs). LQDs arise naturally as the image of classical quadrature domains under the exponential map (Theorem
2.1
). LQDs also appear as the complement of local droplets of the logarithmic potential

Q(w)=\frac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|w|^{2}-2\text{Re}(H(w)),

where
H^{\prime}
is rational (
[
8
]
, §4.1.4). We also note (but do not show here) that LQDs are preserved under Hele-Shaw flow weighted with respect to
\rho_{0}(w)=|w|^{-2}
. Weighted Hele-Shaw flow has received some attention in the literature, particularly in the context of Hele-Shaw flow on Riemann surfaces and the Polubarinova-Galin equation
[
10
,
9
]
.

This work is a generalization of the theory developed in Graven & Makarov
[
8
]
to include
singular
LQDs - domains containing the metric singularity at
w=0
. The presence of the singularity leads to a basic phenomenon absent from the classical and non-singular theory: when
0\in\Omega
, every admissible test function vanishes at the origin, and as a result the quadrature function is no longer uniquely determined by the domain, but only up to the addition of a point charge
q/w
at
0
. Thus the singular theory therefore requires new formulations of the coincidence equation, of the Schwarz function, and of the direct/inverse problems. The main contribution of the present paper is to show that, despite this loss of uniqueness, much of the classical and non-singular structure survives in modified form.

Our first main result is a Schwarz function characterization of singular LQDs. We show that a domain is an LQD if and only if it admits a generalized Schwarz function
S_{0}
with boundary values

S_{0}(w)\dot{=}\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}.

This yields, in particular, a Sakai-type boundary regularity theorem: the boundary of an LQD has only finitely many singular points, each of which is a cusp or a double point. We also establish several basic invariance properties and an electrostatic interpretation of LQDs.

In the simply connected setting, we obtain a sharper description in terms of the Riemann map. Whereas classical QDs are precisely those domains admitting a rational Riemann map, in the log-weighted setting the correct object is the outer factor of the Riemann map. In particular, we show that a simply connected domain is an LQD if and only if the outer part of its Riemann map extends to the exponential of a rational function. This leads to explicit Faber transform formulae relating the quadrature function and the Riemann map. In later sections, we apply these results to null, monomial, and one-point families of LQDs, with complete classifications in some cases and partial classifications in others.

Given a rectifiable domain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}_{r}(w_{0})}fdA(w)

A central problem in the theory of quadrature domains is uniqueness. On the one hand, uniqueness of the quadrature function associated to a given quadrature
domain is well established. However, the inverse problem of existence and uniqueness of quadrature domains
associated to a given quadrature function remains open. Non-uniqueness occurs in the multiply connected setting: for instance, a disk and the complement of an annulus with the same inner radius give distinct examples with the same quadrature data; see also Varchenko and Etingof (
[
1
]
, §2.3). In contrast, it is conjectured that simply connected quadrature domains are uniquely determined by their quadrature function, up to conformal radius. Aharonov and Shapiro (1976) showed that disks are the only one-point quadrature domains among bounded finitely connected domains
[
2
]
, and proved a similar result for the cardioid. Later on, we will establish the uniqueness of null LQDs (§
3.1
), and obtain partial uniqueness results for several other families.

1.2
Simply Connected Quadrature Domains

A core question in the study of quadrature domains is the
inverse problem
of recovering a domain from its quadrature function. The
direct problem
conversely is concerned with recovering the quadrature function associated to a given domain. In the simply connected setting, both questions reduce to a problem of relating the quadrature function to the Riemann map. The following classical theorem characterizes simply connected quadrature domains precisely via their Riemann maps.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
2
]
)
.

A simply connected domain is a quadrature domain if and only if its Riemann map extends to a rational function.

Hence, Theorem
1.2
reduces the inverse and direct problems for simply connected QDs to a problem of relating the finitely many coefficients of the quadrature function to the finitely many coefficients of the Riemann map. Theorems 1.5 and 1.6 in
[
8
]
exactly characterize this relationship via the Faber transform
\Phi_{\varphi}
. The result in the bounded case states:

Theorem 1.3

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded and simply connected QD with quadrature function
h\in\mathrm{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
and Riemann map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
. In this case,

h=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\varphi^{\#}-\overline{\varphi(0)}\right)

(1.5)

and

\varphi=\varphi(0)+\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}(h)^{\#}.

(1.6)

In particular, the Faber transform provides an implicit solution to the inverse problem, and an explicit solution to the direct problem. The result in the unbounded case (
[
8
]
, Theorem 1.6) is analogous. The details of the Faber transform and its properties are covered in the Appendix §
8.2
.

In brief, the
interior Faber transform

\Phi_{\varphi}
associated to a simply connected bounded domain
\Omega
is a bijective linear operator from functions analytic in the exterior disk
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
to functions analytic in the exterior of
\Omega
,
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega_{\ast})
. Similarly, the
exterior Faber transform
is a bijective linear operator
\Phi_{\varphi}:\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})\rightarrow\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{\ast})
.

In particular, if
\varphi
is a Riemann map associated to
\Omega
and
\psi=\varphi^{-1}
, then we compute the Faber transform of
f
by precomposing
f
with
\psi
, then projecting onto the part of
f\circ\psi
which is analytic in
\Omega_{\ast}
:

\Phi_{\varphi}(f):=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[f\circ\psi\right],

where
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}\left[\cdot\right]
is the Cauchy projection, given by

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}\left[f\right](w):=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{f(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi.

The most important three properties of the Faber transform
\Phi_{\varphi}
for our purposes are as follows

•

\Phi_{\varphi}
is an isomorphism;

•

\Phi_{\varphi}
takes rational functions to rational functions;

•

\Phi_{\varphi}
is
computable
: the Faber transform of a rational function can be computed in finitely many steps.

These three properties are crucial for establishing a useful relationship between the quadrature function and Riemann map associated to a given QD or LQD. See Appendix §
8
for a more detailed exposition of the Cauchy projection and Faber transform, as well as their various properties (see also
[
8
]
, §1.5.1).

Theory of Log-Weighted Quadrature Domains

In this section we develop the basic theory of
log-weighted quadrature domains
(LQDs). We begin by clearly defining LQDs and highlighting a method of constructing LQDs from classical QDs via the exponential map (Theorem
2.1
). We then derive the singular and non-singular coincidence equations, use them to establish the essential uniqueness of the quadrature data, and prove a generalized Schwarz-function characterization of LQDs. From this characterization we obtain boundary regularity, an electrostatic characterization, and several basic invariance properties that will be used throughout the remainder of the paper.

We consider domains
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}

\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw,

for all

\Omega
is unbounded, admissible test functions vanish at infinity. To ensure essential uniqueness of the quadrature function

when
\Omega
is bounded, and
h\in\mathrm{Rat}(\Omega)
when
\Omega

Definition 2.1

(Log-Weighted Quadrature Domain)

Let

Theorem 2.1

If

2.1
The Generalized Coincidence Equation

The primary distinction of LQDs from classical QDs is the existence of a metric singularity at
w=0
. We refer to LQDs containing zero as
singular
. We likewise refer to LQDs not containing
0
as
non-singular
. The effect of this singularity is captured by the additional
\frac{q}{w}

for singular LQDs (Equation

[

Theorem 2.2

(
[

If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
is a domain not containing zero, then there exists a unique

\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}\dot{=}h(w)+G(w).

(2.2)

Moreover,
G(w)=C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w)+\frac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w}
for any

C_{\rho}^{U}
denotes the
\rho-

Cauchy transform
of

, which is given by

C_{\rho}^{U}(w)=\int_{U}\dfrac{\rho(\xi)}{w-\xi}dA(\xi).

Obtaining a generalized coincidence equation for singular LQDs is complicated by the fact that the Cauchy kernel
k_{w}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\xi-w}
is no longer an element of the test class
L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
. Hence, the quadrature identity cannot be directly applied to

to obtain the quadrature function in terms of the weighted Cauchy transform. Instead, the modified Cauchy kernel
\frac{\xi}{\xi-w}
, vanishing at
0
is employed. We obtain the following generalization of Theorem
2.2
to singular LQDs.

Theorem 2.3
.

If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
is a domain containing zero, then there exist unique
q\in\mathbb{C}
and
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
such that

\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}\dot{=}h(w)+\dfrac{q}{w}+G(w),

(2.3)

where
G=C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}}
.

In order to proceed with the proof of Theorems
2.2
and
2.3

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
|w|>r>0

C_{\rho_{0}}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w)=\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}-\ln|r|^{2}}{w}

(2.4)

To see this, we express the left side in Cauchy principal value and apply Green’s theorem

\displaystyle C_{\rho_{0}}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w)

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{r<|\xi|<R}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}}{w-\xi}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}+\lim_{R\to\infty}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{R}}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{\xi(w-\xi)}d\xi-\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{r}}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{\xi(w-\xi)}d\xi

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}+\lim_{R\to\infty}\ln|R|^{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{R}}\dfrac{d\xi}{\xi(w-\xi)}d\xi-\ln|r|^{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{r}}\dfrac{d\xi}{\xi(w-\xi)}.

Applying the residue theorem to the latter two terms, we obtain
0
and
-\frac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w}
respectively. The desired formula follows.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Fix
0\notin\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
and
r<d(0,\partial\Omega)
. Note that when
w\in\Omega_{\ast}
, the quadrature identity implies

\displaystyle C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega}(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}}{w-\xi}dA(\xi)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{h(\xi)}{w-\xi}d\xi=h(w).

On the other hand if
w\in\Omega
, then
C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}
is analytic and extends continuously to the boundary (
[
4
]
, Chapter 17). Hence, for each

,

\displaystyle C_{\rho_{0}}^{\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w)=C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w)+C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega}(w)=h(w)+C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w).

Hence, by Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}\dot{=}h(w)+\left(C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w)+\dfrac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w}\right).

Equation
2.2
follows by setting
G
equal to the rightmost term. For uniqueness of
G
: Suppose there existed
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
such that Equation
2.2
holds. Subtracting these equations from each other, we immediately find that
G_{1}\dot{=}G_{2}
. As
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
extend continuously to the boundary, it follows from the maximum modulus principle that
G_{1}=G_{2}
on
\Omega
.
∎

The proof Theorem
2.3
has two steps. In the bounded case, the modified kernel
\frac{\xi}{\xi-w}
directly yields the required decomposition. In the unbounded case, one instead uses a two-point kernel to compensate for the behavior at infinity, and the resulting constant term is again absorbed into the charge
q
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Fix
0\in\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
and
r<d(0,\partial\Omega)
.
We first consider the case in which
\Omega
is bounded. If
w\in\Omega_{\ast}
then, applying the quadrature identity to
\left(\xi\mapsto\frac{\xi}{w-\xi}\right)\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\xi|\xi|^{-2}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\xi h(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi=-wh(w).

On the other hand, if
w\in\Omega
then, by Green’s theorem (
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
by assumption, so its boundary is rectifiable),

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\xi|\xi|^{-2}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\ln|w|^{2}+\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{\xi-w}d\xi=-\ln|w|^{2}+w\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{\xi(\xi-w)}d\xi+\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{\xi}d\xi.

Applying Green’s theorem to the first integral, and splitting the second to isolate the pole at
0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\xi|\xi|^{-2}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\ln|w|^{2}-w\int_{\Omega_{\ast}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}}{\xi-w}d\xi+\left(\oint_{\partial(\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r})}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{\xi}d\xi+\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{r}}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{\xi}d\xi\right)

We recognize the first integral as a weighted Cauchy transform, and note that the second two integrals integrate to a constant depending only on
\Omega
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\xi|\xi|^{-2}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\ln|w|^{2}+wC_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}}(w)+q_{\Omega}.

where
q_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}|\xi|^{-2}dA(\xi)+\ln|r|^{2}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}

\displaystyle\dot{=}h(w)+C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}}(w)+\dfrac{q_{\Omega}}{w}

Finally, we consider the case in which
\Omega
is unbounded. Fix
w_{0}\in\Omega_{\ast}
. If
w\in\Omega_{\ast}
then, applying the quadrature identity to
\frac{\xi}{(w-w_{0})(w-\xi)}\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\xi|\xi|^{-2}}{(\xi-w_{0})(\xi-w)}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\xi h(\xi)}{(\xi-w_{0})(\xi-w)}d\xi=-\dfrac{wh(w)-w_{0}h(w_{0})}{w-w_{0}}.

On the other hand, if
w\in\Omega
then, by Green’s theorem,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\xi|\xi|^{-2}}{(\xi-w_{0})(\xi-w)}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w-w_{0}}+\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{(\xi-w_{0})(\xi-w)}d\xi

\displaystyle=\dfrac{-1}{w-w_{0}}\left(\ln|w|^{2}-w\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{\xi(\xi-w)}d\xi+w_{0}\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{\xi(\xi-w_{0})}d\xi\right)

Applying Green’s theorem to the first integral, and splitting the second to isolate the pole at
0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\xi|\xi|^{-2}}{(\xi-w_{0})(\xi-w)}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{-1}{w-w_{0}}\left(\ln|w|^{2}+w\int_{\Omega_{\ast}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi)+w_{0}\left(\oint_{\partial(\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r})}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}d\xi}{\xi(\xi-w_{0})}+\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{r}}\dfrac{\ln|r|^{2}d\xi}{\xi(\xi-w_{0})}\right)\right)

We recognize the first integral as a weighted Cauchy transform, the second integral is also recognized as a weighted Cauchy transform after applying Green’s theorem, and the third integral may be calculated directly via the residue theorem.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\xi|\xi|^{-2}}{(\xi-w_{0})(\xi-w)}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{-1}{w-w_{0}}\left(\ln|w|^{2}-wC_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}}(w)+w_{0}\left(\int_{\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}}{\xi-w_{0}}dA(\xi)-\dfrac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w_{0}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{-1}{w-w_{0}}\left(\ln|w|^{2}-wC_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}}(w)-w_{0}C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w_{0})+\ln|r|^{2}\right).

Hence for each
w\in\partial\Omega
,

\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}\dot{=}h(w)+C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega_{\ast}}(w)+\dfrac{q_{\Omega,w_{0}}}{w},

where
q_{\Omega,w_{0}}=w_{0}C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w_{0})-w_{0}h(w_{0})+\ln|r|^{2}
. For the uniqueness of
G
and
q
, suppose there existed two pairs
G_{1},q_{1}
and
G_{2},q_{2}
for which Equation
2.3
holds. Subtracting these from each other, and multiplying through by
w
, we find that
wG_{1}(w)+q_{1}\dot{=}wG_{2}(w)+q_{2}
. Both sides of the equation are analytic in
\Omega
and extend continuously to the boundary, it follows from the identity theorem that
wG_{1}(w)+q_{1}=wG_{2}(w)+q_{2}
on
\Omega
. Plugging in
w=0
, we find that
q_{1}=q_{2}
, from which it follows that
G_{1}=G_{2}
on
\Omega
.
∎

With the generalized coincidence equation established, our next goal is to characterize the conditions under which the quadrature function
h
associated to a given LQD is unique. In particular, we show that
h
is unique outright when
0\notin\Omega
, and unique modulo an additional point-charge term
q/w
when
0\in\Omega
. Next, we recover an analogue of the Schwarz function for LQDs (Lemma
1.1
): we introduce a
generalized Schwarz function

S_{0}
whose boundary values satisfy
S_{0}(w)\dot{=}\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}
, and show that
\Omega
is an LQD if and only if such an
S_{0}
exists. We then record three further consequences that will be used repeatedly later: a Sakai-type boundary regularity theorem (only finitely many singular boundary points, each a cusp or double point), an electrostatic characterization of LQDs, and basic invariance properties of the class under scaling, inversion, and power maps.

2.2
Essential Uniqueness of the Quadrature Function

A direct consequence of Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
is the essential uniqueness of the quadrature function associated to a given LQD. For non-singular LQDs, we obtain:

Corollary 2.1
.

If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
is non-singular, then
h
is unique.

Proof.

Suppose
0\notin\Omega
is an LQD with two quadrature functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
(that is,
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h_{1})\cap\text{QD}_{0}(h_{2})
). Then,
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
each satisfy Equation
2.2
for the same
G
, as
G
is independent of
h
. Subtracting these equations, we obtain
h_{1}\dot{=}h_{2}
. It follows from the identity theorem that
h_{1}=h_{2}
.
∎

On the other hand, if
0\in\Omega
, then we only obtain uniqueness up to a one-parameter family of quadrature functions.

Corollary 2.2
.

If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
is a domain containing zero, then
h
is unique modulo the addition of a point charge
\frac{q}{w}
at the origin. In particular,
\left\{\widetilde{h}:\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(\widetilde{h})\right\}=\left\{h(w)+\frac{q}{w}:q\in\mathbb{C}\right\}
.

Proof.

Suppose
0\in\Omega
is an LQD with two quadrature functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
(that is,
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h_{1})\cap\text{QD}_{0}(h_{2})
). Then,
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
each satisfy Equation
2.3
. Noting that
G
is independent of
h
(it depends only on the domain), we obtain
\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}\dot{=}h_{j}(w)+\frac{q_{j}}{w}+G(w)
(
q_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
,
j\in\{1,2\}
). Subtracting these equations and applying the identity theorem yields
h_{1}(w)-h_{2}(w)=\frac{q_{2}-q_{1}}{w}
for all
w\in\mathbb{C}
. In particular, there exists
q\in\mathbb{C}
such that
h_{2}(w)=h_{1}(w)+\frac{q}{w}
.

On the other hand, if
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
, then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(h(w)+\frac{q}{w}\right)
for all
q\in\mathbb{C}
by inspection of the quadrature identity, using the fact that
f\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})\Rightarrow f(0)=0
.
∎

Considering Theorem
2.3
together with Corollary
2.2
, we arrive at an apparent paradox: The former asserts that
q
(in Equation
2.3
) is uniquely associated to
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
, whereas the latter asserts that
h(w)+\frac{q}{w}
is also a quadrature function for
\Omega
for any
q\in\mathbb{C}
. What is happening here? The root of the problem is that
q
(in Equation
2.3
) depends on both
\Omega
and
h
. Hence we must instead consider the sum
h(w)+\frac{q}{w}
which is, indeed uniquely associated to
\Omega
(this follows from essentially the same argument as in the proof of Corollary
2.2
). Now consider the decomposition

h(w)+\frac{q}{w}=\left(h(w)-\dfrac{\underset{0}{\mathrm{Res}\;}h}{w}\right)+\frac{q+\underset{0}{\mathrm{Res}\;}h}{w},

where the first term is the part which is residue-free at
0
, and the second captures the residue at
0
. It follows that the second term is, in fact, independent of
h
. This motivates the introduction of the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

Fix
q\in\mathbb{C}
and
h
rational such that
\underset{0}{\mathrm{Res}\;}h=0
. We denote by
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h;q)
that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
, and Equation
2.3
is satisfied with this value of
q
.

It is immediate that if
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h;q)
, then the pair
(h,q)
is uniquely associated to
\Omega
. From the perspective of potential theory,
q
can be interpreted as the charge at
0
.

2.3
The Generalized Schwarz Function and Boundary Regularity

Recall that quadrature domains may be equivalently characterized as those domains which admit a Schwarz function (Lemma
1.1
). The coincidence equations suggest that the correct analogue of the classical Schwarz function should have boundary values
\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}
. We now show that this is indeed the right notion. In particular, we show that a domain
\Omega
is an LQD if and only if it admits a
generalized Schwarz function
: A function
S_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
extending continuously to
\partial\Omega
such that
S_{0}(w)\dot{=}\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}
.

It is important to note that
S_{0}
always has finitely many poles
- a fact which is clear when
\Omega
is bounded. When
\Omega
is unbounded, this follows from the fact that
S_{0}
is meromorphic at
\infty
, which means that the singularity at infinity (if any) is isolated.

Theorem 2.4
.

If
\Omega
is a domain with
0,\infty\notin\partial\Omega
, then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
if and only if it admits a generalized Schwarz function.

In this case, the generalized Schwarz function
S_{0}
is given by the RHS of Equation
2.2
when
0\notin\Omega
and RHS of Equation
2.3
when
0\in\Omega
. In order to complete the proof of Theorem
2.4
, we will need the following regularity result:

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\Omega
admits a generalized Schwarz function, then
\partial\Omega
has finitely many singular points, and each is a cusp or a double point.

Proof.

Let
S_{0}
be a generalized Schwarz function for
\Omega
. Define

S(w):=\dfrac{e^{wS_{0}(w)}}{w}.

For
w\in\partial\Omega
, the boundary condition for
S_{0}
gives

S(w)\dot{=}\dfrac{e^{\ln|w|^{2}}}{w}=\dfrac{|w|^{2}}{w}=\overline{w}.

Since
S_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
and is continuous up to
\partial\Omega
, its poles are finite in number and bounded away from
\partial\Omega
. Choose
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small that
S_{0}
is holomorphic in

N_{\epsilon}=\{w\in\Omega:d(w,\partial\Omega)<\epsilon\},

and
d(0,\partial\Omega)>\epsilon
. Then
S
is holomorphic on
N_{\epsilon}\cap\text{Cl}(\Omega)
with boundary values
S(w)\dot{=}\overline{w}
. Thus
S
is a local Schwarz function at every boundary point of
\Omega
. Sakai’s regularity theorem (
1.1
) then implies that
\partial\Omega
has finitely many singular points, each a cusp or a double point (
\partial\Omega
has no degenerate points on account of the assumption that
\Omega=\text{Int}(\text{Cl}(\Omega))
). The finiteness of the singular set follows exactly as in the classical quadrature-domain case (§3.2 of
[
13
]
).
∎

With this regularity result in hand, we are now prepared to prove Theorem
2.4
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Suppose
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
. Then by Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
,
\Omega
satisfies Equation
2.3
(with
q=0
when
0\notin\Omega
). Set
S_{0}
equal to the RHS of the equation:
S_{0}(w):=h(w)+qw^{-1}+G(w)
. As
S_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
and extends continuously to the boundary (
0\notin\partial\Omega
by assumption), we find that
S_{0}(w)\dot{=}\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}
is a generalized Schwarz function for
\Omega
.

On the other hand, suppose
\Omega
admits a generalized Schwarz function
S_{0}
. By Lemma
2.2
, we know that
\partial\Omega
is piecewise
C^{1}
, so Green’s theorem applies. Hence, for each
f\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)S_{0}(w)dw.

Since
S_{0}
has finitely many poles in
\Omega
, let
h
be the rational function obtained by summing the principal parts of
S_{0}
at those poles, normalized so that
h\in\mathrm{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
in the bounded case and
h\in\mathrm{Rat}(\Omega)
in the unbounded case. Then

G=S_{0}-h

is holomorphic in
\Omega
and extends continuously to
\partial\Omega
. Hence,

\int_{\partial\Omega}f(w)G(w)dw=0

by Cauchy’s theorem. It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw,

so
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
.
∎

An immediate consequence of Theorem
2.4
and Lemma
2.2
is the following boundary regularity result for LQDs:

Theorem 2.5
.

If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
then
\partial\Omega
has finitely many singular points, and each is a cusp or a double point. In particular,
\partial\Omega
is piecewise
C^{1}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.4
,
\Omega
has a generalized Schwarz function. The conclusion follows by Lemma
2.2
.
∎

2.4
LQDs: An Electrostatic Perspective

LQDs have a natural physical interpretation from the perspective of electrostatics. In particular,
a domain
\Omega
is an LQD if and only if there is a finite configuration of point charges (and multipoles) on the interior of
\Omega
whose electrostatic field in
\Omega^{c}
is equal to that of a charge density
\rho_{0}
on
\Omega
. More formally,

Theorem 2.6
.

Fix a domain
\Omega
such that
0,\infty\notin\partial\Omega
, and
r\in(0,d(0,\partial\Omega))
. Then,

1.

If
0\notin\Omega
then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
if and only if
C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega}(w)=h(w)
on
\Omega_{\ast}
;

2.

If
0\in\Omega
then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h;q)
if and only if
C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w)+\frac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w}=h(w)+\frac{q}{w}
on
\Omega_{\ast}
.

See Appendix
9.1
for the proof.

For example, when
0\notin\Omega
,
\overline{C_{\rho_{0}}^{\Omega}}
is precisely the electrostatic field due to the charge density
\rho_{0}
on
\Omega
, and
\overline{h}
is the electrostatic field due to a collection of point charges (and multipoles) at the poles of
h
. This phenomenon is illustrated in Figure

.

2.5
Invariance Properties

The class of log-weighted quadrature domains has a number of invariance properties. In particular, if
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, then

1.

Scale invariance (§
2.5.1
):
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}

\iff

a^{-1}\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
,

2.

Inversion invariance (§
2.5.2
):
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}

\iff

\Omega^{-1}\in\text{QD}_{0}
,
2
2

2

Where
\Omega^{-1}=\{w^{-1}\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}:w\in\Omega\}
.

3.

Power invariance (§
2.5.3
):
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}

\iff

\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}\in\text{QD}_{0}
, (assuming
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
is connected),

where
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}:=\{w\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}:w^{k}\in\Omega\}
is the full
k
th root preimage of
\Omega
. The specific relationships between the quadrature functions of these domains and their transformed counterparts are given in the §
2.5.1
-
2.5.3
. Some of these properties are genuinely new while others are, in some sense, “inherited” from invariance properties of classical quadrature domains via Theorem
2.1
. For example, the scale-invariance property of
\text{QD}_{0}
can be interpreted as a consequence of the translation invariance of
QD
on account of the fact that the exponential map takes addition to multiplication. Of course, LQDs are not generally translation invariant, as this is not a symmetry of the underlying weight
\rho_{0}(w)=|w|^{-2}
.

We will begin by covering scale invariance and its implications for “traveling wave” families of LQDs.

2.5.1
Scaling Law and Traveling Waves

Fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
, and note that
f\in L_{a}^{1}(a^{-1}\Omega;\rho_{0})

\implies

f(a^{-1}w)\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
. Applying the quadrature identity, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{a^{-1}\Omega}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(a^{-1}w)}{|a^{-1}w|^{2}}|a|^{-2}dA(w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(a^{-1}w)h(w)dw=\oint_{\partial a^{-1}\Omega}f(w)h(aw)adw.

Hence,
a^{-1}\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h(aw)a)
. Carrying out the same argument with
a^{-1}
, we conclude that

Theorem 2.7
.

For each
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and domain
\Omega
,
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
if and only if
a^{-1}\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h(aw)a)
.

We say that a family
\{\Omega_{t}\}_{t}\subseteq\text{QD}_{0}(h)
is a traveling wave if there exists
t_{0}>0
and a smooth increasing function
\alpha(t)
such that
\alpha(\infty)=\infty
and for each
t>t_{0}
,
\Omega_{t}=\alpha(t)\Omega_{t_{0}}
.

Corollary 2.3
.

If
\{\Omega_{t}\}_{0<t<\infty}\subseteq\text{QD}_{0}(h)
is a traveling wave, then it consists of either disks or exterior disks centered at the origin.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.7
,
\Omega_{t_{0}}\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)\cap\text{QD}_{0}(h(\alpha(t)w)\alpha(t))
. Then by Corollaries
2.1
and
2.2
, either

1.

h(w)=h(\alpha(t)w)\alpha(t)
, when
0\notin\Omega
,

2.

h(w)=h(\alpha(t)w)\alpha(t)+\frac{q}{w}
for some
q\in\mathbb{C}
, when
0\in\Omega
.

Applying the assumption that
h
is rational, and expanding each functional equation in a Laurent series about
w=0
, we find that
h(w)=\frac{c}{w}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
.

In the first case, we find that
c=0
because
h\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{\ast})
and
0\in\Omega_{\ast}
. Hence,
\Omega_{t}\in\text{QD}_{0}(0)
for all
t>t_{0}
. By Theorem
3.1
, each
\Omega_{t}
must be an exterior disk centered at the origin. Similarly, in the second case, we find that
\Omega_{t}\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{c}{w}\right)
. By Corollary
2.2
we conclude that
\Omega_{t}\in\text{QD}_{0}(0)
. Again by Theorem
3.1
, each
\Omega_{t}
must be a disk centered at the origin.
∎

2.5.2
Invariance Under Inversion

Another interesting property of the class of LQDs is their invariance under inversion. This is essentially a consequence of the fact that
(w^{-1})^{\ast}\frac{dA(w)}{|w|^{2}}=\frac{|-w^{-2}|^{2}dA(w)}{|w^{-1}|^{2}}=\frac{dA(w)}{|w|^{2}}
, where
(w^{-1})^{\ast}
is the pullback by
w\mapsto w^{-1}
. The following theorem is a direct generalization of the Graven & Makarov’s
[
8
]
result for non-singular LQDS.

Theorem 2.8
.

If
\Omega\subseteq\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
if and only if
\Omega^{-1}\in\text{QD}_{0}(-h(w^{-1})w^{-2})
.

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
then
f\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega^{-1};\rho_{0})

\implies

f(w^{-1})\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
. Taking the following integral, inverting, applying the quadrature identity, and inverting back, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{-1}}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w^{-1})}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w^{-1})h(w)dw

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega^{-1}}f(w)(-h(w^{-1})w^{-2})dw

so
\Omega^{-1}\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(-h(w^{-1})w^{-2}\right)
. This implies the backwards direction as well because
\widetilde{h}(w)=-h(w^{-1})w^{-2}

\implies

-\widetilde{h}(w^{-1})w^{-2}=h(w)
.
∎

When
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h;q)
is singular and unbounded (in which case
\Omega^{-1}\in\text{QD}_{0}(\widetilde{h},\widetilde{q})
is likewise singular and unbounded), we obtain a direct relation between
q
and
\widetilde{q}
in terms of the
\rho_{0}-
weighted area of
\Omega_{\ast}
,
A_{\rho_{0}}(\Omega_{\ast})=\int_{\Omega_{\ast}}|\xi|^{-2}dA(\xi)
.

Corollary 2.4
.

If
\Omega\subseteq\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
contains both
0
and
\infty
, then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h;q)
if and only if
\Omega^{-1}\in\text{QD}_{0}(-h(w^{-1})w^{-2};-q-A_{\rho_{0}}(\Omega_{\ast}))
.

Proof.

That
q\mapsto-q
, follows by substituting
w\mapsto w^{-1}
in Equation
2.3
, and multiplying through by
-w^{-2}
to obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}

\displaystyle\dot{=}-h(w^{-1})w^{-2}-\dfrac{q}{w}-\dfrac{G(w^{-1})}{w^{2}}.

Note that
\frac{G(w^{-1})}{w^{2}}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega^{-1})
, unless
\infty\in\Omega
. In this case,
0\in\Omega^{-1}
, and
G(\infty)=0
, so

-\dfrac{G(w^{-1})}{w^{2}}=\dfrac{\underset{\infty}{\mathrm{Res}\;}G}{w}-\dfrac{G(w^{-1})+w\underset{\infty}{\mathrm{Res}\;}G}{w^{2}}=\dfrac{\underset{\infty}{\mathrm{Res}\;}G}{w}+\widetilde{G}(w),

where
\widetilde{G}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega^{-1})
. Finally, note that

\displaystyle G(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\ast}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}dA(\xi)}{w-\xi}=\dfrac{1}{w}\int_{\Omega_{\ast}}|\xi|^{-2}dA(\xi)+O(w^{-2}),

so
\underset{\infty}{\mathrm{Res}\;}G=-A_{\rho_{0}}(\Omega_{\ast})
.
∎

An important consequence of inversion invariance is that it gives a bijective correspondence between bounded singular LQDs and unbounded non-singular LQDs. Hence, we can study one family by considering corresponding domains in the other.

2.5.3
The Power Map

Considering that
\ln|w^{k}|=k\ln|w|
, another natural transformation to investigate is the power map
w\mapsto w^{k}
. For a domain
\Omega\subseteq\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, we therefore introduce its full
k
th root preimage,
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}:=\{w\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}:w^{k}\in\Omega\}
, which is
k-
fold rotationally symmetric. The theorem below shows that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
if and only if
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
is a disjoint union of LQDs (if
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
is connected, then it is a disjoint union of one LQD). The key point is that the weight
\rho_{0}(w)=|w|^{-2}
interacts especially well with the change of variables induced by
w\mapsto w^{k}
, so the log-weighted quadrature identity is preserved. In particular, this result gives a systematic way to generate symmetric examples and, conversely, to represent symmetric LQDs via simpler ones.

Theorem 2.9
.

Fix a domain
\Omega
, and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}

\iff

\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}=\bigsqcup_{j}\Omega_{j}
, where
\Omega_{j}\in\text{QD}_{0}
. In this case, there exist rational functions
h
and
\{h_{j}\}_{j}
such that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
,
\Omega_{j}\in\text{QD}_{0}(h_{j})
, and
\sum_{j}h_{j}=\frac{1}{k}w^{k-1}h(w^{k})
.

When
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
is connected, Theorem
2.9
reduces to the statement
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)

\iff

\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{1}{k}w^{k-1}h(w^{k})\right)
.
Before proceeding with the proof, we will need the following lemma (proof in Appendix
9.3
) which asserts a that
k-
fold rotational symmetry of an LQD implies a certain symmetry of its quadrature function.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\Omega=\bigsqcup_{j}\Omega_{j}
, where
\Omega_{j}\in\text{QD}_{0}(h_{j})
, is a
k-
fold rotationally symmetric disjoint finite union of LQDs, then there exists a rational function
g
such that
\sum_{j}h_{j}(w)=:h(w)=w^{k-1}g(w^{k})
.

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

For the forward direction, we suppose that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
. By Theorem
2.4
,
\Omega
admits a generalized Schwarz function
S_{0}(w)\dot{=}\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}
. We then consider
w\in\partial\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
(
\implies w^{k}\in\partial\Omega
), so

\widetilde{S_{0}}(w):=\frac{1}{k}w^{k-1}S_{0}(w^{k})=\frac{1}{k}w^{k-1}\frac{\ln|w^{k}|^{2}}{w^{k}}=\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}.

Fix a connected component
\Omega_{j}\subseteq\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
and note that at each
w\in\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}\supseteq\Omega_{j}
(
\implies w^{k}\in\Omega
),
\widetilde{S_{0}}
is meromorphic in
\Omega_{j}
as the product and composition of a meromorphic function with a polynomial. It also inherits continuity up to the boundary from
S_{0}
. Hence,
\widetilde{S_{0}}
is a generalized Schwarz function for each connected component
\Omega_{j}
of
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
, and we conclude that
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
is a disjoint union of LQDs.

For the reverse direction, suppose that
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}=\bigsqcup_{j}\Omega_{j}
, where
\Omega_{j}\in\text{QD}_{0}(h_{j})
. As
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
is
k-
fold symmetric about the origin, Lemma
2.3
implies that there exists a rational function
g
such that

\sum_{j}h_{j}(w)=:h(w)=\frac{1}{k}w^{k-1}g(w^{k}).

If
w^{k}\in\Omega
, that means that there exists
z\in\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
such that
w^{k}=z^{k}
. In particular,
e^{2\pi i\frac{j}{k}}w\in\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. As
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
is
k-
fold rotationally symmetric, this means that
e^{2\pi i\frac{j}{k}}w\in\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
for every
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. In particular,
\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
is a
k-
to
-1
cover of
\Omega
under the map
w\mapsto w^{k}
(and hence on the boundary as well). Fix
f\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
, so that
f(w^{k})\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}};\rho_{0})
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{k}\int_{\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}}\dfrac{f(w^{k})}{|w^{k}|^{2}}k^{2}|w|^{2(k-1)}dA(w)=k\sum_{j}\int_{\Omega_{j}}\dfrac{f(w^{k})}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)=k\sum_{j}\oint_{\partial\Omega_{j}}f(w^{k})h_{j}(w)dw

As each
h_{j}
is residue-free in
\Omega_{j}^{c}
, we may deform each contour
\partial\Omega_{j}
to the contour
\partial\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}
without changing the value of the integral. Doing this, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=k\oint_{\partial\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}}f(w^{k})\sum_{j}h_{j}(w)dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega^{\frac{1}{k}}}f(w^{k})w^{k-1}g(w^{k})dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)g(w)dw.

That is,
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(g)
. The desired conclusion follows.
∎

3
The Inverse and Direct Problems for LQDs

The general results of §2 make it possible to begin addressing the inverse and direct problems for log-weighted quadrature domains in explicit families. The aim of the present section is to analyze the simplest such families, both in order to obtain concrete classification theorems and to illustrate the new geometric features introduced by the singular weight
\rho_{0}(w)=|w|^{-2}
. The null case,
\text{QD}_{0}(0)
, provides the most basic test of the theory: the singularity at the origin induces an additional family of null LQDs.

3.1
Classification of Null LQDs

An LQD
\Omega
is referred to as a
null LQD
if
0
is its quadrature function:
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(0)
. We prove the following classification theorem for null LQDs.

Theorem 3.1
.

A domain is a null LQD if and only if it is a disk or exterior disk centered at the origin.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(0)
. We first note that
\Omega
must contain at least one of
0,\infty
because otherwise
1\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
and

0<\int_{\Omega}|w|^{-2}dA(w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}1\cdot 0dw=0.

Then, by Equations
2.2
and
2.3
, we find that
\ln|w|^{2}\dot{=}wG(w)+q
for some
q\in\mathbb{C}
(
q=0
if
0\notin\Omega
) and
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
.

If
\Omega
is unbounded then
G\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
and there exists
q^{\prime}
,
\widetilde{G}\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

\ln\left|we^{-q^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\dot{=}\widetilde{G}(w)\;\;\;\;\implies\;\;\;\;\left|we^{-q^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\dot{=}e^{\widetilde{G}(w)},

where
e^{\widetilde{G}(w)}-1\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
. Hence rearranging the above equation we obtain
U\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
and
A\in\mathbb{C}
for which
|w|^{2}\dot{=}U(w)+A
. Thus if
f\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
,

\int_{\Omega}f(w)dA(w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\overline{w}dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\dfrac{U(w)+A}{w}dw.

If
0\notin\Omega
, then this equals
0
. On the other hand, if
0\in\Omega
, then we obtain
f(0)\left(U(0)+A\right)
. In particular,
\Omega
is either a (classical) null quadrature domain or a (classical) one-point quadrature domain with its quadrature node at
w=0
.

Aharonov & Shapiro
[
2
]
showed that a finitely connected bounded domain is a one-point QD if and only if it is a disk. When combined with Lee & Makarov’s
[
13
]
topological bounds for QDs, we are able to drop the assumption of finite-connectedness. Hence, if
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(0)
is bounded, it is a disk centered at
0
.

On the other hand, Sakai
[
16
]
showed that a domain with compact boundary is a null QD with respect to the test class
L_{a}^{1}(\Omega)
if and only if it is an exterior disk or an exterior ellipse. However, when considered with respect to the larger test class
A_{0}(\Omega)
, only exterior disks are null (that ellipses are not follows from direct computation). Hence, if
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(0)
is unbounded, it is an exterior disk centered at
0
.

For the reverse direction, take
r>0
and
f\in L_{a}^{1}(\mathbb{D}_{r};\rho_{0})
, which implies
f(0)=0
. Integrating against
\rho_{0}
and changing to polar coordinates, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}_{r}}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{r}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\dfrac{f(sw)}{sw}dwds=0

The last equality follows from the fact that
f(0)=0
, so
\frac{f(sw)}{sw}
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}
. The argument for
(\mathbb{D}_{r})_{\ast}
is entirely analogous.
∎

3.2
Partial Classification of Non-Singular One-Point LQDs

A classic result due to Aharonov & Shapiro
[
2
]
states that a finitely connected bounded domain is a one-point classical QD if and only if it is a disk. We prove the following analog of this result for LQDs: A simply connected bounded domain not containing zero is a one-point LQD if and only if it is biholomorphic to a disk via the exponential map
w\mapsto e^{w}
. In particular, we show the following:

Theorem 3.2
.

Fix
\alpha,w_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. If
\Omega
is a simply connected bounded domain not containing zero, then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-w_{0}}\right)
if and only if
0<\alpha\leq\pi^{2}
and
\Omega=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|\ln(ww_{0}^{-1})|^{2}<\alpha\}
.

Moreover,
\partial\Omega
develops a double point at
\alpha=\pi^{2}
. Here and throughout the paper,
\ln
denotes the principal branch of the natural logarithm, with branch cut along the negative real axis.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Beginning with the reverse direction, consider the bounded domain

\Omega=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:\left|\ln(ww_{0}^{-1})\right|^{2}<\alpha\}

for some
w_{0}\neq 0
and
\alpha>0
. To solve the direct problem for
\Omega
, we would like to show that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
and determine its quadrature function. We proceed by direct computation of the quadrature identity. Note that if
w\in\partial\Omega
, then
\left|\ln(ww_{0}^{-1})\right|^{2}=\alpha
, so

\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}=\dfrac{\ln(\overline{ww_{0}^{-1}})+\ln(w\overline{w_{0}})}{w}=\dfrac{1}{w}\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{\ln(ww_{0}^{-1})}+\ln(w\overline{w_{0}})\right)=\dfrac{1}{w}\dfrac{\alpha w_{0}}{w-w_{0}}+G(w)=:S_{0}(w)

for some
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
.
S_{0}
is meromorphic in
\Omega
and extends continuously to the boundary with boundary values
\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}
. Hence
S_{0}
is a generalized Schwarz function for
\Omega
so,
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
by Theorem
2.4
.

We will now compute the associated quadrature function
h
directly. Note that
0\notin\Omega
so, by Green’s theorem and Cauchy’s integral theorem, for each
f\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}dw

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\left(\dfrac{1}{w}\dfrac{\alpha w_{0}}{w-w_{0}}+G(w)\right)dw

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\dfrac{\alpha}{w-w_{0}}dw+\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\left(G(w)-\dfrac{\alpha}{w}\right)dw

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\dfrac{\alpha}{w-w_{0}}dw.

Hence,
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-w_{0}}\right)
.

For the forward direction, suppose that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-w_{0}}\right)
for some
\alpha,w_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and that
\Omega
is a bounded simply connected domain not containing zero. We will show that
\alpha>0
and
\partial\Omega=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|\ln(ww_{0}^{-1})|^{2}=\alpha\}
. Firstly, the fact that
\alpha>0
follows immediately from applying the quadrature identity to
1\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{0})
:

\displaystyle 0<\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{dA(w)}{|w|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\alpha}{w-w_{0}}dw=\alpha.

By Theorem
2.7
,
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-w_{0}}\right)
if and only if
w_{0}^{-1}\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-1}\right)
. Hence, we can wlog consider the case in which
w_{0}=1
. Next, by Theorem
2.2
, there exists
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
such that
\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}\dot{=}\frac{\alpha}{w-1}+G(w)
. Hence,

\displaystyle\ln|w|^{2}

\displaystyle\dot{=}\dfrac{w\alpha}{w-1}+wG(w)

As
0\notin\Omega
is bounded and simply connected, we know that
\Omega_{\ast}
is connected and contains both
0
and
\infty
. Hence, isolating the simple pole of
\frac{1}{\ln(w)}
at
w=1
, we find that
\frac{w\alpha}{w-1}=\frac{\alpha}{\ln(w)}+\widetilde{G}(w)
for some
\widetilde{G}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
. Therefore

\displaystyle\ln(\overline{w})+\ln(w)

\displaystyle\dot{=}\frac{\alpha}{\ln(w)}+\widetilde{G}(w)+wG(w)

Finally, rearranging, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\ln(w)}

\displaystyle\dot{=}\frac{\alpha}{\ln(w)}+\widetilde{G}(w)+\ln(w)+wG(w).

Hence,
\widehat{G}(w):=\alpha+\ln(w)(\widetilde{G}(w)+\ln(w)+wG(w))
is a function analytic in
\Omega
for which

\displaystyle|\ln(w)|^{2}

\displaystyle\dot{=}\widehat{G}(w).

Therefore
\text{Im}(\widehat{G})=0
so, by the maximum principle,
\widehat{G}
is real-valued and analytic in
\Omega
and hence constant on
\Omega
. As
\text{Re}(\widehat{G})\geq 0
on
\partial\Omega
, we conclude that
\widehat{G}
is a non-negative real constant on
\Omega
. In particular,
\exists c\geq 0
such that
|\ln(w)|^{2}\dot{=}c
. Then, applying the quadrature identity calculation from the first part, we find that
c=\alpha
. In particular, this demonstrates that if
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-1}\right)
, then
\partial\Omega\subseteq\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|\ln(w)|^{2}=\alpha\}=:\Gamma_{\alpha}
.

Finally, we must determine which connected component of
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{c}
is
\Omega
. Note that
1\in\Omega
because
1
is the location of the unique quadrature node. Furthermore,
|\ln(1)|^{2}-\alpha=-\alpha<0
, so
\Omega
must be the connected component of
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{c}
on which
|\ln(w)|^{2}<\alpha
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.2
.
∎

The Riemann map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
associated to
\Omega
such that
\varphi(0)=w_{0}
and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0
is given by
\varphi(z)=w_{0}e^{z\sqrt{\alpha}}
. We note that
\varphi
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
if and only if
0<\alpha\leq\pi^{2}
, which is precisely the set of
\alpha
for which
|\ln(ww_{0}^{-1})|^{2}<\alpha
is simply connected.

4
Simply Connected Log-Weighted Quadrature Domains

This section is primarily concerned with solving the inverse and direct problems for simply connected log-weighted quadrature domains. Recall that the
inverse problem
for log-weighted quadrature domains is concerned with recovering an LQD from its quadrature function. Conversely, the
direct problem
for LQDs is concerned with determining whether a given domain is an LQD and, if so, identifying its quadrature function.

In the simply connected setting, domains are represented by their associated Riemann map. Hence, we may reduce the inverse and direct problems to those of characterizing the Riemann map in terms of the quadrature function and vice-versa. In the case of classical quadrature domains, this scheme is aided by the fact that the associated Riemann map is rational: the inverse and direct problems reduce to the determination of the relationship between the finitely many coefficients of the quadrature function and the finitely many coefficients of the Riemann map.

The situation is more complicated for log-weighted quadrature domains. While the Riemann map associated to a simply connected LQD is not necessarily rational, it turns out that it can still be represented in terms of rational functions. In particular, if
\varphi
is the Riemann map associated to a simply connected domain
\Omega
and has the
inner-outer factorization

\varphi(z)=\varphi_{\rm in}\varphi_{\rm out}
(§
4.1
) then

Theorem 4.1
.

\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
if and only if
\varphi_{\rm out}
extends to the exponential of a rational function.

The proof is in §
4.1
, following a more formal discussion of the inner/outer factorization. As we shall see,
\varphi_{\rm in}
is a product of Blaschke factors, so it is also rational. Combining these facts reduces the inverse and direct problems to the determination of the relationship between the finitely many poles and coefficients of the quadrature function and those of the rational functions comprising the Riemann map.

4.1
Inner/Outer Function Factorization

We use only the simplest form of the inner–outer factorization, namely the case of bounded analytic functions that extend continuously to the boundary and have no boundary zeros. In this setting no singular inner factor appears: the inner part is a finite Blaschke product determined by the zeros, and the outer part is given by the Poisson integral formula. We record the corresponding forms on
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
.

Lemma 4.1

(Inner/Outer Factorization)
.

(a)

Suppose
f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\cap C^{0}(\text{Cl}(\mathbb{D}))
is non-vanishing on
\partial\mathbb{D}
. Then
f
admits a unique factorization
f=f_{\rm in}f_{\rm out}
, where
f_{\rm in}
is the finite Blaschke product consisting of the roots of
f
in
\mathbb{D}
, and

f_{\rm out}(z)=\exp\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\dfrac{\xi+z}{\xi-z}\dfrac{\ln|f(\xi)|^{2}}{\xi}d\xi\right),\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

(4.1)

(b)

Suppose
f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})\cap C^{0}(\text{Cl}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast}))
is non-vanishing on
\partial\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
. Then
f
admits a unique factorization
f(z)=f_{\rm in}f_{\rm out}
, where
f_{\rm in}
is the finite Blaschke product consisting of the roots of
f
in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
, and

f_{\rm out}(z)=\exp\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\dfrac{\xi+z}{\xi-z}\dfrac{\ln|f(\xi)|^{2}}{\xi}d\xi\right),\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}_{\ast}

(4.2)

Part (a) is the standard inner/outer factorization in the absence of boundary zeros, so the singular inner factor is absent, and the inner part reduces to a finite Blaschke product (see
[
15
]
, Theorem 1.12 for a more detailed discussion). Part (b) follows by applying part (a) to the reflection of
f
,
f^{\#}
on
\mathbb{D}
, then reflecting back.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, let

b_{\lambda}(z):=\frac{\overline{\lambda}}{|\lambda|}\frac{z-\lambda}{\overline{\lambda}z-1},

and set
b_{0}(z):=z
. We will use the following elementary properties of
b_{\lambda}
repeatedly:
b_{\lambda}
has a simple root at
\lambda
,
|b_{\lambda}|=1
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
, and
b_{\lambda}^{\#}(z)=b_{\lambda}(z)^{-1}
.

We now consider the consequences of this representation when
f
is a Riemann map. The boundary regularity result established in §
2
means Lemma 4.1 applies directly. In this case, the preceding integral representation can be rewritten in terms of the Faber transform.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
\varphi
is the Riemann map associated to a simply connected domain
\Omega
with compact piecewise
C^{1}
boundary such that
0\notin\partial\Omega
, then
\varphi(z)=\varphi_{\rm in}\varphi_{\rm out}
, where

\varphi_{\rm out}(z)=C\exp\left(\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\ln|w|^{2}\right]\right)^{\#}(z)\right),

4.3

In this section, we provide a classification of simply connected “constant” LQDs:

Fix

Proof.

By Equation

, we know that

, where

is the unique root of

where

5
Monomial Log-Weighted Quadrature Domains

In this section, we consider simply connected LQDs with quadrature function

5.1
Symmetric Non-Singular Monomial LQDs

Suppose that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(\alpha kw^{k-1})

\Omega
is unbounded, Theorem

tells us that the associated Riemann map

\displaystyle r(z)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\alpha kw^{k}\right)(z)-\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\alpha kw^{k}\right)(0).

We recognize

We now consider the implications of the symmetry of the quadrature function. By Theorem

fold rotational symmetry about the origin. It follows via inspection of the Laurent expansion that if a simply connected domain has

\varphi\left(ze^{\frac{2\pi i}{k}}\right)=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{k}}\varphi\left(z\right)

.

\varphi(z)=cze^{\frac{\lambda}{1-z\overline{z_{1}}}},

(6.1)

where
\varphi(z_{1})=w_{0}
, and the coefficients satisfy the relation
\lambda=\frac{\overline{\alpha}\overline{w_{0}}}{\overline{z_{1}}\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(z_{1})}}
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

For the forward direction, suppose that
0\notin\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-1}\right)
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
is unbounded and simply connected with Riemann map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}_{\ast}\rightarrow\Omega
, with
\varphi(z_{1})=1
, and
\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)=c>0
. By Theorem
4.3
,
\varphi(z)=cze^{r^{\#}(z)}
, where
r(z)=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\frac{\alpha w}{w-1}\right)(z)-\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\frac{\alpha w}{w-1}\right)(0)
. Computing the Faber transform via Equation
8.10
, we find

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\dfrac{\alpha w}{w-1}\right)(z)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\alpha\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{w-1}\right)(z)=\alpha\left(1+\dfrac{\psi^{\prime}(1)}{z-\psi(1)}\right)

so
r(z)=\frac{\alpha}{z_{1}\varphi^{\prime}(z_{1})}\frac{z}{z-z_{1}}
, and we recover Equation
6.1
by setting
\lambda=\frac{\overline{\alpha}}{\overline{z_{1}}\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(z_{1})}}
. Also,
z_{1}\in\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
because otherwise
\varphi
wouldn’t be analytic.

For the reverse direction, suppose that there exist
c>0
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, and
z_{1}\in\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
for which
\Omega=\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
, where
\varphi
is univalent and given by Equation
6.1
. Then, by Theorem
4.1
,
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
for some
h
. We will now compute
h
using Theorem
4.3
, which tells us that there exists
C\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle h(w)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\frac{\overline{\lambda}z}{z-z_{1}}\right)(w)-C}{w}=\dfrac{z_{1}\overline{\lambda}}{w}\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{z-z_{1}}\right)(w)+\dfrac{\overline{\lambda}-C}{w}.

Computing the Faber transform via Equation
8.10
and substituting the formula for
\lambda
, we find

\displaystyle h(w)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{z_{1}\overline{\lambda}}{w}\dfrac{\varphi^{\prime}(z_{1})}{w-\varphi(z_{1})}+\dfrac{\overline{\lambda}-C}{w}=\dfrac{\alpha}{w-1}+\dfrac{\overline{\lambda}-C-\alpha}{w}.

Finally, as
h\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{\ast})
and
0\in\Omega_{\ast}
(
\varphi(z)=0
has no solutions in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
, so
0\notin\Omega
), we find that
C=\overline{\lambda}-\alpha
, so
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-1}\right)
.
∎

6.2
Singular One-Point LQDs

We now turn our consideration to the more exotic case of singular one-point LQDs. In particular, we consider simply connected domains
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-w_{0}}\right)
which contain zero (recall that we can wlog take
w_{0}=1
).

Singular Bounded One-Point LQDs

Theorem 6.2
.

Take
w_{0},\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. There exists a bounded simply connected domain
\Omega

It remains to show the formula for

6.2.1.1  Univalence criteria

We would like to characterize the set of values of
z_{0}
and
\lambda
for which
\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{|z_{0}|}b_{z_{0}}(z)e^{\lambda z}
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
. Notice that if we set
f(z):=\varphi\left(\frac{|\lambda|}{\lambda}z\right)=\frac{1}{|z_{1}|}b_{z_{1}}(z)e^{|\lambda|z}
, where
z_{1}=\frac{\lambda}{|\lambda|}z_{0}
, then
\varphi
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
if and only if
f
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
. In particular, this shows that it is sufficient to consider
\varphi
for
\lambda>0
.

Note that
|z_{1}|<1
is necessary because otherwise
f
is not analytic in
\mathbb{D}
. This is also a sufficient condition for
f
to be analytic in
\mathbb{D}
. Hence, it is necessary and sufficient that
|z_{1}|<1
and
f(\partial\mathbb{D})
has no self-intersections. Suppose there existed
z\neq w\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
f(z)=f(w)
. Taking the modulus of both sides and recalling that
|b_{z_{1}}(z)|=1
, we find
e^{|\lambda|\text{Re}(z)}=e^{|\lambda|\text{Re}(w)}
, which implies
\text{Re}(z)=\text{Re}(w)
. It follows that
w=z^{-1}
, so there exist
z\neq w\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
f(z)=f(w)
if and only if there exists
z\in\partial\mathbb{D}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Im}(z)>0\}
such that
f(z)=f(z^{-1})
. Rearranging, we find
f
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
if and only if the following equation has no solutions
z\in\partial\mathbb{D}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Im}(z)>0\}
.

\displaystyle e^{2i|\lambda|\text{Im}(z)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(zz_{1}-1)(z\overline{z_{1}}-1)}{(z-\overline{z_{1}})(z-z_{1})}.

Note that
\frac{(zz_{1}-1)(z\overline{z_{1}}-1)}{(z-\overline{z_{1}})(z-z_{1})}=\frac{\left(\overline{z^{-1}(z-\overline{z_{1}})(z-z_{1})}\right)}{z^{-1}(z-\overline{z_{1}})(z-z_{1})}=e^{-2i\text{Arg}(z^{-1}(z-z_{1})(z-\overline{z_{1}}))}
. Hence, setting
z=e^{i\theta}
(
0<\theta<\pi
), and
z_{1}=\frac{\lambda}{|\lambda|}z_{0}=|z_{0}|e^{i(\text{Arg}(z_{0})+\text{Arg}(\lambda))}
, we find
e^{2i(|\lambda|\sin(\theta)+\delta(\theta))}=1
, where

\delta(\theta)=\text{Arg}\left((1+|z_{0}|^{2})\cos(\theta)-2|z_{0}|\cos(\text{Arg}(z_{0})+\text{Arg}(\lambda))+i(1-|z_{0}|^{2})\sin(\theta)\right)

(6.3)

As
\delta(\theta)\in(0,\pi)
and
|\lambda|\sin(\theta)\in(0,|\lambda|)
when
0<\theta<\pi
, we find that if there is a solution to
e^{2i(|\lambda|\sin(\theta)+\delta(\theta))}=1
, there must be a solution such that
|\lambda|\sin(\theta)+\delta(\theta)=\pi
. Hence, if we set

\lambda_{\max}=\min_{0<\theta<\pi}\dfrac{\pi-\delta(\theta)}{\sin(\theta)},

(6.4)

then
f
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
if and only if
0<|\lambda|\leq\lambda_{\max}
. In particular, we have shown that

Corollary 6.1
.

\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
from Equation
6.2
is univalent if and only if
|z_{0}|<1
, and
0<|\lambda|\leq\lambda_{\max}
.

Example: A Family of Two-Point LQDs

In this section, we consider a doubly symmetric family of simply connected LQDs with quadrature function

for

Theorem 6.4
.

Fix
\alpha>0
and

. If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-1}+\frac{\alpha}{w+1};q\right)
is a simply connected singular LQD symmetric about the real and imaginary axes, then
\Omega=\varphi(\mathbb{D})

\varphi(z)=\dfrac{z}{z_{+}}e^{\lambda\frac{z^{2}-z_{+}^{2}}{(zz_{+})^{2}-1}}.

(6.6)

Moreover,

,

Proof of Theorem
6.4
.

Suppose that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-1}+\frac{\alpha}{w+1}\right)
for some
\alpha>0
. If the Riemann map associated to
\Omega
,
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
takes

\varphi(z)=cze^{r^{\#}(z)}
, where

\varphi(z_{\pm})=\pm 1

. Applying the Faber transform formulae of Equation
8.10
, we find

\displaystyle r(z)

\displaystyle=\alpha\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{w-1}\right)(z)-\alpha\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{w+1}\right)(z)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha}{\varphi^{\prime}(z_{+})}\dfrac{1}{z-z_{+}}-\dfrac{\alpha}{\varphi^{\prime}(z_{-})}\dfrac{1}{z-z_{-}}.

Assuming the domain is symmetric about the real and imaginary axes, we find that
\varphi(-z)=-\varphi(z)
,
\varphi^{\prime}(-z)=\varphi^{\prime}(z)
, and

, so this simplifies to

, and we obtain

\varphi(z)=cze^{\frac{2z_{+}\alpha}{\varphi^{\prime}(z_{+})}\frac{z^{2}}{1-z^{2}z_{+}^{2}}}.

(6.7)

Using the relations for
\varphi(z_{+})
and
\varphi^{\prime}(z_{+})
, we find that

\displaystyle\varphi(z)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{z}{z_{+}}e^{\frac{\ln(cz_{+})}{z_{+}^{2}}\frac{z^{2}-z_{+}^{2}}{(zz_{+})^{2}-1}}.

We now compute

- that is, we know

, and we would like to determine a relationship between the coefficients of
\varphi
and the charge

at

. We compute the Schwarz function,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}

\displaystyle\dot{=}\dfrac{\ln(\varphi\varphi^{\#})\circ\psi(w)}{w}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\dfrac{\ln(cz_{+})}{z_{+}^{2}}\left(\frac{z^{2}-z_{+}^{2}}{(zz_{+})^{2}-1}+\frac{1-(zz_{+})^{2}}{z_{+}^{2}-z^{2}}\right)\circ\psi(w)-\ln(z_{+}^{2})}{w}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ln\left(\frac{wz_{+}}{\psi(w)}\right)+\left(\frac{\ln(cz_{+})}{z_{+}^{2}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\ln\left(\frac{wz_{+}}{\psi(w)}\right)}}{w}-\dfrac{\ln(z_{+}^{2})}{w}

By L’Hopital’s rule,

\lim_{w\to 0}\dfrac{wz_{+}}{\psi(w)}=\dfrac{z_{+}}{\psi^{\prime}(0)}=z_{+}\varphi^{\prime}(0)=cz_{+}.

So, expanding about

\displaystyle\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}

\displaystyle\dot{=}\dfrac{\ln(cz_{+})(z_{+}^{-4}+1)-\ln(z_{+}^{2})}{w}+O(w)

Hence,

. Setting

, we recover Equation
6.6
. Finally, combining Equations
6.7
and

in the theorem.
∎

7
Conclusion

This paper develops the theory of log-weighted quadrature domains - plane domains satisfying a quadrature identity with respect to the weight
\rho_{0}(w)=|w|^{-2}
. The presence of the singularity at the origin leads to several new features absent from the classical theory. Principal among these is the fact that, when
0\in\Omega
, the quadrature function is no longer uniquely determined by the domain, but only up to the addition of a point charge at the origin. At the structural level, the paper establishes a generalized Schwarz function characterization of LQDs (Theorem
2.4
) and derives a corresponding boundary regularity result (Theorem
2.5
), parallel to the classical theory of quadrature domains.

The main result in the simply connected setting is that a domain is an LQD if and only if the outer factor of its Riemann map extends to the exponential of a rational function (Theorem
4.1
). This gives a natural analogue of the Riemann map characterization of classical QDs, while showing that in the log-weighted setting the correct object is not the full Riemann map itself, but rather its rational inner part together with the rational data encoded by the outer factor. From this characterization one obtains explicit Faber transform formulae relating the quadrature function to the Riemann map (Theorem
4.3
) which, in turn, renders the inverse and direct problems effectively computable in a number of cases. These general results are then applied to classify null LQDs (Theorem
3.1
), to obtain a partial classification of one-point LQDs (§
6
), and to analyze monomial and one-point families in both the singular and non-singular settings.

Several natural directions remain open. First, while the simply connected case admits a clean description in terms of the inner/outer factorization of the Riemann map, it would be of interest to generalize this to the multiply connected setting. One promising direction is the work of Crowdy and Marshall
[
5
]
, which uses Schottky–Klein prime functions to address the multiply connected inverse problem for classical quadrature domains. Second, the families treated here suggest the possibility of sharper classification theorems for singular one-point and monomial LQDs, especially in the absence of symmetry. More broadly, this study of LQDs should be viewed as part of a larger program of understanding
weighted quadrature domains
- especially those with singular/degenerate weights. It would be interesting to determine to what extent the methods developed here extend to other weighted theories. My hope is that the present work provides a useful framework for such problems by demonstrating that much of the classical QD machinery survives in this weighted setting, albeit in a modified form.

The Faber Transform

The Faber transform is a linear operator between functions analytic in the disk (or exterior disk) and functions analytic in a given simply connected domain. In the present paper it serves as the main mechanism for passing between the quadrature function and the Riemann map. In particular, it converts rational data on one side into rational data on the other, and it admits explicit formulae for rational inputs. See
[
8
]
§1.5.1, and
[
7

12

3
]
, for further details on the Faber transform, Faber polynomials, and their properties. We will begin by defining the

Definition 8.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded simply connected domain with Riemann map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
, normalized so that
\varphi(0)=w_{0}\in\Omega
, and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0
. Also write
\psi:=\varphi^{-1}
. The associated interior Faber transform is the map
\Phi_{\varphi}:\mathcal{A}_{0}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega_{\ast})
given by

\Phi_{\varphi}(f)(w):=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[f\circ\psi\right](w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega_{\ast}}\dfrac{f\circ\psi(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi.

(8.2)

Its inverse
\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}:\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega_{\ast})\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{0}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
is given by
\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}(f):=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[f\circ\varphi\right]

The important properties for our purposes are the following:

•

\Phi_{\varphi}

\mathcal{A}_{0}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega_{\ast})

•
