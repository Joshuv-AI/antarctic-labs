Co-assembly of rice and macadamia proteins for enhanced plant protein functionality: Fabrication, properties, and mechanism
Authors: Xixiang Shuai, Mingfeng Fang, David Julian McClements, Ming Zhang, Zhipan Wang, Wei Zhou, Liqing Du, Jihua Li, Jun Chen, Taotao Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant proteins are promising sustainable ingredients, but their use is limited by poor solubility and inadequate functionality. In this study, rice protein (RP) and macadamia protein isolate (MPI) were co-assembled to develop blended plant proteins with improved solubility and functional properties. Soluble RP-MPI composites were fabricated via co-assembly, primarily stabilized by hydrophobic interactions between unfolded polypeptide chains. At an RP/MPI ratio of 1:1, the composites showed optimal colloidal properties with the smallest particle diameter, lowest polydispersity index, and highest water dispersibility (94%), resulting in markedly improved emulsifying (120-fold) and foaming (3.2-fold) performance compared to RP alone. Moreover, the composites effectively stabilized oil-in-water emulsions with uniform droplet size distributions and showed a more complementary essential amino acid profile than the individual proteins. These results indicate that heteroaggregation of complementary proteins is an effective approach for improving the functional and nutritional quality of insoluble plant proteins.Keywords: Rice protein, macadamia protein isolate, Composite proteins, Water solubility, Oil-in-water emulsion

Introduction

Global population growth and an increased awareness of the potentially negative impacts of foods on health and the environment have increased the requirement for sustainable, environmentally friendly, safe, and nutritious protein-rich products (Li et al., 2025). Due to their greater abundance, lower costs, better sustainability, and fewer adverse environmental impacts, there has been great interest in using plant proteins to replace animal proteins as functional ingredients in foods and beverages (Fu et al., 2023). Rice in a widespread agricultural crop that is a stable food in many regions of the world. The rice protein (RP) extracted from this crop have been reported to exhibit a range of beneficial physiological functions, including hormone regulation, antioxidant activity, antihypertensive effects, cholesterol reduction, and anti-obesity properties (Mohidem et al., 2022). Consequently, they may be a good source of plant proteins for application in healthier and more sustainable food products. However, approximately 98% of the subunits in RP are tightly cross-linked through hydrophobic attraction and disulfide bonding, leading to extremely low solubility and poor functionality in common food solvents such as weakly acidic to neutral aqueous solutions, which greatly limits their application in liquid food and beverage formulations (Zhao et al., 2020). Macadamia nuts are another widely consumed agricultural commodity with a beneficial nutritional profile (Shuai et al., 2022). Macadamia protein isolate (MPI) is a byproduct of the processing of these nuts, which is rich in essential amino acids (Birch et al., 2009). However, its widespread application as a functional ingredient in foods and beverages is also limited by its poor water-solubility and functionality. We hypothesized that the limitations of RP and MPI could be overcome by forming composite RP-MPI systems.

This hypothesis is based on the results of previous studies that have shown that complexes consisting of different kinds of plant proteins can have better solubilities and functionalities than either of the individual proteins (Song et al., 2024). In recent years, a pH-shift-based strategy has been developed to improve the dispersibility and functionality of hydrophobic proteins by combining them with other proteins under strongly alkaline conditions followed by neutralization (Tang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2018). During this process, alkaline treatment promotes partial unfolding of the constituent proteins, whereas subsequent neutralization facilitates intermolecular reassociation and the formation of mixed composite structures stabilized by non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic attraction, and van der Waals forces (Ouyang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). In related studies, this pH-induced formation of hetero-protein composite structures has often been described as co-assembly (Pan & Zhong, 2016; Wang et al., 2021). This strategy has been successfully applied to improve the solubility and functionality of several hydrophobic protein systems. For instance, combining wheat gluten and soybean protein using this pH-shift-induced co-assembly approach led to an increase in water solubility from 4.5% to 72.4% (He et al., 2020). Similarly, combining rice and soybean proteins resulted in a more than 20-fold increase in solubility, along with 3.33- and 1.89-fold improvements in emulsifying and foaming capacities, respectively (Yang et al., 2025). We therefore speculated that pH-shift-induced co-assembly of RP and MPI could improve the solubility and functionality of these two plant proteins, while also providing a more complementary amino acid profile.

The overall goal of this study was therefore to construct and characterize RP-MPI composites with improved solubility and functionality. Structural modifications of the proteins were characterized to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms driving RP-MPI composite formation, with a particular focus on changes in secondary and tertiary structures. Furthermore, the functional properties of the composite proteins were systematically assessed, including their potential as a stabilizer in oil-in-water emulsions. The knowledge gained from this study could therefore lead to plant protein ingredients with improved functional performance and a broader range of applications in the food industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

Broken rice was purchased from a local market. Macadamia press cake, the by-product from the oil extraction process of macadamia nuts, was provided by the South Subtropical Crop Research Institute, China Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences (Zhanjiang, China). Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, ≥99.0%), urea (≥99.5%), dithiothreitol (DTT, ≥99.4%), 1-anilinonaphthalene-8-sulfonic acid (ANS, ≥97%), and 5,5-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB, ≥98%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Inc. (Shanghai, China). Other chemical reagents (analytical grade) were purchased from Xilong Science Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). All subsequent deionized water used for dilution was adjusted to pH 7.

Sample preparation

Rice proteins: RP was prepared using an alkali extraction and acid precipitation method following the procedure described by Wang et al. (2022), with slight modifications. Briefly, broken rice was ground using a mechanical mill and passed through an 80-mesh sieve. The resulting rice flour was mixed with distilled water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:20 (w/v). The slurry was then adjusted to pH 12 using 1 mol/L NaOH before being continuously stirred for 4 h at 25 °C. After centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant containing the soluble proteins was collected. The supernatant was then adjusted to around the isoelectric point of the proteins (pH 4.5) using 1 mol/L HCl under constant stirring to promote protein precipitation. The sample was then centrifuged using the same conditions described earlier (10,000 ×g, 4 °C, 20 min) to collect the protein precipitate. The wet protein pellet was then washed three times using deionized water, resuspended in deionized water, and then neutralized to pH 7 with 0.1 mol/L NaOH. Finally, the resulting protein solution was frozen at −80 °C for 12 h and then lyophilized using a freeze dryer (Alpha 2–4 LSCbasic, Martin Christ Gefriertrocknungsanlagen GmbH, Osterode am Harz, Germany) with a program of pre-freezing at −90 °C for 20 min, preheating for 10 min, and freeze-drying at −85 °C for 48 h under 0.3 mbar. The obtained RP powder had a protein purity of 88.0% and a protein recovery yield of 69.8%, and was stored at 25 °C for subsequent experiments.

Macadamia proteins: MPI was prepared using a procedure similar to that of RP, which was also based on alkali extraction and acid precipitation with some minor modifications. Briefly, for laboratory-scale protein preparation, the macadamia press cake was first defatted with petroleum ether (1:20, w/v) to obtain defatted powder. The powder was then mixed with deionized water (1,20, w/v), adjusted to pH 10 using 1 mol/L NaOH, and continuously stirred at 25 °C for 4 h. After centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and adjusted to the isoelectric point of the protein (pH 4.5) using 1 mol/L HCl. The resulting precipitate was then recovered by centrifugation under the same conditions (10,000 ×g, 20 min, 4 °C). The crude protein was then washed three times with deionized water, resuspended in deionized water, and neutralized to pH 7 with 1 mol/L NaOH. The dispersion was frozen and freeze-dried using the same procedure as described above for RP preparation. The obtained MPI powder had a protein purity of 94.0% and a protein recovery yield of 74.2%, and was stored at 25 °C for subsequent experiments.

Preparation of RP-MPI composites: Different amounts of powdered MPI were added to a preprepared 1% (w/v) aqueous RP suspension to obtain mixed protein dispersions with different RP-to-MPI ratios (R/M, w/w): 1:0.2, 1:0.5, 1:1.0, 1:1.5, and 1:2.0. These mixtures were then adjusted to pH 12 using 1 mol/L NaOH and stirred continuously in a water bath at 55 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the pH was readjusted to 7 with 1 mol/L HCl. The resulting samples were then divided into two portions depending on the subsequent analysis. One portion was retained as the dispersion for analyses of the whole dispersed system. The other portion was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected for analyses requiring more homogeneous samples. Both portions were desalted by dialysis against deionized water for 48 h using dialysis bags with a molecular weight cut-off of 3.5 kDa. To better demonstrate the effect of protein co-assembly, RP and MPI were individually dispersed in water to prepare 1% (w/v) protein dispersions, which served as controls. Additionally, both proteins were adjusted to pH 12 and subsequently neutralized to pH 7, and were designated as RP-pH and MPI-pH, respectively. After dialysis, the actual protein content/concentration of each sample was determined by the Kjeldahl nitrogen determination method, and the measured values were used to prepare samples at the required protein concentrations for subsequent analyses.

Aqueous dispersions of the protein samples were centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove any insoluble proteins. The protein contents of the overall dispersions and the corresponding supernatants were determined by the Kjeldahl nitrogen determination method using an automatic Kjeldahl apparatus (K9840, Haineng Instruments Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China). Particle size and charge analysis

The mean particle diameter and particle size distribution of the protein dispersions were determined using a static light scattering instrument (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Ltd., Worcestershire, UK). Each protein dispersion was introduced dropwise into a measurement chamber filled with deionized water until an appropriate obscuration level was achieved. The Z-average particle diameter and zeta potential of the protein supernatants were measured using a combined dynamic light scattering-electrophoresis instrument (Zetasizer Nano-ZS, Malvern Ltd., UK). In this case, the protein supernatants were diluted to 1 mg/mL using deionized water, and 1 mL of each sample was analyzed at 25 °C. For both measurements, the particle refractive index and absorption index were set to 1.45 and 0.01, respectively, and the refractive index of water was set to 1.33 (Hu, Yu, et al., 2023).

Microstructure of samples

The microstructures of dried and wet samples were assessed using electron and light microscopy instruments, respectively.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning electron microscopy analysis of the dried samples was carried out following the method described by Li et al. (2023). Dispersions of RP, MPI, and RP-MPI composites (10 mg/mL) were first desalted by dialysis, then applied onto a conductive silicon wafer, and dried at 25 °C. The wafer was then mounted on conductive adhesive tape, sputter-coated with a thin gold layer, and observed using a scanning electron microscope (JSM-6701F, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV at a magnification of 1000 × .

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) with fluorescence staining

Confocal fluorescence microscopy images of the wet samples were acquired using the experimental procedure described by Zhang, Xu, et al. (2025). Briefly, dispersions of RP, MPI, and RP-MPI composites (10 mg/mL) were stained with Nile Blue solution (1 mg/mL) at a volume ratio of 1:50 (v/v). Fluorescence images were then acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) at an excitation wavelength of 633 nm.

Characterization of protein structure

Intrinsic fluorescence analysis

The intrinsic fluorescence emission spectra of the protein supernatants were acquired using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-7000, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) following the method described by Zhang et al. (2023). Initially, freshly prepared protein supernatants were diluted to 0.3 mg/mL using deionized water. Measurements were carried out at an excitation wavelength of 280 nm, with emission spectra collected from 290 to 500 nm. Both excitation and emission slit widths were set to 2.5 nm.

Surface hydrophobicity

The surface hydrophobicity (H₀) was determined following the method described by Dong et al. (2023), with minor modifications. Briefly, protein supernatants were diluted to concentrations ranging from 0.02 to 0.1 mg/mL. Then, 10 μL of ANS solution (8 mM in 10 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.0) was added to 4 mL of each diluted protein solution. After homogenization by vortex mixing, the mixture was incubated in the dark for 5 min. Fluorescence intensity was then measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-7000, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) with excitation and emission wavelengths set to 390 nm and 484 nm, respectively. Both slit widths were maintained at 2.5 nm, and the scanning speed used was 12,000 nm/min. The H₀ value was calculated as the initial slope of the fluorescence intensity versus protein concentration plot.

Circular dichroism (CD)

According to a method described previously (Salama et al., 2025), a circular dichroism spectrometer (MOS-450, Bio-Logic SAS, France) was used to measure the CD spectra of the protein solutions over the wavelength range from 190 to 250 nm at 25 °C. The concentration of protein supernatants used was 0.1 mg/mL. The spectrometer scanning speed was set to 50 nm/min. The secondary structure content of the proteins was determined by deconvoluting the CD spectra using the DICHROWEB CONTIN online program.

Sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

SDS-PAGE analysis of RP, MPI and RP-MPI composites was performed using the method outlined by Igartúa et al. (2024), with slight modifications. Protein supernatants (1 mg/mL) were combined with loading buffer containing DTT to analyze the protein subunit profiles under reducing conditions. The mixtures were then denatured by heating at 100 °C for 8 min, cooled to 25 °C, and then centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 5 min. Subsequently, 10 μL of each supernatant along with 5 μL of protein ladder were loaded onto precast gels. Electrophoresis was then conducted at 25 °C at 80 V for 15 min, followed by 120 V for 55 min. Following electrophoresis, the gels were stained using Coomassie Blue fast staining solution and then destained in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions (Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., China).

Amino acid analysis

Amino acid analysis of the different protein samples was performed according to the method described by Wang et al. (2021), with minor modifications. Briefly, 100 mg of each protein sample was weighed into a sealed hydrolysis tube, mixed with 8 mL of 6 mol/L HCl, and purged with nitrogen for 3 min before being tightly sealed. Hydrolysis was then conducted at 120 °C for 24 h. The resulting hydrolysate was neutralized with 10 mmol/L NaOH, followed by volumetric dilution, filtration, and centrifugation (10,000 ×g, 4 °C, 30 min). The amino acid composition of each sample was then determined using an amino acid analyzer under the following conditions: column temperature 38 °C, flow rate 1.0 mL/min, and detection wavelength 254 nm. The amino acid content was expressed as g per 100 g of protein. Tryptophan was not analyzed due to its chemical degradation under acidic hydrolysis conditions.

Results and discussion

Appearance, dispersibility, and stability

The dispersibility of proteins in aqueous solutions is a critical determinant of their functional attributes, as a low dispersibility typically severely restricts their application as functional ingredients in fluid food and beverage systems (Yan et al., 2023). Immediately after preparation, all the protein dispersions had a turbid beige appearance, which suggested that they contained some colloidal particles that were large enough to scatter light strongly (Fig. 1A). The beige color of the composite dispersions increased as the MPI concentration increased, which can be attributed to the more brownish appearance of the powdered MPI. Notably, both the control RP and MPI dispersions exhibited visible sedimentation after one day of storage, with the RP dispersion consisting of a clear serum layer above a white sediment and the MPI dispersion consisting of a turbid layer above a white sediment. This result suggests that the pure protein samples contained relatively large protein aggregates that rapidly moved downward due to the forces of gravity. At a relatively high 1:0.2 R/M, a thick white sediment was observed at the bottom of the vials after one day of storage, which suggests that some aggregation of the proteins occurred in this system. However, stable RP-MPI dispersions were obtained at lower 1:0.5 R/M, with no visible precipitation or stratification being observed, even after 30 days of storage at 25 °C. This result suggests that the co-assembly of the proteins enhanced the dispersibility and storage stability of the composite system. Presumably, this was because the RP-MPI composites formed smaller colloidal particles in the aqueous solutions than the individual proteins.

As shown in Fig. 1B, the untreated RP and MPI samples exhibited poor water dispersibility, with NSI values of only 2.06% and 10.65%, respectively. After undergoing the pH-shifting treatment individually, the solubility of RP-pH and MPI-pH increased to 16.12% and 90.33%. The NSI increased sharply as the MPI content in the composites increased. When the R/M reached 1:1, the NSI value peaked at 94.07%, with further increases in MPI content above this value not affecting the solubility. The improved water dispersibility of the RP–MPI composites was likely associated with the combined effects of pH-shift-induced structural modification and subsequent intermolecular interactions between RP and MPI during co-treatment. In particular, interactions between the two proteins, including hydrophobic association, may have promoted the formation of smaller and more stable colloidal particles, thereby reducing the exposure of non-polar regions to the surrounding aqueous phase and decreasing the tendency of the proteins to form larger aggregates (Tang et al., 2022). Given that the primary focus of this study was on the co-treated composites, the further analysis of individually treated samples (RP-pH and MPI-pH) were not characterized instead of their initial solubility assessment.

Particle size and zeta potential analysis

To provide further insights into the nature of the colloidal particles present in the different samples, the particle size distributions, mean particle diameters, and zeta potentials of the RP, MPI, and RP-MPI composite dispersions were measured (Fig. 2). The particle size distributions of the individual RP and MPI dispersions determined by static light scattering showed that they contained a broad range of relatively large particles (> 2–100 μm) (Fig. 2A), suggesting that they contained large proteins aggregates, which would account for their rapid sedimentation during storage. The 1:0.2 R/M composites also contained relatively large particles, which suggested that they also contained large protein aggregates. In contrast, the other RP-MPI composites had distinct bimodal distributions, with a peak around 0.4 μm and another one around 100 μm, suggesting that they contained a mixture of smaller and larger colloidal particles. The height of the peak corresponding to the larger colloidal particles increased as the R/M ratio increased, suggesting that the MPI may contribute to this peak. The Z-average particle diameters determined by the dynamic light scattering instrument were relatively small for all samples (Fig. 2B). These results should be treated with caution because this type of instrument cannot detect particles exceeding about 10 μm because they move too slowly. Instead, this instrument will only detect the faster moving smaller colloidal particles. In most of the composite systems, these particles were relatively small (< 120 nm) and had a low polydispersity index (< 0.2), which may account for their relatively good resistance to sedimentation during storage. Notably, the colloidal particles in the 1:1 R/M composites had the smallest particle size (116.35 nm) and lowest polydispersity (0.188), suggesting that this protein ratio was most suitable for creating small protein aggregates (Gao et al., 2023).

All of the protein dispersions had a negative charge under the conditions used (Fig. 2C), which can be attributed to the fact that the pH was above the isoelectric point of the proteins. Notably, the negative charge was greater for the pure MPI than for the pure RP dispersions, which may have been due to differences in amino acid composition and protein conformation, which led to more negatively charged groups and/or less positively charged groups exposed at the MPI surfaces. For the RP-MPI composites, the magnitude of the negative charge increased with increasing MPI content, which again reflects the higher anionic nature of this protein. However, the negative charge remained relatively high and constant for R/M ratios of 1:1 to 1:2, suggesting that the surface charge of the colloidal particles did not change appreciably as more MPI was added over this range. This effect may have been because the composition of the composite colloidal particles formed remained relatively constant.

Morphology of colloidal particles

SEM and CLSM were used to characterize the microstructures of the colloidal particles in the different protein dispersions (Fig. 2D). Large irregular shaped aggregates were observed in both the untreated RP and untreated MPI samples. Notably, these aggregates were larger in the RP samples, which is consistent with the particle size measurements obtained by static light scattering. Some large aggregates were obtained in the 1:0.2 R/M composite dispersions, which is again consistent with the static light scattering analysis. However, few large particles were observed by SEM or CLSM for the other R/M composite dispersions, indicating that alkalinization-neutralization, together with intermolecular association between RP and MPI, contributed to the disruption of larger aggregates and the formation of smaller colloidal structures.

Analysis of protein molecular properties

Insights into alterations in protein properties caused by the preparation methods used to assemble the RP-MPI composites were then obtained using a variety of analytical methods.

Fluorescent spectroscopy analysis

The intrinsic fluorescence of aromatic amino acid residues, such as tryptophan (Trp), tyrosine (Tyr), and phenylalanine (Phe), is highly sensitive to changes in the polarity of their microenvironment, which can provide valuable insights into alterations in protein conformation and aggregation state (Zhu et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 3A, the wavelength where the emission maximum (λmax) of the RP-MPI composites occurred exhibited a pronounced blue shift compared to the untreated RP (from 355 nm to 340 nm). The emission peaks of all the RP-MPI composites were greater than that of the individual proteins, which suggests that the proteins may have interacted with each other (Zhu et al., 2024). The maximum fluorescence peak intensity (FImax) of the RP-MPI composites first increased and then decreased with increasing MPI content, indicating changes in the exposure of aromatic amino acids to the surrounding aqueous solvent (Zhang et al., 2024). This change may be attributed to the combined effects of alkaline treatment and subsequent intermolecular association during co-treatment of the proteins. The strongly alkaline conditions likely promoted protein unfolding, thereby exposing non-polar groups and increasing the fluorescence intensity, whereas the co-assembly of the proteins may have reduced the exposure of these groups, thereby decreasing the fluorescence intensity (Rawat & Saini, 2023).

(A) Intrinsic fluorescence curves, (B) Surface hydrophobicity, (C) Sulfhydryl-groups and disulfide bonds contents, (D) CD curves, (E) SDS-PAGE under reducing condition of RP, MPI, and RP-MPI composite proteins. The different letters indicated significant differences (p < 0.05).

Surface hydrophobicity

The H₀ serves as a useful indicator of changes in protein conformation and aggregation, as well as impacting emulsification, foaming, and gelation properties (Dong et al., 2023). Notably, the surface hydrophobicity of the untreated MPI was much greater than that of the untreated RP, suggesting that the MPI had more non-polar groups exposed on their surfaces. For the RP-MPI composites, the H₀ value first increased and then decreased as the MPI content increased, reaching a maximum value at a R/M mass ratio of 1:1 (Fig. 3B). The initial increase may simply have been because there were more MPI molecules present and so more non-polar groups exposed. However, the subsequent decrease in H0 may have been because some of the MPI molecules bound to the surfaces of the RP-MPI composites through hydrophobic attraction, thereby reducing the number of non-polar groups exposed.

Sulfhydryl groups and disulfide bonds

The free and total sulfhydryl contents in the RP-MPI composites were higher than those in untreated RP but lower than those in untreated MPI (Fig. 3C). The total sulfhydryl content remained relatively consistent across the different RP-MPI composites. However, the free sulfhydryl content initially decreased and then increased slightly with increasing MPI content, whereas the disulfide bond content showed the opposite trend. These observations may reflect the combined influence of pH-induced unfolding and subsequent intermolecular reassociation in the composite system. First, the unfolding of the protein molecules induced by the strong alkaline conditions used to prepare the composites may have increased the exposure of buried sulfhydryl groups at the protein surfaces (Hu et al., 2022). Second, the exposed sulfhydryl groups interacted with other sulfhydryl groups leading to the formation of new disulfide bonds, thereby resulting in a reduction in free sulfhydryl content (Chen et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2023).

Characterization of secondary structure

The co-assembly method used induced notable changes in the secondary structure of the proteins within the RP-MPI composites (Fig. 3D). The negative ellipticity observed at 222 nm increased with increasing MPI content, along with a rise in β-sheet content and a reduction in α-helix content, which is consistent with greater unfolding of the proteins within the RP-MPI composites. The secondary structure of the proteins was also assessed by fitting a model to the measured CD spectra (Table 1). The α-helix and β-turn contents initially decreased and then increased with an increase in R/M ratio, whereas the β-sheet content exhibited the opposite trend. A lower α-helix content suggests greater unfolding of the protein molecules and a more flexible and disordered conformation (Zhu et al., 2020). The initial increase in β-sheet content indicated partial unfolding of the tertiary structure, which promoted molecular interactions with the aqueous environment (Zhu et al., 2024).

SDS-PAGE analysis

SDS-PAGE was used to separate and identify the different protein subunits in the protein dispersions, as well as to estimate their molecular weights (Chen et al., 2023). The SDS-PAGE profiles of the RP, MPI, and RP-MPI composite dispersions under reducing conditions are shown in Fig. 3E. The electrophoretic patterns of the RP-MPI composites retained all the characteristic subunit bands from the two parent proteins, indicating the they contained both RP and MPI (Wang et al., 2018). These results indicated that the RP-MPI composites retained the major characteristic subunit bands of the parent proteins under reducing conditions, suggesting that the co-assembly process did not markedly alter their primary subunit composition.

Amino acid composition

The amino acid compositions of the different proteins and their composites were analyzed to provide insights into the nutritional quality. The Lys content of the RP-MPI composites exceeded that of pure RP, while the Met content exceeded that of pure MPI (Table 2). The nutritional quality of the RP-MPI composites was further assessed by calculating their amino acid scores according to the FAO recommended amino acid scoring pattern (2013). As summarized in Table 2, the amino acid scores of Thr, Ile, Leu, Tyr + Phe, and His in the RP-MPI composites were all above 100, while Val was close to adequacy and reached or slightly exceeded 100 in the composite samples. The sulfur-containing amino acid score (Met + Cys) was below 100 at the ratio of 1:0.2, but exceeded 100 in the samples from 1:0.5 to 1:2.0, whereas Lys remained below 100 in all composite samples, indicating that it was the limiting amino acid. These results indicate that co-assembly of the two proteins improved the complementarity of the amino acid profile of the system.

Functional properties

The emulsifying and foaming properties of the different proteins and their composites were them measured to provide insights into their potential for application as functional ingredients in the food industry.

Emulsifying properties

As shown in Fig. 4A, the RP/MPI composites had a maximum EAI of 2.90 m2/g when the R/M was 1:1, indicating that co-assembly improved the emulsifying performance of the proteins (Wang et al., 2018). Notably, the EAI of the untreated RP was only 0.02 m2/g, which was likely related to its poor solubility, large particle size, and low surface hydrophobicity, which reduced its ability to adsorb to oil–water interfaces. In contrast, the good emulsifying properties of the composites may be associated with their enhanced water-dispersibility, smaller particle size, and increased surface hydrophobicity, which facilitated their rapid adsorption to the oil–water interfaces during homogenization (Shao et al., 2025). Fig. S1 further showed that EAI was positively correlated with NSI and surface hydrophobicity, but negatively correlated with Z-average size, supporting these associations. The RP/MPI composites exhibited higher ESI values than the individual proteins (RP or MPI), indicating that they were better at stabilizing emulsions (Fig. 4A). However, the ESI values gradually decreased as the proportion of MPI increased (lower R/M values). These results suggest that co-assembly improved the interfacial behavior of the protein system and may have contributed to the formation of more stable interfacial films, which may be important for creating emulsified foods with good long-term stability (Yang et al., 2023).

Foaming properties

As shown in Fig. 4B, all RP-MPI composites had around 3-fold higher foaming activity values than the untreated RP and MPI samples, which have FA values of about 7.37% and 6.25%, respectively. The relatively high water dispersibility, small particle size, and high surface hydrophobicity of the RP-MPI composites may have enhanced their ability to adsorb to air-water interfaces during foaming (Wang et al., 2020). Fig. S1 indicated that FA was positively correlated with NSI and surface hydrophobicity and negatively correlated with Z-average size. Notably, however, no differences in foam stability were detected among the RP/MPI composites with different R/M ratios (Fig. 4B), suggesting they were all equally effective at stabilizing the foams (Wang et al., 2018).

Stability of O/W emulsions

The physical stability of emulsified foods is important as it determines their processing and shelf life. In general, the stability of emulsions depends on their droplet characteristics, such as their droplet size, charge, and surface hydrophobicity (Zou et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 4C, all emulsions initially had monomodal particle size distributions, however, there were appreciable differences in the size of the droplets produced. Notably, the RP produced the largest oil droplets, then the MPI, and then the composites. This effect was probably because the protein aggregates in the RP and MPI ingredients were relatively large and/or had a low surface hydrophobicity. Consequently, they were less effective at adsorbing to oil-water interfaces and forming small oil droplets during homogenization. In contrast, the protein aggregates in the composites were smaller and had a higher surface hydrophobicity, which made them more effective emulsifiers, leading to a smaller droplet size. The calculated volume-weighted (D[4,3]) and surface-weighted (D[3,2]) mean particle diameters of the emulsions formed also showed that the composites were much more effective emulsifiers than RP, and somewhat more effective than MPI (Fig. 4D). The microstructural images (Fig. 5) further confirmed that the emulsions stabilized by the RP-MPI composites contained smaller and more uniform oil droplets than those formed from the individual proteins.

OPM images and CLSM images of emulsions stabilized by RP, MPI, and RP-MPI composite proteins.

The zeta potential values of all the emulsions were strongly negative, which can be attributed to the fact that the pH was well above the isoelectric point of both proteins (Fig. 4E). For the emulsions stabilized by the composite proteins, the absolute value of the zeta potential increased with MPI content, which can be attributed to the fact that the MPI had a higher net negative charge than the RP. Typically, a higher negative charge on the droplet surfaces would lead to a stronger electrostatic repulsion between the oil droplets, thereby reducing their tendency to flocculate or coalesce, resulting in a more stable emulsion (Gao et al., 2023).

The impact of protein type on the stability of the emulsions was assessed by measuring their creaming index over time during storage (Fig. 4F). The emulsions formulated using the RP-MPI composites had greater stability to creaming than those formulated using RP or MPI, which can be attributed to the formation of smaller oil droplets that were more resistant to gravitational forces (Zhao et al., 2022). In particular, the emulsions formulated with the untreated RP exhibited the worst stability, which can be attributed to the very large oil droplets in this sample.

Formation mechanism of RP-MPI composites

The potential molecular and physicochemical processes involved in the formation of the RP-MPI composites, as well as in determining their enhanced functional attributes are shown in Fig. 6. The untreated RP and MPI samples contain large dense aggregates that do not disperse well in aqueous solutions and are highly prone to sedimentation due to the strong gravitational forces. When mixed RP and MPI dispersions are adjusted to strongly alkaline conditions the negative charge on the proteins increases, which promotes conformational changes and the dissociation of aggregates due to the strong intra- and inter-molecular electrostatic repulsive interactions. As a result of these processes, there is greater exposure of charged, non-polar, and sulfhydryl groups on the protein surfaces. These transformations facilitate the subsequent co-assembly of the two proteins when the mixed solution is neutralized, resulting in the formation of smaller protein aggregates with a higher surface hydrophobicity and negative charge. These amphiphilic colloidal particles have a greater water-dispersibility and are more effective at adsorbing to oil-water and air-water interfaces, thereby forming and stabilizing emulsions and foams more effectively than the untreated individual properties.

Conclusion

In this study, RP-MPI composites were successfully prepared by incubating RP and MPI mixtures under strongly alkaline conditions to unfold and dissociate them, and then neutralizing the mixture to promote the formation of small amphiphilic protein particles. The results suggest that hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions were the main driving forces for the formation of these composite proteins. Changes in the secondary and tertiary structures of the proteins promoted by the method used to prepare the composite particles were confirmed using a variety of spectroscopy methods. The RP–MPI composites contained colloidal particles with a broad size distribution, including a relatively small particle population characterized by high surface hydrophobicity and surface charge. These particles exhibited good water-dispersibility (>94%), emulsifying and foaming properties. Oil-in-water emulsions with uniform droplet size distributions could be prepared from the RP-MPI composite.

In summary, this study demonstrates that protein co-assembly is an effective approach for improving the water-dispersibility and functional attributes of plant proteins. In addition, combining RP and MPI provided a more complementary amino acid profile than the individual proteins alone. However, the potential allergenicity associated with macadamia-derived proteins, as well as practical limitations related to laboratory-scale petroleum ether defatting, alkaline processing, dialysis, and large-scale application, should be carefully considered before future food applications. Moreover, further studies including full parallel characterization of individually pH-shifted controls would help to more clearly distinguish the relative contributions of pH-induced structural modification and hetero-protein association.
