A rare case report of cysteiniphilumlitorale infection: clinical presentation and diagnostic challenges
Authors: Weijie Zhou, Tingting Yang, Xiaodi Zou, Fangyu Yi, Zheng Lv, Yanzhao Dong, Ahma Alhaskawi, Jia Hu, Dongsheng Han, Hui Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cysteiniphilum litorale is a Gram-negative, non-motile, non-spore-forming bacterium. It is dependent on L-cysteine for growth. This report describes the case of a 46-year-old male who developed a severe soft-tissue infection after a sea-shrimp puncture injury to the finger, which was subsequently identified as Cysteiniphilum litorale infection. The patient’s clinical course, diagnosis, and treatment are discussed, along with an emphasis on the need for early identification and appropriate management of Cysteiniphilum litorale infections.

Introduction

Cysteiniphilum litorale was originally isolated from the waters of Weepy Island near the Pearl River Estuary in southern China.Only a few cases have been reported in the literature, and the clinical manifestations and diagnostic challenges associated with these infections are not well understood [1]. Cysteiniphilum litorale can cause serious skin and soft tissue infections and even lead to complications such as osteomyelitis through Marine organisms. In this case, multiple abscesses developed rapidly after the patient was stabbed by sea shrimp and involved bone tissue, suggesting that the pathogen has the ability to break through local barriers and cause systemic invasion [1].The case highlights the need for early identification and appropriate management of Cysteiniphilum litorale infections, as well as the importance of microbiological testing in cases of atypical infections, particularly those linked to aquatic environments.

Case presention

A 46-year-old male was admitted to our hospital approximately 15 days after sustaining a puncture injury to the left ring finger caused by a sea shrimp. The initial prick site was located at the distal part of the left ring finger, close to the periungual region. According to the patient, local pain, swelling, erythema, and exudation developed approximately 1 days after the injury.The patient had no significant past medical, family, psychosocial, or relevant genetic history. On physical examination, the puncture site had progressed to a ruptured ulcerative lesion with mild surrounding erythema, local swelling, and a small amount of purulent exudation. The ulcer surface was irregular, with macerated margins and inflammatory changes in the adjacent soft tissue. In addition to the primary lesion on the left ring finger, several discontinuous skip lesions were observed along the ipsilateral upper limb. These lesions presented as localized erythematous and swollen nodules or abscess-like ulcers, some of which showed superficial skin breakdown and purulent discharge (Fig. 1). Blood routine and C-reactive protein were normal. The T-SPOT.TB assay (T-cell spot test for tuberculosis) was positive. After thorough debridement and treatment of necrotic tissue, the wound was covered with antibiotic bone cement after operation (Fig. 3). During surgery, purulent secretion and debrided deep tissue specimens were collected aseptically from the infected wound and immediately submitted to the clinical microbiology laboratory for bacterial culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. The specimens were inoculated onto routine bacterial culture media, including Columbia sheep blood agar and chocolate agar, and incubated under standard laboratory conditions. After 48 h of incubation, visible bacterial colonies were observed. The intraoperative purulent specimen was inoculated onto Columbia sheep blood agar and chocolate agar for routine bacterial culture. After 48 h of incubation, small gray-white to whitish colonies were observed on Columbia sheep blood agar. The colonies were round, smooth, moist, and had entire margins, without an obvious hemolytic zone. Similar gray-white, smooth colonies were also observed on chocolate agar. The isolate was subsequently identified as Cysteiniphilum litorale using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry, MALDI-TOF MS. Because Cysteiniphilum litorale is a rarely reported and recently described organism, species-specific susceptibility breakpoints and reliable comparative susceptibility data are unavailable. A T-SPOT.TB assay was performed during the diagnostic workup and yielded a positive result. However, mycobacterial culture was not performed. Because the patient had an acute clinical course following a definite sea-shrimp puncture injury, presented with purulent soft tissue infection and multiple abscesses, and routine bacterial culture yielded Cysteiniphilum litorale, the infection was clinically managed as an acute marine injury-associated bacterial infection. The positive T-SPOT.TB result was interpreted cautiously and was not considered sufficient to establish active mycobacterial infection. Given the history of a marine-related puncture injury, the possibility of infection caused by uncommon aquatic pathogens, and the presence of multiple discontinuous abscesses, the patient received empiric antimicrobial therapy combined with surgical debridement and local wound management. The antimicrobial regimen included oral faropenem sodium granules 0.1 g three times a day, azithromycin 1 g once a day, moxifloxacin 0.4 g once a day, rifampicin 0.45 g once a day. The patient did not undergo secondary surgery or skin grafting, the abscess lesion of the upper limb also disappeared, and the patient recovered well (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Cysteiniphilum litorale is an emerging pathogen that has been rarely implicated in human infections. Initially isolated from coastal seawater near the Pearl River Estuary, it has been associated with environmental exposures and injuries sustained in aquatic settings [1].This case report presents one of the first documented infections caused by this organism, underscoring the clinical and diagnostic challenges associated with its identification.

Cysteiniphilum litorale is characterized by its unique microbiological features, including a negative Gram stain, non-motility, and the inability to form spores [2, 3]. It is also dependent on L-cysteine for growth, which may contribute to its limited pathogenicity in humans.

The clinical presentation in this case was notable for multiple skipping abscesses, a rare and aggressive feature of soft tissue infections. Despite normal inflammatory markers, the infection progressed, necessitating surgical intervention, including debridement, drainage, and bone cement application. Primary lesions can be effectively controlled by surgical debridement, and multiple skipping abscesses can be effectively controlled by anti-infective treatment. Cysteiniphilum litorale is resistant to cephalosporins, but sensitive to ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, gentamicin, tetracycline levofloxacin, and doxycycline [2].The patient was injured by marine organisms and it was not ruled out that mycobacteria or Vibrio vulnificus infection occurred.Therefore, empirical treatment was given for anti-mycobacterial therapy based on experience. The patient’s jumping infection was effectively controlled.

A review of the available literature indicates that human infection caused by Cysteiniphilum litorale is extremely rare. The most relevant previously reported case described a 19-year-old immunocompetent female who developed an abscess of the right middle finger after minor trauma while handling estuarine shrimps at home. In that case, C. litorale was cultured from wound exudate and identified by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Similar to the present case, the infection occurred after shrimp-related exposure and involved the finger, supporting the possibility that marine or estuarine organisms may serve as a source of human inoculation through minor penetrating trauma [4].

In this case, next-generation sequencing (NGS) was not performed. The diagnosis of Cysteiniphilum litorale infection was established by routine intraoperative bacterial culture and microbiological identification. Therefore, NGS did not contribute to pathogen identification, confirmation of the diagnosis, exclusion of co-infections, or antimicrobial selection in this patient. Nevertheless, given the rarity of Cysteiniphilum litoral infection and the unusual clinical presentation with multiple skipping abscesses, NGS could have served as a useful adjunctive tool to screen for potential co-infecting pathogens and to provide genomic information on this uncommon organism [5, 6]. The absence of NGS is therefore acknowledged as a diagnostic limitation rather than an additional diagnostic method used in this case.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges associated with rare marine-derived pathogens and suggests that advanced molecular techniques may be considered as adjunctive tools in future similar cases, particularly when culture results are negative, inconclusive, or when co-infection is suspected. The infection, triggered by a seemingly minor marine injury (sea shrimp sting), progressed to severe soft tissue involvement and osteomyelitis, highlighting the organism’s potential for invasive disease despite its fastidious growth requirements. Successful management relied on a combination of aggressive surgical debridement, targeted antibiotic therapy (faropenem), and localized antibiotic bone cement application.
