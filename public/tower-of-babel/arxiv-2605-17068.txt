Abstract
I propose Nonparametric Bayesian Policy Learning (NBPL) as a framework for uncertainty-aware treatment choice. I consider a decision-maker (DM) seeking to select an expected welfare-maximizing treatment rule using observable characteristics. A key observation is that, for a given welfare criterion and policy class, uncertainty about welfare-relevant objects is entirely induced by uncertainty about a reduced-form distribution. I assume the DM places a nonparametric Dirichlet process prior on this reduced-form parameter and uses the resulting posterior to conduct inference on optimal treatment assignments, optimal welfare, and comparisons across policy classes. The NBPL framework is flexible, and its implementation via the Bayesian bootstrap is highly tractable. I establish two main theoretical properties of NBPL. First, posterior welfare regret under NBPL converges at the minimax-optimal rate. Second, posterior model comparison across policy classes is pointwise consistent. I illustrate NBPL in two empirical applications: the bednet subsidy experiment of Bhattacharya and Dupas 2012 and the JTPA experiment studied by Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018 .



Keywords:
Bayesian bootstrap, Dirichlet process, program evaluation, risk bound, statistical treatment rules.

Introduction

1.1
Overview

In many economic settings, policymakers must allocate scarce interventions across heterogeneous populations.
This motivates a growing literature on statistical treatment rules, or policy learning, pioneered by
Manski 2004
, which studies how to design treatment rules using observable individual characteristics to maximize social welfare; see, for example,
Hirano and Porter 2009
,
Stoye 2009
,
Bhattacharya and Dupas 2012
,
Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018
, and
Athey and Wager 2021
, among others.
1
1

1

Throughout the paper, I use the terms “treatment rule” and “policy” interchangeably.

A growing empirical literature shows that such individualized rules can deliver substantial welfare gains across a wide range of settings, including labor market and education programs
(
Athey et al. 2025b
;
Goller et al. 2025
)
, anti-poverty interventions in developing countries
(
Haushofer et al. 2025
;
Higbee 2025
)
, and health and precision medicine
(
Kosorok and Laber 2019
;
Inoue et al. 2023
;
Athey et al. 2025a
)
.

In this paper, I consider a decision-maker (DM) seeking to select an expected welfare-maximizing treatment rule using observable characteristics.
A key observation is that, for a given welfare criterion and policy class, the expected welfare function is fully determined by a reduced-form data distribution. Consequently, the sole source of statistical uncertainty in the DM’s problem is uncertainty about this distribution. This blends seamlessly with a Bayesian approach in which the DM assumes a nonparametric prior for the reduced-form parameter and uses the resulting posterior to conduct simultaneous inference on optimal treatment assignments, optimal welfare, and comparisons across policy classes. I call this framework
Nonparametric Bayesian Policy Learning
(NBPL).

NBPL has several appealing features. First, it is grounded in a well-defined posterior, so uncertainty enters the decision problem explicitly. A growing literature shows that ignoring uncertainty in treatment or policy choice can lead to suboptimal decisions
(
Dehejia 2005
;
Christensen et al. 2025
;
Moon 2026
, e.g.,)
. Second, to the best of my knowledge, NBPL is the first unified inferential framework for optimal treatment assignments, optimal welfare, and comparisons across policy classes. The last component incorporates uncertainty about the policy class and is difficult to handle with frequentist methods.
Third, NBPL is flexible: the prior for the reduced-form data distribution is a Dirichlet process
(
Ferguson 1973
)
, which offers a level of flexibility similar to that of frequentist nonparametric approaches to policy learning
(
Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018
, e.g., the important Empirical Welfare Maximization (EWM) framework of)
. Fourth, posterior computation is highly tractable: the recommended Bayesian-bootstrap implementation
(
Rubin 1981
)
simply reweights the data using standard exponential weights, reuses off-the-shelf welfare maximization algorithms, and is naturally parallelizable.

I also establish three important theoretical properties of NBPL. First, I show that posterior welfare regret under NBPL converges to zero at the minimax-optimal rate (Theorem
1
), matching the rate for EWM in
Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018
. This shows that explicitly accounting for uncertainty in the decision problem does not slow the rate of learning; to the best of my knowledge, it is the first posterior welfare-regret guarantee for policy learning.
Second, I establish pointwise consistency of posterior model comparison across policy classes (Theorem
2
); in particular, when two classes are strictly separated at the truth, the posterior asymptotically assigns vanishing probability to the inferior class.
Third, I show that EWM arises as a Bayes rule within the NBPL framework (Proposition
1
). Specifically, EWM first averages welfare over posterior uncertainty and then optimizes, whereas decisions based on the posterior over optimal treatment assignments instead optimize first and then aggregate. The two procedures need not coincide.

I illustrate the NBPL framework using two empirical applications: the Job Training Partnership Act (JTPA) experiment studied by
Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018
and the anti-malaria bednet subsidy experiment analyzed by
Bhattacharya and Dupas 2012
. In both applications, EWM welfare estimates tend to fall below the posterior median of welfare under NBPL. NBPL also yields credible intervals for welfare that are tighter than the corresponding EWM confidence intervals. In addition, the posterior model comparison procedure provides strong evidence that decision-tree rules achieve higher welfare than linear rules.

1.2
Literature

This paper connects to three literatures in economics. The first is the literature on statistical treatment rules, or policy learning. A leading approach is the Empirical Welfare Maximization (EWM) framework of
Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018
, which selects treatment rules as if the empirical distribution were the true data-generating process and achieves the minimax-optimal regret convergence rate. By contrast, NBPL places a Dirichlet process prior on the reduced-form data distribution, explicitly incorporates uncertainty into the decision problem through the posterior, and delivers the same minimax-optimal posterior regret rate.
Chamberlain 2011
is closely related in spirit, using Dirichlet priors on outcome distributions to derive posterior-expected-utility treatment decisions in a limited-information Bayesian framework. NBPL builds on this approach by accommodating continuous distributions and constrained policy classes, while also delivering asymptotic regret guarantees.
NBPL is also related to the broader literature on uncertainty-aware decision-making
(
Dehejia 2005
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2025
;
Christensen et al. 2025
;
Moon 2026
, e.g.,)
.
Other contributions to policy learning include
Hirano and Porter 2009
,
Stoye 2009
,
Bhattacharya and Dupas 2012
,
Kallus and Zhou 2018
,
Athey and Wager 2021
,
Kitagawa and Tetenov 2021
,
Mbakop and Tabord-Meehan 2021
,
Zhou et al. 2023
,
Adjaho and Christensen 2025
,
Kitagawa et al. 2025
,
Olea et al. 2025
,
Viviano 2025
, and
Ida et al. 2026
.

Another related literature studies Bayesian inference through reduced-form parameters. In many economic models, an identifiable reduced-form parameter maps into a structural parameter of interest, so a posterior on the former induces a posterior on the latter. The key distinction is whether the structural object is point or partially identified.
2
2

2

In partially identified settings,
Moon and Schorfheide 2012
show that placing a prior directly on the structural parameter can lead to asymptotic divergence between Bayesian credible sets and frequentist confidence sets because the prior is not revisable by data.
Giacomini and Kitagawa 2021
address this issue through a robust prior specification.

In NBPL, the optimal welfare value is point identified, whereas optimal treatment rules may be set-valued and thus only partially identified.
Point-identified examples include
Chamberlain and Imbens 2003
and
Walker 2026b
, who study structural parameters defined by unconditional and conditional moment restrictions, respectively. NBPL is also closely related to the loss-likelihood bootstrap of
Lyddon et al. 2019
, with welfare interpreted as a negative loss. For the partially identified component, my inference procedure is closely related to
Kline and Tamer 2016
and
Florens and Simoni 2021
, which place a prior only on the reduced-form parameter and conduct inference on the structural object through the induced posterior; among these,
Florens and Simoni 2021
is especially close in spirit because it places a Dirichlet process prior on the population distribution.
Additional related contributions include
Norets and Tang 2014
and
Liao and Simoni 2019
. More broadly,
Andrews and Shapiro 2025
develop a regret-based framework for evaluating approximate posterior reports and justify bootstrap distributions, including the Bayesian bootstrap, as devices for communicating uncertainty.

A final related literature studies frequentist inference for optimal treatment rules and optimal welfare values. Inference on the optimal welfare value is important because it quantifies the maximum gains from policy learning, but frequentist inference is challenging because this parameter is irregular; see, for example, the impossibility results in
Hirano and Porter 2012
.
Luedtke and Van Der Laan 2016
,
Ponomarev and Semenova 2025
, and
Whitehouse et al. 2025
develop, respectively, confidence intervals, lower confidence bands, and smoothing-based inference procedures for this parameter.
Inference on optimal treatment assignments is also crucial because it quantifies the strength of the evidence in favor of treating individuals selected for treatment by the rule, but it has received comparatively less attention; two frequentist examples are
Rai 2018
and
Armstrong and Shen 2023
.
Relatedly,
Kitagawa et al. 2023
develop a quasi-Bayesian inference framework for linear rules that places a prior directly on the policy class rather than on the data distribution.
The NBPL framework provides a Bayesian complement to this literature.

Remark 1

(Reduced-form Data)
.

This paper adopts a limited-information Bayesian approach
(
Sims 2006
)
. Specifically, I assume that the DM places a nonparametric prior on the distribution of the reduced-form vector
\widetilde{D}\coloneqq(Z_{1},Z_{0},X)
, where
Z_{1}\coloneqq YT/e(X)
and
Z_{0}\coloneqq Y(1-T)/[1-e(X)]
.
This yields a well-defined Bayesian inference problem for the transformed data
\widetilde{D}
.
The reduced-form approach is motivated by two considerations.
First, the reduced-form distribution contains all welfare-relevant information in the data while avoiding the need to specify a global probability model, thereby mitigating concerns about Bayesian misspecification
(
Müller 2013
;
Lyddon et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.
Second, it avoids two difficulties with standard Bayesian causal inference under Bayesian ignorability (see, e.g.,
Li et al. 2023
for a review). One is that incorporating knowledge of the propensity score is difficult because it does not enter the outcome likelihood; common approaches include propensity-dependent priors
(
Ray and van der Vaart 2020
, e.g.,)
and augmenting the outcome model with the propensity score as a covariate
(
Hahn et al. 2020
;
Walker 2026a
, e.g.,)
. Another is that Bayesian ignorability can imply implausible priors on the magnitude of selection bias in many high-dimensional settings
(
Linero 2024
)
.

2.2.2
Posterior Reporting

The posterior distributions (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) introduced above naturally imply a set of reportable summaries for optimal welfare, optimal treatment rules, and comparisons across policy classes.

Bayesian inference on the optimal welfare is straightforward, since (
2.5

(1-\alpha)

(\alpha/2)
and
(1-\alpha/2)
quantiles of (
2.5
). This offers a Bayesian complement to existing frequentist approaches to inference on optimal population welfare
(
Luedtke and Van Der Laan 2016
;
Ponomarev and Semenova 2025
;
Whitehouse et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

Bayesian inference on optimal treatment rules is more subtle, since (
2.4
) is a posterior distribution over
sets
. Such inference can be conducted within the framework of
Kline and Tamer 2016
, where each treatment rule is viewed as a parameter and
G^{\star}(P)
is interpreted as the identified set of optimal rules.
10
10

10

My prior specification fits naturally into the framework of
Kline and Tamer 2016
, in which a point-identified reduced-form parameter
\mu
(here

) maps into the identified set of a partially identified structural parameter
\theta
(here the optimal treatment rule) through a known set-valued mapping (here

The posterior in (
2.4

Likewise, quantities induced by the set of optimal rules, such as the treatment share, may themselves be set-identified.

In addition to inference on

\mathcal{G}_{1}

P\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\omega}_{i}\,\delta_{D_{i}}
, where
\widetilde{\omega}_{i}=\omega_{i}/\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}
and
\omega_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Exp}(1)

(
Rubin 1981
)
.
Thus, Bayesian bootstrap draws can be viewed as randomly reweighted (and hence smoothed) versions of the empirical distribution.

The
S
posterior draws can be computed in parallel.

Given posterior draws
\{G^{\star,[s]},W_{\mathcal{G}}^{\star,[s]},\pi^{[s]}\}_{s=1}^{S}
, one may estimate optimal welfare by the empirical median of
\{W_{\mathcal{G}}^{\star,[s]}\}_{s=1}^{S}
, and quantify uncertainty using a
(1-\alpha)
equal-tailed credible interval based on the
(\alpha/2)
and
(1-\alpha/2)
empirical quantiles. Model comparison between two classes
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
can likewise be based on the empirical fraction of draws for which
W_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}^{\star,[s]}>W_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}^{\star,[s]}
.

Remark 2

(General Base Measure)
.

The Bayesian bootstrap posterior
\mathrm{DP}(n\mathbb{P}_{n})
can be viewed as the weak limit of
\mathrm{DP}(\alpha+n\mathbb{P}_{n})
as the total mass
|\alpha|\coloneqq\alpha(\mathcal{D})\to 0
.
In practice, researchers may wish to employ DP priors with a nondegenerate base measure
\alpha
to incorporate external information, expert knowledge, or predictions from auxiliary models. This reflects the broader idea of evidence aggregation and meta-analysis, where information from prior studies or related populations is combined with current data to inform decision-making
(
An and Schorfheide 2007
;
Ishihara and Kitagawa 2024
;
Athey et al. 2025c
, e.g.,)
.
Recent work extends this approach by using generative AI to construct base measures from synthetic data
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2023
;
O’Hagan and Ročková 2025
)
.
Appendix
C
extends Algorithm
1
to this setting by sampling posterior draws from
\mathrm{DP}(\alpha+n\mathbb{P}_{n})
using a standard stick-breaking representation.

4
Theoretical Properties of NBPL

This section presents two theoretical results for NBPL. First, I establish a minimax-optimal convergence rate for posterior welfare regret (Theorem
1
). Second, I show that this regret convergence implies pointwise consistency for posterior model selection across policy classes ranked by optimal welfare (Theorem
2
). I conclude by briefly comparing NBPL with empirical welfare maximization (EWM)
(
Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018
)
.

4.1
Assumptions On the Policy Class

Both theoretical results rely on controlling the complexity of the policy class
\mathcal{G}
. Following
Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018
, I assume that
\mathcal{G}
has finite Vapnik--Chervonenkis (VC) dimension.
14
14

14

The Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) dimension of a class of sets
\mathcal{G}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{X}}

measures its complexity in a combinatorial sense.
For any finite collection of points
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,
consider the subsets obtained by intersecting this set with elements of
\mathcal{G}
.
The class
\mathcal{G}
is said to
shatter

\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\}

if it can pick out all
2^{\ell}
possible subsets.
The VC dimension is the largest
\ell
for which some collection of
\ell
points is shattered by
\mathcal{G}

(
van der Vaart and Wellner 2023
, see e.g.,)
.

Assumption 2
.

The policy class
\mathcal{G}
has a finite VC dimension

v<\infty
.

This assumption covers many tractable policy classes, including linear rules and decision-tree rules, both of which yield interpretable policy prescriptions. At the same time, it does not cover all classes; for example, it excludes monotone rules
(
Mbakop and Tabord-Meehan 2021
, see, e.g.,)
.

Example 1

(Linear Rules)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{lin}}\coloneqq\{G_{\beta,c}:G_{\beta,c}(x)=\mathds{1}\{\beta^{\top}x\geq c\},\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p},c\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Then
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{lin}})=p+1
. Each rule in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{lin}}
assigns treatment based on whether a linear eligibility score
\beta^{\top}x
exceeds a threshold
c
.

Example 2

(Decision Trees)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Let
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tree},L}
denote the class of treatment rules induced by decision trees of depth at most
L
over
\mathcal{X}
, where each internal node partitions the space using a threshold rule of the form
\{x_{j}\leq t\}
for some coordinate
j
and threshold
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
It is well known that this class has VC dimension of order
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tree},L})=O(2^{L}\log p)
, up to polylogarithmic factors
(
Athey and Wager 2021
, see, e.g.,)
.

Remark 3

(Policy Class Flexibility)
.

The policy classes in the preceding examples are highly flexible. For instance, fairness considerations can be incorporated by restricting the set of admissible covariates (e.g., excluding race). Capacity constraints can also be imposed by limiting treatment assignment to at most a fraction
\lambda
of the population, for example by augmenting the covariate vector with an auxiliary random variable distributed as
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\lambda)
.

4.2
Regret Guarantee

Following
Manski 2004
;
Manski 2009
, I evaluate a treatment rule by its welfare loss relative to the best feasible policy in the class
\mathcal{G}
.

Definition 2
.

The welfare regret of treatment rule
G^{\star}(P)
is
R(P_{0};P)\coloneqq W_{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}(P_{0})-W(P_{0};G^{\star}(P))
.

The regret
R(P_{0};P)
measures the welfare loss from not knowing the true reduced-form distribution
P_{0}
, and can be viewed as a decision-relevant discrepancy between
P
and
P_{0}
. Posterior regret asymptotic analysis asks whether the posterior concentrates on distributions that incur small regret. This parallels posterior contraction in Bayesian models
(
Ghosal and Van der Vaart 2017
, see, e.g., Chapter 8 of)
, where one fixes a true data-generating parameter and studies whether the posterior concentrates around it.

Theorem
1
shows that posterior regret contracts to zero at the minimax-optimal rate. To the best of my knowledge, this is the first posterior welfare-regret guarantee for policy learning.
15
15

15

Although
R(P_{0};P)
is infeasible because
P_{0}
is unknown, it nevertheless admits a posterior distribution induced by the posterior for
P
, namely the pushforward measure
\Pi(R\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})=\Pi(P\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\circ R^{-1}
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
2
hold. Let
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}P_{0}
denote the observed sample, and let
\Pi(P\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
denote the Dirichlet process posterior
P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathrm{DP}(\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{D_{i}})
.
Let
\{C_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be any sequence with
C_{n}\to\infty
(arbitrarily slowly).
Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\Pi\left.\left(P:R(P_{0};P)\leq C_{n}\,\sqrt{\frac{v}{n}}\,\,\right|\,\,\mathcal{D}_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P_{0}}}{{\to}}1.

(4.1)

Proof.

See Appendix
D.4
.
∎

Theorem
1
has two key implications.
First, posterior welfare regret under NBPL attains the minimax-optimal rate of empirical welfare maximization (EWM) in
Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018
, up to an arbitrarily slowly diverging factor
C_{n}
.
16
16

16

Such slowly diverging factors are standard in posterior contraction theory and typically arise from testing and entropy arguments
(
Ghosal et al. 2000
;
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2007
;
van der Vaart and van Zanten 2008
;
Ghosal and Van der Vaart 2017
, see, e.g.,)
. They are generally regarded as inessential and do not affect comparisons of optimal rates; see also
Ročková and van der Pas 2020
and
Zhang and Gao 2020
for recent developments.

Thus, incorporating posterior uncertainty does not compromise the rate of learning relative to frequentist approaches.
Second, NBPL controls welfare regret over the full posterior distribution: posterior mass concentrates on distributions
P
for which
R(P_{0};P)
is small. In this sense, NBPL yields a finer form of asymptotic control than EWM, whose regret guarantees concern the expected performance of the single rule selected from the sample.

To derive the rate in (
4.1
), I control the terms of the upper bound

R(P_{0};P)\leq\rho_{\mathcal{G}}(P_{0},\mathbb{P}_{n})+\rho_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{P}_{n},P),

where
\rho_{\mathcal{G}}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\coloneqq\sup_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\lvert W(Q_{1};G)-W(Q_{2};G)\rvert

measures the maximal welfare discrepancy between
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
over the policy class.
The first term,
\rho_{\mathcal{G}}(P_{0},\mathbb{P}_{n})
, is the usual sampling error. Since
\mathcal{G}
has finite VC dimension, closeness of the empirical distribution
\mathbb{P}_{n}
to the truth
P_{0}
yields uniform
n^{-1/2}
control of welfare over the class.
The second term,
\rho_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{P}_{n},P)
, is specific to NBPL and reflects posterior uncertainty. Under the Dirichlet process posterior, a draw
P
is a shrinkage estimator of the empirical distribution: it is a convex combination of a base-measure component and a randomly reweighted empirical distribution. The shrinkage weight on the base-measure component is
O_{P}(n^{-1})
and is therefore asymptotically negligible. The remaining empirical component uses normalized exponential weights, which fluctuate around the uniform weight
1/n
at order
O_{P}(n^{-1})
. Hence the reweighted empirical distribution remains close to
\mathbb{P}_{n}
, and finite VC dimension again yields uniform
n^{-1/2}
control of welfare over
\mathcal{G}
.

More generally, Assumption
2
can be relaxed to allow a sequence of policy classes
\{\mathcal{G}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
whose VC dimension grows with the sample size. Provided that
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{G}_{n})=o(n^{\zeta})
for some
0<\zeta<1/2
, posterior regret still converges to zero at the minimax optimal rate
(
Athey and Wager 2021
)
, as shown in the next result.
17
17

17

For example, the regret bound still converges to zero for linear rules when the covariate dimension satisfies
p_{n}\leq n^{\zeta}
for some
\zeta<1/2
, and for decision-tree rules when the maximum depth satisfies
L_{n}=\lfloor\zeta\log_{2}n\rfloor
for some
\zeta<1/2
.
Minimax rate optimality follows by combining the upper bound in Theorem 1 with the lower bound in Theorem 5 of
Athey and Wager 2021
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds.
Let
\{\mathcal{G}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of policy classes with
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{G}_{n})\leq n^{\zeta}
for some
0<\zeta<1/2
and all sufficiently large
n
.
Let
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}P_{0}
denote the observed sample, and let
\Pi(P\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
denote the Dirichlet process posterior
P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathrm{DP}(\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{D_{i}})
.
Let
\{C_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be any sequence with
C_{n}\to\infty
(arbitrarily slowly).
Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\Pi\left.\left(P:R(P_{0};P)\leq C_{n}\,\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{G}_{n})}{n}}\,\,\right|\,\,\mathcal{D}_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P_{0}}}{{\to}}1.

(4.2)

4.3
Model Selection

The NBPL framework can also quantify uncertainty about the policy class through posterior comparison across classes.
Consider two non-nested classes
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, such as linear rules and decision-tree rules, and suppose the goal is to compare them by their optimal population welfare. Under NBPL, the posterior for
P
induces a joint posterior for
(W_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}^{\star}(P),W_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}^{\star}(P))
, so the posterior probability
\Pi(W_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}^{\star}>W_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}^{\star}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
provides a natural measure of the evidence that
\mathcal{G}_{1}
yields higher optimal welfare. To the best of my knowledge, NBPL is the first framework for model comparison across non-nested policy classes in policy learning.

Theorem
2
provides a pointwise consistency guarantee for this comparison: when the two classes are strictly separated at the truth, the posterior probability of assigning the correct sign to the welfare difference converges to one, and hence the posterior probability of selecting the inferior class converges to zero. In the equality case, the posterior instead concentrates on arbitrarily small welfare differences, ruling out any fixed non-negligible gap asymptotically.

Theorem 2
.

Consider two policy classes
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
.
Suppose Assumption
1
holds and that each class
\mathcal{G}_{i}
satisfies Assumption
2
.
Let
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}P_{0}
denote the observed sample, and let
\Pi(P\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
denote the Dirichlet process posterior
P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathrm{DP}(\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{D_{i}})
.

(i)

If
W_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}^{\star}(P_{0})\neq W_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}^{\star}(P_{0})
, then, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\Pi\left(P:\,\sign\,\bigl(W_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}^{\star}(P)-W_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}^{\star}(P)\bigl)\,=\sign\,\bigl(W_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}^{\star}(P_{0})-W_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}^{\star}(P_{0})\bigr)\,\middle|\,\mathcal{D}_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P_{0}}}{{\to}}1.

(4.3)

(ii)

If
W_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}^{\star}(P_{0})=W_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}^{\star}(P_{0})
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\Pi\left(P:\,\bigl|W_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}^{\star}(P)-W_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}^{\star}(P)\bigr|<\varepsilon\,\middle|\,\mathcal{D}_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P_{0}}}{{\to}}1.

(4.4)

Proof.

See Appendix
D.6
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
2
draws on intermediate steps from the proof of the regret convergence rate. If the two classes are strictly separated at the truth, an incorrect posterior ranking can occur only if, for at least one class
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, the optimal welfare under
P
deviates sufficiently from its value under
P_{0}
. Since
|W_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}^{\star}(P)-W_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}^{\star}(P_{0})|\leq\rho_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}(P,P_{0})
,
such a mistake requires at least one discrepancy term
\rho_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}(P,P_{0})
to be non-negligible. The control established in the proof of Theorem
1
then implies that posterior mass on such draws vanishes asymptotically. The knife-edge case is handled similarly.

As a corollary of Theorem
2
, the posterior probability of correctly ranking any two fixed treatment rules converges to one in
P_{0}
-probability as
n\to\infty
. This applies, for example, when a policymaker compares competing rules proposed by different analysts, or benchmarks a proposed targeting rule against the status quo of assigning no treatment. The result follows by applying Theorem
2
to the singleton classes
\mathcal{G}_{1}=\{G_{1}\}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}=\{G_{2}\}
.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds.
Let
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}P_{0}
denote the observed sample, and let
\Pi(P\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
denote the Dirichlet process posterior
P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathrm{DP}(\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{D_{i}})
.
Consider two treatment rules
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.

(i)

If
W(P_{0};G_{1})\neq W(P_{0};G_{2})
, then, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\Pi\left(P:\,\sign\,\bigl(W(P;G_{1})-W(P;G_{2})\bigl)\,=\sign\,\bigl(W(P_{0};G_{1})-W(P_{0};G_{2})\bigr)\,\middle|\,\mathcal{D}_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P_{0}}}{{\to}}1.

(ii)

If
W(P_{0};G_{1})=W(P_{0};G_{2})
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\Pi\left(P:\,\bigl|W(P;G_{1})-W(P;G_{2})\bigr|<\varepsilon\,\middle|\,\mathcal{D}_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P_{0}}}{{\to}}1.

More generally, both Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
remain valid—hence the Bayesian model selection procedure is consistent—when
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{G}_{n})\leq n^{\zeta}
for some
0<\zeta<1/2
.

4.4
Comparison with Empirical Welfare Maximization

This section relates empirical welfare maximization
(
Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018
, EWM;)
to the Nonparametric Bayesian Policy Learning (NBPL) framework. EWM is an as-if optimization rule
(
Manski 2021
)
: it treats the empirical distribution as the true population distribution and selects

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{\mathrm{EWM}}\in\arg\max_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\,W(\mathbb{P}_{n};G).

By contrast, NBPL first represents uncertainty about the reduced-form distribution through a posterior. This posterior can then be propagated through the optimization map
P\mapsto G^{\star}(P)
, yielding a posterior distribution over optimal treatment assignments and optimal welfare.

For any treatment rule
G
, define the welfare-regret loss at distribution
P
by

L(P;G)\coloneqq W_{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}(P)-W(P;G).

Kitagawa and Tetenov 2018
study the frequentist risk

\mathcal{R}_{n}(P_{0})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[L(P_{0};\widehat{G}_{\mathrm{EWM}})],

and show that it converges to zero at the minimax-optimal rate
\sqrt{v/n}
uniformly over a class of data-generating processes.

The next result gives EWM a Bayesian decision-theoretic interpretation.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathrm{DP}(n\mathbb{P}_{n})
.
A Bayes rule solves

\displaystyle\widehat{G}\in\arg\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\int L(P;G)\,d\Pi(P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}).

Then any solution
\widehat{G}
coincides with an empirical welfare maximizer
\widehat{G}_{\mathrm{EWM}}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
D.7
.
∎

Proposition
1
places EWM within the NBPL framework. Under the limited-prior-informativeness Dirichlet process posterior
\mathrm{DP}(n\mathbb{P}_{n})
, EWM is exactly the Bayes rule that minimizes posterior expected welfare regret. It is therefore an average-then-optimize rule: EWM first averages welfare-regret over posterior uncertainty and then selects a treatment rule. By contrast, NBPL is an uncertainty-aware treatment choice framework that retains the full posterior distribution over optimal treatment rules and welfare. Decisions obtained by first optimizing under each posterior draw and then aggregating the induced posterior over optimal treatment assignments need not coincide with EWM.

5
Conclusion

This paper studies a decision-maker (DM) seeking to select an expected welfare-maximizing treatment rule using observable characteristics. The key observation is that, for a given welfare criterion and policy class, the sole source of uncertainty in the DM’s problem is statistical uncertainty about a reduced-form distribution.
I therefore propose
Nonparametric Bayesian Policy Learning
(NBPL), which places a nonparametric Dirichlet process prior on this reduced-form distribution and use the resulting posterior to conduct simultaneous inference on optimal treatment assignments, optimal welfare, and comparisons across policy classes. NBPL provides a natural, flexible, and computationally tractable framework for uncertainty-aware treatment choice. I show that posterior welfare regret converges at the minimax-optimal rate and that posterior model comparison across policy classes is pointwise consistent.

More generally, transparent uncertainty communication in policy analysis must account for both statistical uncertainty and ambiguity
(
Manski 2013
;
Manski 2019
;
Manski 2025
)
, arising from limited data and unsupported assumptions, respectively. Under ambiguity, expected welfare may be only partially identified. NBPL provides a nonparametric Bayesian approach to statistical uncertainty in policy learning, complementing existing work on uncertainty-aware treatment choice
(
Chamberlain 2011
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2025
;
Christensen et al. 2025
;
Moon 2026
, e.g.,)
. Developing a framework that balances statistical uncertainty and ambiguity is an important direction for future research. Another important extension is to allow for unknown propensity scores.

Appendix A
Extensions

A.1
Alternative Welfare Criteria

The main text focuses on utilitarian welfare. More generally, alternative welfare criteria differ in the reduced-form information they require. Criteria that depend only on the marginal distributions of potential outcomes can be incorporated into NBPL by suitably augmenting the reduced-form data. Criteria that depend on the joint distribution of potential outcomes, such as the externally valid welfare criterion of
Adjaho and Christensen 2025
, require richer modeling of conditional outcome distributions and are beyond the scope of this appendix.

A.1.1
Equality-minded Welfare

Kitagawa and Tetenov 2021
propose a rank-dependent welfare criterion,

\displaystyle W_{\Lambda}(P_{0}^{\star};G)=\int_{0}^{1}\Lambda\!\left(F_{P_{0}^{\star},G}(\tau)\right)\,d\tau,

where
\Lambda:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is nonincreasing and convex, with
\Lambda(0)=1
and
\Lambda(1)=0
, and

\displaystyle F_{P_{0}^{\star},G}(y)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}^{\star}}\!\left[\mathds{1}\{Y(1)\leq y,\,X\in G\}+\mathds{1}\{Y(0)\leq y,\,X\notin G\}\right]

denotes the distribution of realized outcomes under rule
G
. Relative to utilitarian welfare, this criterion places greater weight on lower-ranked outcomes.

A.1.2
Quantile-optimal Welfare

Wang et al. 2018
consider maximizing a specified quantile of the realized outcome distribution under rule
G
:

\displaystyle\max_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\;\mathcal{Q}_{P_{0}^{\star},G}(\tau),

where
\mathcal{Q}_{P_{0}^{\star},G}(\tau)\coloneqq\inf\{y:F_{P_{0}^{\star},G}(y)\geq\tau\}

denotes the
\tau
-quantile of realized outcomes under
G
, with
\tau\in(0,1)
. This criterion targets the lower tail of the realized outcome distribution directly, rather than aggregating welfare over the full distribution.

A.1.3
Fairness-constrained Welfare

Fang et al. 2023
study welfare maximization subject to a lower bound on a specified quantile of the realized outcome distribution:

\displaystyle\max_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\,W(P_{0}^{\star};G)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathcal{Q}_{P_{0}^{\star},G}(\tau)\geq q,

where
\tau\in(0,1)
and
q\in\mathbb{R}
. This constraint ensures that the policy does not leave the lower tail of the outcome distribution below a prescribed threshold.

A.1.4
Incorporating These Criteria into NBPL

Let
F_{d}(y\mid x)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(Y(d)\leq y\mid X=x)
for
d=0,1
. The preceding three criteria are accommodated by NBPL because they depend only on these conditional marginals. In particular,

\displaystyle F_{P_{0}^{\star},G}(y)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}^{\star}}\!\left[F_{1}(y\mid X)\mathds{1}\{X\in G\}+F_{0}(y\mid X)\mathds{1}\{X\notin G\}\right].

Under Assumption
1
,
F_{P_{0}^{\star},G}
is point identified (since
F_{0}(\cdot\mid X)
and
F_{1}(\cdot\mid X)
are point identified):

\displaystyle F_{P_{0}^{\star},G}(y)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}^{\star}}\left[\left(\frac{T}{e(X)}\mathds{1}\{X\in G\}+\frac{1-T}{1-e(X)}\mathds{1}\{X\notin G\}\right)\mathds{1}\{Y\leq y\}\right],

so
F_{P_{0}^{\star},G}(\cdot)
is a deterministic function of the distribution of the augmented reduced-form data vector

\check{D}\coloneqq\left(Y,\frac{T}{e(X)},\frac{1-T}{1-e(X)},X\right).

Accordingly, the NBPL framework and Algorithm
1
extend directly to these criteria after replacing the reduced-form vector in the main text by
\check{D}
. Regret convergence rates under these criteria generally require separate analysis, which I do not pursue here.

A.2
Multi-valued Discrete Treatment

A.2.1
Setup: Welfare Maximization

Suppose treatment takes values
T\in\{0,1,\ldots,J\}
with
J\geq 2
, covariates
X\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and realized outcome
Y
. A
treatment rule
is a map
G:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,\ldots,J\}
.

Assumption 3
.

(a)

(External Validity)
The target and experimental populations share the same distribution of potential outcomes and covariates. That is,
P_{0}^{\star}\overset{d}{=}Q_{0}^{\star}
.

(b)

(Unconfoundedness)

(Y(0),Y(1),\ldots,Y(J))\perp\!\!\!\perp T\mid X
.

(c)

(Outcome Moments)

\mathbb{E}_{Q_{0}}|Y|^{2+\delta}<\infty
for some
\delta>0
.

(d)

(Generalized Overlap)
There exists
\kappa\in(0,1/(J+1))
such that the generalized propensity scores
e_{j}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{0}}[\mathds{1}\{T=j\}\mid X=x]
satisfy
e_{j}(x)\geq\kappa
for all
j\in\{0,\ldots,J\}
and
Q_{0}
-almost every
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, the propensity scores are known.

Under Assumption
3
, welfare under any treatment rule
G
is point-identified as

W(P_{0};G)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\!\left[\,\sum_{j=0}^{J}\frac{Y\cdot\mathds{1}\{T=j\}}{e_{j}(X)}\;\mathds{1}\{G(X)=j\}\right].

(C.1)

Define the
inverse-probability-weighted (IPW) score
for arm
j
:

Z_{j}\coloneqq\frac{Y\cdot\mathds{1}\{T=j\}}{e_{j}(X)},\qquad j\in\{0,\ldots,J\}.

(C.2)

Welfare can then be written compactly as
W(P_{0};G)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\bigl[\sum_{j=0}^{J}Z_{j}\,\mathds{1}\{G(X)=j\}\bigr]
.
18
18

18

When
J=1
this reduces to the binary formulation (
2.1
), since
Z_{1}-Z_{0}=YT/e(X)-Y(1-T)/(1-e(X))
.

The NBPL framework then places a Dirichlet process prior on the reduced-form distribution of
(Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{J},X)
. Given a policy class
\mathcal{G}
, the welfare maximization problem is to solve
\arg\max_{G\in\mathcal{G}}W(P_{0};G)
. In what follows, I consider multi-treatment analogues of linear and decision-tree rules, both of which have finite VC dimension.

A.2.2
Implementation

The EWM problem.

Given an i.i.d. sample
\mathcal{D}_{n}\coloneqq\{(Y_{i},T_{i},X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and a policy class
\mathcal{G}
, the empirical welfare maximization problem is

\widehat{G}\in\arg\max_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{J}Z_{ij}\;\mathds{1}\{G(X_{i})=j\},

(C.3)

where
Z_{ij}\coloneqq Y_{i}\,\mathds{1}\{T_{i}=j\}/e_{j}(X_{i})
.
Since each rule assigns exactly one treatment arm, so that
\sum_{j=0}^{J}\mathds{1}\{G(X_{i})=j\}=1
for every
i
, subtracting the baseline term
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i0}
does not change the optimizer. Hence one may equivalently maximize the welfare contrast

g_{ij}\coloneqq Z_{ij}-Z_{i0},\qquad j\in\{0,\ldots,J\},

with
g_{i0}=0
.

The NBPL problem.

Given
S\geq 1
independent draws of exponential weights
\{\omega_{i}^{[s]}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, with normalized weights

\widetilde{\omega}_{i}^{[s]}\coloneqq\omega_{i}^{[s]}/\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega_{k}^{[s]}
,
Algorithm
1
extends naturally by solving, for each posterior draw
s=1,\ldots,S
,

G^{\star,[s]}\in\arg\max_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\widetilde{\omega}_{i}^{[s]}\,g_{ij}\,\mathds{1}\{G(X_{i})=j\}.

(C.4)

A.2.3
Linear Policy Class

Rule structure.

In the binary case, a linear rule takes the form
G(x)=\mathds{1}\{x^{\top}\beta\geq 0\}
. A natural multi-armed extension assigns

G(x)=\arg\max_{j\in\{0,\ldots,J\}}x^{\top}\beta_{j},

(C.5)

with normalization
\beta_{0}=0
for the status quo arm. The free parameters are
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{J}\in\mathbf{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The resulting treatment regions are the polyhedral cells

\mathcal{R}_{j}=\bigl\{x\in\mathcal{X}:x^{\top}\beta_{j}\geq x^{\top}\beta_{k}\text{ for all }k\neq j\bigr\}.

MILP formulation.

Since
g_{i0}=0
, the sample objective under linear rules can be written as

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{J}\in\mathbf{B}\end{subarray}}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{J}g_{ij}\,\mathds{1}\!\bigl\{G_{\beta}(X_{i})=j\bigr\}.

(C.6)

Introduce binary assignment variables
u_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=0,\ldots,J
, where
u_{ij}
replaces
\mathds{1}\{G_{\beta}(X_{i})=j\}
. Allowing arbitrary tie-breaking among maximizing arms, the problem can be written as the mixed-integer linear program (MILP):

\displaystyle\max_{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{J},\;u}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{J}g_{ij}\,u_{ij}

(C.7)

s.t.

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\top}(\beta_{j}-\beta_{k})\geq-M(1-u_{ij}),\qquad\forall i,\ \forall k\neq j\in\{0,\ldots,J\},

(C.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}u_{ij}=1,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

(C.9)

\displaystyle u_{ij}\in\{0,1\},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,\ \ j=0,\ldots,J.

(C.10)

where
M>0
is a suitably large constant.
19
19

19

Any constant
M
satisfying
M\geq\max_{i}\max_{j\neq k}\sup_{\beta_{j},\beta_{k}\in\mathbf{B}}X_{i}^{\top}(\beta_{k}-\beta_{j})
is valid.
In particular, if
\mathbf{B}=\{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\beta\|_{2}\leq B\}
, then one may take

M=2B\max_{i}\|X_{i}\|_{2}
.
Constraint (
C.8
) ensures that
u_{ij}=1
only if arm
j
attains the largest linear score at
X_{i}
. Constraint (
C.9
) requires each unit to be assigned to exactly one arm.

A.2.4
Tree Policy Class

Rule structure.

A depth-
d
decision tree partitions
\mathcal{X}
into
L=2^{d}
leaves through axis-aligned splits on the covariates. Each leaf
\ell
is assigned a treatment arm
a_{\ell}\in\{0,\ldots,J\}
, so that

G(x)=a_{\ell}\qquad\text{if }x\in\mathrm{Leaf}_{\ell}.

In the binary case, there are
2^{L}
possible leaf labelings; with
J+1
treatment arms, there are
(J+1)^{L}
.

There are two natural ways to solve the empirical optimization problem for tree-based policies: one may either fix the tree partition in advance and optimize only the leaf labels, or jointly optimize over both splits and leaf labels.

Optimization with fixed splits.

Given a fixed tree structure—for example, with splits chosen by Classification and Regression Tree (CART) on a pilot conditional average treatment effect (CATE) estimate—the optimal leaf labels solve:

a_{\ell}^{\star}\in\arg\max_{j\in\{0,\ldots,J\}}\sum_{i:\,X_{i}\in\mathrm{Leaf}_{\ell}}Z_{ij},\qquad\ell=1,\ldots,L.

Thus, conditional on the partition, the problem decomposes into
L
independent maximization problems over
J+1
treatment arms.

Joint optimization over splits and leaf labels.

Following
Zhou et al. 2023
, one may jointly optimize over the tree structure and leaf labels. Let
r_{i\ell}\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether observation
i
falls in leaf
\ell
, and let
w_{\ell j}\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether leaf
\ell
is assigned arm
j
, with
\sum_{j=0}^{J}w_{\ell j}=1
for each
\ell
. The resulting mixed-integer formulation has objective

\displaystyle\max\,\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{j=0}^{J}Z_{ij}\,r_{i\ell}\,w_{\ell j}

(C.11)

subject to constraints encoding the tree splits and the assignment and labeling structure. The bilinear term
r_{i\ell}w_{\ell j}
can be linearized using standard techniques, yielding an MILP.

Capacity constraints.

If arm
j
is subject to a capacity constraint
\mathbb{P}(G(X)=j)\leq q_{j}
, the sample analogue adds the linear constraints

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}r_{i\ell}w_{\ell j}\leq q_{j},\qquad j=1,\ldots,J.

(1)

This is the direct multi-arm analogue of the single capacity constraint in the binary case.

Appendix C
Posterior Sampling Under a General Base Measure

I provide an algorithm for sampling posterior draws from a Dirichlet process prior with arbitrary base measure, based on the stick-breaking representation
(
Ghosal and Van der Vaart 2017
, see, e.g., Section 3.2.2 of)
.

Definition 3

(Stick-breaking Representation of DP)
.

Let
\beta>0
denote the concentration parameter and let
H
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{D}
. Let
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Beta}(1,\beta)
and
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}H
. Define the weights

\displaystyle p_{1}=V_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{j}=V_{j}\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}(1-V_{i}),\quad j\geq 2.

Then the (discrete) random probability measure
P=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}\delta_{\mu_{i}}

follows a Dirichlet process with concentration parameter
\beta
and base distribution
H
, denoted
P\sim\mathrm{DP}(\beta H)
.

Algorithm 2

Sampling from
\mathrm{DP}(\alpha+n\mathbb{P}_{n})
via Truncated Stick-Breaking

1:

Input:
Data
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, base measure
\alpha
, truncation level
K
.

2:

Set

M_{n}:=\alpha(\mathcal{D})+n,\qquad H_{n}(\cdot):=\frac{\alpha(\cdot)+n\mathbb{P}_{n}(\cdot)}{M_{n}}.

3:

for

k=1,\ldots,K-1

do

4:

Draw
V_{k}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,M_{n})
.

5:

Set

p_{k}:=V_{k}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k-1}(1-V_{\ell}).

6:

D.7
Proof of Proposition
1

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Since
W_{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}(P)
does not depend on
G
, minimizing posterior expected loss is equivalent to maximizing posterior mean welfare:

\arg\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\int L(P;G)\,d\Pi(P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})=\arg\max_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\int W(P;G)\,d\Pi(P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}).

Let

f_{G}(D)=\left(\frac{YT}{e(X)}-\frac{Y(1-T)}{1-e(X)}\right)\mathds{1}\{X\in G\},

so that
W(P;G)=\int f_{G}\,dP
.

Now suppose
P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathrm{DP}(n\mathbb{P}_{n})
. A basic property of the Dirichlet process is that if
P\sim\mathrm{DP}(\nu)
, then for any integrable measurable function
f

(
Ghosal and Van der Vaart 2017
, see e.g., Proposition 4.3 of)
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int f\,dP\right]=\int f\,d\bar{\nu},\qquad\bar{\nu}\coloneqq\frac{\nu}{\nu(\mathcal{D})}.

Applying this with
\nu=n\mathbb{P}_{n}
, whose normalized version is simply
\mathbb{P}_{n}
, yields

\int W(P;G)\,d\Pi(P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})=\int\left(\int f_{G}\,dP\right)\,d\Pi(P\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})=\int f_{G}\,d\mathbb{P}_{n}=W(\mathbb{P}_{n};G).

Thus posterior mean welfare coincides with empirical welfare for every
G\in\mathcal{G}
, so the maximizers coincide.

Proof of Lemma

The result follows from Theorem 14.37 (with

Step 1: Gamma Process Representation.

Let
\mu_{n}:=\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{D_{i}}
, so that
P\mid\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\mathrm{DP}(\mu_{n})

By the definition of the Dirichlet process via a normalized Gamma random measure, I have:

\displaystyle P(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{\widetilde{G}(\cdot)}{\widetilde{G}(\mathcal{D})},

where
\widetilde{G}
is a Gamma random measure on
\mathcal{D}
with base measure
\mu_{n}
. For any integrable
h

Ph\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\widetilde{G}h/\widetilde{G}(\mathcal{D})

Using the additivity property of Gamma random measures, I decompose
\widetilde{G}
based on the structure of

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}=G_{\alpha}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}\delta_{D_{i}},

where, conditional on
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

•

G_{\alpha}
is a Gamma random measure with base measure
\alpha
,

•

J_{1},\dots,J_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Gamma}(1,1)=\mathrm{Exp}(1)

•

G_{\alpha}
and
\{J_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are mutually independent.

Step 2: Algebraic Decomposition.

Let
T:=G_{\alpha}(\mathcal{D})
and
S:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}

\displaystyle T\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(M,1),\quad S\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(n,1),\quad T\perp\!\!\!\perp S.

V_{n}:=T/(T+S)
. By the relationship between Gamma and Beta distributions,
V_{n}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(M,n)
. I then rewrite the integral
Ph

\displaystyle Ph

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{G_{\alpha}h+\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}h(D_{i})}{T+S}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{T}{T+S}\right)\frac{G_{\alpha}h}{T}+\left(\frac{S}{T+S}\right)\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}h(D_{i})}{S}

\displaystyle=V_{n}\cdot Qh+(1-V_{n})\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}h(D_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}},

where
Q:=G_{\alpha}/T
. By the construction of the DP,

.

To establish the final representation, I verify the mutual independence of the underlying components:

•

Q\perp\!\!\!\perp T
:
By Lukacs’s Theorem, the normalized Gamma measure
Q
is independent of its total mass
T=G_{\alpha}(\mathcal{D})
.

•

\mathbf{D}\perp\!\!\!\perp S
:
For the i.i.d. Gamma variables
\{J_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

\mathbf{D}\coloneqq(J_{1}/S,\dots,J_{n}/S)
is independent of their sum
S
.

•

(Q,T)\perp\!\!\!\perp(\mathbf{D},S)

\mathbf{D}

Proof of Lemma
4
.

The proof proceeds in three steps.

By the triangle inequality and the bound
|f(D_{i})|\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|f(D_{i})|
, I have for any
f\in\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{n}V_{n}\left(Qf-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}f(D_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{n}V_{n}|Qf|+\sqrt{n}V_{n}\left|\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}f(D_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{n}V_{n}|Qf|+\sqrt{n}V_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|f(D_{i})|\right).

Now, taking the supremum over
\mathcal{F}
on both sides, and noting that
|f(D_{i})|\leq F(D_{i})
where
F(D)\coloneqq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|f(D)|
is the envelope function, I obtain:

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\text{LHS}\right|\leq\sqrt{n}V_{n}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|Qf|\right)+\sqrt{n}V_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}F(D_{i})\right).

To establish the result, it suffices to show that as
n\to\infty
, for
P_{0}^{\infty}
-almost every realization of
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, both terms on the right-hand side are
o_{P}(1)
.

Step 2: Control of the Base Measure Term.

First, consider the scaling of
V_{n}
. Since
V_{n}\sim\text{Beta}(|\alpha|,n)
, its first two moments are:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{n}]=\frac{|\alpha|}{|\alpha|+n}=O(n^{-1}),\quad\text{var}(V_{n})=\frac{|\alpha|n}{(|\alpha|+n)^{2}(|\alpha|+n+1)}=O(n^{-2}).

By Chebyshev’s inequality,
V_{n}=O_{P}(n^{-1})
, which implies
\sqrt{n}V_{n}=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.

Next, I bound the supremum of the Dirichlet Process term. By the definition of

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|Qf|\leq Q\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|f|\right)=QF.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{Q\sim\text{DP}(\alpha)}[QF]=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[F]/|\alpha|<\infty
, I have
QF<\infty
, a.s.-
\mathrm{DP}(\alpha)
. Thus:

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}V_{n}\cdot(QF)=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})\cdot O_{\text{a.s.}}(1)=o_{P}(1).

Step 3: Control of the Empirical Term.

To control the second term, I analyze the asymptotic behavior of
n^{-1/2}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}F(D_{i})
.

Under the condition
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[F^{2}]<\infty
, I apply a standard result for the growth of maxima of i.i.d. random variables (e.g., Lemma 5 of
Ray and van der vaart 2021
).
Specifically, if
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with
\mathbb{E}|X_{i}|^{r}<\infty
, then
n^{-1/r}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|X_{i}|\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}0
.

By setting

and

, I have that for
P_{0}^{\infty}
-almost every realization of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}F(D_{i})\to 0.

Combining this with the scaling of

established in Step 2, I have:

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}V_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}F(D_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=(nV_{n})\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}F(D_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=O_{P}(1)\cdot o_{\text{a.s.}}(1)=o_{P}(1).

Combining the results from Step 2 and Step 3, the proof of (

) is complete.

, Lemma 3.7.6,)
, I have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{Z}}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\leq 4\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{N}}\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{N}_{i}\delta_{D_{i}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}.

Step 2: Multiplier Inequality.

In the above equation, I may replace
\tilde{N}_{i}
by
|\tilde{N}_{i}\varepsilon_{i}|
since
\varepsilon_{i}
are i.i.d Rademacher. Then, applying a conditional multiplier inequality (Lemma
5
) with
\tilde{Z}_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{D_{i}}
,
\xi_{i}=|\tilde{N}_{1}|
, and
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})
fixed yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{Z}}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\lVert\tilde{N}_{i}\rVert_{2,1}\cdot\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,R}\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{D_{R_{i}}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{F}},

where
R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
are i.i.d. indices drawn uniformly from
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Notice that for a fixed
k
, the expectation over the indices
R
represents the average over all possible subsamples of size
k
from
\{D_{1},\dots,D_{n}\}
.
It can be shown that
\sqrt{n/k}\lVert\tilde{N}_{i}\rVert_{2,1}\leq 2\sqrt{2}
for all
n,k
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the
\sigma
-field generated by all functions
f:\mathcal{X}^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}
that are symmetric in their first
n
coordinates (i.e., the symmetric
\sigma
-field). By symmetry of the i.i.d. sequence, I have the identity:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,R}\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{D_{R_{i}}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathbb{E}(U_{k}\mid\mathcal{S}_{n}),

where
U_{k}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\lVert k^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{D_{i}}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}
is the Rademacher complexity of the first
k
elements of the infinite sequence.

Step 3: Convergence via Hewitt-Savage.

By Jensen’s inequality and monotonicity of
\max_{1\leq k\leq n}U_{k}
in
n
, I have:

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{E}(U_{k}\mid\mathcal{S}_{n})\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq n}U_{k}\mid\mathcal{S}_{n}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{k\geq 1}U_{k}\mid\mathcal{S}_{n}\right).

The random variable
\sup_{k}U_{k}
is integrable
(
van der Vaart and Wellner 2023
, e.g., Lemma 2.9.9 of )
.

The sequence of symmetric
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{S}_{n}
decreases to the tail symmetric
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{S}=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{S}_{n}
. By the Reverse Martingale Convergence Theorem:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{k\geq 1}U_{k}\mid\mathcal{S}_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\to}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{k\geq 1}U_{k}\mid\mathcal{S}\right).

By the Hewitt-Savage zero-one law, the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{S}
is trivial for an i.i.d. sequence. Thus, the conditional expectation collapses to the constant expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{k\geq 1}U_{k}\mid\mathcal{S}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{k\geq 1}U_{k}\right).

Combined with the constant
2\sqrt{2}
from Step 2, this completes the proof.

Proof of Lemma
9
.

Step 1: Sub-Gaussian Property of the Rademacher Process.

I fix a realization of the sequence
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and condition on it throughout. For a fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define the stochastic process
\{X_{k}(f):f\in\mathcal{F}\}
indexed by
\mathcal{F}
as:

\displaystyle X_{k}(f)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{i}f(D_{i}).

By Hoeffding’s inequality (Lemma
10
), the process
\{X_{k}(f):f\in\mathcal{F}\}
is
sub-Gaussian
with respect to the
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{k})
-seminorm. Specifically, for any
x>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\left(|X_{k}(f)-X_{k}(g)|>x\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2d_{k}^{2}(f,g)}\right),

where
d_{k}(f,g)\coloneqq\|f-g\|_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2}=\sqrt{k^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(f(D_{i})-g(D_{i}))^{2}}
. This sub-Gaussian increment condition holds for
P_{0}^{\infty}
-almost every realization of
\{D_{i}\}
.

Step 2: Maximal Inequality and Entropy Integral.

By the maximal inequality for sub-Gaussian processes (Lemma
14
), there exists a universal constant
K
(independent of the sample size and the specific realization of the data) such that for any
f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\|X_{k}\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}|X_{k}(f_{0})|+K\int_{0}^{\text{diam}_{k}(\mathcal{F})}\sqrt{\log N(\eta,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{k}))}\,d\eta,

(A.21)

where
N(\eta,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{k}))
is the
\eta
-covering number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to the
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{k})
-seminorm. The diameter of the class is bounded by the
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{k})
-norm of the envelope function,
\text{diam}_{k}(\mathcal{F})\leq\|F\|_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2}
. Without loss of generality, I assume
0\in\mathcal{F}
(corresponding to a null policy), which implies
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}|X_{k}(0)|=0
.

Applying the change of variables
\zeta\coloneqq\eta/\|F\|_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2}
, I rewrite the bound as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\|X_{k}\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leq(K\lVert F\rVert_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2})\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log N(\zeta\lVert F\rVert_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2},\mathcal{F},L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{k}))}\,d\zeta.

(A.22)

To ensure this bound holds for
P_{0}^{\infty}
-almost every realization of
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, I verify that the envelope norm is finite. Since
\|F\|_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2}^{2}=k^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}F(D_{i})^{2}
and I assume
P_{0}F^{2}<\infty
(implied by Assumption
1
), the Strong Law of Large Numbers implies:

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2}^{2}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}P_{0}F^{2}<\infty.

Step 3: VC Covering Number Bound.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is a VC-subgraph class with VC-dimension
v<\infty
, I apply the universal covering number bound for VC classes (Lemma
15
). For any probability measure
Q
such that
\|F\|_{Q,2}>0
, there exists a universal constant
M
such that for every
0<\eta<1
:

\displaystyle N(\eta\|F\|_{Q,2},\mathcal{F},L_{2}(Q))\leq Mv(16e)^{v}\left(\frac{1}{\eta}\right)^{2v}.

Setting
Q=\mathbb{P}_{k}
(the empirical measure of
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
), I take the logarithm of this bound:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\log N(\eta\|F\|_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2},\mathcal{F},L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{k}))}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\log(Mv)+v\log(16e)+2v\log(1/\eta)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{v}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log(Mv)}{v}+\log(16e)+2\log(1/\eta)}.

Note that
M
is a universal constant independent of
k
,
v
, and the realization
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Substituting this into equation (
A.22
) gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\|X_{k}\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leq(K\|F\|_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2})\sqrt{v}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log(Mv)/v+\log(16e)+2\log(1/\eta)}\,d\eta.

The integral on the right-hand side is a finite and bounded by a constant independent of
k
and
v
, since
\log(Mv)/v
is bounded for
v\geq 1
and
\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log(1/\eta)}d\eta<\infty
.

Finally, as established in Step 2,
\|F\|_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2}
converges
P_{0}^{\infty}
-almost surely to a finite constant. By absorbing the integral, the envelope norm
\|F\|_{\mathbb{P}_{k},2}
, and the constant
K
into a single universal constant
C
, I conclude that for
P_{0}^{\infty}
-almost every realization of
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{D_{i}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\leq C\sqrt{v}.

This completes the proof.

Proof of Lemma
