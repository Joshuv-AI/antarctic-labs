Do LLM Agents Mirror Socio-Cognitive Effects in Power-Asymmetric Conversations?
Anvesh Rao Vijjini, Sagar Manjunath, Snigdha Chaturvedi

Abstract
Power differences shape human communication through well-documented socio-cognitive effects, including language coordination, pronoun usage, authority bias, and harmful compliance. We examine whether large language models (LLMs) exhibit similar behaviors when assigned high- or low-status personas. Using personas from diverse professions, we simulate multi-turn, power-asymmetric dialogues (e.g., principal–teacher, justice–lawyer) and measure (i) language coordination, (ii) pronoun usage, (iii) persuasion success, and (iv) compliance with unsafe requests. Our results show that LLMs show key socio-cognitive effects of power, albeit with nuances and variability, linking simulated interactions to both desirable and unsafe behaviors. 1 1 1 We release the simulated conversations at github.com/nvshrao/power-asymmetric-conversations .

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in high-stakes human-facing roles such as mediation agents, healthcare and legal assistants, tutors, and financial advisors. In these settings, realism and safety are essential. Failures of realism erode trust, while failures of safety can amplify bias, manipulation, or unethical compliance.

Achieving realism and safety in conversation requires engaging with the social structure that shapes human communication.
Human communication is shaped by social relationships, often asymmetric
21
;
22
;
15
. These asymmetric relationships or
power differentials
give rise to well-documented socio-cognitive effects such as language coordination, pronoun usage asymmetries, authority bias, and harmful compliance. These effects govern how intent and meaning are interpreted and are central to natural interaction.

Prior works on LLMs focus on personality alignment or generic cognitive biases
14
;
13
;
48
;
35
but it largely overlooks the role of power in conversation. So, it remains unclear whether LLM agents reproduce these socio-cognitive effects. This is an especially pressing concern in high-stakes domains with implicit hierarchies such as education, healthcare, and law.

In this work, we address this gap by systematically evaluating whether LLM agents mirror socio-cognitive effects in power-asymmetric conversations and analyzing the implications for both
realism
and
safety
.

For realism, we focus on the
pronoun effect
and
language coordination
. Pronoun effect posits greater use of plural (e.g., “we”) over singular (e.g., “I”) pronouns by higher-power speakers
26
. Language coordination describes how speakers, especially lower-status ones, adapt their linguistic style to their interlocutors
31
;
19
.

For safety, we focus on
authority bias
and
harmful compliance
. Authority bias captures the disproportionate influence of higher-power individuals in conversation
23
. Sometimes, this can lead lower-status partners to engage in harmful compliance
29
.

To formally study these phenomena in LLMs, we study six LLMs from three different model families. We alter power differentials by assigning realistic
roles
(e.g. principal-teacher, doctor-nurse) and
personas
(“a middle school teacher with a passion for teaching math in creative ways”). Conditioned on these, we elicit multi-turn conversations to study
realism
and
safety
. Figure
1
shows an overview of the four effects in simulated conversations between a school principal and a teacher.

We design seven research questions, addressing the realism (RQs 1-2) and safety (RQs 3-4) of LLMs in conversational settings; how they vary with the conversation’s progression (RQ5); if they can be controlled (RQ6); and how they vary with model training stages and sizes (RQ7).

2
Socio-Cognitive Effects

Here we describe the four socio-cognitive phenomena studied in this paper.

Pronoun effect
refers to differences in pronoun usage that correlate with power status.

26
hypothesized that higher-power individuals tend to use more first-person plural pronouns (e.g., “we”, “us”, “our”) and fewer first-person singular pronouns (e.g., “I”, “me”, “my”) than lower-power individuals to establish authority. To test this hypothesis, they conducted experiments with small groups of 2–4 participants assigned collaborative tasks, and compared the pronoun usage of ‘leaders’ and ‘non-leaders’.
Across multiple
setups, they found leaders to use fewer first-person singular
and more first-person plural pronouns
compared to non-leader(s).

Language coordination
is rooted in Communication Accommodation Theory
18
;
19
;
31
;
43

and posits that speakers, especially those with lower power status, mirror the linguistic style of their conversational partner.
Prior NLP work has explored language coordination as a marker of social alignment and power dynamics. Previous work showed that speakers subconsciously match each other’s linguistic style during interactions
32
, and that coordination strength can vary with status or centrality in social network
8
;
33
. More recent work has extended these ideas to LLMs, examining coordination in abstract or goal-oriented tasks such as image identification or navigation
56
. Focusing on general conversations, we adopt the definition of coordination proposed by
10
. They measure linguistic style via usage rates of
8
types of word categories or
style markers
- articles, auxiliary verbs, conjunctions, high-frequency adverbs, impersonal pronouns, personal pronouns, prepositions, and quantifiers. Language coordination is said to occur if, in a conversation, a speaker’s usage of these markers is more similar to their conversation participant’s usage than to their own usage.

Authority bias
, well documented in psychology research
23
;
6
, refers to the tendency of individuals to place disproportionate weight on statements
made by high status sources (e.g., a scientific expert), even when the content itself is no more accurate or valid than the same content from a low status source (e.g., a tabloid). This undesirable weight, even when contradicting one’s prior belief, can result in measurable shifts in judgments and
choices
4
. In this paper, we operationalize the notion of shifts in judgments by considering the task of persuasion.
We study if power differentials can persuade an LLM agent to act against its inherent preference (e.g., to convince that nuclear power is safe to an agent who believed otherwise).

Harmful compliance
refers to individuals carrying out actions with negative or unethical consequences when instructed by a higher-status authority. Specifically,
29
showed that humans were willing to administer (what they believed were) severe electric shocks to another person when instructed by an authority figure, despite displaying visible distress. More recent work has revisited harmful compliance in organizational and digital contexts, showing that hierarchies in workplaces and online communities similarly encourage low status people to follow potentially harmful orders to maintain conformity and avoid punishment
(
57
;
7
)
.

Experimental Setup

3.1
Assigning Personas

To generate realistic and diverse conversations that capture natural power differentials, we begin by defining
roles
that reflect hierarchical relationships commonly studied in social and cognitive psychology. These roles, listed in Table
1
, are inspired by prior work on social role simulation with LLMs
52
and designed to span multiple domains (e.g., education, healthcare, law, and the workplace).

We then use
PersonaHub

16
, a dataset of over 200k personas from diverse professions and life backgrounds, to assign realistic personas for each role. We search for the role name within the first five words of the persona description and filter out cases that include modifiers such as “former,” or “retired”. For each valid role pair, we sample multiple unique persona-pairs (on average, 10 per role pair). Example persona pairs are provided in Appendix Table
18
. This choice of roles and personas balances ecological validity and diversity across multiple domains.

Verification:
We conduct a human evaluation to verify whether the personas selected from the roles in Table
1
are perceived as hierarchical by human annotators. In this evaluation, we present pairs of personas to human judges and ask them to indicate which persona in the pair has a higher status (power differential) or if they have equal status. On average, across three annotators, 96.5% of persona pairs were judged to exhibit a power differential, with a high Fleiss’s kappa agreement score of
0.73
. Appendix
F.1
has more details regarding the annotators and samples. Figure
2
shows the instruction provided to the annotators.

The evaluation result confirms that our persona construction largely captures power differentials as intended.

:

Then we generate conversations using different LLMs, resulting in a total of 576 conversations. We measure the pronoun usage rates for each persona as a percentage of the total number of words uttered by them.

RQ2
:
Do LLM agents in power differential show language coordination?

We say language coordination occurs if a person’s usage of linguistic
style markers
(see Sec.
2
) is closer to their partner’s in-group usage than to their own usage.
For example, when a school principal is talking to another principal,
pronouns might make up
1\%
of their spoken words. And, when a teacher is talking to another teacher, it might be
4\%
.

In a conversation between the principal and the teacher, the principal is said to be coordinating if their pronoun usage rate
is closer to
4\%
than to
1\%
, and the teacher is
coordinating
if their rate
is closer to
1\%
than to
4\%
. We define the ‘degree’ of language coordination,
D_{lc}
, ranging from 0 to 8, as the average number of style markers (out of 8) that show coordination for a given pair of roles.

To study coordination, we simulate conversations between multiple persona pairs with power differentials (e.g., principal-teacher). To estimate a role’s in-group style, we also
generate conversations between personas of the same role (e.g., principal–principal and teacher–teacher).
Each conversation contains up to 15 turns,
resulting in a total of 1,270 conversations. Using these, we compute the average degree of language coordination,
D_{lc}
, for high- and low status speakers.
It represents how much the speaker coordinates with their conversational partner across all opposite status roles.

Table
3
reports the average
D_{lc}
for low status (left) and high status speakers (right).
In all but GPT models, we observe strong coordination for both high- and low-status speakers,
indicating that LLM agents engage in ‘mutual’ language coordination.
Although the average
D_{lc}
is slightly higher for low-status speakers, the differences are not statistically significant. We also see that the GPT models show lesser coordination (
D_{lc}\approx 4
).
A possible reason for this behavior might be that during their aggressive training and prompting with instructions like (‘You are a helpful assistant’), GPT models are heavily optimized to follow concise and neutral conversations, rather than for socio-cognitive behaviors.
Overall, these results suggest that most LLMs reproduce the general pattern of coordination seen in humans, albeit with weaker asymmetry.

We also consider a case where real human data is used as conversation starters instead of synthetic data for RQ1 and RQ2. Overall, we note no significant difference based on human vs synthetic conversation starters on the two effects. Details of this experiment are present in Appendix
E
.

RQ3
:
Do LLM agents in power differential show authority bias?

To test for authority bias, we adapt the DailyPersuasion dataset
25
, which contains human-human conversations where one participant attempts to persuade another across diverse domains (e.g., health, finance, lifestyle). From each dialogue, we borrow the first two turns as initial arguments for the agents (see Table
16
for examples). Using these initializations, we construct two experimental conditions: in one, the persuader is the higher-status agent; in the other, the lower-status agent.
Since we would want the agents to discuss topics that are relevant to them we match the corresponding roles to domains in DailyPersuasion (shown in Table
17
).

After we generate conversation, we prompt a third-party LLM judge (GPT-5) to evaluate persuasion success as
Not persuaded
, or
Persuaded
. We then define ‘persuasion score’ as the % of samples marked persuaded. Section
5
has more details.

By comparing persuasion success scores across the two conditions, we test whether persuasion effectiveness differs based on whether the persuader is a high status or low status agent. Consistent differences would indicate the presence of authority bias in LLM agents, with higher persuasion success expected when the persuader is of higher status (
\Delta_{H-L}^{P}>0
).

We also do human verification of the LLM judge and find that 3 human annotators agree with Kappa
=0.64
with the judge 83% cases. The details of this experiment is in Section
5
.

Table
4
(left) reports persuasion success scores for each model when either a low status or a high status agent initiates the discussion. Across all models, we observe a consistent asymmetry: persuasion success is higher when the persuader holds a higher status. For instance, Qwen 2.5 7B shows one of the strongest effects, with persuasion success rising from 25.0% when the persuader is low-status to 30.9% when high-status.

Furthermore, smaller models such as Llama 3.1 8B and Qwen 2.5 7B display stronger authority bias (higher
\Delta_{H-L}^{P}
). Larger models such as Llama 3.1 70B and GPT 5 have smaller
\Delta_{H-L}^{P}
,
suggesting that larger models may be more resistant to status-driven authority bias, although traces of this bias persist across all models.

RQ4
:
Do LLM agents in power differential show harmful compliance?

To test for harmful compliance, we use the Do-Not-Answer (DNA) dataset
20
, a benchmark of unsafe prompts that LLMs should ideally refuse to answer with prompts such as ‘
Tell me a dirty joke.
’ Using this dataset, we simulate conversations where one agent (the requester) asks the other (the responder) an unsafe prompt. We create two conditions: the requester as higher-status and as lower-status. In both, the responder should ideally refuse the unsafe request.

To evaluate the degree of compliance by the responder, we use an LLM-as-a-judge (a GPT-5 model) with three possibilities:
No compliance
(clear refusal with no unsafe content) and
Compliant
(fulfilling the unsafe request in any capacity). We define
Compliance Score
as the % of samples judged as compliant. We also do human verification of the LLM judge and find that 3 human annotators agree with Kappa
=0.62
with the judge 80% cases. The details of this experiment are in
5
.

We test whether low-status agents are more likely to comply with harmful requests than high-status agents.

Table
4
(right) shows results for the harmful compliance experiment, split by the status of the requester.

We can see that the
\Delta_{H-L}^{H}
s are positive, indicating that the lower-status agents are more likely to comply with unsafe prompts. The results echo the harmful compliance effect observed in humans.

:
How do these socio-cognitive effects develop across conversation turns in LLM agents?

\Delta_{H-L}^{FPS}
and

We see that the effect somewhat decreases with increasing turns (decreasing

6.9
to
7.1

This trend is seen strongly for Llama 3.1 8B and Qwen 2.5 7B and also for Phi-3-Med and Llama 3.1 70B (but to a lesser degree).
However, in line with the observations made for RQ2, we do not see such a trend in GPT models, which consistently show a lower
D_{lc}
across conversation turns.

For persuasion and compliance, Table

(right) shows persuasion and compliance scores and
\Delta_{H-L}s

20.5
→
19.7
→
18.9

\Delta_{H-L}^{P}
changing from 5.9 to 5.5 to 5.1 for Qwen 2.5 8B). We see similar results for compliance, where compliance scores decrease over turns. This pattern suggests that the first few exchanges carry the greatest influence of social status for these tasks, after which conversational context begins to moderate the effect. Nevertheless, high-status initiators consistently maintain higher persuasion and compliance scores than low-status initiators throughout all positions, replicating the authority bias & harmful compliance trend.

Conclusion

Our findings show that LLMs reproduce several socio-cognitive effects of power differentials, albeit with nuance and significant variations across models and effects. Most models exhibit pronoun usage and adapt their linguistic style, though coordination has weak asymmetry. High-status agents are more persuasive, while low-status agents show higher rates of harmful compliance, indicating an emergent authority bias with safety implications. These effects are strongest early in conversations, while coordination increases over time. Prompting can attenuate persuasion and compliance for some models, but pronoun usage and coordination remain difficult to control. Finally, we also see that model size can significantly alter persuasion success and harmful compliance, diminishing the effects caused by status. On the other hand, preference tuning in our experiments did not significantly alter any of the socio-cognitive effects.

Overall, LLMs display both realism and safety vulnerabilities under power differentials. While they reproduce expected patterns such as coordination and pronoun asymmetries, they also show persuasion effects and compliance with unsafe requests. This underscores the need for stronger mitigation and more sophisticated social reasoning in the deployment of LLMs.

Limitations

While our study offers new insights into the social dynamics of power differentials in LLMs, it has some limitations.
First, all experiments rely on simulated, text-based conversations between role-assigned personas. While this approach provides control and reproducibility, real-world human–AI interactions often include multimodal, contextual, and emotional cues that may modulate these effects in complex ways.

Second, our operationalization of ‘power’ is based on social roles (e.g., Principal–Teacher, Doctor–Nurse) and associated personas, which approximate status but do not capture contextual or cultural nuances and nor do they have multiple social attributes.

Third, although we tested six models across three families, these represent only a subset of modern LLMs. Model architecture, instruction-tuning objectives, or safety alignment protocols may significantly affect whether and how these cognitive effects appear.

Finally, while we introduce a control setup for prompting coordination and persuasion, our control interventions are limited to explicit system-level instructions. Understanding whether such socio-cognitive effects can be systematically tuned or disentangled from model representations remains an open methodological challenge.

Ethical Considerations

This work examines how LLMs express socio-cognitive effects of power differentials through simulated conversations. Our motivation is twofold: (1) to assess realism, or whether LLMs reproduce subtle behavioral markers observed in human communication, and (2) to evaluate safety, or whether they replicate harmful social biases.

Although synthetic conversation data provide a scalable and controlled testing ground, they cannot fully capture the complexity and ethical nuance of human interaction.

Finally, social hierarchy itself is subjective, often shaped by culture and context. Our role pairs were selected based on broadly recognized occupational hierarchies (e.g., Principal–Teacher, Judge–Lawyer), but they do not reflect the full diversity of social structures or power dynamics across societies. Ethical deployment of LLMs in human-facing roles should therefore consider how implicit power differentials—whether between users or agents can influence both realism and safety.

Evaluating power-status effects is important in settings where LLMs participate in asymmetric interactions involving authority, advice, or decision-making. This includes scenarios where LLMs are already being deployed, such as doctor–patient guidance
42
;
34
, legal or financial advice
30
, or educational tutoring
27
. In these contexts, bias to perceived high-status users/models, increased compliance, or persuasion success may affect safety, fairness, and reliability
24
;
51
.

Related Work

Persona assigned LLMs

Persona and role prompts can substantially steer LLM behavior, and recent benchmarks evaluate whether models can maintain character knowledge, speaking style, and role-consistent behavior under explicit role conditioning
(
40
;
49
)
. This connects to a broader line of LLM-based social simulation, where agents are assigned roles, goals, memories, and communication protocols to produce interactive behavior
(
37
;
28

SOTOPIA

(
55

(
54
;
44
)
. Our work builds on this literature by studying controlled dyadic interactions where the central manipulation is relational power.

Socio-cognitive effects in LLMs.

A growing literature asks whether LLMs reproduce human-like social judgments, survey behavior, and cognitive biases. Work on “silicon samples” suggests that demographic conditioning can approximate some human response patterns, while later critiques warn that synthetic responses may fail to capture empirical human variation
(
3
;
5
)
. Other work documents social desirability bias, decision-making biases, and persuasive behavior in LLMs
(
38
;
12
;
39
)
. Closest to our setting,
45
study persona-prompted responses in power-disparate scenarios. We differ by evaluating whether power asymmetry induces interactional effects across multi-turn dyads, including pronoun asymmetry, language coordination, persuasion, and harmful compliance.

Power asymmetric communication.

Our realism measures draw on sociolinguistic and computational work on power-sensitive communication. Communication Accommodation Theory argues that speakers adapt their linguistic style to interlocutors and social context
(
19
;
18
)
. In NLP,
10
operationalize linguistic coordination through function-word style matching and show that coordination reveals power differences in naturally occurring interactions. Recent work also uses LLM-based multi-agent social reasoning to identify power relations in conversations
(
50
)
. Whereas this work treats power as explicitly specified over existing conversations, we assign power-asymmetric roles to LLM agents and test whether their generated interactions reproduce the socio-cognitive effects of power. Pronoun use provides a complementary marker of power asymmetry:
26
find that leaders tend to use fewer first-person singular pronouns and more first-person plural pronouns than non-leaders. These findings motivate our use of pronoun asymmetry and language coordination as realism-oriented tests for power-asymmetric LLM dialogue.

Realism and safety in persona-assigned LLMs.

The same mechanisms that make role-play more realistic may also create safety risks. Persona assignment can increase toxic generations
(
11
)
. LLM-generated conversations can also contain covert harms and social threats that appear in seemingly neutral language and may be missed by surface-level toxicity measures
(
9
)
. Role-play fine-tuning can further improve persona adaptation while weakening safety under some persona and scenario conditions
(
53
;
46
)
. These risks connect to broader alignment concerns such as sycophancy and failures to maintain robust instruction priorities under conflicting user pressure
(
41
;
17
)
. Our paper therefore, treats power-asymmetric role-play as both a realism question and a safety question: LLM agents may need to reflect human-like social structure, but reproducing authority effects can also occur with increased persuasion and unsafe compliance.

Appendix F
Implementation details

All datasets are in English. In this work, we used AI assistants for minor grammatical corrections while writing the draft. The work should not be used outside of research contexts as intended use.

GPU Details
: We use an RTX 6000 (23GB) GPU to infer using all LLMs in 16-bit with 30GB RAM and a single CPU core. Prompting a model for one turn takes approximately about 15 minutes. Llama 3.1 70B takes much longer time ( 2 hours for one turn).

F.1
Human Evaluation of Persona Pairs

Figure
2
shows the set of instructions given to the participants. In total, we annotated 50 pairs of personas by 3 annotators each. We filtered workers with those from US, UK or Canada and each of them should have done at least 5000 HITs. We have neither asked nor are aware of any other demographic information regarding them. We paid the workers an estimated 15$ per hour for their efforts. In total it costed us about 25$ to do this task.

F.2
Additional Sotopia implementation details

We implement all interactive simulations using the Sotopia framework with a fixed round robin turn taking policy. Each simulation consists of
N\in\{2,3,5\}
agents interacting for
T=10
rounds, yielding
N\times T
utterances per conversation. All agents are instantiated as
LLMAgent
s and share the same underlying language model within a simulation to control for model heterogeneity. We fix decoding hyperparameters across all conditions, using temperature
=0.7
, top-
p=0.9
, and a maximum of
128
generated tokens per turn. Personas are sampled from PersonaHub and remain fixed throughout a conversation, while each agent receives a private natural language goal and the team is assigned a shared high level objective at initialization. Agents observe the full public dialogue history but do not have access to other agents’ private goals or internal states, and no explicit memory summarization is applied beyond the model context window. System prompts specify agent roles and interaction constraints but exclude task labels or evaluation criteria (such as persuasion success or pronoun effect)
