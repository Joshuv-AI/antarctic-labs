FDA Approval of Donanemab‐azbt: A New Dawn in Alzheimer's Disease Treatment
Authors: Rumaisa Riaz, Marrium Fatima, Sadia Ramzan, Diya Rathi, Filzah Fatima, Maha Farooq, Ayesha Shaukat, Nawal Khaliq, Aymar Akilimali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlzheimer's disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative condition marked by the accumulation of beta‐amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, leading to neuronal death and cognitive decline. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChEIs) such as donepezil, galantamine, and rivastigmine are commonly used to enhance cognitive function by increasing acetylcholine levels, but they can cause side effects like nausea, bradycardia, and headaches. NMDA receptor antagonists, like memantine, reduce glutamatergic activity and are used to manage symptoms, yet are also associated with adverse effects including dizziness and agitation. Recently, monoclonal antibodies such as aducanumab have been developed to target amyloid‐beta aggregates, though they are associated with amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA).AimsThis article aims to summarize current pharmacological approaches to AD and to highlight the emerging role of Donanemab‐azbt, an FDA‐approved monoclonal antibody for early symptomatic AD, in reducing amyloid plaques and slowing cognitive decline.MethodsThis overview synthesizes data from clinical trials and therapeutic experience with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, NMDA receptor antagonists, and monoclonal antibodies, with a particular focus on Donanemab‐azbt, its mechanism of targeting amyloid‐beta aggregates, and its efficacy and safety profile in early symptomatic AD.ResultsDonanemab‐azbt has demonstrated efficacy in clinical trials, significantly reducing amyloid plaque burden and slowing cognitive decline in patients with early symptomatic AD. However, its use may result in ARIA and other adverse effects, necessitating careful clinical and radiological monitoring during treatment.ConclusionDespite the risks of ARIA and other adverse events, Donanemab‐azbt represents a promising addition to AD therapy, offering the potential for improved outcomes in patients with early symptomatic disease and expanding the therapeutic options beyond traditional symptomatic treatments.Keywords: Alzheimer disease; antibodies, monoclonal; cognitive dysfunction; donanemab; plaque, amyloid

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative condition characterized by the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates, namely beta‐amyloid plaque and neurofibrillary tangles [1, 2]. The precursor of beta‐amyloid is amyloid precursor protein; hence, the accumulation of beta‐amyloid plaque results from an imbalance between its production and degradation [3]. These amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles accumulate between neurons, disrupt cellular signaling and cause inflammation and oxidative stress, promoting neuronal cell death. Neuronal death leads to the loss of cholinergic, glutaminergic, GABAergic, and monoaminergic neurons, which compromises cognitive function in patients with Alzheimer's disease [3, 4]. Age, genetics and family history are the most critical risk factors for AD development. With increasing age, the risk of Alzheimer's increases. The presence of the APO‐E e4 (Apolipoprotein E) allele is known to increase the risk of AD. Other modifiable risk factors include traumatic brain injury, depression, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease and smoking [5]. Epidemiological studies have also established the association between Alzheimer's disease and air pollution [6, 7].

The prevalence of AD is equal to 0.3 to 1.0 per 100 people in individuals aged 60 to 64 years and increases to 42.3 to 68.3 per 100 people in individuals 95 years and older [8]. The prevalence of AD increases with age. Among individuals aged over 70 years, AD is ranked as the fourth leading cause of death. The global number of people with Alzheimer's disease and related dementias increased to 51.62 million in 2019, indicating a significant increase of 161% compared to 19.79 million in 1990. By 2050, the number of affected individuals is estimated to reach 152.8 million [9].

Alzheimer's disease has an insidious onset and progressive impairment of behavioral and cognitive functions. Depending on the degree of cognitive impairment, AD is classified into preclinical, mild, moderate, and late‐stage stages [10]. The initial presenting symptom is recent memory loss. This is followed by impairment in problem‐solving, judgement, lack of motivation and disorganization, leading to problems with multitasking and abstract thinking [5].

Due to the progressive nature of Alzheimer's disease, a drug was required that could treat the disease in its early stages to prevent its progression into complicated phases. For this, on July 2nd,2024, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved Donanemab‐azbt, once monthly IV injections, for the treatment of patients with early symptomatic Alzheimer's who have mild cognitive impairment (MCI) with confirmed amyloid buildup in the brain.

Acetylcholinesterase Inhibitors

Acetylcholine has been known for its role in memory and learning for a long time. Similarly, depletion of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine is an important stage in the pathophysiology of Alzheimer's disease. Drugs such as donepezil, galantamine, metrifonate, physostigmine, rivastigmine, and tetrahydroaminoacridine target and inhibit human acetylcholinesterase activity [12]. Acetylcholinesterase typically breaks down Acetylcholine; thus, its inhibition results in increased levels of Acetylcholine in the Central Nervous system. Despite their widespread use, Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors have been known to cause side effects such as weight loss, nausea, vomiting, lightheadedness, palpitations, vertigo, bradycardia, muscle cramps, rhinitis, headache and asthenia [13]. In addition, reports of cardiac side effects such as QT‐prolongation and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia necessitate regular cardiac monitoring in patients [14].

NMDA Receptor Antagonists

Overstimulation of the glutamate receptor constitutes an essential feature of the pathophysiology of the disease. The drugs block Glutamate receptors, particularly N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate (NMDA) [13]. Memantine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is a useful pharmacological intervention for Alzheimer's as it decreases glutamatergic activity in the brain [15]. Memantine acts non‐competitively on the NMDA receptor and is known to be neuroprotective because it prevents neuron loss and improves symptoms by restoring the function of damaged neurons [16]. Owing to its considerable clinical efficacy, the increasing use of Memantine as monotherapy or in conjunction with the acetylcholinesterase inhibitors has led to the development of its significant side effect profile, which includes agitation, falls, dizziness, accidental injury, influenza‐like symptoms, headache, and diarrhea [17].

Monoclonal Antibodies

Development of Amyloid plaques due to abnormal accumulation of Amyloid Beta (Aβ) is a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease. Antibodies such as Aducanumab have been developed that specifically target Aβ aggregates. Aducanumab's binding promotes the clearance of these neurotoxic Aβ aggregates through the activation of microglial phagocytosis [18]. These monoclonal antibodies were developed not long ago, and several side effects have been noticed since their advent. The most common adverse events observed are amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA). Among those receiving the drug, ARIA manifesting as brain edema or effusion (ARIA‐E) and hemorrhage (ARIA‐H) are commonly seen. Other potential side effects, especially falls, headaches, and diarrhea, should also be considered when advising these antibodies in therapy [19].

Advent of Donanemab‐azbt

About 47 million people around the globe are presumed to be suffering from Alzheimer's, making its prevalence known as the 5th major cause of death in the elderly population. It's a substantial public health strain and considerably increases one's lifetime expenditures; this entails the administration of a novel drug to prevent its occurrence [20]. On July 2nd, the US Food and Drug Administration affirmed using Donanemab to diminish the effect of Alzheimer's disease, in which cognitive abilities of the brain are disrupted, resulting in impaired analytical and logical reasoning [21].

It was approved based on double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial programs comprising various subject populations, including White, Asian, Hispanic and African American. It continually displayed a statistical decline on the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale (CDR) [21].

Mechanism of Action

Donanemab is a humanized, monoclonal immunoglobulin gamma 1 (IgG1) antibody aimed at combating insoluble trimmed pyroglutamate amyloid beta plaques [22]. Donanemab is intravenously infused for close to 30 min, with a recommended dosage of 700 mg every 4 weeks for three doses and later increased to 1400 mg every 4 weeks. This drug helps damage amyloid beta plaque formation that accumulates, resulting in the loss of neurons and impaired cerebral activities (Figure 1) [21, 22].

Clinical Efficacy of Donanemab‐azbt

The effectiveness of Donanemab‐azbt was evaluated in a double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, parallel‐group study (Study 1, NCT04437511) involving patients with Alzheimer's disease who had confirmed amyloid pathology and were in the mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia stages (Stage 3 and Stage 4 of Alzheimer's disease). Participants were selected based on a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score between 20 and 28 and had shown progressive memory decline for at least 6 months. Tau PET imaging with flortaucipir and standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was used for patient inclusion [23, 24].

In total, 1,736 patients with an average age of 73 were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either 700 mg of Donanemab‐azbt every 4 weeks for the first three doses, followed by 1,400 mg every 4 weeks (N = 860) or a placebo (N = 876) for up to 72 weeks. The treatment was switched to a placebo based on a prespecified reduction in amyloid levels, measured by positron emission tomography (PET) at weeks 24, 52, and 76 [23, 24].

The primary efficacy endpoint was the change in the integrated Alzheimer's Disease Rating Scale (iADRS) score from baseline to 76 weeks. Patients treated with Donanemab‐azbt demonstrated a significant reduction in clinical decline on the iADRS compared to the placebo at week 76 (2.92, p < 0.0001). Significant improvements were also noted on the iADRS component scales, including the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐Cognitive subscale (ADAS‐Cog13) (−1.33, p = 0.0006) and the Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study – instrumental Activities of Daily Living (ADCS‐iADL) scale (1.70, p = 0.0001). Additionally, Donanemab‐treated patients showed a marked reduction in clinical decline on the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale – Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) compared to placebo at week 76 (−0.70, p < 0.0001) (Table 2) [23, 24].

The study also monitored amyloid plaque levels using PET scans throughout treatment. Results showed significant and promising reductions in amyloid plaques among patients receiving Donanemab‐azbt, with reductions of 61% at 6 months, 80% at 12 months, and 84% at 18 months compared to baseline levels. These results suggest that Donanemab not only targets cognitive decline but also effectively reduces amyloid pathology, a key feature of Alzheimer's disease progression, offering hope for the future of Alzheimer's treatment [25].

Drug Safety

Donanemab safety and tolerability were thoroughly assessed through a study involving 853 patients treated with donanemab and 874 patients on placebo. Amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) were found in patients in early treatment and usually asymptomatic. ARIA refers to monoclonal antibody treatment‐related abnormal differences like edema, effusion or hemorrhage on Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain in patients with Alzheimer's disease [26]. ARIA may present as ARIA with edema (ARIA‐E) or ARIA with micro hemorrhage or superficial siderosis (ARIA‐H). ARIA (asymptomatic) was observed in 36% of patients treated with Donanemab‐azbt as compared to 6% of patients who developed ARIA with symptoms. ARIA usually resolves over time, as 98% of cases resolve at follow‐up imaging. ARIA‐E often occurs between 3 and 6 months, with incidence dropping after the first 9 months [22].

Radiographically (MRI), the severity of ARIA was estimated. Maximum radiographic severity or ARIA – E in patients treated with Donanemab‐azbt was found to be mild in 7% of patients, moderate in 15% and severe in 2% of patients. Maximum radiographic severity of ARIA‐H micro hemorrhage was mild in 17% of patients, moderated in 4% and severe in 5% treated with Donanemab‐azbt. For ARIA‐H, superficial siderosis results were mild in 6%, moderate in 4% and severe in 5% of patients treated with the mentioned drug. Incidence of ARIA was higher in APOE e4 homozygotes than heterozygotes and non‐carriers. For safety purposes, APOE e4 screening is advised before administration of the drug [22]. Another study involving 257 patients (131 treated with donanemab and 126 on placebo) showed (mostly) asymptomatic cerebral edema or effusion [22].

Adverse Effects

ARIA and headache were found to be the most common undesired effects of the drug. Other adverse effects include the following: Hypersensitivity reactions that include anaphylaxis and angioedema. Infusion‐related reactions include headache, chest pain, changes in blood pressure, breathing difficulty, nausea, vomiting, chills and skin irritation [21]. There is no adequate data on Donanemab‐azbt's effects on pregnancy, breastfeeding and the pediatric population. No studies are available regarding its role in cancer causation and mutagenesis. Donanemab‐azbt is contraindicated in patients with severe hypersensitivity to donanemab. Although the drug has a few risks, it has significant efficacy in modifying disease progression by reducing the decline in cognitive and amyloid plaques [22].

Conclusion

In conclusion, FDA‐approved Donanemab‐azbt has shown significant and promising results in declining cognitive impairment and effectively reducing amyloid plaque buildup in early symptomatic Alzheimer's patients. Its neuroprotective properties, including antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and anti‐amyloidogenic effects, offer a multifaceted approach to slowing disease progression and alleviating symptoms. Along with its potential benefits, it is essential to acknowledge the treatment‐related risks like amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), which may present as edema or hemorrhage on brain MRI. Patients must be advised to have brain MRI scans before and during the treatment. Although the therapy carries few risks, its significant efficacy weighs more. Therefore, FDA approval of Donanemab‐azbt could become a valuable addition to the therapeutic landscape for Alzheimer's, providing hope for improved quality of life for patients and their families.
