More on modal logics and deduction
Zalán Molnár, Övge Öztürk

Abstract
We study the relation between additivity and deduction theorems in the algebraic semantics of congruential modal logic. Additivity of the modal operator is well-known to imply the local deduction-detachment theorem. Our main theme is that deduction properties of modal logic persist far beyond the additive setting. We introduce the notion of a strongly non-additive variety, and then we prove that there are continuum many strongly non-additive minimal discriminator varieties of Boolean frames; equivalently, continuum many strongly non-additive maximal congruential modal logics with deduction-detachment theorem. Moreover, every normal modal logic can be transformed, in an injective way, into a strongly non-additive one while preserving the (local) deduction theorem. Finally, we show that neither the class of congruential modal logics with the local deduction theorem nor its complement is elementary.

4

If an algebra

\mathfrak{A}\in\mathbf{K}
has
\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{A})=\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{A})

. We begin with a general transfer result concerning finite products in congruence-distributive varieties.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be algebras in a congruence-distributive variety. If

\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{A})=\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{A})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{B})=\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{B}),

then

\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{B})=\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{B}).

In particular, this holds for Boolean frames.

Proof.

For every algebra
\mathfrak{X}
one always has
\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{X})\subseteq\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{X})
, thus it suffices to prove that

\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{B})\subseteq\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{B})

Let
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{B})

\mathfrak{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{B}
and a congruence

such that
\mathfrak{D}\cong\mathfrak{C}/\theta
.
Let
\pi_{A}\colon A\times B\to A
and
\pi_{B}\colon A\times B\to B

\alpha=\ker(\pi_{A}\upharpoonright C),\qquad\beta=\ker(\pi_{B}\upharpoonright C).

Since

\alpha\wedge\beta=\Delta_{\mathfrak{C}}
.
Consider the homomorphism

i:\mathfrak{C}/\theta\longrightarrow\mathfrak{C}/(\theta\vee\alpha)\times\mathfrak{C}/(\theta\vee\beta),\qquad c/\theta\longmapsto\bigl(c/(\theta\vee\alpha),\,c/(\theta\vee\beta)\bigr).

Its kernel is, by the second isomorphism theorem,

\ker(i)=\nicefrac{{(\theta\vee\alpha)\wedge(\theta\vee\beta)}}{{\theta}}.

By congruence distributivity,

(\theta\vee\alpha)\wedge(\theta\vee\beta)=\theta\vee(\alpha\wedge\beta)=\theta\vee\Delta_{\mathfrak{C}}=\theta.

Hence
\ker(i)=\Delta_{\mathfrak{C}/\theta}
, and therefore

is injective. It follows that

Since

, the quotient

\mathfrak{C}/(\theta\vee\alpha)
is a homomorphic image of
\mathfrak{C}/\alpha
, and so
\mathfrak{C}/(\theta\vee\alpha)\in\mathbf{H}(C/\alpha)
.
But
\mathfrak{C}/\alpha\cong\pi_{A}(C)
, and
\pi_{A}(\mathfrak{C})\subseteq\mathfrak{A}
. Thus

\mathfrak{C}/(\theta\vee\alpha)\in\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{A})=\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{A}).

Therefore, there exist
\varphi_{\mathfrak{A}}\in\operatorname{Con}(\mathfrak{A})
and a subalgebra
\mathfrak{A}_{0}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{A}}
such that

\mathfrak{C}/(\theta\vee\alpha)\cong\mathfrak{A}_{0}
.
Similarly,

\mathfrak{C}/(\theta\vee\beta)\in\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{B})=\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{B}),

so there exist
\varphi_{\mathfrak{B}}\in\operatorname{Con}(\mathfrak{B})
and a subalgebra

\mathfrak{B}_{0}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{B}}
such that
\mathfrak{C}/(\theta\vee\beta)\cong\mathfrak{B}_{0}
.
We obtained
\mathfrak{C}/\theta\in\mathbf{S}(\mathfrak{A}_{0}\times\mathfrak{B}_{0})
.
As
\mathfrak{A}_{0}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{A}}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{0}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{B}}
, it follows that

\mathfrak{A}_{0}\times\mathfrak{B}_{0}\subseteq(\mathfrak{A}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{A}})\times(\mathfrak{B}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{B}}).

Hence

\mathfrak{C}/\theta\in\mathbf{S}\bigl((\mathfrak{A}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{A}})\times(\mathfrak{B}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{B}})\bigr).

By the Fraser-Horn property of congruence distributive varieties we have

(\mathfrak{A}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{A}})\times(\mathfrak{B}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{B}})\cong(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{B})/(\varphi_{\mathfrak{A}}\times\varphi_{\mathfrak{B}}),

showing that
(\mathfrak{A}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{A}})\times(\mathfrak{B}/\varphi_{\mathfrak{B}})\in\mathbf{H}(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{B})
, and therefore
\mathfrak{C}/\theta\in\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{B})
.
∎

5
CEP is not elementary

While certain identities (e.g. additivity) can force a Boolean frame to have the CEP, no identity can force an algebra not to have the CEP. The reason is that the trivial algebra satisfies every identity and has the CEP. There are, of course, first-order formulas that force an algebra not to have the CEP. A straightforward example is any first-order formula that describes the isomorphism-type of a finite algebra that does not have the CEP. We show in this section that the dividing line between CEP and no-CEP (in the case of Boolean frames) cannot be first-order defined. Formally: there are elementarily equivalent Boolean frames
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
such that
\mathfrak{A}\in\mathsf{CEP}
, but
\mathfrak{B}\notin\mathsf{CEP}
. We reach this goal by constructing an algebra
\mathfrak{A}\in\mathsf{CEP}
such that its ultrapower
\mathfrak{A}^{\omega}/U\notin\mathsf{CEP}
. In particular, this shows that the class
\mathsf{CEP}
is not closed under ultrapowers. The example we construct is such that
\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{A})=\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{A})
, while
\mathbf{HS}(\mathfrak{A}^{\omega}/U)\neq\mathbf{SH}(\mathfrak{A}^{\omega}/U)
showing that the property
\mathbf{SH}=\mathbf{HS}
, which is weaker than CEP, is also not preserved under ultrapowers.

Take the powerset Boolean algebra

(\wp(\mathbb{N}),\sqcap,-,\varnothing)

for

if and only if

Lemma 5.1
.

Every subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}

Proof.

From any nonempty

the top element
\mathbb{N}
. If
\equiv

for a suitable finite

is the largest congruence.

Problem 5.6
.

Are there Boolean frames
\mathfrak{A}_{i}
(or Boolean algebras with
finitely
many extra unary functions) such that every
\mathfrak{A}_{i}
fails to have the CEP, while an ultraproduct
\Pi_{i\in I}\mathfrak{A}_{i}/U
admits CEP?

In order to deal with the congruence extension property of an ultraproduct some kind of a uniform bound on the generation of congruences would be useful. The closest we could get to this is the result below, which might be interesting on its own: For the CEP of a Boolean frame it is enough to check whether principal congruences of
2
-generated subalgebras extend to the whole algebra.

Theorem 5.7
.

A Boolean frame
\mathfrak{A}
has the CEP if and only if every principal congruence of every
2
-generated subalgebra extends to a congruence of
\mathfrak{A}
.

Proof.

We split the proof into several lemmas.

Lemma 5.8
.

For a Boolean frame
\mathfrak{A}
and principal congruences
\Theta,\Psi\in\operatorname{Con}(\mathfrak{A})
the join

\Theta\vee\Psi\in\operatorname{Con}(\mathfrak{A})
is a principal congruence.

Proof.

Since for a principal congruence
\Theta(a,b)
we have

\Theta(a,b)=\Theta(a\oplus b,0)
, it is enough to prove

\Theta(x,0)\vee\Theta(y,0)=\Theta(x\vee y,0)\,.

This follows from the Boolean structure:

(x\vee y,0)\in\Theta(x\vee y,0)
, and then using

x,y\leq x\vee y
we also have
(x,0),(y,0)\in\Theta(x\vee y,0)
, and thus
\Theta(x,0)\vee\Theta(y,0)\subseteq\Theta(x\vee y,0)
. On the other hand,

(x,0),(y,0)\in\Theta(x,0)\vee\Theta(y,0)
, and
thus
(x\vee y,0)\in\Theta(x,0)\vee\Theta(y,0)
,
showing
\Theta(x\vee y,0)\subseteq\Theta(x,0)\vee\Theta(y,0)
.
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

For a Boolean frame
\mathfrak{A}
and principal congruences

\Theta_{i}\in\operatorname{Con}(\mathfrak{A})
(
i\in I
) the union
\bigcup_{i\in I}\Theta_{i}
is a congruence of
\mathfrak{A}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.8
the system
\{\Theta_{i}:\;i\in I\}
is an upward directed system of congruences.
For any algebraic closure operation (in particular for the closure operation of taking generated congruences)
the union of an upward directed set of closed sets is closed (see
[
2
]
). It follows that

\bigcup_{i\in I}\Theta_{i}=\bigvee_{i\in I}\Theta_{i}\;\operatorname{Con}(\mathfrak{A})\,.

∎

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a subalgebra of the Boolean frame
\mathfrak{A}
, and let
\Theta^{\mathfrak{B}}(x,y)\in\operatorname{Con}(\mathfrak{B})
be a principal congruence. If
\Theta^{\mathfrak{B}}(x,y)
extends to a congruence of
\mathfrak{A}
, then
\Theta^{\mathfrak{A}}(x,y)
is such an extension, that is
\Theta^{\mathfrak{B}}(x,y)=\Theta^{\mathfrak{A}}(x,y)\cap(B\times B)
.

Lemma 5.10
.

A Boolean frame
\mathfrak{A}
has the CEP if and only if it has the PCEP.

Proof.

That the CEP implies the PCEP is obvious. For the other direction assume that
\mathfrak{A}
has the PCEP. Take a subalgebra
\mathfrak{B}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}
and a congruence
\Theta^{\mathfrak{B}}
. Then

\displaystyle\Theta^{\mathfrak{B}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{(x,y)\in\Theta}\Theta^{\mathfrak{B}}(x,y)\overset{\text{PCEP}}{=}\bigcup_{(x,y)\in\Theta}\big(\Theta^{\mathfrak{A}}(x,y)\cap(B\times B)\big)

\displaystyle=\Big(\bigcup_{(x,y)\in\Theta}\Theta^{\mathfrak{A}}(x,y)\Big)\cap(B\times B)\,.

This latter union is a congruence by Lemma
5.9
.
∎

Getting back to the proof of Theorem
5.7
, let
\mathfrak{B}\leq\mathfrak{A}
, and let
a,b\in B
.
We must show that the principal congruence

\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{B}}(a,b)

extends to a congruence of
\mathfrak{A}
. Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{B}}(a,b)
does not extend to
\mathfrak{A}
. Then

\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a,b)\cap(B\times B)\neq\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{B}}(a,b),

so there exist
c,d\in B
such that

(c,d)\in\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a,b)\qquad\text{but}\qquad(c,d)\notin\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{B}}(a,b),

equivalently,

\displaystyle(c\oplus d,0)\in\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a\oplus b,0)\qquad\text{but}\qquad(c\oplus d,0)\notin\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{B}}(a\oplus b,0).

(5.2)

Consider now the subalgebra

\mathfrak{C}=\operatorname{Sg}^{\mathfrak{B}}(\{a\oplus b,c\oplus d\})
.
It is
2
-generated, hence by assumption the principal congruence

\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{C}}(a\oplus b,0)

extends to some congruence
\Phi\in\operatorname{Con}(\mathfrak{A})
.
Since
(a\oplus b,0)\in\Phi
, by the minimality of the principal congruence generated by
(a\oplus b,0)
in

\mathfrak{A}
,

\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a\oplus b,0)\subseteq\Phi
.
As
(c\oplus d,0)\in\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a\oplus b,0)
, we get

(c\oplus d,0)\in\Phi
. Now
c\oplus d\in C
, and
\Phi
extends

\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{C}}(a\oplus b,0)
, so

(c\oplus d,0)\in\Phi\cap(C\times C)=\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{C}}(a\oplus b,0).

On the other hand,

\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{C}}(a\oplus b,0)\subseteq\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{B}}(a\oplus b,0)
,
because the restriction of

\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{B}}(a\oplus b,0)
to
C
is a congruence of
\mathfrak{C}
containing
(a\oplus b,0)
.
Therefore
(c\oplus d,0)\in\operatorname{Cg}^{\mathfrak{B}}(a\oplus b,0)
,
contradicting
5.2
.
∎
