Single-level cervical disc arthroplasty is associated with higher early subsequent surgery rates and similar adjacent segment disease rates compared with anterior cervical discectomy and fusion
Authors: Muhammad Waheed, Abdul-Lateef Shafau, Sazid Hasan, Alqasim Elnaggar, Tarek Harhash, Kareem Oulabi, Ahmad Almaat, Abdel Kareem Hilo, Syed Kazi, Bashar Jawich, Haytham Alqasmi, Rahul Vaidya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) is a motion-preserving alternative to anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), but its comparative long-term durability remains debated. This study aimed to compare short- and long-term postoperative outcomes following single-level CDA versus ACDF.MethodsA retrospective propensity-matched cohort study was performed using a large national administrative database. Adult patients undergoing single-level CDA or ACDF without prior spine surgery were identified and matched 1:1, yielding 3,138 patients per cohort. Outcomes were assessed at 1, 2, 5, and 10 years postoperatively.ResultsAt 1 year, CDA was associated with lower rates of pulmonary embolism/deep venous thrombosis (PE/DVT) [0.6% vs. 1.2%; risk ratio (RR) 0.526, P=0.02] and neurovascular injury (0.4% vs. 1.3%; RR 0.333, P<0.001), while other short-term complications were similar. At 2 and 5 years, CDA demonstrated higher rates of subsequent cervical spine surgery (1.5% vs. 0.7%, P=0.003; 1.9% vs. 1.1%, P=0.02), although this difference was no longer significant at 10 years. CDA also showed lower long-term rates of infection, PE/DVT, and nerve injury. No significant differences were observed in adjacent segment disease (ASD) or cervical spondylolisthesis.ConclusionsSingle-level CDA was associated with higher early revision and subsequent surgery rates and similar ASD rates compared with ACDF. Although CDA showed lower rates of select complications, these differences may not outweigh the increased intermediate-term reoperation burden.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) is associated with lower rates of pulmonary embolism/deep venous thrombosis (PE/DVT), infection, and nerve injury across short- and long-term follow-up.

• CDA has higher rates of subsequent cervical spine surgery at 2–5 years, with no difference by 10 years.

What is known and what is new?

• Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is the standard surgical treatment, but it carries risks such as pseudoarthrosis and adjacent segment disease (ASD). CDA is a motion-preserving alternative proposed to reduce adjacent segment stress and reoperation

• This study provides a large, propensity-matched comparison with follow-up extending to 10 years. CDA was not associated with reduced ASD or 10-year subsequent cervical spine surgery and was associated with higher rates of subsequent cervical spine surgery at 2 and 5 years.

What are the implications, and what should change now?

• CDA and ACDF should be selected based on individualized risk-benefit tradeoffs rather than assumed superiority.

• Surgeons should counsel patients on the higher intermediate-term reoperation risk with CDA while recognizing ACDF as a reliable standard.

Introduction

Cervical degenerative disc disease is a musculoskeletal condition characterized by progressive degeneration of intervertebral discs in the cervical spine, leading to neck pain, myelopathy, and radiculopathy (1). Radiographic evidence of cervical disc degeneration is found in most individuals by age 60 years and approaches 100% by age 70 years (2). However, the relationship between imaging findings and symptoms remains incompletely understood, and surgical intervention is not always indicated (2). When conservative management no longer provides adequate relief, surgery may become necessary, with anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) being the historic standard of care (3,4). Cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) is a motion-preserving alternative to ACDF, with increasing evidence of comparable or superior outcomes (4,5).

ACDF achieves decompression and stabilization through interbody fusion, eliminating motion at the treated segment. While effective for symptom relief and stability, fusion has been associated with adjacent segment disease (ASD), pseudoarthrosis, and long-term mechanical complications. Pseudoarthrosis rates for ACDF have been reported as high as 13–32%, and meta-analyses have reported an 11% incidence of clinical ASD and a 30% incidence of radiographic ASD (6-10). CDA was developed to preserve physiological segmental motion and reduce mechanical stress at adjacent levels. Early randomized controlled trials and registry studies suggested lower rates of ASD and reoperation over time after CDA (4,5,11). Heterotopic ossification has been reported in approximately 47–52% of patients who underwent CDA, and recent studies have demonstrated higher reoperation rates after ACDF at the 5-year follow-up (12-15).

Despite the expanding literature comparing ACDF and CDA, most existing studies are limited by short follow-up periods, small sample sizes, or single-center designs. Few analyses have evaluated outcomes from the perioperative period through 10 years, leaving comparative long-term safety and durability incompletely defined. Given conflicting literature regarding complications, ASD, and reoperation, a large propensity-matched analysis is warranted. The purpose of this study was to compare the long-term durability and overall complication profile of a single-level CDA and ACDF at 1, 2, 5, and 10 years postoperatively. We evaluated short-term medical complications, subsequent cervical spine surgery, thromboembolic events, neurovascular injury, infection, ASD, and pseudoarthrosis. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0094/rc).

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study utilized the TriNetX Research Network, a federated multi-institutional administrative healthcare database containing longitudinal deidentified patient-level data from participating healthcare organizations across the United States. The database includes diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values, and longitudinal follow-up encounter information derived from electronic health records. Patients undergoing index procedures between August 1, 2007 and December 31, 2015 were eligible for inclusion to permit longitudinal postoperative outcome assessment through 10 years following the index procedure. Outcomes were identified using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM), Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED), Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system (ATC), Veterans Affairs (VA) drug class codes, and TriNetX encounter definitions. Because the study utilized only aggregated deidentified patient data, it was considered exempt from Institutional Review Board approval at Wayne State University School of Medicine. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Cohort identification

Adult patients undergoing single-level ACDF or CDA were identified using procedural terminology recorded at the time of the index operation. Patients in the CDA cohort were required to have single-level cervical total disc arthroplasty, whereas ACDF patients were required to have a single-level anterior cervical arthrodesis below C2. To restrict the analysis to single-level procedures and exclude patients with prior cervical or spinal operative history, additional procedural and diagnostic exclusion criteria were applied to both cohorts. The index event for each cohort was defined as the qualifying single-level CDA or ACDF procedure meeting these criteria.

Propensity score matching was performed using 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching within the TriNetX platform with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations. Propensity score matching incorporated available demographic variables (age, sex, race, and ethnicity), baseline comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, bone density disorders, and metabolic diseases), as well as available clinical variables including body mass index and hemoglobin A1c when recorded within the database. These variables were selected to minimize baseline differences and account for factors potentially influencing postoperative cervical spine surgery outcomes. After matching, 3,138 patients remained in each cohort. Full cohort-definition codes and exclusion criteria are provided in Table S1.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of interest were subsequent cervical spine surgery and ASD, as these represent the principal comparative endpoints between ACDF and CDA and have remained inconsistent in the existing literature. ASD was defined using postoperative diagnostic coding consistent with new degenerative pathology, radiculopathy, myelopathy, stenosis, or degenerative disc disease occurring at cervical levels adjacent to the index procedure following surgery. ASD was identified using longitudinal coded diagnoses recorded after the index operation. Secondary outcomes included the remaining postoperative events assessed in this study, including thromboembolic events, postoperative infection, neurovascular injury, healthcare utilization, acute kidney injury (AKI), wound disruption, and sepsis.

All outcomes were assessed as postoperative events occurring after the index cervical procedure and were defined using coded diagnoses, procedures, encounters, and prescription records available within the database.

Subsequent cervical spine surgery was defined as any additional cervical spine procedure following the primary indexed operation, including revision surgery at the indexed level or any further cervical spine surgery at adjacent or non-indexed levels. These subsequent procedures included repeat CDA or ACDF, additional-level anterior cervical fusion, removal or revision of anterior instrumentation, and other related reconstructive anterior cervical procedures. Medical complications included stroke, pulmonary embolism (PE)/deep venous thrombosis (DVT), infection, AKI, wound disruption, and sepsis. Neurovascular injury was evaluated separately to capture both neurologic and vascular injuries associated with cervical surgery. Healthcare utilization outcomes included emergency department (ED) visits, hospital readmissions, and post-operative opioid prescription utilization. Full outcome definitions and associated code sets are provided in Table S2, and healthcare utilization and medication codes are provided in Table S3.

Time-to-event assessment

Outcomes were assessed at 1, 2, 5, and 10 years following the index procedure. The reported cohort size of 3,138 matched patients per group reflects the number of patients continuously observed through the full 10-year interval. Outcomes at each time point were cumulative time-to-event estimates among patients with available follow-up data within the TriNetX network. Patients without further recorded encounters were censored at the time of their last available healthcare interaction. Time-to-event was calculated from the date of the index cervical procedure to the first occurrence of the outcome of interest, with censoring occurring at death, last recorded encounter, or the end of the observation period, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the CDA and ACDF cohorts (Table S4). Postoperative outcomes were then compared between matched CDA and ACDF patients at each follow-up interval. Categorical outcomes were reported as event rates and compared using risk ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value <0.05.

Results

One-year outcomes

At the 1-year follow-up, 3,138 matched patients were included in each cohort. Rates of stroke, infection, AKI, wound disruption, and sepsis were not significantly different between CDA and ACDF patients. CDA was associated with significantly lower rates of PE/DVT, occurring in 0.6% of patients compared to 1.2% in the ACDF cohort [risk ratio (RR) 0.526, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.307–0.902, P=0.02]. Neurovascular injury was also significantly less common following CDA (0.4% vs. 1.3%, RR 0.333, 95% CI: 0.182–0.609, P<0.001). Healthcare utilization was not significantly different between cohorts, including ED visits, readmissions, and opioid prescription utilization (Table 1).

Two-year outcomes

Within the matched cohorts of 3,138 patients per group, cumulative outcomes were assessed through 2 years of available follow-up. Subsequent cervical spine surgery occurred significantly more frequently following CDA, affecting 1.5% of patients compared to 0.7% in the ACDF cohort (RR 2.09, 95% CI: 1.273 to 3.422, P=0.003). Although the relative risk was elevated, the absolute risk difference between cohorts was small, corresponding to 0.8% at 2 years. CDA demonstrated lower rates of PE/DVT, which occurred in 1.0% of patients versus 1.6% in the ACDF cohort (RR 0.627, 95% CI: 0.404–0.974, P=0.04), and significantly lower rates of nerve injury (0.3% vs. 1.2%, RR 0.263, 95% CI: 0.131–0.527, P<0.001). Infection rates were similar between groups. There were no significant differences in ASD and cervical spondylolisthesis. Pseudoarthrosis was only reported in the ACDF cohort (2.2%) (Table 2).

Five-year outcomes

Within the matched cohorts of 3,138 patients per group, cumulative outcomes were assessed through 5 years of available follow-up. Subsequent cervical spine surgery remained significantly more common following CDA, occurring in 1.9% of patients compared to 1.1% in the ACDF cohort (RR 1.639, 95% CI 1.086–2.473, P=0.02). Similarly, the 5-year difference represented a small absolute increase of 0.7% despite statistical significance. CDA was associated with lower rates of infection (1.0% vs. 1.8%, RR 0.554, 95% CI: 0.358–0.856, P=0.007). Nerve injury remained significantly lower following CDA (0.5% vs. 1.7%, RR 0.288, 95% CI: 0.163–0.511, P<0.001). No significant differences were observed in ASD or cervical spondylolisthesis (1.2% vs. 1.7%, P=0.11). Pseudoarthrosis occurred at a rate of 2.6% in the ACDF cohort (Table 3).

Ten-year outcomes

Within the matched cohorts of 3,138 patients per group, cumulative outcomes were assessed through 10 years of available follow-up. Subsequent cervical spine surgery rates were similar between groups. CDA was associated with significantly lower rates of infection (1.1% vs. 1.9%, RR 0.559, 95% CI: 0.366–0.854, P=0.006), PE/DVT (1.8% vs. 2.5%, RR 0.7, 95% CI: 0.499–0.981, P=0.037), and nerve injury (0.5% vs. 1.8%, RR 0.286, 95% CI: 0.164–0.497, P<0.001). There were no significant differences in ASD and cervical spondylolisthesis. The rate of pseudoarthrosis in the ACDF cohort was 2.9% (Table 4).

Discussion

Overview of key findings

This study compared 3,138 propensity-matched patients undergoing single-level CDA versus ACDF and demonstrated important differences in both short- and long-term postoperative outcomes. At 1 year, CDA was associated with lower reported rates of PE/DVT and neurovascular injury, while other short-term outcomes were similar. At 2 and 5 years, CDA demonstrated higher rates of subsequent cervical spine surgery, but this difference was no longer statistically significant at 10 years. Over long-term follow-up, CDA consistently demonstrated lower rates of infection and nerve injury than the ACDF cohort. However, no significant differences were observed in ASD and cervical spondylolisthesis between the two cohorts. Overall, these findings highlight a potential, noteworthy tradeoff between the lower early medical complication rates observed with CDA and the greater intermediate-term procedural durability associated with ACDF.

Early postoperative medical complications

The lower 1-year rates of PE/DVT and neurovascular injury after CDA suggest that, in appropriately selected patients, motion-preserving surgery may offer a favorable early perioperative risk profile relative to fusion. At the same time, the absence of significant differences in stroke, infection, AKI, wound disruption, sepsis, and healthcare utilization suggests that early safety profiles between CDA and ACDF are otherwise largely comparable. These findings parallel much of the current literature on CDA, which has generally demonstrated comparable or improved perioperative safety profiles of CDA relative to ACDF in appropriately selected patients (3,5). This is important because prior literature has focused more on long-term biomechanical outcomes than short-term medical complications, and these findings help clarify perioperative risk during counseling (16-18).

Revision and subsequent cervical spine surgery

The central finding of this study was that CDA was associated with significantly increased rates of subsequent cervical spine surgery at both 2- and 5-year follow-up compared with ACDF.

These findings are particularly important, as one of the traditional theoretical advantages of CDA has been to reduce long-term reoperation (11,16,17). Our results directly challenge this, suggesting that this advantage may not be uniform and that intermediate-term durability of CDA may be more complex than initially proposed. These observed differences may reflect variation in construct biomechanics, including implant-specific factors, heterotopic ossification, and other mechanical differences between CDA and ACDF (12,19-21). Whereas ACDF depends on fusion to provide long-term stability, CDA remains dependent on continued implant integrity and preservation of motion at the treated level (19,22). Disruption of any of these factors may predispose patients to further increases in prosthesis-related failure or symptomatic degeneration (22). It is important to note that our endpoint of ‘subsequent cervical spine surgery’ was defined broadly to capture any reoperation, including index-level revisions, adjacent-level extensions, and non-adjacent procedures. While this captures the overall surgical burden to the patient, it limits the mechanistic interpretation of construct failure. The higher intermediate-term reoperation rate observed with CDA may reflect index-level implant failures or heterotopic ossification rather than adjacent-level breakdown.

However, these differences diminished by 10 years postoperatively and no longer reached statistical significance, suggesting that the risk of subsequent cervical spine surgery is greatest at early and intermediate postoperative periods and does not continue to widen over time. This 10-year finding is important because long-term reoperation data remain limited in the literature (3,5). Patients most prone to early failure may declare themselves within the first several postoperative years, whereas those who remain clinically stable represent a more durable long-term subgroup. Importantly, the absolute differences in subsequent cervical spine surgery were small, measuring 0.8% at 2 years and 0.7% at 5 years. Therefore, while the relative risk was statistically higher after CDA at intermediate follow-up, the clinical magnitude of this difference should be interpreted cautiously.

Long-term medical complication profile

Despite the increased intermediate-term risk of subsequent surgery after CDA, decreased rates of infection, PE/DVT, and nerve injury persisted across long-term follow-up. At 5 and 10 years postoperatively, CDA patients continued to demonstrate lower infection rates. Thromboembolic events were lower at 10 years, and nerve injury remained consistently less common at 2, 5, and 10 years. These findings likely reflect differences in surgical exposure, construct-related morbidity, postoperative biomechanics, and patient selection (4,17,18). Regardless of the mechanisms, the consistency of these associations across multiple time points strengthens the argument that CDA may be associated with advantages beyond simple motion preservation alone. Although CDA demonstrated lower rates of select complications, these benefits should be weighed against the higher rates of revision or subsequent spine surgery observed at intermediate follow-up.

Long-term cervical spine-specific outcomes

Although prior studies suggest the theoretical biomechanical rationale for CDA in reducing adjacent segment degeneration, this study found no significant differences in ASD at 2, 5, or 10 years (3,16). Similarly, cervical spondylolisthesis was not significantly different between procedures over 2, 5, and 10 years postoperatively. Pseudoarthrosis as an outcome was only observed in the ACDF cohort and therefore was reported separately as a fusion-specific complication. These findings suggest that the advantages of CDA may not translate into measurable reductions in all long-term cervical spine-specific complications at the population level. In contrast, pseudoarthrosis was documented only in the ACDF cohort, which is expected given that pseudoarthrosis is inherently a fusion-specific complication. Although CDA avoids fusion-related pseudoarthrosis, that advantage was not accompanied by a measurable reduction in ASD.

Comparison with prior literature and clinical interpretation

Prior randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses have often reported CDA as comparable or superior to ACDF in selected outcomes, including motion preservation, ASD, and similar or lower reoperation rates over time (3-5,11,15-17). At the same time, other studies have raised concerns regarding implant durability, heterotopic ossification, and the potential for later reoperation (21,23,24). In that context, the present study adds important nuance to the literature comparing single-level CDA and ACDF by demonstrating statistically higher rates of subsequent cervical spine surgery after CDA at 2 and 5 years, although the absolute differences were small and no significant differences were observed at 10 years. These differences from prior RCT, meta-analysis, and clinical literature may reflect variation in patient selection, implant type, surgical experience, coding-based outcomes, and the broad definition of subsequent cervical spine surgery used in this investigation (3-5,11,16,17,21-30). While CDA was associated with lower rates of several short- and long-term medical complications, these findings should be interpreted in the context that patients selected for CDA are often those considered the best candidates for motion preservation and may therefore have greater recovery potential at baseline (1,31). Nevertheless, CDA was notably associated with higher rates of subsequent spine surgery at intermediate 2–5-year follow-up. Furthermore, similar rates of ASD and cervical spondylolisthesis call into question the long-term clinical benefit of CDA’s proposed biomechanical advantages. Clinically, these results suggest that CDA and ACDF should not be viewed simply through the lens of motion preservation, but rather as procedures with distinct complication profiles and tradeoffs that must be matched to the individual patient’s goals, risk profile, and tolerance for potential future surgery.

Strengths

This study has several important strengths. It utilized a large national database with longitudinal follow-up, allowing comparison of single-level CDA and ACDF across multiple clinically meaningful postoperative intervals through 10 years. The large matched cohort represents, to our knowledge, one of the largest comparative analyses of its kind, and the use of propensity score matching further strengthened the study design by improving cohort balance and reducing measured baseline differences between groups. By restricting the analysis to single-level cases without prior spine surgery, the study also improved cohort homogeneity and allowed for a more focused comparison between the two procedures. In addition, it evaluated both short-term medical complications and longer-term cervical spine-specific outcomes, while also incorporating healthcare utilization measures such as ED visits, readmissions, and opioid prescription use. Finally, the study was not externally funded, reducing concern for financial sponsorship influencing study design or interpretation.

Limitations

These findings should nevertheless be interpreted in the context of several limitations. As an administrative database study, the analysis relied on the accuracy and completeness of coded diagnoses, procedures, encounters, and prescription records, introducing the possibility of misclassification. Although propensity score matching improved comparability, residual confounding remains possible, particularly for variables not well captured in database research, such as symptom severity, cervical alignment, implant selection, operative technique, and surgeon preference. The database also lacked radiographic outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, and detailed operative information, limiting assessment of heterotopic ossification, construct-specific performance, and functional recovery. Furthermore, the incidence of ASD in our cohorts was higher than the 20–30% long-term rates often reported in the clinical literature. This likely reflects differences in outcome ascertainment, as administrative databases may capture broader diagnostic coding, symptom-driven encounters, or nonspecific cervical degeneration rather than only clinically adjudicated symptomatic ASD or surgically treated ASD.

In addition, the subsequent cervical spine surgery endpoint was intentionally broad and included both index-level and non-index cervical procedures, which may limit mechanistic interpretation. The database also could not reliably identify the specific clinical indication for subsequent surgery after CDA, so this outcome should be interpreted as a coded procedural endpoint rather than a diagnosis-specific failure mechanism. Finally, pseudoarthrosis could only be reliably assessed in the ACDF cohort, limiting direct comparison of that outcome between procedures.

Conclusions

In this large propensity-matched analysis of single-level CDA versus ACDF, CDA was associated with statistically higher rates of subsequent cervical spine surgery at 2 and 5 years; however, the absolute differences were small, and no significant difference was observed at 10 years postoperatively. CDA was also associated with lower rates of several coded complications, including infection, thromboembolic events, and nerve injury, with no significant differences observed in ASD or cervical spondylolisthesis. These findings suggest that CDA and ACDF should be interpreted as procedures with distinct risk profiles rather than one approach being uniformly superior. Careful patient selection and individualized counseling remain essential when considering CDA versus ACDF.
