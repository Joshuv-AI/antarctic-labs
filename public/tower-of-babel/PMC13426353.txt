Efficacy and Safety of CLAG-Based Sequential Conditioning Followed by Allogeneic Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation in Non-Remission Relapsed or Refractory Acute Leukemia
Authors: Shiyu Chen, Xiaohan Zhang, Xiaoqing Li, Xin Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal bridge-to-transplant strategy for patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) acute leukemia who remain in non-remission (NR) is undefined. We evaluated the strategy-level use of CLAG (cladribine, cytarabine, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor) as part of sequential conditioning, rather than as stand-alone salvage therapy, before allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT).MethodsThis single-center retrospective case series included nine consecutive patients with R/R acute leukemia and bone marrow blasts ≥10% who underwent CLAG-based sequential conditioning followed by allo-HSCT between January 2021 and July 2023. Engraftment, transplant-related complications, non-relapse mortality, relapse, disease-free survival (DFS), and overall survival (OS) were evaluated descriptively; no control group was available.ResultsAll nine patients achieved neutrophil and platelet engraftment and complete donor chimerism at approximately day +30. At a routine MFC/NGF analytical sensitivity of 10−4, all patients had negative bone marrow assessments at day +30 and month +3; abnormal/leukemic cells subsequently re-emerged in all five patients who relapsed, at months 4.1–9.8. Seven of nine patients (77.8%) developed infections during neutropenia, including one severe pulmonary infection with probable Aspergillus that improved with antifungal therapy. GVHD occurred in 3/9 patients (33.3%), and hemorrhagic cystitis occurred in 2/9 (22.2%). No non-relapse mortality occurred. Five patients (55.6%) relapsed, and all five deaths were relapse-related. Estimated 2-year DFS and OS were both 44.4% (95% confidence interval, 13.6–71.9%).ConclusionIn this highly selected cohort of nine NR patients, CLAG-based sequential conditioning permitted engraftment without non-relapse mortality, but infection and relapse remained frequent. These findings support technical feasibility only and should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating; efficacy or superiority cannot be established without larger prospective, multicenter, controlled studies.

Introduction

Relapsed or refractory acute leukemia (R/R AL) remains a major therapeutic challenge and is associated with limited survival. Conventional intensive salvage regimens, including FLAG (fludarabine, cytarabine, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor [G-CSF]), produce incomplete and often short-lived responses in many patients.1 Mutation-directed agents and venetoclax-based combinations have expanded treatment options in selected patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML),2 whereas blinatumomab and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy have improved response and bridging opportunities in relapsed/refractory acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL).3 Nevertheless, target availability, prior resistance, treatment-related toxicity, and rapid disease progression continue to limit the applicability of these approaches.

For patients who remain in non-remission (NR), allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) remains a potentially curative treatment option.4–8 Higher pre-transplant disease burden is associated with increased relapse and inferior survival after allo-HSCT,9–11 and failure to achieve complete remission is generally associated with poorer survival in AML.12 A clinically relevant knowledge gap is therefore how to achieve additional cytoreduction immediately before allo-HSCT without causing prohibitive toxicity or preventing timely transplantation. Existing approaches include intensive salvage or CLAG-based bridging,1,13,14 venetoclax- or mutation-directed treatment in selected patients,2 and intensified conditioning strategies.15,16 However, evidence specifically derived from NR populations remains limited and heterogeneous.

CLAG combines cladribine, cytarabine, and G-CSF. Cladribine has demonstrated antileukemic activity in AML.17,18 Mechanistically, cladribine may enhance the intracellular activity of cytarabine, whereas G-CSF priming is intended to recruit quiescent leukemic cells into the cell cycle and increase chemosensitivity.18,19 CLAG has also demonstrated clinical activity as salvage therapy in relapsed/refractory AML.2,20 A recent propensity-score-matched study evaluated CLAG as a separate bridging therapy before allo-HSCT in relapsed/refractory AML.14 The present study examines a related but distinct institutional approach in which CLAG was incorporated into an uninterrupted sequential conditioning platform immediately followed by allo-HSCT. This represents a strategy-level application rather than a new drug combination or mechanistic innovation.

We hypothesized that CLAG-based sequential conditioning could be delivered to selected NR patients without compromising engraftment or causing excessive non-relapse mortality. We therefore assessed engraftment, transplant-related complications, relapse, DFS, and OS in a consecutive single-center case series. Given the cohort size and absence of a comparator, the study was designed to evaluate feasibility and generate hypotheses rather than establish treatment efficacy.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective, single-center case series included nine consecutive patients with R/R acute leukemia who remained in NR immediately before conditioning and underwent CLAG-based sequential conditioning followed by allo-HSCT at The First Affiliated Hospital of Shenzhen University/Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital between January 2021 and July 2023. NR was operationally defined as bone marrow blasts ≥10% before conditioning. The screening frame comprised 323 consecutive patients with acute leukemia who underwent evaluation for allo-HSCT at our center from January 2014 to July 2023. The nine included patients were not selected according to post-transplant outcome. Nevertheless, the need to be clinically eligible for myeloablative allo-HSCT and to complete the institutional protocol introduces unavoidable selection bias. No contemporaneous control group treated with another bridge or conditioning strategy was available; consequently, all outcome analyses were descriptive and no causal attribution to CLAG was attempted.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Shenzhen University/Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital (approval No. 2024-433-01PJ). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before treatment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Donor and Graft Procedures

All donors were related: six were siblings and three were offspring-to-parent donors. High-resolution HLA typing identified eight 5–9/10 matched related donors and one 10/10 matched related donor (Table 1). Donors received G-CSF mobilization for four consecutive days. Peripheral blood stem cells were collected on day 0 and quantified by flow cytometry. Target peripheral-blood graft doses were mononuclear cells (MNCs) ≥5 × 108/kg and CD34+ cells ≥2 × 106/kg. As part of the institutional routine combined-graft protocol, approximately 400 mL of bone marrow was collected from the same donor on the following day for all patients, irrespective of whether the target peripheral-blood MNC or CD34+ cell dose had been achieved. Prophylaxis and Post-Transplant Monitoring

GVHD prophylaxis consisted of cyclosporine A (CsA), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and short-course methotrexate (MTX). CsA was started on day −1 at 2.5 mg/kg by continuous infusion in adults, with trough concentrations maintained at 100–250 microg/mL. MTX was administered at 15 mg/m2 on day +1 and 10 mg/m2 on days +3 and +6. MMF was started on day −11 at 30 mg/kg/day orally and continued until day +50.

Institutional infection prophylaxis included antibacterial, antiviral, and antifungal agents. Posaconazole was continued after transplantation for antifungal prophylaxis. Hemorrhagic cystitis prophylaxis included urinary catheterization before cyclophosphamide, mesna at 0, 4, and 8 h after cyclophosphamide, urine alkalinization, hydration (2000 mL/m2/day), and furosemide-supported diuresis.

Bone marrow disease assessment by multiparameter/next-generation flow cytometry (MFC/NGF), short tandem repeat (STR) chimerism, and patient-specific genetic monitoring were scheduled at baseline, day +30, and months 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24. MFC/NGF had a routine analytical sensitivity of 10−4; a reported value of 0 was classified as negative. Patient-level serial results are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Contemporary AML guidance emphasizes standardized, disease- and marker-specific interpretation of flow-cytometric and molecular MRD.21 Because this cohort included both AML and ETP-ALL, the sample size was small, and recurrent values frequently represented overt disease rather than low-level MRD, no formal MRD-outcome association was tested. Molecular or MFC/NGF recurrence could prompt individualized interventions such as donor lymphocyte infusion (DLI), interferon, or earlier tapering of immunosuppression. The two FLT3-ITD-positive patients (cases 8 and 9) received post-transplant maintenance with gilteritinib beginning at approximately month +3 after allo-HSCT. Gilteritinib was started at a reduced dose of 80 mg once daily and was subsequently adjusted on an individual basis according to treatment tolerability and serial MFC/NGF findings. Because post-transplant interventions were individualized and were not administered within a standardized prospective protocol, their independent effects on relapse or survival could not be determined.

Outcomes and Definitions

Neutrophil engraftment was defined as the first of three consecutive days with an absolute neutrophil count ≥0.5 × 109/L. Platelet engraftment was defined as the first of seven consecutive days with a platelet count ≥20 × 109/L without platelet transfusion. Complete donor chimerism was assessed by STR analysis at approximately day +30. Non-relapse mortality was defined as death without preceding leukemia relapse or progression. Relapse/progression was determined from bone marrow morphology, molecular/cytogenetic evidence, or extramedullary disease as documented by the treating team. Acute and chronic GVHD diagnoses, involved organs, and severity grades were retrospectively abstracted as documented by the treating physicians in the source medical records. Because the original grading framework could not be uniformly verified, no retrospective reclassification was attempted. OS was calculated from allo-HSCT to death from any cause or last follow-up. DFS was calculated from allo-HSCT to documented relapse/progression or death; patients alive without morphologic disease were censored at last follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10. Categorical outcomes are presented as exact counts with percentages, and continuous variables as medians and ranges. OS and DFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method with 95% confidence intervals. Given the sample size (n = 9), disease heterogeneity, and absence of a comparator, no subgroup hypothesis testing, multivariable modeling, or treatment-effect estimation was performed.

Infections and Other Transplant-Related Complications

Seven of nine patients (77.8%) developed an infection during the neutropenic period. The median time to the first infection was day +3 (range, day −10 to day +17), with most events occurring between day −3 and day +7. One patient developed a severe pulmonary infection; bronchoalveolar lavage findings were consistent with probable Aspergillus infection, and the patient improved with voriconazole plus caspofungin. No infection-related death occurred before relapse. Intensive care unit utilization was not consistently available in the retrospective dataset and is therefore not reported.

GVHD occurred in 3/9 patients (33.3%): one patient developed skin/liver acute GVHD on day +62, one developed severe intestinal chronic GVHD at month 4, and one developed skin chronic GVHD at month 5. Relapse, Post-Transplant Interventions, and Survival

Five patients (55.6%) relapsed at a median of 5.7 months (range, 4.1–9.8); four relapses occurred between months 3 and 6 and one between months 6 and 12. Both ETP-ALL patients (2/2) and three of seven AML patients relapsed. All five patients who relapsed subsequently died, generally in the context of severe infection during post-relapse therapy or, for case 9, after a second transplantation. At last follow-up, four patients (all with AML) were alive and disease-free.

Serial MFC/NGF assessments showed negative bone marrow results in all nine patients at day +30 and month +3. Abnormal/leukemic cells subsequently re-emerged in each of the five patients who relapsed: 13% in case 1 at month +4.1, 31% in case 7 at month +4.9, 22% and 58% in cases 4 and 9, respectively, at month +6, and 42% in case 2 at month +9.8. Where repeat assessments were available, the abnormal-cell percentage increased further (case 4, 87% at month +9; case 9, 91% at month +9; case 2, 67% at month +12). Cases 3, 5, 6, and 8 remained MFC/NGF-negative through their last available assessments. These observations are descriptive only and are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Cases 8 and 9 received gilteritinib maintenance beginning at month +3 after allo-HSCT. Case 8 remained alive and disease-free at 30.6 months, whereas case 9 experienced relapse at 5.8 months and died at 9.3 months. Given the two-patient sample and the absence of a standardized comparator, no causal inference regarding the efficacy of gilteritinib maintenance can be made.

Discussion

This case series evaluates a strategy-level application of CLAG as part of sequential conditioning immediately before allo-HSCT in patients with R/R acute leukemia who remained in NR. The principal feasibility signals were universal engraftment, complete donor chimerism at approximately day +30, and no non-relapse mortality. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of efficacy or superiority because only nine highly selected patients were treated, no control group was available, and the conditioning and post-transplant interventions were heterogeneous.

Historical studies of allo-HSCT in refractory or NR acute leukemia have reported approximately 14–40% 3-year OS, with relapse as the dominant cause of failure.6–8 Our estimated 2-year OS of 44.4% falls within the broad range of these heterogeneous reports, but the 95% confidence interval was wide (13.6–71.9%), and cross-study comparison is not a substitute for a concurrent control. Reports of cladribine-containing sequential or conditioning approaches suggest that such regimens can be delivered before allo-HSCT.22–25 In addition, a larger propensity-score-matched study specifically evaluated CLAG as bridging therapy before allo-HSCT in relapsed/refractory AML.14 Differences in disease status, response before transplantation, treatment sequence, donor type, conditioning platform, and maintenance strategy preclude direct comparison with the present cohort.

The biological rationale for CLAG is consistent with its use as an immediate cytoreductive component: cladribine may enhance cytarabine activity, cytarabine inhibits leukemic proliferation, and G-CSF priming may increase cell-cycle entry and chemosensitivity.18,19 The contribution of the present work is therefore a patient-level description of CLAG incorporated into an uninterrupted sequential conditioning platform for selected NR patients, rather than a new drug combination or evidence that this strategy is superior to other bridging approaches. The current data cannot separate the contribution of CLAG from FBCA/BuCy, graft-versus-leukemia effects, donor characteristics, or post-transplant therapy.

Toxicity requires cautious interpretation. Although there was no non-relapse mortality and only one transient episode of drug-induced liver injury, infection occurred in 7/9 patients (77.8%), including one severe probable Aspergillus pneumonia. We therefore revised the description from broadly “acceptable toxicity” to a more specific statement: engraftment was preserved and no non-relapse death occurred, but the infectious burden was substantial. Similarly, relapse in 5/9 patients (55.6%) indicates that disease control remained limited despite successful transplantation.

Post-transplant relapse prevention is likely important in this population. FLT3-inhibitor maintenance, immunosuppression tapering, DLI, interferon, and other immune- or target-directed approaches may be considered according to molecular risk and clinical status. In a randomized study of post-transplant gilteritinib for FLT3-ITD-positive AML, relapse-free survival was not significantly improved in the overall population, although the findings suggested greater benefit in patients with detectable peri-transplant FLT3-ITD MRD.26 This evidence provides clinical context but does not establish benefit in our two treated patients, who received individualized lower-dose maintenance and had divergent outcomes. Serial MFC/NGF findings in this cohort were clinically coherent: all nine patients were negative at day +30 and month +3, abnormal/leukemic cells re-emerged at or near relapse in all five patients who relapsed, and the four patients alive and disease-free remained negative through their last available assessments. However, this pattern should not be interpreted as evidence of predictive validity or treatment effect because the cohort was very small, no comparator was available, and recurrent values commonly represented overt disease rather than low-level MRD. Prospective protocols should standardize post-transplant interventions and incorporate harmonized flow-cytometric and molecular MRD assessment in accordance with disease-specific guidance.21

This study has several major limitations: retrospective single-center design; very small sample size; absence of a control group; selection of patients sufficiently fit to complete myeloablative transplantation; inclusion of both AML and ETP-ALL; heterogeneity in donor HLA matching and conditioning (FBCA, BuCy, and decitabine); individualized supportive and post-transplant treatment; lack of harmonized disease-specific molecular MRD testing; inability to formally evaluate the prognostic value of serial MFC/NGF kinetics; and limited statistical power. These limitations preclude treatment-effect estimation and restrict generalizability. The results should be viewed as a feasibility signal that justifies, but does not replace, prospective multicenter evaluation.

Conclusion

CLAG-based sequential conditioning followed by allo-HSCT was technically feasible in this highly selected nine-patient NR cohort, with universal engraftment and no non-relapse mortality. However, infections were frequent and more than half of the patients relapsed. The study is exploratory and hypothesis-generating and does not support routine clinical adoption or superiority over other bridge/conditioning strategies. Larger prospective, multicenter, controlled studies with standardized MRD assessment and post-transplant relapse-prevention strategies are required.
