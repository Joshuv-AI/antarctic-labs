Global o ⁡ ( 1 / k 2 ) o(1/k^{2}) Merit Complexity of Regularized Newton Methods for Convex Multiobjective Optimization
Yuqia Wu, Yue Wang, Yaohua Hu

Abstract
We investigate a regularized Newton method for unconstrained convex multi-objective optimization with twice continuously differentiable objectives whose Hessians are Lipschitz continuous. At each iteration, the method minimizes the quadratically regularized max-envelope of the local quadratic models. Using a Tanabe-type merit function, we prove that this merit decays at the global asymptotic rate o ⁡ ( 1 / k 2 ) o(1/k^{2}) under the compactness assumption on the initial component-wise lower level set. This result also covers the single-objective case as a special case. Finally, we construct an explicit one-dimensional convex bi-objective family showing that no uniform merit estimate of order 𝒪 ⁡ ( k − ( 2 + δ ) ) \mathcal{O}(k^{-(2+\delta)}) can hold for any fixed δ > 0 \delta>0 . Thus the exponent 2 2 is essentially sharp in the uniform polynomial sense, despite the o ⁡ ( 1 / k 2 ) o(1/k^{2}) decay on each fixed trajectory.



1
Introduction

We consider the unconstrained convex multi-objective optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(x):=(f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{m}(x)),

(1)

where each component function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and twice continuously differentiable, and
\nabla^{2}f_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous with modulus
H_{i}>0
. We write

H:=\max_{i\in[m]}H_{i}.

Problems of the form (
1
) arise when several competing criteria must be optimized simultaneously, and the natural goal is to compute a weak Pareto optimal point rather than a minimizer of a prescribed scalarization.

For smooth multi-objective optimization, global complexity theory has been developed for first-order descent methods. A foundational contribution is the steepest descent method by Fliege and Svaiter
[
4
]
, which computes a common descent direction by solving a convex subproblem and does not require a fixed scalarization in advance. More precisely, at a current iterate
x^{k}
, the steepest descent method updates

x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\alpha_{k}d^{k}\ \ {\rm with}\ \ d^{k}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\max_{i\in[m]}\langle\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),d\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|d\|^{2}\right\},

where
\alpha_{k}>0
is chosen by a suitable stepsize rule. The subproblem selects a direction that balances descent among all component functions and preserves the scalarization-free nature of the method. Later work clarified the worst-case complexity of this method. In particular, Fliege, Vaz, and Vicente
[
5
]
showed that multi-objective gradient descent has complexity bounds parallel to those of scalar gradient descent, including an
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1/2})
stationarity rate for nonconvex objectives, an
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
rate in the convex case, and a linear rate under strong convexity.

This first-order min-max viewpoint has also been extended beyond the smooth setting. For composite multi-objective problems, Tanabe, Fukuda, and Yamashita
[
17
]
introduced proximal gradient methods, and provided rates of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1/2})
,
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
, and linear convergence based on suitable merit functions in nonconvex, convex, and strongly convex or PL-type settings, respectively
[
19
,
20
]
. A distinctive feature of accelerated multi-objective methods is that the active convex combination of objective gradients may vary along the iterates. El Moudden and El Mouatasim
[
2
]
obtained an
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
rate for an accelerated diagonal steepest descent method under an eventual fixed-multiplier assumption. Tanabe, Fukuda, and Yamashita
[
18
]
later proposed an accelerated proximal gradient method and proved a global
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
rate for a merit function under less restrictive assumptions. This rate was refined by Zhang and Yang
[
22
]
, which showed that the convergence rate of the multi-objective accelerated proximal gradient method can be improved from
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
to
o(k^{-2})
. Related inertial and Nesterov-type approaches obtain
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
- and
\mathcal{O}(t^{-2})
-type rates in discrete and continuous-time settings, respectively
[
16
,
15
]
. These results indicated that the merit complexity is now well developed for first-order multi-objective methods, especially in the convex setting.

By contrast, although second-order methods for multi-objective optimization have been studied for a long time, their global merit complexity theory is far from completion. Fliege, Graña Drummond, and Svaiter
[
3
]
proposed a Newton method whose direction is obtained from minimizing a max-envelope of the quadratic models of the component functions, and proved the convergence to Pareto critical points, as well as local quadratic convergence under additional strongly convex assumptions. Quasi-Newton methods were developed to reduce the cost of Hessian evaluations, including the methods of Qu, Goh, and Chan
[
13
]
, Povalej
[
11
]
, and recent BFGS-type schemes
[
12
]
for nonconvex multi-objective problems. Regularization has also been explored at this line of work. For convex multi-objective optimization, Wang and Liu
[
21
]
proposed a regularized Newton method and proved the global convergence under compact level-set assumptions. Recently, cubic-regularization methods have provided stationarity-type guarantees for nonconvex multiobjective optimization, including
O(k^{-2/3})
-type rates in
[
6
,
7
]
, and the latter also covers inexact derivative information and finite-difference implementations.

These results leave open a different question in the convex setting. In scalar convex optimization, a central complexity measure is the objective gap
f(x^{k})-f^{\star}
, not only stationarity residuals. For multi-objective problems, an analogous role is played by merit functions
[
20
]
, which vanish precisely on the weak Pareto solution set. The available second-order theory controls convergence to Pareto criticality, local behavior, or the number of iterations needed to make a stationarity residual small, but it does not provide decay estimates for such merit functions along the generated iterates. This is the complexity gap addressed in the present paper.

The scalar optimization theory provides a useful reference. For smooth convex optimization, gradient descent has the classical
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
rate, while Nesterov’s accelerated method has
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
rate
[
9
]
. In the composite case, FISTA gives the same order
[
1
]
. For second-order methods with Lipschitz continuous Hessians, the cubic regularized Newton method of Nesterov and Polyak
[
10
]
achieves a global
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
rate on convex problems. Recently, Mishchenko
[
8
]
showed that a regularized Newton method with a square-root regularization rule also attains a global
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
rate.
Compared with the mature scalar theory, second-order methods for convex multi-objective optimization still lack sharp global rates for merit functions, which play the role of objective gaps in the scalar case.

Motivated by this observation, the present paper develops a multi-objective regularized Newton method. At iteration
k
, the method computes
d^{k}
by minimizing the max-envelope of the local quadratic models, regularized by
\eta_{k}\|d\|^{2}/2
, where
\eta_{k}
is related to the current multi-objective stationarity measure.
A central issue in the analysis of multi-objective methods is the choice of a scalar merit function, because the objective value is vector-valued and therefore does not by itself define a scalar optimality gap. We use the Tanabe-type merit function
[
20
]
:

\mathcal{U}(x):=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\min_{i\in[m]}\big\{f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(z)\big\}.

(2)

This function is exact for weak Pareto optimality in the sense that
\mathcal{U}(x)\geq 0
and
\mathcal{U}(x)=0
if and only if
x
is a weak Pareto optimal point; see
[
20
, Theorem 3.1]
. Therefore, complexity estimates for
\mathcal{U}(x^{k})
play the role of objective gap estimates in scalar convex optimization.

The paper makes two main contributions.

(i)

We prove the global merit complexity estimate

\mathcal{U}(x^{k})=o(1/k^{2})

for the regularized Newton method. This gives a second-order counterpart to the accelerated first-order merit rates known for convex multi-objective optimization
[
18
,
22
]
. In particular, when
m=1
, the method reduces to the square-root regularized Newton method studied by Mishchenko
[
8
]
, and the merit function
\mathcal{U}
reduces to the usual scalar objective gap. Thus, our result recovers the scalar
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{2})
bound and further refines it to
o(1/k^{2})
.

Moreover, it is worth noting that the analysis is not a direct extension of the scalar argument of Mishchenko
[
8
]
. In the multi-objective setting, there is no distinguished objective gap
f(x^{k})-f^{\star}
, and the active convex combination of component models may vary along the trajectory. These features require a separate merit analysis for the multi-objective method.

(ii)

We show that the polynomial exponent
2
is essentially sharp in a uniform worst-case sense. We construct an explicit one-dimensional convex bi-objective family, and show that no estimate of the form

\mathcal{U}(x^{k})=\mathcal{O}(k^{-(2+\delta)})

can hold uniformly over the family for any
\delta>0
. Hence the exponent
2
cannot be improved uniformly, even though the actual rate is
o(1/k^{2})
.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the notations and collects preliminary facts on weak Pareto optimality and the stationarity measure. Section
3
presents the multi-objective regularized Newton method, establishes its descent estimates, and proves the global
o(1/k^{2})
merit rate. Section
4
constructs an explicit barrier family and shows the uniform sharpness of the polynomial exponent
2
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

Throughout this paper,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space equipped with the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the induced norm
\|\cdot\|
. We write
\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,\infty)
.
For a positive integer
m
, let
[m]:=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
. For vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the inequalities
u\leq v
and
u<v
are understood component-wise, that is,
u_{i}\leq v_{i}
and
u_{i}<v_{i}
for all
i\in[m]
, respectively. For matrices,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the induced operator norm, and
A\succeq 0
means that the symmetric matrix
A
is positive semidefinite.
Let

\Delta_{m}:=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}:\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}

be the unit simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
For a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\operatorname{conv}(S)
denotes its convex hull.
Given an initial point
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the component-wise lower level set of
F
at
x^{0}
is

\operatorname{Lev}_{F}(x^{0}):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ f_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(x^{0}),\ \forall i\in[m]\}.

2.2
Basic properties and Pareto optimality

We collect two elementary consequences of the Hessian Lipschitz continuity of the component functions. These estimates will be used in the analysis of the regularized Newton step. Recall that
H>0
is chosen as a common Hessian Lipschitz constant, namely

\|\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x)-\nabla^{2}f_{i}(y)\|\leq H\|x-y\|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ i\in[m].

We recall the following two inequalities established in
[
10
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
x,d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
i\in[m]
,
it holds that

\displaystyle f_{i}(x+d)\leq f_{i}(x)+\langle\nabla f_{i}(x),d\rangle+\frac{1}{2}d^{\top}\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x)d+\frac{H}{6}\|d\|^{3},

(3)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{i}(x+d)-\nabla f_{i}(x)-\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x)d\|\leq\frac{H}{2}\|d\|^{2}.

(4)

We next recall the basic notions of optimality for the multi-objective problem (
1
).

Definition 2.2
.

A point
x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
Pareto optimal point
if there is no
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

F(y)\leq F(x^{\star})\quad{\rm and}\quad F(y)\neq F(x^{\star}).

A point
x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
weak Pareto optimal point
if there is no
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

F(y)<F(x^{\star}).

We denote the weak Pareto solution set by
\mathcal{X}_{w}
.

The following characterization states that weak Pareto optimality is equivalent to the nonexistence of a direction that strictly decreases all first-order linearizations; see, e.g.,
[
4
,
3
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

x
is a weak Pareto optimal point of (
1
).

(ii)

there is no direction
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\langle\nabla f_{i}(x),d\rangle<0,\ \forall i\in[m].

(iii)

There exists
\lambda\in\Delta_{m}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\nabla f_{i}(x)=0
. Equivalently,

0\in\operatorname{conv}\{\nabla f_{i}(x):i\in[m]\}.

We also use the stationarity measure and the associated set of minimizing
multipliers

s(x):=\min_{\lambda\in\Delta_{m}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\nabla f_{i}(x)\right\|,\qquad\Lambda(x):=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\lambda\in\Delta_{m}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\nabla f_{i}(x)\right\|.

(5)

The value
s(x)
is the distance from the origin to
\operatorname{conv}\{\nabla f_{i}(x):i\in[m]\}
. Hence, by Lemma
2.3
,
s(x)=0
if and only if
x
is weak Pareto optimal. The set
\Lambda(x)
is nonempty because
\Delta_{m}
is compact.

The continuity of
s(\cdot)
is a standard consequence of the continuity theorem for marginal functions over compact parameter sets; see, e.g.,
[
14
, Theorems 1.17]
. We include a short direct proof for
completeness.

Lemma 2.4
.

The function
s
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let

G(x,\lambda):=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\nabla f_{i}(x)\right\|,\quad\forall(x,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Delta_{m}.

Then
G
is continuous and

s(x)=\min_{\lambda\in\Delta_{m}}G(x,\lambda).

Let
x^{j}\to\bar{x}
. For any
\bar{\lambda}\in\Lambda(\bar{x})
,

\limsup_{j\to\infty}s(x^{j})\leq\lim_{j\to\infty}G(x^{j},\bar{\lambda})=G(\bar{x},\bar{\lambda})=s(\bar{x}).

For the reverse inequality, take a subsequence, denoted by
\{x^{j}\}
for the sake of simplicity, such that
s(x^{j})\to\liminf_{j}s(x^{j})
. Choose
\lambda^{j}\in\Lambda(x^{j})
. Since
\Delta_{m}
is compact, passing to a further subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
\lambda^{j}\to\lambda^{\ast}\in\Delta_{m}
. Hence

\liminf_{j\to\infty}s(x^{j})=\lim_{j\to\infty}G(x^{j},\lambda^{j})=G(\bar{x},\lambda^{\ast})\geq s(\bar{x}).

Therefore
\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}s(x^{j})=s(\bar{x})
, and the proof is complete.
∎

3
Multi-objective Regularized Newton Method

In this section, we introduce the multi-objective regularized Newton method. Recall that the stationarity measure
s(\cdot)
and the multiplier set
\Lambda(\cdot)
are defined in (
5
).

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and direction
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we approximate the objective functions by the local quadratic models

q_{i}(d;x):=\langle\nabla f_{i}(x),d\rangle+\frac{1}{2}d^{\top}\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x)d,\quad\forall i\in[m].

(6)

The quantity
q_{i}(d;x)
is the second-order model of the increment
f_{i}(x+d)-f_{i}(x)
. Since each
f_{i}
is convex,
\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x)\succeq 0
, and hence
q_{i}(\cdot;x)
is convex.

Based on these definitions, the detailed iterative procedure of the multi-objective regularized Newton method is formally presented in Algorithm
1
.

1:

Initial point
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2:

for

k=0,1,2,\dots

do

3:

Compute the stationarity measure
s_{k}=s(x^{k})
.

4:

if

s_{k}=0

then

5:

stop
and return
x^{k}
.

6:

end

if

7:

Set the regularization parameter
\eta_{k}=2\sqrt{Hs_{k}}
.

8:

Compute the search direction
d^{k}
by solving the subproblem:

9:

d^{k}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\max_{i\in[m]}q_{i}(d;x^{k})+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|d\|^{2}\right\}

(7)

10:

Update the iterate:
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+d^{k}
.

11:

end

for

Remark 3.1
.

(i) The subproblem (
7
) in Algorithm
1
is well defined. Indeed, whenever
s_{k}>0
, the regularization parameter
\eta_{k}=2\sqrt{Hs_{k}}
is positive. Since each
q_{i}(\cdot;x^{k})
is convex, the function

d\mapsto\max_{i\in[m]}q_{i}(d;x^{k})+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|d\|^{2}

is strongly convex and coercive. Hence the subproblem has a unique minimizer, and the direction
d^{k}
is uniquely determined.

(ii) When
m=1
, we have
s(x)=\|\nabla f_{1}(x)\|
, and the subproblem reduces to

\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\langle\nabla f_{1}(x^{k}),d\rangle+\frac{1}{2}d^{\top}\nabla^{2}f_{1}(x^{k})d+\sqrt{H\|\nabla f_{1}(x^{k})\|}\|d\|^{2}\right\}.

Thus

d^{k}=-\bigl(\nabla^{2}f_{1}(x^{k})+2\sqrt{H\|\nabla f_{1}(x^{k})\|}I\bigr)^{-1}\nabla f_{1}(x^{k}),

which is the square-root regularized Newton step
[
8
]
. For
m\geq 2
, the method replaces this scalar quadratic model by the max-envelope of the component quadratic models, and therefore computes a common descent direction.

To proceed with the convergence analysis, we denote the step length at iteration
k
as

r_{k}:=\|d^{k}\|=\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|.

(8)

We impose the following compactness assumption on the initial component-wise lower level set.

Assumption 1
.

The initial level set
\mathcal{L}_{0}:=\operatorname{Lev}_{F}(x^{0})
is compact. That is, there exists a constant
D_{0}>0
such that
\|x-y\|\leq D_{0}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
.

In the single-objective case, Assumption
1
reduces to the bounded level set condition used in
[
8
, Assumption 2.5]
, up to the choice of the diameter constant. Thus it is a natural component-wise extension of that assumption to the multiobjective setting.

To measure the progress of the algorithm, we use the merit function proposed by Tanabe et al.
[
20
]
; see (
2
). Although the definition in (
2
) is taken over the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the descent property of Algorithm
1
will be shown to keep the iterates inside the initial component-wise lower level set
\mathcal{L}_{0}
. For the complexity analysis, we further need to ensure that
\mathcal{U}
is finite along the generated trajectory and to relate it to the stationarity measure
s(\cdot)
. The following proposition establishes these properties on the initial component-wise lower level set.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds. For any
x\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
, the following hold:

(i)

\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}\neq\varnothing
, and

\mathcal{U}(x)=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}}\min_{i\in[m]}\{f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(z)\}.

In particular,
\mathcal{U}(x)
is finite.

(ii)

\mathcal{U}(x)\leq D_{0}\,s(x)
.

Proof.

(i) For any
z\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
, define

\mathcal{S}_{z}:=\{u\in\mathcal{L}_{0}:\ f_{i}(u)\leq f_{i}(z),\ i\in[m]\}.

Then
\mathcal{S}_{z}
is nonempty and compact. Let
\hat{z}
minimize
\Phi(u):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(u)
over
\mathcal{S}_{z}
. We claim that
\hat{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{w}
. Otherwise, there exists
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
F(y)<F(\hat{z})
. Since
\hat{z}\in\mathcal{S}_{z}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{0}
, we have

F(y)<F(\hat{z})\leq F(z)\leq F(x^{0}),

and hence
y\in\mathcal{S}_{z}
. This gives
\Phi(y)<\Phi(\hat{z})
, contradicting the minimality of
\hat{z}
. Thus
\hat{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
F(\hat{z})\leq F(z)
. Taking
z=x^{0}
shows that
\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}\neq\varnothing
.

Now fix
x\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
and write

\psi_{x}(z):=\min_{i\in[m]}\{f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(z)\}.

If
z\notin\mathcal{L}_{0}
, then for some
j\in[m]
,

f_{j}(z)>f_{j}(x^{0})\geq f_{j}(x)
, and therefore

\psi_{x}(z)\leq f_{j}(x)-f_{j}(z)<0.

On the other hand,
\psi_{x}(x)=0
. Hence points outside
\mathcal{L}_{0}
cannot increase
the supremum in (
2
), and

\mathcal{U}(x)=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{L}_{0}}\psi_{x}(z).

For every
z\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
, the observation above gives

\hat{z}\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}
with
F(\hat{z})\leq F(z)
. Hence, for every
i\in[m]
,

f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(z)\leq f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(\hat{z}).

Taking the minimum over
i\in[m]
gives

\psi_{x}(z)\leq\psi_{x}(\hat{z})\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}}\psi_{x}(\xi).

Taking the supremum over
z\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
yields

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{L}_{0}}\psi_{x}(z)\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}}\psi_{x}(\xi).

The reverse inequality follows from
\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{0}
. Hence

\mathcal{U}(x)=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}}\min_{i\in[m]}\{f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(z)\}.

Since
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is compact and the component functions are continuous,

\mathcal{U}(x)
is finite.

(ii) Fix
x\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
, and choose a multiplier
\omega\in\Lambda(x)
. By definition,
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\nabla f_{i}(x)\right\|=s(x)
. Let
z\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}
. Utilizing the convexity of
f_{i}
, we obtain

f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(z)\leq\langle\nabla f_{i}(x),x-z\rangle,\quad\forall i\in[m].

Since
\omega\in\Delta_{m}
, we can bound the minimum difference as follows:

\min_{i\in[m]}\{f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(z)\}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\bigl(f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(z)\bigr)\leq\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\nabla f_{i}(x),x-z\right\rangle\leq s(x)\|x-z\|.

Because both
x,z\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
, Assumption
1
guarantees that
\|x-z\|\leq D_{0}
. Taking the supremum over all
z\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}
and using part (i) yields
\mathcal{U}(x)\leq D_{0}\,s(x)
, completing the proof.
∎

\max_{i}q_{i}(d^{k};x^{k})+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2}\leq 0.

(13)

By convexity,
\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x^{k})\succeq 0
, and hence

q_{i}(d^{k};x^{k})\geq\langle\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),d^{k}\rangle,\quad i\in[m].

Pick
\omega_{k}\in\Lambda(x^{k}).
Therefore,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\max_{i}q_{i}(d^{k};x^{k})+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2}\geq\max_{i}\langle\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),d^{k}\rangle+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}r_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}^{k}\langle\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),d^{k}\rangle+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}r_{k}^{2}=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}^{k}\nabla f_{i}(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}r_{k}^{2}\geq-s_{k}r_{k}+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}r_{k}^{2},

where the last inequality follows from the definition of
s_{k}.
This
proves (
10
).

Since
\eta_{k}=2\sqrt{Hs_{k}}
, we have
\eta_{k}^{2}=4Hs_{k}
. Multiplying
(
10
) by
H/\eta_{k}
gives

Hr_{k}\leq\frac{2Hs_{k}}{\eta_{k}}=\frac{\eta_{k}}{2},

which establishes (
11
).

It remains to bound
s_{k+1}
. Let
\alpha^{k}=(\alpha_{1}^{k},\ldots,\alpha_{m}^{k})\in\Delta_{m}
be the multiplier vector given by Lemma
3.3
. Since
\alpha^{k}
is feasible in the definition of
s(x^{k+1})
, we have

s_{k+1}\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}^{k}\nabla f_{i}(x^{k+1})\right\|,

which by the triangle inequality implies

\displaystyle s_{k+1}\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}^{k}\bigl(\nabla f_{i}(x^{k+1})-\nabla f_{i}(x^{k})-\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x^{k})d^{k}\bigr)\right\|+\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}^{k}\bigl(\nabla f_{i}(x^{k})+\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x^{k})d^{k}\bigr)\right\|.

By Lemma
2.1
and (
9
), we obtain

s_{k+1}\leq\frac{H}{2}r_{k}^{2}+\eta_{k}r_{k}.

Using (
11
), we get
\frac{H}{2}r_{k}^{2}\leq\frac{\eta_{k}}{4}r_{k}
, hence

s_{k+1}\leq\frac{5}{4}\eta_{k}r_{k}.

Combining this with (
10
) proves the second inequality in
(
12
).
∎

The estimates above imply a component-wise descent property, which keeps the iterates in the initial level set and yields a one-step decrease of the merit function.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds, and let
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be
generated by Algorithm
1
. Then, for every nonterminal iteration

k
with
s_{k}>0
, the following statements hold:

(i)

For every
i\in[m]
,

f_{i}(x^{k+1})\leq f_{i}(x^{k})-\frac{5}{12}\eta_{k}r_{k}^{2}.

Consequently,
F(x^{k+1})\leq F(x^{k})
, and hence
x^{k}\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
for all
generated iterates.

(ii)

Let
u_{k}:=\mathcal{U}(x^{k})
. Then

u_{k+1}\leq u_{k}-\frac{5}{12}\eta_{k}r_{k}^{2}.

Proof.

(i) Fix
i\in[m]
. Since
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+d^{k}
, Lemma
2.1

gives

f_{i}(x^{k+1})\leq f_{i}(x^{k})+q_{i}(d^{k};x^{k})+\frac{H}{6}r_{k}^{3}.

Moreover, since
d=0
is feasible in (
7
), the optimality
of
d^{k}
yields

\max_{j\in[m]}q_{j}(d^{k};x^{k})+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}r_{k}^{2}\leq 0.

Thus we get

q_{i}(d^{k};x^{k})\leq\max_{j\in[m]}q_{j}(d^{k};x^{k})\leq-\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}r_{k}^{2}.

Combining the last two estimates, we obtain

f_{i}(x^{k+1})\leq f_{i}(x^{k})-\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}r_{k}^{2}+\frac{H}{6}r_{k}^{3}.

By (
11
),
Hr_{k}\leq\eta_{k}/2
, and hence

\frac{H}{6}r_{k}^{3}\leq\frac{\eta_{k}}{12}r_{k}^{2}.

Therefore, it holds that

f_{i}(x^{k+1})\leq f_{i}(x^{k})-\frac{5}{12}\eta_{k}r_{k}^{2}.

This proves the component-wise descent
F(x^{k+1})\leq F(x^{k})
. Since
x^{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
, the inclusion
x^{k}\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
for all generated iterates follows by induction.

(ii) Fix any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By part (i), for every
i\in[m]
,

f_{i}(x^{k+1})-f_{i}(z)\leq f_{i}(x^{k})-f_{i}(z)-\frac{5}{12}\eta_{k}r_{k}^{2}.

Taking the minimum over
i\in[m]
gives

\min_{i\in[m]}\{f_{i}(x^{k+1})-f_{i}(z)\}\leq\min_{i\in[m]}\{f_{i}(x^{k})-f_{i}(z)\}-\frac{5}{12}\eta_{k}r_{k}^{2}.

Since this inequality holds for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the definition of
\mathcal{U}
implies

u_{k+1}\leq u_{k}-\frac{5}{12}\eta_{k}r_{k}^{2}.

∎

We now distinguish iterations according to the behavior of the stationarity measure. The following set collects the iterations at which
s_{k+1}
does not decrease too much relative to
s_{k}
:

\mathcal{I}_{\infty}:=\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\ s_{k+1}\geq\frac{1}{4}s_{k}\right\}.

(14)

For each
k\geq 1
, define the truncated set

\mathcal{I}_{k}:=\mathcal{I}_{\infty}\cap\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}.

(15)

The next lemma shows that every iteration in
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
produces a quantitative decrease of the merit function.

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds. If
k\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{5}{12}\eta_{k}r_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s_{k}^{3/2}}{120\sqrt{H}},

(16)

\displaystyle u_{k+1}\leq u_{k}-\tau

\displaystyle u_{k}^{3/2},\quad\tau:=\frac{1}{120D_{0}^{3/2}\sqrt{H}}.

(17)

Proof.

Let
k\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
and
(
12
),

\frac{1}{4}s_{k}\leq s_{k+1}\leq\frac{5}{4}\eta_{k}r_{k}.

Hence

\eta_{k}r_{k}\geq\frac{1}{5}s_{k}.

Since
\eta_{k}=2\sqrt{Hs_{k}}
, we also obtain

r_{k}\geq\frac{s_{k}}{5\eta_{k}}=\frac{\sqrt{s_{k}}}{10\sqrt{H}}.

Combining the two bounds gives

\frac{5}{12}\eta_{k}r_{k}^{2}=\frac{5}{12}(\eta_{k}r_{k})r_{k}\geq\frac{5}{12}\cdot\frac{s_{k}}{5}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{s_{k}}}{10\sqrt{H}}=\frac{s_{k}^{3/2}}{120\sqrt{H}}.

This proves (
16
).

By Proposition
3.2
(ii),
u_{k}\leq D_{0}s_{k}
, and therefore

s_{k}^{3/2}\geq\frac{u_{k}^{3/2}}{D_{0}^{3/2}}.

Consequently,

\frac{5}{12}\eta_{k}r_{k}^{2}\geq\frac{u_{k}^{3/2}}{120D_{0}^{3/2}\sqrt{H}}=\tau u_{k}^{3/2}.

Combining this estimate with Lemma
3.5
(ii) gives

u_{k+1}\leq u_{k}-\tau u_{k}^{3/2},

which proves (
17
).
∎

We now turn to the asymptotic merit complexity of Algorithm
1
. The following localization lemma is the key step in the proof of the global little-
o
rate.

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds. If the algorithm does not terminate finitely, then

\mathcal{U}(x^{k})=o(s_{k}).

Proof.

Let

u_{k}:=\mathcal{U}(x^{k}).

Since the algorithm does not terminate finitely, we have
s_{k}>0
for all
k
.
By Lemma
3.5
, the sequence
\{u_{k}\}
is nonincreasing and
nonnegative. Hence it admits a limit.

We first show that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}u_{k}=0.

(18)

If
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
is finite, then for all sufficiently large
k
one has

k\notin\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
, and hence

s_{k+1}<\frac{1}{4}s_{k}.

Thus
s_{k}\to 0
, and Proposition
3.2
(ii) gives
u_{k}\leq D_{0}s_{k}\to 0
.

If
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
is infinite, then Lemma
3.6
gives

u_{k+1}\leq u_{k}-\tau u_{k}^{3/2},\quad k\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}.

If
\lim_{k}u_{k}=\bar{u}>0
, then every sufficiently large
k\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
would decrease
u_{k}
by at least
\frac{1}{2}\tau\bar{u}^{3/2}>0
, which is impossible since

\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is nonincreasing and bounded below. Therefore
\bar{u}=0
. Hence, in
all cases, equation (
18
) holds.

Since
x^{k}\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
for all
k
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is compact, every subsequence of
\{x^{k}\}
has a cluster point. Let
x^{k_{j}}\to\bar{x}
. If
\bar{x}\notin\mathcal{X}_{w}
, then there exists
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
f_{i}(y)<f_{i}(\bar{x})
for all
i\in[m]
. Hence, for some
\delta>0
and all
sufficiently large
j
,

f_{i}(x^{k_{j}})-f_{i}(y)\geq\delta,\qquad i\in[m],

which gives
u_{k_{j}}\geq\delta
, contradicting (
18
). Thus every cluster point of
\{x^{k}\}
belongs to
\mathcal{X}_{w}
. We now show that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}s_{k}=0.

(19)

Indeed, if not, there exist
\varepsilon>0
and a subsequence
\{k_{j}\}
such that
s_{k_{j}}\geq\varepsilon
. Since
\{x^{k}\}\subset\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is compact, we may pass to a further subsequence such that
x^{k_{j}}\to\bar{x}
. The preceding argument gives
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{w}
, and hence
s(\bar{x})=0
by Lemma
2.3
. By Lemma
2.4
,
s
is continuous, and hence

s_{k_{j}}\to s(\bar{x})=0
, contradicting
s_{k_{j}}\geq\varepsilon
. Thus, equation (
19
) is true.

By Lemma
3.5
, each sequence
\{f_{i}(x^{k})\}
is
nonincreasing. Since
\{x^{k}\}\subset\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is compact, the limits

\ell_{i}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{i}(x^{k}),\quad i\in[m],

exist. Moreover, every cluster point
\bar{x}
of
\{x^{k}\}
satisfies

f_{i}(\bar{x})=\ell_{i}
for all
i\in[m]
.

We now prove
u_{k}=o(s_{k})
by contradiction. Suppose that there are
\varepsilon>0
and a subsequence, still denoted by
\{k_{j}\}
, such that

u_{k_{j}}\geq\varepsilon s_{k_{j}}.

Passing to a further subsequence if necessary, assume that
x^{k_{j}}\to\bar{x}
. Choose
\lambda^{k_{j}}\in\Lambda(x^{k_{j}})
. Since
\Delta_{m}
is compact, we may also assume that
\lambda^{k_{j}}\to\bar{\lambda}\in\Delta_{m}
. Since
\lambda^{k_{j}}\in\Lambda(x^{k_{j}})
, the definition of
\Lambda
gives

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{k_{j}}\nabla f_{i}(x^{k_{j}})\right\|=s_{k_{j}}.

Letting
j\to\infty
and using (
19
),

x^{k_{j}}\to\bar{x}
, and
\lambda^{k_{j}}\to\bar{\lambda}
, we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bar{\lambda}_{i}\nabla f_{i}(\bar{x})=0.

Let
S:=\{i\in[m]:\bar{\lambda}_{i}>0\}
. Then
S\neq\varnothing
. The convex function

\phi_{\bar{\lambda}}(z):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bar{\lambda}_{i}f_{i}(z)

has zero gradient at
\bar{x}
, and hence
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer of

\phi_{\bar{\lambda}}
. Therefore, for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists

i(z)\in S
such that

f_{i(z)}(z)\geq f_{i(z)}(\bar{x})=\ell_{i(z)}.

Consequently,

\min_{r\in[m]}\{f_{r}(x^{k_{j}})-f_{r}(z)\}\leq f_{i(z)}(x^{k_{j}})-f_{i(z)}(z)\leq\max_{i\in S}\{f_{i}(x^{k_{j}})-\ell_{i}\}.

Taking the supremum over
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
gives

u_{k_{j}}\leq\max_{i\in S}\{f_{i}(x^{k_{j}})-\ell_{i}\}.

(20)

Since
\bar{\lambda}_{i}>0
for
i\in S
, there exists
\gamma>0
such that
\lambda_{i}^{k_{j}}\geq\gamma
for all
i\in S
and all sufficiently large
j
. Together with
f_{i}(x^{k_{j}})-\ell_{i}>0
, equation (
20
) implies

u_{k_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{k_{j}}\bigl(f_{i}(x^{k_{j}})-\ell_{i}\bigr).

Using
f_{i}(\bar{x})=\ell_{i}
and the convexity of
f_{i}
, we obtain

f_{i}(x^{k_{j}})-f_{i}(\bar{x})\leq\langle\nabla f_{i}(x^{k_{j}}),x^{k_{j}}-\bar{x}\rangle.

Therefore,

u_{k_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{k_{j}}\nabla f_{i}(x^{k_{j}}),x^{k_{j}}-\bar{x}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}s_{k_{j}}\|x^{k_{j}}-\bar{x}\|.

Dividing by
s_{k_{j}}>0
and letting
j\to\infty
contradicts
u_{k_{j}}\geq\varepsilon s_{k_{j}}
. The proof is complete.
∎

We now derive the main merit complexity estimate. The result in Lemma
3.7
is the additional ingredient that sharpens the usual
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{2})
complexity to the asymptotic
o(1/k^{2})
bound below.

Theorem 3.8

(Global
o(1/k^{2})
rate for the merit function)
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds, and let
\{x^{k}\}
be generated by Algorithm
1
. If the algorithm does not terminate in finitely many iterations, then

\mathcal{U}(x^{k})=o(1/k^{2}).

(21)

If the algorithm terminates, the terminal point is weakly Pareto optimal.

Proof.

If the algorithm terminates at some iteration
j
, then
s_{j}=0
, and Lemma
2.3
implies
x^{j}\in\mathcal{X}_{w}
, for which the desired result automatically holds. We therefore assume that the algorithm does not terminate in finite iterations. In particular,
s_{k}>0
for every
k
. Let
u_{k}:=\mathcal{U}(x^{k})
. By the definition of
\mathcal{U}
and Lemma
2.3
,
u_{k}>0
for every
k
.

Set

\rho_{k}:=\frac{u_{k}}{s_{k}}.

By Lemma
3.7
,
\rho_{k}\to 0
. For each
k\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
, Lemma
3.6
gives

\frac{5}{12}\eta_{k}r_{k}^{2}\geq\frac{s_{k}^{3/2}}{120\sqrt{H}}.

Combining this estimate with Lemma
3.5
(ii), we get

u_{k+1}\leq u_{k}-\kappa s_{k}^{3/2},\qquad\kappa:=\frac{1}{120\sqrt{H}}.

(22)

Hence, for
k\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
,

u_{k+1}\leq u_{k}-\kappa\rho_{k}^{-3/2}u_{k}^{3/2}.

If
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
is finite, by the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
, the sequence
\{s_{k}\}
then decays geometrically from some index onward. Proposition
3.2
gives
u_{k}\leq D_{0}s_{k}
, and hence (
21
) follows. It remains to consider the case in which
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
is infinite. Write

\mathcal{I}_{\infty}:=\{i_{0}<i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots\},\quad v_{t}:=u_{i_{t}},\quad b_{t}:=\kappa\rho_{i_{t}}^{-3/2}.

Since
\rho_{k}\to 0
, we have
b_{t}\to\infty
. Moreover,
b_{t}>0
and
v_{t}\geq 0
. By the monotonicity of
\{u_{k}\}
and (
22
),

v_{t+1}=u_{i_{t+1}}\leq u_{i_{t}+1}\leq v_{t}-b_{t}v_{t}^{3/2}.

Thus the assumptions of Lemma
A.1
are satisfied, and hence

v_{t}=o(1/t^{2}).

(23)

We now pass from the subsequence indexed by
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
to the full
sequence. For
k\geq 1
, define

N_{k}:=|\mathcal{I}_{k}|.

If
N_{k}\leq k/2
, then using (
12
) on the indices in

\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
and the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
on the remaining

s_{k}\leq\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^{N_{k}}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{k-N_{k}}s_{0}\leq\left(\sqrt{\frac{5}{8}}\right)^{k}s_{0}.

(ii) gives

. Let

.
Then

is the last index in
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
before

. By the

,

Moreover, for all sufficiently large

) implies

u_{k}\leq v_{t}=o(1/t^{2})=o(1/k^{2}).

).
∎

4
Essential Sharpness of the Polynomial Exponent

In this section, we show that the exponent
2
in Theorem
3.8
is essentially unimprovable in a uniform worst-case sense. The construction is one-dimensional and bi-objective, but along the generated trajectory the multi-objective subproblem reduces to a scalar regularized Newton step.

4.1
A one-dimensional barrier family

Fix
p>2
. Define the even function
\psi_{p}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\psi_{p}(x):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{|x|^{p+1}}{p+1},&|x|\leq 1,\\[3.99994pt]
\dfrac{1}{p+1}+(|x|-1)+\dfrac{p}{2}(|x|-1)^{2},&|x|\geq 1.\end{cases}

Then
\psi_{p}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is convex and coercive, and
\psi_{p}^{\prime\prime}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
H_{p}:=p(p-1)
. Consider

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl(f_{1}(x),f_{2}(x)\bigr),\quad f_{1}(x):=\psi_{p}(x),\quad f_{2}(x):=2x+\psi_{p}(x).

(24)

Fix
x^{0}\in(0,1)
and define
\mathcal{L}_{0}
accordingly. For
x\in(0,1)
,

f_{1}^{\prime}(x)=x^{p},\quad f_{2}^{\prime}(x)=2+x^{p},\quad f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x)=f_{2}^{\prime\prime}(x)=px^{p-1}.

Therefore

s(x)=\min_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left|\lambda x^{p}+(1-\lambda)(2+x^{p})\right|=x^{p}.

(25)

Moreover,
0\in\mathcal{X}_{w}
, and no positive point is weak Pareto optimal because
0
strictly improves both objectives at every
x>0
. Hence every
z\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}
satisfies
z\leq 0
. For
x\in(0,1)
and
z\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}
,

f_{2}(x)-f_{2}(z)=f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(z)+2(x-z)>f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(z).

Thus

\min\{f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(z),f_{2}(x)-f_{2}(z)\}=f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(z).

Since
f_{1}
is minimized at
0
and
0\in\mathcal{X}_{w}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}
, Proposition
3.2
(i) gives

\mathcal{U}(x)=f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(0)=\frac{x^{p+1}}{p+1}.

We next examine the subproblem. For
x\in(0,1)
, define

\phi_{x}(d):=\max\{q_{1}(d;x),q_{2}(d;x)\}+\frac{\eta}{2}d^{2},\quad\eta>0.

Since

q_{2}(d;x)=q_{1}(d;x)+2d,

we have
q_{2}(d;x)\geq q_{1}(d;x)
for
d\geq 0
and

q_{2}(d;x)<q_{1}(d;x)
for
d<0
. If
d\geq 0
, then

\phi_{x}(d)=q_{2}(d;x)+\frac{\eta}{2}d^{2}=(2+x^{p})d+\frac{1}{2}px^{p-1}d^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}d^{2}\geq 0.

On the other hand, for all sufficiently small
d<0
,

\phi_{x}(d)=q_{1}(d;x)+\frac{\eta}{2}d^{2}=x^{p}d+\frac{1}{2}px^{p-1}d^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}d^{2}<0.

Therefore, the minimizer must lie in
(-\infty,0)
, and the active model is
q_{1}
, so the subproblem reduces to the scalar quadratic problem

\min_{d<0}\left\{x^{p}d+\frac{1}{2}px^{p-1}d^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}d^{2}\right\}.

(26)

4.2
Asymptotic rates of the recursion

To clarify the role of the square-root choice and to show the sharpness barrier in a slightly broader form, we analyze the more general regularization rule

\eta_{k}=C\,s(x^{k})^{\alpha},\quad{\rm with}\ \ C>0,\quad\alpha\in(0,1].

The square-root rule used in Algorithm
1
corresponds to

\alpha=1/2
and
C=2\sqrt{H_{p}}
. For a current point
x\in(0,1)
, recall that
s(x)
is derived in (
25
), and this combines with (
26
) gives

d(x)=-\frac{x^{p}}{px^{p-1}+Cx^{p\alpha}},

(27)

and the trajectory is
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+d(x^{k})
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
C>0
. For the family (
24
), consider the iteration generated by (
27
) with initial point
x^{0}\in(0,1)
. Then:

(i)

If
\alpha<1-\frac{1}{p}
, then
x^{k}\downarrow 0
and there exist
constants
c_{2}>c_{1}>0
and
K\geq 1
such that, for all
k\geq K
,

c_{1}k^{-q_{p,\alpha}}\leq\mathcal{U}(x^{k})\leq c_{2}k^{-q_{p,\alpha}},\qquad q_{p,\alpha}:=\frac{p+1}{p(1-\alpha)-1}.

(ii)

If
\alpha=1-\frac{1}{p}
, then

x^{k+1}=\left(1-\frac{1}{p+C}\right)x^{k}.

(iii)

If
\alpha>1-\frac{1}{p}
, then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{x^{k+1}}{x^{k}}=1-\frac{1}{p}.

Proof.

From (
27
),

\frac{-d(x)}{x}=\frac{1}{p+Cx^{p\alpha-p+1}},

which implies that
0<-d(x)<x/p
for all
x\in(0,1)
. Since
x^{0}\in(0,1)
and
p>2
, the iterates remain in
(0,1)
and strictly decrease. Their limit must be
0
, since any positive limit would imply a nonzero limiting decrease.

(i) If
\alpha<1-\frac{1}{p}
, then
p\alpha<p-1
. Thus

\lim_{x\searrow 0}\frac{-d(x)}{x^{p(1-\alpha)}}=\frac{1}{C}.

Set
\beta_{p,\alpha}:=p(1-\alpha)>1
. Lemma
A.2
gives

\lim_{k\to\infty}k^{1/(\beta_{p,\alpha}-1)}x^{k}=\bigl(C^{-1}(\beta_{p,\alpha}-1)\bigr)^{-1/(\beta_{p,\alpha}-1)}.

Since
\mathcal{U}(x^{k})=(x^{k})^{p+1}/(p+1)
, the claimed two-sided bound follows.

(ii) If
\alpha=1-\frac{1}{p}
, then
p\alpha=p-1
, and

d(x)=-\frac{x}{p+C}.

This gives the stated linear recursion.

(iii) If
\alpha>1-\frac{1}{p}
, then
p\alpha>p-1
, and therefore

\lim_{x\searrow 0}\frac{-d(x)}{x}=\frac{1}{p}.

Since
x^{k}\to 0
, this implies

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{x^{k+1}}{x^{k}}=1-\frac{1}{p}.

∎

We now apply the preceding result to the square-root regularization rule used in Algorithm
1
. This gives the rate on the constructed trajectories and shows that its exponent can be arbitrarily close to
2
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose
p>2
and use the square-root rule

\eta_{k}=2\sqrt{H_{p}s(x^{k})}.

Then there exist constants
c_{2}>c_{1}>0
and an integer
K\geq 1
such that, for all

k\geq K
,

c_{1}k^{-q_{p}}\leq\mathcal{U}(x^{k})\leq c_{2}k^{-q_{p}},\qquad q_{p}:=\frac{2(p+1)}{p-2}=2+\frac{6}{p-2}.

Consequently, for every
\delta>0
, no uniform estimate of the form

\mathcal{U}(x^{k})=\mathcal{O}(k^{-(2+\delta)})

can hold over this family.

Theorem
3.8
and Corollary
4.2
together clarify the role of the exponent
2
for the square-root regularized Newton method. The merit function satisfies the global asymptotic bound
o(1/k^{2})
along every generated sequence, while the barrier family shows that this exponent cannot be improved in a uniform polynomial sense.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied a regularized Newton method for unconstrained convex multi-objective optimization. Under the compactness of the initial component-wise lower level set and Lipschitz continuity of the Hessians, we established the global asymptotic merit estimate
\mathcal{U}(x^{k})=o(1/k^{2})
. In the scalar specialization, the little-
o
estimate improves the
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{2})
rate for the regularized Newton method established by Mishchenko
[
8
]
.

We also constructed a one-dimensional bi-objective family showing that the exponent
2
is essentially sharp in a uniform sense, that is, for any fixed
\delta>0
, no uniform estimate of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{-(2+\delta)})
holds over this family. Thus, the
o(1/k^{2})
refinement does not imply a uniform improvement to any higher fixed polynomial rate.

Appendix A
Two auxiliary lemmas

The first lemma is a simple variant of the standard scalar recursion estimates used in regularized Newton complexity analysis.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\{v_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a nonnegative sequence and let

\{b_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a positive sequence with
b_{t}\to\infty
. Suppose that

v_{t+1}\leq v_{t}-b_{t}v_{t}^{3/2},\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0,

Then

v_{t}=o(1/t^{2}).

Proof.

If
v_{t}=0
for some
t
, the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise,
v_{t}>0
for
all
t
. Since

0<v_{t+1}\leq v_{t}-b_{t}v_{t}^{3/2}=v_{t}(1-b_{t}\sqrt{v_{t}}),

we have
b_{t}\sqrt{v_{t}}\in(0,1)
for every
t
.

Using
(1-a)^{-1/2}\geq 1+a/2
for
a\in(0,1)
, we obtain

\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{t+1}}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{t}-b_{t}v_{t}^{3/2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{t}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-b_{t}\sqrt{v_{t}}}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{t}}}+\frac{b_{t}}{2}.

Summing from
0
to
t-1
yields

v_{t}\leq\frac{4}{\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{t-1}b_{\ell}\right)^{2}}.

Since
b_{t}\to\infty
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\ell=0}^{t-1}b_{\ell}=\infty.

Therefore,

t^{2}v_{t}\leq\frac{4}{\left(t^{-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{t-1}b_{\ell}\right)^{2}}\to 0,

which proves
v_{t}=o(1/t^{2})
.
∎

The next lemma turns an asymptotic one-step recursion into the corresponding polynomial decay rate. Its proof is elementary and is included for completeness.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\{t_{k}\}
be a positive sequence with
\lim_{k\to\infty}t_{k}=0
and suppose there exist constants
c>0
and
\beta>1
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{t_{k}-t_{k+1}}{t_{k}^{\beta}}=c.

(28)

Then the sequence satisfies

\lim_{k\to\infty}k^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}t_{k}=\bigl(c(\beta-1)\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{\beta-1}}.

Proof.

Set

\gamma:=\beta-1>0,\quad y_{k}:=t_{k}^{-\gamma}.

Since
t_{k}\to 0
, we have
y_{k}\to\infty
. By the hypothesis (
28
), we write

t_{k+1}=t_{k}-ct_{k}^{\beta}+r_{k},

where
r_{k}
satisfies
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{r_{k}}{t_{k}^{\beta}}=0.
Dividing both sides by
t_{k}
, we get

\frac{t_{k+1}}{t_{k}}=1-ct_{k}^{\gamma}+\frac{r_{k}}{t_{k}}=1-u_{k},\ \ {\rm with}\ \ u_{k}:=ct_{k}^{\gamma}-\frac{r_{k}}{t_{k}}.

From the definition of
u_{k}
,

\frac{u_{k}}{t_{k}^{\gamma}}=c-\frac{r_{k}}{t_{k}^{\beta}}.

Since
\frac{r_{k}}{t_{k}^{\beta}}\to 0
, we have
\frac{u_{k}}{t_{k}^{\gamma}}\to c
, for which
u_{k}\to 0
, and
u_{k}>0
for all sufficiently large
k
.

Note that

\displaystyle y_{k+1}-y_{k}

\displaystyle=t_{k+1}^{-\gamma}-t_{k}^{-\gamma}=t_{k}^{-\gamma}\left[\left(\frac{t_{k+1}}{t_{k}}\right)^{-\gamma}-1\right]=t_{k}^{-\gamma}\left[(1-u_{k})^{-\gamma}-1\right].

Since
u_{k}\to 0
, the Taylor expansion of
(1-u)^{-\gamma}
at
u=0

gives

(1-u_{k})^{-\gamma}-1=\gamma u_{k}+\rho_{k}u_{k},\quad{\rm with}\ \ \lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{k}=0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle y_{k+1}-y_{k}

\displaystyle=t_{k}^{-\gamma}\left(\gamma u_{k}+\rho_{k}u_{k}\right)=\left(\gamma+\rho_{k}\right)\frac{u_{k}}{t_{k}^{\gamma}}.

Using
u_{k}/t_{k}^{\gamma}\to c
and
\rho_{k}\to 0
, we obtain

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}y_{k+1}-y_{k}=\gamma c.

Thus,

\frac{y_{k}}{k}=\frac{y_{0}}{k}+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(y_{\ell+1}-y_{\ell})\to\gamma c.

Since
y_{k}=t_{k}^{-\gamma}
, we have

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}kt_{k}^{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\gamma c},

and hence

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}k^{1/\gamma}t_{k}=(\gamma c)^{-1/\gamma}.

Finally, substituting
\gamma=\beta-1
gives

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}k^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}t_{k}=\bigl(c(\beta-1)\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{\beta-1}},

and this proves the desired result.
∎
