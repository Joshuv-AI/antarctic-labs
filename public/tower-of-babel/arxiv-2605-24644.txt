Quadratically Regularized Optimal Transport: Localization Bounds and Affine Case Analysis
Long Nguyen-Chi, Nam Nguyen, Binh T. Nguyen

Abstract
Quadratic regularization has emerged as a potential alternative to the popular entropic regularization in computational optimal transport, offering the theoretical advantage of producing sparse couplings through its hinge density structure. Despite recent progress in one-dimensional settings and general upper bounds, fundamental questions about the localization rate of QOT optimizers around the Monge coupling have remained open. In this work, we establish a general lower bound showing that the support of the QOT optimizer cannot concentrate around the Monge graph faster than order ε 1 d + 2 \varepsilon^{\frac{1}{d+2}} in the directed Hausdorff distance, matching the conjectured optimal exponent under standard regularity assumptions in 35 . We also show that the QOT value gap controls the mean-squared deviation 𝔼 π ε ​ ‖ y − T ⁡ ( x ) ‖ 2 \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}\|y-T(x)\|^{2} by the scale of ε 2 d + 2 \varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}} . As a corollary, in the affine Brenier regime, which includes Gaussian-to-Gaussian transport, we derive a sharp pointwise tube bound of order ε 1 d + 2 \varepsilon^{\frac{1}{d+2}} by reducing the problem to self-transport and applying recent self-transport sparsity results. Finally, we validate our theoretical bound with a synthetic experiment in high-dimensional settings.



1
Introduction

The optimal transport (OT) problem concerns the task of moving probability measures from a source to a target location
(
27
)
.
It has seen multiple applications in machine learning in recent years, including distribution alignment for (unsupervised) domain adaptation
(
8
)
, training generative models with Wasserstein/Sinkhorn OT losses
(
1
;
14
)
, and using Wasserstein-based losses in structured prediction (including multi-label learning)
(
12
)
.
OT also appears in modern generative modeling: flow matching can be instantiated with OT displacement interpolation (Wasserstein geodesics) as the probability path
(
22
)
, and related ODE-based transport methods (e.g., rectified-flow variants) are explicitly OT-motivated
(
23
)
.
In computational biology, OT-based models are used for single-cell data integration and for learning perturbation responses
(
32
;
6
;
5
)
.
In NLP (Natural Language Processing), the Word Mover’s Distance casts document comparison as an OT problem over word embeddings
(
21
)
.

Formally, let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be Borel probability measures and let
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable cost.
In Monge’s original formulation, one seeks a measurable map
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
pushing
\mu
to
\nu
(i.e.
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
) and minimizing

\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{T:T_{\#}\mu=\nu}\int c\bigl(x,T(x)\bigr)\mu(dx),

where
T_{\#}\mu(B)\coloneqq\mu\bigl(T^{-1}(B)\bigr)
for all Borel sets
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The Kantorovich optimal transport formulation
(
20
)
relaxes the mapping constraint by couplings and yields the convex problem

\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int c(x,y)\pi(dx,dy),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of couplings with marginal
\mu,\nu
:

\displaystyle\Pi(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}):

\displaystyle\pi(\cdot\times\mathbb{R}^{d})=\mu,

\displaystyle\pi(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\cdot)=\nu\}.

Throughout this paper, we focus on the quadratic cost
c(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm.
In this case, if
\mu\ll\mathrm{Leb}
, and
\mu,\nu
have finite second moments, Brenier’s theorem
(
4
)
asserts that the Monge and Kantorovich problems coincide and admit a unique optimizer, which means, there exists a convex function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that the map
T\coloneqq\nabla\varphi
satisfies
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
and the optimal plan is
\pi_{\star}=(\mathrm{Id},T)_{\#}\mu
.
We refer the readers to
34
for background and further details.

Regularization and sparsity.

Regularization is ubiquitous in computational OT and in machine learning applications by improving the scalability of the OT problem
(
30
)
.
A classical choice is entropic regularization
(
9
)
, which leads to Sinkhorn-type algorithms but produces dense couplings: the optimizer
\pi^{\mathrm{ent}}_{\varepsilon}
has a strictly positive density w.r.t
P=\mu\otimes\nu
.
Quadratically regularized optimal transport (QOT) replaces the entropic penalty by a squared
L^{2}(\mu\otimes\nu)
penalty (equivalently a
\chi^{2}
divergence up to an additive constant), and has been advocated as a sparsity-promoting alternative in both discrete and continuous settings
(
3
;
33
;
11
;
24
)
.
A key structural difference from entropic OT is that the QOT optimizer admits a hinge (truncation) density formula (cf. (
2.2
)), in contrast to the strictly positive Gibbs density of entropic OT.
This hinge structure produces exact zeros whenever the dual slack is negative, and therefore directly exposes sparsity through strict inequalities in the dual slack
(
24
;
29
)
.

More precisely, fix
\varepsilon>0
and set the reference measure
P\coloneqq\mu\otimes\nu
.
For
\pi\ll P
, we study the
\chi^{2}/L^{2}
(quadratic) regularization

\mathrm{QOT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\\
\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\Big\{\int c(x,y)d\pi(x,y)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big\|\frac{d\pi}{dP}\Big\|_{L^{2}(P)}^{2}\Big\},

(1.1)

which differs from the
\chi^{2}
-divergence
D_{\chi^{2}}(\pi\|P)=\int(\frac{d\pi}{dP}-1)^{2}dP
only by an additive constant in the objective.
Here,
\varepsilon>0
is the regularization parameter and
d\pi/dP
denotes the Radon-Nikodym derivative.

Localization around the Monge coupling.

We denote by
\pi_{\varepsilon}
the unique optimizer of the QOT problem (
1.1
).
A natural question is: as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, how fast does the optimizer
\pi_{\varepsilon}
concentrate around the Monge graph
\gr T
?
This question quantifies the intrinsic sparsity-accuracy tradeoff of QOT: the hinge density promotes sparse support, but a small
\varepsilon
is needed for
\pi_{\varepsilon}
to approximate the Monge coupling
\pi_{\star}
.
Controlling the tube thickness around
\gr T
is also practically relevant when
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is used as a sparse correspondence/graph (or via barycentric projection to estimate transport maps), rather than only through its scalar cost value
(
26
;
31
)
.
Recent statistical analyses of sparsity-promoting regularized OT further suggest that quantitative control of the geometry of
\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}
is a key input for obtaining parametric sample complexity for sparse regularizers, including quadratic regularization
(
15
;
16
)
.

We quantify this by the directed Hausdorff distance and vertical bias

r\coloneqq\dist(\spt\pi_{\varepsilon};\gr T),b\coloneqq\sup_{(x,y)\in\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}}\|y-T(x)\|.

When
T
is
L
-Lipschitz,
r\leq b\leq\sqrt{1+L^{2}}r
(Lemma
3.1
).
Recent work has begun to quantify how
\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}
concentrates around the Monge coupling as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, obtaining sharp rates in one dimension and the first general-dimensional upper bounds under geometric and spread assumptions
(
17
;
35
;
18
)
.
However, outside the self-transport case
\mu=\nu
, the available general-dimensional upper bounds do not match the conjectured
\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
scale under standard assumptions.
Moreover, prior to this work, a general lower bound ruling out faster-than-
\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
concentration was not available.
Our contribution can be summarized as follows.

Contributions.

We provide the following results for the QOT problem with the quadratic cost
c(x,y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
.

1.

A general lower bound with the conjectured exponent.

Under standard regularity assumptions on
\mu,\nu
and the Lipschitzness of the Monge map
T
, we prove that
\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}
cannot concentrate around
\gr T
faster than order
\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
in directed Hausdorff distance (Theorem
3.3
).

2.

Mean-squared and high-probability bias from the QOT value gap.

We show that the QOT value gap
\Delta_{\varepsilon}
upper bounds the mean-squared deviation from the Monge map under
\pi_{\varepsilon}
(Lemma
3.5
).
Combining this with sharp value-gap rates from
10
yields the
\varepsilon^{2/(d+2)}
mean-square scale and a simple tail bound for
\|Y-T(X)\|
when
(X,Y)\sim\pi_{\varepsilon}
(Theorem
3.6
).

3.

Affine regime: sharp pointwise upper bound.

When
T
is affine (
T(x)=Ax+b
with
A\succ 0
, including Gaussian-to-Gaussian transport), we obtain the sharp pointwise bias upper bound
\sup_{(x,y)\in\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}}\|y-T(x)\|\lesssim\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(A)}\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
(Theorem
3.7
).

Organization.

Section
2
introduces the background for understanding the whole paper, Section
3
gives the three results above, Section
4
shows the important results in QOT, Section
5
provides synthetic experiments validating the
\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
scaling in the affine Brenier regime, and Section
6
summarizes the whole paper and provides some suggestions for the future works.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

For a map
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote its graph by

\gr F\coloneqq\{(x,F(x)):x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

For a point
m
in a metric space
(M,d)
and a set
B\subseteq M
, define

\dist(m,B)\coloneqq\inf_{b\in B}d(m,b).

For sets
A,B\subseteq M
, define the directed Hausdorff distance

\dist(A;B)\coloneqq\sup_{a\in A}\dist(a,B)=\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{b\in B}d(a,b).

In our setting, we always take
M=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the Euclidean distance.
In particular, for a coupling
\pi
we have:

\dist(\spt\pi;\gr T)=\sup_{(x,y)\in\spt\pi}\inf_{x^{\prime}}\big\|(x,y)-(x^{\prime},T(x^{\prime}))\big\|.

Thus
\dist(\spt\pi;\gr T)
measures how far the support of
\pi
can deviate from the graph of the Monge map.
For a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, we denote by

f^{*}(y)\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl[\langle x,y\rangle-f(x)\bigr]

its convex conjugate.

Let
B(y,r)
denote the (closed) Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered at
y
with radius
r>0
. Let
\omega_{d}=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)
be the unit-ball
B(0,1)
volume.

2.2
Dual formulation of quadratically regularized optimal transport

33
derived the Lagrangian dual of (
1.1
), and
24
proved strong duality and the existence of dual optimizers.
The dual problem associated with (
1.1
) reads

\sup_{f\in L^{1}(\mu),g\in L^{1}(\nu)}\Big\{\int f(x)d\mu(x)+\int g(y)d\nu(y)\\
-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int\bigl[f(x)+g(y)-c(x,y)\bigr]_{+}^{2}dP(x,y)\Big\},

(2.1)

where
[\cdot]_{+}\coloneqq\max\{\cdot,0\}
, and
(f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
denotes any maximizer of (
2.1
); we call them optimal dual potentials.
It is well known from
24
that for some continuous
f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon}
, the unique optimizer
\pi_{\varepsilon}
of (
1.1
) is absolutely continuous w.r.t
P
, with density

\frac{d\pi_{\varepsilon}}{dP}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigl[f_{\varepsilon}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}(y)-c(x,y)\bigr]_{+},

(2.2)

The support of
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is:

\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}=\overline{\bigl\{(x,y)\in\spt P:f_{\varepsilon}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}(y)>c(x,y)\bigr\}}.

(2.3)

Up to constant shifts, they are uniquely defined by

\displaystyle\int\bigl[f_{\varepsilon}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}(y)-c(x,y)\bigr]_{+}\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\ \forall y\in\spt\nu,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\int\bigl[f_{\varepsilon}(x)+g_{\varepsilon}(y)-c(x,y)\bigr]_{+}\nu(dy)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\ \forall x\in\spt\mu.

(2.5)

A key structural feature is that the optimal density is a truncated affine function of the dual slack, cf. (
2.2
); this directly exposes sparsity through the strict inequality in (
2.3
).

3
Results

Our first main result gives a lower bound on
r
(hence also on
b
) with exponent
1/(d+2)
, which coincides with the QOT bias conjecture rate.
Our second result shows that the value gap
\Delta_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\mathrm{QOT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)-\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)
controls the mean-squared bias
\int\|y-T(x)\|^{2}d\pi_{\varepsilon}
and yields an
\varepsilon^{2/(d+2)}
scale.
Finally, in the affine Brenier regime
T(x)=Ax+a
, we reduce QOT
(\mu,\nu)
exactly to a self-transport QOT problem and obtain the sharp pointwise upper bound
b\lesssim\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(A)}\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
.

We begin by specifying the standing assumptions on
(\mu,\nu)
and the Monge map
T
.
Note that we work with compactly supported probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assumption 1
.

There exists a bounded connected open Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\Omega\subset B(0,1)
such that
\mu(dx)=\rho_{\mu}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}(x)dx
, and there are constants
0<\underline{\lambda}_{\mu}\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\mu}<\infty
such that

\underline{\lambda}_{\mu}\leq\rho_{\mu}(x)\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\mu}\text{ for Lebesgue-a.e. }x\in\Omega.

This leads to
\spt\mu=\overline{\Omega}
.

Assumption 2
.

The measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies
\nu\ll\mathrm{Leb}
,
\spt\nu\subseteq B(0,1)
and there exist constants
0<\overline{\lambda}_{\nu}<\infty
such that
d\nu/dy\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\nu}
on
\spt\nu
.

The normalization
\spt\mu,\spt\nu\subseteq B(0,1)
is convenient and can always be achieved by rescaling.
These assumptions ensure, in particular, that
\mu
and
\nu
are compactly supported, since supports of Borel probability measures are closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Besides Assumptions
1
-
2
, we use the following standard assumption:

Assumption 3
.

The Monge map
T=\nabla\varphi
is
L
-Lipschitz for some
L>0
.

Remark on assumptions.

Assumption
1
-
2
(bounded Lipschitz domain and density bounded above/below) are standard “no-holes, no-vanishing-density” conditions.
This is the typical regime in which the
\varepsilon
-spread scales as
\delta(\varepsilon)\asymp\varepsilon^{1/(d+1)}
, matching the canonical benchmark in previous work on QOT sparsity.
Here, we define the
\varepsilon
-spread of
\mu
by

\displaystyle\delta(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{r>0:r\rho(r)>\varepsilon\bigr\},

\displaystyle\rho(r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf_{x\in\spt\mu}\mu\bigl(B(x,r)\bigr).

where
B(x,r)
denotes the closed Euclidean ball of radius
r
centered at
x
.
Intuitively,
\delta(\varepsilon)
captures how uniformly the probability mass of
\mu
is spread over its support: if every ball of radius
r
inside the support carries at least some fixed amount of mass, then
\rho(r)
is not too small, and therefore
\delta(\varepsilon)
is also small.

This type of assumption is standard in statistical guarantees for regularized OT maps; see, e.g.,
31
.
The “density bounded from 0 and infinity” assumption is also used by
17
to show the sharp rate of the distance to the optimizer in 1D.
Assumption
3
mirrors the regularity used in the bias upper bound of
35
and in statistical analyses of OT map
(
19
;
28
;
2
)
.

We now derive a nontrivial lower bound that does not suffer from a global subtraction term and matches the optimal exponent in
\varepsilon
.

3.1
A general lower bound to the Monge map

We first state the following elementary bound on distance to a Lipschitz graph.

Lemma 3.1

(Distance to a Lipschitz graph)
.

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
L
-Lipschitz. Then for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\dist\big((x,y);\gr T\big)\geq\frac{\|y-T(x)\|}{(1+L^{2})^{1/2}}.

Remark on distance vs bias.

If
T
is
L
-Lipschitz, then for all
(x,y)
,

\displaystyle\dist\bigl((x,y);\gr T\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\|y-T(x)\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+L^{2}}\dist\bigl((x,y);\gr T\bigr).

Hence the directed distance
\dist(\spt\pi_{\varepsilon};\gr T)
and the vertical bias
\sup_{(x,y)\in\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}}\|y-T(x)\|
are equivalent up to the factor
\sqrt{1+L^{2}}
.

Lemma 3.2

(Fiberwise
L^{2}
lower bound by marginal constraints)
.

Let
\pi_{\varepsilon}\ll P
with density
h=\frac{d\pi_{\varepsilon}}{dP}
and let
r\coloneqq\dist(\spt\pi_{\varepsilon};\gr T)
.
For every
y
, define
X_{y}\coloneqq\{x\in\spt\mu:h(x,y)>0\}
.
Then:

(i)

\int h(x,y)^{2}\mu(dx)\geq\frac{1}{\mu(X_{y})}

(ii)

If
T
is
L
-Lipschitz, then

X_{y}\subseteq T^{-1}\big(B(y,(1+L^{2})^{1/2}r)\big)\text{ up to a $\mu$-null set},

hence
\mu(X_{y})\leq\nu\big(B(y,(1+L^{2})^{1/2}r)\big)
and, under Assumption
2
,

\displaystyle\mu(X_{y})

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\nu}\mathrm{Leb}\big(B(0,(1+L^{2})^{1/2}r)\big)

\displaystyle=\overline{\lambda}_{\nu}(1+L^{2})^{d/2}\omega_{d}r^{d}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\big\|h\big\|_{L^{2}(P)}^{2}=\int\int h^{2}d\mu d\nu

\displaystyle\geq\int\frac{1}{\mu(X_{y})}\nu(dy)

(3.1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\overline{\lambda}_{\nu}(1+L^{2})^{d/2}\omega_{d}}r^{-d}.

Recall the value gap

\Delta_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{QOT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)-\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)\geq 0.

For the
\chi^{2}
(i.e.,
L^{2}
) regularization, because
\spt\mu,\spt\nu\subseteq B(0,1)
, we have
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2+\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every
\delta>0
. So, we can use
10
, Corollary 3.1, Proposition 4.2
, which gave convergence rates in the quadratic-cost case:

C_{\mathrm{val}}^{-1}\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{2+d}}\leq\Delta_{\varepsilon}\leq C_{\mathrm{val}}\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{2+d}}

(3.2)

for
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

Lemma
3.2
gives the lower bound for
\|h\|_{L^{2}(P)}^{2}
.
Since the quadratic penalty contributes
(\varepsilon/2)\|h\|_{L^{2}(P)}^{2}
to
\mathrm{QOT}_{\varepsilon}
, we can get the lower bound of
r
, proved in Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem 3.3

(General lower bound with optimal exponent)
.

Let
\pi_{\varepsilon}
be the QOT optimizer and let Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
hold.
Set

C_{0}\coloneqq\overline{\lambda}_{\nu}(1+L^{2})^{d/2}\omega_{d}

and

C_{\mathrm{lin}}\coloneqq\int cdP-\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)+\frac{1}{2}.

Then there exist constants

c_{\mathrm{sm}}=\Big(\frac{1}{2C_{0}C_{\mathrm{val}}}\Big)^{1/d},c_{\mathrm{lg}}=\Big(\frac{1}{2C_{0}C_{\mathrm{lin}}}\Big)^{1/d}>0

(depending only on
d,L,\overline{\lambda}_{\nu}
and
C_{\mathrm{val}}
) such that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\dist\bigl(\spt\pi_{\varepsilon};\gr T\bigr)\geq\begin{cases}c_{\mathrm{sm}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2+d}},&\varepsilon\in(0,1],\\
c_{\mathrm{lg}},&\varepsilon\geq 1.\end{cases}

(3.3)

In particular,
\dist(\spt\pi_{\varepsilon};\gr T)\geq\min\{c_{\mathrm{sm}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2+d}},c_{\mathrm{lg}}\}
for all
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof sketch.

When
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, we combine this with the sharp upper bound on the value gap to obtain the first result.
When
\varepsilon\geq 1
, we use the competitor
\pi=P=\mu\otimes\nu
in (
1.1
), giving
\mathrm{QOT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)\leq\int cdP+\varepsilon/2
.

We focus on the small
\varepsilon
regime, so we get this corollary:

Corollary 3.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.3
, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,

\dist(\spt\pi_{\varepsilon};\gr T)\geq c_{\mathrm{sm}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{d+2}}

and hence

\sup_{(x,y)\in\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}}\|y-T(x)\|\geq c_{\mathrm{sm}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{d+2}}.

3.2
A value-gap bound on the mean-squared bias

This subsection provides a simple inequality that converts the QOT value gap
\Delta_{\varepsilon}
convergence into map-level control.

Lemma 3.5

(Value gap controls mean-squared bias)
.

Assume
\varphi
is differentiable and Assumption
3
holds.
Let
\pi_{\varepsilon}
be the optimizer of (
1.1
).
Then

\int\|y-\nabla\varphi(x)\|^{2}\pi_{\varepsilon}(dx,dy)\leq 2L\Delta_{\varepsilon}

(3.4)

In fact, the stronger inequality holds:

\displaystyle\int\|y-\nabla\varphi(x)\|^{2}\pi(dx,dy)\leq 2L\Big(\int cd\pi-\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)\Big)

\displaystyle\ \forall\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu),

and (
3.4
) follows since
\int cd\pi_{\varepsilon}-\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)\leq\Delta_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof sketch.

The cost gap
\int cd\pi-\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)
equals
\int D_{\varphi}d\pi
, where
D_{\varphi}(x,y)=\varphi(x)+\varphi^{*}(y)-\langle x,y\rangle
is the Fenchel-Young slack.
If
\nabla\varphi
is
L
-Lipschitz, smoothness implies the pointwise bound
D_{\varphi}(x,y)\geq\|y-\nabla\varphi(x)\|^{2}/(2L)
; integrating gives the claim.

We also prove that this is the sharp bound in
\varepsilon
for the mean-squared bias through Lemma
A.1
.
Lemma
3.5
yields the sharp
\varepsilon^{2/(d+2)}
mean-square scale and, by a Markov argument, a corresponding high-probability bound under
\pi_{\varepsilon}
, which helps us control how most of the weight of
\pi_{\varepsilon}
spreads, shown in Theorem
3.6
below.

Theorem 3.6

(Mean-squared and high-probability bias from the value gap)
.

Assume Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
hold.
For every
t>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,

\pi_{\varepsilon}\Bigl(\{(x,y):\|y-T(x)\|\geq t\}\Bigr)\leq\frac{2LC_{\mathrm{val}}}{t^{2}}\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}.

(3.5)

Equivalently, for every
\eta\in(0,1)
, choosing
t=\sqrt{2LC_{\mathrm{val}}}\eta^{-1/2}\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
yields

\pi_{\varepsilon}\Bigl(\bigl\{(x,y):\|y-T(x)\|\leq\sqrt{2LC_{\mathrm{val}}}\eta^{-1/2}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{d+2}}\bigr\}\Bigr)\geq 1-\eta.

The bound (
3.5
) is a tail bound under the measure
\pi_{\varepsilon}
and does not directly control
\sup_{(x,y)\in\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}}\|y-T(x)\|
.

3.3
Affine Monge maps (Gaussian case): reduction to self-transport and sharp pointwise bias

This subsection gives the
\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
pointwise bias in the regime where the Monge map is known and affine, which includes some truncated Gaussian-Gaussian case.
The main point is that in the affine Brenier regime, the quadratically regularized problem reduces exactly to a self-transport problem, and we can then invoke the sharp self-transport tube thickness of
35
.
In this subsection, we assume that:

Assumption 4
.

The Monge map is affine:

T(x)=Ax+a,A\in S^{d}_{++},a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This is a stronger version of Assumption
3
.
Assumption
4
holds, for instance, when
\mu,\nu
are nondegenerate Gaussians (then
T
is affine with
A\in S^{d}_{++}
).

We have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.7

(Affine case)
.

Assume Assumption
1
and Assumption
4
.
Let

4
Related works

4.1
Quadratically regularized OT (QOT)’s structure, potentials, and behavior

Quadratic regularization of OT has been studied both for computational reasons and as a means to obtain sparse couplings.

3
is one of the earliest to propose applying strongly convex primal regularization (including squared
\ell^{2}
penalties) to promote sparsity.
In the discrete/graph setting,
11
analyzed quadratically regularized OT (minimum-cost flows) and exploited its second-order dual structure to derive Newton-type algorithms, with a detailed description of how the solution structure changes as the regularization vanishes.

On the asymptotic side,
13
connected the infinitesimal behavior of QOT to porous-medium-type self-similar profiles.
Finally,
29
proved the existence of QOT potentials under square-integrable costs, analyzed non-uniqueness phenomena (linked to sparsity), and provided qualitative results of sparsity in continuous settings.

Relation to other regularization schemes.

The tube-versus-density mechanism used in Theorem
3.3
is not specific to the quadratic exponent.
Using Hölder’s inequality instead of the Cauchy-Schwarz step in Lemma
3.2
, we get the lower bound
r^{-d(p-1)}
for the
L^{p}
norm of the density.
Thus any sharp value-gap upper bound for the corresponding
L^{p}
-regularized problem can be converted into a support lower bound by the same argument.
The Orlicz-space framework of
25
provides the natural functional-analytic setting for such power-type penalties.

ENOT is different in nature: it regularizes the dual Kantorovich potentials for neural optimal transport rather than imposing a primal
f
-divergence penalty on couplings with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu

(
7
)
.
Therefore, the support statements proved here do not transfer directly to ENOT.
Nevertheless, the hinge/slack viewpoint in QOT suggests a useful conceptual bridge: regularizing positive dual infeasibility can control bias while preserving sparsity or near-sparsity, but the exact localization exponent for ENOT requires a separate analysis of its dual-side objective.

A fundamental quantitative object is the value gap
\Delta_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{QOT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)-\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)
.
Under quantization-type assumptions,
10
derived convergence rates for a broad class of regularizers; specializing to
L^{2}
regularization yields

\Delta_{\varepsilon}=\Theta\left(\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}\right)(\varepsilon\downarrow 0),

which is the scaling used throughout our analysis.

Recent work shows that sparsity-promoting regularization need not forfeit parametric statistical rates.

15
established parametric sample complexity and CLTs for QOT costs, couplings, and dual potentials.
This further motivates quantitative localization bounds such as ours, since geometric control of
\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}
directly enters both map estimation and statistical complexity arguments.

4.2
Known quantitative bounds for QOT

We briefly recall the main quantitative bounds on the support of
\pi_{\varepsilon}
, which serve as benchmarks for our results
(
17

35
)
.

One-dimensional case.

A key feature of quadratically regularized OT is that optimal couplings are typically supported on a thickened graph.
To quantify this, we look at vertical fibers over
x
and their diameters.
For a coupling
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\spt\pi
over

\mathcal{Y}_{\pi}(x)\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:(x,y)\in\spt\pi\},

with (fiber) thickness

\tau_{\pi}(x)\coloneqq\diam\mathcal{Y}_{\pi}(x)=\sup_{y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\pi}(x)}\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|.

When the coupling is the quadratically regularized optimizer

(defined below), we abbreviate

and
\tau_{\varepsilon}(x)\coloneqq\tau_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}(x)

When

\mu,\nu
have continuous densities bounded away from

and

17

\sup_{x}\tau_{\varepsilon}(x)=\Theta(\varepsilon^{1/3}),\dist(\spt\pi_{\varepsilon};\gr T)=\Theta(\varepsilon^{1/3}),

and establish uniform strong convexity bounds for
f_{\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}

General dimension case.

35

\spt\mu

Here,

to

If

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we quantified how the QOT optimizer
\pi_{\varepsilon}
localizes around the Monge coupling for the quadratic cost as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
First, under standard regularity of the marginals and a Lipschitz Monge map, we proved an information-theoretic lower bound showing that the directed Hausdorff distance from
\spt\pi_{\varepsilon}
to the Monge graph
\gr T
cannot shrink faster than order
\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
.
Second, we linked geometry to optimality by showing that the value gap
\Delta_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{QOT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)-\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)
controls the mean-squared deviation from the Monge map under
\pi_{\varepsilon}
, and we combined this inequality with sharp value-gap rates in
10
to obtain the
\varepsilon^{2/(d+2)}
scale and corresponding high-probability bounds.
Third, in the affine Brenier regime (including Gaussian-to-Gaussian transport), we gave an exact reduction to a self-transport QOT problem and obtained a matching pointwise tube bound of order
\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(A)}\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
via sharp self-transport sparsity estimates.
We also outlined synthetic experiments designed to directly probe this predicted scaling in the affine setting.

Limitations.

The matching pointwise upper bound proved here is restricted to the affine Brenier regime, so closing the gap between existing general upper bounds and the
\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
barrier in the fully non-affine case remains open.

Open questions.

A central theoretical direction is to close the remaining gap between general upper bounds and the
\varepsilon^{1/(d+2)}
lower bound in the fully non-affine setting, ideally under minimal assumptions on
(\mu,\nu)
and on the regularity of the Brenier potential.
From a modeling and computational perspective, extending the analysis to non-compact marginals (e.g., untruncated Gaussians, which can be achieved by extending Wiesel’s proof in
35
to the unbounded support case) and quantifying the effects of truncation and discretization on sparsity and bias would strengthen the connection to practice.
However, this is a challenging task because the distributions are no longer compactly supported.
We may start with
29
, where Polish probability spaces are considered.

, and we conclude

\int D_{\varphi}d\pi=\int cd\pi-\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu)\ \forall\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu).

(A.2)

Next, we lower bound the slack by the squared bias.
Since
\nabla\varphi
is
L
-Lipschitz, the standard smoothness (descent) inequality gives

\varphi(z)\leq\varphi(x)+\langle\nabla\varphi(x),z-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|z-x\|^{2}.

Fix

and choose

.
Then, by the definition of

,

\varphi^{*}(y)=\sup_{w}\{\langle y,w\rangle-\varphi(w)\}\geq\langle y,z\rangle-\varphi(z),

and plugging the smoothness bound at

yields the pointwise inequality

D_{\varphi}(x,y)=\varphi(x)+\varphi^{*}(y)-\langle x,y\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2L}\|y-\nabla\varphi(x)\|^{2}.

(A.3)

This is the standard “smoothness
\Rightarrow
quadratic lower bound on Fenchel-Young slack”.
Integrating (
A.3
) against any

and using (

) gives

\frac{1}{2L}\int\|y-\nabla\varphi(x)\|^{2}d\pi\leq\int D_{\varphi}d\pi=\int cd\pi-\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu).

Apply this to

and note

to obtain (

∎

Lemma A.1

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

Let
(W,\mathcal{A})
and
(Z,\mathcal{B})
be measurable spaces, let
\mathcal{F}:W\to Z
be measurable, and let
\widetilde{\pi}
be a measure on
(W,\mathcal{A})
.
Define the pushforward (image measure)
\pi\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{\#}\widetilde{\pi}
on
(Z,\mathcal{B})
by

\pi(B)\coloneqq\widetilde{\pi}(\mathcal{F}^{-1}(B))\ \forall B\in\mathcal{B}.

We prove that for every
\mathcal{B}
-measurable

\int_{Z}\psi(z)\pi(dz)=\int_{W}\psi(\mathcal{F}(w))\widetilde{\pi}(dw).

(A.5)

(If
\psi
is integrable, the same identity follows by applying the result to
\psi^{+}
and
\psi^{-}

Step 1: indicator functions.

Let
\psi=\mathbf{1}_{B}
for some

. Then, by definition of the integral of an indicator and of pushforward,

\displaystyle\int_{Z}\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)\pi(dz)

\displaystyle=\pi(B)=\widetilde{\pi}(\mathcal{F}^{-1}(B))=\int_{W}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{F}^{-1}(B)}(w)\widetilde{\pi}(dw)

\displaystyle=\int_{W}\mathbf{1}_{B}(\mathcal{F}(w))\widetilde{\pi}(dw)=\int_{W}\psi(\mathcal{F}(w))\widetilde{\pi}(dw).

Step 2: nonnegative simple functions.

Let
\psi
be simple:
\psi=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\mathbf{1}_{B_{k}}
with
a_{k}\geq 0
and
B_{k}\in\mathcal{B}
.
Using linearity of the integral and Step 1,

\displaystyle\int_{Z}\psi(z)\pi(dz)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\int_{Z}\mathbf{1}_{B_{k}}(z)\pi(dz)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\int_{W}\mathbf{1}_{B_{k}}(\mathcal{F}(w))\widetilde{\pi}(dw)

\displaystyle=\int_{W}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\mathbf{1}_{B_{k}}(\mathcal{F}(w))\Big)\widetilde{\pi}(dw)=\int_{W}\psi(\mathcal{F}(w))\widetilde{\pi}(dw).

Step 3: general nonnegative measurable functions.

Let
\psi:Z\to[0,\infty]
be
\mathcal{B}
-measurable.
For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, define the Borel (simple) function
\phi_{m}:[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty)
by

\phi_{m}(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}2^{-m}\lfloor 2^{m}t\rfloor,&0\leq t<m,\\
m,&t\in[m,\infty],\end{cases}

and set
\psi_{m}\coloneqq\phi_{m}\circ\psi
.
Then each
\psi_{m}
is a nonnegative simple
\mathcal{B}
-measurable function, and

Applying Step 2 to each

\int_{Z}\psi_{m}d\pi=\int_{W}(\psi_{m}\circ\mathcal{F})d\widetilde{\pi}\ \forall m.

Since
\psi_{m}\uparrow\psi
implies
\psi_{m}\circ\mathcal{F}\uparrow\psi\circ\mathcal{F}
, the monotone convergence theorem gives

\int_{Z}\psi d\pi=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{Z}\psi_{m}d\pi=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{W}(\psi_{m}\circ\mathcal{F})d\widetilde{\pi}=\int_{W}(\psi\circ\mathcal{F})d\widetilde{\pi},

which proves (

) for all
\psi\geq 0
.

Step 4: integrable (signed) functions.

If
\psi\in L^{1}(\pi)
is real-valued, write
\psi=\psi^{+}-\psi^{-}
with
\psi^{\pm}\geq 0
and
\int\psi^{\pm}d\pi<\infty
.
Applying Step 3 to
\psi^{\pm}
and subtracting gives

\int_{Z}\psi d\pi=\int_{W}(\psi\circ\mathcal{F})d\widetilde{\pi}.

Moreover, applying Step 3 to

yields

\int_{Z}|\psi|d\pi=\int_{W}(|\psi|\circ\mathcal{F})d\widetilde{\pi}.

Hence
\psi\in L^{1}(\pi)
if and only if
\psi\circ\mathcal{F}\in L^{1}(\widetilde{\pi})
, and in that case the integrals coincide.

Compactly supported test families.

Choose a bounded Lipschitz domain

We use the compatible truncated Gaussian family: Fix

\displaystyle d\mu(x)=

\displaystyle d\nu(y)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{1}}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}(y-m_{1})^{\top}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}(y-m_{1})\Big)

By Proposition

In this family,

Finite-sample discretization.

We generate empirical measures as follows. Sample

B.3
Discretization and the Discrete QOT Problem

Let
\mu_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\nu_{M}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}b_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}
be discrete approximations of
\mu
and
\nu
.
In the affine pushforward setting
\nu=T_{\#}\mu
, it is convenient to sample
\nu
by pushforward:

x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mu,\tilde{x}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mu,y_{j}\coloneqq T(\tilde{x}_{j})\sim\nu,a_{i}=\frac{1}{N},b_{j}=\frac{1}{M}.

(One may also sample
y_{j}\sim\nu
directly; this formula guarantees the exact affine relation at the population level.)

Dual/KKT form (support-revealing).

Define the discrete quadratic cost matrix

c_{ij}\coloneqq c(x_{i},y_{j})=\frac{1}{2}\|x_{i}-y_{j}\|^{2},i=1,\dots,N,j=1,\dots,M.

The discrete quadratically regularized OT problem with reference weights
P_{ij}=a_{i}b_{j}
is

\mathrm{QOT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{N},\nu_{M})=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(a,b)}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}c_{ij}\pi_{ij}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{\pi_{ij}^{2}}{a_{i}b_{j}}\Big\},

(B.4)

where

\Pi(a,b)\coloneqq\Bigl\{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N\times M}:\sum_{j=1}^{M}\pi_{ij}=a_{i}\ \forall i,\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{ij}=b_{j}\ \forall j\Bigr\}.

The discrete analogue of (
2.2
)-(
2.4
)-(
2.5
) is: there exist dual vectors
(f_{i})_{i\leq N}
and
(g_{j})_{j\leq M}
such that

\pi_{ij}=\frac{a_{i}b_{j}}{\varepsilon}\bigl[f_{i}+g_{j}-c_{ij}\bigr]_{+},

(B.5)

and the marginal constraints are equivalent to the nonlinear system

\sum_{j=1}^{M}b_{j}\bigl[f_{i}+g_{j}-c_{ij}\bigr]_{+}=\varepsilon\ \forall i,\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\bigl[f_{i}+g_{j}-c_{ij}\bigr]_{+}=\varepsilon\ \forall j.

(B.6)

Equations (
B.6
) admit efficient solvers (nonlinear Gauss-Seidel / coordinate descent and semismooth Newton methods)
(
24
)
and directly identify the discrete support
\{(i,j):\pi_{ij}>0\}
via (
B.5
).

Dual objective (discrete
\chi^{2}
regularization).

For
\mu_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\nu_{M}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}b_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}
, the discrete dual associated with (
1.1
) is the unconstrained concave maximization

\max_{f\in\mathbb{R}^{N},g\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}f_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}b_{j}g_{j}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{i}b_{j}[f_{i}+g_{j}-c_{ij}]_{+}^{2}\Big\}.

(B.7)

The (everywhere-defined) gradient is

\displaystyle\nabla_{f_{i}}(\text{obj})

\displaystyle=a_{i}-\frac{a_{i}}{\varepsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{M}b_{j}[f_{i}+g_{j}-c_{ij}]_{+},i=1,\dots,N,

(B.8)

\displaystyle\nabla_{g_{j}}(\text{obj})

\displaystyle=b_{j}-\frac{b_{j}}{\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}[f_{i}+g_{j}-c_{ij}]_{+},j=1,\dots,M.

Thus the first-order optimality conditions
\nabla(\text{obj})=0
are exactly the marginal system (
B.6
).
In large-scale settings, (
B.7
) can be optimized by (stochastic) gradient ascent using mini-batches to estimate the sums in (
B.8
).
