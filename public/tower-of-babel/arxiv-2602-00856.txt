Higher-order transformations of bidirectional quantum processes
Luca Apadula, Alessandro Bisio, Giulio Chiribella, Paolo Perinotti, Kyrylo Simonov

Abstract
Bidirectional devices are devices for which the roles of the input and output ports can be exchanged. Mathematically, these devices are described by bistochastic quantum channels, namely completely positive linear maps that are both trace-preserving and identity-preserving. Recently, it has been shown that bidirectional quantum devices can, in principle, be used in ways that are incompatible with a definite input-output direction, giving rise to a new phenomenon called input-output indefiniteness. Here we characterize the most general forms of input-output indefiniteness, associated with a hierarchy of higher-order transformations built from transformations of bistochastic quantum channels. Some levels of the hierarchy correspond to transformations that combine bistochastic channels in a definite causal order, while generally using each channel in an indefinite input-output direction. For other levels of the hierarchy, the indefiniteness can involve both the local input-output direction of each process and the global causal order among the processes. On the foundational side, the hierarchy of higher-order transformations characterized here can be regarded as the largest set of physical processes compatible with a time-symmetric variant of quantum theory, where the possible state transformations are restricted to bistochastic channels.

Introduction

An important class of quantum devices is bidirectional, meaning that the roles of the input port (through which the system enters) and the output port (from which the system exits) are interchangeable. For example, optical crystals such as half-wave plates and quarter-wave plates can be traversed in two opposite directions, generally giving rise to different rotations of the photon’s polarization. Mathematically, bidirectional devices correspond to bistochastic quantum channels

By definition, a bidirectional quantum device can be used in two alternative ways, depending on which port is treated as the input port. Recently, it was shown that bidirectional quantum devices can also be used in new ways, in which it is impossible to determine which port is the input port and which port is the output port
26

The basic framework for quantum operations with indefinite input-output direction was developed in Ref.

However, the case of general higher-order transformations with arbitrary numbers of channels in the output has remained open until now. These transformations represent deeper layers in the manipulation of bistochastic devices and can potentially provide new insights into time symmetry in quantum mechanics.

In this paper, we provide a complete characterization of all admissible transformations with indefinite input-output direction. We define an infinite hierarchy of higher-order transformations, in which transformations of bistochastic channels provide the first level, and transformations at lower levels serve as inputs for transformations at higher levels. By characterizing the various levels of the hierarchy, we identify a variety of processes with interesting causal structures. For example, one of the levels of the hierarchy contains transformations that combine bistochastic channels in a definite causal order, while using each individual channel in an indefinite input-output direction. Physically, these transformations can be thought of as ways of connecting the operations performed in a sequence of local laboratories, in which the input-output direction is not fixed, but the causal order of the laboratories is. For these transformations, we prove a realization theorem, showing that they can be decomposed into a causally ordered sequence of transformations of individual bistochastic channels.

Quantum supermaps were originally defined as transformations of quantum channels, that is, completely positive trace-preserving maps. The corresponding hierarchy of higher-order transformations was then built by recursively defining higher-level transformations starting from quantum channels at the basic level

The key difference here is that we consider supermaps defined on bistochastic channels, a proper subset of the set of all quantum channels. From a foundational point of view, bistochastic channels can be viewed as the dynamics in a time-symmetric variant of quantum theory, where the allowed state preparations are ensemble decompositions of the maximally mixed state

.

(Extended event). Let

.

Definition IV.5
.

(Admissible event). Let

be the linear maps whose Choi operators are

and

the set of

and

((\mathcal{M}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}_{i})\otimes\mathcal{I}_{E})[T(x||E)]\subseteq T(y||E)

It is clear from Definition

We now proceed to characterize the set of admissible events for an arbitrary type, thereby covering the entire hierarchy of higher-order maps.

Theorem IV.1
.

(Characterization of admissible events)
Let

be a type and

M\in T(x)\Leftrightarrow M\geq 0\wedge\exists D\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(x)\;\mbox{s.t.}\;M\leq D.

(16)

Let
x,y
be two types,
M\in T(x\to y)
be an event of type
x\to y
, and let
\mathcal{M}:T_{\mathbb{R}}(x)\to T_{\mathbb{R}}(y)
be the linear map whose Choi operator is

\displaystyle M\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(x\to y)\!\iff\!\!\left\{\!\begin{array}[]{l}M\geq 0,\\
\mathcal{M}[\mathsf{T}_{1}(x)]\subseteq\mathsf{T}_{1}({y})\end{array}\right.

Theorem IV.2
.

(Characterization of
\mathsf{T}_{1}(x)
). Let

be a type. Then we have:

\displaystyle R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(x)\Leftrightarrow R=\lambda_{x}\mathds{1}_{x}+X_{x},

(19)

\displaystyle X_{x}\in\Delta_{x}\subseteq\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{x}),\quad R\geq 0,

(20)

\lambda_{x}

are defined recursively as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda_{E}=\frac{1}{d_{E}},

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{\hat{X}A\to\hat{Y}B}=\frac{1}{d_{Y}d_{B}},\quad\lambda_{x\rightarrow y}=\frac{\lambda_{y}}{d_{x}\lambda_{x}},

(21)

\displaystyle\Delta_{A_{1}\dots A_{n}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}\dots A_{n}}),

(22)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\hat{X}A\rightarrow\hat{Y}B}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{XAY})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B})

\displaystyle\qquad\oplus\big(\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{X})\otimes\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{A})

\displaystyle\qquad\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{Y})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{B}\big),

(23)

\displaystyle\Delta_{x\rightarrow y}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{x})\otimes\Delta_{y}\big)\oplus\big(\overline{\Delta}_{x}\otimes\Delta^{\perp}_{y}\big),

where, for a given Hilbert space

is the space of all Hermitian operators on
\mathcal{H}_{A}

and

are the orthogonal complements of

in
\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{x})
and

Proof.

V.1
Basic higher-order operations and bistochastic supermaps

In order to derive some relevant instances of higher-order maps for bistochastic devices, we still need to introduce the notion of a linear functional on a type

Definition V.1
.

Given a type
x
, we define the type of functionals on events of type
x
as follows:

\overline{x}=x\rightarrow I.

(25)

Given two types
x
and

(26)

The deterministic events of types

and

Lemma V.1
.

For types

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{\overline{x}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\Delta}_{x},\quad\lambda_{\overline{x}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{x}d_{x}},

(27)

\displaystyle\Delta_{x\otimes y}

\displaystyle=(\mathsf{I}_{y}\otimes\Delta_{x})\oplus(\Delta_{y}\otimes\Delta_{x})\oplus(\Delta_{y}\otimes\mathsf{I}_{x}),

(28)

\displaystyle\lambda_{x\otimes y}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{x}\lambda_{y}.

(29)

Proof.

See Lemma 2 and Lemma 5 of

\,\blacksquare

As a relevant example, functionals on bistochastic channels, corresponding to higher-order maps transforming the latter into unit probability, are characterized by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}((\hat{A}\rightarrow\hat{B})\rightarrow I)\iff\\
&R=\frac{1}{d}\mathds{1}_{AB}+X_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}+\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes X_{B},\\

The following lemma shows that such functionals correspond operationally to choosing one of the two input-output directions probabilistically, preparing a state at the chosen input, and discarding the chosen output.

Lemma V.2
.

Let
R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}((\hat{A}\rightarrow\hat{B})\rightarrow I)
. Then there exist

and density operators
\rho_{A}
on
A
and
\sigma_{B}
on

such that

R=p\rho_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}+(1-p)\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}.

(31)

Proof.

See Appendix
C
.

\,\blacksquare

This result shows that any deterministic functional on a bistochastic channel yields probabilities by classically selecting whether the device is used in the
A\rightarrow B
or
B\rightarrow A
direction
36
.

V.2
Networks of higher-order maps and bistochastic generalization of quantum combs

The notion of indefinite input-output direction captures the fact that in such a generalisation of quantum theory a physical device may not possess a predetermined orientation between its input and output systems: the same operation may be used in either direction. While a single bistochastic device already allows for such local bidirectional use, one may consider scenarios involving multiple such devices arranged in a definite order. To formalize this setting, we first recall the standard hierarchy of quantum combs, which describes higher-order maps where each inserted operation has a fixed input-output orientation and where all operations are composed in a definite causal order.

Definition V.3
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, an
n
-comb is a higher-order map of type

\mathbf{n}\coloneqq(\mathbf{n-1})\rightarrow(P\rightarrow F),\qquad\mathbf{0}\coloneqq A\rightarrow B.

(33)

The distinctive feature of this class is that every element of
\mathsf{T}_{1}(\mathbf{n})
can be implemented as a quantum circuit with
n
open slots
40
:

\includegraphics[width]{ncomb.pdf}.

Each slot represents a local laboratory where the agent inserts an arbitrary quantum operation by connecting its input-output wires. This architecture enforces a fixed causal order among the operations and is known as a
quantum network
.

The simplest case is the
1
-comb, a deterministic event of type
\mathbf{1}=(A_{1}\rightarrow B_{1})\rightarrow(P\rightarrow F)
, corresponding to a
quantum supermap
transforming quantum operations into quantum operations:

\begin{array}[]{l}\includegraphics[width]{supermap+channel.pdf}\end{array}=\begin{array}[]{l}\includegraphics[width]{channel1.pdf}\end{array}.

Having recalled the standard notion of quantum combs, it is natural to ask how this hierarchy can be extended when the local operations are bistochastic. Such an extension should describe local laboratories where the agents can implement operations in either input-output direction, while still preserving a well-defined global causal order. Clearly, this is a special instance of the more general scenario in which (globally) causally ordered agents can implement operations of a given type in their local laboratories. We are therefore led to consider a notion of a
network of higher-order maps
(see Fig.
1
).

Figure 1:
A network of higher-order maps allows local agents to implement more general quantum operations (namely, higher-order maps), thereby inducing an indefinite causal structure among the local quantum systems, while the corresponding local laboratories remain causally ordered with respect to one another.

Definition V.4

(Network of higher-order maps)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a set of types. An operator
R\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{E_{0}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{x_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E_{n}})
is a deterministic network of
n
higher-order maps of types
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
if

\displaystyle R=M_{1}*M_{2}*\dots*M_{n},

(34)

\displaystyle M_{i}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(\overline{x_{i}}\to(E_{i-1}\to E_{i})).

(35)

We denote by
\mathtt{HN}_{\mathbf{n}}
the set of deterministic networks of
n
higher-order maps.

Note that the family of networks of higher-order maps is not identified by a type within the hierarchy, but rather by a constructive definition based on the composition of higher-order maps. This raises the question of whether the composition rule introduced in Equation (
34
) always defines an admissible higher-order transformation. In Appendix
D
, we answer this question in the affirmative.

The following result provides the mathematical characterization of the set of deterministic networks of
n
higher-order maps.

Proposition V.1
.

An operator
R
is a deterministic network of
n
higher-order maps if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} R&\geq 0,\\
R&=\lambda\mathds{1}_{E_{0}x_{1}\ldots x_{n}E_{n}}+X,\\
\lambda&=d^{-1}_{E_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{x_{i}},\quad X\in\Delta_{\mathtt{HN}_{\mathbf{n}}},\\
\end{aligned}

(36)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathtt{HN}_{\mathbf{n}}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{E_{0}})\otimes\Biggl[\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{x_{i}})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{E_{n}})

\displaystyle\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\Biggl(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{x_{j}})\otimes{\Delta}_{x_{i}}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=i+1}^{n}\mathsf{I}_{x_{j}}\Biggr)\otimes\mathsf{I}_{E_{n}}\Biggr].

(37)

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on
n
. For
n=1
, the statement reduces to the characterization of deterministic events of type
\overline{x_{1}}\to(E_{0}\to E_{1})
via Theorem
IV.2
. Hence, the claim holds.

Necessity.

Assume the statement holds for
n-1
, and let
R
be an operator satisfying (
36
) for
n
. We define:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\coloneqq R*\Bigl(\lambda_{\overline{x_{n}}}\mathds{1}_{x_{n}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E_{n}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=R*\Bigl(\frac{1}{\lambda_{x_{n}}d_{x_{n}}}\mathds{1}_{x_{n}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E_{n}}\Bigr).

(38)

Using
\operatorname{Tr}_{x_{n}E_{n}}[\mathds{1}_{x_{n}E_{n}}]=d_{x_{n}}d_{E_{n}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathds{1}_{E_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{n-1}}+Y,

(39)

where

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{1}{d_{x_{n}}\lambda_{x_{n}}}\operatorname{Tr}_{x_{n}E_{n}}[\mathds{1}_{x_{n}E_{n}}]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{x_{i}},

(40)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=X*\Bigl(\frac{1}{d_{x_{n}}\lambda_{x_{n}}}\mathds{1}_{x_{n}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E_{n}}\Bigr).

(41)

A straightforward calculation of (
41
) shows that the only non-vanishing components are ones with
\mathsf{I}_{x_{n}}\otimes\mathsf{I}_{E_{n}}
, proving that

Y\in\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{E_{0}})\otimes\Biggl[\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n-1}\Biggl(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{x_{j}})\otimes{\Delta}_{x_{i}}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=i+1}^{n-1}\mathsf{I}_{x_{j}}\Biggr)\Biggr],

(42)

i.e.,
Y\in\Delta_{\mathtt{HN}_{\mathbf{n-1}}}
with trivial
E_{n-1}
. Therefore,
S
obeys Equation (
36
) for
n-1
, and therefore it is a deterministic network of
n-1
higher-order maps.

Let us now define
\mathcal{H}_{E_{n-1}}\coloneqq\operatorname{supp}(S)
and introduce the operator

\displaystyle S^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{|S^{1/2*}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle S^{1/2*}|},

(43)

\displaystyle|S^{1/2*}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\mathds{1}_{E_{n-1}}\otimes S^{1/2})|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle,

(44)

Then

S^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}\mathds{1}+Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}\Bigl(\mathcal{H}_{E_{0}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{H}_{x_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E_{n-1}}\Bigr),

(45)

where
\alpha^{\prime}>0
and
Y^{\prime}
is a Hermitian traceless operator. Since

, we obtain

\alpha^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha}{d_{E_{n-1}}}.

(46)

We now expand

with respect to the decomposition
\mathsf{Hrm}(E_{n-1})=\mathsf{Trl}(E_{n-1})\oplus\mathbb{C}\mathds{1}_{E_{n-1}}

Y^{\prime}=\sum_{k}O_{k}\otimes A_{k}+W\otimes\mathds{1}_{E_{n-1}},

(47)

where
\{A_{k}\}
is a basis of traceless Hermitian operators on
\mathcal{H}_{E_{n-1}}
and
O_{k},W
are Hermitian and Hermitian traceless operators on
\mathcal{H}_{E_{0}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{H}_{x_{i}}
, respectively. Taking the partial trace over

and using
\operatorname{Tr}_{E_{n-1}}[S^{\prime}]=S
, we obtain

(48)

It follows that
S^{\prime}
satisfies Equation (
36
) for
n-1
slots, and therefore
S^{\prime}
is a deterministic network of
n-1
higher-order maps.

We now define

\displaystyle R^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(S^{-1/2}\otimes\mathds{1}_{x_{n}E_{n}})R(S^{-1/2}\otimes\mathds{1}_{x_{n}E_{n}})

(49)

\displaystyle=\beta\mathds{1}+Z\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{E_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{x_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E_{n}}),

(50)

with
\beta>0
and
Z
being a Hermitian traceless operator. Computing the trace yields

(51)

Since
S
acts trivially on
\mathcal{H}_{x_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E_{n}}
, the operator

satisfies Equation (
36
) for
n=1
, i.e.

R^{\prime}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(\overline{x_{n}}\to(E_{n-1}\to E_{n})).

(52)

Finally, using the associativity of the link product and the identity
S^{\prime}*R^{\prime}=R
, we conclude that
R
is a deterministic network of
n
higher-order maps.

Sufficiency.
Assume the statement holds for
n-1
, and suppose that
R
is a deterministic network of
n
higher-order maps. By definition, there exist deterministic maps
M_{i}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(\overline{x_{i}}\to(E_{i-1}\to E_{i}))
such that

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=S^{\prime}*M_{n},\qquad S^{\prime}\coloneqq M_{1}*M_{2}*\dots*M_{n-1}.

(53)

Accordingly,

\displaystyle S^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\prime}\mathds{1}+X,\qquad S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}\Bigl(\mathcal{H}_{E_{0}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{H}_{x_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E_{n-1}}\Bigr),

(54)

\displaystyle M_{n}

\displaystyle=\mu\mathds{1}+Y,\qquad M_{n}\in\mathcal{L}\Bigl(\mathcal{H}_{E_{n-1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{x_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E_{n}}\Bigr),

(55)

where

and

are traceless Hermitian operators. Since

, every

is positive. The link product of positive operators is positive, hence

.

Since
M_{n}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(\overline{x_{n}}\rightarrow(E_{n-1}\rightarrow E_{n}))
, it obeys Equation (
36
) for
n=1
. By the inductive hypothesis,

obeys Equation (
36
) for
n-1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}

\displaystyle=d^{-1}_{E_{n-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{x_{i}},

(56)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=d^{-1}_{E_{n}}\lambda_{x_{n}},

(57)

and
X\in\Delta_{\mathtt{HN}_{\mathbf{n-1}}}
and

. Expanding the link product, we obtain

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=(\lambda^{\prime}\mathds{1}_{E_{0}x_{1}\ldots x_{n-1}E_{n-1}})*(\mu\mathds{1}_{E_{n-1}x_{n}E_{n}})+Z

(58)

\displaystyle=(\lambda^{\prime}\mu d_{E_{n-1}})\mathds{1}_{E_{0}x_{1}\ldots x_{n}E_{n}}+Z

(59)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(d^{-1}_{E_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{x_{i}}\Bigr)\mathds{1}_{E_{0}x_{1}\ldots x_{n}E_{n}}+Z,

\,\blacksquare

The distinctive feature of this extended notion of network is that its local building blocks (namely, higher-order quantum maps) can represent processes with indefinite causal structure. The present characterization therefore provides a descriptive framework for the correlations that can arise in specific spacetime scenarios. In particular, it captures settings in which the causal structure may be indefinite locally, within each spacetime region or laboratory, while the causal relations between distinct regions remain definite. As a consequence, the spacetime regions themselves are constrained to follow a fixed sequential causal order. A pictorial representation of such a generalized network is shown in Fig.
1
. Higher-order maps are depicted as polygonal shapes, reflecting the enlarged set of possible local interventions available to each agent, in contrast to the single open slot characterizing standard quantum combs.

A particularly relevant subclass of deterministic networks of higher-order maps is obtained by choosing each
x_{i}
to correspond to a bistochastic channel.

Definition V.5

(Bi-tooth

As we have seen, it extends naturally to scenarios where the local operations are higher-order maps, while retaining a global causal ordering. ).

VI
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, we developed a framework for higher-order quantum theory based on bistochastic transformations, that is, transformations that preserve the maximally mixed state. The framework is grounded in an operational construction and provides a setting in which elementary devices can be applied in both temporal directions. The main goal is to formulate higher-order quantum theory without requiring a predefined assignment of input and output systems, thereby allowing bidirectional devices to be used in scenarios involving indefinite input-output direction. This is achieved through an operational notion of admissibility that leads to a recursive characterization of higher-order maps of arbitrary types involving bistochastic transformations.

From a pragmatic perspective, our framework allows for quantum information processing and computation with bidirectional devices whose input-output direction can be indefinite. Several examples of higher-order maps discussed in this work, such as the quantum time flip (already implemented experimentally in photonic setups), the flippable quantum
SWITCH
, and the Liu–Chiribella processes, can be described within our framework while lying outside ordinary higher-order quantum theory, including the standard process matrix formalism. Consequently, we introduced nontrivial bistochastic extensions of quantum combs and process matrices. In particular, by defining a hierarchy of bistochastic counterparts of quantum combs, which we call bi-slot quantum combs, we showed that although such maps are not necessarily implementable within the standard quantum circuit model, they can always be decomposed into sequences of local transformations acting on bidirectional devices. As a result, global causal order is preserved at the level of local devices, while their temporal direction is not fixed.

Several questions remain open. An important direction is the study of bidirectional operations as a resource for potential advantages in communication, computation, and channel discrimination. A particularly relevant line of research concerns quantum thermodynamics, where bistochastic transformations coincide with entropy-nondecreasing operations. Another promising direction concerns the signaling structure of higher-order maps involving bidirectional devices. In standard higher-order quantum theory, there is a fundamental correspondence between compositional structure and signaling relations
3
. However, allowing both ports of a device to act as input and output may alter this connection. This motivates further investigation into quantum causal modeling
2
, where our framework may serve as the basis for a new class of models in which nodes do not have a fixed assignment of input and output systems.

\mathcal{M}[\mathsf{T}_{1}(x)]\subseteq\mathsf{T}_{1}(y)
, the claim follows.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
IV.2

We now conclude this section with the proof of Theorem
IV.2
. First, we have the following lemma.

Lemma A.8
.

Let
x
be a type. Then
R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(x)\iff R^{T}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(x)
.

Proof.

The statement is obvious for
x\in{\sf A}
. It is also easy to check that
R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(\hat{A}B\to\hat{C}D)\iff R^{T}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(\hat{A}B\to\hat{C}D)
. Hence, the statement holds for any
x\in{\sf EleTypes}
. The proof for non-elementary types is the same as that of Lemma 5.5 of Ref.
13
.

\,\blacksquare

We can now complete the proof of Theorem
IV.2
. The validity of Equations (
22
) and (
23
) can be checked by direct inspection. Let us consider the case in which
x=y\to z
is not elementary. From Equation (
IV.1
), we know that
M\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(y\to z)
if and only if
M\geq 0
and
\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{T}_{1}(y))\subseteq\mathsf{T}_{1}(z)
, i.e.,
\operatorname{Tr}_{y}[M(\mathds{1}_{z}\otimes S_{y}^{T})]\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(z)
for any
S_{y}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(y)
. From Lemma (
74
), we have

\forall S_{y}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(y):\operatorname{Tr}_{y}[M(\mathds{1}_{z}\otimes S_{y}^{T})]\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(z)\iff\forall S_{y}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(y):\operatorname{Tr}_{y}[M(\mathds{1}_{z}\otimes S_{y})]\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(z).

(75)

Then Equation (
24

).

Appendix B
Quantum time flip

Definition B.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{B}))
be a completely positive map with Kraus operators
\{C_{i}\}_{i}
. The quantum time flip is a supermap that transforms
\mathcal{C}
into a new operation
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{P_{t}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{P_{c}}),\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{F_{t}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{F_{c}}))
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})[\rho]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}F_{i}(\rho\otimes\omega)F_{i}^{\dagger},\qquad F_{i}\coloneqq C_{i}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|+C_{i}^{T}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|,

(76)

where
\mathcal{H}_{P_{t}}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{A}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{B}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{F_{t}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{P_{c}}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{F_{c}}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}
,
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{P_{t}})
and
\omega\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{P_{c}})
, with indices
t
and
c
corresponding to target and control systems, respectively.

A straightforward calculation shows that the quantum time flip is defined by a Choi operator

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{F}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{F}|,

(77)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle_{P_{t}A}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle_{BF_{t}}\otimes|0\rangle_{P_{c}}\otimes|0\rangle_{F_{c}}

\displaystyle\qquad+|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle_{P_{t}B}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle_{AF_{t}}\otimes|1\rangle_{P_{c}}\otimes|1\rangle_{F_{c}},

(78)

and the supermap (
76
) can be obtained as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})[\rho]

\displaystyle=R*\rho*\omega*C

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}_{ABP}\!\left[|\mathcal{F}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{F}|\bigl(\mathds{1}_{F}\otimes\rho^{T}\otimes\omega^{T}\otimes C^{T}\bigr)\right],

(79)

\displaystyle F_{i}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle C_{i}|^{T}|\mathcal{F}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=C_{i}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|+C_{i}^{T}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|,

(80)

for the corresponding
C\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(\hat{A}\rightarrow\hat{B})
with Kraus operators
\{C_{i}\}_{i}
.

In order to verify that
R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}((\hat{A}\rightarrow\hat{B})\rightarrow(P\rightarrow F))
, we take into account that, by Theorem
IV.2
, this is equivalent to:

R=\lambda_{R}\mathds{1}_{PABF}+X_{R},\qquad\lambda_{R}=\frac{1}{d_{F}d_{A}},\qquad X_{R}\in\Delta_{(\hat{A}\rightarrow\hat{B})\rightarrow(P\rightarrow F)},

(81)

where

\Delta_{(\hat{A}\rightarrow\hat{B})\rightarrow(P\rightarrow F)}=\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{PAB})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{F})\oplus\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{P})\otimes\bigl(\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{B}\oplus\mathsf{I}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\bigr)\otimes\mathsf{I}_{F}.

(82)

Equivalently,

X_{R}\notin\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{P})\otimes\bigl(\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\oplus\mathsf{I}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{I}_{B}\bigr)\otimes\mathsf{I}_{F}.

(83)

For comparison, the corresponding ordinary supermaps are characterized by

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\lambda_{R}\,\mathds{1}_{PABF}+X_{R},\qquad\lambda_{R}=\frac{1}{d_{F}d_{A}},

(84)

\displaystyle X_{R}

\displaystyle\notin\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{P})\otimes\bigl(\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\oplus\mathsf{I}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\oplus\mathsf{I}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{I}_{B}\bigr)\otimes\mathsf{I}_{F}.

(85)

Hence, a bistochastic but non-ordinary supermap must contain traceless components in the difference

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{P})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{A}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{F}.

(86)

First, expand
R
:

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{P_{t}A_{I}}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{A_{O}F_{t}}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{P_{c}}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{F_{c}}

(87)

\displaystyle\quad+|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{P_{t}B_{O}}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{A_{I}F_{t}}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|_{P_{c}}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|_{F_{c}}

(88)

\displaystyle\quad+\ldots,

(89)

where
\ldots
denotes the off-diagonal terms in the control subsystems
P_{c}
and
F_{c}
. These off-diagonal terms are traceless on
F_{c}
and therefore belong to the subspace

\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{PAB})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{F})\subsetneq\Delta_{(\hat{A}\rightarrow\hat{B})\rightarrow(P\rightarrow F)}.

(90)

Positivity of
R
is immediate from its definition as a rank-one operator:

R=|\mathcal{F}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\mathcal{F}|\;\geq\;0.

(91)

Moreover, using
\operatorname{Tr}(|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{XY})=d_{X}
for each maximally entangled pair, one checks that

\lambda_{R}=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[R]}{\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{H}_{PABF})}=\frac{1}{2d^{2}},

(92)

in agreement with the general normalization for a bistochastic supermap.

For each pair of isomorphic systems
X,Y
, we use the standard decomposition

|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{XY}=\frac{1}{d}\,\mathds{1}_{XY}+\sum_{\mu=1}^{d^{2}-1}F_{\mu,X}\otimes F_{\mu,Y}^{*},

(93)

where
\{F_{\mu,X}\}_{\mu=1}^{d^{2}-1}\subset\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{X})
is a traceless orthonormal operator basis on
X
. Inserting this into each maximally entangled link in (
77
) and expanding
R
, we now focus on those contributions whose local factor on
F=F_{t}F_{c}
is proportional to the identity operator
\mathds{1}_{F}
. Denote their sum by
R^{(0)}
, which is given by

\displaystyle R^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\,\mathds{1}_{P_{t}ABF_{t}P_{c}F_{c}}+\frac{1}{2d}\sum_{\mu}\Bigl(F_{\mu,P_{t}}\otimes F_{\mu,A}^{*}\otimes\mathds{1}_{BF_{t}}+F_{\mu,P_{t}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes F_{\mu,B}^{*}\otimes\mathds{1}_{F_{t}}\Bigr)\otimes\mathds{1}_{P_{c}F_{c}}.

(94)

No term in
R^{(0)}
matches (
83
), so the quantum time flip belongs to the class of bistochastic supermaps. On the other hand, the last term in (
94
) is forbidden for ordinary supermaps, as (
86
) suggests. Therefore, the quantum time flip cannot be implemented within the quantum circuit model.

Appendix C
Proof of Lemma
V.2

In what follows, we adopt the strategy used in the proof of Lemma 2 in
36
. Let
R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}((\hat{A}\rightarrow\hat{B})\rightarrow I)
, so that it satisfies Eq. (
30
). We decompose
R
as

R=R_{\rm fwd}+R_{\rm bwd}-\frac{1}{d}\mathds{1}_{AB},

(95)

where

R_{\rm fwd}\coloneqq\frac{1}{d}\mathds{1}_{AB}+X_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B},\qquad R_{\rm bwd}\coloneqq\frac{1}{d}\mathds{1}_{AB}+\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes X_{B}.

(96)

Since
X_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}
and
\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes X_{B}
commute, the operators
R_{\rm fwd}
and
R_{\rm bwd}
commute and therefore admit a common eigenbasis
\{|u_{i}\rangle\}_{i}
, with

R_{\rm fwd}=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}|u_{i}\rangle\langle u_{i}|,\qquad R_{\rm bwd}=\sum_{i}\tilde{\mu}_{i}|u_{i}\rangle\langle u_{i}|,

(97)

and positivity of
R
implies
\mu_{i}+\tilde{\mu}_{i}\geq\frac{1}{d}
for all
i
. Let
\mu_{\rm min}\coloneqq\min_{i}\mu_{i}
. Because
\sum_{i}\mu_{i}=\operatorname{Tr}(R_{\rm fwd})=d
and the average eigenvalue equals
\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i}\mu_{i}=\frac{1}{d}
, we have
\mu_{\rm min}\leq\frac{1}{d}
. Moreover, defining

R^{\prime}_{\overline{A\rightarrow B}}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{d}-\mu_{\rm min}\Bigr)\mathds{1}_{AB}+X_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B},\qquad R^{\prime}_{\overline{B\rightarrow A}}=\mu_{\rm min}\mathds{1}_{AB}+\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes X_{B},

(98)

the eigenvalues satisfy

\mu_{i}-\mu_{\rm min}\geq 0,\qquad\tilde{\mu}_{i}-\frac{1}{d}+\mu_{\rm min}\geq 0,

(99)

where the second inequality follows from
\mu_{i}+\tilde{\mu}_{i}\geq\frac{1}{d}
. Thus
R^{\prime}_{\overline{A\rightarrow B}}
and
R^{\prime}_{\overline{B\rightarrow A}}
are positive, and

R=R^{\prime}_{\overline{A\rightarrow B}}+R^{\prime}_{\overline{B\rightarrow A}}.

(100)

Define

p\coloneqq d\Bigl(\frac{1}{d}-\mu_{\rm min}\Bigr)=1-d\mu_{\rm min},

(101)

so
p\in[0,1]
. If
\mu_{\rm min}<\frac{1}{d}
, define

\displaystyle R_{\overline{A\rightarrow B}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}R^{\prime}_{\overline{A\rightarrow B}}

(102)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\mathds{1}_{AB}+Y_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B},

where
Y_{A}\in\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
. By Theorem
IV.2
, any operator
R_{\overline{A\rightarrow B}}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}((A\rightarrow B)\rightarrow I)
must be of the form

\displaystyle R_{\overline{A\rightarrow B}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\mathds{1}_{AB}+Y_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}

(103)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl(\frac{1}{d}\mathds{1}_{A}+Y_{A}\Bigr)\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\rho_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B},

where
Y_{A}\in\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and therefore
\rho_{A}
is Hermitian, positive (because
R_{\overline{A\rightarrow B}}=\rho_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}\geq 0
), and satisfies
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho_{A}]=1
. Thus
\rho_{A}
is a density operator. By symmetry, if
\mu_{\rm min}>0
, then
\frac{1}{1-p}R^{\prime}_{\overline{B\rightarrow A}}
is an element of
\mathsf{T}_{1}((B\rightarrow A)\rightarrow I)
and must be of the form
\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}
for some density operator
\sigma_{B}
. Finally,

R=p(\rho_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})+(1-p)(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}),

(104)

proving the thesis.

Appendix D
Networks of higher-order maps are well-defined

In this appendix, we prove that a network of higher-order maps given by Definition
V.4
corresponds to an admissible event. For this purpose, we recall the notion of admissible composition from Ref.
3
.

Definition D.1
.

Let
x
and
y
be two types such that they share a set of elementary types. We say that the composition of
x
and
y
is
admissible
if

\displaystyle\begin{split}\forall R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(x),\,\forall S\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(y),\;\exists z\ \mbox{s.t.}\ R*S\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(z).\end{split}

(105)

First, we prove the following result.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
x
and
y
be arbitrary types, and let
R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(x\to(A\to B))
and
S\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(y\to(B\to C))
. Then

\displaystyle R*S\in\mathsf{T}_{1}((x\otimes y)\to(A\to C)).

(106)

Proof.

The proof is a direct computation. From Theorem
IV.2
we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{x\to(A\to B)}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{x})\otimes\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\oplus\overline{\Delta}_{x}\otimes\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{B},

(107)

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}_{x\otimes y}

\displaystyle=\overline{\Delta}_{x}\otimes(\mathsf{I}_{y}\oplus\Delta_{y})\oplus\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{x})\otimes\overline{\Delta}_{y}.

(108)

Then, for any
R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(x\to(A\to B))
and

S\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(y\to(B\to C))
, we have

\displaystyle R=\lambda\mathds{1}_{x}\otimes\mathds{1}_{AB}+\sum_{i}O_{i}\otimes Q_{i}+\sum_{m}D_{m}\otimes K_{m}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B},

(109)

\displaystyle S=\lambda^{\prime}\mathds{1}_{y}\otimes\mathds{1}_{BC}+\sum_{j}O^{\prime}_{j}\otimes Q^{\prime}_{j}+\sum_{n}D_{n}^{\prime}\otimes K^{\prime}_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{C},

(110)

where
\lambda=\frac{1}{d_{x}d_{B}\lambda_{x}}
,
\lambda^{\prime}=\frac{1}{d_{y}d_{C}\lambda_{y}}
, and

\displaystyle O_{i}\in\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{x}),\quad Q_{i}\in\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B}),\quad D_{m}\in\overline{\Delta}_{x},\quad K_{m}\in\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),

(111)

\displaystyle O^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{y}),\quad Q^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{C}),\quad D^{\prime}_{n}\in\overline{\Delta}_{y},\quad K^{\prime}_{n}\in\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{B}).

(112)

A direct evaluation of the link product yields

\displaystyle R*S

\displaystyle=\lambda\lambda^{\prime}d_{B}\,\mathds{1}_{x}\otimes\mathds{1}_{y}\otimes\mathds{1}_{AC}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i,j}O_{i}\otimes O^{\prime}_{j}\otimes(Q_{i}*Q^{\prime}_{j})+\sum_{j}\mathds{1}_{x}\otimes O^{\prime}_{j}\otimes\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[Q^{\prime}_{j}]+\sum_{m,j}D_{m}\otimes O^{\prime}_{j}\otimes K_{m}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[Q^{\prime}_{j}]

\displaystyle\quad+\Bigl(\lambda\sum_{n}\operatorname{Tr}[K^{\prime}_{n}]\,\mathds{1}_{x}\otimes D^{\prime}_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{A}+\lambda^{\prime}d_{B}\sum_{m}D_{m}\otimes\mathds{1}_{y}\otimes K_{m}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\sum_{m,n}\operatorname{Tr}[K^{\prime}_{n}]\,D_{m}\otimes D^{\prime}_{n}\otimes K_{m}+\sum_{i,n}O_{i}\otimes D^{\prime}_{n}\otimes(Q_{i}*K^{\prime}_{n})\Bigr)\otimes\mathds{1}_{C}.

(113)

By Theorem
IV.2
, an operator
W\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{AC})
belongs to

\mathsf{T}_{1}((x\otimes y)\to(A\to C))
if and only if it is positive and can be written as

W=\tilde{\lambda}\,\mathds{1}_{x}\otimes\mathds{1}_{y}\otimes\mathds{1}_{AC}+X,\qquad\tilde{\lambda}=\frac{1}{d_{x}d_{y}d_{C}\lambda_{x}\lambda_{y}},\qquad X\in\Delta_{(x\otimes y)\to(A\to C)},

where

\Delta_{(x\otimes y)\to(A\to C)}=\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{x})\otimes\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{y})\otimes\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{C})\;\oplus\;\overline{\Delta}_{x\otimes y}\otimes\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{C}.

(114)

Comparing it with (
113
), we conclude that

R*S\in\mathsf{T}_{1}\!\bigl((x\otimes y)\to(A\to C)\bigr),

which proves the claim.

\,\blacksquare

Recalling Equations (
34
) and (
35
), we can now show that a network of higher-order maps defines an admissible composition, and therefore an admissible event. Indeed, each link product appearing in Equation (
34
) involves operators

R\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(\overline{x_{i}}\to(E_{i-1}\to E_{i})),\qquad S\in\mathsf{T}_{1}(\overline{x_{i+1}}\to(E_{i}\to E_{i+1})),

for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
. By Lemma
D.1
, each such link product is an admissible composition. Applying Lemma

iteratively to all links in the network therefore proves that the entire composition is admissible.

Quantum

A bi-slot
n

R_{n}=\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{k=1}^{n}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{F}_{k}|,

(122)

Hence, a bistochastic but non-ordinary process matrix must contain traceless components in the difference

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathtt{BSP}_{2}\setminus\mathtt{P}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{P})\otimes\Bigl(\mathsf{I}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{2}})\oplus\mathsf{I}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{A_{2}B_{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\oplus\mathsf{I}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{A_{2}}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{2}})

\displaystyle\quad\oplus\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{A_{2}}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{2}})

\displaystyle\quad\oplus\mathsf{I}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{2}})\Bigr)\otimes\mathsf{I}_{F}.

F.1
Flippable quantum
SWITCH

Definition F.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}),\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}))
and
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}}),\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{2}}))
be completely positive maps with Kraus operators
\{A_{i}\}_{i}
and
\{B_{j}\}_{j}
, respectively. The flippable quantum
SWITCH
is a supermap transforming the set
\{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\}
into an operation
\mathcal{FS}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{P_{t}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{P_{c}}),\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{F_{t}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{F_{c}}))
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{FS}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})[\rho]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}K_{ij}(\rho\otimes\omega)K_{ij}^{\dagger},

(124)

\displaystyle K_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{j}A_{i}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|+(B_{j}A_{i})^{T}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|,

(125)

where
\mathcal{H}_{P_{t}}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{A_{2}}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{B_{2}}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{F_{t}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{P_{c}}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{F_{c}}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}
,
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{P_{t}})
and
\omega\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{P_{c}})
, with indices
t
and
c
corresponding to target and control systems, respectively.

A straightforward calculation shows that the flippable quantum
SWITCH
is defined by the Choi operator

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{FS}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{FS}|,

(126)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{FS}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle_{P_{t}A_{1}}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle_{B_{1}A_{2}}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle_{B_{2}F_{t}}\otimes|0\rangle_{P_{c}}\otimes|0\rangle_{F_{c}}

\displaystyle\qquad+|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle_{P_{t}B_{2}}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle_{A_{2}B_{1}}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle_{A_{1}F_{t}}\otimes|1\rangle_{P_{c}}\otimes|1\rangle_{F_{c}},

(127)

which can be expanded as follows:

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{FS}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{FS}|

(128)

\displaystyle=|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{P_{t}A_{1}}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{B_{1}A_{2}}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{B_{2}F_{t}}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{P_{c}}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{F_{c}}

(129)

\displaystyle\quad+|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{P_{t}B_{2}}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{A_{2}B_{1}}\otimes|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{A_{1}F_{t}}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|_{P_{c}}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|_{F_{c}}

(130)

\displaystyle\quad+\ldots,

(131)

where
\ldots
denotes the off-diagonal terms in the control subsystems
P_{c}
and
F_{c}
. These off-diagonal terms are traceless on
F_{c}
and therefore belong to the subspace

\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{PA_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}})\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{F})\subsetneq\Delta_{\mathtt{BSP}_{2}}.

(132)

Positivity of
R
is immediate from its definition as a rank-one operator,

R=|\mathcal{FS}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathcal{FS}|\geq 0.

(133)

Moreover,

\lambda_{R}=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[R]}{\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{H}_{PA_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}F})}=\frac{\langle\!\langle\mathcal{FS}|\mathcal{FS}\rangle\!\rangle}{\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{H}_{PA_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}F})}=\frac{1}{2d^{3}},

(134)

where the identity
\mathrm{Tr}(|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{XY})=d_{X}
is used for each maximally entangled pair. Hence,
\lambda_{R}
agrees with the general form (
118
) for the given local dimensions.

To relate
R
to the structure of
\Delta_{\mathtt{BSP}_{2}}
, we use the standard decomposition

|\mathds{1}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\mathds{1}|_{XY}=\frac{1}{d}\mathds{1}_{XY}+\sum_{\mu=1}^{d^{2}-1}F_{\mu,X}\otimes F^{*}_{\mu,Y},

(135)

where each
F_{\mu,X}\in\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{X})
is traceless. Inserting this decomposition into each maximally entangled link and expanding
R
, we now focus on those contributions
R^{(0)}
whose local factor on
F=F_{t}F_{c}
is proportional to the identity operator
\mathds{1}_{F}
. The result is

\displaystyle R^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2d^{3}}\mathds{1}_{P_{t}A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}F_{t}P_{c}F_{c}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\sum_{\mu}\Bigl(F_{\mu,P_{t}}\otimes F_{\mu,A_{1}}^{*}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}F_{t}}+2\mathds{1}_{P_{t}A_{1}}\otimes F_{\mu,B_{1}}\otimes F_{\mu,A_{2}}^{*}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B_{2}F_{t}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+F_{\mu,P_{t}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}}\otimes F_{\mu,B_{2}}^{*}\otimes\mathds{1}_{F_{t}}\Bigr)\otimes\mathds{1}_{P_{c}F_{c}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2d}\sum_{\mu,\mu^{\prime}}\Bigl(F_{\mu,P_{t}}\otimes F_{\mu,A_{1}}^{*}\otimes F_{\mu^{\prime},B_{1}}\otimes F_{\mu^{\prime},A_{2}}^{*}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B_{2}F_{t}P_{c}F_{c}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+F_{\mu,P_{t}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{A_{1}}\otimes F_{\mu,B_{1}}\otimes F_{\mu^{\prime},A_{2}}^{*}\otimes F_{\mu^{\prime},B_{2}}^{*}\otimes\mathds{1}_{F_{t}P_{c}F_{c}}\Bigr).

(136)

No term in
R^{(0)}
matches (
120
), so the flippable quantum
SWITCH
belongs to the class of bistochastic process matrices.

Finally, one finds explicit traceless contributions forbidden by ordinary process matrices. For example,

\frac{1}{2d^{2}}F_{\mu,P_{t}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}}\otimes F_{\mu,B_{2}}^{*}\otimes\mathds{1}_{F_{t}P_{c}F_{c}}\in\mathsf{Hrm}(\mathcal{H}_{P})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{2}})\otimes\mathsf{I}_{F},

(137)

as in (
123
). Therefore, the flippable quantum
SWITCH
is a valid bistochastic process matrix of type
\mathtt{BSP}_{2}
, but it cannot be implemented within ordinary process matrices.

F.2

LC processes are higher-order operations that transform two maps into unit probability
46

\mathtt{BSP}_{2}^{P,F=I}=\overline{(\hat{A}_{1}\rightarrow\hat{B}_{1})\otimes(\hat{A}_{2}\rightarrow\hat{B}_{2})}.

For

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\mathds{1}_{A_{1}B_{1}}+Y_{A_{1}}\otimes Y_{B_{1}},

\displaystyle F_{4}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d-1}{d^{2}}\mathds{1}_{A_{2}B_{2}}-X_{A_{2}}\otimes X_{B_{2}},

\displaystyle E_{5}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d-2}{d^{2}}\mathds{1}_{A_{1}B_{1}}-Y_{A_{1}}\otimes X_{B_{1}}-Y_{A_{1}}\otimes Y_{B_{1}},

\displaystyle F_{5}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d-2}{d^{2}}\mathds{1}_{A_{2}B_{2}}-X_{A_{2}}\otimes X_{B_{2}}-X_{A_{2}}\otimes Y_{B_{2}}.

A direct substitution yields

\sum_{k=1}^{5}E_{k}^{(0)}\otimes F_{k}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\,\mathds{1}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}},

(170)

thus establishing the required form:

R=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\mathds{1}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}+X_{R},\qquad X_{R}\in\overline{\Delta}_{(\hat{A}_{1}\to\hat{B}_{1})\otimes(\hat{A}_{2}\to\hat{B}_{2})}.

(171)

Finally, one finds explicit traceless contributions forbidden by ordinary process matrices, e.g.

\frac{1}{d^{2}}\mathds{1}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}}\otimes(X_{B_{2}}+Y_{B_{2}})\in\mathsf{I}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}}\otimes\mathsf{Trl}(\mathcal{H}_{B_{2}}),

(172)

which lies in
\Delta_{\mathtt{BSP}_{2}^{P,F=I}\setminus\mathtt{P}_{2}^{P,F=I}}
. Thus, the LC
(2,2,?)
process is a valid bistochastic process matrix but cannot arise as an ordinary process matrix.
