High-flow nasal cannula oxygen versus Venturi facemask in women with cardiovascular disease undergoing cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xiaorui Zhou, He Jing, Jiakai Lu, Jinglan Zhang, Dawei Zhang, Yuqiu Lu, Peirong Lin, Shuwen Li, Mu Jin, Sheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen with cardiovascular disease undergoing cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia are vulnerable to perioperative hypoxemia, particularly during sedation after fetal delivery. We compared high-flow nasal cannula oxygen (HFNC) with Venturi facemask oxygen in this setting.MethodsIn this prospective single-blind randomized controlled trial, 44 women with cardiovascular disease undergoing elective cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia were randomly assigned to HFNC [40 L/min, fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) 0.40] or Venturi facemask oxygen (10 L/min, FiO2 0.40). Remimazolam sedation was initiated 5 min after fetal delivery. The primary outcome was maternal arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) measured 10 min after achievement of the target sedation level. Secondary outcomes included perioperative oxygenation and ventilation variables, hemodynamic variables, airway or oxygenation-related interventions, maternal recovery outcomes, neonatal outcomes, postoperative biomarkers, and tolerance to the oxygen delivery device. Between-group comparisons were performed using appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests, and effect estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsAll 44 randomized participants were included in the final analysis. During sedation, PaO2 was significantly higher in the HFNC group than in the facemask group (177.3±40.9 vs. 137.5±37.6 mmHg), with an adjusted mean difference of 48.8 mmHg (95% CI: 29.8 to 67.8; P<0.001). Minimum intraoperative peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) was also higher with HFNC [99% (98–99%) vs. 98% (96–98%); Hodges-Lehmann difference 1.0%, 95% CI: 0.0–2.0%; P=0.02]. Arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) during sedation did not differ significantly between groups. The incidence of hypotension, vasoactive drug use, airway interventions, postoperative organ injury markers, oxygen-interface tolerance scores, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and neonatal outcomes were similar. Postoperative B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) was numerically lower in the HFNC group after baseline adjustment, but the difference was not statistically significant [geometric mean ratio 0.67, 95% CI: 0.42–1.08; P=0.10].ConclusionsIn women with cardiovascular disease undergoing cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia, HFNC significantly improved maternal PaO2 during remimazolam sedation after fetal delivery compared with Venturi facemask oxygen. This physiological improvement was not accompanied by statistically significant reductions in airway interventions, maternal complications, ICU admission, or adverse neonatal outcomes. Larger studies powered for clinical outcomes are needed.Trial RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2400087925.Keywords: High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), cesarean delivery, cardiovascular disease, neuraxial anesthesia, remimazolam

Highlight box

Key findings

• High-flow nasal cannula improved maternal arterial oxygenation during remimazolam sedation after fetal delivery in women with cardiovascular disease undergoing cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia.

• Minimum intraoperative oxygen saturation was also higher with high-flow nasal cannula.

• Maternal adverse events, neonatal outcomes, and tolerance to the oxygen delivery device were similar between groups.

What is known and what is new?

• Women with cardiovascular disease are particularly vulnerable to hypoxemia during cesarean delivery, especially during sedation after fetal delivery. Conventional facemask oxygen increases inspired oxygen concentration but provides limited respiratory support.

• This randomized trial provides evidence that high-flow nasal cannula improves maternal arterial oxygenation, a physiological endpoint, in this high-risk obstetric setting.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• High-flow nasal cannula may be considered as a perioperative oxygen delivery option for selected high-risk obstetric patients requiring sedation after fetal delivery under neuraxial anesthesia, while its effect on patient-centered clinical outcomes remains uncertain.

• The findings support further multicenter studies to evaluate whether specific cardiac subgroups may derive greater oxygenation benefit, and to clarify the broader clinical role of high-flow nasal cannula for perioperative oxygen delivery in obstetric anesthesia.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease complicating pregnancy has become the leading non-obstetric cause of maternal death, and perioperative safety during cesarean delivery in this population is a major concern in obstetric anesthesia (1). In late pregnancy, maternal oxygen consumption increases by approximately 20% to 30% compared with the non-pregnant state. In women with severe cardiovascular disease, particularly those with pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, or right-to-left shunting, tolerance of hypoxemia is further reduced, making optimization of oxygenation an important measure to prevent acute perioperative cardiopulmonary decompensation (2,3). If indicated after fetal delivery for patient comfort or inadequate neuraxial blockade, moderate sedation may attenuate pain- or anxiety-related sympathetic activation and help avoid abrupt increases in heart rate and blood pressure (2). However, reduced functional residual capacity and airway edema in late pregnancy limit oxygen reserve, while sedation-induced relaxation of the pharyngeal musculature may promote upper airway collapse and severe hypoxemia (4). Conventional facemask oxygen therapy can only passively increase inspired oxygen concentration and may be inadequate when sedation depresses respiratory drive, especially in the presence of obesity or obstructive sleep apnoea. An oxygenation strategy that can maintain airway patency while providing effective respiratory support during sedation is therefore desirable in pregnant women with cardiovascular disease.

High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen therapy has emerged as a non-invasive respiratory support technique of increasing clinical interest. In addition to delivering a precise high oxygen flow, HFNC can wash out anatomical dead space and generate a flow-dependent low level of positive airway pressure, with potential to improve oxygenation and reduce the work of breathing in high-risk non-pregnant patients (5,6). However, direct clinical evidence for HFNC in pregnant women with cardiovascular disease remains limited. Whether HFNC provides more reliable oxygenation than conventional oxygen therapy during cesarean delivery, particularly during moderate sedation under neuraxial anesthesia, remains uncertain. Likewise, its potential hemodynamic effects in this vulnerable population have not been systematically evaluated.

We therefore conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial to compare the efficacy and safety of HFNC with Venturi facemask oxygen in pregnant women with cardiovascular disease undergoing cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia with moderate sedation after fetal delivery. We hypothesized that HFNC would improve maternal arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) during sedation after fetal delivery. Secondary outcomes included perioperative oxygenation and ventilation variables, hemodynamic variables, airway or oxygenation-related interventions, maternal recovery outcomes, neonatal outcomes, postoperative biomarkers, and tolerance to the oxygen delivery device. We present this article in accordance with the CONSORT reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0911/rc) (7).

Methods

Ethics and registration

This prospective, single-blind, parallel-group, superiority randomized controlled trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Ethical approval was granted by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. KS2024006), and the trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400087925; registered on 7 August 2024; registry record available at https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=230504). No separate statistical analysis plan was publicly available before trial commencement. There were no important changes to the trial protocol, prespecified outcomes, or analyses after trial commencement. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

Women were recruited at Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University, between September and December 2024. Eligible participants were aged >18 years, had cardiovascular disease confirmed by echocardiography or specialist assessment, and were scheduled for elective cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia. Diagnoses included congenital heart disease, valvular heart disease, peripartum cardiomyopathy, ischaemic heart disease, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and severe pre-eclampsia with cardiac involvement. Only women with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status II or III were included.

Exclusion criteria were multiple pregnancy, planned concomitant cardiovascular surgery, conditions precluding HFNC use (severe nasal obstruction, facial trauma, or recent upper airway surgery), allergy to remimazolam, and refusal to participate. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this trial.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were assigned in a 1:1 ratio to HFNC oxygen therapy (HFNC group) or Venturi facemask oxygen therapy (facemask group). Simple randomization without blocking or stratification was chosen to maintain procedural simplicity and allocation concealment in this single-center trial. The allocation sequence was computer-generated by an independent statistician and concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes held by an independent researcher. Envelopes were opened only after baseline measurements had been recorded in the operating theatre. Participants were enrolled by one of the attending anesthetists, who had no access to the random allocation sequence; group assignment was implemented by the independent researcher who opened the sealed envelope.

Blinding of patients and anesthetists was not feasible because of the obvious differences between devices. Investigators responsible for data collection, follow-up, and statistical analysis remained blinded to group allocation.

Study protocol

On arrival in the operating theatre, electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, and invasive arterial pressure monitoring were established. A radial arterial catheter was inserted under local anesthesia for continuous blood pressure monitoring and arterial blood sampling, and peripheral venous access was obtained.

Neuraxial anesthesia was performed at the L2–3 or L3–4 interspace by one of two senior obstetric anesthetists using single-shot spinal, combined spinal-epidural, or continuous epidural anesthesia. For spinal or combined spinal-epidural anesthesia, 0.5% bupivacaine was injected slowly; for continuous epidural anesthesia, 1% ropivacaine was administered incrementally. The sensory block was maintained between T4 and T6. Blood pressure and heart rate were managed within 20% of baseline where possible, with norepinephrine or dopamine given when required. All cesarean deliveries were performed by the same senior obstetric team.

Allocated oxygen therapy was started immediately after neuraxial anesthesia and supine positioning and continued until the end of surgery. In the HFNC group, warmed humidified oxygen was delivered at 40 L/min, fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) 0.40, and 31 ℃. In the facemask group, oxygen was delivered via a Venturi mask at 10 L/min with FiO2 0.40.

Five minutes after fetal delivery, remimazolam sedation was started with an intravenous loading dose of 0.05 mg/kg followed by infusion at 0.3 mg/kg/h (8,9). Sedation depth was assessed using the Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation scale, targeting a score ≤3. If necessary, 1-mg rescue boluses were given. If hypoxemia occurred during sedation (SpO2 <94%) (10), rescue measures included verbal stimulation, jaw thrust, positive-pressure ventilation with 100% oxygen via a tight-fitting facemask, and intravenous flumazenil if required.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was maternal PaO2 at T4, defined as 10 min after achievement of the target sedation level following remimazolam administration 5 min after fetal delivery.

Secondary outcomes included perioperative oxygenation and ventilation variables, hemodynamic variables, airway or oxygenation-related interventions, maternal recovery outcomes, neonatal outcomes, postoperative biomarkers, and tolerance to the oxygen delivery device. Arterial blood gas analysis was performed at four predefined time points: T1, baseline after radial arterial cannulation while breathing room air; T2, 5 min after oxygen administration in the supine position following neuraxial anesthesia; T3, 5 min after fetal delivery; and T4, 10 min after attainment of the target sedation level. PaO2, PaCO2, and lactate were recorded. Intraoperative hypoxemia and airway rescue interventions were also documented.

Heart rate and mean arterial pressure were recorded at T1–T4. Hypotension, hypertension, bradycardia, tachycardia, myocardial ischaemia, arrhythmia, nausea, vomiting, and intraoperative vasoactive drug use were recorded. On postoperative day 1, venous blood was sampled for B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), high-sensitivity cardiac troponin I (hsTnI), serum creatinine, alanine aminotransferase, and aspartate aminotransferase. Major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) within 7 postoperative days were recorded (11), together with intensive care unit (ICU) admission and postoperative hospital stay.

Umbilical arterial and venous blood samples were obtained immediately after cord clamping for blood gas analysis, and Apgar scores were recorded at 1, 5, and 10 min. Tolerance to the oxygen delivery device was assessed at the end of surgery using a 5-point Likert scale, with lower scores indicating greater comfort with the oxygen interface (12).

Sample size and statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome, PaO2 at T4. According to internal preliminary observations, a between-group difference of approximately 40 mmHg with a common standard deviation of 45 mmHg was assumed for PaO2 at FiO2 40%. Using a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and a power of 0.80, 21 participants were required in each group (G*Power 3.1.9.7). Allowing for an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 5%, we planned to enroll 44 participants.

All analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle. Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation) or median [interquartile range], and categorical variables as number (%). The primary outcome and selected continuous secondary outcomes were analyzed using analysis of covariance adjusted for the corresponding baseline value. Postoperative BNP was log-transformed before analysis. Other continuous variables were analyzed using the independent-samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. All tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. No interim analyses or formal stopping guidelines were planned. To address potential confounding related to the numerical imbalance in cardiac diagnostic categories, we performed a post hoc exploratory adjusted analysis for the primary outcome using a linear regression model. Treatment group was included as the main exposure, and baseline PaO2 and major cardiac diagnostic category were included as covariates. Because of the small sample size and sparse diagnostic categories, cardiac diagnoses were collapsed into three clinically interpretable categories: congenital heart disease, cardiomyopathy, and valvular/other cardiovascular disease. This analysis was considered exploratory.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 54 women were assessed for eligibility, of whom 10 were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria or declined participation. Forty-four women were randomly assigned to the HFNC group (n=22) or the facemask group (n=22). All randomized participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Recruitment ended after the planned sample size had been reached; the trial was not stopped early.

Primary outcome: oxygenation and metabolic variables during sedation

Ten minutes after the target depth of sedation had been achieved [Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score ≤3; T4], PaO2 was significantly higher in the HFNC group than in the facemask group (Table 2). There was no significant between-group difference in arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) at T4 (P=0.66). Lactate at T4 was slightly higher in the HFNC group than in the facemask group (P=0.03), although absolute values remained within the normal physiological range in both groups (Table 2).

Secondary outcomes: intraoperative management, safety, and tolerance to the oxygen delivery device

The minimum intraoperative SpO2 was significantly higher in the HFNC group than in the facemask group (P=0.02; Table 3 and Figure S1). Temporal trends in heart rate and mean arterial pressure were similar in the two groups throughout surgery and sedation, with no significant between-group differences at any time point (Figure S2).

The incidence of intraoperative hypotension was 50.0% (11/22) in the HFNC group and 40.9% (9/22) in the facemask group, with no significant difference between groups (P=0.76). There were no significant between-group differences in vasoactive drug use, perioperative adverse events, or oxygen-interface tolerance scores (all P>0.05; Table 3).

Postoperative recovery and neonatal outcomes

Postoperative markers of organ injury are shown in Table 4. After adjustment for baseline values, postoperative BNP was numerically lower in the HFNC group than in the facemask group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (geometric mean ratio 0.67, 95% CI: 0.42 to 1.08; P=0.10). There were no significant between-group differences in postoperative creatinine, or in perioperative changes in hsTnI, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (all P>0.05).

With regard to clinical recovery, ICU admission, ICU length of stay, and total postoperative hospital stay did not differ significantly between groups (all P>0.05). Neonatal outcomes were also similar, with no significant between-group differences in umbilical arterial or venous blood gas values, or in Apgar scores at 1, 5, and 10 min (all P>0.05; Table 4).

Discussion

The main finding was that, in pregnant women with cardiovascular disease undergoing cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia, HFNC significantly increased maternal PaO2 during remimazolam sedation after fetal delivery compared with Venturi facemask oxygen. This finding should be interpreted as improvement in a physiological oxygenation endpoint rather than evidence of improved patient-centered outcomes. Airway interventions, maternal complications, ICU admission, and neonatal outcomes did not differ significantly between groups.

The oxygenation benefit of HFNC was not evident throughout the whole perioperative period, but emerged specifically at T4, after fetal delivery and during deeper sedation. This temporal pattern is physiologically plausible. Because the cohort included women with different cardiovascular diagnoses, including shunt lesions and ischaemic heart disease, preoperative ward SpO2 values varied. However, once patients arrived in theatre, baseline PaO2 differed much less, probably reflecting compensatory hyperventilation and the steep portion of the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve. After adjustment for baseline values, no between-group difference in PaO2 was seen at T2 or T3, suggesting that while patients were awake and maintaining spontaneous compensatory ventilation, facemask oxygen therapy was generally adequate. The difference became apparent only at T4, when remimazolam had produced mild respiratory depression and late-pregnancy supine positioning had further reduced functional residual capacity. At that point, the adjusted mean difference in PaO2 was 48.81 mmHg in favor of HFNC. This finding is consistent with the recognised physiological actions of HFNC (5). High flow can reduce entrainment of room air, wash out upper airway dead space, and generate low-level positive airway pressure. In late pregnancy, these effects may be particularly relevant because airway edema, reduced functional residual capacity, and sedation-related loss of pharyngeal tone all favour upper airway collapse and ventilation-perfusion mismatch. Previous studies in procedural sedation have similarly shown that HFNC increases arterial oxygen reserve towards the end of sedation (13,14). Our results extend that observation to high-risk cesarean delivery in women with cardiovascular disease and suggest that the value of HFNC lies mainly in periods of peak physiological vulnerability rather than throughout the entire procedure.

Postoperative biomarkers showed no statistically significant between-group differences. Within this biomarker panel, BNP is most directly related to ventricular wall stress and cardiac loading. The point estimate for postoperative BNP was numerically 33% lower in the HFNC group after adjustment for baseline values, but the between-group difference was not statistically significant (geometric mean ratio, 0.67; 95% CI: 0.42 to 1.08; P=0.10). Therefore, the present data do not establish a hemodynamic benefit of HFNC. This finding may nevertheless be worth noting as an exploratory signal. HFNC can generate low-level positive airway pressure, which could theoretically influence venous return, ventricular loading conditions, and myocardial wall stress (15-17). These mechanisms have mainly been discussed in non-obstetric populations receiving HFNC or other forms of respiratory support (18,19), and cannot be directly extrapolated to pregnant women with heterogeneous cardiovascular disease. In the present trial, HFNC was applied only intraoperatively for a short period, and respiratory mechanics, cardiac output, ventricular filling pressures, and echocardiographic indices were not directly measured. Accordingly, the BNP finding should be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken as evidence that HFNC reduces cardiac workload.

Sedation after fetal delivery may be useful when needed for patient comfort or inadequate neuraxial blockade (20). However, it may also increase the risk of hypoventilation and upper airway obstruction. This risk is amplified in late pregnancy by reduced functional residual capacity, increased oxygen consumption, airway edema, supine positioning, and sedative-related loss of pharyngeal tone (21). These factors may lead to rapid oxygen desaturation during sedation. HFNC can reduce entrainment of room air, wash out upper airway dead space, and generate low-level positive airway pressure (22). These effects may explain the higher PaO2 observed during sedation. However, they should not be interpreted as evidence that HFNC reduced clinically important airway events. In this trial, airway interventions were infrequent and did not differ significantly between groups. Jaw thrust was less frequent in the HFNC group, but the number of events was small and the difference was not statistically significant. Therefore, our data support improved arterial oxygenation with HFNC, rather than a demonstrated reduction in airway rescue requirements.

Respiratory support during cesarean delivery sedation must also be considered in the context of regurgitation risk and patient tolerance. In late pregnancy, increased intra-abdominal pressure and delayed gastric emptying increase the risk of reflux and aspiration. In the present study, intraoperative nausea and vomiting were uncommon in both groups (0% vs. 4.5%), markedly lower than the approximately 36% reported previously (23). This may reflect several factors. Remimazolam may have intrinsic antiemetic properties through GABA receptor agonism and reduced neurotransmitter release in the chemoreceptor trigger zone (24). HFNC, as an open system, is unlikely to increase gastric insufflation because the airway pressure it generates remains well below the threshold required to overcome lower oesophageal sphincter resistance (25). Careful perioperative management, including avoidance of hypotension and restrained use of oxytocin, may also have contributed (23). Importantly, despite continuous gas delivery at 40 L/min, oxygen-interface tolerance scores were similar between groups, suggesting that the heated humidified system offset the discomfort usually associated with high flow.

With respect to fetal safety, no signal of adverse neonatal effect was observed in this small trial. Umbilical arterial and venous blood gas values and Apgar scores were similar between groups. Although lactate at T4 was slightly higher in the HFNC group than in the facemask group, the absolute values in both groups remained within the normal physiological range, making a clinically meaningful difference in tissue perfusion unlikely.

Several limitations should be considered. First, this single-center trial was powered for PaO2 at T4, a physiological endpoint, and not for less frequent but clinically important outcomes such as airway interventions, cardiovascular complications, ICU admission, or neonatal morbidity. Therefore, the absence of significant differences in these outcomes should be interpreted cautiously rather than as evidence of equivalence. Second, simple randomization without blocking or stratification may have contributed to numerical imbalances in cardiac diagnostic categories in this small heterogeneous cohort. Although an exploratory adjusted analysis accounting for major diagnostic category yielded results consistent with the primary analysis, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Third, respiratory mechanics and detailed hemodynamic variables were not directly measured; thus, mechanistic explanations regarding airway patency, positive airway pressure, or cardiac loading conditions remain inferential. Finally, because the study included selected women undergoing elective cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia with remimazolam sedation after fetal delivery, the findings may not apply to emergency procedures, general anesthesia, or other sedation strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, among women with cardiovascular disease undergoing cesarean delivery under neuraxial anesthesia, HFNC improved maternal PaO2 during remimazolam sedation after fetal delivery compared with Venturi facemask oxygen therapy. However, this physiological improvement was not accompanied by statistically significant reductions in airway interventions, maternal complications, ICU admission, or adverse neonatal outcomes. HFNC may be a feasible oxygen delivery option in selected high-risk obstetric patients, but larger studies powered for clinical outcomes are needed to define its clinical role.
