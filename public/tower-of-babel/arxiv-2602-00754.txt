Hardness Condensation for Decision Tree Measures by Restrictions
Chandrima Kayal, Rajat Mittal, Manaswi Paraashar, Nitin Saurabh

Abstract
For any Boolean function f : { 0 , 1 } n → { 0 , 1 } f:\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{0,1\right\} with a complexity measure having value k ≪ n k\ll n , is it possible to restrict the function f f to Θ ⁡ ( k ) \Theta(k) variables while keeping the complexity preserved at Θ ⁡ ( k ) \Theta(k) ? This question, in the context of query complexity, was recently studied by Göös, Newman, Riazanov and Sokolov (STOC 2024). They showed, among other results, that query complexity can not be condensed losslessly. They asked if complexity measures like block sensitivity or unambiguous certificate complexity can be condensed losslessly? In this work, we show that decision tree measures like block sensitivity and certificate complexity, cannot be condensed losslessly. That is, there exists a Boolean function f f such that any restriction of f f to O ⁡ ( ℳ ⁡ ( f ) ) O(\mathcal{M}(f)) variables has ℳ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathcal{M}(\cdot) -complexity at most O ~ ​ ( ℳ ​ ( f ) 2 / 3 ) \widetilde{O}(\mathcal{M}(f)^{2/3}) , where ℳ ∈ { bs , fbs , C , 𝖣 } \mathcal{M}\in\{\textnormal{bs},\textnormal{fbs},\textnormal{C},\mathsf{D}\} . This also improves upon a result of Göös, Newman, Riazanov and Sokolov (STOC 2024). We also complement the negative results on lossless condensation with positive results about lossy condensation. In particular, we show that for every Boolean function f f there exists a restriction of f f to O ⁡ ( ℳ ⁡ ( f ) ) O(\mathcal{M}(f)) variables such that its ℳ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathcal{M}(\cdot) -complexity is at least Ω ⁡ ( ℳ ​ ( f ) 1 / 2 ) \Omega(\mathcal{M}(f)^{1/2}) , where ℳ ∈ { bs , fbs , C , 𝖴𝖢 𝗆𝗂𝗇 , 𝖴𝖢 1 , 𝖴𝖢 , 𝖣 , deg ~ , λ } \mathcal{M}\in\{\textnormal{bs},\textnormal{fbs},\textnormal{C},\mathsf{UC_{min}},\mathsf{UC}_{1},\mathsf{UC},\mathsf{D},\widetilde{\deg},\lambda\} . We also show a slightly weaker positive result for randomized and quantum query complexity.

Introduction

The problem of proving circuit lower bounds is one of the most challenging problems in computational complexity theory. Buresh-Oppenheim and Santhanam
[
8
]
introduced the concept of
hardness condensation
as a way to prove better circuit lower bounds. Their idea was to design a procedure that takes as input a hard to compute function
f
and outputs another function
f^{\prime}
, possibly on fewer input bits, such that the hardness of
f^{\prime}
with respect to its input length is better than
f
. In particular, they showed hardness condensation for the complexity measure circuit-size. A decade later this strategy was successfully implemented by Razborov
[
21
]
to establish strong lower bounds in proof complexity. Since then the basic technique of hardness condensation has found many applications in proof complexity
[
22
,
23
,
6
,
12
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
.

Clearly, the paradigm of hardness condensation is quite general and one could ask the following question for any complexity measure
\mathcal{M}(\cdot)\colon

Given a function
f
on
n
variables with complexity
\mathcal{M}(f)=\lambda(n)
, can we transform
f
, in some explicit manner, into another function
f^{\prime}
on a smaller number of variables
t
such that
\mathcal{M}(f^{\prime})=\Theta(\mathcal{M}(f))
while also being maximal over all functions on
t
variables?

Indeed, the aforementioned question has also been explored recently in the context of communication complexity and matrix ranks
[
14
,
15
,
13
]
, and query complexity
[
13
]
.
Now hardness condensation can be done in different ways depending upon the model of computation, or the problem at hand, or the application in mind. Typically to reduce the number of variables one does
variable substitutions
and/or composes the variables of
f
with
small gadgets
(e.g., XOR, OR, Indexing).
The recent work of Göös, Newman, Riazanov and Sokolov
[
13
]
explored whether hardness condensation can also be achieved using the simplest possible transformation of
restricting
variables by constants.

In this work, we further explore the phenomenon of hardness condensation in the setting of query complexity using restrictions.
For
f\colon\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{0,1\right\}
, the query complexity
\mathsf{D}(f)
of

is the minimum number of queries made by a deterministic decision tree algorithm

in the worst-case.

Following
[
13

, we say that

condenses
to
k

\rho\colon[n]\to\left\{0,1,*\right\}
with
|\rho^{-1}(\ast)|=k
, such that the restricted function
f|_{\rho}
on
k
variables has large query complexity, i.e.,
\mathsf{D}(f|_{\rho})=\Theta(k)
. We further say that

condenses

if it condenses to
\Theta(\mathsf{D}(f))
variables.

The authors of
[
13
]
studied the question whether every

can be condensed losslessly. They answered it in the negative. That is, they showed a function

such that for every restriction
\rho
that fixes all but at most

of the restricted function

is
\widetilde{O}(\mathsf{D}(f)^{3/4})
(where
\widetilde{O}

\mathsf{D}(f)
).
In other words, deterministic (and even randomized) query complexity cannot be condensed losslessly in general. The authors wondered if there is a function that witnesses greater loss, i.e., for which the hardness is even less condensible.
Observe that the best possible bound cannot be better than
O(\mathsf{D}(f)^{1/3})
.
It follows from the fact that every

condenses to
\deg(f)
variables

In

In this work, we initiate the study of hardness condensation for many other decision tree measures. In particular, we
show that lossless condensation cannot be achieved for

We also improve upon the bound of

1.1
Our Results

We will be dealing with many complexity measures in this work, so we need to specialize the definition of condensation as follows.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be any complexity measure like sensitivity, block sensitivity, certificate complexity, query complexity, unambiguous certificate complexity, etc.
We say that a Boolean function

\rho\colon[n]\to\left\{0,1,*\right\}

\Theta(\mathcal{M}(f))

condenses losslessly if every function

\mathcal{M}

Observe that sensitivity and degree are two such measures that condenses losslessly.
It was shown in

that deterministic query complexity cannot be condensed losslessly.

Our first result (proved in Section

We now define the query model of computation. In this model, the input bits can be accessed by queries to an input oracle and the complexity of computing a Boolean function

Definition 11

(Deterministic Decision Trees)
.

Let
f\colon\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{0,1\right\}
be a Boolean function. Let
A
be a deterministic algorithm that computes

\text{cost}(A)=\max_{x}\text{cost}(A,x)

\min_{A}\{\text{cost}(A)\}

We note the following easy to observe relations.

Fact 2.1
.

\deg(f)\leq\mathsf{UC_{min}}(f)\leq\mathsf{UC}_{1}(f)\leq\mathsf{UC}(f)\leq\mathsf{D}(f)

\textnormal{bs}(f)\leq\textnormal{C}(f)\leq\mathsf{UC}(f)

We now define randomized decision trees which, in short, is just a probability distribution over deterministic decision trees.

Definition 12

.

A randomized query algorithm

We will also consider a

We now finish the last, but the most important, task of showing that indeed the decision tree complexity of the restricted function reduces; thereby finishing the counterexample for the hardness condensation of decision tree complexity.

Lemma 36
.

For every partial assignment
\rho
to the variables of
f_{\textup{CS}}
with
|\rho^{-1}(\ast)|=O(\mathsf{D}(f_{\textup{CS}}))=O(k^{1.5}\log k)
, we have
\mathsf{D}(f_{\textup{CS}}|_{\rho})=O(k\log^{2}k)
.

Proof.

Fix a partial assignment
\rho
with
|\rho^{-1}(\ast)|=O(\mathsf{D}(f_{\textup{CS}}))=O(k^{1.5}\log k)
. Our query algorithm for
f_{\textup{CS}}|_{\rho}
on an unknown input
X|_{\rho}=(x_{1}|_{\rho},\ldots,x_{\log t}|_{\rho},Y_{0}|_{\rho},\ldots,Y_{t-1}|_{\rho})
has two parts like the unrestricted case.

In the first part we identify a cheat-sheet cell (to be verified later) by zeroing in on the string
z\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{\log t}
that the address part
f(x_{1}|_{\rho})f(x_{2}|_{\rho})\cdots f(x_{\log t}|_{\rho})
can take if the restricted function
f_{\textup{CS}}|_{\rho}
were to evaluate to
1
.

In the second part, like the unrestricted case, we verify the certificates described in
Y_{z}
by querying the variables in
Y_{z}|_{\rho}
and the corresponding certificate variables in
(x_{1}|_{\rho},\ldots,x_{\log t}|_{\rho})
.
If the verification passes, the algorithm outputs
1
, else outputs
0
.
Since the algorithm needs to verify the contents of one cell, the total number of queries made in the second part is
O(k\log k\log t+\textnormal{C}(f)\log t)=O(k\log^{2}k)
.

We now give the details of the algorithm for the first part.
Recall that the address part of the input contains
\log t
copies of the base function
f
. We will run (at most)
\log t
many iterations of the algorithm. At the end of each iteration
i
, we will have a candidate value for
f(x_{i}|_{\rho})
, i.e., either we know the value of
f(x_{i}|_{\rho})
or there are no valid cheat-sheet cells for
f(x_{i}|_{\rho})=0
.
At the end of the
\log t
iterations, there will only be one remaining (possibly valid) cheat-sheet cell to be verified. We now give details of an iteration of the algorithm.

Let
c
be a constant such that
|\rho^{-1}(\ast)|=O(\mathsf{D}(f_{\textup{CS}}))\leq ck^{1.5}\log k
.

Details of a particular iteration:

Fix an
i\in[\log t]
. At any given point of the iteration let
\mathcal{V}
denotes the set of “alive” cheat-sheets (cheat-sheets which have not been found inconsistent) and
\mathcal{V}_{0}
denotes the cheat-sheet cells in
\mathcal{V}
where, supposedly,
f(x_{i}|_{\rho})=0
. On the other hand, let
\mathcal{A}_{1}
denote the set of
\and_{j}
’s (
j\in[k]
) in the
i
-th copy of
f
which could still possibly be
1
. Initially,
\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{1}\right\rvert=k
and, in the first iteration,
|\mathcal{V}|=t
and
|\mathcal{V}_{0}|=t/2
. Also, note that each cell in
\mathcal{V}_{0}
is supposed to contain a
0
-certificate for
k
many
\and
s.

The idea of the iteration is to have two stages; Where the first stage (and arguably the more involved one) will get us to a situation which can be handled by previous techniques. More formally, after the first stage, either
\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{1}\right\rvert\leq c\sqrt{k}\log k
or
\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\rvert\leq 2k
.

The second stage takes care of these two cases. In the first case we can check all the alive
\and
’s exhaustively. For the second case, using Claim
34
we will get a single cheat-sheet cell in
\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\rvert
which is then verified to ascertain whether indeed it contains a
0
-certificate for
f(x_{i}|_{\rho})
.

Stage 1
: We can assume that
\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{1}\right\rvert>c\sqrt{k}\log k
and
\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\rvert>2k
, otherwise we move to the second stage.

Looking at
\mathcal{V}_{0}
, for each
\and_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, we call an
\and_{j}
to be
revealed
if the
0
-certificate for that particular
\and_{j}
is completely fixed by
\rho
(i.e., not a single
\ast
) in at least
\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\rvert/2
many cells in
\mathcal{V}_{0}
.

Notice that if there are no revealed
\and
’s, then for more than
c\sqrt{k}\log k
many
\and
’s there are at least
\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\rvert/2>k
many
\ast
’s. This is a contradiction because the number of
\ast
’s, by our assumption, is at most
ck^{1.5}\log k
.

For a revealed
j
, there exists a
popular
variable, say
l\in[\sqrt{k}]
, which appears in at least
\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\rvert/2\sqrt{k}
many cells in
\mathcal{V}_{0}
. We query
x_{i,j,l}
from the input part
x_{i}
. If it is
0
then
\and_{j}
can be discarded from
\mathcal{A}_{1}
; if it is
1
then at least
\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\rvert/2\sqrt{k}
many cells in
\mathcal{V}_{0}
can be thrown out.

Essentially, the first stage of the iteration can be described as: find a revealed
\and
and query the popular variable till
\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{1}\right\rvert\leq c\sqrt{k}\log k
or
\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\rvert\leq 2k
. The existence of such a revealed
\and
follows from the argument described above. We now bound the query complexity in this stage.

Notice that after every query either
\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{1}\right\rvert
reduces by
1
(popular variable being
0
) or
\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\rvert
reduces by a factor of
(1-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k}})
(popular variable being
1
). So the query complexity of the first stage is bounded by
O(k+\sqrt{k}\log t)=O(k)
.

Stage 2
: We know that either
\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{1}\right\rvert\leq c\sqrt{k}\log k
or
\left\lvert\mathcal{V}_{0}\right\rvert\leq 2k
. As alluded before, for the first case, we can query all the
\and
’s in
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and find the value of
f(x_{i}|_{\rho})
. Since the arity of each
\and
is
\sqrt{k}
, this requires
O(k\log k)
queries.

For the second case, use Claim
34
to reduce the size of
\mathcal{V}_{0}
to one using
O(k\log k)
many queries. Then, in the remaining cell, the claimed
0
-certificate for
f(x_{i}|_{\rho})
can be verified by reading the entire certificate in
O(k\log k)
queries.

In other words, the second stage can be done in
O(k\log k)
many queries. Hence, the total number of queries made in both stages of the iteration is
O(k\log k)
. This finishes one iteration
i\in[\log t]
of the algorithm.

Query complexity of the entire algorithm
: Since there are
\log t
many iterations, the total queries made in the first part of the algorithm is
O(k\log^{2}k)
. After these
\log t
iterations, in the second part, there is at most one possible valid cheat-sheet, that can be checked in
O(k\log^{2}k)
many queries (as described in the beginning). Therefore, the entire algorithm makes at most
O(k\log^{2}k)
many queries.

Correctness
: The correctness of the algorithm follows from the fact that a cheat-sheet cell is removed from the set of valid cells if and only if the cell is found to be inconsistent with respect to input
(x_{1}|_{\rho},\ldots,x_{\log t}|_{\rho})
and
f
or when we know the value of
f(x_{i})
. Further, the only remaining valid cell is verified in the second part.
∎
As a corollary to Lemmas
33
,
35
and
36
, we obtain an improved example for the incondensability of query complexity.

Theorem 37
.

Let
f_{\textup{CS}}
be the Boolean function on
\Theta(k^{2.5}\log^{2}k)
variables as defined in (
4.1
). Then, the following holds

•

\mathsf{D}(f_{\textup{CS}})=\widetilde{\Theta}(k^{1.5})
, and

•

for all restrictions
\rho
with
|\rho^{-1}(\ast)|=O(\mathsf{D}(f_{\textup{CS}}))

5
Positive Results

It is a natural question to ask if the gaps in Theorems
27
and
37
are optimal. In other words, does there exists a function for which hardness is even less condensible than that is shown?

In particular, for deterministic query complexity, it asks if there exists a function
f
such that for all restrictions
\rho
with
|\rho^{-1}(\ast)|=O(\mathsf{D}(f))
, we have
\mathsf{D}(f|_{\rho})=O(\mathsf{D}(f)^{\alpha})
where
\alpha<2/3

Observe that
\alpha\geq 1/3
, since
\mathsf{D}(f)\leq\deg(f)^{3}

[
17
]
and
\deg(f)
condenses losslessly. Note that if the improved bound
\mathsf{D}(f)\leq\deg(f)^{2}
were known to hold then we will have a better lower bound for the exponent.

Similarly, for block sensitivity, the exponent
\alpha\geq 1/4
, since
\textnormal{bs}(f)\leq\textnormal{s}(f)^{4}

[
16
]
and
\textnormal{s}(f)
condenses losslessly. Note again that if the improved bound
\textnormal{bs}(f)\leq\textnormal{s}(f)^{2}
were known to hold then we will have a better lower bound for the exponent.

In this section we will establish an improved lower bound for the exponent for almost all decision tree based complexity measures.

We now present a stronger hardness condensation for the measures sensitivity and degree.
We need this to obtain lower bounds for measures like approximate degree, quantum query complexity, etc. It shows that sensitivity and degree can be condensed to any parameter below itself.

Lemma 41

(Stronger hardness condensation)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\in\{\textnormal{s},\deg\}
and
f\colon\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{0,1\right\}
be a Boolean function. Let
k\leq\mathcal{M}(f)
. Then, there exists a restriction

\rho\in\left\{0,1,*\right\}^{n}
with
|\rho^{-1}(\ast)|=k
such that
\mathcal{M}(f|_{\rho})=k
.

Proof.

We argue each case separately.

\mathcal{M}=\textnormal{s}
. Let
a\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
be an input to
f
such that
\textnormal{s}(f,a)=\textnormal{s}(f)
. Given any integer
k\leq\textnormal{s}(f)
, consider a restriction
\rho
that leaves any
k
sensitive variables of
a
unset and sets the rest of the variables according to
a
. Clearly,
|\rho^{-1}(\ast)|=k
and
\textnormal{s}(f|_{\rho})=k
as well.

\mathcal{M}=\deg
. Let
k\leq\deg(f)
. Let
p
be the unique multilinear polynomial that represents
f
. Suppose
p
contains a monomial
\mathfrak{m}
of degree
k
. Consider a restriction
\rho
that leaves variables in
\mathfrak{m}
unset and sets the rest of the variables to
0
. Clearly,
p|_{\rho}
is the unique polynomial of degree
k
that represents
f|_{\rho}
over
k
variables.

Now suppose
p
does not contain any monomial of degree
k
. Let
d>k
be the smallest positive integer such that there exists a monomial
\mathfrak{m}
of degree
d
in
p
, and there does not exist a monomial of degree
i\in[k,d-1]
in
p
. Consider a restriction
\rho
that leaves any
k
variables in
\mathfrak{m}
unset, sets the rest of
d-k
variables in
\mathfrak{m}
to
1
, and set the rest of variables not in
\mathfrak{m}
to
0
. The resulting monomial in the restriction (from
\mathfrak{m}
) will have a non-zero coefficient and cannot be cancelled by any other monomial from the original polynomial
p
. So, such a restriction
\rho
will give us a polynomial of degree
k
that represents
f|_{\rho}
over
k
variables.
∎

Conclusion

We establish negative results (Theorem
1
) that show that lossless condensation is not possible for block sensitivity, fractional block sensitivity and certificate complexity. We also improve (Theorem
2
) upon previously known result for deterministic decision tree complexity. To complement these negative results, we also show that there exists variable restrictions that yields lossy condensation (Theorem
3
) for almost all complexity measures. However, a gap remains (Table
1
) in the understanding of the tightest possible condensation bounds and the known examples that achieve them.
