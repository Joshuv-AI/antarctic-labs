 ABSTRACT

The Miniature Fuel Molybdenum-99 (Mini99) experiment is a first-of-a-kind experiment in the High Flux
Isotope Reactor. Mini99 is the first MiniFuel irradiation experiment to use liquid metal inside the
MiniFuel subcapsule for additional temperature control. In this work, the initial post-irradiation
examination is reported. As-built simulations were conducted and compared with designs, and physical
post-irradiation examinations were conducted to determine actual test temperatures via SiC thermometry
samples, to evaluate the extent of fission gas release from the samples, and to remove the fuel from the
subcapsules. As-built simulations compared well with experiment designs and were in reasonable
agreement with the measured temperatures inferred from the passive SiC thermometers. Additionally,
fission gas release values were relatively small (on the order of 3%); one sample reached approximately
8%. Although some of this behavior is attributable to recoil, most of the gas release was likely caused by
the physics of the fuel behavior during the test. Finally, disassembly showed that at least four of the fuel
samples degraded into small components and granules at some point during the test, with the state of the
other two samples unproven. Additional work is planned for further examination of these samples via
various microscopy techniques.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The Miniature Fuel Molybdenum-99 (Mini99) experiment is a first-of-a-kind experiment utilizing liquid
sodium cooling within subcapsules of the MiniFuel experimental irradiation vehicle. The MiniFuel
vehicle is a specialized tool for the irradiation of novel fuel forms in the High Flux Isotope Reactor
(HFIR) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). It has been used for the past half decade to examine a
wide range of fuel forms, from bare kernels to full compacts to metallic fuel disks [1-8].

MiniFuel is an irradiation vehicle that places up to nine targets inside an experimental facility tube in
HFIR. Historically, the vertical experiment facility (VXF) positions within the permanent beryllium core
of HFIR have been used for this purpose due to the lower flux and lower energies of the neutrons in this
region [2], which permit closer-to-prototypical irradiation conditions. Each target contains six
subcapsules, each of which houses a fuel dish with fuel, a gamma heater, and a SiC thermometer. The SiC
thermometer is a passive thermometer that provides a single-point temperature reading. This reading is
based on defect recovery within SiC. Defect recovery is temperature-driven, particularly above SiC’s
amorphization temperature, where defects within SiC begin to become mobile [9-11]. Historically, SiC
has been used as a thermometer only within the range of 300°C–800°C due to the predominance of point
defects stemming from radiation damage in this region. However, irradiation temperatures as low as the
amorphization temperature of ~200°C can be detected, and recovery of higher-order defects above the
point defect region may also be possible with sufficient resolution. Each irradiation is designed to achieve
a precise fuel temperature by tailoring the fill gas of the target and subcapsule and varying the exact
diameter of the subcapsule to the control the gas gap between the subcapsule wall and the target wall,
which is cooled by the HFIR coolant [12].

Historically, the MiniFuel irradiation vehicle was limited to temperatures between approximately 300°C
and 800°C based upon the geometry of the VXF positions within HFIR as well as properties of
constituent components and HFIR flow conditions, the latter of which are unchangeable [2]. The Mini99
project set out to achieve much lower fuel irradiation temperatures of 220°C, corresponding to
thermometry temperatures as low as 190°C [13]. This was accomplished by the first-of-a-kind design
using the addition of a sodium filler around the fuel disk. In an ordinary disk irradiation, such as the UMo
irradiation, most the heat from the fuel is dispersed only through the fuel dish underneath of the fuel, with
the rest of the fuel remaining in contact with pure helium [8, 14, 15]. Because the fuel disks are thin (< 1
mm thick), little thermal variation across the thickness is observed. However, the heat sink is still finite.

Adding sodium above the fuel disk causes a liquid to be formed during irradiation. This liquid can rapidly
conduct heat to the subcapsule walls [16, 17], thereby allowing greater reduction in the temperature of the
fuel during irradiation. Figure 1 shows a schematic representation of the subcapsule as-designed.

Prior to this work, the Mini99 irradiation was completed, and the fuel was transported to the Irradiated
Fuels Examination Laboratory (IFEL) for disassembly and testing. In this work, the disassembly, post-
irradiation as-built simulations, and initial post-irradiation examination (PIE) are presented and discussed.
Future work efforts are also described.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

2.1 NEUTRONICS AND FINITE ELEMENT ANALYSIS

The as-planned Mini99 irradiation was to consist of three targets, each containing six subcapsules to be
irradiated at similar temperatures: 200°C, 300°C, and 400°C. However, fabrication difficulties due to the
sodium inside the subcapsules limited the irradiation to six subcapsules and one target. The target
contained two subcapsules from each of the intended target temperatures, thereby spanning a final
intended fuel temperature range of 193°C–340°C. However, small differences in the initial plan compared
with actual irradiation—such as the exact VXF position used, as-irradiated history of the HFIR cycle, true
geometry of the components compared with the as-designed geometry, and other factors—can slightly
alter the irradiation conditions from the as-planned state [8]. Thus, following irradiation, the as-built and
as-irradiated neutronic and thermal simulations were reperformed in accordance with procedures
documented elsewhere [17].

2.2 POST-IRRADIATION EXAMINATION

In 2024, disassembly of the of the Mini99 target was completed by low-speed saw cutting, and a plan was
developed for puncture of the subcapsules for fission gas release (FGR) followed by disassembly of the
subcapsules to retrieve the fuel samples for PIE in the form of thickness evaluation via confocal scanning
profilometry and laser flash analysis (LFA) [13, 15, 18, 19]. As documented in Section 3, no material was
recovered that was suitable for LFA or confocal scanning profilometry. Therefore, neither technique was
employed in this work.

 2

FGR analysis was performed per the established method for MiniFuel samples in the IFEL cells [20]. The
procedure utilized a gas transfer table that was connected to a puncture head. The subcapsule was fully
inserted into this head, and the head was sealed. The system was then evacuated and checked for leaks. A
series of evacuations and refills was performed to determine the system tare volume, and the system was
then evacuated. Two charcoal-filled traps were connected to the output end of the puncture head, and the
whole system was thoroughly purged with helium. Liquid nitrogen was then used to cool the traps, and
they were again purged. The system was configured for a puncture, and a puncture head broke open the
subcapsule to release interior gases. After recording pressure, these gases were then swept through the
cold traps, where the Kr and other heavy gases were sequestered [21]. Krypton-85 was then detected
using a germanium detector.

 Figure 2: Schematic of the FGR collection table. Reproduced from [20].

After puncture, the samples were disassembled per established procedure using dummy specimens [13].
This procedure required initially scoring the sample with a low-speed saw at 5.0 mm from the subcapsule
base. Then, the neck of the subcapsule was cut to extract the thermometry. The subcapsule was then cut at
the initial score location and rinsed; then, additional slices were removed from the subcapsule until the
fuel dish was fully exposed. At this point, the subcapsule remnant was removed from the saw and placed
in methanol to dissolve additional sodium and release the fuel from the fuel dish for recovery. The
referenced literature [13] contains details of the rationale for this procedure. After the initial sample was
examined, the initial rinse after the first cut at the score mark was removed from the procedure due to
failure of recovery and concern about losing the sample during the initial rinse.

Finally, the SiC thermometers were examined via dilatometry using a Netzsch DIL 402CD horizontal
dual-pushrod dilatometer in the Low Activation Materials Development and Analysis (LAMDA)
Laboratory to extract the single-point irradiation temperature. Collection followed best practices
established by Campbell et al. and Field et al. [10, 11], wherein heating is performed at 1°C/s up to at
least 300°C beyond the expected irradiation temperature. A cooling curving was then collected at 2.5°C/s
down to room temperature. A Python script implementing quantitative analysis in this work can be found
in APPENDIX A. This script is substantially similar to a script used previously [8] to implement the
analysis method by Campbell et al. and Field et al.

 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1 AS-BUILT NEUTRONIC ANALYSIS

Figure 3 shows the fission rate history of each sample over the course of the single HFIR cycle. In this
irradiation, the samples were arranged with M99-1-2 closest to the core, followed by M99-1-3, M99-3-3,
M99-2-1, M99-2-3, and finally M99-3-5 farthest from the core [13, 17]. The initial fission rate densities
of each sample followed the expected trend: Samples farther from the core fissioned less than samples
closer to the core. Moreover, the shape of the curves is consistent with prior MiniFuel fission rate
histories [2, 8] with some limited exceptions. The Mini99 experiment used a relatively high enrichment of
8% to achieve large burnups in a single cycle. Ordinarily, MiniFuel uses approximately 1% enrichment,
which allows for more event fission rate histories over multiple cycles. For example, former MiniFuel
irradiations would typically drop from an initial fission rate density to a plateau followed by a further
increase to a maximum fission rate density at the end of the cycle (due to the behavior of the control
mechanisms in HFIR). In the Mini99 experiment, the three samples closest to the core center began at a
high fission rate density that decayed to a lower one over time. The three samples farther from the core
center fissioned much more evenly over the irradiation cycle (note that fission rate history for M99-3-5
was the most like former MiniFuel experiments). Irrespective of these minor differences in shape of the
fission rate history between the samples, the fission rate densities were reasonably constant over time with
up to 20% variation about the mean. This translated into nearly linear burnup profiles (Figure 4) that
followed the same trends as the fission rate density profiles.

3.2 AS-BUILT THERMAL CALCULATIONS AND THERMOMETRY DATA

Figure 6 shows results of thermal simulations over the history of the exposure combined with the results
of the thermometry analysis. Figure 7 summarizes the fuel temperature data from Figure 6 (the red
squares) into maximum, minimum, and average. Beginning with Figure 6, the thermometry for M99-1-2
was not available for analysis; therefore, no PIE data exist to validate the temperature measurements.
However, analysis of the other samples sufficiently describes the validity of the thermal model. The first
note to make is the choice of comparison for the thermometry data. Thermometry analysis depends on the
number of point defects present in the sample. Because irradiation defects in SiC will thermally anneal
within the point defect temperature regime of SiC, the instantaneous coefficient of thermal expansion
rapidly decreases once the dilatometry test temperature approaches the irradiation temperature. This is
due to a reduction in the swelling of the SiC due to lattice contractions as the thermometry is heated
during the dilatometry test.

As such, the signal seen in the dilatometry test corresponds to the last day of irradiation. Ordinarily, and
for Figure 6(c – f), the last day of irradiation is also the hottest day, resulting in an annealing out of the
lower-activation point defects formed earlier in the irradiation at lower temperature. As discussed in
Section 3.1, based on fission rate history, the two samples in Figure 6(a and b) are atypical of previous
MiniFuel experiments. Specifically, note how the fuel temperature was lower at the end of the irradiation
cycle compared to the beginning of the cycle. However, the same was not true of the thermometry, which
experienced similar or slightly greater temperatures at the end of irradiation compared with the full cycle
history. Thus, it is appropriate to compare the inferred thermometry temperatures to the simulated
thermometry temperatures on the last day of irradiation, as is done in Figure 6.

Figure 6 shows that the thermometry simulations agree reasonably well with measured thermometry but
with a few caveats. First, all five measured temperatures are higher than simulated. Although SiC
thermometry measurements are known to have an error of about 20°C compared with thermocouple
measurements [10, 11], the results in Figure 6 are not normal scatter but systematic discrepancy. Figure
6(b) agrees well within the experimental uncertainty of the measurement inference, but the thermometry
piece from Figure 6(b) was broken on arrival and required smoothing analysis to examine, which drove
up the experimental uncertainty. Figure 6(c and d) show that measurement agreed well with the
simulation but still ran a few degrees Celsius hotter. Finally, Figure 6(e and f) measurements were outside
the 1σ error bars and ran about 25°C hotter than the simulation. Altogether, these results suggest that the
simulation may have underestimated the true irradiation temperature by anywhere from 10°C to 20°C,
which still represents very good agreement overall considering the inevitable uncertainties in geometric
and thermal transport parameters that go into the thermal models [22].

 8

Regarding the simulations themselves, Figure 7 shows that the irradiation was, as intended, reasonably
isothermal. The 1σ temperature spread about the mean was between 3°C and 9°C. The temperature is also
not even distributed across the irradiation; this is inferable from Figure 6, but the hottest temperatures
represent a 2σ outlier from the mean. It is also worth noting that the lower-temperature irradiations have a
simulated average temperature around 225°C, and the higher-temperature irradiations have a simulated
average temperature around 315°C. Although there is minor variation between the higher- and lower-
temperature groups, all samples within both groups were irradiated at nearly the same condition as other
members of the group and only a few degrees hotter than initial design.

Figure 8 plots the FGR data for each of the samples at their respective burnups with irradiation
temperature (printed with the sample name), as calculated via finite element analysis. The solid squares
represent the total FGR fraction. However, FGR comes from two sources: One source is the diffusion-
based release out of the solid matrix, and the second source is recoil release into the void. This recoil is
limited to the first 5–7 µm of fuel on the exterior of the sample. For MiniFuel, the constrained geometries
ordinarily limit the amount of detectable recoil release due to secondary implantation of the large majority
of recoiled gases [8]. In the Mini99 experiment, however, recoiling gases will be captured by the sodium,
which, being a liquid, will permit these gases to percolate up into the plenum portion of the subcapsule
and be available for FGR detection. Therefore, all FGR detected intuitively should be larger than
geometrically expected FGR.

 recoil from the surface based upon the pre-irradiation geometry of the sample. A puncturing error occurred in

the range of 60 MeV krypton in alpha uranium. The fraction of atoms released by recoil was crudely
estimated using a Monte Carlo simulation in which 60 MeV krypton was born at 4 µm beneath the
surface up to the surface, assuming no diffusion and random orientation relative to the surface during
birth. The gas was considered to have recoiled if its range was greater than the distance to the surface
based upon its angle and birth depth. The code used to implement this approach is documented in
APPENDIX B.

It is important to note that this analysis is approximate. Aside from the higher-order effects of diffusion of
implanted ions near surfaces, this analysis assumes a uniform sample during irradiation with negligible
changes in sample condition. As discussed in Section 3.4, significant changes in shape and size occurred
during irradiation. Thus, actual recoil fraction is likely higher than estimated in Figure 8, although it is not
clear when during the irradiation the changes in shape and size occurred. Although it is possible given the
form changes that all the FGR is actually recoil-driven FGR, the differences in calculated recoil and total
FGR for the M99-2-3, M99-2-1, M99-3-3, and M99-1-3 samples are significant (a factor of 5.6–15.4),
suggesting that at least some FGR did come from the samples themselves.

Notably, gas release, regardless of the ultimate cause, was limited to < 10% up to the 2.4% FIMA
achieved in this work. Moreover, FGR was negligible (0.8%) at doses up to 1.4% FIMA at 230°C (sample
M99-3-5) but increased to 5.1% at 2.2% FIMA for M99-1-3. Similarly, some increase in FGR with
burnup was observed at 350°C. The 350°C samples all released more fission gases than the 220°C–230°C
samples, and from 1.65% FIMA to 2.02% FIMA, the 310°C–330°C samples increased in FGR from 3.3%
to 7.4%, although not linearly. Over such a small increase in burnup, and without the trend being clear, it
is most likely that at least the higher-temperature sample FGR is driven more by changes in sample
geometry (observed on disassembly) than by any FGR-related physics.

3.4 DISASSEMBLY

All six samples were disassembled with varying degrees of success. As mentioned in earlier sections of
this report, no disassembly resulted in a fully intact disk. In the first disassembly, M99-1-2, the cuts
successfully opened the sodium cavity at the anticipated location. However, after the sample was removed
from methanol, no fuel could be located. Either the fuel had fallen into pieces during the irradiation (and
these pieces were lost during the initial rinse) or the sample was debonded from the fuel dish due to the
first rinse and fell away from the dish during transport to the methanol bath.

The procedure was then modified to remove the initial methanol rinse. This necessitates combining any
cutting dust with the sample material in the fuel debonding immersion but preserves all material in one
place, even if the fuel has broken apart. Cutting of M99-2-1, M99-1-3, and M99-2-3 yielded successful
cuts in the correct location above the fuel dish. Figure 9 shows two images from the disassembly process
of M99-2-1. After initial cutting, the moisture in the cell reacted with the sodium metal in the cut well and
caused it to visibly bubble. Upon removal and methanol immersion, several small black pieces were
observed (Figure 9[b]), but no large or disc-like objects were observed with them. Each of the two pieces
was estimated to be 2 mm on the largest side, and from different angles, the pieces appeared to possibly
fit together. From this, and the fact that no such cutting debris has previously been observed in the M99
dummy cutting process [13], these pieces were assumed to be fuel. One piece was dose-rated and found to
be in excess of 500 rad/h beta dose, which is consistent with expectations of the fuel particles and
inconsistent with cutting debris dose rates.

Microscopy data were not available for this report and thus discussion on failure modes is highly
speculative. At present, the fragments discussed in this Section generally have a size and shape of grains
in the as-fabricated uranium foils, suggesting irradiation growth may have led to disk failure. However,

 4. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

This report details initial PIE on subcapsules and fuel coming from the first-of-a-kind Mini99 irradiation.
The initial PIE discussed in this report consisted of as-built as-irradiated simulations and physical testing
of the MiniFuel samples. Compared with as-designed conditions, the as-built and as-irradiated
simulations were found to be similar to predictions, showing a slightly elevated temperature bias. Burnup,
fission rate, and isotopic calculations were similar to expectations for this experiment. However, the
unique design of the Mini99 experiment compared to other MiniFuel experiments led to differences from
past MiniFuel irradiations. These were discussed.

Moreover, a finite element analysis based on the burnup and fission rate data compared well with physical
PIE. After disassembly, the SiC thermometry was measured and was found to generally agree with
simulated data. As-built simulations found the following temperatures: M99-3-5 (231°C),
M99-2-3 (308°C), M99-2-1 (313°C), M99-3-3 (329°C), and M99-1-3 (227°C). Thermometry data
consistently were measured 10°C–20°C higher than the last-day simulation temperature. This agreement
is reasonable when the uncertainties in the thermal simulation parameters and in the experimental
measurement technique are considered. The simulations were thus considered to represent the experiment
well.

Total FGR was surprisingly low (under 8%) for the burnups of up to 2.4% FIMA achieved in the
irradiation. Total FGR was adjusted for expected recoil from the intact disk during irradiation. After
adjustment, most of the measured FGR was still present, indicating some mechanism of release. However,
FGR was still low enough as to not present significant engineering challenges if the material were to be
used in an irradiation vehicle for commercial purposes.

Finally, disassembly of the samples was moderately successful. For M99-1-2, no material was recovered.
M99-3-5 was not able to be fully disassembled to assess the state of the fuel. For the other four samples,
disassembly was successful, and material was recovered. However, no disassembly resulted in an intact
disk, suggesting that during the irradiation itself, the disks broke into various pieces.

Additional work will focus on preparation of metallographic mounts for thermal property analysis and
electron microscopy. In particular, light and scanning electron microscopy will be used to assess local
void and precipitate formation within the sample, as well as to assess sample edges to identify possible
reasons for fracture, which are at present speculative. Transmission electron microscopy will be used to
examine small bubble formation in the fuel matrix. Bubble and void distributions within the matrix
impact the thermal and mechanical properties of the fuel. Thermal properties will also be directly assessed
at the Idaho National Laboratory using the thermal properties microscope.

def AsymptoticSigmoid(T,B,S,C,A,x):
  return B+(T-B)/np.power((1+np.exp(S*(C-x))),A)

def Asymptotics_deriv(T,B,S,C,A,x,order):
  if (order == 1):
     return A*S*(T-B)*np.divide(np.exp(S*(C-x)),np.power(1+np.exp(S*(C-x)),A+1))
  exp_val = np.exp(S*(C-x))
  if (order == 2):
     return A*S*S*(T-B)*exp_val*((A+1)*np.divide(exp_val,np.power(1+exp_val,(A+2)))-
np.power(1+exp_val,-A-1))
  ret_val = A*S*S*S*(T-B)*exp_val*(np.power(1+exp_val,-A-1)-
3*(A+1)*np.divide(exp_val,np.power(1+exp_val,A+2))+\
          (A+2)*(A+1)*np.divide(np.exp(2*S*(C-x)),np.power(1+exp_val,A+3)))
  return ret_val

def SigmoidResid(Params,x,data):
  T,B,S,C,A = Params
  return np.abs(np.sqrt(np.abs(data-AsymptoticSigmoid(T,B,S,C,A,x))))

import math as m

def loadfile(file,density):
  # takes filename and density from SRIM range output. density is in g/cm^3
  # returns arrays of energy (MeV), total de/dx (MeV/mm), projected range (mm),
  # distance travelled per lost bin (mm), and energy lost per bin (MeV)
  f = open(file)
  lines = f.readlines()[24:]
  f.close()
  energy =[]
  dedx_tot = []
  ranges = []

  for line in lines:
     if line[0] == "-": break
 split = line.split()
     if (split[1]) == 'keV':
 energy.append(float(split[0])/1000) # MeV
     elif (split[1] == "eV"):
 energy.append(float(split[0])/1e6) # MeV
 else:
 energy.append(float(split[0]))
 dedx_tot.append((float(split[2])+float(split[3]))*density*100) # MeV/mm
     if split[5] == "A":
         ranges.append(float(split[4])/10000000)
     elif split[5] =="um":
 ranges.append(float(split[4])/1000) # mm
 else:
 ranges.append(float(split[4]))
  dedx_tot = np.array(dedx_tot)
  energy = np.array(energy)
  dxdE = []
  ELost = []
  dxdE.append(energy[0]/dedx_tot[0])
  ELost.append(energy[0])
  for i in range(1,len(energy)):
 dxdE.append((energy[i]-energy[i-1])/dedx_tot[i])
 ELost.append(energy[i]-energy[i-1])
  return energy,dedx_tot,np.array(ranges),np.array(dxdE),ELost
