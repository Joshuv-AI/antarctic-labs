1 Introduction
1.1 Object to be transported
The object to be transported is a 650 MHz Superconducting Radio Frequency
(SRF) Low Beta (LB) cavity, equipped with a high power coupler. This compo-
nent is a key element in accelerator technology, and due to its delicate geometry
and sensitivity, special care must be taken during handling and transportation.

 High power coupler

 650 MHz SRF cavity

Superconducting radio frequency (SRF) cavities
SRF cavities are resonant structures used in particle accelerators to efficiently
transfer electromagnetic energy to charged particle beams. They operate by
sustaining radio frequency (RF) electromagnetic fields inside a hollow, precisely
shaped cavity, made of niobium and cooled to cryogenic temperatures (around
2 K) using liquid helium supplied through the helium feedlines beneath the cav-
ity. At such low temperatures, niobium becomes superconducting, drastically
reducing the surface resistance of the cavity walls. This results in minimal power
losses and allows very high quality factors (Q ∼ 109 − 1010 ) to be achieved.

 Figure 1: Section view of the cavity with the coupler and antenna

 1

High power coupler
The high power coupler is the interface between the RF power source and the
cavity. It transfers the required RF power into the cavity while maintaining
vacuum integrity and thermal insulation. The coupler consists of an antenna
inserted into the cavity beam pipe and is designed to withstand high average
power levels, ensuring stable operation of the accelerating structure.
The coupler is connected to the lateral flange of the cavity, as shown in Figure 1,
with its antenna extending inside the cavity. Before transportation, the entire
assembly is placed under vacuum and hermetically sealed; therefore, no modifi-
cations or reinforcements can be made inside either the cavity or the coupler. As
a consequence, the design of the supporting frame must rely exclusively on ex-
ternal components, which can be added around the assembly without interfering
with its internal configuration.

1.2 Modes of transport
The cavity with coupler will be transported via road (truck) and air (cargo).
To define conservative requirements for vibration isolation and shock mitigation,
previous transportation studies at Fermilab for complete cryomodules have been
considered, particularly the Design of a Transport System for the PIP-II HB650
Cryomodule [1]. These studies addressed the risks associated with trans-Atlantic
transport of cryomodules and established design requirements for the transport
frame to prevent damage to sensitive components.

Figure 2: Shock and vibration envelope for road transport based on multiple
standards.

 2

 These requirements were derived from shock and vibration profiles estab-
lished in standards such as MIL-STD-810H for air and road transport condi-
tions, ISO 13355:2016 for road transport vibration, and ASTM D4169-16 for
shipping container performance testing. To remain conservative, [1] considered
the envelope of these different standards, as shown in Figure 2, and the same
approach will be adopted in this work.
 The design approach for the 650 MHz cavity assembly will follow a method-
ology similar to that described in [1] to ensure safe transport, building on the
proven practices successfully applied to the HB650 cryomodules at Fermilab.
This approach includes the use of wire rope isolators mounted between an outer
frame and an inner frame rigidily connected to the cavity assembly, providing
effective shock and vibration isolation during transport.

1.4 Design objectives
The objective of the supporting frame design is to ensure the structural integrity
and safety of the cavity during transportation. First, the frame must be designed
to prevent resonance phenomena. In particular, its natural frequencies must be
sufficiently separated from the critical excitation frequencies identified during
transport. This requirement minimizes the risk of amplifying vibrations that
could otherwise compromise the integrity of the cavity or its coupler.
 [1] specifies that vibrations at 10 Hz must be attenuated by at least 80How-
ever, in our case the optimal isolation frequency should be defined according to
the specific dynamic characteristics of the cavity assembly, as will be determined
in the subsequent modal analyses.
 In addition to vibration isolation, the frame must provide adequate pro-
tection against accidental shocks and impacts that may occur during loading,
unloading, or transport. To achieve this, the frame is designed to minimize the
accelerations experienced by the cavity and coupler, while keeping deformations
and stresses in each component within the allowable limits of their respective
materials.
 The final configuration must guarantee safe transportation by both attenu-
ating harmful vibrations and absorbing potential impact loads, without trans-
mitting damaging forces to the cavity or coupler.

2 Roadmap
The following steps summarize the methodology used for the design and verifi-
cation of the transportation frame:
 1. Modal analysis of the cavity assembly: Perform a detailed modal
 analysis of the cavity with the coupler to determine its natural frequencies
 and identify the critical modes that could be excited during transportation.
 2. Frame conceptual design: Design the supporting frame considering
 the dimensional constraints imposed by the shipping crate and ensuring
 compatibility with the cavity assembly. Select materials with sufficient
 strength and stiffness to support the cavity and withstand handling loads.
 3. Integration of isolators: Select vibration isolators such that their nat-
 ural frequency is well below the critical frequency of the cavity-coupler as-
 sembly. This ensures decoupling from critical-frequency vibrations while
 providing damping for shocks and transport-induced excitations.

 4. Static analysis of the frame: Perform a static structural analysis of the
 frame with the cavity assembly installed, in order to verify that stresses
 and deformations remain within the allowable limits of the selected ma-
 terials. Additionally, ensure that the static deflection of the frame, due
 to the compliance of the isolators, remains within acceptable limits for
 transportation.

 4

 5. Dynamic analysis of the integrated system: Conduct a dynamic
 analysis of the frame with the cavity, including the effect of the isola-
 tors, under expected transportation vibrations. Verify that resonances
 are avoided and that the assembly is protected against harmful shocks.

3.3 Modal analysis of the cavity with the coupler
A modal analysis of the cavity with the coupler was performed to study the
vibrational characteristics of the coupled system. The results are summarized
as follows.

 Figure 13: First vibration mode of the complete assembly

 13

 The first two modes correspond to the oscillation of the coupler antenna
alone, vibrating in directions orthogonal to each other, and appear at a fre-
quency of approximately 38.0 Hz. The third mode involves the vibration of
the niobium resonant cells, occurring at approximately 43.0 Hz. Notably, the
numerical values of these first three modes are essentially unchanged compared
to the analysis of the individual components.
 The fourth mode of the complete assembly involves the vibration of the
entire coupler. In the analysis of the coupler alone, this mode occurred at 148.8
Hz; in the complete assembly, it shifts to 52 Hz because the coupler is no longer
anchored to a rigid support but is connected to a structure (the cavity) with its
own compliance. Since this mode does not correspond to the lowest-frequency
vibration of the system, the frequency at which the system is most sensitive
remains that of the coupler antenna oscillating alone. Therefore, the critical
frequency to be considered in the design and analysis is

 fcritical = 38 Hz,

corresponding to the vibration of the coupler antenna.

4 Frame design
The support frame has been designed to carry the entire cavity assembly, with
a total mass of approximately 150 kg (comprising 140 kg for the cavity and
9 kg for the coupler). The center of mass of the cavity-coupler assembly is lo-
cated at coordinates (−3.35, 0.426, 0.142) in, which correspond to approximately
(−85.1, 10.8, 3.6) mm.
 To optimise manufacturing time and reduce costs, the frame is largely built
from off-the-shelf components. The main load-bearing members are 1515 ex-
truded aluminium profiles from the 80/20 series, selected for their combination
of light weight, ease of assembly, and sufficient stiffness for this application
(Figure 14).

 Figure 14: 1515 80/20 aluminium profile used for the frame

 The frame connects to the four support pads already welded onto the cavity.
The chosen interface solution is a bolted sandwich connection, implemented
through two plates and illustrated in Figure 15:

 • a lower plate, rigidly fixed to the aluminium profiles of the frame;

 14

 • an upper plate, clamping the welded cavity support between itself and the
 lower plate.
Since the support pads serve as functional surfaces that must maintain their
integrity, a thin rubber gasket is placed between the metal plates and the cavity
support to prevent surface damage such as scratches during clamping.

Figure 15: Bolted sandwich connection between the frame and the cavity pads

It should be noted that in similar frames designed for cavity transportation,
alternative attachment solutions have been adopted, such as the one shown in
Figure 16. This approach involves creating collars that surround the cavity
and connect to longitudinal tubular members. To maintain design simplicity,
the current solution uses the four existing support pads for attachment. How-
ever, nothing prevents switching to an alternative attachment method like the
one illustrated; the conclusions obtained would be analogous, with negligible
differences.

Figure 16: Alternative collar-based attachment system used in similar cavity

4.1 Dual-frame system
The support frame consists of two main structures:
 • Inner frame: Rigidly connected to the cavity (see Figure 17). It provides
 the primary structural support for the cavity.

 15

 • Outer frame: Connected to the Inner frame via wire rope isolators,
 providing vibration isolation from external excitation.

 Figure 17: Inner frame rigidly connected to the SRF cavity.

 Since the Inner frame is rigidly connected to the cavity and the majority
of the system’s compliance is provided by the wire rope isolators, alternative
connection methods between the Inner frame and the cavity, such as the collar-
based system previously illustrated, would not significantly affect the dynamic
analysis. Regardless of the specific attachment approach, the connection re-
mains rigid, and the primary vibration isolation characteristics are determined
by the isolator properties rather than the frame-to-cavity interface.

Inner frame validation A preliminary static analysis was carried out on
the Inner frame to verify the structural adequacy of the selected 1515 80/20
aluminium profiles.
The results show a maximum bending moment of approximately 11 N m and a
maximum axial force of 265 N. These loads induce a maximum stress of 1.1 MPa,
which is significantly below the yield strength of 6063-T6 aluminium (172 MPa).
This confirms that the frame is structurally safe under static loading conditions.
 A dynamic analysis will be conducted in the next phase, once the wire rope
isolators are incorporated into the model. The subsequent section presents a
sizing study aimed at selecting the most suitable isolators for vibration mitiga-
tion.

4.2 Choice of the isolators
The isolators are designed to attenuate vibrations during transportation by en-
suring their natural frequencies are well below the cavity’s critical frequency of
38 Hz. This approach effectively decouples the assembly from external excita-
tions above the isolator natural frequency, preventing transmission of potentially
harmful vibrations to the cavity structure.

 16

 Consequently, the entire assembly (cavity with coupler) is free to oscillate
only at low frequencies. In fact, these low-frequency oscillations may even be
slightly amplified. These oscillations correspond to the rigid-body motion of the
system as a whole, which does not pose a risk to the fragile antenna component
previously identified as the most critical element.

4.2.1 Selection of the isolator series
For this application, a model from the M Series Wire Rope Isolators by Isolation
Dynamics Corporation (shown in Figure 18) has been selected, as it offers several
advantageous features:
 • Rugged all-metal construction

 • Wide operating temperature range: -200°F to 500°F (−129 ◦ C to 260 ◦ C)
 • Three-axis capability (6 degrees of freedom)
 • Fail-safe construction
   • High damping: C/Cc ≈ 0.15 − 0.20

 • Long life and maintenance-free operation
The high damping capability ensures that the energy transmitted to the frame
through transport-induced vibrations is effectively dissipated by friction within
the isolators.

 Figure 18: M Series Wire Rope Isolators by Isolation Dynamics Corporation

 Previous studies, such as the one on the SSR1 cryomodule transportation [2],
have shown that these isolators do not behave as simple linear spring-damper
systems, but rather exhibit a non-linear hysteretic response that can be de-
scribed by the Bouc–Wen model. However, since such a model is not straight-
forward to implement in ANSYS, and in order to maintain a simplified analysis
approach, a conservative margin has been considered in the analysis.
 Future developments of this project could include experimental testing of the
selected isolators to assess their actual behavior and validate the assumptions
made in the present analysis.

nances. Transportation requirements for the cryomodule, reported in [1], specify
that 80% isolation at 10 Hz is required. However, based on the modal analy-
sis results, which show the first critical frequency at 38 Hz, we concluded that
achieving 80% isolation at this frequency would be sufficient.
 This optimized threshold provides an adequate safety margin while limiting
the compliance of the isolator. By avoiding excessively soft isolators, the sys-
tem maintains sufficient stiffness to prevent large static deflections or unwanted
tilting, which could compromise transportation stability and handling. In par-
ticular, the static deflection is required to remain below 50 mm, according to
ASTM D4169-16.

5.3 Model reduction using Component Mode Synthesis
 (CMS)
To optimize computational efficiency for subsequent analyses of the cavity as-
sembly mounted on the support frame, Component Mode Synthesis (CMS) was
implemented. This technique enables a significant reduction in the number of
degrees of freedom while preserving the essential dynamic characteristics of the
system.

Theoretical Background of CMS
Component Mode Synthesis, also known as substructuring, is a model reduction
technique that represents large structural components as superelements with
significantly fewer degrees of freedom. CMS includes truncated sets of normal
mode generalized coordinates defined for components of the structural model
and reduces the system matrices to a smaller set of interface DOFs between
substructures (components) and truncated sets of normal mode generalized co-
ordinates.
 The method is particularly useful for large, complex assemblies where in-
dividual teams design different components, as it allows each component to be
analyzed separately before being coupled together in the final assembly analysis.

 24

 In ANSYS, CMS is primarily implemented through the Craig-Bampton method,
which is the most widely used variant of component mode synthesis. The Craig-
Bampton method is a technique for reducing the size of a finite element model,
particularly where two or more subsystems are connected. It combines the mo-
tion of boundary points with modes of the subsystem assuming the boundary
points are held fixed.

Implementation in ANSYS Workbench

 Figure 24: ANSYS Workbench setup for Superelement Generation

In ANSYS, the implementation of CMS involves the creation of superelements
through the following process:

 • Superelement generation: The cavity assembly is condensed into a
 superelement that retains only the essential interface degrees of freedom
 and a selected number of internal modes
 • Interface definition: Connection points between the cavity and the sup-
 port frame are identified and preserved as interface nodes.
 • Mode selection: A truncated set of the most significant internal modes
 is retained based on frequency content and modal participation factors.
 • Matrix condensation: The superelement represents a collection of ele-
 ments that are reduced to act as one element, which can then be used in
 the actual analysis.

Figure 24 shows the Workbench schematic for the creation of the superelement.

Benefits for the current analysis
The application of CMS to the cavity assembly provides several advantages:

 Figure 25: Reduction of the cavity model

 • Computational efficiency: The reduced model contains significantly
 fewer degrees of freedom, decreasing from approximately 540,000 nodes in
 the full model to about 240 nodes in the reduced one. This leads to much
 faster solution times for subsequent frame-mounted analyses.
 • Memory optimization: Reduced memory requirements, which is par-
 ticularly beneficial for handling large models.
 • Modular analysis: The cavity can be analyzed independently and then
 efficiently coupled with different frame and isolator configurations.

Validation of the CMS model
The CMS model successfully preserved the critical frequency of fcritical = 38 Hz
while significantly reducing the computational burden compared to the full as-
sembly model. This condensed geometry enables efficient integration with the
support frame for comprehensive system-level vibration analysis.

Limitations and applicability of the CMS approach
Although CMS offers substantial computational benefits, it was not applicable
to all analyses performed in this study. The primary limitation of CMS imple-
mentation in ANSYS at present is the lack of support for acceleration-based
loading conditions, although this constraint may be resolved in future software
releases. Consequently, when analyses required the application of accelerations,
the complete finite element model had to be employed instead of the reduced-
order CMS model.

5.4 Random vibration analysis
To evaluate the dynamic response of the cavity assembly under realistic trans-
portation conditions, a random vibration analysis was performed. This analysis
employs Power Spectral Density (PSD) input profiles derived from transporta-
tion standards to simulate the stochastic nature of vibrations encountered during
road and air transport.

5.4.1 Input excitation
As defined in Section 1.2, the input excitation was defined using an envelope
PSD profile that encompasses the most severe conditions from multiple trans-
portation standards. While ASTM D4169 typically requires testing only in the
vertical (z) axis due to the normal shipping orientation, a conservative approach
was adopted for this analysis. To provide comprehensive coverage of all possible
excitation scenarios, three separate analyses were conducted with identical PSD
acceleration input applied in each principal direction, namely:
 • the longitudinal axis (x), aligned with the cavity axis,

 • the lateral axis (y), perpendicular to the cavity axis and pointing sideways,
 • the vertical axis (z), oriented upward and normal to the support plane.

Figure 26 presents the transmissibility results on a logarithmic scale, where val-

The results demonstrate the expected behavior of a well-designed isolation sys-

Low-frequency regime (f < 8.8 Hz): At certain frequencies in this range,
the transmissibility exceeds unity, indicating amplification. This behavior is
characteristic of isolation systems operating below their effective natural fre-
quency, where the entire assembly undergoes rigid-body and coupled motions.
The prominent peak observed at approximately 8.65 Hz corresponds to a nat-
ural mode of the complete assembly, as confirmed by modal analysis. While
the theoretical natural frequency of individual isolators was calculated to be
3.5 Hz, the actual complete system behavior extends the amplification region
due to coupling effects between the distributed mass of the cavity-frame assem-
bly and the overall system stiffness. This amplification is acceptable as these
low-frequency oscillations do not excite the critical structural resonances of the
cavity.

Isolation regime (f > 8.8 Hz): In the isolation regime, the transmissibility
consistently remains below unity, confirming effective vibration isolation. The
transition from a mixed regime (characterized by both amplification and attenu-
ation) to pure attenuation occurs at 8.8 Hz, indicating that the isolation system
successfully decouples the cavity from external vibrations in the frequency range

the most challenging condition for the cavity assembly during transportation,
which aligns with the focus of ASTM D4169 on vertical testing.

5.5 Transient response (shock analysis)
A transient structural analysis was conducted to evaluate the system response
under shock loading conditions. According to [1], based on the MIL-STD-810H
Method 516.8 procedure II, the worst-case on-road transportation shock profile
is a terminal peak sawtooth wave with 11 ms duration and 7.6 g peak accelera-
tion.
 However, previous transportation campaigns of similar cavities monitored
by INFN Milano have recorded acceleration peaks up to 10–12 g, which exceed
the standard specification. To ensure adequate safety margins and account for
these observed conditions, a conservative shock profile with a maximum peak
acceleration of 12 g was adopted for the analysis. This conservative approach
provides additional confidence in the system’s ability to withstand real-world
transportation conditions that may exceed standard specifications.
 The shock loading was applied in each of the three principal directions (verti-
cal, longitudinal, and transverse) to comprehensively assess the system response.
The analysis utilized shock stiffness values provided by the isolator manufacturer
to accurately model the system behavior under transient loading conditions.

5.5.1 Analysis objective and approach
The analysis aimed to investigate the dynamic behavior of the assembly, with
particular attention to the response of the antenna when subjected to shock
loading conditions. We are particularly interested in the displacement and stress
of the antenna, but also the displacement of the entire cavity to estimate a
maximum envelope in which the cavity remains during vibrations.
 Performing a full transient analysis of the complete model is computationally
prohibitive due to several factors:

 • The full assembly contains a very large number of nodes because it consists
 of many complex components that must be modeled accurately.
 • Accurate stress evaluation in the coupler requires extremely fine mesh
 discretization, further increasing the node count.

 • Transient shock analysis requires very small time steps to capture high-
 frequency responses, resulting in enormous computational times.

 Given the limited time available for the analysis, a two-stage approach was
adopted to efficiently assess the structural response:

Stage 1 - Transient analysis of the frame: A transient analysis was per-
formed on the supporting frame structure, with the cavity and coupler repre-
sented as lumped masses. This simplified model allowed for the determination
of acceleration transfer functions from the outer frame to the attachment points
on the inner frame where the cavity is mounted, providing the maximum accel-
eration levels transmitted to the cavity assembly.

Stage 2 - Static equivalent analysis: The maximum acceleration values
obtained from the transient frame analysis were subsequently applied as static
equivalent loads in a static analyses of the cavity-coupler assembly.

This approach enabled the evaluation of stress distributions and structural be-
havior of the critical components under the most severe loading conditions, while
maintaining computational feasibility.

5.5.2 Stage 1 - Transient analysis of the frame
The coordinate system used in this analysis is the same as the one in the random
vibration analysis:
 • the longitudinal axis (x), aligned with the cavity axis,

 • the lateral axis (y), perpendicular to the cavity axis and pointing sideways,
 • the vertical axis (z), oriented upward and normal to the support plane.

 30

Six separate shock analyses were performed by applying acceleration inputs
along each principal axis in both directions.
 Unlike the previous analyses (modal and random vibration), where gravity
could be neglected since it only shifts the equilibrium position without affecting
natural frequencies or transmissibility, in this case gravity must be included to
correctly evaluate the actual response envelope. To account for the initial static
displacement of the cavity due to gravity, the transient analysis was carried out
in three steps:

  1. Step 1 (static preload): Application of gravitational load with time
 integration disabled, in order to establish the initial equilibrium configu-
 ration for the subsequent shock analysis.
  2. Step 2 (dummy transient): Time integration was enabled, but no
 additional loads were applied. This step ensures that the static solution
 from Step 1 is correctly transferred as the initial condition for the dynamic
 analysis, avoiding spurious transient effects due to initialization.
  3. Step 3 (transient shock): Application of the actual shock pulse on the
 system, starting from the initial condition obtained in the previous steps.

The durations of the first two steps were 1 s and 2 s, respectively. The third step,
corresponding to the transient shock, was set with the following parameters:

 • Step end time: 3.15 s (0.15 s from the start of this step)
 • Initial time step: 0.01 ms

 • Minimum time step: 0.001 ms
 • Maximum time step: 0.25 ms
 • Auto time stepping: enabled

 The shock pulse was defined as a triangular acceleration profile, with a linear
rise to a peak value of 12 g over 11 ms, followed by an instantaneous drop to
zero at 11.001 ms.
 The simulations show that, after the frame is subjected to the shock, the
cavity is displaced until it reaches a maximum deflection, at which point the
motion reverses due to the elastic recovery of the isolators. This reversal occurs
approximately 50 ms after the start of the simulation. Therefore the total sim-
ulation time of 150 ms chosen for Step 3 fully capture the relevant post-shock
behavior of the assembly.

Displacement envelope analysis
A key aspect of the system design is the determination of the maximum dis-
placement envelope within which the cavity assembly moves during dynamic
excitation. This analysis is essential for defining the required clearances.
 Due to the isolation system design, the cavity’s dynamic response is domi-
nated by the characteristics of the isolators. The cavity behaves as a rigid body,
moving in unison with the inner frame structure. This rigid-body assumption
is justified because:

 • The coupling between the cavity and the inner frame is sufficiently rigid
 compared to the isolator stiffness.
 • The only permitted mode is the rigid motion of the inner frame, together
 with the cavity and coupler, relative to the outer frame.
 Consequently, the displacement response of the inner frame alone provides
an accurate representation of the cavity’s motion envelope. The maximum dis-
placement values obtained from the frame analysis closely approximate those
of the complete inner frame-cavity system, since the cavity moves as a rigid
extension of the inner frame during the dominant response modes.
 This analysis enables the definition of a maximum operational volume that
bounds the cavity’s excursion under the most severe oscillation conditions.

6 Conclusion
A transport system has been designed to carry 650 MHz superconducting radio
frequency cavities and high power couplers for both road and air transportation.

 35

Modal analysis identified the coupler antenna as the critical component with a
natural frequency of 38 Hz. The two-frame configuration with M8-260-08 wire
rope isolators provides 82% vibration attenuation at this critical frequency while
maintaining structural integrity under transportation loads.
 The analysis indicates that the design meets the specified isolation and shock
mitigation requirements. The complete assembly fits within the S-14460 ship-
ping crate specifications and stress analysis confirms that all components remain
below their yield strengths during transport conditions.
 Future work includes a more detailed analysis of the simulation results to
further characterize the vibration response and stress distribution under vary-
ing transport scenarios. In addition, the effectiveness of alternative solutions
for connecting the frame to the cavity, such as the collar-based attachment sys-
tem shown in Figure 16, will be evaluated. Finally, the transport frame will
be constructed, and validation testing will be performed with a mock-up cav-
ity assembly to verify the design performance before transporting the actual
superconducting cavities.
 This comprehensive approach ensures system reliability and provides confi-
dence in the safe transportation of these sensitive accelerator components.
