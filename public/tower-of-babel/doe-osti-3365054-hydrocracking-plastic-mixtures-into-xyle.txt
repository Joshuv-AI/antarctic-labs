Public Executive Summary
Our research aims to tackle the challenge in recycling end-of-life plastics, the
accumulation of which has become a grand challenge to our society, sustainability and
economy. Guided by life-cycle assessment (LCA) and tech-economic analysis (TEA), we
specifically target the catalytic conversion of low-cost #3-7 plastic mixtures ($5/ton) into
p-xylene (>$1000/ton), one of the most valuable hydrocarbon products. This goal is
achieved by carrying out integrated research including catalysis, process engineering, and
reactor design. The ultimate goal of this project is to enable energy-efficient and
economically viable depolymerization of end-of-life plastic mixtures into value-added
commodity chemicals.

2. Results and Discussion

2.1 Systematic study of the conversion of HDPE to BTX
2.1.1 Effect of H2 concentration
The effect of H2 molar fraction in the carrier gas was studied by mixing the typical 5%
H2/N2 carrier gas with pure H2 in varying ratios to achieve the desired overall concentration
of H2, between 5% and 40%, while keeping all other process parameters fixed. As
discussed in chapter 2, based on the known mechanisms of hydrocracking,
(de)hydrogenation, and hydrogen transfer reactions that occur on ZSM5, altering the
available amount of gas-phase H2 should have an effect on the reaction products for both
kinetic and thermodynamic reasons. The increasing partial pressure of H2 results in
increasing competitive adsorption with hydrocarbon fragments on the acidic sites of ZSM5,
as the Sabatier principle dictates that increasing H2 partial pressure shifts the equilibrium
of H towards the adsorbed state. This affects all reaction steps which require C-H bond
activation, including (de)hydrogenation, oligomerization/cracking, isomerization, and
cyclization, which will have the overall effect of slowing down the reaction and lowering
the BTX yield. Increasing H2 partial pressure also shifts the equilibrium product mixture
towards more saturated products because all dehydrogenation and cyclization steps result
in the generation of one H2 molecule, also as predicted by the Sabatier principle.
Indeed, we do observe these results as predicted. With increasing H2 partial pressure, we
observe a steady decrease in the BTX yield and selectivity with a concomitant increase in
the paraffin and olefin yields. The coke yield is also observed to increase with H2 partial
pressure. This result is not as predicted, as the competitive adsorption of H2 should decrease
the excessive extent of condensation reactions that proceed coke buildup.

2.1.2 Effect of catalyst temperature
The catalyst temperature was found to significantly affect the product selectivity and yield.
Although, the BTX selectivity is quite similar regardless of catalyst temperature (~50%,)
the light hydrocarbon product selectivity appears to be significantly dependent on the
catalyst temperature, and the ratio of B/T/X is affected as well. With increasing catalyst
temperature, the amount of benzene in relation to toluene and xylenes increases, likely due
to the increasing conversion of the latter two compounds via transalkylation or
disproportionation reactions. And although the olefin selectivity increases with
temperature, so too does the methane selectivity. This indicates increased dehydrogenation
activity, particularly of C2 and C3, with increasing temperature. However, beta-scission to
C1 also appears to be accelerated.

2.1.3 Effect of pressure
Increasing pressure is expected to increase the overall molecular weight of the reaction
products. We can control the pressure in the reactor by using a backpressure regulator
located downstream of the reactor outlet. We systematically studied three pressures: 1, 2,
and 3 atm (absolute pressure.) As the pressure increases, the overall yield of C1-C8 products
decreases, in agreement with our prediction. Heavier products are formed as pressure is
increased, as indicated by our GC results, we see peaks occurring at retention times after
BTX. But because these peaks are unknown species, their quantification is difficult.
Regardless, the remaining mass balance is informative and confirms the decrease in light
products in favor of heavier ones. An increase in coke is also observed as condensation
reactions become more favored as well.
The increase in system pressure has been found to decrease the rate of volatilization and
rate of double bond formation in the pyrolysis of HDPE, with the effect of pressure
increasing at higher temperature. Both of those trends have a negative effect on our reaction
performance and BTX because they would result in slower catalytic kinetics. Slow
volatilization would likely result in larger molecules reaching the catalyst surface,

                                                               Page 10 of 35                             AR-370-07.14

requiring more cracking reactions to occur before the intermediates enter the zeolite pores,
and a decrease in double bonds of the pyrolysis products would require slower protolytic
cracking rather than beta-scission cracking.
Meanwhile, the catalytic reactions will also be affected by increasing pressure. The BTX
selectivity may decrease in favor of naphthalenics, and the increased pressure may increase
the rate of protolytic cracking. Additionally, the reactions of aromatic formation from
linear molecules involve the release of several mols of H2 per mol of aromatics. The large
release of H2 will be suppressed at increasing pressures.

2.2.1 Conversion of LDPE to BTX
It may be tempting to directly compare LDPE with HDPE, considering that they both share
the same monomer ethylene. However, as their full names imply, the difference is in their
densities. This is not simply a difference in their physical properties, but also in their
molecular structure. The low density of LDPE is the result of increased branching, so
molecules of LDPE are not simply long, straight hydrocarbon chains, but also contain side
chains, resulting in a bulkier molecule giving rise to lower density compared to HDPE,
where molecules without side chains can pack more closely together. Compared with
HDPE, the BTX yield from the conversion of LDPE is higher at 65%. Branch points
(tertiary carbons) in the LDPE molecular structure act as weak points compared with the
linear (secondary carbons) which make up HDPE during the thermal pyrolysis process.
This may result in the formation of lower molecular weight compounds during LDPE
pyrolysis compared with HDPE, which will assist in the catalytic conversion of the
intermediates.

2.2.2 Conversion of PP to BTX
The conversion of PP results in the lowest BTX yield at 42% and increased C1-C4 yields

                                                     Page 12 of 35                                    AR-370-07.14

compared with the other virgin polymers. This decrease in selectivity can be attributed to
the presence of methyl groups in PP, which are preserved in the pyrolysis products. For
example, it has been shown that cis-2-butene has higher selectivity towards aromatics than
isobutene on HZSM5 at conversions ranging from 0-75% at 530°C. HZSM5 was found to
have poor selectivity towards n-butenes in the isomerization of isobutene. And in the
conversion of butane and isobutane over HZSM5 at 550°C and at ~36% conversions, it
was found that isobutane produced about twice as many moles of methane as n-butane.
Isobutane was more selective towards propene and butenes, while n-butane was more
selective towards ethane, ethylene, and propane. Additionally, in our experiments, we
found that isobutane has no conversion to BTX at 500°C on HZSM5, while butane
produced a BTX yield of 2.8% under the same conditions. Using 0.05IrZSM5, the BTX
yield from isobutane increased to 1.5% while the yield from n-butane was 2.6%. The
selectivity shifted from toluene over HZSM5 to xylene over 0.05IrZSM5. All of these
observations suggest that more-branched reactants (alkanes or alkenes) result in lower
aromatic yield compared to linear compounds.

2.2.3 Conversion of PS to BTX
The most obviously different result is that for PS, which exhibits extremely high selectivity
towards BTX, as well as the styrene monomer. The yield of BTXS (BTX plus styrene) is
the highest of all plastics at 87%. This is due to the stability of the aromatic groups, which
maintain their aromatic functionality through the pyrolysis and catalytic reactions.
However, it also has the highest coke yield of 9%. Both of these results can be attributes to
the presence of aromatics groups in the PS polymer. Coke will result from condensation
reactions among the aromatic groups, while BTX theoretically can be formed from the
scission of the ethenyl branch of styrene which produces ethylene or methane. This
explains the particularly high yield of benzene (52%) and toluene (19%), while the xylene
yield is only 1.5%. The formation of xylene would require additional isomerization or
methyl transfer reactions. However, this mechanism would result in a methane/toluene
ratio of ~1 and ethylene/benzene ratio of ~1, assuming no further reactions occur after the
C-C scission. The actual methane/toluene ratio is ~0.7 while the ethylene/benzene ratio is
~0.1, so clearly there are other reactions at play here. The discrepancy may result from
benzene and toluene formation coming from a more complex set of reactions during coke
formation rather than simple scission of C-C bonds in styrene. The C1-C4 yield is very low
<5%.

2.3 Studies on the conversion of waste plastic to BTX
Real post-consumer plastic waste samples were collected from the Baltimore County
Material Recovery Facility (MRF) located in Towson, Maryland. The MRF facility accepts
single-stream recycling from county residents and sorts them into various streams
including paper, metals, and plastics. PET, colorless HDPE, and colored HDPE are
positively identified and sorted by a combination of automatic and manual means, and are
ultimately baled and sold to plastic recyclers. Other plastics and any PET or HDPE that are
not positively identified are considered to be waste and are disposed of in the adjacent
landfill, referred to here as “reject plastic.” Thus, three types of waste plastics were

                                      Page 13 of 35                              AR-370-07.14

collected for use in our system:
 1. Colorless HDPE (MRF CL HDPE)
 2. Colored HDPE (MRF C HDPE)
 3. Reject plastic mixture (MRF Reject Mix.) By manual sorting, the following
 composition by mass of the reject mixture was determined: 9.8% PET, 32.1%
 HDPE, 32.6% PP, and 25.5% other or unidentified plastic.

2.3.1 Thermogravimetric analysis of plastic waste samples
Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed with the three MRF plastic types and
compared with virgin HDPE. The TGA revealed that the different types of plastic exhibited
different decomposition behaviors and resulted in different amount of residual mass
leftover, even after reaching 600°C. The decomposition behavior of virgin HDPE and MRF
colorless HDPE was similar, aside from the amount of residual mass. MRF colored HDPE
and MRF reject plastic featured broader mass loss curves in the differential TGA and lower
temperature of peak mass loss. The residual mass fraction leftover for the four plastic types
was in the order of MRF reject (7.5%) > MRF colored HDPE (6.3%) > MRF colorless
HDPE (2.9%) > virgin HDPE (0.6%.) The unevaporated mass was also observed during
the catalytic upcycling experiments. In the following sections, the residual mass
determined during TGA is excluded in the calculation of yields because it is not a
polymeric material that could contribute to the hydrocarbon mass balance, discussed more
below.

2.4 ASPEN Plus Process Simulation, Technoeconomic Analysis, and Life Cycle
Assessment
A process model of the plastic catalytic upcycling process using the ASPEN Plus program.
The plastic feedstock was modeled as C50 while the wax was modeled as C18. The pyrolysis
and catalytic reactions were modeled using two separate stages of reactors, both of which
are yield reactors, meaning the reaction inputs and outputs are directly programed by the
user. The yields were based on the experimental results in the kg/day scale reactor
presented here. These reactors lack rigorous reaction dynamics but still allow for the
quantification of heat and mass balances. A recirculation loop was also implemented for
recirculating the light C1-C4 products after the reaction.

Life cycle assessment (LCA) was also performed in order to compare the carbon footprint
of our plastic upcycling process for producing BTX versus the traditional naphtha
reforming process. There are important benefits of the plastic upcycling process that are
beneficial to its carbon footprint. Firstly, upcycling plastic to BTX will offset the carbon
emissions related to existing end-of-life plastic treatment, namely landfilling, incineration,
and recycling. These processes emit 0.1, 1.3, and 0.9 kg CO2/kg plastic, respectively.
Weighing the carbon emissions by the fraction of plastic treated by each end-of-life process
results in a weighted average of 0.36 kg CO2/kg plastic, since most plastic in the US is
disposed of by landfilling. We can convert these avoided emissions by considering our
yield of BTX is assumed to be 75%, so this results in saving 0.48 kg CO2eq/kg BTX.
Second, we also do not consider the emissions related to the initial production, distribution,
usage, etc. of the plastic waste, since plastic waste is considered to be a waste product is
not primarily used for the production of BTX.

2.5 Preliminary Pilot Plant Design, Mass Balance, and Energy Balance
Consultant engineers at Process Engineering Associates were hired to assist in the
development of the preliminary 100 kg/day pilot plant process flow diagram as well as the
associated mass and energy balances. A summary of their assessment and suggestions is
summarized below:
Catalytic Reactor Design Options
The same palette of gas/solid pyrolysis reactor options also holds for the catalytic reactor,
though in this case the current fixed bed arrangement with gas in the downflow direction
appears to be best for the next phase of scale-up due to its simplicity. The size and number
of reactors in parallel will be determined by the decoking and catalyst regeneration
procedure relative to the future optimization of conditions to prolong catalyst life.

A fixed bed reactor has been shown to convert HDPE pyrolysis products to high olefin
content products using high acidity HZSM-5 type catalyst. Tailoring the catalyst to

                                           Page 29 of 35                                       AR-370-07.14

maximize aromatic production is expected to be similarly successful.

The current catalyst life is on the order of 4 hours of operation, and the current catalyst
regeneration procedure takes close to that time as well. If no improvement in regeneration
or catalyst life occurs, two reactors (one in standby, one in regen mode) will likely be
required for each reactor in operation to guarantee uninterrupted continuous operation. If
the regen time can be shortened relative to the coking time, then a two-reactor operation
can be considered. An alternative to this would be to simply flare the pyrolysis gas
generated during any gap in catalytic reactor operation, assuming this would be an
acceptable alternative.

Experimental work shows that hydrogen is produced as a byproduct of the aromatization
reactions, similar to catalytic reforming reactions. The hydrogen is a valuable byproduct
and can be used to supplant purchased H2 in the pyrolysis reaction and, if generated in
sufficient quantity, a profit source once purified.

There will likely be a need to purify the pyrolysis gas before it is introduced to the catalytic
reactor once mixed plastic feeds are used, as these could generate sulfur, nitrogen, and
chlorine containing byproducts that poison the catalyst. Space for one or more adsorbent
beds should be included between the pyrolysis and catalytic reactors.

The sensible (including latent) heats as well as heats of reaction for the feeds and reactions
were estimated in the previous section. The heat required is provided by an electric
resistance heater encasing the tubular reactor sections. Neither the type nor size of reactors
for the next phase of development are known yet, but they will need to meet the minimum
heat flux currently seen at the lab scale to ensure isothermal operation.

OSBL Equipment Considerations
Aside from the Inside Battery Limit (ISBL) equipment (plastics feed vessel, gas heater,
reactors, condenser, and piping) there are a number of Outside Battery Limit (OSBL)
systems that need to be considered depending on the level of product processing and
recycling desired in the next phase of pilot work. These are discussed below.

Nitrogen
Nitrogen can be supplied from cylinders as has been done at the lab scale, but at a larger
scale a dedicated liquid nitrogen tank with periodic delivery may prove to be more
economical. The projected nitrogen consumption is 230 kg/day assuming 24-hour
operation, but that is for a scaled up fixed bed reactor. For a version of a fluidized bed
reactor the consumption is likely higher.

Hydrogen

                                      Page 30 of 35                                AR-370-07.14

Hydrogen could continue to be supplied from cylinders for the next phase. Given the fact
that more than enough hydrogen is generated in the catalytic reactor for this purpose, it
may make economic sense to recycle hydrogen. The level of hydrogen purity required for
recycle has not been determined. If recycled hydrogen is not an option and cylinder
hydrogen is too expensive, electrolytic hydrogen could be considered.

Regeneration Air Supply
A 100-fold increase in the laboratory regeneration air flow translates to about 3.5 scfm.
This should be within the capability of most utility systems (depending on where the pilot
facility will be built), but if not, air compression and drying will be required. It will also
likely require a dedicated heater to ensure the required regeneration temperature can be
maintained. To avoid a temperature excursion during regeneration, air is often diluted
with nitrogen, in which case the nitrogen consumption for the pilot plant will increase and
be taken into account in the system design.

Reactor Product Separation
A mixture of products leaves the catalytic reactor in the vapor state and needs to be
separated. Compounds heavier than benzene are easily condensed and purified.
Compounds in the C2-C5 range, particularly olefins, are the building blocks of aromatic
compounds and need to be recycled to increase the yield. Low molecular hydrocarbons
such as ethane and methane do not appear to contribute to aromatic yield and in fact may
increase coking. However, these gases are mixed with hydrogen, which is valuable beyond
its use as a fuel. For these reasons some manner of distillation to separate and purify these
various compounds may be desirable in the next phase of pilot work. Depending on the
level of purification desired, a packed distillation column with bottom and overhead liquid
products and an overhead vapor product may be sufficient. The overhead vapor could be
further separated into a hydrogen rich stream for recycle by pressure swing adsorption,
leaving a methane/ethane rich stream for use as a fuel or flaring.

Aromatics Separation
If separation of aromatics is required, a three-column distillation system is typically used.
The first column separates benzene as a side stream with a benzene-contaminated light
vapor stream overhead that is typically recycled to the reactor. Toluene is taken off the top
of the 2nd column, while xylenes are taken off the top of the third column with heavy
aromatics leaving the bottom. This unit operation is not likely required for the pilot plant.

Intermediate and Final Product Storage
Buffer tanks are recommended to hold intermediate liquid products (condensed LPG for
recycle, mixed aromatics, etc) to handle interruptions in continuous production. These
should be sized for 1-2 days of production (approximately 10-20 gallons)

                                      Page 31 of 35                              AR-370-07.14

Final product storage tanks may be required either for mixed or separated products. These
should hold approximately one week of product (roughly 20 gallons for benzene and
toluene each, 10 gallons for xylenes, and 20 gallons for heavy aromatics).

Flare System
The pilot unit should have a flare system to handle routine off gases and regen gases, gases
generated during upset conditions, as well as collection of any liquid products introduced
into the flare lines.

3. Project Outcome

Technical Achievements:
 • Efficient catalytic hydrocracking process is developed to convert various types of
 plastics, including #3-7 mixtures, into BTX aromatics.
 • All technical milestones have been met in the project, including >60% single-pass
 yield and >80% multi-pass yield of BTX.
 • New catalysts are developed that can sustain operations online for several hours
 before regeneration is needed.
 • Based on the catalytic performance achieved, a FEL 2.5 plant is designed in the
 dual-bed configuration to avoid expensive FCC type of design with real-time
 catalyst regeneration loop.
 • The main lesson learned from the technical work is that zeolite-based catalysts still
 are subject to coking and require regeneration. It makes the plant design
 complicated even at reduced regeneration frequencies. This is mainly due to the
 strong Brosteid acid sites present in zeolites which intend to cause coking for
 hydrocarbon processing. It is suggested that alternative catalysts without such
 strong acidity may be able to survive elongated plastics upcycling operations
 without being subject to coking.

T2M:
  • The PI co-founded CUPTech to commercialize the technologies developed in this
 project. The founders have developed pilot plant design (FEL 2.5) and secured
 partners (SwRI) for the pilot plant test. Currently the company is working on
 fundraising for the pilot project, including proposals pending at DOE.
  • The main lesson from this project for T2M is the challenge to penetrate the well-
 established supply chain of hydrocarbon chemicals. The T2M team of our project
 has spent extensive efforts on talking with petrochemical industries and commodity
 chemical brokers for BTX, but they are not interested in considering small streams
 of aromatics from distributed plastics refineries. It turns out alternative products
 such as diesel or gasoline that still have high value but can be combusted locally
 may have better marketing opportunities. The PI is currently developing alternative
 catalysts that redirect the products toward liquid fuels. One potential target of
 interest is to make ~C12 alkanes. Plus the aromatics we have obtained, we can make
 blends of synthetic aviation fuel (SAF) which has high market value and more open
 supply chains as of today.
