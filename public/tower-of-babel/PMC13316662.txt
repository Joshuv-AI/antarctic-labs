Ultra-widefield optical coherence tomography demonstrates subtle vitreoretinal interface changes driving conversion of retinoschisis to retinal detachment
Authors: Alberto Quarta, Rouzbeh Abbasgholizadeh, Saghar Bagheri, SriniVas R Sadda, Michael S Ip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a case of retinoschisis associated with retinal detachment, documented with ultra-widefield optical coherence tomography (OCT), and to highlight subtle vitreoretinal interface changes not previously described.MethodsA patient with retinoschisis was evaluated using multimodal imaging, including ultra-widefield OCT. Serial OCT scans were reviewed to assess structural alterations of the outer and inner retinal layers, vitreoretinal interface, and hyaloid status.ResultsUltra-widefield OCT demonstrated the development of a retinal detachment secondary to an isolated outer retinal hole within the region of retinoschisis, occurring in the context of traction from a thickened inner retinoschisis leaf, without evidence of communication with an inner retinal hole. Additionally, undulations on the inner surface of the outer leaf of the retinoschisis cavity were observed possibly representing residua of a tractional separation between the outer and inner leaf of the retinoschisis cavity. These subtle features were only appreciable through the extended coverage of ultra-widefield OCT imaging.ConclusionUltra-widefield OCT provided critical structural insights into the progression from retinoschisis to a retinal detachment. It revealed the development of an isolated outer retinal hole with associated inner leaf traction and undulations on the inner surface of the outer leaf of the retinoschisis cavity without detectable inner leaf holes, underscoring OCT's value in detecting and better understanding subtle vitreoretinal changes in this condition.Keywords: UWF OCT, Retinoschisis, Vitreoretinal interface

Introduction

Retinoschisis is characterized by a splitting of the neurosensory retina typically at the outer plexiform layer and is often considered a benign entity that remains stable or slowly progresses over time. However, in a minority of cases retinoschisis can evolve into a retinal detachment (schisis-detachment) posing diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.1 Ip et al., first described the OCT features that can unequivocally differentiate retinoschisis from true retinal detachment and OCT has become a key tool in differentiating retinoschisis from true retinal detachment.2 However, conventional OCT is largely limited to the posterior pole which restricts its ability to easily and reliably monitor peripheral retinal changes or tractional components outside of the macula.3 Advances in ultra-widefield (UWF) OCT now permit imaging of the peripheral retina and vitreoretinal interface over a broader angle, facilitating identification of peripheral holes, traction, and early subretinal fluid extension.4

In this report, we present a case of retinoschisis progressing to retinal detachment captured by UWF OCT scans. Our observations include an isolated outer leaf retinal hole under traction without an inner leaf break, and undulations on the inner surface of the outer leaf of the retinoschisis cavity reminiscent of spontaneous internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling. These findings may highlight the potential of UWF OCT for revealing subtle changes that may be critically important for understanding the pathophysiology of schisis-to-retinal detachment progression and for guiding intervention.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old male presented with new flashes and floaters in the right eye. Best-corrected visual acuity was 20/20 in both eyes. Anterior segment examination was unremarkable. UWF pseudocolor fundus photographs (Optos Silverstone; Optos plc, Dunfermline, Scotland, UK) of the affected eye showed temporal retinoschisis with a smooth, dome-shaped elevation of the retina and indistinct demarcation between schitic and normal retina (Fig. 1A). The lesion had the typical transparent, cystic appearance of retinoschisis; however, no obvious retinal break or localized detachment could be visualized on color imaging alone or by indirect ophthalmoscopy with scleral indentation. Swept-source UWF-OCT (DREAM OCT, Intalight) 26 mm B-scans passing through the schisis front demonstrated the splitting of the neurosensory retina at the outer plexiform layer with marked intraretinal separation. Within this cavity, an outer retinal hole was clearly visualized, allowing subretinal fluid accumulation consistent with a localized retinal detachment (Fig. 1B and C). Importantly, there was no inner retinal hole, confirming that the detachment developed solely from the outer layer under the influence of inner leaf traction. OCT also revealed a thickened inner retinoschisis leaf exerting traction on the outer retinal hole (Fig. 1D and E). Furthermore, the OCT scans identified fine peripheral undulations on the inner surface of the outer leaf of the retinoschisis cavity (Fig. 1E, arrowheads), similar to the inner retinal dimples previously reported following macular surgery. Based on the identification of an isolated outer retinal hole with localized subretinal fluid on UWF-OCT, the patient was treated with laser retinopexy. A posterior vitreous detachment was already present at presentation. Unfortunately, the patient did not come back for subsequent follow-up visits, and no additional OCT scans were available. OCT findings informed clinical decision making, as the demonstration of an outer retinal hole associated with localized subretinal fluid and tractional changes supported treatment rather than observation.

Discussion

Retinoschisis is a relatively common peripheral retinal condition generally regarded as benign and slowly progressive, remaining stable in the majority of patients with complications occurring primarily in eyes with outer layer holes.5 In our case, UWF-OCT revealed the precise sequence of events: an isolated outer retinal hole developed under traction from a thickened inner retinoschisis leaf leading to localized subretinal fluid accumulation and retinal detachment despite the absence of an inner retinal break or hole. Although no inner retinal hole was identified on the available OCT sections, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that a small inner retinal hole was present but not captured within the scanned planes. The literature describing posterior or large outer-layer breaks producing schisis-detachment further supports our mechanism.1 Byer's long-term cohorts documented the frequency of outer layer holes and clarified when schisis-detachment can arise guiding the traditionally conservative management of uncomplicated lesions.5 Prior reports catalogued the surgical dilemmas posed by extensive posterior outer layer breaks and the variable success of buckling, cryotherapy, or vitrectomy. However these prior reports, lacked peripheral cross-sectional OCT to show how traction from the inner leaf may precipitate the event.6,7 In our case, UWF OCT delineated a thickened, taut inner leaf spanning the extent of the schitic cavity, imparting presumed focal traction at the outer leaf hole. These interface details are typically not visible on pseudocolor UWF fundus photography which cannot resolve the anatomy that may determine whether a schisis remains stable or progresses to a detachment.8 More recent work directly argues that UWF-OCT deepens anatomic understanding of peripheral lesions and improves early recognition of sight-threatening changes that may be missed on ophthalmoscopy or color imaging alone.8 UWF-OCT imaging in this case extends this concept by allowing visualization of a non-communicating outer leaf hole with progressive subretinal fluid, and by documenting the inner leaf configuration—a tractional driver long hypothesized but seldom captured in vivo across the periphery. In at least one prior reported case, vitreous traction may have precipitated progression of retinoschisis into a retinal detachment.2 OCT-based descriptions of posterior vitreous detachment and vitreomacular traction reinforce the primacy of OCT for staging the status of inner leaf attachment and detecting subtle, partial PVD or adherent cortex that are difficult to appreciate clinically.9 Another aspect of our case is the presence of undulations on the inner surface of the outer leaf in the schitic region. Histologically, some Müller cell columns remain partially intact or as “stretched bridges” across the schisis cavity.1 These bridges act like micro-anchors or hinges connecting discrete points between the two leaves. The inner traction pulls these Müller columns taut. The outer leaf, tethered intermittently by these bridges, may be compressed between anchoring points producing local buckling between attachments. This periodic tethering may generate sinusoidal waveforms or undulations along the outer leaf, a physical pattern of differential stress relief between the bridges likely caused by asymmetric stress distribution after retinal splitting. Clinically, this may identify a “stress concentration zone”, a region at high risk of further structural deformation or potential retinal break development, particularly an outer leaf hole, which can allow liquefied vitreous to enter the subretinal space and cause retinal detachment.

OCT studies of myopic traction maculopathy depict stepwise evolution from retinoschisis to localized detachment when tractional forces persist, lending a mechanistic parallel to our observations.10 Extrapolating this paradigm our case suggests that peripheral traction from an inner leaf can analogously convert a stable retinoschisis to retinal detachment via an outer leaf breach. Retinoschisis can be stable and the appearance of sinusoidal ONL folds or undulations may suggest a dynamic phase in which the tissue may be responding elastically to ongoing deformation making the schisis mechanically active. Further studies should investigate if patients with such undulations should be followed closely for changes in schisis contour, height, or associated outer retinal breaks as this may precede progression toward schisis detachment.

Thus, UWF OCT can help clinicians in defining subtle peripheral signatures, outer leaf holes under traction, preretinal cortical strands, and ONL undulations. Recognizing these may allow better risk stratification and management of cases with retinoschisis or schisis-detachment.
