Certified and accurate computation of function space norms of deep neural networks
Johannes Gründler, Moritz Maibaum, Philipp Petersen

Abstract
Neural network methods for PDEs require reliable error control in function space norms. However, trained neural networks can typically only be probed at a finite number of point values. Without strong assumptions, point evaluations alone do not provide enough information to derive tight deterministic and guaranteed bounds on function space norms. In this work, we move beyond a purely black-box setting and exploit the neural network structure directly. We present a framework for the certified and accurate computation of integral quantities of neural networks, including Lebesgue and Sobolev norms, by combining interval arithmetic enclosures on axis-aligned boxes with adaptive marking/refinement and quadrature-based aggregation. On each box, we compute guaranteed lower and upper bounds for function values and derivatives, and propagate these local certificates to global lower and upper bounds for the target integrals. Our analysis provides a general convergence theorem for such certified adaptive quadrature procedures and instantiates it for function values, Jacobians, and Hessians, yielding certified computation of L p L^{p} , W 1 , p W^{1,p} , and W 2 , p W^{2,p} norms. We further show how these ingredients lead to practical certified bounds for PINN interior residuals. Numerical experiments illustrate the accuracy and practical behavior of the proposed methods.



Keywords:
deep neural networks; Sobolev norms; certified integration; interval arithmetic; adaptive refinement; PINNs

Introduction

Deep neural networks have become a standard tool across engineering, computer science, and applied mathematics, driven by their empirical success and their ability to approximate high-dimensional nonlinear maps
[
18
]
. In recent years, they have also been used extensively for the numerical solution of partial differential equations (PDEs), most prominently in physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)
[
19
]
and related approaches such as deep Galerkin methods and operator learning
[
27
,
14
,
13
]
.

From the viewpoint of numerical analysis, these methods suggest a paradigm that can complement and sometimes replace classical discretizations such as finite element methods (FEMs) based on spline spaces
[
28
]
. In the neural-network-based setting, the ansatz space is no longer a hand-crafted finite-dimensional space, but instead a trainable class of functions. Crucially, for many applications, it is essential that these methods be complemented with
reliable and certifiable error control
. A natural approach, rooted in PDE theory and numerical analysis, is to measure errors in function space norms and to bound residual quantities through integrals of derivatives. For spline-based methods, such quantities can typically be accessed and computed directly
[
2
,
1
,
28
]
.

In comparison, neural networks are much more of a black box: after training, they are essentially available only as
queryable
objects, i.e., one can evaluate a neural network (and, via automatic differentiation, its derivatives) at chosen points, but global information in function space norms remains inaccessible.
Current practice in neural-network-based PDE solvers relies predominantly on pointwise evaluations on (random) samples, and error assessment is therefore typically phrased in probabilistic terms (e.g. via statistical learning theory arguments such as Rademacher complexity bounds), yielding guarantees only “with high probability”
[
21
,
17
]
.
Moreover, it has been shown that pointwise sampling information alone can be fundamentally insufficient to obtain certified uniform bounds or function space norm information because neural networks can represent highly localized functions; see, e.g.,
[
11
]
.

In this work, we address this challenge by enabling certified and accurate computation of integrals and function space norms of neural network functions. Concretely, we seek to compute (higher-order) Sobolev norms of neural networks. We provide a precise description of our contributions and results in the next section.

1.1
Our contribution

Our contribution is a framework for the certified computation of integrals of trained neural networks on bounded domains. Specifically, our goal is to turn a neural network from a purely
queryable
object into one for which
certified
quantitative information in function space norms can be computed. To this end, we combine (i) local interval-arithmetic-based enclosures for a neural network and its derivatives on axis-aligned boxes, (ii) adaptive refinement schemes that mark and refine boxes with large uncertainties, and (iii) quadrature rules that aggregate local certificates into global bounds.

More precisely, for a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a differential operator
\mathcal{D}
, a differentiable function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, and
1\leq p<\infty
,
we aim to compute
\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{D}f(x)|^{p}\,dx
.
We achieve this by adaptively constructing a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
\Omega
and, for each
K\in\mathcal{P}
, upper and lower bounds
\overline{|\mathcal{D}f(K)|^{p}}
,
\underline{|\mathcal{D}f(K)|^{p}}
such that
\underline{|\mathcal{D}f(K)|^{p}}\leq|\mathcal{D}f(x)|^{p}\leq\overline{|\mathcal{D}f(K)|^{p}}
for every
x\in K
. This implies

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}}\underline{|\mathcal{D}f(K)|^{p}}\mathrm{vol}(K)\leq\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{D}f(x)|^{p}dx\leq\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}}\overline{|\mathcal{D}f(K)|^{p}}\mathrm{vol}(K),

where
\mathrm{vol}(K)
denotes the Lebesgue measure of
K
.
As a consequence, the output is a guaranteed interval (rather than a point estimate) for the target integral. In particular, we obtain certified lower and upper bounds for
\|\mathcal{D}f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
, and combining such estimates for different differential operators yields certified bounds for higher-order Sobolev norms.
On the theoretical side, we prove a general convergence result for this class of certified adaptive quadrature schemes and instantiate it for function values, Jacobians, and Hessians. On the algorithmic side, we provide practical procedures for constructing the bounds, including exact integration on boxes where ReLU networks are affine. Numerically, we show that the resulting certificates are both informative and practically computable.

1.2
Related work

We briefly summarize related work on
certificates
(i.e., deterministic upper/lower bounds) for neural network function values, derivatives, or derived quantities.

A common approach for obtaining local upper bounds uses interval bound propagation and related abstract-interpretation methods
[
10
,
25
]
.
These methods propagate intervals or more general abstract domains through the layers of a neural network to yield certified enclosures of the output values. The approaches introduced in this manuscript are also fundamentally grounded in interval arithmetic.
Interval arithmetic itself has a long history in providing appropriate certificates. Early foundational work goes back to Moore
[
16
]
.

Another way to obtain certified bounds on the outputs of deep neural networks is via linear relaxation methods
[
6
,
30
,
24
]
. In these works, (piecewise) linear relaxations of nonlinearities (most notably ReLU) are constructed, and associated optimization problems are solved. While these tools are generally more computationally demanding than interval approaches, they typically yield more accurate certificates.

We also mention branch-and-bound-type verification methods
[
3
,
22
]
. Here, global certification can be improved by splitting the input domain and tightening bounds recursively. This approach is closely aligned with our adaptive refinement philosophy, albeit typically not applied to integrals or norms.

In addition to certificates for function values, certified bounds for Jacobians and Lipschitz constants have been studied extensively. A prominent line of work uses backward/recursive bound propagation through the chain rule to upper bound the norm of the (Clarke) Jacobian, which in turn provides local Lipschitz bounds; this includes RecurJac
[
33
]
and subsequent bound-propagation frameworks that tighten and generalize it
[
23
]
. Related verification-oriented linear relaxation methods (e.g. CROWN/LiRPA and their optimized variants) also yield certified sensitivity bounds and can be combined with branch-and-bound for tightening
[
32
,
29
,
31
]
.
Complementary approaches are based on convex optimization and semidefinite programming. For neural networks with nonlinearities that have bounded derivatives, Lipschitz upper bounds can be identified by solving a semidefinite program with quadratic constraints
[
9
]
.

Compared to first-order certification methods, the literature on bounding second-order derivatives of neural networks is limited. Analytic Hessian bounds for shallow architectures are derived in
[
22
]
and extended to deep networks by propagating operator norm bounds on second derivatives through the network using compositional inequalities.
Bounds on the spectral norm of the Hessian are studied in
[
26
]
. These bounds are used during training to reduce the curvature of trained neural networks.
Moreover, recursive bounds on the Lipschitz constant of the Jacobian are derived in
[
7
]
.

In contrast to the above literature, which primarily targets pointwise properties, our focus is on
certified computation of global integral quantities
such as Lebesgue and Sobolev norms, by combining local derivative enclosures with adaptive partition refinement and quadrature.

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce preliminary notions that are used throughout the manuscript.

2.1
Function spaces and measures

All integrals in this work are taken with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
For a measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
\mathrm{vol}(A)
the Lebesgue measure of
A
.
The main objects of interest are the Lebesgue and Sobolev norms defined below.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
d,m,k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the Lebesgue space of vector-valued functions
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
as the set of measurable maps
\Phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

with finite norm,

||\Phi||_{L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}\coloneqq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}|\Phi_{i}(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{1/p}

(1)

where functions that agree almost everywhere are identified.
We define the Sobolev space of vector-valued functions
W^{k,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
as the set of
\Phi\in L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that, for every multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
|\alpha|\leq k
, the weak derivative
D^{\alpha}\Phi=(D^{\alpha}\Phi_{1},\dotsc,D^{\alpha}\Phi_{m})
belongs to
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
. We define the Sobolev norm

||\Phi||_{W^{k,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}\coloneqq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\int_{\Omega}|D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{1/p}

(2)

where
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}
.

Remark 2.2
.

For
1\leq p<\infty
, we use the convention that
W^{0,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})=L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
and that
D^{\alpha}
is the identity map for
\alpha=0\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
.

2.2
Neural networks

We consider standard feedforward neural networks. To fix notation, we include the following definition from
[
18
]
.

Definition 2.3

(Feedforward neural network)
.

A feedforward neural network
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L+1}}
consists of
L\in\mathbb{N}
hidden layers, layer widths
d_{0},\dots,d_{L+1}\in\mathbb{N}
, an activation function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, and weight matrices

W^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell+1}\times d_{\ell}}
and bias vectors
b^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell+1}}
for
\ell=0,\dots,L

such that

\displaystyle{x}^{(0)}

\displaystyle:=x,

\displaystyle z^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle:=W^{(\ell-1)}x^{(\ell-1)}+b^{(\ell-1)},\quad

(pre-activation)

\displaystyle x^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle:=\sigma(z^{(\ell)}),\quad

(activation)

\displaystyle x^{(L+1)}

\displaystyle:=W^{(L)}x^{(L)}+b^{(L)},

we have

\Phi(x)=x^{(L+1)}\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}.

We call
L
the depth,
w_{\Phi}:=\max_{\ell=1,\dots,L}d_{\ell}
the
width
, and
(\sigma;d_{0},\dots,d_{L+1})
the
architecture
of the neural network
\Phi
.

When bounding derivatives of a neural network in Section
4
, we iterate backward through the network. This motivates the following definition of a tail network.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L+1}}
be a neural network. For
\ell=0,\dotsc,L
, define maps

T_{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell+1}},\quad T_{\ell}(x)=W^{(\ell)}x+b^{(\ell)},

with
W^{(\ell)}
and
b^{(\ell)}
from
\Phi
. For
\ell=0,\dotsc,L
, we define the tail neural networks
\Phi^{(\ell)}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L+1}}
by

\Phi^{(\ell)}(x)=T_{L}\circ\sigma\circ T_{L-1}\circ\dots\circ\sigma\circ T_{\ell}(x).

In particular, we have
\Phi^{(0)}=\Phi
and
\Phi^{(\ell)}(x^{(\ell)})=\Phi(x)
.

Remark 2.5
.

For a neural network
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L+1}}
, it is useful to view the layers as functions: the pre-activations are
z^{(\ell)}(u)=W^{(\ell-1)}x^{(\ell-1)}(u)+b^{(\ell-1)}
, and the activations are
x^{(\ell)}(u)=\sigma(z^{(\ell)}(u))
for
\ell=1,\dots,L
,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
, with
x^{(0)}(u)=u
.

The Jacobian and Hessian of a neural network involve diagonal matrices with activation derivatives evaluated at pre-activations on the diagonal. We define these matrices below.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L+1}}
be a neural network with
L
hidden layers and activation function
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
For each
\ell=1,\dots,L
and each vector

D^{(\ell)}(u)=\operatorname{diag}\!\big(\sigma^{\prime}(u_{1}),\dots,\sigma^{\prime}(u_{d_{\ell}})\big),

i.e.,

D^{(\ell)}(u)_{ii}=\sigma^{\prime}(u_{i}),\qquad D^{(\ell)}(u)_{ij}=0\ \ (i\neq j),

for
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}}
.

Definition 2.7
.

If
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by
\sigma(x)=\max\{x,0\}
, we call it a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function.

Interval arithmetic can be expressed purely in terms of endpoints.

Proposition 2.10

(
[

]
)

Given two intervals

•

When bounding neural network function values and derivatives, the input is a box (Cartesian product of intervals), and the output consists of lower and upper bounds. This can be understood as an interval function.

Definition 2.18
.

A function that maps intervals, interval vectors, interval matrices, etc. to intervals, interval vectors, interval matrices, etc. is called an interval function.

We will mostly encounter interval functions mapping interval vectors to intervals, interval vectors, or interval matrices. A desirable property is preservation of set inclusion, called inclusion isotonicity.

Definition 2.19
.

Let

3
Adaptive quadrature

Our aim is to design an adaptive quadrature that yields, after
n\in\mathbb{N}
steps, two values
\mathrm{Q}_{n},\eta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
for
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
with

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,dx=\mathrm{Q}_{n}+\mathrm{R}_{n}

(12)

where
\eta_{n}
is an upper bound on the error
|\mathrm{R}_{n}|
and converges to zero, i.e.

|\mathrm{R}_{n}|\leq\eta_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{n}\to 0\text{ for }n\to\infty.

(13)

To bound the error of the quadrature, note that we have

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,dx\in f(\Omega)\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)

for a continuous function
f
. If the quadrature lies within the interval
f(\Omega)\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)
, its width rigorously bounds the error.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Omega\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}
. We call a function
\mathcal{I}
with arguments
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and

K\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}
mapping into
\mathbb{R}
, defined by

\mathcal{I}(f,K)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}f(x_{i}),

with parameters
w_{1},\dotsc,w_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
and
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N}\in K
a quadrature rule.
We say that
\mathcal{I}
is exact for a class of functions
\mathcal{F}
if

\mathcal{I}(f,K)=\int_{K}f(x)\,dx\qquad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{F}.

If
\mathcal{P}_{m}
denotes the space of polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of total degree
at most
m
, then we say that
\mathcal{I}
has degree of exactness (or precision)
m

if it is exact for
\mathcal{P}_{m}
but not exact for
\mathcal{P}_{m+1}
.

If
\mathcal{I}
is a positive-weight quadrature rule that is exact for constants, then
\mathcal{I}(f,\Omega)\in f(\Omega)\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)
and we obtain the width

\tilde{\eta}_{\Omega}=w\big(f(\Omega)\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)\big)=w(f(\Omega))\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)

as an upper bound for
|\mathrm{R}_{n}|
.
The computation of

w(f(\Omega))=\overline{f(\Omega)}-\underline{f(\Omega)}

requires knowledge of the maximum
\overline{f(\Omega)}
and minimum
\underline{f(\Omega)}
of
f
on
\Omega
. Instead of applying computationally costly global optimization methods, we seek interval-valued maps
F:\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{IR}
satisfying

f(K)\subset F(K)

for all
K\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}
. This yields

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,dx\in F(\Omega)\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)

and

\eta_{\Omega}=w(F(\Omega))\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)

satisfying
|\mathrm{R}_{n}|\leq\eta_{\Omega}
.
The interval-valued map
F
contains the range of
f
and therefore yields rigorous error bounds. This motivates the following definition of a problem instance.

Definition 3.2
.

We call the triple

P=(f,F,\Omega)

(14)

consisting of a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, an interval enclosure
F:\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{IR}
of
f
and a domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}
a problem instance.

We have seen above how a suitable interval-valued function
F
can yield an upper bound
\eta_{\Omega}
to the error of the quadrature. Let us now turn to the second part of Equation (
13
) and motivate how to achieve convergent error decay. To this end, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}\subset\mathbb{IR}^{d}
be a partition of
\Omega
. We then have

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,dx\in\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}F(K)\mathrm{vol}(K)

with the right-hand side having width
\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}w(F(K))\mathrm{vol}(K)
. Setting

\eta_{K}=w(F(K))\mathrm{vol}(K)

(15)

and

\eta_{n}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\eta_{K}

(16)

yields
|\mathrm{R}_{n}|\leq\eta_{n}
, provided

\mathrm{Q}_{n}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathcal{I}(f,K)

with local quadratures satisfying
\mathcal{I}(f,K)\in F(K)\mathrm{vol}(K)
. Considering Equation (
16
), we hope to create partitions
\mathcal{P}_{n}
such that the local error bounds
\eta_{K}
converge to zero as the boxes get smaller. This motivates the definition of a state instance.

Definition 3.3
.

We call the triple

S=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{E})

(17)

a state instance. The components are:

•

A partition
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}
of
\Omega
into boxes.

•

A set
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}
of pairs
\mathcal{Q}=\{(K,Q_{K})\,:\,K\in\mathcal{P}\}
assigning a quadrature value
Q_{K}
to each
K\in\mathcal{P}
.

•

A set
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
of pairs
\mathcal{E}=\{(K,\eta_{K})\,:\,K\in\mathcal{P}\}
assigning an error bound
\eta_{K}
to each
K\in\mathcal{P}
.

We write
1^{T}\mathcal{Q}:=\sum_{(K,Q_{K})\in\mathcal{Q}}Q_{K}
and
1^{T}\mathcal{E}:=\sum_{(K,\eta_{K})\in\mathcal{E}}\eta_{K}
.

We can create partitions either by uniformly subdividing the problem domain into smaller boxes or iteratively refining a partition by choosing only a subset of boxes to subdivide. Inspired by adaptive finite element methods (see, e.g.
[
28
]
) we follow the latter approach of adaptively generating partitions. This introduces an additional selection process which is called marking. We will collect the local quadrature, marking and refinement rule in an algorithm instance.

Definition 3.4
.

We call the triple

A=(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{R})

(18)

an algorithm instance. The components are:

•

A quadrature rule
\mathcal{I}
with arguments
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
K\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}
which returns a real number.

•

A marking rule
\mathcal{M}
with argument
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that returns a subset
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathcal{P}
.

•

A refinement rule
\mathcal{R}
taking as argument a box
K\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}
and returning a partition
\mathcal{P}_{K}\subset\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{K}
of
K
into boxes.

We initialize the first state as the trivial partition and the corresponding error bound and quadrature.

Next, we will make rigorous what conditions the problem and algorithm instances must satisfy in order for
\mathrm{AdaQuad}
to satisfy Equation (
13
).

3.1
Certification and convergence

Let us start by giving conditions on the problem and algorithm instance which are sufficient to certify the adaptive quadrature, i.e. satisfy the first part of Equation (
13
).

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
P=(f,F,\Omega)
and
A=(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{R})
be a problem and algorithm instance, respectively. If
f
is continuous and
\mathcal{I}
satisfies

\mathcal{I}(f,K)\in F(K)\mathrm{vol}(K),

(19)

then
(\mathrm{Q}_{n},\eta_{n})=\mathrm{AdaQuad}_{n}(P,A)
satisfy

|\mathrm{R}_{n}|\leq\eta_{n}

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Since
f
is continuous, it maps connected compact sets to connected compact sets and because it is also real-valued
f(K)\in\mathbb{IR}
for all
K\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}
. Thus, we can write
f(K)=[\underline{f(K)},\overline{f(K)}]
where the endpoints are the minimum and maximum of
f
over
K
. By the monotonicity of the integral, we have

\underline{f(K)}\mathrm{vol}(K)=\int_{K}\underline{f(K)}\,dx\leq\int_{K}f(x)\,dx\leq\int_{K}\overline{f(K)}\,dx=\overline{f(K)}\mathrm{vol}(K),

showing

\int_{K}f(x)\,dx\in f(K)\mathrm{vol}(K).

By assumption we have
\mathcal{I}(f,K)\in F(K)\mathrm{vol}(K)
. It follows that

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,dx,\mathrm{Q}_{n}\in\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}F(K)\mathrm{vol}(K),

by the additivity of the integral and definition of
\mathrm{Q}_{n}
. By definition

\eta_{n}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}w(F(K))\mathrm{vol}(K)

which is equal to

w\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}F(K)\mathrm{vol}(K)\right),

finishing the proof.
∎

Definition 3.6
.

Let
P=(f,F,\Omega)
and
A=(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{R})
be a problem and algorithm instance, respectively. If
P
and
A
satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
3.5
, we call them a certifiable pair.

Next, we want to derive a sufficient condition that yields the convergence of the global error to zero, i.e. the second part of Equation (
13
). The following proposition gives properties for the marking and refinement rule to satisfy in order to obtain linear convergence for the error decay. These assumptions are standard in the proof of convergence of adaptive finite element methods (see, e.g.
[
28
, Sec. 1.14]
).

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\theta,\rho\in(0,1)
as well as
P=(f,F,\Omega)
and
A=(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{R})
be a problem and algorithm instance, respectively. If for a partition
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{IR}^{d}
of
\Omega
and
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
the inequality

1^{T}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\geq\theta 1^{T}\mathcal{E}

(20)

is satisfied for
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}=\{(K,\eta_{K})\in\mathcal{E}\,:\,K\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\}
with
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E})
; and
\mathcal{P}_{K}=\mathcal{R}(K)
satisfies

\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{K}}w(F(K^{\prime}))\mathrm{vol}(K^{\prime})\leq\rho w(F(K))\mathrm{vol}(K)

(21)

for every
K\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}
, then for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
(\mathrm{Q}_{n},\eta_{n})=\mathrm{AdaQuad}_{n}(P,A)
we have

\eta_{n}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{n+1}\leq q\eta_{n}

(22)

with
q=1-\theta(1-\rho)
satisfying
q\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

Let
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
(\mathrm{Q}_{n},\eta_{n})=\mathrm{AdaQuad}_{n}(P,A)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
S_{n}=(\mathcal{P}_{n},\mathcal{Q}_{n},\mathcal{E}_{n})
be a state instance. From the definition of
\mathrm{Step}_{A}(S_{n},P)
and
\eta_{n}
it follows that

\eta_{n+1}=\eta_{n}-\eta_{M}+\eta_{R},

where
\eta_{n}=1^{T}\mathcal{E}_{n}
,
\eta_{M}=1^{T}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
with
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}=\{(K,\eta_{K})\in\mathcal{E}_{n}Ar\,:\,K\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E}_{n})
,
\eta_{R}=1^{T}\mathcal{E}_{n+1,R}
with
\mathcal{E}_{n+1,R}=\{(K,w(F(K))\mathrm{vol}(K))\}_{K\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1,R}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n+1,R}=\bigcup_{K\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}\mathcal{R}(K)
. Since the marking rule returns subsets of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, inequality (
20
) implies
\eta_{M}\in[\theta\eta_{n},\eta_{n}]
. Moreover, inequality (
21
) implies
\eta_{R}\in[0,\rho\eta_{M}]
. Inserting the upper bound for
\eta_{R}
yields

\displaystyle\eta_{n}-\eta_{M}+\eta_{R}

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{n}-\eta_{M}+\rho\eta_{M}.

Factoring out
-\eta_{M}
and inserting the lower bound for
\eta_{M}
yields

\displaystyle\eta_{n}-\eta_{M}+\rho\eta_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{n}-\theta(1-\rho)\eta_{n},

and finally

\displaystyle\eta_{n}-\theta(1-\rho)\eta_{n}

\displaystyle=\big(1-\theta(1-\rho)\big)\eta_{n}.

Since
\rho,\theta\in(0,1)
it follows that
q\in(0,1)
where
q=1-\theta(1-\rho)
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Definition 3.8
.

Let
P=(f,F,\Omega)
and
A=(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{R})
be a problem and algorithm instance, respectively. If
P
and
A
satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
3.7
, we call them a converging pair.

3.2
Application to Lebesgue and Sobolev norms

We want to compute Lebesgue and Sobolev norms of vector-valued functions with the
\mathrm{AdaQuad}
algorithm. Firstly, we define suitable integrands.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Omega\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
1\leq p<\infty
and
\Phi\in W^{k,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Define

f_{\Phi,k,p}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}|D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}(x)|^{p}.

(23)

Then

\left(\int_{\Omega}f_{\Phi,k,p}(x)\,dx\right)^{1/p}=||\Phi||_{W^{k,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}

(24)

holds.

Proof.

The result follows directly from Definition
2.1
and the linearity of the integral.
∎

Next, we want to construct an interval enclosure for
f_{\Phi,k,p}
from a box enclosure for
\Phi
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Omega\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
1\leq p<\infty
and
\Phi\in W^{k,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Furthermore, let for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
with
|\alpha|\leq k
,
i\in\{1,\dotsc,m\}
the function
F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}:\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{IR}
be an interval enclosure of
D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}
. Define

F_{\Phi,k,p}(K)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|^{p}.

(25)

Then
F_{\Phi,k,p}
is an interval enclosure of
f_{\Phi,k,p}
.

Proof.

Let
K\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}
. We have

D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}(K)\subset F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)

by assumption.
By Definition
2.15
we have

|D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}(x)|\in|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|

for every
x\in K
, which yields

|D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}(K)|\subset|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|.

Moreover, by Definition
2.15
we have

|D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}(x)|^{p}\in|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|^{p}

for every
x\in K
, which yields

|D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}(K)|^{p}\subset|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|^{p}.

Finally, for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
satisfying
|\alpha|,|\beta|\leq k
,
i,j\in\{1,\dotsc,m\}
we have

|D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}(x)|^{p}+|D^{\beta}\Phi_{j}(x)|^{p}\in|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|^{p}+|F_{\Phi,\beta,j}(K)|^{p}

for every
x\in K
by Proposition
2.10
, which yields

|D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}(K)|^{p}+|D^{\beta}\Phi_{j}(K)|^{p}\subset|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|^{p}+|F_{\Phi,\beta,j}(K)|^{p}.

Summing over all
|\alpha|\leq k
and
i\in\{1,\dotsc,m\}
yields

f_{\Phi,k,p}(K)\subset F_{\Phi,k,p}(K).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Next, we show that
F_{\Phi,k,p}
is Hölder continuous, if the interval extensions
F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}
of
D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}
are Hölder continuous.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Omega\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
1\leq p<\infty
and
\Phi\in W^{k,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Furthermore, let for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
with
|\alpha|\leq k
,
i\in\{1,\dotsc,m\}
the function
F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}:\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{IR}
be a Hölder continuous interval enclosure of
D^{\alpha}\Phi_{i}
with constant
C_{\alpha,i}>0
and exponent
\gamma_{\alpha,i}\in(0,1]
. Then
F_{\Phi,k,p}
is a Hölder continuous interval enclosure of
f_{\Phi,k,p}
with exponent
\min_{\alpha,i}\gamma_{\alpha,i}
.

Proof.

It remains to be shown that
F_{\Phi,k,p}
is Hölder continuous. We have

\displaystyle w\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|^{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}w(|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|^{p})

by Remark
2.13
. The map
t\mapsto t^{p}
for
t\in[0,M]
,
M>0
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{p}=pM^{p-1}
. Therefore, by Remark
2.25
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}w(|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|^{p})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}L_{p}w(|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|),

with
M=\max_{\alpha,i}\overline{|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(\Omega)|}
. By Definition
2.15
it is easy to see that taking the absolute value of an interval can only decrease its width. This yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}L_{p}w(|F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)|)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}L_{p}w(F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)).

By assumption the
F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K)
are Hölder continuous. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}L_{p}w(F_{\Phi,\alpha,i}(K))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}L_{p}C_{\alpha,i}w(K)^{\gamma_{\alpha,i}}.

Let
\tilde{\gamma}=\min_{\alpha,i}\gamma_{\alpha,i}
be the smallest of the Hölder exponents. Factoring out
w(K)^{\tilde{\gamma}}
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}L_{p}C_{\alpha,i}w(K)^{\gamma_{\alpha,i}}

\displaystyle=L_{p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}C_{\alpha,i}w(K)^{\gamma_{\alpha,i}-\tilde{\gamma}}\right)w(K)^{\tilde{\gamma}}

We have
w(K)\leq w(\Omega)
since
K\subset\Omega
. This yields

\displaystyle L_{p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}C_{\alpha,i}w(K)^{\gamma_{\alpha,i}-\tilde{\gamma}}\right)w(K)^{\tilde{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq L_{p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}C_{\alpha,i}w(\Omega)^{\gamma_{\alpha,i}-\tilde{\gamma}}\right)w(K)^{\tilde{\gamma}}

and finishes the proof.
∎

3.3
Application to energy norms

Let us now compute the integral of the squared residuals of boundary-value problems.
This is especially relevant when solving partial differential equations with neural networks (see
[
19
]
). Here the loss function is taken to be the mean squared residual of the differential equation in the interior and on the boundary evaluated on a set of collocation points. To evaluate the generalization error it is common to simply increase the number of collocation points and scale by the volume of the domain (Monte Carlo integration). We show how
\mathrm{AdaQuad}
can compute generalization errors for PINNs with rigorous error bounds. Consider the following boundary-value problem (see
[
8
, Chapter 6]
). Let
\Omega\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}
for
d\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the boundary-value problem

\begin{cases}\mathcal{D}u=g&\text{in }\mathring{\Omega}\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

(26)

where
\mathring{\Omega}
denotes the interior of
\Omega
and
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the unknown. Here the function
g:\mathring{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
is given and
\mathcal{D}
denotes a second-order partial differential operator given in nondivergence form

\mathcal{D}\Phi(x)=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\Phi_{x_{i}x_{j}}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}(x)\Phi_{x_{i}}(x)+c(x)\Phi(x),

(27)

for given coefficient functions
a_{ij},b_{i},c
for
i,j=1,\dotsc,d
and trial function
\Phi
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Omega\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}
,
\Phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and for every
i,j=0,\dotsc,d
the function
F_{\Phi,i,j}:\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{IR}
be an interval enclosure of
\Phi_{x_{i}x_{j}}
, where
\Phi_{x_{0}}=\Phi
. Furthermore, for
i,j=1,\dotsc,d
let
A_{ij},B_{i},C:\mathbb{IR}^{d}\cap 2^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{IR}
be interval enclosures of
a_{ij},b_{i},c
, respectively. Define

Proof.

Proposition 3.13

Let

\Omega\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}

Proof.

In Proposition

is an interval enclosure of
\mathcal{D}\Phi
. It remains to be shown that

is Hölder continuous.
In the proof of Proposition

\mathrm{mag}(F_{1}(K))w(F_{2}(K))+\mathrm{mag}(F_{2}(K))w(F_{1}(K))\leq\mathrm{mag}(F_{1}(K))C_{2}w(K)^{\gamma_{2}}+\mathrm{mag}(F_{2}(K))C_{1}w(K)^{\gamma_{1}}

\mathfrak{A}_{\Phi}(\mathrm{vert}(K))

Proof.

\mathfrak{A}_{\Phi}(K)=\{a\}

\mathrm{vert}(K)\subset K

\mathfrak{A}_{\Phi}(\mathrm{vert}(K))=\{a\}
. Now let
\mathfrak{A}_{\Phi}(\mathrm{vert}(K))=\{a\}
. Since by
[

the activation piece is convex, it follows
\mathfrak{A}_{\Phi}(K)=\{a\}
from
\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{vert}(K))=K

∎

Since the activation pieces are contained in affine linear pieces, we obtain a sufficient condition by only comparing the activation patterns of the vertices.

Proposition 4.15
.

\Phi

Proof.

\mathfrak{A}_{\Phi}(K)
be a singleton. By Proposition
4.14
the set

is contained in an activation piece. By
[

the activation pieces are contained in affine linear pieces. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.21
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathbb{IR}^{d_{0}}
,
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L+1}}

which is in particular Hölder continuous for every exponent in
(0,1]
and constant zero.

\displaystyle w(\mathrm{Jac}_{\Phi,\ell,\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}}(K))

\displaystyle=w\left(\mathrm{Jac}_{\Phi,{\ell}+1,\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}}({K})\operatorname{diag}(\Sigma^{\prime}(\mathrm{Fval}_{\Phi,{\ell}+1,\Sigma}({K})))W^{(\ell)}\right)

and applying Equation (
9

\displaystyle w\left(\mathrm{Jac}_{\Phi,{\ell}+1,\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}}({K})\operatorname{diag}(\Sigma^{\prime}(\mathrm{Fval}_{\Phi,{\ell}+1,\Sigma}({K})))W^{(\ell)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+||\Delta^{(\ell+1)}(K)||w(\mathrm{Jac}_{\Phi,{\ell}+1,\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}}({K})),

where we define

\displaystyle||\operatorname{diag}(\Sigma^{\prime}(\mathrm{Fval}_{\Phi,{\ell}+1,\Sigma}({K}))||w(W^{(\ell)})+||W^{(\ell)}||w(\operatorname{diag}(\Sigma^{\prime}(\mathrm{Fval}_{\Phi,{\ell}+1,\Sigma}({K}))))

||\mathrm{Jac}_{\Phi,{\ell}+1,\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}}({K})||\leq||\mathrm{Jac}_{\Phi,{\ell}+1,\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}}({\Omega})||\quad\text{and}\quad||\Delta^{(\ell+1)}(K)||\leq||\Delta^{(\ell+1)}(\Omega)||.

\displaystyle w(\mathrm{Hess}_{\Phi,i,{\ell},\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}({K}))

4.5
Numerical Experiments

This section presents a series of numerical experiments to validate the theoretical performance of the adaptive refinement and bounding algorithms.
Our primary quantity of interest is the gap between our upper and lower bounds.
Let
\overline{B},\underline{B}\in\mathbb{R}
denote local upper and lower bounds computed by Algorithm
3
on a partition
\mathcal{P}
of a domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{IR}^{d}
.
For the norms considered here—
L^{p}
-, Sobolev-, and energy-norms—we evaluate bounds for the corresponding
p
-th powers.
We define the global bound gap
G\in\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle G=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}}\big(\overline{B}(K)-\underline{B}(K)\big).

The bound gap directly quantifies the error of our algorithm.

First, we provide a one-dimensional analysis of Sobolev (Corollary
4.22
,
4.27
) and
L^{p}
(Corollary
4.9
) norm bounds in Sections
4.5.1
and
4.5.2
.
We analyze the convergence of the global bound gap for both untrained and trained networks in deep and wide architectures for increasingly refined partitions.
Next, in Section
4.5.3
, we study convergence of the global bound gap and the local bound gap for deep and wide architectures fitted to the two-dimensional smooth disk function (
48
).
Finally, in Section
4.5.4
, we examine convergence of the global bound gap and the local bound gap for the energy norm in an elliptic PINN setting.

For the
L^{p}
norm bounds, we use the Hölder refinement strategy (Algorithm
5
). For Sobolev and energy-norm bounds, we use Dörfler marking (Algorithm
4
) with uniform refinement, where each box is split along coordinate axes into
2^{d}
subboxes (see
4.2
). Although this refinement strategy does not provide the controlled error reduction described in Equation (
21
), it avoids the need to compute the Hölder constant and exponent. In the Lipschitz-continuous case, available estimates of the Lipschitz constant for neural networks are often overly conservative, which can lead to refinements with an excessive number of subdivisions.
