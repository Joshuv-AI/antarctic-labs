EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This final technical report documents the work performed under DOE Award
DE‑EE0008799 by GTI Energy over the course of the project period. The project
focused on advanced fueling methods for compressed natural gas (CNG) vehicles
which have historically suffered from underfilling. Unlike gasoline or diesel vehicles that
can be filled until the liquid fuel reaches a certain level in the fuel tank, CNG vehicles
must be filled to a pressure/temperature target onboard the vehicle. This makes it more
difficult to determine when the vehicle is full because the pressure target varies with
temperature and the temperature of the gas onboard the vehicle changes dynamically
throughout the fueling process. To compensate for this uncertainty, CNG dispenser
controls often underfill the vehicle to ensure the cylinders do not reach an unsafe
pressure, but this also results in reduced range for a given CNG cylinder volume. This
issue increases the cost and payback period of CNG vehicles because larger cylinders
are needed to achieve a vehicle’s target range.

To overcome these challenges, the project team demonstrated a fueling method that
measured the real time pressure and temperature of the CNG onboard the vehicle and
wirelessly transmitted that information to the CNG station. This simple change allowed
the team to safely and consistently add 10-15% more fuel than existing state-of-the-art
CNG dispensers during each fueling event. Further, the team realized that the
technology had substantial benefits beyond improved fast fills, and that the technology
could also be used to improve time fills, fleet logistics, and safety.

The team demonstrated that a single station controller could monitor every vehicle and
dispenser at a station, making it possible to monitor vehicles being time filled in addition
to those being fast filled. The team was able to use that information to determine when
time filled vehicles were full while also monitoring them for any possible safety issues
like rapid depressurization or higher than expected temperatures that might indicate a
tank rupture or fire. These deviations could then be used to shut down the station unit
the issue could be addressed. Lastly, knowing the vehicle volume, temperature, and
pressures allowed the team to accurately quantify the daily fuel consumption of each
CNG vehicle, allowing fleets to track driver performance over time.

Overall, GTI was able to demonstrate the Smart CNG Station can improve fleet
logistics, improve safety, and improve fueling of CNG vehicles. These benefits appear
to far outweigh the nominal cost added sensors and communication hardware that are
needed to implement the technology.

INTRODUCTION
Background
Compressed natural gas (CNG) has long been recognized as a viable alternative
transportation fuel with a lower cost relative to conventional petroleum-based fuels. As a
result, CNG has seen sustained adoption across light-, medium-, and heavy-duty
vehicle fleets, particularly in centrally fueled commercial applications such as transit
buses, refuse trucks, concrete mixers, and municipal service vehicles. Despite the
maturity of CNG vehicle and station technology, persistent inefficiencies in the fueling
process continue to limit the achievable range, operational consistency, and economic
performance of natural gas vehicles.
At the station level, most commercially deployed CNG dispensers rely on control
strategies that estimate when a vehicle’s onboard storage system has reached a “full”
condition using indirect measurements. These approaches are rooted in legacy
assumptions about vehicle configuration, fueling behavior, and thermodynamic
response, and they do not explicitly account for the actual state of charge of the
vehicle’s fuel system at the time of fueling. These limitations routinely result in
systematic underfilling of CNG vehicles, particularly under warm ambient conditions or
during fast-fill events.
Existing CNG Dispenser Control Approaches
Three primary classes of CNG dispenser control strategies are commonly used in
commercial fueling stations today: regulator-controlled designs,
temperature-compensated pressure-based designs, and mass-based fueling designs.
Each of these approaches exhibit fundamental limitations when applied to real-world
fueling scenarios.
Regulator-controlled designs represent the most basic fueling approach and are still
present in many older or simplified station configurations. In these systems, gas flow to
the vehicle is controlled primarily through pressure regulators and mechanical valves
that limit maximum delivery pressure. The dispenser terminates fueling once a preset
pressure threshold is reached at the dispenser outlet. While this approach is simple and
robust, it implicitly assumes that the pressure measured at the dispenser accurately
reflects the pressure inside the vehicle’s onboard storage cylinders. In practice,
pressure drops across hoses, fittings, and receptacles, as well as transient thermal
effects during fueling, make this assumption invalid. Furthermore, there may or may not
be any adjustments made for varying temperatures, leading to inaccuracies in this
approach.
To address some of these shortcomings, many modern dispensers incorporate
temperature compensation designs based on ambient temperature. These systems
attempt to correct for the heat of compression by adjusting the target fueling pressure
based on measured or assumed ambient conditions. The underlying assumption is that
ambient temperature can serve as a proxy to estimate the final stabilized gas
temperature inside the vehicle cylinders once thermal equilibrium is reached. While this
approach represents an improvement over purely pressure-based control, it remains an
approximation. Ambient temperature often differs significantly from the actual gas

 Page 4

temperature inside the cylinders before, during, and immediately after fueling,
particularly during high-flow fast-fill events. Consequently, ambient temperature-based
compensation schemes still result in conservative fueling targets and continued
underfilling.
More recently, some stations have adopted mass-based fueling designs, which aim to
deliver a prescribed mass of natural gas to the vehicle rather than relying solely on
pressure thresholds. In theory, mass-based fueling offers a more direct link to energy
content and usable fuel capacity. However, these systems typically rely on assumptions
about tank volume, gas composition, and initial state of charge that are not dynamically
updated during fueling. Without real-time knowledge of the vehicle’s actual storage
configuration and thermal state, mass-based systems must still apply conservative
safety margins, limiting their ability to consistently achieve full fills across a diverse
vehicle fleet.
Fundamental Limitations of Existing Designs
Despite their differences, all three of these fueling approaches share a common
limitation: they lack sufficient information about the true state of charge of the vehicle’s
onboard CNG storage system at the time of fueling. Accurately determining when a
vehicle has reached a full condition requires knowledge of several interrelated
parameters, including gas pressure, gas temperature, total storage volume, and gas
composition. These variables are linked through the ideal gas law,

and its real-gas extensions, which govern the relationship between pressure,
temperature, and the amount of gas stored in a fixed volume.
Commercial dispensers, however, typically only observe a subset of these variables,
most often pressure at the dispenser outlet and, in some cases, ambient temperature.
They do not have direct visibility into the vehicle’s internal storage volume, cylinder
configuration, or real-time gas temperature inside the tanks. Nor do they have access to
information about gas composition variations that can influence compressibility and
density. As a result, existing dispenser control algorithms must rely on conservative
assumptions to ensure safety across all possible vehicle configurations and operating
conditions. These conservative assumptions, while necessary from a safety standpoint,
lead directly to underutilization of onboard storage capacity.
Field data collected by GTI Energy and documented in prior research has shown that
underfilling on the order of 10–25 percent is common, depending on ambient conditions,
fueling rate, and vehicle configuration. This lost capacity translates directly into reduced
driving range, increased fueling frequency, and higher operational costs for fleet
operators. It also undermines confidence in CNG as a competitive alternative fuel,
particularly in applications where route length and operational flexibility are critical.
Smart Dispenser Concept and Advancement Beyond State of the Art
The Smart CNG Station Deployment project builds directly on this body of prior work
while addressing its fundamental limitations through a communications-based fueling
strategy. Rather than attempting to infer the state of charge using indirect

 Page 5

measurements at the dispenser, the Smart Dispenser architecture enables direct,
real-time communication between the vehicle and the fueling station. By securely
transmitting vehicle-specific data—including tank pressure, tank temperature, total
storage volume, cylinder configuration, and other relevant parameters—the dispenser
gains access to the information required to accurately calculate the vehicle’s true state
of charge during fueling.
This approach represents a significant departure from conventional dispenser designs.
Instead of relying on fixed pressure targets or ambient temperature proxies, the Smart
Dispenser continuously evaluates the thermodynamic state of the vehicle’s storage
system as fueling progresses. With real-time knowledge of pressure, temperature, and
volume, the dispenser can dynamically adjust fueling behavior to safely deliver the
maximum achievable fill under prevailing conditions. Importantly, this is accomplished
without exceeding cylinder pressure limits or compromising safety, as the algorithm
continuously monitors all relevant parameters rather than extrapolating from limited
external measurements.
The Smart Dispenser concept leverages vehicle-station communications, wireless data
transfer, and advanced fueling algorithms. Similar communication-enabled approaches
have been explored in in applications such as hydrogen vehicle fueling; however, this
project distinguishes itself by utilizing a completely wireless solution with no driver
interaction needed to initiate a fill and a design that allows existing dispensers to be
retrofit with the solution, allowing them to always default back to their baseline fueling
algorithms. By embedding smart modules that interface with existing dispenser control
systems and vehicle data acquisition hardware, the project accelerates the transition
from laboratory concepts to commercially viable solutions.
Project Objectives
The objective of the Smart CNG Station Deployment project was to develop,
demonstrate, and validate a Smart CNG fueling system capable of increasing the
usable compressed natural gas storage capacity of vehicles by approximately 10 to 25
percent. By enabling more accurate and consistent full fills, the project seeks to improve
vehicle range, fueling efficiency, and operational reliability while maintaining or
enhancing safety.
At a national level, these improvements directly support the utilization of U.S. energy
resources by strengthening the economic and operational case for natural gas vehicles.
Natural gas vehicles already benefit from significantly cheaper fuel prices because a
gas gallon equivalent (GGE) of natural gas costs most fleets well under dollar per
gallon. The cost premium comes from the station and vehicle infrastructure, in
particular, the high-pressure vehicle cylinders that are required for every vehicle.
Therefore, increasing the usable storage capacity directly reduces the cost of natural
gas vehicles by reducing the volume of storage needed to achieve a certain range. This
lowers the capital cost for fleets, improves the vehicle’s reliability by achieving more
consistent fills, and reduces the driver’s range anxiety. Collectively, these benefits
contribute to reduced fuel costs, enhanced energy security, and more efficient
deployment of alternative fuel technologies that can help to lower transportation costs.

To achieve these outcomes, the project was structured into three budget periods, each
with clearly defined technical objectives, tasks, milestones, and Go/No-Go decision
points, as specified in the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO).
Budget Period 1: Preliminary Smart Station Design and Baseline Data Acquisition
focused on establishing the technical foundation for the project. During this phase, GTI
Energy assembled and deployed data acquisition systems on demonstration vehicles to
collect baseline fueling data, including pressures and temperatures, during fueling
events. In parallel, the project team initiated the design of smart vehicle and dispenser
modules capable of securely transmitting real-time data between the vehicle and
station. Key milestones in this period included the completion of a baseline data
acquisition system design, installation of data acquisition hardware, validation of
underfilling and overfilling using baseline data, and completion of the detailed design of
smart modules. A formal Go/No-Go decision governed continuation into subsequent
budget periods.
Budget Period 2: Prototype Validation and Field Deployment emphasized laboratory
testing, system integration, and controlled deployment at GTI’s facility. Prototype smart
modules were installed in a test environment and evaluated across a matrix of vehicle
tank configurations and operating conditions to ensure safe and accurate operation. The
smart dispenser control algorithm was integrated into commercial dispenser hardware,
enabling the system to identify smart vehicles, override default fueling logic, and deliver
communication-based full fills. Following laboratory validation, smart modules were
installed in a commercial dispenser at GTI’s facility in Des Plaines, IL. This unit was
used to demonstrate safer, improved fills.
Budget Period 3: Smart CNG Station Demonstration focused on extended data
collection, performance evaluation, and commercialization planning. Data collected
during smart fueling events were analyzed and compared to baseline results to quantify
improvements in fill quality, consistency, and achievable storage utilization. In parallel,
the project team conducted preliminary economic analyses to evaluate the net benefits
of improved filling performance, accounting for any incremental costs associated with
smart components. These analyses informed development of a commercialization
strategy intended to support broader adoption of the Smart CNG fueling system within
the industry.
Across all budget periods, GTI Energy performed comprehensive project management
activities, including planning, financial management, risk management, data
management, and reporting, in accordance with DOE requirements. The project also
committed to a robust set of deliverables, including annual progress summaries,
technical presentations, periodic technical briefings, and dissemination of results
through technical papers, presentations, and other materials as appropriate.

METHODS
Vehicle Data Acquisition Units
To enable communication-based fueling and establish a high-quality baseline for
evaluating dispenser performance, the project deployed ruggedized vehicle-side data
acquisition systems capable of measuring and recording key thermodynamic
parameters governing CNG storage capacity. Consistent with the objectives of Budget
Period 1, GTI Energy designed, fabricated, and tested two complementary vehicle data
acquisition architectures to ensure flexibility across vehicle platforms and operating
environments.
The first vehicle data acquisition system was based on Campbell Scientific hardware,
selected for its reliability, configurability, and widespread use in long-term field
monitoring applications. The Campbell Scientific–based design integrated pressure and
temperature sensors installed on the vehicle’s CNG storage system. These
measurements were combined with data sampled from the vehicle’s CAN bus and then
recorded. Unfortunately, the Campbell Scientific design ran into issues reliably
capturing the CAN bus data, making it impossible to move the design forward, so it was
abandoned.
In parallel, a second vehicle-side data acquisition architecture was developed using the
HEM Data Acquisition System (HEM DAS) platform. The HEM DAS design was
intended to support integration with commercial vehicle systems and to evaluate
alternative hardware pathways for future commercialization. Similar to the Campbell
Scientific system, the HEM DAS collected pressure and temperature data from the
onboard CNG storage system; however, instead of also recording the CAN bus data, it
uploaded the measured data to the vehicle CAN bus so all the data could be monitored
and transmitted by a separate streamer. This made the HEM system the preferred
design moving forward as the dedicated streamers were a much more reliable method
of gathering all the vehicle information in one location.
On the vehicles, data was transmitted using two methods. 1) When outside of the
station, the data was recorded locally and then regularly transmitted to a cloud server
via a cellular connection. 2) When at the station, streamers onboard the vehicle
connected to a local Wi-Fi network that allowed the vehicle data to be transmitted in real
to time devices like the station dispensers.
Unfortunately, the team faced some cyber security and technical uses while operating
the servers, so while under and overfilling were measured, the team did not collect
nearly as much data as was originally planned.
Dispenser Communication Architecture
A core innovation of the Smart CNG Station Deployment project was the development
of a robust, secure wireless communication pathway between the vehicle and the
dispenser, allowing real-time transmission of vehicle-specific fueling data. This
communication layer was implemented using a modular architecture consisting of a
vehicle-side wireless transmitter and a station-side processing unit.

 Page 8

On the vehicle side, data captured by the vehicle data acquisition units were transmitted
using an ESP32-based wireless transmitter. The ESP32 platform was selected due to
its low cost, integrated Wi-Fi and Bluetooth capabilities, and ability to support custom
firmware tailored to industrial communication requirements. The transmitter interfaced
directly with the vehicle CAN bus, packaging pressure, temperature, and vehicle data
into structured messages for transmission during fueling. Communication was initiated
only during active fueling events, minimizing power consumption and reducing exposure
to interference outside of defined station interactions.
At the dispenser, a second ESP32 was initially developed that would be installed in a
single dispenser. This device would identify the connected vehicle and then control the
dispenser during fueling. However, this was later upgraded to a Raspberry Pi that was
used as a station-wide controller that served as the interface between all smart vehicle
and smart dispenser interactions. Smart vehicles arriving at the station would connect
to the station’s Wi-Fi network. Vehicle data being transmitted was received by the
Raspberry Pi controller and used to determine that status of that vehicle.
Simultaneously, the Raspberry Pi was wired to the station dispenser using existing
Modbus connections. This allowed the Raspberry Pi to simultaneously monitor the
status of every dispenser and every smart vehicle. When a fill was initiated at a
dispenser, the station controller received that notice and then used data from the
dispenser and nearby vehicles to determine if any of the smart vehicles were connected
to that dispenser. If they were not connected, the smart controller would simply do
nothing, and the dispenser would fill the vehicle using the standard fueling algorithm
installed on the dispenser. However, if a smart vehicle was identified and validated,
then the Raspberry Pi would take control of the fill, overriding the control valve on the
dispenser to fill the vehicle until it was 100% full or another safety limit was reached.
This architecture allowed smart functionality to be added to existing commercial stations
and dispensers without requiring a full redesign of the proprietary dispenser control
hardware. By isolating the smart logic within the Raspberry Pi module, the project
enabled rapid prototyping, controlled testing, and iterative refinement of communication
protocols and control strategies.
The modular nature of the ESP32–Raspberry Pi communication also supported future
scalability and adaptation to different dispenser manufacturers, aligning with the
project’s commercialization objectives.
Dispenser Test Setups and Validation Environments
To ensure safe and repeatable validation of the smart fueling concept, GTI Energy
employed a staged testing approach that combined simulated and physical dispenser
environments.
Initial dispenser testing was conducted using a LabVIEW-based dispenser. This setup
emulated the logic and sensor responses of a commercial CNG dispenser allowing the
smart dispenser logic to be tested prior to receiving a commercial dispenser. Using
LabVIEW, CNG cylinders were filled from GTI’s CNG station storage and the ESP32
hardware and software was tested and refined to demonstrate that it could
communicate with LabVIEW, identify the correct vehicle being filled, and then control

 Page 9

the fill. This preliminary testing allowed the team to work through many of the early
bugs in the system and test the general architecture of the system.
Following successful LabVIEW testing, the smart communication and control system
were integrated with an ANGI Energy Systems CNG dispenser. The ANGI dispenser
provided a representative commercial platform through which the project team could
evaluate interoperability, system latency, and control handoff between the smart module
and the native dispenser logic. Smart fueling events conducted on the ANGI dispenser
validated that the system could correctly identify a smart-enabled vehicle, receive
real-time tank data, and adjust fueling behavior while maintaining all safety interlocks
and pressure limits inherent to commercial operation.
This progression from simulation to commercial hardware ensured that all smart fueling
functions were validated under controlled conditions.
Summary of Methodological Approach
Collectively, the methods employed combined robust vehicle-side sensing, modular
wireless communication, and staged dispenser testing to enable deployment-ready
validation of a communication-based CNG fueling system. By designing flexible data
acquisition systems, isolating smart logic within add-on modules, and validating
performance across simulated and commercial dispenser environments, the project
established a technically sound and commercially relevant pathway for improving CNG
fill quality and consistency. These methods directly supported the project’s objective of
increasing usable onboard CNG storage capacity while maintaining safety and
compatibility with existing infrastructure.

PROJECT RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The overarching goal of the project was to build, deploy, and demonstrate 5 smart
dispensers at test sites. Unfortunately, the team ran into issues with the original
partners, making actual deployments nearly impossible. Therefore, GTI Energy worked
to develop and demonstrate a Smart Dispenser at GTI’s headquarters to prove the
viability of the technology and provide a path for other interested parties to adopt the
technology. A summary of the anticipated and realized results are listed below.
Anticipated versus realized results:
 • Develop vehicle data acquisition systems (DAS): The team worked on two
 versions of a DAS but ultimately deployed multiple DAS built around the HEM
 Data hardware. The DAS, powered by the vehicle’s battery, was designed to
 monitor the CNG fuel system pressure and each cylinder’s temperature in real
 time. That data was then added to the CAN Bus where it could be combined with
 other vehicle specific information such as the engine fuel utilization measured by
 the engine’s mass flow meter.
 • Develop a Smart Vehicle transmitter: The team initially utilized off-the-shelf
 transmitters that could be programmed to transmit certain vehicle CAN data to a
 remote device. However, these were eventually replaced with custom
 transmitters that were easier to program with specific vehicle information such as
 the tank volume and age. This static data was combined with information
 recorded on the CAN Bus, including the HEM DAS data, before transmitting the
 everything to the station controller.
 • Develop a Smart device for the CNG dispenser: The project team initially
 developed a smart controller that could be installed in each individual dispenser.
 The device was initiated at the same time as the dispenser and was able to
 detect if the connected vehicle has the DAS and Smart transmitter installed. If
 they did then the controller could override the dispenser’s programming. This
 original design was developed on an ESP32 microcontroller. Later, the team
 determined the functionality of the system could be improved by monitoring the
 entire station. A full station controller was developed using a Raspberry Pi. The
 Station Controller was designed to monitor every Smart Vehicle and CNG
 dispenser simultaneously.
 • Integrate Smart Module into commercial dispenser: The team purchased and
 installed a commercial dispenser from ANGI Energy Systems. The Smart
 controller was connected to the dispenser’s Modbus connection to enable real
 time monitoring of the dispenser’s status. Later, additional Modbus connected
 hardware was added to the dispenser that also allowed the Station Controller to
 override the dispenser’s CNG shutoff valve so that gas could continue flowing
 into the target vehicles until the smart Controller stopped the fill.
 • Deploy and demonstrate dispensers: The demonstration was limited to a
 representative simulation of the Smart Dispenser’s performance at GTI Energy’s
 facility in Des Plaines, IL. The original plan was to deploy the equipment in the
 field following safety certification by a commercial dispenser manufacturer that

 Page 11

 was interested in commercializing the technology. However, the manufacturer
 that was originally partnered with the team was unable to continue working on
 the project, and the remainder of the team was not in the position to take on the
 liability of a public deployment. The demonstration at GTI was designed to be as
 representative of real world fueling as possible.

For lab testing, the vehicle volume was estimated and programmed as a fixed value on
the vehicle transmitter and the molecular weight was estimated using an average
natural gas composition. However, these values could also be calculated onboard the
vehicle as a vehicle specific constant. Using the mass flow meter onboard many CNG

Using the above formula, the station controller eliminated any vehicle whose estimated
mass was not within ~10% of the mass dispensed by the dispenser. This dynamic
comparison relies on the filling rate, vehicle volume and total mass dispensed to all
match between the vehicle and dispenser, making it nearly impossible to incorrectly

identify the wrong vehicle at the station. However, in the unlikely scenario that two
identical vehicles were being filled simultaneously and the station controller could not
determine which vehicle was connected to which dispenser, then the controller would
not take control of either fill and would just allow the dispensers to fill using their native
control algorithms. However, if a single vehicle was identified, then the controller would
override the dispenser and fill the vehicle until it was 100% full, the flow had dropped
too low, or the pressure/temperature limits of the tank or dispenser had been reached.
This ensured that the vehicle received the maximum amount of fuel possible.
Beyond the Smart filling functionality, the Station Controller was also programmed to
track the fuel consumption of each vehicle that visited the station. This was a feature
requested by several fleets to track their driver’s daily fuel consumption. Using the
formula above, the mass added to each vehicle could be estimated whether they were
fast filling at a dispenser or slow filling overnight. By tracking the mass added to each
vehicle, the vehicle specific fuel consumption could be tracked and recorded daily and
reported to the fleet without needing expensive flow meters to accurately track the fuel
transferred to each vehicle. This functionality is useful when fleets are accessing vehicle
and driver performance.
The station controller was also programmed with other time fill benefits that included the
ability to monitor temperature or pressure deviations on every vehicle when filling
overnight. If a deviation was detected that may have been caused by a fire or tank
rupture, then the station could be shut down reducing the risk to the remaining vehicles
and first responders. This direct monitoring of each vehicle would need to be tested in
real world scenarios, but is likely a more robust safety monitoring system than solutions
like fire eyes that can often miss incidents or be accidentally trigger by light reflecting off
the vehicles at the station.
Dispenser Override
The final component needed to demonstrate the Smart Dispenser was the dispenser
override system. GTI originally planned to directly control the dispenser vis the Modbus
connection, which is theoretically possible, except the dispenser code was not editable
and forced the Modbus connections to be read only during operation. To work around
this limitation and demonstrate full fills, GTI installed a Modbus connected relay that
was used to force open the gas supply valve on the dispenser. These modifications are
pictured inside the dispenser cabinet in Figure 7. If the Smart Controller correctly
identified the vehicle fueling at the dispenser, then the controller would active the
override, holding the gas supply valve open until the vehicle was full. During testing, the
mass flow meter on the dispenser continued to measure the gas that was added to the
vehicle and the GTI team was able to demonstrate 100% full fills.

Equipment Summary
The equipment that was required to make the prototype Smart Dispenser was
somewhat complicated; however, this was mostly because GTI was forced to create
some of these solutions from scratch. The reality is that the functionality of many of
these components could be built into the hardware that already exists onboard most
vehicles and within most CNG stations.
Most vehicles already have controllers integrated into the CNG fuel system and these
microcontrollers are already monitoring fuel system pressures and temperatures. The
biggest change is that the temperature measurements need to be moved into the
cylinders to get an accurate measurement of the fueling conditions.
Vehicle transmitters also exist and most fleets already use various types of vehicle to
station communication. By simply modifying these communication protocols to include
the real time pressure and temperature data from the fuel system, the vehicles can
transmit the filling information to the station.
Most CNG stations also have a central controller that is used to monitor the equipment
status, including the CNG dispensers. With a simple software update, these existing
controllers could also manage the vehicle to dispenser communication and control
smart fills.
Lastly, the dispenser doesn’t need any hardware modifications at all. The software just
needs to be modified so that the station controller can send the dispenser information
and continue fueling the vehicle until it is 100% full.
So nearly all the pieces are in place for the CNG industry to adopt this technology. GTI
is working with various industry partners to continue trying to make this a reality.

Fueling Results
Using the Smart Dispenser hardware and software, GTI was able to safely deliver 10-
15% more fuel to the test vehicles during almost every test. Figure 8 shows this in
action. When the fill is initiated, the dispenser estimates the vehicle’s starting pressure
through the hose of the dispenser. The measured pressure and ambient temperature
are used to estimate the target pressure when the vehicle will be considered full. In the
test below, the dispenser sets this target pressure to just above 3,500 psig at the start of
the fill. This target is called the “Dispenser Max Pressure”. The commercial algorithm
never adjusts this target again and stops the fill once it is reached. In this test, the
commercial dispenser’s target would have been 90% full. However, the Smart
Dispenser continues to fill the vehicle until the fill percent reaches 100%. The last few
percent are slow because the CNG station supply pressure was low during this test, but
the vehicle eventually reached a 100% full state of charge at 3940 psig and 90.4F.

Economics
The cost of the Smart Dispenser appears to be very easy to justify as the majority of the
design can be implemented with software changes to existing hardware. As described
in the hardware descriptions above, the design can theoretically be implemented with
no hardware changes to the station assuming there is already a networked station

 Page 22

controller connected to the existing dispensers. By modifying the code in the existing
station controller and onboard the dispensers, stations can utilize the Smart Station
design without any extra hardware. Even if hardware changes are required, they can
be implemented with very basic controllers. The Raspberry Pi retails for a few hundred
dollars or less, a very affordable investment if it can improve station logistics and
improve vehicle fueling by greater than 10%.
Smart Vehicles will require additional temperature sensors in the cylinders, but most
already have pressure sensors in place. They may also need a modified fuel system
controller, but most modern fuel system controllers already measure pressure,
temperature, and communicate with the CAN bus, so if this approach is adopted, the
incremental cost of the new fuel system controller should be minimal as existing designs
have the majority of the required functionality already in place. For vehicles that need to
be fully modified, the cost to modify the project vehicles was about $2,000 per vehicle.
Considering CNG fuel systems can cost upwards of $1,000 per GGE of storage, the
smart fueling hardware only needs to increase the fuel system utilization by about 2
GGEs to justify installation. Considering testing showed that fills could be consistently
improved by 10-15%, then any vehicle with at least 20 GGE of storage should benefit
from the Smart Fueling system.

SIGNIFICANT ACCOMPLISHMENTS AND CONCLUSIONS
Overall Outcome of Smart CNG System Development
The Smart CNG Station Deployment project successfully developed, demonstrated, and
validated a communications-enabled CNG fueling architecture that directly addresses
long-standing underfilling, safety, and operational variability issues associated with
conventional dispenser control strategies. Through the integration of vehicle-side
sensing, station-side smart control, and real-time communication, the project advanced
the state of practice beyond indirect pressure-based fueling toward an
information-driven approach capable of safely maximizing usable onboard fuel capacity.
Further, this was done wirelessly compared to line of sight or wired methods used in
applications such as hydrogen fueling. This wireless approach provides seamless
integration into existing stations as the driver simply fills the vehicle as they normally
would, unaware that the smart components may be working behind the scenes. The
wireless design also helps to protect the communication equipment as it no longer
needs to be handled by drivers, so it is less likely to be damaged.
Results documented across multiple quarterly reports show consistent improvement in
fill quality, repeatability, and operational consistency, confirming that the Smart CNG
architecture can reliably increase achievable storage utilization without compromising
safety or requiring wholesale replacement of existing fueling infrastructure.
Real-Time Communication Between Smart Station Controller and Smart
Dispenser
A central accomplishment of the project was the successful implementation of real-time
communication between the Vehicle, Smart Station Controller, and Smart Dispenser,
enabling continuous visibility into vehicle-specific fueling conditions. The system
demonstrated the ability to simultaneously monitor multiple vehicles and dispensers, a
requirement for deployment at large fleet fueling facilities with parallel fueling
operations.
During smart fueling events, the Station Controller received real-time pressure and
temperature data directly from each vehicle’s onboard storage system, eliminating
dependence on indirect pressure estimates or ambient temperature proxies. This direct
data stream allowed the Station Controller to automatically identify which vehicle and
dispenser were physically connected, ensuring that vehicle-specific parameters were
correctly associated with the active fueling event.
With continuous awareness of tank conditions and the vehicle storage configuration, the
system could calculate real-time percent fill throughout the fueling process. Rather than
terminating fueling at a fixed pressure limit, the Smart Dispenser dynamically evaluated
the vehicle’s thermodynamic state and filled the vehicle to a specified target percent or
alternatively stopped at pressure and temperature limits defined by the dispenser, tank
design, or applicable codes. This capability represented a fundamental shift from static
fueling logic to adaptive control based on actual vehicle state.
Retrofit Capability for Existing Dispensers

 Page 24

Another significant achievement was the demonstration that existing commercial CNG
dispensers can be retrofitted with Smart Station hardware, avoiding the need for full
dispenser replacement. The project validated a retrofit approach in which the Smart
Controller selectively overrides the default dispenser logic during smart fueling events,
while leaving native functionality unchanged for non-smart vehicles.
A key element of this retrofit capability was the implementation of a solenoid controlled
by the Smart Controller that overrides the CNG supply valve, keeping it open and
flowing until the smart termination criteria are met. This allowed the dispenser to remain
open beyond traditional pressure-based shutoff points, enabling more complete fills
while preserving all inherent safety interlocks. Ideally, dispenser manufacturers will be
able to modify the dispenser’s code in the future to enable more seamless integration of
the smart components while also certifying the final design with all applicable codes.
Applicability to Time-Fill Stations
Beyond fast-fill applications, the Smart CNG architecture demonstrated clear
applicability to time-fill station configurations, where vehicles fuel unattended over
extended periods. In these environments, conventional dispensers lack visibility into
individual vehicle tank conditions and must rely on conservative assumptions that
frequently limit achieved fills.
The Smart Station system enables continuous monitoring of pressure and temperature
for every vehicle connected to a time-fill manifold, allowing fills to be maximized on a
per-vehicle basis. By observing thermal response and pressure stabilization over time,
the system can identify opportunities to safely deliver additional fuel beyond nominal
assumptions.
In parallel, the system enhances safety by monitoring for abnormal pressure or
temperature deviations during extended fueling events. These diagnostics provide early
indication of potential flow restrictions, sensor faults, or vehicle-side anomalies,
improving overall station situational awareness compared to passive time-fill systems.
General Safety and Industry Improvements
The Smart CNG system delivers broader safety and reliability benefits extending
beyond fill optimization. By providing more accurate and consistent fills, the system
improves driver confidence, reduces ambiguity about vehicle range, and minimizes
operational variability across a fleet. Consistency in fueling directly reduces the
incentive for repeated or extended fueling attempts that can introduce safety risks.
In addition, the availability of vehicle-specific data enables automated safety checks and
warnings that are not feasible with conventional dispensers. The system can identify
vehicles with expired tanks or tanks requiring mandated three-year inspections, alerting
operators before fueling proceeds. This capability supports enhanced compliance with
safety regulations and adds a new layer of preventive oversight at the point of fueling.
Collectively, these features represent an industry-level improvement by combining
fueling optimization, safety assurance, and compliance monitoring within a single
integrated system.
Conclusions

 Page 25

The Smart CNG Station Deployment project conclusively demonstrated that
communication-enabled fueling is a practical, deployment-ready solution for addressing
chronic underfilling, inefficiency, and safety limitations in CNG fueling infrastructure. By
shifting the fueling paradigm from indirect inference to real-time vehicle data, the project
achieved measurable improvements in usable fuel capacity, operational consistency,
and safety robustness.
Equally important, the project established a commercially feasible pathway for adoption
by emphasizing retrofit compatibility, modular design, and alignment with existing
industry practices. These accomplishments position the Smart CNG architecture as a
scalable and economically compelling technology capable of strengthening the
long-term competitiveness of natural gas as a transportation fuel while supporting
national energy objectives.

PATH FORWARD
The Smart CNG Station Deployment project has advanced the underlying technology
from concept through successful demonstration, establishing a strong foundation for
broader industry adoption. With the core technical objectives achieved and the system
validated in representative fueling environments, the path forward focuses on
technology dissemination, commercialization support, and continued industry
engagement.
Technology Licensing and Commercialization Strategy
GTI Energy is actively pursuing pathways to license the Smart CNG fueling technology
to interested industry partners, including dispenser manufacturers, station operators,
and fleet fueling service providers. The project was intentionally structured to minimize
dependency on proprietary hardware architectures and to support integration with
existing commercial systems. This approach positions the technology for adoption
through multiple commercialization models, ranging from licensed integration with
original equipment manufacturers to third-party retrofit solutions.
Availability of Operational Hardware Demonstration
A fully functional Smart CNG hardware demonstration system remains operational at
GTI Energy, providing a tangible reference implementation of the technology. This
demonstration environment allows potential adopters to observe real-time vehicle–
station communication, adaptive fueling behavior, and safety monitoring in a controlled
but realistic setting.
Maintaining an operational demonstration has several forward-looking benefits. It
supports technical due diligence by prospective partners, provides a platform for
workforce training and knowledge transfer, and serves as a testbed for incremental
improvements or adaptations tailored to specific station configurations. The availability
of a working system reduces commercialization risk by allowing stakeholders to move
beyond theoretical evaluation to direct observation of system behavior under fueling
conditions.
Software Availability and Replicability
In addition to the hardware demonstration, the Smart CNG software package developed
under this project is available for use by commercial entities interested in replicating or
extending the technology. The software architecture was designed to be modular and
hardware-agnostic, enabling implementation on widely available industrial computing
platforms.
This software package encapsulates the core logic required for vehicle identification,
real-time data acquisition, state-of-charge calculation, and adaptive fueling control. By
making this software accessible, GTI enables commercial companies to integrate Smart
CNG functionality into their own systems, whether through direct deployment,
modification for proprietary environments, or incorporation into next-generation
dispenser control platforms.

 Page 27

The availability of both hardware and software significantly lowers the technical barrier
for industry replication and supports a range of deployment pathways, from pilot-scale
trials to full commercial rollouts.
Broader Industry Adoption and Future Development
Looking ahead, the Smart CNG Station concept offers opportunities for continued
evolution driven by industry needs rather than fundamental research gaps. Future
development efforts are expected to focus on scaling deployment, integration with
additional dispenser models, and standardization of communication protocols to support
interoperability across vehicle and station manufacturers.
The demonstrated ability to retrofit existing dispensers, support both fast-fill and time-fill
operations, and enhance safety and compliance monitoring positions the technology as
a practical upgrade rather than a disruptive replacement. As a result, the primary
remaining challenges are related to adoption, awareness, and commercial alignment
rather than technical feasibility.
In summary, the project has transitioned Smart CNG fueling from a research innovation
to a deployment-ready capability. Through a combination of licensing efforts, open
availability of technical resources, an operational demonstration system, and a
replicable software framework, GTI Energy has established a clear and flexible path
forward that supports near-term industry uptake and long-term impact on CNG fueling
efficiency and safety.
