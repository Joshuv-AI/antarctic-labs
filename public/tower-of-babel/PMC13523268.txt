Socioeconomic inequalities in quality of life among older adults in northwestern Iran: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hosein Azizi, Elham Davtalab Esmaeili, Ali Reza Shafiee-Kandjani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of the present study was to investigate the association between socioeconomic status (SES) and quality of life (QOL) among older adults in northwestern Iran.MethodsIn this population-based cross-sectional study, 806 adults aged 60 years and older were enrolled through stratified random sampling method. QOL was assessed using the WHOQOL-BREF questionnaire, while SES was evaluated with the validated Short SES Questionnaire. Total QOL is derived from the sum of scores across various subscales, with a possible range from 26 to 130 (higher is better). Associations between SES and QOL were assessed using simple and multiple linear regression models.ResultsA distinct socioeconomic gradient in QOL was identified. Compared to participants in the very low SES group, the adjusted regression coefficients for QOL in the low SES group were β = 3.33 (95% CI: 0.85to 6.42), in the medium group β = 5.12 (95% CI: 2.87 to 8.44), in the high group β = 7.99 (95% CI: 5.11 to 12.21). Participants aged 60–65 years reported a higher QOL than those aged 75 years and older (β = 4, 95% CI: 1.23 to 7.01), while widowhood was associated with a lower QOL (β = -7.38, 95% CI: -12.1 to -3.02). After adjustment, gender was not significantly associated with QOL. ConclusionHigher SES was associated with higher QOL. Older age and widowhood were also associated with lower QOL. Thus, enhancing financial support, expanding social services, and prioritizing the socioeconomically disadvantaged and widowed elderly may contribute to promoting equitable and healthy aging.Keywords: Aged, Quality of Life, Socioeconomic Factors, Health Status Disparities, Cross-Sectional Studies

Background

Population aging has emerged as one of the most important demographic developments of recent decades, posing significant challenges to health systems and social welfare structures worldwide [1]. Low- and middle-income countries, including Iran, are experiencing this transition at an accelerating pace, often without the parallel development of adequate social infrastructure and healthcare [2, 3]. In such a context, an exclusive focus on increasing life expectancy, without considering the quality of these long years, may lead to an increased burden of disability, dependency, and health inequalities among older adults [4].

Quality of life (QOL) is widely recognized as a key health outcome in old age, encompassing physical health, psychological well-being, social functioning, and environmental conditions [5, 6]. It is increasingly accepted as a key indicator for assessing the equity and effectiveness of health systems in aging societies. Poor QOL among older adults has been consistently associated with adverse outcomes, including higher prevalence of chronic diseases, decreased functioning, depression, social isolation, and increased use of healthcare services [7].

Within the framework of social determinants of health, socioeconomic status (SES) is considered one of the most fundamental drivers of health inequalities across the lifespan [8]. SES is associated with QOL through several pathways. Material resources play a pivotal role; individuals with higher SES possess greater capacity to access superior housing, more adequate nutrition, and higher-quality healthcare [9]. These material conditions directly influence physical and mental health, thereby shaping overall QOL; higher SES is frequently associated with a greater sense of perceived control over one’s life and more active engagement in social activities, which collectively enhance psychological well-being and life satisfaction [10]. Furthermore, access to formal support services, such as public health programs, career counseling, or financial aid, is often more readily available to individuals with higher SES [11]. These services may help mitigate barriers and improve several aspects of life [12]. Considerable international evidence suggests that older adults with lower SES report significantly lower QOL compared with their more affluent counterparts [13, 14]. Iran is particularly well-suited to examining socioeconomic inequalities in old age, as it faces persistent social and economic inequalities while experiencing rapid population aging [15].

Understanding regional specificities is crucial for accurate analysis and effective policy-making aimed at improving well-being across diverse populations in Iran [16, 17]. Northwestern Iran has been reported to include a combination of urban and rural settings, with economic activities largely based on agriculture and industry, as documented in national statistical reports [18]. In addition, variations in cultural and ethnolinguistic composition across regions may shape social relationships and access to resources [19]. These contextual characteristics may influence the relationship between SES and QOL, consistent with broader evidence on social determinants of health [20]. For instance, family-based support systems may mitigate the adverse effects of low SES on QOL, while structural economic conditions may create both constraints and opportunities that differ from more service-oriented urban settings. Despite its importance, empirical evidence from this region is limited, and existing studies often lack a multidimensional assessment of SES or a specific focus on social inequalities in QOL [21]. In addition, older adults in Iran face multiple challenges in specific areas, including rising healthcare costs, inadequate coverage of formal social protection systems, economic dependence on family members, and gender inequalities in access to resources. These factors may exacerbate the adverse effects of socioeconomic deprivation on QOL in old age [22].

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study in northwestern Iran has simultaneously employed a validated multidimensional SES measure and the WHOQOL-BREF to assess the graded association between SES and QOL while adjusting for key demographic factors. In this regard, the present study aimed to examine the association between SES and QOL among older adults in northwestern Iran, in order to provide context-specific evidence that may help inform future public health planning and research on socioeconomic inequalities in aging. The findings of this study contribute to the growing evidence base on SES-related disparities in QOL among older adults within this specific regional context.

Methods

Study design and setting

This population-based cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024 among adults aged 60 years and older in Tabriz, the capital city of East Azerbaijan Province, Iran. According to the recent population data, the city of Tabriz has an estimated population of approximately 1.73 million people. Based on available national census information and demographic analyses, about 12% of the population is aged 60 years and older.

Sampling procedure

Data collection was carried out in 2024. In the present study, a stratified random sampling was employed. Each of the 10 municipal districts of Tabriz was considered a stratum. Subsequently, two comprehensive health centers were randomly selected from each district (total 20 centers). In accordance with the Ministry of Health’s directives, the population within each region has been registered in the Integrated Health System of health centers following the census. At the beginning of each year, the registered information in this system is updated. For this investigation, from the comprehensive population lists available at each health center, all individuals aged 60 years and above were selected as the initial target subgroup. Subsequently, considering the list of individuals aged 60 years and above at each health center as the sampling frame, the process of sample selection was carried out using the simple random sampling method. To this end, a standard random number table was utilized to ensure that each eligible individual had a known and non-zero chance of being selected for the final sample. The unit of sampling and analysis in this study was the individual older adult. In cases where more than one eligible older adult resided in a household, only one participant was randomly selected for inclusion in the study to avoid within-household clustering.

Participants and sample size

Eligibility criteria included being aged 60 years or older, residing in Tabriz, and providing informed consent to participate. Individuals who completed less than 50% of the questionnaire items or those who were unable to complete the questionnaire due to severe cognitive impairment, significant communication difficulties, or poor health status that precluded meaningful participation were excluded. Based on previous studies assessing QOL among older adults using the WHOQOL-BREF, the mean (SD) QOL score was reported to be approximately 69 (11.5) [23]. Accordingly, the sample size was calculated using the formula for estimating a population mean, assuming a 95% confidence level and a margin of error (d) of 1. The initial required sample size was estimated at 508 participants. Considering the stratified cluster sampling design, a design effect of 1.4 was applied to account for clustering and intra-cluster correlation, resulting in an adjusted sample size of 711. This value was selected based on methodological recommendations for cluster surveys, where design effects typically range from 1.2 to 2.0 depending on the degree of clustering [24]. After accounting for an anticipated non-response rate of approximately 13%, the final sample size was determined to be 806 older adults.

Data collection

Trained interviewers collected demographic information through face-to-face interviews and provided standardized explanations of the study instruments. The other two questionnaires, the WHOQOL-BREF and SES Questionnaire, were completed by participants in a self-administered format, with assistance provided when necessary to ensure completeness and accuracy.

QOL was assessed using the World Health Organization Quality of Life–BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) questionnaire. This instrument has been validated in the Iranian population, demonstrating satisfactory reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficients exceeding 0.70 and Cronbach’s alpha values greater than 0.70 across all domains. The WHOQOL-BREF assesses QOL over the preceding month and includes 26 items across four domains: physical health (7 items), mental health (6 items), environmental health (8 items), and social relationships (3 items). Two additional items assess overall QOL and general health. All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating better perceived QOL [25].

SES was measured using the ultra-short version of the SES Questionnaire. This self-reported instrument demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the present study (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.65). The questionnaire consists of six items assessing occupation, education, housing value, car value, and health-related expenditures and income. Items 1 to 3 are scored on a 7-point Likert scale, items 4 and 5 on a 6-point scale, and item 6 on a 5-point scale. The first item was reverse-coded, and items 3 to 6 were weighted by a factor of 0.25. The total SES score was calculated by summing all item scores, with higher scores reflecting higher SES. Finally, SES scores were categorized into quintiles based on the sample distribution: very low (≤ 20th percentile), low (21st–40th), medium (41st–60th), high (61st–80th), and very high (> 80th) [26].

Data management

We categorized age into four groups (60–65, 65–70, 70–75, and > 75 years) based on commonly used age brackets in geriatric research (https://apps.who.int/iris/handle/10665/186463). These cutoffs reflect distinct stages of functional and health changes in older adults and are widely applied in epidemiologic studies. Five-year intervals ensure adequate sample size within each category while maintaining homogeneity and interpretability. The > 75 category captures the ‘oldest-old’ group, who typically experience the most pronounced declines in health and QOL.

Missing data were minimal (1.95%) due to incomplete questionnaire responses. For the WHOQOL-BREF, domain scores were calculated as the mean of available items when at least 80% of items within a domain were completed, in accordance with standard guidelines. The total QOL score used in the regression analyses was then calculated as the sum of the four WHOQOL-BREF domain scores. No imputation was performed for SES variables, and analyses were conducted using available data. Also, we have performed a test for linear trend across SES categories.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as means and standard deviations for quantitative variables and as frequencies and percentages for qualitative variables. Data normality was assessed using Q–Q plots and skewness–kurtosis tests. The total QOL score was considered the dependent variable [27].

Initially, simple linear regression was performed to examine the associations between QOL and its independent variables. Variable selection for the multiple analyses was not based solely on univariate p-values. Although variables with a p-value < 0.20 in univariate analyses were initially considered to avoid premature exclusion, the final model was constructed using a combination of statistical criteria and substantive knowledge. Key covariates, including age, sex, and socioeconomic-related variables, were included a priori based on evidence from previous studies and their established role as potential confounders. Given the stratified sampling design and clustering of participants within health centers, survey-adjusted analyses were conducted using the “svy” procedures in Stata. Municipal districts were specified as strata, and health centers were treated as primary sampling units to account for potential intra-cluster correlation and design effects. Regression coefficients and standard errors were estimated using survey-adjusted linear regression models.

All classical assumptions of linear regression—including linearity (assessed using the residual–fitted plot), independence of errors (evaluated with the Durbin–Watson test), homoscedasticity (tested using the Breusch–Pagan test), normality of residuals (examined via the Q–Q plot and Shapiro–Wilk test), and the absence of multicollinearity (based on VIF values)—were thoroughly evaluated. The results indicated no systematic patterns; no evidence of heteroscedasticity or deviations from normality, and all VIF values remained within acceptable ranges. Therefore, all model assumptions were met, confirming the statistical validity of the regression model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using the STATA software package (version 16).

Results

Of 901 eligible older adults approached, 806 completed the interview (response rate 89.45%). Non-response was mainly due to refusal (8.6%) or inability to complete the full interview (1.95%). A total of 806 older adults participated in the study. (Fig. 1) The majority of participants were aged 60–65 years (69.23%), and 86.4% were married. Overall, 53% were male, and the mean age was 65.30 ± 6.53 years. Approximately one-third of participants had primary or no formal education (32.6%), while 27.2% had a university education. Regarding SES, participants were fairly evenly distributed across SES categories, with 19.5% falling into the very low SES category and 20.2% into the very high SES category (Table 1). A significant linear trend was observed across SES categories (p for trend = 0.02).

In the multiple linear regression models, SES was associated with QOL. Compared with the very low SES group, individuals with low (β = 3.33, 95% CI: 0.85 to 6.42), medium (β = 5.12, 95% CI: 2.87 to 8.44), high (β = 7.99, 95% CI: 5.11 to 12.21), and very high (β = 14.57, 95% CI: 10.49 to 18.7) SES reported significantly higher QOL scores (all p < 0.05). The magnitude of these coefficients indicates that SES-related differences correspond to moderate to large variations in QOL scores, with increases of 3–5 points in the low and medium SES groups and up to nearly 15 points in the very high SES group.

After adjustment for potential confounders, QOL scores were significantly lower in older age groups compared to younger groups widowed participants had significantly lower QOL compared with married participants (β = −7.38, 95% CI: −12.1 to −3.02). Gender differences in QOL were also weakened after adjustment and did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

The present study assessed the association between SES and QOL among older adults in northwestern Iran, revealing a clear graded socioeconomic pattern in QOL. Our findings indicate that higher socioeconomic status is independently associated with better QOL, even after adjusting for key demographic and social factors. This gradient was observed across all SES categories, with the strongest association seen among individuals in the highest SES group. Compared with older adults in the very low SES group, those in the very high SES group reported significantly higher QOL scores, and the association remained robust after multivariable adjustment. These findings are consistent with a large body of international literature demonstrating socioeconomic inequalities in QOL in later life [28–30]. Similar patterns have been reported in European populations, where income, and material resources are among the strongest determinants of QOL in older age [31]. In low- and middle-income countries, socioeconomic disadvantage may exert a stronger influence on QOL due to limited social protection systems and higher out-of-pocket health expenditures [32]. In the Iranian context, healthcare costs and limited pension coverage may further contribute to socioeconomic disparities in well-being among older adults [33]. These findings support the social determinants of health framework, which emphasizes the role of material conditions and access to resources in shaping health and well-being across the life course [34, 35].

Our findings indicate that adults aged 60–65 years reported a significantly higher QOL compared with older adults over 75 years. This finding aligns with previous studies showing a decline in QOL with increasing age, largely due to functional limitations, multi morbidity, and reduced social participation [36, 37]. However, some studies in high-income settings have reported more stable or U-shaped patterns of QOL in older age, possibly reflecting stronger health and social care systems [38, 39].

Although women reported lower QOL in univariate analyses, this association was attenuated and no longer significant after adjustment. This suggests that socioeconomic and marital factors may partly explain observed gender differences in QOL. Previous studies in Iran and other settings have similarly reported lower QOL among older women [40, 41]. However, some studies have reported persistent gender differences in specific QOL domains even after adjustment [42].

Widowhood was independently associated with lower QOL than married individuals. This is consistent with evidence linking spousal loss to reduced social support, emotional distress, and economic vulnerability in older adults [43, 44]. In contexts where family structures play a central role in support systems, such as Iran, widowhood may have particularly important implications for well-being.

Strengths and limitations

The present study provides regional evidence on socioeconomic inequalities in QOL among older adults in northwestern Iran, using a validated multidimensional measure of SES. The observed socioeconomic gradient may help inform future discussions on equity-oriented approaches to healthy aging, particularly regarding financial protection and social support for socioeconomically disadvantaged older adults. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, causal inferences and definitive policy recommendations should be interpreted cautiously.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and the findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Second, QOL was self-reported and may be subject to reporting bias. Although a composite QOL score was used, future studies should examine domain-specific associations to better capture differential effects of SES. In addition, unmeasured psychosocial factors such as depression, anxiety, and social support may have confounded the observed associations; therefore, results should be interpreted as adjusted only for measured variables. The study was conducted in a single urban setting (Tabriz), which may limit the generalizability of findings to other regions of Iran, particularly rural areas with different socioeconomic and cultural contexts. Therefore, caution is needed when extrapolating results to broader populations. The age distribution was skewed toward younger older adults (60–65 years), with relatively few participants aged ≥ 75 years. This may limit the applicability of findings to the oldest-old population, who often have distinct health and functional profiles. Recruitment through health centers may have underrepresented socially isolated older adults or those with limited access to healthcare services, potentially biasing estimates toward healthier individuals. Finally, although variable selection in multivariable models was guided by statistical criteria and prior knowledge, residual confounding cannot be ruled out.

Conclusion

This study indicates significant socioeconomic inequalities in QOL among older adults in northwestern Iran. A clear graded association was observed, with higher SES consistently associated with better QOL after adjustment for age, sex, marital status, and other included covariates. Older age and widowhood were also identified as important factors associated with lower QOL. These findings may help inform future public health planning aimed at addressing socioeconomic inequalities among older adults. Our findings reveal clear socioeconomic inequalities in QOL among older adults, with participants from higher SES groups reporting substantially better outcomes. These inequalities may indicate areas where further research and supportive public health strategies could be considered..

The present findings may provide useful evidence for policymakers and health authorities when considering strategies to support socially and economically vulnerable older adults. Nevertheless, longitudinal and interventional studies are needed before drawing firm policy conclusions.
