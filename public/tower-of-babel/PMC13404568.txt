Sample size assumptions, timing asymmetries, and endpoint selection limit the interpretability of the CLEANSE hemoadsorption trial in septic shock
Authors: Gonzalo Ramírez-Guerrero, Thiago Reis, Claudio Ronco
Published: 2026

Septic shock remains associated with substantial short-term mortality despite advances in resuscitation and organ support, and extracorporeal cytokine removal continues to be explored as an adjunctive strategy in the most vasopressor-dependent patients. We read with interest the CLEANSE trial by Wongtirawit et al. [1], a prospectively registered, multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating adjunctive hemoadsorption with the HA-330 cartridge in septic shock requiring norepinephrine ≥ 0.2 mcg/kg/min. In brief, the HA-330 is a polystyrene-divinylbenzene cartridge designed for the nonselective removal of protein-bound compounds or soluble middle molecules such as cytokines. The trial was stopped early for slow recruitment and funding constraints after enrolling 128 of a planned 206 patients, and reported a non-significant reduction in 28-day mortality between the control and the intervention group (58% vs. 44%; risk ratio [RR] 0.76, 95% CI 0.54–1.07). The investigators are to be commended for conducting a pragmatic trial addressing an important clinical question in a field otherwise dominated by small, uncontrolled, or retrospective studies. We believe, however, that several design features deserve discussion, not to diminish the authors’ considerable effort, but because a trial with these characteristics risks being cited as evidence either for or against hemoadsorption, when its result is in fact inconclusive.

First, the sample size was calculated assuming an absolute 28-day mortality reduction of 20% points (60% to 40%). Overly optimistic effect-size assumptions are a well-recognized driver of inconclusive critical care RCTs [2]: the EOLIA trial of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation in acute respiratory distress syndrome was powered for a 20-percentage-point absolute mortality reduction and concluded “no significant difference” despite mortality rates of 35% vs. 46% (P = 0.09) [2, 3], and the ANDROMEDA-SHOCK trial was powered for a 15-percentage-point reduction and likewise reported a non-significant result (34.9% vs. 43.4%, P = 0.06) [2, 4]. Both were subsequently shown, in Bayesian reanalyses, to carry a > 95% posterior probability of benefit [2]. The effect size assumed in CLEANSE exceeds both examples, and the observed 14% point difference suggests the trial was structurally underpowered from the outset, independent of how well it was otherwise conducted.

Second, enrollment was permitted up to 24 h after patients first met the norepinephrine threshold, with vasopressor dose (i.e., norepinephrine or equivalent) ≥ 0.2 mcg/kg/min as the only enrichment criterion. This wide, biomarker-free window allowed inclusion of patients at heterogeneous stages of their inflammatory trajectory, some potentially beyond a therapeutically modifiable window, analogous to the post-hoc observation in EUPHRATES that patients with endotoxin activity assay > 0.9 derived no benefit from polymyxin B hemoadsorption, unlike those with more moderate endotoxin activity assay [5], a distinction that motivated the narrower enrichment range subsequently tested in TIGRIS [6].

Third, the definition of “hour 0”, the reference point for all outcomes, introduced an asymmetry between arms: fixed at 6 h post-randomization in the standard treatment group, but variable and later (median 7.5 h, interquartile range up to 13 h) in the hemoadsorption group, reflecting time needed for vascular access and circuit preparation. While the authors’ rationale is transparent, this design subtly delays and dilutes the earliest treatment window specifically for the intervention arm. Furthermore, in an RCT with a comparable intervention [6], data assessment started immediately after randomization.

Fourth, each hemoadsorption session lasted only 3 h, for a cumulative 6 h of blood purification over the 48-hour protocol, a comparatively brief dose in a hyperinflammatory state where endogenous cytokine production can exceed a cartridge’s adsorptive capacity within hours [7]. If mortality was the outcome of interest, it is unclear why treatment duration was capped rather than continued until a proximal physiological target, such as vasopressor weaning, was reached.

Finally, and consequently, 28-day mortality is difficult to reconcile with a 48-hour intervention whose organ-support co-interventions were not protocolized beyond that window. Critical care RCTs that focus on mortality alone convey comparatively little statistical information and generally require very large samples to detect plausible effects [2]; more granular, mechanistically proximal outcomes, vasopressor-free days, shock reversal, or organ support-free days in the first 1–2 weeks, may be more sensitive to a true, if modest, effect [2]. A recent illustration of this principle is the ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 trial, which found no significant difference in 28-day mortality between arms (26.5% vs. 26.6%; P=0.91) but detected a significant treatment effect using a hierarchical composite outcome of mortality, duration of vital support, and length of hospital stay analyzed with the win ratio method (win ratio, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.02–1.33), driven mainly by a shorter duration of vital support [8]. We recognize this recommendation is not free of trade-offs: disease- or physiology-centered endpoints, while statistically more efficient, do not always track what patients and families consider most important, and their use can inflate the apparent rate of “positive” trials without necessarily reflecting real clinical benefit [9]. Both mortality and a proximal, biologically coherent outcome should ideally be reported together.

We wish to emphasize that our intent is neither to argue for nor against the clinical use of hemoadsorption with polystyrene-divinylbenzene cartridge in septic shock. A trial combining an overly optimistic effect-size assumption, a wide and unenriched enrollment window, an asymmetric treatment-initiation reference point, a plausibly under-dosed intervention, and a mortality endpoint poorly matched to a brief treatment protocol cannot be reliably interpreted as either positive, neutral, or negative. We would encourage future definitive trials in this field to incorporate biomarker or severity-based enrichment, a treatment initiation reference point applied symmetrically across arms, treatment intensity calibrated to a physiological target, and endpoints that are temporally and mechanistically coherent with the intervention under study, ideally embedded within a broader research program rather than a single, isolated trial [2].
