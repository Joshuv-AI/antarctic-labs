Microstructural enhancement of concrete paver blocks using steel sludge as a sustainable fine aggregate replacement
Authors: Muhammad Tariq Bashir, Ratan Lal, Ahmed Almutairi, Md. Munir Hayet Khan, Ahmed Othman Alanazi, Muhammad Ali Sikandar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growing demand for sustainable construction materials has accelerated the need for alternatives to natural aggregates in concrete. Although steel slag has been extensively studied, the use of steel sludge as a fine aggregate substitute in concrete paver blocks has not been sufficiently examined, especially regarding durability and microstructural characteristics. This study investigates the performance of steel sludge as a micro-filler and partial replacement of fine aggregate at 10%, 20%, and 30% by mass, maintaining a constant water–cement ratio of 0.38. Mechanical and durability properties were evaluated using compressive strength, water absorption, sorptivity, rapid chloride penetration, and weight loss tests, complemented by SEM, EDX, and XRD analyses. The results indicate a consistent improvement in performance with increasing steel sludge content. The 28-day compressive strength increased from 42.77 MPa for the control mix to 54.13 MPa at 30% replacement, representing an approximate 26.6% increase. Water absorption decreased from 6.12% to 4.20%, the initial absorption rate decreased from 0.0332 to 0.0250 mm/min⁰·⁵. and RCPT values declined from 640 to 552 Coulombs, reflecting reduced permeability. Microstructural analysis demonstrated pore refinement, a lower Ca/Si ratio, and enhanced formation of polymerized C–(A)–S–H gel. XRD analysis confirmed the absence of new crystalline phases. Within the investigated range, steel sludge enhances strength and durability through matrix densification and microstructural refinement. These findings demonstrate its potential as a sustainable industrial waste management to natural sand in paver block applications.

Introduction

The construction industry plays a pivotal role in global infrastructure development; however, its heavy reliance on natural resources has resulted in significant environmental and sustainability challenges. Concrete remains the most widely used construction material due to its high compressive strength, durability, and versatility1. The extensive use of concrete has led to the over-exploitation of natural aggregates, particularly river sand, causing ecological imbalance, riverbed degradation, groundwater depletion, and increased carbon emissions. Consequently, identifying alternative materials that can partially replace natural aggregates without compromising performance has become a critical research priority.

Industrial by-products and waste materials have recently attracted significant attention as environmentally sustainable alternatives in cementitious composites2,3. The use of these wastes reduces environmental pollution from landfill disposal, conserves natural resources, and supports circular economy principles4. Among these, steel industry by-products have emerged as promising construction materials5. The majority of existing literature examines steel slag, a coarse and crystalline by-product, as a replacement for coarse or fine aggregates in concrete and pavement applications6,7. These studies often report enhancements in compressive strength, abrasion resistance, and durability when steel slag is used at optimal replacement levels. Recent investigations into mortar and concrete incorporating steel slag and similar industrial by-products have also demonstrated improved durability and microstructural refinement, further supporting the feasibility of utilizing fine waste in cementitious systems8,9.

In contrast, steel sludge, a fine-grained waste produced during steel manufacturing, rolling, and finishing, has received limited research attention10,11. Steel sludge differs significantly from steel slag in particle size, morphology, and physical properties. It consists primarily of ultra-fine particles rich in iron oxides, with smaller amounts of silica, calcium, and alumina, and is frequently discarded as industrial waste due to handling and storage challenges. The fine particle size of steel sludge indicates its potential suitability as a micro-filler in cementitious systems, where it may contribute to pore refinement and matrix densification rather than functioning as a structural aggregate12,13.

Although some studies have examined metallurgical sludge and other fine industrial residues as sand alternatives in conventional concrete, there is a lack of controlled research specifically investigating steel sludge as a partial fine aggregate replacement in concrete paver blocks14,15. This research gap is significant because paver blocks are widely used in sidewalks, parking lots, industrial floors, and low-traffic pavements, where durability properties such as water absorption, permeability, and resistance to environmental exposure are more critical than compressive strength16,17. Furthermore, most existing studies focus primarily on strength performance, with limited assessment of durability metrics such as sorptivity, chloride penetration resistance, and weight loss behavior18,19.

Another limitation in the current literature is the absence of clear microstructural explanations that connect observed performance improvements to underlying material mechanisms20,21. Although increased strength and durability are frequently reported, the influence of fine industrial wastes on pore structure, interfacial transition zones, and matrix continuity is rarely addressed systematically22,23. Addressing this aspect is essential to substantiate the long-term applicability of waste-based paver blocks and to promote their adoption in engineering practice.

This study examines the use of steel sludge as a partial replacement for fine aggregate in the production of concrete paver blocks. Steel sludge was incorporated at replacement levels of 10%, 20%, and 30% by weight of fine aggregate, and its effects on mechanical and durability performance were systematically evaluated. The experimental program assessed compressive strength, water absorption, sorptivity, rapid chloride penetration resistance, and weight loss, all of which are directly relevant to the service performance of paver blocks. In addition, microstructural analysis was performed to interpret observed performance trends in terms of matrix densification and pore refinement.

Despite the growing interest in industrial by-products for sustainable construction, the application of steel sludge as a fine aggregate replacement in concrete paver blocks remains underexplored, especially regarding durability and microstructural characteristics. Most existing research emphasizes strength-based assessments or focuses on coarser by-products such as steel slag, with minimal attention to ultra-fine sludge materials and their physicochemical interactions within cementitious matrices. This study addresses this gap by systematically integrating mechanical performance, durability evaluation (including water absorption, sorptivity, rapid chloride penetration, and weight loss), and multi-scale microstructural analysis (SEM, EDX, and XRD) to clarify the function of steel sludge as a micro-filler. The findings establish explicit relationships among pore structure refinement, gel evolution, and transport properties, thereby providing a comprehensive structure–property framework for paver block applications. Additionally, the results indicate that, when adequately processed and applied at optimal replacement levels, steel sludge can be a viable and sustainable substitute for natural sand, supporting eco-efficient construction without compromising essential performance criteria.

Materials and methods

Raw materials

All paver block mixtures utilized Ordinary Portland Cement of 43 grade, meeting the requirements of IS 811222. Natural river sand, conforming to IS 383:2016, served as the fine aggregate to ensure adequate interlocking and load transfer between paver blocks. Crushed coarse aggregate with a nominal maximum size of 10 mm was also incorporated as fine aggregate24. Mixing and curing were conducted using potable water free from impurities. To maintain workability at lower water-cement ratios, a polycarboxylate-based superplasticizer was added at a dosage of 0.5% by weight of cement. Steel sludge was sourced from a local steel manufacturing plant and partially substituted for natural fine aggregate. The sludge was air-dried to remove free moisture, then mechanically ground and sieved to achieve particle sizes comparable to fine aggregate fractions. Prior to mixing, the processed steel sludge was stored in closed containers to prevent moisture absorption.

Physical and chemical properties of steel sludge

The processed steel sludge appeared dark grey, was fine-grained, and exhibited irregular particle morphology. In comparison to natural sand, steel sludge has a finer particle size distribution, lower specific gravity, and a more angular particle shape. These characteristics enhance its micro-filler effect and pore-refinement capability within cementitious matrices25,26. Prior to mix preparation, the physical properties of natural sand and steel sludge were experimentally determined. Specific gravity, bulk density, water absorption, moisture content, and particle size distribution were measured according to ASTM C128, ASTM C29, ASTM C566, and ASTM C136, respectively. The measured specific gravity of steel sludge (2.48) was approximately 6.1% lower than that of natural sand (2.64), and its bulk density was approximately 9.8% lower. Although steel sludge is not classified as a structural lightweight aggregate, these results support its use as a comparatively lightweight micro-filler material. Steel sludge also exhibited a significantly finer particle size distribution, with higher percentages passing the 300 μm and 150 μm sieves. The finer particles allow steel sludge to occupy interparticle voids within the cementitious matrix, thereby improving particle packing, reducing porosity, and enhancing durability-related performance. The lower fineness modulus (1.92) and smaller mean particle size (185 μm), compared with natural sand (510 μm), further confirm its suitability as a pore-refining filler material. X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis indicated that steel sludge was composed primarily of iron oxide (Fe₂O₃), with moderate amounts of silica (SiO₂) and calcium oxide (CaO), and smaller quantities of alumina (Al₂O₃), magnesium oxide (MgO), and manganese oxide (MnO). The loss on ignition (LOI) value was within acceptable limits, indicating negligible organic contamination. Although the chemical composition indicates that steel sludge lacks significant cementitious activity, its fine particle size and mineralogical composition contribute to matrix densification, pore refinement, and improved microstructural integrity through a microfiller mechanism.

Mix proportioning and sample preparation

The concrete paving block mixtures were designed in accordance with the recommendations for interlocking concrete pavement structures provided by IRC: SP: 63-200427(Fig. 1). One control mix (Mc) and three steel-sludge-modified mixes were prepared by partially replacing natural fine aggregate with steel sludge at replacement levels of 10% (M1), 20% (M2), and 30% (M3) by mass. These replacement levels were selected based on previous studies involving industrial by-products, which reported optimum mechanical and durability performance within this range without adversely affecting workability or strength8,9.

Mix proportions were calculated on a per-cubic-meter basis to ensure reproducibility and facilitate comparison among mixtures. A constant water-to-cement ratio (w/c) of 0.38 was maintained for all mixes to isolate the effect of steel sludge incorporation on the performance of concrete paver blocks. The detailed mix proportions adopted in this study are presented in Table 2.

Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) served as the primary binder, while natural sand and crushed coarse aggregate with a nominal maximum size of 10 mm were used as fine and coarse aggregates, respectively. Steel sludge was introduced as a partial replacement for natural sand without altering the total fine aggregate content, thereby maintaining a consistent aggregate-to-binder ratio and paste volume across all mixtures. This approach enabled the influence of steel sludge on the mechanical, durability, and microstructural properties of the paver blocks to be evaluated independently.

A polycarboxylate-based superplasticizer was incorporated at a fixed dosage of 0.5% by weight of cement to achieve adequate workability and ensure uniform compaction of the fresh mixtures. Prior to mixing, steel sludge was air-dried, ground, and sieved to obtain a particle size distribution comparable to that of the fine aggregate fraction. All dry constituents, including cement, aggregates, and steel sludge, were thoroughly blended to ensure uniform material distribution. Subsequently, mixing water and the required quantity of superplasticizer were gradually added, and mixing continued until a homogeneous concrete mixture was obtained.

The fresh concrete was cast into steel molds measuring 200 mm × 160 mm × 60 mm, corresponding to the dimensions of the paver blocks used in this study. Compaction was performed using mechanical vibration to minimize entrapped air and achieve adequate densification. Specimens were demolded after 24 h and subsequently cured in potable water at room temperature until the designated testing ages.

Mechanical and durability tests were conducted at the specified curing periods using three representative specimens for each mixture. The reported results correspond to the arithmetic mean of three measurements, while statistical analyses, including standard deviation and analysis of variance (ANOVA), were performed to assess data reliability and the significance of observed differences among the mixtures (Fig. 2).

Mechanical and durability testing

Compressive strength was evaluated at 7 and 28 days using a calibrated digital compression testing machine with a 3000 kN capacity, operated under displacement-controlled loading at a uniform rate in accordance with IS 516 standards. All specimens were prepared to ensure uniform load distribution. The compressive strength values reported are the mean of three specimens per mix. Durability was assessed through water absorption, sorptivity, rapid chloride penetration test (RCPT), and weight loss measurements. Water absorption was measured according to IS 3495 (Part 2), requiring oven-dried samples to be immersed in water overnight. Water absorption kinetics were evaluated in accordance with ASTM C1585 by determining the initial and secondary absorption rates from cumulative absorption curves. Table 3 summarizes the mix proportions used in this study. RCPT was performed in accordance with ASTM C1202 to evaluate chloride ion penetration resistance as an indicator of permeability28,29. Weight loss measurements were used to assess resistance to environmental degradation under exposure conditions.

Furthermore, statistical analysis including mean, standard deviation, and ANOVA was performed to validate experimental results.

Material characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was employed to investigate the microstructural characteristics of concrete paver blocks with varying steel sludge content. Microstructural analysis was conducted using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, JEOL JSM-7600 F, JEOL Ltd., Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 15–20 kV. After 28 days of curing, representative specimens from each mixture were selected. Fractured surfaces were prepared to expose the internal matrix, oven-dried to remove moisture, and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity and imaging quality.

Micrographs at multiple magnifications were analyzed to assess pore structure, morphology, interfacial continuity, and matrix densification30. The analysis was primarily qualitative, focusing on microstructural evolution due to steel sludge incorporation rather than direct phase identification. The observed features provided insight into pore refinement and the development of a denser cementitious matrix with increasing sludge content. SEM provides morphological information, while phase identification was supported by EDX and XRD analyses.

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

Energy-dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX), in conjunction with Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), was utilized to analyze the elemental composition of the cementitious matrix and to assess the distribution of steel sludge constituents within concrete paver blocks. EDX spectra and elemental maps were collected from specific matrix regions to determine the presence and relative abundance of key elements, such as calcium (Ca), silicon (Si), aluminum (Al), iron (Fe), and oxygen (O), which are integral to hydration products and matrix development.

EDX measurements were conducted using an Oxford Instruments X-Max 80 mm² detector mounted on a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM). Spectra were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a working distance of approximately 10 mm. To ensure reproducibility and representative characterization, three distinct matrix regions were analyzed per specimen, and the reported values represent the average of these measurements. Elemental compositions were initially determined as normalized elemental weight percentages, with carbon excluded from the analysis to reduce potential interference from surface coatings, carbonation, and background contamination.

Elemental concentrations were subsequently converted to oxide-equivalent compositions using standard stoichiometric conversion factors and reported in accordance with established materials characterization protocols. The notation Fe₂O₃ refers to the oxide-equivalent representation of detected iron content and does not imply that all iron is present solely as Fe₂O₃. Identification of specific crystalline iron-bearing phases was independently confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. EDX was therefore primarily employed to evaluate elemental distribution, Ca/Si ratios, and compositional variations related to gel formation, matrix densification, and the incorporation of steel sludge within the cementitious system. Quantitative analysis of the EDX data was used to establish correlations between compositional changes and the mechanical and durability performance observed in the paver blocks.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted to identify the crystalline phases present in steel sludge and selected concrete paver block specimens. Powdered samples were prepared from crushed portions of hardened specimens after 28 days of curing. Diffraction data were collected using a diffractometer with Cu–Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over a 2θ range of 10° to 80°, at a scanning rate of 2°/min and a step size of 0.02°. The operating voltage and current were set at 40 kV and 30 mA, respectively.

Phase identification was performed by comparing the obtained diffraction patterns with standard reference data from the International Centre for Diffraction Data31. Major peaks corresponding to portlandite (Ca(OH)₂), quartz (SiO₂), calcite (CaCO₃), and iron oxide phases were identified based on their characteristic 2θ positions and relative intensities. This method ensured consistency with established crystallographic data, thereby enhancing the reliability and reproducibility of the analysis.

The XRD results were subsequently used to determine whether steel sludge incorporation led to the formation of new hydration products or if the observed performance improvements were primarily due to physical mechanisms such as particle packing and pore refinement.

Results and discussion

Compressive strength performance

Figure 3 presents the compressive strength performance of paver blocks incorporating steel sludge as a replacement for fine aggregate, with results reported for both 7-day and 28-day curing periods. An increase in compressive strength was observed with higher steel sludge content as curing progressed. At 28 days, the compressive strength of mixes M1, M2, and M3 improved by approximately 15, 20, and 27%, respectively, compared to the control mix. These results indicate that steel sludge does not adversely affect cement hydration or load-bearing capacity. The micro-filler effect of steel sludge is likely the primary factor contributing to the observed strength enhancement. Steel sludge particles fill micro-voids within the cement matrix, resulting in increased packing density and reduced porosity. This densification enhances the ability of the cementitious matrix to transfer stress under compressive loading. Similar effects have been reported for other fine industrial by-products used as sand substitutes, further supporting the validity of these findings.

Statistical analysis was conducted on compressive strength data collected from three specimens per mix to ensure the reliability of the experimental results (Table 4). Mean values and standard deviations were calculated to quantify variability. In addition, a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) test demonstrated that the differences between the control and modified mixes were statistically significant (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that the observed improvements in compressive strength result from the micro-filler effect and matrix densification caused by steel sludge incorporation, rather than random variation.

Bulk density and water absorption

Figure 4 presents the bulk density of paver blocks at various steel sludge replacement levels. Bulk density increased from 1756 kg/m³ for the control mix to 1841 kg/m³ for the M3 mix, indicating improved particle packing and reduced internal voids. This trend aligns with the enhanced compressive strength reported in Sect. 3.1.

Water absorption values decreased significantly as steel sludge content increased, dropping from 6.12% for the control mix to 4.20% for the M3 mix, representing a reduction of approximately 31%. This decrease indicates reduced pore connectivity and improved resistance to moisture ingress, which is essential for durability in outdoor pavement applications. The trend demonstrates that steel sludge functions as an effective micro-filler, filling capillary voids and refining the pore structure to restrict water transport pathways (Fig. 5).

Table 5 summarizes the detailed bulk density and water absorption values for all mixes. Statistical analysis confirmed that these differences are significant (p < 0.05), indicating that the improvements result from the incorporation of steel sludge rather than experimental variability.

Initial and secondary absorption rate and rapid chloride penetration test

In accordance with ASTM C1585, cumulative water absorption per unit area was plotted against the square root of time to determine the initial and secondary absorption rates of the tested mixtures. The initial absorption rate corresponds to the slope of the linear portion obtained during the first six hours of exposure and characterizes early-age capillary suction, whereas the secondary absorption rate was determined from measurements obtained between one and eight days and represents long-term fluid transport through the pore network. As illustrated in Fig. 6a and b, both the initial and secondary absorption curves exhibited progressively lower slopes with increasing steel sludge content, indicating reduced capillary pore connectivity and enhanced matrix densification.

The results demonstrate that the incorporation of steel sludge significantly reduced water absorption kinetics throughout the testing period. The initial absorption rate decreased from 0.0332 mm/min⁰·⁵ for the control mix (Mc) to 0.0250 mm/min⁰·⁵ for the M3 mix, representing a reduction of approximately 24.7%. Similarly, the secondary absorption rate decreased from 0.0148 to 0.0101 mm/min⁰·⁵, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 31.8%. These reductions indicate a substantial improvement in resistance to capillary water ingress and confirm the refinement of the pore structure at higher steel sludge replacement levels. The observed behavior is attributed to the micro-filler effect of steel sludge, whereby finer particles occupy void spaces within the cementitious matrix, resulting in improved particle packing and reduced pore continuity.

The reduction in both initial and secondary absorption rates is consistent with the decrease in water absorption reported in Sect. 3.2 and further supports the development of a denser microstructure. The M3 mixture exhibited the lowest absorption rates among all mixes, indicating superior resistance to moisture transport and long-term fluid penetration. Detailed values of initial absorption rate, secondary absorption rate, rapid chloride penetration test (RCPT), and weight loss are presented in Table 6.

Similarly, the results of the rapid chloride penetration test (RCPT) demonstrated a progressive decrease in the total charge passed with increasing steel sludge content, indicating enhanced resistance to ionic penetration. The charge passed decreased from 640 Coulombs for the control mix to 552 Coulombs for the M3 mixture, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 13.8%. According to ASTM C1202 classification, all mixtures fall within the low permeability category; however, the gradual reduction in charge passed with increasing steel sludge content confirms the beneficial influence of steel sludge on transport properties.

The improved chloride penetration resistance can be attributed to the densification of the cementitious matrix and the refinement of capillary pores resulting from the incorporation of fine steel sludge particles. Although concrete paver blocks are generally not reinforced, RCPT provides a useful indication of pore connectivity and permeability characteristics. The reduction in charge passed therefore confirms the formation of a denser and less permeable matrix, which is consistent with the microstructural observations obtained from SEM and the compositional changes identified through EDX analysis. SEM microstructural analysis

Figure 9(a–d) shows SEM micrographs of the control mix and modified mix of various magnifications to show how hydration products and densification of the matrix have evolved. SEM image of the control specimen shows a non-homogeneous and relatively porous microstructure that is defined by loosely packed hydration products and open micro-voids. The plate-like and flaky crystals that are indicative of the calcium hydroxide (CH) are easily observable, which proves to be typical of the Portland cement hydration. The existence of unoccupied pores and poor interfacial cavities implies the failure of hydration products to occupy the pore fully, and it usually leads to the low mechanical strength and high permeability. The morphology reveals minimal secondary hydration and a reduced binding matrix.

M1 has a refined and moderately densified microstructure as compared to the control. The matrix comprises granular and foil-like hydrated products that fill in the pore spaces partially. The decreased visibility of large CH plates implies that there was the partial use of calcium hydroxide owing to second-order reactions. Increased hydration activity is also demonstrated by the creation of finer gel-like products, which contribute to the increased particle bonding and decreasing porosity. Nevertheless, there are still some micro-voids, which means that the densification has been enhanced, yet not optimized.

The micrograph of M2 in the SEM mode illustrates that the microstructure is much more homogeneous and dense. Compact and interlocked hydration products are predominant in the matrix, and the number of observable pores is lower. The fibrous and foil-like morphologies are explained by the fact that C–S–H gel is well-developed and, thus, serves well in attaching the particles. The lower level of unreacted grains and CH crystals proves to the improved pozzolanic or auxiliary reactions, which result in a tightly packed microstructure. This densification justifies the high mechanical and durability performance that this mix has exhibited.

The apparent difference between calcium hydroxide (CH) and calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) phases can be seen in Fig. 9d. The red circles point out the preeminence of a network of dense C–S–H gel, and few traces of CH are observed. Extensive coverage of C–S–H gel saturates the pores of the capillaries and reinforces the transition zones between the interfaces, which leads to the formation of a very thick and continuous structure. This microstructural refinement shows that there is an efficient use of calcium ions and a high level of secondary hydration, and this is directly related to the development of increased strength and lower permeability.

The SEM observations confirm a progressive transformation from a porous and heterogeneous microstructure (control) to a dense, C–S–H-rich matrix (M3). The reduction in CH content and pore volume, coupled with increased formation of compact hydration products, demonstrates the effectiveness of the modified mixes in improving microstructural integrity. These findings strongly correlate with the observed improvements in mechanical strength and durability performance.

EDX elemental analysis

The EDX spectrum of the control mixture indicated that calcium (Ca), oxygen (O), and silicon (Si) were the predominant elements, confirming the presence of typical Portland cement hydration products. The relatively high Ca/Si ratio in the control specimen aligns with the abundance of calcium hydroxide (CH) and lower levels of polymerized calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel. Minor amounts of aluminum (Al), iron (Fe), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na), and potassium (K) were also detected, attributable to cement clinker phases, aggregate minerals, and naturally occurring impurities. The elevated calcium content and lower silicon concentration suggest limited secondary reactions and correspond with SEM observations of prominent CH crystals and a relatively porous microstructure. Representative EDX compositions are provided in Table 7.

EDX analysis of the M1 mixture revealed a reduction in calcium content and increased concentrations of silicon and aluminum compared to the control mixture. This compositional change indicates partial consumption of calcium hydroxide and enhanced formation of hydration products. The lower Ca/Si ratio reflects greater development of C–S–H gel, consistent with SEM observations of a more refined and uniformly distributed microstructure. The increased aluminum content likely contributes to the formation of C–(A)–S–H phases, further improving matrix cohesion and microstructural refinement.

EDX results for the M2 mixture demonstrated a more balanced elemental composition, with a further decrease in the Ca/Si ratio and increased silicon content. These changes indicate enhanced formation of hydration products and more efficient utilization of calcium ions within the cementitious matrix. The higher aluminum concentration suggests increased incorporation of aluminum into the gel structure, promoting the formation of cross-linked C–(A)–S–H networks and contributing to the dense and homogeneous microstructure observed in SEM analysis. The improved elemental distribution aligns with the enhanced compressive strength and durability performance recorded for this mixture.

The EDX spectrum of the M3 mixture exhibited the highest silicon concentration and the lowest Ca/Si ratio among all tested mixtures. The reduced calcium content indicates a lower proportion of free calcium hydroxide and a greater extent of polymerized hydration products. The increased concentrations of silicon and aluminum suggest continued development and stabilization of C–(A)–S–H gel networks, resulting in a highly compact and continuous matrix. These findings are consistent with SEM observations, which revealed extensive matrix densification, minimal pore connectivity, and improved interfacial bonding.

Overall, EDX analysis identified iron as a significant constituent in the steel sludge-containing mixtures, along with calcium, silicon, aluminum, and oxygen, which are associated with the formation and evolution of cement hydration products. The Fe₂O₃ values reported in Table 7 represent oxide-equivalent compositions derived from elemental iron concentrations using standard stoichiometric conversion factors and should not be interpreted as direct evidence that all iron exists exclusively in the Fe₂O₃ phase. Thus, EDX results primarily indicate elemental distribution and compositional trends rather than definitive phase identification. The presence of iron-bearing crystalline phases was independently confirmed through XRD analysis. Additionally, the progressive reduction in the Ca/Si ratio from approximately 1.75 in the control mixture to 0.70 in the M3 mixture indicates enhanced formation and polymerization of C–(A)–S–H gel networks. This compositional evolution corresponds with SEM observations of a denser microstructure and supports the improved compressive strength, reduced water absorption, lower initial and secondary absorption rates, enhanced chloride penetration resistance, and superior overall durability performance observed in the steel sludge-modified paver blocks.

Analysis of XRD

Figure 10 presents the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the control mix and steel sludge-modified paver blocks (M1–M3) within a 2θ range of 5–70°. The similarity in diffraction features across all mixes suggests that the incorporation of steel sludge does not alter the fundamental crystalline phase structure of the cementitious system.

The control mix exhibits pronounced reflections of portlandite (CH) at approximately 18°, 34°, and 47° (2θ), indicating a calcium-rich hydration environment with a substantial amount of residual calcium hydroxide. Distinct peaks corresponding to quartz (~ 26.6°) and calcite (~ 29.4°) are also observed, attributed to fine aggregates and minor carbonation, respectively. Additionally, a broad amorphous hump appears in the 20–35° range, associated with poorly crystallized C–S–H gel, although the intensity of this hump remains relatively low.

In the M1 mix containing steel sludge, the intensity of the CH peak is markedly reduced, accompanied by a slight increase in the amorphous hump. This trend suggests preferential consumption of portlandite and the formation of gel-like hydration products. The quartz and calcite peaks remain largely unchanged, indicating that steel sludge does not promote the formation of new crystalline phases.

For the M2 mix, CH reflections are further suppressed, and the amorphous hump becomes broader and more intense, indicating an increased presence of C–(A)–S–H-type gel. The reduced intensity of clinker-related and CH peaks suggests enhanced matrix densification and improved efficiency of hydration products. The M3 mix exhibits the lowest CH peak intensities among all samples, along with the most pronounced amorphous contribution in the 20–35° range. This pattern indicates extensive consumption of CH and a predominance of gel-based binding phases, consistent with a highly compact and refined microstructure. The persistence of quartz and calcite peaks confirms phase stability and supports the conclusion that performance improvements are primarily due to physical densification and pore refinement, rather than the formation of new crystalline compounds.

The XRD results corroborate the findings from SEM and EDX analyses, demonstrating a progressive transformation from a CH-rich system in the control mix to a C–(A)–S–H-dominated system in M3. This transformation aligns with observed increases in compressive strength and durability index, confirming the effectiveness of steel sludge as a micro-filler in concrete paver block applications.

Influence of mix design on mechanical and microstructural performance

A mix design strategy maintaining a constant water-to-cement (w/c) ratio and controlled fine aggregate replacement enabled direct assessment of steel sludge effects on paver block performance. The progressive increase in compressive strength from Mc to M3 is attributed to the ultra-fine size of steel sludge particles, which enhance particle packing and minimize intergranular gaps. This micro-filler effect densifies the matrix without altering cement content or water demand.

Durability properties, including water absorption, sorptivity, and resistance to chloride ion penetration, systematically improved with increasing steel sludge content. These changes indicate reduced connectivity within the pore and capillary transport systems, consistent with the refined microstructure observed in SEM images. The constant w/c ratio ensures that these enhancements are primarily due to physical densification rather than changes in hydration.

Microstructural evidence from SEM, EDX, and XRD analyses further supports this interpretation. XRD patterns show a steady decrease in portlandite peak intensity with increasing steel sludge concentration, alongside a more pronounced amorphous hump associated with C–(A)–S–H gel. EDX analysis indicates a decreasing Ca/Si ratio as more aluminum is incorporated into the binding phase, suggesting the formation of a more complex and chemically stable C–A–S–H network. SEM observations confirm a transition from a relatively porous structure in control mixes to a compact, gel-rich structure in M3.

Conclusion

The findings demonstrate that steel industry sludge serves as an effective component in cement-based inorganic polymer composites for concrete paver block production. Partial replacement of natural fine aggregate with steel sludge at 10–30% by mass, under a constant water–cement ratio, enhances composite performance primarily through physicochemical interactions rather than chemical activation.

Mechanical testing indicates a consistent increase in compressive strength with higher steel sludge content, reaching 54.13 MPa at 28 days for the 30% replacement mixture. This represents an improvement of approximately 26.6% over the control. The strength enhancement is attributed to improved particle packing and the formation of a denser polymerized gel matrix, which facilitates more efficient stress transfer within the composite.

Durability-related properties, such as water absorption, sorptivity, chloride-ion permeability, and weight loss, decreased systematically with increasing steel sludge incorporation. These results indicate a refined pore network and reduced transport pathways, consistent with the formation of a compact inorganic polymer matrix. The observed reductions in permeability parameters confirm that steel sludge acts as a micro-filler, enhancing matrix continuity and interfacial stability.

Microstructural and chemical analyses provide direct evidence of gel evolution and polymerization mechanisms. SEM observations reveal a transition from a heterogeneous and porous microstructure in the control mix to a dense, interconnected gel-rich morphology at higher steel sludge contents. EDX analysis shows a significant decrease in the Ca/Si ratio from 1.75 to 0.70, along with increased aluminum participation, indicating enhanced polymerization and cross-linking of C–(A)–S–H gel networks. XRD analysis confirms the absence of new crystalline phases, supporting the conclusion that performance improvements result from inorganic polymer gel densification and microstructural refinement rather than the formation of new hydration products.

Within the investigated range of 0–30%, the 30% steel sludge replacement level demonstrated the best overall performance regarding strength, durability, and microstructural stability. These findings establish steel sludge as a viable material for sustainable cementitious composites, thereby supporting sustainable materials design and industrial waste valorization.

However, the present study is limited to compressive strength and durability-related properties. Future research should address flexural and tensile behavior, long-term durability under aggressive environmental conditions, and field-scale performance validation.

In summary, this study advances sustainable construction materials by presenting a comprehensive structure–property framework that connects microstructural evolution with macroscopic performance. The findings offer practical insights for the large-scale utilization of steel industry waste in pavement applications.
