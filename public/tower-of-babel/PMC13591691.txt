Multidrug resistance, biofilm formation, and pathotype diversity of Escherichia coli isolated from diarrheic lambs in intensive sheep farms, Aksu, Xinjiang, China
Authors: Longxing Shi, Weiqian Tian, Mengjiao Xu, Linjin Yu, Xin Chen, Qinglu Cai, Duo Xu, Bo Liu, Baoqin Long, Haoran Chen, Haolai Qin, Yahui Han, Haerleha. Amantai, Jingxin Gu, Mingxuan Tang, Aisha. Zharmukhametova, Jia Chen, Youwen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnder the One Health framework, the antimicrobial resistance and pathogenicity of Escherichia coli isolated from diarrheic lambs pose a potential threat to public health security. Aksu in Xinjiang is a major intensive mutton sheep breeding base in China. This study aimed to investigate the antimicrobial resistance, virulence gene carriage, biofilm-forming ability, pathotype distribution and phylogenetic group characteristics of E. coli from diarrheic lambs in this region, and analyze their potential correlations.MethodsA total of 150 samples were collected from diarrheic lambs in 6 intensive mutton sheep farms. E. coli was isolated and identified via differential media and specific gene detection; the disk diffusion method was used for antimicrobial susceptibility testing. PCR was performed to detect resistance and virulence gene carriage, and E. coli pathotypes were determined based on molecular characteristics. Mouse challenge assay was adopted to evaluate bacterial pathogenicity, crystal violet staining to assess biofilm-forming ability, and PCR to conduct phylogenetic group typing.ResultsA total of 121 E. coli strains were isolated with an isolation rate of 80.7%, including 38.0% multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains and 49.6% pathogenic strains (5 single pathotypes and 7 hybrid pathotypes). Mouse models showed all tested pathogenic strains had pathogenicity. Phylogenetic group analysis showed that group B1 was the predominant phylogroup (76.0%). Moreover, 78.6% of the strains had biofilm-forming ability, and MDR strains exhibited significantly higher biofilm-forming ability than non-MDR strains. The isolated strains harbored abundant resistance and virulence genes, with 13 different types of resistance genes and 18 virulence genes with distinct functions detected in total.ConclusionsThis is the first systematic report on the diversity characteristics of pathogenic E. coli from diarrheic lambs in intensive sheep farms in Aksu, clarifying the phylogenetic and antimicrobial resistance characteristics of the strains and revealing a significant association between biofilm formation and bacterial resistance. These findings provide a basis for the coordinated management of cross-domain public health risks in underdeveloped regions under the One Health framework, and contribute to the sustainable development of the mutton sheep industry and the safeguard of public health security.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05358-0.

Introduction

Against the global One Health backdrop, the cross-species transmission of antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) zoonotic pathogens from livestock to humans has become a major threat to global public health security [1, 2]. Among these pathogens, Escherichia coli (E. coli) has garnered considerable attention from the academic community and public health sectors due to its widespread distribution in global livestock production systems, robust environmental adaptability, and capacity to acquire and transmit both antimicrobial resistance genes and virulence factors [3]. As a facultative anaerobic Gram-negative bacterium, E. coli typically colonizes the intestines of humans and warm-blooded animals as a commensal [4]. Based on its pathogenicity, E. coli can be classified into pathogenic and non-pathogenic strains. Pathogenic E. coli can be further subdivided into multiple subtypes according to molecular characteristics, mainly including enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC), enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC), and enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC) [5]. Such pathogenic E. coli is not only a major pathogen causing human intestinal infections but also the primary etiological agent of neonatal lamb diarrhea in intensive sheep farms.

In Aksu Prefecture, Xinjiang, most intensive sheep farms are distributed in rural residential areas. Livestock manure from production is often directly used for farmland fertilization without standardized harmless treatment. Additionally, the water sources utilized by these farms share the same water supply system as surrounding villages. A study conducted in Kenya demonstrated that pathogenic E. coli strains isolated from the feces of diarrheic children in densely populated suburban areas with intensive human-animal cohabitation showed high genotypic consistency with strains recovered from nearby livestock farms and drinking water [6]. This result directly confirms that without the implementation of One Health-related intervention measures, pathogenic E. coli can achieve cross-host transmission through the animal-environment-human transmission chain, posing a persistent threat to public health.

The rapid development of intensive farming models has significantly improved mutton production efficiency. However, high-density feeding and the extensive use or even abuse of antimicrobial agents have also become key drivers for the prevalence of pathogenic E. coli and the emergence of multidrug resistance [7, 8]. A 2025 systematic review from Africa confirmed that E. coli was the most prevalent species of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) at this interface, accounting for 58.35% of all isolates [9]. The study also noted that the inevitable environmental exposure during rectal swab sampling is an important route for capturing cross-domain circulating resistant strains. Surveillance on E. coli from commercial pig farms across China during 2018–2019 showed that 91% of the isolates were multidrug-resistant (MDR) [10]. Recent data demonstrate that intensive sheep farms in western China generally have higher antimicrobial consumption, which leads to a continuously expanding resistance spectrum and widespread occurrence of MDR E. coli of ovine origin [7, 11]. Bacterial resistance is mediated by multiple molecular mechanisms, including enzymatic inactivation of drugs by β-lactamases, target site mutations, enhanced efflux pump activity, and plasmid-mediated horizontal transfer of resistance genes [12–14]. Furthermore, biofilms formed by E. coli can resist the effects of antimicrobial agents through physical barriers and gene regulation, and to a certain extent, enhance the colonization ability of E. coli strains harboring both multidrug resistance and pathogenicity in hosts [15, 16].

To further explore the evolutionary history and pathogenicity evolution mechanism of E. coli, it is essential to conduct phylogenetic classification studies. Existing research has confirmed that extraintestinal pathogenic strains mainly belong to phylogenetic group B2, followed by group D, while most commensal strains are classified into groups A and B1 [17]. Notably, beyond extraintestinal pathogenic strains, multiple studies worldwide have indicated that groups A and B1 serve as reservoirs for pathotype-positive E. coli [18, 19].

In summary, lamb diarrhea caused by MDR pathogenic E. coli not only restricts the development of the sheep industry in Aksu, Xinjiang but also poses a threat to public health within the One Health framework. Currently, systematic investigations on the biofilm properties, resistance characteristics, virulence phenotypes, and phylogenetic features of E. coli strains isolated from diarrheic lambs in this region are still lacking, with baseline data and underlying mechanisms remaining unclear.

This study systematically analyzed E. coli isolated from diarrheic lambs in Xinjiang and obtained valuable regional epidemiological data. The novelty of this work lies in the combined analysis of multiple indicators, including antimicrobial resistance phenotypes, virulence genes, biofilm formation, pathogenicity and phylogenetic grouping. Conducted under the framework of the One Health concept, this study fills the shortage of local baseline data on related research in pastoral areas of China’s border regions. Our findings can provide solid empirical evidence for formulating targeted prevention and control strategies in local animal husbandry.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A total of 150 rectal swabs and fecal samples were collected from diarrheic lambs at 6 intensive sheep farms in Aksu Prefecture, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China. Sampling was performed in two separate phases: winter 2024 (farms located in Wensu County, Keping County and Aksu City) and spring 2025 (farms located in Kuche County, Wushi County and Awati County). The geographical distribution of the sampled farms is shown in Fig. 1, covering 6 regionally representative areas of Aksu Prefecture. To minimize selection bias, clear and standardized farm selection criteria were adopted in this study. Only intensive sheep farms with standardized feeding management, complete breeding records, and naturally occurring diarrheic lamb cases during the sampling period were included. Small-scale free-range farms, farms with disordered management, and those without diarrheic cases were all excluded. During the sampling period, all diarrheic lambs on each farm were comprehensively sampled without selective screening or individual omission, ensuring full coverage of all clinically affected animals and sufficient representativeness of the samples. All fresh samples were stored at low temperature in a portable ice box and immediately transported to the College of Animal Science and Technology, Tarim University for subsequent laboratory testing.

Isolation and identification of E. coli

Each sample was inoculated into a sterile test tube containing 5 mL of LB liquid medium and incubated overnight on a shaking incubator at 37 °C with 180 r/min. After enrichment culture, the bacterial suspension was streaked onto Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar plates (Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Cat. No.: HB0107) using an inoculation loop and aerobically cultured at 37 °C for 14–18 h. Single colonies with metallic luster were picked from each petri dish and purified by three successive streaking cultures. Subsequently, the microscopic morphological identification of strains was performed using a Gram staining kit (Beijing Kulaibo Technology Co., Ltd., Cat. No.: SL7040). Genomic DNA of the strains was extracted with a Bacterial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Cat. No.: DP302), and molecular identification was conducted using specific primers for the phoA gene of E. coli (F: CGATTCTGGAAATGGCAAAAG; R: CGTGATCAGCGGTGACTATGAC). Finally, the isolated E. coli strains (one strain per diarrheic lamb sample) were suspended in 25% glycerol broth and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experimental analysis.

Experimental animals

Six-week-old Kunming mice were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Xinjiang Medical University. Pathogenicity tests were performed under standard laboratory conditions, and all animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Tarim University (Approval No. 2023009). Humane endpoints were strictly followed during trials. Moribund mice with severe lethargy or complete loss of feeding and drinking capacity were humanely euthanized via rapid cervical dislocation performed by trained technicians. This standardised method induces instantaneous unconsciousness and rapid death in mice according to laboratory animal welfare guidelines, and therefore no pre-euthanasia anaesthesia was administered in this work.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing and identification of multidrug-resistant strains

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the disk diffusion method in strict accordance with the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, M100-Ed32, 2022), with E. coli ATCC 25,922 as the quality control strain. A total of 14 antimicrobial agents (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA, Cat. No.: HP0053A) belonging to 5 classes were tested in this study: β-lactams (ampicillin, amoxicillin, imipenem, cefoxitin, ceftazidime, cefotaxime); aminoglycosides (gentamicin, amikacin, neomycin); tetracyclines (tetracycline); quinolones (ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin); and amphenicols (florfenicol, chloramphenicol). The specific concentrations of all antimicrobial discs and the criteria for result interpretation are detailed in Table 1. Strains resistant to three or more classes of antimicrobial agents were defined as MDR strains [20].

Detection of antimicrobial resistance genes

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays were used to detect 16 antimicrobial resistance genes belonging to 6 classes in the isolated E. coli strains, specifically including: β-lactam resistance genes (blaTEM, blaCTX-M4, blaSHV); aminoglycoside resistance genes (aadA1, aacC2, aadB); tetracycline resistance genes (tetA, tetM); amphenicol resistance genes (cmlA, cat1); quinolone resistance-related genes (parC, oqxA, oqxB, oqxAB, qnrA); and colistin resistance gene (mcr-1) [5, 21]. The PCR reaction conditions and primer sequences were detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Detection of virulence factors and identification of pathogenic E. coli

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to detect 20 virulence-associated genes, which were classified into the following categories based on their functions: adhesion and colonization-associated genes fimH, eae, k88, papC, malX, sfaS, and bfpA; toxin-associated genes stx1, stx2, astA, LT, and ST; iron uptake-associated genes irp2, iucD, and ireA; hemolysin-associated gene hlyA; membrane structure and virulence regulation-associated genes ompA, ompC, and crl; and invasion-associated gene invE [5, 22, 23]. The classification and sequences of the primers are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Based on this, E. coli strains carrying stx were identified as EHEC; strains carrying bfpA or eae were identified as EPEC; strains carrying astA were identified as EAEC; strains carrying any one of k88, ST, and LT were identified as ETEC; and strains carrying invE were identified as EIEC.

Mouse pathogenicity assay

Based on the molecular identification results of pathogenic E. coli, single-pathotype strains (including EPEC, EHEC, EAEC, etc.) and hybrid-pathotype strains with different combined pathotypes (e.g., EPEC/EHEC) were selected. A total of 17 strains were used in the assay. Mice were randomly divided into 17 experimental groups and 1 control group, with 3 mice in each experimental group and 10 mice in the control group. E. coli strains were first cultured on EMB agar plates, then expanded in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) under standardized conditions (37 °C, 180 r/min, 12 h). The resulting bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 5000 r/min for 5 min to collect the bacterial pellet, which was washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Cat. No.: G4202-100ML) and adjusted to the target concentration (1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). Mice in each experimental group were intraperitoneally injected with 0.5 mL of the bacterial suspension, while mice in the control group were intraperitoneally injected with 0.5 mL of sterile PBS. During a 10-day observation period, the mice were continuously monitored for the presence of clinical signs of systemic infection, including lethargy, piloerection, anorexia, diarrhea, somnolence, and death.

Phylogenetic group typing

Referring to previously reported methods [24], the chuA and yjaA genes and the TspE4.C2 DNA fragment were amplified by PCR to classify the E. coli strains into four phylogenetic groups (A, B1, B2, D). The interpretation criteria for PCR results were referenced from relevant studies.

Determination of biofilm-forming ability

A standardized 96-well microtiter plate assay was used for the semi-quantitative detection of the biofilm-forming ability of E. coli. Strains were inoculated into LB liquid medium and aerobically cultured overnight on a shaking incubator at 37 °C with 180 r/min to the logarithmic phase. The bacterial suspension was diluted 1:100 with sterile LB medium, and 200 µL of the diluted suspension was added to a 96-well polystyrene microplate, followed by static incubation at 37 °C for 24–36 h. After incubation, the microplate was washed three times with PBS to remove unadhered bacteria. The adherent biofilms were air-dried at room temperature and heat-fixed at 56 °C for 15 min, then stained with 1% (m/v) crystal violet solution for 5 min. Excess stain was removed by washing twice with PBS, and 200 µL of 95% ethanol was added to each well for decolorization. The optical density at 570 nm (OD570) was measured using a microplate reader. The biofilm-forming ability was determined based on the critical optical density (OD570C, the average OD570 of blank control wells): OD570 ≤ OD570C (no biofilm-forming ability); OD570C < OD570 ≤ 2OD570C (weak biofilm-forming ability); 2OD570C < OD570 ≤ 4OD570C (moderate biofilm-forming ability); OD570 > 4OD570C (strong biofilm-forming ability) [5].

Statistical analysis

All data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Statistical figures and charts were plotted to visually present the results for auxiliary interpretation.

Results

Isolation and antimicrobial susceptibility of E. coli

After selective culture of 150 samples from diarrheic lambs in Aksu, Xinjiang, a total of 121 E. coli strains were isolated and identified, with an isolation rate of 80.7%. It is worth noting that E. coli is a common conditional pathogen and normal commensal bacterium in the intestine of lambs, which can easily cause diarrhea when host immunity is compromised. Moreover, diarrhea is often accompanied by intestinal dysbiosis and significantly increased bacterial shedding. Therefore, the 80.7% isolation rate in this study includes both pathogenic and non-pathogenic E. coli. All isolated strains formed typical round, smooth colonies with metallic luster on EMB agar plates and were identified as Gram-negative pink short rods by Gram staining (Supplementary Figure S1). Molecular detection of the E. coli-specific phoA gene confirmed that all isolates harbored the phoA gene.

Antimicrobial susceptibility results are summarized in Fig. 2A and B. Tetracycline resistance was the most prevalent at 61.2%, while resistance rates to β-lactams, aminoglycosides, quinolones, and amphenicols varied by individual agent. Based on resistance phenotypes, 38.0% of the isolates were identified as MDR strains, as shown in Fig. 2C.

Detection of antimicrobial resistance genes in E. coli

A total of 13 different antimicrobial resistance genes were detected in the isolated strains by PCR assay, and the distribution of these resistance genes was shown in Fig. 3A. The parC gene (a quinolone resistance-related gene) had the highest detection rate of 100%, followed by the β-lactam resistance gene blaTEM with a detection rate of 97.5%. The detection rates of the tetracycline resistance genes tetA and tetM were 34.7% and 5.0%, respectively. Among the other β-lactam resistance genes, the detection rates of blaSHV and blaCTX-M4 were 13.2% and 9.1%, respectively. Only the aminoglycoside resistance gene aadA1 was detected, with a detection rate of 10.0%, and no other aminoglycoside resistance genes were identified in this study. The detection rates of the amphenicol resistance genes cmlA and cat1 were 31.4% and 4.1%, respectively. Besides parC, the detection rates of the quinolone resistance-related genes oqxA, oqxB and oqxAB were 15.7%, 1.7% and 4.1%, respectively. In addition, the colistin resistance gene mcr-1 was detected with a detection rate of 2.5%.

Detection of virulence genes and pathotype analysis of E. coli

The distribution of virulence-related genes in the isolates is shown in Fig. 4A and B. The detection rates of adhesion and colonization-related genes (malX, papC, k88, eae, and fimH) ranged from 6.7% to 97.5%. Toxin-related genes (LT, astA, stx2, and stx1) exhibited detection rates of 3.3%–9.1%. Iron uptake-related genes (ireA, iucD, and irp2) were detected at 5.8%–30.6%. The hemolysin gene hlyA was detected at 21.5%. The detection rates of outer membrane structure and virulence regulatory genes (ompA, ompC, and crl) ranged from 91.0% to 97.5%. The invasion-related gene invE had a detection rate of 14.0%. According to the typing criteria, 49.6% of the isolates were identified as pathogenic E. coli, including five single pathotypes (EPEC, EIEC, EHEC, ETEC, and EAEC) and seven hybrid pathotypes (Fig. 4C).

Analysis of pathogenicity in mouse

A mouse infection model was established to evaluate the pathogenicity of the selected strains, and 12 out of the 17 E. coli strains were pathogenic and capable of causing mouse death. The survival curve of the mice was shown in Fig. 5. Both single-pathotype and hybrid-pathotype pathogenic E. coli strains induced mouse death, and the survival rate of mice infected with single-pathotype strains was slightly higher than that of mice infected with hybrid-pathotype strains. Infected mice exhibited typical clinical signs including lethargy, reduced food and water intake, tachypnea, and rough fur. Necropsy was performed on the dead mice (Supplementary Figure S2), and pathological changes in multiple organs were observed in mice infected with both single-pathotype and hybrid-pathotype strains. The lesions were mainly concentrated in the liver, spleen and intestine, with a small number of mice showing renal lesions. The liver and spleen presented varying degrees of swelling and congestion, and the small intestine showed focal dilation, intestinal mucosal atrophy and hemorrhage.

Analysis of biofilm-forming ability of E. coli

Crystal violet staining was used to determine the biofilm-forming ability of the 121 E. coli strains, and the results were shown in Fig. 6A. Among the isolates, 8.3% exhibited strong biofilm-forming ability, 21.5% had moderate biofilm-forming ability, 48.8% showed weak biofilm-forming ability, and 21.4% had no biofilm-forming ability. As shown in Fig. 6C, the biofilm-forming ability of MDR E. coli strains was significantly higher than that of non-MDR strains.

Phylogenetic group typing of E. coli

The phylogenetic group distribution of the 121 isolated E. coli strains was shown in Fig. 6B: group A accounted for 8.3%, group B1 for 76.0%, group B2 for 6.6%, and group D for 9.1%. These results indicated that phylogenetic group B1 was the predominant group of E. coli isolated from diarrheic lambs in the Aksu region.

Discussion

Under intensive farming, the heavy use of antimicrobials drove the emergence of drug-resistant bacteria, creating a critical reservoir for antimicrobial resistance transmission across the animal-environment-human interface [25]. In this study, the MDR rate among E. coli isolates was 38.0%. Notably, we detected resistance to imipenem, cefotaxime, and chloramphenicol—agents restricted exclusively to human clinical use in China—with chloramphenicol resistance being particularly prominent. This indicates cross-domain transmission of resistance determinants between human and animal-derived strains, with the breeding environment serving as an intermediate exchange hub. This underscores the urgent need for joint antimicrobial resistance surveillance spanning human clinical cases, livestock farms, and environmental samples in the Aksu region.

Systematic studies on E. coli isolated from diarrheic lambs in the Aksu region remain limited to date. Previous reports have shown that the MDR rate of E. coli in this region reached 61.4%, with resistance rates to tetracycline, ampicillin, amoxicillin, chloramphenicol and cefoxitin being consistent with the findings of this study, while the resistance rates to quinolones and some β-lactams were higher than those observed here [5]. Studies have indicated that from 2015 to 2019, E. coli isolated from cattle and sheep in northern Xinjiang exhibited resistance rates of 31.9%–91.9% to most antimicrobial agents except imipenem, with an MDR rate of up to 100% [21]. In intensive farming areas of Shaanxi, Ningxia and Inner Mongolia in China, sheep-derived E. coli showed resistance rates of 17.8%–72.0% to various antimicrobials, with an MDR detection rate of 75.0% [7]. In Slovakia, E. coli from diarrheic calves had resistance rates of 22.0%–76.0% to five antimicrobial agents and an MDR rate of 71% [26]. Equine-origin Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) isolates from Nigeria exhibited 100% multidrug resistance [27]. In Canada, cattle-derived E. coli displayed resistance rates of 4.2%–41% to ceftriaxone, ampicillin, chloramphenicol and tetracycline, with 37% being MDR strains [20]. Multiple investigations on E. coli recovered from breeding environments, animal-derived products and pediatric patients have revealed widespread antimicrobial resistance across the globe. In Germany, E. coli isolated from abattoir wastewater displayed high resistance to cephalosporins, with an MDR rate of 46.51% [28]. In Portugal, pork-derived E. coli showed high resistance to tetracycline, ampicillin and other agents, with an MDR rate of 40.7% [29]. A study in Xiamen, China, revealed that E. coli causing invasive infections in neonates from 2012 to 2019 had strong antimicrobial resistance, with resistance rates exceeding 70% to ampicillin and piperacillin, varying degrees of resistance to most cephalosporins, and an MDR rate of approximately 45%–50% [30]. The cross-interface spread of antimicrobial resistance poses greater difficulties in preventing and controlling animal-derived E. coli infections, and also creates persistent risks to the clinical performance of antimicrobials and public health security.

Detection of antimicrobial resistance genes revealed that E. coli in the Aksu region harbors a variety of resistance determinants. As core carriers for the cross-interface transmission of antimicrobial resistance across the animal-environment-human continuum, these genes directly reveal the transmission potential and associated risks via their detection characteristics [31]. The quinolone resistance gene parC was detected at a rate of 100%, while the detection rates of oqxAB, oqxB and oqxA ranged from 1.7% to 15.7%, showing a significant discrepancy with the phenotypic resistance profile. parC is a key target of quinolone antibiotics, and its mutation is a major mechanism underlying quinolone resistance [32]; this thus implies that parC gene mutations are likely the primary driver of quinolone resistance in local E. coli isolates. The β-lactam resistance genes blaTEM, blaCTX-M4 and blaSHV were detected at rates of 97.5%, 13.2% and 9.1%, respectively, whereas porcine-derived E. coli from Hubei Province, China, exhibited corresponding rates of 61.5%, 57.4% and 39.2%, showing obvious discrepancies between the two groups [33]. The detection rates of the tetracycline resistance genes tetA and tetM were 34.7% and 5.0%, respectively, indicating that tetA is the major gene mediating tetracycline resistance in E. coli in this region. Similarly, previous studies have demonstrated that tetA acts as a major resistance determinant for tetracyclines among swine-derived E. coli isolates in Sichuan Province [34]. Only the aminoglycoside resistance gene aadA1 was detected with a rate of 10.0%, which is consistent with previous reports on sheep-derived E. coli in the Aksu region [5]. The amphenicol resistance genes cmlA and cat1 were detected at rates of 31.4% and 4.1%, respectively, a profile similar to that reported in studies on E. coli from lambs in southeastern Brazil [35]. Notably, we identified the colistin resistance gene mcr-1. A 12-year longitudinal surveillance study on the antimicrobial resistance of Escherichia coli in China reported an mcr-1 positive rate of 18.42% among diseased swine-derived isolates [36]. The widespread circulation of mcr-1 among domestic livestock not only impedes clinical infection management but also creates notable public health risks of cross-species transmission [37, 38]. Co-occurrence analysis (Fig. 3B) revealed strong correlations among parC, blaTEM, cmlA, and tetA, matching the dominant multi-resistant phenotypes and suggesting synchronous transmission of these determinants across hosts and environments.

Virulence factors determine the pathogenicity of E. coli by mediating adhesion, invasion, toxin production and other biological processes [39]. A total of 18 virulence genes with diverse functions were detected in this study. Among them, the detection rates of genes related to bacterial membrane structure, virulence regulation and partial adhesion exceeded 95.0%. Although these genes are not directly responsible for pathogenicity, they can enhance the survival and colonization ability of E. coli in animal hosts and the breeding environment, thereby facilitating cross-species transmission [40, 41]. Meanwhile, several key zoonotic virulence genes were identified in this study, including stx (Shiga toxin), eae (intimin), astA (heat-stable enterotoxin), LT (heat-labile enterotoxin), invE (intestinal invasion factor) and bfpA (bundle-forming pilus adhesin). This indicates the presence of important zoonotic pathogenic E. coli strains that can infect both lambs and humans in the isolates. High-density intensive farming environment further accelerates the transmission of these pathogenic bacteria across the animal-environment-human interface under the One Health framework, posing a threat to public health.

Based on molecular marker identification, 49.6% of the isolates were classified as pathogenic E. coli, which were divided into single pathotypes and hybrid pathotypes. Among the single pathotypes, EPEC, EIEC, EHEC, ETEC and EAEC accounted for 10.7%, 9.1%, 7.4%, 5.0% and 5.0%, respectively. EHEC accounted for 12.1% of E. coli isolated from sheep in Ghana, and EPEC accounted for 19.2% of sheep-derived E. coli in southern Brazil [42, 43]. These findings indicate that diarrheagenic E. coli from lambs in Aksu exhibits a more diverse spectrum of pathotypes. In contrast, diarrheagenic E. coli isolated from pigs in southern China were predominantly ETEC and EPEC, with detection rates of 8.10% and 1.29%, respectively [44]. A recent survey covering five provinces across China reported that the detection rate of ETEC from diarrheic pigs was 9.02% [45]. Overall, pathogenic E. coli from different geographical regions and animal hosts show distinct differences in dominant pathotypes and prevalence. Moreover, these five pathogenic E. coli pathotypes are also the major pathogens causing human intestinal infections. These five pathotypes are major human pathogens; EAEC and ETEC drove travelers’ diarrhea, EPEC and EHEC caused infantile diarrhea and hemorrhagic colitis, and EIEC mimicked bacillary dysentery [46, 47].

Notably, seven types of hybrid pathotypic strains were detected in this study. A study on porcine-derived E. coli from multiple regions in China identified four hybrid pathotypes: ExPEC/ETEC, ExPEC/EPEC, ExPEC/EHEC and ExPEC/EHEC/ETEC [1]. Studies have demonstrated that both STEC/EPEC and STEC/ETEC pathotypes are common, with hybrid STEC/EPEC strains persistently causing a substantial clinical and public health burden of gastrointestinal infectious diseases worldwide over the past four decades [48]. A study in Brazil isolated EPEC/EHEC hybrid pathotypic E. coli from the feces of diarrheic children [19]. Collectively, these observations indicate that hybrid pathotypic E. coli strains possess a broad host range and exhibit enhanced pathogenicity across different host species. To further verify this result, we assessed the pathogenicity of E. coli via mouse pathogenicity assays. Of note, mice were challenged by intraperitoneal injection rather than the oral route. Although some pathogenic E. coli, such as ETEC, naturally infect hosts orally and normally cause localized intestinal infection without systemic dissemination, the present study aimed to uniformly evaluate the overall lethality and pathogenic potential of all isolated strains. Intraperitoneal inoculation can rapidly establish a stable infection model with excellent reproducibility, which is well suited for the preliminary screening of bacterial virulence. The results revealed that 70.6% of the tested strains were lethal to mice. Meanwhile, the survival rate of mice challenged with hybrid pathotype strains decreased significantly, further demonstrating the stronger pathogenicity of these strains. Notably, each group contained three mice in the pathogenicity assay. Although this sample size may reduce statistical power, this study only aimed at the preliminary assessment of strain pathogenicity, and such small-sample design is commonly used for preliminary virulence evaluation in related studies. Hybrid pathotypic strains not only increase pathogenicity to lambs but also expand the host range, elevating the risk of human infection through contaminated livestock products or environmental contact, which is consistent with global reports of hybrid pathotypic E. coli in both humans and animals.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that phylogroup B1 was dominant among E. coli isolated from diarrheic lambs in this region. Previous studies indicated that E. coli from cattle and sheep in northern Xinjiang mainly belonged to phylogroups A and B1 [21]. Similarly, bovine mastitis pathogenic E. coli in Brazil were predominantly classified into groups A and B1 [49]. In contrast, human uropathogenic E. coli reported in Iran, Ethiopia, Colombia and Argentina were mostly assigned to phylogroups B2 and D [50–53]. Traditionally, phylogroups A and B1 are regarded as typical commensal strains, while groups B2 and D are primarily associated with extraintestinal pathogenic strains [17]. Notably, a large number of pathogenic isolates in our study fell into the commensal phylogroup B1, which is consistent with findings on bovine EHEC in Xinjiang and child-derived EPEC from diarrheal patients in Brazil [18, 19]. EAEC isolated from diarrheic children in India were mainly distributed in groups D and B1, showing obvious differences from our results [54]. Overall, commensal phylogroups in Aksu can serve as an important reservoir for pathogenic determinants, enabling the persistent circulation of pathogenic E. coli between animal hosts and farming environments.

In addition to antimicrobial resistance and pathogenicity, biofilm-forming ability is a key factor for E. coli to adapt to the environment, achieve survival and transmission, and enhance antimicrobial resistance [55]. In the animal-environment-human system, biofilms enable E. coli to persist on breeding equipment, pipelines and fecal sludge, reduce the permeability of antimicrobial agents, promote horizontal gene transfer, and stabilize antimicrobial resistance and virulence in the environment [56, 57]. In this study, 78.6% of the E. coli isolates exhibited biofilm-forming ability of varying intensities, which is consistent with the characteristic of a high biofilm-positive rate of E. coli in intensive farming systems worldwide [58, 59]. We analyzed the relationships between antimicrobial resistance, pathogenicity and biofilm-forming ability of E. coli from multiple dimensions and found that MDR strains had significantly stronger biofilm-forming ability than non-MDR strains (Fig. 6C). This finding is consistent with a study on clinically isolated E. coli from a pediatric hospital in China, which reported that strains with robust biofilm-forming ability may account for a larger proportion of MDR isolates [16]. This positive correlation reinforces the environmental persistence and cross-host transmission capacity of MDR E. coli, enabling them to form a persistent reservoir across the animal-environment-human continuum and reducing the therapeutic efficacy of antimicrobial agents in both veterinary and clinical practice.

Previous relevant studies have mainly focused on bacterial isolates from inland China and generally adopted a single-dimensional analysis of antimicrobial resistance. In comparison, the present study focused on Xinjiang isolates and performed a comprehensive analysis of antibiotic resistance, biofilm formation, and pathogenicity. This study not only supplements novel regional epidemiological data for border pastoral areas, but also further highlights the research significance under the One Health perspective.

Although valuable results have been obtained in this study, there are still several limitations. This work is a descriptive investigation of field isolates, focusing on phenotypic characteristics including antimicrobial resistance, biofilm formation and pathogenicity, and we did not conduct further research on the underlying molecular mechanisms. In addition, the in vivo pathogenicity assay was only performed for routine preliminary screening. In our follow-up work, we will elucidate the relevant molecular mechanisms and further verify the conclusions drawn in this study.

Conclusion

This study performed a systematic characterization of E. coli isolated from diarrheic lambs in intensive sheep farms of Aksu, Xinjiang. The results reveal that these isolates are featured by a high prevalence of multidrug resistance, diverse pathotypes and strong biofilm-forming ability. Accordingly, local sheep farms are recommended to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship and standardize medication practices to slow the progression of antimicrobial resistance. Given the zoonotic potential and strong environmental adaptability of the strains, it is necessary to improve farm biosecurity measures, conduct regular environmental disinfection and proper waste disposal, so as to block the cross-interface transmission of pathogens and resistance genes. The findings of this study provide important baseline data for targeted prevention and control of lamb diarrhea and scientific management of antimicrobial resistance in local intensive sheep farms. They also offer a valuable reference for cross-sector collaborative prevention and control of public health risks under the One Health concept.
