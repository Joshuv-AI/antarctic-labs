Standardized Analytical Verification of ctDNA ESR1 Mutation Testing in Metastatic HR+/HER2− Breast Cancer: A European Multicentre Study Using dPCR and NGS-Based Liquid Biopsy

Introduction

Activating mutations in the ESR1 gene are a well-established mechanism of resistance to aromatase inhibitors (AIs) in hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative (HR+/HER2−) metastatic breast cancer (mBC) [1]. These mutations typically cluster within the ligand-binding domain (LBD), including hotspot codons 380, 536, 537, and 538, and are associated with a more aggressive disease course and reduced sensitivity to endocrine therapy (ET) [2, 3]. Their prevalence varies according to prior treatment exposure and metastatic site, reaching up to 40% in patients relapsing after AI-based therapy [2, 4–8].

Liquid biopsy has emerged as the preferred approach for ESR1 mutation testing because it is minimally invasive and can detect subclonal alterations that may not be captured in tissue samples [3]. Both the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommend blood-based ESR1 testing at progression or recurrence on ET, with dPCR and next-generation sequencing (NGS) considered the preferred technical platforms [13, 14]. In this context, the phase III EMERALD trial (NCT03778931) demonstrated a clinically meaningful benefit of elacestrant, an oral selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD), in patients with ESR1-mutated mBC, leading to regulatory approval by both the FDA and the EMA in 2023 [11, 12]. In parallel, Guardant360® Dx was approved by the FDA as the companion diagnostic assay for selecting patients eligible for elacestrant treatment [12]. By contrast, European regulatory practice allows the use of validated technical methods other than the companion diagnostic assay, provided that assay performance is adequately verified and clinically appropriate.

Despite these advances, broader implementation of ESR1 testing in routine practice requires robust analytical standardization [10]. Diagnostic reliability depends on appropriate preanalytical handling of plasma samples to preserve circulating free DNA (cfDNA) integrity, as well as on rigorous local verification of assay sensitivity, specificity, and background noise [15]. Although both dPCR and NGS show high concordance for ESR1 mutation detection, NGS may offer broader mutational coverage, including less frequent or atypical variants within the LBD [16].

In this context, we conducted a European multicenter analytical verification study involving six referral academic laboratories. Using standardized reference materials, we evaluated dPCR- and NGS-based liquid biopsy workflows to define key analytical performance characteristics relevant to routine ESR1 testing. Our aim was to provide practical guidance for the implementation and analytical verification of ESR1 testing workflows, thereby supporting reliable and clinically actionable molecular testing in patients with HR+/HER2− mBC.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participating Institutions

Six European referral institutions for molecular biomarker testing participated in this multicenter analytical verification study: Fundación Jiménez Díaz University Hospital (Spain), Istituto Europeo di Oncologia, Università degli Studi di Napoli Federico II, and Veneto Institute of Oncology / University of Padova (Italy), The Ralph Lauren Centre for Breast Cancer Research (UK), and University Hospital Leuven (Belgium). Each institution performed local verification of ESR1 mutation testing workflows according to internally validated laboratory procedures. Both digital PCR (dPCR) and next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based liquid biopsy assays were included, in line with methodologies commonly used in clinical trials and routine molecular diagnostics in breast cancer. Analytical performance was evaluated using standardized reference materials containing clinically relevant ESR1 mutations. All participating centers verified dPCR workflows, and four laboratories also verified NGS-based liquid biopsy assays.

Design of Workflows for the Detection of ESR1 Mutations

Selected Mutations and Control Materials

All institutions focused on the detection of the two most frequently tested ESR1 hotspot mutations located in the ligand-binding domain, p.Y537S (COSMIC ID1074639) and p.D538G (COSMIC ID94250) [17, 18]. These variants were selected to allow direct comparison of analytical performance across platforms using standardized reference materials. Five of the six participating laboratories used commercially available reference materials containing the p.Y537S and p.D538G mutations, whereas one center used in-house constructed plasmid vectors containing wild-type and mutant ESR1 sequences for assay verification. Details on control materials and sources are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Limit of Detection and Limit of Blank

The limit of detection (LoD), analytical sensitivity, and limit of blank (LoB) were determined for each experimental workflow. LoD95 was defined as the lowest concentration that can be reliably distinguished from the limit of blank with a probability of 95%, according to CLSI EP17-A2 recommendations.

Analytical sensitivity was assessed using serial dilutions of contrived positive controls prepared by mixing mutant reference material with wild-type DNA. The type of mutant material, wild-type control, initial concentration, and dilution ranges expressed as variant allele frequency (VAF), for both dPCR- and NGS-based liquid biopsy assays, are summarized in Table 1.

The limit of blank (LoB) represents the highest signal expected in a true negative sample with probability equal to 1−α, where α is the probability of a false-positive result. LoB was calculated using a nonparametric approach adapted from CLSI EP17-A2 guidelines. The number of negative samples analyzed reflected routine verification procedures performed in accredited clinical molecular laboratories rather than full analytical validation studies.

In five of the six participating centers, 14 independent plasma samples from healthy individuals with no known history of cancer were analyzed for each assay. Sample origin varied according to local availability. At Fundación Jiménez Díaz University Hospital, samples were obtained from the institutional biobank under ethics committee approval. At Istituto Europeo di Oncologia, Veneto Institute of Oncology/University of Padova, and University Hospital Leuven, plasma samples were collected from healthy volunteers after written informed consent. The Ralph Lauren Centre for Breast Cancer Research used commercially available plasma from healthy donors (BIO-IVT, USA). Università degli Studi di Napoli Federico II used validated reference materials for internal verification. Different cfDNA-stabilizing blood collection tubes were used across centres according to local routine procedures, including Streck (Ralph Lauren Centre, Fundación Jiménez Díaz, and the Veneto Institute of Oncology/University of Padova), Nonacus (University Hospital Leuven), and Roche Cell-Free DNA (Istituto Europeo di Oncologia and Università degli Studi di Napoli Federico II) tubes. Plasma separation was performed before cfDNA extraction following locally validated workflows. These samples were analyzed exclusively for LoB estimation and analytical specificity assessment, allowing identification of stochastic background events and platform-specific artifacts at very low VAF.

Plasma separation was performed according to standardized preanalytical procedures for circulating free DNA analysis. Whole blood samples were processed within 48 h of collection. Plasma was obtained by manual double centrifugation, consisting of a first centrifugation at 1600 × g for 10 min followed by a second centrifugation at 6000×g for 10 min at room temperature, in order to minimize cellular contamination. The plasma fraction was carefully transferred to clean tubes avoiding disturbance of the buffy coat and stored at − 80 °C until cfDNA extraction. These preanalytical conditions were applied to ensure optimal cfDNA preservation and to reduce the risk of background genomic DNA contamination that could affect LoB and LoD measurements. Internal and external quality controls, including positive, negative, and no-template controls, were included in each analytical run.

cfDNA Isolation

All participating institutions adopted standardized procedures for cfDNA purification from plasma samples. cfDNA extraction was performed from approximately 4 mL of plasma using commercially available kits according to manufacturer instructions. QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen) was used at Fundación Jiménez Díaz University Hospital, QIAsymphony DSP Circulating DNA Kit (Qiagen) at The the Ralph Lauren Centre for Breast Cancer Research and Università degli Studi di Napoli Federico II, and Maxwell RSC Plasma Kit (Promega) at Veneto Institute of Oncology/University of Padova, Istituto Europeo di Oncologia, and University Hospital Leuven. cfDNA concentration was measured using the Qubit™ dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and fragment size distribution was assessed using the TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies).

Only samples fulfilling internal quality criteria were included in subsequent analyses. Purified cfDNA was stored at − 80 °C until molecular analysis to avoid DNA degradation. The amount of cfDNA used for downstream assays ranged between 10 and 30 ng per reaction for dPCR workflows and between 15 and 50 ng per reaction for NGS-based assays, depending on platform-specific requirements. DNA input is reported as total nanograms per reaction, as this parameter determines the number of genome equivalents analyzed and directly influences analytical sensitivity. Molecular testing was performed using locally validated dPCR or NGS workflows according to the procedures routinely used in each laboratory. Extraction details are summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Methodological Overview of NGS-Based Liquid Biopsy Analysis

NGS-based liquid biopsy assays were verified in four of the six participating institutions using locally validated workflows. For each assay, LoD and LoB were determined using the same standardized reference materials described above.

Library preparation methods varied across institutions depending on the commercial panel used. Amplicon-based library preparation was applied for the Plasma-SeqSensei Breast Cancer IVD kit (Sysmex) and the Oncomine Precision Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), whereas hybrid capture-based library preparation was used for the AVENIO ctDNA Expanded Kit (Roche). Sequencing was performed using either single-end or paired-end reads depending on the platform, with read lengths ranging from 100 to 151 base pairs. Sequencing throughput per sample ranged from approximately 10–40 million reads depending on assay requirements and DNA input.

NGS workflows differed in DNA input requirements, sequencing depth, and variant-calling criteria, which may influence analytical sensitivity. For the Plasma-SeqSensei panel (Sysmex), mutation detection required more than six mutant molecules across barcoded unique molecular families in the presence of at least 10,000 wild-type molecules, typically using ~ 43 ng of cfDNA input, corresponding to ~ 0.06% VAF. For the AVENIO ctDNA Expanded kit (Roche), variant calling was based on the manufacturer’s bioinformatics pipeline, and optimal performance required > 40 million reads from approximately 50 ng cfDNA input, allowing detection down to ~ 0.5% VAF. For the Oncomine Precision Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), positive calls required ≥ 4 supporting reads after unique molecular identifier (UMI) deduplication using ~ 40 ng cfDNA input, corresponding to ~ 0.1% VAF.

Sequencing data were analyzed using platform-specific bioinformatics pipelines. Fundación Jiménez Díaz University Hospital and Veneto Institute of Oncology/University of Padova used the Plasma-SeqSensei IVD Software (v1.3.1) for alignment, variant calling, and reporting. Università degli Studi di Napoli Federico II used the Ion Torrent Genexus Software (v6.8.4.0), complemented by visual inspection of BAM files using GenomeBrowse (Golden Helix). Istituto Europeo di Oncologia used the AVENIO Oncology Analysis Software. Analytical Performance Assessment

Analytical sensitivity was assessed by determining the LoD, defined as the lowest VAF at which a mutation could be reliably distinguished from wild-type background. Analytical specificity was assessed by determining the LoB, defined as the highest signal observed in true negative samples, expressed as mutant copies per µL or number of positive partitions for dPCR assays.

Results

Sensitivity Assessment: Confirmation of LoD

LoD by dPCR

Verification of the limit of detection (LoD) for ESR1 mutation detection by dPCR was performed in the six participating laboratories. Despite differences in platforms, assay design, and DNA input, comparable analytical sensitivity ranges were observed across centers and were consistent with the expected performance of each system.

At Università degli Studi di Napoli Federico II, the LoD using the QuantStudio Absolute Q Digital PCR System (Supplementary Fig. 1) was determined as 0.01% for p.D538G and 0.04% for p.Y537S, based on the mean VAF obtained from 10 independent replicates performed at theoretical allele frequencies of 0.01% and 0.05%, respectively, using 10 ng of DNA input per reaction. At Istituto Europeo di Oncologia, using the same platform, a LoD ≥ 0.05% was established on the basis of the median results of 10 independent runs using contrived positive controls. Two DNA input conditions were evaluated (10 ng and 30 ng per reaction). With 10 ng input, corresponding to approximately 1 ng/µL, the lowest reliably detectable VAF was ≥ 0.2%, whereas with 30 ng input (approximately 4 ng/µL), detectable VAF values ranged between 0.2% and 0.01% for both mutations, confirming the influence of DNA input on analytical sensitivity.

Fundación Jiménez Díaz University Hospital, using the QX200 Droplet Digital PCR system (Supplementary Fig. 1), determined a LoD of 0.05% fractional abundance for both p.D538G and p.Y537S mutations based on nine independent experimental replicates using 10 ng of DNA input per reaction. Below this allele frequency, detection criteria were not met, as only two replicates were positive at 0.02% and 0.01% VAF. At University Hospital Leuven, also using the QX200 system, a mean LoD of 0.059% was obtained for both mutations, calculated from 30 independent replicates using 10 ng of DNA input per reaction. In an additional experiment, using approximately 8000 genome equivalents from positive controls, mutations at 0.01% VAF could be detected, confirming the impact of DNA input on assay sensitivity.

At the Veneto Institute of Oncology/University of Padova, using the QX600 system (Supplementary Fig. 1), mutations were reproducibly detected down to 0.05% VAF using 30 ng of DNA input per reaction, with a mean of 4–5 positive droplets observed across 10 independent repetitions. Allele frequencies at 0.01% were rarely detected, and one positive droplet was observed in one wild-type control replicate. LoD was, therefore, established at 0.05% VAF for both mutations, requiring at least two positive droplets per reaction to classify a sample as positive. The Ralph Lauren Centre for Breast Cancer Research established a LoD of 0.05% VAF using the QX600 system with 30 ng of DNA input per reaction. Although the ddPLEX ESR1 assay used in this laboratory allows multiplex detection of several ESR1 hotspot mutations, the verification protocol in the present study was restricted to the p.D538G and p.Y537S to ensure consistency across participating centers.

LoD by NGS-Based Liquid Biopsy

Four out of six participating institutions validated an NGS workflow to evaluate ESR1 mutations on standard reference material. The University of Naples Federico II successfully detected p.D538G and p.Y537S using the Oncomine Precision Assay on a Genexus Platform (Thermo Fisher), achieving a mean LoD of 0.01% for p.D538G and 0.04% for p.Y537S. At Fundación Jiménez Díaz, both mutations were detected using the Plasma-SeqSensei™ Breast Cancer IVD kit on a NextSeq 550Dx system (Illumina), requiring at least six mutant molecular families according to manufacturer specifications. Median LoD values were 0.06% for p.Y537S and 0.07% for p.D538G. At the Istituto Europeo di Oncologia, a median LoD ≥ 0.05% was obtained using reference material with different DNA input conditions, starting from 1 ng/μL cfDNA input, confirming that analytical sensitivity improved with higher cfDNA input per reaction. Finally, at the Veneto Institute of Oncology/University of Padova, the median LoD for p.Y357S and p.D538G was determined at 0.07% and 0.1% respectively, and a LoD of 0.1% was adopted for routine diagnostic reporting. Assessment of Specificity: Confirmation of LoB

LoB by dPCR

Across laboratories performing dPCR workflows, high analytical specificity was observed for both ESR1 mutations. During LoB assessment using plasma samples from healthy donors, only minimal background signal was detected, with the number of positive droplets ranging between 0 and 2 per reaction. According to internally validated thresholds, Fundación Jiménez Díaz, University Hospital Leuven, and The Ralph Lauren Centre for Breast Cancer Research considered samples as wild type when up to two positive droplets were observed, as these events were interpreted as stochastic background noise rather than true positive signals.

Discussion

This multicenter analytical verification study evaluated the technical performance of ESR1 mutation testing workflows across six European referral laboratories using dPCR- and NGS-based liquid biopsy platforms. The study focused on p.D538G and p.Y537S, two of the most frequently reported ESR1 hotspot mutations within the ligand-binding domain and among the most commonly targeted variants in current clinical assays in patients with HR+/ HER2− BC. These alterations were selected to enable direct comparison of analytical performance across laboratories using shared reference materials. However, additional ESR1 activating mutations involving codons 536, 537, and other positions within the ligand-binding domain have also been described and may be clinically relevant in selected clinical contexts. Broader mutation coverage, particularly with NGS-based approaches, may therefore be required in routine practice to fully characterize ESR1 mutational status and to capture less frequent or atypical variants.

ESR1 mutations have clear clinical relevance in HR+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer, guiding the clinical decision-making process, particularly in the context of treatment selection after progression on endocrine therapy, where ESR1 activating mutations can select patients to a new generation of oral SERDs [19]. Because these alterations emerge under the selective pressure of aromatase inhibitors and may be subclonal, liquid biopsy has been recommended by several international societies as the preferred biological source for ESR1 testing at progression. In this setting, the low abundance and polyclonal nature of circulating tumor DNA require highly sensitive analytical methods capable of identifying low-frequency variants in plasma. Both dPCR and NGS have demonstrated adequate analytical performance for this purpose and are now established as clinically relevant technologies for ESR1 testing [20].

Following the approval of elacestrant, the FDA granted Guardant360® Dx (Guardant Health, USA) as the companion diagnostic assay for ESR1 mutation detection in this setting. In Europe, however, regulatory and diagnostic implementation pathways also allow the use of locally validated methods, provided that assay performance is adequately verified. This has created a strong need for decentralized yet analytically robust testing strategies that can be implemented across accredited molecular pathology laboratories. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have shown that an integrative algorithm using NGS and dPCR systems is a viable approach for molecular testing [2, 9, 19, 21–26]. Both technologies are widely accessible in Europe and have proven to be key tools for analyzing ESR1 mutations in liquid biopsy [4]. In this context, analytical verification of platform-specific workflows is essential to ensure reliable ESR1 mutation detection and timely clinical decision-making.

Our results support the analytical suitability of both dPCR and NGS for the detection of p.D538G and p.Y537S ESR1 mutations in plasma. Given the subclonal evolution of ESR1 alterations in patients with mBC under the pressure of AI treatment, several data have highlighted that identifying ESR1 activating mutations below 1.0% becomes crucial to successfully stratify patients with HR+/HER2− mBC to oral elacestrant [27, 28]. To fully align with the technical approaches used in the EMERALD trial, a LoD of 0.5% should be adopted when implementing ESR1 clinical testing [11]. Across centers, LoD values obtained with dPCR ranged from 0.01% to 0.07% VAF, whereas those obtained with NGS ranged from 0.01% to 0.1% VAF, depending on platform, DNA input, and variant-calling criteria. These results are consistent with the expected analytical sensitivity of the assays evaluated under controlled conditions. At the same time, they should be interpreted as analytical LoD values derived from standardized reference materials rather than as direct estimates of biological sensitivity in clinical plasma samples.

The impact of experimental conditions in the interpretation of analytical sensitivity, particularly cfDNA input, was evident across workflows. Higher DNA input was associated with improved sensitivity, as illustrated by the difference observed between assays using approximately 8000 genome equivalents and those using lower inputs closer to 3000 copies (0.01% versus 0.07%). Based on the verification procedures performed in participating laboratories, a DNA input range of 10–30 ng per reaction appeared appropriate for routine dPCR-based testing, while higher input amounts were required by some NGS workflows. However, direct comparison of LoD values across platforms should be made cautiously, since analytical sensitivity depends not only on DNA input but also on assay design, number of analyzed partitions, sequencing depth, and platform-specific calling thresholds. This variability reflects the reality of decentralized clinical testing and represents an important consideration when implementing ESR1 testing across laboratories.

Preanalytical handling remains a critical determinant of assay reliability. Although preanalytical variables were not directly tested as experimental endpoints in this study, they are highly relevant to the successful implementation of ctDNA testing in clinical practice. Appropriate blood collection tubes, using DNA-preservative tubes are recommended when liquid biopsy samples are to be shipped to central laboratories for molecular testing to prevent intra-site variability, timely plasma separation within 2 h following venepuncture if peripheral blood is collected in nonpreservative containing blood collection, careful avoidance of cellular contamination, standardized cfDNA extraction, and adequate DNA quantification are all necessary to minimize background genomic DNA contamination and preserve assay sensitivity [29]. These considerations are supported by published evidence and by the practical experience of the participating laboratories and are included here as implementation guidance rather than as direct outcomes of the verification experiments themselves.

Amplicon design may also influence performance in liquid biopsy assays. cfDNA is typically highly fragmented, and longer amplicons may theoretically reduce amplification efficiency in some contexts. In the present study, one center used an assay with an amplicon length of approximately 167 bp. Despite this potential limitation, the assay achieved a LoD within the expected range for dPCR-based ESR1 testing, suggesting that platform-specific assay optimization may compensate, at least in part, for the effect of fragment length.

From a practical perspective, the choice between dPCR and NGS for ESR1 testing should be guided by clinical objectives, local infrastructure, and the breadth of molecular information required. dPCR is particularly attractive for focused testing of predefined ESR1 hotspot mutations because of its high analytical sensitivity, quantitative accuracy, and operational feasibility in laboratories with limited sample throughput [30, 31], with a turnaround time of approximately 10 working days [32]. The high sensitivity and specificity of dPCR for predefined mutations, such as ESR1, makes it ideal for focused and time-sensitive mutation monitoring in patients with mBC [33]. Its main limitation derives from low positive pickup rates to detect less recurrent ESR1 mutations owing to its predefined nature which would hamper the ability to expand the number of patients with HR+/HER2− mBC selected for targeted therapy after progression on endocrine therapy. By contrast, NGS offers broader mutational coverage and may be preferable when simultaneous assessment of ESR1 and additional clinically relevant alterations, such as PIK3CA, AKT1, or PTEN, is required. NGS-based liquid biopsy analysis can provide a broader understanding of the tumor genetic landscape, which is valuable for personalized treatment strategies and tracking tumor evolution over time [6, 9]. Rather than representing competing approaches, dPCR and NGS should be viewed as complementary tools that can be selected according to the diagnostic question and the clinical context.

This study has several limitations. First, the verification protocol was restricted to two ESR1 hotspot mutations, p.D538G and p.Y537S. Although this choice facilitated inter-laboratory comparison using standardized materials and focused on highly relevant clinically actionable variants, broader mutation coverage may be necessary in routine diagnostics. In particular, the degree of multiplexing in dPCR assays and the extent of coverage of NGS panels represent additional variables that may affect analytical sensitivity, specificity, and concordance. Future standardization studies should therefore address not only selected hotspot mutations but also the impact of assay multiplexing and panel design on overall clinical performance.

Second, the study was performed using standardized reference materials rather than clinical plasma samples. This design enabled controlled comparison of analytical performance across laboratories, but it cannot fully reproduce the biological complexity of real-world ctDNA, including variation in fragment size, tumor DNA shedding, and contamination from leukocyte-derived genomic DNA. Accordingly, the LoD values reported here should be interpreted as analytical verification end points obtained under controlled conditions. Nevertheless, the workflows evaluated in this study are not purely theoretical. The same technologies have been applied in prospective trials and real-world cohorts of metastatic breast cancer. Digital PCR-based ESR1 testing was successfully used in the phase III PADA-1 trial to guide endocrine therapy switching [4]. The methodologies included in this study, performed in one of the participating laboratories in this work, have also been used in translational analyses from the FLIPPER clinical trial [34] and the GEICAM/2014-03 RegistEM cohort [35], where ESR1 mutations were detected in approximately 35–45% of patients progressing after endocrine therapy plus CDK4/6 inhibition. In addition, similar testing strategies were applied in European early access programs with oral SERDs presented at ESMO Breast 2025 [36], where the observed ESR1 mutation frequency in routine practice was 38%, using dPCR and NGS technologies in all laboratories involved in this project. The clinical applicability of dPCR-based ESR1 testing has also been demonstrated by the extensive experience generated at The Royal Marsden Hospital in the monitoring of endocrine resistance and clonal evolution in advanced breast cancer [19]. Together, these data support the clinical relevance of the workflows analyzed here, even though the present study was designed as an analytical verification rather than a clinical validation study.

Third, the participating laboratories used heterogeneous testing platforms and locally validated workflows, which reflects real-world decentralized testing conditions but may introduce variability in analytical parameters. Differences in LoD and LoB values across centers are expected when different dPCR or NGS systems are used, as analytical sensitivity depends on factors such as DNA input, number of analyzed partitions, genome equivalents, sequencing depth, and variant calling thresholds. Despite these differences, comparable limits of detection were obtained across platforms when assays were performed under validated laboratory conditions.

Finally, the number of negative samples used for LoB estimation was limited and reflects routine laboratory verification rather than full-scale analytical validation. Larger series of blank plasma samples would be expected to refine LoB estimation further, particularly for assays targeting very low-frequency variants. Nevertheless, the LoB thresholds obtained were consistent across participating centers and were within the range expected for clinically implemented ctDNA assays.

Conclusions

This European multicenter study shows that ESR1 mutation testing in plasma can be analytically verified across different accredited laboratories using either dPCR- or NGS-based liquid biopsy workflows. Although the participating centers used different platforms and locally validated procedures, the study demonstrates that comparable analytical performance can be achieved when common reference materials and clearly defined verification criteria are applied. These findings are particularly relevant in the European setting, where ESR1 testing is increasingly being implemented through decentralized, laboratory-developed workflows rather than through a single companion diagnostic assay.

The present work does not propose a universal protocol for ESR1 testing, nor does it replace the need for platform-specific local verification. Rather, it provides a practical framework for analytical verification and highlights the main technical variables that should be considered during implementation, including DNA input, assay-specific sensitivity thresholds, background signal, and preanalytical handling. By documenting the analytical behavior of both dPCR and NGS workflows under routine laboratory conditions, this study helps define realistic expectations for assay performance and supports the reliable introduction of ESR1 testing into clinical molecular diagnostics.

The results also reinforce the complementary role of dPCR and NGS in clinical practice. dPCR offers a highly sensitive and operationally feasible solution for focused testing of recurrent ESR1 hotspot mutations, whereas NGS provides broader mutational coverage and may be advantageous when more comprehensive molecular characterization is required. The choice between both approaches should therefore be guided by the clinical question, local resources, and the extent of genomic information needed for patient management.

Importantly, the study should be interpreted within its intended scope: an analytical verification exercise performed with standardized reference materials. Further work using larger series of clinical plasma samples will be required to refine biological sensitivity estimates, extend verification to additional ESR1 variants, and evaluate the impact of multiplexing strategy and broader panel design. Even so, the present data provide robust evidence that analytically sound ESR1 mutation testing can be achieved across multiple European laboratories, thereby supporting reliable and timely treatment selection for patients with HR+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer. In this sense, the experience gained here may also serve as a model for the verification of future liquid biopsy biomarkers in routine clinical practice (Table 7).
