CA1 parvalbumin interneurons display alterations of myelin patterning driven by microgliosis in temporal lobe epilepsy
Authors: Alexandre Vigier, Céline Boileau, Sophia Faresse, Catherine Faivre-Sarrailh, Valérie Crépel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTemporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is one of the most common forms of epilepsy, characterized by significant reorganization of hippocampal neuronal circuits. While changes in GABAergic inhibitory circuits are a major feature of this reorganization, other prominent alterations include hippocampal myelination disruption, neuronal loss, and pronounced glial and inflammatory responses. Recent studies have established that parvalbumin (PV +) interneurons in the hippocampus are partially myelinated under normal conditions, representing a notable structural feature of this interneuron subtype. However, whether interneuron myelination becomes altered in TLE has remained unexplored. Using a mouse TLE model, we investigated CA1 PV+ interneurons during epileptogenesis and chronic disease phases, examining numerical density and myelination patterns. We simultaneously tracked microglial responses and oligodendrocyte lineage cell populations (mature oligodendrocytes and their precursors). Our findings reveal that CA1 PV+ interneurons maintain their numerical density throughout disease progression but undergo marked myelination alterations. This dysmyelination occurs concurrently with pronounced microgliosis and changes in the dynamics of oligodendrocyte lineage cell populations, predominantly during epileptogenesis. Notably, pharmacological intervention with GW2580, a CSF1R inhibitor, administered for 8 days around the status epilepticus, prevented both PV+ interneuron myelination alterations and changes in oligodendrocyte lineage cell populations by blocking microglial proliferation. These results establish a link between microgliosis and alterations of myelin patterning in CA1 PV+ interneurons, advancing our understanding of interneuronopathy and TLE pathophysiology. Introduction

Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by significant structural and functional changes in the brain, particularly within the hippocampus1,2. A hallmark of TLE is widespread neuronal loss affecting both excitatory and inhibitory neurons, leading to extensive pathological alterations3–6. Interestingly, despite this widespread degeneration, certain neuronal populations, such as parvalbumin-positive (PV +) interneurons, demonstrate remarkable resistance to cell death, remaining largely preserved even in severely damaged areas.

In both the hippocampus and cortex, PV+ interneurons are partially myelinated, a feature that may contribute to their characteristic fast-spiking activity7. This myelination is crucial for efficient signal transmission and the proper functioning of neuronal circuits8–11. In TLE, alterations in myelin integrity have been reported throughout the central nervous system (CNS), including both white and gray matter12–16. Notably, changes in the dynamics of oligodendrocyte lineage cells, including both progenitors and mature oligodendrocytes, are prominent in the hippocampus of rats with TLE14, suggesting a link between myelin abnormalities and disease pathology.

Microgliosis, a robust neuroinflammatory response, is frequently observed in both animal models of acquired epilepsy17,18 and in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy19,20. This process involves the acquisition of reactive morphology21, shifts in molecular and immunophenotypic profiles, and increased phagocytosis 18,22. Microglia play a dual role: they are vital for normal myelination during development23,24, yet also serve as key mediators of inflammation in TLE25–27. PV+ interneurons are highly vulnerable to inflammatory environments28–30. Based on these lines of evidence, we hypothesize that PV+ interneurons in TLE exhibit myelin alterations primarily as a consequence of microgliosis.

Using an established mouse model of TLE31, we investigated temporal changes in CA1 PV+ interneurons during both epileptogenesis and chronic phases. We focused on the numerical density of PV+ interneurons in CA1 and on their myelination status in the stratum radiatum (SR), a region in which the vast majority of myelinated axons, approximately 80%, are PV +10,32. Our study revealed that, while CA1 PV+ interneurons maintain their numerical density throughout disease progression, they display abnormalities of myelin patterning early during epileptogenesis that persist into the chronic phase. Myelin alteration occurs concurrently with pronounced microgliosis and changes in the dynamics of oligodendrocyte lineage cell populations. Critically, pharmacological intervention with GW2580 to inhibit microglial proliferation for 8 days around the status epilepticus (SE) prevented both the myelin alteration and changes in the dynamics of oligodendrocyte lineage cell populations in CA1.

These findings demonstrate that TLE pathology affects PV+ interneuron myelination through microgliosis, sharing fundamental characteristics with both dysmyelinating and neurodegenerative diseases. This novel perspective on TLE pathophysiology may offer promising therapeutic opportunities for both TLE and related neurological disorders characterized by inflammation-mediated myelin dysfunction.

Results

PV+ interneuron numerical density remains stable in CA1 throughout epileptogenesis and chronic epilepsy

To evaluate whether SE affects the survival of PV-expressing interneurons in the hippocampus of our mouse model of TLE, we performed immunofluorescence analysis to quantify PV+ cell numerical density in the CA1 region at different time points following pilocarpine-induced SE (the early and chronic post-SE phases), compared to their age-matched control groups (Fig. 1A,B). The quantification revealed that the numerical density of PV+ interneurons remained stable throughout both the early and chronic post-SE phases (p > 0.05).

In conclusion, our data confirmed that CA1 PV+ interneurons were resistant to neuronal death in a mouse model of TLE, consistent with previous reports observed in a rat model of TLE3,5.

Altered myelination of PV+ interneurons in the CA1 stratum radiatum following status epilepticus

In the hippocampus, it has been well established that PV+ interneurons exhibit partially myelinated axons8–11 and alterations in myelin integrity have been reported throughout the CNS in epileptic patients and animal models12–16.

To assess potential alterations in myelin patterning in CA1 PV+ interneurons following SE, we performed a temporal analysis of Myelin Basic Protein (MBP) in the CA1 stratum radiatum (SR) during the early and chronic post-SE phases. MBP + and MBP + /PV+ segments were quantified in the SR using high-resolution confocal microscopy and manual reconstructions with ImageJ software (see Methods and Fig. 2). This method enabled accurate tracking of MBP + and MBP + /PV+ segments regardless of their length, from short to long fragments (from approximately 1 to 100 µm), as shown in Fig. 2. Analysis at the early post-SE phase, relative to age-matched controls, showed a shift toward atypical and shorter MBP + /PV+ segments, as indicated by the frequency and cumulative distributions of segment length (Fig. 2B,C). Consistent with this shift (Fig. 3 and Supp. Figure 2), the mean segment length was significantly reduced for both MBP + and MBP + /PV+ segments (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01, respectively) in mice in the early post-SE phase (Fig. 3B,C). This reduction persisted at 2 months post-SE (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B,C). However, at 4 months post-SE, the reduction was not significant for MBP + segments (p > 0.05) but remained weakly significant for MBP + /PV+ segments (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3B,C). Moreover, in the early post-SE phase, paranodal Caspr immunostaining revealed a helical paramesaxon (Supp. Figure 1A, B, blue arrows) that was not covered by MBP + segments, a feature which was never observed in controls. This is in contrast to the typical compact paranodal clusters (white and red arrows), which are confined to the ends of MBP + segments as observed in control and post-SE mice33,34. We analyzed the density of paranodes ending MBP + segments classified as unpaired or paired Caspr + paranodal clusters (Supp. Figure 1C, D). The density of paranodes appeared similar to age-matched controls in both early and chronic post-SE phases suggesting that the number of myelin internodes may be preserved. However, the abnormalities in Caspr immunostaining at internodes reinforce the evidence that myelin degradation may occur along the axons in PV+ interneurons.

Status epilepticus changes the dynamics of oligodendrocyte lineage cell populations

In the CNS, myelin is produced by mature oligodendrocytes (OLs), which extend processes that wrap around axons. In the hippocampus, the OL lineage cell populations comprise mature myelinating OLs (Olig2 + /PDGFRα − /CC1 +), pre-myelinating OLs (PreOLs) at intermediate stages of maturation (Olig2 + /PDGFRα + /CC1 +), and OL precursor cells (OPCs; Olig2 + /PDGFRα + /CC1 −), which typically remain quiescent but can be recruited to differentiate into PreOLs35–37 (Fig. 4A,B).

Immunohistochemical quantification in the CA1 region revealed time-dependent changes in oligodendroglial populations after SE, most prominently affecting PreOLs and mature OLs. During the early post-SE phase, the numerical density of PreOLs and OLs increased significantly relative to age-matched controls (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively), before returning to control-like levels at 2 and 4 months post-SE (Fig. 4D,E). In contrast, OPCs showed only a non-significant trend toward increased numerical density in the early post-SE phase and no apparent change in the chronic phase (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4C).

Taken together, these findings indicate that SE transiently stimulates oligodendrocyte lineage dynamics, with a short-lived expansion of the PreOL and mature OL pools that normalizes during the chronic post-SE period.

Overall, the data support the idea that myelin alterations during epileptogenesis are accompanied by rapid, time-limited remodeling of oligodendrocyte lineage cell populations.

Blocking microglial proliferation prevents both myelin alterations and changes in the dynamics of oligodendrocyte lineage cell populations

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the CNS, are key regulators of myelination through their phagocytic activity and microenvironment modulation23,38,39 as well as key mediators of inflammation in TLE25–27. Analysis of microglial dynamics in the CA1 region during TLE revealed a significant increase in Iba1 + cell density during epileptogenesis (Fig. 5). Specifically, during the early post-SE phase, microglial density was markedly elevated compared to the control group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5A,D), before returning to baseline levels at 2 months post-SE and remaining stable through 4 months. Notably, the increase in microglial density was associated with a change in their morphotype from ramified (Fig. 5B) to a bushy (Fig. 5C) shape, reinforcing the concept of early microgliosis21 during the early post-SE phase. This microgliosis (Fig. 5) temporally coincides with both myelin alteration (Figs. 2 and 3) and shifts in oligodendrocyte lineage cells in the epileptogenic hippocampus (Fig. 4), strongly suggesting that microgliosis may play a role in these pathological changes.

To determine whether microglial proliferation is implicated in these myelin alterations and oligodendrocyte lineage population shifts, we administered the selective CSF1R inhibitor GW2580 to TLE mice18,40. Our analysis of the CA1 region demonstrated that epileptic mice treated with GW2580 (early post-SE phase + GW2580) exhibited significantly lower microglial density compared to their vehicle-treated counterparts (early post-SE phase + vehicle; Fig. 6A,B). Importantly, GW2580 treatment effectively normalized microglial density to levels comparable to those of GW2580-treated control animals, although microglial cells maintained a reactive morphology (Fig. 6A). This validates its efficacy in preventing microglial proliferation, as previously reported18,40.

Having confirmed effective suppression of microgliosis, we next investigated whether GW2580 treatment could also prevent myelin alterations. Quantification of the mean length of MBP + and MBP + /PV+ segments revealed a significant preservation of myelin integrity following GW2580 treatment (Fig. 7A,B). Specifically, the mean length of MBP + segments was significantly greater in GW2580-treated epileptic mice (early post-SE phase + GW2580) compared to vehicle-treated epileptic mice (early post-SE phase + vehicle; p < 0.05; Fig. 7B and Supp. Figure 2). Similarly, the length of MBP + /PV+ segments was also significantly preserved with GW2580 treatment (p < 0.05; Fig. 7B and Supp. Figure 2). Notably, in both measures, GW2580-treated epileptic mice exhibited segment lengths comparable to those of GW2580-treated control animals, suggesting a substantial preservation of PV+ myelin integrity.

To further characterize the neuroprotective effects of inhibiting microglial proliferation, we assessed the impact of blocking microglial proliferation on changes in the dynamics of oligodendrocyte lineage populations by quantifying the densities of OPCs, PreOLs, and OLs in treated epileptic mice (Fig. 7C–E).

While OPC density displayed no significant change across all experimental groups (Fig. 7C), GW2580 treatment effectively prevented the increase in both PreOL and OL populations in epileptic mice (early post-SE phase + GW2580) compared with vehicle-treated epileptic mice (early post-SE phase + vehicle, p = 0.0087 and p = 0.0012, respectively; Fig. 7D,E). Strikingly, GW2580-treated epileptic mice showed PreOL and OL densities similar to those of treated controls, demonstrating that microglial inhibition prevents the SE-induced increase in oligodendrocyte lineage cell density.

Taken together, these data demonstrate that inhibiting microglial proliferation with GW2580 prevents myelin alterations in PV+ interneurons and normalizes oligodendrocyte lineage cell dynamics, supporting a role for microglia in the dysmyelination of PV+ interneurons during epileptogenesis.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that CA1 PV+ interneurons in TLE maintain their numerical density throughout disease progression while exhibiting significant alterations of myelin patterning during both epileptogenesis and chronic disease phases. These myelin alterations occur alongside pronounced microgliosis and dynamic changes in oligodendrocyte lineage cell populations. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of microglial proliferation with GW2580, a CSF1R inhibitor, prevents both myelin disruption and changes in the oligodendrocyte cell populations, supporting a role of microglia in the myelination disturbance of PV+ interneurons in TLE.

In line with previous findings3,5,41,42, we first confirmed that PV+ interneurons in the CA1 region, primarily basket cells and bistratified cells43, survive during epileptogenesis. It has also been reported in TLE animal models that somatic inhibitory inputs from PV+ basket cells onto pyramidal cells are not reduced, indicating that their axonal arborization is preserved3,42,44. It is well established that GABAergic interneurons are frequently myelinated in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex of mice and humans8,10,45,46. In the present study, we show that, despite their survival, these interneurons exhibit abnormal myelin patterns, characterized by shorter MBP + segments. Indeed, our immunofluorescence analysis revealed significant reductions in the mean lengths of both MBP + and MBP + /PV+ segments during the early stages of epileptogenesis, which persisted into the chronic phase. Previous studies have shown that the expression of the PV marker is downregulated42 in animal models of TLE47 and in the epileptic human hippocampus48,49. For this reason, it could be hypothesized that the PV+ axon subpopulation has been underestimated. Since the number of PV+ neurons remained unchanged, consistent with previous reports3,5,41,42, and given that myelinated PV+ axons account for approximately 80% of all myelinated axons in the stratum radiatum10,32, the similar abnormalities detected in both MBP + and MBP + /PV+ axons indicate that these alterations are not simply a consequence of reduced PV expression. To further characterize the structural nature of these myelin alterations, we also examined the density of Caspr + paranodes. We observed that it remained unchanged, which suggests that the number of myelin internodes may be preserved. This is consistent with the reported preservation of the axonal arbor3,42,44. Yet, the presence of aberrant helical Caspr immunostaining along internodes directly aligns with our observation of disrupted myelin integrity in the CA1 stratum radiatum of pilocarpine-treated mice.

In the hippocampal CA1 region, myelinated PV+ interneurons include basket, bistratified and chandelier cells10, all of which contribute to the regulation of network inhibition43,50,51. It has been suggested that myelin damage may contribute to epileptogenesis. Indeed, myelination alterations impair the capacity of these interneurons to sustain high-frequency firing7, which may compromise local inhibitory control. This, in turn, could create a demyelination-induced glutamatergic imbalance driving hippocampal hyperexcitability52 and favoring epileptiform activity. Along these lines, myelin abnormalities are well-established features of TLE, having been reported across both gray and white matter in human patients and animal models12,14,16,53. Furthermore, impaired PV+ interneuron-mediated inhibition has been associated with altered theta rhythms and interictal epileptic discharges in toxic and genetic models of de- and dys-myelination11.

In our study, we found that myelin alterations in PV+ interneurons were closely associated with changes in the dynamics of the oligodendrocyte lineage cell population during epileptogenesis. During the early post-SE phase, there is no significant change in OPC density but an increase in the number of mature OLs and PreOLs, which suggests that OPCs are differentiating. This may represent an adaptive response to alterations in the myelin of GABAergic axons13,54. Furthermore, remodeling of the inhibitory network may induce changes in GABAergic inputs on OPCs and play a role in oligodendrogenesis7,55. Alternatively, the increase in mature OLs and PreOLs could result from microglial activation23. Along these lines, we observed prominent microgliosis characterized by an increased density of Iba1 + cells, as well as their morphological transformation from a ramified to a bushy phenotype21.

We observed intense microgliosis during the epileptogenic phase, which normalized in the chronic phase. This microgliosis may contribute directly to the observed myelin alterations and reshape the oligodendrocyte lineage in TLE. Indeed, microglia may contribute to myelin alterations in TLE through multiple pathways, including CSF1R signaling via microglial phagocytosis, as demonstrated in a toxic model of demyelination56. In this context, myelin phagocytosis by microglia has recently been described in an LPS-induced model of encephalopathy, in which robust microglial activation in the CA1 hippocampus was accompanied by active myelin engulfment57. To evaluate the impact of microgliosis on the reduction in mean lengths of MBP + and MBP + /PV+ segments during the epileptogenic phase, we used a CSF1R inhibitor (GW2580) to selectively inhibit the proliferation of microglia. We found that this pharmacological intervention significantly preserved the mean lengths of MBP + and MBP + /PV+ segments in the CA1 stratum radiatum. Therefore, our data support a role of microglia in the dysmyelination of PV+ interneurons during the epileptogenesis phase.

Microgliosis is associated with inflammatory processes involving elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, which have been extensively described in epileptic tissue58. These cytokines may also play a beneficial role in OPC survival and differentiation23,59. In this context, our results support a model in which transient microgliosis during epileptogenesis alters myelination of PV+ interneurons, while contributing to oligodendrocyte lineage remodeling, with increased PreOL and mature OL populations as a possible compensatory mechanism. Consistent with this interpretation, GW2580 prevented both the shortening of MBP + and MBP + /PV+ segments and the changes in PreOL and mature OL populations. Thus, our findings support a role for microgliosis in coordinating oligodendroglial reactivity and PV+ interneuron myelination disturbances during epileptogenesis.

Our study provides novel insights into TLE pathophysiology, highlighting the role of glial components, particularly microglia and myelination, in disease development. The similarities between TLE and multiple sclerosis, including seizure susceptibility, myelination alterations, interneuronopathy, and glial responses45,60–63, suggest a new paradigm that extends beyond the traditional excitatory/inhibitory imbalance model of epilepsy. By supporting a link between microgliosis and alterations of myelin patterning in CA1 PV+ interneurons, our findings advance understanding of both interneuron pathology and TLE pathophysiology. However, the functional impact of preventing these myelin alterations remains to be determined. Indeed, it has been reported that GW2580 treatment during early disease development did not reduce SE severity or subsequent spontaneous seizure development, despite providing hippocampal neuroprotection18. This observation highlights the complexity of epileptogenesis, which cannot be reduced to inflammation alone, but involves multiple interacting cellular and network mechanisms17. Thus, future studies combining PV+ interneuron myelin analysis with EEG monitoring will be required to determine whether preventing these myelin alterations translates into improved network function and/or reduced seizure burden. This perspective may inform future therapeutic strategies for TLE and related neurological disorders characterized by inflammation-mediated myelin abnormalities.

Methods

Pilocarpine-caffeine-induced model of TLE

Chronic epilepsy was induced in seven-week-old male CD1 mice (Charles River Laboratories) using the pilocarpine-induced status epilepticus (SE) model, as previously described31; in this refined TLE model, all caffeine-pilocarpine-treated mice developed spontaneous interictal events and ictal seizures31. Because the TLE model is also associated with altered social behavior, both pilocarpine-treated mice and their age-matched controls were single-housed in enriched cages (with cardboard igloos or tubes, nesting paper, etc.)31,41. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with European Community Council directives (2010/63/UE) and were approved by the French Ministry for Research following ethical evaluation by the Aix-Marseille University institutional animal care and use committee (protocol numbers: #9896 and #38075). A total of 34 mice were included in the histological analysis. To enable comprehensive analysis across different stages of epilepsy development, from the initial epileptogenic phase through the chronic phase, the animals were divided into three main experimental groups: the early-phase group, corresponding to the epileptogenic period and comprising mice at 5–15 days post-SE; the chronic-phase group, consisting of mice at 2 and 4 months post-SE; and a subset of 5-days-post-SE mice that also underwent oral gavage treatment with GW2580 (see below). Age-matched control animals (n = 24) were analyzed in parallel with the experimental groups.

GW2580 treatment

GW2580 (LC Labs) is a selective colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) inhibitor that acts on microglia through the cFMS kinase pathway. Its effectiveness in inhibiting microglial proliferation has been well-documented across epileptic, neurodegenerative, and physiological conditions18,40,64–67. Based on previously published protocols, we administered GW2580 through daily oral gavage at 80 mg/kg, a dose shown to inhibit microglial proliferation in mice within 8 days18,40. The drug preparation protocol was as follows: (i) GW2580 powder was first solubilized in Tween80 using ultrasonication (Branson sonifier 450) to maximize solubility. (ii) After complete dissolution, a mixture of Tween80 (10%) and corn oil (90%) (both from Sigma-Aldrich) was added to achieve the required concentration. (iii) A vehicle solution (10% Tween80 + 90% Corn oil) was prepared as a control to account for potential effects of solvents or oral gavage. (iv) All solutions were freshly prepared daily under sterile conditions. The 8-day treatment protocol (200 μl/day) comprised three groups: pilocarpine-induced SE mice treated with GW2580 (early post-SE + GW2580, n = 5), pilocarpine-induced SE mice treated with vehicle (early post-SE + Veh, n = 6), and non-SE controls treated with GW2580 (Control + GW2580, n = 6). SE was induced with pilocarpine using the same protocol as previously described31. In the post-SE groups, GW2580 (or vehicle) administration started 3 days before SE induction and continued for 5 days after SE, to limit the microglial proliferation occurring in the hippocampus during this period. Importantly, the two pilocarpine groups (vehicle and GW2580) experienced SE of comparable duration (≈1 h) following similar pilocarpine doses (375 ± 33.5 mg/kg in early post-SE + Veh vs 360 ± 36.4 mg/kg in early post-SE + GW2580; n = 6 and n = 5, respectively; p > 0.05, Mann–Whitney U test). The GW2580-treated control group followed the same dosing schedule but did not undergo SE induction. Following the treatment period, animals were intracardially perfused with PBS/heparin (0.1%) followed by paraformaldehyde (4%), and their brains were collected for histological analysis.

Immunostaining protocol

Epileptic mice and their controls were sacrificed at four time points: 5 days, 15 days, 2 months, and 4 months post-SE. Animals were anesthetized with Domitor/Zoletil before sequential intracardiac perfusion with PBS/heparin (0.1%) followed by paraformaldehyde (PFA, 4%, AntigenFix DiaPath). After decapitation, brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for up to 24 h, then washed in PBS. For cryopreservation, brains were immersed in 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C, embedded in optimum cutting temperature medium (OCT, Tissue-Tek), and stored at − 80 °C. Coronal Sects. (60 μm) were cut at − 25 °C using a Leica CM3050S Cryostat and stored in PBS-NaN3 (0.01%) at 4 °C. Sections were first permeabilized for 2 h at room temperature in PBS containing Triton X-100 (0.5%), horse serum (10%), and bovine serum albumin (1%). The sections were then incubated for 2 days at 4 °C under constant agitation with primary antibodies diluted in the permeabilization solution. Primary antibodies included goat anti-PV (1:1000, Swant), rat anti-MBP (1:1000, Abcam), rabbit anti-Caspr (1:2000, gift from L. Goutebroze; Bonnon et al., 2007), rabbit anti-Olig2 (1:3000, Sigma), rat anti-PDGFRα (1:250, Merck), mouse anti-CC1 (APC-7) (1:400, Merck), and rabbit anti-Iba1 (1:2000, Abcam). Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Invitrogen) for 2 h under agitation at room temperature. Finally, sections were counterstained with DAPI (1:2000, 5 min, room temperature) and mounted with Fluoromount (Thermo Fisher).

Confocal microscopy and image analysis

Confocal imaging: Fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope equipped with a 20 × objective. Excitation was achieved using solid-state lasers (405 nm, 488 nm, 568 nm and 647 nm) for Alexa Fluor-stained slices. Images were collected automatically with two-frame scan averaging at Airy 1, with consistent laser intensity and gain settings maintained across control and epileptic conditions. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software (NIH). Regions of interest were imaged in the dorsal hippocampus of the CA1 region, spanning ± 200 µm around the following coordinates: ML = 1.1 mm, AP = − 2.0 mm, and DV = 1.3 mm (according to labs.gaidi.ca). DAPI nuclear staining was used to delineate the different CA1 layers: stratum oriens (SO), stratum pyramidale (SP) and stratum radiatum (SR). Regions of interest (ROIs) per animal were analyzed, either encompassing all three CA1 subregions (SO, SP and SR), or being restricted to the SR, depending on the analyzed parameter (see below for details).

PV + interneuron analysis

Sections were immunostained with goat anti-PV and AlexaFluor568 conjugated donkey anti-goat antibodies. Images were acquired using a 20 × objective with 0.5 × zoom to analyze a 640 µm × 640 µm field of view, within which 3 systematic ROIs were defined per mouse, corresponding to the SO, SP, and SR. The numerical density of PV+ cell bodies was quantified on maximum intensity projections generated from 5 z-stacks (1 µm intervals). The numerical density of PV+ cell bodies under control and epileptic conditions was determined through manual counting.

Myelin segment analysis

Sections were double-immunostained with goat anti-PV and rat anti-MBP antibodies, followed by Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated donkey anti-goat and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-rat secondary antibodies. Myelin segment analysis: Images were acquired using a 20 × objective with 2.5 × zoom to analyze a 128 µm × 128 µm ROI per mouse, localized at the same level in the SR of CA1, contiguous to the SP area; this approach enabled the detection and analysis of a large number of myelin segments, with an average of 162 reconstructed segments per ROI (Supp. Table 1). MBP + segment reconstruction was performed manually using ImageJ tools on maximum-intensity projections of 5 z-stacks acquired at 1 µm intervals. Only MBP + segments and MBP + /PV+ double-labeled segments were included in the quantification; PV-only processes, which may include dendritic segments, were excluded, so that the analysis reflects myelinated axonal segments exclusively and does not assess unmyelinated PV+ processes. To ensure consistent reconstruction, each z-plane was examined individually and the MBP + segments were reconstructed using maximum-intensity projection images that were systematically examined alongside single confocal sections using the ImageJ “Synchronize Windows” tool. A segment was included only when its MBP + signal was clearly above background (~ 2x). Reconstruction was terminated upon detection of a nodal gap, or when the fiber exited the focal plane.

Paranode analysis

Double-immunostaining was performed using rabbit anti-Caspr and rat anti-MBP antibodies, and AlexaFluor568 conjugated donkey anti-rabbit and AlexaFluor488 conjugated donkey anti-rat antibodies. Images were acquired using a 20 × objective with 2.5 × zoom to analyze ROIs of 128 µm × 128 µm localized in the SR of CA1, contiguous to the SP area. The analysis was performed in maximum projection images of 13–15 z-stacks of 1 µm intervals (2 ROIs/mouse). Caspr + profiles were classified as unpaired or paired Caspr + paranodal clusters, since myelination along interneuron axons can be discontinuous.

Characterization of the oligodendrocyte lineage

Sections were triple-immunostained with rabbit anti-Olig2, rat anti-PDGFRα, and mouse anti-CC1 antibodies and then AlexaFluor568 conjugated donkey anti-rabbit, AlexaFluor488 conjugated donkey anti-rat, and AlexaFluor647 donkey anti-mouse antibodies. Images of oligodendrocyte lineage cells were acquired using a 20 × objective with 0.5 × zoom to analyze a 640 µm × 640 µm field of view, within which 3 systematic ROIs were defined per mouse, corresponding to the SO, SP, and SR. Quantification was performed on maximum intensity projections of 5 z-stacks (2 µm intervals). Cells were identified via triple immunostaining: Olig2 for the pan-oligodendrocyte lineage; PDGFRα for oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs); and CC1 for mature oligodendrocytes (OLs). Cell types were classified as follows: OPCs (Olig2 + /PDGFRα + /CC1 −), pre-oligodendrocytes (PreOLs) (Olig2 + /PDGFRα + /CC1 +) and mature OLs (Olig2 + /PDGFRα − /CC1 +). The numerical density of OPC, PreOL and mature OL cell bodies under control and epileptic conditions was determined through manual counting.

Microglial analysis

Sections were immunostained with rabbit anti-Iba1 and then AlexaFluor568 conjugated donkey anti-rabbit. Microglial images were acquired using a 20 × objective with 0.5 × zoom to analyze a 640 µm × 640 µm field of view, within which 3 systematic ROIs were defined per mouse, corresponding to the SO, SP, and SR. Quantification was performed on maximum intensity projections of 5 z-stacks (2 μm intervals). Iba1 + immunostaining was used to analyze microgliosis throughout TLE progression. The numerical density of Iba1 + cell bodies under control and epileptic conditions was determined through manual counting.

Statistical methods

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and “n” represents the number of mice per experimental group. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Prior to statistical analysis, data distributions were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data and comparisons between multiple experimental groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by an appropriate post hoc multiple comparisons test. For comparisons of cumulative frequency distributions, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used. For comparisons between two experimental groups displaying non-normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Significance thresholds were set at p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****), with non-significant differences (p > 0.05) denoted as “ns” in figures. Detailed values and statistics are provided in Supplementary Table 1.
