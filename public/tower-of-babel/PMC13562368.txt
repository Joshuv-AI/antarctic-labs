VIABAHN stent grafts for traumatic arterial injury: A case series
Authors: Autumn Bertch, Dani Douri, Timothy Pansegrau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe a single-center experience using VIABAHN stent grafts (W. L. Gore & Associates) for endovascular repair of traumatic arterial injuries.MethodsWe performed a retrospective case series of adults who underwent repair of acute traumatic arterial injury using VIABAHN stent grafts at a single tertiary center from 2014 to 2025. Demographics, injury characteristics, procedural details, and outcomes were collected from the electronic medical record. Outcomes included technical success, limb salvage, complications, reintervention, mortality, and primary patency at last follow-up.ResultsNine patients were included. Median age was 47 years (interquartile range, 28-58), and six patients (67%) were male. Penetrating trauma accounted for five injuries (56%). Injured vessels included the superficial femoral artery in six patients, the popliteal artery in five patients, and the axillary artery in one patient. Technical success was achieved in all patients (100%), and limb salvage was achieved in eight patients (89%). Reintervention was required in three patients (33%), and thrombosis occurred in two patients (22%). There were no bleeding complications, infections, or deaths. At a median follow-up of 24 months (interquartile range, 9.75-34.5), primary patency was maintained in six of seven evaluable patients (86%).ConclusionsVIABAHN stent graft repair of traumatic arterial injuries was feasible in this small single-center series, with high technical success, favorable limb salvage, and acceptable midterm patency. Larger studies are needed to better define long-term durability and optimal patient selection.Keywords: Traumatic arterial injury, Vascular trauma, Endovascular repair, VIABAHN stent graft, Peripheral arterial injury, Limb salvage

Traumatic arterial injuries are associated with significant morbidity and risk of limb loss, particularly when involving major peripheral vessels. Rapid restoration of perfusion is critical to prevent irreversible ischemia and improve clinical outcomes. Traditionally, open surgical repair has been the standard of care for these injuries; however, operative management can be challenging in the setting of polytrauma, soft tissue damage, or hemodynamic instability.

Endovascular techniques have emerged as an alternative approach for the management of arterial injuries, offering the advantages of rapid hemorrhage control while preserving distal perfusion. Covered stent grafts, including the heparin-bonded VIABAHN endoprosthesis, have gained increasing interest due to their flexibility and ability to conform to tortuous or mobile vascular segments, making them particularly suitable for anatomically complex regions such as the femoropopliteal and axillary arteries.1

Several retrospective studies have demonstrated high technical success rates and favorable short-term outcomes with VIABAHN stent graft placement in the setting of arterial injury, including both extremity and visceral vascular beds.2,3 Covered stent grafts have been shown to provide effective hemostasis while maintaining vessel patency, with technical success rates approaching 100% in emergent settings.4 However, existing data are limited by heterogeneity in patient populations, frequent inclusion of iatrogenic or nontraumatic injuries, and a relative lack of focused reports on traumatic peripheral arterial injuries, which remain largely limited to small case reports and heterogeneous series.5

Furthermore, most available studies emphasize purely endovascular approaches, whereas real-world management of vascular trauma often involves a combination of percutaneous and open surgical access techniques. Additional data are needed to better characterize the role of VIABAHN stent grafts in traumatic arterial injury, including procedural variability and clinical outcomes.

The purpose of this study is to describe our institutional experience with the use of VIABAHN stent grafts in the management of traumatic arterial injuries, with a focus on procedural characteristics, technical success, and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study is a retrospective case series conducted at a single tertiary care center, Sanford Medical Center in Bismarck, North Dakota. Institutional review board approval was obtained with a waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patient selection

Patients were identified through review of electronic medical records and operative logs at Sanford Medical Center in Bismarck, North Dakota, between 2014 and 2025. Adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent repair of acute traumatic arterial injury using a VIABAHN stent graft were included. Selection for VIABAHN stent graft repair was based on surgeon assessment of injury anatomy, feasibility of endovascular exclusion, need for rapid hemorrhage control or revascularization, associated soft tissue injury, and anticipated morbidity of open exposure or bypass. Open repair or bypass was considered when clinically appropriate; however, stent graft repair was selected when endovascular or hybrid treatment was felt to provide effective arterial repair while minimizing additional dissection through injured tissue planes. Inclusion required availability of complete clinical, procedural, and follow-up data. Patients were excluded if medical records or follow-up data were incomplete; if the indication for stent graft placement was nontraumatic (eg, pseudoaneurysm or peripheral arterial disease); or if previous intervention in the target vessel or active systemic infection was present. In penetrating trauma cases, infection prevention measures included perioperative antibiotic administration and operative irrigation and debridement when indicated based on associated soft tissue injury. Additional exclusions included patients with known hypercoagulable disorders or vascular injuries not amenable to endovascular repair.

Data collection

Clinical and procedural data were collected from the electronic medical record (Epic Systems). Variables included patient demographics (eg, age, sex, and body mass index), comorbidities, smoking status, mechanism of injury, injured vessel(s), injury type, and presence of associated injuries. Preoperative clinical findings, including pulse deficit and limb ischemia, as well as time to intervention, were also recorded. Procedural variables included access type (such as percutaneous or open exposure), number and size of stent grafts, total stent length, operative time, estimated blood loss, transfusion requirements, intraoperative anticoagulation, and postoperative antiplatelet regimen. Postoperative data included technical success, limb salvage, fasciotomy, thrombosis, infection, bleeding complications, reintervention (including thrombectomy, thrombolysis, or amputation), length of hospital stay, follow-up duration, and primary patency. Detailed patient-level data are provided in Supplementary Tables I-III (online only).

Outcomes

Outcomes of interest included technical success, limb salvage, and postoperative complications. Technical success was defined as successful deployment of the stent graft with restoration of flow across the injured arterial segment. Postoperative complications included thrombosis, bleeding, infection, and need for reintervention. Reinterventions included thrombectomy, thrombolysis, or amputation. Fasciotomy was also recorded as a relevant clinical outcome. Additional outcomes included length of hospital stay, follow-up duration, and primary patency at last follow-up, as assessed by clinical examination and available imaging.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used. Continuous variables are reported as median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables are reported as frequency and percentage. No comparative or inferential statistical analyses were performed.

Results

A total of nine patients undergoing endovascular repair of traumatic arterial injuries with VIABAHN stent grafts were included. Patient demographics and injury characteristics are summarized in Table I. The median age was 47 years (IQR, 28-58). Six of nine patients (67%) were male, and three (33%) were female. The median body mass index was 26 kg/m2 (IQR, 24.5-31.8). Comorbidities included hypertension in three (33%) patients and obesity in three (33%), whereas four patients (44%) had no comorbid conditions. Current, former, and never smoking status were each present in three patients (33%).

Injuries were due to penetrating trauma in five cases (56%) and blunt trauma in four (44%). The most commonly injured vessels were the superficial femoral artery in six patients (67%) and the popliteal artery in five patients (56%), with axillary artery injury in one patient (11%). Injury patterns included transection in three patients (33%), dissection in three patients (33%), occlusion in three (33%), and laceration in one (11%). Associated injuries were present in six patients (67%). Preoperatively, seven patients (78%) had a pulse deficit, and six (67%) demonstrated ischemia. Most patients (eight [89%]) underwent intervention within 6 hours of presentation. Individual patient baseline characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table I (online only).

Procedural characteristics and clinical outcomes are summarized in Table II. Endovascular repair was performed via percutaneous access in six (67%) cases and open access in three (33%). The median number of stents placed was one (IQR, 1-2), with a median total stent length of 100 mm (IQR, 100-150). Median operative time was 255 minutes (IQR, 168-278), and median estimated blood loss was 550 mL (IQR, 80-925). Median perioperative transfusion requirement was 0 units (IQR, 0-5). All patients received intraoperative heparin. Postoperative antiplatelet therapy varied among patients and was individualized based on associated injuries, bleeding risk, and surgeon preference. Six patients received dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel, two patients received aspirin alone, and one patient received no postoperative antiplatelet therapy.

Technical success was achieved in all nine cases (100%). Limb salvage was achieved in eight patients (89%). Fasciotomy was performed in six patients (67%), and thrombosis occurred in two (22%). Reintervention was required in three patients (33%). The two patients with thrombosis were included among the patient’s requiring reintervention and were treated with thrombectomy and thrombolysis, respectively. Thrombus management was individualized based on angiographic findings, distal runoff, and severity of limb ischemia, with thrombectomy or thrombolysis performed at the discretion of the treating surgeon. The remaining reintervention was amputation in one patient, which was attributed to severe associated extremity trauma despite technically successful arterial revascularization. There were no cases of infection, bleeding complications, or mortality. At a median follow-up of 24 months (IQR, 9.75-34.5), primary patency was maintained in six of seven evaluable patients (86%). The median length of hospital stay was 17 days (IQR, 5-22). Individual procedural and outcome data are provided in Supplementary Tables II and III (online only).

Discussion

In this case series, endovascular repair of traumatic arterial injuries using VIABAHN stent grafts demonstrated high technical success, favorable limb salvage, and acceptable midterm patency. These findings support the feasibility of VIABAHN stent grafts as a treatment option in the management of peripheral vascular trauma.

The use of endovascular techniques in vascular trauma has increased in recent years, driven by the need for rapid hemorrhage control with preservation of distal perfusion. Previous studies evaluating VIABAHN stent grafts in arterial injury have reported technical success rates approaching 100%, with low rates of periprocedural complications.1 Similarly, larger retrospective series have demonstrated high clinical success and favorable long-term patency, further supporting the safety and durability of this approach.2 Our findings are consistent with these reports, with 100% technical success and no procedure-related mortality observed.

Previous studies have increasingly described the use of covered stent grafts for arterial injury, including both traumatic and nontraumatic etiologies. Ueda et al1 reported successful emergency VIABAHN stent graft use for upper and lower extremity arterial bleeding, supporting its role in rapid hemorrhage control in selected patients. Brendel et al2 similarly reported high technical success and favorable long-term outcomes with VIABAHN stent grafts for arterial injury management across multiple vascular beds. However, the available literature remains limited by heterogeneous injury mechanisms, variable anatomic locations, small traumatic subgroups, inconsistent antiplatelet regimens, and incomplete long-term follow-up.1,2,5 These limitations are particularly relevant in patients with trauma, in whom contamination, soft tissue injury, stent thrombosis, medication adherence, and surveillance reliability must be considered when selecting endovascular repair.

An important distinction of the present study is the exclusive focus on traumatic arterial injuries. Much of the existing literature includes heterogeneous patient populations, often combining traumatic, iatrogenic, and oncologic etiologies.2,3 In contrast, our series highlights the application of VIABAHN stent grafts specifically in trauma, where rapid revascularization is critical and operative exposure may be limited by soft tissue injury or hemodynamic instability.

In addition, this study reflects real-world procedural variability, including the use of both percutaneous and open surgical access. Although many reports emphasize purely endovascular approaches, vascular trauma management often requires hybrid techniques tailored to the clinical scenario. The ability to deploy VIABAHN stent grafts through either percutaneous or open access provides flexibility in complex cases, particularly when vessel exposure is required or percutaneous access is not feasible.

In this series, VIABAHN stent graft repair was selected when rapid hemorrhage control and restoration of distal perfusion were prioritized, particularly in the setting of complex trauma, associated soft tissue injury, or concern that open exposure and bypass would increase operative time or morbidity. Endovascular repair also allowed treatment without extensive dissection through injured tissue planes. In several cases, open access was still used, reflecting a hybrid approach tailored to the clinical scenario rather than a purely percutaneous strategy. Open repair or bypass remains appropriate when endovascular exclusion is not anatomically feasible, when contamination or infection is present, or when durable reconstruction cannot be achieved with a covered stent graft.

Despite high technical success, complications such as thrombosis and need for reintervention were observed. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which have identified stent graft occlusion as a known limitation of endovascular repair.3 Factors such as vessel size, flow dynamics, and antiplatelet therapy may influence long-term patency. In our series, primary patency remained acceptable at midterm follow-up, though continued surveillance is necessary.

The role of VIABAHN stent grafts in limb salvage is particularly noteworthy. In selected patients with trauma, endovascular repair may offer a less invasive alternative to open bypass, avoiding the need for conduit harvest and minimizing additional soft tissue disruption. This may be especially advantageous in high-acuity settings or when traditional surgical options are limited, as demonstrated in previous case reports.5

This study has several limitations. The small sample size and single-center design limit generalizability. The retrospective nature of the study introduces potential selection bias, and follow-up was not uniform across all patients. Because associated injury details, concomitant venous injury, and access rationale were inconsistently documented, we could not reliably assess their influence on procedural decision-making. Long-term surveillance is particularly important after endovascular repair, and reliability of follow-up should be considered when selecting patients for stent graft repair. Socioeconomic factors, including insurance status, transportation, access to vascular follow-up, and ability to obtain antiplatelet therapy, may influence postoperative surveillance and outcomes. These factors were not uniformly available in the retrospective record and could not be formally analyzed in this series. In addition, the absence of a comparison group limits conclusions regarding superiority over open surgical repair.

Conclusions

Endovascular repair of traumatic arterial injuries using VIABAHN stent grafts is a feasible and effective treatment strategy, demonstrating high technical success and favorable limb salvage. This approach offers a flexible and minimally invasive alternative to traditional open repair in appropriately selected patients. Further studies with larger cohorts are needed to better define long-term outcomes and optimal patient selection.
