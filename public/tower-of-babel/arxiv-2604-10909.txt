Long-range phase coexistence models with degenerate potentials.
Francesco De Pas, Serena Dipierro, Enrico Valdinoci

Abstract
This survey offers an overview of recent advances in nonlocal phase transition problems, modeled by Ginzburg–Landau type energies of the form 1 4 ​ ∬ ℝ 2 ​ n ∖ ( ℝ n ∖ Ω ) 2 | u ⁡ ( x ) − u ⁡ ( y ) | 2 | x − y | n + 2 ​ s ​ 𝑑 x ​ 𝑑 y + ∫ Ω W ⁡ ( u ⁡ ( x ) ) ​ 𝑑 x . \frac{1}{4}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega)^{2}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\;+\;\int_{\Omega}W(u(x))\,dx. 2
Classical results and background

In this paragraph we provide a brief overview of some of the main contributions to the Allen–Cahn equation developed over the years. As mentioned above, the study of qualitative properties of monotone minimizers for energies of this type has a long and well-established history in the literature.

In particular, this line of research traces back to a conjecture formulated by De Giorgi in the late
1970
s, originally stated in the local setting. The conjecture can be formulated as follows.

Problem 1

Let
n\leq 8
and
u
be a bounded entire solution of

-\Delta u=W^{\prime}(u),

(4)

with
\partial_{x_{1}}u>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Is it true that
u
is one-dimensional? Namely, does there exist
e\in S^{n-1}
and a function
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
u(x)=u_{0}(e\cdot x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
?

Equivalently, the conjecture asks whether the level sets of such monotone solutions must necessarily be hyperplanes. De Giorgi’s conjecture was proved to be true for
n\leq 3
see
MR1655510
;
MR1775735
. The cases
4\leq n\leq 8
were settled under the additional assumption that

\lim_{x_{n}\to\pm\infty}u(x^{\prime},x_{n})=\pm 1\quad\mbox{for any }x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},

as shown in
MR2480601
. For
n\geq 9
, a counterexample was constructed in
MR2473304
, showing that the dimensional threshold in the conjecture is sharp.

Analogous symmetry questions have also been investigated in the nonlocal setting, where the Laplacian in (
4
) is replaced by the fractional operator
L_{s}
. In this framework, the conjecture has been proved in dimension
n=2
in
MR2498561
.
In dimension
n=3
, it was established for
s\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
in
MR2644786
;
MR3148114
,
and later extended to the full range
s\in(0,1)
in
MR4124116
;
MR3740395
.
The case
n=4
and
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
was treated in
MR4050103
.
Moreover, the validity of the conjecture for a broad class of truncated kernels was proved in
MR3610941
.

Another key aspect of the Allen–Cahn equation is its deep connection with minimal surface theory. This relationship was first unveiled in the seminal work of Modica and Mortola in
MR445362
for the local case, where the authors identified the sharp–interface limit of the Ginzburg–Landau energy. Later, in
MR1310848
;
farina
, uniform density estimates for minimizers of the Allen–Cahn energy were established.

These ideas were subsequently extended to the nonlocal setting. In particular, in
SV12
the authors investigated the connection between solutions of the fractional Allen–Cahn equation and (non)local minimal surfaces, obtaining a fractional analogue of the classical
\Gamma
-convergence result of Modica and Mortola.
Subsequently, uniform density estimates for solutions and minimizers in the fractional framework were proved in
SV14
, playing a role analogous to their local counterparts in rigidity and classification results.

For completeness, we also refer the reader to
AB94
;
PV20
for
\Gamma
-convergence results in one dimension, corresponding respectively to the cases
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
s\in(0,1)
.

More recently, density estimates in the fractional setting have been established for
degenerate
double-well potentials in
DFGV26
;
DFGV25
.

Altogether, these results show that the geometry of the level sets of solutions to the Allen–Cahn equation reflects, at the interface, the structure of minimal surfaces . From this perspective, rigidity results for level sets—such as those predicted by De Giorgi’s conjecture for monotone solutions—can be interpreted as diffuse counterparts of classical rigidity phenomena in minimal surface theory.

3
Monotone heteroclinic connections in dimension
n=1

As discussed in Sections
1
and
2
, transition layers connecting stable states of a double-well potential exhibit a rich and well-structured behavior. In this section, we collect a number of results concerning existence, regularity, and decay properties of such minimizers in the one-dimensional setting
n=1
.

We begin by clarifying the notion of minimizers for the energy functional (
1
).

Definition 3

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}
. A measurable function
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is called a
local minimizer
of
\mathcal{E}_{s}
in
\Omega
if
\mathcal{E}_{s}(u;\Omega)<+\infty
and

\mathcal{E}_{s}(u;\Omega)\leqslant\mathcal{E}_{s}(u+\phi;\Omega),

for every
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Moreover, we say that a measurable function
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
class A minimizer
of
\mathcal{E}_{s}
if it is a local minimizer in every bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
.

The introduction of class A minimizers is dictated by a structural feature of the problem itself. Indeed, due to the nonlocal nature of the energy
\mathcal{E}_{s}
, its value may diverge when computed over unbounded domains. For this reason, it is customary to keep track of the growth rate of
\mathcal{E}_{s}
via the auxiliary functions

\Psi_{s}(\rho):=\begin{cases}\rho^{1-2s},&\mbox{if }s\in(0,1/2),\\
\log\rho,&\mbox{if }s=1/2,\\
1,&\mbox{if }s\in(1/2,1).\end{cases}

and

\mathcal{G}(u):=\limsup_{\rho\to+\infty}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{s}(u;[-\rho,\rho])}{\Psi_{s}(\rho)}.

This scaling naturally arises from
(
CozziValdNONLINEARITY
, Proposition
3.1
)
, where it is shown that any local minimizer
u\in\mathcal{X}
of the energy
\mathcal{E}_{s}(\cdot,B_{R+2})
satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{s}(u,B_{R})\leq C\Psi_{s}(R),

for any
R\geq 3
.

A first fundamental result on one-dimensional heteroclinic connections is due to

2

4
Reconstructing a potential from the layer in
n=1

As recalled in Section
3
, the classical approach to phase transition problems consists in fixing a double-well potential
W
, typically chosen on phenomenological or heuristic grounds, and then studying the existence, regularity, and qualitative properties of the associated minimizers. In this perspective, results such as Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
show that sufficiently regular double-well potentials give rise to transition layers solving the Allen–Cahn equation (
3
), and that the behavior of
W
and its derivatives near the wells governs the asymptotic decay of the layer.

This naturally raises the converse question, which lies at the core of this subsection.

Problem 2

Let
n=1
. Given a transition layer whose asymptotic behavior is prescribed a priori, is it possible to construct a double-well potential
W
such that (
3
) is satisfied? If so, how does the shape of the layer determine the behavior of
W
and its derivatives near the wells?

This inverse viewpoint is consistent with the phenomenological nature of phase transition models. In many applications, the potential is not derived from first principles but postulated in the spirit of Landau’s theory for phase transition models, where the free energy is expanded in powers of an order parameter. From this perspective, reconstructing the potential from observable features of an interface—such as decay rates toward the pure phases—appears both natural and conceptually meaningful.

The relevance of Problem
2
is already suggested by the works
CS14
;
MR3280032
. Indeed, their results show that the mere existence of a monotone transition layer imposes strong structural constraints on the potential.

Theorem 4.1
(Theorem
2.4
in
MR3280032
)

Let
f\in C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
with
\gamma>\max\{0,1-2s\}
and let
W^{\prime}=f
. Then there exists a monotone increasing solution
u\in\mathcal{X}
of (
3
) if and only if

W^{\prime}(\pm 1)=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad W>W(1)=W(-1)\ \mbox{in }(-1,1).

If, in addition,
W^{\prime\prime}(-1)>0
and
W^{\prime\prime}(1)>0
, then such a solution is unique up to translations.

This theorem shows that the existence of a layer solution forces the potential to be of double-well type, thus providing a first partial answer to Problem
2
.

An alternative perspective arises in the study of crystal dislocation models.
In
DPV15
, the authors explicitly build a potential starting from a prescribed transition layer. More precisely, they define an even and positive function
w\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
by

w(x):=\frac{1}{|x|^{1+2s}}\qquad\mbox{for }|x|\geq 1.

Then, they let
A:=\|w\|_{L^{1}(0,+\infty)}
and introduce the function
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
defined as

\phi(x):=\frac{1}{A}\int_{0}^{x}w(t)\,dt.

They then set

\begin{split}&g(t):=L_{s}\phi(t)\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R},\\
&h(r):=g(\phi^{-1}(r))\quad\text{for all }r\in(-1,1),\end{split}

and define the potential

V(r):=\int_{-1}^{r}h(\rho)\,d\rho\quad\mbox{for any }r\in(-1,1).

(11)

The resulting potential enjoys the following properties.

Theorem 4.2
