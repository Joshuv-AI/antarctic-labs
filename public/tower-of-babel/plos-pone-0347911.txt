Sex differences in early and late neonatal all-cause mortality and disease burden in Kazakhstan, 2010–2023: A population-based analysis of Global Burden of Disease estimates

Yelena Tsay, Bekzhan Namet, Saltanat Urazova, Nazym Galimgozhina, Lyailyum Saparova, Nazym Amanbayeva, Madina Syzdykova, Yuliya Ganzhula

ABSTRACT
Background: Neonatal mortality remains a major contributor to child mortality globally, with the greatest burden concentrated in the early neonatal period. Sex differences in neonatal survival are consistently reported, yet population-level evidence on sex-specific patterns of all-cause burden across neonatal subperiods in Central Asia remains limited. This study aimed to quantify temporal trends and sex differences in early (0–6 days) and late (7–27 days) neonatal all-cause mortality and disease burden in Kazakhstan from 2010 to 2023, and to examine projected trajectories to 2030. Methods: We conducted a population-based time-trend study using Global Burden of Disease 2023 estimates for Kazakhstan. Outcomes included mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years of life lost (YLLs), and years lived with disability (YLDs), expressed as age-specific rates per 100,000 person-years within the corresponding neonatal age interval. Analyses were stratified by sex and neonatal subperiod. Temporal patterns were described, and sex differences were assessed using comparative rates. Log-linear models were used to estimate projected trends to 2030. National mortality statistics were used for external trend consistency. Results: Neonatal mortality and DALY rates declined between 2010 and 2023 in both early and late neonatal periods, with substantially higher rates observed in the early neonatal period. Across all years, males consistently exhibited higher mortality, YLLs, and DALYs than females in both neonatal subperiods; YLD rates followed a different pattern, being modestly higher among females in the early-neonatal period but nearly equal between sexes in the late-neonatal period. YLLs accounted for the vast majority of DALYs, indicating that mortality rather than non-fatal disability drives neonatal burden. Projections suggest continued declines through 2030, male point estimates for mortality and DALYs remained higher than female estimates throughout, although the magnitude of this difference narrowed substantially and became statistically uncertain in the most recent years, with rate-ratio uncertainty intervals spanning or approaching unity. Conclusions: Despite substantial reductions in neonatal mortality in Kazakhstan, male point estimates for mortality and burden remained higher than female estimates throughout the study period, although this difference narrowed substantially and became uncertain in recent years; mortality remained heavily concentrated in the early neonatal period. These findings highlight persistent, though narrowing, epidemiologic inequalities within improving population health trends.

1. Introduction Neonatal mortality and disability remain fundamental indicators of health system performance, as survival and healthy development in the first month of life depend on timely, continuous, and high‑quality care spanning pregnancy, childbirth, the immediate postnatal period, and early infancy. From a theoretical standpoint, the WHO Health Systems Framework positions neonatal outcomes as a reflection of system performance across its core building blocks—service delivery, health workforce, financing, governance, information systems, and access to essential medicines [ 1 ]. At the same time, the Life Course perspective underscores the neonatal period as a critical developmental window, where early disadvantage can accumulate into long‑term health, educational, and socioeconomic inequities [ 2 ]. Together, these frameworks highlight neonatal survival not only as a measure of immediate system effectiveness but also as a predictor of population well‑being across the lifespan. Despite substantial global improvements in neonatal outcomes over recent decades, progress has been uneven across countries and regions, with preventable causes—such as complications of prematurity, neonatal infections, and acute respiratory or gastrointestinal conditions—continuing to drive early life losses [ 3 ]. UNICEF estimates that 2.3 million infants died during the neonatal period in 2023, equivalent to roughly 6,300 deaths each day worldwide [ 4 ]. Current projections suggest that between 2020 and 2030, approximately 48 million deaths among children under five could occur, with nearly half expected during the neonatal period [ 5 ]. In middle‑income settings, persistent gaps often reflect barriers in early identification of risk, delayed care seeking, discontinuities between facility and community services, and variable access to responsive primary health care [ 6 ]. Moreover, sustained socioeconomic hardship and psychosocial adversity in the earliest years of life can produce long‑lasting harms, including worse health, lower educational attainment, and reduced adult income, underscoring early childhood as a critical window for prevention [ 7 ]. Families with fewer socioeconomic resources often face greater obstacles to accessing health and social support services, magnifying developmental risks for children. Kazakhstan, as an upper-middle-income country in Central Asia [ 8 ], has experienced notable improvements in maternal and child health over the past two decades. Neonatal mortality has declined in parallel with broader health system strengthening, increased access to skilled birth attendance, and improvements in perinatal and neonatal care [ 9 , 10 ]. However, neonatal outcomes remain a key component of national health priorities, particularly given persistent regional disparities and the relatively high contribution of neonatal deaths to overall child mortality. Previous national and regional studies have primarily focused on mortality indicators, with limited attention to the broader burden of disease, including disability and long-term health consequences. Furthermore, there is limited evidence on future trajectories of neonatal burden in Kazakhstan, which is essential for informing policy planning and resource allocation. To address this gap, this study aimed to quantify temporal trends and sex differences in early and late neonatal mortality and disease burden in Kazakhstan from 2010 to 2023 using Global Burden of Disease 2023 estimates. In addition, we examined the relative contribution of fatal and non-fatal components of burden and explored projected trajectories to 2030. Most existing GBD-based analyses of neonatal mortality report all-age neonatal burden or focus on single-country settings outside Central Asia; sex-specific stratification by early- and late-neonatal subperiod, combined with disaggregation of fatal and non-fatal burden and forward projection to 2030, has not previously been reported for Kazakhstan or, to our knowledge, for a Central Asian country. Kazakhstan is a useful setting for this analysis because it combines upper-middle-income status, rapid recent health-system reform, and a relatively data-complete vital registration system feeding into GBD estimation, making it a plausible case for observing how sex-specific neonatal disparities evolve alongside health-system strengthening in a transitional-economy context underrepresented in the neonatal GBD literature. 2. Methods 2.1. Study design, data source, and study outcomes This study was a population-level descriptive and modeling analysis of neonatal mortality and disease burden in Kazakhstan using estimates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 study. The GBD study provides comprehensive and standardized estimates of mortality and morbidity for a wide range of diseases and injuries across countries and over time using a combination of epidemiological data sources, statistical modeling, and comparative risk assessment frameworks. The detailed description of the GBD methods for data collection and estimation process is provided elsewhere [ 11 – 13 ]. The GBD framework generates internally consistent estimates of mortality and other health indicators by age, sex, location, and year using a combination of primary data sources, statistical modeling, and cause-of-death ensemble methods. Information on all data inputs used in generating GBD 2023 estimates is available through the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) Sources Tool for GBD 2023 [ 14 ]. For this study, data specific to Kazakhstan were extracted from the Global Burden of Disease 2023 database for the period 2010–2023. The data were accessed for research purposes on 19 January 2026. Neonatal mortality was defined according to GBD age classifications and disaggregated into early neonatal (0–6 days) and late neonatal (7–27 days) periods. Estimates were retrieved separately for males and females to enable sex-specific analyses, as well as for both sexes combined where appropriate. GBD age-specific rates are expressed per 100,000 person-years within the early-neonatal (0–6 days) or late-neonatal (7–27 days) age interval and should not be interpreted as mortality probabilities per live birth. Data were extracted from the GBD 2023 Results Tool for Kazakhstan (location_id 36), cause “All causes” (cause_id 294), sex Male/Female/Both (sex_id 1/2/3), ages Early Neonatal (0–6 days, age_id 2) and Late Neonatal (7–27 days, age_id 3), measures Deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs (measure_id 1/2/4/3), metric Rate (metric_id 3), for years 2010–2023. All-cause burden was extracted directly as the pre-aggregated “All causes” GBD category rather than calculated by summing individual causes. The authors did not have access to any information that could identify individual participants during or after data extraction, as the study used only aggregated, de-identified secondary data. GBD 2023 produces estimates across a comprehensive set of diseases, injuries, and risk factors, stratified by age group, sex, geographic location, and year [ 11 – 13 ]. These estimates are derived using standardized modeling approaches that integrate multiple data sources, including vital registration systems, surveys, and surveillance data, to ensure comparability across settings. For Kazakhstan, neonatal mortality estimates therefore reflect modeled outputs that account for data completeness, potential misclassification, and temporal trends, enabling consistent comparisons over time and between population subgroups. The primary outcomes of interest were all-cause neonatal mortality and disease burden, assessed using four standard GBD health metrics: Deaths, Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), Years of life lost due to premature mortality (YLLs), Years lived with disability (YLDs). DALYs represent the sum of fatal and non-fatal health loss and are defined as the combination of YLLs and YLDs. These metrics allow quantification of both mortality and morbidity components of neonatal health burden. For the primary analysis, we focused on all-cause neonatal burden, which captures the aggregate health loss across all neonatal conditions. Uncertainty intervals provided by the GBD study (lower and upper bounds) were incorporated into descriptive visualizations to illustrate the uncertainty surrounding modeled estimates. To provide additional context for the findings, we incorporated an external trend comparison using officially reported national mortality indicators. Specifically, we used the indicator “infant mortality rate in the Republic of Kazakhstan per 1,000 live births” obtained from national statistical sources for the same study period [ 15 ]. Given differences in age definitions (infant vs. neonatal), denominators, and estimation methodologies between GBD outputs and national statistics, direct numerical equivalence between these measures was not expected. Because GBD estimates themselves incorporate national vital-registration and government data as inputs, the national statistics used here are not fully independent of the GBD estimation process; accordingly, this comparison is presented as a trend concordance check rather than an external validation, and does not on its own establish that GBD-derived estimates are free of modeling artifacts. In addition, we examined male infant mortality rates per 1,000 live births derived from national data to assess whether the sex-specific patterns identified in the GBD neonatal analysis were also reflected in routinely collected national infant mortality statistics, while recognizing that infant and neonatal mortality are not the same measure (see Limitations). Ethical approval for this study was waived by the Local Bioethics Committee of Astana Medical University, as the analysis was based exclusively on secondary, aggregated data obtained from publicly available sources and did not involve identifiable human participants. Patient consent was not applicable, as the study did not involve identifiable individual participants. 2.2. Statistical analysis and modeling description Analyses for original GBD 2023 were implemented in Python, Stata, and R, with full code made publicly available [ 16 ]. Analyses for the present study were conducted using R version 4.5.1 (2025-06-13) [ 17 ] within the RStudio environment (version 2025.9.0.387) [ 18 ]. Descriptive analyses summarized annual estimates of deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs by sex and neonatal age group. Temporal patterns were visualized using line plots with uncertainty ribbons representing the GBD-provided lower and upper bounds of the estimates. To quantify temporal changes in neonatal burden, we evaluated trends in each outcome measure between 2010 and 2023. Percentage change between the first and last year of observation was calculated for each outcome, sex, and neonatal age group. In addition, estimated annual percent change (EAPC) was calculated using log-linear regression models of the form: log ( r a t e t ) = α + β × y e a r t where r a t e t represents the annual rate per 100,000 person-year in year t . The estimated annual percent change was derived as: E A P C = 1 0 0 × ( e β − 1 ) This approach allows consistent comparison of temporal trends across outcomes and demographic strata. Sex differences were assessed by comparing rates between males and females over time and across neonatal age groups. Differences were visualized using sex-specific trend plots and male-to-female comparisons in projected estimates. Male-to-female rate ratios (RRs) were calculated by dividing the male rate by the corresponding female rate for each year, outcome, and neonatal subperiod. Because GBD posterior draws were not available, approximate 95% uncertainty intervals for the RRs were derived from the reported sex-specific GBD uncertainty intervals using a delta-method approximation on the log scale. This calculation necessarily assumes independence between the male and female estimates because their joint covariance was unavailable. The resulting intervals should therefore be regarded as approximate and do not reproduce draw-level GBD uncertainty. GBD uncertainty intervals were incorporated into descriptive visualizations only ( Figs 1 – 4 ) and were not propagated into the EAPC or forecasting models, which were fitted to annual point estimates; because posterior draw-level data were not available to the authors, the reported forecasting intervals reflect only sampling uncertainty in the fitted trend and likely understate the true uncertainty of the projections (see Limitations). To estimate future neonatal burden in Kazakhstan, projections of deaths and DALYs were generated through 2030 using log-linear regression models fitted to observed rates from 2010–2023. For each outcome, age group, and sex, a regression model was fitted to the natural logarithm of the rate as a function of calendar year. Predicted rates for future years were then obtained by exponentiating the fitted values. For each outcome, age group, and sex, 95% prediction intervals for the year-2030 forecast were obtained from the fitted regression model (predict(..., interval = “prediction”)), reflecting uncertainty in a new observation around the fitted trend rather than uncertainty in the mean trend itself. The log-linear model was retained as the primary forecasting approach because it assumes proportional change in rates over time, yields an interpretable constant proportional annual-change parameter (EAPC), and has been widely used in comparable GBD-based trend studies. To formally test whether a single constant rate of change adequately describes the observed trend, we additionally fitted a segmented (broken-stick) regression to the early-neonatal log(rate) series for Deaths and DALYs (both sexes combined) and tested for a non-constant slope using Davies’ test. To evaluate the robustness of projected estimates, two additional forecasting approaches were implemented as sensitivity analyses: 1) Linear regression models on the original rate scale, which assume constant absolute change over time. 2) Exponential smoothing time-series models (ETS), which allow data-driven estimation of trend patterns without imposing a predefined functional form. For each series, the ETS model form (error, trend, and seasonal components) was selected automatically by minimizing AICc using the ets() function in the R forecast package; the selected form is reported for each series in the Results. Projected values for 2030 were compared across models using a three-year rolling-origin out-of-sample root-mean-square error (RMSE; holdout years 2021–2023) as the primary, scale-consistent metric of forecast performance; in-sample AIC/BIC are also reported for the two regression models but are not comparable across response scales (log vs. original) or to ETS. Because the GBD early- and late-neonatal rates are age-specific person-time rates with different denominators, they were not combined into a single 0–27-day rate. Instead, each GBD age-specific mortality series was compared separately with the national infant mortality rate. Descriptive concordance was assessed using Pearson and Spearman correlations for annual levels, Pearson correlations for first differences, and the proportion of consecutive year-pairs showing changes in the same direction. These comparisons were intended to assess broad temporal concordance rather than numerical agreement or external validation. 2.3. Results Neonatal mortality rates declined in Kazakhstan between 2010 and 2023 in both early and late neonatal periods, with consistently higher mortality observed in the early neonatal period across all years. Across the study period, males exhibited higher mortality rates than females in both neonatal subperiods. The male excess was persistent for mortality, YLLs, and DALYs, whereas YLD patterns differed by neonatal subperiod. Fig 1 presents trends in all-cause neonatal mortality rates in Kazakhstan from 2010 to 2023, stratified by neonatal age group and sex. Overall, neonatal mortality declined substantially during the study period in both early neonatal (0–6 days) and late neonatal (7–27 days) populations, although reductions were more pronounced during the early neonatal period. In 2010, the early neonatal (0–6 days) mortality rate was 32,705 deaths per 100,000 person-years among males and 23,486 per 100,000 person-years among females, corresponding to a combined rate of 28,227 per 100,000 person-years (95% UI 25,303–31,856; Table 1 ). Mortality declined steadily over the following decade, reaching 19,524 per 100,000 person-years among males and 18,105 per 100,000 person-years among females by 2023. The combined early neonatal mortality rate decreased to 18,831 per 100,000 person-years, representing an overall decline of approximately 33% compared with 2010 levels (EAPC –3.96% per year, 95% CI –5.39 to –2.51; Table 2 ). Mortality during the late neonatal period (7–27 days) was substantially lower than in the early neonatal stage but also declined over time. By 2023, these values had declined to 3,613 per 100,000 person-years among males and 3,346 per 100,000 person-years among females. Trends in all-cause DALY rates are presented in Fig 2 . Consistent with the mortality trends, the overall disease burden during the early neonatal period also declined substantially. In 2010, the DALY rate was 2,945,416 per 100,000 person-years among males and 2,116,368 among females (95% UI 1,803,638–2,459,879; Table 1 ). By 2023, these values had declined to 1,758,727 per 100,000 person-years among males and 1,631,487 among females (95% UI 1,309,134–2,039,918; Table 1 ). The combined DALY rate decreased from 2,542,756 per 100,000 person-years in 2010–1,696,597 per 100,000 person-years in 2023 (EAPC for DALYs, both sexes: –3.96% per year early-neonatal, –2.07% per year late-neonatal; Table 2 ). The early neonatal burden was overwhelmingly driven by YLL rather than disability. In 2010, YLL rates reached 2,942,609 per 100,000 person-years among males and 2,113,138 among females, whereas YLD rates were substantially lower at 2,807 and 3,230 per 100,000 person-years, respectively. A similar pattern persisted in 2023, with YLL rates remaining orders of magnitude higher than YLD rates. Across all years, male mortality rates consistently exceeded female mortality, indicating a persistent sex disparity in early neonatal survival, although the magnitude of this disparity narrowed substantially over the study period (see Table S1 in S1 File ). Patterns of disease burden during the late neonatal period were similar to those observed in the early neonatal stage but at substantially lower levels. DALY rates decreased from 438,783 per 100,000 person-years among males in 2010–327,478 in 2023, while female DALY rates declined from 356,504–303,418 per 100,000 person-years. As in the early neonatal period, the majority of DALYs were attributable to premature mortality (YLLs) rather than disability. During early neonatal period YLL rates declined markedly over the study period for all sex groups. Among males, YLLs decreased from approximately 2,942,609 per 100,000 person-years in 2010–1,756,681 in 2023, representing a reduction of roughly 40%. A similar pattern was observed among females, with YLL rates declining from 2,113,138–1,629,012 per 100,000 person-years, while the combined population decreased from 2,539,743–1,694,341 per 100,000 person-years. The decline was most pronounced between 2010 and 2016, after which rates stabilized and showed a modest increase around 2020–2021, followed by a subsequent decline toward 2023. Segmented regression of the both-sex early-neonatal mortality and DALY series identified a change in trend around 2018 (Davies’ test p < 0.001, assuming independent residuals; Table S2 in S1 File ), with an average annual decline of approximately 7% before the breakpoint followed by a flat to slightly increasing trend of approximately 2% per year during 2018–2023; because the underlying regression residuals showed significant autocorrelation (Table S3 in S1 File ), this p-value likely understates the true uncertainty of the breakpoint and should be interpreted as nominal rather than autocorrelation-robust (see Section 2.2 ). In contrast, YLD rates were substantially lower and declined more gradually. Male YLD rates decreased from approximately 2,807 per 100,000 person-years in 2010–2,046 in 2023, while female YLDs declined from 3,230–2,476 per 100,000 person-years. The combined YLD rate decreased from 3,013–2,256 per 100,000 person-years over the same period. In the early-neonatal period, female YLD rates consistently exceeded male rates, in contrast to YLL patterns. Late neonatal YLL rates were substantially lower than those observed in the early neonatal period but followed a similar declining trajectory. Among males, YLL rates decreased from approximately 435,512 per 100,000 person-years in 2010–325,033 in 2023, while female YLLs declined from 353,346–301,038 per 100,000 person-years. The combined YLL rate decreased from 395,576–313,313 per 100,000 person-years. YLD rates in the late neonatal period were again minimal relative to YLLs. Male YLDs declined from approximately 3,271 per 100,000 person-years in 2010–2,445 in 2023, and female YLDs decreased from 3,159–2,380 per 100,000 person-years, with the combined rate falling from 3,216–2,413 per 100,000 person-years — male and female late-neonatal YLD rates were nearly identical throughout, unlike the clearer female excess seen in the early-neonatal period. Fig 4 presents male-to-female RRs for neonatal mortality and disease burden in Kazakhstan from 2010 to 2023, stratified by age group and outcome, together with approximate 95% uncertainty intervals computed by the delta method from the GBD-reported male and female uncertainty intervals (Table S1 in S1 File ). A rate ratio greater than 1 indicates a higher burden among males. In the early neonatal period, male point estimates exceeded female point estimates across all mortality-related outcomes. For DALYs, the male-to-female RR remained elevated throughout the study period, fluctuating around 1.35–1.40 between 2010 and 2019 (95% UI excluding 1 in these years), before declining sharply to approximately 1.08 by 2022–2023, with the approximate 95% UI in the most recent years spanning or closely approaching 1. A similar pattern was observed for mortality and YLLs, which closely mirrored the DALY pattern given the dominant contribution of mortality to overall burden, declining from approximately 1.35–1.40 to approximately 1.08–1.10 by 2023. In contrast, early-neonatal YLDs exhibited an inverse pattern, with RRs consistently below 1 throughout the study period (approximately 0.87 in 2010 declining modestly to 0.82–0.84 in later years), indicating a modestly higher disability burden among females, with relatively narrow temporal variation compared to mortality-related measures. Sex disparities in the late neonatal period were less pronounced but followed a similar overall trajectory for mortality-related outcomes: the DALY RR rose from approximately 1.23 in 2010 to 1.30 around 2016 before declining to approximately 1.08 by 2023, with mortality and YLL RRs following a comparable path. In contrast, YLD RRs in the late neonatal period were close to unity throughout the study period, fluctuating around 1.00–1.05 and, in most years, marginally favoring a small male excess rather than the female excess seen in the early-neonatal period — the reverse pattern from early-neonatal YLDs. Across both neonatal age groups and outcomes, projections indicate continued declines in mortality and DALY rates, with reductions occurring across all sex groups. In the early neonatal period, DALY rates are projected to decline substantially by 2030. Among males, DALYs are projected to decrease to approximately 1,117,929 per 100,000 person-years, while rates among females are projected to reach 1,057,849 per 100,000 person-years. Discussion This study provides a population-level assessment of neonatal mortality and disease burden in Kazakhstan, demonstrating substantial declines between 2010 and 2023 alongside persistent sex differences and marked concentration of mortality in the early neonatal period. Overall, the findings indicate substantial progress in reducing neonatal mortality, YLLs, YLDs, and DALYs over the study period. The decline was observed in both males and females and across both neonatal age groups. Mortality, YLL, and DALY point estimates remained higher among males across both neonatal subperiods, whereas YLD patterns differed by subperiod. Another important finding was the continued predominance of YLLs within total DALYs, indicating that premature mortality, rather than non-fatal disability, remains the principal contributor to neonatal burden in Kazakhstan. Although the primary log-linear projections extrapolate further declines, the segmented analysis identified a marked change in the observed early neonatal trajectory around 2018, with subsequent stalling and possible partial reversal. Together with the model dependence observed in the forecasting sensitivity analyses, this finding argues against interpreting continued improvement as assured. The marked decline in neonatal burden observed in this study is consistent with global and regional evidence showing gradual improvements in neonatal survival over the past two decades, particularly in settings that have expanded access to facility-based childbirth, neonatal resuscitation, infection management, and care for preterm infants [ 9 , 10 , 19 , 20 ]. At the same time, our findings suggest that further reductions may become more difficult to achieve as mortality levels decline, since the remaining burden is often concentrated in more complex and less easily preventable conditions, including complications of prematurity, intrapartum-related events, and severe neonatal infections. In this context, the persistent dominance of YLLs is especially important, as it suggests that the greatest opportunities for further improvement lie in preventing fatal outcomes during the earliest and most vulnerable period of life. The persistent male excess is consistent with the established male disadvantage in neonatal survival. Although the male-to-female rate ratio declined from approximately 1.35–1.40 in the early years to 1.08 by 2023, with recent uncertainty intervals approaching or including 1, the remaining disparity suggests persistent biological and perinatal vulnerability among male neonates despite substantial sex convergence. Male disadvantage in neonatal survival has been documented across diverse settings and is commonly attributed to biological vulnerability, including greater susceptibility to respiratory complications, prematurity-related morbidity, inflammatory responses, and adverse perinatal adaptation [ 21 , 22 ]. The narrowing of sex disparities documented here may reflect general improvements in neonatal and perinatal care that benefited both sexes. The continued, though narrowing, excess burden among males supports incorporating sex as a factor in population-level neonatal health surveillance and epidemiological risk stratification. We do not extend this to specific clinical recommendations such as NICU triage protocols, which would require individual-level clinical evidence (e.g., birth weight, gestational age, or admission-level outcome data) beyond the scope of this population-level GBD-based analysis. Rather than implying the need for sex-specific policy in isolation, these findings support the value of incorporating sex into broader, population-level risk-based neonatal care strategies. From a public health perspective, this study not only documents national progress but also identifies persistent vulnerabilities. Although Kazakhstan’s neonatal mortality is relatively low by regional and international standards, direct comparison with the SDG 3.2.2 threshold of 12 deaths per 1,000 live births is not appropriate because the present analysis used the GBD age-specific person-year rate rather than the live-birth-based probability of death [ 23 , 24 ]. Segmented regression identified a change in early-neonatal mortality and DALY trends around 2018 (Davies’ test, p < 0.001 under the standard assumption of independent residuals; Table S2 in S1 File ), with an average annual decline of approximately 7% before 2018 followed by a flat or slightly increasing trend of about 2% per year through 2023; because the underlying regression residuals showed significant autocorrelation (Table S3 in S1 File ), this p-value should be interpreted as nominal rather than autocorrelation-robust (see Section 2.2 ). This pattern suggests a stalling, and possible partial reversal, of earlier progress rather than a continued but slower decline. Potential contributors, including changes in reporting, healthcare utilization, and the COVID-19 pandemic, warrant further investigation. Sustained reductions will therefore require continued improvement in antenatal, intrapartum, and immediate postnatal care, together with equitable implementation across the health system. The observed national trends should be interpreted within the context of broader maternal and child health reforms in Kazakhstan that may plausibly have contributed to them, including the modernization of perinatal care, strengthening of primary healthcare, and efforts to improve continuity of care for mothers and infants, though none of these associations can be tested causally with the present ecological design. In the Kazakhstani context, rural healthcare modernization is a potentially relevant contextual factor given the country’s vast territory and the challenges of ensuring access to services in remote areas [ 25 ]. Expanded rural health infrastructure and service capacity may plausibly have contributed to more favorable conditions for maternal and neonatal care, though this cannot be established from the present data. The Universal Progressive Patronage Model is a further plausible contextual contributor, particularly through risk-stratified home visiting and earlier identification of family vulnerabilities [ 26 ]. In addition, implementation of the Compulsory Social Health Insurance system increased health expenditures and may have supported improvements in service availability and quality of care [ 27 ]. Because this study was based on secondary analysis of GBD estimates and used a descriptive design, it cannot determine the extent to which any of these reforms directly caused the observed declines; each is presented above as a plausible contextual contributor rather than a confirmed explanatory factor, and this limitation applies uniformly to the reforms discussed in this paragraph. These findings are consistent with previous international studies reporting higher neonatal mortality among males and greater vulnerability in the early neonatal period. However, evidence from Central Asia remains limited, and this study contributes region-specific data to the global literature. The projections presented in this study extrapolate historical 2010–2023 trends and do not incorporate future changes in health policy, healthcare financing, demographic structure, infectious disease outbreaks, or socioeconomic conditions, any of which could accelerate, slow, or reverse the observed trajectory; the 2030 estimates should therefore be interpreted as an extension of past patterns under continuity assumptions, not as an expected or most-likely future outcome. Females exhibited modestly higher YLD rates than males in the early-neonatal period ( Fig 3 , Fig 4 ), a pattern also reported in some other GBD-based analyses, though its explanation remains uncertain and may partly reflect sex differences in the cause composition of non-fatal neonatal morbidity captured by GBD disability weights; this pattern was not present in the late-neonatal period, where YLD rates were nearly equal between sexes. Because YLDs contributed only a small fraction of total early-neonatal DALYs (well under 1%), the clinical and public-health significance of this sex difference is likely limited relative to the mortality-driven component of the male excess burden, and we present it descriptively rather than as a target for intervention. These findings have implications for neonatal health monitoring and underscore the importance of focusing on early neonatal survival. Continued improvements in neonatal outcomes are likely to depend on interventions targeting the immediate perinatal period. First, because fatal outcomes continue to account for the overwhelming share of neonatal burden, interventions should remain focused on preventing avoidable neonatal deaths, especially those related to preterm birth complications, intrapartum hypoxia, and severe infections. Second, the persistence of male disadvantage suggests that neonatal risk assessment frameworks may benefit from incorporating sex as one component of early population-level stratification. Third, the apparent stalling of progress after 2018 highlights the need to move beyond simple coverage expansion toward improvement in care quality, including timely identification of complications, neonatal resuscitation capacity, infection prevention and control, specialized care for vulnerable newborns, and strengthened follow-up during the first month of life. Finally, continued strengthening of primary healthcare and community-based support systems, including further expansion and optimization of the Universal Progressive Patronage Model, may help sustain gains by addressing maternal risk factors, improving care-seeking, and reinforcing postnatal monitoring, particularly among vulnerable families. This study has several strengths. It provides a long-term national assessment using a standardized and internationally comparable data source, examines multiple dimensions of burden rather than mortality alone, stratifies findings by sex and neonatal age group, and extends the analysis to projected trends through 2030. These features enhance the relevance of the study for national planning and for situating Kazakhstan within the broader global neonatal health agenda. This study also has several limitations. First, although GBD estimates are standardized and allow comparison across time and settings, the estimates are model-based and depend on the quality and completeness of underlying data sources; in settings with limited primary data, GBD estimates rely more heavily on statistical modeling and covariate-based imputation, which can increase uncertainty in ways not always fully reflected in the reported intervals. GBD estimates are also aggregated at the national level and cannot capture individual-level determinants of neonatal mortality (e.g., birth weight, gestational age, congenital anomalies, or intrapartum complications), limiting the clinical specificity of population-level findings such as these. Second, the study was ecological and descriptive; therefore, causal inferences regarding the impact of policies, reforms, or health system changes cannot be made. Third, the analysis was conducted at the national level and did not account for potential regional, rural-urban, socioeconomic, or ethnic inequalities that may shape neonatal outcomes within Kazakhstan. Fourth, the forecasting approach was based on historical trend extrapolation, fitted to GBD point estimates without propagating the underlying GBD uncertainty intervals (which were not available to the authors as posterior draws) and without correcting for the significant residual autocorrelation detected in most series; accordingly, the reported prediction intervals likely understate the true forecast uncertainty, and the projections should be interpreted as trend-based scenarios under continuity assumptions rather than precise forecasts, consistent with the forecasting sensitivity analysis, which did not show a consistent out-of-sample advantage for the primary log-linear model over the linear or ETS alternatives. This same residual autocorrelation also affects the EAPC 95% CIs and p-values ( Table 2 ) and the Davies’ test p-value for the 2018 breakpoint (Table S2 in S1 File ), both of which were computed under a standard assumption of independent residuals and should therefore be interpreted as nominal rather than autocorrelation-robust statistics (Section 2.2 ). Fifth, the approximate uncertainty intervals reported for the male-to-female rate ratios were computed under an independence assumption between the sex-specific GBD estimates, which is not strictly correct and may not fully capture the true correlation structure of the underlying GBD estimation process. Finally, the external comparison was used to assess trend concordance rather than one-to-one numerical agreement, given differences in age scope (0–27 days vs. 0–1 year), denominators, and the fact that GBD estimates are not fully independent of the national data sources used for comparison. Despite these limitations, the study offers a useful national perspective on progress, remaining challenges, and future priorities for neonatal survival in Kazakhstan. 5. Conclusion Neonatal mortality and disease burden in Kazakhstan declined substantially between 2010 and 2023, with the greatest burden concentrated in the early neonatal period. Male point estimates for mortality, YLLs, and DALYs remained higher than female estimates throughout the observation period, although male-to-female differences narrowed substantially and uncertainty intervals for recent rate ratios included or approached unity. Because neonatal DALYs were overwhelmingly driven by YLLs, further reductions in burden depend principally on preventing early neonatal deaths. The observed stalling of early-neonatal progress after approximately 2018 warrants continued surveillance, while the model dependence of the 2030 projections indicates that these estimates should be interpreted as trend-based scenarios rather than precise forecasts.
