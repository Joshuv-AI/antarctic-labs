 INTRODUCTION
The purpose of this letter report is to document the mitigation of adverse effects of a proposed
undertaking that would demolish the foundation of Building 25-3113/3113A and the adjoining test pad at
Test Cell A in Area 25 of the Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) in compliance with the terms of the
2024 Programmatic Agreement among the U.S. Department of Energy and the Nevada State Historic
Preservation Officer and the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation Concerning the Protection of
Historic Properties on the Nevada National Security Site, Nye County, Nevada (NNSS PA). The
foundation of Building 25-3113/3113A (State Historic Preservation Office [SHPO] Resource # B2443) is
a contributing element to the Nuclear Rocket Development Station (NRDS) Historic District (SHPO
Resource # D424), which has been determined eligible for listing in the National Register of Historic
Places (NRHP) under Criteria A, B, C, and D (Reno et al. 2023).
The proposed demolition constitutes an adverse effect. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) National
Nuclear Security Administration Nevada Field Office (NNSA/NFO) consulted with the SHPO on an
adverse effect finding for the undertaking and notified the SHPO of its intent to use the standard
mitigation in the NNSS PA on December 11, 2024 (Julian to Reed). The SHPO concurred on December
30, 2024 (Reed to Julian). Therefore, NNSA/NFO has prepared mitigation pursuant to the standard
mitigation in the NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A for contributing elements to recorded, NRHP-eligible
districts.
 MITIGATION CONTENTS
The standard mitigation for contributing elements to recorded, NRHP-eligible districts consists of an
updated Architectural Resource Assessment (ARA) form to document current conditions (Appendix
D.I.A), including high-quality digital images (Appendix D.I.A.1) and plans, drawings, or annotated
sketch plans (Appendix D.I.A.2), and a brief letter report describing mitigation contents and summarizing
the building’s historic significance (Appendix D.I.B).
This mitigation report documents the completion of mitigation for the foundation of Building 25-
3113/3113A in accordance with NNSS PA Appendix D. An ARA Update form was prepared to satisfy
NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A (see Appendix A). It provides additional information about the historic
property, an individual NRHP evaluation, and interior description, engineering drawings, and current
photographs. This letter report fulfills NNSS PA Appendix D.I.B.

as documented in a series of recordings in 2000, 2001, 2021, and 2024. See the original site form and
subsequent ARA forms in Appendix A for a description of each.

 SIGNIFICANCE SUMMARY
In 1956, the Atomic Energy Commission (now the U.S. Department of Energy) and the National
Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (later the National Aeronautics and Space Administration)
selected an area in the southwestern portion of today’s Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) to test
reactors and engines developed by Los Alamos National Laboratory and other private contractors
under the Rover and NERVA nuclear thermal rocket propulsion programs. The overall objective was
to design an efficient and reliable system to boost both piloted and autonomous rockets to the moon,
Mars, and beyond. Construction began in 1957 on the specialized test stands, maintenance, assembly,
and disassembly buildings, transportation system, and infrastructure that became the NRDS.
Constructed in 1958, Building 25-3113 and the adjoining test pad served as the initial testing location
at the NRDS. Three Kiwi-A series tests were conducted using the original building configuration –
Kiwi A on July 1, 1959; Kiwi A’ (Kiwi A Prime) on July 8, 1960; and Kiwi A3 on October 19, 1960.
Shortly after these tests were conducted, construction began on the Building 25-3113 addition (25-
3113A) and a series of dewar foundations, valve control pits, and fill stations.
The building addition was called the Flow Control Room and it facilitated the flow of liquid
hydrogen, nitrogen, and helium from the adjacent dewars into the test reactor. Flow from the dewars
was operated remotely from the Reactor Control Point nearly two miles away, eliminating the need

Building 25-3113/3113A Mitigation                    Page 4 of 7                                  LR120424-1-MIT

for personnel within the complex while tests were active. Liquid hydrogen acted as the fuel that
perpetuated the tests, and liquid nitrogen was used to flush the reactor before and after each of the
tests (LANL 2018). Liquid helium and nitrogen were used to lower the reactor core temperature to a
safe level at the end of the tests.
In 1966, testing moved out of Test Cell A and into Test Cell C. Though there were attempts to reuse Test
Cell A over the decades, it was never fully operational again. Parts of the facility were moved to other
locations within the NRDS. Eventually, Building 25-3113/3113A was demolished in May 2005.
Building 25-3113/3113A was recorded as part of the larger Test Cell A facility in 2000 (26NY11260)
(Beck and Drollinger) and determined eligible for the NRHP under the Secretary of the Interior’s
Significance Criteria A and C (Baldrica, August 4, 2000). In anticipation of demolition, the DOE
completed mitigation consisting of a Historic American Engineering Record (Beck and Drollinger
2001). In 2023, the entirety of the NRDS was evaluated as a historic district and the remnant
foundations of Building 25-3113/3113A and adjoining test pad were determined to be a contributing
element to the NRHP-eligible district (Reno et al. 2023). NNSA/NFO provided full documentation
on its NRDS determinations and requested concurrence via letter on November 11, 2023 (Huck to
Reed). On February 2, 2024, the SHPO replied via email stating that the office was unable to
complete a timely review of the documentation (Reed to Gallo). NNSA/NFO finalized its
determinations as a result and archived the documentation at the Nuclear Testing Archive and on
OSTI.gov.
Individual Significance Evaluation
In its present state, Building 25-3113/3113A is not recommended as individually eligible for the NRHP.
While it has significance under Criterion A for its association with Project Rover, an important series of
nuclear tests related to rocket propulsion, it can no longer convey this significance due to a lack of integrity.
Because the building has been demolished, the resource retains integrity of location, but no longer has the
integrity of design, materials, or workmanship. Additionally, it has lost some of integrity of setting,
association, and feeling since many of the other buildings and structures within the facility have also been
demolished.
It was not associated with the life of a particular significant person, so it is not considered significant under
Criterion B. At one time the building was determined eligible under Criterion C for its unique design and
construction. However, since it was demolished, it lacks the integrity required to convey this significance
and is no longer significant under Criterion C. The little physical material of the resources that remains is
very unlikely to provide additional information about human history; therefore, it is not recommended
eligible for listing under Criterion D.

20. Site Description:

 Test Cell A was one of seven separate but interconnected complexes associated with
 the NRDS in Area 25 of the Nevada Test Site. The mission of the NRDS was to
 develop and test nuclear reactors and engines for rockets as part of the Rover Program.
 Test Cell A, the first facility at the NRDS for the testing of the reactors and engine
 systems, was part of the Rover program, a Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory (now Los
 Alamos National Laboratory) government research project.

 The Test Cell A facility is surrounded by a chain link fence and contains 18.4 ac (7.4
 ha). Entry was through three main gates with multiple ancillary gates built into the
 fencing system. The buildings and structures within the complex are the equipment test
 laboratory, the compressor station, the tank farm building, the pumping station, the
 distribution building, the tunnel headhouse, the tunnel, three horizontal dewars, an open
 corrugated- metal-clad steel monument frame structure, a spherical dewar with a
 120,000 gallon capacity for liquid hydrogen, the LH2 fill station, the test cell, a large
 concrete testing pad, the moveable shed, the shed drive house, a hemispherical bunker,
 a 100,000 gallon water tank elevated on a tower, the tank farm (an area that contained
 banks of horizontal fuel tanks, now removed. Also present are a vaporizer shed a

 6. Narrative Eligibility Justification
Provide a detailed explanation of the resource’s eligibility for the National Register, including supporting historic information, methods
for evaluation under the four criteria, discussion of the seven aspects of integrity, and conclusions about eligibility.

 In 2000 as part of a historical evaluation for planned decontamination and decommissioning activities, the Test Cell
 building, including the test pad (25-3113/3113a, B2443) was recorded as part of the Test Cell A facility
 (26NY11260). (Beck et al. 2000). During this original recording, the facility was neither delineated nor identified in
 any detail; rather, the focus was on recording the test cell. In consultation with the SHPO, the Department of Energy
 (DOE) determined Test Cell A was eligible for the NRHP under the Secretary of the Interior’s Significance Criteria A
 and C (Baldrica 2000). The DOE planned to demolish the facility, and mitigation consisting of a Historic American
 Engineering Record of the building was completed (NPS 2021).
 For the purposes of the present survey, resources within the boundary of the Nuclear Rocket Development Station
 (NRDS) Historic District were evaluated to determine if they contribute to the significance of the district. The NRDS
 Historic District is recommended eligible for listing to the NRHP under all four criteria as the testing center for the
 Rover/NERVA (Nuclear Engine for Rocket Vehicle Application) program from 1956 to 1973.
 At present, all that remains of the 12,180 sq ft test building and test stand (Building 25-3113/3113a) is the concrete
 slab of the test stand with the adjacent building foundation.
 When constructed in 1958, Building 25-3113/3113A was the only test stand in the United States designed to conduct
 hot tests of nuclear reactors for rocket propulsion. These tests included the Kiwi and NRX series of test reactors,
 none of which were intended for actual flight use (Figures KIWI A-PRIME, NRX-A2 T-6). For most of this period, the
 reactors were assembled at R-MAD and transported to Test Cell A by rail for testing, and then returned there for
 dismantling and what was called post-mortem analysis. Test Cell A remained active until 1966, with removal
 completed in 2005. Due to demolition, the resource has lost sufficient integrity to convey individual significance
 under Criteria A and C. A change in eligibility status to not individually eligible is recommended.
 As part of this current condition recording, the remaining concrete foundation and test pad were recorded as the
 primary resource. Except for three standing buildings recorded on individual ARA forms, other identified standing
 structures within the facility were recorded as accessory resources (ARs).
 Although the building has been removed, the foundation, test pad, and accessory resources serve as a visible
 reminder of the layout of the Test Cell A facility during the NRDS period of significance. Importantly for the integrity
 of location, feeling, and association, the ground zero point where the reactors were secured with their rail car to the
 test slab for the hot tests is still intact along with the surrounding slab. The Test Cell A building foundation and test
 pad (the primary resource) is a contributing element of the NRDS Historic District.

 7. Narrative Architectural Description
Provide a detailed description of the resource, including all character defining features, potential construction methods,
potential alterations (both historic and non-historic), and any accessory resources.

 For a lengthy historical background and detailed descriptions of 25-3113/3113A when it was still standing, see Beck
 et al. (2000) and Beck and Drollinger (2021). The building was razed after February 2005 following mitigation for
 adverse effects, which supplemented the written description in the DRI Short Report with completion of an IMACS
 form, a SHPO Historic Resources Information Form (now remodeled as the Architectural Resource Assessment
 form), and HAER documentation (HAER NV-33). Aside from the differing formats, the contents of the four
 documents are practically identical. As shown in the photos, nothing remains of the Test Cell A Building and its
 attached dependencies other than an extensive concrete foundation and slab.
 It appears that the notion that the facility was “mitigated” was interpreted somewhat broadly since several buildings
 or structures were removed without formal mitigation other than some exterior photographs (see NPS 2001). These
 missing resources include the Spherical LH2 Dewar, Vaporizer Shed, 50,000 gallon “Ground Reservoir” water tank,
 Distribution Building (25-3109), Moveable Shed (25-3130), Shed E-28576, Shed 29830, and the Access Tunnel with
 its entry building (25-3114).
 The test stand concrete slab with adjacent building foundation are within the perimeter fence and serve as the
 primary resource for the attached resource map. The field crew did not attempt to cross the perimeter fence line. To
 do so would have required special precautions due to radioactive contamination. The recording of resources within
 the perimeter fence was limited to describing standing structures from the fence line. These resources are ARs 1, 2,
 and 4-8. The fence line is AR3. Three standing buildings are recorded on separate ARA forms.
 ACCESSORY RESOURCES (25-3113)
 AR3 PERIMETER FENCE: (AR3)
 The chain link perimeter security fence is 8 feet tall, topped with barbed wire. Posts are the galvanized steel H-posts
 typical of early construction at the NRDS. Two double gates at the southwest corner are 80 feet apart. A third double
 gate is at the south end of the complex. Double gates are also located where the railroad entered the complex.
 There are several emergency personnel exits spaced around the fence line. There is also a permanent instrument

 power outlets.

 the earth cover. This part of the building was still largely intact when photographed in 2000. Since that time, it has
 failed, allowing some of the cover to erode off, exposing the concrete and steel reinforcement bands of the formerly
 buried building. Two large steel doors, which are open, provide access. An entry ramp wide enough for vehicles runs
 down from the ground surface to the doorway. The floor of the bunker is recessed, somewhat below ground level.

 OUTSIDE OF THE SECURITY-FENCED AREA (ARs 9-18)
 AR9 CRYOGENIC PIPELINE: This pipeline runs downslope from the edge of Road F from a point south of the liquid
 hydrogen fill station (25-3134) of which only foundations remain. This station was attached to the principal resource.
 Despite this proximity, the AR9 Pipeline could not have been attached to that facility without closing off Road F. It is
 possible that a portable connecting line was run across the road since the pipeline is aimed directly at the center of a
 double gate in the Security Fence. The pipeline is a mystery since it both begins and ends without any special
 fixtures, foundations or in fact, anything at all to suggest how it was used. No information was found regarding it in
 the limited literature search for this project. The 4-inch diameter pipeline is made of an alloy that weathers to an
 appearance like weathered bronze. There is no doubt that this was used for transporting cryogenic gases since it
 rests on a series of roller fittings on brackets which raises the line about a foot above the ground. This would have
 allowed for the expansion and contraction of the line resulting from extreme temperature fluctuations. Its design is
 nearly identical to the cryogenic pipeline at the nearby Equipment Test Laboratory (ETL 25-3124, B19001). The
 raised platform (AR11) may be related to this pipeline (see discussion below).

 AR10 STORMWATER DRAINAGE SYSTEM: The complex is on an active alluvial fan surface. It is well-protected
 from floodwaters resulting from intermittent storms by dikes and adjacent ditches that intercept the water and direct it
 around either the northwest or southeast edges of the facility. When the ETL was constructed in 1963 the dike and
 ditch along the west side of the complex was routed in a loop farther to the west and then back eastward to
 reconnect with the original channel just north of Road F. As can be seen in the complex aerial photo, a remnant of
 the original dike and ditch survives between the ETL and this junction at Road F. The dike varies in height above the
 surrounding surface from three to five feet. The top is wide enough for a vehicle to drive upon it, about 10 feet. Ditch
 width is highly variable due to bank alterations during storm episodes. Sediments deposited by floodwaters have
 obscured the railroad grade just east of the switch providing access to the Test Cell A Test Slab.
 AR11 RAISED PLATFORM: A 10 x 20-foot level platform has been bladed onto the top of a mound of earth that
 stands about 15 feet tall. An access ramp has been bladed up the northeast side of the pile. It is probable that this
 platform is related in some way to Pipeline AR9 since the platform and ramp are precisely aligned on that pipeline
 rather than aiming toward the testing ground zero, as would have been the case if this platform was used for mobile
 test monitoring equipment. This alignment is apparent in photo 1915, taken from the top of the platform and looking
 down the ramp toward the cryogenic pipeline.
 AR12 NON-PROCESS SEPTIC SYSTEM: This consists of a septic tank with two iron “SEPTIC TANK” manholes, a
 “SEWER” manhole about 30 feet to the south, and a scatter of red clay pipe fragments. The location of the leach
 field is not known. It could be just downslope to the south or farther away on the south side of F Street. Due to its
 position at the west edge of the facility, it likely served the nearby ETL. The main sewage facility for the main
 complex is south of Road F and just downslope from Trailer Park AR15. This facility treated processed sewage. It
 has been remediated as a hazardous radioactive waste deposit. It is thus now a completely disturbed area which
 was not formally recorded.
 AR13 DOSAGE MEASUREMENT AERIAL TROLLEY: A series of nine steel pipe poles in a line running southeast
 from near the Test Slab to the edge of Road F. Each pole has an elaborate angle brace with a top pully for the
 haulage cable and with an open bottom for passage of the instrument package. The brackets are on various heights
 on the poles so the line can run level. The present northwestern end pole has a cross-arm about three feet below its
 bracket. The original end pole was on the slab and is no longer present. It is the only pole missing from the line.
 The southeast end pole has a pipe brace to the ground in the direction of the line and has a cantilevered platform for
 the end pullies and motor, all of which are present (photo 1864). Instead of a side bracket, the pole has a solid
 square frame at the top of the pole for parking the instrument package. An electrical box containing a switch for
 moving the package “IN” or “OUT” of the enclosure is attached to one of the angle braces for the motor platform. The
 activation toggle switch for setting the package in motion is located on the outside of the box.
 The southeast terminus of the trolley is a heavy braced steel frame set in concrete (photo 1864). It was attached to
 the top motor mount on the terminal trolley post by two 1/8” diameter cables, which have broken.
 In addition to the moveable trolley, a static 3/16” diameter solid steel cable (which is still partly attached to the poles)
 served to suspend a bundle of electrical and communication wires the full length of the system.
 The trolley is first depicted on the attached 1965 plan of the facility (Koogle and Pouls, page 11), suggesting it was
 likely constructed during the early 1960s upgrade.
 AR14 JATO ROCKET SYSTEM: A small concrete pad raised on a low earth mound supported two steel Jet Assisted
 Takeoff (JATO) rocket arrays, each with a capacity of 10 rockets. One of the arrays is still in place. The other is

 in the tubes so their exhaust plume would rise in the sky, providing a visual marker of the movement of the exhaust

 The highly visible JATO exhaust plume was extremely useful since the hydrogen exhaust from the reactor was
 practically invisible. The rockets were fired individually as needed. The JATO installation is located in a position
 somewhat to the side of the prevailing westerly course of test exhaust plumes. The JATO ignition canisters are tilted
 slightly away from the Test Cell.

 6. Narrative Eligibility Justification
Provide a detailed explanation of the resource’s eligibility for the National Register, including supporting historic information,
methods for evaluation under the four criteria, discussion of the seven aspects of integrity, and conclusions about eligibility.

In 1956, the Atomic Energy Commission (now the DOE) and the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics
(later the National Aeronautics and Space Administration) selected an area in the southwestern portion of the
land known today as the NNSS to test reactors and engines developed by Los Alamos National Laboratory and
other private contractors under the Rover and NERVA nuclear thermal rocket propulsion programs. The overall
objective was to design an efficient and reliable system to boost both piloted and autonomous rockets to the
moon, Mars, and beyond. Construction began in 1957 on the specialized test stands, maintenance, assembly,
and disassembly buildings, transportation system, and infrastructure that became the NRDS.
Constructed in 1958, Building 25-3113 and the adjoining test pad served as the initial testing location at the
NRDS. Three Kiwi-A series tests were conducted using the original building configuration – Kiwi A on July 1,
1959; Kiwi A’ (Kiwi A Prime) on July 8, 1960; and Kiwi A3 on October 19, 1960. Shortly after these tests were
conducted, construction began on the Building 25-3113 addition (25-3113A) and a series of dewar foundations,
valve control pits, and fill stations.
The building addition was called the Flow Control Room and it facilitated the flow of liquid hydrogen, nitrogen,
and helium from the adjacent dewars into the test reactor. Flow from the dewars was operated remotely from the
Reactor Control Point nearly two miles away, eliminating the need for personnel within the complex while tests
 Nevada SHPO – ARA Form Page 1

were active. Liquid hydrogen acted as the fuel that perpetuated the tests, and liquid nitrogen was used to flush
the reactor before and after each of the tests (LANL 2018). Liquid helium and nitrogen were used to lower the
reactor core temperature to a safe level at the end of the tests.
In 1966, testing moved out of Test Cell A and into Test Cell C. Though there were attempts to reuse Test Cell A over
the decades, it was never fully operational again. Parts of the facility were moved to other locations within the NRDS.
Eventually, Building 25-3113/3113A was demolished in May 2005.
Building 25-3113/3113A was recorded as part of the larger Test Cell A facility in 2000 (26NY11260) (Beck and
Drollinger) and determined eligible for the NRHP under the Secretary of the Interior’s Significance Criteria A and
C (Baldrica, August 4, 2000). In anticipation of demolition, the DOE completed mitigation consisting of a Historic
American Engineering Record (Beck and Drollinger 2001). In 2023, the entirety of the NRDS was evaluated as a
historic district and the remnant foundations of Building 25-3113/3113A and adjoining test pad were determined
to be a contributing element to the NRHP-eligible district (Reno et al. 2023). NNSA/NFO provided full
documentation on its NRDS determinations and requested concurrence via letter on November 11, 2023 (Huck
to Reed). On February 2, 2024, the SHPO replied via email stating that the office was unable to complete a
timely review of the documentation (Reed to Gallo). NNSA/NFO finalized its determinations as a result and
archived the documentation at the Nuclear Testing Archive and on OSTI.gov.
Building 25-3113/3113A is a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District. It is recommended not individually
eligible for listing in the NRHP in its current condition.
Criterion A. To be individually eligible for the National Register under Criterion A, properties must be associated with
events or a series of events that have made a significant contribution to broad patterns of our history. The building
was directly and importantly associated with Project Rover, an important series of nuclear tests related to rocket
propulsion. However, in its present state, Building 25-3113/3113A can no longer convey this significance due to a lack of
integrity. Because the building has been demolished, the resource retains integrity of location, but no longer has the
integrity of design, materials, or workmanship. Additionally, it has lost some of integrity of setting, association, and
feeling since many of the other buildings and structures within the facility have been also demolished.
Therefore, Building 25-3113/3113A is no longer recommended eligible for listing under Criterion A.
Criterion B. Properties may be eligible for the National Register if they are associated with the lives of persons
significant in our past. Although President Kennedy made a visit to the Test Cell A facility in 1962 it is clear that his
visit was not one of his significant achievements during his life. It is certain that prominent scientists from Los Alamos
National Laboratory were involved with Project Rover, however, research did not indicate that this was the location in
which they did their most significant work. Even if it were, the resource would no longer be able to convey its
association with significant individuals as a only a remnant foundation. Therefore, it does not appear to be significant
under Criterion B.
Criterion C. Properties may be eligible under Criterion C if they embody the distinctive characteristics of a type,
period, or method of construction, or that represents the work of a master, or that possesses high artistic values.
Properties may also be eligible under this criterion if they represent a significant and distinguishable entity whose
components may lack individual distinction. At one time Test Cell A was determined eligible under Criterion C for its
unique construction style that represents functionality and protection of the test cell during reactor tests. However,
since Building 25-3113/3113A was demolished in 2005 and all that remains is the concrete foundation, it can no
longer convey significance under Criterion C.
Criterion D. Properties significant under Criterion D must have the potential to yield further information about human
history that can only be answered by the actual physical material of the resources. There are two requirements for a
resource to be found significant under Criterion D: the property must have or have had information to contribute to our
understanding of human history or prehistory, and the information must be considered important. All that remains of
the resource are concrete foundations; therefore, little physical material remains to provide additional data. The
foundations and the former building have also been subject to research on their role within the NRDS complex and it
is unlikely that additional research would yield further information. Building 25-3113/3113A does not appear to be
significant under Criterion D.
Integrity
The resource retains integrity of location, but no longer has integrity of design, materials, or workmanship.
Additionally, it has lost some of integrity of setting, association, and feeling due to the fact that many of the other
buildings and structures within the facility have been demolished.

 7. Narrative Architectural Description

 Building 25-3113/3113A was demolished in 2005. Currently, all that remains of Building 25-3113/3113A and the
 adjoining test pad are the concrete foundations (see Figures 1 - 4). Building 25-3113 and the test pad were built in
 1958; the part of the building known as 3113A was added in 1960. The remnant foundations are all of varying
 heights and in varying stages of deterioration. Signs are posted around and on top of the foundations that read
 “CAUTION UNDERGROUND RADIOACTIVE MATERIAL”.
 Previous recordings of the Test Cell A building grouped the entire facility into one trinomial (26NY11260) (Beck and
 Drollinger 2000). Sparse descriptions are given of the other resources with the emphasis on the description of the
 test cell itself, Building 25-3113/3113A, which had been placed on the demolition list. Demolition occurred in May
 2005; see NNSS 2009 for a link to the demolition video. A subsequent visit in 2021 to document the NRDS Historic
 District identified several resources within the Test Cell A facility that had been previously demolished (Reno et al.
 2023). The 2021 documentation efforts took place outside the fence line, and, at the time, it was unknown if any
 remnants of the resources remained. In 2024, access was granted within the fence line to document the full extent
 of the remaining concrete pads for 25-3113/3113A and the test stand. These efforts confirmed the presence of four
 previously unrecorded accessory resources.
 ACCESSORY RESOURCES:
 AR19 LIQUID HYDROGEN DEWAR PAD: This concrete pad measures approximately 40 ft. × 25 ft. It once held a
 horizontal dewar filled with liquid hydrogen, which was used as propellant during testing. Based on available
 historical imagery, it was added to the Test Cell A compound sometime after December 1961 and it is present on
 engineering drawings and aerials from April 1965. The dewar was removed sometime prior to June 2000 and the
 concrete pad is all that remains.
 AR20 VALVE PIT #1: Excavation for Valve Pit #1 began in February 1961 based on historical imagery. It is
 approximately 18 ft. 2 in. × 11 ft. at the surface and approximately 9 ft. deep. It contains two deluge pits and a
 series of Schedule 40 pipe sleeves. Previous demolition efforts did not alter the surface appearance of this
 accessory resource, and it appears to be in the same or similar condition as during the period of significance.
 AR21 DEWAR PAD: Construction for the dewar foundations began in February 1961. Initial construction consisted
 of heavy metal framing atop four concrete pylons which supported two large horizontal dewars, identified as dewars
 A and B in engineering drawings, which were installed in May 1961. Additional metal framing encased the
 horizontal dewars, which were connected to various pipes leading into 25-3113/3113A. The metal framing
 underneath the dewars is identified in engineering drawings as a weighing structure, presumably to gauge how full
 the dewars were; the metal framing that encased the dewars is simply identified as an operating platform. By 1963,
 a 120,000-gallon spherical dewar, dewar C, was added just to the west of dewars A and B. Sometime between
 October 1963, when dewar C was added, and July 1966, when the facility was deactivated and testing moved to
 Test Cell C, concrete was poured around the existing dewar foundations to create a concrete pad that
 encompassed all three dewars. Remnants of the original dewar foundations are still visible within the pad, which
 can be seen in a photo on page 28. Dewars A and B were moved to Engine Test Stand 1 (ETS-1) sometime after
 1966 and dewar C remained until demolition in 2005. The pad is all that remains.
 AR22 TRAILER PARKING AREA WITH FILL STATIONS: Immediately south of AR21 is the former trailer parking
 area, which was chip sealed during the period of significance but is now a dirt lot. At the northern end of the lot
 where it meets AR21, there were two fill stations. Construction began in 1961 for fill station #1 (25-3134), which
 was a three-sided concrete lean-to with a concrete roof that opened to the south. It measured 15 ft. wide × 24 ft.
 long and was 19 ft. tall at the opening and sloped down to 16 ft. tall on the back wall. The back wall, or north wall,
 had ten 4 in. × 4 in. square openings near the ceiling for pipes. A cement stairwell with eight steps and handrails
 was on the west side of the fill station, connecting it to AR21. Various piping inside the fill station facilitated the
 unloading of liquid hydrogen from tankers into the dewars. Immediately east of the 25-3134 fill station was another
 smaller fill station (building designation unknown). Of similar design, fill station #2 was slightly smaller but fulfilled a
 similar function. The date of construction is not known, but likely coincided with the addition of dewar C, and it is
 present in imagery from the NERVA A3 test in April 1965. To the west of fill station #1 was an another surface tank.
 There is no documentation of its construction or purpose; however, it was also likely built with the addition of dewar
 C. One undated image of it (see page 30) shows that it was a horizontal tank with an overflow or spill catchment
 basin at the southern end of the tank. The concrete foundations are present in aerials from 1983, indicating the
 tank had been removed by that time, likely when dewars A and B were removed.
