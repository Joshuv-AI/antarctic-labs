Application of gamified interventions in children with type 1 diabetes: a scoping review
Authors: Yinghui Guan, Jianhui Xie, Fangyuan Wang, Yaqi Ouyang, Jiaqi Tian, Qiong Wang, Huicong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 1 diabetes poses substantial self-management challenges for children. Gamified interventions are a promising strategy to support this population, yet evidence on their design, implementation, and reported outcomes remains scattered. This scoping review aims to systematically map the international evidence on gamified interventions for children with T1DM, focusing on their characteristics, delivery, and outcomes.MethodsFollowing the Arksey and O’Malley framework, this scoping review systematically searched six databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Web of Science) from January 1, 2010, to January 25, 2026. We also examined reference lists and performed citation tracking.ResultsTwenty-three of 762 retrieved articles were included. Interventions were primarily delivered via mobile applications (52%) and websites (17%). The most common gamification elements were goal setting, challenges, and fun (each 96%); social features were less frequent (35%). Most studies (70%) lacked an explicit theoretical framework, and intervention durations varied widely. The interventions demonstrated benefits for glycemic control, self-management, knowledge, and psychological distress, but inconsistent effects on quality of life. They were generally feasible, usable, and acceptable.ConclusionGamified interventions represent a promising approach to T1DM management in children, aligning well with their developmental needs. However, current studies often lack a theoretical foundation and evidence of sustained benefits. Future work should prioritize theory-driven design and rigorous long-term evaluation.Protocol RegistrationThis review protocol is registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) and accessible via the following link: 10.17605/OSF.IO/MN6AE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07142-5.Keywords: Gamified interventions, Type 1 diabetes, Child, Scoping review

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is a metabolic disorder characterized by chronic hyperglycemia resulting from absolute insulin deficiency due to damage to pancreatic beta cells caused by the combined effects of environmental and genetic factors [1]. It ranks among the most common endocrine metabolic diseases in childhood, with a globally rising incidence trend [1]. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas 2025 reveals that approximately 1.81 million individuals aged 20 years and younger worldwide have T1DM, with roughly 219,000 new pediatric diagnoses annually [2]. T1DM has emerged as a significant global public health issue affecting children’s health, profoundly impacting their physical, psychological, and social functioning.

Currently, T1DM requires lifelong management, including insulin replacement therapy and comprehensive self-management support. After diagnosis, children need regular physical activity, appropriate dietary management, systematic diabetes education, strict blood glucose monitoring, standardized insulin injections, and adjunctive medication when needed [3, 4].

Poor disease management can lead to acute and chronic complications such as diabetic ketoacidosis, cardiovascular disease, and renal impairment [5, 6]. Research indicates that T1DM children generally exhibit low self-management adherence, with treatment compliance potentially declining significantly during adolescence. This low adherence stems not only from the complexity and intrusiveness of disease management but also from the child’s stage of cognitive development, psychosocial factors, and insufficient awareness of long-term health risks [7, 8]. Traditional diabetes management strategies often rely on education, medication, and regular monitoring. However, these approaches are often unidirectional, struggling to sustainably motivate children and translate knowledge into conscious behaviors in daily contexts, resulting in limited effectiveness in improving adherence and self-management capabilities [9]. Consequently, exploring innovative, engaging intervention strategies tailored to children’s psychological development to enhance their self-management motivation, knowledge, and skills has become an urgent need in pediatric diabetes care.

Against this backdrop, gamification—an innovative behavioral intervention strategy—is gaining increasing attention for its application in health promotion. Gamification refers to the systematic integration of game design elements such as points, leaderboards, progress bars, and badges into non-game contexts. Its aim is to enhance user engagement, boost intrinsic motivation, and foster the formation and maintenance of positive health behaviors [10, 11]. Existing research indicates that gamified interventions demonstrate positive effects in health behavior promotion, rehabilitation training, and chronic disease management, effectively boosting participants’engagement motivation and behavioral adherence [12–14]. In the field of diabetes, preliminary research has confirmed the promising potential of gamification applications. Studies indicate that such interventions not only convey disease-related knowledge in a more accessible and engaging manner, promoting meaningful learning about diabetes management among pediatric patients, but also effectively enhance their self-care skills, self-efficacy, and intrinsic motivation. This supports the establishment of long-term self-management behaviors [15].

Despite the growing body of research on gamified interventions among children with T1DM, the existing literature remains fragmented. Intervention formats vary widely, outcome measures and theoretical frameworks differ, and research quality and effectiveness assessments show inconsistencies. However, no comprehensive systematic review addressing this specific topic has been identified. Given the heterogeneity in research designs and measurement methods across studies, a scoping review represents a suitable knowledge synthesis method for addressing large-scale, diverse, and complex heterogeneous topics. For these reasons, a scoping review may be a preferable choice over a systematic review, as it can rapidly describe research progress in a field, demonstrate the scope, depth, breadth, and gaps in research, and provide more information for future studies [16]. Therefore, this study aims to systematically synthesize and describe current gamified intervention research for children with type 1 diabetes through a scoping review. Specific objectives include: (1) mapping overall trends and geographic distribution of research in this field; (2) identifying and categorizing core design elements, application platforms, and theoretical frameworks of gamified interventions; (3) Summarize key outcome measures and their measurement tools; (4) Identify existing research gaps based on findings and provide evidence-based recommendations for designing, implementing, and translating high-quality future interventions. The review’s findings are expected to offer valuable insights for pediatric diabetes care providers, health intervention designers, and researchers, thereby advancing the development of more scientifically sound, effective, and child-friendly T1DM management support tools.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted according to the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [17]. The process comprised five key stages: (1) defining the research question; (2) identifying relevant studies; (3) study selection; (4) data extraction; (5) synthesizing, summarizing, and reporting results. The scope review was conducted according to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines [18], utilizing the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses—ScR (PRISMA-ScR) 2020 framework to guide the research process [19].

This review protocol is registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) and accessible via the following link: 10.17605/OSF.IO/MN6AE.

Defining research questions

This review primarily examines existing information regarding the development or application of gamified interventions in children with T1DM. Therefore, research questions were purposefully formulated to encompass the breadth and nature of the existing literature. The research questions are as follows: ① How are gamified intervention programs designed for children with T1DM? This includes gamification mechanisms, intervention duration, intervention tools, theoretical frameworks, etc. ② What are the effects of gamified interventions when applied to children with T1DM?

Search strategy

GYH and WFY collaboratively developed and conducted the literature search strategy in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. Comprehensive searches were performed in PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and relevant references were manually searched. The search period spanned January 1, 2010, to January 25, 2026, and the search was restricted to studies published in English because of feasibility considerations and limited resources for translation and multilingual screening. The search strategy combined MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms and keywords. Search terms included “Gamification,” “serious game*,” “mobile game*,” “active video game*,” “exergaming,” “Diabetes Mellitus, Type 1,” “Type 1 Diabetes,” “IDDM,” “Child,” “Pediatrics,” and “Adolescent.” To supplement the electronic searches and reduce potential publication bias, additional manual searches were conducted as follows: (1) backward citation tracking—the reference lists of all included studies as well as highly cited systematic or scoping reviews in the field published within the last five years were manually screened; (2) forward citation tracking—the “Cited Reference Search” function of Web of Science was used for 10 core articles highly relevant to this review to identify later-published studies. A detailed retrieval strategy is provided in Multimedia Appendix 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: ① Study subjects diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, aged ≤ 19 years. This age range aligns with the WHO definition of children and adolescents and captures the developmental period during which self-management behaviors are typically established; ② Research content involving the development of gamified intervention protocols, as well as the creation and application of required gaming software. This ensures that the review focuses on interventions where gamification is a core component rather than an incidental feature; ③ Study types encompassing randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, qualitative research, mixed-methods research, and case report designs. To ensure the broadest possible overview of existing evidence on this topic, research protocols were also included. These designs were included to capture the full breadth of available evidence, from efficacy to feasibility and user experience.

Exclusion criteria: ① Original articles unavailable, review studies, conference abstracts, duplicate publications, or animal studies; ② Studies with incomplete data and unsuccessful attempts to contact authors; ③ Non-English language literature;

Study selection and data extraction

All relevant studies were imported into EndNote X9 (reference management software; Clarivate Analytics) to remove duplicates. Two researchers (WFY, OYYQ) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records to assess compliance with predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, they independently performed full-text reviews of all articles that passed the initial screening to confirm each study’s eligibility. During title and abstract screening, inter-rater reliability between the two reviewers (WFY and OYYQ) was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic (k = 0.86, 95% CI:0.79–0.93), indicating almost perfect agreement. In total, 12 disagreements (4.2% of screened records) occurred during the full-text review, which were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (TJQ). A data extraction form was designed based on the research questions to record key information from included articles. Two reviewers pilot-tested the form on the first five articles and refined it further after discussion. Two authors (GYH, XJH) independently extracted data from all included studies and entered it into a pre-designed data extraction form. Information collected included: authors, publication year, country, study design, participant age, sample size, intervention, intervention duration, intervention tool, and key findings. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third researcher (XL).

Results

Literature screening results

A total of 762 articles were identified through literature searches. After removing 345 duplicates, an additional 220 articles were excluded during title and abstract screening for non-compliance with inclusion criteria. The remaining 197 articles underwent full-text review, with 174 subsequently excluded due to mismatched study populations, inappropriate interventions, non-compliant study designs, or unavailability of full-text documents. Ultimately, 23 studies were included for data synthesis. Characteristics of gamified intervention programs

To clarify the implementation approach of gamified interventions and address Research Questions 1 and 2, this section outlines the core components of the gamified intervention measures incorporated into the study. Detailed descriptions of the interventions are provided in Multimedia Appendix 2.

Gamification elements in gamified intervention programs

This study extracted data based on the gamification elements proposed by Cugelman [13]: ① Goal Setting. Twenty-two studies [20–23, 25–42] (96%) established clear, quantifiable short- or long-term task goals for pediatric patients, focusing on blood glucose monitoring, physical activity, and dietary management. ② Challenge Overcoming. Twenty-two studies [20–29, 31–42] (96%) incorporated task ladders or progressive levels of difficulty for children with T1DM, stimulating competitive drive. ③ Experiential Feedback. Seventeen studies [20–27, 29, 32, 34–39, 41] (74%) enhanced audiovisual engagement and behavioral feedback loops through motion-sensing games, virtual reality scenarios, narrative-driven gameplay, and real-time display of activity data, training performance, and progress trends on digital platforms. ④ Reinforcement through rewards and penalties. Thirteen studies [23, 25–27, 29, 32–35, 37–40] (57%) utilized point accumulation, virtual achievement badges, character customization unlocks, and redeemable physical rewards to bolster children’s commitment to persistence. ⑤ Progress monitoring. Eleven studies [23, 27, 28, 32–34, 36, 38–40, 42] (48%) employed electronic health logs, dynamic data charts, personalized progress dashboards, and periodic email/push notifications to enable children with T1DM to promptly understand their goal attainment status. ⑥ Social connection. Eight studies [23, 27, 32, 37–39, 42] (35%) fostered social bonds by involving family and friends in gameplay or enabling peer encouragement and discussion via social platforms. ⑦ Engagement. Twenty-two studies [20–36, 38–42] (96%) incorporated the principle of fun. This was manifested through narrative design, character personalization, interactive entertainment formats, and scenario simulations to enhance the gaming experience and enjoyment for children with T1DM, thereby encouraging sustained engagement.

Application platforms for gamified intervention programs

The application platforms for gamified interventions in children with T1DM include websites, mobile applications, virtual reality devices, and CDs. Among the 23 included studies, 4 studies [22, 28, 29, 34] (17%) utilized websites; 12 studies [20, 23, 26, 27, 31–33, 40, 42] (52%) employed mobile applications; 3 studies [36, 38, 39] (13%) utilized virtual reality devices; 2 studies [21, 25] (9%) used CDs; and 2 studies [24, 30] adopted traditional therapeutic games without relying on digital platforms for intervention delivery.

Theoretical framework and guidelines for gamified intervention programs

Among the 23 included studies, seven (30%) designed gamified interventions based on theoretical frameworks and guidelines. Two studies developed gamified interventions according to the International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes (ISPAD) 2022 Physical Activity Guidelines for Children and Adolescents with Diabetes [36] and the joint ISPAD-American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines [37], respectively. The remaining five studies developed gamified interventions based on distinct behavioral science or disease management theoretical models: the Seven-Domain Self-Management Framework proposed by the Chinese Diabetes Society [38], Social Cognitive Theory [39], Disease Adjustment Model from an Integrative Perspective (DAMIP) [41], the Diabetes Resilience Model [32], and the Information-Motivation-Behavioral Skills Model [28].

Duration of gamified intervention programs

Among these 23 studies, intervention duration exhibited significant heterogeneity, ranging from single 30-minute sessions to 12-month programs. They can be categorized into four types: One study [21] (9%) employed ultra-short interventions (≤ 1 week), focusing on rapid science communication about specific therapeutic procedures to reduce children’s unfamiliarity and distress; Eight studies [29, 31–33, 38–42] (35%) employed short-term interventions (2–8 weeks), typically involving single sessions of 20–60 min with clearly defined weekly frequencies, serving as the primary format for knowledge transfer and initial behavioral guidance; Seven studies [20, 22, 25, 28, 34–37] (30%) employed medium-term interventions (9 weeks–6 months), primarily for long-term behavior shaping and clinical indicator improvement; Five studies [21, 24, 27, 30, 35] (22%) specified only the total intervention period without detailing session duration; Only one study [23] (9%) involved long-term intervention (≥ 12 months), focusing on sustained behavioral reinforcement; Additionally, one study [26] (9%) did not fully report the total intervention period or frequency.

Outcome measures and evaluation tools for gamified interventions

Among the 23 studies included in this research, outcome measures primarily encompassed the following six domains: glycemic control, self-management behaviors, disease-related knowledge, psychosocial functioning, quality of life, and the feasibility, usability, and acceptability of the intervention measures.

Regarding glycemic control, eight studies [20, 23, 25, 28, 34, 36, 39, 42] used glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) as the primary indicator to assess long-term glycemic control during the intervention period. Additionally, four studies [31, 36, 39, 40] employed continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) technology to collect metrics including average blood glucose levels, percentage time within the normal range (70–180 mg/dL), percentage time with hyperglycemia (> 180 mg/dL), and percentage time with hypoglycemia (< 70 mg/dL), thereby comprehensively evaluating glucose variability.

Regarding self-management behaviors, one study [38] employed the Self-Management Scale for Type 1 Diabetes Children (SMS-T1DC); one study [22] utilized the Diabetes Self-Management Profile (DSMP); and two studies [20, 40] adopted the Self-Care Inventory (SCI). One study [25] employed a researcher-developed 20-item Diabetes Diet and Exercise Program Adherence Questionnaire, focusing on assessing adherence to dietary and exercise management. Two additional studies [23, 24] utilized behavioral indicators related to glycemic control, such as daily blood glucose monitoring frequency, lancet replacement frequency, and number of insulin self-adjustments, to measure self-management behaviors.

Regarding disease-related knowledge, one study [22] used the Pediatric Carbohydrate Knowledge Questionnaire (PCQ) to assess children’s mastery of carbohydrate-related knowledge; two studies [20, 21] employed researcher-developed diabetes knowledge questionnaires for evaluation.

Regarding psychosocial functioning, one study [41] used the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIP-Q) and the Social Skills Questionnaire (CHASO) to assess illness perception and social skills, respectively; another study [42] employed the Hacettepe Psychological Adaptation Scale (HPAS) to evaluate psychological adaptation; one study [30] assessed anxiety and fear related to insulin injections using the Children’s State Anxiety Scale and the Children’s Fear Scale; another study [21] evaluated behavioral distress during insulin administration using the Observational Scale of Behavioral Distress-Revised (OSBD-R).

Regarding quality of life, four studies [28, 34, 36, 42] employed the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory™ 3.0 Diabetes Module (PedsQL 3.0); two studies [20, 23] used the Diabetes Quality of Life for Youth (DQOLY) questionnaire; and one study [40] utilized the KIDSCREEN-27 questionnaire.

Regarding feasibility, usability, and acceptability, five studies [27, 32, 38, 39] assessed feasibility through metrics such as engagement, intervention completion rate, safety, and interview outcomes. For usability evaluation, one study [37] employed the User Version of the Mobile Application Rating Scale (uMARS). Two studies [29, 38] employed the System Usability Scale for Children (SUS-C). For acceptability assessment, five studies [23, 32, 35, 39, 40] utilized self-designed questionnaires to measure participant satisfaction.

Application outcomes of gamified interventions

Glycemic control

Twelve studies reported outcomes related to glycemic control. Regarding the effects of interventions on glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), existing research findings exhibit considerable heterogeneity: two studies [36, 42] reported significant reductions in HbA1c levels post-intervention, while two studies [34, 39] showed a downward trend in HbA1c without reaching statistical significance. While three studies [20, 23, 25] observed no statistically significant difference in HbA1c levels before and after intervention.

Regarding the intervention effects on continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)-related indicators, multiple studies demonstrated positive outcomes: Three studies [31, 36, 40] showed a significant increase in time in range (TIR, 70–180 mg/dL) after intervention; One study [39] reported a significant improvement in the percentage of time spent in hypoglycemia (< 70 mg/dL) post-intervention; another study [31] confirmed a significant reduction in the mean CGM glucose level among pediatric patients following intervention.

Self-management behaviors

A total of seven studies reported outcomes related to self-management behaviors. Four studies [22, 25, 38, 40] reported significant improvements in self-management behaviors among children with T1DM following intervention. Two studies [23, 24] demonstrated that interventions effectively increased the frequency of daily bloodglucose self-monitoring and related operational skills among these children. However, one study [20] showed no significant difference in self-management levels before and after intervention.

Disease-related knowledge

Three studies reported outcomes related to disease-related knowledge. All three studies [22, 33, 35] indicated that children with T1DM demonstrated a significant improvement in their overall knowledge of type 1 diabetes following the intervention.

Psychosocial functioning

Three studies reported outcomes related to psychosocial functioning. One study [41] indicated that children with T1DM showed a significant reduction in perceived disease threat and a trend toward improved social skills following intervention. Another study [21] found that children exhibited significantly lower levels of behavioral distress during insulin injections compared to the control group. Additionally, one study [42] reported significant improvements in psychological adaptation among children with T1DM.

Quality of life

Six studies reported outcomes related to quality of life. Three studies [34, 36, 41] reported significant improvements in quality of life following intervention, while the other three studies [20, 23, 41] reported that the intervention did not statistically significantly improve quality of life scores.

Feasibility, usability, and acceptability

A total of 12 studies reported outcomes related to feasibility, usability, and acceptability. Regarding feasibility, four studies [27, 32, 38, 39] demonstrated good feasibility of the intervention through metrics such as participation rates, intervention completion rates, safety, and user feedback. Regarding usability, three studies [26, 33, 37] reported high system usability. Concerning acceptability, five studies [23, 32, 35, 39, 40] found consistently high satisfaction with the intervention among children with T1DM and their parents.

Discussion

This review found that existing gamified interventions for children with T1DM primarily utilize mobile applications (52%) and websites (17%) as delivery platforms. These interventions integrate core gamification elements such as goal setting, challenges, feedback, and fun, aiming to transform cumbersome self-management tasks into more engaging interactive experiences. Mobile applications have emerged as the dominant platform, aligning closely with adolescents’ digital media usage habits and reflecting the broader trend in digital health interventions toward convenience and accessibility. However, this relative concentration of delivery formats may limit the interventions’ universality. For instance, coverage and applicability could face challenges among preschool-aged children, families with limited access to digital devices, or those lacking sufficient digital literacy. Simultaneously, according to Fogg’s Behavior Model [43], different gamification elements yield varying effects in promoting health behaviors. Among the 23 included studies, goal setting (96%), overcoming challenges (96%), and fun (96%) constitute the core design components universally integrated into current gamified interventions. These elements enhance children’s behavioral motivation by providing clear direction, stimulating competitive drive, and reducing task aversion, respectively. Elements like experiential feedback (74%) and progress monitoring (48%) directly enhance children’s perceived ability to perform self-management tasks through visual data presentation and skill guidance. In contrast, social linkage elements are applied at a lower rate (35%), despite serving as a potent social trigger mechanism crucial for sustaining long-term behavior—particularly during adolescence when peer recognition is sought. Reward and punishment reinforcement (57%), another trigger category, may influence both the immediacy and sustainability of behavior depending on its inclusion and form. Furthermore, the included studies exhibited significant deficiencies in theoretical application, with nearly 70% failing to explicitly report any theoretical framework—a recurring issue highlighted in previous gamification intervention research [44, 45]. This theoretical gap limits understanding of the underlying mechanisms of intervention effectiveness and hinders the optimization of intervention design. In summary, future research should place greater emphasis on the introduction and application of theoretical frameworks, focusing on exploring theoretically guided optimal combinations of gamification elements. Furthermore, hybrid intervention models adaptable to diverse clinical and contextual needs should be developed to enhance the stability and scalability of intervention outcomes.

Regarding intervention duration, this systematic review reveals significant heterogeneity in the duration settings of gamified interventions for children with T1DM. Intervention cycles range from single sessions or ultra-short interventions lasting as little as one week to long-term interventions spanning 6–12 months. Overall, short-to-medium-term interventions are most prevalent in existing studies, while research with genuine long-term follow-up remains extremely limited. This finding aligns closely with previous reviews on digital health interventions for pediatric chronic diseases, which consistently highlight a research focus on short-term feasibility and preliminary efficacy assessments, with insufficient attention to long-term behavior maintenance [46, 47]. Prior research shows that short-term gamified therapies can improve children with T1DM’s self-management abilities, engagement motivation, and disease-related information in a short amount of time, but they have no influence on long-term metabolic outcomes [15]. Medium-to-long-term interventions, on the other hand, have a higher potential to support sustained self-management practices; nonetheless, they are vulnerable to factors such as intervention fatigue and diminishing involvement [48]. Furthermore, some studies failed to clearly report intervention frequency and duration, limiting assessments of the “dose-response relationship.” Given the long-term and dynamic nature of T1DM self-management, future nursing intervention research should explore phased, sustainable gamified intervention models while maintaining feasibility. Systematic reporting of intervention duration and follow-up timepoints is essential to better support evidence-based nursing practice.

Regarding outcomes, although results showed heterogeneity in improving glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c)—a key indicator of long-term glycemic control—this aligns with the 2024 meta-analysis by Nishi et al. [10], which concluded that digital health gamified interventions exhibit significant variability in their impact on metabolic markers. Further clarification of key moderating factors is required. Regarding self-management behaviors and knowledge, both prior studies [49] and the studies included in this review reported that gamified interventions were associated with improvements in skills and knowledge. In terms of psychosocial functioning, gamified interventions demonstrated positive potential for reducing treatment-related distress, improving disease perception, and enhancing social skills, consistent with research showing gamification can alleviate psychological burdens in children with chronic diseases [50]. However, regarding the multidimensional core outcome of quality of life, the studies included in this review did not yield consistent conclusions. This aligns with findings from some systematic reviews indicating that the long-term impact of gamification on the quality of life of chronic disease patients remains unclear [51, 52]. This inconsistency may stem from variations in quality-of-life measurement tools, insufficient intervention duration, or uneven control of confounding factors, suggesting the need for future studies with rigorous designs and long-term follow-up to clarify its effects. Furthermore, regarding feasibility, usability, and acceptability, existing studies generally report high user engagement, system usability, and satisfaction, laying a preliminary foundation for the clinical implementation of these interventions.

Although the reported outcomes were heterogeneous, some common characteristics were observed among studies reporting positive outcomes. Interventions associated with improvements in glycemic control or self-management behaviors were more likely to incorporate multiple gamification elements simultaneously, particularly real-time feedback, progress monitoring, and reward mechanisms. Several effective interventions were also delivered through mobile applications or CGM-integrated systems, which enabled continuous engagement and personalized feedback. In addition, studies grounded in theoretical frameworks or clinical guidelines appeared to demonstrate more consistent behavioral and psychosocial benefits compared with interventions lacking an explicit theoretical basis. Medium-term interventions (9 weeks to 6 months) also seemed more likely to report sustained improvements than ultra-short interventions. However, due to the heterogeneity of study designs, outcome measures, and intervention components, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in future comparative research.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic scoping review investigating the role and effectiveness of gamified interventions for children with type 1 diabetes. We employed a rigorous and comprehensive three-step search strategy, retrieving 23 articles from six databases. Our systematic review is registered. However, several limitations exist. First, the current evidence base remains dominated by feasibility and usability studies, along with small-sample quasi-experimental research. Methodologically rigorous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are relatively scarce (7 studies), with two being protocol-only. This reflects the early exploratory phase of an emerging field, though inadequate methodological reporting is a common issue. Second, studies not published in English were excluded, potentially introducing language bias and limiting the scope of internationally published evidence. Finally, significant heterogeneity existed among included studies in terms of design type, sample size, intervention content, and outcome measures, limiting the comparability of results. Furthermore, most studies had small sample sizes and limited follow-up periods, making it difficult to assess the long-term effects of gamified interventions.

Future research

Based on the above findings, we propose the following recommendations: (1) Strengthen Theoretical Guidance and Personalized Design: Future research should develop gamified intervention programs under clear theoretical frameworks and further explore personalized adaptations based on children’s age characteristics and cognitive developmental stages to enhance the intervention’s specificity and effectiveness. (2) Conduct High-Quality Efficacy Validation: There is an urgent need to design rigorous, multicenter RCTs comparing gamified interventions with standard care, alongside cost-effectiveness analyses, to provide evidence-based decision support for their implementation within healthcare systems. (3) Deepen Exploration of Mechanisms of Action: Research focus should expand from “whether it works” to “how it works,” further elucidating the potential mechanisms underlying gamified interventions and identifying key moderating factors influencing intervention outcomes. (4) Promote clinical integration: Healthcare providers should recognize the potential of gamified tools and explore their safe, effective integration into outpatient health education, discharge preparation, and long-term follow-up support. This will establish a new “human-machine collaboration” model for diabetes management.

Conclusion

This study represents the first comprehensive systematic review examining the current application of gamified interventions among children with T1DM. It focuses on analyzing the design characteristics, delivery platforms, theoretical foundations, and overall reported outcomes of these interventions in areas such as glycemic control, self-management, psychosocial functioning, and quality of life. Overall, gamification, as an emerging intervention strategy aligned with children’s psychological development, demonstrates potential value in enhancing disease management engagement, knowledge, and behavior among pediatric T1DM patients. It provides evidence-based support for innovative practices in pediatric diabetes care. However, the stability and generalizability of its intervention effects require further validation.
