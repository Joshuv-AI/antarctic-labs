Complex multicellularity is linked with expanded specialized metabolite production in microorganisms
Authors: Rauf A. Salamzade, Lindsay R. Kalan, Cameron R. Currie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Single-celled microorganisms evolving to form cooperative multicellular units represent a key innovation in the history of life. Multicellularity enables cellular differentiation and, as such, is critical to the emergence of biological complexity. Here we investigate the hypothesis that multicellularity facilitated massive expansions in specialized metabolite production, including polyketides, non-ribosomal peptides and terpenes, across bacteria and fungi. Systematic investigations reveal that unicellular taxa are largely limited in biosynthetic potential for secondary metabolite production. By contrast, profound biosynthetic expansions in Actinomycetota, Cyanobacteriota, Myxococcota, Pezizomycotina and Agaricomycetes coincide with independent origins of multicellular development, such as the formation of mycelia and fruiting bodies. These multicellular lineages are concomitantly enriched in carbohydrate-active enzymes, suggesting that ancient chemical innovations were further shaped by catabolic processes. By linking intraspecific cooperation to the evolution of specialized metabolism, this study advances our understanding of major evolutionary transitions and provides a framework for discovering chemical compounds to counter antimicrobial resistance.

Main

Evolving the ability to produce and secrete specialized metabolites, including antibiotics, represents a key innovation in microbial life1,2. Unlike primary metabolites essential for basic cellular functions, such as amino acids and vitamins, specialized metabolites are leveraged by microorganisms to directly mediate complex interspecies interactions, compete for resources and establish ecological niches3,4. Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) encode the critical molecular machinery that enable microorganisms to produce this vast diversity of specialized chemicals. By encoding complex enzymatic pathways for synthesizing bioactive compounds, BGCs have allowed microorganisms to evolve potent, diverse and often highly specific metabolites that dramatically expand their functional capabilities to interact with and adapt to complex environmental challenges. The natural products derived from microbial specialized metabolites represent a critical source of bioactive compounds with immense potential for drug discovery, having yielded numerous life-saving pharmaceuticals such as antibiotics, immunosuppressants and anticancer agents5. Understanding the evolution of natural product synthesis by microorganisms is crucial for deciphering the role secreted metabolites have in mediating ecological interactions and shaping microbial diversity, and has transformative potential in drug discovery.

Multicellularity represents another major transition in the history of microbial life, enhancing biological complexity and biodiversity6. The advantages conferred by multicellularity include improved resource acquisition, environmental resilience and division of labour7. Multicellularity has emerged independently multiple times across the tree of life, including within the bacterial phyla Cyanobacteriota, Myxococcota and Actinomycetota. Cyanobacteriota represent one of the earliest origins of multicellularity, with some species forming filamentous chains and differentiated cells that carry out specialized functions such as nitrogen fixation in heterocysts7. The emergence of multicellularity in cyanobacteria has also been linked with taxonomic diversification and the Great Oxidation Event8. Some members of the bacterial phylum Myxococcota show a form of collective behaviour in which individual cells coordinate to form complex multicellular structures called fruiting bodies in response to starvation conditions, with species such as Myxococcus xanthus showing sophisticated cell-to-cell communication and cooperative movement through social motility7,9.

The Actinomycetota represent a particularly fascinating example of bacterial multicellularity, with some taxa forming complex filamentous structures through hyphal growth that enables them to colonize and penetrate solid substrates such as soil particles and plant material10. The multicellular lifestyle, as particularly evident in the genus Streptomyces, supports sophisticated developmental programmes regulated by complex signalling networks, including the formation of aerial hyphae and spores in response to environmental cues11. Although other bacterial taxa can also show allorecognition12 or form structured biofilms13, these traits often pale in complexity to the multicellular structures and life cycles of Actinomycetota, Myxococcota and Cyanobacteriota or have emerged more recently and, as such, did not shape the genomic content of the deeper ancestral lineage from which it evolved.

Historically, filamentous bacteria and fungi within the phyla Actinomycetota and Ascomycota, respectively, have served as the two primary microbial sources of natural products, contributing many lifesaving drugs, such as streptomycin, tetracycline and erythromycin from bacteria, alongside penicillin, cyclosporine and lovastatin from fungi10. Filamentous Ascomycota and Actinomycetota show complex life cycles during which they can form mycelia and intricate multicellular structures with distinct cell types as well as generate spores to survive stressful conditions and aid geographic dispersal to colonize new niches10,14. More recently, species belonging to the bacterial phyla Cyanobacteriota and Myxococcota have emerged as valuable and rich sources of novel natural products with biomedical potential15–17. In addition to containing taxa enriched in their ability to produce natural products, these microbial lineages also contain taxa that form cooperative multicellular units7. The concomitant occurrence of multicellularity and enriched biosynthetic repertoires in such microbial lineages4,18,19 suggests that the emergence of multicellularity is closely associated with the expansion of genomes and carriage of biosynthetic machinery. This is an intriguing idea that until now has not been systematically investigated across all major domains of microbial life.

Building on the intriguing pattern of key sources of natural products being multicellular, we systematically investigate biosynthetic capacities across the bacterial domain and fungal kingdom to test our hypothesis that multicellularity facilitated expansions in specialized metabolite production. To evaluate the breadth of known microbial specialized metabolite classes—including polyketides, non-ribosomal peptides and terpenes—we comprehensively annotated for over 80 types of BGCs using antiSMASH20. We highlight that the majority of bacterial and archaeal taxonomic orders are on average very limited in the number of specialized metabolites they encode per genome. In stark contrast, orders within the phyla Cyanobacteriota, Myxococcota and Actinomycetota are broadly composed of extant genera rich in machinery for specialized metabolite production. Within all three phyla, the ancestral enrichment in biosynthetic repertoire coincides with early, independent origins of multicellularity. For Actinomycetota, we show that the emergence of multicellularity and biosynthetic trait expansions align with the origin of the class Actinomycetes, the most sourced reservoir of bacterial natural products. We next investigate biosynthetic potential across the fungal kingdom and show that most lineages are substantially limited in canonical specialized metabolism relative to the Dikarya, which are renowned for forming complex multicellular structures such as fruiting bodies. Through comparative and association analyses, we show that increased biosynthetic capacity across microbial lineages is strongly associated with enrichments in carbohydrate-active enzymes as well as proteins mediating self-recognition in fungi. Finally, we discuss the association between multicellularity and genomic expansions of machinery for specialized metabolism. We frame these genomic expansions through a public goods context, wherein extracellular molecules are subject to tragedy of the commons dynamics that shape their production and regulation.

Results

Ancestral expansions in biosynthetic capacity are limited across the bacterial tree of life

To begin evaluating the association of multicellularity with enrichments in specialized metabolism, we first determined distributions of per-genome biosynthetic carriage for 74 well-sequenced bacterial and archaeal orders. Both the median number of BGCs as well as the median BGC-ome size, the summed length of predicted BGC regions in a single genome, were assessed across 4,732 genus-representative genomes from the orders (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). A strong correlation was observed between the two metrics, and while complete genomes were preferentially used where available, BGC-ome size was largely assessed throughout this study to alleviate overassessment of biosynthetic capacity arising from assembly fragmentation issues. The distribution of biosynthetic genomic content was heavily skewed, with the vast majority (65 of 74) of bacterial and archaeal orders having a median count of 5 or fewer BGC regions or a maximum median BGC-ome size of less than 150 kilobase pairs (kb) (Fig. 1a). None of the eight archaeal orders had a median count of five or more BGCs, confirming their known lack of biosynthetic traits21. Large-scale annotation efforts have also previously suggested that BGC carriage is limited in many bacterial species22, and databases cataloguing the taxonomic origins of known natural products have shown that they are disproportionately sourced from just six microbial phyla23,24.

To establish which taxonomic orders show substantial increases in their median BGC-ome size relative to their phylogenetic contexts, we next constructed a timetree of the 74 bacterial and archaeal orders and applied a phylogeny-informed approach leveraging Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes (Fig. 1b,c and Supplementary Table 2). This analysis highlighted five distinct orders belonging to three phyla: Cyanobacteriota, Myxococcota and Actinomycetota. Because each of the three phyla are enriched in biosynthetic content across multiple genera, we posit that they correspond to cases of ancestral enrichments in biosynthetic traits. While it is difficult to resolve whether these orders experienced large-scale acquisition of biosynthetic machinery via ancestral horizontal gene transfer (HGT) events, they do not appear to have elevated rates of recent BGC-specific transfer relative to other orders (Extended Data Fig. 2a–c).

In alignment with recent research highlighting the immense diversity of BGCs that exists within Cyanobacteriota, Myxococcota and Actinomycetota17, we observed that specific orders within these phyla also featured more distinct domains within key biosynthetic proteins than would be expected based on the extent of their ribosomal diversification (Fig. 1b). In addition, as described in the introduction, the three phyla highlighted feature some of the most well-characterized model bacterial species used for studying complex multicellularity and life strategies. Four other bacterial orders have median counts of six or more BGCs per genome and also include species that show multicellularity-like or cooperative behaviour. They include the orders Desulfobacterales, which feature multicellular magnetotactic bacteria25, and Paenibacillales, which encompasses species that have complex physiologies and can perform organized colony swarming26.

Ancestral enrichments in bacterial biosynthetic capacity are associated with multicellularity and other factors

We next investigated whether a variety of genomic, functional and ecological traits correlate with increased biosynthetic capacity across the three phyla highlighted as having undergone ancestral biosynthetic expansions, in addition to the phyla Pseudomonadota and Bacillota (Supplementary Notes, Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 3). Across 17,844 species-level representative genomes from the phyla, multiple genomics-inferred features were positively correlated with BGC-ome size, including (1) carriage of oxidative phosphorylation-related enzymes, (2) increased coding usage of specific amino acids and (3) an inferred preference for mesophilic growth. After genome size, increased BGC-ome size was most strongly associated with enrichment for carbohydrate-processing enzymes, including in Pseudomonadota and Bacillota. Furthermore, multiple independent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) across phyla revealed that several plant-related carbohydrate usage enzyme families were significantly associated with biosynthetic capacity. The only family found to be associated with BGC-ome size in four or more of the five phyla was glycosyl hydrolase family 19, chitinases that are rare in microorganisms. Within Streptomyces, homologues of this enzyme family are hypothesized to have been acquired via HGT from plants27. These analyses support a pronounced increase in biosynthetic capacity that occurred across diverse bacteria coinciding with the increased availability of biopolymers from land plants28.

Within the three phyla determined to have had ancestral expansions in biosynthetic content, positive associations were observed between counts for known protein markers of multicellularity and sporulation29–31 with BGC-ome size (Fig. 1d–f). For Cyanobacteriota, carriage of eight or more multicellularity-associated genes and three or more akinete-associated genes was significantly associated with increased BGC-ome size (P = 2.59 × 10−59, Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic = 16.19). Similarly, for Myxococcota, genomes with greater than 200 social lifestyle-associated genes and 2 or more sporulation-associated genes had larger BGC-ome sizes (P = 4.65 × 10−13, Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic = 7.14). The substantial overlap in social lifestyle-associated orthologues among genomes spanning diverse taxonomic orders further suggests that sociality was ancestral to the phylum Myxococcota or, at least, multiple classes within it (Extended Data Fig. 4). Among Actinomycetota, the carriage of ssgB homologues, which are involved in the process of sporulation and associated with increased genome size and complex physiology30, was also a clear indicator of increased biosynthetic potential (P < 1 × 10−300, Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic = 39.96). Furthermore, many species within the class Actinomycetes, even those without ssgB homologues, had larger BGC-omes compared with other classes in the phylum (P = 4.50 × 10−126, Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic = 23.85; Fig. 1e).

The last common ancestor of Actinomycetes was multicellular and possessed a large reservoir of biosynthetic traits

To further understand the enrichment of biosynthetic content and emergence of multicellularity in the class Actinomycetes, we first used phylogenomics to determine the evolutionary relationship between 133 genera with high-quality genomes from across the phylum Actinomycetota (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 4). A maximum-likelihood phylogeny was constructed based on single-copy-core orthologue groups revealing two clades within the Actinomycetes class, which we refer to as clade 1 and clade 2 (Fig. 2a,b and Supplementary Table 4). The phylogenetic positioning of filamentous genera suggests that multicellularity was ancestral to the Actinomycetes class. This is supported by multicellularity being present across most clade 1 lineages as well as clade 2 featuring the early-diverging and filamentous family of Streptomycetaceae. This suggests that morphologically simpler genera from clade 2, such as Micrococcus, have experienced large-scale gene loss and a loss of physiological complexity. While ancestral loss of morphological complexity has previously been suggested within the order of Mycobacteriales in clade 1 (ref. 11), to our knowledge, it has not been hypothesized to have occurred at large scale, beyond one order35, within clade 2 Actinomycetes. Due to low bootstrap values at certain ancestral nodes in our phylogenomic model, we further assessed the proposed topology for Actinomycetes evolution using several additional phylogenomic and evolutionary approaches (Supplementary Notes, Fig. 2c–g and Extended Data Fig. 5). This process included developing a statistical framework to assess highly conserved sites along conserved and single-copy orthologues, which revealed consensus support that unicellular taxa from clade 2 share a more recent last common ancestor (LCA) with Streptomycetaceae than with morphologically complex families from clade 1.

A multicellular LCA of Actinomycetes coinciding with the broad enrichment of biosynthetic traits in its extant genera further supports that a large biosynthetic repertoire was ancestral to this class. Genome streamlining in less morphologically complex taxa across Actinomycetes also probably explains why BGCs are elevated across the class but natural product discovery has still been largely limited to filamentous actinomycetes24 (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Some BGCs were also probably lost in these genera together with the ability to filament and form mycelia. To investigate the conservation of specialized metabolism across the phylum Actinomycetota, we assessed orthologue groups found to be key components of BGCs in two or more Actinomycetes. Of the 428 biosynthetic orthologue groups identified, 366 (85.5%) were found within BGC contexts in both major clades of the class (Fig. 2g, Extended Data Fig. 6b and Supplementary Table 5). Furthermore, of those 366 orthologue groups, 266 (72.7%) were either exclusive to Actinomycetes or exclusively found within a BGC context within Actinomycetes. This includes the orthologue group corresponding to 3-dehydroquinate (DHQ) and aminoDHQ synthases, involved in shikimate and 3-amino-5-hydroxybenzoic acid (AHBA) biosynthesis, respectively. Phylogenetic investigations further revealed that key enzymes involved in AHBA biosynthesis, AHBA synthase and aminoDAHP synthase, emerged within Actinomycetes (Supplementary Notes and Extended Data Fig. 7). AHBA is a key precursor of the functionally diverse ansamycin class of specialized metabolites36, which the antibiotic rifamycin belongs to.

While rifamycin resistance is observed across multiple bacterial phyla37, within Actinomycetota, genetic and protein markers associated with rifamycin resistance were largely specific to Actinomycetes and common across clade 1 and clade 2 of the class, including many genera lacking morphological complexity (Supplementary Table 6). Additional investigations into known and uncharacterized ansamycin-related BGCs revealed that although horizontal transfer has probably impacted their evolution, sequence divergence due to evolution by descent has also expanded the breadth of chemicals they synthesize. Specifically, when comparing closely related BGCs for the production of naphthomycin ansamycins, BGCs from diverse clade 1 orders were found to show greater overlap in genetic content with each other than with BGCs from clade 2 genomes (Supplementary Notes and Extended Data Fig. 7). Together, these results suggest that the ansamycin and AHBA biosynthesis machinery were encoded by early Actinomycetes and build on past evolutionary studies that estimate the origins of other biosynthetic enzymes to overlap with the emergence of Actinomycetes38. They also show how some genes in specialized metabolism can arise from those involved in primary metabolism1,39.

Fungal biosynthetic enrichments associate with developments in complex multicellularity and haplontic life cycles

In addition to the domain Bacteria, the eukaryotic kingdom Fungi has historically served as another key reservoir of natural products and the source of several important drugs including the antibiotic penicillin, the immunosuppressant cyclosporine and the cholesterol-lowering statins. The kingdom also contains diverse species capable of forming complex cooperative units, such as some Ascomycota, which can form mycelia resembling structures produced by some bacterial Actinomycetes. Similar to filamentous Actinomycetes, filamentous Ascomycota are also renowned for their diverse chemical repertoire, suggesting that enrichment in biosynthetic potential in Fungi is also associated with multicellularity. Next, we conducted comparative genomics exploring the biosynthetic repertoire across the fungal tree of life. As with bacteria, we show that most fungal lineages are composed of taxa with limited biosynthetic potential, with ~77% of distinct non-dikaryotic genera having ten or fewer BGC regions or BGC-ome sizes of 500 kb or shorter (Fig. 3a). Among Fungi, two lineages (Rozella, Paramicrosporidium and Mitosporidium and the Blastocladiomycota) have the smallest BGC-ome sizes and lack polyketide synthatases (PKSs) and non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs) (Fig. 3b). These trends and the lack of BGCs in Fonticula, a close outgroup for Fungi, support the conclusion that limited biosynthetic potential is symplesiomorphic in Fungi—that is, limited biosynthetic potential is probably an ancestral characteristic of the kingdom. Fungal genomes from the Chytridiomycota, Mucoromycota and Zoopagomycota all have relatively small BGC-ome sizes but contain a diverse set of terpene domains as well as some PKS and NRPS containing BGCs, including those substantially diverged from sequences underlying the synthesis of known specialized metabolites (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 8b,c). Interestingly, the phylum Neocallimastigomycota showed enrichment of BGCs relative to chytrids and other non-dikaryon Fungi (P = 2.48 × 10−3, Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic = 2.81; Extended Data Fig. 8d). These anaerobic fungi commonly inhabit the guts of herbivores, can form morphologically complex rhizoids41 and have a high biosynthetic capacity that recent research has suggested was mostly acquired from bacteria via ancient lateral transfer events42. Although we found limited genomic indications of recent HGT of BGCs in fungi (Extended Data Fig. 2de), over the course of fungal evolution, HGT has probably contributed extensively to the biosynthetic diversity of the fungal kingdom43,44.

Next, in our evaluation of the evolution of biosynthesis in Fungi, we find support for two independent origins of BGC enrichment occurring separately within subkingdom Dikarya, which are able to form dikaryons, cells with two nuclei containing separate genomes that are physically associated and co-replicate18,40. Many members of this subkingdom show physiological complexity and form macroscopic sexual fruiting bodies, such as cup-shaped ascocarps and mushroom basidiocarp structures. Specifically, phylogenetic analyses indicated that minimal BGC enrichment is symplesiomorphic in the Dikarya, with the sister lineage to Dikarya, the Mucoromycota, and early-diverging lineages of both the Ascomycota and Basidiomycota having relatively small BGC-ome sizes. Among Ascomycota, a monophyletic subdivision within the Pezizomycotina was determined to be highly enriched in BGCs relative to all other Fungi (P = 2.34 × 10−29, Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic = 11.19; Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 8e), a result previously identified by others45,46. While hyphal multicellularity evolved early in fungal evolution47, consistent with our observations in bacteria, the ancestral expansion of biosynthetic content in the Pezizomycotina coincides with developments in complex multicellularity, such as the formation of macroscopic fruiting bodies. Within Basidiomycota, the class Agaricomycetes represents a second origin of BGC enrichment with taxa generally featuring larger BGC-ome sizes than other Basidiomycota species as well as non-dikaryotic fungal species (P = 3.87 × 10−12, Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic = 6.84 and P = 6.68 × 10−14, Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic = 7.40, respectively; Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 8f). As with the Pezizomycotina, the Agaricomycetes represent the other major fungal group well known for possessing complex multicellularity48.

We next investigated which orthologous families of proteins associate with increased biosynthetic capacity in Fungi. To do so, we performed comparative genomics and GWAS (Fig. 4a). One trait revealed as enriched in BGC-rich dikaryotic fungal clades are proteins implicated in functioning as heterokaryon incompatibility (HI) factors. Heterokaryon or vegetative incompatibility (HI) proteins enable self-identification among filamentous fungi49,50 and mechanistically involve autophagy, which are key properties of many multicellular organisms. A search for proteins with domain architectures associated with HI revealed that many species within BGC-enriched Pezizomycotina and Agaricomycetes feature hundreds of proteins with these domains in their genomes, in particular the heterokaryon incompatibility protein (HET) domain (Fig. 4bc, Extended Data Fig. 9a,b and Supplementary Table 9). The correlation between BGC-ome size and the number of proteins with HI-associated domains was further supported at the genus resolution within Aspergillus (Extended Data Fig. 9c–h and Supplementary Table 10). Given their function, the enrichment of HI-associated proteins across Pezizomycotina is in alignment with perspectives that they primarily exist in a vegetative state as haplontic homokaryons18,40. To assess the genomic impacts of life cycles, we next developed a phylogenetically standardized measure of genome fluidity and used it to determine that clades with greater genomic fluidity tended to be haplontic (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 10a). Similar trends were observed for BGC-ome size distributions, with haplontic clades generally showing greater biosynthetic potential (Extended Data Fig. 10b). Despite having fewer BGCs, Agaricomycetes might simply appear to have more expansive pangenomes than Pezizomycotina because they are more likely to be in a dikaryotic form and harbour two distinct genomes at the time of sampling for genome sequencing40.

Our investigations further revealed that several specific orthologue groups predicted to function in carbohydrate processing or plant virulence are either associated with increased biosynthetic capacity across Fungi or enriched in BGC-rich Pezizomycotina and Agaricomycetes relative to other Dikarya (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Tables 11 and 12). Carbohydrate-processing enzyme enrichment was more strongly associated with biosynthetic capacity than even genome size in fungi (Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. 8g,h). Thus, provided that both Pezizomycotina and Agaricomycetes probably originated around the same time as the emergence of land plants51,52, intricate relationships with plants and the development of certain life strategies are probably associated with biosynthetic enrichments in the two dikaryotic clades in addition to developments in multicellularity and mechanisms for self-recognition53–55.

Discussion

The production of bioactive specialized metabolites is a hallmark of microbial diversity and drug discovery. In this Article, we systematically confirm that most lineages of microbial life carry a limited number of BGCs, as inferred by genomic annotation of canonical biosynthetic content. We then show that in three bacterial phyla and two fungal subphyla, signatures of ancestral expansions in biosynthetic traits coincide with early developments of multicellularity. Furthermore, our evolutionary analyses infer that the LCA of the class Actinomycetes, formerly Actinomycetia, which features many biosynthetically gifted genera including Streptomyces, was multicellular and potentially able to form advanced structures such as aerial hyphae. While hyphal formation is probably ancestral to the fungal subkingdom Dikarya47, complex multicellular fruiting bodies likely arose through separate, independent evolutionary events in the biosynthetically rich lineages of Pezizomycotina and Agaricomycetes. The broad co-occurrence of complex multicellularity with BGC enrichment in extant lineages of bacterial and fungal taxa suggests that intraspecific cooperation helped spur ancestral expansions in biosynthetic capacity to produce specialized metabolites, including natural products that serve as key drugs in human health.

Other multicellular organisms, especially plants, are known to produce various specialized metabolites56. Interestingly, a reanalysis of recent biosynthetic characterizations across the plant phylum Streptophyta57 suggests that vascular land plants also experienced an expansion in BGCs that parallels increased morphological complexity (Supplementary Notes). Given this, as well as reports that other multicellular Eukarya, such as slime moulds and animals, also contain machinery for the biosynthesis of specialized metabolites58,59, future systematic studies in the Eukarya might reveal that our findings apply across the tree of life.

The benefits microorganisms derive from producing natural products are obvious. These potent bioactive molecules mediate complex intraspecies and interspecies interactions within free-living and host-associated microbial communities, including in the human microbiome. However, these specialized metabolites require complex enzymatic machinery and are energetically expensive compounds to produce. Multicellular growth and cellular differentiation probably help mitigate the fitness costs associated with parallel production of specialized metabolites. For example, multicellular organization in Streptomyces has recently been shown to mitigate the fitness costs associated with the production of multiple specialized metabolites through division of labour owing to genetic heterogeneity60. Such dynamics probably facilitated the evolution of expanded genomes that encode more BGCs.

The higher biomass obtained from multicellular growth would also facilitate the use of secreted carbohydrate-active enzymes to deconstruct the more complex biopolymers that would otherwise be unavailable, such as chitin and cellulose, which would concomitantly support the biosynthesis of bioactive molecules to suppress competitors for these energy-rich substrates. Indeed, our finding that BGC expansions in both fungi and bacteria coincide with substantial enrichments in carbohydrate utilization enzymes suggests that catabolic processes help these microorganisms obtain the energy required to produce specialized metabolites at ecologically relevant scales. Furthermore, microorganisms and their specialized metabolites have been shown to participate in intricate relationships with terrestrial and marine eukaryotic hosts, such as defensive symbiosis61,62. Taken together, our study supports a model in which several biosynthetic expansions have occurred over geological time, with an earlier expansion coinciding with early developments in multicellularity and a more recent expansion owing to emergent relationships with algae, invertebrates and plants. The subsequent expanded access to energy through the use of carbohydrate-active enzymes probably further fuelled developments in more complex multicellular structures, offset costs of carriage of a greater arsenal of specialized metabolites and promoted the transition from diffusion-feeding life histories to invasive growth strategies.

In addition to carbohydrate utilization machinery, we highlight the high prevalence of proteins suspected to function in HI in the two BGC-rich fungal clades of Pezizomycotina and Agaricomycotina. HI proteins allow filamentous dikaryons to self-recognize and initiate autophagy to prevent the formation of heterokaryons. The ability to self-distinguish is probably important for fungi when competing against other fungal species for access to potentially limited carbon sources within their natural habitats; however, HI could have other roles in fungal biology, including viral defence63 and promoting haplontic life cycles.

Many specialized metabolites must be secreted to mediate interactions within an organism’s ecological niche and, as such, operate as ‘public goods’64,65. The production of costly public goods makes cells vulnerable to exploitation by ‘cheater’ cells that benefit from the specialized metabolite without investing metabolic resources in their production, setting up ‘tragedy of the commons’ dynamics. Some unicellular bacteria use quorum-sensing systems to help resolve tragedy of the commons dynamics; these chemical communication networks enable synchronized group behaviours only when sufficient population density is reached, thereby preventing premature and inefficient production of costly public goods. In multicellular organisms, genetic relatedness among cells reduces the incentive to ‘cheat’. Kin selection conferred by multicellularity thus avoids the tragedy of the commons dynamics, which cause breakdowns in cooperative behaviour when individual cells pursue self-interest rather than invest in costly specialized metabolite production and secretion.

The evolution of multicellularity presents a fundamental paradox in evolutionary biology, as it requires individual cells to sacrifice their direct reproductive fitness to benefit the collective organism. Our findings suggest that a major benefit conferred by the emergence of multicellularity is the concomitant ability to evolve a high capacity to produce potent chemical mediators of intra- and interspecific interactions. These chemical compounds may serve yet unrecognized roles in mediating within-species dynamics, enforcing cooperative dynamics within multicellular entities, as hinted at by our finding of abundances of HI-associated proteins within BGC-rich and fruiting body-forming fungi. Indeed, such metabolites could even have played important roles in the origin of multicellularity itself. Ultimately, our findings advance understanding around the evolution of multicellularity and suggest that multicellular taxa, especially those that are understudied or from less commonly investigated habitats, might be prime sources for the discovery of truly novel classes of specialized metabolites.

Methods

Construction of a bacterial and archaeal timetree and determination of evolutionary expansions in biosynthetic traits

A bacterial and archaeal timetree was constructed for 74 representative bacterial and archaeal orders that have been well sequenced. To construct the phylogeny, a two-tiered approach was used. First, high-resolution phylogenies were constructed for each of the five bacterial phyla with the most number of characterized BGCs based on predetermined gene sets shown to be largely single copy and highly conserved for bacteria66,67 using IQ-TREE (v2.2.0.3)67. These were assessed to determine more reliable topologies for individual phyla than what could be achieved with a more limited number of single-copy core genes that are more broadly conserved across both bacteria and archaea. To construct the final phylogeny of the 74 representative bacterial and archaeal orders, a guiding tree constraining intra-phylum topologies and specifying a partition between Terrabacteria and Gracilicutes68 was provided to IQ-TREE (v1.6.12) together with trimmed protein alignments of eight conserved ribosomal proteins69, which featured a combined total of 1,095 amino acid sites and were conserved and non-ambiguous in ≥90% of genomes. IQ-TREE was run requesting an edge-unlinked partition model with ModelFinder limited to the Le and Gascuel (LG) and Whelan and Goldman (WAG) substitution matrices.

Next, to establish a timetree, we followed a previously described protocol with some adaptations to parameter configurations70. Fossil and previous-inference-based calibration information for node ages were gathered from literature71,72 (Supplementary Table 2). These calibrations were encoded as upper and lower bounds for select nodes in the phylogeny provided as input for MCMCtree (v4.10.0)73. The software antiSMASH (v7.0.0)20 was used for BGC annotation of genus-level representative genomes. Finally, to determine evolutionary shifts in the median BGC-ome size across the phylogeny, we used an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process-based phylogenetic lasso approach via the l1ou74 (v1.43) R library (Fig. 1c). A set of 4,732 genus-representative genomes across the 74 orders were used to assess their functional profile and biosynthetic potential (Supplementary Table 1).

For additional details on the construction of the phylum-specific phylogenies, including construction with and without filtering for compositional heterogeneity, construction of the overall bacteria–archaea phylogeny and details on parameter configurations for MCMCTree, please see the section with the same name in Supplementary Methods.

Genome selection and annotation for systematic investigations of genetic traits and ecological trends correlated with bacterial biosynthetic potential

Species-resolution representative genomes were selected for each of the five bacterial phyla with the greatest number of characterized BGCs. Only species belonging to genera with either ≥5 (for Myxococcota), ≥10 (for Actinomycetota and Cyanobacteriota) or ≥20 (Bacillota and Pseudomonadota) genomes in GTDB R214 were considered (Supplementary Table 3)75. Different thresholds for different phyla were used to limit the number of genomes yet maintain an adequate sampling breadth for proper phylogenetic investigation of the focal phylum. For Bacillota, genomes belonging to related but distinct phyla in GTDB R214 (for example, Bacillota_A, which includes the genus Clostridioides) were also included. A total of 17,844 species-representative genomes were assessed.

Functional annotation of coding genes for BGCs was performed using antiSMASH. Large-scale annotations for known phylum-specific protein markers associated with multicellularity and sporulation29–31,76–79 were primarily performed using HMMER (v3.3.2)80 with profile HMMs gathered or constructed following de novo orthologue group inference across a more limited set of representative genomes81. SandPiper (v0.2.0)82 was used to extract ecological context based on metagenomic profiling for various GTDB taxa. For additional information on functional analysis of bacterial species-representative genomes, see the section with the same name in Supplementary Methods.

In-depth phylogenomic and evolutionary investigations of the phylum Actinomycetota

Orthologue groups were identified across a set of 133 genus-level representative genomes from the phylum of Actinomycetota using OrthoFinder (v2.5.4)81. The 133 representative genomes were a subset of those used for phylum-specific phylogeny (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4) consisting of only assemblies that were designated ‘Complete’ or ‘Chromosome’ level for completeness status in the Genome database maintained by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Coarse orthologue groups, rather than refined hierarchical orthologue groups, were used to prevent possible inappropriate paralogy splitting by OrthoFinder’s algorithm when applied to bacterial genomes (Supplementary Table 6).

A total of 34 orthologue groups were identified as being single-copy core, mostly corresponding to ribosomal proteins (26; 76.5%). Protein alignments for the 34 orthologue groups were generated using MUSCLE (v5.1), filtered using trimAl (v1.4.1) in strict mode, and subsequently used as input for IQ-TREE for phylogenetic modelling of the phylum. IQ-TREE was run using an edge-unlinked partition analysis with ModelFinder limited to the LG and WAG substitution models and 1,000 bootstraps requested. The resulting phylogeny was largely topologically concordant with those based on a subset of the 74 universal bacterial genes by GToTree detection. The phylogeny was rooted by the genome for Rubrobacter_D, which was observed to be an early-diverging genus in previously constructed phylum-specific phylogenies that included genomes from other phyla as outgroups.

Detailed methods for analyses pertaining to investigations of Actinomycetes evolution can be found in Supplementary Methods. This includes sections describing the D.stat metric for assessing topological models based on highly conserved sites of core proteins, the annotation of multicellularity and sporulation-associated proteins, and comprehensive assessment of ansamycin BGCs and AHBA biosynthesis enzymes in 33,804 Actinomycetota genomes.

Phylogenomic investigations of biosynthetic potential across the fungal kingdom

A total of 312 genomes were selected from across the fungal kingdom featuring representatives of different dikaryon families, representatives of different classes from other fungal subkingdoms and phyla, and genomes included in a recent study40, in which several non-dikaryotic lineages of fungi, including many diverse chytrids, were sequenced (Supplementary Table 7). Representative genomes for dikaryon families and non-dikaryotic classes were selected from species-level representative genomes that did not feature ‘fam_Incertae_sedis’ or ‘unidentified’ in their taxonomic designations in the Universal Fungal Core Genome (UFCG) database83 based on assembly N50 measured using abyss-fac (v2.3.9). To properly assess biosynthetic potential, we required genomes to have a minimum N50 of 10 kb. Four non-fungal eukaryotic genomes were included in analyses as outgroups: GCF_000151315.2, Capsaspora owczarzaki; GCF_000001215.4, Drosophila melanogaster; GCA_000388065.2, Fonticula alba; and GCA_000002865.1, Monosiga brevicollis. Genomes were downloaded using ncbi-genome-download, and taxonomic names were systematically gathered from NCBI using taxonkit84.

Coding sequence (CDS) coordinates for assemblies were gathered in GFF format from the RefSeq database of NCBI or, if not available, from the GenBank database of NCBI. If CDS coordinate information was not available in either database or there were difficulties with using them to run antiSMASH for BGC predictions downstream (for example, lack of CDS features or errors with overlapping CDS coordinates), then funannotate (v1.8.15; https://github.com/nextgenusfs/funannotate) was used to first mask repeat sequences, clean and sort assemblies before performing gene prediction85.

The UFCG profile (v1.0.5)83 was systematically applied to identify universal core protein sequences directly within genomic assemblies. Protein sequences for each of the 51 core universal gene markers found in ≥90% of genomes were extracted, aligned using MUSCLE and trimmed using trimAl in strict mode. Afterwards, these alignments were provided as input to IQ-TREE to construct a maximum-likelihood phylogeny using an edge-unlinked partition analysis with ModelFinder, allowing only for LG and WAG substitution models, and requesting 1,000 bootstraps. A monophyletic clade within Pezizomycotina enriched for BGCs was identified through visual examination and validated to include taxonomic subphyla described as BGC enriched in previous studies45,46. This clade encompasses the taxonomic classes Sordariomycetes, Dothideomycetes, Eurotiomycetes, Leotiomycetes, Lecanoromycetes, Xylonomycetes and Arthoniomycetes. For comparative analyses of biosynthetic genes or gene clusters across taxonomic groups, only a single representative genome per genus was regarded, the one with the largest BGC-ome size. Most of the genera with multiple genomes were non-Dikarya (18 of 20), and this approach would allow us to more conservatively assess whether they are depleted in BGCs relative to the higher Fungi.

For additional details on methods pertaining to further typing and assessing the novelty of NRP synthetases or PK synthases86,87, annotation of HI-associated domain architectures and CAZymes88,89, and comparative genomics and GWAS to understand enrichments in genetic factors potentially contributing to biosynthetic enrichment in Agaricomycetes and Pezizomycotina, please see the corresponding section in Supplementary Methods.

Visualization and select statistical tests

Visualizations were primarily created using libraries in R and iTol90. Limits of box plots shown alongside phylogenies correspond to the 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 95th percentiles, whereas stand-alone box plots are always shown in Tukey’s style, in which they extend to the largest value that is less than 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR) from the top hinge of the box and the smallest value that is greater than 1.5 times the IQR from the bottom hinge of the box.

Spearman correlation coefficients and significance P values of genetic and ecological quantitative measures with BGC-ome size were obtained using the spearmanr function in scipy with a two-sided alternative model. One-sided rank sum tests were used to assess the statistical significance of differences in BGC-ome size between Actinomycetes with ssgB, Actinomycetes without ssgB and other Actinomycetota without ssgB.

BGC-ome sizes and the proportion of total proteins with HI-associated domain architectures were compared for genus-representative genomes between different taxonomic groupings using one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Taxonomic partitions within early-diverging fungal lineages were largely defined following highlighted taxonomic groupings40.

A phylogenetic linear regression model was fit to assess the relationship between fungal BGC-ome size with genome size, the count of total proteins and the count of predicted carbohydrate-processing proteins using the phylolm package in R. The BGC-ome and genome size were provided in Mb units. Pagel’s lambda model was used to model the covariance.

Additional details on other statistical models and tests can be found in Supplementary Methods.
