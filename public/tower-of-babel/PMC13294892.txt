Enhanced Explosion Characteristics of Methane/Air Induced by Barium Nitrate under a Large Horizontal Pipeline
Authors: Weifu Sun, Yangchaoyue Chen, Dafang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High explosion pressure is desired for fracturing shale to extract shale gas. In this work, the role of barium nitrate (Ba­(NO3)2) in enhancing the explosion characteristics of CH4–O2–N2 mixtures by a 500 L horizontal pipeline has been explored. First, the key factors that influence explosion characteristics, including particle size and loading amount, have been studied. It is found that there exist two optimum formulations under which the explosion overpressure can be enhanced significantly. They are particle diameters of 65.3 and 29.2 μm corresponding to loading amounts of 100 and 75 g, respectively. Furthermore, the effects of different equivalence ratios under the two optimum formulations and the underlying improved mechanisms of Ba­(NO3)2 have been probed. The results demonstrate that the loading approach of spraying Ba­(NO3)2 into methane not only enhances the explosion overpressure and average pressure rise rate but also greatly shortens the pressure rise time. Besides, the pure experimental gas reaches its maximum overpressure when the equivalence ratio of methane is 1, whereas the maximum overpressure after adding Ba­(NO3)2 is attained at a methane equivalence ratio of 1.25. Given a methane concentration of 13% and an equivalence ratio of 1.25, a maximum explosion overpressure of 22.52 MPa with an average pressure rise rate of 2.104 GPa/s has been achieved. These findings provide methodological and data support for the development of explosive fracturing technology in horizontal and vertical wells.

Introduction

The production and utilization of shale gas have been considered one of the promising approaches to address energy shortages and achieve a low-carbon economy. − However, as the mainstream mining technology of shale gas, hydraulic fracturing technology is confronted with several demerits, including the heavy consumption and pollution of water resources. Newly emerging in situ methane explosive fracturing is regarded as a promising technology because of its several advantages, such as zero pollution from combustion products, low cost, elimination of the transport process of methane, etc., to extract deep shale gas by increasing the permeability of the deep shale reservoir. − Therefore, the crux of implementing this technology lies in achieving as high an explosion overpressure as possible to fracture the shale without fragmentation.

The initial conditions of a premixed gas, including the initial pressure and concentration, play a crucial role in its explosion characteristics. It has been discovered that the explosion overpressure is proportional to the initial pressure, and the maximum can be attained close to the stoichiometric ratio. − For example, when the concentration of methane increases from 8.5 to 9.5% volume fraction, the explosion overpressures at the pipe end are 1.27 and 1.849 MPa, respectively. For a given initial pressure of 0.6 MPa, the maximum explosion overpressure of methane–air (CH4–O2–N2) mixtures can almost reach 5 MPa. Besides, the temperature and turbulence are also key factors in the influence of explosion overpressure. − Cui et al. discovered that there exists a negative correlation between the initial temperature and the maximum explosion overpressure. In particular, under identical circumstances, except for the temperature declining from 273 to 123 K, the maximum explosion overpressure has increased by a factor of 43.4%. In contrast, a turbulent environment plays a positive role in enhancing explosion overpressure compared with a quiescent environment. Therefore, the explosion overpressure of methane can be enhanced by increasing the initial pressure, decreasing the temperature, and creating a turbulent environment. However, it is difficult to change these intrinsic states of premixed gas in shale gas mining.

Apart from varying the initial conditions of premixed gas, the explosion overpressure can also be enhanced by adjusting external parameters, such as altering the ignition energy − and adding a flammable gas. − For instance, the work conducted by Ajrash et al. indicates that the maximum explosion overpressure of methane has been enhanced by 8.3 times when the ignition energy is increased to 10 KJ from 1 KJ. Zhang et al. found that with the proportion of H2 increasing by 20%, the overpressure increased by 32%. Additionally, aluminum powder can also be adopted to enhance the methane explosion overpressure. − However, although the explosion intensity by these methods is improved, the generated overpressure is still inadequate to meet the needs of fracturing the shale.

In 2011, a major discovery manifested that methane would produce an anomalous overpressure, which can reach approximately 24 MPa at atmospheric pressure and room temperature under pure oxygen conditions, and this phenomenon is named combustion-induced rapid phase transition (CRPT). , The reason for the occurrence of CRPT arises from the rapid evaporation and condensation of the water produced by the combustion reaction. , Previous research studies have demonstrated that the CRPT phenomenon provides a solid foundation for the anomalous oscillating overpressure of methane, and this high overpressure can be generated under some specific conditions, such as an oxygen-rich atmosphere. , As a result, the oxidants that can provide a sufficient oxygen environment should be considered to enhance the overpressure. Because of the thickness and disordered natural cracks of reservoirs, the gas and liquid oxidants can hardly be transported safely into deep shale reservoirs. By contrast, the solid oxidant not only takes up a small space but is also easier to transport. Its decomposition, subjected to the high temperature generated by the explosion, releases oxygen, which thus provides a high concentration of oxygen for the whole explosion process to improve the explosion intensity. For example, the maximum explosion overpressure of 30.64 MPa has been attained when both Al and KMnO4 with a diameter of 20 μm are added to methane. Although the enhanced overpressure by adding KMnO4 is adequate to fracture shale, the decomposition of KMnO4 produces K2O and MnO ultimately, which will be a new threat to the surrounding environment. Therefore, it is necessary to find another solid oxidant that is environmentally friendly. By contrast, the final decomposition products of Ba­(NO3)2 include N2 and O2, which are friendly to the environment. Although the oxygen element content of KMnO4 is high (up to 40.5%), the content of oxygen element that can be converted to oxygen gas is actually only 25.3%; the other oxygen element will convert to K2O and MnO. In contrast, the proportion of oxygen element in Ba­(NO3)2 that can be transformed to oxygen gas reaches up to 30.65%, and the density of Ba­(NO3)2 is higher than that of KMnO4, indicating that Ba­(NO3)2 has a larger oxygen release per unit volume than KMnO4.

In this work, Ba­(NO3)2 is used as the combustion improver to explore the explosion characteristics of methane. Experiments are performed using a 500 L horizontal pipeline under CH4–O2–N2 mixtures. First, the factors that influence explosion characteristics, such as particle size, loading amount, and the specific surface area of the solid oxidant, have been studied. It is found that there are two formulations with which the explosion overpressure can be enhanced significantly. Additionally, the effects of different methane equivalence ratios on explosion characteristics under the two optimum formulations and the underlying improved mechanisms of Ba­(NO3)2 were probed.

Experimental Section

Experimental Setup

The experiment is mainly performed using a 500 L pipeline with a 16 m length and an inner diameter of 0.2 m, as shown in Figure . In addition, the experimental device also includes a filling and exhaust module, an ignition device, a spraying module of chemicals, and a data acquisition module. In total, eight sensors are installed on the pipeline. Sensors with numbers I to V are located at 1.4, 2.8, 4.2, 5.6, and 7.0 m along the pipeline with an interval of 1.4 m, and numbers VI to VIII are located at 9.8, 12.6, and 15.4 m with 2.8 m intervals to monitor the overpressure at the end of the pipeline. Note that all experiments have been conducted at RT and atmospheric pressure, and each experiment is repeated more than 3 times.

Setup of a 500 L horizontal pipeline for gas–liquid–solid multiphase fuel explosion.

At first, experimental gases and combustion improvers are loaded into the pipeline and chemical bin, respectively. The exhaust air method is used to fill the experimental gases, which can effectively prevent combustion improvers from being sucked into the pipeline during gas filling. After that, the gases need to be premixed for at least 30 min to ensure their uniformity. Second, the air is not stopped from being injected into the gas tank by the air compressor until the internal pressure of the tank reaches 0.8 MPa. Subsequently, the data acquisition instrument and power controller are started. The sampling rate of the data acquisition device is 10 kHz, and the energy of ignition is 40 J. Both the ignition and solenoid valve are triggered by the power controller, and chemical spraying is activated by the solenoid valve. When the power controller is started, the spraying module is first activated, driving the combustion improver into the pipeline to form a dust cloud, which explodes with the experimental gases when ignition is triggered after 320 ms. At last, the experimental data are saved, and the exhaust gases are discharged. After each experiment is finished, the chemical bin and pipeline are checked to see whether any combustion improver is left; if so, it is cleaned up. The chemical bin can be opened to sweep, whereas the pipeline can be cleaned by opening its end and injecting high-pressure air to flush out any remaining combustion improver. More detailed parameters and design basis of the experimental device can be obtained from previous work.

The experimental conditions were divided into three groups. First, group I is conducted to observe the explosion characteristics of Ba­(NO3)2 itself. Second, group II focuses on exploring the effects of different particle diameters and loading amounts of Ba­(NO3)2 on the explosion characteristics of methane when the equivalence ratio of methane is 0.75 to find the optimum formulations (particle diameter and loading amount) in which the overpressure can be enhanced significantly. At last, group III studies the influence of different equivalence ratios of methane on explosion characteristics under optimum formulations.

In this work, Ba­(NO3)2 needs to be suspended in air to produce dust clouds and explode with experimental gases; the particle diameter of the dust is one of the important factors in generating suspension. Because of a relatively large particle diameter, it is difficult for pristine Ba­(NO3)2 to form dust clouds, but its particle diameter can be reduced by mechanical grinding. When Ba­(NO3)2 is ground into smaller powders, its particle diameter becomes smaller, whereas the specific surface area (s) increases. Irregular block powders are regarded as approximately spherical, and the specific surface area can be calculated by eq . 1 s=SρV where s, S, ρ, and V are the specific surface area, surface area, density, and volume of Ba­(NO3)2 in the units of m2/g, m2, g/cm3, and m3, respectively. ρ(Ba­(NO3)2) = 3.24 g/cm3.

The morphology and particle diameter of Ba­(NO3)2 after mechanical grinding have been characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The power of the machine is 1600 W. Due to the nonuniformity of mechanical grinding, particles have various sizes; therefore, particle diameters are estimated to obtain a particle diameter distribution figure. It shows that the particle diameter of pristine Ba­(NO3)2 is large, which can reach up to 183.2 μm, and most of them are irregular blocks.

With increasing grinding time, the particle diameter gradually decreases (Figure ). Results and Discussion

Effect of Ba­(NO3)2 on the Explosion Characteristics of Methane

Prior to exploring the effect of Ba­(NO3)2 on the explosion characteristics of methane, the explosion characteristics of methane, including the maximum explosion overpressure (p max) and average pressure rise rate (dp/dt avg) of Ba­(NO3)2, are first explained.

Explosion Characteristics of Ba­(NO3)2

The maximum explosion overpressure (p max) and average pressure rise rate (dp/dt avg) using different particle diameters of Ba­(NO3)2 have been studied, and the results are shown in Figure . The p max values are 0.01869, 0.02588, 0.02211, 0.02431, and 0.01722 MPa, corresponding to particle diameters of 183.2, 79.1, 65.3, 29.2, and 12.3 μm, respectively. All of these pressure values are slightly higher than atmospheric pressure, and the corresponding dp/dt avg values are 0.03749, 0.06417, 0.02898, and 0.03051 MPa/s, respectively. This indicates that individual Ba­(NO3)2 mixed with oxygen will not explode after ignition. In short, Ba­(NO3)2 cannot explode by itself, but acts as a catalyst in its reaction with methane. As an oxidant, it provides a high concentration of oxygen for the whole reaction. The reason for the rise of the pressure curve in Figure a is the high-pressure air in the gas tank, and the Ba­(NO3)2 powders will be taken into the pipeline by virtue of the high-pressure air. Figure b,c shows that across different particle diameters, the maximum explosion overpressure and pressure rise rate remain almost unchanged. After clarifying the explosion characteristics of Ba­(NO3)2 itself, the effect of Ba­(NO3)2 on the explosion characteristics of methane can be better revealed.

Effect of Ba­(NO3)2 on p max

To explore the effect of the properties of Ba­(NO3)2, including loading amount (m), particle diameter (d), and specific surface area (s), on the maximum explosion pressure (p max) in a direct manner, a comparison has been made, as shown in Figure .

Maximum explosion overpressure as a function of (a) loading amount, (b) particle diameter, and (c) specific surface area under different loading amounts. The error bar represents the standard deviation of the explosion characteristics in parallel tests.

As observed in Figure a, when the particle diameters are 183.2, 79.1, and 65.3 μm, p max always increases with increasing loading amount from 0 to 100 g, but when the particle diameter is reduced to 29.2 and 12.3 μm, p max first increases and then decreases with the increase of loading amount. This shows that both diameter and loading amount have a joint effect on p max. Therefore, the influence of particle diameter on p max is further discussed, as displayed in Figure b.

Figure b shows the influence of the particle diameter of Ba­(NO3)2 on the maximum explosion overpressure. On one hand, with the decrease of particle diameter, p max first increases and then decreases. For example, when the particle diameters are 183.2, 79.1, 65.3, 29.2, and 12.3 μm at a loading amount of 100 g, the corresponding p max values are 3.876, 5.04, 11.857, 6.11, and 5.17 MPa, respectively. The reason for the occurrence of this phenomenon is that when pristine Ba­(NO3)2 is ground into a smaller particle size, its specific surface area increases. Thus, smaller particles will have a larger area in contact with methane. However, when the particle diameter of Ba­(NO3)2 decreases further, the agglomeration phenomenon occurs, resulting in a larger agglomerated particle diameter during the spraying process. At this point, the contact area of the reaction between Ba­(NO3)2 and methane becomes smaller, and the reactants reduce, leading to a decrease in p max. Moreover, the influence of the specific surface area on p max is also discussed (Figure c). It is similar to particle diameter: with the increase of specific surface area, p max also first increases and then decreases.

On the other hand, there always exists an optimal formulation (particle diameter and loading amount) that can greatly enhance the explosion overpressure. In 10 vol % CH4–O2–N2 mixtures at an equivalence ratio of 0.75, there are two formulations that can enhance the explosion overpressure significantly, i.e., particle diameters of 65.3 and 29.2 μm correspond to loading amounts of 100 and 75 g, respectively. The former formulation leads to 11.87 MPa, a 3.7 times increase compared to the p max (3.207 MPa) of pure methane explosion, and the latter formulation results in 8.978 MPa, a 2.8 times increase. It seems that a larger particle diameter generates p max at a high loading amount, and a smaller particle diameter produces p max at a low loading amount. Under different loading amounts, the particle diameter that produces p max is not unique. When the loading amount is too much, the smaller particles are squeezed tightly together, and the agglomeration phenomenon becomes more obvious. On the contrary, the agglomeration phenomenon of larger particles is inferior to that of smaller particles at a high loading amount. Consequently, p max can be generated at a larger particle diameter and a high loading amount, and a smaller particle diameter can produce the maximum explosion overpressure at a lower loading amount, since the particles are dispersed readily. SEM has been used in a bid to observe the specific morphology of Ba­(NO3)2 after mechanical grinding (Figure ). With increasing grinding time, the particle diameter becomes smaller, but the agglomeration phenomenon becomes more and more obvious, as shown in Figure , especially for the cases of 6 and 8 s.

Agglomeration phenomenon of Ba­(NO3)2 under different grinding times.

Effect of Ba­(NO3)2 on dp/dt avg

Apart from the effect of Ba­(NO3)2 on the maximum explosion overpressure, it is necessary to explore the effect of Ba­(NO3)2, including loading amount (m), particle diameter (d), and specific surface area (s), on the average pressure rise rate (dp/dt avg). Figure a shows the effect of the loading amount on dp/dt avg when Ba­(NO3)2 is ground into different particle diameters. The rate of the chemical reaction of reactants in a closed pipeline is one of the important factors affecting dp/dt avg. When the particle diameters of Ba­(NO3)2 are 183.2 and 65.3 μm, dp/dt avg is positively associated with loading amount. However, when the particle diameter of Ba­(NO3)2 is decreased to 29.2 and 12.3 μm, dp/dt avg first increases and then decreases with increasing loading amount. When the loading amount of the small particle is too much, dp/dt avg begins to drop. The main reason is that when the loading amount is too high, small particles of Ba­(NO3)2 in the chemical bin agglomerate together. Due to the strong attractive force between small particles, they adhere to each other or to the surface of larger particles. Therefore, the concentration of dust clouds formed during the actual spraying process is reduced, and both the chemical reaction rate and average pressure rise rate decrease.

Average pressure rise rate at different loading amounts (a), particle diameters (b), and specific surface areas (c). The error bar represents the standard deviation of the explosion characteristics in parallel tests.

Consequently, there exists an optimum formulation in which the concentration of dust clouds is the highest during the actual spraying process, and the average pressure rise rate can be enhanced to the fastest. Figure b shows the influence of particle diameter on dp/dt avg. It can be found that with decreasing particle diameter, dp/dt avg increases first and then decreases. Notably, when the particle diameter of Ba­(NO3)2 is 65.3 μm at a loading amount of 100 g, dp/dt avg can reach up to 1.692 GPa/s. Similar to the trend of particle diameter, the influence trend of specific surface area on dp/dt avg first increases and then decreases.

Effect of Different Equivalence Ratios of Methane

Based on the above analysis, two optimum formulations have been obtained. At a methane equivalence ratio of 0.75, the particle diameters of formulations I and II are 65.3 and 29.2 μm, respectively, and the corresponding loading amounts are 100 and 75 g, respectively. Subsequently, the effects of different equivalent ratios of methane on p max and dp/dt avg have been studied under the two optimum formulations. Figure shows the effect of the two optimum formulations on overpressure at different equivalence ratios of methane. It is found that the p max under formulation I is always higher than that under formulation II. When the equivalence ratios of methane are 0.50, 0.75, 1.0, 1.25, and 1.5, the corresponding explosion overpressures under formulation I are 7.739, 11.870, 15.867, 22.520, and 3.168 MPa, respectively. The overpressure reaches a maximum of 22.520 MPa when the equivalence ratio of methane is 1.25.

Effect of Different Equivalence Ratios on p max

The effects of pure methane and the two optimum formulations of Ba­(NO3)2 on the maximum explosion overpressure at different methane equivalence ratios are shown in Figure . It can be found that pure methane reaches its maximum overpressure of 8.732 MPa when the equivalence ratio of methane is 1. However, the maximum overpressure after adding Ba­(NO3)2 is attained at a methane equivalence ratio of 1.25. The p max values under formulations I and II can be enhanced up to 22.52 and 15.739 MPa, respectively.

Maximum explosion overpressure for pure methane and formulations I and II at different equivalence ratios of methane, including the effects of the equivalence ratio (a) and the formulation of the combustion improver (b). The error bar represents the standard deviation of the explosion characteristics in parallel tests.

The main reason is that when the methane equivalence ratio is 1.25, the actual amount of methane required for oxygen to be completely consumed is greater than the theoretical amount. That is, methane is in a fuel-rich state. As an oxidant, the decomposition of Ba­(NO3)2 generates oxygen, which reacts with extra methane. Therefore, the explosion overpressure reaches the maximum at an equivalence ratio of greater than 1 at 1.25. Nevertheless, when the equivalence ratio of methane is further increased to 1.5, the overpressure shows a sharp decline. For instance, under formulation I, methane equivalence ratios of 1.25 and 1.5 correspond to the maximum explosion overpressures of 22.52 and 3.168 MPa, respectively. This is because it exceeds the upper explosion limit of methane, where the methane only burns instead of exploding. The overpressure of methane burning is considerably lower than that of an explosion; therefore, the overpressure drops dramatically when the equivalence ratio of methane is 1.5.

Effects of Different Equivalence Ratios on dp/dt avg

Similarly, the influence of different equivalence ratios of methane on the average pressure rise is analyzed (Figure ). Consistent with the effect of different methane equivalence ratios on p max, the dp/dt avg of pure methane also reaches its maximum of 0.5246 GPa/s at an equivalence ratio of 1. However, after adding Ba­(NO3)2, dp/dt avg reaches the maximum at an equivalence ratio of 1.25 instead of 1. The values of dp/dt avg under formulations I and II reach 2.104 and 1.60 GPa/s, respectively. As compared to the case of pure methane at an equivalence ratio of 1, the addition of Ba­(NO3)2 provides more oxygen to react with the superfluous methane when the equivalence ratio of methane is 1.25. Therefore, dp/dt avg can be enhanced to the maximum after adding Ba­(NO3)2 at a methane equivalence ratio of 1.25. However, dp/dt avg has a dramatic drop from 2.104 to 0.049 GPa/s when the equivalence ratio is further increased from 1.25 to 1.5 under formulation I. The main reason is that it exceeds the upper explosion limit of methane, and methane only burns instead of exploding at this time. The chemical reaction rate of methane burning is far below that of methane explosion, resulting in a sharp decline of dp/dt avg.

Improved Mechanism of Ba­(NO3)2 on Methane Explosion Characteristics

Figure shows the decomposition products of Ba­(NO3)2 and the methane–oxygen reaction in the high-temperature environment. At high temperatures, Ba­(NO3)2 first decomposes into Ba­(NO2)2 and O2 and then further decomposes into BaO, N2, and O2. Methane reacts with oxygen to form H2O and CO2. During this period, the methane explosion releases a large amount of heat, causing a rise in temperature, the acceleration of molecular thermal motion, and an increase in intermolecular collision; hence, the pressure is enhanced. Besides, as an oxidant, the decomposition of Ba­(NO3)2 provides a high concentration of oxygen for the methane reaction, which not only increases the concentration of reactants in the reaction but also creates an important condition to generate the CRPT phenomenon. , Notably, because the decomposition reaction of barium nitrate generally takes longer than the combustion reaction of methane and oxygen, the combustion-enhancing effect of barium nitrate is most pronounced in the middle and rear sections of the horizontal pipeline. In the front section of the pipeline, as heat is released from the combustion of methane and oxygen, barium nitrate gradually decomposes and begins to create a high-concentration oxygen atmosphere; after the flame and shock wave reach the middle and rear sections of the pipeline, methane can burn fully in the high-concentration oxygen atmosphere, releasing intense heat. Especially in lean mixtures, where incomplete combustion and residual methane are present, the combustion-enhancing effect of barium nitrate is even more pronounced. At the same time, the decomposition of Ba­(NO3)2 also generates gas products, including N2, besides O2, whereas the increase of gas products also contributes to the increase of pressure, since the volume of the pipeline is constant. Figure also indicates the improved mechanism of the whole reaction when Ba­(NO3)2 is sprayed into the methane. The decomposition of Ba­(NO3)2 by heat provides more oxygen for methane, and the methane explosion creates a high-temperature environment for the decomposition of Ba­(NO3)2. The water generated by the methane explosion turns into H2O­(g) at high temperatures, and the H2O­(g) quickly condenses into H2O­(l) when it encounters a cold pipe wall, resulting in the CRPT phenomenon. Under the synergistic action of methane and Ba­(NO3)2, the overpressure of the methane explosion is enhanced tremendously.

Mechanism of Ba­(NO3)2 on enhancing methane explosion characteristics.

Besides, as an oxidant, oxygen can only originate from the oxygen element of Ba­(NO3)2. The amount of oxygen plays a crucial role in the reaction. Therefore, EDS mapping has been used to measure the contents of the oxygen element of Ba­(NO3)2 subjected to different grinding times, as shown in Figure . The distributions of three elements of pristine Ba­(NO3)2 with a particle diameter of 183.2 μm are shown in Figures a–d, and the atomic ratio of oxygen element is 66.8% (Figure e). Subsequently, with the increase of grinding time, the amount of oxygen element in ground Ba­(NO3)2 subjected to different grinding times decreases gradually, as shown in Figure f. Ba­(NO3)2 is ground by a machine with a power of 1600 W, and the mechanism of grinding mainly relies on the high-speed rotation of the slice blade. The set of grinding times decides the time of blade rotation. The higher the grinding time, the longer the blade rotates. When the blade rotates at a high speed, it releases heat, and the decomposition of Ba­(NO3)2 is mainly influenced by temperature. Under the heat released by the high-speed grinding, Ba­(NO3)2 decomposes into Ba­(NO2)2 and O2, thus resulting in the loss of part of the oxygen element. Longer blade rotation results in greater heat release, leading to more oxygen escaping. Consequently, the decrease of oxygen with the increase of grinding time is also one of the reasons for the reduction of overpressure.

Conclusions

In this work, the improved effects of Ba­(NO3)2 on the explosion characteristics of CH4–O2–N2 mixtures by a 500 L pipeline have been explored. To begin with, the influence of particle diameter, loading amount, and specific surface area on the maximum explosion overpressure and average pressure rise rate has been discussed. It is found that there are two optimum formulations in which the explosion overpressure can be enhanced significantly. In addition, the influence of different methane equivalence ratios on explosion characteristics under the two optimum formulations and the underlying improved mechanisms of Ba­(NO3)2 have been probed. The findings generated in this work not only provide an effective method to enhance the methane explosion overpressure but also facilitate the development of in situ methane explosive fracturing technology. The main conclusions are as follows:(1)The loading approach of spraying Ba­(NO3)2 into methane not only enhances the maximum explosion overpressure (p max) and the average pressure rise rate (dp/dt avg), but also greatly shortens the pressure rise time. At a 10% volume fraction and a 0.75 equivalence ratio of methane, there are two optimum formulations in which overpressure and dp/dt avg can be enhanced significantly. They are particle diameters (d) of 65.3 and 29.2 μm corresponding to loading amounts of 100 and 75 g, respectively. This approach holds great promise in horizontal and vertical wells for fracturing shale reservoirs.
