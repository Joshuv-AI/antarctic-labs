Abstract
. We give an online algorithm that keeps a symmetric random walk inside a convex body by discarding some of its steps. The expected number of discarded steps is controlled by a Fisher-information-type quantity associated with the body. For the cube, this gives a dimension-free bound: a walk with unit Euclidean steps can be kept bounded in all coordinates while discarding only a small constant fraction of the steps on average.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Random walks

Consider a symmetric random walk in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with given steps:

S_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j},\quad k=1,2,\ldots

where
\varepsilon_{j}=\pm 1
are independent Rademacher random variables and
v_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are given vectors. If the steps
v_{j}
have unit length, the random walk drifts arbitrarily far from the origin over time. In particular, it exits any given compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in finite time almost surely.

At what cost can one keep the random walk inside
K
indefinitely? In particular, can this be achieved by discarding only a small fraction of the steps?

1.2.
Fisher information

A possible answer can be formulated in terms of the information content of
K
. A fundamental notion in statistics is the Fisher information. For a family of probability densities
p_{\theta}(\cdot)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, parametrized by a vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the
Fisher information matrix
is a symmetric, positive-semidefinite
m\times m
matrix defined by

\mathcal{I}(\theta)\coloneqq\E\big(\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(X)\big)\big(\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(X)\big)^{\mathsf{T}},\quad\text{where}\quad X\sim p_{\theta}.

The random vector
\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(X)
is called the
score
.
It measures the sensitivity of the log-likelihood
\log p_{\theta}(X)
to
infinitesimal changes in the parameter
\theta
. Under the usual regularity
assumptions, the score has mean zero. Hence
\mathcal{I}(\theta)
is the covariance
matrix of the score.

Thus, for any given unit vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the quadratic form

v^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{I}(\theta)v

is the variance of the score in the direction of
v
. It quantifies how much information the data carries about the parameter
\theta
along that direction.

As an example, let’s fix one probability density
\pi(\cdot)
and consider the family of all translates:

p_{\theta}(x)=\pi(x+\theta),\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then the Fisher information matrix, evaluated at the origin, is

(1.1)

\mathcal{I}_{\pi}\coloneqq\mathcal{I}(0)=\E\big(\nabla\log\pi(X)\big)\big(\nabla\log\pi(X)\big)^{\mathsf{T}},\quad\text{where}\quad X\sim\pi.

1.3.
Discrepancy

The following is our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex, centrally symmetric set with nonempty interior. Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be fixed vectors, and let
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
be independent Rademacher random variables. Let
\pi(\cdot)
be a probability density supported in the interior of
K
. Then there exists an online algorithm that discards some steps
\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}
such that, for each
k
,

\sum_{j\leq k\text{ accepted}}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}\in 2K\quad\text{deterministically},

and

(1.2)

\E\#\{\text{discarded}\}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{v_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{I}_{\pi}v_{j}},

where
\mathcal{I}_{\pi}
denotes the Fisher information matrix defined in (
1.1
).

The proof will be given in Section
2
and is based on the Metropolis algorithm with stationary distribution
\pi
. Intuitively, when
v_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{I}_{\pi}v_{j}
is small, the density
\pi
is only weakly affected by the next step
\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}
, so this step is unlikely to be rejected by the Metropolis rule.

We will now explain how to choose a suitable
\pi
in the theorem, and then specialize it to the case of the cube
K=[-T,T]^{d}
.

1.4.
Information capacity

To get a more handy bound in Theorem
1.1
, note that

v_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{I}_{\pi}v_{j}\leq\lVert\mathcal{I}_{\pi}\rVert\lVert v_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2},

where
\lVert\mathcal{I}_{\pi}\rVert
denotes the operator norm of
\mathcal{I}_{\pi}
. Now we can optimize the density
\pi
, and get:

\E\#\{\text{discarded}\}\leq\frac{1}{2}I(K)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lVert v_{j}\rVert_{2},

where
I(K)
is the “information capacity” of
K
, defined as

I(K)\coloneqq\inf_{\supp(\pi)\subset K}\lVert\mathcal{I}_{\pi}\rVert^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The infimum is over smooth probability densities
\pi
supported in the interior of
K
.

1.5.
Dirichlet eigenvalues

It may not be easy to compute (approximately or exactly) the information content

I(K)
for a given domain
K
. The operator norm is not an easy quantity to handle, and it is not clear how to find a density
\pi
that makes it small. To get around this problem, let’s instead of the operator norm consider a simpler
quantity – trace. Assume that

\pi=\psi^{2}

for some function
\psi
supported in the interior of
K
. Hence we have

\int_{K}\psi^{2}=\int_{K}\pi=1\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\log\pi(x)=\frac{2\nabla\psi(x)}{\psi(x)}.

Substitute
x=X
to express the Fisher information matrix (
1.1
) as

(1.3)

\mathcal{I}_{\pi}=4\int_{K}\nabla\psi(x)\nabla\psi(x)^{\mathsf{T}}\,dx.

Then

(1.4)

\tr\mathcal{I}_{\pi}=4\int_{K}\lVert\nabla\psi(x)\rVert_{2}^{2}\,dx.

The integral on the right-hand side is the Dirichlet energy of
\psi
. The first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
K
is defined by

(1.5)

\lambda_{1}(K)=\inf\left\{\int_{K}\lVert\nabla\psi\rVert_{2}^{2}\,dx:\psi\in C^{\infty},\;\supp(\psi)\subset\operatorname{int}K,\;\int_{K}\psi^{2}\,dx=1\right\}.

Assume for now that

K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the closure
of a bounded connected domain with smooth boundary. This smoothness assumption
is sufficient for our application: any compact convex set with nonempty interior can be approximated by a smooth, convex, set, obtained by an arbitrarily small perturbation of
K
. For bounded, smooth
K
, this infimum is attained
1
1

1

The infimum in
(
1.5
) is attained in
W^{1,2}_{0}(K)
, the Sobolev closure of

C^{\infty}_{0}(K)
, where
C^{\infty}_{0}(K)
denotes the space of smooth functions
supported in
\operatorname{int}K
.
by the first Dirichlet eigenfunction; this eigenfunction satisfies:

(1.6)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=-\lambda_{1}(K)\psi&\text{in the interior of }K,\\
\psi=0&\text{on }\partial K,\end{cases}\qquad\int_{K}\psi^{2}=1.

Using this
\psi
in (
1.4
), we get:

(1.7)

\tr\mathcal{I}_{\pi}=4\lambda_{1}(K).

See
[
8
]
for the classical Dirichlet problem on smooth domains. The
work
[
11
]
covers substantially weaker assumptions; in particular, it applies to convex domains and allows one to bypass the smoothing step above.

Corollary 1.2

(Isotropic random walk)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact, convex, centrally symmetric set with nonempty interior. Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
be independent symmetric isotropic random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. (That is, for each
j
, the random vectors
v_{j}
and
-v_{j}
have the same distribution, and
\E v_{j}v_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}=I_{d}
.) Then there exists an online

2
2

2

Here “online”
means that, upon observing
v_{j}
,
one must immediately decide whether to accept or discard it; the decision may depend on the previous steps, but not on future steps, and it cannot be changed later.
algorithm that discards some steps
v_{j}
such that, for each
k
,

\sum_{j\leq k\text{ accepted}}v_{j}\in 2K\quad\text{deterministically},

and

\E\#\{\text{discarded}\}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(K)}\,n,

where
\lambda_{1}(K)
is the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
K
.

Proof.

By symmetry and independence, the random vectors
v_{j}
have the same joint distribution as
\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}
. Apply Theorem
1.1
conditionally on
(v_{j})
and for
\pi=\psi^{2}
, where
\psi
is the first Dirichlet eigenfunction of
K
. Then, by isotropy and (
1.7
), we have

\E\sqrt{v_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{I}_{\pi}v_{j}}\leq\sqrt{\E v_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{I}_{\pi}v_{j}}=\sqrt{\tr\mathcal{I}_{\pi}}=2\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(K)}.\qed

1.6.
Example: the cube

Let’s specialize to the benchmark domain
K
in combinatorial discrepancy theory – the cube, or a ball of the
\ell^{\infty}
norm.

Corollary 1.3

(Combinatorial discrepancy)
.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be any fixed vectors, and let
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
be independent Rademacher random variables. Then there exists an online algorithm that discards some terms
\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}
so that, for each
k
,

(1.8)

\Bigg\lVert\sum_{j\leq k\text{ accepted}}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}\Bigg\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2T\quad\text{deterministically},

and

\E\#\{\text{discarded}\}\leq\frac{\pi}{2T}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lVert v_{j}\rVert_{2}.

In particular:
if all
v_{j}
are unit vectors, then we can keep all the coordinates of the random walk bounded by
32
for any finite horizon by discarding at most
10\%
of terms on average.

Proof.

For the cube
K=[-T,T]^{d}
, the first Dirichlet eigenvalue and eigenfunction are

\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=T^{-d/2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi x_{i}}{2T}\right),\quad\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}(K)=\frac{\pi^{2}d}{4T^{2}}

Indeed, this pair solves the PDE (
1.6
), and the value
\lambda_{1}>0
above is the smallest for which a solution exists. Now choose the density
\pi=\psi^{2}
. Its Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{I}_{\pi}
, defined in (
1.3
), has zero off-diagonal entries and equal diagonal entries (by symmetry), so
\mathcal{I}_{\pi}
is a multiple of identity. Since
\tr\mathcal{I}_{\pi}=4\lambda_{1}(K)
by (
1.7
), it follows that

\mathcal{I}_{\pi}=\frac{4\lambda_{1}(K)}{d}I=\frac{\pi^{2}}{T^{2}}I

where
I
denotes the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Plugging into Theorem
1.1
completes the proof.
∎

Related results

Corollary

in
[

improves to

.

Beginning from the seminal work
[
2
]
, a substantial body of research has been devoted to discrepancy minimization, see e.g.
[

6
,
5
,

,

,

. These works study the

remain small. The novelty of Theorem

is that completely

1.8.
Optimality

For unit vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
, Corollary
1.3
says that one can maintain the low discrepancy bound (
1.8
) by discarding
O(n/T)
vectors. This discard rate is optimal even in dimension
d=1
. The next result shows that in order to keep the random walk within
[-T,T]
, one must discard at least
\Omega(n/T)
vectors.

Proposition 1.4

(Optimality)
.

Let
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
be independent Rademacher random variables.
Consider any discarding algorithm
3
3

3

Here, an algorithm can be any function that maps a sign string
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
to a subset of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
of the terms to be discarded.
(online or not) that maintains, for each
k
,

\Bigg\lvert\sum_{j\leq k\text{ accepted}}\varepsilon_{j}\Bigg\rvert\leq T\quad\text{deterministically}.

Then

\E\#\{\text{discarded}\}\geq\frac{n}{2T+1}-T.

We will prove this proposition in Section
3
by showing that the reflected random walk on
[-T,T]
achieves the smallest discard rate.

2.
Upper bound: proof of Theorem
1.1

Let
\pi
be a probability density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose support lies in the interior of
K
.
We use the Metropolis algorithm with the classically defined acceptance probability

(2.1)

a(x,y)\coloneqq\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi(x)}\wedge 1,

see e.g.
[
9
]
. Specifically,
let us arrange the discards as follows:

•

Sample
w_{0}\sim\pi
.

•

Upon receiving
\varepsilon_{k}v_{k}
, propose
w^{\prime}=w_{k-1}+\varepsilon_{k}v_{k}
.

•

With probability
a(w_{k-1},w^{\prime})
, set
w_{k}=w^{\prime}
; otherwise set
w_{k}=w_{k-1}
.

•

Repeat for
\varepsilon_{k+1}v_{k+1}
.

Since
\varepsilon_{k}
is a fair sign, the resulting Markov chain is reversible, and hence the distribution is preserved:

w_{k}\sim\pi\text{ for all }k.

Also, if
w^{\prime}\notin K
, then
\pi(w^{\prime})=0
, so the move is
rejected. Hence,

w_{k}\in K
for all
k
, and

w_{k}-w_{0}\in 2K\text{ for all }k,

as claimed. Moreover, from the algorithm we see that

(2.2)

\E\,\#\{\text{discarded}\}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\{\varepsilon_{k}v_{k}\text{ is discarded}\}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\E\bigl[1-a(w_{k-1},w_{k-1}+\varepsilon_{k}v_{k})\bigr].

We can compute the expectation above by conditioning on
\varepsilon_{k}v_{k}
, which is independent of

w_{k-1}\sim\pi
. Let’s do this computation separately.

For a random vector
X\sim\pi
and a fixed vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

(2.3)

\E a(X,X+v)\overset{\eqref{eq: axy}}{=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\pi(x+v)}{\pi(x)}\wedge 1\right)\pi(x)\,dx\\
=1-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\pi(x+v)-\pi(x)\rvert\,dx,

where the last equation follows from

\left(\frac{\pi(x+v)}{\pi(x)}\wedge 1\right)\pi(x)=\pi(x+v)\wedge\pi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\pi(x+v)+\pi(x)-\lvert\pi(x+v)-\pi(x)\rvert\Big].

Now,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\pi(x+v)-\pi(x)\rvert\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla\pi(x+tv),v\rangle\,dt\right\rvert dx\leq\int_{0}^{1}\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\langle\nabla\pi(x+tv),v\rangle\right\rvert\,dx}_{\text{does not depend on $t$}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\langle\nabla\pi(x),v\rangle\right\rvert\,dx=\E\left\lvert\frac{\langle\nabla\pi(X),v\rangle}{\pi(X)}\right\rvert\quad\text{(since $X\sim\pi$)}

\displaystyle=\E\left\lvert\langle\nabla\log\pi(X),v\rangle\right\rvert\leq\sqrt{\E\langle\nabla\log\pi(X),v\rangle^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{v^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{I}_{\pi}v}\quad\text{by definition of Fisher information matrix \eqref{eq: Ipi}.}

Plugging this into (
2.3
), we get

\E\bigl[1-a(X,X+v)\bigr]\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{v^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{I}_{\pi}v}.

Finally, substitute this into (
2.2
) to obtain (
1.2
). Theorem
1.1
is proved. ∎

3.
Lower bound: proof of Proposition
1.4

Without loss of generality, assume
T\geq 0
is an integer, since partial sums are integers. Given a walk with steps
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
starting at some point
s\in[-T,T]
, consider all ways of discarding steps so that the resulting walk remains within
[-T,T]
:

Definition 3.1

(
s
-valid subsequences)
.

Fix a sign sequence
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
and an integer
s\in[-T,T]
. We say that an index subsequence
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leq n
is
s
-valid if

\max_{1\leq k\leq m}\left|s+\sum_{r=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{i_{r}}\right|\leq T.

Denote by
\ell(\varepsilon,s)
be the maximum length of an
s
-valid index subsequence.

Lemma 3.2

(The starting point does not matter much)
.

\ell(\varepsilon,0)\leq\ell(\varepsilon,s)+\lvert s\rvert
.

Proof.

Assume
s>0
(the case
s<0
is symmetric). Let
I
be a longest
0
-valid subsequence. Scan
I
left-to-right and delete indices with
\varepsilon_{j}=+1
until
s
such indices are deleted (or none remain). The new subsequence
I^{\prime}
is
s
-valid; hence,
\ell(\varepsilon,0)\leq\lvert I^{\prime}\rvert+s\leq\ell(\varepsilon,s)+\lvert s\rvert
.
∎

A simple algorithm to produce an
s
-valid subsequence is to run a
reflected walk
on
[-T,T]
started at
s
:

•

Initialize
S\coloneqq s
and
I\coloneqq\emptyset
.

•

For
k=1,2,\dots,n
:

–

if
\lvert S+\varepsilon_{k}\rvert\leq T
, append
k
to
I
and update
S:=S+\varepsilon_{k}
;

–

otherwise, discard
k
.

Let us show that the reflected random walk outputs a longest
s
-valid subsequence. To show this, let
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}
and
j_{1}<\cdots<j_{m}
be two index subsequences of the same length. We define the lexicographic order by

(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})

if, at the first
k
with
i_{k}\neq j_{k}
, we have
i_{k}<j_{k}
.

Lemma 3.3

(Optimality of the reflected walk)
.

Fix
s\in[-T,T]
and
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
. The reflected walk on
[-T,T]
started at
s
produces the lexicographically smallest among all longest
s
-valid subsequences.

Proof.

Among all longest
s
-valid subsequences, let
I=(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m})
be lexicographically smallest. Fix
1\leq k\leq n
and set:

S:=s+\sum_{r:\,i_{r}<k}\varepsilon_{i_{r}}.

It suffices to show the following implication:

\lvert S+\varepsilon_{k}\rvert\leq T\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k\in I.

(Then, by induction, the reflected walk must produce
I
, proving the lemma.)

To show this, we use an exchange argument. Assume
\varepsilon_{k}=+1
(the other case is symmetric). Suppose for contradiction that
I
does not contain
k
while
S\leq T-1
.

Let
j>k
be the smallest index in
I

with
\varepsilon_{j}=+1
, if it exists. If no such
j
exists, insert
k
into
I
; the new sequence remains

s
-valid and is longer than
I
. This contradicts that
I
has maximum length.

Otherwise, form
I^{\prime}
by the index inserting

k
and deleting
j
. Then
I^{\prime}
is
s
-valid. It has the same length as
I
but is lexicographically smaller. This contradiction completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
1.4
. It suffices to consider the reflected walk, since Lemma
3.3
shows that any other discarding algorithm must discard at least as many terms.

From now on, let
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n})
be a vector of independent Rademacher random variables. Let
S_{0}
be an independent random variable uniformly distributed over the integers in
[-T,T]
. Let us run the reflected walk started at
S_{0}
:

S_{k}:=\begin{cases}S_{k-1}+\varepsilon_{k},&\text{if }\lvert S_{k-1}+\varepsilon_{k}\rvert\leq T\quad(\text{accept}),\\
S_{k-1},&\text{otherwise}\quad(\text{discard}).\end{cases}

A discard at time
k+1
occurs iff
(S_{k},\varepsilon_{k+1})=(T,+1)
or
(-T,-1)
. Hence:

\E\#\{\text{discards}\}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\big(\mathbb{P}\{S_{k}=T\}+\mathbb{P}\{S_{k}=-T\}\big)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}\{S_{k}=T\}=\frac{n}{2T+1}.

To see the last equality, note that the reflected random walk is reversible, so the uniform distribution on
\{-T,\ldots,T\}
is stationary for
S_{k}
, hence
\mathbb{P}\{S_{k}=T\}=1/(2T+1)
.

According to Lemma
3.3
, the reflected walk above has length
\ell(S_{0},\varepsilon)
, so we proved that

\E\ell(S_{0},\varepsilon)=n-\frac{n}{2T+1}.

Finally, by Lemma
3.2
, we have

\E\ell(\varepsilon,0)\leq\E\ell(\varepsilon,S_{0})+\E\lvert S_{0}\rvert\leq n-\frac{n}{2T+1}+T,

and the proof of Proposition
1.4
is complete.
