Reforming Medicare payment for skin substitutes—ripping off the bandage
Authors: Jad F Zeitouni, Xiaodan Hu, Sanket S Dhruva, Joseph S Ross, Vinay K Rathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn January 2026, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) implemented a major overhaul of Medicare reimbursement for skin substitutes in an effort to curb runaway spending. Between 2019 and 2024, annual Medicare Part B spending on these products increased more than 40-fold, from $250 million to more than $10 billion. Prior to the recent reform, CMS reimbursed clinicians for skin substitutes administered in nonfacility settings (eg, physician offices and patient homes) at a rate of an average sales price (ASP) of +6%. However, skin substitute manufacturers were omitted from federal ASP reporting requirements. Some manufacturers and clinicians exploited this exemption to enter spread pricing arrangements, whereby clinicians profited on substantial differences between Medicare reimbursement (calculated based on undiscounted list prices) and actual acquisition costs. The newly implemented payment policy eliminates the potential for spread pricing by setting a flat, site-neutral payment rate. In 2027, CMS intends to establish a 3-tiered payment system for skin substitute products based on US Food and Drug Administration regulatory classification. On January 1, 2026, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) completely overhauled reimbursement for a single medical product class: skin substitutes.1 Between 2019 and 2024, annual Medicare Part B spending on skin substitutes increased more than 40-fold, from $250 million to more than $10 billion (15% of total Part B drug spending).2 This rapid spending growth was spurred by perverse financial incentives and amplified by fraud, waste, and abuse.3,4 While these reforms represent an important step forward to address excess spending, additional steps may be necessary to achieve CMS's complementary goal of promoting evidence-based practice.

There are hundreds of commercially available skin substitute products, which are composed of synthetic, xenogeneic, or allogeneic materials. Skin substitutes are used for wound care following burns, acute injury (eg, trauma), and chronic healing failure. Among Medicare beneficiaries, the most common treatment indications are for chronic wounds such as diabetic foot and venous leg ulcers.5 Clinical consensus recommends that skin substitutes should only serve as an adjunctive, second-line treatment for chronic wounds when standard-of-care measures fail.5

US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) oversight varies widely by product regulatory classification. Most (∼80%) skin substitutes are Human Cells, Tissues, And Cellular and Tissue-Based Products (HCT/Ps),1 which are exempt from premarket review. The CMS permits manufacturers to self-determine whether products qualify for HCT/P categorization based on criteria meant to ensure safety (eg, same basic function as donor site). Most remaining products are regulated via the 510(k) process for moderate-risk medical devices, which typically only need nonclinical data for authorization. Very few skin substitute products are regulated through the Premarket Approval (PMA) pathway for high-risk devices, which requires premarket clinical evidence demonstrating safety and effectiveness. FDA-permitted use scales by regulatory classification: HCT/P products are only intended to protect or cover wounds, 510(k) products may additionally absorb exudate or maintain moisture balance, and PMA products may interact with wounds to promote healing.

Pre-reform Medicare fee-for-service payment for skin substitutes differed by care setting. In facility settings (eg, hospital outpatient departments), CMS bundled reimbursement for skin substitutes into facility fees, as it does for many devices. In nonfacility settings (eg, physician offices), CMS separately reimbursed clinicians at the statutory rate (average sales price [ASP] + 6%) for Part B biologics, even though FDA has not approved any skin substitutes as biologics. By law, manufacturers must report ASPs—defined as volume-weighted ASPs net of price concessions and rebates—for biologics to CMS quarterly. However, skin substitutes were historically omitted from reporting requirements. Some skin substitute manufacturers capitalized on this oversight by withholding price data from CMS, which calculates payment based on list prices or invoices when ASPs are unavailable.6 This information asymmetry enabled manufacturers and clinicians to profit from “spread pricing.” For example, the Department of Health and Human Services Office of Inspector General identified a skin substitute with list and undisclosed net prices of $1274/cm2 and $947/cm2 (>25% discount),2 respectively, which translates to $346/cm2 in Medicare overpayment ([list price – net price] × 106%) and $403/cm2 in clinician reimbursement above acquisition costs ([list price × 106%] – net price); Medicare fee-for-service spending on this single product was approximately $1.4 billion in 2023.

US Congress and CMS had previously made several attempts at reform. In 2022, Congress began mandating ASP reporting for skin substitutes, but the impact was limited by manufacturer noncompliance and product-hopping tactics, whereby manufacturers serially launched high-priced, but negligibly different product versions to continuously exploit the lag between ASP reporting and payment updates.2,6 In November 2024, CMS finalized a rule that would have restricted Medicare coverage for diabetic foot and venous leg ulcers to 17 products with published clinical evidence. However, CMS withdrew this policy in December 2025 before implementation.7 In June 2025, the CMS Innovation Center announced that skin substitutes were included in the new WISeR model, a pilot program evaluating artificial intelligence (AI)–enabled prior authorization in Medicare fee-for-service.8 The CMS intends to apply lessons in utilization management from the Medicare Advantage program, which spends approximately 93% less on skin substitutes due to a combination of reduced utilization (no. of beneficiaries: 3800 vs 24 000), lower intensity utilization (units per beneficiary: 69 vs 82), and less expensive product selection (price per cm2: $730 vs $1470).2

In contrast to prior reforms, CMS’ latest policy fundamentally alters how Medicare reimburses for skin substitutes by establishing a flat payment rate and implementing site-neutral payment. Medicare now pays for these products as “incident-to-supplies” (2026 rate: $127.28/cm2) across all care settings and will not reimburse clinicians for discarded amounts; this payment amount reflects the volume-weighted ASP of products utilized during quarter (Q) 4 2024 in facility settings, where bundled payments incentivize clinicians to balance clinical effectiveness and cost efficiency. Further, in 2027, CMS will establish a 3-tiered, flat-rate payment system based on FDA regulatory classification,1 reasoning that payment amounts should be commensurate with the extent of FDA's regulatory review and align with permitted uses. The CMS projects that the new policy will save Medicare nearly $20 billion in 2026, which exceeds estimated annual savings ($12 billion) from the most recent round of Medicare drug price negotiations. Such drastic spending reductions are plausible; the 2026 rate is less than 10% of prepolicy reimbursement,2 and reducing product profitability may also curb utilization by some clinicians. If CMS introduces a 3-tiered payment system as planned, this policy could also spur manufacturers to compete within product tiers by lowering prices or generating stronger evidence for use.

While these reforms will curb excess spending, CMS must navigate challenges ahead. The new payment methodology still reimburses clinicians on a per unit basis, thereby retaining incentives for overutilization; there is already substantial geographic variation in utilization rates (ie, penetrance), frequency (ie, no. of applications per beneficiary), and intensity (ie, no. of units per claim), which suggests provider-induced demand.9 In addition, CMS will need to reconcile the proposed 3-tiered payment system with the fact that more rigorous premarket review does not always guarantee more robust supporting clinical evidence for existing products with postmarket data. Under the recently withdrawn coverage determination, the majority of products for which CMS planned to provide evidence-based coverage were HCT/P products (HCT/P: 58.8%; 510(k): 23.5%; PMA: 17.6%).

The CMS positioned 2026 as a transitional year for skin substitute reimbursement and now seeks feedback. To further deter overutilization, CMS could consider several strategies. First, CMS could (re)bundle reimbursement into professional (nonfacility setting) and facility fees, which would deter incentives to utilize excess product. Second, CMS could broaden prior authorization requirements for skin substitutes by expanding the WISeR model to all states or directing Medicare administrative contractors to implement requirements, although the WISeR model is untested and prior authorization is unpopular with patients and providers. Third, CMS could reinstate the coverage determination policy withdrawn in December 2025, restricting reimbursement to products supported by clinical evidence of benefit. Fourth, CMS could partner with the FDA to identify HCT/P products being marketed or used for nonpermitted indications and take enforcement action against manufacturers, such as requiring violative products to undergo either 510(k) or PMA premarket review.

The CMS’ overhaul of skin substitute reimbursement was much needed to stop runaway Medicare spending on these largely underregulated and unproven products. As clinicians and manufacturers adapt to this sea change in reimbursement policy, close surveillance and additional reforms will likely be necessary to ensure financially sustainable delivery of more evidence-based wound care.
