Influenza-associated myocarditis: a case series and structured narrative review
Authors: Shuroug A. Alowais, Afnan Aljuhani, Mohammed D. Alotaibi, Abdulrahman Alyousef, Abdulaziz Alanazi, Mohamed Aziz, Lama Alfehaid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfluenza infection is a significant cause of global illness and death. Although mainly a respiratory disease, it can be complicated by extra-pulmonary conditions like myocarditis and myopericarditis. The prevalence and range of influenza-associated myocarditis are not well understood, especially in the Middle East, where case reports are limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective case series of adult patients admitted to King Abdulaziz Medical City in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, from September 2024 to July 2025, with clinically diagnosed influenza-associated myocarditis. Diagnosis was based on the 2024 ACC Expert Consensus Decision Pathway criteria and a positive influenza PCR. Clinical data, diagnostic results, management strategies, and outcomes were collected from electronic medical records. Additionally, we performed a structured literature review of case reports and case series on influenza-associated myocarditis to provide context for our findings.ResultsFour patients (ages 37–79 years; three women) were identified with clinically diagnosed influenza-associated myocarditis. Presentations ranged from mild myopericarditis with preserved hemodynamics to fulminant myocarditis complicated by cardiogenic shock requiring venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and Impella support. All patients received oseltamivir, and selected patients received colchicine, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or immunomodulatory therapy. Three patients recovered with normalization of cardiac function, while one patient with fulminant disease died despite aggressive support. The literature review identified 24 published cases of influenza-associated myocarditis. Most patients were previously healthy young adults (median age 42.5 years), and 79.2% achieved complete recovery. However, 20.8% died, often following rapid progression to cardiogenic shock and multi-organ failure.ConclusionInfluenza-associated myocarditis is a rare, potentially fatal complication affecting healthy adults. Our findings highlight the importance of early recognition, prompt antiviral therapy, and access to mechanical circulatory support when needed. This case series, among the first from Saudi Arabia, along with a literature review, offers insights into the clinical spectrum, management, and outcomes of this underrecognized condition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-026-03224-z.

Introduction

Influenza is an acute respiratory viral infection that has a significant impact on global health, caused by viruses belonging to the Orthomyxoviridae family, which are segmented, single-stranded RNA viruses [1]. Influenza viruses are classified into three types (A, B, and C) that differ in their viral RNA segments and typically manifest as a spectrum of respiratory symptoms, ranging from mild to severe and critical illness [2]. Acute respiratory viral infections, including influenza, are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) estimate that 3 to 5 million severe influenza cases occur worldwide, resulting in approximately 290,000 to 650,000 deaths annually [3]. In Saudi Arabia, influenza is responsible for approximately 0.8 deaths per 100,000 individuals [4]. Moreover, Al-Dorzi et al. reported that Intensive care unit (ICU) admission was needed in 22.4% of patients admitted with influenza, and the associated mortality was significant at 7.4% for all patients and 22.5% for ICU patients [5]. Although influenza is primarily a viral infection that is limited to the respiratory system, severe extra-pulmonary complications, such as cardiac complications, have been reported through all major pandemics, including Asian influenza in 1957, Hong Kong influenza in 1968, Russian influenza in 1977, and most recently the 2009 swine influenza pandemic [6]. Recent studies have reported an increase in the incidence of influenza-associated myocarditis since the 2009 influenza A (H1N1) outbreak. Consequently, most cases are associated with the influenza A virus, with only 8% of cases resulting from influenza B virus infection [7]. Moreover, fulminant influenza myocarditis is a fatal complication reported in approximately 10% of all myocarditis cases, which rapidly deteriorates into heart failure, arrhythmia, and cardiogenic shock within a few days [8]. In Japan, fulminant influenza myocarditis led to a fatal outcome with a reported mortality rate of 28% among myocarditis patients in the pandemic and post-pandemic periods [9]. Furthermore, Myopericarditis cases were more commonly associated with cardiogenic shock, requiring mechanical circulatory support (MCS) in 41.4% of cases and a mortality rate of 19% [10]. The prevalence of influenza-associated myocarditis and its complications remain unclear due to the limited number of reported cases and insufficient clinical data, particularly from the Middle East, where case series are scarce. Our study contributes to the literature by presenting one of the first detailed case series from Saudi Arabia, combined with a comprehensive review of previously published cases, to better characterize clinical presentation, management strategies, and outcomes. Specifically, we describe four cases of influenza-associated myocarditis and myopericarditis, highlighting the spectrum of severity and identifying common predisposing factors.

Methods

Study design and setting

This case series was conducted at King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC) in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, from September 1, 2024, to July 31, 2025. It aimed to describe the clinical characteristics, management strategies, and outcomes of patients diagnosed with influenza-associated myocarditis. A literature review was conducted to identify and synthesize previously reported cases of influenza-associated myocarditis and myopericarditis, enabling comparison with our case series regarding clinical presentation, management strategies, and outcomes.

Study participants

This case series included all adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with a clinically diagnosed influenza-associated myocarditis during the study period. Diagnosis was based on the American College of Cardiology’s Expert Consensus Decision Pathway on Strategies and Criteria for the Diagnosis and Management of Myocarditis [11], along with a concurrent positive viral polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for any influenza virus subtype.

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records, including demographic information, presenting symptoms, laboratory and imaging findings (e.g., cardiac biomarkers, electrocardiogram (ECG), echocardiography (Echo), cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR), treatment modalities, and in-hospital outcomes.

Literature review

A structured narrative literature review was conducted to contextualize our case series within the broader evidence base. The literature search was conducted in August 2025 through PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Google Scholar for studies published up to July 31, 2025, using combinations of the following keywords: “influenza,” “myocarditis,” “myopericarditis,” “fulminant myocarditis,” and “influenza-associated cardiac complications.” The literature search initially identified 100 records. After removing five duplicate records, titles and abstracts were screened for eligibility using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

A total of 37 conference abstracts, five review articles, and 29 studies involving pediatric populations or containing incomplete diagnostic data were excluded. Two authors independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Results

Case presentations

Between September 2024 and July 2025, six patients at our institution were clinically suspected of having influenza-associated myocarditis. Of these, four patients met the predefined diagnostic criteria, including a positive influenza viral PCR, and were included in the final case series. Table 1 summarizes the patients’ demographic characteristics, clinical presentations, diagnostic findings, management strategies, and clinical outcomes. Detailed descriptions of the individual cases are provided below.

Case 1

In December 2024, a 62-year-old female with no known medical history, who had just returned from the Al-Umrah pilgrimage in Makkah, presented to the Emergency Department (ED) with fatigue for the past 6 days. The patient also reported sharp chest pain and a productive cough that started three days ago, along with mild abdominal pain exacerbated by coughing. The patient had initially been presented at another small hospital, where she received antibiotics and was found to have an elevated troponin level of 700 pg/mL, prompting her to be transferred to our cardiac center for further investigation. Upon examination, her vital signs were stable, with a temperature of 36.5 °C, blood pressure (BP) of 102/92 mmHg, heart rate (HR) of 96 beats/min (bpm), a respiratory rate of 14 breaths/min, and a normal oxygen saturation (SaO2%) of 97%.

Laboratory tests were normal except for an elevated troponin I level at 2064.5 pg/ml, C-reactive protein (CRP) 11.4 mg/dl, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 52 mm/hr, and creatine kinase MB (CK-MB) 7.4 ug/L. A chest X-ray was performed and showed no significant findings. However, a chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a small pericardial effusion, indicating pericardium involvement (Fig. 2). A subsequent cardiac angiography showed mild coronary artery disease.

Fig. 2CT angiography of the chest showing a small pericardial effusion

CT angiography of the chest showing a small pericardial effusion

The initial Echo showed mild-to-moderate global hypokinesis of the left ventricle, with a Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 40%. The patient was admitted to the Medical Cardiac Intensive Care Unit (MCICU) for closer observation of her myopericarditis when she remained hemodynamically stable with normal renal function, started oral indomethacin 25 mg twice daily, and oral colchicine 0.6 mg twice daily. On day two, a respiratory multiplex panel returned a positive result for Influenza A Virus. Two hours later, the patient started on oral oseltamivir 75 mg every 12 h. The troponin level began trending downwards, reaching 942.8 pg/ml. Serial Echo showed stable findings with an LVEF of 45–50%. To further characterize myocardial involvement, the patient then underwent a CMR scan, which was consistent with myocarditis, showing myocardial edema as a high signal on T2-weighted images (Fig. 3). The Patient stayed under observation for days in the MCICU and was instructed to rest in bed and prescribed ivabradine to control HR and venous thrombosis prophylaxis. The patient was then transferred to a step-down unit for a day, then discharged with oseltamivir for 5 days, indomethacin for 2 weeks, and colchicine for 3 months.

Case 2

In March 2025, a 49-year-old female with a history of pre-diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and iron deficiency anemia presented to the ED with a one-week history of fever, shortness of breath, and a non-productive cough associated with chest pain. The patient received antibiotics at another hospital, though the specific agents were unknown. Despite this, her symptoms persisted, and she developed a productive cough.

On arrival at the ED, her vital signs were temperature 37.2 °C, HR 114 bpm, BP 135/60 mmHg, respiratory rate 22 breaths/min, and SaO2 95%. Initial laboratory tests revealed a high troponin I level of 1125.9 pg/ml. A chest x-ray showed worsening bilateral lower lung airspace opacities.

The Echo resulted in normal left and right ventricular sizes, with no pericardial effusion. Given the elevated troponin, a coronary angiogram via cardiac catheterization was performed, which showed mild coronary artery disease, and CMR showed a trace to small bilateral effusion with a trace to small pericardial effusion (Fig. 4). Later, a cardiac PET/CT scan revealed active inflammation in the lateral wall of the left ventricle.

A respiratory panel multiplex test was requested, which was positive for influenza A. Based on the findings of myocardial inflammation in the setting of a recent viral illness, the patient was diagnosed with myocarditis. Subsequently, the patient was transferred to the MCICU for close monitoring. The treatment plan for myocarditis was oseltamivir 75 mg every 12 h for 5 days (started 2 days after presentation), and colchicine 0.6 mg twice daily. Over the course of hospitalization, her symptoms were improving, and she was hemodynamically stable with BP 133/61 mmHg, HR 95 bpm, RR 20 Freq/m, and SpO2 98%. The troponin I level, which peaked at 18606.7 pg/mL, began to decline, and the patient was transferred to the CCU for closer monitoring. Following hospitalization, the patient demonstrated clinical improvement, with resolution of signs and symptoms and a steady decline in troponin I levels. Consequently, the patient was deemed stable for discharge. Later, the patient presented at a follow-up visit in May, where an Echo was done, showing a normal left ventricle size and function, LVEF of 55%, with a transmitral spectral Doppler flow pattern that is normal for his age and normal right ventricular size and function, with pulmonary arterial systolic pressure (PASP) of 25 mmHg.

Case 3

At the end of November 2024, a 79-year-old female with no known medical history presented to the ED with a four-day history of chest pain. The patient reported that her chest pain was central and radiated to both shoulders and her back. She stated that rest did not relieve the pain. She also reported episodes of vomiting and a loss of appetite. Two weeks prior, she experienced flu-like symptoms, and last week, she had a cough along with an upper respiratory tract infection.

Upon examination, she looked tired. Her BP was 88/60 mmHg, for which she received IV 0.9% normal saline. Upon auscultation, the chest showed equal bilateral air entry, with mild right-sided crackles. A chest x-ray showed hypoventilatory lungs with faint left retrocardiac opacity. ECG indicated low voltage QRS (68 ms), QT (294 ms), and a ventricular and atrial rate of 112 bpm. Laboratory tests showed an elevated troponin I level at 3335.6 pg/mL, creatine phosphokinase (CPK) at 1843 U/L, CK-MB at 19.2 µg/L, and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 38 mL/min/1.73 m².

The patient was immediately admitted to the MCICU for urgent cardiac catheterization. On the same day, an Echo was performed, which showed a reduced LVEF of 25%, severe hypokinesis of the left ventricle, mild dilation of the left ventricle, and severely reduced left ventricular systolic function. Cardiac angiography showed mild disease in the left main, left anterior descending, left circumflex, and right coronary arteries.

Due to hemodynamic instability, norepinephrine 0.1 mcg/kg/min was initiated, a septic work-up was performed, and piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g every 6 h was started empirically. The septic work-up included a blood culture and a respiratory multiplex panel, which tested positive for Influenza B, prompting the initiation of oseltamivir 30 mg orally BID. Troponin I levels were trending, reaching 6849.4 pg/ml, while lactic acid levels were trending from 5.9 to 2.41. The team planned to do a CMR to confirm the diagnosis of myocarditis once she was stable. On day 3, troponin I levels were trending down to 1,902.4 pg/mL, and lactic acid levels normalized to 1.8 mmol/L. On day four, the patient’s hemodynamics and kidney function improved, and the oseltamivir dose increased to 75 mg every 12 h. The Echo showed improvement in LVEF 47% with normalized right ventricular function and left ventricular global longitudinal strain (GLS) of -15.9. Piperacillin-tazobactam was stopped after day 8, and oseltamivir was stopped after day 7. The patient showed significant improvement and was stable enough to be discharged, with a plan to follow up within three months. However, the patient has never shown up at the follow-up visit.

Case 4

In January 2025, a 37-year-old female with a history of dyslipidemia and iron deficiency anemia presented to the ED complaining of epigastric pain that was worsening with lying associated with nausea, vomiting, chest pain, dizziness, and shortness of breath for the last 2 days. On arrival at the ED, the patient was hypotensive, BP 78/63 mmHg, and tachycardic with a HR up to 145 bpm and not responsive to fluid; she ultimately required the initiation of vasopressor. Her respiratory rate was 35 breaths/min, and her SaO2 was 99%. ECG was performed and resulted in sinus tachycardia and possible acute pericarditis. Initial laboratory tests showed a positive troponin I of 5600.8 pg/ml, CK-MB 56.3 mcg/L, lactic acid 6.22 mmol/L, PH 7.1, pCO2 46 mmHg, and HCO3 14.3 mEq/L. The Echo resulted in an LVEF < 25%. A respiratory panel multiplex test was requested, which was positive for influenza B and rhinovirus. Given the unstable hemodynamics and findings consistent with myopericarditis, the patient was transferred to the MCICU for close monitoring. The patient was transferred to the MCICU for close monitoring.

A right femoral central venous catheter CVC was inserted, and the patient’s BP dropped to 50–70 mmHg despite vasopressor support at maximum doses, and the patient was intubated due to a low Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 5/15. An emergency bedside venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was decided. The patient was diagnosed with fulminant heart failure due to perimyocarditis and cardiogenic shock. The treatment plan for peri-myocarditis was oseltamivir suspension 75 mg every 12 h for 5 days (started 2 days after presentation), Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) 60 g once daily for 2 days, methylprednisone, metronidazole 500 mg IV every 8 h, tigecycline 50 mg IV every 12 h which was escalated to 100 mg, vancomycin 250 mg oral every 6 h, linezolid 600 mg IV every 12 h, ciprofloxacin 400 gm IV every 12 h, and ceftazidime/avibactam 1250 mg IV every 8 h. Additionally, an Impella was inserted to unload the left ventricle, and it was removed after 4 days due to active bleeding. The patient underwent VA-ECMO decannulation after 14 days, following gradual improvement in LVEF to 35%.

During 38 days of hospitalization, the patient remained ill; she developed necrotizing pneumonia, bilateral pneumothorax, extensive tissue emphysema, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). She was maintained on high-dose vasopressors and broad-spectrum antibiotics. Multiple attempts for extubation failed due to low GCS. On February 11, the patient experienced a pulseless electrical activity cardiac arrest, possibly caused by respiratory failure. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was performed for two cycles, after which death was declared.

Literature review

From the retrieved literature, 24 case reports of influenza-associated myocarditis and myopericarditis were identified after excluding reviews, conference abstracts, reports with incomplete data, and those with uncertain diagnoses based on the 2024 ACC Expert Consensus Decision Pathway [11].

We extracted and summarized patient demographics, comorbidities, clinical presentation, diagnostic findings, treatment strategies, and outcomes (Table 2). The majority of patients were relatively young adults, with a median age of 42.5 years, and women represented a slight majority (54.2%). Most patients were previously healthy, with comorbidities reported only in a minority.

The most common presenting symptoms included fever (87.5%), tachycardia (75%), dyspnea (66.7%), hypotension or shock (62.5%), and chest pain (25%). Electrocardiographic abnormalities were frequent, with sinus tachycardia observed in 54.2% and ST-segment elevation in 33.3%. Echo demonstrated a reduced LVEF in over 80% of cases, while pericardial effusion was present in 41.7% of cases, although cardiac tamponade was rare.

Management most often included antiviral therapy, with oseltamivir administered in 79.2% of cases. Inotropic agents and/or MCS were required in 65.5% of patients, with 41.7% receiving advanced support such as ECMO or other devices. Despite the severity of presentations, nearly 80% of patients achieved full recovery, though five deaths (20.8%) were reported.

The distribution of influenza antigenic types is illustrated in Fig. 5, while detailed clinical and diagnostic characteristics of fatal cases are presented in Table 3. Fatal cases were notable for their unpredictable and rapid progression, often occurring in previously healthy young adults. Initial symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, epigastric or chest pain, and altered mental status were nonspecific, potentially delaying diagnosis and escalation of care. Most fatal cases progressed swiftly to cardiogenic shock, malignant arrhythmias, or multi-organ failure despite aggressive interventions including vasopressors, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and in one case, ECMO as a bridge to transplant. Notably, only one fatal case involved a patient with a documented comorbidity (diabetes mellitus). Vaccination history was either absent or not reported across all fatal cases. Overall, these findings highlight the fulminant potential of influenza-associated myocarditis, even in young, otherwise healthy individuals. They highlight the importance of early clinical recognition, timely initiation of antiviral therapy, and the critical role of routine influenza vaccination in reducing the burden of this life-threatening complication.

Discussion

We present one of the largest recent case series of influenza-associated myocarditis from the Middle East, reporting 4 cases over a 10-month period. The patients exhibited considerable variability in age, comorbidities, symptom severity, and clinical outcomes, which ranged from mild myopericarditis managed with anti-inflammatory agents and antivirals to fulminant myocarditis necessitating MCS.

Although uncommon, influenza-associated myocarditis is a potentially life-threatening complication of influenza infection. Reported prevalence varies widely depending on diagnostic criteria, ranging from 0.02% to 9% when broader criteria are applied [17, 18]. Despite its rarity, the condition holds clinical importance due to the risk of rapid progression to fulminant heart failure, arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death.

The pathophysiology of influenza-associated myocarditis is believed to involve both direct viral invasion of the myocardium and immune-mediated injury, leading to inflammation and cardiac dysfunction. The clinical spectrum ranges from subclinical or mild myopericarditis to fulminant myocarditis. Certain populations, particularly younger individuals and those infected with certain strains such as H1N1, appear to be at higher risk. Moreover, adult fulminant myocarditis carries a mortality rate exceeding 50%, especially among patients unresponsive to initial therapies such as inotropes, intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP), and volume resuscitation [17–20].

Our four reported cases are largely consistent with those described in the literature and summarized in Table 2. Similar to prior reports, our patients were all adults, and most were previously healthy or had minimal comorbidities. Fever, chest pain, and respiratory symptoms were the most common presenting complaints, mirroring trends reported in prior studies. All four patients had elevated troponin levels and evidence of myocardial dysfunction, with three demonstrating reduced ejection fraction on Echo. Notably, two of our cases involved Influenza B, including the most severe case, which required VA-ECMO and Impella support, supporting the observation that both influenza A and B can cause fulminant myocarditis. Antiviral therapy was consistently used in our series, and colchicine was administered to two patients. Similar to the 20.8% mortality seen in our literature review, one of our four patients (25%) died despite advanced supportive care, while the remaining three recovered. This outcome underscores both the potential severity of influenza-associated myocarditis and the importance of timely diagnosis and access to advanced circulatory support.

Clinically, influenza-associated myocarditis presents with a broad range of manifestations, from mild chest discomfort and palpitations to fulminant cardiogenic shock. Diagnosing this condition can be challenging, as cardiac manifestations may mimic other respiratory or infectious illnesses; therefore, maintaining a high index of suspicion is crucial, especially during influenza outbreaks. Diagnostic tools such as Echo, cardiac biomarkers, and cardiac MRI are essential for assessing myocardial involvement [19]. Not all patients underwent cardiac MRI or endomyocardial biopsy due to clinical instability and real-world feasibility constraints, potentially limiting uniform diagnostic confirmation across cases.

The management of influenza-associated myocarditis primarily focuses on supportive care aligned with evidence-based heart failure protocols. Advanced interventions, such as MCS, are typically reserved for severe or refractory cases. Given the significant risk of adverse outcomes, maintaining heightened clinical vigilance and promptly initiating therapeutic measures are crucial to optimizing patient prognosis. In rapidly deteriorating patients, empirical immunosuppression, such as intravenous corticosteroid boluses, may be critical before obtaining PCR confirmation. For cases presenting with fulminant symptoms, temporary MCS (especially VA-ECMO) can serve as a bridge to recovery or transplantation, although the timing of implementation and device selection remain subject to debate. Early ECMO deployment has been associated with improved survival, and when no clinical improvement is observed, early evaluation for heart transplantation should be pursued. Prognostic assessment in these cases is challenging due to the heterogeneous etiology, variable clinical phenotypes, and the absence of standardized protocols [20].

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design and small sample size limit generalizability. Second, uniform confirmatory diagnostic testing, including cardiac MRI and endomyocardial biopsy, was not feasible for all patients due to clinical instability and constraints in real-world practice. Third, the accompanying literature review was structured as a narrative review rather than a formal systematic review, which may introduce selection bias. Finally, long-term follow-up data were limited in some patients.

In conclusion, our case series underscores the importance of early recognition through vigilant clinical assessment, supported by cardiac imaging and biomarker evaluation. Prompt initiation of antiviral therapy, combined with supportive care tailored to disease severity, can lead to favorable outcomes in many patients, though mortality remains a real risk even with advanced care. Given the potential for rapid deterioration, especially in young and otherwise healthy individuals, heightened awareness and timely multidisciplinary management are essential. Additionally, these findings underscore the ongoing need for influenza vaccination as a crucial preventive measure to mitigate the burden of this severe complication.
