A high-throughput, end-to-end pipeline for extracellular miRNA biomarker discovery from human biofluids
Authors: Abbas Hakim, Jennifer N. Chousal, Srimeenakshi Srinivasan, Anelizze Castro-Martinez, Basant ElGhayati, Marina Mochizuki, Tyler Ostrander, Cassandra Wauer, Peter De Hoff, Priyadarshini Pantham, Louise C. Laurent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe developed a scalable pipeline for extracellular miRNA (ex-miRNA) profiling that integrates automated exRNA extraction, small RNA sequencing, and bioinformatic analysis including data processing and normalization. Automated extraction protocols, including doubling input volume or lyophilization to increase RNA yield, were benchmarked against leading manual methods, with donor pregnancy status serving as the primary biological variable. Small RNA sequencing was performed across conditions, enabling systematic evaluation of data quality and comparison of normalization strategies. Across methods and biofluids, DESeq2 most effectively reduced technical variability while preserving biological signal. Comparing specimen types, plasma exhibited the highest reproducibility and retention of biological signal, followed by serum, while urine exhibited greater variability and less differentially expressed miRNAs. Pregnancy-associated ex-miRNA signatures, including C19MC miRNAs, were consistently detected in both plasma and serum. Together, this study establishes a robust framework for scalable exRNA extraction and profiling, supporting standardized assay development for biomarker discovery and clinical applications.

Introduction

Extracellular RNAs (exRNAs) have gained popularity in recent years given their clinical potential as non-invasive diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic biomarkers, while also providing insight into physiological and pathological states.1,2,3,4,5,6,7 There is growing evidence that exRNAs play a role in cell-cell communication, directing biology by allowing communication between distant cells and tissue types.8,9,10,11 Found in plasma, serum, urine, and other biofluids, specific exRNA-based biomarkers have been proposed for several conditions, including preeclampsia,5,6,7,12 Alzheimer’s disease,2,3 liver disease,13 and certain hematologic malignancies.4 exRNAs are often associated with, or packaged inside, a variety of carriers which are thought to protect them from degradation by endogenous RNases, including extracellular vesicles (EVs), ribonucleoprotein complexes (RNPs), and lipoprotein particles (LPPs).8,14,15,16 While many types of RNA have been identified in the extracellular space, including microRNAs (miRNAs), transfer (t)RNAs, ribosomal (r)RNAs, small nucleolar (sno)RNAs, protein-coding messenger (m)RNAs, and long noncoding (lnc)RNAs, miRNAs have received the most attention, particularly in the setting of biomarker discovery.1,17,18

Despite their promise, exRNA biomarker studies face several technical challenges, including the innately low concentration of RNA in the extracellular space, the high degree of variability in the methods used for extraction and measurement, and the non-uniformity in data processing and analyses. RNA extraction represents a critical first step: downstream analyses such as sequencing or RT-qPCR are only as robust as the material recovered. Thus, extraction protocols must be both efficient and reproducible to support clinical translation, where consistency is essential, and to enable rigorous, comparable research studies. A major source of variability arises from the extraction method itself. Commercial kits can preferentially enrich for specific carrier classes, introducing systematic biases in RNA biotype.19,20 Additional sources of variability occur during preanalytical processing, library preparation, and bioinformatic analyses, prompting systematic investigations to identify best practices across these steps.19,20,21,22,23,24

Prior studies have compared manual extraction methods,25 evaluated library preparation protocols for both small26 and long RNA sequencing,27 assessed hybridization-based quantification methods,28 and profiled exRNA carrier cargo across biofluids to examine variability between biofluid type.29 These efforts have established reliable manual exRNA extraction protocols and standard data processing pipelines.30 However, three key challenges that are commonly encountered in large-scale biomarker discovery and validation studies remain: (1) scalability of exRNA extraction, (2) data normalization, and (3) batch correction. Manual extraction methods are labor-intensive and naturally prone to variability, posing challenges for downstream analytical phases. Automated, high-throughput extraction methods can improve consistency and scalability. However, these methods are typically not designed for low-input samples and can be less flexible in handling variable sample input volumes, necessitating optimization to improve efficiency and address preanalytical variability.

After sequencing data are generated, it must be processed, normalized, and analyzed. As mentioned above, despite prior systematic efforts to standardize NGS data processing,30 a variety of normalization approaches continue to be used.31,32 Proper normalization is critical for ensuring that the RNA expression levels reflected in sequencing results align with true biological signal. Normalization ensures accurate comparisons by addressing differences in sequencing depth and composition across samples. Some commonly used normalization approaches include downsampling, counts per million (CPM), trimmed mean of M-values (TMM), and DESeq2. Understanding the utility and limitations of these normalization approaches will facilitate robust biomarker discovery study design. In addition, even in high-throughput workflows, studies are often conducted in multiple batches, requiring batch correction approaches to remove technical variation in data. Recent advances have allowed for batch correction approaches that blend seamlessly with established bioinformatic workflows.33

Here, we aimed to address these critical gaps by developing a robust, automated exRNA extraction protocol and identifying a data normalization workflow that optimized suppression of technical biases and enhanced the detection of biological differences for extracellular miRNAs. We leveraged prior work, including a large study that compared commercial manual exRNA extraction methods, which identified the Norgen Plasma/Serum Circulating and Exosomal RNA Purification Kit as one of the more efficient and reproducible methods for plasma and serum25,26 and the miRCURY Exosome Cell/Urine/CSF Kit followed by the miRNeasy Micro Kit as a similarly robust approach for urine.34,35,36 On this basis, these protocols were selected as manual comparator methods. We then systematically compared their performance to optimized variations of the MagMAX mirVana Kit on the KingFisher Flex platform across three biofluids. To ensure that differences reflected extraction performance rather than downstream variability, all samples underwent identical small RNA library preparation using our previously established automated and miniaturized protocol,25,26 followed by processing via the exceRpt pipeline.30 We leveraged the biologically distinct groups of female vs. male and pregnant vs. non-pregnant to benchmark extraction and normalization performance. Our goal was to optimize an automated exRNA extraction protocol as well as a small RNAseq data normalization approach, allowing us to build a complete end-to-end workflow, from biofluid to normalized data, for exRNA biomarker discovery and validation.

Results

Automated and manual exRNA extraction are comparable in RNA yield and composition

To optimize and directly assess automated RNA extraction performance, we compared a single, previously validated manual exRNA extraction protocol to several automated variations, using a pooled-sample design to minimize biological and preanalytical variability. Equal volumes from 10 donors (healthy male, non-pregnant female, and pregnant female in the third trimester) were pooled within each biofluid type—plasma, serum, and urine—and divided into matched technical replicates. This strategy controlled for variability in donor biology, sample handling, and processing, thereby allowing differences in RNA yield and composition to be attributed to the extraction method itself.

The manual comparator methods included the Norgen Plasma/Serum Circulating and Exosomal RNA Purification Kit for plasma and serum, and the miRCURY Exosome Cell/Urine/CSF Kit followed by the miRNeasy Micro Kit for urine (Nor and miR, respectively, hereafter collectively referred to as MNL). These manual methods were compared against the MagMAX mirVana automated manufacturer’s protocol (MM) and three modified automated protocols (MMS, MMDD, and MMDbl) implemented on the KingFisher Flex platform (Figure 1A). These variations included the addition of a binding enhancer (MMS), RNA concentration via desiccation (MMDD), and a doubled sample input volume (MMDbl) (see “methods” for details). Incorporating the binding enhancer improved overall yield and was included in other variations. After RNA was extracted from matched technical replicates via each method, total RNA obtained per sample was quantified. In plasma and serum, the MMS, MMDD, MMDbl, and MNL extraction protocols yielded comparable RNA quantities, while the MM protocol yielded the lowest concentration (MNL 2.2 ± 1.1; MM 1.4 ± 0.3; MMS 3.0 ± 4.2; MMDD 2.5 ± 1.4; MMDbl 3.3 ± 4.7 ng/μL). In urine, the MNL and MMDD protocols resulted in the lowest and highest RNA concentrations, respectively (MNL 1.0 ± 0.4; MMS 1.7 ± 0.6; MMDD 3.1 ± 1.0; MMDbl 2.7 ± 1.4 ng/μL) (Figure S1A; Table S1). RNA extracted from pregnant females was more highly concentrated than non-pregnant females and males (pregnant 4.0 ± 4.7; non-pregnant 2.0 ± 1.2; male 1.8 ± 0.8 ng/uL) (Figure S1A; Table S1). Bioanalyzer traces confirmed the presence of small RNAs (<200 nucleotides) with little to no 18 S and 28 S ribosomal RNA species (Figure S1B). Extraction of RNA from serum via the automated protocol per the manufacturer’s recommendations (MM) produced very low RNA yield and was not tested in subsequent plasma extractions (Table S1).

Following RNA extraction, small RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Small RNA Library Prep Kit (cat. no. E7330) including spike-in calibrator oligonucleotides (see “methods”; Table S2) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (RRID: SCR_016387) (raw counts in Table S3). The quality of sequencing data was evaluated via three quality control metrics: proportion of calibrator to total reads, proportion of reads mapping to miRNAs, and library complexity. Exogenous small RNA calibrator sequences were added in equimolar concentration to each sample during library preparation, allowing relative quantification of input via assessment of calibrator ratios. Increased ratios of calibrator to total reads were observed for samples extracted via the MM and MMS methods, indicating lower RNA input compared to other extraction methods (Figures 1B and S2).

Next, we examined the proportion of reads mapping to miRNA (as well as tRNA, piRNA, rRNA, and mRNA) sequences and observed an enrichment of %miRNAs in our modified MagMAX methods (68%–85%) as compared to manual methods (49%–60%) for both plasma and serum. In contrast, urine samples showed lower %miRNAs for automated methods (5%–7% vs. 18% for manual) (Figure 1C). In addition, variability in RNA biotype proportions was reduced for automated vs. manual protocols, particularly for plasma samples. The %miRNA enrichment also differed modestly between biological groups in plasma and urine. In plasma, pregnant females had higher %miRNA compared to males and nonpregnant females (83% vs. ∼77%, p = 0.03). In urine, %miRNA was higher in pregnant females and males than in nonpregnant females (∼10% vs. 6%; p = 0.001). In serum, %miRNA was similar across biological groups (62%–67%, p = 0.11) (Figure 1C). Thus, pregnancy appears to be associated with a modest but significant increase in the proportion of miRNA in plasma and urine. Comparing other RNA biotypes within biofluids, urine had higher percentages of reads mapping to tRNA (29%) and rRNA (19%). In comparison to plasma, serum had higher proportions of both tRNA (4% vs. 0.3%) and rRNA (6% vs. 2%) (Figure 1C).

Finally, we assessed library complexity, defined as the number of detectable miRNAs expressed at a minimum of 10 counts. Complexity increased with sequencing depth before reaching a plateau, with all methods achieving saturation (maximal complexity) at specific read depths. MMDD samples exhibited a distinct trajectory compared to other methods, plateauing at lower miRNA counts which suggests loss of detectable miRNAs during desiccation that cannot be recovered by increasing sequencing depth (Figure 1D). Interestingly, while the MM and MMS methods used lower RNA input compared to the MMDbl and MNL methods, they ultimately achieved comparable complexity. In contrast, although the MMDD method used the same RNA input as the MMDbl and MNL methods, complexity was still lower. Thus, we concluded that the MMDbl method offers both robust RNA yield and high complexity, on par with the MNL method.

Normalization reduces technical variation across biofluids and biological group

Following quality assessment, depending on biofluid type, we either applied filtering (≥5 read counts in ≥60% samples) or performed downsampling followed by filtering and batch correction if needed, before normalization (Figure 2A). In general, normalization approaches attempt to correct for technical bias, including differences in sequencing depth, transcript length, and input RNA. There are multiple commonly used methods that account for one or more of these variables in generating corrected counts.31,32 Downsampling reduces read counts to a uniform level, mitigating depth-related biases but reducing sensitivity for low-abundance miRNAs. CPM adjusts for library size but does not account for compositional bias from highly expressed transcripts or other sources of variation. TMM applies scaling to address these biases, providing more balanced normalization in uneven datasets. To assess variability across biofluids and extraction methods, we generated relative log expression (RLE) plots (Figures 2B and S3A) and examined the coefficients of variation (CV) (Figure S3B). The RLE reflects deviation in gene expression from the median across samples, providing a measure of heterogeneity and normalization performance. As expected, raw reads exhibited substantial variability, underscoring the need for normalization, and most normalization methods reduced unwanted variation as evidenced by narrower percentile ranges and centering around zero (Figure 2B). Plasma samples showed the lowest variability and urine the highest. While normalization performed well for plasma and serum, no approaches achieved ideal normalization of the urine data (Figure S3A). CV analysis across expression quantiles revealed higher variability among lowly expressed miRNAs in serum and plasma, while urine displayed higher variability for mid-range miRNAs. In general, CVs were high for raw reads across all biofluids. DESeq2 most effectively reduced CVs across biofluids, extraction methods, and biological groups, whereas CPM and TMM achieved only modest improvements. Interestingly, pregnant female plasma samples demonstrated lower CVs across all methods (Figure S3B). Based on these analyses, DESeq2 normalization emerged as the most effective approach, consistently improving CVs and RLE measures across biofluids and extraction methods. It was thus used for downstream visualizations.

To assess variability in miRNA profiles and relationships among sample pools across biological groups and methods, we performed principal component analysis (PCA). Serum and plasma samples clustered by biological group along PC1, with pregnant samples separating from non-pregnant females and males, accounting for 37% and 64% of the variance, respectively (Figure 2C). Along PC2, separation of manual from the automated methods was evident, indicating that biological differences were preserved across extraction method and sequencing batches and were the primary drivers of variation in these biofluids. In contrast, urine was more sensitive to technical variation. Despite a similar batch-based sequencing approach as serum and plasma, urine required downsampling and batch correction prior to DESeq2 normalization to enable PCA (Figures S4A and S4B). In urine, extraction method was the primary source of variance (PC1), with only partial separation by biological group, highlighting the challenges of generating robust data from this biofluid.

Technical precision is high across methods, but reproducibility varies by biofluid and biological group

To assess precision within technical replicates for each extraction method, as well as reproducibility within biological groups across biofluids, we performed complementary correlation analyses. Pairwise Spearman correlations were calculated and visualized as correlation matrices to assess similarity of miRNA profiles (Figure 3A). Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were used to quantify agreement within technical replicates, reporting both ICC(C,1) (single-measure) with 95% CIs and ICC(C,k) (average-measures), which reflects the reliability of the mean of k replicates (Figure 3B; Table S4). Plasma samples exhibited the highest precision within methods for a given biological group, with raw reads already showing high ICC (∼0.93) and narrow CIs, and normalization approaches such as TMM and DESeq2 achieving near-perfect ICCs (∼0.98–1.00) with correspondingly tight CIs (Figure 3B). ICC(C,k) approached unity across plasma replicates, confirming that averaging replicates yields near-perfect precision. Correlation matrices also demonstrated robust separation of biological groups in plasma, with pregnant, non-pregnant female, and male samples clustering distinctly, and strong within-method agreement (Figure 3A). Serum samples displayed slightly lower precision than plasma, with raw reads achieving lower ICCs (∼0.80) and wider CIs, requiring normalization to improve ICCs (∼0.94–0.96) (Figure 3B). ICC(C,k) again approached unity, particularly after normalization. In correlation matrices, pregnant serum samples clustered robustly, whereas non-pregnant female and male samples showed greater variability and partial overlap (Figure 3A). Urine samples demonstrated the lowest precision, with raw reads showing lower ICCs (∼0.60–0.70) and the widest CIs, which improved with normalization, particularly with downsampling and DESeq2 (∼0.88–0.94) and moderately narrowed CIs (Figure 3B). Although ICC(C,k) increased relative to single-measure ICCs, it remained lower than in plasma and serum. To integrate these findings, we generated radar plots summarizing key quality metrics across biofluids: exRNA yield, complexity index (number of unique miRNAs per million reads), miRNA enrichment, and ICC (precision) (Figure 3C). Across biofluids, the MMDbl method consistently demonstrated the most balanced performance, achieving high yield and complexity together with strong miRNA enrichment and precision. In plasma and serum, automated approaches aligned closely with the manual comparator with respect to library complexity, miRNA enrichment, and precision, while improving yield. In urine, automated methods exceeded the manual comparator with respect to complexity and yield while maintaining similar precision and miRNA enrichment.

Finally, we assessed agreement between methods using Bland-Altman analyses, which evaluate the mean difference (bias) and limits of agreement between paired measurements. In general, we observed strong agreement of miRNA counts between manual and automated, and within automated, methods for serum, plasma, and urine (Figures 4 and S5). However, a trend toward reduced agreement was observed for highly expressed miRNAs, particularly with TMM normalization. In contrast, DESeq2-normalized and downsampled counts showed the highest agreement. Notably, the MMDD method exhibited a proportional bias, with lower miRNA expression relative to other methods that became more pronounced at higher expression levels. However, all normalization approaches corrected this bias, underscoring the value of proper normalization in miRNA sequencing datasets, particularly in the setting of RNA desiccation. Collectively, these analyses confirm high technical precision across extraction methods, with reproducibility decreasing from plasma to serum to urine. Pregnancy-specific miRNA signatures are consistent across RNA extraction methods

We next sought to identify expression profiles associated with the pregnant state within each biofluid using unsupervised hierarchical clustering. In plasma, pregnant samples clustered robustly and distinct from non-pregnant females and males, with pregnancy-enriched miRNAs including members of the chromosome 19 miRNA cluster (C19MC), demonstrated to be placenta-specific.37,38 Automated methods showed expression patterns comparable to MNL, with a slight trend toward increased expression of some miRNAs in MNL samples (Figure 5A, center). In serum, clustering was driven primarily by extraction method rather than biological group, reflecting a greater sensitivity to technical variation. However, within each method-based cluster, pregnant samples separated from non-pregnant females and males, and C19MC miRNAs were consistently enriched in pregnant samples across methods (Figure 5A, left). In urine, clustering was dominated by extraction method with no clear separation by biological group, and C19MC miRNAs were not consistently detected in pregnant samples (Figure 5A, right). Next, we conducted directed differential expression analysis comparing pregnant vs. non-pregnant female samples within each extraction method to identify miRNAs enriched in pregnancy (Table S5). Consistent with prior analyses, we observed the most robust and consistent results in plasma. Across all methods, 184 and 146 miRNAs were upregulated and downregulated in pregnancy (q < 0.05, log2FC > 0), respectively, with the majority overlapping across methods (54.4% (100/184) upregulated; 63.0% (92/146) downregulated) (Figures 5B and S6A). The MMDbl extraction method, which doubles input volume compared to other automated methods, identified the highest number of differentially expressed miRNAs (158 upregulated, 125 downregulated), exceeding even the manual comparator. In serum, 146 and 142 miRNAs were significantly upregulated and downregulated in pregnancy, with lower overlap across methods (34.3% (50/146) upregulated; 22.5% (32/142) downregulated), and samples extracted via the MMDbl method again contained the largest set of differentially expressed miRNAs (119 upregulated, 102 downregulated) (Figures 5B and S6A). In urine, consistent with prior findings, high variability between methods resulted in limited overlap for both upregulated (4.2% (2/48)) and downregulated (1.8% (1/56)) miRNAs among methods, though MMDbl again identified the largest number of differentially expressed miRNAs (44 upregulated, 57 downregulated) (Figures 5B and S6A).

We next used C19MC miRNAs to assess method sensitivity (Sn) and specificity (Sp) within each biofluid. We chose a subset of the C19MC cluster (n = 20) based on the level and consistency of expression across all datasets. For each method and biofluid, we calculated Sn and Sp at the optimal threshold (Youden’s Index) using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, enabling comparison of individual miRNA classification performance (pregnant vs. nonpregnant). miRNAs not expressed in a given biofluid received Sn and Sp values of 0 (see “methods” for further details). Although individual miRNAs in both plasma and serum achieved perfect classification, Sn and Sp values were compiled across the full classifier set to enable method and biofluid comparisons. Plasma samples showed high classification performance across all methods (mean Sn = 0.94, Sp = 0.99), with samples extracted via the MMDbl protocol producing data that best classified the pregnant state (Sn = 0.97, Sp = 0.99). Similarly, serum samples showed good classification performance (mean Sn = 0.82, Sp = 0.99). In contrast, urine samples exhibited low overall performance (mean Sn = 0.01, Sp = 0.08) (Table S6). Notably, the majority of classifier miRNAs were not robustly detected in urine, limiting the conclusions that can be drawn from this analysis. Given these results, as proof-of-concept, we applied DESeq2-based feature selection to identify alternative urine classifiers, ranking candidate miRNAs by adjusted p value and cross-method consistency. The top 20 showed improved but modest classification performance (Sn = 0.50, Sp = 0.61), with no individual miRNA achieving perfect classification (Table S6). Overall, these findings demonstrate that plasma and serum robustly express placenta-associated miRNAs that allow for accurate classification of the pregnant state and represent an endogenous biological system with which to assess methodologies for ex-miRNA biomarker discovery. However, urine requires additional investigation into alternative markers, and/or the development of alternative extraction methods or data normalization strategies.

Comparison of miRNA expression patterns across biofluids

To assess miRNA expression across biofluids, we quantified the number of miRNAs robustly expressed in each biofluid (≥10 raw counts in ≥60% of samples). As expected, plasma demonstrated the highest number (n = 365), followed by serum (n = 281), and urine (n = 138). A substantial proportion of miRNAs were shared across all biofluids (n = 115), while the majority were common to plasma and serum (n = 279), reflecting their similarity (Figure 6A). Hierarchical clustering reinforced this, with plasma and serum clustering together, showing expression profiles distinct from urine. Within plasma and serum, samples from the same biological group (e.g., pregnant or non-pregnant) clustered more closely than those from the same biofluid, though each biofluid retained a unique expression signature. To evaluate whether systematic biases among methods impact detection of biological differences beyond pregnancy, we examined four sets of miRNAs: one comprised of miRNAs that are differentially expressed across tissues; and three comprised of the most highly abundant miRNAs for each biofluid. Using a previously published dataset of tissue-derived smRNAseq profiles,6 we examined liver-, kidney-, and red blood cell (RBC)-enriched miRNAs. As expected, RBC- and liver-enriched miRNAs were enriched in plasma and serum relative to urine, whereas kidney-enriched miRNAs showed increased expression in urine. Importantly, there were no consistent method-associated biases across these cell- and tissue-specific miRNA classes, highlighting the robustness of our methods for detection of biologically meaningful miRNAs (Figure S6B). We also assessed the most abundant miRNAs within each biofluid. Plasma and serum shared a number of these “most abundant” miRNAs, including miR-122-5p (enriched in the liver) and miR-451a (associated with RBCs). Urine exhibited biofluid-specific enrichment, including miR-30a-5p and miR-10a-5p. Again, no consistent biases were detected among methods (Figure S6C). These results further underscore both shared and biofluid-specific signatures, while confirming reproducibility across extraction methods.

Given the high degree of similarity between the most abundant miRNAs in plasma and serum, we conducted a direct comparison of miRNA expression levels in these two biofluids across all methods and biological groups. This demonstrated strong correlation (Spearman R = 0.93), with a trend toward higher expression in plasma (Figure 6C). Differential expression analysis between plasma and serum using both manual (MNL) and our most robust automated method (MMDbl) further confirmed concordance, with both methods capturing similar expression patterns (Figure 6D). Together, these results highlight the overall robust and reproducible detection of miRNAs across methods for each biofluid, emphasizing the close relationship between plasma and serum miRNA profiles.

Discussion

The discovery and clinical translation of exRNA biomarkers would be facilitated by the development of a standardized workflow that offers reproducible and scalable preanalytic, analytic, and informatic components. Considerable progress toward this goal has been made by our group and others, with numerous benchmarking studies comparing manual exRNA extraction methods. Among these, one particularly comprehensive study identified the Norgen Plasma/Serum Circulating and Exosomal RNA Purification Kit as the most efficient and reproducible method for ex-miRNA extraction in plasma and serum,25 and this protocol has since been widely adopted for exRNA biomarker discovery.7,39,40,41 Likewise, a companion comparison of exRNA extraction methods in urine found that EV isolation with the miRCURY Exosome Cell/Urine/CSF Kit followed by miRNeasy Micro Kit for extraction was optimal for urine specimens.36 While these manual methods are highly effective, their lack of scalability remains a major limitation; an experienced technician can process only ∼24 samples per day. To address this, we evaluated variations of the MagMAX method automated on the Kingfisher platform alongside manual comparator methods for profiling exRNAs in plasma, serum, and urine. Our optimized MMDbl protocol—the automated variation that doubles input volume—produced exRNA yields comparable to the Norgen protocol for plasma and serum and to miRCURY/miRNeasy for urine (Figures S1A and S1B), while adding scalability. With this protocol, one technician can extract 192 samples (two plates) per day on a single instrument. Small RNAseq libraries generated via the MMDbl protocol demonstrated superior reproducibility while retaining comparable RNA biotype distributions, complexity, and biological signal, as compared to manual protocols. Notably, increased RNA yield did not necessarily correspond to improved data quality. For example, the MMDD protocol produced the highest yield of RNA, but the resulting small RNAseq libraries had the lowest complexity (Figures S1 and 1C).

Current small RNAseq library preparation methods are generally highly reproducible within and across laboratories,42 but each has the potential to introduce systematic biases, making it important to use a consistent approach for any study or set of studies analyzed together. We previously developed a cost-effective, scalable, and reproducible miniaturized automated workflow for small RNAseq library construction,7,25,43,44,45,46,47,48 which we used here. Data processing, including trimming, cleaning, and mapping, was performed via a pipeline developed for the Extracellular RNA Communication Consortium30 and has been used for many basic and translational research projects.6,7,9,43,49,50,51,52,53,54 Library preparation and data analysis were performed identically across samples, negating these as variables. Although several normalization strategies for small RNAseq data have been proposed, an optimal approach for ex-miRNA data has not yet been established. Building on previous studies,31,32,55,56 we systematically compared multiple approaches, including count-based (downsampling and CPM), scaling (TMM), and distribution-matching methods (DESeq2). Our findings reinforce the importance of normalization to mitigate technical variability and enable robust differential expression analysis, particularly in highly variable datasets, such as urine. These samples benefited most from initial downsampling, which reduced variability but required compromises in sensitivity for low-abundance miRNAs (Figures 2, 3, and S4). Among the methods assessed, and consistent with previous studies,55 DESeq2 was the most reliable approach, effectively addressing variability while maintaining biological signal integrity (Figures 2B, 3B, 4, and S3). However, we also reinforce the need for strategies tailored for unique characteristics of certain samples, such as the dominance of a small subset of highly expressed miRNAs,56 differences in sample processing such as RNA desiccation (Figures 1D, 4, and S5), and challenges innate to biofluid types (Figures 1, 3, 5, 6, S1, S4, and S5). In urine specifically, several normalization strategies have been proposed to account for its variable composition, including adjusting for preanalytical factors such as osmolality, creatinine, or specific gravity, as well as the use of stable “housekeeping” RNAs as endogenous controls.36,57,58 While promising, the performance of these approaches varies across studies, and none have been widely adopted. In this study, because of our pooling strategy, we focused normalization efforts on sequencing-based metrics rather than preanalytical variables. Future clinical studies may benefit from incorporating these biofluid-specific normalization approaches, particularly for urine, which presents unique analytical challenges discussed below. Best practices for collection, storage, and analysis of urine samples in large scale clinical cohorts for exRNA analysis have been outlined elsewhere and stringent reporting criteria should be followed to improve and standardize processes across studies.59,60 Taken together, our findings support the use of automated RNA extraction protocols and standardized data processing and normalization approaches to improve reproducibility within and across exRNA biomarker studies, particularly those that are longitudinal and/or multi-center.

The use of this workflow allowed us to evaluate and compare biofluid characteristics and their suitability for exRNA biomarker assessment. Among the three tested biofluids, plasma samples consistently exhibited the lowest variability and highest reproducibility, while serum and urine displayed greater biological and technical variability to a moderate and marked degree, respectively (Figures 3 and S3). Plasma and serum ex-miRNA profiles showed a high degree of overlap, suggesting that ex-miRNA biomarkers identified from one of these biofluids may be good candidates for the other (Figures 6 and S6C). Overall, the reproducibility and complexity of small RNAseq libraries generated from the three tested biofluids suggest that while plasma may be preferred due to decreased variability, serum is a good alternative. In fact, despite a lower total number of differentially expressed miRNAs in pregnant vs. non-pregnant specimens in serum compared to plasma, serum specimens demonstrated expression of more miRNAs from the placenta-specific C19MC cluster, making serum an attractive biofluid candidate for studies in pregnancy (Figure 5). For other tissue and cell types, including liver and red blood cells, enriched miRNAs did not show preference for plasma or serum as a biofluid (Figure S6B).

Urine, by contrast, presents unique challenges. Compared to plasma and serum, urine typically contains lower concentrations of RNA (Figures S1 and 6A), increasing the likelihood of stochastic sampling effects and contributing to greater inter- and intra-sample variability. Urine also contains compounds such as urea, salts, and protein aggregates (e.g., uromodulin) that can interfere with enzymatic reactions during nucleic acid extraction and library preparation,25,36,60,61,62 and is sensitive to diurnal variation, as well as changes in diet, hormones, and exercise.63 All of these properties make urine highly sensitive to technical variability, contributing to lower reproducibility, lower sensitivity, and more pronounced batch effects, which we observed among both pooled technical replicates and sequencing batches. Moreover, methodological differences between our manual and automated extraction approaches likely contributed to the low overlap observed between protocols. The manual miR comparator method included an EV precipitation step prior to lysis, whereas the automated MagMAX workflow involves direct lysis, capturing miRNAs from all sources (e.g., EVs, ribonucleoproteins, and lipoproteins), potentially yielding a broader but more variable miRNA profile. Other commercial methods, such as column-based total RNA extraction kits (e.g., Norgen) or alternative EV enrichment approaches (e.g., ultrafiltration, size-exclusion chromatography), have also been applied to urine, each with trade-offs in yield, RNA species recovered, scalability, and susceptibility to inhibitors.36,60 However, no single method has yet emerged as a clear standard. Despite these challenges, urine remains an attractive biofluid in many settings because it can be collected non-invasively and in large volumes, especially in studies involving pregnant women or children. Moreover, certain disease processes or tissue types (i.e., those that involve the kidney and urinary tract) may be better assayed via the urine. Thus, further optimization of exRNA extraction, library preparation, data processing, and analysis for urine is warranted.36

In addition to these biofluid-specific technical considerations, our study design introduces one important limitation. We chose a pooling strategy in which 10 specimens for each biofluid were pooled, thoroughly mixed, and aliquoted as technical replicates. While this strategy minimizes individual variability and preanalytical contributions—and is ideal for this study design—we note that this approach may obscure inter-individual differences and limit generalizability. Thus, we want to reinforce that these results are limited to technical benchmarking and that preanalytical and biological variables must be examined in a separate study. Looking ahead, studies with larger sample size and the incorporation of donors from diverse populations will enhance the generalizability of these findings. Additionally, integrating multi-omics approaches, such as combining exRNA profiling with proteomics or metabolomics, could provide complementary insights into pathophysiological processes, and identify alternate biomarker targets. Ultimately, developing streamlined workflows for clinical implementation, including the validation of automated methods in multi-center studies, will be critical for translating exRNA-based biomarkers into routine diagnostic applications.

In summary, this study establishes a robust workflow for high-throughput exRNA extraction, small RNA sequencing, and data processing and normalization. By addressing variability across biofluids and standardizing key workflows, this work enhances the reproducibility and translational potential of exRNA biomarker studies and lays the foundation for clinical implementation.

Limitations of the study

A key limitation of this study is the use of pooled specimens as technical replicates, which, while ideal for assessing technical performance, may not capture inter-individual variability or fully reflect the complexity of real-world clinical samples. Preanalytical and biological variables remain to be examined in future work.

Experimental model and study participant details

Biofluid collection

Whole blood and urine were collected from healthy male, female (non-pregnant), and pregnant female donors (≥18 years of age) under protocols approved by the UC San Diego Institutional Review Board (IRB# 141792). Prenatal samples were obtained from pregnant women late in the third trimester (>27 weeks gestation). Serum, plasma, and urine specimens were collected from ∼10 male, and ∼10 female (non-pregnant), and ∼10 pregnant female donors for this study. Biofluid specimens and extracted RNA were deidentified and assigned unique identifiers prior to analysis.

Biofluid processing

For serum preparation, blood was collected without anticoagulant in a standard red-top tube, allowed to clot for ∼30 min (but not >2 h), and centrifuged at 2000 xg for 10 min at room temperature. For plasma, whole blood was collected in K2EDTA tubes and centrifuged at 2000 xg for 10 min at room temperature within 2 h of collection. Serum and plasma were divided into 1 mL aliquots and stored at −80°C. Urine specimens were centrifuged at 2000 xg for 10 min at room temperature within 2 h of collection. The supernatant was divided into 4 mL aliquots (avoiding the debris collected at the bottom of the tube) and stored at −80°C.

Prior to extracellular (ex)RNA extraction, serum and plasma samples were thawed on ice, while urine samples were thawed in a 37°C water bath. To generate sufficient input material for testing across multiple extraction methods, equal volumes from ∼10 donors within each biofluid type and biological group were combined to create pooled serum, plasma, and urine samples. All aliquots of pooled material were only thawed once prior to RNA extraction for each extraction method tested. Expanded donor and collection metadata contributing to pooled specimens is available in dbGaP upon request (dbGaP: phs003972.v1).

Method details

RNA extraction

To minimize the impact of preanalytical variability and enable direct comparison of extraction methods, pooled biofluid specimens were used for all RNA extractions. Equal volumes from ∼10 donors were combined for each biological group and biofluid type (serum, plasma, or urine) as described above. All extractions were performed from these pooled aliquots, ensuring that differences in RNA yield or quality reflected extraction method performance rather than biological variability among individual donors. Manual extractions were processed in groups (triplicate at minimum) for every combination of biofluid and method, as is standard in our laboratory. Total RNA was extracted using one manual protocol for each biofluid type and multiple variations of an automated protocol, as summarized below.

Manual methods (MNL)

For serum and plasma, total RNA was extracted using the Norgen Plasma/Serum Circulating and Exosomal RNA Purification Kit (cat. 51000) per the manufacturer’s protocol with modifications. A 500 μL input was processed with 100 μL of the kit provided slurry, and RNA was eluted in 14 μL of nuclease-free water pre-heated to 50°C using a two-step elution (12 μL followed by 2 μL). Samples were processed manually using individual spin columns.

For urine, exosomes were first isolated using the miRCURY Exosome Cell/Urine/CSF Kit (cat. no. 76743) from 3500 μL of urine, then lysed for RNA extraction via the miRNeasy Micro Kit (cat. no. 217084). RNA was eluted in 14 μL of nuclease-free water pre-heated to 50°C. Samples were processed manually using individual spin columns.

Automated methods

Automated extractions were performed on the KingFisher Flex platform using variations of the MagMAX mirVana Kit (cat. A27828). Several modifications to the manufacturer’s instructions were tested to optimize RNA yield with this automated methodology.

Method MM: MagMAX mirVana Kit Manufacturer. Baseline automated protocol, using 100–200 μL of sample and eluted in 50 μL of elution buffer. Manufacturer’s recommendations for increased lysis binding reagents to match increased sample input were followed. This version of the automated extraction was used only for serum, as preliminary experiments demonstrated reduced RNA yield.

Method MMS: MagMAX mirVana Kit Single. Total RNA was isolated from 166.67–200 μL of sample using a reduced volume of binding beads supplemented with kit-provided lysis/binding enhancer. RNA was eluted in 50 μL of elution buffer.

Method MMDD: MagMAX mirVana Kit Single Drydown. Dry-down protocol identical to MMS through extraction; however, RNA was eluted into 1:10 low EDTA buffer (1mM tris, 0.01mM EDTA, pH 8.0) and then concentrated by SpeedVac (Thermo Scientific Savant SpeedVac Kit 230V) down to salt. Once completely dried, the RNA was resuspended in 10 μL of nuclease-free water.

Method MMDbl: MagMAX mirVana Kit Double. Total RNA was extracted from 400 μL of sample split across two plates (200 μL each). The binding bead and lysis binding enhancer mixture used in MMS was applied to both plates, and the magnetic head performed a second “dip” to capture additional RNA. Bead collection times on the Kingfisher Flex System were also extended to 10 min to accommodate the larger input.

RNA quantification

RNA quantification platforms were used to assess RNA yield, purity and electropherogram traces across extraction methods. RNA was also quantified using the Quant-iT RNA High Sensitivity kit on the Infinite 200 PRO microplate reader. Electropherogram traces of the extracted RNA were obtained using the Agilent RNA 6000 Pico Kit on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system. The Pico kit was used preferentially over the Small RNA kit due to its greater sensitivity for low quantities of RNA.

Small RNA sequencing library preparation

To ensure that differences in sequencing results reflected RNA extraction method performance and not downstream methods, all samples underwent identical small RNA library preparation and sequencing workflows. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina per the manufacturer’s protocol, with the following modifications.26 Reaction volumes were scaled to 1/5th of the recommended volume, and supplier oligonucleotides were further diluted to 1:6 of the stock concentration. For each sample, 1.2 μL of RNA was used as input, along with 0.2 μL of known spike-in calibrator sequences (Table S2). Library amplification was performed with 17 cycles (but was kept consistent across all runs). Upon library construction, 20 μL of each library was purified using the Zymo DNA Clean & Concentrator-5 and eluted in 30 μL TE Buffer [1×]. Library quality was assessed using the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit to generate electropherograms. Equal volumes of each library were pooled and concentrated to 35 μL using the aforementioned Zymo kit. Of this, 5 μL was reserved, while the remaining 30 μL underwent automated size selection on the Pippin Prep (RRID:SCR_020403) via pulse-field electrophoresis using the 3% Agarose, dye-free gel DNA cassette. The lower and upper size cutoffs were set to 125 bp and 135 bp, respectively, to remove adapter dimers and enrich for miRNA-encoding fragments. Both pre- and post-size-selected pools were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Kit and analyzed on an Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit chip to confirm concentration and fragment size distribution.

A shallow “balancing run” was then performed on the Illumina MiSeq Platform (RRID:SCR_016379, MiSeq Reagent Nano Kit v2, 75 single-end cycles) to assess relative sample representation within the pool (∼1 million reads total). The resulting read counts were used to calculate adjusted pooling volumes for each sample to achieve a final “balanced” pool targeting approximately equal miRNA read counts across all samples. This balanced pool was then concentrated and size selected again under the same conditions as the prior equal-volume pool. The balanced pool was sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 Platform using an SP100 flowcell for deep sequencing.

Data processing

Raw sequencing data generated on both the MiSeq and NovaSeq platforms were first demultiplexed using Illumina bcl2fastq (v2.20) to generate per-sample FASTQ files. Adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed as part of the exceRpt small-RNA analysis pipeline30. Reads were mapped to the human genome (hg19) with alignment allowing for no mismatches, and only reads with a minimum length of 15 nucleotides after adapter trimming were retained. Reads passing these filters were hierarchically assigned to RNA biotypes in the following order: miRNA, tRNA, piRNA, Gencode transcripts, and circRNA. Calibrator sequences were concatenated to the reference genome for parallel mapping and quantification.

For downstream analyses, miRNA read counts were further processed to control for differences in sequencing depth. To mitigate the influence of low-depth, total miRNA read counts were downsampled to 250,000 reads per sample. Samples with fewer than 250,000 total miRNA reads prior to downsampling were retained at their original depth. A minimum count filter of 5 reads was then applied to the downsampled count matrix to remove lowly-expressed miRNAs within each sample. Additionally, miRNAs were required to be present in at least 60% of replicates per group, where each group was defined by the combination of extraction method, biological group, and biofluid type. The resulting list of miRNAs represented those that passed both the read count and representation requirement for at least one group. For normalization comparisons, raw counts (prior to downsampling) corresponding to this filtered list of miRNAs were extracted for each sample and used in subsequent analyses.

Normalization

Raw reads of the filtered list of miRNAs were normalized using several commonly used methods to compare approaches for small exRNA data:

Counts per Million (CPM). Raw reads were scaled by the total reads, normalizing for differences in sequencing depth. Gene length is not accounted for.

Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM). Raw reads were normalized using the Bioconductor package edgeR.58 This method uses the weighted trimmed mean of the log expression ratios between samples, including sequencing depth, gene length, and RNA composition as factors.

DESeq2. Using the Bioconductor package DESeq2,59 this function divides the counts by sample-specific size factors (as determined by the median ratio of gene counts relative to the geometric mean per gene). Both sequencing depth and RNA composition are accounted for.

Normalization methods were assessed by examining Relative Log Expression (RLE) using the RUVSeq package (version 1.36.0), and by custom scripts using tidyverse (version 2.0.0) and dplyr (version 1.1.4) to calculate the Coefficient of Variation grouped by quantiles of expression. Visualization of results was carried out using the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.4).

Data analysis and differential gene expression

RNA extraction methods were compared via multiple computational approaches. Pairwise spearman correlation between individual samples was calculated via the Hmisc package (version 5.1–1) and visualized using corrplot (version 0.92). Bland-Altman plots were used to assess the agreement between two methods by plotting the differences between measurements against the average of the measurements and was calculated via a custom script and visualized via ggplot2. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated via the irr package (version 0.84.1) and visualized via ggplot2. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (version 1.42.1). DESeq2 was used to normalize counts, estimate dispersion, perform PCA, and calculate differential expression between conditions (using the negative binomial generalized linear model). Genes with an adjusted p-value <0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Heatmaps were generated using variance stabilized counts from DESeq2 via heatmap (version 1.0.12). The RVenn package (version 1.1.0) was used to examine the intersection of gene lists and generate setmaps comparing differentially expressed genes detected via the various methods. For urine samples, library preparation batch effects were corrected using ComBat-Seq from the sva package (version 3.50.0), which adjusts for batch-related variation while preserving biological differences in RNA-seq count data.33

ROC analysis

We evaluated the classification performance of 20 C19MC miRNAs, selected based on consistent expression and adequate levels across datasets, in distinguishing pregnant from non-pregnant samples. For each biofluid and experimental method, miRNA expression data were extracted and samples categorized into binary labels (1 for pregnant and 0 for non-pregnant female). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for each miRNA using the pROC package (version 1.18.5). The optimal threshold for classification was determined using Youden’s Index, which maximizes the sum of Sensitivity (Sn) and Specificity (Sp). At this threshold, Sn and Sp were calculated for each miRNA within each experimental method and biofluid. For miRNAs with undetectable expression in a given dataset (method subset within a given biofluid), Sn and Sp could not be calculated and both of were assigned a value of 0. Results were compiled and grouped by method, enabling a direct comparison of miRNA classification performance across methods and biofluids. Reported Sn and Sp values are mean values of the 20 miRNAs within a given biofluid and method; however, it should be noted that perfect classifiers distinguishing pregnant vs. non-pregnant samples could be identified for both plasma and serum (Table S6).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) and relevant Bioconductor packages, including Hmisc, irr, DESeq2, and base R functions, as detailed above. Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of p < 0.05, with adjustments for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, unless otherwise stated.

For method comparison, pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the rank-based similarity between extraction methods, and Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC) were used to evaluate reproducibility across replicates and methods. Bland–Altman plots were used to visualize agreement between methods by plotting differences versus means for each miRNA.

For differential expression analyses, normalized count data were analyzed using the DESeq2 negative binomial generalized linear model framework. Dispersion estimates and PCA were generated within DESeq2, and genes with adjusted p-values <0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Batch effects in urine libraries were corrected using ComBat-Seq (sva package, version 3.50.0).

For classification analyses, ROC curves were generated using the pROC package to evaluate the performance of 20 C19MC miRNAs in distinguishing pregnant from non-pregnant groups across methods and biofluids. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated at optimal thresholds defined by Youden’s Index.

All visualization and figure generation were performed in R using ggplot2 (version 3.4.4) and supporting packages, as described in the Data Analysis section.
