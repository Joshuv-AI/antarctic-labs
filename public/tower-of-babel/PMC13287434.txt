Geographic Disparities in People with Epilepsy: Differences in Self-Efficacy and Quality of Life
Authors: Lisa Tan, Rachel Branning, Haitong Yu, Nicole Fiorelli, Jessica Black, Clara Adeniyi, Maegan Tyrrell, Farren Briggs, Gena R Ghearing, Martha Sajatovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeApproximately 19% of the U.S population live in rural locales, which are disproportionally afflicted by health disparities. While people with epilepsy (PWE) living in rural areas may have difficulties receiving appropriate care, little is known about differences in PWE due to geography. This study evaluated whether clinical and demographic characteristics among PWE differed by geographic region to better characterize potential differences in care and outcomes faced by rural people with epilepsy (RPWE).MethodsThis cross-sectional baseline data analysis from an epilepsy self-management clinical trial examined quality of life, functional status, self-management, self-efficacy, depression, and social support. The independent variable of interest was geography of residence (categorized using Rural-Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC): metro areas [RUCC 1–3], adjacent to metro areas [RUCC 4, 6, 8], and rural [RUCC 5, 7, 9]). Amongst covariates were other social determinants of health (SDOH): income and education. Multivariable linear regression models were conducted, including the three SDOH and other covariates.ResultsAdjusting for likely confounders, geography was independently associated with three outcomes: the epilepsy self-efficacy scale (ESES) scores, quality of life in epilepsy (QOLIE-31) total scores, and QOLIE-31 cognition sub scores. RPWE had lower ESES scores (p=0.045), lower QOLIE-31 total scores (p=0.025), and lower QOLIE-31-cognition sub scores (p=0.043) compared to urban PWE. On average, RPWE had an 11-point lower QOLIE-31 total score.ConclusionsRPWE have lower self-efficacy, worse quality of life, and perceived cognitive functioning compared to urban PWE. These geographic disparities may reflect disparities in access to epilepsy care that are associated with worse outcomes among RPWEKeywords: Rural, Geography, Epilepsy, Seizures, Quality of life

Introduction

According to the U.S. Bureau of the Census, 19.3% of the U.S. population lives in a rural area.1 Rural areas have a higher percentage of children living in poverty, higher rates of all-cause mortality, and more uninsured residents under age 65. These are significant health disparities compared to urban populations.2 In addition, rural residents face longer travel times and distance to medical care, which result in greater perceived difficulty in access to care relative to their urban counterparts.3 There is a significant rural-urban healthcare gap that is compounded by the high prevalence of epilepsy and associated morbidity.4 Thus, it is important to investigate the impact of health disparities on rural people with epilepsy (RPWE). Past research has not been able to conclusively document rural status as an independent factor for worsened health outcomes in RPWE. However, it has been found that disease-associated disability, preventing at tainment of advanced education and long-term employment can aggravate pre-existing rural health disparities.2 In addition to the neurodevelopmental comorbidities of epilepsy that add to the burden of disease, low levels of knowledge, stigma, and negative attitudes towards people with epilepsy (PWE) can also impact individual motivation to start disease self-management and seek medical care. Given the limited data and inconclusiveness of prior literature reviews on outcome differences between PWE from rural and urban areas,2,5 this analysis, drawn from baseline data from a randomized controlled trial (RCT) testing a self-management approach in PWE, examined demographic and clinical variables in a sample that included RPWE.

There is a lack of overall data on U.S. rural and semi-rural communities; therefore, comparison of health outcomes with urban communities is limited. Rural communities may have lower retention rates in epilepsy research studies due to lack of transportation, possibly due to a combination of lack of access to resources and education, and more stigmatizing community attitudes.6 The data in this study uses a standard measure of rurality to further explore how rurality correlates with health outcomes beyond the rural/urban dichotomy. However, rurality has no universal definition and varies depending on the country or region. Rurality involves not just geography but also social characteristics such as cultural identity, economic structures, and access to services. Two given rural areas can be equally remote but vary in terms of infrastructure, culture, or community cohesion. The objective of this study is to address health disparities in rural areas but also closely examine how the standardized scales (epilepsy self-management scale [ESMS], epilepsy stigma scale [ESS], epilepsy self-efficacy scale [ESES], patient health questionnaire [PHQ-9], quality of life in epilepsy inventory 31 [QOLIE-31] etc.) differ according to the degree of rurality. We hypothesize that geography is one of the factors that are important to epilepsy self-management and that there are significant associations between rural geography and clinical variables such as self-efficacy, stigma, self-management, depression, and quality of life.

Methods

Ethics/IRB statement: self-management for PWE and a history of negative health events (SMART) 2 was approved by the University of Iowa's IRB. The IRB project number is 201910824.

Overview

This analysis used baseline data from a recently completed centers for disease control and prevention (CDC) funded RCT testing a novel epilepsy self-management program in 160 adults with epilepsy called SMART.6 SMART is an evidence-based intervention that aims to improve self-management skills and reduce stigma around epilepsy. The RCT used the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Rural-Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC) as a standardized measure to assess geographic residence (rural/urban)7 as well as other standardized measures of factors relevant to an individual’s ability to manage and cope with epilepsy.

Setting and recruitment

The study was conducted in Ohio and in Iowa, by investigators from Case Western Reserve University in Ohio and from the University of Iowa in collaboration with stakeholders in epilepsy lived experience and in rural healthcare.6 Participants were required to have a self-reported diagnosis of epilepsy; be at least 18 years old; have experienced at least one negative health event, defined as a seizure, emergency department visit, hospitalization, or self-harm attempt within the past 6 months; and be capable of providing written informed consent and engaging in study procedures. Of the 176 individuals assessed for eligibility, 16 were excluded, and 160 were randomized to the SMART curriculum or a 6-month waitlist control. The trial enrolled all 160 randomized participants; therefore, the sampling proportion was 100% of the randomized SMART trial sample. Sampling included intentional oversampling of rural residents using RUCC-based ZIP code filtering, but seizure burden was not used as a stratification or sampling factor. Efforts were made to ensure that the sample was representative of RPWE. The study team intentionally oversampled PWE who lived in rural or semi-rural areas by filtering health system electronic health records for ZIP codes in more rural locations according to RUCC codes. In addition, the study's entry criteria were intentionally broad in order to be more representative of the population of PWE. People who were actively suicidal or homicidal, as well as those with dementia, were excluded, as were pregnant women who would likely require different and more specialized treatments.

At the Iowa site, participants were recruited from local clinical and community referral networks, such as the Epilepsy Foundation of Iowa and primary care providers. In Ohio, recruitment efforts focused on individuals outside the Cleveland metropolitan area, with support from the Epilepsy Association, Epilepsy Alliance of Ohio, and epilepsy care professionals at the University of Cincinnati. This included advertisements and brochures. The study was approved by both recruiting site’s local Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) and was conducted from June 21, 2021 to December 6, 2024.

Measures

Geographical residence: clinical and demographic characteristics were assessed based on geography or degree of rurality. Participants were divided into three groups based on RUCC. The RUCC is a classification system that defines degree of rurality and urbanization in each U.S. county. It is a more nuanced way to distinguish regions beyond the binary classification of urban or rural. RUCC categories are based on population size and proximity to a metropolitan area. RUCC categories greater than or equal to four are considered nonmetro (U.S. Department of Agriculture Economic Research Service). The metro counties are defined according to population size of the metro area of which they are part, not the population of the county itself. The non-metro counties (RUCC codes of four or greater) are classified according to two dimensions: the size of the urban population and whether or not the county is adjacent to one or more metro areas. The sample in this study was divided into three geographical groups: metro as defined by RUCC=1, 2, 3; adjacent metro as defined by RUCC=4, 6, 8; and rural as defined by RUCC=5, 7, 9. A nonmetro county is considered adjacent if it borders one or more metro areas and has at least 2% of its labor force commuting to metro areas. Those counties that do not meet these adjacency criteria are considered nonadjacent (RUCC=5, 7, 9) (U.S. Department of Agriculture Economic Research Service).

Clinical scales

The ESMS is a 38-item scale that measures how well patients manage their lives in the following domains (subscales): seizure management, medication management, safety management, lifestyle management, and information management.8 Scores range from 39 to 190 with higher scores indicating higher utilization of self-management strategies.

The ESES is a 33-item scale that measures how confident an individual is in their ability to carry out tasks associated with epilepsy self-management in three domains: medication management, seizure management, and general management.9 Items are rated on an 11-point likert rating scale from 0 (“I cannot do at all”) to 10 (“Sure I can do”).

PHQ-9 is a nine-item scale that measures depression severity. Clinical depression is associated with scores of 10 or greater with scores of 15 or greater indicating major depressive disorder.10 It has been validated in adult patients with epilepsy.11

ESS is a 10-item scale that measures how much PWE perceives that epilepsy is negatively perceived in general and interferes with their relationships. Scores range from 0 to 7 with higher scores indicating greater perceived stigma.8

The rapid estimate of adult literacy in medicine, revised (REALM-R) is an 11-item scale that measures health literacy through word recognition of common medical terms. It asks participants to pronounce medical terms of increasing difficulty. Scores range from 0 to 8 with a score of 5 or less indicating low health literacy.12

QOLIE-31 is a 31-item questionnaire consisting of questions about emotional well-being and social functioning. The validity of the QOLIE-31 has been validated in previous studies in PWE.13 It is a screening tool for quality of life. Scores range from 0 to 100 with higher scores indicating a higher quality of life. It includes seven subscales: seizure worry, medication effects, overall quality of life, emotional well-being, energy/fatigue, cognitive functioning, and social functioning. The seizure worry subscale measures fear and anxiety about having seizures as well as their consequences (embarrassment, injury, stigma). The medication effects subscale measures perceived cognitive and physical side effects of antiepileptic drugs. The overall quality of life subscale measures one’s overall life satisfaction and perceived quality of life. The emotional well-being subscale assesses mood and symptoms of depression and anxiety. The energy subscale measures physical energy and fatigue levels. The cognitive functioning subscale assesses memory, concentration, attention, and problem-solving difficulties. The social functioning subscale assesses the impact of epilepsy on social activities and interpersonal relationships.14

The short form health survey (SF-36) is a 36-question survey that measures functional health status and consists of a mental component (MCS) and a physical component (PCS).15 It measures the overall burden of disease by assessing how much one’s health limits their physical and social activities. Scores range from 0 to 100 with higher scores indicating higher functioning.

The multidimensional scale of perceived social support (MSPSS) is a scale consisting of 12 items which measures perceived social support.16 Scores range from 12–84, higher scores are indicative of greater perceived social support.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics and descriptive statistics were generated in the full population and then by geography of residence. Distributions of variables amongst the residence locations were compared using Fisher's exact test and Kruskal-Wallis equality of populations rank test for categorical/binary and continuous variables, respectively. Multivariable linear regression models were conducted for each clinical scale and their subscales with geography of residence (0, metro; 1, adjacent metro; 2, rural) as the independent variable of interest including likely confounders. To identify confounders from amongst all available variables, we took a data-based approach to identify variables with a nominal association (p<0.25) with geography of residence as the dependent variable in a multivariable multinomial logistic regression models with potential covariates as independent variables. A stepwise backward process was used to identify the subset of covariates nominally associated with geography. These variables which included social determinants of health such as income and education were then included as covariates in the multivariable linear regression models where the clinical scales were the dependent variables. Normality of the clinical scales were evaluated, and ESMS seizure management score (SEM), ESMS safety management (SM), ESMS medication management (MM), ESES, quality of life in epilepsy, 31-item version total QOLIE-31 seizure worry, QOLIE-31 emotional wellbeing, QOLIE-31 energy, QOLIE-31 cognition, QOLIE-31 social functioning, PHQ-9, MSPSS, short-form 36 item functional health index, physical component score (SF-36 PCS), and short-form 36 item functional health index, mental component score (SF-36 MCS) were inverse rank-normalized to mean 0 with a standard deviation of 1. A two-sided alpha of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted in STATA ver 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Overview sample description

As shown in Tables 1, 2, 10.6% of the sample lived in rural areas, 20.6% lived in adjacent metro areas, and 68.8% lived in metro areas. The mean age of the entire sample was 39.37 years. It was comprised of 32.50% males and 66.88% female. 82.50% of the overall sample was white, 8.75% was black, and 8.75% was other. The overall study population had a relatively high level of education with 38.13% having completed some college and 39.38% having completed a Bachelor’s degree or more. A majority of the sample 69.18%, had one or more seizures in the past 30 days. There were no significant differences in means and proportions in demographic or standardized measure variables across the three geographic samples.

Correlates of geographic location residence

Table 3 shows that rurality, after adjusting for confounders (see Supplementary Table 1), had a statistically significant association with three outcomes: self-efficacy (ESES), QOLIE-31 total scores, and QOLIE-31 cognition sub scores. RPWE had lower ESES (p=0.045), lower QOLIE-31 total scores (p=0.025), and lower QOLIE-31-cognition sub score (p=0.043) compared to urban PWE. On average, RPWE had an 11-point lower QOLIE-31 total score, a 0.53-point lower ESES score, and a 0.53-point lower QOLIE-31 cognition sub score. Notably, there was no significant association between rurality and the following clinical scales: ESMS, QOLIE-31 medication effects, QOLIE-31 seizure worry, QOLIE-31 emotional wellbeing, QOLIE-31 energy, QOLIE-31 social functioning, ESS, PHQ-9, MSPSS, SF-36 PCS, or SF-36 MCS.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study using baseline data from an epilepsy self-management clinical trial examined demographic and clinical correlates of geographical location of residence, an area that has been understudied. Strengths of this study include the use of a standardized measure for rurality as well as validated measures of health domains that are critical for PWE. Overall, findings did not suggest significant demographic differences between RPWE and their urban/suburban counterparts and the groups were similar with respect to seizure burden, depression, stigma, social support, epilepsy self-management competency and general physical and mental health. However, RPWE reported worse overall quality of life, worse perceived cognition and lower self-efficacy. While several definitions of rurality exist, we define it as not being adjacent to a major metropolitan area, which assumes decreased access to tertiary care centers. Rural locales are especially underserved by neurologists and tertiary care centers17 and our findings align with existing literature that suggest health disparities for RPWE. Social determinants of health, including household income and education, differed across geographic groups and were included as confounders in adjusted models. After accounting for these and other covariates, rurality remained significantly associated with lower ESES scores, lower QOLIE-31 total scores, and lower QOLIE-31 cognition subscores.

Self-efficacy

The ESES is used to measure an individual’s confidence in carrying out tasks associated with epilepsy self-management.18 Previous studies have shown that factors that explain the variations in ESES include education, income status, gender, episode frequency, and depressive symptoms.19,20 While accounting for these confounding variables, our study demonstrated that geographic location independently was significantly associated with ESES scores. Compared to patients living in metro areas, patients located in a rural environment have lower ESES (p=0.045). This result is congruent with a previous study in Turkey indicating urban patient population have a higher ESES.21 Given the negative impact of low ESES on health outcomes in PWE, patients living in rural areas might particularly benefit from evidence-based epilepsy self-management such as the SMART program (University of Iowa and Case Western Reserve University, Iowa City and Cleveland, IA and OH, USA)22 and the Home-Based Self-management and Cognitive Training Changes lives (HOBSCOTCH) program (Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Center, Lebanon, NH, USA)23 developed by the U.S. Centers for Disease and Prevention (CDC)’s Epilepsy Self-Management Network. Such approaches could potentially reduce health disparities for RPWE.24

Quality of life

Many studies have demonstrated that PWE often experience reduced quality of life, which is frequently associated with increased seizure burden.25 However, seizure frequency is not the sole determinant of quality of life among PWE. Other significant factors that can contribute to quality of life in PWE are stigma, psychiatric comorbidities, physical health, employment and cognition.26 Our study found that RPWE in this sample reported lower global QOL than their urban counterparts. RPWE also reported worse subjective cognitive burden compared to urban counterparts. This suggests that cognitive difficulties may be particularly salient in rural settings. Interestingly, we did not find differences by geographical location for other sub-scales of QOL. It is possible that because having poorly controlled seizures was an inclusion criterion, this could have obscured differences that might otherwise be observed in QOL or general functioning. The findings of this study highlight the need for further research on quality of life in RPWE and what factors might contribute to improved quality of life in PWE beyond seizure frequency.

Cognition

Our study showed that RPWE scored lower on QOL cognition scale as compared to their urban counterparts. Around 20–50% of PWE have cognitive difficulties.27 These cognitive changes are due to the disease (seizure type, frequency of seizures, existing lesions) itself as well as medication side effects.28 Cognitive side effects include poor attention, difficulty with information processing and retrieval.29

Recent studies evaluating the HOBSCOTCH program (Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Center), another epilepsy self-management intervention designed specifically to address cognitive concerns in epilepsy, have shown promising improvements in subjective executive functioning of PWE. One RCT found that HOBSCOTCH participants experienced significant improvements in several domains of executive functioning including working memory, shifting, initiation, and inhibition.30 Similarly, a larger multi-center trial demonstrated that in-person HOBSCOTCH participants reported significant improvements in subjective cognition and memory perception. Within-group analysis also found that participants in both the in-person and virtual interventions had improvements in quality-of-life scores. This suggests that PWE who report cognitive problems might particularly benefit from HOBSCOTCH and it seems reasonable to expect that RPWE, who often have travel and in-person access limitations, could benefit from a virtual modality of this intervention.

Clinical and health policy implications

This study highlights the unique challenges that RPWE face beyond seizure control or physical health. Although causal mechanisms cannot be established from this cross-sectional analysis, these observed geographic differences underscore the need for more resource allocation to rural communities and how to increase the density of medical professionals that specialize in epilepsy in rural areas as well as how to better support general neurologists and primary care providers that treat RPWE. Policymakers should also consider integrated care models in rural regions that address not just medical care but also the psychosocial needs of PWE.31

Our findings also suggest the need for increasing telehealth-based cognitive and behavioral support programs for rural communities. It is important that clinicians do routine screening for cognitive functioning and self-efficacy in rural patients to help identify those at risk for poorer health outcomes. It is also important for health practitioners to encourage patients with epilepsy in rural areas to seek interventions that bolster self-management skills and target cognitive support.

Study limitations

While the study provides insight into geographic disparities among PWE, some limitations should be considered. First, the use of RUCC codes can mask within-county variation potentially leading to misclassification of participants’ rurality. Second, this was a cross-sectional study of baseline data which limits causal inference between rurality and poorer outcomes. Third, the sample size of rural participants was relatively small (10.6% of total sample) which may limit generalizability and statistical power. Finally, there may be selection bias as the participants enrolled in this self-management trial may represent a more motivated and engaged subset of the PWE population.

Our findings show that RPWE reported lower self-efficacy, poorer quality of life, and greater perceived cognitive impact on quality of life compared with their urban counterparts. Further research should explore how perceived cognitive deficits alter quality of life as well as interventions to increase self-efficacy.
