A comparative evaluation of canine meningioma supporting the canine patient as a naturally occurring animal model for human meningioma: a report from the NCI Comparative Brain Tumor Consortium (CBTC) meningioma pathology board
Authors: Molly E. Church, Daniel R. Rissi, Jey W. Koehler, Andrew D. Miller, Jessica A. Beck, Sara Belluco, Mirielle Bitar, Rati Chkheidze, Kara N. Corps, Kaspar Matiasek, Joanna J. Phillips, Sharika Rajan, Anat Stemmer-Rachamimov, Stephen Yip, Joanna H. Shih, Christina Mazcko, Amy LeBlanc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiagnostic classification criteria of canine meningioma remain poorly codified and have not been linked to outcome data in large scale studies employing standardized review of slides by a board of multiple pathologists.MethodsThe National Cancer Institute-led multidisciplinary Comparative Brain Tumor Consortium (CBTC) convened a meningioma pathology board, comprising both veterinarian pathologists and physician neuropathologists, and conducted a comprehensive review of 190 cases of canine meningioma. Comparative histologic and cellular features were assessed and discussed by the board for their similarities and differences to human meningioma. All cases were morphologically classified by histologic subtypes defined by the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) and by histologic grading system for human meningioma.ResultsThe majority of canine meningiomas were classified as meningothelial (140/190); most cases (109/190) were diagnosed as grade 2, 79 as grade 1, and two cases as grade 3. The histologic features with the highest agreement between reviewers were necrosis and central nervous system (CNS) invasion (intraclass correlation (ICC) of 0.44 for both features). Although morphologic features of the human grading scheme could be applied to canine tumors, there was no statistically significant difference in outcomes between grade 1 and grade 2 tumors in our patient subset with either detailed progression and cause of death information (n = 37) or data restricted to live/dead status (n = 84).ConclusionsWhile similarities exist between human and canine meningioma, a revised grading framework must be utilized for canine cases, distinct from the World Health Organization classification scheme for humans. Additional studies evaluating larger numbers of similarly treated dogs are necessary, and a framework for studies to enable future meta-analysis is suggested.

Background

Meningioma is the most diagnosed intracranial neoplasm in dogs, representing approximately 50% of all canine primary intracranial tumors [1]. Depending on the size, location, and degree of tumor invasion, dogs with meningioma can suffer from neurologic disease and decreased quality of life. Treatment typically consists of surgical excision, if the mass is accessible, followed by radiation and/or chemotherapy. Although surgical advances have been made, post-surgical recurrence is still common. Correlative data comparing gross and histologic tumor features with post-surgical outcome remains under-reported, and available investigations typically have a low number of patients, precluding accurate assessment of the clinical significance of these morphologic parameters [1–3]. Therefore, developing a standardized set of pathologic criteria that can be correlated with clinical outcomes will help guide prognosis and monitor therapeutic intervention, identify novel biomarkers that will improve diagnosis, and enhance discovery of therapeutic targets to improve treatment efficacy of canine meningioma.

The overarching goal of the Comparative Brain Tumor Consortium (CBTC) is to discover and develop new treatments and diagnostic strategies for both human and canine brain tumor patients through integration of the canine patient into all aspects of brain tumor research [4]. One of the top strategic priorities identified by the CBTC steering committee is the validation of the canine brain tumor patient as a model for human patients through comprehensive characterization of histologic, genomic, epigenomic, and clinical features of brain tumors. Therefore, a catalog of specific tumor subtypes and relevant biomarkers of disease is being developed and linked to clinical outcomes in both species. By doing so, we are positioning canine patients as a naturally occurring translational model for cancer research, and importantly, gaining knowledge and understanding of canine meningiomas for the benefit of canine patients.

While clinically informative molecular biomarkers have been identified in human meningioma, comparative morphologic and molecular investigations have yet to be carried out for canine meningioma [5]. In addition to NF2 alterations (including mutations, copy number losses, and even fusion events in post-XTRx meningioma), which are present in around half of human meningioma cases, recent next generation sequencing studies have identified other recurrent alterations (i.e. SMO, AKT1, TERT promoter) in recurrent and therapeutically refractory tumors [6–9]. Studies examining epigenetic modifications and histone methylation have also demonstrated promising diagnostic and prognostic value of genetic alterations in human meningiomas [10–12]. Importantly, single locus epigenetic biomarkers including losses of BAP1 and SMARCE1 as well as loss of H3K27me3 status, all identifiable with immunohistochemistry (IHC), are increasingly integrated into the molecular grading scheme and are of potential utility in prognosis [13–15]. The copy number and methylation landscapes of meningioma, generated by methylation microarray, is of use in stratifying the tumor subtype and is informative in prognosticating clinical behavior either independently or when integrated with morphology [5, 16].

A few smaller-scale studies evaluating canine meningiomas have shown differentially expressed genes in WHO grade 1 vs. grade 2 tumors at the transcriptional level and for selected markers at the protein level, as well as transcriptional differences between grade 1 tumors and normal meninges [17–19]. Several studies examining gene expression in canine meningiomas highlight expression profiles similar to human meningiomas and other cancers, but small sample sizes of these studies have hindered identification of significant clinical correlations [17, 19]. Such findings suggest that further molecular interrogation of canine meningiomas is needed to further validate their utility as a naturally occurring model of human meningiomas and to identify future therapeutic targets for treatment of canine and potentially human disease. Importantly, accurate usage of molecular data can only be done if it is informed by the histologic landscape of canine meningioma.

Our goals are to systematically describe the comparative histologic aspects of canine meningiomas and compare those with human meningiomas, fostering cross-disciplinary collaboration, encouraging future large-scale retrospective and prospective multi-institution studies, and further defining the role of the canine meningioma patient as a naturally occurring animal model of human meningioma. Secondly, we aim to report on inter-observer agreement on detection and quantification of histologic features within a large case set of canine meningiomas, highlighting similarities and differences between the diagnoses generated by veterinary and physician pathologists. Based on our results, we aim to apply the WHO classification guidelines for grading canine meningioma given that these histologic features were applied with relatively high concordance. This study builds upon CBTC development of a histologic classification scheme of canine glioma, which was subsequently applied to tumor biopsies, and is currently being used to correlate with genomic characterization in a subset of cases used in the classification scheme [20–22].

Methods

Canine patients and tumor samples

Canine meningiomas were collected, with a target of 200 confirmed cases, from archived tissues primarily from the diagnostic services of board members’ institutions and from institutions within the larger CBTC membership, including Auburn University College of Veterinary Medicine, Colorado State University College of Veterinary Medicine, Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine, Mississippi State University College of Veterinary Medicine, Ohio State University College of Veterinary Medicine, Texas A&M University School of Veterinary Medicine, University of California at Davis School of Veterinary Medicine, University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine, University of Pennsylvania School of Veterinary Medicine, and Virginia Tech College of Veterinary Medicine.

All tumors were collected from treatment-naïve patients, and priority was placed on cases that were collected within the past five years and had some measure of clinical follow-up data such as signalment, duration of clinical signs, date of diagnosis, tumor location (brain vs. spinal cord, with specific localization within areas of the brain or spinal cord), date and type of therapy (if any), ongoing clinical signs, date of progression, and date of death or euthanasia. Patients treated for clinical signs secondary to the meningioma were included (i.e. steroid therapy) as the therapy was not directed at the meningioma. Cause of death was determined, when possible, based on review of information provided by the treating veterinary clinician(s) that included assessment of clinical signs, imaging-based evidence of progression, or confirmatory autopsy data. Cases collected at surgery for diagnostic biopsy purposes were prioritized above autopsy specimens. A review of submitted case material was conducted by MEC and ADM to ensure original institutional diagnosis and quality of material available for analysis, leading to curation of the final set of tumors for review by the board (n = 190).

Histology slides were routinely prepared, cut at 4 μm, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and optically scanned as digital image files with Leica Aperio AT2 slide scanner (Leica Biosystems, Inc., Buffalo Grove, IL) at 40X resolution (approximately 0.25 μm/pixel). Scanned slides were viewed using ImageScope (Leica Biosystems, Inc., Buffalo Grove, IL) or PathoZoom® Digital Lab (Smart In Media AG, Cologne, Germany) for histologic evaluation. IHC was performed on a Leica Bond Max automated platform, which limited artifacts and variabilities between runs. Glass-mounted paraffin scrolls were dewaxed (Bond dewax solution; Leica), followed by heat epitope retrieval solution 2 (Leica) for 30 min.

The panel of veterinary pathologists and physician neuropathologists worked together to develop clear, concise consensus criteria for classification and grading. These criteria were based on the 2016 WHO classification guidelines for human meningiomas to allow for comparison, but incorporated species-specific features as identified and agreed upon. One aspect of the consensus evaluation that deviates from standard diagnostic practice is that reviewers were asked to give a primary morphologic subtype (meningothelial, transitional, etc.) even in cases of unconventional morphology in which there was little recognizable histologic pattern.

The features evaluated histologically consisted of (1) histologic subtype (meningothelial, transitional, microcytic, psammomatous, fibrous, rhabdoid, papillary, metaplastic, clear cell, chordoid, angiomatous, lymphoplasmacyte-rich), distinguished as major and minor histologic subtypes (major histologic subtype defined as the subtype demonstrated by 70% or greater of neoplastic cells examined), (2) mitotic count (number of mitotic figures, marked with the counter tool, that were observed in the most mitotically active areas using the rectangle tool with a fixed region size of 2.37 mm2), (3) presence of invasion into surrounding CNS parenchyma, (4) presence of geographic coagulative necrosis (thereby excluding single cell necrosis), and (5) features defined as atypical in human meningioma (sheeting architecture, small-cell formation, hypercellularity, and macronucleoli). Additionally, each reviewer applied the WHO grading scheme for human meningiomas and, based on the features listed above, assigned a grade to each tumor (Table 1).

An initial virtual meeting was held in October 2020 to familiarize physician neuropathologists with typical histologic features of canine meningioma. Representative examples of common human and canine histologic subtypes of meningioma were presented to demonstrate histologic features (such as arrangement of neoplastic meningothelial cells) and to define necrosis, CNS invasion, and atypical features. Selected de-identified human case material was also reviewed (led by SY) to demonstrate common human meningioma features. Three additional virtual meetings (January 2021, March 2021, September 2021) were held to review specific cases for which diagnostic features created a dilemma, especially regarding atypical features, including interpretation of macronucleoli and rhabdoid morphology, as two examples. A final assessment by all the reviewers of all case material according to the agreed upon rubric was performed and completed by 1 October 2021.

For statistical analysis, a consensus diagnosis for histologic subtype for each case was made based on a simple majority of diagnoses from the group. The mitotic count for each case was expressed as the median score for that case as a continuous variable. Other features (necrosis, CNS invasion, sheeting architecture, small-cell formation, hypercellularity, macronucleoli) were analyzed based on a simple majority of Yes or No from the reviewers. For association between histologic features and major histologic subtype, only subtypes with at least 20 entries were included.

Statistical analysis

Association of histologic features with major histologic subtype, tumor location and grade were evaluated using Wilcoxon rank-sum or Kruskal test for clustered data [23] in continuous variables and Chi-square test for clustered data [24] in categorical variables to account for multiple reviewers evaluating each case. Consensus across reviewers per case was determined by majority vote for categorical variables and by median for continuous variables. Regular Wilcoxon rank-sum/Kruskal test or Chi-square test were applied to consensus data for association analysis.

Chance-corrected reviewer agreement on the evaluation of dichotomous histologic features was determined by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC was obtained from variance components which were estimated by fitting a two-way random effects model with case and reviewer as the random intercepts and histologic feature as the binary response [25, 26]. For nominal histologic feature with more than 2 categories, the unweighted kappa statistic was used to measure reviewer agreement [26]. Confidence intervals of ICC and kappa were obtained by bootstrapping on the case level with replacement. The limits of the 95% confidence intervals were taken from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1,000 bootstrap samples. Agreement among DVM reviewers was compared against agreement among MD reviewers using the Z test with bootstrap standard error. P-values < 0.05 were considered significant in all statistical analysis. Analyses were performed in R version 4.0.4 using packages irr, lme4 and clusrank.

Overall survival (OS) was calculated as the time from date of diagnosis to death. Canines alive at the date of last follow-up, May 12, 2022, were censored. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to determine the time-to-event endpoints. Log-rank test was used to compare the difference in OS between subgroups. Progression was defined as animals that re-presented with clinical signs referable to the prior diagnosis of meningioma, regardless of confirmatory imaging or biopsy. A calculation of relative risk ratio was performed in an effort to capture data from animals still alive at the data collection point. Experimental conditions were defined as grade 2 vs. grade 1, and outcome was assigned alive or not alive of any cause, and the RRR along with confidence interval and P value were calculated using data from all animals whose live/dead status was known at 1, 2, and 3 years post-diagnosis. For relative risk ratios of grade 2 vs. grade 1 tumors, the numbers of animals with live/dead information available for the 1, 2, and 3 years timepoints was 101.

Results

Canine meningioma patients: clinical features and tumor characteristics

The final dataset contained 190 canine meningiomas. The median age was 10 years (range 3–16 years). A variety of breeds were represented, particularly mixed breed dogs (n = 53; 28%) followed by Golden Retrievers (n = 22; 12%), Labrador Retrievers (n = 15; 8%), Boxers (n = 12; 6%), and West Highland Terriers (n = 7; 4%). Most meningiomas were present overlying the brain (n = 155; 82%), with the majority of these located in the olfactory lobe (n = 47; 25%) or frontal lobe (n = 46; 24%). The brainstem was the next most common location (n = 22; 12%) followed by cerebellum (n = 10; 5%), occipital lobe (n = 6; 3%), parietal lobe (n = 4; 2%), temporal lobe (n = 1; 1%). No specific brain site was designated in 19 cases (10%). Of the 29 spinal cord meningiomas (15% of the total cases), distribution by region in order of decreasing frequency was cervical (n = 13, 45%), lumbar (n = 4, 14%), and thoracic (n = 2, 7%), with ten cases lacking segment localization.

Tumor collection method

In 122 cases (64%), meningiomas were collected during surgery with the intent of definitive resection, while 9 tumors (5%) were collected via incisional biopsy for diagnostic purposes only. Approximately one third of cases (n = 59; 31%) were collected during autopsy.

Treatment

All tumor samples were evaluated prior to any treatment, apart from routine use of steroid therapy, which was commonly administered as an adjunct palliative measure along with anti-convulsant medications. Therapy was elected for 125 of the 131 dogs diagnosed antemortem; in six cases patients underwent a biopsy to confirm the diagnosis and did not receive any subsequent therapy. Most of these 125 dogs (n = 87; 69.6%) underwent surgery alone, while the remaining dogs underwent some combination of surgery, radiation, immunotherapy, or chemotherapy (Table 2). A specific timeframe of therapy and of progression post-diagnosis were not uniformly reported or discernable from the retrospective data provided by multiple institutions.

Recurrence/clinical progression

Of the 103 dogs that were diagnosed with meningioma antemortem by biopsy, the dates of death reported, survival times ranged from 0 to 2,246 days (median = 404 days). These patients had either a non-therapeutic diagnostic biopsy procedure (n = 6) or some form of excisional or debulking surgical therapy (n = 95). For dogs that received surgery as some part of their treatment strategy and had follow up data available (n = 94), the median survival time (MST) was 437 days, with 40% surviving under 300 days and 60% surviving over 300 days. Outcome data that specifically documented tumor recurrence and/or progression based upon re-imaging, surgical findings, or neurological examination was available for 37 cases (about 20% of cases). Most of these cases (35/37; 95%) had worsening clinical signs consistent with recurrence including seizures or nebulous neurologic signs (i.e. rear leg twitching). Twenty-four (24/37; 65%) had repeat imaging or surgery that confirmed regrowth of the primary tumor including MRI (n = 17). Of the 37 cases with outcome data, the date of progression was available for 25. In these cases, the median time to progression was 369 days (range 32–1066 days). The median survival for cases with recurrence was 553 days (range 21–2246 days). A second, de novo meningioma was diagnosed in 3 cases via MRI (2 in the brain and 1 in the spinal cord).

Histologic findings

The major histologic subtypes were meningothelial (n = 140; 74%, Fig. 1A), transitional (n = 26; 14%, Fig. 1B), microcystic (n = 8; 4%, Fig. 1C), psammomatous (n = 7; 4%, Fig. 1D), rhabdoid (n = 3; 1.5%, Fig. 1E), fibrous (n = 3; 1.5%, Fig. 1F), metaplastic (n = 2; 1%, Fig. 1G), clear cell (n = 1; 0.5%, Fig. 1H), and chordoid (n = 1; 0.5%, Fig. 1I) (Table 3). While some histologic subtypes such as papillary (Fig. 1J), angiomatous (Fig. 1K), secretory (Fig. 1L), and lymphoplasmacyte-rich were not identified as a major subtype in any of our cases, features of most subtypes described in humans were present in at least some areas of our canine tumors. For example, none of the meningiomas in this set were papillary as the primary histologic subtype, however, 22 of the tumors had papillary features as at least one of the minor histologic subtypes. Additionally, while rhabdoid morphology was detected as the major subtype in 3 cases, 19 cases had minor features of this subtype.

Histologic features associated with higher grade meningioma in humans were commonly noted in our canine meningiomas (Table 4). Of the 111 cases with adjacent neuroparenchyma to assess, approximately 60% (n = 67) had CNS invasion; however, invasion was not significantly associated with a specific histologic subtype. Other atypical features (geographic necrosis, macronucleoli, sheeting architecture, small cell formation, and hypercellularity) were observed in many of the canine cases (Fig. 2). Clear cell meningiomas were most likely to lack necrosis (1/26 cases with clear cell features had necrosis called by most pathologists), while meningiomas with any degree of rhabdoid morphology were the most likely to have necrosis (29/40 cases).

Some cytomorphologic features of canine meningioma are unique. Regardless of histologic subtypes, neoplastic canine meningothelial cells frequently have a prominent nucleolus and many have intranuclear cytoplasmic invaginations that may appear as longitudinal barring of nuclei, imparting a “coffee bean” appearance or as clear or eosinophilic nuclear pseudoinclusions. Additionally, intratumoral infiltrates of neutrophils unassociated with necrosis were present in some cases. These infiltrates were not considered to be true necrosis and, therefore, were not quantified.

Evaluation of potential associations between histologic features and tumor location revealed a significant association only between location (brain vs. spinal cord) and the presence of geographic necrosis. Invasion, sheeting architecture, small cell formation, hypercellularity, and presence of macronucleoli did not differ significantly between tumors in the brain compared to those in the spinal cord. There was also no difference between specific regions in the brain (olfactory, frontal, or other lobes) nor specific regions in the spinal cord (cervical, lumbar) and histologic features.

Reviewers applied the histologic criteria of the WHO grading scheme for human meningiomas (invasion and atypical features of necrosis, sheeting, small cell formation, hypercellularity and macronucleoli) to the 190 canine meningiomas. Grade 1 was assigned by most of the reviewers to 79 meningiomas (42%), grade 2 was assigned to 109 (57%) meningiomas, and grade 3 was assigned to 2 (1%) meningiomas. In human meningiomas, the presence of invasion is indicative of a grade 2 meningioma. By consensus, 77% of grade 2 canine meningiomas with adjacent neuroparenchyma (n = 86) had brain/spinal cord invasion (n = 66). Of these 66 cases, the majority (n = 42) exhibited two or fewer other features of atypia (i.e. necrosis, mitotic count > 4/10HPF, sheeting architecture, small cell formation, hypercellularity, or macronucleoli). Only 24 cases exhibited 3 out of 5 atypical features.

Most grade 2 canine meningiomas demonstrated necrosis (66%) by consensus. Additionally, histologic features of atypical human meningiomas that are designated as grade 2 include necrosis, sheeting, small cell formation, hypercellularity and macronucleoli. Approximately 56% of grade 2 canine meningiomas by consensus demonstrated hypercellularity.

In humans, mitotic activity is standardized to grade 1 (fewer than 6), grade 2 [8–30], and grade 3 (greater than 30) in 2.37 mm2 (the equivalent area of ten high power 40X fields on a microscope with 10X objectives). Mitotic count in the cases reviewed here tended to be low for all meningiomas, with the average for all cases being 1.5 mitoses in 2.37 mm2. Additionally, the cases diagnosed in this study with histologic subtypes indicative of grade 2 or 3 did not have increased mitotic activity. For example, none of the three rhabdoid subtypes diagnosed had average mitotic counts greater than 2. The one chordoid meningioma in this series had an average mitotic count of 2.6. Ten cases had mitotic counts greater than 4; nine of these cases were meningothelial and one was transitional subtype. At the individual reviewer level, there was a significant association between tumor grade and mitotic count in 2.37 mm2 when the mitotic count threshold was set at both 1 and 4. At the consensus level, there was a significant association between tumor grade and mitotic count in 2.37 mm2 with a threshold set at 1 but not 4.

Only 50% (n = 54) of grade 2 meningiomas demonstrated sheeting by consensus of reviewers, 41% (n = 45) of grade 2 meningiomas had most of the reviewers confirm the presence of macronucleoli, and 17% (n = 19) of grade 2 meningiomas demonstrated small cell formation according to most reviewers. When association between histologic features and grade were considered individually and not by consensus (i.e. treating each reviewer’s grade assignment as a distinct data point rather than as a part of the majority consensus among the group), necrosis, CNS invasion, sheeting architecture, and hypercellularity were correlated with grade 2 canine meningiomas. For evaluation of small cell formation, the absence of these features correlated with grade assignment (p < 0.001, p = 0.002, and p = 0.001, respectively).

Histologic and clinical correlation

Of the 37 cases with progression data, 34 also had anatomic location data. Eight cases were in the spinal cord and 26 cases were located in the brain. Of those in the brain, 24 (92%) demonstrated progression.

Canine meningioma subtypes mirror the diverse morphologic patterns observed in human meningioma, including the specific histologic subtypes that are designated as grades 2 and 3, such as chordoid, clear cell, papillary, and rhabdoid. Of the 37 cases with progression data, 28 were meningothelial meningiomas; there were five transitional, two microcystic, and single each of clear cell and metaplastic subtypes. Of these 28 meningothelial meningiomas, 78% (n = 22) were grade 2. Of these higher-grade subtypes, there was only one clear cell subtype with available clinical follow up data in this study. This dog lived 1,290 days post diagnosis and was euthanized for recurrent seizures without diagnostic imaging confirming the clinical progression of the tumor.

Regardless of histologic subtype, 65% of cases with progression data (n = 24) were grade 2 meningiomas. When comparing grade 2 tumors (n = 53) to grade 1 tumors (n = 48) within the cohort for which overall survival data was available, the relative risk for death one year after diagnosis was 0.75; two years after diagnosis was 0.93 and three years after diagnosis was 1.02; none of these were statistically significant (P = 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 respectively). Too few animals had grade 3 tumors to perform statistical analysis for that group. In addition to grade 1 versus 2 being unassociated with an increased risk of death, none of the assessed morphologic features were associated with overall survival or with progression/recurrence at the consensus or reviewer levels.

Evaluation of CNS invasion, which is indicative of WHO grades 2 and 3 in humans, was evaluated in regions of neuroparenchyma adjacent to tumor tissue when available in the submitted sample. Of the 112 cases where tumor invasion could be evaluated, 19 cases had progression data available. Ten of these cases had both invasion and clinical progression (55%), whereas 8 (45%) cases without evidence of invasion at the time of diagnosis also had clinical progression. Importantly, the survival time in 5 of these 8 cases was greater than 181 days. There was 1 case that lacked histologic evidence of invasion and did not have clinical progression. None of tumors in the spinal cord had sufficient adjacent tissue for assessing invasion.

Necrosis, another atypical histologic feature used in grading human meningioma [27] was also evaluated. A majority of cases evaluated histologically lacked necrosis (113/190). Interestingly, of the 37 outcome-linked cases with clinical progression, only 14 (40%) exhibited necrosis, while 21 (60% of these cases) did not exhibit necrosis. The two outcome-linked cases without clinical progression also lacked necrosis. Finally, none of the spinal cord tumors that progressed clinically demonstrated necrosis histologically.

Consensus across board members

Cases were reviewed by 7 DVM/VMDs and 6 MDs. Reviewer concordance was assessed for major histologic subtype, tumor grade, and all other specific histologic features listed in Tables 3, 4 and 5. There was significant correlation among reviewers (p < 0.001) with application of histologic features as they are applied to grading of human meningiomas for brain/spinal cord invasion, necrosis, hypercellularity, and sheeting. Concordance was good (> 70%) for most histologic subtypes assessed, especially those with distinct cytomorphologic appearance (like clear cell and microcystic), with exceptions often being in the less common tumors (i.e. metaplastic subtype).

For agreement across reviewers and specific assessment of training background (DVM/VMD vs. MD), ICC was generally low for all histologic features and major histologic subtype. Lowest agreement was seen for detection of mitoses and assessment of mitotic count, while highest ICCs were seen for detection of necrosis and CNS invasion, which are atypical features. DVMs agreed more than MDs, and agreement was significant for assignment of major histologic subtype, hypercellularity, and macronucleoli. At the consensus level, there was a significant association between tumor grade (grade 1 vs. grade > 1) and mitotic count in 2.37 mm2 with a mitotic count threshold set at 1 but not 4.

While our interobserver agreement was lower than the concordance typically achieved by medical pathologists for human meningiomas [28], it was statistically classified as good concordance. Canine and human meningioma share important histologic features, but the reproducibility of tumor grading varies. Similar to what we found, brain invasion and geographic necrosis were the features with the highest agreement among reviewers in human and canine tumors [28, 29]. The poor concordance on objective features such as metaplastic areas and the psammomatous subtype likely reflects the extreme rarity of these variants in our dataset.

More subjective histologic features such as sheeting and small cell formation consistently had the lowest concordance in human and canine tumors [28, 29]. In addition, low agreement was reported for the diagnosis of tumor necrosis in another set of canine meningioma [29]. Notably, the higher consensus among veterinary pathologists compared to medical pathologists was statistically significant for the assignment of histologic subtypes, hypercellularity, and macronucleoli in our cases. This suggests that species-specific experience may be critical for diagnostic consistency. For instance, medical pathologists observed that macronucleoli appeared more frequently in canine than in human tumors, leading to different thresholds for identification compared to their veterinary colleagues.

Discussion

Patient demographics and tumor location

Meningiomas are the most common primary brain tumor in both humans and dogs, accounting for about 40% and 50% of all primary brain tumors, respectively [1, 30–32]. This is the first study to systematically review and grade a large cohort of canine meningiomas and examine relationships between tumor subtype and grade with clinical outcomes. The median age of dogs in our study (10 years) is similar to what has previously been reported (10.5 years) for canine meningioma [1]. Additionally, while there was no dominant canine breed in our study, Golden Retrievers, Labrador Retrievers, and Boxers were the most commonly reported breeds (12%, 8%, 6%, respectively) after mixed breed dogs (28%). Previous studies have found an overrepresentation of Golden Retrievers and Boxers for intracranial meningiomas, which may simply reflect their breed popularity and not a true predisposition [31–33]. The propensity for meningiomas to occur in the rostral telencephalon (frontal and olfactory lobes) and cervical spinal cord in our cases also correlates with veterinary neuropathology textbooks and the diagnostic experience of the veterinary pathologists in the group [1].

Histologic findings

The majority of canine meningiomas in our study were meningothelial. Since pathologists in this study were asked to assign a histologic subtype even if the tumor had relatively featureless (atypical) architecture, it is possible that meningothelial tumors were over-diagnosed as they possess the least distinctive morphology and therefore may have been selected for those cases by default. This study method differs from the standard diagnostic workflow in which tumors with a predominance of these features would be designated as atypical meningiomas. The absence of numerous subtypes meeting the minimum 20-case threshold for statistical analysis makes comparing clinical follow-up data between some of the more uncommon histologic subtypes difficult.

Invasion was relatively common in our cases, which could be due to either the inherent biologic behavior of canine meningioma or the tendency for dogs to be diagnosed later in the disease process. Because subtle clinical signs early in the course of disease can be difficult for owners to detect, tumors may grow larger and become more necrotic and invasive before our canine patients can be accurately diagnosed. Importantly, recent consensus in human medicine has found that some meningiomas that only demonstrate brain invasion in the absence of other atypical features (so-called, Brain-invasive, otherwise benign, or BIOB), benefit from additional molecular testing [34]. In this study, the majority of grade 2 meningiomas that demonstrated brain invasion, only had two or fewer atypical features. This suggests that additional molecular characterization could benefit prognostication in canine meningioma, as in humans.

In general, many of the histologic subtypes of canine meningiomas mirror those observed in human meningiomas. However, specific cytomorphologic features associated with higher grade in human tumors (i.e. macronucleoli) were found to be quite common in our canine meningioma cases, independent of tumor grade. As noted above, the lack of large numbers of cases in each histologic subtype as well as the low number of cases with full clinical outcome information precluded our ability to correlate some specific histologic subtypes (i.e. subtypes chordoid, rhabdoid, papillary, etc.) with more aggressive behavior as is the scenario in human meningioma; however, this should be a focus of research exploration moving forward.

Histologic grading

Histologically, canine meningiomas can be classified into many of the same morphologic subtypes as human meningiomas, and many veterinary pathologists classify and grade these tumors according to the WHO classification system developed for human meningiomas [35]. However, few published reports correlate clinical outcome with uniform application of specific grading systems in canine patients [33, 36], including those that recommend applying the WHO grading scheme to canine meningioma [29]. While studies in veterinary pathology have highlighted what is widely known (that canine meningiomas are histologically similar in appearance to meningiomas in humans), correlation of the histologic criteria used in the WHO grading scheme with clinical progression or recurrence in canine patients has not been established in any large-scale study published thus far. In humans, grade 2 meningiomas have higher rates of progression and recurrence than do grade 1 meningiomas, as do some specific histologic subtypes [30]. While this has been assumed to be the same in dogs, sufficiently powered studies demonstrating correlation between histologic characteristics (as assessed by a panel of pathologists performing a systematic review of all tumors) and clinical outcome in canine patients has not been done. The number of cases with detailed clinical progression or recurrence data in our study did not allow for robust correlation of histological features with final clinical outcome when live patients were censored to allow for Kaplan-Meier analysis. However, the relative risk ratios on the larger cohort of 101 patients with live/dead outcomes from all causes at 1, 2, and 3 years suggests that the distinction between grade 2 vs. grade 1 tumors in the dog as defined by human WHO criteria may not be significant to patient survival. Therefore, standardization of a grading system specific to histologic features of canine meningioma predictive of progression and survival is warranted.

Features of canine meningiomas that should be further evaluated further evaluated as components of a unique grading system include tumor invasion and geographic necrosis. Although similar proportions of cases with invasion at diagnosis had disease progression compared to those without invasion, the changing paradigm of this feature in human medicine justifies additional evaluation in the dog, both for the benefit of dogs and in support of their use as a model. The data supporting the inclusion of necrosis in a grading system, on the other hand, is stronger. While we found that many cases that demonstrated clinical progression did not have necrosis, 100% of the cases that had necrosis had progression compared to 57% in cases that did not have necrosis. This suggests that necrosis, when present, may be a significant indicator of more aggressive tumor behavior and is a critical feature to pursue in future prognostic studies. Further studies with larger numbers of cases may be able to expand on these associative trends of atypical features as applied by the WHO grading scheme in canine meningioma.

Although in our study there was a strong association between some of the components of the WHO grading system for human meningiomas (invasion and atypical features of necrosis, sheeting, and hypercellularity) with grading assignment by reviewers (p = 0.002 or less), suggesting that the human classification criteria can be accurately applied to canine meningiomas, it likely is insufficient or inaccurate for stratifying canine patients. Others have applied the WHO grading system to canine meningioma and found that its application can be done with similar accuracy [32]. Importantly, however, some features associated with higher grades (2 and 3) in human meningiomas, specifically small cell formation and the presence of macronucleoli, were correlated with grade 1 canine meningiomas in our study. Designation of macronucleoli was frequently a topic of discussion at our consensus meetings, as MD pathologists commented that subjectively this feature was present more frequently in canine cases, even grade 1 tumors, relative to human cases. The other group studying canine meningioma recommended that macronucleoli not be used in as a grading feature given that in their study there was low reproducibility [29].

Agreement and consensus

In general, there was variable agreement between reviewers. Strong concordance was found for several histologic subtypes and atypical features, whereas less concordance was noted for other features. This is likely due to the relatively small numbers of cases (especially grade 3 tumors and certain histologic subtypes with fewer than ten cases to evaluate, such as chordoid, clear cell, fibrous, microcystic, metaplastic, psammomatous, and rhabdoid) and to the large number of pathologists with varying backgrounds. Additionally, poor or fair ICC on any one individual criterion can be statistically significant without being biologically significant if it does not alter the overall diagnosis, grade, or subsequent patient management. Discussion about certain histologic features, specifically macronucleoli, would have likely improved agreement. Finally, this low degree of agreement highlights the need for additional collaborative project work between DVMs and MDs to achieve better agreement, which is a goal for future CBTC efforts.

Treatment and clinical follow up data

Surgery is currently the mainstay therapy for meningioma and was the most common treatment modality in the study here. However, a recent study demonstrated longer mean survival with radiation therapy compared to surgery [42]. Consistent with other retrospective studies [33], we did not find any association between a particular type of treatment and outcome.

The median survival of dogs with surgery only (437 days, ~ 14 months) is similar to the 10-month MST previously reported for canine meningiomas post debulking [37]. However, with more extensive techniques, like surgery combined with radiation and other agents, the MST for a small number of patients in our cohort increased to 28–55 months, which is also similar to the 16–70 months range that has been previously reported [1].

Adjuvant radiation therapy for canine meningiomas can significantly prolong survival time compared to surgery alone (16.5 months vs. 7 months) [1, 3]. While not statistically significant, the cases in this study that received adjuvant therapy beyond surgery (radiation and chemotherapy), survived longer on average (30 and 24 months, respectively). It is important to note that survival data reported in many studies relies on presumptive diagnoses (primary or recurrent) based on imaging, as brain and spinal cord biopsy is rarely performed in companion dogs. Previously published work has demonstrated a probability ranging between 79.2% and 89.7% in diagnosing the type of intracranial neoplasia present using imaging modalities alone [38]. Additionally, we were only able to evaluate survival in dogs that had a documented date of death. Importantly, the reason for death was not uniformly available, and therefore, it cannot be determined if animals died or were euthanized due to their meningioma or to another disease.

One of the limitations of studies in veterinary medicine in general and of this study specifically, is the number of cases with detailed and final clinical follow-up data. Despite the relatively high number of cases evaluated by pathologists in this case series (190), only a small proportion of cases [37] had reliable clinical follow-up data including confirmed progression by imaging or cause of death that would allow for accurate Kaplan-Meier analysis with death attributed to meningioma. The low number also demonstrates the dire need for large scale cancer databases in veterinary medicine, as exist in human medicine [39, 40]. Veterinary medicine lacks large-scale shared databases with clinical and molecular information for vast numbers of cases analogous to human databases like The Cancer Genome Atlas (National Cancer Institute), the International Cancer Genome Consortium (Ontario Institute for Cancer Research), and the UCSC Cancer Genomics Browser [41]. Germline or somatic alteration of NF2 is implicated in a large number of familial and sporadic meningioma in human, whereas previous genomic profiling efforts have not found any alterations of NF2 in chromosome 26 in dogs [42]. However, these earlier findings should be re-examined using better resolution technologies including second-generation sequencing and microarrays. Furthermore, while there are a small number of veterinary biobanks housed at veterinary schools or focused collection of samples from a single breed of dog or cat [43–45], the establishment of multi-institutional biobanks with systematically collected clinical information matched to biological samples would not only benefit the veterinary patient population but would also streamline molecular comparisons between veterinary and human neoplastic disease that could prove helpful in comparative clinical trials of novel cancer therapies. Finally, combining efforts to enhance study sizes, especially internationally, would be beneficial in veterinary medicine. Our study included a majority of pathologists from the US and two pathologists from Europe. While all the samples were from veterinary teaching hospitals in the United States, future studies would benefit greatly from more diverse international collaborative efforts.

Additional analyses of these and related cases of canine meningioma are already underway to address molecular characterization and determine immune landscape. Eventually immune landscape and tumor microenvironmental features with transcriptional profiles and genomic data will be examined from a related collection of canine tumors to further enhance our ability to diagnose and treat canine patients. These studies will allow for detection of deployable biomarkers that could potentially be targeted by future therapies. A project evaluating transcriptional landscape in a separate set of tumors (n = 70) is underway. These tumors will be reassessed according to this board’s rubric to further the molecular characterization of distinct tumor subtypes.

Conclusions

Because there is currently insufficient evidence to suggest that the features used to delineate human meningiomas have relevance to dogs, and some evidence to suggest that it does not, the CBTC recommends that future studies incorporate the following elements, which will allow for robust meta-analysis of multiple smaller studies at various institutions around the world:

If this long follow-up time is not possible, consideration should be given to the use of alternative statistics (relative risk ratio etc.) to capture information on patients still alive at the time of data collection in addition to Kaplan-Meier curves.

Given that macronucleoli are frequently present in canine cases, there is justification to omit the use of macronucleoli as a criterion for grading in canine tumors, and that therefore the use of the human “three out of the five” atypical features is not appropriate in the dog. It is our hope that using these common-sense guidelines will lead to robust international studies that can be subsequently aggregated and used to determine which histologic features are relevant to outcomes in the dog, as well as form foundational case collections onto which subsequent molecular inquiries can be made.
