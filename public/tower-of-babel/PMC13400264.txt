Endovascular repair of a giant inferior mesenteric artery aneurysm
Authors: Alesha Salby, Nicholas Piening, Javier Vasquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInferior mesenteric artery (IMA) aneurysms are rare visceral artery aneurysms. We present the case of a 65-year-old man with multiple comorbidities who presented with rectal bleeding and was found to have a 7 cm IMA aneurysm with an associated IMA inferior mesenteric vein arteriovenous fistula. Given the aneurysm size and rupture risk, the patient underwent successful endovascular coil embolization with complete occlusion of the aneurysm and fistula on completion angiography. This case highlights the rare coexistence of an IMA aneurysm and an IMA inferior mesenteric vein arteriovenous fistula, for which the temporal and causal relationship remains uncertain, and supports the efficacy of endovascular coil embolization in high-risk patients.Keywords: Inferior mesenteric artery aneurysm, Arteriovenous fistula, Visceral artery aneurysm, Endovascular coil embolization, Gastrointestinal bleeding

Inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) aneurysms are an extremely rare vascular pathology, accounting for approximately 1% of all visceral artery aneurysms.1,2 Most aneurysms within this category are defined as true aneurysms, involving all three-vessel wall layers with dilation ≥1.5 times normal.3 Although the size distribution of IMA aneurysms is poorly characterized in literature, one review reported an average diameter of 3.0 ± 1.8 cm, with 2 cm being the recognized threshold for surgical intervention.1,4

Among the limited reported cases of IMA aneurysms, most patients present asymptomatically and are discovered incidentally. When symptoms do occur, they include abdominal pain, gastrointestinal bleeding, or pulsatile mass.2,3,5 Although a rare pathology, visceral artery aneurysms carry a significant risk of rupture at approximately 25% to 40%, and a mortality rate as high as 76%.3,6 Most reported cases attribute IMA aneurysms to celiac trunk and/or superior mesenteric artery (SMA) occlusion, resulting in a compensatory high-flow state.3,7,8,9

In contrast, IMA aneurysms associated with high-flow states secondary to arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) are extremely rare.10,11 An AVF is an abnormal connection between the arterial and venous system bypassing the capillary beds, resulting in a high pressure arterial system being shunted to a low pressure venous system.12,13 The persistent high-flow state increases arterial shear stress and is accompanied by vascular remodeling, as demonstrated by studies analyzing high-flow vascular conditions.14,15 We present the case of a patient with a 7 cm IMA aneurysm with an associated IMA-inferior mesenteric vein (IMV) AVF. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of the case details and accompanying images.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old man with a past medical history of T cell large granular lymphocytic leukemia (currently in remission), perforated diverticulitis status post-Hartmann's procedure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heterozygous factor V Leiden, portal hypertension with chronic portal venous thrombosis (from presumed liver flukes), and a splenic artery aneurysm was admitted for evaluation of a 7 cm IMA aneurysm (Fig 1). The patient initially presented to the emergency department for an episode of rectal bleeding. Upon admission, the patient was afebrile, with a blood pressure of 132/94 mm Hg, pulse of 121 beats/min, and a respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min. Physical examination was unremarkable, with no signs of acute cardiopulmonary, abdominal, neurological, or musculoskeletal pathology. The patient declined a digital rectal examination. Pertinent laboratory findings included thrombocytopenia (platelets, 49 × 103/μL), mild anemia (hemoglobin, 11.9 g/dL), and a mildly prolonged prothrombin time (15.5 seconds) with an international normalized ratio of 1.3. Lactate was within normal limits at 0.8 mmol/L. Given the presence of active sigmoid diverticulitis, colonoscopy was deferred for 6-8 weeks. A computed tomographic angiography of the abdomen and pelvis was performed in the emergency department for workup of his rectal bleeding, which revealed a 7 cm aneurysm anterior to the aortic bifurcation caused by an AVF between the IMA and IMV (Fig 2). Given the patient's comorbidities, previous computed tomography scans dating back to 2020 were reviewed. No evidence of an IMA aneurysm was reported in the previous imaging results. Recent preintervention imaging demonstrated no evidence of celiac or SMA stenosis. However, imaging demonstrated chronic portal vein thrombosis with cavernous transformation, as well as a partially calcified thrombus involving the superior mesenteric and splenic veins.

Coronal view of computed tomography (CT) scan depicting 7.2 × 6.85 cm inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) aneurysm.

Angiogram showing filling of inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) aneurysm from fistula with inferior mesenteric vein (IMV).

Vascular surgery was consulted for further evaluation and recommendations.

Owing to the size of the IMA aneurysm, the patient was taken the following day for IMA angiogram with coil embolization of the aneurysm. The patient was taken to the operating room, prepped, and draped in the standard fashion. The right common femoral artery was accessed under ultrasound guidance. A 5F sheath and a flush catheter were placed. Aortogram was performed without abnormalities. A 7F steerable sheath was placed. The IMA was cannulated, and an IMA angiogram was performed demonstrating large IMA aneurysm (Fig 2). A wire and catheter were able to be advanced into the aneurysm. An angiogram within the aneurysm was performed showing a fistulous connection with the IMV (Fig 2). We then performed coil embolization of the aneurysm in the standard fashion using multiple Penumbra Ruby XL (Alameda) and packing coils. Completion angiogram was performed showing no filling of the aneurysm or IMV outflow (Fig 3). All wires and catheters were removed, and the case was completed.

Discussion

IMA aneurysms are rare, representing approximately 1% of visceral artery aneurysms.1 Although visceral aneurysms may be identified in up to 10% of autopsies, most remain asymptomatic and are incidentally discovered.3 The average reported diameter of an IMA aneurysm is roughly 3.0 ± 1.8 cm.4

IMA aneurysms carry a substantial risk of rupture, with reported mortality rates up to 20% to 50%.1,3 Risk factors for rupture are poorly defined. However, pseudoaneurysms demonstrate significantly higher rupture risk (up to 76%) compared with true aneurysms (∼3%), with size being a less reliable predictor.16

Due to the rarity of this condition, there are no specific Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines for IMA aneurysms, and management is individualized.17 Intervention is generally recommended for symptomatic, ruptured, or enlarging aneurysms, particularly those >2 cm. Recent literature suggests a lower threshold (1.5 cm) given the high rupture risk and asymptomatic progression.4

This case is notable for the large aneurysm size (7 cm) and its association with an IMA-IMV AVF, an exceedingly rare etiology. Most reported cases of IMA aneurysm have been associated with compensatory high-flow states secondary to celiac trunk or SMA occlusion. However, visceral vessel occlusion represents only one of three proposed mechanisms underlying IMA dilation. Given the rarity of IMA aneurysms, the relative prevalence of these etiologies remains difficult to determine.3,7 In this case, the IMA-IMV AVF was likely a consequence of inflammation related to previous surgery for perforated diverticulitis. Vascular remodeling in the setting of a persistent high-flow state, along with inflammatory arterial wall degeneration, likely contributed to aneurysm formation.

To our knowledge, only two previously reported cases of IMA aneurysms associated with AVFs have been reported. In both cases, aneurysms exceeded treatment thresholds but were managed conservatively due to regression or prohibitive surgical risk.11,18

Given the aneurysm size and symptomatic presentation, intervention was indicated. In the setting of cirrhosis and previous abdominal surgery, an endovascular approach was favored over open repair. Coil embolization provided a minimally invasive and effective treatment strategy.

Conclusions

IMA aneurysms are rare but clinically significant due to their high risk of rupture and associated mortality. We report a 7-cm IMA aneurysm with an associated AVF, a rarely described mechanism of aneurysm formation. Successful treatment with endovascular coil embolization demonstrates the utility of minimally invasive approaches in appropriately selected patients.

This case expands the limited literature on AVF-associated IMA aneurysms and underscores the importance of early diagnosis and individualized management.
