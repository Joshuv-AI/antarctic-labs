ANL/CFCT-25/30

 Towards Portable Laser
 Desorption Postionization Mass
 Spectrometry Utilizing
 Microchip Laser Pulses

by
Jason M. Gross, David J. Bettinardi, Nicholas J. Condon
Chemical and Fuel Cycle Technology Division, Argonne National Laboratory, Lemont, IL 60439

For
U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Science

SEPTEMBER 2025

Project Summary: A 355 nm, 1.5 ns, up to 1 kHz microchip laser system has been tested to both
yield ablation spots and postionize material within a plasma plume. A 30 µJ pulse energy microchip
laser successfully ablated all samples: 6601 aluminum, 316 and 304 stainless steel, and Mo metal.
While visible ablation craters were observed on all samples mentioned above, the stainless-steel
samples displayed visible color changes in the resolidified iron, indicating ionization with the 355
nm pulses during ablation. Additionally, when used to intercept a plasma plume of Cs2CO3 made
with a separate laser system, the microchip laser pulses yielded Cs+ emission signal otherwise not
observed. With these experiments completed, the microchip laser has been successfully verified
for readiness to be integrated into the front end of a mass spectrometer system as the ion source.

 Project Milestones

Procurement of Microchip Laser System: A microchip laser system was procured from Electro
Optical Components. This system has the following parameters: 355 nm, 1.5 ns pulses, adjustable
repetition rate up to 1 kHz, externally triggerable with TTL pulses and air cooled. Once in hand, a
heat sink and custom mounts were connected to the laser for integration into the optical path for
sampling, Figure 1. Once in the path, both the output power and beam quality were verified and
compared to the factory reported values. The average output energy of the 355 nm laser head was
about 28 µJ per pulse with the measured pulse energies shown and plotted in Figure 2. With higher
repetition rates and more thermally consistent conditions, the higher repetition rates consistently
had higher output pulse energies. The beam itself was slightly elliptical with full width at half
maximum major and minor diameters of 2 mm and 1.8 mm and 1/e2 major and minor diameters of
roughly 4 mm by 3.5 mm, as shown in Figure 3. The beam also has a minor divergence of 4 mrad.
This beam spot size and pulse energy properly reflects the specifications reported by the
manufacturer. Furthermore, even when operated at 1 kHz, the heat sinks custom mounted to the
laser head do not noticeably heat up after prolonged use.

Incorporation of Microchip Laser into Optical Path: Once characterized and attached to a proper
holder for mounting onto an optical table, the microchip laser system was incorporated into an
optical path previously set up for laser induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) experiments, as
shown in Figure 4. This optical path includes two steering mirrors, a sample mount and 75 mm
plano-convex lens to diverge and guide the residual 355 nm pulses into a beam dump. The pair of
additional plano-convex lenses are available to collimate and focus the 355 nm beam directly onto
the ablation plume. The tests shown herein did not utilize this additional pair of lenses, as the 3
mm spot size beam was roughly equal in area as the plasma plumes that these pulses intercepted.
The green arrows in Figure 4 represent the optical path of the 532 nm ns-laser that was used to
make comparable ablation spots and well the plasma plumes for two-pulse LIBS. This path also
represents the path of the 1030 nm, fs-laser that was used to make ablation spots using equal
pulse energies as the microchip laser, ~30 µJ/pulse. There were two different sample mounts used
to perform all experiments, as shown in Figure 5. Figure 5A shows the sample mount to hold the
cuvette that contained Cs2CO3(s) for two-pulse LIBS, and Figure 5B shows the mount used to hold
the stainless steels, aluminum plate and Mo wafer at the focus of either the ns, fs or microchip
lasers. For ablation spot comparisons, the ns and fs lasers had a 40 mm focal length, 2” diameter,
uncoated plano-convex lens focus either beam onto the samples, and the microchip laser used a

40 mm, 1” diameter, anti-reflective coated for 355 nm, plano-convex lens. The final spot size for all
beams used was approximately 200 µm.

Ablation spot comparison using the nanosecond, femtosecond, and microchip lasers: Once
integrated into the optical path, the microchip laser was used to make ablation spots on 4 different
samples of interest, which varied in absorption efficiency of the 355 nm wavelength. Additionally,
the ability of the 355 nm pulses to ablate a rough surface, in the form of 316 stainless steel, was
also investigated. The 1030 nm, 500 fs laser was operated at 30 µJ per pulse to illustrate equivalent
pulse energies from a benchtop laser to the portable microchip laser. The 532 nm, 8 ns laser was
operated at its minimal output pulse energy of 2 mJ per pulse. All lasers made ablation spot sets in
triplicate using the following set of shot numbers: 100, 500, 1000, 5000 and 10000. The non-
radioactive metals tested in this study also serve as surrogates for radioactive samples which are
nearly identical in composition. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect exactly the same degree of
ablation and ionization from radioactively hot and cold samples, allowing for valuable isotopics
analysis of radioactive material. Figure 6 illustrates the ablation spots made on Mo and Al with all
three lasers. The ns, fs, and microchip spots on Mo are shown in A, B and C in Figure 6,
respectively. The ns, fs, and microchip laser spots on Al are shown in D, E and F in Figure 6,
respectively. All ablation spots were on the scale of 100-150 µm in diameter, including those
shown in Figure 7. Due to the long temporal duration and pulse energy of the ns laser, these pulses

did the most damage to both surfaces. The fs pulses visibly did less damage than the ns laser, but
more so than the microchip. This visible difference can be attributed to the absorptive efficiency
differences of both surfaces to absorb 1064 nm pulses more effectively than the shorter 355 nm,
microchip laser pulses. Nonetheless, the 355 nm pulses were able to do visible ablation to both
surfaces. In terms of the 316 and 304 stainless steel qualitative differences, the 355 nm, microchip
laser pulses were able to do similar damage to both quality stainless steel surfaces in comparison
to the fs laser. The microchip laser pulses also were visibly able to yield ionized Fe in the plasma
plume, which is indicated by the brownish resolidified material left behind post ablation. The
ablation spots are all important because they demonstrate that the microchip laser can remove
material in a comparable fashion to benchtop lasers, cause visible ionization and can ablate
surfaces that are generally considered to be inefficient at absorbing the microchip laser’s output
wavelength due to its high energy density of the short wavelength.

  Two-pulse LIBS to verify ionization of Cs using the microchip laser: Following the qualitative
  comparison of ablation spots made using the microchip laser system to bulkier benchtop systems,
  the 532 nm, ns laser system was used to ablate Cs2CO3 to generate a plasma plume with the 355
  nm microchip laser pulses orthogonally intercepting said plume. Cs has an ionization energy of
  3.89 eV, which is just above the 3.49 eV output of the 355 nm microchip laser. Thus, when timed
  just right, the non-resonantly excited Cs+ ion from the 532 nm pulses can be ionized with the
  addition of the secondary pulses. Using a photodetector, the timing delay between the trigger
  output of the flashlamp (the master trigger) from the ns laser to when a laser pulse from the
  microchip laser is observed temporally just before the spectrometer detector was determined to
  be 29.5 µs. The trigger of the detector occurs 196.2 µs after the flashlamp output. With these
  delays and operating at 20 Hz, the ns laser pulse energy was increased to 7 mJ/pulse to create
  ample plasma plume volume for the 355 nm, microchip laser pulses to intercept. The spectrometer
  has a 3600 line per mm grating blazed for 300 nm, which allots for a 3.25 nm spectral window. For

Cs, neutral emission can be observed at about 459.4 nm, and the emission from an excited Cs+ ion
can be observed at 460.4 nm. With the microchip laser off initially, only Cs neutral emission was
observed, as shown in Figure 8. When the microchip laser was turned on, increased emission from
Cs neutrals as well as Cs+ ions were observed in the recorded spectra. The presence of these two
Cs peaks indicates that the timing of the 355 nm pulses is close enough to the initial plume
ejection event for multiphoton ionization from the initial ablation event to create these ions, but
also, that the timing of these secondary pulses has room to further ionize these additional neutrals.
It is vital to note here that the microchip laser pulses had a significant temporal jitter on the scale of
about 200 ns, which on its own could lead to a portion of the neutrals that were observed. Thus, an
improved triggering scheme is needed for elements with very short-lived excited states. The broad
intensity of the Cs+ peaks can be further attributed to jitter due to the rapid thermal cooling and
thus induced doppler broadening of said peaks.

 Figure 8. A comparison of the LIBS spectra obtained while ablating a sample of Cs2CO3 powder
 within a cuvette. The blue spectrum illustrates the observed peaks when the microchip laser is
 off, and the orange spectrum shows the spectral difference when the microchip laser is on and
 intercepting the plasma plume from the 532 nm, 7mJ/pulse, ns laser. Both spectra are 200

Project Outlook and Future Work: The results shown above have successfully demonstrated the
exceptional viability of a microchip laser as the ion source for a portable mass spectrometer. There
is considerable room for optimization in the amount of signal collected by the LIBS detector,
namely: fine tuning of the optical path, jitter minimization, reducing laser pulses (213 nm), and sub-

ambient vacuum conditions will yield significantly better results when coupled to a mass analyzer.
The 1+1 multiphoton ionization (MPI) scheme using 355 nm and 213 nm pulses would be sufficient
to investigate all elements that require up to 9.31 eV, which includes all of the lanthanides. If
desired, MPI could be carried out to separate Pu (6.06 eV) from U (6.19 eV) ions using 515 nm and
266 nm pulses, which is the aggregate equivalent to 6.07 eV. The fundamental wavelengths of 1030
nm and 1064 nm as well as both of their many harmonics are available with similar energies to the
355 nm microchip laser investigated herein, which allows for great variability depending upon the
target of interest. This LDRD work serves as the basis for a proposal to the NNSA (NA-24 office). The
results of the LDRD will be presented as a poster presentation at the upcoming Postdoctoral
Research Symposium in late October.
