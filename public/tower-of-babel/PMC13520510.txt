Neuroprotective effects of C-phycocyanin and allophycocyanin from Spirulina purified using a newly developed method: in vitro barrier permeability, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities
Authors: Antonella Girgenti, Pasquale Picone, Laura Palumbo, Rosaria Lauceri, Domenico Nuzzo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhycobiliproteins, particularly C-phycocyanin (C-PC) and allophycocyanin (APC), are major bioactive pigments in Arthrospira platensis (Spirulina) with well-documented antioxidant properties, yet their individual neuroprotective potential and bioavailability remain poorly characterized. Here, we developed an efficient, potentially scalable, two-step ultrasound-based extraction followed by membrane chromatography to obtain analytical-grade purity C-PC and APC from fresh A. platensis biomass. Both purified phycobiliproteins, together with the crude extract (CE), demonstrated relevant cell viability up to 35 µg/mL in SH-SY5Y neuronal and BV2 microglial cells. In vitro assays revealed that APC exhibited significantly higher ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) than C-PC, while both phycobiliproteins effectively reduced intracellular ROS levels and protected SH-SY5Y cells from tert-butyl hydroperoxide-induced oxidative stress, with C-PC and APC showing the strongest cytoprotective effects. All preparations also significantly decreased LPS-induced nitric oxide production in BV2 microglia, indicating anti-inflammatory activity. Using Caco-2 monolayers, we demonstrated for the first time that both purified C-PC and APC exhibit time-dependent, low but moderate transcellular permeability, while preserving monolayer integrity. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed substantial intracellular uptake in SH-SY5Y neurons (punctate cytoplasmic pattern) and lower, yet detectable, internalization in BV2 microglia. These findings highlight distinct yet complementary bioactivities of highly purified C-PC and APC and provide the first evidence of their ability to cross physiological barriers in vitro, offering new mechanistic insights into their potential as neuroprotective nutraceuticals targeting oxidative and neuroinflammatory processes in the central nervous system.Keywords: Arthrospira platensis, Phycobiliproteins, Bioactivity, SH-SY5Y neurons, BV2 microglia, Neuroprotection, Oxidative stress, Blood-brain barrier, Cellular uptake

Introduction

Arthrospira platensis, more commonly known as Spirulina, is a photosynthetic filamentous cyanobacterium (i.e. blue-green microalgae) that has been utilised as a food source since ancient times [1]. It is well documented that extracts of A. platensis contain valuable bioactive molecules that have been shown to benefit human health, including macronutrients such as polyunsaturated fatty acids and micronutrients such as vitamins and minerals. These characteristics have led to the widespread utilisation of cyanobacteria, particularly in the food, cosmetics, biotechnology, and health sciences sectors [1, 2]. Rodrigues et al. (2024) have demonstrated that cyanobacteria constitute a sustainable economic resource that supports health and environmental sustainability [3]. A. platensis extracts have a high concentration of antioxidant molecules that are crucial for the elimination of free radicals and the protection of cells from oxidative stress [4, 5]. Furthermore, numerous pharmacological properties relevant to health have been delineated, including antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral activities, as well as cardioprotective, antitumour, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, anti-ageing, and neuroprotective activities [2]. In this context, A. platensis extracts have been evaluated for their neuroprotective and antioxidant capabilities through a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments. In vivo studies have highlighted the neuroprotective effects of Spirulina against neuroinflammatory insults across brain regions in Parkinson’s disease (PD) models [6]. Other studies have demonstrated that A. platensis enhanced cell viability and reduced markers of oxidative damage, including malondialdehyde and reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby protecting neuronal cells [7]. Furthermore, aqueous extract has been shown to confer neuroprotection in an experimental mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), with improvements in working, episodic, spatial, and aversive memory deficits [8]. It was found to impede astrogliosis and microgliosis by decreasing nitrite and superoxide anion levels and, concomitantly, increasing the activity of endogenous antioxidant, such as glutathione (GSH), in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus [8]. This finding indicates that neuroprotective activity is associated with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities that are induced by the ingestion of bioactive molecules present in Spirulina. A randomised, double-blind study was conducted to evaluate the effects of Spirulina intake (500 mg/day) on clinical and metabolic parameters in patients with AD. The results of the study demonstrated that 12 weeks of Spirulina intake in AD patients resulted in improvements in cognitive function, promotion of insulin sensitivity, and reduction in hs-CRP levels (a marker of chronic inflammation) [9]. In this context, it has been reported that the neuroprotection promoted by A. platensis is mediated by the protein-rich fraction [10], which constitutes approximately 60–70% of its dry weight [11]. In particular, phycobiliproteins (PBPs), C-phycocyanin (C-PC) and allophycocyanin (APC), located in the thylakoid membrane, play a fundamental role in conveying energy flow to photosystems I and II during photosynthesis in cyanobacteria [12]. Blue C-PC and light blue APC are water-soluble highly fluorescent proteins whose chromophore is represented by the tetrapyrrole molecule phycocyanobilin (PCB).

These natural pigments have several biotechnological applications in the food, cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and biomedical sectors thanks to their safe, health-promoting nature. The use in a particular application/technological sector and the commercial value of PBPs is strongly dependent on the purity grade P, which is defined as the ratio of the absorbance at the phycobiliprotein, PBP, maximum in the visible spectrum to the absorbance at 280 nm, which reflects the total amount of proteins in the product. A PBP, with P greater than 0.7 is considered as food grade, greater than 1.5 as cosmetic grade, greater than 3.9 as reactive grade and greater than 4.0 as analytical grade. Usually, a purity greater than 3.0 is required to be used as a fluorescent agent, while analytical grade is required for biomedical and therapeutic applications [13].

C-PC and APC commonly used in foods for their colouring properties, also have a great potential as safe therapeutic agents thanks to their bioactivity, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, immunomodulatory, neuroprotective, antimicrobial [12–14]. To date, research has mainly focused on phycobiliprotein C-PC, the most abundant protein in A. platensis [15]. In a study by Majel Cervantes-Llanos et al. (2018), the synergistic effect of C-PC and PCB from A. platensis was demonstrated to decrease neuroinflammation and protect against demyelination and axonal loss in murine models of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis [16]. In addition, evidence suggests that PCB exerts neuroprotective effects in isolation. In a study by Nancy Pavón-Fuentes et al. (2020), it was demonstrated that PCB protects PC12 neuronal cells during oxygen and glucose deprivation followed by reperfusion in vitro. Analogous effects have also been shown in vivo in a mouse model of focal cerebral ischaemia induced by endothelin-1, thereby establishing PCB as a natural tetrapyrrole antioxidant [17]. In the context of neurodegenerative diseases, numerous studies have been conducted on the neuroprotective potential of cyanobacterial “extracts” or on the beneficial effects primarily attributable to C-PC or its chromophore. However, any hypothesis of neuroprotection mediated by isolated APC and translatable into a clinical model remains vague and lacks a solid scientific description. The potential effect of isolated APC has been under investigated. A solitary study of neuroprotection has demonstrated the anti-ageing and preventative effect in age-related diseases such as AD promoted by APC. Chaubey and collaborators (2020) report that APC isolated from the cyanobacterium Phormidium sp. inhibited the expression of human beta-amyloid (Aβ1–42) and the associated Aβ-induced paralysis in transgenic C. elegans CL4176 [18]. Recently, APC isolated from Spirulina maxima has been reconsidered for exploring potential anti-tumour effects in an in vitro liver model [19], However, the authors report that APC has lower antioxidant activity than isolated C-PC; conversely, APC shows greater antiproliferative activity than C-PC, which promotes Hep-G2 cell proliferation. This finding underscores the notion that C-PC and APC exhibit divergent bioactivities [19]. The authors propose that these discrepancies in bioactivity may be attributed to differences in cyanobacterial species used as sources of PBPs, as well as to disparate extraction methods that can influence the purity of C-PC and APC [19]. Consequently, the exploration of their bioactivity may reveal new avenues for developing novel therapeutic or diagnostic strategies.

Although C-PC and APC are considered safe compounds with reduced side effects, the widespread use of these pigments is still limited both by the high costs of large-scale production processes, and by the limited pigment stability towards light, temperature, high and low pH. Indeed, the design of more efficient, sustainable, environmentally friendly, and scalable extraction/purification processes to obtain high-purity products, with minimal impairment of protein stability, represents one of the major challenges for the large-scale production of C-PC and APC [13].

This study explored the potential neuroprotective effects of C-PC and APC obtained by two recent innovative scalable processes, here for the first time applied sequentially to obtain C-PC and APC with P > 4. The first one, UltraBlu process [20] [WO2022144704] [21], allowed to extract C-PC and APC from A. platensis with high purity grade, the second one [14] [WO2019234614] allowed the effective separation of the two pigments, further improving P (> 4).

The innovative and rapid ultrasound-based UltraBlu extraction process has been recently optimized to obtain bright blue and partially purified C-PC crude extracts from fresh biomass of A. platensis on laboratory scale [20]. The term ‘crude extract’ refers to the solution containing PBP (extract) obtained after separating the liquid phase from the lysed solid biomass, in line with the usage of this term in the literature; however, the UltraBlu crude extract, being partially purified, exhibits an unusually high degree of purity P. In UltraBlu process, entirely carried out in aqueous medium, the extraction of PBPs was decoupled from biomass cell lysis. Cell lysis, performed by ultrasonication in ammonium sulphate solution, was instead combined with purification in one step before pigment extraction/recovery step. Crude extracts with unusually high P (up to 3.5) were obtained on laboratory scale in a few hours (including biomass harvesting) instead of the days usually required by most conventional methods. As a comparison, Na et al. [16] obtained a crude extract with P = 0.9. (the extraction process duration is not reported). Further, a preliminary study on the pilot-scale production of C-PC extracted from A. platensis biomass grown in large-scale facilities for commercial purposes, suggested that the process can also be effective on a large scale [21]. To the best of our knowledge, UltraBlu is currently the only reported example in the literature of a pilot-scale ultrasound-based process for the production of PBPs. UltraBlu crude extracts are suitable for foods, nutraceutical or cosmetic applications, but not for medical use. The pigments still need to be purified to increase P up to 4 and to separate C-PC from APC. The purification of PBPs is a complex and time-consuming process. Usually, analytical purity grade (P > 4) is achieved applying various purification steps, often involving different packed bed column chromatographic methods, which are poorly scalable, reduce product yield and increase the costs, hindering the exploitation at a large scale [22].

We purified C-PC and APC in a few minutes by applying an innovative, potentially scalable process based on membrane chromatography (MC) [22]. MC is in fact a scalable method [23, 24], which combines the power of chromatography with the speed of microfiltration. The MC purification/separation process of the pigments was carried out by using commercial hydrophilic, low protein binding, microfiltration membranes as an ammonium sulphate responsive chromatographic device [19, 22]. Selective and reversible protein-membrane interactions were induced through ammonium sulphate and, changing ammonium sulphate concentration, it was possible to selectively separate C-PC from APC. However, analytical purity grade was achieved only for C-PC [19]. In fact, the final purity of a phycobiliprotein is determined by the overall extraction and purification process. Therefore, using a crude extract with a high purity grade (P) can enhance the purity of the final product under the same purification conditions.

In this study, the two innovative technologies described above are applied in sequence for the first time to obtain, through a simple, rapid extraction and purification procedure, optimally separated C-PC and APC with P > 4, an essential requirement for evaluating the bioactivity of each individual pigment.

The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of C-PC and APC on in vitro neuronal models were evaluated and compared with the CE of A. platensis. For this study, the SH-SY5Y cell line was chosen as a model to evaluate the neuroprotective efficacy of natural compounds [25]. Similarly, to evaluate the anti-inflammatory properties of phycobiliproteins, the BV2 cell line was used as a neuroinflammatory model. This cell line has been described as a valid model of primary microglia for examining brain inflammation and validating the anti-inflammatory effects of our samples [26]. Furthermore, to exert their beneficial properties, phycobiliproteins must reach their target sites by crossing natural physiological barriers. In this regard, the study examined, for the first time, the ability of isolated APC and C-PC to cross an in vitro model of physiological barriers that emulates gastrointestinal and brain barriers, as well as their ability to reach neuronal targets.

Materials and methods

Phycocyanin extraction and purification

C-PC and APC were extracted from fresh A. platensis biomass (inoculum from culture collection of the Institute of Bioeconomy, CNR- IBE - Sesto Fiorentino, Italy) following a two-step ultrasound-based process [20]. Deionized water and analytical grade reagents were used. Briefly, the biomass was separated from the culture medium through a stainless-steel test sieve of mesh size 39 μm, washed with deionized water on the same sieve, recovered with a minimum volume of deionized water in a glass beaker (biomass stock suspension) and used soon after its preparation to avoid osmotic cell lysis. Ammonium sulphate (AS) 3 M and water were added to the biomass suspension in order to have a final AS concentration 1.2 M. To lyse the biomass cells, aliquots of 30 mL of the suspension were sonicated for 8 min (four cycles of 2 min with at least 2 min pause between two consecutive ultrasonication cycles) using a Hielscher Ultrasonic Processor UP200S set to maximum power (200 W) and equipped with an S2 sonotrode. During sonication the suspension was kept in an ice-water bath to avoid pigment degradation due to excessive temperature increase. The lysed biomass samples were separated from the AS solution by centrifuging, suspended in deionized water as an extraction solution and stirred for 3 h to extract PBPs (C-PC and APC). The various supernatants (phycobiliprotein crude extracts, CEs) were then recovered by centrifugation and filtered using Millex-GS syringe filters (cod. SLGS033SS, pore size 0.22 mm, mixed cellulose esters) to remove residual cell debris and chlorophyll aggregates. Finally, the supernatants were unified to obtain one CE sample, and C-PC and APC concentration (Eqs. 1 and 2) and purity grade (P, Eqs. 3 and 4) determined by spectrophotometric analysis.

The phycobiliprotein CE was purified and C-PC separated from APC by membrane chromatography [14] using a prototype purifying unit (Fig. 1) equipped with a hydrophilic PVDF microfiltration membrane (cod. HVLP2932A, Durapore, pore size 0.45 μm) and connected to a vacuum filter flask and a vacuum pump.

A CE aliquot containing about 78 mg of C-PC was diluted with AS 3 M and deionized water to have a final AS concentration 1.115 M. The solution was loaded on the membrane, where C-PC was selectively bound, separated from APC, which remained in the filtered solution (permeate). C-PC was recovered by eluting it with deionized water. To purify APC, AS 3 M was added to the permeate to have a final AS concentration 1.40 M and the solution loaded on the membrane, where APC was now selectively bound. Finally, APC was recovered by eluting it with deionized water.

The purification procedure was executed on a second aliquot of crude extract. The two purified C-PC solutions were unified, and the same was done for the two purified APC solutions. Finally, C-PC and APC concentration (Eqs. 1 and 2) and purity grade (P, Eqs. 3 and 4) were determined by spectrophotometric analysis. Purified C-PC and APC were stored in 70% saturated ammonium sulfate at 4 °C until use (no later than one year from production).

For biological analysis, the CE, C-PC, and APC samples in AS were centrifuged at 10,000 rcf for 30 min to remove salt. The precipitate corresponding to our samples was resuspended in cold Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS). Each sample was then exposed to three cycles of sonication in a sonicator bath for 30 s, alternating with three cycles in ice for 30 s. This process was undertaken to resuspend the phycobiliproteins in solution and to remove any aggregates. Finally, each sample was filtered with a 0.22 μm low protein binding PES filter to ensure sterility. The concentrations of C-PC and APC for each sample were obtained using Eqs. 1 and 2.

SDS-Page

SDS-PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulphate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) was utilised for the analysis of the α and β subunits of C-PC and APC. This was achieved by loading 2.5 µg of protein onto a 1 mm thick, 5% (w/v) polyacrylamide slab gel containing 0.1% (w/v) SDS with a 4% acrylamide and 0.1% bis-acrylamide stacking gel. The samples were subjected to a preliminary incubation with Laemmli sample buffer (2% (w/v) SDS, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 4.5% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol, 0.025% (w/v) bromophenol blue and 0.06 M Tris (pH 6.8)) for a duration of five minutes at 95 °C. This was followed by a five-minute ice-cold incubation and subsequent centrifugation for two minutes. The gel was subjected to an electric current at a constant temperature of room temperature and an applied voltage of 140 V. Following electrophoresis, the gel was scanned on an Odyssey (Li-Cor) in the Coomassie channel and subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R250 to visualise the protein bands. The molecular weight of the subunits was determined using a molecular weight marker (Marcatore Amersham™ ECL™ Rainbow™ - Full range, Cytiva).

Cell culture

The human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y were grown in DMEM/F12 (Sigma Aldrich) with the addition of 10% FBS (Sigma Aldrich), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 U/mL streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine, at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. The murine microglial cell line BV2 were grown in high-glucose DMEM (Sigma Aldrich), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma Aldrich), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 U/mL streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine, at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. The human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line Caco-2 were grown in high-glucose DMEM (Sigma Aldrich), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma Aldrich), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 U/ml streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine, at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTS assay (Promega). MTS [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2 H-tetrazolium] was utilised in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. BV2 cells at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well, SH-SY5Y cells at a density of 7.5 × 10³ cells/well and Caco-2 cells at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/well were seeded on 96-well plates. Twenty-four hours after seeding, the cells were treated with increasing doses (8.5-17-35-70-140 µg/mL) of CE, purified C-PC and purified APC for 72 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. 72 h after treatment with the samples, the conditioned medium was removed and 100 µL of fresh medium was dispensed, to which 20 µL of MTS solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. The absorbance was read at 490 nm using a GloMax® Discover System microplate reader (Promega). The results were expressed as a percentage of viability relative to the untreated control. Morphological analysis of treated and untreated cells was performed by means of bright field microscopy at 20x magnification using an Olympus CKX53.

Protection of vitality in the presence of oxidative stimulus

SH-SY5Y cells were cultivated in 96-well plates at a density of 15 × 10³ cells/well, allowed to grow for 24 h, and then co-treated with 30 µg/mL CE, C-PC, and APC together with tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBH) at 250 µM, which were added simultaneously. The cells were then subjected to an incubation period of one hour, at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Untreated cells were used as a negative control, and cells exposed to the oxidising agent alone as a positive control. After the administration of treatment, the medium was removed and 100 µL of fresh medium and 20 µL of MTS solution were dispensed and incubated for two hours at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. The absorbances were measured at a wavelength of 490 nm using the GloMax® Discover System microplate reader (Promega). The results were expressed as a percentage of viability relative to the untreated control. Morphological analysis of treated and untreated cells was performed by bright-field microscopy at 20x magnification using an Olympus CKX53 microscope.

FRAP assay

The antioxidant potential within a sample of CE, C-PC and APC was evaluated using a quantitative Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) assay kit (Cell Biolabs’ OxiSelect™). Consequently, 100 µL of solution in PBS at two different concentrations (17–35 µg/mL) was prepared for each sample, to which 100 µL of the FRAP reaction reagent was added and incubated at room temperature away from light. Ferric iron (Fe3+) is initially reduced by electron-donating antioxidants present within the sample to its ferrous form (Fe2+). The iron colorimetric probe complex develops a dark blue colour product upon reduction, which can be measured at 560 nm. Absorbance at 560 nm was measured after the designated incubation time using the GloMax® Discover System microplate reader (Promega). To ascertain the antioxidant potential of the samples, they should be compared with the iron standard in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Therefore, the values were expressed as FRAP values, i.e. as µM quantities of iron equivalents of Fe2+.

DCFH-DA assay

The intracellular ROS levels in the neuronal model were measured using 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). The SH-SY5Y cells were cultivated in 96-well plates at a density of 15 × 10³ cells per well. Twenty-four hours after seeding, the SH-SY5Y cells were co-treated with 30 µg/mL of CE, C-PC and APC, and the oxidising agent TBH at 250 µM for 1 h at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ [28]. Finally, the DCFH-DA probe (200 µM) was added. The cells were then incubated for 15 min at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Subsequently, PBS washes were performed to remove excess probe, after which the fluorescence intensity was analysed using a spectrofluorometer (GloMax® reader, Promega) with an excitation of 475 nm and an emission wavelength between 500 and 550 nm.

GRIESS assay

Nitric oxide (NO) levels were measured using the Griess Reagent System (Promega). BV2 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 10 × 10³ cells/well and allowed to grow in an incubator at 37 °C at 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Following this, the cells were treated with 30 µg/mL of CE, C-PC and APC in combination with 1 µg/mL LPS for a further 24 h [29]. Thereafter, 50 µL of supernatant were removed and transferred to a 96-well UV trans plate, and 50 µL of the sulfanilamide solution was dispensed to all experimental samples and incubated for 10 min at room temperature, protected from light. In conclusion, 50 µL of NED (N-1-Naphthylethylenediamine dihydrochloride) solution was added to all wells and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Absorption was measured using a GloMax® plate reader (Promega) with a 560 nm filter. The nitrite concentrations present within the experimental samples were then interpolated using the nitrite standard reference curve provided by the kit as a reference.

Crossing of phycobiliproteins on a barrier model

The Caco-2 cell line was used to assess the permeability C-PC, and APC. Cells were seeded onto a permeable transwell support for 24-well plates with 0.4 μm pores (Falcon®) at a density of 1 × 105 cells. Cells were grown at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for 15 days. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced on both sides of the transwell support every two days. Monolayer integrity was assessed by monitoring cells over time using transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) analysis with a Millicell® ERS 3.0 digital voltmeter (Merck Millipore). After a 15-day seeding period, 30 µg/mL of C-PC, and APC were dispensed into the apical chamber of the transwell holder, and 300 µL of phenol red-free culture medium was dispensed on the basolateral side. Fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran (FITC-Dextran) 40 KDa [0.1 mg/mL] (Sigma-Aldrich) was selected as the reference control for paracellular pathway integrity. At various intervals, fluorescence intensity was measured in the basolateral chamber at wavelengths of ex 627 nm and em in the range 660–720 nm for C-PC, and APC samples. For FITC-Dextran, the wavelength was ex 475 nm and em 500–550 nm. For this purpose, a spectrofluorimeter (GloMax® reader, Promega) was used. The analysis was conducted without volume replacement, with the objective of maximising the mechanical stability of the single-cell layer during the 6-hour test. The calculation was performed in accordance with the progressive accumulation profile, using standard permeability equations, in agreement with the method described by Hubatsch et al. [30]. Uptake cellular

The SH-SY5Y and BV2 cells were cultivated at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well, using a 96-well black plate (Corning). 24 h after seeding, the cells were treated with 30 µg/mL C-PC and APC and then incubated for 24 h, respectively. Subsequent to this period, the cells were washed with PBS containing Ca2+ and Mg2+ in order to remove any free C-PC and APC molecules. The uptake of phycobiliproteins was assessed by measuring fluorescence at an excitation wavelength of 627 nm and emission in the range of 660–720 nm using spectrofluorimeter (GloMax® reader, Promega). Furthermore, the degree of fluorescence was evaluated through the utilisation of a fluorescence imaging procedure, employing an Olympus Evident IX83 fluorescence microscope at 20x magnification in the CY5 channel. Quantifications were performed per field by normalising the total signal to the number of cells in each field by using ImageJ (ver. 1.54 g National Institute of Health, USA).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times, and each experiment was performed in triplicate. All data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistics were performed and graphs were generated with Graphpad Prism software (version 10.4.1). One-way ANOVA was performed, followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test for significance analysis. The analysis of the fluorescence microscopy data was conducted using an unpaired Student’s t-test for the comparison of two groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at ∗p < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Characterisation of extraction fractions

CE, purified C-PC, and purified APC obtained from Spirulina were initially characterized by UV-Vis analysis (Fig. 2) to determine their concentration, purity grade P and separation factor SF. The quantity of processed pigments, the recovery yield of the pigments, P and SF of CE, purified C-PC, and purified APC are reported in Table 1. The extraction method adopted, UltraBlu, enabled the production of an CE with relatively high purity grade (P = 2.86), while SF has an intermediate value compared to the purified and isolated C-PC and APC. P value proves that the content of nucleic acids and other contaminating biomolecules, particularly proteins, in the CE was small. To separate C-PC from APC and further purify the two pigments, membrane chromatography was applied (paragraph 2.1), achieving a good yield and analytical grade purity (P > 4) for both C-PC and APC. Good separation between the two pigments was also testified by the SF values achieved. For C-PC samples, lower is the SF value better is the separation from APC (usually C-PC is considered well separated from APC if SF < 0.3). For APC samples, higher is the SF value better is the separation from C-PC (usually APC is considered well separated from C-PC if SF > 1.25).

To further characterize and assess the purity of the pigments, SDS-PAGE was performed to identify the C-PC and APC protein subunits (Fig. 3b). The analysis identified two intense bands for the APC and C-PC samples. In the APC sample, the two bands observed between the 17 kDa and 15 kDa correspond to the β and α subunits of APC. This result is consistent with previous studies demonstrating the effectiveness of the isolation method [32]. The C-PC sample exhibits two bands between 24 and 17 kDa corresponding to the β and α subunits of C-PC. The molecular weight of the α-subunit of phycocyanin is approximately 17.6 kDa. In comparison, the molecular weight of the β-subunit is approximately 18.05 kDa, and the molecular weight of the monomer (αβ) is approximately 35.65 kDa, as previously demonstrated [33]. In the CE sample, bands common to the C-PC and APC samples are visible, including a distinct band corresponding to the C-PC β-subunit band (at approximately 18 kDa) and a broad band resulting from the overlap of the C-PC α-bands and the APC β-bands, at approximately 17 kDa, which have overlapping molecular weights. Finally, a thin band corresponding to the APC α-subunit is evident, given the very low APC proportion in the CE sample relative to C-PC, approximately 1/3 (Fig. 3b).

Moreover, an evaluation of the fluorescence properties of the CE, C-PC, and APC samples were conducted by recording excitation and emission spectra (Fig. 3c). The excitation and emission spectra were normalised to maximum intensity to facilitate a comparison of the spectral shape and peak position of the different samples, independently of their fluorescence intensity. The normalised fluorescence emission spectra demonstrated that CE and C-PC exhibit a comparable spectrum with an emission maximum around 640–648 nm, which is characteristic of C-PC. This is likely attributable to the fact that the predominant component in the CE sample is C-PC. However, in the case of CE, the bandwidth (which is wider than that of C-PC) and a shoulder towards longer wavelengths are compatible with an APC contribution. In contrast, APC demonstrates a maximum emission peak that is shifted towards the red (~ 665 nm), in accordance with its established spectral characteristics [34].

Cytotoxicity of the CE, C-PC and APC on neuronal models

As a preliminary study, the cytotoxicity of CE, C-PC, and APC was analyzed on microglial and neuronal cell models, BV2 and SH-SY5Y, respectively, to identify the concentrations tolerated by the model systems. The cell models were exposed to increasing concentrations of the samples, ranging from 8.5 µg/mL to 140 µg/mL, for 72 h. The concentration for the CE sample is defined as the total amount of APC and C-PC, calculated according to the equations used to determine the concentration of phycobiliproteins (mg/mL), as described by Bennett and Bogorad [27], and the ratio of C-PC to APC was approximately 3:1. For all three samples analyzed, the highest concentration (140 µg/mL) was found to be toxic to both cell lines (Fig. 4). This result was confirmed by bright-field morphological analysis, which revealed cell clusters resulting from apoptosis (Fig. 4b and d). Therefore, we selected a concentration of 30 µg/mL to explore the potential activities of CE, C-PC, and APC.

Biological activity

The antioxidant capacity of CE, C-PC, and APC was assessed by reducing Fe3+ to Fe2+ using the FRAP assay. This colorimetric assay facilitates the prospective evaluation of antioxidant activity. In this regard, it was observed that the APC sample showed the most effective effect, converting approximately 16.93 µM Fe2+, compared to the C-PC sample (9.84 µM Fe2+) and the CE sample (11.37 µM Fe2+), at a concentration of 35 µg/mL (Fig. 5a). Preliminary investigations revealed a significant improvement in the conversion of oxidized iron to its reduced form, up to a concentration of 35 µg/mL. To evaluate the effect of rapid mitigation of ROS-induced damage, an acute stress condition was simulated on the SH-SY5Y cell line. The cell line was treated with an oxidative insult (TBH [250 µM]) for 1 h, in the presence and absence of CE, C-PC, and APC samples at a concentration of 30 µg/mL. After a 1-hour treatment, intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA assay. It was established that when ROS are generated via oxidative processes, they react with the DCFH probe. Following this reaction, the probe is oxidized and emits fluorescence in the 520–550 nm range, with an excitation wavelength of 475 nm. Interestingly, all samples showed a reduction in ROS levels in response to oxidative stress relative to the positive control (Fig. 5b and c, and 5d). However, the MTS test results indicated that the C-PC and APC samples showed the greatest improvement in cell viability, with a statistically significant increase relative to the positive control. The percentage of cell death observed in the positive control condition was approximately 54%. The co-treatment of CE, C-PC, and APC with TBH significantly reduced the levels of toxicity, with cell death reduced to approximately 32%, 17%, and 25%, respectively. This result was confirmed by bright-field morphological analyses, which demonstrated increased neuronal protection under conditions of elevated ROS levels (Fig. 5b).

The ability of CE, C-PC, and APC samples to inhibit the LPS-induced response in the BV2 microglial cell line was assessed. LPS has been shown to selectively induce iNOS expression in microglial cells, thereby serving as an indicator of ongoing inflammation, which in turn contributes to nitric oxide (NO) production [35]. These cells have been observed to release various mediators, including NO, which plays a significant role in these diseases. However, NO is a free radical that is toxic to all cells at high concentrations [35]. To evaluate the effect of CE, C-PC, and APC in counteracting excessive NO release in the presence of LPS, the BV2 cell line was treated with the samples at 30 µg/mL, in combination with LPS at 1 µg/mL, for 24 h. Interestingly, all samples reduced NO levels compared with the positive control treated with LPS alone (Fig. 5e).

Crossing of phycobiliproteins on a barrier model and cellular uptake

Elevated ROS and NO levels initiate complex crosstalk that ultimately results in detrimental effects on the CNS. Activated microglia are characterised by increased activity of Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Phosphate Oxidase (NADPH oxidase), which leads to increased ROS production and the simultaneous activation of the enzyme iNOS. Recent studies have identified NADPH oxidase as a significant factor contributing to the pathology of brain injuries and neurodegenerative disorders [36]. ROS, in turn, react with NO produced by iNOS, generating peroxynitrite, a highly toxic reactive species that can induce lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage to cellular components [37]. Phycobiliproteins have been shown to counteract oxidative and nitrosative stress. In particular, recent research has identified the chromophore phycocyanobilin (PCB) as a highly effective inhibitor of NADPH oxidase in the CNS [38]. Consequently, the potential for orally administered phycocyanobilins to reach sufficient concentrations in the brain parenchyma to mitigate the neurotoxic effects of neurodegenerative disorders was explored. The protective effects of cyanobacterial extracts and phycobiliproteins on neuronal models, both in vitro and in vivo, are well documented in the literature. However, there is an absence of both evidence and a mechanistic description of how these valuable phycobiliproteins cross natural physiological barriers to exert their healing effects. In this study, the capacity of C-PC and APC to cross a barrier model was evaluated using the Caco2 cell line.

Therefore, the initial assessment was conducted to determine the cytotoxicity of the samples, C-PC and APC, and to identify the concentrations tolerated by in vitro barrier model. As demonstrated by the cytotoxicity analysis, the maximum tolerated concentration for the Caco2 cell line is also 35 µg/mL, as observed for our neuronal models (Fig. 6a and b). Initially, the growth of the Caco2 cell line was monitored over time until a compact monolayer had formed. The integrity of the monolayer was validated by measuring TEER over time until a value of ~ 600 Ω · cm² was reached, indicating restricted diffusion across the barrier [39] (Fig. 6c).

The degree of permeability of C-PC and APC on the cell monolayer was assessed by calculating the apparent permeability coefficient (Papp) [30]. The results of the analysis demonstrated that, after a duration of 6 h, the quantity of compound that had traversed the monolayer was ~ 2 µg/mL for C-PC and ~ 1.6 µg/mL for APC. In accordance with this observation, the apparent permeability values (Papp) were determined to be 3.33 × 10−6 cm s− 1 for C-PC and 2.78 × 10−6 cm s− 1 for APC, indicating a greater permeability of C-PC in comparison to APC (Fig. 6f). Furthermore, the stability of 40 kDa FITC-dextran over time (Fig. 6d) suggests a transcellular transport mechanism. However, the limited permeability of C-PC and APC is consistent with their high molecular weight and hydrophilic nature of these phycobiliproteins, two crucial factors that influence transcellular passage.

After the crossover, it was crucial to ensure that there were no morphological alterations and that the integrity of the barrier was preserved. To this end, brightfield imaging was performed and nuclei were stained with DAPI (Fig. 6e).

Discussion

Oxidative and nitrosative stress plays a central role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders, mainly mediated by microglia activation and the overproduction of ROS and NO. The simultaneous activation of NADPH oxidase and iNOS leads to the formation of Reactive Nitrogen Species, highly reactive species responsible for lipid peroxidation and widespread cellular damage in the CNS. In this context, phycobiliproteins derived from A. platensis, particularly C-PC, have attracted considerable attention due to their well-documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, also attributed to their chromophore, PCB.

In the present study, phycobiliproteins C-PC and APC were obtained from fresh biomass of A. platensis using a new, rapid, efficient process, capable of producing highly purified and isolated pigments in one day. The process globally consists of a two-step ultrasound-based extraction (UltraBlu process) followed by two consecutive cycles of membrane chromatography, MC. The first MC cycle is performed to purify and isolate C-PC, the second to purify and isolate APC. Both pigmnets have P > 4 (Table 1).

Good yields and separation between the two pigments were also achieved (Table 1). In fact, our purified C-PC and APC had SF similar or better than those of available highly purified commercial C-PC (MERK n.52468, SF < 0.3) and APC (MERK n.A7472, SF ≥ 1.3) or reported in literature [14, 19]. The simplicity of the procedure, with only few steps required to obtain the final products, certainly contributes to the good yields obtained for C-PC and APC (87.10% and 4.14% compred to the crude extract content, respectively. See Table 1) and to preserve the bioactivity of the pigments. For example, Chaubey et al. [18] extracted APC from cyanobacterium Phormidium sp. A09DM fresh biomass and achieved a P value of 3.86 and a recovery yield of 59.57% following a purification procedure comprising three salting-out steps with ammonium sulphate, dialization and column ion exchange chromatography. Na et al. [19] extracted C-PC and APC from dried Spirulina maxima biomass and, following a purification/separation procedure comprising two salting-out steps with ammonium sulphate, diafiltration, column ion exchange chromatography (IEC) and membrane chromatography obtained C-PC with P = 4.0 and Y % = 78.8% compared to he pigment recovered after IEC (i.e. Y % = 67.30% compared to he crude extract content), and APC with P = 3.0 and Y% = 33.6% compared to the pigment recovered after IEC (i.e. Y% = 25.74% compared to he crude extract content). Further, it is worth noting that the whole process is potentially scalable. In fact, UltraBlu extraction has been evaluated on pilot scale [21] achieving incoraging results, and MC is recognized to be a scalable method.

The purified phycobiliproteins were characterised. Spectrophotometric analysis and SDS-PAGE confirmed their high purity, also evidenced by the presence of typical protein subunits. The purity and separation factor values demonstrated that the isolation and purification method produced analytical-grade phycobiliproteins, comparable to or superior to commercial standards, allowing for a more confident attribution of the sample’s bioactivity to phycobiliproteins, thanks to reduced interference from contaminating biomolecules, such as proteins or nucleic acids.

Cell viability assay demonstrated that CE, C-PC, and APC were well tolerated by both SH-SY5Y neurons and BV2 microglial cells up to 35 µg/mL, whereas higher concentrations were toxic. Consequently, this concentration was selected for the subsequent biological analyses. In conditions of acute oxidative stress induced by TBH in SH-SY5Y cells, a significant reduction in intracellular ROS levels was observed in all samples, as measured by the DCFH-DA assay. C-PC and APC were found to offer the most significant protection, as evidenced by cell viability assays MTS and the maintenance of neuronal morphology. These results are consistent with the greater reducing capacity of APC observed in the FRAP test, highlighting a correlation between antioxidant potential and intracellular protective effects. As indicated by Ismail et al. [44], the enhanced capacity of APC to reduce Fe2+ is indicative of a superior electron-donating/reducing capability. It is suggested that these variations can be attributed to differences in the chromophore environment, amino acid composition and structural accessibility of the redox-active groups.

In BV2 microglial cells, all samples demonstrated the capacity to curtail LPS-induced NO production, thereby indicating an anti-inflammatory effect. The protective effects of C-PC and its chromophore phycocyanobilin (PCB) may be attributed to their ability to inhibit NADPH oxidase-mediated ROS production, thereby limiting oxidative stress and downstream NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signalling. Furthermore, it has been proposed that PCB may act as an agonist of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), potentially promoting anti-inflammatory Treg activity and inducing phase II antioxidant enzymes through AhR/Nrf2-associated signalling pathways [30]. Concurrently, extant evidence signifies that APC may exert protective effects through modulation of conserved oxidative stress-response pathways. In C. elegans, APC reduced Aβ-induced toxicity through mechanisms involving DAF-16 and SKN-1, orthologs of mammalian FOXO and Nrf2, respectively. These transcriptional regulators have been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in cellular responses to oxidative stress. They do so by acting through complementary mechanisms that enhance antioxidant defences and promote cellular adaptation to stressful conditions [18].

Collectively, these findings lend support to the hypothesis that the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities of C-PC and APC may be a consequence of modulation of Nrf2-related signalling pathways and redox-sensitive inflammatory networks. Nevertheless, a direct analysis of the underlying molecular mechanisms, including the Nrf2 and NF-κB signalling pathways, could represent a significant area of investigation for future mechanistic studies.

We also investigated the cellular uptake of C-PC and APC in SH-SY5Y and BV2 cell models, as well as their ability to cross a cell barrier model. Fluorescence imaging has shown that both phycobiliproteins are internalised by neuronal and microglial cells, albeit with markedly different in the two lines. In particular, SH-SY5Y cells exhibited a more pronounced intracellular fluorescence signal than BV2 cells, suggesting a greater capacity for interaction or uptake in the neuronal model. This differential may be indicative of intrinsic differences in membrane composition, endocytic pathways, and metabolic activity between neuronal and microglial cells, which are known to influence the internalisation of biological macromolecules.

However, even limited uptake may be biologically significant, given the crucial role of microglia in amplifying the oxidative and inflammatory cascade within the CNS. In addition to cellular absorption, a critical yet underexplored facet of the neuroprotective potential of phycobiliproteins concerns their capacity to traverse physiological barriers. Utilising a Caco-2 monolayer model, our study revealed that both C-PC and APC can traverse the in vitro cell model while preserving its structural integrity. The progressive, time-dependent increase in permeability, after 6 h, together with the stability of FITC-dextran transport, suggests that the passage of these phycobiliproteins occurs predominantly via a transcellular mechanism rather than via the paracellular pathway. According to the classifications reported in the literature for Papp [45], C-PC (3.33 × 10−6 cm s-1) and (APC 2.78 × 10−6 cm s-1) exhibit moderate permeability, suggesting that transport across the barrier is not highly efficient, but nevertheless occurs in a measurable and continuous manner after 6 h.

It is important to note that no significant differences in permeability were observed between C-PC and APC, indicating that both proteins share comparable transport properties within the barrier model used. In accordance with the mechanisms previously delineated for the transport of C-PC across epithelial barriers [30] and the moderate apparent permeability values observed in this study (range of 10−6 cm/s), it is probable that translocation is not exclusively governed by passive diffusion, but rather involves interaction-driven processes, such as membrane adsorption and endocytosis-mediated uptake, including clathrin-mediated mechanisms, as demonstrated in Caco-2 models for protein and nanoparticle transport systems [46].

Furthermore, it is important to consider the potential for the chromophore to be absorbed once it has been released intracellularly, with the possibility of this being metabolised into bioactive derivatives In fact, as recently described, the partial extracellular or intracellular processing of C-PC into its phycocyanobilin (PCB) chromophore, as suggested in the literature. It is hypothesised that this may contribute to the observed permeability profile by generating smaller bioactive species that may exhibit greater transport efficiency [30].

In consideration of the neuroprotective properties, the objective of this study was to investigate the permeability of C-PC and APC across an in vitro barrier model, as well as their interaction with neuronal cell models. In this study, Caco-2 monolayers were utilised as a simplified in vitro system, a method that is frequently employed for permeability and epithelial transport studies. Nevertheless, while the model under consideration provides useful preliminary information on barrier transit, it does not fully recapitulate the structural and functional complexity of the blood–brain barrier [47]. However, these findings could collectively provide new mechanistic insights or experimental perspectives on how phycobiliproteins may exert neuroprotective effects despite their unfavourable chemical-physical properties. Combined analyses of cellular detection of phycobiliproteins and their chromophores in neuronal and microglial models, together with limited cell barrier permeability, lend support to the hypothesis that phycobiliproteins or their derived chromophores have the potential to reach targets relevant to the CNS.

This study contributes to the existing literature by bridging the gap between the well-documented antioxidant activity of phycobiliproteins and the still-poorly understood mechanisms governing their bioavailability and CNS accessibility. Further studies are required to elucidate the mechanisms involved in the absorption of phycobiliproteins, to evaluate the influence of microglial activation states on internalisation, and to determine whether the cellular metabolism of C-PC and APC facilitates the release of PCBs in biologically relevant contexts.

Despite the fact that barrier permeability and cellular uptake have been studied in separate experimental systems, these approaches address two consecutive and complementary steps necessary for the biological activity of phycobiliproteins in the CNS. Barrier models provide evidence of the ability of C-PC and APC to cross tightly regulated cellular interfaces, while uptake studies in neuronal and microglial cells demonstrate their ability to interact directly with cell types relevant to the CNS once barrier crossing has occurred. The employment of independent models thus facilitates a stepwise evaluation of the bioavailability and cellular accessibility of phycobiliproteins, reflecting key stages of their potential in vivo trajectory.

It is evident that further studies are required that utilise integrated co-culture models or microfluidic systems to replicate physiological barriers, such as the intestinal barrier or the brain blood barrier (BBB). These studies should enable the concurrent evaluation of barrier permeability and cellular uptake within a unified experimental framework. Concurrently, nanovector-based delivery systems are evolving as a promising approach to enhance transport across the blood-brain barrier and facilitate the selective targeting of brain cells [48–51].

Limitations and perspectives

PBPs, including C-PC and APC, have attracted considerable interest due to their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and antitumour properties. Nevertheless, several limitations hinder their translation into clinical practice. Moreover, these proteins demonstrate substantial physicochemical instability, exhibiting sensitivity to environmental factors such as pH, temperature, and light, which can result in rapid degradation and a consequent reduction in biological activity [52]. As demonstrated in the extant literature, the oral bioavailability of these substances is limited, presumably due to proteolytic degradation in the gastrointestinal tract and poor absorption [53]. These challenges have prompted the development of advanced delivery systems to improve stability and bioavailability [52]. In particular, strategies such as nanophytosomes and PEGylation have been proposed to protect the molecules from gastrointestinal degradation and to improve their permeability and transport. This highlights the need for further research to support their translational potential [53–58]. It is important to note that their pharmacokinetic profiles remain poorly characterised, with a lack of systematic studies addressing biodistribution, metabolism and clearance. Recent meta-analyses of randomised clinical trials focusing on Spirulina supplementation have demonstrated a significant reduction in systemic markers of inflammation and oxidative stress, including C-reactive protein, interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), suggesting a potential translational relevance of the bioactive compounds contained in cyanobacterial biomass. However, these effects are observed within complex matrices rather than in isolated PBPs, and therefore cannot be directly attributed to individual components such as C-PC or APC. Consequently, whilst preclinical and clinical evidence supports the systemic anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of Spirulina, the pharmacokinetics, bioavailability and mechanistic contribution of purified phycobiliproteins remain largely unresolved [59]. In the context of neurodegenerative diseases, further strategies have been explored to overcome biological barriers, in particular the BBB, which represents a significant obstacle to the delivery of therapeutic agents to the CNS. The utilisation of nanoparticle-based delivery systems, which have undergone the necessary functionalisation processes to facilitate receptor-mediated transport or transcytosis, has been demonstrated to enhance the permeation of the BBB and promote brain targeting in preclinical models of neurodegenerative diseases, including AD’s and PD’s diseases [60]. Despite these advances, the majority of the extant evidence remains confined to preclinical studies, and the clinical translation of these strategies remains limited. This underscores the notion that, while advanced delivery systems have the potential to surmount the pharmacokinetic and delivery constraints of phycobiliproteins, their efficacy in clinical settings, particularly in neurodegenerative diseases, remains to be thoroughly validated.

Conclusion

In summary, highly purified and well-separated C-PC and APC have been obtained by applying a new, rapid, innovative, and potentially scalable process. Purified APC emerges as a potent antioxidant, at least as potent as C-PC. The demonstrated moderate ability of both phycobiliproteins to cross barrier models and be internalized by neurons supports their biological relevance in the CNS. The findings suggest that these molecules may have implications in CNS disorders characterised by oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. Future studies should focus on pure fractions, investigate nanocarrier strategies to improve delivery, and utilize more integrated in vitro and in vivo models to better assess bioavailability and therapeutic relevance.
