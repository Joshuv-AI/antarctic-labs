Error analysis of a divergence-preserving mixed finite element scheme for the incompressible Hall–magnetohydrodynamic equations
Beniamin Goldys, Agus L. Soenjaya, Thanh Tran

Abstract
. The incompressible Hall-magnetohydrodynamics (Hall–MHD) system presents substantial analytical and computational challenges due to its stiff, highly nonlinear Hall term and the strict requirement that the magnetic field remains solenoidal. In this paper, we study a Voigt-regularised Hall–MHD system, which is of independent analytical interest and provides a physically consistent, well-posed regularisation of the original model. We propose, analyse, and implement a structure-preserving, linear, fully discrete finite element method for this regularised problem. Using finite element exterior calculus and a mixed formulation, the spatial discretisation enforces the divergence-free condition on the magnetic field exactly, while a skew-symmetric, linearly implicit time discretisation yields unconditional energy stability. We establish optimal convergence rates for the Voigt-regularised problem and, additionally, derive error estimates for the unregularised Hall–MHD system, with the Voigt regularisation playing a crucial role in the non-resistive regime. Finally, numerical simulations in both 2.5D and 3D corroborate the theoretical results and demonstrate the physical fidelity of the scheme.

1.
Introduction

The study of magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) is fundamental to understanding the dynamics of electrically conducting fluids, such as plasmas, liquid metals, and astrophysical flows. Classical MHD models describe the interaction between fluid motion and magnetic fields under a single-fluid approximation, but they fail to capture phenomena at small scales where electron and ion motions decouple.

The Hall–MHD equations extend classical MHD by incorporating the Hall effect, which becomes significant at length scales comparable to the ion skin depth
[
29
]
. This introduces a dispersive, nonlinear term in the induction equation, leading to richer dynamics, including whistler waves and fast magnetic reconnection corresponding to physically observable phenomena in astrophysics
[
5
,
12
,
15
,
31
]
. The Hall term poses significant analytical and numerical challenges due to its strong nonlinearity.

These difficulties motivate the introduction of regularised models that retain the essential physical structure but improve stability and well-posedness.
Among various approaches, the Voigt-type regularisation provides a physically consistent and mathematically convenient modification
[
9
,
26
]
. It can be interpreted as a simplified representation of electron inertia and finite Larmor radius effects in the underlying two-fluid plasma description
[
20
,
22
,
23
]
. These mechanisms introduce corrections that smooth the dynamics at spatial scales comparable to the electron inertial length.

We now introduce the problems considered in this paper.
Let
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

(d=2,3)
be a contractible bounded domain with polytopal boundary. We consider the incompressible resistive Hall–MHD system
[
4
,
23
,
27
]
, which is a coupled system of Navier–Stokes and Maxwell equations, together with a generalised Ohm’s law. The problem consists of the velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}:(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the pressure
p:(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}
, and the magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}:(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
governed by:

\displaystyle\rho\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\rho(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p-\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{f}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{B}+\sigma\,\mathrm{curl}\,(\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{B})-\mathrm{curl}\,(\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{B})+\eta\,\mathrm{curl}\,(\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{B})=\boldsymbol{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{u}=\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{B}=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\boldsymbol{B}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathscr{D},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0,\quad\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad(\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{B})\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }(0,T)\times\partial\mathscr{D},

where
\boldsymbol{n}
is the outward pointing normal vector on
\partial\mathscr{D}
, and the initial data satisfy
\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{u}_{0}=\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{B}_{0}=0
. This system is the form of the Hall–MHD equations in bounded domains most commonly studied in the literature.

The positive coefficients
\rho
,
\nu
,
\sigma
, and
\eta
represent the fluid density, fluid kinematic viscosity, magnetic resistivity, and the strength of the Hall effect, respectively. For clarity of exposition, subsequently we set
\rho=1
,
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and assume
d=3
. The case
d=2
corresponds to the 2.5D Hall–MHD, where the system is defined on a two-dimensional domain but retains all three vector components under the assumption of vanishing partial derivatives of the unknowns in the
z
-component
[
11
,
27
,
30
]
. We provide a brief discussion of this reduction in Section
2.5
.

To design a structure-preserving numerical method, we will rewrite the problem including the underlying physical quantities, namely the electric field
\boldsymbol{E}:(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the current density
\boldsymbol{J}:(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The Hall–MHD system is
then rewritten in the following mixed formulation:

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p-\boldsymbol{J}\times\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{0}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{B}+\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{0}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\sigma\boldsymbol{J}+\eta\boldsymbol{J}\times\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{E}+\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{B}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}-\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{0}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

(1.1e)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{u}=\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{B}=0\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

(1.1f)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\boldsymbol{B}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\mathscr{D},

(1.1g)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0,\quad\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\boldsymbol{J}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }(0,T)\times\partial\mathscr{D}.

In this formulation, (
1.1a
) denotes the momentum equation, while (
1.1b
) and (
1.1c
) represent to the induction equation and generalised Ohm’s law, respectively. Equation (
1.1d
) is the Ampère law. The divergence-free constraints in (
1.1e
) express two physical principles: incompressibility of the fluid yields
\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{u}=0
, whereas
\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{B}=0
reflects the absence of magnetic monopoles (Gauss’ law). The boundary conditions in (
1.1g
) correspond to the perfectly conducting boundaries considered in
[
27
]
.

As discussed previously, the Hall–MHD system exhibits strong nonlinear and dispersive behaviour due to the term
\eta\boldsymbol{J}\times\boldsymbol{B}
in the generalised Ohm’s law (
1.1c
), leading to steep gradients and high-frequency oscillations caused by the whistler waves that make numerical simulation and analysis difficult
[
2
,
17
,
23
,
24
]
. In particular, resolving the small scales induced by the Hall term often requires extremely fine meshes or small time steps, and the global existence of strong solutions in three dimensions remains an open problem.

These difficulties motivate the introduction of Voigt-type regularisation that retains the essential physical structure, but improve stability and well-posedness
[
9
,
26
,
28
]
.
In the Voigt model, this is achieved by replacing the time derivative with its spatially filtered version
(I-\alpha\Delta)\partial_{t}
, where
\alpha>0
denotes the regularisation length scale and
I
is the identity operator. This modification preserves the divergence-free and energy structures of the original Hall–MHD system, while improving numerical stability and permitting coarser spatial discretisations. Beyond its numerical advantages, the Voigt regularisation is also of independent analytical interest, as it yields globally well-posed models with enhanced regularity while remaining asymptotically consistent with the original system as
\alpha\to 0
; see, e.g.,
[
7
,
9
,
28
]
.

Motivated by these considerations, we consider a Voigt-regularised Hall–MHD system
[
22
]
, obtained by applying a spatial filter of the form
(I-\alpha_{1}\Delta)\partial_{t}
to the time derivative in the momentum equation (
1.1a
) and retaining the electron inertia term
\alpha_{2}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{J}
on the right-hand side of (
1.1c
). Despite this regularisation, the equations remain strongly nonlinear due to the convective, Lorentz, and Hall coupling terms, which continue to govern the complex interaction between velocity and magnetic fields. Under this reformulation, the Voigt-regularised Hall–MHD system takes the form:

(1.2a)

\displaystyle(I-\alpha_{1}\Delta)\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p-\boldsymbol{J}\times\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{0}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

(1.2b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{B}+\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{0}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

(1.2c)

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{J}+\sigma\boldsymbol{J}+\eta\boldsymbol{J}\times\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{E}+\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{B}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

(1.2d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}-\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{0}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

(1.2e)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{u}=\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{B}=0\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathscr{D},

(1.2f)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\boldsymbol{B}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{B}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\mathscr{D},

(1.2g)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0,\quad\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\boldsymbol{J}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}\;

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }(0,T)\times\partial\mathscr{D},

For simplicity, periodic boundary conditions are frequently employed in practice, and our analysis remains valid in this setting. We remark that Voigt-type regularisation has been investigated in related contexts, including the Euler, Navier–Stokes, and standard MHD equations
[
7
,
26
,
28
,
33
]
.

Various numerical algorithms for solving the Hall–MHD problem have recently been proposed. For the
stationary
resistive Hall–MHD system, a structure-preserving finite element method (FEM) and an efficient preconditioner are developed in
[
27
]
. For the time-dependent problem, several works have employed scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) approaches. Specifically,
[
17
]
proposes a Legendre–Galerkin spectral method for bounded domains, while
[
18
]
develops a Hermite–Galerkin spectral method to handle variable density flows on unbounded domains. Concurrently,
[
32
]
combines a BDF2-type integrator with a nonlocal SAV-FEM approach, and a second-order pressure projection for the fluid. From a purely solver-oriented perspective,
[
8
]
utilises a multigrid Newton–Krylov method to efficiently advance the implicit time stepping.

However, the aforementioned SAV-based methods share notable mathematical limitations: they dissipate a modified energy functional rather than the true physical energy, and they may fail to enforce the divergence-free condition exactly at the discrete level. Furthermore, the spectral methods in
[
17
,
18
]
are practically restricted to canonical geometries (e.g., idealised boxes or infinite spaces), lacking the geometric flexibility required to model complex physical boundaries. Most importantly, rigorous error analysis remains unavailable for all of the above schemes.

Preserving the divergence-free condition at the discrete level is essential for both stability and physical fidelity, as violations can induce nonphysical magnetic monopoles and trigger severe numerical instabilities
[
6
,
10
]
. To address these challenges, we develop and rigorously analyse a structure-preserving finite element scheme for the Voigt-regularised Hall–MHD system (
1.2
), which serves as a mathematically well-posed and physically consistent approximation of the original equations (
1.1
). The proposed method enforces the solenoidal constraint exactly through finite element exterior calculus, and satisfies a discrete energy law by means of a skew-symmetric, semi-implicit time discretisation. We establish optimal convergence rates for the Voigt-regularised problem under suitable regularity assumptions. In addition, the analysis extends to the unregularised Hall–MHD system, for which convergence can still be shown, although the Voigt regularisation plays a crucial role in the non-resistive regime (
\sigma=0
in (
1.2c
)).

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 introduces the functional setting, notation, and necessary preliminary results. In Section 3, we establish the stability of the proposed scheme and carry out a rigorous error analysis, culminating in the proof of the main convergence theorem (Theorem
2.2
). Finally, Section
4
presents a series of physically relevant numerical experiments, in both 2.5D and fully 3D cases, to validate the theoretical convergence rates and demonstrate the robustness of the structure-preserving framework.

2.3.
Analytical tools

Let

\mathbb{V}_{h}^{0}

2.5.
The 2.5D Hall–MHD (
d=2
)

Let
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The 2.5D Hall–MHD, which assumes translational invariance along the
z
-axis while retaining all three components of the velocity and magnetic vector fields
[
11
,
27
,
30
]
, will be discussed briefly. Under this assumption, the 3D unknowns decouple into in-plane vectors and out-of-plane scalars, namely
\boldsymbol{u}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},u_{3})
,
\boldsymbol{B}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}},B_{3})
,
\boldsymbol{J}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{J}},J_{3})
, and
\boldsymbol{E}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{E}},E_{3})
.

We denote the in-plane gradient and in-plane Laplacian as
\nabla_{\perp}=(\partial_{x},\partial_{y})^{\top}
and
\Delta_{\perp}=\nabla_{\perp}\cdot\nabla_{\perp}
, respectively. The 3D curl operator splits into two planar operators: the
vector curl

\nabla^{\perp}
mapping scalar fields to in-plane vector fields, and the
scalar curl
(
\mathrm{rot}
) mapping in-plane vector fields to scalars. For a scalar function
\phi:\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
and an in-plane vector field
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}=(v_{1},v_{2})
, these are defined by

\displaystyle\nabla^{\perp}\phi:=(\partial_{y}\phi,-\partial_{x}\phi)^{\top},\quad\mathrm{rot}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}:=\partial_{x}v_{2}-\partial_{y}v_{1}.

These operators are consistent with the 2D analogues of the cross product, namely

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\times\phi:=(v_{2}\phi,-v_{1}\phi)^{\top},\quad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\times\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}:=v_{1}w_{2}-v_{2}w_{1}.

With these operators, the continuous 3D Hall–MHD system naturally decomposes into an in-plane system governing the transverse fields and an out-of-plane system describing scalar transport and magnetic stretching:

(2.25a)

\displaystyle(I-\alpha_{1}\Delta_{\perp})\partial_{t}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\nu\Delta_{\perp}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla_{\perp})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}+\nabla_{\perp}p-\big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{J}}\times B_{3}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}\times J_{3}\big)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(2.25b)

\displaystyle(I-\alpha_{1}\Delta_{\perp})\partial_{t}u_{3}-\nu\Delta_{\perp}u_{3}+(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla_{\perp})u_{3}-(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{J}}\times\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}})

\displaystyle=0,

(2.25c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}+\nabla^{\perp}E_{3}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(2.25d)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}B_{3}+\mathrm{rot}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{E}}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.25e)

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\partial_{t}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{J}}+\sigma\widetilde{\boldsymbol{J}}+\eta\big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{J}}\times B_{3}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}\times J_{3}\big)-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{E}}-\big(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\times B_{3}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}\times u_{3}\big)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(2.25f)

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\partial_{t}J_{3}+\sigma J_{3}+\eta\widetilde{\boldsymbol{J}}\times\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}-E_{3}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\times\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.25g)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{J}}-\nabla^{\perp}B_{3}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(2.25h)

\displaystyle J_{3}-\mathrm{rot}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.25i)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\perp}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}=\nabla_{\perp}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}

\displaystyle=0,

subject to the initial data
\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{B}(0)=\boldsymbol{B}_{0}
, and boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0,\quad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=0,\quad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{J}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=0,\quad u_{3}=E_{3}=J_{3}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\mathscr{D},

where
\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{1},n_{2})^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(-n_{2},n_{1})^{\top}
denote the unit outward normal and the unit tangent vector on
\partial\mathscr{D}
, respectively.

This structural decomposition is mirrored at the discrete level, where the discrete unknowns are sought in conforming finite element spaces:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n},u_{3,h}^{n})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{0}\times V_{h}^{0},\quad p_{h}^{n}\in Q_{h}^{0},\quad\boldsymbol{B}_{h}^{n}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}_{h}^{n},B_{3,h}^{n})\in\mathbb{RT}_{h}^{0}\times Q_{h},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}_{h}^{n}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h}^{n},E_{3,h}^{n})\in\mathbb{X}_{h}^{0}\times V_{h}^{0},\quad\boldsymbol{J}_{h}^{n}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{h}^{n},J_{3,h}^{n})\in\mathbb{X}_{h}^{0}\times V_{h}^{0}.

Furthermore, the 3D de Rham complex underlying the structure-preserving scheme reduces to the 2D rotated de Rham complex governing the in-plane magnetic field:

(2.26)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{H}_{0}(\mathrm{div})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}_{0}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nabla^{\perp}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{div}}

In particular, when
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}_{h}^{n}
is sought in an
\mathbb{H}_{0}(\mathrm{div})
-conforming finite element space (e.g., Raviart–Thomas), its evolution is described by the in-plane induction equation analogous to (
2.23b
):

\mathrm{d}_{\tau}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}_{h}^{n}+\nabla^{\perp}E_{3,h}^{n}=\boldsymbol{0}.

Since
\mathrm{div}\,(\nabla^{\perp}\phi)\equiv 0
holds, this update at the discrete level preserves the in-plane solenoidal constraint
\mathrm{div}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}_{h}^{n}=0
exactly; cf. (
2.23b
) and (
3.3
) in the full 3D setting.

Furthermore, because this 2.5D formulation perfectly mirrors the antisymmetric coupling of the full 3D equations, it intrinsically inherits the energy dissipation law. To avoid redundancy, the rigorous numerical analysis and stability proofs will be detailed exclusively for the full 3D case, with the understanding that these theoretical guarantees carry over directly to the 2.5D numerical experiments presented in Section
4.2
.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\big(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}^{n-1}-\boldsymbol{u}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\big(\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{3}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{6}}+\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2s}+C\tau^{2}+C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

For the term
I_{7}
, by Young’s and Poincaré’s inequality, and (
2.11
), we have

\displaystyle\left|{I_{7}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{B}_{h}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{3}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{6}}^{2}\leq Ch^{2s}\left\|{\boldsymbol{B}_{h}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{3}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Next, for
I_{8}
, by Young’s and Poincaré’s inequality, (
2.12
), and (
3.15
), we have

\displaystyle\left|{I_{8}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{J}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{3}}^{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{B}}^{n-1}+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{B}}^{n-1}+\boldsymbol{B}^{n-1}-\boldsymbol{B}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{6}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2s}+C\tau^{2}+C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{B}}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\nabla\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

The terms
I_{9}
and
I_{11}
are estimated in a similar manner as
I_{2}
, leading to

\displaystyle\left|{I_{9}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\mathrm{d}_{\tau}\boldsymbol{B}^{n}-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{B}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{B}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\left|{I_{11}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha_{2}^{2}\left\|{\mathrm{d}_{\tau}\boldsymbol{J}^{n}-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{J}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\sigma}{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Next, we estimate
I_{10}
. Note that by the commutative diagram property (
2.8
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{E}}^{n}=\mathrm{curl}\,(\mathcal{I}_{h}^{\mathbb{X}}\boldsymbol{E}^{n}-\boldsymbol{E}^{n})=\mathcal{I}_{h}^{\mathbb{RT}}(\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{E}^{n})-\mathrm{curl}\,\boldsymbol{E}^{n}.

Therefore, by (
2.14
) and Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left|{I_{10}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2s}+\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{B}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Similarly for
I_{12}
, by (
2.13
) and Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left|{I_{12}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2s}+\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

For
I_{13}
, we write
\boldsymbol{J}_{h}^{n}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}+\Pi_{h}^{\mathbb{X}}\boldsymbol{J}^{n}
, then apply (
2.15
), (
2.11
), (
3.15
), and Young’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\left|{I_{13}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\Pi_{h}^{\mathbb{X}}\boldsymbol{J}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{B}}^{n-1}+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{B}}^{n-1}+\boldsymbol{B}^{n-1}-\boldsymbol{B}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2s}+C\tau^{2}+C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{B}}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

For
I_{14}
and
I_{15}
, by (
2.11
), (
2.18
), and Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|{I_{14}}\right|+\left|{I_{15}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{B}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}+C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{6}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{B}_{h}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{3}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2s}\left(1+\left\|{\boldsymbol{B}_{h}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{3}}^{2}\right)+\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Finally, by a similar argument, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|{I_{16}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2s}+C\tau^{2}+C\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{B}}^{n-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}.

We now substitute these estimates back into (
3
). If
\sigma>0
, then we can choose
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small to absorb the relevant terms, and sum over
m\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Applying the discrete Gronwall lemma, noting (
3.7
) and (
3.16
), we obtain (
3.3
).

If
\sigma=0
, the term
\epsilon\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}
arising in the estimates of
I_{12}
up to
I_{16}
can no longer be absorbed by the dissipative contribution
\sigma\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}
in (
3
). Instead, we make use of the term with coefficient
\alpha_{2}
on the left-hand side of (
3
) to control
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{J}}^{n}}\right\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}
, and apply the discrete Gronwall lemma with sufficiently small
\tau>0
. Consequently, the constant
C
in (
3.3
) depends on
\alpha_{2}^{-1}
in this case. The proof is now complete.
∎

With the preceding estimates established, we are now in a position to prove our main result, Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

This follows from Proposition
3.3
, the error decompositions (
3.8
)–(
3.12
), and the triangle inequality.
∎

4.
Numerical simulations

We perform several physically relevant numerical simulations to assess the performance of our scheme.
To verify convergence, we compute a reference solution on a fine mesh and with a small time step. For any variable
\boldsymbol{v}
, the error
\mathcal{E}_{s}^{\boldsymbol{v}}(h,\tau)
at a fixed time
T
is defined by

\mathcal{E}_{s}^{\boldsymbol{v}}(h,\tau):=\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{N}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{ref}}(T)}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{s}},\quad s\in\{0,1\}.

Here,
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{N}
is the numerical solution with mesh size
h
at time
T=N\tau
, and
\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{ref}}(T)
denotes the reference solution at the same time
T
.

4.3.
Hall-driven reconnection in a confined modulated Harris sheet

We consider a confined Harris-type current sheet in a three-dimensional domain
\mathscr{D}=[0,1]^{3}
, designed to study magnetic reconnection in Hall–MHD under perfectly conducting boundary conditions. The initial fluid velocity is taken to be zero so that the dynamics are driven purely by magnetic forces. The initial magnetic field is constructed from a scalar magnetic potential
A_{0}
of the form

A_{0}(x,y,z)=B_{0}\delta\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)\sin(\pi z)\log\cosh\left(\frac{y-0.5}{\delta}\right),

and we define
\boldsymbol{B}_{0}=\mathrm{curl}\,(A_{0}\hat{\boldsymbol{z}})
. By construction, the magnetic field is divergence-free in
\mathscr{D}
and satisfies
\boldsymbol{B}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
\partial\mathscr{D}
. The parameter
\delta>0
controls the thickness of the current sheet, while the sinusoidal modulation confines the sheet to the interior of
\mathscr{D}
and avoids boundary artefacts. The parameters are chosen to balance numerical stability with the ability to resolve Hall-driven reconnection on coarse meshes. In particular, we set
\nu=0.004
,
\sigma=0.008
,
\eta=0.15
, and
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=10^{-5}
. The simulation is performed on a uniform mesh with
h=1/16
and
\tau=0.01
.

Snapshots of the magnetic field and fluid velocity at representative times are shown in Figure
13
for
\eta\neq 0
and in Figure
14
for
\eta=0
. As the simulation evolves, the initially planar current sheet thins near the centre of the domain and undergoes magnetic reconnection, leading to the formation of magnetic islands in the plane
z=0.5
. In contrast to resistive (non-Hall) MHD, the Hall term accelerates the reconnection process and generates localised small-scale structures around the reconnection region. In particular, a characteristic quadrupolar pattern in selected components of
\boldsymbol{J}
is observed near the reconnection site, which is a hallmark of Hall–MHD dynamics.

The evolution of the energy and the maximum divergence error
\max_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left|{\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{B}}\right|
is shown in Figure
15
. The results confirm that the scheme remains energy-stable and preserves the divergence-free constraint up to solver tolerance, even with relatively coarse spatial and temporal discretisation. This experiment therefore demonstrates the robustness of the proposed scheme in a fully three-dimensional reconnection setting driven by Hall effects.
