Review of Formation Damage Assessment for CO2 Injection and Storage
Authors: Dilyara Sagandykova, Islam Kakharov, Shynggys Aitkazy, Maral Khanjani, Peyman Pourafshary, Muhammad Shahzad Kamal, Shams Kalam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon capture and storage (CCS) has become an essential strategy for reducing CO2 emissions, with injection and storage projects increasingly implemented around the world. However, the long-term effectiveness of geological storage depends on maintaining injectivity and reservoir integrity, both of which face challenges from various formation damage mechanisms that are specific to CO2 injection. First, this review covers recent advances in understanding the physical, chemical, thermal, and biological processes that cause permeability loss, including salt precipitation from brine drying, fines migration, hydrate and ice formation due to Joule-Thomson cooling, acid-induced mineral dissolution, clay swelling, asphaltene precipitation, microbial activity, and phase trapping. The second part of this paper provides an in-depth review of assessment techniques used to diagnose and predict formation damage such as experimental methods, numerical simulations, analytical techniques, machine learning, and molecular modeling. Despite significant progress, many uncertainties remain regarding how these mechanisms interact over operational time scales and under real-world reservoir conditions. Future research should focus on integrating experimental and numerical approaches, expanding studies across different lithologies, and developing advanced in situ monitoring methods to improve the risk assessment and manage injectivity more effectively. Overcoming these challenges is crucial for enabling safe, efficient, and scalable deployment of CO2 storage technologies, with particular emphasis on conducting long-term studies that capture the coupled processes and reservoir responses over extended operational time scales.

Introduction

Global warming is one of the critical environmental problems that the world is facing today. It presents a significant threat to ecosystems, human health, and world economies. One of the factors that caused this is the steady increase in carbon dioxide (CO2) levels in the atmosphere. Over the recent decades, the process of urbanization and industrialization, particularly in developing countries, has accelerated the emission of CO2, driven by increased energy demands and infrastructure development. Planet Earth witnesses unprecedentedly elevated global temperatures and record-high atmospheric CO2 concentrations these days. Global energy consumption will rise by nearly 50% over the next three decades, while the atmospheric content of CO2 has already risen by 12% since the year 1750. The above reality suggests the magnitude of the challenge and the implications it has for the health and security of everyone living in the world. There is a broad array of possible mitigation measures, even if the role of carbon capture and storage (CCS) has been considered by many to be central. The process of CCS takes place in three stages: (1) capture of the CO2 from the source, (2) transportation of the gas, usually by vessels or by pipelines, to an appropriate location, and (3) injection of the gas into the deepest geological formations, where the gas stands the chance of being retained during long periods of time. Alongside energy efficiency policies and the massive deployment of renewable energy, the application of CCS technology can form a basis for meeting net-zero ambitions and mitigating climate change.

Geological sequestration has been identified as the most effective technique among others for CO2 storage. This technique offers safe and long-term storage and can be implemented on a large scale. Moreover, CO2 storage provides dual benefits, as injecting CO2 into depleted oil reservoirs not only reduces greenhouse gas emissions but also enhances the oil recovery. Enhanced oil recovery (EOR) using CO2 is a demonstration of geological sequestration as a low-cost method of storage. Also, it should be noted that the given technique will allow oil operators to prolong the productive life of their fields and reduce their carbon footprints at the same time. These days, most of the oil reserves are depleting, and with this trend, researchers and oil companies are trying to develop EOR techniques.

A variety of geological formations can be suitable for storing CO2. Depleted oil and gas reservoirs are among the most common options. These reservoirs were previously productive and have already undergone hydrocarbon extraction. They are advantageous because of their well-documented geology and high capacity for fluid storage. Another option is deep saline aquifersporous rock formations saturated with brine located several kilometers beneath the Earth’s surface. These formations are widespread and hold significant potential for long-term CO2 storage. Additionally, unmineable coal seamsdeep underground layers too costly to mineare used. The Enhanced Coal Bed Methane Recovery (ECBM) technique involves injecting CO2 to displace methane, effectively storing it. Their ability to adsorb CO2 onto the coal surface makes them a practical storage option. Salt caverns, which are underground cavities within salt formations typically created via solution mining, are also suitable. Formation damage involves various physical, chemical, thermal, and biological processes that decrease the permeability and porosity, thereby impairing fluid flow in the reservoir. It is one of the main challenges during CO2 injection, affecting not only the injectivity but also the overall reservoir performance. These damage processes include salt and mineral precipitation, fine migration, clay swelling, gas hydrate formation, asphaltene deposition, microbial activity, and phase trapping, among others. When CO2 is injected into the reservoir, it reacts with the formation and reservoir fluids, often leading to the development of solid materials such as scales and asphaltenes. There are more issues with offshore CO2 injections, including specific well completion requirements, enhanced structures and platforms, CO2-resistant pipelines, and challenges such as the potential for increased structural loads and hydrate formation. Geomechanical risks exist throughout the entire lifecycle of the CCS project, as pore pressure typically rises significantly during the CO2 injection. This is partly due to the low permeability of CO2 when it replaces viscous brine through flow pathways. In addition, the CO2 leakage in the underground storage formation is also an important issue, especially at the initial stages of injection. Such risks are often linked to the limited geological understanding and complexity of the formations, which come from inadequate site-specific data.

EOR and CO2 storage applications need to be effective, and long-term success depends on using comprehensive assessment tools that can diagnose, monitor, and measure formation damage. Most experimental tools have been developed and employed across a wide range of techniques, each offering its own benefits and limitations, such as core flooding tests, X-ray computed tomography, scanning electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction, and nuclear magnetic resonance. Numerical and molecular simulation models, along with machine learning models (MLs), are increasingly utilized because they enable the prediction of reservoir-scale formation damage caused by injection conditions. This paper provides a critical review of the current understanding of formation damage mechanisms related to the CO2 injection and storage. It aims to describe assessment procedures, including laboratory tests, digital core analysis, simulation, and data-driven models, to create a comprehensive framework that guides the selection of appropriate diagnostic tools, the interpretation of damage indicators, and the design and management of CO2 storage projects.

A number of studies have aimed to investigate the impact of CO2 injection on reservoir formations; however, most either provide a broad general overview of EOR or focus on specific aspects of the CO2-brine-rock interaction. − Some studies are dedicated to CO2 storage infrastructure as well. , Yuan and Wood (2018) provided a multidisciplinary review of the formation damage mechanisms, which involved the mechanical, chemical, biological, and thermal processes under different EOR techniques, namely, chemical, thermal, and gas-based. In their review, they did not discuss the particular mechanisms and did not focus on studying formation damage-assessment techniques. Sori et al. (2024) also analyzed geological CO2 storage with respect to leakage threats, numerical modeling, and monitoring techniques. However, formation damage mechanisms and their assessment were not a specific priority for discussion, even though formation integrity was mentioned. Similarly, Kalam et al. Although the works present their specific contributions, a gap remains in the literature. To the best of our knowledge, all current review articles fail to fully integrate laboratory experimental data, field observations, and modern diagnostics to evaluate the mechanisms and effects of formation damage under CO2 injection and long-term storage conditions. With the increasing attention to CCS and the implementation of large-scale CO2 injection schemes, there is a need for a specific review that consolidates knowledge of formation damage in this area. Mechanisms of Formation Damage during CO2 Injection

Injectivity is a critical factor for the successful storage of CO2 in geological formations as it directly influences injection strategies both economically and technically. Injectivity impairment is caused by formation damage that reduces permeability and porosity near the wellbore by creating an extra pressure drop. Several mechanisms contribute to the decline in rock permeability and the increase in the skin factor during CO2 injection. These mechanisms include chemical processes, such as brine evaporation, dissolution of CO2 into brine, and asphaltene precipitation. Physical processes mainly involve fine migration that clogs small pores. Thermal processes are related to Joule-Thomson cooling, endothermic water vaporization, and the exothermic CO2 dissolution effect, which cause additional salt precipitation. Biological mechanisms are also involved.

Salt Precipitation

Several CO2 storage projects implemented in depleted oil and gas reservoirs and saline aquifers report that brine salinity varies from 12,000 ppm to 200,000 ppm. CO2 injection typically occurs under supercritical conditions, as this state offers advantages such as improved storage efficiency and increased solubility. Moreover, in its supercritical form, CO2 behaves similarly to a liquid, implying that fewer fluid volumes are required, and higher density can be achieved. Additionally, the higher solubility of CO2 enables additional trapping mechanisms, such as solubility trapping. The most critical and active mechanism that reduces well injectivity is salt crystallization due to a combination of chemical and thermal processes during scCO2 reaction with fluids in porous media, especially with remaining brine in depleted oil and gas reservoirs and saline aquifers. According to Sokama-Neuyam et al. (2019), salt precipitation is a known challenge even in natural gas injection and production projects, as it has been reported from pilot implementations in the Snøhvit and Ketzin fields. Evaporation is a natural phenomenon when a liquid turns into a gas under a particular pressure and temperature. Injection of scCO2 is characterized by high temperature and pressure, which implies that it brings heat to the equilibrium system of brine and oil in the reservoir. The result of this is a deposition of solute ions, and the degree of this precipitation directly depends on the concentration of solute in the water phase. The rate of salt crystal precipitation and its amount heavily depend on both fluid and rock properties, such as pressure, temperature, the state of injected CO2, ionic composition of brine, and reservoir heterogeneity. Joule-Thomson cooling during CO2 injection into low-pressure reservoirs further intensifies salt precipitation and reduces gas viscosity, compounding injectivity damage. At high injection rates, inertial (Forchheimer) effects further amplify pressure losses near the wellbore, while partial CO2-brine miscibility accelerates water evaporation and salt dropout, both of which compound injectivity damage. Explicit fractional-flow formulas capturing these coupled rate-dependent and miscibility effects have been derived analytically for CO2-water displacement in geological storage settings. Early analytical models, such as those of Zeidouni et al. (2009), reported that static material balance calculations were used to estimate the solid salt saturation in the dry-out region. Compared with the static salt drop-out equation, Kamyabi et al. (2026) calculated salt precipitation by accounting for the movement of the complete evaporation front.

Mineral Dissolution

CO2-rock interactions can be described by a reaction between CO2 and H2O, which leads to the formation of weak carbonic acid that further produces H+ and HCO3 – ions, reacting with different rock minerals. Geochemical interactions between injected fluid and rock matrix are not always a negative phenomenon; indeed, the dissolution of minerals can improve porosity and permeability by the formation of caverns, vugs, and increasing pore throats. Mineral precipitation and CO2 solubility in water are also essential trapping mechanisms. However, these dissolved minerals and precipitated salt crystals lead to fines mobilization that together reduce the permeability of porous media by plugging pore throats. In carbonate-bearing formations, CO2-saturated water generates carbonate (CO3 2–) and bicarbonate (HCO3 –) ions that dissolve intergranular cement, releasing fines and creating two competing effects on permeability: an increase due to porosity gain and a decrease due to fines straining. Wang et al. (2022) developed a mathematical model with a semianalytical solution capturing these coupled dissolution-fines migration dynamics, which was subsequently applied at the field scale by Machado et al. (2023) to quantify injectivity loss during CCS operations in saline aquifers. Overall, geochemical reactions between CO2-brine-rock strongly depend on different parameters: mineral composition of rock matrix, temperature, salinity and pH of brine, injection rates and time of exposure, pressure, and temperature. Fines Migration

Another mechanism that controls the blockage of small pore throats is fines migration, which is the physical movement of small particles, usually up to 20 μm in radius, within pores due to convection forces of wetting fluid transporting these particles. This phenomenon can either be natural, which occurs when fluids produced from the reservoir encapsulate small particles of silica, quartz, or other minerals and then undergo shear forces that block pores, or it can be induced when high-velocity injection fluids cause clogging of pore throats. It is critically important for CO2 flooding or storage processes because various chemical reactions occur between the injected fluids, formation fluids, and rock minerals, causing the dissolution and/or precipitation of small particles such as salt or dissolved minerals.

Pore plugging results in overall permeability reduction even without significant porosity reduction, since pore throats are the main connections between big pores. Thus, their clogging might cause the blockage of key highly permeable channels in porous media. Moreover, due to the complicated and almost chaotic behavior of fine migration that usually happens at high speeds, it is difficult to predict the impact on permeability reduction both analytically and experimentally. A notable geochemically driven pathway is mineral dissolution-induced fines release, where CO2-saturated water dissolves carbonate cement and dislodges fines that subsequently plug pore throats. Wang et al. (2022) derived a semianalytical solution for this process that was incorporated into field-scale numerical simulations by Machado et al. (2023), who demonstrated that fines migration from mineral dissolution can reduce CO2 injectivity by at least 10% within 5 years of injection. Keykhosravi et al. (2025) investigated permeability impairment in sandstones caused by fine migration during CO2 injection. Kamyabi et al. (2026) derived an exact 1D analytical solution for CO2-brine displacement incorporating both fines migration and salt precipitation, showing that fines straining reduces injectivity while improving sweep efficiency. Extending this framework to more realistic stratified reservoir geometries, Prempeh et al. (2026) developed an analytical vertical equilibrium model for layer-cake aquifers that simultaneously accounts for fines migration and partial CO2-brine miscibility, demonstrating through sensitivity analysis that fines straining delays gas breakthrough, improves sweep efficiency, and raises well impedance, while the humidity of injected CO2 modulates the severity of salt precipitation and associated injectivity decline. Similarly, a previous study by Shokrollahi et al. (2024) demonstrated the impact of formation damage in subsurface CO2 storage, emphasizing critical mechanisms, including salt precipitation, hydrate formation, and fines migration. Both Prempeh et al. (2026) and Shokrollahi et al. (2024) conclude that formation damage plays a dual role during CO2 injection: although fines migration and salt precipitation reduce injectivity, they simultaneously improve sweep efficiency and enhance CO2 storage capacity. Sokama-Neuyam et al. (2017) conducted experiments by using the colloidal suspension, which is artificially made from alumina latex particles that behave like fines during scCO2 injection in sandstone cores and obtained severe injectivity reduction caused by pore blockage. However, these particles are not present in sandstone reservoirs, so they cannot fully represent the real behavior of fines.

Asphaltene Precipitation

Asphaltenes are high-molecular-weight components of crude oil that are polar and nonvolatile molecules that are in equilibrium under reservoir conditions and might start to precipitate when external factors such as pressure, temperature, and some chemicals are introduced into the system by disrupting the equilibrium state. Injection of CO2 causes changes in fluid behavior by shifting equilibrium conditions to favor the precipitation of organic solids, mainly asphaltenes. Destabilization of the equilibrium triggers the formation of asphaltenes that further aggregate into larger particles, which can block pore throats and reduce the flow capacity of the reservoir.

Figure illustrates the formation and aggregation of asphaltene molecules during the introduction of CO2 into a stabilized system in three main steps. CO2 disrupts the equilibrium in the system, and small asphaltene particles accumulate in larger clusters. Pressure, temperature, and oil composition are the main factors in asphaltene deposition. The higher the asphaltene content in the crude oil, the higher the probability of asphaltene precipitation. At high pressures, the solvent interacts better with asphaltenes, and the probability of precipitation decreases. However, low pressure can lead to solvent release, and the probability of asphaltene precipitation increases. The role of temperature is also difficult to overestimate: high temperatures tend to increase the solubility of asphaltenes, and the likelihood of precipitation decreases, while low temperatures can lead to asphaltene solidification and deposition, increasing the likelihood of reservoir damage.

These solid particles cause a reduction of both absolute and relative permeabilities and porosity by pore-through plugging and precipitation on rock surfaces. Shojaei et al. (2024) found that asphaltene precipitation causes wettability alteration by measuring the contact angle before and after CO2 injection. Precipitation of asphaltene on the rock surface causes its polar part to absorb oil components, changing wettability from 144.8° to 161.4° toward oil-wet rock. However, shifting the wettability of the rock toward more oil-wet conditions might decrease the efficiency of CO2-EOR and/or further water treatment because it becomes harder to remove oil droplets from the rock surface, which reduces microscopic sweep efficiency.

A recent study by Mahdavi et al. (2024) examines the mechanism of CO2-induced asphaltene instability in oil reservoirs and indicates that there is no direct link between asphaltene precipitation and the CO2 injection pressure. Higher injection pressure results in increased asphaltene precipitation, as instability grows. However, once a certain point is reached where maximum precipitation occurs, asphaltene dissolves at higher pressures, indicating the supercritical state of CO2. Therefore, it is crucial to maintain such operating constraints to prevent asphaltene precipitation in the near-wellbore region.

Overall, asphaltene precipitation is an active mechanism of formation damage during CO2 injection that involves several submechanisms, such as thermal, physical, and chemical effects. The process of asphaltene precipitation and aggregation is a chemical phenomenon influenced by thermal effects, which are further affected by the advective forces of injected CO2, leading to fine migration and a decrease in the permeability of porous media.

Microbial Activities

The biological mechanism of formation damage is referred to as the impact of biological activities, such as microbes and nutrient streams. Several water injection operations during well stimulation, drilling, and EOR techniques that use bacteria might lead to injectivity impairment. Typically, the growth of bacteria is associated with temperatures below 90 °C, which is higher than the critical point of CO2 and implies that microbial activity can be considered during scCO2 injection operations. However, there is no significant research on the effects of biological activities during CO2 injection. Overall, in water-based operations, there are three main submechanisms of bacterial entrainment: plugging, corrosion, and toxicity. First, microbial biofilms can form on rock surfaces, blocking pore spaces and reducing permeability. Biofilms can also impact fluid flow in the reservoir and equipment like pipes, pumps, and valves. Second, some types of bacteria can facilitate an electrokinetic hydrogen reduction that leads to pitting and hydrogen stress cracking on metallic surfaces. Third, sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) can produce acids that degrade the reservoir rock or metal infrastructure, leading to corrosion of equipment and potential damage to the formation and creation of toxic hydrogen sulfide gas.

Gas Hydrate Formation

Gas hydrates are ice-like crystalline compounds formed when low molecular weight gases, such as CH4 or CO2, combine with water under specific thermodynamic conditions, including low temperatures and high pressures. , The formation of crystalline compounds in porous media has a significant impact on CO2 injectivity and permeability reduction as hydrates block pore spaces and throats in near-wellbore regions and potentially cause well integrity issues. When cold-CO2 is injected into a depleted oil and gas field or aquifer, it can cause a significant drop in temperature due to the isenthalpic expansion of CO2. If the temperature falls below the hydrate formation equilibrium point and water is present, CO2 hydrates may form. Formation of hydrates can be described in 3 steps: nucleation, growth, and agglomeration. Nucleation starts when gas molecules dissolve in water, forming unstable clusters, and once these clusters reach a critical size, growth begins. Several factors affect the likelihood and the extent of hydrate formations during CO2 injection: pressure and temperature, water abundance, and petrophysical properties of porous media. High water saturation increases the potential for hydrates to form, as there is more water available to combine with CO2. Additionally, the presence of residual water in the pore spaces may promote faster nucleation due to the “memory effect” when water that has previously formed hydrates becomes more likely to create new hydrates upon further exposure to CO2. Fine-grained rocks with high surface areas can promote hydrate nucleation by providing numerous sites for the hydrate to form. , Joule-Thomson cooling during CO2 injection into low-pressure reservoirs can trigger hydrate formation near the wellbore. Chesnokov et al. (2025) derived an analytical solution for this nonisothermal process that determines the maximum safe injection rate to prevent hydrate formation.

Gauteplass et al. (2020) experimentally examined various injection rates of the CO2 and N2 gases and tracked the formation of hydrates. They observed the formation of CO2 hydrates within an injection rate range of 0.5–10 mL/min, which suggests that the related risks of hydrate formation are not just during the shut-in phases but also during the steady state of fluid flow. Furthermore, from their microfluidic model, they deduced the extent of heterogeneity to support the formation of the hydrates. Moreover, hydrate formation can coexist with salt precipitation, which makes the situation more complex.

Clay Swelling

Clay swelling is a critical formation damage mechanism during CO2 injection, primarily driven by the interaction between CO2-saturated brine and clay minerals in sandstone reservoirs. When CO2 dissolves in formation water, it forms carbonic acid, significantly lowering the pH to around four under reservoir conditions. This acidic environment facilitates cation exchange between fluid and clay minerals. This process reduces pore throat diameter, increases flow resistance, and decreases permeability, as observed in core flood and imbibition tests, where oil recovery was 15% lower in samples exposed to carbonic acid compared to brine. A previous study showed that various clay minerals can adsorb significant amounts of CO2. In the case of smectite, this adsorption may cause a volumetric expansion and generate swelling pressures. While CO2 adsorption helps in trapping the gas, the resulting swelling can alter local stress conditions and, under specific, unfavorable scenarios, may lead to shear failure. Therefore, clay reactivity must be carefully considered when designing CO2-EOR and storage operations, particularly in reservoirs with high clay content or significant water cut.

Phase Trapping

Phase trapping, also termed capillary or residual trapping, can cause significant formation damage during CO2 injection in enhanced oil recovery operations. When CO2 is injected, it can displace resident fluids such as water or oil, but capillary forces often immobilize portions of these phases within the pore structure. This leads to a reduction in permeability and injectivity, especially in tight or water-wet formations. In these environments, trapped gas or oil clusters occupy pore spaces that would otherwise contribute to flow, obstructing both oil production and CO2 mobility. Additionally, wettability changes and complex pore geometries further amplify trapping, intensifying damage and limiting EOR efficiency. One study demonstrated that gas trapping during near-miscible CO2-WAG injection degraded sweep efficiency by blocking flow paths and isolating oil, directly reducing recovery rates. Measurement Methods of Formation Damage

Experimental Techniques

Formation damage can be measured in the core, in the pores, and in a variety of experimental techniques such as core flooding (Darcy’s law), X-ray computed tomography (CT), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). These procedures give an idea of decreased permeability, mineral precipitation, the movement of fine particles, asphaltene deposition, and the alteration of wettability. − Although all of the mentioned methods are advanced, a combination of multiple types of experimental techniques is suggested to provide a complex characterization of the formation damage mechanisms.

Core Flood Tests

Core flooding is the most direct and fundamental experimental method used to assess formation damage by directly measuring changes in permeability, fines migration, precipitation of minerals, and changes in wettability by CO2 injection under simulated reservoir conditions. ,, The method uses Darcy’s law, which describes the flow of a fluid in porous media through a pressure drop, permeability, and flow velocity relationship. The governing equation is expressed as follows: Q=kAΔPμL where Q represents the volumetric flow rate, k is the permeability of the core, A is the cross-sectional area, ΔP is the pressure drop across the core length L, and μ is the dynamic viscosity of the injected fluid.

The main flooding equipment includes a high-pressure core holder, injection pumps, pressure transducers, and effluent collectors. Figure below shows a schematic diagram of the core flood device. The common procedure of performing the core flooding experiments to replicate the reservoir conditions and to determine the change in the permeability is usually followed. The core preparation step includes the selection of cylindrical rock samples and the determination of the initial permeability. Subsequently, the injection of CO2 under controlled pressure and temperature conditions takes place. X-ray Diffraction

The most common method used to investigate mineralogical variations of the reservoir rock during the injection and sequestration of CO2 is X-ray diffraction. The technique, which is nondestructive, enables the characterization of the existing minerals, their crystal structures, and chemical signatures. The technique facilitates the evaluation of the impacts of dissolution, fine migration, and precipitation, which lead to formation damage. In the XRD testing of rock powder, X-rays are focused, and they are scattered in a characteristic manner according to the crystal structures of the existing minerals. , The resulting diffraction pattern enables the estimation of the types and the relative quantities of the existing minerals. The technique tends to be applied jointly with imaging to identify the damaged structure and nature.

Nightingale et al. (2009) applied XRD to examine the evolution of the permeability of sandstone rocks during the injection of CO2. The study detected extensive clay dissolution and migration. In the same line, Iglauer et al. (2014) analyzed the change of Berea and Fontainebleau sandstones’ permeability and discovered that the Berea sandstone exhibited an extensive drop in its permeability by migration of fine grains and mineral reactions, which was proven by the results of the XRD examination. Mohamed et al. (2012) applied the X-ray examination during their core flooding experiment, in which effluent examination detected the formation of minerals like kaolinite and the formation of carbonate because of the reaction of CO2 and the brine. Furthermore, Ahmat et al. (2022) highlighted the importance of the role of the examination of XRD in tracing the mineralogical transformation during the CO2 geostorage. The examination confirmed that the dissolution of calcite and dolomite was the main cause of the formation damage, which affected the porosity considerably.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance

Nuclear magnetic resonance is also a frequent nondestructive technique applied to examine formation damage. The phenomenon encompasses the spins that are retained predominantly in the protons. They orient themselves in the magnetic field, and after the release of these spins, an electromagnetic signal is measured. The pore distribution directly correlates with the resulting relaxation time spectrum (T 2) and allows an estimation of the connectivity of the pores. The longer the relaxation time, the larger the pores of the flowing fluids, and the shorter the relaxation time, the smaller the pores of the negligible fluid movement. ,

Wang et al. employed NMR to examine asphaltene distribution in the pre- and post-CO2 injection cores. Confirming the previous, the extent of asphaltene deposition was higher in medium to larger pores, leading to a progressive deterioration of permeability. A similar result was achieved in another study, displaying the effect of permeability drop and change of wettability by asphaltene precipitation. In addition, Huang et al. (2022) examined the role of the CO2 injection pressure in asphaltene deposition, and the finding was that higher pressures increase deposition, leading to pore blocking and decreased injectivity. Also, NMR was used by Prunelet and co-workers (2004) to examine the features of the flocculation and aggregation of asphaltenes, attributing the variation of the relaxation time to the increased viscosity and the occurrence of flow assurance problems. In another study, NMR was used to examine T 2 relaxation time periods to monitor the change of porosity and pore diameter distribution. Molecular Simulation

Molecular simulation techniques provide a powerful framework for investigating physicochemical processes across multiple spatial and temporal scales, effectively bridging the theoretical models and experimental observations. Depending on the system’s complexity and the phenomena of interest, different simulation approaches are employed ranging from quantum-level descriptions to mesoscale representations. Quantum mechanical methods such as density functional theory (DFT) are suitable for atomistic interactions and energy calculations at the smallest scales, while statistical and classical techniquesincluding grand canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) and molecular dynamics (MD)enable the analysis of adsorption behavior, molecular transport, and thermodynamic properties over longer length and time scales. To address larger and more complex systems, coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CGMD) and dissipative particle dynamics (DPD) extend simulations into the mesoscale domain by reducing the computational cost while preserving essential physical behavior. Challenges and Future Outlook

Despite considerable progress in understanding formation damage mechanisms during CO2 injection, several critical challenges remain that limit the reliable prediction and management of the injectivity loss in geological storage formations. These challenges become increasingly significant as CO2 storage projects transition from short-term pilot studies to long-term, large-scale deployments.

First, the coupled interaction between geochemical reactions and geomechanical responses under reservoir conditions remains insufficiently understood. CO2-induced brine acidification can trigger mineral dissolution, secondary precipitation, and mechanical weakening of the rock matrix, leading to competing effects on the permeability and injectivity. Accurately capturing these coupled processes, particularly their feedback mechanisms over time, remains a major scientific and modeling challenge.

Second, most experimental insights into formation damage are derived from short-duration laboratory studies, whereas CO2 storage operations span decades to centuries. This disparity in time scales introduces significant uncertainty in predicting long-term permeability evolution, injectivity sustainability, and caprock integrity. The absence of long-term experimental analogues and field-scale observations limits confidence in extrapolating laboratory findings to real storage conditions.

Third, the role of impurities in industrial CO2 streams presents an additional layer of complexity. Reactive components such as sulfur- and nitrogen-bearing species can intensify chemical reactions, alter mineral assemblages, and accelerate pore-scale damage processes. However, the combined effects of multicomponent CO2 streams under realistic reservoir conditions remain poorly constrained, particularly with respect to injectivity degradation and sealing performance.

Fourth, reservoir heterogeneity and natural fracture networks significantly complicate the prediction of the formation damage. Variations in pore size distribution, mineral composition, and structural discontinuities can lead to highly localized damage phenomena, including fine migration, salt precipitation, and flow channeling. These processes are difficult to reproduce in controlled laboratory experiments and remain challenging to scale up using conventional numerical models.

Finally, although advanced numerical and data-driven models offer promising tools for predicting formation damage, their practical application is constrained by limited field-scale validation and incomplete coupling among multiphase flow, geochemical reactions, and geomechanical deformation. The lack of comprehensive data sets for model calibration and validation remains a key barrier to reliable injectivity forecasting.

Looking forward, future research should prioritize integrated experimental–numerical frameworks capable of capturing long-term CO2-brine-rock interactions under realistic reservoir conditions. Expanding studies to mixed lithologies, improving representation of reservoir heterogeneity, and advancing in situ monitoring techniquessuch as time-lapse imaging and reactive diagnosticswill be essential for reducing uncertainty and improving risk assessment. Addressing these challenges is critical for enabling safe, efficient, and scalable deployment of CO2 storage technologies.

Conclusions

This paper presents the varied and interrelated formation damage mechanisms that impact the CO2 injection and storage processes. Unlike conventional injection schemes, CO2 poses novel challenges such as the precipitation of salt resulting from brine evaporation, fines migration due to high-rate injection, and hydrate and ice formation by Joule-Thomson cooling. Other issues are the formation of acid and mineral dissolution, which can compromise the rock matrix and result in secondary precipitation farther into the formation. Asphaltene precipitation in reservoirs containing residual hydrocarbons, clay swelling triggered by changes in water chemistry, and microbial activity that alters the pore structure or promotes biofilm growth all further complicate injectivity management. Phase trapping and wettability changes also influence long-term storage behavior.

Advances in laboratory experiments, pore-scale imaging, machine learning, and molecular simulations have greatly improved our understanding of these processes, but predictive models still struggle to capture their combined effects under realistic field conditions. Future research should focus on integrating experimental and numerical approaches, expanding studies to include mixed lithologies, and improving in situ monitoring tools to observe permeability changes and geochemical transformations over time. Tackling these challenges is crucial for the secure, efficient, and large-scale deployment of the CO2 storage technologies.
