Smallest distances between zeros of Gaussian analytic functions
Renjie Feng, Dong Yao

Abstract
. In this article, we study the smallest distances between the zeros of Gaussian analytic functions over compact Riemann surfaces. Our main result is that, after appropriate rescaling, the point process of the smallest distances converge to a Poisson point process with a universal rate. Furthermore, the locations where these smallest distances occur tend to follow a uniform measure with respect to the volume form. As a consequence, the limiting density of the k k -th rescaled smallest distance is proportional to x 4 ​ k − 1 ​ e − x 4 x^{4k-1}e^{-x^{4}} for any k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 . Analogous results hold for the classical Gaussian Entire Functions.



1.
Introduction

Let
M
be a smooth compact Riemann surface polarized by a positive holomorphic line bundle
L
.
We give
L
a Hermitian metric
h
with positive curvature
\Theta_{h}
and give
M
the Kähler form
\omega
such that
\omega/\pi=\Theta_{h}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the line bundle
L
has degree one.
Since
\Theta_{h}
is a de Rham representative of the first Chern class
c_{1}(L)
(see, e.g.,
[
14
, Chapter 1]
), the degree of
L
is given by the integral of
\Theta_{h}
. It follows that

\int_{M}\frac{\omega}{\pi}=\int_{M}\Theta_{h}=\deg(L)=1,

allowing
\omega/\pi
to be interpreted as a probability measure on
M
.

Given two points
z
and
w
on the Riemann surface
(M,\omega)
, we denote
d_{g}(z,w)
as the geodesic distance between them with respect to the Hermitian metric induced by the Kähler form
\omega
.

Given
s
, a Gaussian random holomorphic section of the line bundle
L^{n}
(as described in §
2.2
), we define
Z_{n}(s)
as its random zero set. The cardinality satisfies
|Z_{n}(s)|=n
since the degree of
L^{n}
is
n
. Dropping the dependence on
s
for simplicity, we write

Z_{n}=Z_{n}(s)=\{z_{1},z_{2},....,z_{n}\}.

(1)

Globally, the limiting distribution of zeros is given by the probability measure
\omega/\pi
(see (
18
) below). Locally, for a given zero, the universal rescaling limit of the correlation functions (see (
25
)) implies that, on average, there is another zero within a geodesic distance of order
n^{-1/2}
. In other words, the nearest neighbor distance between zeros is, on average, of order
n^{-1/2}
.

In this article, we investigate a different local statistic, namely the smallest distances among all zeros. For random zeros in
Z_{n}
, there are
(n-1)n/2
geodesic distances between them, and these distances are almost surely distinct. We can arrange them in ascending order as
\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}<\cdots<\sigma_{{(n-1)n}/2}
. The purpose of this article is to study the decay order of the
k
-th smallest distance
\sigma_{k}
and its limiting distribution as
n\to\infty
for any given
k\geq 1
.

For each pair of distinct zeros
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
in
Z_{n}
, we choose
\widehat{z}_{ij}
uniformly from the set
\{z_{i},z_{j}\}
, i.e., each point is selected with a probability of 1/2, to mark the location of the pair. We consider the following point process of the rescaled smallest distances together with their locations,

\mathcal{S}_{n}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\delta_{n^{3/4}d_{g}(z_{i},z_{j}),\,\widehat{z}_{ij}}.

(2)

Our main result is

Theorem 1
.

As
n\to\infty
,
\mathcal{S}_{n}
converges in distribution to a Poisson point process
\mathcal{S}
on
[0,\infty)\times M
with a universal rate

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}([0,a]\times U)]=\Big(\frac{1}{8}a^{4}\Big)\int_{U}\frac{\omega}{\pi},

for any
a\in(0,\infty)
and the measurable subset
U\subseteq M
.

As a direct corollary, we have

Corollary 1
.

Let
\sigma_{k}
be the
k
-th smallest distance between all pairs of zeros,
then its limiting density satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\big(\frac{1}{8}\big)^{1/4}n^{3/4}\sigma_{k}\in[x,x+dx]\Big)=\frac{4x^{4k-1}}{(k-1)!}e^{-x^{4}}dx.

Furthermore, let
\pi_{k}
be the location of
\widehat{z}_{ij}
such that
d_{g}(z_{i},z_{j})
equals
\sigma_{k}
, then
\pi_{k}
is asymptotically independent of
\sigma_{k}
, and tends to the uniform measure with respect to the volume form, i.e., for any
U\subseteq M
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\pi_{k}\in U)=\int_{U}\frac{\omega}{\pi}.

The results indicate that the smallest distances of random zeros exhibit a decay of order
n^{-{3}/{4}}
. These findings are universal and apply specifically to classical
\operatorname{SU(2)}
random polynomials on the complex projective space
\mathbb{CP}^{1}\cong S^{2}
and Gaussian random Riemann theta functions on the 1-dimensional complex torus (see §
2
for the precise definitions).

For the classical Gaussian Entire Functions (GEF), one can derive the smallest distances for its zeros similarly. The GEF is given by

g(z)=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{b_{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}z^{j},

where
b_{j}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) standard complex Gaussian random variables.
The distribution of the zeros of GEF is invariant under translations of
\mathbb{C}
, and it is given by
d\ell/\pi
, where
d\ell
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}
. Now we define
Z_{R}
as the set of zeros of GEF falling in the disk
B_{R}(0)
centered at the origin with radius
R
. We also define the point process of the smallest distances
\mathcal{S}_{R}
, in the same manner as
\mathcal{S}_{n}
earlier,

\mathcal{S}_{R}=\sum_{z_{i}\neq z_{j}\in Z_{R}}\delta_{R^{1/2}d(z_{i},z_{j}),\,\widehat{z}_{ij}/R},

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Euclidean distance. Analogously to
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we have

Theorem 2
.

As
R\to\infty
,
\mathcal{S}_{R}
converges in distribution to a Poisson point process
\mathcal{S}
on
[0,\infty)\times B_{1}(0)
with the rate

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}([0,a]\times U)]=\Big(\frac{1}{8}a^{4}\Big)\int_{U}\frac{d\ell}{\pi},

for any
a\in(0,\infty)
and the measurable subset
U\subseteq B_{1}(0)
, where
d\ell
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}
.

Again, Theorem
2
implies the limiting density of the
k
-th smallest distance between zeros of GEF as in Corollary
1
, and the locations where the smallest distances occur follow the uniform distribution. The proof of Theorem
2
follows the same arguments as the ones in Theorem
1
, and we omit its proof.

In comparison, if one samples
n
points independently from a Binomial distribution on the unit sphere
S^{2}
with respect to the uniform measure, the average value of the nearest neighbor distances is still of the order
n^{-1/2}
. However, the smallest distance between these Binomial points is of the order
n^{-1}
, which has a smaller decay order than the random zeros (e.g.,
[
6
]
). The reason for this is that nearby Binomial points are neutral, whereas random zeros on the 1-dimensional Riemann surfaces discussed in this article repel each other (see (
28
) below).

In a related but different setting, the smallest and largest gaps between successive zeros of smooth stationary Gaussian processes with sufficiently fast decaying covariance kernels were recently investigated in
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
, respectively.
In both works, it was shown that the point processes formed by suitably rescaled extreme gaps converge to Poisson point processes, from which one can determine the corresponding orders of the extreme gaps.

So far we only discuss the cases of Riemann surfaces. For general complex
m
-dimensional compact Kähler manifolds, Bleher-Shiffman-Zelditch considered the intersection of zero locus of
1\leq\ell\leq m
Gaussian random holomorphic sections, and they proved that the point correlation functions of zeros still have some universal limit (
[
7
, Theorem 3.6]
and
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
). For example, by choosing the normal coordinate around a point
z_{0}\in M
, the 2-point correlation function admits a pointwise universal limit

\rho^{\infty}_{2,\ell,m}(u,v)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{2}(z_{0}+\frac{u}{\sqrt{n}},z_{0}+\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}})/n^{2\ell}.

In particular, when
\ell=m
, the zeros locus are discrete, and one has (
[
9
, Theorem 1.3]
)

\rho^{\infty}_{2,m,m}(u,v)=\frac{m+1}{4}|u-v|^{4-2m}+O(|u-v|^{8-2m})\,\,\,\mbox{as}\,\,\,u\to v.

(3)

This implies that the discrete zeros are repulsive for
m=1
, neutral for
m=2
and attractive for
m\geq 3
. This indicates that the behavior of
the smallest distances of the nearest neighbor zeros may be quite different depending on dimensions.

Notation.
The symbols
c
and
C
represent positive constants independent of
n
, but their specific values may vary from line to line.

2.
Gaussian random holomorphic fields

In this section, we review some basic concepts in complex geometry and introduce Gaussian random holomorphic sections over Kähler manifolds of arbitrary dimension. However, we only focus on 1-dimensional Riemann surfaces later on. For a general introduction to complex geometry, see
[
14
, Chapters 0 and 1]
.

2.1.
Kähler geometry

Let
(M,\omega)
be a smooth compact Kähler manifold of complex dimension
m
.
Locally, on a coordinate chart
U\subset M
, the Kähler form can be written as

\omega=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\partial\bar{\partial}\phi,

where
\phi
is a real-valued smooth Kähler potential.

Let
(L,h)\to M
be a positive holomorphic line bundle equipped with a smooth
Hermitian metric
h
. Its Chern curvature form is given by

\Theta_{h}=-\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2\pi}\partial\bar{\partial}\log h(e,e),

where
e
is any local non-vanishing holomorphic section of
L
over
U
.

We assume that
(L,h)
polarizes
(M,\omega)
, namely that

\Theta_{h}=\frac{\omega}{\pi}.

Therefore, one may choose a local
holomorphic frame
e
of
L
such that

h(e,e)=e^{-\phi}:=|e|_{h}^{2}.

Finally we equip
M
with the Hermitian metric
g
corresponding to the Kähler form
\omega
such that the induced Riemannian volume form is

dV:=\frac{\omega^{m}}{m!}.

We denote by
H^{0}(M,L^{n})
the space of global holomorphic sections of the
n
-th tensor power of
L
, and let
d_{n}:=\operatorname{dim}H^{0}(M,L^{n})
. The Hermitian
metric
h
induces a Hermitian metric
h^{n}
on
L^{n}
as
|e^{\otimes n}|_{h^{n}}=|e|_{h}^{n}
. The Hermitian metric
h^{n}
on
L^{n}
, together with the volume form
dV
on
M
,
induces an inner product on the space of square-integrable sections
\mathcal{L}^{2}(M,L^{n})
,
defined by

\langle s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{h^{n}}:=\int_{M}h^{n}(s_{1},s_{2})\,dV,\qquad s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(M,L^{n}).

(4)

We denote by

\Pi_{n}:\mathcal{L}^{2}(M,L^{n})\rightarrow H^{0}(M,L^{n})

the orthogonal projection. The integral kernel of the projection operator
\Pi_{n}
is called the Bergman kernel. Let
\big\{s_{1},...,s_{d_{n}}\big\}

be an orthonormal basis for
H^{0}(M,L^{n})
with respect to the inner product (
4
), then the Bergman kernel reads,

\Pi_{n}(z,w)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}s_{j}(z)\otimes s_{j}(w)^{*}.

A classical example is the Fubini-Study metric of the hyperplane line bundle over the complex projective space
(\mathcal{O}(1),h_{FS})\to(\mathbb{CP}^{1},\omega_{FS})
. In an affine coordinate, the Kähler form and the Kähler potential for the Fubini-Study metric are

\omega_{FS}=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\frac{dz\wedge d\bar{z}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}},\,\,\,\phi_{FS}(z)=\log(1+|z|^{2}).

We equip

\mathcal{O}(1)
with its
Fubini-Study metric, where we can choose a non-vanishing local frame
e(z)
such that

|e(z)|^{2}_{h_{FS}}=e^{-\phi_{FS}}=\frac{1}{1+|z|^{2}}.

An orthonormal basis of
H^{0}(\mathbb{CP}^{1},\mathcal{O}(n))
under the inner product (
4
) is now given by

\left\{\sqrt{\pi^{-1}(n+1){n\choose j}}z^{j}\right\}_{j=0}^{n}.

(5)

Another classical example is the Riemann theta functions over Abelian varieties. We refer to
[
17
, p. 1–11]
and
[
14
, Section 6, Chapter 2]
for more details. For simplicity, consider 1-dimensional complex torus
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{Z}+\sqrt{-1}\mathbb{Z}
. Write
z=x+\sqrt{-1}y
, where
x
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
can be viewed as the periodic coordinate of
\mathbb{T}
with period 1. Consider the Riemann theta function

\theta(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\pi k^{2}+2\pi ikz}.

The zero divisor of
\theta
on
\mathbb{T}
consists of a single point
p=\frac{1+\sqrt{-1}}{2}\quad(\mathrm{mod}\ \mathbb{Z}+i\mathbb{Z})
. Define the line bundle
L:=\mathcal{O}(p)\to\mathbb{T}
.
By Riemann-Roch Theorem,
\dim H^{0}(\mathbb{T},L^{n})=n
. Moreover,
H^{0}(\mathbb{T},L)
is spanned by
\theta(z)
; and
H^{0}\left(\mathbb{T},L^{n}\right)
is generated by:

\theta_{j}(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\pi n(\frac{j}{n}+k)^{2}+2\pi\sqrt{-1}n(\frac{j}{n}+k)z},\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}.

In addition,
\theta_{j}(z)
’s are holomorphic functions over
\mathbb{C}
and satisfy the following quasi-periodicity relations:

\theta_{j}(z+1)=\theta_{j}(z),\quad\theta_{j}(z+\sqrt{-1})=e^{-2\pi\sqrt{-1}nz+n\pi}\theta_{j}(z).

The set of functions

\left\{{\pi^{-1/2}(2n)}^{1/4}\theta_{j}(z)\right\}_{j=0}^{n-1}

forms an orthonormal basis with respect to the inner product (
4
), under the Kähler form and the Kähler potential,

\omega_{\mathbb{T}}=\frac{\pi\sqrt{-1}}{2}{dz\wedge d\bar{z}},\quad\phi_{\mathbb{T}}=2\pi y^{2}.

Finally, we introduce the Kähler normal coordinate.
Consider a complex
m
-dimensional Kähler manifold
(M,\omega)
, and let
z_{0}
be a fixed point in
M
. We can choose a Kähler normal coordinate
\big\{z^{1},..,z^{m}\big\}
in a neighborhood
U\subset M
around
z_{0}
such that
z_{0}=0
, and
the Kähler form at
z_{0}
is (
[
24
, equation (1.3)]
):

\,\,\omega(z_{0})=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}dz^{j}\wedge d\bar{z}^{j},

and it has the following Taylor expansion around
z_{0}
(
[
14
, p. 107]
),

\omega(z)=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\sum\big(\delta_{ij}+[2]\big)dz^{i}\wedge d\bar{z}^{j},

(6)

where the
[2]
here represents a term that vanishes up to order 2 at
z_{0}=0
. The same expansion holds for the Hermitian metric
g
induced by
\omega
.

2.2.
Gaussian random holomorphic fields

The inner product (
4
) induces a complex Gaussian probability measure on the space of global holomorphic sections
H^{0}(M,L^{n})
. Such Gaussian random holomorphic section has the expression:

s:=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}b_{j}s_{j},

(7)

where
\big\{s_{1},...,s_{d_{n}}\big\}

is an orthonormal basis for
H^{0}(M,L^{n})
with respect to the inner product (
4
), and
\big\{b_{1},...,b_{d_{n}}\big\}
are i.i.d. standard complex Gaussian random
variables that satisfy,

\mathbb{E}b_{i}=0,\quad\mathbb{E}b_{i}b_{j}=0,\quad\mathbb{E}b_{i}\bar{b}_{j}=\delta_{ij}.

(8)

The covariance kernel of the Gaussian random holomorphic sections is given by the Bergman kernel

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(s(z),s(w))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big(s(z)\otimes s(w)^{*}\big)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}s_{j}(z)\otimes s_{j}(w)^{*}=:\Pi_{n}(z,w).

Under the local coordinate, we write
s_{j}=f_{j}e^{\otimes n}
, then locally

s(z)=\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}b_{j}f_{j}(z)\Big]e^{\otimes n}.

(9)

In most cases, it is more convenient to work with the locally defined Gaussian process

\widetilde{s}(z):=\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}b_{j}f_{j}(z)\Big]e^{-n\phi(z)/2},

(10)

whose covariance kernel is given by

\operatorname{Cov}(\widetilde{s}(z),\widetilde{s}(w))=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}f_{j}(z)\overline{f_{j}(w)}e^{-n\phi(z)/2-n\phi(w)/2}=:\widetilde{F}_{n}(z,w).

(11)

Actually
\widetilde{s}
should be interpreted as the lift of the holomorphic section
s
of the line bundle to its equivariant function on the unit circle bundle induced by
h
, and
\widetilde{F}_{n}(z,w)
is the lift of the Bergman kernel which is the Szegö kernel of level
n
of the circle bundle. We refer to
[
7
, Section 1.2]
for more background on circle bundle, Szegö kernel, etc. By convention, throughout this article we will continue to refer to
\widetilde{F}_{n}(z,w)
as the Bergman kernel.

On diagonal, the Bergman kernel has the following
C^{\infty}
full expansion of the Tian-Yau-Zelditch Theorem
[
10
,
24
,
25
]
,

\widetilde{F}_{n}(z,z)=\frac{n^{m}}{\pi^{m}}(1+a_{1}(z)n^{-1}+a_{2}(z)n^{-2}+\cdots),

(12)

where all terms
a_{j}
are computable and they are polynomials of curvatures, in particular,
a_{1}(z)
is half of the scalar curvature of
\omega
.

For the general setting of the compact
almost complex symplectic manifolds
, under the normal coordinate and preferred frame, Shiffman-Zelditch proved that the Bergman kernel admits the
C^{\infty}
full expansion about the rescaling limit around a point (
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
),

\begin{split}&\widetilde{F}_{n}(z_{0}+\frac{u}{\sqrt{n}},z_{0}+\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}})\\
=&\frac{n^{m}}{\pi^{m}}e^{u\cdot\bar{v}-\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|v|^{2}}\left(1+\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}n^{-r/2}p_{r}(u,v)+n^{-(\ell+1)/2}e_{n\ell}(u,v)\right),\end{split}

(13)

where each
p_{r}(u,v)
is a polynomial,
e_{n\ell}
and its derivatives are bounded on any compact subset in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Actually, given any
\epsilon>0
, for any
\ell\geq 0
, any
|u|,|v|\leq\log^{2}n
and any order of partial derivatives
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
, one has the uniform estimate (see
[
11
]
and also
[
19
, Theorem 4.2.1]
),

\begin{split}&\Big|\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|+|\alpha^{\prime}|}}{\partial^{\alpha}u\partial^{\alpha^{\prime}}\bar{v}}\Big(\frac{\pi^{m}}{n^{m}}\widetilde{F}_{n}(z_{0}+\frac{u}{\sqrt{n}},z_{0}+\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}})\\
&-(1+\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}n^{-r/2}p_{r}(u,v))e^{u\cdot\bar{v}-\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|v|^{2}}\Big)\Big|=O(n^{-(\ell+1-\epsilon)/2}).\end{split}

(14)

Moreover, if we further restrict
u,v
to stay in some compact set of
\mathbb{C}
(independent of
n
), then we can remove the
\epsilon
from the right hand side of (
14
).

As a corollary of (
13
), Shiffman-Zelditch proved that the Bergman kernel and its derivatives exhibit exponential decay as
n
large (
[
22
, Lemma 5.2]
),

\|\widetilde{F}_{n}(z,w)\|_{C^{k}}\leq Cn^{m+\frac{k}{2}}\exp\left(-c{d}_{g}(z,w)\sqrt{n}\right)

(15)

where
c>0
is some uniform constant,
C>0
only depends on
k
, and the norms of the derivates of
\widetilde{F}_{n}(z,w)
is induced by the Riemann metric
g
.

In the special case of polarized compact Kähler manifolds, one has
p_{1}=0
(see, e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 4.1.22, Theorem 4.1.25 and Remark 4.1.26]
and
[
16
, Theorem 2.3]
). In particular, one has,

\begin{split}&\widetilde{F}_{n}(z_{0}+\frac{u}{\sqrt{n}},z_{0}+\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}})=\frac{n^{m}}{\pi^{m}}e^{u\cdot\bar{v}-\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|v|^{2}}\Big(1+n^{-1}\big(\frac{1}{2}s(z_{0})\\
&+\frac{1}{8}R(u,\bar{u},u,\bar{u})+\frac{1}{8}R(v,\bar{v},v,\bar{v})-\frac{1}{4}R(u,\bar{v},u,\bar{v})\big)+n^{-3/2}e_{n3}\Big),\end{split}

(16)

where the curvature tensor
R(s,\bar{t},u,\bar{v})=R_{j\bar{k}p\bar{q}}(z_{0})s_{j}\bar{t}_{k}u_{p}\bar{v}_{q}
. For example, in the case of
(\mathcal{O}(1),h_{FS})\to(\mathbb{CP}^{1},\omega_{FS})
, the Bergman kernel reads,

\widetilde{F}_{n}(z,w)=\frac{n+1}{\pi}\frac{(1+z\bar{w})^{n}}{\sqrt{(1+|z|^{2})^{n}(1+|w|^{2})^{n}}}.

And its rescaling admits the full expansion (
[
16
, equation (33)]
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{n}(\frac{u}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}})=\frac{1}{\pi}e^{u\cdot\bar{v}-\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|v|^{2}}\Big(1+n^{-1}\big(1-\frac{(u\cdot\bar{v})^{2}}{2}+\frac{|u|^{4}+|v|^{4}}{4}\big)+\cdots\Big).

2.3.
Two classical models

At the end of this section, we introduce two classical Gaussian random holomorphic fields that have been extensively studied.

The first model is defined on complex projective space. Recalling the orthonormal basis (
5
), in the affine coordinate, the Gaussian random holomorphic section reads

p(z):=\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{\pi}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{j}\sqrt{{n\choose j}}z^{j}

which is equivalent to the so-called
\operatorname{SU(2)}
random polynomials up to a common factor of magnitude
[
5
,
15
]
.

The second model is defined on the complex plane. It is the Gaussian Entire Function (GEF) introduced in the introduction:

g(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{b_{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}\,z^{j},

(17)

where

\left\{\frac{z^{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}\right\}_{j=0}^{\infty}

forms an orthonormal basis of the Bargmann–Fock space of
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-integrable holomorphic functions

\mathcal{H}\big(\mathbb{C},\pi^{-1}e^{-|z|^{2}}d\ell\big),

and
d\ell
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}
.

3.
Random zeros

From now on, we restrict our attention to the case where
M
is a compact Riemann surface. In this section, we review and derive basic properties of the correlation functions of zeros of Gaussian holomorphic sections over such surfaces.

We recall the Gaussian holomorphic section defined in (
7
) and its local expression (
9
). It was proved in Theorem 1.1 of
[
21
]
that the expected density of its zeros satisfies

\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z_{i}\in Z_{n}}\delta_{z_{i}}\Big)\to\frac{\omega}{\pi},\quad\mbox{as $n\to\infty$}

(18)

in the sense of distribution. In particular, for
\operatorname{SU(2)}
random polynomials, the expected density is given by
{\omega_{FS}}/\pi
, i.e., the uniform measure on
S^{2}
, exactly.

Given
Z_{n}
in (
1
),
we define another point process on
M^{k}:=\underbrace{M\times\cdots\times M}_{k}
by

Z_{n}^{(k)}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{i_{1}},\cdots,z_{i_{k}}\in Z_{n}\\
\textrm{pairwise distinct}\end{subarray}}\,\,\delta_{(z_{i_{1}},\cdots,z_{i_{k}})}.

(19)

Then the
k
-point correlation function
\rho_{k}
of random zeros is defined as,

\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}^{(k)}(B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{k})]=\int_{B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{k}}\rho_{k}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{k})\frac{\omega(z_{1})}{\pi}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{\omega(z_{k})}{\pi},

(20)

where
B_{1},\cdots,B_{k}
are Borel sets in
M
. In particular, when
B_{1}=\cdots=B_{k}=B
, it yields the
k
-th factorial moment of the number of points falling in
B
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{(Z_{n}(B)!)}{(Z_{n}(B)-k)!}\right)=\int_{B^{k}}\rho_{k}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{k})\frac{\omega(z_{1})}{\pi}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{\omega(z_{k})}{\pi}.

(21)

In fact, for any point process on Riemann surfaces, its correlation function, if exists, can always be defined in the same manner as (
20
).

We define the diagonal set of

It’s important to note that the choice of connection is arbitrary in our setting, since we are considering zeros of holomorphic sections, which are independent of the choice of derivative. This can be seen directly from the conditional expectation in formula (

Given a smooth function
f:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and a collection of pairwise distinct points
\underline{z}:=(z_{1},...,z_{k})\notin\Delta^{(k)}
, the divided difference of

is

[f]_{k}\left(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{}}}{{:=}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{f\left(z_{j}\right)}{\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\left(z_{j}-z_{j^{\prime}}\right)}.

By the definition of the divided differences, we have:

\begin{bmatrix}f\left(z_{1}\right)\\
\vdots\\
f\left(z_{k}\right)\end{bmatrix}=T(\underline{z})\begin{bmatrix}{[f]_{1}\left(z_{1}\right)}\\
\vdots\\
{[f]_{k}\left(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\right)}\end{bmatrix},

(31)

where

is a

where an empty product is set to be 1. Thus we have

\operatorname{det}T(\underline{z})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\left(z_{j}-z_{i}\right).

Note that by definition

[f]_{k}(z,..,z)=\frac{f^{(k-1)}(z)}{(k-1)!}.

We refer to
[
3

The next result establishes the splitting property of correlation functions. Given a configuration of distinct points
(z_{1},..,z_{k})\in M^{k}\backslash\Delta^{(k)}
, for two nontrivial subsets
I,J\subset\{1,2,..,k\}
such that
I\cap J=\emptyset
, the distance between
I
and
J

d_{g}(I,J):=\min_{i\in I,j\in J}d_{g}(z_{i},z_{j}).

Given a partition of
\{1,2,..,k\}
, denoted as
\mathcal{P}=\{I_{1},..,I_{\ell}\}
, we define the minimal distance between all clusters (a cluster corresponds to a subset in the partition of
\{1,\ldots,k\}
),

\eta:=\min_{1\leq i\neq j\leq\ell}d_{g}(I_{i},I_{j}).

Given a nontrivial subset
I\subset\{1,2,..,k\}
, we again denoted by
\underline{z}_{I}=\left(z_{i}\right)_{i\in I}
as a cluster of variables. Then we have

Theorem 4
.

Given
(z_{1},...,z_{k})\in M^{k}\backslash\Delta^{(k)}
and a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{I_{1},..,I_{\ell}\}
of
\{1,...,k\}
such that the minimal distance
\eta
between different clusters is at least
\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}
, then the correlation function exhibits the following splitting property,

\rho_{k}(z_{1},..,z_{k})=\prod_{I_{i}\in\mathcal{P}}\rho_{|I_{i}|}\left(\underline{z}_{I_{i}}\right)+O\big(n^{-\infty}\big),

(40)

where

is an error term that decays faster than
n^{-c}
for any
c>0
.

For the case

[

Based on the method of divided differences, (

[

(see also
[

\rho_{k}^{\infty}(z_{1},..,z_{k})=\prod_{I_{i}\in\mathcal{P}}\rho^{\infty}_{|I_{i}|}\left(\underline{z}_{I_{i}}\right)+O\big(e^{-c\delta}\big),

\frac{1}{n^{k\ell}}\rho_{k}(z_{0}+\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},..,z_{0}+\frac{z_{k}}{\sqrt{n}})=\rho^{\infty}_{k,\ell,m}(z_{1},...,z_{k})+O\big({n}^{-1}\big).

(42)

Here,
\rho^{\infty}_{k,\ell,m}(z_{1},...,z_{k})
is a universal function.

However, it is important to note that, unlike the
1
-dimensional case as in Corollary
2
, the error term
O(n^{-1})
only holds on any compact subsets
K\subset(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{(k)}
, where

(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{(k)}:=\{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\in(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{k},z_{i}\neq z_{j},\forall 1\leq i\neq j\leq k\}.

This is because, for higher dimensional cases,
\rho_{k}(z_{1},...,z_{k})
may blow up on diagonal. The same behavior applies to
\frac{1}{n^{k\ell}}\rho_{k}(z_{0}+\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},..,z_{0}+\frac{z_{k}}{\sqrt{n}})
and
\rho^{\infty}_{k,\ell,m}(z_{1},...,z_{k})
.

For example, by recalling the Taylor expansion (
3
) for the limiting 2-point correlation of discrete zeros with
\ell=m
,
one obtains distinct short-distance behaviors depending on the dimension of the complex manifold
(see the discussion following Theorem 1.3 in
[
9
]
). For
m\geq 3
, we have

\rho^{\infty}_{2,m,m}(u,v)\to\infty\,\,\,\mbox{as}\,\,\,u\to v,\,\,

i.e., nearby zeros are attractive for
m\geq 3
, and thus Theorem
3
and Theorem
5
should both fail. For
m=2
, we have

\rho^{\infty}_{2,2,2}(u,v)\to\frac{3}{4}\,\,\,\mbox{as}\,\,\,u\to v,\,\,

i.e., nearby zeros are neutral, and thus Theorem
3
should also fail, but it is quite possible that Theorem
5
holds for
m=2
.

In all cases, we may expect that Theorem
4
always holds, as it is essentially the consequence of the exponential decay of the Bergman kernel.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1

Now we prove Theorem
1
. Fix any
a>0
and set

a_{n}:=n^{-{3}/4}a.

We first prove the following Lemma
1
which provides the limit of the integration of the 2-point correlation function over a small geodesic ball with radius
a_{n}
. This limit determines the intensity of the limiting Poisson point process of the smallest distances between random zeros on Riemann surfaces. Given a point
z\in M
, we denote
B_{a_{n}}(z)
as the geodesic ball centered at
z
with radius
a_{n}
.

Lemma 1
.

The integration of the 2-point correlation function over the small geodesic ball of radius
a_{n}
has the universal limit,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{B_{a_{n}}(z_{0})}\rho_{2}(z_{0},w)\frac{\omega(w)}{\pi}=\frac{a^{4}}{4},\mbox{ uniformly in }z_{0}\in M.

(43)

The proof of Lemma
1
relies on the rescaling estimates in Kähler normal coordinates for correlation functions and the volume form, many of which also appear in the proof of Lemma 3.2 in
[
26
]
.

Let
z_{0}=0
be the origin of the normal coordinate. In what follows, fix a local coordinate chart around
z_{0}
and consider the rescaling map

\phi_{n}:z\mapsto z/\sqrt{n}.

For any differential form
\alpha
on
M
, we write

\alpha(z/\sqrt{n}):=(\phi_{n}^{*}\alpha)_{z}.

In other words,
\alpha(z/\sqrt{n})
denotes the pullback of
\alpha
under the rescaling map
\phi_{n}
in these local coordinates.
Recall (
6
), for sufficiently large
n
, around
z_{0}
, the Kähler form satisfies

\omega(z/\sqrt{n})={n}^{-1}\Big(1+O\left(n^{-{1}}|z|^{2}\right)\Big)d\ell_{z},

(44)

where we denote

d\ell_{z}:=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}.

This implies that the geodesic distance between
z_{0}+z/{\sqrt{n}}
and
z_{0}
satisfies

d_{g}(z_{0},z_{0}+\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}})=\frac{|z|}{\sqrt{n}}+O\big(n^{-{3/2}}|z|^{3}\big).

(45)

By the small geodesic distance estimate (
45
), the volume estimate (
44
), the convergence statement (
25
), Corollary
2
and the Taylor expansion for the 2-point correlation function in (
26
) and (
27
), we have

\begin{split}&\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{w\in B_{a_{n}}(z_{0})}\rho_{2}(z_{0},w)\frac{\omega(w)}{\pi}\\
=&\pi^{-1}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{|w|\leq a_{n}}\rho_{2}(0,w){\omega(w)}\\
=&\pi^{-1}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{|z|\leq n^{1/2}a_{n}}\rho_{2}(0,\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}})\omega\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\\
=&\pi^{-1}\lim_{n\to\infty}n\int_{|z|\leq n^{1/2}a_{n}}[\frac{1}{n^{2}}\rho_{2}(0,\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}})]d\ell_{z}\\
=&\pi^{-1}\lim_{n\to\infty}n\int_{|z|\leq n^{-1/4}a}[\rho_{2}^{\infty}(0,z)+O(n^{-1})]d\ell_{z}\\
=&\pi^{-1}\lim_{n\to\infty}n\int_{|z|\leq n^{-1/4}a}\rho_{2}^{\infty}(0,z)d\ell_{z}\\
=&\pi^{-1}\lim_{n\to\infty}n\int_{|z|\leq n^{-1/4}a}\frac{1}{2}|z|^{2}d\ell_{z}\,\,\\
=&\frac{1}{4}a^{4},\end{split}

(46)

which completes the proof of Lemma
1
.

4.1.
An auxiliary point process

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
in (
2
). Given a measurable subset

and a bounded measurable set

, to prove Theorem

,
it suffices to show that

\mathcal{S}_{n}(A\times U)\Rightarrow\mbox{Poisson}\left(\int_{A}\frac{x^{3}}{2}\mathrm{d}x\int_{U}\frac{\omega}{\pi}\right)\quad\mbox{in distribution}.

(47)

We will give a detailed proof for
A
of the form
[0,a]
where
a>0
. Other forms of

can be treated in the same way. In other words, our goal is to establish that

\mathcal{S}_{n}([0,a]\times U)\Rightarrow\mbox{Poisson}\left(\frac{a^{4}}{8}\int_{U}\frac{\omega}{\pi}\right)\quad\mbox{in distribution}.

(48)

Given the set of random zeros
Z_{n}=\{z_{1},\cdots,z_{n}\}
, we define the following set containing all pairs of zeros whose distances are within

,

\mathcal{I}_{n}:=\Big\{(i,j):\,1\leq i<j\leq n,\,d_{g}(z_{i},z_{j})<a_{n}\Big\}.

(49)

By definition, we have

\left|\mathcal{I}_{n}\right|=\mathcal{S}_{n}([0,a]\times M).

We define a subset of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
as

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{n}:=\Big\{&(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}_{n}:\nexists(k,\ell)\in\mathcal{I}_{n},(k,\ell)\neq(i,j),\\
&d_{g}(\{z_{i},z_{j}\},\{z_{k},z_{\ell}\})\leq 4\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}\Big\},\end{split}

(50)

where we define the distance between two pairs of points as

d_{g}(\{z_{i},z_{j}\},\{z_{k},z_{\ell}\})=\min_{x\in\{z_{i},z_{j}\},y\in\{z_{k},z_{\ell}\}}d_{g}(x,y).

(51)

Recall that
\widehat{z}_{ij}
is a point uniformly chosen from
\{z_{i},z_{j}\}
. We define an auxiliary random process as

\chi_{n}:=\sum_{(i,j)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{n}}\delta_{\widehat{z}_{ij}}.

(52)

We first have the following

Lemma 2
.

As
n\to\infty
, one has the convergence,

\left|\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{n}\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{ }}}{{-}}\left|\mathcal{I}_{n}\right|\to 0\,\,\mbox{ in probability. }

Once Lemma
2
is established, to prove (
48
),
it is sufficient to prove

{\chi}_{n}(U)\Rightarrow\mbox{Poisson}\left(\frac{a^{4}}{8}\int_{U}\frac{\omega}{\pi}\right)\hskip 11.74988pt\mbox{in distribution},

which is implied by

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{{\chi}_{n}(U)!}{({\chi}_{n}(U)-k)!}\Big)=\Big(\frac{a^{4}}{8}\int_{U}\frac{\omega}{\pi}\Big)^{k}\quad\mbox{ for any $k\geq 1$.}

(53)

Let
\widetilde{\rho}_{k}(z_{1},..,z_{k})
denote the
k
-point correlation function of the auxiliary point process
{\chi}_{n}
. By the definition of the point correlation function in (
21
), (
53
) is equivalent to

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{{U}^{k}}\widetilde{\rho}_{k}(z_{1},...,z_{k})\frac{\omega(z_{1})}{\pi}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{\omega(z_{k})}{\pi}=\Big(\frac{a^{4}}{8}\int_{U}\frac{\omega}{\pi}\Big)^{k}.

(54)

The proof of (
54
) will be given in the Section
4.2
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

By the definitions of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{n}

0\leq|\mathcal{I}_{n}|-|\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{n}|\leq|D|,

where
3

3

\displaystyle\int_{B_{a_{n}}(z_{1})}\frac{\omega(w_{1})}{\pi}\cdots\int_{B_{a_{n}}(z_{k})}\frac{\omega(w_{k})}{\pi}\int_{B_{2a_{n}}(z_{1})}\frac{\omega(u_{1})}{\pi}\rho_{2k+1}\left(z_{1},w_{1},\ldots,z_{k},w_{k},u_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Big[\int_{B_{2a_{n}}(z_{1})^{2}}\rho_{3}(z_{1},w_{1},u_{1})\omega(w_{1})\wedge\omega(u_{1})\Big]\Big[\prod_{i=2}^{k}\int_{B_{a_{n}}(z_{i})}\rho_{2}(z_{i},w_{i})\omega(w_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{B_{2a_{n}}(z_{1})^{2}}\rho_{3}(z_{1},w_{1},u_{1})\omega(w_{1})\wedge\omega(u_{1})=O(n^{-3/2}),

where the last estimate follows the same lines of reasoning as in (
55
). There are only
k
terms in the summation, and thus
E_{1}
decays with an order of
O(n^{-3/2})
, which is negligible in the limit.

Step 3.
Finally we consider the error term
E_{2}
in (
59
).
Since
d_{g}(z_{i},z_{j})\geq 4\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}+2a_{n}
for all
i\neq j
, we have

\cup_{i=1}^{k}\left(\cap_{j\neq i}[B_{2\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}+a_{n}}(z_{j})]^{c}\right)=M.

For any
x\in M
, there must exist an
i
such that
x\in\cap_{j\neq i}[B_{2\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}+a_{n}}(z_{j})]^{c}
.
Consequently, for
n
large, we have
D_{4}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=\cup_{i=1}^{k}D_{4,i}
where

D_{4,i}=D_{4}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\cap\{(x,y)|x,y\in\cap_{j\neq i}[B_{2\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}}(z_{j})]^{c}\}.

Here, we used the fact that, if
x\in\cap_{j\neq i}[B_{2\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}+a_{n}}(z_{j})]^{c}
, then for
n
large enough,
y\in\cap_{j\neq i}[B_{\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}}(z_{j})]^{c}
provided that
d_{g}(x,y)<a_{n}
.

Then
E_{2}
can be bounded above by

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\int_{B_{a_{n}}(z_{1})}\frac{\omega(w_{1})}{\pi}\cdots\int_{B_{a_{n}}(z_{k})}\frac{\omega(w_{k})}{\pi}\int_{D_{4,\ell}}\frac{\omega(u_{1})}{\pi}\wedge\frac{\omega(u_{2})}{\pi}

\displaystyle\quad\rho_{2k+2}\left(z_{1},w_{1},\ldots,z_{k},w_{k},u_{1},u_{2}\right).

Without loss of generality, assume
(u_{1},u_{2})\in D_{4,1}
. In this case, the two pairs
(z_{1},w_{1})
,
(u_{1},u_{2})
are considered to be in the same cluster, and
(z_{i},w_{i})
are in the same cluster for
i=2,..,k
, i.e., we have the partition
\mathcal{P}

=

\{\{z_{1},w_{1},u_{1},u_{2}\}
,
\{z_{2},w_{2}\}
,
\dots
,
\{z_{k},w_{k}\}\}
, and the distance between any two clusters is greater than
\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}
.
By (
40
) again, as
n
large enough, we have the asymptotic expansion,

\displaystyle\rho_{2k+2}(z_{1},w_{1}..,z_{k},w_{k},u_{1},u_{2})=\rho_{4}(z_{1},w_{1},u_{1},u_{2})\rho_{2}(z_{2},w_{2})\cdots\rho_{2}(z_{k},w_{k})+O(n^{-\infty}).

As we argued before, the integration of
\prod_{i=2}^{k}\rho_{2}(z_{i},w_{i})
with respect to variables
w_{2},..,w_{k}
is bounded above by some constant. Therefore, the integration of
\rho_{2k+2}
has the upper bound,

\displaystyle C\int_{d_{g}(z_{1},w_{1})<a_{n},(u_{1},u_{2})\in D_{4,1}}\quad\rho_{4}(z_{1},w_{1},u_{1},u_{2})\omega(w_{1})\wedge\omega(u_{1})\wedge\omega(u_{2}).

(63)

We now define

D_{4,1,1}=D_{4,1}\cap\{(x,y)|d_{g}(x,z_{1})<2\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}\}.

And, for
2\leq j\leq k
, let

D_{4,1,j}=D_{4,1}\cap\{(x,y)|d_{g}(x,z_{1})\geq 2\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n},d_{g}(x,z_{j})<5\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}\}.

Then by the definition of
D_{4}
, we have
D_{4,1}=\cup_{j=1}^{k}D_{4,1,j}
and the integration in (
63
) can be bounded above by

C\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{d_{g}(z_{1},w_{1})<a_{n},(u_{1},u_{2})\in D_{4,1,j}}\quad\rho_{4}(z_{1},w_{1},u_{1},u_{2})\omega(w_{1})\wedge\omega(u_{1})\wedge\omega(u_{2}).

By the definition of the correlation function, the term for
j=1
can be bounded above by the expected number of tuples of

(w_{1},u_{1},u_{2})
satisfying the conditions
d_{g}(z_{1},w_{1})<a_{n}
,
d_{g}(u_{1},u_{2})<a_{n}
, and
d_{g}(u_{1},z_{1})<2\log^{4}n/\sqrt{n}
, which is of the order
O(\log^{4}n/n)
, as derived in (
56
).

By the definition of
D_{4,1,j}
for
j\geq 2
,

d_{g}(\{z_{1},w_{1}\},\{u_{1},u_{2}\})\geq\log^{2}n/\sqrt{n}
. And thus
we have the factorization

\rho_{4}(z_{1},w_{1},u_{1},u_{2})=\rho_{2}(z_{1},w_{1})\rho_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})+O(n^{-\infty}).

The integration of
\rho_{2}(z_{1},w_{1})
with respect to
w_{1}
gives rise to a bounded factor as before. To estimate the integration of
\rho_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})
, we choose a normal coordinate around
z_{j}
to get

\displaystyle\int_{(u_{1},u_{2})\in D_{4,1,j}}\rho_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})\omega(u_{1})\wedge\omega(u_{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{|u_{1}-u_{2}|<n^{1/2}a_{n},|u_{1}|<5\log^{2}n}\quad\rho_{2}(\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{u_{2}}{\sqrt{n}})\omega(\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{n}})\wedge\omega(\frac{u_{2}}{\sqrt{n}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{|u_{1}-u_{2}|<n^{1/2}a_{n},|u_{1}|<5\log^{2}n}|u_{1}-u_{2}|^{2}d\ell_{u_{1}}\wedge d\ell_{u_{2}}=O(\log^{4}n/n).

Since
D_{4}=\cup_{1\leq i,j\leq k}D_{4,i,j}
which consists of
k^{2}
sets, and the integration on each
D_{4,i,j}
decays with order
O(\log^{4}n/n)
, we can conclude that the third term on the right hand side of (
60
) also tends to 0 in the limit, and thus we complete the proof of Lemma
3
.
