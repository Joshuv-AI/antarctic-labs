Abdominal aortic aneurysm and renal cell carcinoma in the presence of crossed fused renal ectopia
Authors: Chloé Moran, Zeeshan Kazmi, Stephen Ball
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAbdominal aortic aneurysms, renal cell carcinomas and crossed fused renal ectopias are significant conditions within the fields of vascular and urological surgery. The simultaneous occurrence of all three is exceptionally rare; to date, there have been no reports of them occurring concurrently. This case, however, describes what we believe to be the first documented case of all three conditions presenting simultaneously in a single patient. The management of this rare case involved a combined surgical approach, including open aneurysm repair and partial ectopic nephrectomy.Keywords: Vascular surgery; Urological surgery; Surgical Procedure, Operative; Cancer intervention; Urological cancer

Background

Abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) and renal cell carcinomas (RCCs) are relatively common conditions in adult populations, often discovered incidentally through routine imaging studies.1 2 An AAA is the dilation of the abdominal aorta.3 It is more common in men and has an incidence rate of 55 per 100 000 in those aged 65–74 years, 112 per 100 000 in those aged 75–84 years and 298 per 100 000 in those aged 85 years and over.4 AAAs can be classified into origin, histology, clinicopathologic manifestation or anatomical site.5 Classification into anatomical site includes suprarenal, pararenal/juxtarenal and infrarenal.5 These are important to consider as aneurysm location may vary in presentation and subsequent management.5

Screening for an AAA is done by an abdominal ultrasound scan, and the size of the aneurysm is categorised into the following:

In England and Wales, approximately 4000 people die from a ruptured AAA each year.4 6 The death rate in men is around 1.5 times higher than in women, and the mortality of ruptured AAA in men aged 65 years and over is 0.6%.7 Men in this age category are invited for a one-off aneurysm screening.8 Patients with an AAA undergo elective repair when it reaches the treatment threshold of either 5.5 cm or shows rapid growth (>1 cm/year).9 This can be achieved by either open surgical repair or endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR). The decision regarding the type of repair depends on the patient’s anatomy, risk factors and preference.10

RCCs are the most common malignant tumour affecting the kidney, accounting for up to 90% of renal malignancies.11 They represent approximately 3% of all malignant diseases in adults and rank as the seventh most common cancer among men globally.11 A proportion of patients (37%–61%) are diagnosed by chance through abdominal imaging, including ultrasound and CT scans.12 Notably, around 70% of individuals diagnosed with RCC are identified at stage I.12

Variations in renal anatomy account for up to 50% of congenital abnormalities, including the number of kidneys (agenesis or supernumerary), location, shape and vasculature.13 A rarer variation is crossed renal ectopia (CRE), where both kidneys are fused on the same side, often with additional vascular variants.14 The cause of renal ectopia is poorly understood. However, it has been hypothesised to be related to abnormal ureteric bud development in the early embryo.15 There are four documented types of CRE:

Case presentation/investigations/imaging

A male patient in his late 60s was incidentally found to have an AAA on routine screening. His medical history included a myocardial infarction, for which he underwent primary coronary intervention with stenting. Despite this, he remained fit and active. Physical examination revealed a soft, non-tender abdomen with bilateral palpable femoral pulses.

CT angiography demonstrated a 62 mm juxtarenal AAA not involving the iliac arteries. Additionally, two further incidental findings were noted. First, the patient had a left-sided CFRE, characterised by the absence of a right kidney and the presence of two kidneys on the left side, each supplied by its own artery, vein and ureter. Second, the fused kidney exhibited a 23×24 mm mass on its anterior lower pole, raising suspicion for malignancy (figure 3) .

(A, B and C) CT aorta angiogram in the coronal (A) and axial (B and C) planes showing CFRE (A). The accessory renal artery arises from the anterior aspect of the aneurysmal component of the aorta (B), and a 23×24 mm mass arises from the inferior moiety of the CFRE (C), medial to which the AAA is visualised.

The AAA was not suitable for EVAR, and the vascular multidisciplinary team (MDT) recommended open repair. The patient was also discussed at the urology MDT meeting; the renal mass was concerning for RCC, potentially requiring future excision. To minimise the risks associated with multiple procedures and anaesthesia, a combined surgical approach involving both vascular surgery and urology was decided. This consisted of an open AAA repair (tube graft) and partial left nephrectomy.

A standard approach to open AAA repair was adopted with good knowledge of the aberrant anatomy. The accessory renal vein, draining the lower kidney, crossed anteriorly to the aneurysm . The accessory renal artery (supplying the lower kidney) originated from the anterior wall of the aneurysm (figures 4 and 5). The accessory renal vein was dissected free and slung to allow retraction cranially. The aneurysm neck was prepared for clamping inferior to the left renal artery, as were the common iliac arteries. The accessory renal artery was identified, dissected free and slung (figure 6). Following administration of intravenous heparin, the aorta and iliacs were clamped, as was the accessory renal artery.

The accessory renal vein was retracted cranially to facilitate access to the aneurysm sac. The aneurysm was repaired using an 18 mm Dacron tube graft. A hole was created in the aneurysm sac to facilitate tunnelling of the accessory renal artery, which was re-implanted onto the tube graft with an end-to-side anastomosis. The renal tissue was deemed viable, and a partial nephrectomy was performed as planned.

Histological analysis revealed a well-circumscribed clear cell RCC. This was classified as WHO/International Society of Urological Pathology Grade 3. There was no evidence of necrosis, rhabdoid or sarcomatoid change and no lymphovascular invasion. The renal capsule remained intact, and the tumour was confined to the kidney. It was close to the resection margin (<1 mm) but did not extend to the inked margin in the sections examined. There was no invasion of perinephric fat.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient had normal renal function pre-operatively, which remained unaffected post-operatively. He was discharged 1 week after an uneventful postoperative course and was followed up in both vascular and urology surgical clinics with no further concerns.

Discussion

The phenomenon of AAAs and CFRE occurring together has only been documented a few times.18–25 In most of these cases, an EVAR option was preferred. One 1977 case report described an open repair of an AAA in the presence of type A CRE, both of which were picked up in imaging for uncontrolled hypertension.24 The patient had both kidneys present on the left side with the accessory renal artery arising from the aneurysm. A straight tube graft was used for the repair; it is unclear how the accessory renal artery was managed during the operation. However, follow-up scans demonstrated intact renal anatomy.24 Of note, a ureteric stent was used to locate and protect the accessory ureter during the procedure. A second 1989 case report describes open repair of a concurrent right-sided aortoiliac aneurysm and left common iliac artery aneurysm in the presence of left-sided CFRE.25 The left renal artery originated from its usual location; the accessory renal artery arose from the aneurysmal component of the right common iliac artery and crossed the midline to supply the ectopic kidney. A bifurcated graft was used for the aneurysm repair, and the accessory renal artery was reimplanted into the right limb of the graft. The accessory kidney had a total ischaemic time of 45 min, during which cold Ringer’s solution was used to preserve renal tissue.25

The presence of RCC in CFRE is an equally rare occurrence, with similarly few documented cases.26–29 Surgical removal of the tumour appears to be the preferred method of treatment. This tends to involve either partial nephrectomy or removal of the whole ectopic kidney, with an aim to preserve the normal kidney. One 2019 case report described the presence of a 6×4×3.5 cm RCC in a CFRE incidentally in an asymptomatic 39-year-old individual.27 In this case, a partial nephrectomy of the ectopic kidney was performed. The only complication was a postoperative urinary leak, which self-resolved. The operation was otherwise a success, and follow-up at 6 months demonstrated no recurrence of disease.27 Another 2018 case report described a much larger and more symptomatic presentation of RCC in CFRE. The 57-year-old patient had a 7.5×8.5 cm RCC, which caused anorexia, abdominal discomfort, haematuria and weight loss.28 This patient also underwent partial nephrectomy and similarly had a postoperative urinary leak, which was managed with ureteral catheter placement.28

The occurrence of an AAA and RCC together is also a rare finding, with relatively few documented cases.30–32 Simultaneous aneurysm repair and RCC removal appears to be the preferred method of management. One 2006 paper describes 10 cases of simultaneous RCC and AAA managed through the combined procedure of aneurysmectomy and nephrectomy.30 In all 10 cases, the aneurysm repair was performed first, followed by nephrectomy—similar to our case described above. In these 10 cases, there was no postoperative mortality and only one postoperative complication, pneumonia, in one patient. A median follow-up of 28 months demonstrated no disease recurrence. Similarly, a 2020 case report describes concurrent AAA and RCC managed by open aneurysm repair and radical nephrectomy.31 The patient underwent a CT scan for a pulsatile abdominal mass and was found to have an incidental 69×56 mm mass in the left kidney. Open AAA repair was conducted, followed by complete nephrectomy of the affected kidney. There were no recorded postoperative complications.31

There are no documented cases of AAA and RCC in the background of CFRE. The closest documented case is that of AAA and RCC in a patient with a horseshoe kidney described in a case report from 2000.33 The patient underwent open AAA repair and had a complete left-sided nephrectomy with division of the isthmus.33 The uniqueness of our case, therefore, contributes significantly to the limited body of literature on managing these conditions simultaneously. With the increasing use of cross-sectional imaging, more incidental findings of CFRE may come to light. This case, therefore, brings about awareness and guidance in this context.

Learning points

With increasing use of cross-sectional imaging, unusual triads of pathology may be encountered more often than expected. This case provides guidance on managing abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) in the setting of crossed fused renal ectopia, whereby the current literature remains limited.
