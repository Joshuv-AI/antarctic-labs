On the Generation and Mitigation of Harmful Geometry in Image-to-3D Models
Yule Liu Yilong Yang Jiale Teng Hanze Jia Zeren Luo Jingyi Zheng Zifan Peng

Abstract
Recent advances in image-to-3D models have significantly improved the fidelity and accessibility of 3D content creation. Such a powerful reconstruction capability that enables creative design can also be misused by the adversaries to generate harmful geometries, which can be further fabricated via 3D printers and pose real-world risks. However, such risks are largely underexplored: it remains unclear how well current image-to-3D models can produce these harmful geometries, and whether existing safeguards can reliably prevent such generation. To fill this gap, we conduct a systematic measurement study of harmful geometry generation and mitigation. We first describe this risk through three kinds of unsafe categories: direct-use physical hazards, risky templates or components, and deceptive replicas. Each category is instantiated with representative objects. We evaluate both open-source and commercial image-to-3D models under original, degraded, viewpoint-shifted, and semantically camouflaged inputs. We consider different evaluation metrics, including geometric validity, multi-view VLM-based semantic scoring, targeted human validation, and controlled physical fabrication. The results reveal a concerning reality that current image-to-3D models can effectively reconstruct the harmful geometries, while fewer than 0.3% of such geometries trigger commercial moderation flags. Notably, harmful 3D structures can still be clearly generated even when the input images are taken from non-standard viewpoints, corrupted with noise, or intentionally obscured. As a first step toward mitigation, we evaluate three representative safeguard families, including input moderation, model-level benign alignment, and output-level filtering. We find that existing safeguards have distinct weaknesses: off-the-shelf input moderators achieve only low recall, while the others improve recall but incur over 30% false positives on ambiguous benign/harmful pairs. We further develop a stacked defense that can reduce harmful retention to <1%, but still at 11% overall false-positive cost. Taken together, our findings demonstrate the risk in the current system and encourage better geometry-aware safeguards for moderation.

Threat Model.

To better align with the real-world scenario, we consider an adversary who can only query current image-to-3D models and use standard downstream fabrication steps, but does not possess professional CAD expertise.
Depending on the adversary’s goal, the resulting risk may manifest as a direct-use physical hazard, a geometric reference or component that lowers the cost of downstream replication, or a visually deceptive replica (formalized in
Section

4

Building on this threat model, we perform risk evaluation and mitigation as follows.

Risk Evaluation.

We frame risk evaluation as a measurement pipeline.
To construct the dataset, we instantiate unsafe categories with representative object families, i.e., kinetic weapons, security-sensitive structural components, and visually realistic weapon-like replicas.
These families are grounded in legal and regulatory frameworks across major jurisdictions (e.g., the United States, the European Union, and China).

For data collection, we define explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria that restrict the scope to externally visible, image-recoverable, and policy-relevant geometry-mediated risks.
These criteria allow geometry-retention metrics to capture risk-relevant features within this scope, since the selected objects’ risk-relevant functional cues (e.g., blade edges, barrel hollows) are inherently expressed in external geometry.
For each risk category, we follow a funnel-based collection process: starting from high-quality prototype images and expanding them with structured perturbations, including viewpoint shifts, image degradation, and semantic camouflage.
This design allows us to measure whether risk-relevant geometry is preserved when inputs become visually non-canonical.

For behavioral measurement, we use two complementary sets of signals (see
Section

6
for more details):
(i) geometric validity, which captures whether the generated mesh is structurally usable, and
(ii) semantic geometry-retention signals, including harmful-cue preservation, functional-cue preservation, and visual fidelity assessed through multi-view VLM-based scoring.
To reduce reliance on any single proxy, we cross-check these automated signals with targeted human validation (240 samples, 5 annotators), finding that VLM scores are broadly consistent with human judgments and preserve the relative ranking of outputs.
We further verify scorer sensitivity using controlled visible-fault edits, where both VLM and human scores decrease as risk-relevant cues are weakened.

In total, we evaluate 6 image-to-3D models (open-source and commercial) on 114 seed images expanded to 1,057 perturbed variants, and conduct controlled physical fabrication on a curated subset.
On our 1–5 ordinal rubric for harmful-cue preservation, current systems score 3.12–3.66 on prototype inputs, indicating that risk-relevant geometry and proportions are consistently recovered with only fine-grained interfaces degraded.
They often remain at similarly elevated levels even under non-canonical inputs, yet only 3 requests (0.28%) in our commercial evaluation trigger moderation flags.
We further conduct physical fabrication experiments on a highest-scored subset spanning three risk categories, which are drawn from the VAST-3D outputs.
We evaluate properties such as dimensional consistency and structural integrity to assess functionality.

Risk Mitigation.

To understand whether existing safeguards can reliably prevent such generation, we consider three representative safeguard families: input moderation, model-level benign alignment, and output-level geometric filtering.
Our evaluation shows that each stage is individually insufficient.
For input moderation, off-the-shelf visual moderators miss functionally hazardous but visually benign inputs (0–27.3% recall), exposing a fundamental semantic–geometric mismatch in 2D moderation.
Although fine-tuning an image foundation model raises F1 to 0.887, it still incurs 7.4% false positives on hard negatives, suggesting that ambiguous benign/harmful pairs cannot be reliably separated from 2D appearance alone.
For model-level benign alignment, suppression improves in some settings but does not transfer reliably across 3D representations and introduces measurable benign-utility trade-offs, indicating that alignment is entangled with model-specific geometric priors.
For output-level geometric filtering, moderate-to-strong F1 of 0.7–0.8 is achievable, but hard negatives remain challenging.
A stacked combination of these safeguards reduces measured harmful-geometry retention to
<
1% on our benchmark, at an 11% overall false-positive cost.
Overall, these results suggest that effective mitigation requires defenses that are geometry-aware, representation-aware, and integrated across the pipeline, rather than stronger classifiers at any single stage.

Notably, our study focuses on safety measurement and safeguards benchmarking, and no printable assets will be released.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We present a systematic measurement of safety risks in modern image-to-3D models, focusing on risk-relevant object categories grounded in existing legal frameworks.

•

We evaluate model behavior across diverse input conditions and show that current models often preserve harmful cues even under perturbed inputs.
Even commercial API services largely overlook this risk, with near-zero moderation observed in our evaluation.

•

We evaluate three representative safeguard families and find that no single defense is sufficient; even stacked defenses incur a non-trivial usability cost.

Related Work

3D Representation.

To enable the learning and representation of 3D geometries, a variety of methods have been proposed, each tailored to different application contexts.
Unstructured 3D representations include mesh, voxel, point clouds, Signed Distance Fields (SDF), Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)
[
23
]
, and Gaussian Splatting
[
18
]
, each of which plays a distinct role in 3D modeling and reconstruction tasks.
Importantly, these representations are geometry-grounded, which means they can be interconverted through algorithms.

Recently, the focus of 3D geometry representation has shifted toward developing compact latent formulations.
These approaches compress geometric information into highly lightweight forms, which enables efficient integration with diffusion models
[
14
,
8
]
.
Amid extensive exploration and methodological competition, two dominant paradigms have emerged in the field of 3D generation:
(1)VecSet
[
46
,
3
]
, which encodes 3D shapes as sets of 1D latent vectors;
(2) Sparse voxel
[
44
,
34
]
, which embed 3D assets into features anchored to sparsely distributed voxels.

3D Generation.

In the domain of image-to-3D generation, early approaches explored a range of architectures, including reconstruction-based methods
[
15
,
37
]
and autoregressive models
[
35
,
42
]
.
However, these methods often struggle to reconstruct high-fidelity geometry in regions unobserved in the input image, and potential inconsistencies across multiple views may further degrade generation quality.
More recently, the field has shifted toward developing 3D-native diffusion models, which typically consist of two core components: a geometry encoder and a flow-matching transformer.
While conditioning the flow-matching transformer
[
8
]
on input images has become standard practice, the choice of geometry encoder, as discussed above, remains a source of performance variation.
Building upon the VecSet encoder, several works, including Michelangelo
[
49
]
, CLAY
[
47
]
, Hunyuan3D-2
[
17
]
, and TripoSG
[
19
]
, have demonstrated impressive results in high-quality 3D synthesis.
Concurrently, another promising direction leverages the sparse voxel encoder, with representative models such as Trellis series
[
44
,
43
]
and Spar3D
[
20
]
achieving competitive performance.
The high fidelity of these models is now enabling real-world deployment in industrial applications, such as Vast AI and Hunyuan3D.

Despite these advances in generation quality, security implications, particularly in image-to-3D generation, have remained largely unexamined.
Notably, such models can directly convert 2D images into printable 3D objects, raising novel safety concerns.
To date, the only related study
[
38
]
investigates risks in text-to-3D models by adapting harmful prompts from text-to-image systems
[
32
]
.
In this work, we identify and expose a distinct and critical risk tied to physical harm, which arises specifically in the context of image-conditioned 3D generation.

3D Classification.

To capture the geometric characteristics of 3D objects, current approaches primarily operate on either point cloud or mesh representations.
For point-cloud-based methods, models such as PointNet
[
31
]
and its successors
[
21
]
directly process unordered point sets using permutation-invariant neural networks to produce classification outputs.
For mesh-based methods, the 3D shape is represented as a graph where vertices and edges encode surface connectivity.
Graph neural networks, such as MeshCNN
[
13
]
and follow-up architectures
[
29
]
, leverage this structure to learn both local geometric features and global topological patterns.
In this work, we adapt and evaluate both paradigms for the task of harmful geometry moderation, training classifiers to distinguish between benign and regulated or dangerous 3D objects.

Threat Model

Since online regulated 3D assets and consumer-grade fabrication have long raised physical safety concerns
[
27
,
6
,
1
,
45
]
, we study whether current image-to-3D models can recover misuse-relevant geometry from images, and whether existing safeguards can reliably prevent such recovery.
Our goal is to establish the existence of this risk in current systems, rather than to claim that every generated output is immediately functional as an end-use artifact.

Adversary’s Capability.

We consider an adversary who does not possess professional CAD skills and does not manually reconstruct complex 3D geometry from scratch.
Instead, the adversary uses an image-to-3D model as the main source of geometric inference from one or a few reference images.
We assume access only to commodity post-processing and fabrication tools commonly available to ordinary users, such as object rescaling from a known reference, automatic mesh repair by slicing software, and consumer-grade 3D printing with standard materials (e.g., PLA).
We do not assume specialized manufacturing expertise, high-end equipment, or substantial manual redesign after generation.

Adversary’s Goal.

The adversary’s goal is not necessarily to obtain a fully functional end-use object directly from the model, but to recover geometry that is sufficiently accurate to enable downstream physical misuse.
We therefore distinguish three
unsafe outcomes
:

•

Direct-use physical hazards:
geometries whose harmfulness is primarily encoded in visible shape and structural form, such as impact-bearing surfaces or sharp tips.
These objects are security-relevant
as printed
: even when fabricated using consumer-grade materials, they may still present a direct risk of bodily harm.

•

Risky templates or components:
geometries that may not be immediately operational in a consumer-grade print, but preserve sufficient spatial references for downstream duplication, fitting, or refinement.
These objects are security-relevant because they lower the barrier to misuse by recovering interface-critical geometry, even when full functionality requires stronger materials or downstream re-creation in a different material.

•

Deceptive replicas:
geometries whose main risk lies in visual realism rather than complete mechanical functionality.
These outputs can resemble regulated or threatening objects closely enough to support intimidation or deception, even when hidden internal mechanisms are absent.

Relationship to Existing 3D Platforms.

CAD/STL repositories (e.g., Thingiverse, Cults, MakerWorld) already host weapon-related geometries, but their coverage is coarse.
We manually searched each of our 83 seed objects across the three platforms.
At the category level (i.e., any broadly similar item exists), 85.54% find a match.
At the instance level (i.e., a geometrically precise match to the specific seed object), the rate drops to 42.17%.
Public repositories concentrate on standardized shapes; highly customized variants, such as exact key bittings,
niche firearm accessories, or ATM skimmers, are largely absent.
Some platforms further restrict availability: Cults, for instance, blocks search queries for specific firearm brands such as Glock and Sig Sauer, though other brands remain accessible.
Image-to-3D bypasses both limitations by generating the exact geometry depicted in an arbitrary reference photograph, without depending on community uploads or platform coverage.

Evaluation Scope

We select representative object families through a two-step process.
First, we restrict scope to objects whose security relevance is primarily carried by external geometry and that are plausibly recoverable and fabricable under consumer-grade workflows.
Second, we reviewed recurring legal treatment across major jurisdictions to identify object families that are consistently subject to restriction rather than merely controversial.
This process yields three families below; it is intended as principled scoping rather than an exhaustive legal survey.

Kinetic Weapons.

This family instantiates
direct-use physical hazards
: external topology (e.g., sharp or impact-bearing geometry) that remains harmful even when printed with consumer-grade materials.
Knuckle dusters, for instance, face near-universal regulation.
They are classified as controlled implements in China
[
36
]
, banned weapons in Germany
[
10
]
, and prohibited in multiple U.S. states
[
11
]
.
We collect two representative cases: knuckle dusters and tactical spikes, and assess whether generated geometry preserves sharp, reinforced, or impact-bearing structural features.
In our evaluation, we assess whether the generated geometry exhibits sharp, reinforced, or otherwise unsafe structural features characteristic of kinetic weapons.

Structural Components.

This family instantiates
risky templates or components
: fit-critical or interface-critical geometry that enables downstream duplication or refinement.
Multiple jurisdictions treat regulated structural parts equivalently to complete weapons—e.g., EU Directive 2021/555 classifies firearm receivers under the same regime as complete firearms
[
9
]
, and 18 U.S.C. §1029 criminalizes hardware designed to facilitate access-device fraud
[
41
]
.
We collect three sub-classes: firearm structural parts (e.g., receivers), intrusion tools (e.g., bump keys), and fraud-related components (e.g., skimmer faceplates).
We assess whether generated geometry preserves interface-critical dimensions and spatial references, such as receiver cavity profiles, key bitting patterns, and card-slot clearances, that could serve as functional templates for downstream replication.

Visual Threats.

This family instantiates
deceptive replicas
: visually realistic geometry that supports intimidation or deception.
The UK Violent Crime Reduction Act prohibits manufacture of Realistic Imitation Firearms
[
28
]
; U.S. federal law requires imitation firearms to carry a blaze orange tip
[
7
]
; China bans replicas matching the color and contour of military firearms
[
24
]
.
We collect three cases: real firearms, toy guns, and sci-fi guns—the latter two to test whether models suppress toy-like appearance and default to generating visually realistic non-compliant replicas.
Our evaluation specifically examines whether the model defaults to generating visually realistic non-compliant weapon-like replicas.

To ensure that our dataset is both representative and diverse, we adopt a funnel-based collection strategy.
We begin by curating a seed set of 114 high-quality prototype images for each risk category.
Each seed is then expanded into 15 distinct variants using a suite of augmentation techniques—including 456 viewpoint shifting variants, 405 image quality degradation variants(e.g., blur, compression artifacts, or low resolution), and 82 semantic camouflaging variants (e.g., stylization, sketch-to-photo translation, or color modification).
This process yields a final dataset of 1,057 examples, improving coverage across a wide range of real-world input conditions that image-to-3D models may encounter (see

Each prototype is carefully chosen to reflect canonical or commonly encountered visual patterns of the target harmful object, such as brass knuckles, firearm frames, or bump keys.
Specifically, they are sourced from three distinct image types in a 4:3:3 ratio:

•

Real-world Images

•

Illustrative Images

Sourced from 3D printing communities (e.g., Thingiverse concept sketches) or created from scratch, these include abstract drawings, CAD renderings, or stylized illustrations.

View-shifting Augmentation.

To simulate partial observation scenarios where only a limited perspective of the object is available, we leverage the state-of-the-art image generation model Nano Banana
3
3

3

https://gemini.google.com
to synthesize four viewpoints for each prototype:

•

Top-viewpoint
: A direct top-down (flat-lay) view, with the camera oriented vertically above the object.

•

Side-viewpoint
: A side profile (edge) view, obtained by rotating the object 90 degrees to reveal only its thickness or silhouette, intentionally occluding internal features such as holes or cavities.

•

Iso-viewpoint
: A 45-degree isometric view that simultaneously exposes the top surface and one side, providing clear depth cues and geometric context.

•

Bottom-viewpoint
: A bottom-up view, capturing the object from underneath, which is an uncommon but plausible angle.

For each prototype, we craft four variants.
This augmentation strategy explicitly tests the model’s ability to reason about and reconstruct full 3D geometry from sparse or incomplete visual cues, thereby evaluating its capacity for 3D imagination under partial observability.

Degradation Augmentation.

This augmentation evaluates the model’s ability to generate coherent and complete 3D geometry from low-quality or incomplete visual inputs.
Specifically, we assess whether the model can reconstruct harmful objects in real-world adversarial conditions, where critical visual details are degraded or obscured.
We apply three types of realistic degradations to each prototype image:

•

Gaussian Blur
: Simulates out-of-focus snapshots, mimicking casual or surreptitious photography.

•

Random Noise
: Emulates low-light conditions (e.g., grainy surveillance footage), where signal-to-noise ratio is poor.

•

Random Masking
: Represents deliberate obfuscation, where key portions of the object are explicitly hidden.

For each prototype, we craft nine variants, using the degradation.
Our evaluation focuses on whether the model’s generative priors enable the recovery of regulated or dangerous structures.

Virtual Evaluation

Our evaluation is designed to measure harmful geometry retention in generated 3D assets.
We aim to measure whether image-to-3D models preserve visible geometric cues that are relevant to downstream misuse, and how this preservation changes across models and input perturbations.

Evaluation Metrics

Regarding evaluation metrics, we use a multi-layer measurement protocol.
First, we measure low-level generation quality and mesh validity.
Second, following previous work
[
43
,
44
]
, we use an anchored ordinal rubric to assess visible semantic and geometric cues from multi-view renderings.
Third, we validate the semantic rubric through human annotation, geometry-level fault sensitivity, and selected physical fabrication studies.

Quantitative Metric

To objectively assess the foundational quality of the generated meshes, we employ two metrics:

•

DINO Score
[
43
,
17
]
:
To measure visual consistency between the generated 3D asset and the reference image, we use DINOv2-Large
[
25
]
, which provides robust dense visual representations.
We render each generated geometry from 18 viewpoints and compute the cosine similarity between the embedding of the input reference image
I_{ref}
and each rendered view
I_{render}^{v}
.
To account for pose ambiguity, we report the maximum similarity across views:

S_{visual}=\max_{v}\cos(E(I_{ref}),E(I_{render}^{v}))
,
where
E(\cdot)
denotes the DINO embedding function.

•

Geometry Integrity
:
To assess whether the generated asset is structurally usable as a mesh, we use the
Trimesh
library
[
39
]
to detect common geometric defects.
We report
Watertightness
, i.e., the percentage of meshes that form a closed volume without holes, and
Non-Manifold Edges
, i.e., the average number of edges shared by more than two faces.

Semantic Geometry Retention

The quantitative metrics above capture low-level visual consistency and mesh validity, but they do not directly characterize higher-level properties such as category fidelity, misuse-relevant shape cues, or apparent harmful-cue preservation.
We therefore use two state-of-the-art VLMs (Gemini-3-Flash and Qwen-3-VL) as a scalable semantic scoring proxy.
They provide an ordinal signal for understanding whether generated geometries preserve harmful and functional cues.

Rubric Description.

For each generated object, we construct a
2\times 3
diagnostic collage consisting of:
(1) the reference image,
(2) the best-matching RGB render,
(3) the best-matching normal map
5
5

5

Normal mapping is a texture-based visualization of local surface orientation and is used here to expose geometric detail independently of texture.
, and
(4–6) normal maps from three additional rotated views (
+90^{\circ},+180^{\circ},+270^{\circ}
).
An example is shown in
Figure

2
.
This multi-view collage exposes appearance similarity and view-consistent geometric structure.
The VLM assigns a
5-level ordinal rubric
on three dimensions (The detailed prompt and scoring rubric are provided in
Section

C.1
):

•

Visual Fidelity
:
The degree to which the generated asset preserves the visible characteristics of the reference image.
A higher score indicates stronger visual and shape alignment.
For augmented inputs, we instead report
Reference Alignment
, which evaluates the generated geometry against the original clean prototype rather than the degraded or altered input.

•

Functional-cue Preservation
:
The degree to which the generated geometry preserves visible cues related to handling, interfacing, fitting, or downstream refinement.
Examples include contact surfaces, grip regions, holes, slots, tips, or interface boundaries.

•

Harmful-cue Preservation
:
The degree to which the generated geometry preserves visible cues associated with the risk mechanism of the corresponding category, such as impact-bearing surfaces, sharp tips, interface-critical templates, or realistic replica geometry.
This dimension measures harmful-cue preservation.

For ordinal semantic scores, we use the mean across the two VLMs as the central estimate.
For harmful-cue preservation, we report both the mean score across the two VLMs and a conservative aggregation using the maximum score across them.
This aggregation is intended to reduce false negatives.

Validation of Semantic Proxy

As a scalable semantic proxy, we calibrate the VLM score with a targeted human validation study on 240 generated samples from two representative models (Trellis-1 and VAST-3D), each annotated by five raters on Functional-cue Preservation and Harmful-cue Preservation using a 5-point scale.

Human Calibration.

The human study evaluates whether the ordinal rubric produces rankings broadly consistent with human assessment of visible geometry cues.
First, we show that the inter-annotator agreement is substantial overall.
For harmful-cue preservation on the Trellis-1 subset, the intraclass correlation reaches ICC(2,k)=0.67, indicating good reliability
[
4
]
.
On VAST-3D, ICC values are more moderate (0.31-0.45), which we attribute in part to reduced variance among higher-quality outputs (e.g., functional-cue preservation Std = 0.64).

VLM Sanity Check.

To examine the sensitivity of the semantic scorer, we introduce controlled visible faults that progressively weaken category-relevant cues while preserving the overall object context (see
Section

C.2
for details).
This test evaluates whether the scorer responds to the major visible defects emphasized by our rubric: a useful semantic geometry evaluator should assign lower scores as these cues become less visible.
We observe that both VLM scores and human judgments decrease consistently as fault severity increases.
For example, the harmful-cue preservation score decreases from 4.68 to 4.37 and then to 3.19 as the visible faults become more severe.
A similar trend is observed for functional-cue preservation, which drops from 4.56 to 4.31 and then to 3.19.

Case Study: Physical Fabrication

In this section, we use physical fabrication as a bounded case study to ground our virtual geometry-retention metrics in observable physical properties.
We select 10 objects from the highest-scoring VAST-3D outputs, stratified across all three risk categories, ensuring coverage of distinct mechanism-specific properties (fit, dimensional consistency, structural integrity, and visual realism).

Fabrication Setup.

To match the adversary capability defined in
Section

3
, we restrict fabrication to commodity hardware and materials accessible without specialized expertise or procurement.
Specifically, we use the Bambu Lab X1C with standard PLA filament—a setup representative of the consumer-grade 3D printing workflow available for under $1,000 in total equipment cost, requiring no prior manufacturing experience beyond default slicer settings (Bambu Studio).

Since current image-to-3D models output unit meshes (i.e., the geometry has relative proportions but lacks absolute physical scale), we use a known dimension of the target object (e.g., the standard width of a keyway) to establish a scaling factor.
Then, in the slicing software, the mesh is uniformly scaled until the reference feature matches the real-world metric.
This step requires no CAD modeling expertise, only a caliper or a ruler, yet it effectively recovers the functional dimensions of the generated artifact.
In our experiments, we applied this method to all printed objects, ensuring they matched the target physical specifications.

Functionality Evaluation.

The printed objects exhibit high geometric fidelity (see
Appendix

D
).
We further evaluate real-world usability through controlled physical tests (see
Figure

7
).
The printed knuckles and spikes fit adult hands comfortably.
When struck against corrugated cardboard, they produce clean punctures and structural deformation.
While PLA is lighter than metal, the sharpened geometry demonstrates a clear capacity for bodily harm as an improvised weapon.

For the printed keys, the PLA TSA key inserts fully into a standard TSA residential lock cylinder, validating the accuracy of the generated profile.
However, we observed that standard PLA lacks the shear strength to manipulate pins under spring tension (the bit twisted upon torque application).
Consequently, we characterize this threat not as a direct tool, but as a functional template.
Using the printed PLA key as a physical guide, we successfully commissioned a local locksmith to cut a metal duplicate.
The metal copy opened the target lock on the first attempt.
This case study demonstrates that, for at least one restricted-key example, the generated model can serve as a physical template for restricted keys.

Direct functional testing of the auto sear and card skimmer was ethically and legally restricted.
For the card skimmer, the printed housing accepts a standard-thickness card through its slot and provides sufficient internal clearance to seat a compact magnetic read-head module, confirming that the generated geometry serves as a viable enclosure for skimming hardware.
For the auto sear, dimensional measurements confirm that critical interfaces match operational specifications within functional tolerance (
Table

6
).
While PLA lacks the shear strength for sustained cycling, the printed geometry can serve as a dimensionally accurate template for metal duplication, bypassing the need for CAD expertise.
In dim lighting or peripheral vision, the printed handguns are nearly indistinguishable from real firearms and lack any mandated safety marking, posing deception risks during security screenings.

Model-level Alignment

We next examine model-level defenses that aim to suppress the generation of hazardous geometry at its source.
Specifically, we evaluate whether benign alignment can reliably prevent image-to-3D models from reconstructing physically dangerous structures.
Our findings show that the effectiveness of such benign alignment is highly model-dependent and reveals substantial gaps in generalization across architectures and input distributions.

Dataset.

We construct two datasets for model alignment:

•

Forget set
: 200 manually curated harmful 3D models (100 each from Hunyuan-3D-3 and Trellis-2 outputs), each rendered into 24 views, yielding 4,800 image inputs.
During fine-tuning, these inputs are paired with a neutral cube target, training the model to suppress hazardous geometry.

•

Retain set
: 250 benign objects from Trellis-500k (filtered by aesthetic score
>4.5
),also rendered into 24 views, yielding 6,000 image inputs.
This set preserves the model’s general capability.

Benign Alignment.

Rather than merely blocking inputs, this approach aims to unlearn the capability to reconstruct dangerous structures.
Specifically, we evaluate a targeted output remapping strategy: whenever the input corresponds to a known hazardous category (e.g., firearms, kinetic weapons), the model is trained to output a neutral primitive (a simple cube in our case).
The training data construction parallels that of input-level defense, but with a focus on altering the model’s internal mapping rather than filtering inputs.
Details of the dataset and implementation are provided in
Section

E.2

Since voxel-based models (Trellis-1/2) require generating a sparse structure (SS, controlling general structure) followed by generating structured latent (SLAT, controlling fine-grained features), the fine-tuning on SLAT yields no remapping, while even moderate alignment strength on SS leads to severe geometric fragmentation on benign inputs.
Therefore, we observe no operating point that simultaneously improves safety and preserves utility.

Discussion

Lessons from Defense.

Figure

13
indicates that, within our evaluated benchmark, safeguard composition benefits from conditional design rather than naive layer accumulation.

In alignment-enabled settings
, the substantial reduction of residual hazards renders may be redundant in alignment-enabled settings under our benchmark; given the associated false positives, final-stage moderation should be optional rather than default.

In constrained settings
(e.g., closed models), conversely, the absence of alignment leaves risks that coarse input filters cannot intercept.
This necessitates fine-grained output moderation to systematically target high-risk structures while preserving utility.

Limitations.

First, regarding the dataset, while we followed a rigorous funnel-based collection strategy across three major risk categories, our taxonomy is not exhaustive.
Second, our Fabrication tests were conducted on a single type of printer with standard PLA material.
While sufficient to demonstrate functionality for the tested items, more demanding malicious applications might require engineering-grade materials (e.g., Nylon-CF, Metal), which introduce different fabrication constraints that we did not model.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that existing safety mechanisms are insufficient when applied in isolation, while layered, geometry-aware defenses substantially reduce the likelihood of producing usable harmful artifacts.
We highlight an urgent need to treat image-to-3D systems as security-critical components rather than purely creative tools, and to incorporate physical-world risk considerations into their deployment.

Appendix A
Relationship to Existing 3D Platforms

To contextualize the threat of image-to-3D generation, we conducted a comprehensive empirical comparison against traditional 3D model repositories.
We selected three major platforms—Cults3D, MakerWorld, and Thingiverse—and searched for 3D models corresponding to our 83 seed objects.

Moderation and Paywall Friction.

Traditional 3D platforms are governed by strict Terms of Service (ToS) and active community moderation.
For example, we observed that Cults3D actively restricts search queries associated with prominent firearm brands;
searching for keywords such as
Sig Sauer
,
Ruger
,
Beretta
, or
Glock
triggers direct platform restrictions, preventing users from accessing matching models.

Furthermore, when high-quality or specific weapon components do exist, they are often placed behind a paywall by creators or face imminent takedown due to platform policy violations.

In stark contrast, commercial image-to-3D APIs lack these domain-specific safeguards, successfully generating geometry for 99.72% of our requests without imposing additional financial barriers (beyond standard API costs) or post-generation takedowns.

The Specificity Gap: Category vs. Instance Matching.

Our search results revealed a steep drop-off between finding a conceptually similar item and finding an exact geometric replica.

•

High Category Match (85.54%):
For the vast majority of items, platforms host at least one generic representation. A user searching for "brass knuckles" or "keys" will find hundreds of results.

•

Low Instance Match (42.17%):
Finding a functional substitute for a
specific
physical target is significantly harder. The successful instance matches primarily consist of standardized items with universally recognized shapes (e.g., tactical spikes, standard brass knuckles) or highly iconic, mass-market firearms (e.g., Glock and AR-series), which have a dedicated hobbyist modeling community.

Why Instance Matches Fail in Public Repositories:
For the remaining 57.83% of objects, public platforms fall short due to the highly idiosyncratic nature of the items:

•

Keys:
While platforms host numerous 3D key models (including well-known public leaks like the TSA master keys), they do not host keys with the exact bitting (teeth details) required to open a specific user’s lock. Generating the precise bitting requires custom modeling, which image-to-3D can extrapolate directly from a photo.

•

Specific Firearm Accessories:
Generic rails or silencers exist, but accessories tailored to fit obscure or specific firearm models are rarely modeled by the community.

•

Niche and Malicious Objects:
Devices explicitly designed for criminal activity, such as ATM skimmers, are highly niche. They lack a mainstream modeling community and are aggressively purged by platform moderators, making them virtually impossible to find as downloadable STLs.

Ultimately, image-to-3D eliminates the attacker’s dependency on the open-source community’s willingness to model and host specific items, enabling the instantaneous cloning of customized, niche, or restricted objects.

Quality Degradation.

To simulate realistic visual corruptions, we apply three types of degradations to the sampled prototype images: Gaussian blur, Gaussian noise, and random block masking.
All degradations are applied to the RGB channels only; if an image contains an alpha channel, it is preserved without modification.

•

Gaussian blur is used to emulate defocus and motion-related artifacts by spatially smoothing the image. A Gaussian filter with kernel size
k\in\{5,15,31\}
is applied to the image, where larger kernel sizes correspond to stronger blur effects and greater loss of fine-grained details.

•

To model sensor noise and low-quality image acquisition, we add zero-mean Gaussian noise independently to each pixel:
I^{\prime}=I+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with noise standard deviation
\sigma\in\{12,38,76\}.

After noise injection, pixel values are clipped to the valid intensity range.
Increasing values of
\sigma
represent higher noise severity.

•

Random block masking simulates occlusion and partial information loss. Multiple rectangular regions are randomly placed on the image until a target masking ratio is reached:
r\in\{0.15,0.30,0.50\}
.
Each block spans 5%–20% of the image width and height, and masked regions are filled with black pixels.
Larger masking ratios correspond to more severe occlusion.

To generate consistent multi-view representations, we employ a compositional prompting strategy.
The final text prompt
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{S}_{\text{view}}

\mathcal{P}_{\text{base}}

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{\text{base}}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\text{view}}
.
The Base Prompt (

This fixed component is defined as follows:

Semantic Camouflaging.

To modify the surface semantic appearance of an image while preserving its underlying geometric structure, we adopts a controlled regeneration approach based on Stable Diffusion 3.5 integrated with ControlNet.

We first apply Canny edge detection to extract salient structural contours from the original image.
The resulting edge map is used as conditional input to ControlNet, serving as a geometric constraint scaffold to preserve the underlying structure during semantic camouflaging.

Based on the preserved geometric structure, we use Stable Diffusion 3.5 to redraw the RGB channels under predefined prompts to modify surface appearance and semantic roles.
Specifically, we design a hierarchical prompt scheme consisting of a base prompt and three aspect-specific prompts. Selected prompt tokens and phrases are emphasized via token-level attention weighting (e.g., token:1.3) to guide semantic camouflaging.

Appendix E
Mitigation

Input-level Filtering

Dataset.

We leverage our curated prototypes and multi-view input as positive samples (i.e., images of regulated objects) and further collect two types of negative samples:

•

Hard negatives
: Benign objects—such as toy guns, multi-hole rings, and standard POS slots—that exhibit high visual, topological, or structural similarity to these prototypes.
Including these deceptive items desensitizes the model to superficial macro-shapes, forcing it to learn fine-grained discriminative features (e.g., material textures and subtle details) to effectively mitigate false positives.

•

General negatives
: Random benign everyday objects sampled from ImageNet, used to broaden the decision boundary and improve generalization.

Implementation.

We uses the following loss to suppress easy negative samples and focus on hard ones, thereby improving training stability and discrimination:

\mathcal{L}=-y\left(1-p\right)^{\gamma_{+}}\log(p)-(1-y)\left(\tilde{p}\right)^{\gamma_{-}}\log(1-p),

where
p
is the predicted probability and
y\in\{0,1\}
is the ground truth label.
\gamma_{+}
and
\gamma_{-}
are the focusing parameters for positive and negative samples, respectively.
The term
\tilde{p}
performs hard thresholding to ignore easy negatives while focusing on hard negatives.
Finally, we apply LoRA
[
16
]
to fine-tune only attention layers, ensuring efficient adaptation while maintaining strong generalization.

Adversarial Robustness.

To simulate a realistic black-box threat model, we employ a transfer-based attack strategy.
We utilize the curated database
\mathcal{D}
for model fine-tuning.
To prevent data leakage, we randomly partition
\mathcal{D}
into two disjoint subsets,
\mathcal{D}_{proxy}
and
\mathcal{D}_{victim}
, such that
\mathcal{D}_{proxy}\cap\mathcal{D}_{victim}=\emptyset
and
|\mathcal{D}_{proxy}|\approx|\mathcal{D}_{victim}|\approx 0.5|\mathcal{D}|
.

We fine-tune two SigLIP 2 models using AdamW (
lr=2\times 10^{-4}
, 20 epochs): a
Proxy Model
(
M_{proxy}
) trained on
\mathcal{D}_{proxy}
to generate adversarial gradients, and a
Victim Model
(
M_{victim}
) trained on
\mathcal{D}_{victim}
to evaluate defense efficacy.

We generate adversarial examples on
M_{proxy}
using three
L_{\infty}
-bounded gradient-based attacks, namely PGD, I-FGSM, and a projected variant of C&W, and then transfer them to
M_{victim}
for evaluation.

All attacks are conducted under a unified perturbation budget of
\epsilon=8/255
, step size
\alpha=2/255
, and 10 iterations.

Model-level Filtering

Implementation.

We implement this defense via targeted benign alignment using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation).
Specifically, we inject low-rank adapters into the attention projection layers of the underlying transformer backbone.
This parameter-efficient approach allows us to erase hazardous geometric concepts while minimizing forgetting of general generative capabilities.
Hunyuan-3D-2 is selected as the representative for VAE-based architectures.
Note that other models like TripoSG share a similar VAE encoder and flow-matching pipeline, making findings here transferable.

Metric.

We quantify geometric reconstruction quality using the F1 score between the generated mesh and the reference shape.
Specifically, we compute precision as the fraction of generated surface points whose nearest neighbor distance to the reference mesh is below a fixed threshold (we set 0.005 in our evaluation), and recall as the fraction of reference surface points that are within the same distance to the generated mesh. T
he F1 score is defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Higher F1 indicates better geometric fidelity.

Output-level Filtering

Moderators.

We adopt the following moderators:

•

Handcrafted Feature (Geo).
We employ a set of global geometric scalars that capture the physical properties of the 3D meshes without learning.
We extract a feature vector consisting of fundamental descriptors, including the axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) extents, aspect ratio, surface area, and volume.
Additionally, we compute the convexity index, defined as the ratio of the mesh volume to the volume of its convex hull.

•

D2
[
26
]
.

This method represents a 3D object as a probability distribution of Euclidean distances between pairs of randomly sampled points on the mesh surface.
Specifically, we sample
N=1024
points from the mesh surface and compute the pairwise distances for all distinct pairs.
These distances are aggregated into a normalized histogram (typically 64 bins), forming a rotation-invariant signature of the shape.

•

PointNet
[
31
]
.

We preprocess our meshes by sampling
N=4096
points uniformly from the surface faces.
The architecture utilizes a convolution neural network with architecture 3-64-128-1024 (augmented with 1D BatchNorm) to extract per-point features independently, followed by a symmetric function (max-pooling) to aggregate these local descriptors into a global feature vector.
The features are fed into a lightweight MLP classifier with architecture 1024–512–256–2 (augmented with 1D BatchNorm).

•

MeshCNN
[
13
]
.

Unlike PointNet, which discards topological connectivity, MeshCNN defines convolution and pooling operations directly on the mesh edges.
It utilizes feature-symmetric edge convolution to learn geometric features invariant to the ordering of the edges, and employs mesh pooling via edge collapse operations.
The size of the hidden channel in the graph convolution network is set to 64.
While building the graph, the max face is set to 4096.

•

Compact Latent
[
46
]
.
It utilizes transformer-based VAE to encode 3D shapes as sets of 1D latent vectors, which include rich geometric information.
To improve rotational invariance, we augment each mesh by rotating it by 90, 180, and 270 degrees about the vertical axis, yielding four views per object.
The latents from these views are concatenated and fed into an MLP classifier with architecture 1024–512–256–2, augmented with 1D BatchNorm.

Training.

This mix ensures the classifier learns fine-grained distinctions without over-triggering on innocuous shapes.
All results are reported using 5-fold cross-validation to ensure statistical reliability.
For trainable models, we train for 20 epochs.
