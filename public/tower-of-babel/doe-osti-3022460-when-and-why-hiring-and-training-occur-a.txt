Executive Summary
The transition to high-performance buildings depends on a skilled workforce that can design,
install, and maintain advanced building technologies. Employers across the buildings sector
report persistent difficulties in hiring and retaining qualified workers, highlighting skill gaps
(U.S. Department of Energy 2025). This report shares findings from a national survey of
building energy performance and related contractors that was designed to better understand why
companies hire and train workers, what they look for in new hires, and what helps or hinders
their ability to invest in training. The findings can help building energy workforce development
program administrators craft programs and outreach strategies that reflect and respond to the
needs, perspectives, and realities of contractors.

The survey targeted contractors that primarily complete energy performance improvement
projects in buildings. Eligible respondents included business owners, decision makers, and
employees across trades such as heating, ventilating, and air conditioning (HVAC); insulation;
electrical work; plumbing; and building performance. A total of 209 individuals completed the
survey, located in 37 U.S. states; Washington, D.C.; Guam; and the Marshall Islands.

The results highlight several themes:

 • Contractors hire to maintain quality and keep up with workload. Top reasons to hire are
 maintaining quality (73% of survey respondents), replacing a worker (70%), and meeting
 new business demand (61%). If workforce programs support stable pipelines of workers
 qualified with the right skills and certifications, firms can maintain high-quality
 installations.

 • Skill gaps, and the need to keep up with new technologies, codes or certifications are key
 motivation for contractors to invest in upskilling their existing workers rather than hiring
 new staff. Workforce programs focused on these areas may be more effective if marketed
 to contractors as upskilling opportunities for existing workers versus investing in
 programs for new workers.

 • Hiring is difficult largely due to skill and experience shortages. Lack of technical skills
 and experience was the most commonly cited barrier to hiring. If training programs
 include hands-on experience, companies can fill roles faster and complete more energy
 upgrades that lower consumer bills. Contractors prefer training workers when skill gaps
 are small. Companies often train when workers show reliability, fit, or only need
 incremental upskilling (mentioned 24 times). If workforce programs offer modular, short-
 duration training aligned with real job tasks, then companies can retain workers, build
 internal expertise, and strengthen the resilience of the U.S. workforce.

 • Cost is the main barrier to training—even when companies see its value. Sixty percent of
 respondents cited the cost of training as the primary or secondary reason they do not
 regularly train workers. Programs that reduce direct expenses or lost productivity might
 encourage firms to invest more in skill development, supporting a larger, more capable
 workforce.

 v

 • Contractors prefer hands-on, on-the-job training. If offered training subsidies,
 respondents most frequently said that they would prefer funding for on-the-job learning
 (37% of respondents). This was followed by in-person certification courses and testing,
 preferred by 35% of respondents.

 • Many companies report that large shares of their workforce are not well trained. Less
 than half of respondents said at least 70% of their workers are well trained; gaps still exist
 in hands-on proficiency, safety, and soft skills.

 • Workers often leave to start their own businesses. Fifty-nine percent cited employees
 leaving to start new companies as a top reason for turnover. Training that expands
 entrepreneurial capacity in a supportive ecosystem may benefit regions as a whole, as the
 contractor base is more distributed and resilient. This also underscores the importance of
 recruiting and adequately training new workers to ensure continued high-quality work as
 experienced workers venture out to start their own businesses.

These findings can help state and local agencies, workforce development practitioners, training
providers, and community organizations design programs that meet contractors where they are—
reducing perceived risk, strengthening career pathways, and supporting the large-scale energy
performance efforts needed to improve energy affordability for U.S. households and grid
reliability.

1 Introduction
The buildings sector in the United States consumes 75% of the country’s electricity and accounts
for 40% of total U.S. energy consumption (U.S. Department of Energy [DOE] n.d.), with
expenditures on annual building energy costs estimated at $374 billion (Langevin and Wilson
2025). Improving the energy performance of these buildings is essential to achieving energy
affordability and improving grid resilience and has the potential to provide significant financial
relief to U.S. households. 1

Energy efficiency is a proven strategy that reduces building energy use and costs while
optimizing the comfort and health of occupants. The energy efficiency sector is also a key
economic driver across the nation, employing 2.3 million workers, a third of whom are in
installation or repair occupations, and providing a median wage 20% higher than the U.S.
median wage, with lower educational requirements to entry (DOE 2025). As the country
accelerates efforts to modernize buildings and revitalize aging housing stock, the demand for
skilled workers who can design, build, install, and operate high-performance, energy-efficient
systems is growing rapidly, especially as older generations retire. However, the 2025 U.S.
Energy and Employment Report found that at least 79% of employers across energy technologies
report that it is “somewhat difficult or very difficult” to find qualified workers, with the most
common reasons being a lack of experience, training, or technical skills, as well as insufficient
nontechnical skills. Other reasons cited by employers include insufficient qualifications and
small applicant pools (DOE 2025).

As industry and governments nationwide confront persistent worker shortages and critical skill
gaps, workforce development has emerged as a crucial strategy for cultivating needed talent—
both by training new entrants and by upskilling existing employees. To support these efforts,
research has explored ways to design effective training programs, examining which occupations
face the greatest demand, the barriers to entering the energy efficiency sector, employers’
experiences in the labor market, and proven strategies for workforce development. Yet much of
this work has not focused on understanding the motivations and decision-making practices of a
key set of workforce stakeholders: building efficiency contractors. Contractors play a pivotal role
in shaping the workforce through hiring, offering on-the-job learning opportunities, and defining
training and credentials when engaged by workforce development practitioners. This report
attempts to address this gap by investigating the motivations and risk perceptions of contractors
when it comes to hiring and training employees to implement advanced building technologies.
The report begins with an overview of the project’s methodology, then provides detailed
information on the survey results. Finally, the conclusion offers key insights and takeaways.

Gaining insight into these decision-making processes is vital for developing workforce programs
that are responsive to contractors’ needs and effectively mitigate perceived risks. Because energy
efficiency workers and contractor firms access training in a variety of ways, the findings from
this research study can inform the design and messaging of local, state, federal, community-

2 Review of Existing Research
To achieve the objective of the study, the research teams began by conducting a review of
previous work on this topic to determine what information already exists and to identify
knowledge gaps. For each survey report reviewed, the purpose was to identify the main objective
of the research, the research methodology, the survey participants, the study findings, and
whether contractor perspectives and decision-making regarding training and hiring were
considered. A total of 17 reports were reviewed, five of which were highly relevant to this study;
these are shown in Table 1.

Surveys commonly focused on profiling contractors and exploring challenges with the labor
market and technologies and the types of training contractors partake in. Some surveys asked
about contractors’ familiarity with rebates for building upgrades and their expectations for
installation increases in the coming years; however, there was a lack of further inquiry into the
implications of these findings for hiring and training decisions. There was a gap in exploring
which factors influence contractors’ decisions to hire new employees or upskill their existing
workforce—this includes a limited assessment of what kind of information and messaging from
other stakeholders in the market influence contractor behavior. This study addresses that gap by
directly examining when and why contractors choose to hire and train other contractors.

3 Survey Methodology
The objective of this study is to examine why building efficiency contractors decide to train,
upskill, and hire new workers, and understand how these decisions are made, including what
information influences decision makers, where they access that information, and what their
perceptions are of the different training and hiring pathways available to them. Contractors are a
key stakeholder in the workforce development ecosystem; understanding their decision-making
processes can inform policies, programs, and strategies to increase the energy efficiency
workforce and more effectively connect training resources and trainees to contractors.

Following the literature review, the research team worked with an internal research advisory
group to frame the scope of the research and formulate survey questions, with the goal of
ensuring that questions would be relevant to the target audience. The research plan and survey
design were reviewed and approved by the Central U.S. Department of Energy Institutional
Review Board to meet standards and ethics for human-subjects research.

3.1 Participants
The target population for this survey was contractors and contractor businesses whose primary
work is completing energy performance improvement projects in residential buildings, including
single-family houses, manufactured housing, and multifamily buildings. Responses were
received from contractors who also work in commercial buildings. Responses were allowable
from business owners, decision makers, and employees of the following contractor groups:
HVAC/R mechanics, installers, or technicians; electricians; insulation workers (floor, ceiling,
and walls); plumbers; building performance specialists; weatherization technicians; general
contractors; remodeling contractors; solar installers; and energy auditors, HERS raters, and
energy consultants. The survey was promoted through professional contacts, industry
organizations’ blogs and newsletters, and national conferences as an effort to amass broad
geographic representation of respondents. Detailed descriptors of survey respondents are
included in the results section of this report.

Contractors were recruited through targeted online outreach, including trade-related newsletters,
trade-specific articles, and affiliated events. To gain a broader geographic region, Midwest
Energy Efficiency Alliance was hired to conduct survey outreach through their newsletters and
network with the Southeast Energy Efficiency Alliance, South-central Partnership for Energy
Efficiency as a Resource, and Northeast Energy Efficiency Partnerships.

3.2 Materials and Procedure
The final survey was designed to be completed online within 10–15 minutes and consisted of 40
questions, including one attention check embedded to ensure quality and remove bots. Response
formats included Likert scales, multiple choice, ranking, and open text as applicable to each
question. Survey questions focused on understanding how employers engage with the labor and
training market, employers’ priorities in a new hire, and factors that influence hiring and training
decisions. Some firmographic and demographic information was also collected, including the
size of the firm, sector of energy efficiency, types of buildings serviced, age, race, gender, and
geographic location.

 6

Hosted and administered on the Alchemer platform, the survey was available in English and
designed to be accessible from both desktop and mobile devices. Important information was
presented to participants on the survey landing page, including the purpose of the research, the
predicted risks and benefits of participating, how the collected information would be used, and
contact information for researchers and the institutional review board for questions and concerns.
The participants were then asked to consent to the survey before proceeding. For those who did
not consent, the survey ended immediately.

Open responses were analyzed for themes using a thematic analysis and open coding. For
questions about whether to train or hire, responses were bifurcated into hiring or training
categories. When responses were too vague about whether they were responding to hiring or
training, they were omitted. The codebooks for each question’s analysis are found in Appendix
A. A complete list of survey questions is available in Appendix C.

4 Survey Findings
4.1 Respondent Demographics
Survey respondents reflect a relatively diverse cross section of the building trades workforce.
Most respondents identified as male, with about one-third identifying as female. Respondents
consisted of predominantly white and Black/African American respondents, with a smaller
representation from Asian, Native Hawaiian, Hispanic, and other racial and ethnic groups.
Respondents were largely between 25 and 54 years old. A majority of respondents (87%)
indicated that they make decisions about hiring or training workers as part of their job. Almost a
third of the companies where respondents worked were woman- and/or minority-owned.

Figure 1 shows the geographic distribution of states where survey respondents primarily conduct
their work.

 Figure 1. Geography of respondents
Respondents work in 37 U.S. states; Washington, D.C; Guam; and the Marshall Islands, with the
top three primary states of work being California, New York, and Texas. This distribution
indicates that the findings are nationally relevant, while also reflecting the concentration of
residential energy and building activity (i.e., higher populations) in certain markets.

Industry and Building Types

Respondents reported working across a variety of industry segments, but many best described
themselves as general contractors or as firms providing multiple building services. Specialized
trades, such as HVAC/R, insulation, electrical, and weatherization, were also well represented,
reflecting the mix of roles in delivering energy performance upgrades.

In terms of building types, respondents indicated that they primarily work on commercial and
multifamily buildings but also reported substantial activity in residential buildings. This
significant overlap suggests that contractors often operate across multiple market segments,
which may influence both their hiring needs and training priorities. Figure 2(a) shows how
respondents described their primary industry, while Figure 2(b) shows the types of buildings
their organizations most commonly work on.

Company Size and Respondents’ Number of Years in Industry

Contractors in the sample ranged from very small firms with only a few employees to larger
companies with thousands of employees. Small (1–50 employees) and medium (51–200
employees) firms made up 63%, a significant portion of respondents, underscoring the
importance of workforce strategies that are accessible to businesses with less administrative
capacity relative to larger firms.

Growth aspirations were widespread, with 82% of all respondents reporting that they wanted to
grow their companies. This desire was especially pronounced among small to medium firms,
with 91% of companies with fewer than 50 employees and 95% of those with 51–200 employees
expressing interest in growth, compared to just 65% of firms with more than 200 employees.
These findings underscore the critical importance of tailoring business development and
workforce initiatives to the needs of small- and medium-sized contractor firms, which represent a
sizeable portion of the sector and have the potential to be the strongest drivers of future
workforce expansion.

Reported Busy Season

More than 40% of respondents shared that their company is busy year-round, with the remainder
having a busier season(s). For companies that are not busy year-round, the majority are busiest
during the summer, followed by winter.

 Figure 5. Reported busy season

Seasonal variation in workload has direct implications for hiring and training. Contractors may
be more likely to seek additional workers or invest in training ahead of anticipated busy periods,
whereas slower seasons may offer more flexibility for staff to attend off-site or classroom-based
training.

4.3 Hiring Practices
Reasons Companies Start Hiring

Contractors reported a range of motivations for bringing on new workers, with quality, staffing
needs, and business growth all playing central roles. The five most common reasons contractors
cited for hiring a worker were: to keep quality high (73%), to replace someone who left (70%),
to secure a great candidate (64%), to increase profits (63%), and to keep up with new business
(61%). Figure 6 presents the top reasons companies report for starting to hire new workers.

 13

 Figure 6. Top five reasons respondents hire new workers

These findings suggest that hiring decisions are often closely tied to multiple factors, primarily
related to growth, employee turnover, and the objective of maintaining or improving work
quality. The top response indicates that contractors are primarily driven by a commitment to
producing high-quality work on their projects. This is also shown by the responses about hiring
when “a great candidate is found,” highlighting that contractors also hire even when they do not
have vacancies; they seize opportunities when attractive candidates who can add value to their
work become available. This suggests that increasing the number of well-trained workers and
fostering connections between qualified job seekers and contractors can spur hiring and
encourage contractors to grow their businesses. This is a crucial finding for workforce
development and training programs, as it may indicate a need to expand programming beyond
technical training to focus on connecting contractors with trainees and program graduates.

Additionally, employee turnover is a significant factor in hiring. This may mean that hiring
activity does not always correlate with company growth but may simply reflect replacing
workers who have separated. Nevertheless, in line with the high percentage of respondents who
indicated a desire to grow their companies in the previous section, a high number of respondents
hire because “more employees mean more profit,” because their “company has new business,” or
because of new rebates or tax credits. This reflects a growth-oriented mindset, where contractors
respond to demand signals and actively seek to increase revenue rather than simply responding to
staffing needs. Other important factors influencing hiring include remaining competitive in the
market, as shown by those who hire when their competitors do. Finally, some contractors hire
when they feel overwhelmed, suggesting a potential opportunity for workforce development
programs to assess regions where contractor availability or capacity may be limited relative to
project demand and engage small- to medium-sized firms that may need to expand their
workforce to meet demand.

Survey responses revealed distinct circumstances that influence contractors when deciding

In contrast, companies choose to hire new workers when workload growth or turnover creates

5 Conclusion
This report presents findings from a national survey of building efficiency contractors designed
to understand their behavior regarding hiring and training. Responses were collected from 209
contractors across 37 U.S. states; Washington, D.C.; Guam; and the Marshall Islands, covering
both residential and commercial buildings. Consistent with findings from other studies on labor
market experiences, many contractors expressed a desire to grow their business but are
struggling to find and hire qualified workers. This challenge is largely attributed to gaps in
technical and soft skills. The issue of skill gaps also affects the existing workforce, as many
contractors reported that a significant portion of their current staff are not well trained.

To support workforce programs that are working to address these industry needs, this study
offers valuable insights into contractors’ training decisions. When looking at training versus
hiring, upskilling existing staff is preferred when skill gaps are small—for example, when new
technologies, codes, or programs require it, which is often how energy efficiency work is
addressed. Hiring, on the other hand, is often preferred when time is limited, the skill gap is too
large to close through training, or a new hire brings a fresh perspective or unique experience.

Although delivering high-quality work is a primary concern and skilled workers are a priority,
contractors often hesitate to invest in training due to the costs of training and fears that
employees may leave shortly after completing training. The demands of busy work schedules
and the potential loss of income while employees are training exacerbate these concerns,
underscoring the need for programs to offer short, flexible training options for employees during
off-peak seasons, as well as subsidies to not only help offset training costs, but also to
compensate companies for workers’ time spent taking the training. The survey results also
emphasize the importance of hands-on, on-the-job training for both new trainees and current
employees seeking upskilling. Many contractors indicated that, if offered training subsidies for
their workers, their top preference would be to use funds to conduct training in-house and on the
job, with other in-person courses being the second most preferred.

Furthermore, contractors place significant value on demonstrated experience and competence
when hiring new workers, often finding candidates through recommendations and apprenticeship
or internship programs. Investing in training programs that offer practical experience and
connect employers to trainees—for example, by enhancing contractors’ capacity to host interns
or apprentices—can effectively bridge the gap between job seekers and employers, thereby
expanding contractor capacity to implement building efficiency projects.

The findings from this national survey highlight how building efficiency contractors make
decisions about hiring and training. These decision-making processes may vary from other
industries, based on the nature of work, business economics, and specific challenges for building
efficiency employers. There could also be regional variations in different parts of the country,
urban versus rural areas, or in states or jurisdictions that have different building and energy
programs, or energy efficiency incentive or other programs. Workforce development and training
program administrators should always undertake a more local needs assessment that includes
contractor engagement before developing or rolling out new programs. Understanding this
perspective is essential to the success of workforce development efforts that seek to engage and

 25

support these contractors, and ultimately to the growth of the energy efficiency industry, which
provides savings and healthy, safe living environments for households across the country.

Appendix B: Embedded Behavioral Economics
Experiment
The researchers incorporated a behavioral economics experiment into the survey to test the
influence of framing information on participants’ responses. The experiment is based on
construal level theory, which describes psychological distance and the extent to which a person’s
thinking is abstract or concrete (Trope & Liberman, 2010). Research shows that the more
abstract an idea or action, the less likely a person is to act on it and the greater the psychological
distance, which refers to the degree of cognitive separation between a person and something else.
Priming, a technique in which the introduction of one stimulus influences how people respond to
a subsequent stimulus, and framing, which refers to the way information is presented, have been
proven to affect psychological distance and decision-making (Flusberg et al., 2024; Henderson &
Wakslak, 2010; Zhu et al., 2017). Researchers used a 2 x 2 factorial design whereby message
content and framing served as the independent variables, as shown in the table below. The local
and general content priming factors were an estimated additional number of employees needed
by each company versus the overall percentage of additional workers needed across the industry,
respectively. The quality of building performance work versus the societal and homeowner
benefits from energy efficiency provided the content framing. The dependent variable was the
number of employees contractors think they will have in 5 years.
