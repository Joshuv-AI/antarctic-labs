Preface

This report summarizes the work carried out with support of the United States Department of
Energy under Award DE-SC0023410. The theme of this project was to update electronically grand-
canonical atomistic simulations to properly incorporate pH effects, on a rigorous thermodynamic
basis, and to compare this to experimental findings. This project was primarily conducted by PI
Andrew Peterson and his group at Brown University, with an unfunded collaboration with the
Quantum Simulations Group at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory with Dr. Joel Varley,
who hosted the primary graduate student (Alexander Lackey) funded by this project and con-
tributed to all major intellectual pursuits, utilizing the expertise and capabilities of the national
laboratory to advance the project goals. Postdoctoral associate Dr. Juye Kim was also a major
contributor to this project, and implementation of the final section included cooperation with sev-
eral electronic-structure open-source software developers, including Colin Baker (Brown), Sandeep
Sharma (CalTech), Georg Kastlunger (DTU), and Jens Jørgen Mortensen (DTU).
 This report has four major sections, aligning with the major objectives we pursued under this
award. The first section (§1) develops the intellectual framework to link pH effects to electronically
grand-canonical calculations, providing a rigorous thermodynamic link and building off of our
previous work that introduced a model known as the ‘decoupled computational electrode’. This
approach adopts a standard thermodynamic systems approach, and links it to the grand-canonical
framework, treating both the electrons and the protons grand-canonically. The framework is generic
enough to apply to any proton-consuming reaction, although it is only described in-depth here for
reactions with H3 O+ donors.
 Next (§2), we provide a detailed implementation of this methodology, which provides a di-
rect link to existing experimental data. These results provide multiple insights into experimental
data, including the intrinsic-barrier difference between protonating a surface from a hydronium ion
(H3 O+ ), which is expected to be active at lower pH’s, and from a water molecule (H2 O), which
is expected to dominate at medium and higher pH’s. More significantly, we highlight a crucial
difference in proton and electron transfer: the proton transfers stepwise, while the electron trans-
fers incrementally over the sub-steps of the reaction (for an extended system). This results in an
unequal contribution of the proton and the electron to the free-energy shift at the transition state,
even when the full driving force is kept constant. We describe in detail how the relative placement
of the proton insertion in the case of H3 O+ and H2 O donors results in opposite behavior, which
explains the experimentally observed acceleration of the reaction rate at both ends of the pH scale
(in opposite directions). Finally, we quantitatively link our atomistic predictions to experimental
linear-sweep voltammetry shifts, and show an excellent agreement in the both the direction and
magnitude of the shift.
 Conventional investigations of electrocatalytic reactions focus on just the final ion-destruction
reaction; that is, the hydronium has localized near the surface in what we refer to as a pseudo-
initial state (pIS). However, this ignores the movement of the ion to the surface, which can have
a significant effect on the kinetics of the reaction, and can challenge the fundamental assumptions
of the conventional model. In §3, we examine the ion movement to the surface in detail, which is
non-trivial in that it can follow a “Grotthuss” mechanism, in which protons hop between water
molecules, rather than diffusing as bulk ions. We find this has as strong influence on the reaction,
and that in all cases examined the true barrier lies at the pIS in the first layer, validating the use of
simpler approaches. We also find the diffusion itself to be potential-activated, as each proton hop is
accompanied by a small amount of electron transfer. We justify this via elementary electrostatics
models based upon Gauss’s Law.
 Finally, in §4 we briefly describe a major update to our capabilities to model pH-dependence

through electronically grand-canonical calculations. In this section, we briefly present the mathe-
matical framework to port our existing implementation from a finite-difference basis into a plane-
wave basis. This approach has been implemented in a development version of a widely used open-
source electronic-structure code, and preliminary tests show an acceleration of calculations by nearly
an order of magnitude while maintaining the same levels of accuracy.

1 pH effects in electronically grand-canonical calculations

1.1 Rigorous thermodynamic inclusion of pH effects
The major goal of this project was to develop rigorous means to include the effect of pH into
electronically grand-canonical atomistic simulations of reactions at electrified interfaces. pH ef-
fects are known to be prevalent in electrochemical experimental results, and the effect has been
conclusively shown to not be a trivial thermodynamic effect, as could be deduced with traditional
electronic structure calculations of elementary electrochemical steps. This project aimed to use
grand-canonical electronic structure calculations in a new scheme that allows a clear separation
of potential and pH thermodynamic effects. While the free energy of elementary steps involving
an (equal) integer proton and electron transfer will be equally influenced by changes in potential
or pH, our goal is to show the portions of the elementary step (namely the pseudo-initial state
and the barrier) that are affected independently by the potential and pH, due to the non-coupled
nature of their transfer at this stage of the reaction. These frameworks are developed in the con-
text of the simple hydrogen evolution reaction, which also has unambiguous experimental points
of comparison.
 Here we briefly describe the thermodynamic strategy to rigorously incorporate pH effects into ab
intio calculations; the relevant control volumes are shown in Figure 1. Let’s consider the simplest
elementary reaction of depositing a proton on a surface; that is, the so-called Volmer reaction. In
this case, we’ll consider the proton donor to be H3 O+ , although in the simulation it if free to exist
as H5 O2 + or H7 O2 + , etc. Ultimately, this methodology will allow us to distinguish an excess-proton
donor (H+ ) from a neutral proton donor (H2 O), but for the current discussion we’ll assume it is
H+ . We can consider this elementary reaction to consist of three elementary processes connecting
four distinct states; which we can write as:

1.2 Electronically grand-canonical electronic-structure calculations
The approach above necessitates the use of an electronic-structure methodology that treats the
electrons grand-canonically: that is, the number of electrons is treated as a free variable in order
to hold the potential of the simulated system constant. To accomplish this, we utilize the solvated
jellium method (SJM) [3] developed in Peterson’s group and in use at LLNL. The philosophy
behind the SJM approach is to use the simplest approach that captures the physics of interest.
SJM controls the potential (manifested as the top-side work function) by changing the fractional
number of electrons within the periodic unit cell; that is, it operates in an electronically grand-
canonical manner. In this method, we counter the fractional electron with an equal and opposite

 7

charge delocalized within a “jellium slab”, located above a layer of explicit solvent molecules.
This is shown in Figure 2A. This jellium region is nothing other than a homogeneous region of
continuous charge (consistent with the original definition of jellium [5]). The inclusion of such
charge separation would result in an unphysical electrical field across the explicit solvent layer.
Therefore, we include an implicit solvent, whose sole purpose is to screen the large field, although
it also provides a modicum of consistent energetic stabilization to the explicit solvent layer, which
does not vary throughout the course of a reaction. In practice, the shape of the counter charge in
the jellium region has no impact on the energetics in the Stern layer, so long as an explicit solvent
is used. This is consistent with an electrostatic understanding of the system rooted in Gauss’
law [6]. Using this method, the potential is automatically controlled via an internal control loop
implemented in the GPAW [4] electronic structure code. In practice, the computational penalty
for running SJM simulations is only about 30%, due to the smart control loop we have built into
our software package.

2 Non-Nernstian pH acceleration at both ends of the scale

2.1 Experimental connection to atomistic pH calculations
Many important electrochemical reactions—such as hydrogen evolution/oxidation [7, 8], oxygen
evolution/reduction [9, 10], CO2 reduction [11, 12], aqueous Li-ion batteries [13], and catalyst dis-
solution [14] processes—are strongly dependent upon pH, and the effect is typically not trivial.
The ability to control the pH of energy-conversion reactions is of fundamental importance, but the
inclusion of pH effects has remained a great challenge in atomistic calculations, hindering basic
research.
 Obviously, pH effects are important in electrochemical reactions that involve the reaction of
a proton (H+ ) or a hydroxide (OH – ); for simplicity we’ll focus our discussion on protons in this
report, but the methods readily apply to OH – as well. When a proton is involved in a half reaction,
it is almost always paired with an electron to preserve charge neutrality; this occurs in numerous
important half reactions summarized in Table 1. For this reason, the driving force of such net
reactions is a function of both potential and pH. Using units of eV and focusing on a one-proton–
one-electron reaction, a change of 0.1 V results in a difference in driving force of 0.1 eV, whereas a
change of 1 pH unit results in a difference of 0.059 eV at room temperature (that is, 1 × kB T ln 10).
For this reason, one often uses a thermodynamic potential scale, here denoted ϕt , to describe the
driving forces, where
                                       ϕt = ϕ + pH × kB T ln 10                                   (12)
where ϕ is the absolute electrode potential [15], typically expressed on the SHE (standard hydrogen
electrode) scale, and ϕt is conventionally expressed on the RHE (reversible hydrogen electrode)
scale [16]. For reactions where the number of electrons and protons is equal, the driving force when
expressed vs. RHE is independent of pH.
 Comparing reaction kinetics at equivalent values of ϕt highlights differences that are not directly
attributable to the driving force. As an example, in Figure 3 we show hydrogen evolution reaction
(HER) data collected by Koper and colleagues [8] at high pH values alongside data collected by
Markovic and colleagues [7] at low pH values, both on Au crystals exposing the fcc (111) facet. Data
are plotted on both an SHE and an RHE scale. In both cases, we see that on the RHE scale—that
is, at equivalent driving force—that there is a significant pH dependence to the reaction at both
ends of the pH scale. Further, we see that the trend is in opposite directions: in acidic conditions,
the kinetics are better at lower pH, while in alkaline conditions, the kinetics are better at higher
pH. We will qualitatively interpret of this counter-intuitive phenomenon as we describe our research
thrusts (in the Task 1 description). The data is also plotted on an SHE scale, where a trend is still
observed, at least partially ruling out trivial explanations such as a rate-limiting step that does not

simple explanation. Here we highlight a few other cases where the pH effect is significant. For
example, the oxygen-evolution reaction exhibits large pH effects, [10] and tends to operate most fa-
vorably at high pH levels; in a device that splits water this leads to suboptimal performance as HER
(the counter-electrode reaction) operates preferentially at low pH levels. In CO2 electroreduction,
the product selectivity is effected particularly strongly by the pH; further, since both protons and

the local CO2 –bicarbonate–carbonate equilibrium (which is strongly coupled to the pH). [12] CO2

is not commonly performed in alkaline pH’s because the equilibrium with (bi-)carbonate species
consumes most of the reactive CO2 . Essentially all of the half reactions in Table 1 have similar
levels of challenges with respect to pH.
 Understanding electrochemical pH effects from the ab initio level is a timely topic, prominently
highlighted in a perspective in Science this year [17]; and many efforts are underway to describe
pH effects. Numerous explanations of pH effects have been advanced in recent years, examples
of which include changes in proton entropy [18], change in proton donor [19, 20], mass transport
limitations [21], water-dissociation kinetics [7], ion distributions [22], mechanistic switching [23],
metal-ion hydrolysis [24], unexpected hydroniums at high pH [25], and near-surface ion concentra-
tions [8]. Although we believe that all these effects are important, we argue that there is still a
lack of a fundamental, rigorous methodology to separate pH effects from potential effects across
all portions of an elementary reaction. Here, we leveraged the combined expertise of Peterson’s
research group with that of the Quantum Simulations Group at LLNL, to utilize and combine
cutting-edge tools from both groups that allowed a clean separation of pH, potential, and ion-type
effects across all phases of a reaction free-energy diagram.

This semi-quantitative description is visualized in Figure 4. We start the description for the case
of H3 O+ with a base case at ϕ = -0.6 VSHE and pH 7, which corresponds to a thermodynamic
potential of ϕt ≈ -0.1 VRHE . In this reaction, the free energy diagram contains entries for the
initial state (IS), corresponding to a neutral H2 O layer near the surface, and a pseudo-initial state
(pIS), corresponding to the movement of a proton from the reservoir to this layer; this process is
associated with a partial electron transfer as treated in-depth by Chen et al. [26] and described
elsewhere in this report. In a thought experiment, we hold the potential constant while raising the
pH to 8.7, corresponding to ϕt of 0.0 VRHE . This raises the free energies of the pIS, TS, and FS
uniformly by 0.1 eV relative to the IS, since it is only the IS→pIS transition that contains a proton.
The net reaction barrier rises by 0.1 eV, consistent with a 0.1 V change in the thermodynamic
potential (that is, the driving force). Now, we envision holding the pH at 8.7 and lowering the
potential to ϕ = -0.7 VSHE . This restores the thermodynamic potential to -0.1 VRHE , as in the
base case. The pIS and TS are lowed by approximately n1 e∆ϕ and (n1 + n2 )e∆ϕ, respectively,
although in the latter case this is only a linear approximation to the rigorous Marcus-like forms.
Thus, at an equal driving force (ϕt = −0.1 VRHE ), we conclude that raising the pH has the effect
of raising the barrier, suggesting we should not expect reactions with equivalent thermodynamics
to lead to equivalent kinetics, even with identical proximate proton donors.
 We can perform a parallel analysis for the case where H2 O is the donor, shown in the right panel
of Figure 4. We start with the same base case with ϕt = −0.1 VRHE , and in the first hypothetical

step we raise the pH to 8.7, which in the case of an H2 O donor only affects the pFS → FS transition,
raising the energy of the FS. Next we lower ϕ by 0.1 V, thus restoring the thermodynamic driving

opposite effect of the H3 O+ donor case.
 While the analyses above are approximate as they rely on a linearized treatment of the barrier
as a function of potential, they nevertheless illustrate an important point: we should not expect
kinetics to be equivalent at equivalent driving forces—that is, at equivalent potentials on the RHE
scale—as some researchers often assume. Instead, we should expect differences in reaction barriers
even when the free energy change of the elementary step remains constant. This sort of analysis is
only possible by using grand-canonical methodologies that rigorously account for partial electron
transfer throughout a reaction, combined with the nonlinear response of barriers to changes in
potential (which will remove the linear approximation of the above analysis).

  2.3 Implications for pH-dependent kinetics
  We next turn to interpreting these results and providing a kinetic-context for them, culminating
  in a quantitative comparison to experiment. Figure 9 concisely explains the key kinetic concepts
  for both the case of a H3 O+ and a H2 O donor. Focusing first on the H3 O+ donor case, presented
  in the left column of the figure, the thermodynamic states are shown schematically across the top,
  with the IS, pIS, TS, and FS labels corresponding to the same states as in equation (13). Over
  the course of the elementary step, a formal charge of 1 electron is transferred, although this will
  not be precisely one due to changes in the surface-normal dipole in the IS and FS. Crucially, it’s
  important to note that this electron is not transferred in any single part of this elementary step,
  but that each distinct stage is accompanied by partial electron transfer, denoted n1 , n2 , and n3
  in both the figure and the equation. (Note this assumption is valid for an extended surface where
  averaging takes place; for a truly single-site electrocatalyst this assumption may break down and a
  more conventional Marcus-theory approach may be more appropriate.) The sum n1 + n2 + n3 will

be approximately equal to one, again with the deviation due to surface-normal dipoles between the
IS and FS. A powerful aspect of electronically grand-canonical calculations is that these values are
direct outputs of the calculation; that is, they are inferred rather than assumed. For the sake of
simplicity in this discussion, we’ll assume that the electron transfer is roughly equally distributed
among the three steps (n1 ≈ n2 ≈ n3 ≈ 13 ); typically they are all of the same order of magnitude
in the inferred results.
 However, the same cannot be said of the proton transfer: it is uniquely transferred into the
thermodynamic system in a well-defined step; that is, the IS → pIS step. We show these (partial)
transferred in the plot labeled ‘species transferred’ in Figure 9: the proton steps from 0 to 1 in a
single step from the IS to the pIS, while the electron transfers in roughly equal increments among
each of the three sub-steps of the elementary reaction.
 In the next panel of Figure 9, we ask what happens to the free energy (G) if the pH shifts
by +1 unit, as a result of these separated proton and electron transfers. Here, we assume a
simple Nernstian treatment within the elementary step, even though we would claim the net effect
is non-Nernstian. First, the effect of the proton is to shift in the positive direction by 59 meV
(from pH × kB T ln 10, presented earlier in equation (12)). This entire shift occurs in the IS → pIS
transition, as this is when the proton enters the thermodynamic system. To hold the overall driving
force constant—that is, to maintain a constant potential on the RHE scale—the absolute electrode
potential must be shifted negative by an identical amount, of - 59 meV. However, this does not occur
in a discrete jump, but rather more continuously throughout the elementary reaction step, shown
by the green shaded region. The net G shift is also shown on this panel as the black line; we can
see that although it begins and ends at 0 (Nernstian behavior), it is not zero at intermediate stages
of the reaction, and crucially is positive at the point of the reaction barrier (TS), indicating the net
effect of this pH shift will be to kinetically slow the reaction rate, even though the thermodynamics
are by design not affected.
 To formalize, these shifts are applied to a free energy diagram in the bottom panel of the figure,
with the original reaction pathway shown in gray. The proton shift pulls up the free energy diagram,
while the electron shift pulls it down. As we just described, these effects offset over the course of
the entire reaction, but at the barrier the net effect is to push the barrier upward, since all of the
proton shift is felt at this stage, but only ∼ 32 of the electron shift is felt. Thus, we see the barrier
shifting upwards with this positive pH shift, at constant driving force.
 We describe the H2 O-donor case in less detail, and focus on the key differences. In this case,
there is on pIS, but instead there is a pFS (pseudo-final state), as a proton transfers in from the
bulk reservoir to restore the OH – ion to an H2 O molecule. While the electron transfer profile will
be similar across the three stages of this elementary step, the stepwise proton transfer occurs at the
end, at the pFS → FS conversion. The electron’s net effect on G (from a positive pH shift) is similar
to the previous case, but the proton’s affect is not felt until the end of the reaction. Therefore, at
the transition state, the barrier feels the shift from ∼ 13 of the electron’s transfer, but no shift from
the proton’s transfer, as this happens after the TS. In net, this means that the barrier feels a net
downshift from this positive pH change—the exact opposite behavior of when the donor is H3 O+ .

3 Non-local proton-transfer effects in acidic solutions

The results of the previous sections showed that H3 O+ is a more facile proton donor than H2 O,
focusing on the proximate proton donor to the surface. However, the ultimate proton donor is
a bulk H3 O+ (or H2 O) molecule in solution. We properly accounted for the thermodynamics of
this process (which automatically captures the correct kinetics through the free-energy diagrams).
However, this oversimplifies the process, and there are two distinct considerations in capturing
the movement of the proton from the bulk reservoir to the elctrocatalyst surface: (1) there is a
possibility that a prior proton transfer may limit the kinetics of the reaction (that is, may have a
higher barrier than the surface-protonation reaction) and, (2) there is a possibility that the proton
transfer is fully concerted; that is, the lowest-energy mechanism may involve the transfer of multiple
protons at the same time, in a Grotthus-style mechanism. In the former case, a new kinetically
important barrier would arise. In the latter case, the pseudo-initial state (pIS) assumption itself
would be challenged as a workable model of surface protonation. In this part of the project, we study
the dynamics of the proton shuttling to the surface, focusing on protons moving in to the suface
from higher water layers, and ultimately protonating the surface, in a single reaction pathway.
 As discussed earlier, density functional theory (DFT) has been a powerful tool for atomic-scale
predictions, however, its conventional implementation is unsuitable for elucidating electrochemi-
cal reactions due to the grand-canonical properties of electrolysis. Conventional canonical DFT
introduces work function changes during reactions since the charge is maintained at a constant
(that is, neutral) value. [29] However, these changes in work function, which can be interpreted as
changes in the applied electrode potential in electrolysis, hinder accurate mechanism analysis, as
electrolysis is highly influenced by these potentials. These voltage changes are significant, consid-
ering that a few hundred millivolts of potential change in real experiments can lead to substantial
differences in product selectivity. Therefore, a grand-canonical approach which maintains a con-
stant work function, while accomodating various charges during the reaction, offers a more suitable
method for electrochemical analysis. As in other sections, this study employs the Solvated Jellium
Method (SJM) [3], in which the countercharge is placed within a jellium region positioned above
both the metallic slab and the explicit solvent. This setup effectively screens the otherwise strong
electric field by incorporating an implicit solvent, ensuring a more realistic electrochemical envi-
ronment. Beyond controlling the electrode potential, a comprehensive electrochemical model must
also capture the critical role of protons in aqueous environments. Reactions such as the hydrogen
evolution reaction (HER), carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CRR), and oxygen evolution reaction
(OER) all feature proton involvement at the interface, where proton movement is often the initial
trigger. Moreover, understanding how protons behave within the electrochemical double layer at
solid–liquid interfaces is pivotal for grasping the fundamental principles of electrochemistry, since
many unique electrochemical properties arise there. Consequently, elucidating these mechanisms at
the electrode–electrolyte interface is essential for a comprehensive understanding of electrochemical
processes.
 In computational studies of electrochemical reactions involving proton transfer (e.g., the Volmer
reaction), the proton is often modeled using a pseudo-initial state (pIS), as we described earlier
in this report. This pIS assumes the proton resides just above the electrode surface, poised to
adsorb onto it. In contrast, the real initial state (IS) places the proton deeper in the bulk phase,
within a thermodynamic reservoir. For simplicity, however, most simulations begin from the pIS
rather than the more distant, bulk-based IS. In doing so, one implicitly assumes that the proton
transiently occupies the near-surface region on its way from the bulk to the electrode surface—a
notion that, though widely used, has not been rigorously justified. In this study, we examine the
proton’s progression from the bulk to the near-surface region and assess whether the pIS accurately

 To set up this calculation environment, we focused on the Grotthuss mechanism (also known
as proton hopping), which is one of the primary proton transfer mechanisms in aqueous systems.
Compared to the diffusion of a single H3 O+ molecule (or complex, such as H5 O2 + ) through the
solution—which is known as the ‘vehicle’ mechanism—the Grotthuss mechanism involves the relay
of protons between adjacent water molecules through the formation and breaking of hydrogen bonds.
This process explains the remarkably fast diffusion of protons in bulk water, which surpasses the
rate predicted for conventional ion diffusion. We can relate this concept to the Volmer reaction
(proton deposition), where proton transfer occurs through the Grotthuss mechanism in the bulk
aqueous solution, and the transferred proton adsorbs onto the surface by accepting electrons from
the electrode. As we will explain in detail later, even under the Grotthuss mechanism, the pIS may
or may not exist depending on the specific process. Therefore, we aim to examine both possibilities
and investigate how this process influences the kinetics near the electrode surface.
 Throughout this study, we employed proton pathways in a grand canonical system using SJM [3]
combined with machine learning [30] to accelerate saddle point searches. This approach bridges the
gap between atomistic modeling with limited simulation sizes and realistic experimental conditions.
As we will demonstrate, we precisely analyzed charge variations during proton transfer to gain
deeper insights into electrode–electrolyte interfaces and the kinetics of proton pathways.
 Figure 11 illustrates our hypothesis and modeling setups for proton-transfer pathways. Two
possible scenarios in the Grotthuss mechanism are shown in Figures 11a and 11b. In the ‘concerted

 21

proton transfer’ scenario (Figure 11a), all hydrogen atoms involved in the process move simultane-
ously to adjacent water molecules; in this case, the H atom reaches the surface without localizing
as a pIS in the near-surface layer. In contrast, the ‘stepwise proton transfer’ scenario (Figure
11b) involves sequential proton transfer, effectively generating numerous H3 O+ intermediates at
each step. In principle, these H3 O+ intermediates could be stable or unstable, where stability is
characterized by the presence of a local minimum in free-energy space. The stepwise mechanism
would provide support for the plausibility of the pIS, validating simpler reaction-barrier schemes,
while the concerted mechanism would indicate much more sophisticated simulations are necessary
to capture the rate-limiting kinetics. Thus, our goal is to investigate proton transfer by examining
potential intermediates along the pathway, as well as the associated kinetics.
 Our model system consisted of two explicit water layers forming hexagonal ice-like structures, as
shown in Figure 11c, which are often used to model near-surface water configurations. To account
for system diversity, we prepared two metal slabs, Pt and Au, both widely studied metals for the
Volmer reaction but with significant differences in both hydrogen binding energy [31] and intrinsic
reaction barriers [32]. For the local initial state, we placed an extra H atom in the second water
layer (see the green-shaded region in Figure 11d). This choice is based on the findings of Chen
et al., [33] who demonstrated that from the 2nd layer onward, the behavior shifts away from the
near-surface characteristics and aligns more closely with bulk properties. This allows us to explore
a distinct H source separate from the near-surface region.
 We introduced two distinct water configurations: parallel and cross water configurations relative
to the first water layer, while maintaining an ice-like structure in each layer. This setup provides
a foundation for understanding fundamental processes, with the discussion of disordered water
layers left for future work. By varying the alignment in this way, we systematically sampled a
broader range of possible configurations. We then performed grand-canonical DFT calculations
using SJM, with all calculations at a work function of 4.4 eV (unless otherwise stated), roughly
corresponding to 0 V vs. the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE). To expedite these simulations
in charged systems, we integrated SJM with an electronically grand-canonical atomistic machine-
learning workflow employing a double neural network. [30] In this approach, the trained model
rapidly predicts initial structures, which are subsequently refined via DFT to ensure accuracy.

3.1 Unbiased path searches
Our goal is to transfer a proton from the water to the surface, finding a route through the 17
hydrogen atoms present. The combinatorics of this system make brute-force exploration impossible:
there are more than 17! ≈ 350 trillion paths through this water network, so we must adopt some
scheme to find likely paths, preferentially in a way that avoids heavy reliance on a user’s chemical
intuition. To accomplish this, we adopted a philosophy of letting spontaneous energy relaxations
dictate the pathway, as follows:

  1. We start with an excess hydrogen atom (proton) in the outer water layer, as described earlier,
 and relax this structure at constant potential. This is shown in Figure 12a and 12e, and is
 considered the local initial state.

  2. We next assume that any of the 8 hydrogen atoms in the near-surface water layer are candi-
 dates to be surface-adsorbed hydrogen, and in separate simulations we “manually” move each
 of these hydrogens to the nearest surface on-top site (Figure 12b and 12f). We relax each of
 these structures to their final-state geometry (Figure 12c and 12g), and choose the state with
 the lowest energy (Figure 12d and 12f) as the most-favorable final state. This configuration
 is chosen for further exploration.

4 → Image 5). Overall, the transfer progresses from the second water layer to the near-surface
layer, eventually stabilizing temporarily in the near-surface layer during the parallel movement
(Image 2 → Image 4). This transition supports the formation of a pIS, with Images 2, 3, and
4 serving as candidate representations of this state. These findings suggest that proton transfer
follows a stepwise mechanism, generating multiple pIS intermediates along the way, rather than
occurring simultaneously. Moreover, this pathway not only follows a stepwise mechanism but also
progresses in a geometrically rational manner, minimizing disruptions to the existing H2 O network.
Specifically, when the green H atom moves to the lower layer, it selects a position that minimizes
the displacement of surrounding H2 O molecules. Additional pathway variants that involve different
combinations of the four participating protons are provided in Figures 17 and 18. As a result, the
pathway inherently dictates the number and location of participating H atoms by favoring routes
that reduce structural rearrangements and minimize energetic penalties, thereby preserving the
integrity of the wire-like progression.
 In the CI-NEB result graph shown in Figure 14a, the energy barrier for H adsorption (i.e., the
Volmer reaction) is approximately 0.2 eV, aligning with the typical energy barrier for the Volmer
reaction on Pt at a potential of 0.0 V (vs. SHE). We can also see that the barrier is dominated
by the surface protonation step, as opposed to movement within the water layer itself, which is
consistent with typical assumptions made in studying barriers from a near-surface pseudo-initial
state. Interestingly, the right ordinate of the graph reveals a continuous increase in excess electrons
over the course of the elementary reaction, suggesting that electrons are introduced not only to
support surface adsorption but also to provide the necessary charge for the proton’s transition
closer to the surface. This observation confirms that proton transfer wire is intricately coupled with
electron transfer, and therefore can be accelerated by a more neagtive applied potential, consistent
with higer forces on a charge in an increasing field. We further discuss the excess electron increase
during the proton’s vertical migration in the charge analysis section.

the initial guess of the reaction path presented to the MEP algorithm.

3.4 Charge behavior

Before discussing the complete pathway, we first examined how proton motion alone perturbs the
surface charge, employing Gauss’s law. The most prominent displacement is the H movement from
the 2nd to the 1st water layer (Image 1 → Image 2). To quantify how this positive charge affects
the metal charge required to maintain a same workfuction, we adopted the one-dimensional (1-D)
electrostatic model of Ge et al. [34] (Figure 21).

3.5 Solvent restoration
In practice, proton delivery to the surface is part of a continuous cycle wherein protons successively
hop through the solvent network, adsorb onto the surface, and are eventually removed via surface
reactions such as hydrogen evolution or further chemical transformation. For this cycle to proceed
consistently, the structure of the water layer following proton adsorption must reasonably return
to a state resembling the initial interfacial configuration. Although multiple pathways could be
developed by adapting to the new water configurations generated after each H hopping event,
the proton must eventually flip from an H-up to an H-down orientation to approach the surface,
making water flipping an inevitable step in the transfer process. To explore a plausible pathway
that completes the full proton transfer cycle, we extended our investigation to explicitly include
the water flipping process, as illustrated in Figure 25a. By performing constant-potential NEB
calculations between the pIS and the IS′ with an adsorbed proton (Figure 26), we identified that
the transition involves two elementary steps: (1) proton hopping from the 2nd water layer to the
adsorption site, and (2) reorientation of the water molecules to restore the original configuration.
 To distinguish the contribution of each step, we constructed two separate NEB calculations.

water-flipping step, from FS to IS′ . Specifically, NEB1 is similar to the pathway in Figure 3a
but includes slight differences. In Figure 3a, the final state was taken from the PES minimum,
which already involves some water flipping. For NEB1, we regenerated the pIS by adding an extra
proton to the final state to obtain a configuration in which water flipping was minimized, and then

3.6 Implications for psuedo-states
This study confirms the presence and significance of a pIS during proton transfer in aqueous elec-
trochemical environments. By explicitly modeling solvent layers and tracking proton motion via
grand canonical DFT with the SJM, we demonstrated that proton transfer proceeds in a stepwise
Grotthuss manner and transiently stabilizes near the metal surface—justifying the use of pIS in
electrochemical modeling. To construct this study in an unbiased manner, we explored a wide
range of candidate pathways and consistently observed sequential proton transfer, where the pro-
ton initially moves away from the surface before approaching it, even when the reverse direction
was attempted.
 Our charge analysis revealed that proton hopping within the solvent is electronically decoupled
from electron transfer, whereas proton adsorption onto the metal triggers PCET. Application of
Gauss’s law and excess electron tracking confirmed that the metal slab must receive increasing
electron density to maintain constant potential as the positively charged adsorbate approaches the
surface.
 Extending this understanding, we completed the full proton transfer cycle by including a water
flipping step, which is necessary to make complete catalytic cycle. This step incurs an energy barrier
and is shown to be potential-dependent as evidenced by the gradual increase in excess electrons
during the pathway, indicating that applied potential can accelerate not only adsorption but also
the reorientation of water configurations.
 These findings validate the use of a pIS in modeling electrochemical reactions involving proton
transfer and offer deeper insight into charge dynamics under grand canonical conditions. Accord-
ingly, they support the general assumption that the pIS is an essential intermediate in calculat-
ing electrochemical reaction energetics. Furthermore, by accelerating grand canonical simulations
through dual neural network-based machine learning, this framework enables not only enhanced
efficiency and accuracy but also expands the accessible time and spatial scales of simulation, nar-
rowing the gap between theoretical modeling and practical electrochemical systems.

recipe. In summary, the steps of the recipe written compactly are:

  1. Start with known values of ϕ̃ and ϵ̃. (Note ϕ̃ is an iterative approximation, while ϵ̃ is well-
 defined and unchanging.)

 42

 FT
  2. ϕ̃ −−→ ϕ̂

  3. α̂x = ϕ̂ ⊙ Gx , and likewise for y, z.
 IFT
  4. α̂x −−→ α̃x , and likewise for y, z.

  5. β̃ x = ϵ̃ ⊙ α̃x , and likewise for y, z.

  6. γ̂ x = −β̂ x ⊙ Gx , and likewise for y, z.

  7. δ̂ = γ̂ x + γ̂ y + γ̂ z

 IFT
  8. δ̂ −−→ δ̃

δ̃ is the result of the LHS operator on ϕ̃.
