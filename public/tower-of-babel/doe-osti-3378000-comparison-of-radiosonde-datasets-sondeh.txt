ABSTRACT
SondeHub aggregates radiosonde telemetry data uploaded from community-run radiosonde receiver
stations. This radiosonde telemetry dataset is open-source, available to anyone through Amazon S3.
There are also other public radiosonde datasets such as National Centers for Environmental
Information (NCEI)’s Integrated Global Radiosonde Archive (IGRA). While there are many
similarities between the two datasets, there are many differences as well due to the nature of the two
datasets: one is community-run, while the other is managed by a government agency. This report
presents the result of analyzing and comparing the two datasets.

1. INTRODUCTION
A radiosonde is a small instrument package that measures pressure, temperature, and relative
humidity. It often contains Global Positioning System (GPS), which measures the location of the
radiosonde. The change in the location is used to gather data on wind speed and direction. This
instrument is attached under a weather balloon to gather above-ground atmospheric data. Weather
balloons generally fly over 2 hours, up to 20-35 kilometers altitude. While in flight, the radiosonde
gathers and transmits data back to a ground station using a radio transmitter. For consistent data,
these radiosondes are usually launched twice a day at each launch station across the world.
Radiosonde data is used for a variety of applications, including infrasound signal analysis. Infrasound
is low frequency sound, below 20 Hz, that is used to detect chemical and nuclear explosions,
volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, and other sources of interest. Radiosonde data can be incorporated
into atmospheric specification models for infrasound propagation modeling, which estimates where
an infrasound wave will travel. Propagation modeling is important as it allows researchers to better
understand the presence or absence of an infrasound signal following an event of interest. This
report discusses two specific radiosonde datasets in an effort to better understand their utility for
infrasound signal analysis.

1.1. Integrated Global Radiosonde Archive (IGRA)
In the United States, the majority of radiosondes are launched by National Oceanic and
Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) National Weather Service (NWS). National Centers for
Environmental Information (NCEI) is another agency under NOAA’s National Environmental
Satellite, Data, and Information Service (NESDIS) which compiles the data from radiosondes across
the world to create a comprehensive dataset called the Integrated Global Radiosonde Archive
(IGRA; Durre et al., 2016). It also contains historical radiosonde data starting in 1905. Before NCEI
publishes data for IGRA, the data goes through quality control measures, which helps IGRA’s
consistency.

1.2. SondeHub
Since radiosondes send out radio waves, anyone with a receiver can obtain the signal. This signal can
be obtained using publicly available decoders such as radiosonde_auto_rx, an open-source software
available on GitHub (Jessop and Wheeler, n.d.). The decoded data can be uploaded to SondeHub,
an open source repository for radiosonde data (Sondehub Radiosonde Telemetry). If using the
radiosonde_auto_rx software, this will be done automatically. SondeHub aggregates radiosonde data
uploaded from community-run radiosonde receiver stations and can be accessed through Amazon
web services. For easy and less robust access, SondeHub also has a website which shows recently
launched radiosondes on a map, as well as a search feature for a specific radiosonde launches using
their corresponding serial number.

2. COMPARISON

2.1. United States Data Availability
Radiosonde launch availability across the United States is similar in both the SondeHub and IGRA
datasets. Figure 1 shows radiosonde data from SondeHub (yellow) and IGRA (blue). As discussed in
the previous section, most of these radiosondes are launched twice a day from a single station. The
size of the circles in Figure 1 represents the number of radiosonde data entries throughout the year.
The total data count for most of the stations is in the low 700s. In most cases, the number of
launches at each station is identical. Differences can be attributed to either duplicate data or missing
data in SondeHub.

from IGRA. The darker yellow represents the overlap. The size of the circles represents the number of
radiosonde launches recorded at that station.

2.2. Global Data Availability
The story changes when comparing these two datasets across the world. As can be seen in Figure 2,
blue circles (IGRA) are more prevalent than the yellow circles (SondeHub). Outside of United
States, Europe, Australia, and South America, there are more launch stations and radiosondes
captured in IGRA. It is important to note that the size of the circles in Figure 2 is not only scaled by
the number of radiosondes observed, but also by the Mercator projection. This disproportionally
makes some circles much bigger than others. An example of this is the northernmost launch station
of Canada, right next to Greenland. While this circle is much bigger than any of the circles in the
United States, they have approximately the same number of data entries (~730).

2.3. Data Quality
SondeHub receives raw radiosonde data from ground stations that is uploaded via telemetry, which
often contains erroneous frames. The problems may be due to radiosonde or ground station
equipment failure, incorrect encoding/decoding, and/or severe weather. For example, erroneous
data may include unrealistic jumps from normal to very low temperature values, breaking the
measurement continuity. This presents problems for using data with errors in atmospheric models;

 12

models are only as good as the data. If no measures are taken to address this issue, erroneous data
can cause problems. To avoid this problem, IGRA implements quality control measures prior to
publishing the data, providing clear advantages to the end user.

2.4. Number of Frames
The number of frames in each data entry was also analyzed. A frame is every instance that the
radiosonde measures and transmits data, typically once per second during flight. Nearby receivers
can obtain each frame every time it is transmitted. SondeHub contains all raw received data, which
often results in over 10,000 frames. Depending on the use case, this can be impactful as it provides
magnitudes more data to the user and more detail between smaller timeframes. IGRA’s quality
control measures result in most of the frames being dropped, leaving a smaller number of data
points that capture the overall trend of the data. Published IGRA radiosonde data has less than 100
rows, often in the 10s or 20s.

2.5. Radiosonde Ascent and Descent
Radiosondes are tethered to weather ballons, ascending immediately after launch. The weather
balloon bursts around 30-40 km altitude due to lower atmospheric pressure. Once this occurs, the
radiosonde descends with the help of a parachute. IGRA only contains data for the ascent period. In
contrast, SondeHub data captures all transmitted frames and contains both the ascent and descent
periods. If there is a need for longer duration or further distance of atmospheric measurements, this
additional information from SondeHub can be helpful.

3. CONCLUSIONS
The IGRA and SondeHub datasets are similar in that they are both radiosonde datasets. In fact, they
often contain data from the same exact radiosonde. Each dataset provides unique advantages over
the other. Two main advantages of IGRA are that it has data consistently across the world, and that
its data is preprocessed and ready-to-use. In most cases, the IGRA dataset will be sufficient for use
in infrasound signal analysis and propagation modeling. However, the advantage of the SondeHub
dataset is that it contains more detailed information about each launch, including the ascent and
descent data. While this may require more analyst time to preprocess the data, the descent
information could provide a better understanding of the temporal and spatial variations in the
atmosphere. An average atmosphere could be created from these observations and used for
infrasound propagation modeling. Therefore, it is important to use the analysis provided in this
report to choose the best dataset for a project’s objective and scope.
