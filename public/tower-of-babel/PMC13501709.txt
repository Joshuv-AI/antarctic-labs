Cervical cancer screening policy inequality worldwide: a systematic review of disparities in access and implementation
Authors: Ruijun Xin, Meixi Gong, Jiaqi Guo, Jiahui Tian, Xiaomei Wu, Bo Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rising global burden of cervical cancer and persistent inequalities in screening access underscore the need to strengthen implementation. Therefore, we compiled the cervical cancer screening guidelines from different countries/regions and evaluated their implementation status and coverage situation.MethodsIn this study, we conducted comprehensive search of the PubMed, Embase and Web of Science databases (from their establishment to February 1, 2025), websites and the World Health Organization (WHO) database. Studies included in our analysis provide detailed information on the official cervical cancer screening recommendations for each country. All indicators were stratified based on factors such as human development index (HDI), income level, geographical location, WHO regions, disease risk, and age. The systematic review was prospectively registered at PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251087263).ResultsOur study incorporated data from 77 countries/regions, only 62.3% (48/77) of them provided the year of published/updated protocol. Cytology was the primary screening test in 62 (80.5%) of 77 countries. Visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) was the most recommended test in low HDI countries. Relatively few countries had adopted Human papillomavirus (HPV) testing as a primary screening method. Screening coverage was associated with HDI/income (low HDI: 12.43% vs. very high HDI: 49.74%; high-income: 51.68% vs. low income and middle-income countries [LMICs]:34.45%), with high-risk regions (29.88%) significantly lower than other areas (46.76%). Age-stratified analysis showed minimal coverage in the 20–29 and 70–79 groups, while coverage peaked in the 30–39 group. Our results also showed substantial heterogeneity in screening quality indicators (positive rate, further assessment rate, detection rate, and positive predictive value [PPV]) due to factors such as differences in the HDI.ConclusionsThis study highlights global inequities in cervical cancer screening guidelines, particularly in the transition of screening methods among different countries/regions. Global cervical cancer coverage remains significantly below the target of 70%. Accelerate screening strategies, improve screening-diagnosis-treatment accessibility, establish monitoring systems, and strengthen global cooperation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08460-z.

Introduction

As one of the four common cancers among women, cervical cancer is a preventable disease. However, the disease burden continues to rise, with approximately 660,000 new cases in 2022. This burden varies significantly between countries/regions, with approximately 94% of the estimated 350,000 cervical cancer deaths occurring in LMICs [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa, Central America, and Southeast Asia have the highest incidence and mortality rates [1]. In 2020, WHO launched a strategy to reduce cervical cancer incidence below 4/100,000 women/year and narrow disparities [2]. Considering the long pre-cancerous period of cervical cancer [3], coupled with the availability of current screening methods, population-based cervical cancer screening provides significant opportunities to reduce the burden of cervical cancer and to screen and treat before it becomes invasive cervical cancer [4, 5]. Numerous observational epidemiological studies and randomized trials have confirmed that cervical cytology, HPV test, and VIA can significantly reduce cervical cancer incidence and mortality [6–9]. However, the accessibility and utilization of these screening methods vary markedly across different regions [10, 11].

To promote the implementation of screening, many countries/ regions have formulated cervical cancer screening guidelines/suggestions. With the development of new screening methods and changes in the epidemiology of cervical cancer, a number of technical guidelines and suggestions have been formulated and updated over the years [12]. Monitoring cervical cancer screening is crucial to evaluate obstacles, facilitators, variations, and differences between methods and systems.

Screening coverage rate is one of the core indicators reflecting the detection capabilities provided by a country/region for primary screening. Currently, most current studies assessing the progress of eliminating cervical cancer focus mainly on this single indicator [13, 14]. Achieving target coverage, however, results from complex policy environments and implementation processes. It is profoundly influenced by: a country/region’s screening strategies (e.g., screening methods/intervals); organizational models (e.g., invitation mechanisms); and the completeness of subsequent treatment pathways [13, 15]. Understanding the policy framework at the national level and its correlation with implementation effects (such as participation rate, treatment connection) is crucial for optimizing resource allocation and accelerating the achievement of global goals.

This study comprehensively synthesizes global cervical cancer screening evidence to characterize policy-to-quality inequities and identify barriers to equity. By comparing findings with WHO targets, we identified critical gaps and proposed evidence-based improvement strategies.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was conducted according to the PRISMA checklist and was prospectively registered in the National Institute for Health Research International PROSPERO, (No. CRD420251087263, July 4, 2025).

Search strategy

Firstly, we systematically searched three major databases: PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase, with the search period spanning from the establishment of the databases to 2025. Based on the PICO principle, we constructed the search formula, combining the MeSH terms and free words. The core term was “uterine cervical neoplasms/diagnosis” [MeSH], supplemented by keywords such as “mass screening” and “early detection”. (full strategy detailed in eTable 1) Secondly, we systematically accessed the official websites of WHO and the EU Public Health Platform (CanScreen5 [16] and NordScreen [17]). We conducted a comprehensive analysis of the global cervical cancer guidelines and the estimated values of their coverage (from administrative data and survey data) in the CanScreen5 database. In response to the characteristics of screening practices in the United States, we obtained data for the period 2019 to 2023 from the ‘National Breast and Cervical Cancer Early Detection Program’ (NBCCEDP) at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) [18], focusing on extracting behavioral data from women participating in cervical cancer screening.

When multiple sources are available, we prioritize administrative data from countries demonstrating organized planning and accurate registration, as well as data that is comprehensive. Two independent researchers extract the data, resolving any discrepancies through discussion with a third party when necessary.

Data extraction and indicator definition

Based on retrieved literature, government websites, and official documents, we extracted quantifiable metrics for each country/region’s screening program—including invitation numbers, participation counts, and screening uptake figures—stratified by demographic age and available screening interval data. The data on cervical cancer coverage rates included in the literature come from official estimates and survey data. And all indicators were defined according to CanScreen5.Even the terms used in the qualitative data collection form are clearly defined on the website to ensure the consistency of data collection [19]. Some key definitions are presented in eTable 2.

Data analysis

We conducted a systematic analysis of all included indicators. To ensure comparability across the heterogeneous data from 77 countries/regions, our analysis adhered to the standardized procedures and terminology provided by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)’s CanScreen5 platform [19]. This approach provided unified definitions for key metrics—including the target population, screening coverage, and other performance indicators—thereby minimizing heterogeneity arising from differing national reporting practices. To quantify the global inequality situation, the indicators was stratified, and comparisons were made in the following dimensions: the HDI level [20], the World Bank income level [21], WHO regions, cervical cancer risk categories [1] and age groups.

For countries/regions reporting quantitative cervical cancer screening data, we performed age-stratified analyses to identify coverage barriers across key demographic groups. All coverage estimates were calculated with 95% CI to ensure reliability. Analyses were conducted using RStudio (v4.4.3).

Results

Study selection and study characteristics

According to the search criteria set in databases, a total of 126 studies were screened. After removing duplicates, 22 articles were excluded. The titles and abstracts of the remaining 104 articles were screened, and another 76 studies were excluded. The full texts of the remaining 28 articles were evaluated, and finally 7 studies were included. At the same time, the WHO, CanScreen5, NordScreen and NHIS databases were systematically collected, and a total of 82 national/regional data were identified. After removing duplicates, 77 national/regional data remained. We compared the data of the 7 studies with the 77 national/regional data, selected the higher-quality and more representative data, and finally we determined the official cervical cancer screening recommendations and related data for 77 national regions. Screening project implementation indicators

As for the number of cervical cancer screening invitations reported in eTable 6 and eTable 7, only 28.6% of the countries/regions provided relevant data; further analysis of the invitation coverage rate showed that only 27.3% of the countries/regions worldwide reported the invitation rate, with Sweden topping the list at 131.3%, and Hungary at only 15.2%; the participation rate: only 28.6% of the countries/regions reported data; Denmark had the highest rate at 67.7%, Croatia had the lowest at 10.3% (Fig. 2). Regarding the screening coverage rate, 54.5% of countries/regions provided screening coverage data, but there are significant differences: countries in the low HDI group reported the highest data (80%), while those in the high HDI group reported the lowest (28.6%); in terms of the scale of screening, 92.2% of the countries/regions focused their screening scale within the range of ≤ 1.1 million.

The screening coverage rate is determined by the invitation rate and the participation rate. Our data observed three types of problems in this chain. The first type is the weak invitation stage, where 37% of countries/regions (such as Finland, Estonia) have an invitation rate of 0 for some age groups, directly resulting in a 0-screening coverage rate. The second type is the inhibition of the participation stage, where Belgium and Republic of Korea (for the 20–29 age group) have an invitation rate of over 60%, but the participation rate is significantly low. The third type is the difficulty in the conversion from participation to screen, where Denmark (for the 40–49 age group) shows high invitation rate and high participation rate, but the screening coverage rate is only 53.3% (eFigure 2- eFigure 18).

The difference in screening coverage levels

There are significant differences in the screening coverage rate of cervical cancer screening among different countries/regions (eTable 8 and Fig. 3). By analyzing the screening coverage rate of those countries that have available data, we found that the average screening coverage rate was 42.67% (95%CI:34.51%-50.83%). Compared to upper-middle-income (UMI) and lower-middle-income (LMI) countries, high-income countries have a higher screening coverage rate. The disease risk dimension shows that the screening coverage in high-risk areas is lower than that in other regions (29.88% vs. 46.76%).

Age stratification

We further focused on the age-stratified situation of these indicators. In many countries/regions, the invitation rate, participation rate, and screening coverage are relatively low among aged 20–29 and 70–79, while they are comparatively high among those aged 30–39, 40–49 and 50–59 (eFigure 19). The overall screening participation rate showed an inverted U-shaped distribution. There were significant differences in the screening coverage rates of the same age group among different countries/regions.

Performance indicators

The cytological screening rate ranges from 1.85% in Kenya to 93.2% in Hungary. Some performances are unable to provide data for further evaluation. The estimated participation rate for further evaluation varies from 15.0% in Mexico to 98.8% in Finland. The detection rate and PPV can only be evaluated for those providing final histopathological diagnosis data. (eFigure 20).

Discussions

Global inequities in screening coverage

High-quality cervical cancer disease management is crucial for achieving the global goal of eliminating cervical cancer. However, there are still significant disparities in the current levels of cervical cancer disease management and screening coverage among countries with different HDI. In this context, this study, as the first comprehensive global assessment of cervical cancer screening, integrates extensive data from 77 countries/regions and systematically addresses the practical challenges. Through this comprehensive analysis of the evidence, we have revealed significant global differences in terms of the policy framework, implementation process, screening coverage, accessibility of services, and performance quality. The most significant finding reveals alarming global inequalities in screening coverage, strongly correlated with socioeconomic development, disease risk, and geographic location. Our results indicate persistent global gaps in resource allocation, screening capacity and policy implementation.

The screening process: from invitation to coverage

The invitation rate and participation rate jointly affect the screening coverage rate. United States, UK, Canada, and many European Union countries have established systematic invitation mechanisms, but due to language barriers, cultural stigma, and low risk perception, the actual participation rate of young and minority women is significantly lower than the invitation rate, revealing the deficiencies of the existing system in terms of fairness and cultural adaptability [22]. A systematic review supports this view and further emphasizes that these barriers do not exist in isolation but are interrelated with other factors such as geography, economy, and system trust, jointly leading to the insufficient screening participation rate [23]. Therefore, improving the screening coverage rate not only involves increasing the participation rate of the “covered population”, but more importantly, extending screening services to countries and populations that have not been covered by the system [24]. Therefore, strengthening population registration and establishing an effective invitation mechanism are crucial for improving the coverage rate [25]. Nevertheless, high invitation rate and participation rate do not always translate into high coverage rate (as seen in Denmark), indicating a loss phenomenon between consent and the completion of screening. This may involve issues such as referral delays, geographical barriers, or failure in follow-up [23, 26–27].

Adoption and barriers of screening methods

Our research findings revealed that countries encounter numerous challenges and significant inequalities in the process of achieving the goal of “70% of women receiving high-quality screening”. Although cervical cancer screening has been proven to be an effective means of reducing incidence and mortality rates [28–31], surprisingly, there are still many countries/regions where the database does not provide guidelines for cervical cancer screening. WHO strongly recommends high-sensitivity HPV testing as the initial screening method, as it has higher sensitivity and potential for longer screening intervals, which can improve efficiency and cost-effectiveness [12, 32]. However, our study showed that cytological examinations still dominate globally, while the prevalence of HPV testing as the initial screening is extremely low (only 20.8%). VIA, as a low-cost and visual method, remains a practical screening option in areas with extremely scarce resources where cytology or HPV testing cannot be implemented. This is because it requires very few devices and can be operated by trained non-professional health workers. However, its poor repeatability and accuracy in detecting precancerous lesions limit the long-term effectiveness of related projects [33, 34]. Cytology remains suitable for regions with long-established, well-organized screening systems and strict quality assurance. However, its substantial requirements for laboratory infrastructure and professional personnel make it difficult to sustain in remote or underfunded areas [35]. In contrast, HPV testing offers higher sensitivity and potential for longer screening intervals, but the feasibility of transitioning to HPV testing in low-resource environments remains challenging due to cost, infrastructure requirements, and need for training [36, 37]. Our research indicates that in LMICs, only a few countries recommend HPV testing. Accurate, reliable, user-friendly, and cost-effective testing methods are essential for the successful implementation of HPV screening.

Socioeconomic disparities and service accessibility

Our research showed that the overall free rate of the screening service throughout the entire process was extremely low, significantly weakening the overall effectiveness of the screening program. This could lead to a situation where, even if a positive result is detected, patients may discontinue treatment due to an inability to bear the subsequent diagnostic or treatment costs [38]. This rendered the screening efforts futile and made it extremely difficult to achieve the WHO goal of “90% of patients receiving treatment.

Screening coverage was closely related to HDI, income level, geographical region, and disease risk. Our results showed that the coverage rate in countries/regions with a low HDI was only 12.43%, and the coverage rate in low-income countries/regions was 2.30%. In contrast, the coverage rates in countries with a very high HDI and high-income countries were much higher. This gap is driven by limited access to health resources combined with low health literacy. In resource-scarce settings, these two factors reinforce each other, perpetuating a cycle of disadvantage [39]. On the one hand, cervical cancer screening strategies impose high requirements on human resources, testing equipment, and technological platforms. Consequently, lower-middle income countries encounter systematic challenges in this regard [40, 41]. Countries with low HDI, such as Ethiopia, struggle to establish nationwide screening network due to the lack of medical resources [42]. In countries with very high HDI, such as Sweden, the coverage rate has been significantly improved through organized invitation mechanisms.

On the other hand, there are also significant differences in the health literacy of populations in different regions. The screening compliance rate in high-income countries can be more than three times that in low- and middle-income countries. The coverage rate in the European region reaches over 80%, while that in the African region is only about 6% [39]. It is worth noting that the low-compliance group is often the group with the highest risk of cervical cancer [43]. This is jointly influenced by multiple social determinants such as low educational levels, cultural and religious barriers, insufficient awareness of the disease, and residence in rural or marginal areas [44]. These factors further limit the acceptance of screening services by this group, thus exacerbating the unequal distribution of the cervical cancer disease burden globally. Notably, there are also significant differences between continents and cervical cancer risk regions [45, 46]. Coverage is higher in Europe and the Americas but significantly lower in high-risk areas of Africa and Asia. This disparity reflects vast inequalities in resource allocation and screening capacity.

Age-specific challenges

Our research observed that within the age stratification, participation rate among the core age group of 30–59 years was relatively high, while screening coverage for young women aged 20–29 and elderly women aged 70 and above was severely insufficient, and even completely absent. Young women participate insufficiently because of low personal-risk awareness or fear of discomfort and embarrassment, whereas elderly women are overlooked—due to perceptions of reduced risk, mobility limitations, lack of targeted services, and project-imposed upper age limits—resulting in similarly low participation [47–50]. In fact, the risk of cervical cancer in 85-year-old women is dozens of times that in 70-year-old women, indicating that elderly women need enhanced monitoring [51]. This demographic trend highlights the importance of targeted interventions for different age groups, such as expanding the scope of routine screening, developing outreach programs suitable for the elderly, and integrating artificial intelligence-based tools to improve the detection rate among the elderly population. If this shift is not addressed, the elimination goal may be difficult to achieve even if the overall system capacity is sufficient.

Limitations in quality monitoring and data reporting

This research has revealed critical gaps in the quality monitoring of global cervical cancer screening programs. Disparities in HDI levels, varied screening implementation methods, and a lack of data from some countries result in heterogeneous and sparse datasets. This creates significant obstacles for cross-national comparisons, internal quality assessment and improvement of programs, and the accurate tracking of global elimination progress. The absence of a standardized, systematic quality monitoring system represents the core obstacle to enhancing screening effectiveness. Future studies should prioritize the adoption of standardized indicator definitions and reporting frameworks across countries to facilitate valid comparisons. Additionally, actively integrating data from underrepresented regions (e.g., Oceania and Asia) and employing modeling techniques to estimate coverage in data-poor settings would be crucial to bridge these evidence gaps.

Our study did not include the estimates from Oceania and most Asian countries/regions. This might reflect the limited availability of cervical cancer screening tests in these regions, the lack of population-based screening programs, or the failure of these countries/regions to provide data in the CanScreen5 database in a timely manner. Nevertheless, the cost-effectiveness of cervical cancer screening tests has been confirmed in some parts of Asia [52–54]. In China, most cervical cancer screening programs are mainly conducted at the provincial and municipal levels. Recent literature [55] shows that the 2025 target of 50% cervical cancer screening coverage has been surpassed for women aged 30–49 years and 35–64 years by 2023–2024. Nevertheless, screening uptake remains suboptimal, particularly in rural areas, certain provincial level administrative divisions (PLADs), and among women of lower socioeconomic status.

Strengths and limitations

The key advantage of this study lies in integrating literature searches with official data sources. This approach ensures data representativeness through nationally/regionally validated databases and provides a solid foundation for systematically analyzing the impact of cervical cancer screening policies and guidelines on actual utilization. However, this study also has certain limitations. Although we calculated according to a unified indicator definition, inconsistencies in denominator definitions for screening coverage across countries/regions—such as variations in the target population’s age range—introduce heterogeneity in regional indicators. Furthermore, as the included data primarily derive from databases, the potential for recall bias, reporting biases, and overestimation or underestimation of screening coverage cannot be ruled out.

The findings of this study align with previous studies on cervical cancer screening coverage in 202 countries [13], highlighting persistent concerns about global inequality and implementation challenges in cervical cancer screening. This study innovatively integrated policies and screening indicators, and for the first time conducted a comprehensive analysis of these factors. The results show that the global cervical cancer screening coverage, especially in LMICs and high-risk areas, still has a gap from the target set by WHO. The study points out that the main obstacles to achieving the global elimination goal include: the update and implementation of guidelines, the lag in the transformation of screening methods, the insufficient coverage of young and elderly populations, and the weak monitoring of accessibility and quality of the screening-diagnosis-treatment chain. Our study used systematic national/regional-level data to provide robust evidence for these challenges.
