Overcoming the thermo-refractive noise limit for a full-spectrum Hertz-linewidth microcomb
Authors: Chao Zhou, Runlin Miao, Pan Han, Mingxin Yang, Xing Zou, Ke Wei, Ke Yin, Tian Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChip-scale microcombs combining high repetition rates and Hertz-level linewidths are critical for precision measurements and parallel coherent communications. Self-injection locking (SIL) has been widely used but faces a fundamental limitation: the microresonator must simultaneously generate the comb and suppress noise, creating a coherence ceiling set by thermo-refractive noise (TRN). This imposes a fundamental trade-off between repetition rate and linewidth. Here, we transcend this barrier with a cascaded architecture that decouples these two functions. First, SIL to a microresonator produces a soliton comb, defining the repetition rate while releasing the intracavity pump. Second, optical injection locking targets this pump via a fiber delay, extending the system’s effective coherence time to overcome the TRN-imposed linewidth ceiling. This functional decoupling suppresses noise by ~70 dB, yielding a 100-GHz microcomb with uniform 3–15 Hz Lorentzian linewidth across all comb teeth. Our modular design enables scalable and coherent microcombs for next-generation metrology, sensing, and communications.Subject terms: Frequency combs, Mode-locked lasers

Introduction

Optical frequency combs, which produce spectra of equally spaced, phase-coherent laser lines, have become foundational tools in precision metrology, spectroscopy, and communications. The recent development of chip-scale microcombs1–3, based on dissipative Kerr solitons in high-quality-factor (high-Q) microresonators, promises to transfer these capabilities from laboratory benches to portable systems for applications such as optical atomic clocks4,5, coherent light detection and ranging (LiDAR)6–8, and parallel coherent communications9–12. These applications demand microcombs that simultaneously exhibit two critical characteristics: a high repetition rate (typically >50 GHz) to support high-speed data channels or precise sampling, and Hertz-level narrow linewidths across all comb teeth to ensure long coherence times and ultralow phase noise. The conventional routes to generating such microcombs rely on pumping high-Q microresonators with external, ultra-stable continuous-wave (CW) fiber lasers13–15, transferring the lasers’ frequency stability to the combs via Kerr nonlinearity. While effective, this method often relies on external, ultra-stable continuous-wave (CW) fiber lasers16–19, or in some advanced demonstrations like on chip-scale Brillouin lasers20, which nevertheless require their own complex stabilization and pumping schemes. The pursuit of full chip-scale integration motivates alternative approaches, including PZT tuning21 and heater-kick technique22.

Another significant advance toward system miniaturization emerged with self-injection locking (SIL, Fig. 1a, upper panel)23–25, where a compact semiconductor laser (e.g., a DFB laser) is directly coupled to a high-Q microresonator. The coherent feedback, primarily from intracavity Rayleigh backscattering, locks the laser to a cavity resonance, producing a noise reduction factor (NRF) scaling as NRF∝Q/QLD2 (Fig. 1c, d), where Q and QLD are the quality factors of the microresonator and the DFB pump laser. Despite its integration advantages, the SIL architecture faces a fundamental constraint: the microresonator must simultaneously generate the frequency comb and suppress its noise. This dual role inherently ties the final coherence to the cavity’s intrinsic thermo-refractive noise (TRN)26–29, creating a performance limit that cannot be surpassed within the SIL framework. Recent efforts to mitigate TRN by using ultrahigh-Q (~108) resonators with large bending radii and reduced thickness have achieved Hertz-level linewidths30,31, yet only at the cost of manufacturability and free spectral range (typically <30 GHz), without overcoming the fundamental TRN limit. Common-mode noise suppression is also taken into consideration to bring the noise down below the TRN limit32, yet this approach primarily targets microwave signals rather than optical comb linewidth.

Optical-injection locking (OIL, Fig. 1a, lower panel) has recently emerged as a potential route to circumvent TRN33. By feeding a selected comb line back into the microresonator through a long external delay, the effective photon lifetime is extended, enabling a NRF scaling as NRF∝lg(1+τpf/τr) (Fig. 1c, d), where τpf and τr are the effective photon lifetimes in the feedback loop and the microresonator, respectively. This extended lifetime enables the realization of sub-Hertz Lorentzian linewidths33,34. Nevertheless, in conventional OIL implementation, the pump laser remains an external continuous-wave source with fixed frequency noise. Consequently, only the injection-locked sideband is significantly narrowed. The pump line remains unaltered and other comb lines follow an elastic tape model, given by S(μ)=Ss1−μ/m2+Soμ/m2, where S(μ), Ss(△νs) and S0(△νo) are the frequency noises (linewidths) of an arbitrary comb (mode μ), the pump (mode 0) and the locked sideband (mode m), respectively35,36 (see yellow-blue curve in Fig. 1d). This results in a highly non-uniform distribution where comb lines far from the locked sideband are not narrowed and can even broaden, severely restricting the number of usable comb lines in broadband applications.

In this work, we introduce a cascaded injection locking architecture (Fig. 1b) that overcomes these limitations by decoupling comb generation from coherence optimization. The first stage employs SIL in a foundry-compatible Si₃N₄ microresonator to generate a 100-GHz soliton comb, simultaneously providing initial noise suppression and, crucially, releasing the intracavity pump from the external fixation. The second stage implements pump-OIL by filtering and feeding the released pump comb back into the resonator through a fiber delay. This extends the photon lifetime beyond the microcavity’s intrinsic limit, thereby overcoming the microresonator’s TRN-imposed linewidth ceiling (Fig. 1d). As the pump drives the parametric gain, its refined Hertz-level coherence is uniformly imprinted onto all comb teeth, producing a fully coherent microcomb across the entire spectrum.

Results

Noise performance of the SIL and cascaded SIL&Pump-OIL microcomb

We developed a compact hybrid-integrated soliton microcomb source by butt-coupling a commercial distributed-feedback (DFB) laser to a high-Q Si₃N₄ microresonator (Q ≈ 5.46 × 10⁶)37. We employ thick-Si3N4 waveguides (800 nm thickness) due to their strong confinement, anomalous dispersion, and compatibility with foundry manufacturing, which together enable stable soliton formation and high-repetition-rate operation essential for practical microcomb sources. The device was packaged within a standard butterfly module along with a thermal phase shifter and a thermoelectric cooler for phase control and frequency stabilization (Fig. 2a, see Supplementary Note 1 and 2 for details). This highly integrated design not only facilitates portability but also enhances mechanical and thermal stability, which is critical for maintaining narrow-linewidth operation under practical conditions. Using the packaged device, we first triggered SIL by carefully tuning the laser current and phase shifter, generating single-soliton microcombs at 100 GHz repetition rate with a 3 dB bandwidth of approximately 12.9 nm, corresponding to a Fourier-transform-limited pulse duration of 195 fs (Fig. 2b, see Supplementary Note 3 for SIL microcomb details). The comb frequency noise performance Sν(f) was characterized using a delayed self-heterodyne interferometer with a 200-meter fiber delay, where the spikes observed in the high-frequency region (above 1 MHz, Fig. 2d) originate from the discrete resonances of the interferometer itself rather than the laser noise. Having acquired Sν(f), the Lorentzian (Δν1) and integral (Δν2) linewidths can then be derived accordingly (see Method for details).

Substituting these parameters yields a predicted NRF of approximately 41 dB. Experimentally, as illustrated in Fig. 2d, the SIL state significantly suppresses the noise by ~40 dB across the measured offset frequency range, demonstrating excellent agreement with theoretical prediction. Through this SIL process, the Lorentzian linewidth is narrowed from an initial 80.6 kHz to 31 Hz, while the integral linewidth is reduced from 803.5 kHz to 32.7 kHz. However, further linewidth narrowing encounters a fundamental limitation imposed by the thermo-refractive noise (TRN, see Method for detailed calculations) of the microresonator, which ultimately constrains the achievable frequency stability.

To overcome this constraint, we implemented a cascaded locking architecture where OIL follows the initial SIL stage. Specifically, an arbitrary but precise comb line (mode μ, counted from the pump line) of the SIL output is selected by a TBPF and fed back into the microresonator through an external fiber delay (See Supplementary Note 4 for experimental setup). This approach effectively extends the photon lifetime beyond the microresonator’s intrinsic limit, enabling superior frequency stabilization. In our implementation, we have the flexibility to select either the pump line (pump-OIL, μ = 0, λ = 1548.8 nm) or any sideband comb line (SB-OIL, μ ≠ 0) for feedback, each offering distinct characteristics. We firstly focus on pump-OIL (Fig. 2c), the alternative SB-OIL will be discussed in next section.

Experimentally, as shown in Fig. 2d, pump-OIL suppresses the frequency noise of the feedback pump line (μ = 0) by a further ~30 dB relative to the SIL state, precisely matching the theoretical prediction. The cascaded SIL & pump-OIL strategy yields remarkable results: a pump Lorentzian linewidth of 3.1 Hz (corresponding to a white noise floor of 1 Hz²/Hz) and an integral linewidth of 1.1 kHz, achieving performance that rivals commercial fiber lasers such as the Koheras Basik E15 (Fig. 2d). This breakthrough can be attributed to two complementary mechanisms40. First, photons re-entering from the external feedback loop maintain phase-coherence with the intracavity field, thereby extending the effective photon lifetime and mitigating quantum noise. Second, the fiber feedback circuit increases the overall optical mode volume, distributing thermal fluctuations over a larger effective volume. This combined effect produces a NRF that allows the system to transcend the TRN limit of the 100-GHz microresonator, reaching a TRN noise level typically associated with a 1-GHz resonator (see detail discussions in Supplementary Note 5). Importantly, the suppression of pump frequency noise does not extend to the repetition-rate phase noise (see Supplementary Note 7 for details). This suggests that the pump noise is not the limiting factor for the repetition rate.

The refined coherence of the pump line is, crucially, imprinted uniformly across the entire comb spectrum. This uniform coherence transfer originates from the four-wave mixing process underlying microcomb generation, as evidenced by the similar noise levels of representative comb teeth at μ = ±16 (Fig. 2d). These teeth exhibit noise levels comparable to the pump, with noise floors confined to a narrow range of 1 ~ 5 Hz2/Hz, corresponding to Lorentzian linewidths of 3–15 Hz. The uniform coherence enhancement is further quantified in Fig. 2e, which presents the integral linewidth of comb modes from μ = −20 to 20 before and after pump-OIL (at optimal feedback ratio of η = 0.577). All modes exhibit integral linewidths of ~1.5 kHz—a 22-fold improvement over the SIL-only state, and critically, all lie well below the ~4 kHz TRN limit. Notably, even at a lower feedback ratio down to η = 0.025, where the single-soliton state dominates the comb output (Section 3 and Supplementary Note 6), the linewidth still approaches the TRN limit. This uniform coherence preservation across the spectrum stands in sharp contrast to conventional CW-pumped microcombs, where Raman-induced noise transduction causes parabolic linewidth deterioration at high powers35. This difference arises from two factors. The first is that SIL microcombs inherently exhibit uniform linewidths26,30,41, where the tightly locked pump transfers its refined coherence uniformly to all comb teeth via four‑wave mixing. The second is that our low on‑chip pump power (7.5 mW) yields a broad 195 fs soliton pulse width, more than three times that of typical high‑power combs (~62 fs)15. Because the Raman self‑frequency shift scales inversely with the fourth power of the pulse width, this broader pulse suppresses Raman‑induced noise transduction by a factor of ~98, eliminating the dominant mechanism for mode‑dependent broadening42.

Cascaded SIL&SB-OIL microcomb performance

We now turn to the dynamics and limitations of sideband optical-injection locking (SB-OIL, Fig. 3a) implemented after the initial SIL stage. In this configuration, an arbitrary sideband comb line (e.g., μ = +16, ±8) is selectively fed back into the microresonator, and the frequency noise is measured across different comb lines of the multi-soliton microcomb, as illustrated in Fig. 3b. Although the feedback sideband itself (Fig. 3c, μ = +16) successfully achieves a remarkably low noise floor of 1 Hz²/Hz—comparable to pump-OIL—the noise propagation across the comb spectrum adheres to the elastic tape model35,36. The pump line (μ = 0) exhibits noise similarly to but, indeed slightly below that of the SIL-only state, which resembles the behavior of CW-pumped OIL microcomb shown in ref. 33, where the pump noise is strictly pinned to the external light source. Nevertheless, such reduced pump noise suggests that the SIL-released pump is not fully pinned and may be partially stabilized by the distant sideband’s OIL effect—a fine dynamic meriting future study. Moving further away to the remote sideband (μ = –16), the frequency noise deteriorates as expected, even exceeding that of the original SIL state.

The integral linewidth results for SB-OIL, included in Fig. 2e, further corroborate this non-uniform narrowing. While the directly locked sideband and its immediate neighbors experience significant linewidth reduction, comb lines farther from the injection point exhibit pronounced broadening, with those at the spectral extremes even exceeding their linewidths in the SIL state. The flexibility of SB-OIL is further demonstrated by locking onto different comb lines (Fig. 3b), all of which achieve similarly low noise levels at low-offset frequencies (Fig. 3d). At high offset frequencies (>100 kHz), however, a slight increase in noise is observed for μ = +8 and –8 compared to μ = +16, likely due to the increased sensitivity of SB-OIL to environmental perturbations as well as its inherently more fragile soliton state compared to pump-OIL (Supplementary Note 9).

Both theoretical and experimental analyses confirm that OIL, whether applied to the pump or a sideband, effectively extends the photon lifetime and reduces frequency noise. Critical distinctions, however, dictate their practical utility. First, although both the Pump-OIL and SB-OIL links in our current experiments employ an EDFA to compensate for the substantial insertion loss along the feedback path (~13.5 dB total loss, see Supplementary Note 4), Pump-OIL can eliminate this requirement in future fully on-chip integrated systems. As discussed in Supplementary Note10, with the reduced insertion loss of an integrated feedback path (~5.4 dB), the feedback ratio η naturally falls within the optimal regime (see discussion below) by directly leveraging the high-power residual pump line without any amplification. In stark contrast, SB-OIL fundamentally requires optical amplification regardless of integration level. This is because sideband comb lines inherently possess lower power (generated via FWM) and suffer from additional coupling losses when extracted from and reinjected into the microresonator. The estimated total loss of ~18.7 dB (Supplementary Note 4), therefore, remains prohibitive even in a fully integrated scenario. This fundamental asymmetry renders SB-OIL more susceptible to environmental perturbations and less amenable to full on-chip integration. Second, and most critically, since the pump acts as the primary driver for the parametric gain, its refined coherence is uniformly imprinted onto all nascent comb teeth via the FWM process. In contrast, SB-OIL results in a non-uniform, parabolic linewidth distribution governed by the elastic tape model. Therefore, Pump-OIL emerges as the optimal strategy for generating a fully coherent, broadband microcomb where each tooth can be usable for precision applications.

OIL microcomb dynamics

We proceed to examine how the feedback power ratio η governs the coherence and dynamical state of the pump-OIL microcomb (see Supplementary Note 9 and 11 for the SB-OIL case, which exhibits similar but more fragile dynamics). Figure 4a presents the measured pump-delayed self-heterodyne beating spectra with increasing η, revealing three distinct dynamical regimes. In regime ① (η < 0.126), weak feedback progressively suppresses frequency noise, leading to continuous linewidth narrowing. In regime ② (0.126 <η < 0.577), the line is optimally narrowed, reaching its optimum at η = 0.577. Further increasing the feedback strength into regime ③ (η > 0.577) induces coherence collapse43, characterized by the emergence of multiple stochastic oscillation frequencies and a severe degradation of phase coherence (Fig. 4b). Notably, in contrast to conventional lasers, our cascaded system exhibits exceptional resilience to optical feedback, maintaining single-mode operation up to η = –2.4 dB (~57.7%)—a 37.6 dB improvement over conventional DFB lasers (generally –40 dB)43,44. This enhanced stability originates from the significantly extended photon lifetime within the coupled laser-microresonator-fiber loop system, which effectively averages out phase perturbations introduced by the feedback.

To gain deeper insight into the underlying soliton dynamics, we performed numerical simulations based on a modified Lugiato-Lefever equation incorporating both optical injection and backscattering (Supplementary Note 11). The simulated intracavity field evolution (Fig. 4c) corroborates the three experimental regimes observed experimentally. In regime ① (η < 0.07), the single-soliton state remains stable, represented by a single soliton at a polar angle of θ = 0°. In regime ② (0.07 <η < 0.45), moderate feedback power triggers a spontaneous transition from a single-soliton to a multi-soliton state, with the soliton distribution becoming increasingly uncertain near the upper boundary. Finally, within regime ③ (η > 0.45), strong feedback drives the system into a chaotic state, erasing the coherent soliton structure. Beyond the feedback strength, the phase of the reinjected field may also play a role in the soliton dynamics in principle. Simulations indicate that the feedback phase can shift the boundaries between the three dynamical regimes (Supplementary Note 11). However, this phase dependence was not clearly resolved in our experiments, likely due to phase-averaging effects induced by ambient perturbations. Nonetheless, by combining theoretical and experimental insights, we identify a robust operating window for η < 0.35 where the system remains free from coherence collapse regardless of phase.

Figure 4d–e shows the evolution of the measured pump line’s frequency noise and integral linewidth with η. The integral linewidth decreases monotonically from 32 kHz (SIL state) to an optimum of 1.1 kHz as η increases, until the onset of coherence collapse at η > 0.577, beyond which it degrades abruptly. A key finding is that the system has already surpassed the intrinsic TRN limit at a feedback ratio as low as η ≈ 0.025. Subsequently, across a broad range from η = 0.025 to 0.577, the noise remains consistently suppressed below the TRN limit, demonstrating the system’s robustness to variations in feedback strength. However, as η increases to 0.700, noise rises at high offset frequencies (>1 MHz) due to proximity to the external cavity resonance at 5.6 MHz. A further increase to η = 0.862 leads to a comprehensive collapse of coherence. Overall, this dynamics investigation identifies a robust operational window, regime ②, where the comb linewidth is minimized and the overall coherence is maximized. The same frequency noise dynamics hold for all other comb lines, which achieve the same linewidth reduction (see Supplementary Note 8 for details).

Discussion

Our work establishes a new architecture for integrated microcombs through functional modularization. In this scheme, the SIL stage is primarily responsible for soliton generation—setting the comb’s repetition rate and spectral span—while providing initial noise suppression limited by the cavity’s intrinsic TRN. The subsequent OIL stage then acts as a specialized noise reduction unit, operating on the already released pump comb to extend the coherence time and overcome the TRN-imposed linewidth ceiling of the microresonator. This functional decoupling allows the use of standard, foundry-compatible thick-Si₃N₄ microresonators with high FSR for comb generation, while delegating the ultimate coherence optimization to the external feedback loop. Crucially, the spatial separation of these two modules enables independent control over key parameters such as free spectral range and linewidth, ensuring broad applicability across diverse microresonator platforms and repetition rates.

As summarized in Fig. 5 and Table 1, our system uniquely combines a high repetition rate (100 GHz) with uniform, Hertz-level coherence (3 ~ 15 Hz Lorentzian linewidth) across all comb teeth—performance that surpasses many existing integrated systems with small microresonator radius. Unlike SIL schemes using thick Si3N426,28,45,46, or integrated external-cavity lasers47–50, our architecture overcomes the TRN barrier for the entire comb and reaches the well-known fiber laser coherence of the Koheras Basik E15. Our scheme also possesses the advantage of low cost and has the potential to replace commercially available expensive fiber lasers in the future. While ultrahigh-Q thin-film Si3N4 resonators20,21,51–55 can reach Hertz-level linewidths, they do so only at the expense of low repetition rates (typically <30 GHz), and fundamentally, cannot surpass their intrinsic TRN limit30,31,56,57. Our approach, in contrast, simultaneously possesses Hertz-level coherence, high FSR, and foundry-compatible thick Si₃N₄ waveguides, and may be applied to the thin Si₃N₄ waveguides architectures as well. The cascaded scheme also holds critical advantages over other OIL implementations. For instance, while sideband-OIL can achieve exceptional localized coherence (e.g., 0.31 Hz in ref. 33), it is inherently restricted by the elastic tape model, resulting in non-uniform linewidths spanning over three orders of magnitude across the comb. Our cascaded strategy, by contrast, ensures uniform coherence throughout the spectrum. Furthermore, by eliminating the external master laser, our system achieves a level of integration and operational simplicity unattainable with conventional OIL.

Looking forward, our proposed heterogeneous integration scheme (Supplementary Fig. 14) outlines a viable path toward a fully on-chip system, addressing the primary obstacle—the fiber-based feedback loop. This architecture leverages recent advances in multiple domains: III–V DFB lasers and Si₃N₄ waveguides can be heterogeneously integrated as demonstrated in refs. 28, 51, providing a compact SIL platform. A Vernier dual-ring resonator would serve a dual function—as a tunable comb filter to select the pump line and as a feedback power adjuster via integrated thermal actuators, thereby replacing the discrete TBPF and electronic variable optical attenuator (EVOA). Crucially, the external fiber delay can be supplanted by a low-loss, on-chip spiral waveguide, with ref. 50 having already demonstrated a 10-meter-long Si₃N₄ coil that provides sufficient photon lifetime extension for effective noise suppression. This comprehensive integration strategy would not only drastically reduce the system’s size, weight, power, and cost (SWaP-C), but also eliminate fiber-induced environmental sensitivity, thereby ensuring superior long-term phase stability. This architecture can also be extended to other photonic platforms like thin-film Si₃N₄, thin-film lithium niobate, etc. Furthermore, on-chip amplification via erbium-doped Si₃N₄58 or parametric waveguides59,60 could boost the microcomb output power. The inherent flexibility of this platform also enables potential migration to visible wavelengths61–64, opening exciting opportunities in atomic physics and quantum information processing65.

Methods

Characterization method for microcomb linewidth

The linewidth of the dissipative Kerr soliton (DKS) comb was quantified experimentally via the delayed self-heterodyne technique, a high-precision method by introducing of a short optical delay to circumvent the stringent coherence-length requirements of conventional heterodyne interferometry. After optical amplification by an erbium-doped fiber amplifier, the DKS comb was split into two interferometric arms via a 1 × 2 fiber-optic coupler. In the reference arm, a frequency shift of 40 MHz was imposed by an acousto-optic modulator, whereas the signal arm was passed through a 200-m fiber loop to introduce a precise temporal delay. A polarization controller was adjusted to align the polarization states of the two beams, thereby maximizing the fringe visibility of the interference. The two beams were then recombined using a second 1 × 2 coupler. The resulting heterodyne beat signal was detected by a balanced photodetector and analyzed by a phase noise analyzer, which measures the differential phase noise between the two interfering beams.
