Executive Summary

Hydrogen production via two-step thermochemical water splitting redox cycles using
nonstoichiometric redox-active metal oxides has the potential to dramatically increase
fuel production rates. At moderate-to-low water splitting temperatures, surface reaction
kinetics co-limit the process. In such cases, stable and high surface area
microstructures that allow exploitation of the full thermodynamic potential of the
materials are essential as is tight thermal integration of the reactor module. This
project’s goals were the development of novel nonstoichiometric perovskite oxides with
high stability and favorable thermodynamic and kinetic properties, to optimize their
microstructure for maximizing the fuel productivity, and to build a prototype reactor train
system (RTS) comprising at least one reactor to meet specific performance targets: (1)
capable of an in-house solar thermochemical hydrogen (STCH) productivity ≥ 12 mL g-1
for stable continuous operation ≥ 20 cycles; and (2) demonstration of scalable solar
fuels production at practical solar reactor level in an industrial-scale concentrated solar
tower (CST) using developed perovskites to achieve a hydrogen production rate ≥ 1 g h -
1. A summary of the major achievements through the first budget period follows:

 • Synthesized single-phase cubic CTM5050 perovskite and Ca(TixMn1-x)O3-δ
 (CTMx, 0.4 ≤ x ≤ 0.8) compositions and prepared porous monolith samples with
 different sintered porosity (40%-66%) and particle sizes.
 • Showed that above 1150 °C, the water splitting behavior was largely limited by
 the gas phase thermodynamics rather than material kinetics.
 • Identified compositions in the Ca(TixMn1-x)O3-δ system which maintained a fuel
 productivity >12 mL H2/g oxide after prolonged cycling using a thermal reduction
 temperature of 1350°C and an oxidation (water splitting) temperature of 1200 °C.
 • Studied different design concepts and converged on a ceramic multi-tubular
 reactor design to be used in an RTS.
 • Developed a high-fidelity Multiphysics model of a representative reactor tube
 undergoing RTS cycling and used it to study reactor design, operation
 parameters, and to predict performance metrics.
 • Investigated available materials for high temperature reactor components using
 thermomechanical stress and failure analysis, selected alumina as a suitable
 reactor shell material.
 • Built a bench-top test setup for component testing, de-risking and model
 validation.
 • Developed a system level model to analyze the RTS performance and the
 dependencies of material choices and subsystem configurations. This model was
 further extended in collaboration with NREL to conduct a technoeconomic
 analysis of the hydrogen production system.
 • Initiated the process development for 3D printing of porous perovskite
 components for the integration into the RTS reactor.

Background
Solar thermochemical hydrogen (STCH) production via two-step thermochemical water
splitting redox cycles using nonstoichiometric redox-active metal oxides has emerged
as a promising approach for large-scale generation of solar fuels. The STCH technology
offers benefits such as full utilization of the entire solar spectrum and strong
compatibility with the existing energy infrastructures and will play a significant role in
hydrogen production technologies for meeting DOE’s target cost of hydrogen at ≤ $2/kg.
Recent advances in perovskites significantly lower the operation temperature (i.e., 1350
°C versus 1500 °C in ceria), while maintaining fuel productivity. Despite this promise,
perovskites have not achieved the required metrics for ultimately reaching the DOE
target cost of $1/kg, largely due to slow hydrogen release from the reduced oxide upon
oxidation. Given this barrier, there has been much less effort to innovate in systems and
reactor design tailored for the reduced temperature operation of perovskite materials.

Here we propose to build on the novel developments of Mn 4+ based perovskites from a
recently completed EERE program at Northwestern University (NU) and implement it in
a reactor design pioneered at MIT, with solar field testing at Heliogen. The PI, Dr. Xin
Qian, led the experimental studies during the NU EERE program. Two key findings
have informed the activities proposed here: (1) CaTi0.5Mn0.5O3-δ (CTM55) yields an
unprecedented combination of the fuel productivity (10.0 mL g -1) and fuel production
rate (0.166 mL g-1 min-1) by using a reduction temperature of just 1350 °C; and (2) the
thermodynamic properties of Sr(TixMn1-x)O3-δ (0 < x < 1) (unpublished) can be
systematically varied by changing B-site cation chemistry. Furthermore, extensive
studies by Co-PI Dr. Haile have revealed that oxygen and hydrogen production rates
are governed largely by the magnitude of the thermodynamic driving forces for the
respective reactions, rather than material kinetics. Tailoring thermodynamic properties
has the potential to dramatically increase fuel production rates. At moderate-to-low
water splitting temperatures, surface reaction kinetics co-limit the process. In such
cases, stable and high surface area microstructures that allow exploitation of the full
thermodynamic potential of the materials are essential.

Project Objective
The proposed efforts aim to develop novel nonstoichiometric perovskite oxides with high
stability and favorable thermodynamic and kinetic properties, to optimize microstructure
for maximizing the fuel productivity, and to build a prototype reactor train system (RTS)
comprising at least one reactor to meet specific performance targets: (1) capable of an
in-house solar thermochemical hydrogen (STCH) productivity ≥ 12 mL g-1 for stable
continuous operation ≥ 20 cycles; and (2) demonstration of scalable solar fuels
production at practical solar reactor level in an industrial-scale concentrated solar tower
(CST) using developed perovskites to achieve a hydrogen production rate ≥ 1 g h-1.
Specific objectives are to rationally identify and optimize perovskite oxides and to
demonstrate their capability in practical reactors for scalable solar fuels production by
STCH technology.

2.2: Assessment of phase stability boundaries and structural evolution behavior
of Gen1 perovskites under STCH relevant conditions (M1-M9)
Stability of CaTixMn1-xO3-δ for x= 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8 was investigated via a
combination of quenching and thermogravimetric analysis experiments. Going forward
in this report, materials will be identified by their Ti:Mn ratio, such as CTM4060 for
CaTi0.4Mn0.6O3-δ, CTM6040 for CaTi0.6Mn0.4O3-δ, and so on. Quenching experiments
focused on CTM4060 and are summarized in Table 2.2.1. In preliminary experiments,
porous monoliths were held at their specified temperature and gas conditions for at
least an hour before quenching to room temperature. Ex-situ X-ray diffraction revealed
that at 1200°C and pO2 = 3 x 10-4 atm, CTM4060 remains stable in the cubic perovskite
phase. When pO2 was lowered to 9 x 10-5 atm, phase separation occurred with a Ti-rich
cubic perovskite phase, CaTiO3 (PDF no. 98-000-3911, Pbnm 62), and Mn-rich rock salt
phase, Ca0.5Mn0.5O (PDF no. 01-080-8034, Fm-3m 225).

In further quenching experiments to clarify the stability conditions of CTM4060, the
same porous monolith was held for two hours at the specified temperature and gas
conditions before quenching. After phase separation, samples were annealed at 1000°C
for 2 hrs in synthetic air. Ex-situ X-ray diffraction revealed complete recovery of
materials to the orthogonal phase present at ambient conditions.

Task 3.0: Preliminary Design of Reactor Train System (M1-M15)
A thermomechanical COMSOL multiphysics model was developed for analyzing the
impact that different reactor geometries, configurations, and materials have on the
mechanical robustness, thermal performance and hydrogen productivity of the reactor.
This model was used to test different design configurations and converge on a reactor
design for in-depth analysis and testing.

Figure 3.0.1 shows a schematic of the first design iteration based on a windowed cavity
that was developed with further details and analyzed in COMSOL. The following list is a
summarized layout of key design considerations and options to be investigated via
simulations and component-level experiments for individual reactors of the reactor train
system (RTS), with a focus on novel design challenges compared to state-of-the-art
STCH receiver-reactors (RR). We also identified and narrowed down a list of structural
materials that are viable under RTS operating conditions.

 Figure 3.0.1. Schematic of the first design iteration based on a windowed cavity.

 1. Infrared spectrum: RTS reactors are irradiated by infrared (IR) radiation from an
 emitter and not the solar spectrum. RR’s use quartz windows, which have high
 transmissivity to the solar spectrum (> 85% transmission, < 10% absorption).
 However, quartz is not suitable for the RTS because it absorbs strongly in the IR
 spectrum. High absorption can raise quartz temperature, which is challenging
 because quartz devitrifies at >1200 °C and melts at ~1700 °C. Consequently, two
 alternative window materials were considered in this study: partially transmissive
 sapphire windows, and opaque silicon carbide absorber-emitter. The SiC

                                       Page 20 of 63

 Saint-Gobain Ceramics & Plastics, Inc.

 absorber-emitter takes heat from the emitter, heats up, and irradiates the redox
 material on the other side. In the following text, a ‘window’ can refer to both
 sapphire and SiC.

2. Uncooled window: While quartz is highly transmissive in the solar spectrum,
 materials with similar transmissivity in the IR spectrum are not available to the
 best of our knowledge. For example, a 4mm thick sapphire window has ~60%
 transmissivity and ~20% absorptivity in the IR spectrum. If the sapphire window
 were actively cooled, for every 3 kJ heat transmitted to the redox material, 1 kJ
 would be lost to active cooling. In comparison, quartz would only lose 1 kJ per 8
 kJ of transmitted heat. Thus, an uncooled sapphire window was considered for
 the preliminary design. Sapphire maintains high strength and chemical inertness
 at high temperatures (~1400 °C).

3. High temperature window flange: Previous work shows that an uncooled
 sapphire (or SiC) window would reach roughly the same temperature as the
 redox material. Thus, the flange holding the window must be capable of
 withstanding temperatures ~1400 °C. The gas environment of the
 thermochemical reactor changes from low-pressure oxygen during reduction, to
 steam-hydrogen mixtures during oxidation. The flange must also be chemically
 resistant to these species at high temperatures. SiC is a promising flange
 material because of its high-temperature strength and resistance to oxidation.

4. High temperature window-flange gaskets: The gas environment of the
 thermochemical reactor must be isolated from the surroundings: e.g. to prevent
 reoxidation of the redox material with air as it cools down. Moreover, the total
 pressure of the reactor may vary during a cycle: e.g. vacuum of up to 0.1 mbar to
 remove oxygen during reduction. Vacuum is particularly challenging, because: (i)
 a good seal is needed at the flange-window interface, and (ii) the flange material
 must now be capable of withstanding a pressure difference between the inside
 and outside of the reactor. The RTS reactor with sapphire windows would need
 high-temperature gaskets for sealing. Graphite is a viable gasket material,
 although it would have to be shielded from the oxidizing gases within the reactor.
 Preliminary screening shows that a SiC flange would have enough strength to
 withstand 1 bar pressure difference.

5. No re-radiation losses: RTS reactors in the indirectly irradiated configuration do
 not suffer from re-radiation losses that RR’s do (radiation emitted by RTS reactor
 is returned to emitter source). This relaxes some design constraints of
 concentrated solar receivers like small aperture and deeper absorbing cavity.

6. Lower incoming radiative heat flux: The hot emitter emits lower heat flux than
 concentrated solar (black body at 1500°C emits ~500 suns, while state-of-the-art
 RR’s operate at > 1500 suns). This necessitates larger window area for the same
 thermal power. In other words, the weight of redox material per unit window area
 (kg-redox/m2-window) must be lower than RR’s. As mentioned above, unlike RR,

                                   Page 21 of 63

 Saint-Gobain Ceramics & Plastics, Inc.

 low redox loading per unit window area will not incur higher re-radiation losses. In
 combination this leads to RTS reactor designs with large windows and shallow
 redox cavities.

The initial design was tested with the thermomechanical COMSOL undergoing a
heating/ cooling cycle in which the emitter fluctuates between 1600°C and 800°C over
an hour-long period and the ceria is assumed to be in thermodynamic equilibrium at
10Pa oxygen partial pressure. The simulations assumed a single reactor with no heat
recovery, as would likely be the case in prototype testing. Non-heating energy
requirements like pumping were not considered in this analysis. Several variants of the
model were tested in order to determine which system parameters are most crucial for
the effective operation of an indirectly irradiated reactor (see results in Figure 3.0.2).

Figure 3.0.2. Left: a description of the design variants studied. Right: Energy input
during reactor heating stages, divided into domain-level sensible heat, reduction
reaction, and losses.

It was found that the baseline configuration of the initial design should be able to
produce 2.47 gH2/hr, which would satisfy the requirements of the prototype. However,
heat losses during the heating phase due to radiation to the environment and active
cooling were upwards of ~70%. Cases 1 and 2, which increased the size of structural
components, resulted in more energy being used for sensible heating of these
components and less hydrogen production. Raising the temperature of the active
cooling boundary and eliminating cooling (cases 3 and 4) both had beneficial effects on
heat losses and productivity, although fully eliminating cooling resulted in unsuitably
high temperatures in the steel shell. In case 5, replacing the window with opaque SiC
was detrimental for performance, but some hydrogen was still produced. Cooling of the
entire flange instead of just the connection point to the steel in case 6 resulted in the
highest cooling losses. Cases 7 and 8 brought in the reflectors to eliminate irradiation of
the heat shield, which significantly reduced heat losses and overall heat input. Case 8
used a hypothetical window with better transmissivity than sapphire and performed

                                       Page 22 of 63

 Saint-Gobain Ceramics & Plastics, Inc.

significantly better, highlighting the unavoidable downside of using an IR-transmissive
window. The following takeaways from the simulations of the design variants showed
that there is significant room for performance improvement:
 1. Active cooling of the reactor results in high heat losses and a drop in hydrogen
 productivity. This should be avoided if possible.
 2. Decreasing the size of non-redox components has beneficial effects due to
 reducing the thermal inertia of the system.
 3. The irradiation of non-redox components also results in high heat losses and
 should be kept to a minimum.

Another downside of this design is the complexity and design feasibility. The
window/flange interface would require bolting and sealing, and gasketing solutions for
oxidizing environments are only rated to ~1050°C. We determined that the amount of
cooling required to keep the window/ flange interface at or below this temperature would
be a significant detriment for system performance. Additionally, it was unclear how such
a reactor would be scaled up beyond the size of a prototype, due to structural
limitations.

Based on the above findings, a second design iteration of the RTS reactor prototype
featuring a ceramic pressure vessel that aims to reduce energy losses from cooling was
proposed. The second major reactor design iteration consists of a pressure vessel
made of SiC. and addresses the key findings from above:
 1. Due to the SiC design, active cooling is not needed. Previously, the metal
 pressure vessel shell, and potentially the sealing/gasketing points, would have
 required cooling. The gasketing point in the second iteration design can be
 placed at the back of the pressure vessel, separated from the hot areas by
 insulation.
 2. The thermal inertia of non-redox components is greatly reduced due to the
 smaller form factor of the pressure vessel, which can more closely surround the
 redox material due to its heat tolerance.
 3. Reflectors are used to impose a high view factor between the emitter and the
 pressure vessel.

This design also offers a large improvement in engineering complexity due to the
elimination of the window/flange/shell interfaces and the active cooling infrastructure.
The design is also modular, and scale-up can be achieved by stacking the SiC vessels
adjacent to each other, offering a more promising outlook for larger-scale designs after
the prototype phase.

Thermomechanical design studies show that thinner walls result in lower tensile
thermomechanical stresses in the SiC and are also favorable for heat transfer. The
vessel is shaped with domed ends to address thermomechanical stresses in the SiC
vessel. Since the SiC does not transmit IR radiation and instead acts as an absorber-
emitter, heating of the ceria is less rapid. However, due to the benefits mentioned
above, the design can outperform the best variants of the earlier design in productivity

                                       Page 23 of 63

 Saint-Gobain Ceramics & Plastics, Inc.

while requiring far less heat input. Observed heat losses were in the ~20% range, which
far outperformed the reactors of the first design iteration.

Figure 3.0.3 shows cross-sectional schematic of the refined design based on the
preliminary results above. The design is based around a sintered silicon carbide vessel
as a combined absorber-emitter interface and structural component sealed by a metal
baseplate at the back of the reactor, which would clamp to the flange of the SiC vessel.
Metal is a much more versatile material for manufacturing a baseplate component that
would need various ports for gas inlets and outlets, sensors, and thermocouples. Using
a removable, clamped baseplate also allows for easy assembly, loading and removal of
internal redox material and insulation. The metal would be a high-temperature alloy
such as stainless steel or Inconel and the clamped SiC to metal connection needs
careful design and dimensioning. Our studies show that by adding sufficient inner
insulation the gasketing temperatures stay within the limits of commercially available
sealing solutions.

The developed Multiphysics model also allows to investigate the impact of redox
material geometry within the reactor. We ran seven different geometries against each
other and evaluated them based on the maximum and minimum average temperatures
they were able to attain, as well as the resultant stresses they induced in the SiC shell.
The redox material in our model is still assumed to be ceria RPC, as not all material
data was available yet for CTM, however we assume that similar trends would emerge
with any porous redox material. Redox material weight is kept constant at 5 kg. The
imposed emitter temperature profile was 700-1600°C with four 15-minute segments of
low-temperature, linear heat up, high-temperature, and linear cooldown. Reported in
Figure 3.0.4 are results from the 10th cycle.

Results show that case 2 and the relatively similar case 6 have high thermal inertia and
are unable to attain high temperatures. However, performance of this sort of geometry
might be improved if a porosity gradient were used. Cases 1, 3, and 5 reached higher
temperatures with middle-of-the-pack temperature swings. Cases 4 and 7 performed
best thermally, achieving temperatures of 1441°C and 1445°C, respectively. Case 4
attained a notably lower minimum temperature to achieve the largest temperature
swing, demonstrating the viability of using cut-out channels to aid radiative heat
transfer. As for the mechanical results, the block geometries 2 and 6 performed worse
than geometries with less material abutting the side of the SiC vessel, as they seemed
to induce higher thermal gradients at the part of the vessel where the front dome meets
the cylinder sidewalls. This underscores the importance of surrounding insulation &
redox material geometry on thermal gradients and stresses.

To further investigate the structural stability of the reactor vessel the thermomechanical
Multiphysics model was extended with a probabilistic fast-fracture failure model to
estimate the chance of failure in the critical SiC vessel. This model is needed because
unlike metals, ceramics do not repeatably fail at a given critical stress, but fracture due
to random flaws in the material. The specific model implemented, known as Shetty’s
Mixed-Mode Criteria (penny-shaped crack), has been chosen as it is considered to be
the most conservative and accurate fast-fracture failure model. The implementation was
validated by comparing it to test cases from literature and it was found that the results
match within 1%.

The inclusion of the probabilistic failure model allows to investigate the optimal shape
for the absorber-emitter end of the ceramic shell of the reactor, or “face” of the reactor.
A flat reactor face geometry was compared to elliptically domed faces with varying radii.

The flat-faced geometry consistently achieved lower maximum tensile stress values and
therefore lower component failure probability than the domed geometries, even under

external pressure. This indicates that stresses resulting from pressure forces are less
consequential than thermal expansion stresses under our operating conditions. Figure

Flat geometries also outperformed domed geometries thermally, achieving faster heat
transfer to the reactive material and less overall heat losses. This is due to the higher
view factor to the emitter/ opposing reactor instead of the insulation, as well as having
less ceramic material and therefore less sensible heating needed. Consultation with
manufacturing experts showed both geometries to be feasible with a minimum edge
radius between the face and the cylinder of 1”. However, results from material tests
showed that SiC is susceptible to damage from active oxidation, which occurs at high
temperatures and low oxygen partial pressures, even when a pre-oxidized protective
layer is grown beforehand. It was determined that SiC is therefore not a viable material
for the reactor shell, as the interior surface would lose mass and its strength degrades
in time. In response to these findings, alumina was investigated as potential alternative
for the shell.

In order to compare alumina to SiC, a simulation of a 12cm radius cup shaped reactor
undergoing thermal cycling from 700 to 1500°C over an hour-long period was run. With
SiC as a shell material, the max stress was 45 MPa and the corresponding failure
probability was very low (0.0002%), whereas using alumina resulted in a max stress of
128 MPa and an unacceptably high failure probability of nearly 60%, one of the reasons
why initially SiC was considered. To address this issue, a third iteration of the reactor
design was introduced aiming to significantly reduce mechanical stresses to a point
where alumina can be viable. This reactor geometry consists of an array of smaller
tubular shells, collectively housing the same amount of redox material as the previous
design, irradiated from the sides instead of one large flat-faced shell irradiated at the
front. A schematic comparison of two design concepts can be found in Figure 3.0.6.

more uniformly. This is beneficial for productivity, efficiency, and could enable faster
cycling times. Mullite was also investigated in these studies and performed similarly to
alumina.

In the limit of Rohmic being dominated by the resistance of an infinitely thick electrolyte

Newman equation, expressed as Rohmic = 1/(2*d*𝜎𝑌𝑆𝑍 ), where d is the microelectrode

expected, though only at relatively small microelectrode diameters; the adherence to this
scaling behavior at large diameters is somewhat puzzling. In the absence of Pt, the ohmic
resistance is relatively insensitive to microelectrode radius, reflecting the limited electronic
conductivity. The non-ohmic resistance follows a scaling of close to -2 (Figure 6.1.4.d) for
both samples, implying that the electrochemical reaction area scales with the electrode
area. The factor of three difference between the two results is roughly consistent with the
loss of ~ 80% of the active area in the case of the electrode with the Pt grid.

7.1: Reactor design and modeling for perovskite materials (M16-M19)
The thermomechanical modeling capabilities developed for task 3.0 were expanded to
more accurately simulate the heat transfer that the tubes of the multi-tubular reactor
would be subjected to in RTS cycling. One representative tube of the multi-tubular
reactor was modeled using a 2D axisymmetric geometry in COMSOL Multiphysics
under the assumption that this tube is surrounded by identical tubes. A diagram of the

                                      Page 44 of 63

 Saint-Gobain Ceramics & Plastics, Inc.

simulated geometry is shown in Figure 7.1.1.a. While ceria RPC (reticulated porous
ceramic) was used as the redox material in this modeling study, it can be replaced in
the future with CTM or other perovskites. Fluid flow and chemical kinetics were
excluded, and a thermodynamic equilibrium model was used to model oxygen evolution
with complete re-oxidation assumed. Heat transfer to the opposing emitter, reactor, and
heat sink tubes was simulated by a boundary condition (boundary 2 in Figure 7.1.1.a)
surrounding the tube with an effective emissivity value that accounts for the 2D
axisymmetric assumption, with circumferential differences demonstrated in our models
to be negligible.

To model the heat recovery phase of the RTS cycle, an iterative method was
implemented to determine the temperature profiles of the surrounding boundaries. The
method assumes a very large number of reactors and works on the principle that
because the tubes only view each other, the temperature that the tubes are exposed to
going from the cool zone to the hot zone is the time inverse of the tube surface
temperatures going from the hot zone to the cool zone. Using this method, the boundary
temperatures reliably converged to a temperature profile that would be physically
expected in RTS operation, enabling the simulation of RTS cycling using a finite
element model of just one tube in one reactor. This convergence is shown in Figure
7.1.1.b.

Overview of the Integrated System-Level Thermodynamic Model
In addition to the detailed Multiphysics model presented above, we also created a
system level model to analyze the RTS performance and the dependencies of material
choices and subsystem configurations.

                                      Page 48 of 63

 Saint-Gobain Ceramics & Plastics, Inc.

The following pages summarize the fully integrated thermodynamic model that links
redox material thermodynamics, reactor configuration, auxiliary subsystems, and waste-
heat recovery into a single physics-based framework for evaluating thermochemical
hydrogen production. The purpose of the model is to establish a consistent and
quantitative way to determine how a material’s intrinsic thermodynamics influence
subsystem requirements, optimal operating conditions, and overall heat-to-fuel
efficiency. Because the model connects every subsystem through physically rigorous
mass and energy balances, it reveals system-level constraints that cannot be captured
by analyzing materials or components independently.

Figure 7.1.5. A schematic of the TCH system considered in this study, comprising of the
reactor subsystem, O2-removal subsystem, a power cycle and a H2 separation and
steam handing subsystem.

The governing structure of the model is zero dimensional and equilibrium based as
shown in Figure 7.1.5. The redox material is represented through its standard enthalpy
and entropy of reduction as functions of the non-stoichiometry δ. These quantities,
which originate from experimentally validated measurements and defect-chemistry fits,
determine the equilibrium extent of reduction and oxidation at specified temperatures
and oxygen partial pressures. For each material, the model solves the Gibbs condition
for reduction at high temperature and low oxygen partial pressure and similarly
determines equilibrium re-oxidation in contact with steam at an optimized oxidation
temperature. The difference in equilibrium δ values defines the hydrogen yield per cycle
per unit mass. Because both the magnitude and slope of ΔH⁰(δ) and ΔS⁰(δ) govern the
oxidation driving force, this module ensures that downstream subsystem requirements
arise directly from material thermodynamics.

The reactor subsystem is represented by two isothermal reaction steps. Reduction
occurs at a fixed reduction temperature and oxidation at a separate oxidation
temperature chosen by the optimizer. The temperature difference between these two

                                      Page 49 of 63

 Saint-Gobain Ceramics & Plastics, Inc.

steps determines the sensible heat release during cooling and the sensible heat input
required during reheating. To capture this effect accurately, the model includes a solid-
state heat recovery factor that quantifies the fraction of sensible heat released during
cooling that can be transferred back during reheating. This representation allows the
model to distinguish between reactor designs that enable high solid–solid heat
recuperation, such as fixed-bed flow configurations, and designs that offer stronger
oxidation driving forces but limited solid-state heat exchange, such as counter-current
flow oxidation in fluidized arrangements. In this way, the reactor configuration becomes
a parameter that can either amplify or mitigate the thermodynamic behavior of different
materials.

The reduction step requires maintaining a very low oxygen partial pressure, and for this
reason the model includes an explicit oxygen-removal subsystem. In the baseline
configuration, staged vacuum pumps are modeled using multi-stage compression
relations and linked to the power cycle through their electrical work consumption. The
required vacuum level depends directly on the equilibrium δ at the reduction
temperature, and therefore on the material’s reduction thermodynamics. Materials that
demand deeper reduction to achieve significant δ swings require more vacuum work,
which appears directly in the system energy balance. Alternative oxygen-removal
technologies, including thermochemical and electrochemical oxygen pumping, are
included within the model framework but the staged vacuum pump serves as the
baseline for comparison.

The H2 separation and steam-handling subsystem is strongly shaped by the oxidation
thermodynamics of the redox material. During oxidation, steam reacts to form hydrogen
at a conversion ratio dictated by the material’s δ-dependent thermodynamics. When this
conversion is low, large quantities of steam must be heated, vaporized, and circulated
to push the oxidation reaction forward. The model evaluates these requirements by
explicitly calculating steam generation energy, steam reheating energy, vaporization of
make-up water, and the latent heat associated with condensing the steam–hydrogen
mixture after oxidation. The baseline separation process is simple condensation,
although the model also includes a mechanical vapor recompression heat pump that
can recover much of the latent heat and return it to the steam-generation process. This
subsystem becomes especially important when evaluating perovskites, which generally
possess weaker oxidation thermodynamics and therefore require higher steam inputs.

The final component of the integrated model is the power cycle and waste-heat
utilization network. The oxidation step releases both exothermic and sensible heat,
portions of which can be converted to electricity using a supercritical carbon dioxide
cycle with a fixed conversion efficiency. This electricity can be used to power the
vacuum pumps and, if applied, the vapor recompression system. The waste heat from
the oxidation reactor and from the power cycle is divided into intermediate-temperature
and low-temperature streams, both of which can be used for steam preheating or steam
generation, depending on their quality. The model specifies two utilization fractions that
determine how much of this waste heat can be recovered in practice. These fractions

                                       Page 50 of 63

influence the net external heat requirement and therefore strongly affect the final heat-
to-fuel efficiency.

All subsystems are tightly coupled through mass and energy balances. The model
therefore performs a simultaneous optimization of the oxidation temperature, the
reduction oxygen partial pressure, and the re-oxidation extent. The goal of the
optimization is to minimize the required external heat input relative to the higher heating
value of the hydrogen produced. The result is a complete set of operating conditions
and subsystem energy flows for every material and configuration.

Figure 7.1.6. (a) Integrated system efficiency and (b) H2 productivity vs. materials at

The parametric results in Figure 7.1.6 clearly demonstrate why perovskites do not
achieve the efficiencies of ceria within this integrated framework. Under a consistent
baseline configuration that includes vacuum pumping for oxygen removal, simple
condensation for hydrogen separation, and representative heat recovery assumptions,
ceria reaches an efficiency close to 20% at a reduction temperature of 1500°C. The
same conditions applied to LCM40 and related perovskites yield efficiencies closer to
10%.

In addition to being less efficient, perovskites are also not superior in productivity when
the system is operated at its optimal efficiency. Although perovskites can in principle
reach larger δ swings than ceria, the steam demand associated with fully exploiting this
larger swing becomes energetically prohibitive. As the optimizer seeks the maximum
system efficiency, it reduces the extent of re-oxidation for perovskites to avoid extreme
steam loads. This reduces the effective δ swing to values that are comparable to, and in
some cases lower than, those of ceria. As a result, under system-optimal operating
conditions, perovskites do not deliver greater hydrogen yield per cycle per unit mass

                                       Page 51 of 63

than ceria, despite their theoretical capacity to do so. This finding contradicts the
common assumption that perovskites inherently offer superior productivity. The
integrated model shows that productivity cannot be evaluated in isolation. The material
must be operated under conditions that balance steam requirements, vacuum work, and
reheating demands. Under those conditions, perovskites are not productivity
advantaged.

The difference arises from the oxidation step as shown in Figure 7.1.7. Ceria maintains
a strong oxidation driving force at moderate oxidation temperatures, which leads to high
steam-to-hydrogen conversion ratios and therefore modest steam-generation
requirements. Perovskites, by contrast, exhibit much weaker oxidation driving forces.
Their equilibrium conversion ratios remain low, forcing the supply of very large excess
steam. The associated steam generation and reheating loads overwhelm the energetic
gains from their higher reduction extent, resulting in significantly lower heat-to-fuel
efficiency.

Even when all subsystems are upgraded to their most favorable configurations, which
include counter-current oxidation, substantial solid-state heat recovery, and latent-heat
recycling through a vapor recompression unit, ceria achieves system efficiencies in
excess of 40%, while perovskites such as LCM40 remain close to 19%. Although this
represents an improvement relative to the baseline, the gap remains substantial. The
model’s structure makes it clear that this behavior is driven by fundamental
thermodynamics rather than operational choices. The intrinsic δ-dependent enthalpy
and entropy of oxidation in perovskites require far more steam to achieve the same
conversion, and the energetic cost of handling this steam dominates the overall thermal
demand.

The unified representation of redox thermodynamics, subsystem technologies, and
reactor-level heat recovery means that the model also serves as a natural foundation for
technoeconomic analysis. All major contributors to operating energy demand are
already present, including vacuum work, steam generation, solid reheating, and latent
heat rejection. Once capital costs, reactor-scale heat losses, and kinetics-driven cycle
times are incorporated, the model can be used to compute levelized cost of hydrogen
and establish the optimal tradeoffs between efficiency, productivity, material selection,
and subsystem design. Its modular structure, which cleanly separates materials,
reactors, and auxiliary subsystems, allows technoeconomic metrics to be inserted
without altering the fundamental physics. For this reason, the integrated thermodynamic
model provides not only a rigorous basis for comparing redox materials but also a
framework for the subsequent economic evaluation of advanced thermochemical
hydrogen systems. The resulting technoeconomic analysis of the hydrogen production
system has been developed and conducted in close collaboration with NREL.

Path Forward
There are many opportunities to utilize the knowledge and experience gained from this
short project to inform other projects in the domain of fuels and chemicals production
using thermochemical or hybrid approaches. The material screening approach was
shown to be valuable for quick down-selection and is applicable to TCH materials in
general. The final modular reactor design can be applied to any industrial high-
temperature heat source such as Small Modular Reactors.

Intermediate-fidelity, thermodynamics-based, system-level models in which active
materials’ energy-relevant properties are embedded but also integrated with other unit
processes (compression/expansion, gas separation, heating/cooling, etc.) are essential
in selecting the best candidate material (and other system components) for overall
performance including efficiency, productivity, emissions, etc. When ported into techno
economic models, these system models are essential for estimating the levelized cost
of the product. In both, stand-alone material properties are essential but not sufficient.
These models are also important for determining optimal operating conditions which
inform and guide reactor design and other components.

At the reactor level, high-fidelity multiphysics models are essential for screening design
candidates while incorporating redox active materials and others used for reactor shell,
insulation, etc., operating at the conditions determines using the system model.
Operating under harsh thermal and chemical conditions requires careful selection and
imposes its own criteria. Same for the design of the redox-active material morphology
that impact not only chemistry, but also other heat and mass transport processes whose
roles are often important but overlooked. Ultimately, bench scale experiments are
essential for validation but selecting from a large design space, scaling and optimization
can benefit significantly from the modeling study.

The processes examined in this project belong to the generic technology of chemical
looping (better word would be thermochemical looping) largely used in combustion,
fuels, chemicals production, and has been suggested for energy storage. Similar
methodology and models to those developed in the work performed for this project can
be applied to analyze the application of chemical looping in other overall endothermic
processes, e.g., efficient steam-methane reforming, reverse gas-water shift, oxidative
coupling of methane, thermal and chemical energy storage, etc., irrespective of the heat
source as long as the later can meet the requirements. In all, redox active material of
special morphology (potentially 3D printed) should be housed in specially designed
reactors and integrated into a system to deliver max efficiency and productivity, with
related economic and environmental considerations.
