Validation of the regular physical exercise adherence scale (REPEAS) in Brazilian older adults
Authors: Leiane Mota Costa, Aldair Darlan Santos-de-Araújo, Victória Pereira Frutuoso, Fabio Henrique Ferreira Pereira, Marinete Rodrigues de Farias Diniz, Abraão Albino Mendes Júnior, Almir Vieira Dibai-Filho, Daniela Bassi-Dibai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe objective of this cross-sectional observational study was to validate a scale that highlights the personal and environmental barriers that hinder adherence to regular physical exercise in older adults.MethodsIndividuals aged 60 years or over, sedentary and/or ex-practitioners, regular practitioners of physical exercise, with Brazilian Portuguese as their mother tongue. In addition to the Regular Physical Exercise Adherence Scale (REPEAS), the following instruments were applied: sociodemographic data sheet and Baecke Habitual Physical Activity Questionnaire. The measurement properties tested were: structural validity, construct validity, reliability, consistency internal and ceiling and floor effects.ResultsThis research involved 200 older adults’ participants, predominantly women. The confirmatory factor analysis supported the two-domain, 12-item structure of the REPEAS, demonstrating good model fit with the following indices: chi-square/degrees of freedom = 1.60, comparative fit index = 0.965, Tucker-Lewis index = 0.957, root mean square error of approximation = 0.078, and standardized root mean square residual = 0.078. Construct validity was evidenced by a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) in the personal factors domain between groups. Additionally, the domains of the REPEAS showed appropriate correlations with the items in the factor analysis. Reliability was acceptable, with composite reliability ≥ 0.84 and intraclass correlation coefficient ≥ 0.89.ConclusionThis scale presented adequate measurement properties when tested on Brazilian older adults to assess environmental and personal barriers to regular physical exercise. However, as this was a study conducted with a convenience sample, generalization of the findings should be made with caution. Despite this, the REPEAS can serve as a practical tool for health professionals and researchers to identify modifiable barriers and guide strategies to promote physical activity among older adults.Trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

Although population aging is a positive thing in terms of celebrating medical advances and social, economic, political, and health achievements in any society, aging brings many individual and collective challenges, including an increase in the number of people at risk of chronic diseases [1, 2], decreased physical activity [3], psychological and social vulnerability [4], and the difficulty of the health system in providing health services [5, 6].

Around 32 million Brazilians are considered older adults, representing approximately 15.8% of the country’s total population [2], of which approximately 60% are considered insufficiently active [7]. Aging causes functional and morphological damage to several body systems; it is known that greater sedentary time in old age has also been associated with several unfavorable health outcomes, in addition to cardiovascular diseases, we can also mention stroke and mortality from all causes [8, 9]. Additionally, the cardiovascular diseases and cerebrovascular diseases, result from multiple risk factors, including a sedentary lifestyle [10, 11]. It is important to highlight that sedentary lifestyle is common in the routine of Brazilians, even during leisure time, regular physical exercise is not a habit [12].

Given the increase in the older adults population and its complications, active aging appears as a way to manage this situation [13]. The goal of active aging is to empower older adults to become active participants in their health care; comprehensive planning is necessary for this to happen [13]. Barriers to regular physical exercise are considered to play a significant role in explaining health-damaging behaviors such as a sedentary lifestyle [14, 15]. Studies on factors associated with physical activity indicate that the presence of environmental and/or personal barriers are strongly correlated with low levels of physical exercise. Thus, identifying these barriers is essential for the development of effective health promotion and intervention strategies [16]. In this sense, with the aim of identifying the environmental and personal barriers that hinder adherence to regular physical exercise, the Regular Physical Exercise Adherence Scale (REPEAS) was created and validated in Brazil, initially for a population of apparently healthy adults, consisting of 12 questions that assess environmental and personal domains [17]. Additionally, the REPEAS was also validated for a population with chronic pain [18].

Several instruments exist to assess barriers or adherence to physical exercise, focusing on either personal or environmental factors. However, most are limited to specific clinical populations, or single domains, for example the Inventory of Physical Activity Barriers (IPAB), focus primarily on environmental factors [19], while the Exercise Self-Efficacy Scale (ESES) assesses only personal factors [20], and the Barriers to Physical Activity in Older Adults Questionnaire (BPAEQ) targets specific clinical populations [21].

In addition, few instruments are available that incorporate both personal and environmental factors, are validated for use in the older adult population, and are brief enough to administer, making them feasible for clinical practice. Consequently, a gap persists in the literature concerning the barriers specifically faced by older adults [22], underscoring the need to validate an instrument such as the REPEAS to assess barriers to regular physical exercise adherence in this population [17]. The validation of such a tool is warranted, given the high prevalence of sedentary behavior and the increased burden of comorbidities within this age group. Therefore, the present study aimed to validate the REPEAS for Brazilian older adults.

Materials and methods

Study design

This analytical cross-sectional research focused on the validation of a questionnaire and was carried out following the recommendations of the COnsensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) [23]. The data were gathered at two locations in São Luís (Maranhão, Brazil): the Integrated Action Program for Retirees and the Association of Ladies of Charity São Vicente de Paula.

The Integrated Action Program for Retirees is a government initiative that provides retired state employees with access to physical and cultural activities, physiotherapy, art workshops, various courses, as well as social and health support. The Association of Ladies of Charity São Vicente de Paula is a charitable organization dedicated to assisting and promoting the human person. Thus, the sample in this study consisted of community-dwelling (i.e., non-institutionalized) individuals.

Ethical aspects

The Research Ethics Committee of Ceuma University approved this investigation (approval number 5.328.899), ensuring that all procedures adhered strictly to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to participation, individuals received clear explanations regarding the study’s aims and methods, presented on the initial page of the instrument. Only after providing their consent by signing the informed consent form were they permitted to proceed to the subsequent sections, where the questionnaire necessary for the research was made available. Older adults were instructed to read and complete the questionnaires independently. In cases of reading difficulties, the examiner read the questionnaire items and response options aloud, without influencing the participants’ answers.

Sample size and participants

The determination of the sample size followed the COSMIN recommendations [23], adopting a ratio of seven participants per questionnaire item, with a minimum threshold of 100 individuals. The sample was composed of older adults, aged 60 years or above, who self-identified as sedentary and/or ex-practitioners, regular practitioners of physical exercise, with the group of practitioners used as the basis for construct validation. All participants were native speakers of Brazilian Portuguese, that is, who had Brazilian Portuguese as their mother tongue. Recruitment occurred through verbal invitations within social groups. Participation was confirmed by the signing of the informed consent form.

Exclusion criteria encompassed older adults with mobility limitations or dependence on walking aids that hinder engagement in physical activities, those with a clinical diagnosis of cognitive deficits that impair their comprehension and response to the questionnaires, and individuals with medical contraindications, such as cardiovascular diseases, that restrict physical exercise.

Regular physical exercise adherence scale (REPEAS)

The purpose of this scale is to detect and assess obstacles that adversely affect individuals’ commitment to maintaining regular physical exercise routines [17]. It consists of 12 items that examine both personal and environmental aspects that may interfere with adherence to physical activity. Each item is scored on a continuum from 0 to 10, where 0 signifies “Does not interfere with engaging in physical exercise” and 10 indicates “Strongly interferes with engaging in physical exercise.” To obtain the domain score, the sum of responses for all items is divided by the total number of items answered, yielding a value between 0 and 10. This value is then multiplied by 10, resulting in a final score ranging from 0 to 100. Elevated scores per domain denote a greater presence of barriers to adhering to physical activity [17].

Baecke habitual physical activity questionnaire

To characterize physical activity levels, the previously mentioned self-administered questionnaire, culturally adapted and validated for Brazilian Portuguese speakers, was used to evaluate participants’ physical activity over the past 12 months [24, 25].

Structural validity

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to assess the internal structure of REPEAS, based on the theoretical model that posits two domains—environmental and personal factors—as identified in prior research [17, 26].

Construct validity

To perform construct validity, the score of each REPEAS domain was compared between two recognized distinct groups: regular exercisers versus non-practitioners/ex-practitioners/irregular exercisers. In addition, the REPEAS domains were correlated.

Reliability

A subsample of 40 individuals classified as non-practitioners, former practitioners, or irregular exercisers completed the REPEAS on two separate occasions, with a 7 to 15-day interval between assessments, for the purposes of evaluating test-retest reliability [26, 28].

Statistical analysis

Structural validity was examined through CFA conducted in R Studio (Boston, MA, USA), utilizing the lavaan and semPlot packages. Since the REPEAS employs an ordinal Likert-type scale, the CFA used a polychoric correlation matrix combined with the robust diagonally weighted least squares (RDWLS) estimation technique. The adequacy of the model was assessed through multiple fit indices, including the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with its 90% confidence interval, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), and the ratio of chi-square to degrees of freedom (χ²/df). For this analysis, values exceeding 0.90 for both CFI and TLI were interpreted as indicators of good model fit, while RMSEA and SRMR values below 0.08 were considered acceptable. Additionally, a χ²/df ratio under 3.00 was used as a benchmark for satisfactory fit [29]. Factor loadings equal to or greater than 0.40 were considered adequate for the domain [29].

In the present study, reliability was assessed based on a test-retest model, using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The interpretation of the ICC value will be performed as follows: for values below 0.40, reliability will be considered low; between 0.40 and 0.75, moderate; between 0.75 and 0.90, substantial; and, finally, values greater than 0.90, reliability will be considered excellent [30]. In addition, we calculated the standard error of measurement (SEM) and minimum detectable difference (MDD) [31]. The effect size was calculated based on the Cohen d, according to the website: <https://www.psychometrica.de/effect_size.html%3E. It was considered the following interpretation of the d value: 0.2 (weak), 0.5 (moderate) and > 0.8 (large effect size) [32]. Internal consistency was assessed using composite reliability (CR) and value ≥ 0.70 was considered adequate [33]. We used the online calculator from The Statistical Mind, available at: https://www.thestatisticalmind.com/calculators/comprel/composite_reliability.htm.

To determine construct validity, a comparison was made between groups that were recognized as distinct (regular exercisers versus non-practitioners/ex-practitioners/irregular exercisers) using the t-test for independent samples. The hypothesis is that there will be a significant difference in the scores of the REPEAS domains in the comparisons between the groups [23].

Ceiling and floor effects were evaluated in the present study. By definition, these effects occur when a number of study participants (more than 15%) reach the minimum or maximum values for the total questionnaire score. The processing of the descriptive analysis, construct validity and identification of floor and ceiling effects was performed using SPSS software, version 17.0 (Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Sample characterization

The sample consisted of 200 older adults, and their characteristics are presented in Table 1 according to the level of physical activity: non-practitioners and former/irregular practitioners of physical exercise (Group 1, n = 100) and regular practitioners of physical exercise (Group 2, n = 100). The groups did not differ in terms of age, weight (kg), height (m), or BMI (kg/m²). However, the sample was predominantly female in both groups (92% and 94%, respectively), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.579). Education levels were also comparable, with most participants having completed secondary or higher education (p = 0.053). Most participants were retired in both groups (92% and 96%; p = 0.058). No significant differences were found between groups in the occupational and leisure domains of the Baecke Habitual Physical Activity Questionnaire (p = 0.447 and p = 0.298, respectively). However, a significant difference was observed in the sport domain, with higher scores among the physically active older adults compared to the inactive ones (p < 0.001; effect size = 1.04). In the group 2, the physical exercises practiced were: Pilates (33%), walking (28%), water aerobics (14%), weight training (10%), dancing (7%), yoga (4%), swimming (2%) and gymnastics (2%).

Structural validity

For this analysis, only data from Group 1, composed of non-practitioners and former/irregular exercisers, were used. Thus, using CFA, the internal structure with two domains and 12 items of the REPEAS presented satisfactory fit indices: χ²/df = 1.60, CFI = 0.965, TLI = 0.957, RMSEA (90% CI) = 0.078 (0.045 to 0.108), SRMR = 0.078. Construct validity

Construct validity was assessed by comparing groups known to differ, specifically non-practitioners and former practitioners of physical exercise versus regular exercisers. A statistically significant difference was found in the personal factors domain (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the environmental factors domain showed a small effect size (d ≈ 0.20), while the personal factors domain exhibited a moderate effect size (d ≈ 0.50). Although the environmental factors domain did not reach statistical significance between groups, its validity is supported by the correlation between the REPEAS domains observed in the factor analysis (Fig. 1).

Reliability and internal consistency

Both domains of the REPEAS demonstrated satisfactory reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) exceeding 0.75, and acceptable internal consistency, as indicated by CR greater than 0.70, as detailed in Table 3.

Floor and ceiling effects

In the environmental factors domain, 14% of participants achieved the lowest possible score, while 13% reached this minimum in the personal factors domain. No participants attained the highest possible score in either domain, indicating the absence of floor and ceiling effects within the sample.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to validate an instrument for use in the Brazilian older adult population, aiming to identify personal and environmental barriers to adherence to regular physical exercise, given the lack of a validated tool for this purpose in the literature. The instrument demonstrated adequate structural and construct validity, test-retest reliability and internal consistency.

Physical inactivity remains one of the main factors responsible for the development and worsening of various chronic diseases in the general population. In this context, the World Health Organization (WHO) has established global targets to reduce sedentary behavior, proposing a decrease of at least 10% by 2025 and 15% by 2030 [34]. Despite the wide recognition of the physical and mental health benefits of exercise, poor adherence to this non-pharmacological intervention has become a growing concern [35, 36]. Therefore, identifying the barriers that hinder or prevent regular participation in physical activity is essential for planning effective health promotion and disease prevention strategies [35, 36].

Seeking to better understand these determinants, the REPEAS was developed for the Brazilian adult population [17]. The validation study included 228 individuals aged 18 to 59 years, comprising regular exercisers as well as irregular and former practitioners. The findings of this study corroborate the findings of our research, presenting good measurement properties, including acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha was 0.908 for environmental domain and 0.915 for personal domain) and high reliability (ICC of 0.86 for environmental domain and 0.94 for personal domain) [17]. Similar to our study, which presented acceptable CR of 0.84 (environmental domain) and 0.87 (personal domain) and ICC of 0.90 (environmental domain) and 0.89 (personal domain). However, it was a tool developed to assess environmental and personal barriers to adherence to regular physical exercise, aimed only at young adults, while the target population of our study is the older adult.

Interestingly, in the study that developed the instrument, the group of individuals who did not engage in physical exercise reported greater barriers to adherence compared to those who were active [17]. This finding not only highlights the instrument’s sensitivity in distinguishing different behavioral profiles regarding physical activity but also reinforces the construct validity, as a significant difference between the groups was observed. In our study, we observed that older adults presented personal barriers to adherence to physical exercise. However, this does not compromise the validity of the construct, since the other domains showed consistent correlations, indicating that the instrument maintains its ability to reflect the different aspects related to barriers to physical activity in older adults.

The results of the sociodemographic profile indicated a predominance of women in both groups of this study, corroborating with the current literature that highlights the feminization of the aging process. This phenomenon is evidenced by the greater number of women over 60 years old compared to men, especially in the Northeast region [37]. However, other factors may have influenced the composition of the sample, such as greater female participation in health and community activities, higher interest of women in research related to health and physical activity, sociocultural patterns that facilitate women’s availability and engagement in studies, and differences in lifestyle or health-seeking behavior between men and women [38]. These aspects should be considered when interpreting the findings, as they may have contributed to the observed gender distribution.

The literature presents some instruments to measure adherence to physical exercise, however, the REPEAS has specific characteristics that differentiate it, such as being structured in two well-defined domains, assessment with simple scoring, easy to understand, and quick to apply [17]. In this context, the purpose of developing the IPAB was to provide health professionals with an effective tool to assess and organize the barriers that hinder the practice of physical exercise and guide appropriate treatment [19]. This study was conducted with individuals over 50 years of age, using a scale composed of 27 items distributed across 7 factors, including environmental factors, in which the results are similar to our research, with excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91), and high test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.99) [19]. However, this research did not include personal factors, diverging from our study [19].

It is interesting to note that both the IPAB and the REPEAS scales are quick to apply, allowing health professionals to understand the barriers inherent to adhering to this practice, enabling individualized assessment and guidance, allowing for an increase in the level of exercise and consequently reducing complications resulting from aging [17, 19].

Another instrument is the ESES [20], composed of 5 items that assess the person’s confidence to perform physical exercise, in the face of various emotional states, including only personal factors. This tool was translated, adapted and validated for the Portuguese population [20], with people between 50 and 95 years old. The scale showed good internal consistency, with an adequate Cronbach’s alpha (0.86), corroborating our findings with CR for personal domain (0.87). However, the application of the ESES was carried out with people under 60 years old, and only personal behaviors were assessed.

The Barriers to Physical Activity in Elderly Questionnaire (BPAEQ) is an instrument developed in Portuguese, including environmental and personal domains, characteristics similar to those of the REPEAS [17, 21]. The internal consistency measured by Cronbach’s alpha was 0.35 for the personal domain and 0.65 for the environmental domain, below the acceptable standard of 0.70. In contrast, our results showed acceptable CR (> 0.70) for the same domains [39]. This questionnaire was designed specifically for older adults with Parkinson’s disease, differing from our study, which focuses on older adults in general.

Understanding the barriers that older adults face in adhering to physical activity offers valuable insights for both practice and policy [40]. By identifying personal and environmental obstacles, health professionals and policymakers can develop targeted interventions to enhance participation [41]. Strategies may include accessible exercise programs, safe community spaces, and educational campaigns tailored to the needs and preferences of older adults, particularly women, who represented the majority of the sample. Addressing these barriers through coordinated public health policies can promote healthier aging, reduce the risk of chronic diseases, and improve overall quality of life in this population [42, 43].

Limitations

The REPEAS assess environmental and personal barriers to adherence to regular physical exercise; other types of barriers, for example specifically linked to diseases or other health contexts, are not covered by the instrument. Our results are applicable to Brazilians; however, since the present sample is not fully representative of the Brazilian population, caution should be exercised when generalizing the findings. Therefore, it is important that future studies conduct cross-cultural adaptations of this scale and evaluate its validity across different regions of the country, considering Brazil’s vast territorial dimension and its climatic, sociocultural, demographic, and economic diversity.

Another important limitation is the sample size for reliability calculations (n = 40). Unfortunately, most non-practitioners/ex-practitioners/irregular exercisers did not answer the call for retesting, so we excluded these participants only from this analysis, limiting the sample size. In view of this, we identified higher values of sample dispersion, a fact that generated higher SEM and MDD values (since these are measures calculated based on the standard deviation). However, previous studies of the REPEAS found similar values for these measures (SEM ranging from 6.17 to 11.95 and MDD ranging from 17.10 to 33.12) [17, 18]. Construct validity was assessed to verify differences in the REPEAS scores among groups known to be distinct (non-practitioners or former/irregular practitioners versus regular practitioners of physical exercise), in accordance with COSMIN recommendations [44]. However, it is important that future studies examine the convergent validity of the instrument.

In particular, the sample exhibited a higher level of education compared to the general Brazilian population, with more than 80% of participants having completed secondary or higher education. This aspect may influence physical activity behaviors and perceptions of barriers, and should be considered when interpreting the results. It is important that future studies investigate the influence of educational level and income on adherence to regular physical exercise, including among older adults.

This study was not designed to assess content validity or to make modifications to the instrument’s items, as the REPEAS has demonstrated stable psychometric properties in two distinct populations (healthy individuals and those with chronic pain), according to previous studies [17, 26]. Finally, it should be noted that this study lacks information on the duration of participants’ physical activity, which would be important to provide a more detailed characterization of their exercise habits.

Conclusion

The REPEAS presented adequate internal structure, constructs, reliability and internal consistency when tested on Brazilian older adults to assess environmental and personal barriers to regular physical exercise. However, the present sample is not fully representative of the Brazilian population, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should evaluate the scale across more diverse populations and regions to confirm its broader applicability.
