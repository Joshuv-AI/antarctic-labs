Sequential Multiple Testing: A Second-Order Asymptotic Analysis
Jingyu Liu, Yanglei Song

Abstract
We study sequential multiple testing with independent data streams, where the goal is to identify an unknown subset of signals while controlling commonly used error metrics, including generalized familywise rates and false discovery and non-discovery rates. For these problems, procedures that are first-order optimal are known, in the sense that the ratio of their expected sample size (ESS) to the minimal achievable ESS converges to one as the error tolerance levels vanish. In this work, we develop a unified theory of second-order asymptotic optimality. We establish general sufficient conditions under which second-order Bayesian optimality implies second-order frequentist optimality for broad classes of sequential testing procedures. As a consequence, several procedures previously known to be first-order optimal are shown to be second-order optimal: for every signal configuration, the difference between their ESS and the minimal achievable ESS remains uniformly bounded as the error tolerance levels tend to zero. In addition, we derive a second-order asymptotic expansion of the minimal achievable ESS, which refines the classical first-order approximation by identifying the second-order correction term arising from a boundary-crossing problem for a multidimensional random walk. We apply this result to several commonly used error metrics.

Introduction

Multiple testing concerns the simultaneous assessment of
K

In many applications, such as industrial process control, clinical trials, and multichannel signal detection, data are collected sequentially and decisions must adapt to the accumulated observations

Sequential procedures can substantially reduce sampling effort while maintaining prescribed

Specifically, we consider

Our contributions.

This paper establishes second-order frequentist optimality for several sequential multiple testing procedures and provides a second-order characterization of the minimal achievable ESS under a broad range of error metrics, including the generalized misclassification rate, generalized familywise error rates, false discovery/non-discovery rates, and settings with structural information on the signal configuration.

First, we develop in Theorem
1
a general framework that provides explicit and verifiable sufficient conditions under which a family of rules is second-order asymptotically optimal.
Recall that for each
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
,
\Delta(\bm{\theta})
denotes a class of sequential procedures.
A family
\{\delta(\bm{\theta})=(T(\bm{\theta}),D(\bm{\theta}))\in\Delta(\bm{\theta})\}
is said to be
second-order asymptotically optimal
with respect to
\{\Delta(\bm{\theta})\}
if, for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
,

\mathbb{E}_{A}[T(\bm{\theta})]=T_{A}^{\min}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))+O(1)\quad\text{as }\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0}.

That is, the excess ESS remains uniformly bounded as the error tolerance level vanishes.

Our framework builds on the Bayesian optimality theory developed in

[
20
,
23
,
24
]
.
In the associated Bayesian formulation, the signal configuration
A
is endowed with a prior distribution, and performance is measured by an integrated risk consisting of a sampling cost and a decision loss, defined through a suitably chosen cost parameter and loss function tailored to the class
\Delta(\bm{\theta})
.
We show that if, for each
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
, the stopping time
T(\bm{\theta})
does not exceed that of an appropriately constructed Bayes rule almost surely, and if the induced integrated loss due to incorrect decisions is uniformly controlled over
\Delta(\bm{\theta})
at the relevant asymptotic scale, then the procedure is second-order asymptotically optimal in the frequentist sense.

Second, we derive in Theorem
2
a second-order accurate asymptotic expansion of
T_{A}^{\min}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))
, which characterizes the fundamental efficiency limit within this class.
Under suitable conditions, we show that

T_{A}^{\min}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))=\frac{\log(c_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1})}{\kappa_{A}}+\kappa_{A}^{\prime}\sqrt{\log(c_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1})}+O\left((\log(c_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}))^{1/4+\epsilon}\right),\quad\text{ as }\;\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0},

for any fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1/4)
, where the cost parameter
c_{\bm{\theta}}>0
is a function of
\bm{\theta}
, and
\kappa_{A}
and
\kappa_{A}^{\prime}
are constants independent of
\bm{\theta}
.
This refines the existing first-order approximation

T_{A}^{\min}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))=\frac{\log(c_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1})}{\kappa_{A}}(1+o(1)),

by identifying the explicit second-order correction term
\kappa_{A}^{\prime}\sqrt{\log(c_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1})}
.
This term arises from a boundary-crossing problem for a multidimensional random walk.
Our analysis relies on nonlinear renewal theory
[
27
]
, and appears to be new in the present context; see Remark
8
.

Finally, we apply the above framework to several sequential multiple testing procedures previously known to be first-order asymptotically optimal under various error metrics and information structures, and establish their second-order optimality. In addition, we specialize the second-order asymptotic approximation of the minimal achievable ESS for the corresponding classes of procedures.

For concreteness, consider the class
\Delta^{\textup{gmr}}_{m_{0}}(\alpha)
and the Sum-Intersection rule
\delta_{S}(b_{\alpha})
mentioned above. In the left panel of Fig.
1
, the circle markers represent the second-order asymptotic approximation of
T_{A}^{\min}(\Delta^{\textup{gmr}}_{m_{0}}(\alpha))
for
A=\emptyset
, which has the form

{|\log(\alpha)|}/{\kappa_{A}}+\kappa_{A}^{\prime}\sqrt{|\log(\alpha)|}
,
where
\kappa_{A}^{\prime}>0
is independent of
\alpha
. The middle and right panels display the corresponding difference and ratio between
\mathbb{E}_{A}[T_{S}(b_{\alpha}^{*})]
and this second-order approximation, respectively. The boundedness of the excess ESS as
\alpha\to 0
, shown in the middle panel, confirms the second-order optimality of
\{\delta_{S}(b_{\alpha})\}
established in this paper.

Frequentist formulation, Optimality, and Minimum ESS

Consider
K\geq 2
independent streams of random vectors
\{\mathbf{X}^{k}:k\in[K]\}
, where
[K]=\{1,\ldots,K\}
.
For each
k\in[K]
,
\mathbf{X}^{k}=\{\mathbf{X}_{t}^{k}\in\mathcal{X}^{k}:t=1,2,\ldots\}
is an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sequence of
\mathcal{X}^{k}
-valued random vectors, where
\mathbf{X}_{t}^{k}
denotes the observation from the
k
-th stream at time
t
.
At each time
t\geq 1
, define

\mathbf{X}_{t}^{[K]}:=(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{t}^{K})^{\top}\in\mathcal{X}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{X}^{K}

to be the vector of observations across all streams at time
t
, and let

\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{[K]},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{t}^{[K]})

denote the
\sigma
-field generated by the observations up to time
t
.

For each
k\in[K]
, let
\mu^{k}
be a
\sigma
-finite measure, and let
f^{k}
denote the density of the marginal distribution of
X_{1}^{k}
with respect to
\mu^{k}
. We consider the pair of simple hypotheses

H_{0}^{k}:f^{k}=f_{0}^{k}\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}^{k}:f^{k}=f_{1}^{k}.

(2)

We impose the following assumption, which requires that the Kullback-Leibler divergences between
f_{0}^{k}
and
f_{1}^{k}
are positive and that the log-likelihood ratio has a finite second moment under both the null and the alternative.

Assumption 1
.

For each stream
k\in[K]
, we assume that for
(i,i^{\prime})\in\{(0,1),(1,0)\}
,

\int\log\left(f_{i}^{k}/f_{i^{\prime}}^{k}\right)f_{i}^{k}\,d\mu^{k}>0,\quad\text{and}\quad\int\left[\log\left(f_{i}^{k}/f_{i^{\prime}}^{k}\right)\right]^{2}f_{i}^{k}\,d\mu^{k}<\infty.

In addition, for each
k\in[K]
, let
P_{0}^{k}
and
P_{1}^{k}
denote the corresponding distributions of the
data stream

\mathbf{X}^{k}
under
H_{0}^{k}
and
H_{1}^{k}
, respectively. For any subset
A\subset[K]
, interpret
A
as the set of indices for which the alternative hypothesis is true, referred to as the
signal subset
, and let
\mathbb{P}_{A}
denote the joint distribution of
\{\mathbf{X}^{k}:k\in[K]\}
under this configuration. In particular, by independence across streams,

\mathbb{P}_{A}=\bigotimes_{k\in[K]}P^{k},\quad\text{ where }\quad P^{k}=\begin{cases}P_{1}^{k},&k\in A,\\
P_{0}^{k},&k\in A^{c},\end{cases}

where
\bigotimes
denotes the product measure. Denote by
\mathbb{E}_{A}
the expectation with respect to the measure
\mathbb{P}_{A}
.
Under
\mathbb{P}_{A}
, the distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{1}^{[K]}
admits a density with respect to
\mu^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu^{K}
, given by

f_{A}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{K})=\prod_{i\in A}f_{1}^{i}(x^{i})\prod_{j\in A^{c}}f_{0}^{j}(x^{j}),\quad\text{ for }\;\;x^{k}\in\mathcal{X}^{k},\ k\in[K].

(3)

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset 2^{[K]}
denote the collection of all possible signal subsets, where
2^{[K]}
is the power set of
[K]
. The case
\mathcal{A}=2^{[K]}
corresponds to the absence of any prior information about the signal configuration, whereas
\mathcal{A}\subsetneq 2^{[K]}
reflects the availability of prior knowledge on the structure of the signals.

A sequential multiple testing procedure
\delta
is defined as a pair
(T,D)
, where
T
is an
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
-stopping time at which sampling in all streams is terminated, and
D=(D^{1},\ldots,D^{K})^{\top}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable decision rule. Each component
D^{k}
,
k\in[K]
, is a Bernoulli random variable, with
D^{k}=0
corresponding to the selection of
H_{0}^{k}
and
D^{k}=1
corresponding to the selection of
H_{1}^{k}
. Accordingly, upon stopping and based on the information available up to time
T
, we declare the presence of a signal (resp. noise) in the
k
-th stream if
D^{k}=1
(resp.
D^{k}=0
). With a slight abuse of notation, we also write

D:=\{k\in[K]:D^{k}=1\}

to denote the subset of streams declared to contain signals. We restrict attention to decision rules satisfying
D\in\mathcal{A}
, and denote by
\Delta
the class of
all
sequential testing procedures.

Our objective is to minimize the expected sample size (ESS)
\mathbb{E}_{A}[T]
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
, while simultaneously controlling prescribed notions of error associated with the decision rule
D
.

2.1
Multiple Testing Error Rates

In this subsection, we define several subclasses of sequential testing procedures corresponding to different notions of error metrics and information structures.

Given a decision
D\in\mathcal{A}
and the true signal subset
A\in\mathcal{A}
, there are two natural types of errors associated with each stream
k\in[K]
: a Type-I error occurs if the true hypothesis for the
k
-th stream is
H_{0}^{k}
but
D^{k}=1
(i.e., a false positive), while a Type-II error occurs if the true hypothesis is
H_{1}^{k}
but
D^{k}=0
(i.e., a false negative).

We first recall three subclasses of procedures from the literature that assume no prior information, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}=2^{[K]}
, and control different multiple testing error criteria.

Class 1
.

Given a user-specified integer
1\leq m_{0}\leq K
and a tolerance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we define the class of sequential procedures that control the generalized misclassification rate (GMR)
[
22
,
25
,
32
]
as

\mathcal{A}=2^{[K]},\quad\text{ and }\quad\Delta_{m_{0}}^{\mathrm{gmr}}(\alpha)=\left\{(T,D)\in\Delta:\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\bigl(|D\triangle A|\geq m_{0}\bigr)\leq\alpha\right\}.

Class 2
.

Given two user-specified integers
1\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq K
such that
m_{1}+m_{2}\leq K
and tolerance levels
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
, we define the class of sequential procedures that control the generalized familywise error rates (GFWER)
[
1
,
9
,
32
]
, as
\mathcal{A}=2^{[K]}
and

\Delta_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\mathrm{gfr}}(\alpha,\beta)=\left\{(T,D)\in\Delta:\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\bigl(|D\setminus A|\geq m_{1}\bigr)\leq\alpha,\;\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\bigl(|A\setminus D|\geq m_{2}\bigr)\leq\beta\right\}.

Class 3
.

Given tolerance levels
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
, we define the class of sequential procedures that control the false discovery rate (FDR) and the false non-discovery rate (FNR)
[
19
,
4
,
15
]
as
\mathcal{A}=2^{[K]}
and

\Delta^{\mathrm{fdr}}(\alpha,\beta)=\left\{(T,D)\in\Delta:\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[\frac{|D\setminus A|}{\max\{|D|,1\}}\right]\leq\alpha,\;\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[\frac{|A\setminus D|}{\max\{|D^{c}|,1\}}\right]\leq\beta\right\}.

A few remarks are in order. First, for each of the three classes above, error control is required uniformly over all possible signal subsets
A\subset[K]
. Second, in contrast to Class
1
, the GFWER and FDR/FNR criteria distinguish between Type-I and Type-II errors. Third, for GFWER, when
m_{1}=m_{2}=1
, the criterion reduces to the classical familywise error rates
[
2
,
7
,
8
,
3
]
; more generally, GFWER allows up to
m_{1}-1
Type-I errors and
m_{2}-1
Type-II errors. A similar discussion applies to the
generalized
misclassification rate. Finally, Class
3
focuses on controlling the expected proportions of Type-I and Type-II errors.

In some applications, prior knowledge about the structure of the signal subset may be available. For instance, the exact number of signals may be known, or lower and upper bounds on the number of signals may be specified. We next recall a subclass of sequential procedures from the literature designed for settings in which
\mathcal{A}\subsetneq 2^{[K]}

[
31
,
15
]
.

Class 4
.

Suppose the number of signals is known to be
m\in[1,K)
. For a tolerance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we define the class of sequential procedures that control the probability of a wrong decision as

\mathcal{A}=\{A\subset[K]:|A|=m\},\qquad\Delta_{m}^{\textup{kns}}(\alpha)=\left\{(T,D)\in\Delta:\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\bigl(D\neq A\bigr)\leq\alpha\right\}.

(4)

We review additional subclasses of sequential procedures in Appendix
D.2
. For a given subclass, the objective is to identify a procedure within the class that minimizes the ESS in an appropriate asymptotic sense. In the next subsection, we review several notions of asymptotic optimality.

2.2
Asymptotic Optimality

Let
\Delta(\bm{\theta})\subset\Delta
denote a general subclass of procedures indexed by an error tolerance vector
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
, where
r\in\{1,2\}
. For example,
\Delta(\bm{\theta})
may correspond to one of the four classes of procedures defined above, with
r=1
for Class
1
and
r=2
for Class
2
.
Recall from (
1
) that
T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))
denotes the minimum achievable expected sample size (ESS) over the class
\Delta(\bm{\theta})
under the true signal subset
A\in\mathcal{A}
.

For many classes of sequential procedures, such as Classes
1
–
4
, the minimum achievable ESS diverges as the error probability requirements
\bm{\theta}
become more stringent, that is,

\displaystyle T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))\to\infty\;\;\text{ as }\;\;\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0},\quad\text{for each }A\in\mathcal{A},

(5)

where
\bm{0}
denotes the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. We now formally define two notions of asymptotic optimality.

Definition 1

(Asymptotic Optimality)
.

Let
\{\delta(\bm{\theta})=(T(\bm{\theta}),D(\bm{\theta})):\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}\}
be a collection of sequential multiple testing procedures such that
\delta(\bm{\theta})\in\Delta(\bm{\theta})
for all
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
. Then:

(a)

\{\delta(\bm{\theta})\}
is called
first-order asymptotically optimal
in
\{\Delta(\bm{\theta})\}
if, for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
,

\lim_{\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{A}[T(\bm{\theta})]}{T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))}=1.

(b)

\{\delta(\bm{\theta})\}
is called
second-order asymptotically optimal
in
\{\Delta(\bm{\theta})\}
if, for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
,

\limsup_{\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0}}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}_{A}[T(\bm{\theta})]-T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))\Bigr)<\infty.

We emphasize that the definition of asymptotic optimality (AO) requires the stated limits to hold for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
. While first-order AO ensures that the ESS is asymptotically optimal in a relative sense, it does not preclude the possibility that, for some
A\in\mathcal{A}
, the excess

\mathbb{E}_{A}[T(\bm{\theta})]-T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))

diverges as
\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0}
; see Figure
1
for an illustration. When (
5
) holds, the notion of second-order AO imposes stronger requirements by controlling the excess in an additive sense. We refer to Subsection
4.4
for further discussion of higher-order optimality.

For Classes
1
–
4
and additional classes in Appendix
D.2
, the existing literature proposes testing procedures and establishes their first-order asymptotic optimality
[
31
,
32
,
15
]
; see Section
4
and Appendix
D.1
. However, it remains open whether these first-order optimal procedures also enjoy the stronger notion of second-order optimality. In this work, we provide an
affirmative
answer to this question.

The above formulations are frequentist in nature, aiming to control the relevant error probabilities uniformly over
A\in\mathcal{A}
, as well as the ESS for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
. To establish the
second-order
optimality of existing procedures, our strategy is to leverage optimality results from associated
Bayesian
formulations, which average errors and ESS with respect to a prior distribution over
\mathcal{A}
, and then translate these results into their frequentist counterparts. To this end, in Subsection
3.1
we introduce the Bayesian formulation and develop a framework linking Bayesian and frequentist optimality, which we then apply to general subclasses of sequential procedures.

2.3
Minimum ESS Characterization

The second-order optimality results make it possible to derive a more accurate asymptotic approximation to the minimum ESS than what is currently available in the literature.
Next, we define two notions of asymptotic approximation.

Definition 2

(Asymptotic approximation)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
, and
\bm{\Theta}\subset(0,1)^{r}
. Let
\{Q_{1}(\bm{\theta}),Q_{2}(\bm{\theta}):\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\}
be a collection of positive numbers indexed by
\bm{\theta}
.

(a)

\{Q_{1}(\bm{\theta}):\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\}
is called a
first-order accurate approximation
to
T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))
if, as
\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0}
with
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
,

T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))=Q_{1}(\bm{\theta})\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).

(b)

\{Q_{1}(\bm{\theta}),Q_{2}(\bm{\theta}):\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\}
is called a
second-order accurate approximation
to
T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))
if, as
\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0}
with
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
,

T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))=Q_{1}(\bm{\theta})+Q_{2}(\bm{\theta})\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q_{2}(\bm{\theta})=Q_{1}(\bm{\theta})o(1).

Remark 1
.

In later applications, when
r=1
, we let
\bm{\Theta}=(0,1)
. When
r=2
, we may impose conditions on the way the two components of
\bm{\theta}
approach zero. For this reason, we introduce
\bm{\Theta}
in the above definition.

From the above definition, if
\{Q_{1}(\bm{\theta})\}
is first-order accurate, then
T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))
is characterized up to an error term that is negligible compared to
Q_{1}(\bm{\theta})
. Thus,
Q_{1}(\bm{\theta})
is the first-order dominant term in the asymptotic approximation.

On the other hand, if
\{Q_{1}(\bm{\theta}),Q_{2}(\bm{\theta}):\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\}
is second-order accurate, the error of the approximation
Q_{1}(\bm{\theta})+Q_{2}(\bm{\theta})
is negligible compared to
Q_{2}(\bm{\theta})
, which is more precise than an
o(Q_{1}(\bm{\theta}))
error since
Q_{2}(\bm{\theta})=Q_{1}(\bm{\theta})o(1)
. This also implies that
Q_{2}(\bm{\theta})
is the second-order dominant term in the asymptotic approximation, which justifies the terminology.

As detailed in Section
4
, for various classes of sequential procedures there exist first-order accurate approximations in the literature, which we strengthen to second order. To give some preview, for now assume
r=1
and denote
\bm{\theta}
by
\alpha
. For several classes, the existing first-order approximation to
T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\alpha))
is of the form
Q_{1}(\alpha)=|\log(\alpha)|/\kappa_{A}
, where
\kappa_{A}>0
is a constant. In this work, we show that as
\alpha\to 0
,

T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\alpha))=\frac{|\log(\alpha)|}{\kappa_{A}}+\kappa_{A}^{\prime}\sqrt{|\log(\alpha)|}+O\left(|\log(\alpha)|^{\epsilon_{0}}\right),

where
\kappa_{A}^{\prime}\geq 0
is another constant, and
\epsilon_{0}\in(1/4,1/2)
is a fixed constant.

Note that when
\kappa_{A}^{\prime}>0
, the first- and second-order approximations have different forms. Furthermore, roughly speaking, the existing results are accurate up to
o(|\log(\alpha)|)
, while our results are accurate up to
O(|\log(\alpha)|^{\epsilon_{0}})
, where
\epsilon_{0}
can be made arbitrarily close to
1/4
under appropriate moment conditions. Finally, when
\kappa_{A}^{\prime}=0
, the approximation
{|\log(\alpha)|}/{\kappa_{A}}
in fact achieves an
O(1)
error.

3
A Framework for Frequentist Optimality via Bayesian Formulations

In this section, we first introduce a general Bayesian formulation of the sequential multiple testing problem and review a procedure that is second-order optimal in the Bayesian sense (see Subsection
3.1
). We then establish sufficient conditions under which a procedure can be shown to be second-order optimal in the frequentist sense by comparison with this second-order Bayesian optimal procedure (see Subsection
3.2
). Finally, we derive a second-order accurate asymptotic approximation to the minimum achievable expected sample size (ESS) under the frequentist formulation (see Subsection
3.3
).

3.1
Bayesian Formulation and Bayesian Optimality

Recall that
\mathcal{A}\subset 2^{[K]}
denotes the collection of all possible signal subsets, and let
\pi_{0}
be the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{A}
, that is,
\pi_{0}(A)=|\mathcal{A}|^{-1}
for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
.
In the Bayesian formulation, the true signal subset
A
is treated as a random variable drawn from the prior distribution
\pi_{0}
.

Let
\delta=(T,D)\in\Delta
be a sequential procedure, and recall that the decision rule
D
takes values in
\mathcal{A}
. We define the integrated risk of
\delta
as the sum of the cost due to sampling and the loss due to incorrect decisions.
Specifically, let
c>0
denote the cost incurred per additional set of observations. The integrated sampling cost is defined as

\textup{ESS}^{\textup{int}}(\delta;c):=c\,\mathbb{E}[T]=c\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{0}(A)\,\mathbb{E}_{A}[T],

(6)

where the expectation
\mathbb{E}
is taken with respect to both the prior distribution of
A
and the randomness of the observations. In addition, let

\mathcal{W}(\cdot\mid\cdot):\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,\infty)

be a nonnegative loss function, where
\mathcal{W}(D\mid A)
denotes the loss incurred by declaring
D
when the true signal subset is
A
. We assume that
\mathcal{W}(A\mid A)=0
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
. The integrated error loss associated with incorrect decisions is then defined as

\textup{ERR}^{\textup{int}}(\delta;\mathcal{W}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{W}(D\mid A)\right]=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{0}(A)\sum_{d\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{P}_{A}(D=d)\,\mathcal{W}(d\mid A).

(7)

Thus, given
c>0
and
\mathcal{W}
, the integrated cost is defined as

\displaystyle r^{\textup{int}}(\delta;c,\mathcal{W}):=\textup{ESS}^{\textup{int}}(\delta;c)+\textup{ERR}^{\textup{int}}(\delta;\mathcal{W}).

(8)

Remark 2
.

To connect frequentist optimality with Bayesian optimality, it is necessary to specify the cost parameter
c
and the loss function
\mathcal{W}
.

Next, we review the notion of asymptotic optimality in the Bayesian sense. Let

r^{\textup{int}}_{\textup{min}}(c,\mathcal{W}):=\inf_{\delta\in\Delta}\,r^{\textup{int}}(\delta;c,\mathcal{W})

(9)

denote the minimum achievable integrated risk.

Definition 3

(Second-order Bayesian optimality)
.

Let
\{\delta(c):c>0\}\subset\Delta
be a collection of sequential procedures. The family
\{\delta(c)\}
is called
second-order Bayesian optimal
with respect to
\mathcal{W}
if there exists a constant
M>0
such that

r^{\textup{int}}(\delta(c);c,\mathcal{W})\leq r^{\textup{int}}_{\textup{min}}(c,\mathcal{W})+Mc,\quad\text{ for all }c>0.

We defer discussion of this definition to Remark
3
.

Finally, we review a second-order Bayesian optimal procedure from the literature. Recall the definition of the density
f_{A}(\cdot)
in (
3
). For
t\geq 1
, the posterior distribution
\pi_{t}:\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
of the signal subset
A
given the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is defined as

\pi_{t}(A):=\frac{f_{A,[t]}}{\sum_{B\in\mathcal{A}}f_{B,[t]}},\quad\text{for each }A\in\mathcal{A},\qquad\text{where }f_{A,[t]}:=\prod_{s=1}^{t}f_{A}(\mathbf{X}_{s}^{[K]}).

(10)

Given
c>0
and a loss function
\mathcal{W}
, the following procedure
\delta_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c,\mathcal{W})=(T_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c,\mathcal{W}),D_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c,\mathcal{W}))
, studied in
[
29
,
20
,
23
]
, is defined as

T_{\mathrm{Ld}}=\min\Bigl\{t\geq 1:\min_{D\in\mathcal{A}}\;\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{t}(A)\,\mathcal{W}(D\mid A)<c\Bigr\},\quad\;D_{\mathrm{Ld}}\in\argmin_{D\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{T_{\mathrm{Ld}}}(A)\,\mathcal{W}(D\mid A),

(11)

where ties are broken arbitrarily, and the dependence on
(c,\mathcal{W})
is omitted to simplify the notation. In words, the procedure stops at the first time
t
when there exists a decision
D\in\mathcal{A}
whose posterior expected loss falls below the threshold
c
, and upon stopping selects a decision that minimizes the posterior expected loss. It is shown in Lorden’s paper
[
23
]
that the family
\{\delta_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c,\mathcal{W})\}
is second-order Bayesian optimal, which motivates the subscript “Ld”.

Remark 3
.

In
[
20
]
, it is shown that
r^{\textup{int}}_{\textup{min}}(c,\mathcal{W})
is of order
c|\log c|
as
c\to 0
, and that the family
\{\delta_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c,\mathcal{W})\}
is
first-order
asymptotically optimal in the sense that

\lim_{c\to 0}\frac{r^{\textup{int}}(\delta_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c,\mathcal{W});c,\mathcal{W})}{r^{\textup{int}}_{\textup{min}}(c,\mathcal{W})}=1.

The second-order optimality of
\{\delta_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c,\mathcal{W})\}
, as defined in Definition
3
and established in
[
23
]
, is therefore a strictly stronger notion of optimality.

3.2
Second-Order Frequentist Optimality via Bayesian Optimality

In this subsection, we present one of the main results, which provides a technical scheme for establishing second-order frequentist optimality using Bayesian optimality as a tool. Specifically, for each
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
with
r\in\{1,2\}
, let
\Delta(\bm{\theta})\subset\Delta
be a subclass of sequential multiple testing procedures. Recall the definition of
T_{A}^{\min}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))
in (
1
), which is the minimum achievable ESS over this class.

Now, let
\delta_{0}(\bm{\theta})=(T_{0}(\bm{\theta}),D_{0}(\bm{\theta}))
,
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
, be a collection of procedures such that
\delta_{0}(\bm{\theta})\in\Delta(\bm{\theta})
for each
\bm{\theta}
. Our first result establishes sufficient conditions under which
\{\delta_{0}(\bm{\theta})\}
is second-order frequentist optimal with respect to
\{\Delta(\bm{\theta})\}
. The proof is given in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds. Assume that there exists a cost
c_{\bm{\theta}}>0
for each
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
, a loss function
\mathcal{W}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,\infty)
, and a constant
L>0
such that the following conditions hold:

\displaystyle\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{W}(D\mid A)>0,\quad\text{for each }D\in\mathcal{A};

(12)

\displaystyle T_{0}(\bm{\theta})\leq T_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c_{\bm{\theta}},\mathcal{W})\;\;\mathbb{P}_{A}\text{-almost surely},\;\;\text{for each }A\in\mathcal{A}\text{ and }\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r};

(13)

\displaystyle\textup{ERR}^{\textup{int}}(\delta;\mathcal{W})\leq Lc_{\bm{\theta}},\quad\text{for any procedure }\delta\in\Delta(\bm{\theta})\text{ and each }\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}.

(14)

Then there exists a constant
M>0
, depending only on
\mathcal{W}
and not on
\bm{\theta}
, such that

\mathbb{E}_{A}[T_{0}(\bm{\theta})]-T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))\leq(2L+M)\,|\mathcal{A}|,\quad\text{ for each }A\in\mathcal{A}\text{ and }\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}.

Note that Theorem
1
is non-asymptotic. Nevertheless, letting
\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0}
immediately implies the second-order frequentist optimality of
\{\delta_{0}(\bm{\theta})\}
with respect to
\{\Delta(\bm{\theta})\}
, in view of Definition
1
.

The key to applying Theorem
1
is to specify a cost
c_{\bm{\theta}}
that depends on
\bm{\theta}
, and a loss function
\mathcal{W}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
that is independent of
\bm{\theta}
, such that the three conditions therein can be verified.
In particular, condition (
12
) requires that for each decision
D\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists a signal subset
A\in\mathcal{A}
under which
D
is incorrect, in the sense that
\mathcal{W}(D\mid A)>0
. Condition (
13
) requires that the stopping time
T_{0}(\bm{\theta})
under consideration stops no later than
T_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c_{\bm{\theta}},\mathcal{W})
defined in (
11
). Finally, condition (
14
) requires that the integrated error loss of
any
procedure in
\Delta(\bm{\theta})
is bounded above by
Lc_{\bm{\theta}}
. In Section
4
and Appendix
D.1
, we apply Theorem
1
to Classes
1
–
4
to show that the existing procedures in the literature are second-order frequentist optimal.

Remark 4
.

The idea of deriving frequentist optimality from Bayesian optimality has appeared previously in the sequential analysis literature. In particular, some arguments in the proof of Theorem
1
are similar in spirit to those in the proof of Theorem 4 of
[
24
]
. However, the existing results are tailored to specific procedures and settings. Our contribution is to formulate explicit and verifiable sufficient conditions, stated in Theorem
1
, under which second-order frequentist optimality follows from second-order Bayesian optimality. The theorem is presented at a level of generality that accommodates multiple error metrics, including Class
1
and Class
2
, and does not require the assumption
\mathbb{P}_{A}(D(\bm{\theta})\neq A)\to 0
as
\bm{\theta}\to 0
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
, as assumed in
[
24
]
.

3.3
Second-Order Characterization of the Minimum ESS under the Frequentist Formulation

In this subsection, we characterize
T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))
up to the second-order as
\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0}
for the family of subclasses
\Delta(\bm{\theta})\subset\Delta
indexed by
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
. We consider a general loss function
\mathcal{W}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,\infty)
and introduce some notation and assumptions.

For each
k\in[K]
, define

\mathcal{I}_{1}^{k}:=\int\log\left({f_{1}^{k}}/{f_{0}^{k}}\right)f_{1}^{k}\,d\mu^{k},\quad\mathcal{I}_{0}^{k}:=\int\log\left({f_{0}^{k}}/{f_{1}^{k}}\right)f_{0}^{k}\,d\mu^{k},

(15)

which are the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergences between the densities
f_{1}^{k}
and
f_{0}^{k}
. Furthermore, for
A,C\in\mathcal{A}
and given that the true signal subset is
A
, we denote the KL divergence between the densities
f_{A}
and
f_{C}
(see (
3
)) by

\textup{KL}(f_{A}\mid f_{C}):=\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[\log\left(\frac{f_{A}}{f_{C}}(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{[K]})\right)\right]=\sum_{k\in A\setminus C}\mathcal{I}_{1}^{k}+\sum_{k\in C\setminus A}\mathcal{I}_{0}^{k}.

(16)

For each
D\in\mathcal{A}
, denote the collection of signal subsets under which
D
is incorrect with respective to the loss function
\mathcal{W}
by

\mathcal{H}_{D}^{\mathcal{W}}:=\{C\in\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{W}(D\mid C)>0\}.

(17)

In addition, for
A,D\in\mathcal{A}
, define

\textup{KL}_{A,D}^{\mathcal{W}}:=\min_{C\in\mathcal{H}_{D}^{\mathcal{W}}}\textup{KL}(f_{A}\mid f_{C}),\quad\text{ and }\quad\textup{KL}_{A,*}^{\mathcal{W}}:=\max_{B\in\mathcal{A}}\;\textup{KL}_{A,B}^{\mathcal{W}}.

(18)

That is, given the true signal subset
A
,
\textup{KL}_{A,D}^{\mathcal{W}}
is the minimum KL divergence between
f_{A}
and
f_{C}
over all signal subsets
C
for which the decision
D
is incorrect, and
\textup{KL}_{A,*}^{\mathcal{W}}
is the maximum of
\textup{KL}_{A,D}^{\mathcal{W}}
over all
D\in\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 4
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
. We say that
A
admits a
unique most favorable subset
with respect to
\mathcal{W}
if

\textup{KL}_{A,*}^{\mathcal{W}}
is attained at a unique subset
D_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}\in\mathcal{A}
, that is,

\textup{KL}_{A,D_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}}^{\mathcal{W}}=\textup{KL}_{A,*}^{\mathcal{W}}>\textup{KL}_{A,D}^{\mathcal{W}},\quad\text{for all }D\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{D_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}\}.

Remark 5
.

We call
\mathcal{W}
a
zero-one loss
if
\mathcal{W}(D\mid A)>0
for all
D\neq A
with
A,D\in\mathcal{A}
; that is, the loss is positive unless
D=A
. If
\mathcal{W}
is a zero-one loss, then clearly
\mathcal{H}_{D}^{\mathcal{W}}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\{D\}
.
Now fix
A\in\mathcal{A}
. Under Assumption
1
, we have

\textup{KL}_{A,A}^{\mathcal{W}}>0,\quad\textup{KL}_{A,D}^{\mathcal{W}}\leq\textup{KL}(f_{A}\mid f_{A})=0,\qquad\text{for all }D\neq A.

As a result, under a zero-one loss
\mathcal{W}
, each
A\in\mathcal{A}
admits a
unique most favorable subset
with respect to
\mathcal{W}
, namely
D_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}=A
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
admit a
unique most favorable subset
with respect to
\mathcal{W}
. We denote by

\mathcal{C}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}:=\left\{C\in\mathcal{H}_{D_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}}^{\mathcal{W}}:\textup{KL}(f_{A}\mid f_{C})=\textup{KL}_{A,*}^{\mathcal{W}}\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}:=\left|\mathcal{C}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}\right|

(19)

the collection of signal subsets attaining the minimum KL divergence over
\mathcal{H}_{D_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}}^{\mathcal{W}}
and its cardinality, respectively.
The resulting asymptotic behavior under
\mathbb{P}_{A}
differs qualitatively depending on whether
r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}=1
or
r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}\geq 2
. We refer to the former as the
asymmetric
case and the latter as the
symmetric
case.

Now, let
\left(\mathcal{C}_{A,1}^{\mathcal{W}},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{A,r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}}^{\mathcal{W}}\right)
be an ordered listing of the elements of
\mathcal{C}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}
. We define an
r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}
-dimensional random vector
\bm{R}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}
, where for each
1\leq j\leq r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}
,

\bm{R}_{A,j}^{\mathcal{W}}:=\log f_{A}\left(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{[K]}\right)-\log f_{\mathcal{C}_{A,j}^{\mathcal{W}}}\left(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{[K]}\right)-\textup{KL}_{A,*}^{\mathcal{W}}.

We note that
\mathbb{E}_{A}(\bm{R}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}})=\bm{0}
, and denote by

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}:=\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[\bm{R}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{R}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}})^{\top}\right],

(20)

the
r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}\times r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}
covariance matrix of
\bm{R}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}
when
A
is the true signal subset. Finally, let
\bm{Y}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}})
, the
r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}
-dimensional normal distribution with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}
. Denote the expected value of its largest component by

\displaystyle h_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}:=\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[\max\left\{\bm{Y}_{A,1}^{\mathcal{W}},\ldots,\bm{Y}_{A,r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}}^{\mathcal{W}}\right\}\right].

(21)

Remark 6
.

When
r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}=1
,
h_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}=0
. For the symmatric case
r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}\geq 2
, we have
h_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}\geq 0
, which can be estimated via a Monte Carlo method by sampling random vectors from
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{\mathcal{W}})
and taking the average of the largest component in each sample.

In the following, we characterize the second-order approximation of the minimum achievable expected sample size (ESS) for
\Delta(\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
. For some
q\geq 2
to be determined later, we assume

\int\bigl|\log(f_{i}^{k}/f_{i^{\prime}}^{k})\bigr|^{q}f_{i}^{k}\,d\mu^{k}<\infty,\quad\text{ for }(i,i^{\prime})\in\{(0,1),(1,0)\}\text{ and }k\in[K].

(22)

In addition, we consider the case where, for each
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
, whether a procedure belongs to
\Delta(\bm{\theta})
depends only on its decision rule and not on its stopping rule.

Assumption 2
.

Let
\delta=(T,D)\in\Delta
and
\delta^{\prime}=(T^{\prime},D^{\prime})\in\Delta
be arbitrary sequential procedures. If
\delta\in\Delta(\bm{\theta})
for some
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
and
D=D^{\prime}
, then
\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta(\bm{\theta})
.

This assumption is satisfied by classes of sequential procedures, such as Class
1
–
4
, which are defined in terms of error probabilities induced by incorrect decisions. The proof of the following theorem is provided in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Let
\delta_{0}(\bm{\theta})=(T_{0}(\bm{\theta}),D_{0}(\bm{\theta}))
,
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
, be a collection of procedures such that
\delta_{0}(\bm{\theta})\in\Delta(\bm{\theta})
for each
\bm{\theta}
. Assume that there exist a cost
c_{\bm{\theta}}>0
for each
\bm{\theta}\in(0,1)^{r}
, a loss function
\mathcal{W}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,\infty)
, and a constant
L>0
such that (
12
), (
13
), and (
14
) hold. Furthermore, assume
c_{\bm{\theta}}\to 0
as
\bm{\theta}\to\bm{0}
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
, and assume that
A
admits a unique most favorable subset with respect to
\mathcal{W}
.

(a)

Consider the case
r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}=1
. Assume (
22
) holds for

q=3
. Then, as
\bm{\theta}\rightarrow\bm{0}
,

T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))=\frac{\log(c_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1})}{\textup{KL}_{A,*}^{\mathcal{W}}}+O(1).

(b)

Consider the case
r_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}\geq 2
. For
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, assume
(
22
) holds for some
q
satisfying

q>\epsilon^{-1}\quad\textup{ and }\quad q\geq 3.

(23)

Then, as
\bm{\theta}\rightarrow\bm{0}
,

T_{A}^{\textup{min}}(\Delta(\bm{\theta}))=\frac{\log(c_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1})}{\textup{KL}_{A,*}^{\mathcal{W}}}+\frac{h_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}\sqrt{\log(c_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}})}{(\textup{KL}_{A,*}^{\mathcal{W}})^{3/2}}+O\left((\log(c_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}))^{1/4+\epsilon/2}\right).

A few remarks are in order. First, Assumptions
1
and (
22
) impose moment conditions on the log-likelihood ratios. In particular, the second part of Assumption
1
is implied by (
22
) when
q\geq 2
. Second, both the condition
c_{\bm{\theta}}\to 0
as
\bm{\theta}\to 0
and Assumption
2
are natural and hold for most classes of procedures, in particular for Classes
1
–

Remark 7
.

In the proof of Theorem
2
, we define a procedure
\delta_{1}(\bm{\theta})
that shares the same stopping rule as
\delta_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c_{\bm{\theta}},\mathcal{W})
and the same decision rule as
\delta_{0}(\bm{\theta})
. This serves two purposes: (i) the shared decision rule allows us to avoid verifying that
\delta_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c_{\bm{\theta}},\mathcal{W})\in\Delta(\bm{\theta})
; (ii) the shared stopping rule ensures, by Theorem

\Delta(\bm{\theta})
coincides with

\delta_{\mathrm{Ld}}(c_{\bm{\theta}},\mathcal{W})

Moreover, since

admits a unique most favorable subset

\tau_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}(c)=\min\left\{t\geq 1:\bm{R}_{t,G}\geq\log(c^{-1})\ \text{for all }G\in\mathcal{H}_{D_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}}^{\mathcal{W}}\right\},

and
\bm{R}_{t,G}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\log\left({f_{A}(\mathbf{X}_{s}^{[K]})}/{f_{G}(\mathbf{X}_{s}^{[K]})}\right)

\tau_{A}^{\mathcal{W}}(c)
is the first time at which all components of the multidimensional random walk
\{\bm{R}_{t}:t\geq 1\}

[
27

to approximate

(see Appendix
C

Remark 8
.

For the asymmetric case

More importantly, in the symmetric case

[

4.1
Generalized Misclassification Rate

In this subsection, we focus on Class
1
,
\Delta_{m_{0}}^{\mathrm{gmr}}(\alpha)
, which controls the generalized misclassification rate below level
\alpha\in(0,1)
and assumes no prior information, that is,
\mathcal{A}=2^{[K]}
.
We start by reviewing the Sum-Intersection rule, denoted by

\delta_{S}(b)=(T_{S}(b),D_{S}(b))
, proposed in

[
32
]
, where
b>0
is a threshold parameter.
For each
t\geq 1
, denote by

0<\tilde{\lambda}_{t}^{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{t}^{(K)}

(25)

the order statistics of the absolute LLRs

.
Note that if

is small in absolute value, it provides

Then, for

\displaystyle T_{S}(b)

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{t\geq 1:\sum_{i=1}^{m_{0}}\tilde{\lambda}_{t}^{(i)}\geq b\right\},\quad D_{S}(b):=\left\{k\in[K]:\lambda_{T_{S}(b)}^{k}>0\right\}.

That is, the procedure stops at the first time when the sum of the

m_{0}
least significant LLRs exceeds

hypothesis is rejected in every stream whose LLR is positive.

It is shown in Theorem 3.1 of
[

that, for

(see Theorem 3.3 in
[
32
]
).
The next theorem establishes the second-order optimality of
\{\delta_{S}(b_{\alpha})\}
. We include the proof here to illustrate the application of Theorem
1
.

.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds. For each
\alpha>0
, let the threshold be chosen as

in (
27

\{\Delta_{m_{0}}^{\mathrm{gmr}}(\alpha)\}

\pi_{0}
be the uniform distribution on
2^{[K]}

\pi_{0}(A)=1/2^{K}
for

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(D\mid A)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\;\;|D\triangle A|\geq m_{0}\\

That is,

m_{0}

4.2
Generalized Familywise Error Rates

In this subsection, we focus on Class
2
,
\Delta_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\mathrm{gfr}}(\alpha,\beta)
, which controls the generalized familywise false positive and false negative rates below levels
\alpha
and
\beta
, respectively, and assumes no prior information, that is,
\mathcal{A}=2^{[K]}
.
We start by reviewing the Leap rule, denoted by

\delta_{L}(a,b)=(T_{L}(a,b),D_{L}(a,b))
, proposed in

[
32
]
, where
a,b>0
are threshold parameters.

Recall the LLRs
\{\lambda_{t}^{k}:k\in[K],\,t=1,2,\ldots\}
defined in (
24
). For each time
t\geq 1
, denote by

0<\hat{\lambda}_{t}^{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\hat{\lambda}_{t}^{(p(t))}

the ordered statistics of the positive LLRs
\{\lambda_{t}^{k}:\lambda_{t}^{k}>0,\,k\in[K]\}
, where
p(t)
is the number of strictly positive LLRs at time
t
. Similarly, denote by

0<\check{\lambda}_{t}^{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\check{\lambda}_{t}^{(K-p(t))}

the ordered statistics of the absolute values of the nonpositive LLRs
\{|\lambda_{t}^{k}|:\lambda_{t}^{k}\leq 0,\,k\in[K]\}
.

We also denote the corresponding stream indices by
\{\hat{\ell}_{1}(t),\ldots,\hat{\ell}_{p(t)}(t)\}
such that
\hat{\lambda}_{t}^{\hat{\ell}_{i}(t)}=\hat{\lambda}_{t}^{(i)}
for
i\in[p(t)]
, and by
\{\check{\ell}_{1}(t),\ldots,\check{\ell}_{K-p(t)}(t)\}
such that
|\lambda_{t}^{\check{\ell}_{j}(t)}|=\check{\lambda}_{t}^{(j)}
for
j\in[K-p(t)]
. For the statistic
\hat{\lambda}_{t}^{(i)}
, a smaller index
i
indicates weaker evidence for rejecting the
k
-th null hypothesis (and thus a higher risk of a Type-I error). Similarly, for
\check{\lambda}_{t}^{(i)}
, a smaller index
i
indicates weaker evidence for accepting the
k
-th null hypothesis (and thus a higher risk of a Type-II error).

For thresholds
a,b>0
, we first define a collection of rules as follows: for
i=0,\ldots,m_{1}-1
,

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{i}:=\min\left\{t\geq 1:\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}-i}\hat{\lambda}_{t}^{(j)}\geq b,\ \sum_{j=i+1}^{i+m_{2}}\check{\lambda}_{t}^{(j)}\geq a\right\},\;\;\hat{D}_{i}:=\{k\in[K]:\lambda_{\hat{\tau}_{i}}^{k}>0\}\ \cup\ \{\check{\ell}_{1}(\hat{\tau}_{i}),\ldots,\check{\ell}_{i}(\hat{\tau}_{i})\},

and for
i=1,\ldots,m_{2}-1
,

\displaystyle\check{\tau}_{i}:=\min\left\{t\geq 1:\sum_{j=i+1}^{i+m_{1}}\hat{\lambda}_{t}^{(j)}\geq b,\ \sum_{j=1}^{m_{2}-i}\check{\lambda}_{t}^{(j)}\geq a\right\},\;\;\check{D}_{i}:=\{k\in[K]:\lambda_{\check{\tau}_{i}}^{k}>0\}\ \setminus\ \{\hat{\ell}_{1}(\check{\tau}_{i}),\ldots,\hat{\ell}_{i}(\check{\tau}_{i})\}.

The dependence on
(a,b)
is suppressed for simplicity. Furthermore, we adopt the convention that
\hat{\lambda}_{t}^{k}=\infty
for
k>p(t)
and
\check{\lambda}_{t}^{k}=\infty
for
k>K-p(t)
.

Then, the leap rule
\delta_{L}(a,b)
with parameters
a,b>0
is defined by

\displaystyle T_{L}(a,b):=\min\{\hat{\tau}_{0},\cdots,\hat{\tau}_{m_{1}-1},\check{\tau}_{1},\ldots,\check{\tau}_{m_{2}-1}\},\quad D_{L}(a,b)=\begin{cases}\hat{D}_{i},&\text{if }\hat{\tau}_{i}=T_{L}(a,b),\\
\check{D}_{i},&\text{if }\check{\tau}_{i}=T_{L}(a,b),\end{cases}

(31)

where, if the minimum is attained by multiple stopping rules,
D_{L}
is defined as the union of the corresponding decisions. We refer the interested reader to Section 4 of
[
32
]
for motivation and discussion of the Leap rule.

It is shown in Theorem 4.1 of
[
32
]
that for any
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)

[
32

\alpha
and
\beta

C^{-1}\alpha\leq\beta\leq C\alpha,\quad\text{for some absolute constant }C>0.

4.4
Discussion of Optimality

It is instructive to compare our second-order results with classical optimality results in related settings.

In the single stream case (
K=1
), Wald’s sequential probability ratio test (SPRT)
[
35
]
, which coincides with the Intersection rule in (
35
), is
exactly
optimal in Class
2
with
m_{1}=m_{2}=1
, i.e.,
\Delta_{1,1}^{\mathrm{gfr}}(\alpha,\beta)
. When the thresholds
(a,b)
are chosen so that the Type-I and Type-II error probabilities equal
(\alpha,\beta)
, the SPRT simultaneously minimizes
\mathbb{E}_{0}[T]
and
\mathbb{E}_{1}[T]
over all sequential tests satisfying the same error constraints; see, e.g.,
[
34
,
36
]
. Thus, in this one stream setting, the minimal achievable ESS is attained exactly by the SPRT.

Next, we review the classical multi-hypothesis testing problem

[
24
,
12
,
11
]
,
which is closely related to the multiple-stream setting considered in this paper.
In this formulation, one observes i.i.d. data
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
whose common distribution belongs to a finite collection
\{f_{i}:i\in[M]\}
of competing
\mu
-densities. A sequential procedure consists of a stopping time
T
and a terminal decision
D\in[M]
. Error control is typically expressed either through the family of error probabilities
\{\mathbb{P}_{i}(D=j):i\neq j\in[M]\}
or through weighted risks of the form
\{\sum_{j\in[M]}\omega_{i,j}\mathbb{P}_{i}(D=j):i\in[M]\}
, where
\omega_{i,j}\geq 0
are fixed weights and
\mathbb{P}_{i}
denotes the distribution under hypothesis
i
.

In contrast to the single hypothesis case, exact optimality is generally unavailable once
M\geq 3
. Lorden
[
24
]
proposed the
matrix SPRT
, a multidimensional extension of Wald’s SPRT, and established its
third-order
asymptotic optimality under each
\mathbb{P}_{i}
,
i\in[M]
. More precisely, with appropriately chosen thresholds, the ESS of the matrix SPRT differs by
o(1)
from the minimal achievable ESS among procedures whose error probabilities are no larger than those of the matrix SPRT, either in terms of the full collection
\{\mathbb{P}_{i}(D=j)\}
or the weighted risks.

It is important to note that this optimality result holds within a restricted comparison class of procedures satisfying
\mathbb{P}_{i}(D=j)\to 0
for all
i\neq j
as the error control becomes more stringent. Such a property does not hold for several of the multiple testing classes considered in this paper, including Class
1
when
m_{0}>1
, Class
2
when
m_{1}+m_{2}>2
, and Class
3
. The procedures studied in the previous subsections, while computationally efficient and second-order optimal, are not expected to achieve third-order optimality within their respective classes.

6
Conclusions and Future Work

We establish a unified second-order asymptotic theory for sequential multiple testing with simultaneous stopping across independent data streams. Through an associated Bayesian formulation with a properly specified cost parameter and loss function, we provide sufficient conditions under which second-order Bayesian optimality implies second-order frequentist optimality. Under these conditions, several procedures previously known to be first-order optimal under various error metrics and information structures are in fact second-order optimal: for every signal configuration, their excess ESS over the minimal achievable one remains uniformly bounded as the error tolerances vanish.

We also obtain a second-order asymptotic expansion of the minimal achievable ESS. Under suitable moment conditions and a uniqueness condition on the most favorable alternative induced by the loss, we identify the second-order correction to the leading logarithmic term. Its form depends on whether the associated boundary-crossing problem is asymmetric or symmetric. These results sharpen the classical first-order theory.

Several directions remain open. First, for all classes considered in this paper except Class
2
, each signal subset
A\in\mathcal{A}
admits a unique most favorable subset under a properly chosen loss function
\mathcal{W}
. For Class
2
, we provide a counterexample showing that this uniqueness may fail and give sufficient conditions under which it holds. A complete second-order characterization of the minimal achievable ESS for Class
2
remains open.

Second, in the symmetric case, the remainder term in the ESS expansion is of order
O((\log(c_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}))^{\epsilon})
for arbitrary
\epsilon\in(1/4,1/2)
. Numerical evidence suggests that the approximation error may in fact remain bounded, indicating that sharper analysis could improve the remainder bound. Third, for several classes, including Class
2
and Class
3
, first-order asymptotic optimality does not require the error tolerances (e.g.,
\alpha
and
\beta
) to converge to zero at the same rate (see condition (
33
)). Extending the second-order theory to such more general regimes is of interest. Finally, it would be natural to investigate extensions to composite hypotheses and to settings with dependent data streams as in
[
6
]
.

. Since

\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[\log\frac{f_{A}(\mathbf{X}_{s}^{[K]})}{f_{G}(\mathbf{X}_{s}^{[K]})}\right]=\textup{KL}(f_{A}|f_{G})>0,

by Theorem 1 in
[
18
]
, as
c\rightarrow 0
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[(\tau_{G}(0))^{2}\right]=O(1)
. Finally, by Markov’s inequality and since
1/\mu^{*}-1/\theta>0
, for each
G\in\mathcal{H}_{A,*}^{\mathcal{W}}
, as
c\rightarrow 0
,

\mathbb{P}_{A}\left(\tau_{G}(\widehat{T}_{A,G}(c))\geq\frac{L_{c}}{\mu^{*}}-\frac{L_{c}}{\theta}-1\right)=O(L_{c}^{-2}).

The proof is complete.
∎

C
Asymptotic ESS of stopping times associated with multidimensional random walks

Let
\bm{V}_{1},\bm{V}_{2},\ldots
be a sequence of
d

\bm{R}_{n}=\bm{V}_{1}+\bm{V}_{2}+\cdots+\bm{V}_{n}
for

the associated random walk. For each

, we denote by
\mu_{j}:=\mathbb{E}[\bm{V}_{1,j}]
, where
\bm{V}_{1,j}
represents the
j

\bm{V}_{1}
. Moreover, we denote by
\tilde{\bm{V}}_{t,j}:=\bm{V}_{t,j}-\mu_{j}
for

, and then

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{n,j}-n\mu_{j}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\tilde{\bm{V}}_{t,j},\quad\text{ for }n\geq 1.

(48)

We consider the stopping time

T_{b}:=\inf\left\{n\geq 1:\min_{j\in[d]}\bm{R}_{n,j}\geq b\right\},

(49)

that is, the first time that all components of the random walk exceed a threshold. In this section, we assume

0<\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{d}.

Let
1\leq r_{*}\leq d
be the unique integer such that

\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\ldots=\mu_{r_{*}}<\mu_{r_{*}+1},

(50)

where
\mu_{d+1}:=+\infty
.
We refer to the case
r_{*}=1
as the asymmetric setup, and to
r_{*}\neq 1
as the symmetric setup.

In this section, for some
q\geq 2
to be specified, we assume that

\mathbb{E}\left(|\tilde{\bm{V}}_{1,j}|^{q}\right)<\infty\text{ for all $j\in[d]$}.

(51)

Theorem 9
.

Consider the asymmetric setup, i.e.,
r_{*}=1
. Assume (
51
) holds for some

.

[

for

, and rewrite

as a perturbed random walk:

]

We note that the proof of Theorem 3.3 in

appears to be incorrect. In (A.14) and (A.15) therein, they define

where

is a fixed hypothesis,

are constants,

|B_{0}^{-}\cap L_{1}|\geq|B_{0}^{-}|-|L_{4}|=m_{2}+i^{*}-|L_{4}|=m_{2}-|L_{2}|.

Next, since

This completes the proof.
∎
