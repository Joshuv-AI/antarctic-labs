AI-assisted improvement of endoscopic diagnosis of ampullary lesions
Authors: Wootaek Seo, Youngmi Park, Hee-Eon Lim, Namyoung Park, Kwangrok Jung, Jong-chan Lee, Soyeon Ahn, Jin-Hyeok Hwang, Jaihwan Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ampullary neoplasms are often challenging to detect during forward-viewing esophagogastroduodenoscopy. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) assistance on the accuracy of endoscopists in identifying ampullary lesions. The stand-alone AI model achieved an accuracy of 84.2%, with AUCs ranging from 83.2% to 90.9% across binary classification tasks. With AI assistance, mean diagnostic accuracies significantly improved for endoscopy fellows (63.5%–75.8%, P < 0.001) and gastrointestinal endoscopists (68.4%–76.5%, P = 0.012), while no significant difference was observed for pancreatobiliary endoscopists (70.8%–73.1%, P = 1.000). In binary tasks, AI assistance significantly enhanced specificity for two fellows in distinguishing normal from abnormal ampullae (P = 0.023 and P = 0.041) and for another fellow in differentiating malignancy (67.4%–89.1%, P = 0.004). These improvements in accuracy and specificity, particularly among less-experienced endoscopists, were achieved without compromising sensitivity for the detection of abnormal lesions. AI assistance enhanced diagnostic accuracy and specificity for ampullary lesions, particularly among non-pancreatobiliary endoscopists, without compromising sensitivity. These findings demonstrate the retrospective proof-of-concept potential of AI assistance, highlighting the need for subsequent prospective and video-based validation to confirm its clinical utility in the evaluation of ampullary lesions.

Introduction

Ampulla of Vater (AoV) carcinoma is a rare but clinically significant malignancy arising at the junction of the bile and pancreatic ducts1,2. This malignancy is clinically notable because it can cause obstruction of the bile or pancreatic ducts, leading to complications such as cholangitis or pancreatitis. Based on population-based cancer registry data from the United States, the age-adjusted incidence of ampullary carcinoma is approximately 0.74 per 100,000 person-years3. Ampullary adenomas are recognized precursor lesions of AoV carcinoma. Although adenomas can undergo malignant transformation, early-detected mucosal adenomas can be removed by endoscopic papillectomy, thereby preventing progression to carcinoma without the need for surgery4.

Early detection of asymptomatic ampullary lesions remains challenging. In clinical practice, such lesions are often incidentally detected during forward-viewing esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and subsequently evaluated using side-viewing duodenoscopy with histologic examination. Although EGD is an initial modality for detecting ampullary lesions, distinguishing these lesions from the normal AoV is difficult because they are only partially visualized and are often small or flat5.

Deep learning models trained on endoscopic images have shown promise in improving the detection of gastrointestinal lesions, particularly colorectal polyps. Several studies have demonstrated that artificial intelligence (AI)–based models can achieve performance comparable to or even superior to that of expert endoscopists in polyp detection and histologic prediction6–8.

Given the anatomical complexity and diagnostic challenges associated with ampullary lesions, we hypothesized that a deep learning model trained on EGD images could improve diagnostic accuracy in detecting and classifying AoV lesions. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of AI assistance on the diagnostic performance of endoscopists in the diagnosis of AoV lesions.

Discussion

In this study of ampullary lesion classification using forward-viewing endoscopic images, AI assistance from a no-code deep learning model demonstrated the potential to improve diagnostic performance; however, this benefit was not uniform across all experience levels or performance metrics. While significant improvements in accuracy were observed among endoscopy fellows and gastrointestinal endoscopists, the impact was less pronounced in pancreatobiliary experts, likely due to their high baseline performance. Furthermore, the improvement in specificity was mainly observed among less-experienced endoscopists. These findings suggest that the output of the model can augment human image interpretation and support diagnostic decision-making, with more substantial benefits for less-experienced endoscopists. As a result, this study provides proof-of-concept evidence that AI assistance can improve endoscopists’ diagnostic evaluation and suggests potential roles through which AI could contribute to clinical practice in endoscopy.

This is consistent with recent paradigm shifts in gastrointestinal endoscopy where AI mitigates the performance disparity between novices and experts, thereby standardizing interpretation and reducing inter-endoscopist variability9,10. In particular, a landmark multicenter randomized trial demonstrated that real-time AI assistance significantly increased the adenoma detection rate during colonoscopy, highlighting its clinical impact in reducing the risk of missed lesions11. Although research specific to AI-assisted AoV diagnosis remains limited, our findings align with prior evidence suggesting that AI provides notable benefits to less-experienced clinicians12–14.

While prior studies primarily established AI’s role in lesion detection within the colon12–14, our findings highlight a notable distinction. Unlike the relatively uniform colonic mucosa, where AI primarily aids in identifying distinct polyps, AoV assessment inherently presents diagnostic ambiguity. Our study adds value to the literature by proving that in the context of the AoV, AI’s primary utility extends beyond simple identification to refined characterization. This shift in focus is essential for navigating the inherent complexity of the ampullary region, a clinical challenge that has been less emphasized in prior colonoscopy-based AI research. The clinical importance of improved characterization using AI is underscored by existing quality standards of EGD. According to the guidelines of the KSGE and the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE), documentation of the AoV is considered a mandatory quality indicator during screening EGD15,16; therefore, the AoV is routinely imaged during EGD in patients with normal anatomy.

The morphological complexity of the ampulla often results in diagnostic ambiguity, leading to potential over-diagnosis and unnecessary interventions17. Our results also support this, particularly for less-experienced endoscopists who showed higher baseline false-positive rates. AI assistance effectively addressed this by enhancing specificity in certain individuals within the fellow group; however, no significant improvement was observed among gastrointestinal or pancreatobiliary endoscopists. This suggests that the impact of AI on reducing false positives may be limited for endoscopists who have already surpassed a certain threshold of diagnostic expertise. While sensitivity showed minor, statistically non-significant fluctuations, this selective improvement in specificity among trainees is clinically valuable as it reduces the need for unnecessary biopsies or diagnostic workups. Although a comprehensive work-up remains essential18, our findings demonstrate that AI could streamline the diagnostic process and optimize resource utilization, particularly in settings where specialized expertise is limited.

In this study, the impact of AI assistance was less pronounced in the pancreatobiliary endoscopists, as their diagnostic performance was already high at baseline. Therefore, AI assistance did not compromise diagnostic quality, and its performance remained stable between the unassisted and assisted sessions. These results suggest that AI can serve as a reliable second opinion or triage tool, achieving diagnostic performance comparable to that of experts19. This approach promotes synergy by pairing machine-based functions with clinician expertise20.

Regarding the baseline differences in age and sex, these disparities are unlikely to have influenced the primary outcomes. All diagnostic assessments were performed based solely on the morphological features of the images without access to patient demographic information. Because each endoscopist served as their own control in this paired study design, the potential confounding effects of patient characteristics on the comparative results were effectively minimized.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective still-image design may not fully reflect the dynamic conditions of real-time endoscopy, including variations in lighting, viewing angle, motion, and artifacts. In addition, this was a single-center study without external validation, and the model may therefore be affected by institution-specific image characteristics or algorithmic bias. Future studies should validate the model prospectively in multicenter, video-based datasets that include heterogeneous endoscopic systems and endoscopists with varying levels of experience. Second, selection and spectrum bias may have been introduced by requiring histopathological confirmation. Although this approach allowed inclusion of diagnostically challenging benign variants that were initially suspected to be abnormal, it may limit generalizability to routine real-world screening settings, where many normal ampullae have more typical appearances. Third, due to the de-identified nature of the retrospective image dataset, the dataset split was conducted at the image level rather than at the patient or examination level. Although identical image files were not duplicated across subsets, different images captured from varying angles or orientations of the same patient could potentially be distributed between the training and test sets. This overlap introduces a risk of data leakage, which may inadvertently inflate the standalone performance metrics of the AI model. Fourth, the relatively small test set is a limitation regarding the generalizability of our findings, and further validation with larger, more diverse cohorts is warranted. Specifically, a precision-based assessment indicates that this test-set size of 57 images supports a 95% confidence interval half-width of approximately ±13 to ±10 percentage points as the observed proportion ranges from 50% to 80%. Nevertheless, the consistent experience-dependent pattern of AI benefit among endoscopists provides some internal support for the coherence of our findings. Additionally, the fixed sequential reader design may have introduced order effects or second-look learning effects despite the 3-month washout period. Furthermore, group-level confidence intervals were computed at the marginal level, pooling reader-by-image classifications within each group, and therefore do not explicitly model the correlation induced by multiple endoscopists reading the same images. Our analysis focused on each reader-level group's performance and AI-assisted changes, while image difficulty was controlled by evaluating the same images under both conditions. In a sensitivity analysis using a reader-clustered bootstrap, the resulting intervals were of similar width (clustered-to-marginal half-width ratio 0.47–1.32, predominantly below 1), indicating that reader-level correlation was unlikely to have affected the results. We also considered the risk of automation bias; while AI assistance effectively reduced unnecessary workups, marginal increases in false negatives were observed in specific cases. This underscores the importance of clinician oversight and suggests that future models should be optimized with more conservative decision thresholds for malignant lesions to minimize the clinical cost of missed diagnoses. Finally, the inability of the model to autonomously learn from new cases post-training or provide explicit interpretability for its logic remains a significant barrier to establishing the trust necessary for routine clinical practice21.

In conclusion, AI assistance was associated with improved diagnostic accuracy for ampullary lesions, particularly among endoscopy fellows, though the effect was smaller in more experienced groups. Our findings demonstrate the retrospective proof-of-concept potential of AI assistance and highlight the need for subsequent external, prospective, and video-based validation to confirm generalizability and clinical utility.

Methods

Study design and population

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital between March 2003 and June 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients who underwent EGD (GIF-H290; Olympus Optical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and were suspected of having ampullary lesions, and (2) patients with a definitive pathological diagnosis of a normal ampulla, ampullary adenoma, or ampullary carcinoma, established through subsequent evaluation using side-viewing duodenoscopy (TJF-240 or JF-240; Olympus Optical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

The exclusion criteria were: (1) patients who had suspected ampullary lesions on other imaging modalities prior to EGD, and (2) patients without a pathological diagnosis. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (No. B-2407-915-108). Informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study. To protect patient privacy, all data were de-identified by removing personal identifiers, including names, medical record numbers, and dates of birth from both the image frames and metadata before analysis.

Image curation and reference standard

Ampulla-focused images were retrieved from the institutional digital archive. Endoscopic images were excluded if they met objective criteria for technical inadequacy based on the quality assurance guidelines of the Korean Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (KSGE)15: (1) severe motion blur or defocusing, (2) >50% mucosal obscuration by bubbles or bile, (3) inadequate luminal expansion or improper exposure, or (4) incomplete visualization of the ampullary circumference or significant structural distortion by active bleeding. Images were selected by two endoscopists and labeled according to endoscopic reports. The remaining cases were categorized into three diagnostic groups (normal, adenoma, or carcinoma) based on histopathological examination, which served as the reference standard. Importantly, all cases in the normal ampulla group underwent histological evaluation (via biopsy) to definitively rule out adenoma or carcinoma, ensuring a rigorous reference standard. Histopathological diagnosis was based on the final clinical pathology report issued after routine institutional review. In our pathology workflow, specimens were initially assessed by a pathology resident and finalized by a gastrointestinal pathologist. Because the study used finalized clinical pathology reports rather than a separate blinded dual-pathologist review, inter-rater agreement statistics were not available.

AI model development

The AI model was developed using a no-code deep learning pipeline provided by Neurocle Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). An EfficientNetV2-B1 architecture was employed for the image classification model, targeting medical image analysis. This specific architecture consists of a total of 6,931,124 parameters (6,860,052 trainable parameters and 71,072 non-trainable parameters). The model was initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights and subsequently fine-tuned on the study dataset. The model was trained for 268 epochs (using the Adam optimizer) with a learning rate of 0.0005 and a batch size of 14. To enhance model generalizability and mitigate overfitting, data augmentation techniques were applied during training, including brightness adjustment, saturation adjustment, horizontal and vertical flipping, and rotation. Model performance was evaluated using a stratified hold-out validation strategy, in which the dataset was split into training and validation subsets while preserving the class distribution within each subset. Crucially, the 57-image test set remained completely independent and sequestered; it was never used for hyperparameter tuning, threshold optimization, or model selection decisions during development. The hardware system used for model development included an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 GPU (16 GB), a 12th-generation Intel® Core™ i7-12700 CPU, and 64 GB of RAM, ensuring efficient processing of the study dataset.

Endoscopist subgroups and review process

Thirteen endoscopists participated in the study, including three pancreatobiliary endoscopists, five gastrointestinal endoscopists, and five endoscopy fellows. All endoscopists independently reviewed the same test image set and classified each image as normal, adenoma, or carcinoma. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to these three endoscopist categories.

Each endoscopist completed two separate review sessions in a fixed sequential order: an initial session without AI support (unassisted) to establish a baseline, followed by a second session with access to the AI model output (AI-assisted). This fixed order was employed to prevent the AI model’s predictions from influencing the baseline (unassisted) interpretation. To minimize recall bias and potential order effects, a washout period of at least three months was strictly maintained between the two sessions. During the AI-assisted session, endoscopists were provided with the model’s predicted class label before submitting their final assessment. All responses were collected using structured survey forms.

Diagnostic classification and performance evaluation

Two diagnostic frameworks were evaluated. The first was a three-class classification distinguishing normal, adenoma, and carcinoma. The second consisted of two binary classification tasks: (1) normal versus abnormal (adenoma or carcinoma), and (2) non-malignant (normal or adenoma) versus malignant (carcinoma). These classifications were used to calculate sensitivity, specificity, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with corresponding areas under the curve (AUCs), and to facilitate paired comparisons among AI-only predictions, unassisted endoscopist assessments, and AI-assisted endoscopist assessments.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the comparison of diagnostic performance metrics between endoscopists with and without AI assistance. Performance metrics included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, confusion matrices, and AUCs. Endoscopist performance was summarized both at the individual level and by subgroup. For each reader group and condition, all reader-by-image classifications were aggregated into a single 3 × 3 contingency table cross-classifying the reference standard against the reader classification. Marginal accuracy and macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 score were computed from this table. Multi-class outcomes were summarized using confusion matrices, whereas binary classification tasks were evaluated using ROC curves and AUCs. To visualize within-endoscopist changes, dumbbell plots were generated to depict per-endoscopist variation in sensitivity following AI assistance. To evaluate categorical shifts between unassisted and AI-assisted assessments, 3 × 3 contingency tables were constructed based on the true diagnostic categories.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, and comorbidities, were compared across diagnostic groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. All subsequent analyses employed paired designs using identical image sets. Proportions are reported with 95% confidence intervals. The 95% confidence interval for accuracy was derived from the Wald binomial interval. Because precision, recall, and F1 are nonlinear functions of multiple correlated counts, their 95% confidence intervals were estimated by nonparametric bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations). Paired comparisons of diagnostic performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy, were conducted using McNemar’s test. The Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons in subgroup analyses. Paired ROC curve comparisons were performed using the DeLong test. As a sensitivity analysis, reader-clustered bootstrap intervals were compared with the marginal-level intervals, so that the widening factor would indicate the contribution of the reader-level correlation structure. All dumbbell plots and statistical significance testing were performed using R software version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Results were reported both at the individual endoscopist level and aggregated by endoscopist group.
