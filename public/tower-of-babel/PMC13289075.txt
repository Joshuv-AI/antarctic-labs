Enhancing screening, early diagnosis and treatment initiation of oral, breast and cervical cancer in selected districts of India: an implementation research protocol
Authors: Ankita Kankaria, Pallavi Shukla, M Vijayakumar, Amit Sachdeva, Malliga J Subramanian, Prasanta Kumar Borah, Soumya Swaroop Sahoo, Abhay Nirgude, Ranjan Kumar Prusty, Selvavinayagam T S, Krishna Asuri, Pulkit Verma, Jyoti Sharma, Rupinder Singh Dhaliwal, Shahina Begum, Tanvir Kaur, ICMR-NHRP Cancer Screening Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite implementation of the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD), screening coverage for oral, breast and cervical cancers remains below 2%. Screening quality is inadequately addressed and delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation continue to persist. This multisite implementation research aims to improve district-level coverage and quality of screening, early diagnosis and timeliness of treatment initiation through a model co-developed within the NP-NCD context.Methods and analysisThe study will be conducted in three phases across seven districts in diverse regions of India. In phase I (formative), the current status, barriers and facilitators of cancer screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation under NP-NCD will be assessed. In phase II (optimisation), a model (package of implementation strategies) will be co-developed and iteratively optimised with multistakeholder engagement at the subdistrict level to improve screening coverage and quality and strengthen the referral system for early diagnosis and treatment initiation. In phase III (scale-up and evaluation), the model will be implemented at the district level and evaluated for improvements in screening, early diagnosis and treatment initiation. A convergent mixed-methods design will be used, incorporating household surveys, facility assessments and stakeholder interviews. Implementation Research Logic Model will guide planning, execution and evaluation in the present study. Determinants of screening coverage and quality, early diagnosis and treatment initiation will be assessed using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research. Implementation strategies for the model will be finalised using the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change framework. Implementation and service outcomes will be evaluated using the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance framework.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been obtained from all study sites. The study findings will be disseminated at the state, national and global levels through meetings and conferences and submitted to a peer-reviewed journal for publication.Trial registration numberCTRI/2025/08/092672.Keywords: Mass Screening, Implementation Science, India, Health Services, Health Services Accessibility

Introduction

Globally, substantial disparities persist in cancer diagnosis and treatment, with low- and middle-income countries bearing a disproportionate share of the growing cancer burden. India ranks third globally, with an estimated 1.41 million cancer cases and 0.91 million cancer-related deaths annually.1,3 Approximately one in nine individuals in India is likely to develop cancer during their lifetime, and the cancer-associated disability-adjusted life-years are projected to increase from 26.7 million in 2021 to 29.8 million by 2025. Breast, cervix uteri, lip and oral cavity cancers collectively account for 463 305 new cases and about 30% of cancer-related deaths.13,5 . Nearly 70% of cancer cases are detected at advanced stages because of a lack of awareness and suboptimal implementation of organised breast, cervical and oral cancer screening programmes.6 Cost-effective screening methods aid in early detection, reducing cancer-associated morbidity and mortality and increasing survival.7,9 The National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS) was launched in 2010 with an aim to improve opportunistic screening of oral, breast and cervical cancers.10 The initial focus was on facility-level screening; however, in 2016, population-based screening of cancers started to ensure access to the community and to ensure continuity of care.11 Despite these efforts, cancer screening coverage in India remains extremely low (below 2%). For women, the rates of participation in cancer screening are cervical (1.9%), breast (0.9%) and oral cavity (0.9%). For men, the rate of participation in oral cavity cancer screening is 1.2%.12 Documented reasons for poor coverage include limited awareness and stigma at the community level and health system constraints such as a shortage of trained staff, multitasking, weak data management, interrupted supply of logistics, inadequate functional referral linkages and a lack of quality assurance protocols.13,17 In a diverse country like India, with cultural variations and region-specific health system challenges, implementation research (IR) can help identify and address gaps in government programmes.1 IR examines what works, why and how to improve the implementation and scale-up of evidence-based interventions in specific contexts. Hence, within the context of the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD) (formerly named NPCDCS),18 this multisite IR study across seven Indian districts aims to develop and evaluate models to improve screening, early diagnosis and treatment initiation for oral, breast and cervical cancers.

Aim and objectives

Aim

This study aims to develop a model to improve the coverage and quality of screening and decrease delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation for oral, breast and cervical cancer within the context of the NP-NCD programme in study districts, with district implementation partners, and evaluate its performance.

Objectives

To assess the current status, barriers and facilitators in screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation for oral, breast and cervical cancer within the NP-NCD programme in the target population in the district.

Methods and analysis

The study will be conducted over a period of 39 months from 1 September 2024 to 1 December 2027, including a preparatory phase (3 months), formative phase (phase I, 6 months), model co-development and optimisation phase (phase II, 12 months) and scale-up and evaluation phase (phase III, 18 months).

Study setting

The study will be conducted in seven districts of India selected purposively for geographical representation: Bathinda (Punjab), Shimla (Himachal Pradesh), South Delhi (Delhi) —North, Dibrugarh (Assam)—North East, Palghar (Maharashtra)—West, Dakshina Kannada (Karnataka) and Tiruvannamalai (Tamil Nadu)—South (figure 1). A Technical Coordinating Unit (TCU) will be established at Post Graduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh. This study will be conducted among urban and rural populations and in public health facilities of these districts. These public health facilities are mandated to provide preventive and curative cancer care at primary/secondary and tertiary levels, respectively. Primary level facilities include Sub Centres (SC)/Health and Wellness Centres (HWC)/Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (AAM)/Primary Health Centres (PHC)/Urban Primary Health Centres (UPHC)/Maternal and Child Welfare (MCW) Centres; secondary level of facilities include Community Health Centres (CHC)/Urban Community Health Centres/Sub District Hospitals (SDH)/District Hospitals (DH), and tertiary level of facilities include medical colleges. ASHAs conduct a house-to-house count and complete the Community-Based Assessment Checklist (CBAC) for every 1000 rural people or one USHA/ASHA for every 2000 urban people (figure 2). ASHAs enrol all eligible individuals and facilitate their referral to primary facilities (SC/HWC/AAM/PHC/UPHC/MCW centres) for screening of three common cancers and for thermal ablation/cryotherapy of eligible women with cervical lesions. All screen-positive cases are referred to CHC, SDH or DH for confirmation of diagnosis. Diagnosed cases are then referred to tertiary facilities, such as medical colleges/cancer centres for comprehensive cancer care, including prevention, early detection, diagnosis, treatment, palliative care and rehabilitation. The details of district-wise healthcare facilities and the total population, with the target population, are given in the online supplemental file I: tables 1 and 2, respectively. We will use the Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies checklist as a reporting standard.19

Study design

This study is a multi-phase mixed-methods implementation research study.

Study population

Under the NP-NCD programme, the target population for breast and cervical cancer screening includes females aged ≥30 years and, for oral cancer, both males and females aged ≥30 years. The study population will include a subset of the target population in the selected districts, healthcare providers (Accredited Social Health Activist (ASHA)/Auxiliary Nurse Midwife/Community Health Officer/Medical Officer, specialists), programme officers, voluntary organisations, cancer patients and their caregivers.

Study team

The study team will be divided into an implementation team (government partners) and a research team (investigators). The research team will be subdivided into the implementation support team (IST), the programme learning team (PLT) and the outcome monitoring team (OMT). Study framework

The Implementation Research Logic Model (IRLM) will be used to identify determinants, processes/strategies and outcomes throughout different phases of the study (figure 3). The determinants (facilitators and barriers) of coverage and quality of cancer screening, early diagnosis and treatment initiation will be assessed using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). These determinants will be assessed across five domains: intervention characteristics (evidence-based guidelines, adaptability in local settings, cost of screening, diagnostic and treatment modalities, etc); inner setting (physical infrastructure suitable for screening and diagnosis, NCD portal for data-driven monitoring and decision-making, funds for awareness, screening and referral, system readiness for implementation, priority for cancer care, etc); outer setting (NP-NCD guidelines, Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana, state-specific financial schemes, societal norms around cancer care seeking, etc); and individuals (role in PBCS, knowledge about cancer prevention, detection and treatment, belief, motivation and capabilities needed to ensure continuum of cancer care, etc). The Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) framework will be used to develop strategies to address barriers and co-develop an implementation model (model ‘X’). Some of these strategies can include developing stakeholder relationships and identifying champions (for supporting and sustaining streamlined cancer care in the district), engaging beneficiaries (for improving screening coverage), customisation (of the available health promotion and training material in local language), periodic training (of healthcare providers), formation of quality improvement teams (for supportive supervision) and regular feedback (to policymakers for required changes). Model ‘0’ refers to the package of current practices for cancer screening, diagnosis and treatment under NP-NCD. These practices will be assessed during the formative phase (phase I). Model ‘0 Plus’ refers to the package of strategies that will be co-developed to address the gaps identified in phase I through a literature review and consultation with researchers and programme implementers. Model ‘X’ will be iteratively optimised, and its outcome will be evaluated through the RE-AIM framework: reach (coverage and quality of screening, diagnostic, treatment and referral services); effectiveness (% change in screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation from baseline to endline); adoption (willingness to deploy model ‘X’); implementation (health providers’ consistency in delivering model ‘X’) and maintenance (policy adoption of model ‘X’) (figure 3).20 21

Phase-wise implementation strategy

Phase I (formative phase-06 months)

The formative study employs a convergent mixed-methods design, including qualitative data gathered by the PLT and quantitative data collected by the OMT. The purpose of formative research includes assessing the implementation status of the NP-NCD programme (model ‘0’), exploring individual, family, community and system-level enablers and barriers to cancer screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation, and documenting key indicators for screening, diagnosis and treatment of cancers. Phase-wise details of the study are presented in figure 4.

(a) Overall design of implementation research, (b) Phase wise objectives, study tools and outcomes. FGD, Focus Group Discussion; IDI, In-Depth Interview; NFI, Non-Formal Interviews; NP-NCD, National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases.

Phase II (model co-development and optimisation-12 months)

This phase seeks to collaboratively create and continuously refine an implementation model based on key performance metrics within a selected PHC. Model ‘0 Plus’ will be developed during the first 3 months and will be implemented over the next 3–4 months. A first rapid survey will be done to evaluate the model performance and to iteratively optimise the model ‘0 Plus’ to model ‘X’. Model ‘X’ will then be rolled out for a period of 3–4 months and evaluated for acceptability and feasibility by a second rapid survey.

Fidelity (delivering the intervention as designed) versus adaptation (making contextual modifications) during model ‘X’ optimisation (phase II) will be systematically evaluated. We will use a hybrid approach with three fidelity levels: delivery (whether the planned strategies and materials were provided as intended), receipt (assessed through provider and community engagement, understanding via competency checks and participation metrics), and enactment (monitored at two levels: routine use of the intervention in practice and proximal outcomes such as screening and referral rates at the community level, as well as healthcare facility preparedness at the facility level).22 All modifications will be made while documenting adaptations via Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications to Evidence-based Implementation Strategies (FRAME-IS) domains (what, when, how and why modified).23

Co-development

The current framework for cancer screening, diagnosis and treatment under NP-NCD will be designated as model ‘0’. Following the formative research, the investigators will collaboratively develop an evidence-based implementation model (model ‘0 Plus’). This model will be built in a workshop format involving all stakeholders over 3 months. These workshops will be conducted periodically to finalise model ‘X’ during the optimisation process.

Optimisation

The proposed model will be implemented for 4 months in one of the blocks of the chosen districts. The PLT will enhance programme execution through capacity building, supportive supervision and regular meetings with the implementation team. Multisource feedback from the PLT, monthly record data, survey data from the OMT and qualitative assessment by the PLT will facilitate model iteration. An identical cycle will recur quarterly for model optimisation. Model ‘X’ will be finalised after assessing its acceptability and feasibility at the PHC level through the RE-AIM framework to improve cancer screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation.

Phase III (Final implementation and evaluation-18 months)

In this phase, the optimised model will be expanded district-wide and refined based on concurrent evaluation. Recommendations will be formulated for broader implementation.

Implementation of model ‘X’

The final model will be implemented by the Implementation Team across districts, with the support of IST, within 12 months. Strategies such as engaging beneficiaries, training and capacity building, supportive supervision, strengthening stakeholder interrelationships, quality data management and technical support will be adapted and implemented. The key outcome of phase III is the improvement in the coverage of screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation for three common cancers. A convergent mixed-methods approach will be used to explore the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance (RE-AIM) of model ‘X’ in the field.21 The RE-AIM framework helps evaluate the appropriateness of implementation outcomes vis-à-vis health service outcomes to test external validity and improve sustainable adoption and implementation in real-world settings. It will help in assessing population engagement, intervention success, organisational adoption, fidelity and long-term sustainability. By addressing both individual and system-level factors, RE-AIM ensures effective translation of research into practice.21

Sample size and sampling

Quantitative data

Quantitative data will be collected through household survey and facility assessment. For phases I and III, the sampling universe will be the entire district.

Sample size for the household survey (phases I and III)

As per NFHS 5 (2019–2021), the percentage of beneficiaries aged 30–49 years who have ever undergone screening for three cancers across these seven districts ranges from 0% to 8% (online supplemental file II). In the absence of accurate data on coverage among beneficiaries aged ≥30, the screening coverage was taken as 10% based on expert consultation. With a screening coverage of 10%, an absolute precision of 2.5, a design effect of 1.5 and a 15% non-response rate, the required sample size is approximately 954.4–1000. A total of 1000 males and 1000 females aged ≥30 years will be included in the study from each district. Additionally, 200 screen-positive/diagnosed cancer patients will be surveyed to assess the cancer care pathway, assuming a 10% screen positivity rate.17 24

Sampling strategy for household survey

Multistage random sampling will be used to select the eligible population in urban and rural areas. In the first stage, PHC/UPHC will be selected using random sampling. In the second stage, villages/ASHA areas (equivalent to a village in a rural area) will be selected by probability proportionate to size sampling. In the third stage, eligible households will be selected using systematic random sampling. In the fourth stage, eligible males and females will be randomly selected using the KISH method.

For rapid survey in phase II (household survey)

Two rapid surveys will be conducted among 600 individuals (male/female), 400 from rural areas and 200 from urban areas. These rapid surveys will be conducted within the population covered by one PHC.

For facility assessment (phases I and III)

For facility assessment at baseline and end line, all CHCs, SDH, DH, 25% of all PHCs from the district and 5% of the sub-centres from the list of SC (minimum 10 PHCs and 10 subcentres) and one UPHC from all the sites would be included.

For rapid survey in phase II (facility assessment)

A subset of facilities will be assessed, and these facilities will be selected by purposive sampling.

Development of study tools

A thorough literature review will be conducted to develop study tools for the different phases. Existing guidelines and assessment checklists will be reviewed and adapted. Experts in cancer care, public health and health policy and programmes will be consulted to finalise both quantitative and qualitative tools.

Quantitative data

The OMT will collect primary and secondary data on cancer screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation from a subset of the district population through surveys and interviews. Secondary data will be collected from the existing health management information system (HMIS) and from district-level records on training and budget allocation. A facility assessment checklist will be used to evaluate the current manpower, infrastructure, drugs and equipment in the facility. OMT will measure input, process, output and outcome indicators (online supplemental file IIIa) for cancer screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation across the study phases. Cancer-specific data or indicators in the HMIS will be monitored monthly throughout the study period.

Qualitative data

PLT will use interviews, focus group discussions and checklists to explore the contextual factors, barriers, facilitators and stakeholder perspectives that influence implementation and outcomes. In phase I, the PLT will explore the barriers and enablers to screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation. They will explore the determinants of implementing model ‘0 Plus’, followed by its optimisation to model ‘X’ in phase II and scaling up of model ‘X’ in phase III.

Outcomes

Overall, the primary outcomes will include improved coverage and quality of screening, early diagnosis and treatment initiation for three common cancers in the selected districts. Secondary outcomes will include improved facility preparedness, enhanced NCD portal reporting, increased skilled manpower and improved community awareness. The phase-wise indicators are given in online supplemental file IIIa, and these will be refined after Phase I. Additionally, to ensure the quality of screening, indicators will be measured across the following domains: completeness, facility and provider readiness, coverage, equity in access, appropriateness, accuracy and timeliness (online supplemental file IIIb). In phase I, the baseline status of the coverage and quality of screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation for three prevalent cancers will be assessed. An evaluation will be conducted to determine the percentage of resources that offer primary, secondary and tertiary cancer care. Factors that facilitate and hinder the process of cancer screening, diagnosis and treatment will be explored. The outcome of phase II is expected to be a feasible and acceptable model ‘X’ that shows a consistent improvement in coverage and quality of screening, early diagnosis and treatment initiation over the rapid surveys.11 This model will incorporate site-specific community and facility-based strategies, developed based on the findings of phase I. At the community level, awareness and uptake of screening can be improved through targeted health promotion sessions, CBAC enrolment and referrals for screening by ASHA. At the facility level, enhanced screening quality, early diagnosis and treatment initiation may be facilitated by an increase in trained staff, improved infrastructure, improved decision-making through the NCD portal and strengthened referral linkages. The key outcome of phase III will be improvements in the coverage and quality of screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation for three common cancers with the implementation of model ‘X’ in the selected districts.

Data management and quality control

Data will be collected electronically and multilevel quality assurance measures will be taken to ensure the correctness, completeness and timeliness of the data. The database will be managed in REDCap and the data entry system will be equipped with mechanisms for performing range and consistency checks. A convergent mixed-methods design will be used, where quantitative and qualitative data will be collected simultaneously and analysed separately. These findings will be compared and triangulated during the interpretation phase.

Quantitative data

The study will analyse quantitative data using SPSS V.29, presenting categorical and continuous variables as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons will be made using the χ² test/Fisher’s exact test, independent samples Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney U tests. p value<0.05 will be considered statistically significant. The trends in indicators regarding coverage and quality of screening, early diagnosis and treatment initiation will be analysed and compared between baseline and endline. Geospatial mapping will be used to visualise key variables’ spatial distributions, revealing regional patterns and potential geographical influences on cancer screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation at different study sites.

Being a multiphase, mixed-methods implementation research study, the analysis will be structured phase-wise and conducted after incorporating sampling weights. For phases I and III, the assessment will be done at the district level. In phase II, two rapid surveys will be conducted in the same PHC and the results will be compared. Some key indicators, such as changes in facility readiness, trained manpower and community awareness, will be finalised. These indicators will evaluate the performance of co-development strategies. They will be compared using interrupted time-series analysis to study changes in the level and trend across three points: baseline (prior to the interventions), the first rapid survey and the second rapid survey.

Qualitative data

Interviews will be conducted in the regional languages of India, including English, Hindi, Punjabi, Marathi, Kannada and Tamil. The transcripts will be prepared within 48 hours, translated into English and back-translated. The translated data will be checked for quality and confirmed by language experts from the languages and cultures involved. The codebook will be developed through an iterative process involving familiarising with the data, reading the transcripts, interpreting the data, hybrid coding (deductive and inductive) and refining the codes through comparison among experienced public health experts. The trustworthiness of the data will be ensured using Lincoln and Guba’s evaluative criteria for credibility (rapport building, reflexivity and data triangulation), transferability (research context description, participants and methods), confirmability (team debriefing) and dependability (audit trail). ATLAS.ti V.8 software will be used and a framework analysis will be done.25

Quality assurance and quality control

A Technical Advisory Group (TAG) at Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) will spearhead the study protocol and survey instrument development, design and management of electronic data capture projects, build capacity, provide technical support to data-collection teams, convene stakeholder meetings, oversee knowledge dissemination and facilitate policy engagement at the institutional and state levels. External routine site visits by the TAG, with the TCU PGIMER, Chandigarh, will confirm protocol compliance, while the internal quality assurance team will conduct 10% supervision checks. Expert-led initial and refresher training will ensure service and data quality. Electronic data capture systems will enable real-time quality assurance, analysis and prompt dissemination of results for informed decision-making. A standardised protocol will be established and its tools will be pretested at each location to ensure uniformity in training, data collection, monitoring and evaluation.

Based on the findings of phase I, SOPs to ensure continuity of care (screening to treatment initiation and follow-up) and adherence to standard screening protocols will be co-developed during phase II within the NP-NCD framework.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public are not involved in protocol design. The public and cancer patients are important stakeholders for the study. They will be involved in designing and implementing strategies for models ‘0 Plus’ and ‘X’.

Discussion

Despite national screening initiatives, less than 1% of eligible individuals undergo routine cancer screening, highlighting systemic barriers such as a lack of awareness and inadequate healthcare access and infrastructure.2 The ICMR started the National Health Research Priority Program in June 2023 to fund projects on key research priorities (including cancer) identified through multistakeholder consultations. To address existing gaps, regional disparities and achieve policy-relevant outcomes, multisite interdisciplinary projects with the involvement of district and state health officials as key implementers were approved.26 A recent review of the Indian cancer screening programme in three states reiterated the need for multistakeholder coordination and government support to address poor coverage and quality of screening.17 Hence, the present study aims to enhance the district-level coverage and quality of cancer screening, early diagnosis and treatment initiation for oral, breast and cervical cancers through an implementation model co-developed with the district/state public health system within the NP-NCD framework.

This multisite, multiphase mixed-methods study is planned in seven Indian states. The study uses existing public health infrastructure and programmatic guidelines and incorporates evidence-based practices, aligning with India’s primary objective of attaining universal health coverage via HWCs.27 28 This study uses an IRLM to plan, execute, and evaluate multi-phase mixed -methods implementation research using the CFIR, ERIC and RE-AIM frameworks for the formative, implementation and evaluation phases, respectively. In contrast to previous static intervention models, which lack adaptability to changing situations, this study’s approach is dynamic. During this study, a feasible, scalable and sustainable model will be developed and optimised through continuous iterations and adaptations with the state and district NP-NCD programme officers. Because NP-NCD is an evolving national programme, improvements may occur independently of model ‘X’. Regular meetings with state and district programme officers will be held to discuss project updates, take feedback and explore any changes in the NP-NCD programme. Although the study lacks an internal comparator, a pre-post comparison between model ‘0’ and ‘X’ will be conducted to assess changes in the coverage and quality of screening, diagnosis and treatment initiation for three cancers. Comprehensive data management solutions, such as REDCap, combined with geospatial mapping and real-time monitoring, will enhance programme evaluation. The incorporation of supportive supervision, referral links and quality assurance procedures, including frequent audits and quality improvement teams, will enhance service delivery.

Collaborative alliances with stakeholders, such as local governments and academic institutions, will enhance technical support, foster community mobilisation, and ensure the viability of spreading the model to more locations. Moreover, this study involves an interdisciplinary team from various departments, including experts in preventive, diagnostic, curative, and palliative care, which will facilitate a continuum of care from screening to palliative care. The outcomes of this study are expected to help strengthen the linkage between cancer screening and treatment with amenable approaches through implementation research. These solutions collectively support India’s overarching objective of attaining universal health coverage while addressing systemic deficiencies in cancer screening, diagnostic and treatment services.
