Cryoelastic and cryochromic organic crystals
Authors: Jiechang Wang, Linfeng Lan, Hongyu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cryoelastic and cryochromic luminescent organic crystals offer a powerful platform for adaptive photonics and sensing technologies, yet the structural origins of their temperature-dependent color changes remain unclear. This study reveals that flexible naphthalene-based Schiff base single crystals maintain reversible elasticity and crystallinity across the 298–77 K temperature range while exhibiting distinct thermofluorochromic responses: some crystals undergo progressive blue shifts, while others display pronounced red shifts (up to ~92 nm) accompanied by extended excited-state lifetimes. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction reveals fully reversible lattice contraction without disorder, and identifies a correlation between emission shift direction and π⋯π contact area changes. Enhanced overlap strengthens exciton coupling and drives red shifts, while the lack of overlap maintains monomeric blue emission. This structure-property correlation provides geometric descriptors for predicting low-temperature color-changing behavior, enabling temperature-gated optical encryption and low-temperature luminescent sensing technologies based on mechanically robust organic crystals.

Introduction

Temperature exerts a profound influence on the optoelectronic behavior of molecular solids, often revealing subtle structure–property relationships that remain hidden under ambient conditions1–3. In organic crystals, where weak noncovalent interactions govern both molecular packing and excitonic communication, cooling can either sharpen emission bands or shift them markedly across the visible–near-infrared region4–12. Such thermofluorochromic (TFC) responses have long intrigued chemists and physicists, yet their molecular origins remain elusive13–15. Most chromophores display a blue shift of luminescence at cryogenic temperatures due to restricted intramolecular motion, but an increasing number of systems exhibit the opposite, a pronounced red shift16,17. Understanding what dictates this directionality is not only of academic interest but also essential for the predictive design of organic emitters for cryogenic photonics18–20, flexible displays21, temperature-encoded encryption22–24, and encrypted data transfer25. Recent studies on flexible organic crystals have shown that these materials provide an ideal platform for uncovering such correlations26–34. Their ordered yet deformable lattices allow simultaneous monitoring of photophysical and mechanical responses, bridging microscopic electronic interactions with macroscopic elasticity35,36. However, most reports on low-temperature luminescent crystals remain at a phenomenological level, describing color changes without clarifying why the emission direction differs among systems of comparable composition and morphology37–39. Even within the same chemical family, small variations in molecular planarity, substituent polarity, or π⋯π stacking geometry can result in markedly different TFC behaviors40. This complexity has hindered the establishment of a unified structural principle linking crystal packing to emission polarity41.

Here, we address this issue in the context of cryoelastic and cryochromic luminescent organic crystals by identifying a geometric rule, based on π⋯π overlap, that determines whether luminescence red-shifts or blue-shifts upon cooling. Using a series of five elastic naphthalene-based Schiff-base crystals as model cryoelastic and cryochromic systems, we systematically correlate low-temperature emission evolution (298–77 K) with precise structural parameters obtained from variable-temperature single-crystal X-ray diffraction. These crystals, derived from 2-hydroxy-1-naphthaldehyde and para-substituted anilines, form a coherent structural family differing mainly in their substituent-controlled packing motifs. Two members exhibit blue-shifted emission at 77 K, while the other three show pronounced red shifts of up to 92 nm, all maintaining reversible elasticity across the temperature range. This coexistence of mechanical resilience and tunable emission provides a rare opportunity to probe how local π⋯π interactions dictate optical responses under thermal stress in cryoelastic and cryochromic luminescent organic crystals. Our analysis shows that the direction of the luminescence shift is governed by the evolution of the π⋯π overlap area upon cooling: crystals with enhanced overlap exhibit stronger excitonic coupling and energy delocalization, leading to aggregate-like red shifts, whereas those with reduced overlap behave as isolated emitters and show conventional blue shifts associated with vibronic relaxation. This structural–photophysical correlation bridges the gap between molecular aggregation theory and real-crystal thermodynamics. Beyond fundamental insight, this mechanism provides a geometry-based design logic for low-temperature adaptive photonic materials. The reversible and fatigue-resistant TFC of these cryoelastic and cryochromic luminescent organic crystals enables temperature-dependent color encoding accessible only under cryogenic conditions, as demonstrated by optical encryption patterns. More broadly, identifying π⋯π overlap as a structural determinant of emission polarity offers a predictive framework for engineering chromic behavior in flexible organic solids, advancing from descriptive thermochromism toward a geometry-driven understanding of how molecular packing governs luminescence evolution under extreme conditions.

Results

The five naphthyl Schiff base (NSB) compounds studied here were synthesized via the condensation of 2-hydroxy-1-naphthaldehyde with aniline derivatives bearing different substituents42, and are referred to as NSB-3I (1), NSB-3,4,5-Cl3 (2), NSB-4Cl (3), NSB-3,5-F2 (4) and NSB-3,4-Cl2 (5) according to their substituents (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Figs. 1–11). All five compounds can readily form elastic crystals with sizes ranging from micrometers to centimeters through a slow solvent diffusion process (see Methods for details). Single crystals of all five NSB crystals were obtained and retain elastic deformability and crystallinity on cooling from 298 K to 77 K (Fig. 1b), allowing us to correlate structural and photophysical changes across this temperature range. Under cooling, the crystals show markedly different luminescence responses. 1 and 2 undergo modest blue shifts at 77 K, whereas 3–5 display bathochromic shifts, with one crystal reaching a red shift of up to 92 nm. To elucidate the underlying mechanism of these opposite spectral shifts, single-crystal X-ray diffraction analyses were conducted at different temperatures. The data show that the dihedral angles between the benzene and naphthalene units increase appreciably in 4 and 5 upon cooling to 100 K (Fig. 1c), indicating larger intramolecular distortion in these cases. Analysis of intermolecular contacts reveals that 1 and 2 have negligible π⋯π overlap at both temperatures, consistent with monomer-like emission and the observed blue shifts (Fig. 1d, e). In contrast, 3–5 already exhibit discernible π⋯π overlap between naphthalene rings at 298 K, and this overlap becomes more significant upon cooling. In particular, 4 and 5 show larger overlap areas at 298 K and a more substantial increase in phenyl–phenyl stacking at 100 K (Fig. 1d, e). In contrast, 3–5 undergo bathochromic (red) shifts of 15, 62, and 92 nm, respectively, together with moderate increases in emission intensity (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 13). Notably, 4 and 5 exhibit the most pronounced red shifts, with the emission color visibly changing from yellow to deep red under cryogenic conditions. CIE coordinate analysis (Fig. 2d) further confirms that while the emission of 1–3 remains largely within the green spectral region, 4 and 5 shift from yellow to red, moving from (0.47, 0.51) to (0.64, 0.35). Long-term thermal stability was confirmed as all five crystal samples retained robust luminescence and reliable wavelength conversion after 1000 cooling–heating cycles between 298 K and 77 K. The emission spectra and consistent CIE 1931 chromaticity coordinates (Supplementary Figs. 14–17) underscore the exceptional structural integrity and reliability of these materials for sustained optical applications. Time-resolved PL measurements reveal distinct excited-state dynamics between the blue- and red-shifting systems. At 298 K, all crystals show nanosecond-scale decay lifetimes, consistent with emission originating from short-lived excited states of individual molecular units. Upon cooling to 77 K, the lifetimes of 1 and 2 decrease slightly, consistent with reduced thermal relaxation and limited intersystem coupling (Fig. 2e). The lifetime of 3 increases slightly from 4.39 ns at 298 K to 5.07 ns at 77 K, whereas 4 and 5 show a pronounced extension from 1.16 ns and 1.73 ns to 5.08 ns and 7.48 ns, respectively, corresponding to red-shifted emission and an overall lifespan increase of up to 300% (Fig. 2f, g and Supplementary Table 1). Such a marked elongation suggests enhanced exciton delocalization or the formation of thermally stabilized aggregated states, indicative of strengthened π⋯π electronic interactions at cryogenic temperatures.

To gain mechanistic insight into the divergent TFC behavior, we first investigated compound 2 in three distinct states: crystalline, amorphous powder, and dispersed in a 50% polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) matrix. Powder X-ray Diffraction (PXRD) analysis confirms that the ground powder and the center of the PDMS composite are amorphous, as evidenced by the broad diffuse peaks. In contrast, sharp Bragg peaks are exclusively detected in the original crystals and the edge-precipitated sections, confirming their crystalline nature (Supplementary Fig. 18). This spatial difference occurs because, in the 50% mixture, the rapid evaporation of the dichloromethane (DCM) solvent causes the majority of compound 2 to migrate and recrystallize at the film edges. Consequently, only a trace amount remains within the bulk PDMS matrix, where it solidifies into an amorphous state upon curing. Further morphological characterization strongly supports the aforementioned conclusions. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) images confirm the disordered, irregular morphology of the milled powder. Although crystal features at the film edges were obscured by surface encapsulation of the PDMS substrate and thus not directly identifiable in SEM observations (Supplementary Fig. 19a, b), regular crystals precipitated at the edges were clearly observable via fluorescence and bright-field modes of optical microscopy, while the central region remained smooth (Supplementary Fig. 19c, d). This multiscale characterization unequivocally confirms the coexistence of crystalline edges and amorphous interiors within the film. Across the temperature range of 298–77 K, all three states exhibited blue shifts upon cooling (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 20). The crystalline state of compound 2 displays monomer-like emission, indicating minimal intermolecular π⋯π interactions even in the ordered lattice. Similarly, PDMS films containing edge-precipitated microcrystals of compound 2 show low-temperature spectra that can be interpreted as a superposition of crystalline and amorphous contributions, reflecting partial order within a largely disordered matrix (Fig. 3a–c). In contrast, compound 5 exhibits markedly different behavior. Its crystalline state undergoes a pronounced red shift upon cooling, with the emission peak shifting to longer wavelengths (Fig. 3d, e). In the PDMS-dispersed state, however, only slight blue shifts are observed under identical cooling conditions (Supplementary Fig. 21). Edge-precipitated microcrystals of compound 5 in PDMS display low-temperature spectra that represent a mixture of crystalline and amorphous emissions, consistent with partial aggregation (Fig. 3d–f). These results indicate that the low-temperature red shift is a distinctive property of the fully aggregated crystalline state, arising from enhanced intermolecular interactions and exciton delocalization. To further elucidate the origin of this behavior, time-dependent density-functional theory (TDDFT) calculations were performed on both monomeric and dimeric configurations of compound 5 (Supplementary Tables 2–5)44. At 298 K, the vertical emission energy of the dimer is lower than that of the monomer, indicating an intrinsic tendency for red-shifted emission in the aggregated state (Fig. 3g). Upon cooling to 100 K, the dimer experiences a further reduction in vertical emission energy, whereas the monomer is largely unaffected (Fig. 3h). These computational results are fully consistent with the π⋯π overlap–dependent mechanism proposed earlier, where the specific molecular packing and resultant intermolecular interactions in compound 5 enable the pronounced low-temperature red shift, a feature absent in compound 2 due to its weaker π⋯π contacts and distinct packing arrangement. Furthermore, Hirshfeld surface analysis indicates that the relative contributions of various noncovalent interactions (such as H–H, C–H, and halogen bonds) remain largely consistent from 298 K to 100 K (Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23). The molecular conformation and packing arrangement of the crystals are critical in determining their mechanical elasticity and fluorescence behavior. All five crystals share similar packing motifs, predominantly herringbone or fishbone, and exhibit elastic moduli ranging from 6 to 11 GPa, as measured by three-point bending tests (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 24). The bending flexibility was assessed by measuring the maximum elastic strain (ε) at both room and low temperatures, using crack-free bent crystals mounted on a 0.52 mm capillary tube (Supplementary Fig. 25). Each crystal exhibited comparable deformation values at both room and low temperatures (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 26 and Supplementary Tables 6 and 7), confirming persistent elasticity across temperatures. To further assess the mechanical robustness, fatigue resistance was evaluated by subjecting 2 and 5 to 50 bending-recovery cycles at 77 K (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28). No fractures were observed, and the fluorescence color remained stable throughout the cycling process, indicating the excellent long-term mechanical durability. A detailed analysis of the single-crystal structures at both 298 K and 100 K (Supplementary Figs. 29 and 30) reveals that all five crystals exhibit consistent molecular packing upon cooling. As illustrated by the Oak Ridge Thermal Ellipsoid Plot (ORTEP) diagrams (Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32), the molecular conformations of 1–5 remain stable across the tested temperature range. A noticeable contraction of the thermal ellipsoids is observed at 100 K, reflecting the suppression of atomic thermal motion at low temperatures. Analysis of weak intermolecular interactions reveals that π⋯π stacking, C–H···O, and C–H···π contacts are generally strengthened at low temperatures due to lattice contraction (Supplementary Table 9). Specifically, 1 (C2/c space group) stacks along the (100) plane through π⋯π interactions between benzene (3.431 Å) and naphthalene (3.406 Å) rings, complemented by C–H···O (2.676 Å) and C–H···π (2.616 Å) interactions, allowing for molecular reorientation under external stress (Supplementary Fig. 33a, c and Supplementary Fig. 34a); 2 (P2₁2₁2₁ space group) displays similar π···π and weak interactions along the (010) plane (benzene 3.419 Å, naphthalene 2.789 Å; C–H···O 2.455 Å, C–H···π 2.672 Å; Fig. 4c, f and Supplementary Fig. 34b); 3 (P2₁/n space group) stacks along the (001) plane via π···π interactions between benzene (3.520 Å) and naphthalene (3.417 Å) rings, along with additional C–H···O (2.484 Å) and C–Cl···π (3.220 Å) interactions (Fig. 4d, g and Supplementary Fig. 35a); 4 (P2₁/c space group) and 5 (C2/c space group) exhibit parallel cross-stacking with π⋯π contacts between benzene (3.383–3.428 Å) and naphthalene (3.396–3.412 Å) rings, along with multiple weak interactions, including C–H···O, C–H···F/Cl, and C–H···π contacts (Fig. 4e, h, Supplementary Figs. 33b, d, 35b, c). Upon cooling, most crystals undergo axial contraction, predominantly along the crystal growth direction, while exceptions include expansion along the b-axis in 2 and along the a-axis in 5 (Supplementary Figs. 34, 35 and Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). These observations indicate that changes in isotropic or anisotropic axes are not the main factors determining the mechanical flexibility of the crystals. Instead, subtle conformational adjustments coupled with lattice contraction result in denser, more stable packing at low temperatures, thus preserving the flexibility of these crystals under cryogenic conditions. Leveraging their thermofluorochromic properties, which exhibit reversible red and blue fluorescence shifts at low temperatures, we demonstrate the potential of Schiff-base crystals for advanced optical encryption applications. For example, at 298 K, the 5 × 4 microcrystal arrays composed of 3 and 5 display a consistent color pattern under both daylight (yellow) and UV light (yellow-green) (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 36). Upon cooling to 77 K, the fluorescence pattern shifts to a red-green array, which allows the decryption of the Morse code45,46 sequences “1949” and “2025”. This demonstrates the ability to modulate information storage and retrieval via precise control of light and temperature, emphasizing their utility as dynamic keys for data encryption. 2 exhibits a blue shift in fluorescence during cooling, with its color changing from yellow-green to green. 3 shows a slight red shift, but the color change is minimal. 5 demonstrates a significant red shift, with its color transitioning from yellow-green to orange and then to red (Supplementary Movies 1–3). To further explore the dynamic relationship between color-change response and waveguide performance, we investigated the optical waveguiding properties of 2 and 5 under varying conditions (Supplementary Figs. 37 and 38). Experiments demonstrated that at 77 K, despite significant lattice contraction inducing a substantial fluorescence redshift (~ 92 nm), optical transmission capability remained unaffected. Notably, the optical loss coefficient measured at 77 K remained essentially unchanged from that at 298 K. Even when bent, the crystals maintained stable optical signal transmission, demonstrating their potential for applications in extreme environments such as polar fiber optic sensors. We further explore a temperature-programmable encryption system based on the Vigenere cipher47 (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 39). The system employs eight color modules (green square, green circle, yellow square, yellow circle, orange square, orange circle, red square, red circle), each corresponding to a letter (B, I, W, D, F, N, K, R). By arranging microcrystals 2, 3, and 5 into two-column modules, distinct color combinations are formed at temperatures of 298 K, 168 K, and 77 K, resulting in different encrypted messages. At 298 K and 168 K, the decoded messages read “EJEEJ” and “OEJNE”, respectively, whereas at 77 K, the correct decryption, “SENSE”, is obtained. This highlights the high security of temperature as an encryption key, where only specific temperature conditions allow for accurate decryption. Furthermore, taking crystal 5 as an example, its maximum emission wavelength exhibits a nearly monotonic redshift phenomenon as the temperature decreases from 298 K to 77 K (Fig. 5c, d). Although the redshift rate and fluorescence intensity versus temperature shows nonlinearity, the overall change remains smooth and continuous (Supplementary Fig. 40). Remarkably, the CIE 1931 chromaticity coordinates of the emission shift linearly from orange-yellow at room temperature (0.47, 0.51) to red at 77 K (0.64, 0.35), tracing a nearly linear trajectory on the chromaticity diagram (Fig. 5e). This strong linear correlation between color and temperature enables direct, rapid readout using simple RGB color recognition or a colorimeter. In contrast, crystal 2 demonstrates a pronounced nonlinear response during cooling, with its emission wavelength, CIE coordinates, and fluorescence intensity all exhibiting abrupt, non-monotonic changes from 298 K to 77 K (Supplementary Figs. 41 and 42). This threshold-like, highly nonlinear behavior renders crystals particularly suitable for high-sensitivity temperature alarms or precise calibration over specific temperature ranges. These results highlight that NSB crystals are promising candidates for constructing wide-range colorimetric or ratiometric optical thermometers. Discussion

In summary, this study reveals that the evolution of π⋯π overlap in cryoelastic and cryochromic luminescent organic crystals is the key structural parameter determining the direction of low-temperature emission shift. Through combined photophysical and crystallographic analysis, we find that enhanced intermolecular stacking during cooling promotes exciton delocalization, leading to red-shift emission, while minimal π⋯π overlap induces a blue-shift phenomenon similar to that in monomers. Reversible lattice contraction not only preserves the elasticity and crystallinity of the material but also ensures fatigue stability across the 298–77 K temperature range. Based on this discovery, the study establishes a framework for π⋯π overlap-controlled luminescence, providing predictive guidance for the engineered design of cryoelastic and cryochromic luminescent organic crystals. Leveraging these robust, tunable, and reversible color-transition properties, we further demonstrate applications in temperature-gated optical encryption and ratio-based thermometry, successfully integrating mechanical adaptability with information functionality. Starting from phenomenological observations, this study delves into the geometric regulation mechanisms governing luminescence evolution in responsive molecular crystals. It provides a theoretical foundation for designing low-temperature elastic and low-temperature color-changing luminescent organic crystals, advancing the development of adaptive photonic materials.

Methods

Synthesis procedures for Compounds 1–5

All chemicals for synthesis were purchased from commercial sources. The solvents for syntheses were analytical-reagent grade and were used without further purification. The steps for the synthesis of compounds 1−5 can be found in Supplementary Figs. 1–11.

Crystal growth

0.326 g compounds 1–5 powder was dissolved in 50 mL DCM to form a saturated solution. 2 mL of this solution was added to a test tube, and 4 mL of anhydrous ethanol (EtOH) was added along the wall of the test tube. The test tube was sealed with a sealing film and kept in the darkness at 25 °C. High-quality crystals were obtained after about a week.

Characterization

1H NMR spectra were recorded on an Agar Scientifica 400 MHz spectrometer (1H: 400 MHz; 13C {1H}: 100 MHz) with tetramethylsilane as the internal standard. Three-point bending tests were carried out on the Instron 5944 universal materials tester produced by Instron Corporation with a capacity of 5 N Instron 2530 load cell. UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded by a PerkinElmer Lambda 1050 + spectrophotometer. The emission spectra of crystals were measured using a Maya2000 Pro CCD spectrometer (Ocean Optics, Dunedin, Florida, America) or a Shimadzu RF-5301 spectrometer. The fluorescence lifetimes were measured on the Edinburgh FLS920 instrument by using a time-correlated single-photon (TCSPC) module. For the optical measurements, the crystal was irradiated with the third harmonic (355 nm) of a Nd: YAG (yttrium–aluminum–garnet) laser at a repetition rate of 10 Hz and pulse duration of about 10 ns. The energy of the laser was adjusted by using the calibrated neutral density filters. The beam was focused into a stripe whose shape was adjusted to 3.3 × 0.6 mm by using a cylindrical lens and a slit. The temperature was measured by using a Miaoxin T10R − PT temperature sensor with 0.1 K resolution (reading error: ± 0.3 K). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained on a Regulus 8100 field emission transmission electron microscope operated at 3–5 kV.

X-ray crystallographic analysis

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) data was collected on a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer with CuKα radiation. Single crystal X-ray diffraction data were collected using a Bruker D8 Quest diffractometer (CuKα, λ = 1.54178 Å). Indexing and data integration were performed using APEX4 (Difference Vectors method)48. Absorption correction was applied by the multiscan method implemented in SADABS49. The structures were solved using SHELXL-2014 (direct methods) and refined using SHELXL-2014 (full-matrix least-squares on F2) with anisotropic displacement parameters and using the APEX4 program packages. Hydrogen atoms on carbon and nitrogen were placed at ideal positions with their isotropic displacement parameters set to 1.2 × Ueq of the atoms to which they are bonded. The graphics related to the structures were generated by using Mercury 4.2.050.

Computational details

Based on the atomic coordinates obtained from single-crystal diffraction measurements, a constrained structural optimization of the H atoms was performed using Gaussian 16 AVX2 software51 at the B3LYP/6-311 G(d,p) level, with D3 dispersion correction52 applied, while the positions of the other heavy atoms were fixed. This was primarily because the H atom coordinates obtained from the single-crystal diffraction measurements were empirical. Subsequently, the vertical excitation and emission energies of the optimized molecule were calculated using the same functional and basis set. Key intermolecular interactions present in the crystal structures of crystals 1–5 were analyzed using Hirshfeld surface analysis in Crystal Explorer 17.553.

Methods for constructing Morse code and the Vigenere cipher

First, we dissolved 2, 3, and 5 separately in dichloromethane. After rotary evaporation of the solvent, we obtained microcrystals of the three compounds. Circular and square patterns were fabricated from the microcrystals of these three compounds. Initially, Morse code was employed using microcrystals 3 and 5. Photographs capturing their daylight and fluorescence images at 298 K and 77 K were taken with a camera. At 298 K, both microcrystals exhibit a yellow daylight appearance. At 77 K, microcrystal 3 emits yellow-green fluorescence while microcrystal 5 emits red. We define microcrystal 3 as “-”, microcrystal 5 as “·”, and subsequently decode the numbers using Morse code. Part II of the Virginia Code employs the fluorescent colors of microcrystal 2, microcrystal 3, and microcrystal 5 at three distinct temperatures for encoding. The green square and green circle denote the fluorescent color of microcrystal 2 at 77 K, representing B and I, respectively. The yellow square and yellow circle represent the fluorescent colors of microcrystal 3 at 77 K, denoting W and D, respectively. The orange square and orange circle represent the fluorescent colors of microcrystal 5 at 168 K, denoting F and N, respectively. The red square and red circle represent the fluorescent colors of microcrystal 5 at 77 K, denoting K and R, respectively. These eight letters are defined as the Virginia Cipher.

Preparation of PDMS membranes

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, MW 4200 g·mol–1) was prepared from commercial SYLGARD 184 silicone elastomer base and curing agent manufactured by Dow Corning Corporation and purchased from Energy Chemical. The elastomer base, namely Part A, was SYLGARD 184 Silicone Elastomer Base (product code: 01064291). The Part A formulation contains tetra(trimethylsiloxy)silane (CAS No. 3555-47-3) as a listed component. The physical properties of the Part A formulation were as follows: viscosity, 5000 cSt; specific gravity, 1.11. The curing agent, namely Part B and used as the PDMS crosslinker, was SYLGARD 184 Silicone Elastomer Curing Agent (product code: 01015311). The Part B formulation contains tetramethyl tetravinyl cyclotetrasiloxane (CAS No. 2554-06-5) as a listed component. The physical properties of the Part B formulation were as follows: viscosity, 110 cSt; specific gravity, 1.03. The specific method is as follows: (1) Preparation of PDMS mixture: Weigh 1.0 g of PDMS elastomer base into a sample vial and add 0.1 g of PDMS crosslinker. Mix thoroughly using a magnetic stirrer until completely blended. (2) Preparation of compound solution: Weigh 0.1 g of crystalline compound into a sample vial. Add 2 mL of dichloromethane (DCM) and stir until the crystals are completely dissolved. (3) Preparation of composite solution: Weigh 0.1 g of the PDMS mixture and add it to the prepared compound solution. Stir thoroughly to achieve complete homogeneity. (4) Film preparation: Using a pipette, dispense 0.1 mL of the mixed solution onto a clean microscope slide. Allow the solvent to evaporate naturally, forming the desired film with a PDMS content of 50 wt%.
