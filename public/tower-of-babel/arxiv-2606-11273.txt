Preconditioning for near-contacts in large 2D Stokes flows: a locally compressed method of fundamental solutions
Anna Broms, Anna-Karin Tornberg, Alex H. Barnett

Abstract
We tackle two key difficulties in the simulation of the viscous hydrodynamics of a large dense collection of rigid particles: (i) the poor convergence rate of an iterative solution of the discretized linear system as particle gaps shrink, and (ii) the large number of unknowns needed to accurately discretize the resulting lubrication-driven flows. Our focus is the 2D Stokes resistance and mobility boundary value problems for nearly-touching disks. To address both challenges, we introduce a general two-body preconditioning strategy, and implement it with the method of fundamental solutions. For each close particle pair, the hard-to-resolve interaction is represented in a basis precomputed by solving a local boundary value problem on a fine grid. In an iterative solve, the resulting flow field corrects that obtained from a coarse representation of all particles. The local fine-grid correction can furthermore be compressed so that all particles except the pair itself are affected by an equivalent set of coarse sources. Numerical experiments demonstrate rapid GMRES convergence in challenging multi-particle settings, with iteration counts remaining low even in densely packed suspensions. For example, the mobility problem is solved for a random close packing with area fraction φ = 0.65 \varphi=0.65 , P = 10000 P=10000 monodisperse disks, and minimum separation 10 − 3 10^{-3} , in 47 GMRES iterations, achieving five digits of accuracy with 72 vector unknowns per body.



Key words:
Elliptic PDE; Stokes flow; mobility; preconditioning; near-contact; potential theory

Computational challenges.

When rigid particles undergo relative motion at close separations, their hydrodynamic interactions become especially challenging to determine, both in the resistance and mobility settings
[
44
,
42
, p. 175]
. Two distinct difficulties appear:

•

Lubrication-driven fine scales.

In narrow inter-particle gaps, the fluid velocity develops steep gradients. Accurate capture of these gradients requires very high spatial resolution:
volume-based methods, such as finite elements, must deploy extremely fine meshes
[
44
]
.
Potential based methods, such as boundary integral equations (BIEs) or the
method of fundamental solutions
(MFS), avoid the need for volume discretization, but not the demand for local refinement. Relative motion drives sharp peaks in the surface force density.
For two disks, this peak scales like
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta})
with gap width
\delta

[
9
,
62
]
, reflecting the rapid amplification of lubrication forces between the particles.
If the density peaks are under-resolved, the error in computed hydrodynamic quantities—forces and torques in a resistance problem, or velocities in a mobility problem—can be substantial. Moreover, refinement in close-to-touching regions significantly increases computational cost because elliptic kernels are globally coupled: locally introduced fine-scale degrees of freedom must interact with the entire suspension.

•

Ill-conditioning.

Regardless of discretization, the resulting linear system inherits the singular nature of the physics.
As
\delta\to 0
, or as the number of particles grows,
the conditioning deteriorates

[
9
,
60
]
.
When using an iterative solver such as GMRES, the number of iterations needed to reach fixed accuracy rises without limit as
\delta\to 0

[
47
]
; for instance
in the 3D resistance setting
[
14
]

this was empirically found to be
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{-1/2})
.
The problem is therefore not merely one of accuracy, but also of efficiency.

Related work.

We briefly overview numerical methods for Stokes flows with near-contacts,
focusing on PDE-based approaches. We first note the long tradition of using far-field approximations of the
hydrodynamic interactions between bodies (usually spheres in 3D),
such as in Stokesian dynamics
[
10
]
,
with lubrication corrections added in a pair-wise manner
[
62
,
44
]
.
Regularized effective particle interactions are also popular, as in the rigid multiblob
[
7
,
15
]
, force coupling
[
66
]
or regularized Stokeslets
[
21
]
methods.
While useful, neither method is
convergent
, in the sense that the
error in solving the BVP (
1
) may be reduced as
close to zero as desired by adjusting numerical parameters.

PDE-based approaches—which in contrast do converge to the true Stokes solution—either
discretize the fluid volume (e.g. finite element methods with a conforming mesh),
exploit potential theory and linearity to discretize only the boundaries
(as in BIE and MFS), or combine both (as in immersed boundary methods and cut finite element methods).
Despite recent advances in accurate quadrature for BIEs
[
33
,
11
,
9
,
1
]
,
resolving the layer density—which closely mirrors the physical force density—remains challenging.
In near-contact situations, the density develops sharp peaks that demand refinement, for example via dyadic (exponential) clustering of quadrature nodes near points of closest approach.
One way to curb the resulting growth in degrees of freedom is recursively compressed inverse preconditioning (RCIP), introduced for corners
by Helsing & Ojala
[
32
]
, demonstrated for close-to-touching bodies in
[
36
]
and extended to Stokes flow in
[
17
,
5
]
.
Starting from a dyadic local refinement in a neighborhood of the singularity, RCIP
uses a sequence of small dense direct linear solves to compress the refined system to a much smaller effective one.
For static geometries, this is highly effective, but in dynamic problems
where the geometry changes at every time step this can be expensive.
A further major limitation is that RCIP has not been generalized to 3D.

The MFS (also known as the method of auxiliary sources or the charge simulation method
[
2
,
25
,
8
,
3
,
45
,
41
,
4
]
) moves the
potential sources off the boundary,
removing the need for singular quadratures that complicates BIE.
An exterior solution is represented as a linear combination of Stokeslets placed inside each particle. For instance, for the unit disk, a standard choice is to place “proxy” sources uniformly on an interior curve of radius
R_{p}<1
, enforcing boundary conditions in a least-squares sense at collocation points. The resulting rectangular matrix
becomes exponentially ill-conditioned in the high-accuracy regime,
so that a backward-stable solve—typically via dense singular value decomposition (SVD)—is essential.

In near-contact situations, simply increasing the number of proxy sources to resolve lubrication forces becomes prohibitive. For Stokes spheres in 3D
[
14
]
,
we enhanced the basic MFS setup for every pair of particles by adding various types of sources clustered toward
image accumulation points
.
We also stabilized the multi-particle solve via right-preconditioning using the particle self-interaction matrix blocks (“one-body” or rectangular block-Jacobi preconditioning)
[
45
,
65
]
.
This enabled accurate resolution of lubrication effects with a modest number of degrees of freedom for all gaps larger than
10^{-3}R
, covering what we argued to be all physically relevant separations for Stokes flow. For the mobility problem we introduced a “recompleted” formulation in
[
13
]
in which force/torque constraints are automatically satisfied, allowing an unconstrained least-squares solve. Combined with one-body preconditioning and fast multipole acceleration, this yielded a robust linear-complexity scheme for general smooth shapes, demonstrated on clusters of
10^{4}
ellipsoids.

There are other PDE-based approaches that share with MFS the idea of
least-squares collocation. Examples include Crowdy et al.’s conformal mapping method for Laplace problems exterior to close cylinders
[
23
]
and the Stokes lightning method of Trefethen and collaborators
[
16
,
72
]
. These exploit complex analysis to express the solution in terms of analytic Goursat functions (for Stokes) or Laurent series (for Laplace). The expansions in
[
23
]
even include terms centered at image accumulation points, similar to our MFS enhancement. While accurate, they are difficult to accelerate with fast summation techniques and, to our knowledge, have not been applied to close-to-touching rigid bodies in Stokes flow.
Such complex analytic methods are also intrinsically tied to 2D.

We know of no existing convergent method that combines the flexibility of the MFS with a preconditioning strategy able to resolve lubrication effects at physically relevant small gaps, while avoiding a global fine discretization—crucial for simulations with very large numbers of particles. Here, we present one.

1.1
Summary of contributions

The paper presents a
two-body preconditioner
that mitigates both the severe ill-conditioning,
and growth in the number of unknowns, that usually occur when seeking accurate Stokes solutions with close rigid particles in relative motion.
It is a hybrid of direct and iterative methods:
it directly solves a set of local finely-discretized BVPs—each involving
only two nearly-touching particles—in order to construct a (preconditioned) coarse discretized global system involving far fewer unknowns.
The global system is then solved iteratively using fast multipole (FMM) acceleration.
The idea generalizes the
pairwise Laplace image-sum basis functions of Cheng & Greengard
[
19
,
20
]

to “two-body basis functions” which may be precomputed with
any
convenient
BVP solver.
We use MFS for this task in the present work, due to its excellent performance
for large scale Stokes flows
[
14
,
13
]
.
An overview of the scheme is given in Figure
1
.

Our contribution has two main elements, each targeting one of the above-mentioned
challenges:

1.

Stabilizing the ill-conditioned global system.
The preconditioner is built by solving directly for pairwise corrections to the one-body basis for each particle in the suspension as necessary, with each correction capturing the
near-field interaction with a close neighbor.
The result is a two-body basis representation that can approximate all possible
near-contact interactions.
The unknown coefficients of these basis functions are then solved for
iteratively, using an FMM-accelerated global matrix-vector product.
The use of two-body bases greatly reduces GMRES iteration counts,
even for gaps as small as
10^{-3}R
, in both resistance and mobility problems.

2.

Capturing lubrication-driven fine scales locally.
Each pairwise correction is obtained by solving a small but
high-resolution
BVP with only two particles (Figure
1(c)
, inset), fully resolving the steep surface-force peaks induced by lubrication. This is achieved using an image-enhanced MFS—a 2D version of that of
[
14
]
—which also uses a hairpin curve to give high accuracy with only a single source type (Stokeslets).
We apply near-contact MFS image enhancement to the mobility problem for the first time. A subsequent compression step replaces the fine pair representation with an equivalent coarse set of sources (Figure
1(c)
, background), so that the global discretization remains coarse.
This addresses the local resolution requirements without increasing the global degrees of freedom (passed to the FMM) in the iterative solve.

Remark 1

(Connection to hybrid PDE solvers)
.

This “hybrid” of

direct
solution operators for local problems, compressing them to reduce
their effective number of unknowns, and then solving the remaining global problem

iteratively
(block-diagonal preconditioning) recurs in diverse settings. These include:

•

RCIP
[

Other local preconditioners can be advantageous with BIE

•

The two-body basis functions of Cheng–Greengard

The

[

. See
[

•

where the grouping of fine unknowns is often done via

[

Sometimes the coarse nodes are a subset of the fine; this is known as

For porosity problems without relative body motion, Quaife et al.
[

[

In each of these settings, both the number of unknowns and the iteration count is reduced

rather than square solves because of the nature of the MFS; this complicates the linear algebra but removes the need for a quadrature scheme for singular integrals.

Together, these components yield a fast, accurate, and robust MFS framework for dense suspensions. Numerical experiments confirm stable performance in challenging multi-particle configurations. Although demonstrated in 2D, the ideas are readily applicable in 3D.

General framework for two-body basis construction

We construct an efficient basis for the flow field due to
P
particles in three steps:

1.

Solve one-body BVPs for particle
i
in isolation (see inset in Figure
1(a)
) to obtain the one-body basis functions associated with particle
i
.

2.

For each close neighbor of particle
i
, add a correction to this basis involving
BVPs for the pair comprising
i
and the neighbor (see inset in Figure
1(c)
).

3.

Represent the flow due to all
P
particles as a superposition of such pair-corrected basis functions.

In this section we outline these steps separately for the resistance and mobility problems, in a deliberately high-level manner.
We assume only the existence of a local BVP solver (for one or two particles)
whose input is discretized surface data.
The reader is reassured that the abstraction will be made concrete
in Section
3
and the sequel, where we specialize to the MFS as the local BVP solver
and to disks as the particles.

2.1
The resistance (Dirichlet) problem

Consider the
i
th body, and
let
\{\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
be its set of
coarse
boundary nodes, sufficient for accurate discretization of
{\partial\Omega}^{(i)}
in isolation.
We discretize boundary velocity data on these nodes to give the vector

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}=\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}^{(i)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{M}^{(i)}\}
.
Smooth boundary functions may then be accurately interpolated from these nodes.
Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}
denote the
velocity solution operator
for the
i
th body in isolation,
meaning that it maps
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}
to the resulting flow field

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x})

which solves
the Stokes BVP (
1
) in the exterior of
\Omega^{(i)}
alone, with Dirichlet

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}

(Recall Remark
2
for the condition at infinity.)
This means that for any vector
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}
the reproducing property holds:

\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}_{j})={\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}_{j}
,

.
Note that here and beyond we use “representation” notation

\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}]

By linearity, one may view
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}
as a set of

\{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{2M}

\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}_{j}

\boldsymbol{u}

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}

is set to the

For the full system of

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\qquad\mbox{ (one-body representation).}

(5)

The full resistance problem (
1
) may now

) as an ansatz with
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}

The resulting linear system has a

In practice, one never forms the matrix, but applies it to vectors using a fast algorithm

]

Remark 3
.

In the context of boundary-based PDE solvers (BIE, MFS, etc) the above method
is known as one-body (block diagonal) preconditioning, and transforms the unknowns from densities (or source strengths) to surface data. In the MFS case we recap this
in Section
3.1
.

However, when two particles become close (forming a
near contact
), two problems arise:
i) the boundary functions become
nonsmooth, thus cannot be accurately discretized or interpolated with the coarse nodes, and
ii) the conditioning of the above linear system deteriorates.
One solution to problem i) is simply to use a finer set of one-body boundary nodes (larger
M
);
however, this would slow down each matrix-vector apply, while doing
nothing to address ii).
This motivates a two-body preconditioning method. This uses pairwise BVPs
discretized on fine nodes to modify the above coarse one-body
bases
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}
to two-body bases denoted by
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(i)}
, giving the
global representation for the flow field

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(i)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\qquad\mbox{ (two-body representation).}

(6)

The coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}
are then still determined by collocation at the coarse boundary nodes, so that the linear system remains of size
2PM\times 2PM
.

For each body (without loss of generality we describe this for body
i=1
),
its set of
2M
two-body basis functions are constructed as follows.
If body 1 has no near contacts, we simply set
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}=\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(1)}
.
If it has a single near contact, with body 2 (say), we define

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}=\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,2)}
. Here

\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,2)}
denotes a
correction basis
that solves the exterior Stokes BVP
for the pair
{\partial\Omega}^{(1)}\cup{\partial\Omega}^{(2)}
with Dirichlet velocity data

\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,2)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}](\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{0},&\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega^{(1)}\\
-\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(1)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}](\boldsymbol{x}),&\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega^{(2)}\end{cases}\qquad\mbox{ (correction data),}

(7)

which is solved numerically using a fine discretization of both particle boundaries.
In particular, the
j
th correction function,
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,2)}_{j}
, is found
by solving the pair BVP using boundary data given by evaluating the
j
th one-body basis
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(1)}_{j}
with a negative sign on body 2,
and zero data on body 1.
The correction thus cancels the one-body Dirichlet data on body 2.
Thus if bodies 1 and 2 have no other close neighbors, performing the analogous
correction for body 2 completes a pair of two-body bases
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(2)}

that
completely solves the pairwise Dirichlet BVP
, obeying the reproducing property

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}](\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{(k)})=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}_{j},&k=1,\\
\boldsymbol{0},&k=2,\\
\end{cases}\qquad\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(2)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(2)}](\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}_{j})=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{0},&k=1,\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(2)}_{j},&k=2,\end{cases}\qquad j=1,\dots,M.

(8)

In this case the system matrix would now have a
4M\times 4M
identity block on the
diagonal (for the unknowns of both bodies 1 and 2).
This bypasses any accuracy loss and ill-conditioning associated with the
one-body representation of the near contact,
at the cost of solving
4M
finely-discretized local BVPs.

If body 1 has multiple near contacts, their pairwise corrections are simply summed:

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}=\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(1)}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{C}^{(1)}}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,k)}\qquad\mbox{ (two-body basis for body 1), }

(9)

with
\mathcal{C}^{(1)}
the set of neighbor indices of body
1
.
This is repeated for the rest of the bodies
i=2,\dots,P
.
The resulting multi-contact construction of two-body bases for
P=5
particles is illustrated in Figure
3
.
This idea avoids the large system sizes associated with fully coupled multi-body treatments for near contacts,
while maintaining low GMRES iteration counts, which we demonstrate in Section
6
.

.

The above generalizes a method of Cheng & Greengard for the iterative solution of Laplace Dirichlet BVPs with multiple disks

2.2
The mobility problem

For mobility, the one-body flow field representation is

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x})+\sum_{i=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{C}^{(i)}\left[\boldsymbol{f}^{(i)},t^{(i)}\right](\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\quad\mbox{(mobility one-body representation).}

(10)

The first term is a sum of mobility-specific one-body basis functions, each producing zero net force and torque, while
\boldsymbol{C}^{(i)}
is a known
completion flow

[
55
,
57
,
6
,
47
,
13
]
, that is,
an explicit exterior Stokes solution
carrying (in the sense of (
3
)) the prescribed force and torque for body
i
, but in general not a rigid body motion on any body.
The construction of each above one-body function differs from the resistance setting.
Rather than the plain reproducing property, we have a rank-3 perturbation of it:
for any surface velocity vector
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}
on body
i
,

\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{(i)})={\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}_{j}+({\cal R}^{(i)}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)})_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

(11)

where
{\cal R}^{(i)}
is a

2M\times 2M
matrix of rank 3 whose range is the subspace of rigid body velocity data, namely

\{\boldsymbol{v}+\omega(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{(i)}-\boldsymbol{c}^{(i)})^{\perp}\}_{j=1}^{M}

for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.
In practice,
{\cal R}^{(i)}
is fixed by an ansatz
made for the specific local BVP solver used, that couples rigid body motions to unknowns
(e.g. for BIE see
[
47
, Eq. (38)]
, while for MFS see
[
13
, §3.2]
).
To solve the full mobility problem, one treats the one-body coefficients
\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}

as unknown, and uses collocation to enforce that the representation (
10
) equals
the ansatz
{\cal R}^{(k)}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(k)}
at the coarse nodes of the
k
th body, for all
k=1,\dots,P
.
As with the resistance problem,
the resulting system matrix has size
2PM\times 2PM
, with identity blocks along the diagonal,
and enables an accelerated apply.
However, now the right-hand side becomes the surface data of the completion flow
from (
10
) with a negative sign.
Full details are given in Section
4
.

Applying pair-corrections to the mobility one-body basis functions we similarly get

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(i)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x})+\sum_{i=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{C}^{(i)}\left[\boldsymbol{f}^{(i)},t^{(i)}\right](\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\mbox{(mobility two-body representation).}

(12)

with
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(i)}
again a two-body basis constructed with additive corrections to the one-body basis
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}
as in (
9
), but now with the additional constraints on the corrections
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(i,k)}
that they produce no net force or torque on the bodies within the pair.
With body 1 and body 2 sufficiently far away from any other neighbors, so that no other corrections are needed, the pair corrected basis functions for the two bodies satisfy

\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}](\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{(k)})=&\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}_{j}+({\cal R}^{(1)}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(1)})_{j},&k=1,\\
\boldsymbol{0},&k=2,\\
\end{cases}\\
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(2)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(2)}](\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{(k)})=&\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{0},&k=1,\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(2)}_{j}+({\cal R}^{(2)}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(2)})_{j},&k=2,\end{cases}\end{aligned}\qquad j=1,\dots,M.

(13)

Details on how these local mobility problems are solved using MFS are given in Section
4
.

3
Solving the resistance problem via MFS

We now apply the MFS to the approach outlined in Section
2.1
, specializing
to the case where
\Omega^{(i)}
are unit-radius (monodisperse) disks. We start with the MFS itself, followed by one-body preconditioning (recapping prior work). Recasting the latter in the new basis-function framework then provides a natural route to the new two-body algorithm.

The fundamental solution to the Stokes equations—the 2D
Stokeslet
—describes the velocity field induced by a point force. It is given by the
2\times 2
matrix-valued kernel

\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{4\pi\mu}\left[-\log\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|\boldsymbol{I}_{2}+\frac{(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})^{T}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}}\right],

(14)

with
\boldsymbol{I}_{2}
the
2\times 2
identity matrix.
A general velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}
solving the Stokes equations in the multiply-connected exterior can be approximated as a linear superposition of Stokeslets:

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}_{j})\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i)}_{j},\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega},

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
, are source points
inside disk
i
, and the associated vector source strengths
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i)}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are to be determined.
For well-separated disks, source points may be chosen equispaced on the concentric circle of radius
R_{c}<1
, with typically
0.6\leq R_{c}\leq 0.8
.
However, for lubrication driven problems with near-touching disks, more elaborate choices
are needed (as in Section
5
).

For ease of notation, let
\boldsymbol{Y}^{(i)}=\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}^{(i)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i)}=\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{(i)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
denote the stacked source points and strengths. We can then write the representation (
15
) compactly as

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{(i)})\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i)}.

(16)

The source strengths vectors

\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i)}
,
i=1,\dots,P
, are determined by enforcing boundary conditions in the least-squares sense at all sets of target coarse collocation points
\boldsymbol{X}^{(i)}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}

on the
i
th particle boundary.
It has been found that setting
M
slightly larger than
N
increases MFS accuracy
[
8
]
.
Letting
\boldsymbol{g}
again denote the stacked boundary data at all such coarse collocation nodes, and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
the stacked source coefficients, we obtain an
overdetermined least-squares linear system

\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{g},

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{S}
is a dense
2MP\times 2NP
matrix whose blocks are given by evaluating
\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
between all pairs of target and source points. As already alluded to, the matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
is exponentially ill-conditioned. In addition, it is typically too large for a dense backward-stable solve
if
P\gg 1
. Hence, efficient preconditioning is needed to transform (
17
) into a well-conditioned square system amenable for an iterative solve.

3.1
One-body preconditioning

We first review the one-body preconditioning approach of
[
45
,
14
,
13
]
, and then formulate it in the general framework of one-body basis functions of Section
2
.
We will sometimes refer to the point sets
\{\boldsymbol{X}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}
,
\{\boldsymbol{Y}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}
respectively as the coarse collocation and source points.
We will use the bar notation
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
to indicate the
vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
obtained via one-body preconditioning alone.

3.1.1
Standard formulation

The global target-from-source MFS matrix
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{2MP\times 2NP}
has the block structure,

\boldsymbol{S}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{S}^{(11)}&\boldsymbol{S}^{(12)}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{S}^{(1P)}\\
\boldsymbol{S}^{(21)}&\boldsymbol{S}^{(22)}&\cdots&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{S}^{(P1)}&\cdots&\cdots&\boldsymbol{S}^{(PP)}\end{bmatrix},

(18)

with the block

\boldsymbol{S}^{(ik)}_{mn}=\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}^{(k)}_{n}),\quad m=1,\dots,M,\quad n=1,\dots,N

(19)

mapping source points on particle
k
to target points on particle
i
.

Following
[
45
,
14
,
13
]
, we precondition the ill-conditioned overdetermined system
\boldsymbol{S}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\boldsymbol{g}
from the right using a block-diagonal matrix with entries
{\boldsymbol{S}^{(ii)}}^{+}
, the pseudoinverses of the one-body self-interaction blocks.
Since all particles are identically shaped and sized, the self-interaction block
{\boldsymbol{S}^{(ii)}}
is shared across bodies, so we
denote it simply by
\boldsymbol{P}
. For each particle, define the preconditioned unknown vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{P}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M}
, so that
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{P}^{+}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}
. The pseudoinverse
\boldsymbol{P}^{+}
is determined e.g. using the SVD,
\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{T}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is the diagonal matrix containing the singular values
\sigma_{1}^{(i)}\geq\sigma_{2}^{(i)}\geq\dots\sigma_{N}^{(i)}
.
To ensure numerical stability, one has to apply the pseudoinverse in a backward-stable fashion
[
68
,
43
,
48
,
65
,
54
]
, so that

\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}\left(\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}\right),\quad i=1,\dots,P,

(20)

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}
denotes the truncated pseudoinverse of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
; its diagonal entries are set to
1/\sigma_{j}^{(i)}
when
\sigma_{j}^{(i)}>\sigma_{1}^{(i)}\epsilon_{\text{trunc}}
, or zero otherwise. The truncation level
\epsilon_{\text{trunc}}
is typically set smaller than the desired error, but somewhat larger than machine precision.

Since
N<M
, each diagonal block
\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{+}
of the resulting preconditioned target-from-source matrix has a nontrivial nullspace: it annihilates some
(2M-2N)
-dimensional subspace of inputs. Thus, a well-conditioned matrix can only be
achieved by replacing the diagonal blocks by
\boldsymbol{I}
, which
\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{P}^{+}
approximates for vectors smooth on each body.
The preconditioned system is now square and of size
2MP\times 2MP
:

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}&\boldsymbol{S}^{(12)}\boldsymbol{P}^{+}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{S}^{(1P)}\boldsymbol{P}^{+}\\
\boldsymbol{S}^{(21)}\boldsymbol{P}^{+}&\boldsymbol{I}&\cdots&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{S}^{(P1)}\boldsymbol{P}^{+}&\cdots&\cdots&\boldsymbol{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{g}^{(1)}\\
\boldsymbol{g}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{g}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix}.

(21)

Once this has been solved iteratively for
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}
,
the MFS strengths are recovered via (
20
).

Remark 5

(New unknowns)
.

One-body preconditioning reformulates the problem so that the unknowns,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, are collocation velocity values at the boundaries rather than interior source strengths. Although this increases the number of unknowns slightly (typically, for the coarse grid,
we choose
M=1.2N)
, the resulting system is square and much better conditioned, at least for sufficiently well-separated particles. For close-to-touching configurations, however, both
N
and
M
must be increased substantially, often with
M\gg N
, leading to much larger linear systems. This provides another indication that one-body preconditioning alone is insufficient for large-scale simulations of dense suspensions. By contrast, the two-body basis will resolve the near-contact interactions locally while retaining a coarse global discretization. The resulting reduction in the number of unknowns is quantified in Section
6.1
.

3.1.2
One-body basis reformulation and accelerated matrix-vector product

We now recast (
21
) in terms of one-body basis functions.
It is easy to check that if we define
the MFS-solved one-body basis function for the
i
th body as

\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}^{(i)},\quad&\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{m}^{(i)},\\
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}_{n})(\boldsymbol{P}^{+}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)})_{n},\quad&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(22)

then the one-body basis collocation procedure described at the beginning of Section
2.1
gives precisely the preconditioned MFS linear system (
21
).
Recall that for numerical stability,
\boldsymbol{P}^{+}
must be applied as in (
20
).
Converting the resulting solution vector
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
using (
20
),
the MFS evaluation of the flow
\boldsymbol{u}
at new targets using (
15
) is
identical to the one-body basis sum (
5
).
In short, one-body (block-diagonal) preconditioning is equivalent to
using
one-body basis functions that solve isolated-particle BVPs
.

A practical aspect is to apply the huge matrix in (
21
) using a fast algorithm.
This matrix-vector apply follows the general algorithm outlined in Algorithm
1
. We will refer back to this basic algorithm later, when two-body corrections are added.

3.2
Two-body preconditioning

We now introduce pairwise corrections to the MFS one-body representation. Pair interactions are resolved locally per pair on a fine grid of
N_{f}
source points and
M_{f}
collocation points per particle (their precise location choices are deferred to Section
5
).
We use superscripts
(i,k)
to denote quantities associated with
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(i,k)}
, the basis correction to particle
i
due to near contact with particle
k
, but
(i\text{-}k)
to denote quantities needed
jointly
for
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(i,k)}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(k,i)}
. The stacked vector of all
2N_{f}
fine source points for the
(i\text{-}k)
pair is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(i\text{-}k)}
, and the corresponding
2M_{f}
collocation points by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(i\text{-}k)}
. To distinguish fine and coarse discretizations, let
N_{c}
and
M_{c}
now denote the number of coarse source and collocation points per particle.

Our goal is to use the fine MFS to derive formulae for the pair correction basis
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,2)}
(for concreteness we pick
i=1
,
k=2
). This requires two ingredients: an algorithm to evaluate
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,2)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(1)}](\boldsymbol{x})
, and a procedure to compute the associated internal fine source strengths for the pair, denoted by
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}^{(1,2)}\}_{q=1}^{2N_{f}}
. The detailed steps outlined here are specific to the resistance problem, but the corresponding corrections to the one-body basis functions appearing in the mobility problem (see Section
4.3
) follow the same structure.

The first ingredient is simple:
once
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}
is known, we define
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,2)}
via a
“fine” sum of Stokeslets:

\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,2)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(1)}](\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{q=1}^{2N_{f}}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{q}^{(1\text{-}2)})\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}^{(1,2)},

(25)

with
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}
some linear function of
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}
.
With
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}
known, the global representation (
6
) can be
evaluated with an FMM that includes
the fine source points
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(1\text{-}2)}
and strengths
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}
.

It remains to define the second ingredient: the linear map from
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(1)}
to
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}
.
For this we use the fine MFS with strength vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}
to solve
the near-contact pairwise BVP as in (
7
).
Enforcing its boundary data at the fine collocation nodes
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(1,2)}

gives the overdetermined least-squares

4M_{f}\times 4N_{f}
system (again recalling the Stokes vector character where
each
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}^{(1,2)}
is a 2-vector),

\sum_{q=1}^{2N_{f}}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(1\text{-}2)}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(1\text{-}2)}_{q})\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}^{(1,2)}\;=\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\boldsymbol{0},&\ell=1,\dots,M_{f}\qquad\qquad\mbox{(on body 1)}\\
-\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(1)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(1)}](\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(1\text{-}2)}_{\ell}),&\ell=M_{f}+1,\dots,2M_{f}\qquad\mbox{(on body 2)},\\
\end{array}\right.

where we assume indexing of the fine collocation points for the pair,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(1\text{-}2)}_{\ell}
, such that the first
M_{f}
lie on
\partial\Omega^{(1)}
and the rest on
\partial\Omega^{(2)}
.
Compactly, we write this as

\boldsymbol{F}^{(1\text{-}2)}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\
-\boldsymbol{H}^{(1,2)}\end{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(1)},

(26)

where
\boldsymbol{H}^{(1,2)}
is a
2M_{f}\times 2M_{c}
one-body basis evaluation matrix from the “active” body 1 to its neighbor.
Via the definition of the one-body basis in (
22
),
\boldsymbol{H}^{(1,2)}
has its own factorization as
\boldsymbol{H}^{(1,2)}=\boldsymbol{Q}^{(1,2)}\boldsymbol{P}^{+}
, where
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\ell n}^{(1,2)}\coloneqq\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(1\text{-}2)}_{l+M_{f}},\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)}_{n})
,
l=1,\dots,M_{f}
,
n=1,\dots,N_{c}
. The actions of the four matrices
{\boldsymbol{F}^{(1\text{-}2)}}^{+}
,
\boldsymbol{H}^{(1,2)}
,
\boldsymbol{Q}^{(1,2)}
and
\boldsymbol{P}^{+}
are illustrated in Figure
4
.

In practice the stable solution of the ill-conditioned linear system (
26
)
has two stages:
in a precomputation the matrices are filled, densely factorized via
\boldsymbol{F}^{(1\text{-}2)}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{T}
,
and the factors
\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}

and
\boldsymbol{U}^{T}[\boldsymbol{0};-\boldsymbol{H}^{(1,2)}]

stored, noting that

3.3
Peanut compression

The above two-body representation uses finely resolved MFS source points for each contact pair. To accelerate evaluation of the correction bases in the far field, we now introduce a recompression step that replaces these fine sources with effective strengths back at the original coarse source points. Fine sources are only retained for evaluations in the vicinity of the particle pair.

(1\text{-}2)
pair.

\boldsymbol{Y}^{(1)}

\boldsymbol{Y}^{(2)}

3.4
Extracting forces and torques

In a resistance problem, the final output is particle net forces and torques,
\{\boldsymbol{f}^{(i)},\,t^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}
.
Following Gauss’ law for Stokes, all Stokeslet sources in the interior of a particle contribute to these quantities. For particle 1, the force and torque pair
(\boldsymbol{f}^{(1)},\,t^{(1)})
can be determined from the computed coarse sources
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(1)}
stemming from the one-body basis, together with the fine sources
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,k)}
associated with all close neighbors
k
of particle 1:

\boldsymbol{f}^{(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i}^{(1)}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{C}^{(1)}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,k)}_{i},\quad t^{(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i}^{(1)}\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)})^{\perp}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{C}^{(1)}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}^{(1,k)}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{i}^{(1\text{-}k)}-\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)})^{\perp}.

(32)

With more compact notation, (
32
) can be written as

\left[{\boldsymbol{f}^{(1)}}^{T},t^{(1)}\right]^{T}=\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(1)}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{C}^{(1)}}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(1,k)}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,k)},

(33)

with the rigid body matrices

\boldsymbol{K}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}_{2}&(\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)}_{1}-\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)})^{\perp}\\
\boldsymbol{I}_{2}&(\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)}_{2}-\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)})^{\perp}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{I}_{2}&(\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)}_{N_{c}}-\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)})^{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{c}\times 3}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(1,k)}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}_{2}&(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(1\text{-}k)}_{1}-\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)})^{\perp}\\
\boldsymbol{I}_{2}&(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(1\text{-}k)}_{2}-\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)})^{\perp}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{I}_{2}&(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(1\text{-}k)}_{N_{f}}-\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)})^{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{f}\times 3}.

(34)

The second term in (
33
) can be viewed as a correction to the net forcing due to all near contacts. We assume that the coarse matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
is equal for every disk,
and hence add no superscript. The fine matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(1,k)}
however depend on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(1\text{-}k)}
,
k\in\mathcal{C}^{(1)}

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,k)}
,
k\in\mathcal{C}^{(1)}

that for the
(1\text{-}k)
pair extracts the correction to the net forces and torques, given the coarse one-body sources only. This means for the
(1\text{-}2)
near contact with no other close neighbors that

\boldsymbol{f}^{(2)}\\

\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{K}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(1)}\\
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(2)}\end{bmatrix}+\boldsymbol{E}_{ft}^{(1\text{-}2)}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(1)}\\

(35)

(Fine sources are needed)
.

It is not possible to recover the net forces and torques on the individual particles from the
coarse sources computed via peanut compression. Although these sources reproduce the same far-field flow and the same total force for the pair, they do
not
in general preserve the forcing on each particle
separately

3.5
The full resistance algorithm

Pseudocode summarizing the full resistance solve is given in Algorithm
2
. It assumes a uniform discretization of coarse proxy and collocation point sets per body, takes as input the stacked vector of rigid body velocities evaluated at all coarse collocation nodes, and returns net forces and torques on all bodies. More specifically, we solve the
two-body collocation system

\sum_{i=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(i)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\{\boldsymbol{X}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P},

(37)

and use internal one-body coarse sources to recover forces and torques (via the force/torque correction matrix for each close pair).
In the pseudocode, a boolean flag
flowEval
indicates whether the full sets of coarse and fine sources,

(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i\text{-}k)},k\in\mathcal{C}^{(i)},i=1,\dots,P\}),

(38)

are returned, to enable flow field evaluations both far from and near to the particles in a separate routine. Returning the triplet in (
38
) instead of merely
\boldsymbol{\mu}
enables direct flow field evaluations, e.g. via the FMM.

The detailed matrix–vector apply, needed in each GMRES iteration, is outlined separately in Algorithm
3
. In each call, the flow field due to the corrected sources is evaluated globally via the FMM, and then modified locally on a per-pair basis, following the general structure of Algorithm
1
. For the
(i\text{-}k)
pair, the coarse source correction
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i\text{-}k)}
cannot replace the fine sources when evaluating the field on the pair itself. Hence, the contribution from
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i\text{-}k)}
should in principle be subtracted off and the flow field due to the fine sources
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i\text{-}k)}
added back in. This would however mean that fine sources would need to be retrieved, at extra cost. We can avoid this by replacing the flow field due to fine sources by the evaluation of the one-body basis on the neighboring particle, which is precisely the quantity that the fine sources are constructed to match; see (
7
) and Step 2b of Algorithm
3
. For a reminder of the action of the matrices
\boldsymbol{H}^{(i,k)}
and
\boldsymbol{P}
, see Figure
4
. As a last step, the one-body blocks must be corrected, as with the one-body basis. This procedure ensures that the
4N_{c}\times 4N_{c}
two-body system matrix obtained for an isolated contact pair is approximately the identity.

This completes the interpretation of the peanut-compressed two-body MFS scheme as a
1-level fast direct solver coupled to an iterative solver for the coarse unknowns.

Remark 9

(Fast pair corrections)
.

In practice, the loop over close pairs in Algorithm
3
can be replaced by two applications of precomputed sparse matrices, with nonzero entries only for coarse sources associated with close pairs. The first matrix maps one-body coarse source strengths
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
to corrected coarse source strengths
\boldsymbol{\lambda}

4
Solving the mobility problem via MFS

We now show how the same type of basis representation can be used to efficiently solve a mobility problem. As a reminder, the forces and torques,
\{\boldsymbol{f}^{(i)},t^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}
, are prescribed and
particle velocities,
\{\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)},\omega^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}
, are unknown in this setting. The coarse and fine point sets and their respective sources follow the conventions introduced in Tables
2
and Table
3
.

4.1
One-body representation

We begin with a dilute suspension, where a set of coarse sources per body suffices to resolve all interactions, and specify the representation in (
10
). In Section
4.2
, we will see that also the mobility one-body basis functions lead to a system equal to that stemming from one-body preconditioning, as was the case for the one-body basis functions used for resistance.

We first focus on particle
i
and construct the corresponding one-body basis functions. The slip boundary condition (
11
) will by design be satisfied exactly at the collocation nodes. We therefore now first determine
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x})
,
\boldsymbol{x}\notin\boldsymbol{X}^{(i)}
and soon return to the case
\boldsymbol{x}\in\boldsymbol{X}^{(i)}
.
The discretized slip boundary condition of (
11
) can in the one-body problem be written as
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{B}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\\
\omega^{(i)}\end{bmatrix}+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}
. Here, the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
maps rigid body velocities to boundary velocity. It is defined analogously to
\boldsymbol{K}
in (
34
), but has size
2M_{c}\times 3
, and is formed by replacing
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}^{(1)}
,
j=1,\dots,N_{c}
, with
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{(1)}
,
j=1,\dots,M_{c}
. Using the representation in (
15
) with
P=1
, we formulate the constrained least-squares problem
\boldsymbol{S}\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\boldsymbol{s}
subject to
\boldsymbol{f}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
t^{(i)}=0
. To avoid enforcing these constraints explicitly, we use a “recompleted” formulation as in
[
13
]
. The idea is to construct
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}
so that the constraints are automatically satisfied. The force and torque constraints then read
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{0}
. This is satisfied by choosing
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}=(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L})\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}
, where
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{K})^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{c}\times 2N_{c}}
projects onto rigid body motions and where
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
emphasizes that these are the one-body source strengths, as in the resistance setting. We thus represent
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}
as

\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{(i)})\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\notin\boldsymbol{X}^{(i)},

(39)

where the dependence on
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}
is encoded in the coefficient vector
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}
, whose explicit form is derived below.
An unused subspace can be exploited as an ansatz for the unknown rigid body velocities, allowing us to express them as

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\\
\omega^{(i)}\end{bmatrix}=-\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}.

(40)

The unknown boundary data at the collocation nodes can then be written as

\boldsymbol{s}^{(i)}=-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}.

(41)

One solves for
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}
by evaluating (
39
) at
\boldsymbol{X}^{(i)}
and matching the result with (
41
) such that

\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)},

(42)

where
\boldsymbol{S}_{L}=\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L})+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}
.
At the collocation nodes, we
express the unknown rigid body velocity pair
(\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)},\omega^{(i)})
as linear functionals of
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}
via (
40
) and (
42
). Hence,
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}
can be expressed as

\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)}](\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{j=1}^{M_{c}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}^{(i)}-\left(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}\right)_{m},\quad\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{m}^{(i)},\\
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N_{c}}\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}_{n})\left[\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)(\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(i)})\right]_{n},\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(43)

This specifies the rigid-body matrices in (
11
) as

{\cal R}^{(i)}=-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}
, for all
i
.

By construction,
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}
carries zero net force and torque on particle
i
.
To represent flows with non-zero prescribed force and torque, we add a known completion flow to the sum of one-body basis functions, as in (
10
).
In the present MFS setting, we construct it using Stokeslet sources at the coarse source points,

\boldsymbol{C}^{(i)}\left[\boldsymbol{f}^{(i)},t^{(i)}\right](\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{(i)})\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}^{(i)},\qquad i=1,\dots,P.

(44)

Its source strengths
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}^{(i)}
are set per particle to sum exactly to the prescribed force and torques:
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{F}
(compare (
33
)), determined via the ansatz
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}=\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{a}
, for some rigid body velocity components stacked in
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This completes the description of the representation in (
10
).

4.2
Equivalence with one-body preconditioning

This subsection explains the equivalence of the above to our prior one-body preconditioning method for mobility
[
13
]
, and connects it to the general formulation of
Section
2.2
.
Written in terms of internal proxy source strengths
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}
instead of boundary unknowns
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}
, the representation in (
10
) may be expressed as

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{(i)})\left[\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}^{(i)}\right].

(45)

This was the representation of the flow field in
[
13
]
, upon a split of the source vector into one component in the null-space of the constraint matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
and one component that sums to the net force and torque on each particle. Evaluating (
45
) at the collocation nodes
\{\boldsymbol{X}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}
, expressing the boundary data as
\boldsymbol{g}^{(i)}=-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)},\,i=1,\dots,P
, and reordering terms, the system to solve for the mobility problem takes the form

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{S}^{(11)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\boldsymbol{S}^{(12)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)&\dots&\boldsymbol{S}^{(1P)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)\\
\boldsymbol{S}^{(21)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)&\boldsymbol{S}^{(22)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\dots&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{S}^{(P1)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)&\dots&\dots&\boldsymbol{S}^{(PP)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(1)}\\
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{(1)}\\
-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix},

(46)

with the completion flow surface velocity data

\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{(i)}=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{X}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{Y}^{(k)})\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}^{(k)}.

(47)

This large unconstrained least-squares problem can be solved by applying the same type of one-body preconditioning from the right as was applied for the resistance problem in Section
3.1.1
. The result is

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}&\boldsymbol{S}^{(12)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}&\dots&\boldsymbol{S}^{(1P)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}\\
\boldsymbol{S}^{(21)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}&\boldsymbol{I}&\dots&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{S}^{(P1)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}&\dots&\dots&\boldsymbol{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{(1)}\\
-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix},

(48)

where the diagonal blocks have been replaced by
\boldsymbol{I}
to regularize the system, as in the resistance setting.
The above linear system is precisely the one described in Section
2.2
when
using one-body basis functions that obey (
11
), recalling the above
definition of
{\cal R}^{(i)}
.

4.3
Two-body preconditioning

The two-body correction
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,2)}
is constructed so that its internal sources exert no net force or torque on either body 1 or 2.
As in the one-body case (
43
), this is achieved by a projection—now applied to the fine source strengths on both particles in the pair. The correction basis takes the form

\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(1,2)}[{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(1)}](\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{q=1}^{2N_{f}}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{q}^{(1\text{-}2)})\left[(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{(1\text{-}2)})\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}\right]_{q},

(49)

with the fine pair projection matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{(1\text{-}2)}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{(1,2)}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{(2,1)}\end{bmatrix},\text{ where }\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{(i,k)}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(i,k)}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(i,k)}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(i,k)}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(i,k)}}^{T}

(50)

and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(i,k)}
is the fine rigid body matrix of (
34
).

Equation (
49
) leaves an unused image space of
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(1,2)}}^{T}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(2,1)}}^{T}
, which we now exploit to close the system—together with the corresponding image space of
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}
from the one-body basis.
For the contact pair
(1\text{-}2)
, assumed to be isolated from other particles, we make the ansatz

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}^{(1)}\\
\omega^{(1)}\\
\boldsymbol{v}^{(2)}\\
\omega^{(2)}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(1,2)}}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(2,1)}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(2,1)}\right)-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(1)}\\
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(2)}\end{bmatrix}

(51)

for the unknown rigid body velocities.
At the particle boundaries, the no-slip velocity field at the coarse collocation nodes is, as before, expressed as

\boldsymbol{g}^{(1)}=\boldsymbol{B}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}^{(1)}\\
\omega^{(1)}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{g}^{(2)}=\boldsymbol{B}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}^{(2)}\\
\omega^{(2)}\end{bmatrix},

which gives the combined form:

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{g}^{(1)}\\
\boldsymbol{g}^{(2)}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(1,2)}}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{B}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(2,1)}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1,2)}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(2,1)}\right)-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(2)}\end{bmatrix}.

(52)

We now match the velocity field produced by (
12
) with
P=2
to the unknown no-slip boundary data in (
52
). After reordering terms, the resulting equation to be satisfied at the coarse collocation nodes
\boldsymbol{X}^{(1\text{-}2)}
becomes:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{2}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(i)}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}]\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{m}^{(1\text{-}2)}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{M_{c}}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}\end{bmatrix}_{mj}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(2)}\end{bmatrix}_{j}

(53)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{2N_{f}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2N_{f}}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1\text{-}2)}_{m},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{j}^{(1\text{-}2)})(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{(1\text{-}2)})_{jn}+\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(1,2)}}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{B}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(2,1)}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}_{mn}\right)\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}^{(1,2)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{2N_{f}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2N_{f}}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1\text{-}2)}_{m},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{j}^{(1\text{-}2)})(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{(1\text{-}2)})_{jn}+\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}^{(1,2)}}^{T}&\boldsymbol{0}\\

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\boldsymbol{C}^{(i)}\left[\boldsymbol{f}^{(i)},t^{(i)}\right](\boldsymbol{X}^{(1\text{-}2)}_{m}),m=1,\dots,2M_{c}.

The block-diagonal matrix appearing in the second term cancels the same-body contribution
\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{+}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)}
appearing in

Terms three and four represent the two-body correction bases

and
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(2,1)}
and their associated contributions to the no-slip boundary velocity.
It remains to express

(54)

4.4

It remains to express the correction fields

Specifically, we solve

\sum_{j=1}^{2N_{c}}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p}^{(1\text{-}2)},\tilde{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(1\text{-}2)}_{j})\left[\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}^{\text{pair}}\right)\tilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(1\text{-}2)}\right]_{j}=\sum_{q=1}^{2N_{f}}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p}^{(1\text{-}2)},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(1\text{-}2)}_{q})\left[(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{(1\text{-}2)})\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(1\text{-}2)}\right]_{q},\quad p=1,\dots,M_{p},

(56)

where
\boldsymbol{L}^{\text{pair}}
is block-diagonal with two copies of
\boldsymbol{L}
on the diagonal. The full source vector is formed as in (
29

45

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\mathbb{S}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{Y}^{(i)})\left[\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\right)\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}^{(i)}\right].

\{\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)},\omega^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{P}

5
The fine MFS representation for near-contact pairs

Recall that the
N_{c}
coarse MFS sources are equispaced on the concentric circle of radius
R_{c}
; the
M_{c}
coarse collocation nodes are equispaced on the disk boundary, and we will
fix
M_{c}=1.2N_{c}
in this paper.
The rest of this subsection describes
the MFS sources and collocation nodes used for the local fine pairwise BVPs.
This is inspired by the method of images of Cheng & Greengard
[
19
]
and extends our earlier 3D approach for spheres
[
14
]
to 2D disks.

The pairwise fine source sets
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(i\text{-}k)}
include a fixed set of
N_{fp}
fine “proxy” points per disk, equispaced on the concentric circle of radius
R_{f}
.
Close enough pairs additionally carry sources adapted to the pair contact separation
\delta
, lying on two arcs designed to enclose known
singularities in the analytic continuation of the exterior BVP solution into the disk interiors.
See Figure
6
. These singularities arise from successive image
reflections of the centers through the disk boundaries
(in the Laplace case see, e.g.,
[
19
]
), giving an infinite series
along the line connecting the two centers, that accumulates at radius

R_{\text{acc}}(\delta)=1+\delta/2-\sqrt{\delta+\delta^{2}/4}.

(59)

The formula is the same for 2D and 3D. In the Stokes case
(see
[
14
]
) it is
expected that the entire line from center to radius
R_{\text{acc}}
is singular.
In
[
14
]
, to handle this singularity we distributed a mixture of fundamental solution types along the outer pieces of the lines, clustered toward the accumulation points.
Instead here we present a scheme based solely on Stokeslets:
for pairs close enough that
R_{\text{acc}}(\delta)>R_{f}
, the additional Stokeslets lie on
elliptical arcs that “shield” the line singularities. The shielding property is necessary for strengths to remain of bounded magnitude
as the MFS solution converges
[
24
, Thm 2.4, Ch. 4]

(also see
[
8
, Conj. 11]
), required for numerical stability.

:

R_{\text{acc}}(\delta)
from each particle’s center.

The shielding elliptical arcs are shown in Figure
6
, where both centers lie
on the
x
-axis.
In body-local coordinates, the ellipse associated with particle 1 has foci at
x=0
and
x=a
, while its rightmost point is located at
x=a+\gamma(1-a)
, where
0<\gamma<1
is a tunable parameter. In complex notation centered at the center of disk 1,
the ellipse is parametrized by

z(\theta)=\frac{a}{2}[1+\cos(\theta+i\alpha)],\qquad\mbox{ where }\quad\alpha=\operatorname{arcosh}\left(1+\frac{2\gamma(1-a)}{a}\right).

We sample the ellipse on the half-shifted angular grid

\theta_{j}=\frac{(j+1/2)\pi}{N_{\rm ell}},\qquad j=0,\dots,2N_{\rm ell}-1,

which clusters nodes toward the tips of the ellipse. We then retain only the nodes satisfying
|z(\theta_{j})|>R_{f}
. This gives an ellipse segment lying outside the fine proxy circle with a high density of nodes in a neighborhood of the image accumulation points. If

R_{\rm acc}(\delta)\leq R_{f}
, we do not add any enhancing nodes, since
singularities are already shielded by the existing fine proxy circle.

For the pair
(1,2)
with unit center–center direction
e^{i\varphi}
, the
enhancing source sets in complex notation are

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(1,2)}_{\rm enh}=\left\{\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)}+\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{e^{i\varphi}z(\theta_{j})\}\\
\Im\{e^{i\varphi}z(\theta_{j})\}\end{bmatrix}:\ |z(\theta_{j})|>R_{f}\right\},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(2,1)}_{\rm enh}=\left\{\boldsymbol{c}^{(2)}-\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{e^{i\varphi}z(\theta_{j})\}\\
\Im\{e^{i\varphi}z(\theta_{j})\}\end{bmatrix}:\ |z(\theta_{j})|>R_{f}\right\}.

This pair’s full fine source set is then

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(1\text{-}2)}=\left\{\boldsymbol{c}^{(1)}+R_{f}\begin{bmatrix}\cos(t_{i})\\
\sin(t_{i})\end{bmatrix}_{i=1}^{N_{fp}}\right\}\cup\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(1,2)}_{\rm enh}\cup\left\{\boldsymbol{c}^{(2)}+R_{f}\begin{bmatrix}\cos(t_{i})\\
\sin(t_{i})\end{bmatrix}_{i=1}^{N_{fp}}\right\}\cup\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(2,1)}_{\rm enh}.

Since at most
2N_{\text{ell}}
ellipse nodes are sampled, one always has

N_{f}\leq N_{fp}+2N_{\text{ell}}
. In practice,
R_{f}
is chosen so that
N_{f}\leq N_{fp}+N_{\text{ell}}
.
Based on empirical performance, we set
\gamma=0.3
.

The local two-body MFS solve requires a set of
2M_{f}
boundary collocation nodes,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{(i\text{-}k)}
. For this, we first place
M_{f}=\lceil a_{f}N_{fp}\rceil
equispaced nodes on each disk boundary.
This grid is then augmented by additional pair-adapted collocation nodes obtained through a fixed Möbius angle map. Specifically, we sample
M_{\text{ell}}=a_{\text{ell}}N_{\text{ell}}
angles
\theta_{j}
uniformly and reparameterize them according to
\tilde{\theta}_{j}=\pi+\arg\left(\frac{e^{i\theta_{j}}+r}{1+re^{i\theta_{j}}}\right).
This concentrates nodes where the boundary data vary most rapidly, without refining the entire geometry. Throughout the paper, we use
a_{f}=1.2
,
r=0.6
and
a_{\text{ell}}=5
.

The above fine MFS parameters were chosen via convergence studies, that, since they are not
directly relevant to the proposed compressed two-body preconditioning method, will be reported elsewhere.
Recall that in this method, the fine MFS matrices are used only in precomputations;
the main iterative solution involves only coarse surface unknowns (
2M_{c}
unknowns per body).

Remark 10

(Unknowns with one-body preconditioning)
.

With one-body preconditioning, each body must carry the full fine boundary discretization required to resolve all of its near-contact interactions, leading to very large linear systems. As in our earlier work on spheres
[
14
]
, this remains true even when using an image-enhanced discretization, essential for resolving near-contact pair interactions efficiently. Without such enhancement, dramatically more source and collocation points would be required, especially in three dimensions.

Remark 11

(Combining fine sources)
.

In the evaluation of the flow field, where the fine grid is needed, we combine the fine proxy source strengths together from multiple contacts to speed up the computations.
This is easy since the fine proxy grids do not rotate; they will be coincident. The fine
grid from ellipse segments, however, cannot be combined.

6.1
Resistance and mobility: two-body vs. one-body preconditioning

We compare the proposed scheme—peanut-compressed
two-body preconditioning—against the one-body preconditioner, for geometries where small inter-particle distances can be controlled systematically.
With one-body preconditioning, to resolve all near-contacts the source and collocation points are formed by taking the union of the fine discretizations associated with all near contact pairs.
We generate random clusters so that each disk has separation exactly
\delta
to at least one neighbor, and vary
\delta
while fixing
P
(the number of disks). For the resistance problem, we take
P=20
, while for mobility,
P=50
. The solves use input quantities (forces/torques or translational/angular velocities) sampled from a standard normal
distribution, with forces subsequently shifted
so that the total force in the system sums to zero.

pointwise residual

\epsilon_{\text{res}}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\|\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{g}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{2},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega^{(i)}.

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})

\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)},\omega^{(i)}

Mobility for random disk packings

We next demonstrate the proposed peanut-compressed two-body preconditioned

We will study the effect of area fraction upon iteration count.

We use a geometry generation method inspired by
[

[

52

Repeating these accept/reject moves produces a disordered packing at the desired area fraction while preserving a controlled minimum inter-particle distance.

7
Conclusions

We introduce a two-body preconditioned method of fundamental solutions for the 2D Stokes resistance and mobility problems in close-to-touching suspensions of circular particles. Starting from a local basis constructed for each particle in isolation, a new two-body basis is built that incorporates corrections obtained from local high-resolution two-body boundary value problems. These corrections resolve the lubrication-driven fine scales that arise near close contacts while simultaneously regularizing the ill-conditioning caused by shrinking particle gaps.

A central feature of the scheme is the compression of the local fine-grid pair representation into an equivalent coarse representation on a geometrically natural “peanut” separation surface. The geometry of the particles therefore directly dictates the compression of the fine grid into a coarse one, allowing the global discretization to remain uniformly coarse while still resolving particle separations down to a thousandth of the radius. As a result, the solve is dominated by all-to-all evaluation of coarse sources at coarse collocation points, accelerated in our implementation by a linearly scaling fast multipole method.

Compared to standard block-diagonal right preconditioning, the two-body preconditioner not only dramatically reduces the GMRES iteration count, but also stabilizes the solve sufficiently to achieve higher accuracy at significantly lower computational cost. For the mobility problem, we observe iteration counts essentially independent of the number of bodies,
P
, depending primarily on the local near-neighbor count per particle. Although not shown explicitly in Section
6
, the iteration count for resistance grows with
P
, consistent with earlier observations in
[
70
,
60
,
13
]
. An important direction for future work is therefore to couple the present approach with long-range preconditioning strategies
[
34
,
35
]
that address ill-conditioning arising from far-field effects.

Our framework is not specific to Stokes flow or to the method of fundamental solutions. We believe that the idea of local pair corrections extends naturally to other elliptic PDEs and to other boundary-based solvers, provided an accurate technique is available for solving the local two-body boundary value problems. Precomputing corrections for all near-contact pairs is currently the dominant cost of the scheme, and an important direction for future work is the development of strategies to reduce or amortize this setup cost.

Future directions also include extending the method to more general geometries, such as non-circular particles and polydisperse suspensions, as well as to three-dimensional problems, building on our earlier work for spheres
[
14
,
13
]
. Since the global solve remains coarse while all singular near-contact interactions are resolved locally, the proposed scheme is fast, accurate, and robust, providing a scalable and flexible foundation for large-scale simulations of dense suspensions.
