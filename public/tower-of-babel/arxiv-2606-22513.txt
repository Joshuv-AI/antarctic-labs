Betti Numbers of Sequentially Cohen–Macaulay Co-Chordal Graphs and Their Applications
Mohammed Rafiq Namiq

Abstract
. We study sequentially Cohen–Macaulay co-chordal graphs through the glued clique complexes of their chordal complements. Using the characterization of these complements as ( d 1 , … , d q ) (d_{1},\ldots,d_{q}) -trees, we derive explicit formulas for the graded Betti numbers of the associated edge ideals, yielding a complete homological characterization of sequentially Cohen–Macaulay co-chordal graphs. As applications, we determine exact homological invariants for several important graph families, including split graphs, threshold graphs, and prime ideal graphs, and classify their Cohen–Macaulay cases. We further characterize the sequentially Cohen–Macaulay nilpotent graphs of finite direct products of Artinian chain rings and establish a closed formula for their graded Betti numbers in terms of local nilpotency indices and residue field cardinalities. Finally, we classify the zero-divisor graphs of ℤ n \mathbb{Z}_{n} , proving that they are sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if n = 2 ​ p n=2p or n = p a n=p^{a} , where p p is a prime.

1.
Introduction

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite simple graph on
N
vertices, and let
S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
be a polynomial ring over a field
\mathbb{K}
. The edge ideal
I(G)
maps the combinatorial topology of the graph to the homological invariants of the Stanley-Reisner ring
S/I(G)
associated with the independence complex
\Delta_{G}
. Fröberg
[
11
]
established that the edge ideal
I(G)
admits a linear resolution if and only if
G
is a co-chordal graph, meaning its complement
\overline{G}
contains no induced cycles of length four or greater. Fröberg’s theorem established a fundamental connection between graph structure and homological invariants, leading to extensive studies of graded Betti numbers, projective dimension, regularity, and Cohen–Macaulay properties through combinatorial and topological methods
[
1
,
9
,
17
,
21
,
23
,
30
]
. This structural equivalence restricts algebraic complexity to the linear homological strand, forcing the graded Betti numbers to satisfy
\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\beta_{i,i+1}(S/I(G))
for all
i\geq 1
. We note that
\beta_{0}(S/I(G))=1
trivially, and the Betti numbers of the edge ideal
I(G)
itself are obtained via the homological shift
\beta_{i}(I(G))=\beta_{i+1}(S/I(G))
for all
i\geq 0
.

Among co-chordal graphs, split graphs and threshold graphs are particularly important. Split graphs, originally introduced by Földes and Hammer
[
10
]
, admit a partition of the vertex set into a clique and an independent set
[
14
,
19
]
, whereas threshold graphs, first defined by Chvátal and Hammer
[
7
]
, form a more rigid subclass whose neighborhoods are linearly ordered by inclusion
[
15
,
16
,
18
]
. These graph classes also appear naturally in algebraic structures, especially in the study of zero-divisor graphs, initiated by Beck
[
5
]
and refined to its modern form by Anderson and Livingston
[
2
]
. This algebraic approach naturally extended to other structures, including annihilator graphs, introduced by Badawi
[
4
]
, and prime ideal graphs, introduced by Salih and Jund
[
27
]
and further investigated from a homological perspective by Rasheed
[
24
]
. Despite their importance, explicit formulas for graded Betti numbers remain limited. Most existing results rely on recursive constructions, mapping cone techniques, or computational methods
[
8
,
20
,
25
,
26
]
. For example, Singh and Verma
[
28
]
obtained upper bounds for the Betti numbers of split graphs. Recent work by Fröberg
[
13
]
proposed an explicit Betti formula for this family. Building on this foundation, our
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree approach resolves the remaining combinatorial subtleties, yielding exact and universally applicable homological descriptions across all cases.

A major advance in this direction was obtained by Ahmed, Mafi, and Namiq
[
1
]
, who proved that a co-chordal graph is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if its chordal complement is a
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree. Independently, Namiq
[
23
]
derived explicit formulas for the linear strand Betti numbers of glued simplicial complexes. These results suggest that the homological invariants of sequentially Cohen–Macaulay co-chordal graphs should be completely determined by the clique structure of their chordal complements.

The purpose of this paper is to make this connection mathematically explicit. We emphasize that the sequentially Cohen–Macaulay property is not merely a structural classification, but the precise topological condition required to collapse the general alternating Betti sums of glued complexes into purely combinatorial closed formulas. We show that, for sequentially Cohen–Macaulay co-chordal graphs, the Betti formula reduces to a closed binomial expression depending only on maximal clique data. This yields exact formulas for the graded Betti numbers, projective dimension, and depth of the associated edge ideals, providing a complete homological characterization of this class.

As applications, we derive exact homological invariants for several foundational graph families. For split graphs and threshold graphs, we obtain closed formulas for all graded Betti numbers and characterize the Cohen–Macaulay members. Consequently, the classification of Cohen–Macaulay split graphs shows that Villarreal’s whisker construction
[
29
]
represents a special case of a broader attachment criterion. We further prove that prime ideal graphs of finite commutative rings are complete split graphs, allowing their homological invariants to be computed explicitly.

Expanding our focus to broader algebraic structures, we investigate the nilpotent and zero-divisor graphs of commutative rings, the former introduced by Chen
[
6
]
and the latter by Beck
[
5
]
and refined by Anderson and Livingston
[
2
]
, whose homological invariants have been the subject of recent algorithmic and combinatorial attention
[
8
,
25
,
26
]
. We establish a classification theorem completely characterizing the co-chordal and sequentially Cohen–Macaulay nilpotent graphs over finite direct products of Artinian chain rings. By computing the explicit valuation layers and attachment degrees, we obtain a closed Betti formula depending only on the local nilpotency indices and residue field cardinalities. This framework elegantly specializes to yield exact homological invariants for finite chain rings. We contrast this with the full zero-divisor graph of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, where the presence of non-nilpotent zero-divisors severely restricts the topology, forcing the sequentially Cohen–Macaulay property to occur precisely when
n=p^{a}
or
n=2p
. These results provide a unified framework for studying the homological invariants of a broad class of graph ideals, revealing that their algebraic behavior is governed by a common
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree structure.

2.
Glued Complexes and
(d_{1},\dots,d_{q})
-Trees

The results developed in this paper are based on the combinatorial structure of glued clique complexes and their connection with sequentially Cohen–Macaulay co-chordal graphs. Throughout, let
G
be a finite simple graph on
N
vertices, and let
\Delta(G)
and
\delta(G)
denote its maximum and minimum vertex degrees, respectively.

2.1.
Glued Complexes and Betti Numbers

A simplicial complex is a
glued complex
if it is obtained by successively attaching facets along common faces
[
23
]
. Let

\Delta_{r}(n_{1},\dots,n_{e})

be a simplicial complex with facets
S_{1},\dots,S_{e}
, where
|S_{m}|=n_{m}
and the intersection

(S_{1}\cup\dots\cup S_{m})\cap S_{m+1}

is an
(r_{m}-1)
-simplex for each
m=1,\dots,e-1
. The corresponding facet sizes and sequential intersection sizes are recorded by the multisets

\mathcal{N}=\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{e}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{R}=\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{e-1}\}.

When
\Delta_{r}
is the clique complex of the chordal complement
\overline{G}
of a co-chordal graph
G
, the edge ideal
I(G)
admits a
2
-linear resolution. The graded Betti numbers of the linear strand for
i\geq 1
are given by
[
23
]

(2.1)

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{m=1}^{e-1}\binom{N-r_{m}}{i+1}-\sum_{m=1}^{e}\binom{N-n_{m}}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1,

where
N=|V(G)|
. Since
\binom{N-r_{m}}{i+1}\neq 0
precisely when
i\leq N-r_{m}-1
, the largest homological degree appearing in (
2.1
) is determined by the smallest intersection size. Hence, it follows that

\operatorname{pdim}(S/I(G))=N-\min_{1\leq m\leq e-1}\{r_{m}\}-1.

2.2.
Stable Connectivity and
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-Trees

Transitioning from arbitrary glued complexes to sequentially Cohen–Macaulay structures requires strict topological connectivity conditions.

Definition 2.1
.

A pure simplicial complex
\Delta
is
strongly connected
if, for any two facets
F
and
F^{\prime}
, there exists a sequence of facets

F=F_{0},F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}=F^{\prime}

such that
F_{j}\cap F_{j+1}
is a face of codimension 1 (ridge) for every
j
.

Definition 2.2

(
[
22
]
)
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is
stably connected
if every pure skeleton
\Delta^{[i]}
,
-1\leq i\leq\dim\Delta
, is strongly connected.

Stable connectivity is a fundamental topological requirement for the sequentially Cohen-Macaulay property
[
22
]
. The graph theoretic counterpart of stable connectivity is the class of
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-trees.

Definition 2.3

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
d_{1}\geq d_{2}\geq\cdots\geq d_{q}>0
. A graph
H
is a
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree if it is constructed inductively by
H_{1}=K_{d_{1}}
, and

H_{i}=H_{i-1}\cup_{K_{d_{i}-1}}K_{d_{i}},\qquad 2\leq i\leq q.

The final graph
H_{q}
is denoted by
H
.

The following characterization will be used throughout the paper.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
G
be a co-chordal graph. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

The independence complex
\Delta_{G}
is vertex decomposable.

(2)

The independence complex
\Delta_{G}
is shellable.

(3)

S/I(G)
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay.

(4)

The independence complex
\Delta_{G}
is stably connected.

(5)

The complement
\overline{G}
is a
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree.

(6)

The sequential intersections satisfy
r_{m-1}=n_{m}-1
for all
2\leq m\leq e
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (1), (2), (3), (4), and (5) follows from Corollary 5.6 of
[
22
]
and Theorem 3.2 of
[
1
]
. Specifically, a chordal clique complex
\Delta_{G}
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is stably connected, which is equivalent to its
1
-skeleton being a
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree.

To prove
(5)\Longleftrightarrow(6)
, suppose that
\overline{G}
is a
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree. By Definition
2.3
, each clique
K_{d_{m}}
is attached to the previously constructed graph along a clique
K_{d_{m}-1}
. Writing
n_{m}=d_{m}
and denoting the corresponding intersection size by
r_{m-1}
, we obtain

r_{m-1}=d_{m}-1=n_{m}-1,\qquad 2\leq m\leq e.

Conversely, assume that
r_{m-1}=n_{m}-1
for all
2\leq m\leq e
. Then every maximal clique of size
n_{m}
is attached along a codimension 1 face. Ordering the clique sizes as
d_{1}\geq d_{2}\geq\cdots\geq d_{e}
, the resulting construction coincides with the recursive definition of a
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{e})
-tree. Hence
\overline{G}
is a
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{e})
-tree.
∎

3.
Algebraic Simplification of the Betti Formula

The characterization of sequentially Cohen–Macaulay co-chordal graphs by
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-trees allows the general Betti formula for glued complexes to be simplified considerably. The resulting expression depends only on the maximal clique sizes of the chordal complement and provides a homological characterization of the sequentially Cohen–Macaulay property.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a co-chordal graph on
N
vertices, and let
n_{1},\ldots,n_{e}
denote the sizes of the maximal cliques of
\overline{G}
in a gluing order. Then
S/I(G)
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{m=2}^{e}\binom{N-n_{m}}{i}-\binom{N-n_{1}}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Proof.

Since
G
is co-chordal, Equation (
2.1
) yields

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{m=1}^{e-1}\binom{N-r_{m}}{i+1}-\sum_{m=1}^{e}\binom{N-n_{m}}{i+1}.

By Theorem
2.4
, the sequentially Cohen–Macaulay assumption implies

r_{m-1}=n_{m}-1\qquad(2\leq m\leq e).

Substituting these relations into the general Betti formula gives

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{m=2}^{e}\binom{N-n_{m}+1}{i+1}-\sum_{m=1}^{e}\binom{N-n_{m}}{i+1}.

Rearranging terms yields

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{m=2}^{e}\left[\binom{N-n_{m}+1}{i+1}-\binom{N-n_{m}}{i+1}\right]-\binom{N-n_{1}}{i+1}.

Applying Pascal’s identity

\binom{a+1}{b}-\binom{a}{b}=\binom{a}{b-1},

we obtain

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{m=2}^{e}\binom{N-n_{m}}{i}-\binom{N-n_{1}}{i+1},

which is the desired formula.

(\Longleftarrow)
Conversely, suppose that the Betti numbers evaluate to the collapsed summation as stated above. Using Pascal’s identity in reverse,

\binom{N-n_{m}}{i}=\binom{N-n_{m}+1}{i+1}-\binom{N-n_{m}}{i+1},

we obtain

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{m=2}^{e}\binom{N-n_{m}+1}{i+1}-\sum_{m=1}^{e}\binom{N-n_{m}}{i+1}.

Comparing this expression with the general formula (
2.1
), it follows that

\{N-r_{m}\mid 1\leq m\leq e-1\}=\{N-n_{m}+1\mid 2\leq m\leq e\}.

Hence
r_{m-1}=n_{m}-1
for all
2\leq m\leq e
. By Theorem
2.4
,
\overline{G}
is a
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree, and therefore
S/I(G)
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Theorem
3.1
reduces the general alternating Betti formula to an expression depending only on the maximal clique sizes of the chordal complement. In particular, the projective dimension is determined by the smallest maximal clique. The following result recovers
[
1
, Theorem 5.4]
and is also contained in
[
22
, Corollary 5.11]
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
G
be a co-chordal graph. If
S/I(G)
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay, then

\operatorname{pdim}(S/I(G))=\Delta(G).

Proof.

Let
n_{\min}=\min\{n_{m}:1\leq m\leq e\}
. Since
G
is co-chordal and
S/I(G)
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay, Theorem

3.1
gives

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{m=2}^{e}\binom{N-n_{m}}{i}-\binom{N-n_{1}}{i+1}.

The projective dimension is the largest homological index for which a Betti
number is nonzero. Since

\binom{N-n_{m}}{i}\neq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i\leq N-n_{m},

the largest index occurring among the positive summands is

\max_{2\leq m\leq e}(N-n_{m})=N-n_{\min}.

Hence
\beta_{N-n_{\min}}(S/I(G))\neq 0
, while
\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=0
for all
i>N-n_{\min}
. Therefore

\operatorname{pdim}(S/I(G))=N-n_{\min}.

Since
\overline{G}
is a
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree by Theorem
2.4
, a simplicial vertex in a smallest maximal clique has degree
n_{\min}-1
in
\overline{G}
. Thus

\delta(\overline{G})=n_{\min}-1.

Using
\deg_{G}(v)+\deg_{\overline{G}}(v)=N-1
, we obtain

\Delta(G)=N-1-\delta(\overline{G})=N-n_{\min}.

Therefore,

\operatorname{pdim}(S/I(G))=N-\min\{n_{m}\}=\Delta(G).

∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
G
be a sequentially Cohen–Macaulay co-chordal graph on
N
vertices. Then

\operatorname{depth}(S/I(G))=n_{\min}.

Proof.

The Auslander–Buchsbaum formula gives

\operatorname{depth}(S/I(G))=N-\operatorname{pdim}(S/I(G)).

Applying Corollary
3.2
yields
\operatorname{depth}(S/I(G))=N-(N-n_{\min})=n_{\min}
.
∎

4.
Connectivity and Cohen–Macaulay Homological Rigidity

In the Cohen–Macaulay case, the topology of the independence complex completely determines the homological behavior of a co-chordal graph. As a specialization of Theorem
2.4
, we obtain the following characterization, which unifies and recovers several known results, including
[
1
, Corollary 3.4]
,
[
11
, Theorem 2]
,
[
12
, Theorem 1]
, and
[
22
, Corollary 5.5]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
be a co-chordal graph. The following are equivalent:

(1)

S/I(G)
is Cohen–Macaulay.

(2)

The independence complex
\Delta_{G}
is strongly connected.

(3)

The complement
\overline{G}
is a pure
d
-tree.

(4)

The sequential intersections satisfy
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{e-1}=n_{1}-1=\cdots=n_{e}-1
.

Proof.

Each of the conditions above implies that
\Delta_{G}
is pure. Therefore, the equivalences follow immediately from Theorem
2.4
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G
be a co-chordal graph whose complement has
e
maximal cliques. Then
S/I(G)
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=i\binom{e}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Proof.

(\Longrightarrow)
If
S/I(G)
is Cohen–Macaulay, then by Theorem
4.1
,
\overline{G}
is a pure
d
-tree. Hence all maximal cliques have equal size and each new clique introduces exactly one new vertex, so
N-n=e-1
. Substituting into Theorem
3.1
yields

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=(e-1)\binom{e-1}{i}-\binom{e-1}{i+1}=i\binom{e}{i+1}.

(\Longleftarrow)
Conversely, assume
\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=i\binom{e}{i+1}
. We can rewrite this value by using the algebraic identity

i\binom{e}{i+1}=(e-1)\binom{e}{i+1}-e\binom{e-1}{i+1}.

Comparing this identity directly with the general Betti formula (
2.1
) shows that all intersection and maximal cliques must be uniform. Because the sequence of binomial coefficients forms a linearly independent basis, their upper indices must match exactly. Equating the positive intersection terms forces
N-r_{m}=e
for all
1\leq m\leq e-1
. Similarly, equating the negative clique terms forces
N-n_{m}=e-1
for all
1\leq m\leq e
. This implies that
r_{m}=n_{m}-1
for all
m
. By Theorem
4.1
,
\overline{G}
is a pure
d
-tree, which proves that
S/I(G)
is Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

A
co-tree
is a graph whose complement is a tree. The next result shows that its homology depends only on its size.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
G
be a co-tree on
N\geq 3
vertices. Then
S/I(G)
is Cohen–Macaulay and

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=i\binom{N-1}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Proof.

A tree on
N
vertices has
N-1
edges, each corresponding to a maximal clique of size
2
. Hence
\overline{G}
forms a pure
(2,\dots,2)
-tree, so Theorem
4.2
applies with
e=N-1
, giving the stated formula.
∎

5.
Applications to Classical Graph Families

The algebraic framework from Sections
3
and
4
provides exact homological invariants for classical graph classes. Embedding these families into the structure of
(d_{1},\dots,d_{q})
-trees systematically replaces algorithmic bounds with exact combinatorial evaluations.

5.1.
Split Graphs

A graph
G
is a
split graph
if its vertex set admits a partition

V(G)=C\sqcup S,

where
C
induces a complete subgraph and
S
is an independent set.

The defining structure of a split graph immediately implies that both the graph and its complement are chordal. Thus, the classical characterization of Földes and Hammer
[
10
]
is recovered as a consequence of the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Every split graph is a sequentially Cohen–Macaulay co-chordal graph.

Proof.

Let
G
be a split graph with vertex partition

V(G)=C\sqcup S,

where
C
is a clique and
S
is an independent set. First, we show that
G
is co-chordal. Indeed, let

v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{t}v_{1}

be an induced cycle in
\overline{G}
with
t\geq 4
. The set
S
induces a clique in
\overline{G}
and
C
becomes an independent set. Since
C
is independent, at least two vertices of the cycle belong to
S
. As
S
is a clique in
\overline{G}
, these vertices are adjacent, producing a chord, a contradiction. Therefore,
\overline{G}
is chordal.

Next, for each vertex
x_{k}\in C
, define

n_{k}=|N_{G}(x_{k})\cap S|.

In
\overline{G}
, the vertex
x_{k}
is adjacent precisely to the vertices of
S\setminus N_{G}(x_{k})
. Consequently,

F_{k}=\{x_{k}\}\cup\bigl(S\setminus N_{G}(x_{k})\bigr)

is a maximal clique of
\overline{G}
with
|F_{k}|=m-n_{k}+1
and
m=|S|
. Since
C
is independent in
\overline{G}
, every maximal clique other than
S
contains exactly one vertex of
C
. Thus

F_{k}\cap S=S\setminus N_{G}(x_{k}),\quad\text{and}\quad|F_{k}\cap S|=m-n_{k}=|F_{k}|-1.

Therefore each maximal clique
F_{k}
is attached to the previously constructed complex along a face of codimension one. Starting from the clique
S
and successively adjoining the cliques
F_{k}
, the graph
\overline{G}
is obtained as a
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree. By Theorem
2.4
,
S/I(G)
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
G
be a split graph with vertex partition
V(G)=C\sqcup S
, where
|C|=n
. For each
x_{k}\in C
, set
n_{k}=|N_{G}(x_{k})\cap S|
. Then the graded Betti numbers are given explicitly by

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n+n_{k}-1}{i}-\binom{n}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.1
, the complement
\overline{G}
decomposes into a central clique of size
m
together with peripheral cliques of sizes
c_{k}=m-n_{k}+1
. Writing
N=n+m
, we substitute these data into Theorem
3.1
(where the central clique size
m
serves as the subtracted term), obtaining

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{N-c_{k}}{i}-\binom{N-m}{i+1}.

Now, evaluating the upper indices gives
N-m=(n+m)-m=n
, and
N-c_{k}=(n+m)-(m-n_{k}+1)=n+n_{k}-1
. Substituting these identities yields the claimed formula.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Theorem
5.2
strengthens existing results by replacing the combinatorial bounds of Singh and Verma
[
28
]
with exact formulas for the Betti numbers of any split graph. By applying the
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q})
-tree structure of the chordal complement, our approach yields a unified formula that determines the exact graded Betti numbers for all split graphs.

Corollary 5.4
.

For a split graph
G
, the projective dimension satisfies

\operatorname{pdim}(S/I(G))=n+\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\{n_{k}\}-1.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.2
, the projective dimension equals the maximum vertex degree
\Delta(G)
. Because the vertices in
C
are mutually connected, the degree of any
x_{k}\in C
is exactly
(n-1)+n_{k}
. Taking the maximum over all
k
yields

\Delta(G)=n+\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\{n_{k}\}-1.

∎

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
G
be a split graph with vertex partition
V(G)=C\sqcup S
, and let
|C|=n
and
n_{k}=|N_{G}(x_{k})\cap S|
for each
x_{k}\in C
.
Then
S/I(G)
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if either
n_{k}=0
for all
k
, or
n_{k}=1
for all
k
. In these cases, the graded Betti numbers are given by

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\begin{cases}i\binom{n}{i+1}&\text{if }n_{k}=0\text{ for all }k,\\[6.0pt]
i\binom{n+1}{i+1}&\text{if }n_{k}=1\text{ for all }k,\end{cases}

for all
i\geq 1
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.1
, the quotient
S/I(G)
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\overline{G}
is a pure
d
-tree. Let
V(G)=C\sqcup S
, where
C
is a clique and
S
is an independent set in
G
. Then
S

\overline{G}
. For each vertex
x_{k}\in C

F_{k}=\{x_{k}\}\cup\bigl(S\setminus N_{G}(x_{k})\bigr).

Since
x_{k}
is adjacent in
\overline{G}
precisely to the vertices of
S\setminus N_{G}(x_{k})
, each
F_{k}
is a maximal clique of
\overline{G}
with

|F_{k}|=|S|-n_{k}+1,

where
n_{k}=|N_{G}(x_{k})\cap S|
.

If
n_{k}\geq 1
for some
k

S
is not contained in any clique larger than itself and hence is a maximal clique of
\overline{G}
of cardinality
|S|
. Since
\overline{G}
is pure, every maximal clique must have the same cardinality, yielding

|S|-n_{k}+1=|S|

for all

for every

\overline{G}
are precisely

n

, so

On the other hand, if

|F_{k}|=|S|+1
. Purity forces every maximal clique of
\overline{G}
to have the same cardinality, and therefore
n_{k}=0
for all

. In this case
S
is properly contained in each
F_{k}
and is not maximal. Hence the maximal cliques of
\overline{G}
are exactly
F_{1},\ldots,F_{n}
, giving precisely
n

Therefore,
\overline{G}
is pure if and only if either
n_{k}=1
for all
k
or
n_{k}=0
for all
k
. The corresponding numbers of facets are
n+1
and
n
, respectively. Substituting these values into the Betti number formula of Theorem
4.2
yields

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=i\binom{n+1}{i+1}

when
n_{k}=1
for all
k
, and

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=i\binom{n}{i+1}

when
n_{k}=0
for all
k
, completing the proof.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

Villarreal
[

]
proved that attaching a whisker (a pendant edge) to every vertex of a graph yields a Cohen–Macaulay ring. When the base graph is a complete graph

5.2.
Complete, Nearly Complete, and Pineapple Graphs

The exact expression in Theorem
5.2
admits several important specializations corresponding to uniform or nearly uniform bipartite attachments between the clique and the independent set. Applying our unified formula, the following corollaries provide the exact homological values for the specific subclasses recently studied by Fröberg
[
13
]
.

Corollary 5.8

(Complete split graphs)
.

Let
G
be a complete split graph, i.e., every vertex of
C
is adjacent to every vertex of
S
. If
|C|=n
and
|S|=m
, then

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=n\binom{n+m-1}{i}-\binom{n}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Proof.

In a complete split graph, each vertex
x_{k}\in C
satisfies
n_{k}=m
for all
1\leq k\leq n
. Hence,

n+n_{k}-1=n+m-1\quad\text{(independent of }k\text{)}.

Therefore the summation in Theorem
5.2
becomes

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n+n_{k}-1}{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n+m-1}{i}=n\binom{n+m-1}{i},

yielding the stated formula.
∎

Corollary 5.9

(Nearly complete split graphs)
.

Let
G
be a split graph in which exactly one edge between
C
and
S
is missing. Then

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=(n-1)\binom{n+m-1}{i}+\binom{n+m-2}{i}-\binom{n}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Proof.

Exactly one vertex, say
x_{1}\in C
, satisfies
n_{1}=m-1
, while
n_{k}=m
for all
k\geq 2
. Hence the summation splits as

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n+n_{k}-1}{i}=\binom{n+m-2}{i}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom{n+m-1}{i}.

The second term contributes
(n-1)\binom{n+m-1}{i}
, and substituting into Theorem
5.2
gives the result.
∎

Corollary 5.10

(Pineapple graphs)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{a}^{b}
be a pineapple graph consisting of a clique
K_{a}
and a stable set of size
b
, where all vertices of the stable set are attached to a single fixed vertex in the clique. Then

\beta_{i}(S/I(\mathcal{P}_{a}^{b}))=\binom{a+b-1}{i}+(a-1)\binom{a-1}{i}-\binom{a}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Proof.

Let
C
denote the clique with
|C|=a
. Exactly one vertex
x_{1}\in C
satisfies
n_{1}=b
, while all remaining vertices satisfy
n_{k}=0
for
k\geq 2
. Hence

\sum_{k=1}^{a}\binom{a+n_{k}-1}{i}=\binom{a+b-1}{i}+\sum_{k=2}^{a}\binom{a-1}{i}=\binom{a+b-1}{i}+(a-1)\binom{a-1}{i}.

Substituting into Theorem
5.2
yields the stated expression.
∎

Remark 5.11
.

Corollary
5.5
determines the Cohen–Macaulay members of the preceding subclasses. A complete split graph, for which
n_{k}=m
for all
k
, is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
m\in\{0,1\}
, and hence reduces to a pure complete graph in both cases.

Nearly complete split graphs and pineapple graphs fail the required uniform attachment condition due to non-constant attachment degrees. Consequently, a nearly complete split graph with
n_{1}=m-1
and
n_{k}=m
for
k\geq 2
is Cohen–Macaulay only in the exceptional cases
n=1
and
m\in\{1,2\}
. Likewise, a pineapple graph
\mathcal{P}_{a}^{b}
with
n_{1}=b
and
n_{k}=0
for
k\geq 2
is Cohen–Macaulay precisely when
b=0
or
(a,b)=(1,1)
.

5.3.
Threshold Graphs

Threshold graphs form a distinguished subclass of split graphs. They may be constructed recursively from a single vertex by repeatedly adding either an isolated vertex or a dominating vertex
[
21
]
. Equivalently, a split graph is threshold if and only if the neighborhoods of the clique vertices within the independent set are linearly ordered by inclusion.

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
G
be a threshold graph with split partition
V(G)=C\sqcup S
. Let
C=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be ordered so that
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq\cdots\geq n_{n}
, where
n_{k}=|N_{G}(x_{k})\cap S|
. Then

\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n+n_{k}-1}{i}-\binom{n}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1,

and

\operatorname{pdim}(S/I(G))=n+n_{1}-1.

Proof.

Since every threshold graph is a split graph, the Betti formula follows directly from Theorem
5.2
. Moreover, Corollary
5.4
yields

\operatorname{pdim}(S/I(G))=n+\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\{n_{k}\}-1.

Because the neighborhoods of the vertices in
C
are nested, the chosen ordering implies

n_{1}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\{n_{k}\},

and the stated formula follows.
∎

5.4.
Prime Ideal Graphs

We next apply the split graph structure to prime ideal graphs of finite commutative rings. Let
R
be a finite commutative ring and let
P
be a proper prime ideal of
R
. The
prime ideal graph

\Gamma_{P}(R)
is the graph whose vertex set is
R\setminus\{0\}
, where two distinct vertices
x
and
y
are adjacent whenever
xy\in P

[
27
,
24
]
.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
R
be a finite commutative ring and let
P
be a proper prime ideal of
R
. Then

\Gamma_{P}(R)\cong K_{|P|-1}\vee\overline{K}_{|R|-|P|}.

In particular,
\Gamma_{P}(R)
is a complete split graph.

Proof.

Let

C=P\setminus\{0\},\qquad S=R\setminus P.

Since
P
is an ideal,

x,y\in C\implies xy\in P,

and therefore
C
induces a clique of cardinality
|P|-1
.

Moreover, for every
x\in C
and
y\in S
,

xy\in P,

because
P
is closed under multiplication by arbitrary ring elements. Hence every vertex of
C
is adjacent to every vertex of
S
.

Finally, if
x,y\in S
, then
x,y\notin P
. Since
P
is prime,

xy\in P\implies x\in P\ \text{or}\ y\in P,

which is impossible. Thus
xy\notin P
, and no two vertices of
S
are adjacent. Consequently,
S
is an independent set of cardinality
|R|-|P|
.

Therefore,

\Gamma_{P}(R)\cong K_{|P|-1}\vee\overline{K}_{|R|-|P|},

and hence
\Gamma_{P}(R)
is a complete split graph.
∎

The homological invariants now follow immediately from Corollaries
5.4
and
5.8
.

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
R
be a finite commutative ring and let
P
be a proper prime ideal of
R
. Then
S/I(\Gamma_{P}(R))
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay and

\beta_{i}(S/I(\Gamma_{P}(R)))=(|P|-1)\binom{|R|-2}{i}-\binom{|P|-1}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Moreover,

\operatorname{pdim}(S/I(\Gamma_{P}(R)))=|R|-2.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.13
,

|C|=|P|-1,\qquad|S|=|R|-|P|.

Since every vertex of
C
is adjacent to every vertex of
S
,

n_{k}=|S|=|R|-|P|\qquad(1\leq k\leq|P|-1).

Applying Corollary
5.8
yields

\beta_{i}(S/I(\Gamma_{P}(R)))=(|P|-1)\binom{(|P|-1)+(|R|-|P|)-1}{i}-\binom{|P|-1}{i+1},

which simplifies to

\beta_{i}(S/I(\Gamma_{P}(R)))=(|P|-1)\binom{|R|-2}{i}-\binom{|P|-1}{i+1}.

Similarly, Corollary
5.4
gives

\operatorname{pdim}(S/I(\Gamma_{P}(R)))=(|P|-1)+(|R|-|P|)-1=|R|-2.

∎

Corollary 5.15
.

Let
R
be a finite commutative ring and
P
a prime ideal. Then
\Gamma_{P}(R)
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
R
is a finite field.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\Gamma_{P}(R)
is Cohen–Macaulay. If
P\neq(0)
, then Proposition
5.13
yields

\Gamma_{P}(R)\cong K_{|P|-1}\vee\overline{K}_{|R|-|P|}.

By Corollary
5.5
, a Cohen–Macaulay split graph satisfies either
n_{k}=0
for all
k
or
n_{k}=1
for all
k
. Since
\Gamma_{P}(R)
is a complete split graph, every vertex of the clique is adjacent to every vertex of the independent set, and therefore

n_{k}=|S|=|R|-|P|\quad\text{for all }k.

Since
P
is a proper ideal,
|R|>|P|
. Thus, the condition
|R|-|P|=0
is impossible, meaning the case
n_{k}=0
cannot occur. Consequently,
n_{k}=1
for all
k
, which implies

|R|-|P|=1.

Using
|R|=|P||R/P|
, we obtain

|P|(|R/P|-1)=1,

which has no solution with
|P|\geq 2
. Hence
P=(0)
. Therefore
(0)
is a prime ideal, so
R
is an integral domain. Since
R
is finite, it follows that
R
is a finite field.

Conversely, let
R=\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then
P=(0)
is the unique prime ideal. Because every nonzero element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a unit, there are no nonzero zero-divisors. Consequently,

\Gamma_{(0)}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong\overline{K}_{q-1}.

Its edge ideal is zero, and hence

S/I(\Gamma_{(0)}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\cong S,

which is Cohen–Macaulay. Therefore
\Gamma_{P}(R)
is Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Remark 5.16
.

While Corollary
5.15
shows that
\Gamma_{P}(R)
is Cohen–Macaulay whenever
R
is a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the resulting graph is the edgeless graph
\overline{K}_{q-1}
. In particular, it is disconnected for all
q>2
. If one further imposes connectivity, then necessarily
q-1=1
, i.e.,
q=2
. Consequently, a prime ideal graph
\Gamma_{P}(R)
is both Cohen–Macaulay and connected if and only if
R\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

6.
Homological Invariants of Nilpotent and Zero-Divisor Graphs

Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity. The zero-divisor graph
\Gamma(R)
, introduced in its modern form by Anderson and Livingston
[
2
]
following Beck’s foundational coloring work
[
5
]
, is the graph whose vertices are the nonzero zero-divisors of
R
, where distinct vertices
x
and
y
are adjacent if
xy=0
. Similarly, we define the nilpotent graph
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
as the graph whose vertices are the non-zero nilpotent elements of
R
, with adjacency defined by
xy=0
. These graphs encode the algebraic annihilation properties of the ring into a combinatorial structure, and their associated edge ideals have been widely studied, particularly in relation to co-chordal and linear resolution properties
[
8
,
25
,
26
]
. The structural results developed in the preceding sections allow these invariants to be computed exactly in cases where a split or threshold decomposition exists.

6.1.
Nilpotent Graphs of Artinian Rings

We first establish a universal topological characterization and homological formula for the nilpotent graph of any finite direct product of Artinian chain rings.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
R\cong\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{i}
be a finite direct product of commutative Artinian chain rings, where each local component
R_{i}
has maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
, nilpotency index
a_{i}
, and residue field cardinality
q_{i}=|R_{i}/\mathfrak{m}_{i}|
.
Then
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
is a threshold graph if and only if at most one local component of
R
possesses a nilpotency index
a_{i}\geq 3
.

Moreover, suppose that exactly one factor, say
R_{1}
, satisfies
a_{1}\geq 3

and that
a_{i}\leq 2
for all
i\geq 2
. Define
M=\prod_{i=2}^{k}q_{i}^{a_{i}-1}
. Then the exact graded Betti numbers are given by

(6.1)

\beta_{i}(S/I(\Gamma_{Nil}(R)))=\sum_{t=\lceil a_{1}/2\rceil}^{a_{1}-1}|W_{t}|\binom{|C|+n_{t}-1}{i}-\binom{|C|}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1,

where

\displaystyle|W_{t}|

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}M\left(q_{1}^{a_{1}-t}-q_{1}^{a_{1}-t-1}\right)&\text{if }t<a_{1}-1,\\
Mq_{1}-1&\text{if }t=a_{1}-1,\end{cases}

\displaystyle|C|

\displaystyle=Mq_{1}^{\lfloor a_{1}/2\rfloor}-1,

\displaystyle n_{t}

\displaystyle=M\left(q_{1}^{t}-q_{1}^{\lfloor a_{1}/2\rfloor}\right).

Proof.

Since each local component
R_{i}
is an Artinian chain ring, its nilpotent elements coincide with its maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{i}

[
3
]
. Hence, the Jacobson radical is given by

Nil(R)=\mathfrak{m}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{m}_{k},

and the vertex set of the nilpotent graph is precisely

V(\Gamma_{Nil}(R))=(\mathfrak{m}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{m}_{k})\setminus\{0\}.

We prove the equivalence by analyzing both directions.

(\Longrightarrow)
Assume that
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
is a threshold graph. Suppose for contradiction that at least two local components have a nilpotency index of at least
3
. Without loss of generality, let
a_{1}\geq 3
and
a_{2}\geq 3
. Define
u_{1}=\pi_{1}^{a_{1}-1}
and
v_{1}=\pi_{1}
in
R_{1}
, and similarly
u_{2}=\pi_{2}^{a_{2}-1}
and
v_{2}=\pi_{2}
in
R_{2}
. These definitions ensure that for
i\in\{1,2\}
, we have
u_{i}^{2}=0
and
u_{i}v_{i}=0
, while
v_{i}^{2}\neq 0
.

Consider the following four vertices in
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=(v_{1},u_{2},0,\dots,0),

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=(u_{1},v_{2},0,\dots,0),

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=(v_{1},0,0,\dots,0),

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=(u_{1},v_{2}+u_{2},0,\dots,0).

A direct calculation of their pairwise products shows that
A\cdot B=0
,
B\cdot C=0
,
C\cdot D=0
, and
D\cdot A=0
. Thus, these four pairs constitute edges in
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
. On the other hand,
A\cdot C=(v_{1}^{2},0,\dots,0)\neq 0
because
v_{1}^{2}\neq 0
, and similarly
B\cdot D=(0,v_{2}^{2},0,\dots,0)\neq 0
because
v_{2}^{2}\neq 0
. Since neither
AC
nor
BD
is an edge, the induced subgraph on the set
\{A,B,C,D\}
is a chordless cycle of length four (
C_{4}
) in
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
. This directly contradicts the assumption that
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
is a threshold graph, as threshold graphs strictly forbid induced cycles of length four. Therefore, at most one local component can satisfy
a_{i}\geq 3
.

(\Longleftarrow)
Conversely, assume that at most one local component has a nilpotency index of at least
3
. We consider two subcases. First, suppose that
a_{i}\leq 2
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. Then
\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{2}=(0)
in every component. Let

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),\quad y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})

be distinct vertices in
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
. Since
x_{i},y_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{2}=(0)
, it follows that

x_{i}y_{i}=0\quad\text{ for all }i.

Consequently,
xy=(0,\dots,0)
, meaning every pair of distinct vertices is adjacent. Therefore,
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
is a complete graph. Because complete graphs are trivially threshold graphs, they are co-chordal and their edge ideals are sequentially Cohen–Macaulay.

Second, suppose that exactly one component has a nilpotency index of at least
3
. Without loss of generality, let
a_{1}\geq 3
, while
a_{i}\leq 2
for all
i\geq 2
. For any two vertices
x,y\in\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
, we have
x_{i}y_{i}=0
for all
i\geq 2
because
\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{2}=(0)
. Hence,
xy=0
in
R
if and only if
x_{1}y_{1}=0
in
R_{1}
. Thus, adjacency in
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
is completely determined by the first coordinate. Since
R_{1}
is a chain ring, every nonzero element of
\mathfrak{m}_{1}
has a unique valuation

v_{1}(x_{1})=t,\quad 1\leq t\leq a_{1}-1,

and

x_{1}y_{1}=0\iff v_{1}(x_{1})+v_{1}(y_{1})\geq a_{1}.

Therefore, vertices with larger valuations annihilate all vertices having sufficiently large valuations, forcing the neighborhoods to be linearly ordered by inclusion:

N(t_{1})\subseteq N(t_{2})\quad\text{ whenever }t_{1}<t_{2}.

This nested neighborhood structure implies
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
is a threshold graph, and thus it is co-chordal and sequentially Cohen–Macaulay.

To compute the exact graded Betti numbers, we determine the sizes of the threshold components. Define

M=\prod_{i=2}^{k}|\mathfrak{m}_{i}|=\prod_{i=2}^{k}q_{i}^{a_{i}-1}.

For each valuation level
1\leq t\leq a_{1}-1
, let
V_{t}=\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{t}\setminus\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{t+1}
. Since
R_{1}
is a finite chain ring,

|V_{t}|=q_{1}^{a_{1}-t}-q_{1}^{a_{1}-t-1}=q_{1}^{a_{1}-t-1}(q_{1}-1).

Each element of
V_{t}
may be combined with an arbitrary element of
\mathfrak{m}_{2}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{m}_{k}
, contributing the factor
M
. Thus, for
t<a_{1}-1
, the size of the corresponding valuation layer in
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
is

|W_{t}|=M|V_{t}|=M(q_{1}^{a_{1}-t}-q_{1}^{a_{1}-t-1}).

The vertices with a first coordinate equal to zero are exactly
\{0\}\times\mathfrak{m}_{2}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{m}_{k}\setminus\{0\}
, and their number is exactly
M-1
. Because these vertices annihilate every other vertex in the graph, they share the identical universal neighborhood of the highest valuation layer (
t=a_{1}-1
). Combining them with that layer yields

|W_{a_{1}-1}|=M(q_{1}-1)+(M-1)=Mq_{1}-1.

The central clique
C
of the threshold decomposition consists of all vertices satisfying
2t\geq a_{1}
, together with the
M-1
vertices having a first coordinate of zero. Evaluating the geometric sum yields

|C|=M\sum_{t=\lceil a_{1}/2\rceil}^{a_{1}-1}|V_{t}|+(M-1)=M\left(q_{1}^{\lfloor a_{1}/2\rfloor}-1\right)+(M-1)=Mq_{1}^{\lfloor a_{1}/2\rfloor}-1.

For a clique vertex of valuation
t
, adjacency to the independent set occurs precisely when
s+t\geq a_{1}
. Therefore, its bipartite attachment degree
n_{t}
is obtained by summing over the valid independent set layers

n_{t}=M\sum_{s=a_{1}-t}^{\lceil a_{1}/2\rceil-1}|V_{s}|=M\left(q_{1}^{t}-q_{1}^{\lfloor a_{1}/2\rfloor}\right).

Applying the threshold graph Betti formula derived in Theorem
5.2
to these component sizes yields exactly the expression in Equation (
6.1
), completing the proof.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
R\cong\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{i}
be a finite direct product of commutative Artinian chain rings, where
a_{i}
denotes the nilpotency index of the maximal ideal of
R_{i}
. Then
S/I(\Gamma_{Nil}(R))
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
a_{i}\leq 2
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. Equivalently,
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
is a complete graph on
|Nil(R)|-1=\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}^{a_{i}-1}-1
vertices.

Proof.

(\Longleftarrow)
Assume first that
a_{i}\leq 2
for all
i
. Then
\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{2}=(0)
in every component, so the product of any two nonzero nilpotent elements of
R
is zero. Since the vertex set consists of all nonzero nilpotent elements,
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
is identically the complete graph on
|Nil(R)|-1=\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}^{a_{i}-1}-1
vertices. Its complement is an edgeless graph, which is a pure chordal graph. Therefore,
S/I(\Gamma_{Nil}(R))
is Cohen–Macaulay.

(\Longrightarrow)
Conversely, suppose that
S/I(\Gamma_{Nil}(R))
is Cohen–Macaulay. Then it is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay, and Theorem
6.1
implies that at most one component may satisfy
a_{i}\geq 3
. Assume, for contradiction, that exactly one component, say
R_{1}
, has
a_{1}\geq 3
.

By the proof of Theorem
6.1
,
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
is a threshold graph. Vertices corresponding to elements of valuation
a_{1}-1
are adjacent to every other vertex in
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
and therefore become isolated vertices in the complement
\overline{\Gamma}_{Nil}(R)
. Thus,
\overline{\Gamma}_{Nil}(R)
contains maximal cliques of size
1
.

On the other hand, since
a_{1}\geq 3
, there exist nilpotent elements of valuation
1
. Any two such elements satisfy

v(x)+v(y)=2<a_{1},

and hence are not adjacent in
\Gamma_{Nil}(R)
. Therefore they are adjacent in
\overline{\Gamma}_{Nil}(R)
, producing a maximal clique of size at least
2
.

Consequently,
\overline{\Gamma}_{Nil}(R)
contains maximal cliques of different sizes and is not unmixed. Since Cohen–Macaulay chordal graphs must be unmixed, this is impossible. Therefore no component can satisfy
a_{i}\geq 3
, and hence
a_{i}\leq 2
for all
i
.
∎

We now derive the homological invariants for single local finite chain rings as a direct consequence of the above Theorem. Recall that a finite commutative ring
R
is a
chain ring
if its ideals form a totally ordered set under inclusion:

\mathfrak{m}\supset\mathfrak{m}^{2}\supset\cdots\supset\mathfrak{m}^{a-1}\supset\mathfrak{m}^{a}=(0),

where
\mathfrak{m}
is the unique maximal ideal and
a
is the nilpotency index.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
R
be a finite chain ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
, residue field cardinality
q=|R/\mathfrak{m}|
, and nilpotency index
a
. Then
\Gamma(R)
is a threshold graph and its exact graded Betti numbers are

\beta_{i}(S/I(\Gamma(R)))=\sum_{t=\lceil a/2\rceil}^{a-1}q^{a-t-1}(q-1)\binom{q^{t}-2}{i}-\binom{q^{\lfloor a/2\rfloor}-1}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Proof.

Since
R
is a finite chain ring, it is a local Artinian ring whose ideals form the chain

R\supset\mathfrak{m}\supset\mathfrak{m}^{2}\supset\cdots\supset\mathfrak{m}^{a-1}\supset\mathfrak{m}^{a}=(0).

In a local Artinian ring, every zero divisor is nilpotent. Hence
Z(R)=Nil(R)=\mathfrak{m}
, and therefore the zero divisor graph and nilpotent graph coincide:

\Gamma(R)=\Gamma_{Nil}(R).

Viewing
R
as a direct product consisting of a single local factor, we have
k=1
. Consequently, Theorem
6.1
applies directly and implies that
\Gamma(R)
is a threshold graph.

We now evaluate the Betti numbers. If
a=1
, then
R
is a field and
\Gamma(R)
is the empty graph. Hence
I(\Gamma(R))=(0)
, and all graded Betti numbers vanish for
i\geq 1
. The stated formula is consistent with this case, since the summation range is empty and the binomial term reduces to
\binom{q^{0}-1}{i+1}=0
.

For
a\geq 2
, the graded Betti numbers are derived from the formula in Theorem
6.1
with the multiplicative factor

M=\prod_{i=2}^{k}q_{i}^{a_{i}-1}=1.

Substituting
M=1
into the expressions of Theorem
6.1
, we obtain

|C|=q^{\lfloor a/2\rfloor}-1,\quad n_{t}=q^{t}-q^{\lfloor a/2\rfloor}.

It remains to simplify the coefficients
|W_{t}|
. For
1\leq t<a-1
, Theorem
6.1
gives

|W_{t}|=q^{a-t}-q^{a-t-1}=q^{a-t-1}(q-1),

and this expression also holds for
t=a-1
, since it gives
q-1
. Moreover,

|C|+n_{t}-1=\bigl(q^{\lfloor a/2\rfloor}-1\bigr)+\bigl(q^{t}-q^{\lfloor a/2\rfloor}\bigr)-1=q^{t}-2.

Substituting the simplified expressions for
|W_{t}|
,
|C|
, and
|C|+n_{t}-1
into the Betti formula of Theorem
6.1
, we obtain

\beta_{i}\!\left(S/I(\Gamma(R))\right)=\sum_{t=\lceil a/2\rceil}^{a-1}q^{a-t-1}(q-1)\binom{q^{t}-2}{i}-\binom{q^{\lfloor a/2\rfloor}-1}{i+1},

for all
i\geq 1
, as claimed.
∎

Example 6.4
.

We apply the explicit formula of Theorem
6.3
to two classical families of finite chain rings. By extracting the residue field cardinality
q
and the nilpotency index
a
, we obtain exact homological invariants without any algorithmic computation.

(1)

Truncated polynomial rings.

For
R=\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/\langle x^{c}\rangle
, where
p
is prime and
c\geq 2
, the unique maximal ideal is
\mathfrak{m}=\langle x\rangle
, and the ideals form the chain
\langle x\rangle\supset\langle x^{2}\rangle\supset\cdots\supset\langle x^{c-1}\rangle\supset(0)
. Hence,
R
is a finite chain ring with nilpotency index
a=c
. The residue field is
R/\mathfrak{m}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, which gives
q=p
. Therefore,

\beta_{i}(S/I(\Gamma(R)))=\sum_{t=\lceil c/2\rceil}^{c-1}p^{c-t-1}(p-1)\binom{p^{t}-2}{i}-\binom{p^{\lfloor c/2\rfloor}-1}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

(2)

Gaussian quotient rings.

For
R=\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i]\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[x]/\langle x^{2}+1\rangle
, where
m\geq 1
, the relation
(1+i)^{2}=2i
implies that the ideal
\langle 1+i\rangle^{2}
is exactly
\langle 2\rangle
. Since
R
is of characteristic
2^{m}
, it follows that
\langle 1+i\rangle^{2m}=(0)
. Thus,
R
is a finite chain ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=\langle 1+i\rangle
and nilpotency index
a=2m
. The residue field is
q=2
.
Substituting
q=2
and
a=2m
, and using
\lfloor 2m/2\rfloor=\lceil 2m/2\rceil=m
, we obtain

\beta_{i}(S/I(\Gamma(R)))=\sum_{t=m}^{2m-1}2^{2m-t-1}\binom{2^{t}-2}{i}-\binom{2^{m}-1}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
R
be a finite chain ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
and nilpotency index
a
. Then
S/I(\Gamma(R))
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
a\leq 2
. Equivalently,
\Gamma(R)
is a complete graph on
|\mathfrak{m}|-1=q^{a-1}-1
vertices.

Proof.

Since
R
is a finite chain ring, it is a local Artinian ring. Consequently, every zero-divisor is nilpotent, and hence

\Gamma(R)=\Gamma_{Nil}(R).

Applying Corollary
6.2
with
k=1
shows that
S/I(\Gamma(R))
is Cohen–Macaulay precisely when
a\leq 2
.

If
a=1
, then
R
is a field and
\Gamma(R)
is the empty graph. If
a=2
, then
\mathfrak{m}^{2}=(0)
, so every pair of nonzero zero-divisors annihilates each other, and consequently
\Gamma(R)
is a complete graph on
|\mathfrak{m}|-1=q^{a-1}-1
vertices.

Conversely, if
a\geq 3
, Corollary
6.2
implies that
S/I(\Gamma(R))
is not Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

6.2.
Zero-Divisor Graphs of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}

While Theorem
6.1
completely classifies the nilpotent graphs of Artinian direct products, the full zero-divisor graph
\Gamma(R)
of a non-local ring contains additional vertices (non-nilpotent zero-divisors) that significantly alter its topology. We now characterize the full zero-divisor graph for the foundational ring
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, establishing exactly when its edge ideal is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Then
S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}))
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
n\in\{p^{a},2p\}
, where
p
is a prime number. Moreover, in these cases
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is a threshold graph, and the exact graded Betti numbers are given as follows:

(1)

If
n=p^{2}
, then
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}})\cong K_{p-1}
, and

\beta_{i}(S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}})))=i\binom{p-1}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

(2)

If
n=2p
, where
p
is an odd prime, then
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2p})\cong K_{1,p-1}
, and

\beta_{i}(S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2p})))=\binom{p-1}{i},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

(3)

If
n=p^{a}
with
a\geq 3
, then
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a}})
is a threshold graph and

\beta_{i}(S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a}})))=\sum_{t=\lceil a/2\rceil}^{a-1}p^{a-t-1}(p-1)\binom{p^{t}-2}{i}-\binom{p^{\lfloor a/2\rfloor}-1}{i+1},\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

Proof.

Assume that
n
is not of the form
p^{a}
or
2p
. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, we have a decomposition

\mathbb{Z}_{n}\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}^{a_{i}}},\qquad t\geq 2,

so every element can be written as
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})
with
x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}^{a_{i}}}
. For each component, let
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
denote the unique maximal ideal of
\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}^{a_{i}}}
. Define the support of
x
by

\operatorname{supp}(x)=\{\,i\mid x_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}_{i}\,\}.

Then
x
is a zero divisor if and only if
\operatorname{supp}(x)\neq\varnothing
. More precisely, for any nonempty proper subset
J\subsetneq\{1,\dots,t\}
, this yields a family of zero-divisors supported on
J
, and these families are mutually disjoint across different subsets
J
. Moreover, adjacency in
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is coordinatewise:

xy=0\iff x_{i}y_{i}=0\text{ in }\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}^{a_{i}}}\ \text{for all }i.

Hence annihilation occurs independently in each component.

Since
t\geq 2
, the graph admits zero divisors supported on different coordinate subsets. In particular, maximal cliques in the complement graph
\overline{\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})}
arise from distinct annihilation patterns across components. Because
n\notin\{p^{a},2p\}
, the canonical prime factorization
n=\prod_{i=1}^{t}p_{i}^{a_{i}}
guarantees that
n
possesses at least two distinct prime factors (
t\geq 2
). In
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, an element is a zero-divisor if and only if it is a multiple of at least one prime factor
p_{i}
. For any prime factor
p_{i}
, the number of nonzero multiples of
p_{i}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is
\frac{n}{p_{i}}-1
. Because
n\notin\{p^{a},2p\}
, it follows that
n>2p_{i}
is true for every prime factor. Consequently,
\frac{n}{p_{i}}-1\geq 2
, meaning every prime direction independently contributes at least two distinct nonzero zero-divisors to the graph.

Let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
be maximal cliques in
\overline{\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})}
arising from different prime annihilations. Because each direction contributes at least two independent nonzero elements, separating these configurations requires removing at least two vertices. Consequently,

|S_{1}\cap S_{2}|\leq\min(|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|)-2.

This contradicts the codimension one gluing condition required for stably connected
(d_{1},\dots,d_{q})
-tree decompositions, namely
r_{m-1}=n_{m}-1
. Consequently,
\overline{\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})}
is not a
(d_{1},\dots,d_{q})
-tree, and hence
S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}))
is not sequentially Cohen–Macaulay. Therefore, sequential Cohen–Macaulayness is possible only when
n=p^{a}
or
n=2p
.

(
\Longleftarrow
)
Case
n=2p
,
p
odd:

The nonzero zero divisors of
\mathbb{Z}_{2p}
are
\{p,2,4,\dots,2(p-1)\}
. Since

p\cdot(2k)\equiv 0\pmod{2p},\qquad(2i)(2j)\not\equiv 0,

the vertex
p
is adjacent to all even elements, while no two even elements annihilate each other. Thus,

\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2p})\cong K_{1,p-1},

a star graph. Star graphs are threshold graphs, as its central vertex forms a clique of size one and the independent vertices all share the exact same linearly ordered neighborhood. Applying Theorem
5.2
gives the stated Betti formula.

Case
n=p^{a}
:

Here
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a}}
is a finite chain ring. Its zero-divisor graph coincides with its nilpotent graph, which is a threshold graph by Theorem
6.1
. Substituting
q=p
into Theorem
6.3
yields the stated Betti formula.
∎

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Then
S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}))
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
n=p
or
n=p^{2}
for some prime
p
.

Proof.

6.6
, the graph
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
n=p^{a}
or
n=2p
.

n=p^{a}
. Since
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a}}
is a finite chain ring with nilpotency index
a
, Corollary
6.5

S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a}}))
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if

n=p

n=p^{2}
.

Now let
n=2p
with
p

6.6
,
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2p})
is the star graph
K_{1,p-1}
. Its complement is

\overline{\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2p})}\cong K_{p-1}\sqcup K_{1}.

The maximal cliques therefore have sizes
p-1
and
1
. Since
p>2

\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2p})
cannot be Cohen–Macaulay. We conclude that the only integers
n
for which
S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}))
is Cohen–Macaulay are
n=p
and
n=p^{2}
.
∎

7.
Concluding Remarks

This paper establishes explicit homological invariants for sequentially Cohen–Macaulay co-chordal graphs, with a particular focus on zero-divisor graphs of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. The results reveal a natural interplay between combinatorial commutative algebra and classical arithmetic functions.

As shown in Theorem
6.6
, for
n=p^{a}
the coefficient
p^{a-t-1}(p-1)
arising in the Betti number formulas coincides with the cardinality of the valuation layer
V_{t}=\mathfrak{m}^{t}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^{t+1}
, giving

|V_{t}|=p^{a-t}-p^{a-t-1}=\phi(p^{a-t}),

where
\phi
is Euler’s totient function. Hence, the graded Betti numbers of
S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a}}))
admit the arithmetic expression

\beta_{i}\left(S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a}}))\right)=\sum_{t=\lceil a/2\rceil}^{a-1}\phi(p^{a-t})\binom{p^{t}-2}{i}-\binom{p^{\lfloor a/2\rfloor}-1}{i+1},\quad i\geq 1.

This shows that the homological invariants of zero-divisor graphs over
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a}}
are determined by Euler’s totient function, connecting the algebraic topology of graph ideals with classical number theory.
