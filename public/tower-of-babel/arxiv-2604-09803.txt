MAGE: Modality-Agnostic Music Generation and Editing
Muhammad Usama Saleem, Ravi Tejasvi, Tianyu Xu, Rajeev Nongpiur, Ishan Chatterjee, Mayur Jagdishbhai Patel, Pu Wang

Abstract
Multimodal music creation requires models that can both generate audio from high-level cues and edit existing mixtures in a targeted manner. Yet most multimodal music systems are built for a single task and a fixed prompting interface, making their conditioning brittle when guidance is ambiguous, temporally misaligned, or partially missing. Common additive fusion or feature concatenation further weakens cross-modal grounding, often causing prompt drift and spurious musical content during generation and editing. We propose MAGE , a modality-agnostic framework that unifies multimodal music generation and mixture-grounded editing within a single continuous latent formulation. At its core, MAGE uses a Controlled Multimodal FluxFormer , a flow-based Transformer that learns controllable latent trajectories for synthesis and editing under any available subset of conditions. To improve grounding, we introduce Audio–Visual Nexus Alignment to select temporally consistent visual evidence for the audio timeline, and a cross-gated modulation mechanism that applies multiplicative control from aligned visual and textual cues to the audio latents, suppressing unsupported components rather than injecting them. Finally, we train with a dynamic modality-masking curriculum that exposes the model to text-only, visual-only, joint multimodal, and mixture-guided settings, enabling robust inference under missing modalities without training separate models. Experiments on the MUSIC benchmark show that MAGE supports effective multimodal-guided music generation and targeted editing, achieving competitive quality while offering a lightweight and flexible interface tailored to practical music workflows.

Introduction

Recent advances in deep generative modeling have substantially improved conditional audio synthesis, making it increasingly feasible to generate plausible sounds from high-level prompts and multimodal cues
[
17
,
31
,
29
,
14
]
.
However, practical music creation requires more than producing a plausible waveform from a prompt.
In real workflows, users iteratively
edit
and refine audio: they may want to emphasize a violin phrase in a dense mix, suppress a competing instrument, or alter a musical passage to match a visual scene or a textual instruction.
These operations impose constraints that go beyond realism.
Edits must preserve rhythm and temporal structure, retain timbral identity, and remain faithful to the provided guidance, even when the guidance is incomplete or loosely aligned with the audio.
This motivates a unified framework for
multimodal music generation and targeted editing
—a single system that can both synthesize music from non-audio cues and perform mixture-grounded manipulation, rather than relying on separate models for isolated tasks.

Most existing methods address only part of this objective.
A large body of work on music and audio–visual instrument separation follows a
discriminative
reconstruction paradigm, estimating the target signal via time–frequency masks or spectrogram reconstruction from a mixture, optionally using visual cues
[

,
11
,
9
,
28
,
1
,
33
,
3
]
.
While effective when sources are moderately separable, such methods are fundamentally constrained by the mixture: they often degrade under strong overlap, heavy suppression of the target component, or visually ambiguous scenes
[
28
,
3
]
.
This limitation is also reflected in music demixing systems such as Demucs and Hybrid Demucs, which achieve strong separation performance but are typically trained for a fixed set of stems, limiting their suitability for open-ended, promptable editing
[
4
,
5
,
23
,
18
,
22
]
.
Importantly, these systems are not designed to support
zero-mixture generation
and do not offer a common mechanism that smoothly transitions between creating music from scratch and editing existing mixtures.

Generative formulations offer a promising path toward unification.
Rather than predicting a deterministic mask, generative models represent the conditional distribution of the target audio, which can improve fidelity in challenging overlaps and enables “fill-in” behavior that is valuable for editing.
Recent work has explored diffusion and flow matching for speech separation and sound extraction
[
2
,
25
,
19
,
24
,
7
,
31
,
29
]
, and DAVIS
[
14
]
shows that conditional generation can be effective for visually guided sound separation.
Yet these methods are primarily formulated as extraction interfaces: they assume a mixture is present, optimize separation-oriented objectives, and typically treat generation and editing as distinct settings.
For music creation, a practical system should instead support both regimes within a single controllable engine.

A central obstacle is
cross-modal grounding
.
Music editing may be guided by text, visual cues, or both, but the guidance can be ambiguous (multiple plausible instruments), temporally misaligned (visual evidence does not precisely match the sounding event), or partially missing (only text or only video is provided).
Widely used conditioning strategies such as additive fusion or feature concatenation offer limited control over
how
external signals influence the audio representation.
Under difficult alignment or missing-modality conditions, these strategies can lead to prompt drift, weak temporal correspondence, and spurious musical components—failures that are particularly salient in music because small deviations in rhythm, structure, or timbre are immediately perceptible.
In addition, most systems are built around fixed prompt types or task-specific conditioning paths: text-queried methods
[
17
,
6
,
31
,
29
]
and visual-queried methods
[
32
,
8
,
16
,
14
]
improve flexibility, but they typically assume a specific interface and do not explicitly train for robust behavior across heterogeneous prompting configurations.
A practical multimodal music model should therefore provide a
modality-agnostic
interface: it should accept any available subset of mixture audio, visual cues, and text, and degrade gracefully when some modalities are absent.

In this paper, we present
MAGE
, a modality-agnostic framework for
multimodal music generation and targeted editing
in a unified continuous latent space.
Rather than treating synthesis and editing as separate problems, MAGE models both as
conditional latent trajectory learning
under multimodal guidance.
At its core is the
Controlled Multimodal FluxFormer
, a flow-based Transformer with long skip connections that learns controllable latent dynamics for music generation (starting from noise) and mixture-grounded editing (conditioning on the mixture latent).
To strengthen cross-modal grounding, we introduce
Audio–Visual Nexus Alignment
, which selects temporally consistent conditioning evidence for the audio timeline, together with a cross-gated modulation mechanism that applies
multiplicative
control from aligned visual and textual cues.
This design provides finer control than additive conditioning by suppressing unsupported components rather than injecting features indiscriminately.
Finally, we train with a dynamic modality-masking curriculum that exposes the model to text-only, visual-only, joint multimodal, and mixture-guided settings, enabling robust inference under missing or heterogeneous modalities without training separate systems.

We focus on music as a challenging testbed for multimodal generation and editing due to its strong temporal organization, polyphonic overlap, and sensitivity to timbral artifacts.
Experiments on the MUSIC benchmark show that a single lightweight architecture can support both multimodal-guided music generation and targeted editing with competitive quality, while offering a flexible prompting interface tailored to practical music workflows.

Related Work

2.1
Music Separation and Audio–Visual Instrument Separation

Music source separation (
music demixing
) aims to decompose a mixture into constituent stems and has been driven largely by domain-specific architectures optimized for fixed output ontologies. Representative systems such as Demucs and Hybrid Demucs combine time-domain modeling with spectro-temporal refinement to capture long-range harmonic structure and sharp transients, achieving strong performance on benchmarks such as MUSDB18
[
4
,
5
,
23
,
18
,
22
]
. In parallel, audio–visual instrument separation leverages synchronized video to associate visible instruments with their sound sources. Early work introduced visually guided separation via mask-based learning from unlabeled videos
[
32
,
10
]
, and subsequent approaches improved object-centric reasoning, motion sensitivity, and cross-modal interaction through attention and cyclic consistency
[
11
,
9
,
28
,
1
,
33
,
3
]
.
Despite steady improvements, these methods are primarily formulated as
discriminative
reconstruction problems (mask prediction or spectrogram regression) and are evaluated as extraction systems. As a consequence, they are tightly coupled to the observed mixture and can degrade under severe overlap or when the desired component is weak or partially suppressed. More importantly, they do not address the broader music-creation setting where users wish to
edit
musical content under multimodal intent and also
generate
music without a mixture. Our work targets this more general objective by unifying multimodal music generation and targeted editing within a single model and a single conditioning interface.

2.2
Promptable and Generative Sound Separation

To move beyond fixed taxonomies, recent work explores promptable and open-vocabulary separation. Ontology-driven systems such as Universal Sound Separation scale weakly supervised separation using AudioSet-derived classes, but remain tied to predefined label sets and training ontologies
[
15
,
12
]
.
Open-vocabulary approaches instead condition on language embeddings: AudioSep leverages CLAP-aligned audio–text representations for language-queried separation
[
17
]
, while CLIPSep trains text-queried separation models from unlabeled videos using CLIP-style representations
[
6
,
20
]
.
Generative formulations further replace deterministic masks with conditional generation of the target signal, improving recovery in ambiguous overlaps and enabling a more natural path toward controllable manipulation. FlowSep adopts rectified flow matching in a latent space conditioned on text
[
31
,
21
]
, and SoloAudio uses a diffusion-transformer architecture augmented with synthetic audio to improve generalization for target extraction
[
29
]
. In the audio–visual domain, DAVIS formulates separation as diffusion-based conditional generation from mixture audio and video, improving reconstruction of complex time–frequency structures
[
14
]
.
These works establish the value of generative modeling for extraction, but their interface and objectives remain primarily separation-centric: they assume a mixture is present and do not explicitly unify
zero-mixture generation
with
mixture-grounded editing
. In contrast, we focus on a music-centric workflow that treats generation and targeted editing as two operating modes of the same conditional latent dynamics, enabling a single lightweight model to support both.

2.3
Multimodal Conditioning, Grounding, and Modality-Agnostic Control

A persistent challenge in multimodal audio modeling is
grounding
: visual or textual cues should guide the output while avoiding spurious content when guidance is ambiguous, temporally misaligned, or partially missing—a failure mode that is especially salient in polyphonic music.
Many systems inject conditioning through additive fusion or feature concatenation, which provides limited control over
how
external signals influence the audio representation, and can lead to prompt drift or hallucinated components when the conditioning evidence is weak.
Recent foundation-style systems such as SAM Audio unify multiple prompt types for open-domain audio extraction and show strong generalization across prompting modalities
[
26
]
.
However, this line of work is primarily centered on separation/segmentation-style interfaces rather than music generation and editing, and typically relies on large-scale architectures that may be costly for lightweight, interactive music workflows.

Our method is distinguished along two axes. First, we explicitly target
multimodal music generation and targeted editing
within a single continuous latent formulation, rather than designing a task-specific extraction model. Second, we strengthen grounding through
alignment-guided, multiplicative control
: Audio–Visual Nexus Alignment selects temporally consistent visual evidence for the audio timeline, and cross-gated modulation uses this aligned context to suppress unsupported content rather than injecting features indiscriminately. Combined with dynamic modality-masked training, this yields a modality-agnostic interface that remains robust under heterogeneous or missing conditions, while being tailored to the temporal and timbral sensitivity of music.

3
Proposed Method: MAGE

Problem Formulation
.
We study a unified setting for
multimodal music generation
and
targeted music editing
under heterogeneous and potentially incomplete conditioning.
Let
x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
denote the desired target waveform and
\hat{x}
the model output.
At inference time, the user may provide (i) an
optional
mixture waveform
x_{m}
, (ii) a visual input
v
(a video clip or a set of frames), and (iii) a text prompt
c
describing the target or the intended edit.
Our goal is to model a single conditional distribution that supports both regimes:

generation
without a mixture,
\hat{x}\sim p_{\theta}(x\mid v,c)
, and

mixture-grounded editing
,
\hat{x}\sim p_{\theta}(x\mid x_{m},v,c)
, where the output should remain consistent with the mixture structure while following multimodal intent.
Crucially, a practical system must remain reliable when any subset of modalities is missing (e.g., text-only, visual-only, mixture-only), and must preserve musically salient properties—temporal structure, rhythm, and timbral identity—while performing targeted manipulation.
We therefore seek a
single
model and a
single
inference procedure that smoothly transitions between pure synthesis and mixture-guided editing without retraining task-specific heads or relying on fixed output ontologies.

Method Overview
.
MAGE performs controllable music generation and editing in a
continuous latent space
designed for stable refinement (Fig.
2
).
Audio is first encoded into latent tokens using
MixWavCodec
, a mixture-aware codec trained on isolated tracks and synthetic mixtures to produce representations robust to source overlap.
Generation and editing are then modeled by the
Controlled Multimodal FluxFormer
, a flow-based Transformer that learns conditional latent trajectories.
The same backbone supports both modes: it generates from noise under
(v,c)
when no mixture is provided, and performs mixture-grounded editing when conditioned on the mixture latent. To ensure reliable multimodal control, we address cross-modal grounding explicitly.

Audio–Visual Nexus Alignment (AVNA)
selects temporally consistent visual evidence for each audio latent step, while a
cross-gated modulation
mechanism applies multiplicative conditioning from aligned visual and textual cues, suppressing unsupported components and improving temporal correspondence. Finally, we employ a
dynamic modality-masking curriculum
during training, randomly dropping conditioning modalities so the model learns to operate under text-only, visual-only, multimodal, or mixture-guided settings.
This yields a unified and lightweight framework capable of robust multimodal-guided music generation and targeted editing.

3.1
MixWavCodec: Noise-Robust Adversarial Latent Codec

We introduce
MixWavCodec
, a continuous latent codec designed for robust music generation and editing from mixtures.
Given a waveform
x\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
, the codec maps

z=\mathcal{E}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D},\qquad\hat{x}=\mathcal{D}(z),

(1)

where
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{D}
denote the encoder and decoder. We adopt a
continuous
latent space rather than discrete tokens so that downstream flow-based generation can refine audio through smooth latent trajectories without quantization artifacts.

Mixture-aware training
.
To support mixture-grounded editing, the codec is trained on both isolated tracks and randomized mixtures.
At each iteration we sample two audio segments
x^{(1)}
and
x^{(2)}
and form

x_{m}=x^{(1)}+\lambda x^{(2)},\qquad\lambda\sim\mathcal{U}[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}],

(2)

allowing the encoder to learn representations that remain stable for both single-source and overlapping musical content.

Noise-robust latent decoding
.
Since the generative model modifies latent tokens during editing, we improve decoder robustness by injecting Gaussian noise during training:

\tilde{z}=z+\sigma\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),

(3)

and reconstructing from
\tilde{z}
.
This encourages smooth latent-to-audio mapping and stable decoding under latent perturbations.

Objectives
.
MixWavCodec is trained using reconstruction and adversarial objectives:

\min_{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}}\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fm}},\qquad\min_{D}\;\mathcal{L}_{D},

(4)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
combines waveform and spectral reconstruction losses, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fm}}
are adversarial and feature-matching losses computed with an EnCodec-style discriminator
D
. This training yields a compact, mixture-aware, and noise-robust latent representation that serves as the editable canvas for MAGE.

3.2
Controlled Multimodal FluxFormer

Flow-based Latent Trajectory Modeling
.
To unify music generation and mixture-guided editing, we model audio synthesis as a conditional trajectory in latent space.
Let
z_{m}=\mathcal{E}(x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
denote the latent representation of an optional mixture input (or
\varnothing
for pure generation), and let
\mathbf{c}
denote the available conditioning signals.
We learn a conditional flow that transports a noise latent
z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
to a target latent
z_{1}=\mathcal{E}(x^{\star})
corresponding to the desired output audio.

Following flow matching, we sample a time
t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
and construct an interpolated latent

z_{t}=(1-t)z_{0}+tz_{1},

(5)

with a target velocity field
u_{t}
.
The
Controlled Multimodal FluxFormer

F_{\theta}
predicts the conditional velocity

\hat{u}_{t}=F_{\theta}(z_{t},\,z_{m},\,\mathbf{c},\,t),

(6)

and is trained using the flow-matching objective

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}=\mathbb{E}_{z_{0},z_{1},t}\big[\|\hat{u}_{t}-u_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\big].

(7)

At inference, we integrate the learned velocity field from
t=0
to
t=1
to obtain the predicted latent
\hat{z}_{1}
, which is decoded to audio via
\hat{x}=\mathcal{D}(\hat{z}_{1})
.
This formulation naturally supports both operating modes: when
z_{m}=\varnothing
the model performs unconditional generation guided by multimodal cues, and when
z_{m}
is provided it performs mixture-grounded editing by conditioning the latent trajectory on the mixture structure.

Multimodal Condition Encoders
.
MAGE supports both textual and visual guidance.
A
Prompt Encoder
maps a text instruction
c
into a semantic embedding
p=\phi_{\text{text}}(c)
, while a
Scene Encoder
extracts frame-level representations from a visual input
v=\{I_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
:

\{s_{k}\}=\phi_{\text{vis}}(v).

(8)

Since music editing is highly sensitive to temporal correspondence, we aggregate frame features using
Temporal Scene Attention
, producing a temporally-aware representation
\tilde{s}
that emphasizes frames most relevant to the audio timeline.
The resulting condition set is
\mathbf{c}=\{p,\tilde{s}\}
, with missing modalities handled via masking during training (Sec.
3.3
).

Cross-Modal Grounding via Audio–Visual Nexus Alignment.
.
A major challenge in multimodal music modeling is aligning visual evidence with the audio timeline.
Direct feature fusion often introduces spurious components when visual cues are weak or temporally misaligned.
To address this, we introduce
Audio–Visual Nexus Alignment (AVNA)
, which explicitly aligns visual features with audio latent tokens before conditioning the generator.

Given audio latent tokens and scene features
\tilde{s}
, AVNA selects temporally consistent visual evidence for each latent index using nearest-neighbor matching in a shared embedding space, producing aligned visual tokens
\bar{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
.
This alignment establishes a fine-grained correspondence between audio structure and visual context.

We then inject multimodal control through a
cross-gated modulation
mechanism that multiplicatively modulates intermediate audio features:

\mathrm{CGM}(h;\,p,\bar{s})=h\odot\sigma(W_{p}p+W_{s}\bar{s}),

(9)

where
h
denotes audio features within the FluxFormer,
\sigma(\cdot)
is a sigmoid gating function, and
W_{p},W_{s}
are learnable projections.
Unlike additive fusion, this multiplicative gating allows multimodal cues to selectively
suppress
unsupported components while amplifying evidence-consistent structure.

Finally, a
Visual-Aware Mixture Audio Filter
further refines the representation by attenuating irrelevant mixture content and preserving structures supported by multimodal evidence.
Together, AVNA and cross-gated modulation provide precise cross-modal control over latent evolution, improving temporal grounding and reducing unintended artifacts compared with conventional conditioning strategies.

3.3
Flow-Matching Masked Training and Inference Strategy

Dynamic modality masking for unified control
.
A practical multimodal music model must operate when any subset of guidance is available (mixture audio, visuals, text).
To make MAGE robust to missing or heterogeneous inputs, we train a
single
conditional flow under
dynamic modality masking
.
Let the full condition set be

\mathcal{C}=\{z_{m},\;p,\;\tilde{s}\},

(10)

where
z_{m}=\mathcal{E}(x_{m})
is the (optional) mixture latent,
p=\phi_{\text{text}}(c)
is the text embedding, and
\tilde{s}
is the temporally aggregated scene representation.
During training, we sample a binary mask
m=(m_{a},m_{t},m_{v})\in\{0,1\}^{3}
and construct

\tilde{\mathcal{C}}=\{m_{a}z_{m},\;m_{t}p,\;m_{v}\tilde{s}\},

(11)

so the model is explicitly optimized over text-only, visual-only, mixture-only, and joint multimodal regimes within one objective, rather than learning separate interfaces.

Given target latent
z_{1}
and noise
z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, we sample
t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
and form

z_{t}=(1-t)z_{0}+tz_{1}.

(12)

The FluxFormer predicts the masked-conditional velocity

\hat{u}_{t}=F_{\theta}(z_{t},\tilde{\mathcal{C}},t),

(13)

and we optimize the masked flow-matching loss

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}=\mathbb{E}_{z_{0},z_{1},t,m}\Big[\big\|F_{\theta}(z_{t},\tilde{\mathcal{C}},t)-u_{t}\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big],

(14)

where
u_{t}
is the target velocity under our parameterization.
This trains a
single
conditional vector field that generalizes across prompt configurations and prevents brittle specialization to one modality.

Classifier-free guidance in a multimodal setting
.
To strengthen controllability at inference, we adopt classifier-free guidance (CFG) by treating the
fully masked
condition (
m=(0,0,0)
) as the unconditional branch.
Concretely, we train with a non-zero probability of dropping
all
conditions, so the model learns both conditional and unconditional velocity fields.
At inference, we compute

\hat{u}_{t}^{\mathrm{cfg}}=\hat{u}_{t}^{\mathrm{uncond}}+\gamma\big(\hat{u}_{t}^{\mathrm{cond}}-\hat{u}_{t}^{\mathrm{uncond}}\big),

(15)

where
\hat{u}_{t}^{\mathrm{cond}}=F_{\theta}(z_{t},\tilde{\mathcal{C}},t)
,
\hat{u}_{t}^{\mathrm{uncond}}=F_{\theta}(z_{t},\mathbf{0},t)
, and
\gamma
is the guidance scale.
Importantly, CFG is
modality-agnostic
:
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
may itself correspond to any subset of modalities (e.g., text-only or visual-only), enabling consistent control strengthening without changing the architecture.

Inference for generation and editing
.
At test time, MAGE receives any available subset of conditions and integrates the learned flow without architectural changes.
For
zero-mixture generation
, we start from
z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and solve the conditional ODE (optionally with CFG) to obtain
\hat{z}_{1}
, then decode
\hat{x}=\mathcal{D}(\hat{z}_{1})
.
For
mixture-grounded editing
, we additionally provide
z_{m}=\mathcal{E}(x_{m})
in the condition set so the trajectory remains anchored to mixture structure while following the prompt.
Because masking is part of training, performance degrades gracefully when modalities are missing, yielding a single model that supports text-only, visual-only, joint multimodal, and mixture-guided editing/generation.

Why this matters.
.
Dynamic masking and CFG serve complementary roles: masking enforces
interface robustness
across missing-modality regimes, while CFG provides a principled knob to trade off adherence to conditioning against diversity and mixture faithfulness.
Together they enable a genuinely modality-agnostic inference procedure without training separate systems for each prompt type or operating mode.

4
Experiments

Dataset
.
We evaluate on the MUSIC dataset
[
32
]
, which contains in-the-wild musical performances with strong audio–visual correspondence (visible instruments that produce sound) and coarse semantic labels that can be used as text prompts.
MUSIC is a challenging testbed for our goals because clips frequently exhibit polyphonic overlap and partial occlusion, where effective editing requires both (i) preserving mixture-consistent musical structure and (ii) following multimodal intent without introducing spurious components.
We follow the standard MUSIC splits for training/validation/testing and use the associated instrument labels to construct text prompts, while video frames provide visual grounding for the target source.

Evaluation Metrics
.
Our evaluation reflects the dual nature of MAGE as both an
editing/separation
model (mixture provided) and a
generative
model (mixture absent).
For mixture-guided editing, we report the standard BSS_Eval metrics SDR, SIR, and SAR, which quantify overall reconstruction fidelity, interference suppression, and artifact introduction, respectively.
Because separation metrics alone do not capture perceptual realism or prompt faithfulness—especially for generative editing—we additionally report metrics commonly used in audio generation: Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD) to measure distributional realism against real audio, CLAP-based audio–audio similarity to assess semantic consistency between generated and reference audio, and CLAPScore to quantify alignment between generated audio and the conditioning text prompt. Together, these metrics characterize both
signal-level correctness
and
conditioning faithfulness
, which are necessary to evaluate a unified multimodal generation-and-editing framework.

Notation
.
Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
denote an audio waveform and
x_{m}
an observed mixture waveform (when available).
We encode audio into a continuous latent sequence using a codec
\mathcal{E}
and decode with
\mathcal{D}
:

z=\mathcal{E}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
and
\hat{x}=\mathcal{D}(z)
.
We denote the mixture latent by
z_{m}=\mathcal{E}(x_{m})
and the target output latent by
z_{1}=\mathcal{E}(x^{\star})
, where
x^{\star}
is the ground-truth target waveform.
Text and visual conditions are encoded as
p=\phi_{\text{text}}(c)
and
s=\phi_{\text{vis}}(v)
, and AVNA produces time-aligned visual tokens
\bar{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
matched to the audio latent timeline.
To support missing modalities, we use a binary mask
m=(m_{a},m_{t},m_{v})\in\{0,1\}^{3}
and define the effective condition set
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}=\{m_{a}z_{m},\;m_{t}p,\;m_{v}\bar{s}\}
.

Implementation Details
.
We implement MAGE in PyTorch and train on MUSIC using fixed 11.025 kHz audio segments of 64,640 samples. Audio is encoded by a pretrained latent autoencoder into
N\!=\!202
tokens of dimension 128; we keep this autoencoder frozen so training focuses on learning
controllable latent dynamics
rather than re-learning reconstruction. Visual conditioning is extracted with a CLIP ViT-B/32 encoder from 47 frames sampled at 8 fps (stride 1), providing sufficient scene context while preserving temporal correspondence to the audio timeline. Text prompts are embedded by a frozen CLAP text encoder and injected as a single conditioning token to provide stable semantic guidance.

Our generative backbone is a flow-matching UDiT operating on 202 audio tokens with hidden size 1024. We form audio tokens by projecting and concatenating target and mixture latents and adding learned positional embeddings. Multimodal control uses
multiplicative
audio–visual gating (rather than additive fusion) to suppress unsupported structures and reduce prompt-induced artifacts. We optimize an
\ell_{1}
flow-matching objective on the predicted velocity field, which is stable in latent audio space. To support modality-agnostic inference, we train with dynamic modality masking: per sample we choose mixture-guided extraction (70%), zero-mixture generation (20%), or unconditional/CFG fallback (10%), with random dropping of text/visual inputs and a static-frame augmentation (25%) when visual is present. We use AdamW with learning rates
10^{-4}
for both the transformer and visual encoder, batch size 32, gradient clipping at 5.0, and train for 2000 epochs. At inference, we integrate the learned flow with a small-step Euler solver for efficient generation and editing.

systems that predict deterministic masks from a mixture and are optimized solely for SDR, SIR and SAR under a fixed mixture-conditioned interface. In contrast, MAGE is a unified model that supports both mixture-guided editing and
zero-mixture
generation while remaining robust to missing modalities.

Multimodal music editing
.
Methods such as iQuery
[

]
and DAVIS
[

]

[

]
is a large-scale foundation model for universal audio extraction and performs strongly across diverse categories. However, in the MUSIC multimodal editing setting, MAGE achieves stronger performance despite using a significantly more compact architecture. Our alignment-guided conditioning and cross-gated modulation improve multimodal grounding while supporting both mixture-grounded editing and zero-mixture generation within a unified modality-agnostic framework.

6
Conclusion

We presented
MAGE
, a modality-agnostic framework that unifies multimodal music generation and targeted editing within a single continuous latent formulation. By combining
MixWavCodec
, the
Controlled Multimodal FluxFormer
,
Audio–Visual Nexus Alignment
, and cross-gated modulation, MAGE enables controllable music synthesis and mixture-grounded editing under flexible multimodal conditions. Experiments on the MUSIC benchmark show that explicit cross-modal grounding and modality-masked training enable robust performance even when conditioning signals are incomplete or heterogeneous. Beyond improved performance, MAGE demonstrates that multimodal music generation and editing can be treated as two operating modes of the same conditional generative process, rather than separate tasks requiring specialized models. We hope this perspective encourages future research toward unified multimodal audio systems capable of interactive creation, flexible prompting, and fine-grained control over complex audio mixtures.

Supplementary Material

A
Implementation Details

We implement
MAGE
in PyTorch and train on MUSIC using fixed audio–video segments. The implementation keeps generation and mixture-grounded editing within a unified latent pipeline: the same audio representation, the same multimodal conditioning interface, and the same flow solver are used in both regimes.

MixWavCodec: Latent Codec
. All audio is resampled to 11.025 kHz, converted to mono, and cropped to 64,640 samples (
\approx 5.86
s). We first train
MixWavCodec
as a mixture-aware continuous latent codec and then use it as the audio representation layer in
MAGE
. The codec is trained on both isolated sources and synthetic mixtures so that the latent space remains stable under source overlap and suitable for mixture-grounded editing. The encoder–decoder follows an Oobleck-style architecture with weight-normalized 1D convolutions, residual units, and Snake activations. The encoder uses base width 128, channel multipliers
[1,2,4,8]
, stride pattern
[2,4,4,10]
, and a VAE bottleneck; the decoder mirrors this hierarchy with transposed convolutions and residual refinement. The resulting temporal downsampling ratio is 320, corresponding to approximately 35 latent tokens per second. Under our segment length, each clip is represented by a continuous latent sequence of length
N=202
with token dimension 128.

During codec training, the encoder observes both single-source and overlapping musical content, while the decoder is trained to remain robust to latent perturbations. This improves reconstruction stability when the downstream generator modifies or transports latent trajectories. In the second stage, when training
MAGE
for generation and mixture-grounded editing, we freeze the pretrained MixWavCodec and optimize only the multimodal generative backbone. This two-stage design separates
representation learning
from
conditional latent dynamics
: the codec learns a compact and mixture-aware audio manifold, while
MAGE
learns how to move within that manifold under text, visual, and mixture guidance. This preserves musically meaningful timing and timbral structure while reducing the sequence length to a scale suitable for flow-based latent modeling.

Figure 1
:

Illustration of
Audio–Visual Nexus Alignment
(AVNA). Video frames and audio events are sampled at different temporal resolutions, leading to a mismatch between the visual stream and the audio timeline. AVNA resolves this by performing nearest-neighbor temporal resampling on normalized timestamps, assigning each audio event to the closest visual observation. The aligned visual sequence therefore matches the audio timeline and provides temporally consistent conditioning for multimodal generation and mixture-grounded editing.

Controlled Multimodal FluxFormer
. As shown in Figure
2
, the generative backbone is the proposed
Controlled Multimodal FluxFormer
(
FluxFormer
), implemented as a U-shaped diffusion transformer (
UDiT
) operating on the audio latent sequence. We use hidden size 1024. The architecture follows an encoder–bottleneck–decoder layout with skip-connected output blocks. This structure preserves local mixture-consistent detail through skip pathways while allowing deeper layers to reshape global source identity and prompt-level semantics. At each latent step, the current latent state and the mixture latent are projected separately from
\mathbb{R}^{128}
to
\mathbb{R}^{512}
, concatenated into a 1024-dimensional token, and combined with learned positional embeddings. This formulation gives each transformer layer simultaneous access to the evolving target state and the mixture anchor, which is important for editing scenarios where modification and preservation must be balanced.

Text and Visual Conditioning
. Text prompts are encoded using a frozen CLAP text encoder (
laion/clap-htsat-unfused
)
[
30
]
. We use the pooled 768-dimensional representation, project it to the transformer hidden space, and prepend it as a semantic token to the audio sequence. This design keeps the text interface lightweight while allowing prompt semantics to propagate through transformer self-attention. Visual conditioning is extracted using CLIP ViT-B/32
[
20
]
. For each clip, we uniformly sample 47 frames at 8 fps across the 5.86 s segment. Frame embeddings are 512-dimensional and projected into the shared hidden space. In addition to the framewise sequence, we compute a temporally averaged visual summary that serves as a global timbre vector and conditions the transformer through adaptive layer normalization. The framewise stream provides time-local scene evidence, while the global summary provides a stable scene-level prior for source identity and timbral characteristics.

Audio–Visual Nexus Alignment
. As shown in Figure
1
, we implement
Audio–Visual Nexus Alignment
(AVNA) to synchronize the visual stream with the audio latent timeline before fusion. Because the generator operates on audio latents rather than video frames, direct fusion leads to temporal mismatch. AVNA resolves this by resampling the visual sequence to the audio latent length using nearest-neighbor matching on normalized time grids. Given visual length
T_{v}
and audio latent length
N
, each audio token is assigned the nearest visual timestamp, producing an aligned visual sequence in
\mathbb{R}^{N\times 512}
. This explicit alignment step maintains temporal consistency between modalities without introducing an additional trainable cross-modal module. In short clips such as MUSIC, this deterministic correspondence provides stable synchronization and avoids the instability of learned temporal warping.

Cross-Gated Modulation
. After alignment, visual features are fused through
Cross-Gated Modulation
(CGM). Let
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{audio}}
denote the audio token stream and
\bar{\mathbf{s}}
the aligned visual tokens. We apply

\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{joint}}=\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{audio}}\odot\sigma(W_{s}\bar{\mathbf{s}})

where
W_{s}
is a learned projection and
\sigma(\cdot)
is a sigmoid gate. This multiplicative control acts as a selective conditioning mechanism over the latent trajectory. Compared with additive fusion, it is less prone to injecting unsupported components when the visual signal is weak or ambiguous. This behavior is particularly beneficial for editing from mixtures, where the objective is to refine an existing latent structure under multimodal guidance rather than overwrite it. In our implementation, text provides global semantic control through the prepended token, while aligned visual evidence modulates local latent evolution.

Figure 2
:

Architecture of the proposed
Controlled Multimodal FluxFormer
. Each
FluxFormer Block
applies multi-head self-attention over the latent audio tokens followed by a feedforward layer, with residual connections and layer normalization. Multimodal conditioning is injected through prepended text tokens and cross-gated modulation from aligned visual features. The full backbone stacks
N
such blocks and injects the diffusion timestep embedding at every layer, while skip connections preserve lower-level mixture-consistent structure across depth. This design allows the model to jointly refine global semantic intent and local acoustic detail during both zero-mixture generation and mixture-grounded editing.

Flow-Matching Objective
. We train
MAGE
using continuous-time flow matching in latent space. Given target latent
z_{1}
and Gaussian noise
z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, we sample
t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
and define

z_{t}=tz_{1}+\bigl(1-(1-\sigma_{\min})t\bigr)z_{0},

with
\sigma_{\min}=10^{-4}
. The target velocity is

u_{t}=z_{1}-(1-\sigma_{\min})z_{0}.

The FluxFormer predicts
\hat{u}_{t}=F_{\theta}(z_{t},\tilde{\mathcal{C}},t)
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
denotes the available masked condition set. We optimize

\mathcal{L}=\|\hat{u}_{t}-u_{t}\|_{1}+\|z_{1}-(\hat{u}_{t}+(1-\sigma_{\min})z_{0})\|_{1}.

This formulation treats zero-mixture generation and mixture-grounded editing as two instances of the same latent trajectory problem: the backbone always learns a conditional velocity field, while only the condition set changes across operating modes.

Dynamic Modality Masking
. To support modality-agnostic inference, we train with dynamic modality masking. For each sample, we randomly select one of three regimes: mixture-guided editing (70%), zero-mixture generation (20%), or unconditional fallback for classifier-free guidance (10%). Within the mixture-guided branch the model observes full multimodal input, text-dropped input, or visual-dropped input. Within the zero-mixture branch it observes text+visual, visual-only, or text-only prompting. This training strategy exposes a single conditional backbone to heterogeneous prompt configurations. As a result, missing modalities are handled within the learned interface itself rather than requiring separate task-specific heads.

Static-Image Fallback
. When visual conditioning is present, we apply static-image augmentation with probability 0.25 by repeating a randomly selected frame across the entire visual sequence. This improves robustness to weakly dynamic clips and still-image guidance, and prevents the model from relying solely on motion cues.

Optimization and Data Construction
. We train with AdamW using learning rate
10^{-4}
for both the FluxFormer and the visual encoder branch. Gradients are clipped at 5.0. Training runs for 1K epochs, and the learning rate is decayed by a factor of 0.5 at epochs
\{100,200,300\}
. The training set is duplicated five times per epoch to increase the diversity of sampled source pairings and temporal crops.

Each training example contains two sources. Their waveforms are averaged to form the mixture, and both sources and the mixture are encoded into the latent space. During audio loading, amplitudes are randomly scaled in
[0.5,1.5]
and clipped to
[-1,1]
. Standard spatial augmentations are applied to the video stream during training.

Inference
. At inference time, we solve the learned latent flow using a lightweight Euler integrator with two function evaluations. For zero-mixture generation, the trajectory starts from Gaussian noise and is guided by the available non-audio conditions. For mixture-grounded editing, the mixture latent is included in the condition set so that the trajectory remains anchored to the observed structure while responding to prompt-level control. The predicted latent sequence is decoded to waveform using the frozen MixWavCodec decoder. Because the same latent space, multimodal interface, and solver are used in both modes, the implementation remains compact while maintaining a unified interpretation of generation and editing as conditional latent transport.

B
Evaluation Metrics

The evaluation protocol reflects the dual functionality of
MAGE
:
(i)
mixture-guided editing
, where a mixture is provided and the goal is to recover or refine a target source, and
(ii)
generative editing
, where audio is synthesized or modified under multimodal conditioning.
Accordingly, we report both classical source separation metrics and perceptual or semantic metrics commonly used in generative audio evaluation.

Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SDR)
. Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SDR) measures the overall fidelity between the predicted source waveform
\hat{s}
and the ground-truth source
s
.
Following the BSS_Eval formulation, the estimated signal is decomposed into target and error components. Let

\hat{s}=s_{\mathrm{target}}+e_{\mathrm{interf}}+e_{\mathrm{artif}},

where
s_{\mathrm{target}}
is the projection of
\hat{s}
onto the reference source
s
,
e_{\mathrm{interf}}
represents interference from other sources, and
e_{\mathrm{artif}}
represents algorithmic artifacts. The SDR is defined as

\mathrm{SDR}=10\log_{10}\frac{\|s_{\mathrm{target}}\|^{2}}{\|e_{\mathrm{interf}}+e_{\mathrm{artif}}\|^{2}}.

Higher SDR indicates better reconstruction fidelity. In the context of multimodal editing, SDR reflects how closely the generated signal matches the desired target audio when the mixture context is provided.

Signal-to-Interference Ratio (SIR)
. Signal-to-Interference Ratio (SIR) evaluates the ability of the model to suppress unwanted sources present in the mixture. Using the same decomposition as above, SIR is defined as

\mathrm{SIR}=10\log_{10}\frac{\|s_{\mathrm{target}}\|^{2}}{\|e_{\mathrm{interf}}\|^{2}}.

A higher SIR indicates stronger suppression of interfering sources.
For mixture-guided editing, SIR is particularly important because it reflects whether the model can isolate the visually or textually conditioned source while removing other mixture components.

Signal-to-Artifacts Ratio (SAR)
. Signal-to-Artifacts Ratio (SAR) measures distortions introduced by the model during reconstruction. It is defined as

\mathrm{SAR}=10\log_{10}\frac{\|s_{\mathrm{target}}+e_{\mathrm{interf}}\|^{2}}{\|e_{\mathrm{artif}}\|^{2}}.

Higher SAR values indicate fewer algorithmic artifacts. In multimodal editing scenarios, SAR captures the perceptual cleanliness of the output signal after conditioning and latent transport.

Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD)
. While separation metrics quantify signal-level correctness, they do not capture perceptual realism when the model generates new audio.
To measure distributional similarity between generated and real audio, we compute Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD). Given embeddings extracted from a pretrained audio feature network, real audio embeddings are modeled as a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{r},\Sigma_{r})
and generated audio embeddings as
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})
. The FAD is computed as

\mathrm{FAD}=\|\mu_{r}-\mu_{g}\|^{2}+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Sigma_{r}+\Sigma_{g}-2(\Sigma_{r}\Sigma_{g})^{1/2}\right).

Lower FAD indicates that generated audio samples lie closer to the distribution of real audio recordings. This metric is particularly useful when evaluating the generative capabilities of
MAGE
in the absence of a mixture reference.

CLAP Audio–Audio Similarity
. To measure semantic consistency between generated audio and the reference target, we compute similarity in the joint audio–text embedding space of CLAP. Let
f_{a}(\cdot)
denote the CLAP audio encoder. Given generated audio
\hat{s}
and reference audio
s
, the similarity score is computed using cosine similarity:

\mathrm{Sim}_{\mathrm{AA}}=\frac{f_{a}(\hat{s})\cdot f_{a}(s)}{\|f_{a}(\hat{s})\|\,\|f_{a}(s)\|}.

Higher similarity indicates stronger semantic correspondence between generated and reference audio content. This metric is useful when evaluating whether multimodal conditioning preserves the intended source identity.

CLAPScore (Audio–Text Alignment)
. To quantify alignment between generated audio and the conditioning text prompt, we compute CLAPScore using the cosine similarity between CLAP audio and text embeddings. Let
f_{t}(\cdot)
denote the CLAP text encoder and
y
the conditioning text prompt. The CLAPScore is defined as

\mathrm{CLAPScore}=\frac{f_{a}(\hat{s})\cdot f_{t}(y)}{\|f_{a}(\hat{s})\|\,\|f_{t}(y)\|}.

Higher CLAPScore values indicate stronger alignment between generated audio and the semantic description provided in the text prompt. Together, these metrics evaluate complementary aspects of the model’s behavior.
SDR, SIR, and SAR measure signal-level correctness in mixture-guided editing, while FAD, CLAP audio similarity, and CLAPScore capture perceptual realism and semantic alignment in generative settings. This combination provides a comprehensive evaluation of
MAGE
as a unified framework for multimodal audio generation and editing.

C
Fusion Operator Ablation

We study how different multimodal fusion strategies influence audio–visual grounding in MAGE. All variants share the same backbone, training schedule, and conditioning encoders, differing only in the mechanism used to integrate aligned visual features into the audio latent stream. Simple additive fusion combines audio latents
h
with projected visual features
W_{s}\bar{s}
through direct addition. While straightforward, this strategy injects visual information uniformly into the audio representation without regulating the strength of the visual signal. Consequently, visually induced features may introduce unsupported structures, which degrades separation quality. Gated residual fusion partially addresses this issue by modulating the visual contribution using a
\tanh
gate before adding it to the audio stream. This mechanism allows the model to regulate the magnitude of visual influence and stabilizes cross-modal interaction. However, the formulation remains additive, meaning visual features still perturb the audio representation rather than explicitly controlling it. Attention-based fusion offers a more flexible interaction by allowing the model to dynamically attend to visual features. Both self-attention and cross-attention improve over simple additive fusion by enabling context-aware integration of visual cues. Nevertheless, these mechanisms treat visual inputs primarily as additional information sources rather than explicit control signals over the audio representation.

The proposed Cross-Gated Modulation instead introduces multiplicative control,

h\odot\sigma(W_{s}\bar{s}),

which filters the audio latents using aligned visual evidence. Rather than injecting visual features directly, CGM selectively modulates the existing audio representation. This suppresses mixture components that are not supported by the visual stream while preserving relevant structure. As shown in Table
1
, this selective modulation produces consistent improvements across all metrics, with particularly strong gains in SIR, indicating improved interference suppression during multimodal editing.

We analyze the impact of temporal visual cues on multimodal music editing. This removes motion cues while preserving object-level semantics such as instrument identity and scene context. This study highlights two properties of the proposed framework. First, the model remains functional under static visual guidance, demonstrating that the multimodal interface can operate even when temporal cues are absent. Second, temporal visual information provides stronger grounding signals for the audio timeline, enabling more precise alignment between visual evidence and latent audio events. As shown in Table

, using full temporal visual sequences yields consistent improvements across SDR, SIR, and SAR, indicating that motion cues help the model better localize sounding sources and suppress unsupported mixture components during editing.
