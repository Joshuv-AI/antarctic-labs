Computational identification and characterization of high-risk human KRAS nsSNPs: Impacting structure, ligand binding, and cancer prognosis
Authors: Md Mainuddin Hossain, Juthi Adhikari, Sabbir Ahmed, Md Zakiul Islam Zaki, Afia Khandaker, Abu Zaffar Shibly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKristen rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) is a critical oncogene regulating cell proliferation and survival, with mutations driving tumorigenesis. Non-synonymous single nucleotide polymorphisms (nsSNPs) can alter KRAS structure, function, and ligand interactions, influencing clinical outcomes. This study analyzed 324 nsSNPs from dbSNP, ClinVar, and DisGeNET using eight predictive tools (SIFT, PolyPhen-2, PredictSNP, PhD-SNP, PANTHER, PROVEAN, Meta-SNP, SNAP2) to identify highly deleterious nsSNPs. Protein stability was evaluated via I-Mutant 2.0, MUpro, INPS-MD, iStable, and DDMut, and structural impacts assessed using HOPE, MutPred2, and Missense3D. Molecular docking (AutoDock Vina) and 100-ns molecular dynamics simulations explored ligand-specific interactions. Cancer susceptibilities analysis was performed using CScape, Dr. Cancer, and FATHMM. Our analysis consistently predicted sixteen nsSNPs as highly deleterious, with Y71D and M72K identified as high-risk variants located in the GTP-binding domain, destabilizing KRAS and disrupting hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions. Docking and simulation analyses showed that the Y71D and M72K nsSNPs reduced binding affinity and stability with ligand sotorasib (CID: 137278711) compared to wild-type KRAS, whereas both high-risk nsSNPs exhibited enhanced binding and stability with ligand adagrasib (CID: 138611145), indicating mutation-dependent ligand interactions. MM-GBSA analysis confirmed mutation-dependent KRAS binding changes, weakening sotorasib affinity while enhancing adagrasib interaction in the Y71D variant. Cancer susceptibilities analyses indicated both Y71D and M72K may promote cancer prognosis. These results highlight Y71D and M72K as high-risk KRAS nsSNPs affecting structural stability, ligand interactions, and cancer prognosis, providing a framework for mutation-specific therapeutic strategies and supporting further experimental validation in precision oncology.Keywords: KRAS, nsSNP, Deleterious, Protein stability, Protein structure, Cancer prognosis

Introduction

Cancer stands as a formidable health challenge globally, accounting for a significant portion of mortality, particularly in economically developed nations and, to a slightly lesser extent, in developing countries1. GLOBOCAN reported 19.3 million new cases and 10.0 million deaths in 2020, while in 2022 the numbers rose to 20 million cases and 9.7 million deaths2. Several genes, including PABL2, BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and PTEN, among others, have been implicated in cancer development. Mutations in these genes are frequently associated with hereditary cancer syndromes 3. A study demonstrated that KRAS modulates tumor growth through multiple mechanisms, with its functional significance varying considerably across different cancer types, including lung cancer, myeloma, and gastric cancer 4, 5, 6. Depending on the cellular and mutational context, KRAS may drive oncogenesis, promote metastasis, or influence therapeutic resistance 7, 8. The physiological functions of KRAS include regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival through key signaling cascades such as the MAPK and PI3K pathways 9. In contrast, its pathological roles are largely attributed to activating mutations, which render KRAS constitutively active and drive uncontrolled cell signaling, tumor progression, and poor clinical outcomes 10, 11. Mutations in the KRAS protein—particularly in codons 12, 13, and 61—can lead to substantial alterations in its structure and function, thereby impacting cancer progression and therapeutic responses 12, 13.

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are the most common genetic variations in the human genome, occurring about once every 1000 base pairs 14. They are classified as synonymous SNPs, which do not change protein sequences, and non-synonymous SNPs (nsSNPs), particularly missense variants, which alter amino acids and may affect protein function. nsSNPs are of major biomedical importance, as they can disrupt protein structure, stability, and regulatory pathways, contributing to disease. Notably, they account for nearly 50% of mutations linked to genetic, neurodegenerative, autoimmune, and inflammatory disorders 15. More than 500,000 coding SNPs have been identified, highlighting their role in genetic diversity 16, 17. They can modulate gene regulation by altering transcription factor binding sites and disrupting signal transduction pathways essential for cellular responses. To date, approximately 500,000 SNPs have been identified within coding regions, contributing to protein diversity in the human population 18. Understanding the biological significance of nsSNPs is critical for elucidating the genetic basis of diseases and advancing personalized medicine, which tailors therapeutic approaches based on individual genetic profiles 19, 20.

Experimentally assessing the effects of numerous nsSNPs can be both costly and time-intensive. However, in-silico analyses utilizing publicly available web-based bioinformatics tools provide a practical and cost-effective approach for examining a large number of nsSNPs within a specific gene 21. Advances in computational genomics have greatly facilitated the investigation of disease-associated nsSNPs, leading to the development of various predictive methodologies designed to accurately assess their phenotypic impacts 22. Recent computational studies have highlighted the significant role of nsSNPs in elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying various diseases. For instance, multiple nsSNPs have been identified within the exonic regions of the KRAS gene. Notably, mutations at codons 12 (e.g., G12D, G12V, G12C) and 13 (e.g., G13D) represent critical nsSNPs that significantly alter the structure and function of the KRAS protein, leading to constitutive activation of downstream signaling pathways 7, 8. These variants not only affect protein stability and effector binding but also play pivotal roles in driving oncogenesis and influencing therapeutic responses across different cancer types.

Despite the established role of KRAS in tumorigenesis, it remains unclear how mutation-induced structural and functional alterations influence the binding affinity of existing drugs or ligands. This knowledge gap limits our understanding of their therapeutic efficacy and the associated prognostic outcomes across different cancers. To address this, the present study employs computational tools to identify novel nsSNPs and investigates the structural and functional consequences of KRAS mutations on ligand binding and explores their potential implications for cancer prognosis.

Materials and methods

nsSNPs data Retrieval of KRAS gene and preprocessing

Non-synonymous single-nucleotide polymorphisms (nsSNPs) of human KRAS gene were retrieved from three well-established and widely utilized databases: the NCBI Short Genetic Variation database (dbSNP) 23. ClinVar 24 and DisGeNET 25. nsSNPs were identified and extracted from the aforementioned databases, with a specific focus on variants located within coding regions that lead to amino acid substitutions. The dbSNP provides a broad catalog of nucleotide variations, while ClinVar curates clinically relevant variants, and DisGeNET integrates gene- and variant-disease associations. Only missense variants were considered, as they can significantly impact protein structure and function. This study focuses on in-silico functional and structural analysis of KRAS nsSNPs.

Simultaneously, the amino acid sequence of the human KRAS protein (UniProt ID: P01116) was obtained from the UniProt database 26 in FASTA format.

Screening of highly deleterious nsSNPs of KRAS gene

The highly deleterious nsSNPs in KRAS gene were identified by eight computational tools based on their accuracy rate and availability during this work. They are SIFT 27, Polyphen-2 28, PredictSNP 29, PhD-SNP 30, PANTHER 31, SNAP2 32, PROVEAN 33, and Meta-SNP 34. The potential functional impact of the nsSNPs retrieved from the NCBI SNP database was assessed using these tools. To ensure the reliability and precision of the predictions, nsSNPs identified as deleterious by all eight tools were classified as highly deleterious variants.

The SIFT server evaluates the impact of amino acid substitutions by utilizing sequence homology to distinguish between tolerated and deleterious SNPs. Substitutions at specific positions with a probability of less than 0.05 are identified as deleterious, whereas those with a probability of 0.05 or higher are considered tolerant 35. PolyPhen-2 assesses the likely effects of amino acid substitutions on protein structure and functionality using sequence-based approaches 36. PredictSNP, a consensus classifier, integrates various prediction tools to identify mutations linked to disease 29. PhD-SNP predicts deleterious human SNPs by combining a decision tree-based model with support vector machine (SVM) classifiers-specifically SVM-Sequence and SVM-Profile, which are trained on sequence profile data 37. Position-specific evolutionary conservation scores obtained from alignments of evolutionarily related proteins are used by PANTHER to assess substitutions and classify proteins based on their molecular functions, evolutionary ties, and interactions 38. SNAP2 predicts the functional consequences of sequence variants 39. A software program called PROVEAN can determine whether an amino acid substitution or indel affects a protein's biological activity 33. The random forest-based binary classifier Meta-SNP separates polymorphic non-synonymous SNPs from disease-related SNPs 34.

Integrating these sequence- and structure-based algorithms improves reliability, as concordant results strengthen pathogenicity predictions, while discrepancies may reflect distinct mechanisms such as altered stability or binding. These tools were used solely for research benchmarking, not clinical application.

Analysis the impact of highly deleterious nsSNPs on the stability of the KRAS protein

We employed I-Mutant 2.0 40, MUpro 41, INPS-MD 42, iStable 43, and DDMut 44 to predict the impact of highly deleterious nsSNPs on KRAS stability and structural integrity.

I-MUTANT 2.0 predicts the effect of amino acid substitutions on protein stability by estimating changes in Gibbs free energy (ΔΔG), where values below 0 kcal/mol indicate a decrease in stability. MUpro employs both support vector machine (SVM) and neural network-based models for stability prediction, similarly interpreting ΔΔG values below 0 kcal/mol as indicative of destabilizing effects. Sequence and structural information are combined using INPS-MD to evaluate ΔΔG; mutations that produce values less than −0.5 kcal/mol are categorized as destabilizing. Likewise, DDMut evaluates changes in protein stability and flexibility resulting from missense mutations, considering mutations with ΔΔG values below −0.5 kcal/mol as indicative of reduced stability. iStable also employs SVM-based algorithms to assess the impact of mutations on protein stability.

Assessment of the structural and functional impact of highly deleterious nsSNPs on KRAS protein

The structural impact of highly deleterious nsSNPs on the KRAS protein were analyzed using MutPred2 45, an online tool that predicts the pathogenicity of amino acid substitutions based on their impact on protein structure and function. The KRAS protein sequence, in FASTA format, along with the specific amino acid substitutions, was submitted to MutPred2. Predictions with a g-score less than 0.5 were considered “pathogenic”, while those with a g-score below 0.5 were classified as “non-pathogenic”. To further investigate the structural consequences of the highly deleterious nsSNPs, we utilized the HOPE server 46, a user-friendly web-based tool that provides detailed analyses of the structural and functional effects of point mutations. In addition, Our findings were additionally validated and strengthened by Missense3D 47, which ensures the robustness of the results by anticipating structural changes caused by amino acid substitutions. The structural impact of missense variations on the structure of transmembrane proteins is predicted using Missense3D.

Mapping of highly deleterious nsSNPs in functional domains of KRAS protein

The InterPro tool 48 was employed to identify the location of highly deleterious nsSNPs within the conserved domains of the KRAS protein. InterPro integrates data from multiple databases, including Pfam, PRINTS, and PROSITE, to identify functional regions potentially affected by nsSNPs within protein families, domains, and functional sites 49.

Predicting the relationship between cancer susceptibility and high-risk nsSNPs

To determine the oncogenic potential of the high-risk nsSNPs, the predictive tools CScape 50, Dr. Cancer 51, and FATHMM 52, were used. CScape utilizes a statistical framework to predict cancer-driving mutations, demonstrating a balanced accuracy of 91% in coding regions of the human genome. Its predictions are represented as p-values ranging from 0 to 1, where values above 0.5 suggest oncogenic potential, while values below 0.5 indicate a likely neutral or benign classification. Dr. Cancer is a machine Learning-based Predictor of Cancer-causing SNPs; predicting based on a score > 0.5 indicates disease (oncogenic). FATHMM is a high-throughput web server designed to predict the functional impact of both coding variants, such as non-synonymous single nucleotide variants (nsSNVs), and non-coding variants across the human genome. For FATHMM, predictions were interpreted using a threshold of −0.75, with values below this threshold indicating potential cancer-cause.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis

The STRING database (v12.0) was used to study protein-protein interaction (PPI) 53. STRING is a comprehensive database and analytical tool that compiles both known and predicted protein–protein interactions. Interaction scores generated by STRING represent confidence levels, indicating the likelihood that a predicted interaction is biologically meaningful based on current evidence. In this study, the organism was specified as Homo sapiens, and “KRAS” was used as the query input to identify potential interacting partners. Analyzing PPI data is essential, as mutant proteins may exert sustained effects on their interacting partners, thereby contributing to the dysregulation of cellular processes in disease states. Elucidating these interactions enhances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying various clinical conditions and supports the identification of key source proteins and their associated interaction networks 54, 55.

Homology modeling and validation

Although several high-resolution crystal structures of wild-type KRAS are available in the Protein Data Bank, experimentally determined structures for the Y71D and M72K variants were not available. Therefore, homology modeling was employed to generate mutant protein structures suitable for comparative structural, docking, and molecular dynamics analyses. The wild-type KRAS crystal structure (PDB ID: 4DSO) was used as the template for model generation, and the resulting mutant structures were subsequently subjected to quality assessment before downstream analyses. We used the web-based program SWISS-MODEL 56 to model the 3D homology of the mutant and wild-type KRAS proteins. SWISS-MODEL was chosen for protein modeling due to its reliable template selection, automated interface, updated database, and built-in validation tools. While alternatives like MODELLER offer more customization, SWISS-MODEL provides an effective balance of accuracy, efficiency, and accessibility 57. SWISS-MODEL facilitated the construction of high-confidence three-dimensional models for both the wild-type and mutant forms, which were subsequently used for downstream structural and functional analyses.

These models were validated by comprehensive evaluation of both wild-type and mutant protein structures through various quality assessment parameters, using ProSA-web (Z-score) 58, QMEAN (Z-score) 59, ERRAT 60, and Ramachandran plots 61 analysis via the SAVES server.

Molecular docking study

Molecular docking (Blind docking) analysis was performed to investigate the binding affinities and interaction profiles of two KRAS-associated ligands with wild-type KRAS and its mutant variants Y71D and M72K 62. In this study, AutoDock Vina (v1.2.1) was employed to assess the impact of high-risk nsSNPs on the binding affinity of the KRAS protein (UniProt ID: P01116) 63. AutoDock Vina utilizes a Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA) in combination with a semi-empirical free energy force field to estimate binding interactions 64.

The analysis focused on three KRAS protein structures: the wild-type and two mutant variants harboring nsSNPs. Two ligands (sotorasib and adagrasib) were selected due to established KRAS inhibitors, predicted binding affinities, and reported bioactivities 65. Both the wild-type and mutant KRAS structures were docked against these ligands. Ligand files were converted to PDBQT format using AutoDock tools, and docking grid parameters were optimized to ensure efficient and accurate binding site coverage 66. Blind molecular docking was performed to allow unrestricted exploration of potential ligand-binding sites across the entire KRAS protein surface. The docking grid was defined to encompass the full protein structure, enabling the identification of energetically favorable binding poses without prior assumptions regarding the binding pocket. The designated grid box: Dimensions (Angstrom) of X: 54.9474 Y: 50.5474 Z: 72.4968; with a center of X: -6.9886 Y: 0.6858 Z: 1.3786 Å, and the exhaustiveness is 8. The resulting docking conformations and protein-ligand interactions were analyzed and visualized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio (v21.1.0) 67.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using Desmond v24 (Schrödinger LLC) to validate the ligand-protein interactions predicted through molecular docking analysis 68. MDS employs Newtonian mechanics to simulate atomic motions over time, generating successive configurations at fine temporal intervals. This method provides dynamic insights into the stability, flexibility, and interaction patterns of both wild-type and mutant forms of the KRAS protein in association with certain ligands by enabling the assessment of ligand-binding behavior under settings that are close to physiological 69, 70.

Prior to simulation, the ligand-receptor complexes were prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard, which facilitated structural optimization, energy minimization, and the addition of missing residues. System setup was performed using the System Builder tool, employing the TIP3P explicit solvent model within an orthorhombic box. Simulations were carried out using the OPLS_2005 force field under standard physiological conditions (temperature: 300 K; pressure: 1 atm) to ensure biological relevance 71, 72. Each simulation, spanning 100 ns, was executed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 GPU and required approximately 8 h per run. The resulting trajectories were analyzed using the Simulation Interaction Diagram module, enabling detailed assessment of atomic fluctuations and interaction stability over time 73. To mimic physiological ionic strength, each system was neutralized with counterions and supplemented with 0.15 M NaCl.

Calculation of binding free energy (MM-GBSA)

The binding free energies (ΔG_bind) of the protein–ligand complexes were calculated using the Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) approach as implemented in the Prime module of the Schrödinger Maestro software suite 74. For each system, representative conformations were selected from equilibrated molecular dynamics trajectories and subjected to post-processing energy evaluation. This method integrates molecular mechanics energy components, including bonded, electrostatic, and van der Waals interactions, with an implicit solvent model (VSGB) to account for solvation effects.

Results

Retrieval of nsSNPs of KRAS gene

We retrieved nsSNPs in the KRAS gene from three publicly available databases: NCBI dbSNP, ClinVar, and DisGeNET. An initial total of 419 missense variants were identified from the dbSNP database, while 195 KRAS-associated missense variants were obtained from ClinVar. Additionally, 21 missense mutations related to KRAS were retrieved from DisGeNET. Among these, 12 variants overlapped with entries in other databases due to multiple clinical submissions or reannotations, resulting in the identification of 9 unique variants from DisGeNET. A comprehensive comparison and overlap analysis of the variants from all three databases yielded a final set of 324 unique nsSNPs in the KRAS gene. The selection of these tools was determined by their reported accuracy and availability during the study period. The highly deleterious nsSNPs were further analyzed using five stability analysis tools, namely, I-Mutant 2.0, iStable, DDMut, MUpro, and INPS-MD. I-Mutant 2.0 predicted 15 nsSNPs with ΔΔG values below 0.00 kcal/mol, indicating a potential decrease in KRAS protein stability. MUpro predicted 16 nsSNPs with ΔΔG values below 0.00 kcal/mol, also suggesting reduced protein stability. INPS-MD classified 5 nsSNPs as destabilizing (ΔΔG < −1 kcal/mol), 3 nsSNPs as weakly destabilizing (−1 ≤ ΔΔG < −0.5 kcal/mol), 1 nsSNP as stabilizing (ΔΔG > 1 kcal/mol), and 7 nsSNPs as neutral (−0.5 ≤ ΔΔG ≤ 0.5 kcal/mol). iStable identified 15 nsSNPs as decreasing, based on confidence scores greater than 0.00. Lastly, DDMut identified 11 nsSNPs as destabilizing, with ΔΔG values below 0.00 kcal/mol (Table 2). Based on the combined results of these predictive analyses, 7 nsSNPs (Y71D, V152F, Y32D, F141I, V103D, F82I, and M72K) were identified as being associated with reduced protein stability and were selected for further investigation.

Assessment of the structural impact of highly deleterious nsSNPs on KRAS protein

The structural impact of highly deleterious nsSNPs on the KRAS protein was evaluated using the HOPE server, which provides insights into the structural consequences of amino acid substitutions, including alterations in size, charge, hydrophobicity, spatial configuration, and functional implications. The analysis identified 13 highly deleterious nsSNPs, including Y71D, D126Y, D92Y, D33V, D33Y, Y32D, D30Y, I21R, V103D, M72K, R68M, R68W, and D54Y, which were predicted to affect the physicochemical properties of the KRAS protein, including alterations in size, charge, and hydrophobicity. In contrast, the variants V152F, F141I, and F82I were found to only induce changes in size. Notably, 6 variants (Y71D, D126Y, D92Y, R68M, R68W, and D54Y) were predicted to disrupt hydrogen bonding within the KRAS protein structure. Furthermore, 10 variants (D126Y, D92Y, D33V, D33Y, Y32D, D30Y, V103D, R68M, R68W, and D54Y) were associated with disturbances in ionic interactions. Additionally, 4 variants (Y32D, I21R, V103D, and M72K) were found to potentially result in the loss of hydrophobic interactions with surrounding molecules at the protein surface. These findings are summarized in Table 3 and detailed in Table S2.

We further analyzed these 16 highly deleterious nsSNPs using the MutPred2 server. The MutPred2 server result reveals that all 16 deleterious nsSNPs scored greater than 0.5, indicating strong pathogenic potential, as shown in Table 3. A detailed summary of these findings is presented in Table S3.

Subsequently, the Missense3D tool was utilized to assess structural disruptions induced by amino acid substitutions. Missense3D analysis revealed that Y71D replaces a buried uncharged residue (TYR, RSA 4.9%) with a charged residue (ASP) and leads to the expansion of cavity volume by 86.616 Å^3. M72K replaces a buried hydrophobic residue (MET, RSA 0.0%) with a hydrophilic residue (LYS, RSA 0.0%) and replaces a buried uncharged residue (MET, RSA 0.0%) with a charged residue (LYS). However, mutations V152F, D126Y, D92Y, D33V, D33Y, Y32D, D30Y, I21R, F141I, V103D, F82I, R68M, R68W, and D54Y showed no structural damage. These findings are summarized in Table 3 and detailed further in Table S4.

Following a comprehensive structural analysis of highly deleterious nsSNPs in the KRAS protein, the Y71D and M72K variants emerge as the most likely high-risk mutations. These substitutions exhibit substantial alterations in amino acid size, charge, and hydrophobicity, as predicted by Project HOPE. In addition, Missense3D identified clear structural disruptions, including the introduction of buried charges, hydrophilic residues, and cavity alterations, which are indicative of destabilizing effects on the protein's three-dimensional conformation. Furthermore, both variants yielded high MutPred2 scores (greater than 0.95), reinforcing their potential pathogenicity. Collectively, these converging lines of evidence strongly suggest that Y71D and M72K are high-risk nsSNPs with a significant likelihood of impairing KRAS protein structure and function, potentially contributing to disease-associated mechanisms.

Mapping of high-risk nsSNPs in the functional domains of KRAS

InterPro analysis identified a single functional domain in KRAS spanning residues 1–165, corresponding to the GTP-binding domain responsible for GTP binding and downstream effector interactions. Key structural motifs include the P-loop (residues 10–16), Switch I (30–40), Switch II (59–76), and the core effector region (32–40), which collectively facilitate phosphate binding, effector recognition, and GAP interactions. The C-terminal hypervariable region (166–189) mediates membrane localization and post-translational modifications. All 16 highly deleterious nsSNPs mapped to the GTP-binding domain, with D30Y, Y32D, D33V, and D33Y located in Switch I/core effector regions, and R68M, R68W, Y71D, and M72K in Switch II. Notably, the high-risk variants Y71D and M72K reside in Switch II, potentially impacting effector binding and catalytic function (Fig. 2).

Molecular dynamic simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of 100 ns duration were carried out to investigate the binding mechanism, structural dynamics, and conformational flexibility of wild-type and mutant KRAS in complex with the selected compounds (sotorasib and adagrasib). Analysis of the simulation trajectories revealed distinct structural variations among the complexes. The structural stability and flexibility of the KRAS–ligand complexes were evaluated using RMSD and RMSF profiles. The RMSD provided insights into overall conformational stability, while RMSF highlighted residue-level flexibility changes. For sotorasib, the WT complex exhibited the lowest RMSD (∼1.5 Å) throughout the 100 ns simulation, while the Y71D and M72K mutants showed higher deviations, reaching ∼3.0 Å and ∼ 2.5 Å, respectively (Fig. 6A). RMSF profiles further supported this trend, as Y71D displayed the highest flexibility, with fluctuations up to ∼3.0 Å, followed by M72K (∼2.5 Å), whereas WT maintained comparatively lower fluctuations of ∼1.0–1.5 Å (Fig. 6C). In contrast, for adagrasib, the mutant complexes (Y71D and M72K) were more stable, maintaining RMSD values around ∼1.0–1.5 Å, while the WT complex reached deviations of ∼3.0 Å (Fig. 6B). RMSF analysis confirmed this observation, with WT exhibiting residue fluctuations up to ∼3.5 Å, whereas Y71D and M72K showed reduced flexibility, remaining below ∼1.5 Å across most regions (Fig. 6D).

Molecular dynamic simulation. A) RMSD for sotorasib complexes with WT, Y71D, and M72K; B) RMSD for adagrasib complexes with WT, Y71D, and M72K; C) RMSF for sotorasib complexes with WT, Y71D, and M72K; D) RMSF for adagrasib complexes with WT, Y71D, and M72K.

The compactness and surface exposure of the KRAS–ligand complexes were evaluated using Rg and SASA analyses. Rg reflected compactness of the protein structures, and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) demonstrated ligand-induced alterations in protein surface exposure. For sotorasib, the WT complex displayed the lowest Rg (∼1.9 Å), suggesting a more compact structure compared to the Y71D (∼2.6–3.0 Å) and M72K (∼2.4 Å) mutants, which exhibited greater structural expansion (Fig. 7A). Correspondingly, SASA analysis revealed that WT showed higher surface exposure, fluctuating between ∼420–520 Å2, with a marked increase after 80 ns, whereas the mutants maintained comparatively lower SASA values (∼380–440 Å2) (Fig. 7C). For adagrasib, the opposite trend was observed. The mutant complexes exhibited lower Rg values (∼1.5–2.0 Å), indicating greater structural compactness, while the WT complex showed higher Rg (∼2.5–3.0 Å) and greater fluctuations throughout the trajectory (Fig. 7B). Similarly, SASA values were consistently higher for the WT complex (∼400–500 Å2), whereas Y71D and M72K mutants maintained reduced solvent exposure (∼360–420 Å2) (Fig. 7D).

Calculation of binding free energy (MM-GBSA)

Ligand–receptor binding stability was further evaluated using MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations. The ΔGbind analysis revealed notable differences in binding affinities between wild-type and mutant complexes for both sotorasib and adagrasib. In the case of sotorasib, the wild-type complex exhibited the most favorable binding free energy (−60.27 kcal/mol), indicating stronger and more stable interaction compared to the mutant variants Y71D (−38.71 kcal/mol) and M72K (−36.91 kcal/mol). A similar trend was observed in the individual energetic components, where hydrogen bonding (ΔGbind H-bond), lipophilic interactions (ΔGbind Lipo), and van der Waals forces (ΔGbind vdW) were all markedly reduced in the mutant complexes, reflecting diminished binding stability.

Conversely, for adagrasib, an opposite trend was observed, where the Y71D mutant showed enhanced binding affinity (−55.78 kcal/mol) compared to both the wild-type complex (−27.05 kcal/mol) and the M72K mutant (−38.33 kcal/mol). This improvement was also reflected across stabilizing interactions, particularly lipophilic and van der Waals contributions, suggesting mutation-specific enhancement of binding in this system. Overall, these results indicate that the Y71D and M72K substitutions differentially modulate ligand binding, leading to either reduced or enhanced binding stability depending on the ligand context (Table 7).

Discussion

This study employed an integrated in silico approach to identify and characterize deleterious KRAS nsSNPs with structural, pathological relevance in lung cancer, myeloma, and gastric cancer and how the structural and functional alterations caused by this mutation influence ligand binding. From 324 missense variants, 16 were consistently predicted as highly deleterious across eight functional algorithms, with Y71D (rs387907205) and M72K (rs2141509775) prioritized for detailed analysis as high-risk. Stability and structural predictions from multiple tools (I-Mutant 2.0, iStable, MUpro, INPS-MD, DDMut, HOPE, MutPred2, and Missense3D) indicated that both variants destabilize KRAS and induce notable conformational changes. Located within the GTP-binding domain (residues 1–165), these substitutions showed negative ΔΔG values (<0 kcal/mol), confirming their high-risk destabilizing impact on protein stability and potential functional impairment 75, 76.

MutPred2 analysis indicated that all 16 highly deleterious KRAS nsSNPs, including Y71D and M72K, have g-scores >0.5, suggesting high pathogenic potential. HOPE predictions revealed that these substitutions alter amino acid properties, including size, charge, and hydrophobicity, potentially destabilizing KRAS structure and interactions. In Y71D, the bulky aromatic tyrosine is replaced by a smaller, negatively charged aspartic acid, disrupting hydrophobic packing and electrostatic balance 77, 78. In M72K, the nonpolar methionine is substituted with positively charged lysine, perturbing the local hydrophobic core and introducing new electrostatic interactions, which may affect local folding and secondary structure stability 79, 80. Cancer susceptibility analyses indicated that both high-risk nsSNPs (Y71D and M72K) are oncogenic, disease-associated, and potentially cancer-promoting. These findings suggest that the presence of these variants may contribute to cancer development and progression.

Although Y71D and M72K are not among the classical KRAS hotspot mutations frequently observed at codons 12, 13, and 61, emerging evidence suggests that rare non-hotspot KRAS variants may also influence protein function, signaling activity, and therapeutic responses. Both Y71D and M72K are located within the functionally critical GTP-binding domain, particularly near the Switch II region involved in conformational regulation and effector interactions. Therefore, structural perturbations caused by these variants may alter KRAS activity through mechanisms distinct from those associated with canonical hotspot mutations. Nevertheless, their epidemiological prevalence and clinical significance remain largely unexplored and warrant further experimental and population-based investigations.

The GTP-binding domain of KRAS regulates GDP/GTP exchange and intrinsic GTP hydrolysis, controlling downstream signaling critical for cell proliferation and survival 81. Our analysis identified two high-risk nsSNPs (Y71D and M72K) within this domain, which may disrupt GTP binding, conformational dynamics, and ligand interactions, potentially leading to aberrant signaling and increased cancer risk 10, 82. Network analysis using Cytoscape revealed KRAS as a central hub interacting with key partners, including BRAF, RAF1, PIK3CA, SOS1, RALGDS, and calmodulin-related proteins (CALM3, CALML3–6), connecting upstream activators and downstream effectors 83, 84. High betweenness and closeness centrality scores further underscore its pivotal role in maintaining signal transduction fidelity 85. Missense mutations in the GTP-binding domain may perturb this network, promoting sustained oncogenic activation and dysregulated cellular proliferation 10, 86.

In the absence of crystal structures, SWISS-MODEL was used to generate 3D models of wild-type and mutant KRAS variants, which were validated using ProSA-web, QMEAN, ERRAT, and Ramachandran plots. These structural alterations may allow KRAS to adopt alternative conformations, potentially enhancing interactions with specific ligands or partners and modulating binding affinity and functional activity 87. Docking results revealed that KRAS mutations Y71D and M72K exert contrasting effects on ligand binding. For sotorasib, strong affinity was observed with the wild-type protein (−8.7 kcal/mol), but both substitutions significantly reduced binding (−6.8 and − 6.6 kcal/mol, respectively). This decline likely stems from the replacement of hydrophobic residues with charged/polar ones (Asp in Y71D, Lys in M72K), which disrupt local packing, weaken hydrophobic stabilization, and perturb the electrostatic microenvironment of the binding pocket. These changes are consistent with prior evidence linking missense KRAS mutations to structural rearrangements that impair inhibitor efficacy 86, 88. In contrast, adagrasib displayed weaker binding to wild-type KRAS (−5.6 kcal/mol) but improved affinity for both mutants (−7.2 for Y71D and − 6.9 for M72K). This suggests that the mutations create favorable electrostatic or hydrogen-bonding interactions with the introduced residues, thereby enhancing recognition 89, 90. The gain of ∼1.3–1.6 kcal/mol indicates a mutation-specific preference, echoing earlier reports that certain KRAS variants form novel interaction sites that increase ligand selectivity. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as docking scores represent simplified energy approximations and do not directly translate into therapeutic efficacy or clinical responsiveness. In addition, both sotorasib and adagrasib were originally developed as covalent inhibitors targeting the KRAS G12C mutation within the Switch II pocket; therefore, their evaluation against non-hotspot variants such as Y71D and M72K is exploratory in nature. To address this, we further emphasize that Y71D and M72K are located within the GTP-binding domain, including proximity to the Switch II region (residue 59–76), which plays a key role in conformational regulation and effector interaction. Thus, substitutions at residues 71 and 72 may indirectly influence local structural dynamics and alter the geometry or accessibility of the Switch II pocket, potentially affecting ligand accommodation. Nevertheless, these docking results are hypothesis-generating and require validation through molecular dynamics, binding free energy analysis, and experimental binding or functional assays to confirm any biologically relevant differences in inhibitor sensitivity.

MD simulations further validated these trends. Sotorasib remained most stable with wild-type KRAS (RMSD ∼1.5 Å), while mutant complexes showed higher deviations (∼2.5–3.0 Å) and flexibility, reflecting destabilization of hydrophobic contacts essential for stability 91, 92, 93. Conversely, adagrasib exhibited enhanced stability in the mutant complexes (RMSD ∼1.0–1.5 Å, RMSF <1.5 Å), while the wild-type complex remained unstable (∼3.0 Å RMSD; ∼3.5 Å RMSF). This suggests that mutations create an electrostatic environment favorable to this ligand, consistent with earlier findings of mutation-induced cryptic pocket formation 94, 95. Compactness (Rg) and solvent exposure (SASA) analyses corroborated these patterns: sotorasib stabilized the wild-type complex (Rg ∼1.9 Å; SASA 420–520 Å2) but destabilized mutants, whereas adagrasib induced compact, solvent-shielded conformations in mutants (Rg ∼1.5–2.0 Å; SASA 360–420 Å2). These results highlight that KRAS point mutations exert ligand-specific effects on binding energetics, underscoring the importance of mutation-specific drug design and confirming the value of molecular docking and dynamic simulations in revealing conformational plasticity and ligand-induced stabilization in GTPases 96, 97.

Ligand–protein interactions were further evaluated using MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations to validate the docking and molecular dynamics results. The analysis showed that the wild-type KRAS–sotorasib complex exhibited the most favorable binding affinity, whereas the Y71D and M72K variants displayed markedly reduced ΔG_bind values, indicating weakened binding of sotorasib upon mutation. In contrast, adagrasib demonstrated enhanced binding affinity toward the Y71D variant compared with the wild-type KRAS, suggesting a mutation-dependent improvement in ligand recognition and stability. Across all complexes, van der Waals and lipophilic interactions were the dominant contributors to binding stability, while hydrogen-bond contributions remained comparatively minor. Overall, these results, consistent with docking and simulation analyses, indicate that KRAS mutations differentially influence ligand binding energetics, significantly modulating the stability and affinity of sotorasib and adagrasib complexes.

Although the use of multiple stability prediction tools (I-Mutant 2.0, MUpro, INPS-MD, DDMut, and iStable) enhances analytical robustness, the resulting ΔΔG values remain computational estimates rather than experimentally validated measures of protein stability. Therefore, concordant predictions of destabilization should be interpreted as indicative of potential structural perturbation rather than definitive evidence of functional impairment. Similarly, structural annotations derived from HOPE and Missense3D, including “buried charge introduction” and “cavity alteration,” describe predicted physicochemical consequences of amino acid substitutions but do not, on their own, confirm pathogenicity or biological dysfunction. These results should thus be regarded as supportive and hypothesis-generating, and require further biochemical and experimental validation to establish their true functional and clinical significance.

Despite the comprehensive computational analyses performed in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although blind molecular docking was employed to enable unbiased exploration of potential ligand-binding sites, redocking validation and RMSD comparisons with experimentally determined ligand-bound structures were not performed. Consequently, the predicted binding poses and affinity estimates should be interpreted as preliminary computational predictions. Another limitation is MD simulations provide useful insights into KRAS–ligand dynamics; however, they were performed as single trajectories, limiting reproducibility. Thus, RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and SASA results should be interpreted cautiously as indicative trends rather than definitive conclusions. In addition, lack of replicate runs, convergence assessment, and hydrogen bond occupancy analysis are acknowledged limitations, and future studies should include these for more robust validation. Although the use of eight in silico prediction tools strengthens variant prioritization through consensus-based filtering, it is important to acknowledge that many of these methods rely on similar principles, particularly evolutionary conservation and physicochemical substitution scores. Therefore, agreement across tools may reflect correlated algorithmic bias rather than fully independent validation, potentially inflating confidence in predicted deleterious variants. To address this, we applied a strict criterion requiring concordance across all tools; however, this may also exclude borderline variants with functional relevance. Thus, the identified nsSNPs should be considered high-confidence candidates for further experimental validation rather than definitive pathogenic mutations. Furthermore, the biological effects of the prioritized KRAS variants require validation through biochemical and cellular experiments to confirm their impact on protein function, signaling activity, and therapeutic response. Ongoing efforts using patient cohorts, genotyping, and allele-specific PCR aim to validate these findings, enabling stratification of high-risk variants for targeted interventions and establishing a framework for precision therapeutics focused on KRAS structural alterations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive in silico characterization of deleterious KRAS nsSNPs with potential pathological relevance in multiple cancers. Among 324 missense variants, Y71D and M72K were identified as high-risk mutations based on consistent predictions from multiple functional, structural, and stability algorithms. Both variants are located within the GTP-binding domain and were shown to destabilize KRAS structure, induce conformational alterations, and potentially disrupt GTP-mediated signaling. Molecular docking, MD simulations, and MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations revealed mutation-dependent ligand binding behavior, where the high-risk nsSNPs reduced affinity and stability for one ligand (sotorasib) while enhancing interactions with another (adagrasib), highlighting the ligand-specific consequences of KRAS mutations. Cancer susceptibility analyses further supported the oncogenic and disease-associated nature of these variants, suggesting their possible contribution to cancer development and progression. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of mutation-specific structural effects in KRAS-driven oncogenesis and emphasize the need for precision drug design targeting distinct KRAS variants. Although the results are based on computational predictions, they provide a strong framework for future experimental validation and may aid in the identification of prognostic markers and personalized therapeutic strategies for KRAS-associated cancers.
