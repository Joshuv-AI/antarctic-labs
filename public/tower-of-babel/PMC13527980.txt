Unrecognized intraoperative thermal injury to the foot following arthroscopic anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction: a case report
Authors: Chun-Chieh Yeh, Han-Ying Wang, Chung-Hsun Chang, Wen-Tung Hsieh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerioperative burn or pressure–thermal skin injury is a rare but preventable complication of surgery. Although burns related to warming devices or electrosurgical instruments have been reported, such injuries following arthroscopic anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction are exceedingly uncommon.Case presentationWe report an unrecognized perioperative deep partial-thickness skin injury involving the dorsum of the foot, most consistent with a thermal or combined pressure–thermal injury, following revision arthroscopic ACL reconstruction under general anesthesia. A pneumatic tourniquet was applied for 120 min. A forced-air warming system was used for upper-body warming without direct contact with the operative limb, which was loosely covered with a stockinette without additional padding. Shortly after transfer from the post-anesthesia care unit to the ward, a 8 × 4 cm deep partial-thickness skin injury was identified on the ipsilateral dorsum of the foot. Conservative wound care was initiated, and the lesion gradually epithelialized over approximately three months. The injury resulted in transient soft-tissue contracture with plantar flexion restricted to approximately 30°, interfering with early postoperative rehabilitation. At 9 months after surgery, the wound had fully healed, ankle range of motion had recovered to 15° of dorsiflexion and 40° of plantar flexion, and the patient reported no resting pain, shoe-wear difficulty, or limitation in daily activities.ConclusionClinically significant perioperative deep partial-thickness skin injury can occur during routine knee arthroscopy even in the absence of an obvious direct thermal source. This case suggests that local pressure, drape-related insulation, impaired distal heat dissipation due to tourniquet use, and retained ambient heat may interact to produce occult injury. This case highlights system-level prevention opportunities, including distal extremity padding, avoidance of heat trapping under drapes, and routine whole-limb skin inspection in the post-anesthesia care unit and after ward transfer to facilitate early recognition of occult injuries.

Introduction

Arthroscopic anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction is commonly performed in sports traumatology and is generally considered safe, although reported complications include infection, thromboembolism, graft failure, postoperative stiffness, and other procedure-related adverse events [1]. Perioperative burn injury is an uncommon but preventable complication of surgical care and has been associated with electrosurgical devices, radiofrequency instruments, fiberoptic light cables, patient-warming systems, chemical preparation solutions, and operating-room fire events [2–12].

In arthroscopic surgery, heat-related complications have been reported mainly in association with radiofrequency and electrocautery devices, with dermal burns and chondrolysis being among the most frequently described adverse events [3]. Cutaneous burns caused by fiberoptic light cables have also been reported during knee arthroscopy, emphasizing that thermal injury may occur outside the primary intra-articular surgical field [4, 5]. Patient-warming devices are another recognized source of perioperative thermal injury, particularly when used improperly, positioned incorrectly, or when heat is retained beneath surgical drapes and insulating materials [6–8]. Chemical burns related to antiseptic solution pooling beneath tourniquets have also been described in orthopedic procedures, including ACL reconstruction [10, 11].

Here, we report a rare case of an unrecognized perioperative deep partial-thickness skin injury of the foot identified shortly after arthroscopic ACL reconstruction performed under general anesthesia. Notably, no peripheral nerve block or conventional open-surgery electrosurgery (bipolar electrocautery) was used, and warming was applied only briefly without direct coverage of the operative limb. This case highlights an underrecognized perioperative risk and underscores the importance of careful postoperative inspection of distal extremities. To our knowledge, reports of perioperative deep partial-thickness skin injury of the foot following ACL reconstruction without an identifiable direct heat source are exceedingly rare.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old male presented with progressive instability of the right knee that had worsened over the preceding five years. He had undergone primary anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction of the same knee 18 years earlier following a sports-related injury. Despite conservative management, he continued to experience recurrent episodes of giving way during daily activities. Physical examination was consistent with ACL insufficiency, with positive Lachman and pivot-shift tests; therefore, revision ACL reconstruction was indicated.

The patient had no significant medical comorbidities and denied diabetes mellitus, peripheral vascular disease, neuropathy, or smoking. His body mass index was 25 kg/m², and he was classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I. Revision arthroscopic double-bundle ACL reconstruction using an allograft was performed under general anesthesia without peripheral nerve block. No conventional open-surgery electrosurgery (bipolar electrocautery) was used during the procedure. The total operative time was approximately 150 min.

The patient was positioned supine, with the operative knee flexed and hanging freely over the edge of the operating table. The operative foot was intentionally kept suspended near the edge of the table and was not in contact with the table edge, positioning devices, metallic components, or any external heat source. The distal limb was only loosely covered with a stockinette, without additional padding, tight wrapping, or thermal insulation. A pneumatic tourniquet was applied to the ipsilateral proximal thigh and inflated to 300 mmHg for 120 min without intermittent deflation; no intraoperative assessment of dorsalis pedis circulation was documented.

Perioperative warming was provided using a forced-air warming system (3 M™ Bair Hugger™), set at 38 °C, with an upper-body blanket. The warming setup was intended for upper-body coverage, and no forced-air warming component was positioned at or directed toward the lower extremities. The arthroscopy light source and associated equipment were positioned near the patient’s abdomen rather than near the foot; given standard handling of delicate and valuable instruments, they were not placed at the distal end of the table. Although inadvertent displacement is theoretically possible, there was no indication that the light source or cables were positioned at the foot side during the procedure. Skin preparation was performed using Hibitane (chlorhexidine with 75% alcohol) according to the routine protocol, and no obvious pooling of the antiseptic solution around the distal foot was noted intraoperatively. Surgical draping was applied in the usual manner without excessive compression of the distal extremity. The surgery was completed without intraoperative complications.

At the conclusion of the procedure, the lower limb was wrapped with elastic bandages and supported with a knee brace. Upon arrival at the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), the patient’s core body temperature was within normal limits, and the warming device was discontinued approximately 10 min later. No abnormality of the foot was documented during the initial PACU assessment. The lesion was first recognized only after transfer to the ward, highlighting a missed opportunity for earlier postoperative whole-limb inspection. The affected area measured approximately 8 × 4 cm and was assessed as a deep partial-thickness skin injury. Plastic surgery was consulted, and conservative management was initiated, including local cooling/ice application and topical burn ointment. The injury was managed with ongoing topical therapy and regular wound care without the need for surgical debridement or skin grafting (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6).

The wound gradually epithelialized over approximately three months without the need for surgical debridement or skin grafting. During the wound-healing period, scar tightness over the dorsum of the foot temporarily restricted ankle motion, with plantar flexion limited to approximately 30°, interfering with early postoperative knee rehabilitation. At the final follow-up of 9 months after surgery, the wound had completely healed without recurrent ulceration, infection, or neurovascular compromise. Ankle range of motion had recovered to dorsiflexion of 15° and plantar flexion of 40°. The patient reported no resting pain, no shoe-wear difficulty, and no limitation in independent activities of daily living. Knee rehabilitation was resumed after wound stabilization, and no graft-related complication was noted. A summary of the perioperative course and wound-healing timeline is provided in Table 1.

Discussion

Perioperative burn or pressure–thermal skin injury is an uncommon but recognized complication of surgical care. Most reported cases are associated with electrosurgical devices, radiofrequency probes, dispersive electrode pads, or patient warming systems, typically involving direct contact, prolonged exposure, equipment malfunction, or technical misuse [2]. In arthroscopic surgery, thermal injuries are reported predominantly in shoulder procedures, whereas reports involving knee arthroscopy—particularly anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction—are exceedingly rare [3].

Although direct thermal sources were not used in the present case, perioperative burns may occur even in the absence of electrocautery. Arthroscopic light sources and fiberoptic cables can generate substantial heat at their distal tips and have been implicated in cutaneous burns during knee arthroscopy when inadvertently in contact with drapes or skin [4, 5]. However, no such contact was documented in this case.

Patient-warming devices represent another recognized source of perioperative thermal injury. Forced-air warming systems are widely used and are generally considered safe when used according to manufacturer instructions and perioperative warming guidelines. Nevertheless, burns have been reported in association with forced-air warming because of misuse, improper positioning, prolonged exposure, or heat retention beneath drapes and other insulating materials [6–8]. In the present case, the upper-body warming blanket did not directly cover the operative foot, but indirect heat retention beneath drapes cannot be completely excluded.

To clarify this proposed indirect mechanism, we added a schematic illustration of the intraoperative setup (Figs. 7 and 8). The forced-air warming blanket was applied to the upper body and did not directly contact or cover the operative foot. However, surgical drapes may limit ventilation and allow warm air or retained ambient heat to accumulate beneath the draped field. In this setting, the stockinette-covered distal foot may have been exposed to a localized warm, poorly ventilated microenvironment. Although this does not prove that the forced-air warmer was the primary cause, it provides a plausible route by which upper-body warming could have contributed indirectly, particularly when combined with local pressure or insulation and tourniquet-related impairment of distal heat dissipation.

Tourniquet use may further contribute to susceptibility to thermal injury. Prolonged inflation can reduce distal perfusion, impair heat dissipation, and increase tissue vulnerability to pressure-related ischemia [9]. In our patient, a tourniquet was inflated to 300 mmHg for 120 min without intermittent deflation. While this duration is not extraordinary for revision ACL reconstruction, it may still have reduced the ability of the distal extremity to dissipate heat.

The relatively linear wound margins in this case warrant specific consideration. In our routine ACL reconstruction setup, the foot is not circumferentially wrapped with Coban or any tight adhesive dressing. Nevertheless, the linear configuration suggests that a contact-related factor may have contributed to the injury. Although no direct heat-generating device or tight wrap was applied around the foot, localized pressure or contact from a stockinette edge, stockinette fold, or drape fold cannot be excluded. The dorsum of the foot has minimal soft-tissue coverage and may be particularly vulnerable to pressure-related ischemia, local insulation, and impaired heat dissipation. Under general anesthesia, the patient could not perceive discomfort or reposition the limb, allowing local pressure or thermal stress to persist unnoticed. In combination with prolonged tourniquet-related distal hypoperfusion, these factors may have increased tissue susceptibility to a combined pressure–thermal injury.

Chemical burns related to antiseptic solutions must also be considered in the differential diagnosis. Cases of partial-thickness burns beneath pneumatic tourniquets following ACL reconstruction have been attributed to pooled povidone-iodine solution or alcohol-based preparations [10, 11]. However, the lesion in the present case was located on the dorsum of the foot rather than beneath the tourniquet, and there was no documentation of solution pooling or a characteristic chemical-burn pattern, making this mechanism less likely.

Taken together, the most plausible explanation for the present injury is not a single direct thermal source, but rather a multifactorial process involving localized contact or pressure from a stockinette or drape fold, insulation beneath surgical drapes, reduced distal perfusion and impaired heat dissipation due to prolonged tourniquet inflation, and possible retained ambient heat from perioperative sources such as the forced-air warming system. The relatively linear wound margins further support the possibility of a contact-related component. The principal limitation of this report is the inability to identify a single definitive heat source; thus, the proposed mechanism remains inferential and was likely multifactorial. The clinical importance of this complication lies not only in its rarity but also in its functional consequences. Although the wound healed without surgery, the resulting soft-tissue contracture temporarily impaired ankle motion and interfered with early postoperative rehabilitation. This is especially relevant after ACL reconstruction, where timely mobilization and structured rehabilitation are central to functional recovery. Early mobilization is a recognized component of postoperative recovery pathways and is associated with improved functional outcomes and fewer secondary complications [1]. The burn in this case therefore had consequences extending beyond the skin lesion itself.

More broadly, perioperative burns should be considered within the same preventable patient-safety paradigm as operating room fires. A recent analysis of operating room fires emphasized that these events are preventable and are commonly related to interactions among heat sources, oxidizers, fuels, equipment use, and workflow-related factors [12]. Although the present case did not involve an operating room fire, it similarly highlights the importance of system-level prevention, careful management of thermal sources, avoidance of heat retention beneath drapes, and routine postoperative inspection of areas outside the primary surgical field.

From a patient-safety perspective, this case highlights several preventive opportunities. Distal extremities outside the operative field should still be regarded as vulnerable areas and should be adequately padded, particularly when prolonged tourniquet use is anticipated. Excessive insulation or drape-related heat trapping around dependent body parts should be avoided. Warming devices should be positioned carefully and used strictly according to instructions, and arthroscopic light cables should never be left illuminated in contact with the patient or drapes [2, 4, 5, 8]. Finally, postoperative skin inspection should include the entire limb rather than only the operative site, both in the post-anesthesia care unit and again after transfer to the ward, to facilitate earlier recognition of occult injury.

Conclusions

Perioperative deep partial-thickness skin injury may occur after routine arthroscopic ACL reconstruction even without an obvious direct thermal source. In this case, the injury was most consistent with a multifactorial pressure–thermal mechanism involving localized stockinette/drape contact, drape-related insulation, tourniquet-related impairment of distal heat dissipation, and possible retained ambient heat. Careful padding of dependent distal extremities and routine postoperative whole-limb inspection may improve early recognition and patient safety.
