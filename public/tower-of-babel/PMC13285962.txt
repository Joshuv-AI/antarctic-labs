Proteomic Mendelian randomization and machine learning reveal causal plasma biomarkers in cardiorenal comorbidity
Authors: HaiQuan Huang, Wenkang Liu, YaNan Yan, Juan Cao, JiePeng Wang, ChaoYi Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) significantly increases the risk of coronary artery disease (CAD), but the causal plasma proteins linking these conditions are poorly understood. This study aimed to identify genetically validated plasma biomarkers and therapeutic targets for this cardiorenal link.MethodsWe integrated multi-omics data using a Mendelian randomization (MR) framework. The analysis incorporated protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) data and gene expression datasets for CAD and CKD. A mediation MR approach identified plasma proteins that genetically mediate the causal pathway from CKD to CAD. Machine learning, including Boruta feature selection and support vector machine (SVM) modeling, was used to refine a diagnostic panel from the candidate proteins.ResultsAnalysis identified six key causal mediator proteins: LIMA1, PLCG1, and PZP (which promoted CAD risk in CKD), along with HGF, SERPINE2, and TXNDC15 (which exerted protective effects). A diagnostic panel based on these proteins showed strong predictive performance. Functional analysis indicated these proteins converge on lipid-inflammation pathways and modulate immune responses. Molecular docking suggested imatinib as a high-affinity binder to HGF, indicating drug repurposing potential.ConclusionThis study defines a genetically anchored plasma protein panel that mechanistically links CKD to CAD via a lipid-inflammation axis. The findings support the development of precision blood-based tools for early risk stratification and highlight potential targets for therapeutic intervention in cardiorenal disease.Keywords: Cardiorenal comorbidity, causal plasma biomarkers, Mendelian randomization, proteomic pQTL analysis, machine learning diagnostics, lipid–inflammation axis

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), a major global health burden, is defined as kidney damage or a glomerular filtration rate (GFR) of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 for three months or more, irrespective of cause [1]. It is characterized by a progressive decline in kidney function, leading to the accumulation of toxins and metabolic waste in the body, and may ultimately progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). According to epidemiological data, the global prevalence of CKD is approximately 9.5% [2]. Mounting evidence indicates that CKD is associated not only with impaired renal filtration but also with abnormal lipid metabolism, activation of inflammatory pathways, and elevated oxidative stress [3–5]. Consequently, patients with CKD frequently develop coronary artery disease (CAD), a narrowing or blockage of the coronary arteries, typically due to the buildup of plaque [6], which results in a markedly increased risk of cardiovascular events, particularly among those with advanced renal impairment. The mortality from heart disease is substantially higher in individuals with CKD than in those without renal dysfunction [7]. Moreover, CKD is recognized as an independent predictor of poor prognosis in CAD [8], while CAD, primarily caused by atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries, leads to myocardial ischemia and is a major contributor to myocardial infarction and heart failure. Studies have identified CAD as the leading single cause of death worldwide [9]. The high comorbidity rate and the complex, bidirectional interactions between these two diseases are particularly noteworthy [10]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying CKD-CAD comorbidity remain complex and incompletely understood. Thus, investigating the pathogenic link between CKD and CAD holds substantial clinical significance. Against this backdrop, artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) technologies offer powerful tools for deciphering the complex mechanisms underlying CKD-CAD comorbidity. By integrating multimodal healthcare data—such as medical imaging, pathological sections, genomics, and clinical parameters—AI/ML models can identify early biomarkers and risk patterns that are difficult to detect using conventional methods, significantly improving the accuracy of disease prediction and subtyping. These advancements not only promote the shift toward dynamic and personalized diagnosis and treatment of kidney diseases, but also provide critical methodological support for elucidating the molecular pathways of cardiorenal interactions through multi-omics data integration and causal inference [11].

Proteins are complex biomolecules that result from intricate interactions between genetic and environmental factors. Proteomics, the comprehensive study of the composition, structure, function, and interactions of proteins within biological systems, has emerged as a cornerstone discipline in modern life sciences. With ongoing technological advancements, proteomics has become an indispensable tool in biomedical research, showing considerable promise for the early diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of diverse diseases [12]. Among various biological sources, plasma proteins are particularly advantageous because they are easily obtainable and exhibit strong clinical relevance, making them ideal candidates for studying disease mechanisms [13]. However, the specific protein molecules commonly involved in both CKD and CAD remain largely unknown. It also remains unclear whether proteins contributing to CKD progression could serve as predictive biomarkers for CAD [14]. Therefore, applying advanced proteomic technologies to develop a novel diagnostic model for cardiorenal comorbidity is of great significance [15].

The lipid-inflammation axis describes the interaction between lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses, which plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of multiple diseases, particularly CAD and CKD. Dysregulated lipid metabolism frequently coexists with chronic inflammation, a key pathological feature and a major driving force in both CAD and CKD. Recent studies indicate that the lipid-inflammation axis modulates lipid-mediated inflammatory cascades, thereby disrupting cardiovascular and renal homeostasis [16,17]. In the pathogenesis of CAD, dyslipidemia and inflammation are well-established contributing factors. Lipid accumulation not only accelerates atherosclerosis but also amplifies inflammatory signaling, thereby accelerating disease progression [18,19]. Similarly, abnormal lipid metabolism in CKD is closely linked to persistent systemic inflammation, which worsens renal injury and accelerates functional decline [20,21]. Activation of the lipid-inflammation axis induces synergistic damage to both the renal and cardiovascular systems, ultimately leading to poorer clinical outcomes [22]. Consequently, elucidating the mechanisms underlying the lipid-inflammation axis in CAD-CKD comorbidity may yield novel insights and therapeutic targets to improve patient prognosis.

This study integrated protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) data for over 4,000 plasma proteins with GWAS data based on tissue-specific mRNA expression profiles in the heart and kidney. Using multiple bioinformatics approaches, including bidirectional MR between the proteome and transcriptome, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, and gene enrichment analysis, combined with Boruta–SVM machine learning for cross-validation across two cohorts, we systematically identified protein biomarkers that mediate the comorbidity of CKD and CAD, offering new insights and potential strategies for precision therapeutic strategies for cardiorenal diseases. In summary, this study integrates MR, pQTL proteomics, shared transcriptomics, machine learning, immune infiltration profiling, and molecular docking to identify causal biomarkers of CAD-CKD comorbidity.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and data sources

The GWAS summary statistics for CKD were obtained from the CKDGen consortium (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/), comprising a sample of 452,264 individuals of European ancestry [23]. The chronic kidney disease (CKD) GWAS summary statistics were obtained from the CKDGen consortium, which primarily includes individuals with mild to moderate CKD stages and explicitly excludes end-stage renal disease (ESRD) or dialysis patients. Summary-level data for coronary artery disease (CAD) were retrieved from the IEU Open GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk), accession number ebi-a-GCST005195, comprising 122,733 CAD cases and 424,528 controls [24]. This case includes a composite endpoint of myocardial infarction, stable or unstable angina, and significant stenosis confirmed by coronary angiography. In addition, cis-pQTLs were defined as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located within 1 Mb of the gene encoding the corresponding plasma protein. The pQTL dataset was derived from the DECODE database (https://www.decode.com/) based on the 2021 study by Ferkingstad et al. which included 35,559 participants and quantified 4907 plasma proteins [25]. Gene expression data were downloaded from the GEO database. GSE113079 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE113079) contained blood microarray data from 93 patients with CAD and 48 healthy controls (CTLs), generated using the GPL20115 platform. GSE66494 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE66494) included blood microarray data from 53 patients with CKD and 8 CTLs, profiled on the GPL6480 platform (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [26] (Table 1, Supplemental Table S1).

Mendelian Randomization (MR) has emerged as a powerful approach for inferring causal relationships in observational data [27]. To ensure the robustness of our causal inferences, we adhered to the core principles and appraisal checklist outlined in specialized guidelines for MR studies in nephrology [27]. Analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR R package [28]. Specifically, we estimated (i) the forward causal effect of CKD on CAD, (ii) the reverse causal effect of CAD on CKD, and (iii) the causal effects of plasma protein pQTLs on CAD. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified from the GEO datasets were integrated with plasma proteomic data to identify 230 proteins potentially associated with cardiorenal comorbidity, which were subsequently subjected to MR analysis. Core proteins were identified through sensitivity analyses, including heterogeneity testing (Cochran’s Q test) and pleiotropy assessment using the MR-Egger intercept test. A mediation model was used to quantify the three-step causal chain linking CKD to CAD via plasma proteins. Specifically, the effect of CKD on plasma proteins (β2) and the effect of plasma proteins on CAD (β1) were estimated, and the mediated effect was expressed as the product (β1 × β2). The proportion of mediation was calculated as (β1 × β2)/β_total, thereby delineating the “CKD–Plasma Protein–CAD” causal pathway. Six diagnostic proteins (LIMA1, PLCG1, HGF, PZP, SERPINE2, and TXNDC15) identified through machine learning were validated for their mediating roles using MR within the CKD–diagnostic protein pathway. In addition, functional enrichment analyses (Gene Ontology [GO] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes [KEGG]), protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction via the STRING database, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)-based immune infiltration profiling, and molecular docking simulations were performed to systematically investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying cardiorenal comorbidity and to identify potential therapeutic targets (Figure 1).

Research flowchart for CKD-CAD comorbidity analysis, covering data integration, causal mediation, machine learning-based biomarker identification, and drug target prediction.

All analyses were conducted using five MR methods: inverse variance weighting (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode. Among these, IVW served as the primary analytical approach [29,30]. In the mediation analysis, CKD was treated as the exposure variable, and plasma protein pQTLs were treated as the outcome variable. When the number of instrumental variables (IVs) exceeded three, all five methods were applied, with IVW prioritized for inference. Based on a three-step MR framework, the mediation effect was defined as the product of the causal effect of CKD on plasma proteins (β2) and the causal effect of plasma proteins on CAD (β1). The direct effect was calculated by subtracting the mediation effect from the total effect, and the proportion mediated was calculated as the ratio of the mediation effect to the total effect [30]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the causal estimates. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (Q_pval > 0.05 indicating no significant heterogeneity) [31], while horizontal pleiotropy was tested using both the MR-Egger intercept (p > 0.05) and the MR-PRESSO global test [32]. Leave-one-out analysis [28] and Steiger directionality tests [30] were performed to further validate the consistency and direction of causality. Selection of valid IVs adhered strictly to the three fundamental assumptions of MR—relevance, independence, and exclusion restriction—to ensure methodological rigor [29,30].

Functional enrichment analysis

To better understand the biological functions of the causal proteins, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler package. Enrichment analysis of the target protein set was performed with clusterProfiler (version 4.12.0) and the annotation package org.Hs.eg.db (version 3.19.1) [33], focusing primarily on the GO [34] and KEGG [35] databases. Enrichment results were considered statistically significant when the adjusted p-value (p.adjust) was below 0.05.

Construction of the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network

To identify potential interactions among the causal genes, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) [36], applying a minimum interaction confidence score threshold of 0.1. Further module and clustering analyses were performed using the Metascape platform to explore functional relationships among the identified proteins.

Machine learning algorithms

This study utilized the caret package (version 6.0-94) to train a support vector machine classifier with a radial basis function kernel (SVM-RBF) [37], which is effective in handling nonlinear data. Feature importance was evaluated using permutation-based methods implemented in the DALEX package (version 2.4.3), and candidate proteins were selected based on their importance scores. To ensure robust and unbiased feature selection, the Boruta algorithm (version 8.0.0) was further applied for comprehensive feature screening across the entire feature space [38]. Boruta generates randomized shadow features to establish a significance threshold and retains features classified as Confirmed (those exhibiting importance scores significantly higher than random noise (p < 0.01)). Finally, diagnostic models were constructed using the intersecting features identified by both approaches, specifically proteins ranked within the top 20% by DALEX-based feature importance scores and confirmed by Boruta.

GeneMANIA and GSEA

The GeneMANIA database (https://genemania.org/) was utilized to analyze interactions among the diagnostic proteins, thus predicting associations based on co-localization, shared protein domains, co-expression, genetic interactions, and pathway relationships [39]. Diagnostic proteins were classified into high and low-expression groups based on their median expression, and DEGs were identified using the limma package with thresholds of |log2FC| > 0.263 and p < 0.05. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was then performed using clusterProfiler (version 4.14.4), ranking genes by log2FC and referencing the KEGG pathway database. Pathways with p.adjust < 0.05 were considered statistically significant [40].

ssGSEA immune infiltration analysis

Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was used to quantify enrichment scores for specific immune cell gene sets in each sample. This approach utilizes curated immune gene signatures to estimate the relative abundance and distribution of immune cell subsets within bulk transcriptomic profiles. The ssGSEA analysis was conducted using the IOBR package (version 0.99.8), which calculates sample-specific immune infiltration scores across 28 predefined immune cell types. Pearson correlation analysis (|r| > 0.4, p < 0.01) was performed to evaluate associations between immune cell infiltration patterns and diagnostic protein expression [41].

Target prediction and molecular docking

The Drug–Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb) was used to identify potential drug candidates targeting the diagnostic proteins. Molecular docking simulations were then performed to assess the binding affinities between the core active compounds and diagnostic proteins. Three-dimensional (3D) structures of the diagnostic proteins were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) and converted into PDBQT-format receptor files using AutoDockTools after adding charges and merging non-polar hydrogens. In addition, structures of the core active compounds were retrieved from PubChem, converted into 3D conformations, and exported as PDBQT-format ligand files. Docking simulations were carried out using AutoDock Vina to calculate binding free energies. Visualization and structural analyses were conducted with PyMOL and Discovery Studio [42].

Justification for threshold selection methods in the analysis

Differentially Expressed Gene (DEG) cutoff: |log2FC| > 0.263

The threshold of |log2FC| > 0.263 (corresponding to a |FC| ≥ 1.2) for screening differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was selected based on biological rationale and field consensus. This cutoff aims to balance sensitivity and specificity, enabling the capture of subtle yet stable expression changes while avoiding the omission of key signals with an overly high threshold or the introduction of noise with an overly low one. This specific threshold was successfully applied in the study by Ren et al. [43], where they used this criterion, combined with an adjusted p-value < 0.05, to identify 831 DEGs and construct a high-accuracy diagnostic model for ischemic stroke (AUC 0.81–0.933), thereby validating its reliability. In the current cardiorenal syndrome study, adopting this same threshold ensures comparability with published literature and helps focus on functionally relevant genes (e.g. those involved in inflammation and fibrosis pathways). This supports subsequent machine learning and pathway analyses, enhancing the study’s rigor and interpretability.

DALEX feature importance threshold: Top 20%

Selecting the top 20% of features based on importance scores calculated using the DALEXpackage is a widely adopted strategy in interpretable machine learning to balance model performance, complexity, and interpretability. DALEX uses permutation importance, where the contribution of each feature is estimated by measuring the drop in model performance (e.g. accuracy) when its values are randomly shuffled. A larger drop indicates greater feature importance. The top 20% cutoff enables the model to retain its most influential predictors while reducing data dimensionality, filtering out redundant or noisy variables, and improving both interpretability and computational efficiency. Relative importance ranking is generally more stable than absolute importance values, especially in high-dimensional biological datasets. In complex disease contexts such as cardiorenal syndrome, this strategy allows identification of key biomarkers (e.g. specific genes or proteins) that drive pathological processes, providing a clear basis for subsequent biological validation and mechanistic exploration [44].

STRING PPI network confidence score: > 0.1

For protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction using the STRING database, a confidence score cutoff > 0.1 was selected to balance coverage and reliability. STRING integrates multiple sources of evidence—experimental validation, co-expression, and text mining—to compute interaction confidence scores. A threshold > 0.1 allows for the inclusion of all potentially biologically meaningful interactions, including those with lower confidence, thereby helping to avoid missing genuine weak but functionally relevant associations within signaling pathways. For instance, in the study by Bai et al. [45], the authors used this threshold to preliminarily screen the PPI network of potential targets for EPX granules in treating functional dyspepsia. They subsequently refined the core targets (e.g. EGFR, CTNNB1, NFκB1) through topological analysis (e.g. degree centrality, closeness centrality), ensuring enhanced specificity in downstream analysis while retaining broad interaction information. This approach is particularly suitable for exploratory research or mechanistic studies of multi-component treatments, as it helps capture more candidate pathways (e.g. PI3K/AKT, inflammatory response), providing a solid foundation for further experimental validation.

In summary, the selection of these three thresholds reflects both methodological robustness and biological relevance. The |log2FC| criterion emphasizes sensitivity and comparability; the DALEX top 20% rule prioritizes model interpretability and focus; and the STRING confidence score ensures comprehensive yet reliable network construction. Together, these parameters strengthen the analytical validity and scientific rigor of the study.

Results

MR analysis of CKD and CAD

Using 891 stringently selected genetic IVs (p < 5 × 10−8, kb > 10,000, and r2 < 0.001), MR analysis demonstrated that CKD is associated with an elevated risk of CAD (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.033–1.047) (Figure 2(A)). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the causal inference, indicating no heterogeneity (Q test, p = 1.000), no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept, p = 0.750), and a well-supported causal directionality (Steiger test, p < 1 × 10–15). In contrast, reverse MR analysis, conducted using 126 harmonized SNPs, showed no evidence of a causal effect of CAD on CKD (OR = 0.999, 95% CI: 0.962–1.037) (Figure 2(B)). All supporting figures and tables are available in the supplementary materials (Figure S1; Table S2).

Two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) results showing causal effects of CKD on CAD and sensitivity analyses confirming inference robustness.

Shared DEGs between CKD and CAD

Differential expression analysis of CAD versus CTL samples identified 7,326 DEGs based on thresholds of p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.263 [43]. Among these, 3,404 genes were upregulated, and 3,922 were downregulated in CAD (Figure 3(A,B)). Similarly, comparison between CKD and CTL samples yielded 6,095 DEGs, including 4,709 upregulated and 1,386 downregulated genes (Figure 3(C,D)). Intersection analysis of upregulated and downregulated DEGs across both diseases identified 3,906 shared DEGs, suggesting overlapping molecular signatures that may underlie the comorbidity of CKD and CAD (Figure 3(E)).

Volcano plots, heatmaps, and an UpSet plot revealing shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between CKD and CAD.

MR analysis of shared plasma protein pQTLs and CAD

By intersecting the plasma proteins corresponding to the shared DEGs, a total of 230 proteins related to CKD–CAD comorbidity were identified (Figure 4(A)). A total of 9,053 SNPs were selected based on pQTL data from the DECODE database (p < 5 × 10−8, kb > 10,000, and r2 < 0.001). After harmonization using the harmonise_data function, 5,864 valid IVs were retained. MR analysis identified 25 plasma proteins with a genetically causal association with CAD (Figure 4(B,C)). After sensitivity analysis, 15 proteins were retained as genetically causal candidates for CAD (Figure 4(B,C)). The robustness of these causal associations was supported by sensitivity analyses, which revealed no evidence of heterogeneity (Q test, p > 0.05), no horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global test, p > 0.05), and strong support for causal directionality based on the Steiger test (p < 1 × 10−10) (Table 2). Furthermore, the scatter plot showed intercepts of IVs close to zero, and the funnel plot appeared symmetric without outliers, indicating minimal bias among the instruments (Figure 4(D); Supplemental Figs. S2–S26).

Functional enrichment analysis and PPI network of core proteins

GO enrichment analysis revealed that the 15 proteins were significantly associated with 208 functional terms (FDR < 0.05), including 174 under the biological process category. Key enriched terms included the regulation of the smoothened signaling pathway (Figure 5(A–D); Supplemental Figs. S27–29). KEGG pathway analysis further revealed that these proteins were collectively involved in seven critical signaling pathways (Figure 5(E); Figure S30; Table S3). PPI analysis, performed using a confidence threshold of 0.1, identified interaction pairs among 10 of the 15 proteins. The resulting interaction network, visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.8.2) (Figure 5(F)), highlighted HGF and SERPINE2 as nodes with the highest degree centrality, underscoring their potential regulatory importance within the network. Functional annotation showed that these proteins were predominantly enriched in signaling pathways associated with organ development and pathophysiological processes involving coagulation and fibrosis. Notably, the HGF-SERPINE2 interaction hub appeared to serve as a central mediator in the progression of cardiorenal fibrosis. Overall, the enriched pathways were organized into two major functional clusters: (1) developmental pathways involved in angiogenesis and tissue morphogenesis, and (2) pathological pathways associated with thrombosis, inflammatory responses, and fibrotic remodeling.

Functional enrichment and PPI network of MR-validated plasma proteins in CKD-CAD.

Selection and evaluation of diagnostic proteins

Candidate diagnostic proteins were identified via cross-validation using the Boruta algorithm and an SVM model with a radial basis function (SVM-RBF) in independent CAD and CKD cohorts (Figure 6). A five-fold cross-validation strategy was employed, wherein the dataset was randomly divided into five equal subsets. During each iteration, four subsets were used for model training and one for validation, such that each subset served once as the validation set. The final performance was calculated as the average of all five validation results. Based on this strategy, six robust diagnostic biomarkers were identified: LIMA1, PLCG1, HGF, PZP, SERPINE2, and TXNDC15. Based on its suboptimal performance (AUC = 0.685, below the 0.7 threshold) in the CAD cohort, SPINT2 was excluded from the final diagnostic panel. This decision was based strictly on the pre-defined performance threshold. In the CAD cohort, the AUC values of the selected proteins ranged from 0.759 (LIMA1, PLCG1, TXNDC15) to 0.804 (SERPINE2). In contrast, the CKD cohort demonstrated markedly higher predictive performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.887 (SERPINE2) to 0.995 (PLCG1), the latter indicating near-perfect diagnostic accuracy (Table 4). A nomogram model constructed using these six biomarkers (Figure 7) enabled quantitative estimation of disease risk. For instance, a total score of 392 corresponded to a 99.9% probability of CAD, whereas a score of 305 corresponded to an approximately 100% probability of CKD. This model offers a clinically applicable tool for visualizing individual risk and facilitating early diagnosis.

MR analysis of CKD–diagnostic protein pQTLs

MR analysis demonstrated robust causal associations between genetically predicted CKD and six plasma diagnostic proteins: LIMA1 (OR = 1.065, 95% CI: 1.058–1.072), PZP (OR = 1.053, 95% CI: 1.046–1.061), and PLCG1 (OR = 1.046, 95% CI: 1.040–1.053) were identified as genetic risk factors for CAD; whereas SERPINE2 (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.983–0.998), TXNDC15 (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.973–0.986), and HGF (OR = 0.956, 95% CI: 0.950–0.960) acted as protective factors (Figure 8(A)). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings, indicating no significant heterogeneity (Cochran’s Q test, p > 0.05), no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO tests, p > 0.05), and no directional bias (Steiger test, p < 1 × 10−10). The validity of IVs was further supported by scatter plots and funnel plots (Supplemental Figs. S31–36; Table S4). Mediation analysis revealed that LIMA1, PZP, and PLCG1 mediated the positive causal effect of CKD on CAD, while HGF, SERPINE2, and TXNDC15 mediated protective effects, collectively constituting the causal pathway of “CKD–plasma protein–CAD” (Figure 8(B); Table 3).

ssGSEA of immune infiltration and diagnostic proteins

The ssGSEA of immune infiltration revealed distinct immune landscape alterations in both CAD and CKD. In CAD, seven pro-inflammatory immune cell populations, including dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and neutrophils, exhibited significantly increased infiltration scores (p < 0.01), whereas ten lymphocyte subsets, such as CD8+ T cells and cytotoxic cells, showed decreased infiltration scores (Figure 11(A). Correlation analysis between key diagnostic proteins and immune cell abundance indicated disease-specific immune associations. Drug–protein interaction prediction and molecular docking of diagnostic proteins

Potential drug–protein interactions were predicted using the DGIdb, which identified multiple drugs targeting HGF, PLCG1, and PZP (Table 5, Figure 12(A)). Among these, compounds with the highest “interaction_group_score” were selected for molecular docking analyses. The three-dimensional crystal structures of the proteins and the corresponding small-molecule ligands were obtained for docking simulations (Table 6). The docking results demonstrated stable interactions between the selected compounds and their protein targets. In HGF, three amino acid residues—TYR-369, ARG-331, and SER-378—formed hydrogen bonds with imatinib mesylate, yielding a docking affinity of −9.7 kcal/mol (Figure 12(B)). In PLCG1, three residues (GLN-659, GLU-548, SER-665) formed hydrogen bonds with phosphomethylphosphonic acid adenosyl ester, resulting in a docking affinity of −6.7 kcal/mol (Figure 12(C)). For PZP, two residues (SER-92, ASN-41) formed hydrogen bonds with norethindrone, with a docking affinity of −8.3 kcal/mol (Figure 12(D)). These docking interactions suggest that HGF, PLCG1, and PZP may serve as potential therapeutic targets for pharmacological modulation in cardiorenal comorbidity, warranting further experimental validation.

Discussion

Background and pathogenic mechanisms of the lipid-inflammation axis

Recent advances in microarray and high-throughput sequencing technologies have markedly enhanced the understanding of molecular features and pathogenic mechanisms underlying complex diseases [46,47]. Meanwhile, the integration of bioinformatics with machine learning has become an increasingly powerful strategy for identifying novel pathogenic genes, diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, elucidating disease mechanisms, and guiding targeted therapeutic development [48,49].

The lipid-inflammation axis serves as a critical bridge in the comorbidity of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and coronary artery disease (CAD). This bidirectional pathogenic loop arises from the interplay between lipid metabolism disorders and chronic inflammation, which together exacerbate cardiorenal injury. In CKD, impaired renal function leads to defective lipid clearance, causing elevated triglycerides, reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels, and accumulation of atherogenic low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particles [50]. These dyslipidemic changes promote lipid deposition in renal and vascular tissues, triggering lipotoxic injury and macrophage activation, with subsequent overproduction of inflammatory mediators like IL-6 and TNF-α [51]. Inflammation further amplifies lipid abnormalities by impairing lipoprotein metabolism and increasing oxidized lipid species [52]. Clinical evidence highlights biomarkers such as oxidized LDL and hs-CRP for early risk identification [53]. Therapeutic strategies like statins, which combine lipid-lowering and anti-inflammatory effects, target this axis [54]. CAD exacerbates the pathology through ischemia-induced hypoxia, activating RAAS and sympathetic pathways, which promote vasoconstriction and oxidative stress, advancing renal fibrosis and atherosclerosis [55]. This creates a positive feedback loop, where CAD-related hypoxia worsens lipid-inflammation dysregulation, accelerating CKD progression [56]. Thus, the axis integrates hemodynamic, metabolic, and immune responses, underpinning bidirectional microenvironment-dependent regulation in cardiorenal comorbidity.

To integrate the aforementioned mechanisms, we propose a conceptual framework termed the ‘CKD→Plasma Protein→CAD Lipid-Inflammation Mediation Axis’ (Fig. S51), which delineates how dysregulated lipid metabolism and chronic inflammation synergistically exacerbate cardiorenal injury via specific plasma protein mediators. This model provides a foundational roadmap for interpreting the causal roles of the identified protein biomarkers (e.g. LIMA1, HGF) in bridging CKD-driven metabolic dysfunction to CAD progression.

Research methodology and key findings

In this study, we performed a three-stage MR analysis integrating transcriptomic and pQTL data, combined with the Boruta and SVM algorithms, to identify six core plasma proteins mediating cardiorenal comorbidity (LIMA1, PLCG1, HGF, PZP, SERPINE2, and TXNDC15). A diagnostic nomogram model constructed from these biomarkers demonstrated excellent predictive performance across independent cohorts. Immune infiltration analysis further revealed distinct immune microenvironment profiles in CAD and CKD. Specifically, CAD was characterized by the activation of myeloid inflammation—evidenced by elevated macrophage, neutrophil, and Th17 cell scores—along with CD8+ T cell exhaustion. In contrast, CKD displayed a broad suppression of lymphocyte subsets, including T and natural killer (NK) cells, consistent with the immunodeficiency typically observed in uremic conditions. Collectively, these results suggest that the six core proteins may contribute to disease progression through the differential regulation of immune cell phenotypes—for instance, HGF appears to promote macrophage infiltration while attenuating CD8+ T cell function—thereby linking immune dysregulation to lipid metabolic imbalance and chronic inflammation. These findings provide a foundation for elucidating the core molecular mechanisms and the remodeling of the immune microenvironment underlying cardiorenal comorbidity, thus offering theoretical support for the development of targeted diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic strategies. Notably, molecular docking analysis demonstrated a strong binding affinity between imatinib and HGF (binding energy = −9.7 kcal/mol), offering structural and computational evidence to support drug repurposing strategies.

Clinical implications and therapeutic potential

The clinical management of CAD in patients with CKD has long been constrained by diagnostic and therapeutic limitations. Diagnostic approaches have traditionally relied on single indicators—such as eGFR to assess renal function or coronary artery calcium scoring to evaluate vascular lesions [57], while treatment strategies have primarily focused on non-organ-specific interventions, such as lipid-lowering statins [58]. This compartmentalized approach overlooks the shared pathophysiological mechanisms underpinning cardiorenal interactions, leading to inadequate risk stratification and delayed therapeutic intervention. Furthermore, therapeutic choices remain limited for patients with advanced CKD, who are frequently excluded from major cardiovascular outcome trials. As a result, cardiovascular management in CKD is often extrapolated from non-CKD populations, diminishing its clinical applicability [59]. By integrating transcriptomic and plasma proteomic genetic data, this study provides a novel MR-validated multi-protein biomarker signature involving a six-protein panel (LIMA1, PLCG1, HGF, PZP, SERPINE2, and TXNDC15) with robust diagnostic potential. Machine learning–based feature selection validated its strong discriminative capacity in both CAD (AUC = 0.759–0.804) and CKD (AUC = 0.887–0.995) cohorts. This plasma protein panel represents a shift from isolated organ assessment toward a coordinated, multi-target diagnostic strategy. The use of quantifiable circulating biomarkers offers a more comprehensive and biologically grounded approach for the early identification of individuals at high risk of developing cardiorenal comorbidity.

Dyslipidemia has long been recognized as a shared risk factor for both CAD and CKD [60]. Therapeutic strategies targeting lipid metabolism have dual benefits in cardiorenal comorbidities: statins not only reduce the risk of cardiovascular events in CKD patients but also improve renal function [61]; combination therapy with statins and fenofibrate further decreases the risk of fatal heart failure in patients with diabetes [62]; and agents such as ezetimibe and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors provide additional cardiorenal protection [63]. These findings suggest that dysregulation of lipid metabolism may be a core mechanism underlying cardiorenal comorbidity. Our GeneMANIA network analysis identified six core diagnostic proteins—LIMA1, PZP, PLCG1, SERPINE2, TXNDC15, and HGF—that cooperatively regulate the sterol/cholesterol biosynthetic process, forming a key hub in cardiorenal crosstalk. This pathway integrates lipid imbalance, oxidative stress, and inflammation, exemplified by HGF-mediated lysosomal degradation and PLCG1-driven inhibition of inflammatory signaling.

Several studies have shown that small-molecule compounds have achieved breakthroughs in clinical application. Due to their favorable tissue penetration, tunable half-life, and excellent oral bioavailability, small molecules offer distinct pharmacological advantages and represent a highly promising therapeutic strategy [64]. Our multi-target molecular docking analysis highlights the feasibility of precision therapy for cardiorenal comorbidity. Using drug-gene interaction predictions from the DGIdb database and molecular docking performed with AutoDock Vina (Tables 5–6; Figure 12), we identified high-affinity binding between the core diagnostic proteins HGF, PLCG1, and PZP and several small-molecule compounds (binding energy ≤ −8.3 kcal/mol). These findings suggest several potential mechanisms of action. First, the cardiorenal protective mechanism of the HGF–imatinib interaction: imatinib forms a stable hydrogen-bond network with HGF’s binding pocket at residues TYR-369, ARG-331, and SER-378 (Figure 12(B), with a binding energy of −9.7 kcal/mol. Konijnenberg et al. demonstrated that imatinib significantly reduces myocardial infarction size by preserving microvascular integrity [65]. Our docking results corroborate this observation, suggesting that imatinib may exert dual protective effects on the heart and kidneys through modulation of the HGF/c-Met signaling pathway. Specifically, imatinib may act via a twofold mechanism—activating lysosomal–autophagy pathways in the heart to mitigate ischemic injury (Figure 10), while attenuating renal fibrotic remodeling by targeting the HGF/c-Met pathway (Figure 6–7). This “dual-target, single-drug” mechanism provides a unique therapeutic window for the treatment of cardiorenal comorbidity. The previously demonstrated microvascular protection of imatinib, together with our docking-based evidence for its multi-target potential, provides a foundation for cardiorenal intervention.

The regulatory role of macrophage polarization and immune microenvironment in cardiorenal comorbidity

For decades, macrophage polarization has been recognized as a key determinant in the pathogenesis of both CKD and CAD. In CKD, infiltrating M1-type macrophages promote renal interstitial fibrosis by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, while M2-type macrophages contribute to tissue repair—an established concept supported by numerous studies [66,67]. Similarly, earlier studies on CAD have reported highly similar mechanisms, suggesting that macrophage dysfunction may disrupt immune tolerance in CAD patients, thereby accelerating disease progression. Modulating macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype has been shown to improve cardiovascular health and mitigate the development of atherosclerosis [68]. This cross-organ paradigm of macrophage polarization thus provides mechanistic support for the proposed “cardiorenal lipid-inflammation axis.” In this study, immune infiltration analysis revealed potential protein-mediated modulation of macrophage functional plasticity. In the CAD group (Figure 11(A/C)), macrophage infiltration scores were significantly increased and positively correlated with HGF, SERPINE2, and TXNDC15 (r > 0), suggesting that these proteins may promote macrophage activation. Conversely, macrophage scores exhibited significant negative correlations with PLCG1 and PZP (r < 0), implying suppressive effects on macrophage activity.

Comprehensive immune infiltration profiling (Figure 11(A–B)) further revealed that in the CAD group, myeloid immune cell populations—including macrophages, neutrophils, and T helper 17 (Th17) cells—were markedly increased, whereas cytotoxic lymphocytes, particularly CD8+ T cells, were significantly reduced. This pattern reflects the classical paradigm of a myeloid inflammation–dominated atherosclerotic microenvironment [69]. CD8+ T cell exhaustion, a phenomenon known to underlie the failure of cancer immunotherapy and autoimmune diseases [70], has also been implicated in atherosclerosis. Transcriptomic profiling of CD8+ T cells from carotid atherosclerotic plaques has revealed molecular signatures of exhaustion [71]. Consistent with this, Iqneibi et al. reported that CD8+ T cells in CAD patients were significantly enriched in five exhaustion-related pathways, including suppression of T cell receptor (TCR) signaling, programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) signaling, the role of myocyte enhancer factor 2D (MEF2D) in T cell apoptosis, TCR-mediated apoptotic regulation, and mitochondrial apoptosis. Additionally, the exhaustion-associated genes PRDM1 and LAG3 were markedly overexpressed in these cells [72]. In the CKD group, extensive lymphocyte depletion was observed, with T and NK cells reduced by over 90%. This pattern is similar to previous findings from observational studies in CKD patients, especially those with uremia, where significant immune dysfunction has been documented [73]. Moreover, Wu et al. reported signs of premature T cell senescence in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD), with those experiencing cardiovascular comorbidities showing elevated levels of senescence markers p16 and p21. Furthermore, T cell aging in MHD patients was linked to hyperlipidemia and the accumulation of uremic toxins, suggesting that lipid metabolism may serve as a key therapeutic target. This observation aligns with our findings that six core plasma proteins identified in this study regulate the lipid–inflammation axis underlying cardiorenal comorbidity [74]. Together, these results support the hypothesis that cardiorenal syndromes may exhibit distinct immune subtypes: CAD is characterized by myeloid inflammation, while CKD is marked by lymphocyte exhaustion. This immunological divergence may offer a theoretical framework for developing stratified management strategies and precision immunomodulatory therapies for cardiorenal comorbidities. Furthermore, studies have shown that the PD‑1/PD‑L1 axis directly contributes to pathological fibrosis in cardiorenal organs by regulating macrophage‑to‑myofibroblast transdifferentiation (M2F) and extracellular matrix deposition [75]. Although our bioinformatics analysis did not directly enrich for the PD‑1 signaling pathway, the concurrent dysregulation of the Ras, MAPK, Rap1, and calcium signaling pathways—all established upstream inducers or downstream effectors of PD‑1—provides a robust molecular link to PD‑1 axis activation. We therefore speculate that the PD‑1 signaling axis may represent a key driver underlying both T‑cell exhaustion and the progression of cardiorenal fibrosis.

Mechanisms and therapeutic implications of core plasma proteins mediating cardiorenal comorbidity via modulation of the lipid-inflammation axis

Notably, the known role of LIMA1 in intestinal cholesterol absorption [76] aligns with our findings on its regulatory function in lipid metabolism, thus further supporting the central role of lipid dysregulation in cardiorenal disease. Based on these insights, we propose that targeting the lipid–inflammation axis (for instance, modulating LIMA1-mediated cholesterol metabolism) may serve as a novel therapeutic strategy for simultaneously mitigating cardiac and renal injury. This approach not only builds on the established clinical benefits of lipid-lowering therapy but also provides a mechanistic foundation for the development of precision-targeted interventions in cardiorenal syndrome.

As a multi-domain protein activated through proteolytic cleavage, HGF regulates critical processes, including cell migration, proliferation, and apoptosis. In response to tissue injury, upregulated HGF expression activates anti-apoptotic pathways, promotes angiogenesis, and enhances the secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10 (IL-10). Upon binding to its receptor Met, HGF exerts cardioprotective effects by inhibiting hypoxia-induced autophagy and has been identified as a potential biomarker for cardiovascular diseases [77–79]. In this study, MR analysis confirmed that HGF may serve as an endogenous protective factor that mitigates both renal and cardiovascular injury. GSEA revealed significant activation of lysosomal function and autophagy regulatory pathways in CAD patients with high HGF expression (Figure 10(a)). These results are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that HGF prevents autophagic cell death and maintains cardiomyocyte homeostasis via the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [80]. Remarkably, this study uncovers a novel bidirectional regulatory role of HGF in the cardiorenal system. In the CKD cohort, HGF significantly activated ferroptosis pathways (Figure 10(b)), which could be interpreted as evidence of a potential link to lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death in renal tubular cells. Given that iron overload and ferroptosis induces oxidative damage in renal cells and directly promotes fibrosis and inflammation [81]. We propose a “microenvironment-dependent dual-target effect” hypothesis: HGF may exert distinct regulatory effects in cardiovascular and renal microenvironments. In CAD, HGF mitigates injury by inhibiting autophagic cell death and enhancing cardioprotective signaling. In contrast, in CKD, the observed positive correlation between HGF expression and ferroptosis suggests potential interaction within ferroptosis-associated pathways, although the precise biological consequences require validation in real-world studies. This tissue-specific functional diversity positions hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) as a promising dual-target therapeutic candidate. Specifically, activating the HGF/c-Met axis could enhance cardioprotection in CAD, whereas in the context of CKD, HGF may alleviate renal fibrosis by suppressing ferroptosis through the modulation of associated defense genes.

Phospholipase C gamma 1 (PLCG1), a pivotal signaling enzyme in the phospholipase C family, catalyzes the hydrolysis of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to generate diacylglycerol (DAG) and inositol trisphosphate (IP3). This process activates protein kinase C (PKC) and mediates calcium ion release, thereby regulating cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis [82]. Functional studies in zebrafish models have demonstrated that loss-of-function mutations in PLCG1 (e.g. C412R and R557H) cause segmental arterial developmental defects, confirming its critical role in vascular morphogenesis [83]. These findings align with our molecular docking predictions, which show that the nucleotide analog phosphomethylphosphonic acid adenosyl ester binds to the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain of PLCG1 (residues Glu-548/Gln-659) with a binding energy of −6.7 kcal/mol (Figure 12(C)). This suggests a potential allosteric inhibition mechanism targeting the intrinsic phosphohydrolase activity of PLCG1. In this study, computational predictions suggest that PLCG1 may exert distinct immunomodulatory roles in CKD and CAD. However, these findings require validation through enzymatic assays and other experimental approaches. Moreover, the translational relevance of zebrafish data to human cardiorenal comorbidity should be interpreted with caution. Immune infiltration analysis revealed that PLCG1 expression was positively correlated with dendritic cell activity in CKD, but negatively correlated with dendritic cell activity in CAD, indicating that its immunomodulatory function is context-dependent.

PZP is a 360 kDa α2-glycoprotein [84] that has emerged as a key biomarker with diagnostic and prognostic utility across multiple diseases. In children with congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), PZP has been proposed as a cardiovascular risk–specific biomarker [85]. In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), PZP expression is significantly reduced and closely associated with disease stage, indicating its potential as an independent indicator of poor prognosis [86]. Within the context of CKD, PZP has been identified as a candidate biomarker for aging-related CKD progression [87]. Notably, our findings highlight a potential immunoregulatory role of PZP–steroid complexes. Molecular docking analysis revealed a strong binding affinity between PZP and NORETHINDRONE (binding energy: −8.3 kcal/mol), with the interaction localized at the SER-92 and ASN-41 residues (Figure 12(D)). Immune infiltration analysis further indicated that PZP expression was positively correlated with cytotoxic T cells and memory T cell subsets but negatively correlated with dendritic cells and macrophages (p < 0.05), suggesting a role in shaping the immune microenvironment. Given that the ligand-binding domain of PZP lies adjacent to its known complement C3b interaction site, we hypothesize that steroid binding may allosterically modulate PZP–complement interactions through steric hindrance. Although this mechanism remains to be experimentally verified, our results provide a novel perspective for investigating therapeutic strategies targeting complement–immune crosstalk and support the potential utility of PZP as a pharmacological target [88]. Collectively, these findings highlight PZP as a promising diagnostic biomarker for patients with comorbid CKD and CAD and underscore the translational value of developing targeted therapeutics based on its regulatory functions.

SERPINE2 is a serine protease inhibitor belonging to the serpin family. It modulates disease progression by regulating biological processes, including cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. Recent studies have linked SERPINE2 to a spectrum of pathologies, such as neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and various cancers, highlighting its potential as a biomarker [89,90]. Cao et al. reported that SERPINE2 levels were 54.5% higher in patients with type 2 diabetic nephropathy compared to healthy controls, and that its expression was independently correlated with decreased eGFR (r = −0.561), supporting its potential as a marker for renal impairment [91]. Furthermore, in a mouse model of cardiac fibrosis, SERPINE2 expression was significantly upregulated and promoted collagen production via activation of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway, thereby accelerating the progression of cardiac fibrosis [92]. These findings provide new insights into SERPINE2 as a potential therapeutic target in cardiovascular disease. Our GSEA analysis revealed that SERPINE2 upregulation in CKD is significantly and positively correlated with the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway (p < 0.001), suggesting that SERPINE2 may promote lipid accumulation via activation of the lipid–inflammation axis. Immune infiltration analysis demonstrated a strong negative correlation between SERPINE2 expression and NK cell activity in CKD (r < −0.6), indicating that SERPINE2 may attenuate tissue injury by suppressing innate immune responses. Collectively, these findings expand the functional role of SERPINE2 to include regulation of the lipid–inflammation axis in cardiorenal syndrome, providing new mechanistic insights into cross-organ protective interactions.

TXNDC15 is a member of the thioredoxin protein family and primarily functions in the regulation of ciliary activity. To date, studies on its role in cardiorenal diseases remain scarce [93]. GSEA analysis revealed that TXNDC15 is significantly associated with the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway, indicating a potential role in maintaining the homeostasis of cholesterol-derived steroids, such as aldosterone. However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which TXNDC15 regulates steroidogenesis require further experimental validation. Moreover, the MR-derived mediation effect of TXNDC15 was relatively modest (MR derived mediation effect was 1.185%), implying that its biological influence may depend on additional synergistic or compensatory factors within the cardiorenal axis.

Methodological comparison and positioning: the relationship between this study and previous Mendelian randomization-machine learning research in the field of cardiorenal disease

The research paradigm integrating Mendelian randomization and machine learning employed in this study is highly consistent with recent trends in biomarker discovery for cardiovascular and renal diseases published in top-tier international journals, while providing distinctive refinement for the complex disease model of cardiorenal comorbidity. For instance, a landmark 2024 study published in European Heart Journal (IF = 37.6) systematically analyzed the associations between 259 plasma proteins and myocardial infarction (MI) risk using large-scale cohorts and a two-sample MR approach, successfully identifying 45 associated proteins and specifically revealing significant sex-specific differences in 13 of them, with stronger associations in women [94]. Furthermore, a systematic study published in Protein & Cell (IF = 12.8) in 2025 analyzed 14-year follow-up data from 53,026 participants, linked 2,920 plasma proteins to 14 cardiovascular disease outcomes, and through machine learning models and two-sample MR analysis, not only screened high-performance predictive biomarkers but also validated their causal relationship with the diseases, providing crucial evidence for precise early warning [95]. In the field of kidney disease, a study published in Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism (IF = 5.7) integrated machine learning, MR, and mediation analysis to successfully pinpoint key genes for diabetic kidney disease and elucidated their mechanism of influencing disease progression by regulating pathways such as renal fibrosis and inflammation, demonstrating the depth of this method in deciphering complex kidney pathologies [96].

Compared to these studies, our research remains methodologically aligned with the international forefront, embodying the advanced approach of screening biomarkers from large-scale proteomic data, using MR to infer causal relationships, and then leveraging machine learning to build predictive models. Our distinctive contribution lies in focusing the research on the clinically challenging problem of cardiorenal comorbidity. Not only did we validate the causal role of proteins, but more importantly, through a rigorous screening pipeline, we ultimately focused on a more compact core biomarker panel with greater potential for clinical translation, and sought to elucidate the shared pathophysiological pathway of the “lipid-inflammation axis,” thereby laying a solid foundation for developing potential targeted therapies for cardiorenal comorbidity.

Novelty and future applications

The core innovation of this study lies in the identification and validation of a specific combination of six proteins, which may provide a precise tool for risk assessment of cardiorenal comorbidity that is potentially superior to traditional clinical indicators [97]. At the translational application level, this protein signature can be further developed as a key component of a polygenic risk scoring system; research indicates that integrating proteomic information with genetic risk data can significantly enhance the identification of high-risk populations for cardiovascular events and renal function progression [98–99]. A dynamic risk stratification model constructed based on this score could enable precise recommendations for personalized screening initiation times and monitoring frequencies for patients at different risk levels, thereby optimizing the allocation of clinical resources [100]. More importantly, as key mediators of specific pathophysiological pathways, these proteins can be directly incorporated into drug screening pipelines. For instance, levels of proteins such as hepatocyte growth factor are significantly associated with cardiovascular mortality risk, providing direction for rapidly identifying candidate compounds that can modulate these targets and opening new avenues for developing targeted therapies and individualized treatment strategies for cardiorenal comorbidity [101].

Based on our multi omics data and machine learning analysis, combined with new discoveries in molecular biology in the real world, we boldly and reasonably speculate that the LIMA1-HGF-PLCG1 axis can serve as the core mechanism axis connecting lipid metabolism disorders and chronic inflammation, playing a key driving role in the pathological process of cardiovascular and renal comorbidities. The mechanism axis begins with the regulation of intestinal cholesterol absorption by LIMA1: LIMA1 protein expressed at the brush like edge of the small intestine acts as a scaffold, anchoring cholesterol transporter NPC1L1 to myosin Vb to form a complex that promotes dietary cholesterol absorption; The loss or mutation of its function significantly reduces cholesterol absorption and plasma LDL-C levels, forming the starting point of lipid metabolism regulation [102].Subsequently, lipid abnormalities mediate inflammatory responses through HGF: acting as a pleiotropic cytokine activated under tissue injury or metabolic stress, HGF can modulate immune cell infiltration (e.g. macrophage activation), while its expression is regulated by microenvironmental signals (e.g. oxidized lipids or inflammatory factors), exhibiting context-dependent roles such as potentially inhibiting apoptosis in early stages and fibrosis in later stages, thereby directly linking lipid accumulation to inflammatory responses [103–105]. Ultimately, PLCG1 amplifies inflammatory signaling through cellular signal transduction: as a key phospholipase member, gain-of-function mutations in PLCG1 enhance its enzymatic activity, leading to excessive production of second messengers like IP3 and DAG, which aberrantly activate inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-6, TNF-α), thereby cementing and amplifying the inflammatory state and forming a lipid-inflammation positive feedback loop [106–107]. Consequently, the LIMA1–HGF–PLCG1 axis, through regulating cholesterol absorption (LIMA1), microenvironmental inflammatory response (HGF), and intracellular signal amplification (PLCG1), forms a coherent lipid-inflammation cross-talk network, offering a novel perspective for understanding the molecular mechanisms of cardiorenal comorbidity and providing potential multi-target strategies for targeted therapy development. Schuett et al. (2023) emphasized the limited therapeutic options for advanced CKD patients, highlighting the need for trials in this population. Our biomarkers warrant prospective validation in such cohorts to assess their utility in stratified management [59]. This biomarker axis can serve as a foundation for future large-scale cohort validation, experimental mechanistic testing, and AI-driven therapeutic screening.

Limitations of this study

This study has several limitations. First, potential bias in sample sources must be acknowledged, as the GWAS summary statistics and pQTL data were mostly derived from European populations (e.g. CKDGen, IEU Open GWAS, deCODE), thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Future multi-ancestry analyses will therefore be crucial to validate the observed associations across diverse populations. Second, although the key assumptions of MR, particularly the absence of horizontal pleiotropy and the exclusion restriction assumption, were evaluated through multiple sensitivity analyses, residual confounding or unknown pleiotropy cannot be entirely excluded. Third, mediation effect estimations were based on simplified linear models without interaction terms, which may inadequately represent the complex regulatory networks and feedback loops operating in vivo. Moreover, causal mediation using summary-level data is limited in its ability to capture non-linear relationships and bidirectional influences between CKD and CAD. Additionally, functional enrichment and protein–protein interaction (PPI) analyses were constrained by the small number of identified core proteins; focusing on only six core proteins limits the ability to construct biologically comprehensive interaction networks. This limitation may have led to an incomplete depiction of molecular pathways involved in CKD–CAD comorbidity. Current bioinformatics databases also lack sufficient annotation depth and cross-organ interaction data. For instance, among widely used public databases such as STRING and BioGRID, only HGF has documented cardiorenal interactions, while the remaining proteins lack cross-organ interaction data. Finally, drug prediction and molecular docking results presented in this study are computational in nature and should be regarded as exploratory. Their biological and therapeutic relevance requires rigorous validation through well-designed in vitro and in vivo experiments. In addition, no external validation cohort was employed to independently confirm the diagnostic or causal associations observed. Future research should therefore include multicenter and multiethnic validation cohorts to strengthen the reproducibility and generalizability of the findings. Finally, the associations between plasma proteins and diseases revealed by Mendelian randomization analysis in this study are based on statistical inferences using genetic instrumental variables (i.e. genetic proxies of proteins, such as pQTLs). These findings essentially provide proxy-based evidence derived from genetic variations, clarifying potential causal directions, but they have not been directly validated through wet-laboratory experiments (e.g. cell-based or animal models).

Focus of this study within precision nephrology and cardiometabolic risk prediction

As highlighted in the review by Gallo et al. although traditional risk assessment models relying on clinical indicators such as eGFR and UACR provide fundamental value, they exhibit limitations in capturing the early and complex molecular subclinical phenotypes of cardiorenal comorbidity. The review further emphasizes that multi-marker strategies can more comprehensively reflect the pathophysiological processes of the disease, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and risk stratification efficacy. This provides strong theoretical support for the present study’s identification of a core biomarker panel, which aims to achieve more dynamic and precise risk stratification [108]. Our findings help to fill this gap. These biomarkers derived from proteomics reflect specific pathophysiological pathways in the cardiorenal axis (such as inflammation, fibrosis, and oxidative stress), and their value may surpass the current CKM staging system based on clinical phenotypes [109]. For example, a specific expression pattern of a biomarker may help identify high-risk individuals who are in CKM stage 2 (with metabolic risk factors or moderate to high-risk CKD) but are about to rapidly progress to stage 3 (subclinical cardiovascular disease), thereby achieving “restratification within the stage” and providing a time window for ultra-early intervention. Integrating such novel biomarkers into existing multi-omics data platforms is a key step toward precision nephrology. For example, combining our protein biomarkers with emerging metabolomics features (such as specific polar metabolite profiles in serum)[110] or data from imaging and electronic health records could potentially build more robust predictive models. Machine learning algorithms can process such high-dimensional heterogeneous data to identify patient subgroups with unique disease progression trajectories and treatment responses [111]. This deep phenotypic analysis can help achieve truly personalized risk prediction. At the treatment level, accurate risk assessment ultimately leads to precise intervention. The core biomarkers discovered in this study correspond to biological pathways that may themselves be potential intervention targets. This provides a framework for implementing “stratified treatment” in CKM patients: for example, patients whose biomarker profiles indicate that inflammatory pathways are dominant may benefit more from SGLT2 inhibitors, which have been shown to have cardiovascular and renal protective effects [112]; while for patients with activated fibrosis signals, novel anti-fibrotic therapies may be a potential option. In the future, validating the predictive ability of these biomarkers for treatment responses to specific drugs (such as SGLT2 inhibitors, Finerenone, etc.) in prospective cohorts will be a core task in promoting their clinical translation. In summary, this study, by integrating the set of core proteins related to cardiorenal comorbidity identified through genetic and machine learning methods, provides a new perspective for optimizing cardiometabolic risk prediction and management strategies in the era of precision medicine. Future work should focus on validating the clinical utility of these biomarkers in multicenter, prospective cohort studies and incorporating them into user-friendly risk calculation tools, ultimately achieving early identification and individualized prevention and control for high-risk individuals with CKM syndrome.

Future research directions

Based on our Mendelian randomization study, which has not been experimentally validated, future research should prioritize the prospective validation of the identified plasma biomarkers in independent chronic kidney disease–cardiovascular disease (CKD–CVD) cohorts to establish their diagnostic and prognostic reliability. Experimental assays and mechanistic studies are equally essential to confirm protein-mediated cross-organ signaling pathways and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying cardiorenal communication. Furthermore, integrating these validated proteins into polygenic and proteomic risk models could enhance the early prediction of CKD–CVD comorbidity and facilitate the development of personalized therapeutic strategies. Finally, longitudinal multi-omics analyses that combine genomics, spatial transcriptomics, metabolomics, and single-cell transcriptomics may systematically clarify the spatiotemporal dynamics of the cardiorenal lipid axis during disease progression, thereby revealing the reprogramming of lipid metabolic networks and the interplay among different cell subpopulations.
