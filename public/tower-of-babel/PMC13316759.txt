The influence of different raw material processing methods on the quality of mulberry fruit wine
Authors: Yuanxia Wang, Tianhang Wang, Sixian Tan, Xiaocui Liu, Yu Rao, Dejian Huang, Ping Xie, Pingkang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study systematically compared the quality attributes and volatile profiles of mulberry wine produced from mulberry pulp (MPW) and mulberry juice (MJW) throughout fermentation and subsequent storage. Yeast fermentation markedly enhanced antioxidant activity, achieving a 90% radical scavenging rate. The presence of pulp matrix facilitated higher retention of phenolics and anthocyanins. Sixty-three volatile compounds were identified, with MPW fermentation enriching flavor complexity and introducing herbaceous notes via stem retention. During storage, MPW exhibited slower degradation of phenolics and anthocyanins, maintaining higher bioactivity and stability. The volatile compounds decreased after 25 d, particularly alcohols and esters. These findings indicate that pulp-based fermentation is more effective at preserving bioactive compounds. This approach provides a practical strategy for industrial processing, serving as a viable method to extend shelf-life without exogenous additives and offering a strategic pathway for developing high-value fermented fruit products.

Introduction

With the rising global emphasis on health, berries have attracted significant attention due to their nutritional profiles and potential health benefits. Mulberries (Morus Linn.) are nutritional, low-calorie fruits containing in vitamins, minerals (e.g., zinc, calcium), and diverse bioactive compounds (Bian et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2018). These constituents, including anthocyanins, polysaccharides, rutin, and resveratrol, are associated with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and organ-protective properties (Kumar et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2019; Wen et al., 2019). However, the inherent high moisture content and rich nutrient profile of fresh mulberries make them highly susceptible to spoilage, significantly constraining their commercial viability (Yuan et al., 2024). Consequently, post-harvest processing is essential to extend shelf life and enhance economic value, leading to the development of various products such as dried goods, juices, and fermented beverages (Herman et al., 2022; Yuan & Zhao, 2017). Fermentation is a traditional, safe method that both inhibits microbial spoilage and shows potential to enrich the diversity and bioavailability of functional components. Current research on mulberry fermentation primarily focuses on two distinct pathways: alcoholic fermentation mediated by Saccharomyces cerevisiae and lactic acid fermentation facilitated by lactic acid bacteria, each yielding unique metabolic profiles (Qin et al., 2024, Hu et al., 2023).

Previous studies have reported several beneficial properties of fermented mulberry products. Butkhup et al. (2013) reported that sequential inoculation with two distinct yeast strains during mulberry wine fermentation enhanced the bioactive compound content and antioxidant activity. Additionally, fermented mulberry juice has been associated with enhanced antioxidant capacity, free amino acid content, and antimicrobial properties (Cheng et al., 2023). These findings provide valuable insights for the design of fermentation systems to transform mulberries into functional foods. Recent advances in food processing have further emphasized that optimizing processing conditions is critical for retaining bioactive compounds and improving product quality. Innovative juice processing techniques have been shown to significantly influence nutritional stability and antioxidant retention. For instance, studies have indicated that treating naturally aged mulberry wine with a low-voltage electrostatic field (LVEF) significantly increases the volatile compound diversity and concentration, particularly promoting the synthesis of esters, aldehydes, and ketones, which enriches the aromatic characteristics of the wine (Ekumah et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2025). These findings provide valuable insights for the design of fermentation systems to transform mulberries into functional foods.

Despite advances in mulberry fermentation, existing research on fruit wine mainly focuses on yeast strains (Yuan et al., 2026), fermentation conditions (Abera et al., 2026), and raw material varieties (Awasthi et al., 2025). While studies indicate that both pulp-based and juice fermentation effectively retain bioactive compounds and develop complex flavor profiles (Liu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2026), the critical role of raw material pretreatment in determining wine quality remains underexplored. Few studies have systematically compared the differences between pulp-based fermentation, which retains solid components such as pulp and fiber, and juice fermentation that employs pressed and filtered liquid. This knowledge gap is particularly significant given that in other fruits, retaining the fruit matrix components has been shown to enhance the extraction and stability of phenolic compounds, and the intact matrix contributes to higher phenolic retention and antioxidant capacity (Tang et al., 2025). From a sustainability perspective, retaining fruit matrix components through pulp-based processing not only preserves bioactive compounds but also reduces food waste and improves resource utilization efficiency (Bushueva et al., 2024). Therefore, a systematic comparative study of these two processing modes is necessary to clarify the differential impacts of retaining the whole fruit matrix versus removing solid residues on the final product quality.

Although fermentation-based processing techniques combine the dual advantages of raw fruit materials and fermentation metabolites (Ma et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2024), their sensory characteristics can be relatively monotonous, presenting certain limitations (Hu et al., 2021). Therefore, this study aims to optimize the fermentation processes for mulberry wine and enhance product quality by comprehensively comparing the quality indices and volatile profiles of pulp-based (MPW) and juice-based (MJW) wines during fermentation and accelerated aging.

Materials and methods

Materials

The mulberries were sourced from the Sichuan International Agricultural Products Trading Center (Sichuan, China) and Shandong Yantai Dongshun Food Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Food-grade Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain SY, selected for its high ethanol tolerance, strong fermentation activity under low-nutrient conditions, and favorable ester-producing capacity, was obtained from Angel Yeast Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China). The α-glucosidase and α-amylase were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All reagents were of analytical grade, and included gallic acid (CAS: 149-91-7, 97.7%), rutin (CAS: 153-18-4, 98%), aluminum chloride (CAS: 7446-70-0, 99.99%), and potassium persulfate (CAS: 7727-21-1, GR). The food-grade absolute ethanol and pectinase were obtained from Hefei Bomei Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Preparation of the MPW and MJW

Mulberry wine was prepared using a method described by Ekumah et al. (2021), with minor modifications. The fresh mulberries were washed and pulped. The pulping step was used to disrupt fruit tissues, promote the release of soluble solids and bioactive compounds, and ensure that the subsequent comparison mainly reflected the effect of pulp retention versus juice filtration. The resulting slurry was used directly for MPW preparation, whereas another portion was filtered through three-layer gauze for MJW preparation. Next, 60 mg/kg K₂S₂O₅ and 0.05 g/kg pectinase were added to 200 mL of the mulberry pulp and juice, respectively, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at 20 °C for 5 h. Pectinase-assisted treatment under controlled temperature was applied to facilitate juice clarification, while minimizing over-degradation that could affect the sensory profile or yield. Then, 200 g of soft white sugar was added and homogenized to adjust the initial total soluble solids to approximately 20°Brix, a level commonly used in fruit wine production to obtain a final alcohol content of approximately 10–12% (v/v). Yeast powder (3 g) was activated in 10 mL of warm water (35–40 °C) and inoculated into the fermentation vessel (BPX-82; Chengdu Century Ark Technology Co., Ltd.; China) at 3% (v/v). The mixtures were fermented at 17–20 °C while stirring and monitoring the sugar content daily. The fermentation was terminated via racking when the sugar level remained constant for 3 d consecutively. The fermented wine was then clarified by filtration through three-layer gauze. Unfermented mulberry juice, which underwent identical preparation steps but was sampled immediately after yeast inoculation without undergoing fermentation, served as the control (CK). According to a method delineated by Gao et al. (2023), with modifications, the mulberry wine was stored at 40 °C for 25 d to accelerate aging and sampled at 5 d intervals, as 40 °C is a commonly used accelerated storage condition for evaluating the stability of fruit wine within a practical experimental period.

Determination of the basic physicochemical properties of the mulberry wine during fermentation and storage

A digital pH meter (PHS-320; Chengdu Century Ark Technology Co., Ltd.; China) was used to measure the pH levels of the three mulberry wines (MPW, MJW, and CK) throughout fermentation (1 d to 8 d) and storage (1 d to 25 d). The AOAC (2023) method and a digital refractometer (MyBrix; Mettler-Toledo GmbH; Switzerland) were used to determine the total soluble solid (TSS) content in the mulberry wine at ambient temperature. The alcohol concentration was determined by measuring the density of the distillate using the OIV-MA-BS-01 method (Compendium of International Methods of Wine and Must Analysis, Section 3.1.3: Organic Compounds).

Determination of the color differences

For the colorimetric analysis (ΔE), 10 mL of the specimen was transferred into a vessel and measured in a light-sealed environment using a colorimeter (WF32-16MM, Shenzhen Weifu Optoelectronics Technology Co., Ltd., China). Each test was repeated three times to ensure data reliability.

Determination of the total phenolic content (TPC)

The TPC was quantified according to the protocol outlined by Wang et al. (2022), with slight modifications. A 100 μL mulberry wine sample was combined with 7 mL of distilled water, after which 0.5 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent was added and mixed thoroughly. Next, 1.5 mL of a 20% Na₂CO₃ solution was incorporated, followed by the addition of 0.9 mL of distilled water to increase the final volume to the required level. The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark for 60 min, after which the absorbance was recorded at 765 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV2400; Shanghai Sunny Hengping Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China). All assays were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

The standard curve was obtained by analyzing gallic acid solutions at concentrations of 0 mg/L, 10 mg/L, 20 mg/L, 30 mg/L, 40 mg/L, and 50 mg/L in the same conditions. The subsequent calibration curve was defined by the equation y = 0.0149× + 0.0771 (R2 = 0.9921).

Determination of the total flavonoid content (TFC)

The TFC was quantified using a method described by Granato et al. (2011), with slight modifications. A 0.5 mL mulberry wine sample was diluted to 5 mL with 15% ethanol, followed by the addition of 0.3 mL of a 0.5 mol/L NaNO₂ solution and mixed thoroughly. Next, 0.3 mL of a 0.3 mol/L AlCl₃ solution was added and allowed to stand for 5 min, after which 2 mL of a 1 mol/L NaOH solution was introduced. The final volume was adjusted to 10 mL with 15% ethanol, after which the absorbance was measured at 506 nm using a solution devoid of color-developing reagents as CK. All assays were performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as catechin equivalents. The standard curve was established using catechin standard solutions, yielding the following regression equation: y = 0.0004× + 0.0008 (R2 = 0.995).

Determination of the anthocyanin content (TAC)

The TAC was determined using a procedure described by Jiang et al. (2014), with minor modifications. A 1 mL aliquot of the sample was separately combined with 9 mL of KCl buffer (0.025 M, pH 1.0) and 9 mL of CH₃COONa buffer (0.4 M, pH 4.5), followed by incubation in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 514 nm and 700 nm using a spectrophotometer, with distilled water as a blank. All measurements were performed in triplicate. The TAC was calculated using Eq. (2.1):(2.1)Total anthocyanin contentmg/L=A×MW×DF×1000ε×1where A = [(A₅₁₄ − A₇₀₀) at pH 1.0] − [(A₅₁₄ − A₇₀₀) at pH 4.5]; MW (the molecular weight of cyanidin-3-glucoside) = 449.2 g/mol; DF = dilution factor; 1 = pathlength (cm); and ε (the molar extinction coefficient of cyanidin-3-glucoside) = 26,900 L/(mol·cm).

Determination of the antioxidant activity

The antioxidant potential of the mulberry wine samples was characterized by evaluating their free-radical-scavenging abilities, using DPPH· and ABTS+· scavenging assays.

DPPH· radical scavenging assay

The DPPH· radical-scavenging capacity was evaluated according to a protocol delineated by Hu et al. (2021), with minor modifications. Here, 2 mL aliquots of the sample solution were combined with an equal volume of a 0.1 mM DPPH· solution, with vitamin C as the positive CK. The mixtures were incubated in the dark at ambient temperature for 30 min, after which the absorbance was determined at 517 nm and defined as A1. The CK measurements encompassed A0 and As, which represented the absorbance of the sample solution in the absence of DPPH. The scavenging activity was determined using Eq. (2.2).(2.2)DPPH·scavenging efficiency%=A0−A1+As/A0×100

ABTS+ radical scavenging assay

The ABTS+ radical scavenging potential was assessed using a procedure detailed by Hu et al. (2021). The working solution was generated by reacting ABTS+· with potassium persulfate and incubating the subsequent mixture at 4 °C in the dark. Before analysis, the stock solution was adjusted with ethanol to obtain an absorbance of 0.7–0.8 at 734 nm. The reaction was initiated by mixing the sample solution with the ABTS+· working solution and allowing it to stand for 3 min at room temperature, after which the absorbance was recorded at 734 nm. The scavenging activity was calculated using Eq. (2.3). A1 represents the absorbance of the blank CK (without sample), and A2 denotes the absorbance of the sample solution.(2.3)ABTS+radical scavening activity%=A1−A2/A0×100

Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation was performed using a method delineated by Sui et al. (2024), with minor modifications. A trained sensory panel comprising 10 food science students (five males and five females) assessed the sensory attributes of the mulberry wine based on predefined criteria. The evaluation employed a 100-point scale, with the results expressed as the average of the cumulative scores of the panelists. Table S1 provides the detailed evaluation criteria.

Electronic nose (E-nose) analysis of the different fermentation processes

The odors in the different mulberry wines were analyzed using a PEN3 E-nose (AIRSENSE Analytics; Germany), featuring a detection unit with 10 metal oxide sensors (W1C, W1S, W1W, W2S, W2W, W3C, W3S, W5S, W5C, and W6S). Here, 5 g of each sample was placed in a 20 mL headspace vial and sealed. The analytical protocol settings included a sampling interval of 1 s, a flush duration of 200 s, and a preparation period of 5 s. Detection lasted for 180 s, at a constant gas flow rate of 400 mL/min. All measurements were performed in an odor-free environment at 20 °C, with six replicates analyzed per group (Xu et al., 2025).

Determination of the volatile flavor compounds

The volatile compounds in the mulberry wine were analyzed according to a method delineated by Sui et al. (2024) and Wang et al. (2021), with modifications. A 4 mL sample was placed in a headspace vial with 1.5 g NaCl and 2 μL of a 2-methyl-3-heptanone solution (0.816 μg/mL, internal standard), sealed immediately, and equilibrated for 20 min at 55 °C for extraction, followed by desorption for 3 min.

The GC conditions included an injector temperature of 250 °C, splitless mode, and a solvent delay of 1 min. The initial temperature of the column oven was held at 40 °C for 5 min, raised to 120 °C at 4 °C/min (held 2 min), and then increased to 240 °C at 7 °C/min (held 5 min).

The MS conditions included electron impact (EI) ionization at 70 eV, an interface temperature of 240 °C, and a mass scan range of m/z 41–330.

Statistical analysis

All experimental results were presented as the mean ± SD from three replicates. To ensure statistical robustness and account for variability in the fermentation process, three independent fermentation batches were conducted for each treatment (MPW and MJW). One-way ANOVA was employed to evaluate the significant differences among the groups, followed by Dunnett's post hoc test for multiple comparisons using SPSS (version 25.0, Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). The threshold for statistical significance was established at p < 0.05. Before ANOVA, the normality of the data distribution was confirmed via the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was verified using Levene's test.

Results and discussion

Effect of different raw materials on the initial quality of the mulberry wine

As shown in Fig. 1, no significant differences were observed in the key physicochemical parameters (TSS, pH, and alcohol content) of MPW and MJW (p > 0.05), indicating that the raw materials had minimal effect on yeast fermentation progression and basic product composition.Fig. 1The effect of the raw materials on the physicochemical properties of the mulberry wine: TSS (A), alcohol content (B), and pH (C).Fig. 1

The effect of the raw materials on the physicochemical properties of the mulberry wine: TSS (A), alcohol content (B), and pH (C).

Monitoring the sugar content is crucial for identifying the fermentation endpoints and ensuring the normal progression of wine fermentation. The initial TSS content of 20% decreased rapidly during the first 3 d of fermentation as the sugars were actively metabolized by Saccharomyces cerevisiae via glycolysis and other metabolic pathways, then gradually slowed and stabilized after 6 d (Fig. 1A) (Che et al., 2024). This was likely due to the depletion of available carbon sources and the inhibitory effect of the increasing alcohol concentration on the yeast activity.

The alcohol content directly reflects the progress of fermentation and serves as a key indicator of the fermentation endpoints. Contrary to TSS, these levels increased gradually over time (Fig. 1B), stabilizing between 6 d and 8 d and reaching final values of 10.13 ± 0.01% vol for MPW and 10.0 ± 0.03% vol for MJW, which could be attributed to esterification reactions (Zhu et al., 2025). Total acidity is an important physicochemical parameter in fruit wine fermentation, affecting both flavor and taste while reflecting microbial activity (Maslov Maslov Bandić et al., 2019). The pH, which influences yeast metabolism and enzyme activity during fermentation, decreased continuously throughout the process (Fig. 1C). The initial pH of 4.93 ± 0.01 decreased to 3.49 ± 0.01 in MPW and 3.54 ± 0.01 in MJW. These values declined rapidly during the first 24 h and then slowed. This was attributed to the continuous dissolution of organic acids from the mulberry pulp into the fermentation broth and acid production associated with yeast growth and reproduction (Maslov Maslov Bandić et al., 2019), while the increasingly acidic environment later inhibited yeast activity and sugar conversion.

Effect of different raw materials on bioactive compounds and antioxidant activity of mulberry wine

While both raw materials had a minimal impact on yeast fermentation progression and basic product composition, MPW exhibited significantly higher TPC, TFC, and TAC values than MJW (p < 0.05, Table 1), highlighting enhanced polyphenol retention during pulp fermentation. Specifically, the TPC of MPW was 7.1% higher than that of MJW, alongside an 8.1% increase in TFC and approximately 5.0% greater anthocyanin retention. This aligned with reports that indicated an increase in polyphenols in fermented fruit products, likely related to β-glucosidase activity, which aided in phenolic aglycone release (Kwaw et al., 2018a; Minnaar et al., 2019). Studies have confirmed that yeast fermentation enhances the levels of phenolic compounds such as caffeic, chlorogenic, and coumaric acids, consequently increasing the TPC. MPW exhibited an intact fruit structure rich in cell wall-embedded phenolics, which facilitated enzymatic degradation during fermentation, effectively hydrolyzing bound phenolics into their free forms (Hornedo Hornedo Ortega et al., 2017). Additionally, its pulp matrix provided physical protection that slowed oxidative and enzymatic degradation (Yuan et al., 2025). Although fermentation reduced the TAC compared to the raw material (MPW: 487.7 ± 0.9 mg/L; MJW: 464.3 ± 1.4 mg/L), potentially due to acidic degradation and enzymatic activity (Ma et al., 2025), MPW maintained higher levels via non-covalent binding with cell-wall components that mitigated degradation (Chinnici et al., 2009). Consistent with these differences in bioactive compounds, MPW and MJW both exhibited significantly enhanced antioxidant activity after fermentation, with DPPH· and ABTS+· scavenging rates reaching over 90% (Fig. 2A).Fig. 2A shows the DPPH· and ABTS+· scavenging activity in the mulberry wine samples. From an industrial perspective, the higher retention of phenolics, flavonoids, and anthocyanins in MPW aligns with the growing market demand for antioxidant-rich fermented products, particularly in the nutraceutical and functional beverage sectors (Ishaq et al., 2025). Therefore, pulp fermentation can be leveraged by wine producers to develop value-added mulberry wines with enhanced health claims, thereby increasing product differentiation, consumer acceptability, and commercial competitiveness(Carbone et al., 2026).

Effect of different raw materials on the color differences in the mulberry wine

The ΔE value represents the total color difference of a sample, with higher values indicating more pronounced color variations between specimens. The color measurements (Table 2) yielded L* values of 28.4 ± 0.6 for MPW and 31.2 ± 0.8 for MJW, a* values of 8.7 ± 0.3 and 6.9 ± 0.2, and b* values of 5.1 ± 0.2 and 6.3 ± 0.3, respectively. The ΔE values ranged from 2.11 to 2.69, indicating minimal color differences that were visually indistinguishable, which was consistent with the instrumental measurements. The negative correlations between the L*/b* values and total acidity suggested acid-induced pigment instability, similar to the pigment precipitation observed in wine (Ekumah et al., 2021). Furthermore, MPW exhibited lower L* and higher a* values, yielding a more intense red color. The acidic environment likely induced changes in solution polarity, while hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding interactions restored shielding of the anthocyanin chromophore. This enhanced the color intensity and red hue, which correlated with the higher anthocyanin and polyphenol levels in MPW. The anthocyanins extracted from fruit skins play a crucial role in improving color stability and reducing the yellowish hue in wine (Wu et al., 2025). Effect of different raw materials on the sensory evaluation results of the mulberry wine

Sensory characteristics are key determinants of consumer acceptance of fermented beverages. This study evaluated the sensory quality of mulberry wine fermented using two distinct raw materials, each with three biological replicates. The sensory assessment examined various factors, including color, aroma, taste, texture, and overall acceptability. The results (Fig. 3) showed no significant differences between the color or aroma of the two wines. Although both exhibited slight astringency, MJW demonstrated more pronounced acidic notes, resulting in a higher overall acceptability score for MPW.Fig. 3The effect of the raw materials on the sensory evaluation of the mulberry wine.Fig. 3

E-nose detection of the mulberry wine after pretreatment with different raw materials

The E-nose is an efficient tool for rapidly identifying and classifying aroma compounds in samples, utilizing a multisensor array capable of cross-sensitive responses to gas molecules. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the data collected by the E-nose sensors. As shown in Fig. 4A, differences in the response values of sensors W3C and W6S suggested the possible presence of hydrogen-containing compounds and ammonia derivatives in the samples, while the comparable responses of the remaining sensors indicated potential similarities in the types of aromatic components. The PCA results summarized the volatile compounds into two principal components, with higher cumulative contribution rates reflecting a stronger representation of the original information. The results (Fig. 4B) showed that the first and second principal components accounted for 91.47% and 8.37% of the variance, respectively, with a cumulative contribution rate of 99.84%, indicating that these two components effectively capture all essential sample information. A larger distance between samples in the PCA plot reflected greater differences in the volatile composition, and vice versa. The observed overlap between the samples (Fig. 4B) suggests similarities in the volatile compound profiles of the two mulberry wines.Fig. 4The E-nose analysis (A) and PCA (B) of the mulberry wine produced using different raw materials.Fig. 4

Determination of the volatile flavor compounds in the mulberry wine after pretreatment with different raw materials

HS-SPME-GC × GC–MS was employed to analyze the volatile compounds in mulberry wines and to characterize their complex aroma profiles. As shown in Fig. 5A and Table S2, a total of 63 volatile compounds were detected across all samples, including 22 alcohols, 17 esters, 2 ketones, 2 phenols, 12 acids, 1 ether, 1 aldehyde, 3 terpenes, and 3 other compounds (Fig. 5B). Alcohols and esters exhibited relatively high diversity and abundance.Fig. 5The number (A) and content (B) of the volatile compounds in the mulberry wine produced using two distinct raw materials. In terms of volatile diversity, fermentation increased the number of esters to 11 in MPW and 13 in MJW, accounting for 27.7% and 26% of total volatiles, respectively (Fig. 5C). Several critical esters such as diethyl succinate and ethyl 2-phenylacetate contributed to the characteristic wine profile (Zhu et al., 2018). Acids, as precursors to esters, rose to 10 varieties in MPW and 11 in MJW, with shared compounds including 3-methylbutanoic acid and hexanoic acid (El El Hadi et al., 2013). MPW uniquely contained n-decanoic acid, which imparts chocolate malt notes, and green leafy alcohols such as 3-hexen-1-ol, likely derived from mulberry stems, adding herbaceous complexity (Zhu et al., 2018).

To definitively identify the compounds driving sensory differentiation, quantitative odor activity value (OAV) analysis was employed to evaluate the significant contributions of aroma-active compounds to the overall flavor profile, with detailed results presented in Table S3 (Gemert, 2003). Among the detected volatiles, ethyl octanoate showed remarkably high OAVs in both MPW (295) and MJW (638.5), indicating its dominant role in the fruity aroma. Ethyl hexanoate showed a stronger fruit-enhancing potential in MPW (OAV = 6.25), whereas it remained below the threshold in MJW. Notably, ethyl lactate exhibited an extremely high OAV in MJW (587.67) but a moderate OAV in MPW (2.69). The combination of a very high ethyl lactate OAV and a low ethyl hexanoate OAV in MJW may create a perception of sharpness or imbalance, whereas MPW displayed a more balanced ester profile with a favorable ratio of ethyl hexanoate to ethyl lactate. This OAV-based difference directly explains the sensory results: although no major difference in overall aroma was detected between the two wines, MJW was perceived as having more pronounced acidic notes, while MPW received higher overall acceptability. The more balanced ester acid profile of MPW, particularly the higher ethyl hexanoate to ethyl lactate ratio, likely suppresses acidic perception and enhances fruitiness. Additionally, the presence of stem derived volatiles in MPW may contribute to a more harmonious flavor. Thus, the superior acceptability of MPW is partially attributable to its favorable ester acid balance and the unique herbaceous compounds from mulberry stems.

Physical and chemical properties of the mulberry wine during storage

During the first 15 days of storage, no significant differences were observed in TSS, pH, or alcohol content between MPW and MJW (p < 0.05, Fig. 6), indicating that raw material pretreatment had a limited impact on basic physicochemical properties during this early stage. However, statistically significant distinctions emerged from day 20 onwards (p > 0.05), particularly in TSS and pH values. Specifically, the initial TSS in the MPW decreased from 4.4% to 4.3% at 25 d, while the value in MJW started at 4.5% and ended at 4.4% (Fig. 6A). The TSS remained relatively constant throughout storage, indicating good stability with minimal temperature influence, likely due to the dual effect of soluble solid consumption and degradation during storage (McDougall et al., 2016). The pH decreased gradually during the first 15 d (p < 0.05, Fig. 6B) before stabilizing, possibly due to ethanol oxidation to acetaldehyde and further to acetic acid, as well as the depletion of fermentable sugars (Castellanos et al., 2021). The alcohol content showed a slight decline during storage before stabilizing at 15 d (Fig. 6C), which was consistent with the findings of Huang et al. (2024a), potentially resulting from the oxidative decomposition of ethanol.Fig. 6The changes in physicochemical properties of the mulberry wine during storage: TSS (A), alcohol content (B), and pH (C).Fig. 6

Changes in bioactive compounds and antioxidant activity of mulberry wine during storage

Fig. 6A shows the changes in the TPC of the mulberry wine during storage. Both MPW and MJW exhibited similar decreasing trends over time, which was consistent with previous reports. The reduction may be attributed to the condensation or oxidative polymerization of monomeric phenolics, forming high-molecular-weight compounds such as ellagic acid polymers or proanthocyanidin oligomers (Huang et al., 2024b). These polymeric formations reduce soluble phenolic levels and may compromise antioxidant activity (Pati et al., 2019). Additionally, phenolic compounds can bind or precipitate with non-volatile compounds, further decreasing the detectable content (Li et al., 2024). Notably, aside from a divergence in TPC after day 20, no significant differences were observed between treatments for TFC, TAC, or antioxidant activities (p < 0.05). The TFC showed a gradual decline during storage (Fig. 7B), likely due to the hydroxyl radical-induced oxidation of phenolic hydroxyl groups generated by dissolved oxygen (Gao et al., 2023). Furthermore, flavonols may condense with other phenolics (e.g., anthocyanins and ellagic acid) to form insoluble or low-activity complexes (Zhang et al., 2022). Flavonoid oxidation into quinones during storage also contributes to the observed reduction (Danilewicz, 2003; Oliveira et al., 2011). The TAC decreased rapidly during storage (Fig. 7C). Tsai et al. (2004) reported that the acetaldehydes in mulberry wine condensed with anthocyanins to generate undetectable pigments or precipitates, particularly during later storage stages. Anthocyanin stability is jointly affected by pH, temperature, sulfur dioxide, metal ions, and enzymatic activity (Hu et al., 2021), with the yeast population and metabolites also influencing the content (Mangani et al., 2011). Correspondingly, the antioxidant activity progressively decreased during prolonged storage (Figs. 7D and E). The DPPH· and ABTS+· scavenging rates showed positive correlations with the changes in TPC, TFC, and TAC. These results were consistent with those reported by Tsai et al. Changes in volatile flavor compounds in the mulberry wine during storage

The volatile flavor compounds in the mulberry wine samples were identified after storage at 1 d and 25 d using comprehensive two-dimensional technology (Table S4). A total of 59 volatile compounds were detected, including 18 esters, 25 alcohols, nine acids, two aldehydes, one ketone, and five other compounds (Figs. 8A-C). To identify the compounds most relevant to sensory perception, OAV were calculated based on published odor thresholds (Gemert, 2003), with key results shown in Table S5.Fig. 8The volatile compounds in the mulberry wine during storage: the number (A), content (B), and heatmap analysis of the key volatile flavor characteristics (C).Fig. 8

The volatile compounds in the mulberry wine during storage: the number (A), content (B), and heatmap analysis of the key volatile flavor characteristics (C).

Among alcohols, 1-nonanol and phenethyl alcohol exhibited OAVs above the threshold in all samples, contributing persistent rosy and orange-like floral notes (Liu et al., 2024). While both compounds declined during storage, 1-nonanol remained perceptible longer in MPW (OAV = 6.03 at day 25) compared to MJW (OAV = 4.65), indicating better retention of these fruity/floral characteristics. Notably, the green leaf-like 2-hexen-1-ol was uniquely detected in MPW (decreasing from 8.8 at day 1 to 4.3 at day 25), suggesting that stem-derived herbaceous volatiles were mainly associated with pulp fermentation and persist through storage. Furthermore, while isoamyl alcohol decreased over time and became undetectable in MJW by day 25, it remained perceptible in MPW (OAV = 1.92), suggesting better retention of this alcoholic note. Overall, after 25 days of storage, MJW lost a greater number of active alcohol compounds than MPW. This aligns with previous findings that MPW maintains higher OAVs for key fruit-enhancing esters throughout storage. Ultimately, this more balanced and persistent volatile profile in MPW likely suppresses acidic off-notes and enhances fruitiness, while the unique stem-derived green volatiles add harmonious complexity, thereby explaining its higher overall acceptability.

In addition to alcohols, esters and acids also played crucial roles in shaping the dynamic flavor profile. Esters such as ethyl acetate, isobutyl acetate, and ethyl hexanoate dominated the aroma, providing prominent fruity and floral notes (Styger et al., 2011). Meanwhile, abundant acids like butanoic acid and 3-methylbutanoic acid contributed complex apple-like and malt nuances (Carpena et al., 2020; Guan et al., 2024). However, the concentrations of these key volatiles generally decreased during prolonged storage. This decline is primarily driven by dynamic chemical transformations such as esterification, oxidation, and polymerization, along with the natural evaporation of highly volatile short-chain compounds (Kwaw et al., 2018b). These continuous reactions lead to the gradual loss or conversion of specific volatiles, resulting in the observed temporal evolution of the wine's overall flavor composition.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrated that yeast fermentation significantly enhanced the content of antioxidant compounds in mulberry wine. MPW retained higher levels of bioactive compounds and showed stronger in vitro antioxidant activity compared to MJW, indicating the advantage of pulp fermentation in extracting and preserving polyphenols. Furthermore, 25 novel volatile flavor compounds were generated during fermentation, particularly stem-derived green-leaf alcohols, which enhanced flavor profile complexity. These findings indicated that pulp fermentation better preserved bioactive components and maintained wine stability. From an industrial perspective, pulp-based fermentation eliminates the juicing step, reduces water and energy consumption, minimizes solid waste, and enables whole fruit utilization. To optimize this process for commercial production, key parameters should be adjusted, including maceration time, pectinase dosage, and fermentation temperature, to balance phenolic extraction with desired flavor profiles.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study did not analyze microbial community dynamics during fermentation. Moreover, pulp-based fermentation inherently poses higher microbial stability risks than juice-based fermentation due to its solid particles and higher water activity, which may harbor spoilage organisms. Although the use of a pure Saccharomyces cerevisiae culture under controlled conditions suppresses undesirable microorganisms, the decline in alcohol content and pH during storage could reduce the natural antimicrobial barrier. Future studies should therefore systematically evaluate microbial load throughout fermentation and storage and establish critical control points for hazard analysis. Storage stability was evaluated under accelerated conditions at 40 °C for 25 days, which, while useful for predicting degradation trends, may not fully replicate real-time ambient storage over extended periods; therefore, the actual shelf-life and flavor evolution under commercial storage conditions require further validation. The sensory evaluation involved a relatively small panel (n = 10), which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the acceptability assessment. These constraints suggest that the interpretations of stability and flavor evolution, particularly the rate of bioactive compound degradation and the perception of acidic notes, should be considered preliminary. Looking forward, the integration of pulp fermentation with emerging technologies such as membrane filtration for targeted polyphenol fractionation, or the use of non-Saccharomyces yeasts in co-culture to modulate volatile profiles, could further elevate product quality. Future work should also validate the stability of MPW under real-time commercial storage conditions and explore its application in functional beverage formulations, and incorporate comprehensive safety assessments including microbiological and chemical contamination analyses.
