Compliance and completeness of WHO Surgical Safety Checklist in a tertiary-level neurosurgical center in Nepal: a retrospective descriptive audit
Authors: Pratyush Shrestha, Prakriti Medhasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
One million deaths and seven million complications occur in an estimated 234 million procedures carried out worldwide each year; half of these deaths are preventable. Studies have reported a reduction in the mortality rate by 50% after the introduction of the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (WHO-SSC). Surgical errors resulting from poor safety practices have had devastating outcomes; by identifying current practices and potential gaps in checklist adherence, this research aims to enhance patient safety and improve surgical outcomes.A retrospective, descriptive, cross-sectional study was performed at Upendra Devkota Memorial National Institute of Neurological and Allied Sciences to assess the compliance and completeness of the WHO-SSC in 50 consecutive patients undergoing cranial and spinal surgery under general anesthesia before and after COVID (Jan–Feb 2020 and 2023). The difference in compliance and completeness in elective and emergency cases, as well as in the two timelines, was also evaluated.In 2020, there were 19 emergencies and 31 electives; and in 2023, there were 16 emergencies and 34 electives. The WHO-SSC was not filled in 21% of emergency cases in 2020 and in 38% of emergency cases in 2023. In elective cases, around 25% of cases didn’t have the WHO-SSC filled in both timelines. If not regularly emphasized, the compliance with conducting WHO-SSC wanes over time, especially in emergency neurosurgery compared to elective procedures, which can result in devastating consequences.

Introduction

One million deaths and seven million complications occur in an estimated 234 million procedures carried out worldwide each year[1]. Half of these deaths are believed to be preventable[1,2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) started the “Safe Surgery Saves Lives” campaign in 2007 in response to widespread concerns about avoidable surgical complications and deaths. The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (WHO-SSC) was created as part of this effort in cooperation with global surgeons, anesthesiologists, nurses, and patient safety specialists. The checklist was first introduced in 2008 after being piloted in eight countries that represented a variety of healthcare settings[3]. The Checklist was launched officially in Washington, DC, United States of America, on 25 June 2008[4].

The three pause points that make up the WHO-SSC – Sign In (prior to anesthesia), Time Out (prior to skin incision), and Sign Out (prior to the patient leaving the operating room) – are intended to confirm the patient’s identity, the appropriate surgical site, side, and procedure, the availability of the required equipment, and to prepare for any potential complications. A study on a diverse group of hospitals showed that after introducing the WHO-SSC, the mortality rate decreased by 50% (from 1.5% to 0.8%), and the complication rate declined from 11% to 7%[5].

In Nepal, surgical errors resulting from poor safety practices have had devastating outcomes. For example, a patient scheduled for a thyroidectomy was mistakenly subjected to a hysterectomy – a clear case of identity and procedure mismatch[6]. In another tragic incident, a patient who was supposed to undergo left leg surgery was operated on his right[6]. Both cases were surgical errors that could have been prevented with systematic safety protocols.

Upendra Devkota Memorial National Institute of Neurological and Allied Sciences (UDM-NINAS) is a 100-bedded tertiary-level hospital catering to patients with neuro and allied conditions. The use of WHO-SSC in UDM-NINAS officially began in November 2016; however, the compliance and completeness of the WHO-SSC had not been studied. The aim of the study was to evaluate the compliance in filling out the WHO-SSC in the neurosurgical operating theatres of UDM-NINAS, as well as to evaluate its completeness through a thorough audit. By comparing the WHO-SSC across two different timelines, we intend to see if practices wane over time when not reinforced and how they differ in emergency and elective settings. By identifying current practices and potential gaps in checklist adherence, this research aimed to enhance patient safety and improve surgical outcomes.

Methodology

A retrospective, descriptive, cross-sectional study was performed at UDM-NINAS. Compliance with the WHO-SSC and its completeness at two different timelines, i.e., before and after COVID, were assessed. A thorough literature review on WHO-SSC and surgical errors was performed on PubMed using the keywords WHO Surgical Safety Checklist, Surgical Error, and Audit. A few medical errors that were reported in major national newspapers were also studied. The record files of fifty consecutive patients operated on from January–February 2020 (before COVID) and January–February 2023 (after COVID) were retrieved from the record section. Cranial and spinal operations done under general anesthesia were only included. The operations were sub-grouped as emergency and elective. The WHO-SSC in each of these record files was thoroughly evaluated for completeness in the three domains: Sign In, Time Out, and Sign Out. The WHO-SSC filled before and after COVID were compared to identify how the practice had changed over time. The WHO-SSC of elective cases was compared with that of emergency cases.

The WHO-SSC compliance rate served as our study’s main outcome measure. Whether or not the checklist was included in the patient’s chart determined compliance. If every item on the checklist was ticked, we considered it complete; if one or more items were left unchecked, we considered it incomplete. The findings were tabulated in graphs and charts using Excel. A retrospective descriptive audit of the WHO-SSC was conducted in two different time periods, examining differences between emergency and elective procedures. Statistical analysis of individual subsets was not performed.

Result

Of the total of 50 consecutive patient files reviewed in 2020, 19 were emergencies and 31 were electives. In 2023, 16 were emergencies and 34 were electives.

In 2020, 4 out of 19 emergency cases (21%) did not have the WHO-SSC; however, in 2023, 6 out of 16 emergency cases (38%) did not have the WHO-SSC filled. In the elective cases, however, 8 out of 31 (24%) in 2020 and 8 out of 34 (26%) in 2023 did not have the WHO-SSC filled. The compliance in filling the WHO-SSC in elective cases was comparable in the two timelines; however, it had decreased markedly in emergency cases.

Regarding the completeness of the WHO-SSC, the three domains – Sign In, Time Out, and Sign Out – were evaluated in detail from the WHO-SSC checklists retrieved. The completeness of the WHO-SSC in elective surgeries has been tabulated separately in the three domains and studied.

In the Sign-In section of the WHO-SSC, patient identification, allergy, and airway risks were found to be adequately completed in both 2020 and 2023. The most notable progress was seen in site marking, which was done in only 61% of cases in 2020 but increased to 92% in 2023 (Fig. 1). In the Sign-In domain, there was a stark difference noted between the elective and emergency subgroups. The patient’s identity was confirmed only 53% of the time, and site marking was done in only 20% of cases. In neurosurgical emergencies, “time” is a very important factor; however, surgical errors can occur in emergency surgeries as well as in electives. Discussion of allergy and airway risks was also found to be inadequately addressed in emergency cases, around 30–35%.

In the Time-Out domain too, the WHO-SSC was less adequately filled in emergency cases (around 67%) compared to elective cases (around 90%).

Probably, when the neurosurgical emergency was over and while the patient was being shifted out, the various categories of the WHO-SSC were comparably filled in both emergency and elective cases, around 70%.

Discussion

The result of this study revealed that out of 100 patient files retrieved, the compliance rate was found to be only 74% in 2020 and 76% in 2023 for elective cases, both of which are comparable. Likewise, for emergency cases, the compliance rate was 79% in 2020, which markedly decreased to 62% in 2023. The fact that 100% compliance with this critical document was not achieved is of significant concern, as it compromises patient safety and the quality of surgical care[7]. This finding was supported by a similar study conducted in a peripheral hospital in Sri Lanka, where out of 102 files retrieved, only an 87.25% compliance rate was found, while 12.75% lacked this critical document[8].

The completeness was assessed in all three domains – Sign In, Time Out, and Sign Out – and components such as site marking were not filled in 39% of the files retrieved, which is a huge number. Additionally, confirmation of whether the prophylactic antibiotic was given or not was not checked in 12% of the files retrieved. Wrong-side surgery and omission of prophylactic antibiotics are among the most consequential preventable complications[9] and incompleteness in these two parameters is of significant concern. In a European study conducted in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology, evaluating the completeness of components in each of the three domains of the WHO-SSC in gynecologic surgery, the mean completeness was found to be only 22%, while the mean compliance was found to be 93%[10].

In an Iranian hospital, with the implementation of the WHO-SSC, the incidence of complications reduced from 23% to 10%[11]. A study conducted in the Netherlands reported a significant reduction in perioperative mortality when the WHO-SSC was completed. A univariate study was conducted comparing the outcomes when the WHO-SSC was completed, incompletely filled, or not completed. When the WHO-SSC was completed, the odds ratio (OR) was 0.44 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.28–0.70]; when the WHO-SSC was incomplete, the OR was 1.09 (95% CI 0.78–1.52); and when the WHO-SSC was not completed, the OR was 1.16 (95% CI 0.86–1.56)[12]. Though we have not evaluated the compliance of the WHO-SSC with patient outcomes, the results of all of these studies support the fact that compliance impacts the outcomes.

A study conducted in New Zealand, which evaluated compliance in the use of the WHO-SSC, found similarly low rates of checklist administration. In that study, the checklist usage in the Sign-In domain was 99%, while it was only 2% in the Sign-Out domain[13]. However, in our study, the completeness in the Sign-Out domain was comparatively high, consistently more than 83% in elective cases.

Regular audits of these critical practices, such as filling out the WHO-SSC, should be conducted. We found that, although in elective cases the compliance with filling out the WHO-SSC was comparable in the years 2020 and 2023, the compliance in emergency cases in UDM-NINAS fell from 79% to 62%. In a study by Anwer et al[14] conducted in the Department of General Surgery at Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre, Karachi, it was observed that after starting to fill out the WHO-SSC over a period of 4 years in 3638 patients, the compliance improved from 20.4% in the first year to 89.9% in the fourth year. It is likely that, following the conduction of this study, the compliance with filling out the WHO-SSC with completeness has improved in UDM-NINAS.

In our study duration, there was no wrong side/site surgery documented; however, in a study by Panesar et al[15], where the National Reporting and Learning Services database of the year 2008 was reviewed for all incidents of wrong site surgery in orthopaedics, it was identified that 14.9% of all wrong side errors could have been prevented by the WHO-SSC.

The limitations of our study are that it was not conducted in real time but as a retrospective review from the patient’s charts. The absence of the WHO-SSC in the patient chart could have been an error in record-keeping by the nurses while sending the records to the record section, but it was taken as non-compliance. As there are multiple domains in Sign In, Time Out, and Sign Out, the completeness of the WHO-SSC was assessed by the investigators, and a clear definition of completeness was not decided prior to the conduction of the study. A limited number of consecutive patients’ charts were studied – 50 in 2020 and 50 in 2023 – and it was a single-institution study.

Conclusion

WHO-SSC is a proven tool helping reduce surgical errors, morbidity, and even mortality. Regular audits and discussions of this important tool are imperative in clinical practice. If not regularly stressed, the compliance with conducting WHO-SSC wanes over time. The outcome of emergency neurosurgery is time-bound, and neurosurgeons tend to rapidly start operations to save lives; however, there can be human errors in emergency surgeries too, so WHO-SSC should be carried out even in neurosurgical emergencies and not just in electives, to prevent errors that can result in devastating consequences.
