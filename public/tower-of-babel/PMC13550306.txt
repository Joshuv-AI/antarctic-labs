Glutathione peroxidase gene regulates heat stress response via trehalose synthesis in Pleurotus ostreatus
Authors: Guang Zhang, Jiajia Wang, Li Shang, Mengmeng Ding, Zhongwei Wu, Qian An, Yanjie Wang, Chaohui Zhang, Bin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractHeat stress (HS) is a critical bottleneck restricting the commercial cultivation of Pleurotus ostreatus, severely compromising its yield and quality. Glutathione peroxidase (GPX) is a conserved antioxidant enzyme essential for stress adaptation, yet its regulatory mechanism in the fungal heat stress response (HSR) remains unclear. This study characterized a GPX homolog PoGPX in P. ostreatus and elucidated its functions in fungal growth, abiotic stress resistance, and HSR. PoGPX was downregulated during fruiting body development and upregulated under multiple abiotic stresses (especially HS). Silencing PoGPX promoted mycelial growth, biomass accumulation, and hyphal hyperbranching, accompanied by elevated intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and compensatory changes in antioxidant enzyme activities. Notably, silencing PoGPX increased cytosolic trehalose content by 0.45–0.62-fold compared with the wild type (WT) strain, and this elevation was reversed by treatment with the ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine, implying PoGPX modulates trehalose biosynthesis through ROS signaling. Functionally, silencing PoGPX alleviated HS-induced oxidative damage (reduced malondialdehyde content) and suppressed the upregulation of heat shock protein (HSP) genes expression (HSP60, HSP90, HSP104). Critically, inhibiting trehalose biosynthesis restored the HSR phenotypes in PoGPX-silenced strains, indicating trehalose as the key downstream mediator of PoGPX-regulated HSR. These findings uncover that PoGPX regulates HSR in P. ostreatus through trehalose biosynthesis induced by intracellular ROS levels. This study provides novel insights into fungal HSR mechanisms and identifies PoGPX as a promising target for genetic engineering to improve the thermotolerance of P. ostreatus in commercial cultivation.Key points • PoGPX acts as a key negative regulator on mycelial growth and hyphal branching in P. ostreatus. • The PoGPX silencing alleviates the heat stress response of P. ostreatus. • PoGPX regulates the heat stress response in P. ostreatus by modulating cytosolic trehalose biosynthesis. Introduction

Pleurotus ostreatus, commonly known as the oyster mushroom, is a commercially significant edible fungus widely cultivated around the world. Currently, it has emerged as a model mushroom in genetics, cell biology and material sciences (Nakazawa et al. 2024). Valued for its considerable nutritional and medicinal properties, P. ostreatus contains bioactive polysaccharides, triterpenoid saponins, flavonoids, a low lipid content and high levels of unsaturated fatty acids. These components contribute to its antioxidant and immunomodulatory activities, establishing it as an important functional food (Shamugam and Kertesz 2023). To date, P. ostreatus ranks among the most cultivated mushroom species globally. However, its cultivation often occurs in horticultural facilities, such as plastic greenhouses that lack sophisticated environmental control systems (Zhang et al. 2023). As a result, the fungus is frequently subjected to heat stress (HS) during high-temperature conditions in summer, which often leads to diminished yield and quality. Elucidating the mechanisms underlying its heat stress response (HSR) has therefore become increasingly urgent and is essential for developing strategies to improve cultivation practices.

HS is a major abiotic stressor that impairs fungal growth and development in edible fungi. Extensive research has been devoted to elucidating the HSR of P. ostreatus, making it a focal point in current mycology research (Yuan et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023). In P. ostreatus, HS induces mycelial growth retardation (Lei et al. 2019), compromises cell wall integrity (Qiu et al. 2018), upregulates the expression of HSP (Zou et al. 2018) and reduces resistance to pathogens (Hu et al. 2022). Moreover, HS triggers excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in oxidative damage to cellular macromolecules and apoptotic-like cell death of P. ostreatus (Song et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2017b). ROS are recognized as key secondary messengers that regulate diverse physiological processes through intracellular signaling pathways (Gerber et al. 2025). Previous studies have indicated that ROS accumulation can stimulate cytosolic trehalose biosynthesis to modulate the HSR in P. ostreatus (Lei et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2023). Further investigations revealed that trehalose suppresses glycolytic flux and lactate accumulation during HS, while also enhancing the production of reduced nicotinamide-adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) and glutathione (GSH), thereby mitigating HS-induced damage in P. ostreatus (Yan et al. 2021). In Rhizoctonia solani, trehalose significantly inhibits the activity of ROS-related enzymes and oxidative burst (Wang et al. 2018). Similarly, trehalose pretreatment in tomato enhances salt tolerance by regulating ROS and trehalose metabolic pathways (Yang et al. 2022). These findings suggest a close interplay between trehalose metabolism and ROS homeostasis. However, the relationship between ROS and trehalose metabolism under HS in P. ostreatus remains unclear.

Glutathione peroxidase (GPX) is a crucial antioxidant enzyme involved in ROS metabolism. It catalyzes the conversion of GSH into glutathione disulfide (GSSG) while reducing H2O2 into H2O, thereby helping to maintain redox homeostasis by scavenging intracellular ROS and modulating ROS-mediated signaling (Lennicke and Cochemé 2021). Evidence shows that GPX not only protects cells from oxidative damage but also acts as a redox sensor, transducing oxidative signals to downstream effectors in stress-responsive cascades (Tyagi et al. 2018; Zhou et al. 2022). While GPX has been extensively studied in animals and plants, its role in in filamentous fungi remains underexplored. Overexpression of GPX in Cordyceps militaris improved redox homeostasis under oxidative stress and restored the fruiting capacity in sterile mutants (Xiong et al. 2013). In Ganoderma lucidum, GPX plays an essential role in hyphal branching, antioxidant stress tolerance and ganoderic acid biosynthesis (Li et al. 2015). In Volvariella volvacea, GPX modulates redox homeostasis, influences substrate degradation ability and promotes fruiting body regeneration in degenerated strains (Wang et al. 2025b). Although the role of GPX in ROS homeostasis is established in several filamentous fungi, whether and how GPX interacts with trehalose-mediated stress adaptation pathways remains unknown.

To clarify the relationship between GPX-mediated redox homeostasis and trehalose-mediated stress adaptation under HS, we identified and characterized a GPX homolog (PoGPX) in P. ostreatus. Using RNA interference, we investigated the functional role of PoGPX and its connection with trehalose metabolism under HS. Our results demonstrate that PoGPX is essential for mitigating HSR by regulating trehalose biosynthesis through ROS-mediated signaling. This study unveils a novel link between GPX-dependent redox control and trehalose metabolism, providing new insights into the HSR mechanism in edible fungi and suggesting potential strategies for enhancing thermotolerance in cultivation.

Materials and methods

Strains and growth conditions

The P. ostreatus P89 dikaryon (ACCC50596, Agricultural Culture Collection of China) was used as a parental strain to construct PoGPX-silenced strains (Zhang et al. 2023). The potato dextrose agar (PDA), complete yeast medium (CYM) and seed cultures were prepared following our established protocols (Zhang et al. 2022, 2023). The wild type (WT), empty vector control (SiControl) and PoGPX-silenced strains were grown on CYM (2% glucose, 1% maltose, 0.2% tryptone, 0.2% yeast extract, 0.046% KH2PO4, and 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O) at 28 °C. The fungal fermentation experiments were conducted in liquid CYM at 150 rpm for 7 days as described in our previous study (Zhang et al. 2022). The P. ostreatus fruiting body growth experiment on solid culture compost was performed as previously described (Wang et al. 2017; Hou et al. 2022). The Escherichia coli DH5α strain (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) used for plasmid construction and the Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (IMCAS, Beijing, China) used for fungal transformation were cultured as described elsewhere (Zhang et al. 2023).

Cloning and sequence analysis of the PoGPX gene

Using the sequences of GPX from G. lucidum (ASU11070.1) and Cryptococcus neoformans (AUB25123.1), homologs of GPX were identified in the genome database of P89 as described previously (Zhang et al. 2022). A close homolog was identified and designated PoGPX (PP681125). The genomic DNA and cDNA sequences of PoGPX were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from P89 using the primers listed in Supplementary Table S1. The theoretical molecular weight and isoelectric point (pI) of PoGPX were predicted using the Expasy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The conserved domains present in GPX were analyzed with SMART (https://smart.embl.de/) and NCBI's Conserved Domains Database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd) (Yang et al. 2020). The phylogenetic tree of fungal GPX proteins was developed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA 7.0 software (Kumar et al. 2016).

Abiotic stress treatments

Preliminary studies indicated that GPX mediates thermotolerance and oxidative stress resistance. Therefore, we first examined the expression of PoGPX in the WT strain under HS, oxidative stress, cell wall stress and osmotic stress. For HS treatment, fungal strains cultured in liquid CYM at 28 °C for 3 days were exposed to 40 °C for 12 h, followed by recovery at 28 °C for 3 days (Zhang et al. 2023). For other stress treatments, fungal strains were cultured on CYM plates supplemented with H2O2 (4 mM), Congo red (CR, 2 mg/mL), lysing enzyme (1 mg/mL) or sorbitol (0.6 M) at 28 °C for 7 days.

Generation of PoGPX-RNAi plasmids and strains

RNA interference (RNAi) plasmids and PoGPX-silenced strains were constructed according to our previous study (Zhang et al. 2022). Briefly, an antisense fragment of of PoGPX (302 bp) was amplified by PCR, digested with ApaⅠ and BglⅡ, and inserted into the RNAi plasmid pHPG (Zhang et al. 2022) at the corresponding restriction sites, generating the PoGPX-silenced vector RNAi-PoGPX (Supplementary Fig. S1). The RNAi-PoGPX plasmid was then introduced into the P. ostreatus WT strain via A. tumefaciens-mediated transformation (Lei et al. 2017). A control strain (SiControl) was generated using the empty pHPG vector without the PoGPX fragment. The transformants were randomly selected using hygromycin B (hyg) and verified by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) as reported earlier (Zhang et al. 2022). Three independent PoGPX-silenced strains (GPXi-7, GPXi-13 and GPXi-16) exhibiting high silencing efficiency were selected for further experiments. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Detection of GSH

The P. ostreatus strains (WT, SiControl, and PoGPX-silenced strains) were cultured in liquid CYM at 28 °C for 7 days. Fungal mycelia were then collected, ground into powder with liquid nitrogen, and used for GSH content measurement. The GSH level was determined using a commercial GSH assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) by measuring the absorbance at 420 nm with a spectrophotometer.

Measurement of the length between hyphal branches

To determine the length between hyphal branches, P. ostreatus strains (WT, SiControl, and PoGPX-silenced strains) were cultured on CYM plates, after which sterilized cover slips were inserted into the medium as described previously (Zhang et al. 2021). The cover slips with adhered mycelia were stained with lactophenol cotton blue solution for 10 min. Hyphal morphology was observed using a Leica DM500 microscope equipped with a Leica ICC50 camera (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), and the distance between hyphae branches was analyzed as described elsewhere (Zhang et al. 2021). Additionally, protoplasts generated from the mycelia of PoGPX-silenced strains were analyzed as in our previous study (Zhang et al. 2021). Briefly, the fungal mycelia were collected from liquid CYM for 3 days, and resuspended in a 0.6 M mannitol solution containing 2% (w/v) lysing enzyme (Guangdong Microbiology Culture Center, China) for 2 h at 30 °C. Then, the number of protoplasts generated from fungal mycelia was analyzed using a hemocytometer.

Detection of intracellular ROS level

Intracellular ROS levels were measured as described previously with minor modifications (Zhang et al. 2021). Briefly, fungal mycelia grown on cover slips in CYM plates were stained with 10 μM 2′, 7′- dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) at 28 °C for 30 min. Fluorescence signals from intracellular H2O2 were observed using a LSM780 confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany), and fluorescence intensity was quantified with ZEN 3.3 (blue edition) software (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany) according to our established method (Liu et al. 2024). Additionally, the H2O2 content in mycelia harvested from liquid CYM were measured using a commercial H2O2 assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Enzymatic activity assays

Fungal strains (WT, SiControl and PoGPX-silenced strains) were cultured in liquid CYM under the specified conditions. Fungal mycelia were then collected and processed as described previously (Zhang et al. 2022). Protein content in fungal samples was measured using the Bradford method. The activities of GPX, NADPH oxidase (NOX), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) were determined using corresponding commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), following procedures outlined in our earlier studies (Zhang et al. 2017a, 2018, 2021, 2022).

Determination of cytosolic trehalose content

The fungal strains (WT, SiControl and PoGPX-Silenced strains) were cultured in liquid CYM at 28 °C for 7 days. Fungal mycelia were then collected, washed with distilled water, dried at 60 °C, and ground into fine powder. The cytosolic trehalose content was measured according to previously described methods (Lei et al. 2019). In brief, samples were suspended in 0.25 M Na2CO3 followed by supersonic extraction (20 kHz) at 60 °C for 1 h. Acetic acid (1 M) and sodium acetate (0.2 M) were subsequently added into the mixture. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. Half of the homogenates were incubated with 0.025 U/mL trehalase at 37 °C overnight, and the remaining half was used as the control. Finally, samples were assayed for glucose with a Glucose Assay Kit (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

Determination of malondialdehyde (MDA) content

The fungal strains (WT, SiControl and PoGPX-silenced strains) were cultivated in liquid CYM under normal or HS conditions as indicated above. The intracellular MDA content was analyzed using a MDA assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) based on the thiobarbituric acid method (Hou et al. 2019).

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from fungal strains grown under the indicated conditions, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized following our previous study (Zhang et al. 2023). qRT-PCR was carried out on a Realplex2 System (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) using EvaGreen 2 × qPCR MasterMix-S (ABM, Richmond, Canada) as reported elsewhere (Zhang et al. 2021). Relative gene-specific mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method, with the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase gene (GAPDH) as the internal control (Yan et al. 2021). The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Data were obtained from at least three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla CA, USA; http://www.graphpad.com). The results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significant differences between the analyzed samples (p < 0.05) were determined by Duncan′s multiple range test and are indicated by different lowercase letters.

Results

Cloning and sequence analysis of the PoGPX gene

To identify potential GPX homologs in P. ostreatus, BLAST searches were performed against the P89 genome database using GPX sequences from G. lucidum (ASU11070.1) and C. neoformans (AUB25123.1) as queries. A close homolog was identified and designated PoGPX (GenBank accession number: PP681125), with E-values of 3 × 10⁻⁷3 and 3 × 10⁻54, respectively. The open reading frame of PoGPX is 483 bp and was interrupted by four introns. The encoded protein of PoGPX consists of 160 amino acids, with a predicted molecular weight of 17.95 kDa and a theoretical pI of 6.83.

The instability index and aliphatic index of PoGPX were calculated as 27.75 and 77.38, respectively, indicating that PoGPX is a stable protein with potential multifunctional roles. Domain analysis revealed that PoGPX contains a thioredoxin/glutathione peroxidase BtuE domain, belonging to the BtuE superfamily (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, PoGPX showed a reciprocal best hit to GPX from G. lucidum and Mycena maculata (Fig. 1B), with sequence similarities of 80.39% and 74.21%, respectively. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that PoGPX shares the highest homology with GPX from G. lucidum and clusters separately from those of ascomycetes (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Expression patterns of PoGPX across developmental stages and under abiotic stress

The development of P. ostreatus comprises six stages: mycelia, primordia, morula, coral, forming, and mature stages (Zhang et al. 2022). We first examined the expression of PoGPX during these developmental stages and in different tissues of the fruiting body in the WT strain. Results showed that the expression of PoGPX was significantly downregulated in primordia (0.48-fold), morula (0.02-fold), coral (0.09-fold), forming (0.18-fold) and mature (0.18-fold) stages, compared with that in the mycelia stage (Fig. 2A). In fruiting bodies, PoGPX expression was upregulated in gills (2.72-fold) and downregulated in stipes (0.07-fold), compared to that in caps (Fig. 2B).

HS is a major abiotic stress affecting fungal development. To evaluate the response of PoGPX to HS, P. ostreatus was exposed to 40 °C for varying durations. Expression of PoGPX in mycelia stage of P. ostreatus increased significantly by 3.86-, 4.26-, and 5.37-fold after 9, 12, and 24 h of HS treatment, respectively, compared to the control (Fig. 2C). However, expression of PoGPX in primordia stage of P. ostreatus upregulated by 1.00-, 1.49-, and 1.82-fold after 3, 6, and 9 h of HS treatment, respectively, compared to the control (Supplementary Fig. S3). We also assessed PoGPX expression under other common abiotic stress. Under oxidative stress induced by H2O2, PoGPX expression was 1.86-fold that of the control (Fig. 2D). Under cell wall stress triggered by CR or lysing enzyme, PoGPX expression levels rose to 2.51- and 2.50-fold of the control, respectively (Fig. 2D). Under osmotic stress with sorbitol, PoGPX expression was elevated 2.42-fold compared to the control (Fig. 2D). Together, these findings suggest that PoGPX is involved in the regulatory response of P. ostreatus to multiple abiotic stresses.

Construction of PoGPX-silenced strains

To investigate the physiological functions of PoGPX, silencing vectors (RNAi-PoGPX) were constructed using the pHPG vector (Zhang et al. 2022) and introduced into P. ostreatus. Silencing efficiency of the transformants was confirmed by qRT-PCR. Three independent PoGPX-silenced strains (GPXi-7, GPXi-13 and GPXi-16) were randomly selected from approximately 52 positive transformants. Compared to the WT strain, transcript levels of PoGPX in GPXi-7, GPXi-13, and GPXi-16 were reduced by 0.84-, 0.93-, and 0.87-fold, respectively (Fig. 3A). Consistently, GPX activity in these strains decreased by 0.83-, 0.84-, and 0.92-fold (Fig. 3B), respectively, compared with the WT strain. Since GPX catalyzes the conversion of GSH to GSSG, we also measured GSH content in the silenced strains. The GSH content in GPXi-7, GPXi-13, and GPXi-16 was increased by 1.09-, 0.99-, and 1.04-fold, respectively, relative to the WT strain (Fig. 3C). These validated PoGPX-silenced strains were used in subsequent experiments.

Silencing PoGPX promotes mycelial growth and hyphal branching in P. ostreatus

To examine the effect of PoGPX silencing on fungal growth, PoGPX-silenced strains were cultured on CYM plates at 28 °C for 7 days, and mycelial diameters were measured. Silencing of PoGPX significantly enhanced mycelial growth (Fig. 4A). The mycelial diameters of GPXi-7, GPXi-13 and GPXi-16 strains were 5.93 cm, 6.27 cm and 6.80 cm, representing increases of 0.16-, 0.23- and 0.33-fold, respectively, compared with the WT strain (Fig. 4B). Mycelial biomass and pellet morphology were further evaluated in liquid CYM. The PoGPX-silenced strains produced larger and bristlier mycelial pellets than the WT strain (Fig. 4A). Biomass of GPXi-7, GPXi-13, and GPXi-16 reached 0.49 g, 0.46 g, and 0.50 g, corresponding to increases of 0.40-, 0.32-, and 0.41-fold, respectively (Fig. 4C), compared with the WT strain. These results suggest that PoGPX is involved in regulating normal growth in P. ostreatus.

As hyphal branching is a key determinant of mycelial colony formation and fungal growth (Tominaga et al. 2023), we next examined the hyphal branching phenotype of PoGPX-silenced strains on CYM plates. The aerial hyphae of PoGPX-silenced strains exhibited a highly branched morphology compared to the WT strain (Supplementary Fig. S4A). The lengths between hyphal branches in GPXi-7, GPXi-13 and GPXi-16 were 143.98 μm, 152.27 μm and 109.33 μm, respectively, corresponding to reductions of 0.67-, 0.65-, and 0.75-fold relative to the WT (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Given that hyphal branching influences protoplast release (Zhang et al. 2021), we also quantified protoplast production from fresh mycelia. The number of protoplasts released from GPXi-7, GPXi-13 and GPXi-16 was 1.76-, 1.62- and 1.82-fold that of the WT (Supplementary Fig. S4C), respectively. Together, these data indicate that silencing PoGPX induces a hyperbranching phenotype in P. ostreatus.

Silencing PoGPX increases intracellular ROS levels in P. ostreatus

To further investigate the role of PoGPX in oxidative stress, we used a H2O2 fluorescence probe DCFH-DA to detect H2O2 levels in PoGPX-silenced strains. Fluorescence intensity was significantly stronger in PoGPX-silenced strains than in the WT (Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis showed fluorescence values were 68.88, 63.67, and 69.55 for GPXi-7, GPXi-13, and GPXi-16, respectively, corresponding to 1.93-, 1.79-, and 1.95-fold increases over the WT (Fig. 5B). Intracellular H2O2 levels in GPXi-7, GPXi-13, and GPXi-16 were 38.51, 42.16, and 42.57 mM/g protein, which were 1.86-, 2.03-, and 2.05-fold higher, respectively, than those in the WT (Fig. 5C).

To explore how PoGPX influences intracellular ROS, we analyzed key antioxidant enzyme activities in PoGPX-silenced strains. Activities of NOX (which generates ROS), CAT (which decomposes H2O2) and SOD (which dismutates superoxide) in PoGPX-silenced strains were increased by approximately 0.59–0.68-, 0.91–1.55-, and 0.46–0.57-fold, respectively, compared to the WT (Supplementary Fig. S5). In contrast, APX activity in PoGPX-silenced strains decreased by approximately 0.34–0.44 fold relative to the WT (Supplementary Fig. S5). We also examined the expression of genes encoding these enzymes. Transcript levels of NoxA, CAT1, SOD1, SOD2, and SOD4 were significantly upregulated in PoGPX-silenced strains, while APX expression was downregulated compared to the WT (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Silencing PoGPX enhances cytosolic trehalose accumulation

Since ROS accumulation has been shown to induce trehalose biosynthesis in P. ostreatus (Lei et al. 2019), we examined the cytosolic trehalose content in PoGPX-silenced strains. The cytosolic trehalose contents in GPXi-7, GPXi-13 and GPXi-16 strains were 113.33 mg/g, 101.67 mg/g and 111.00-mg/g dry weight (DW), representing increases of approximately 0.62-, 0.45-, and 0.59-fold, respectively, compared to the WT strain (Fig. 6A). We further analyzed the expression of genes encoding two key enzymes in trehalose biosynthesis: trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS) and trehalose-6-phosphate phosphatase (TPP). The transcript levels of TPS and TPP in PoGPX-silenced strains were approximately 3.02–3.41-fold and 3.15–4.57-fold higher, respectively, than those in the WT (Fig. 6B).

Effects of NAC on trehalose biosynthesis in PoGPX-silenced strains

Given that intracellular ROS levels can induce trehalose biosynthesis (Lei et al. 2019), we used the ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAC) to examine whether PoGPX regulates trehalose production via ROS signaling. As expected, the elevated trehalose content in PoGPX-silenced strains was reduced to WT levels upon NAC treatment and increased again when H2O2 was added together with NAC (Fig. 7A). Similarly, the expression of TPS in PoGPX-silenced strains followed the same trend as trehalose content (Fig. 7B). These results indicate that PoGPX modulates trehalose biosynthesis through intracellular ROS levels in P. ostreatus.

Silencing PoGPX alleviates the HSR in P. ostreatus

Since our previous study demonstrated that cytosolic trehalose plays a key role in the HSR of P. ostreatus (Zhang et al. 2023), we examined the HSR of PoGPX-silenced strains exposed to HS. The MDA content and HSP (HSP60, HSP90, and HSP104) expression are two indicators of HSR in P. ostreatus (Zhang et al. 2023). MDA serves as a marker for oxidative damage (lipid peroxidation) induced by excessive ROS accumulation under HS (Mejía-Barajas et al. 2017; Lei et al. 2019). Under normal conditions, the MDA contents in GPXi-7, GPXi-13, and GPXi-16 were increased by approximately 0.62-, 0.68-, and 0.71-fold, respectively, compared to the WT (Fig. 8A). However, under HS treatment, the MDA contents in PoGPX-silenced strains returned to those of the WT (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, the HS-induced upregulation of HSP60, HSP90, and HSP104 expression observed in the WT was significantly attenuated in the PoGPX-silenced strains (Fig. 8B–D). These results indicate that silencing PoGPX alleviates the HSR in P. ostreatus.

Effects of inhibiting cytosolic trehalose biosynthesis on HSR of PoGPX-silenced strains

To further elucidate the role of cytosolic trehalose in the PoGPX-mediated HSR, we inhibited trehalose biosynthesis in PoGPX-silenced strains using validoxylamine A (Vdm), a known transition-state inhibitor of fungal TPS1 proteins (Miao et al. 2017). Under HS, Vdm treatment reduced both the elevated trehalose accumulation and the upregulated TPS expression in PoGPX-silenced strains to levels comparable to the WT. This effect was reversed when trehalose was supplied together with Vdm (Supplementary Fig. S7). Consistently, Vdm treatment increased the MDA content in PoGPX-silenced strains under HS to match that of the WT, while the addition of exogenous trehalose together with Vdm restored MDA content to the level observed in PoGPX-silenced strains under HS (Fig. 9A). Similarly, the attenuated expression of HSP60 in PoGPX-silenced strains under HS was elevated by Vdm to WT levels, and this effect was again reversed by concurrent trehalose supplementation (Fig. 9B). Together, these results indicate that PoGPX regulates the HSR in P. ostreatus by modulating cytosolic trehalose content.

Discussion

As a core antioxidant enzyme in organisms, GPX is well-recognized for its irreplaceable role in scavenging ROS and mitigating oxidative damage to cells (do Carmo Santos et al. 2025). Extensive studies have further confirmed that GPX acts as a key regulator of intracellular ROS homeostasis, which is essential for maintaining normal cellular metabolism and stress adaptation. Although the functions of GPX in filamentous fungi have been partially reported (Li et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2025b), systematic investigations into its regulatory role and underlying molecular mechanisms in the HSR of fungi remain scarce. Notably, previous work on P. ostreatus showed intracellular ROS plays a pivotal role in the HSR (Zhang et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024), indicating that GPX may be involved in this process. To address this, we characterized the PoGPX gene, and explored its roles in abiotic stresses, fungal growth, trehalose biosynthesis, and HSR in P. ostreatus. Our results revealed that PoGPX alleviates the HSR by modulating trehalose biosynthesis regulated by intracellular ROS in P. ostreatus.

PoGPX functions as a negative regulator of mycelial growth and hyphal branching in P. ostreatus. PoGPX showed a downregulated expression patterns across the developmental stages compared with that of the mycelial stage (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, silencing PoGPX enhanced mycelial expansion, increased biomass accumulation (Fig. 4), and induced a hyperbranching phenotype, accompanied by elevated protoplast release (Supplementary Fig. S4). The growth-promoting effect of PoGPX silencing is likely mediated by perturbed intracellular ROS homeostasis. As a key ROS-scavenging enzyme, PoGPX normally maintains ROS at basal levels; its silencing leads to moderate ROS accumulation, which acts as a signaling molecule to stimulate hyphal branching and polar growth. This is consistent with previous reports that controlled ROS elevation regulates filamentous growth in fungi (Mu et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2025b). In V. volvacea, silencing GPX alters the homeostasis of the redox system and affects the substrate degradation ability of the fungus, which alters the physiological traits of V. volvacea mycelium (Wang et al. 2025b). However, silencing GPX induces a less hyphal branching phenotype in G. lucidum, and GPX regulates hyphal branching via intracellular ROS levels, which is independent of cytosolic Ca2+ level (Li et al. 2015). Similarly, ROS can act as signaling molecules to regulate root hair initiation and tip growth in plants (Gerber et al. 2025). Together, we speculate that PoGPX modulates fungal growth and branching in P. ostreatus by balancing ROS signaling and redox metabolism, ensuring coordination between oxidative protection and developmental demands.

GPX is a highly conserved enzyme with multifaceted roles in stress adaptation across diverse biological systems. In mammalian cells, accumulating evidence highlights that GPX, together with thioredoxin reductase, forms a key defense module against oxidative stress and nitrosative stress, thereby maintaining cellular redox homeostasis (Benhar 2018; Chai and Mieyal 2023). In plants, GPXs are not only central to maintaining redox balance but also act as critical mediators of stress signaling. For example, silencing TaGPX3.2A in wheat compromises resistance to the fungal pathogen Fusarium graminearum, underscoring its importance in plant-pathogen interactions (Jiang et al. 2023). Building on this conserved role of GPX, we explored the critical role of PoGPX against multiple abiotic stresses in P. ostreatus. The transcription of PoGPX was significantly upregulated under diverse stress conditions, including HS (Fig. 2C), oxidative stress, cell wall stress, and osmotic stress (Fig. 2D). Notably, PoGPX exhibited the strongest induction under HS at the mycelia stage, indicating it is particularly important for redox regulation during HS. Furthermore, the changes in PoGPX transcripts under HS at the mycelia stage were greater than those at the primordia stage of P. ostreatus under HS (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. S3), implying that HS impairs P. ostreatus mycelia far more severely than primordia and fruiting bodies, suppressing overall growth and development. In addition, silencing PoGPX disrupted the redox balance in P. ostreatus, which in turn led to excessive ROS accumulation (Fig. 5) and compensatory changes in the activity of other antioxidant enzymes (Supplementary Fig. S5). For cell wall and osmotic stress, the upregulation of PoGPX likely mitigates the oxidative burst commonly triggered by cell wall damage or osmotic imbalance (Hopke et al. 2018). Consistently, previous studies in other fungi have supported this role of GPX. In G. lucidum, GPX plays a central role in fungal resistance to oxidative stress, as evidenced by the upregulation of GPX transcript levels under H2O2 treatment (Li et al. 2015). Besides, deletion of AaGPX3 in Alternaria alternata increases its sensitivity to H2O2 and ROS-generating compounds (Yang et al. 2016). Our findings thus further confirm the conserved role of GPX in fungal adaption to multiple stresses, offering a key regulatory target for future genetic engineering strategies to enhance stress resistance in fungi.

As a nonreducing disaccharide, trehalose acts as a key mediator in multiple dimensions of stress-related physiology in yeasts and fungi (Ribeiro et al. 2024). Previous studies have clearly demonstrated that trehalose plays a pivotal role in fungal resistance to HS in P. ostreatus (Lei et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2023). Building on this understanding, we observed a significant increase in cytosolic trehalose content in PoGPX-silenced strains in the present study (Fig. 6). Additionally, the intracellular polysaccharide content in PoGPX-silenced strains was decreased compared with that in the WT strain (data not shown). These observations collectively suggest that PoGPX is involved in regulating metabolic processes in P. ostreatus. Consistent with our findings, GPX has been reported to modulate ganoderic acid biosynthesis in G. lucidum (Li et al. 2015), further supporting the conserved role of GPX in fungal metabolic regulation. Notably, the intracellular ROS levels were significantly elevated in PoGPX-silenced strains (Fig. 5). Moreover, treatment with the ROS scavenger NAC effectively reversed the elevated TPS expression and trehalose biosynthesis in PoGPX-silenced strains (Fig. 7). These results are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that intracellular ROS levels can induce trehalose biosynthesis in P. ostreatus under HS (Lei et al. 2019; Yan et al. 2021). In G. lucidum, glycogen synthase kinase 3 can interact with TPS and phosphorylate it at serine 529, thereby enhancing its enzymatic activity under HS (Wang et al. 2025a). In plants exposed to various stresses, the expression of TPSs and TPPs is regulated at the transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and post-translational stages, resulting in changes in trehalose-6-phosphate and trehalose accumulation (Eh et al. 2025). However, the mechanism by which intracellular ROS regulates trehalose synthesis in P. ostreatus remains poorly understood and requires further investigation in future studies. Nevertheless, our results here imply that PoGPX regulates trehalose biosynthesis by modulating intracellular ROS levels.

HS severely impacts the growth and development of P. ostreatus, leading to accumulated ROS levels (Lei et al. 2019), impaired cell wall integrity (Qiu et al. 2018), spawn burning and Trichoderma contamination (Qiu et al. 2017). In the present study, HS induced increased MDA content and upregulated expression of HSP (HSP60, HSP90 and HSP104) in P. ostreatus (Fig. 8), which is consistent with previous reports (Zou et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2023). Moreover, silencing PoGPX effectively reversed the HS-induced increase in MDA content and upregulation of HSP expression (Fig. 8), indicating that PoGPX is involved in the HSR of P. ostreatus. It has been reported that trehalose, induced by intracellular ROS, alleviates HS in P. ostreatus by affecting central carbon metabolism (Yan et al. 2021). Similarly, when trehalose biosynthesis was inhibited by Vdm in PoGPX-silenced strains exposed to HS, both MDA content and HSP60 expression returned to levels comparable to those in the WT strain (Fig. 9). This implies that PoGPX regulates HSR in P. ostreatus by modulating trehalose biosynthesis. Our findings further reveal the mechanism by which PoGPX regulates HSR in fungus, providing new insights into the regulation of HSR in edible fungi.

In summary, we systematically elucidate the multifunctional roles and regulatory mechanisms of PoGPX in P. ostreatus. As a core antioxidant enzyme, PoGPX acts as a key negative regulator in mycelial growth and hyphal branching. Meanwhile, PoGPX plays a pivotal role in safeguarding this fungus against multiple abiotic stresses including HS, oxidative stress, cell wall stress and osmotic stress. Furthermore, we uncovered a critical mechanism that PoGPX modulates trehalose biosynthesis through intracellular ROS levels, and this metabolic link further mediates HSR in P. ostreatus (Fig. 10). Our findings provide novel insights into the key mechanism underlying fungal HSR, and uncover a promising target for genetic engineering to support the optimization of edible mushroom production systems.
