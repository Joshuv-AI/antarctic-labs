A diameter-adaptive, suture-free conduit with magnetoelectric responsiveness promotes peripheral nerve regeneration
Authors: Bo Ma, Hailin Ma, Kangling Xu, Baixin Gu, Junyu Su, Fengshi Zhang, Lingxin Duan, Yichong Zhang, Fei Yu, Yuhui Kou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe repair of peripheral nerve defects necessitates the use of nerve guidance conduits (NGCs). However, the diameter mismatch between NGC and nerve, the complex suturing process, and the slow rate of axonal growth collectively leads to unsatisfactory nerve regeneration. Therefore, an ideal NGC should be diameter-adaptive, suture-free, and capable of providing electrical signals to enhance nerve regeneration. Inspired by the heat shrink tubing, we present an IR-triggered contractile and suture-free NGC with magnetoelectric responsiveness named P/A/PFeC@PAA. P/A/PFeC@PAA is composed of Pluronic F127 diacrylate mixed with Pluronic F127 (collectively referred to as P), alginate (A), and polydopamine-chelated carbon nanotube–Fe3O4 nanohybrid (PFeC), with an adhesive inner layer formed by polymerized acrylic acid and acrylic acid-N-hydroxysuccinimide (PAA). Under IR irradiation, the photothermal effect of PFeC induces the micellar aggregation of P and subsequent contraction of conduit, while PAA mediates adhesion to the nerve stumps, together enabling diameter-adaptive, suture-free repair. In addition, under an alternating magnetic field, anisotropic PFeC generates electrical stimulation in a noninvasive and controllable manner. This multifunctional NGC can greatly simplify the surgical procedure and provide effective post-operative support for nerve regeneration.Keywords: Peripheral nerve injury, Nerve guidance conduit, Diameter-adaptive, Suture-free, Magnetoelectric responsiveness

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) refers to the structural and functional damage of nerve fibers caused by trauma, compression, or metabolic disorders, and it mainly manifests as sensory abnormalities, motor deficits, and autonomic dysfunction [1]. Afflicting over 20 million individuals in the United States, PNI frequently causes long-term disability that substantially compromises quality of life, highlighting the urgent need for effective therapeutic interventions [2,3].

Current clinical management of PNI employs diverse approaches. Neurorrhaphy is used to treat complete nerve transections without tissue loss by directly suturing the proximal and distal stumps to reconstruct nerve continuity [4]. However, when a nerve defect exists between the two stumps, tension-free direct repair becomes unfeasible, and bridging the gap between the nerve ends remains a major challenge in peripheral nerve reconstruction. For severe nerve defects exceeding 5 cm, autografting remains the gold standard and most effective strategy for functional reconstruction [5]. However, this approach has several significant limitations, including the limited availability of suitable donor nerves, sensory deficits at the donor site, neuroma formation, and potential nerve torsion or displacement after transplantation [6]. Allografting, such as the human acellular nerve allograft developed by Sun Yat-sen University, have been widely applied in the treatment of brachial plexus injuries and are considered a promising alternative to autografting for repairing nerve defects [7]. However, they require prolonged administration of immunosuppressive agents, which may cause systemic side effects and increase the risk of infection [8]. In recent years, numerous synthetic nerve conduits have been developed to repair long-gap nerve defects by incorporating hydrogels into the lumen or employing aligned electrospinning techniques. However, the efficacy of these NGCs still requires further clinical validation [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]].

For short or moderate nerve defects, such as those in the hand or maxillofacial region, as well as in nerve transposition repair, direct neurorrhaphy may induce excessive tension at the nerve stumps, thereby increasing the risk of surgical failure, whereas autografting, allografting, or customized NGCs are often less cost-effective [16,17]. To address this challenge, our team has developed a small-gap tubulization neurorrhaphy technique, which bridges short and moderate nerve defects by suturing the proximal and distal stumps into a small conduit, and has successfully enhanced nerve transposition repair outcomes in rat models [1,18]. However, the regenerative performance of the conduits remains unsatisfactory for several reasons. First, the surgical procedure is technically demanding and may cause secondary injury. Conventional neurorrhaphy requires multiple precise Epineurium-to-conduit suturing under a microscope, which demands highly skilled microsurgical techniques and prolongs the operation time, thereby increasing anesthesia risks and surgical costs. Moreover, the sutures and needles act as foreign bodies that penetrate neural tissue, leading to additional trauma and local inflammation, which disrupts the formation of a favorable regenerative microenvironment [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Second, the issue of diameter mismatch remains a major obstacle in conduit repair. Different nerves vary in diameter, and the proximal and distal stumps within the same nerve often differ in size due to injury-induced swelling or retraction, making it difficult to fabricate conduits with precisely matched inner diameters. Forced suturing between mismatched nerves and conduits may result in fascicular misalignment, excessive tension at the anastomosis site, or intraluminal scar formation, all of which impede axonal regeneration and functional recovery [23,24]. Third, most current conduits lack sustained regenerative support. Although bioactive factors or drugs are often incorporated into the conduit wall or lumen to enhance Schwann cell activity and axonal elongation, their release kinetics and long-term efficacy are usually suboptimal, and the intraluminal fillers may physically hinder directed axonal growth, ultimately compromising the overall repair outcome [25,26].

To supplant traditional suturing, some studies have modified NGC inner surfaces to adhere directly to the epineurium using chemical bonds—such as Schiff base bonds formed by polydopamine (PDA), hydrogen bonds via acrylic acid (AAc), and amide bonds via acrylic acid-N-hydroxysuccinimide (AAc-NHS) [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. However, as previously mentioned, the mismatch between the nerve diameter and the inner diameter of the NGC can markedly impair the adhesion performance. Diameter mismatch at the NGC–nerve interface frequently reduces contact area, consequently weakening adhesion and compromising the effectiveness of these suture alternatives. Furthermore, when the diameter of NGC substantially exceeds the nerve ends, axons and Schwann cells lose essential contact guidance, increasing the likelihood of both neuroma and scar tissue formation [31]. Therefore, developing NGCs with robust inner adhesion and diameter adaptability presents a viable strategy to eliminate suturing, thereby simplifying surgical procedures and reducing the risk of complications.

Beyond surgical process improvements, continuous regenerative stimulation is also essential for achieving effective functional recovery [[32], [33], [34]]. Electrical stimulation promotes nerve regeneration through multiple mechanisms [33,35]. The applied electric field enhances calcium activity in neuron, stimulating expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and its receptor tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB). This subsequently elevates intracellular cAMP levels, upregulating regeneration-associated phospholipid expression and thereby facilitating axonal growth. Furthermore, electrical stimulation also elevates intracellular Ca2+ levels in Schwann cells, activating the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway to promote Schwann cell proliferation and migration—processes critical for supporting axonal regeneration [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. Therefore, various conductive materials (e.g., carbon nanotube [CNT], gold, graphene, polyaniline, and polypyrrole) and piezoelectric fabrication techniques (e.g., 3D printing, dip-coating, electrospinning, and salt leaching) have been integrated into NGCs to deliver localized electrical stimulation [[40], [41], [42], [43]]. However, establishing stable, intimate contact at the NGC-nerve interface is a fundamental requirement for effective electrical signal delivery [44]. Therefore, robust inner-layer adhesion combined with diameter self-adaptability, enabling the conduit to conform to nerves of different sizes and maintain adequate interfacial contact, is equally critical for efficient electrical coupling.

In this study, inspired by heat shrink tubing—widely used for insulation and protection of electronic components, which can actively contract upon heating and tightly conform to the target—we designed a multifunctional NGC named P/A/PFeC@PAA (Fig. 1). The conduit scaffold consists of a dual-network hydrogel formed by ionically crosslinked alginate (A) and a UV-crosslinked network incorporating Pluronic F127 diacrylate (PF127DI) blended with unmodified Pluronic F127 (PF127), collectively designated as (P). Anisotropically aligned PDA-chelated CNT–Fe3O4 (PFeC) nanohybrids were homogeneously dispersed within this hydrogel matrix. The conduit's inner surface is further modified with a UV-crosslinked polymerized layer of AAc-co-AAc-NHS, referred to as (PAA). Under IR irradiation, CNT embedded in PFeC convert light into heat, elevating the local temperature and inducing thermo-responsive contraction of the PF127-based network through enhanced hydrophobic interactions and micelle aggregation of poly (propylene oxide) (PPO) blocks [45]. In this way, the P/A/PFeC@PAA conduit achieves IR-triggered self-contraction to match nerve diameter, mimicking the heat shrink tubing. As the conduit wraps the nerve stumps, the PAA layer forms tight adhesions, collectively eliminating the need for sutures. Beyond simplifying the surgical procedure, the anisotropic PFeC nanohybrids in the conduit confer magnetoelectric responsiveness. Unlike conventional conductive devices requiring implanted electrodes and external power sources which often cause secondary tissue damage [46], or piezoelectric materials which generate passive, unpredictable currents upon mechanical deformation [47], the anisotropically distributed PFeC nanohybrids provides electrical stimulation simply by placing the injured limb with conduit implantation into an alternating magnetic field, offering a noninvasive and clinically practical neurotrophic support. Collectively, this diameter-adaptive, suture-free, magnetoelectric-responsive P/A/PFeC@PAA conduit demonstrates significant clinical potential.

Methods

Materials

FeCl3·6H2O (I431122), dopamine (DA, D103111), carbon nanotube (CNT, C313046), alginate (A, S100126), Pluronic F127 (PF127, P477925) were bought from Aladdin. FeCl2·4H2O (220299) was bought from Sigma.

Fabrication of PFeC nanohybrid

The PFeC nanohybrids were synthesized via a PDA-mediated precipitation method. Briefly, 200 mg of DA and 2.0 g of CNT were dispersed and stirred in 12.5 mL of deionized water for 30 min. Subsequently, 2.0 g of FeCl2·4H2O and 5.2 g of FeCl3·6H2O were added to the dispersion, followed by the addition of 0.85 mL of 4.25 M HCl to maintain the Fe2+/Fe3+ balance. The mixture was heated to 100 °C in a water bath. Then, 250 mL of 1.5 M NaOH was added dropwise under constant stirring at 700 rpm, and the reaction proceeded for 3 h to precipitate Fe3O4 onto the CNT surface. The magnetic PFeC nanohybrids were magnetically collected, washed with deionized water until neutral pH, and lyophilized at −80 °C. Chemical composition, crystalline structure, and magnetic properties were characterized by XPS, XRD, and VSM, respectively.

Fabrication of P/A/PFeC@PAA conduit

The P/A/PFeC@PAA hydrogel was fabricated via a two-step crosslinking process followed by surface coating. First, a precursor solution containing 5 wt% PF127DI, 15 wt% PF127, 2 wt% A, 0.5 wt% LAP photoinitiator, and 1 wt% PFeC was thoroughly mixed and injected into a mold. To induce anisotropic alignment of PFeC, the mixture was exposed to an external magnetic field prior to crosslinking. Photopolymerization was then initiated by UV irradiation (365 nm, 10 mW/cm2) for 30 s, followed by immersion in 100 mM CaCl2 solution for 1 min to achieve ionic crosslinking of A, forming a dual-network anisotropic P/A/PFeC conduit. To create a tissue-adhesive inner surface, a prepolymer solution composed of 20% (w/v) AAc, 1% (w/v) AAc-NHS, and 0.2% (w/v) α-ketoglutarate was coated onto the inner surface wall of the conduit and subsequently exposed to UV light for 30 min to form a stable PAA layer, yielding the final P/A/PFeC@PAA conduit.

Mechanical testing

The compressive and tensile properties of the hydrogels were measured using a universal testing machine (Instron 5944, USA). For compression tests, cylindrical hydrogel samples (diameter: 10 mm, height: 5 mm) underwent uniaxial compression at 1 mm/min. For tensile tests, rectangular samples (width: 10 mm, thickness: 1 mm) were stretched at a constant rate of 5 mm/min until failure.

Rheological characterization

The rheological properties of the hydrogel precursor solutions were evaluated using a rotational rheometer (Anton Paar MCR 302, Austria). Frequency sweep tests were conducted over a range of 0.1–100 rad/s at a constant strain of 1% to measure the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″). Shear-thinning behavior was examined by recording viscosity over shear rates ranging from 0.01 to 1000 s−1. Sol–gel transitions were characterized by temperature ramps (5–40 °C, 1 °C/min).

Conductivity test

The electrical conductivity of the hydrogels was measured using a four-point probe system (KeithLink, China). Conductivity values of disk-shaped hydrogel samples with uniform thickness were calculated based on the measured current–voltage relationship using the standard van der Pauw method. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was conducted using a CHI660E electrochemical workstation with a three-electrode setup in 0.1 M PBS. The hydrogel served as the working electrode, scanned from −1.0 V to +1.0 V at 100 mV/s to assess electrochemical activity.

Magnetic field stimulation

A figure-eight coil (Magstim Rapid 2, UK) was operated in a pulsed mode (10 Hz, 3 pulses per cycle, 60% power) at a coil-to-sample distance of 10 cm to induce voltage generation in conduits. An oscilloscope was used to monitor the voltages. The same stimulation protocol was applied for 10 min, once daily to cell cultures and to postoperative rats.

Adhesive properties

The tissue adhesive strength of the hydrogels was assessed by 180° peeling tests. Hydrogel–porcine skin assemblies were peeled using a universal testing machine at a speed of 10 mm/min. Force–displacement curves were recorded, and interfacial toughness was calculated. To assess adhesion stability, the hydrogel conduit was attached to the nerve and subjected to 60 cyclic tensile tests (2 mm displacement per cycle, 1 mm/min) using a universal testing machine. Load–displacement curves were monitored throughout.

Biocompatibility evaluation

Cell viability was assessed by live/dead staining of Schwann cells cultured on the hydrogel surface. After 24 h incubation, cells were stained with Calcein-AM and PI, and imaged using a fluorescence microscope. Hemocompatibility was evaluated by a hemolysis assay using rabbit red blood cells. Hydrogel samples were incubated with diluted blood at 37 °C for 1 h. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 545 nm to calculate the hemolysis rate.

In vitro experiments

Primary rat Schwann cells and RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded onto thin, semi-transparent hydrogel layers prepared from a small amount of precursor solution and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Schwann cell proliferation was evaluated using CCK-8 assays and EdU staining. Schwann cells and macrophages were fixed and immunofluorescently stained for Ki67/GFAP and CD86/CD206, respectively, followed by observation under a fluorescence microscope. Macrophage polarization was further analyzed by flow cytometry. To analyze neurotrophic factor secretion, culture supernatants collected from Schwann cell were analyzed by ELISA for VEGF, NGF, CNTF, and BDNF concentrations following manufacturer protocols. Culture supernatants collected from macrophage were analyzed by ELISA for IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-10 and TGF-β concentrations. The ELISA results were further normalized to the corresponding factor concentrations produced by 5 × 10^4 cells.

Ethical statement and surgical procedure

All procedures were approved by Shenzhen University's IACUC (Approval No. IACUC-202400101). Sprague-Dawley rats (Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions (22–26 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to sterile pellet food and water at Shenzhen University's Laboratory Animal Center. Experiments complied with ARRIVE guidelines and the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 and related regulations. Surgical procedures were performed as stated subsequently, followed by euthanasia at certain time post-operation for sample collection.

Nerve regeneration evaluation

At first week post-surgery, proximal nerve stumps near the injury site were harvested for Western blot analysis to assess the expression of key signaling pathways involved in nerve regeneration, including PI3K/AKT, ERK/MAPK, and p38 MAPK. At 2 weeks post-surgery, immunohistochemical staining for IL-10 and TNF-α, along with immunofluorescence staining for CD86 and CD206, was performed on nerve sections to evaluate the local inflammatory response and macrophage polarization. At 12 weeks post-surgery, regenerated nerves distal to the injury site were harvested. Axons and myelin sheaths were labeled with NF200 and S100, respectively, and visualized using fluorescence confocal microscopy. For ultrastructural analysis, samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed with osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in graded ethanol, and embedded in resin. Semi-thin sections with toluidine blue staining were examined by bright-field microscopy; ultrathin sections were analyzed by TEM. All images were quantified using ImageJ.

Motor function assessment

To evaluate motor recovery, the gastrocnemius muscles from the operated limb were harvested and stained with Masson's trichrome, and muscle fiber cross-sectional areas were measured in three randomly selected regions under a bright-field microscope. Quantification was performed using ImageJ software. Nerve conduction was evaluated by a Medelec synergy electrophysiological instrument (Oxford Instrument, UK) before tissue collection. To be specific, electrical stimulation was applied to the proximal and distal ends of the tibial nerve, while compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) were recorded from the belly of the gastrocnemius muscle. Gait analysis was conducted using the gait analysis system (Zhongshi Dichuang Technology, China). Statistical analysis

Experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result and discussion

Fabrication of the PFeC nanohybrids

PFeC nanohybrids, the key functional component of the conduit hydrogel, were synthesized via PDA-mediated in situ mineralization of Fe3O4 on CNT, wherein the catechol groups of dopamine chelated Fe2+/Fe3+ ions and promoted their deposition along the CNT surface (Fig. 2a) [48]. TEM images showed that CNT exhibited a smooth surface, and Fe3O4 nanoparticles had a diameter around 10 nm with a tendency to aggregate. Where as PDA-assisted in situ deposition enabled uniform anchoring of Fe3O4 onto CNT to form PFeC (Fig. 2b). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping of PFeC also confirmed homogeneous Fe and O distribution along the carbon backbone in PFeC, with PDA-derived N signal colocalized with Fe, O, and C (Fig. 2c and Fig. S1). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of surface elemental composition and chemical states revealed the emergence of N 1s peaks at ∼399.0 eV and ∼401.0 eV in PFeC, corresponding to the C=N and C–N bonds introduced by PDA coating, which were absent in pristine CNT. Additionally, distinct Fe 2p doublets at ∼710.3/723.6 eV and ∼712.7/725.8 eV indicated the coexistence of Fe2+ and Fe3+, confirming successful deposition of iron oxides in PFeC. The dominant O 1s peak also shifted from ∼531.5 eV (C=O in CNTs) to ∼529.6 eV (Fe–O in PFeC), further validating the formation of iron oxides on the surface of CNT in PFeC (Fig. 2d and e). X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to characterize the crystalline structure of the materials. The results revealed a peak at 26° for CNT (002 plane), multiple peaks for Fe3O4 at 30°, 36°, 43°, 54°, 57°, and 63°, as well as the combination of all these peaks for PFeC (Fig. 2f). These results collectively confirm the successful synthesis of PFeC. Next, the magnetic behavior of PFeC was evaluated. While the hydrophilic PDA coating ensured stable dispersion in water, the Fe3O4 content enabled rapid magnetic aggregation near an external magnet, resulting in solution clarification (Fig. 2h). Vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) analysis showed that both Fe3O4 and PFeC exhibited S-shaped magnetic hysteresis loops with near-zero remanence and coercivity, indicating superparamagnetic behavior. In contrast, CNT showed no significant magnetic response. The saturation magnetization of PFeC (39.17 emu/g) was lower than that of pure Fe3O4 (55.68 emu/g), likely due to the presence of nonmagnetic CNT and PDA, which diluted the magnetic content and interfered with interparticle magnetic coupling through surface interactions and coating effects (Fig. 2g).

Fabrication and photothermal contraction of the P/A/PFeC conduit

The P/A/PFeC hydrogel was fabricated by physically mixing P, A, and PFeC, followed by a two-step crosslinking strategy (Fig. 3a). First, acrylate groups were grafted onto PF127 to yield UV-crosslinkable PF127DI. FTIR analysis showed a distinct ester C=O stretching vibration at ∼1726 cm−1 in PF127DI, absent in unmodified PF127. The 1H NMR spectra confirmed new vinyl proton peaks (–CH=CH2) at 5.8, 6.1, and 6.4 ppm, along with a 4.2 ppm peak indicating esterification of terminal –CH2OH groups. These results collectively verify successful PF127DI synthesis, with 80% grafting efficiency (Fig. S2). Subsequently, the P component (5% PF127DI + 15% PF127) underwent UV crosslinking, followed by ionic crosslinking of A to complete gelation. The inverted vial test demonstrated initial flowability of the precursor solution. After sequential UV and calcium-induced ionic crosslinking, the hydrogel maintained structural integrity upon inversion, confirming successful gelation (Fig. 3b). Macroscopically, the P/A/PFeC hydrogel displayed a homogeneous black appearance due to the uniform dispersion of PFeC, which endowed the hydrogel with stable magnetic responsiveness, enabling consistent attraction to external magnets (Fig. 3c and d). SEM analysis revealed denser pore structures in P/A and P/A/PFeC hydrogels compared to the P hydrogel, likely due to the introduction of ionic crosslinking (Fig. 3e). Corresponding swelling curves indicated the pure P hydrogel exhibited the highest swelling ratio, reaching approximately 370%. In contrast, the swelling ratios of the P/A and P/A/PFeC hydrogels were markedly lower, stabilizing at approximately 220% and 200%, respectively (Fig. 3f).

Adequate compressive resistance is the essential mechanical requirement for NGC materials. The UV-crosslinked P hydrogel exhibited inadequate rigidity, with excessive softness potentially leading to lumen collapse under physiological loads, thereby obstructing axonal regeneration pathways and potentially induce neuroma formation [49]. This limitation motivated incorporating ionically crosslinked A to enhance mechanical integrity of conduit. Compressive stress–strain analysis revealed that both P/A and P/A/PFeC hydrogels exhibited significantly enhanced compressive strength compared to the P hydrogel, with stress values at 35% strain increasing from 0.26 MPa (P) to 0.47 MPa (P/A) and 0.49 MPa (P/A/PFeC) (Fig. 3g and h). In addition to compressive strength, appropriate elasticity is also required to ensure structural compliance with surrounding tissues and maintain conduit integrity under physiological conditions. Tensile testing revealed moderately higher tensile strength (1.3 MPa vs. 1.1 MPa) with marginally reduced elongation at break (28% vs. 30%) in P/A/PFeC versus P/A hydrogel (Fig. S3). These results suggest that the incorporation of A significantly enhanced the compressive mechanical performance while preserving adequate elasticity, whereas the subsequent addition of PFeC had minimal further impact on mechanical properties [50]. Additionally, P/A/PFeC hydrogels with varying concentrations (1%, 2%, 3% w/v) of A were prepared. The results showed that increasing A concentration enhanced the hydrogel's toughness but reduced its porosity. To balance mechanical strength and permeability for nutrition transport, 2% w/v was selected as the final concentration of A (Fig. S4).

Hydrogel precursor solutions were further characterized rheologically. Viscoelastic measurements revealed consistently dominant storage modulus (G′) over loss modulus (G″) across all formulations, confirming elastic-dominated behavior. Sequential addition of A and PFeC to the P system produced stepwise G′ enhancement, indicating progressively strengthened mechanical integrity and network density (Fig. 3i). The steady-state viscosity measurements confirmed that all hydrogel precursor solutions exhibited shear-thinning behavior, which facilitates injection into NGC mold for subsequent crosslinking. Notably, the P/A/PFeC group exhibited highest low-shear viscosity, suggesting superior network interconnectivity (Fig. 3j). Temperature-dependent modulus analysis demonstrated sharp sol-gel transitions in all systems. This thermo-responsiveness originates from P's amphiphilic triblock architecture (PEO-PPO-PEO), where unimers transform into micellar networks upon heating (Fig. 3k). Leveraging this feature, the P/A/PFeC conduit enables photothermal contraction functionality: Under IR irradiation, PFeC generates localized heat, triggering sol-gel transition of the thermosensitive P matrix. This phase change drives rapid volumetric contraction, yielding measurable conduit diameter reduction—functionally mimicking the heat shrink tubing (Fig. 3l and Movie S1). Quantitatively, for a conduit with an initial diameter of 2 mm, the diameter of the P/A/PFeC conduit decreased to approximately 69% of its original value after IR irradiation, corresponding to a maximum shrinkage of about 31% (Fig. S5).

Fabrication and magnetoelectric responsiveness of anisotropic P/A/PFeC hydrogels

Anisotropic P/A/PFeC hydrogels were fabricated by applying a directional magnetic field to precursor solutions, aligning embedded PFeC nanohybrids to enhance directional conductivity and facilitate cellular alignment (Fig. 4a) [48,51]. SEM and EDS results revealed that application of the directional magnetic field induced an anisotropic surface morphology and corresponded distribution of Fe and N elements in the P/A/PFeC hydrogel, in contrast to the isotropic structure observed without magnetic alignment (Fig. 4b and c). This magnetic field-induced orientation of PFeC was further confirmed by polarized optical microscopy (Fig. S6). As a result, light scattering, indicative of internal microstructural orientation, revealed an elliptical-shaped pattern in the anisotropic hydrogel and a circular pattern in the isotropic group under laser illumination (Fig. 4d and e). These results confirm the successful fabrication of anisotropic P/A/PFeC hydrogel.

Biomaterials with a proper conductivity could enhance cellular activities [52]. PFeC incorporation substantially elevated overall conductivity relative to pure P/A hydrogels, visually confirmed by successful LED illumination upon circuit completion (Fig. 4f). Crucially, PFeC alignment directionally modulated conductivity: Anisotropic specimens reached 1.24 S/m when measured parallel to alignment orientation, decreasing to 0.71 S/m perpendicularly. Isotropic controls demonstrated intermediate conductivity (0.818 S/m). This directional conductivity enhancement of the parallel group originates from continuous conductive pathways established by anisotropic PFeC nanohybrids, whereas charge transport in the perpendicular direction is hindered by the interruption of these aligned pathways (Fig. 4g). Current-voltage (C-V) profiling revealed near-linear behavior with minimal current in P/A hydrogels, indicating poor conductivity. Both isotropic and anisotropic P/A/PFeC variants exhibited expanded hysteresis loops and elevated current densities. In particular, anisotropic group showed maximal response, reflecting optimized directional charge transport and reduced internal resistance through aligned PFeC networks (Fig. 4h).

Beyond enhancing electrical conductivity, PFeC simultaneously imparted magnetoelectric responsiveness to hydrogel. Specifically, Fe3O4 in PFeC provided magnetic responsiveness, while CNT served as the conductive component. Consistent with Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction, the anisotropic P/A/PFeC hydrogel generated significant induced voltage under alternating magnetic fields, attributable to its aligned conductive network. In contrast, the isotropic counterpart, lacking directional pathways, exhibited only minimal voltage output (Fig. 4i–l, Movie S2). Moreover, in the anisotropic group, the voltage amplitude gradually decreased as the vertical distance between the coil and the sample increased (Fig. S7). Accordingly, under the influence of an alternating magnetic field, Schwann cells cultured on anisotropic P/A/PFeC hydrogel exhibited an aligned distribution consistent with the orientation of PFeC, whereas cells on isotropic hydrogels displayed a random orientation (Fig. 4m). Due to its superior conductivity and magnetoelectric performance, the anisotropically structured P/A/PFeC hydrogel was selected for subsequent experiments.

Fabrication and adhesive properties of P/A/PFeC@PAA hydrogels

Proper and stable tissue adhesion critically promotes healing and ensures efficient tissue repair [53,54]. Robust adhesion at the conduit's inner layer is essential for achieving suture-free implantation and maintaining effective magnetoelectric stimulation [44,55]. To confer adhesiveness to the inner surface, AAc and AAc-NHS were UV-polymerized to form a poly AAc-co-AAc-NHS adhesive layer (PAA). Upon contact with moist tissue, gentle pressure initiates rapid physical adhesion through hydrogen bonding between carboxyl/hydroxyl groups of AAc and amine/hydroxyl groups on tissue surfaces. This initial interaction stabilizes the interface, enabling NHS esters on AAc-NHS to subsequently form covalent amide bonds with tissue amines, establishing strong adhesion within 3 min (Fig. 5a). This dual-adhesion mechanism endowed P/A/PFeC@PAA hydrogel with robust, conformal adhesion to diverse tissues (heart, liver, spleen, gastrocnemius) and sustained attachment to a 50 g weight. Notably, adhered tissues remained securely affixed during vertical ruler shaking (Fig. 5b). Dynamic adhesive performance was also evaluated through deformation and immersion tests: the hydrogel maintained firm adhesion to porcine skin after distortion and during water immersion with distortion, demonstrating stability for in vivo applications (Fig. 5c). The interfacial toughness between the P/A/PFeC@PAA hydrogel and wet tissue was evaluated using a 180° peeling test (Fig. 5d). To clarify the contribution of covalent amide bonding, a control hydrogel coated with UV-polymerized AAc alone (i.e., without NHS ester groups) was tested in parallel. The results showed that the PAA adhesive layer (poly AAc-co-AAc-NHS) exhibited significantly stronger tissue adhesion than the poly AAc-only coating (Fig. 5e). This enhancement was attributed to the addition formation of covalent amide bonds via NHS esters, in contrast to the reversible hydrogen bonding provided by AAc alone. Quantitatively, the PAA coating exhibited an interfacial toughness of 97.00 ± 10.54 J/m2, which was approximately 1.7-fold higher than that of the poly AAc control (Fig. 5f).

These results highlight the excellent adhesive performance of the PAA coating, which, together with the photothermal-induced contraction of the P/A/PFeC@PAA conduit, enables diameter-adaptive sealing to the nerve epineurium upon IR irradiation, thereby eliminating the need for conventional suturing (Fig. 5g and h). Beyond surgical simplification, postoperative mechanical stability is critical for preventing nerve displacement during movement. However, conventional suture-based nerve repair often struggles to maintain interfacial integrity under repetitive traction, as ultrafine suture stiches can exert concentrated stress and even cut into the conduit during limb movement. Cyclic tensile testing revealed the intact nerve tissue exhibited optimal mechanical stability with negligible peak load reduction after 60 cycles. In contrast, nerves connected to the P/A/PFeC conduit via conventional suturing showed a marked decline, retaining only 33% of the initial peak load by cycle 60. Notably, the diameter-adaptive, suture-free repair using the P/A/PFeC@PAA conduit, which relies on IR-triggered contraction and adhesion, maintained approximately 72% of its initial load-bearing capacity after 60 cycles, indicating significantly improved interfacial stability (Fig. 5i–l). These findings indicate the P/A/PFeC@PAA conduit not only eliminates suture-induced complications but also enhances nerve-conduit integration robustness which is essential for functional recovery.

In vitro cell behavior assessment

The biocompatibility of the hydrogel is closely linked to its ability to promote tissue repair [56]. In vitro hemolysis assays were performed using rabbit blood samples. The results showed that all hydrogel groups exhibited similar hemolysis rates, all below 5%, indicating excellent hemocompatibiliy (Fig. S8a–b). The culture medium preconditioned by 48-h soaking of each hydrogel group was used to culture Schwann cells. Live/dead staining revealed high cell viability in all groups, confirming the favorable cytocompatibility of all hydrogel groups (Fig. S8c–d).

As principal glial cells in peripheral nerves, Schwann cells support axonal function and proliferate post-injury to orchestrate regeneration [57]. Electrical stimulation has been reported to promotes Schwann cell proliferation, dedifferentiated phenotypes, and neurotrophic factor secretion [58]. To evaluate the magnetoelectric biofunction of P/A/PFeC@PAA, Schwann cells were cultured on each hydrogel group and exposed to an alternating magnetic field (Fig. 6a). EdU staining and CCK-8 assay confirmed that both P/A/PFeC and P/A/PFeC@PAA significantly enhanced Schwann cell proliferation (Fig. 6b and Fig. S9a). Ki67, a proliferation-associated nuclear protein, serves as a widely recognized marker of actively dividing cells and is commonly used to evaluate Schwann cell proliferative activity during nerve regeneration [59]. GFAP, an intermediate filament protein, critically mediates cytoskeletal reorganization to facilitate Schwann cell migration and proliferation post-injury [60]. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed significantly elevated Ki67 and GFAP expression in Schwann cells cultured on PFeC-containing hydrogels versus other groups (Fig. 6c and Fig. S9b). ELISA tests confirmed that after 5-day culture, Schwann cells in the P/A/PFeC and P/A/PFeC@PAA groups secreted significantly higher levels of NGF, BDNF, VEGF and CNTF compared to the control and P/A groups (Fig. 6d). These neurotrophic factors critically enhance Schwann cell function, and facilitate axonal regeneration during peripheral nerve repair [61]. Collectively, magnetoelectrically responsive P/A/PFeC@PAA hydrogel enhances Schwann cell proliferation, dedifferentiation, and neurotrophic secretion to support nerve regeneration.

In addition, electrical stimulation has also been reported to modulate macrophage polarization by promoting transition from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes [62,63]. To evaluate immunomodulatory effect of the magnetoelectric P/A/PFeC@PAA hydrogel, macrophages were cultured on each hydrogel group and exposed to an alternating magnetic field (Fig. 6e). Immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometric analysis revealed that macrophages cultured on the PFeC-containing hydrogels exhibited a more pronounced CD206-positive phenotype, indicating enhanced polarization toward the M2 phenotype under magnetoelectric stimulation (Fig. 6f–h). ELISA results further supported this observation, showing significantly lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α) and markedly elevated levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10 and TGF-β) compared to the P/A and control groups after 3-day culture (Fig. S10). These findings suggest that, under an alternating magnetic field, P/A/PFeC and P/A/PFeC@PAA could promote macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby helping to establish an anti-inflammatory microenvironment favorable for nerve regeneration [64].

In vivo nerve regeneration evaluation

Six-week-old female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats served as the standard animal model for this clinical study. The rats were allocated into four groups: P/A, P/A/PFeC, P/A/PFeC@PAA, and Autograft. In the Autograft group, a 10 mm segment of the sciatic nerve was transected and reconnected by reverse anastomosis, representing the clinical gold standard for peripheral nerve repair. In the P/A and P/A/PFeC groups, the nerve ends were inserted into the respective conduits and fixed with conventional stitches. In the P/A/PFeC@PAA group, nerve stumps were sealed using a suture-free approach: upon IR irradiation, the PFeC component converted light into heat, inducing rapid contraction of the thermo-responsive P matrix, while the PAA inner layer formed tight adhesion with the nerve epineurium. This process recapitulates the heat shrink tubing, whereby tissues exhibit precise responsiveness to external optical stimuli (Fig. 7a–c, Movie S3). Such a biomimetic sealing strategy not only simplifies the surgical procedure but also significantly reduces operation time. In addition, during IR irradiation, the local temperature on the inner surface of the conduit increased from approximately 20.5 °C before irradiation to 30.8 °C at 20 s and 34.3 °C at 35 s, indicating a relatively mild thermal exposure (Fig. S11). Following surgery, all groups received identical daily alternating magnetic field stimulation (Fig. S12).

Two weeks postoperatively, inflammation at the nerve stumps was evaluated. Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent staining demonstrated that the P/A/PFeC@PAA group showed significantly lower expression of TNF and CD86, alongside higher levels of IL-10 and CD206, compared to the P/A and P/A/PFeC groups (Fig. 7a,e–f, Fig. S13). This transition was attributed to the combined effects of the suture-free design, which minimized secondary mechanical trauma from stitches and reduced the risk of nerve stump misalignment, a condition that can impair axonal targeting and result in excessive scar formation or neuroma development, as well as the magnetoelectric stimulation, which promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype [65,66]. Interestingly, it was observed that the autograft group displayed the mildest inflammatory response overall. This may be due to the fact that, despite advancements in conduit design and the absence of suture, the host's immune system still exhibited a more robust recognition of foreign materials compared to autograft tissue [67].

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the early-stage repair response, cell signaling pathways were investigated at 1 week after surgery, a time point considered relevant for capturing early inflammatory and regenerative signaling changes [[68], [69]]. The PI3K/AKT and ERK/MAPK pathways—critical regulators of neuronal survival, proliferation, and differentiation during regeneration [[70], [71], [72]]—showed significantly elevated P-AKT and P-ERK1/2 expression in both P/A/PFeC@PAA and P/A/PFeC groups versus the P/A group at postoperative day 7. Conversely, the p38 MAPK pathway—a key modulator of inflammatory responses, axonal regeneration, and myelination [68]—exhibited markedly reduced P-p38 levels in the P/A/PFeC@PAA group relative to both P/A/PFeC and P/A groups (Fig. 7a,d,m,o,q). These results suggest the application of this suture-free, magnetoelectric responsive P/A/PFeC@PAA conduit may suppress early inflammation by inhibiting the p38 MAPK signaling pathway, while simultaneously promoting early nerve regeneration via activation of the PI3K/Akt and ERK/MAPK pathways.

At 12 weeks post-surgery, the morphology of regenerated nerves underwent systematic evaluation. Immunofluorescence for NF200 demonstrated higher axonal fiber density in the P/A/PFeC group versus P/A group, principally due to magnetoelectrically induced current stimulation from PFeC under alternating magnetic fields [73,74]. Furthermore, the nerve fiber density in the P/A/PFeC@PAA group was higher than P/A/PFeC group, with S100 staining revealing a parallel trend in myelin sheath regeneration (Fig. 7a,g,h). This enhancement may be attributed to the suture-free sealing strategy, which minimizes mechanical disruption and local inflammation at the nerve interface, thereby fostering a more favorable microenvironment for Schwann cell proliferation and axonal elongation [19]. Toluidine blue staining showed the highest nerve fiber density in the Autograft group, gradually decreasing through P/A/PFeC@PAA and P/A/PFeC, with the lowest in P/A (Fig. 7i and l). Low-magnification TEM analysis revealed that the regenerated nerve fibers in the P/A/PFeC@PAA group exhibited more uniform morphology with longer diameter, second only to the Autograft group (Fig. 7j and n). High-magnification TEM images showed that the myelin sheath thickness followed a similar trend to axon diameter (Fig. 7k and p). Collectively, these data indicate that IR-induced, diameter-adaptive, suture-free sealing and magnetically-triggered electrical stimulation act synergistically to potentiate nerve regeneration.

In vivo functional recovery assessment

Beyond nerve morphology assessment, evaluating the functional status of target organs is equally important, as prolonged denervation after nerve injury often leads to severe muscle atrophy and functional impairment. Accordingly, the gastrocnemius muscles were harvested and analyzed, revealing that the Autograft group exhibited the least degree of atrophy of the injured side, as expected. In the remaining three groups, the muscle wet weight ratio increased progressively from P/A to P/A/PFeC, and was highest in the P/A/PFeC@PAA group (Fig. 8a–b,g). Masson staining further demonstrated significantly larger muscle fiber cross-sectional areas in P/A/PFeC@PAA versus both P/A/PFeC and P/A groups (Fig. 8c and h). Electrophysiological testing was conducted to directly assess restoration of muscle innervation. The thickness of the myelin sheath is inversely related to the latency of compound muscle action potential (CMAP), whereas the number of innervated muscle fibers is positively correlated with CMAP amplitude. Representative CMAP waveforms revealed a progressive reduction in latency from the P/A group to the Autograft group, reflecting enhanced nerve conduction velocity. In parallel, CMAP peak amplitudes increased sequentially in the P/A/PFeC, P/A/PFeC@PAA, and Autograft groups compared to the P/A group, indicating improved functional reinnervation of target muscles (Fig. 8b,d,i-j,l). To further assess motor function recovery, gait analysis was conducted. Representative footprints and 3D pressure distribution maps demonstrated a progressive improvement in the gait of the right hind limb from the P/A group to the Autograft group (Fig. 8b,e-f). As a result, sciatic functional index (SFI) quantification confirmed: Autograft > P/A/PFeC@PAA > P/A/PFeC > P/A (Fig. 8k). The morphological findings of the gastrocnemius muscle, along with the electrophysiological results and gait analysis, suggested that our novel P/A/PFeC@PAA conduit application could effectively promote nerve regeneration and muscle reinnervation.

The in vivo degradation rates of the P/A, P/A/PFeC, and P/A/PFeC@PAA conduits increased progressively over time, reaching approximately 23%-24% at 4 weeks, 48%-50% at 12 weeks, and 81%-85% at 24 weeks, with no significant differences among the groups at each time point. This degradation behavior indicates that the conduits could provide sufficient structural support during the early stage of nerve regeneration without premature loss of integrity, while still undergoing gradual degradation thereafter, which is favorable for subsequent tissue remodeling and reduction of long-term foreign body retention (Fig. S14). In addition, at 12 and 24 weeks after surgery, HE staining of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys demonstrated favorable in vivo biocompatibility, with no obvious histopathological abnormalities observed in any group (Fig. S15).

Conclusion

This study presented a diameter-adaptive, suture-free NGC with magnetoelectric responsiveness, termed P/A/PFeC@PAA, for peripheral nerve repair. Upon exposure to IR, embedded PFeC nanoparticles generate localized heat that triggers P-micelle aggregation and subsequent conduit contraction, achieving precise diameter matching with nerve stumps. Concurrently, the inner PAA coating establishes robust epineurial adhesion, enabling suture-free sealing that reduces surgical complexity and complication risks. Furthermore, when exposed to an external alternating magnetic field, the anisotropic PFeC nanohybrids generate localized electrical stimulation without the need for implanted electrodes. This noninvasive, on-demand electric stimulation enhances Schwann cell activity, promotes axonal elongation and contributes to a more favorable microenvironment for nerve regeneration. In addition, this platform also holds potential for further functionalization by incorporating neurotrophic drugs or bioactive factors to synergistically enhance regeneration outcomes. Taken together, with continued refinement and translational studies, the P/A/PFeC@PAA conduit represents a promising strategy for clinical application in peripheral nerve repair.
