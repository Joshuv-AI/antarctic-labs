The sine Gordon equation in light-cone coordinates on the half lines revisited: a Riemann–Hilbert approach
Iryna Karpenko

Abstract
. In this work, we study the initial boundary value (IBV) problems for the sine-Gordon (sG) equation in the light-cone coordinates u x ​ t = sin ⁡ u u_{xt}=\sin u in the quarter planes x > 0 x>0 , t > 0 t>0 and x < 0 x<0 , t > 0 t>0 assuming a suitable decay as x → + ∞ x\to+\infty or as x → − ∞ x\to-\infty . Employing the Riemann–Hilbert (RH) problem framework, we demonstrate that these two IBV problems differ significantly with respect to the boundary data required for well-posedness. Specifically, the solution of the ‘‘right problem’’ ( x ≥ 0 x\geq 0 ) is uniquely determined by the initial data u ⁡ ( x , 0 ) u(x,0) , x ≥ 0 x\geq 0 alone whereas for the ‘‘left problem’’ ( x ≤ 0 x\leq 0 ), the boundary data u ⁡ ( 0 , t ) u(0,t) has to be prescribed in addition to the initial data in order to obtain a well-posed problem.



1.
Introduction

The sine–Gordon (sG) equation is one of the classical integrable nonlinear equations and has been extensively studied. In the laboratory coordinates
(y,\tau)
it takes the form

u_{yy}-u_{\tau\tau}+\sin u=0.

The sG equation arises in differential geometry in the theory of surfaces of constant negative curvature and appears in several areas of physics, including the theory of crystal dislocations and the dynamics of Josephson junctions (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
22
,
1
,
6
]
).

Introducing the light-cone variables

x=\frac{1}{2}(y+\tau),\quad t=\frac{1}{2}(y-\tau)

the equation transforms into

(1.1)

u_{xt}=\sin u.

In these variables,

x
and
t
correspond to the characteristic directions of a hyperbolic equation.

The Cauchy problem on the whole line for the sG equation was solved using the inverse scattering transform by Ablowitz, Kaup, Newell, and Segur (see
[
2
,
15
]
). This method reveals the rich structure of the sG equation, including the existence of localized solutions such as kinks and breathers, which correspond to discrete eigenvalues of the associated spectral problem (see
[
15
]
).

The analysis becomes
considerably more complicated when the sG equation is posed in domains
with boundaries, such as quarter-planes and semi-strips. To address such problems, Fokas introduced a general and systematic framework known as the Unified Transform Method (UTM), widely referred to as the Fokas method (see
[
10
]
), which has subsequently been developed and extended by many authors
[
11
,
4
,
7
,
5
,
14
,
16
]
. The method is based on the simultaneous spectral analysis of both equations of the associated Lax pair.

In
[
8
]
, Fokas and Its studied the sG equation in laboratory coordinates in the quarter-plane with prescribed initial data and boundary values (see also
[
10
,
13
]
). In a later work
[
9
]
, the equation
u_{xt}=-\sin u
was considered on a finite spatial interval
(0,l)
. In both formulations, the initial profile and the
boundary values are assumed to be prescribed as part of the data of the problem.

An inverse spectral approach was used by Sakhnovich, who investigated the
sG equation in semi-strip domains using the Weyl function associated
with an auxiliary Dirac-type system arising from the Lax pair
(see
[
20
,
21
]
). In particular, this approach makes it possible to study the
evolution of the corresponding spectral data and to describe situations in which solutions become unbounded in the quarter-plane. In
[
24
]
,
the Dirichlet problem on the half-line is considered with prescribed initial data

u(x,0)
and boundary data

u(0,t)
. These data are required to satisfy appropriate compatibility conditions, and the solution is reconstructed from the corresponding scattering data.

A different inverse spectral approach to the Dirichlet problem on the half-line was proposed by Leon
[
19
]
. In this work, the sG equation in light-cone coordinates (in the form
u_{xt}=-\sin u
, which can be related to (
1.1
) by the change of variable
x\mapsto-x
) is analyzed using an alternative Lax pair associated with a Schrödinger-type spectral problem on the half-line.

Despite the extensive literature on initial-boundary value (IBV) problems for the sine-Gordon equation, several questions concerning the well-posedness of these problems have remained open, particularly regarding the decay conditions as
|x|\to\infty
. In the present paper, we address these questions by studying two initial-boundary value problems for the sG equation in the light-cone coordinates (
1.1
), posed on the half-lines
x\geq 0
(Problem I) and
x\leq 0
(Problem II) building on the Fokas method for half line problems.

In what follows, we will use standard functional spaces

\displaystyle H^{2,2}(\Omega):=\{f(x)\in L^{2}(\Omega):\penalty\ x^{2}f(x),f^{\prime}(x),x^{2}f^{\prime}(x),f^{\prime\prime}(x),x^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)\in L^{2}(\Omega)\},

\displaystyle C([0,T],X):=\left\{u:[0,T]\to X:u\text{ is continuous on }[0,T]\text{ with values in }X\right\},

\displaystyle C^{k}([0,T],X):=\{u:[0,T]\to X:\partial_{t}^{,j}u\in C([0,T],X),\quad j=0,1,\ldots,k\},

and

\tilde{H}^{1,2}(\Omega):=\{f(x)\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega):(1+x^{2})\sin\frac{f(x)}{2},(1+x^{2})f_{x}(x)\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}

with a pseudometric

For Problem I, we assume that
the initial condition
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)
for
x\geq 0

is given and
u_{0}(x)\in H^{2,2}((0,+\infty))+2\pi k
. We consider the following problem

(1.2)

\displaystyle u_{xt}=\sin u,\quad x\geq 0,\quad 0\leq t<T<\infty,

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\geq 0

and seek solutions
u(x,t)
such that
u(x,t)\in C^{1}([0,T],\tilde{H}^{1,2}(0,\infty))
.

For Problem II, we assume that
the initial condition
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\in H^{2,2}((-\infty,0))+2\pi k
for
x\leq 0
, and the boundary condition
u(0,t)=v_{0}(t)\in H^{1}(0,T)

are given. We consider the following IBV problem

(1.3)

\displaystyle u_{xt}=\sin u,\quad x\leq 0,\quad 0\leq t<T<\infty,

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\leq 0,

\displaystyle u(0,t)=v_{0}(t),\qquad t\in[0,T]

and seek solutions
u(x,t)
such that

u(x,t)\in C^{1}([0,T],\tilde{H}^{1,2}((-\infty,0)))

The outline of the paper is as follows.

In Section
2
, we present the basic tools for our analysis derived from the Lax pair representation of the sG equation, namely the Jost solutions (“eigenfunctions”) and the associated spectral functions (scattering coefficients).

In Section
3
, we discuss the compatibility conditions for the initial and boundary values.

In Section
4
, we describe the inverse spectral problems for the initial and boundary values in terms of solutions of the associated RH problems.

In Section
5
, the master RH problems are constructed,
whose solutions provide the solutions to Problem I and Problem II.

In Section
6
, we show how a local solution of the sG equation can be constructed from the solution of a RH problem parametrized by
x
and
t
.

In Section
7
, the differentiability of the solution of the RH problem is discussed.

In Section
8
, we address Problem I, for which we find
that the initial data alone uniquely determine its solution.

In Section
9
, we focus on Problem II, where we show that in addition to the initial data, the boundary value

should be prescribed to uniquely determine the solution.

2.
Eigenfunctions and Spectral Functions

The sine-Gordon equation (
1.1
) is the compatibility condition for the Lax pair

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{x}

\displaystyle=U\tilde{\Phi},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{t}

\displaystyle=V\tilde{\Phi},

where the coefficients
U\equiv U(x,t,k)
and
V\equiv V(x,t,k)
are defined by

(2.2a)

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}k\sigma_{3}+\frac{u_{x}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},

(2.2b)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\mathrm{i}k}\begin{pmatrix}\cos u&-\sin u\\
-\sin u&-\cos u\end{pmatrix}.

The first step in the spectral analysis is to investigate the analyticity properties, in the complex spectral parameter
k
, of the eigenfunctions
associated with the Lax pair. Studying their analyticity, symmetry, and
boundedness in appropriate regions of the complex
k
-plane leads to precise
relations among the different eigenfunctions. These relations can be
formulated as a matrix Riemann–Hilbert factorization problem in the spectral
variable.

Moreover, the coefficient matrices
U
and
V
in (
2.2
) are traceless.
Consequently, for any matrix solution
\Phi(x,t,k)
of the Lax pair
(
2.1
) (constructed from two linearly independent vector solutions), we have

\partial_{x}\det\Phi=0,\qquad\partial_{t}\det\Phi=0.

Hence
\det\Phi
is independent of both
x
and
t
, and therefore
is constant throughout the domain.

Throughout this section,

9.3.
Existence result for Problem II

Proposition 9.3
.

Let
\{u_{0}(x)\in H^{2,2}((-\infty,0))+2\pi k;\penalty\ v_{0}(t)\in H^{1}(0,T)\}
be a set of functions satisfying internal compatibility condition

u_{0}(0)=v_{0}(0)
.
Then the IBVP

(9.11a)

\displaystyle u_{xt}=\sin u,

(9.11b)

\displaystyle u(0,t)=v_{0}(t),\quad 0\leq t<T\leq\infty;

(9.11c)

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\leq 0;

(9.11d)

\displaystyle u(x,t)\in\tilde{H}^{1,2}((-\infty,0)),\quad x\to-\infty,\quad 0\leq t<T<\infty.

has a unique solution,
u(x,t)
, which can be represented in terms of the solution of the associated RH problem
\widecheck{\text{RH}}^{(xt)}
via (
9.2
)–(
9.3
).

Proof.

The existence of a solution of the RH problem
\widecheck{\text{RH}}^{(xt)}
follows from Zhou’s vanishing lemma. Furthermore, Section
6
shows that
u(x,t)
satisfies the sG equation, while Section
7.3
implies that
u(x,t)
is
C^{1}
in both
x
and
t
, and Section
9.2
establishes that
u(x,t)\in\tilde{H}^{1,2}((-\infty,0))
.

Therefore, it remains to prove that:

1.

u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)
.

2.

u(0,t)=v_{0}(t)
.

1.
Following the approach used for the right half-line problem, we establish a relation between the
solution
\check{M}^{(xt)}(x,t,k)
of the Riemann–Hilbert problem
\widecheck{\text{RH}}^{(xt)}
problem evaluated at
t=0
and the solution
\check{M}^{(x)}(x,k)
of the
\widecheck{\text{RH}}^{(x)}
in the form of the multiplication by an matrix factor:

P^{(x)}(x,k)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kx\sigma_{3}}P_{0}^{(x)}(k)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx\sigma_{3}}

with

P_{0}^{(x)}(k)=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{B(k)}{a^{*}(k)d^{*}(k)}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\setminus\{|k|<\epsilon\}\\
\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{\tilde{B}(k)}{\tilde{a}^{*}(k)\tilde{d}^{*}(k)}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cap\{|k|<\epsilon\}\\
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\frac{B^{*}(k)}{a(k)d(k)}&1\end{pmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}\setminus\{|k|<\epsilon\},\\
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\frac{\tilde{B}^{*}(k)}{\tilde{a}(k)\tilde{d}(k)}&1\end{pmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}\cap\{|k|<\epsilon\}.\end{cases}

Thus, the problem reduces to show that
{N}^{(x)}(x,k)
defined by

(9.12)

{N}^{(x)}(x,k)=\check{M}^{(xt)}(x,0,k)P^{(x)}(x,k)

satisfies the Riemann–Hilbert problem
\widecheck{\text{RH}}^{(x)}
.

•

The jump conditions match by construction.

•

Notice that
-\frac{B(k)}{a^{*}(k)d^{*}(k)}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}kx}=O\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}kx}}{k}\right)
. In particular, we have
-\frac{B(k)}{a^{*}(k)d^{*}(k)}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}kx}=O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
for
x\leq 0
in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, and thus the normalization condition is satisfied.

•

Let’s check that the residue conditions match.

For
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, we have

(9.13)

\displaystyle{N}^{(xt)(1)}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\check{M}^{(xt)(1)}(x,0,k),

(9.14)

\displaystyle{N}^{(xt)(2)}(x,k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{B(k)\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}kx}}{a^{*}(k)d^{*}(k)}\check{M}^{(xt)(1)}(x,0,k)+\check{M}^{(xt)(2)}(x,0,k).

Taking into account the residue condition (
5.16b
) and the symmetries (
2.38
) and (
2.45
), the singularities of the second column at
k=-\eta_{j}
cancel.

On the other hand, at
k=-k_{j}
, the second column is singular due to the singularity of
\frac{1}{a^{*}(k)}
, and, using
d^{*}(-k_{j})=b^{*}(-k_{j})B(-k_{j})
, the corresponding residue condition takes the form (
4.29b
).

The singularities in
\mathbb{C}^{-}
can be treated in a similar way.

Thus, by uniqueness of solution of RH problem
\widecheck{\text{RH}}^{(x)}
, we conclude that
\check{M}^{(xt)}(x,0,k)P^{(x)}(x,k)=\check{M}^{(x)}(x,k)
.

Recall that
u_{0x}(x)
is expressed in terms of the second coefficient in the expansion of
\check{M}^{(x)}(x,k)
near
k=\infty
. In this regard, notice that
-\frac{B(k)}{a^{*}(k)d^{*}(k)}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}kx}
decays exponentially fast for
x<0
in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
,
which implies that

{u}_{x}(x,0)={u}_{0x}(x).

2.

Introduce

P^{(t)}_{0}(k)=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{A(k)}{d^{*}(k)}&0\\
b^{*}(k)&\frac{d^{*}(k)}{A(k)}\end{pmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cap\{|k|>\epsilon\},\\
\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\tilde{A}(k)}{\tilde{d}^{*}(k)}&0\\
\tilde{b}^{*}(k)&\frac{\tilde{d}^{*}(k)}{\tilde{A}(k)}\end{pmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cap\{|k|<\epsilon\},\\
\begin{pmatrix}\frac{d(k)}{A^{*}(k)}&-b(k)\\
0&\frac{A^{*}(k)}{d(k)}\end{pmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}\cap\{|k|>\epsilon\},\\
\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\tilde{d}(k)}{\tilde{A}^{*}(k)}&-\tilde{b}(k)\\
0&\frac{\tilde{A}^{*}(k)}{\tilde{d}(k)}\end{pmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}\cap\{|k|<\epsilon\},\end{cases}

and define

\hat{{N}}^{(xt)}(t,k):=\check{M}^{(xt)}(0,t,k)P^{(t)}(t,k)

with
P^{(t)}(t,k)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}t}{4k}\sigma_{3}}P^{(t)}_{0}(k)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}t}{4k}\sigma_{3}}
.

(1)

The jump condition
for
\hat{{N}}^{(xt)}(t,k)
coincides with that for
\check{M}^{(t)}(t,k)

by construction.

(2)

Noticing that
\frac{A(k)}{d^{*}(k)}=1+O(\frac{1}{k})
and
b^{*}(k)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}t}{2k}}=O(\frac{1}{k})
in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
for all

(3)

Let’s check that the residue conditions match.

For
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, we have

(9.15)

\displaystyle\hat{{N}}^{(xt)(1)}(t,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{A(k)}{d^{*}(k)}\check{M}^{(xt)(1)}(0,t,k)+b^{*}(k)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}t}{2k}}\check{M}^{(xt)(2)}(0,t,k),

(9.16)

\displaystyle\hat{{N}}^{(xt)(2)}(t,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{*}(k)}{A(k)}\check{M}^{(xt)(2)}(0,t,k).

Taking into account the residue condition (
5.16b
) and
A(-\eta_{j})=-\frac{b^{*}(-\eta_{j})B(-\eta_{j})}{a^{*}(-\eta_{j})}
together with the symmetries (
2.38
) and (

On the other hand, at

The singularities in

10.
Concluding remarks

Throughout this paper, we consider the generic case, in the sense that the initial data for Problem I and the initial and boundary data for Problem II are assumed to generate the corresponding spectral functions with finitely many zeros, all of which are simple. Under this assumption, the associated RH problems can be formulated by imposing residue conditions at the zeros of the spectral functions. The restriction to simple zeros is not essential, however. Indeed, the general case can be treated by replacing the residue conditions with a jump condition on a sufficiently large circle enclosing all the zeros; see
[
3
,
5
,
4
]
.
