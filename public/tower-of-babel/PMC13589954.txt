Metrnl in mitigating radiation enteritis via label-retaining cells protection by modulating the c-KIT-WNT pathway
Authors: Sailong Zhang, Simin Chen, Bingjie Guo, Qisheng Ling, Shuling Wang, Zhuwei Miao, Zhi Wang, Yuchen Wei, Sili Zheng, Zhiyong Li, An-Qiang Feng, Feng-Hua Qian, Chao-Yu Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Currently, there exists no definitive standardized therapy for radiation enteritis. This study unveils Metrnl as a pivotal regulator of intestinal radioprotection through multi-dimensional single-cell profiling and mechanistic dissection. We first demonstrate that radiation dynamically reprograms Metrnl expression in a time-dependent manner, revealing its role as a radiation-responsive mediator. Crucially, Metrnl confers potent protection against radiation-induced intestinal injury through double mechanisms; Metrnl safeguards label-retaining cells (LRCs) rather than conventional Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells, and activates a novel c-KIT-WNT signaling axis via ERK/GSK3β-mediated pathway crosstalk, both revitalizing intestinal epithelial regeneration. Therapeutically, recombinant Metrnl emerges as a potential biotherapeutic, effectively attenuating radiation enteritis. These findings establish Metrnl as a key mediator bridging radiation biology and regenerative medicine, offering a transformative potential for radiation injury management.

Introduction

Radiation enteritis encompasses a spectrum of intestinal complications arising from the detrimental effects of radiation therapy on normal intestinal tissue during the treatment of malignant tumors located in the pelvic or abdominal cavity1,2. Presently, there exists no definitive standardized treatment for radiation enteritis, with the primary objective being the alleviation of clinical symptoms through three primary approaches: pharmacological interventions, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and surgical management3, 4, 5. While these modalities have exhibited some degree of effectiveness, they primarily focus on symptomatic relief and do not fundamentally halt the progression of radiation enteritis. Consequently, unraveling the underlying pathogenesis of radiation enteritis holds promise for establishing a theoretical groundwork for the identification of biomarkers, therapeutic targets, and the subsequent development of pertinent medications.

Intestinal stem cells (ISC), positioned at the base of intestinal crypts, play a pivotal role in the continuous renewal and repair of intestinal epithelial cells6, 7, 8. Under normal physiological conditions, the rapid turnover of intestinal epithelia is primarily fueled by Lgr5+ (Leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 5-positive) ISC located at the crypt base. These Lgr5+ ISCs are highly susceptible to radiation, undergoing massive apoptosis upon exposure, which disrupts the overall intestinal renewal process and ultimately leads to the onset of radiation enteritis9,10. Within the crypts, there also reside another type of cell, known as low-proliferative label-retaining cells (LRCs), which belong to the ISC population but exhibit low levels of Lgr5 expression11,12. Due to their resistance to radiation, LRCs serve as a reservoir of stem cells, stepping in to sustain the renewal of the entire intestine following the widespread demise of Lgr5+ ISC13,14. However, the precise mechanism through which LRCs contribute to intestinal renewal remains elusive. Therefore, further research into the role of LRCs in intestinal regeneration following radiation damage is imperative, laying the groundwork for the identification of novel therapeutic targets for radiation enteritis.

Metrnl (meteorin-like), a newly discovered secreted protein by our research team, comprises 311 amino acids, including a 45-amino-acid N-terminal signal peptide15. Our prior investigations into this protein have established Metrnl as a unique cytokine and have illuminated its crucial mechanism in facilitating insulin sensitization via the PPARγ signaling pathway16. Beyond this, Metrnl demonstrates diverse functionalities, including the promotion of adipocyte differentiation, enhancement of metabolism, and suppression of inflammation17,18. Notably, we have observed high expression levels of Metrnl in both human and mouse intestinal tissues. In a study involving dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced mice with specific knockout of Metrnl in intestinal epithelial cells, we found that decreased autophagy in intestinal epithelial cells exacerbated the progression of ulcerative colitis19. Nevertheless, no studies to date have reported on the impact of Metrnl on radiation enteritis. Thus, investigating the role of Metrnl in radiation enteritis holds promise for uncovering novel therapeutic targets and insights for the clinical management of this condition.

This study delves into the role of Metrnl in radiation enteritis and demonstrates its radioprotective efficacy in radiation enteritis through a novel mechanism targeting LRCs preservation. Our research unveils that Metrnl possesses a distinctive ability to safeguard LRCs by interacting with the c-KIT receptor, thereby initiating a novel signaling cascade. This particular mechanism functions to initiate the activation of the MEK–ERK pathway and then inhibit GSK3β, resulting in an enhanced effect on WNT signaling. The discovery of the novel c-KIT/MEK–ERK/GSK3β–WNT signaling axis marks the establishment of the initial therapeutic strategy aimed at treating radiation enteritis by modulating intestinal stem cells.

Materials and methods

Animals

The Metrnl intestinal epithelial cell-specific knockout mice (MetrnlloxP/loxPVillin-Cre, referred to as MetrnlKO) were previously established by our research group16. The Metrnl intestinal epithelial cell-specific overexpressing mouse (R26-LSL-Metrnl+/−Villin-Cre), abbreviated as MetrnlTg mouse, was constructed based on the LoxP-Cre system. Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-CreERT2 (Lgr5cre−ERT2) genotype mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology through homologous recombination. TetO-H2B-GFP mice were crossed with Villin-rtTA2-M2 mice to generate Villin-rtTA2-M2/TetO-H2B-GFP mice (H2B-GFPInt).

Details of the mice are provided in the Supporting Information The primer sequences used for genotyping all the mentioned genotypes are provided in Supporting Information Table S2. All primers for quantification of mRNA by real-time PCR are listed with their sequences in Supporting Information Table S3. All antibodies for immunoblotting, immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence are listed with their sequences in Supporting Information Table S4. All the mice mentioned above were housed in a comfortable living environment with relatively stable conditions, including a temperature range of 22‒25 °C, humidity of 50%, atmospheric pressure of 20‒40 Pa, and a light cycle from 8:00 to 20:00. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Committee on Ethics of Medicine, Naval Medical University, PLA (SYXK2023-0007).

Mouse treatments

4-Hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) (ST1015, Beyotime) (i.g.) was administered to mice with the following genotypes: Lgr5tdTomato, MetrnlKO−Lgr5−tdTomato, MetrnlKO(WT)-Lgr5−tdTomato, MetrnlTg−Lgr5−tdTomato, and MetrnlTg(WT)-Lgr5−tdTomato. The mice were then administered a dose of 20 mg/mL for five consecutive days, followed by a 1-week drug withdrawal period before proceeding with subsequent experiments.

For mice with the H2B-GFPInt, MetrnlKO−H2B−GFP, MetrnlKO(WT)-H2B−GFP, MetrnlTg−H2B−GFP, and MetrnlTg(WT)-H2B−GFP, doxycycline (ST039B, Beyotime) was administered through their drinking water. After continuous induction of 4 mg/mL for 7 days, there was a 2-week drug withdrawal period before proceeding with subsequent experiments.

Radiation

For mice, on the day of irradiation, the weight of each mouse is recorded, and 1% sodium pentobarbital is administered at a volume of 0.1 mL per 10 g of body weight (i.p.). The mice are then transported to the Co60 γ-ray source at the Radiation Center of Naval Medical University. Lead blocks are arranged to expose a 2 cm gap for abdominal irradiation. After the irradiation is completed, the mice are returned to their cages, and their weights are measured and recorded at a fixed time each day.

For cells, after counting, the cells are plated in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well in culture medium. The cells are then transported to the Co60 γ-ray source at the Radiation Center of Naval Medical University. The total irradiation dose is set to 10 Gy, and subsequent experiments are conducted after completion of the irradiation.

Cell culture

The human colorectal cancer cells HCT-116 were purchased from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The HCT-116 cells were cultured in DMEM (HyClone, USA), which was supplemented with 1% streptomycin and penicillin and 10% FBS (Gibco, USA). Cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Adenovirus overexpressing the human metrnl gene

Shandong Weizhen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (WZ Biosciences) has constructed the coding region sequence of the human METRNL gene into the pADM-CMV-C-FH-mCMV-copGFP vector. A kozak sequence GCCACC was added before the ATG start codon, and the restriction enzyme sites at both ends are SfaAII/RsrII. The specific coding region sequence can be found in the CDS sequence on NCBI at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/NM_001004431.3.

Lentivirus-mediated knockdown of metrnl in cells

Lentiviruses encoding small hairpin RNA (shRNA) directed against human Metrnl were constructed by Shanghai Bio-Link Company (Shanghai, China). Briefly, the following three shRNA target sequences were designed: 5′-CACGCTTTAGTGACTTTCAAA-3′. Both the recombinant lentivirus encoding shRNA targeting Metrnl and the lentivirus encoding scrambled shRNA were prepared and titered to 108 transfection units (TU)/mL.

EdU proliferation assay

The mice were injected with 1 mg/mL EdU solution at a dosage of 5 mg/kg of body weight. For the click reaction, immerse the sections in 1 × PBS for 10 min to observe hydration of the embedding agent. Drop DAPI onto the tissue to be stained and let it sit for 10 min in the dark. Discard the DAPI and rinse the sections in 1 × PBS for another 15 min. Add 20 μL of anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium to each tissue. Capture images using a confocal microscope.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction

After thawing the “Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit” (Beyotime P0027) at room temperature, mix the reagents and then add PMSF (at a ratio of 100:1). Briefly, centrifuge the samples to collect the supernatant, which is the cytoplasmic protein. Next, add 50 μL of nuclear protein extraction reagent (supplemented with PMSF) to the pellet. Vortex and centrifuge at 15,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, carefully aspirate the supernatant, which contains the extracted nuclear protein.

Treatment with recombinant metrnl

Human recombinant Metrnl protein (rhMetrnl) (R&D Systems, #9339) was diluted to 20 μg/mL and aliquoted for future use. For mouse experiments, rhMetrnl (3 μg) was administered via tail intravenous injection, while for cell experiments, rhMetrnl (0.5 μg) was added per well in 6-well plates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism V9.5 software. Two-way ANOVA was employed to determine statistical significance (P-value) among means of at least three groups. Results are presented as mean ± SEM. Kaplan‒Meier survival curves for mice were analyzed using the Mantel‒Cox test. Western blot (WB) results represent data from at least three independent experiments. IF, H&E, and IHC results represent data from at least three independent mice, respectively. For in vitro experiments, at least three independent experiments were performed and subjected to quantitative analysis. For in vivo experiments, at least three mice were used per group.

Results

Time-dependent metrnl expression emerges as radiation response

We initially established the optimal experimental conditions by anesthetizing and securing C57 mice (Supporting Information Fig. S1A), followed by exposing them to a range of radiation doses to monitor their mortality rates (Fig. S1B). As shown in the figure, when the mice were irradiated with a dose of 20 Gy, the final survival rate was 55.56%. Conversely, at a dose of 30 Gy, mortality initiated on the 4th day, with a drastically reduced final survival rate of 11.11%. Consequently, the radiation dose of 20 Gy was chosen as the appropriate dose for subsequent experimental procedures.

In order to better explore the role of Metrnl after irradiation, we chose GSE165318 as the research object. We analyzed the data from mice collected before irradiation and at 3 days post-irradiation, and the results showed that 13,403 cells were left and divided into 18 clusters, which were visualized on the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) dimensional reduction map (Supporting Information Fig. S2A). We clustered them into eight cell types, namely Enteroendocrine cells, Paneth cells, Goblet cells, Tuft cells, B cells, stem cells, Enterocytes, and T cells (Fig. S2B). Upon analyzing the distribution of Metrnl across these cell types, we found that Metrnl is expressed in nearly all cell types, suggesting the widespread expression of Metrnl in the intestinal tissue (Fig. S2C). However, it is noteworthy that Metrnl exhibits higher expression levels in Goblet cells, Stem cells, Enterocytes, and Tuft cells compared to other cell types (Fig. S2C‒E). The specific markers in each cluster were as follows: Cd79a, Cd3d, Dclk1, Chgb, Mptx2, Muc 2, and Apoa 1 (Fig. S2D and E). At the same time, we analyzed the distribution of each cell type after irradiation (Fig. S2F). We observed an increase in Enterocytes and a decrease in Stem cells following irradiation.

Following irradiation, the expression level of Metrnl in C57 mice displayed a dynamic pattern, first rising and then declining. As depicted in the figures, the mRNA level of Metrnl exhibited an upward trend initially, peaking on the 2nd day, followed by a gradual decrease, with the lowest expression noted on the 5th day (Supporting Information Fig. S3A). Similarly, the Metrnl protein level demonstrated a comparable trend, with significantly elevated expression on the 1st and 2nd days, followed by a decrease, reaching its lowest point on the 5th day (Fig. S3B). The immunofluorescence findings further corroborated this observation, indicating an initial surge in Metrnl expression post-radiation, followed by a decline (Fig. S3C).

In addition to tracking the variations in Metrnl, we carried out comprehensive tests to analyze the changes in C57 mice at various time points following irradiation. Initially, we focused on investigating the baseline physiological conditions of these mice at different post-irradiation intervals. Specifically, the period from the 1st to the 2nd day post-irradiation was identified as the DNA damage phase, whereas the 3rd to the 4th days marked the crypt regeneration phase (Supporting Information Fig. S4). The findings indicated that upon exposure to 20 Gy of radiation, the mice experienced a notable decline in body weight, which bottomed out on the fifth day before gradually recovering (Fig. S4A). Furthermore, the length of the small intestine tended to shorten after irradiation, with a statistically significant reduction observed on the 4th day compared to the control group (0 Day) (Fig. S4B). On the 5th day, diarrhea was significantly more frequent in irradiated mice compared to the control group (Fig. S4C). The length of the small intestinal villi decreased progressively over time post-irradiation, showcasing a clear time-dependent effect (Fig. S4D). Similarly, the extent of small intestinal crypt dispersion also demonstrated a time-dependent pattern, with crypt deformation, dispersion, and eventual disappearance occurring as time elapsed (Fig. S4E).

Following irradiation, C57 mice displayed a dynamic pattern of small intestine differentiation, characterized by an initial decline followed by a subsequent increase. The analysis revealed that the differentiation markers for intestinal epithelial cells, specifically alkaline phosphatase (AP), goblet cell markers (AB/PAS), and Paneth cell markers (Lysozyme), exhibited a notable decrease within the DNA damage phase (Days 1 and 2) and a subsequent increase during the regenerative phase (Days 3 and 4) (Fig. S4F‒H). Similarly, the proliferation of intestinal crypts in irradiated C57 mice followed a comparable trend, with a decrease during the DNA damage phase and an increase during the crypt regeneration phase (Fig. S4I). Moreover, the gene damage in the small intestine crypts of irradiated C57 mice also showed a fluctuating trend, with an initial surge followed by a decline (Fig. S4J). Specifically, P53 levels rose on Days one and 2 post-irradiation and decreased on Days three and 4, as clearly depicted in the figure.

Metrnl confers a protection against radiation enteritis

To explore the impact of Metrnl in intestinal epithelium on radiation-induced intestinal injury, we developed two mouse models: MetrnlKO, featuring a specific knockout of Metrnl in intestinal epithelium, and MetrnlTg, exhibiting specific overexpression of Metrnl in intestinal epithelium. The methodology for creating the MetrnlKO model and its corresponding wild-type model, MetrnlKO(WT) (Supporting Information Fig. S5A), and validation at both gene and protein levels was performed (Fig. S5B‒S5E). Furthermore, a comprehensive assessment of the basic physiological status of MetrnlKO mice revealed no notable differences in survival rates, body weight, small intestine length, or villus-crypt morphology when compared to control mice MetrnlKO(WT) (Fig. S5F‒S5J). Additionally, our observations on the differentiation status of the small intestine in both mouse models indicated no significant variation in the number of goblet cells and Paneth cells (Fig. S5K‒S5M). However, it was intriguing to note that the crypt proliferation in MetrnlKO mice was markedly lower than that in control mice, hinting at a potential role of Metrnl in promoting crypt proliferation in mice (Fig. S5N).

The construction procedure for the MetrnlTg mouse model and its corresponding wild-type model, MetrnlTg(WT), is comprehensively outlined (Supporting Information Fig. S6A and S6B), with subsequent validation at both gene and protein levels presented (Fig. S6C‒S6F). Furthermore, an extensive assessment of the basic physiological status of MetrnlTg mice revealed no notable differences in survival rates, body weight, small intestine length, or villus-crypt morphology when compared to their control counterparts MetrnlTg(WT) (Fig. S6G‒S6K). Additionally, our observations on the differentiation status of the small intestine in both mouse models showed no significant variation in the number of goblet cells and Paneth cells (Fig. S6L‒S6N). However, intriguingly, the crypt proliferation in MetrnlTg mice was markedly higher than that in control mice, in stark contrast to the findings observed in MetrnlKO mice. These results provide evidence that Metrnl may play a role in promoting crypt proliferation in mice (Fig. S6O).

Irradiation was performed on both MetrnlTg and MetrnlKO mice, along with their respective control groups. The findings revealed a marked difference in survival rates between MetrnlTg and MetrnlKO mice, with MetrnlKO mice exhibiting a survival rate of just 22.22%, whereas no deaths were observed in the MetrnlTg (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the body weight fluctuations were less pronounced in MetrnlTg mice compared to MetrnlKO mice. Specifically, MetrnlKO mice experienced the most rapid weight loss, with a greater decrease than that observed in MetrnlTg mice on the 3rd day post-irradiation (Fig. 1B). Diarrhea observations indicated no occurrence in MetrnlTg mice. In contrast, MetrnlKO mice had a significantly higher incidence of diarrhea compared to MetrnlKO(WT) (Fig. 1C). Upon measuring the small intestine length, it was found that MetrnlTg mice had significantly longer intestines compared to their control counterparts MetrnlTg(WT), whereas MetrnlKO mice had notably shorter intestines than their control group MetrnlKO(WT) (Fig. 1D). Examination of the small intestine morphology further revealed that the condition of the small intestine in MetrnlTg was significantly better than that of MetrnlTg(WT) post-irradiation, with improved villus length and crypt morphology. Conversely, MetrnlKO mice exhibited crypt dispersion and shortened villi (Fig. 1E). Detection of small intestine crypt proliferation showed that MetrnlTg mice had significantly higher levels of crypt proliferation compared to MetrnlTg(WT), whereas MetrnlKO mice displayed almost no proliferation (Fig. 1F). Metrnl mediated protection operates independently of Lgr5+ ISC

To investigate the protective mechanism of Metrnl in intestinal epithelium, we initially performed pulse-chase labeling with ISC-specific fluorescent mice, designated as Lgr5cre−ERT2; Rosa 26lsl-tdTomato (Lgr5tdTomato) (Supporting Information Fig. S7A‒D). Subsequently, we crossed MetrnlKO and MetrnlTg with Lgr5tdTomato to create Metrnl-specifically knocked out Lgr5-fluorescent mice MetrnlKO−Lgr5−tdTomato and their corresponding wild-type controls MetrnlKO(WT)-Lgr5−tdTomato, as well as Metrnl-specifically overexpressing Lgr5-fluorescent mice MetrnlTg−Lgr5−tdTomato and their wild-type controls MetrnlTg(WT)-Lgr5−tdTomato (Fig. S7E). These four strains of mice were induced with 4-Hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) pulsing for 5 days (4-OHT ON), followed by a 1-week chase (4-OHT OFF), and then exposed to irradiation. Post-irradiation analysis was conducted 72 h later (Fig. 2A). The findings revealed no significant difference in the count of Lgr5+ ISC between MetrnlKO−Lgr5−tdTomato and MetrnlTg−Lgr5−tdTomato mice compared to their respective control groups after irradiation (Fig. 2B and C). Furthermore, we examined the apoptosis levels in these four strains of mice and analyzed them 12 h post-irradiation (Fig. 2D). The results demonstrated no significant difference in the number of cleaved-caspase three in Lgr5+ ISC between MetrnlKO−Lgr5−tdTomato and MetrnlTg−Lgr5−tdTomato mice compared to their respective control groups after irradiation (Fig. 2E and F). Specifically, TetO-H2B-GFP (H2B-GFP) mice were crossed with Villin-rtTA2-M2 transgenic mice to generate H2B-GFPInt. In these mice, the H2B-GFP fusion protein can be pulse-chased during the aging process, allowing for the investigation of LRCs (Supporting Information Fig. S8A‒S8C). Upon induction of luminescence, we found that approximately 15 days post-induction, each crypt harbored an average of only two luminescent cells, a number that remained consistent until 21 days, thereby confirming their identity as LRCs (Fig. S8C).

Metrnl defends against radiation enteritis by activating WNT signaling pathway

To delve deeper into the molecular mechanisms of Metrnl, we subjected small intestinal crypts from irradiated MetrnlTg and MetrnlTg(WT) mice to RNA-Seq analysis. Consistent trends were also evident in the protein expression profiles of the small intestine tissues (Supporting Information Fig. S9). At the cellular level, HCT-116 cells were transfected with an adenovirus harboring a Metrnl overexpression vector. Prior to irradiation, Metrnl overexpression led to marked alterations in nuclear β-catenin, Axin 2, c-MYC, and phosphorylated GSK3β (S9) levels when compared to the control group, and these changes were exacerbated following irradiation (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, we investigated c-MYC expression in LRCs. MetrnlTg−H2B−GFP and its wild-type control MetrnlTg(WT)-H2B−GFP, along with MetrnlKO−H2B−GFP and its wild-type control MetrnlKO(WT)-H2B−GFP, were induced to luminesce using Doxy pulsing for a week (Doxy ON), followed by a 2-week chase (Doxy OFF). Subsequently, these mice were subjected to 20 Gy of irradiation, and observations were conducted 48 h post-irradiation (Fig. 4E). These findings collectively imply that Metrnl may exert a protective role against radiation-induced damage by activating the WNT pathway in LRCs.

C-Kit, as a receptor tyrosine kinase, undergoes dimerization and autophosphorylation upon binding to its ligand, stem cell factor, which in turn activates downstream signaling pathways21,22. Among these, the MAPK signaling pathway is one of the crucial downstream routes following c-Kit activation, playing a pivotal role in regulating various cellular activities such as growth, differentiation, apoptosis, and stress responses23, 24, 25. Thus, we validated the classic downstream pathway protein MEK-ERK of c-KIT and discovered that overexpressing Metrnl led to an upregulation of intracellular p-MEK1/2 and p-ERK1/2 levels, with irradiation further amplifying this effect (Fig. 5D). When c-KIT was knocked down, the differential expression of p-MEK1/2 and p-ERK1/2 vanished, indicating that Metrnl regulates the downstream MEK-ERK pathway via c-KIT (Fig. 5E). To investigate whether Metrnl could interact with the WNT pathway through the MEK-ERK pathway, we irradiated cells after applying an ERK inhibitor. Our results demonstrated that following the administration of GDC0994, both nuclear β-catenin and p-GSK3β (S9) in the downstream WNT pathway were notably decreased, with the difference between the overexpressing group and the control group diminishing (Fig. 5F). Thus, it appears that Metrnl may regulate the WNT pathway by binding to c-KIT and activating the downstream MEK-ERK pathway.

To delve deeper into the interplay between the MEK-ERK and WNT pathways, we employed both the ERK inhibitor GDC0994 and the GSK3β inhibitor Chir99021. The results showed that after using GDC0994, the downstream target proteins c-MYC and Axin-2 of WNT were significantly downregulated, but when Chir99021 was added simultaneously, both were upregulated, and the difference between the two groups disappeared (Fig. 5G). This indicates that Metrnl is likely to function through the interaction between ERK and GSK3β.

The WNT signaling pathway is a complex signal transduction system capable of participating in the regulation of various cellular functions. Its activation mechanisms can be divided into extracellular and intracellular pathways. The extracellular pathway primarily involves the binding of WNT ligands to receptors on the cell membrane, while the intracellular pathway mainly concerns the transduction of signals within the cell and the regulation of target genes26, 27, 28. Therefore, to exclude the possibility of Metrnl regulating WNT through an extracellular pathway, we administered the Wnt3a blocker C59 while overexpressing Metrnl. Our results indicated that despite the blockade, Metrnl remained capable of upregulating the WNT pathway (Fig. 5H). Hence, it seems that Metrnl regulates the WNT pathway not through the extracellular pathway, but via intracellular processes. Furthermore, we verified the direct interaction between ERK and GSK3β (Fig. 5I and J). Collectively, these results imply that the role of Metrnl in regulating the WNT pathway is mediated through ERK-GSK3β.

Discussion

This study identified intestinal Metrnl as a critical protective factor in radiation enteritis by employing Lgr5+ ISC and LRCs-specific bioluminescent mouse models with Metrnl knockout/overexpression. We unveiled a novel pathway mechanism wherein Metrnl binds the c-KIT receptor to activate MEK-ERK signaling and suppress GSK3β, thereby facilitating β-catenin nuclear translocation and ultimately activating the WNT pathway. This sequence of events exerts a protective effect on LRCs, mitigating radiation damage. The identification of the c-KIT/MEK-ERK/GSK3β-WNT axis establishes the first therapeutic framework addressing radiation enteritis through LRCs modulation.

Our study demonstrates that the c-KIT/MEK-ERK/GSK3β-WNT pathway is essential and exclusively responsible for Metrnl-mediated radioprotection. Genetic or pharmacological disruption of c-KIT, ERK, and GSK3β consistently attenuated Metrnl-mediated protection and impaired subsequent WNT activation. This suggests that signaling through this axis appears necessary for Metrnl to promote epithelial regeneration under radiation stress. Specifically, our findings indicate that Metrnl-initiated c-KIT activation engages ERK-dependent phosphorylation, which subsequently inhibits GSK3β activity. This ERK/GSK3β crosstalk could facilitate WNT pathway activation, positioning it as a crucial regulatory node. Unlike canonical radioprotective mechanisms targeting active Lgr5+ ISC, Metrnl primarily influences the LRCs. Furthermore, the sequential integration of c-KIT/MEK–ERK/GSK3β–WNT represents a signaling architecture that, to our knowledge, has not been previously implicated in Metrnl's function or radiation response. Notably, the observed ERK-mediated suppression of GSK3β diverges from canonical WNT regulation, suggesting a context-specific mechanism that merits further investigation.

In 2014, our team, in collaboration with other researchers, unveiled a novel secreted protein, Metrnl (also known as Meteorin-like protein, Cometin, Subfatin, IL-39, IL-41, Meteorin-β, and Metrn-β), which can be released into the bloodstream by specific tissues29. Existing studies have demonstrated that Metrnl derived from different sources exhibits diverse functional properties, such as acting as an adipocyte-derived factor for insulin sensitization15, a myocardial factor against cardiac hypertrophy and cardiac insufficiency30,31, and an endothelial-derived factor that improves endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis32. Meanwhile, numerous clinical studies have delved into the associations between blood Metrnl levels and metabolic diseases, inflammatory diseases, and cardiovascular diseases18,33,34. Given the relatively consistent conclusions drawn from preclinical studies, Metrnl appears to possess the characteristics of being a protective factor and a potential therapeutic target or drug for these diseases. Notably, our research findings indicate that endothelial-derived Metrnl is the primary contributor to circulating Metrnl levels32. In our studies, due to the widespread expression of Metrnl in intestinal epithelia, with significantly higher expression compared to other tissues, we speculated that Metrnl plays a role in intestinal diseases. Our previous work had already identified a protective effect of Metrnl in ulcerative colitis, but its role in radiation-induced intestinal disease had not yet been discovered.

Our findings highlight the multifaceted role of Metrnl in promoting intestinal homeostasis and radioprotection. For instance, Metrnl sustains intestinal antimicrobial peptide expression, crucial for maintaining the gut's primary defense against pathogens, particularly in intestinal disease35. Beyond microbial defense, Metrnl exerts direct cellular protection through potent antioxidant effects against oxidative stress. This activity could limit oxidative damage to DNA and proteins within the intestinal epithelium30,36. Importantly, Metrnl also operates mucosal recovery by facilitating efficient mucosal repair and restoration of barrier function following injury37. Collectively, these mechanisms may operate synergistically to shield the intestine from radiation-induced damage. This expanded understanding of Metrnl's actions moves beyond its canonical roles and positions it as a central coordinator of intestinal resilience. Targeting these interconnected pathways may represent a promising therapeutic strategy not only for radiation enteropathy but also potentially for other gastrointestinal disorders characterized by barrier dysfunction, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Future studies could explore the relative contributions of each mechanism in vivo and their translational potential in clinical applications.

Secreted proteins occupy a pivotal position in living organisms, serving not only as crucial messengers for intercellular communication but also profoundly influencing various physiological functions and disease processes in organisms38,39. Secreted proteins exhibit considerable promise in the therapeutic management of radiation enteritis. Growth factors, including EGF and FGF, effectively stimulate the proliferation and repair of intestinal epithelial cells40, 41, 42. Anti-inflammatory factors, like IL-10 and TGF-β, exert inhibitory effects on intestinal inflammatory responses43,44. Intestinal barrier protective proteins, such as mucin, bolster the intestinal barrier function45. Additionally, macroglobulin alleviates symptoms by inhibiting plasma kallikrein46. These secreted proteins, acting through diverse mechanisms, facilitate intestinal mucosal repair, diminish inflammation, safeguard barrier function, and mitigate pain. In this research, Metrnl is also a secreted protein that demonstrates protective effects against radiation-induced injury. This protein holds great potential in inhibiting the occurrence and progression of radiation enteritis, improving patient prognosis, and offering promising new strategies and methods for the treatment.

Although short-term Metrnl therapy shows promise for radiation enteritis in mechanistic studies, the potential risks of long-term use warrant caution. A primary concern is the potential carcinogenic risk of Metrnl. Specifically, our study found that Metrnl promotes intestinal epithelial regeneration by activating c-KIT–WNT signaling. However, sustained activation of the WNT pathway is a known driver of tumorigenesis, particularly in tissues with pre-existing genetic mutations or radiation-induced DNA instability47,48. Additionally, given that the MAPK and WNT pathways regulate diverse cellular fate decisions, their persistent activation might inhibit normal apoptosis, thereby disrupting the balance between tissue repair and carcinogenesis48. Finally, current research has not established the minimum effective dose or optimal dosing interval required for long-term use.

However, the abovementioned concerns remain purely theoretical. To date, no reports indicate that Metrnl recombinant protein significantly increases cancer incidence. It needs to be investigated in a future designed study. Also, the potential risk can be further minimized through multiple strategies, including strict limitation of the patient population to severely ill patients only, controlled short-term treatment duration, development of more precise dosage forms, and enhanced post-treatment monitoring with long-term follow-ups and screening colonoscopies.

In our study, we focused on two major types of ISC and developed ISC-specific bioluminescent mouse models for tracing purposes. It is important to mention that the Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-creERT2 mouse, anticipated to exhibit bioluminescence, failed to display glowing crypts post-construction (data not shown). Consequently, we cross-bred this mouse with the Rosa-LSL-Tdtomato mouse to produce Lgr5cre−ERT2; Rosa 26lsl-tdTomato mice, which demonstrated fluorescent crypts. This observation aligns with findings from other researchers, and the probable cause is the complexity or bulkiness of the genes, impeding the expression of EGFP49. Our results can provide valuable insights and serve as a reference for future researchers in this domain.

Given the extreme scarcity of LRCs within the crypts (typically only 1‒2 per crypt), the observed phenotypic changes following Metrnl knockout are inherently minimal. Consequently, the interpretation of specific results, such as those presented in Figs. 3, 4F and G, is susceptible to potential divergence. This inherent limitation motivated our subsequent use of Metrnl overexpression for mechanistic validation. It is biologically implausible that significantly more LRCs undergo apoptosis in the Metrnl knockout group post-irradiation compared to controls. Due to this intrinsically low baseline abundance, even a genuine increase in LRC apoptosis severity induced by Metrnl knockout combined with irradiation would rapidly approach a statistical plateau effect. Given these considerations, the study deliberately avoided emphasizing endogenous Metrnl and Metrnl overexpression/recombinant on radiation enteritis of Metrnl action. Thus, the fundamental biological characteristics and scarcity of LRCs constrain the statistical analysis, inevitably complicating the interpretation.

Certainly, our study acknowledges certain limitations. Firstly, it has become evident that ISC extends beyond just Lgr5+ ISC and LRCs, with additional reports suggesting alternative potential LRCs, such as Clu+ ISC and URI+ ISC49,50. These ISC are activated following injury to restore the balance of the stem cell pool and facilitate the regeneration of the intestinal epithelium. Our study primarily concentrated on Lgr5+ ISC and LRCs. Consequently, there is a possibility that Metrnl may exert its protective effects on radiation enteritis through alternative crypt ISC or other cells. Secondly, unlike blood vessels, which are primarily composed of endothelial cells, the intestinal epithelium is a diverse assembly of cells with secretory functions, including goblet cells, Paneth cells, enteroendocrine cells, and tuft cells51. As a result, pinpointing which specific intestinal epithelial cells secrete Metrnl is significantly more intricate and demanding compared to studying vascular endothelial cells. Moreover, our findings revealed that Metrnl is ubiquitously expressed in intestinal epithelial cells, thereby further exacerbating the difficulty in identifying its precise cellular origin. Hence, our study did not extensively explore it.

Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated for the first time that intestinal Metrnl exhibits protective effects on radiation enteritis. This protection is mediated through binding to the c-KIT receptor, which activates the downstream MEK–ERK pathway. Subsequently, ERK promotes the nuclear translocation of β-catenin by binding to GSK3β, thereby activating the WNT pathway. Our research brings about a novel contribution in radiation biology by elucidating the protective mechanism of Metrnl on LRCs under radiation enteritis, while simultaneously providing a potential biomarker for radiation enteritis.
