Towards Settling the Complexity of the Lettericity Problem
Mario Grobler, Nils Morawietz, Silas Cato Sacher

Abstract
The lettericity of a graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) is defined as the smallest size of an alphabet Σ \Sigma such that there is a word w 1 ​ … ​ w | V | ∈ Σ ∗ w_{1}\dots w_{|V|}\in\Sigma^{*} and a decoder 𝒟 ⊆ Σ 2 \mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma^{2} with the property that G G is isomorphic to the letter graph G ⁡ ( 𝒟 , w ) G(\mathcal{D},w) , that is, the graph with vertex set { 1 , … , n } \{1,\dots,n\} and edge set { i j ∣ 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n , w i w j ∈ 𝒟 } \{ij\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n,w_{i}w_{j}\in\mathcal{D}\} . Note that G ⁡ ( 𝒟 , w ) G(\mathcal{D},w) can be seen as a graph with inherent coloring χ : V ⁡ ( G ) → Σ \chi\colon V(G)\rightarrow\Sigma . It is unknown whether the lettericity of a given graph can be computed in polynomial time. The problem to determine the lettericity of a given graph is called the lettericity problem . As a step towards answering the complexity of this problem, we investigate the following retrieval problems : given a graph G G together with two of the three solution-objects (word w w , decoder 𝒟 \mathcal{D} , and coloring χ \chi ), the goal is to compute the third solution-object. We show that word retrieval and decoder retrieval are solvable in polynomial time, while coloring retrieval is equivalent to the graph isomorphism problem. Beyond this, we introduce symmetric lettericity which is a restricted version of lettericity where each decoder needs to be symmetrical ( a ​ b ∈ 𝒟 ab\in\mathcal{D} if and only if b ​ a ∈ 𝒟 ba\in\mathcal{D} ). As we show, the symmetric lettericity of a graph always equals the neighborhood diversity of the graph, which in fact can be computed in linear time.

Introduction

For a given alphabet
\Sigma
, a word
w=w_{1}\dots w_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
and a
decoder

\mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma^{2}
, we define the
letter graph

G(\mathcal{D},w)
as the graph with the vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
and the edge set
\{ij\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n,w_{i}w_{j}\in\mathcal{D}\}
. The
lettericity
of a graph
G
is defined as the smallest size of an alphabet
\Sigma
such that there is a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and a decoder
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma^{2}
, such that
G
is isomorphic to
G(\mathcal{D},w)
. Lettericity was introduced by Petkovšek in 2000
[
13
]
. For instance, for every
m\geq 2
, the star graph
S_{m}
with
m
leaves has lettericity
2
. This can be certified by the word
w:=ab^{m}
and the decoder
\mathcal{D}:=\{ab\}
. Note that,
S_{m}
does not have lettericity
1
because only complete and edge-less graphs have lettericity
1
. The
lettericity problem
is the problem of determining the lettericity of a given graph
G
.

In their original work, Petkovšek gave bounds or precise values for the lettericity of some graph classes such as threshold graphs, cycles and paths. For paths, the exact value was determined to be within one of two values depending on the length of the path.
The problem of determining the lettericity of paths precisely remained open for approximately 20 years and was solved by Ferguson in 2020
[
10
]
.
This goes to show that determining the exact lettericity even of relatively simple graph classes is far form trivial.
Petkovšek already proved in 2000 that for each constant
k
, the recognition problem for
k
-letter graphs, that is, graphs with lettericity at most
k
, is solvable in polynomial time because the set of
k
-letter graphs is well-quasi-ordered and has a finite set of minimal forbidden subgraphs
[
13
]
.
In 2024, Alecu et al.
[
2
]
improved upon this by showing that determining if a given graph has lettericity at most
k
is FPT for the parameter
k
using MSO-formulas. However the complexity of the lettericity problem remained open and they conjectured it to be NP-complete.
For more literature on lettericity, see
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
9
,
11
]
. Furthermore, Feng et al. introduced a generalization of lettericity
[
8
]
, based on a generalized definition of decoders.

A graph parameter related to lettericity is
thinness
, which was introduced by Mannino and Oriolo
[
12
]
. They define the
thinness
of a graph
G
as the smallest number
k
such that there exists an ordering of the vertices
v_{1}<\dots<v_{|G(V)|}
and a partition of
V(G)
into
k
classes
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}
such that for any triple
(r,s,t)
with
r<s<t
, if
v_{r}
and
v_{s}
belong to the same class and
v_{t}v_{r}\in E(G)
, then
v_{t}v_{s}\in E(G)
.
The
thinness problem
, that is the problem of determining the thinness of a given graph, was proven to be
\mathsf{NP}
-complete by Shitov in 2025
[
14
]
.
They used a result by Bonomo and de Estrada
[
6
]
, who showed that the thinness problem with a given partition is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.
Shitov reduced the problem with a given partition to the general thinness problem, which implies that determining the thinness of a graph is NP-hard.
In 2026, Feng et al.
[
7
]
observed that any
k
-letter graph is a
k
-thin graph. In their proof they used the inherent coloring
\chi\colon V(G)\to\Sigma
as a partition.
This motivates the idea that it might be possible to prove a complexity lower bound or upper bound for the lettericity problem with a similar approach, that is, studying the lettericity problem with a given part of the solution already fixed.
In this work we call this kind of problem a
retrieval problem
: given a graph
G
together with two of the three
solution objects
, word
w
, decoder
\mathcal{D}
, and coloring
\chi
, the goal is to compute the third solution object. Indeed, we show that the retrieval of the coloring
\chi
is
\mathsf{GI}
-complete. Furthermore, we show that retrieving the word or the decoder can be done in polynomial time.
Whether there exist reductions between the lettericity problem and these retrieval problems that would clarify the complexity of the lettericity problem remains open.
Finally, we also show that a version of the lettericity problem where the decoder has to be symmetric (that is, where
ab\in\mathcal{D}
if and only if
ba\in\mathcal{D}
) can be solved in polynomial time.
Hence, if the lettericity problem would not be solvable in polynomial time, then a respective hardness proof would need to explicitly construct instances for which no decoder can be symmetrical.

Proofs of statements marked with
(\star)
are omitted due to space constraints.

Preliminaries

We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set of positive integers (excluding 0) and define

.
For a set

2.1
Words and Languages

Let
\Sigma

For a word

and denoted by

We say a word

of a word

, if

can be obtained from

by removing symbols at arbitrary positions of

. Formally,

is a subsequence of

if there is a function
f\colon[m]\rightarrow[n]
such that
f(i)<f(j)
if

and
v_{i}=w_{f(i)}
for all
i\leq m
. For a word

,

is a
factor
of

if

for some
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
.
For a word
w
and a letter

, we call a maximal non-empty factor of

that only contains the letter

a

-run
.
For a set of letters
\Sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma
and a word

, we denote by

the unique maximal subsequence of

that only contains letters of
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
For sets of constant size, we may omit the set-brackets in this notation, that is, we may for example write

instead of

.

2.2
Graph Theory

Throughout this paper mainly we consider undirected, simple, loopless graphs.
A graph
G=(V,E)
consists of its vertex set
V(G):=V
and edge set
E(G):=E
, where
E
is a subset of all size-2 subsets of
V
.
For vertex sets
A
and
B
, we denote by
E(A,B)
the set of all edges of
E
that have one endpoint in
A
and one endpoint in
B
.
We define the (open) neighborhood of a vertex

by
N(v)=\{u\in V\mid\{u,v\}\in E\}
. Similarly, we define the closed neighborhood of
v
by
N[v]=N(v)\cup\{v\}
. We call a pair of distinct vertices
u,v\in V

(true) twins
if
N[u]=N[v]
and

if
N(u)=N(v)
.
Moreover, we call two vertices
u
and
v

, if
N(u)\setminus\{v\}=N(v)\setminus\{u\}
.
Note that
u
and
v
are generalized twins if and only if they are true twins or false twins.
Given a subset
U\subseteq V
of vertices, we denote by
G[U]
the graph induced by
U
, that is, the graph with vertex set
U
and edge set
\{\{u,v\}\in E\mid u,v\in U\}
. A
graph isomorphism
between two graphs

and

is a bijection
f\colon V(G)\to V(G^{\prime})
such that for each
u,v\in V(G)
we have
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
if and only if
f(u)f(v)\in E(G^{\prime})
. An instance of the
Graph Isomorphism
problem consists of two graphs
G_{1},G_{2}
, and the goal is to decide whether there exists a graph isomorphism between
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Recall that
Graph Isomorphism
is
{\mathsf{GI}}
-complete by definition.

A
permutation
is a sequence of the elements of a finite set
X
that contains each element from
X
exactly once. In this sense a permutation of
X
can be seen as a word over the alphabet
X
.
A directed graph
H=(V,A)
consists of a set of vertices and a set of arcs
A\subseteq V\times V
.
A
topological ordering
of a directed graph
H=(V,A)
is a permutation of
V
satisfying
(v,u)\notin A
if
u
precedes
v
.
A
directed acyclic graph (DAG)
is a directed graph without cycles.
A directed graph is a DAG if and only if it has a topological ordering.

2.3
Letter Graphs

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet of size
k
. We call a language
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma^{2}
a
decoder
. For a word
w=\Sigma^{*}
and a decoder
\mathcal{D}
, we define the
k
-letter graph

to be the graph with vertex set
V(G(\mathcal{D},w))=[|w|]
and edge set
E(G(\mathcal{D},w))=\{ij\mid 1\leq i<j\leq|w|,w_{i}w_{j}\in\mathcal{D}\}
.
The
lettericity
of a graph
G
is the smallest value

such that
G
is isomorphic to a
k
-letter graph. Note that every letter graph inherently comes with a vertex coloring
\chi_{w}\colon V(G(\mathcal{D},w))\rightarrow\Sigma
defined as
\chi_{w}(i)=w_{i}
for each
i\in[|w|]
.
For each graph
G=(V,E)
isomorphic to a letter graph

, we say that the graph isomorphism
f\colon V\to V(G(\mathcal{D},w))

respects the coloring

\chi\colon V\to\Sigma
if
\chi(v)=w_{f(v)}
. Note that this is well-defined because
V(G(\mathcal{D},w))=[|w|]
.

Retrieval Problems

3.2
Decoder Retrieval

An instance
I
of
Decoder Retrieval
consists of a graph
G=(V,E)
, an alphabet
\Sigma
, a coloring
\chi\colon V\to\Sigma
, and a word
w\in\Sigma^{|V|}
.
For each letter
a\in\Sigma
, we denote by
V_{a}
the vertices of color
a
, that is,
V_{a}:=\chi^{-1}(a)
.
We further assume that for each letter
a\in\Sigma
,
V_{a}\neq\emptyset
and that
w
contains the letter
a
exactly
|V_{a}|
times.
The goal is to decide whether there is some
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma^{2}
, such that
G
can be obtained from
\chi
and
w
, by using
\mathcal{D}
as the decoder.
We call each such
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma^{2}
a
solution
(for
I
).
If the vertex set of the graph is clear, we may simply define the edge set of the graph in an instance of
Decoder Retrieval
.
That is, we may say that
(E,\Sigma,\chi,w)
is an instance of
Decoder Retrieval
, where

is a set of edges.

We show that

can be solved in polynomial time. To present the algorithm, we proceed in four steps. In the first step, we show that we can decide whether a given decoder
\mathcal{D}
is in fact a solution for our given
Decoder Retrieval
-instance.
Afterwards, we show that we can make the choice on whether
ab
or

is in a solution greedily for many letter pairs

.
The only cases where we cannot do this are pairs
\{a,b\}
where

is a palindrome and edges between

and
V_{b}
are non-trivial, that is, some possible edges between these vertex sets exists and some do not exist.
For these remaining letter pairs

and

\{ab,ba\}
in a solution immediately forces a choice for the decoder word of

in the same solution.
Based on this, we finally reduce the decision on whether there is a solution for the

to True satisfies the formula
F
.
As
\mathcal{D}
is a solution and for each
\{a,b\}\in\mathcal{P}
is a one-sided letter pair,
Lemma

3
implies that
|\mathcal{D}\cap\{ab,ba\}|=1
.
Hence, the clauses of
Item

1
are satisfied.
Further,
Lemma

5
implies that for each
\{a,b\}\in\mathcal{P}
where
w[a,b]
is not a palindrome, the word
d_{ab}
is part of
\mathcal{D}
.
Hence, the clauses of
Item

2
are satisfied.
Now, let
\{a,b\}\in\mathcal{P}
and
\{b,c\}\in\mathcal{P}
be distinct letter pairs where both
w[a,b]
and
w[b,c]
contain at least two
b
-runs and where
w[b,c]
is a palindrome.
Let
d_{ab}
be the unique word of
\{ab,ba\}
that is contained in
\mathcal{D}
.
By
Lemma

6
, there is a unique
d_{bc}\in\{bc,cb\}
, such that
d_{bc}\in\mathcal{D}
.
Hence, the clauses of
Item

3
are satisfied.
Finally, we consider the clauses of
Item

4
.
To this end, let
a\in\Sigma
and we distinguish whether
P_{a}^{n}=\emptyset
.

If
P_{a}^{n}\neq\emptyset
, let
b\in P_{a}^{n}
be as chosen in
Item

4a
.
As
\mathcal{D}
is a solution,
Lemma

5
implies that for each
c\in P_{a}^{n}
,
d_{ac}\in\mathcal{D}
.
In particular,
d_{ab}\in\mathcal{D}
.
Thus,
Lemma

6
implies that
d_{ac}\in\mathcal{D}
for each
c\in P_{a}^{p}
.
This in particular implies that
\mathcal{D}\cap\{ac,ca\mid c\in P_{a}\}=\{d_{ac}\mid c\in P_{a}\}
is a solution for
I^{\prime}
.
Hence, the clause
\neg d_{ab}
was not added in
Item

4a
, which implies that the clauses of
Item

4a
for letter
a
are satisfied.

If
P_{a}^{n}=\emptyset
, let
b\in P_{a}^{p}
be as chosen in
Item

4b
.
Moreover, let
d_{ab}
be the unique word of
\{ab,ba\}\cap\mathcal{D}
.
As
\mathcal{D}
is a solution,
Lemma

6
implies that
d_{ac}\in\mathcal{D}
for each
c\in P_{a}^{p}
.
This in particular implies that
\mathcal{D}\cap\{ac,ca\mid c\in P_{a}\}=\{d_{ac}\mid c\in P_{a}\}
is a solution for
I^{\prime}
.
Hence, the clause
\neg d_{ab}
was not added in
Item

4b
, which implies that the clauses of
Item

4b
for letter
a
are satisfied.

Thus,
F
is satisfied by assigning exactly the variables of
A
to True.

(\Leftarrow)

Let
A
be exactly the variables assigned to True by an arbitrary but fixed satisfying assignment of
F
.
We show that there is a solution
\mathcal{D}
for
I
with
\mathcal{D}\cap\{ab,ba\mid\{a,b\}\in\mathcal{P}\}=A
.
In particular, we show that
\mathcal{D}:=A\cup B
is a solution for
I
, where
B:=\{aa\mid a\in\Sigma,G[V_{a}]~\text{is a clique}\}\cup\{ab,ba\mid\{a,b\}\in\binom{\Sigma}{2}\setminus\mathcal{P},|E(V_{a},V_{b})|=|V_{a}|\cdot|V_{b}|\}
.
As we only constructed the formula
F
after our sanity check in which we checked whether for each
a\in\Sigma
,
G[V_{a}]
is a clique or an independent set,
1
and
2
imply that the choice of
B
satisfies the first two items of
Lemma

7
.
To show that
\mathcal{D}
is a solution, it thus remains to show that also the last point of
Lemma

7
holds.
This follows immediately by the fact that the clauses of
Item

4a
and
Item

4b
are satisfied by setting exactly the variables of
A
to True.
Hence,
Lemma

7
implies that
\mathcal{D}
is a solution for
I
.

\Box

This thus implies the following, as the formula
F
can be computed in polynomial time and the previous sanity check also runs in polynomial time.

Theorem 3.2

Decoder Retrieval
can be solved in polynomial time.

3.3
Coloring Retrieval

The
Coloring Retrieval
problem is defined as follows. Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and a decoder
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma^{2}
, decide whether there is a coloring
\chi\colon V\rightarrow\Sigma
that is consistent with the inherent coloring of
G(\mathcal{D},w)
.

Theorem 3.3

Coloring Retrieval
is
\mathsf{GI}
-complete.

Proof

The containment in
\mathsf{GI}
holds immediately: An instance
(G,\Sigma,\mathcal{D},w)
of
Coloring Retrieval
is a yes-instance if and only if
G
and
G(\mathcal{D},w)
are isomorphic.
As
G(\mathcal{D},w)
can be computed in polynomial time, there is a trivial reduction from
Coloring Retrieval
to
Graph Isomorphism
.

The
\mathsf{GI}
-hardness can be seen as follows.
Let
I:=(G=(V,E),H=(U,F))
be an instance of
Graph Isomorphism
.
We obtain an instance
I^{\prime}:=(G^{\prime},\Sigma,\mathcal{D},w)
of
Coloring Retrieval
as follows:
We set
G^{\prime}:=G
,
\Sigma:=U
,
\mathcal{D}:=\{uv,vu\mid\{u,v\}\in F\}
, and define
w
to be an arbitrary permutation of
U
.
Note that
H=G(\mathcal{D},w)
.
This immediately implies that
I
is a yes-instance if and only if
G
is isomorphic to
G(\mathcal{D},w)
.
Hence,
Coloring Retrieval
is also
\mathsf{GI}
-hard.
\Box

4
Symmetric Lettericity

In this section, we consider the lettericity problem, when each allowed decoder needs to be
symmetric
, that is,
ab\in\mathcal{D}
if and only if
ba\in\mathcal{D}
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
We let
\mathrm{sl}(G)
denote the smallest size of any alphabet
\Sigma
, such that there is a symmetric decoder
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma^{2}
and a word
w\in\Sigma^{|V|}
for which
G
is isomorphic to
G(\mathcal{D},w)
.
Note that
G(\mathcal{D},w)
is isomorphic to
G(\mathcal{D},w^{\prime})
for every permutation
w^{\prime}
of
w
.

Observation 3

For each graph
G
we have
l(G)\leq sl(G)
.

We show that
\mathrm{sl}(G)
equals the neighborhood diversity of
G
, a parameter that can be computed in linear time.
Here, the neighborhood diversity
\mathrm{nd}(G)
of
G
denotes the smallest partition
(V_{1},\dots,V_{p})
of
V
, such that for each
i\in[1,p]
and for any two vertices
u,v\in V_{i}
,
u
and
v
are generalized twins, that is
N(u)\setminus\{v\}=N(v)\setminus\{u\}
.
Note that this implies that for each
i\in[1,p]
,
V_{i}
is a clique or an independent set.

We start by showing that two vertices that are not generalized twins cannot receive the same color under.

Lemma 9

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let
u,v\in V
with
N(u)\setminus\{v\}\neq N(v)\setminus\{u\}
.
Then for every symmetric decoder
\mathcal{D}
and each word
w
for which there is a graph isomorphism
f\colon V\to V(G(\mathcal{D},w))
,
w_{f(u)}\neq w_{f(v)}
.

Proof

Assume towards a contradiction that
w_{f(u)}=w_{f(v)}
.
As
N(u)\setminus\{v\}\neq N(v)\setminus\{u\}
, there is a vertex
x\in V\setminus\{u,v\}
that has exactly one neighbor in
\{u,v\}
.
Assume without loss of generality that
x
is adjacent to
u
and non-adjacent to
v
.
As
f
is a graph isomorphism between
G
and
G(\mathcal{D},w)
,
\mathcal{D}
contains at least one of
\{w_{f(u)}w_{f(x)},w_{f(x)}w_{f(u)}\}
.
In fact, since
\mathcal{D}
is a symmetric decoder,
\mathcal{D}
contains both these word.
Hence, by
w_{f(u)}=w_{f(v)}
, we get that
f(v)
is adjacent to
f(x)
in
G(\mathcal{D},w)
.
This contradicts the fact that
f
is a graph isomorphism between
G
and
G(\mathcal{D},w)
, as
v
and
x
are non-adjacent in
G
.
Thus,
w_{f(u)}\neq w_{f(v)}
.

\Box

Note that this immediately implies that for each graph
G
,
\mathrm{sl}(G)\geq\mathrm{nd}(G)
.
We now show that the inequality also holds in the other direction.

Theorem 4.1

For every graph
G
,
\mathrm{sl}(G)=\mathrm{nd}(G)
.

Proof

Due to
Lemma

9
, it remains to show that
\mathrm{sl}(G)\leq\mathrm{nd}(G)
.
Let
p:=\mathrm{nd}(G)
and let
(V_{1},\dots,V_{p})
be a partition of
V
, such that for each
i\in[1,p]
and for any two vertices
u,v\in V_{i}
,
N(u)\setminus\{v\}=N(v)\setminus\{u\}
.
Recall that this implies that for each
i\in[1,p]
,
G(V_{i})
is a clique or an independent set.
Moreover, it implies that for all
1\leq i<j\leq p
,
|E(V_{i},V_{j})|\in\{0,|V_{i}|\cdot|V_{j}|\}
.
That is, either each vertex of
V_{i}
is adjacent to each vertex of
V_{j}
, or there are no edges between vertices of
V_{i}
and vertices of
V_{j}
.

To show that
\mathrm{sl}(G)\leq\mathrm{nd}(G)=p
, we define an alphabet
\Sigma
of size
p
, a symmetric decoder
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma^{2}
, and a word
w\in\Sigma^{|V|}
such that
G(\mathcal{D},w)
is isomorphic to
G
.
We set
\Sigma:=[1,p]
and
w:=1^{|V_{1}|}\cdot\ldots\cdot p^{|V_{p}|}
.
Next, we define the symmetric decoder
\mathcal{D}
.
We initialize
\mathcal{D}
as
\{ii\mid i\in[1,p],G[V_{i}]~\text{is a clique}\}
.
For each
1\leq i<j\leq p
, we add the words
ij
and
ji
to the decoder if and only if
|E(V_{i},V_{j})|=|V_{i}|\cdot|V_{j}|
.
This completes the definition of
\mathcal{D}
.
We show that
G
is isomorphic to
G(\mathcal{D},w)
.
To this end, let
f\colon V\to V(G(\mathcal{D},w))
be an arbitrary bijection fulfilling for each
v\in V
and
i\in[1,p]
,
w_{f(v)}=i
if and only if
v\in V_{i}
.
Since
w
contains exactly
|V_{i}|
many times the letter
i
, such a function
f
exists.
We show that
f
is a graph isomorphism between
G
and
G(\mathcal{D},w)
.

Let
u
and
v
be distinct vertices of
V
.
We show that
\{u,v\}\in E
if and only if
\{f(u),f(v)\}
is an edge of
G(\mathcal{D},w)
.
Let
i,j\in[1,p]
, such that
u\in V_{i}
and
v\in V_{j}
.

Case:
i=j
.

Hence,
\{u,v\}\in E
if and only if
V_{i}=V_{j}
is a clique in
G
.
This is the case if and only if
ii\in\mathcal{D}
, which is the case if and only if
\{f(u),f(v)\}
is an edge of
G(\mathcal{D},w)
, as
w_{f(u)}=w_{f(v)}=i
.

Case:
i\neq j
.

Hence,
\{u,v\}\in E
if and only if
|E(V_{i},V_{j})|=|V_{i}|\cdot|V_{j}|
.
This is the case if and only if
\{ij,ji\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
, which is the case if and only if
\{f(u),f(v)\}
is an edge of
G(\mathcal{D},w)
, as
w_{f(u)}=i
and
w_{f(v)}=j
.

Thus,
G
is isomorphic to
G(\mathcal{D},w)
, which implies that
\mathrm{sl}(G)\leq p
by the fact that
\mathcal{D}
is a symmetric decoder and
\Sigma
has size
p
.

\Box

5
Conclusion

We now briefly discuss another direction to approach the complexity of the lettericity problem.
Namely, in contrast to asking whether we can derive/retrieve one of the three objects for a solution of the lettericity problem, we can ask for a single given such object, whether we can derive the other two objects.
We call the respective problems
extension problems
.
For example,
Decoder Extension
would be the problem, where we are given a graph
G
, an alphabet
\Sigma
, and a decoder
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\Sigma^{2}
, and we ask whether there is a word
w
and a coloring
\chi
, such that
G(\mathcal{D},w)=G
, while respecting the coloring
\chi
.

For example,
Decoder Extension
can easily be observed to be
\mathsf{GI}
-hard.
This hardness follows from the reduction behind
Theorem

3.3
, as the chosen word
w
for the
Coloring Retrieval
-instance was chosen arbitrarily among all possible permutations of the alphabet.
Moreover, we can ensure that no two vertices in the
Graph Isomorphism
-instance are generalized twins, since subdividing each edge in both
G
and
H
ensures this property (assuming there are no connected components of size at most
2
) and thus yields an equivalent instance.

For future work, it would be interesting to analyze the other two extension problems, namely
Word Extension
, where we try to find a decoder and a coloring for a given word, and
Coloring Extension
, where we try to find a decoder and a word for a given coloring.
Our presented algorithms for the retrieval problems do not seem to be adaptable to work for these extension problems, as the algorithms highly relied on having two of the three objects being known.

Other interesting directions for future work could be to look into generalizations of the lettericity problem. For example, what if we allow each vertex to receive an arbitrary set of color instead of just a single one. That is,
w
would not be a word of
\Sigma^{n}
anymore but rather a sequence of
n
subsets of
\Sigma
.
For such a generalization, multiple possible rules for when an edge is added to the letter graph can be considered.
For example, let
i
and
j
be positions in
w
with
i<j
and let
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
be the set of colors of these two positions respectively.
One way to define the edges of the letter graph could be to require that for each
\alpha\in C_{i}
and each
\beta\in C_{j}
,
\alpha\beta
is a word of the decoder
\mathcal{D}
.
Other edge definition rules could require that (i) there is at least one
\alpha\in C_{i}
and at least one
\beta\in C_{j}
, such that
\alpha\beta\in\mathcal{D}
or (ii) the number of pairs
(\alpha,\beta)\in C_{i}\times C_{j}
with
\alpha\beta\in\mathcal{D}
is at least a given threshold
t
(for example
t=\frac{|C_{i}\times C_{j}|}{2}
).
Such a generalization of letter graphs could allow for an even more compact representation of graphs.
Note that for the normal definition of
w
, that is, where each vertex as only a single color, all three example definitions coincide.
