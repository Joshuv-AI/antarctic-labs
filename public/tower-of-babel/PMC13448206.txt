3D biomimetic microgel platform for multiple myeloma: Approaching tumor microenvironment on cell lines and patient-derived cells
Authors: M. Inmaculada García-Briega, Joaquín Ródenas-Rochina, Alison Carroll, Ludovica Rossi, Carolina Vicente-Tomás, Maricarmen García Garay, Rafael Andreu Lapiedra, Lourdes Cordón, Amparo Sempere, José Luís Gómez Ribelles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work aimed to develop and characterize a three-dimensional culture platform that recreates the tumor microenvironment of multiple myeloma cells (MMCs), focusing on how atypical plasma cells (aPCs) interact with other bone marrow cells and extracellular matrix (ECM) components to develop drug resistance. The biomimetic platform consists of a static system based on a conical agarose geometry that facilitates the agglomeration and proliferation of aPCs. Magnetic alginate microgels, manufactured by microfluidics, configure the ECM through a layer-by-layer functionalization with collagen, hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulfate, and heparin. In addition, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMMSC) pellets were integrated to simulate direct and indirect interactions between BMMSCs and MMCs. The platform has been validated with three lines of MMCs (RPMI8226, MM1S, U266) and the patients' mononuclear cell fraction collected from bone marrow aspirates extracted in two Spanish Hospitals. The microgel analysis aimed to verify stability and to characterize the presence of the different coatings. Biological studies have investigated the proliferation and resistance to drug treatments in MM cell lines. In addition, patient-derived cells were cultured for up to 14 days to study the survival and possible immunophenotypic changes of the atypical plasma cell (aPC) fraction.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is the second most common hematologic malignancy [1]. This disease is a B-cell neoplasm characterized by a clonal proliferation of atypical plasma cells (aPCs) in the bone marrow (BM). This proliferation leads to abnormal production of monoclonal immunoglobulins (usually IgG or IgA type or free light chain) and infiltration of the aPCs into the BM, resulting in organ damage and various clinical complications, such as anemia, renal failure, and hypercalcemia [2,3]. Although therapeutic advances have made it possible to extend the life of patients, MM remains incurable in most cases [4,5]. The persistence of this disease is due to the development of drug resistance by these tumor cells [3,6,7], allowing them to remain protected within the BM even after intensive treatment [5].

In vitro disease models have established themselves as an essential tool in the study of factors involved in tumor development and their response to antitumor drugs. Their use in MM involves the assessment of cell lines and aPCs extracted from patients [[8], [9], [10]]. The response of both is essentially different. While cell lines are genetically identical populations with stable and continuous growth over time [11,12], primary cells have a very limited lifespan, with undetermined growth and a more heterogeneous population [13,14]. Immortalized aPCs have been used in some in vitro models aimed at recreating these cells in patients. The response to drugs and stimuli in these immortalized lines represents the intra- and inter-patient heterogeneity itself [[15], [16], [17]]. In fact, in vitro models enable the understanding of what happens in the biological niche of the BM, studying how its different elements regulate the functions of tumor cells, thereby affecting their survival and sensitivity to antitumor drugs [18,19]. The realistic interaction between aPCs and other BM cells, extracellular matrix (ECM) biomolecules, and/or soluble factors (SFs) present in the environment will allow the development of new drugs and even personalized treatments, which can be relevant in such a heterogeneous pathology as MM [20]. Currently, only 5% of the new antitumor molecules developed are used in therapeutic regimens [21,22].

A realistic environment, where cellular and non-cellular components are introduced for tumor progression, requires three-dimensionality that promotes cell-cell and cell-ECM interactions. In this direction, some approaches are based on organoid technology, in which the main objective is to better replicate the cellular composition and intercellular interactions rather than the complexity of the 3D microenvironment [23,24]. Other strategies place greater emphasis on reproducing a more biomimetic 3D environment and therefore develop three-dimensional scaffolds that provide mechanical support and seek to recapitulate the 3D ultrastructure of the BM microenvironment [[25], [26], [27]]. These models often include, in total or partially, the elements such as co-cultures, ECM components, or hypoxia, of the BM niche as a permanent part of the platform. Bone Marrow Mesenchymal Stem Cells (BMMSCs) are incorporated in co-culture with tumor cells and ECM elements, such as collagen, hyaluronic acid (HA), or fibronectin (FN), as part of the whole in vitro model. In other cases, Matrigel is directly used as a non-cellular component, presenting all the elements of the ECM without differentiation [25,28]. When the goal is to study how each component individually affects the pro-survival pathways, unwanted difficulties will arise, as it is not possible to specifically include each element and observe its influence on disease progression. Therefore, the versatility of an in vitro model should be considered when designing disease models. Due to their ability to introduce new factors while maintaining or excluding others, it is possible to create in vitro models that are increasingly applicable for studying disease evolution.

To control the ECM elements and their proportion in the MM model, we focused on generating a 3D environment using biomimetic microgels. Microgel is understood as an agglomerate of microspheres in a liquid medium. The cells are cultured occupying the space between the microspheres, not inside them, thus having mobility and interaction with the surfaces of the biomaterial. Key cell-ECM interaction biomolecules are grafted onto the surface of the microspheres, thereby creating a biomimetic environment. In these models, three-dimensionality is achieved by keeping the cells in suspension together with the microspheres [29,30] using orbital [[31], [32], [33], [34]] or reciprocating shaking [35]. The role of collagen, HA, and acrylic acid in generating resistance to the drugs Bortezomib and Dexamethasone was studied in the work of Marín-Payá et al. [31,33], and Clara-Trujillo et al. [34], while García-Briega et al. [32] described the effect of the combination of microgel and co-culture with BMMSCs cells on the proliferation of aPCs.

Results and discussion

The 3D multiple myeloma model

In the in vitro model described in this study, tumor cells (derived from BM aspirates of MM patients or from three different MM cell lines) were cultured in a 3D environment that mimics their niche in the BM. This 3D environment was generated by a dispersion of microspheres whose surface was coated with ECM biomolecules considered to be significant for tumor development. The synthesis and characterization of these microspheres were described in the results section 2.2.

The microgel is used in static conditions because cells and microspheres are deposited on the tip of a conical well made of a non-adherent agarose hydrogel. The conical well ensures both that the environment is bathed with the culture medium and all components remain mobile. The fluidity of the in vitro platform enables the diffusion of nutrients and SFs secreted by the cells, as well as their self-directed spatial reorganization, displacing microspheres. Moreover, the model is equally capable of embodying the cellular components of the tumor niche, particularly the co-culture with BMMSCs. In the model proposed, BMMSCs maintained direct contact when a pellet of BMMSCs was introduced into the agarose conical-shaped well alongside the aPCs. BMMSCs indirect contact with aPCs was provided via cytokines and other soluble factors (SFs) secreted by each type of cells. The BMMSC pellet was placed outside the well and the SFs go through the well wall because it was made of a hydrogel permeable to water.

The proposed model simulates and recapitulates several factors that may be decisive in tumor progression. However, some limitations were detected during this work for immortalized MM cells and primary cells and are reflected in the results 2.3 and 2.4 sections. However, the authors would like to highlight the limitations associated with the direct co-culture condition. BMMSC pellet introduction into the agarose cone together with the aPCs, sample collection, and analysis, proved to be technically complex compared with the other options of the proposed 3D platform. If the direct co-culture platform without ECM elements was already more complex than the other options, adding the microgel would further increase this complexity. This led to only assessing direct co-culture without microgel as one of the proposed model's variants in the platform's validation results, but not the direct co-culture with microgel. Furthermore, since there was no direct co-culture composition capable of also including the acellular stimulus, its behavior was not studied when investigating drug resistance nor included as a condition when assessing the performance of the model with the primary cells culture. However, verifying how direct simple co-culture behaves was important to provide other researchers to fully exploit the potential of the proposed model.

Magnetic behavior was provided due to a homogeneous distribution of Iron Ferrite Nanoparticles (IFNP), which remain inside the core of the microsphere after the crosslinking (Fig. 1A). The Calcium carbonate, which contains the aqueous alginate solution acting as a discontinuous medium, releases Ca2+ ions when the acidic collector media drops its pH. This mechanism jellifies the alginate chains from the inside to the outside of the microspheres, as described in Lui et al. The mean diameter of the moMAG in pure water, when manufactured with flow fluxes of 1.5 mL h−1 in the discontinuous medium and 55 mL h−1 in the continuous one, was 124.2 ± 14.5 μm. Meanwhile, the size distribution followed a positive asymmetric non-normal distribution as shown in Fig. 1B, indicating that some droplets coalesce in the coaxial collector before gelling, resulting in larger microspheres. The moMAG diameter was sensitive to the media used and the time spent immersed in them, in good agreement with a previous work [35]. Thus, the diameter in cell culture media was 146.0 ± 20.0 μm in both Red and White RPMI1640, significantly higher than the mean diameter in water (Fig. 1C). After 14 days at 37°C in the three different liquids, the moMAG Feret diameter in water remained stable, unlike the diameters in culture media, which were smaller than those under the same conditions at the beginning of the experiment (Fig. 1C). The primary reason for this phenomenon was that the hydrogel network progressively lost water and crosslinking ions, resulting in a tighter polymer matrix. In moMAG, Ca2+ ions were exchanged with Na + or other ions present in the culture medium, reducing the crosslink density and leading to bead shrinkage [35,37,38].

Microspheres were functionalized employing the Layer-by-Layer (LbL) technique, with poly-L-lysine (PLL) as the polycation and heparin (Hep), chondroitin sulfate (CS), and hyaluronic acid (HA) as polyanions. CaCl2 was the salt used in all LbL solutions to prevent the migration of calcium ions from the alginate network, thereby maintaining the consistency of the microspheres. On the other hand, the selection of PLL as a polycation reinforced the retention of alginate inside the microsphere, as well as reduced microsphere aggregation during functionalization [35]. The presence of the IFNP facilitated the manipulation of the microspheres during the LbL process and helped prevent material loss in successive washes.

The glutaraldehyde chemistry has been used to link the amino groups of the collagen to the amino groups of the PLL by covalent bonds. Four series of microspheres were prepared: three of them, named hereafter P/Hep, P/HA, and P/CS, produced by LbL and exhibiting in the last layer the polysaccharide; and one with grafted collagen at the surface, named COL. These coatings exhibited functional groups that could be recognized by cell receptors or capture and present growth factors or cytokines to cells, thereby mimicking the BM niche.

Fig. 1D–G showed that the different pairs of polyelectrolytes provided different behaviors to the microspheres, even though the thickness of the multilayer should not exceed a few microns. It was remarkable that PLL could maintain the diameter of the control microspheres in the simple coatings under similar conditions, but the collagen grafting caused the microspheres to swell somewhat. Specifically, P/Hep (Fig. 1D) maintained the diameter of the microspheres in water over time, but when immersed in media, they swelled and remained swollen throughout the culture duration.

The opposite occurred with P/CS (Fig. 1E), which retained its original diameter despite changes in salinity. In water, the diameter dispersed significantly over time, although this happened on all the media at this coating. P/HA (Fig. 1F) only increased the diameter in white RPMI1640 compared with the control, but all the other conditions maintained a similar diameter. In fact, although the diameters decreased over time in the cell culture media, they remained within the original diameter. In Fig. 1G, the COL diameters show an increase compared with the control in all conditions. In culture medium, the microspheres' diameter was significantly reduced over time in culture, possibly due to collagen liquefaction, as this experiment was carried out at 37°C.

In addition to this morphological study of the functionalized microspheres, specific coating characterizations were performed to demonstrate the presence of each ECM element. Firstly, Fourier Transform InfraRed (FTIR) spectroscopy evidenced the presence of the characteristic functional groups of the coatings due to several peak changes and shifts (Fig. 2A). The distinctive peak of alginate (1590 cm−1) shifted to 1630 cm−1 due to the overlapping of the asymmetric COO- stretching of alginate and the O-H vibration of water [35,39,40]. In functionalized samples, this peak broadened (1600-1630 cm−1). In P/CS and P/HA, a peak predominated at 1600 cm−1 due to a higher number of free carboxylate groups (COO-) [40], since CS and HA had more functional groups than Hep or collagen, while the number of amines (provided by PLL) was constant [41]. This explained that, although there was an amide bond formation in the samples (peak at 1650 cm−1) [40], it was eclipsed by the peaks related to the free carboxylate groups [42]. In contrast, in P/Hep and P/COL, the peak at 1640 cm−1 (Amide I) was more intense, indicating more efficient crosslinking [43]. Hep had more sulfate groups (in detriment of carboxylate ones) [44] and collagen favored the amide signal because of its protein structure and the use of glutaraldehyde. The alginate peak at 1420 cm−1 (symmetric COO- stretching) remained constant in all samples [45]. The peak at 1240 cm−1 (S=O and amide III) [40] appeared most marked in P/Hep and P/CS, moderate in COL, and absent in P/HA and moMAG. Finally, the glycosidic bonds (β1→4 or 1 → 3) in Hep, CS, and HA originated peaks at 1160 and 940 cm−1 [40], which were absent in the controls (moMAG), qualitatively confirming the functionalization of the samples.Fig. 2Coating detection on the microspheres: FTIR spectra of wet microspheres moMAG, P/CS, P/Hep, P/HA, COL (A), and TGA spectra of the microspheres (B). Secondly, thermogravimetric assays (TGA) were performed on all the samples as stated in the Materials and Methods section. In TGA, the basis of the calculation lies in the characteristic processes of thermal degradation of the alginate core of the microspheres, and then, to study the differences between the moMAG and the coated ones. The humid microspheres (moMAG) had thermograms with a remarkable weight drop (before 200°C) due to water evaporation (Fig. 2B). Even though the coated samples were drier when the TGA assay was performed, they exhibited a weight drop due to water evaporation before 200°C. This means the 200°C weight could be considered the dry mass of the microsphere. The temperature derivative of the weight loss, normalized to the dry mass of each sample, dw(T)/dT·(1/w(200°C)) (Fig. 2C), allowed for the identification of the breakage of the different covalent bonds present in the various components of the sample using the deconvolutions of each peak.

moMAG showed two overlapped characteristic peaks, I and II, in dw/dT between 200 and 350°C (Fig. 2D) due to ionic calcium crosslinking [46,47]. The area under these two overlapping peaks determined that alginate lost 41% of its weight in this temperature interval. These two peaks were identified in the thermograms of the coated samples by calculating the weight fraction of alginate in the coated microspheres and, consequently, the weight fraction of the coating.

Thus, P/CS I and II peaks in dw/dT represented a loss of 37% of the sample's weight (Fig. 2E), which means that the weight fraction of the coating layer was 10%. It was not surprising that the very thin coating layer made up an appreciable weight fraction of the microsphere because the alginate core was formed from a solution containing only 1.5% alginate; therefore, the core of the microsphere contained little material. Analogously, the coating layer was 5% by weight in P/Hep microspheres, 10% in P/HA, and 36% in COL microspheres (see S1 in the Supplementary Material). The COL result was expected, considering that collagen molecules were grafted across the surface of an almost finished P/CS coating, and it already accounted for 10% of the weight.

In addition to the qualitative detection of the coatings provided by diameters and FTIR, and the quantitative detection through TGA, Blyscan, and hydroxyproline assays were performed to quantify the COL, CS, and Hep deposited in the P/CS, P/Hep, and COL microspheres. While 1 μL of P/Hep microspheres contained 198 ± 6 ng of Hep, 1 μL of P/CS microspheres contained 253 ± 45 ng of CS. In addition, 1 μL of COL microspheres contained 268 ± 30 ng of collagen type I. Due to the alginate core, HA could not be directly quantified with biochemical analysis, although its presence in the coating was evident from the results of the other techniques.

Cell lines cultures

As stated in the results section 2.1, this work aimed to develop a modulated 3D in vitro platform for both primary and immortalized cells in vitro. This section explains and validates all the platform possibilities explored and demonstrates its use as a drug resistance model.

The first step was to verify the effect of both the microgel and the three-dimensional agarose-based construct. We also assessed the platform's capabilities regarding the introduction of mesenchymal stem cell co-cultures. Briefly, all the configurations of our culture platform that have been tested (see further details in the Materials and Methods section) were an agarose cubical hydrogel that fits a P12 well and has four conical holes of an effective volume of 120 μL. The aPCs culture occurs inside these holes (HG cones henceforth). There were five different culture conditions proved in this paper (Fig. 3). The simplest one, which acts as a control, is the “suspension condition" (SUSP), with only a suspension of 6 × 104 aPCs inside the HG cone. Then, the “direct co-culture" (DIR) was a co-culture condition with 6 × 104 BMMSCs pellet placed inside the HG cone with the aPCs, and the “indirect co-culture" (COX) was a co-culture in which the aPCs were seeded normally, but one BMMSCs pellet per HG cone filled was placed at the bottom of the P12 well, outside the HG; two additional series with ECM-microgel were used to evaluate the influence of the ECM elements and complete the platform. The “microgel condition" (MCR) with the 6 × 104 aPCs placed in the HG cone and a mix of microspheres P/HA, P/Hep, COL, and P/CS in equal proportions. Finally, the “indirect co-culture with microgel" (COW) consisted of the indirect co-culture condition adding the microgel.Fig. 3Schematic representation of the different variants of the proposed platform, featuring an agarose 1.5% w/v hydrogel block with four conical-shaped wells arranged in a P12 well-plate. The microgel conditions were based on the inclusion of 8.4 μL of the four types of microspheres in equal proportions, mixed with the aPCs and introduced into the HG cone. This decision was motivated by other microgel-related models, where the volume of microspheres in the volume of the construct was 7% [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Also, the use of equal ECM ratios was intentionally selected as a simplified and controlled baseline, since MM cells interact dynamically with multiple BM microenvironments, and there was currently no established physiological ECM composition representative of the MM niche [48,49]. Moreover, this equal-ratio design provided a modular and experimentally robust framework that enabled the independent evaluation of each ECM component by selectively modifying or removing individual elements while minimizing confounding effects caused by changes in overall matrix composition.

To prove the influence of the 3D environment and the ECM microgel, an experiment with RPMI8226 cell line was carried out. The SUSP and MCR conditions were compared with 2D aPCs culture with or without microgel (2D-MCR and 2D, respectively). During the first 3 days of culture, RPMI8226 proliferation was comparable between all conditions (Fig. 4A). Nevertheless, from day 3 onwards, a higher proliferative rate was evident in the 3D environments. After 5 days, it was clear that not only the 3D environment, but also the presence of microgels, resulted in higher cell numbers. To explore the effect in more depth, inert microspheres for the cells (moMAG functionalized with a PLL-Alginate 3-bilayer surface coating, ALG) were cultured and compared with no microspheres (SUSP) and the previously used bioactive microsphere mix (MCR) (Fig. 4B). From day 3, both types of microspheres significantly increased proliferation compared to the control without them (SUSP), maintaining this growth until day 7 without any signs of saturation. Therefore, the higher proliferation of RPMI8226 was not only due to the conical geometry, previously described as promoting proliferation [27], but to an intrinsic effect of the microgel since both MCR and ALG outperformed the SUSP. This response was attributed, on the one hand, to the creation of denser cellular microenvironments, where large microspheres (13 times larger than cells) generated niches that favored the cells' proximity and concentrated their SFs, triggering autocrine pro-survival pathways [[50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. On the other hand, the presence of microspheres intensified hypoxic gradients generated by the own aPCs oxygen consumption while growing, recreating the BM and activating pathways such as HIF-1 [[55], [56], [57]]. Moreover, the differences between 5 and 7 days with or without ECM functionalization demonstrated that the presence of ECM elements improved the outcome. Because the ECM components added by MCR conditions could retain more bioactive molecules (e.g., IL-6, Heparinase) [[58], [59], [60], [61], [62]] and adhered to the aPCs [[62], [63], [64], [65]], amplifying the proliferative effects through different pathways.

To explore the potential of this platform, direct and indirect interactions between aPCs and BMMSCs were examined using BMMSC pellets (DIR, COX, and COW conditions). It could be easier to include the BMMSCs seeded at the bottom of the P12 well. However, BMMSCs were included as a pellet because they release more key factors (e.g., IL-6, VEGF) in 3D than in 2D [[66], [67], [68]]. In addition, the BMMSCs pellets provide a 1:1 relation with the aPCs, which could not be incorporated otherwise. The growth of aPCs cell lines RPMI8226 and MM1s happened regardless of the culture conditions up to 5 days of culture (Fig. 4C and D). The selection of these 2 cell lines was based on the intra-patient representation. Both cell lines have different biological and phenotypic characteristics than U266 cell line, including differences in proliferation, cytokine dependence, and drug sensitivity [69,70]. Nevertheless, RPMI8226 and MM.1s exhibited the most representative and contrasting responses under the evaluated conditions, enabling a clearer interpretation of the platform-associated effects.

Focusing on aPCs' experimental results, it is relevant to point out that some conditions became saturated, and cell numbers at 7 days were similar to those obtained at 5 days. This saturation was line-dependent rather than condition-dependent. On the one hand, RPMI8226 saturated when it had no MCR (COX and DIR conditions). On the other hand, in MM1s, it happened when there were co-cultures (COW, DIR, and COX conditions). The results suggested that the most significant effect on proliferation in the proposed three-dimensional environment was provided by MCR, which positively affected proliferation up to 7 days after seeding. Its effect alone was very high, as no differences were seen in RPMI8226 when adding or not the BMMSCs, regardless of whether the microgel was present (MCR and COW conditions). The hypothesis was that aPCs (especially MM1s) required more nutrients as the culture progressed [71], and the maximum number of cells varied depending on the cell line. The fact that co-cultures did not grow after 5 days unless there were ECM elements (DIR and COX conditions) was attributed to the fact that a replacement of culture medium every 2 days was insufficient to maintain the growth tendency of aPCs due to the BMMSCs' nutrient consumption. In addition, too frequent renewal of the culture medium could prematurely remove the secretome from both BMMSCs and aPCs themselves, reducing their positive effect on aPCs progression.

To complete the characterization of the predictive and biomimetic capacity of the platform presented in this paper, drug resistance experiments were performed. For drug-response assays, IC50 experiments with Bortezomib (BTZ) and Dexamethasone (DEX) were first performed in agarose hydrogels under the SUSP condition (see S2 in the Supplementary Material), establishing 5 nM BTZ and 1 μM DEX as the IC50 doses for the basic 3D culture platform. Drug-response studies were then carried out using the SUSP, MCR, and COW conditions, together with conventional 2D culture without biomaterials as a control. These conditions enabled the evaluation of the effects of the 3D structure alone (2D vs SUSP), the combined influence of the 3D structure and ECM elements (MCR), and the effect of the synergy of all those elements and the co-culture (COW). The DIR condition, consisting of direct co-culture without ECM microspheres, was not included in the drug-response assays because the objective was to evaluate the combined contribution of both cellular and non-cellular stimuli within the same microenvironment. To account for intra-patient variability, RPMI8226, MM1s, and U266 multiple myeloma cell lines were cultured in all the conditions [69,70].

Drugs reduced MM1s and U266 populations in 2D and 3D following the same trend (Fig. 5B and C). In detail, the MM1s line was the most affected by both drugs, almost completely killing the cell population in both 2D and 3D. In turn, U266 proliferation was only affected by BTZ treatment, but not by DEX. Both results agreed with those observed when obtaining the IC50 (see S2 in the Supplementary Material), where the U266 line would likely have tolerated a higher dose of DEX, but MM1s would not. Regarding the RPMI8226 line, the trend in 3D and 2D was different (Fig. 5A). In 2D, BTZ significantly reduced the population of aPCs compared to non-treated (NT), DEX-treated, and BTZ-treated 3D culture (SUSP, MCR, and COW). When 3D culture (SUSP) was performed, it was seen that only the DEX-treated population was significantly reduced compared to the non-treated, but not to the BTZ-treated. This implies that the spatial arrangement of aPCs alone could modify the neoplasm's ability to resist treatments [27,72,73]. Regarding ECM functionalized microspheres (MCR conditions), an effect on resistance to BTZ was observed in the MM1s line (Fig. 5C). In the other lines, it was not observed that the MCR improved the proliferation of aPCs, either with BTZ or DEX, maintaining the trends they had in the SUSP condition. The outcome was not surprising as the microgel had full proliferative potential beyond the 3 days of the drug resistance experiment (days 5 and 7, Fig. 4C and D). Thus, the HG cone maintained the hypoxia influence over drug resistance, and the microgel enhanced the survival by boosting positive loops related to the aPCs' SFs. In addition, the effect on BTZ resistance was attributed to the fact that COL, Hep, CS, and HA promote integrin and CD44 binding on aPCs, thereby activating the pro-survival PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK pathways [[75], [76], [77], [78]].

When microgel was combined with indirect co-cultures (COW condition), the outcomes were completely different. All COW conditions treated with BTZ or DEX had higher proliferation than the non-treated COW conditions (Fig. 5A–C). Particularly, RPMI8226 COW protected cell proliferation against DEX more than SUSP and MCR (Fig. 5A). In MM1s, proliferation in BTZ-COW was higher than in BTZ-MCR, non-treated-COW, and DEX-COW. Also, DEX-COW populations became resistant to the treatment, proliferating significantly more than DEX-SUSP, DEX-MCR, and non-treated-COW (Fig. 5C). In U266, DEX-COW proliferated more than non-treated-COW, while BTZ-COW increased relative proliferation compared to BTZ-SUSP (Fig. 5B).

Due to the effect observed at the COW condition, the conclusion was that BMMSCs' SFs were able to reach the aPCs diffusing through the agarose HG, promoting aPCs survival through the activation of diverse signaling pathways [73,[78], [79], [80], [81], [82]]. In the same way, aPCs could transform BMMSCs' phenotype by means of the SFs, which were able to penetrate the agarose and induce a “malignant” phenotype. However, further studies should be performed in this sense.

Cell cycle results complemented MTS ones, allowing for a deeper investigation into the mechanisms that enabled drug resistance. BTZ inhibited the proteasome, preventing it from executing its functions and resulting in excessive protein accumulation. This effect could lead to arrest in both proliferative and non-proliferative stages and induce apoptosis, as it affected all cyclins and proteins involved in cell cycle progression and survival. The overall computation was that cells in proliferative phases would be reduced, as BTZ ultimately blocks the proliferation of aPCs [[83], [84], [85]]. In turn, DEX was a glucocorticoid (GC) that caused cell arrest in the G1 phase and induced apoptosis through the upregulation of CDK inhibitors, downregulation of key cyclins for progression to the S phase, and repression of protein transcription (e.g., NF-κB, AP-1) [86].

From the results of the cell cycle (Fig. 5D–F), it was evident that the behavior of both drugs and the presence of the different elements of the bone marrow changed how the drugs affected the cell cycle. DEX-SUSP had a lower percentage of cells in proliferative phases in all lines, which corresponds to the arrest in G1 caused by this treatment [86,87]. Similarly, BTZ yielded lower proliferative phase (S-G2-M) values than the non-treated SUSP, consistent with previous reports [84,[88], [89], [90]]. The microgel environment in MCR condition promoted proliferation when any treatment was applied in RPMI8226 and MM1s (Fig. 5D–F). Moreover, DEX-MCR still induced arrest in non-proliferative phases in all lines, as significant differences with non-treated MCR were maintained (Fig. 5D–F). In contrast, at BTZ-MCR, percentages equal to those of the non-treated MCR could be observed in the RPMI8226 and U266 cell lines. In addition, RPMI8226 and U266 BTZ-MCR presented a higher rate of cells in proliferative stages than SUSP, resulting in an increase in cell numbers in RPMI8226 but not in U266 (Fig. 5A, B, D, E). For the MM1s cell line, BTZ-MCR slightly reduced the percentage of cells at proliferative stages compared with non-treated MCR, but they had a significant increase in the proliferation ratio (Fig. 5C–F). This could indicate that the effect of BTZ was reduced when ECM elements were present, as they were helping to stimulate the development of drug resistance [80,88,91].

Finally, combining indirect BMMSCs co-cultures with functionalized microspheres (COW condition) results in the most significant changes in the cell cycle. There was an increase in cells at proliferative stages in all treatments (non-treated, BTZ, DEX) compared to their counterparts in the SUSP condition. This supports the idea that the biological niche promotes the proliferation and survival of aPCs through a combination of ECM and BMMSCs' SFs, which enhance proliferation [32,72,79,82] In addition, the results showed that all biological niche elements present at COW were determinant on the resistance to DEX and BTZ, more than “solo”cells 3D suspension (SUSP) and partially biomimetic environments like the microgel (MCR) [73,80,89,[92], [93], [94]]. Deepening into detail, there was a lower percentage of RPMI8226 in G2-M/S with the drugs than without them (Fig. 5D). Compared to the results without the biomimetic environment, it seems that COW has supplied part of the arrest in G0/G1 generated by these treatments, which was reflected in the proliferation ratios of the cell line (Fig. 5A). In the other two lines, proliferation under treated conditions was practically equal to that in non-treated conditions (Fig. 5E and F), which would explain why we had the same or higher proliferation ratios as in untreated conditions (Fig. 5B and C).

Although the present study demonstrates the potential of the platform, several additional analyses could provide a more detailed understanding of culture evolution over time. For example, hypoxia levels within the cell–microsphere aggregates could be evaluated using oxygen probes. Other possible future experiments could be the characterization of the proteins adsorbed onto the microspheres of the microgel or the expression of cell surface markers of immortalized aPCs. Moreover, the key of this model is its modular design, which enables other experiments that fall beyond the scope of the present study. For example, the independent evaluation of individual ECM biomolecules, either by culturing cells in microgels containing only one specific coating or by selectively removing components from the complete microgel formulation and comparing the resulting biological responses. Therefore, the proposed platform could represent both the scaffold elements and the live components of the biological niche of the BM, in which cultured aPCs responded as expected. In particular, the COW condition successfully reproduced in vivo-like behavior by generating drug resistance to DEX and BTZ, a phenomenon observed in clinics.

Primary cell cultures

To evaluate the applicability of our 3D model for ex vivo propagation of primary aPCs, all mononuclear cells (MNCs) from BM aspirates of de novo diagnosed patients with MM were cultured in the COW configuration. The limited number of primary cells obtained from patient samples restricted the number of experimental conditions that could be evaluated, making it necessary to prioritize the most comprehensive and physiologically relevant version of the platform. This is the reason why COW condition was selected. Looking over the experimental details, which are discussed in greater detail in the Materials and Methods section, a total of 2.4 × 105 MNCs were seeded in each HG cone together with 8.4 μL of the microgel mix. Outside the cones, 6 × 104 BMMSCs by HG cone filled outside. Commercial BMMSCs were used for the pellet, rather than the patient's own cells, due to the difficulty in making the expansion times compatible with the culture of other cell types. The change in the initial cell number ensured a sufficient aPCs' representation, as at least 10% of the MNCs were aPCs (assessed by morphologic and flow cytometry techniques, more information at T2 in Supplementary Materials), confirming infiltration by MM [4]. MNCs from 4 patients were used, and cell culture was maintained for 14 days. As stated in the Materials and Methods section 4.6, the usual Annexin-V combined with propidium iodide (PI) viability assay was modified to include different surface markers to differentiate the aPCs population from the rest of the MNCs. Additionally, at each time point, a comprehensive immunophenotype of the MNCs was performed. This enabled the possibility of studying the evolution of the fraction of aPCs in the entire MNCs pool for different culture times, as well as the fraction of viable cells in both aPCs and the entire MNCs population, using flow cytometry.

As shown in Fig. 6A, the viability of the aPCs varied among patients. Nevertheless, in all of them, viability after 14 days was not worse than after 1 day on the platform. The PAC1 and PAC4 samples initially increased but tended to decrease the viability of aPCs later in culture. PAC2 lost some viability after 1 day in culture but maintained stability throughout the rest of the assay, while PAC3 increased viability throughout the experiment. If the viability of the aPCs was compared with the viability of the whole group of MNCs (see S3 in the Supplementary Material), it was observed that, except for PAC1, the trend was the same as with the aPCs. This means that the platform maintained the survival of the whole group of cells, not only that of the cell population of interest. Likewise, if focusing on PAC1, it was seen that although MNCs lost viability over time on the platform, aPCs only lost viability within 8 to 14 days in culture. To complement the viability results, the study of the evolution of the fraction of aPCs was performed (Fig. 6B). Firstly, a general overview was performed by calculating the absolute percentage of aPCs present in each condition. The graph showed the different BM aspirates behavior, although in all cases, the rate of aPCs remained above 20% of the total MNCs. PAC4 lost a significant portion of its aPCs population, decreasing from the initial 50% to a final 38%. Losing the aPCs phenotype might be expected from ex vivo culture, where aPCs typically do not survive over time. However, the reduction of the aPCs fraction could be attributed to the fact that there were more MNCs over time (Fig. S3), which artificially changed the ratio. In this patient, the decline in aPCs may be due to a loss of immunophenotypic expression rather than increased aPC death, based on the viability results obtained (Fig. 6A). Likewise, the PAC4 had a higher % of aPCs, together with PAC1, after 14 days in culture. In contrast to PAC4, in PAC1, the % of aPCs rises with time from 25% to 38%. It was seen that the MNCs population was lost, but the aPCs were maintained with similar viability. This implies that a percentage of aPCs increased because the rest of the cell populations died on the platform over time, not due to the proliferation of aPCs (Fig. S3). Finally, PAC2 and PAC3 exhibited more neutral behaviors, with aPCs' percentages ranging between 23% and 35%, although both ended up below 30%. What can be stated from these results was that PAC3 increased the population of aPCs over time, not because there were more of them, but because aPCs grow in line with the rest of the cells. Furthermore, the same thing happens with PAC2, because although the % of aPCs may change over time, it was anecdotal because there were no changes in the aPCs and MNCs ratios when looking at their viabilities. Some differences were detected over time when culturing the aPCs. The histograms of the 8 markers studied in this work for the 4 patients (Fig. 6C, D, E, F), evidenced that the platform's culture affected some of the typical MM markers. Among the specific markers used to distinguish aPCs from normal PCs, CD38—along with CD138—was particularly relevant, as aPCs showed reduced CD38 expression intensity compared to their regular counterparts [95,96]. Analyzed by patient, CD38 expression increased in PAC1 after only 1 day of in vitro culture and then decreased slightly, although it remained always above the expression level after thawing. In PAC2, the positivity was heterogeneous, indicating a loss of expression after 8 days in culture, while PAC3 and PAC4 maintained their phenotype for this marker. Overall, the use of the platform did not appear to modify the aPCs' CD38 expression.

Regarding CD56, the expression remained positive without changes in patients PAC2 and PAC4. While in PAC1 and PAC3, the positive expression became bimodal over the course of the culture, with a CD56+weak expression favored at 14 days. CD56+ is associated with stronger cell-cell interaction and PC-BM stroma interaction [97,98], so the loss of some CD56 expression could suggest that aPCs are less anchored to the BM niche. This, in the long term, could suggest that the microenvironment has an impact on the evolution and behavior of aPCs, as these adhesion molecules are less expressed and are associated with extramedullary infiltration and a poor prognosis [96,98]. In the case of CD27 expression, positivity is associated with a better prognosis. In literature, smoldering forms of the disease, or MGUS, exhibit an expression shift from negative to positive over time in all patients [95,96,99,100]. This result could suggest that aPCs lost malignancy when on the platform. This change in CD27 expression could occur with some frequency in the biological niche during the disease evolution. The CD19 expression remained stable in the 4 patients over time: 3 were CD19− and 1 was CD19+weak. The loss of this marker's expression is associated with advanced malignancy [96,101], and in the PAC1 (Fig. 6C), it maintained the CD19+weak phenotype. This suggested that the increase in malignancy did not occur on the platform. The CD117 gained positivity in all patients, including those who initially were CD117-. The expression of CD117 in aPCs has been associated with favorable outcomes in patients [95,102]. The fact that the cells in the platform gained CD117 expression could be due to the same factor as CD27. The expression of CD81 evolved differently in each patient. In PAC1, the phenotype was predominantly CD81− at day 0, and it was reversed to mostly CD81+ at day 1. Over time in culture, the positivity softens, but it remained more positive than it was at the beginning. In PAC2, CD81+ was maintained, although its intensity decreased over time, as indicated by the mode in the histogram. In PAC3, CD81 became slightly more negative in the sample over time. In PAC4, a phenomenon similar to PAC1 occurred, but CD81+ cells remained weaker on day 14 compared to day 0. The CD45 marker increased in intensity over time on the platform, except in PAC3, where it showed a slight decrease in positivity. The effect of CD45 inhibition or expression on patient outcomes is currently being investigated, as some studies have associated CD45− with an aberrant PC phenotype and a worse prognostic [102,103]. In contrast, other studies have suggested that CD45+ identifies worse disease progression and outcome and a MM that is more aggressive and resistant [104]. This complicates the evaluation of the general increase of CD45+ in the aPCs in the in vitro model with the 4 cases studied. Finally, the evolution of the CD138 marker was studied, as it was relevant for understanding how the platform affects aPCs cultures. CD138 expression became more positive over time in all 4 patients, indicating that the platform did not induce a loss of CD138 expression in the aPCs. This is relevant since the literature shows that CD138+ is lost when aPCs are in stressful environments, because they revert to more immature phenotypes or due to senescence of the PCs and aPCs [105]. In this case, as the microenvironment did not induce plasticity or dedifferentiation of aPCs, it appears to fulfill its objective of maintaining the cells in conditions as similar as possible to those in vivo.

In summary, it was observed that some markers changed in general terms, while others remained constant after 14 days in culture. Some, such as CD138, changed in a manner that suggests the biomimetic environment was suitable for aPCs. Others, such as CD56 or CD117, change in an unclear way. Given the limited number of patients presented, more biological replicates are needed for a broader understanding of the immunophenotypic changes and the general trends with a stronger statistical basis. Similarly, it may be beneficial to slightly modify the culture protocol and medium renewal rate when the culture time exceeds 8 days. However, it was a good starting point to demonstrate that aPCs did not de-differentiate and survive up to 14 days ex vivo in a complete environment. The aPCs not only influenced the rest of the BM MNCs but also contained BMMSCs SFs that would not be present in the sample, and that the literature shows as very relevant in MM progression. Likewise, hypoxia, three-dimensional geometry, and ECM elements were also present to aid aPCs growth and prevent phenotype loss. These ingredients should be enough to enable the proposed platform to serve as a model for personalized medicine and a reliable drug screening platform for MM or other diseases with non-adherent cells.

Conclusions

The conical-shaped agarose hydrogel promoted aPCs growth, and the presence of microspheres generated a proliferative environment by tightening the cells and accelerating their communication and proliferation. The presence of ECM elements enhances the proliferative effects provided by the microspheres themselves. The microgel played a pivotal role in the cell culture of MMCs and may mask the individual impact of the co-culture conditions on proliferation when combined. In fact, co-culture did not seem to favor proliferation after 5 days of culture. The platform proposed in its simplest form (SUSP) provided different responses to drugs than 2D, highlighting the importance of properly recreating not only the BM niche but also the three-dimensionality in vitro. The microgel alone did not affect the resistance of MMCs to DEX, but when combined with BTZ, it helped two of the three lines survive. The combination of cellular and non-cellular components of the BM niche (COW) may promote drug resistance in MM, as the behavior of aPCs in disease progression has demonstrated.

The trend was to increase or maintain the viability of the patients' aPCs population. Overall, the surface marker expression was similar to diagnostic. Culturing in this platform enables the design of experiments to study the influence of single or complex cellular and non-cellular microenvironment components on aPCs' response in terms of proliferation, viability, drug resistance, and specific signaling pathways. Despite its limitations, the model can be used for drug testing in MM and extended to other hematological malignancies.

Materials and methods

Materials

In the production of the microspheres by microfluidics, a solution of sodium alginate from brown algae (Sigma-Aldrich), iron oxide magnetic nanoparticles (Fe3O4 Ferrotec), and calcium carbonate (Mw 100.09 Da, Scharlab) in ultra-pure water was used as a discontinuous medium. The continuous medium comprised extra-virgin olive oil (La Masía) and soy lecithin (Guinama). Acetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to reticulate the microsphere. Milli-Q water, 98% ethanol (ETOH), and chloroform (Scharlab) were also used to obtain microspheres. The microspheres were coated employing Sigma-Aldrich products Poly-L-lysine hydrobromide (PLL), shark cartilage chondroitin sulfate (CS), sodium salt of Streptococcus Hyaluronic acid (HA), heparin sodium salt from porcine intestinal mucosa (Hep), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), glutaraldehyde (GA), and glycine (Gly). Additionally, bovine collagen type I (COL, Purecol-Advanced Biomatrix), Calcium chloride (CaCl2, PANCREAC), and Carbodiimide (EDC, Iris Biotech GmbH) were used for the functionalization of microspheres.

Sigma-Aldrich reagents citric acid monohydrate, sodium acetate trihydrate, sodium acetate anhydrous, isopropanol, chloramine-T, perchloric acid, 4-(dimethylamine) benzaldehyde, lithium acetate dihydrate, ninhydrin, hydrindantin, ethylene glycol, commercial B1000 Blyscan kit from Bicolor, and Fluka's trans-hydroxy-L-proline were used. To assess the stability of the microspheres, cell culture media RPMI1640 low glucose without L-glutamine with (Red RPMI1640) and without (White RPMI1640) phenol red (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 100ud/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin solution (P/S, Gibco), amphotericin B (Gibco), L-glutamine 200 mM (Lonza) and sodium azide (Sigma-Aldrich) were used.

To fabricate the conical-shaped agarose hydrogel, polydimethylsiloxane (Sylgard 184 silicone) and Liqcreate Bio-Med Clear resin were employed to print the molds. The agarose for molecular biology, low EEO from Sigma-Aldrich, was used at a concentration of 1.5% w/v in Milli-Q water.

Regarding cell culture, in addition to the RPMI1640 media, P/S, DPBS, and L-glutamine, fetal bovine serum (FBS-HI, Capricorn), PromoCell MSC Growth Medium 2 with a 10% v/v of its specific supplements (PromoCell), Trypsin/EDTA 1x (ThermoFisher) were used. For the cell assays, ABCAM colorimetric 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium reagent (MTS assay kit) and cell cycle kit with Annexin-V-FITC and PI (Miltenyi Biotech apoptosis kit) were purchased. Finally, monoclonal antibodies fluorochrome-conjugated CD19-PECy7, CD27-PerCPCy5.5, CD38-APCH7, CD45-HV450, CD45-HV500, CD56-APC, CD81-APCH7, CD117-APC, CD117-HV450, CD138-HV500, CD138-HV421 (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), CD19-PECy7, CD45-PECy7 (Beckman Coulter, San Diego, USA), CD38 multiepitope-FITC, and CD56-PE (Cytognos, Salamanca, Spain) were used.

Microspheres synthesis

Alginate microspheres were fabricated in a microfluidic system with flow focusing geometry following the protocol and assembly described by García-Briega et al. [35] with small modifications. Briefly, the continuous medium (CM) and the collecting medium were based on olive oil with 1% w/w soy lecithin. In the collecting medium, 2% v/w acetic acid was added to this solution to decrease the pH. On the other hand, the discontinuous medium (DM) was composed of a 1.5% w/v alginate suspension with 0.25M CaCO3 and 5% w/walg of iron ferrite nanoparticles (IFNP). The suspension was homogenized using the Ultraturrax T25 (IKA Technologies) disperser. Both solutions were pumped into the circuit using syringe pumps with flow rates of 55 mL/h for CM and 1.5 mL/h for DM.

To prevent precipitation of IFNP, the DM in the syringe was gently but continuously homogenized using a magnetic field generated by a magnetic stirrer (Fig. 7). The CM cuts the DM into microdroplets at the microfluidic device, and they gel on contact with the collecting medium in a coaxial tube outside the chip. The collecting medium decreases the pH of the droplets, releasing Ca2+ from CaCO3 and crosslinking the alginate chains. The magnetic microspheres were collected. To properly clean the microspheres, several washes with chloroform (98% purity) and Milli-Q water were performed to rinse them, and finally, they were subjected to sterilization in an autoclave in Milli-Q water with a 10:1 vwater:vmoMAG ratio.Fig. 7Schematic representation of the experimental setup adapted from García-Briega et al. for the fabrication of the moMAG [35].

Schematic representation of the experimental setup adapted from García-Briega et al. for the fabrication of the moMAG [35].

Microspheres functionalization

The negative surface charge of the alginate magnetic microspheres obtained (moMAG) was utilized to carry out functionalization through the Layer-by-Layer (LbL) technique. The process was performed in sterile conditions. The solutions of the polyanions (Hep, CS, and HA) were prepared in Milli-Q water with CaCl2 75 mM at a concentration of 1 mg mL−1, while that of PLL (polycation) was prepared at 0.5 mg mL−1. The washing solution (WS) consisted of Milli-Q water with CaCl2 at a concentration of 75 mM. Solutions and WS pH were adjusted to 7 on P/CS and to 5.5 on P/Hep and P/HA. LbL coating was performed in 15 mL Falcon tubes in a PTR-35 (Grant-Bio) rotational shaker. The moMAG requires preconditioning to bring the core to the pH of the LbL, which consists of 30′ in WS. After that, sequential immersions in the polycation, WS, and polyanion solutions of 10′ each were performed under rotational shaking to generate a 3-bilayer coating. A magnet was used to facilitate each change of dilution. The ratio was 10:1 vsolution:vmoMAG in all steps. To maximize the stability of the functionalization, the coated microspheres were incubated overnight in a solution of 60 mM EDC, 30 mM NHS, and 75 mM CaCl2. The microspheres were washed twice for 15′, and stored in water at 4°C.

For the type I collagen (COL) functionalization, the protein was grafted onto the last layer of the PLL in the P/CS pair. After the third PLL layer, the microspheres were incubated for 60′ in GA (0.5% w/v). Then, washed twice 15′ and immersed in a 1 mg mL−1 Collagen I solution overnight, washed twice again for 15′ and immersed in a 200 mM Gly bath overnight to block the unreacted aldehyde. On the third day of functionalization, microspheres were washed twice with water for 10′ and stored in water at 4°C.

Microspheres characterization

Several techniques were used to analyze the morphology of the microspheres and confirm that they exhibit the surface biomolecules of interest. Optical microscopy (Nikon Eclipse TS100-F, Japan) was used to evaluate the size and sphericity of coated microspheres after 60′ immersed in water, Red RPMI 1640, or White RPMI1640. Stability was assessed after 14 days of immersion in the same medium at 37°C. For this experiment, the microspheres were 1% v/v concentrated, and 0.01% v/v Sodium Azide was added. Fiji Software was used to analyze all the samples as particles, measuring Feret diameter and circularity. Samples with circularity lower than 0.70 were excluded.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Thermo Nicolet Nexus, Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to detect the different components of the coatings qualitatively. To quantify the mass fraction of the coating in the coated microspheres, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) thermograms were recorded using a Mettler Toledo TGA (USA) between 25°C and 1000°C, with a heating rate of 10°C·min−1 and a nitrogen flow rate of 50 mL min−1. The weight of the samples when the temperature reached 200°C was considered the dry weight of the sample and was used as a reference in the analysis [35]. To determine the concentration of heparin and chondroitin sulfate in P/Hep and P/CS, a Blyscan assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions [106]. Finally, the hydroxyproline assay was used to quantify collagen I grafting following the method of Kafienah and Sims [107].

Cell lines culture

3D platform design and preparation

The commercial multiple myeloma cell lines (aPCs) used in this article were RPMI8226, U266, and MM1s (ATCC, USA). All three lines were cultured in RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 1% L-glutamine and 1% P/S. The percentage of FBS was 10% in RPMI8226 and MM1S and 15% in the U266 cell line. All the cultures were performed at 37°C and a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs) (PromoCell, Germany) were used for co-culture conditions. They were cultured at 4.000 cells·(cm2)−1 with PromoCell growth media 2 supplemented at 10% v/v with their own supplements (PromoCell hMSC medium). After 4 days, they were harvested to prepare the pellet. The pellet of BMMSCs was prepared with the protocol described by García-Briega et al. [32] with minor modifications. Briefly, 3 μL of Cytodex 1 microspheres, 6 × 104 BMMSCs, and 300 μL of PromoCell hMSC medium were placed in a cryotube. The contents were resuspended every 30′ for 3 h to expose the empty areas of the microspheres to nearby cells and stimulate their adhesion. Subsequently, an extra 300 μL of PromoCell MSCs medium was added, and the tubes were left in static culture for 72 h.

The agarose hydrogels (HGs) were produced in house in sterile conditions. 1.5% w/v sterile liquid agarose was poured into silicone molds with a cubical shape (16 × 16 × 15 mm). Before its physical crosslinking, a cap with 4 conical inserts (ø = 5 mm and high = 15 mm) was placed on top of the agarose cube. The HGs were unmolded when solidified and stored in DPBS. HGs were placed in p12 plates, pre-incubated overnight in culture medium. A second change of media was done on the day of cell seeding.

With the preconditioned HGs, 5 different culture configurations representing distinct microenvironmental conditions where stablished (Fig. 3): suspension (SUSP), microgel (MCR), direct contact co-culture (DIR), co-culture without direct contact (COW), co-culture without direct contact nor microgels (COX). SUSP condition had 6 × 104 aPCs in each HG cone. For co-culture conditions, the ratio of BMMSCs:aPCs is 1:1 [32]. So, 1 BMMSCs pellet was placed inside (DIR) or outside (COW, COX) of the HG cone. Microgel-containing conditions (MCR and COW) consisted of the SUSP configuration supplemented with a 7% v/v microgel fraction of the total volume of each microwell of a homogeneous mixture (all microspheres in the same proportion) of functionalized microspheres with each ECM component (P/HA, P/CS, P/Hep, and COL) [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Microspheres were added inside the HG cone together with the aPCs considering that the working volume of each HG cone was 120 μL (8.4 μL of a mixture of functionalized microspheres). The P12 well with the HGs was filled with 2.5 mL of RPMI1640 culture medium to fill the space, keeping the liquid below the top face of the HGs. Within the first 72 h, the media remained unchanged. After that, the complete volume outside the HG was removed and replaced with fresh media every 48 h.

Proliferation experiments

To prepare samples for cell assays, cells were transferred from HGs into Eppendorf tubes with holes in their lids to ensure gas diffusion. For SUSP and COX samples, cells were collected by homogenizing the contents of the cone, followed by three washes of media to ensure complete collection, and were then verified under a microscope. For the DIR condition, cells were collected without homogenization using cut micropipette tips, and the contents were passed through a 70 μm filter to separate BMMSCs from aPCs. Then, four additional washes of the cone were made, and the supernatant was passed through the filter. In samples with microgels (COW and MCR), the cone content was first transferred to an intermediate tube, where the microspheres were separated from the aPCs using a magnet. Three washes of the cone and three of the intermediate cones were performed to collect the aPCs in the final Eppendorf tube with a hole in the lid.

To measure the proliferation of the cell lines, the MTS assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were centrifuged (250G, 5′) in the Eppendorf tubes, the volume of supernatant was levelled to the volume required for the test, and the MTS reagent was added and incubated for 1.5 h at 37°C. The supernatant was pipetted onto a 96-well plate and read at 490 nm using a Victor 3 microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA). After blank subtraction, absorbance data were converted to cell numbers using a calibration curve obtained simultaneously and in the same way as the samples.

Drug culture assays

Cell response to Bortezomib (BTZ) and Dexamethasone (DEX) was assessed in the different configurations studied. An initial IC50 assay was performed by seeding 6 × 104 RPMI8226, MM1s, and U266 cells in the HG conical wells (SUSP condition). After 72h of treatment with the drugs, the MTS assay was performed. From the results obtained (see S2 in the Supplementary Material), doses of 5 nM BTZ and 1 μM DEX were selected for the experiments. 6 × 104 RPMI8226, MM1s, and U266 cells were seeded after a G0/G1 synchronization. The culture conditions studied were 2D, SUSP, MCR, and COW, with non-treated samples included as controls for all options. After 72h of treatment with the drugs, MTS and cell cycle assays were performed.

A PI-based cell cycle assay was conducted only on the aPCs after 72 h of culture in all conditions except the 2D condition. The samples were transferred to Eppendorf tubes (without holes in the lids) as described in the Materials and Methods section 4.5.2. In the Eppendorf tubes, the samples were fixed with 66% EtOH and stored at 4°C until analysis. Cells were transferred from the Eppendorf tubes to cytometry tubes and processed according to the manufacturer's instructions. The data were acquired in the flow cytometer FACSCanto-II (BD Biosciences) at low flow rate. The results obtained were analyzed using the FlowJo flow cytometry software (BD Biosciences) to determine the percentages of cells in the G0/G1, G2/M, and S phases.

Primary cell culture

Primary cells from de novo diagnosed MM patients were obtained after informed consent, in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 and the Ethics Committee of Hospital Universitario y Politécnico La Fe and Hospital Universitario Virgen de la Arrixaca. The BM aspirates were used for their diagnostic, and the surplus was used as the sample in this work. The sample preparation protocol for cryopreservation was performed by the Biobank at IIS La Fe, isolating the mononuclear fraction of the cells (MNCs) from the rest of the BM aspirate using Leucosep (GREINER, ref.: 163290) and Lymphoprep (Serumwerk. Ref.: 04-03-9388/03). The selection of samples for the platform was based on the presence of ≥10% aPCs, as determined by morphological diagnostic criteria and assessed by flow cytometry (data shown at Table T2 in the Supplementary Material). Likewise, specimen selection required enough MNCs to perform the experiments at all the selected moments, so at least the conservation of 5 × 106 MNCs must be conserved to be selected for the experiments. The samples were cultured using COW configuration, including 2.4 × 105 MNCs in each conical-shaped agarose well, and the media was changed every 24h.

MNCs immunophenotyping

The phenotype of the aPCs could change over time due to unknown mechanisms influenced by the ex vivo culture conditions. To study this phenomenon, a MM immunophenotypic panel was performed on the MNCs after thawing, prior to their inclusion in the platform (Day 0). This panel consisted of 8 surface markers, selected according to the EuroFlow guidelines: CD38-FITC, CD56-PE, CD27-PerCP-Cy5.5, CD19-PE-Cy7, CD117-APC, CD81-APC-H7, CD45-V450, and CD138-V500. The same panel was performed on MNCs cultured on the platform after 1, 4, 8, and 14 days in culture. A temporal evolution of the intensity and distribution of these 8 surface antigens in the aPCs population was performed to evaluate whether the platform changed the properties of the plasma cell population. The gating strategy shown in Fig. S4 (see S4 in the Supplementary Material) was optimized to select the aPCs population among the MNCs. The gating strategy was influenced not only by the flow cytometry panels obtained, but also by the phenotype of aPCs at diagnostic.

Viability assay

To determine the cell viability of both MNCs and aPCs in the sample of MNCs, a modified apoptosis assay based on Annexin-V and PI was performed. Firstly, the appropriate monoclonal antibodies for each patient were included to stain the aPCs in the MNCs sample. The samples were stained according to the immunophenotype of the BM aspirate: PAC1 (CD38, CD56, CD138) and PAC2/PAC3/PAC4 (CD38, CD56, CD19, CD45, CD138). After staining, the Annexin-PI assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions, and the data were acquired using a flow cytometer (FACSCanto-II, BD Biosciences). The percentages of viable, apoptotic, and dead aPCs and MNCs were determined using the flow cytometry software FlowJo (BD Biosciences). The data shown in Results section 2.6. were normalized by the viability at day 1 of each sample.

Statistics

GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used for all statistical analyses and graph generation. For materials results, Blyscan and Hydroxyproline were performed with 3 biological replicates of each condition. The stability test included samples of various sizes depending on the condition being studied (see T1 in the supplementary materials). Outliers were identified in the stability assay by the ROUT method with a Q of 5%. After removing the outliers, normality was discarded for all samples using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test (α = 0.05), and comparisons between them were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Differences among groups were stated as p ≤ 0.05 (*), p ≤ 0.01 (**), p ≤ 0.001 (***), p ≤ 0.0001 (****) in the box plots, even though they are non-normal distributed groups.

For cell culture experiments, 4 biological replicates were performed in each condition when the cells used were immortalized. For patient samples, a single sample was used for each time point in the immunophenotyping experiments, and three separate replicates were used for each time point in the viability assays. Outliers were identified by the ROUT method with a Q of 5%. After removing the outliers, the data were analyzed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test (α = 0.05). As all the samples were normal, unpaired T-Student tests (p-value = 0.05) were used to compare two single data groups, and two-way ANOVA and Tukey's post-hoc test (p = 0.05) were used to perform comparisons of three or more groups. The bar graphs show the mean ± 95% Confidence Interval (CI). Differences among groups were stated as p ≤ 0.05 (*), p ≤ 0.01 (**), p ≤ 0.001 (***), p ≤ 0.0001 (****).
