Abstract
This report covers three lines of effort in the U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear Energy, Advanced Reactor Safeguards and Security
program, carried out on operational telemetry from a liquid metal research facility. No attacks were executed and no vulnerability scanning was
performed; every perturbation was synthesised offline from data the facility had already recorded.
Part I tests eleven anomaly detectors — classical statistics, neural networks, sensor-dependency models and causal-structure learners — against
eleven kinds of disturbance injected into real recorded telemetry at four levels of severity, the mildest three sized to stay inside what the plant has
been seen to do and the fourth deliberately outside it. The evaluation covers 3,080 test cases.
Two findings do not depend on anything left open. First, how a detector is tuned and thresholded matters more than which family it belongs to: at
the severest level the two leading detectors are never more than 0.05 apart, yet at one false alarm in a hundred the higher-ranked one misses 38.9%
of the windows in which the plant’s physics were violated and the other misses none. Judged at that alarm rate the panel is not redundant either —
three of the eleven detectors are the only member that catches some disturbance — so a monitoring suite chosen on overall scores would discard
detectors it needs. Second, 852 of the 1,540 primary test cases rest on too little evidence to tell apart from guessing, so a benchmark reporting them
as scores reports noise in the format of a result.
The expectation that motivated the study did not hold: classical methods took the top two places and led on nine of the eleven disturbances,
though they ran against elaborate methods given a fifth of the sensors and no tuning. Picking the best detector per disturbance catches eight of
eleven almost perfectly at the severest level and four of eleven at the mildest, which is the level that matters operationally. Three disturbances
defeat everything tried, topping out between 0.53 and 0.88.
Part II develops a physics-based consistency framework separating equipment degradation from signal perturbation, and finds attribution
capability tracking the number of independent physical relations the instrumentation supports rather than detector complexity. Part III reports a
multi-modal collection capability at an operating facility and the data, library and computing allocation delivered from it; it makes no detection
claims.

1 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report covers three lines of effort in the U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear
Energy, Advanced Reactor Safeguards and Security program. All three use operational
telemetry from a liquid metal research facility. No attacks were carried out and no vul-
nerability scanning was done against that facility or its control systems: every distur-
bance studied here was created in software, afterwards, from data the facility had already
recorded.
  Part I asks how reliably anomaly detectors catch disturbances that the plant could
physically produce.
  Part II asks the question that comes next, once something has been detected: is the cause
a piece of equipment wearing out, or a sensor reading that has been tampered with? One
calls for maintenance and the other for a security response. Part II builds a method that
tells them apart using the physics of the system, and on a simulated pump loop it also
identifies which path the tampering came through — the measurement, the actuator, or the
controller.
  Part III reports the data collection capability built at the facility and what was delivered
from it.

 What is new, and who can use it. Four contributions are intended to be usable outside this
project, each given here with the detail behind it and the limit that bounds it. Detection
quality is reported as a score between 0 and 1, where 0.5 is no better than guessing and 1.0
is perfect.
 A way to build realistic test disturbances. Operating limits are worked out per measurement
channel from the plant’s own history and validated process constraints. For the seven
disturbance types whose size scales with those limits, four severity levels are set against
them: three inside the limits, the fourth deliberately outside as a control. Limits are enforced
one channel at a time, which does not guarantee a whole sequence of readings is something
the plant could produce. Section 2.9.3 writes this up as a procedure another facility can
follow from its own data — the part most likely to be useful elsewhere.
 Knowing when a detection score means nothing. Of the 1,540 test cases in the primary set,
852 rest on too few measurements to tell apart from guessing, and we report them as
unresolved rather than as results. The threshold for “too few” is a rough screen, not an
exact test, and we have not established which way it errs. The finding survives that — the
fraction stays between two fifths and two thirds even if the count is off by a factor of four
in either direction.

12 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

 Telling equipment failure apart from tampering. Part II uses an asymmetry: equipment that
degrades still obeys physical law, while a falsified reading need not, because faking one
signal convincingly is easier than keeping every physical relationship consistent at once.
The method checks a set of those relationships and reads which ones break. It is shown on
a simulated pump loop, where the true answer is known, and on real cold trap data whose
healthy baseline is real but whose faults and tampering were added in software. Across
the three cases what determined how precisely a cause could be identified was how many
independent physical relationships the instruments supported, not how sophisticated the
method was — a pattern across examples rather than a controlled experiment.
 Data and infrastructure delivered. Part III records process telemetry, network traffic, packet
capture and intrusion-detection alerts at an operating facility on a common collection
clock, though we did not measure how closely the streams agree and the period where all
four overlap is known only to the day. A February portion of the roughly four-terabyte
collection went to the collaborating institution with network documentation, a reader
library, a Polaris allocation and instructions. It makes no detection claims.

  Why this matters for U.S. energy security and competitiveness. Advanced reactors
have to show they can be run securely, and monitoring is part of that argument. This work
tests what the argument rests on.
 ∙ Published detector scores are shakier ground than they look. Between two fifths
 and two thirds of the results an evaluation like this produces cannot be told apart
 from guessing. We give a way to tell which ones can.
 ∙ Buying on headline scores does not buy detection. Two detectors with the same
 overall score missed 38.9% and none of the same tampering, once false alarms were
 held to a rate a plant could live with. What separated them was how each was tuned
 and where its alarm was set — and neither shows up in a product’s numbers.
 ∙ Some tampering is not a detection problem, and costs far less to handle before
 the plant is built. One kind here was missed by everything we tried. If that holds
 up, the answer is checking that readings are genuine as they arrive over the network
 — a design choice, not a retrofit.
 ∙ A plant can run this test on itself, without waiting to be attacked. The test distur-
 bances are sized from the facility’s own operating records, so the argument can be
 checked on the plant it is written for.

  What this means for a design decision. A designer trading protocol choices against monitor-
ing is assuming detection can compensate for a weaker data path. That needs checking case

by case, and this work supplies the procedure rather than a settled case. Replaying real
readings is the candidate: nothing beats guessing on it, and a value-based detector should
fail because the values are genuine. But as we built it this disturbance is narrow rather than
weak. It rewrites two fifths of the readings on the one channel it reaches, which is not a
small change — but it reaches only one of the three channels it targets, because the other
two never vary at this facility, and its severity ladder runs backwards, with the severest
level altering half as much as the mildest. Both are faults in how the disturbance was
generated rather than findings about detection. If the structural reading survives once they
are fixed, the remedy is authenticating readings as they enter the network — a design-time
choice, not a monitoring purchase.

  How a detector is tuned and thresholded matters more than which family it comes from — and
much of what a study like this reports is noise. Those two do not depend on anything still open,
and they are what a program can act on. A third would matter more if it holds — that
what simple methods miss may not be a monitoring problem at all — but that is where the
evidence points, not where it has arrived.
  We expected the newer methods — neural networks, dependency graphs, causal in-
ference — to catch disturbances simple statistics miss. The opposite happened: simple
methods took the top two places and led on nine of the eleven types. The causal methods
largely did not fill the gaps they were included for. The best placed third and led on two
types, one of which nothing detects above chance; another gave different answers on
different runs; a third produced scores too coarse to read.
  At the most severe level the best and second-best methods score within 0.05 of each other
on every disturbance type — not so at the mildest, where five separate them by more and
one by 0.237 — and on six of the eleven the runner-up is another simple statistical method,
so this is not agreement across families.
  That near-tie disappears once a false-alarm limit is imposed. Holding false alarms to one
percent, the strongest single method misses 38.9% of the windows in which the plant’s
physics were violated, where the method ranked immediately behind it misses none.
Choose a detector by how it behaves at the alarm rate it will run at, not by an overall
ranking — and the same for choosing a set of them, since three of the eleven are the only
method that catches some disturbance. Requiring several to agree almost never helped,
beating the best single method on two of forty-four cases, though that calculation assumes
they raise false alarms independently, which we did not measure.
  Breadth of coverage is not where the return is. Three disturbance types resist everything
we tried, topping out between 0.53 and 0.88 where eight others are caught almost perfectly.

Whether that residual is also where the risk sits this work does not answer: we measured
how hard each disturbance is to see, not how likely it is or what it would cost. For the two
valve disturbances the readings do move substantially, so the replay explanation fails there
and the reason is unresolved.
  One more result matters for how such evaluations are designed. Detectors gain most of
what they are going to gain at the first step up in severity and almost nothing above it. The

  Progress since the FY25 report. FY25 named five directions for follow-on work and four
were addressed. Physics in the loop arrives twice over: the operating limits that now con-
strain how test disturbances are built in Part I, and Part II’s method, which uses physical
relationships as the detector itself. More methods and combinations of them — the field grew
from four detectors to eleven across four families, and the question of whether combining
them helps was settled rather than left open. Clearer severity levels — FY25 found severity
barely affected detectability, which is what motivated the physics-anchored levels that
replaced it. Network traffic — Part III delivers the capability and the data, though nothing
has yet been detected by combining network and process data. Not addressed: systematic
tuning of detector settings, and live red teaming.
  The evaluation was also rebuilt, from 243 single-run experiments to 3,080 test cases across
up to five runs and two ways of constraining the disturbances. It now reports which cases
rest on too little data to interpret, includes a pre-registered check that passes on all 320 cases
where a detector cannot possibly see the disturbance, and compares the two constraint
modes directly. The two years’ scores are not comparable, because the disturbance catalog,
severity definitions and data preparation all changed, and this report does not compare
them.

  What this does not establish. Everything in Part I comes from one facility, using disturbances
applied to recorded data rather than to a running plant, and the detectors were trained
and tested on the same period of operation. Part II’s method is shown in simulation for
the pump and on a single component elsewhere. Section 2.9.5 gives these and the rest in
full, including five cases where a detector scored perturbed data as more normal than clean
data, and the two valve disturbances whose low ceiling we could not explain.

2 PART I: ANOMALY DETECTOR ROBUSTNESS UNDER PHYSICS-BOUNDED
 PROCESS PERTURBATIONS
Part I addresses a single operational question: when an unsupervised anomaly detector
is deployed on a real industrial process, how reliably does it catch a perturbation that
the process could actually produce, and which detectors are worth deploying at all? We
answer it empirically, using five months of operational telemetry from a liquid metal
research facility. We generate a catalog of 12 perturbation signatures, bounded where their
magnitudes are envelope-derived by the facility’s own physical admissibility envelope
and swept across four severity tiers, and evaluate eleven unsupervised detectors spanning
four paradigm families (classical statistical, neural reconstruction and prediction, sensor-
dependency modeling, and causal structure learning). The output is a per-detector, per-
signature, per-tier capability matrix. The remainder of Part I motivates the physics-bounded
framing; describes the facility, the perturbation catalog, and the evaluation methodology;
presents the capability matrix and the paradigm-specific failure modes it exposes; and
closes with the operational implications, a transferable robustness-assessment protocol,
and the data-integrity lessons learned in building the pipeline.
 Progress since the FY25 report. Because the high-level framing, anomaly-detector evalu-
ation for this facility, is superficially similar to last year’s, we highlight what is new this
cycle. FY25 established the capability-matrix evaluation concept on prior testbed work [6];
FY26 (i) introduces the physics-bounded admissibility envelope and the four-tier severity
ladder, so detectability is measured at the constrained-adversary boundary rather than on
easily separable injections; (ii) expands the evaluated set to eleven detectors across four
paradigm families; and (iii) produces new scientific results together with the transferable
robustness-assessment protocol and the data-integrity invariant framework described
below.

2.1 Motivation
Anomaly detection for industrial control systems (ICS) occupies a critical gap in the
resilience architecture of modern infrastructure: automated monitoring must detect de-
viations that indicate either equipment fault or adversarial perturbation, without access
to labeled perturbation data and without disrupting continuous operations. The practical
challenge is that an attacker with knowledge of the process — or a fault with physics-
consistent behavior — can produce deviations that are difficult to distinguish from normal
operating variation [11, 15, 39]. Such stealthy perturbations remain within the statistical

16 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

envelope of normal behavior precisely so that detectors keyed to statistical deviation stay
below alarm thresholds [3, 13].
  Existing ICS anomaly detection benchmarks evaluate detectors against perturbation sce-
narios derived from attack catalogs or synthetic fault models. Three recur in this literature:

 ∙ SWaT, a six-stage water-treatment testbed, attacked by spoofing sensor and actuator
 values on the fieldbus [16, 27].
 ∙ BATADAL, a simulated SCADA dataset for the C-Town water-distribution network,
 in which attacks and their concealment are injected by simulation [36].
 ∙ HAI, a hardware-in-the-loop testbed coupling a boiler, a turbine and a water-
 treatment process, attacked by manipulating control-loop set-points and read-
 ings [35].

 These campaigns are built for separable evaluation rather than to probe the boundary
of what a physically constrained adversary can produce, and it shows in two ways. The
injected manipulations aim at operational effect, so the process leaves its normal operating
range: tanks are driven to overflow or underflow in SWaT and BATADAL, and control-loop
variables are moved outside their normal limits in HAI, whose authors note that “abnormal
behaviors occurred when some of the parameters were not within the limits of the normal
range”. And where stealth is a design goal, it is pursued by falsifying the reported signal
rather than by keeping the physical perturbation admissible: false sensor values injected
into SWaT’s PLC packets, replay and offset concealment in BATADAL, hidden set-point
changes at HAI’s operator station. While valuable for comparing detectors against a shared
test set, such benchmarks do not answer the operationally critical question: what is the
probability that the detector misses a perturbation that a physically constrained process can actually
produce? A perturbation that exceeds the facility’s physical limits is not operationally
realistic; a detector that catches it provides no operational assurance.
 This work fills that gap by grounding the evaluation in the facility’s own physics,
extending the capability-matrix evaluation approach of our prior testbed work [6]. We
derive admissibility envelopes from historical operational telemetry and validated process
constraints, use them to place a four-tier severity ladder whose lower tiers sit inside the
envelope and whose top tier deliberately crosses it, and evaluate eleven unsupervised
anomaly detectors against 11 signatures across four severity tiers. The result is a detection
capability matrix that characterizes not just whether a detector catches a perturbation, but
at what severity threshold and for which perturbation type.

  We frame perturbation signature design and detector evaluation using vocabulary from
System-Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA) [22] and its cyber-physical extension STPA-
SafeSec [14]. We adopt this framing because it yields a systematic, control-theoretic taxon-
omy of perturbation targets rather than an ad-hoc list, so coverage claims are auditable
against a defined threat space. STPA models a system as a hierarchical control structure in
which controllers issue control actions to controlled processes and receive feedback. An
unsafe control action (UCA) is a control action that, in a particular context, will lead to a
system hazard. STPA-SafeSec extends this framing to cyber-physical systems, enumerating
integrity threats (codes CSTR-I-1 through CSTR-I-8) and availability threats (CSTR-A-1
through CSTR-A-4) on control loop links. Our 12-signature catalog covers 7 of the 12 STPA-
SafeSec codes (all integrity codes except command drop; availability codes are deferred
to future work). This coverage scope defines the threat space for which the evaluation
provides evidence.

2.2 Contributions
 (1) Physics- and empirically bounded perturbation signatures derived from research
 facility telemetry and process constraints: 12 signatures designed and 11 evaluated,
 across 4 admissibility tiers (T1 within historical variation; T4 crossing physics
 constraints).
 (2) Systematic characterization of which detector scoring assumptions fail under which
 perturbation classes, organized by four paradigm families (Classical, Neural, De-
 pendency, Causal).
 (3) Quantified detection gap and probability-of-missing analysis: AUC distributions,
 5-seed mean AUC with standard deviation, a per-cell resolution gate that withholds
 cells carrying too little independent evidence to read, and oracle-vs-realizable false-
 negative-rate sweeps across all 11 signatures. A paired Wilcoxon test over the mode
 comparison is reported but is not offered as a contribution: at five seeds its smallest
 attainable two-sided 𝑝 is 0.0625, so it cannot reject for any input.
 (4) A transferable admissibility envelope methodology: derivation of physics-bounded
 perturbation classes from operational telemetry and validated process constraints,
 presented as a robustness evaluation template for any industrial monitoring system
 with historical data and engineering limits.
 (5) A structured robustness assessment protocol for ICS AI/ML monitoring: 12-signature
 perturbation taxonomy, 4-tier severity framework, and per-paradigm detection capa-
 bility matrix providing a repeatable, evidence-based vulnerability characterization.

2.3.1 Facility Description. The evaluation uses operational telemetry from a liquid metal
thermal-hydraulic test loop (Figure 1). The facility consists of a primary circulation loop and
an instrumented diagnostic subsystem; the monitored diagnostic subsystem includes 146
channels: 120 read-only sensor readings (temperature, flow, and pressure measurements
at multiple axial and radial positions along the heater and cold trap sections) and 26
operator-writable setpoint and command channels (controller setpoints, manual outputs,
and alarm thresholds). These 146 are a curated subset of the subsystem’s much larger
sensor population, selected as the measurement patterns consistently present and stable
across the full five-month collection span.
  The facility operates in two primary regimes: operating, in which all subsystems run at
nominal process conditions, and idle, in which process flow is reduced. A cold trap (CT)
subsystem for impurity removal operates intermittently during steady-state operation;
cold trap active (CTon ) windows comprise approximately 7% of steady-state operating
time.

2.3.2 Dataset. Telemetry was collected from February through July 2025 (approximately
5 months). For the baseline we retain only steady-state operating windows: days flagged
for instrument calibration anomalies (intervals whose readings reflect recalibration rather
than process state) are excluded first, and runs shorter than 60 seconds are absorbed into
their neighbours rather than being carried as segments of their own. Regime statistics are
computed on a 5-minute rolling window, which sets the timescale the labeller resolves but
is not itself a minimum segment length. The preprocessed corpus these windows are drawn
from comprises approximately 46 million rows across 146 sensor and setpoint channels;
the steady-state subset of it is not separately counted.
 Regimes are assigned by an unsupervised pipeline, not by hand-tuned thresholds, so
“normal” is defined by the data and not by an analyst’s cut points. For each hour of
telemetry, a multivariate change-point detector (PELT with a radial-basis cost function)
segments a panel comprising all setpoint channels plus the highest-variance process-
variable channels. Each segment is summarized by four features measured against per-
sensor calibration statistics: mean 𝑧-score, peak absolute 𝑧-score, volatility (rolling standard
deviation), and mean absolute rate of change. Segments are pooled across the full collection
period, standardized, and clustered: the cluster count is chosen by a Bayesian-information-
criterion sweep over Gaussian mixtures and then fixed with 𝑘-means.
 Each cluster centroid is then labeled by scoring it against five operating archetypes
defined over those same four features: steady-state (all four features low: little deviation,

 That guard is also a tripwire, and its reading depends on which population it runs

 Cyclical time features are encoded as a sine and cosine pair, not a single sinusoid:

 (for hour-of-day, 03:00 and 09:00 both map to sin(𝜋/4)), and a detector cannot then
 tell morning operation from afternoon. The pair (sin 𝜃, cos 𝜃) is a point on the unit

 by construction, so no further scaling is applied. Categorical identifiers are label-

 The floor, rather than a tight post-scaling clip, is what bounds per-sensor influence
 here, and for the eighteen zero-IQR channels no clip is even available: the unfloored
 denominator is zero, so the scaled value is not finite and there is nothing for a clip to
 bound. On the channels where a clip could act, one tight enough to control a 543×
 inflation would sit far below the scaled magnitude of the perturbations themselves
 and would collapse tiers 2 through 4 onto a single value, erasing the severity ladder
 the study is built to measure. Correcting the denominator removes the need for
 such a clip.

 bation before it reaches the detectors. In bounded mode (primary) each perturbed

 the nearest admissible value when it falls outside; the multi-sensor conditions of
 Section 2.4.1 shape that support but are not tested per row (see the enforcement
 paragraph there). In unbounded mode the clamp is disabled and the generated value
 is passed through unchanged.

  What bounded mode is not. Three distinctions are easy to conflate and are kept separate
throughout this report.

 ∙ The historical envelope is the range a sensor was actually observed to occupy. Only
 tier 1 is confined to it; tiers 2–4 exceed it by design, in both modes.
 ∙ The physical admissibility envelope is the wider region bounded by process physics.
 Tiers 1–3 draw inside it; tier 4 draws from a support deliberately extended ±10𝜎
 beyond it.
 ∙ Bounded mode is neither of those. It is a per-value filter that fires only where a

2.4 Perturbation Taxonomy: Signatures and Admissibility Tiers
We frame perturbation signature design and detector evaluation in the vocabulary of
System-Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA) [22] and its cyber-physical extension STPA-
SafeSec [14]. STPA models a system as a hierarchical control structure in which controllers
issue control actions to controlled processes and receive feedback. A loss is something of value
to stakeholders that the analysis aims to prevent (e.g., loss of process control authority,
loss of operator confidence in instrumentation). A system-level hazard is a system state that,
in worst-case environmental conditions, will lead to a loss. An unsafe control action (UCA)
is a control action that, in a particular context, will lead to a hazard. STPA enumerates
UCAs in four exhaustive categories: provided when it should not be, not provided when it
should be, provided with incorrect timing or order, and applied for incorrect duration [22].
STPA-SafeSec extends this framing to cyber-physical systems by enumerating integrity
threats (CSTR-I-1 through CSTR-I-8) and availability threats (CSTR-A-1 through CSTR-A-4)
applicable to any link in the control loop — the command path, the feedback path, the
controller, the actuator, or the sensor [14].

2.4.1 Admissibility Envelope Construction. Admissibility envelopes are derived from the
baseline telemetry and validated process constraints. For each sensor channel, the envelope
specifies:
 ∙ Empirical envelope: per-sensor percentile distributions (p1–p99), rate-of-change
 bounds, and spatial uniformity bounds computed from baseline operating windows.
 ∙ Physics constraint boundaries: absolute temperature bounds (e.g., 100–300∘ C for
 sodium systems), spatial uniformity limits (∆𝑇 ≤ 5∘ C across radial thermocouples),
 and thermal time constant bounds extracted from validated process constraints.
 Perturbation targeting draws from the 95 continuous channels carried in the admissibility
envelope. That figure is not a subset of the instrumented channels and the difference matters
when reconciling it against them. The envelope is built over 105 instrumented channels,
of which eleven are excluded because they are discrete state channels (valve and damper
positions, pump and blower power indicators) for which a continuous admissibility range
is not defined. The remaining 94, together with one derived channel (the temperature rise
∆𝑇 across the subsystem, which is a difference of two instrumented channels and not a
measurement of its own), give the 95 entries perturbation targeting draws from. This is a
separate set from the 146 curated channels the detectors score. Six of the eleven score those
146 columns; change point detection additionally receives fourteen cyclic time encodings,
for 160; the remaining four operate under sensor budgets that exclude the encodings by
construction (Section 2.5).

  Constraint Derivation and Enforcement. The physics constraint boundaries are not hand-
specified; they are derived from an exploratory analysis of a dedicated historical operating
run, supplemented by conservative engineering estimates where a parameter cannot be
measured directly.
  A perturbed state is admissible only if it satisfies all of the following at once:
 ∙ absolute sodium temperature stays within [100, 300]∘ C;
 ∙ inlet temperature stays at or above outlet temperature;
 ∙ the temperature rise across the subsystem stays within [15, 55]∘ C while the cold trap
 is active, and is consistent with whether the electromagnetic pump is running or
 idle;
 ∙ the implied thermal efficiency falls within [0.5, 1.2];
 ∙ the quantitative limits derived below are respected.
  The first four conditions follow directly from the design basis and require no derivation.

 Tier parameters are not fixed magnitudes; for each sensor they are drawn from a per-

per sensor per tier: a value distribution over [physmin , physmax ] controlling perturbation

moves. A generator draws a magnitude from the value distribution and sets duration
= magnitude / rate, coupling timing to magnitude through the rate-of-change envelope
rather than through arbitrary timing constants.
 The tiers differ in how the value distribution’s shape (𝛼, 𝛽) and support are set (Table 2).
Writing 𝜇 = (mean − physmin )/support for the sensor’s empirical mean mapped onto [0, 1]
and 𝜎 for its normalized standard deviation, tier 1 reproduces the observed operating

the physics-admissible band; and tiers 3–4 use sub-unity shape parameters that push

 Comparing tiers needs a single number for how large a perturbation each one produces
on a given sensor, and neither obvious candidate works. The distribution’s parameters are
on a normalized scale and say nothing about physical size; a raw value is not comparable
between a thermocouple and a flow meter. We therefore summarize each (sensor, tier)
pair by how far a drawn perturbation typically lands from that sensor’s normal operating
mean: E |𝑋 − mean|, the mean absolute displacement, obtained by drawing 10,000 values
from the tier’s value distribution and averaging their distance from the mean. Measuring
distance from normal rather than the value itself is what makes the number comparable
across sensors carrying different units and ranges. The draws use a fixed seed, so the figure
is a reproducible property of the (sensor, tier) pair rather than one that shifts slightly on
every run. This is the quantity the tier-growth figures later in this section are computed
from: a sensor’s growth factor is its tier-4 magnitude divided by its tier-1 magnitude.
 Applied literally, the shape rules break down on certain sensors: four cases produce
a distribution that cannot be drawn from at all, and one distorts what a tier is meant to
represent. A guard handles each:

 ∙ A sensor that normally sits hard against the top or bottom of its physical range. Its average
 maps to exactly 0 or 1 on the normalized scale, where the fit is undefined. The
 mapped average is nudged just inside, to [0.001, 0.999].

 ∙ A sensor that never moves. Its measured spread is zero, and the fit divides by that

 ∙ A sensor near an edge of its range, at tier 2. Tier 2 widens the spread, and for such a

 ∙ Tiers 3 and 4, which deliberately push values toward the edges of the range. Pushed too far,

 ∙ A sensor whose recorded range has no width. There is nothing to draw from, so it falls

2.4.4 One signature is designed but not evaluated. Delayed propagation († in Tables 3
and 5) is part of the designed catalog and is excluded from every result in this report. It is
generated by time-shifting a chain of sensors so downstream readings lag the upstream
ones. The chain head at this facility reports a constant value throughout the collection

the same constant, so at every tier the perturbed data comes out identical to the clean
data it was built from. There is nothing in the result for a detector to find, and an AUC
scored against it would measure how detectors respond to unperturbed data. The generator
catches this itself — it verifies that the channel it targeted actually changed, and that check
fails here rather than yielding a corpus that would look valid downstream.

alone: the mechanism is sound and would perturb a facility whose cascade head varies.

2.5 Anomaly Detection Framework
We evaluate eleven unsupervised anomaly detectors from four paradigm families. Each
detector is trained exclusively on unperturbed telemetry and evaluated without labeled
perturbation examples. “Unperturbed” is the only filter applied to the training read; it is
not restricted to steady-state operation (Section 2.5).

  Sensor budgets. Seven detectors score the full feature matrix. The four structure-learning
detectors do not: their cost grows steeply in the number of variables, so each caps the
channels it scores at a fixed budget: DYNOTEARS and NTS-NOTEARS at 30, the Bayesian
network at 25, PCMCI+ at 20. This is a compute decision with an evaluation consequence:
a perturbation confined to channels outside the budget cannot be detected by construction,
however good the detector is. What that costs on this signature catalog is measured in
Section 2.6.6.

  Equipment-stratified sensor selection. Which channels fill a budget matters as much as its
size, and the obvious rule is the wrong one. Ranking candidates by variance and taking
the top 𝑁 does not omit sensors at random; it omits whole signal classes. Digital-input
position bits and read–write command channels barely move in steady state, so they

34 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

rank last regardless of operational importance. Ranking the fit-set channels by standard
deviation and taking the top 𝑁 , no channel of a command type (Manual Output or
Damper Command) appears in the top 20 or the top 25; the first four enter only by the
top 30. At the budgets this study uses, then, a purely variance-ranked budget of 20 or 25
contains no command channel at all, and one of 30 contains four. Where none is selected,
any perturbation delivered through a command path is invisible by construction.
  The selection used here is therefore equipment-stratified. Candidate sensors are partitioned
into strata by equipment class, and the budget is filled round-robin across strata, taking the
highest-variance channel not yet chosen from each stratum in turn. Allocation is equal per
stratum rather than proportional to stratum size, because proportional allocation starves
exactly the strata this exists to protect: the singleton classes, such as a damper command
channel or an individual setpoint, that would otherwise be outnumbered by the large
thermocouple stratum. Within a stratum the ordering is still by variance, so the rule keeps
the informative-channel preference that motivates variance ranking and discards only its
blindness to device class. Stratum order is sorted, so selection is deterministic across runs.
  The rule is pre-registered on device-function grounds, namely that every device class
should be instrumented, and is statable without reference to any attack. That property is
deliberate: a selection rule tuned on the perturbations it is later evaluated against would
leak the evaluation’s answer into its own design. What stratification actually recovers on
this catalog, and what it does not, is reported in Section 2.6.6.
  Almost every detector drops the sine/cosine time encodings; change point detection is
the only one that scores them, and the exclusion reaches the rest by two different routes.
Seven detectors — the autoencoder, LSTM, dependency violation, the Bayesian network,
𝑇 2 , Isolation Forest and PCA — set an explicit exclusion flag; the six of those without a
sensor budget score 146 columns as a result. CPD sets no such flag and has no budget, so it
scores 160: the fourteen extra columns are the encodings. The three remaining structure
learners never see them either, but for a different reason: their sensor budgets are filled
from the process channels before scoring, so the encodings are not candidates in the first
place. The Bayesian network is subject to both mechanisms at once.
  The reason for excluding them is the same in either case. A clock encoding is a deter-
ministic function of the timestamp, and within a sine/cosine pair the two are perfectly
functionally dependent, violating the faithfulness assumption constraint-based discovery
rests on; nothing in the plant can cause the hour of day, so any edge incident on one is
uninterpretable as a causal claim while still counting toward the graph distance these
detectors score. Where the temporal features are retained they are always included and are
not charged against any sensor budget.

  Top-𝐾 score aggregation. The reconstruction and prediction detectors (autoencoder, PCA,
LSTM) produce a per-sensor error vector and must reduce it to one score per sample. They
do so by averaging the 𝐾 largest components, with 𝐾 = 30, roughly 20% of the 146 feature
columns.
  𝐾 is a swept parameter, not a default, because both extremes fail, in opposite ways.
Taking the mean over all columns dilutes a perturbation confined to a handful of sensors
below the noise of the rest. Taking a very small 𝐾 fails less obviously: a few background
channels have reconstruction error that rises under any perturbation context, so they
occupy the top slots regardless of which signature is present, and the score stops carrying
per-signature information while still looking like a working detector. 𝐾 was therefore
selected by sweep against tier discrimination rather than against headline AUC; the sweep
and its outcome are in Appendix A.
  This is the aggregation the per-sensor scale floor above exists to protect: a single channel
with inflated scaled variance would otherwise occupy the top-𝐾 slots by construction.

2.5.1 Family 1: Classical Statistical Methods.

 Change Point Detection (CPD) Scores each timepoint from the magnitude of change
 in each sensor rather than from its level [4, 49]. Per sensor the score combines
 absolute first differences (0.5), absolute second differences (0.3) and a short rolling
 variance (0.2), then takes the elementwise maximum against a slope-CUSUM term
 that accumulates the first-difference series so that a slow ramp, which produces near-
 zero individual differences, still registers. Sensor scores are combined by maximum
 across channels, not by an average or a top-𝐾 mean, so a perturbation confined to
 one channel is not diluted by the others. Scoring assumption: perturbations produce
 a detectable change in one or more sensor channels; CPD keys on that a change
 occurred, not on its direction or level.
 Hotelling T2 (T2 ) Mahalanobis distance of the full sensor vector from the baseline
 mean [5, 34]. Mahalanobis distance measures how far a reading is from normal
 while accounting for how the channels usually vary together: a distance of one
 unit means one typical spread’s worth in whatever direction is being measured,
 so a small move on a normally steady channel counts as far as a large move on a
 noisy one, and a combination of readings that individually look ordinary counts as
 distant if those channels do not normally take those values at the same time. Scoring
 assumption: perturbations lie outside the multivariate Gaussian envelope of normal
 operation.

36 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

 Isolation Forest (IF) An ensemble of trees, each built by repeatedly picking a channel
 at random and splitting its range at a random point [8, 41]. A reading is isolated once
 these random splits have separated it from every other reading, and the score is
 how many splits that took, averaged over the trees. The premise is that an unusual
 reading sits away from the crowd and so gets cut off after a few splits, whereas an
 ordinary one sits among many neighbours and takes many splits to separate. Fewer
 splits therefore means more anomalous. Scoring assumption: perturbations occupy
 sparse regions of the feature space.
 PCA Reconstruction (PCA) Projects the sensor vector onto the leading principal com-
 ponents and computes per-sensor reconstruction error [18, 25]; the per-sample score
 uses the same top-𝑘 (𝑘 = 30) aggregation as the autoencoder (the mean of the 𝑘
 largest per-sensor errors), so AE and PCA differ only in the reconstruction model.
 Scoring assumption: perturbations have higher projection error onto a low-rank
 normal subspace.

2.5.2 Family 2: Neural Reconstruction and Prediction.

 Autoencoder (AE) Encoder-decoder architecture; anomaly score is the per-window
 mean squared reconstruction error [32, 40, 44]. Scoring assumption: perturbations
 produce high reconstruction error on a manifold learned from normal operation.
 Top-𝑘 adaptive aggregation (𝑘 = 30) selects the highest-error sensors per window
 before scoring.
 LSTM Anomaly Detection (LSTM) Sequence model trained to predict the next win-
 dow from the prior context; anomaly score is the prediction error [12, 37]. Scoring
 assumption: perturbations produce unexpected sequence transitions not captured
 by the learned temporal dynamics. Top-𝑘 adaptive aggregation (𝑘 = 30) applied.

2.5.3 Family 3: Sensor Dependency Modeling.

 Dependency Violation (DV) Learns pairwise sensor dependency constraints from
 baseline; scores each test window by the count of violated constraints after thresh-
 olding with an adaptive per-channel violation floor [1, 26]. Scoring assumption:
 perturbations disrupt learned sensor-pair relationships.
 Bayesian Network (BN) Fits a linear-Gaussian Bayesian network to the baseline
 multivariate distribution; scores by negative log-likelihood under the learned
 graph [19, 20, 24]. We adopt the linear-Gaussian variant rather than a discretized

Anomaly Detector Robustness 37

 network to avoid binning artifacts in continuous sensor channels. Scoring assump-
 tion: perturbations produce low-likelihood configurations under the joint baseline
 distribution.

2.5.4 Family 4: Causal Structure Learning.

 DYNOTEARS (DYN) Linear structural equation model with temporal lags built on
 the continuous-optimization NOTEARS formulation [33, 47]; score is the structural
 Hamming distance (SHD) between the graph estimated from the test window and
 the baseline graph. Scoring assumption: perturbations alter the learned causal graph
 structure.
 NTS-NOTEARS (NTS) Nonlinear extension of DYNOTEARS using neural network
 functional forms; score is the deviation of the estimated graph from the baseline
 graph, on the same footing as DYNOTEARS. It is evaluated and reported on the
 same protocol as the other ten detectors. The augmented Lagrangian formulation it
 optimises is known to converge unreliably at this data scale [9, 29]; whether that
 occurs here is a question for the across-seed measurement in Section 2.7.1, not an
 assumption of the method.
 PCMCI+ (PCMCI+) Constraint-based causal discovery applying iterative conditional
 independence tests over windowed telemetry [17, 46]; standard PCMCI assumes
 causal sufficiency, with latent-confounder handling provided by its LPCMCI exten-
 sion. Score is the deviation from the baseline causal structure.

  These three work differently from the rest of the panel, and the difference explains a
limitation that recurs throughout the results. Each learns a graph over the sensors: the
sensors are the nodes, and an edge means one sensor’s recent past helps predict another’s
present. A baseline graph is learned from clean windows, a second graph is learned from
the window being scored, and the score is a measure of how far the two graphs differ. For
DYNOTEARS and NTS-NOTEARS that measure is structural Hamming distance, a count
of the edges present in one graph and absent from the other.
  Counting edges is what makes these scores coarse. The distance is an integer, and over the
sensor budgets these detectors run at the learned graphs are sparse, so the count has only
a few dozen values available to it. Every window whose graph differs from the baseline
by the same number of edges receives an identical score, no matter how different those
windows are otherwise. A detector scoring a continuous residual has thousands of distinct
values to separate windows with; these have a median of twenty-four for NTS-NOTEARS,
twenty-two for DYNOTEARS and eleven for PCMCI+.

38 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

 That matters because the metric used throughout this report, AUC, is a ranking: it asks
how often a perturbed window scores above a clean one. Windows sharing a score cannot
be ranked against each other, so when scores land on a coarse lattice most comparisons are
ties and the metric cannot resolve differences smaller than one step of the lattice. This is
the mechanism behind the coarse lattice noted for these detectors in the results, behind
PCMCI+ having no cell this study will report as resolved, and behind the degenerate case
in Section 2.8, where the partition leaves so few windows that all three assign a single score
to every one of them and no ranking exists at all.
 All three report a graph distance, which is on no fixed scale and is not comparable across
signatures as a raw number, so each is normalised against the distribution of distances
over the detector’s own baseline windows and scored as |𝑧| = |(𝑥 − 𝑐)/𝑠|. The centre 𝑐
and scale 𝑠 are robust estimators: the median and 1.4826× the median absolute deviation,
falling back to the interquartile range where the MAD is zero, and halting instead of
scoring if the baseline has no spread by either measure. Robust estimators are required, not
merely preferred, here. These baselines are short (the DYNOTEARS sensor-drift baseline,
for instance, is eleven windows), so a single atypical window moves a mean far enough to
place the centre outside the range any clean window can produce, and the folded |𝑧| then
scores an attack whose graph distance rises through that centre as increasingly normal. The
median is unmoved by one window in eleven.

2.5.5 Training and Evaluation Protocol. All detectors are trained on baseline data —
telemetry with no perturbation applied — and see no perturbation examples. “Baseline”
here means unperturbed, not steady-state: the regime tagging of Section 2.3.3 does not filter
the training read. The February 24 to June 17, 2025 window named in the configuration
is narrower than the collection span, excluding a low-speed anomaly in mid-May and
two later intervals in which the pump regime changed and two cold-trap thermocouples
stopped reporting.
 That window governs perturbation-episode selection, not the detector training read. Training
reads the unfiltered corpus, which spans February 18 to July 8, 2025; 10,365,528 of its
46,415,942 rows — 22.3% — lie outside the narrower window and do reach the detectors.
The exclusions above therefore describe where perturbations were placed, not what the
models were fitted on, and no claim in this report should be read as asserting that training
saw only the curated interval. Evaluation follows a fixed protocol:
 (1) Draw the training sample across the whole period, in blocks. The training read
 draws contiguous blocks from up to thirty-two row groups spaced across the full
 training corpus rather than reading from the head of the corpus, so a detector is

Anomaly Detector Robustness 39

 not fitted to one month of operation; how many blocks the budget affords is what
 varies between detectors, not where they are taken from. Blocks are contiguous time
 spans, not randomly sampled rows, and each is floored at twice what the detector
 consumes in one unit: one hundred timestamps for LSTM and CPD, which build
 50-sample sequences and windows, eight and six for the lag-based causal detectors.
 The floor is not cosmetic: row-level shuffling splices data from months apart into
 a single sequence, and change point detection then detects the splices. Detectors
 that treat rows as independent take no floor and spend the same budget on wider
 spread instead.
  (2) Fit the detector on that sample, with no perturbation examples.
  (3) Score each (signature, tier, seed) cell. Load the preprocessed perturbation set for
 the cell and run the fitted detector. Detectors differ in scoring granularity: most emit
 one score per sliding window, which is then broadcast across the rows that window
 covers, while dependency violation emits one score per timepoint. This distinction
 is not cosmetic: it sets how much independent evidence a cell’s AUC actually rests
 on, and is the basis of the resolution gate in Section 2.6.6.
  (4) Build the negative class per cell, episode-matched. The negative class is not the
 pooled baseline corpus. For each cell it is the clean twin of that cell’s own perturba-
 tion episode: the same time span and operating conditions with no perturbation
 applied, so that the comparison isolates the perturbation and not any difference
 between the episode and the corpus mean. Pooling the baseline instead makes an
 ordinary drift between one episode and the corpus read as detection.
  (5) Label the positive class from the raw values. A row is positive only where a
 perturbed sensor is actually active at that timestamp, derived before cleaning and
 scaling; rows in a perturbed-signature file outside the active interval are negatives,
 not positives. Rows no scoring window covers are marked with an explicit sentinel
 and excluded from the ROC and from threshold-derived metrics rather than being
 silently treated as normal.
  (6) Compute AUC over that labeled set.
  (7) Repeat for 5 seeds (42, 1, 2, 3, 4) for the six detectors whose scoring path contains a
 random step, and report the 5-seed mean and standard deviation. The other five are
 run once: replicating a detector that cannot vary measures nothing, and reporting
 the resulting zero alongside measured spreads would read as the most stable result
 in the panel. Five seeds are enough to detect seed-driven instability but not to bound
 it; this is why NTS-NOTEARS’ severity response is withheld rather than assigned a

40 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

 corrected value. Seeds do not probe every source of run-to-run variation — see the
 caveat on batch execution in Section 2.7.1.

 Two gates run over the completed matrix before any cell is read as a result. The null
control is pre-registered: for every cell in which a detector provably scores none of the
perturbed channels, the AUC must fall within two standard errors of 0.5, since a detector
that cannot observe a perturbation cannot detect it. The resolution gate converts each cell’s
count of distinct score values into a sampling error and withholds any cell that does not
sit at least two standard errors from chance. Both are reported in Section 2.6.6; the second
in particular is what keeps low-resolution cells that happen to land below 0.5 from being
read as inversion findings.
 The evaluation uses the bounded preprocessing mode (Section 2.3.3). All 11 detectors are
evaluated in both bounded and unbounded modes; primary results use bounded mode
(hypothesized to be the more difficult case). Figure 2 illustrates the full evaluation pipeline.

 A natural explanation for the low ceiling is that these perturbations leave each channel’s
marginal distribution intact and move only the relationships between channels, so a
detector scoring departures from a learned marginal has little to work with. We measured
it, and it accounts for one of the three. Replay-based FDI shifts its perturbed channel
by 0.019 clean standard deviations, which is marginal preservation in the strict sense
and follows from its construction: it replays recorded normal segments. The two valve

signatures do not behave that way. Sequential valve manipulation moves its perturbed
channels by between a fraction of a clean standard deviation and several multiples of
one. Phantom valve operation pins channels that are almost perfectly constant in clean
operation to a new fixed value, which is a conspicuous change to a marginal rather than a

 The low ceiling on those two therefore remains unexplained.
 Within that group the field is uniform. On replay-based FDI no detector extracts anything:
the ceiling is DYNOTEARS’ 0.534 over PCMCI+’s 0.518, and the whole eleven-detector
field spans 0.435 to 0.534 — the top of that range is not distinguishable from chance, and
six of the eleven sit below it. On the other two a classical method leads by a small margin:
phantom valve operation is led by PCA at 0.882 with 𝑇 2 a little behind, and on sequential
valve manipulation 𝑇 2 leads CPD by 0.014 (Figure 3).
 Replay-based FDI is the only one of the three whose value distributions the measurement
above found intact, and it is also the only one on which nothing works at all. The two facts
fit together: a perturbation that preserves the value distributions is hidden from detectors
that examine values, and this panel offers no method that succeeds where they fail. The
account is therefore negative only: it explains why value-based detectors fail without
identifying anything that works. In particular the change-point detector, which scores a
departure over time rather than an unusual value and might be expected to survive, does
not — it reads 0.484, ninth of eleven.
 If the account holds, it carries a design consequence that no detector in this panel or any
extension of it addresses. A replay substitutes values the plant genuinely produced, so
there is no anomaly in the values to find — the manipulation is visible in the provenance
of a reading rather than in its magnitude or its relationship to other readings. Process
monitoring of the kind evaluated here scores magnitudes and relationships, so a better
detector of that kind is not the remedy. The countermeasures that do address it act on
the transport rather than the signal: sequence numbering, freshness or nonce fields, and
authentication of the sensor-to-controller path, so that a recorded segment replayed later
fails a check that never examines its values at all. Those are specification decisions taken at
design time, before a plant exists, and they sit outside the scope of this evaluation — which
is the point. A facility that specifies them does not need this signature detected; one that
does not will not detect it.
 The observation fits that account but does not establish it. To show that the perturbation
rather than the detectors sets the ceiling, we would have to test a method chosen because it
should catch what replay cannot hide — the repetition of an earlier segment — and find
that it fell short too. We did not run one. For the two valve signatures even this much

2.6.2 Classical Statistical Methods. The classical family spans the widest performance
range of any group in the study, so its mean is not a usable summary: change point
detection, Hotelling’s 𝑇 2 , PCA reconstruction and Isolation Forest occupy the top, middle
and bottom of the tier-4 ordering between them (Table 7). Two of the four are the strongest
detectors in the study, one sits mid-field, and the fourth is in the bottom third.
  CPD is the mid-field one, and its mean describes it worse than any other detector’s
describes it. Its profile is sharply bimodal: five of its eleven signatures reach 0.99 or better
at tier 4 and four sit at or below chance, with only two in between (Figure 3). Its tier-4
mean is an average of two populations and characterises neither. None of the five strong

Anomaly Detector Robustness 47

cells is already at the ceiling at tier 1 — they average close to chance there — so unlike
the other classical methods CPD builds its detection across the ladder rather than arriving
with it, and it carries the second-steepest severity response in the panel (Table 7). The four
weak cells are a distinct phenomenon rather than a softer version of the strong ones, and
the deepest of them, physics violation at 0.437, is accounted for in Section 2.6.6: a detector
scoring change against a perturbation that suppresses change is anti-correlated with it by
construction.
 𝑇 2 and PCA score different quantities, displacement of the mean vector versus residual
off the fitted principal subspace, and their outcome profiles remain close to each other and
close to binary, and both are at chance on replay-based FDI and nowhere else. They differ
in how much sits at the top: 𝑇 2 is at or within a hundredth of a perfect score on seven of
the eleven signatures, leaving the two valve signatures and flow oscillation in between,
while PCA reaches that band on six and leaves four — the two valve signatures, flow
oscillation and oscillatory instability — below it. PCA is also the one of the pair that gives
ground on sequential valve manipulation (Figure 3, Table 8). Where the transition happens
it takes one tier step, not four: both jump most of their range between tiers 1 and 2 on
physics-consistent manipulation (Figure 5). This is the envelope-crossing pattern described
in Section 2.6.7.
 Isolation Forest is the weakest of the family and its summary statistics point in opposite
directions. It has one of the better-fitting linear tier responses in the study and the worst
discrimination in the family. These are consistent: 𝑅2 measures how closely the response
follows a line, not how far the line rises. IF’s line is both well-fitted and shallow, leaving it
below every classical peer at tier 4 (Table 7). At tier 4, its severest condition, only one of
its eleven signatures exceeds 0.70: physics violation, at 0.776. Precision trust decay is the
nearest miss at 0.687 and does not clear it (Table 8).
 Failure modes:

 ∙ Marginal-preserving perturbations defeat the family almost uniformly. Replay-

 behind, while on sequential valve manipulation 𝑇 2 holds most of its range and PCA
 gives up much of it. IF is the only one of the three near chance on both (Figure 3).
 CPD escapes flow oscillation fully at 0.999, escapes sequential valve manipulation
 partly at 0.845, and does not escape replay-based FDI at all, reading 0.484.

2.6.3 Neural Reconstruction and Prediction. The autoencoder leads the LSTM in aggre-
gate by a modest margin, and the two differ more in the shape of their severity response
than in its endpoint. The autoencoder has the highest 𝑅2 in the study and the least front-
loaded response of any detector with a reportable one (Table 7). Those two are the closest
thing in the suite to detectors that grade severity rather than merely reporting whether the
envelope was crossed. LSTM sits with the majority, and the two neural detectors start from
almost the same intercept.
 Their per-signature profiles agree at the extremes and separate in between. Both are
strong on operator mimicry, physics violation and coordinated thermocouple manipulation,
and both sit at or below chance on replay-based FDI (Figure 3). Elsewhere the autoencoder
is clearly ahead: on physics-consistent manipulation, sensor drift with dropouts and
precision trust decay it reads 0.975, 0.928 and 0.825 against the LSTM’s 0.577, 0.629 and
0.592. Running both still adds little over running one, but for a different reason than parity:
at the 0.70 threshold the autoencoder is at risk on four of eleven signatures and the LSTM
on eight, and the autoencoder’s four are a subset of the LSTM’s eight (flow oscillation,
phantom valve operation, replay-based FDI, sequential valve manipulation). The LSTM
contributes no signature the autoencoder does not already cover.
 One cell behaves differently. The autoencoder returns approximately chance at all four
tiers on replay-based FDI, reading 0.4999 at tier 4. A reading at chance is the expected
result when a detector’s scored input does not change: it is the same signal the null control
uses to certify a well-behaved detector, so this is a correct null rather than a defect. LSTM’s

counterpart is 0.435, below chance rather than at it. That departure is not resolvable: against
the effective resolution of that cell (Section 2.6.6) it sits 1.62 standard errors from chance,
inside the two-SE bar this report requires before reading a cell as a measurement. We
therefore report it as at chance.
  Failure modes:
 ∙ Both are residual detectors and share one coverage gap. Reconstruction error and
 next-step prediction error are both residuals against a learned model of normal
 operation. A perturbation that keeps every channel inside its learned range and
 preserves short-horizon predictability would generate no residual to score. That is
 consistent with the observed failures on replay-based FDI, flow oscillation, sequen-
 tial valve manipulation and phantom valve operation, and the neural pair fails all
 four.
 ∙ The pair is redundant, not complementary. The identical at-risk set above holds at
 the 0.70 threshold; any argument for deploying both must rest on something other
 than signature coverage.
 ∙ LSTM is anti-correlated on one signature. Its replay-based FDI trajectory has a
 negative slope overall, though not a monotone decline (Figure 5). It is not large
 enough to be useful inverted, and it is not tracked as a defect; it is reported as
 measured.
 ∙ LSTM’s precision-trust response is a late step. A shallow ramp across the first three
 tiers, then a jump of more than 0.2 into tier 4 (Figure 5). Any operating point set
 from tiers 1–3 would badly misestimate tier 4 behaviour here, which is the general
 hazard of reading a step response as a trend.

2.6.4 Sensor Dependency Modeling. The two members of this family share a nominal
paradigm — scoring a sample by how far it departs from learned inter-sensor structure —
and share nothing in their results. The Bayesian network is among the stronger detectors
in the study; dependency violation sits at chance (Table 7). The gap between them, roughly
a quarter of an AUC point, is the widest between any two detectors nominally doing the
same thing.
  Dependency violation is a null result, and unusually well-measured as one. Its mean
AUC stays within a few hundredths of chance at all four tiers. One cell is a real exception
— operator mimicry detects at tier 1 and tier 2 — but it decays through tier 3 and reaches
exactly chance at tier 4, so it is a detection that disappears precisely as the perturbation
becomes severe, which is not a usable operating regime. No other cell clears 0.70 at any tier;
the nearest is physics violation at tier 4, which reaches 0.636 (Figure 3). What distinguishes

2.6.5 Causal Structure Learning. The causal family carries the highest per-cell compute
cost in the study, and its three members land in three different places (Table 7). Only
DYNOTEARS supports a substantive severity claim. NTS-NOTEARS’ per-signature results
are reported, but its severity response is withheld because seed noise exceeds it, and
PCMCI+ is withheld entirely on a stronger ground developed below: not one of its 220
cells is statistically distinguishable from chance.
 DYNOTEARS is the strongest causal detector and is not a weak one in absolute terms.
It outperforms Isolation Forest and the LSTM, and falls just short of the autoencoder
(Table 7). Six signatures reach 0.90 or above at tier 4: sensor drift with dropouts, precision
trust decay, physics violation, oscillatory instability, physics-consistent manipulation and
operator mimicry (Figure 3). It does not lead on any signature, and on the three low-ceiling
signatures it is no better than the rest of the field.
 Its severity response is real and front-loaded, second only to the Bayesian network in
how much of the rise lands in the first tier step (Table 7). DYNOTEARS registers that the
perturbation has left the historical envelope more readily than it registers how far beyond
it the perturbation goes.
 Its least orderly cell is coordinated thermocouple manipulation: it gains sharply in the
first tier step, gives back roughly half of that in the second, and does not recover (Figure 5).
No DYNOTEARS cell sits materially below chance at tier 4; the lowest is phantom valve
operation at 0.508, with replay-based FDI just above it.
 NTS-NOTEARS has no reportable severity response: its total tier-1 to tier-4 rise is smaller
than its own across-seed standard deviation, the largest in the study (Tables 7 and 10). Its
worst cell, replay-based FDI, has a spread more than ten times its entire distance from
chance. Only physics violation and operator mimicry clear 0.70 at tier 4.

and its best cell anywhere in the matrix is physics violation at tier 4, 0.731 (Figure 3). But

2.6.6 A Taxonomy of Failure Modes. An AUC near 0.5 is reported the same way whatever
produced it, and the conditions that produce it are not interchangeable. A detector that
never sees the perturbed channels, one that sees them and cannot separate the classes,
one whose answer changes from run to run, and one whose score is consistently inverted
all read as “failed” in a results table and imply entirely different responses. The design
decision that follows from each is different.
  Instrumentation. For every column a detector actually scores, we recorded how far that
column’s average moved between tier 1 and tier 4. The measurement is taken at scoring
time rather than reconstructed afterwards, so it describes the matrix the detector saw. A
column counts as having moved if its average shifted by more than 0.01, in units where
one standard deviation is 1.
  That threshold is not a tuned choice, because the distribution it cuts has two distinct
parts rather than one continuous pile. Most scored columns do not move at all: 74% shift
by exactly zero, and 87% fall below the threshold. The columns that do move are spread
across four orders of magnitude, with a median of 0.011 and a maximum of 45.3. The
cut therefore separates a large did-not-move population from a broad tail, and moving it
within a wide range would not change which columns land on either side.
  The instrumentation runs for all eleven detectors across all eleven signatures. Table 6
shows four of them, one from each paradigm family, which is what the argument below
turns on.

2.7 Statistical Analysis
2.7.1 Across-Seed Stability. Cells were run at five seeds ({42, 1, 2, 3, 4}) for the six detec-
tors whose scoring path contains a random step, and at one seed for the five where it does
not. Five replicates screen for gross instability; they do not characterise a distribution, and
nothing below should be read as demonstrating that a detector is stable. The asymmetry is
worth keeping in view: a large across-seed spread is strong evidence of instability, while
a small one at 𝑛 = 5 is weak evidence of its absence. The five single-seed detectors are
reported as not applicable rather than as zero: replicating a detector that cannot vary mea-
sures nothing, and printing the resulting zero in the same column as a measured spread
invites reading it as the most stable result in the table. Table 10 reports, for each replicated
detector, the mean across its eleven signatures of the across-seed standard deviation of
tier-4 AUC (sample SD, 𝑛 = 5, bounded mode). The aggregate figure is not reported,
because it is meaningless: the per-detector values span more than an order of magnitude,
and averaging them would let the stable detectors mask the one that is genuinely unstable.

 A near-null result here is less surprising than it first appears. The severity ladder is
built on physical admissibility, so it is reasonable to expect that enforcing admissibility
would change what a detector sees. Three things limit how much it can.
 The first is how little of the data the clamp reaches. At tier 4 it moves 12.5% of the rows
actually carrying a perturbation, but those rows are a small share of a cell, so the figure

against every row a detector scores is 0.80%. A detector reads windows, and almost every
row in a window is identical between the two arms.
 The second is that most of the ladder can barely show an effect. Tiers 1 and 2 are
constructed inside the envelope, so for most signatures there is nothing to clamp: eight
of the eleven clamp nothing at tier 1 and seven nothing at tier 2. Even at tier 4, four of
the eleven still clamp nothing. Two signatures are exceptions to the inside-the-envelope

magnitudes from a hard-coded per-tier table rather than from the sensor’s envelope —
they are two of the four signatures that never consult envelope magnitudes at all — so the
admissible band is not what their ladder is built against and there is no reason for their
clamping to respect it.
 The third is what clamping does to a value. It moves it to the nearest admissible value,
which is the edge of the sensor’s support. A perturbation driven past the physics boundary
and then clamped still arrives at the far edge of what the plant can produce. A detector
asking how far a reading sits from normal sees an extreme value either way; the clamp
changes how far past the boundary the excursion goes, not whether the reading is anoma-
lous. Since these detectors gain little from severity beyond the first step (Section 2.6.7), that
is precisely the distinction they are worst placed to resolve.
 The exception fits the same account. Dependency violation scores departures from
learned relationships between sensors rather than distance from normal on any one of
them. Pinning several channels to their own rails changes how those channels move
together in a way that scaling them all past the boundary does not. The one detector that
responds to the clamp is the one measuring the thing clamping alters.
 This comparison does not settle whether physical plausibility buys an attacker stealth.
What the mode comparison shows is narrow: clamping out-of-range values back inside
the envelope changes nothing measurable for ten of eleven detectors, and for the eleventh
makes the clamped version easier to detect. Clamping is not an adversary strategy — it
pins values to a rail, an artefact of the generator — so this bears on the generator, not on a
capable attacker.

and replay-based FDI. On operator mimicry the arms hold 3,199,339 and 4,434,001 rows.

  Bounded is reported as the primary mode throughout, on design grounds and not
statistical ones. An unbounded perturbation may drive a sensor outside every value
the facility has ever recorded, which makes it detectable by a range check that needs no
model at all, and which an adversary with any knowledge of the plant would avoid for
exactly that reason. Bounded mode is the case in which per-channel support constraints
are enforced, and therefore the conservative one. That choice was fixed before any of these
numbers existed.

2.9 Discussion
2.9.1 Detection Gap and Probability of Missing. The central evaluation question is: for
each perturbation type and severity tier, what is the probability that a deployed detector
misses the perturbation?
 That question has an operating point in it, so AUC cannot answer it. AUC is the prob-
ability that a randomly chosen perturbed window outranks a randomly chosen normal
window; it is a summary of the whole ranking and is not a miss rate at any threshold. The
difference is not a rounding margin on this matrix: the Bayesian network scores physics
violation at tier 1 at AUC 0.982, so 1 − AUC would put the miss probability at 0.018, while
its measured false-negative rate at a 1% false-positive budget is 0.983: a factor of fifty-five.
 The miss probability is therefore reported as the false-negative rate FNR = 1 − TPR at a
stated false-positive budget, read from the seed-averaged ROC curve. Table 14 sweeps that
budget across the five levels fixed in the evaluation protocol rather than reporting a single
one, for the reason given below.

nothing discriminates is an artefact of taking a minimum over eleven noisy values, not a

by construction. It selects up to 100 sensor pairs ranked by baseline correlation strength,
i.e. the couplings most stable in normal operation. On this facility those are dominated

temperature. A physics-admissible perturbation is, almost by definition, one that respects
that common-mode structure, since that is what the admissibility envelope enforces, so
the relationships DV watches are precisely the ones a constrained adversary least disturbs.
High column coverage and low sensitivity are then consistent: DV observes all those
columns through roughly 100 relationships selected for stability, not for responsiveness.

and at most three parents each, a sparse graph in which an individual broken dependency is
a large share of the score. Two measurements would test this: what fraction of DV’s selected
pairs involve a perturbed sensor at all, and whether re-selecting pairs by responsiveness

2.9.3 Transferability: the Protocol and What It Requires. The experimental design con-
stitutes a transferable robustness assessment protocol for ICS environments with AI/ML
monitoring. The protocol consists of five steps:
 (1) Derive admissibility envelopes from facility historical telemetry and validated
 process constraints. Per-sensor percentile distributions, rate-of-change bounds, and
 physics-limit boundaries define what a physically constrained adversary can pro-
 duce.
 (2) Generate physics-bounded perturbation scenarios across the severity tier ladder.
 For the seven signatures whose magnitudes are envelope-derived, tiers 1–3 are
 constructed inside the derived admissibility envelopes; tier 4 extends the support
 beyond them by design, as the physics-violating positive control. Each scenario
 maps to one or more STPA-SafeSec threat codes.
 (3) Evaluate each detection paradigm against each scenario to produce a detection
 capability matrix. For each paradigm family: report the first severity tier at which
 AUC ≥ threshold, the breadth of detectors individually achieving threshold, and
 whether any coverage gap exists.
 (4) Identify which scoring assumption fails for each coverage gap. The paradigm
 taxonomy (Section 2.5) provides the vocabulary: mean shift (Classical), manifold
 reconstruction (Neural), dependency graph (Dependency), causal structure (Causal).
 A coverage gap that persists across all four families is evidence that no scoring as-
 sumption in the panel fits, which is a stronger and more useful finding than a single
 detector’s miss. It is not by itself a proof of unobservability: shared preprocessing,
 sensor budgets, score resolution and the choice of paradigms are common to the
 whole panel, and any of them can produce a panel-wide gap.
 (5) Quantify residual detection gap per perturbation class. The output is a structured
 capability report: which perturbation classes the monitoring catches, which it misses,
 and at what severity threshold — with a mechanism where the diagnostics establish
 one, and the gap recorded as open where they do not. This study leaves several
 open, and Section 2.9.5 lists them.
  The STPA-SafeSec threat taxonomy provides the audit lens: the catalog reaches 7 of 12
CSTR threat codes, which bounds which codes the protocol speaks to. It does not establish
that the signatures exhaust the ways any one of those codes could be realised. Establishing
that would need the facility-specific unsafe-control-action decomposition this study did not

Anomaly Detector Robustness 85

perform (Section 2.9.5). The two coverage gaps (CSTR-I-2 command drop and CSTR-A-1..4
availability threats) bound where this evaluation’s conclusions do and do not apply.
  What transfers is that procedure, not the detectors it was used to evaluate. Detectors
trained on one facility rarely transfer directly to another because process dynamics, sensor
configurations, and operating practices differ [21, 45]; what transfers here is the methodol-
ogy, not the fitted models. The methodology is applicable to any ICS with the following
prerequisites:
 (1) Historical normal-operation telemetry sufficient to fit per-sensor envelope statistics.
 Twenty-one qualifying operating days sufficed here; that figure is what this process
 required and was not established as a general minimum.
 (2) Validated process constraints defining physics limits (absolute sensor bounds, spa-
 tial uniformity limits, thermal time constants). Published engineering specifications
 or commissioning documents typically provide these.
 (3) Detector taxonomy classification: the evaluating engineer classifies their existing
 monitoring suite by scoring assumption using the paradigm taxonomy in Sec-
 tion 2.5.
  Given these prerequisites, the admissibility envelope computation, perturbation gen-
eration pipeline, and evaluation harness are not specific to this facility and require no
labeled perturbation data, no operational deployment of the perturbation scenarios, and
no facility-specific modifications to the detectors.
  Transfer beyond processes resembling this one is untested. The envelope construction
assumes a predominantly continuous process with a steady-state regime that per-sensor
percentile bounds can describe. A facility with discrete operating states, several interacting
regimes, frequent transients, campaign-driven configuration changes, or safety-system
constraints that dominate the admissible region would need the envelope formulation
revisited before the rest of the pipeline applies. The twenty-one-day figure quoted above is
what sufficed here and is not a general sufficiency threshold.

2.9.4 Data-Integrity Invariants: A Lessons-Learned. A substantial fraction of the engi-
neering effort in this study went not into the detectors but into fail-loud invariant checks that
guard the data pipeline. This proved essential: the most dangerous failures in an evaluation
like this are not crashes but silent-by-construction bugs, where no single stage looks wrong
yet the data is corrupted in a way that only surfaces downstream as a plausible-but-wrong
AUC. Because such a bug yields publication-quality numbers from the wrong data, we
treat the invariant layer as a first-class result and summarize the most instructive lessons
here.

86 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

 Validate twice: in memory and again on disk. Most preprocessing stages validate the
in-memory result, write it, then re-read the written file and validate again. The producer-
side gate catches computation bugs; the on-disk gate catches corrupt or partial writes the
in-memory check never sees. Several classes of schema drift (an unknown sensor-bus type,
a row that lost its value without losing the row) are only visible at the re-read boundary.
 You can gate the symptom even when you cannot find the cause. A change in the
perturbation-generation path once silently dropped roughly three-quarters of the sensors
from the written output, from ∼1,100 to ∼276 unique channels. A yield invariant, the
unique-sensor count in the written output must meet a runtime-computed floor, halts the
pipeline on the symptom regardless, preventing a downstream evaluation on a silently
truncated sensor set.
 Cross-cell checks catch what per-cell checks structurally cannot. A stale carry-over of
an internal start index once made all four severity tiers of a signature byte-identical no-ops
on every sample after the first. Every per-tier validator passed, because each tier’s output
was internally valid; only a cross-tier distinctness check, the four tier outputs for a fixed
window must hash to four distinct values, could detect the collapse. Any fan-out (tier ×
seed × signature) needs at least one cheap cross-cell invariant for exactly this reason.
 Structural validity is not substantive correctness. After unit-variance scaling, per-
sensor standard deviation was once found to span roughly fifteen orders of magnitude:
RobustScaler was applied correctly, but its IQR underestimate let a few outlier sensors
dominate, which would have pinned the top-𝑘 aggregation to those sensors’ noise and
decoupled AUC from perturbation severity. Every schema, dtype, and NaN check passed:
catching this required a statistical contract (per-sensor standard-deviation spread bounded,
and the top-10 sensors contributing under 80% of total variance). Detector-output guards
play the same role at the end of the pipeline: an AUC-band check (halt at exactly 0.0 for
inverted labels, warn in the [0.45, 0.55] near-chance band) caught a cluster of detectors
stuck at AUC ≈ 0.54, which a naive “= 0.5” test would have missed, before they consumed
hours of compute producing meaningless scores.
 An invariant that cries wolf gets ignored. The false-positive rate of a check is itself a
design constraint. An early over-sensitive gate emitted enough spurious warnings per run
to be duly ignored until it was retuned; every check is therefore tiered into halt, warn, and
silent modes. Lighter-weight practices, unit tests and a continuous-integration test that
asserts every specified invariant is actually implemented (no specced-but-missing guards),
support this layer but are secondary to the runtime invariants that gate the data itself.

3 PART II: DISTINGUISHING NATURAL DEGRADATION FROM SIGNAL
 PERTURBATION USING PHYSICS-BASED CONSISTENCY RELATIONS
3.1 Background
Signature-based and black-box data-driven detectors have two important limitations in
this application. First, a perturbation that remains within the normal data distribution
may evade pattern-based detection. Second, a purely data-driven anomaly score generally
provides limited information about the physical location or cause of an inconsistency, even
though such information is essential for selecting an appropriate operational response [39].
Physics-based models address these limitations by incorporating the conservation of mass,
momentum, and energy into the monitoring process [15, 38, 43]. These governing relation-
ships provide an intrinsic reference against which reported measurements can be evaluated.
If a set of measurements violates an evaluated conservation relation, the inconsistency
follows directly from the governing balance and cannot be eliminated without modifying
one or more of the participating signals. Moreover, because physical laws couple variables
across components and subsystems, a localized disturbance can generate a nonlocal pattern
of residuals when it disrupts observable consistency relations. Because each residual is
derived from an explicit equation, it identifies the affected physical balance rather than
producing only an opaque anomaly score.
 The model-based diagnostic platform employed in this work implements this principle
through three mechanisms [31].
 Virtual sensors. Systems rarely instrument every variable needed to close a physical
relation. Unmeasured variables are inferred analytically from neighboring measurements
and conservation laws — an unmeasured branch flow, for instance, back-calculated from a
nodal mass balance and the measured inflows and outflows. This extends diagnostic reach
without new hardware while staying anchored in physics. The validity of each virtual
sensor is continuously reassessed: if a residual implicates the equations used to construct
it, its estimate is flagged as unreliable rather than used.
 Analytical redundancy relations. Starting from component-level mass, momentum, and
energy balances, a library of algebraic and differential relations is assembled that must
hold under fault-free operation [30]. Each relation ties together a subset of measurements:
pressures, flows, temperatures, currents, spanning one or more components. Each is evalu-
ated online and its residual computed as the difference between the model estimate and the
measured or virtual-sensor value. A residual exceeding its uncertainty band signals that at
least one underlying assumption has failed, whether in signal integrity or in component
health.

94 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

 Explainability via attribution. Explainability in this framework is achieved through at-
tribution based on explicit consistency relations. Each residual corresponds to a specific
physical, actuation, or control relationship, and different perturbation entry points produce
different residual patterns. By identifying which relations are violated and which remain
consistent, the framework attributes the observed anomaly to its most likely source. The
explanation is therefore embedded directly in the detection process: the output indicates
not only that an inconsistency has occurred, but also which relationship failed and which
part of the system is associated with that failure.
 The framework’s diagnostic capability depends on the availability of physical and
control-logic relations that are both observable from the available measurements and
sufficiently distinct across competing hypotheses. If the relevant physical behavior is not
observable, multiple causes generate indistinguishable residual patterns, or a coordinated
manipulation preserves all evaluated consistency relations, the framework may detect an
inconsistency without uniquely identifying its cause or may not detect it at all. In such
cases, improved discrimination requires additional sensing, independent information, or
an expanded physical model rather than a more sophisticated classifier alone.

3.2 Physics-based Diagnostic Framework: Turning Physics Features into System
 Resilience
This physics-first framework can provide two advantages over the current perturbation
countermeasures, especially data-driven ones.
 The first advantage is the causal explainability. Instead of issuing a generic “anomaly”
flag, physics-based tool returns an attribution: which equations failed, which sensors are
inconsistent, and which physical principles were violated. Engineers can thus replicate
the diagnosis mentally or with a calculator, a critical requirement for high-stakes decision
making.
 The second advantage is fault–perturbation discrimination. Component faults and forged
signal perturbations often manifest with similar symptoms, e.g., mismatched sensor read-
ing. The framework can distinguish them by testing whether residual patterns conform to
known fault physics. If no physical fault explains the deviations, the likelihood of signal
manipulation rises sharply. Statistical hypothesis testing guards against false positives
stemming from noise or modelling uncertainty.
 Based on the aforementioned capabilities, in this study, the physics-based online diag-
nostic workflow unfolds as three-stage processes, as shown in Figure 8. Signals first pass
through a general data-driven screening layer. For safety- or operation-critical functions,
the screened signals are subsequently evaluated by the physics-based diagnostic layer. The

resulting physics-derived features support the regime classification described in the fol-
lowing section. The assigned regime then determines the appropriate operating procedure
and the verification step used to close the diagnostic and response loop.

 In the physical regime all readings satisfy the redundancy relations, evolve continu-
ously, and are consistent with control logic and commanded operational changes: normal
operation.
 The mixed regime covers behavior that obeys physics but violates performance expec-
tations, and divides by temporal character. Sudden changes are typically component,
instrument, or actuator faults; gradual changes indicate progressive degradation. In both
cases the change originates in a real physical event, so the affected variable drives consis-
tent and correlated changes across all physically related signals. That coordinated response
is the characteristic that separates a genuine fault or degradation from an introduced
perturbation, which generally leaves related signals inconsistent.
 The unphysical regime is characterized by violations of physical law, with or without
control-logic inconsistency, and divides by entry point: measurement path, control or
actuation path, or both simultaneously. In the measurement-path case the reported values
violate physical constraints while the controller still responds correctly to what it was
shown, producing a mismatch between physical behavior and control action. In the control-
path case the readings may remain physically plausible while the commanded action
contradicts them — a heater that stays energized after the reported temperature has passed
its set point. The combined case is the difficult one, since a perturbation applied consistently
to both paths can preserve apparent agreement; it is addressed by widening the set of
relations checked, on the premise that a perturbation source may know some inter-signal
relationships but is unlikely to know all of them and how they must co-evolve [23].

3.3 Method: Consistency Relations and the Entry-Point Argument
3.3.1 Information views and access control. Three views of each run are defined and
kept strictly separate. The ground-truth view holds the true physical state — true flow, true
executed shaft speed, true efficiency — and exists only in simulation; it is used for scoring
and for nothing else. The operator view holds what supervisory instrumentation can observe.
The perturbation-source view holds the injection applied to each compromised channel.
  The detector is confined to the operator view. This is enforced at runtime rather than by
convention: a guard raises if any ground-truth key reaches the detector. The point is not
stylistic. An earlier iteration computed a signature metric using the true executed speed,
which made the detector look far better than it was; the guard exists so that regression is
impossible rather than merely discouraged. The perturbation-source view is derived from
the injection hooks a scenario installs, rather than declared alongside them, so it cannot
drift out of agreement with the perturbation actually applied.

3.6 Discussion
The two case studies, evaluated across three configurations, define a common observability
axis for the proposed framework. Across the three configurations, operator-visible mea-
surements are compared with an expected system representation—learned model in the
transient cold-trap case and physics-based in the steady-state cold-trap and controlled
pump cases. The main transferable result is that attribution capability depends primarily
on the number and independence of available consistency relations rather than on detector
complexity.
 In the cold-trap case, a learned state-transition model is used to represent transient
behavior that is difficult to capture with compact analytical equations. The model can detect
departures from expected state evolution, but the quality of detection depends on model
accuracy and the representativeness of the training data. This case illustrates how learned
models can extend consistency-based monitoring to transient regimes. For steady state,
two explicit physics-based relations are employed: a heat-transfer relation, represented by
𝑟𝑈 𝐴 , and an energy-conservation relation, represented by 𝑟𝑏𝑎𝑙 . Under healthy operation,
both residuals remain below the sustained 3𝜎 threshold. A 20% fouling increases 𝑟𝑈 𝐴 to
21.03𝜎, while 𝑟𝑏𝑎𝑙 remains near its healthy value of 2.60𝜎. By contrast, the two temperature
offsets increase both residuals substantially. The distinction therefore comes from the
residual pattern: a large heat-transfer residual with a normal balance is consistent with
physical degradation, whereas simultaneous violations of both relations indicate signal
perturbation.
 The PID-controlled pump case extends this principle by adding actuation and control-
law consistency. Three residuals are used to distinguish measurement-path (physics),
actuation-path, and control-logic perturbations. The pure-degradation case is especially
important because the operating point changes substantially while the detector remains
silent. As system resistance increases, the controller increases pump speed to maintain
the flow setpoint, but the physical, actuation, and control relationships remain mutually
consistent. The false-alarm rate therefore remains 0.000.

Anomaly Detector Robustness 113

  Across the three cases, the progression in attribution capability follows the available
redundancy. When more independent relations are available and respond differently to
different perturbation paths, more specific attribution becomes possible. This provides a
practical applicability condition that can be evaluated from the instrumentation before
a detector is implemented. It also connects detection with sensor placement: if several
hypotheses remain indistinguishable, the missing measurement can be identified by deter-
mining which additional relation would separate them.
  Another advantage of the framework is interpretability. The operator-facing output can
identify which relation was violated, by how many standard deviations it exceeded healthy
variability, and which measurements contributed to that relation. This is more transparent
than a single anomaly score and allows the reasoning behind an alarm to be reproduced
directly.
  Several limitations remain. The controlled pump case is currently demonstrated in
simulation and on a single-component loop, so hardware validation and extension to
multi-component systems are needed. The learned transient model also has incomplete
provenance, including an undocumented train/test split, and the transient evaluation is
based mainly on one substantial facility transient. In addition, the steady-state conductance
relation was fitted over a relatively narrow operating range, and the degradation scenarios
are synthesized rather than measured degradation histories.
  More generally, because this is a physics-based consistency method, perturbation must
produce an observable violation of the monitored physical, actuation, or control behavior
to be detected. Perturbations that are too small to have a measurable physical impact, or
that do not violate the available consistency relations, may therefore remain undetected.
This limitation follows directly from the intended scope of the framework.

4 PART III: TELEMETRY COLLECTOR AND CLIENT LIBRARY
Part III describes the multi-modal data collection infrastructure and the artifacts delivered
from it. Where Part I asks what a detector can see in process telemetry alone, the work
reported here addresses a prior question: what it would take to place process telemetry,
network traffic, and intrusion-detection output on a common timeline at a real facility, so
that a security operations capability could reason across all three at once.

4.1 Relationship to Part I
The two efforts draw on the same instrument and the same facility, and they are not the
same dataset. Part I evaluates detectors against process telemetry captured from February
through July 2025 and retained for that purpose. The collection reported in Part III was
a separate instrumented campaign run from February to May 2026, in which telemetry
was captured alongside network and intrusion-detection streams from the same plant at
the same moments. The difference that matters is not the calendar but the intent: the FY25
capture records what the process did, while the FY26 campaign records what the process
did and what the network carried while it did it. Only the second supports cross-modal
correlation, and only the second required the facility network to be tapped.
  That distinction also sets the limits of what Part III claims. This is an infrastructure and
data-delivery contribution. It reports what was built, what it captured, and what was
handed to the collaboration; it makes no detection claims, and none of Part I’s results
depend on it.

4.2 Collection Architecture
The system is three repositories with distinct roles. arss collector runs at the facility
edge and acquires the streams. arss collector server is an Apache Arrow Flight
endpoint that receives completed files from one or more collector nodes and lands them in
a partitioned store. arss collector library is the analysis-side client, which is what
a downstream consumer actually programs against.
  The collector is four containerised services sharing a filesystem rather than one process.
A telemetry service polls the facility historian API; a network service listens for NetFlow
v5 and captures packets; an intrusion-detection service tails Suricata’s EVE JSON output
using inotify; and a transfer service ships completed Parquet files onward over Arrow
Flight. Separating them means a fault in one acquisition path, or a restart of one container,
does not interrupt the others, which matters when the capture window is fixed by facility
access rather than by the experimenter.

Anomaly Detector Robustness 115

4.3 Cross-Stream Activation
The streams differ in how they are acquired, and that asymmetry motivates the one design
decision that follows from it. Network flow, packet capture, and intrusion detection are
passive: they record what crosses the tap when it crosses it, and there is no rate to choose.
Telemetry is different. It is obtained by polling the historian API, so its resolution is set
by how often the collector asks, and asking continuously at high rate for months is not
affordable in storage.
 A polling rate fixed in advance is therefore the wrong instrument. A security-relevant
event is not confined to the stream that first shows it: a network intrusion has process
consequences, and the interesting question is what the process was doing while the network
activity occurred. Answering it requires the telemetry resolution to rise at the moment the
network gives a reason, which the telemetry collector cannot know on its own.
 The collectors therefore coordinate, and the coordination is deliberately asymmetric. The
three sensing services (telemetry, NetFlow, and the intrusion-detection tail) each evaluate
trigger criteria against their own stream and publish a vote to a small JSON state file,
giving a verdict, a priority, a human-readable reason, and the specific values that fired it.
Only the telemetry collector reads the aggregate, because it is the only service with a rate
to change: packet capture and the transfer service are rate-free and neither publishes nor
consumes votes. If any publishing stream votes for the active mode, the polled collector
moves from a background interval to an active one: 300 seconds and 5 seconds respectively,
in the collector’s shipped defaults. Coordination is through atomic file operations on
shared storage rather than through a message broker or a central coordinator, so there is
no additional service to keep alive at the facility edge and no ordering dependency among
the collectors at startup.
 The practical effect is that a Suricata alert or a NetFlow condition raises the telemetry
sampling rate, without either collector knowing the other exists beyond the shared directory.
The state files also serve as an audit record: each carries the reason and the triggering
values, so a rate change in the archive can be explained after the fact rather than inferred.

4.4 Unified Schema
The four streams share a storage contract, not a single row schema: Parquet, one Hive
partition layout, and the two time columns described below. Telemetry additionally has
a declared schema, and it is the one that carries process semantics: an ISA-95 equipment
hierarchy of facility, area, unit, equipment, and point. Alongside the measurement itself
that schema carries engineering units, a quality flag, access mode, and the provenance of

the reading, including source system, source bus, and source address. The network and
intrusion-detection streams keep their own native field sets, which is deliberate: coercing a
NetFlow record or a Suricata alert into an equipment hierarchy would discard the fields

4.5 Storage, Transfer, and Access
 Time alignment. Placing four streams on one timeline is the purpose of the campaign, so
the basis for alignment needs stating precisely, and it is not the same for every stream. Each
record carries two times: a timestamp and a collection timestamp. The second is
written by the collector on the capture host at the moment it handles the record, and is
therefore on one clock across all four streams by construction, with no synchronisation to
fail and no offset to estimate. Alignment on that column is bounded by the collectors’ own
scheduling jitter.
 The timestamp column is not uniform, and a consumer correlating streams on it is
not working in one clock domain. For telemetry and packet capture it is also a capture-
host time: stamped when the poll result is processed and when the packet is received,
respectively. For Suricata it is the time in the EVE record, which is the IDS’s own reading;
that process ran on the capture host during this campaign, so in practice it is the same
physical clock, but it belongs to the sensor, not to the collection system. For NetFlow it
is the timestamp in the export packet header, which is the exporter’s clock: the collector
records the exporter address alongside it precisely because it is built to accept flows from
exporters it does not own. Nothing measured the offset between those clocks during the
campaign.
 The practical guidance follows from the two columns. Cross-modal correlation should
use collection timestamp, which is single-clock and whose error is scheduling jitter.
Analysis needing event time (flow start, alert time) should use timestamp and treat cross-
stream offsets as unquantified. The further limitation on telemetry applies to both columns:
the historian is polled, so what is recorded is when the collector obtained a value and not
when the historian sampled it. That age was not measured and this report does not bound

Anomaly Detector Robustness 117

it; the polling interval bounds how often a fresh value could arrive, not how stale the value
returned already was.

 Transfer security. Files move over Arrow Flight with bearer-token authentication. The
transport supports TLS and both client and server default to it, though the client does not
verify certificates by default, so self-signed material is accepted. The deployed configuration
is not recorded in this report and should be captured in any repeat. The exposure is
bounded by the topology: transfers ran over a dedicated point-to-point fiber link between
two buildings with no other traffic and no routed path, so transport encryption protects
against an adversary with physical access to that fiber rather than against a network-
resident one. At-rest protection of the four terabytes of packet capture, which contains
facility network topology and protocol detail, is the more consequential control and is
handled outside the scope of this report.
 Files are written in Hive-style partitions by facility, stream type, and calendar date, which
allows a query restricted to a date range to skip unrelated partitions without reading them.
The same layout is preserved end to end: the collector writes it, the Flight server maintains
it on ingest, and the client library relies on it for partition pruning. Transfer is authenticated
with a bearer token.
 The client library exposes one entry point per stream, returning Polars frames. It is the
interface a collaborator is expected to use, and it exists so that consuming the data does
not require knowing the partition layout, the Parquet schema, or the collection system at
all. The packet-capture reader can export a selected set of packets back out to a PCAP file,
so that a period identified analytically in the library can be opened directly in Wireshark or
any other conventional network-analysis tool without leaving the workflow.

4.8 Status and Constraint
The collection system is no longer running. The sequence was as follows.
 Telemetry collection stopped partway through the campaign. The cause was a facility
control-system upgrade carried out between the FY25 and FY26 work; the project learned of
it after the collection errors began. The migrated historian API did not sustain continuous
polling: it crashed every few hours and required a manual reset to resume.

  The failures were on the historian, not on the collector, and the corpus establishes that
as far as it can: the same host, running the same code, kept NetFlow, packet capture, and
Suricata collecting for three months after telemetry stopped, so no fault common to the
collection system explains it. The corpus cannot exclude a fault specific to the telemetry
collector, since that path and the historian failed together. The attribution rests additionally

contemporaneous record of API crashes requiring manual reset.
  The project worked with facility staff to diagnose and restore the interface over ap-

knowledge left the laboratory during that period. The facility subsequently determined
that the entire control system would be replaced, with no schedule announced, and that
the legacy historian would not be returned to service.
  The replacement will present a different interface and a different schema. The integration
described here is specific to the legacy historian’s API and to a sensor and setpoint configu-
ration that does not carry across, so the telemetry collector will require rework rather than
reconfiguration before it can be used against the new system, and no date exists against
which to plan it.
  Once the facility system was confirmed down and not returning, the fiber patch was
removed and the capture and storage servers were repurposed as compute nodes, where

would require standing the capture path back up as well as building an integration against
the replacement historian.
  What survives the facility is the larger part of the work. The collection architecture, the
cross-stream activation design, the unified schema, and the client library are not specific to
this facility, and only the telemetry acquisition path is coupled to the retired interface. The
captured data remains available as a correlated multi-modal reference from an operating
research facility, and the compute allocation supporting its analysis is unaffected.

5 CONCLUSION
Three lines of effort are reported here, and across all three the returns came from what
was instrumented and what relations that instrumentation supports, rather than from
the sophistication of the method applied to it. That is an observation about where effort
paid off in this work, not a demonstration that observability explains every result. Part I
is explicit that a panel-wide miss does not establish that a perturbation is unobservable.
The three parts reach it from different directions, which is the reason for reporting them
together.

 Part I: the perturbation matters more than the detector. Across 3,080 test cases, the expectation
that motivated the study did not hold. We expected the neural, dependency-graph and
causal-structure detectors to catch what classical statistics miss. Classical methods took
both leading places and led on nine of the eleven disturbances. The causal family largely
did not fill the gap it was included for: its strongest member came third and led on two
disturbances, one of which nothing detects better than guessing; another gave different
answers on different runs, and a third produced scores too coarse to read at all.
 The overall score turned out to be a poor guide to what a detector does in service, and
that is the most useful thing in Part I. On the summary measure the top two detectors are
inseparable at the most severe level — never more than 0.05 apart on any disturbance there,
and at most 0.030. That does not hold lower down the ladder: at the mildest level the largest
gap is 0.237 and five disturbances exceed 0.05. Hold them instead to one false alarm in a
hundred and the picture changes completely: on physics violation the top-ranked detector
misses 38.9% of the affected windows and the second-ranked one misses none. The same
applies to choosing a set of detectors rather than one. Judged at that alarm rate, three of
the eleven are the only member that catches some disturbance, across four cases that hold
under both ways of constraining the injection; two further candidates were examined and
rejected, one because the alarm rate happened to fall just below a near-vertical step in
another detector’s curve, the other because the case was too close to guessing to read. A
monitoring suite chosen on overall score would discard detectors it needs.
 Requiring several detectors to agree almost never helped: consensus beat the best single
detector on two of forty-four cases, both where that single detector was itself weak. Twenty
of the remaining cases are ties where both approaches already catch everything and six are
ties where both catch almost nothing. That result assumes the detectors raise false alarms
independently of one another, which we did not measure, and it tests the full set rather
than a chosen subset — so it is not a statement about how a deployed ensemble would
behave.

122 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

 What decides whether a disturbance is caught is the disturbance, not the detector. Three
defeat everything we tried, topping out between 0.53 and 0.88 where eight others are caught
almost perfectly. For one of the three the reason is understood and measured: replay-based
falsification feeds back readings the plant genuinely produced, so the statistics a detector
watches barely change, and nothing in the panel beats guessing on it. That one cannot
be fixed with a better detector of this kind — it has to be addressed where the readings
enter the network, through sequence numbering, freshness fields or authentication of the
sensor path. We tested the same explanation on the two valve disturbances and it fails:
their readings do move, and why no method converts that movement into a detection is
unresolved. Five cases where disturbed data scores as more normal than clean data are
likewise measured and unexplained; a sixth is accounted for.
 Two lessons follow for anyone running this kind of evaluation. Detectors gain most of
what they are going to gain at the first step up in severity and almost nothing after it, so
testing at a single severity will misjudge them badly in either direction. And a large share
of our own results — 852 of the 1,540 primary cases on the estimate used here, between
two fifths and two thirds across the range of assumptions we tested — cannot be told apart
from guessing, because what limits the evidence behind a score is the number of windows
scored, not the number of rows of data. A benchmark that reports those cases as scores
reports noise in the format of a result.

 What to do next, in order. Three questions follow from Part I, and they are ordered by
what they cost against what they settle. Each is stated with the measurement that answers
it, because the absence of that discipline is the main methodological lesson of this year’s
work.
 First, repair the replay disturbance and re-run it. Two faults in how it is generated have
to be cleared before its result means anything. It targets three channels and reaches one:
the other two hold a single constant value throughout the collection period, so replaying
them changes nothing — thirty-seven of this facility’s hundred and twenty-five channels
are constant, so target selection has to be checked against the data rather than assumed.
And its severity ladder is inverted, the severest level altering half as many readings as the
mildest, because a longer recording phase leaves less of the episode available to replay into.
On the channel it does reach the change is substantial, rewriting two fifths of its readings,
so this is not a signature too weak to detect — it is one that reaches a fraction of what
it was designed to. Fix both, apply the presence test that retired the twelfth disturbance
(Section 2.4.4), and re-run. If nothing detects it then, the structural reading holds and the
design recommendation follows; if detection recovers, the FY26 result was a property of

Anomaly Detector Robustness 123

the generator. This costs one signature regeneration and one row of the matrix, and it gates
the most consequential claim in Part I.
 Second, test whether a temporal detector sees what value-based detection cannot. No classical
time-series baseline was in this panel — residual monitoring against a fitted ARIMA
or state-space model, which is what is deployed in practice — so the neural sequence
model had no fair comparator. It is also the one method class with a mechanism to catch
a replay that the others lack: not the values, which are real, but the splice where the
replayed segment begins and ends and the drift that fails to accumulate across it. Report
false-negative rate at the alarm-rate sweep and the leave-one-out contribution, both modes.
 Third, settle whether the causal methods are weak or were starved. They ran here at a fifth of
the channel access given to the others and without per-detector tuning, so their showing
is a statement about the configuration we could afford rather than about the approach. A
resource-matched rerun — equal channels, comparable decision cadence, nested validation
— answers it either way, and a negative result obtained at parity would be considerably
stronger than the one reported here. Before committing the full matrix, check the resolution
gate on a few cells per detector: one method returned no interpretable cell at all this year,
and an unmeasurable detector at parity is a different finding from a weak one.

  Part II: attribution is a property of the available relations. Where Part I asks whether a
departure can be seen, Part II asks what can be concluded about its cause. A consistency
framework compares operator-visible measurements against an expected representation of
the system, analytical for steady-state cases and learned where transient behaviour resists
compact equations, and reasons over the pattern of residuals rather than over a single score.
That pattern is what carries the attribution. On the cold trap, against a healthy baseline
taken from the facility record with the degraded and perturbed conditions synthesized on
top of it, a heat-transfer residual driven to 21.03𝜎 while the energy-balance residual stays
near its healthy 2.60𝜎 marks fouling, where both relations violated together marks signal
perturbation. Attribution to a specific path requires actuation and control relations that the
cold trap does not have; that is shown on the simulated pump loop, where three residuals
separate the measurement, actuation and control-logic paths and the pure-degradation
case produces no alarm at all even though the operating point moves substantially, because
the relations remain mutually consistent.
  The transferable observation is that attribution capability tracked the number and inde-
pendence of the available consistency relations rather than the complexity of the method.
The three configurations differ in system, regime and model type as well as in relation
count, so this is a pattern across cases and not a controlled ablation; its value is that the

124 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

condition can be assessed in advance. Given the instrumentation together with the plant
description needed to write the relations down — topology, measurement semantics, con-
trol architecture, component characteristics — one can enumerate which relations can be
formed, what distinctions they would support, and which additional measurement would
be needed to separate hypotheses that remain confounded. No instrument was added or
removed to confirm that a predicted distinction then appears; that test remains to be run.
Where the relation is an explicit equation the framework is correspondingly interpretable,
naming the violated relation, the margin over healthy variability and the contributing
measurements; the learned model used for the transient regime does not support the same
attribution, and a perturbation applied to one of its inputs can be absorbed into its own
prediction. In every case the method is bounded by its premise: a perturbation that violates
no available relation produces no residual to detect.

 Part III: what cross-modal reasoning would require. The third effort addresses a prior ques-
tion: what it takes to capture process telemetry, network flow records, packet capture and
intrusion-detection output together at an operating facility, so that a security operations
capability could later reason across them at once. The collection was built and run under a
common collection timestamp, though record-level clock alignment between the streams
was not measured and four-stream overlap is established at day resolution. A February
subset of the roughly four-terabyte campaign, with network topology documentation,
a client library, a computing allocation and onboarding materials, was delivered to the
collaborating institution. No cross-modal analysis was performed on the captured streams,
no detection claims are made, and no Part I result depends on any of it. The binding
constraint on future cross-modal work is stated plainly in that part: it is the interval over
which all streams hold records, which is narrower than any single stream’s extent.

  Taken together. A monitoring program for an advanced reactor appears to gain more from
what it instruments than from which detector it selects. Part I found eleven detectors largely
interchangeable in tier-4 AUC wherever a perturbation was detectable at all, though not at
a fixed operating point, where the choice is consequential. They failed together where it
was not. It does not establish that those shared failures are failures of observability, and
for two of the three low-ceiling signatures the channels demonstrably move. Part II found
attribution becoming available as independent physical relations did. Part III found the
same shape at the level of the data platform, where what can later be correlated is set by
what was captured together. None of this argues against better detectors, and this study
did not measure what any of them cost to deploy. It argues that the questions worth asking
early are which channels are scored, which physical relations they support, and whether

Anomaly Detector Robustness 125

the streams can be brought onto a common timeline at all, because those bounded what
was achievable here in a way that the choice among detection methods, in aggregate, did
not. For designs still in development, those are choices that can still be made.
  The limitations remain as stated in each part, and several substantive questions are left
open rather than resolved: the two valve signatures, the five inversions, the attribution of
clamp causes, and hardware validation of the consistency framework beyond simulation
and a single-component loop.

({42, 1, 2, 3, 4}) with 8-hour training chunks, and use percentile-based thresholding. The
reconstruction/prediction detectors (AE, LSTM, PCA) aggregate per-sensor scores with a
robust top-𝐾 mean (𝐾 = 30, median/MAD normalization, MAD floor 10−6 ). Dependency

  Choosing 𝐾. 𝐾 was selected by sweep rather than taken as a default, for the reason given
in Section 2.5: at small 𝐾 the top slots are occupied by a few background channels whose
reconstruction error responds to any perturbation context. The failure is not a low score but
a misleading one. At 𝐾 = 3, two of the three slots are held by the same two such channels
in essentially every cell, and AUC collapses to a fixed value near 0.811 independent of both
signature and tier, which is a detector that appears to work while carrying no per-signature
information. Median AUC across the sweep was 0.826 at 𝐾 = 30 against 0.811 at 𝐾 = 3;
the median difference is small and is not the reason for the choice. The decisive criterion
was tier discrimination, which 𝐾 = 3 does not have at all because its score does not vary
with severity.

B AI-ASSISTED DEVELOPMENT: PRACTICE AND FINDINGS
  Applicability. This appendix describes practice on Part I, where AI coding agents were
used throughout, and in a smaller way on Part III, where they were used for implementation
but not for experimental design or analysis. It does not describe Part II, which was produced
independently and under different practice. The findings are offered as a practice note for
other ARSS participants considering similar tooling.

132 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

  Throughout, agent means a large language model given a task, access to the project’s files,
and the ability to run commands, as distinct from a model used only to answer questions
in a chat window. The distinction matters: an agent changes the repository, so its errors
persist.

B.1 What the practice consists of
Four written records carry the method. Each is kept under version control and reviewed
like source code.

 A standards file the agent is required to follow. Engineering and research-code con-
 ventions live in the repository and are loaded into every agent session. The content
 that earns its place is not style guidance but the small set of rules that prevent whole
 categories of defect: fail loudly instead of substituting a default when something is
 missing, define how a routine behaves when its inputs are wrong before defining
 what it does when they are right, and never treat passing tests as evidence that
 a scientific result is sound. A companion document records the measurement be-
 hind each rule and the commits that motivated it, so a rule that looks arbitrary or
 expensive can be traced to what it cost to learn.
 A technical narrative document. A single running record, currently 64 dated entries,
 of what changed, why, and what was measured. This is the record that most repays
 the effort. Agents have no memory across sessions, and reconstructing why a
 decision was made is expensive and unreliable; a narrative the agent reads at the
 start of a session supplies it directly.
 It also gives working conclusions a place to be revised. Some entries record that
 an interim interpretation formed during development was superseded by a later
 measurement, together with the evidence that settled it. Keeping that inside the
 development record is what allows an interpretation to be tested and discarded
 cheaply, before it reaches an analysis or a draft. The reported results in this document
 are the output of that process, not a stage within it.
 Issue tracking for commitments. Deliverable-level work is tracked separately from
 the technical narrative, referenced by number from commits and merge requests.
 Keeping these layers distinct matters: the narrative holds technical reasoning that
 would swamp a milestone view, and the tracker holds schedule commitments that
 do not belong in a document reviewed as source.
 Git history as an audit record. Any commit touching scoring, caching, data selection,
 or evaluation is required to state the defect in observable terms, the mechanism,

Anomaly Detector Robustness 133

 why earlier checking missed it, what was verified afterward with concrete values,
 and what remains open. The third item is the one that changes future behavior, and
 it is the one most often omitted without an explicit requirement.

B.2 How the work is checked
 Data contracts. The analysis runs as a sequence of stages, each consuming the previous
stage’s output. The structure of the data handed between stages is declared explicitly and
checked at each handoff, so a record with a missing or misnamed field is rejected where
it is produced instead of being misread later. Automated type checking is applied to all
235 source files, in its strictest setting. This is unglamorous and it removes a large class
of agent error: an agent that misunderstands a data structure then writes code that fails
immediately, instead of code that reads the wrong fields and produces plausible numbers.

 Invariants and metamorphic testing. Unit tests confirm that a function does what its author
expected, which is of limited value when the author and the implementer are the same
agent. Two complements carry more weight.
 The first is a written specification, one for each of the 35 data generators, listing properties
the generator’s output must always satisfy no matter how it is implemented. These are
checked automatically against real output.
 The second is a technique known as metamorphic testing, which is worth describing
because it suits this setting unusually well. Instead of asserting what the output should be,
it asserts how the output must change when an input changes. Raising a severity setting
must produce a larger effect. Two configurations that differ only in a label must produce
distinguishable results. The advantage is that nobody has to know the right answer in
advance, which is exactly the position one is in when reviewing code an agent wrote to
compute something new. It also catches the case where an implementation is internally
consistent and still wrong, which ordinary unit tests will not.

  Test volume. The repository holds roughly 72,000 lines of test code against 98,000 lines
of implementation, and 3,674 tests run on every change before acceptance. The ratio is
the point, not the absolute figure. Agent- assisted development shifts the constraint from
writing code to establishing that the code is correct, and a test suite sized for hand-written
work will not keep up with the volume of code that arrives.

  Measure the real system, not a copy of it. A common debugging move is to rebuild a small
standalone copy of what the pipeline does, then study the copy. When the copy disagrees
with what the pipeline actually produced, the copy is the tempting thing to investigate,

134 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

because it is fast and fully under the investigator’s control, and an agent will keep refining
it indefinitely. The productive move is to add logging inside the running pipeline instead.
On this project one such disagreement absorbed five successive explanations before anyone
recorded the actual values from the live run, at which point the cause was immediate. A
small amount of detail is enough, provided each logged line identifies which case it came
from.

  Outputs are labelled by the code that produced them. Each stage writes into a directory whose
name is derived from the version of the code and the settings used, with a separate marker
recording which directory is current. When the code changes, the next run writes some-
where new, and output from the previous version stops being visible to later stages rather
than being picked up by accident. This is the single most effective protection against the
case where a corrected pipeline is evaluated against results left over from the uncorrected
one, which is otherwise very hard to notice.

B.3 Cross-model code review
The primary development agent throughout was Claude, using a combination of Opus and
Sonnet models across versions that changed over the project’s life. Review was performed
by different models from different families: various versions of GPT, Gemini, Gemma, and
Laguna were used at different points.
  Review was not organized around individual changes. Each pass was a whole-repository
review, with the reviewing model given the codebase and the standards file but not the
reasoning that produced any of it, and asked to find defects. Findings were adjudicated
against the code and the data rather than accepted on assertion, and a finding two models
raised independently was treated as more likely to be real than one raised once. This scope
matters. The defects that survive on this kind of project are rarely visible in a single change;
they live in the relationship between a generator and the validator that is supposed to
check it, or between a stored intermediate result and the code that reads it. A reviewer
looking at one change at a time cannot see them.
  Using a different model family for review was deliberate. An agent re-examining its own
work inherits its own assumptions and will usually confirm them, and models within a
family share enough behavior that the same effect appears in weaker form.
  The same approach applied to prose. A reviewing model re-derived every quantitative
claim in a draft back to the data files it came from, without access to the analysis that
generated them. One such pass over a draft of this report identified 34 incorrect figures,
three of which contradicted other statements in the same document.

  None of this replaces human review. It changes what human review is spent on, by
disposing of the mechanical checks before a person reads the work.

B.4 Challenges
 Plausible output is the default failure mode. The defects that cost this project the
 most ran to completion and produced output that looked reasonable. A validator
 gated on a field that was always empty never executed, and the two experimental
 conditions it distinguished were identical for the working life of the study. Nothing
 crashed, no test failed, and the code read correctly.
 Confident and incorrect diagnoses. An agent asked for a root cause will supply one,
 fluently, whether or not it is right. The mitigation is procedural: require the diagnosis
 to name a specific mechanism and require a measurement that distinguishes it from
 the alternatives before any fix is written.
 Verification that passes for the wrong reason. A check measuring something adja-
 cent to what the code consumes will report healthy while the real data is malformed.
 When a check is written, state which file or value it reads and confirm that is the
 same one the next stage consumes.
 Silent repairs that destroy their own evidence. Clamps, retries, and default substi-
 tutions make bad data look corrected. If a correction fires where it logically should
 never fire, treat it as a fault report rather than as the correction working.
 Plausible scientific explanations for what are pipeline defects. When a result sug-
 gests the phenomenon under study is unexpectedly hard to detect, the cause on
 this project has been a defect in scoring, caching, or data selection every time it
 has arisen. The working rule is to treat such results as defects until the pipeline is
 cleared.
 Reproducibility conditions that are easy to overlook. Two runs of the same com-
 mand, with identical settings, produced different results: 4,780 of 104,536 values
 differed. The cause was a language-level setting that randomizes the internal order-
 ing of data structures unless it is fixed explicitly. Any experiment that compares two
 runs will absorb this kind of uncontrolled variation and report it as a real effect.
 Expensive paths proposed before cheap ones. Agents default to proposing a full re-
 run where recomputing from results already on disk would answer the question
 in seconds. On shared computing allocations this is a direct cost. Require that
 diagnostics exhausting existing data be tried first.
 Shell access against finite resources. An agent driving a scheduler can consume an
 allocation quickly, and destructive filesystem operations are irreversible in a way

136 B. Blakely, Y. Li, et al

 that a bad code change is not. Operations that delete data, rewrite history, or submit
 batch work should be prepared and shown for confirmation, not executed on
 inference about intent. Naming those categories explicitly in the standards file is
 what makes the boundary enforceable.
 Verification does not scale with generation. Gains in the speed of producing code
 are real and large. They do not reduce the checking required, and code arrives faster
 than unaided review can absorb. Budget review effort against the volume produced.

B.5 Cost profile
 Model access. For the duration of this project, frontier model access was provided at
no cost to the project through the laboratory’s Argo service, which brokers Claude, GPT,
and Gemini models through Azure and Google Vertex. Adoption of that service across
the laboratory, and the concerns its users report, are surveyed in [42]. Hardware was
separately available for running moderately sized open-weight models locally, meaning
models whose parameters can be downloaded and run on local machines without a paid
service. This is a privileged position and other teams should not assume it. Access on these
terms is unlikely to continue indefinitely, and projects should expect to carry model usage
as a budget line of their own.
 Metered at retail rates, usage during the most active periods of this work is estimated
at $1,500 to $2,000 per month. That figure already accounts for the discount from prompt
caching, a billing feature that charges less for the repeated portion of a conversation. The
figure is an estimate and it varies substantially with activity: drafting and analysis cost
a small fraction of active implementation. It is quotable at all only because usage was
instrumented. A local proxy records every request, which makes consumption trackable
against a budget and forecastable ahead of one. Any team adopting this tooling should put
that instrumentation in place at the start, because reconstructing spend after the fact is not
practical and an unmetered agent gives no warning before an invoice arrives.

  Most corrective effort went to code written days earlier. The larger effort cost was not paying
down old technical debt. It was fixing work the project had just produced. Attributing
a recent cluster of corrective commits by the age of the code each one changed, the split
was close to even: nine addressed code less than a week old, ten addressed older work
between two months and just under a year. Two feature landings each required three to
four corrective commits within forty-eight hours of the original. Roughly half of corrective
effort in that window went to code that had only just been written.

Anomaly Detector Robustness 137

  The codebase did stabilize. Over a longer baseline the pattern is one of subsystems closing
and staying closed. One central module absorbed twenty-five corrective commits across
four weeks and then none in the following two and a half months.
  What verification finds also changes character over time. Early corrections concern
whether code computes what it claims. Later ones concern whether a comparison is
constructed validly, and that second class only becomes visible once the first is cleared. A
continuing rate of findings in mature code is therefore the expected signature of verification
apparatus that is itself maturing, and it should not be read as a project failing to settle.

  Planning implications. Budget corrective effort as a fraction of generated volume, since it
does not shrink in proportion to the speed of generation. Expect the verification apparatus,
and not the implementation, to be the slowest thing to mature: the checks described in this
appendix were built incrementally in response to specific findings, and a project adopting
them at the outset would pay much of this cost once instead of repeatedly.

B.6 Basis for scientific defensibility
The case rests on properties of what the project produced, not on confidence in how it was
produced. Every published figure traces to a command that emits it. Every stage output
is addressed by a hash of the code and configuration behind it, so a result cannot silently
inherit a superseded input. Every known defect is recorded with the measurement that
exposed it and an account of why earlier checking missed it, which is what allows the
checking to improve.
 These are ordinary research software engineering practices. The observation from this
project is that agent assistance raises the cost of not having them, because it increases the
rate at which plausible and unverified material enters a repository. Applied early they are
inexpensive. Applied after a body of results exists they require revisiting those results,
which this project has now done more than once.
