A Mesopore-Confined and Graphene Oxide-Localized Ruthenium Catalyst Increases Rates of Mid-Chain Polyolefin Hydrogenolysis
Authors: Kajol Tonk, Simin Sun, Olajide H. Bamidele, Minghui Niu, Xiaobing Zuo, Jessica V. Lamb, A. Jeremy Kropf, Massimiliano Delferro, Byeongdu Lee, Andreas Heyden, Wenyu Huang, Aaron D. Sadow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A catalyst architecture with mesoporous silica coating Ru nanoparticles on reduced graphene oxide (mSiO2/Ru/rGO) localizes the 2 nm Ru solely at the closed bottoms of 2.9 nm diameter mesopores. mSiO2/Ru/rGO catalyzes the rapid, selective hydrogenolysis of polyolefins at wax formation rates (νwax) up to 1700 gwax·gRu –1·h–1 and a turnover frequency (TOF) for C–C bond cleavage of 130 ± 8 min–1. The νwax and TOF for mSiO2/Ru/rGO are 23× and 16× those of nonporous Ru/rGO, respectively, indicating that faster chain cleavage is also more selective inside mesopores. The methane yield from mSiO2/Ru/rGO is ca. 30% of the value obtained from Ru/rGO. Methane decreases, and wax selectivity improves with narrow-pore (2.3 nm) mSiO2/Ru/rGO. The reaction rate decreases at lower and higher H2 pressures from its maximum at 37 bar. Log­(TOF)-log­(P H2) plots reveal rates ∝ [P H2]2.8 or [P H2]−2.0 in the lower or higher pressure ranges, respectively. Corresponding plots for Ru/C give rates ∝ [P H2]1 or [P H2]−2.4. Ru is hydrocarbon-covered at low pressure; thus, the higher H2 order for mSiO2/Ru/rGO indicates that mesopores increase the density of cleavable unsaturated moieties on the Ru surface. The lower H2 order on Ru/C implies a lower density of cleavable groups because saturated segments occupy a larger fraction of the Ru surface. Higher surface occupancy by the hydrocarbon reactant correlates with more methane formation. Therefore, pore confinement, narrower pore diameter in mSiO2/Ru/rGO, or higher H2 pressure leads to lower methane yields. Ru nanoparticles in mSiO2/Ru/rGO maintain equivalent activity and selectivity over five recycling tests.

Introduction

Plastics are indispensable in modern life due to their low cost, strength under strain-to-weight ratio, thermal stability, and chemical durability. Their wide-ranging applications have resulted in massive-scale manufacture, currently consuming almost 10% of fossil-derived hydrocarbons and generating millions of tons of plastics as waste each year. − Chemical conversion offers a promising avenue for high-yielding transformation of plastic waste into value-added products in a new manufacturing economy. − Hydrogenolysis of polyolefins has therefore attracted recent attention; , however, these efforts also highlight new fundamental scientific questions regarding strategies to target specific products. In addition, classic hydrocarbon hydrogenolysis has been primarily studied at solid–gas interfaces, − whereas the melt-phase conditions for reactions of polymers and solid catalysts can affect the mechanism.

Supported Pt nanoparticle catalysts, such as Pt/C and Pt/SrTiO3, , catalyze the hydrogenolysis of polyolefins into lubricating liquids and wax. Hydrocarbon hydrogenolysis on Pt (or Ru) surfaces typically invokes reversible metal-catalyzed dehydrogenative formation of M x –C2R2 surface-bonded species (where M x represents the x surface metal sites bonded to the organic moiety), rate-limiting C–C bond cleavage of carbon atoms bonded to the metal surface, and a kinetically invisible, low barrier hydrogen addition/alkane desorption of the M y –CR and M z –CR intermediates. The apparent activation energies and operating temperatures for ethane hydrogenolysis are higher for Pt than for Ru, as are DFT-calculated barriers for C–C bond cleavage of the surface-bonded, dehydrogenated intermediates from ethane. Hydrogenolysis of melt-phase polyolefins appears to match this trend. Specifically, supported Ru materials − catalyze polyolefin hydrogenolysis from 200 °C, whereas Pt catalysts are most effective around 300 °C.

Increasing hydrogen pressure (e.g., above 20 bar), reducing the Ru nanoparticle to subnanometer size, and support effects point toward faster rates with higher hydrogen surface coverages, even though C–C bond scission is typically proposed as the rate-limiting step on Ru metal surfaces. DFT models suggested Ruδ+ species formed on these surfaces have a lower barrier for C–C bond cleavage than Ru0 itself. , Unfortunately, the destruction of polyolefin or the formation of product, used as a surrogate for measurement of C–C bond cleavage rates, can be convoluted by reaction selectivity. Kinetic analyses based on the extent of polymer destruction can be misleading because this outcome is mass-based and thus influenced by the products’ length. For example, each methane molecule generated corresponds to one C–C bond cleavage step but minimally affects the polymer itself. Thus, mid-chain C–C bond cleavage, rather than chain-end cleavage to methane, is essential for efficient polymer deconstruction.

Methane formation, which is often severe with Ru catalysts, is additionally undesirable and counterproductive because the current industrial synthesis of hydrogen involves energy-intensive reformation of methane itself. Ru/C is reported to generate over 50 wt % methane during polyethylene hydrogenolysis under some conditions. , Methane production often increases after some polymer is consumed, , implying shorter hydrocarbon chains are more likely to produce methane. Ru-catalyzed methane production decreases (e.g., to 5% methane at 73% conversion) under high pressures (20–60 bar) of H2 ,,, or by storing hydrogen on WO x clusters dispersed on zirconia catalyst support. , Smaller Ru particles or alloys diluting Ru (e.g., in Pt) also give lower yields of CH4, with single-atom Ru catalysts affording only 2.2% at 97.5% polymer conversion. A cascade process, in which a M y -CR surface intermediate undergoes further dehydrogenation and cleavage by unsaturated neighboring Ru atoms, has been proposed to be responsible for high methane yields. Diversion of the M y -CR intermediate from further dehydrogenation by hydrogen-induced desorption at high H2 pressure is then proposed to limit the pathway that leads to methane. Under this model, high hydrogen coverage would also shift the first dehydrogenation equilibrium and should slow the reaction rate, which is inconsistent with the trend assayed by polymer conversion.

An alternative approach for selective polyolefin hydrogenolysis to liquids (<C20-centered distributions) or wax products (C20–C30-centered distributions), with lowered gas production, has encapsulated Pt or ZrO2 nanoparticles (NPs) at the bottom of mesoporous silica wells. − In addition, Ru NPs deposited throughout large (9 nm) mesopores of SBA-15 give high rates of solid polymer conversion. Under melt-phase conditions of catalytic hydrogenolysis reactions, physical measurements of polymer absorption , and kinetic models of pore-encapsulated polyethylene hydrogenolyses show that polymers are more favored to fill the mesopores than small molecule products. This effect helps limit the over-hydrogenolysis of smaller liquid and wax products into light gases. Particle/polymer diffusion in the bulk are not mass transfer limited under these hydrogenolysis conditions.

In a mesoporous shell/Pt/mesoporous core catalyst architecture, for example, the mesoporous shell increases the rate of polymer conversion and wax formation by producing longer-on-average products. Longer products form because the active site location, in the middle of a pore, allows products to easily escape while preventing their return, in accordance with molecular dynamics simulations. Excluding wax products from the active site prevents them from being further cut. In this Pt system, encapsulation does not affect the C–C bond cleavage rate compared to related nonconfined catalysts (measured by quantification of H2 consumption or the increase in the total number of hydrocarbon molecules).

We hypothesized that the properties from the mSiO2/localized active-site catalyst architectures could synergistically combine with the high activity of Ru NPs to give high liquid/wax selectivity for polymer hydrogenolysis under mild, low-temperature conditions. Here we describe the synthesis of localized, pore-encapsulated Ru NPs on reduced graphene oxide (rGO) represented by mSiO2/Ru/rGO (Figure ) and investigate its mass-specific wax formation rate (νwax), turnover frequency (TOF) as the rate of C–C bond cleavage normalized by exposed Ru, methane formation selectivity, and rate dependence on H2 pressure in catalytic polyolefin hydrogenolysis. mSiO2/Ru/rGO is more than an order of magnitude faster in terms of TOF and generates less gas (including methane) than Ru/rGO (lacking the mesoporous silica coating), a calcined analogue in which the rGO has been removed by calcination (Ru@mSiO2), or Ru/C. The comparisons between nonconfined and pore-confined catalysts reveal a unique synergy between the mesoporous architecture and Ru.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterizations of mSiO2/Ru/rGO

The sandwich-structured catalyst architecture mSiO2/Ru/rGO was synthesized by precipitation/deposition of Ru­(OH) x on graphene oxide (GO), followed by growth of a layer of mesoporous silica with pores perpendicularly oriented to the GO surface (Figure ). The use of GO proved important for the localization of Ru at the bottom of pores and preventing nanoparticle sintering in the final catalyst. The RuCl3·xH2O precursor precipitated on GO as Ru­(OH) x under basic conditions. Subsequently, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) was used as the pore-directing agent for the growth of mesoporous (m)­SiO2 layers on Ru­(OH) x /GO substrate. After forming mSiO2 layers, the CTA+ ions within the pores were replaced by NH4 + ions, which were then removed by washing with ethanol. The Ru­(OH) x and GO were reduced at 220 °C for 2 h under an H2 atmosphere to form metallic Ru NPs and rGO. The mSiO2/Ru/rGO material has been prepared in 0.2 g batches, and the protocol has been successfully scaled up to 1 g.

N2-sorption isotherm measurements indicated a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) total surface area of approximately 1170 m2 g–1 and a Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) pore diameter of about 2.9 nm (Figure S1). Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) analysis of mSiO2/Ru/rGO revealed a Ru loading of 4.2 ± 0.2 wt % and ruled out other transition metals (Table S1). Peaks associated with Ru were not detected by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD, Figure S2), possibly due to small particle size. Weak first-order diffraction peaks of Ru were observed in synchrotron-based wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) experiments (Figure S3). We prepared microtome samples to alleviate the shielding of mSiO2 and improve Ru signal intensity in analysis by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). Low- and medium-magnification STEM of microtome-sliced mSiO2/Ru/rGO showed a cross-section of 60 ± 2 nm thick nanoplatelets (Figure a,b). Both medium- and high-magnification images confirmed the existence of pores perpendicularly oriented to the rGO surface (Figure b,c). Ru NPs measured at 2.0 ± 0.1 nm were observed in the middle of two 30 nm-thick sheets of mSiO2, which matched the bright band of Ru signals exhibited through energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) elemental mapping (Figure d). This mSiO2/Ru/rGO catalyst was the focus of most studies in this work. An additional material with a narrower pore diameter of 2.3 nm and otherwise similar properties (loading, pore length, Ru NP size, Ru dispersion) was also synthesized (Figures S4 and S5) specifically to investigate the effects of pore size on selectivity in this mSiO2/Ru/rGO architecture. Narrower pore diameters in the mesoporous silica-coated Pt nanoparticles supported on a solid silica sphere (mSiO2/Pt/SiO2) architecture gave shorter-on-average products in polyolefin hydrogenolysis compared to catalysts with wider pores. On the other hand, the shorter pore lengths of mSiO2/Ru/rGO platelets (30 nm) could lead to broader distributions than the longer pores in the Pt system.

(d) Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy elemental maps of the cross-section of mSiO2/Ru/rGO (top left, Ru and Si; top right, Si; bottom left, O; bottom right, Ru). Inset in panel (b) shows the total thickness of mSiO2 coating on Ru/rGO, 60 ± 2 nm. Inset in panel (c) shows the statistical diameter of Ru NPs, 2.0 ± 0.1 nm.

Both rGO and mSiO2 components of the catalytic architecture affected the structure of the Ru NPs (Table S3). In particular, the 3.1 ± 0.2 nm diameter Ru NPs in the Ru/rGO were larger than those in mSiO2/Ru/rGO (2.0 ± 0.1 nm) at the same Ru to rGO ratio (Figure S6), even though the two materials were synthesized from a common Ru­(OH) x /GO precursor. The exposed Ru sites in mSiO2/Ru/rGO (21.7%) calculated from dispersion, however, were lower than in Ru/rGO (47.6%), presumably because the mSiO2 overcoat covered a portion of the Ru surface (Figure S7 and Table S3). Calcination of mSiO2/Ru/rGO at 550 °C (to remove rGO) led to the aggregation of Ru NPs with an average size of 8.9 ± 0.3 nm in Ru@mSiO2 (Figure S8).

Catalytic Activity of mSiO2/Ru/rGO for Hydrogenolysis of Polyolefins

Polyolefin hydrogenolysis is typically evaluated by polymer conversion or by wax yield, normalized into catalyst mass-specific rates. The C22–C23 average chain length of the extracted products leads to a low melting wax (see Figures S18 and S21 for TGA-DSC and GC characterization of the wax). The mass-specific wax formation rate (νwax) is expressed as m wax·(m Ru·t)−1 (where m wax is the mass of wax obtained after the reaction, m Ru is the mass of Ru in the reactor, and t is time). At 250 °C under 19 bar H2, 58% of a LDPE sample (3 g, number-averaged molecular weight (M n) = 3.2 kDa, mass-averaged molecular weight (M w) = 5.9 kDa, density (ρ) = 0.94 g/mL) is deconstructed via catalytic hydrogenolysis over 7 h into soluble wax with a νwax of 540 gwax·gRu –1·h–1 by only 0.011 g of mSiO2/Ru/rGO (0.015 wt % Ru with respect to LDPE). Longer reaction times using higher H2 pressures give complete deconstruction of the LDPE, however most experiments were designed to keep polymer conversion under 75%, and often less than 35%, for comparisons between Ru catalysts. These conditions limit secondary hydrogenolysis reactions of the wax product and avoid effects of changing reaction conditions, especially H2 pressure (see below). A higher νwax, 1700 gwax·gRu –1·h–1, is observed at higher pressure (37 bar H2) and 250 °C, affording 53% yield of soluble wax after 2 h (Tables S4 and S12). In contrast, Ru/rGO requires 96 h for equivalent LDPE deconstruction with a νwax of only 23 gwax·gRu –1·h–1 (at 19 bar, 250 °C), more than 20× slower than the pore-encapsulated, localized catalyst (at high polymer conversion). In general, the νwax of mSiO2/Ru/rGO for LDPE hydrogenolysis exceeds that of other Ru catalysts, such as Ru/C (88 gwax·gRu –1·h–1), Ru-15WZr, Ru/CeO2, ,, or Ru/TiO2 (Figure ).

Rates of wax formation (νwax) in LDPE hydrogenolysis for Ru catalysts under their reported best operating conditions (data and citations are given in Table S4).

The intrinsic activity of a catalyst for C–C bond hydrogenolysis, however, is not necessarily accurately assessed by rates of polymer conversion or even product formation, which also depend on selectivity (see below). For example, Ru/C is 40% faster than mSiO2/Pt/MCM-48 for hydrogenolysis of C–C bonds (at 20.6 bar, 300 °C), yet the latter, pore-encapsulated catalyst deconstructs solid LDPE more than 2× faster than Ru/C.

Quantification of C–C bond scission (n cuts, in mmol), based upon measurements of H2 consumed during an experiment, indicates that the typical high activity associated with Ru for C–C bond cleavage of small molecules increases with the mesoporous silica shell of mSiO2/Ru/rGO (Figure ). As an aside, catalyst-free background degradation of the LDPE under otherwise equivalent conditions (250 °C, 19 bar H2, 7 h) is negligible, as analyzed by H2 consumption and gas and wax formation (Table S14). The catalyst mass-specific C–C bond cleavage rate νcuts is defined as n cuts·(m metal·t)−1 where m metal is total Ru or Pt mass in the reactor. For example, νcuts for mSiO2/Ru/rGO of 10.0 ± 0.2 mol C–C bonds·gRu –1·h–1 is ca. 5× that of Ru/rGO and Ru/C under equivalent conditions (Table S3). Even though more than 50% of the Ru in Ru/rGO is covered by the silica shell in mSiO2/Ru/rGO, the comparison of mass-specific νcuts shows that the rate enhancement from encapsulation in mesopores more than makes up for the decrease in accessible Ru.

Mass-specific C–C bond cleavage rate (νcuts) and TOF for hydrogenolysis catalysts. Conditions: 250 °C, 19 bar H2, 0.015 wt % metal with respect to LDPE. **37 bar H2 (shaded bars). Data in Table S3.

Furthermore, the lower dispersity (d) of mSiO2/Ru/rGO compared to that of Ru/rGO or commercial Ru/C (60.9%) results in more than an order-of-magnitude higher turnover frequency (TOF) for the pore-encapsulated catalyst, calculated as n cuts·(n Ru·d·t)−1 where n Ru is mmol Ru. At 37 bar H2 where mSiO2/Ru/rGO gives the fastest νwax (see below), the νcuts and TOF increase to 17.0 ± 0.8 mol C–C bonds·gRu –1·h–1 and 130 ± 8 min–1, respectively.

The high C–C bond cleavage rate for mSiO2/Ru/rGO may partly result from the Ru-SiO2 interaction or from a pore confinement effect, considering that the hydrogenolysis TOF of Ru@mSiO2 (15 ± 2 min–1) is ca. 3× that of Ru/C and 2× that of Ru/rGO. In addition, the 5× faster TOF of mSiO2/Ru/rGO vs Ru/SiO2 may be partly attributed to the structure-sensitive hydrogenolysis reaction itself, ,,, since the Ru NPs are larger in the latter material. At low conversion, most of the increase in νwax comes from the faster C–C bond cleavage rate, and the increased selectivity from mSiO2/Ru/rGO has only a small effect on νwax. Specifically at ∼15% LDPE conversion, νwax of mSiO2/Ru/rGO is 6× that of Ru/rGO while the νcuts is 5× faster (at 19 bar; Tables S3 and S4). On the other hand, at high polymer conversion, there is an additional increase in wax formation rate that is not only coming from the faster C–C bond cleavage rate, but also from enhanced selectivity. Thus, at ∼65% LDPE conversion, the νwax is ca. 23× faster with mSiO2/Ru/rGO than with Ru/rGO, while the νcuts is only 7× faster (at 19 bar; Tables S13). The apparent TOF of mSiO2/Ru/rGO is 16× that of Ru/rGO under these conditions.

Polymer mass conversion is (usually) kept below 35% for the above rate comparisons because mSiO2/Ru/rGO shows time-dependent rates, as evidenced by solid, wax, and gas yields as well as C–C bond cleavage, in experiments over 14 h in a 100 mL reactor (Figure a). For example, the insoluble polymer fraction first decreases from 79% after 2 h to 34% after 7 h, but then to only 25% after 14 h, while the yield of soluble alkanes increases until 7 h and then plateaus at ca. 58% in a pseudo-steady state. Gases increase substantially, from 8% to 16%, over the last 7 h of the experiment.

Time-dependent reaction composition and n cuts for mSiO2/Ru/rGO-catalyzed hydrogenolysis of LDPE (M n = 3.2 kDa) at 250 °C, 19 bar H2 (initial pressure), and 0.015 wt % Ru with respect to LDPE in (a) 100 mL reactor (see Table S6) or (b) 300 mL reactor (see Table S7).

The n cuts are fully accounted for by H2 quantification since 1H NMR spectroscopy and GC-MS analysis indicate that all the soluble wax products are fully saturated, and calculation of n cuts(t) from the change in M n of the entire reaction population agrees with H2 consumed. Thus, C–C bond cleavage also tracks with product time-yields, increasing linearly over the first 3 h and then increasing more slowly until 10 h under the reaction conditions of Figure a. Even fewer C–C bonds are broken after 10 h. Catalyst deactivation is ruled out by the gas production noted above and catalyst recycling studies (see below), indicating that the ruthenium retains its initial high activity after being used in an experiment. We also note that the melt-suspended catalyst is exposed to a similar effective concentration of C–C bonds in every stage of the experiment, which typically leads to (pseudo)-zero-order kinetics for pore-confined Pt or L-ZrO2@mSiO2 catalysts. ,

The initial 19 bar H2 partial pressure decreases to 10.8 bar over the 14 h experiment. We therefore examined the rate of LDPE hydrogenolysis in a larger (300 mL) reactor to minimize the change in H2 pressure at high polymer conversion. The smaller and larger volume conditions gave similar initial rates (e.g., νcut 100 mL and νcuts 300 mL were 10.0 ± 0.2 and 9.3 ± 0.2 mol C–C bonds·gRu –1·h–1, respectively), indicating that the reactor volume did not substantially affect the intrinsic rate. At longer times or higher polymer conversion or higher H2 consumption (Figure b) however, the reaction rate in the larger vessel was constant (νcuts 14h of 9.1 ± 0.3 mol C–C bonds·gRu –1·h–1), indicated by a linear correlation of n cuts vs time in contrast to the slowed transformation in the smaller vessel. Quantitative consumption of LDPE was achieved in the larger reactor, at which point the H2 pressure had decreased to 14.2 bar. Although the decrease in H2-partial pressure appears to be responsible for the slowing νcuts, that trend contrasts the inverse pressure dependence ([P H2]−1.3) reported for Ru/SiO2-catalyzed hydrogenolysis of light gases under solid–gas conditions. An initial report and our initial observations suggest that Ru-catalyzed hydrogenolysis of melt-phase polyolefins do not match the solid–gas pressure dependence. Specifically, Ru/C shows volcano-like H2 pressure dependence in conversion of PE and product yields, albeit in unstirred reactors at 175 °C. Furthermore, as noted above, the νwax, νcuts, and the TOF increase as initial H2 pressure increases from 19 to 37 bar.

Because of these differences, we examined H2 pressure effects on the rate of LDPE hydrogenolysis in greater detail. In hydrogenolysis experiments catalyzed by mSiO2/Ru/rGO at 230 °C, increasing the initial H2 partial pressure to 37 bar monotonically increases the polymer conversion and n cuts at a fixed time (Figure ), indicating that the νcuts and TOF are also increasing. These data further support the conclusion that the slowing C–C bond cleavage rates are related to the large decrease in H2 pressure at high conversion in the time-varied data of Figure a.

H2 pressure effects on polymer conversion, wax yield, and n cuts in the catalytic hydrogenolysis of LDPE (M n = 3.2 kDa) using mSiO2/Ru/rGO (at <10% H2 conversion). Conditions: 230 °C, 550 rpm, 7 h, and 0.015 wt % Ru with respect to PE. See Table S8.

We then measured initial C–C bond cleavage rates. Plots of n cuts vs time at <30% polymer conversion are linear for all tested H2 pressures (see Figure S133). The slope of the log­(TOF)-log­(P H2) plot for this data from 19 to 37 bar reveals a (positive) 2.8 order in H2 (Figure S134). Above 37 bar, the catalytic activity (polymer conversion rate, νwax, νcuts, and TOF) of mSiO2/Ru/rGO for LDPE hydrogenolysis decreases as the initial H2 partial pressure increases. The slope of the log­(TOF)-log­(P H2) plot for experiments from 37 to 55 bar at 230 °C indicates a negative order in H2 of −2.0.

Ru/C also shows a volcano-like response of LDPE conversion, wax yield, and n cuts to varying initial H2 pressure (Figure S108), indicating this general behavior should not be attributed to confinement of Ru in pores. All experiments were performed using overhead, mechanically driven stirrers with mixing speeds of 250–550 rpm. The stirring rate did not affect the reaction, also ruling out mass transport effects. For this reason, the H2 chemical potential is equal in the three phases of the reaction mixture (gas phase, melted polymer, and catalyst surface) and changes as a function of H2 pressure. We also note that the >1 reaction order in H2 further indicates that mSiO2/Ru/rGO is not mass transfer limited, as severe H2 mass-transfer limitation would result in a first-order reaction. The peak rate for Ru/C at 230 °C occurs at 28 bar. At this pressure, Ru/C is still much slower for wax formation (νwax = 29 gwax·gRu –1·h–1) and C–C bond cleavage (TOF = 0.92 ± 0.07 min–1) than mSiO2/Ru/rGO (νwax = 460 gwax·gRu –1·h–1; TOF = 30 ± 2 min–1). The slopes of log­(TOF)-log­(P H2) plots from experiments at low conversion (Tables S9 and S11 and Figure S195) provide [P H2]1.0 and [P H2]−2.4 for Ru/C in the low- and high-pressure regimes, respectively (Figure S196).

These result show that H2 accelerates the C–C bond cleavage reaction on Ru at low partial pressure and inhibits the rate at high H2 pressure. The P H2 dependence, particularly the inverse order in H2 at high pressure, suggests that C–C bond cleavage is turnover limiting (eq 3 ). We formulate a mechanism based on the Langmuir–Hinshelwood framework, where rapid dissociative adsorption of H2 on Ru (eq ) and dehydrogenation and polymer adsorption (eq ) are quasi-equilibrated: H2+2*⇌2H(*) 1 polymer+β*⇌DP(β*)+γH2 2 DP(β*)+ξ*→products(ratecontrolling) 3

DP­(β*) is the adsorbed, dehydrogenated polymer, and ξ is the number of additional sites that are needed to cleave the carbon–carbon bond of the adsorbed, dehydrogenated hydrocarbon moiety in the rate controlling step. Surface H atoms then combine with the cleaved, unsaturated hydrocarbyl-surface species, leading to desorption of final alkane products in kinetically facile/invisible steps. When there is a large excess of H or hydrocarbon polymer on the Ru surface, small changes in H2 pressure do not significantly affect the coverage of dominant surface species. We thus approximate the Ru surface as fully covered with H in the high H2 pressure regime and as fully covered with hydrocarbon polymer chains in the low H2 pressure regime (Figure ). This approximation is valid at most H2 pressures, except near the region where the reaction order in H2 transitions from positive to negative; however, its overall validity and application to these systems are supported by the linearity of the log­(TOF)-log­(P H2) plots at both the low and high pressure extremes.

The inverse orders in H2 at high partial pressures with Ru/C and mSiO2/Ru/rGO indicate that H2 inhibits C–C bond cleavage as the pressure increases, as previously reported for Ru/SiO2-catalyzed hydrogenolysis of ethane. This behavior indicates that the surface is H-covered at high pressure as shown on the right side of Figure . That idea can be investigated using the Langmuir–Hinshelwood framework above to consider the individual contributions of the steps in the mechanism to the H2 reaction order.

In this analysis, the H-covered ruthenium surface is approximated as θH → 1. Using that substitution, a constant activity for the polymer, and a rearranged eq , the rate of C–C bond cleavage may be written in terms of the H2 pressure contributions. Substituting into eq gives eq : rC−Ccuts∝PH2−[γ+β+ξ2] 6

Given that at least two sites are needed for the cleavage products to bind to the Ru surface (i.e., β + ξ ≥ 2), the physisorption of CH2 and chemisorption of cleavable segments could lead to β ≥ 1, and at least one H is removed from the polymer (i.e., γ ≥ 0.5 and likely ≥1), the model predicts a maximum H2 reaction order of −1.5 when γ = 0.5 and β + ξ = 2. Thus, [P H2]−1.3 is measured for ethane hydrogenolysis by Ru/SiO2 where there are no additional CH2 groups in the reactant to further occupy Ru sites. When the hydrocarbon is further dehydrogenated (e.g., γ = 1), the maximum H2 reaction order is predicted by the kinetic model to be −2. [P H2]−2.0 and [P H2]−2.4 for LDPE hydrogenolysis by mSiO2/Ru/rGO and Ru/C, respectively, are thus consistent with turnover-limiting C–C bond cleavage of polymers from a melt-phase on Ru. In this situation, either more H is removed from the polymer prior to C–C bond cleavage (possibly less likely at high H2 pressure) or more surface sites are occupied by the polymer due to CH2 physisorption relative to ethane. To summarize, this kinetic model is in good agreement with the experimentally observed inverse dependence of C–C bond cleavage rate on H2 at high partial pressure. Moreover, the model indicates that, as qualitatively expected and in alignment with the proposed mechanism for hydrogenolysis of light gases on metal surfaces, hydrogen must be removed from polymer prior to the cleavage of the C–C bond.

In the low H2 pressure regime, we observe positive orders in H2 pressure on C–C bond cleavage rate for both mSiO2/Ru/rGO and Ru/C. As noted above, the order of 2.8 is larger for mSiO2/Ru/rGO than the order of 1.0 for Ru/C, which indicates that the rate is more sensitive to H2 pressure for the catalyst with Ru at the bottom of mesopores. In the high-pressure regime discussed above, the more negative order in H2 pressure on rate came from H covering the Ru surface and either more H2 being extruded or more surface sites being occupied by the polymer. Conversely, a less negative (i.e., more positive) reaction order and this higher sensitivity to H2 pressure at low pressure might again come from either more H2 being extruded or fewer sites being occupied by the polymer on the ruthenium surface.

Instead of H, the surface is covered with polymer chains, and each of these chains occupies a certain number of Ru sites (designated β as noted above) from both unsaturated and saturated segments as shown on the left side of Figure . Presumably, physisorbed CH2 groups contribute by increasing the enthalpy of adsorption and positioning the unsaturated chain segments on the Ru surface for cleavage.

For Ru/C, polymer adsorption involving unsaturated segments and CH2 moieties occurs primarily on Ru. The physisorbed moieties occupy surface sites that could have been used for other steps of the process (e.g., the sites for rate-determining bond-breaking or for polymer dehydrogenation) or for cleavage of other dehydrogenated polymer segments. In mSiO2/Ru/rGO, chains can additionally adsorb on the silica walls of the mesopores, which also influence the saturated hydrocarbon segments to favor anti conformations and extend along the pore wall. This adsorption of chains in the pores and on the pore walls could lead to less physisorption on Ru itself, leaving more space for more unsaturated polymer segments to be cleaved. This perturbation of polymer chain adsorption would thus be manifested in a larger positive H2 order for mSiO2/Ru/rGO than Ru/C.

We support this qualitative interpretation with analysis of the kinetic model for these low H2 pressure conditions, which again begins with the rate dependence of eq from the Langmuir–Hinshelwood model. The Ru surface is covered with hydrocarbon polymer, and the surface coverage (θDP) is assumed to be constant as H2 pressure is varied (just as the θH was constant in the high H2 pressure range). Rearranging eq to write θH in terms of P H2 leads to θH∝(PH2)γ/β+1/2 . Under these conditions, the rate is proportional to P H2 as given in eq : rC−Ccuts∝{θH}ξPH2−ξ/2∝{(PH2)γ/β+1/2}ξPH2−ξ/2∝PH2γξ/β 7

This kinetic model shows that under constant (high) hydrocarbon coverage, the reaction order in P H2 is positive, as experimentally observed for both mSiO2/Ru/rGO and Ru/C. Note that the analysis shows that the positive order in H2 comes from the free site coverage increasing with hydrogen pressure in this regime, rather than H2 addition to the surface or hydrocarbyl species prior to or as part of the turnover-limiting step (note: r C–Ccuts ∝ k θDP(θ*) ξ ∝ (θ*) ξ and Le Chatelier principle for eq shows that θ*∝PH2γ/β leading again to eq ).

This model is then used to analyze the different reaction orders arising from mSiO2/Ru/rGO and Ru/C. H2 extrusion (γ) and the additional sites needed to bond to the hydrocarbyl fragments (ξ) from C–C bond cleavage are intrinsic to the reaction and likely to be similar for both Ru/C and mSiO2/Ru/rGO. The most likely difference in the H2 order for the two catalysts is therefore attributed to the sites occupied upon adsorption of the specific dehydrogenated and saturated polymer chain segments onto the active metal surface (β). Because β is in the denominator of the H2 pressure order ( γξ/β ) in eq , a higher β corresponds to a lower reaction order and a smaller β gives a higher reaction order. The smaller β would also provide more space for unsaturated “cleavable” hydrocarbon segments to adsorb onto Ru. The pore and especially the silica pore walls in mSiO2/Ru/rGO contribute to chain adsorption but do not contribute to β because H2 does not undergo dissociative adsorption on silica.

On this basis, we interpret the lower reaction order in H2 for Ru/C as reflecting each chain occupying more Ru sites than polymers undergoing cleavage in mSiO2/Ru/rGO (compare IA and IB in Figure ). Conversely, the larger reaction order in H2 for mSiO2/Ru/rGO implies that its Ru sites are more efficient in cleaving C–C bonds than those in Ru/C. Thus, the analysis also provides a rationale for the higher C–C bond cleavage activity of mSiO2/Ru/rGO (higher νcuts and faster TOF).

We also note that high H2 reaction orders are typically not observed in small alkane hydrogenolysis because species containing multiple double bonds (e.g., alkatrienes) cannot be formed. This behavior is fundamentally different from polymer hydrogenolysis at low H2 pressure, where products containing multiple double bonds are commonly observed. Accordingly, the parameter γ in eq is likely larger for polymer hydrogenolysis than for small alkane hydrogenolysis and large positive H2 reaction orders are possible.

Selectivity in mSiO2/Ru/rGO-Catalyzed Hydrogenolysis of LDPE

The high selectivity of mSiO2/Ru/rGO for wax products, defined as mass of wax divided by mass of converted polymer, partially contributes to the high νwax. This selectivity is relatively insensitive to variation of H2 pressure, polymer conversion, or temperature; however, selectivity decreases slightly at higher temperature, and significantly at high conversion with lower H2 partial pressure. Waxes are formed with >87% selectivity up to 70% polymer conversion at 250 °C and 19 bar initial H2 partial pressure, but selectivity decreases to 81% at higher polymer conversion, due to a ∼50% decrease in H2 partial pressure. At 37 bar, a slightly higher wax selectivity of 91% is achieved at 75% conversion (Table S12). Because n cuts is more sensitive to gas formation than wax yield, the effect of hydrogen pressure on product selectivity is better highlighted by comparing the mass of wax produced per mole of C–C bonds cleaved (S n). The S n was 65 and 101 gwax·molC–C –1 for mSiO2/Ru/rGO at 250 °C under 19 and 37 bar of H2, respectively. At 230 °C and 37 bar, wax selectivity and S n further increase to 94% and 120 gwax·molC–C –1, respectively. The similar selectivity in experiments at 200 °C and 37 bar (94% wax selectivity) however, limited further study of low temperature conditions.

For comparison, Ru/rGO gives only ∼54% selectivity for condensed-phase hydrocarbons (Figure ) and S n of 20 gwax·molC–C –1 at 65% polymer conversion (19 bar, 250 °C). The higher S n of mSiO2/Ru/rGO also indicates a more efficient use of H2. Only half the H2 (26 mmol) is needed to deconstruct ∼3 g LDPE using mSiO2/Ru/rGO compared to Ru/rGO (50 mmol). Ru/C also has poor wax yield (9%) and poor selectivity (75%) under these conditions, even at only 13% polymer conversion where wax hydrogenolysis should be insignificant (Tables S4 and S5). Lower temperature results in higher wax selectivity for Ru/C. Thus, the wax selectivity for Ru/C improves to ∼90% at 230 °C and increases slightly as H2 pressure increases from 10 to 55 bar (Table S9). The Ru/C wax selectivity decreased, however, at full polymer conversion (e.g., 73% at 230 °C, 28 bar).

Pore encapsulation leads to higher wax selectivity at equivalent PE conversion. Conditions: 250 °C, 19 bar H2, and 0.015 wt % Ru with respect to LDPE. See Table S13.

We also note that the molecular weight distribution (MWD) of the liquid and/or wax products of platinum catalysts is affected by mesopore confinement, localization, and pore geometry. ,, The hydrocarbon products from the short, closed-at-the-bottom mesopores in mSiO2/Ru/rGO provided a distribution centered at ∼C20, which results from mid-chain polymer cleavage and increases in yield as the reaction proceeds. At low conversion, however, the hydrocarbon distribution tails to include longer chains (up to ca. C50 are observed by GC-FID). The longer chains are consumed at high conversion (≥80%) to provide a symmetrical, C19-centered distribution (see Figure S236). Thus, the mode of the mSiO2/Ru/rGO catalyst distribution corresponds to lower C n as the reaction consumes more polymer. Larger proportions of longer chain intermediates are observed with Ru/rGO, Ru/C, and Ru@mSiO2, but these species also diminish as conversion increases. Likely, the free energy of long polymer chain adsorption in the short, 2.9 nm-diameter mesopores of mSiO2/Ru/rGO is not very different from that of the pores of Ru@mSiO2 or the surface of Ru/C or Ru/rGO, allowing the formation of >C30-length intermediates.

The other products from LDPE hydrogenolysis are gases, and Ru/rGO produces ca. 3.4× more gas (29.4% wt %) than the pore-localized catalyst (8.4 wt %) at ∼65% conversion (Figure ). As noted in the Introduction, methane forms via cleavage of terminal M-CR intermediates, which are generated either from dehydrogenation of a polymer chain end or from a previous C–C bond cleavage step in a cascading polymer chain deconstruction. Methane is, by far, the largest component of the gaseous products from Ru-based catalysts, accounting for ∼70% of the gases produced by both Ru/rGO and mSiO2/Ru/rGO. For this reason, the gas and methane selectivity are correlated, and methane selectivity is also significantly influenced by the Ru catalyst architecture. At higher (37 bar) H2 pressure, the methane selectivity decreases (i.e., improves) with mSiO2/Ru/rGO, giving 3.2 wt % methane yield at 57% polymer conversion. The methane yield is even lower at 230 °C (2.2 wt % CH4 at 53% LDPE conversion). Methane selectivity increases at higher conversion and at low H2 partial pressure, for example, up to 13% methane yield at 75% LDPE conversion at 250 °C (at a final H2 pressure of 10 bar). Even at the point of the experiment when waxes are undergoing over-hydrogenolysis, the total gas formation catalyzed by mSiO2/Ru/rGO is still relatively low (16%). The 6% methane yield from mSiO2/Ru/rGO, with 2.9 nm diameter mesopores, is ca. 30% that of Ru/rGO under equivalent conditions of Figure .

The mSiO2/Ru/rGO catalyst with narrower (2.3 nm) diameter pores gives similar total gas formation (by mass) as the material with 2.9 nm diameter pores studied throughout this work (at similar polymer conversion of ∼60% after 5 h at 250 °C under 19 bar H2). The methane yield from the narrow-pore catalyst, however, is 67% of that produced by the 2.9 nm pore diameter catalyst (Table S17). A smaller fraction of the chain cleavage events inside narrower pores leads to gaseous products compared to inside 2.9 nm pores. Thus, under these LDPE hydrogenolysis conditions, the wax selectivity improves from 88% to 92% and the S n improves from 60 to 85 gwax·molC–C –1 with the narrow-pore catalyst. This higher selectivity is perhaps even more notable given that the sharper distribution (Đ = 1.1) formed in the narrow pores is on average shorter (M n = 248 Da, corresponding to C17) than the broad distribution (Đ = 1.4) and longer chains (M n = 324 Da, corresponding to C23) that tail up to C50 produced by the 2.9 nm pore catalyst (Figure ). While this distribution-tightening phenomenon is consistent with previous observations from mSiO2/Pt/SiO2 catalyst architectures with narrow (yet long) pores, concurrently narrowed chain length distribution and lower methane yield here further suggest the outsized influence of polymer adsorption within mesopores on reaction mechanism. Specifically, narrower pores lead to elongated chain conformations that induce C–C bond cleavage steps closer to the polymer chain end, but not at the terminal position.

A narrow pore diameter leads to sharper wax distribution and higher selectivity. Conditions: 250 °C, 5 h, 19 bar H2, and 0.015 wt % Ru with respect to LDPE. See Table S17.

Because H2 pressure affects the rate of C–C bond cleavage, it could also affect the methane yield. Methane formation, normalized by polymer conversion, decreases as H2 pressure increases to 37 bar; in the higher H2 pressure regime from 27 to 55 bar, methane formation barely decreases as H2 pressure increases (Figure S83). The inhibition of methane formation at high H2 pressures or at high H surface coverages in polyolefin hydrogenolysis catalyzed by supported Ru catalysts has been previously noted. ,,,

The methane formation pathway is a dominating feature of most Ru catalysts, especially at lower H2 pressures. As the H2 pressure is lowered in the low H2 partial pressure regime with mSiO2/Ru/rGO, an increase in unwanted production of methane coincides with a decrease in the νcuts. This apparent relationship, along with the different reaction order in P H2 noted above for mSiO2/Ru/rGO vs Ru/C and the generally higher rate of C–C bond cleavage with mSiO2/Ru/rGO, suggests that the C–C bond cleavage mechanism of Ru (i.e., the chemistry of the Ru active site itself) is perturbed by pore confinement. At high H2 partial pressure, where methane formation is suppressed for Ru catalysts in general, ,,, the more favorable adsorption of polymer chains in pores most likely also suppresses mSiO2/Ru/rGO-catalyzed over-hydrogenolysis, as observed with mSiO2/Pt/SiO2.

These effects uncover a substantial difference between Pt and Ru NPs localized inside pores compared to the NPs on a support for LDPE hydrogenolysis. In contrast to the increased activity of Ru sites and active site selectivity for mid-chain cleavage as a result of pore confinement discussed above, the primary effect of the mesoporous architecture on Pt-catalyzed polyolefin hydrogenolysis is more favorable adsorption of longer polymers over shorter chains to increase selectivity, which limits hydrogenolysis of wax products (termed over-hydrogenolysis). , The nonconfined Pt catalysts have only a statistical bias toward C–C bond cleavage of longer chains, leading to conversion-dependent wax selectivity and gas formation. Confinement of Pt in pores, however, does not substantially increase νcuts.

Catalytic Reusability

Additional evidence that catalyst deactivation is not responsible for slower C–C bond cleavage by mSiO2/Ru/rGO at longer reaction times in Figure a comes from catalyst recycling tests. The reusability of mSiO2/Ru/rGO was investigated under conditions chosen to ensure maximum polymer conversion into waxes, so that the catalyst could be isolated and reused in subsequent experiments without any additional steps, such as washing, or reductive or oxidative treatments. At 28 bar and 250 °C, mSiO2/Ru/rGO catalyzes the complete conversion of the LDPE within 10 h to give >80% yield of wax. While these conditions result in some over-hydrogenolysis, the equivalent wax yields and n cuts over six consecutive cycles (Figure ) demonstrate that the slowing catalysis at high conversion at 19 bar is not from catalyst deactivation and that complete polymer conversion is readily obtained with mSiO2/Ru/rGO. Most importantly, mSiO2/Ru/rGO exhibits outstanding reusability, in terms of the number of hydrogenolyzed C–C bonds and the wax yields after 10 h. Each experiment, run to high polymer conversion, produces waxes with a similar narrow C20-centered distribution.

Reusability of mSiO2/Ru/rGO for catalytic hydrogenolysis of LDPE (M n = 3.2 kDa). Conditions: 250 °C, 28 bar H2, 550 rpm, 10 h, and 0.015 wt % Ru with respect to LDPE. See Table S15.

After the fifth experiment, the catalyst was isolated and analyzed by TEM, which revealed 2.1 ± 0.2 nm Ru NPs (Figure S257) that matched those of mSiO2/Ru/rGO before any hydrogenolysis experiments. In contrast, Ru NPs in nonporous Ru/C increase in size after hydrogenolysis reactions. , The robust catalyst structure further differentiates the mSiO2/Ru/rGO from other supported Ru nanoparticle hydrogenolysis catalysts. A similar contrast between Pt nanoparticle catalysts embedded in a mesoporous silica architecture with nonporous Pt/SiO2 ,,, further suggests that introduction of a mesoporous silica coating can generally embody robustness upon nanoparticle hydrogenolysis catalysts.

Polyolefin Reactants

The high rate of mSiO2/Ru/rGO for hydrogenolysis of low molecular weight LDPE (M n = 3.2 kDa) studied in detail above is also achieved for higher molecular weight LDPE samples. At 19 bar and 250 °C, the (7 h-averaged) apparent TOFs achieved for M n of 13.0 kDa and 19.0 kDa LDPE are 59 ± 3 and 51 ± 3 min–1, respectively, compared to 62 ± 3 min–1 for the low molecular weight polymer (Table S16). Under the lower H2 pressure conditions, the wax selectivity is poorer, and more methane is formed from these samples with higher M n. As a result, the νwax is also slightly lower (e.g., 380 gwax·gRu –1·h–1).

The time-averaged νwax and TOF for hydrogenolysis of HDPE (M n = 42.0 kDa) of 295 gwax·gRu –1·h–1 and 41 ± 3 min–1, respectively, at 19 bar H2 are slightly reduced compared to the LDPE samples, however, wax, gas, and methane selectivities are similar for linear and branched polyethylene. For example, HDPE hydrogenolysis using mSiO2/Ru/rGO gives 81:19 wax:gas at 45% conversion, the gas product contains 70% methane, and most wax products are linear.

The 16 ± 1 min–1 TOF of mSiO2/Ru/rGO for hydrogenolysis of isotactic polypropylene (iPP, M n = 5.0 kDa) is lower than that of polyethylene. mSiO2/Ru/rGO is still faster for iPP hydrogenolysis than bulk-facing catalysts. For example, 0.5% Ru/C gives a TOF of 0.2 min–1 (at 250 °C, 30 bar). The mSiO2/Ru/rGO-catalyzed iPP hydrogenolysis is also much more selective, giving soluble hydrocarbon products in 95% selectivity and producing only 0.6% CH4 (Table S16). 13C­{1H} NMR analysis of the wax product indicated that the stereocenters of isotactic chains had been epimerized (Figures S274–S277). iPP hydrogenolysis catalyzed by Ru on oxides often generates high quantities of methane, sometimes even greater than 50 wt % (Table S18). , Despite the slower C–C bond cleavage rate, the νwax of 325 gwax·gRu –1·h–1 for hydrogenolysis of iPP using mSiO2/Ru/rGO is slightly higher than that of HDPE. Deconstruction of LLDPE packaging films (M n = 13.0 kDa) into wax, however, requires 96 h at 300 °C under 28 bar hydrogen. These forcing conditions afford a TOF of 2.3 ± 0.2 min–1. Experiments using LLDPE at lower temperatures results in the liner rotating with the mechanical stirrer in the reactor, leading to inefficient mixing (Table S16).

Conclusions

The synthesis of mSiO2/Ru/rGO, as a graphene oxide-supported catalyst having 2 nm Ru NPs encapsulated at the bottom of mesoporous silica pores, created a well-defined platform to investigate the effects of architecture on Ru-catalyzed hydrogenolysis of polyolefins and has led to new insights into the mechanism of C–C bond cleavage in macromolecules. The mesoporous architecture yields extremely high wax formation and polyethylene deconstruction rates driven by high selectivity for mid-chain cleavage. Methane formation is also suppressed compared to catalysts with similarly sized and supported Ru NPs, and only single-atom (0.2 wt %) Ru/CeO2 produces less methane than mSiO2/Ru/rGO. The mesoporous silica-coated Ru catalyst with a narrower pore diameter generates even less methane than the standard mSiO2/Ru/rGO catalyst, achieves higher LDPE conversion under equivalent conditions, and affords higher selectivity for a sharper C17-centered distribution of wax products. This higher selectivity with 2.3 nm diameter-pore catalyst leads to a greater νwax, even though νcuts and TOF are equivalent for the two mesoporous silica-coated Ru catalysts. In addition, a survey of high molecular weight polymers of LDPE, HDPE, iPP and even formulated plastic films used for food packaging containing LLDPE suggests that the effect of pores, to improve selectivity and activity of Ru NPs, can be broadly applied in polyolefin deconstructions, over many catalyst reuse cycles.

Analysis of both νwax and TOF proved essential to understand the basis for high activity of mSiO2/Ru/rGO for polyolefin hydrogenolysis. The effects can be separated into higher selectivity for wax production by the pore-confined catalyst, as previously observed with pore-encapsulated Pt catalysts, and an increase in νcuts that appears to be unique to the Ru active site. That is, the rate of C–C bond cleavage, which occurs only at the Ru nanoparticles, increases when Ru nanoparticles are confined in narrow mesopores.

H2 pressure affects the kinetics of C–C bond cleavage on ruthenium nanoparticle catalysts, revealing two domains of the volcano-like behavior. The negative order in H2 in the high-pressure domain is similar for Ru/C and mSiO2/Ru/rGO. This pressure dependence follows typical behavior of gas–solid Ru-catalyzed hydrogenolysis, indicating rate-controlling C–C bond cleavage. The low-pressure domain shows a positive order in H2 pressure, following the order Ru/C < mSiO2/Ru/rGO. This difference reflects each adsorbed polymer chain occupying more Ru sites on the external-surface facing Ru catalyst, and fewer Ru sites occupied by each polymer chain adsorbed on Ru in mSiO2/Ru/rGO. The mesoporous silica coating and pores in this catalyst allow polymer adsorption to partially occur on the walls of pores, thereby modulating the chain conformation and active site binding configuration to require less Ru surface. We suggest that fewer free Ru sites needed for catalysis inside the pore structure, as reflected by a smaller β, leads to a faster C–C bond hydrogenolysis rate over the mSiO2/Ru/rGO catalyst relative to Ru/C.

H2 pressure affects methane formation as well. In most Ru catalysts for polyolefin hydrogenolysis, increasing pressure lowers methane formation. , High methane yield on Ru is typically attributed to cascading C–C bond cleavage, where Ru hydrocarbyl intermediates undergo further dehydrogenation and cleavage. The H2 pressure effect has previously been interpreted as H on the Ru (at high surface coverage) intercepting the cascade and disfavoring dehydrogenation. The proposed role of H2 in this cascade mechanism is not consistent with our observations of rate and methane selectivity for mSiO2/Ru/rGO. In the high H2 pressure region where the C–C bond cleavage is slowed as H2 increases (inverse H2 dependence), the yield of methane is constant (i.e., not decreased) with increasing H2 pressure, in conflict with the prediction of the role of H2 in the methane cascade mechanism. That interpretation is also inconsistent with the positive H2 reaction order in the low pressure regime, where increasing partial pressure increases the bond cleavage rate while decreasing methane formation.

Instead, we suggest that the higher number of metal sites on Ru/C required to adsorb longer segments of each polyolefin chain leads to cascade methane formation, and that this adsorption mode is disfavored on Ru sites in pores or with high H surface coverages. The lower methane yield and tighter distribution of chain lengths generated by Ru coated with narrower diameter mesopores support this idea that polymer adsorption on inert silica walls of the mesopores governs both the interaction of long chains with the active Ru sites and the upright conformation of chains within the pores. Because a single polymer chain adsorbed on more of the Ru surface results in slower C–C bond cleavage, this analysis accounts for the effect of H2 pressure on both rate and methane selectivity.
