Circadian syndrome and its components in relation to incident gastroesophageal reflux disease: a prospective cohort study from the UK Biobank
Authors: He Lu, Zhiyu Wu, Xingyan Zhou, Wenwen Yang, Yuyu Lv, Jing Shi, Ya Zheng, Rui Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCircadian Syndrome (CircS), a cluster encompassing metabolic dysregulation, short sleep duration, and depression, has been implicated in various chronic diseases. Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a highly prevalent digestive disorder; however, its prospective association with CircS remains unclear.MethodsThis prospective cohort study utilized data from the UK Biobank, including 330,925 participants free of GERD at baseline. CircS was defined as meeting at least four of seven components. The primary outcome was incident GERD, identified through the first recorded diagnosis code from linked hospital, primary care, death registry, and self-report data. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios with sequential adjustments for demographics, socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, and medication use.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 13.05 years, 28,554 incident GERD cases were documented. In the fully adjusted model, CircS was associated with a significantly increased risk of GERD (HR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.16–1.23). All seven individual components were each associated with GERD risk in separate fully adjusted models, with the strongest associations observed for depression (HR = 1.38) and short sleep (HR = 1.29). A significant dose-response relationship was evident as the number of components increased. Subgroup analyses revealed stronger associations among individuals aged under 65 years, women, non-White participants, and those not using proton pump inhibitors.ConclusionsCircadian Syndrome is independently associated with incident GERD, with depression and short sleep as core driving components. These findings support the integration of circadian, metabolic, and psychological health assessments into GERD risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28206-2.Keywords: CircS, GERD, Prospective Cohort, Sleep Deprivation, Depression

Introduction

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a common chronic digestive disorder worldwide. Its characteristic symptoms of heartburn and regurgitation significantly impair quality of life and constitute well-established risk factors for serious complications, including esophagitis, Barrett’s esophagus, and esophageal adenocarcinoma [1]. While traditional etiological models of GERD have predominantly centered on local mechanical factors and specific lifestyle choices, recent research trends suggest a broadening of perspectives [2]. This shift emphasizes the potential role of systemic metabolic dysregulation and neuroendocrine imbalances as pivotal contributors to GERD pathogenesis. Accumulating evidence now suggests that GERD may not merely be a localized disorder but rather a clinical manifestation of complex interactions involving systemic metabolic dysregulation, neuroendocrine imbalances, and psychological factors [3–5]. This paradigm shift drives the search for integrative risk markers that better capture such systemic dysregulation.

Against this backdrop, supports Circadian Syndrome (CircS) has emerged as an integrative concept of interest. Expanding upon the classic metabolic syndrome (MetS), CircS incorporates the core metabolic components while adding two key elements intrinsically linked to circadian rhythms and mental health: short sleep duration and depression [6]. This integration is well-founded. Physiological processes are orchestrated by the internal biological clock, which coordinates fundamental rhythms [7]. Yet, features of modern life are precipitating widespread chronic circadian disruption [6]. Such persistent internal misalignment is recognized as a key driver behind the epidemic of metabolic, cardiovascular, and mood disorders [8]. Intriguingly, initial research suggests that CircS may outperform traditional MetS in predicting risks for cardiovascular and renal diseases, likely because it more holistically captures the clinical reality of co-existing abnormalities [9–11].

From a pathophysiological perspective, plausible multi-faceted links exist between CircS and GERD. Its components could contribute through synergistic or parallel pathways. The metabolic components primarily act on structural and functional levels [12]. In contrast, the circadian and neuropsychiatric components likely exert their influence via neuroendocrine and gut-brain axis pathways [13]. Furthermore, disruption of core clock genes can exacerbate metabolic dysfunction and inflammation, creating a vicious cycle [14].

While individual CircS components have been previously linked to GERD, the prospective association of the clustered CircS phenotype remains unexamined. Despite these plausible biological links, direct evidence from large-scale prospective studies is lacking. To address this gap, we conducted this study to quantify the magnitude of the association between CircS and incident GERD, analyze risk gradients, and identify high-risk population characteristics. We hypothesized that CircS is positively associated with an increased risk of incident GERD, and that this association exhibits a dose-response pattern and may vary across different population subgroups.

Methods

Study design and data source

This prospective cohort study utilized data from the UK Biobank, a large population-based study that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years from 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010. Participants provided extensive baseline data through touchscreen questionnaires, physical measurements, and biological samples. Health outcomes are tracked through linked hospital inpatient records, primary care data, and death registries. Details of the UK Biobank have been described elsewhere [15]. The study received ethical approval from the Northwest Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent. Data access was approved under UK Biobank application number 456,996. Follow-up for the current study continued until May 31, 2022, with the baseline assessment date defined using Field ID 53 (date of attending assessment centre instance 0).

Study population

From the initial UK Biobank cohort (N = 501,962), we first excluded [n = 46,991] participants with a prevalent diagnosis of peptic ulcer disease or GERD at baseline (identified through self-report or linked health records), resulting in [N1 =454,971] participants. To minimize potential reverse causation, we further excluded [n = 4,261] participants who developed incident GERD within the first two years of follow-up, yielding an intermediate cohort of [N2 =450,710] participants. We then excluded 119,785 participants with missing data on any component of CircS. The resulting cohort of 330,925 participants served as the baseline for covariate imputation and subsequent analysis. A detailed flowchart of participant selection is provided in Fig. 1.

Outcome

The primary outcome was incident GERD, defined as the first recorded diagnosis of gastroesophageal reflux disease (ICD-10 code K21) following the baseline assessment. This information was obtained from the UK Biobank’s “First occurrence” data-fields within Category 1712, specifically Field ID 131,584: Date K21 first reported (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease), which provides the earliest recorded date of diagnosis across all linked data sources including hospital inpatient records, primary care data, death registries, and participant self-report. Follow-up time was calculated from the baseline assessment date (Field ID 53) to the earliest of: incident GERD diagnosis, death (Field ID 40000), loss to follow-up, or the administrative censoring date (May 31, 2022).

Covariates

A comprehensive set of covariates, considered potential confounders in the association between CircS and incident GERD, were included in the analyses. Age was analyzed as a binary variable (under 65 years vs. 65 and over), with this cut-off chosen based on current European clinical guidelines [16]. Sex was categorized as female or male, and ethnicity as White or Non-White. Socioeconomic status was assessed using the Townsend Deprivation Index (analyzed as quartiles), household income (<£18k, £18-30k, £31-51k, £52-100k, or >£100k), employment status (employed or unemployed), and education level (low, medium, or high). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (smoker vs. non-smoker) [17], alcohol drinking status (never, former, or current drinker), physical activity, diet quality. Body mass index (BMI) was measured at baseline but was not included in the main Cox regression models (it was used only for descriptive purposes and as a stratification variable; see Table 1; Figs. 3, 4 and 6). Physical activity was quantified using the summed MET minutes per week for all activity, with participants categorized into four levels: Inactive (0 MET-minutes/week), Low (< 600 MET-minutes/week), Moderate (600–3000 MET-minutes/week), and High (> 3000 MET-minutes/week). Diet quality was assessed using a diet score ranging from 0 to 5, with one point awarded for meeting each of the following criteria based on self-reported food frequency: vegetable intake ≥ 3 servings/day, fruit intake ≥ 3 servings/day, fish intake ≥ 2 servings/week, unprocessed red meat intake ≤ 2 servings/week, and processed meat intake ≤ 1 serving/week [10, 18]. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) / height (m²) and categorized into underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²). Finally, self-reported use of PPIs and NSAIDs at baseline were recorded as binary variables (Yes/No) [19, 20].

Missing data and multiple imputation

Missingness was significantly associated with age, sex, and CircS status (all P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 1), supporting the missing at random (MAR) assumption. Missing data were present only among covariates and were handled using multiple imputation. Exposure variables (CircS and its seven components) and outcome variables (GERD status and follow-up time) were complete and were included as predictors in the imputation models. The following covariates were imputed (missingness rates shown): physical activity (21.8%), education level (16.8%), household income (14.0%), diet score (3.9%), smoking status (1.5%), employment status (0.4%), ethnicity (0.3%), BMI (0.2%), Townsend Deprivation Index (0.2%), and alcohol drinking status (0.1%) (Supplementary Table 2). Multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was used to generate five complete datasets, with imputation models tailored to variable type: logistic regression for binary variables, proportional odds logistic regression for ordered categorical variables, and predictive mean matching for continuous variables. All covariates, exposure variables, and the survival outcome (time-to-event and GERD status) were included in the imputation procedure. Imputation was performed with 20 iterations to ensure convergence, and a random seed (20250120) was set for reproducibility. All subsequent statistical analyses were conducted separately on each imputed dataset, and results were pooled using Rubin’s rules.

Statistical analysis

Main analysis

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between CircS and incident GERD, with follow-up time in years as the time scale. Three hierarchical models were specified: Model 1 (crude, unadjusted); Model 2 (adjusted for demographic factors, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle factors: age, sex, ethnicity, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, diet score, income, employment status, education level, and Townsend Deprivation Index); and Model 3 (fully adjusted, further adjusted for medication use: PPIs and NSAIDs). The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. For covariates that violated the assumption (age and smoking), a sensitivity analysis using stratified Cox models with these variables as stratification variables was performed.

Dose-response and component analysis

To examine dose-response relationships, participants were categorized by the number of CircS components (0–3, 4, 5, 6, and 7), with hazard ratios estimated from Model 3 using the 0–3 group as reference. Each of the seven individual CircS components was also examined separately in Model 3 to identify key drivers of the association.

Subgroup analysis and effect modification

Predefined subgroup analyses were conducted across 13 strata (age, sex, ethnicity, BMI, smoking, alcohol, physical activity, education, income, Townsend Deprivation Index, employment status, PPIs use, NSAIDs use). Interaction terms were introduced to test for effect modification, with P-values derived from likelihood ratio tests. Only subgroups with statistically significant interactions (P < 0.05) are presented in the forest plot; complete results for all 13 strata are available in (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the findings, including: (1) a complete-case analysis excluding participants with missing covariates or self-reported GERD; (2) an analysis using an alternative definition of short sleep (< 7 h) to redefine CircS; and (3) stratified Cox models to address violation of the proportional hazards assumption by age and smoking. Details of these analyses are provided in the Results section and Supplementary Materials.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0), and a two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Among the 330,925 participants included in the final analysis, 43,221 (13.1%) were classified as CircS-present, while 287,704 (86.9%) were classified as CircS-absent. Statistically significant differences were observed across all measured baseline characteristics between the two groups (all P -values < 0.001, see Table 1). Percentages for categorical variables were calculated based on non-missing cases, with missing categories excluded from percentage calculations but their frequencies reported separately. Compared to the CircS-absent group, participants in the CircS-present group were older, with a higher proportion aged ≥ 65 years (23.5% vs. 17.6%) and had a higher percentage of males (48.2% vs. 45.7%). Moreover, the prevalence of obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) was markedly higher in the CircS-present group (65.4% vs. 17.0%). This group also exhibited lower levels of physical activity and a greater degree of socioeconomic deprivation (e.g., proportion in the most deprived TDI (Townsend Deprivation Index) quantile: 32.4% vs. 24.1%; unemployment rate: 20.0% vs. 14.5%). Notably, the proportion of participants who developed GERD was significantly higher in the CircS-present (11.6%) than in the absent group (8.2%), providing preliminary evidence for a potential link between CircS and GERD. (see Table 1)

Association of CircS overall with incident GERD

During a median follow-up of 13.05 years, 28,554 incident GERD cases were documented. Cox regression analysis across hierarchically adjusted models revealed the following findings. In Model 1 (Crude), individuals in the CircS-present group exhibited a significantly elevated risk of GERD compared to the CircS-absent group (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.45–1.54). This association was attenuated yet remained significant after adjustment for demographic, lifestyle, and socioeconomic covariates (Model 2; HR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.29–1.37). Crucially, in the fully adjusted model (Model 3), which additionally accounted for medication use (PPIs and NSAIDs), the CircS-present group still showed a significant 20% higher risk of GERD (HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.16–1.23, P < 0.001). The persistence of this association after comprehensive adjustment supports CircS as an independent risk marker for GERD. (Table 2)

Association of individual CircS components with incident GERD

We examined each CircS component individually in seven separate fully adjusted models (adjusted for all covariates in Model 3). The components were ranked by the magnitude of their hazard ratios as follows: Depression (HR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.33–1.43) > Short Sleep (HR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.23–1.35) > Elevated Waist Circumference (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.19–1.25) > Elevated triglycerides (HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.14–1.19) > Low HDL-cholesterol (HR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.07–1.14) > Elevated blood pressure (HR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.10) > Hyperglycemia (HR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.03–1.09) (Fig. 3).

Dose-response relationship

A significant positive dose-response relationship was observed between the number of CircS components and GERD risk (P for trend < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model, using the group not meeting the CircS criteria (0–3 components) as the reference, the hazard ratios for GERD were 1.18 (95% CI: 1.14–1.22) for those with 4 components, 1.21 (95% CI: 1.14–1.28) for those with 5 components, and 1.39 (95% CI: 1.23–1.58) for those with 6 components, demonstrating a clear stepwise increase in risk (Fig. 4). Although participants with all 7 components also had an elevated HR of 1.48, it did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.083), which is likely attributable to the limited statistical power in this small subgroup (Events = 20) rather than indicating a true absence of association.

The GERD-free survival curves stratified by the number of CircS components (Fig. 5) further corroborated this dose-response pattern. The rate of curve decline accelerated progressively with an increasing number of components, indicating a stepwise rise in cumulative incidence risk (log-rank test P < 0.001). The curve for individuals with 7 components showed the highest cumulative risk. This pattern supports the hypothesis of a “cumulative biological burden” from multiple components and aligns with research suggesting that combined circadian disruption and metabolic abnormalities exacerbate health risks.

Subgroup analysis findings

Subgroup analyses revealed substantial heterogeneity in the association between CircS and incident GERD across several demographic and clinical characteristics (Fig. 6). The association was significantly stronger among younger participants (< 65 years: HR.

= 1.24, 95% CI: 1.20–1.29) compared to those aged ≥ 65 years (HR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.14; P for interaction < 0.001). A pronounced sex difference was also evident, with women exhibiting a higher risk (HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.26–1.37) than men (HR.

= 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00–1.11; P for interaction < 0.001). Furthermore, the association was more pronounced in Non-White participants (HR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.18–1.53) compared to White participants (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.15–1.22; P for interaction = 0.042).

Effect modification was also observed across socioeconomic and medication-use strata. The association between CircS and GERD varied by household income (P for interaction = 0.013), with the strongest point estimate observed in the highest income group (>£100k: HR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.11–1.72). Unemployed individuals demonstrated a higher risk (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.19–1.36) than those employed (HR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.13–1.22; P for interaction = 0.021). Regarding medication use, the association was attenuated among participants using proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.05–1.21) compared to non-users (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.18–1.26; P for interaction < 0.001), and similarly attenuated among non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAIDs) users (HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.11–1.23) compared to non-users (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.17–1.27; P for interaction = 0.002).

In contrast, no significant effect modification was observed for smoking status, drinking behavior, physical activity level, or area deprivation index (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating consistent associations across these strata.

Sensitivity analysis and assessment of selection bias

To evaluate the robustness of our primary findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses addressing missing covariate data, outcome misclassification, alternative definition of short sleep, and the use of stratified Cox models to account for violation of the proportional hazards assumption.

Sensitivity analysis for missing data and outcome definition

From the intermediate cohort of 450,710 participants (Fig. 1), we excluded those with missing data on any covariate and those whose GERD diagnosis was based solely on or partially on self-report. This yielded a cohort of 196,614 participants with complete data, among whom 13,627 incident GERD cases were identified through hospital records, primary care data, or death registries. As shown in Supplementary Table 3, the association remained highly consistent with the primary analysis. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), CircS positivity was associated with a 21% increased risk of GERD (HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.15–1.27), virtually identical to the HR of 1.20 in the main analysis (difference < 1%). The dose-response pattern persisted (P for trend < 0.001), and depression (HR = 1.40) and short sleep (HR = 1.32) remained the strongest individual components (see Supplementary Fig. 2). A significant dose-response relationship was also observed, with HRs increasing from 1.18 (4 components) to 1.49 (6 components) (P for trend < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings demonstrate that our results are robust to missing data imputation and potential self-report bias. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis using an alternative definition of short sleep (< 7 h per night) to redefine CircS yielded consistent findings (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.18–1.25, P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 4).

Assessment of selection bias

Compared with excluded participants (n = 119,785), included participants (n = 330,925) were generally similar across most baseline characteristics (Supplementary Table 5). The standardized mean differences (SMDs) were below 0.1 for most variables, indicating negligible differences between groups. Slightly larger differences (SMD between 0.1 and 0.2) were observed for BMI (SMD = 0.163) and household income (SMD = 0.124), suggesting small but acceptable imbalances. These findings indicate that selection bias due to exclusion of participants with missing CircS components is unlikely to substantially influence our conclusions.

Sensitivity analysis for proportional hazards assumption

The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for CircS but violated for age and smoking. Stratified Cox models yielded hazard ratios for CircS ranging from 1.194 to 1.197, which were virtually unchanged from the main analysis. (Supplementary Table 6, Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this large-scale prospective UK Biobank study, we identified Circadian Syndrome (CircS) is significantly associated with incident GERD. After full adjustment, individuals with CircS had a significantly higher risk, and a clear dose-response pattern was evident, with risk increasing alongside the number of components. Notably, among the seven components, depression and short sleep duration—the two non-metabolic components, exhibited the strongest independent associations. These findings suggest that circadian and mood-related factors may contribute to GERD susceptibility, extending beyond traditional metabolic risk factors.

Our component-level analysis provides additional insights into the differential strengths of association across CircS components. We found that not all factors showed equivalent associations with GERD. Depression and short sleep stood out — their links to GERD were much stronger than the traditional metabolic ones. Since these two are core to Circadian Syndrome and tied to the body’s clock, it really points to circadian and neuropsychiatric disruption being a key driver of GERD [9, 11]. This finding aligns with prior evidence from Mendelian randomization studies suggesting a potential causal relationship between depression and GERD [21]. Depression likely exerts its influence through alterations in the gut-brain axis, affecting both esophageal motility and visceral pain perception [22]. Sleep deprivation and GERD form a bidirectional vicious cycle: nighttime reflux disrupts sleep, while sleep deprivation lowers esophageal pain thresholds and impairs esophageal clearance, heightening acid sensitivity [23–25]. This bidirectional relationship underscores the clinical importance of addressing sleep quality in GERD management.

Complementing these pathways, molecular circadian clock dysregulation within the gastrointestinal tract itself may influence patterns of acid secretion and mucosal defense, providing a potential biological pathway linking circadian disruption to GERD susceptibility [26].

The metabolic components of CircS operate through distinct but synergistic pathways. Abdominal obesity promotes reflux primarily through mechanical effects via increased intra-abdominal pressure [2, 3], while also contributing to systemic inflammation through adipokine dysregulation that may compromise esophageal mucosal integrity [27–29]. Hypertriglyceridemia likely contributes through multiple mechanisms: postprandial lipemia delays gastric emptying, increases intragastric pressure, and modulates lower esophageal sphincter tone via cholecystokinin-mediated pathways [5, 30–32]. Hyperglycemia and insulin resistance impair autonomic neuronal function and gastrointestinal motility through advanced glycation end-products, oxidative stress, and systemic inflammation, thereby predisposing to esophageal dysmotility and delayed gastric emptying [30, 33, 34]. The association was more pronounced among participants aged under 65 years than in older individuals. This age-related difference may reflect a higher burden of competing risk factors in older populations, which could attenuate the relative contribution of CircS. Alternatively, middle-aged adults may have greater susceptibility to circadian and metabolic disruption, as their physiological reserve may be more vulnerable to such insults. Notably, recent global burden of disease data indicates that GERD incidence in adolescents and young adults has increased substantially over the past three decades, underscoring the growing relevance of early-life risk factor identification [35].

A pronounced sex difference was evident, with women showing a substantially higher risk than men. This aligns with known sex-specific patterns in GERD. Fluctuations in estrogen and progesterone may influence lower esophageal sphincter tone and gastric emptying [36, 37]. Furthermore, while women are more likely to report symptoms and have non-erosive reflux disease, men are predisposed to severe complications like erosive esophagitis and Barrett’s esophagus [38]. Our finding adds to this landscape by suggesting that the impact of systemic factors like CircS may also be sex-dependent.

The stronger association in Non-White participants underscores the need for race-specific risk assessment and further investigation into potential genetic or environmental modifiers [39, 40]. Socioeconomic factors also modified the association. The variation across income strata suggests that the relative impact of CircS may be more detectable in populations with fewer competing material disadvantages. Recent Mendelian randomization studies have also identified household income as a factor that may be causally related to GERD, partially mediated by sleep duration—a finding that resonates with the circadian framework of our study [41]. Unemployed individuals exhibited higher risk than employed participants, potentially reflecting the compounding effects of stress and reduced healthcare access.

Regarding medication use, the attenuated association among users of PPIs and NSAIDs suggests that these medications may partially mitigate the risk conferred by CircS. This finding has clinical implications, suggesting that CircS-related GERD risk may be at least partially pharmacologically modifiable, particularly through acid suppression [42]. For NSAIDs, the attenuated association may be explained by concurrent use of gastroprotective agents or more frequent healthcare contact.

In contrast, the association between CircS and GERD was consistent across strata of smoking status, drinking behavior, physical activity, and area deprivation index (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating that the detrimental effect of CircS is robust and not substantially altered by these lifestyle and environmental factors [43].

A comprehensive understanding of our findings requires balanced consideration of the methodological framework’s strengths and limitations:

This study benefits from several key design strengths. It is grounded in the UK Biobank, a large-scale prospective cohort with prolonged follow-up, providing substantial statistical power and minimizing selection bias inherent to retrospective designs. The ascertainment of GERD cases was enhanced by utilizing a composite endpoint derived from multiple linked national health data sources, including hospital inpatient, primary care, and death registry records, which offers a more complete capture of clinically recognized disease than single-source registries. To strengthen the temporal relationship between exposure and outcome, we meticulously excluded participants with pre-existing GERD and applied a two-year lag period between baseline exposure assessment and outcome follow-up. Furthermore, the rich phenotyping within the cohort enabled extensive adjustment for a wide array of demographic, lifestyle, socioeconomic, and clinical confounders.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, incident GERD was defined using the UK Biobank’s “First occurrence” field, which integrates hospital records, primary care data, death registries, and self-report. While comprehensive, this approach may under-ascertain milder, undiagnosed cases, and the recorded onset date may be imprecise due to delays in healthcare-seeking. The potential direction of bias from this imprecision is difficult to determine, as it depends on whether healthcare-seeking behavior differs by CircS status. However, a sensitivity analysis excluding self-reported cases yielded consistent findings (HR = 1.21 vs. 1.20 in main analysis), suggesting that any bias from outcome misclassification is unlikely to have materially influenced our conclusions. Second, despite extensive adjustment for demographic, lifestyle, and socioeconomic factors, residual confounding cannot be fully ruled out. It is important to note that our definition of hypertension already included the use of antihypertensive medications (e.g., calcium channel blockers) as part of the CircS component. Therefore, these medications were not additionally adjusted for in the models to avoid over-adjustment. In contrast, the depression component was defined solely based on ICD-10 diagnosed mood disorders and did not incorporate antidepressant use. While we did not adjust for antidepressants in the main analysis—as they may reflect depression severity or treatment status—we acknowledge that unmeasured confounding related to pharmacological management of depression cannot be entirely excluded. We also did not adjust for other medications that may affect GERD (e.g., bisphosphonates, clindamycin), which we acknowledge as a limitation.

Consequently, our estimates reflect the overall association of CircS—including both the conditions and, for hypertension, their pharmacological treatment—with GERD risk. Third, CircS status was measured only at baseline and may have changed over time, which could lead to exposure misclassification and bias towards the null. Other exposures and covariates were measured only at baseline, precluding analysis of their changes over time and the precise temporal relationship with disease onset. Fourth, the exclusion of participants with missing CircS component data (N = 119,785) may raise concerns about selection bias. However, comparison of included and excluded participants revealed negligible to small differences across all baseline characteristics (all SMDs < 0.2; Supplementary Table 5), and sensitivity analysis using complete cases produced virtually identical results (HR = 1.21), indicating that selection bias is unlikely to substantially alter our conclusions. Finally, the predominantly White European ancestry of the cohort necessitates caution in extrapolating these results to other ethnic groups and underscores the need for validation in diverse populations.

Looking forward, this research opens several important avenues for investigation. First, validation in ethnically diverse cohorts is essential to establish generalizability and potentially reveal population-specific patterns. Second, future studies employing advanced methodologies like Mendelian randomization could help further clarify the direction and nature of these associations, while sophisticated mediation analyses might elucidate the complex interplay between different pathways. Third, and most clinically relevant, our results provide a compelling rationale for developing and testing integrated intervention strategies in randomized controlled trials. Such trials could evaluate whether simultaneously addressing sleep architecture, psychological wellbeing, and metabolic status—including careful selection of antihypertensive regimens—yields superior outcomes compared to standard GERD management.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this investigation positions CircS as a significant and integrative risk marker for gastroesophageal reflux disease, suggesting that dysregulated sleep and mood are involved in its pathogenesis while also confirming the role of metabolic components including hypertension. Our findings, observed across diverse populations—including those not typically considered high-risk—suggest that GERD involves more than just gastric acid. The condition appears closely linked to interactions among circadian rhythms, metabolism, and psychological state. This broader perspective may help refine how we assess and manage GERD in clinical practice, encouraging more integrated approaches. Ultimately, these insights may pave the way for more personalized and effective strategies for preventing and managing this burdensome condition.
