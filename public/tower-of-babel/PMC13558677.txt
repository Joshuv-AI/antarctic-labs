MM-derived exosomes promote M2 macrophage polarization and immunosuppressive microenvironment: therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Qi Li, Yilei Shi, Wenzhuo Zhuang, Bingzong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple myeloma (MM) progression involves extensive immunosuppressive remodeling of the bone marrow microenvironment. In this study, we performed a comprehensive mass spectrometry–based proteomic analysis of exosomes derived from MM cells (MM-Exos), revealing that these exosomal proteins are predominantly enriched in pathways associated with immune regulation. We demonstrated that MM-Exos effectively drive macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby facilitating the establishment and maintenance of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Mechanistically, we identified the ERK1/2 signaling pathway as a critical mediator of MM-Exo-induced macrophage polarization. Importantly, we found that simvastatin, a widely used inhibitor of cholesterol biosynthesis, effectively impaired exosome secretion from MM cells and subsequently reduced M2 macrophage polarization. Collectively, our study reveals a novel mechanism of immune evasion in MM, in which MM-Exos promote the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype via ERK1/2 pathway activation. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting exosome-mediated intercellular communication within the MM bone marrow microenvironment to improve clinical outcomes. Subject terms: Cancer microenvironment, Myeloma

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM), the second most prevalent hematologic malignancy, is characterized by the clonal proliferation of malignant plasma cells within the bone marrow [1]. Over the past two decades, advancements in therapies, including proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory agents, monoclonal antibodies, and autologous stem cell transplantation, have significantly improved patient response rates and survival outcomes [2]. However, MM remains incurable, with most patients eventually experiencing disease progression and relapse.

The bone marrow immune microenvironment plays an essential role in the progression of MM [3]. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies have highlighted its immunosuppressive nature during MM progression, characterized by the exhaustion of natural killer (NK) cells and CD8+ T cells and the accumulation of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [4]. Macrophages demonstrate remarkable phenotypic plasticity, capable of polarizing into either the pro-inflammatory, tumor-suppressive M1 phenotype or the anti-inflammatory, tumor-promoting M2 phenotype in response to microenvironmental stimuli [5]. In MM, TAMs are predominantly polarized toward the M2 phenotype, contributing to the development of MM through multiple mechanisms: (1) secreting cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-10 to promote MM cell proliferation [6]; (2) promoting angiogenesis via the release of pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF, FGF-2, and IL-8 [7]; (3) suppressing anti-tumor immune response through the interaction of TAM-expressed PD-L1 with PD-1 on CD8+ T cells [8]; and (4) enhancing drug resistance by protecting MM cells from apoptosis [9]. Developing TAM-targeted therapeutic strategies may provide a promising avenue to mitigate MM progression and improve patient outcomes.

Exosomes, nanoscale extracellular vesicles secreted by various cell types, have emerged as crucial mediators of intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment [10]. Tumor-derived exosomes, enriched with bioactive molecules such as proteins, lipids, mRNA, and miRNA, are internalized by immune cells, including macrophages, T cells, and dendritic cells, regulating the phenotype and function of immune cells and promoting an immunosuppressive microenvironment [11]. Prostate cancer cell-derived exosomal IL-8 enhanced starvation of CD8+ T cells inducing exhaustion [12]. Renal cancer cells-derived exosomes impair differentiation and maturation of DCs and T cell immune response [13]. Moreover, tumor-derived exosomes can upregulate the expression of immune checkpoint molecules like PD-L1 on immune cells, further impairing anti-tumor immunity. Colorectal cancer cell-derived exosome up-regulate the expression of PD-L1 in TAM, inhibit the cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells, and promote tumor growth [14]. Exosomes derived from multiple myeloma cells (MM-Exos) have been shown to suppress both innate and adaptive immune responses, thereby promoting immune escape [15]. MM-Exos can reduce the cytotoxic activity of NK and CD8+ T cells, induce apoptosis in CD4+ T cells, and enhance regulatory T cell activity [16, 17]. Furthermore, MM-Exos have been reported to drive macrophage polarization toward the tumor-supportive M2 phenotype [18]. Despite these findings, the molecular mechanisms underlying the immunosuppressive effects of MM-Exos, particularly their role in modulating macrophage polarization, remain poorly defined.

In this study, we demonstrate that MM-Exos can be internalized by macrophages, leading to the polarization of these cells toward the M2 phenotype. Through a comprehensive analysis of the underlying molecular mechanisms, our research aims to provide novel insights into the immunosuppressive functions of MM-Exos and to identify potential therapeutic strategies for modulating the bone marrow immune microenvironment.

Methods

Cell culture and preparation

Human MM cell lines H929 and LP-1, human monocytic cell line THP-1, murine MM cell line 5TGM1 and murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were freshly isolated from the blood of healthy donors using Ficoll-PaqueTM PLUS (Cytiva). Monocytes were isolated from PBMCs using a CD14 Positive Selection Kit (Stem cell) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All cell lines were tested free of mycoplasma contamination. Cell line authentication was performed by cell line characterization core using short tandem repeat profiling (Genetic Testing Biotechnology Corporation, China).

ELISA

For detection of IL-10, the supernatant of TDMs treated with MM-Exo f were harvested and measured by Human IL-10 (Interleukin 10) ELISA Kit (Elabscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Victor microtiter reader (FilterMax F5, Molecular Devices) was used for analysis at 450 nm absorption.

In vivo studies

Four-to six-week-old C57BL/KaLwRij mice were housed and maintained in accordance with institutional guidelines for the use of laboratory animals and after acquiring permission from the ethics committee of Soochow University for animal experimentation. A total of 1 × 106 luciferase labeled 5TGM1 cells were injected via the lateral tail vein. Tumors were allowed to grow for 2 weeks, the mice were randomly divided into three groups: control group, simvastatin (SV) group and 5TGM1 Exo group (n = 5 for each group). Sample sizes were chosen based on our preliminary experiments and similar previously published studies. Mice were treated with 50 mg/kg SV, 5 mg/kg 5TGM1 Exo, or PBS as a control, once every 2 days for 2 weeks. Mice were imaged after injection of 15 mg/kg of D-luciferin (Promega, Madison, WI) using an IVIS Lumina II optical imaging system (Caliper Life Sciences, Hopkinton, MA). Tumor burden was assessed by serial bioluminescence imaging every three days. The mice were sacrificed 2 days after the last administration, and the tibiae and femurs were carefully dissected and separated for the extraction of bone marrow cells. During the animal experiments, investigators were not blinded to group allocation. Subsequent outcome assessments were performed by investigators blinded to the group allocations.

Isolation and characterization of exosomes

Exosomes were isolated from the cell culture medium by a differential centrifugation method, as previously described. In brief, the collected cell-conditioned medium was centrifuged at 300 ×g for 5 minutes, 2000 ×g for 15 min, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, and then filtered at 100,000 ×g at 4 °C for 2 h. The pellet was resuspended in PBS and subsequently centrifuged at 100,000 ×g for another 2 h. The pellets were resuspended with PBS and stored at −80 °C for further use. Exosomes were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Exosomes were lysed and the expression of CD63, CD81 and TSG101 was determined by western blot.

Detected internalization of Exosomes

Exosomes were labeled using and PKH67 (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, exosomes were incubated for 5 min with PKH67 dye at room temperature, terminated with FBS followed by centrifugation at 100,000 ×g for 2 h at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in PBS and subsequently centrifugated at 100,000 ×g for another 2 h. Macrophages were treated with PKH67 labeled exosomes for 24 h. The cells were centrifuged at 300 ×g for 5 min and wash with PBS for twice. The internalization of exosomes by cells were verified by flow cytometer using the FACSDiva software (Becton Dickson, San Diego CA).

Bone marrow cells preparation and flow cytometry

After the mice were sacrificed, bone marrow cells were extracted from tibiae and femurs, the cell suspension was filtered through a 70 µM nylon mesh to obtain a single-cell suspension and centrifuged at 1500 ×g for 5 min. Next, we resuspended the cells with ACK lysis buffer and incubated for 10 min in order to lyse red blood cells (RBCs). Residual cells were stained with various antibodies and analyzed on flow cytometer using the FACSDiva software (Becton Dickson, San Diego CA). Flow cytometry antibodies for murine CD206 (E-AB-F1135E), F4/80 (E-AB-F0995C) were purchased from Elabscience.

Cell viability assays

At 48 h post-treatment with SV, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution (Biosharp, China) was introduced into the culture medium of H929 cells, LP-1 cells and 5TGM1 cells. The plate was analyzed with a Victor microtiter plate reader (FilterMax F5, Molecular Devices), and the absorbance of the well was measured at 450 nm.

Apoptosis and cell-cycle analysis

H929 cells, LP-1 cells and 5TGM1 cells were treated with SV for 48 h. Apoptotic cells were stained with annexin-V and 7-AAD and analyzed by flow cytometry. Cell cycle analysis was performed with a cell cycle analysis kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Prior to statistical comparisons, data were evaluated for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and for homogeneity of variances using the F-test or Levene’s test. Results are shown as mean plus or minus standard deviation (SD) of values obtained in independent experiments. The paired student’s t-test was used to compare the differences between paired samples. Differences were considered significant at P value below 0.05. All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates to ensure reproducibility.

Results

Characterization and proteomic profiling of MM-derived exosomes

Exosomes derived from multiple myeloma cells (MM-Exos) were isolated by differential ultracentrifugation. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that MM-Exos exhibited the characteristic cup-shaped and spherical morphology with a well-defined bilayer membrane (Fig. 1A). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) further confirmed their expected size distribution, with a mean particle diameter ranging from 40 to 120 nm (Fig. 1B). Moreover, exosomal identity was further confirmed by the detection of the canonical markers CD63 and CD81 (Fig. 1C).

To further elucidate the molecular profile of MM-Exos, we performed liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS)–based proteomic analysis of MM-derived exosome, which identified 1827 unique proteins in H929 Exo and 1926 proteins in LP-1 Exo. Comparative analysis revealed 1040 overlapping proteins shared between the two exosomal proteomes (Fig. 1D). Notably, this number substantially exceeds the ~300 proteins reported in previous studies, highlighting the greater depth and sensitivity of our analysis [19]. Functional enrichment analysis of these exosomal proteins was performed using Cytoscape, revealing their involvement in diverse biological processes, including regulation of immune responses, regulation of intracellular signaling transduction, and protein complex assembly (Fig. 1E and Fig. S1A). Moreover, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated significant overrepresentation of signaling pathways implicated in tumor progression, such as the ERK1 and ERK2 cascade, Wnt signaling, and Ras protein signal transduction (Fig. 1F and Fig. S1B).

MM-Exos induce M2 polarization of macrophages via Erk1/2 signaling pathway

In light of the mass spectrometry analysis and the demonstrated immune modulatory activity of MM-Exos, we sought to investigate their impact on immune cells within the MM microenvironment. Macrophages are abundant and functionally important components of the MM immune milieu [20]. To explore the effects of MM-Exos on macrophages, THP-1 monocytes were differentiated into macrophages using phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), leading to a morphological shift from single, round, suspended cells to adherent cells with transparent cytoplasm, strong refractive properties (Fig. 2A), accompanied by increased expression of the macrophage marker CD68 (Fig. 2B). The uptake of MM-Exos by macrophages was assessed by incubating PKH67-labeled exosomes with macrophages. Flow cytometry revealed an elevated fluorescence intensity in macrophages after co-incubation with PKH67-labeled MM-Exos (Fig. 2C). Consistently, immunofluorescence demonstrated the accumulation of fluorescently-labeled MM-Exos within the macrophages, confirming efficient internalization (Fig. 2D) and laying the foundation for subsequent investigations into their effects on macrophage.

Unpaired two-tails Student’s t-test determined significance. Data are shown as mean ± SD. Statistical significance: ***P < 0.001. TDMs were treated with MM-Exos. Given that M2 macrophages are implicated in supporting tumor progression and mediating immunosuppression in MM [6], we next investigated whether MM-Exos contribute to the polarization of macrophages toward an M2 phenotype. To enhance the robustness and translational relevance of our findings, both THP-1 cell line–derived macrophages (TDMs) and human monocyte-derived macrophages (hMDMs) differentiated from primary monocytes isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were employed. Macrophages were incubated with MM-Exos, followed by assessment of their polarization status. The results revealed that MM-Exos significantly promoted M2 polarization, as indicated by the upregulation of M2-associated markers, including ARG-1 (Fig. 3A) and CD206 (Fig. 3A), CD163 (Fig. 3B), and IL-10 (Fig. 3C). Conversely, MM-Exos did not induce a classical M1 polarization program. Although modest variations in IL-1β and iNOS expression (Fig. 3A) were observed among exosomes derived from different MM cell lines, these changes were not consistent with M1 activation overall. Instead, M1-associated markers were generally unchanged or reduced, with no significant change in CD16 (Fig. 3B). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MM-Exos actively drive macrophage polarization toward an M2-like immunosuppressive phenotype within the MM microenvironment.

To further validate the clinical relevance of these findings, CD138⁺ plasma cells were isolated from bone marrow samples of MM and MGUS patients, followed by exosome purification and characterization (Fig. S2). Macrophages exposed to exosomes derived from MM patients exhibited significantly higher expression of M2-associated markers, including ARG-1 (Fig. S3A), CD206 (Fig. S3A), and CD163 (Fig. S3B), compared to those treated with MGUS-derived exosomes. In line with the results observed in MM cell lines, MM patients-derived exosomes did not induce M1 polarization (Fig. S3A and S3B). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis of bone marrow immune cells demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of CD14⁺ CD11b⁺CD206⁺ M2-like macrophages in MM patients compared to MGUS controls (Fig. S3C). These findings further support the notion that MM-derived exosomes contribute to macrophage M2 polarization within the bone marrow microenvironment.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we next investigated how MM-Exos mediate this phenotypic shift. Proteomic analysis of MM-Exos by LC-MS, followed by KEGG pathway enrichment, revealed significant enrichment of proteins linked to the ERK1/2 signaling pathway, a pathway known to promote M2 macrophage polarization [21, 22], including “ERK1 and ERK2 cascade” and “regulation of ERK1 and ERK2 cascade” (Fig. 1E and Fig S1 B). Consistently, macrophages exhibited a marked increase in phosphorylated ERK1/2 levels following exposure to MM-Exos, indicating activation of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway (Fig. 3D). These findings suggest that MM-Exos drive M2 polarization in macrophages, at least in part, through activation of ERK1/2 signaling.

Simvastatin inhibits exosome secretion and suppresses proliferation of MM cells

Statins have been reported to inhibit exosome biogenesis by suppressing cholesterol biosynthesis, a process essential for the formation, transport, and secretion of exosomes [23]. Simvastatin (SV), a first-generation statin, has been shown to reduce exosome secretion [24]. Based on these findings, we investigated whether SV could suppress exosome secretion in MM cells. MM cells were treated with a non-cytotoxic concentration of SV for 48 hours, resulting in a significant reduction in exosome secretion as determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (Fig. 4A). This inhibitory effect was further validated by a decrease in total exosome protein content (Fig. 4B) and reduced expression of exosome-associated markers CD81 and TSG101 (Fig. 4C). In addition to its effects on exosome release, SV treatment significantly inhibited MM cell proliferation (Fig. 4D). Flow cytometric analysis using 7-AAD/Annexin V-PE double staining revealed a significant increase in apoptotic cells following SV treatment (Fig. 4E). Additionally, SV induced cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase in MM cells (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SV not only suppresses exosome secretion but also inhibits cell proliferation, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent with dual functions.

As the human MM cell lines H929 and LP-1 cannot be used to establish immune-competent tumor models in mice, we utilized the murine MM cell line 5TGM1 for in vivo research. We performed LC–MS–based proteomic analysis of 5TGM1 Exo, identifying a total of 1589 proteins. Comparative analysis revealed that approximately 630 proteins were shared among 5TGM1-, H929-, and LP-1-derived exosomes (Fig. 1D). Functional enrichment analysis indicated the exosomal proteins from 5TGM1 cells were significantly enriched in pathways related to ERK1/2 signaling, integrin-mediated signaling, and immune regulation (Figs. S4A and S4B).

In vitro, 5TGM1 Exo exerted similar effects on murine macrophages RAW264.7 as those observed with H929 Exo and LP-1 Exo in human macrophages, significantly promoting M2 polarization through ERK1/2 signaling pathway and suggesting a conserved mechanism across species (Fig. 5D–F). Furthermore, SV effectively inhibited the secretion of 5TGM1 Exo (Fig. 5G–I) and demonstrated potent anti-myeloma properties by suppressing proliferation (Fig. 5J), inducing apoptosis (Fig. 5K), and arresting the cell cycle of 5TGM1 cells (Fig. 5L).

Next, immune-competent orthotopic MM xenograft models were established. Luciferase-labeled 5TGM1 cells were injected via the lateral tail vein into C57BL/KaLwRij mice. After tumor establishment, mice were randomly assigned to one of three treatment groups: PBS (control), 5TGM1 Exo, and SV. Tumor burden was assessed through serial bioluminescence imaging (Fig. 6A). The bioluminescence signal revealed that 5TGM1 Exo significantly enhanced tumor progression, whereas SV treatment markedly attenuated the tumor burden (Fig. 6B). Body weight remained stable across all groups, indicating good tolerability (Fig. 6C). At the endpoint, mice were sacrificed, and bone marrow cells were harvested for immunophenotypic analysis of macrophages. MM-Exos treatment led to a substantial increase in M2 macrophage infiltration within the bone marrow, while SV significantly attenuated this effect (Fig. 6D). Histopathological analysis of major organs—including the kidney, lung, heart, liver, and spleen—showed no evident toxicity or tissue damage in SV-treated mice (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these results highlight the pro-tumorigenic role of MM-derived exosomes through the induction of M2 macrophage polarization and demonstrate the therapeutic potential of simvastatin in counteracting this immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Discussion

Multiple myeloma (MM) is characterized by the clonal proliferation of malignant plasma cells within the bone marrow [1]. Despite significant advancements in treatment, MM remains an incurable disease, with most patients eventually relapsing or becoming refractory to current treatments [2]. Emerging evidence suggests that the relapse and therapeutic resistance are not only driven by intrinsic mechanisms of malignant plasma cells but is also profoundly influenced by the bone marrow microenvironment [25]. Among its key components, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) play a crucial role, and in MM, they predominantly exhibit an M2 phenotype, which promotes tumor progression, immunosuppression, and angiogenesis [6–8].

Our study demonstrates that MM-derived exosomes (MM-Exos) serve as pivotal mediators of macrophage reprogramming, driving their polarization toward an M2-like phenotype. MM-Exos were shown to be efficiently internalized by macrophages, as confirmed by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry. Upon uptake, MM-Exos induced upregulation of M2-specific markers such as CD206 and Arg-1, accompanied by enhanced secretion of IL-10, a key immunosuppressive cytokine. IL-10 suppresses T-cell activity and antigen presentation, thereby contributing to immune evasion and disease progression [26]. These findings align with observations in other malignancies, such as non-small-cell lung cancer and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, where tumor-derived exosomes similarly contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [27–29].

To further investigate the mechanism underlying MM-Exos-induced M2 polarization, mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis revealed that proteins within MM-Exos were significantly enriched in the ERK1/2 signaling pathway. ERK1/2, a well-characterized component of the MAPK cascade, is known to play a pivotal role in promoting cell proliferation and driving M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages. [30, 31]. Our in vitro experiments demonstrated that treatment with MM-Exos markedly increased the phosphorylation levels of ERK1/2 in macrophages, indicating that MM-Exos serve as upstream activators of ERK1/2 signaling. Notably, comparative proteomic profiling of H929 and LP-1 derived exosomes revealed consistent enrichment of multiple integrin subunits (ITGB3, ITGB1, ITGAV, ITGA2B, ITGA4, ITGB5, ITGB6, ITGA6, ITGB7). Increasing evidence suggests that exosomal integrins are functional molecules capable of activating ERK signaling in recipient cells following membrane engagement or internalization [32, 33]. Collectively, these findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting MM-Exos or inhibiting the ERK1/2 pathway as a strategy to suppress M2 polarization and re-establish effective anti-tumor immune responses.

Given the central role of exosomes in mediating communication between MM cells and immune components, disrupting exosome-mediated signaling offers a promising strategy to counteract tumor-induced immune suppression. Various strategies have been explored to inhibit exosome biogenesis, secretion, or uptake, including the use of GW4869, and siRNA-based knockdown of key exosome-regulating proteins [34, 35]. While these approaches have shown promise in preclinical cancer models, none have received clinical approval for clinical application to date. Identification of alternative strategies with greater feasibility for clinical translation remains an urgent need. In this context, our findings highlight the potential of simvastatin as a novel strategy to suppress exosome release from MM cells. Given that exosome secretion is tightly linked to intracellular cholesterol homeostasis and that lipid rafts facilitate exosome formation, simvastatin may interfere with exosome biogenesis by inhibiting cholesterol synthesis and disrupting lipid raft integrity [24, 36, 37]. Simvastatin is one of the most widely prescribed statins among the elderly—a demographic heavily overlapping with MM patients [38]. Notably, large-scale retrospective data in MM demonstrate that background statin therapy is robustly associated with a significant reduction in all-cause and MM-specific mortality [39, 40]. Considering the widespread clinical use and favorable safety profile of statins, their potential repurposing in MM treatment warrants further exploration, particularly in combination with existing immunotherapies or chemotherapy regimens.

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence that MM-Exos drive M2 macrophage polarization through the activation of ERK1/2 signaling, thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive bone marrow microenvironment that facilitates MM progression. Furthermore, we propose simvastatin as a potential therapeutic intervention to inhibit MM-Exos secretion. These efforts may pave the way for novel therapeutic strategies that reprogram the tumor microenvironment and enhance anti-tumor immune responses, ultimately improving clinical outcomes for MM patients.
