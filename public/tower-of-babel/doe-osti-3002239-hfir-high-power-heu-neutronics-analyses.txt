 ABSTRACT

Department of Energy National Nuclear Security Administration Office of Material Management
and Minimization’s mission includes the conversion of civilian research reactors from highly
enriched uranium (HEU) to low-enriched uranium (LEU) fuel. Analyses have shown that the Oak
Ridge National Laboratory High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) will need to operate at 95 MW for
the LEU silicide dispersion fuel designs to match key performance metrics obtained with HEU fuel
at 85 MW. To prove safe operation of HFIR after installation of plant modifications to increase
power, a high power HEU test cycle was proposed. Neutronics model updates and reactor
physics analyses are performed to support the development of safety design reports for the high
power (HP) HEU test cycle. Reactor physics metrics evaluated herein include fuel depletion,
actinide production, cycle length, fission rate density distributions, axial power peaking factors,
and reactor kinetic parameters. These reactor physics analyses support the development of
future LEU safety design reports by providing key input for future HP HEU HFIR thermal
hydraulics and reactor transient safety analyses.

 x

 1. INTRODUCTION

The High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) is a pressurized, light-water research reactor operated at
Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) on behalf of the US Department of Energy (DOE). HFIR
currently operates at 85 MW with highly enriched uranium (HEU) fuel. The HFIR core consists of
radial concentric regions with a central flux trap surrounded by a fuel assembly, a control element
(CE) region, and then by a beryllium reflector. The fuel assembly includes an inner fuel element
(IFE) with 171 involute-shaped fuel plates and an outer fuel element (OFE) with 369 involute fuel
plates. Fuel plates are separated by water channels that provide core cooling and neutron
moderation. The fuel plates are welded between two annular side plates and are filled with HEU
in the form of U3O8-Al and with Al filler regions within aluminum cladding. The IFE also includes
burnable 10B poison in the form of B4C within its filler region. CEs consisting of Ta-Al and Eu2O3-Al
neutron poison regions provide the power regulation and reactivity control along with rapid scram
capabilities. The CEs travel between the fuel assembly and the Be reflector. The HFIR core
configuration is shown in Figure 1.1 below.

 Figure 1.1. HFIR Core Mockup [1].

The CEs consist of four outer safety plates and an inner control cylinder. The safety plates are
withdrawn upwards while the control cylinder is withdrawn downwards to move poison away from
the core and to expose the fuel to the beryllium reflector, increasing core reactivity. The CEs are
withdrawn over the course of a cycle to maintain core criticality as fuel depletes, which is
visualized in Figure 1.2

 Figure 1.2. Withdrawal of CEs [2].

HFIR includes a flux trap target bundle (also known as the flux trap target), experimental facilities
within the Be reflector, horizontal beam tubes, and pneumatic facilities that support a broad range
of neutron science missions including neutron scattering, isotope production, materials and fuels
irradiation research, and neutron activation analysis. The HFIR experiment facilities are shown
in Figure 1.3 below. Further details on the HFIR core design and experiment facilities are found
in References [1, 2, 3, 4, 5].

 Figure 1.3. HFIR Experiment Facilities [6].

DOE National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA) Office of Material Management and
Minimization’s mission includes the conversion of civilian research reactors from HEU to LEU fuel.
Analyses documented in References [7, 8] have shown that HFIR will need to operate at 95 MW
for the LEU silicide dispersion fuel designs to match key performance metrics obtained with HEU
fuel at 85 MW. To prove safe operation of HFIR after installation of plant modifications to increase
power, a high power (HP) HEU test cycle was proposed. Plant modifications are necessary to
ensure that the additional heat produced can be safely and reliably removed by the cooling
system.

This report documents neutronics model updates and reactor physics analyses for HFIR with HEU
fuel at increased operating power of 95 MW. Reactor physics metrics evaluated herein include
fuel depletion, actinide production, cycle length, fission rate density distributions, axial power
peaking factors, and kinetics parameters. These reactor physics analyses support the
development of future safety design reports for the HP HEU test cycle by providing key input for
future HP HEU HFIR thermal hydraulics and reactor transient safety analyses.

 2. CODES

Reactor physics analyses are performed with HFIRCON version 1.0.5 [9] on the ORNL Research
Reactors Division computation cluster lise. HFIRCON is a radiation transport and depletion code
developed at ORNL for performing HFIR fuel and target depletion and activation analyses.
HFIRCON uses LAVAMINT, a parallel MCNP model interrogator, to stochastically compute cell
volumes and bounding boxes, the ADVANTG code package for variance reduction, the ORNL-
Transformative Neutronics (TN) transport solver, and the ORIGEN/MSX_DEPLETE module to
perform depletion and decay analyses. ORNL-TN is a modified version of MCNP5-v1.60
developed to improve scalability and performance.

Fuel depletion, actinide production, cycle length, and fission rate density distributions are
computed in-line with the HFIRCON depletion code for every time step. For each model case,
one day time steps are used for most of the cycle while 0.1 day time steps are used near the end
of the cycle to improve the accuracy of the predicted cycle length. A total of 1.2e8 particle histories
are used for each depletion step and 2.0e8 particles histories are used for the critical CE position
search analyses. The 2.0e8 particles for the critical CE search analyses were distributed over 8
simulations with different CE positions at each time step to determine the critical CE position. Ten
times more particle histories are used than what is recommended in the HFIRCON 1.0.5 user
manual [9] for most problems. This was done to have minimal statistical error and thus increase
the accuracy of the analyzed parameters. For the volume analyses, a ±0.0025 (1σ) relative
tolerance is used. This indicates the minimum relative uncertainty required by the LAVAMINT
volume computations.

For the axial power peaking factors, small mesh tallies are used to analyze the thermal neutron
flux near the top and bottom edges of the fuel. These analyses are performed with ORNL-TN for
the BOC and EOC inputs created by HFIRCON using 2.0e9 histories. See Section 4.6 for details
regarding these analyses. The follow-up ORNL-TN analyses are also used to analyze the reactor
kinetics parameters documented in Section 4.4.

 3. MODELS

The MCNP HFIR HEU explicit fuel representative model from Reference [6] is the highest fidelity
HFIR MCNP model reviewed for safety basis analyses to date. This model was chosen as the
starting point for the reactor physics analyses. The following model updates were made to make
this model compatible with HFIRCON.

 1) Removed VESTA input cards:
  VESTA is a depletion code which was used in Reference [6]. HFIRCON usage
 replaces VESTA and thus the VESTA input cards were removed from the model input.
 2) Updated cell and material indices to be compatible with HFIRCON:
  HFIRCON requires the indices used for fuel and target cells to not be indices used for
 material definitions. This is because HFIRCON will create material definitions using
 these indices to represent the changing material composition throughout the cycle.
 3) Updated nuclear cross-sections to use ENDF/B-VII.1:
  ENDF/B-VII.1 are the most up to date cross-sections available within HFIRCON 1.0.5
 and the verification and validation documented in the HFIRCON 1.0.5 User Guide [9]
 justify the use of ENDF/B-VII.1.

The 238Pu production experiments within the beryllium reflector vertical experiment facilities were
replaced with Be plugs since these experiments are qualified for the specific irradiation conditions
(e.g., heat deposition) associated with 85 MW operation, and it is unlikely for them to be requalified
for the 95 MW HP HEU test cycle. Furthermore, fissions generated in these targets slightly reduce
the fission rate density that occurs within the fuel for constant reactor power. The large facilities
are typically occupied by Be plugs when not in use, and the small facilities are typically occupied
by water and stainless steel liners with an orifice, but they can also be filled with a Be plug. Thus,
it is appropriate and more limiting for the downstream thermal hydraulics evaluations to remove
these 238Pu production targets from the reflector. Figure 3.1 below shows the removal of these
238Pu production targets. While only a small amount of fuel plates are included in the model

geometry plots to improve rendering times, the actual transport analyses include all fuel plates.

 Figure 3.1. Removal of 238Pu Production Targets in the Be Reflector.

 5

Many experiments within the target bundle (TB) will likely not be qualified for the 95 MW HP HEU
test cycle due to the change in irradiation conditions. Thus, a light TB loading is anticipated where
many, perhaps all, experiments are replaced with aluminum dummy rods. To determine the
potential impact this may have on quantities of interest, a second model is created with all target
basket experiments replaced with aluminum targets. For the peripheral target positions (PTP),
all seven axial positions are modeled with aluminum spacers. These aluminum spacers are
identical to the aluminum spacers modeled in the unmodified representative model for the first
two axial positions of the D7, G4, and G7 PTP positions. For the non-PTP targets including the
hydraulic tube position B-3, aluminum dummy rods are modeled. These aluminum dummy rods
are identical to the dummy rods modeled in the unmodified representative model in positions B4,
C5, E3, and E6. Figure 3.2 and Figure 3.3 show the models with and without the TB experiments
for xy and yz midplane plots. See Reference [6] for additional details regarding the modeling of
the TB experiment targets.

 Figure 3.2. Removal of Target Basket Experiments, XY Reactor Centerline.

 6

 Figure 3.3. Removal of Target Basket Experiments, YZ Reactor Centerline.

The replacement of the representative experimental targets with aluminum changes the volume
of water within the flux trap. There are two opposing factors for this change in volume:

 1) The aluminum dummy rods have a smaller radius than the experiment targets, which
 increases water volume (see Figure 3.2).
 2) Most of the representative loading experiment target rods have empty upper capsule
 positions and therefore have water in those upper axial regions. Swapping these
 experiments with the aluminum dummy rods displaces water within these upper axial
 regions which decreases water volume (see Figure 3.3).

The net change is an increase of ~300 cc in the water content for the aluminum TB loading. Since
the flux trap region is overmoderated, this will result in a small decrease in reactivity. However,
the replacement of various experiment materials that absorb neutrons with aluminum, which is
largely transparent to neutrons, will increase reactivity. It is expected that the overall impact will
be an increase in reactivity and a radial shift inward of the power distribution. The axially non-
uniform change in water content will result in a small axial shift upwards in the power distribution
compared to the representative TB loading.

The last model update included modifying the water compositions to reflect higher temperature
from the power increase. It is assumed that the inlet temperature remains constant since there
are no plans to change the inlet temperature. For an estimate of the increase temperature
change, the change of temperature between the inlet and outlet is multiplied by the power ratio,
95/85. The updated water densities were obtained from Reference [10] and are shown in Table
3.1. Inlet pressure and pressure drop are assumed to be unchanged since a small change in
pressure would have an insignificant impact on water densities.

the outlet due to the change in water temperature is about 4 times greater. Furthermore, the
change in water temperature was found to have an insignificant impact on the quantities of interest
with preliminary sensitivity analyses. Regardless, the updated density inputs were left in the
model as they represent a slight improvement to the model accuracy and because there was no
benefit to reversing this model update.

step included in the depletion analysis.

For the 95 MW cases, EOC timesteps have critical CE positions close to the maximum withdrawal
(27.00 inches). Thus, these final critical time steps are a better estimate of the EOC time than
using the midpoint method with the following timestep. For the 95 MW cases, the estimated cycle
lengths are 23.6 days and 24.4 days for the representative TB loading and aluminum TB loading
models respectively.

Figure 4.1 shows the critical CE positions with time. Increasing power from 85 MW to 95 MW
shortens the cycle by about 2.75 days. Replacing the representative TB loading with an aluminum
TB loading increased cycle length by 0.8 days. This is reasonable since various experimental
materials within the TB that provide neutron absorption have been replaced with aluminum which
is largely transparent to neutrons. Figure 4.2 shows the critical CE positions as a function of
burnup, megawatt days (MWD). The 85 MW and 95 MW curves are almost directly on top of
each other showing that the difference in cycle length is almost entirely due to the change in
power. The aluminum TB loading case increased cycle length by about 76 MWD.

 4.4. REACTOR KINETICS

Kinetics parameters of interest include the effective delayed neutron fraction, 𝛽𝑒𝑓𝑓, and the mean
neutron generation time, Λ. These parameters are important for transient/shutdown analyses and
reactivity feedback studies. The Rossi-𝛼 parameter is the prompt neutron decay constant.
Associated with 𝛽𝑒𝑓𝑓 and Λ, Rossi-𝛼 is useful for characterizing how the prompt neutron
population grows or decays in response to reactivity changes.

The reactor kinetics parameters are analyzed using the MCNP KOPTS method and are presented
in Table 4.3 below for BOC and EOC along with their 𝑘𝑒𝑓𝑓 and average number of neutrons per
fission, 𝜈. The bounding relative errors (RE) associated with the parameters are also presented
in Table 4.3. Only small differences exist between the three model cases. Comparing BOC and
EOC, 𝛽𝑒𝑓𝑓 decreases by about 2-3% due to the buildup of 239Pu which has a smaller delayed
neutron yield than 235U. These results agree well with Reference [13] with BOC values that are
very close and EOC values that have small differences due to the increased cycle length. The
mean neutron generation time, Λ, nearly doubles from BOC to EOC primarily due to the softening
of the neutron spectrum at EOC. Consequently, rossi-𝛼 decreases nearly by half, indicating a
slower response to reactivity changes at EOC than BOC.

 4.5. FISSION RATE DENSITIES

HFIRCON output includes the fission rate density within each fuel cell at each depletion time step.
The fission rate density time evolution and spatial distribution are presented in the subsections
below. The fission rate densities are specifically for the fuel cells and do not include other core
materials (i.e., filler, clad, and coolant).

 4.5.1. Fission Rate Density Time Evolution

The mean fission rate density as function of time is presented in Figure 4.20 for the IFE and in
Figure 4.21 for the OFE. The annotated values are the ratios relative to the 85 MW representative
TB loading model. All results follow a similar trend where the IFE and OFE fission rate density
curves with time roughly mirror each other. There is an initial downward spike in the IFE and
corresponding upward spike in the OFE, which is due to the close proximity of the CEs to the
OFE along with the initial quick withdrawal that occurs at BOC as shown in Figure 4.1. Over the

 27

next 6-7 days, the fission rate density slowly increases in the IFE while slowly decreasing in the
OFE as the 10B burnable poison in the IFE is depleted. For the rest of the cycle, the fission
distribution slowly shifts from the IFE to the OFE as the CEs continue to withdraw.

For most of the cycle, the fission rate density for the 95 MW representative TB loading is
approximately 11-12% greater than the 85 MW representative TB loading case, which matches
the power increase (95 MW/85 MW). As the cycle progresses, the composition of the fuel starts
to differ due to the quicker burnup of fuel and poison and quicker production of actinides as was
seen in Section 4.3. In the IFE, the 95 MW aluminum TB loading mean fission rate density is
greater than the 95 MW representative TB loading by 2-3%. In contrast, the OFEs of the 95 MW
aluminum TB loading have mean fission rate densities 1-2% smaller than the 95 MW
representative TB loading. This shows that there is a radial power shift inward when replacing
the representative TB loading with aluminum targets. This is reasonable since experiment
materials that absorb neutrons are being replaced with aluminum, which is largely transparent to
neutrons. This agrees well with the change in 235U depletion shown in Section 4.2.

 4.6. U25 UNCERTAINTY FACTORS

As seen in the fission rate density distributions in Section 4.5, significant axial variations occur in
the fission power distribution, with peaks observed near the fuel plate edges that are nearest to
the moderating and reflecting regions. These peaks can be intensified by axial misalignment of
the fuel plates as a result of manufacturing and assembly tolerances. As shown in Figure 4.44,
the thermal flux increases with axial distance beyond the fuel top edge (25.4 cm). Consequently,
an axial shift of a fuel zone increases the thermal flux incident on the fuel, thereby raising the
fission rate density near the edge of the fuel plate.

Figure 4.44. Thermal flux near Top Edge of Fuel for 95 MW Representative TB Loading Model.

To account for the power peaking caused by fuel misalignment, HFIR Steady State Heat Transfer
Code (HSSHTC) applies a U25 uncertainty factor. As shown in Section 4.5, the fission power
distribution evolves through the cycle due to changing CE positions, burnup of fuel and poisons,
and production of actinides. Thus, the U25 factors will also be time dependent.

U25 factors are computed as the ratio of the thermal flux (𝜙𝑡ℎ) at the specific coordinate to the
thermal flux at the fuel edge. This is done using a cylindrical mesh tally in MCNP using 0.2 cm
radial and 0.1 cm axial increments. For radial position (𝑟) and axial position (𝑧) at time (𝑡), the
U25 factor is computed with:

                                                     𝜙𝑡ℎ(𝑟,𝑧,𝑡)
 𝑈25(𝑟,𝑧,𝑡) = 𝑓𝑢𝑒𝑙 𝑒𝑛𝑑
 𝜙𝑡ℎ (𝑟,𝑡)

For the sake of comparison of upper fuel edge and lower fuel edge U25 factors, results are
presented as function of a relative axial offset position where positive values represent distance
outside of the fuel. U25 factors are analyzed for two time steps, BOC and EOC.

Allowable tolerances from end-clad defects ( ± 0.250 inch) combined with fuel plate loading
tolerance ( ± 0.015 inch) yield a maximum offset of 0.265 inch. Two fuel plates with this maximum
offset in opposite directions results in the worst-case scenario with total axial difference of 0.530
inch. Thus, the 0.265 inch (0.6731 cm) and 0.530 inch (1.3462 cm) offsets are of particular
importance and will be highlighted accordingly in the below results.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

Analyses [7, 8] have shown that HFIR must operate at 95 MW for LEU silicide dispersion fuel
designs to match the key performance metrics achieved with HEU fuel at 85 MW. To demonstrate
safe HFIR operation at increased power, a high-power HEU test cycle was proposed. Neutronics
model updates and reactor physics analyses were performed to support the safety design reports
for this test cycle.

Two MCNP models were developed for the 95 MW neutronics analyses. The first model includes
a representative TB loading, while the second model replaces the experiments with aluminum
dummy rods since many experiments will not be qualified for the test cycle due to changes in
irradiation conditions. A third model, with the representative TB loading at 85 MW, was also
created to quantify the impact of increased power and to provide consistency checks against prior
HFIR analyses.

Reactor physics analyses were performed with the three MCNP model cases. HFIRCON was
used for neutron transport and depletion analyses and follow-on MCNP analyses were performed
to analyze other important metrics. Parameters evaluated include fuel depletion, actinide
production, cycle length, fission rate density distributions, axial power peaking factors, and
kinetics parameters. Section 4 provides a detailed analysis of the results, including the impacts
of increased power and target basket loading. Overall, the results reflect the expected impacts
of the higher power rating and TB loading differences. The 85 MW results show good agreement
with previous HFIR neutronics analyses.

The reactor physics analyses support the development of safety design reports for the high-power
HEU test cycle by providing key inputs for future HFIR thermal hydraulics and reactor transient
safety analyses. To enable efficient use of the analyzed reactor physics parameters in
downstream evaluations, all necessary inputs and outputs have been archived in a ORNL backup
repository, along with the Python post-processing scripts used for data extraction, manipulation,
and visualization.
