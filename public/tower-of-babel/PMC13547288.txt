Prognostic impact of intensive chemotherapy in patients with TP53-mutated AML
Authors: Laurène Fenwarth, Loïc Vasseur, Nicolas Duployez, Augustin Boudry, Claude Gardin, Dominique Penther, Juliette Lambert, Stéphane de Botton, Karine Celli-Lebras, Pascal Turlure, Thomas Cluzeau, Thorsten Braun, Maël Heiblig, Christian Récher, Sylvain Chantepie, Jean-Baptiste Micol, Cécile Pautas, Hervé Dombret, Claude Preudhomme, Raphael Itzykson
Published: 2026

Recurrent gene mutations are key determinants of prognosis in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and are incorporated into the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) classification. TP53 mutations (TP53mut), present in 10–20% of cases, identify a subgroup with particularly adverse outcomes, frequently associated with complex karyotype (CK). TP53 alterations, including deletions and point mutations, disrupt critical pathways involved in genomic stability and apoptosis. TP53mut have been recognized as adverse-risk group and integrated into the ELN classification since 2017 [1]. Importantly, TP53 allelic state further refines risk stratification, with poorer outcomes reported in patients harbouring multi-hit TP53 alterations [2, 3]. Accumulating evidence supports the use of hypomethylating agent (HMA)–based regimens as a common frontline strategy in TP53mut AML, particularly in patients not eligible for intensive chemotherapy [4]. However, fit patients with lower TP53 variant allele frequency (VAF) may benefit from intensive chemotherapy. Here, we report the outcomes of TP53mut AML patients treated with intensive chemotherapy (IC) in prospective ALFA studies.

We included all patients screened for TP53mut at AML diagnosis enrolled between 2008 and 2016 in one of the following prospective trials: ALFA-0701 [5, 6] (#NCT00927498), ALFA-0702 [7] (#NCT00932412) or ALFA-1200 [8] (#NCT01966497) (Supplementary Figure 1). Patients included in the studies had previously untreated de novo AML, except in the ALFA-1200 trial, which also included AML secondary to myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) (additional information available in Supplementary Methods).

Among the 978 retrospectively screened patients, 83 patients (8%) displayed at least one TP53mut, including 70 (84%) with a VAF ≥ 10%, consistent with the definition of ‘AML with mutated TP53’ according to the International Consensus Classification of Myeloid Neoplasms and Acute Leukemias (ICC) [9]. Median age at diagnosis was 62 years in TP53mut and TP53wt groups (p = 0.23). The proportion of secondary AML was 8% in both groups (p = 0.83). White blood cell (WBC) counts were lower at diagnosis in TP53mut patients compared to TP53wt patients (3.0 × 109/L vs 8.8 × 109/L, p < 0.0001). Proportion of bone marrow blasts was also lower in TP53mut patients than in TP53wt patients (40% vs 58%, respectively, p = 0.002) (Supplementary Table 1).

Although distributed throughout the gene, 90% of TP53mut were located in the DNA-binding domain (Supplementary Figure 2). Median TP53 VAF was 43% (IQR:21-69), with 13 patients harbouring TP53mut with VAF < 10% (TP53<10%). TP53mut were strongly associated with CK (85%), including 64% among patients with TP53<10% (Supplementary Figure 3A). Conversely, CK were less frequent in TP53wt patients (6%, corrected p value [q]< 0.0001). Only 6% of TP53mut patients had a normal karyotype, compared with 60% in TP53wt patients (q < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table 1). Adverse cytogenetic alterations (monosomy 5/5q deletion, 7q deletion, and 17p abnormalities) were significantly more frequent in TP53mut cases (all q < 0.0001). Notably, 12 TP53wt patients had 17p alterations, of whom 75% had a CK (Fig. 1A). TP53mut patients harboured a median of 3 additional mutations (IQR:2-4). The most frequent co-mutations were NF1 (19%), DNMT3A (12%), NRAS (11%), TET2 (11%), with 39% of TP53mut patients harbouring at least one concurrent signaling mutation (NF1, NRAS, KRAS, PTPN11, CBL, RIT1, FLT3 and KIT) (Supplementary Figures 3B, C).

Among TP53mut patients, 17% displayed monoallelic TP53 alterations (TP53mono) and 83% had biallelic TP53 alterations (TP53bi), including 50 patients with TP53 deletion, 18 with copy-neutral loss-of-heterozygosity (CN-LOH) at the TP53 locus and one patient with at least 2 TP53mut. Demographic and biological characteristics were similar in TP53mono and TP53bi patients (Table 1). CK were more frequently observed in TP53bi than in TP53mono patients (99% vs 21%, q-value < 0.0001). However, CK remained more frequent in TP53mono than in TP53wt patients (21% vs 6%, p = 0.042). Patients with TP53bi had fewer additional mutations than those with TP53mono (median: 1 vs 3, p = 0.021). Signaling mutations occurred in 36% of TP53bi vs 50% of TP53mono patients (q = 0.68). Myelodysplasia-related mutations were more frequent in TP53mono than in TP53bi patients (57% vs 14%, q = 0.003).

With a median follow-up of 5.4 years, TP53mut AML was associated with lower complete remission (CR)/complete remission with incomplete platelet recovery (CRp) rates (52% vs 76% in TP53wt, p < 0.0001) and remained a strong independent negative predictor of response (OR = 0.29, 95%CI:0.18–0.47, p < 0.0001) after adjusting for age, WBC, and trial type.

TP53mut patients had markedly lower 2-year overall survival (2y-OS) (11% vs 56%, p < 0.0001) and higher 2-year cumulative incidence of relapse (74% vs 42%, p < 0.0001) than TP53wt, with TP53mut remaining an independent predictor of poor OS (HR = 2.69, 95%CI:2.07-3.49, p < 0.0001) and relapse (csHR=2.18, 95%CI:1.48-3.22, p < 0.0001) in multivariable analyses (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Figure 4A, Supplementary Table 2).

In a 2-month landmark analysis from diagnosis, TP53mut patients achieving CR/CRp had significantly higher 2y-OS than those who did not (19% vs 0%, p = 0.0001) (Fig. 1C). Among the 14 patients undergoing HSCT in first CR/CRp, considering HSCT in first CR/CRp as a time-dependent variable, HSCT showed a non-significant trend toward improved OS (HR = 0.56, 95%CI:0.27-1.19, p = 0.13) (Supplementary Figure 4B).

TP53bi patients had lower 2y-OS than TP53mono (4% vs 43%, p = 0.0002) and TP53wt/ELN-2022 adverse-risk group patients (p < 0.0001), while TP53mono outcomes were similar to the TP53wt/ELN-2022 adverse-risk group (p = 0.6; Fig. 1D). TP53bi remained an independent predictor of poor OS in multivariable analysis accounting for age and WBC count (HR = 4.13, 95%CI:1.87-9.13, p = 0.0004; Supplementary Table 3).

Among TP53<10% patients, 64% had a TP53mono alteration, and they showed higher CR/CRp rate (91% vs 46%, p = 0.008) and superior 2y-OS (45% vs 6%, p = 0.010) than TP53≥10% patients (Supplementary Figure 4C).

Among the 965 patients with available karyotype, 12% had a CK, 59% of whom were TP53mut. Compared with TP53wt/CK− patients, the presence of either TP53mut or CK was associated with a significantly reduced 2y-OS (42% and 39%, respectively), while co-occurrence of TP53mut and CK conferred the worst prognosis (2y-OS: 6%, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1E). In TP53mut AML, CK was associated with inferior 2y-OS (6% vs 42% in CK−, p = 0.007).

In TP53mut patients, signaling mutations were associated with lower CR/CRp rates (34% vs 63%, p = 0.014), a trend toward inferior OS (2y-OS: 9% vs 12%, p = 0.10), and remained an independent predictor of CR/CRp in multivariable analysis (OR = 0.31, 95%CI: 0.10-0.88, p = 0.032) (Supplementary Table 4). In TP53bi AML, signaling mutations conferred markedly inferior 2y-OS (0% vs 7%, p = 0.002) (Fig. 1F). In multivariable analysis accounting for age, WBC count, TP53 allelic state, TP53 VAF, signaling mutation and CK, only TP53 allelic state (HR = 7.20, 95%CI:1.20-43.15, p = 0.031 for TP53bi) and presence of signaling mutations (HR = 1.94, 95%CI:1.16–3.23, p = 0.011) were associated with worse OS (Supplementary Table 5).

This study highlights both the molecular profile and the prognostic impact of TP53mut according to allelic state in a large cohort of newly diagnosed AML, predominantly de novo (92%), treated with intensive chemotherapy. TP53mut were observed in 8% of patients, likely underestimated as secondary and therapy-related AML were excluded from all ALFA trials except the ALFA-1200 trial. As expected, TP53mut was associated with poor CR/CRp rates, high relapse risk, and reduced OS. Despite lower CR/CRp rates, TP53mut patients achieving CR/CRp showed improved OS compared to non-responders, supporting the benefit of first-line IC. Among responders, a non-significant trend toward improved OS with HSCT was observed, consistent with retrospective data in TP53mut AML, highlighting its potential role as a post-remission consolidation strategy [10]. However, interpretation is limited by the small number of transplanted patients in our study (n = 14). Although IC remains a valid first-line option, about half of patients require salvage therapy, emphasizing the need for novel strategies. This study was conducted in patients intensively treated, before the era of less intensive treatments. A meta-analysis showed similar CR rates for IC and venetoclax in combination with HMA, but OS remained poor across treatments [11].

As expected, a high proportion of TP53bi (83%) was observed among TP53mut patients, consistent with prior reports associating TP53 multi-hits with disease progression [12]. TP53 multi-hit defined an ultra-high-risk subset within the ELN adverse-risk group. CK was highly associated with TP53 mutations (85%), particularly in TP53bi cases (99%), supporting its role as a surrogate marker of TP53 multi-hit status, as suggested by ICC [9]. In contrast, TP53mono patients showed relatively better outcomes, likely reflecting a distinct ontogenic pathway in which TP53 acts as a subclonal event within an MDS-like genetic background enriched for myelodysplasia-related mutations. The 10% VAF threshold in ELN-2022 aims to exclude TP53-related clonal haematopoiesis [13]. Patients with TP53<10% or TP53mono remain heterogeneous and may harbour CK. Although its prognostic impact could not be evaluated here, prior studies suggest that CK confers a poorer prognosis in such patients [14]. These findings underscore the importance of assessing TP53 allelic state and loss of heterozygosity, with CK serving as a practical surrogate marker for TP53 allelic state assessment in clinical practice.

Consistent with prior reports, CK were frequently observed in TP53mut, especially in TP53bi, underscoring TP53’s role in maintaining genomic stability. Interestingly, TP53wt patients harbouring CK showed similarly poor OS, indicating that CK independently confers an adverse prognosis and that additional mechanisms contribute to chromosomal instability.

Although co-mutations are rare in TP53mut patients, signaling mutations (NF1, NRAS, PTPN11) and epigenetic mutations (DNMT3A, TET2) were the most common concurrent alterations. In TP53bi patients, signaling mutations — suggestive of later clonal events based on integrated analysis of VAFs and copy number variation [15] — were associated with particularly poor outcomes and represented a hallmark of aggressiveness. In a mouse model of the TP53 R175H hotspot mutation, Nras mutations cooperated with p53 GOF to dysregulate hematopoietic transcription, trigger NF-κB–driven inflammation, and accelerate AML development [16].

This study highlights the prognostic impact of TP53 allelic state in intensively treated, newly diagnosed TP53mut AML patients. In this cohort, achievement of CR/CRp was associated with improved OS, likely reflecting underlying differences in disease biology among TP53mut patients. TP53mut AML remains a biologically heterogeneous entity, with TP53bi defining a very high-risk subgroup. Prognosis should consider both TP53 allelic state and concurrent alterations. CK should serve as a surrogate for TP53bi, while signaling mutations represent a hallmark of aggressiveness and may drive treatment resistance. Integrating genomic and cytogenetic alterations is essential to refine prognostic stratification in TP53mut AML.
