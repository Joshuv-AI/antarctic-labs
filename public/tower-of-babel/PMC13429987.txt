Rare occurrence of an intumescent cataract in a diabetic domestic longhair cat
Authors: Jonathan H Chin, Matthew T Sanders, Robin G Stanley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCase summaryA domestic longhair cat in Australia was referred to an ophthalmology service with the complaint of lens opacity. An incipient cataract was diagnosed in the right eye and an intumescent cataract with an equatorial lens capsule rupture in the left eye. The patient was being treated for diabetes mellitus, and previous concerns for the eyes had included conjunctivitis and left-sided periocular swelling and epiphora. Systemic work-up performed over the 7 months before ophthalmic referral included complete blood counts, biochemistry, serology (feline immunodeficiency virus, feline leukaemia virus, toxoplasmosis, cryptococcosis), thoracic radiographs, and ocular ultrasound and electroretinography performed before phacoemulsification. The patient did not have evidence of active uveitis at the initial ophthalmological examination, and historical serology excluded some common infectious causes of uveitis. The cataracts were treated successfully by phacoemulsification in both eyes, restoring vision. However, an intraocular lens was implanted only in the right eye, as the pre-existing lens capsule tear in the left eye precluded intraocular lens placement. Based on the medical history and clinical presentation, diabetes mellitus was suspected to be the cause of the cataracts.Relevance and novel informationIntumescent cataracts are reported in humans and dogs but have rarely been reported in domestic cats. Nonetheless, the intumescent cataract in this patient had similar characteristics to those in diabetic canine cataracts.Keywords: Intumescent cataract, diabetes mellitus, phacoemulsification, cataract surgery

Introduction

Cataracts are a common cause of vision loss in dogs and humans but are comparatively uncommon in cats. 1 Cataract intumescence results from swelling and congestion of lens fibres from osmotic pressure related to lens protein degeneration in humans, 2 and sorbitol accumulation in canine diabetes mellitus (DM). 3 A clouded leopard with intumescent cataracts is discussed in a case report 4 ; however, to the authors’ knowledge, there are no publications reporting intumescent cataracts in domestic cats. The aim of this case report was to describe the clinical features and treatment of an intumescent cataract in a domestic longhair (DLH) cat.

Case description

An 8-year-and-9-month-old, male castrated DLH cat that had always resided in Australia was referred to an ophthalmology service for lens opacity.

The patient was first treated for DM in April 2022 with glargine insulin, entering remission in July 2022 but relapsing in January 2023. In July 2023, the cat presented to his regular veterinarian for intermittent tremoring with the glargine dose at 2.5 IU q12h. Blood work found glucose 14.7 mmol/l (reference interval [RI] 3.2–7.5), alkaline phosphatase 54 IU/l (RI 19–100) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 103 IU/l (RI 19–100). Serology was negative for feline immunodeficiency virus, feline leukaemia virus, toxoplasmosis and cryptococcosis. The cause of the tremoring was not determined and no treatment changes were made. In December 2023, the cat presented collapsed to the emergency department of a multispecialty facility; problems included glucose 0.5 mmol/l (RI 3.5–6.8), creatinine 56 umol/l (RI 71–212), urea 2.4 mmol/l (RI 5.7–12.9), ALT 248 U/l (RI 12–130), hypotension, hypothermia and bradycardia. The patient was admitted and treatment included warming, oxygen support, intravenous fluid therapy with 5% glucose, a dopamine infusion and antibiotics. During hospitalisation, he had absent menace response at both eyes (OU), although this was attributed to a neurological problem or hypoxic injury related to the collapse. When stable and discharged, the follow-up appointment at his regular vet also noted absent menace in grossly normal eyes. In February 2024, he re-presented to the emergency department with the complaint of a swollen left eye (OS). The OS lens was noted to be cloudy with periocular swelling and epiphora. The differential diagnosis list included feline herpes, conjunctivitis or trauma, although no specific traumatic incident had been observed. Treatment commenced with transmucosal buprenorphine and topical chloramphenicol 1% (Chlorsig; Aspen Australia). The patient was referred to the standalone ophthalmology practice 2 days later for lens opacity OS.

A complete ophthalmic examination performed by a specialist ophthalmologist included neuro-ophthalmic examination, slit lamp biomicroscopy (SL-17; Kowa Japan), a fundus examination (Scanoptics SO-2700; Scan Optics) and rebound tonometry (Tonovet TV01; Jorgensen Laboratories). Menace response was positive at the right eye (OD) and negative at OS, with dazzle reflex positive OU. A pupillary light reflex (PLR) was present and consensual OU. The OD PLR was normal and complete, but the OS PLR was reduced/incomplete owing to the lens impeding complete iris constriction. There was no aqueous flare OU. The anterior chamber was normal depth OD but shallow OS due to the intumescent cataract. Intraocular pressure (IOP) measured 13 mmHg OD and 20 mmHg OS, Schirmer Tear Test-1 measured 18 mm wetting/min OU and fluorescein stain uptake was negative OU. There was an incipient cataract OD with anterior cortical and posterior subcapsular opacities (Figures 1 and 2) and an intumescent cataract with widened suture clefts and shallow anterior chamber OS (Figures 3 –5). The iris OS exhibited mild haemorrhage ventrally, and some pigment was noted at the ventral anterior lens capsule (Figure 5). The fundus was normal OD and unable to be assessed OS. No significant conjunctivitis, signs of trauma or periocular swelling were noted on this examination; therefore, it was presumed the periocular swelling noted by the owner and emergency room vet was associated with the change in intraocular appearance due to the intumescent cataract. The patient was planned for phacoemulsification in both eyes because of concern that the right cataract may replicate the rapid maturation and swelling noted in the left cataract.

On the day of surgery, the patient was premedicated with methadone (0.3 mg/kg IM, Ilium Methadone; Troy Labs) and acepromazine (0.02 mg/kg IM, ACP2; Ceva). Electroretinography (RetinoGraphics BPM-300; Retinographics) showed b-wave amplitudes of 311 μV OD and 257 μV OS. Transcorneal B-mode ultrasonography (MyLabOne; Esaote) showed anterior and posterior cataracts OD (Figure 6), and an intumescent cataract with a poorly defined echogenicity at the equatorial region OS raising concern for a capsular rupture (Figure 7). There were no posterior segment abnormalities OU. Both eyes received phenylephrine 2.5% (Minims; Bausch and Lomb), dexamethasone 0.1% (Maxidex; Alcon) and tropicamide 1% (Mydriacyl; Alcon) immediately preoperatively.

General anaesthesia was induced with alfaxalone (5 mg/kg IV to effect, Alfaxan; Jurox) and maintained with isoflurane (Isothesia NX; Henry Schein). Neuromuscular block occurred with atracurium (0.2 mg/kg IV, Tracrium; Aspen Australia) administered at the first corneal incision. Phacoemulsification (Whitestar Signature Pro; Johnson & Johnson Vision) was performed. At OS, the shallow anterior chamber precluded our ability to perform a pre-phacoemulsification continuous curvilinear capsulorrhexis (CCC); therefore, the phacoemulsification surgery was modified by extracting the lens material first through a small incision in the anterior capsule, then the CCC was completed after lens removal. An intraocular lens (IOL) (Univers feline; Dioptrix) of +53.0 dioptres was implanted in OD; however, a pre-existing capsular tear in the ventral equatorial region in OS precluded our ability to place an IOL, leaving OS aphakic. IOP remained normal OU during routine monitoring in hospital.

Postoperative medications were ketorolac 0.5% (q8h OU, Acular; Allergan), sodium hyaluronate 0.2% (q8h OU, Hylo-forte; Ursapharm), brinzolamide 1% (q12h OU, Brinzoquin; Novartis Pharmaceuticals), amoxycillin-clavulanic acid (12.5 mg/kg q12h PO, Amoxyclav 50; Dechra), meloxicam (0.05 mg/kg PO q24h, Loxicom; Norbrook) and glargine (Lantus; Sanofi).

One day postoperatively, menace was positive OU, IOPs were 40 mmHg OD and 14 mmHg OS with aqueous flare 1/4+ OU. OD had an erosion in the dorsal cornea, with a fibrin strand between the ventral iris and the corneal incision. Brinzolamide 1% (Brinzoquin; Novartis Pharmaceuticals) and latanoprost 0.005% (Xalatan; Pfizer) was administered OD with the subsequent IOP 27 mmHg 1 h later. Ketorolac was discontinued OD until the erosion resolved. By day 3 postoperatively, the corneal defect OD had resolved and IOPs were 16 mmHg OD and 11 mmHg OS with trace flare OU. Vision and IOP continued to be normal at subsequent rechecks, and aqueous flare resolved by postoperative day 32. Meloxicam and brinzolamide were weaned over a few weeks, and the patient continued ocular lubrication and ketorolac q24h (Figure 8).

Discussion

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first reported case of an intumescent cataract in the domestic cat. In addition, the cataract was suspected to be caused by DM.

One French study of 404 canine cataracts found DM was the fourth most common cause, accounting for 4.7% of cataracts, 5 while another French study of 268 cats found DM caused only 0.4% of cataracts. 6 DM is a common endocrine disease of cats, 7 with prevalences reported in the range of 0.2–0.58%,7,8 with the median age at diagnosis being 13 years. 7 DM in dogs has been reported at a similar prevalence of 0.34–0.36%.9,10 Cataracts are common in diabetic dogs, with one study finding that 75% of diabetic dogs developed cataracts within 12 months from DM diagnosis. 11 DM-associated cataracts are comparatively rare in cats, and although 56% of diabetic cats in one study population were observed to have lens opacity, none of these opacities were blinding, in contrast to what is usually observed in diabetic dogs. 1

Cataract formation in diabetic dogs occurs when glucose levels exceed the phosphorylating capacity of the enzyme hexokinase, with the excess glucose shunted into the alternate sorbitol pathway involving aldose reductase.3,8 Sorbitol cannot easily diffuse through the lens capsule leading to osmotic forces causing lens fibre swelling and rupture, vacuole formation, and subsequent cataractogenesis and intumescence. 12 An in vivo study found aldose reductase activity was significantly lower in the lenses of older cats (aged 10–18 years), compared with dogs and young cats. 8 As more than 80% of diabetic cats and dogs are over 7 years old, 13 this difference in enzyme activity is thought to be partially responsible for the disparity in prevalence of cataracts in cats and dogs affected by DM.

In diabetic dogs, intumescent cataracts are the most common type, representing 53.4% of cataracts, 14 and spontaneous lens capsule rupture was associated with rapid progression and intumescence of the cataract. 12 Cataracts that progressed rapidly were more likely to have spontaneous lens capsule rupture, 12 and the location of the capsular rupture was equatorial in 96.7% of affected eyes. 12 The intumescent cataract and capsule rupture in this cat developed within 2 months, as lens opacity was first noted just before referral and clinical examination 2 months earlier did not reveal lens opacity. The reason for the variation in cataract maturity in the eyes is not known, although regardless of the initial cause, the cataract in OS matured first and became intumescent. This was possibly caused by an osmotic gradient developing from lens protein degeneration or DM-associated accumulation of intra-lenticular sorbitol.

Cats potentially have better outcomes than dogs after cataract surgery, 15 with reports that postoperative inflammation is easier to control.15,16 One multicentric study of 71 cats found overall visual success of 92.6% at 12 months, 17 with uveitis, postoperative ocular hypertension and corneal ulceration being the most common complications in the immediate postoperative period, 17 as observed in this patient. Corneal erosions are more common in cats than dogs after g﻿eneral anaesthesia; 18 the cause is likely multifactorial and due to a combination of reduced tear production, exposure, trauma, medications and surgical positioning. 18 Another consideration in feline vs canine cataract treatment is the rare incidence of feline postoperative ocular sarcoma, 17 including risk factors such as chronic uveitis and previous intraocular surgery.

Another cause of feline cataracts is Encephalitozoon cuniculi.19,20 Serology for E cuniculi antibodies and PCR analysis of aqueous and lens material are possible, but were locally unavailable at the time.19,21 E cuniculi most commonly causes focal anterior cortical cataracts that can progress in younger cats, 21 and intumescent cataracts have not been reported. Another limitation in this case is that the serology for common causes of feline uveitis was performed 7 months earlier, and the last complete blood count and biochemistry were performed 2 months before the ophthalmology referral. There is the possibility, therefore, that a more recent infectious or systemic problem contributed to the formation of the cataracts. With these limitations in mind, the history of DM and the similarities of the intumescent cataract to those in diabetic dogs, it is reasonable to suspect DM as the cause of the cataracts.

Conclusions

This case report highlights the rare occurrence of an intumescent cataract in a domestic cat. although difficult to prove definitively, the cataract was suspected to be caused by DM, with the cataract sharing features with the intumescent cataracts seen in diabetic dogs. Further reports of intumescent cataracts will help us to understand the risk factors in cats.
