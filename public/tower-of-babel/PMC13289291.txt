Cost-Effectiveness of promoting preventive oral self-care in China: a population-based modelling study
Authors: Chang Ma, Fan Cheng, Xuanjing Li, Qian Li, Feng Yu, Xiang Gao, Shan Zhao, Jianxiang Tao, Yan Li, Yipeng Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction Oral diseases affect nearly half of the global population and impose substantial health and economic burdens, particularly in China. However, evidence on the cost-effectiveness of preventive oral health interventions remains limited. This study aims to evaluate the long-term cost-effectiveness of promoting population-based oral self-care behaviours across China.MethodsWe developed the China Oral Health Model, a prevention-oriented population policy simulation that projects annual transitions across major oral health states (dental caries, periodontitis, tooth loss and edentulism) among individuals aged ≥12 years. Preventive self-care strategies were modelled as relative reductions in the probabilities of disease onset and progression, implemented through changes in intermediate clinical states. The model was parameterised using nationally representative data from the Fourth National Oral Health Survey of China, 2015 to 2017, supplemented by age-specific prevalence estimates from the literature. We evaluated three key oral self-care behaviours: brushing teeth twice daily, using dental floss and using mouthwash. Intervention effect parameters for brushing, flossing and mouthwash were obtained from published clinical and epidemiological studies, with uncertainty assessed in sensitivity analyses. We conducted a societal-perspective cost-effectiveness analysis, stratified by rural and urban populations, including intervention costs estimated from market price data. Outcomes included lifetime reductions in tooth loss, total costs, quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) compared with current practice. Cost-effectiveness was assessed against a willingness-to-pay threshold of $12,720.20 per QALY (one time GDP per capita), with threshold sensitivity analyses using one to three times GDP per capita.ResultsBrushing teeth twice daily was the only cost-saving intervention, reducing periodontitis by 50.2% relative to status quo, dental caries by 6.5% and tooth loss by 24.8%, while generating $718.82 billion in medical cost savings. The intervention yielded 148.27 million QALYs nationally (98.10 million in urban areas and 50.17 million in rural areas), with a greater reduction in periodontitis observed in rural regions (1.38 percentage points higher than urban). In contrast, dental flossing and mouthwash use increased costs and were not cost-effective at the one time GDP-per-capita threshold (ICERs of $49,677.58/QALY and $84,283.51/QALY, respectively). Findings were robust in threshold sensitivity analyses using one to three times GDP per capita.ConclusionPromoting twice-daily toothbrushing is cost-saving and improves oral health outcomes in China, particularly by reducing periodontitis and tooth loss, with larger periodontitis reductions in rural areas. These findings support the integration of basic oral hygiene practices into national health promotion and disease prevention strategies.

Introduction

Oral health is essential to overall health and well-being.1 Globally, oral diseases affect an estimated 3.5 billion people, with nearly 75% of those affected residing in low- and middle-income countries.2 From 1990 to 2019, the number of oral disease cases rose by over 1 billion—outpacing population growth over the same period.3 In 2015, China bore the second-largest global burden of oral conditions, with an estimated economic impact of $60.11 billion.4

Among major oral diseases, untreated dental caries in permanent teeth is the most prevalent, affecting approximately 2 billion people, followed by severe periodontal disease, which affects about 1 billion.5 These two conditions account for the majority of the global oral disease burden and contribute to significant functional, aesthetic and systemic health consequences. Poor oral health is associated with increased risks of systemic conditions including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, rheumatoid arthritis, preterm birth, respiratory illness, colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease and Alzheimer’s disease.616 According to China’s Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey (2015–2017), both dental caries and periodontitis remain highly prevalent, with a marked decline in periodontal health among middle-aged and older adults between 2005 and 2015.1720 However, access to dental care remains uneven: services are disproportionately concentrated in urban areas, while many rural communities lack specialised providers and face financial barriers due to limited insurance coverage.21 Preventive oral self-care behaviours are important for oral disease prevention. The WHO specifically encourages twice-daily tooth brushing with fluoride-containing toothpaste, while other practices considered in our analysis, such as interdental cleaning and mouthwash use, are supported by relevant clinical guidance.5 22 Systematic reviews and clinical studies indicate that these behaviours not only reduce caries incidence but also improve periodontal parameters, including gingival inflammation and bleeding indices.2326 The WHO’s Global Strategy and Action Plan on Oral Health (2023–2030) emphasises a shift from treatment to prevention, advocating oral health promotion in families, schools and workplaces.27 Despite this, adherence to recommended oral hygiene practices remains low in China. Although 80% of individuals report daily brushing, only 30% brush twice daily as recommended, and fewer than 2% regularly use dental floss.19 28 29 These shortfalls are more pronounced in rural areas, contributing to widening oral health disparities.

While the clinical benefits of preventive oral self-care are well documented, evidence on the long-term, population-level cost-effectiveness of basic oral self-care behaviours (eg, twice-daily tooth brushing, interdental cleaning and mouth rinse use) among adolescents and adults remains limited, particularly in low- and middle-income settings such as China. Although some economic evaluations have been conducted in China, these have largely focused on specific populations (eg, children) or targeted interventions rather than population-wide preventive self-care strategies.30 31 Existing economic evaluations have largely focused on school-based oral health promotion programmes for children and broader self-care systems for non-dental minor ailments.3234 However, long-term economic evaluations of basic oral self-care strategies in China remain limited.

To address this gap, we developed a prevention-oriented population policy simulation to evaluate the long-term cost-effectiveness of improving three key oral self-care behaviours—twice-daily tooth brushing, dental flossing and mouthwash use—among individuals aged 12 years and older in China. We assessed outcomes separately for rural and urban populations to inform resource prioritisation and support policy development aimed at reducing the burden of oral disease and improving population oral health.

Method

Model design and study population

We developed the China Oral Health Model, a prevention-oriented population simulation that projects annual transitions across major oral health states among individuals aged ≥12 years. Individuals younger than 12 years were not included due to the influence of mixed dentition and tooth eruption processes, which were beyond the scope of the current model. Preventive self-care strategies were modelled as relative reductions in the probabilities of disease onset and progression, implemented through changes in intermediate clinical states (eg, dental plaque and gingivitis) (online supplemental text I). The model includes the following health states: healthy, dental caries, periodontitis, comorbid caries and periodontitis, tooth loss, edentulism and death (figure 1). Comorbid conditions are permitted, reflecting the simultaneous occurrence of dental caries and periodontitis.

Overview of modelling framework and transmission model structure. ((A) The transmission model used in oral health preventive model. (B) Schematic of the China Oral Health Model. The model begins with a synthetic population aged 12 and older, derived from the Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey and the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study. It outlines various health states, including dental caries, periodontitis and tooth loss, with transitions indicating disease progression. Interventions focus on self-care behaviours such as brushing twice daily, using dental floss and regular mouthwash use, which impact health outcomes. Primary outcomes include Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) and the incidence of tooth loss.

At the structural level, once an individual develops an oral disease, the model assumes no return to a never-diseased baseline state. While it is recognised that early caries lesions can be reversed with fluoride or remineralisation therapy, our model adopts a conservative, public health-oriented perspective focused on the progression of clinically manifest disease. Therefore, we do not model remission from the ‘dental caries’ and ‘periodontitis’ states, which represent established disease requiring treatment. Even after treatment, affected teeth or periodontal tissues do not return to a never-diseased baseline state. Instead, preventive strategies influence disease burden through reductions in disease incidence and progression risks.

For the purpose of this population-level model, ‘tooth loss’ is modelled as an absorbing state that signifies the onset of clinically meaningful tooth loss. While individuals may lose multiple teeth over time, the model captures the progression of population-level dentition deterioration through subsequent transitions from the ‘tooth loss’ state to ‘edentulism’. This approach simplifies the modelling of cumulative disease burden without imposing an artificial limit on the number of tooth loss events per individual.

The model runs on annual cycles and follows individuals from age 12 until death. Intermediate biological processes, including dental plaque accumulation and gingival inflammation, were not modelled as separate health states because they are highly prevalent, fluctuate over time, and are not consistently reported as stable population-level states in epidemiological data. Instead, these processes were incorporated as underlying mechanisms used to derive direct transition probabilities from healthy to clinically defined disease states. Initial health states distribution was derived from the actual prevalence of oral diseases among the Chinese population aged 12 and older, as reported in the fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey in China. Other model parameters were derived from published epidemiological studies, national survey data, and publicly available reports (table 1). Full derivation methods and equations are provided in online supplemental text I and III. The study used de-identified, publicly available data and was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval.

Parameter estimation

All analyses were conducted separately for urban and rural populations to reflect differences in baseline disease prevalence, oral hygiene practices and cost inputs. Setting-specific parameter values are provided in table 1 and online supplemental tables 26-33. Age-specific epidemiological data and behavioural risk information were combined to estimate cumulative disease risks, which were subsequently converted into annual transition probabilities using explicit mathematical relationships (online supplemental text I). Across scenarios, transition probabilities varied by age group and intervention strategy, with values generally ranging from low-probability annual risks for disease onset to higher probabilities for disease progression. Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) were calculated by multiplying the time spent in each health state by its corresponding utility weight. Utility weights were derived from published studies35 and ranged from 1.0 for the healthy state to 0.9326 for edentulism, with intermediate values of 0.9898 for dental caries and 0.9932 for periodontitis.

Costs were estimated from a societal perspective and categorised as direct medical costs, preventive self-care product costs and indirect productivity losses. Direct medical costs included treatment expenditures for dental caries, periodontitis, tooth loss, and edentulism, with residence-specific inputs where applicable. Preventive self-care product costs were estimated using publicly available retail price data for oral care products (toothbrushes, dental floss, and mouthwash) obtained from a major e-commerce platform in China (JD.com). Products with the highest sales volumes were selected to reflect typical consumer choices. Across conditions, direct medical costs varied substantially by disease severity and treatment type, ranging from relatively low costs for early-stage treatments to higher costs associated with tooth loss and prosthetic rehabilitation. Indirect costs captured productivity losses attributable to clinically manifest oral conditions. In the base-case analysis, two evidence-supported pathways were included: periodontitis-associated presenteeism among working-age adults and caregiver productivity loss related to additional school absence among children with dental caries. Other potential productivity pathways were not included due to limited or heterogeneous evidence. Cost estimates varied by age group and residence, with representative values and ranges summarised in table 1 and detailed calculations provided in (online supplemental text III and tables 5-25,30-33).

Primary health outcomes included QALYs gained and the number of tooth loss events averted. Age-specific natural mortality rates were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study.36 All costs were converted to USD using an exchange rate of 1 CNY=0.1403 USD. Both costs and QALYs were discounted at an annual rate of 3.5%.37

Model validation

Model validity was assessed by comparing model-predicted age-specific prevalence of dental caries and periodontitis with external epidemiological benchmarks, including estimates from the Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey and GBD Study.19 36 For each age group, model-predicted prevalence estimates were quantitatively compared with the corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals reported by GBD. Predicted values were considered consistent when they fell within these intervals and showed comparable age-specific patterns to the survey estimates. Across all modelled age groups, predicted prevalence estimates were consistent with national survey data and remained within the corresponding GBD uncertainty intervals, indicating acceptable external validity of the model (online supplemental tables 1-4).36

Simulated interventions

The effects of oral self-care behaviours on oral disease incidence were derived from published clinical studies evaluating oral hygiene behaviours, including antimicrobial mouthrinse and toothbrushing.38 39 These studies reported intervention effects on intermediate clinical indicators, including dental plaque and gingival inflammation, which were then used to inform the estimation of transition probabilities from healthy to disease states through explicit mathematical relationships. ‘Mouthwash use’ was defined as once-daily use of an over-the-counter antimicrobial mouthrinse containing 0.1% cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC) and 0.025% sodium fluoride, consistent with the trial used to parameterise effectiveness.38 To enhance comparability across strategies, we preferentially used evidence with intervention definitions and age ranges consistent with the modelled population and with outcomes measured under comparable follow-up periods; remaining uncertainty in effect estimates was explored in sensitivity analyses. These behaviours were modelled as interventions influencing the risk of dental plaque and gingivitis, thereby altering downstream risks of caries and periodontitis. Each intervention scenario was compared with a status quo (no intervention) scenario to estimate health gains, medical cost savings and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs). Primary outcomes included QALYs gained, tooth loss averted and cost per QALY.

Sensitivity analysis

Both one-way deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the primary outcomes. In the one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis, key model parameters, including epidemiological inputs (eg, prevalence, utility weights, and transition probabilities) and cost parameters, were varied using commonly adopted ranges in deterministic sensitivity analyses when precise parameter-specific CIs are unavailable. Specifically, epidemiological parameters were varied by ±10%, while cost parameters were varied by ±20% (online supplemental tables 26-28 and 31-33). The wider range applied to cost parameters reflects the greater inherent variability in treatment expenditures and regional price dispersion observed in real-world data. In the probabilistic sensitivity analyses, probability distributions were assigned to input parameters, and 10 000 Monte Carlo simulations were performed (online supplemental text II). Beta distributions were used for probabilities and utilities, and gamma distributions were used for cost parameters.

We applied a GDP-based willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold, defining an intervention as cost-effective if the ICER was less than the per capita GDP of China (US$ 12,720.20 per QALY).40 Interventions were considered ‘favourable’ when they were cost-saving or had an ICER below the WTP threshold, whereas ‘unfavourable’ interventions had ICERs above the threshold. We additionally conducted threshold sensitivity analyses using one to three times the per capita GDP.41 Results were visualised using tornado diagrams to identify parameters with the greatest impact on ICERs. All analyses were conducted using TreeAge Pro 2022 and R V. 4.2.2. The study adhered to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) 2022, with reporting details provided (online supplemental table 41).

Effectiveness of oral self-care interventions

All three modelled oral self-care interventions were associated with reductions in future oral disease prevalence compared with the status quo (table 2 and online supplemental tables 34-37). Among them, brushing teeth twice daily yielded the greatest overall health benefits, reducing periodontitis by 50.2%, dental caries by 6.5% and tooth loss by 24.8%. Rural populations derived slightly greater benefit from this intervention, with periodontitis reduction exceeding that in urban areas by 1.38 percentage points.

Mouthwash use showed moderate effectiveness, particularly against caries, reducing prevalence by 7.22 percentage points in urban areas and 6.47 percentage points in rural areas. However, its impact on periodontitis was limited to a 3.2% reduction. Dental flossing produced minimal impact on either outcome across all settings.

Age-specific analyses revealed that intervention effectiveness peaked between ages 50 and 70 (figure 3). Among individuals aged 55–59, brushing twice daily reduced periodontitis prevalence from 29.88% to 13.60%, a 16.28 percentage point decline. In the same age group, mouthwash reduced caries prevalence from 31.29% to 27.07%, an 4.22 percentage point reduction. Flossing and mouthwash yielded negligible benefits in other age groups.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

Brushing teeth twice daily was found to be a cost-saving intervention in both urban and rural settings (table 2). In contrast, dental flossing yielded an ICER of $49,677.58 per QALY ($45,968.38 in urban areas and $50,312.43 in rural areas), while mouthwash use produced an ICER of $84,283.51 per QALY ($81,254.93 in urban areas and $91,123.23 in rural areas). Both exceeded the cost-effectiveness threshold of $12,720.20 per QALY, based on China’s 2023 GDP per capita.

Compared with the status quo, brushing twice daily led to substantial QALY gains: 0.09 billion QALYs in urban areas and 0.05 billion QALYs in rural areas. It also produced net savings of $718.82 billion. By contrast, dental flossing increased total costs by $904.65 billion, and mouthwash use raised expenditures by $3,359.41 billion, without proportionate health benefits.

Sensitivity analyses

Results of the one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis (online supplemental figure 5 and tables 38-40) showed that most input parameters had limited influence on ICERs, with the exception of the utility weight for edentulism. Nonetheless, brushing twice daily remained a cost-saving strategy in all tested scenarios across both urban and rural populations.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis confirmed these findings. In 100% of the 10 000 simulations, twice-daily brushing was cost-effective (figure 4A and online supplemental figure 3-4). The cost-effectiveness acceptability curve showed that at the current WTP threshold, the intervention was the dominant strategy in 94% of simulations overall, including 93% in urban areas and 96% in rural areas (figure 4B), demonstrating a high degree of robustness across model uncertainty. Conclusions were unchanged in threshold sensitivity analyses using 1–3×GDP per capita.

Discussion

In this study, we developed the China Oral Health Model, a prevention-oriented population policy simulation designed to project the health and economic consequences of preventive oral self-care interventions across the lifespan. To our knowledge, this is the first model-based economic evaluation of preventive oral hygiene behaviours. Our findings highlight that brushing teeth twice daily is a highly effective and cost-saving intervention for improving oral health, particularly in reducing periodontitis and tooth loss. In contrast, daily use of dental floss and mouthwash, while offering some health benefits, are not cost-effective under current cost and adherence assumptions and do not justify large-scale investment as stand-alone public health interventions.

Our findings are consistent with and extend existing evidence on preventive oral self-care. Previous studies have shown that improved toothbrushing behaviours and fluoride use can reduce dental caries incidence, particularly among children and adolescents.33 Other studies have reported favourable economic results for preventive oral health strategies, including school-based toothbrushing programmes and fluoride-related interventions, in specific settings.4244 Trial-based research has also shown that behavioural and educational interventions can increase toothbrushing frequency and improve oral hygiene practices.45 However, these studies have mainly focused on short-term outcomes, single interventions, or specific populations such as children. In contrast, our study provides a long-term population-level economic evaluation across the life course and assesses multiple oral self-care behaviours within a unified modelling framework. Moreover, brushing has been shown to significantly reduce periodontal inflammation and slow disease progression.23 46 47 However, few studies have quantitatively assessed its cost-effectiveness in the context of population-wide prevention of periodontitis—a gap our study addresses.48 49 Our model confirms that regular brushing, when practised at recommended frequency and duration, is not only clinically effective but economically beneficial, yielding significant cost savings and gains in QALYs.

In contrast, our model suggests that mouthwash and flossing—while effective in reducing dental plaque—offer limited impact on clinical endpoints like periodontitis and tooth loss when implemented in isolation. Mouthwash has demonstrated efficacy against gingivitis and dental plaque in prior trials,50 51 but evidence linking it to significant reductions in periodontitis or caries remains scarce. Some studies have raised concerns about potential adverse effects of prolonged use, including microbial resistance, alterations in oral microbiota, and enamel degradation.5257 Additionally, cost-effectiveness appears limited except when used adjunctively with professional mechanical therapies.58 These findings support our conclusion that mouthwash is not cost-effective as a primary preventive measure.

Similarly, while flossing may reduce plaque accumulation and inflammation, its effectiveness in preventing caries and periodontitis remains uncertain.5963 Multiple trials have failed to demonstrate meaningful reductions in tooth decay, especially in adult populations. The benefits may be more pronounced in children or when used in combination with brushing to target proximal surfaces.64 65 Nonetheless, the relatively high cost and modest benefits of flossing translate into poor cost-effectiveness at a population level. These findings suggest that both mouthwash and floss are better suited as adjuncts to clinical care rather than as stand-alone strategies in national prevention programmes.

Our age-stratified analysis further reveals that the benefits of twice-daily brushing are most pronounced between ages 50 and 70, when the risk of tooth loss and periodontitis increases substantially. For example, among individuals aged 55–59, brushing reduced periodontitis prevalence by over 16 percentage points. These results underscore the importance of targeting middle-aged and older adults in oral health promotion efforts, as this group stands to benefit most from preventive practices. Nonetheless, our findings also demonstrate that early adoption—beginning at age 12—yields substantial cumulative gains. If this cohort adopts twice-daily brushing, national prevalence of periodontitis, tooth loss and edentulism could decline by 50.2%, 24.8% and 51.3%, respectively, generating average per capita savings of $510 in medical expenditures over the life course.

Given China’s marked urban-rural disparities in oral health status, dental care access and self-care behaviours, our results carry important implications for equity.17 21 66 Rural residents bear a higher burden of oral disease and face greater barriers to dental care and oral health education.17 21 Rural residents bear a higher burden of caries, periodontitis and tooth loss, yet have less access to dental care and health education.66 Our analysis found that the rural population may derive even greater relative benefit from improved brushing habits. Tailored public health initiatives—such as community-based health worker programmes, school interventions in rural areas and subsidised access to oral hygiene products—are needed to promote equitable health gains and reduce oral health disparities.

As with all modelling studies, our simulations represent an idealised scenario in which individuals consistently perform effective oral self-care as defined in the evidence base. In real-world settings, sustained adherence and technique are heterogeneous and may be constrained by manual dexterity limitations, motivation and competing life priorities, cognitive decline in older age and socioeconomic barriers. Accordingly, the estimated health gains and cost savings should be interpreted as achievable under sustained adoption and effective technique, and may be attenuated where adherence is lower or technique is suboptimal. At the same time, twice-daily toothbrushing is a simple, low-cost behaviour; implementation may therefore depend more on effective behaviour-change support and reinforcement over time than on product affordability alone.

The study has several limitations. First, the model is based on prevalence data from 2015, which may underestimate the current burden or overestimate future gains given improvements under the Healthy China 2030 initiative. Second, while the model tracks individuals from age 12 onward, it does not incorporate birth cohorts or population growth, limiting its ability to fully capture long-term population dynamics. Third, cost estimates were derived exclusively from the Chinese healthcare context; generalisability to other countries may be limited, particularly where oral care product pricing or healthcare financing structures differ. Fourth, the state-transition structure is simplified. We do not model explicit return-to-health pathways after disease onset, and we represent tooth loss as an aggregate functional state rather than tracking multiple sequential tooth-loss events. This structure is appropriate for a prevention-focused population policy model, but future work could explore more granular treatment-remission-relapse trajectories and tooth-level counting as stronger population data on treatment uptake, adherence, recurrence and the timing of tooth-loss events become available. Fifth, the model does not explicitly represent upstream behavioural and structural determinants (eg, sugar intake, smoking and dental service utilisation), which are instead captured implicitly in the baseline transition probabilities. Finally, the model does not incorporate behavioural barriers to adoption (eg, socioeconomic constraints, knowledge gaps), which may influence real-world effectiveness.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide robust and timely evidence on the health and economic value of preventive oral self-care behaviours in China. The China Oral Health Model offers a scalable framework for evaluating the cost-effectiveness of other oral health interventions, including professional treatments or school-based prevention programmes. Our results strongly support the promotion of twice-daily brushing as a cornerstone of national oral health strategy. This intervention is not only effective and equitable, but also economically sound, generating large-scale savings and health gains.

Health policymakers should prioritise widespread promotion of brushing behaviours through integrated campaigns across schools, workplaces and community settings. These efforts should be tailored to reduce urban–rural disparities and ensure access to affordable oral hygiene products. Given the global burden of oral diseases, the model and findings from this study may also inform international efforts to implement cost-effective oral health promotion strategies in other middle-income countries.
