Stereoselective Syntheses of Octadecanoids, 9(S)‑HODE, and 9(S),10(S),13(S)‑TriHOME
Authors: Myeongsu Shin, Youngjoo Byun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Octadecanoids and their metabolites are known to be biologically active lipid mediators. 9­(S)-HODE and 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME, the main metabolites of linoleic acid produced by 15-lipoxygenase, exhibit enantioselective potency and function as endogenous ligands in biological systems. However, the structural modification of 9­(S)-HODE and 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME in pharmaceutical applications remains largely underexplored. Herein, we report the stereoselective syntheses of 9­(S)-HODE and 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME to enable access to bioactive octadecanoid analogues.

Introduction

Octadecanoids, oxygenated metabolites of linoleic acid (LA), have been reported as a diverse class of bioactive lipid mediators involved in the inhibition of cancer metastasis, anti-inflammatory signaling pathways, pain perception, epidermal barrier formation, and antioxidant defense. , Several metabolites of linoleic acid have been reported to act as potent lipid mediators, including 9­(S)-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (9­(S)-HODE, 11a), 9-oxo-octadecadienoic acid (9-oxo-ODE, 12), and 9,10,13-trihydroxyoctadecenoic acid (9,10,13-TriHOME, 26a and 26b) (Figure ). 15-Lipoxygenase-2 (15-LOX-2) catalyzes the stereospecific oxygenation of LA to form 9­(S)-hydroperoxyoctadecadienoic acid (9S-HpODE), which can be reduced by glutathione peroxidase (GPx) to 9­(S)-HODE with a conjugated diene system. 9­(S)-HODE is subsequently metabolized to 9-oxo-ODE by hydroxy fatty acid dehydrogenase. Both 9­(S)-HODE and 9-oxo-ODE function as endogenous ligands of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ). , In addition to PPARγ activation, 9­(S)-HODE acts as a ligand for the G2A (GPR132) receptor and promotes pro-inflammatory signaling pathways in human keratinocytes, contributing to skin photoinduced skin aging. , It has also been reported to modulate nociceptive signaling via TRPV1 activation, suggesting a role as a lipid mediator of pain. Consistent with its PPARγ activity, 9-HODE induces the expression of fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4) in macrophages and monocytes in a PPARγ-dependent manner. In the 15-LOX-1 metabolic pathway, 15-LOX-1 oxygenates a stereospecific position on LA, generating 13­(S)-hydroperoxylinoleic acid (13­(S)-HpODE), which is further metabolized by GPx to 13­(S)-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (13­(S)-HODE) with a conjugated diene. 13­(S)-HODE has been identified as an endogenous inhibitor of mTOR, directly binding to the kinase domain and suppressing its activity. Subsequently, 13­(S)-HODE is oxidized to 13-oxo-ODE by hydroxy fatty acid dehydrogenase. 13-Oxo-ODE mediates biological effects through PPARγ-dependent signaling pathways. Alternatively, hydroperoxide isomerization of 13­(S)-HpODE leads to the formation of 11­(E)-13­(S)-OH-EpOME, which can be converted into racemic 9,10,13­(S)-TriHOME by enzyme-mediated or spontaneous hydrolysis. It is known that TriHOMEs are predominantly found in the (S) configuration at position C-13, which is consistent with lipoxygenase-mediated oxidation of linoleic acid. They have been detected in biological samples such as bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from COPD patients, highlighting their physiological relevance. In addition, 9,10,13-TriHOME has gained increasing importance due to its involvement in human diseases. Esterified TriHOMEs also contribute to the formation of the mammalian epidermal barrier, while free TriHOME modulates nociception via TRPV1 activation. Recently, octadecanoids are increasingly recognized as bioactive lipid mediators involved in various physiological and pathological processes. Targeted metabolomic analyses of lipid oxidation identified 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME as an oxidized lipid within oxylipin networks that converge in inflammation-related lipid pathways. Therefore, the synthesis of enantiomerically pure 9­(S)-HODE and 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME, as well as their enantiomers, offers the potential to be used as tool compounds for elucidating pharmacological functions.

Recent advances in synthetic chemistry have focused on functionalization strategies that enable the rapid diversification of complex molecules and facilitate applications in drug discovery and chemical biology. − These advances have provided access to structurally diverse bioactive lipid mediators. Several 9,10,13-triHOME isomers and analogues have been studied to understand the relationship between their chemical structure and biological activities. In addition, the syntheses of 13­(S)-HODE and its analogues have been previously reported by us. , However, the structural modifications of 9­(S)-HODE and 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME for pharmaceutical applications have not yet been comprehensively investigated. Several total syntheses of 9­(S)-HODE and 9­(R),10­(R),13­(R)-TriHOME have been reported to date, ,− and two representative studies are summarized in Figure A. The total synthesis of 9­(S)-HODE was reported in 2007 by Naidu et al. The key step involved the cross-coupling reaction between 1-heptyne and a vinyl iodide derived from an enantiomerically enriched alkyne, followed by partial hydrogenation under Lindlar conditions, elimination of the protecting group with 2,3-dichloro-5,6-dicyanobenzoquinone (DDQ), and final oxidation to furnish 9­(S)-HODE in 16 steps. For the synthesis of 9­(S)-HODE, our method is shorter than the previously described synthesis (12 steps compared with 16 steps). While the enantiomeric purity was determined in the earlier study using the diol intermediate (99% ee), we determined the ee value of the final product, 9­(S)-HODE (99% ee). Furthermore, a chemical enantioselective synthesis of 9­(R)-HODE has not yet been reported. In the case of TriHOME, Davis et al. reported the synthesis of 9­(R),10­(R),13­(R)-TriHOME methyl ester using a chiral pool strategy starting from commercially available (D)-tartaric acid. In this approach, the (9R, 10R)-aldehyde intermediate was condensed with a ketophosphonate to afford the corresponding 9­(R),10­(R)-enone, followed by an asymmetric reduction to furnish the major isomer 9­(R),10­(R),13­(R)-TriHOME in 12 synthetic steps. However, this approach required diastereomer separation (9:1 d.r.; determined at an intermediate stage) and relied on Mosher ester analysis, without quantitative determination of ee for the final product. Our method, while not shorter in terms of the total number of steps, offers significantly improved stereoselectivity (greater than 99.5% ee compared to the previously reported d.r. of 9:1). Importantly, our stereochemical results are confirmed by chiral HPLC (see the Supporting Information), thereby enabling access to stereochemically defined isomers. Furthermore, an enantioselective total synthesis of 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME has not been described previously. More importantly, our synthesis strategy enables the efficient production of a variety of fatty acids. The late-stage synthesis approach allows for the bulk synthesis of common intermediates, which can then be coupled with diverse fragments using modular methods, such as the Sonogashira reaction. This accelerates the expansion of chemical libraries. Furthermore, performing (Z)- or (E)-selective reduction and asymmetric catalysis in a late-stage synthesis minimizes the risk of racemization while maximizing high stereochemical purity. We describe the synthesis of 9­(S)-HODE and 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME and their enantiomers 9­(R)-HODE and 9­(R),10­(R),13­(R)-TriHOME.

Results and Discussion

The retrosynthesis of 9­(S)-HODE is shown in Scheme . 9­(S)-HODE (11a) can be traced back to intermediate 9a via hydrolysis and a (Z)-selective reduction. Intermediate 9a is derived from vinyl iodide 8a through Sonogashira cross-coupling with hept-1-yne, representing a key C–C bond disconnection. Vinyl iodide 8a was selected as a key intermediate because it contains the stereocenter and allows convergent bond formation. Intermediate 8a was successfully synthesized on a gram scale from the commercially available starting material, azelaic acid. This late-stage functionalization approach can be significantly expanded, enabling the synthesis of both natural and non-natural fatty acids through the precise linkage of alkynes of varying chain lengths with diverse vinyl iodides. The resulting internal alkyne structures can be converted into unsaturated fatty acids with cis- or trans-alkenes by controlled partial hydrogenation at specific sites.

Retrosynthesis of the Core Structure for (9S)-HODE (11a)

The synthesis of 9­(S)-HODE is shown in Scheme . The synthesis of compound 1 started with the cyclization of azelaic acid with acetic anhydride in quantitative yield, followed by the conversion of the resulting anhydride to Weinreb amide 2 using N,O-dimethylhydroxylamine in 48% yield. Subsequent addition of TIPS-acetylene to Weinreb amide 2 afforded the corresponding alkynyl ketone 3 in 59% yield. Esterification of the resulting carboxylic acid then furnished ketone 4 in 91% yield. Efficient conversion was achieved when the carboxylic acid functionality was retained in compound 2, while the corresponding ester substrate led to a lower conversion under the same conditions (Supporting Information Table S1). Asymmetric reduction of ketone 4 using Noyori’s catalyst − gave the desired enantiomer of alcohol (S)-5a in 96% yield (98% ee; determined by chiral HPLC of the corresponding derivative 28a; see the Supporting Information). TBAF-mediated desilylation of alkyne 5a, followed by silyl protection of the hydroxyl group of alkyne 6a, afforded the corresponding TES ether 7a in 86% yield over 2 steps. The conversion of terminal acetylene 7a into the corresponding (E)-vinyl iodide was achieved in two steps consisting of regioselective palladium-catalyzed hydrostannylation and subsequent iododestannylation to afford (E)-vinyl iodide 8a in 60% yield in the presence of Pd­(PPh3)4. The Sonogashira coupling of iodide 8a with hept-1-yne in the presence of CuI, Pd­(PPh3)4, and Et3N and the subsequent silyl deprotection afforded enyne 9a in 69% yield over two steps. The (Z)-selective reduction of diene-yne intermediate 9a afforded compound 10a using a Zn­(Cu/Ag) catalyst. The Zn­(Cu/Ag)-mediated reduction proceeds with high chemoselectivity, enabling late-stage formation of the (Z)-alkene without over-reduction. Finally, 9­(S)-HODE (11a) was synthesized by ester hydrolysis of 10a with aqueous LiOH in 81% yield (99% ee, determined by chiral HPLC). Oxidation of 11a using Dess–Martin periodinane afforded the corresponding carbonyl compound 12 in 89% yield. The enantiomeric counterpart 9­(R)-HODE (11b) was accessed from intermediate 4 via asymmetric transfer hydrogenation using RuCl­[(R,R)-TsDPEN]­(mesitylene), affording 11b in 8 steps (21.7% overall yield, 99% ee). The yields of compounds 2 and 8a are moderate, which could be due to purification losses. Further optimization of the purification protocol could improve efficiency.

Synthesis of 9­(S)-HODE (11a), 9­(R)-HODE (11b), and 9-Oxo-ODE (12)

The retrosynthetic analysis of 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME is shown in Scheme . The structure of 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME (26a) is accessible from intermediate 23a via an (E)-selective reduction of the alkyne. Intermediate 23a can be generated by Sharpless asymmetric dihydroxylation of enyne (S)-22a, furnishing the vicinal diol with high enantioselectivity. This late-stage reaction enables highly selective formation of the C9 and C10 stereocenters through reagent control, independent of the existing chirality at C13. Enyne intermediate (S)-22a can be obtained from chiral intermediate 16a and key intermediate 20 via Sonogashira coupling, representing a key C–C bond formation enabling convergent assembly of various carbon skeletons. Fragment 16a and vinyl iodide 20 can be prepared on a gram scale from commercially available starting materials.

Retrosynthesis of the Core Structure for 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME (26a)

The synthesis of key intermediate 20 is shown in Scheme . (E)-Vinyl iodide 20 was prepared in a four-step sequence starting from commercial 8-bromooctanoic acid. Alkynylation of 8-bromooctanoic acid afforded the corresponding acetylene 17 in 93% yield. Esterification of the acid and subsequent TIPS deprotection gave alkyne 18 in 95% yield over two steps. DIBAL-H reduction of the resulting alkyne 18 furnished the corresponding aldehyde. Due to the incompatibility of the carbonyl group with the subsequent reducing conditions (e.g., DIBAL-H, LiHBEt3, and Red-Al), the aldehyde was protected as the corresponding cyclic acetal 19, which was obtained in 63% yield over two steps. Acetal 19 was then subjected to hydrozirconation with in situ-generated Schwartz reagent, followed by iodination to provide the key intermediate iodide 20 in 59% yield. The combination of Cp2ZrCl and LiHBEt3 proved optimal, affording higher yields with significantly reduced reaction time compared to the other conditions (Supporting Information Table S2).

Synthesis of Key Intermediate 20

The synthesis of 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME (26a) is shown in Scheme . Following a previously published procedure, the enantioselective synthesis of intermediate 16a was achieved in 63% yield over four steps. Activation of known hexanoic acid afforded the corresponding Weinreb amide 13 in 86% yield, and the subsequent reaction with TIPS-acetylene furnished ketone 14 in 91% yield. Asymmetric reduction of ketone 14 using Noyori’s catalyst afforded the desired enantiomer of 15a in 90% yield. Subsequent desilylation of alcohol 15a furnished chiral alkyne 16a in 90% yield. The Sonogashira coupling of alkyne 16a and iodide 20 with CuI, Pd­(PPh3)4, and Et3N afforded enyne 21a in 43% yield. Benzoylation of enyne 21a afforded protected intermediate (S)-22a in 89% yield, and subsequent Sharpless asymmetric dihydroxylation using (DHQ)2PHAL and K2OsO4·2H2O furnished vicinal diol 23a in 80% yield with high enantiomeric excess (ee ≥ 99.5%). Deprotection of compound 23a with LiOH in THF/H2O removed the benzoyl group, and subsequent reduction of the crude residue with Red-Al (15 equiv) afforded the desired triol 24a in 60% yield over two steps. Red-Al was chosen for this conversion due to its compatibility with acetal protecting groups and based on literature examples. Under these conditions, the reaction proceeded efficiently and quickly (see Supporting Information Table S3). The total synthesis of TriHOME was completed in four further steps. Silylation of triol 24a proceeded in 87% yield, followed by selective removal of cyclic acetal 25a in the presence of TMSOTf and 2,6-lutidine while retaining the TES protecting groups to afford the crude aldehyde. Oxidation of the aldehyde using NaClO2 and NaH2PO4, followed by deprotection of the TES groups with TBAF, afforded 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME (26a) in 51% yield over two steps. Although the yield in this step was not high, further optimization of the isolation process could lead to higher yields. The (R)-enantiomer was prepared from precursor (R)-22b via Sharpless asymmetric dihydroxylation using (DHQD)2PHAL, providing 9­(R),10­(R),13­(R)-TriHOME (26b) in 6 steps (19.3% overall yield, ee ≥ 99.5%; determined by chiral HPLC of the corresponding ester derivative 29b; see the Supporting Information). The synthetic and analytical data of 9­(S)- and 9­(R)-HODE, as well as 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)- and 9­(R),10­(R),13­(R)-TriHOME, were consistent with previously reported data. ,,, 9­(S)- and 9­(R)-HODE were obtained in 5.6% and 5.5% overall yields, respectively, over 12 steps (99% ee in each case). 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)- and 9­(R),10­(R),13­(R)-TriHOME were prepared in 2.7% and 2.9% overall yields, respectively, over 15 steps (ee ≥ 99.5% in each case).

Conclusion

In summary, the stereoselective syntheses of 9­(S)-HODE (11a) and 9­(S),10­(S),13­(S)-TriHOME (26a), and their enantiomers 9­(R)-HODE (11b) and 9­(R),10­(R),13­(R)-TriHOME (26b), were successfully achieved in 12 and 15 synthetic steps, respectively, with high enantioselectivity (ee > 99%). The reagent-controlled approach in the late stage of synthesis is suitable for the synthesis of fatty acid analogues. By integrating various alkyne fragments with diverse vinyl iodides via Sonogashira coupling, followed by (Z)- or (E)-selective hydrogenation and chiral induction in the late stage of synthesis, this synthetic methodology enables the efficient generation of fatty acid isomers from a common intermediate and is useful for structure–activity relationship studies. This modular approach enables structural diversification and supports future medicinal chemistry studies of octadecanoids, which have the potential to modulate intracellular target proteins such as fatty acid-binding proteins and mTOR kinases.

Experimental Section

All of the chemicals and solvents used in the reaction were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, TCI, or Thermo Fisher Scientific and were used without further purification. All reactions were carried out in oven-dried glassware under an inert atmosphere, and the reaction temperature was controlled by an oil bath. Reactions were monitored by TLC on 0.25 mm Merck precoated silica gel plates (60 F254). Reaction progress was monitored by TLC analysis using a UV lamp and/or KMnO4 staining or p-anisaldehyde staining solution for detection purposes. Column chromatography was performed on silica gel (230–400 mesh, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded at room temperature (298 K) in CDCl3 (7.26 ppm/77.16 ppm), CD3OD (3.31 ppm/49.00 ppm), or CD3CN/D2O (2.53 and 4.79 ppm/1.32 and 118.26 ppm) on a Bruker Ultrashield 600 MHz Plus spectrometer and referenced to an internal solvent. NMR solvents, including CDCl3, CD3OD, CD3CN, and D2O, were used as received from the Eurisotop company. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm). Coupling constants (J) are given in Hertz. Splitting patterns are indicated as s, singlet; d, doublet; t, triplet; q, quartet; m, multiplet; dd, double of doublet; dt, double of triplet; br, broad for 1H NMR data. High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were recorded on an Agilent 6530 Accurate mass Q-TOF LC/MS spectrometer. The purity of all final compounds was measured by an analytical C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, Nacalai Cosmosil Cholester packed column) using water/ACN containing 0.1% formic acid as the mobile phase. The enantiomeric ratio was determined by chiral HPLC (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Daicel Chiralpak IG) using hexane/MTBE containing 0.05% glacial acetic acid as the mobile phase. All compounds were detected at 220 nm using a UV detector. The purities of the tested compounds were >95%. Optical rotation was measured at λ = 589 nm, corresponding to the sodium D line, at the indicated temperature using a Krüss P8000 polarimeter.

Ethyl (S)-9-Hydroxyundec-10-ynoate (6a)
