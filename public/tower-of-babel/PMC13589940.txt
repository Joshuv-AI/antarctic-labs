Emerging organoids and organoids-on-chip platforms for translational development of antibody‒drug conjugates and next-generation bioconjugates
Authors: Zhuoliang Hu, Yuheng Liao, Mengxian Niu, Zhanghong Kong, Hugo Vankelecom, Jianjun Cheng, Michela Deleidi, Chenzhong Li, Zhengjin Jiang, Sumin Bian, Qiqin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid evolution of antibody‒drug conjugates (ADCs) and emerging X–drug conjugates (XDCs) has revolutionized targeted cancer therapy, yet their clinical translation is severely bottlenecked by the lack of human-relevant preclinical models. To bridge this translational gap, patient-derived organoids and organoids-on-chip (OoC) platforms recapitulate key features of human tumor biology, offering powerful tools for investigating cancer heterogeneity and improving the predictive evaluation of bioconjugated therapeutics. In this review, we systematically summarize the current landscape and challenges of ADCs and XDCs development across existing preclinical models, critically discuss the concepts, biological principles and application potential of organoid- and OoC-based systems, and highlight recent global policy developments and collaborative initiatives shaping their translational implementation. Finally, we highlight key challenges and future perspectives for leveraging organoids and OoCs in the druggability assessment, optimization, and translational development of ADCs and XDCs.Key words: Organoids, Organoids-on-chip, Antibody‒drug conjugates, X-drug conjugates, Targeted therapies, Druggability assessment and drug screening

Introduction

Antibody‒drug conjugates (ADCs) consist of a monoclonal antibody (mAb) that selectively binds to antigens on the surface of tumor cells, a chemically stable linker designed to release the drug payload at the tumor site, and a highly potent cytotoxic agent that induces targeted cell death1,2. In 2000, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted accelerated approval for Mylotarg, Pfizer's first-in-class ADC for acute myeloid leukemia patients. However, due to safety concerns, the drug was voluntarily withdrawn from the market in 20103. Despite this setback, advances in site-specific conjugation technologies have enabled the successful development of next-generation ADCs, such as Enhertu and Trodelvy, which have demonstrated efficacy in solid tumors and represent a major milestone in the field4. Furthermore, next-generation bioconjugates have expanded beyond conventional monoclonal antibodies and cytotoxins. These platforms now incorporate alternative targeting moieties (e.g., peptides, aptamers) and a broader range of payloads, including oligonucleotides, radionuclides, and enzymes, thereby driving the transition from traditional ADCs to the more versatile class of X‒drug conjugates (XDCs). “X” denotes the highly customizable targeting or effector modules that extend beyond conventional antibody-based platforms. Representative milestones of this paradigm shift include antibody‒oligonucleotide conjugates (AOCs), which leverage antibodies for the targeted delivery of gene therapeutics5, and radionuclide drug conjugates (RDCs), which enable precise targeted radiotherapy6. Moreover, Liu et al.7 developed prodrug antibody‒bottlebrush conjugates (ABCs), consisting of mAb covalently conjugated to the terminus of a compact bivalent bottlebrush prodrug. These ABCs achieved tunable average drug-to-antibody ratios (DAR) up to two orders of magnitude greater than those of traditional ADCs, representing a significant advancement over conventional conjugates designs. As of 2025, there are 21 ADCs have been approved globally, with over 400 candidates in development, including more than 200 currently in clinical trials8. Despite their considerable promise, the development of ADCs and XDCs remains challenged by tumor heterogeneity, limited predictive power of preclinical models, on-target off-tumor and off-target toxicities, and complex resistance mechanisms9, all of which contribute to their high attrition rates.

To model cancer development and therapeutic responses, researchers have traditionally relied on animal models, particularly rodents. However, species-specific differences represent a major limitation, especially in predicting toxicity. Numerous studies have highlighted the limited translational predictivity of animal models, often evidenced by adverse events observed in clinical trials but not in preclinical studies10,11. These discrepancies largely arise from interspecies differences in absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME) parameters, genetic background, target expression, and overall physiology. For instance, higher telomerase activity in mice confers resistance to cellular senescence, allowing malignant transformation with fewer genetic alterations than required in human tumors, underscoring fundamental differences in cancer biology between species12,13. To overcome some of these limitations, human tumor-derived cancer cell lines grown in two-dimensional (2D) cultures have been widely adopted. While instrumental in elucidating fundamental cancer mechanisms, 2D systems fail to recapitulate the complexity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and intratumoral heterogeneity12. Moreover, they impose non-physiological selective pressures, including altered mechanotransduction, uniform nutrient and oxygen exposure, and lack of extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions, ultimately limiting their predictive relevance.

In response to these challenges, three-dimensional (3D) organoid models have emerged as a powerful alternative. Organoids derived from human tumors recapitulate key histopathological, genetic, and phenotypic features of the parental tissue14, thereby addressing several limitations of traditional models. These systems enable the study of tumor architecture, cellular interactions, and therapeutic responses in a more physiologically relevant context15, 16, 17. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs), in particular, provide a scalable platform for personalized drug testing, encompassing chemotherapies18, 19, 20, immunotherapies21 and emerging modalities such as ADCs and XDCs22,23. Notably, Sachs et al.24 established a living biobank of more than 100 breast cancer (BC) organoid lines derived from both primary and metastatic tumors, preserving genetic and clinical diversity. Importantly, pharmacological responses observed in these organoids retrospectively correlated with clinical outcomes in several patients. Similarly, Fujii et al.25 generated a living biobank of 55 colorectal cancer (CRC) organoid lines derived from a broad spectrum of tumor phenotypes including both primary and metastatic lesions. By enabling precise genetic characterization and controlled experimental manipulation, organoid technology effectively bridges the gap between conventional preclinical models and clinical studies, providing a robust platform for evaluating the druggability of novel bioconjugates.

In this review, we comprehensively examine how organoid-based systems can support the entire research and development pipeline of ADCs and XDCs as advanced platforms for druggability assessment (Fig. 1). We outline the current landscape and key challenges in ADC/XDC development and compare existing preclinical models. We then discuss the generation of organoids and multi-scale engineered systems, alongside emerging global policy trends supporting their adoption. We further highlight the application of organoids in evaluating therapeutic efficacy, safety, and resistance mechanisms of next-generation bioconjugates. Finally, we address current limitations and future perspectives, with the aim of illustrating how organoid technologies are reshaping the paradigm of bioconjugate drug development and accelerating the translation of safer and more effective therapies.

Antibody‒drug conjugates and next-generation bioconjugated drugs

Current development status and challenges of antibody‒drug conjugates

To date, multiple classes of ADCs have been developed and can be broadly categorized into cleavable and non-cleavable types based on linker design26. Cleavable ADCs are engineered to release their cytotoxic payload within the TME27, enabling a phenomenon known as the bystander effect, whereby both antigen-positive (Ag+) and antigen-negative (Ag−) tumor cells can be eliminated28. In contrast, ADCs with non-cleavable linkers typically lack this effect29, limiting their applicability primarily to hematological malignancies or tumors with high antigen expression.

Despite significant advances, ADC development continues to face major challenges, including limited efficacy, off-target toxicity, and the emergence of drug resistance30,31. Insufficient efficacy remains a leading cause of clinical failure and may result from low payload potency, inadequate antigen density, or inefficient internalization. Notably, high antigen expression does not necessarily guarantee therapeutic success, as limited tumor penetration of antibodies can restrict drug delivery32. Off-target toxicity is often associated with linker instability1. For example, maleimide-based linkers may undergo premature deconjugation in circulation, leading to off-target cytotoxicity33. Even with improved linker stability, on-target off-tumor toxicity can arise from antigen expression in normal tissues34. In addition, drug resistance represents a major obstacle. Mechanisms include alterations in the TME35, downregulation of target antigens following prolonged therapy36,37, and defects in intracellular trafficking pathways that impair ADC internalization and processing38. Together, these challenges contribute to high attrition rates and increased development costs.

A critical underlying issue is the limited predictive power of current preclinical models39,40. Inaccurate preclinical data can lead to late-stage clinical failures, resulting in substantial financial losses and potential risks to patients41. Therefore, there is an urgent need for more physiologically relevant models that accurately recapitulate the tumor environment and improve the evaluation of ADCs and next-generation bioconjugates.

Preclinical models for the development of antibody drug conjugates

A range of preclinical models is currently employed in ADC development, including 2D cell cultures, animal models, patient-derived xenografts (PDXs), spheroids, and organoids42, 43, 44. Each model offers distinct advantages and limitations, and they are often used in combination to enhance the robustness of preclinical evaluation (Fig. 2). 2D cell cultures are widely used due to their simplicity, low cost, and suitability for high-throughput screening45. They are particularly valuable for target validation, mechanistic studies, and preliminary safety assessments46,47. However, these systems fail to recapitulate the architectural complexity and cellular interactions of tumors48. They also impose non-physiological conditions, leading to altered cell behavior, loss of phenotypic diversity, and genomic drift over prolonged culture49,50.

Common preclinical models for ADCs development: A critical comparison of their advantages and limitations.

Animal models provide a more integrated biological context, preserving vascularization, immune components, and tissue architecture. Nevertheless, their clinical relevance is limited by interspecies differences in physiology, genetics, and tumor biology51. As a result, therapeutic responses observed in animal studies often fail to reliably predict clinical outcomes in humans. Additionally, animal models are time-consuming, costly, and unsuitable for high-throughput applications52. Notably, several ADCs that demonstrated favorable safety profiles in preclinical animal studies later exhibited severe toxicities in clinical trials, highlighting their limited predictive value53,54. One example is DHES0815, a novel ADC targeting HER2, which was well tolerated in safety studies in macaque monkeys55. However, in a phase I clinical trial involving patients with HER2-positive metastatic BC, higher doses led to persistent and irreversible dermal, ocular, and pulmonary toxicities, ultimately resulting in premature termination of the trial55.

PDX models, generated by engrafting patient tumor tissues into immunodeficient mice, better preserve tumor heterogeneity and histological features56. However, they do not fully recapitulate all stages of tumor progression57 and lack a functional human immune system. PDX models are also costly and time-consuming due to low engraftment rates and prolonged expansion periods58, which constrain their use in drug development. In addition, both inter- and intra-tumor heterogeneity, which are present in PDX models as well as in the original tumor samples, poses a further limitation for their application59. AXL-107-MMAE, an AXL-targeting ADC, demonstrated strong tumor growth inhibition in melanoma PDX models60. However, its development was ultimately discontinued due to excessive toxicity observed in Phase I/II clinical studies.

3D cultures recapitulate the spatial organization of cells found in vivo43 and can be broadly classified into spheroids and organoids based on the culture techniques used45. Spheroids are typically derived from stem or tumor cells and grown as free-floating aggregates61. Compared with 2D cultures, spheroids retain aspects of tumor heterogeneity, cell‒cell interactions, and polarity62,63, and they can be readily scaled for high-throughput drug screening62. However, a major limitation of spheroids is their limited histological resemblance to the original tumor58, which reduces their predictive accuracy for drug efficacy.

Given the inherent limitations of preclinical models, including the lack of biological fidelity in 2D cell cultures and the presence of interspecies differences in animal models, data reported from these models often diverge from the actual realities observed in clinical practice. This highlights the need for new preclinical models that can better predict the efficacy of antibodies and their drug conjugates.

PDOs have become powerful preclinical models and have been utilized for assessing therapeutic efficacy46,47, evaluating in vitro safety64 and investigating mechanisms of drug resistance to bioconjugated therapies65. Compare with 2D cultures, PDOs can better simulate the human TME65. Importantly, they preserve key features of the original tumor, including genetic mutations, heterogeneity, molecular signatures, and genomic alterations66,67. Moreover, the architecture and organization of organoids in a 3D environment differ from those in 2D culture, which can affect cell behavior and drug responses43. Although PDX models can partially mimic the TME, their lack of functional immune components, along with their time- and resource-intensive nature, introduces experimental biases that limit their utility in drug efficacy studies68,69. In contrast, organoids are amenable to high-throughput applications, can be expanded at scale, and support long-term co-culture with immune cells70, 71, 72. These features make them particularly suitable for predicting the antitumor activity of bioconjugated drugs.

Overall, organoids represent a highly promising class of preclinical models for evaluating therapeutic efficacy, safety, and mechanisms of drug resistance, thereby advancing the development and clinical translation of antibodies and their drug conjugates.

Organoids and multi-scale engineered models

Concepts and biological principles of organoids

Organoids: Developmental and structural foundations

Organoids are 3D multicellular structures derived from pluripotent or adult stem cells that self-organize into tissue-specific architectures, mimicking key structural, cellular, and functional aspects of their in vivo counterparts73,74. Since the establishment of the first long-term intestinal organoids in 200975, the field has rapidly advanced toward increased physiological relevance, broader tissue diversity (including liver, gut, tumor, and brain organoids), and enhanced translational utility. Current trends in organoids research emphasize standardization76, scalability, multi-organoid integration, vascularization, and incorporation of immune components. Additionally, coupling organoid systems with microfluidic technologies and artificial intelligence (AI)-based analytical tools is improving reproducibility and predictive power for disease modeling and drug discovery (Fig. 3A). In this context, assembloids have emerged as next-generation multicellular systems generated by fusing or co-culturing distinct organoids or cell populations to model higher-order tissue interactions and inter-regional communication77,78. These platforms enable the study of complex biological systems, such as cortico-subpallial circuits, gut–brain interactions, and tumor–immune microenvironments, which cannot be adequately captured by individual organoids (Fig. 3B)79.

The intrinsic properties of organoids make them highly valuable for investigating interactions between therapeutic antibodies, ADCs, and their targets80. By bridging the gap between conventional 2D cultures and animal models, organoids provide physiologically relevant platforms for both basic and translational research. For example, Arshad et al.81 demonstrated that organoids derived from HER2-low tumors responded to neratinib, either as monotherapy or in combination with trastuzumab, highlighting their utility in refining patient stratification strategies. Similarly, Wu and colleagues established a comprehensive BC PDO biobank that enabled systematic drug sensitivity profiling across diverse tumor subtypes, revealing substantial heterogeneity in therapeutic responses and supporting precision oncology approaches.

Specialized organoid variants: Vascularized and immune organoids

Vascularized organoids incorporate endothelial cells and stromal components to form perfusable vascular networks, enabling efficient delivery of oxygen and nutrients and removal of metabolic waste82,83. This vascular integration promotes tissue maturation and functional stability. Vascularization can be achieved through co-differentiation, bioengineering strategies, or transplantation into host organisms, with the goal of recapitulating physiological circulation and paracrine signaling. These systems are particularly critical for modeling metabolically active tissues such as liver, kidney, and brain, thereby enhancing their relevance for pharmacokinetics and toxicity studies84. For instance, Abilez and colleagues have recently demonstrated that micropatterned human pluripotent stem cell-derived gastruloids can model early vascularization processes, revealing conserved developmental programs governing vascular network formation (Fig. 3C)85.

Immune organoids represent advanced 3D systems that recapitulate key features of the human immune microenvironment. These models enable the study of immune cell development, activation, and interactions with surrounding tissues (Fig. 3D)86. Immune organoids can be generated from stem cells, primary immune cells, or by integrating immune components into existing organoid systems, thereby mimicking lymphoid structures such as thymus, spleen, or lymph nodes87,88. By preserving spatial organization and cytokine signaling, these platforms provide physiologically relevant models for investigating host–pathogen interactions, tumor-immune dynamics, autoimmune diseases, and vaccine responses. Furthermore, they serve as valuable tools for preclinical immunotherapy testing and for dissecting mechanisms of immune tolerance and inflammation89,90. A recent review comprehensively examined the generation of immune organoids from patient-derived lymphoid tissues for translational immunology, highlighting their structural, cellular, and functional features, and outlining bioengineering strategies, such as 3D bioprinting, micropatterning, and microfluidic systems, to advance the next generation of immuno-engineered organoids91.

Engineering principles of microphysiological systems and organoids-on-a-chip

Overview of microphysiological systems and organoids-on-chips

Microphysiological systems (MPS), often referred to as OoCs, are microengineered in vitro platforms designed to emulate the structure, function, and dynamic microenvironment of human tissues and organs. These systems integrate living human cells within microfluidic devices that recreate key physical and biochemical parameters, such as tissue architecture, fluid flow, mechanical forces, and cell‒cell interactions, to mimic physiological and pathological conditions at the organ or multi-organ level92,93. By enabling continuous perfusion and real-time monitoring, MPS provide a powerful alternative to static cultures and animal models, offering improved predictive accuracy for human responses to drugs, chemicals, and environmental factors. Furthermore, the modular nature of these platforms allows multiple tissue units to be interconnected, forming multi-organ MPS capable of reproducing systemic processes such as metabolism, immune responses, and drug pharmacokinetics, thus, advancing the field toward integrated human-on-chip models.

Building upon the foundational principles of MPS, OoC extend this technology by incorporating the inherent self-organization and multicellular complexity of organoids94. Whereas conventional MPS primarily rely on engineered microenvironments to emulate tissue function, OoCs incorporate biologically driven programs that more faithfully capture human physiology and patient-specific features. As such, OoCs represent an emerging hybrid platform that integrates organoid biology with the precise spatiotemporal control afforded by microfluidic MPS systems. This approach enables continuous perfusion, mechanical stimulation, and spatiotemporal modulation of biochemical cues within microscale compartments, thereby recreating critical aspects of the physiological niche95,96. By coupling cellular complexity with engineered microarchitecture, OoCs circumvent major limitations of static organoid culture, such as nutrient diffusion barriers, heterogeneity in tissue maturation, and restricted dynamic control, while supporting real-time monitoring, high-throughput drug screening, and modeling of dynamic processes including tissue‒tissue communication, immune interactions, and drug metabolism. Ultimately, OoCs provide a powerful convergence of developmental biology, stem cell science, and microengineering for advancing personalized medicine, disease modeling, and predictive toxicology.

Overview of vascularized and immune organoids-on-chip

Vascularized OoCs are advanced microengineered platforms that integrate organoid technology with functional vascular networks and microfluidic perfusion systems to recapitulate in vivo tissue physiology. By combining endothelialized microchannels with organoid cultures, these systems establish dynamic perfusion that delivers oxygen and nutrients, removes waste, and reproduces shear stress and biochemical gradients similar to those experienced in living tissues97,98. The incorporation of vascular elements not only enhances tissue viability and maturation but also supports modeling of angiogenesis, inflammation, and drug distribution under physiologically relevant flow conditions. This approach bridges the gap between static organoid models and complex in vivo systems, enabling precise investigation of vascular‒tissue crosstalk, immune cell trafficking, and pharmacokinetics in a controlled microenvironment. As such, vascularized OoCs hold significant promises for advancing regenerative medicine, disease modeling, and preclinical drug testing.

Recent advances in vascularized OoCs have markedly improved the ability to recapitulate the complex TME and evaluate therapeutic efficacy under physiologically relevant conditions. By integrating tumor organoids with perfusable endothelial networks and microfluidic circulation, these systems reproduce critical in vivo features such as angiogenesis, hypoxia, nutrient gradients, and immune infiltration98. The dynamic perfusion provided by these models enables controlled drug delivery and real-time visualization of drug distribution, penetration, and metabolism within tumor tissues, allowing precise assessment of therapeutic response and resistance mechanisms. In 2025, several landmark studies further expanded the field: a Theranostics report developed a vascularized renal carcinoma-on-chip platform that visualized tumor-induced neovascular sprouting and anti-vascular drug responses; meanwhile, a high-throughput 24-well microfluidic system (OrganiX™) enabled perfusable vascularized liver-tumor models that revealed heterogeneous responses to targeted therapies and CAR-T cell infiltration99. Such advancements highlight the growing integration of biosensors, immune cells, and vascular elements for multiparametric monitoring of treatment outcomes100,101. Collectively, vascularized OoCs are emerging as transformative tools for precision oncology, enabling patient-specific testing of anti-angiogenic, immunotherapeutic, and combinational regimens while bridging the gap between preclinical studies and clinical translation.

Immune OoCs are an emerging class of MPS that integrate immune organoids with microfluidic chip technology to recreate dynamic and spatially organized immune microenvironments in vitro. By incorporating immune cell populations, such as lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells, within perfused organoid cultures, these systems enable real-time investigation of immune responses, cell migration, cytokine signaling, and immune-tissue crosstalk under physiologically relevant flow conditions102,103. The microfluidic architecture supports controlled exposure to antigens, drugs, or pathogens, facilitating precise modeling of inflammation, infection, and immunotherapy mechanisms. Moreover, immune OoCs provide a translationally relevant platform for studying human-specific immune phenomena that are difficult to replicate in animal models, such as adaptive immune activation, immune checkpoint regulation, and vaccine-induced memory formation. By combining the structural complexity of immune organoids with the tunable microenvironment of OoC systems, these platforms hold great promises for advancing personalized immunology, drug screening, and the development of next-generation immunotherapies. The translational potential of this strategy is underscored by a landmark milestone reported by Fierce Biotech in October 2025104, in which the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved the world's first IND supported exclusively by human vascularized organoid efficacy data, enabling a BAL0891–immune checkpoint inhibitor combination trial based on preclinical results generated with Qureator's OoC vTIME™ platform and signaling a paradigm shift away from mandatory animal POC testing.

Overview of multi- and biosensor-integrated organoids-on-chip

Multi-OoCs, often referred to as body-on-chip systems, are interconnected microphysiological platforms that integrate multiple organ-specific models within a single microfluidic circuit to recapitulate systemic human physiology and inter-organ communication in vitro. These systems combine tissue modules, such as liver, kidney, heart, gut, lung, or brain organoids, connected through vascular-like channels that allow controlled fluidic exchange, thereby mimicking blood circulation and metabolic coupling between organs105,106. By reproducing physiological interactions such as drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, multi-OoCs enable more accurate prediction of whole-body pharmacokinetics and toxicological outcomes than traditional cell culture or animal models107. Furthermore, their modularity allows the study of organ-organ crosstalk in complex diseases, including inflammation, cancer metastasis, and neuro-immune disorders. These systems represent a crucial step toward developing “human-on-chip” or “body chip” technologies, bridging in vitro experimentation with clinical translation and paving the way for personalized drug testing and precision medicine108,109.

Biosensor-integrated OoCs represent a cutting-edge evolution of MPS in which organoid cultures are combined with embedded biosensors to enable continuous, real-time monitoring of biochemical, biophysical, and electrophysiological parameters110. These integrated platforms employ micro- and nanoscale sensors, such as electrochemical, optical, or impedance-based devices, to detect dynamic changes in metabolites, oxygen levels, pH, cytokine secretion, or drug concentrations within the organoids microenvironment111,112. The seamless integration of sensing technology within OoC architecture provides a non-invasive means of assessing tissue functionality, viability, and response to external stimuli over extended periods. Moreover, biosensor integration enhances the analytical power of organoid models by allowing multiplexed and high-throughput data acquisition, supporting applications in drug screening, toxicology, and personalized medicine113,114. By bridging biological complexity with precision measurement, sensor-integrated OoCs offer a transformative approach for dynamic phenotyping, quantitative systems pharmacology, and the development of adaptive, self-regulating OoC platforms111.

Global policies and consortia supporting organoids and organoids-on-chips as new approach methodologies (NAMs) to phase out animal testing

Global policies supporting organoids and organoids-on-chip as NAMs

From 2022 to 2026, an unprecedented global policy shift has accelerated the integration of organoids and MPS into regulatory frameworks as NAMs for replacing animal testing (Fig. 4). In the United States, several key initiatives formally recognize human-based NAMs—including OoCs—as valid alternatives for Investigational New Drug (IND) and Biologics License applications115, 116, 117, 118, 119, 120. These include the FDA Modernization Act 2.0 (Sep. 2022), the FDA Roadmap to Reducing Animal Testing in Preclinical Safety Studies (Apr. 2025), the joint FDA and National Institutes of Health (NIH) announcement ending funding for animal-only studies (Jul. 2025), the first establishment of NIH Standardized Organoid Modeling (SOM) Center (Sep. 2025), the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)'s call to end all monkey research (Nov. 2025), the FDA Modernization Act 3.0 (Dec. 2025) and the FDA releases draft guidance on alternatives to animal testing in drug development (Mar. 2026). In May 2026, the FDA releases draft guidance on oncology pharmaceuticals: streamlined nonclinical safety studies for biologics and conjugated products121. The U.S. environmental protection agency takes new action to eliminate animal testing (Jun 2026)122. These initiatives, supported by the NIH, NICEATM and CDC, outline contexts of use and performance criteria for MPS in toxicology and biotherapeutics. The United Kingdom followed suit with its cross-government strategy Replacing Animals in Science (Nov. 2025), establishing funding mechanisms, validation pipelines, and regulatory confidence measures for NAMs123. In parallel, the European CEN-CENELEC OoCs Standardization Roadmap (Jul. 2024), the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Guidance Document No. 417 (Nov 2025), the Dutch Knowledge and Innovation Agenda Key Enabling Technologies published Organoids-on-Chip development (Jan 2026), and the European Commission released roadmap towards phasing out animal testing for chemical safety assessments (Jun 2026) have collectively laid the foundation for international harmonization bydefining data-quality, reporting, and interoperability standards essential for regulatory acceptance124, 125, 126, 127.

Overview of the global regulatory landscape and strategic initiatives supporting organoids and OoCs as NAMs.

Global consortia supporting organoids and organoids-on-chips as NAMs

The rapid growth of organoids and OoC technologies has stimulated the formation of international consortia that collectively aim to enhance standardization, reproducibility, data harmonization, and translational readiness128. These alliances now constitute an emerging global infrastructure that supports the maturation of organoid systems as robust research tools and NAMs.

Multinational consortia. Global initiatives such as the Human Cell Atlas (HCA) integrate organoid models with multi-omics profiling and high-content imaging to establish harmonized reference maps and data standards. Parallel efforts from the International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR) provide ethical and governance frameworks for organoids research, particularly relevant to brain and patient-derived models, and facilitate international consensus around responsible innovation.

European networks. Europe has established the most comprehensive organoid standardization landscape. Programs including the European Organoids-on-Chip Society (EUROoCS), the LifeTime Initiative, and the EU-NETVAL platform focus on protocol harmonization, inter-laboratory benchmarking, organoids biobanking, and regulatory validation. These networks have developed best-practice guidelines, cross-site ring trials, and qualification pipelines that link academic laboratories, biotechnology partners, and policy stakeholders.

North American consortia. In the United States, the NIH NCATS Tissue Chip Program and the broader MPS consortium have created a national preclinical testing ecosystem that unites organoids, OoCs, and MPS. Together with the FDA's Organoid Regulatory Science Consortium, CERSIs (Centers of Excellence in Regulatory Science & Innovation) and ongoing GMP-focused initiatives from CIRM, these programs advance regulatory science, dataset transparency, and IND-enabling acceptance criteria for human-relevant in vitro platforms.

Asia-Pacific initiatives. Major national programs in China, Japan, and South Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) have accelerated high-throughput organoid biobanking, clinical integration, and large-cohort drug screening. China's national organoid alliance and Japan's J-ORGNET have established some of the world's largest patient-derived organoid repositories, often coupled with automated imaging, deep phenotyping, and AI-based analysis.

Clinical translational networks. Consortia such as the Tumor Organoid Biobank and global PRECODE (Pancreatic Cancer Organoids Research Network) integrate standardized workflows across tissue procurement, organoid culture, drug screening, and clinical reporting. These platforms are now shaping precision oncology by linking PDOs with therapeutic response prediction and real-world evidence.

Industrial global network. Pluristyx launched the Organoid COMMONS (Consortium for Organoid Manufacturing, Measurement, Optimization, and Network for Standards), a public-private consortium that unites industry leaders to accelerate the adoption of human-relevant organoid models for revolutionizing drug discovery. Most recently, the Industry Alliance for Microphysiological Systems (IAMPS) was launched in February 2026 as the world's first industry association dedicated to representing MPS providers across Europe. It serves as a unified European industry voice to support the development, adoption, and regulatory acceptance of these innovative NAMs129.

Ethical, societal, and governance considerations

The rapid emergence of organoids and OoC as NAMs presents both unprecedented opportunities and ethical challenges. While these human-relevant systems hold great promise to replace animal models in preclinical research, their responsible development and implementation require careful consideration of human tissue sourcing, data governance, capability boundaries, and global standardization frameworks117,130,131.

A primary ethical dimension concerns tissue sourcing and informed consent. NAMs rely on primary human cells, stem cells, and patient-derived samples, demanding renewed consent frameworks96,132. Donors should receive transparent information about how biological materials and derived data will be used. Consent should cover long-term and commercial uses, recontact policies, and possibility of withdrawal, while avoiding “consent creep” that stretches altruistic intentions toward commercialization133. Material transfer agreements (MTAs) must ensure donors’ rights are protected and tissues are not misused for reproductive or controversial applications.

Closely linked is the question of privacy and data governance. As NAMs increasingly generate data that can re-identify individuals, privacy-by-design principles have become essential. Governance frameworks should integrate pseudonymization, access control, and transparent data-sharing procedures consistent with FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) standards134,135. To strengthen public trust, datasets derived from NAMs studies should include detailed provenance and metadata while being protected through controlled-access repositories and clear audit trails131.

The moral and cognitive status of advanced organoid systems, in particular brain organoids, assembloids, and embryo-like models—raises additional ethical scrutiny136, 137, 138. These systems can develop spontaneous electrophysiological activity or mimic early developmental processes, challenging traditional moral boundaries. Ethical oversight must therefore include predefined capability checkpoints and stopping criteria when signs of functional organization arise. Embryoid and chimera studies must remain under strict legal limits, ensuring minimal contribution to neural or germline lineages and maintaining species-specific welfare standards139.

Another aspect is equity and benefit sharing. The expansion of NAMs risks inequities where biological materials are sourced from resource-limited populations but commercialized elsewhere133,140. Standardization should not privilege high-resource laboratories; instead, open-access protocols, cell lines, and calibration materials should be distributed via international consortia134,141. Commercialization and intellectual property demand ethical vigilance. Biosafety, biosecurity, and environmental responsibility add a layer of obligation: the creation and manipulation of organoids, particularly those involving genetic modifications or pathogenic materials, must adhere to biosafety-level protocols, including rigorous risk assessment, containment, and responsible disposal to prevent environmental release142,143.

Another ethical issue in NAMs is validity and reproducibility. Replacing animal testing is only ethically justified if the replacement models are scientifically reliable135,144. NAMs must therefore be designed for defined ‘contexts of use’ with clear performance metrics, such as sensitivity, specificity, and uncertainty ranges. Inter-laboratory validation and publication of both positive and negative results are ethical imperatives to prevent bias and ensure transparency134. Standardization is central to ensuring that NAMs truly advance beyond proof-of-concept. International effort should emphasize harmonized terminology, reference materials, and reporting standards131. Inter-lab ring trials and transparent reporting systems ensure that NAMs gain regulatory trust without perpetuating inequitable or non-reproducible practices134,135.

Moreover, ethical adoption must align with animal welfare principles. NAMs should replace, not merely supplement, animal testing, and their upstream production should minimize animal use141. When animals remain involved in hybrid workflows, refinement and reduction principles must apply, maintaining analgesia, minimizing harm, and continually assessing alternatives117,119. Finally, cross-border harmonization of consent, data sharing, and oversight will help prevent ethics dumping and strengthen global trust in NAM-based regulatory science117,131.

Application of organoids in ADCs/XDCs development

ADCs/XDCs represent a transformative class of targeted biopharmaceuticals, enabling the selective delivery of cytotoxic or immunomodulatory payloads to diseased cells. However, their optimization and druggability assessment remain constrained by the lack of human-relevant preclinical models. Organoid technologies, by recapitulating key aspects of human physiology and pathology, offer a highly promising solution. Application of organoids in therapeutic efficacy evaluation

The evaluation of ADC/XDC therapeutic efficacy is complex and influenced by multiple biological and pharmacological factors. Conventional preclinical models often fail to accurately predict in vivo performance, particularly with respect to the bystander effect and the tumor-immune microenvironment14. Organoids provide patient-specific, physiologically relevant systems that enable comprehensive assessment of key mechanisms underlying ADC efficacy, including antigen binding, internalization, Fc-mediated immune effects, and bystander activity.

First, the efficacy of ADCs critically depends on the ability of the antibody component to selectively recognize and bind tumor-associated antigens. Traditional models are limited in this regard: established cancer cell lines lack tumor heterogeneity, while xenograft models fail to fully recapitulate the TME and immune context11,145. In contrast, PDOs preserve the histopathological and molecular features of the original tumor, enabling evaluation of drug responses in a patient-specific context146. Importantly, differences in receptor organization between 2D and 3D systems can influence drug response. For example, HER2 tends to form heterodimers in 2D cultures, whereas homodimerization predominates in organoids, enhancing sensitivity to HER2-targeted therapies147. These findings highlight the importance of 3D models for accurately assessing on-target cytotoxicity.

Building on this concept, Zhang et al.46 employed bladder cancer organoids to evaluate the HER2-targeted RC48-ADC in vitro (Fig. 6A). By stratifying PDOs based on HER2 expression levels, they demonstrated that RC48-ADC induces pronounced apoptosis and blocks proliferation in high-HER2 organoids, confirming a positive correlation between HER2 levels and therapeutic response. Notably, organoid-based evaluation of ADCs extends beyond HER2 targeting. In EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the antitumor activity of HER3-DXd ADC showed in multiple solid tumor murine xenograft models. On the basis of these nonclinical data, a Phase I clinical trial was initiated and patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC were enrolled; however, more than 60% of patients failed to respond to the ADC148. To explore the efficacy of ADCs in tumors with low HER3 expression, Meng et al.47 established corresponding organoid models to evaluate the potent and durable antitumor activity of T moiety-exatecan conjugate (AMT-562). To gain cellular resolution of ADC potency and payload distribution, tumor penetration of MTX-1000 and T-DXd was evaluated by cell imaging and HER2-positive PDOs. MTX-1000 induced greater cell death and more effectively inhibited organoids growth than T-DXd149. These findings further highlight the predictive and translational value of organoids in assessing ADC efficacy across heterogeneous tumor types. By preserving tumor-specific architecture, target heterogeneity, and drug accessibility, organoids not only enable more accurate preclinical assessment of ADC potency but also help bridge the translational gap between experimental modeling and clinical response prediction.

Second, internalization is a key determinant of ADC efficacy, as payload release often depends on intracellular processing within endosomal or lysosomal compartments150. However, antigen binding alone does not guarantee efficient internalization, which helps explain the variable clinical performance of ADCs despite their therapeutic promise in cancer151. Traditional 2D tumor cell monolayers fail to recapitulate tumor architecture and microenvironmental complexity, limiting their ability to predict ADC internalization and efficacy. In contrast, PDOs preserve tumor morphology, polarity, and heterogeneity, and better reflect patient-specific responses, providing a more physiologically relevant in vitro platform152,153. For instance, Ying et al. compared the internalization of two SN38-conjugated ADCs (n501-SN38 and m603-SN38) in both 2D tumor cell lines and PDOs (Fig. 6B)154. While 2D cultures suggested superior internalization of m603-SN38, PDOs revealed enhanced internalization of n501-SN38, which correlated with greater tumor growth inhibition in xenograft models. This discrepancy highlights the superior predictive value of PDOs for assessing ADC internalization and efficacy in solid tumors. Consistently, Mansfield et al.151 showed that PROTACs can further potentiate ADC activity in organoids by promoting forced internalization.

Following internalization, ADCs are trafficked from endosomes to lysosomes, where linker cleavage enables payload release and induces tumor cell death155. Using Cy5-labeled FGFR3-targeted ADCs, Wang et al.156,157 tracked intracellular distribution in 2D cultures and observed progression from membrane binding to lysosomal accumulation, confirmed by colocalization with the lysosome marker lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP-1). However, precise spatiotemporal control of payload release remains difficult due to the complexity of intracellular trafficking and processing. To address this, Vendrell et al.158 developed a tandem activatable imaging platform enabling real-time monitoring of ADC trafficking and payload release across different molecular designs. Extending such approaches to organoid systems will be critical for capturing these processes under more physiologically relevant conditions and clarifying ADC intracellular fate in vivo.

Third, beyond direct cytotoxicity, ADCs also rely on Fc-mediated immune mechanisms, including antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody-dependent cell-mediated phagocytosis (ADCP), and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC)159. For example, trastuzumab induces ADCC through engagement of FcγRIII on immune effector cells160. In 2D tumor models, cancer cells are cultured as a monolayer, which fail to replicate the native tumor environment and result in the loss of critical signaling pathways involved in growth, metabolism, and differentiation161. These models also overlook the importance of ECM composition and cell–matrix interactions, which are essential for recapitulating the complexity of the TME162. While xenograft models are widely used to study metastasis and evaluate drug responses, they do not fully recapitulate human physiology due to the absence of key immune components and species-specific growth factors, such as interleukin-8 (IL-8)163. This limitation may partly explain why a substantial proportion of therapies that show efficacy in animal models fail in Phase II clinical trials164. Collectively, these shortcomings highlight the need for advanced 3D tumor models that more accurately mimic physiological conditions and enable dynamic assessment of tumor behavior during treatment165.

To address these limitations, organoids-immune cell co-culture systems have been developed to better reconstruct the tumor-immune microenvironment. Poggi et al.166 established CRC PDOs derived co-cultured with Vδ2 T cells to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the Cet-ZA ADC (Fig. 6C). This system enabled direct assessment of ADCC activation and tumor cell elimination, resulting in precise targeting of CRC and promoting a dual antitumor immune response that could potentially enhance therapeutic efficacy. In addition, patient-derived CRC organoids combined with autologous T lymphocytes represent a feasible, cost-effective, reliable, and reproducible 3D platform for personalized preclinical therapeutic testing. Furthermore, the biological and therapeutic effects of CLDN6-DM1 ADC were evaluated in vitro, with tumor regression primarily attributed to CLDN6 antibody-mediated ADCC within the TME167. The potential benefit of combining CLDN6-targeted ADCs with immune checkpoint inhibitors also warrants further investigation. Overall, organoids-immune co-culture systems effectively recapitulate patient-specific tumor–immune interactions, underscoring their value for validating Fc-mediated immunological mechanisms.

Finally, beyond direct cytotoxicity toward Ag+ tumor cells, many ADCs exert their therapeutic effects through “bystander killing”, whereby membrane-permeable payloads diffuse into adjacent Ag− tumor cells168,169. Notably, pharmacodynamic responses in bystander cells typically reach only approximately 50% of the maximal effect observed in directly targeted cells170. To better understand the determinants of this attenuation, preclinical models must recapitulate the transport barriers present in vivo. 3D organoid systems more faithfully reproduce these tumoral transport constraints compared with 2D or acellular models. In conventional 2D co-culture systems, Ag+ and Ag− cells are uniformly mixed, facilitating unrestricted diffusion of payloads to Ag− cells171. In contrast, solid tumors often exhibit spatial compartmentalization of Ag+ and Ag− cell populations, creating a perivascular binding-site barrier (BSB) that limits ADC penetration172. As a result, 2D in vitro systems may overestimate payload efficacy by failing to capture these spatial restrictions and cell–cell interactions. Consistent with this, DEVD ADC demonstrated significant bystander cytotoxicity in co-cultures of HER2+ and HER2- cells, whereas no effect was observed in HER2- monoculture (Fig. 6D)173. Similarly, T-DXd showed activity in both HER2+ and HER2- PDO models, likely reflecting payload-mediated bystander effects174. This phenomenon is particularly relevant in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), a highly heterogeneous and aggressive malignancy. Machinaga et al.22 leveraged PDAC PDO co-culture systems to evaluate payload diffusion and bystander activity in both CEACAM6-negative tumor cells and stromal components. In this setting, the CEACAM6-targeted ADC (84-EBET) induced potent tumor regression with significant bystander activity, underscoring the ability of organoid models to mirror the in vivo conditions. Notably, these organoids phenotypically mirrored their corresponding PDX tumors, further strengthening their translational relevance. Organoid-based models therefore extend efficacy evaluation beyond direct cytotoxicity, enabling quantitative evaluation of bystander effects within physiologically relevant spatial and stromal contexts.

In summary, organoids provide a robust platform for the quantitative assessment of the antitumor activity of ADCs and XDCs by mirroring tumor heterogeneity and accurately simulating the complex tumor-immune microenvironment. This includes key mechanisms such as target binding affinity, internalization, Fc-mediated effects, and bystander effects. By addressing the translational limitations of conventional 2D culture systems and animal models, organoid systems are accelerating the development of next-generation bioconjugated therapeutics and rational combination therapies, while improving the prioritization of candidates for clinical evaluation.

Application of organoids in safety assessment

Safety assessment is a key component of preclinical and clinical drug development, with the choice of species for non-clinical studies critically influencing the interpretation of findings. Critical safety risks of ADCs and XDCs arise from interconnected sources, including (i) species differences in target expression and (ii) limited predictive accuracy of conventional preclinical models175,176. Despite rigorous preclinical testing, a study of 169 clinical trials involving 22,492 patients revealed that 91.2% of ADCs were associated with treatment-related adverse events177. Interestingly, significant species-specific differences in drug hepatotoxic response were found between mice and human microtissues178. Factors such as interspecies differences in drug metabolism, target homology, and immune responses hinder accurate prediction of off-target effects in humans176,178, 179, 180. Consequently, data from conventional models often fail to fully reflect human physiology, complicating the clinical translation of ADCs/XDCs and highlighting the need for more human-relevant platforms. Organoid technology could offer a promising solution to address these challenges by providing more accurate, human-relevant models with greater predictive value for safety testing (Fig. 7A)181.

For instance, different HER2-targeted therapies drugs were approved, including antibodies (trastuzumab, pertuzumab), ADCs (such as T-DM1 and T-DXd), as well as small molecule kinase inhibitors182. Among these, T-DXd caused pulmonary toxicity in monkeys at ≥30 mg/kg (every 3 weeks for 3 doses), but not in rats, which are antigen non-binding species. Similarly, T-DM1 caused the infiltration of mononuclear cells into the lung interstitium in cynomolgus monkeys at ≥10 mg/kg (every 3 weeks for 4 doses), but not in rats183. Phillips et al.184 demonstrated that trastuzumab and T-DM1 do not bind to the rodent HER2 receptor, rendering mice and rats unsuitable for target safety assessment. Similarly, Dokter et al. showed SYD983, a trastuzumab-conjugated ADC, exhibited 1000-fold greater cytotoxicity in HER2-positive human cell line than in rodent models, emphasizing that certain target-dependent effects cannot be modeled in non-binding species53. These findings underscore the importance of appropriate species selection in early in vivo safety studies to enhance the translatability of preclinical data and reduce unnecessary animal testing.

Human-derived organoids represent a transformative preclinical model by recapitulating the morphological and functional aspects of human organs, addressing species-specific limitations, and improving translational predictability. Hepatotoxicity induced by ADCs/XDCs involves multifactorial mechanisms, which poses challenges for preclinical safety assessment due to species diversity175. For example, sacituzumab govitecan (SG), an anti-trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (anti-Trop-2) ADC linked with the topoisomerase-inhibitor SN-38, is associated with significant hepatotoxicity, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) elevation incidence of 25.3%185. Normal hepatic organoids can replicate human-specific metabolic pathways such as SN-38 glucuronidation, enabling the prediction of hepatotoxicity undetectable in animal models due to the absence of human CYP450 enzymes186. The ASCENT trial further highlighted how UGT1A1 polymorphisms influence SG-related adverse events, demonstrating the utility of organoids in revealing mechanistic insights into off-target effects187. Cardiovascular adverse reactions, a critical concern for many ADCs/XDCs, can also be evaluated using organoids. Cardiac organoids with electromechanical coupling capture arrhythmias associated with microtubule-disrupting payloads188. Paired patient tumor-normal organoids enable simultaneous assessment of tumor cytotoxicity and normal tissue vulnerability, delineating ADC therapeutic windows188. For instance, a T moiety-exatecan ADC demonstrated improved gastrointestinal safety and minimal myelotoxicity compared to exatecan149. These models support ICH S6 guidelines, which discourage toxicity studies in non-relevant species189.

Off-site ADC toxicity can be categorized as “on-target” or “off-target”. On-target off-tumor toxicities significantly limit the clinical potential of antibody-related therapies, because they arise from by the binding of ADCs to antigens expressed on healthy tissues190. A key reason is that most ADCs are designed to specifically target human antigens with minimal cross-reactivity in mice. This high specificity complicates the differentiation between on-target and off-target toxicities in conventional mouse models. Additionally, on-target off-tumor toxicity may occur when the target antigen is expressed at low levels in healthy tissues, as observed with Nectin-4 in the urothelium, leading to cutaneous and ocular side effects191,192. Such limitations mean that on-target off-tumor toxicities are rarely captured in early preclinical studies and are typically only addressed in nonhuman primate toxicity studies or phase I clinical trials193. An illustrative example is the HER3-targeting DB-1310 ADC, which binds to pneumocytes in monkeys but not in humans, suggesting that humans may be more resistant to DB-131-induced pulmonary toxicity194. In addition, ADCs induced interstitial lung disease accounts for 3%‒5% of all interstitial lung disease (ILD) cases, primarily caused by antineoplastic agents195. These unexpected off-target toxicities often arise from significant deficiencies in preclinical models. These models suffer from poor biomimicry in 2D cell cultures and limited interspecies translatability, and genetic drift, all of which reduce confidence in their ability to faithfully simulate human disease. In fact, discordance in toxicity prediction across preclinical models is frequently caused by cross-species target differences196. Organoid cultures provide a promising alternative, overcoming many of the limitations inherent to conventional 2D and xenograft models.

Human-derived organoids exhibited high similarity to their native organs, possessing structural and functional characteristics that enhance toxicity prediction69. Unlike conventional preclinical models, organoids improve the translational value of preclinical findings and help define safer therapeutic margins. For example, Ortiz et al.64 developed bronchoalveolar organoids derived from human, rat, and nonhuman primate lung cells to investigate off-target toxicities associated with ADCs with three linker-payload combinations (mafodotin, vedotin, and deruxtecan). Human bronchioalveolar organoids modeled the drug responses observed in humans, while nonhuman primates mirrored in vivo responses, highlighting the potential of organoids to reduce reliance on animal studies. Furthermore, human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived organoids can capture inter-individual susceptibility variations across critical organs, such as the liver, heart, kidney, intestine, allowing for a more personalized approach to safety testing197. Gjorevski et al.198 leveraged patient-derived healthy intestinal organoids to study the on-target off-tumor toxicities of T-cell-engaging bispecific antibodies (TCBs) (Fig. 7B). Healthy organoids-immune cell co-cultures were able to capture clinical toxicities that were overlooked by animal models, demonstrating the capability of these systems to predict human relevant responses.

Overall, organoids are poised to reshape the preclinical safety assessment paradigm for ADCs/XDCs. They address the critical limitations of species-specific target expression and the inaccuracies of conventional toxicity prediction by incorporating human-specific drug metabolism, tissue microenvironment, and inter-individual variability. Driven by technological innovation and evolving regulatory guidance, organoid platforms are increasingly positioned to replace animal testing and emerge as a new standard for preclinical drug safety evaluation.

Application of organoids in drug resistance

Drug resistance remains a major limitation to the clinical efficacy of ADCs. Mechanisms of ADCs resistance can be broadly categorized as: (i) antibody-mediated resistance, (ii) defects in internalization and trafficking pathways, (iii) lysosomal dysfunction, (iv) payload-related resistance, and (v) TEM-driven resistance199. While 2D cell cultures offer advantages such as low cost, reproducibility, and scalability, they fail to fully recapitulate the complex mechanisms underlying drug resistance. In contrast, organoids faithfully model both cell-autonomous mechanisms, including target downregulation, impaired internalization, lysosomal dysfunction, and upregulated transporter proteins, and non-cell-autonomous mechanisms, such as immune modulation within the TME24,38,200.

Firstly, antibody-mediated downregulation of target antigen reduces antigen-antibody engagement, limiting intracellular payload release and cytotoxicity. This is exemplified by the observation that the sustained exposure to T-DXd or T-DM1 induced resistance in BC cells; over 60% of patients fail to respond to HER3-targeting ADC (U3-1402)47,201. A key reason is that reduction of HER2 expression impairs ADC binding and internalization, limiting intracellular payload release and cytotoxicity. Moreover, multiple studies support HER2 loss as a key mechanism of T-DM1 resistance, with resistant cell lines consistently exhibiting reduced HER2 expression or binding capacity201,202. PDO-based studies further demonstrate that organoids faithfully recapitulate inter-patient heterogeneity in target expression observed in tumors203. Organoid models have also been used to evaluate antibody-mediated resistance and alternative targeting strategies. For example, PDOs have been employed to assess EGFR-targeted photodynamic therapy using both antibody- and nanobody-based photosensitizer conjugates24. Studies leveraging organoid models demonstrated that T moiety-exatecan ADCs remain effective against T-DXd or SG-resistant organoids with low target expression, showing potent and durable antitumor activity149. Promisingly, T moiety-exatecan ADCs with greater potency exhibited no toxicity in nonhuman primates. To gain cellular resolution of ADC potency and payload distribution, HER3-positive patient-derived colon cancer organoids were integrated with cell imaging to evaluate tumor penetration of AMT-562 and U3-1402 47. Compared with U3-1402, the exatecan-conjugated HER3-targeting ADCs showed an improved therapeutic window, overcoming resistance and producing more durable responses in insensitive tumors. Importantly, 3D organoids profiling provides a more comprehensive analytical platform than 2D cell culture, as it preserves spatial architecture, cellular morphology, and cell-cell interactions of native tissues204. Integrating organoids with complementary systems, such as transcriptomic datasets and resistant cell lines, further enhances mechanistic insights. For instance, analysis combining I-SPY2 trial data, PDO cohorts, and T-DXd-resistant cell lines identified sorting nexin 10 (SNX10) deficiency as a driver of resistance to HER2-targeting ADCs in HER2-positive BC (Fig. 8A)205. Organoids integrated with multi-omics approaches thus facilitate the development of next-generation bioconjugated drugs by elucidating resistance mechanisms and identifying predictive biomarkers.

Secondly, decreased internalization efficiency and impaired endocytosis pathways may compromise ADC efficacy. Effective ADC function requires internalization followed by lysosomal degradation. In resistant cells, T-DM1 is preferentially internalized into caveolin-1 (CAV-1)-positive endosomes, impairing trafficking to lysosomes (Fig. 8B)206. Furthermore, elevated CAV-1 expression correlates with reduced T-DM1 internalization, indicating alternative endocytic pathways in drug resistance207. Endocytosis mechanisms, including clathrin-mediated and caveolin-dependent pathways, are key determinants of ADC uptake and can be faithfully recapitulated in organoid systems208. To address impaired internalization, bispecific ADCs (BsADCs) have been developed to enhance uptake and tissue penetration209. For example, biparatopic HER2-directed ADCs, which bind two non-overlapping epitopes, induce receptor clustering and improve internalization efficiency210. Organoids faithfully recapitulate the CAV-1 pathway as a key driver of therapeutic resistance and enable the visualization of the cellular underlying ADC-cell interactions (Fig. 8B)208,211. ZW49 is anti-HER2 biparatopic ADC composed of ZW25 conjugated to an N-acyl sulfonamide auristatin payload. Several studies have demonstrated that ZW49 is rapidly internalized in vitro in HER2-expressing organoids compared with monospecific trastuzumab-based ADCs212. Another promising strategy involves the use of nanobodies (Nbs), which exhibit exceptional precision and internalization efficiency in organoid systems. For instance, the uptake rate of A5 nanobodies exceeded 80%, markedly outperforming conventional CD155 antibodies about 25%, with substantial fluorescence accumulation observed in both the membrane and cytoplasm213. This enhanced internalization is largely attributed to the smaller molecular size of nanobodies, approximately one-tenth that of conventional monoclonal antibodies, which confers a unique advantage for drug delivery. Building on this property, Jung et al.213 developed A5 Nbs conjugated to liposomes loaded with doxorubicin and validate their therapeutic potential in PDOs, demonstrating improved efficacy by overcoming limitations associated with low internalization efficiency. Looking ahead, organoids integrated with advanced imaging techniques will empower the real-time analysis of ADCs and XDCs internalization and the dissection of endocytosis-related resistance mechanisms.

Thirdly, lysosomal dysfunction represents a central mechanism of ADC resistance. The efficacy of ADCs relies on lysosomal degradation within an acidic environment and the activity of proteolytic enzymes. Studies have shown that lysosomal dysfunction in T-DM1-resistant BC cell lines is characterized by increased lysosomal pH, which impairs protease activity (Fig. 8C)214. In addition, the cytotoxic effects of T-DM1 have been linked to the SLC46A3 transporter, highlighting the importance of intracellular trafficking for payload activity215. Together, these findings indicate that both lysosomal enzymes and the microenvironment are critical for efficient degradation and drug release, particularly for ADCs carrying non-cleavable linker payloads such as T-DM1. Conventional 2D cell lines only partially recapitulate the metabolic and organelle heterogeneity of clinical tumors. In contrast, PDOs maintain key features of the tissue of origin, including gradients of proliferation, hypoxia and nutrient availability, all of which are tightly linked to lysosomal function and autophagy216. This enhanced physiological relevance positions organoids as an ideal platform to study lysosome-dependent drug responses. Indeed, studies in drug-resistant tumor organoids have shown that lysosomal dysfunction can lead to sequestration, accumulation, and exocytosis of various targeted anticancer drugs217. This process may reduce drug bioavailability at the target site and may contribute to resistance mechanisms. Furthermore, impaired lysosomal proteolytic activity has been identified as a key pathological feature in resistant organoids218. To overcome lysosome-related resistance, several strategies have been proposed. One approach is to enhance the bystander effect, thereby reducing dependence on intact lysosomal processing. Linker innovations, such as the incorporation of caspase-3-sensitive motifs (e.g., DEVD), introduce an alternative cleavage mechanism that can bypass lysosomal degradation. The DEVD linker is cleaved by caspase-3 following its activation in the cytoplasm, enabling extracellular release and broader diffusion of the cytotoxic payload, thereby amplifying the bystander effect in regions with inefficient ADC processing (Fig. 8C)173. Although bystander-competent ADCs can partially mitigate cell-to-cell variability in lysosomal function, they still rely on lysosomal processing in a subset of high antigen-expressing cells to initiate payload release. Building on these advances, AOCs have emerged as a novel class of therapeutics that combine the specificity of antibodies with siRNA or antisense oligonucleotides219. AOCs equipped with cleavable linkers may undergo cleavage in early or late endosomes through hydrolysis, protease-mediated cleavage, or disulfide bond reduction, thereby bypassing lysosomal transport220. Although AOCs exhibit great promise in addressing resistance associated with lysosomal dysfunction, their behavior in complex 3D organoid models remains insufficiently explored and warrants further investigation.

Fourthly, existing mechanisms may arise from the payload of ADCs. Tumor cells can develop resistance by upregulating drug efflux transporters, particularly ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters such as P-glycoprotein, BC resistance protein, and multidrug resistance-associated proteins, thereby reducing intracellular drug accumulation (Fig. 8D). However, due to the lack of appropriate modeling systems, the regulatory interactions between these transporters and tumor cells remain incompletely understood. Studies have shown that organoids form 3D structures with lumen-like cavities and exhibit transporter expression and localization patterns comparable to those observed in native tissues221. These findings underscore the physiological relevance of organoids for modeling drug transport and elucidating mechanisms of ADC resistance. To overcome payload-related resistance, alternative cytotoxic payloads and next-generation bioconjugated drugs are being developed. ADCs incorporating pyrrolobenzodiazepine (PBD) dimers are currently under clinical evaluation in both hematologic and solid tumors222. Another strategy involves the use of the tesirine payload, as exemplified by the approved ADCs ADCT-301 (anti-CD25) and ADCT-402 (anti-CD19). In addition, Kelly et al.223 validated a humanized B7-H3-targeting ADC conjugated with a PBD payload in clinically relevant metastatic prostate cancer (mPC) PDX and organoid models, which faithfully recapitulate transporter expression patterns observed in human mPC. Despite these advances, single-payload ADCs remain prone to resistance during treatment199. To address this limitation, multi-payload ADCs have emerged as a promising strategy224. For example, Levengood et al.224 successfully demonstrated that the dual-payload ADCs exhibit strong therapeutic efficacy in PDO models of anaplastic large cell lymphoma resistant to single-agent therapies. Similarly, dual-payload ADCs co-delivering the topoisomerase I inhibitor exatecan and the RNA polymerase II inhibitor triptolide have shown significant antitumor activity in both in vitro PDO models and in vivo xenograft systems225. These findings highlight the potential of multi-payload ADCs to overcome resistance associated with conventional monotherapies. In addition, combination therapies can further enhance ADC efficacy. For instance, the combination of a DR5-targeting ADC (Oba01) with the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor abemaciclib synergistically inhibited tumor growth in PDO models derived from advanced MSS and MSI-H CRC (Fig. 8D)226. Multi-omics analyses, including RNA sequencing and proteomics, demonstrated that these PDO models retain key molecular features of primary tumors and identified CDKs as synergistic targets of Oba01.

Finally, beyond tumor-intrinsic mechanisms, the TME plays a critical role in drug resistance. In addition to cell-autonomous processes, the high failure rate of therapeutics is significantly driven by both inherent and acquired resistance mechanisms arising from the TME. Alterations within the TME can impede the penetration of large molecules such as ADCs into tumor tissue, thereby compromising their biodistribution and therapeutic efficacy35. Modeling TME heterogeneity in vitro is therefore essential for understanding ADC resistance and for developing treatment strategies that comprehensively address factors influencing tumor progression and therapeutic response. To investigate immunomodulatory effects, Qi et al.227 utilized SG-resistant triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) PDOs, which faithfully recapitulate key features of the in vivo TME, and demonstrated the enhanced efficacy of AIE780-SG (Fig. 8E). Furthermore, IR780-based homodimers were shown to reprogram the TME by promoting the recruitment and activation of immune cells, thereby exerting dual antitumor effects—simultaneously inhibiting tumor growth and enhancing antitumor immunity in TNBC models228. To better capture clinically relevant resistance mechanisms, advanced organoids co-culture systems have been developed that integrate tumor, stromal, and immune components, enabling more accurate modeling of the in vivo TME. These systems allow the identification of microenvironment-driven resistance mechanisms and facilitate the evaluation of ADC/XDC efficacy in the presence of physiological barriers229. In addition, combining PDOs with single-cell RNA sequencing has enabled the characterization of dynamic changes in the TME before and after neoadjuvant treatment with ADCs in combination with ICIs, providing insights into tumor heterogeneity and variability in treatment responses230. Collectively, these approaches highlight the unique capacity of organoid models to dissect TME-driven resistance mechanisms and support the development of next-generation bioconjugated drugs capable of overcoming such barriers.

In summary, organoids recapitulate clinically relevant cell-autonomous and non-cell-autonomous mechanisms, serving as a platform for novel ADCs/XDCs functional validation assays. When combined with multi-omics and advanced imaging approaches, organoids enable dynamic, high-resolution analysis of resistance pathways and drug distribution. These capabilities position organoids as key tools for guiding personalized therapeutic strategies and accelerating the development of next-generation bioconjugated drugs.

Application of organoids in other fields

The implementation of personalized medicine for ADCs/XDCs requires reliable tools to assess and predict patient-specific responses. However, no animal model fully recapitulates the full spectrum of human disease phenotypes, making it challenging to translate therapeutic effects observed in preclinical models into clinical efficacy231. Organoids not only serve as preclinical models for evaluating the efficacy, safety, and resistance of ADCs/XDCs, but also provide a platform for individualized treatment selection232. Notably, PDOs established from the same patient at different time points displayed dynamic changes in genetic profiles and drug sensitivities, mirroring in vivo tumor progression233. The application of organoids in ADC/XDC selection can be broadly divided into two areas: the selection of existing approved drugs (including repurposing and repositioning) and the screening of novel bioconjugated drugs.

Drug repurposing of ADCs represents a promising strategy for cancer therapy (Fig. 9A)234. Given the heterogeneous nature of tumors, no single treatment approach is universally effective, resulting in highly variable clinical response rates232. Unlike the relatively static genomics of 2D cultures, PDOs capture genomic heterogeneity and its functional consequences, including target expression, internalization, and payload sensitivity, within a human-relevant context235,236. PDOs-based platform enable in vitro patient stratification by screening anticancer agents to predict clinical responses237. The concept of personalized medicine has increasingly been explored for ADCs. For instance, Klinghoffer et al.238 developed a screening panel of 60 PDOs representing 10 solid tumor types, using clinically relevant ADCs such as T-DXd and SG. Differential gene expression analyses revealed that ADC efficacy is influenced by predictive biomarkers beyond target expression, including genes such as VIL1 and MYO7B, which regulate processes like cell–cell adhesion. Furthermore, pharmacogenomic profiling of a multi-region PLC organoid biobank revealed substantial heterogeneity in drug responses and resistance mechanisms, ultimately informing the design of effective combination therapies239. A growing biobank of HER2-positive PDOs has also been used to evaluate sensitivity to T-DXd and its exatecan payload240. Notably, T-DXd induced significant tumor regression even in PDO models with low HER2 expression, supporting its potential application in personalized treatment strategies. Collectively, these findings highlight the ability of organoid platforms to capture patient-specific response heterogeneity and guide ADC repurposing and combination therapies across cancer types.

(A) Utilizing genomic profiling of PDOs to identify opportunities for repositioning existing ADCs/XDCs to treat rare or drug-resistant cancers. (B) Leveraging PDO biobanks to efficiently evaluate the efficacy of novel bioconjugated drug candidates and identify therapeutic vulnerabilities in vitro through high-throughput screening. (C) Integrating organoid platforms with high-throughput screening and multi-omics analysis to facilitate the development and selection of effective next-generation bioconjugated drugs tailored to individual patients.

Screening for next-generation bioconjugated drugs remains a major focus of cancer research (Fig. 9B). Organoid biobanks constitute a unique resource that links advanced disease modelling with individualized treatment. By serving as patient-specific “avatars”, they enable high-throughput screening (HTS) and direct measurement of drug sensitivity, thereby accelerating the development of personalized therapies239. Organoid-based HTS platforms bridge the gap between high-throughput but reductionist models (such as 2D cell lines) and more physiologically relevant but low-throughput systems (such as animals models)241. For instance, a large-scale functional screen of bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) was conducted using a heterogeneous CRC PDO biobank alongside matched healthy colonic mucosa samples242. High-content imaging enabled the screening of over 500 therapeutic BsAbs, leading to the identification of MCLA-158 (anti-EGFR/LGR5) as a potent inhibitor of tumor organoids growth with minimal toxicity toward normal stem cell compartments. Similarly, the “OrganoidXplore” platform, which integrates PDOs with advanced 3D high-content imaging, has been used to visualize target engagement and ADC responses in organoid systems243. PDO biobanks thus offer a scalable, cost-effective, and time-efficient platform that captures diverse mutational landscapes and drug response profiles.

In parallel, organoid systems are increasingly used to inform the design of novel bioconjugated drugs. Matrix-embedded 3D PDO models are particularly well suited to investigate how protease activity influences drug penetration, linker cleavage, and payload release244. By restoring key cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, these systems more accurately recapitulate tumor architecture and aggressiveness, thereby improving the assessment of drug delivery and efficacy245,246. In BC, Vicent and colleagues247 developed matrix-embedded spheroids and a PDO biobank to evaluate the internalization and cytotoxicity of novel polypeptide-drug conjugates. Their findings demonstrated that patient-specific responses to a polypeptide-based nanomedicine correlated with cathepsin B activity and glutathione levels, highlighting the potential of functional biomarkers for guiding patient-tailored therapies.

In conclusion, the central promise of organoid technology lies in advancing personalized precision medicine (Fig. 9C). Organoids have the potential to reduce ineffective and toxic treatments by enabling preclinical drug sensitivity testing in patient-derived systems. Moreover, the use of paired normal and tumor organoids from the same patient allows for precise delineation of therapeutic windows. Ultimately, the integration of these platforms into clinical pipelines is expected to accelerate the development of more effective and better-tolerated next-generation bioconjugated therapies.

Conclusions and perspectives

Organoids, serving as both drug screening and druggability evaluation platforms, have demonstrated unprecedented potential for assessing the therapeutic efficacy, safety, and drug resistance of ADCs and XDCs, providing a promising bridge between preclinical studies and clinical applications. Recent advances have enabled the integration of organoids-based studies into Phase I-III clinical trials, representing a significant milestone in their translational application (e.g., NCT06102824). Nevertheless, organoid technology remains in a phase of rapid development, and its broader adoption continues to face technical and practical challenges. In this review, we summarize the latest design considerations and advances in the use of organoids for ADC/XDC evaluation, with the goal of providing a roadmap for future targeted drug development. Despite notable progress, the ongoing development and application of organoids encounter specific challenges, which are described hereafter (Fig. 10A).

Challenges and technological perspectives

While organoids offer unprecedented opportunities for advancing ADCs and XDCs, their broader application is currently limited by several inherent challenges, including high costs, restricted scalability, lack of functional vascularization, and issues with standardization. To fully harness the potential of organoid models in ADCs and XDCs research, future technological innovations are likely to focus on three pivotal aspects: standardization, complexification, and intelligentization. These advancements are expected to systematically address the limitations of current models, ultimately transforming organoids from a research tool into a robust, widely adopted foundation platform.

Firstly, standardization remains major hurdles, compounded by high costs, and limited scalability. Traditional PDX models require long development cycles and are expensive, whereas PDO models are influenced by batch-to-batch variations in matrix gel, making stable replication of the ECM challenges. Biologically, 3D bioprinting offers a solution by enabling standardized batch production through programmable automation, better recapitulating the in vivo TME. This approach has validated robust 3D bioprinted cancer models, allowing drug sensitivity testing to be completed within 10 days, with reduced costs and superior reproducibility248. By integrating 3D bioprinting with automated culture systems, organoids production costs can be significantly lowered, while the physiological relevance and translational potential of organoids are enhanced. Moreover, the combination of organoids with microfluidics has given rise to OoC platforms, and integrating miniaturized biosensors is expected to allow real-time, continuous assessment of drug candidates, further improving standardization and reproducibility. Analytically, the field is moving toward consensus metrics enabled by automated imaging and machine learning (ML), complemented by multi-omics data for benchmarking. Advances in automated imaging and ML now allow high-throughput quantification of organoid morphology, cellular composition, and dynamic behavior. Operationally, adopting industrial quality management practices, automation, and ready-to-use cryopreserved kits ensures scalable and reproducible manufacturing. From a policy perspective, on September 25, 2025, the US NIH awarded $87 million to establish the first-of-its-kind Standardized Organoid Modeling Center, a national resource aimed at establishing large-scale organoid biobanks to reduce reliance on animal models249. Collectively, these coordinated efforts toward standardization provide a comprehensive roadmap for building robust, reliable, and scalable platforms to accelerate drug discovery and advance precision medicine. Ultimately, community-wide collaboration through international consortia (to develop guidelines, conduct inter-laboratory trials, and establish minimum reporting standards) is essential to achieve regulatory acceptance.

Secondly, the lack of vascularization and the incomplete immune microenvironment in conventional organoid cultures severely limit their physiological fidelity. To enhance TME simulation, immune infiltration can be restored by co-culturing tumor organoids with immune organoids. By applying different stimuli to immune organoids, the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of the tumor-immune microenvironment can be modeled, effectively reproducing the full immune cycle of “recognition-infiltration-killing” in vitro. Immune organoids are thus expected to serve as a crucial bridge between basic research and clinical translation. In avascular organoids, tight junctions impede oxygen and nutrient diffusion, leading to extensive necrosis and apoptosis in their cores. This structural limitation distorts the authentic penetration and distribution of bioconjugated drugs. In contrast, 3D vascularized organoids provide critical insights into tumor growth, invasion, and therapeutic responses, revealing how the vascular network influences drug delivery250. Unlike 2D or poorly vascularized 3D models, where drugs often accumulate at the periphery, perfusion through vascularized organoids results in cytotoxicity predominantly in central regions, better reflecting tissue distribution and penetration efficiency. Furthermore, cascading multiple OoC platforms enables validation of drug interactions and offers an efficient system for personalized pharmacokinetic prediction and safety assessment. The use of OoCs aligns closely with the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) by reducing reliance on animal experimentation. These approaches not only improve animal welfare and address ethical concerns but also enhance the predictive power and translational relevance of preclinical studies. Although still in early stages, these advances suggest that vascularized and immune-enhanced organoid systems can more faithfully capture the complexity of human tumors than the previous models.

Thirdly, analytical bottlenecks and data interpretation challenges hinder the high-throughput application of 3D models. It is difficult to capture and quantify the spatial features of organoids, as 3D high-content imaging is often confounded by the multifocality and intrinsic heterogeneity of the cultures. To overcome this, AI and ML are rapidly transforming organoids and OoC technologies, accelerating their evolution from biologically rich systems toward digitalization and intelligence (Fig. 10B). As global regulatory agencies increasingly promote NAMs to replace animal testing, AI-organoids integration is becoming central to next-generation drug discovery, toxicology, and personalized medicine251. Several drugs evaluated using organoids and OoCs (e.g., Petosemtamab, TNT005, and HRS-1893) have been approved by regulatory authorities to enter clinical trials based on the efficacy and safety data from organoids and OoC models. By adopting a multimodal strategy that combines computational pathology, single-cell in situ spatial imaging, and AI-driven modeling, researchers can uncover novel targets and predict the efficacy of bioconjugated drugs in organoids252. Another critical challenge in organoids research is the analysis of high-throughput multi-omics data generated during drug evaluation. ML provides a powerful solution, enabling exploration of complex relationships between different omics layers and phenotypic outputs. AI can streamline these analyses, extracting meaningful biological insights from otherwise overwhelming datasets. Overall, the convergence of AI with organoids and OoCs represents a paradigm shift toward intelligentization in vitro human models. As computational and experimental frameworks mature, AI-enabled organoid platforms are poised to become foundational for next-generation human biology studies, accelerate drug discovery, and serve as a cornerstone in the global transition away from animal experimentation.

Application challenges and perspectives

Druggability evaluation of next-generation bioconjugated drugs requires a robust preclinical model capable of revolutionizing the entire drug development pipeline. With the advancing sophistication of organoid technology, its application scope is rapidly expanding from basic biology to encompass the full spectrum of drug development. By leveraging the interdisciplinary integration of materials engineering, biosensing, and AI, organoids and OoC technologies are increasingly overcoming the limitations of conventional preclinical research.

Firstly, target discovery and validation are essential for the development of bioconjugated drugs, as they directly determine therapeutic efficacy. Extensive research on ADCs and XDCs has focused on targets such as HER2 and TROP2; however, discovering additional clinically relevant targets remains a significant challenge, often relying on preclinical models that differ biologically from humans. While traditional cancer cell line models fail to recapitulate the pathophysiology of the original tumor, near-physiological organoid culture systems better preserve histological architecture, gene expression patterns, and the genomic landscape of the patient's tumor. Integrating multi-omics profiling can further quantify differences between organoids and patient tumors, providing a deeper understanding of organoid heterogeneity for target discovery. Network analyses of organoids-derived proteomics revealed that organoid culture reproduces 94% of the tissue proteome, compared with only 70% in 2D monolayer culture253. With advances in co-culture technologies, interactions between tumor organoids, immune cells, and stromal cells can facilitate the identification of novel targets and biomarkers, while providing an authentic platform for drug evaluation. Additionally, combining organoids with multi-omics platforms and ML analysis enhances the discovery of predictive biomarkers, addressing the limitations of conventional genetic tests. Overall, organoids-based platforms hold promise for discovering novel targets that improve the therapeutic efficacy and reduce toxicity of bioconjugated drugs.

Secondly, drug screening of bioconjugated drugs is a critical process for identifying potential therapeutic candidates, encompassing efficacy evaluation, toxicity assessment, and ADME studies. Compared to 2D cell culture models, which lack the complexity of the native microenvironment, 3D organoid cultures provide a more physiologically relevant system and allow more precise prediction of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Organoids also enable toxicity assessment by recapitulating organ-specific metabolic and detoxification functions, facilitating evaluation of drug metabolism, adverse effects, and interactions. Liver, intestinal, and kidney organoids are used to predict organ toxicity and pharmacokinetics of bioconjugated drugs, enhancing both the accuracy and efficiency of safety assessment. Future drug screening may employ high-throughput strategies using organoid biobanks, enabling not only identification of patient-specific responders to approved treatments but also the discovery of novel bioconjugated drugs. Integrating multi-OoC systems can deliver drugs to multiple functional organoids while monitoring real-time biomarker changes, providing an all-in-one platform for efficacy and safety evaluation. Combining organoids with high-resolution 3D imaging further enables dynamic tracking of cellular behaviors and molecular interactions, and AI-driven analysis can capture and interpret these complex morphological and functional features, guiding drug development within TME.

Thirdly, managing drug resistance is evolving toward a precision paradigm integrating “multi-omics”, “multi-modality”, and “multi-temporal” strategies. Single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and proteomics of organoids generate ultra-high-resolution profiles of tumor–immune–matrix interactions, uncovering novel mechanisms of resistance. Simultaneously, AI-driven drug-target-disease network predictions accelerate rational design of bioconjugated drugs, including bispecific ADCs, AOCs, and multi-payload ADCs. Tumor cell drug resistance is a major determinant of treatment efficacy and patient outcomes. 3D organoid cultures allow assessment of how various drugs influence resistance, providing valuable insights for the development of antitumor therapies. In contrast, conventional 2D cultures fail to replicate in vivo microenvironments and cellular behaviors, limiting their utility for resistance studies. Although tumor organoids recapitulate molecular and pathological features of parental tumors, they primarily contain epithelial tumor cells, lacking stromal components. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), key elements of the TME, modulate tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and drug resistance through multiple mechanisms. Reconstructing the TME with patient-derived CAFs, immune cells, vascular networks, and biomechanical cues to simulate disease progression remains a major challenge. Co-culture organoid models integrating CAFs with multi-omics analyses can reveal drug resistance genes or novel antigens, informing strategies to overcome resistance to bioconjugated drugs. Thus, co-culture approaches represent a critical direction for future organoids-based research.

Fourthly, organoids provide a novel avenue for personalized treatment by generating patient-derived PDOs that reflect individual tumor characteristics. These models can predict the efficacy and prognosis of bioconjugated drugs, potentially improving treatment outcomes and minimizing adverse effects. Prior to initiating therapy, PDO-based companion diagnostic testing can verify target engagement and avoid administration of ineffective or harmful treatments. Despite this potential, organoids derived from the same tumor can exhibit heterogeneous drug responses, and spatial variability within tumors necessitates multiple sampling to fully capture treatment sensitivity. Combining organoids with liquid biopsy techniques allows dynamic monitoring of treatment responses, enabling real-time adjustment of drug administration. Overall, organoid technology is poised to bridge ex vivo drug sensitivity data with patient responses, guiding precision medicine in the future.

Last but not least, joint efforts from academia, industry, and government are essential to address ethical considerations and regulatory compliance, including data privacy, algorithmic transparency, informed consent, and the responsible use of AI predictions and human-derived organoids and OoCs254. Government involvement is particularly critical in providing funding, regulatory oversight, standardization guidelines, and ethical frameworks, as well as in facilitating collaborations among academia, industry, and the public sector to support the successful clinical translation of drug development discoveries. Journal editors and reviewers also play a key role in promoting awareness of these advanced models, ensuring recognition of the robustness and scientific value of the data they generate.

In conclusion, the development of organoid models have driven innovation in the field of bioconjugated drugs, enabling next-generation therapeutics with enhanced targeting specificity, improved safety profiles, and more controlled payload release. Although still in the early stages of research and development, organoid platforms show great potential in recapitulating human tumor microenvironment and heterogeneity. Beyond organoids alone, integrating OoC systems and complementary technologies is expected to make landmark contributions to the design and evaluation of novel bioconjugated drugs.
