Hybrid CNN–GCN network with dynamic gating and scale-aware preprocessing for pedestrian crossing intention prediction in Indian road conditions
Authors: Rajesh Kannan Megalingam, Naveen Prasaad Selvarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pedestrian crossing intention prediction is a critical task for autonomous driving that requires high accuracy and real-time inference capability. In India, this is particularly challenging in unstructured and crowded road environments where occlusions, vulnerable road users, and non-compliant pedestrian behavior are very common. Existing methods are primarily trained on non-Indian datasets which often struggles to generalize to such conditions and rely on static combinations of visual and non-visual features, leading to a high computational complexity. To address these limitations, we propose a hybrid CNN-GCN based network with a dynamic modality gating module that adaptively prioritizes visual (pose, local and global context) and non-visual (motion, bounding boxes) features depending on the scene context. This enables the model to emphasize reliable modalities under visual degradation while maintaining computational efficiency. In addition, a novel scale-aware HRNet based preprocessing module with adaptive scaling is introduced to robustly extract pedestrians pose key points for distant and partially occluded pedestrians. To support evaluation in unstructured traffic scenarios, a custom Indian Road Dataset (IRD) is collected in Trivandrum city, India covering urban and suburban roads to capture diverse traffic scenarios with dense crowds, non-compliant pedestrian behaviors, adverse weather and frequent occlusions. The proposed model achieves moderate accuracy of 88% on JAAD_All dataset and 82% on the IRD dataset, while maintaining the lower inference latency (1.5ms on IRD and 4.6 ms on JAAD_All) among compared methods achieving a favorable trade-off between predictive performance and computational efficiency. Extensive experiments including ablation studies on modality gating, attention mechanisms, and scale-aware preprocessing, along with cross-dataset generalization analysis(PIE dataset) and failure case evaluation demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach. Further analysis under varying conditions including occlusion, pedestrian scale variations, and modality weight adaptation shows that the model dynamically adjusts feature reliance and retains consistent predictive capability in visually degraded scenarios. Additionally, deployment on the Jetson Xavier NX demonstrates near real-time performance, highlighting the practical feasibility of the proposed method for autonomous driving applications.

Introduction

Autonomous vehicles (AV) aim to replace human drivers in navigating safely through complex road environments. AVs are expected to significantly improve road safety, mobility, and efficiency as the stats show human error makes up nearly 94% of crashes. However, AVs face major challenges in perception and decision-making, particularly in understanding pedestrian behavior. Pedestrian crossing intention prediction has emerged as an important task for preventing accidents1,2, yet it remains highly challenging due to the variability and unpredictability in pedestrian’s behavior3. The challenge is severe in India. According to the report published by the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (MoRTH), the pedestrians account for nearly 20% of traffic fatalities making over 35,000 deaths in 2023, with fatalities increasing at 2.6% annually4. Road crossings are frequent hotspots for crashes5, and pedestrians often share space with vehicles due to the absence of crosswalks, sidewalks, and signalized infrastructure6,7. Unlike structured environments in North America and Europe, where datasets such as PIE8 and JAAD9 were collected, Indian roads are characterized by high density of pedestrians, frequent occlusions, and non-compliant pedestrian behavior, making direct transfer of existing models challenging. Recent advancements have explored LSTM10,11, GCN12,13, and Transformer14 based architectures, inspired by the self-attention mechanism introduced in15, for pedestrian intention prediction. However, three significant gaps remain. First, there is no dataset that captures the complexities of Indian road scenarios, limiting model generalizability. Second, multimodal fusion methods often merge visual and non-visual cues with fixed weights16–18, which is brittle under conditions such as occlusion, glare, and sensor degradation19,20. Third, although pose-based features are important for action and intention recognition21,22, existing pose estimation models degrade with small-scale, distant, and occluded pedestrians which are common in Indian traffic23,24. While HRNet preserves high-resolution representations25, several improved HRNet variants have also been explored to enhance pose estimation performance under challenging visual conditions26. However its performance drops with distant or partially visible pedestrians in cluttered environments27. Recent attention-augmented variants28 and robust perception frameworks29 show promise, but their application to unstructured traffic environments remains underexplored. Pedestrian intention prediction in unstructured traffic environments introduces additional challenges beyond those addressed in existing works. In particular, visual cues such as pose and appearance often become unreliable due to frequent occlusions and small-scale pedestrian instances, while motion-based cues alone may be insufficient to capture complex behavioral intent. Conventional multimodal fusion approaches typically rely on static or globally learned weights, which fail to adapt when the reliability of individual modalities varies across scenarios. As a result, existing methods struggle to maintain consistent performance in dense and visually degraded environments. To effectively address these challenges, it is necessary to design a framework that can adapt to varying modality reliability, improve feature extraction for small-scale and occluded pedestrians, and generalize to unstructured traffic environments. This requires both adaptive fusion mechanisms30 and robust preprocessing strategies tailored to real-world conditions. To address these challenges, we propose a hybrid CNN–GCN framework with a dynamic modality gating mechanism that performs sample-dependent, reliability-aware fusion of visual (pose keypoints) and non-visual (motion dynamics) features. Unlike conventional fusion strategies with fixed or globally learned weights, the proposed gating module adaptively prioritizes modalities based on their reliability, particularly under occlusion and small-scale pedestrian conditions. In addition, we introduce a scale-aware HRNet-based preprocessing module that enhances keypoint extraction for distant and partially occluded pedestrians. By dynamically scaling pedestrian regions based on bounding box dimensions and applying contrast enhancement and noise reduction, the proposed approach improves the quality of pose features under challenging visual conditions. To further address the lack of generalizability in existing datasets, we curate a custom Indian Road Dataset (IRD) that captures dense, occluded, and unstructured traffic scenarios. This enables evaluation in realistic environments where existing models often fail.Experiments on both JAAD_all and the custom IRD dataset show that our modelachieves moderate performance while maintaining low computational latency, demonstrating its suitability for real-time deployment in intelligent transportation systems.

Related works

Pedestrian crossing intention prediction has been extensively studied using a variety of methods, from classical machine learning to advanced deep learning architectures. Temporal deep learning models, particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), dominate recent research due to their ability to capture temporal dependencies in pedestrian motion. For example, Ahmed et al.20 combined 2D pose estimation with LSTMs to predict intentions using spatio-temporal joint data, achieving 87% accuracy on JAAD. Lin et al.10 employed an Attention-LSTM model with panoptic segmentation to predict trajectories and intentions one second ahead, addressing dynamic interactions in traffic scenes. Sidharta et al.31 leveraged OpenPose-derived angular features with a Multi-Input-Single-Output (MISO) model to resolve crossing ambiguity in group pedestrian scenarios, attaining 93% accuracy. While effective in structured environments, these approaches often struggle with occlusions, distant pedestrians, and dense, unstructured traffic, which are common in Indian roads32.

Graph-based methods (GCNs) have been widely adopted to model spatial relationships and pedestrian interactions. Ling et al.12 proposed PedAST-GCN, integrating pose, bounding boxes, and vehicle speed, improving prediction accuracy over benchmarks. Recent works have also explored modeling multi-pedestrian interactions to improve crossing intention prediction in crowded environments33. Zhang et al. developed a spatial-temporal GCN using skeleton data alone, while Chen et al.11 combined graph autoencoders with LSTMs to encode pedestrian-centric environmental graphs, emphasizing scene semantics. These GCN approaches improve spatial reasoning but often rely on local features and static adjacency matrices, limiting robustness under occlusion or in crowded environments. Pose-based methods focus on extracting biomechanical and skeletal features. Zhang et al.34 analyzed joint angles and facial cues with Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Random Forests, achieving an AUC of 0.849 for red-light crossing prediction. Boda and Ramadevi35 integrated OpenPose with VGG-19 for real-time pose classification on PIE, attaining 92.5% accuracy. However, pose-based approaches are vulnerable when pedestrians are partially occluded, distant, or captured at low resolution.

Multi-modal fusion methods combine visual, motion, and contextual cues to enhance prediction robustness. Yang et al.36 fused CNN, RNN, and attention modules to integrate bounding boxes, pose, and semantic segmentation, improving prediction horizons to 2 s. Chen et al.37 proposed PedCMT, a cross-modal transformer combining bounding boxes and ego-vehicle speed, while Zhao et al.38 fused facial expressions with 2D skeletons using GCNs. Despite improved robustness, static fusion strategies often incur high computational cost and have limited adaptability under varying conditions. Lightweight architectures such as SqueezeNet-GRU have also been explored for real-time pedestrian intention prediction in computationally constrained environments33. However, lightweight models may experience reduced robustness under complex traffic conditions involving occlusions, dense pedestrian interactions, and small-scale targets. Classical machine learning approaches remain relevant in low-resource and interpretable settings. Moreno et al.39 applied Random Forests to drone-captured trajectories by achieving high accuracy of 98%, while Xin et al.40 used Gradient Boosting Decision Trees to model pedestrian behavior, outperforming logistic regression. These approaches, however, lack fine-grained temporal and spatial modeling capabilities.

Finally, datasets have been instrumental in standardizing evaluation. PIE8 integrates intention labels with ego-vehicle speed, while JAAD focuses on pedestrian-centric annotations. These benchmarks enable evaluation of models like PedAST-GCN12 and PIT14, but they mostly capture structured roads with moderate pedestrian density, leaving performance under unstructured traffic environments largely unexplored.

Recent advances have explored the use of large vision-language models to improve pedestrian intention prediction through enhanced contextual reasoning. VLMPed-CoT41 introduces a vision-language framework with a chain-of-thought (CoT) mechanism that integrates multimodal inputs and leverages structured reasoning to improve prediction accuracy and interpretability across datasets such as JAAD and PIE. While such approaches demonstrate strong performance, they typically involve complex architectures and multi-stage training strategies, which may limit their applicability in real-time and resource-constrained settings. In addition, recent survey works Y. Ling et al.42 provide a comprehensive overview highlighting that multimodal fusion of pedestrian behavior, motion dynamics, and environmental context is critical for robust prediction. The survey also identifies key open challenges, including generalization across datasets, handling behavioral ambiguity, and modeling uncertainty in complex real-world scenarios.

Existing multimodal fusion approaches in pedestrian intention prediction typically rely on static concatenation or globally learned weights to combine visual and contextual features, which has limited adaptability under varying visual conditions such as occlusion and scale variation43,44. Several recent works incorporate attention mechanisms or transformer-based architectures to improve feature interaction and adaptability12,14, including graph attention-based models such as Dual-STGAT45. While these approaches enhance representation learning, they often introduce increased computational complexity, making them less suitable for real-time and edge deployment scenarios. In contrast, the proposed approach focuses on sample-dependent, reliability-aware fusion, where the contribution of each modality is dynamically adjusted based on its quality for a given input. Unlike attention-based fusion, the proposed method adopts a lightweight gating mechanism, enabling adaptive modality selection while maintaining computational efficiency. This design is particularly suitable for pedestrian intention prediction in unstructured environments, where visual features are frequently degraded due to occlusion, small-scale pedestrians, and scene clutter.

Figure 1 represents the implemented architecture. The four modalities (Pose, Bounding box, Global context and Local context) are extracted and fed as input to the network. A scale aware HRNet has been introduced as a preprocessing method to extract the key points of distant pedestrians. These four modalities are further segregated as Visual and Non-visual modalities. To effectively extract the features from the visual modalities, a combination of EfficientNet with (Efficient Channel Attention + Convolutional Attention Module) ECA_CAM block is used which is explained in the visual features extraction section. STA-GCN network is used for extracting features from non-visual modalities. The extracted features are then passed through a temporal pooling layer for temporal aggregation, followed by a concatenation layer. The concatenated features are then processed by a dynamic modality gating module, which adaptively prioritizes visual and non-visual cues based on the scene context. This is followed by a weighted fusion of the extracted modalities. The fused features are passed through dense layers and a classification head to predict whether the pedestrian is crossing or not crossing.

A.IRD Dataset Data Collection and Annotation.

IRD Dataset Data Collection and Annotation.

The Indian Road Dataset (IRD) was curated to capture the unique challenges of pedestrian crossing intention prediction in unstructured Indian traffic. Data was collected in Trivandrum, Kerala, across a variety of road types within both the city and its outskirts, covering diverse local areas, major city zones, and suburban regions. A high-resolution 50MP OIS camera was mounted inside the ego-vehicle just below the rear-view mirror. Videos were recorded at 30 fps, with durations ranging from 15 s to 3 min 2 s, yielding approximately 68,429 frames in total. IRD encompasses a wide range of pedestrian activities such as crossing, waiting, walking along the curb, and sudden road entry. The dataset intentionally covers adverse conditions such as fog, rain, clear and evening weather as shown in Fig. 2 aimed at capturing the variability of real-world Indian driving environments rather than explicitly evaluating weather-specific model performance.

Annotation Protocol: All pedestrian instances were annotated using CVAT (Computer Vision Annotation Tool), with 324 unique pedestrians and over 56,234 bounding boxes labeled. Each pedestrian was assigned a unique identifier videoID_frameID_pedestrianID for consistent pedestrian tracking across frames. To capture occlusion challenges, a binary occlusion label is defined, where a value of 1 corresponds to severe occlusion (< 25% visibility), and 0 indicates partial or no occlusion (> 25% visibility). This definition is adopted to explicitly distinguish scenarios where visual cues are significantly degraded, enabling targeted analysis of model performance under limited visibility conditions. Bounding box annotations were aligned with HRNet-based key point extraction, providing a foundation for pose estimation, tracking, and multi-agent interaction studies, although these tasks were not explicitly benchmarked in this work. The annotation process follows a consistent labeling protocol to ensure uniformity across all samples. Each bounding box is manually inspected to maintain annotation quality, and temporal consistency is preserved through unique pedestrian identifiers across frames. All annotations are manually verified to ensure consistency and accuracy. While formal inter-annotator agreement metrics are not reported, the annotation process follows a consistent labeling protocol with careful quality control through visual inspection.

Dataset Partitioning and Usage: The IRD dataset is partitioned at the video level into 23 clips for training, 4 clips for validation, and 15 clips for testing to ensure that pedestrian instances from the same video do not appear across different splits. This prevents temporal data leakage and enables fair evaluation of model generalization.

Although the dataset size is relatively modest compared to large-scale benchmarks, it is specifically designed to capture complex real-world scenarios such as dense crowds, occlusion-heavy environments, and unpredictable pedestrian behavior.The limited scale of the dataset is acknowledged as a constraint, and future work will focus on expanding its size.

Dataset Availability and Usability : The dataset used in this study is publicly available for academic research purposes at Kaggle repository[https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ammanaveen/ird-dataset-pedestrian-intention-prediction/data]. The dataset is organized in a structured format with annotated video sequences, bounding boxes, and associated metadata, to facilitate reproducibility and ease of use for tasks such as pedestrian intention prediction, tracking, and behavior analysis.

The IRD dataset captures a diverse distribution of pedestrian behaviors and scene characteristics relevant to unstructured traffic environments. The dataset consists of 42 video clips and 68,429 frames, containing 324 unique pedestrians and 56,234 annotated bounding boxes. On average, this corresponds to approximately 1,630 frames per video and 173 bounding box annotations per pedestrian, indicating dense temporal annotation. The large number of bounding box annotations relative to the number of frames reflects frequent pedestrian presence and dense scene interactions. The inclusion of occlusion labels indicates the presence of severe occlusions, while the distribution of pedestrians across diverse scenes suggests variability in pedestrian scale, including small-scale and distant instances. These factors introduce significant variation in visual appearance and motion patterns, making the dataset suitable for evaluating model performance under real-world conditions.

B.Scale- Aware HRNet-Based Preprocessing Module for Pose Keypoint Extraction.

B.Scale- Aware HRNet-Based Preprocessing Module for Pose Keypoint Extraction.

Scale- Aware HRNet-Based Preprocessing Module for Pose Keypoint Extraction.

HRNet is preferred for keypoint extraction due to its ability to maintain high-resolution representations across the entire network, in contrast to conventional architectures that lose spatial detail through progressive downsampling. Given that these keypoints serve as critical input features for pedestrian intention prediction, any localization error will propagate through the entire pipeline. To extract pedestrian pose keypoints, HRNet46 requires a fixed input resolution of 512 × 512 pixels and performs optimally when the pedestrian occupies a sufficiently large region within the image (100 × 70 pixels). However, in real-world scenarios, particularly in dense Indian traffic, pedestrians often appear small or partially occluded, making accurate keypoint detection challenging for HRNet.

To address this limitation, a scale-aware preprocessing module is introduced, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The preprocessing pipeline operates as follows: First, the bounding box dimensions of each pedestrian are computed. If the pedestrian size meets the minimum threshold(100 × 70 pixels), the pedestrian region is directly cropped and passed to the HRNet model. For smaller pedestrians, a dynamic scaling strategy is applied. The pedestrian is cropped based on bounding box coordinates, and the cropped region is dynamically enlarged using a scaling factor formula given in Eq. (5). Overview of the proposed scale-aware preprocessing framework integrated with HRNet-based keypoint estimation.

After calculating the crop resize ratio using the bounding box dimensions, the cropped pedestrian image is resized accordingly and subjected to a series of image processing techniques to enhance key point detection for distant and occluded pedestrians. First, the image is converted from BGR (Blue-Green-Red) to LAB (L represents Lightness, A represents the green–red color component and B represent the blue–yellow color component)) color space to enable more effective contrast manipulation. Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) is then applied on the L channel to improve visibility in poorly lit conditions while avoiding over-amplification of noise. The image is subsequently converted back from LAB to BGR format to maintain compatibility with standard processing pipelines. To further improve image quality, Gaussian blur is applied to reduce noise and smooth small artifacts. Finally, the image is resized while preserving the original aspect ratio, and padding is applied as necessary to match the input size expected by the scale-aware HRNet model (typically 512 × 512 pixels), ensuring that the human figure remains undistorted. The resulting image whether it comes from this enhanced preprocessing method or from a direct masked background crop of sufficiently large pedestrians is then passed to the HRNet module. This ensures accurate key point extraction, even in challenging scenarios involving distant and partially occluded individuals (Fig. 4).

The output of the ECA is further refined through a combination of channel and spatial attention, where channel attention determines the most informative feature channels, while spatial attention highlights the regions within the feature map that the network should prioritize. In channel-wise attention, to obtain a second channel mask, the average-pooled and max-pooled channel descriptors are summed, followed by a sigmoid activation. Multiplying this mask with the feature map emphasizes the most relevant feature channels. On the refined-channel tensor, the channel axis collapses using both max and average pooling to produce two (H, W, 1) maps, where H and W represent the height and width, respectively. These maps are then concatenated and passed through a 7 × 7 depth-wise separable convolution followed by a sigmoid activation. This generates a spatial attention map that indicates the regions of the feature map to which the network should concentrate (e.g., pedestrian areas versus background). The final attended feature is obtained by multiplying this attention map with the input feature map. This sequential use of the ECA_CAM block adaptively re-weights both the most informative channels and the most critical spatial locations. This double attention mechanism preserves richer mid‑level structures (e.g., pose landmarks and small context cues) and suppresses background noise before the collapsing to a 1D feature vector.

E. Dynamic Modality Gating Module:

E. Dynamic Modality Gating Module:

Dynamic Modality Gating Module:

The proposed dynamic modality gating module performs sample-dependent, reliability-aware fusion of visual and non-visual modalities. Unlike conventional fusion strategies that rely on static concatenation or globally learned weights, the proposed approach dynamically estimates the relative importance of each modality for every input sample.

In pedestrian intention prediction, particularly in unstructured traffic environments, the reliability of visual cues (e.g., pose and appearance) vary significantly due to occlusion, small-scale pedestrians, and cluttered backgrounds. In contrast, motion-based cues may remain informative in such scenarios. Therefore, a static fusion strategy leads to suboptimal performance.To address this, the proposed gating mechanism learns a context-aware weighting function that adaptively prioritizes modalities based on their reliability. This enables the model to emphasize motion features when visual information is degraded, and prioritize visual cues when reliable pose information is available.

Experiments and results

Implementation and training details

The proposed framework is evaluated with custom made datasets and with the publicly available benchmark dataset JAAD. JAAD includes 346 video clips totaling 240 h of footage captured at 30 frames per second. These clips, spanning 5 to 15 s each, feature resolutions of 1920 × 1080 and 1280 × 720 pixels. The dataset is divided into two subsets. JAAD_beh documents 495 instances of pedestrians actively crossing roads and 191 cases of individuals preparing to cross. JAAD_all expands the scope by incorporating 2,100 samples of pedestrians engaged in non-crossing activities. JAAD_beh is a subset of JAAD_all containing only crossing and preparatory actions, whereas JAAD_all additionally includes non-crossing instances. Although both JAAD_beh and JAAD_all subsets are mentioned for completeness, all experiments in this work are conducted on the JAAD_all subset to ensure consistency with prior studies and comprehensive evaluation across both crossing and non-crossing scenarios. The dataset is partitioned following the standard benchmark protocol proposed by Kotseruba et al.44, which is widely adopted in pedestrian intention prediction studies. Specifically, the dataset is split at the video level into 177 training, 29 validation, and 117 testing clips (total 323 clips), ensuring that pedestrian instances from the same video do not appear across different splits, thereby preventing data leakage. As defined in44, a subset of 23 videos is excluded due to lower resolution and adverse conditions such as poor illumination. As 23 clips are excluded out of total 346 clips of JAAD_all, 323 clips are used for experimental evaluation ensuring consistency with the standard benchmark protocol.Input Configuration and Temporal Modeling: The model operates on fixed-length temporal sequences extracted from video frames. Each input sample consists of an observation sequence of 15 consecutive frames, sampled at the original frame rate (30 fps) without temporal subsampling, thereby preserving temporal continuity.The prediction task is formulated using a Time-To-Event (TTE) framework, with a prediction horizon of 60 frames (~ 2 s). This configuration is selected based on empirical analysis (Sect. 4.3.3), balancing sufficient temporal context with prediction reliability. A sample is labeled as positive if the pedestrian initiates crossing behavior within the prediction horizon, and negative otherwise. This formulation enables early anticipation of pedestrian intention based on observed temporal cues.

The present study was carried out on an experimental platform with Nvidia RTX 4060 graphics card and Intel I9 CPU. The proposed model was trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 8, optimized using the AdamW optimizer and binary cross-entropy loss function, with a maximum learning rate of 0.00001. The evaluation metrics used are Precision, Recall, Accuracy, F1-score, and AUC.

Latency Evaluation: Inference latency is measured on Intel i9 CPU equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU using a batch size of 1. The reported latency corresponds to the inference time of the proposed fusion network only and does not include feature extraction (HRNet-based keypoint estimation). All measurements are conducted under identical hardware and software conditions to ensure fair comparison across methods.

On the custom IRD dataset, the proposed model achieves an accuracy of 0.82 and an AUC of 0.72 as shown in Table 1. However, a detailed analysis of evaluation metrics reveals important trade-offs. While the accuracy is relatively high, the precision (0.39) and F1-score (0.56) indicate the presence of false positives, particularly in complex traffic scenarios where pedestrian behavior is ambiguous. This behavior can be attributed to the inherent difficulty of distinguishing between crossing intent and non-crossing preparatory actions in unstructured environments. Pedestrians often exhibit transitional behaviors such as standing near the curb, slight body orientation changes, or intermittent motion, which are difficult to categorize clearly. Additionally, frequent occlusions, small-scale pedestrian appearance, and interactions with surrounding vehicles further degrade the reliability of visual cues, leading to both false positives and false negatives.

The model tends to maintain moderate recall (0.50), capturing a portion of true crossing events, while still missing some due to limited discriminative cues in challenging scenarios. From a safety perspective, this trade-off is important, as missing a true crossing event (false negative) is more critical than generating a false alert. However, improving precision and recall remains an important direction for future work. The dataset also exhibits class imbalance between crossing and non-crossing samples, which can bias accuracy toward the dominant class. In such scenarios, high accuracy may be achieved by favoring non-crossing predictions while failing to correctly detect crossing events. Therefore, greater emphasis is placed on precision, recall, and F1-score to provide a more comprehensive and reliable evaluation particularly in safety-critical pedestrian intention prediction task.

On the JAAD_all dataset as shown in Table 2, the model achieves an accuracy of 0.88 and an AUC of 0.82. To gain deeper insight into the behavior of the proposed dynamic modality gating mechanism, we analyze how modality contributions vary under different conditions, specifically occlusion versus non-occlusion and near versus far pedestrians.

Failure case analysis

Despite the observed improvements, the proposed model exhibits certain limitations under challenging real-world conditions.False positives are primarily observed in scenarios where pedestrians exhibit ambiguous motion patterns, such as standing near the curb, slight body orientation changes, or intermittent movements without clear crossing intent. In such cases, the model incorrectly predict crossing behavior due to similarities with pre-crossing motion patterns, which closely resembles actual crossing intent as shown in Fig. 15 (a) and(b). False negatives occur in situations involving severe occlusion, sudden crossing behavior with limited prior motion cues. In these scenarios, the model fails to capture sufficient temporal or visual evidence for accurate prediction. Notably, two types of false negatives are observed: (i) where the pedestrian is correctly detected but misclassified due to incomplete motion cues as shown in Fig. 15(c) and (ii) where the pedestrian is poorly localized, leading to the absence of reliable pose features as shown in Fig. 15(d).

From an error analysis perspective, most errors arise in cases where the distinction between waiting and crossing intent is subtle, particularly in dense and unstructured traffic environments. This contributes to the relatively lower precision observed in the results.These observations highlight the inherent difficulty of pedestrian intention prediction in real-world environments and provide a clear explanation for the observed limitations. They also indicate potential directions for improvement, such as incorporating richer temporal context and more discriminative behavioral cues to better handle such edge cases.

Conclusion and future works

Pedestrian crossing intention prediction remains a critical challenge for autonomous driving, particularly in unstructured, high-density, and frequently occluded road environments such as those in India. Existing models face three key limitations such as brittle static feature fusion, insufficient representative datasets, and degraded pose estimation for distant pedestrians. To address these issues, we propose a hybrid CNN-GCN architecture with a dynamic modality gating module that enables sample-dependent fusion of visual and non-visual features. This design allows the model to adaptively prioritize reliable cues under varying visual conditions. To improve feature quality under challenging scenarios, a scale-aware preprocessing strategy integrated with HRNet was introduced for enhanced keypoint extraction, particularly for distant and partially occluded pedestrians. Additionally, the Indian Road Dataset (IRD) was collected to capture the complexities of unstructured Indian roads. The proposed method was evaluated on JAAD_all, IRD, and in a cross-dataset setting on PIE. The results indicate competitive performance across datasets, while additional analyses under occlusion and pedestrian scale variations provide insights into model behavior under degraded visual conditions. Furthermore, edge-device evaluation on Jetson Xavier NX demonstrates near real-time performance, highlighting the practical feasibility of the approach in resource-constrained environments. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves moderate accuracy on JAAD (88%) and better performance on IRD (82%), while maintaining the lower inference latency (1.5 ms), highlighting its suitability for real-time deployment on edge devices in autonomous driving systems. Despite these improvements, certain limitations remain. The model exhibits reduced precision in scenarios involving ambiguous pre-crossing behaviors and heavy occlusion, reflecting the inherent difficulty of distinguishing subtle pedestrian intent in unstructured settings which leads to a lower precision. Future work will focus on incorporating richer temporal modeling and behavioral cues to better capture subtle intent transitions. Exploring class balancing strategies such as re-weighting and resampling to further improve precision and overall performance is also a potential direction. Extending the model to capture group dynamics and social interactions among multiple pedestrians to improve prediction in crowded environments is a promising research direction. Integrating intention prediction with trajectory forecasting could provide a unified framework for anticipating pedestrian behavior more accurately.
