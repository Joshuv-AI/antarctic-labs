Resilient fractional order sliding mode control for islanded microgrids under cyber-physical attacks
Authors: Mohamad Issa Ibraheem, Mehdi Edrisi, Hassan Haes Alhelou, Mehdi Gholipour, Amer Al-Hinai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) in power system applications, such as Load Frequency Control (LFC), increases the vulnerability to cyber-attacks. Among these, False Data Injection Attacks (FDIA) targeting sensor-controller or controller-actuator signals can destabilize systems with varying loads and renewable energy sources. This paper proposes a Fractional Order Sliding Mode Controller (FOSMC) to ensure frequency stability and resilience in an islanded system under controller-actuator FDIA and external disturbances. An observer is incorporated to estimate system states and detect FDIA signals, with its parameters obtained via Linear Matrix Inequality (LMI) techniques. A fractional order sliding surface is designed to enhance robustness, and system stability is analyzed using the Lyapunov method. The proposed controller is validated through a Model-in-the-Loop (MiL) OPAL-RT setup. Experimental results confirm that the resilient controller maintains stability under different FDIA scenarios and outperforms existing methods in dynamic response.

Introduction

Nations everywhere are moving from traditional to clean energy resources due to fears of climate change caused by fossil fuel combustion. This has witnessed an increase in the use of renewables in modern microgrids, though their intermittent nature creates power balancing challenges1. Centralized controllers utilizing Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) alleviate these issues by receiving sensor signals and providing control commands to actuators. But ICT integration brings cyber-attack vulnerability, with security and stability responsibilities2. Cyber-attacks such as Denial-of-Service (DoS) and Time Delay Attack (TDA) disrupt communication and service provision, while False Data Injection Attacks (FDIA) manipulate control signals, causing frequency deviations and equipment failures3,4. Although many research works focus on sensor-side FDIAs, attacks between the controller and actuators can be equally, if not more, destructive, as they involve manipulating control actions that affect the system’s dynamics in real time. These attacks evade conventional monitoring systems that concentrate on data acquisition layers5. The disturbing incidents of Iranian nuclear program disruption and blackout in Ukraine provide examples of the destructive socioeconomic effects of such attacks6,7. Hence, developing resilient control strategies against controller–actuator FDIAs is crucial to protecting critical energy infrastructure.

The integration of ICT into Load Frequency Control (LFC) systems has spurred extensive research on cyber–attack scenarios. Attack diagnosis involves three major tasks: detection, isolation, and estimation. Detection is the initial step in determining whether the system is operating normally8,9. Then, detection and isolation of the attack involve removing the attacker’s influence from the sensors or actuators within the system; in this case, identifying the attack location is necessary10,11. Even though many strategies have been proposed to detect and isolate FDIAs in power systems, including data–driven approaches such as reinforcement learning12 and unsupervised learning13, to name a few, detection and isolation alone may not provide comprehensive information about the attack, such as its magnitude, location, and nature. This information can be obtained through attack estimation, which plays a crucial role in identifying and mitigating attackers to maintain the stability and security of the closed–loop power system14.

Researchers have become increasingly interested in developing additional strategies to maintain power system stability and security. In15, a robust controller based on a super-twisting single-phase sliding-mode approach was developed for a multi-area power system with high penetration of renewable energy sources. Although the proposed method improves disturbance rejection and mitigates chattering, it primarily focuses on maintaining frequency stability under disturbances and uncertainties, rather than cyber-attacks. In16, a Fractional Order Sliding Mode Controller (FOSMC) integrated with a nonlinear disturbance observer was proposed to estimate nonlinear external disturbances in a hybrid power system. The observer was also evaluated under FDIAs to verify its effectiveness. However, the controller relies on system states that may not always be available in practice. In addition, several studies have investigated the stability and stabilization of LFC systems under various time delays induced by cyber attacks17–19. Where a modified super-twisting Sliding Mode Control (SMC) with a delay-resilient sliding surface is introduced in20. The strategy was developed to enhance robustness against time-varying communication delays and external disturbances. In addition, a Kalman filter-based Integral Sliding Mode Control (ISMC) for power systems in a multi-area context under delay is proposed in21. The strategy integrates the observer and controller to enhance disturbance rejection and minimize the chattering effect. However, even previous studies that utilize integration between the sliding surface and observer aim to improve system stability under disturbance and delay, but they still assume that delay attacks occur on the sensor side. In power systems, ICT is also employed to transmit data between the sensor and the actuator, and any attack on this communication channel can also compromise system stability.

The researcher endeavored to improve the stability and security of the power system in the face of potential threats against the actuator side. Syrmakesis et al. in22 introduce a Sliding Mode Observer (SMO) to detect the FDIA threat on the LFC and identify which parts of the control loop have been compromised. The attack is assumed to happen on both sides of the system (sensor and actuator sides). Moreover, in23, the distributed observer-based event-triggered method has been employed for multi-area LFC systems to detect and isolate the FDIA from the actuator side. Even though the previous studies have succeeded in maintaining system stability and security, the estimation of the attack signal still provides more information about the nature of the attack.

Estimating the threat attack signal can be considered part of a high–level controller; the information derived from the estimated attack signal, such as magnitude, location, and nature, becomes valuable. The researcher aims to identify observers and methodologies to accomplish this objective. For instance, SMO-based control strategies have been investigated in24 to enhance robustness in microgrid environments. In this context, a multi-stage SMC combined with an optimal state estimator was proposed for load-frequency regulation in hybrid-source power systems; however, the study primarily focused on system-state estimation. In another work, Hu et al. in25 introduced an observer designed to estimate false data injection attacks on the actuator side and address the issue of unavailable system states. Then, a resilient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{\infty }$$\end{document} LFC scheme was developed to suppress the DoS and FDIA threats. Even though the controller mitigates the attack and the attack signal is estimated, it is still necessary to test different types of attacks, such as ramp, constant, and variable–increase–time signal attacks, to demonstrate the ability of the observer and controller under this variety of attack types. Similarly, the study in26 proposed two observers, a SMO and an Unknown Input Observer (UIO), to estimate both system states and FDIA signals. However, the use of multiple observers increases system complexity and overall implementation cost, which may limit practical deployment.

Xu et al. i, in27, propose an intermediate–estimator–based approach capable of estimating unbounded FDIA and providing an effective compensation mechanism for multi–area LFC systems. Khalghani et al. in28 introduce the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) controller to maintain stability and security within the LFC system under threats posed by actuator FDIA. The study employs a UIO to estimate potential attack threat signals expected at the system’s input. However, to further enhance the stability and security of LFC systems, it remains important to investigate faster and more modern control strategies.

While many studies have been conducted on cyber-attack mitigation in LFC schemes, many current techniques primarily address attacks on sensors and disruptions to communication channels; at the same time, the attack on the actuator side has not received close. In addition, most strategies for this issue focus on attack detection and suppression rather than estimating the signal attack, and a complete investigation of the nature of an attack. On top of that, several observers and controllers require the availability of certain system states while utilizing simplified attack assumptions, which might prove ineffective against a wide range of complex attacks on controllers and actuators. Overall, current developments have been limited in proposing unified resilient control schemes with estimation of the cyber signal able to maintain the required stability and security of the control process, along with attack signal estimation.

From previous limitations, this paper presents a framework for resilient control and estimation of controller–actuator FDIA signals in isolated microgrids. The proposed method integrates an SMO with FOSMC to simultaneously estimate system states, reconstruct the cyber-attack signal, and maintain frequency stability under different attack scenarios and external disturbances. Where the estimation states are utilized in the controller to reduce dependence on measurement signals. The observer parameters are obtained through LMI-based design; on the other hand, the controller improves the response and mitigates the attack. In addition, the proposed strategy is experimentally validated using a Model-in-the-Loop (MiL) OPAL-RT platform under multiple FDIA patterns, including varying and time-dependent attacks. Dynamic model for LFC model under controller-actuator FDIA

This section describes the dynamic model of LFC under a controller-actuator FDIA. The system, as depicted in Fig. 1, comprises diesel generator sources, an electric vehicle source, solar panels as renewable sources, and a battery for energy storage. Both the diesel generator and the electric vehicle source are controllable units in the system, which means that when the demand power and generator power are not equal, the controller will send feedback to both sources to change their states to satisfy the new conditions in the system by increasing or decreasing the input power. The controller sends a signal to the governor in the diesel generator to adjust the mechanical torque, while the control signal for electric vehicle sources manages power flow between its batteries and the grid. In this model, the aggregated EV source is represented as a fast-responding storage unit that supports frequency regulation, as commonly adopted in the literature29,30. Detailed fast inner-loop dynamics, such as Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) and State-Of-Charge (SOC) charging/discharging, are not explicitly modeled because this does not compromise the validity of the proposed controller design.Figure 1The schematic diagram of islanded microgrid with FDIA on controller signals.

The schematic diagram of islanded microgrid with FDIA on controller signals.

On the other hand, the power generated by Photovoltaics (PV) is significantly affected by solar irradiation, which is uncertain due to continuous changes in weather conditions, as well as the power load variation according to customer demand. These sources have a significant impact on the system stability, which cannot be neglected. Consequently, both the load and power of the PV are considered as disturbances in the system. The relationship between the input and output of the previous system source is nonlinear. However, the linear one can be found in31.

It is necessary to note the difference between the disturbance d(t) and the FDIA signal ϑ (t). Although both signals are modeled as unknown inputs, they have completely different characteristics in terms of physical reality and effects on system dynamics. The disturbance d(t) models a real physical change in the system, such as load dynamics or uncertainties in renewable energy sources. Thus, when the controller responds to the disturbance d(t), the control action represents a real change in the system, and the system stability is maintained through proper control design. On the other hand, the FDIA signal ϑ (t) models a maliciously injected false measurement or control signal. It does not model any real physical change in the system. Thus, if the controller or actuator responds to the FDIA signal ϑ (t) as if it were a real signal, the control action may take the system away from its real operating point, potentially leading to system instability or damage.Figure 2Dynamic model of LFC system with controller-actuator FDIA in a linearized framework.

Fractional order sliding mode controller

In this section, a design method for FOSMC is introduced to address challenges such as unknown external disturbances and controller-actuator FDIAs. The FOSMC provides greater robustness against uncertainties in the system and improves control precision as a result of the fractional order that gives it a degree of freedom.

Figure 3 visually presents the proposed secondary controller method, which consists of an observer and a fractional sliding mode control. According to the figure, the outputs of the system are used in the observer to estimate the system states and the probable FDIA signal. Further, the estimated states, the FDIA signal, and the measured output signals are used by the controller to maintain the system’s stability and security. It is worth noting that in many systems, the system states are often inaccessible, which underscores the value of our approach, which uses the estimation signal in the controller.

Conclusion

This study examined the effect of FDIAs on controller–actuator signals in an islanded microgrid using a MiL test on the OPAL-RT simulator. Results showed that controllers lacking protection against cyberattacks can lead to instability. The proposed strategy achieved faster response and superior performance compared with LQR, PI,and Sliding mode controllers. Under external disturbances and various FDIAs, the proposed method achieved maximum overshoot and undershoot of 6.6e-4 and -2.1e-3, respectively, both lower than those of the other methods. The cooperation between the proposed controller and observer effectively reduced dependence on original system states and provided accurate information about the attack’s magnitude and nature. The results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed FOSMC-based resilient control strategy in enhancing both stability and cybersecurity performance of the islanded microgrid under different attack scenarios. Future work will extend the proposed framework to multi-area interconnected microgrids and more complex coordinated cyber-attack scenarios. Additionally, future work can implement more real-time investigation, such as hardware-in-the-loop (HiL) simulation, to account for practical uncertainties, including communication delays and parameter variations.
