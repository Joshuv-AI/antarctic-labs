The Geometry of Polycons and a Counterexample to Wachspress’ Conjecture Thanks: The author has been partly supported by the DFG grant 502861109.
Clemens Brüser

Abstract
. Polycons, initially introduced by Wachspress in 1975 as a tool in finite element methods, are generalizations of polygons in that they allow conic boundary components. We are interested in the adjoint curve of a given polycon, i.e. the unique curve of minimal degree vanishing in the so-called residual arrangement. It was conjectured by Wachspress that under some regularity assumptions this curve does not vanish in the interior of its defining polycon. However, until recently the only class of polycons for which this was proven were convex polygons. We present a polycon bounded by three conics that constitutes a counterexample to Wachspress’ conjecture. The origin of this counterexample reveals some beautiful geometry of polycons. Replacing one degree two boundary component of a polycon with a line produces a new polycon. We show that the adjoint of the latter is a contact curve to the adjoint of the former. This naturally leads to the consideration of symmetric linear determinantal representations of adjoints, which lets us explicitly describe the fibers of the adjoint map in the case of polycons bounded by three conics. As a corollary we prove that generically the adjoint of a polycon bounded by three conics is smooth.

1.
Introduction

Polycons
are a generalization of polygons originally introduced by
[
17
]
(reprinted in
[
19
, Part I]
) as a tool in finite element methods. Contrary to polygons, a polycon allows for boundary components that are segments of conics.

For a polycon the set of singular points of its (algebraic) boundary that are not vertices is called the
residual arrangement
, and under some regularity conditions it has been shown that there exists a unique curve of minimal and predetermined degree passing through the residual arrangement, called the
adjoint
curve.

One of the main questions in the study of adjoints is whether there exist points in the interior of a real polycon, in which the adjoint vanishes. This problem is commonly referred to as
Wachspress’ Conjecture
and was open even in one of its simplest instances: for polycons bounded by three conics.

Attention has also recently been drawn to the study of
adjoint maps
, which assign to a (potentially complex) polycon with boundary components of fixed degree its adjoint (
[
1
]
and
[
12
, §4]
). While these papers focus on finite adjoint maps, we are interested in continuous parametrizations of polycons giving rise to the same adjoint.

2.
Polycons, Adjoints, and Wachspress’ Conjecture

2.1.
Polycons and their Adjoints

We begin by introducing the notion of a polycon. We take an approach that differs slightly from recent work by
[
12
]
in that we restrict considerations to boundary components of degree at most two. At the same time this allows us to take a less restrictive approach to the regularity of the boundary components. For reference, the content of this section relies mainly on
[
19
]
and
[
12
]
.

As a standing assumption we assume that in any family of objects
O_{12},O_{23},\dots,O_{n1}
the labels are cyclically repeating, i.e. we set
O_{n,n+1}=O_{n1}
. We make an analogous convention for objects labeled just by
1,\dots,n
, i.e.
O_{n+1}=O_{1}
.

Definition 2.1

(Polycon)
.

In the following assume
n\geq 2
. A
polycon

{\mathcal{P}}
consists of

(1)

a tuple
(C_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of reduced curves of degree one or two, called
boundary components
,

(2)

a tuple
(v_{i,i+1})_{i=1}^{n}
of points in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, called
explicit vertices
, such that
C_{i}
and
C_{i+1}
intersect transversally in
v_{i,i+1}
and no other boundary component contains
v_{i,i+1}
.

A polycon is called
real
, if all input data is real, and additionally

(1)

there is a choice of segments
(\sigma(C_{i}))_{i=1}^{n}
on the boundary components with end points
v_{i-1,i}
and
v_{i,i+1}
, called
sides
,

(2)

there exists a closed semi-algebraic set
{\mathcal{P}}_{\geq 0}
, the interior of which is a union of simply connected sets, and whose boundary is the union of the sides of
{\mathcal{P}}
.

If a boundary component
C_{i}
is a singular conic, then we call its unique singular point an
implicit vertex
of
{\mathcal{P}}
. We denote the set of all vertices of
{\mathcal{P}}
by
V({\mathcal{P}})
. The curve
C_{\mathcal{P}}:=\bigcup_{i}C_{i}
is called the
algebraic boundary
of
{\mathcal{P}}
and we call
\deg({\mathcal{P}}):=\deg(C_{\mathcal{P}})
the
degree
of
{\mathcal{P}}
. If working with real polycons, then the union
\partial{\mathcal{P}}:=\bigcup_{i}\sigma(C_{i})
of all sides of
{\mathcal{P}}
is called the
Euclidean
boundary of
{\mathcal{P}}
. Finally, we call the open semi-algebraic set
{\mathcal{P}}_{>0}:={\mathcal{P}}_{\geq 0}\setminus\partial{\mathcal{P}}
the
interior
of
{\mathcal{P}}
.

The constellation of vertices and boundary components of a polycon can display pathological behaviour. The absence of these peculiarities leads to the notions of nodal and regular polycons (see
Figure

1
).

Definition 2.2

(Nodal and regular polycons)
.

A polycon
{\mathcal{P}}
is called
nodal
, if all singularities of its algebraic boundary
C_{\mathcal{P}}
are nodes. A real polycon is called
regular
, if the only singular points of
C_{\mathcal{P}}
on
\partial{\mathcal{P}}
are the vertices of
{\mathcal{P}}
and
C_{\mathcal{P}}
does not intersect
{\mathcal{P}}_{>0}
.

Definition 2.3

(Residual Arrangement)
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a polycon. The set
R({\mathcal{P}}):=\operatorname{sing}(C_{\mathcal{P}})\setminus V({\mathcal{P}})
is called the
residual arrangement
of
{\mathcal{P}}
.

The adjoint curve of
{\mathcal{P}}
aims to be the (unique) curve of minimal degree vanishing in
R({\mathcal{P}})
. If
{\mathcal{P}}
is nodal, then this may be taken as its definition (see
Figure

2
for an illustration). However, we do not want to impose this restriction. In order to still obtain a unique object, one has to impose additional vanishing conditions on the adjoint curve in the residual arrangement.

For this, one considers the desingularization of the algebraic boundary
C_{\mathcal{P}}
in the residual arrangement. This yields a morphism
\nu\colon Z\to C_{\mathcal{P}}
, where
Z
is the strict transform of
C_{\mathcal{P}}
under a sequence of blow-ups of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. By the ideal sheaf
{\mathcal{C}}_{\nu}
on
C_{\mathcal{P}}
we denote the conductor associated with the morphism
\nu
.

Definition 2.4

(Adjoint)
.

An
adjoint polynomial

\alpha_{\mathcal{P}}
of a degree
d
polycon
{\mathcal{P}}
is a global section of the sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(d-3)\otimes\iota_{*}{\mathcal{C}}_{\nu}
where
\iota\colon C_{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
is the inclusion morphism. The vanishing locus
A_{\mathcal{P}}
of
\alpha_{\mathcal{P}}
is called an
adjoint curve
of
{\mathcal{P}}
.

Remark 2.5
.

The conditions imposed on an adjoint curve
A_{\mathcal{P}}
of
{\mathcal{P}}
by the conductor
{\mathcal{C}}_{\nu}
can be translated into explicit vanishing conditions, which must be satisfied by
A_{\mathcal{P}}
in the residual arrangement
R({\mathcal{P}})
(
[
19
, §5.1]
). These can also be motivated by considering limits of nodal polycons. We give the two most common examples:

(1)

two boundary components tangent in a residual point impose that the adjoint’s tangent direction in this point is the same as well.

(2)

if at least three boundary components meet in a residual point, then the adjoint vanishes with multiplicity two in this point.

One can furthermore relax the condition that each vertex of
{\mathcal{P}}
is only contained in only two boundary components, which intersect transversally. Again, two common examples are given below (see also
[
19
, Example 5.4]
):

(1)

if
C_{i}
and
C_{i+1}
are tangent in
v_{i,i+1}
, then the adjoint vanishes in
v_{i,i+1}
.

(2)

if there exists
j\neq i,i+1
with
v_{i,i+1}\in C_{j}
, and if any two of the curves containing
v_{i,i+1}
intersect transversally in this vertex, then the adjoint vanishes in
v_{i,i+1}
with tangent direction equal to that of
C_{j}
.

Proposition 2.6

(
[
12
, Proposition 2.2]
)
.

For every polycon
{\mathcal{P}}
there exists a unique adjoint curve
A_{\mathcal{P}}
. Furthermore
A_{\mathcal{P}}
and the algebraic boundary
C_{\mathcal{P}}
have no irreducible components in common.

2.2.
Wachspress’ Conjecture

Since the inception of the study of adjoints an open problem had been Wachspress’ Conjecture, which is a statement on the real topology of the adjoint curve relative to the interior of its defining real polycon.

Conjecture 2.8

(Wachspress’ Conjecture,
[
18
, p. 396]
,
[
12
, Conjecture 3.4]
)
.

If
{\mathcal{P}}
is a regular real polycon with adjoint curve
A
, then
A({\mathbb{R}})
does not intersect
{\mathcal{P}}_{\geq 0}
.

Since its formulation, progress on this conjecture had been sparse. It had been answered in the positive for convex polygons in
[
19
, p. 96, Example 5.1]
, and a complete topological description of the adjoint has in this case been given in
[
12
, §3.2]
. Furthermore, it is known that the adjoint does not intersect the boundary
\partial{\mathcal{P}}
of
{\mathcal{P}}
. The following lemma is a special case of
[
12
, Lemma 3.5]
.

Lemma 2.9

(
[
12
, Lemma 3.5]
)
.

If
{\mathcal{P}}
is a nodal polycon, then
A_{\mathcal{P}}
intersects
C_{\mathcal{P}}
exactly in
R({\mathcal{P}})
, and the intersection multiplicity in each residual point equals 1. In particular
A
is smooth in
R({\mathcal{P}})
, and if
{\mathcal{P}}
is regular, then
A
does not intersect
\partial{\mathcal{P}}
.

Already, if
{\mathcal{P}}
is bounded by three conics, then the answer to
2.8
was open and only partial results could be established (
[
21
]
,
[
12
, §3.3]
). The authors of
[
12
]
enumerate all (up to equivalence) possible arrangements of three ellipses bounding a real polycon of degree six. Then they prove on a case-by-case basis that in most cases Wachspress’ conjecture holds. The cases, for which they do not present a proof, essentially boil down to the unpredictability whether the adjoint curve has an oval, and where this oval might lie relative to the interior of its defining polycon.

Example 2.10

(Counterexample to
2.8
)
.

Consider the real polycon with vertices

\displaystyle v_{12}=(9,-6),

\displaystyle v_{23}=(0,0),

\displaystyle v_{31}=(-3,-6)

and boundary components being the zero sets of the polynomials

\begin{split}c_{1}(x,y)&=20x^{2}+27y^{2}-120x+108y-864,\\
c_{2}(x,y)&=80x^{2}+102xy+57y^{2}-400x-96y,\\
c_{3}(x,y)&=32x^{2}-26xy+9y^{2}-96x+72y.\end{split}

Here, we work in a fixed affine chart. The adjoint of this polycon is the zero set of the polynomial

\begin{split}\alpha(x,y)&=3440x^{3}-8400x^{2}y-762xy^{2}+1971y^{3}\\
&+20720x^{2}+51168xy-1620y^{2}-193248x-96336y+342144.\end{split}

In

Theorem 2.11
.

Wachspress’ Conjecture is false.

Proof.

Take the data from
2.10
. We need to formally prove that the described polycon
{\mathcal{P}}
is regular and that its adjoint vanishes in a point in

. To facilitate computations the aid of the computer algebra system

(

. Whenever

and
j

2.3.
Adjoint Maps

Underlying the genesis of
2.10
is a good understanding of the set of polycons giving rise to the same adjoint. We are thus looking at the fibers of the so-called
adjoint map
, which we define below. Note that due to our restricted setting (having boundary components of degree at most two), our definition is slightly more general than that given in
[
12
, §4]
. For
1\leq d\leq 2
we denote by
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
the variety of plane curves of degree
d
. Now for a tuple of numbers
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
with
1\leq d_{i}\leq 2
, let

Y_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}^{\circ}\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{d_{1}}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{C}}_{d_{n}}\times(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{n}

denote the set consisting of all tuples
(C_{1},\dots,C_{n},v_{12},\dots,v_{n1})
such that

(1)

C_{i}
and
C_{j}
intersect transversally for
i\neq j
,

(2)

v_{i,i+1}\in C_{i}\cap C_{i+1}
,

(3)

v_{i,i+1}\not\in C_{j}
for
j\not\in\{i,i+1\}
.

The first condition implies that
C=\bigcup_{i}C_{i}
is reduced and that both
C_{i}
and
C_{i+1}
are smooth in
v_{i,i+1}
. The Zariski closure of
Y_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}^{\circ}
in
{\mathcal{C}}_{d_{1}}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{C}}_{d_{n}}\times(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{n}
is denoted by
Y_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}
. Writing
d=d_{1}+\dots+d_{n}
, the adjoint map
\alpha_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}
is the rational map

\alpha_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}\colon Y_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}(k[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]_{d-3}),

which maps a polycon with boundary components
(C_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
satisfying
\deg(C_{i})=d_{i}
and with designated explicit vertices
(v_{i,i+1})_{i=1}^{n}
to its unique adjoint.

One question regarding adjoint maps concerns whether they are finite and — if so — what their degree is. In
[
12
]
the authors determined all cases in which the adjoint map is dominant and generically finite (but note the differences to our definition of the adjoint map). We are, however, interested in the adjoint map
\alpha_{2,2,2}
, which assigns to a polycon bounded by three conics its cubic adjoint and has infinite fibers. The proof of the following result has been communicated by Mario Kummer. A second proof is given in
4.10
.

Lemma 2.14
.

The variety
Y_{2,2,2}
is irreducible.

Proof.

Consider three pairwise distinct points
v_{31},v_{12},v_{23}
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
and the variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}(k[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]_{2})^{3}
comprising all triples of conics
(C_{1},C_{2},C_{3})
with
v_{ij}\in C_{i}\cap C_{j}
for all
i,j
.

The condition that
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
intersect transversally for all
i,j
and that
v_{ij}\not\in C_{k}
for
k\neq i,j
defines a non-empty open subvariety of
X
. Since
\operatorname{GL}_{3}(k)
acts 3-transitively on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
we obtain a dominant rational map

\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\operatorname{GL}_{3}(k)\dashrightarrow Y_{2,2,2}^{\circ},

and since the source of this rational map is irreducible, so must be its target.
∎

In
Section

4.2
we succeed in giving an explicit description of the fibers of
\alpha_{2,2,2}
using the theory of determinantal representations.

2.4.
A Note on Positive Geometries

An emerging field of research is the study of so-called

(

]
,
[

]

3.
Preliminaries on Determinantal Representations

The subsequent results rely on some basic notions of algebraic geometry, namely divisor theory and its relation to determinantal representations. We assume familiarity with the theory of divisors on curves as developed in
[
10
, §II.6]
. At times we also require familiarity with the notation and the basic statements of cohomology of coherent sheaves on curves (e.g. the Riemann–Roch Theorem and Serre duality). By a curve we always mean a projective plane curve.

Given a smooth curve
C
and any other curve
C^{\prime}
not containing
C
, we denote by
C^{\prime}.C
the natural divisor on
C
induced by the intersection
C^{\prime}\cap C
(counted with multiplicities). By
H
we denote the hyperplane divisor, i.e. the divisor on
C
cut out by a projective line. Given a Cartier divisor
D
on
C
, by
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(D)
we denote its associated line bundle.

The main goal of this section is to recall the relation between determinantal representations of a curve
C
and certain divisors/sheaves on
C
. This relation has been discussed extensively throughout the last century. Modern accounts for both smooth and singular curves (and hypersurfaces) are given by
[
16
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
11
]
, and
[
7
, §4]
. Unless stated otherwise
C={\mathcal{V}}(f)
is a smooth projective plane curve of degree
d
over the field
k\in\{{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\}
.

Definition 3.1

(Contact curve)
.

A curve
C^{\prime}
is called a
contact curve
to
C
, if
C^{\prime}.C=2D
for some divisor
D
on
C
. We call
D
the
contact divisor
of
C^{\prime}
on
C
.

Definition 3.2

(Determinantal representation)
.

A
linear determinantal representation
(LDR) of
C
is a matrix
M\in M_{d}(k[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]_{1})
such that
\det(M)=\gamma f
for some
\gamma\in k^{\times}
. We say that
M
is a
symmetric
LDR, if
M=M^{\top}
. We say that two symmetric LDRs
M,M^{\prime}
of
C
are
equivalent
, if there exists
T\in\operatorname{GL}_{d}(k)
such that

TMT^{\top}=\pm M^{\prime}.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
16
, Theorem 5]
)
.

For a smooth curve
C
there is a bijection between equivalence classes of symmetric LDRs of
C
and divisor classes
[D]
on
C
satisfying
2D\sim(d-1)H
and
\ell(D-H)=0
.

The correspondence in
3.3
can be made explicit. A classical result states that if
M
is a symmetric LDR of
C
, then the elements of

{\mathcal{C}}_{M}=\left\{u^{t}M^{\textup{adj}}u\mid u\in k^{d}\right\}

define contact curves of degree
d-1
to
C
(see
[
16
, Theorem 5]
,
[
7
, §4.1.4]
). Conversely, given a single contact curve
C^{\prime}={\mathcal{V}}(a)
, one can construct the adjugate matrix
M^{\textup{adj}}
of a symmetric LDR
M
of
C
following Dixon’s Algorithm below. This algorithm was first attributed to
[
6
]
.

Proposition 3.4

(Dixon’s Algorithm,
[
7
, §4.1]
)
.

Let
C
be a smooth plane projective curve of degree
d
and let
D
be a divisor on
C
such that
2D
is the intersection divisor of
C
with a curve
C^{\prime}={\mathcal{V}}(a_{11})
of degree
d-1
. Furthermore assume

\ell(D-H)=0.

Then the following algorithm determines a symmetric LDR of
C
.

(1)

Extend
a_{11}
to a linearly independent tuple

a_{11},a_{12},\dots,a_{1d}

in
{\mathcal{L}}((d-1)H-D)
, the space of degree
(d-1)
polynomials vanishing in
D
.

(2)

Using Max Noether’s Fundamental Theorem (
[
8
, §5.5]
), for
2\leq i\leq j\leq d
find
a_{ij}
satisfying

a_{11}a_{ij}-a_{1i}a_{1j}\in(f).

(3)

Write
M^{\textup{adj}}=(a_{ij})_{i,j}
, completing the matrix by symmetry.

(4)

Obtain the symmetric linear determinantal representation

M=f^{-d+2}(M^{\textup{adj}})^{\textup{adj}}.

The equivalence class of
M
so constructed is independent of all choices made in this algorithm. In particular, if
\widetilde{C}={\mathcal{V}}(\widetilde{a})
is a curve such that
\widetilde{C}.C\sim C^{\prime}.C
, then there exists a change of basis
T\in\operatorname{GL}_{d}(k)
such that
\widetilde{a}
is the
(1,1)
-entry of
TM^{\textup{adj}}T^{\top}
.

Remark 3.5
.

The adjoint of a polycon need not be smooth. This, however, does not pose a problem. Max Noether’s Fundamental Theorem, on which Dixon’s Algorithm relies, is applicable as long as the contact points of curves occurring in the algorithm are smooth points (
[
8
, §5.5, Proposition 1]
). For us this is guaranteed by
2.9
. The only problem to be addressed is whether the determinant of the supposed determinantal representation is zero or not. For completeness we address this problem in
3.6
below, which is a special case of
[
11
, Proposition 5.4.]
.

Proposition 3.6

(Dixon’s Algorithm for Singular Curves)
.

Let
C
be a plane projective curve of degree
d
and let
D
be a divisor supported in its regular locus such that
2D
is the intersection divisor of
C
with a curve
C^{\prime}
of degree
d-1
. Furthermore assume

h^{0}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(D-H))=0.

Then there exists an associated symmetric LDR of
C
. Its equivalence class is independent of the divisor class of
D
.

Proof.

We want to establish the conditions for the sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(D)
laid out in
[
11
, Proposition 5.4]
, which are the following (compare this also with
[
3
, Proposition 1.11, Theorem B]
):

(1)

{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(D)\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(-D)\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(d-1)
.

(2)

h^{0}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(D-H))=0=h^{1}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(D-H))
.

Since
D
is supported in the smoooth locus of
C
, the sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(-D)
is a line bundle by
[
10
, Proposition 6.13.(a)]
. Then we have
2D\sim(d-1)H
, which proves the first condition. For the second condition we use the dualizing sheaf
\omega_{C}\cong{\mathcal{O}}_{C}((d-3)H)
on
C
and Serre duality (
[
11
, §2.1.3]
), by which we obtain

h^{1}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(D-H))=h^{0}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C}((d-3)H-(D-H))=h^{0}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(D-H)).

This proves the second condition by our hypotheses.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

An explicit algorithm to construct a symmetric LDR from the divisor
D
in the singular case is given in the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 4.3]
. It is an immediate generalization of the algorithm in
3.4
.

Remark 3.8
.

For later reference we note that in Dixon’s Algorithm we obtain divisors
D_{i}
such that
A_{11}.C=2D
and
A_{1i}.C=D+D_{i}
for some effective divisors
D_{i}
. Then
A_{ij}=D_{i}+D_{j}
for
2\leq i,j\leq d
.

4.
Results from Algebraic Geometry

We now present some additional results of this paper, which are based on the concepts introduced in
Section

3
. First, we establish that for the adjoint curve
A
of a polycon
{\mathcal{P}}
there is a natural family of mutually linearly equivalent contact divisors, which give rise to determinantal representations. We show that the corresponding contact curves are the adjoint curves of the polycons, which are obtained by replacing a conic boundary component of
{\mathcal{P}}
with a line.

The second result is an explicit description of the fibers of the adjoint map
\alpha_{2,2,2}
. This tells us how
{\mathcal{P}}
may be deformed while preserving its adjoint curve
A
. This is synonymous to applying a symmetric change of basis to a determinantal representation
M
of
A
. This allows us to conclude that the variety
Y_{2,2,2}
is irreducible and that the adjoint curve of a generic polycon bounded by three conics is smooth.

4.1.
Contact Curves to the Adjoint

We begin by recalling some notation and by making some general assumptions for this section. Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a nodal polycon. This will be a standing assumption for this section as we rely on the regularity part of

. Also note that this is generically satisfied.

We denote the boundary components of
{\mathcal{P}}
by
(C_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
with

and note that

. Finally we let

with the unique line

Remark 4.1
.

Even though we assume

to be a regular polycon, we do not impose the same restriction on

(see

Proposition 4.2

(
[

)

Let

be a nodal polycon whose adjoint curve

is smooth. Then the divisor

2T\sim(d-3)H
and

While we will prove this proposition by our own means, it is useful to draw some immediate conclusions from it. Specifically, effective representatives of the divisor class

correspond to contact curves to

of degree

is such an effective representative: indeed, by Bézout’s theorem there are exactly

2(d-2)-2=2(d-3)

residual points of

vanishes. The remaining residual points (those residual for both

Thus, there exists a unique curve of degree

with multiplicity two in each of the points in

are indeed contact curves (see

Theorem 4.3
.

The curves
A
and
A^{\prime}
are contact curves. More precisely,

A^{\prime}.A=2(R({\mathcal{P}})\cap R({\mathcal{P}}^{\prime})).

Proof.

Note that it makes sense to define the divisor
A^{\prime}.A
in this fashion, since by assumption
{\mathcal{P}}
is nodal and hence by
2.9

R({\mathcal{P}})
is contained in the smooth locus of
A
. The main technical tool that we use in this proof is that adjoints behave additively in “triangulations” of polycons.

We thus define two suitable polycons, into which we intend to partition
{\mathcal{P}}
. The first,
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
with adjoint
A_{0}={\mathcal{V}}(\alpha_{0})
, is the circle segment bounded by
\sigma(C_{1})
and
L
. The second is the polycon
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
with adjoint
A^{\prime}={\mathcal{V}}(\alpha^{\prime})
. The degrees of their respective adjoints are

\deg(\alpha_{0})=0,\qquad\deg(\alpha^{\prime})=d-4.

Now by
[
19
, Theorem 5.1]
there exist real numbers
b,b^{\prime},b_{0}
, not all zero, such that

b\alpha l=b^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}c_{1}+b_{0}\alpha_{0}\prod_{i\geq 2}c_{i}.

We proceed to show that
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
have the same tangent direction in every point
R\in R({\mathcal{P}})\cap R({\mathcal{P}}^{\prime})
. After choosing an affine chart containing
R
and after a linear change of coordinates we may assume

and it suffices to prove that the linear terms of
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
are scalar multiples. Since
\prod_{i\geq 2}c_{i}
vanishes to order two in
R
, it does not have a linear term. This lets us reduce considerations to

(1)

b\alpha l=b^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}c_{1}\mod(x,y)^{2}.

We make a case distinction based on the possibility that
R\in L
.

(1)

R\not\in L
: in this case neither
c_{1}
nor
l
vanish in
R
, hence they are invertible in
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{(x,y)}
. Localizing
Equation

1
at the maximal ideal
(x,y)
thus yields

b\alpha\equiv b^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\mod(x,y)^{2}.

(2)

R\in L
: in this case,
A^{\prime}
has a singular point in
R
(
2.5
). In particular,
\alpha^{\prime}
does not have a linear term, such that the claim is vacuously satisfied. ∎

.

generalizes

Remark 4.5
.

We briefly mentioned in

that real polycons are related to positive geometries. Our proof of

translates line by line to the latter setting with only minor adaptations, using that canonical forms are additive in triangulations of positive geometries (

holds even if

. We now want to prove that the contact divisor between

and

satisfies the condition laid out in

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a nodal polycon. Write
D=R({\mathcal{P}})\cap R({\mathcal{P}}^{\prime})
(with
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
as in
4.3
). Then the associated sheaf of

on

satisfies the conditions of
3.6
.

Proof.

By
2.9
and

the set

of points where

are contained in the smooth locus of

. We thus interpret

as a divisor, namely the contact divisor of

on

. Then

and we claim that

h^{0}(A,{\mathcal{O}}_{A}(D-H))=0.

Note that from

we obtain

h^{0}(A,{\mathcal{O}}_{A}(D-H))=h^{0}(A,{\mathcal{O}}_{A}((d-5)H-D)),

such that it suffices to prove

Assume the contrary, i.e. there exists a curve

of degree

on

is an adjoint of

in the sense of

∎

We now turn to the second result of this section, which relates the fibers of
\alpha_{2,2,2}
to symmetric LDRs of adjoints.

4.2.
Fibers of the Adjoint Map
\alpha_{2,2,2}

We will explicitly construct fibers of
\alpha_{2,2,2}
from determinantal representations. Unless stated otherwise,
A
is a plane projective curve of degree three over
k\in\{{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\}
. We begin with an observation that is an immediate consequence of the previous section.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a nodal polycon with boundary components
C_{i}
and vertices
v_{ij}
. Let
A
be its adjoint. Then there exists a symmetric LDR
M
of
A
such that

M^{\textup{adj}}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&c_{3}&c_{2}\\
c_{3}&\alpha_{2}&c_{1}\\
c_{2}&c_{1}&\alpha_{3}\end{pmatrix}

with
C_{i}={\mathcal{V}}(c_{i})
. Furthermore
A_{i}={\mathcal{V}}(\alpha_{i})
is the adjoint curve of the polycon obtained from
{\mathcal{P}}
by replacing
C_{i}
with a line.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Dixon’s Algorithm (
3.6
) and
3.8

∎

Conversely, given a matrix
M
with linear entries and
A={\mathcal{V}}(\det(M))
, one can immediately read off a polycon with adjoint
A
(provided
M
satisfies some mild conditions).

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
M
be a matrix of linear forms with nonzero determinant and let

M^{\textup{adj}}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&c_{3}&c_{2}\\
c_{3}&\alpha_{2}&c_{1}\\
c_{2}&c_{1}&\alpha_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

Write
A={\mathcal{V}}(\det(M)),A_{i}={\mathcal{V}}(\alpha_{i}),C_{i}={\mathcal{V}}(c_{i})
, and assume the following two conditions:

(1)

\operatorname{rk}(M(P))\geq 1
for all
P\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

(2)

\operatorname{rk}(M(P))=1
if and only if
P\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}
.

Then for
\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}
there exists a unique point
v_{ij}\in C_{i}\cap C_{j}
(if necessary counted with multiplicities) such that
v_{ij}\not\in A_{k}
. In particular,
A
is the adjoint curve of the polycon with boundary curves
C_{i}
and vertices
v_{ij}
.

Proof.

We deal with the case that
A
is smooth separately to give an idea for the involved arguments. By
3.8
there exist divisors
D_{1},D_{2},D_{3}
on
A
with

A_{i}.A=2D_{i},\qquad C_{k}.A=D_{i}+D_{j}.

We then observe that the common intersection of three curves
A_{i},C_{j},C_{k}
on
A
is the (degree three) divisor
D_{i}
. In particular, there exists a unique point
v_{jk}\in C_{j}\cap C_{k}
, in which
\alpha_{i}
does not vanish. Finally, smoothness of
A
implies
\operatorname{rk}(M(P))\geq 2
for all
P\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
(
[
15
, p. 885]
). As a consequence, the points
v_{ij}
are pairwise distinct: if not (say we have
v_{12}=v_{23}
), then
v_{12}\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap C_{3}
, so
\operatorname{rk}(M(v_{12}))=1
by (2), an immediate contradiction. Thus from
M^{\textup{adj}}
we may read off a well-defined polycon with adjoint
A
as described.

We now turn to potentially singular curves. The first additional hypothesis is there to ensure that there exists more than one point, in which two entries of
M^{\textup{adj}}
might have a common zero. Indeed, if there were a point
P
with
\operatorname{rk}(M(P))=0
, then every entry of
M
vanished in
P
. As a consequence, every entry of
M^{\textup{adj}}
would then be singular in
P
, so that the only point where any two of them have a common zero would be
P
.

Now by
[
11
, Proposition 5.4]
,
M
corresponds to a certain coherent sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
on
A
satisfying
{\mathcal{F}}\cong{\mathcal{F}}^{\vee}(d-1)
and

h^{0}(A,{\mathcal{F}}(-1))=0=h^{1}(A,{\mathcal{F}}(-1)).

Following the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 4.3]
we have
h^{0}(A,{\mathcal{F}})=3
, and there exists a choice of basis
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
of
\Gamma(A,{\mathcal{F}})
such that we may identify
s_{i}\otimes s_{j}=(M^{\textup{adj}})_{ij}
. In particular, since
\deg(\alpha_{i})=2
the section
s_{i}\in\Gamma(A,{\mathcal{F}})
has zeros in exactly three points on
A
. Again we conclude that there exists a unique point
v_{jk}\in C_{j}\cap C_{k}
, in which
\alpha_{i}
does not vanish. As before, the second additional hypothesis ensures that the points
v_{ij}
are unique and pairwise distinct, so that we obtain a well-defined polycon.
∎

.

Note that if the first condition in

of

As a corollary we obtain a second proof for the irreducibility of

Corollary 4.10
.

The variety

is irreducible.

Proof.

We define the rational map

\operatorname{Sym}_{3}(k[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]_{1})\dashrightarrow Y_{2,2,2}^{\circ}

that assigns to a symmetric matrix

of linear forms the polycon obtained through

. By

\operatorname{Sym}_{3}(k[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}])

We finally ask, which plane cubic curves can be realized as the adjoint of a polycon. In other words, we want to establish the plane cubic curves that admit a symmetric LDR, the rank of which does not drop to
0
. Since there is a unique flavor to real polycons that is not present in the complex case, we too make a distinction between the real and the complex adjoint map.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
k\in\{{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\}
. If
k={\mathbb{C}}
, then every plane cubic curve admits a symmetric LDR, the rank of which does not drop to
0
. If
k={\mathbb{R}}
, then the unique (up to isomorphism) plane cubic curve without such a representation is
{\mathcal{V}}(x_{0}(x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}))
. Each connected component of any fiber of
\alpha_{2,2,2}
is parametrized by an open subset of
\operatorname{GL}_{3}(k)/(k^{\times})^{3}
, acting via symmetric change of basis modulo scaling.

Proof.

The result on the existence of the symmetric LDRs is taken from
[
14
, Table 7]
. For the statement on the fibers we note that the parametrization is given by symmetric changes of basis modulo scaling. It remains to prove that two inequivalent symmetric LDRs
M
and
M^{\prime}
of a cubic curve will never just differ by the scaling of their entries. Otherwise the corresponding polycons would be the same. This can be verified by evaluating
M^{\textup{adj}}
and
(M^{\prime})^{\textup{adj}}
in suitable points. Without loss of generality we assume after (symmetric) rescaling that the first row and column of
M^{\textup{adj}}
and
(M^{\prime})^{\textup{adj}}
are equal. If now
a_{22}^{\prime}=\gamma a_{22}
for
\gamma\neq 1
, then consider a point
P\in A
, where none of the entries of
M^{\textup{adj}}
vanish. In this point we have the vanishing
2\times 2
minors

\gamma a_{11}(P)\gamma a_{22}(P)-a_{12}(P)^{2}=0=a_{11}(P)a_{22}(P)-a_{12}(P)^{2}

and thus
\gamma=1
. Analogous arguments work for the scaling factors of all other entries.
∎

.

Also contained in

x_{0}&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_{\mathcal{P}}=M^{\textup{adj}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&x_{0}^{2}\\
0&-x_{0}^{2}&x_{0}x_{1}\\
x_{0}^{2}&x_{0}x_{1}&-x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}x_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

We apply the symmetric change of basis
T=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\
0&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
to obtain

TM_{\mathcal{P}}T^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}2x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}x_{2}&x_{0}^{2}+x_{0}x_{1}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}x_{2}&x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}x_{2}\\
x_{0}^{2}+x_{0}x_{1}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}x_{2}&-x_{0}^{2}+2x_{0}x_{1}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}x_{2}&x_{0}x_{1}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}x_{2}\\
x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}x_{2}&x_{0}x_{1}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}x_{2}&-x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}x_{2}\end{pmatrix},

which can then be observed to produce a polycon with adjoint
A
. There exists exactly one residual point
(0:0:1)
, and every pair of boundary conics intersects with multiplicity three in this point. The other intersection points are the vertices of the polycon.

Corollary 4.14
.

The adjoint of a polycon bounded by three conics is generically smooth.

Proof.

By
4.11
the image of
\alpha_{2,2,2}
contains all smooth cubic curves, an open subvariety of all cubic curves. The claim then follows from irreducibility of
Y_{2,2,2}
.
∎

Appendix B
A Note on the Genesis of
2.10

This appendix intends to roughly motivate
2.10
. Our original intention was to
prove

2.8
for polycons bounded by three conics as follows: if there is no immediate argument establishing the conjecture for a polycon
{\mathcal{P}}
, then we deform the polycon while preserving its adjoint
A
using
4.11
, until the situation is more tractable. Ideally, throughout this deformation we retain control over the residual arrangement and preserve regularity. Let
M
be a symmetric LDR of
A
and let

M_{\mathcal{P}}=M^{\textup{adj}}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&c_{3}&c_{2}\\
c_{3}&\alpha_{2}&c_{1}\\
c_{2}&c_{1}&\alpha_{3}\end{pmatrix}

be the corresponding matrix encoding
{\mathcal{P}}
as in

. One family of transformations that proved promising for our goals was

T_{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

for
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Its simple structure implies that large parts of the residual arrangement of
{\mathcal{P}}

Lemma B.1
.

Write
C_{i}={\mathcal{V}}(c_{i}),A_{i}={\mathcal{V}}(\alpha_{i})
and write
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
for the polycon obtained from replacing

with a line

C_{1},A_{2}
and

.

(2)

C_{1}\cap C_{2}
and

(3)

, i.e. the unique line through
v_{12}
and

Proof.

By multiplying matrices we obtain

M_{\mathcal{P}}^{\gamma}=T_{\gamma}M_{{\mathcal{P}}}(T_{\gamma})^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}+2\gamma c_{3}+\gamma^{2}\alpha_{2}&c_{3}+\gamma\alpha_{2}&c_{2}+\gamma c_{1}\\

c_{2}+\gamma c_{1}&c_{1}&\alpha_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}^{\gamma}&c_{3}^{\gamma}&c_{2}^{\gamma}\\
c_{3}^{\gamma}&\alpha_{2}&c_{1}\\
c_{2}^{\gamma}&c_{1}&\alpha_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

From this we immediately read off the first two statements. For the third we first note that

is unchanged, since

L_{2}
.
∎
