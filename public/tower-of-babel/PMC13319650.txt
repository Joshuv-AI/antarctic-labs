Influence of activation systems on the removal and apical extrusion of a bioceramic intracanal medicament in immature teeth: an in vitro study
Authors: Sena Kaşıkçı, Ebru Şirinoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) and sonic activation (SA) on apical extrusion and the removal efficacy of bioceramic (BC), and calcium hydroxide (CH) based intracanal materials in simulated open-apex teeth.Materials and MethodsThirty-two extracted mandibular canines were prepared according to ISO #140, and an open-apex condition was created by apical resection. Specimens were randomly assigned to bioceramic (Bio-C Temp) and CH (DiaPaste) intracanal medication groups and subdivided among PUI or SA methods (n = 8). Extruded intracanal medicament was collected in pre-weighed tubes, and residual medicament was scored under a microscope. Residual intracanal medicament was evaluated using a four-grade scoring system. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; group comparisons were performed with the Kruskal–Wallis test and Dunn's post-hoc test, with significance set at p<0.05.ResultsApical Extrusion values were as follows: Group BC-PUI = 0.048 mg, Group BC-SA = 0.036 mg, Group CH-PUI = 0.052 mg, and Group CH-SA = 0.049 mg. No significant difference was observed in apical extrusion among the groups (p = 0.265). Median residual scores were Group BC-PUI = 0.5, Group BC-SA = 3, Group CH-PUI = 0.5 and Group CH-SA = 1. PUI resulted in significantly lower residual medicament scores than SA (p = 0.002).ConclusionBio-C Temp demonstrated superior removal and apical extrusion characteristics were comparable to CH. These findings support its potential as a reliable alternative to CH.Keywords: apical extrusion, bio-c temp, CH, intracanal medicaments, irrigation activation

Introduction

Pulpal infections and periapical diseases are common in children and adolescents and often result from dental caries or traumatic injuries, frequently leading to teeth with incomplete root development characterized by wide open apices and thin dentinal walls (1). Managing necrotic immature teeth is clinically challenging due to the wide canal space, fragile dentinal walls, and increased risk of fracture, which complicate cleaning, shaping, and obturation (2).

Successful endodontic therapy depends on the effective elimination of microorganisms from the root canal system and the prevention of reinfection (3). Although instrumentation is a fundamental component of root canal treatment, mechanical instrumentation alone cannot adequately reach the complex canal anatomy; therefore, the role of irrigation has become increasingly emphasized (4, 5, 6). The use of irrigation solutions aids in the removal of hard and soft tissue remnants from the apical root canal area and may contribute to limiting the apical extrusion of infected materials toward the periapical tissues (3).

Conventional needle irrigation remains a standard approach; however, it is often insufficient for cleaning the complex anatomy of the root canal system, particularly in difficult-to-reach areas such as the apical region and the isthmus. Therefore, various irrigation activation methods, including manual dynamic activation, laser-activated irrigation, multisonic irrigation systems (e.g., GentleWave), sonic activation (SA), and ultrasonic activation techniques, have been developed to address these limitations (7, 8).

Ultrasonic systems use acoustic energy transmitted through the irrigation solution, generating cavitation and microstreaming that promote active fluid movement and effective debris removal from root canal walls (9). Ultra X (Changzhou Sifary Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Changzhou, China) is a wireless passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) system operating at 45 kHz, enhancing irrigant distribution and intracanal debris removal through acoustic microcurrents and cavitation effects (10).

Sonic activation systems use flexible polyamide tips to promote three-dimensional irrigant movement, facilitating the removal of debris and medicament remnants from canal walls (11). However, their lower frequency may limit mechanical efficacy despite improved irrigant flow (10). The EndoActivator (Dentsply Tulsa Dental Specialities, Tulsa, OK, USA) is a SA system operating at 33–167 Hz with polymer tips.

Intracanal medicaments are important adjuncts in endodontic therapy for infection control, particularly in cases with complex anatomy or persistent infection (12). CH is the most commonly used intracanal medicament due to its antimicrobial activity, which is primarily attributed to the release of hydroxyl ions that create an alkaline environment unfavourable to microbial survival (13, 14). Although CH is widely used as an intracanal medicament and accepted as a gold standard- its high pH may cause cytotoxic or irritative effects on periapical tissues (15). DiaPaste (DiaDent, Cheongju, Korea) is a widely used injectable CH–based medicament composed of CH, calcium carbonate, zinc oxide, barium sulfate, water, and polyethylene glycol (16).

Bioceramic-based materials have recently been introduced into clinical practice. Owing to their lower solubility, enhanced biocompatibility, and ability to set in the presence of moisture, they have found widespread use in various endodontic applications, including vital pulp therapy, root repair, root canal sealers, retrograde fillings, as well as intracanal medicaments (17). Bio-C Temp (Angelus, Londrina, PR, Brazil) is a BC calcium silicate intracanal medicament developed for use in conventional root canal therapy, retreatment, apexification, and regenerative endodontic procedures (18).

Complete elimination of intracanal medicaments before root canal filling is critical for treatment success, as residual materials may adversely affect sealer bonding, dentinal tubule penetration, and sealing ability (19, 20, 21, 22). However, current literature indicates that no irrigation solution or technique can completely remove intracanal medicaments, leading to the widespread use of SA and PUI methods to improve removal efficacy (23, 24). While numerous studies have evaluated the removal of CH using various irrigation activation techniques, data on the removal behavior of BC intracanal medicaments are still scarce (10, 11, 23, 25). However, evidence regarding the behavior of these materials in open-apex conditions, particularly in terms of apical extrusion, remains limited.

In this context, the present study comparatively evaluated the apical extrusion and residual medicament on root canal walls during the removal of Bio-C Temp and CH-based medicaments using PUI and SA techniques in teeth with open apices.

The null hypothesis of this study is that there is no difference between PUI and SA methods in terms of apical extrusion and residual medicament presence when removing Bio-C Temp and DiaPaste.

Materials and Methods

Ethical statement

Ethics committee approval required for the study was obtained from the Kocaeli University Non-Interventional Ethics Committee (KU GOKAEK- 2024/15.23 project no.: 2024/404). The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki. Both written and oral informed consents were obtained from the patients whose teeth were used for this study after extraction.

Sample size calculation

In this in vitro study, a power analysis was conducted to calculate an appropriate sample size for the experimental design, based on a previously published study (30). This calculation was made in the G*Power 3.1 program using the ANOVA model and was based on an effect size of f = 0.745, with α set at 0.05 and a desired statistical power at 0.90. Analysis for four groups, corresponding to the number of groups in the proposed study design, revealed that at least 28 specimens-a minimum of seven specimens per group-would be required to provide adequate statistical power. Initially, additional specimens were prepared to compensate for potential sample loss; however, as no specimens were excluded during the experimental procedures, all samples were included in the final analysis. Therefore, a total of 32 (n=8 per group) extracted human mandibular canines with single canals, obtained for various clinical reasons, were included in the study (31).

Sample selection and eligibility

Inclusion criteria comprised extracted human mandibular canines with a single root and a single canal and a root length of at least 16 mm. Teeth with previous endodontic treatment, anatomical anomalies, internal or external resorption, root fractures or cracks, or canal calcifications were excluded.

Radiographic imaging in buccolingual and mesiodistal directions and CBCT examination were performed to confirm single root and single canal anatomy, exclude internal resorption, and ensure that no previous endodontic treatment had been performed.

Preparation of the samples

A total of 32 extracted human mandibular canines were included in the study. Soft and hard tissue remnants on the teeth were cleaned, and the specimens were stored in saline solution until use. To standardize the specimens, crowns were removed with a diamond disc to obtain root lengths of 16 mm. Then, 3 mm was removed from the apical direction to simulate open apices by eliminating the apical constriction and creating a wide major apical foramen, and the working length was set to 13 mm (11).

Root canals were manually prepared using ISO #140 K-type hand files (Dentsply Tulsa Dental Specialties, Tulsa, OK, USA). Between each file change, 2 mL of 2.5% NaOCl (Wizard; Rehber Kimya, Istanbul, Turkey) was used for irrigation. The absence of apical constriction and the standardization of the major apical foramen were verified radiographically and file examination. Longitudinal grooves were made on the buccal and lingual surfaces of the roots using a diamond disc without penetrating the root canal. After the longitudinal grooves were prepared, a chisel was placed into the grooves, and gentle, controlled tapping with a hammer was performed to split the roots into two longitudinal halves. The separated halves were reassembled with orthodontic elastics and sealed with a bonding agent (Solare Universal Bond, GC, Tokyo, Japan) and a flowable composite resin (Fusion Flo, Prevest DenPro, Jammu, India) to restore the original anatomical integrity.

Allocation of groups and experimental setup

The teeth were randomly allocated into two main groups (n=16). Paper points (EndoArt, Inci Dental, Istanbul, Türkiye) were used to dry the root canals before medicament placement. In the first group, a BC medicament (Bio-C Temp, Angelus, Londrina, PR, Brazil) (Group BC), and in the second group, a CH-based medicament (DiaPaste, DiaDent, Cheongju, Korea) (Group CH), were placed into the root canals to fill the entire root canal space from the apical region to the coronal access cavity using their respective single-use delivery tips provided by the manufacturers. Excess material was removed from the access cavity to ensure uniform placement among samples. Complete canal filling was radiographically confirmed. No specimens required repetition due to void formation. All specimens were stored at 37°C and 100% humidity (Nuve, EN 300, Ankara, Turkey) for 1 week.

Apical Extrusion

The Eppendorf tubes were pre-weighed using a precision balance before the experimental procedures (32, 33, 34). Each tooth was inserted through a perforation created in the cap of a 1,5 mL Eppendorf tube (Labosel, Istanbul, Turkey), allowing the root apex to remain suspended within the tube without contacting the tube walls. Eppendorf tube was inserted through a hole prepared in the rubber stopper of a glass flask (Isolab, Eschau, Germany). The flask was covered with aluminum foil to blind the operator during the experimental procedure (35). A 27-gauge needle was placed alongside the stopper during insertion to equalize the air pressure inside and outside the tube (Figure 1).

Experimental setup for apical extrusion: an Eppendorf tube for collecting extruded material, a glass flask for stabilization, and a needle to equalize the air pressure inside and outside the tube.

Each group was further divided into two subgroups based on the irrigation activation technique used, resulting in a total of four subgroups (n =8) as follows: Group BC-PUI = Bio-C Temp + PUI, Group BC-SA = Bio-C Temp + SA, Group CH-PUI = DiaPaste + PUI, Group CH-SA = DiaPaste + SA. PUI was performed using the Ultra X device at a frequency of 45 kHz, and SA was performed using the EndoActivator device operating at 10,000 cycles per minute (cpm). Before irrigation activation, irrigation was performed using a single side-vented needle (KerrHawe Irrigation Probe; KerrHawe SA, Bioggio, Switzerland) to deliver the irrigant into the canal. The same needle type was used for all specimens, and a new sterile needle was used for each specimen to ensure standardization. A total of 10 mL of 2.5% NaOCl was used as the irrigant, and irrigation activation was performed in two consecutive cycles. In each cycle, the root canals were irrigated with 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl, followed by 30 seconds of activation using the devices positioned 1 mm short of the working length. To determine the amount of extruded intracanal medicament, the Eppendorf tubes were incubated in a dry environment at 37°C for 10 days (36).

Evaluation of extruded intracanal medicament

To determine the amount of extruded medicament, each Eppendorf tube was weighed three times using a precision balance (Radwag, Radom, Poland) and the mean of the three measurements was recorded. The weight of apically extruded intracanal medicament was calculated by subtracting the initial weight of the empty tube from the final weight of the tube containing the intracanal medicament.

Evaluation of residual intracanal medicament

To assess the amount of residual medicament, the teeth were longitudinally separated using a chisel, examined under an operating microscope (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Oberkochen, Germany) at ×25 magnification, and recorded using a scoring system (25, 37) (Figure 2): 0 = The cavity was empty , 1 = Less than half of the cavity was ﬁlled with medicament, 2 = More than half of the cavity was ﬁlled with medicament, 3 = The complete cavity was ﬁlled with medicament. All experimental procedures were performed by a single operator (E.Ş.) to ensure standardization. The evaluation of residual medicament and apical extrusion was conducted by a second examiner (S.K.) who was blinded to both the medicament type and the irrigation activation protocol, thereby minimizing potential bias. To assess intra- examiner reliability, all samples were re-evaluated by the same blinded examiner at a different time point under identical conditions.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS Version 23 (IBM, New York, USA). The normality of the distribution was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparing three or more groups with non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used, and multiple comparisons were conducted with the Dunn test. Results were presented as median (minimum–maximum) and mean ± standard deviation for quantitative data. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. The kappa test was used to analyze intra-observer agreement. Because scoring was performed by a single blinded examiner inter- observer reliability analyze was not applicable.

Results

A total of 32 specimens were evaluated (n=8 per group). No specimens were lost during experiments.

Apical Extrusion

The lowest apical extrusion was observed in Group BC-SA (0.036 mg), whereas the highest was found in Group CH-PUI (0.052 mg).

The median values for apical extruded intracanal medicament did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.265) (Table 2).

Discussion

The null hypothesis was partially rejected due to the fact that PUI provided better residual medicament removal whereas there was no difference in terms of apical extrusion when removing either CH or BC intracanal medicament from immature teeth with open apices.

In immature teeth, the wide and open apical foramen increases the risk of apical extrusion during irrigation (38). Therefore, this should be considered an important factor when selecting the irrigation protocol for the endodontic treatment of teeth with incomplete root development.

In the present study, canal dimensions were standardized, ensuring comparable conditions across experimental groups. No significant difference was observed between PUI and SA systems in terms of apical extrusion which is compatible with previous reports (39, 40, 41).

It is known that intracanal medicaments applied in root canal treatment increase the disinfection efficiency, especially in areas with complex anatomies. However, the retention of these medicaments on the canal walls reduces the penetration of the root canal filling sealer used, paving the way for microleakage and may risk long-term treatment success. Therefore, effective removal of intracanal medicaments before root canal filling is critical for the success of endodontic treatment (42).

In our study, PUI was found to be more effective than SA in removing intracanal medicaments. Removal of intracanal medicaments may differ in larger root canal systems with thinner walls, which may exhibit different attachment characteristics, as well as in cases with open apices that may not provide sufficient resistance for irrigation solutions to effectively contact and clean the canal. This can be attributed to the acoustic microstreaming and cavitation effects produced during PUI, which enhance the action of irrigants. These findings emphasize the clinical value of PUI in immature teeth in addition to what was reported for mature teeth with fully formed apices (29, 43).

In the present study, irrigation activation was performed in two consecutive cycles. This protocol was selected in accordance with a previously reported in vitro study in which activation was repeated twice (23). The use of two consecutive irrigation activation cycles in the present study may be considered a relatively conservative approach compared to previous studies employing multiple activation protocols. While additional activation cycles could potentially enhance cleaning efficacy, previous studies have suggested a cumulative effect over successive cycles, whereas a plateau after repeated activation may also occur (44, 45, 46). Therefore, limiting activation to two cycles may have contributed to maintaining comparable apical extrusion levels while still achieving effective removal.

The findings of this study revealed no significant difference between Bio-C Temp and CH–based medicament in terms of removal ability or apical medicament extrusion in open-apex root canals. Although bioceramic-based materials have been reported to adhere more strongly to dentin and may therefore be more difficult to remove, the irrigation activation techniques applied in the open-apex model may have mitigated these differences by facilitating irrigant circulation (47). Consequently, Bio-C Temp demonstrated a removal profile comparable to CH without increasing the risk of apical medicament extrusion. In this context, the comparable removal performance observed in the present study suggests that Bio-C Temp may represent a viable alternative to CH in regenerative endodontic protocols, particularly given its previously reported biological properties associated with tissue healing (18, 48).

In some previous studies, sodium hypochlorite has been used in combination with EDTA (18, 29). In the present study, irrigation was performed using a NaOCl-based protocol, consistent with some previously published ex vivo studies (49, 50). A previous ex vivo study demonstrated that PUI significantly enhances intracanal medicament removal even in the absence of chelating agents (49). In this context, the present findings suggest that irrigation activation may play a more decisive role in medicament removal and apical extrusion outcomes than the specific irrigant used.

Preliminary radiographic assessment was performed using periapical radiographs taken in buccolingual and mesiodistal directions to evaluate root morphology and exclude obvious anatomical variations. Subsequently, CBCT imaging was employed for three-dimensional confirmation of single-root and single-canal anatomy and to rule out internal resorption or evidence of previous endodontic treatment, thereby enhancing the accuracy of specimen selection. This approach may have helped to reduce anatomical variability among the selected specimens, potentially minimizing confounding factors and allowing for a more consistent comparison between experimental groups. Consequently, the observed differences may be more reliably attributed to the tested irrigation protocols, which could contribute to improved internal validity and overall reliability of the findings.

These findings suggest that both PUI and SA methods offer a comparable safety profile concerning apical extrusion in teeth with open apices, supporting their use in clinical practice based on equipment availability and clinician preference.

This study has several limitations. The irrigation protocol was limited to NaOCl, and the findings may not be directly applicable to protocols including chelating agents such as EDTA. Additionally, the absence of a non-activated control group restricts the assessment of baseline medicament removal and apical extrusion.

Future studies may focus on evaluating different irrigation activation protocols using varying canal anatomies. In addition, assessing intracanal medicament extrusion regarding different irrigant types and experimental conditions may further clarify the clinical implications of irrigation activation methods.

Conclusion

The present findings suggest that bioceramic intracanal medicaments may represent a clinically acceptable alternative to conventional based on the finding that they pose no additional challenge compared to CH in teeth with open apices. PUI may be preferred rather than SA as it removes more material from the root canal while has a comparable apical extrusion.
