Monoids generated by projections
Matthew Fayers

Abstract
We define and explore semireflection monoids on a finite-dimensional vector space. These are monoids generated by semireflections : linear maps fixing a subspace of codimension 1 1 . We mostly focus on the case of projection monoids (where the generating semireflections are non-invertible). After exploring some general theory, we give some important examples, and give classification results for projection monoids on ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} and ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . We then briefly introduce affine projection monoids.

Introduction

The theory of finite reflection groups (both real and complex) is one of the cornerstones of twentieth-century mathematics, with applications in numerous areas, and continues to inspire a great deal of research. We begin by summarising the essentials.

Throughout this paper we let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field
\mathbb{F}
. We define a
reflection
on
V
to be an invertible linear map
r:V\to V
whose fixed-point space has codimension
1
(or equivalently, for which the space
\left\{\left.(r-1)v\ \right|\ \smash{v\in V}\right\}
has dimension
1
). A
reflection group
on
V
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}(V)
generated by reflections. We are interested primarily in
finite
reflection groups. Obviously in a finite reflection group each reflection
r
has finite order, so has eigenvalues
1,\dots,1,\zeta
for a root of unity
\zeta\in\mathbb{F}
. Moreover, if
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=0
then
\zeta\neq 1
, so that
r
is diagonalisable. (What we have called a reflection is often called a
pseudoreflection
, with the term ‘reflection’ being reserved for the case where
\zeta=-1
.)

A finite reflection group
G
on
V
is
irreducible
if there is no subspace
0<W<V
which is preserved by
G
. It is
completely reducible
if for every
W_{1}\leqslant V
which is preserved by
G
there is another subspace
W_{2}
which is preserved by
G
such that
V=W_{1}\oplus W_{2}
. In this case
G
decomposes as a direct product
G_{1}\times G_{2}
, where
G_{i}
is the pointwise stabiliser of
W_{3-i}
, and acts as a completely reducible reflection group on
W_{i}
. Iterating this decomposition shows that for any completely reducible finite reflection group
G
on
V
there is a direct sum decomposition
V=W_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus W_{r}
and a corresponding direct product decomposition
G=G_{1}\times\dots\times G_{r}
, where
G_{i}
acts as an irreducible reflection group on
V_{i}
and fixes
V_{j}
pointwise for each
j\neq i
.

It is essentially a consequence of Maschke’s Theorem that if
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=0
then every finite reflection group is completely reducible, so to classify finite reflection groups it suffices to classify irreducibles. Over
\mathbb{R}
this was done by Coxeter
[
C1
]
, who showed
[
C2
]
that the irreducible finite real reflection groups are precisely the finite Coxeter groups. Over
\mathbb{C}
the irreducible finite reflection groups were classified by Shephard and Todd
[
ST
]
; these consist of the infinite family of groups
G(m,p,n)
, together with 34 exceptional groups
G_{4},\dots,G_{37}
. Finite reflection groups in prime characteristic have been considered in a series of papers by Zaleskii and Serezhkin
[
ZS1
,
ZS2
,
ZS3
]
.

Now we introduce the generalisations of reflection groups that we study in this paper. Suppose
V
is a finite-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}
. We define a
semireflection
to be a (not necessarily invertible) linear map
r:V\to V
whose fixed-point space has codimension
1
. So a semireflection is either a reflection or a projection onto a subspace of dimension
n-1
. For the rest of this paper, we use the term ‘projection’ to mean a projection onto a subspace of codimension
1
; or in other words, an idempotent linear map of nullity
1
.

We define a
semireflection monoid
on
V
to be a submonoid of
\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}}(V)
generated by semireflections. A semireflection monoid is a
projection monoid
if it is generated by projections. We will mainly restrict attention to projection monoids in this paper; we are particularly interested in finding
finite
projection monoids. We will concentrate on irreducibility and classification, leaving the study of the algebraic structure of these monoids to future work.

In the next section we will explore some basic theory of semireflection monoids and projection monoids, particularly relating to irreducibility and complete reducibility. In
Section

3
we introduce two important examples and give come of their properties. In
Sections

4
and
5
we classify irreducible projection monoids on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and in the final section we introduce affine projection monoids.

2
Semireflection monoids

2.1
Some more definitions

Here we give some more basic definitions relating to semireflection monoids. First suppose
M,N
are semireflection monoids on vector spaces
V,W
respectively. Then we say that
M
and
N
are
equivalent
if there is a bijective linear map
f:V\to W
such that
N=\left\{\left.fmf^{-1}\ \right|\ \smash{m\in M}\right\}
.

If
r:V\to V
is a semireflection, then so is the dual map
r^{\ast}:V^{\ast}\to V^{\ast}
. So if
M
is a semireflection monoid on
V
, then there is a dual semireflection monoid
M^{\ast}
on
V^{\ast}
. Unlike in the case of finite reflection groups, a finite semireflection monoid need not be equivalent (or even isomorphic an an abstract monoid) to its dual.

Now we show how to decompose a semireflection monoid into a ‘reflection part’ and a ‘projection part’. Suppose
M
is a semireflection monoid. Then we define
M_{0}
to be the submonoid generated by all the projections in
M
, and
M_{1}
the submonoid generated by the reflections in
M
. Then
M_{1}
is simply the set of invertible elements of
M
and is a reflection group. Moreover,
M_{0}\cap M_{1}=\{1\}
,
M_{0}M_{1}=M
, and
M_{1}
normalises
M_{0}
. (In many cases
M_{0}
comprises all the non-invertible elements of
M
, but this is not true in general.)

Given a projection monoid
M_{0}
, we define its
normalising reflection group
to be the group generated by all the reflections that normalise
M_{0}
. Now to construct a finite semireflection monoid, we can proceed in stages:

\diamond

construct a finite projection monoid
M_{0}
;

\diamond

let
G
be the normalising reflection group of
M_{0}
;

\diamond

let
M_{1}
be a finite subgroup of
G
, and let
M
be the semireflection monoid
M_{0}M_{1}
.

In this paper we will mainly be concerned with the first step, though for many of the projection monoids that we construct we will find the normalising reflection group.

We remark that one can consider instead
projection semigroups
instead of projection monoids, with no practical difference; a projection semigroup is the same as the corresponding projection monoid but with the identity element removed. The choice is largely a matter of taste; our choice to work with monoids simplifies some statements (e.g. the expression
M_{0}M_{1}
above would need to be
M_{0}M_{1}\cup M_{1}
when working with semigroups, and references to direct products below would need modification) while complicating others (in
Section

3
below we repeatedly make reference to the non-invertible elements of a projection monoid, which are simply the elements of the corresponding projection semigroup).

2.2
Irreducibility and complete reducibility

Suppose
M
is a projection monoid on
V
. We say that
M
is
reducible
if there is a subspace
0<W<V
which is mapped to itself by all elements of
M
. We call such a subspace
invariant
for
M
. We say that
M
is
irreducible
if it is not reducible.

We begin with some basic theory which parallels the corresponding theory for reflection groups. We leave the proof of the following simple
Lemma
to the reader.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
M
is a projection monoid on
V
, and
0\leqslant W\leqslant V
. Then
W
is invariant for
M
if and only if for every projection
p\in M
either
\ker(p)\leqslant W
or
W\leqslant\operatorname{im}(p)
.

This lemma allows us to further examine the structure of a reducible projection monoid. Suppose
M
is a projection monoid and
W
is an invariant subspace for
M
. For each projection
p\in M
, we consider the two possibilities in
Lemma

2.1
. If
\ker(p)\leqslant W
, then
p
yields a projection on
W
and the identity on
V/W
; on the other hand, if
W\leqslant\operatorname{im}(p)
, then
p
acts as the identity on
W
and yields a projection on
V/W
.

So we naturally obtain a projection monoid
M_{W}
on
W
, generated by the restrictions to
W
of the projections in
M
, and a projection monoid
M_{V/W}
on
V/W
, generated by the projections induced by the projections in
M
that fix
W
. The direct product
M_{W}\times M_{V/W}
is naturally a quotient of
M
.

Now say that
M
is a
completely reducible
if for every invariant subspace
W
there is another invariant subspace
X
with
V=W\oplus X
, or equivalently, if the sum of the minimal invariant subspaces for
M
is
V
.

In the case where
V
is the direct sum
W_{1}\oplus W_{2}
of two invariant subspaces, we can refine the above discussion. By
Lemma

2.1
we can partition the set of projections in
M
as
P_{1}\sqcup P_{2}
, where
\ker(p)\leqslant W_{i}
and
W_{3-i}\leqslant\operatorname{im}(p)
for each
p\in P_{i}
. Then each projection in
P_{1}
commutes with each projection in
P_{2}
. If we let
M_{i}
be the monoid generated by
P_{i}
for
i=1,2
, then every
m\in M_{i}
fixes
W_{3-i}
pointwise. So
M_{1}\cap M_{2}=\{1\}
and
M\cong M_{1}\times M_{2}
. Each
p\in P_{i}
acts as a projection on
W_{i}
, so we can view
M_{i}
as a projection monoid on
W_{i}
. If
M
is completely reducible, then so is
M_{i}
(as a projection monoid on
W_{i}
).

Iterating this, we find that if
M
is a completely reducible projection monoid on
V
, then there is a direct sum decomposition
V=V_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus V_{r}
and a direct product decomposition
M=M_{1}\times\dots\times M_{r}
, where each
M_{i}
is an irreducible projection monoid on
V_{i}
(and acts as the identity on
V_{j}
for each
j\neq i
). So to classify completely reducible projection monoids, it suffices to classify irreducible ones.

Example
.

In this example we describe all reducible projection monoids in dimension
2
(for any
\mathbb{F}
). As a consequence, we observe that (in contrast to the situation with reflection groups) finite projection monoids need not be completely reducible in characteristic
0
.

Suppose
\dim(V)=2
and let
L
be any line in
V
. We consider the projection monoids on
V
having
L
as an invariant subspace. By
Lemma

2.1
any projection
p
in such a projection monoid must have
L
as an eigenspace (that is,
L
must be either the kernel or the image of
p
).

Let
P
be
any
finite set of projections all having
L
as an eigenspace, and let
M
be the monoid generated by
P
. Then we claim that
M
is finite. To see this, suppose
p,q\in P
, and observe that:

\diamond

if
L=\ker(p)=\ker(q)
, then
pq=q
;

\diamond

if
L=\operatorname{im}(p)=\operatorname{im}(q)
, then
pq=p
;

\diamond

if
L=\operatorname{im}(p)=\ker(q)
, then
pq=0
.

As a consequence, any expression in the elements of
P
reduces to an expression of length at most
2
or to
0
, so
M
is finite.

M
is reducible (because
L
is invariant for
M
), but generally not completely reducible.

2.3
Images and kernels

In this subsection we describe necessary and sufficient conditions for complete reducibility of a projection monoid
M
; here the theory diverges from the theory of finite reflection groups.

First we set out some definitions which will be used throughout the rest of the paper. Suppose
M
is a projection monoid on
V
. Given subspaces
K,L<V
with
\dim(K)=\operatorname{codim}(L)=1
and with
K\nleqslant L
, we write
\mathrm{prj}(K,L)
for the projection with kernel
K
and image
L
. We Say that a line
K
is a
kernel
of
M
if
\mathrm{prj}(K,L)\in M
for some
L
, and we say that a hyperplane
L
is an
image
of
M
if
\mathrm{prj}(K,L)\in M
for some
K
. We write
\operatorname{Ker}(M)
for the set of kernels of
M
, and
\operatorname{Im}(M)
for the set of images of
M
.

We say that
M
is
complete
if the following condition holds: whenever
K\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
and
L\in\operatorname{Im}(M)
with
K\nleqslant L
, the projection
\mathrm{prj}(K,L)
lies in
M
.

Now we can give the main theorem of this section. We define the
trivial
projection monoid on
V
to be the monoid containing just the identity element; clearly this is irreducible if and only if
\dim(V)=1
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose
M
is a non-trivial finite irreducible projection monoid on
V
. Then:

\diamond

the sum of the kernels of
M
is
V
;

\diamond

the intersection of the images of
M
is
0
; and

\diamond

M
is complete.

Let
W
be the sum of the kernels of
M
. If
v\in W
and
p
is a projection in
M
, then
p(v)-v
lies in the kernel of
p
and therefore lies in
W
, so
p(v)\in W
. So
W
is invariant for
M
. Because
M
is non-trivial,
W
must be non-zero, so (because
M
is irreducible)
W
must equal
V
. The second statement follows in a similar way (or by duality).

Now we prove that
M
is complete. Suppose
K
is a kernel of
M
and
L
an image of
M
with
K\nleqslant L
. Let
t\in M
be a projection with kernel
K
.

Define

W=\sum\left\{\left.\ker(v)\ \right|\ \smash{v\in M,\ \operatorname{im}(v)=L}\right\}.

Then we claim that
W=V
. Obviously
W\neq 0
, so to show that
W=V
it suffices to show that
W
is invariant for
M
. We do this using
Lemma

2.1
: we show that if
u
is a projection in
M
and that
W\nleqslant\operatorname{im}(u)
, then
\ker(u)\leqslant W
. Since
W\nleqslant\operatorname{im}(u)
there is an element
v\in M
such that
\operatorname{im}(v)=L
and
\ker(v)\not\leqslant\operatorname{im}(u)
. This last condition means that
vu
has nullity
1
, so that its image is
\operatorname{im}(v)=L
and its kernel is
\ker(u)
. But then
\ker(u)\leqslant W
, as required.

So
W=V
, which means in particular that
W\nleqslant\operatorname{im}(t)
. So there is
v\in M
such that
\operatorname{im}(v)=L
and
\ker(v)\nleqslant\operatorname{im}(t)
. This then means that
vt
has nullity
1
, and therefore has kernel
\ker(t)
and image
\operatorname{im}(v)=L
.

So
M
contains an element
x=vt
with kernel
K
and image
L
. This element acts as an invertible linear map on
L
, and this linear map has finite order because
M
is finite. So there is
a>0
such that
x^{a}
acts as the identity on
L
. But then
x^{a}
is the projection
\mathrm{prj}(K,L)
.
∎

This leads to the following statement for completely reducible projection monoids.

Corollary 2.3
.

Suppose
M
is a finite completely reducible projection monoid on
V
, and let
S
be the sum of the kernels of
M
and
T
the intersection of the images of
M
. Then
V=S\oplus T
, and
M
is complete.

If
M
is irreducible and non-trivial, the result follows from
2.2
. If
M
is trivial, then the result is immediate. So suppose
M
is reducible. Then by complete reducibility we can write
V=W_{1}\oplus W_{2}
, where
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are invariant for
M
. As explained above, we can partition the set of projections in
M
as
P_{1}\sqcup P_{2}
, where
\ker(p)\leqslant W_{i}
and
W_{3-i}\leqslant\operatorname{im}(p)
for all
p\in P_{i}
. Let
M_{i}
be the submonoid generated by
P_{i}
.

Suppose first that one of
P_{1},P_{2}
is empty; say
P_{1}=P
and
P_{2}=\emptyset
, so that
M=M_{1}
. Then
M
acts as a projection monoid on
W_{1}
, and acts trivially on
W_{2}
. In particular,
W_{2}\leqslant T
. By induction on dimension we can assume that the
Section
holds for
M
acting on
W_{1}
, so that
W_{1}=S\oplus(T\cap W_{1})
, giving
V=S\oplus T
. Clearly also
M
is complete in this case.

Now assume instead that both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are non-trivial. For
i=1,2
let

S_{i}=\sum_{p\in P_{i}}\ker(p),\qquad T_{i}=\bigcap_{p\in P_{i}}\operatorname{im}(p)

Then
S=S_{1}+S_{2}
and
T=T_{1}\cap T_{2}
. By induction on
\lvert M\rvert
we can assume that the
Section
holds for
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, so that
V=S_{1}\oplus T_{1}=S_{2}\oplus T_{2}
. Clearly
S_{2}\leqslant W_{2}\leqslant T_{1}
, so that

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=S_{1}+T_{1}

\displaystyle=S_{1}+(T_{1}\cap(S_{2}+T_{2}))

\displaystyle=S_{1}+S_{2}+(T_{1}\cap T_{2})\qquad\text{since $S_{2}\leqslant T_{1}$}

\displaystyle=S+T,

while

\displaystyle S\cap T

\displaystyle=(S_{1}+S_{2})\cap T_{1}\cap T_{2}

\displaystyle=((S_{1}\cap T_{1})+S_{2})\cap T_{2}\qquad\text{since $S_{2}\leqslant T_{1}$}

\displaystyle=S_{2}\cap T_{2}

\displaystyle=0

giving
V=S\oplus T
.

To show that
M
is complete, suppose
K\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
and
L\in\operatorname{Im}(M)
with
K\nleqslant L
. Take projections
p,q\in M
such that
K=\ker(p)
and
L=\operatorname{im}(q)
. Then
p\in P_{i}
and
q\in Q_{j}
for some
i,j\in\{1,2\}
. Now
i
must equal
j
, since if
i\neq j
then
K=\ker(p)\leqslant W_{i}\leqslant\operatorname{im}(q)=L
, a contradiction. So (from the assumption that the
Section
holds for
M_{i}
) the projection
\mathrm{prj}(K,L)
lies in
M_{i}
, and hence in
M
.
∎

Here is a converse statement.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose
M
is a finite projection monoid on
V
, and let
S
be the sum of the kernels of
M
and
T
the intersection of the images of
M
. If
V=S\oplus T
and
M
is complete, then
M
is completely reducible.

Take a subspace
W
which is invariant for
M
. By
Lemma

2.1
we can partition the set of projections in
M
as
P_{1}\sqcup P_{2}
, where
\ker(p)\leqslant W
for each
p\in P_{1}
, and
W\leqslant\operatorname{im}(p)
for each
p\in P_{2}
. For
i=1,2
let
S_{i}
be the sum of the kernels of the elements of
P_{i}
, and
T_{i}
the intersection of the images of the elements of
P_{i}
. Then by definition
S_{1}\leqslant W\leqslant T_{2}
.

Now we claim that
S_{2}\leqslant T_{1}
. Suppose for a contradiction that
S_{2}\nleqslant T_{1}
. Then there are projections
p\in P_{1}
and
q\in P_{2}
such that
\ker(q)\nleqslant\operatorname{im}(p)
. The assumption that
M
is complete means that
M
contains the projection
r=\mathrm{prj}(\ker(q),\operatorname{im}(p))
. So
r
belongs to
P_{1}
or
P_{2}
. But if
r\in P_{1}
, then
\ker(q)=\ker(r)\leqslant W\leqslant\operatorname{im}(q)
, while if
r\in P_{2}
, then
\ker(p)\leqslant W\leqslant\operatorname{im}(r)=\operatorname{im}(p)
. Either way, we have our contradiction.

Next we claim that
S_{2}\cap W=0
. To see this, we observe that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=S\cap T

\displaystyle=(S_{1}+S_{2})\cap T_{1}\cap T_{2}

\displaystyle=T_{1}\cap((S_{2}\cap T_{2})+S_{1})\qquad\text{because $S_{1}\leqslant T_{2}$}

\displaystyle=(S_{1}\cap T_{1})+(S_{2}\cap T_{2})\qquad\text{because $S_{2}\leqslant T_{1}$}

\displaystyle\geqslant S_{2}\cap T_{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant S_{2}\cap W.

Dually, we can show that
V=T_{1}+W
. This, together with the previous two claims, shows that there is a subspace
X
such that
S_{2}\leqslant X\leqslant T_{1}
, and
V=W\oplus X
. The fact that
S_{2}\leqslant X\leqslant T_{1}
, together with
Lemma

2.1
, shows that
X
is invariant for
M
, and we are done.
∎

We end this section by considering semireflection monoids and complete reducibility. Recall that for a semireflection monoid
M
we define the
projection submonoid

M_{0}
and the
reflection subgroup

M_{1}
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose
M
is a finite completely reducible semireflection monoid on
V
. Then
M_{0}
is completely reducible.

It suffices to prove that
V
is the sum of its minimal
M_{0}
-invariant subspaces. Since
M
is completely reducible,
V
is the sum of its minimal
M
-invariant subspaces. So we just need to show that any minimal
M
-invariant subspace
W
is a sum of minimal
M_{0}
-invariant subspaces. Certainly
W
is
M_{0}
-invariant, so it contains a minimal
M_{0}
-invariant subspace
U
. Because
M_{1}
normalises
M_{0}
, the set of
M_{0}
-invariant subspaces is preserved by the reflection group
M_{1}
, so the images of
U
under
M_{1}
are also minimal
M_{0}
-invariant subspaces. So the sum
X
of these images is both
M_{0}
-invariant and
M_{1}
-invariant, so is
M
-invariant. Clearly
X\leqslant W
, because
W
is
M_{1}
-invariant. So the minimality of
W
means that
W=X
, and therefore
W
is a sum of minimal
M_{0}
-invariant subspaces.
∎

Remark
.

It is not generally the case that if a semireflection monoid
M
is completely reducible then the reflection subgroup of
M
must be. We will see an example of this in the next section.

3
Two examples

In this section we construct two general examples of irreducible finite projection monoids. These are defined over any field
\mathbb{F}
, and in any dimension. For these examples we prove irreducibility, find the cardinality and the normalising reflection group, and classify minimal generating sets of projections.

We use
\langle\ \rangle
to denote the
\mathbb{F}
-linear span of a vector or set of vectors.

3.1
Type
A

3.1.1
Definition, irreducibility and cardinality

Take
n\geqslant 1
, and suppose
V
is a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
of dimension
n
. Take a vector space
V^{+}
of dimension
n+1
containing
V
, and choose a basis
\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n}\}
of
V^{+}
such that
e_{i}-e_{j}\in V
for all
i,j
.

Now given
i,j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
with
i\neq j
, define
p_{ij}:V\to V
to be the projection with kernel
\langle e_{i}-e_{j}\rangle
and image
\left\langle\left.e_{k}-e_{l}\ \right|\ \smash{k,l\neq i}\right\rangle
. Define
\mathcal{A}_{n}
to be the monoid generated by the projections
p_{ij}
. Then
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is well-defined up to equivalence.

We remark that if
n\geqslant 3
, then
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is not self-dual. To see this, observe that the kernels of a projection monoid naturally correspond to the images of its dual. So a self-dual projection monoid must have the same number of images as kernels.
\mathcal{A}_{n}
has
n+1
images and
\binom{n+1}{2}
kernels when
n\geqslant 2
, so is not self-dual if
n\geqslant 3
.

However,
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
are self-dual. For
\mathcal{A}_{1}
this is is easy to see. For
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, consider the dual space
(V^{+})^{\ast}
and let
\{\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\}
be the basis dual to
\{e_{0},e_{1},e_{2}\}
. Then the dual space
V^{\ast}
can be realised as the quotient of
(V^{+})^{\ast}
by the subspace spanned by
\epsilon_{0}+\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}
. Now the map
(V^{+})^{\ast}\to V
given by linear extension of

\epsilon_{0}\longmapsto e_{1}-e_{2},\qquad\epsilon_{1}\longmapsto e_{2}-e_{0},\qquad\epsilon_{2}\longmapsto e_{0}-e_{1}

yields a linear bijection
V^{\ast}\to V
under which the projection
p_{ij}
corresponds to the projection
p_{ij}^{\ast}
.

Now we examine the basic properties of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

\mathcal{A}_{n}
is irreducible.

Suppose
W\leqslant V
is invariant for
\mathcal{A}_{n}
; then we must show that
W=0
or
W=V
. Let
G
be the graph with vertex set
\{0,\dots,n\}
and an edge from
i
to
j
if and only if
e_{i}-e_{j}\in W
. Then clearly each connected component of
G
is a complete graph. If
G
is the complete graph then
W=V
and we are done, so assume otherwise. Then we claim that every connected component
C
of
G
consists of a single vertex. To see this, take
i\in C
and
j\notin C
. Then there is no edge from
i
to
j
in
G
, so
e_{i}-e_{j}\notin W
; that is,
\ker(p_{ij})\nleqslant W
. Now
Lemma

2.1
gives
W\leqslant\operatorname{im}(p_{ij})=\left\langle\left.e_{k}-e_{l}\ \right|\ \smash{k,l\neq i}\right\rangle
. In other words,
W
does not contain
e_{i}-e_{k}
for any
k\neq i
, so that
i
is an isolated vertex of
G
, and therefore
C=\{i\}
.

Applying this argument for every connected component, we find that
G
is the empty graph. So
W
does not contain any of the kernels of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, and therefore is contained in every image of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. But it is easy to see that the intersection of the images of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is
0
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

\mathcal{A}_{n}
is finite, with

\lvert\mathcal{A}_{n}\rvert=\begin{cases}(n+1)^{n+1}-(n+1)!-n+1&\text{ if }\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2,\\
(n+1)^{n+1}-(n+1)!-2^{n}\binom{n+1}{2}+\binom{n}{2}+1.&\text{ if }\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=2.\end{cases}.

Recall that we view
V
as a subspace of a vector space
V^{+}
with basis
\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n}\}
. If we define
p_{ij}^{+}:V^{+}\to V^{+}
for each
i,j
by mapping
e_{i}\mapsto e_{j}
, and
e_{k}\mapsto e_{k}
for all
k\neq i
, then
p_{ij}^{+}
is a projection. Now if we define
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
to be the projection monoid on
V^{+}
generated by the projections
p_{ij}^{+}
, then
V
is invariant for
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
and
p_{ij}^{+}
acts on
V
as
p_{ij}
.

The basis
B=\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n}\}
for
V^{+}
is mapped to itself by every element of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
, with the non-invertible elements of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
yielding non-invertible functions from
B
to
B
. Obviously any element of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
is determined by its action on
B
, so we can view
\mathcal{A}_{n}
as a submonoid of the
full transformation monoid

F
of all functions from
B
to
B
. In fact, we can be more precise: the projections
p_{ij}^{+}
correspond to the idempotents in
F
of defect
1
(i.e. with exactly one non-fixed point), and Howie shows
[
H1
, Theorem I]
that these idempotents generate the subsemigroup consisting of
all
non-bijective elements of
F
.

So the number of non-identity elements of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
equals the number of non-bijective elements of
F
, which is
(n+1)^{n+1}-(n+1)!
. But
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is isomorphic to a quotient of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
. To find the order of this quotient, we have to determine when two different functions
f,g:B\to B
yield the same linear map on
V
. For this we consider separately the cases
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
and
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
. For a function
f:B\to B
, let
\check{f}:V\to V
be the map obtained by extending linearly to
V^{+}
and then restricting to
V
.

First suppose
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. Then we claim that
\check{f}=\check{g}
if and only if
f
and
g
are both constant. The ‘if’ is clear, since if
f
is constant then
\check{f}
is the zero map. For the ‘only if’, suppose that
f\neq g
and that
f
is not constant. Then there are
i,j
such that
f(e_{i})\neq f(e_{j})\neq g(e_{j})
. This yields
\check{f}(e_{i}-e_{j})\neq\check{g}(e_{i}-e_{j})
, so that
\check{f}\neq\check{g}
.

Now suppose
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
. Again, if
f
and
g
are both constant then
\check{f}=\check{g}
. If there are
i,j
such that
\lvert\{f(e_{i}),f(e_{j}),g(e_{i}),g(e_{j})\}\rvert\geqslant 3
, then
\check{f}(e_{i}-e_{j})\neq\check{g}(e_{i}-e_{j})
. Likewise, if there are
i,j
such that
f(e_{i})\neq f(e_{j})
while
g(e_{i})=g(e_{j})
, or
f(e_{i})=f(e_{j})
while
g(e_{i})\neq g(e_{j})
, then
\check{f}(e_{i}-e_{j})\neq\check{g}(e_{i}-e_{j})
. If there are no such
i,j
and
f
and
g
are not both constant, then
f
and
g
between them take exactly two values, say
e_{k}
and
e_{l}
, with
f(e_{i})=e_{k}
if and only if
g(e_{i})=e_{l}
. Now
\check{f}(e_{i}-e_{j})
equals
e_{k}+e_{l}
if
f(i)\neq f(j)
, and
0
otherwise, and similarly for
g
, so that
\check{f}=\check{g}
.

Now we can find
\lvert\mathcal{A}_{n}\rvert
: there are
(n+1)^{n+1}
functions
\{0,\dots,n\}\to\{0,\dots,n\}
, and
(n+1)!
of these are bijective. The non-bijective functions correspond to non-invertible elements of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, except that the
n+1
different constant functions all give the same element (namely, the zero element) of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
; furthermore, if
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
, the
(2^{n+1}-2)\binom{n+1}{2}
functions
f
taking exactly two values occur in pairs giving the same
\check{f}
. So the number of non-invertible elements of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is
(n+1)^{n+1}-(n+1)!-n
if
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
, or
(n+1)^{n+1}-(n+1)!-n-(2^{n}-1)\binom{n+1}{2}
if
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
.
∎

3.1.2
Normalising reflections

Now (following the discussion in
Section

2.1
) we find the normalising reflection group of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. For this, we assume
n\geqslant 2
. Clearly an element
r\in\operatorname{GL}(V)
normalises
\mathcal{A}_{n}
if and only if it preserves the set of lines
\langle e_{i}-e_{j}\rangle
.
It is easy to show that in this case there is an element
r^{+}\in\operatorname{GL}(V^{+})
which extends
r
and preserves the set of lines
\langle e_{i}\rangle
. The assumption that
n\geqslant 2
means that
r^{+}
is uniquely defined. Now the fact that
r^{+}
preserves the set of lines
\langle e_{i}-e_{j}\rangle
means that there is a permutation
\pi
of
\{0,\dots,n\}
and an element
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}
such that
r^{+}(e_{i})=\lambda e_{\pi(i)}
for all
i
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
n\geqslant 2
, and
r
is a reflection on
V
which preserves the set of lines
\langle e_{i}-e_{j}\rangle
for
i\neq j
. Let
\pi,\lambda
be as defined above. Then one of the following occurs.

1.

\lambda=1
and
\pi
is a transposition
(i\ j)
.

2.

n=2
,
\lambda=-1
and
\pi
is a transposition
(i\ j)
.

3.

n=3
,
\lambda=-1
and
\pi
is a double transposition
(i\ j)(k\ l)
.

4.

n=2
,
\lambda
is a primitive cube root of unity in
\mathbb{F}
and
\pi
is a
3
-cycle
(i\ j\ k)
.

Recall that for a reflection
r
on
V
the space
\left\{\left.(r-1)v\ \right|\ \smash{v\in V}\right\}
is
1
-dimensional.

First we suppose that
\pi
is the identity permutation. In this case each of the vectors
e_{i}-e_{j}
for
i<j
is an eigenvector for
r
, with eigenvalue
\lambda
. So
r
is a scalar multiple of the identity map on
V
, so is not a reflection, a contradiction.

So
\pi
is a non-trivial permutation. Suppose that
(i_{1}\ \dots\ i_{a})
is a cycle of
\pi
with
2\leqslant a\leqslant n
. If
a\geqslant 4
, then the vectors
(r-1)(e_{i_{1}}-e_{i_{2}})
and
(r-1)(e_{i_{2}}-e_{i_{3}})
are linearly independent, so that
r
is not a reflection. So every cycle of
\pi
has length at most
3
.

Suppose
\pi
has a cycle
(i\ j\ k)
of length
3
. Then
n=2
, since otherwise we can choose a fourth integer
l\neq i,j,k
, and then the vectors
(r-1)(e_{i}-e_{l})
and
(r-1)(e_{j}-e_{l})
are linearly independent, so
r
is not a reflection. Now the eigenvalues of
r
are
\omega\lambda
and
\omega^{2}\lambda
, where
\omega
is a primitive cube root of unity in
\mathbb{F}
, forcing
\lambda=\omega
or
\omega^{2}
.

Now we assume that every cycle of
\pi
has length
1
or
2
. Suppose there are two cycles of length
2
, say
(i\ j)
and
(k\ l)
. Then
r
has linearly independent eigenvectors
e_{i}-e_{j}
and
e_{k}-e_{l}
with eigenvalue
-\lambda
, forcing
\lambda=-1
. Now we claim that
n=3
: if not, let
m\in\{0,\dots,n\}
be distinct from
i,j,k,l
. Then the vectors
(r-1)(e_{i}-e_{m})
and
(r-1)(e_{k}-e_{m})
are linearly independent, so that
r
is not a reflection.

We are left with the case where
\pi
is a transposition
(i\ j)
. Now the eigenvalues of
r
are
-\lambda
(occurring once) and
\lambda
(occurring
n-1
times). We deduce that either
\lambda=1
, or
\lambda=-1
and
n=2
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

The normalising reflection group of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is:

1.

the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
, if
n\geqslant 4
, or if
n=3
and
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
;

2.

the hyperoctahedral group
\mathfrak{S}_{4}\times C_{2}
, if
n=3
and
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
;

3.

the dihedral group
D_{12}
, if
n=2
,
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
and
\mathbb{F}
contains no primitive cube roots of
1
;

4.

the direct product
D_{12}\times C_{3}
, if
n=2
,
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
and
\mathbb{F}
contains primitive cube roots of
1
;

5.

the direct product
\mathfrak{S}_{3}\times C_{2}
if
n=2
,
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
and
\mathbb{F}
contains primitive cube roots of
1
;

6.

the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
, if
n=2
,
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
and
\mathbb{F}
does not contain primitive cube roots of
1
.

Remarks
.

1.

Note that when
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, the group
D_{12}\times C_{3}
appearing in (
4
) is the complex reflection group of type
G(6,2,2)
.

2.

In the case when
n=3
and
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
, the hyperoctahedral group
\mathfrak{S}_{4}\times C_{2}
does not have a natural faithful action. The reflections described in part (
3
) of
3.3
corresponding to double transpositions still exist, but they now lie inside the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{4}
. Effectively, passing to characteristic
2
replaces the hyperoctahedral group with the quotient
\mathfrak{S}_{4}
obtained by quotienting out the longest element.

Now we can give the example promised in
Section

2.2
showing that in a completely reducible semireflection monoid the reflection subgroup need not be completely reducible. Suppose
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic
3
, and consider the projection monoid
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. By
3.4
this is normalised by the reflection subgroup
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
(permuting the vectors
e_{0},e_{1},e_{2}
). Let
M
be the semireflection monoid
\mathcal{A}_{2}\mathfrak{S}_{3}
. This is irreducible (because
\mathcal{A}_{2}
is); but
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
does not act completely reducibly on
V
(the subspace spanned by
e_{0}+e_{1}+e_{2}
is the unique proper non-zero invariant subspace).

3.1.3
Minimal generating sets

In the theory of reflection groups it is an important task to consider minimal generating sets of reflections. Here we describe the minimal sets of projections that generate
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. We do this using results of Howie on minimal generating sets of idempotents for the full transformation semigroup.

We begin with the extended monoid
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
on
V^{+}
described above. As we have explained, the semigroup consisting of the non-invertible elements is isomorphic to the semigroup
S
of non-bijective functions
\{0,\dots,n\}\to\{0,\dots,n\}
, with the projections
p^{+}_{ij}
corresponding to the idempotents of defect
1
. Write
f_{ij}
for the function corresponding to
p^{+}_{ij}
. Given a set
X
of ordered pairs
(i,j)
, we can construct a directed graph on
\{0,\dots,n\}
by drawing an arrow
i\to j
if and only if
(i,j)\in X
. Howie
[
H2
]
shows that if
n\geqslant 2
then
\left\{\left.f_{ij}\ \right|\ \smash{(i,j)\in X}\right\}
is a minimal generating set for
S
if and only if this graph is a strongly connected tournament (that is, for every pair
(i,j)
there is either an arrow
i\to j
or
j\to i
but not both, and there is a directed path from
i
to
j
). The classification of minimal generating sets of projections for
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
immediately follows: the set
\left\{\left.p_{ij}^{+}\ \right|\ \smash{(i,j)\in X}\right\}
is a minimal generating set if and only if the corresponding graph is a strongly connected tournament.

Now we consider
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. Recall that as a monoid
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is isomorphic to a quotient of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
. We claim that under this quotient map the minimal generating sets of projections in
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
correspond to minimal generating sets of projections in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. First we single out the special case where
n=\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
, which can be checked directly. So assume that either
n\geqslant 3
or
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. Clearly if a set of projections generates
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
, then corresponding set of projections generates
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. Conversely, take a generating set
P=\left\{\left.p_{ij}\ \right|\ \smash{(i,j)\in X}\right\}
of projections for
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, and consider the set
P^{+}=\left\{\left.p_{ij}^{+}\ \right|\ \smash{(i,j)\in X}\right\}
of projections in
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
. Given a projection
p_{ij}^{+}
, its restriction
p_{ij}
can be written as a product of elements of
P
. Let
p^{\prime}
be the corresponding product of elements of
P^{+}
. Then
p^{\prime}
and
p_{ij}^{+}
have the same restriction to
V
. From the proof of
3.2
, and because we are assuming that either
n\geqslant 3
or
n=2\neq\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})
, this means that
p^{\prime}=p_{ij}^{+}
. So every projection
p_{ij}^{+}
can be written as a product of elements of
P^{+}
, and therefore
P^{+}
generates
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
.

So generating sets of projections in
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
correspond to generating sets of projections in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, and therefore the same is true for minimal generating sets. As a consequence of this and Howie’s theorem, we deduce the following.

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose
n\geqslant 2
and
P\subseteq\left\{\left.p_{ij}\ \right|\ \smash{i\neq j}\right\}
is a set of projections in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. Then
P
is a minimal generating set for
\mathcal{A}_{n}
if and only if the corresponding directed graph on
\{0,\dots,n\}
is a strongly connected tournament.

3.2
Type
B

3.2.1
Definition, irreducibility and cardinality

Now we introduce our next family of examples. Take
n\geqslant 1
, and suppose
V
is a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
of dimension
n
. Choose a basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
for
V
, and a finite subgroup
Z\leqslant\mathbb{F}^{\times}
. Then
Z
is the set of
t
th roots of unity in
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
for some
t\geqslant 1
.

Given
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
p_{i}
be the projection with kernel
\langle e_{i}\rangle
and image
\left\langle\left.e_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{k\neq i}\right\rangle
. Given
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
with
i\neq j
and given
z\in Z
, define
p_{ij}^{z}
to be the projection with kernel
\langle e_{i}-ze_{j}\rangle
and image
\left\langle\left.e_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{k\neq i}\right\rangle
. Define
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
to be the monoid generated by the projections
p_{i}
and
p_{ij}^{z}
for all
i,j,z
. Then
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
is well-defined up to isomorphism.

Proposition 3.6
.

\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
is irreducible.

Suppose
W\leqslant V
is invariant for
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
; then we must show that
W=0
or
W=V
.

Suppose first that
W
contains one of the coordinate axes
\langle e_{i}\rangle
. Then
W
also contains
p_{ij}^{1}\langle e_{i}\rangle=\langle e_{j}\rangle
for every
j
. Hence
W=V
.

Alternatively, suppose
W
does not contain any of the lines
\langle e_{i}\rangle
. Then
Lemma

2.1
(applied to the projections
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
) shows that
W
is contained in all the hyperplanes
\left\langle\left.e_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{k\neq i}\right\rangle
, so that
W=0
.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
is finite, with

\lvert\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}\rvert=(nt+1)^{n}-t^{n}n!+1.

It is straightforward to write down the action of the generating projections on the vectors
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
:

p_{i}(e_{k})=\begin{cases}0&(k=i)\\
e_{k}&(k\neq i),\end{cases}\qquad p_{ij}^{z}(e_{k})=\begin{cases}ze_{j}&(k=i)\\
e_{k}&(k\neq i).\end{cases}

As a consequence, the (spanning) set
S=\left\{\left.ze_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{z\in Z,\ 1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\right\}\cup\{0\}
is mapped to itself by every element of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
, so
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
is finite.

To find
\lvert\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}\rvert
, we examine exactly which functions from
S
to
S
correspond to elements of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. Take a non-identity element
m\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. Then
m
is singular, which means that either
m(e_{i})=0
for some
i
, or
m(e_{i})=zm(e_{j})
for some
i\neq j
and
z\in Z
. Let
f:\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}\to S
be any function with this property; then we show that there is
m\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
extending
f
. Let
r(f)
be the number of values
k
such that
ze_{k}
lies in the image of
f
for some
z
; then our assumption on
f
is the same as saying that
r(f)\leqslant n-1
. Let
s(f)
be the number of values
k
such that
f(e_{k})=e_{k}
. Our proof proceeds by downwards induction on
r(f)
, and for fixed
r(f)
by downwards induction on
s(f)
.

First we deal with the inductive step where
r(f)<n-1
. Choose
i
such that no element
ze_{i}
is in the image of
f
. Define a new function
g:\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}\to S
by

g(e_{k})=\begin{cases}e_{i}&(k=i)\\
f(e_{k})&(k\neq i).\end{cases}

Then either
r(g)=r(f)+1
or
r(g)=r(f)
and
s(g)=s(f)+1
, so by induction
g
is the restriction to
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
of an element
m\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. Now consider
f(e_{i})
: if
f(e_{i})=0
, then
f
is the restriction of
p_{i}m
; alternatively, if
f(e_{i})=ze_{j}
for
j\neq i
, then
f
is the restriction of
p_{ij}^{z}m
.

Now we consider the case where
r(f)=n-1
. In this case there is a unique
i
such that no
ze_{i}
lies in the image of
f
. Now there are two possibilities.

1.

Suppose there is
j
such that
f(e_{j})=0
. Then there is a unique such
j
. We consider two subcases.

(a)

Suppose
i\neq j
. Then
f(e_{i})=ze_{l}
for some
l\neq i
and
z\in Z
. We define a new function
g
by

g(e_{k})=\begin{cases}e_{i}&(k=i)\\
f(e_{k})&(k\neq i).\end{cases}

Then
r(g)=n-1
and
s(g)=s(f)+1
, so by induction
g
is the restriction of an element
m\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. Now
f
is the restriction of
p_{il}^{z}m
.

(b)

Suppose
i=j
. Suppose there is
l\neq i
such that
f(e_{l})\neq e_{l}
. Write
f(e_{l})=ze_{m}
, and define a new function
g
by

g(e_{k})=\begin{cases}e_{i}&(k=l)\\
f(e_{k})&(k\neq l).\end{cases}

Then
r(g)=n-1
and
s(g)=s(f)
, and by subcase (
1a
)
g
is the restriction of some
m\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. So
f
is the restriction of
p_{im}^{-z}m
.

So we can assume that
f(e_{l})=e_{l}
for all
l\neq i
. Now
f
is the restriction of
p_{i}
.

2.

Suppose instead that there is no
j
for which
f(e_{j})=0
. Then there is a unique pair of integers
j\neq j^{\prime}
such that
f(e_{j})
is proportional to
f(e_{j^{\prime}})
. The approach in this case is similar to case (
1
). Again, we consider two subcases.

(a)

Suppose
i\neq j,j^{\prime}
. Then
f(e_{i})=ze_{l}
for some
l\neq i
and
z\in Z
, and there is no other
k
for which
f(e_{k})\in\langle e_{l}\rangle
. Now define a new function
g
by

g(e_{k})=\begin{cases}e_{i}&(k=i)\\
f(e_{k})&(k\neq i).\end{cases}

Then
r(g)=n-1
and
s(g)=s(f)+1
, so by induction
g
is the restriction of an element
m\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. Now
f
is the restriction of
p_{il}^{z}m
.

(b)

Now suppose that
i
equals one of
j
and
j^{\prime}
, say
i=j^{\prime}
. If there is
l\neq i,j
such that
f(e_{l})\neq e_{l}
and
f(e_{l})\notin\langle e_{j}\rangle
, then we can proceed as in case
1
(
1b
): we write
f(e_{l})=ze_{m}
, and define a new function
g
by

g(e_{k})=\begin{cases}e_{i}&(k=l)\\
f(e_{k})&(k\neq l).\end{cases}

Then
r(g)=n-1
and
s(g)=s(f)
, and by subcase (
2a
)
g
is the restriction of some
m\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. So
f
is the restriction of
p_{im}^{z}m
.

So we can assume that for every
k\neq i,j
either
f(e_{k})=e_{k}
or
f(e_{k})\in\langle e_{j}\rangle
. If in fact
f(e_{k})=e_{k}
for all
k\neq i,j
, then
f(e_{i})=ze_{j}
and
f(e_{j})=ye_{j}
for some
y,z\in Z
. Now
f
is the restriction of
p_{ij}^{z}p_{ji}^{yz^{-1}}
.

Finally, suppose there is
l\neq i,j
such that
f(e_{l})\in\langle e_{j}\rangle
, while
f(e_{k})=e_{k}
for
k\neq i,j,l
. Then there are
x,y,z\in Z
such that
f(e_{i})=ze_{l}
,
f(e_{j})=ye_{l}
,
f(e_{l})=xe_{j}
. Then
f
is the restriction of
p^{z}_{il}p^{x}_{lj}p^{yz^{-1}}_{ji}
.

We have proved our claim, which allows us to complete the calculation of
\lvert\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}\rvert
: there are
(nt+1)^{n}
functions from
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
to
S
. The non-invertible elements of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
correspond to those for which either
f(e_{i})=0
for some

, or

for some

and

. This rules out

functions, so there are
(nt+1)^{n}-t^{n}n!
non-invertible elements of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
, as required.
∎

3.2.2
Normalising reflections

Now we find the normalising reflection group of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. Let
Z^{+}
be the set of
(2t)
th roots of unity in
\mathbb{F}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Suppose
r
is a reflection on
V
. Then
r
normalises
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
if and only if one of the following occurs:

\diamond

there is
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\lambda\in Z\setminus\{1\}
such that

r(e_{i})=\lambda e_{i},\qquad r(e_{k})=e_{k}\text{ for $k\neq i$;}

\diamond

there are
i\neq j
and
\lambda\in Z^{+}
such that

r(e_{i})=\lambda e_{j},\qquad r(e_{j})=\lambda^{-1}e_{i},\qquad r(e_{k})=e_{k}\text{ for $k\neq i,j$.}

It is easy to check that the reflections of the given types normalise
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
, so the ‘if’ part is done. So we concentrate on the ‘only if’ part, and assume that
r
normalises
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. In particular,
r
fixes
\operatorname{Ker}(\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t})
and
\operatorname{Im}(\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t})
.

We assume that
n\geqslant 2
. We partition
\operatorname{Ker}(\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t})
into the following sets of lines:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left.\langle e_{i}\rangle\ \right|\ \smash{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left.\langle e_{i}-ze_{j}\rangle\ \right|\ \smash{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n,\ z\in Z}\right\}.

Then
r
preserves
\mathcal{L}
, since the lines in
\mathcal{L}
are precisely the lines which occur as intersections of
n-1
images of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. Let
\pi
be the permutation of
\{1,\dots,n\}
corresponding to the action of
r
on the lines
\langle e_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle e_{n}\rangle
.

If
\pi
is the identity permutation, then each
e_{i}
is an eigenvector for
r
. Since
r
is a reflection, this means that there is
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\lambda\neq 1
such that

r(e_{i})=\lambda e_{i},\qquad r(e_{k})=e_{k}\text{ for $k\neq i$.}

Now take
j\neq i
. Then
r
maps the line
\langle e_{i}+e_{j}\rangle
to the line
\langle\lambda e_{i}+e_{j}\rangle
. So the latter lies in
\mathcal{M}
, and therefore
\lambda\in Z
.

Now suppose
\pi
is not the identity, and let
(i_{1}\ \dots\ i_{a})
be a cycle of
\pi
, with
a\geqslant 2
. If
a\geqslant 3
, then the vectors
(r-1)e_{i_{1}}
and
(r-1)e_{i_{2}}
are linearly independent, contradicting the assumption that
r
is a reflection; so
a=2
. Moreover,
r(e_{j})=e_{j}
for every
j\neq i_{1},i_{2}
, since otherwise
(r-1)e_{i_{1}}
and
(r-1)e_{j}
are linearly independent. Now there are
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}
such that
r(e_{i_{1}})=\lambda_{1}e_{i_{2}}
and
r(e_{i_{2}})=\lambda_{2}e_{i_{1}}
. The assumption that
r
is a reflection then forces
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}^{-1}
. Now
r
maps the line
\langle e_{i_{1}}+e_{i_{2}}\rangle
to
\langle\lambda_{1}^{-1}e_{i_{1}}+\lambda_{1}e_{i_{2}}\rangle
. This line must lie in
\mathcal{M}
, so that
\lambda_{1}^{2}\in Z
, and therefore
\lambda_{1}\in Z^{+}
.
∎

This allows us to describe the normalising reflection group of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. If we write each element as a matrix with respect to the basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
, then the matrices obtained are precisely those for which

\diamond

each row and each column contains exactly one non-zero entry, which is a
2t
th root of unity;

\diamond

the product of the non-zero entries is a
t
th root of unity.

If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, then this is precisely the complex reflection group of type
G(2t,2,n)
. In general, the normalising reflection group has order
2^{n-1}t^{n}n!
if
\mathbb{F}
contains primitive
2t
th roots of unity, or
t^{n}n!
otherwise.

3.2.3
Minimal generating sets

In this section we classify the minimal generating sets of projections in
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. The end result is similar to
3.5
, though here we cannot rely on an external result.

Suppose we have a set
P
of projections in
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. We draw a directed graph
G_{P}
on
\{1,\dots,n\}
(where we allow multiple arrows) by drawing an arrow
i\to j
for each
z\in Z
such that
p_{ij}^{z}\in P
. Now we have the following result.

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose
P
is a set of projections in
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
, and draw the graph
G_{P}
as above. Then
P
generates
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
if and only if the following conditions are all satisfied:

\diamond

p_{i}\in P
for all
i
;

\diamond

for each
i\neq j
and each
z\in Z
at least one of
p_{ij}^{z}
and
p_{ji}^{z^{-1}}
lies in
P
;

\diamond

G_{P}
is strongly connected.

Suppose first that
P
satisfies the three given conditions. Then we must show that the monoid generated by
P
contains every projection in
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. For the projections
p_{i}
this is given, so take a projection
p_{ij}^{z}
, and suppose
p_{ij}^{z^{-1}}\in P
. Take a directed path
i_{0}\to\dots\to i_{r}
in
G_{P}
, with
i_{0}=i
and
i_{r}=j
. Then there are
z_{1},\dots,z_{r}\in Z
such that the projection
p(a)=p_{i_{a-1}i_{a}}^{z_{a}}
lies in
P
for
a=1,\dots,r
. Now consider the composition

q=p(1)\dots p(r)p_{ji}^{z^{-1}}.

This has rank
n-1
, since each projection has rank
n-1
, and
\ker(p(a))\nleqslant\operatorname{im}(p(a+1))
for each
a
, and similarly
\ker(p(r))\nleqslant\operatorname{im}(p_{ji}^{z^{-1}})
. So

\ker(q)=\ker(p_{ji}^{z^{-1}})=\ker(p_{ij}^{z}),\qquad\operatorname{im}(q)=\operatorname{im}(p(1))=\operatorname{im}(\ker(p_{ij}^{z}).

Since
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
is finite, some power of
q
equals
p_{ij}^{z}
, so
p_{ij}^{z}
lies in the monoid generated by
P
.

Conversely, suppose
P
generates
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. Then every kernel of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
must occur as the kernel of some element of
P
. To see this, take a projection
p\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
, and write
p=p(1)\dots p(r)
, where
p(1),\dots,p(r)\in P
. Then
\ker(p)\geqslant\ker(p(r))
; but both kernels have dimension
1
, giving
\ker(p)=\ker(p(r))
, so
\ker(p)
is the kernel of an element of
P
.

In particular this means that the projections
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
all lie in
P
, since
p_{i}
is the only projection in
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
with kernel
\langle e_{i}\rangle
. Furthermore, given
i\neq j
and
z\in Z
, the only projections in
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
with kernel
\langle e_{i}-ze_{j}\rangle
are
p_{ij}^{z}
and
p_{ji}^{z^{-1}}
, so at least one of these lies in
P
.

It remains to show that
G_{P}
is strongly connected. So take
i\neq j
; then we will show that there is a directed path from
i
to
j
in
G_{P}
. Because
P
generates
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
, the projection
p_{ij}^{1}
can be written as a product
p(1)\dots p(r)
, where
p(1),\dots,p(r)\in P
. Now define an
ij
-tuple
to be a tuple
(p(1),\dots,p(r))
of elements of
P
such that
\ker(p(1)\dots p(r))=\ker(p_{ij}^{1})
and
\operatorname{im}(p(1)\dots p(r))=\operatorname{im}(p_{ij}^{1})
. (This is equivalent to saying that
\operatorname{im}(p(1))=\operatorname{im}(p_{ij}^{1})
,
\ker(p(r))=\ker(p_{ij}^{1})
, and
\ker(p(a))\nleqslant\operatorname{im}(p(a+1))
for
1\leqslant a<r
.)

Choose an
ij
-tuple
(p(1),\dots,p(r))
with
r
as small as possible. Then we claim that none of the
p(a)
can be of the form
p_{k}
. To see this, first note that
p(r)\neq p_{k}
since
\ker(p(r))=\langle e_{i}-e_{j}\rangle\neq\ker(p_{k})
. Next observe that if
a<r
and
p(a)=p_{k}
, then
\ker(p(a))=\langle e_{k}\rangle
, so that
\operatorname{im}(p(a+1))
must equal
\left\langle\left.e_{l}\ \right|\ \smash{l\neq k}\right\rangle=\operatorname{im}(p(a))
; but then
(p(1),\dots,p(a-1),p(a+1),\dots,p(r))
is also an
ij
-tuple, contradicting minimality.

So each of
p(1),\dots,p(r)
is one of the projections
p_{st}^{z}
. Taking
1\leqslant a<r
and writing
p(a)=p_{st}^{z}
and
p(a+1)=p_{uv}^{y}
, the fact that
\ker(p(a))\nleqslant\operatorname{im}(p(a+1))
gives
u=s
or
u=t
. But if
u=s
then
\operatorname{im}(p(a))=\operatorname{im}(p(a+1))
and again minimality is contradicted.

So we can conclude that there are
i_{0},\dots,i_{r}
with
i_{a-1}\neq i_{a}
for each
a
, and
z_{1},\dots,z_{r}\in Z
such that
p(a)=p_{i_{a-1}i_{a}}^{z_{a}}
for each
a
. The fact that
\operatorname{im}(p(1))=\operatorname{im}(p_{ij}^{1})
is the same as saying
i_{0}=i
, while the fact that
\ker(p(r))=\ker(p_{ij}^{1})
gives
\{i_{r-1},i_{r}\}=\{i,j\}
. So we have a directed path
i\to i_{1}\to\dots\to i_{r}
in
G_{P}
, with either
i_{r-1}
or
i_{r}
equal to
j
, and we are done.
∎

Having classified generating sets of projections in
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
, we need to find which of these are minimal. For this, we need a little more graph theory. We do not claim originality for the following
Lemma
, but it is easier to find a proof than to find a reference.

Lemma 3.10
.

Suppose
n\geqslant 3
and
G
is a strongly connected directed graph on
\{1,\dots,n\}
with at least one arrow
i\to j
or
j\to i
for all
i\neq j
. If for given
i,j
there are arrows
i\to j
and
j\to i
, then one of these arrows can be deleted with the resulting graph still being strongly connected.

Suppose there is a pair
i,j
for which this is not true; then there must be a unique arrow
i\to j
and a unique arrow
j\to i
in
G
, and every directed path from
i
to
j
involves the arrow
i\to j
, and every directed path from
j
to
i
involves the arrow
j\to i
. This implies in particular that if
k\neq i,j
then there cannot be arrows
i\to k\to j
, and there cannot be arrows
j\to k\to i
.

So the set
\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i,j\}
can be partitioned as
K\sqcup L
, where for every
k\in K
there are arrows
i\to k
and
j\to k
but no arrows
k\to i
or
k\to j
, while for every
l\in L
there are arrows
l\to i
and
l\to j
, but no arrows
i\to l
or
j\to l
.

Since
n\geqslant 3
, at least one of
K
and
L
is non-empty; without loss of generality suppose
K
is non-empty. Since
G
is strongly connected, there must be an arrow
k\to l
for some
k\in K
and
l\notin K
. But then
l\in L
, and there are arrows
i\to k\to l\to j
, contrary to hypothesis.
∎

Now we can classify minimal generating sets when
n\geqslant 3
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Suppose
n\geqslant 3
and
P
is a set of projections in
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
, and draw the graph
G_{P}
as above. Then
P
is a minimal generating set for
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
if and only if:

\diamond

p_{i}\in P
for all
i
;

\diamond

for each
i\neq j
and each
z\in Z
exactly one of
p_{ij}^{z}
and
p_{ji}^{z^{-1}}
lies in
P
;

\diamond

G_{P}
is strongly connected.

Suppose
P
satisfies the three given conditions. Then by
Lemma

3.9

P
is a generating set for
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
. Furthermore,
P
is minimal, since if any element of
P
is deleted, then (again by
Lemma

3.9
) the resulting set does not generate
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
.

Conversely, suppose
P
is a generating set but does not satisfy all three given conditions. Then there must be some
i,j,z
such that both
p_{ij}^{z}
and
p_{ji}^{z^{-1}}
lie in
P
. Now we claim that we can remove one of
p_{ij}^{z}
and
p_{ji}^{z^{-1}}
from
P
with the resulting set still being a generating set. The graph
G_{P}
satisfies the hypotheses of
Lemma

3.10
, and includes arrows
i\to j
and
j\to i
. So by
Lemma

3.10
one of these arrows, say an arrow from
i\to j
, can be deleted from

with the resulting graph
G_{P}^{-}
still being strongly connected. Now let
Q=P\setminus\{p_{ij}^{z}\}
. Then
G_{Q}=G_{P}^{-}
is strongly connected, so that
Q
is a generating set and

is not minimal.
∎

4
Classification of irreducible projection monoids on
\mathbb{C}^{2}

Our aim in this section is to classify finite irreducible projection monoids on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. In fact, our results yield a classification over any field of characteristic zero, but for simplicity we will work entirely with
\mathbb{C}
from
Section

4.2
onwards.

4.1
The trace group

In this subsection we work over an arbitrary field
\mathbb{F}
. Suppose
M
is a projection monoid on
\mathbb{F}^{2}
, take two non-identity elements
p,q\in M
, and suppose
\operatorname{im}(p)\neq\ker(q)
. Then
pq
equals
\operatorname{Tr}(pq)
times the projection
\mathrm{prj}(\ker(q),\operatorname{im}(p))
. So if
a\in\mathbb{N}
, then
(pq)^{a}
equals
\operatorname{Tr}(pq)^{a}
times
\mathrm{prj}(\ker(q),\operatorname{im}(p))
. Since
M
is finite, this means that
\operatorname{Tr}(pq)
is zero or a root of unity in
\mathbb{F}
; we will call this the “trace condition”. If we define the
trace group
of
M
to be the group
G
generated by all the non-zero traces of the non-invertible elements of
M
, then
G
is a finite subgroup of
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
.

The next result shows how the trace group enables us to find the cardinality of an irreducible projection monoid on
\mathbb{F}^{2}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
M
is an irreducible projection monoid on
\mathbb{F}^{2}
. Then the number of non-zero elements in
M
is
kl\lvert G\rvert+1
, where
k=\lvert\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert
,
l=\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\rvert
and
G
is the trace group of
M
. Furthermore,
0
is an element of
M
if and only if
\operatorname{Ker}(M)\cap\operatorname{Im}(M)\neq\emptyset
.

We prove two claims.

Claim 1.

If
K\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
and
L\in\operatorname{Im}(M)
, then there is an element of
M
with kernel
K
and image
L
.

Proof.

In the case where
K\neq L
this follows from
2.2
:
M
must contain
\mathrm{prj}(K,L)
. Even in the case where
K=L
, the argument from the proof of
2.2
still works to show the existence of an element with kernel and image both equal to
K
.

Claim 2.

If
m
is a non-zero non-invertible element of
M
and
g\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}
, then
gm\in M
if and only if
g\in G
.

Proof.

For each projection
p\in M
, let
G_{p}=\left\{\left.g\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}\ \right|\ \smash{gp\in M}\right\}
. Then
G_{p}\leqslant G
. Since any non-invertible element of
M
with non-zero trace
t
equals
t
times a projection, the trace group
G
is the union of the groups
G_{p}
for all projections
p\in M
.

To prove the claim, first consider the case where
m
is a projection; then we want to show that
G_{m}=G
. If
G_{m}<G
, then there is an element
g\in G\setminus G_{m}
and another projection
p
such that
g\in G_{p}
. Because
M
is irreducible, it has at least two images and at least two kernels, so we can choose a kernel
K\neq\operatorname{im}(p)
and an image
L\neq\ker(m)
. By Claim 1 we can find an element
r\in M
with kernel
K
and image
L
. Similarly, we can find a non-zero element
s
with
\ker(s)\neq\operatorname{im}(m)
and
\operatorname{im}(s)\neq\ker(p)
. This means that the element
mrpsm
is non-zero, and hence has image
\operatorname{im}(m)
and kernel
\ker(m)
, so equals
hm
for some
h\in G_{m}
. Additionally,
M
contains the element
mr(gp)sm
, which equals
ghm
. But then
gh\in G_{m}
, so that
g\in G_{m}
, a contradiction. So
G_{m}=G
.

Next consider the case where
m
is a scalar multiple of a projection; say
m=gm^{\prime}
for some projection
m^{\prime}\in M
. The argument in the paragraph shows that the claim holds for
m^{\prime}
, which immediately shows that it holds for
m
.

Finally consider the case where
m
is not a scalar multiple of a projection; this means that
\operatorname{im}(m)=\ker(m)
. Let
u\in M
be any projection with
\operatorname{im}(u)=\operatorname{im}(m)
, and let
v\in M
be a projection with
\ker(v)\neq\operatorname{im}(m)\neq\operatorname{im}(v)
. Then
um
is a non-zero scalar multiple of
m
. More generally,
gum
is a non-zero scalar multiple of
m
for any
g\in G
, so there are at least
\lvert G\rvert
different multiples of
m
in
M
. On the other hand, if
g\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}
with
gm\in M
, then
gmv
is a scalar multiple of the projection
r=\mathrm{prj}(\ker(v),\operatorname{im}(m))
. So (because the claim holds with
r
in place of
m
) there are most
\lvert G\rvert
scalar multiples of
m
in
M
. So equality holds, and the scalar multiples of
m
in
M
are precisely the elements
gum
for
g\in G
. One of these is
m
itself, and therefore these scalar multiples are precisely the elements
gm
for
g\in G
.

Now we use these two claims to prove the first statement in the
Section
. Suppose
m
is a non-zero non-invertible element of
M
. Then
M
has rank
1
, so
\ker(m)
and
\operatorname{im}(m)
are both
1
-dimensional, and
\ker(m)\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
and
\operatorname{im}(m)\in\operatorname{Im}(M)
. On the other hand, given
K\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
and
L\in\operatorname{Im}(M)
, Claims 1 and 2 show that there are exactly
\lvert G\rvert
elements of
M
with kernel
K
and image
L
. So there are
kl\lvert G\rvert
non-zero non-invertible elements in
M
, which gives the result.

The second statement in the
Section
is straightforward to prove: suppose
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
are projections in
M
. Then
p_{1}\dots p_{r}=0
if and only if
\ker(p_{i})=\operatorname{im}(p_{i+1})
for some
i
. So it possible to write
0
as a product of projections in
M
if and only if
\operatorname{Ker}(M)\cap\operatorname{Im}(M)\neq\emptyset
.
∎

The next result makes it much easier to determine the trace group. Recall the projection monoid
\mathcal{A}_{2}
from
Section

3.1
; this can be characterised as the unique (up to equivalence) complete projection monoid
M
with
\operatorname{Ker}(M)=\operatorname{Im}(M)
and
\lvert\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert=3
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
M
is a finite irreducible projection monoid on
\mathbb{F}^{2}
, and let
P
be the set of projections in
M
. If
M
is not equivalent to
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, then the trace group of
M
is generated by the non-zero elements of the set

\left\{\left.\operatorname{Tr}(pq)\ \right|\ \smash{p,q\in P}\right\}.

In this proof we use the following notation. Given
p,q\in P
with
\operatorname{im}(p)\neq\ker(q)
, we write
(p|q)
for the projection
\mathrm{prj}(\ker(q),\operatorname{im}(p))
. By
2.2

M
is complete, and therefore
(p|q)\in P
. Moreover,
pq=\operatorname{Tr}(pq)(p|q)
.

Let
S
be the set given in the
Section
. Since any element of
M
can be written as a product of projections, it suffices to show that any non-zero element
tuv
with
t,u,v\in P
can be written in the form
xrs
, where
r,s\in P
and
x
is a product of elements of
S
.

The assumption that
t,u,v
are projections and that
tuv\neq 0
immediately gives

\operatorname{im}(t)\neq\ker(t)\neq\operatorname{im}(u)\neq\ker(u)\neq\operatorname{im}(v)\neq\ker(v).

First suppose
\operatorname{im}(t)\neq\ker(u)
. Then
tuv
equals
\operatorname{Tr}(tu)(t|u)v
, which is what we want. Similarly, if
\operatorname{im}(u)\neq\ker(v)
, then
tuv
equals
\operatorname{Tr}(uv)t(u|v)
, which is what we want.

We are left with the case where
\operatorname{im}(t)=\ker(u)
and
\operatorname{im}(u)=\ker(v)
. Now we claim that either:

(a)

there is an image
L
of
M
which is different from
\operatorname{im}(t),\ker(t),\operatorname{im}(u)
, or

(b)

there is a kernel
K
of
M
which is different from
\ker(u),\operatorname{im}(v),\ker(v)
.

To see this, first suppose that
\ker(t)\neq\operatorname{im}(v)
. Then (a) is satisfied with
L=\operatorname{im}(v)
. Alternatively, if
\ker(t)=\operatorname{im}(v)
, then we have three different lines
\operatorname{im}(t),\ker(t),\operatorname{im}(u)
each of which is both a kernel and an image of
M
. The assumption that
M
is not equivalent to
\mathcal{A}_{2}
means that there is a fourth line
L
which is either a kernel or an image of
M
, and so either (a) or (b) is true.

We assume without loss of generality that (a) is true, and let
r
be the projection with image
L
and kernel
\ker(t)
, and
s
the projection with image
L
and kernel
\ker(v)
. Then
tr=t
because
t
and
r
have the same kernel, so that

tuv=truv=\operatorname{Tr}(ru)t(r|u)v=\operatorname{Tr}(ru)\operatorname{Tr}((r|u)v)ts,

which is what we want.
∎

We remark that the exception in
4.2
for monoids equivalent to
\mathcal{A}_{2}
is necessary if
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
: if
p,q\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
are projections, then
\operatorname{Tr}(pq)\in\{0,1\}
, but in fact
\mathcal{A}_{2}
has trace group
\{\pm 1\}
.

4.2
Some examples in
\mathbb{C}^{2}

For the rest of
Section

4
we concentrate on the case
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. In this subsection we describe the projection monoids which will appear in the classification. Each projection monoid
M
described will be complete, which means that we can specify it by giving
\operatorname{Ker}(M)
and
\operatorname{Im}(M)
. In each case we also give the trace group (which will yield
\lvert M\rvert
via
4.1
) and the normalising reflection group of
M
. We also make comments about duality.

We will identify linear endomorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with
2\times 2
matrices (acting on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
on the left). Given a non-zero vector
\binom{x}{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, we’ll write
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x}{y}
for the line it spans.

Projection monoids with only two images

First, let
S
be a finite set of roots of unity in
\mathbb{C}
such that
1\in S
, and choose
i\in\{0,1,2\}
such that
i+\lvert S\rvert\geqslant 2
. Define the complete projection monoids
X_{S}^{(i)}
as follows.

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hline\cr i&\operatorname{Ker}(X_{S}^{(i)})&\operatorname{Im}(X_{S}^{(i)})\\
\hline\cr\\[-10.0pt]
0&\left\{\left.\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{s}\ \right|\ \smash{s\in S}\right\}&\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}\right\}\\[8.0pt]
1&\left\{\left.\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{s}\ \right|\ \smash{s\in S}\right\}\cup\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}\right\}&\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}\right\}\\[8.0pt]
2&\left\{\left.\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{s}\ \right|\ \smash{s\in S}\right\}\cup\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}\right\}&\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}\right\}\\[6.0pt]
\hline\cr\end{array}

Note that in the case where
S
is the group of all
2m
th roots of unity,
X_{S}^{(2)}
is just the monoid
\mathcal{B}_{2}^{m}
from
Section

3.2
.

It is easy to see from
Lemma

2.1
(with the assumption that
i+\lvert S\rvert\geqslant 2
) that
X_{S}^{(i)}
is irreducible.

Proposition 4.3
.

The trace group of
X_{S}^{(i)}
is the subgroup of
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
generated by
S
, and

\lvert X_{S}^{(i)}\rvert=\begin{cases}2\lvert S\rvert\lvert G\rvert+1&(i=0)\\
2(\lvert S\rvert+1)\lvert G\rvert+2&(i=1)\\
2(\lvert S\rvert+2)\lvert G\rvert+2&(i=2).\end{cases}

To find the trace group, we exploit
4.2
. The projections in
X_{S}^{(i)}
are of the form

\begin{pmatrix}1&-s^{-1}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
-s&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

for
s\in S
, and we can check directly that the trace of any product of two matrices of this form is
0
or
st^{-1}
for some
s,t\in S
. Since
1\in S
by assumption, the non-zero traces generate the same subgroup of
\mathbb{C}
as
S
does.

Now the second part of the
Section
follows from
4.1
.
∎

To find the normalising reflection group of
X_{S}^{(i)}
, we introduce the group

G_{S}=\left\{\left.g\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\ \right|\ \smash{gs\in S\text{ for all }s\in S}\right\}.

G_{S}
is then the group of
m
th roots of unity in
\mathbb{C}
, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

1.

If
i=0
or
2
and there is
t\in\mathbb{C}
such that
t^{2}s^{-1}\in S
for all
s\in S
, then the normalising reflection group of
X_{S}^{(i)}
is

\left\{\left.\begin{pmatrix}g&0\\
0&h\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}tg&0\\
0&t^{-1}h\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&g\\
h&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&tg\\
t^{-1}h&0\end{pmatrix}\ \right|\ \smash{g,h\in G_{S}}\right\}.

This is conjugate to the complex reflection group of type
G(2m,2,2)
.

2.

If
i=1
or there is no such
t
, then the normalising reflection group of
X_{S}^{(i)}
is

\left\{\left.\begin{pmatrix}g&0\\
0&h\end{pmatrix}\ \right|\ \smash{g,h\in G_{S}}\right\},

which is isomorphic to
C_{m}\times C_{m}
.

Any normalising reflection
r
of
X_{S}^{(i)}
must permute the images and permute the kernels of
X_{S}^{(i)}
. For
i=1
this means that
r
fixes each of the lines
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
, so acts via a diagonal matrix. The fact that
r
is a reflection that permutes the lines
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{s}
then means that the diagonal entries must be
1
and
g
, for some
g\in G_{S}
. These reflections generate the group in (
2
).

For
i=0,2
the normalising reflection group may additionally include reflections that interchange the two lines
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
. These act via matrices of the form

\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\
x^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},

and the condition that such a reflection permutes the lines
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{s}
gives
x^{2}s^{-1}\in S
for all
s\in S
. This can happen only if
t
exists as in the statement of the
Section
, and
x\in tG_{S}
. In this case, these reflections, together with the reflections given by diagonal matrices, give the group in (
1
).
∎

We can ask when two of the monoids
X_{S}^{(i)}
are equivalent: clearly if
X_{S}^{(i)}
and
X_{T}^{(j)}
are equivalent, then
i=j
(because
i
is the number of images of
X_{S}^{(i)}
which are also kernels, and this number is invariant under equivalence). Now equivalences come in two different types.

\diamond

Take
u\in S
and let
T=\left\{\left.su^{-1}\ \right|\ \smash{s\in S}\right\}
. Then
X_{S}^{(i)}
is equivalent to
X_{T}^{(i)}
(by rescaling the first coordinate by
u
).

\diamond

Take
u\in S
and let
T=\left\{\left.s^{-1}u\ \right|\ \smash{s\in S}\right\}
. If
i=0
or
2
, then
X_{S}^{(i)}
is equivalent to
X_{T}^{(i)}
(by interchanging the two coordinates and rescaling one coordinate by
u
).

We can also consider duality. We will see in
Section

4.3
that the monoids
X_{S}^{(i)}
are the only irreducible finite projection monoids with exactly two images, up to equivalence. As a consequence, their duals are the only irreducible finite projection monoids with exactly two kernels. In the cases where
X_{S}^{(i)}
has exactly two kernels,
X_{S}^{(i)}
is in fact self-dual. There are two cases to consider.

\diamond

Suppose
i=0
and
S=\{1,s\}
. Then the linear map given by
\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
s&-1\end{pmatrix}
interchanges the two images
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
with the two kernels
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{s}
, giving an equivalence between
X_{S}^{(0)}
and its dual;

\diamond

Suppose
i=1
and
S=\{1\}
. Then the linear map given by
\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
interchanges the two images
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
and with the two kernels
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
, giving an equivalence between
X_{S}^{(1)}
and its dual.

A one-parameter family

Now we describe a one-parameter family of projection monoids with three images and three kernels. Suppose
w\neq\pm 1
is a root of unity in
\mathbb{C}
, and let
Y_{w}
be the projection monoid with

\operatorname{Im}(Y_{w})=\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1+w}\right\},\qquad\operatorname{Ker}(Y_{w})=\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-w}{1}\right\}.

It is immediate from
Lemma

2.1
that
Y_{w}
is irreducible.

First we find the trace group and the normalising reflection group of
Y_{w}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose
w\neq\pm 1
is a root of unity in
\mathbb{C}
, and let
G
be the subgroup of
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
generated by
w
and
-w
. Then
Y_{w}
has trace group
G
, and order
9\lvert G\rvert+2
.

We use
4.2
.
Y_{w}
contains eight projections, and it is routine to check that the traces of products of pairs of these projections are
0,1,\pm w^{\pm 1},w^{\pm 2}
. So the non-zero traces generate
G
. Now the order of
Y_{w}
follows from
4.1
.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

The normalising reflection group of
Y_{w}
has order
2
, and is generated by the reflection

\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{w}$}}\begin{pmatrix}1&w-1\\
w+1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Any normalising reflection permutes the kernels of
Y_{w}
and the images of
Y_{w}
, so in particular fixes the line
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1}
. It is a routine exercise to check that the only reflection that accomplishes this is the one given.
∎

Now we consider equivalences between the monoids
Y_{w}
and their duals.

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose
w,x\neq\pm 1
are roots of unity in
\mathbb{C}
.

1.

Y_{w}
is equivalent to
Y_{x}
if and only if
w=x^{\pm 1}
.

2.

Y_{w}
is equivalent to the dual of
Y_{x}
if and only if
w=-x^{\pm 1}
.

1.

Suppose we have a linear map
a:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
which yields an equivalence between
Y_{w}
and
Y_{x}
. Then
a
fixes the line
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1}
, sends the two lines
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1+w}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1+x}
in some order, and sends the lines
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-w}{1}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-x}{1}
in some order. This gives four cases:

(a)

a
fixes the two lines
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
. Then
a
is just a scalar multiple of the identity, so fixes the two lines
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1+w}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-w}{1}
, so that
x=w
.

(b)

a
fixes the line
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
and sends
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-w}{1}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
. Then (up to scaling)
a
is given by
\begin{pmatrix}1&w-1\\
0&w\end{pmatrix}
, so sends
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-w^{-1}}{1}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1+w}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1+w^{-1}}
. So
x=w^{-1}
.

(c)

a
fixes the line
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
and sends
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1+w}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
. Then (up to scaling)
a
is given by
\begin{pmatrix}w&0\\
w+1&-1\end{pmatrix}
, so sends
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1+w^{-1}}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-w}{1}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-w^{-1}}{1}
. So again
x=w^{-1}
.

(d)

a
sends
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1+w}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-w}{1}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
. Then (up to scaling)
a
is given by
\begin{pmatrix}1&w-1\\
w+1&-1\end{pmatrix}
, so sends
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1+w}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
to
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-w}{1}
, so that
x=w
.

2.

First we note that the map
a=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
yields an equivalence from
Y_{w}
to the dual of
Y_{-w}
. Combining this with part (1) yields the result.
∎

Note that

from

. We can easily check that the trace group of

is

Proposition 4.8
.

\diamond

The normalising reflection group of

and of

is conjugate to the complex reflection group of type

, generated by the reflections

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

The normalising reflection group of

The normalising reflection group of

The normalising reflection group of

\diamond

The normalising reflection group of

, this means that our reflections must fix the set of lines

and

). For

, which is a routine exercise.
∎

4.3
Classification

In this section we show that the projection monoids in
Section

4.2
give all the finite irreducible projection monoids on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
up to equivalence and duality.

Theorem 4.9
.

Suppose
M
is an irreducible finite projection monoid on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Then
M
or its dual is equivalent to one of the projection monoids
X_{S}^{(i)}
,
Y_{w}
or
Z^{(i)}
.

The proof splits up into several propositions.

Proposition 4.10
.

Suppose
M
is a finite irreducible projection monoid on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\rvert=2
. Then
M
is equivalent to
X_{S}^{(i)}
for some
S,i
.

By replacing
M
with an equivalent projection monoid, we can assume that the two images of
M
are
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
. Then
M
must have a kernel different from
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
, since otherwise
M
is reducible. By rescaling one coordinate we can assume that one of the kernels is
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1}
. Now
M
contains the projection

\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

If
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{w}
is also a kernel with
w\neq 0
, then
M
also contains the projection

\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
-w&1\end{pmatrix}.

So
M
also contains

\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
-w&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}w&-1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

and the trace condition shows that
w
is a root of unity.

So

\operatorname{Ker}(M)=\left\{\left.\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{s}\ \right|\ \smash{s\in S}\right\}\cup T

for some finite set
S
of roots of unity with
1\in S
, and for some
T\subseteq\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}\right\}
. So
M=X_{S}^{(i)}
for some
i
, except in the case where
T=\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}\right\}
, in which case
M
is equivalent to
X_{\bar{S}}^{(1)}
(where
\bar{S}=\left\{\left.w^{-1}\ \right|\ \smash{w\in S}\right\}
).
∎

Now we can restriction attention to projection monoids with at least three images, and (by duality) at least three kernels.

Proposition 4.11
.

Suppose
M
is a finite projection monoid on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and that
a,b,c
are distinct elements of
\operatorname{Ker}(M)
, and
d,e,f
are distinct elements of
\operatorname{Im}(M)
. Then
\{a,b,c\}\cap\{d,e,f\}\neq\emptyset
.

Suppose for a contradiction that
a,b,c,d,e,f
are distinct. Without loss of generality we can assume that
a,b,c
are the lines
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{-1}
, and we write
d=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x}{1}
,
e=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{y}{1}
,
f=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{z}{1}
for distinct
x,y,z\neq 0,-1
. Now
M
contains the projections

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
x^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}0&y\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}\frac{x}{x+1}&\frac{x}{x+1}\\
\frac{1}{x+1}&\frac{1}{x+1}\end{pmatrix},

and the trace condition implies that
y/x
and
(y+1)/(x+1)
are both roots of unity. Similarly
z/x
and
(z+1)/(x+1)
are roots of unity. But this means in particular that
|x|=|y|=|z|
and
|x+1|=|y+1|=|z+1|
, which is impossible for three distinct complex numbers.
∎

Proposition 4.12
.

Suppose
M
is a finite projection monoid on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, with
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\rvert=\lvert\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert=3
and
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\cap\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert=1
. Then
M
is isomorphic to
Y_{w}
for some root of unity
w\neq\pm 1
.

Without loss of generality we assume that
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
, while
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1},\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}\in\operatorname{Im}(M)
. Write the remaining kernel as
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1-w}{1}
and the remaining image as
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{1-x}
, where
w,x\neq 0,1
. Then
M
contains the projections

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1-x&0\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}1&w-1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

and the trace condition shows that
w
,
x
and
w+x-wx
are roots of unity. The only way this can happen is if
x=-w
, which means in particular that
w\neq-1
, so
M=Y_{w}
.
∎

Now we consider the case where
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\cap\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert\geqslant 2
. We fix a primitive cube root of unity
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
.

Lemma 4.13
.

Suppose
M
is a finite projection monoid on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and that
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
are both images and kernels of
M
.

1.

Suppose
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-1}{1}
is a kernel of
M
. Then the only possible other images of
M
are
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-1}{1}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega}{1}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega^2}{1}
.

2.

Suppose
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-1}{1}
is an image of
M
. Then the only possible other kernels of
M
are
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-1}{1}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega}{1}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega^2}{1}
.

3.

Suppose
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega}{1}
is a kernel of
M
. Then the only possible other images of
M
are
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-1}{1}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega}{1}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-\omega^2}{1}
.

4.

Suppose
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega^2}{1}
is a kernel of
M
. Then the only possible other images of
M
are
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-1}{1}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega^2}{1}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-\omega}{1}
.

For (1), suppose
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x}{1}
is an image, with
x\neq 0,-1
. Then
M
contains the projections

\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}0&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
x^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}

and the trace condition implies that both
x+1
and
(x+1)/x
are roots of unity. The only way this can happen is if
x=\omega^{\pm 1}
.

(2) now follows by duality, and (3) and (4) are proved in a similar way.
∎

Now we are ready to prove our main result.

If
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\rvert
or
\lvert\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert
equals
2
, then by
4.10
either
M
or its dual is equivalent to
X_{S}^{(i)}
for some
S,i
. So assume that
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\rvert\geqslant 3
and
\lvert\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert\geqslant 3
.

If
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\cup\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert=3
, then
M
is equivalent to
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, so assume that
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\cup\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert\geqslant 4
.
4.11
shows that
\operatorname{Im}(M)\cap\operatorname{Ker}(M)\neq\emptyset
; in fact, if
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(M)
and
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(M)
with
\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{L}\rvert=3
, then
\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}\neq\emptyset
.
4.12
shows that if
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\cap\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert=1
(forcing
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\rvert=\lvert\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert=3
) then
M
is isomorphic to
Y_{w}
for some
w
. So assume
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\cap\operatorname{Ker}(M)\rvert\geqslant 2
. Then without loss of generality we can assume that
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
are both images and kernels of
M
, and that
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-1}{1}
is also an image. If there are no other images, then
Lemma

4.13
(2) shows that (up to equivalence)
M
is one of
Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},Z^{(2)},Z^{(3)}
. So we can assume that there are at least four images, and dually we can assume that there are at least four kernels. So at least two of
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-1}{1}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega}{1}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega^2}{1}
are kernels. But if
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega}{1}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega^2}{1}
are kernels, then
Lemma

4.13
(3,4) show that there can only be three images, a contradiction. So (without loss of generality) the images of
M
are
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-1}{1}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega}{1}
. Now
Lemma

4.13
(2,3) show that the kernels of
M
are also
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1}{0}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{1}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-1}{1}
,
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\omega}{1}
, so that
M=Z^{(4)}
.
∎

Remarks
.

1.

From
4.9
we can deduce a classification of
minimal
irreducible projection monoids on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
; that is, those with no irreducible projection submonoid. First we observe that an irreducible projection monoid
M
is minimal if and only if it has exactly two images and two kernels. The “if” part follows from
2.2
. For the “only if”, note that a complete projection monoid with exactly two kernels and two images is irreducible except in the case where the two kernels are the same as the two images. If
M
is irreducible and has at least three images or at least three kernels, then we can easily choose two kernels
k,l
and two images
s,t
such that
\{k,l\}\neq\{s,t\}
. Then the projection monoid with kernels
k,l
and images
s,t
is irreducible, so
M
is not minimal.

We can read off from
4.10
the irreducible projection monoids with exactly two images and two kernels; they are the monoids
X_{S}^{(0)}
with
\lvert S\rvert=2
, and
X_{\{1\}}^{(1)}
.

2.

We can also easily read off the self-dual irreducible projection monoids: these are

X_{S}^{(0)}\ (\text{for }\lvert S\rvert=2),\quad X_{\{1\}}^{(1)},\quad Y_{\mathrm{i}},\quad Z^{(0)},\ Z^{(1)},\ Z^{(5)}.

3.

From the classification over
\mathbb{C}
we can immediately deduce a classification over
\mathbb{R}
: if
M
is a finite irreducible projection monoid on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then
M
or its dual is equivalent to one of the monoids

X_{\{\pm 1\}}^{(0)},\ X_{\{1\}}^{(1)},\ X_{\{\pm 1\}}^{(1)},\ X_{\{1\}}^{(2)},\ X_{\{\pm 1\}}^{(2)},\ Z^{(0)},

of orders
9
,
6
,
14
,
8
,
18
,
20
respectively. The first five of these are all submonoids of
\mathcal{B}_{2}^{2}=X_{\{\pm 1\}}^{(2)}
, and the last is just
\mathcal{A}_{2}
.

5
Finite irreducible projection monoids on
\mathbb{R}^{3}

In this section we consider finite irreducible projection monoids on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In
Section

4
we saw that a finite irreducible projection monoid on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is (up to duality) equivalent to a submonoid of
\mathcal{A}_{2}
or
\mathcal{B}_{2}^{2}
. Here we prove the corresponding statement for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
M
is a finite irreducible projection monoid on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then
M
or its dual is equivalent to a submonoid of
\mathcal{A}_{3}
or
\mathcal{B}_{3}^{2}
.

Throughout this section we assume that
M
is a finite complete projection monoid on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the sum of the kernels of
M
is
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and the intersection of the images of
M
is
0
. We say that
M
is
split
if there are
K\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
and
A\in\operatorname{Im}(M)
such that all the images of
M
other than
A
contain
K
, and all the kernels of
M
other than
K
are contained in
A
.

We will view elements of
M
as
3\times 3
matrices acting on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
on the left. Given
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
b\\
c\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle
for the span of the vector
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
b\\
c\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

To begin with, we consider the case where
M
satisfies the following additional condition:

if
K\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
and
A,B,C\in\operatorname{Im}(M)
with
A\cap B\cap C=0
, then
K\subset A\cup B\cup C
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose
M
satisfies (*) and
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\rvert=3
. Then either
M
is split, or

M
is equivalent to a submonoid of
\mathcal{B}_{3}^{2}
.

By replacing
M
with an equivalent projection monoid, we can assume that the images of
M
are the three coordinate planes. Condition (*) says that every kernel of
M
is contained in at least one of these planes.

Let’s assume that
M
is not split. Then at least two of the following statements must be true:

\diamond

there is
a\neq 0
such that
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
a\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
;

\diamond

there is
b\neq 0
such that
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
;

\diamond

there is
c\neq 0
such that
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\\
c\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
.

By permuting the coordinates if necessary, we assume that the first two statements are true. By rescaling the coordinates if necessary, we can assume that
a=b=1
, so that
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle
are kernels of
M
. Now we claim that the only possible kernels of
M
are

\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle,\ \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle,\ \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle,\ \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle,\ \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
-1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle,\ \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle,\ \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\\
-1\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle,\ \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle,\ \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\\
-1\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle,

which is the same as saying that
M
is a submonoid of
\mathcal{B}_{3}^{2}
.

Suppose first that
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
d\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle
is a kernel, with
d\neq 0,1
. Then
M
contains the projections

\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
-d&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

and so contains

\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
-d&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}d&-1&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

and hence contains all powers of this matrix. For this matrix to have only finitely many different powers,
d
must equal
-1
. Similarly, if
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\\
d\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle
is a kernel of
M
with
d\neq 0,1
, then
d=-1
.

Now suppose
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\\
d\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle
is a kernel of
M
with
d\neq 0
. Then
M
contains the projections

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&-d&1\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
-1&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

and so contains

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&-d&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
-1&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-d&d&-1\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}

In order for this matrix to have only finitely many powers,
d
must equal
\pm 1
. This proves our claim, and hence proves the
Lemma
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
M
satisfies (*), and
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\rvert=4
. Then either
M
is split, or
M
is equivalent to a submonoid of
\mathcal{A}_{3}
.

We consider two possibilities for the configuration of the images of
M
:

(a).

there are three of the images which meet in a line.

(b).

any three of the images of
M
have zero intersection;

First suppose
(a)
holds. Write the images of
M
as
A,B,C,D
, with
B\cap C\cap D
being a line. Suppose
K
is a kernel of
M
which is not contained in
A
. Then (*) applied to
A,B,C
shows that
K\subset B\cup C
. Symmetrically,
K
is contained in
B\cup D
and in
C\cup D
, so that
K=B\cap C\cap D
. Therefore
M
is split.

Now suppose
(b)
holds. Then (*) implies that every kernel of
M
lies in two of the images (since otherwise there is a kernel
K
and three images with zero intersection none of which contains
K
, contradicting (*)). So the only possible kernels of
M
are the intersections of pairs of images. But given any four planes satisfying
(b)
, the complete projection monoid with these four planes as its images and their pairwise intersections as its kernels is equivalent to
\mathcal{A}_{3}
. So
M
is equivalent to a submonoid of
\mathcal{A}_{3}
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose
M
satisfies (*) and
\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\rvert\geqslant 5
. Then
M
is split.

Let
n=\lvert\operatorname{Im}(M)\rvert
. We prove the
Lemma
by induction on
n
, starting with the case
n=5
. If we write the images of
M
as
A,B,C,D,E
, then (permuting these labels if necessary) exactly one of the following holds:

(a).

any three of
A,B,C,D,E
have zero intersection;

(b).

A\cap B\cap C
is a line, but the intersection of any other set of three images of
M
is zero;

(c).

A\cap B\cap C
and
C\cap D\cap E
are lines, but the intersection of any other set of three images of
M
is zero;

(d).

B\cap C\cap D\cap E
is a line, but the intersection of
A
and any two other images is zero.

We address these cases individually.

1.

In this case (*) gives a contradiction, since if
K\in\operatorname{Ker}(M)
then
K
is contained in at most two of the images
A,B,C,D,E
; so there will be three images (with zero intersection) none of which contains
K
, contradicting (*).

2.

In this case (*) shows that every kernel
K
is contained in two of the planes
A,B,D,E
, and also in two of
A,C,D,E
, and also in two of
B,C,D,E
. Hence the line
D\cap E
is the only possible kernel of
M
, a contradiction.

3.

In this case (*) shows that the only possible kernels of
M
are the pairwise intersections of the lines
A,B,D,E
. But (*) applied to
A,C,D
shows that the line
B\cap E
cannot be a kernel of
M
. Similarly none of
B\cap D
,
A\cap E
,
A\cap D
are kernels, so that
M
has at most two kernels, a contradiction.

4.

In this case we argue as in the proof of
Lemma

5.3
to show that the only possible kernel of
M
which is not contained in
A
is the line
B\cap C\cap D\cap E
, so that
M
is split.

Now we suppose
n\geqslant 6
and assume that the
Lemma
holds with
n
replaced by
n-1
. If we can show that there are
n-1
images of
M
that meet in a line, then we can argue as in the proof of
Lemma

5.3
to show that
M
is split.

Given
n-1
of the images of
M
which do not meet in a line, let
M^{-}
be the complete projection monoid with these images and with the same kernels as
M
. Then the inductive hypothesis shows that
M^{-}
is split, so that
n-2
of these images meet in a line. So the following statement is true:

given any
n-1
of the images of
M
, at least
n-2
of them meet in a line.

Write the images of
M
as
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
. If
A_{1}\cap\dots\cap A_{n-1}
is a line, then we are done, so assume otherwise. By (
\dagger
) some
n-2
of
A_{1},\dots,A_{n-1}
meet in a line; by reordering if necessary, we assume that
L=A_{1}\cap\dots\cap A_{n-2}
is a line, and that
A_{n-1}
does not contain
L
. Now we just need to show that
A_{n}
contains
L
. Applying (
\dagger
) to
A_{2},\dots,A_{n}
, we find that some
n-2
of these must meet in a line. These
n-2
planes include at least two of
A_{2},\dots,A_{n-2}
because
n\geqslant 6
, so this line must be
L
. Therefore all but one of the lines
A_{2},\dots,A_{n}
contain
L
; by assumption
A_{n-1}
does not, and therefore
A_{n}
contains
L
, as required.
∎

Now we prove some preliminary results to deal with the case where neither
M
nor its dual satisfies (*).

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose the three coordinate planes are images of
M
, and
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
1\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
a\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle
are kernels, for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
not both equal to
1
. Then at least one of
a
and
b
equals
0
, and the other equals
0
or
-1
.

By assumption
M
contains the projections

\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
-a&1&0\\
-b&0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&-1&1\end{pmatrix},

and therefore contains the matrix

p=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
-a&1&0\\
-b&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&-1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
-a&a&0\\
-b&b-1&1\end{pmatrix}.

In order for
p
to have only finitely many different powers, we need
a\in\{0,1,-1\}
. Symmetrically,
b\in\{0,1,-1\}
.

Now assume that neither
a
nor
b
equals
0
. Then (by permuting the coordinate axes if necessary) we can assume that
a=1
and
b=-1
. Now the matrix
p
above becomes

\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
-1&1&0\\
1&-2&1\end{pmatrix},

and a simple induction shows that

6
Affine projection monoids

6.1
Definitions

In this final section we broaden our definitions, and consider monoids generated by
affine projections
: that is, idempotent affine-linear maps whose image is an affine subspace of
V
of codimension
1
. The projection monoids considered in preceding sections (whose elements are all linear maps) will be called
linear
projection monoids in this section. Affine projection monoids are analogous to affine reflection groups, which play an important role in Lie theory. However, while properly affine reflection groups (i.e. those which are not equivalent to linear reflection groups) are necessarily infinite in characteristic
0
, properly affine projection monoids can be finite. In this section we will mainly restrict attention to definitions and examples, leaving a more comprehensive exploration of affine projection monoids to a future paper.

We start by setting out some definitions and basic theory. If
p:V\to V
is an affine projection, then its image
\operatorname{im}(p)
is an affine subspace of
V
. We abuse notation and define the
kernel
of
p
to be
\ker(p)=\left\{\left.p(v)-v\ \right|\ \smash{v\in V}\right\}
. Thus
\ker(p)
is a (linear) subspace of
V
, of dimension
1
(but is not the kernel in the usual sense of being the preimage of the zero vector under
p
).

If
m:V\to V
is an affine-linear map, then we can define a linear map
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{m}:V\to V
by
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{m}(v)=m(v)-m(0)
. Clearly if
p
is an affine projection then
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{p}
is a linear projection: the image
\operatorname{im}(\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{p})
is the linear subspace obtained as a translation of
\operatorname{im}(p)
, and the kernel
\ker(\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{p})
equals
\ker(p)
.

For any affine-linear maps
l,m
, it is clear that
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{l}=\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{l}\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{m}
. Thus if
M
is an affine projection monoid, then the monoid
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{M}=\left\{\left.\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{m}\ \right|\ \smash{m\in M}\right\}
is a linear projection monoid, naturally isomorphic to a quotient of
M
. We call
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{M}
the
linear projection monoid underlying
M
.

The notion of completeness introduced in
Section

2.2
naturally extends to affine projection monoids:
M
is complete if whenever
K
is a kernel of
M
and
L
is an image of
M
such that
K+L=V
(or equivalently,
K
is not contained in any translate of
L
) the affine projection
\mathrm{prj}(K,L)
lies in
M
. It is easy to see that if
M
is complete, then so is
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{M}
; a partial converse is given by following.

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose
M
is an affine projection monoid, and that the underlying linear projection monoid
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{M}
is complete. Suppose that one of the following holds.

1.

M
does not have two parallel images.

2.

\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=0
and
M
is finite.

Then
M
is complete.

First suppose (
1
) holds. Suppose
K
is a kernel of
M
and
L
an image of
M
with
K+L=V
, and let
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{L}
be the linear subspace obtained by translating
L
. Then
\mathrm{prj}(K,\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{L})\in\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{M}
, because
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{M}
is complete, which means that
M
must contain an affine projection with kernel
K
and image parallel to
L
. But (
1
) tells us that the only image parallel to
L
is
L
itself, so
\mathrm{prj}(K,L)
lies in
M
.

Now suppose (
2
) holds. First we note that if
p,q
are projections in
M
with distinct but parallel images, then
\ker(p)=\ker(q)
. To see this, suppose that the kernels are distinct, and consider the effect of
pq
on
\operatorname{im}(p)
. Because
\operatorname{im}(p)
and
\operatorname{im}(q)
are parallel and distinct, there is a non-zero vector
w\in\ker(q)
such that
q(v)=v+w
for all
v\in\operatorname{im}(p)
. Similarly, there is a non-zero vector
x\in\ker(p)
such that
p(v)=v+x
for all
x\in\operatorname{im}(q)
. So
pq
acts on
\operatorname{im}(p)
as translation by
w+x
. The assumption that
\ker(p)
and
\ker(q)
are not parallel means that
w
and
x
are linearly independent, so that
w+x\neq 0
. So
pq
acts on
\operatorname{im}(p)
by a non-zero translation, so has infinite order, contradicting (2).

Now suppose that
K
is a kernel of
M
and
L
an image of
M
with
K+L=V
. Then, as in the first part of the proof,
M
must contain
\mathrm{prj}(K,L^{\prime})
for some
L^{\prime}
parallel to
L
. If
L^{\prime}=L
, then we have the desired projection
\mathrm{prj}(K,L)
in
M
. So suppose
L^{\prime}\neq L
. Let
p
be a projection in
M
with image
L
. Then from the paragraph above,
\ker(p)=K
; so
p=\mathrm{prj}(K,L)\in M
.∎

We extend the notion of irreducibility to affine projection monoids:
M
is irreducible if there is no proper affine subspace of dimension greater than
0
which is mapped to itself by every element of
M
. (Note that if there is an invariant affine subspace of dimension
0
, i.e. a point fixed by every element of
M
, then
M
is equivalent to a linear projection monoid.)

It is clear that if the underlying linear projection monoid
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{M}
is irreducible, then so is
M
. However, the converse need not hold. For example, take
V
of dimension
2
, and let
K
and
L
be any two (linear) lines in
V
. Choose affine lines
L_{1},\dots,L_{r}
(with
r\geqslant 2
) parallel to
L
, and let
J_{1},\dots,J_{s}
be any affine lines not parallel to
L
. Let
M
be the affine projection monoid generated by

\diamond

the projections
\mathrm{prj}(K,L_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,r
, and

\diamond

the projections
\mathrm{prj}(L,J_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,s
.

Then it is easy to check that
M
is finite, and it is irreducible (unless
s=1
and
J_{1}
is parallel to
K
). But the underlying projection monoid
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{M}
is reducible, because it has
L
as an invariant subspace. Note also that (provided at least one of
J_{1},\dots,J_{s}
is not parallel to
K
)
M
is not complete, so we do not have an analogue of
2.2
for affine projection monoids.

6.2
Examples

We finish by giving two examples of affine projection monoids which derive from
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
.

For our first example, recall the construction in
Section

3.1
: we take
V
to be a vector space of dimension

as a vector space

of dimension

, with basis
\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n}\}
chosen so that

\left\langle\left.e_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{k\neq i}\right\rangle

to be the projection monoid on

\mathcal{A}_{n}

We saw in

that the semigroup of non-invertible elements of

to itself; this isomorphism can be realised directly by observing that the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
map the basis

to itself, with each non-bijective map from

to
B
corresponding to a unique element of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
.

Now define

to be the affine subspace

\left\{\left.\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}=1}\right\}

of

. For
i\neq j
, define

to be the restriction of

to

. Then

is an affine projection: it has kernel
\langle e_{i}-e_{j}\rangle
and image
\left\{\left.\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{\lambda_{i}=0,\ \sum_{k}\lambda_{k}=1}\right\}
. Define
\mathcal{C}_{n}
to be the affine projection monoid on

generated by the projections

. Then
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is naturally isomorphic to a quotient of
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
. In fact
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{+}
, because

contains the basis

still corresponds to a unique element of
\mathcal{C}_{n}
.

Now we give an example based on the dual of the monoid
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}
constructed in

. As in that section, suppose
Z
is a finite subgroup of
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
, let

, and take

to be

with basis

. Now let

be a subset of
\mathbb{F}

\diamond

for each

and each

and image
\left\{\left.\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{\lambda_{i}=z\lambda_{j}}\right\}
.

\diamond

for each

and each

, take the projection with kernel

and image
\left\{\left.\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{\lambda_{i}=x}\right\}

Let
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{t}(X)
be the affine projection monoid generated by these projections. Then it is easy to see that the linear projection monoid underlying
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{t}(X)
is the dual of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}

Proposition 6.2
.

If
X
is finite, then
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{t}(X)
is finite.

We write the elements of

as column vectors with respect to the basis

\diamond

each row of

has at most one non-zero entry, and all non-zero entries of

;

\diamond

for each

of

of

is obviously finite, this will prove the result.

First observe that for each of the generating projections

lies in

\diamond

if

has kernel
\langle e_{i}\rangle
and image
\left\{\left.\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{\lambda_{i}=z\lambda_{j}}\right\}
, then

and

agrees with the identity matrix except that its

-entry is

-entry is

\diamond

if

has kernel
\langle e_{i}\rangle
and image
\left\{\left.\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}e_{k}\ \right|\ \smash{\lambda_{i}=x}\right\}
, then

-entry is zero, while

and

So to prove the

we just need to show that if two affine-linear maps correspond to pairs

lying in

, then so does their product. This amounts to showing that if

∎

By analysing the examples in
Section

4.2
, one can show that every finite irreducible affine projection monoid on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is equivalent either to a linear projection monoid or to a submonoid of one of these examples. It is clear that a great deal more work is needed to understand affine projection monoids in general. It may be that all finite affine projection monoids arise as straightforward variations of linear projection monoids, as with the examples above, or there may be some hidden richness. We leave this investigation for future work.
