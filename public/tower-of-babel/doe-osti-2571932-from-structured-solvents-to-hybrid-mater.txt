Research Project Scope
The goal of this research program was to develop high capacity sorbents amenable for alternative
regeneration approaches for direct air capture (DAC) of CO2. In particular, the research aimed to
develop an understanding of CO2 binding mechanism, thermal and oxidative stability, and
regeneration energetics of functionalized ionic liquids (ILs), deep eutectic solvents (DESs), and
porous materials. ILs and DESs are high-dielectric solvents with structural tunability that permits
the rational-design for energy-efficient regeneration approaches based on electromagnetic (EM)
field and moisture-swing. By further incorporating these solvents into polymeric capsules and
other structural supports, multi-scale interfaces for targeted CO2 and energy transfers were
achieved. Aspects related to CO2 capacity, selectivity, stability, dielectric properties, and binding
energies were examined through experimental and computational design to identify molecular
descriptors to inform future design of structured solvents and hybrid materials for DAC.

Executive Summary of Technical Accomplishments
Development of new solvents: Through this project, several functional ILs and DESs for DAC
were synthesized1, 2 and several hypothetical systems were further screened by high throughput
theory calculations.3 The CO2 absorption mechanism and energetics as examined were found to
be significantly influenced by accessibility to the binding site and the hydrogen bonding
interactions in these solvents.4 As DESs are commonly formed from mixtures of halide salts that
act as the hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs) and alcohols, amines or amides that act as hydrogen
bond donors (HBDs), structure and dynamics are primarily governed by the hydrogen bonding
network. The solvents developed focused on imidazolium and choline based salts or ILs with
amino acid, azole, and phenol functionalities, and DESs formed from mixtures of these salts as
HBAs and ethylene glycol (EG), propylene glycol (PG), 1,2-butanediol (BD), and
monoethanolamine (MEA) type HBDs.5 Capacities as high as 5 moles of CO2 per kilogram of
solvent were achieved at 1 bar of CO2 and 25 oC. The demonstrated CO2 capacities under DAC
relevant conditions (e.g., 400 ppm CO2 in air with 40% relative humidity) remained outstanding
compared to functional liquid absorbers.6, 7
In particular, studies in this project contributed to the understanding of how H-bond interactions
influence reactive separation of CO2 using spectroscopy, density functional theory (DFT), and
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations where the mechanism by which the HBD component
hindered the proton transfer between two active sites (e.g., choline prolinate with ethylene glycol)
was revealed, thus eliminating site deactivation and hence increasing the CO 2 capacity.1 The more
recent studies5, 8 underlines the importance of structure-composition-property relations in these
solvents as varied trends in physical properties and CO 2 binding affinities are possible via
modulation of (i) the number and strength of H-bonding groups in the parent compounds, (ii)
proton sharing equilibrium, and (iii) HBA/HBD composition. Influence of water (e.g., co-adsorbed

with CO2) on hydrogen bonding interactions was also examined as it was found to impact physical
properties as well as CO2 absorption and breakthrough behaviors; the eutectic character was
observed to remain up until 40% of water beyond which the solvent turns into a simple aqueous
mixture.9
Through high-throughput screening and DFT calculations, a library of structures consisting of 5
HBA cations ([HBAc]+), 12 HBA anions ([HBAa]–), and 7 HBDs was investigated (brackets
indicate charged species).3 Hypothetical eutectic solvents were constructed by combining a single
[HBAc]+, [HBAa]–, and HBD species, producing a total of 420 different hypothetical mixtures.
The fully automated computational workflow enabled quick screening of these HBA/HBD
mixtures. The study demonstrated that functional groups around the CO2 binding site can tune
complexation free energies, with electron-donating groups leading to stronger CO2 complexation
and electron-withdrawing groups resulting in weaker complexation. Additionally, the study
suggested potential descriptors, such as proton affinity or pKa, for identifying candidate species
for eutectic solvents and predicting product distributions. In parallel, quantum chemistry-driven
machine learning approaches were implemented to predict properties such as CO2 solubilityof ILs
and DESs.10-17 These tools help leverage the determination of physicochemical properties to
circumvent exhaustive experimental time and cost, and unrealized combinations of solvents. The
descriptors derived from the COSMO-RS quantum chemical-based model yielded excellent
agreement with experimental observations for speed of sound, surface tension, and viscosity.
These studies highlight the tunability of CO2 complexation thermodynamics through
functionalization and molecular descriptors of importance for the design of efficient CO2 sorbents.
Further, by employing the combination of ab-initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) and DFT a
selection of DESs were assessed from machine learning predictions to gain mechanistic insight
into the capture efficiency of CO2 in amine functionalized DESs (publication in progress).
Encapsulation of solvents: Encapsulation of ILs and DESs have been demonstrated to enhance
thermal stability and gaseous transport. Two approaches have been implemented. One of the
encapsulation method followed emulsion-templated technique where a variety of shell materials
(e.g., polyurea, polydimethylsiloxane) stabilized with particle surfactants (i.e., functionalized SiO 2
nanoparticles and graphene oxide nanosheets) aided the formation of isolable capsules.18-20
Encapsulation was used to enhance surface area and reduce core evaporation, specifically for
DESs, by using interfacial polymerization to encapsulate an emulsion droplet. A reduction (up to
40%) in the evaporation rate of the more volatile component in DESs was achieved compared to
unencapsulated mixtures with measurable volatility.18 The capsules were tested for CO2 sorption
over repeated cycles and in breakthrough curves with 500 ppm CO2 in N2, where recyclability was
achieved by thermal-swing (e.g., 75 oC desorption following 25 oC absorption) with no significant
capacity loss or degradation.20 Such capsules have been examined by microwave and radio-
frequency as regeneration approaches (publications in progress).
Another encapsulation method followed the simpler solvent impregnation technique where ILs,
specifically, were confined within the ZIF-8 metal organic framework.21 These IL/MOF
composites have been thoroughly characterized by solid state NMR, gas isotherm measurements
for porosity, FTIR, electron microscopy, and X-ray photoelectron microscopy techniques to
confirm the successful encapsulation of the IL within the MOF framework. These composites
demonstrated simultaneously high CO2 capacities and fast transport for practical applications in
DAC. The bulk liquid structure was further examined by neutron scattering techniques. Further,
the composites are being examined by a variety of techniques including fluorescent microscopy as

we have demonstrated to illuminate transient solute transport in similarly porous materials 22 and

Sorbent regeneration by EM field application: Towards the goal of sorbent development
amenable to alternative regeneration approaches, susceptibility of ILs to microwave (MW)
irradiation and the utility of MWs for dielectric heating, thus CO 2 release, was demonstrated.21
Further, a coupled moisture-swing and EM-based regeneration was demonstrated for the IL/MOF
and Silica/IL composites (more papers in progress).23 To enable EM field application during gas
absorption-desorption, two custom laboratory setups were developed. In one setup, a microwave
reactor was fitted with a breakthrough column and alternatively a liquid sample holder. In the other
setup, a radiofrequency (RF) applicator was applied to the breakthrough column equipped with
FLIR camera for thermal imaging. The intellectual merit in the demonstrated composite approach
is five-fold: (1) synergizing the high CO2 solubility and selectivity of the IL with the high porosity
of the MOF for improved transport; (2) stability against moisture and maintained selectivity; (3)
mild regeneration temperature (e.g., 60 C) via dielectric heating that acts on the entire volume
with minimal MW power; (4) targeted energy transfer to the active IL domain where the
chemisorbed CO2 resides; and (5) suppressed degradation kinetics (e.g., thermal oxidative
degradation) due to the instant nature of the EM irradiation. While the MW-based regeneration
was successful in terms of realizing fast regeneration rates (e.g., within 2-4 minutes as opposed to
hundreds of minutes with conventional thermal heating), it led to hotspot formations over repeated
cycles. With RF based regeneration, stability upon cycling was enhanced with virtually no capacity
loss or degradation due to hot spot formation.23 A change in the dynamic behavior of the core
liquid was observed with CO2 saturation, as the measured change in viscosity with absorption
suggests. The ongoing relaxation and dielectric studies will further provide rationalization to the
frequency-dependent dielectric heating and provide insight to the structural changes upon
polarization of the sorbents.
Oxidative stability: Oxidative and thermal degradation pathways of imidazolium type reactive IL
([EMIM][2-CNpyr] – also used as HBA in eutectic solvents) was examined by a comprehensive
analytical characterization following accelerated degradation/aging. 24 The results suggested that
upon long exposure (i.e., 7 days) to oxygen and high temperature (e.g., 80 °C), oxidation of both
ions and ring opening of the imidazolium occur by radical and SN2 reaction mechanisms,
respectively. These reactions were encouraged by the nucleophilicity of the anion producing
mostly pyrrole dimer, ketones, and alkenes in addition to H 2 and CO2 gases. While the CO2
reactivity of the IL sorbents rely on nucleophilic functional groups, stronger nucleophilicity
negatively impact sorbent stability upon long exposures to thermal cycling as evident by the
measured increase in viscosity and loss of CO2 capacity (as much as 50% after a week-long
exposure to 80 °C under pure O2). DFT calculations also confirmed the anion is the primary
reactive site for O2, and oxidative degradation is an anion-driven process in anion functionalized
ILs (manuscript in progress; preprint available in doi:10.26434/chemrxiv-2025-v7blw). Therefore,
balancing of the nucleophilicity of the sorbent for CO2 capacity and regeneration temperature is
important.
Training Opportunity and Professional Development
A cohort of PhD students and postdoctoral researchers interacted throughput this project for
material exchange, interdisciplinary research discussions, and complementary analysis of
scientific data. The cohort was trained in various techniques including synthesis, thermal analysis,
physical property measurements, spectroscopic characterization, gas absorption, neutron

 4

techniques, machine learning methods, ab-initio theory calculations, and molecular dynamics
simulations; each having one major focus area. Five of the PhD students who were trained and
participated in the project (Lee, Klemm, Vicchio, Dikki, Taylor) graduated (four moved to
postdoctoral positions with two at national laboratories and one moved to industry) and one more
will graduate in near future (Al-Mahbobi). Two of the PhD students completed
internship/fellowship at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. In addition, five postdoctoral researchers
(Zeeshan, Cagli, Mohan, Park, Bhattacharjee), a DOE-SULI (Marland), DOE-Next Generation
High School student (Gergel), a DOE-ECO intern (Gugulothu), two junior graduate students
(Ikponmwosa and Gulam Rabbani), and several undergraduate researchers were trained through
this project. These early career researchers presented their work as part of this project at various
professional society meetings including American Chemical Society, American Institute of
Chemical Engineers, Society of Plastics Engineering International Polyolefins Conference, and
Gordon Research Conferences of Chemical Separations and Ionic Liquids.
