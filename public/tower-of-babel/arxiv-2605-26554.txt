Linear and Neural Dueling Bandits with Delayed Feedback
Xiangyi Wang, Pingchen Lu, Jie Mao, Mingze Kong, Zhi Hong, Zhiyong Wang, Zhongxiang Dai

Abstract
Contextual dueling bandits form a cornerstone of preference-based decision-making, with critical applications in recommender systems and large language model alignment. However, standard algorithms rely on the idealized assumption of immediate feedback, a condition frequently violated in real-world scenarios such as prompt optimization. This setting introduces a unique theoretical challenge: unlike linear bandits, dueling bandit estimators lack closed-form solutions, rendering naive adaptations of standard weighting techniques biased. To address this, we formalize the problem of Contextual Dueling Bandits with Stochastic Delayed Feedback and propose two novel algorithms: Linear (LDB-DF) and Neural (NDB-DF) Dueling Bandits with Delayed Feedback. Central to our approach is a novel estimator that integrates an Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) mechanism directly into the loss function, ensuring unbiased correction for delayed or missing feedback. We provide comprehensive theoretical analysis, establishing an O ~ ​ ( d ​ T ) \tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T}) regret bound for the linear setting and sub-linear guarantees for the neural setting. Extensive experiments on both simulated and real-world datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed methods.



1
Introduction

The contextual multi-armed bandit (MAB) framework serves as a cornerstone of sequential decision-making under uncertainty, finding widespread application in recommendation systems, online advertising, and personalized services
(
Li et al. 2010
;
Lattimore & Szepesvári 2020
)
. While traditional MAB algorithms predicate on absolute numerical rewards, many real-world scenarios—such as calibrating Large Language Models (LLMs) or ranking search results—fundamentally rely on relative preference feedback. In these settings, assigning an absolute scalar score to a response is often cognitively burdensome and noisy for human annotators. In contrast, preference-based feedback, which requires an annotator to simply compare two candidate responses (e.g.,
x_{1}\succ x_{2}
), is widely recognized as being more reliable and consistent
(
Yue et al. 2012
;
Yue & Joachims 2009
)
. This paradigm is formally captured by the Contextual Dueling Bandit (CDB) framework
(
Yue et al. 2012
;
Saha 2021
;
Bengs et al. 2022
)
, where the agent selects a pair of arms and updates its policy based on binary preference outcomes.

Despite the theoretical success of CDBs, a critical aspect of real-world deployment remains largely unaddressed: the temporal dynamics of feedback. Standard CDB algorithms typically operate under the assumption that preference feedback is observed immediately following an action. However, in many practical applications, feedback is inherently delayed. For instance, in display advertising and e-commerce, user reactions such as conversions or clicks can lag behind impressions by hours or even days
(
Chapelle 2014
)
. Similarly, in the optimization of LLMs via human-in-the-loop reinforcement learning (RLHF), human evaluation is asynchronous and slow
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
. Furthermore, feedback systems often impose a “waiting window”; if a response does not arrive within this window, it is treated as missing data
(
Vernade et al. 2017
)
.

Ignoring these delays introduces significant bias into reward estimation. While stochastic delayed feedback has been studied in standard MABs and Bayesian Optimization (BO), the unique mathematical structure of dueling bandits renders existing solutions insufficient. For instance, in delayed BO,
Verma et al. 2022
successfully employed a “weighting” strategy where pending observations are temporarily imputed with minimum values. Such approaches are often feasible in linear or kernelized settings where estimators admit closed-form solutions (e.g., ridge regression), allowing the bias from imputation to be theoretically bounded. However, this presents a unique theoretical challenge for preference learning: parameter estimation in contextual dueling bandits—typically formulated via Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model—lacks a closed-form solution and exhibits complex, implicit dependencies on binary outcomes. Consequently, naively adapting standard weighting techniques induces a systematic bias in the optimization landscape that cannot be easily rectified.

To bridge this gap, we formalize the problem of Contextual Dueling Bandits with Stochastic Delayed Feedback and propose a principled solution that departs from heuristic imputation. We introduce two novel algorithms: Linear Dueling Bandits with Delayed Feedback (LDB-DF) for linear rewards, and Neural Dueling Bandits with Delayed Feedback (NDB-DF) to capture complex, non-linear reward landscapes. Central to our approach is a novel estimator that integrates an Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) mechanism directly into the regularized loss function. By scaling the contribution of observed feedback by the inverse of its arrival probability, this mechanism explicitly ensures that the expected gradient of the surrogate loss remains an unbiased estimator of the true gradient, effectively neutralizing the bias introduced by stochastic observation. We provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis, establishing an
\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T})
regret bound for the linear setting and proving sub-linear regret guarantees for the neural setting via Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) analysis. Extensive experiments on both simulated environments and real-world datasets demonstrate the superior performance of our methods compared to baselines that ignore feedback delays.

Problem Setting

Throughout this paper, we denote vectors using bold lowercase letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{x}
) and matrices using bold uppercase letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{M}
). Let
|\mathcal{A}|
represent the cardinality of a set
\mathcal{A}
, and
[m]
denote the integer set
\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
. Given a positive semi-definite (PSD) matrix
\mathbf{M}
, we define the weighted matrix norm of a vector
\mathbf{x}
as
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{M}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}}
.

Consider a sequential decision-making scenario where a learning agent interacts with an environment over
T
rounds. At each time step
t\in[T]
, the agent receives a set of arms
\mathcal{X}_{t}
and selects a pair
(x_{t,1},x_{t,2})
. In a departure from standard bandit settings that provide scalar rewards, we operate under the dueling bandit paradigm, where the agent only receives a binary preference feedback
y_{t}\in\{0,1\}
indicating which arm in the pair is superior. Distinct from standard settings, the preference feedback
y_{t}
is not immediately available; instead, it is observed after a random delay
D_{t}
.

We impose a hard observation threshold
M
. Under this setting, the feedback arrives within
M
rounds with probability
\rho=\mathbb{P}(D_{t}\leq M)
, while it arrives outside this window (i.e.,
D_{t}>M
) with probability
1-\rho
. Following standard assumptions in delayed feedback literature
(
Vernade et al. 2017
;
Garg & Akash 2020
)
, we assume
\rho
is known. Consequently, from the agent’s perspective, at any given round
t
, we can only utilize the historical data that has already arrived, i.e.,
s+D_{s}\leq t
.

Modeling Preference Feedback.
We assume the existence of a latent reward function
f(x)
. For any pair of arms
x
and
x^{\prime}
, the pairwise preference feedback
y=\mathds{1}(x\succ x^{\prime})
is generated by the Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) model
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
. Specifically,
y
is sampled from a Bernoulli distribution with probability:

P(x\succ x^{\prime})=\mu(f(x)-f(x^{\prime})),

where
\mu(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
is the logistic link function.

Performance Metric.
Consistent with standard contextual dueling bandit literature
(
Bengs et al. 2022
;
Saha & Krishnamurthy 2022
)
, we evaluate the algorithm using cumulative regret:

R_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(2f(x^{*}_{t})-f(x_{t,1})-f(x_{t,2})\right),

where
x^{*}_{t}=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{t}}f(x)
denotes the optimal arm in round
t
.

We adopt the following standard assumptions regarding the preference model
(
Yue et al. 2012
;
Saha 2021
;
Bengs et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
:

Assumption 2.1

(Standard Assumptions)
.

A1.\ |\mu(f(x))-\mu(g(x))|\leq L_{\mu}|f(x)-g(x)|
.

A2.\ \forall x\in\mathcal{X}:\min|\dot{\mu}(f(x))|\geq\kappa_{\mu}>0
.

A3.\ \|\phi(x_{t,1})-\phi(x_{t,2})\|_{2}\leq L,\forall t=1,\dots,T
.

The notation
\dot{\mu}(\cdot)
denotes the first-order derivative of the link function
\mu(\cdot)
. The assumptions listed above are widely adopted in studies involving binary feedback and linear structures. For the specific case where
\mu
is the logistic function, the Lipschitz constant is
L_{\mu}=1/4
. Regarding Assumption (A3), while classical linear bandit analysis typically assumes a bound on the norm of individual feature vectors (i.e.,
\|\phi(x)\|\leq L
), our condition on the pairwise difference is structurally equivalent. By the triangle inequality, the standard assumption implies ours with a scaling factor of
2L
.

Reward Function Realizations.
Following established conventions in contextual bandits
(
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
, we consider two realizations for the latent reward function
f
:

2.1
Linear Dueling Bandits With Delayed Feedback

In the linear setting, we posit that the reward function is linear in a fixed feature space,
f(x)=\theta^{\top}\phi(x)
, where the feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
\|\phi(x)\|_{2}\leq 1
. Under this assumption, the preference probability simplifies to:

P(x_{t,1}\succ x_{t,2})=\mu\left(\theta^{\top}(\phi(x_{t,1})-\phi(x_{t,2}))\right).

The objective is to estimate the unknown parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, the estimator must account for the observing mechanism: at any round
t
, we can only utilize feedback where
s+D_{s}\leq t
.

2.2
Neural Dueling Bandits With Delayed Feedback

In the neural setting,we allow the reward functions
f
to be non-linear functions.
We approximate
f(x)
using a fully connected neural network (NN)
h(x;\theta)
with depth
L\geq 2
, width
m
, and ReLU activations. The network output is defined as:

h(x;\theta)=\mathbf{W}_{L}\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{L-1}\text{ReLU}(\cdots\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{1}x))),

where
\mathbf{W}_{l}
represents the weight matrices. The parameter vector
\theta
comprises all flattened weights, and
g(x;\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}h(x;\theta)
denotes the gradient.

Analogous to the linear case, the primary challenge lies in training the network using only the subset of feedback available within the observation window. To facilitate theoretical analysis via the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime, we adopt standard assumptions from the neural bandit literature:

Assumption 2.2

(Neural Assumptions)
.

B1.\ |f(x)|\leq 1,\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
.

B2.\ H\succeq\lambda_{0}I,\text{ for some }\lambda_{0}>0
.

B3.\ \forall x\in\mathcal{X}:\|x\|_{2}=1\text{ and }x_{j}=x_{j+d/2}
.

The last assumption in Assumption
2.2
, together with the initialization of
\theta_{0}
, follows the standard practice in neural bandits
(
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
, which ensures that
h(x;\theta_{0})=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{t},t\in[T]
. Moreover, the assumption
x_{j}=x_{j+d/2}
is a mild condition commonly used in the neural bandit literature
(
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
and has been recently adopted in extensions to dueling bandits
(
Verma et al. 2025
)
. This assumption is primarily for the convenience of regret analysis: for any context
x
with
\|x\|_{2}=1
, one can always construct a padded context
x^{\prime}=(x^{\top},x^{\top})^{\top}/\sqrt{2}
that satisfies this requirement
(
Zhou et al. 2020
)
.

Algorithms

3.1
Linear Dueling Bandits with Delayed Feedback (LDB-DF)

To address the challenge of parameter estimation under stochastic delays, we introduce the Linear Dueling Bandits with Delayed Feedback (LDB-DF) algorithm. The core difficulty in this setting is that naively utilizing available data introduces systematic bias. LDB-DF overcomes this by integrating an unbiased estimator with an optimistic exploration strategy. Specifically, the algorithm relies on an Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) mechanism to correct the gradient bias in the Maximum Likelihood Estimation.

Estimating Reward Parameters with Observed Data (Line
5
-Line
6
).

Unlike standard approaches that update the model immediately, LDB-DF maintains an estimator derived exclusively from the subset of historical data that has reliably ”arrived” within the valid window. At the onset of round
t
, the algorithm first updates the set of observed feedback
\mathcal{O}_{t}
(Line
5
).

To formalize the observation mechanism, we define the effective observable feedback
y^{\prime}_{s}
for a past round
s
. From the learner’s perspective, any feedback with a delay exceeding the threshold
M
is strictly treated as zero:

y^{\prime}_{s}=y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}.

(1)

Under this model, the observation is governed by the relation
y_{s}^{\prime}=\mu(f(x_{s,1})-f(x_{s,2}))\rho+\epsilon_{s}
, where
\epsilon_{s}
captures the stochasticity of both the preference generation and the random observation. Consequently, a naive Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) update restricted to these raw observations would suffer from significant selection bias: since the effective sample size is systematically reduced by the observation probability
\rho=\mathbb{P}(D_{s}\leq M)
, the estimator would incorrectly treat missing data as non-informative zeros.

To rectify this, we introduce an Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) strategy. We define the effective weight for the feedback from round
s
observed at round
t
as:

\omega_{s,t}\triangleq\frac{\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s)\}}{\rho}.

(2)

By re-weighting observed feedback instances by
\omega_{s,t}
, we compensate for the mass of unobserved instances. Consequently, in Line
6
,
\theta_{t}
is determined by minimizing the following IPW-regularized loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\Big[\omega_{s,t}y_{s}\log\mu(\theta^{\top}\Delta\phi_{s})+

(3)

\displaystyle\left(1-\omega_{s,t}y_{s}\right)\log\mu(-\theta^{\top}\Delta\phi_{s})\Big]+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2},

where
\Delta\phi_{s}\triangleq\phi(x_{s,1})-\phi(x_{s,2})
. The weight
\omega_{s,t}
effectively neutralizes the bias introduced by the observation mechanism where
D_{s}>M
.

Neural Dueling Bandits with Delayed Feedback (NDB-DF)

Recognizing the limitations of linear models in capturing complex reward landscapes, we propose Neural Dueling Bandits with Delayed Feedback (NDB-DF). This algorithm extends our IPW-based approach to non-linear function approximation using neural networks. NDB-DF maintains a neural approximator updated online, crucially learning only from the subset of effectively arrived feedback.

Similar to the linear setting, NDB-DF trains the neural network using only the subset of feedback that has reliably arrived by the current round

D_{s}\leq M

\omega_{s,t}

Defining the predicted preference difference as

4
Theoretical Analysis

This section is dedicated to the theoretical analysis of our algorithms, where we establish rigorous regret bounds and discuss their implications.

4.1
Regret Analysis for Linear Setting (LDB-DF)

The following theorem establishes an upper bound on the cumulative regret for LDB-DF.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
holds. Let
\lambda>\kappa_{\mu}L^{2}
. With probability at least
1-\delta
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
, the cumulative regret of LDB-DF satisfies:

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\left(\sqrt{d}+M\right)d\sqrt{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{M}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}d\sqrt{T}\right).

(13)

The regret bound in Theorem
4.1
explicitly characterizes the impact of feedback latency. The term
\frac{M}{\rho}
acts as a multiplicative factor, underscoring two key intuitions. A smaller observation probability
\rho
implies that the effective sample size is reduced. Consequently, the variance of the estimator increases, scaling the regret by
1/\rho
. Increasing the threshold
M
reduces data observation. A larger
M
implies tolerating longer feedback delays, which necessitates a wider confidence interval to bound the uncertainty, resulting in a linear penalty on
M
. This exacerbates the systematic bias in the estimator, linearly increasing the regret.
In the standard setting where feedback is immediate and lossless (i.e.,
\rho=1
and
M=1
), our regret bound simplifies to
\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T}/\kappa_{\mu})
. This fully aligns with the state-of-the-art regret bounds for standard linear dueling bandits established in prior work
(
Saha 2021
;
Bengs et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2024
)
, demonstrating that LDB-DF is a generalization of existing methods.

The proof hinges on bounding the estimation error
\|\theta-\theta_{t}\|_{V_{t}}
under delayed feedback. A primary challenge lies in managing the composite error introduced by the IPW estimator. To address this, we employ a novel “add-and-subtract” decomposition technique. Specifically, we decompose the gradient error into a martingale noise term and a systematic bias term, then analyze them separately.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose Assumption

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle=\tilde{O}\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{d}}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}+B\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}}+\frac{M}{\kappa_{\mu}m\rho}\right)\sqrt{\tilde{d}T}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{M}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\tilde{d}\sqrt{T}\right),

(14)

where
\tilde{d}
is the effective dimension of the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) matrix.

Mirroring the linear case, the neural regret bound scales linearly with the factor
\frac{M}{\rho}
, capturing the dual sources of difficulty inherent to delayed feedback. Specifically, the inverse dependence on
\rho
reflects the variance inflation in the IPW estimator caused by a reduced effective sample size. Meanwhile, the linear dependence on
M
arises because tolerating longer feedback delays necessitates a wider confidence interval to bound the uncertainty, thereby imposing a linear penalty. This confirms that the fundamental hardness of the problem—governed by the trade-off between observation likelihood and delay magnitude—persists regardless of the reward function’s complexity. When
\rho=1
and
M=1
, our result simplifies to
\tilde{O}(\tilde{d}\sqrt{T}/\kappa_{\mu})
, aligning with the bounds for Neural Dueling Bandits with immediate feedback derived by
Verma et al. 2025
.
The analysis proceeds in the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime. We employ a crucial “add-and-subtract” decomposition strategy on the gradient error. This decomposition allows us to explicitly bound the delay-induced bias by
\frac{M}{\kappa_{\mu}m\rho}
.

5
Experiments

In this section, we empirically evaluate the performance of our proposed algorithms, LDB-DF (Linear) and NDB-DF (Neural). We compare them against baselines that do not explicitly handle the bias caused by delays, demonstrating the necessity of our IPW-based delay-handling mechanism.

5.1
Experimental Setup

We conduct experiments on both controlled synthetic environments and a large-scale real-world prompt optimization benchmark.

Synthetic Environments.

To rigorously test the theoretical properties of our algorithms, we generate synthetic data with varying reward structures.

Linear Setting: We generate a ground-truth preference vector
\theta^{\in}\mathbb{R}^{d}
uniformly at random on the unit sphere. The reward function is strictly linear:
f(x)=\theta^{\top}x
.

Non-Linear (Neural) Settings: To evaluate the capacity of NDB-DF to capture complex dependencies, we design two non-linear environments: (1) a Quadratic setting where
f(x)=(\theta^{\top}x)^{2}
, and (2) a Cubic setting where
f(x)=(\theta^{\top}x)^{3}
.
In all synthetic experiments, we set the arm set size
K=20
and feature dimension
d=20
. The feedback delay
D_{t}
is sampled from a Geometric distribution to simulate stochastic network latency.

Real-world Data.

To demonstrate practical applicability, we apply our contextual dueling bandit framework to the task of Automated Prompt Optimization
(
Fernando et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
. The goal is to identify the optimal prompt that elicits the highest quality response from a Large Language Model (LLM).
We conduct 29 instruction induction tasks from
InstructZero
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
, covering a wide range of settings, including sentiment analysis, reasoning, and translation.
Following the experimental protocol of
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
, we formulate the prompt optimization task as a contextual bandit problem. For each dataset, we generate a candidate pool of
K=500
prompts. The reward of a selected prompt (arm) is calculated as its accuracy on a held-out validation set of size
N=50
.

5.2
Baselines

We compare our proposed methods against two categories of baselines to validate the effectiveness of our bias-correction mechanism.

LDB-Ignore / NDB-Ignore: These are naive baselines that utilize the same underlying model architectures (Linear or Neural) as our proposed methods but simply disregard delayed feedback. Pending observations are treated as missing and are excluded from the training set until they arrive, without any importance weighting.

LDB-Heuristic / NDB-Heuristic: These baselines adopt the “hallucination” strategy adapted from delayed Bayesian Optimization
(
Verma et al. 2022
)
. Instead of ignoring pending feedback, these methods impute the missing outcome using the current model’s predicted preference probability (i.e., the posterior mean estimate). This predicted soft label is then substituted into the loss function to update the estimator, thereby utilizing the pending data albeit with a biased proxy.

Comparing against
Ignore
baselines isolates the benefit of utilizing delayed data, while comparing against
Heuristic
baselines demonstrates the necessity of our unbiased IPW correction versus biased imputation.

6
Related Work

Our work is situated at the intersection of contextual dueling bandits, neural bandits, and online learning with delayed feedback.

6.1
Contextual Dueling Bandits

The Dueling Bandit framework, where the learner receives relative preference feedback (e.g.,
A\succ B
) rather than absolute rewards, was introduced by
Yue & Joachims 2009
;
Yue et al. 2012
to model scenarios like information retrieval. Early algorithms focused on the
K
-armed setting
(
Zoghi et al. 2014
;
Komiyama et al. 2015
)
. To handle large or continuous action spaces, the problem was extended to
Contextual
Dueling Bandits, typically assuming a linear relationship between the context vectors and the latent utility function
(
Saha 2021
;
Bengs et al. 2022
;
Saha & Krishnamurthy 2022
)
. Recently,
Li et al. 2024
and
Di et al. 2023
have further improved the regret bounds for stochastic contextual dueling bandits, refining the theoretical understanding of preference-based learning. However, all these works operate under the assumption of
immediate
feedback. They fail to account for the stochastic delays inherent in human-in-the-loop systems or complex evaluation pipelines, which can lead to suboptimal decision-making in practice.

6.2
Neural Bandits

To address the limitations of linear models in complex real-world applications, Neural Bandits were proposed, utilizing deep neural networks to approximate non-linear reward functions
(
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
. This direction has seen rapid development, with extensions to various settings such as shallow exploration
(
Xu et al. 2020
)
and batch learning
(
Dai et al. 2022
)
. Most relevant to our work is the recent proposal of Neural Dueling Bandits by
Verma et al. 2024
, which combines the expressiveness of neural networks with preference-based feedback. While
Verma et al. 2024
effectively addresses the non-linearity of reward functions, it does not address the temporal aspect of data collection, specifically the challenges posed by latency. Our NDB-DF algorithm extends this line of research by explicitly modeling and correcting for stochastic delays and missing data within the neural training process, offering a more robust solution for real-world deployment.

6.3
Learning with Delayed Feedback

Delayed feedback is a pervasive challenge in online learning, extensively studied in the context of display advertising
(
Chapelle 2014
)
and conversion optimization
(
Vernade et al. 2017
)
.
Joulani et al. 2013
provided a foundational analysis for general online learning with delays. In the specific context of bandits,
Zhou & Gu 2019
and
Vernade et al. 2017
developed algorithms for Generalized Linear Bandits that handle stochastic delays and missing data (where feedback is lost if it arrives after a threshold).
Garg & Akash 2020
and
Grover & Lazic 2018
further explored best-arm identification and parallel experimentation under delay.
However, existing delayed bandit algorithms predominantly focus on absolute numerical rewards (standard MAB or Linear Bandits). To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has established a unified framework that simultaneously handles preference feedback (dueling), non-linear function approximation (neural), and stochastic delays/missing. Our LDB-DF and NDB-DF algorithms bridge this gap, providing a comprehensive solution that integrates these three critical components.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we addressed the practical challenge of Contextual Dueling Bandits under Stochastic Delayed Feedback, a setting pervasive in human-in-the-loop optimization. We formalized the problem and proposed two novel algorithms, LDB-DF and NDB-DF, which leverage Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) to correct for censorship bias. Our theoretical analysis establishes the first regret bounds for this setting, explicitly characterizing the trade-off between observation latency and sample complexity in both linear and neural regimes.

Empirically, our methods demonstrate significant robustness against stochastic delays, consistently outperforming baselines across synthetic environments and large-scale Prompt Optimization benchmarks. These results validate our framework as a principled solution for asynchronous preference learning. Future directions include extending this approach to general delay distributions and exploring applications to preference-based reinforcement learning with feedback delays.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning, specifically in sequential decision-making under realistic constraints like latency. Our proposed framework for Prompt Optimization could significantly enhance the efficiency of Large Language Models in various downstream tasks. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Appendix B
Proofs for the Linear Setting (LDB-DF)

In this section, we provide the complete proof of Theorem
4.1
. The proof proceeds through three key lemmas: (1) a confidence ellipsoid bound showing that the true parameter
\theta
lies within an ellipsoid centered at the estimate
\theta_{t}
(Lemma
B.1
); (2) a pointwise estimation error bound (Lemma
B.2
); and (3) an information gain bound that controls the sum of exploration bonuses (Lemma
B.3
).

Proof Strategy.

The main challenge in our setting, compared to standard linear dueling bandits, is that the MLE is computed from an
incomplete
dataset: only feedback satisfying
D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s)
is available at round
t
. Our approach uses an “add-and-subtract” decomposition to isolate: (i) a martingale noise term (bounded via Theorem
A.1
), (ii) a delay-induced bias term (bounded deterministically by
ML/(\rho\kappa_{\mu})
), and (iii) a regularization-induced term (bounded by
\sqrt{\lambda\kappa_{\mu}}/\kappa_{\mu}
).

B.1
Confidence Ellipsoid for the Linear Estimator

The following lemma establishes that, under delayed feedback, the true parameter
\theta
remains within a confidence ellipsoid centered at the MLE estimate
\theta_{t}
, with a radius that explicitly accounts for feedback delays.

Lemma B.1

(Confidence Ellipsoid—Linear Setting)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, define

\beta_{t}\triangleq\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)+d\log\!\bigl(1+tL^{2}\kappa_{\mu}/(d\lambda)\bigr)}+ML+\sqrt{\lambda\kappa_{\mu}}.

(17)

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
t=1,\ldots,T
:

\|\theta-\theta_{t}\|_{V_{t}}\leq\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}.

(18)

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\phi}_{s}\triangleq\phi(x_{s,1})-\phi(x_{s,2})
denote the pairwise feature difference and
\tilde{f}_{s}\triangleq f(x_{s,1})-f(x_{s,2})=\theta^{\top}\widetilde{\phi}_{s}
the true preference difference. For any
\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the auxiliary function:

G_{t}(\theta^{\prime})\triangleq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\bigl(\mu(\theta^{\prime}{}^{\top}\widetilde{\phi}_{s})-\mu(\theta^{\top}\widetilde{\phi}_{s})\bigr)\widetilde{\phi}_{s}+\lambda\theta^{\prime}.

(19)

We first establish that
G_{t}
is strongly monotone. For any
\theta^{\prime}_{1},\theta^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\bar{\theta}=\lambda^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}_{1}+(1-\lambda^{\prime})\theta^{\prime}_{2}
for some
\lambda^{\prime}\in(0,1)
. By the mean value theorem applied componentwise:

\displaystyle G_{t}(\theta^{\prime}_{1})-G_{t}(\theta^{\prime}_{2})

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}\dot{\mu}(\bar{\theta}^{\top}\widetilde{\phi}_{s})\,\widetilde{\phi}_{s}\widetilde{\phi}_{s}^{\top}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right](\theta^{\prime}_{1}-\theta^{\prime}_{2}).

(20)

Since Assumption
2.1
(A2) ensures
\dot{\mu}(\bar{\theta}^{\top}\widetilde{\phi}_{s})\geq\kappa_{\mu}>0
for all
s
, we obtain the lower bound:

G_{t}(\theta^{\prime}_{1})-G_{t}(\theta^{\prime}_{2})\succeq\kappa_{\mu}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}\widetilde{\phi}_{s}\widetilde{\phi}_{s}^{\top}+\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\mathbf{I}\right](\theta^{\prime}_{1}-\theta^{\prime}_{2})=\kappa_{\mu}\,V_{t}\,(\theta^{\prime}_{1}-\theta^{\prime}_{2}).

(21)

By construction,
G_{t}(\theta)=\lambda\theta
(since the sum telescopes to zero when
\theta^{\prime}=\theta
). Using the strong monotonicity from (
21
):

\displaystyle\|G_{t}(\theta_{t})-\lambda\theta\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|G_{t}(\theta)-G_{t}(\theta_{t})\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\bigl(\kappa_{\mu}V_{t}(\theta-\theta_{t})\bigr)^{\top}V_{t}^{-1}\bigl(\kappa_{\mu}V_{t}(\theta-\theta_{t})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\mu}^{2}\|\theta-\theta_{t}\|_{V_{t}}^{2}.

Therefore, by the triangle inequality:

\|\theta-\theta_{t}\|_{V_{t}}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mu}}\|G_{t}(\theta_{t})\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mu}}\|\lambda\theta\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}.

(22)

Recall that
\theta_{t}
is the minimizer of the IPW-regularized loss in (
3
). Setting the gradient to zero yields the first-order optimality (MLE) condition:

\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left(\mu(\theta_{t}^{\top}\widetilde{\phi}_{s})-\frac{y_{s}}{\rho}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s)\}\right)\widetilde{\phi}_{s}+\lambda\theta_{t}=0.

(23)

Denoting
f_{t,s}\triangleq\theta_{t}^{\top}\widetilde{\phi}_{s}
, we expand and rearrange:

\displaystyle G_{t}(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\bigl(\mu(f_{t,s})-\mu(\tilde{f}_{s})\bigr)\widetilde{\phi}_{s}+\lambda\theta_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left(\mu(f_{t,s})-\frac{1}{\rho}y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}+\frac{1}{\rho}\epsilon_{s}\right)\widetilde{\phi}_{s}+\lambda\theta_{t},

(24)

where we used the identity
\mu(\tilde{f}_{s})=\frac{1}{\rho}\bigl(y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}-\epsilon_{s}\bigr)
, with
\epsilon_{s}\triangleq y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}-\mu(\tilde{f}_{s})\rho
being the observation noise.

Adding and subtracting
\frac{1}{\rho}y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s)\}
and applying (
23
):

\displaystyle G_{t}(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\epsilon_{s}\,\widetilde{\phi}_{s}+\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{s=t-M}^{t}y_{s}\bigl(\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s)\}-\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}\bigr)\widetilde{\phi}_{s}.

(25)

where we denote the first summation as Term (I) (the martingale noise) and the second summation as Term (II) (the delay-induced bias). The cancellation of the MLE term works as follows: for any
s<t-M
, we have
t-s>M
, so
\min(M,t-s)=M
. Thus,
\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s)\}=\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}
, and the difference in Term (II) vanishes. Only rounds
s\in[t-M,t]
contribute to the bias.

For Term (I),
The noise sequence
\{\epsilon_{s}\}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{s}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1}]=0
and
|\epsilon_{s}|\leq 1
, making it conditionally
1
-sub-Gaussian. Moreover,
\widetilde{\phi}_{s}
is
\mathcal{F}_{s-1}
-measurable. Applying Theorem
A.1
with
R=1
and
V=\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\mathbf{I}
:

\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{t}\epsilon_{s}\widetilde{\phi}_{s}\right\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2\log\!\left(\frac{\det(V_{t})^{1/2}}{\delta\cdot\det(\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\mathbf{I})^{1/2}}\right),

(26)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. By Lemma
A.2
, since
\|\widetilde{\phi}_{s}\|_{2}\leq L
:

\det(V_{t})\leq\left(\frac{\lambda/\kappa_{\mu}+tL^{2}/d}{1}\right)^{d}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\lambda/\kappa_{\mu}}\right)^{-d},

(27)

which gives:

\log\frac{\det(V_{t})^{1/2}}{\det(\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\mathbf{I})^{1/2}}\leq\frac{d}{2}\log\!\left(1+\frac{tL^{2}\kappa_{\mu}}{d\lambda}\right).

(28)

Combining with (
26
):

\frac{1}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{t}\epsilon_{s}\widetilde{\phi}_{s}\right\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\leq\frac{1}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)+d\log\!\bigl(1+tL^{2}\kappa_{\mu}/(d\lambda)\bigr)}.

(29)

For Term (II),
Since
|y_{s}|\leq 1
and the difference of indicators satisfies
|\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s)\}-\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}|\leq 1
, at most
M
terms contribute non-trivially. Using
V_{t}^{-1}\preceq\frac{\kappa_{\mu}}{\lambda}\mathbf{I}
and
\|\widetilde{\phi}_{s}\|_{2}\leq L
:

\frac{1}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\left\|\sum_{s=t-M}^{t}\frac{1}{\rho}y_{s}\bigl(\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s)\}-\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}\bigr)\widetilde{\phi}_{s}\right\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\leq\frac{ML}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}.

(30)

For the regularization term,
Since
V_{t}\succeq\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\mathbf{I}
implies
V_{t}^{-1}\preceq\frac{\kappa_{\mu}}{\lambda}\mathbf{I}
, and using the standard assumption
\|\theta\|_{2}\leq 1
:

\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mu}}\|\lambda\theta\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}=\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{\theta^{\top}V_{t}^{-1}\theta}\leq\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{\mu}}{\lambda}}\|\theta\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\lambda\kappa_{\mu}}}{\kappa_{\mu}}.

(31)

Combining,
Substituting (
29
), (
30
), and (
31
) into (
22
):

\|\theta-\theta_{t}\|_{V_{t}}\leq\frac{1}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\left(\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)+d\log\!\bigl(1+tL^{2}\kappa_{\mu}/(d\lambda)\bigr)}+ML+\sqrt{\lambda\kappa_{\mu}}\right)=\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}},

(32)

which completes the proof.
∎

B.2
Pointwise Estimation Error

The confidence ellipsoid immediately implies a pointwise bound on the estimation error for any pair of arms.

Lemma B.2

(Pointwise Estimation Error—Linear Setting)
.

Under the conditions of Lemma
B.1
, for any
t=1,\ldots,T
and all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\bigl|(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))-\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime}))\bigr|\leq\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\left\|\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime})\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}.

(33)

Proof.

Since
f(x)=\theta^{\top}\phi(x)
in the linear setting:

\displaystyle\bigl|(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))-\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime}))\bigr|

\displaystyle=\bigl|(\theta-\theta_{t})^{\top}(\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime}))\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\|\theta-\theta_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}}\cdot\|\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}},

(34)

where the inequality follows from Cauchy–Schwarz in the
V_{t-1}
-norm. Applying Lemma
B.1
yields the desired bound.
∎

B.3
Information Gain Bound

The following lemma bounds the cumulative sum of exploration bonuses, which is essential for converting per-round regret bounds into a cumulative guarantee.

Lemma B.3

(Information Gain—Linear Setting)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, choosing
\lambda\geq\kappa_{\mu}L^{2}
ensures:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2d\log\!\left(1+\frac{TL^{2}\kappa_{\mu}}{d\lambda}\right).

(35)

Proof.

Since
V_{t-1}\succeq\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\mathbf{I}
, we have
V_{t-1}^{-1}\preceq\frac{\kappa_{\mu}}{\lambda}\mathbf{I}
. Therefore:

\|\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{\kappa_{\mu}}{\lambda}\|\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\kappa_{\mu}L^{2}}{\lambda}\leq 1,

(36)

where the last step uses the assumption
\lambda\geq\kappa_{\mu}L^{2}
. Since each summand is at most
1
, the inequality
x\leq 2\log(1+x)
is applicable for
x\in[0,1]
. Thus:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}2\log\bigl(1+\|\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=2\bigl(\log\det V_{T}-\log\det V_{0}\bigr)

\displaystyle=2\log\frac{\det V_{T}}{\det V_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq 2d\log\!\left(1+\frac{TL^{2}\kappa_{\mu}}{d\lambda}\right),

(37)

where the second equality uses the matrix determinant lemma (rank-one updates), and the last inequality follows from Lemma
A.2
with the bound
\det(V_{T})\leq(\lambda/\kappa_{\mu}+TL^{2}/d)^{d}
.
∎

B.4
Proof of the Main Regret Bound (Theorem
4.1
)

We now assemble the preceding lemmas to prove the main regret bound.

Theorem B.4

(Restatement of Theorem
4.1
)
.

Let
\beta_{t}
be defined as in (
17
) and suppose
\lambda>\kappa_{\mu}L^{2}
. With probability at least
1-\delta
:

R_{T}\leq\frac{3}{2}\frac{\beta_{T}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{2dT\log\!\bigl(1+TL^{2}\kappa_{\mu}/(d\lambda)\bigr)}=\tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{M}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\,d\sqrt{T}\right).

(38)

Proof.

Recall that
2r_{t}=f(x_{t}^{*})-f(x_{t,1})+f(x_{t}^{*})-f(x_{t,2})
. We bound each term using Lemma
B.2
:

\displaystyle 2r_{t}

\displaystyle=\bigl[f(x_{t}^{*})-f(x_{t,1})\bigr]+\bigl[f(x_{t}^{*})-f(x_{t,2})\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\left[\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x_{t}^{*})-\phi(x_{t,1}))+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\|\phi(x_{t}^{*})-\phi(x_{t,1})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\left[\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x_{t}^{*})-\phi(x_{t,2}))+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\|\phi(x_{t}^{*})-\phi(x_{t,2})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\right],

(39)

where we applied Lemma
B.2
to both terms (step (a)).

For the second term, we decompose
\phi(x_{t}^{*})-\phi(x_{t,2})=(\phi(x_{t}^{*})-\phi(x_{t,1}))+(\phi(x_{t,1})-\phi(x_{t,2}))
and apply the triangle inequality to the norm (step (b)):

\displaystyle 2r_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 2\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x_{t}^{*})-\phi(x_{t,1}))+2\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\|\phi(x_{t}^{*})-\phi(x_{t,1})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\quad+\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x_{t,1})-\phi(x_{t,2}))+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\|\phi(x_{t,1})-\phi(x_{t,2})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}.

(40)

By the selection rule for the
second arm
(Line
8
of Algorithm
1
),
x_{t,2}
maximizes the UCB over
\mathcal{X}_{t}
. In particular, since
x_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
:

\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x_{t,2})-\phi(x_{t,1}))+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\|\phi(x_{t,2})-\phi(x_{t,1})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\geq\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x_{t}^{*})-\phi(x_{t,1}))+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\|\phi(x_{t}^{*})-\phi(x_{t,1})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}.

(41)

This implies that the first two lines in (
40
) are bounded by the UCB value of
x_{t,2}
(step (c)):

\displaystyle 2r_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 2\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x_{t,2})-\phi(x_{t,1}))+2\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\|\phi(x_{t,2})-\phi(x_{t,1})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\quad+\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x_{t,1})-\phi(x_{t,2}))+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\|\phi(x_{t,1})-\phi(x_{t,2})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x_{t,2})-\phi(x_{t,1}))+3\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\|\phi(x_{t,1})-\phi(x_{t,2})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}.

(42)

By the selection rule for the
first arm
(Line
7
),
x_{t,1}=\argmax_{x}\theta_{t}^{\top}\phi(x)
, so
\theta_{t}^{\top}\phi(x_{t,1})\geq\theta_{t}^{\top}\phi(x_{t,2})
, which means
\theta_{t}^{\top}(\phi(x_{t,2})-\phi(x_{t,1}))\leq 0
(step (d)). Therefore:

2r_{t}\leq 3\frac{\beta_{t}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\|\phi(x_{t,1})-\phi(x_{t,2})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}.

(43)

Using
\beta_{t}\leq\beta_{T}
(since
\beta_{t}
is non-decreasing) and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle R_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\frac{\beta_{T}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\frac{\beta_{T}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\frac{\beta_{T}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{2dT\log\!\bigl(1+TL^{2}\kappa_{\mu}/(d\lambda)\bigr)},

(44)

where the last step applies Lemma
B.3
.

Substituting
\beta_{T}=\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)+d\log(1+TL^{2}\kappa_{\mu}/(d\lambda))}+ML+\sqrt{\lambda\kappa_{\mu}}
and absorbing logarithmic factors into the
\tilde{O}
notation:

R_{T}=\tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}(\sqrt{d}+M)\,d\sqrt{T}\right)=\tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{M}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\,d\sqrt{T}\right),

(45)

where the final simplification holds because
M\geq 1
(a delay threshold of at least one round is necessary). This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Proofs for the Neural Setting (NDB-DF)

In this section, we provide the complete proof of Theorem
4.2
. The analysis proceeds within the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime, where sufficiently wide networks behave approximately as linear functions in the gradient feature space. We leverage this linearization to extend the confidence ellipsoid approach from the linear setting, while carefully controlling the approximation errors that arise from the non-linearity of the neural network.

C.1
Preliminaries: Neural Tangent Kernel Framework

We begin by recalling the key definitions and properties of the NTK framework that underpin our analysis.

NTK Matrix Construction.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{TK}
denote the set of all context-arm feature vectors encountered across
T
rounds with
K
arms per round:
\{x_{t,a}\}_{1\leq t\leq T,1\leq a\leq K}
, indexed as
n=K(t-1)+a
. The Neural Tangent Kernel matrix
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{TK\times TK}
is constructed recursively as follows. Define:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{p,q}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\langle x_{p},x_{q}\rangle,

(46)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{p,q}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{p,p}^{(l)}&\Sigma_{p,q}^{(l)}\\
\Sigma_{q,p}^{(l)}&\Sigma_{q,q}^{(l)}\end{pmatrix},

(47)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{p,q}^{(l+1)}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathbb{E}_{(u,v)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{A}_{p,q}^{(l)})}\bigl[\max\{u,0\}\max\{v,0\}\bigr],

(48)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{p,q}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{p,q}^{(1)},

(49)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{p,q}^{(l+1)}

\displaystyle=2\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{p,q}^{(l)}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(u,v)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{A}_{p,q}^{(l)})}\bigl[\mathds{1}\{u\geq 0\}\mathds{1}\{v\geq 0\}\bigr]+\Sigma_{p,q}^{(l+1)}.

(50)

The NTK matrix is then
\mathbf{H}=\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{p,q}^{(L)}\bigr)+\Sigma^{(L)}
, where
L
is the network depth. Under Assumption
2.2
(B2), this matrix satisfies
\mathbf{H}\succeq\lambda_{0}\mathbf{I}
for some
\lambda_{0}>0
, ensuring the NTK is non-degenerate.

Width Requirements.

Our analysis requires the network width
m
to be sufficiently large, specifically:

m\geq\mathrm{poly}\bigl(T,L,K,1/\kappa_{\mu},L_{\mu},1/\lambda_{0},1/\lambda,\log(1/\delta)\bigr).

(51)

More precisely,
m
must satisfy the following conditions for some absolute constant
C>0
:

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\geq C\,T^{4}K^{4}L^{6}\log(T^{2}K^{2}L/\delta)\,/\,\lambda_{0}^{4},

(52)

\displaystyle m(\log m)^{-3}

\displaystyle\geq C\,\kappa_{\mu}^{-3}\tau^{8}L^{21}\lambda^{-5},

(53)

\displaystyle m(\log m)^{-3}

\displaystyle\geq C\,\kappa_{\mu}^{-3}\tau^{14}L^{21}\lambda^{-11}L_{\mu}^{6},

(54)

\displaystyle m(\log m)^{-3}

\displaystyle\geq C\,T^{14}L^{18}\lambda^{-8},

(55)

where
\tau=2\sqrt{T/(m\lambda)}
is an upper bound on the parameter drift (see Lemma
C.2
below). These polynomial conditions ensure that: (i) the NTK approximation is valid; (ii) gradient perturbation bounds hold; and (iii) linearization errors are negligible relative to the confidence radius.

Effective Dimension.

Define the pairwise NTK Gram matrix
\mathbf{H}^{\prime}\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{(i,j)\in\binom{K}{2}}\frac{1}{m}z_{i,j}^{t}(z_{i,j}^{t})^{\top}
, where
z_{i,j}^{t}\triangleq g(x_{t,i};\theta_{0})-g(x_{t,j};\theta_{0})
. The
effective dimension
is:

\tilde{d}\triangleq\log\det\!\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mu}}{\lambda}\mathbf{H}^{\prime}+\mathbf{I}\right).

(56)

This quantity plays the role of the ambient dimension
d
in the linear setting and captures the intrinsic complexity of the problem in the NTK feature space.

C.2
Key Lemmas for the Neural Setting

We now state and prove the supporting lemmas needed for the main theorem.

Lemma C.1

(Linear Approximation of
f

(
Zhang et al. 2021
, Lemma B.3)
)
.

Suppose the width
m
satisfies (
52
). Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, there exists a parameter
\theta_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
,
t\in[T]
:

f(x)=\langle g(x;\theta_{0}),\,\theta_{f}-\theta_{0}\rangle,\quad\text{with}\quad\sqrt{m}\|\theta_{f}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2\,\mathbf{h}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{h}}\triangleq B,

(57)

where
\mathbf{h}=(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{TK}))^{\top}
is the vector of true rewards and
p
denotes the total number of network parameters.

This lemma ensures that, in the NTK regime, the true reward function
f
admits an exact linear representation in the gradient feature space
g(x;\theta_{0})=\nabla_{\theta}h(x;\theta_{0})
at initialization.

Lemma C.2

(Parameter Norm Bound)
.

For all
t\in[T]
, the estimated parameters satisfy:

\|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2t}{m\lambda}}\triangleq\tau.

(58)

Proof.

Since
\theta_{t}
minimizes
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)
defined in (
7
), we have
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta_{t})\leq\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta_{0})
. From the regularization term:

\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta_{t})\leq\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta_{0}).

(59)

By Assumption
2.2
(B3) and the initialization scheme,
h(x;\theta_{0})=0
for all
x
, so
\mu(h(x_{s,1};\theta_{0})-h(x_{s,2};\theta_{0}))=\mu(0)=1/2
. Evaluating the loss at
\theta_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta_{0})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\left[\omega_{s,t}y_{s}\log\mu(0)+(1-\omega_{s,t}y_{s})\log\mu(0)\right]+0

\displaystyle=-\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\log(1/2)=(t-1)\log 2\leq t\log 2.

(60)

Therefore,
\|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2t\log 2}{m\lambda}\leq\frac{2t}{m\lambda}
. Taking square roots yields the result.
∎

Lemma C.3

(Gradient Stability)
.

Under the width conditions (
53
)–(
55
), for absolute constants
C_{1},C_{3}>0
and with probability at least
1-\delta
, the following hold for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
,
t\in[T]
:

\displaystyle\|g(x;\theta_{t})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\sqrt{m}\,L,

(61)

\displaystyle\|g(x;\theta_{0})-g(x;\theta_{t})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,m^{1/3}\sqrt{\log m}\left(\frac{t}{\lambda}\right)^{1/3}L^{7/2}.

(62)

Proof.

Lemma
C.2
guarantees
\|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\leq\tau=2\sqrt{t/(m\lambda)}
, which satisfies the perturbation radius requirement of the gradient stability results in
Zhang et al. 2021
. Their results then directly yield (
61
) and (
62
).
∎

Lemma C.4

(Linearization Error)
.

Define
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m,t}\triangleq C_{2}\,m^{-1/6}\sqrt{\log m}\,L^{3}(t/\lambda)^{4/3}
for an absolute constant
C_{2}>0
. Then, for all
t\in[T]
and
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
:

\bigl|\langle g(x;\theta_{0})-g(x^{\prime};\theta_{0}),\,\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}\rangle-(h(x;\theta_{t})-h(x^{\prime};\theta_{t}))\bigr|\leq 2\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m,t}.

(63)

Proof.

By the triangle inequality, the left-hand side decomposes as:

\displaystyle\bigl|\langle g(x;\theta_{0})-g(x^{\prime};\theta_{0}),\,\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}\rangle-(h(x;\theta_{t})-h(x^{\prime};\theta_{t}))\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\bigl|\langle g(x;\theta_{0}),\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}\rangle-h(x;\theta_{t})\bigr|+\bigl|h(x^{\prime};\theta_{t})-\langle g(x^{\prime};\theta_{0}),\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}\rangle\bigr|.

(64)

Each term is the single-point linearization error, which is bounded by
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m,t}
using the gradient stability bounds from Lemma
C.3
combined with the Taylor remainder estimate (see
Zhang et al. 2021
). Summing the two terms yields
2\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m,t}
.
∎

C.3
Confidence Ellipsoid for the Neural Estimator

The following lemma is the neural counterpart of Lemma
B.1
. It establishes that the true reward function’s “linear proxy”
\theta_{f}
lies within a confidence ellipsoid around the estimated parameters
\theta_{t}
.

Lemma C.5

(Confidence Ellipsoid—Neural Setting)
.

Define
\beta_{T}\triangleq\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{\tilde{d}+2\log(1/\delta)}
and recall the information matrix
V_{t-1}=\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\phi^{\prime}_{s}(\phi^{\prime}_{s})^{\top}
, where
\phi^{\prime}_{s}\triangleq g(x_{s,1};\theta_{0})-g(x_{s,2};\theta_{0})
. Under the width conditions (
51
), with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\sqrt{m}\,\|\theta_{f}-\theta_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}}\leq\frac{\beta_{T}}{\rho}+B\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}}+1+\frac{M}{\kappa_{\mu}m\rho},\quad\forall\,t\in[T].

(65)

Proof.

For notational convenience, let
\phi^{\prime}_{s}\triangleq g(x_{s,1};\theta_{0})-g(x_{s,2};\theta_{0})
(gradient at initialization),
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{s}\triangleq g(x_{s,1};\theta_{t})-g(x_{s,2};\theta_{t})
(gradient at current estimate), and
\tilde{h}_{s,t}\triangleq h(x_{s,1};\theta_{t})-h(x_{s,2};\theta_{t})
(predicted preference difference).

Define the auxiliary function for any
\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
:

G_{t}(\theta^{\prime})\triangleq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bigl[\mu(\langle\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{0},\phi^{\prime}_{s}\rangle)-\mu(\langle\theta_{f}-\theta_{0},\phi^{\prime}_{s}\rangle)\bigr]\phi^{\prime}_{s}+\lambda(\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{0}).

(66)

By the same mean value theorem argument as in the linear case (using Assumption
2.1
(A2)):

G_{t}(\theta^{\prime}_{1})-G_{t}(\theta^{\prime}_{2})\succeq\kappa_{\mu}\,V_{t-1}\,(\theta^{\prime}_{1}-\theta^{\prime}_{2}),

(67)

where
V_{t-1}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\phi^{\prime}_{s}(\phi^{\prime}_{s})^{\top}+\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\mathbf{I}
.

Since
G_{t}(\theta_{f})=\lambda(\theta_{f}-\theta_{0})
by construction, the same argument as (
22
) yields:

\|\theta_{f}-\theta_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mu}}\|G_{t}(\theta_{t})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mu}}\|\lambda(\theta_{f}-\theta_{0})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}.

(68)

The regularization term is bounded using Lemma
C.1
:

\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mu}}\|\lambda(\theta_{f}-\theta_{0})\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}}\,\|\theta_{f}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}}\,\frac{B}{\sqrt{m}}.

(69)

Denoting
f_{t,s}\triangleq\langle\theta_{t}-\theta_{0},\phi^{\prime}_{s}\rangle
(linearized prediction) and using the identity
\mu(\langle\theta_{f}-\theta_{0},\phi^{\prime}_{s}\rangle)=\mu(f(x_{s,1})-f(x_{s,2}))
from Lemma
C.1
, we expand
G_{t}(\theta_{t})
:

\displaystyle G_{t}(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bigl[\mu(f_{t,s})-\mu(f(x_{s,1})-f(x_{s,2}))\bigr]\phi^{\prime}_{s}+\lambda(\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}).

(70)

Using the observation model
\mu(f(x_{s,1})-f(x_{s,2}))=\frac{1}{\rho}(y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}-\epsilon_{s})
and adding/subtracting the IPW-weighted observation
\frac{1}{\rho}y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s-1)\}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle G_{t}(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m\rho}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\epsilon_{s}\,\phi^{\prime}_{s}+\frac{1}{m\rho}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}y_{s}\bigl(\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t\!-\!s\!-\!1)\}-\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}\bigr)\phi^{\prime}_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bigl(\mu(f_{t,s})-\frac{1}{\rho}y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t\!-\!s\!-\!1)\}\bigr)\phi^{\prime}_{s}+\lambda(\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}).

(71)

We refer to the three groups above as Term (I) (martingale noise), Term (II) (delay bias), and Term (III) (MLE residual with linearization error), respectively.

The first-order optimality condition for the NDB-DF loss (
7
) states:

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bigl(\mu(\tilde{h}_{s,t})-\frac{1}{\rho}y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s-1)\}\bigr)\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{s}+\lambda(\theta_{t}-\theta_{0})=0.

(72)

Note that this condition involves
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{s}
(gradient at
\theta_{t}
) and
\tilde{h}_{s,t}
(network output at
\theta_{t}
), whereas Term (III) involves
\phi^{\prime}_{s}
(gradient at
\theta_{0}
) and
f_{t,s}
(linearized output). We bridge this gap by introducing two auxiliary error terms.

Add and subtract
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{s}
and
\mu(\tilde{h}_{s,t})
inside Term (III):

(III)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bigl(\mu(f_{t,s})-\frac{1}{\rho}y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s-1)\}\bigr)(\phi^{\prime}_{s}-\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{s})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bigl(\mu(f_{t,s})-\mu(\tilde{h}_{s,t})\bigr)\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bigl(\mu(\tilde{h}_{s,t})-\frac{1}{\rho}y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s-1)\}\bigr)\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{s}+\lambda(\theta_{t}-\theta_{0})

\displaystyle\triangleq A_{1}+A_{2}+0,

(73)

where the last line equals zero by the MLE first-order condition (
72
). Therefore,
\text{(III)}=A_{1}+A_{2}
.

We bound each term separately:

For
\|A_{1}\|_{2}
,
Using
|\mu(f_{t,s})-\frac{1}{\rho}y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s-1)\}|\leq 1
and Lemma
C.3
:

\displaystyle\|A_{1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\|\phi^{\prime}_{s}-\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{s}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}2C_{1}\,m^{1/3}\sqrt{\log m}\left(\frac{t}{\lambda}\right)^{1/3}L^{7/2}

\displaystyle=2C_{1}\,m^{-2/3}\sqrt{\log m}\,t^{4/3}\,\lambda^{-1/3}L^{7/2}.

(74)

For
\|A_{2}\|_{2}
,
By the Lipschitz property of
\mu
(with constant
L_{\mu}
) and Lemma
C.4
:

\displaystyle|\mu(f_{t,s})-\mu(\tilde{h}_{s,t})|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mu}|f_{t,s}-\tilde{h}_{s,t}|\leq 2L_{\mu}\,C_{2}\,m^{-1/6}\sqrt{\log m}\,L^{3}\left(\frac{t}{\lambda}\right)^{4/3}.

(75)

Combined with
\|\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{s}\|_{2}\leq 2C_{3}\sqrt{m}\,L
from (
61
):

\displaystyle\|A_{2}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}|\mu(f_{t,s})-\mu(\tilde{h}_{s,t})|\cdot\|\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{s}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4L_{\mu}C_{2}C_{3}\,m^{-2/3}\sqrt{\log m}\,t^{7/3}\,L^{7/2}\,\lambda^{-4/3}.

(76)

Since
V_{t-1}\succeq\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}\mathbf{I}
implies
\|v\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{\mu}/\lambda}\,\|v\|_{2}
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mu}}\|\text{(III)}\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{\mu}}{\lambda}}\bigl(\|A_{1}\|_{2}+\|A_{2}\|_{2}\bigr).

(77)

Under the width conditions (
53
)–(
55
), both
\|A_{1}\|_{2}
and
\|A_{2}\|_{2}
are
o(1/\sqrt{m})
, so their combined contribution satisfies:

\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\kappa_{\mu}}\|\text{(III)}\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\leq 1.

(78)

The noise
\epsilon_{s}=y_{s}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}-\mu(f(x_{s,1})-f(x_{s,2}))\rho
is conditionally
1
-sub-Gaussian (since
|\epsilon_{s}|\leq 1
and
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{s}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1}]=0
). The vectors
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\phi^{\prime}_{s}
are
\mathcal{F}_{s-1}
-measurable. Applying Theorem
A.1
to the sequence
\{\epsilon_{s},\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\phi^{\prime}_{s}\}
:

\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\epsilon_{s}\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{s}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2\log(1/\delta)+\log\frac{\det(V_{t-1})}{\det(V_{0})}.

(79)

Using the effective dimension bound:
\log\frac{\det(V_{t-1})}{\det(V_{0})}\leq\log\det\bigl(\frac{\kappa_{\mu}}{\lambda}\mathbf{H}^{\prime}+\mathbf{I}\bigr)=\tilde{d}
, we obtain:

\frac{1}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\epsilon_{s}\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{s}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\leq\frac{1}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{\tilde{d}+2\log(1/\delta)}=\frac{\beta_{T}}{\rho}.

(80)

By the same argument as in the linear setting, for
s<t-1-M
the difference of indicators vanishes. At most
M
terms contribute, each bounded by
\|\phi^{\prime}_{s}/\sqrt{m}\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{\mu}/\lambda}
. Therefore:

\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\kappa_{\mu}m\rho}\left\|\sum_{s}y_{s}\bigl(\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq\min(M,t-s-1)\}-\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq M\}\bigr)\phi^{\prime}_{s}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\leq\frac{M}{\kappa_{\mu}m\rho}.

(81)

Multiplying (
68
) by
\sqrt{m}
and combining the above bounds:

\displaystyle\sqrt{m}\,\|\theta_{f}-\theta_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{T}}{\rho}+\frac{M}{\kappa_{\mu}m\rho}+1+B\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}},

(82)

which completes the proof.
∎

C.4
Pointwise Estimation Error and Regret Bound

We can now prove the main theorem for the neural setting.

Theorem C.6

(Neural Pointwise Error and Regret—Restatement of Theorem
4.2
)
.

Under the width conditions (
51
) and Assumptions
2.1
–
2.2
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{t}
and
t\in[T]
:

|[f(x)-f(x^{\prime})]-[h(x;\theta_{t})-h(x^{\prime};\theta_{t})]|\leq\nu_{T}\,\sigma_{t-1}(x,x^{\prime})+2\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m,t},

(83)

where
\nu_{T}=(\beta_{T}/\rho+B\sqrt{\lambda/\kappa_{\mu}}+1+M/(\kappa_{\mu}m\rho))\cdot\kappa_{\mu}/\lambda
and
\sigma_{t-1}
is defined in (
12
). Furthermore, the cumulative regret satisfies:

R_{T}=\tilde{O}\!\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{d}}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}+B\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}}+\frac{M}{\kappa_{\mu}m\rho}\right)\sqrt{\tilde{d}\,T}\right)=\tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{M}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\,\tilde{d}\sqrt{T}\right).

(84)

Proof.

Using the linearization from Lemma
C.1
,
f(x)-f(x^{\prime})=\langle\phi^{\prime}_{x,x^{\prime}},\,\theta_{f}-\theta_{0}\rangle
where
\phi^{\prime}_{x,x^{\prime}}\triangleq g(x;\theta_{0})-g(x^{\prime};\theta_{0})
. By the triangle inequality and Lemma
C.4
:

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})-(h(x;\theta_{t})-h(x^{\prime};\theta_{t}))|

\displaystyle\leq|\langle\phi^{\prime}_{x,x^{\prime}},\,\theta_{f}-\theta_{t}\rangle|+|\langle\phi^{\prime}_{x,x^{\prime}},\,\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}\rangle-(h(x;\theta_{t})-h(x^{\prime};\theta_{t}))|.

(85)

The first term is controlled by the confidence ellipsoid (Lemma
C.5
), and the second term is bounded by the linearization error
2\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m,t}
(Lemma
C.4
). For the first term, applying Cauchy–Schwarz and Lemma
C.5
:

\displaystyle|\langle\phi^{\prime}_{x,x^{\prime}},\,\theta_{f}-\theta_{t}\rangle|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{x,x^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{m}},\,\sqrt{m}(\theta_{f}-\theta_{t})\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{x,x^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\cdot\sqrt{m}\,\|\theta_{f}-\theta_{t}\|_{V_{t-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{t-1}(x,x^{\prime})\cdot\frac{\lambda/\kappa_{\mu}}{\sqrt{\lambda/\kappa_{\mu}}}\cdot\left(\frac{\beta_{T}}{\rho}+B\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}}+1+\frac{M}{\kappa_{\mu}m\rho}\right)

\displaystyle=\nu_{T}\,\sigma_{t-1}(x,x^{\prime}).

(86)

Combining (
85
) and (
86
) yields (
83
).

The analysis mirrors the linear case. For the instantaneous regret
2r_{t}=f(x_{t}^{*})-f(x_{t,1})+f(x_{t}^{*})-f(x_{t,2})
, we use the pointwise bound (
83
) (step (a)), the triangle inequality (step (b)), the Lemma
C.4
to relate the linearized inner product to the network output (step (c)), and the arm selection rules (steps (d) and (e)):

\displaystyle 2r_{t}

\displaystyle\leq(h(x_{t,2};\theta_{t})-h(x_{t,1};\theta_{t}))+3\nu_{T}\left\|\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{t,1:2}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}+6\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m,t}

\displaystyle\leq 3\nu_{T}\left\|\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{t,1:2}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}+6\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m,t},

(87)

where
\phi^{\prime}_{t,1:2}\triangleq g(x_{t,1};\theta_{0})-g(x_{t,2};\theta_{0})
and the last step uses
h(x_{t,1};\theta_{t})\geq h(x_{t,2};\theta_{t})
from the first-arm selection rule. The detailed steps (a)–(e) follow the same logical chain as in the linear proof (
Section

B.4
), with the addition of Lemma
C.4
to handle the gap between
f_{t,s}
and
h(\cdot;\theta_{t})
.

Under the width conditions,
6T\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m,T}\leq 1
(the linearization errors are negligible when the network is sufficiently wide). Therefore:

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\nu_{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{t,1:2}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}+3T\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m,T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\nu_{T}\sqrt{T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{t,1:2}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}}+1.

(88)

By Lemma
A.3
(applied with the NTK features
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\phi^{\prime}_{t,1:2}
):

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{t,1:2}}{\sqrt{m}}\right\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2\tilde{d},

(89)

where we used
\log\frac{\det(V_{T})}{\det(V_{0})}\leq\tilde{d}
and verified the unit bound condition
\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\phi^{\prime}_{t,1:2}\|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 1
(which follows from
\lambda/\kappa_{\mu}>1
and
\frac{1}{m}\|\phi^{\prime}_{t,1:2}\|_{2}^{2}\leq c_{0}
for a constant
c_{0}
).

Substituting and using
\beta_{T}=\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{\tilde{d}+2\log(1/\delta)}
:

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\beta_{T}}{\rho}+B\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}}+1+\frac{M}{\kappa_{\mu}m\rho}\right)\frac{\kappa_{\mu}}{\lambda}\sqrt{2\tilde{d}\,T}+1

\displaystyle=\tilde{O}\!\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{d}}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}+B\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\mu}}}+\frac{M}{\kappa_{\mu}m\rho}\right)\sqrt{\tilde{d}\,T}\right).

(90)

When the width
m
is sufficiently large (absorbing the
1/m
factor), the dominant term is
\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{d}}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\sqrt{\tilde{d}\,T}=\frac{\tilde{d}\sqrt{T}}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}
, multiplied by the delay factor. This yields the simplified bound
R_{T}=\tilde{O}(\frac{M}{\rho\kappa_{\mu}}\,\tilde{d}\sqrt{T})
, completing the proof.
∎
