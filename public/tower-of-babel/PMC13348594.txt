A novel Tie2xVEGF bispecific antibody fusion demonstrates enhanced therapeutic efficacy through direct Tie2 activation and VEGF neutralization
Authors: Min-Gi Kwon, Soo Jin Kim, Kangmin Lee, Jin-Hoi Song, Jaemin Lee, Injune Kim, Jeong-Ki Min, Young-Guen Kwon, Dong Gwang Lee, Junyeop Lee, Nam-Kyung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundActivation of endothelial Tie2 signaling has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy for ameliorating vascular abnormalities and hyperpermeability in ocular diseases. Early therapeutic strategies have focused on inhibiting angiopoietin-2 (Ang2), an antagonistic ligand of Tie2, thereby indirectly promoting Tie2 activation. However, accumulating evidence indicates that indirect Tie2 activation via Ang2 blockade is insufficient for enhanced therapeutic efficacy, underscoring the need for Tie2 agonists. In addition, since it is still necessary to inhibit neovascularization induced by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), appropriate therapeutic efficacy could be achieved by direct Tie2 agonism and VEGF neutralization.MethodsAn affinity-matured Tie2-activating antibody, MT-101, was identified by phage display panning using a complementarity-determining region-targeted mutagenesis library. Tie2xVEGF bispecific antibodies were generated with MT-101 fused to five different anti-VEGF modules, and their functionality in Tie2 and VEGF signaling was compared using endothelial cells. The therapeutic efficacy of the bispecific antibody fusion MT-103, comprising MT-101 and VEGFR domains, was evaluated in mouse oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) and laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (LI-CNV) models compared with anti-VEGF agent Aflibercept or Ang2xVEGF bispecific antibody.ResultsMT-101 activated the Tie2 signaling pathway, including AKT-eNOS and ERK cascades, and exhibited efficacy comparable to that of Aflibercept in OIR and LI-CNV models. By directly comparing five different Tie2xVEGF bispecifics, we selected the most potent construct, MT-103, generated by fusing VEGFR1/2 domains to MT-101. MT-103 demonstrated approximately four- and five-fold greater potency than the Ang2xVEGF bispecific antibody in inhibiting VEGF signaling and reducing permeability, respectively, in retinal endothelial cells. MT-103 further demonstrated improved efficacy, reducing neovascularization by 14% compared with Aflibercept in the LI-CNV model and suppressing vascular leakage by 20% compared with Ang2xVEGF bispecific antibody in the OIR model. Moreover, MT-103 elicited robust Tie2 activation and vessel stabilization by enhancing pericyte coverage relative to Ang2xVEGF bispecific antibody.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that a therapeutic strategy combining direct Tie2 activation and VEGF blockade may provide improved therapeutic potential compared to neutralizing VEGF alone or Ang2 and VEGF, representing a promising therapeutic strategy that warrants further validation for the treatment of various ocular diseases.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08457-8.Keywords: Tie2 activation, VEGF neutralization, Bispecific antibody, Tie2xVEGF bispecific antibody fusion, Vessel stabilization, Ocular diseases

Background

Excessive angiogenesis and unstable vessel formation observed in various ocular diseases, such as neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) and diabetic retinopathy (DR), result in vascular leakage and abnormalities. Since vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a crucial angiogenic factor suitable for targeting, anti-VEGF therapeutics have been predominantly employed to treat ocular diseases for more than a decade. Nevertheless, clinical evidence accumulation has revealed therapeutic limitations to neutralizing VEGF alone, such as the appearance of non-responders, anti-VEGF resistance, or macular atrophy with long-term treatment [1–5]. Owing to the indispensability of VEGF neutralization in controlling pathological neovascularization, adjunct therapeutic approaches that can overcome this limitation by synergizing with anti-VEGF therapy are warranted for the optimal treatment of ocular diseases [5, 6]. Potential therapeutic mechanisms, including inflammatory response dampening, choriocapillary regeneration, or vascular stability restoration, have been suggested [7, 8]. In particular, various preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that enhancing vascular stability could be a promising approach for normalizing the pathological vasculature, thereby reducing permeability and macular atrophy progression [9, 10].

Therapeutic approaches have focused on activating the TEK receptor tyrosine kinase Tie2, which plays a pivotal role in maintaining vessels under normal and quiescent condition, for ameliorating vascular abnormalities [11–13]. In the physiological state, the endothelial Tie2 receptor is multimerized and phosphorylated by the Tie2-agonistic ligand angiopoietin-1 (Ang1), which is usually produced by perivascular cells [14]. However, pericyte coverage is reduced during pathological neovascularization, and increased VEGF promotes the secretion of angiopoietin-2 (Ang2), which acts as an antagonist in the interaction between Ang1 and Tie2, resulting in vessel destabilization through Tie2 signaling inhibition [9]. Given this mechanism, Ang2-neutralizing antibodies have been developed to indirectly activate Tie2 signaling by existing Ang1, resulting in the approval of Faricimab, a bivalent bispecific antibody targeting VEGF and Ang2, for the treatment of ocular diseases [15, 16]. However, in the context of Tie2 activation in a pathological environment, no studies have evaluated the functional and therapeutic efficacy between Faricimab, which indirectly activates Tie2 signaling, and a bispecific antibody composed of a direct Tie2 agonist and an anti-VEGF module.

As the Tie2 agonist Ang1 naturally functions as a multimer and exerts therapeutic activity via Tie2 signaling activation in animal models of ocular diseases, previous studies have aimed to develop multimeric Ang1 mimetics [17–20]. However, recombinant Ang1 multimers as a drug modality are unstable and difficult to produce at high yields, indicating poor developability for clinical use [21]. Recently, antibodies that directly target and activate Tie2 signaling have emerged as feasible therapeutics, owing to their excellent developability. For example, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-conjugated anti-Tie2 Fab hexamers or tetravalent Tie2-binding antibodies inhibit vascular permeability via potent Tie2 signaling activation [22, 23]. Additionally, monoclonal Tie2-activating antibodies, including those employed in our previous study, have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in several disease models, including diabetic erectile dysfunction, Clarkson disease, and glioblastoma [24–26]. However, few reports have described the therapeutic potential of antibody-based Tie2 agonists in ocular disease models, and no studies have reported the potential therapeutic rationale for simultaneous direct Tie2 activation and VEGF neutralization using bispecific antibodies.

In this study, we aimed to develop a Tie2xVEGF bispecific antibody exerting optimal therapeutic efficacy against current clinical drugs. An affinity-matured Tie2-activating monoclonal antibody, MT-101, enhanced the potency of Tie2 and cascade signaling activation. Monotherapy with MT-101 demonstrated efficacy comparable to that of Aflibercept in alleviating choroidal neovascularization (CNV) and retinopathy. We generated bispecific variants utilizing MT-101 as a backbone immunoglobulin G (IgG) and five different anti-VEGF/VEGFR2 modules, and found that a bispecific antibody fusion with certain domains of VEGFRs, MT-103, demonstrated the best performance in activating Tie2 and neutralizing VEGF simultaneously. MT-103 exhibited greater efficacy in reducing CNV areas than Aflibercept in laser-induced CNV (LI-CNV) and oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) models. Furthermore, MT-103 was more effective than the Ang2xVEGF bispecific antibody in inhibiting CNV formation and vascular leakage with robust Tie2 activation. Thus, for the first time, we revealed that a bispecific antibody comprising a direct Tie2 agonist and an anti-VEGF module could be considered more beneficial than targeting Ang2 and VEGF for the treatment of ocular diseases.

Methods

Recombinant protein production

pFUSE-hIgG1-Fc and pFUSE-mIgG1-Fc (InvivoGen, USA) expression vectors were used to generate human Fc (hFc) and the extracellular domain of human Tie2 fused with mouse Fc (hTie2-ECD-Fc), respectively. The Fc region in the pFUSE-hIgG1-Fc vector was replaced with a 6xHis tag, and the hTie2-ECD gene was subcloned into the vector to generate 6xHis-tagged hTie2-ECD (hTie2). The expression vector was transfected with HEK293-F cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Collected supernatants were sterilized by a 0.22 μm filter (Merck, USA), and purified by an AKTA Avent 150 (Cytiva, USA) equipped with a MabSelectTM PrismA or a HisTrapTM HP column (Cytiva). Purified proteins were dialyzed against phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4).

Affinity maturation of a Tie2-activating antibody

The parental Tie2-activating antibody, MT-100, was subjected to affinity maturation to optimize the potency of Tie2 signaling activation [24]. Single or multiple amino acid mutations were introduced in the third complementarity-determining region (CDR-H3) of the MT-100 variable heavy (VH) chain and subcloned into the pADL-22c phagemid vector (Antibody Design Labs, USA) with a (G4S)3 linker and variable light (VL) chain of MT-100 to generate a CDR-H3-mutated single-chain variable fragment (scFv) library as described previously [27]. Phage display panning was performed using the extracellular domain of mouse Tie2 fused to human Fc (mTie2-ECD-Fc; R&D Systems, USA), as described previously with some modifications [27]. Briefly, recombinant mTie2-ECD-Fc and hFc were coated on the immunotube (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 0.5 μg/mL and 5 ug/mL in PBS, respectively, and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Phages rescued from the CDR-H3-mutated library were blocked in PBS and 2% nonfat milk for 1 h, followed by depletion in hFc-coated immunotubes for 1 h. Unbound phages were subsequently transferred into mTie2-ECD-Fc-coated immunotubes and further incubated for 1 h. Immunotubes were washed with PBS supplemented with 0.05% Tween20 (PBST), and the bound phages were eluted using 100 mM triethylamine and propagated by infecting into E.coli TG1 strain. Individual scFv clones were screened from the 3rd round output using recombinant hTie2-ECD-Fc and mTie2-ECD-Fc as previously described [24]. Screened scFvs that demonstrate higher binding over the parental MT-100 scFv to both antigens were submitted for Sanger sequencing analysis (Bioneer, Republic of Korea) using a pelB-Forward primer (5′-AATACCTATTGCCTACGGCTG-3′) to identify the VH or VL region sequence. Two CDR-H3 mutant clones, MT-101 and MT-111, were selected from the screening.

IgG production

The VH and VL chains of each affinity-matured scFv were amplified by polymerase chain reaction and subcloned into heavy chain (HC) and light chain (LC) expression vectors, respectively, as previously described [24]. For transient expression of IgGs, a pair of HC and LC expression vectors was co-transfected into ExpiCHO (Thermo Fisher Scientific) cells (6 × 106 /ml, 100 ml), followed by culturing for 14 days. Collected supernatants were centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 40 min, sterilized using a 0.22 μm bottle-top vacuum filter (Merck), and purified by an AKTA Avent 150 (Cytiva) equipped with a MabSelectTM PrismA column (Cytiva). Purified IgG was dialyzed in PBS (pH 7.4) and analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE).

Biolayer interferometry

Biolayer interferometry (BLI) analyses were performed using the Octet R2 system (Saturious, Germany) for affinity measurements of monoclonal or bispecific antibodies as described previously [24]. For monoclonal MT-100 and its derivatives, hTie2-ECD-Fc or mTie2-ECD-Fc was immobilized on an amine-reactive 2nd generation (AR2G) biosensor. The biosensor was allowed to bind to the parental MT-100 IgG or IgG clones selected after affinity maturation at various concentrations in PBS. For Tie2xVEGF bispecific antibody fusion, MT-103, hTie2, or recombinant human VEGF-A (hVEGF; R&D Systems) was immobilized on an AR2G biosensor, and the biosensor was incubated with varying concentrations of MT-103. Association and dissociation kinetics were monitored for 600 s or 700 s, and data were analyzed using Octet Analysis Studio 13.0 software (Saturious) to calculate the Kon, Koff, and KD values.

To evaluate the influence of MT-101 on Ang2-to-Tie2 interactions, recombinant hTie2 was immobilized on an AR2G biosensor, and the biosensor was allowed to bind with or without MT-101 for 600 s. The sensor tip was incubated in PBS for 600 s to set a baseline, and then allowed to bind recombinant human Ang2 (R&D Systems) to determine the association and dissociation kinetics for 600 s, respectively. For the comparison of MT-101 and MT-103, each antibody was immobilized on a biosensor and allowed to bind hTie2. Recombinant Ang2 was subsequently bound to the biosensor, and the sensor was further incubated with recombinant hVEGF (R&D Systems). The sensor was analyzed for respective 600 s for each association and dissociation in the assays. Binding kinetics were visualized using the Octet Analysis Studio software (version 13.0; Saturious) to show the binding interactions.

To assess the Fc-inert function of the bispecific antibody fusion, recombinant human CD16a (hCD16a; R&D Systems) was immobilized on an AR2G biosensor, and MT-103 with or without the L234A/L235A (LALA) mutation was allowed to bind to the sensor tip. Association and dissociation were monitored for respective 600 s, and the binding kinetics were analyzed.

Phospho-RTK arrays

Phosphorylation of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) by Tie2-activating antibodies in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) was analyzed using a Proteome Profiler human phospho-RTK array (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HUVECs were incubated with MT-101 IgG (5 μg/ml) for 30 min, and cell lysates were extracted before or after antibody treatment. Protein concentrations of cell lysates were quantified by BCA assays, and RTK arrays were incubated with 600 µg of the lysates. An anti-phospho-tyrosine antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Merck) was used to detect phosphorylated RTKs in the array.

Flow cytometry

Full-length Tie2 cDNA of humans, monkeys, mice, rats, or dogs was cloned into the pcDNA3.4 vector (Geneart, USA). HEK-293T cells were transiently transfected with mock or Tie2-expressing vectors and used to evaluate the cell-binding activity of the antibodies, as described previously [24]. Briefly, transfected cells were collected and prepared in assay buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated with Tie2-binding antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were then washed and labeled with anti-human Fc antibody labeled with fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Labeled cells were analyzed using a NovoCyte 2000 R combo (Agilent Technologies, USA), and data were visualized using ACEA NovoExpress software (Agilent Technologies).

Cell culture and western blot analysis

HUVECs and human retinal endothelial cells (HRECs) were obtained from Cell Systems (USA) and cultured using EGM-2 (Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 BulletKit; Lonza, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Endothelial cells at passages 2–7 were used for western blot analysis by incubation with or without various concentrations of antibodies, recombinant human VEGF, human angiopoietin-1 (Ang1), or human Ang2 (R&D Systems). Cells were then lysed using 1 ml of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and 1% NP-40), and cell lysates were collected by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 10 min. Supernatants were subjected to immunoprecipitation using an anti-Tie2 antibody (BD Biosciences, USA) at 4 °C overnight, then the precipitated antibody-Tie2 complex was collected and used for western blotting analysis along with whole cell lysates, as previously described [24]. Anti-phospho-tyrosine (PY20; BD Biosciences), anti-phospho-Tie2 (P-Tie2), anti-Tie2, anti-phospho-AKT (P-AKT), anti-AKT, anti-phospho-ERK (P-ERK), anti-ERK, anti-phospho-eNOS (P-eNOS), anti-phospho-FOXO1 (P-FOXO1), anti-FOXO1, anti-phospho-VEGFR2 (P-VEGFR2), anti-VEGFR2, anti- cleaved Caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technologies, USA), anti-eNOS, anti-VCAM-1, anti-ICAM-1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), and anti-β-actin (AbClon, Republic of Korea) antibodies were used for western blot analysis. Immunoreactive protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Cytiva). The intensity of the protein bands was quantified using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health [NIH] 1.34, http://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij/).

Endothelial cell survival assays

Survival of HUVECs was analyzed as previously described with some modifications [24]. Briefly, the HUVECs were plated at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in 24-well plates. The culture medium was changed the next day to M199 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) without FBS for 2 h for serum deprivation, and then treated with antibodies at various concentrations for 40 h at 37 °C in a hypoxic atmosphere (5% CO2 and 1% O2). Cell viability was measured using crystal violet staining. The experiment was carried out in triplicate.

VE-cadherin staining

HRECs (0.5 × 105 cells) were plated in confocal dishes coated with 1% gelatin solution. The following day, the culture medium was replaced with M199 medium without FBS for 2 h for starvation. The cells were treated with VEGF and antibodies at various concentrations for 4 h. After washing with cold PBS, cells were fixed with 10% formalin solution for 15 min at room temperature and then permeabilized with PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 for 30 min on ice. The cells were subsequently incubated with assay solution (PBS supplemented with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA)) for 1 h at room temperature for blocking. The cells were incubated with anti-VE-cadherin antibody (R&D Systems) solution at 4 °C overnight with gentle agitation. After washing with cold PBS, the cells were incubated with secondary antibody solution for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After washing with cold PBS, a mounting solution containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories, USA) was added to each dish, and a coverslip was placed on the mounting solution. Cells were visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), and the fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ZEN microscopy software (Carl Zeiss, Germany). The experiment was carried out in triplicate.

Competitive ELISA analysis

To assess the competitive binding activity of bispecific antibodies, hTie2-ECD was coated overnight at 4 °C on 96-well enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) plates at 0.6 μg/well. The plate was blocked with 2.5% skim milk for 2 h and washed using PBST twice. Varying concentrations of MT-103 or Faricimab (RG7716; MedChemExpress, USA) mixed with recombinant Ang2 with a 6xHis tag (2.5 nM) were added to the plate for 1 h. After incubation, the plate was washed with PBST, and bound Ang2 was detected using an anti-His tag antibody labeled with HRP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at room temperature for 1 h. After washing with PBST thrice, 100 μL of tetramethylbenzidine (BD Bioscience) was added and incubated for 5 min, and the reaction was stopped using 50 μL of 2N H2SO4. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a plate reader, Synergy H1 (Agilent Technologies) and normalized to that of Ang2 alone treated without antibodies to determine the relative binding of Ang2 to Tie2. The experiment was carried out in triplicate.

FITC-dextran permeability assay

HRECs were plated at 1 × 105 cells in 500 μL of EGM-2 in 12-well transwell inserts coated with 1% gelatin solution, and basal wells were loaded with 1 mL of EGM-2. After 3 days, the cells were treated with human VEGF (50 ng/mL), Ang-2 (300 ng/mL), and antibodies at various concentrations in VEGF-excluded EGM-2. After 24 h of incubation, each insert was transferred to an empty 12-well plate and the medium was carefully aspirated. After washing cells with M199 medium, each insert was added with 100 μg of 40 kDa FITC-dextran (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in 500 μL of VEGF-excluded EGM-2 and transferred to a fresh 12-well plate loaded with 1 mL of VEGF-excluded EGM-2. The plates were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Each medium in the basal well was transferred to a 96-well microplate, and fluorescence was measured at 520 nm using a plate reader (Agilent Technologies). The experiment was carried out in triplicate.

Generation of Tie2xVEGF bispecific variants

Five different bispecific antibodies were designed in symmetric tetravalent form by appending anti-VEGF modules to MT-101 with a (G4S)3 linker. The cDNA of VEGFR1-domain 2 fused to VEGFR2-domain 3, Bevacizumab-scFv, Ranibizumab-scFv, Brolucizumab-scFv, and Ramucirumab-scFv was subcloned at the C-terminal end of MT-101 HC to generate MT-103–1, MT-103–2, MT-103–3, MT-103–4, and MT-103–5, respectively. A pair of HC and LC expression vectors was co-transfected into ExpiCHO (Thermo Fisher Scientific) cells (6 × 106 /ml, 100 ml) and transiently expressed bispecific antibodies in supernatants were purified as described in the Methods section under IgG Production. MT-103–1, selected as the final candidate, was further engineered by inserting two alanine mutations at Leu234 and Leu235 in the CH2 region.

Generation of Ang2xVEGF Faricimab surrogate

Faricimab surrogate (Faricimab-S) was generated in the same way as that used by the original developer for efficacy evaluation in murine disease models [15]. Briefly, the constant and variable regions of the HC and LC of the anti-Ang2 arm were designed based on publicly available Faricimab amino acid sequences. For the anti-VEGF-A arm, the variable regions of the HC and LC were substituted with the murine-reactive antibody clone B20-4.1 [28]. Each heavy- and light-chain cDNA was cloned into the pcDNA3.4 vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by constructing four different expression plasmids. Equal amounts of each plasmid were co-transfected into ExpiCHO (Thermo Fisher Scientific) cells (6 × 106 /ml, 100 ml) and the surrogate in culture supernatants was purified using a Protein A column as described in the Methods section under IgG Production. The eluted Faricimab-S was buffer-exchanged with 50 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.0) containing 50 mM NaCl and subjected to further purification via cation-exchange chromatography using a HiScreen Capto S Impact column (Cytiva). Monomeric purity of the surrogate was assessed by size-exclusion high-performance liquid chromatography (SE-HPLC) using an Agilent Infinity 1260 LC (Agilent Technologies) system equipped with an AdvanceBio SEC 300 Å column (2.7 μm, 4.6 × 300 mm) (Agilent Technologies). Fractions corresponding to the monomer peak were collected, buffer-exchanged with PBS (pH 7.2), and analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

OIR

C57BL/6J mice at postnatal day 7 (P7) were used to establish the OIR model. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Asan Medical Center, Seoul, Republic of Korea (IACUC approval protocol #2024–30-057). P7 pups and their nursing mothers were placed in a 75% oxygen chamber for five consecutive days (P7–P12). The oxygen levels were continuously monitored and regulated using the COY oxygen control system. On P12, mice were returned to room air (21% oxygen) until P17 to induce relative hypoxia in the retina, thereby promoting pathological retinal neovascularization; A total of 1 μL of antibodies or Aflibercept was intravitreally injected into the pup vitreous at P12 using a 38 G syringe needle (Incyto, Republic of Korea) to minimize tissue damage. Mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups, and outcome assessment and image quantification were performed in a blinded manner. The number of animals used in each group is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. The mice were sacrificed at P17, and the eyeballs were enucleated for subsequent analysis.

LI-CNV

Three-month-old C57BL/6J or Cdh5-creERT2;tdTomato mice were used for establishing the LI-CNV model. In Cdh5-creERT2;tdTomato mice, tamoxifen was administered three times on alternate days, beginning 2 weeks prior to CV induction, to fluorescently label preexisting vascular endothelial cells. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Asan Medical Center, Seoul, Republic of Korea (IACUC approval protocol #2024–30-057). The mice were anesthetized, and the pupils were dilated before laser treatment. A glass coverslip was placed on the cornea, using 2% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HyCell Solution 2%; Samil Pharmaceutical, Republic of Korea) as the contact medium. Laser burns were induced using a 525 nm argon laser (Pascal Photocoagulator; Topcon, Japan) with the following parameters: 60 µm spot size, 500 mW power, and 0.2 s exposure duration. Antibodies or Aflibercept were intravitreally injected at either 3 days (D3) or 7 days (D7) after laser treatment, depending on the experimental design. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Outcome assessments, including imaging and lesion quantification, were performed in a blinded manner. The number of animals per group is specified in the figure legends. The mice were sacrificed on D8 or D15 and their eyeballs were enucleated for analysis. In vivo imaging of the retinal and choroidal CNV lesions was performed at D7 or D14 using ultra-widefield fundus photography and angiography (Optos California, OPTOS, Scotland). For retinal angiography, fluorescein sodium (Fluorescite Inj. 10%; Alcon, USA) was administered via intraperitoneal injection and visualized using a fluorescein angiography (FAG) channel. For choroidal angiography, indocyanine green dye (Diagnogreen Injection; Daiichi Sankyo, Japan) was similarly injected and imaged using an indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) channel. For western blot analysis, the eyeballs were collected in cold PBS, and the choroid was separated immediately and subjected to protein extraction in RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

In vivo dextran permeability assays

OIR studies were performed with control IgG, MT-103, and Faricimab-S as described in the Methods section. At the end of the study, the mice were anesthetized by injecting a mixture of ketamine (40 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg) intraperitoneally, and lysine-fixable 10 kDa dextran conjugated to FITC (S250; Sigma-Aldrich) was administered intraperitoneally at a concentration of 100 mg/kg body weight. The tracer was allowed to circulate for 40 min, after which the animals were reanesthetized and transcardially perfused with PBS. Retinas were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 2 h, followed by subsequent immunostaining procedures.

Immunohistochemistry

For whole-mount staining, eyeballs were fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight. For whole-mount imaging, the fixed retinas and the choroids were carefully dissected. Choroidal tissues were bleached in 3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) at 4 °C for 3 days to achieve mild depigmentation. Fixed tissues were blocked in 10% BSA or 5% goat serum in PBS/0.5% Triton X-100 overnight at 4 °C and further incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-CD31 (BD Bioscience), anti-Endomucin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-NG2 (Millipore), and anti-P-Tie2 (R&D Systems). After washing with PBS/0.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h, the samples were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with species-specific Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA). Antibody-labeled tissues were mounted using Fluoroshield mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich) and imaged using a Zeiss LSM710 or LSM980 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss) with the ZEN software (SP1 version, Carl Zeiss).

Image analysis and statistical evaluation

In vivo angiography and antibody-labeled retinal and choroidal images were quantified and analyzed using FIJI, ImageJ-based software. Quantitative angiographic measurements were processed and statistically evaluated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Statistical analyses included unpaired Student’s t-tests for two-group comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

An affinity-matured Tie2-activating antibody exhibits a potent Tie2 signaling activation and hinders Ang2 binding to Tie2

We previously reported that a Tie2-activating antibody, MT-100, exhibits therapeutic efficacy against diabetic erectile dysfunction by activating endothelial Tie2 signaling [24]. As the signaling activation potency could depend on the affinity of Tie2-targeting antibodies [29], we attempted to improve the binding affinity of MT-100 in the nanomolar range (Table S1, Additional file 1). A phage library for affinity maturation was constructed by inserting random mutations in CDR-H3 of MT-100 scFv, which was utilized for phage display panning with recombinant hTie2, followed by ELISA screening using human and mouse Tie2 (Figure S1A, Additional file 1). Two dominant mutants, MT-101 and MT-111, were selected based on their increased binding to antigens (Figure S1B, Additional file S1). The clones were then produced as whole IgGs and subjected to affinity measurements with parental MT-100. MT-101 demonstrated KD values approximately seven- and two-fold higher than those of MT-100 for hTie2-ECD and mTie2, respectively (Table S1 and Fig. 1A). Next, we investigated whether MT-101 maintained Tie2 signaling activation. MT-101 specifically phosphorylated Tie2 in HUVECs (Figure S1C, Additional file 1) and activated Tie2 and AKT/ERK signaling even at sub-nanomolar concentrations (Fig. 1B), indicating that signaling activation potency is correlated with the affinity of the antibody. Quantitative analysis indicated a concentration-dependent increase in Tie2 and AKT phosphorylation with MT-101 treatment (Figure S9A, Additional file 1). MT-101 also demonstrated increased phosphorylation of eNOS and FOXO1, which are involved in the Tie2-AKT downstream pathway, for up to 4 h in HUVECs (Fig. 1C). In addition to signaling regulation, MT-101 enhanced endothelial survival with an EC50 of 0.17 nM under serum-deprived and hypoxic conditions, inhibiting the cleavage of caspase-3 (Fig. 1D and E). For regulating vascular inflammation, we investigated whether MT-101 affects the endothelial expression of cell adhesion molecules involved in immune cell binding. We found that vascular inflammatory VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 levels, which were increased by VEGF treatment in HUVECs, were reduced by the addition of MT-101, suggesting an anti-inflammatory ability of MT-101 (Fig. 1F).

MT-101 induces potent Tie2 signaling activation and interferes with Ang2 binding to Tie2. (A) Affinity measurement. Recombinant hTie2-ECD or mTie2-ECD was immobilized on an AR2G biosensor and varying concentrations of MT-101 were allowed to bind to Tie2. KD values were calculated based on measured Kon and Koff kinetics by global fitting analysis. (B) Tie2 signaling activation in endothelial cells. MT-101 at indicated concentrations was incubated with serum-deprived HUVECs, and Tie2 phosphorylation (P-Tie2) was determined from cell lysates immuno-precipitated using an anti-Tie2 antibody. Total and phosphorylated AKT and ERK involved in the Tie2 signaling cascade were detected from whole cell lysates. The relative densitometric quantification of phosphorylated Tie2, AKT, and ERK normalized to their respective total forms is presented in Figure S9A. (C) Evaluation of AKT downstream regulation. HUVECs were treated with 0.5 μg/mL for the indicated times, and phosphorylated AKT, eNOS, and FOXO1 were detected from whole cell lysates. (D) Assessment of endothelial cell survival. Varying concentrations of MT-101 were incubated with HUVECs in a serum-deprived and hypoxic condition for 40 h. Cellular survival activity was measured by trypan blue staining, and relative survival was calculated against the no antibody condition. (E) Determination of the anti-apoptotic effect. HUVECs were incubated in a serum-deprived and hypoxic condition with 5 μg/mL of control IgG or MT-101, and cell lysates were subjected to cleaved caspase-3 detection by western blotting. Cell lysates from HUVECs cultured with serum in a normoxia condition were used as a negative control. (F) Evaluation of cell adhesion molecules related to vascular inflammation. Because direct Tie2 agonists should function in the presence of the antagonist Ang2, it is necessary for MT-101 to activate Tie2 signaling in such an environment. To evaluate this, we investigated whether MT-101 affects the binding of Ang2 to Tie2. In the direct molecular interaction analysis utilizing BLI, when MT-101 occupied Tie2, Ang2 exhibited minimal bonding to the Tie2 and MT-101 complex (Fig. 1G). Notably, flow cytometry analysis revealed that MT-101 binds to the membrane-proximal fibronectin domain of Tie2 rather than to the Ig2 domain, which is located at the ligand-binding site, suggesting that MT-101 engagement with Tie2 may hinder Ang2 binding to Tie2 via the steric hindrance (Figure S3, Additional file 1). In addition to the interference of Ang2 binding to Tie2, we further validated the Tie2 activation effect of MT-101 in the presence of Ang2 on endothelial cells. As shown in Fig. 1H, MT-101 effectively augmented Tie2 and AKT phosphorylation in HUVECs cultured with pre- or co-treated Ang2, as well as without Ang2. These findings indicate that MT-101 not only directly activates the Tie2 signaling pathway but also functionally interferes with the binding of Ang2, supporting its therapeutic potential in pathological conditions associated with elevated Ang2 levels.

MT-101 with monotherapy exerts therapeutic efficacy comparable to that of Aflibercept

Therapeutic antibodies, such as IgG1, may stimulate an unexpected immune response by interacting with Fc gamma receptors on immune cells. We generated two MT-101 variants to minimize the effector function of the Fc region: MT-101 as IgG1 with L234A/L235A mutations in the CH2 region (MT-101 IgG1-LALA), and MT-101 as the IgG4 isotype (MT-101 IgG4). All MT-101 derivatives exhibited comparable binding activity across human, monkey, mouse, rat, and dog Tie2 transiently expressed in HEK-293 cells and showed similar levels of Tie2 phosphorylation activity in HUVECs (Figure S2A and S2B, Additional file 1). As no significant differences in Tie2 phosphorylation were observed among the derivatives, selection was based on Fc engineering considerations rather than signaling potency. MT-101 IgG1-LALA was therefore selected for further development due to its ability to minimize Fcγ receptor–mediated effector functions while preserving Tie2 agonistic activity comparable to the parental IgG1. Accordingly, MT-101 IgG1-LALA was used in subsequent studies (hereafter referred to as MT-101).

Since MT-101 has a distinct therapeutic mode of action compared to that of anti-VEGF drugs, we assessed the in vivo efficacy of MT-101 with the representative anti-VEGF drug Aflibercept in OIR and LI-CNV models. As shown in Fig. 2A, retinopathy was induced by oxygen exposure, and antibodies or Aflibercept were injected via the intravitreal (IVT) route. Flat mount retinas exhibited extensive vascular regression in the group treated with control IgG, whereas Aflibercept or MT-101 significantly reduced the avascular area compared to the control (Fig. 2B). Neovascular tuft (NVT) formation was also decreased by MT-101 or Aflibercept injection, with MT-101 showing a trend toward greater suppression of pathological neovascularization (Fig. 2C). In addition to evaluating MT-101 in the OIR model, we used the LI-CNV model to assess the in vivo efficacy of MT-101. Antibodies or Aflibercept were injected via the IVT route 3 days after laser induction, and angiography analyses were conducted on day 7 (Fig. 2D). The FAG and ICGA results revealed that MT-101 induced a significant reduction in the leakage in the CNV area, to an extent comparable to that observed with Aflibercept (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, the choroidal vessels stained by endomucin progressed in the neovascularized region in the control group, but less so in the MT-101 or Aflibercept treatment group (Fig. 2F). By quantifying the related parameters from the immunostaining results, we found that MT-101 reduced not only the total wound area, but also the CNV area and CNV volume, with efficacy comparable to that of Aflibercept (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MT-101 monotherapy provides marked anti-angiogenic efficacy in vivo, achieving therapeutic outcomes comparable to those of Aflibercept across multiple ocular neovascularization models. Although MT-101 with monotherapy shows promising efficacy, further optimization strategies may enhance its therapeutic potential.

A Tie2xVEGF bispecific fusion consisting of MT-101 and VEGFR1/2 domains shows enhanced regulation of Tie2 and VEGF signaling

Given that MT-101 with Tie2 agonism was as effective as VEGF antagonism alone, we investigated the synergistic effects of MT-101 and anti-VEGF agents. Currently approved drugs inhibiting VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling include Aflibercept (VEGFR1/2 domain–Fc fusion), Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF-A IgG), Ranibizumab (anti-VEGF-A Fab), Brolucizumab (anti-VEGF scFv), and Ramucirumab (anti-VEGFR2 IgG). We designed and generated five bispecific antibody constructs, MT-103–1–MT-103–5, using MT-101 as the IgG backbone (Figure S4A, Additional file 1). These constructs were evaluated in VEGF-stimulated HUVECs to compare their effects on Tie2 downstream AKT/ERK signaling and VEGFR2 inhibition. Among these, MT-103–1, which harbors the D2 domain of VEGFR1 and the D3 domain of VEGFR2 fused to MT-101, exhibited the most potent inhibition of VEGFR2 phosphorylation while maintaining AKT/ERK activation (Figure S4B, Additional file S4). Based on this balanced profile of VEGFR2 inhibition and Tie2 signaling activation, MT-103–1 was selected as the lead bispecific format. We further incorporated LALA mutations to minimize FcγR binding (hereafter referred to as MT-103) (Fig. 3A).

Next, we investigated the binding activity of MT-103. In the BLI analyses, MT-103 demonstrated high affinity for hTie2 (KD = 0.26 nM) and hVEGF (KD = 0.16 nM), while showing markedly reduced binding to hCD16a, as expected from its design (Fig. 3B and C). In addition to its binding properties, MT-103 functionally retained Tie2-agonistic potency comparable to that of monospecific MT-101, activating Tie2 and downstream signaling cascades in HUVECs (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, under VEGF stimulation, MT-103 potently suppressed VEGFR2 phosphorylation and preserved Tie2 activation at sub-nanomolar concentrations, demonstrating its dual signaling regulation capability (Fig. 3E). When compared with the combination of MT-101 and Aflibercept, MT-103 achieved comparable VEGFR2 inhibition but induced a greater enhancement of Tie2–AKT signaling, suggesting that the bispecific format may provide functional advantages over co-administration of individual agents (Fig. 3F). Consistently, MT-103–4, a bispecific antibody containing Brolucizumab scFv, exhibited efficacy comparable to or exceeding that of the combination of MT-101 and Brolucizumab. Since both VEGF and Ang2 coexist under pathological conditions, we further assessed MT-103 activity in the endothelial cells exposed to both ligands. As shown in Fig. 3G, MT-103 functioned simultaneously as a Tie2 agonist and VEGF antagonist in the presence of both VEGF and Ang2. Moreover, in assays of vascular integrity performed with HRECs, MT-103 significantly restored VE-cadherin expression in a dose-dependent manner, counteracting VEGF-induced reduction (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these findings indicate that MT-103 enables coordinated modulation of Tie2 and VEGF signaling, resulting in improved regulation of endothelial function under pathophysiological conditions.

MT-103 inhibits vascular leakage and choroidal neovascularization more effectively than Aflibercept

We next sought to evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of MT-103. To determine the appropriate dosing regimen, we performed LI-CNV studies using the design shown in Fig. 2D, administering MT-103 at 2, 10, or 20 μg per eye. The LI-CNV model was established using C57BL/6J mice. FAG and ICGA revealed that MT-103 reduced vascular leakage and CNV lesion size in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4A and B). In addition, the size of CNV vessels within the choroidal region decreased most strongly at the highest dose (Fig. 4C). The efficacy of MT-103 was also confirmed in a progressive LI-CNV model. As shown in Figure S6A, MT-103 was administered intravitreally 7 days after laser induction, and FAG and ICGA analyses were performed on day 14 (Additional file 1). In this setting, the lowest dose of MT-103 did not significantly reduce vascular leakage, unlike in the conventional CNV model; however, both 10 and 20 μg doses effectively suppressed pre-existing CNV lesions (Figure S6B and S6C, Additional file 1). Moreover, the CNV vessel area decreased at all tested doses, suggesting that MT-103 not only prevented neovascularization but also reduced established CNV lesions (Figure S6D, Additional file 1).

To further investigate the comparative efficacy of MT-103, we evaluated MT-103 alongside Aflibercept and MT-101 in an LI-CNV model using Cdh5-CreERT2;tdTomato transgenic mice, in which VE-cadherin expression was induced by tamoxifen to precisely visualize the blood vessels [30]. As shown in Fig. 4D, all three agents significantly reduced vascular leakage compared to control IgG; however, MT-103 showed greater suppression than Aflibercept under the tested conditions. VE-cadherin immunofluorescence analyses further demonstrated that MT-103 was more effective than either Aflibercept or MT-101 in reducing the CNV area (Fig. 4E). At the protein level, MT-103, but not Aflibercept or MT-101, simultaneously induced Tie2 activation and suppressed VEGFR2 phosphorylation, indicating that its efficacy is associated with dual pathway modulation (Figure S5 and Fig. 4F). These results indicate that MT-103 provides enhanced therapeutic efficacy relative to either Tie2 agonism or VEGF antagonism alone, supporting its potential as an optimized strategy for pathological ocular angiogenesis.

MT-103 exhibits distinct multifunctional activity compared with Faricimab

To benchmark Tie2xVEGF bispecific MT-103 against Faricimab, an Ang2xVEGF bispecific antibody, we assessed the molecular characteristics and signaling profiles of MT-103. The multifunctional properties of MT-103 were evaluated using BLI-based molecular interaction analysis. Similar to MT-101, MT-103 bound to Tie2 and effectively hindered Ang2 binding to Tie2, and the subsequent addition of VEGF resulted in increased binding activity only to the MT-103–Tie2 complex, but not to the MT-101–Tie2 complex (Fig. 5A). As illustrated in Fig. 5B, the BLI results suggested that MT-103 could simultaneously engage Tie2, block Ang2 function, and neutralize VEGF under pathological conditions. Ang2-blocking activity was further evaluated by ELISA, where both MT-103 and Faricimab demonstrated comparable maximal inhibition of Ang2 binding to Tie2; however, MT-103 exhibited a higher IC50 (12.5 nM) than Faricimab, indicating that the monomeric Fab arm for Ang2 in Faricimab might limit its Ang2-binding ability (Fig. 5C). From the VEGF-inhibition perspective, MT-103 suppressed VEGF-induced VEGFR2 phosphorylation in endothelial cells as effectively as Faricimab, yet with higher potency. While the maximum inhibitory effect of Faricimab was demonstrated at 5 nM, MT-103 achieved comparable inhibition even below 1 nM (Fig. 5D). MT-103 also induced robust AKT phosphorylation, attributable to its direct Tie2 agonism. In terms of vascular permeability, we compared MT-103 and Faricimab in endothelial monolayers, where VEGF and Ang2 compromised endothelial integrity. Both MT-103 and Faricimab significantly reduced dextran permeability at 5 nM, and MT-103 maintained endothelial integrity even at 1 nM, which was comparable to that in the ligand-free conditions (Fig. 5E). These results demonstrated that MT-103 uniquely combines VEGF inhibition, Tie2 activation, and Ang2 blockade, providing superior multifunctional activity and therapeutic potential than Faricimab.

MT-103 ameliorates pathological angiogenesis compared with an Ang2xVEGF bispecific surrogate, accompanied by robust Tie2 activation

Since the anti-VEGF arm of Faricimab selectively targets human VEGF, its use in mouse models of ocular disease is limited. To enable in vivo efficacy comparison of MT-103 and Faricimab, we generated a Faricimab surrogate (Faricimab-S) by substituting the original anti-VEGF Fab in Faricimab with a Fab arm that could bind mouse VEGF, as previously reported [15]. The resulting Faricimab-S was purified as an intact bivalent IgG form that could bind effectively to mouse VEGF, whereas the original Faricimab exhibited no binding activity to mouse VEGF (Figure S8, Additional file 1). We compared equimolar doses of MT-103 and Faricimab-S in the LI-CNV and OIR models. In C57BL/6J mice subjected to LI-CNV, FAG analysis revealed that MT-103 suppressed vascular leakage to a degree similar to that of Faricimab-S (Fig. 6A). Both agents significantly reduced the CNV area compared with the control IgG; however, MT-103 showed a greater reduction in CNV area under the tested conditions (Fig. 6B). As this comparison essentially reflected a direct Tie2 agonist versus an Ang2 antagonist, we investigated whether MT-103-induced direct Tie2 activation outperformed Faricimab-S. In the same LI-CNV model, MT-103 robustly induced Tie2 phosphorylation in choroidal vessels, whereas Faricimab-S demonstrated minimal Tie2 activation (Fig. 6C). A similar pattern was observed in retinal vessels, where Tie2 phosphorylation was detected with MT-103 but not with Faricimab-S (Fig. 6D), highlighting the distinct mechanism of direct Tie2 agonism versus indirect Ang2 blockade. Given that Tie2 activation sustains pericyte coverage and thereby reduces vascular leakage [7, 9], we further compared the two agents in the OIR model to assess the maintenance of vascular integrity by MT-103. Immunofluorescence analysis of CD31 and NG2 revealed a significant increase in co-localized regions in MT-103-treated mice, indicating enhanced pericyte coverage (Fig. 6E). To assess vascular permeability, FITC-conjugated dextran was administered prior to sacrifice. As shown in Fig. 6F, MT-103 enhanced the stabilization of the vascular architecture in the retinas compared to control IgG or Faricimab-S, resulting in greater suppression of dextran leakage relative to Faricimab-S. Quantitative image analyses further confirmed that MT-103 significantly reduced the avascular and NVT areas compared to Faricimab-S (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these results indicate that MT-103 confers improved regulation of pathological angiogenesis relative to an Ang2xVEGF bispecific surrogate, associated with its ability to directly activate Tie2 signaling.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated the significant therapeutic efficacy of the direct Tie2 agonist MT-101 in ocular disease models and enhanced efficacy of a Tie2xVEGF bispecific antibody under the tested conditions compared with clinically approved agents. We generated and systematically compared five Tie2xVEGF bispecific constructs, each fused with a different VEGF- or VEGFR2-inhibitory module. Among them, MT-103, which incorporates VEGFR domains, exhibited the most potent inhibition of VEGF signaling, surpassing antibodies that directly target VEGF-A or VEGFR2. Furthermore, MT-103 led to improved control of CNV progression and vascular leakage, showing greater effects compared with not only the anti-VEGF drug Aflibercept but also the Ang2xVEGF bispecific antibody. In the context of controlling Tie2 signaling, Tie2 engagement differentiates MT-103 from the Ang2xVEGF bispecific antibody, suggesting that Tie2 activation by direct Tie2 agonism may contribute to improved efficacy relative to Faricimab, which neutralizes Ang2. Taken together, these data provide evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of a Tie2xVEGF bispecific antibody, through the more robust activation of Tie2, in ocular diseases.

MT-101 induces not only potent and durable Tie2 signaling activation but also provides a dual mechanism by inhibiting the Ang2-Tie2 interaction. This intrinsic property of MT-101 may offer advantages over Ang2-blocking agents that lack agonistic activity. As MT-103 also retains this binding characteristic of MT-101, while additionally neutralizing VEGF, we propose its multifunctionality as a competitive advantage (Fig. 5B). Domain mapping confirmed that MT-101 bound to the fibronectin domain of Tie2 (Figure S3). Since Ang2 interacts with the N-terminal Ig2 domain of Tie2, which constitutes the ligand-binding region with the Ig1 domain [20, 31], it is unlikely that MT-101 directly blocks Ang2 binding through a competitive effect. Instead, these results suggest that MT-101 binding may interfere with the docking of Ang2 via steric hindrance of the Tie2 extracellular region. Although both MT-101 and MT-103 were shown to effectively activate Tie2 not only in the presence of Ang2 on endothelial cells but also in pathological conditions in vivo, the precise molecular mechanism by which MT-101 binding interferes with Ang2 engagement and induces sustained Tie2 activation remains to be further elucidated.

In the TENAYA/LUCERNE phase III trials with patients with nAMD, Faricimab reduced the central subfield thickness (CST) and improved the resolution of intra- and sub-retinal fluid and pigment epithelial detachments (PEDs) compared with Aflibercept, whereas it demonstrated a non-inferior visual acuity gain [16]. This non-inferiority to Aflibercept was still observed in the YOSEMITE/RHINE clinical trials, in which patients with diabetic macular edema (DME) were followed for 2 years [32–34]. Recent real-world data report that switching to Faricimab resulted in no significant changes in visual acuity in patients with nAMD who received prior anti-VEGF treatment or were refractory to anti-VEGF therapy, with persistent retinal fluid in certain patients [35–37]. This clinical evidence suggests that although Faricimab still improves CST compared to anti-VEGF therapy, the efficacy for achieving superior vision gains and reverting leaky vessels may be insufficient and could be further optimized for therapeutic improvement.

Importantly, our study identifies enhanced pericyte coverage as a key mechanistic basis underlying the improved vascular stabilization induced by MT-103. Mechanistically, MT-103 exerts its effects through coordinated modulation of vascular signaling pathways, simultaneously activating Tie2–AKT signaling to promote endothelial stabilization while suppressing VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling to inhibit pathological angiogenesis. Consistent with this, MT-103 was associated with increased pericyte coverage and enhanced vascular stabilization compared with Faricimab-S in LI-CNV and OIR models (Fig. 6). Pericyte recruitment and coverage are critical for maintaining endothelial barrier integrity and reducing vascular permeability; thus, the observed increase in pericyte coverage provides a mechanistic link between Tie2 activation and vascular stabilization. In this context, enhanced pericyte recruitment represents a key downstream consequence of Tie2 activation, linking intracellular signaling to improved vascular integrity and reduced permeability. Although direct comparison was limited to the surrogate (Faricimab-S) due to species-specific VEGF binding, the robust Tie2 activation and improved vascular architecture observed with MT-103 suggest its potential to confer enhanced therapeutic benefit.

Multiple injections of Faricimab significantly reduced the aqueous concentrations of Ang2 and VEGF in the eyes of patients with DME in a recent study; however, significant reductions were also observed in Ang1 and the Ang1/Ang2 ratio [38]. This finding indicates that despite Faricimab treatment, the pathological milieu is still characterized by abnormally low levels of Ang1 or an imbalanced Ang1/Ang2 ratio compared to physiological conditions, suggesting that repeated administration of Faricimab may be insufficient to activate Tie2 signaling and that therapeutic strategies directly inducing Tie2 agonism are warranted. Consistent with this hypothesis, Faricimab is currently being evaluated in a phase II clinical trial in combination with RO7446603, a PEG-conjugated anti-Tie2 Fab hexamer, which implies that Faricimab alone may not adequately activate Tie2 [39]. Meanwhile, in a phase I clinical trial evaluating RO7446603 either as a monotherapy or in combination with Aflibercept or Faricimab, all treatment groups were well tolerated [40], suggesting that the use of MT-103, comprising a direct Tie2 agonist and VEGFR domains, is likely safe in humans. From a therapeutic perspective, the advantage of MT-103 is its ability to simultaneously induce Tie2 agonism and suppress VEGF-driven neovascularization with a single agent, which may offer better clinical development potential than the aforementioned combination strategies. Furthermore, MT-103 may provide substantial therapeutic benefits in patient populations that demonstrated a limited response to Faricimab or are characterized by Ang1 deficiency.

Despite these promising findings, this study has several limitations. First, the efficacy of MT-103 was evaluated in murine models, which may not fully recapitulate human ocular pathophysiology. Second, due to species-specific VEGF binding, a Faricimab surrogate (Faricimab-S) was used for in vivo comparison, which may not fully reflect the properties of the clinical molecule. Third, although our data support a role for Tie2 activation and pericyte recruitment in vascular stabilization, the detailed intracellular signaling pathways linking Tie2 activation to pericyte dynamics and barrier function require further investigation.

Conclusions

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the unique multifunctionality of the bispecific fusion MT-103 enables coordinated modulation of Tie2 and VEGF signaling, supporting its potential as a promising therapeutic strategy for pathological ocular angiogenesis. Further studies and clinical validation will be required to fully establish its therapeutic potential in various ocular diseases.
