Floristic composition and plant community types in Mehal Wonz-Zego forest, north Shewa zone, Amhara regional state, Ethiopia
Authors: Wosen Gultie, Abyot Dibaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study was conducted in Mehal Wonz - Zego Natural Forest, North Shewa Zone, Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia, to identify the plant species, classify plant community types, and relate the community to the environmental variables. The vegetation data were gathered along five transects lines laid around five gogrges distriuted across the forest. Sixty-seven sample plots of 20 m × 20 m (400 m2) were laid at 100 m altitudinal drop in each transect from the top of the forest to the bottom of the forest. Environmental variables such as altitude, grazing, disturbance, aspect, slope, and geographical coordinates were measured in each plot. One hundred six plant species, categorized into 96 genera, 59 families, were recorded. The agglomerative hierarchical cluster analysis identified five plant communities. Asteraceae was the most species rich family while herbs were the dominant growth forms. The canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) showed that altitude, grazing, and disturbance were among the major environmental variables in determining plant species distribution patterns and community type formation. The presence of high species diversity and richness reveals the potential of the area for biodiversity conservation. Thus, all stakeholders, particularly the Ankober district natural resource management office, the Amhara forest enterprise and the regional government, should devise a relevant conservation plan to save this valuable resource.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57719-4.Keywords: Disturbance, Dominant, Floristic composition, Plant community, ZegoSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Forest ecosystems are one of the most important terrestrial biomes, as they play a key role in biodiversity conservation, regulation of hydrological cycles, maintenance of soil fertility, carbon sequestration and provision of socioeconomic services to human populations. Tropical and subtropical forests contain a large proportion of the world’s terrestrial biodiversity and play a central role in global ecological stability. However, forest ecosystems globally are facing escalating threats from rapid deforestation, habitat fragmentation, agricultural expansion, overgrazing, fuelwood extraction, and climate change, leading to significant biodiversity loss and ecological degradation1.

Floristic composition and plant community analyses are important tools in vegetation ecology for providing baseline information on species diversity, vegetation structure, ecological relationships and environmental gradients influencing plant distribution2,3. Vegetation classification also contributes to the identification of ecologically important habitats, the determination of conservation priority areas, and the support of sustainable ecosystem management and biodiversity conservation strategies2,4.

Ethiopia is one of the biologically diverse countries in Africa due to its broad altitudinal variation, complex topography, diverse climatic conditions, and geological history. The country encompasses several vegetation types, including Afroalpine vegetation, Ericaceous belts, dry evergreen Afromontane forests, moist evergreen montane forests, woodlands, and desert scrublands5. The Ethiopian Highlands form part of the Eastern Afromontane biodiversity hotspot, which is internationally recognized for its high species richness and endemism. Ethiopian forests provide essential ecosystem services such as watershed protection, climate regulation, medicinal resources, timber production, grazing land, and habitat for endemic flora and fauna. Despite their ecological and economic significance, most natural forests in Ethiopia are currently under severe anthropogenic pressure, leading to continuous habitat degradation and reduction in plant diversity1,6.

Ethiopia is also known for its diverse flora and abundance of native species. Among the 6027 vascular plants in Ethiopia, 10% are endemic7. This percentage puts the country in the fifth-largest floral diversity in tropical Africa8. However, Ethiopia’s plant diversity has faced severe threats due to the rapidly increasing population and various human-induced factors9. The majority of Ethiopia was once thought to be covered by forests and woodland areas10. Over 44% of the nation’s land area was covered by forests in the 19th century; by the 1950s, that percentage had decreased to 16%11,12, and more recently, it has declined to less than 3%13.

The anthropogenically induced pressures that have significantly impacted Ethiopian flora diversity and coverage include a growing demand for agricultural land expansion, illegal exploitation of forests and forest products, overgrazing, and extensive agricultural investment, increased road construction10,14–19.

Different forest ecosystems in Ethiopia such as Guard Forest in northwestern Ethiopia, Gerba Dima Forest in southwestern Ethiopia, Kenech Natural Forest in Kaffa Zone, Kibate Forest in Wonchi Highlands and Jibat Humid Afromontane Forest in western Ethiopia have been studied in terms of floristic composition and community structure. These studies documented a large variation in species richness, community composition and vegetation-environment relationships among Ethiopian forest ecosystems6,20–22. Thus, there is considerable ecological research on vegetation in Ethiopia, but most of the previous studies have focused mainly in the southwestern, southeastern and western parts of the moist and dry Afromontane forests. Ecological information of the remnant forest patches in North Shewa Zone, Amhara Regional State is very limited. Mehal Wonz-Zego Forest is one of the remaining natural forest ecosystems in central Ethiopia where comprehensive studies on floristic composition, plant community types and vegetation-environment relationships have not been adequately carried out. The forest probably comprises a diversity of woody and herbaceous plant species and could serve as an important refuge for native flora under increasing anthropogenic disturbance. However, the lack of baseline ecological information constitutes a barrier to effective biodiversity conservation and sustainable forest management interventions in the area.

This work contributes to the global scientific effort to document and conserve remnant tropical Afromontane forest ecosystems under increasing anthropogenic and climatic pressures. Recent ecological research has increasingly stressed local biodiversity assessments as important tools to understand ecosystem resilience, species turnover and conservation priorities in fragmented landscapes23. In this respect, the present study adds to the national vegetation database of Ethiopia, and to a broader understanding of Afromontane forest ecology in tropical Africa.

Hence, the present study was designed to investigate floristic composition and plant community types of Mehal Wonz-Zego Forest, North Shewa Zone of Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia. This study hypothesizes that the floristic composition, species diversity, and plant community types of the forest under study are significantly influenced by environmental gradients and anthropogenic disturbances, resulting in ecologically distinct and statistically separable community types. The findings of this study are expected to provide important ecological baseline data for the purpose of biodiversity conservation, ecological monitoring and sustainable forest management in the region.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The Mehal Wonz-Zego forest is found in the North Shewa Zone of the Amhara Regional State in central Ethiopia. The study forest is located at around 50 km East of Debre Berhan town, the capital of the North Shewa Zone, and 180 km north of Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia, at 9°22’-9°45’N and 039°40’-039°53’E. The elevation of the research area ranges from 2074 to 2988 m above sea level, and the ArcGIS-created map (Fig. 1) shows that the total area of the forest is approximately 485 hectares. The Mehal Wonz-Zego Forest is a state-owned natural forest managed by the Amhara Forest Enterprise. This forest is part of the Eastern Afromontane hot spot notable for its diverse flora and fauna, while simultaneously facing severe threats to its continued survival. The geology of the study area consists of Rocky Mountains, rolling terrain, and an escarpment that extends from 2074 to about 2988 m above sea level. The escarpment is divided by several valleys. Some peaks are covered by the Mehal Wonz-Zego forest (AWAO, 2014). Many of the peaks are covered with lianas, herbs, trees, and bushes. The majority of the forest and the study site are surrounded by mountains such as Eme-Mihret, Ayandity, Garakoso, and Bargie Gedel, so it looks like a fence has been put up around the forest (Fig. 2).

The agroecology of the Mehal Wonz-Zego Forest encompasses arid, semi-arid, temperate, and alpine zones. According to 13 years (2010–2022) of climate data recorded from the National Meteorological Services Agency’s at Ankober station, the nearest station to the study site, the area receives an annual average rainfall of 853 mm. The area experiences unimodal rainfall which extends for nine months, from February to September. The average annual temperature, the average monthly maximum and minimum temperatures of the study area are 20.2 °C, 30.8 °C and 9.8 °C respectively (NMSA, 2022) (Fig. 3).

The Mehal Wonz-Zego forest is best classified as a dry evergreen Afromontane forest on the eastern escarpment of the North East highlands. Its floristic composition varies significantly by elevation5. Maesa lanceolata, Clutia abyssinica, Juniperus procera, Croton macrostachyus, Ekebergia capensis, Gymnosporia obscura, and Myrica salicifolia were among the common tree species found at higher elevations. At moderate elevation, Olea capensis, Juniperus procera, Olea europaea subsp. cuspidata, Maesa lanceolata, Podocarpus falcatus, Bersama abyssinica, and Rhus natalensis were the dominant species. Common species found in the forest included Olea capensis, Calpurnia aurea, Carissa spinarum, Lobelia rhynchopetalum, Discopodium penninervium, Maerua aethiopica, Premna schimperi, Podocarpus falcatus, Juniperus procera, Bersama abyssinica, Gymnosporia obscura, and Maesa lanceolata.

Sampling design

The vegetation data were collected using the transect sampling technique2,3. We set up sample plots along five transect lines laid around five gogrges distriuted throughout the forest. The plots were placed at 100 m altitudinal drop along these lines. To gather representative samples from the forest, we established a total of sixty-seven (67) plots. Each plot measured 20 m x 20 m (400 m²) for tree species. We also used four subplots measuring 3 m x 3 m (9 m²) for shrubs and climbers, along with two subplots of 1 m x 1 m (1 m²) for herbaceous species.

We visually estimated the percentage cover of all plant species in the sample plots and the percentage cover was transformed using the 1–9 Braun-Blanquet scale to standardize abundance values2. We checked ground cover for herbaceous plants and lower shrubs while measuring canopy cover for trees and taller shrubs.

We documented every plant species found in each plot. Plant specimens were collected, pressed, dried, and sent to the botany laboratory in the biology department at Debre Berhan University for identification and deposited in temporary herbarium found at the botany laboratory. We identified the specimens by referring to published volumes of the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea along with taxonomic keys (Fig. 4).

Layout of the study plot.

We recorded environmental factors such as altitude, slope, and aspect for each quadrat using GPS, a clinometer, and a compass. We also noted any disturbances present. We coded the aspect values according to Zerihun Woldu et al.27, where N = 0, NE = 1, E = 2, SE = 3, S = 4, SW = 3.25, W = 2.5, NW = 1.25, before we started the analysis. For each sample plot, we assessed the level of disturbance using a five-point scale based on the method from Anderson and Currier28. We assigned disturbance scores subjectively based on visible signs of tree stumps. The scale was defined as follows: 0 = No disturbance, 1 = 0–20% of the quadrat disturbed, 2 = 21–40% of the quadrat disturbed, 3 = 41–60% of the quadrat disturbed, 4 = 61–80% of the quadrat disturbed, 5 = 81–100% of the quadrat disturbed. The grazing intensity is estimated on the following subjective scale: 0 = nill; 1 = slight; 2 = moderate; 3 = heavy; 4 = destructive29.

Data analysis

Vegetation data from 67 plots and 106 species were grouped into plant communities using agglomerative hierarchical clustering with the similarity ratio and Ward’s linkage method. The decision on the number of groups (clusters) was based on objective methods of obtaining an optimal number of clusters. From the output of objective method, a sharp bend at the fifth cluster in the plot could be a good indication of the number of clusters in the data30. The community types identified from the cluster analysis were further refined in a synoptic table where species occurrences were summarized as synoptic cover-abundance values31. Dominant species of each community type were identified based on their synoptic values and community types were named after one or more dominant species2.

We further identified indicator species for each group using the indicspecies package in R, which uses randomization to test for statistical significance. Finally, species richness, Shannon-Wiener diversity (H’) and evenness (J) were calculated in R32 to characterize the diversity of the identified communities.

We correlated environmental variables and vegetative data through Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA). In this study, (CCA) ordination was used in describing the pattern of plant communities along an environmental gradient since the preliminary analysis of the vegetation data using Deterended Correspondence Analysis (DCA) revealed that the longest axis of DCA for the dataset was greater than 3 (= 3.5). Pearson’s product-moment correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate the relationship between the environmental variables. In addition, data of the five environmental variables were tested using Monte Carlo permutation tests to show their significance. Absence of soil parameters is one of the limitations of this study in showing how environmental factor determine plant community distribution.

Results

Floristic composition of Mehal Wonz - Zego forest

The Mehal Wonz-Zego forest’s floristic analysis result revealed 106 species of ferns, conifers, and flowering plants, arranged in 59 families and 96 genera (Table 1). Two were pteridophytes, three were gymnosperms, and 101 were angiosperms. 50% of the families had more than one species. Asteraceae (8 genera, 12 species), Oleaceae (2 genera, 8 species), Cyperaceae (2 genera, 7 species), Urticaceae (3 genera, 7 species), Poaceae, and Euphorbiaceae (4 genera, 5 species), and Aspleniaceae, Boraginaceae, and Myrsinaceae (four species each) were the families with the greatest number of species. Acanthaceae, Asclepiadaceae, Fabaceae, Lamiaceae, Melianthaceae, and Rosaceae were represented by three species each.

Community 1(Maesa lanceolata - Clutia abyssinica Community)

This community was distributed in the altitudinal range of 2539–2988 m.a.s.l., primarily on the 2–37% steep slope section of the forest. The community possessed fifteen plots, totaling 0.6 hectares. In the tree layer, the most prevalent species was Maesa lanceolata. The most prevalent tree species included Bersama abyssinica, Juniperus procera, Croton macrostachyus, Ekebergia capensis, Gymnosporia obscura, and Myrica salicifolia. Discopodium penninervium and Clutia abyssinica were the two most common shrubs. Calpurnia aurea and Inula confertiflora were among the most frequent shrub species. The dominating liana in the community was Mikaniopsis clematoides. The common species in the ground layer include Peperomia abyssinica, Rumex nepalensis, Oplismenus hirtellus, Kalanchoe petitiana, Hypoestes forskaolii, Asplenium unilaterale, Cyperus longus, and Cynodon dactylon. Table 4 shows that five of the 25 species in this community had significant indicator values (P < 0.05, p < 0.01, and P < 0.001).

Community 2 (Olea capensis - Juniperus procera Community)

This community had a slope of 4 to 30% and an elevation of 2634 to 2751 m above sea level. Eleven plots totaling 0.44 hectares make up this community. The two most prevalent species in the tree layer were Juniperus procera and Olea capensis. Maesa lanceolata, Bersama abyssinica, Rhus natalensis, Podocarpus falcatus, Gymnosporia obscura, Olea europaea subsp. cuspidata, and Halleria lucida were among the well-known tree species. The most common shrubs in this community were Discopodium penninervium and Clutia abyssinica. Mikaniopsis clematoides is the dominant liana in this community. The most prevalent species in the ground layer were Hypoestes forskaolii, Peperomia abyssinica, Oplismenus hirtellus and Asplenium unilaterale. Seventeen (17) species were linked to this community and have three indicator species with significant indicator values (P < 0.05, P < 0.01 (Table 4).

Community 3 (Olea europaea subsp. cuspidata- Discopodium penninervium Community)

This community was distributed at elevations ranging from 2513 to 2736 m above sea level, with a slope of 2 to 28%. Twenty-one plots (0.84 hectare) were associated to the community. The tree layer’s prominent species included Olea europaea subsp. cuspidata, Juniperus procera, Maesa lanceolata, Bersama abyssinica, Podocarpus falcatus, and Rhus natalensis. The common tree species included Gymnosporia obscura and Halleria lucida. Discopodium penninervium and Clutia abyssinica were the predominant shrub species. Common species in the shrub layer included Inula confertiflora and Lobelia rhynchopetalum. Mikaniopsis clematoides was the most often grown liana in this community. Peperomia abyssinica, Hypoestes forskaolii, and Oplismenus hirtellus were the dominant species in the ground strata. This community’s common herb species included Asplenium unilaterale, Kalanchoe petitiana, and Pennisetum thunbergii. Twenty-one species were linked with this community and have one indicator species with significance values (P < 0.01 (Table 4).

Community 4 (Rhus natalensis - Podocarpus falcatus Community)

The community was located between 2517 and 2619 m above sea level, with a slope ranging from 3 to 15%. It consists of 11 plots (0.44 hectares). The community’s dominant tree species include Rhus natalensis, Podocarpus falcatus, Juniperus procera, Bersama abyssinica, Gymnosporia obscura, and Maesa lanceolata. The common species in this community are Olea europaea subsp. Cuspidata and Myrica salicifolia. Discopodium penninervium, Calpurnia aurea, and Clutia abyssinica were also common shrubs in this area. The lianas are Jasminum abyssinicum, Embelia schimperii, and Periploca linearifolia. The field layer is dominated with species of Oplismenus hirtellus, Hypoestes forskaolii, and Peperomia abyssinica. Kalanchoe petitiana, Pennisetum thunbergii, and Cyperus fischerianus were common herbs in this community. Twenty-one species were associated with this community as indicator species, and six of the indicator species exhibit significant indicator values (P < 0.05), (P < 0.01), and (P < 0.001 (Table 3).

Community 5 (Carissa spinarum - Gymnosporia obscura Community)

This community was distributed in the altitudinal range of 2074–2251 m.a.s.l., and on relatively lower slope ranging from 4 to 28%. Nine plots (0.36 hectare) were associated to the community. The community’s dominant tree species included Gymnosporia obscura, Juniperus procera, Olea capensis, Podocarpus falcatus, Calpurnia aurea, and Bersama abyssinica. This community’s common herbs included Hypoestes forskaolii, Asplenium unilaterale, Bidens pilosa, Cyperus dichroostachyus, and Tagetes minuta. This community had 29 indicator species, with 12 exhibiting indicator values (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001, as shown in Table 4).

The concentration of the sample plots in the biplot center enabled their classification into five community groups, which depict the overall forest vegetation patterns in the Mehal Wonz-Zego forest.

Correspondence analysis (CCA) ordination biplot.

As depicted from the CCA ordination biplot, community one was correlated with altitude while community two was more closely related to the aspect. Community five was distributed at a lower altitude. Altitude is a constraining variable with a high positive correlation with CCA1 of the ordination diagram (r = 0.99; p < 0.05), but slope does not correlate with the CCA1 ordination axis (CCA1) (r = 0.027; p > 0.05). However, aspect demonstrated a considerable negative correlation with the second axis (Table 8). Thus, altitude was the most important constraining variable in weighing axis one of the ordination results, followed by grazing, human disturbance, and aspect. Hence, altitude accurately described the distribution and patterns of plant species found in the Mehal Wonz-Zego forest. CCA2 demonstrates the effects of grazing (r = 0.56) and disturbance (r = 0.131) on the distribution of plant species in Mehal Wonz-Zego forest (Table 8).

Discussion

Floristic composition of Mehal Wonz - Zego forest

The species richness of the Mehal Wonz-Zego forest is higher than some dry afromontane forests in Ethiopia which are comparable in their size. For example, Debrelibanos monastery has 60 species34. It is roughly the same as Kenech, which has 108 species35. Wof-Washa also has 108 species36. However, the species richness in the Mehal Wonz-Zego forest is much lower than other dry afromontane forests in Ethiopia. For instance, Zegie has 113 species37, Mena Angetu has 212 species38, and Wof Washa has 394 species39. The difference in species richness among the compared forests could be attributed to the variations of forest sites with regard to geographical location, altitude, anthropogenic impact, rainfall and other climatic, physiographic and edaphic factors40.

In the Mehal Wonz-Zego forest, the family Asteraceae has the highest number of species followed by Oleaceae, Urticaceae, Cyperaceae and Poaceae and Euphorbiaceae respectively. The dominance of these families: Asteraceae, Oleaceae, and Poaceae align with research showing their strong presence in the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea. Their dominance in the study area could be attributed to their effective pollination, dispersal, and germination strategies that allow them to thrive in the landscape18. Good dispersal strategies, diverse growth forms, and the ability to adapt to different environments all contribute to species richness41,42.

The predominance of herbaceous growth forms in the study forest could be associated to significant human disturbances and an open canopy that supports herb growth43. Human activities and selective logging for various purposes may promote the growth of herbaceous species in Ethiopia’s dry afromontane forests38,44. This observation aligns with the prevalence of herbaceous species in other dry evergreen afromontane forests39,45.

Community types

The observed variation in species diversity between plant communities in the study area could be explained by differences in altitudinal gradients, the effect of environmental variables, habitat disturbance caused by cattle barns, the presence of traditional beekeeping, roads within the forest, and undulated peaks in the northwestern and western parts. Furthermore, Mehal Wonz - Zego forest is subjected to severe anthropogenic pressure, particularly timber production from Podocarpus falcatus, Juniperus procera, and Hagenia abysinica, fuelwood production from Olea europaea subsp.cuspidata and Allophylus abyssinicus, and year-round free grazing, which contributes significantly to the variation in floristic composition among community types. Similar findings were reported by Hedberg41], Wassie et al.46, and Neelo et al.47. The five plant communities showed a slight variation in their species composition, which could be attributed primarily to the communities’ differences in terms of location, altitude, human impact, grazing, rainfall, slope, and other factors.

The Shannon-Weiner index revealed high species diversity (3.06–3.68) and evenness across all five plant communities. This diversity is driven by topographical variability, habitat productivity, and rainfall. Additionally, altitude, slope, and aspect significantly influence species distribution, while selective disturbances impact overall community compositions48,49.

The relationship between plant communities with environmental variables

In the Mehal Wonz-Zego forest, plant communities are heavily shaped by environmental factors, particularly grazing and human disturbance. While shifting environmental gradients cause most plant groups to share species, allowing them to adapt to varied habitats, altitude acts as the primary constraint on vegetation distribution, supported by changes in local humidity, temperature, and soil50. Altitude acts as the most critical constraining variable, showing a strong positive correlation with ordination axis one (r = 0.99), (p < 0.05). Altitudinal shifts drive changes in humidity, temperature, and soil type, directly dictating plant growth and development. Grazing and disturbance were identified as the most significant environmental variables shaping community structure. Anthropogenic disturbance negatively impacts tropical forests by severely hindering natural regeneration and seedling establishment51,52. With the exception of community type five, plant communities commonly share species with their neighbors as environmental gradients shift. This overlap enables species to endure a wide array of habitat conditions. These findings align with established ecological principles and previous research in Afromontane forests, reinforcing that geography, climate, and human disturbance heavily influence plant community distribution21,53–57.

Conclusion

The Mehal Wonz-Zego forest exhibits rich biodiversity, evidenced by the documentation of 106 plant species. This high floristic diversity is driven by topographic heterogeneity, featuring plateaus, peaks, and deep gorges which creates distinct microhabitats for various organisms. Vegetation distribution is heavily influenced by altitudinal gradients, which showed a strong positive correlation with CCA1 (Canonical Correspondence Analysis). Plant diversity varies significantly across community types due to altitude, grazing, slope, and human disturbance.

The abundance of species diversity demonstrates the area’s potential for biodiversity conservation. As a result, all parties, including the Amhara forest enterprise, the regional government and the Ankober district where the forest is found, must implement a robust conservation strategy to protect this unique resource. Furthermore, the competent entity should take adequate action to ban the widespread unlawful cutting for charcoal and timber production in the study forest. Awareness creation and training on ecosystem services and wise use of the forest resource on sustainable manner should be given to the local community by the Ankober district natural resource management office58.
