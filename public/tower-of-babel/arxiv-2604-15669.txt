Self-Organization to the Edge of Ergodicity Breaking in a Complex Adaptive System
Nixie Sapphira Lesmana 1,2, Ling Feng 3,1, Kan Chen 4,5, Choy Heng Lai 1

Abstract
Self-organized criticality (SOC) is widely proposed as a fundamental mechanism for collective behavior, yet its role in objective-driven, heterogeneous adaptive systems underpinning real complex systems remains less understood. We introduce EvoSK, a minimal evolutionary model in which agents perform memory-dependent reinforcement learning on a rugged Sherrington–Kirkpatrick landscape while the population evolves through extremal replacement of the least-fit agents. We demonstrate that this coupled dynamics drives the system to a critical state residing on the transition boundary between ergodic and non-ergodic phases. At this boundary, the system exhibits scale-free evolutionary avalanches with a mean-field exponent τ ≈ − 1.5 \tau\approx-1.5 , while simultaneously achieving collective rewards that surpass those of any manually fine-tuned, non-evolutionary regime. Our results provide a mechanistic link between the statistical physics of ergodicity breaking and the functional optimality of complex adaptive systems, suggesting that the edge of ergodicity breaking acts as a robust attractor for systems adapting on rugged, high-dimensional landscapes.

Introduction

Understanding how adaptive systems organize collective behavior in complex environments remains a central challenge across physics

In contrast, living and adaptive systems – from biological to social and economic – typically feature heterogeneous interactions and pursue explicit objectives such as survival, energy efficiency, or profit maximization, making true optimization under such constraints highly nontrivial. Key open questions therefore remain: Do genuinely complex adaptive systems self-organize toward criticality without external tuning? And if so, what is the fundamental nature of that criticality?

To address these questions we construct a minimal evolutionary spin-glass model (EvoSK) that embodies the three essential ingredients of genuinely complex adaptive systems: (a) many-body nonlinear heterogeneous interactions, realized via quenched random couplings in the mean-field Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) framework
15
;
10
; (b) adaptive agents that optimize local payoffs through memory-dependent reinforcement learning
10
;
13
;
19
; and (c) Darwinian evolution through extinction and replacement of the least-fit agents, implemented via extremal dynamics
1
. The system is a direct extension of the Bak–Sneppen rules
1
but explicitly incorporates heterogeneous interactions and adaptive learning, thereby capturing both the complexity and the adaptive selection pressures in living systems.

Our analysis on the experiments reveal three main results. First, the system autonomously evolves to a steady state whose collective performance surpasses that achieved by any manually tuned non-evolutionary dynamics with the same adaptive behaviors. Second, avalanche-size distributions exhibit clear power-law scaling with exponent
\tau\approx-1.5
, consistent with the mean-field universality class of evolutionary SOC
1
and empirical observations in biological neural systems
3
. Third, and perhaps most importantly, this self-organized optimal state resides at the transition between ergodic and non-ergodic phases, i.e., at the edge of ergodicity breaking. The identification of ergodicity (breaking) is done through probing the evolved temperature distribution through a total-variation-based ergodicity coefficient and characterizing collective cascades via avalanche statistics. We find that within a broad range of adaptivity levels characterized by memory strength, the system spontaneously organizes to this edge of ergodicity breaking, and at the same time the systems as a whole achieves near-optimal rewards that outperform both fixed-temperature and non-evolutionary baselines, despite the fact that individual agents only optimize their local payoffs.

II
Model

II.1
SK Games: Reinforcement Learning on a Spin-Glass Landscape

We consider the
SK
game
10
as a stylized model of learning in a complex environment. The system consists of
N
interacting
i
-indexed agents, each choosing a binary action
\sigma_{i}(t)\in\{-1,+1\}
at every discrete times
t
. Agent interactions are encoded in a quenched random coupling matrix
[J]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, with entries

J_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/N),J_{ii}=0,

corresponding to the mean-field Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) spin glass model. Throughout this work, we focus on the fully symmetric case
J_{ij}=J_{ji}
, which admits an underlying energy function known to be favorable for collective learning and optimization. Asymmetric cases have been discussed in prior works, such as
10
, and are beyond our current scope.

Each agent maintains an action value (or
q
-value) function
q^{\sigma}_{i}(t)
that evolves according to a simple reinforcement-learning rule,

q^{\sigma}_{i}(t+1)=(1-\alpha)q^{\sigma}_{i}(t)+\alpha R^{\sigma}_{i}(t),

(1)

where a memory parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
controls the update rate after receiving each instantaneous reward
R^{\sigma}_{i}(t)
:

R^{\sigma}_{i}(t):=\sigma\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}\sigma_{j}(t).

(2)

Actions are chosen stochastically via a softmax policy
\mathbb{P}
:

\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{i}(t)=\sigma)=\frac{e^{\beta q^{\sigma}_{i}(t)}}{\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}=\pm 1}e^{\beta q^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{i}(t)}}.

(3)

where
\beta=\frac{1}{T}
is an inverse temperature that controls exploration randomness characterizing the agent’s behaviour.

Equations (
1
)-(
3
) define the baseline
SK
learning dynamics. The parameter
\alpha
defines the agents’ effective memory (small
\alpha
: long memory, large
\alpha
: rapid forgetting), while the parameter
T
sets the exploration level.
At
t=0
, all
q_{i}^{\sigma}
are initialized to zero, corresponding to purely random actions.
q
-value updates and action choices are performed simultaneously across agents.

In this baseline dynamics without evolutionary adaptation, both
\alpha
and
T
are fixed externally. Previous studies have shown that the
SK
game behavior is highly sensitive to these two parameters; for instance, low
T
leads to frozen, ordered dynamics while high
T
produces noisy, inefficient exploration. In this work, the
SK
game provides a reference framework upon which our evolutionary extension is built and against which its adaptive dynamics are benchmarked.

II.2
Evolutionary SK Game with Endogenous Noise Adaptation

The
SK
Game described in Sec.
II.1
assumes a fixed population of learning agents with static learning parameters. To study how learning dynamics interact with evolutionary selection, we embed the
SK
Game into an evolutionary environment in which agents are periodically replaced or mutated based on their performance (fitness). To allow for diversity in exploration behavior, we randomly assign a unique
T_{i}
value to each agent
i
, uniformly sampled from the range
[0,2]
. This range of temperature is chosen to cover diverse exploration levels, from below the known critical temperature
T_{c}=1
of the SK model to above it.

Our construction follows the logic of extremal dynamics, as in the Bak-Sneppen mechanism
1
.
In our system, at each discrete time step
t
, agents first update their action values
q_{i}^{\sigma}(t)
and choose actions
\sigma_{i}(t)
according to the baseline
SK
Game. Let us define the instantaneous fitness of agent
i
as

f_{i}(t):=q_{i}^{\sigma_{i}(t)}(t),

(4)

i.e., the value associated with the action actually taken. An evolutionary
replacement
then takes place. Here, the least-fit agent
i
, i.e. with the lowest fitness value
f_{i}(t)
,
is removed and replaced by a new agent. A new agent
i^{*}
replaces
i
and is initialized with neutral
q
-values,

q_{i^{*}}^{\sigma}(t)=0\quad\forall\sigma\in\{\pm 1\},

(5)

corresponding to an untrained learner.
Its learning behavior
T_{i^{*}}
is again sampled uniformly from the interval
[0,2]
, the same as the original population.
Following the evolutionary replacement steps, we perform two
SK
game updates in (
1
)-(
3
) to initialize the new agent’s actions and propogate its influence, before advancing to the next time step of
EvoSK
game.

Emergent Optimality, Criticality, and Avalanches

III.2
Evolution to the Edge of Ergodicity

For each memory level
\alpha
,
EvoSK
produces a stationary set of agent temperatures
\{T_{i}\}
. Notably,
\{T_{i}\}
acts as the effective noise strength in the underlying
SK
dynamics. To determine if our evolutionary pressures have driven exploration behavior toward a critical regime, we assess where the system dynamics under
\{T_{i}\}
lie in a phase diagram by evaluating its total variation-based ergodicity coefficient
\epsilon

18
;
20

\epsilon(\lambda)
can then be derived by quantifying the sensitivity of the system’s ergodicity by perturbing
\{T_{i}\}
: all agents’ temperatures are rescaled by a factor of
2^{\lambda}
. Using
\epsilon(\lambda)
, we can distinguish ergodic(
\epsilon\approx 0
), non-ergodic (
\epsilon\approx 1
), and edge (where
0<\epsilon<1
) regimes. Formal definitions and implementations are given in Appendix
A.1
.

Fig.
2
shows
\epsilon(\lambda)
associated with
EvoSK
dynamics at different memory level
\alpha
.
It can be seen that for a broad range of
\alpha
values, the steady state of the system lies in the edge, where
\epsilon
is between
0
and
1
.
For very short memory (
\alpha=0.3
), the system (at
\lambda=0
) settles close to the ergodicity-breaking phase, exhibiting higher
\epsilon
values slightly less than 1.
For intermediate memory (
\alpha=0.5–0.8
), the evolved temperatures consistently lie within the narrow region where
\epsilon
varies most rapidly with perturbation
\lambda
, a clear indicator of the edge of ergodicity breaking. At very long memory (
\alpha=0.9
), the system settles close to the ergodic phase, with
\epsilon
values slightly larger than
0
.

These results demonstrate that evolution, as mediated by
\alpha
, drives agent exploration to a phase boundary without any explicit tuning of the exploratory temperature.
It is worth noting that, the initial temperature distribution
\{T_{i}(0)\}
places the system in the ergodic phase at all
\alpha
values tested, indicating that the SOC behavior is not inherited from initialization but produced by the evolutionary replacement and mutation pressures.

III.3
Critical Avalanche Dynamics Under Evolutionary Pressure

A second hallmark of edge-seeking dynamics is the emergence of scale-free cascades from agents’ collective adaptation. In this section, we show that the same memory regimes that bring the system to the ergodicity edge (Sec.
III.2
) also exhibit avalanche dynamics of power-law form. Similar to the Bak–Sneppen model
1
, in our system, we define avalanches as the contiguous cascades during which some agents’ fitness values fall below a fitness threshold, parameterized by
\delta
. An avalanche size
S
is defined as the total number of agents participating in each interval of a cascade. For each
\alpha
, avalanche events are accumulated over independent
EvoSK
runs. The detailed method and implementations are given in Appendix
A.2
.

Fig.
3
displays the avalanche size distributions
P(S)\sim S^{\tau}
for
\alpha=0.75
. On log-log axes,
P(S)
follows an approximately linear decay over nearly four decades, with effective exponents
\tau\approx-1.58
, comparable to those reported in classical self-organized critical systems. Similar
P(S)
characteristics can be observed for
\alpha=0.5-0.8
(see Fig.
S2
), which coincide with the range shown to drive towards the edge of ergodicity breaking in Sec.
III.2
.

For very small or large
\alpha
values, it is less clear that avalanche distribution follow a strict scale-free distribution (see Fig
S3
). In a short-memory setting (
\alpha=0.3
), our dynamics are dominated by small avalanches associated with steeper decay of
P(S)
for small values of
S
. Although a few large events exist, they do not form a very stable scaling relationship spanning decades. In a long-memory setting (
\alpha=.9
), avalanche size distributions exhibit some curvature on log-log axes. Despite the multi-scale spans of
P(S)
, such curvature indicates a breakdown of scale invariance. Importantly, in both short- and long-memory cases, scanning across
\delta
does not reveal any
\delta
-window that produces
P(S)
with simultaneously flat and robust local slopes as observed in the intermediate
\alpha
regime.

These results show that critical avalanche dynamics emerge strongly in the same evolutionary regime that self-organizes the system to the ergodicity edge. This alignment indicates that evolution toward the edge is accompanied by the capacity to propagate evolutionary signals across multiple scales.

IV
Conclusions

In summary, we have presented a framework based on a minimal model where individual adaptive learning and population-level selection endogenously drive a complex system toward the edge of ergodicity breaking. By coupling Sherrington-Kirkpatrick spin-glass dynamics with replacement of the least fit, we demonstrate that the system self-organizes into a critical regime. This emergent state is characterized by three concurrent features of criticality: a scale-free distribution of evolutionary avalanches with an exponent
\tau\approx-1.5

1
, a precise localization at the phase boundary between ergodic and non-ergodic regimes, and the attainment of optimal collective rewards that surpass those found in non-evolutionary benchmarks.

These results provide a physical bridge between traditional self-organized criticality (SOC) and the functional requirements of adaptive systems. By incorporating heterogeneous interactions and objective-driven adaptation, our results establish a mechanistic link between the microscopic adaptivity and the macroscopic optimality through the emergent criticality, and this is robust against the extent of adaptivity of the agents. It offers a mechanistic lens for why diverse biological and socio-economic systems are often observed near some critical point, and the natureof criticality exhibited.

Our findings link the statistical mechanics of disordered systems with the broader theory of complex adaptive systems. This methodology–connecting microscopic behavioral rules to macroscopic phase transitions–offers a scalable approach for investigating criticality in contexts ranging from neural information processing to the training dynamics of large-scale learning models. This minimal model could also serve as a experimental proxy to understand the underlying scientific principles of SOC, since the nature of ‘criticality’ and optimality are well-defined here. Future work could also explore the universality of this phenomenon across different systems satisfying the minimal conditions of adaptive evolution in a heterogeneous environment.

Method

For every fixed
\alpha
,
EvoSK
randomness is determined by (i) the interaction matrix
J
, (ii) action sampling probability, and (iii) initial temperatures
T_{i}(0),\forall i\in[N]
.
Observables: temperatures
T_{i}(t)
, rewards
R_{i}(t)
, and fitness
q_{i}(t)
for each agent
i\in[N]
. Steady state is defined as the time when aggregate rewards plateau.

A.1
Phase Diagnostics via Temperature Rescaling

For each memory parameter
\alpha
, the evolutionary dynamics generate a heterogeneous agent-level temperature set

\mathcal{T}(t;\alpha)=\{T_{i}(t;\alpha)\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
Across all realizations, these distributions stabilize after a transient time of
t_{\mathrm{stat}}=15,000
iterations. We therefore define the emergent temperature ensemble at memory
\alpha
as

\mathcal{T}^{\alpha}:=\{\mathcal{T}(t_{\mathrm{stat}};\alpha,\omega)\}_{\omega},

where
\omega
indexes independent evolutionary realizations.

To probe the dynamical phase associated with a given emergent temperature distribution, we introduce a rescaling parameter

\lambda
and apply a uniform multiplicative perturbation

T_{i}\;\mapsto\;T_{i}\,2^{\lambda},

with
\lambda\in[-6,6]
.
This choice preserves the relative structure of the emergent distribution while continuously varying its effective exploration scale. For each rescaled temperature set in
\mathcal{T}^{\alpha}(\lambda)
, we run the baseline (non-evolutionary)
SK
learning dynamics
until stationarity.

Let
\bar{R}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}R_{i}(t)
denote the instantaneous collective reward. From each baseline
SK
run at fixed
\lambda
, we extract the empirical steady state distribution of
\bar{R}
. Denote by
\Omega_{\lambda}
the set of such steady state reward distributions obtained across multiple baseline run realizations. We define the ergodicity order parameter as

\epsilon(\lambda):=\mathrm{median}_{\mu,\nu\in\Omega_{\lambda}}\left\|\mu-\nu\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}},

(S1)

where the total variation distance between distributions of two realizations
\mu,\nu
is

\left\|\mu-\nu\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x}|\mu(x)-\nu(x)|\in[0,1].

The measure
\epsilon(\lambda)
characterizes the divergence of reward trajectories across run realizations (
\mu
’s and
\nu
’s within
\Omega_{\lambda}
). This metric is adapted from Dobrushin ergodicity coefficient
18
;
20
, where the original coefficient measures how quickly a Markov chain forgets its initial state.

Large values of
\epsilon(\lambda)
indicate sensitivity to initialization perturbations in agent configuration (non-ergodic behavior), while 0 value indicate convergence to a unique macroscopic steady state - reward distribution (ergodic). For each memory parameter
\alpha
and rescaling
\lambda
, the ergodicity order parameter
\epsilon(\lambda;\alpha,\omega)
is computed independently for each evolutionary realization
\omega
. We summarize the resulting ensemble using summary statistics across realizations. The dependence of these statistics on
\lambda
yields phase profiles that distinguish ordered, ergodic, and near-critical regimes.

For the baseline
SK
runs with fixed
\alpha,T
, the ergodicity coefficient (
S1
) can be adapted to
\epsilon(T)
by simply replacing the independent runs to summarize, from
\Omega_{\lambda}
to
\Omega_{T}
, containing reward trajectories. An example of this application is given in Fig.
S1
.

We define avalanches by tracking the minimal fitness of all agents at each time step
t
:

f_{\min}(t):=\min_{i}f_{i}(t)
.
Given a threshold parameter
\delta>0
, an avalanche starts at time
t_{0}

when

f_{\min}(t_{0})<f_{\min}(t_{0}-1)-\delta,

and ends at the first time
t_{1}>t_{0}
such that

f_{\min}(t_{1})\geq f_{\min}(t_{0}-1)-\delta.

During an avalanche, agents with fitness below the reference level

f_{\min}(t_{0}-1)-\delta
form a non-empty set

\mathcal{N}(t):=\{i:f_{i}(t)<f_{\min}(t_{0}-1)-\delta\}.

The avalanche size is defined as

S:=\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}-1}|\mathcal{N}(t)|.

(S2)

The parameter
\delta
plays a role analogous to the fitness threshold
f_{0}
in Bak–Sneppen-type avalanche definitions
16
. Because the critical fitness
f_{c}
is not directly observable in the present
evolutionary dynamics,
\delta
provides an operational way to define and probe cascade statistics. As in standard Bak–Sneppen models, scale-free behavior is not expected to hold uniformly for all threshold values, motivating a systematic scan over
\delta
values.

We scan
\delta
over a dense set of fitness drops

\{|\Delta f_{\min}(t)|:\Delta f_{\min}(t)<0,\,t>t_{\mathrm{stat}}\}
,
where
t_{\mathrm{stat}}
denotes the onset of stationarity (a.k.a. steady state). For each
\delta
, avalanche events are pooled across realizations and used to estimate the size distribution
P(S;\delta)
. Scaling behavior is assessed on log–log axes by computing local slopes
between consecutive points of
\log P(S)
versus
\log S
(typically calculated over

\sim 20
points after log-binning per distribution). This yields an ensemble of effective exponents
\tau(\delta)
, from which we compute the median exponent value and its interquartile range (IQR) as measures for statistical robustness.

The exponent reported in the main text is selected at values of
\delta
for which (i) the median local slope is maximally flat across
\log S
, (ii) the interquartile range of slopes is minimal, and
(iii) the resulting
P(S;\delta)
curves remain stable under small
perturbations of
\delta
. In practice, these criteria jointly identify a well-defined knee in local slope summaries as a function of
\delta
(see
\tau(\delta)
in Insets of Figure (3)), separating a robust scaling regime from threshold choices that distort the apparent
avalanche statistics.
Supplementary results in Fig.
S2
and Fig.
S3
show the estimation of exponents for different memory parameter values
\alpha

S2
, it can be seen that for a broad range of
\alpha

. In Fig.

\alpha

of avalanche sizes deviates from a strict scale-free distribution.
