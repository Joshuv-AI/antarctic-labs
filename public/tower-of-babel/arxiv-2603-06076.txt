Operators arising from invariant measures under some class of multidimensional transformations Thanks: Corresponding author: Janusz Morawiec. Email: janusz.morawiec@us.edu.pl
Oleksandr V. Maslyuchenko , Janusz Morawiec , and Thomas Zürcher

Abstract
. We investigate a linear operator associated with a functional equation that arises from studying some class of invariant measures under multidimensional transformations. By examining its iterates, we derive an explicit solution formula for the functional equation in some class of functions and establish a result on the existence of an absolutely continuous invariant measure under a multidimensional transformation that can be viewed as a generalization of classical p p -adic maps to higher dimensions.

Primary 47A50; Secondary 47A60, 39B12, 26A16

1.
Introduction

The theory of dynamical systems is a concept that provides a unified mathematical framework for understanding how systems evolve over time. It has broad applicability across nearly every scientific field, including physics, biology, economics, and engineering (see, e.g.,
[
RH12
,
Nef13
,
JR15
,
Awr18
,
Kon23
,
Awr24
,
Wil24
]
).

An invariant measure is a probability measure that remains unchanged under the dynamics of a system. Invariant measures are crucial tools for describing the statistical properties of orbits, such as time averages, without the need to track individual trajectories.
They are fundamental in ergodic theory, where they help classify systems according to their mixing behaviour and randomness. In many systems, especially chaotic ones, invariant measures provide a way to analyse the “typical” behaviour of almost all points with respect to the measure. This leads to significant results such as the ergodic theorem and the existence of physical measures. These properties make invariant measures central objects of interest in the theory of dynamical systems (see, e.g.,
[
LM94
,
Zhi96
,
Che02
,
Dem03
,
Yar08
,
Cla20
]
).

In the study of invariant measures, appropriate operators are often used, particularly when investigating absolutely continuous invariant measures (see, e.g.,
[
LY73
,
Buz00
,
GB03
,
BG05
,
Ino12
]
). These measures are especially important because they describe how probability densities evolve.

Continuous and singular invariant measures are also interesting from the purely mathematical point of view. However, unlike the absolutely continuous case, no general method exists for their systematic investigation. An effective approach to studying invariant measures (including continuous and singular ones) is to reformulate the problem in terms of a functional equation in a single variable and analyse its solutions within the class of all probability distribution functions. While this method is not new (see, e.g.,
[
Mat85
,
Nik91
]
), to the best of our knowledge, it has only been employed sporadically in the literature (see
[
MZ22
,
MS23
]
).

The dyadic transformation is one of the maps for which a wide class of continuous and singular invariant measures has been obtained by solving the
Matkowski–Wesołowski problem
(see
[
MZ18
,
MZ21
,
MZ25
]
). This problem was posed in 1985 by Janusz Matkowski (see
[
Mat85
]
) and independently, in an equivalent form during the 47th International Symposium on Functional Equations in 2009 by Jacek Wesołowski (see
[
MZ18
]
). It consists in finding non-linear solutions of the
Matkowski–Wesołowski functional equation

(1)

f(x)=f\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)-f(0)+f\left(\tfrac{x+1}{2}\right)-f\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)

in the class
\mathcal{D}
of all monotone and continuous functions
f\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
. Further results concerning extended versions of the Matkowski–Wesołowski functional equation in the one-dimensional setting can be found in
[
MZ19
]
.

Since iteration is a fundamental technique for solving functional equations in a single variable and iterates usually appear in the formulas for their solutions (see
[
KCG90
, Section 0.3]
), it is natural to associate with the Matkowski–Wesołowski functional equation the
Matkowski–Wesołowski operator

\mathbb{M}\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}
defined by

(2)

\mathbb{M}f(x)=f\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)-f(0)+f\left(\tfrac{x+1}{2}\right)-f\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right).

A counterpart of this operator has been used in
[
MZ24
]
to determine a general formula for probability distribution functions of all invariant measures under the
p
-addic transformations.

The aim of this paper is to study a multidimensional version of the Matkowski–Wesołowski functional equation associated with a certain class of multidimensional transformations. The central focus is the analysis of the corresponding multidimensional Matkowski–Wesołowski operator arising from these transformations. Moreover, motivated by the study of invariant measures, we conclude with a result on the existence and uniqueness of absolutely continuous invariant measures for the class of transformations under consideration.

Preliminaries

r,s\in\mathbb{N}
and set
K=\{1,\ldots,s\}
,

, and
L=K\times N
.

V=\mathbb{R}^{K}

x\in V^{N}

x=(x_{n})_{n\in N}=(x_{l})_{l\in L}
, where
x_{n}=(x_{n,k})_{k\in K}
and

if
l=(n,k)
, that is, we identify
V^{N}
with
\mathbb{R}^{L}
.

We fix a set
D\subseteq V^{N}
such that
0=(0,\ldots,0)\in D
. Furthermore, we fix a countable set
I

\gamma=(\gamma^{i})_{i\in I}

D
.

We denote by
\mu_{n}

\mathbb{R}^{n}
; when the dimension is clear from the context, we simply write
\mu
.
Recall that an indexed family
(A^{i})_{i\in I}

\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mu
-almost disjoint
if
\mu(A^{i}\cap A^{j})=0
whenever
i\neq j
. Given a
\mu
-almost disjoint indexed family
(A^{i})_{i\in I}
of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote its union by
\bigsqcup_{i\in I}^{\mu}A^{i}
.

Given
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
A\mathop{=}\limits^{\mu}B
(and say that
A=B

\mu
-almost everywhere
) if
\mu(A\triangle B)=0
, where

We denote by
[x,y]
the segment joining points
x,y\in V
. For all

, we define

.

The family of all Borel sets in a topological space
X
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}(X)
.

Furthermore, we denote by
\|\cdot\|

.

Let

.

the restriction of

to

. Thus, if
x=(x_{n})_{n\in N}\in V^{N}

x|_{M}=(x_{n})_{n\in M}\in V^{M}
,
x|_{N\setminus M}=(x_{n})_{n\in N\setminus M}\in V^{N\setminus M}
, and
x|_{M}\cup x|_{N\setminus M}=x
.

For a function
f
defined on
D
and all

f_{x}(y)=f(x\cup y)\quad\text{for every }y\in V^{N\setminus M}\text{ such that }x\cup y\in D.

So, the function
f_{x}

D_{x}=\{y\in V^{N\setminus M}:x\cup y\in D\}
.

If
m\in N
, then for each

.

3.
Multidimensional increment of functions

For all
x=(x_{n})_{n\in N}\in V^{N}
and
y=(y_{n})_{n\in N}\in V^{N}
, we define

\Pi(x,y)=\prod_{n\in N}\{x_{n},y_{n}\}.

Note that
\Pi
is an interval operator, which generates a convex structure on
V^{N}
in the sense of
[
vdV93
, Chapter I, §4]
.
So, we use the terminology from
[
vdV93
]
. In particular, we say that a set
A\subseteq V^{N}
is
\Pi
-convex
if
\Pi(x,y)\subseteq A
for all
x,y\in A
. More generally, if
Z
is a set,
\mathcal{P}(Z)
denotes its power set, and
J\colon Z\times Z\to\mathcal{P}(Z)
is an operator, then a set
A\subseteq Z
is called
J
-convex
if
J(x,y)\subseteq A
for all
x,y\in A
.

Furthermore, for all
x=(x_{n})_{n\in N}\in V^{N}
,
y=(y_{n})_{n\in N}\in V^{N}
, and
M\subseteq N
, we define

\pi_{M}(x,y)=x|_{M}\cup y|_{N\setminus M},

i.e.
\pi_{M}(x,y)=(\pi_{M,n}(x,y))_{n\in N}
, where

\pi_{M,n}(x,y)=x_{n}
if
n\in M
, and
\pi_{M,n}(x,y)=y_{n}
if
n\in N\setminus M
.
Therefore, we have

\Pi(x,y)=\{\pi_{M}(x,y):M\subseteq N\}.

Assume that
X\subseteq V^{N}
and let
x,y\in X
be such that
\Pi(x,y)\subseteq X
. The
multidimensional increment of a function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
at
(x,y)
is the number

(3)

\square f(x,y)=\sum_{M\subseteq N}(-1)^{|M|}f\big(\pi_{M}(x,y)\big).

Note that in the case where
X
is
\Pi
-convex, we have
\Pi(x,y)\subseteq X
for all
x,y\in X
, and hence the multidimensional increment of any function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is well-defined at every
(x,y)\in X\times X
.
Moreover, for
r=1
we have
\square f(x,y)=f(y)-f(x)
, and hence the multidimensional increment coincides with the one-dimensional increment. We now present an equivalent formula for the multidimensional increment, which allows for an inductive definition analogous to the one-dimensional case.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
m\in N
and let
f\colon D\to\mathbb{R}
. Then

\square f(x,y)=\square f_{y_{m}{:}m}\big(x|_{N\setminus\{m\}},y|_{N\setminus\{m\}}\big)-\square f_{x_{m}{:}m}\big(x|_{N\setminus\{m\}},y|_{N\setminus\{m\}}\big)

for all
x=(x_{n})_{n\in N}\in D
and
y=(y_{n})_{n\in N}\in D
such that
\Pi(x,y)\subseteq D
.

Proof.

Put
N^{\prime}=N\setminus\{m\}
. Using (
3
), we obtain

(4)

\begin{split}\square f(x,y)&=\sum_{M\subseteq N}(-1)^{|M|}f\big(\pi_{M}(x,y)\big)\\
&=\sum_{M\subseteq N^{\prime}}(-1)^{|M|}f\big(\pi_{M}(x,y)\big)+\sum_{M\subseteq N^{\prime}}(-1)^{|M\cup\{m\}|}f\big(\pi_{M\cup\{m\}}(x,y)\big)\\
&=\sum_{M\subseteq N^{\prime}}(-1)^{|M|}f\big(\pi_{M}(x,y)\big)-\sum_{M\subseteq N^{\prime}}(-1)^{|M|}f\big(\pi_{M\cup\{m\}}(x,y)\big).\end{split}

Fix
M\subseteq N^{\prime}
. Since
m\notin N^{\prime}
, we have

\pi_{M,m}(x,y)=y_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{M\cup\{m\},m}(x,y)=x_{m},

whereas, putting
x^{\prime}=x|_{N^{\prime}}
and
y^{\prime}=y|_{N^{\prime}}
, we have for every
n\in N^{\prime}

\pi_{M,n}(x,y)=\pi_{M,n}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{M\cup\{m\},n}(x,y)=\pi_{M\cup\{m\},n}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

Therefore,

\pi_{M}(x,y)=(y_{m},\pi_{M}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{N^{\prime}})_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{M\cup\{m\}}(x,y)=(x_{m},\pi_{M}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{N^{\prime}})_{m}.

In consequence, returning to (
4
) and again using (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\square f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\subseteq N^{\prime}}\!(-1)^{|M|}f(y_{m},\pi_{M}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{N^{\prime}})_{m}-\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{M\subseteq N\setminus\{m\}}\!\!\!\!\!\!(-1)^{|M|}f(x_{m},\pi_{M}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{N^{\prime}})_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\subseteq N^{\prime}}(-1)^{|M|}f_{y_{m}:m}\big(\pi_{M}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)-\sum_{M\subseteq N^{\prime}}(-1)^{|M|}f_{x_{m}:m}\big(\pi_{M}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=\square f_{y_{m}:m}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-\square f_{x_{m}:m}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),

which completes the proof.
∎

4.
Gradient with respect to groups of variables

Throughout this section, we assume that the set
D
is open in
V^{N}
. We also fix
f\colon D\to\mathbb{R}
and
M=\{m_{1},\dots,m_{p}\}\subseteq N
, assuming that
p
is a positive integer and
m_{1}<\dots<m_{p}
.

For any
l=(n,k)\in L
we denote the
l
-th partial derivative of
f
at
x\in D
by
\partial_{l}f(x)
. We say that
f
is

separately differentiable
if for all
x\in D
and
l\in L
the
l
-th partial derivative
\partial_{l}f(x)
exists. We denote the
gradient
of
f
at
x\in D
by

\nabla f(x)=\big(\partial_{l}f(x)\big)_{l\in L},

and so
\nabla f(x)u=\sum_{l\in L}\partial_{l}f(x)u_{l}
for every
u=(u_{l})_{l\in L}\in V^{N}
.
Next, for any
n\in N
, we define the
n
-th partial gradient
of
f
at
x\in D
by

\nabla\!_{n}f(x)=\big(\partial_{(n,k)}f(x)\big)_{k\in K},

and set
\nabla\!_{n}f(x)v=\sum_{k\in K}\partial_{(n,k)}f(x)v_{k}
for every
v=(v_{k})_{k\in K}\in V
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{k})_{k\in K^{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{M}}
be an indexed family. For any
u\in V^{M}
, we define

(5)

\lambda u^{M}=\sum_{k\in K^{M}}\lambda_{k}\odot^{M}_{k}(u),\quad\text{where }\odot_{k}^{M}(u)=\prod_{m\in M}u_{m,k_{m}}.

Obviously,
\lambda u^{M}
is an
|M|
-linear functional on
V^{M}
.

Now, for every
u\in V^{M}
, we put
\|u\|^{M}=\prod_{m\in M}\|u_{m}\|
. We show that the following analogue of the Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality holds:

(6)

\left|\lambda u^{M}\right|\leq\|\lambda\|\cdot\|u\|^{M}.

Observe first that

\sum_{k\in K^{M}}\prod_{m\in M}u_{m,k_{m}}^{2}=\prod_{m\in M}\sum_{k\in K}u_{m,k}^{2}.

This together with the classical Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality gives

\displaystyle\left|\lambda u^{M}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\!\leq\sum_{k\in K^{M}}\lambda_{k}^{2}\sum_{k\in K^{M}}\!\big(\!\odot^{M}_{k}(u)\big)^{2}=\|\lambda\|^{2}\sum_{k\in K^{M}}\prod_{m\in M}u_{m,k_{m}}^{2}=\|\lambda\|^{2}\prod_{m\in M}\sum_{k\in K}u_{m,k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\lambda\|^{2}\prod_{m\in M}\|u_{m}\|^{2}=\|\lambda\|^{2}\left(\|u\|^{M}\right)^{2}.

Let us pass to the next definition.
For any
k=(k_{m})_{m\in M}\in K^{M}
, we put
\widetilde{\partial}_{k}f=\partial_{(m_{1},k_{m_{1}})}\cdots\partial_{(m_{p},k_{m_{p}})}f
. Then we define the
M
-gradient
of
f
at
x\in D
by

\nabla\!_{M}f(x)=\big(\widetilde{\partial}_{k}f(x)\big)_{k\in K^{M}}.

Observe that using the notation established in (
5
), we have

\nabla\!_{M}f(x)u^{M}=\sum_{k\in K^{M}}\widetilde{\partial}_{k}f(x)\odot^{M}_{k}(u)

for every
u\in V^{M}
.
In the case
s=1
, identifying
(m_{k},1)
with
m_{k}
, we have

\nabla\!_{M}f(x)=\partial_{M}f(p)=\partial_{m_{1}}\cdots\partial_{m_{p}}f(x)
and
\nabla\!_{M}f(x)u^{M}=\partial_{M}f(x)u_{m_{1}}\dots u_{m_{p}}
for every
x\in D
.

5.
Multidimensional analogue of Lipschitz functions

Let
D\subseteq V^{N}
be a closed subset.
We say
f\colon D\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-function
if there exists an extension
\widetilde{f}\colon V^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
of
f
such that for every
M\subseteq N
the gradient
\nabla\!_{M}\widetilde{f}
exists at each point in
V^{N}
and is continuous on
V^{N}
, i.e. each partial derivative
\widetilde{\partial}_{k}\widetilde{f}
is continuous for every
k\in K^{M}
.

Note that
f\in C^{r}(V^{N},\mathbb{R})
implies that
f
is a
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-function; however, the converse implication does not hold.
Observe also that for every
M\subseteq N
the gradient
\nabla\!_{M}\widetilde{f}
may depend on the extension
\widetilde{f}
.

It is possible to show that in the definition of
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-functions we only need to extend
f
to an open superset of
D
. For
r=1
, this follows from Whitney’s Extension Theorem (see
[
EG92
, Theorem 6.5.1]
).

We say that
f
is
N
-dimensional Lipschitz
if there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that

\left|\square f(x,y)\right|\leq C\|x-y\|^{N}\quad\text{for all }x,y\in X\text{ such that }\Pi(x,y)\subseteq D.

Note that in the case where
r=1
, the class of
N
-dimensional Lipschitz functions coincides with the usual class of Lipschitz functions. Observe also that an
N
-dimensional Lipschitz function need not even be continuous.

We now prove the following version of the mean value theorem.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
f\colon V^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-function, then for all
a,b\in V^{N}
there exists
c\in P(a,b)
such that
\square f(a,b)=\nabla\!_{N}f(c)(b-a)^{N}
.

Proof.

We begin with the case
r=1
. We define the function
g\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
by
g(t)=f\left(t(b-a)+a\right)
.
Then, by the mean value theorem (also known as the Lagrange finite-increment formula) there exists
\tau\in[0,1]
such that
g(1)-g(0)=g^{\prime}(\tau)
.
Putting
c=\tau(b-a)+a
, we see that
c\in[a,b]
, and then

\square f(a,b)=f(b)-f(a)=g(1)-g(0)=g^{\prime}(\tau)=\nabla f(c)(b-a)=\nabla_{N}f(c)(b-a)^{N}.

Suppose now that the claim of the proposition holds for every
p\in\{1,\ldots,r-1\}
and the set
N_{p}=\{1,\ldots,p\}\subseteq N
.
Fix a
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-function
f\colon V^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
,

a=(a_{n})_{n\in N}\in V^{N}
and
b=(b_{n})_{n\in N}\in V^{N}
. Put
N^{\prime}=N_{r-1}
,

a^{\prime}=(a_{n})_{n\in N^{\prime}}\in V^{N^{\prime}}
, and
b^{\prime}=(b_{n})_{n\in N^{\prime}}\in V^{N^{\prime}}
.
Moreover, we easily conclude that the formula
g=f_{b_{r}{:}r}-f_{a_{r}{:}r}
defines a
\mathrm{C}^{N^{\prime}}
-function. Then, by
Proposition

3.1
and the inductive hypothesis, we obtain the existence of
c^{\prime}\in P(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
such that

\displaystyle\square f(a,b)

\displaystyle=\square g(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=\nabla\!_{N^{\prime}}g(c^{\prime})(b^{\prime}-a^{\prime})^{N^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\nabla\!_{N^{\prime}}f_{b_{r}{:}r}(c^{\prime})(b^{\prime}-a^{\prime})^{N^{\prime}}-\nabla\!_{N^{\prime}}f_{a_{r}{:}r}(c^{\prime})(b^{\prime}-a^{\prime})^{N^{\prime}}.

Define
h\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
by

h(x)=\nabla\!_{N^{\prime}}f_{x{:}r}(c^{\prime})(b^{\prime}-a^{\prime})^{N^{\prime}}
and note that
h
is differentiable since
f
is a
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-function.
Again, by the inductive hypothesis, we obtain the existence of
c_{r}\in[a_{r},b_{r}]\subseteq V
such that
\square f(a,b)=\square h(a_{r},b_{r})=\nabla h(c_{r})(b_{r}-a_{r})
. Finally, putting
c=(c_{r},c^{\prime})_{r}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\square f(a,b)

\displaystyle=\nabla h(c_{r})(b_{r}-a_{r})=\sum_{k\in K}\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{k}}(c_{r})(b_{r,k}-a_{r,k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in K}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\big(\nabla\!_{N^{\prime}}f_{x{:}r}(c^{\prime})(b^{\prime}-a^{\prime})^{N^{\prime}}\big)\big|_{x=c_{r}}(b_{r,k}-a_{r,k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in K}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}\in K^{N^{\prime}}}\widetilde{\partial}_{k^{\prime}}f(x,c^{\prime})_{r}\odot^{N^{\prime}}_{k^{\prime}}(b^{\prime}-a^{\prime})\right)\right|_{x=c_{r}}(b_{r,k}-a_{r,k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in K}\sum_{k^{\prime}\in K^{N^{\prime}}}\partial_{(r,k)}\widetilde{\partial}_{k^{\prime}}f(c)(b_{r,k}-a_{r,k})\odot^{N^{\prime}}_{k^{\prime}}(b^{\prime}-a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k,k^{\prime})_{r}\in K^{N}}\widetilde{\partial}_{(k,k^{\prime})_{r}}f(c)\odot^{N}_{(k,k^{\prime})_{r}}(b-a)=\sum_{\ell\in K^{N}}\widetilde{\partial}_{\ell}f(c)\odot^{N}_{\ell}(b-a)

\displaystyle=\nabla\!_{N}f(c)(b-a)^{N},

and the proof is complete.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume that
f\colon V^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-function such that
\nabla\!_{N}f
is bounded on a
P
-convex set
X\subseteq V^{N}
. Then
f
is
N
-dimensional Lipschitz on
X
.

Proof.

Since
\nabla\!_{N}f
is bounded on
X
, the number
C=\sup_{x\in X}\big\|\nabla\!_{N}f(x)\big\|
is finite. Fix
a,b\in X
.

Proposition

5.1
together with the
P
-convexity of
X
implies that there exist
z\in P(a,b)\subseteq X
such that

\square f(a,b)=\nabla\!_{N}f(z)(b-a)^{N}
. Finally, by (
6
), we get

\big|\square f(a,b)\big|=\big|\nabla\!_{N}f(z)(b-a)^{N}\big|\leq\big\|\nabla\!_{N}f(z)\big\|\cdot\|b-a\|^{N}\leq C\|b-a\|^{N},

and the proof is complete.
∎

6.
The gradient increment and the average gradient of multidimensional Lipschitz functions

Assume that
f\colon V^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-function. For any compact set
T\subseteq V^{N}
, we define the
N
-gradient increment
of
f
on
T
by

\widetilde{\nabla}\!_{N}f(T)=\int_{T}\nabla\!_{N}f(x)\,d\mu(x)=\left(\int_{T}\widetilde{\partial}_{k}f(x)\,d\mu(x)\right)_{k\in K^{N}},

and for any
u\in V^{N}
, we put

\widetilde{\nabla}\!_{N}f(T)u=\int_{T}\nabla\!_{N}f(x)u\,d\mu(x)=\sum_{k\in K^{N}}\odot^{N}_{k}(u)\int_{T}\widetilde{\partial}_{k}f(x)\,d\mu(x).

Then, assuming that
T
is of positive Lebesgue measure, we define the
average
N
-gradient of
f
on
T
by

\overline{\nabla}\!_{N}f(T)=\tfrac{1}{\mu(T)}\widetilde{\nabla}\!_{N}f(T)

and
\overline{\nabla}\!_{N}f(T)u=\tfrac{1}{\mu(T)}\widetilde{\nabla}\!_{N}f(T)u
for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
.

We conclude this section with a result that shows an equality reminiscent of the fundamental theorem of calculus. Since the result will not be used in this paper, we omit the proof.

Fact 6.1
.

Assume that
f\colon V^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-function. Let
a=(a_{n})_{n\in N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
b=(b_{n})_{n\in N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. If
a_{n}\leq b_{n}
for every
n\in N
, then

\widetilde{\nabla}_{N}f(P(a,b))=\int_{P(a,b)}\partial_{N}f\,d\mu=\square f(a,b).

7.
Multidimensional MW-operators

For any
x\in D
, we put

\Gamma(x)=\big\{\gamma^{i}(x):i\in I\big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{\pi}(x)=\bigcup_{i\in I}\Pi(\gamma^{i}(0),\gamma^{i}(x)).

Definition 7.1

(admissible)
.

A set
X\subseteq D
is said to be
admissible
if
0\in X
and
\Pi(\gamma^{i}(0),\gamma^{i}(x))\subseteq X
whenever
x\in X
and
i\in I
.

Put
\Gamma_{\pi}^{0}(0)=\{0\}
and
\Gamma_{\pi}^{k+1}(0)=\Gamma_{\pi}(\Gamma_{\pi}^{k}(0))=\bigcup_{x\in\Gamma_{\pi}^{k}(0)}\Gamma_{\pi}(x)
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. It is easy to see that the set
S
defined as follows

\textbf{S}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\Gamma_{\pi}^{k}(0)

is the minimal (in the sense of inclusion) admissible set. If the family
\gamma
consists of continuous functions, then it is easy to see that the minimal closed admissible set, is the closure of
S
; let us denote this set by
T
, i.e.

\textbf{T}=\overline{\textbf{S}}.

Given an admissible set
X\subseteq D
, we put

\mathcal{F}(X)=\big\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}:\text{$\sum_{i\in I}\square f\big(\gamma^{i}(0),\gamma^{i}(x)\big)$ converges for every $x\in X$}\big\}.

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{F}(X)
is a vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{X}
.

Definition 7.2

(multidimensional MW-operator)
.

The linear operator
\mathbb{M}\colon\mathcal{F}(X)\to\mathbb{R}^{X}
given by

\mathbb{M}f(x)=\sum_{i\in I}\square f\big(\gamma^{i}(0),\gamma^{i}(x)\big)\quad\text{for every }x\in X

is said to be the
multidimensional MW-operator
(briefly, the
MW-operator
).

The formula for the MW-operator in
Definition

7.2
is given in terms of the multidimensional increment
\square
. Next, we provide an equivalent formula for this operator; however, to do so, we need to introduce one additional notation.

For any
i\in I
,
x\in X
, and
M\subseteq N
, we define

(7)

\gamma^{i}_{M}(x)=\pi_{M}\big(\gamma^{i}(0),\gamma^{i}(x)\big),

i.e.
\gamma^{i}_{M}(x)=\big(\gamma^{i}_{M,n}(x)\big)_{n\in N}
, where

\gamma^{i}_{M,n}(x)=\pi_{M,n}\big(\gamma^{i}(0),\gamma^{i}(x)\big)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\gamma^{i}_{n}(0),&\text{if }n\in M,\\
\gamma^{i}_{n}(x),&\text{if }n\in N\setminus M.\end{array}\right.

Thus, using (
3
), we obtain the following equivalent formula for the MW-operator

(8)

\mathbb{M}f(x)=\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{M\subseteq N}(-1)^{|M|}f\big(\gamma^{i}_{M}(x)\big).

Definition 7.3

(multidimensional MW-functional equation)
.

The functional equation

f=\mathbb{M}f

is called the
multidimensional MW-functional equation
(briefly, the
MW-equation
). The unknown function
f
is assumed to belong to
\mathcal{F}(X)
.

To understand the concepts introduced and the notation adopted, we provide four examples.

Example 7.4
.

Assume that
r=s=1
, i.e.
D=V^{N}=V=\mathbb{R}
. Let
I=\{0,1\}
and

\gamma^{i}(x)=\tfrac{x+i}{2}\quad\text{for all }i\in I\text{ and }x\in\mathbb{R}.

It is clear that the minimal admissible sets
S
and
T
are given by

\textbf{S}=\big\{\tfrac{k}{2^{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N},k=0,1,\dots,2^{n}-1\big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\textbf{T}=[0,1].

By choosing the admissible set
X=\textbf{T}
, we obtain the MW-equation (
1
) and the corresponding MW-operator
\mathbb{M}
given by (
2
).

The next two examples show that the two-dimensional MW-operator may take different forms on the same admissible set, depending on the sets
N
and
K
.

Example 7.5
.

Assume that
r=1
and
s=2
, i.e.
D=V^{N}=V=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
I=\{0,1\}^{2}
and

\gamma^{(i,j)}(x,y)=\big(\tfrac{x+i}{2},\tfrac{y+j}{2}\big)\quad\text{for all }(i,j)\in I\text{ and }(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

It this case, the minimal admissible sets
S
and
T
are given by

\textbf{S}=\big\{\tfrac{k}{2^{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N},k=0,1,\dots,2^{n}-1\big\}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\textbf{T}=[0,1]^{2}.

Choosing an admissible set
X
and using (
8
), we conclude that the two-dimensional MW-operator in the considered case takes the form

\mathbb{M}f(x,y)=\sum_{(i,j)\in I}\left[f\big(\tfrac{x+i}{2},\tfrac{y+j}{2}\big)-f\big(\tfrac{i}{2},\tfrac{j}{2}\big)\right]

for every
(x,y)\in X
.

Example 7.6
.

Assume that
r=2
and
s=1
, i.e.
V=\mathbb{R}
and
D=V^{N}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\gamma
be the same as in
Example

7.5
. Then the minimal admissible sets
S
and
T
are also the same as in
Example

7.5
. Choosing an admissible set
X
and again making use of (
8
), we deduce that in this setting the two-dimensional MW-operator takes the form

\mathbb{M}f(x,y)=\sum_{(i,j)\in I}\left[f\big(\tfrac{x+i}{2},\tfrac{y+j}{2}\big)-f\big(\tfrac{i}{2},\tfrac{y+j}{2}\big)-f\big(\tfrac{x+i}{2},\tfrac{j}{2}\big)+f\big(\tfrac{i}{2},\tfrac{j}{2}\big)\right]

for every
(x,y)\in X
.

The fourth example generalizes all the preceding ones.

Example 7.7
.

Let
I=\{0,1\}^{L}
and

\gamma^{i}(x)=\big(\tfrac{x_{\ell}+i_{\ell}}{2}\big)_{\ell\in L}\quad\text{for all }i\in I\text{ and }x\in\mathbb{R}^{L}.

Now that the minimal admissible sets
S
and
T
are given by

\textbf{S}=\big\{\tfrac{k}{2^{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N},k=0,1,\dots,2^{n}-1\big\}^{L}\quad\text{and}\quad\textbf{T}=[0,1]^{L}.

Applying (
8
) to an admissible set
X
, we arrive at the multidimensional MW-operator in the form

\mathbb{M}f(x)=\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{M\subseteq N}(-1)^{|M|}f\Big(\big(\tfrac{i_{\ell}}{2}\big)_{\ell\in M\times K}\cup\big(\tfrac{x_{\ell}+i_{\ell}}{2}\big)_{\ell\in(N\setminus M)\times K}\Big)

for every
x\in X
.

Our main result, formulated in
Section

9
(see
Theorem

9.1
), implies that a function
f\in C^{r}([0,1]^{L},\mathbb{R})
is a solution of the MW-equation corresponding to the MW-operator from
Example

7.7
if and only if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{N}}
such that

f(x)=\sum_{k\in K^{N}}\lambda_{k}\prod_{n\in N}x_{n,k_{n}}\quad\text{for every }x\in[0,1]^{L}.

In particular, for the cases listed in
Examples

7.4
,
7.5
and
7.6
, we obtain:

(i)

f\in C^{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
is a solution of the MW-equation (
1
) (cf.
Example

7.4
) if and only if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f(x)=\lambda x\quad\text{for every }x\in[0,1],

(ii)

f\in C^{1}([0,1]^{2},\mathbb{R})
is a solution of the MW-equation corresponding to the MW-operator from
Example

7.5
if and only if there exist
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f(x_{1},x_{2})=\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2}\quad\text{for every }(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2},

(iii)

f\in C^{2}([0,1]^{2},\mathbb{R})
is a solution of the MW-equation corresponding to the MW-operator from
Example

7.6
if and only if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f(x_{1},x_{2})=\lambda x_{1}x_{2}\quad\text{for every }(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}.

It is known that in (i) the class
C^{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
can be replaced by the class of absolutely continuous functions (see, e.g.,
[
R5́7
,
LP77
]
).

8.
Iterations of the MW-operators

Throughout this section, we fix an admissible set
X\subseteq D\subseteq V^{N}
.

For all
p\in\mathbb{N}
and multi-indices
i=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{p})\in I^{p}
we define
\gamma^{i}\colon D\to D
by

\gamma^{i}=\gamma^{i_{1}}\circ\gamma^{i_{2}}\circ\dots\circ\gamma^{i_{p}}.

As usual, for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
we denote the
p
-iterate of the MW-operator
\mathbb{M}
by
\mathbb{M}^{p}
. We define the domains of the iterates by recursion, setting
\mathcal{F}^{1}(X)=\mathcal{F}(X)
and

\mathcal{F}^{p}(X)=\big\{f\in\mathcal{F}(X):\,\mathbb{M}^{k}f\in\mathcal{F}(X)\text{ for every }k\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\}\big\}

for every
p\geq 2
. We also put

\mathcal{F}^{\infty}(X)=\bigcap_{p=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}^{p}(X).

Thus,
\mathcal{F}^{p}(X)
is the domain of
\mathbb{M}^{p}
, and
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}(X)
is the common domain of all iterates. Obviously,
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}(X)
is a vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{X}
, and
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}(X)=\mathcal{F}(X)=\mathbb{R}^{X}
in the case where
I
is a finite set.

Proposition 8.1
.

Assume that for any
i\in I
, the function
\gamma^{i}
is of the form

\gamma^{i}(x)=\big(\gamma^{i}_{n}(x_{n})\big)_{n\in N}\quad\text{for every }x=(x_{n})_{n\in N}\in D,

where
\gamma^{i}_{n}\colon D_{n}\to D_{n}
and
D_{n}=\{x_{n}\in V:x\in D\}
for every
n\in N
.
Then for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\mathcal{F}^{p}(X)
we have

\mathbb{M}^{p}f(x)=\sum_{i\in I^{p}}\square f\big(\gamma^{i}(0),\gamma^{i}(x)\big),

or equivalently, extending the definition of
\gamma^{i}_{M}
given in (
7
) to all
i\in I^{p}
,
x\in X
, and
M\subseteq N
,

(9)

\mathbb{M}^{p}f(x)=\sum_{i\in I^{p}}\sum_{M\subseteq N}(-1)^{|M|}f\big(\gamma^{i}_{M}(x)\big).

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
p
to prove (
9
).
For
p=1
, (
9
) reduces to (
8
). Fix
p\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose that (
9
) holds for every
f\in\mathcal{F}^{p}(X)
.

Let us fix
f\in\mathcal{F}^{p+1}(X)
and
x\in X
. Then
\mathbb{M}f\in\mathcal{F}^{p}(X)
. Putting

\Sigma_{M}^{i,j}(x)=\sum_{L\subseteq N}(-1)^{|L|}f\big(\gamma^{i}_{M}(\gamma^{j}_{L}(x))\big)

for all
i\in I
,
j\in I^{p}
and
M\subseteq N
, we have

(10)

\begin{split}\mathbb{M}^{p+1}f(x)&=\mathbb{M}^{p}\big(\mathbb{M}f\big)(x)=\sum_{j\in I^{p}}\sum_{L\subseteq N}(-1)^{|L|}\mathbb{M}f\big(\gamma^{j}_{L}(x)\big)\\
&=\sum_{j\in I^{p}}\sum_{L\subseteq N}(-1)^{|L|}\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{M\subseteq N}(-1)^{|M|}f\big(\gamma^{i}_{M}(\gamma^{j}_{L}(x))\big)\\
&=\sum_{j\in I^{p}}\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{M\subseteq N}(-1)^{|M|}\Sigma_{M}^{i,j}(x).\end{split}

Let
M\subseteq N
and
L=L^{\prime}\cup L^{\prime\prime}
with
L^{\prime}\subseteq M
and
L^{\prime\prime}\subseteq N\setminus M
. Then

(11)

\begin{split}\gamma^{i}_{M,n}(\gamma^{j}_{L}(x))&=\begin{cases}\gamma^{i}_{n}(0),&\text{if }n\in M,\\
\gamma^{i}_{n}\big(\gamma^{j}_{L,n}(x_{n})\big),&\text{if }n\in N\setminus M\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}\gamma^{i}_{n}(0),&\text{if }n\in M,\\
\gamma^{i}_{n}\big(\gamma^{j}_{n}(0)\big),&\text{if }n\in L^{\prime\prime},\\
\gamma^{i}_{n}\big(\gamma^{j}_{n}(x_{n})\big),&\text{if }n\in(N\setminus M)\setminus L^{\prime\prime}.\end{cases}\end{split}

Therefore,
\gamma^{i}_{M}\big(\gamma^{j}_{L}(x)\big)
does not depend on
L^{\prime}
and

\gamma^{i}_{M}\big(\gamma^{j}_{L}(x)\big)=\gamma^{i}_{M}\big(\gamma^{j}_{L^{\prime\prime}}(x)\big)
.
In consequence,

(12)

\begin{split}\Sigma_{M}^{i,j}(x)&=\sum_{L^{\prime}\subseteq M}(-1)^{|L^{\prime}|}\sum_{L^{\prime\prime}\subseteq N\setminus M}(-1)^{|L^{\prime\prime}|}f\big(\gamma^{i}_{M}(\gamma^{j}_{L^{\prime}\cup L^{\prime\prime}}(x))\big)\\
&=\sum_{L^{\prime}\subseteq M}(-1)^{|L^{\prime}|}\sum_{L^{\prime\prime}\subseteq N\setminus M}(-1)^{|L^{\prime\prime}|}f\big(\gamma^{i}_{M}(\gamma^{j}_{L^{\prime\prime}}(x))\big).\end{split}

Observe now that

(13)

\sum_{L^{\prime}\subseteq M}(-1)^{|L^{\prime}|}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }M\neq\emptyset,\\
1,&\text{if }M=\emptyset.\end{cases}

For any
i\in I
and
j=(j_{k})_{k=1}^{p}\in I^{p}
we put
i\smallsmile j=(i,j_{1},\dots,j_{p})\in I^{p+1}
.
Applying (
11
) we obtain

\gamma^{i}_{\emptyset,n}(\gamma^{j}_{L}(x))=\begin{cases}\gamma^{i}_{n}\big(\gamma^{j}_{n}(0)\big),&\text{if }n\in L,\\
\gamma^{i}_{n}\big(\gamma^{j}_{n}(x_{n})\big),&\text{if }n\in N\setminus L\end{cases}=\gamma^{i\smallsmile j}_{L,n}(x_{n})

for every
n\in N
, and hence
\gamma^{i}_{\emptyset}(\gamma^{j}_{L}(x))=\gamma^{i\smallsmile j}_{L}(x)
.
This together with (
13
) lets us continue the computation started in (
12
) to get

\Sigma^{i,j}_{M}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }M\neq\emptyset,\\
\sum_{L\subseteq N}(-1)^{|L|}f\big(\gamma^{i\smallsmile j}_{L}(x)\big)&\text{if }M=\emptyset.\end{cases}

Finally, returning to (
10
) and substituting
i\smallsmile j
by
\ell
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{p+1}f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I,j\in I^{p}}\Sigma_{\emptyset}^{i,j}(x)=\sum_{i\in I,j\in I^{p}}\sum_{L\subseteq N}(-1)^{|L|}f\big(\gamma^{i\smallsmile j}_{L}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in I^{p+1}}\sum_{L\subseteq N}(-1)^{|L|}f\big(\gamma^{\ell}_{L}(x)\big),

and the proof is complete.
∎

9.
The case of affine functions

From now on, we assume that
D=V^{N}
and the following hypotheses:

(H
1
)

For every
i\in I
there exist
\alpha^{i}=(\alpha^{i}_{n})_{n\in N}\in(0,1)^{N}
and
a^{i}=(a^{i}_{n})_{n\in N}\in V^{N}
such that
q=\sup_{i\in I,n\in N}\alpha^{i}_{n}<1
and
\gamma^{i}
is of the form

\gamma^{i}(x)=\big(\gamma^{i}_{n}(x_{n})\big)_{n\in N}=\big(\alpha^{i}_{n}x_{n}+a^{i}_{n}\big)_{n\in N}\quad\text{for every }x=(x_{n})_{n\in N}\in V^{N}.

(H
2
)

There exists a compact admissible set
T\subseteq V^{N}
with
\mu(T)>0
such that

T\mathop{=}\limits^{\mu}{\bigsqcup_{i\in I}}^{\mu}\gamma^{i}(T)\quad\text{and}\quad T=\overline{\bigcup_{i\in I}\gamma^{i}(T)}.

For any
i\in I
, we put

\beta^{i}=\prod\limits_{n\in N}\alpha^{i}_{n}

and observe that
\mu(\gamma^{i}(T))=\beta^{i}\mu(T)
. Then, by
(H2)
, we obtain

\mu(T)=\mu\left({\bigsqcup\limits_{i\in I}}^{\mu}\gamma^{i}(T)\right)=\sum_{i\in I}\mu(\gamma^{i}(T))=\sum_{i\in I}\beta^{i}\mu(T)=\mu(T)\sum_{i\in I}\beta^{i}.

Thus,

(14)

\sum_{i\in I}\beta^{i}=1.

Given a function
g\colon D\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\delta>0
, we put

\omega_{T}(g,\delta)=\sup\left\{\|g(x)-g(y)\|:\,x\in T,y\in D\text{ such that }\|x-y\|<\delta\right\}.

This notion is closely related to the modulus of continuity. It is not difficult to show that if
g
is continuous at each point of the compact set
T
then
\omega_{T}(g,\delta)\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
.

Given a
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-function
\widetilde{f}\colon V^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the operator
\mathbb{L}
by setting

\mathbb{L}\widetilde{f}(x)=\overline{\nabla}\!_{N}\widetilde{f}(T)x^{N}\quad\text{for every }x\in V^{N}.

We are now ready to state the main result of this paper.

Theorem 9.1
.

Assume
(H1)
and
(H2)
.
Let
X\subseteq V^{N}
be an admissible set,
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-function, and
\widetilde{f}\colon V^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be one of its
\mathrm{C}^{N}
-extensions. Then:

(i)

T
is the minimal closed admissible set, i.e.
T=\mathbf{T}
;

(ii)

\mathcal{F}^{\infty}(X)
contains every function that is
N
-dimensional Lipschitz on every compact subset of
X
, in particular
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\infty}(X)
;

(iii)

\lim_{p\to\infty}\mathbb{M}^{p}f(x)=\mathbb{L}\widetilde{f}(x)
for every
x\in X
, and moreover, if
X
is compact, then the convergence is uniform;

(iv)

\mathbb{M}f=f
if and only if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{N}}
such that

f(x)=\lambda x^{N}=\sum_{k\in K^{N}}\lambda_{k}\odot^{N}_{k}(x)\quad\text{for every }x\in X,

i.e.
f
is an
r
-linear functional.

Proof.

We start with some notations and obvious observations.

Fix
p\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\delta=\text{diam}\,T
. In contrast to the setting described at the beginning of this section, we consider multi-indices
i=(i_{k})_{k=1}^{p}\in I^{p}
. Then, for any
n\in N
,
{\gamma^{i}_{n}=\gamma^{i_{1}}_{n}\circ\gamma^{i_{2}}_{n}\circ\dots\circ\gamma^{i_{p}}_{n}}
is an affine function such that
\gamma^{i}_{n}(x)=\alpha^{i}_{n}x+a^{i}_{n}
for every
x\in V
, where
a^{i}_{n}=\gamma^{i}_{n}(0)
and
\alpha^{i}_{n}=\prod_{k=1}^{p}\alpha^{i_{k}}_{n}
.
For the empty multi-index
\varnothing\in I^{0}
, we put
\alpha^{\varnothing}_{n}=1
,
a^{\varnothing}_{n}=0
, and
\gamma^{\varnothing}_{n}(x)=x
for every
x\in V
.

For any
i=(i_{k})_{k=1}^{p}\in I^{p}
we set
\gamma^{i}=(\gamma^{i}_{n})_{n\in N}
,
T^{i}=\gamma^{i}(T)
,
a^{i}=(a^{i}_{n})_{n\in N}
, and
\beta^{i}=\prod_{n\in N}\alpha^{i}_{n}
. Therefore,
\mu(T^{i})=\mu(T)\beta^{i}
and
\beta^{i}=\prod_{k=1}^{p}\beta^{i_{k}}
. So, using (
14
), we have

(15)

\sum_{i\in I^{p}}\beta^{i}=\prod_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{i_{k}\in I}\beta^{i_{k}}=1.

Proceeding by induction and using
(H2)
, we conclude that

(16)

T\mathop{=}\limits^{\mu}{\bigsqcup_{i\in I^{p}}}^{\mu}T^{i}\quad\text{and}\quad T=\overline{\bigcup_{i\in I^{p}}T^{i}}.

Now observe that
(H1)
implies
0\leq\sup_{i\in I^{p},\,n\in N}\alpha^{i}_{n}\leq q^{p}
.
In particular,
\gamma^{i}
is a
q^{p}
-Lipschitz function. Then
\mathrm{diam}\,T^{i}\leq q^{p}\mathrm{diam}\,T=q^{p}\delta
, and hence

(17)

\sup_{i\in I^{p}}\mathrm{diam}\,T^{i}\leq q^{p}\delta.

We begin with proving
Item

(
i
)

.

Note that
a^{i}=\gamma^{i}(0)\in\gamma^{i}(T)=T^{i}
and
a^{i}\in\Gamma^{p}_{\pi}(0)\subseteq\mathbf{S}
for any
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
i\in I^{p}
(recall that
a^{\varnothing}_{n}=0
for
p=0
). Therefore, (
16
) and (
17
) imply

\textstyle T=\overline{\left\{a^{i}:i\in\bigcup_{p=0}^{\infty}I^{p}\right\}}\subseteq\overline{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{T}.

Now, we pass to the proof of

(

⁢
i
i

)

.

Let
g\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be
N
-dimensional Lipschitz on every compact subset of
X
. Fix

. Pick some

and

such that

and

for every

. Set

. Since

is compact, we conclude that

is
N
-dimensional Lipschitz on

with a constant
C\geq 0
.

Observe that for all

and
n\in N
, we have

\gamma^{i}(0)=a^{i}\in T\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma^{i}_{n}(x_{n})=a^{i}_{n}+\alpha^{i}_{n}x_{n}\in[a^{i}_{n},a^{i}_{n}+x_{n}],

because

. In particular,
