Stabilized Proximal Point Method via Trust Region Control
Hanmin Li, Kaja Gruntkowska, Peter Richtárik

Abstract
The Proximal Point Method ( PPM ) ( 57 ) is a fundamental tool for nonsmooth convex optimization. However, its convergence is not linear under general convexity in the absence of strong convexity or other structural assumptions. To address this limitation, we study a trust-region stabilized proximal point scheme in which each proximal update is computed over a localized feasible region. We show that this simple stabilization enforces non-vanishing steps and yields a linear decrease in objective values outside any prescribed neighborhood, without assuming smoothness or strong convexity. Our analysis identifies a displacement condition as the key driver of linear descent and provides two complementary parameter regimes to guarantee it: fixing the trust-region radius and choosing the regularization properly, or fixing the regularization and selecting radii via a uniform displacement lower bound. We further give explicit characterization of the linear regime conditions respectively, and prove that the trust-region is redundant under strong convexity, Finally, we establish an exact equivalence with the Broximal Point Method ( BPM ) ( 22 ) in the active constraint regime.



1
Introduction

Optimization provides a general framework for formulating and solving learning and inference problems across machine learning, signal processing, and statistics. Model training is typically cast as the minimization of an empirical risk or regularized objective, often of the form

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;f(x),

where
x
represents model parameters and
f
encodes a loss function.
Within this broad class of problems, convex optimization plays a key role, as convexity allows local information to characterize global optimality and enables algorithms with strong guarantees.
As such, it provides a setting in which the interaction between optimization, statistics, and learning can be understood in a principled and mathematically complete way.

Throughout this work, we therefore focus on objective functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
that are proper (which means that
\dom f\mathrel{:=}\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:f(x)<+\infty\right\}
is nonempty), closed, convex and have at least one minimizer.

In this setting,
proximal operators
constitute a cornerstone of modern optimization theory and practice.
First introduced by
57
, they underlie a broad class of algorithmic ideas in machine learning, often appearing implicitly even when not explicitly recognized.
At the core of these developments lies the proximal operator itself.

Definition 1.1
.

The proximal operator with parameter
\gamma>0
associated with a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}\!\left(x\right)\mathrel{:=}\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{f(z)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|z-x\|^{2}\right\}.

Although proximal operators were originally introduced in the context of proximal algorithms, their significance extends far beyond this initial motivation.
They have found widespread practical use not only as theoretical constructs, but also as guiding principles for optimizer design, splitting methods, generalized projection viewpoints, and implicit regularization phenomena in modern optimization.
Proximal updates arise, explicitly or implicitly, in contemporary training pipelines, including accelerated methods, local training procedures, and adaptive optimizers such as
AdamW
.
We provide a more detailed discussion of these connections in
Section

3
.

Beyond serving as building blocks for more complex algorithms, one of the most classical and fundamental applications of proximal operators is their iteration as a stand-alone optimization method – the original setting in which they were introduced.
This leads to the Proximal Point Method (
PPM
)
(
45
;
39
)
, which iterates

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma_{k}f}\!\left(x_{k}\right).

(
PPM
)

Here,
\gamma_{k}\geq 0
is commonly referred to as the
step size
at iteration
k
.
This update is well defined whenever
f
is proper, closed and convex, in which case the proximal operator is single valued
(
5
;
6
)
.
In practice, each iteration amounts to solving a proximal subproblem, whose computational cost depends on the structure of
f
and is often handled via an inner optimization subroutine.

When
f
is differentiable, the optimality condition of the proximal subproblem implies that, for any
\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle z=\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f}\!\left(x\right)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;z+\gamma\nabla f(z)=x,

As a result, the
PPM
update can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k+1}).

In this form,
PPM
is closely related to Gradient Descent (
GD
), except that the gradient is evaluated at the future iterate
x_{k+1}
, which makes the update implicit.
Such implicit schemes are known to exhibit better stability properties compared to their explicit counterparts
(
58
)
.
As a result, proximal methods, unlike classical gradient-based algorithms, often exhibit notable robustness to inaccuracies in learning rate specifications
(
4
)
.

Beyond stability, an important advantage of proximal methods over widely used gradient-based approaches such as
GD
and
SGD

(
56
;
48
;
21
)
is that their convergence guarantees typically do not rely on smoothness assumptions on the objective function
(
55
)
.
This property makes them particularly attractive in modern deep learning applications, where loss functions are often nonsmooth or only weakly regularized
(
62
)
.

It is well known that under additional conditions, such as strong convexity or an error bound,
PPM
enjoys a fast
linear
convergence rate.
In the absence of such assumptions, however, no uniform linear rate can be guaranteed, and
PPM
may converge only sublinearly in the worst case.
This phenomenon occurs even for simple one-dimensional problems.

Example 1.2
.

Consider the convex function

\displaystyle f(x)\mathrel{:=}\frac{1}{4}x^{4},

and run
PPM
with a constant step size
\gamma>0
.
The optimality condition of the proximal subproblem yields
x_{k}=x_{k+1}+\gamma x_{k+1}^{3}
, which implies that
x_{k}\to 0
at a polynomial rate.
In particular,
x_{k}=\Theta(k^{-1/2})
, and hence
f(x_{k})=\Theta(k^{-2})
.

For the classical
PPM
, this slow tail behavior is a fundamental limitation. While linear convergence is known to be achievable for general convex objectives without additional assumptions
(
22
;
23
)
,
PPM
lacks such guarantees. This motivates the question of whether
PPM
itself can be modified to achieve linear convergence for any proper, closed, and convex objective. In this work, we answer this question affirmatively.

To this end, we propose a novel trust-region stabilization of
PPM
and show that it provides the key ingredient for achieving a linear decrease in the objective value, as long as the iterates remain outside a prescribed neighborhood of the minimizer set.
Concretely, we introduce the trust-region Proximal Point Method (
TRPPM
), defined by the iteration

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\argmin_{z\in{\mathcal{B}}_{t_{k}}(x_{k})}\left\{f(z)+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\|z-x_{k}\|^{2}\right\},

(
TRPPM
)

where
{\mathcal{B}}_{t_{k}}(x_{k})\mathrel{:=}\left\{z:\|z-x_{k}\|\leq t_{k}\right\}
denotes the trust-region centered at
x_{k}
with radius
t_{k}
, and the parameter
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
controls the strength of the quadratic regularization.
In other words,
TRPPM
coincides with the update rule of the classical
PPM
, but with the optimization restricted to the local neighborhood
{\mathcal{B}}_{t_{k}}(x_{k})
rather than the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This seemingly minor modification plays a central role in both the algorithmic behavior and the theoretical properties of the method. In the remainder of the paper, we develop several complementary perspectives that illuminate the motivation for introducing this trust-region constraint and clarify how it interacts with proximal regularization.

Contributions

We summarize our contributions as follows:

•

Trust-region stabilization yields linear descent under pure convexity.

We show that, for any proper, closed, and convex objective with a nonempty solution set,
TRPPM
achieves a
linear decrease
in the function value suboptimalities as long as the iterates remain outside a prescribed neighborhood of the solution set.
This result requires neither strong convexity nor smoothness, and is achieved by enforcing a uniform lower bound on the step size away from the target neighborhood.

•

Two complementary mechanisms for entering the linear regime.

We identify two distinct yet complementary ways to guarantee linear descent.
In the first regime, the trust-region radius is fixed (
t_{k}\equiv t>0
), and the regularization parameter is chosen such that
\lambda_{k}\leq\lambda_{k}^{\star}
(defined in
Lemma

4.3
), so that the proximal displacement
\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|
exceeds
t
, ensuring that the trust-region constraint is active at every non-tail iterate.
In the second regime, the regularization parameter is fixed (
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda
), and the trust-region radii are selected according to a uniform lower bound
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
(defined in
Definition

4.10
) on the proximal displacement over points away from the solution set.

•

Explicit admissible parameter choices under weak sharp minima.

We provide general estimates for admissible values of the regularization parameter
\lambda_{k}
and characterize the role of the displacement lower bound
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
in controlling the linear descent rate.
Under the classical weak sharp minima condition (
q=1
), we further derive explicit sufficient bounds on
\lambda_{k}
that guarantee entry into the linear regime.
These results turn the existence of a critical regularization threshold and a displacement-based rate constant into verifiable rules depending only on the function value gap and the trust-region radius.

•

Trust-region redundancy under strong convexity.

We show that when the objective is strongly convex,
TRPPM
achieves linear convergence even without enforcing a trust-region constraint, in agreement with classical theory.
In this regime, the contraction factor can be controlled solely through the regularization parameter
\lambda_{k}
, explaining precisely why trust-region stabilization is necessary in the merely convex setting but becomes redundant under strong convexity.

•

A bridge between
PPM
and
BPM
.

We explain the source of
TRPPM
’s improvement over the classical
PPM
by showing that
TRPPM
continuously interpolates between
PPM
and the Broximal Point Method (
BPM
)
(
22
)
(see
Section

3
) through the joint choice of the parameters
(\lambda_{k},t_{k})
.
In particular, when the unconstrained minimizer of the regularized model lies outside the trust-region, the
TRPPM
update coincides exactly with the corresponding
BPM
step.
This equivalence shows that quadratic regularization improves the conditioning of the subproblems while leaving the iterates unchanged in the active constraint regime.

3
The many facets of
PPM

Since their introduction, proximal methods have appeared in a wide range of contexts across optimization and machine learning.
We review their most prominent applications and highlight the connections that motivate our work.

Optimizer Design.

Proximal algorithms have had a lasting impact on the design of modern optimizers in machine learning.
Rather than minimizing the objective function directly, many algorithms can be interpreted as approximating a proximal step, often by linearizing the loss while retaining a proximal treatment of regularization or stabilizing terms.
A notable example is the widely used optimizer
AdamW

(
37
)
, which admits a proximal interpretation
(
63
)
.
From this perspective, the decoupled weight decay (“W”) corresponds to applying the proximal operator of a quadratic regularizer in conjunction with an adaptive gradient step on the loss.
More broadly, trust-region constraints, projection steps, and gradient clipping can be interpreted as heuristic approximations of proximal updates, in the sense that they impose implicit regularization by restricting the update to a bounded neighborhood of the current iterate
(
57
;
51
;
50
)
.

Distributed Optimization.

Motivated by the growing scale and decentralization of modern learning systems, the proximal point framework has also been extended to stochastic and distributed settings
(
29
;
40
)
.
This has led to the Stochastic Proximal Point Method (
SPPM
)
(
7
;
28
)
, also known as
FedProx

(
33
)
in the context of federated learning.
Proximal operators have proven useful in local training, a technique commonly used in federated learning.
This refers to the practice of allowing each participating client to perform several local optimization steps on its own data before a communication-intensive synchronization step is carried out.
From this viewpoint, local training can be understood as each client approximately solving a proximal subproblem centered at the current global iterate.
Variants of local training were already used in earlier work
(
54
;
46
)
, before being popularized in federated learning by
41
.
Proximal algorithms can also be used directly to formulate local training schemes, rather than merely to interpret them, as exemplified by
ProxSkip

(
43
)
, which interleaves local gradient steps with occasional proximal updates to reduce communication while retaining proximal convergence guarantees.

Projections.

It is well known that proximal operators are closely related to projection operators.
In particular, the proximal operator of a convex function can be interpreted as a generalized projection onto a certain level set of the objective
(
31
)
.
Another classical connection arises when the function in the proximal operator is chosen to be the indicator function of a set
{\mathcal{C}}
.
In this case, the proximal operator reduces exactly to the Euclidean projection,

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\iota_{{\mathcal{C}}}}\!\left(x\right)=\Proj_{{\mathcal{C}}}\!\left(x\right).

This identity shows that projection methods are a special case of proximal methods.
Projection algorithms were originally introduced for solving linear systems
(
27
)
, and were later extended to the more general setting of convex feasibility problems
(
16
)
.
In a similar spirit,
SPPM
can be viewed as a generalization of parallel projection methods.

It is known that parallel projection methods can be accelerated by extrapolation, both in practice and in theory
(
15
;
47
)
.
In this setting, extrapolation consists of taking an over-relaxed step beyond the averaged projection point along the direction from the previous iterate, a construction that is closely related to classical over-relaxation in fixed point iterations and proximal splitting methods
(
26
;
17
)
.
More recently, extrapolation has been shown to be effective in the more general setting of
SPPM
, leading to improved performance in both theory and practice
(
31
;
32
;
2
)
.

Splitting Algorithms.

Proximal splitting methods extend the proximal point principle to structured objectives where evaluating the full proximal map of the sum is computationally prohibitive, but the individual terms admit cheap proximal or gradient evaluations
(
14
;
17
)
.
A canonical setting is the composite problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;\left\{f(x)+g(x)\right\},

where splitting replaces a single proximal step on
f+g
by inexpensive operations that process
f
and
g
separately.
When one term is smooth and the other is proximable, this leads to forward-backward splitting (proximal gradient), along with its variants
(
60
)
.
When both terms are nonsmooth but proximable, Douglas-Rachford splitting applies the two proximal maps in an alternating manner, which is closely related to the alternating direction method of multipliers
ADMM

(
36
;
20
;
8
)
.
More general models involving linear operators are naturally treated by primal-dual splitting schemes, which perform explicit primal-dual updates in a product space and avoid inner linear solves
(
13
;
18
;
61
)
.
A unifying viewpoint is that many splitting algorithms can be interpreted as preconditioned or relaxed versions of
PPM
applied to a monotone inclusion, providing insight into step-size rules and over-relaxation
(
5
;
19
;
17
)
.

Acceleration.

Acceleration is closely intertwined with the proximal point framework, both as a mechanism that can be interpreted through
PPM
and as a property that
PPM
itself can inherit.
On the one hand, Nesterov acceleration can be understood as an approximation of a proximal update.
As shown in
1
, Accelerated Gradient Descent (
AGD
) arises by centering a proximal step at an extrapolated point and replacing the exact proximal subproblem by a linearization, i.e.,

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{z}\left\{\left\langle\nabla f(y_{k}),\,z-y_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\alpha_{k}}\|z-y_{k}\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}+\beta_{k}(x_{k+1}-x_{k}),

which enjoys the classical
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{K^{2}}}\right)
rate for convex objectives.
On the other hand,
PPM
itself admits accelerated variants.

24
established that
PPM
can achieve accelerated convergence for convex minimization despite its implicit and non-smooth nature.
Subsequent work showed that acceleration can be retained under inexact solution of proximal subproblems, leading to accelerated inexact and hybrid proximal frameworks
(
25
;
44
)
.
This viewpoint also motivates generic acceleration frameworks such as
Catalyst

(
34
;
35
)
, which can be seen as an inexact accelerated
PPM
applied on top of a base method.
More recently, acceleration has also been obtained by modifying the proximal geometry itself.
In particular, replacing the quadratic regularizer in
PPM
with a higher order one leads to higher-order proximal methods that achieve faster polynomial convergence rates for convex objectives
(
49
)
.

Smoothing.

The proximal operator is closely related to the Moreau envelope
(
45
)
.
It is well known that applying proximal algorithms to the original objective is equivalent to running gradient-based methods on the corresponding Moreau envelope
(
58
;
31
)
.
Importantly, this reformulation does not alter the solution set, since the minimizers of the original objective and its Moreau envelope coincide
(
52
;
53
)
.
Beyond proximal minimization, the Moreau envelope has found applications in other areas, including personalized federated learning
(
59
)
and meta-learning
(
42
)
.

BPM and Trust Region Method.

Another line of work rooted in the proximal framework is the Broximal Point Method (
BPM
)
(
22
;
23
)
.

BPM
replaces the regularization used in the proximal subproblems with an explicit trust-region-type constraint that directly bounds the step size.
Specifically, the method relies on the broximal operator:

Definition 3.1
.

The broximal operator with radius
t>0
associated with a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
is given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{brox}^{t}_{f}\!\left(x\right)\mathrel{:=}\argmin_{z\in{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(x)}f(z),

(1)

where
\|\cdot\|
is the standard Euclidean norm.

From a theoretical perspective,
BPM
is known to enjoy fast linear convergence under the assumptions that the objective is proper, closed, and convex.
However, each iteration of
BPM
requires solving a local optimization problem involving the original objective
f
restricted to a neighborhood of the current iterate.
In the absence of additional structure, this local subproblem can be as challenging as the original global problem.
Recently,
BPM
has been extended to settings in which the trust-region is defined by a non-Euclidean geometry rather than the standard Euclidean norm
(
23
)
.
The broximal framework is closely related to trust-region methods, whose origins can be traced back to early work by
30
and were later formalized by
38
in the context of nonlinear optimization.
The main idea behind these methods is to minimize, at each iteration
k
, a local model
m_{k}
of the objective
f
around the current iterate
x_{k}
, subject to a trust-region constraint that enforces locality.
In the simplest Euclidean setting, this leads to updates of the form

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\argmin_{z\in{\mathcal{B}}_{t_{k}}(x_{k})}m_{k}(z),

where
t_{k}>0
controls the radius of the trust-region.
The use of balls as trust-regions has been studied in several recent contexts.
Acceleration techniques have been combined with ball operators
(
11
)
, and similar constrained formulations have been employed in problems involving minimization of maximum or worst case losses
(
12
;
3
)
.
Across these works, the additional ball constraint provides explicit control over the step length, thereby preventing excessively large updates that may arise from ill-conditioned objectives, inexact subproblem solutions, or imperfect local models.

4
Convergence Theory of
TRPPM

Our proposed algorithm
TRPPM
admits two complementary interpretations.
On the one hand, it can be understood as a trust-region variant of the standard
PPM
.
On the other hand, it can be interpreted as an instance of approximate
BPM
based on the local model

\displaystyle m_{k}(z)\mathrel{:=}f(z)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|z-x_{k}\|^{2},

that is, a local model whose approximation error vanishes at the center.
By appropriate choices of the parameters
t_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
,
TRPPM
interpolates between these two methods: setting
t_{k}=+\infty
recovers
PPM
, while setting
\lambda_{k}=0
yields
BPM
.
To better understand the interplay between
\lambda_{k}
and
t_{k}
, we consider two complementary viewpoints in which
\lambda_{k}
is fixed while
t_{k}
varies, or
t_{k}
is fixed while
\lambda_{k}
varies.
Throughout the analysis, we assume that our objective function satisfies the following assumption.

Assumption 4.1
.

The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
is proper, closed and convex, and has a non-empty set of minimizers
{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
.

4.1
Fixing
t_{k}\equiv t

Before presenting the convergence analysis, we introduce a key quantity that appears in the subsequent arguments.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
For
\lambda>0
and
x\in\dom f
, we define the
displacement function

\phi(\lambda,x)
by

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda,x)\mathrel{:=}\|x-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\|.

In the degenerate case
\lambda=0
, we define
\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\mathrel{:=}\Proj_{{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}}\!\left(x\right)
, so that

\displaystyle\phi(0,x)=\operatorname{dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}).

The function
\phi
measures the distance between a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and its proximal minimizer, that is, the minimizer of the regularized objective.
As such, it provides a convenient way to quantify how far the proximal update would move from the current iterate.

The following lemma characterizes the behavior of
\phi(\lambda,x)
as a function of the regularization parameter
\lambda
.

Lemma 4.3

(Existence of
\lambda
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
, and consider the update equation
TRPPM
with a fixed radius
t_{k}\equiv t>0
.
For any iterate
x_{k}
such that
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})>t
, there exists
\lambda_{k}^{\star}>0
such that

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda_{k}^{\star},x_{k})=t.

Moreover, for any
\lambda_{k}\leq\lambda_{k}^{\star}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\geq t.

The above lemma guarantees that, for any iterate outside the
t
-neighborhood of
{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
, there exists a value
\lambda_{k}^{\star}
for which the displacement matches the trust-region radius.
Further properties of the displacement function are provided in
Section

B.3
.
We now use this characterization to establish the convergence behavior of
TRPPM
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
and let
\{x_{k}\}
be the iterates of
Equation

TRPPM
run with a fixed trust-region radius
t_{k}\equiv t>0
,
where the sequence
\left\{\lambda_{k}\right\}
satisfies
0<\lambda_{k}\leq\lambda_{k}^{\star}
, with
\lambda_{k}^{\star}
defined in
Lemma

4.3
.
Then, for any iterate
K
such that
\operatorname{dist}(x_{K},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})>t
(i.e., outside the
t
-neighborhood of the solution set), the following descent inequality holds:

\displaystyle f(x_{K})-f_{\inf}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\nicefrac{{t}}{{d_{0}}}}\right)^{K}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\inf}\right),

where
d_{0}\mathrel{:=}\operatorname{dist}(x_{0},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})
.

The result shows that when the trust-region radius
t
is fixed, the convergence of
TRPPM
can be explicitly regulated through a suitable choice of the parameter sequence
{\lambda_{k}}
, ensuring linear convergence of the algorithm.
In general, the sequence
\{\lambda_{k}\}
admits the following upper bound.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let assumptions of
Theorem

4.4
hold and suppose that
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})>t
and
\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\geq t
for some
k
.
Then

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{\star}\leq\frac{2\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}\right)}{t^{2}}.

Without additional assumptions on the geometry of
f
, it is in general impossible to obtain a more explicit characterization of the optimal parameter
\lambda_{k}^{\star}
.
However, such bounds can be derived under suitable growth conditions on the objective.
We illustrate this point by introducing a generalized version of the
weak sharp minima condition
.

Assumption 4.6

(Generalized Weak Sharp Minima)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
We say that
f
has
weak sharp minima of order
q\geq 1
with constant
\alpha>0
if

\displaystyle f(x)-f_{\inf}\geq\alpha\cdot\operatorname{dist}^{q}(x,{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

When
q=1
, this condition reduces to the classical weak sharp minima assumption
(
10
;
9
)
, which quantifies a linear growth of the objective away from the solution set.
Notably, it is strictly weaker than strong convexity, but nevertheless enables us to explicitly bound the parameter
\lambda_{k}^{\star}
, as illustrated by the following lemma.
For simplicity, we focus on the case
q=1
; extensions to higher orders are deferred to the appendix.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
and
4.6
with parameters
\alpha>0
and
q=1
.
Let
\{x_{k}\}
be the iterates of
Equation

TRPPM
run with
t_{k}\equiv t>0
, and suppose that the current iterate lies outside the
t
-neighborhood of the solution set, i.e.,
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})>t
.
If the parameters
\lambda_{k}
satisfy

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{k}\leq\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}+\alpha t},

then it holds that

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\geq t.

Combining this bound with
Theorem

4.4
, we obtain the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
and
4.6
with parameters
\alpha>0
and
q=1
.
Let
\{x_{k}\}
be the iterates of
Equation

TRPPM
run with
t_{k}\equiv t>0
and parameter sequence
\{\lambda_{k}\}
such that

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{k}\leq\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}+\alpha t}.

Then the following descent inequality holds:

\displaystyle f(x_{K})-f_{\inf}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\nicefrac{{t}}{{d_{0}}}}\right)^{K}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\inf}\right).

We conclude by comparing this result with the classical convergence guarantees for
PPM
.

Remark 4.9

(Comparison with
PPM
)
.

Under
4.1
, it is well known
(
6
, Theorem 10.28)
that the iterates of
PPM
with a constant step size
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda
satisfy

\displaystyle f(x_{K})-f_{\inf}\leq\frac{d_{0}^{2}}{2\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\lambda_{k}^{-1}}=\frac{d_{0}^{2}}{2K\lambda^{-1}},

(2)

yielding an
\mathcal{O}(1/K)
convergence rate in function values
(
57
)
.
In particular,
Equation

2
indicates that
PPM
does not admit a uniform linear convergence rate without additional assumptions, and forcing a linear decay via step size tuning would require
\lambda_{k}
that shrink in a pathological manner (exponentially fast with respect to
K
), which in turn makes each proximal subproblem increasingly expensive to solve.
In contrast,
TRPPM
achieves linear convergence even with a fixed trust-region radius
t_{k}\equiv t>0
, by choosing
\lambda_{k}
within the admissible range.

TRPPM
controls the per iteration effort by enforcing a trust-region constraint, thereby stabilizing the subproblems while still guaranteeing linear convergence.

4.2
Fixing
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda

We now turn to the complementary regime in which the regularization parameter is held fixed,
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda
, while the trust-region radii
{t_{k}}
are allowed to vary.
We begin by introducing a quantity that captures the minimal proximal displacement away from the solution set.

Definition 4.10
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto R\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
and fix any
x_{\star}^{f}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
,
\varepsilon>0
.
We define

\displaystyle m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)\mathrel{:=}\min_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}}\|x-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\|,

that is, the uniform lower bound of the displacement function
\phi(\lambda,x)
over the set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}\mathrel{:=}{\mathcal{B}}_{\|x_{0}-x_{\star}^{f}\|}(x_{\star}^{f})\cap\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\operatorname{dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})\geq\varepsilon\right\}.

Although
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
depends on the initial point
x_{0}\in\dom f
, we treat
x_{0}
as fixed throughout the discussion to avoid notational clutter.

Lemma 4.11

(Uniform lower bound)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
Then

\displaystyle m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)>0.

Now, let us formalize the convergence guarantee.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
and let
\{x_{k}\}
be the iterates of
Equation

TRPPM
run with fixed
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda\geq 0
and (possibly varying) trust-region radii
t_{k}\geq 0
.
Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, as long as the iterates remain outside the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of the solution set
{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
, one can choose

\displaystyle t_{k}=\theta\cdot m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)>0,\qquad\theta\in(0,1],

where
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
is defined in
Lemma

4.11
.
Under this choice, the iterates satisfy the linear descent inequality

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\inf}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\theta\cdot\nicefrac{{m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)}}{{d_{0}}}}\right)\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}\right).

Remark 4.13
.

In
22
, it is shown that under
4.1
, the iterates of
BPM
satisfy

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\inf}\leq\left(1+\frac{t_{k}}{d_{0}}\right)^{-1}\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}\right).

Specializing
Theorem

4.12
to the case
\lambda=0
and
\theta=1
yields
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)=\varepsilon
. Hence, the two convergence results coincide when
t_{k}=\varepsilon
.

4.3
The quantity
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)

To better understand the role of the quantity
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
in the convergence analysis, we now examine how it depends on the parameters
\varepsilon
and
\lambda
. We begin by discussing several basic properties before illustrating them through explicit examples.

Remark 4.14

(Edge case)
.

When
\lambda=+\infty
, the proximal operator reduces to the identity mapping,
\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\equiv x
.
As a result, the displacement vanishes for all
x
, and hence
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)=0
.

Remark 4.15

(Monotonicity in
\varepsilon
for fixed
\lambda
)
.

The neighborhood radius
\varepsilon>0
directly influences the magnitude of the threshold
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
.
Intuitively, points that are further away from the solution set are expected to exhibit a larger displacement under the proximal mapping.
This intuition can be made precise.
For a fixed minimizer
x_{\star}^{f}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
and any
0<\varepsilon_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{2}
, we have
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon_{2}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon_{1}}
,
which immediately implies the monotonicity property

\displaystyle m_{f}(\varepsilon_{2},\lambda)\geq m_{f}(\varepsilon_{1},\lambda).

Lemma 4.16

(Monotonicity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, the function
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
is a non-increasing function of
\lambda
.

Lemma 4.17

(Continuity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
is a continuous function of
\lambda\geq 0
.

When the objective function
f
admits a simple structure, the quantity
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
can often be computed in closed form.

Example 4.18

(Indicator function)
.

Let
f(x)=\iota_{{\mathcal{C}}}(x)
, where
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a closed convex set.
In this case, the proximal operator reduces to the Euclidean projection onto
{\mathcal{C}}
, namely,

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)=\Proj_{{\mathcal{C}}}\!\left(x\right).

Consequently, the displacement function simplifies to

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda,x)=\|x-\Proj_{{\mathcal{C}}}\!\left(x\right)\|=\operatorname{dist}(x,{\mathcal{C}}).

By definition of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
, it follows that
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)=\varepsilon
.

Example 4.19
.

Let
f(x)=\left\lvert x\right\rvert
. The proximal map for this function is

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)=\sign(x)\cdot\max\left\{\left\lvert x\right\rvert-\frac{1}{\lambda},0\right\},

and the associated displacement function is

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda,x)=\left\lvert x-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\right\rvert=\min\left\{\left\lvert x\right\rvert,\frac{1}{\lambda}\right\}.

Thus, restricting to
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}=\left\{\varepsilon\leq\left\lvert x\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert x_{0}\right\rvert\right\}
, we have

\displaystyle m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)=\min\left\{\varepsilon,\frac{1}{\lambda}\right\}.

Example 4.20
.

Let
f(x)=\frac{\mu}{2}x^{2}
for some
\mu>0
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}x,

and the displacement function is given by

\displaystyle\left\lvert x-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\right\rvert=\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}\left\lvert x\right\rvert.

Therefore,

\displaystyle m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)=\min_{\varepsilon\leq\left\lvert x\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert x_{0}\right\rvert}\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}\left\lvert x\right\rvert=\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}\varepsilon.

The preceding example extends naturally to higher-dimensional quadratic objectives.

Lemma 4.21

(Simple Quadratics)
.

Consider the function
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}{\bm{Q}}x
, where
{\bm{Q}}\succeq{\bm{O}}
and
{\bm{Q}}\neq{\bm{O}}
.
Then

\displaystyle m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)=\frac{\sigma_{+}}{\sigma_{+}+\lambda}\varepsilon,

where
\sigma_{+}
is the smallest positive eigenvalue of
{\bm{Q}}
.

Remark 4.22

(Comparison with
PPM
)
.

For
TRPPM
, fixing the regularization parameter
\lambda
and selecting the trust-region radius according to
Theorem

4.12
yields the linear convergence bound

\displaystyle f(x_{K})-f_{\inf}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\theta\cdot\nicefrac{{m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)}}{{d_{0}}}}\right)^{K}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\inf}\right)

as long as the iterates remain outside the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of the solution set.
Here, this linear rate is achieved by stabilizing the iterates through the trust-region constraint, rather than by forcing the regularization parameter to vary.

4.4
Strongly Convex Case

The preceding discussion shows that, under the general convexity conditions of
4.1
, achieving linear convergence requires the introduction of a trust-region constraint.
However, it is well known that the classical
PPM
already converges linearly when
f
is strongly convex.
This naturally raises the question of whether our theory reflects the fact that a trust-region mechanism is unnecessary in the strongly convex setting.
As we show below, the answer is negative: when
f
is strongly convex, the trust-region constraint becomes redundant, and linear convergence is guaranteed even in its absence.

Theorem 4.23
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
and let
\{x_{k}\}
be the iterates of
Equation

TRPPM
run with some sequences
\left\{\lambda_{k}\right\}
and
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
. Then

1.

For any chosen
t_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\inf}\leq q_{k}\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}\right),

where
q_{k}=\left(1+\frac{\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|}{\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}^{f}\|}\right)^{-1}
.

2.

If we further assume that
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\leq\prod_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(1+\min\left\{\frac{t_{k}}{d_{0}},\frac{\mu}{\lambda_{k}}\right\}\right)^{-1}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{0},

where
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\mathrel{:=}f(x_{K})-f_{\inf}
and
K
is the total number of iterations.

We now interpret the implications of
Theorem

4.23
in both the convex and strongly convex regimes.

• In the convex case, if we guarantee that
t_{k}\leq\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|
in each iteration, then by the first brox theorem (
B.2
), the next iterate is located on the boundary of the ball, which guarantees that
\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|=t_{k}
.
Consequently, we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|}{\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}^{f}\|}\geq 1+\frac{t_{k}}{d_{0}}.

Linear convergence then follows from the fact that the sequence
{t_{k}}
can be uniformly lower bounded on the set
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
when
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda
is fixed.

• In the strongly convex case, we always have

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|=\min\left\{t_{k},\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\right\}.

As in the proof of
Theorem

4.23
,

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|}{\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}^{f}\|}\geq\min\left\{1+\frac{t_{k}}{d_{0}},1+\frac{\mu}{\lambda_{k}}\right\}.

In particular, even if we set
t_{k}=+\infty
(corresponding to the absence of a trust-region constraint), the quantity on the right-hand side remains uniformly lower bounded by
1+\nicefrac{{\mu}}{{\lambda_{k}}}
.
This explains why, under strong convexity, the trust-region constraint is no longer necessary for achieving linear convergence.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we studied a trust-region stabilized proximal point framework and provided a unified analysis of its convergence behavior under minimal convexity assumptions.
By explicitly controlling the step size through the joint choice of the trust-region radius and the regularization parameter, we showed that linear descent can be obtained outside an arbitrary prescribed neighborhood of the solution set without requiring strong convexity.
Our analysis clarified the distinct roles of trust-region constraints and quadratic regularization, established two complementary mechanisms for entering the linear regime, and revealed an exact equivalence between
TRPPM
and
BPM
in the active constraint setting (refer to
Appendix

C
).

Facts and Lemmas

B.1
Facts

Fact B.1
.

If
f

\partial f
is monotone.

Fact B.2

(First Brox Theorem
(
22
)
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
and choose
x\in\dom f
.
Then

(i)

\operatorname{brox}^{t}_{f}\!\left(x\right)\neq\emptyset
.
In addition, if
{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(x)\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{f}^{\star}\neq\emptyset
, then
\operatorname{brox}^{t}_{f}\!\left(x\right)
is a non-empty subset of
{\mathcal{X}}_{f}^{\star}
.

(ii)

If
{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(x)\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{f}^{\star}\neq\emptyset
, then
\operatorname{brox}^{t}_{f}\!\left(x\right)
is a singleton lying on the boundary of
{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(x)
.

Fact B.3

(Second Brox Theorem
(
22
)
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
and choose
x\in\dom f
and
u\in\operatorname{brox}^{t}_{f}\!\left(x\right)

.

c_{t}(x)\geq 0

.

Fact B.4

.

For any
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle x-z,\,y-z\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\|x-z\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|z-y\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

(Second Projection Theorem)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a non-empty closed and convex set.
Let
u\in{\mathcal{C}}
.
Then
u=\Proj_{{\mathcal{C}}}\!\left(x\right)
if and only if

\displaystyle\left\langle x-u,\,y-u\right\rangle\leq 0,\quad\forall y\in{\mathcal{C}}.

(Theorem 3.40 of
6
)
.

Let
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}

, be proper convex functions such that
\cap_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{ri}({\rm dom}f)\neq\emptyset
.

\partial\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}\right)(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial f_{i}(x).

B.2
Useful Lemmas

When analyzing
TRPPM
, a natural first approach is to interpret it as an instance of
BPM
applied to a noisy model
m_{k}
.
Under this viewpoint, one may directly invoke
B.3
, leading to conditions that closely resemble the standard
BPM
analysis.
However, this approach yields suboptimal bounds.
To obtain sharper results, we therefore introduce the following refinement.

Lemma B.7

(Variant of Second Brox Theorem)
.

Let
f
be proper, closed and convex.
Choose
x\in\operatorname{dom}f
and let

\displaystyle u\in\argmin_{z\in{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(x)}\left\{f(z)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|z-x\|^{2}\right\},

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a constant.
Then, there exists some
c_{t}(x)\geq 0
such that

(i)

\left(c_{t}(x)+\lambda\right)(x-u)\in\partial f(u)
.

(ii)

f(y)-f(u)\geq\left(c_{t}(x)+\lambda\right)\left\langle x-u,\,y-u\right\rangle
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark B.8
.

When
\lambda=0
,
Lemma

B.7
reduces to the standard second brox theorem from
22
.
When
\lambda>0
, the coefficient
c_{t}(x)+\lambda
is strictly positive.

B.3
Displacement as a Function of Regularizing Factor

In this section, we consider
\phi(\lambda,x)\mathrel{:=}\|x-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\|
as a function of
\lambda
.

Lemma B.9

(Range)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
Then, for any
x\in\dom f
and
\lambda\in[0,\infty]

\displaystyle 0\leq\|x-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\|\leq\|x-x_{\star}^{f}\|,

where
x_{\star}^{f}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
is any minimizer of
f
.
In particular, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\|x-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\|\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}).

Lemma B.10

(Monotonicity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
For any fixed
x\in\operatorname{dom}f
,
\phi(\lambda,x)
is a monotone non-increasing function of
\lambda\geq 0
.

A natural question is whether a stronger conclusion, such as strict monotonicity, can be established.
The following counterexample demonstrates that this cannot, in general, be achieved.

Remark B.11

(Strict Monotonicity)
.

It is not possible to assert that, for an arbitrary function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
that satisfies
4.1
and any fixed point
x\notin{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
, the displacement function
\phi(\lambda,x)
is strictly decreasing in
\lambda
.
Indeed, consider the function
f(x)=\lvert x\rvert
.
It is known that
(
6

its proximal operator admits the closed-form expression

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)=\left[\lvert x\rvert-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right]_{+}\cdot\sign(x).

Fix
x=0.5
.
For
\lambda=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{1}}}\!\left(0.5\right)=0.

Similarly, for
\lambda=2
, we still have

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{2}}}\!\left(0.5\right)=0.

As a consequence, the corresponding displacement
\phi(\lambda,x)
remains constant over this interval, demonstrating that strict monotonicity with respect to
\lambda
cannot be guaranteed in general.

Lemma B.12

(Limit)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
For any
x\in\dom f
,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\phi(\lambda,x)=0,\qquad\lim\limits_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}\phi(\lambda,x)=\operatorname{dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}_{f}^{\star}).

Lemma B.13

(Continuity I)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
For any
x\in\dom f
,
\phi(\lambda,x)
is a continuous function of
\lambda
for
\lambda\geq 0
.

B.4
Displacement as a Function of the Iterate

In this section, we consider
\phi(\lambda,x)
as a function of

.

Lemma B.14
.

Consider two consecutive iterations

and

of

. If

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{f(z)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|z-x_{k}\|^{2}\right\};\qquad x_{k+2}=\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{f(z)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|z-x_{k+1}\|^{2}\right\},

\|x_{k+2}-x_{k+1}\|\leq\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|

Remark B.15
.

We cannot obtain strict decrease in

without further assumptions.
Indeed, consider
f(x)=\mu\lvert x\rvert
, where

\lambda

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)=\sign(x)\cdot\max\left(\lvert x\rvert-\frac{\mu}{\lambda},0\right).

But then, for an initial point such that
x_{0}>\frac{2\mu}{\lambda}
, we have

\displaystyle x_{1}=x_{0}-\frac{\mu}{\lambda},\quad x_{2}=x_{1}-\frac{\mu}{\lambda},

and so
\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|=\|x_{2}-x_{1}\|
.

Lemma B.16

(Lipschitz Continuity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
Then for any fixed
\lambda>0
, the mapping
\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)
is
1
-Lipschitz, i.e.,

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(y\right)\|\leq\|x-y\|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Lemma B.17

(Continuity II)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
.
Then the displacement function
\phi(\lambda,x)
is a continuous function of
x
for any
\lambda\geq 0
.

B.5
Other Lemmas

The following lemma is a generalized verison of
Lemma

4.7
for
q\geq 1
.

Lemma B.18
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
and
4.6
with parameters
\alpha>0
and
q\geq 1
.
Let
\{x_{k}\}
be the iterates of
Equation

TRPPM
run with a fixed trust-region radius
t_{k}\equiv t>0
, and suppose that the current iterate lies outside the
t
-neighborhood of the solution set, i.e.,
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})>t
.
If the parameters

satisfy

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{k}\leq\frac{2\alpha\left(\operatorname{dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-t\right)^{q-1}}{\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})+t},

then it holds that

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\geq t.

Remark B.19
.

When

, we have

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{k}\leq\frac{2\alpha}{\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})+t}\leq\frac{2\alpha}{\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}\right)+t},

which recovers the result in
Lemma

4.7
.

Appendix C
Relationship to
BPM

We now explain why
TRPPM
attains a linear convergence rate by relating it to
BPM
.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
satisfy
4.1
and fix
x\in\dom f
,
t>0
and
\lambda>0
. If
\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\notin{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(x)
and
x_{\star}^{f}\notin{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(x)
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{brox}^{t}_{f}\!\left(x\right)=\operatorname{brox}^{t}_{f+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot-x\|^{2}}\!\left(x\right).

In particular, for any non-tail iterate
x_{k}
with
\lambda_{k}\leq\lambda_{k}^{\star}
(where
\lambda_{k}^{\star}
is defined in
Lemma

4.3
), we have

\displaystyle x^{\text{BPM}}_{k+1}=x^{\text{TRPPM}}_{k+1}.

This lemma shows that, for non-tail iterates, adding a sufficiently small quadratic regularization term to
f
does not alter the next iterate of
BPM
.
Since
BPM
already converges linearly under convexity, this equivalence explains why
TRPPM
inherits a stabilizing effect and achieves a linear convergence rate.

Moreover, unlike the original objective
f
used in the subroutines of
BPM
, the model
m_{k}=f+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot-x\|^{2}
is strongly convex, and is therefore typically easier to optimize and incurs lower computational overhead.

D.1
Proof of
Lemma

4.3

Proof.

By
Lemma

B.9
, we have
0\leq\phi(\lambda,x_{k})\leq\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})
for all
\lambda\geq 0
.
Moreover, by

B.12
,
\lim\limits_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}\phi(\lambda,x_{k})=\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})>t

Hence, there exists
\lambda_{1}>0

.
Similarly, since
\lim\limits_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\phi(\lambda,x_{k})=0

\lambda_{2}>0

\phi(\lambda_{2},x_{k})<t

By

\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}

B.10

∎

D.2
Proof of
Theorem

4.4

Proof.

Applying
Lemma

B.7
, with
y=x=x_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star}^{f})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star}^{f})-(c_{t_{k}}(x_{k})+\lambda_{k})\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

(3)

On the other hand, applying
Lemma

B.7
with
x=x_{k}
and
y=x_{\star}^{f}
, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star}^{f})

\displaystyle\leq\left(c_{t_{k}}(x_{k})+\lambda_{k}\right)\left\langle x_{k}-x_{k+1},\,x_{k+1}-x_{\star}^{f}\right\rangle\leq\left(c_{t_{k}}(x_{k})+\lambda_{k}\right)\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}^{f}\|.

Assuming
x_{k+1}\neq x_{\star}^{f}
(otherwise the minimizer has already been reached), we may rewrite this inequality as

\displaystyle\frac{f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star}^{f})}{\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}^{f}\|}\cdot\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|\leq\left(c_{t_{k}}(x_{k})+\lambda_{k}\right)\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}.

Substituting this bound into
Equation

3
gives

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star}^{f})\leq f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star}^{f})-\frac{\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|}{\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}^{f}\|}\cdot\left(f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star}^{f})\right).

(4)

At each iteration, we choose
\lambda_{k}\in(0,\lambda_{k}^{\star}]
.
By
Lemma

4.3
, as long as
x_{k}
lies outside the
t
-neighborhood of
{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
, we have
\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\geq t
.
Consequently, the ball constraint is active at every such iteration, and hence
\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|=t
.
Substituting this into the inequality above, and noting that
\left\{\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}^{f}\|\right\}
is a nonincreasing sequence, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{t}{d_{0}}\right)\left(f(x_{k+1})-f_{\inf}\right)\leq f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}.

Unrolling the inequality for
k=0,\ldots,K-1
, where
x_{K}
is the last iterate outside of the
t
-neighborhood, we get

\displaystyle f(x_{K})-f_{\inf}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\nicefrac{{t}}{{d_{0}}}}\right)^{K}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\inf}\right).

∎

D.3
Proof of
Lemma

4.5

Proof.

The case
\lambda_{k}^{\star}=0
is trivial.
Assume henceforth that
\lambda_{k}^{\star}>0
.
From the optimality condition, we have

\displaystyle f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))+\frac{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}{2}\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|^{2}\leq f(x_{k}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}\left(f(x_{k})-f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))\right)\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}\right).

The claim follows by combining the above inequality with the fact that
\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}^{\star}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\geq t
.
∎

D.4
Proof of
Lemma

4.7

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive of the statement.
Assume that
\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|<t
.
By the optimality condition of the proximal subproblem, for any
x_{\star}^{f}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
,

\displaystyle f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|^{2}\leq f(x_{\star}^{f})+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}^{f}\|^{2}.

Rearranging terms,

\displaystyle f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))-f(x_{\star}^{f})\leq\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\left(\|x_{k}-x_{\star}^{f}\|^{2}-\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|^{2}\right)

The above inequality holds for arbitrary
x_{\star}^{f}
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))-f_{\inf}\leq\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\left(\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|^{2}\right).

(5)

On the other hand, invoking
4.6
, we have

\displaystyle f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))-f_{\inf}\geq\alpha\cdot\operatorname{dist}(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right),{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}).

Applying the triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))-f_{\inf}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left(\|x_{k}-\Proj_{{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}}\!\left(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\right)\|-\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\right)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\right).

(6)

Combining
Equation

5
and
Section

D.4
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2\alpha}\left(\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2\alpha}\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|\right)\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})+\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|\right).

Since we assume
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})\geq t
and
\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|<t
, we can divide both sides of the inequality by
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|>0
to obtain

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|+\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})\geq\frac{2\alpha}{\lambda_{k}},

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|\geq\frac{2\alpha}{\lambda_{k}}-\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}).

Now, by
4.6
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}\right).

Combining the above two inequalities yields

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|\geq\frac{2\alpha}{\lambda_{k}}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}\right).

Rearranging

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha}{\lambda_{k}}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}\right)<t,

gives

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}>\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}+\alpha t},

which concludes the proof.
∎

D.6
Proof of
Lemma

4.11

Proof.

We first note that the set
\left\{x:\|x-x_{\star}^{f}\|\leq\|x_{0}-x_{\star}^{f}\|\right\}
is closed and bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and therefore compact.
Moreover, the distance function
\operatorname{dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})
is continuous, which implies that the set
\left\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}_{f}^{\star})\geq\varepsilon\right\}
is closed.
By definition, the set
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
is the intersection of these two sets, and is thus closed and bounded. Hence,
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
is compact.
We know
\phi(\lambda,x)
is a continuous function of
x
for fixed
\lambda
according to
Lemma

B.17
.
By the Weierstrass extreme value theorem,
\phi(\lambda,x)
attains a minimum on the compact set
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
, which we denote as

\displaystyle m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)=\min_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}}\|x-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\|.

Since
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{f}^{\star}=\emptyset
, we know that
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)>0
, as otherwise there would exist
x_{\star}^{f}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
, contradicting the definition of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
.
Thus, for all
x\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle\|x-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x\right)\|\geq m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda).

∎

D.7
Proof of
Theorem

4.12

Proof.

When
\lambda=0
, the method reduces to
BPM
, and the claim holds trivially by choosing
t\equiv m_{f}(\varepsilon,0)=\varepsilon
.

We now turn to the case
\lambda>0
.
Applying
Lemma

B.7
with
B.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star}^{f})\leq\frac{c_{t_{k}}(x_{k})+\lambda}{2}\cdot\left(\|x_{k}-x_{\star}^{f}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}^{f}\|^{2}-\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}\right).

(7)

Thus for any
x_{\star}^{f}

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}^{f}\|\leq\|x_{k}-x_{\star}^{f}\|,\quad\text{ for all }k.

Consequently, for all iterates, we have
\|x_{k}-x_{\star}^{f}\|\leq\|x_{0}-x_{\star}^{f}\|
.
We now focus on the non-tail iterates, namely those satisfying
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})\geq\varepsilon
.
By the above inequality, all such iterates belong to the set
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
.
Applying
Lemma

4.11
, we obtain that for every non-tail iterate
x_{k}
,

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\geq m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)>0.

Since this lower bound is uniform, for any fixed
\theta\in(0,1]
we may set
t_{k}\equiv t=\theta\cdot m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
.
Using this choice in the descent inequality
Equation

4
established earlier, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\theta\cdot m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)}{d_{0}}\right)\left(f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{\star}^{f})\right)\leq f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star}^{f}),

and hence

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\inf}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\nicefrac{{\theta\cdot m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda)}}{{d_{0}}}}\right)\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\inf}\right).

This establishes linear convergence for all non-tail iterates
x_{k}
satisfying
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})\geq\varepsilon
.
∎

Proof.

is a non-increasing function of

D.9
Proof of
Lemma

4.17

Proof.

Consider any sequence
\lambda_{n}\to\lambda
with
\lambda_{n}\geq 0
.

Lower bound.

Let
\left\{n_{k}\right\}
be a subsequence such that
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda_{n_{k}})=\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda_{n})
.
For each
k
, choose
x_{n_{k}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
such that
m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda_{n_{k}})\;=\;\phi(\lambda_{n_{k}},x_{n_{k}})
.
Since
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
is compact, there exists a further subsequence (still indexed by
n_{k}
) such that
x_{n_{k}}\to\bar{x}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
.
We next prove that
\phi(\lambda_{n_{k}},x_{n_{k}})\to\phi(\lambda,\bar{x})
,
which implies

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda_{n})=\lim_{k\to\infty}m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda_{n_{k}})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\phi(\lambda_{n_{k}},x_{n_{k}})=\phi(\lambda,\bar{x})\geq\min_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}}\phi(\lambda,x)=m_{f}(\varepsilon,\lambda).

Continuity of
\phi(\lambda,x)
along
(\lambda_{n_{k}},x_{n_{k}})
.

We consider two cases.

•

\lambda>0
:
For all sufficiently large
k
, we have
\lambda_{n_{k}}>0
.
For the sake of simplicity, let us denote
z_{n_{k}}\mathrel{:=}\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{n_{k}}}}}\!\left(x_{n_{k}}\right)
.
Fix the minimizer
x_{\star}^{f}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
.
By the optimality of
z_{n_{k}}
, we have

\displaystyle f(z_{n_{k}})+\frac{\lambda_{n_{k}}}{2}\|z_{n_{k}}-x_{n_{k}}\|^{2}\leq f(x_{\star}^{f})+\frac{\lambda_{n_{k}}}{2}\|x_{\star}^{f}-x_{n_{k}}\|^{2}.

Since
f(z_{n_{k}})\geq f(x_{\star}^{f})
, we obtain
\|z_{n_{k}}-x_{n_{k}}\|\leq\|x_{\star}^{f}-x_{n_{k}}\|
.
Recall that
x_{n_{k}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
is bounded. Since the right hand side is bounded,
\left\{z_{n_{k}}\right\}
is bounded as well.
Therefore, along a further subsequence (still indexed by
n_{k}
) we may assume
z_{n_{k}}\to\bar{z}
for some
\bar{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Now, take any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By optimality of
z_{n_{k}}
,

\displaystyle f(z_{n_{k}})+\frac{\lambda_{n_{k}}}{2}\|z_{n_{k}}-x_{n_{k}}\|^{2}\leq f(z)+\frac{\lambda_{n_{k}}}{2}\|z-x_{n_{k}}\|^{2}.

Taking
\lim\inf
on the left and limit on the right, using the assumption that
f
is closed, and that

\lambda_{n_{k}}\to\lambda
,
x_{n_{k}}\to\bar{x}
,
z_{n_{k}}\to\bar{z}
, we have

\displaystyle f(\bar{z})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\bar{z}-\bar{x}\|^{2}\leq f(z)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|z-\bar{x}\|^{2}\qquad\forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Thus
\bar{z}
minimizes
z\mapsto f(z)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|z-\bar{x}\|^{2}
, i.e.
\bar{z}=\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(\bar{x}\right)
.
Since the prox minimizer is unique for
\lambda>0
, every convergent subsequence of
\left\{z_{n_{k}}\right\}
must converge to
\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(\bar{x}\right)
, hence the whole sequence satisfies
z_{n_{k}}\to\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(\bar{x}\right)
.
As a result,

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda_{n_{k}},x_{n_{k}})=\|x_{n_{k}}-z_{n_{k}}\|\to\|\bar{x}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda}}}\!\left(\bar{x}\right)\|=\phi(\lambda,\bar{x}).

•

\lambda=0
:
If
\lambda_{n_{k}}=0
for infinitely many
k
, then along that subsequence
\phi(\lambda_{n_{k}},x_{n_{k}})=\operatorname{dist}(x_{n_{k}},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})\to\operatorname{dist}(\bar{x},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})=\phi(0,\bar{x})
by continuity of the distance function.
Otherwise, we may assume
\lambda_{n_{k}}>0
for all sufficiently large
k
.
For any minimizer
x_{\star}^{f}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
, by optimality of
z_{n_{k}}
we have

\displaystyle f(z_{n_{k}})+\frac{\lambda_{n_{k}}}{2}\|z_{n_{k}}-x_{n_{k}}\|^{2}\leq f(x_{\star}^{f})+\frac{\lambda_{n_{k}}}{2}\|x_{\star}^{f}-x_{n_{k}}\|^{2}.

Since
f(z_{n_{k}})\geq f(x_{\star}^{f})
, this implies
\|z_{n_{k}}-x_{n_{k}}\|\leq\|x_{\star}^{f}-x_{n_{k}}\|
.
Taking the infimum over
x_{\star}^{f}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
yields

\displaystyle\|x_{n_{k}}-z_{n_{k}}\|\leq\operatorname{dist}(x_{n_{k}},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}).

(8)

Hence

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|x_{n_{k}}-z_{n_{k}}\|\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(x_{n_{k}},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})=\operatorname{dist}(\bar{x},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}),

where we used continuity of
x\mapsto\operatorname{dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})
.
Conversely, note that
Equation

8
and boundedness of
\left\{x_{n_{k}}\right\}
imply that
\left\{z_{n_{k}}\right\}
is bounded.
Choose a subsequence (still indexed by
n_{k}
) such that
\|x_{n_{k}}-z_{n_{k}}\|
converges to its limit inferior and
z_{n_{k}}\to\bar{z}
.
Comparing objective values at
z_{n_{k}}
and
x_{\star}^{f}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
gives

\displaystyle f(z_{n_{k}})\leq f(x_{\star}^{f})+\frac{\lambda_{n_{k}}}{2}\|x_{\star}^{f}-x_{n_{k}}\|^{2}.

Letting
k\to\infty
and using
\lambda_{n_{k}}\to 0
and boundedness of
\left\{x_{n_{k}}\right\}
gives
\limsup f(z_{n_{k}})\leq f(x_{\star}^{f})
.
By lower semicontinuity of
f
,

\displaystyle f(\bar{z})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}f(z_{n_{k}})\leq f(x_{\star}^{f}),

so
\bar{z}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
.
Therefore,

Upper bound.

be any minimizer of
\phi(\lambda,\cdot)
over
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}

This bound is tight, as equality is achieved when
\|x\|=\varepsilon

{\bm{Q}}
associated with
\sigma_{\min}

with some

•

Active constraint.

Unrolling this inequality for

∎

\displaystyle f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))-f(x_{\star}^{f})\leq\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\left(\|x_{k}-x_{\star}^{f}\|^{2}-\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|^{2}\right).

Since the inequality holds for arbitrary
x_{\star}^{f}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))-f_{\inf}\leq\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\left(\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|^{2}\right).

(17)

On the other hand, invoking
4.6
, we have

\displaystyle f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))-f_{\inf}\geq\alpha\cdot\operatorname{dist}^{q}(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right),{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}).

Applying the triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle f(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right))-f_{\inf}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left(\|x_{k}-\Proj_{{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f}}\!\left(\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\right)\|-\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\right)^{q}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\right)^{q}.

(18)

Combining
Equation

17
and
Section

D.20
, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\right)^{q}\leq\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\left(\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|^{2}\right).

Using the factorization

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\right)\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})+\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\right),

and recalling that
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})\geq t
and

\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|<t
, we may divide both sides of the inequality by
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|>0
to obtain

\displaystyle\alpha\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\right)^{q-1}\leq\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})+\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|\right).

Using again
\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})\geq t
and

\|x_{k}-\operatorname{prox}_{\nicefrac{{f}}{{\lambda_{k}}}}\!\left(x_{k}\right)\|<t
, we further obtain

\displaystyle\alpha\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-t\right)^{q-1}<\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})+t\right).

Rearranging yields

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}>\frac{2\alpha\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})-t\right)^{q-1}}{\operatorname{dist}(x_{k},{\mathcal{X}}_{\star}^{f})+t},

which concludes the proof.
∎
