Genome-wide identification and expression profiling of the HMGR gene family in Bupleurum chinense DC.
Authors: Jun Zhao, Xue Li, Meng Zhang, Qiongyu Gu, Ting Xiao, Chuanxin Mo, Kaimi Dong, Hua Chen, Chao Xin, Ma Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBupleurum chinense DC. is a commonly used medicinal plant, and the main bioactive compounds are saikosaponins (SSs), which significantly influence its therapeutic quality. 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (HMGR) is the first rate-limiting enzyme in the mevalonate (MVA) pathway, providing precursors for SSs biosynthesis. The lack of studies on the HMGR gene family in B. chinense has limited further understanding of the SSs biosynthesis mechanism. This study performed a genome-wide analysis of the BcHMGR gene family, including phylogenetic and gene structure analysis, cis-elements prediction, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) in different tissues, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis after phytohormone treatments, and explored the potential relationship between BcHMGR expression and SSs biosynthesis.ResultsFour Bupleurum chinense HMGR genes (BcHMGRs) were classified into two subfamilies, distributed across two chromosomes, and appeared to have expanded via segmental duplication. The BcHMGR proteins may possess highly conserved catalytic domains, and their promoters contain stress- and hormone-responsive cis-elements. Phytohormone treatments regulated BcHMGR expression and influenced SSs accumulation in a genotype-dependent manner. BcHMGR1, BcHMGR3, and BcHMGR4 showed root-predominant expression across most genotypes. This pattern is consistent with the accumulation sites of SSs and suggests a potential association. In particular, the relative expression levels of BcHMGR3 and BcHMGR4 showed significant increases after exogenous hormone treatment. These results suggest that they may be candidate genes involved in SSs biosynthesis, although their specific functions still require further verification.ConclusionThese findings provide candidate genes for future functional studies and potential regulation of SSs production in B. chinense and offer a theoretical basis for future metabolic engineering and artificial control of SSs biosynthesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09220-3.

Background

Bupleurum chinense DC. (B. chinense) is a species of the genus Bupleurum L. in the family Apiaceae. The dried roots of B. chinense are officially considered medicinal materials [1]. B. chinense has a long history of use in China, first recorded in Shen Nong’s Classic of Materia Medica over two thousand years ago, and has been widely applied in folk medicine from ancient times to the present day [1]. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), it is considered slightly cold in nature, with a bitter and pungent taste, and possesses pharmacological properties such as harmonizing the exterior and interior, soothing the liver, and relieving depression. It is widely used in the treatment of fever, inflammation, and related diseases [2].

The molecular basis for the pharmacological activities of B. chinense mainly lies in its abundant secondary metabolites—saikosaponins (SSs). SSs are a typical class of pentacyclic triterpenoid saponins, among which saikosaponin a (SSa), saikosaponin d (SSd), saikosaponin c (SSc), and saikosaponin b2 (SSb2) are major components with high content and significant biological activity. Modern pharmacological studies have confirmed that SSs exhibit significant therapeutic potential in multiple aspects, including anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and cholagogic effects, immuno-regulation, antiviral, and antitumor activities, thus attracting much attention in the pharmaceutical field [3–5].

At present, about 180 SSs have been isolated and identified from Bupleurum, including SSa, SSd, saikosaponin ac (SSac), saikosaponin b4 (SSb4), saikosaponin e (SSe), and saikosaponin f (SSf) analyzed in this study [6]. Among them, SSa, SSc, and SSd are the major saponins in Bupleurum. These saponin monomers are present in almost all Bupleurum species [5], including B. chinense used in this study. In addition to B. chinense, these SSs have also been detected in many other Bupleurum species, such as Bupleurum scorzonerifolium Willd., Bupleurum falcatum L., Bupleurum kaoi Liu, Chao & Chuang, Bupleurum wenchuanense R. H. Shan & S. L. Li, Bupleurum marginatum Wall. ex DC., Bupleurum longeradiatum Turcz., Bupleurum rockii H. Wolff, Bupleurum polyclonum Hand.-Mazz., Bupleurum kunmingense R. H. Shan & S. L. Li, Bupleurum fruticosum L., Bupleurum gibraltaricum Lam., Bupleurum spinosum Gouan, Bupleurum smithii H. Wolff, Bupleurum subovatum Link ex Spreng., Bupleurum longicaule Wall. ex DC., and Bupleurum rigidum L [5]. In addition, other studies have shown that B. chinense contains higher levels of SSa, SSd, and SSc [7].

SSs mainly accumulate in the roots of Bupleurum. In the primary structure of the root, they are mainly distributed in the pericycle and primary phloem. In mature roots, they are mainly located in the vascular cambium and secondary phloem, with small amounts also found in the secondary xylem [5]. In addition, SSs can also be detected in the aerial parts and seeds of some Bupleurum species [8–10]. In stems, they are mainly distributed in the epidermis, collenchyma, and the epithelial cells of secretory canals, while in leaves they are mainly found in the epidermis, spongy tissue, and palisade tissue cells [9].

The biosynthesis of triterpenoid saponins initiates from the mevalonate (MVA) pathway in the cytoplasm and the 2-C-methyl-D-erythritol-4-phosphate (MEP) pathway in plastids. 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (HMGR) is the first rate-limiting enzyme in the MVA pathway, responsible for catalyzing the irreversible reduction of HMG-CoA to MVA [11, 12]. This step is a key node for carbon metabolic flux entering terpenoid biosynthesis, and plays a crucial regulatory role in the synthesis efficiency of various terpenoids, including triterpenoid saponins [13]. Studies have shown that HMGR is not only involved in plant growth and development (e.g., cell division, fruit ripening) [14, 15] and the accumulation of secondary metabolites, but also plays an important role in plant responses to abiotic stresses (e.g., drought, salt stress) and defense against pests and diseases [16–18]. Its functions have been studied to some extent in model plants (e.g., Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh.) [11] and various medicinal plants (e.g., Salvia miltiorrhiza Bunge [15], Arnebia euchroma (Royle) I. M. Johnst., Glycyrrhiza uralensis Fisch. ex DC [19]).

Plant hormones are organic signaling molecules produced by plant metabolism that can cause clear physiological effects at very low concentrations [20]. They play important roles in plant growth and development, reproduction, stress resistance, and the accumulation of metabolic products [21]. The application of exogenous hormones can activate endogenous signal transduction pathways in plants and specifically induce the expression of metabolic enzyme genes, thereby significantly increasing the biosynthesis and accumulation of plant secondary metabolites [22]. Plant hormones other than jasmonic acid, jasmonate derivatives, and salicylic acid, such as abscisic acid (ABA), gibberellin acid (GA), and auxin (IAA), mainly regulate the accumulation of triterpenoids by controlling key enzyme activity, differential expression of key enzyme genes, and enhancement of promoter activity [23]. Studies have shown that salicylic acid treatment in Panax ginseng C. A. Mey (P. ginseng) [24] and Ganoderma lucidum (Curtis) P. Karst. (G. lucidum) [25, 26] can significantly affect enzyme activity and gene expression. In P. ginseng, the activities of peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and catalase were increased. In G. lucidum, the expression levels of key genes involved in ganoderic acid biosynthesis, including HMGR, squalene synthase, and lanosterol synthase, were significantly upregulated by 4.2-, 119.6-, and 3.2-fold, respectively. Meanwhile, the accumulation of ginsenosides and ganoderic acids was also increased, reaching 0.324% and 23.32%, respectively, higher than in the control. Mertens et al. [27] found that the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) family transcription factors TSAR1 and TSAR2 in Medicago truncatula Gaertn. can respond to jasmonic acid induction. These transcription factors co-regulate and trans-activate the key enzyme gene HMGR and the E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase gene MAKIBISHI1, thereby initiating the downstream biosynthesis of triterpenoid compounds. Linh et al. [28] and Kochan et al. [29] used exogenous ABA to induce hairy root cultures of Panax vietnamensis Ha & Grushv. and Panax quinquefolium L. They found that HMGR promoter activity increased, and the total ginsenoside content was significantly enhanced. However, the effects differed among ginsenoside monomers. The content of protopanaxadiol derivatives (Rb-type ginsenosides, including Rb1, Rb2, Rb3, Rc, and Rd) decreased sharply, while the content of protopanaxatriol derivatives (Rg-type ginsenosides, including Rg1, Rg2, and Re) increased greatly and gradually rose with the increase of ABA concentration. Mansouri et al. [30] used Cannabis sativa L. as the study material and found that exogenous gibberellin treatment decreased the activity of 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate synthase (DXS) enzyme but increased the activity of HMGR enzyme, thereby increasing the content of squalene and phytosterols. Li et al. [31] found in an auxin treatment experiment on Ricinus communis L. that auxin treatment significantly induced the expression of the key enzymes involved in lupeol biosynthesis, including HMGR and lupeol synthase (LUS). As a result, the content of lupeol in castor leaves increased, indicating that auxin acts as a positive regulator of lupeol biosynthesis. These studies indicate that plant hormones can influence the biosynthesis of triterpenoid compounds by regulating the expression of key enzyme genes such as HMGR. Therefore, the use of exogenous hormones to regulate the expression of related genes and promote the biosynthesis and accumulation of triterpenoids has become an important technical approach for studying the regulation of secondary metabolism.

Although HMGR plays a well-recognized role in terpenoid biosynthesis, the HMGR gene family in B. chinense has not yet been comprehensively identified and systematically characterized. It remains unclear how many HMGR members are present in B. chinense, how they evolved, and whether specific BcHMGR genes regulate SSs biosynthesis under hormone-induced conditions.

Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) comprehensively identify and characterize HMGR gene family members based on the latest B. chinense genome data (https://ym-lab.vip.cpolar.cn unpublished); (2) analyze their phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, conserved motifs, and promoter cis-elements; (3) examine their expression characteristics in different tissues and under various hormone treatments; (4) explore the potential relationship between BcHMGR gene expression and SSs accumulation among different genotypes. This study provides new theoretical support and genetic resources for understanding the molecular regulation of SSs biosynthesis and lays an important foundation for improving the medicinal quality of B. chinense through molecular breeding and metabolic engineering. It provides target genes and theoretical support for the artificial regulation and genetic engineering of SSs biosynthesis.

Materials and methods

Identification and characterization of the HMGR gene family in B. chinense

To identify BcHMGR gene family members from the whole genome of B. chinense, we first downloaded Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. (A. thaliana) HMGR proteins (AtHMGRs) from the TAIR database (www.arabidopsis.org) and used them as seed sequences to search for HMGRs in the B. chinense genome via BLASTp. In addition, the HMM profile of the HMGR domain (PF00368) was obtained from the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org). Verification and analysis were performed using the bioinformatics software TBtools v2.042.

Prediction of the physicochemical properties, transmembrane domains, chromosomal localization, and subcellular localization of BcHMGRs

To gain insights into the fundamental characteristics of the identified BcHMGR proteins, their physicochemical properties were analyzed using the ExPASy online tool (http://cn.expasy.org/tools), including parameters such as amino acid length, molecular weight, isoelectric point, instability index, aliphatic index, and grand average of hydropathicity. The transmembrane helices of BcHMGR proteins were analyzed using the TMHMM webserver (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/). For subcellular localization prediction, the Cell-PLoc 2.0 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc) was employed. Based on the genome annotation file of B. chinense, chromosomal positions and densities of BcHMGRs were retrieved and visualized using TBtools v2.042.

Phylogenetic analysis of BcHMGR family members in B. chinense

The reported HMGR protein sequences (Table S1) were downloaded from the NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), JGI (https://genome.jgi.doe.gov/portal/), and CNCB (https://www.cncb.ac.cn/) databases, including Taraxacum kok-saghyz Rodin (T. kok-saghyz), Taraxacum mongolicum Hand.-Mazz. (T. mongolicum), Eucommia ulmoides Oliv (E. ulmoides), Hevea brasiliensis (Willd. ex A. Juss.) Müll. Arg. (H. brasiliensis), Lactuca sativa L. (L. sativa), A. thaliana, Nicotiana tabacum L. (N. tabacum), Oryza sativa L. (O. sativa), Helianthus annuus L. (H. annuus), and Zea mays L. (Z. mays). These sequences were aligned with the BcHMGR proteins using MEGA software (https://www.megasoftware.net/). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates, while other parameters remained default. Finally, iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/) was used to optimize and visualize the phylogenetic tree.

Prediction of conserved motifs and structure of BcHMGR genes

The conserved motifs of BcHMGRs were analyzed using the MEME online tool (https://meme-suite.org), with the maximum number of motifs set to 10. Exon and intron positions of the BcHMGR genes were obtained from the General Feature Format (GFF) annotation file of the latest B. chinense genome (Chuanchai No. 2 (CC2) genome assembly, unpublished data). The gene structures were visualized using the Gene Structure Display module in TBtools v2.042.

Prediction and analysis of multiple sequence alignment, secondary structure, and tertiary structure of BcHMGR proteins

The MUSCLE algorithm of MEGA 11.0 was used to align the BcHMGR proteins, and the alignment was then imported into ESPript 3.0 (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/ESPript/index.php#) for visualization. The secondary structures of BcHMGR proteins were predicted using the SOPMA online tool (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/), including α-helices, β-sheets, extended strands, and random coils. The tertiary structures of these proteins were visualized using the SWISS-MODEL online platform (http://swissmodel.expasy.org/).

Analysis of cis-elements and synteny in the promoter regions of BcHMGRs

Based on phylogenetic analysis, the BcHMGR family members show closer evolutionary relationships with N. tabacum, H. annuus, L. sativa, T. mongolicum, and T. kok-saghyz. Therefore, these five species were considered suitable for collinearity analysis with the BcHMGR family. However, the complete genome annotation files of N. tabacum, H. annuus, and L. sativa could not be obtained from official databases, which made collinearity analysis with these species impossible. Therefore, collinearity analysis was performed between B. chinense and two species, T. mongolicum and T. kok-saghyz, using their genome and annotation files. Collinear relationships were identified using MCScanX-Fast and visualized through the Dual Synteny Plot function in TBtools. The Ka/Ks values were calculated using the Simple Ka/Ks Calculator in TBtools. Genome download sources for related species are provided in Table S1. The 2000 bp upstream promoter regions of BcHMGR genes were analyzed for cis-elements using the PlantCARE database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/). The identified cis-elements were visualized using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2.

Plant materials and hormone treatment on different genotypes of B. chinense

The Chinese Pharmacopoeia records two plant species as the sources of Radix Bupleuri: Bupleurum chinense DC. (Beichaihu) and Bupleurum scorzonerifolium Willd. (Nanchaihu). The six genotypes used in this study—Chuanbeichai No. 1 (CBC1), Miaoling No. 1 (ML1), Miaoling No. 2 (ML2), Chuanchai No. 2 (CC2), Chuanchai No. 3 (CC3), and Fengshun No. 1 (FS1)—all belong to B. chinense. Among them, CBC1 was jointly bred by Professor Jianhe Wei from the Institute of Medicinal Plant Development, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and Professor Dabin Hou from Southwest University of Science and Technology. ML1 and ML2 were collected from Shandong Province. FS1 was collected from Sichuan Province. CC2 and CC3 are new varieties bred by Professor Yu Ma from Southwest University of Science and Technology. These are the Bupleurum genotypes widely cultivated in large-scale production and are also the main genotypes used as medicinal materials. When the seedlings were two months old, the plants were selected and treated with hormones. Based on the studies of Zhan et al. [23, 32] and previous experiments conducted in our lab, the working concentrations of the four hormones were as follows: abscisic acid (ABA), 100 µM; 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA), 20 µM; gibberellic acid (GA), 100 µM; and methyl jasmonate (MeJA), 100 µM. The hormone working solutions were prepared as follows: 0.0526 g of ABA, 0.009 g of 6-BA, 0.0692 g of GA, and 0.0449 g of MeJA were weighed separately and then diluted to 2 L with distilled water, followed by thorough mixing. A total of 100 mL of hormone working solution was applied to the soil around the roots of each B. chinense plant, with three pots used as three biological replicates for each treatment. Samples for qRT-PCR analysis were collected at 0, 0.5, 2, 4, and 8 h after treatment, while samples for HPLC analysis were taken at 72 h after treatment. Root, stem, and leaf samples were collected at the stem elongation stage. Three biological replicates were performed for each treatment, and the samples were subjected to liquid nitrogen snap-freezing or RNA extraction immediately after sampling.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the roots, stems, and leaves of six B. chinense genotypes using the TaKaRa RNA extraction kit (9769, Takara Bio, Beijing, China). Reverse transcription of total RNA was carried out using the TransScript All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (AT341, TransGen, Beijing, China). Primers for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) of BcHMGRs were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 (Table S2). qRT-PCR reactions were performed using the Green qPCR SuperMix UDG kit (AQ111, TransGen, Beijing, China), with three technical replicates set for each biological replicate. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method [33], and BcADF5 [34] was used as the internal reference gene for normalization. Statistical significance was analyzed using SPSS software, and bar graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Analysis of SSs content

Quantitative analysis of multiple SSs was performed using HPLC. Root samples from B. chinense treated with ABA, 6-BA, GA, or MeJA (72 h) were freeze-dried to determine the contents of SSa, SSd, saikosaponin f (SSf), saikosaponin b4 (SSb4), saikosaponin e (SSe), and saikosaponin ac (SSac). Frozen samples (0.05 g) were extracted with 2 mL extraction solution (80% methanol containing 0.1% formic acid) via sonication (60 °C, 15 min, and 100% power). After centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 4 °C, and 5 min), supernatants were collected. This extraction was repeated three times. Combined supernatants were evaporated (60 °C), reconstituted in liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) grade methanol (5 mL final volume), and filtered (0.22 μm) for HPLC analysis. SSs standards were prepared from stock solutions (5 mg/mL in methanol) for calibration curves. The HPLC conditions were as follows: separation was performed on a C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm), with an injection volume of 10 µL, a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, and a column temperature of 30 °C. The mobile phase consisted of HPLC-grade acetonitrile and ultrapure water, and detection was performed using a UV detector at 210 nm. The gradient elution program was set as follows: 20% acetonitrile at 0 min, increased linearly to 80% at 35 min, decreased to 20% at 36 min, and maintained until 40 min [35].

Results

Identification and physicochemical property analysis of the BcHMGR genes

A total of four BcHMGR genes were identified in the B. chinense genome, and the members were named BcHMGR1-BcHMGR4 according to their order of localization on chromosome. The predicted relevant physicochemical properties are shown in Table 1. The physicochemical analysis revealed that the number of amino acids in BcHMGR proteins ranged from 566 to 577, with BcHMGR2 and BcHMGR3 being the largest. The molecular weights of the proteins ranged from 61.11 to 61.56 kDa, with an average of 61.42 kDa. The isoelectric points ranged from 6.6 to 7.9, with an average value of 7.12. The aliphatic index ranged from 68.95 to 93.95, and the instability index ranged from 41.8 to 50.59, all of which were greater than 40, indicating that the proteins are unstable. The grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) values of the four proteins were all greater than 0, indicating that they are hydrophobic proteins. BcHMGR2 and BcHMGR3 are located in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), while BcHMGR1 and BcHMGR4 are located in the plasma membrane (PM). The transmembrane helix prediction revealed that BcHMGR1-BcHMGR3 all contain two transmembrane helices, whereas BcHMGR4 possesses three transmembrane helices (Fig. S1).

Chromosomal localization and collinearity analysis of the BcHMGR genes

Chromosomal localization of the BcHMGR genes was mapped based on the B. chinense genome annotation (Fig. 1). The four BcHMGR members were distributed across two chromosomes: Chr2 and Chr4. BcHMGR1 was located on Chr2, while BcHMGR2, BcHMGR3, and BcHMGR4 were positioned on Chr4. Collinearity and duplication event analyses identified one segmental duplication event in the BcHMGR gene family (Table 2), indicating that segmental duplication may be the main mechanism underlying the expansion of the BcHMGR family. Based on phylogenetic analysis, collinearity analysis was performed between B. chinense, T. kok-saghyz, and T. mongolicum (Fig. 2). The results showed that BcHMGR1 exhibited three collinear relationships with TkHMGRs and two with TmHMGRs, while BcHMGR4 had one collinear relationship with TkHMGRs. Notably, BcHMGR1 displayed more homologous gene connections, suggesting its potential importance in promoting species diversity within the BcHMGR gene family.

Phylogenetic analysis of the BcHMGR gene family

To further investigate the evolutionary relationships of BcHMGRs with other species, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using HMGR protein sequences from B. chinense and ten other species, including H. brasiliensis, T. mongolicum, T. kok-saghyz, L. sativa, N. tabacum, H. annuus, A. thaliana, O. sativa, E. ulmoides, and Z. mays (Fig. 3). All HMGR proteins were classified into two distinct subfamilies. Except for those from O. sativa and Z. mays, which belong to Subfamily I, all other HMGRs, including BcHMGR members, were grouped into Subfamily II. Within Subfamily II, BcHMGR1 and BcHMGR4 clustered together on a single branch and grouped closely with HMGRs from N. tabacum, H. annuus, L. sativa, and T. mongolicum. Similarly, BcHMGR2 and BcHMGR3 showed a closer phylogenetic relationship to each other and also clustered with HMGRs from H. annuus, T. kok-saghyz, L. sativa, and T. mongolicum. These results suggest that, within B. chinense, BcHMGR1 and BcHMGR4 may be relatively closely related, as may BcHMGR2 and BcHMGR3. Among different species, BcHMGRs showed relatively close phylogenetic relationships with HMGRs from H. annuus, L. sativa, and T. mongolicum, which may reflect a certain degree of evolutionary relatedness and suggest potential functional conservation among these proteins.

Phylogenetic relationship, motif composition, and gene structures of BcHMGRs

Based on the phylogenetic analysis, BcHMGR2 and BcHMGR3 are more closely related phylogenetically (Fig. 4A). Gene structure analysis showed that all members contained four exons with conserved exon lengths across the family (Fig. 4C). Ten conserved motifs were identified in BcHMGR proteins, showing nearly identical composition among the four members. This structural and motif conservation suggests that these BcHMGR genes may possess similar functional roles (Fig. 4B).

Multiple sequence alignment and structural analysis of BcHMGR proteins in B. chinense

Multiple sequence alignment analysis of BcHMGR proteins demonstrated that all members contain two HMG-CoA binding sites and two nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP(H)) binding sites (Fig. S2). Two NADP(H) binding sites and one HMG-CoA binding site remained completely conserved during evolution, while one HMG-CoA binding site exhibited amino acid substitutions from valine (V) to isoleucine (I) and tyrosine (Y) to phenylalanine (F) (Fig. S2), indicating that these functional sites are highly conserved during evolution. Secondary structure prediction using BcHMGR1 as the reference and displayed above the alignment, identified 14 α-helices and 11 β-strands without β-turns, and SOPMA analysis (Table 3) further confirmed that helical structures represent the most abundant secondary elements in this protein family, followed by random coils, extended strands, and minimal turn structures. Furthermore, homology modeling of four BcHMGR proteins (Fig. 5) provided additional evidence for their structural conservation throughout evolution.

Cis-elements analysis of promoter regions in the BcHMGR genes

To further investigate the potential roles of BcHMGR genes in environmental responses, we analyzed 2.0-kb promoter regions upstream of each gene. Thirty-two cis-regulatory elements were identified across the BcHMGR promoters (Table S3), categorized into four functional groups: development-related, light-responsive, hormone-responsive, and stress-responsive (Fig. 6). All BcHMGR promoters contained elements from all four categories, with BcHMGR4 possessing the highest element count. The enrichment of stress-responsive cis-elements in the promoters of BcHMGR gene family members, including elements related to hypoxia, drought, cold, salt, oxidative stress, and transcription factors, especially the high frequency of anaerobic-responsive elements (ARE) and MYC-binding sites, suggests that BcHMGR genes may be closely associated with environmental stress adaptation. BcHMGR genes may participate in stress-induced metabolic reprogramming and thereby regulate SSs biosynthesis under changing environmental conditions.

Effects of exogenous hormone induction on SSs content in the roots of B. chinense

To further investigate the dynamics of SSs biosynthesis, HPLC analysis was adopted and revealed the SSs content changes in six B. chinense genotypes under treatment with 6-BA, ABA, GA, and MeJA (Fig. 8). The contents of SSe and SSac in CC3 were significantly reduced after treatment with all four hormones. A similar decreasing trend in SSac content was also observed in CC2. In FS1, five of the six SSs showed no significant changes after hormone treatments, whereas SSac changed significantly. Notably, in ML1, the content of SSb4 increased most significantly after treatment with 6-BA, reaching a 19.86-fold increase. Overall, exogenous hormone treatments tended to promote SSs accumulation in several B. chinense genotypes, although significant decreases in certain SSs were also observed in CC3 and partially in CC2.

Discussion

Identification and analysis of the BcHMGR gene family

SSs, the primary active constituents in B. chinense, possess significant medicinal and commercial value. Their biosynthesis involves over 20 enzymatic steps. HMGR serves as the initial rate-limiting enzyme in the MVA pathway, supplying precursors for SSs biosynthesis and serving as a key enzyme in the formation of these triterpenoid saponins [36]. This study identified four HMGR genes in B. chinense and systematically characterized their sequence features, evolutionary relationships, gene structures, promoter cis-elements, and expression profiles across tissues and hormone treatments. This genome-wide investigation provides insights into the potential association between the HMGR gene family and SSs biosynthesis in B. chinense, laying the groundwork for understanding SSs biosynthetic pathways and facilitating future molecular-level genetic manipulation to enhance triterpenoid saponin production.

The BcHMGR genes are unevenly distributed on two of the five chromosomes of B. chinense. Together with duplication analysis, this distribution suggests that segmental duplication may have contributed to BcHMGR family expansion, facilitating functional conservation and divergence through chromosomal region replication during evolution [37]. A notable feature of plant HMGR proteins is that they usually contain two transmembrane domains in the ER membrane, and their catalytic domains are exposed to the cytoplasm during function. The prediction of transmembrane helices in BcHMGR proteins showed that all BcHMGR proteins contain at least two transmembrane domains at their N-terminus. Since BcHMGR proteins are localized in the ER or PM, it is suggested that BcHMGR proteins are anchored to the membrane through these two transmembrane domains during catalysis, thereby performing their biological functions [38]. Phylogenetic analysis clearly divided the HMGR proteins into two subfamilies, with all BcHMGR members clustering into Subfamily II. This distribution pattern suggests that they may share similar functional specialization or a common evolutionary origin. Notably, most BcHMGRs grouped closely with T. kok-saghyz, L. sativa, N. tabacum, and H. annuus. This clustering may reflect the conservation of HMGR function and terpenoid biosynthesis pathways among these species, indicating potential correlations in both evolutionary relationships and metabolic pathways, and suggesting potentially similar metabolic roles. Further syntenic analysis revealed the highest number of homologous gene pairs between BcHMGR1 and TkHMGRs, with BcHMGR4 also exhibiting one syntenic pair, suggesting their potentially important roles in promoting the diversification of the HMGR gene family [39]. All BcHMGR members primarily consist of α-helices and random coils in their secondary structures. This structural feature is consistent with the characteristic motifs of the HMGR gene [40, 41], indicating these members may adopt similar spatial folding patterns and functional properties. Tertiary structure prediction showed that all four BcHMGR proteins form complex folding conformations centered on α-helical clusters, consistent with the typical structural characteristics of HMGR as a catalytic enzyme [42]. Multiple sequence alignment revealed that all BcHMGR proteins contain two conserved binding sites, indicating that they may be highly conserved during evolution [42–44]. Subcellular localization prediction indicated that BcHMGR2 and BcHMGR3 localize to the ER, suggesting that they may function in ER-associated regulation of the MVA pathway [45]. In contrast, BcHMGR1 and BcHMGR4 were predicted to be localized at the PM, consistent with previous findings [46], indicating their possible involvement in defense-related terpenoid synthesis. The distinct subcellular localizations of these four members suggest potential functional differences, where each may play different roles during growth, development, and secondary metabolism in B. chinense. The number of exons in BcHMGR family members is consistent with that of most plant HMGR genes [47], and high sequence homology among members reflects evolutionary conservation and potential functional redundancy. All members contain 10 conserved motifs and a signature HMG-CoA reductase domain, consistent with the structural characteristics of HMGR genes reported by Zheng et al. [13]. Notably, their promoter regions are enriched with stress-responsive cis-elements, indicating that the expression of these genes may be regulated by endogenous and environmental signals, thereby enhancing the environmental adaptability of B. chinense [38–44].

Tissue-specific and hormone-induced expression analysis of the BcHMGR genes

Expression analysis revealed that BcHMGR1, BcHMGR3, and BcHMGR4 show predominant expression in roots across most genotypes. This root-preferential expression pattern is consistent with the primary accumulation site of SSs, suggesting that these genes may be associated with SSs biosynthesis in roots. Notably, BcHMGR3 exhibited consistently high root expression and responsiveness to multiple hormones, resembling TkHMGR2 regulation in T. kok-saghyz [39]. These features suggest that BcHMGR3 may be involved in the regulation of SSs production under both basal and stress conditions.

Hormonal treatments significantly modulated BcHMGR gene expression and SSs accumulation in a genotype-specific manner. For instance, BcHMGR1 was downregulated by 6-BA and MeJA treatments in ML1 and ML2 genotypes, but upregulated in others. This genotype-dependent regulation suggests BcHMGR1 may function as a genetic-background-dependent regulator. In contrast, BcHMGR3 and BcHMGR4 were generally upregulated under hormonal treatments across most genotypes, suggesting positive regulatory roles in SSs biosynthesis and stress adaptation. This is consistent with hormone-responsive upregulation of MdHMGR2 and MdHMGR4 in Malus domestica Borkh [48, 49]. Although exogenous hormone treatments affected SSs accumulation in a genotype-dependent manner, BcHMGR2 expression remained undetectable in roots, stems, or leaves of B. chinense, possibly due to its low transcript abundance, similar to the reported LerHMGR11 in Lithospermum erythrorhizon Siebold & Zucc [19].

Limitations and future perspectives

This study systematically identified and analyzed the HMGR gene family in B. chinense. Based on bioinformatics analysis, tissue-specific expression patterns, expression changes induced by exogenous hormones, and variations in SSs content, we inferred that BcHMGR genes may be involved in SSs biosynthesis. However, specific functional validation results for the BcHMGR gene family were not obtained in this study. Therefore, the specific regulatory roles of these genes in SSs biosynthesis require further verification. Future studies should integrate transcriptomics, metabolomics, and functional validation approaches to further elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which BcHMGR genes regulate SSs biosynthesis and to evaluate their potential applications in molecular breeding and metabolic engineering of B. chinense.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified four BcHMGR genes distributed across two chromosomes in B. chinense. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that these genes have expanded through segmental duplication events. The encoded proteins contain conserved HMG-CoA domains with characteristic HMG-CoA and NADP(H) binding sites, possess two to three transmembrane helices, and feature stress-responsive promoter elements. Expression profiling demonstrated root-predominant expression of BcHMGR1, BcHMGR3, and BcHMGR4 across most genotypes, coinciding with SSs accumulation sites. Hormonal treatments upregulated BcHMGR3 and BcHMGR4 across genotypes, and their expression patterns were associated with changes in SSs accumulation, suggesting that they may be candidate genes potentially involved in SSs biosynthesis. These results provide insights into the genetic and hormonal regulation of SSs biosynthesis and offer candidate targets for future artificial regulation and metabolic engineering of triterpenoid saponins.
