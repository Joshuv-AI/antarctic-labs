ESSAM: A Novel Competitive Evolution Strategies Approach to Reinforcement Learning for Memory Efficient LLMs Fine-Tuning
Zhishen Sun, Sizhe Dang, Guang Dai, Haishan Ye

Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a key training step for improving mathematical reasoning in large language models (LLMs), but it often has high GPU memory usage, which makes it hard to use in settings with limited resources. To reduce these issues, we propose Evolution Strategies with Sharpness-Aware Maximization (ESSAM), a full parameter fine-tuning framework that tightly combines the zero-order search in parameter space from Evolution Strategies (ES) with the Sharpness-Aware Maximization (SAM) to improve generalization. We conduct fine-tuning experiments on the mainstream mathematica reasoning task GSM8K. The results show that ESSAM achieves an average accuracy of 78.27% across all models and its overall performance is comparable to RL methods. It surpasses classic RL algorithm PPO with an accuracy of 77.72% and is comparable to GRPO with an accuracy of 78.34%, and even surpassing them on some models. In terms of GPU memory usage, ESSAM reduces the average GPU memory usage by 18 × 18\times compared to PPO and by 10 × 10\times compared to GRPO, achieving an extremely low GPU memory usage.

Introduction

Recent breakthroughs in the mathematical reasoning ability of large language models (LLMs) have led to their deep integration with many scientific fields such as mathematics and physics, making them an indispensable part of modern life
(
8
;
19
;
12
;
4
)
. These gains in mathematical reasoning are largely driven by the rise of Reinforcement learning (RL) as a fine-tuning method
(
15
;
16
;
21
;
22
)
. However, despite its effectiveness, applying RL to fine-tune LLMs requires extremely high GPU memory resources
(
5
)
, such as fine-tuning an 8B model with PPO, 314.44 GiB of GPU memory is required (Appendix
D
), which is not friendly to resource constrained open-source communities, and limits the further development of using RL to fine-tune LLMs.

To mitigate the issue that RL requires extremely high GPU memory resources,
14
explore using Evolution Strategies (ES) to fine-tune LLMs. ES is a population based zero-order method, which does not rely on gradients and updates model parameters using only forward generation and reward evaluation. Therefore, ES can significantly reduce GPU memory usage and training cost. However, on mainstream mathematical reasoning tasks such as GSM8K
(
3
)
, the overall performance of ES is often limited and difficult to match the performance of RL methods. The reason lies in the fact that ES only explores within the model’s parameter space and then directly updates the parameters. This update approach tends to drive the model toward sharp minima, resulting in poor generalization performance. In addition, in their ES experiments,
14
use only 200 examples for training and 2,000 examples for evaluation. This split is not a standard setup, and during training the 200 examples are used as a whole without the common practice of shuffling the training data and iterating with small mini batches. This can introduce more randomness and differs from standard LLMs fine-tuning pipelines. So the practical performance of ES still needs further study.

Recent work on Sharpness-Aware Minimization (Maximization) has shown strong gains in generalization
(
6
)
. Its key idea is to reduce both the loss value and the loss sharpness, so that training moves toward flat minima where the loss stays low in a whole neighborhood of the parameters. This can reduce overfitting and make the model more robust. We introduce the idea of SAM into zeroth-order search and propose a new zeroth-order fine-tuning method called Evolution Strategies with Sharpness-Aware Maximization (ESSAM).
As shown in
Figure

1
, ESSAM tightly integrates the sharpness-aware perturbation mechanism of SAM with ES, it uses the update direction that would otherwise move the original parameters toward sharp regions to perform a reverse update, obtains a new update direction at the perturbed parameters, and then uses this new direction to update the original parameters, so that the solution of the model is guided to converge to flatter regions, enhancing the model’s robustness and generalization ability.

We follow a more standard training procedure. We use a standard split between training data and evaluation data, shuffle the training data, and perform multi step updates with small mini batches, rather than training once on a small set of samples as a whole. This reduces randomness from small sample training and better averages the noise induced variation across iterations, and the training signal is smoother and the optimization process is more stable.

Experimental results show that ESSAM extends ES to the mainstream mathematical reasoning task GSM8K and substantially improves the generalization of LLMs. While keeping low GPU memory usage, ESSAM performs much better than standard ES on the GSM8K task. It matches the strong RL methods PPO and GRPO (
Figure

2
), and it even surpasses them on some models (refer to Table
1
). These results indicate improved generalization and highlight the advantages and potential of ESSAM as a zero-order method for fine-tuning LLMs. We summarize contributions as follows:

2
Related Work

Evolution Strategies.
ES is first developed for continuous numerical optimization. The basic idea is to create a population by adding random perturbations to candidate solutions, and then recombine these perturbed solutions based on their fitness to produce the next generation. Within this framework, classic methods such as CMA-ES
(
9
)
and Natural Evolution Strategies
(
18
)
were proposed, which later laid the foundation for using evolutionary search to optimize neural network parameters. In Transformer and LLMs settings, searching over billions of parameters with evolutionary methods was long considered hard, so early work mainly focused on small scale or parameter efficient subspaces. For example,
17
used CMA-ES to fine tune only a small last layer of an mT5 model, and
10
optimized LoRA low rank adapter parameters using CMA-ES and the Fireworks algorithm.
1
explored using a genetic algorithm to fine tune a larger number of parameters in a Transformer encoder, but its overall results were usually weaker than the Adam optimizer. More recently,
14
make engineering and parallelization advances that scale ES to full parameter search in billion parameter LLMs. Although their generalization is still limited and do not transfer well to mainstream math reasoning tasks, their successful practices have provided important inspiration for our work.

Reinforcement learning.
RL fine-tuning is an important method for improving the performance of LLMs
(
2
;
24
;
13
)
and their mathematical reasoning ability
(
8
)
, and it has become a key step in the training pipelines of leading LLMs. PPO
(
15
)
and GRPO
(
16
)
are currently mainstream methods for RL training of LLMs. PPO stabilizes updates by using a clipped surrogate objective to limit the change in the ratio between the new and old policies, and it often combines a value function baseline and advantage estimation to reduce variance.
GRPO is often viewed as an efficient variant of PPO. It removes the need for a value model of comparable size to the policy, and instead uses group scores from multiple sampled outputs under the same prompt as a baseline, together with relative advantage normalization, which can reduce resource cost. Although these RL methods have advantages in different respects and can significantly improve LLMs performance, they still consume substantial GPU memory resources.

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (Maximization).
SAM
(
6
)
can encourages the model to converge to flatter minima and improves generalization. In the language model setting, prior work shows that sharpness aware training can reduce overfitting and improve downstream performance, and it also explores selective perturbations based on parameter importance.
23
propose a Fisher information based mask to balance stability and gains.
11
reduce the extra cost of SAM by changing the perturbation and update procedure from an efficiency and scalability perspective, making it more suitable for large scale training. When gradients are unavailable or only forward passes can be used,
20
introduce sharpness aware ideas into black box optimization through a distribution based reparameterization and provides theoretical analysis.
7
propose ZOSA is a novel zero-order sharpness-aware minimization framework that integrates Rademacher perturbations and adaptive loss-variance scaling to enable efficient prompt tuning for LLMs. Inspired by this work, we extend the idea of combining zeroth-order fine-tuning with SAM to the ES setting and apply it to improve the mathematical reasoning ability of LLMs.

3.1
Evolution Strategies with Sharpness-Aware Maximization

ES generates a population of perturbation models with Gaussian noise and updates parameters in a reward-weighted manner, exploring and updating the parameter space only once in each iteration (Appendix
A
). Different from standard ES, our ESSAM method introduces the SAM mechanism. It first uses the current reward weighted aggregation to move the model parameters in the opposite direction of reward increase to a nearby neighborhood point, and then uses the perturbations and rewards computed at this point to update the parameters. This process helps avoid sharp solutions and drives the model toward flatter and more robust regions, thereby improving generalization.

Specifically,
given a pretrained LLM with parameters
\theta_{0}
and a reward function
R(\theta)
,
we aim to maximize the expected reward by iteratively updating model parameters.
At iteration
t

N
i.i.d. Gaussian noises

\epsilon_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
for
n=1,\dots,N
, and construct perturbed models:

\theta_{t}^{(n)}=\theta_{t}+\sigma\epsilon_{n},\quad n=1,\dots,N.

(1)

We evaluate each perturbed model to obtain reward scores:

r_{n}=R\!\left(\theta_{t}^{(n)}\right),\quad n=1,\dots,N.

(2)

We then apply z-score normalization
(
14
)
to keep the reward scale consistent:

\mu_{r}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}r_{n},\qquad s_{r}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(r_{n}-\mu_{r})^{2}},

(3)

\hat{r}_{n}=\frac{r_{n}-\mu_{r}}{s_{r}+\delta},\quad n=1,\dots,N,

(4)

where

3.3
Understanding the algorithm design of ESSAM

In the SAM setting, when constructing the SAM neighborhood point in the first stage, its structural form is as follows
(
6
)
:

\theta_{t+1}^{\mathrm{SAM}}=\theta_{t}-\rho\cdot\frac{g}{\|{g}\|},

(7)

Where
g
is a gradient direction.

In our setting, the gradient estimate is computed as:

g_{t}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(r_{n}-\mu_{r})\,\epsilon_{n},

(8)

The estimated variance
s_{r}^{2}
is computed as:

s_{r}^{2}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(r_{n}-\mu_{r})^{2},

(9)

In our algorithm, the SAM neighborhood point is constructed as follows:

\theta_{t+1}^{\mathrm{SAM}}=\theta_{t}-\rho\cdot\frac{g_{t}}{s_{r}}.

(10)

Proposition
3.1
shows why the first stage update of our proposed algorithm is essentially performing SAM.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let the stochastic gradient estimation
g_{t}
defined in Eq. (
8
) and the variance
s_{r}^{2}

defined in Eq. (
9
). Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[g_{t}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{N-1}{N}\nabla R(\theta)+\tau,\qquad\tau=O(\sigma)

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|g_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{3}+(d-2)N^{2}+(3-2d)N+d-2}{N^{3}}

(12)

\displaystyle\cdot\bigl\|\nabla R(\theta)\bigr\|^{2}+\gamma,\quad\gamma=O(\sigma).

\mathbb{E}\!\left[s_{r}^{2}\right]=\sigma^{2}\,\bigl\|\nabla R(\theta)\bigr\|^{2}+\zeta,\qquad\zeta=O(\sigma^{3}).

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|g_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{N^{3}+(d-2)N^{2}+(3-2d)N+d-2}{N^{3}}\cdot\sigma^{-2}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[s_{r}^{2}\right].

(14)

where d denotes the number of model parameters.

Detailed proof of Proposition
3.1
is in Appendix
B
.

Since
\frac{N^{3}+(d-2)N^{2}+(3-2d)N+d-2}{N^{3}}\cdot\sigma^{-2}
is a constant,
\frac{g_{t}}{s_{r}}
and
\frac{g_{t}}{\|{g_{t}}\|}
can be regarded as equivalent, scaled only by a constant factor. Therefore, the first stage update of our proposed algorithm is essentially performing Sharpness-Aware Maximization.

Reward function.
For verifiable math reasoning tasks such as GSM8K and Countdown, we use a rule based reward function. The rules are as follows, we split the reward into an outcome accuracy reward and an output format reward:

R_{\mathrm{accuracy}}(\hat{y},y)=\begin{cases}1,&\mathrm{is\_equivalent}(\hat{y},y),\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(15)

R_{\mathrm{format}}(a)=\begin{cases}1.0,&\text{the output follows the full format;}\\
0.6,&\text{it contains both a \emph{thinking marker}}\\
&\text{and an \emph{answer marker};}\\
0.1,&\text{it contains a \emph{thinking marker} only;}\\
0.5,&\text{it contains an \emph{answer marker} only;}\\
0.0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(16)

The final reward we use is a weighted sum of the accuracy reward and the format reward:

R(a,y)=R_{\mathrm{answer}}(\hat{y},y)+0.1\,R_{\mathrm{format}}(a),

(17)

where
y
is the ground truth answer,
\hat{y}
is the model predicted answer, and
a

is the full model response to the question.

On the mainstream mathematical reasoning task GSM8K, ESSAM significantly improves the performance of all tested models and achieves performance on par with mainstream RL methods.
On the mainstream mathematical reasoning benchmark GSM8K, we systematically compare the fine-tuning performance of our proposed ESSAM with ES, PPO, and GRPO across base models of different sizes and architectures, as shown in Table

The average accuracy of ESSAM reaches 78.27%, which is significantly higher than the base models at 70.14%, representing an improvement of 8.13%, and it is also clearly better than standard ES at 75.97%, providing an additional gain of 2.3%. Moreover, ESSAM achieves average performance on par with RL methods, its average accuracy surpasses PPO at 77.71% and is essentially comparable to GRPO at 78.34%.

More specifically, on the Qwen 2.5 family, ESSAM achieves the best results on the 1.5B and 3B models, reaching 78.92% and 87.71% accuracy respectively, both higher than PPO and GRPO. On the 0.5B model, ESSAM is only 0.18% lower than GRPO, and it is higher than PPO with 53.90% accuracy. On the 7B model, ESSAM remains competitive with RL methods and reaches 92.57%, outperforming PPO. On the LLaMA family, ESSAM is also stable. It achieves 61.79%, 83.93%, and 88.93% accuracy on LLaMA-3.2-1B-it, LLaMA-3.2-3B-it, and LLaMA-3.1-8B-it respectively, which are comparable to GRPO and all higher than PPO. These results further indicate that the advantage of ESSAM is not concentrated on a single model or a specific scale. Instead, it is consistent across different model families, and it remains stable from small models to medium and large models, suggesting strong cross model generalization and good transferability. In contrast, while standard ES can bring some gains for LLMs, its improvements are less consistent, and it lags behind PPO and GRPO in both average accuracy and per model accuracy. Overall, these results show that ESSAM effectively alleviates the generalization weakness of standard ES on natural language math word problems. It makes zeroth-order full parameter fine-tuning competitive with RL on mainstream tasks such as GSM8K, and it expands the practical applicability of evolution strategy based fine-tuning for LLMs mathematical reasoning.

4.2.2
GPU Memory Usage and Time Efficiency Analysis

ESSAM can fine-tune LLMs using only inference-level GPU memory, greatly reducing GPU memory usage.
In terms of GPU memory usage, we measure and compare the GPU memory usage across base models of different sizes and architectures on GSM8K, and the results in
Figure

5
show a highly consistent and clear pattern. Across all models, ESSAM maintains the same extremely low, inference-level GPU memory usage as standard ES. This indicates that, with the aid of memory saving techniques, introducing the SAM mechanism, neighborhood probing and the two-stage evaluation update, into the ES framework does not lead to additional GPU memory usage. Therefore, although ESSAM introduces neighborhood probing and a two stage evaluation update, which adds an extra round of sampling and reward evaluation in each iteration and slightly increases the per iteration time compared to ES, ESSAM converges much faster. This allows us to stop training earlier once the target reward is reached, so ESSAM has an advantage in total training time. This is also consistent with the design goal of ESSAM, the SAM helps the optimization reach flatter and more robust regions faster, which improves iteration efficiency and leads to better time efficiency in practice.

Discussion on the trade-off between memory and computation efficiency when using vLLM.
During training, we use vLLM to accelerate inference. vLLM maintains a KV-cache to store key-value tensors of historical tokens, reducing redundant computations during the decoding phase, which enhances throughput and reduces latency. However, this comes with additional memory overhead, which increases with batch size, concurrency, and maximum generation length. By avoiding the need to store activations and gradients for backpropagation, ESSAM operates with memory usage close to inference-level. When memory is limited, the KV-cache usage can be controlled by reducing concurrency or maximum generation length to ensure stable training. When memory is sufficient, ESSAM can take advantage of available memory by increasing parallel request numbers or batch size, fully utilizing the KV cache to shorten the time needed for sampling and evaluation in each iteration, thus accelerating training.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose ESSAM, a zero-order full parameter fine-tuning method that combines Evolution Strategies with the Sharpness-Aware Maximization mechanism, aiming to serve as an alternative to RL methods for improving the mathematical reasoning ability of LLMs under limited computational resources. ESSAM follows the zero-order training setting and relies only on forward generation and rule based reward evaluation. As a result, across different models, ESSAM maintains inference-level GPU memory usage, and its GPU memory usage is also significantly lower than that of PPO and GRPO. Experimental results show that ESSAM brings stable improvements on all models and clearly outperforms standard ES. It raises the overall performance to match PPO and GRPO, and it surpasses them on some models, Therefore, our work provides a practical and resource efficient option for math reasoning fine-tuning in settings with limited compute, and it also offers an additional feasible path for the open source community.

presents the basic framework of using ES to fine-tune LLMs.

Specifically,
Given a pretrained LLM with parameters
\theta_{0}

R(\theta)

At iteration

\theta_{t}^{(n)}=\theta_{t}+\sigma\epsilon_{n},\quad n=1,\dots,N.

(18)

r_{n}=R\!\left(\theta_{t}^{(n)}\right),\quad n=1,\dots,N.

(19)

(22)
