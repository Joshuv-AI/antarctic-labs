Learning to Think from Multiple Thinkers
Nirmit Joshi, Roey Magen, Nathan Srebro, Nikolaos Tsilivis, Gal Vardi

Abstract
We study learning with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) supervision from multiple thinkers, all of whom provide correct but possibly systematically different solutions, e.g., step-by-step solutions to math problems written by different thinkers, or step-by-step execution traces of different programs solving the same problem. We consider classes that are computationally easy to learn using CoT supervision from a single thinker, but hard to learn with only end-result supervision, i.e., without CoT [ 28 ] . We establish that, under cryptographic assumptions, learning can be hard from CoT supervision provided by two or a few different thinkers, in passive data-collection settings. On the other hand, we provide a generic computationally efficient active learning algorithm that learns with a small amount of CoT data per thinker that is completely independent of the target accuracy ε \varepsilon , a moderate number of thinkers that scales as log ⁡ 1 ε ​ log ⁡ log ​ 1 ε \log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon} , and sufficient passive end-result data that scales as 1 ε ⋅ poly ​ log ⁡ 1 ε \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathrm{poly}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon} .

1.1
A Framework of Learning Concepts with Chain-of-Thought

Inspired by learning in autoregressive transformers,
[
28
]
introduced a framework for learning with autoregressive Chain-of-Thought (CoT), which we begin by recalling. A sequence-to-next-token generator, or equivalently a thinker, is a function
f:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}
. For any such function, we define the apply-and-append map
\overline{f}(x):x\mapsto\textnormal{append}(x,f(x))=[x,f(x)]\in\{0,1\}^{\left\lvert{x}\right\rvert+1}
. Autoregressive thinking produces a sequence of tokens by iteratively applying
\overline{f}
, namely
\overline{f}\circ\cdots\circ\overline{f}(x)
.

For any sequence-to-next-token generator
f
, and a thinking (i.e., generation) length
T
, we define the end-to-end mapping realized after
T
steps as a function
f^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}
, given by

f^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}(x):=\underbrace{\overline{f}\circ\overline{f}\circ\ldots\circ\overline{f}}_{\textrm{\small$T$ times}}(x)[-1]\,,

(1)

where
\mathfrak{z}[-1]
denotes the last bit of any string
\mathfrak{z}\in\{0,1\}^{*}
. We refer to all the intermediate tokens leading up to
h=f^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}(x)
as the CoT of
f
on
x
, given by a mapping
f^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}^{T}
,

f^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}(x):=[f^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{1}}(x),\dots,f^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}(x)]\,.

(2)

For any base class of next-token generators
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\{0,1\}^{*}}
, our goal is to learn the end-to-end class realized after
T
steps, given by

{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}:=\{f^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}\mid f\in{\mathcal{F}}\}\,.

(3)

We denote by
({\mathcal{F}}_{d})_{d}
a hierarchy of hypothesis classes. For inputs of length at most
d
, i.e., on the domain
{\mathcal{X}}_{d}:=\{0,1\}^{\leq d}
, we use the class
{\mathcal{F}}_{d}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\{0,1\}^{*}}
in the hierarchy. For a hierarchy parameter

T

d

{\mathcal{F}}_{d}

T

{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}_{d}
on the domain
{\mathcal{X}}_{d}
. Note that the input to the generators

We work in the standard distribution-free
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) learning framework of

1.2

{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}

1.2
Learning from Different Thinkers

Our investigation is motivated by the following question:

We are promised access to a
Consistent
oracle (
Definition

1.1
) for a training set provided by a
single
thinker. When is it possible to learn tractably
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}_{d}
from supervision provided by
multiple
thinkers that express the same concept end-to-end?

The consideration of multiple thinkers is fundamental, in our view, since a single concept often admits several distinct algorithmic procedures that nevertheless produce the same final output. At the same time, the promise of oracle access only in the single-thinker setting is a natural
starting point
: CoTs generated by different procedures may bear little-to-no relation to one another, even when they are in end-to-end agreement. This formulation can be viewed as studying learnability under multiple thinkers
relative
to the single-thinker case, which is known to be powerful enough to yield universality
[
28
,
37
]
.

To first define learning, without computational considerations, consider any base hypothesis class
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\{0,1\}^{*}}
, a domain
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}
, and a thinking length
T
. The goal of a learner is to learn
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}
over the domain
{\mathcal{X}}
under different models of supervision from multiple thinkers, which we now outline.

Models of Supervision.

Let
\kappa
be a parameter for the number of thinkers. The adversary chooses an input distribution
{\mathcal{D}}

{\mathcal{X}}
, an end-to-end function

\kappa

m

The instances

•

End-to-End (E2E) supervision:
Here, the learner only receives

(4)

•

The learner receives a training set

S_{{\sf CoT}}=\bigl\{(x^{(i)},z^{(i)})\mid z^{(i)}=f_{j^{(i)}}^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}(x^{(i)})\,\text{for all }i\in[m]\bigr\}\,.\vskip-5.69046pt

(5)

Here, the thinker identities

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

For each type of CoT supervision, the learner additionally receives

with

Goal.
The goal of the learner is always to minimize the end-to-end disagreement error:

L_{{\mathcal{D}},h_{*}}(\widehat{h})=\Pr_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\widehat{h}(x)\neq h_{*}(x)\right)\,.\vskip-5.69046pt

(6)

For every
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)

Motivation behind different models of supervision.

Our motivation for considering these models of supervision is as follows. An agency training LLMs hires multiple thinkers apriori. The adversarial setting is the most passive form of collection, where the instances
x^{(1:m)}
are released to the thinkers for CoT collection, and upon receiving them, the thinkers may contribute arbitrarily. The Instance-Dependent Selection captures a scenario where different thinkers are more likely to contribute on different parts of the input space captured by a function, which is fixed but not known to the learner. The Uniform Sampling captures the natural possibility that the agency assigns questions to multiple thinkers by some simple stochastic rule. Finally, whether thinker identities are observed is motivated by whether the CoT collection process identifies portions of the training set that are easy to train on during fine-tuning. In our framework, this amounts to knowing the partition induced by the individual thinkers, so that the learner can run the
Consistent
oracle (
Definition

1.1
) on the examples contributed by any one thinker. It is unclear, however, whether this suffices for learning; as we shall see, the answer depends on how the CoTs were collected.

We note that under E2E supervision, no CoT is present at all, and the problem reduces to standard supervised learning of the class
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}
. Moreover, when
\kappa=1
, there is no distinction between the different types of CoT supervision, nor between known or unknown identity, since the same fixed thinker must always contribute.

Sample complexity bounds.

Our focus is on the computational complexity of learning from multiple thinkers. On the sample complexity side, however,
[

]

\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}))=\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}T\cdot\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}))

samples. This upper bound applies in all our settings, since the learner may ignore the thinking traces and use only E2E labels. For a single thinker
(\kappa=1)

[
28

established the sharper bound of

\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}})\log T)
, yielding a dependence on

that is only logarithmic. For multiple thinkers, in
Appendix

B

\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}\kappa\,\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}})\log T),

as long as thinker selection is non-adversarial. This yields an improvement whenever

\kappa=2

Assuming the existence of local PRGs with polynomial stretch (
2

g(d)=\omega_{d}(1)
computable in
\mathrm{poly}(d)

If the learner can efficiently learn the function
\mathsf{id}
, then it can also learn the end-to-end class: it can find
Consistent
predictors from traces provided by different generator identities and, at inference time, use the predictor selected by the learned identity router. Thus, the identity selector
\mathsf{id}:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}
itself must be hard to learn. However, this alone is clearly not enough to establish the hardness we need, since our goal is to learn the concept
\mathcal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle d,{\rm lin}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}
. Indeed, the CoTs from any fixed thinker reveal significant information that makes the target easy to learn. The main idea behind the reduction is to construct certain “distribution forks” so that the information leaked by the first thinker lies entirely in a part of the distribution complementary to that leaked by the second thinker, and vice versa. See
Section

7.3
for details on how this is achieved.

Hardness of Learning from Few Thinkers under Uniform Sampling and Unknown Identities.

We now consider the setting where
j^{(1)},\dots,j^{(m)}\sim_{iid}{\rm Unif}([\kappa])
. If the learner knew the identities
j^{(1)},\dots,j^{(m)}
, then no hardness result such as
Theorem

2
would be possible. This is because, under uniform sampling, if the learner knows the identities, then it suffices to collect enough total examples so that it has sufficient examples from at least one thinker to learn from. Since
j^{(i)}
is completely independent of
x^{(i)}
, if we collect a subsample on which the CoTs are provided by any single thinker, then it is still an i.i.d. sample from the original source distribution. The learner could therefore apply
Consistent
to the CoT examples from a single thinker on a suitable subset, and thereby enjoy the corresponding learnability guarantee. By the pigeonhole principle, the required overall sample complexity increases by at most a factor of
\kappa
.

We ask what happens if the learner is given only the CoT dataset, and does not observe the thinker identities. We show that learning is hard in this setting from
\kappa(d)=\omega_{d}(1)
thinkers.

Theorem 3
.

Assuming local PRGs with polynomial stretch exist (
2

g(d)=\omega_{d}(1)
that is computable in
\mathrm{poly}(d)

\mathrm{poly}(d)

\mathcal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle d,{\rm lin}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T(d)}}
from the CoT supervision of
\kappa(d)
thinkers, for all

We conjecture that learning remains hard even for a mixture of
\kappa=2
thinkers.

Inferring the identities of the thinkers clearly makes learning tractable, as discussed above, by reducing the problem to the single-thinker case. But showing that the identities cannot be inferred from CoT is not enough to establish hardness.
Later in
Section

In conclusion, hardness in the presence of CoTs is delicate: unlike in standard supervised learning hardness (

2.2
Boosting-based Efficient Learner under Active and Adaptive Selection

We now investigate settings in which, given enough samples, learning is trivially possible by using CoTs from only a single thinker, even when CoTs from multiple thinkers are available. As noted in the previous section, if the thinkers are chosen uniformly at random, independently of the input instances, and their identities are
known

The main idea behind the algorithm:

The algorithm is simple once the connection to boosting is made. From any single thinker, we request only enough CoTs to obtain a
weak
predictor, requiring at most
\mathsf{M}_{\star}=O(\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}_{d}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}))
CoT examples, independent of
\varepsilon
. We begin with a “large” training set of end-to-end examples of size
m=\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}_{d}))
, and maintain a distribution over these examples, as in standard boosting.

The main bottleneck is computational: in each round, we must find a weak learner for the current distribution. To do so, we draw a “small” subsample of size
\mathsf{M}_{\star}
from that distribution and request CoTs on those examples from a single unknown thinker. Given these CoTs, we invoke the tractable
Consistent
oracle to find a predictor consistent with all prefix–next-token pairs appearing in the traces. Since the CoTs come from a single thinker, this yields, for
\mathsf{M}_{\star}=O(\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}_{d}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}))
, a predictor whose error on the current distribution is bounded away from
1/2
, e.g., at most
\varepsilon_{0}=0.25
. Under a slightly relaxed protocol (cf.
Remark

2.1
), the same can be achieved but with the improved bound
\mathsf{M}_{\star}=O(\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}_{d})\log T)
.

We then update the distribution according to the end-to-end errors and repeat. Crucially, the CoTs in different rounds need not come from the same thinker, so the learner never needs to return to any specific thinker. The adversary may choose the thinker adaptively based on the full history, and possibly even observing
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
in our protocol in
Figure

2
, while the per-thinker CoT queries remain independent of
\varepsilon
. After
O(\log m\log\log m)=O_{d,T}(\log\varepsilon^{-1}\log\log\varepsilon^{-1})
rounds, the procedure outputs a weighted majority of predictors from
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}_{d}
that is end-to-end consistent with the full training set. The final guarantee then follows from a standard uniform-convergence argument over the effective class containing this weighted-majority predictor.

2.3
Discussion and Future Directions

In this work, we studied the problem of learning from CoTs provided by multiple thinkers. In practice, end-to-end supervision is often cheap but learning from it is computationally hard, while perfect CoT supervision can make learning easy, but is extremely costly, as we discussed in
Section

1
. Our study takes a first step toward understanding how imperfections in the CoT supervision affect the landscape of learning. We argue that supervision from multiple thinkers is a natural form of imperfection and, in our view, a central aspect of learning with CoTs. The resulting landscape interpolates between computationally hard and tractable regimes, depending on how the data is collected (see also
Table

1
). On the one hand, in passive data-collection settings, learning remains hard under cryptographic assumptions. On the other hand, as the learner gains more control over the collection process, learning becomes tractable. In stronger active and adaptive data-collection regimes, it can even admit strong guarantees with the right algorithmic design. We believe our results provide theoretical grounding for some modern practices, such as curating training data to make it easier to train on, and active and adaptive collection of CoT data from varied sources during fine-tuning (see, for example, large-scale training reports
[
18
]
).

Future work.

Our work leaves several questions open regarding the learnability of
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}_{d}
for classes
({\mathcal{F}}_{d})_{d}
with a tractable
Consistent
oracle:

•

Can we match the performance of
Algorithm

1
in non-adaptive settings, and obtain a guarantee while keeping the per-thinker CoT budget
\mathsf{M}_{\star}
independent of
\varepsilon
? For example, can this be done using only active queries, without adaptivity? More generally, is there a fundamental tradeoff between adaptivity and the total number of CoT queries needed for efficient learnability?

•

In the (non-active) uniform sampling and
known
thinker identities setting, is there a
\mathrm{poly}
-time algorithm that learns to accuracy
\varepsilon
using
o(1/\varepsilon)
examples per thinker (hiding factors depending on
d
and
T
)? Is there a
\mathrm{poly}
-time algorithm that even weakly learns from multiple thinkers using a per-thinker CoTs smaller than what is required for weak learning from a single thinker?

•

Is there a
\mathrm{poly}
-time algorithm that learns from
\kappa=2
thinkers if identities are
unknown
(and uniform sampling)? Recall that
Theorem

3
establishes hardness for
\omega_{d}(1)
thinkers, and we conjecture that learning is already hard when
\kappa=2

In the above questions, we demand either efficient algorithms given access to a tractable oracle, or lower bounds for some tractable base class (e.g.,

It is also natural to ask whether one can guarantee not only end-to-end correctness, but also CoT faithfulness, in the sense of matching the CoT of one of the thinkers. Many empirical works identify this as an important challenge for current reasoning models
[
13
, e.g.,]
, especially because RL-based post-training, as typically implemented today, relies primarily on reward signals based on end-to-end feedback. Our main positive result (
Theorem

4
) guarantees only end-to-end accuracy and provides no guarantee of CoT agreement. It would be interesting to understand if there are and tradeoffs when measuring the
\mathsf{pass@}k
-error, in which the learner is allowed multiple attempts to produce a correct CoT. Such objectives are common in benchmarks
[
12
, e.g.,]
and are natural in settings where verifying an answer is easier than finding one and the answer space is combinatorially large.

Furthermore, one can ask what oracles beyond
Consistent
for a single thinker (cf.
Definition

1.1
) are meaningful, perhaps in a way that captures similarity among CoT data. A common way to model the non-uniqueness of CoTs is through a stochastic formulation, in which the generator
\pi
belongs to a policy class
\Pi
. A natural generalization of the
Consistent
oracle in this setting is a likelihood-maximization oracle. This view is also consistent with the stochastic nature of modern LLMs, and how they are trained. It is common to reason about performance under the assumption that such an oracle exists; see, e.g.,
[
42
]
. Our
Theorem

3
, however, rules out the existence of such an oracle when the policy is a uniform mixture of a small number of deterministic linear-threshold generators
(f_{w_{1}},\dots,f_{w_{\kappa}})
. This raises the question of which structural assumptions make such oracles tractable.

Going beyond the settings studied here, it would be interesting to understand other form of imperfections in CoTs. For example, what happens when the learner observes CoTs that are partially erased by a channel only revealing partial CoTs? When does learning remain hard, and under what assumptions can it become possible? One natural example with partial CoTs is to reveal only prefixes from long traces, while providing full long ones only when necessary. Such approaches have already shown some empirical promise
[
3
,
26
, e.g.,]
. An interesting direction is to identify natural concept classes for which learning is possible under this type of supervision.

First

We arrange the weights and biases of the first layer so that they exactly “align” with

Next

The next challenge is to compute the final output

(w_{s},\theta_{s})
align perfectly with
[y_{1},\dots,y_{s-1}]
in the CoT. Note that all weights in the linear threshold that are aligned with the CoT bits
y_{1},\dots,y_{s-1}
up to this point are
0

such that, as it moves to the right in the first

Thus, in order to make sure that

Remark 3.2
.

A couple of remarks are in order. First, the main technicality in the reduction is the construction of the feature map in

Not only could this establish hardness under other cryptographic assumptions, but it is also interesting from the perspective of learnability, as the expressivity can be thought of as learning the class when CoT is provided (by single thinker) using autoregressive linear thresholds. Second, all our hardness results rule out algorithms that learn the function class

3.2
Hardness of Learning from Two Thinkers under Instance Dependent Selection and Known Identities

We now provide a proof overview of
Theorem

2
. We show that learning remains hard under CoT supervision from even two thinkers when the identity of the thinker contributing on a given instance can depend on the instance itself. This hardness holds even if the learner knows the identities, i.e., which thinker provided the CoT on each example.

Recall from
Section

2.1
that the identity selector function
\mathsf{id}:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}
must itself be hard to learn. However, our goal is to establish hardness of learning the class
\mathcal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle d,{\rm lin}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}
, and CoTs from any single thinker can leak significant information about the target, making it easy to learn. The key idea is to create “forks” in the distribution so that one thinker has completely trivial CoTs on the part of the distribution where the final output is
1
, while the other has trivial CoTs on the part where the final output is
0
. Thus, both the CoTs and the identities can be reconstructed from the hard-to-learn end-to-end function itself. Finally, by taking the identity function
\mathsf{id}
to be the end-to-end function itself, we conclude hardness. To create such distribution forks, we proceed with a two-step argument.

CoT-leakage-preserving property.

First, observe that the construction from
Section

3.1
, relating depth-
2
threshold circuits to autoregressive linear thresholds, is CoT-preserving in a certain sense. The leakage in the CoTs generated by
f_{w}
, up to the point of expressing the hard-to-learn circuit, is exactly the sequence of outputs of the intermediate nodes of the circuit, namely
[y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{s}]
. This is because the autoregressive computation produces the outputs of the intermediate nodes in the first
s-1
steps, followed by
s-1
zeros, and then finally outputs
y_{s}
.

Lemma 3.3

(Also formalized in
Lemma

6.2
)
.

The simulation in
Lemma

3.1
is
chain-of-thought preserving
: the autoregressive computation explicitly reproduces the value of each gate in the circuit at a designated time step. In particular, there is a fixed efficiently computable mapping, depending only on
s
, such that
(i) each hidden gate value appears exactly once during the computation,
(ii) all other intermediate steps output
0
, and
(iii) the final step outputs the value of the circuit.

Having said that, even polynomial-size circuits of any depth are easy to learn with CoTs that reveal the outputs of their intermediate nodes
[
37
,
45
]
. By the CoT-preserving relationship between linear thresholds and depth-2 circuits, it suffices to construct such distribution forks for circuits. In particular, under both assumptions, we encode hard distinguishing tasks using two depth-
2
threshold circuits that compute the same function but have a similar “forking” property: the CoTs of the two circuits are trivial on mutually complementary parts of the distribution, corresponding respectively to final outputs
0
and
1
.

Creating distribution forks.

In existing hardness results, one side of this property already holds trivially. For example, for intersections of halfspaces, whenever the final output is
1
, all intermediate gates must also output
1
, making the CoT trivial to generate. However, all the useful information that makes learning algorithmically feasible from a single thinker is present in the CoTs on the part of the distribution where the output is
0
. The main challenge is therefore to encode the same hard function—
not
its negation—by a different depth-
2
circuit for which the intermediate-layer outputs are again trivial on the part where the final output is
0
, thereby burying all the useful information in the complementary part where the output is
1
.

Remark 3.4
.

We emphasize that the other encoding must compute the same function, but in a different way. A tempting idea is to simply negate an intersection of halfspaces, which yields a union of halfspaces with the desired property that whenever the final output is
0
, all intermediate-layer nodes evaluate to
0
. This is enough to conclude hardness of learning unions of halfspaces in the standard supervised setting, but it is not sufficient for our problem: we need two encodings of the same function whose CoT leakage is trivial on different parts of the distribution.

We illustrate such a construction under
1
. The work of
[
33
]
already carries out one side of the construction by implementing the decryption functions of Regev’s
[
44
]
cryptosystem, based on hardness assumptions for
\mathsf{GapSVP}
and
\mathsf{SIVP}
. They showed that the decryption function for a fixed public key is piecewise constant on intervals, and realized this function using degree-
2
polynomial threshold functions (PTFs). Here we show that the same function can also be realized as a
union
of degree-
2
PTFs; see
Figure

3
for a visual demonstration. Thus, we have:

Lemma 3.5

(Formalized in
Lemma

7.1
)
.

Each decryption function of Regev’s cryptosystem can be implemented by two circuits (via degree-
2
PTFs) such that:

•

if the final output of the decryption is
1
, then all the nodes in the first circuit output
1
;

•

if the final output of the decryption is
0
, then all the nodes in the second circuit output
0
.

Figure 3
:
(Left) The construction of
[
33
]
, which encodes the decryption functions of Regev’s
[
44
]
cryptosystem using intersections of degree-
2
PTFs. (Right) Our construction, which encodes the same functions using unions of degree-
2
PTFs.

This construction directly gives a way to generate CoTs from two thinkers based only on the hard-to-learn end-to-end labels. If we take the identity function
\mathsf{id}
to be the end-to-end decryption function itself, then the learner also knows the thinker identities. However, learning under such CoT supervision would imply breaking the cryptosystem. A similar construction is also carried out under
2
, assuming the existence of local PRGs with polynomial stretch. There, we encode the hard-to-learn concept, whose output labels are indistinguishable from random labels, using DNF and CNF formulas. This has the same desired property; see
Lemma

7.2
.

3.3
Hardness of Learning from Few Thinkers under Uniform Sampling and Unknown Identities

We now prove
Theorem

3
, which shows that learning is hard under the supervision of
\omega_{d}(1)
thinkers, with
j^{(1:m)}\sim_{iid}{\rm Unif}([\kappa])
, when the learner
does not
observe the identities.

First of all, here, no such distribution forking is possible, as in
Section

3.2
, because the thinker is chosen uniformly at random, independently of the input instance. Also recall that it must be hard to infer the generator identities from the CoTs. Otherwise, the problem could essentially reduce to the easy case of learning under single-generator supervision. The reason is that, because of the decoupled sampling, each partition is still an i.i.d. sample from the original source distribution. The learner could therefore run
Consistent
on a suitable subset and then invoke the guarantee for the single-thinker setting. By the pigeonhole principle, this increases the required overall sample complexity by at most a factor linear in the number of generators
\kappa
.

However, the fact that the identities cannot be inferred is not by itself sufficient to imply hardness. As we show later in
Section

11
, this phenomenon already appears in the problem of learning noisy parities with CoT supervision, which is again hard to learn from end-to-end supervision alone. We show that a simple correlation-based algorithm can nevertheless tractably learn the target even under
adversarial
contributions from
superexponentially
many CoT generators, despite the fact that identifying the generator identities is impossible.

The main idea behind the reduction.

Thanks to the CoT-leakage-preserving property in
Lemma

3.3
, to establish hardness of learning from CoTs generated by
(f_{w_{1}},\dots,f_{w_{\kappa}})
, it suffices to carry out the desired construction with
\kappa
depth-
2
threshold circuits.

Under the existence of local PRGs with polynomial stretch,
[
16
]
proved that there is a concept class of DNF formulas such that the class is hard to weakly PAC learn, because the corresponding labels are indistinguishable from random Bernoulli labels (for some specific input distribution). For every DNF
\psi
in this hard-to-learn class, we show the existence of
\kappa
different DNF formulas, denoted by
(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{\kappa})
, that satisfy the following property: all of them compute the same function as
\psi
, and by observing the hard-to-learn end-to-end labels, it is possible to efficiently sample from the induced distribution on the outputs of the DNF terms for a formula
\psi_{k}
, when
k\sim{\rm Unif}([\kappa])
. Note that this is impossible for any fixed unknown
\psi_{k}
, because of the learnability result of
[
37
,
45
]
.

Coincidentally, a structural property already implicit in the construction of a single DNF
\psi
in
[
16
]
turns out to be sufficient for our purposes. After making this property explicit, our DNF formulas
(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{\kappa})
are obtained simply by permuting the terms in their formula
\psi
.

Claim 3.6

(Formalized in
Lemma

8.1
)
.

For every formula
\psi
in the hard-to-learn class of DNF formulas with
\omega_{n}(1)
terms, established by
[
16
]
under the existence of local PRGs with polynomial stretch, whenever the output is
1
, exactly one term is satisfied, and whenever the output is
0
, all terms evaluate to
0
.

By simply permuting the terms and looking at a uniform distribution over these formulas, we obtain a distribution in which, whenever the final output is
1
, the unique satisfied term (i.e., gate in the circuit) appears at a uniformly random location. Trivially, if the final output is
0
, then all intermediate output nodes are
0
. Thus, it is efficient to sample from the distribution on CoTs generated by a uniform mixture of the resulting generators, using only the end-to-end labels. This completes our reduction since the E2E class is hard to learn, and CoTs under this model of supervision can be sampled in polynomial time by only observing E2E labels.

We note that if the class of DNFs has
\rho(n)=\omega_{n}(1)
terms, then the number of thinkers grows as the factorial of
\rho(n)
. However, the result of Daniely and Vardi
[
16
]
rules out learning
\rho(n)
-term DNFs for any
\rho(n)=\omega_{n}(1)
. Thus, by choosing
\rho(n)
to grow sufficiently slowly, we can ensure that
\kappa=\rho(n)!
is still only mildly growing, as needed for
Theorem

3
.

Remark 3.7
.

A final note is that the same property also holds under the construction of Klivans and Sherstov
[
33
]
. However, in that case, the number of gates in the circuit (i.e., the number of halfspaces) is
\omega_{n}(\log n\log\log n)
. Therefore, creating a collection of thinkers by permuting the gates leads to hardness for only a super-polynomial number of thinkers. It remains open whether one can show hardness under this assumption with only polynomially many thinkers, or even with just two thinkers, as we conjecture in
Section

2
.

SFT (next-token prediction on CoT traces) and RL (from verifiable rewards).

As discussed in
Section

1
, learning with CoT resembles supervised fine-tuning of language models, where intermediate reasoning traces are provided as supervision. Learning with CoT on a next-token prediction objective that forces supervision on intermediate tokens was popularized by the success of GPT-2
[

[

Theoretical works on the power of Chain-of-Thought.

These developments have, in turn, sparked significant interest within learning theory in understanding how access to such intermediate computations alters the landscape of learnability. Recent works study this question from several perspectives, including the expressive power of transformer architectures with CoT
[

,
35
,

[

In a nutshell, the provided CoT traces break a hard-to-learn concept into easier-to-learn pieces, thereby enabling tractable learnability. In fact,

[

{\mathcal{H}}(S):=\{h(S):h\in{\mathcal{H}}\}.
The growth function
\Gamma_{{\mathcal{H}}}:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
is defined by

\Gamma_{{\mathcal{H}}}(m)=\max_{(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)})\in{\mathcal{X}}^{m}}\left|\{(h(x^{(1)}),\dots,h(x^{(m)})):h\in{\mathcal{H}}\}\right|=\max_{S\in{\mathcal{X}}^{m}}|{\mathcal{H}}(S)|.

We will also abuse notation and write
\Gamma_{{\mathcal{H}}}(S)=|{\mathcal{H}}(S)|
for the number of behaviors of
{\mathcal{H}}
on
S
.

When
{\mathcal{Y}}=\{0,1\}
is binary, we can define the VC dimension of the class and its relationship with the growth function via Sauer’s lemma; see
Lemma

A.2
.

Definition 5.4

(VC Dimension)
.

When
{\mathcal{Y}}=\{0,1\}
, for any
{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{{\mathcal{X}}}
, we say that
{\mathcal{H}}
shatters a set of points
S\in{\mathcal{X}}^{m}
iff
\left|{\mathcal{H}}(S)\right|=2^{|S|}.
The Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension of the class, denoted by
\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{H}})
, is the largest integer
D
such that
\Gamma_{{\mathcal{H}}}(D)=2^{D}.
If no such
D
exists, then we say that
\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{H}})=\infty
.

E2E and CoT Consistent Rules.

For our problems, consider two standard learning rules. First, under E2E supervision, given
S_{{\sf e2e}}=\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\mid i\in[m]\}
:

\text{Return $\widehat{f}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}$, for some $\widehat{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}$ such that for all }i\in[m],\,\,y^{(i)}=\widehat{f}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}(x^{(i)})\,.

(
\textsc{Consistent}_{{\sf e2e}}
)

When the learner additionally observes CoTs from a single thinker, it can further use this information and consider the following rule. Given

S_{{\sf CoT}}=\{(x^{(i)},z^{(i)})\mid i\in[m]\}
:

\text{Return $\widehat{f}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}$, for some $\widehat{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}$ such that for all }i\in[m],\,\,\,z^{(i)}=\widehat{f}^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}(x^{(i)})\,\,.

(
\textsc{Consistent}_{{\sf CoT}}
)

For a fixed domain
{\mathcal{X}}
and thinking length
T
, consider
\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}})
(
Definition

5.4
) over
{\mathcal{X}}
. This characterizes learnability under E2E supervision using the learning rule

Consistent
e2e

. To analyze

Consistent
CoT

, under the availability of CoTs from a single thinker, we consider the following CoT loss class:

{\mathcal{L}}^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}({\mathcal{F}}):=\{\ell_{f}:(x,z)\mapsto\mathbbm{1}\{z\neq f^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}(x)\}\mid f\in{\mathcal{F}}\}\,.

(13)

This is a binary class over the domain
{\mathcal{X}}\times\{0,1\}^{T}
.
[
28
]
provided a bound on the sample complexity of CoT from a single thinker in terms of the VC dimension of the loss class
{\mathcal{L}}^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}({\mathcal{F}})
. By definition, we have
\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{L}}^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}({\mathcal{F}}))\leq\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}})
, since
z^{(i)}
always contains the final label
y^{(i)}
.
[
28
]
showed that the former,
\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{L}}^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}({\mathcal{F}}))
, can be significantly smaller than
\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}})
; see
Proposition

5.6
. For now, we state the statistical learnability results under E2E supervision and single-thinker CoT supervision in terms of these complexity measures.

In particular, we have the following sample complexity bounds, which follow directly from standard uniform convergence guarantees for realizable learning (
Proposition

A.3
), applied to the E2E function class
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}
and the CoT loss class
{\mathcal{L}}^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}({\mathcal{F}})
.

Proposition 5.5

(Theorems 3.1, 3.2, 3.4
[
28
]
)
.

There exists a universal constant
c>0
such that the following holds. For any base class
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\{0,1\}^{*}}
, thinking length
T
, domain
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}
, the rule

Consistent
e2e

learns the class
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}
with sample complexity

m(\varepsilon,\delta)\leq c\left(\frac{\min\{\log|{\mathcal{F}}|,\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}})\log\varepsilon^{-1}\}+\log\delta^{-1}}{\varepsilon}\right)\,.

The rule

Consistent
CoT

learns the class
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}
under the CoT supervision from a single thinker with sample complexity

m(\varepsilon,\delta)\leq c\left(\frac{\min\{\log|{\mathcal{F}}|,\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{L}}^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}({\mathcal{F}}))\log\varepsilon^{-1}\}+\log\delta^{-1}}{\varepsilon}\right)\,.

Benefits of Autoregressive Function Classes.

The central message of
[
28
]
is the benefit of autoregressive learning. They show that, by applying the same function autoregressively, one obtains a form of parameter sharing, and thus the statistical complexity of learning the class
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}
when fixed single-thinker CoT is provided remains nearly independent of
T
, in terms of the VC dimension of the base class
{\mathcal{F}}
. Note that
\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}})
can be thought of as the statistical complexity measure for the corresponding single-step problem of learning to predict the next token. Formally, they prove the following:

Proposition 5.6

(Theorems B.1, B.5
[
28
]
)
.

For any
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\{0,1\}^{*}}
and thinking length
T
,

\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}})\leq O\left(T\cdot\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}})\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{L}}^{{\sf CoT}\textnormal{-}{T}}({\mathcal{F}}))=O\left(\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}})\log T\right)\,.

Here, for any domain domain
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}
, the quantity
\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}})
is taken over the domain
{\mathcal{X}}\times\{0,1\}^{T}
in order to bound the quantities on the left over the domain
{\mathcal{X}}
.

We note that
[
28
, Theorem 3.3]
also shows that the rule
\textsc{Consistent}_{{\sf e2e}}
may require
\Omega(T\cdot\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}}))
samples for some class
{\mathcal{F}}
, and thus the linear dependence on
T
is necessary. On the other hand, combining
Propositions

5.6
and
5.5
implies that, under supervision from a single thinker, the rule

Consistent
CoT

learns the class
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}
with sample complexity depending logarithmically on
T
, as long as
\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}})
is independent of
T
.

Remark 5.7

(Nearly
T
-Independent Guarantees for Multiple Thinkers)
.

The above discussion raises a natural question: can one obtain similar (statistical) benefit of autoregressive learning under the supervision of
\kappa
thinkers? Clearly, one can use

Consistent
e2e

to learn, but then the sample complexity has a linear dependence on
T
for VC classes
{\mathcal{F}}
in the worst case. On the other hand, it is not clear what the right generalization of

Consistent
CoT

is because the data may not be realizable by any single
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
in the case of multiple thinkers. We show that, using an appropriate generalization of

Consistent
CoT

, one can still enjoy a similar benefit and obtain a nearly
T
-independent guarantee; see
Theorem

10
in
Appendix

B
. Under supervision from
\kappa
thinkers, even with unknown identities, and under Instance Dependent Sampling (so only excluding Adversarial Selection), we derive the sample complexity bound

m(\varepsilon,\delta)\leq O\left(\frac{\kappa\cdot\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}})\log(\kappa T)\log\varepsilon^{-1}+\log\delta^{-1}}{\varepsilon}\right)\,.

Here,
\mathrm{VCdim}({\mathcal{F}})
is over the domain
{\mathcal{X}}\times\{0,1\}^{T}
to capture learnability over a domain
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}
. This guarantee is interesting in the regime
\kappa\ll T
, for example when
\kappa=2
and the thinking length is very large. It shows that a learning rule can still achieve nearly
T
-independent sample complexity for VC base classes.

5.3
Cryptographic Assumptions

In this subsection, we state our cryptographic hardness assumptions. In particular, we assume (i) subexponential quantum hardness of some worst-case lattice problems, namely,
\mathsf{GapSVP}
and
\mathsf{SIVP}
, and (ii) the existence of Goldreich’s local PRGs with polynomial stretch
[
21
]
. We do not expect the reader to have prior knowledge on the technical aspects of these problems, cryptography, or the assumptions themselves, to understand the rest of the paper.

Hardness of Worst-case Lattice Problems
\mathsf{GapSVP}
and
\mathsf{SIVP}
.

An
N
-dimensional lattice
L
is a discrete additive subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. It can be specified by a basis
B=[b_{1},\dots,b_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
as
L=B\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. The special case we focus on is when
B
is full-rank. For a lattice
L
and
1\leq i\leq N
, define its
i^{\mathrm{th}}
successive minimum as

\lambda_{i}(L)\;:=\;\inf\Bigl\{r>0:\dim\bigl({\rm span}(L\cap B_{r}(0))\bigr)\geq i\Bigr\},

where
B_{r}(0)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\|x\|_{2}\leq r\}
denotes the Euclidean ball of radius
r
centered at the origin. Let
\alpha=p(N)
be an arbitrary polynomial. We will consider two problems on lattices:

Problem 1

(Gap Shortest Vector Problem
(\mathsf{GapSVP})
)
.

For an approximation factor
\alpha=\alpha(N)
, an instance of
\mathsf{GapSVP}_{\alpha}
consists of an
N
-dimensional lattice
L
and a number
\theta>0
, under the promise that either

\lambda_{1}(L)\leq\theta

or

\lambda_{1}(L)>\alpha\cdot\theta
.
The goal is to decide which of the two cases holds.

Problem 2

(Shortest Independent Vector Problem
(\mathsf{SIVP})
)
.

For an approximation factor
\alpha=\alpha(N)
, an instance of
\mathsf{SIVP}_{\alpha}
consists of an
N
-dimensional lattice
L
. The goal is to output a set of linearly independent lattice vectors, each of length at most

\alpha\cdot\lambda_{N}(L)
.

We make the following assumption.

Hardness Assumption 1
.

For any polynomial
p(N)
, at least one of
\mathsf{GapSVP}_{\alpha=p(N)}
(
1
) or
\mathsf{SIVP}_{\alpha=p(N)}
(
2
) has no quantum algorithm that runs in time
2^{o(N)}
.

All known classical and quantum algorithms for
\mathsf{GapSVP}_{\alpha}
and
\mathsf{SIVP}_{\alpha}
, for polynomial approximation factors, require time
2^{\Omega(N)}
. A falsification of the above assumption would therefore constitute a major breakthrough in lattice algorithms and cryptography; see, e.g.,
[
40
]
and the references therein. A weaker variant, assuming only the absence of polynomial-time algorithms for certain polynomial approximation factors, was used in the classical work of
[
33
]
, which gave the first representation-independent hardness result for learning intersections of
n^{\epsilon}
halfspaces for any fixed
\epsilon
. The exact assumption was recently used in hardness-of-learning results by
[
48
,
49
]
.

Existence of Local Pseudo Random Generators.

Fix integers
N,M
and a constant
K
. An
(N,M,K)
-hypergraph
G
consists of
M
ordered hyperedges

S_{1},\dots,S_{M}\subseteq[N],

where each hyperedge
S_{i}
contains exactly
K
distinct vertices. Let
P:\{0,1\}^{K}\to\{0,1\}
be a Boolean predicate. Given a hypergraph
G
, we define the
Goldreich pseudorandom generator

[
21
]

f_{P,G}:\{0,1\}^{N}\to\{0,1\}^{M}

by:

f_{P,G}(x)=\bigl(P(x_{S_{1}}),\dots,P(x_{S_{M}})\bigr)\quad\text{ for all $x\in\{0,1\}^{N}$\,,}

where
x_{S_{i}}
denotes the restriction of
x
to the coordinates indexed by
S_{i}
.

The parameter
K
is called the
locality
of the generator. When
K
is constant, both the predicate
P
and the generator
f_{P,G}
are said to be
local
. The PRG is said to have
polynomial stretch
if
M=N^{s}
for some constant
s>1
.

Let

{\mathcal{F}}_{P,N,M}:=\{f_{P,G}:G\text{ is an }(N,M,K)\text{-hypergraph}\}

denote the family of generators. Let
{\mathcal{G}}_{N,M,K}
be the distribution over such hypergraphs obtained by sampling each hyperedge independently and uniformly at random from all ordered
K
-tuples of distinct elements of
[N]
. We write
x\sim\{0,1\}^{N}
for a uniform draw from
\{0,1\}^{N}
.

We say that
{\mathcal{F}}_{P,N,M}
is an
\epsilon
-pseudorandom generator (or
\epsilon
-PRG) if for every probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\left|\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\sim{\mathcal{G}}_{N,M,K}\\
x\sim\{0,1\}^{N}\end{subarray}}\bigl[\mathcal{A}(G,f_{P,G}(x))=1\bigr]-\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\sim{\mathcal{G}}_{N,M,K}\\
y\sim\{0,1\}^{M}\end{subarray}}\bigl[\mathcal{A}(G,y)=1\bigr]\right|\leq\epsilon.

That is, given
G
, the distinguishing advantage between a random output of
f_{P,G}
and a uniform
y\in\{0,1\}^{M}
is at most
\epsilon
. We make the following assumption:

Hardness Assumption 2

(Existence of Local PRG with polynomial stretch)
.

For every constant
s>1
, there exists a constant
K
and a predicate
P:\{0,1\}^{K}\to\{0,1\}
such that
{\mathcal{F}}_{P,N,M}
, with
M=N^{s}
, is a
\tfrac{1}{3}
-PRG.

This assumption was used by
[
16
,
15
,
7
]
to prove hardness for several concept classes (see
[
16
]
for further discussion on the assumption).

6
Hardness of Learning from End-to-End Supervision for Short Thinking Length

In this section, we study learning from end-to-end (E2E) supervision, where the learner observes only the final labels (see
Definition

5.2
). This is the most challenging learning setting considered in this work.
Consider the class of autoregressive linear thresholds
\mathcal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle d,{\rm lin}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T(d)}}
over
\{0,1\}^{d}
with thinking length
T(d)
(see

). While this class is statistically learnable under E2E supervision using only

samples, we show that it is not computationally efficiently learnable, even when
T(d)
is very small.

Theorem

1
(restated).

Let
g(n)\leq\mathrm{poly}(n)
be any non-decreasing sequence computable in
\mathrm{poly}(n)
time. Assuming there is no
\mathrm{poly}(n)
-time algorithm that weakly learns the class of depth-
2
threshold circuits of size
g(n)
on the domain
\{0,1\}^{n}
, there is no
\mathrm{poly}(d)
-time algorithm that weakly learns
\mathcal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle d,{\rm lin}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T(d)}}
for all
T(d)\leq 2g(d)
.

Combining the above result, with well-known hardness results for depth-
2
threshold circuits of small size, and their special cases (e.g., DNFs and intersection of halfspaces)
[
33
,
5
,
14
,
16
,
49
,
6
]
, we obtain the hardness for learning
\mathcal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle d,{\rm lin}}
for small values of
T(d)
.

Corollary 6.1
.

The following statements hold.

•

Assuming the quantum hardness of
\mathsf{GapSVP}
or
\mathsf{SIVP}
(
1
), for any non-decreasing
g(d)=\omega_{d}(\log d\log\log d)
computable in
\mathrm{poly}(d)
time, there is no
\mathrm{poly}(d)
-runtime algorithm that weakly learns
\mathcal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle d,{\rm lin}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T(d)}}
for all
T(d)\leq 2g(d)
.

•

Assuming Random K-SAT hypothesis (see
[
14
]
), the same conclusion also holds for any non-decreasing sequence
g(d)=\omega_{d}(\log d)
that is computable in
\mathrm{poly}(d)
time.

•

Assuming local PRGs with polynomial stretch exist (
2
),the same conclusion also holds for any non-decreasing sequence
g(d)=\omega_{d}(1)
that is computable in
\mathrm{poly}(d)
time.

The first part follows from the construction of
[
33
]
after artificially padding zeros to the input, together with the stronger subexponential-time hardness assumption (
1
). This will be directly formalized when proving
Theorem

2
in
Section

7.3
, since the setting of CoT supervision there is more general than that of E2E supervision. The second and third parts follow, respectively, from
[
14
]
and
[
16
]
(refer to
Section

2.1
). We note that, under a certain Sparse LPN assumption
[
6
, Corollary 7.4]
, we also obtain hardness as in the last part of the corollary for
g(d)=\omega_{d}(1)
; refer to
Section

4
. Note also that the thinking lengths in
Corollary

6.1
yield a significant improvement over the thinking length
T=\Theta(d)
for which
[
28
]
established hardness.

The key ingredient underlying
Theorem

1
is an expressivity lemma, which is the main technical contribution of this section. Informally, the lemma shows that even with a small thinking length
T
, autoregressive linear thresholds can simulate all depth-
2
threshold circuits of comparable size, which we specify now.

Depth-2 Threshold Circuits.

Let
{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
denote the input. A depth-
2
linear threshold circuit
C:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is defined as a computation over a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
G=(V,E)
with the following structure:

•

A set of
n
input nodes
V_{\text{in}}=\{v_{\text{in},1},\dots,v_{\text{in},n}\}
, corresponding to the input bits
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
;

•

A set of
s-1
hidden nodes
V_{\text{hid}}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{s-1}\}
, each receiving edges only from input nodes;

•

A single output node
v_{s}
, which receives edges only from the hidden nodes;

•

A weight assignment
w:E\to\mathbb{R}
on the edges.

Each node
v\in V
is assigned a Boolean value
{\rm val}(v)\in\{0,1\}
computed as follows. For input nodes,
{\rm val}(v_{\text{in},k})=x_{k}
for all
k\in[n]
. Each other node
v_{i}\in V_{\text{hid}}\cup\{v_{s}\}
computes a threshold function with bias
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle{\rm val}(v_{i})=\textnormal{thr}\left(\theta_{i}+\sum_{(u,v_{i})\in E}w(u,v_{i})\,{\rm val}(u)\right)=\mathbbm{1}\left[\theta_{i}+\sum_{(u,v_{i})\in E}w(u,v_{i})\,{\rm val}(u)\geq 0\right].

The circuit output is given by
C({x})={\rm val}(v_{s})
. We define
C_{i}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
to be the value of the
i
-th gate, that is
C_{i}({x})={\rm val}(v_{i})
. The
size
of the circuit is the number of non-input nodes, i.e.,
|V\setminus V_{\text{in}}|=s
. This class captures several well-known function classes as special cases, including DNFs, CNFs, and intersections of halfspaces. In particular, a DNF with
s
terms, a CNF with
s
clauses and an intersection of
s
halfspaces, can be represented by a depth-
2
threshold circuit of size
s+1
.

We now state the expressivity lemma formally.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any input size
n
and a circuit size parameter
s
, there exists a
\mathrm{poly}(n,s)
-time computable mapping
\phi:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{d}
, with
d=n(2s-1)+4s-3
, such that for any depth 2 threshold circuit of size
s
computing
C:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, there exists
{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
T=2s-1
, such that
f_{{w}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}(\phi({x}))=C({x})\text{ for all }{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

CoT preserving property:
Moreover, there exists
I\subset[T]
with
|I|=s
and
\mathrm{poly}(s)
time injective mapping
\phi^{\prime}:[s]\to I
such that:

1.

\phi^{\prime},\phi
depend only on
s
.

2.

f^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}}_{w}(\phi({x}))=C_{t}({x}),\text{ for all }{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n},t\in[s]
.

3.

f^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{t}}_{w}(\phi({x}))=0,\text{ for all }{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n},t\in T\setminus\{I\}
.

Our lemma improves upon the expressivity bounds of
[
28
]
. While they simulate circuits of arbitrary depth, their construction specialized to depth-
2
circuits yields parameters
d=\Theta(ns^{2})
and
T=\Theta(ns^{2})
, which are strictly worse than the bounds achieved here of
d=\Theta(ns)
and
T=\Theta(s)
.

The second part of the lemma establishes a stronger structural property: each gate of the circuit is explicitly represented at a designated thinking step, which can be efficiently determined as a function of the circuit size
s
. While this property is not needed for
Theorem

1
, it will be instrumental in our later hardness results for learning with CoT later in
Sections

7
and
8
(also for small values of
T
).

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

Fix some non-decreasing
g(n)
such that
g(n)\leq\mathrm{poly}(n)
and it is computable in time
\mathrm{poly}(n)
. Let
C:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be a target function from the hard class of depth-
2
threshold circuit of size
g(n)
.
Then by
Lemma

6.2
, there exists a
\mathrm{poly}(n)
-time computable mapping
\phi:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{d}
, which depends only on
g(n)

, and a vector
{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
f_{{w}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}(\phi({x}))=C({x})
for all
{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, where
d=d(n)=O(n\cdot g(n))
and
T=T(n)=2g(n)-1\leq 2g(n)
. Since
g
is non-decreasing and
d\geq n
, we also have that
T\leq 2g(n)\leq 2g(d)
. Suppose toward contradiction that there exists a
\mathrm{poly}(d)
-time algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
that learns
\mathcal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle d,{\rm lin}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T(d)}}
under E2E supervision for all
T(d)\leq 2g(d)
. We construct a
\mathrm{poly}(n)
-time learner for the original class as follows. Given labeled samples
({x}^{(i)},y^{(i)})
, we transform them into
(\phi({x}^{(i)}),y^{(i)})
. Since
\phi
is computable in
\mathrm{poly}(n)
time and
d=\mathrm{poly}(n)
, this transformation is efficient. By construction in
Lemma

6.2
, the target satisfies
C({x})=f_{{w}}^{{\sf e2e}\textnormal{-}{T}}(\phi({x}))
for some
T\leq 2g(d)
, so
{\mathcal{A}}
can be applied to learn it in time
\mathrm{poly}(d)=\mathrm{poly}(n\cdot g(n))
, which is also
\mathrm{poly}(n)
. This yields a
\mathrm{poly}(n)
-time learner for the class of depth-
2
threshold circuits of size
g(n)
, a contradiction.

6.1
Simulating Depth-2 Threshold Circuits with Small Thinking Length

We now provide the proof of our expressivity lemma (
Lemma

6.2
).

Proof of
Lemma

6.2
.

Clearly, if
s=1
, the claim follows by taking
\phi({x})=(\theta_{1},x[1],\dots,x[n])\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, where
\theta_{1}
is the bias term, and
{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
to be
(1,{w}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, where
{w}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the circuit weights, so the final output can be computed after
T=1
step. Thus, we can assume that
s\geq 2
. For simplicity, we assume that the first layer contains
s\geq 1
nodes (instead of
s-1
), so the total number of nodes in the circuit is
s+1
. The desired result will follow at the end of the proof after replacing
s
by
s-1
in the final bounds on
T,d
. Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{s},v_{s+1}
be the nodes of the circuit, where

Remark 10.2

From a statistical perspective, it is known (Corollary 4.2 from

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}(\cdot\mid Y=y)={\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}(\cdot\mid Y=y)\quad\text{for all }y\in\{0,1\},

(36)

but differ in class imbalance, then any predictor that performs sufficiently well on the imbalanced distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}
is guaranteed to perform well on the more balanced distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
.

Lemma 10.7

(Error Transfer under Class-Prior Shift)
.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}
be a distribution over
{\mathcal{X}}\times\{0,1\}
such that
\Pr_{({x},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[y=0]\in[1/2-b_{1},1/2+b_{1}]
for some
b_{1}>0
. Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}
be a distribution over
{\mathcal{X}}\times\{0,1\}
such that the class-conditional distribution under
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}
is identical to that under
{\mathcal{D}}
(as in Eq.
36
), but the class imbalance toward label zero is larger. Formally,
\Pr_{({x},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[y=0]\in[1/2+b_{2},1/2+b_{3}]
for
b_{1}<b_{2}<b_{3}<1/2
. Let
h:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be such that
\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}(h):=\mathbb{E}_{({x},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}[h({x})\neq y]\leq\Pr_{({x},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}[y=1]/2
. Then
\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}}(h)\leq 1/4+b_{1}/2
.

Proof.

Let

p:=\Pr_{({x},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[y=0]\in\left[\tfrac{1}{2}-b_{1},\tfrac{1}{2}+b_{1}\right],\qquad q:=\Pr_{({x},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}[y=1]\in\left[\tfrac{1}{2}+b_{2},\tfrac{1}{2}+b_{3}\right].

In particular, since
b_{2}>b_{1}
, we have
q>p
.

Define the class-conditional error rates of
h
by

\alpha:=\Pr\bigl[h({x})\neq y\mid y=0\bigr]=\Pr\bigl[h({x})=1\mid y=0\bigr],\qquad\beta:=\Pr\bigl[h({x})\neq y\mid y=1\bigr]=\Pr\bigl[h({x})=0\mid y=1\bigr].

Because
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
have identical class-conditional distributions,
the quantities
\alpha
and
\beta
are the same under both
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}
.
Therefore,

\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}}(h)=p\alpha+(1-p)\beta,\qquad\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}(h)=q\alpha+(1-q)\beta.

Assume
\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}(h)\leq q/2
, which implies that
\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}(h)\leq\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{b_{3}}{2}
.
First, since
\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}(h)\geq(1-q)\beta
, we have

\beta\leq\frac{\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}(h)}{1-q}\leq\frac{\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{b_{3}}{2}}{1-q}.

Using
q\leq\tfrac{1}{2}+b_{3}
gives
1-q\geq\tfrac{1}{2}-b_{3}
, hence

\beta\leq\frac{\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{b_{3}}{2}}{\tfrac{1}{2}-b_{3}}=\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}(\tfrac{1}{2}-b_{3})}{\tfrac{1}{2}-b_{3}}=\tfrac{1}{2}.

Next, compare the two risks:

\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}}(h)-\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}(h)=\bigl(p\alpha+(1-p)\beta\bigr)-\bigl(q\alpha+(1-q)\beta\bigr)=(q-p)(\beta-\alpha).

Since
\alpha\geq 0
, we have

\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}}(h)=\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}(h)+(q-p)(\beta-\alpha)\leq\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{shift}}}(h)+(q-p)\beta\leq\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{b_{3}}{2}\Bigr)+(q-p)\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}.

Finally, using
p\geq\tfrac{1}{2}-b_{1}
and
q\leq\tfrac{1}{2}+b_{3}
, we obtain

q-p\leq\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+b_{3}\Bigr)-\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}-b_{1}\Bigr)=b_{3}+b_{1},

and therefore

\ell_{{\mathcal{D}}}(h)\leq\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{b_{3}}{2}\Bigr)+\tfrac{1}{2}(b_{3}+b_{1})=\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{b_{1}}{2}.

The next lemma presents a variation of the Birthday Paradox, providing an upper bound on the number of samples that ensures, with high probability, no collisions occur.

Lemma 10.8

(The Birthday Paradox)
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{t}\sim_{iid}{\rm Unif}([n])
. For any
c_{1}>0
, if
t\leq\sqrt{2c_{1}n}
, then

\displaystyle\Pr[\exists i,j:i\neq j\text{ and }x_{i}=x_{j}]\leq c_{1}.

Proof.

Let

E\;=\;\{\exists\,i\neq j\text{ such that }x_{i}=x_{j}\}

be the event that there is at least one collision. For each pair

1\leq i<j\leq t
, define the event
E_{i,j}=\{x_{i}=x_{j}\}
.
By a union bound,

\Pr(E)\;\leq\;\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq t}\Pr(E_{i,j}).

Since
x_{i},x_{j}
are independent and uniform on
[n]
,

\Pr(E_{i,j})=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\Pr(x_{i}=a)\cdot\Pr(x_{j}=a)=n\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}=\frac{1}{n}.

Therefore,

\Pr(E)\;\leq\;\binom{t}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\;=\;\frac{t(t-1)}{2n}\;\leq\;\frac{t^{2}}{2n}.

Therefore, if
t\leq\sqrt{2c_{1}n}
, then

\Pr(E)\;\leq\;\frac{t^{2}}{2n}\;\leq\;\frac{2c_{1}n}{2n}\;=\;c_{1},

as desired.

11
A case study on an average case problem: learning noisy parities

In this section, we present a case study on learning noisy parities with Chain-of-Thought under the supervision of multiple generators. Our goal is to illustrate, in a concrete and transparent setting, that learning can remain tractable even when there are several distinct CoTs available. In particular, we show that a simple polynomial-time algorithm (
Algorithm

2
) recovers the hidden support even when the CoTs come from
super-exponentially
many generators. This holds even when the generator is chosen adversarially on each example and its identity is not revealed to the learner (
Theorem

8
).

Moreover, the sample complexity of the algorithm nearly matches the information-theoretic limit of
Theorem

9
under the supervision of these generators sampled uniformly for each example, even in the case when there is no noise in the generation. Interestingly, this information-theoretic limit is genuinely larger than in the single-generator setting (
Remark

11.2
). Finally, we show numerical simulations of transformers trained with the next-token prediction objective on noiseless parities (
Section

11.3
). We observe that, while increasing the number of generators also increases the sample complexity of the algorithm, training nevertheless succeeds after a reasonable number of samples/gradient updates. In contrast, end-to-end learning without any CoT is hard for a large family of algorithms (that is, statistical query algorithms).

CoT for learning parities.

For learning parities, there is a natural CoT that reveals parities on a hierarchy of growing partial subsets, by xoring in one hidden bit at a time. Consider the class of all size-
k:=k(d)
subset parities over
\{0,1\}^{d}
. For a fixed hidden support
S_{\star}
, a single generator produces a CoT by successively incorporating coordinates of
S_{\star}
one by one, thereby revealing parities of nested subsets. Different generators correspond to different orderings of these coordinates, yielding up to
k!
possible generators. This is formalized below.

Generators.

Fix parameters
(d,k)
. Let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
.
Fix a subset
S\subseteq[d]
with
|S|=k
, and let

\Bij([k],S):=\{\pi:[k]\to S,\,\mathrm{s.t.,}\,\pi\text{ is a bijection}\}

be a set of bijection (an ordering of the elements of
S
). For each
\pi\in\Bij([k],S)
, we have a generator
f_{S,\pi}
, so the total number of generators are given by
|\Bij([k],S)|=k!\,\,

The generator
f_{S,\pi}
produces a length-
k
chain-of-thought as follows:

z_{0}:=0,\qquad z_{t}:=z_{t-1}\oplus x_{\pi(t)},\quad t=1,\dots,k\,.

Note that the final token computes the parity of the subset
S
:

y:=z_{k}=\bigoplus_{j\in S}x_{j}.

Note that all generators produce the same final output, though their CoTs may differ. In this sense, the setting is similar to the general framework introduced in
Sections

1.1
and
1.2
. However, to make end-to-end learning from only the final outputs
(x,y)
nontrivial, we must introduce noise; otherwise, the target parity can be recovered efficiently from end-to-end labels alone by solving a system of linear equations over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Our interest is instead in a regime where learning from the final output alone is computationally hard, and where access to the CoT trace enables tractable learning.

To this end, we consider a noisy generation model in which, at each step of the CoT, the generated bit is flipped with some probability. Because the noise is introduced during generation itself, any error may propagate to subsequent steps. We observe samples generated according to this noisy process, potentially from different generators. This noise model has been studied experimentally before for a combination of next-token prediction and reinforcement learning algorithms applied to transformers
[
50
]
.

Formally, for a noise level
\eta\in(0,1/2)
, we have the following observation model.

Propagating Noise during Generation.

Let
(\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k})\sim_{iid}\Bern(\eta)
.
The noisy chain is generated recursively as

\tilde{z}_{0}:=\xi_{0},\qquad\tilde{z}_{t}:=(\tilde{z}_{t-1}\oplus x_{\pi(t)})\oplus\xi_{t},\quad t=1,\dots,k\,.

Equivalently, the noisy chain satisfies

\tilde{z}_{t}=z_{t}\;\oplus\;\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\xi_{i}\,.

The last token
\tilde{y}:=\tilde{z}_{k}
satisfies

\eta_{\text{eff}}=\Pr[\tilde{y}\neq y]=\Pr\left(\bigoplus_{t=1}^{k}\xi_{t}=1\right)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-(1-2\eta)^{k}\bigr)\,,

where the last equality can be verified inductively.

Remark 11.1

(Noise Accumulation)
.

Let
\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}:=\Pr[\tilde{y}\neq y]
denote the effective noise at the final output. First, even when
\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}
is a sufficiently small absolute constant, learning from end-to-end examples alone is still believed to be hard when
k(d)=\Omega(d)
: for parity learning with noise, the best known algorithm runs in time
2^{O(d/\log d)}

[
8
]
. Thus, hardness already persists at constant effective noise.

Second,
\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}
may be much larger than the per-step generation noise
\eta
, since errors accumulate over the
k
steps of the CoT. Concretely, if
\eta\ll 1/k
, then
\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}=\Theta(k\eta)
; if
\eta=1/(2k)
, then
\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}\to\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-1})
; and if
\eta\gg 1/k
, then
\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}\to\frac{1}{2}
. In particular, when
\eta=1/4
and
k=d/2
, we have
\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}=\Pr[\tilde{y}\neq y]=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{d/2+1}}
, which is exponentially close to
1/2
. Therefore, even very small per-step noise can induce substantial effective noise at the final output, rendering end-to-end learning difficult.

Learning Problem.

For parameters
(d,k)
, the adversary chooses an unknown support
S_{\star}\subseteq[d]
with
|S_{\star}|=k
. We observe
m
samples generated as follows. First,

(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)})\sim_{iid}{\rm Unif}(\{0,1\}^{d}).

Then, for each example
x^{(i)}
, the adversary chooses a generator
f_{S_{\star},\pi^{(i)}}
from the collection
\{f_{S_{\star},\pi}:\pi\in\Bij([k],S_{\star})\}
of
k!
possible generators, corresponding to different orders on the support coordinates. The choice of generator may depend adversarially on the entire input sequence
(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)})
.

Under the noisy generation model with noise level
\eta
, the learner receives CoTs
(\tilde{z}^{(1)},\dots,\tilde{z}^{(m)})
generated from
(f_{S_{\star},\pi^{(1)}},\dots,f_{S_{\star},\pi^{(m)}})
. Thus, the observed dataset is

((x^{(1)},\tilde{z}^{(1)}),\dots,(x^{(m)},\tilde{z}^{(m)})),

and the goal of the learner is to recover
S_{\star}
. By Stirling’s approximation,
k(d)!=2^{\Theta(k(d)\log k(d))}
. Hence, whenever
k(d)=\omega(d/\log d)
, the number of possible orders/CoTs satisfies
k(d)!=2^{\omega(d)}
, and is therefore super-exponential in
d
.

11.1
Recovery Algorithm from Multiple Generators

We now present an algorithm (
Algorithm

2
) that recovers the support tractably, even when the Chain-of-Thoughts are generated adversarially among the
k!
possible generators. The algorithm forms, for each example, a scalar statistic from the adjacent differences of the observed CoT and then computes coordinate-wise correlations between that statistic and the input bits across the dataset to infer the
k
indices of the hidden support.

Algorithm 2

Parity Support Recovery Algorithm under Mixture of CoT generators.

Input:
Samples
\{(x^{(i)},\tilde{z}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{m}
,
where
x^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
and

\tilde{z}^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
; assume
|S|=k
.

Output:
Estimated support
\widehat{S}\subseteq[d]
.

•

For each
i\in[m]
and
t\in[k]
, define increments (with
\tilde{z}^{(i)}_{0}=0
) by

\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{t}\;:=\;1-2\cdot\bigl(\tilde{z}^{(i)}_{t}\oplus\tilde{z}^{(i)}_{t-1}\bigr),\qquad\text{and set}\qquad\tilde{s}^{(i)}:=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{t}.

•

For each coordinate
j\in[d]
, compute

\mathrm{score}(j)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-2x^{(i)}_{j}\right)\,\tilde{s}^{(i)}.

•

Output
\widehat{S}
as the
k
indices with largest
\mathrm{score}(j)
.

We show the following guarantee

Theorem 8

(Guarantee under Propagating Noise)
.

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\leq d
. Let
S_{\star}\subseteq[d]
be unknown with
|S_{\star}|=k
.
Assume the samples
\{(x^{(i)},\tilde{z}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{m}
are generated under the propagating noise during generation with the noise level
\eta\in[0,1/2)
. The generators
(\pi^{(1)},\dots,\pi^{(m)})
can be chosen even adversarially based on
(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)})
. Then the output of
\widehat{S}
of
Algorithm

2
satisfies the following guarantee. There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for every
\delta\in(0,1)
,
if

m\;\geq\;C\cdot\frac{k}{(1-2\eta)^{2}}\,\log\!\Big(\frac{d}{\delta}\Big),\quad\mathrm{then}\quad\Pr\!\big[\widehat{S}=S_{\star}\big]\;\geq\;1-\delta\,.

Proof of
Theorem

8
.

Under our noise model:

\tilde{z}^{(i)}_{t}\oplus\tilde{z}^{(i)}_{t-1}=x_{\pi^{(i)}(t)}^{(i)}\oplus\xi^{(i)}_{t}\,.

Therefore,

\tilde{\delta}_{t}^{(i)}=1-2\left(\tilde{z}^{(i)}_{t}\oplus\tilde{z}^{(i)}_{t-1}\right)=\left(1-2x_{\pi^{(i)}(t)}^{(i)}\right)\left(1-2\xi_{t}^{(i)}\right)=\left(1-2x_{\pi^{(i)}(t)}^{(i)}\right)\gamma_{t}^{(i)}\,,

where we defined

\gamma^{(i)}_{t}=\left(1-2\xi_{t}^{(i)}\right)\in\{\pm 1\}
for which
\Pr[\gamma_{t}^{(i)}=+1]=1-\eta\,.
Therefore, the statistic can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{s}^{(i)}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left(1-2x_{\pi^{(i)}(t)}^{(i)}\right)\cdot\gamma^{(i)}_{t}\,=\sum_{\ell\in S_{\star}}\left(1-2x_{\ell}^{(i)}\right)\cdot\gamma^{(i)}_{\pi^{(i)}_{-1}(\ell)}\,.

We claim that the statistics
\tilde{s}^{(1)},\dots,\tilde{s}^{(m)}
are independent, even though the permutations
\pi^{(1)},\dots,\pi^{(m)}
may be chosen adversarially after observing the entire input sequence
(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)})
. Indeed, conditional on the inputs
(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)})
and the adversarially chosen permutations
(\pi^{(1)},\dots,\pi^{(m)})
, the random variables
\tilde{s}^{(i)}
depend only on the noise vectors
\gamma_{1:k}^{(i)}
. Since the coordinates
\gamma_{1}^{(i)},\dots,\gamma_{k}^{(i)}
are i.i.d., permuting them does not change their joint distribution; hence for each fixed
i
, the conditional law of
\tilde{s}^{(i)}
depends only on
x^{(i)}
and not on the particular order
\pi^{(i)}
. Because the noise vectors are independent across
i
, it follows that
\tilde{s}^{(1)},\dots,\tilde{s}^{(m)}
are independent.

For any
j\in[d]
, we define:

\displaystyle Z^{(i)}_{j}:=\left(1-2x^{(i)}_{j}\right)\tilde{s}^{(i)}\,.

We have
\{Z_{j}^{(i)};i\in[m]\}
are also independent. The rest of the proof follows by applying Bernstein’s lemma with mean and variance calculations.

Mean:

Recall that
\mathbb{E}[\gamma^{(i)}_{t}]=(1-\eta)-\eta=1-2\eta
. And
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-2x_{j}^{(i)}\right)\left(1-2x_{\ell}^{(i)}\right)\right]=\mathbf{1}_{j=\ell}
. Using this:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z^{(i)}_{j}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(1-2x^{(i)}_{j}\right)\tilde{s}^{(i)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(1-2x^{(i)}_{j}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left(1-2x^{(i)}_{\pi^{(i)}(t)}\right)\,\gamma^{(i)}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(1-2x^{(i)}_{j}\right)\left(1-2x^{(i)}_{\pi^{(i)}(t)}\right)\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[\gamma^{(i)}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}_{j=\pi^{(i)}(t)}\cdot(1-2\eta)\,

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{j\in S_{\star}}\cdot(1-2\eta)=:\mu\mathbf{1}_{j\in S_{\star}}\,.

(37)

Variance:

Note that
(1-2x^{(i)}_{j})^{2}=1
and that
\tilde{s}^{(i)}
, and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Z^{(i)}_{j})^{2}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\tilde{s}^{(i)})^{2}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k}\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{t}\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{t})^{2}\bigr]\;+\;2\sum_{1\leq t<u\leq k}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{t}\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{u}\bigr].

Its is easy to verify the diagonal terms satisfy
(\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{t})^{2}=1
, so
\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{t})^{2}]=k
, and for the cross terms
\pi^{(i)}(t)\neq\pi^{(i)}(u)
, and therefore

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{t}\tilde{\delta}^{(i)}_{u}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(1-2x^{(i)}_{\pi^{(i)}(t)}\right)\left(1-2x^{(i)}_{\pi^{(i)}(u)}\right)\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[\gamma^{(i)}_{t}\gamma^{(i)}_{u}\right]=0,

Thus,

\text{Var}(Z^{(i)}_{j})\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Z^{(i)}_{j})^{2}\bigr]=k.

Applying Bernstein.
We now apply Bernstein’s lemma (
Lemma

A.1
). Note that for any fixed
j\in[d]
, we have
|Z^{(i)}_{j}-\mathbb{E}[Z^{(i)}_{j}]|\leq 2k
almost surely. The value of
\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\text{Var}[Z^{(i)}_{j}]\leq k
. Applying
Lemma

A.1
:

For any
j\in[d]
, we have for any
\epsilon=\mu/4
yields,

\Pr\!\left(\left|\Score(j)-\mathbb{E}[Z^{(i)}_{j}]\right|\geq\frac{\mu}{4}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-c\frac{m\mu^{2}}{k}\right)

for a universal constant
c>0
. Doing a union bound over
j\in[d]
: with probability at least

\Pr\left(\forall j\in[d],\,|\Score(j)-\mathbb{E}[Z^{(i)}_{j}]|\leq\frac{\mu}{4}\right)\geq 1-2d\exp\!\left(-c\frac{m\mu^{2}}{k}\right)

Under this event, we have simultaneously:

\Score(j)\geq\frac{3\mu}{4}\quad\forall j\in S_{\star},\qquad\Score(j)\leq\frac{\mu}{4}\quad\forall j\notin S_{\star}.

Thus, the top-
k
rule of
Algorithm

2
returns
\widehat{S}=S_{\star}
with probability at least
1-\delta
, as long as

2d\exp\!\left(-c\frac{m\mu^{2}}{k}\right)\leq\delta\,,\quad\text{ which is implied by }\quad\quad m\geq c\cdot\frac{k}{\mu^{2}}\log\!\Big(\frac{d}{\delta}\Big),

for some
C>0
. Noting
\mu=(1-2\eta)
completes the proof.

11.2
Information Theoretic Limit of Learning under Uniform Sampling

We now show that the
\Omega(k\log d)
bound in the theorem above is also nearly necessary information-theoretically, even in the more favorable case where each generator is chosen uniformly from
\Bij([k],S_{\star})
. By contrast, for any fixed single generator,
O(\log(d/\delta))
samples suffice under constant per-step noise
\eta
(cf.
Remark

11.2
).

We consider the noiseless case
\eta=0
, in which the learner observes CoTs of the form
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
that reveal the partial subset parities exactly. The proof uses Fano’s inequality.

Theorem 9

(Information-theoretic limit from a random CoT generator)
.

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq k\leq d-1
. Let
S_{\star}
be drawn uniformly from

\binom{d}{k}
. Given
S_{\star}
, draw
m
i.i.d. samples
(x^{(i)},z_{i}^{(i)})_{i=1}^{m}
, where
z^{(i)}
is a CoT generated by a random generator
\pi^{(i)}\sim_{iid}\Bij(S_{\star},[k])
.

Then for any (possibly randomized) estimator
\widehat{S}=\widehat{S}\big((x^{(i)},z^{(i)})_{i=1}^{m}\big)
, achieving
\Pr[\widehat{S}=S_{\star}]\geq 1-\delta
requires

m\;\geq\;\frac{(1-\delta)\log\binom{d}{k}-1}{\log(k+1)}.

(where the probability is over
S_{\star}
and samples). Moreover, for
k\leq d/2
, using
\log\binom{d}{k}\geq k\log(d/k)
, the bounds becomes

m\;\geq\;\frac{(1-\delta)\,k\log(d/k)-1}{\log(k+1)}.

Proof.

Let
M:=\binom{d}{k}
and let
S_{\star}
be uniform over the
M
possible supports.
Let the full dataset be

D:=\big((x^{(1)},z^{(1)}),\ldots,(x^{(m)},z^{(m)})\big),

where
x^{(i)}=(x^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(i)}_{d})
and
z^{(i)}=(z^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,z^{(i)}_{k})
.

The sum is a sufficient statistic.

For each sample
i\in[m]
, define

U^{(i)}\;:=\;\big|\{j\in S_{\star}:x^{(i)}_{j}=1\}\big|\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}.

We claim that, conditional on
x^{(i)}
, the conditional law of
z^{(i)}
depends on
S_{\star}

only through
U^{(i)}
, i.e., for every measurable set
A\subseteq\mathcal{Z}^{k}
,

\Pr\!\big(z^{(i)}\in A\mid x^{(i)},S_{\star}\big)\;=\;\Pr\!\big(z^{(i)}\in A\mid x^{(i)},U^{(i)}\big).

(38)

Equivalently,
S_{\star}\perp z^{(i)}\mid(x^{(i)},U^{(i)})
. Indeed, conditioned on
x^{(i)}
, the multiset
\{x^{(i)}_{j}:j\in S_{\star}\}
contains exactly
U^{(i)}

many
1
’s and
k-U^{(i)}
many
0
’s. Since
\pi^{(i)}
is uniform over permutations of
S_{\star}
, the
increment sequence

\big(x^{(i)}_{\pi^{(i)}(1)},\ldots,x^{(i)}_{\pi^{(i)}(k)}\big)

is a uniformly random ordering of this multiset, and therefore its conditional distribution given
x^{(i)}

depends on
S_{\star}
only through
U^{(i)}
. The sequence

\big(z^{(i)}_{1},\dots,z^{(i)}_{k}\big)_{t=1}^{k}

is a deterministic function of these increments,
hence (
38
) holds.

Step 2 (mutual information upper bound).

By (
38
) and the data-processing inequality, conditioning on the entire input sequence

x_{1:m}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
,

\displaystyle I(S_{\star};D\mid x^{(1:m)})\;

\displaystyle\leq\;I(S_{\star};U^{(1:m)}\mid x^{(1:m)}),\qquad U^{(1:m)}:=(U^{(1)},\ldots,U^{(m)}).

\displaystyle\leq H(U^{(1:m)}\mid x^{(1:m)})\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}H(U^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})\;\leq\;m\log(k+1),

(39)

since each

Applying Fano.

Let

be any estimator and let

Fano’s inequality in its standard form gives

(41)

) and (

We conclude by observing that, under a single-generator supervision, one can still recover the parity from only

samples whenever the per-step noise level

is constant.

Remark 11.2

(Learning with
O(\log(d/\delta))
samples from a single generator)
.

Fix any single generator, corresponding to an ordering
(j_{1}^{\star},\dots,j_{k}^{\star})
of the support
S_{\star}=\{j_{1}^{\star},\dots,j_{k}^{\star}\}
. Under the noisy generation model, the increments

\tilde{\delta}_{t}^{(i)}:=1-2(\tilde{z}_{t}^{(i)}\oplus\tilde{z}_{t-1}^{(i)})

satisfy

\tilde{\delta}_{t}^{(i)}=(1-2x_{j_{t}^{\star}}^{(i)})\gamma_{t}^{(i)},

where
\gamma_{t}^{(i)}\in\{\pm 1\}
is independent of
x^{(i)}
and has mean
\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{t}^{(i)}]=1-2\eta
. Thus, for every fixed
t\in[k]
, the variable
\tilde{\delta}_{t}^{(i)}
is simply a noisy copy of the bit
x_{j_{t}^{\star}}^{(i)}
in
\{\pm 1\}
notation.

It follows that for each
j\in[d]
, the correlation statistic

\Score_{t}(j):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(1-2x_{j}^{(i)})\tilde{\delta}_{t}^{(i)}

has expectation

\mathbb{E}[\Score_{t}(j)]=\begin{cases}1-2\eta,&j=j_{t}^{\star},\\
0,&j\neq j_{t}^{\star}.\end{cases}

Hence the correct coordinate
j_{t}^{\star}
has a constant correlation gap over all incorrect coordinates, as long as
\eta<1/2
. Since each summand is bounded in
[-1,1]
, a standard Hoeffding or Bernstein bound implies that for any fixed
t\in[k]
, with probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}
, one can identify
j_{t}^{\star}
correctly from
m=O(\log(d/\delta^{\prime}))
samples by selecting the coordinate with the largest empirical correlation.

Finally, since
k(d)\leq d
, we may recover all coordinates
(j_{1}^{\star},\dots,j_{k}^{\star})
simultaneously by a union bound over the
k
positions. Taking
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/d
therefore yields that

m=O(\log(d/\delta))

samples from any fixed single generator suffice to recover the entire support
S_{\star}=\{j_{1}^{\star},\dots,j_{k}^{\star}\}
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Thus, the
information-theoretic
limit indeed shifts when the learner receives CoTs from a uniform mixture of generators (cf.
Theorem

9
).

Let

, and let

19

19

In both

Private key.

A vector

Public key.

A sequence of pairs

(a_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(a_{m},b_{m}),

To encrypt the bit

Given a ciphertext

Correctness.

The probability of decryption error, over the choice of keys and encryption randomness, is

2^{-\Omega(\gamma(n)^{2}/m)}=2^{-\Omega(n\log n)}.
