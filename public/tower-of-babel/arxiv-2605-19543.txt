The quantum homomorphism orders are universal
Yangjing Long

Abstract
. Mančinska and Roberson introduced quantum graph homomorphisms as the existence of perfect quantum strategies for graph homomorphism games. The resulting relation is a quasi-order on finite graphs, and hence gives a partial order after quotienting by quantum homomorphic equivalence. We prove that the quantum homomorphism orders of both finite directed graphs and finite undirected graphs are universal: every countable partial order embeds into them. For directed graphs, the proof uses the classical universality of the homomorphism order on finite disjoint unions of clockwise directed cycles, together with the fact that quantum homomorphisms between such directed cycles coincide with classical homomorphisms. For undirected graphs we construct an explicit ordered undirected indicator whose terminal vertices are quantum endpoint-forcing. Replacing each directed edge by this indicator embeds the directed-cycle order into the quantum homomorphism order of finite undirected graphs.



1.
Introduction

For finite graphs
X
and
Y
, a homomorphism
X\to Y
is an adjacency-preserving map from
V(X)
to
V(Y)
. The existence of a homomorphism is a reflexive and transitive relation, and hence induces a partial order after quotienting by homomorphic equivalence. This order is very rich: it is universal, in the sense that every countable partial order occurs as a suborder. We use this term in the same order-theoretic sense throughout the paper.

The universality of graph homomorphism orders is a classical theme. Hedrlín proved that the homomorphism order of finite graphs is universal
[
10
]
; see also the categorical representation theory of Pultr and Trnková
[
27
]
and the monograph of Hell and Nešetřil
[
11
]
. Later work showed that universality persists in sparse or restricted classes. Hubička and Nešetřil proved universality for finite oriented paths and for oriented trees and related simple graph classes
[
13
,
14
]
. Fiala, Hubička and the author gave a divisibility-based universality argument using oriented cycles
[
8
]
, and the stronger fractal property of the homomorphism order was subsequently treated by Fiala, Hubička, Long and Nešetřil
[
7
]
. Luchnikov, Wittebol and Zuiddam recently proved an analogous universality theorem for the asymptotic cohomomorphism order arising in the asymptotic spectrum of graphs
[
18
]
.

Other homomorphism-type orders provide useful comparisons. For example, Naserasr, Sen and Sopena studied the homomorphism order of signed graphs and proved lattice properties of that order
[
24
]
; see also the update on signed-graph homomorphisms by Naserasr, Sopena and Zaslavsky
[
25
]
. These signed-graph results are classical rather than quantum, but they illustrate how graph-homomorphism orders change when the underlying category of graphs is modified.

Quantum homomorphisms were introduced by Mančinska and Roberson
[
22
]
as the existence of perfect quantum strategies for the graph homomorphism game. Their work placed quantum coloring, quantum independence and quantum clique numbers in a common homomorphism framework, proved several structural properties, and observed that the quantum homomorphism order is a lattice and a poset homomorphic image of the classical homomorphism order. Roberson’s thesis further developed the order-theoretic viewpoint, including a description of the quantum homomorphism order via measurement graphs
[
29
]
. This naturally leads to the question raised in
[
22
]
: which order-theoretic properties of the classical homomorphism order survive under the quantum relaxation?

The paper
[
22
]
has since become a basic reference for several related directions. On the coloring side, Mančinska and Roberson exhibited unexpected phenomena in quantum colorings, including small separations between classical and quantum chromatic number
[
21
]
. Mančinska, Roberson and Varvitsiotis studied the decidability of perfect entangled strategies for nonlocal games and showed that independent-set games are representative of difficult instances
[
20
]
. Ortiz and Paulsen studied quantum graph homomorphisms through operator systems and associated universal
C^{*}
-algebras, and introduced an operator-system notion of quantum core
[
26
]
. Roberson gave conic formulations of graph homomorphism relations, including quantum and related relaxations
[
30
]
. Helton, Meyer, Paulsen and Satriano developed the algebraic approach to synchronous games and graph coloring games
[
12
]
. Atserias, Mančinska, Roberson, Šámal, Severini and Varvitsiotis introduced quantum and non-signalling graph isomorphism games, linking graph isomorphism, nonlocal games and noncommutative feasibility problems
[
1
]
. Lupini, Mančinska and Roberson connected these games to quantum permutation groups and quantum automorphism groups
[
19
]
. Mančinska and Roberson later proved that quantum isomorphism is equivalent to equality of homomorphism counts from all planar graphs
[
23
]
, showing that homomorphism-counting techniques also capture quantum symmetries.

There has also been renewed activity in the last few years around homomorphism games, homomorphism indistinguishability, and quantum relaxations of constraint satisfaction. Brannan, Ganesan and Harris introduced the quantum-to-classical graph homomorphism game
[
3
]
. Harris proved that graph homomorphism games are universal for synchronous games and used this to study the quantum coloring problem
[
9
]
. Ciardo related quantum advantage in homomorphism-type CSP tasks to the polymorphism-minion framework from algebraic CSP complexity
[
6
]
, and studied the possible size of the gap between classical and quantum chromatic number, including a quantum adjunction theorem for Pultr functors
[
5
]
. Banakh, Ciardo, Kozik and Tułowiecki investigated classical simulation of quantum CSP strategies
[
2
]
, while Ciardo, Joubert and Mottet introduced quantum polymorphisms and commutativity gadgets for quantum CSPs
[
4
]
. Karamlou studied quantum relaxations of CSP and structure isomorphism using the quantum monad viewpoint
[
16
]
. Kornell and Lindenhovius recently constructed quantum graphs of homomorphisms in a closed symmetric monoidal category of quantum graphs; their construction is nonempty precisely when the corresponding homomorphism game has a perfect quantum strategy
[
17
]
. Homomorphism-counting viewpoints have also led to semidefinite-programming characterizations: Roberson and Seppelt related the Lasserre hierarchy for graph isomorphism to homomorphism indistinguishability
[
28
]
, and Kar, Roberson, Seppelt and Zeman gave the analogous NPA hierarchy characterization for quantum isomorphism
[
15
]
. These works use notions of quantum homomorphism in algebraic, categorical, computational, and nonlocal-game settings. Our use of the word “universal” is different from the universality of homomorphism games in
[
9
]
: here universality means embedding every countable partial order into the quantum homomorphism order.

Our main result is that the quantum homomorphism order retains this fundamental richness of the classical order.

Theorem 1.1
.

The quantum homomorphism order of finite directed graphs is universal. The quantum homomorphism order of finite undirected graphs is universal.

The directed part is conceptually simple. If
\overrightarrow{C}_{n}
denotes the clockwise directed cycle of length
n
, then

\overrightarrow{C}_{n}\to_{q}\overrightarrow{C}_{m}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\overrightarrow{C}_{n}\to\overrightarrow{C}_{m}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m\mid n.

It follows that the quantum order on finite disjoint unions of directed cycles agrees with the classical order. Since the latter order is universal
[
8
]
, the directed quantum homomorphism order is universal.

The undirected part is the main point of the paper. Classical indicator and arrow constructions do not automatically transfer to the quantum setting: quantum homomorphisms are given by projections rather than set-valued maps, and support-image intuition can be misleading. To avoid this issue, we construct an ordered undirected indicator
J=J(a,b)
with a quantum endpoint-forcing property. For every irreflexive directed graph
H
and every quantum homomorphism

F:J\to_{q}H*J,

where
H*J
is obtained by replacing every arc
u\to v
of
H
by a copy of
J
with
a
identified with
u
and
b
identified with
v
, one has

F_{a,p}F_{b,q}=0

unless
p,q
are the ordered terminal vertices of a single copy of
J
in
H*J
.

The construction of
J
is finite and explicit. Its spine has color sequence

E,A,A,B,B,A,E,

where the “colors”
A,B,E
are implemented by ordinary graph gadgets rather than by additional structure. The key lemma is a quantum color-localization lemma showing that the projections corresponding to an
A
-root, a
B
-root, or an
E
-root vanish on target vertices not carrying the same gadget. Thus a nonzero summand in the expansion of
F_{a,p}F_{b,q}
forces a color-preserving walk of type
E,A,A,B,B,A,E
in
H*J
, and the combinatorics of the spine implies that such a walk is necessarily the ordered spine of a single copy.

With this indicator in hand, the universality of the undirected order follows by embedding the classical order on finite disjoint unions of directed cycles into the quantum order of finite undirected graphs.

2.
Quantum homomorphisms

All graphs and digraphs in this paper are finite and loopless. Undirected graphs are simple. Directed graphs have no loops; parallel arcs will not be needed. All Hilbert spaces appearing in quantum homomorphisms are finite-dimensional and nonzero.

2.1.
Definitions

Let
X
and
Y
be finite undirected graphs. A
quantum homomorphism
from
X
to
Y
is a family of orthogonal projections

\{E_{x,y}:x\in V(X),\ y\in V(Y)\}

on a nonzero finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in V(Y)}E_{x,y}

\displaystyle=I

\displaystyle\text{for every }x\in V(X),

(2)

\displaystyle E_{x,y}E_{x,y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{whenever }y\neq y^{\prime},

(3)

\displaystyle E_{x,y}E_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{whenever }xx^{\prime}\in E(X)\text{ and }yy^{\prime}\notin E(Y).

We write
X\to_{q}Y
when such a family exists. This is the projective-measurement formulation of quantum graph homomorphisms from
[
22
]
. Since
E_{x,y}
and
E_{x,y^{\prime}}
are orthogonal projections for
y\neq y^{\prime}
and their sum is the identity, each set
\{E_{x,y}:y\in V(Y)\}
is a projective measurement.

For directed graphs we use the same definition, replacing the edge condition (
3
) by

E_{x,y}E_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}=0\quad\text{whenever }(x,x^{\prime})\in A(X)\text{ and }(y,y^{\prime})\notin A(Y).

The relation
\to_{q}
is reflexive and transitive, so it induces a quasi-order; the corresponding partial order is obtained by quotienting by the equivalence relation generated by
X\to_{q}Y
and
Y\to_{q}X
.

2.2.
The walk lemma

The following elementary lemma is the main tool used throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.1

(Walk lemma)
.

Let
X\to_{q}Y
be a quantum homomorphism, represented by projections
E_{x,y}
. Suppose there is a directed walk of length
\ell
from
x
to
x^{\prime}
in
X
, but no directed walk of length
\ell
from
y
to
y^{\prime}
in
Y
. Then

E_{x,y}E_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}=0.

The same statement holds for undirected graphs, with walks interpreted in the undirected sense.

Proof.

For
\ell=0
the claim is exactly (
2
); for
\ell=1
it is (
3
). Suppose the statement holds for walks of length
\ell
. Let

x=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell},x_{\ell+1}=x^{\prime}

be a walk of length
\ell+1
in
X
. Insert the resolution of the identity at
x_{\ell}
:

E_{x,y}E_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}=\sum_{z\in V(Y)}E_{x,y}E_{x_{\ell},z}E_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}.

If there is no walk of length
\ell
from
y
to
z
, then the induction hypothesis gives
E_{x,y}E_{x_{\ell},z}=0
. If there is such a walk from
y
to
z
, then, since there is no walk of length
\ell+1
from
y
to
y^{\prime}
, there is no edge from
z
to
y^{\prime}
; hence
E_{x_{\ell},z}E_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}=0
by the edge-zero condition. Thus every summand is zero.
∎

3.
Directed cycles

Let
\overrightarrow{C}_{n}
denote the clockwise directed cycle on
n
vertices, with arcs
i\to i+1\pmod{n}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
m,n\geq 2
,

\overrightarrow{C}_{n}\to_{q}\overrightarrow{C}_{m}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\overrightarrow{C}_{n}\to\overrightarrow{C}_{m}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m\mid n.

Proof.

If
m\mid n
, the map
i\mapsto i\pmod{m}
is a classical homomorphism
\overrightarrow{C}_{n}\to\overrightarrow{C}_{m}
, and hence gives a quantum homomorphism.

Conversely, suppose
\overrightarrow{C}_{n}\to_{q}\overrightarrow{C}_{m}
, represented by projections
E_{u,v}
. Fix
u\in V(\overrightarrow{C}_{n})
. Since
\sum_{v}E_{u,v}=I
, there exists
v
with
E_{u,v}\neq 0
. There is a directed closed walk of length
n
from
u
to
u
in
\overrightarrow{C}_{n}
. If there were no directed closed walk of length
n
from
v
to
v
in
\overrightarrow{C}_{m}
, Lemma
2.1
would give
E_{u,v}^{2}=0
, a contradiction. A directed closed walk of length
n
exists in
\overrightarrow{C}_{m}
if and only if
m\mid n
.
∎

We also need the standard reduction for disjoint unions.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X
be a weakly connected directed graph, and let
Y=Y_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup Y_{t}
be a disjoint union of directed graphs. If
X\to_{q}Y
, then
X\to_{q}Y_{i}
for some
i
.

Proof.

Let
E_{x,y}
represent a quantum homomorphism
X\to_{q}Y
. For
x\in V(X)
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
, set

P_{i}^{x}=\sum_{y\in V(Y_{i})}E_{x,y}.

For fixed
x
, the projections
P_{i}^{x}
are pairwise orthogonal and sum to
I
. If
xx^{\prime}
is an arc of
X
, then there are no arcs between distinct components of
Y
, and hence
P_{i}^{x}P_{j}^{x^{\prime}}=0
for
i\neq j
. Therefore

P_{i}^{x}=P_{i}^{x}\sum_{j}P_{j}^{x^{\prime}}=P_{i}^{x}P_{i}^{x^{\prime}}\quad\text{and}\quad P_{i}^{x^{\prime}}=\sum_{j}P_{j}^{x}P_{i}^{x^{\prime}}=P_{i}^{x}P_{i}^{x^{\prime}},

so
P_{i}^{x}=P_{i}^{x^{\prime}}
. Since
X
is weakly connected,
P_{i}^{x}
is independent of
x
; write it as
P_{i}
.

Because
\sum_{i}P_{i}=I
, some
P_{i}
is nonzero. Restricting the projections
E_{x,y}
,
y\in V(Y_{i})
, to the nonzero space
\operatorname{Ran}(P_{i})
gives a quantum homomorphism
X\to_{q}Y_{i}
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
D
and
D^{\prime}
be finite disjoint unions of clockwise directed cycles. Then

D\to_{q}D^{\prime}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\to D^{\prime}.

Proof.

Let
D=\bigsqcup_{i}\overrightarrow{C}_{n_{i}}
and
D^{\prime}=\bigsqcup_{j}\overrightarrow{C}_{m_{j}}
. If
D\to_{q}D^{\prime}
, then by Lemma
3.2
, each component
\overrightarrow{C}_{n_{i}}
quantumly maps to some
\overrightarrow{C}_{m_{j}}
. Proposition
3.1
implies
m_{j}\mid n_{i}
, hence
\overrightarrow{C}_{n_{i}}\to\overrightarrow{C}_{m_{j}}
. Thus
D\to D^{\prime}
. The converse is immediate, since every classical homomorphism is a quantum homomorphism.
∎

Let
\mathsf{DiCycles}
denote the class of finite disjoint unions of clockwise directed cycles. The classical homomorphism order on
\mathsf{DiCycles}
is universal
[
8
]
. Therefore:

Theorem 3.4
.

The quantum homomorphism order of finite directed graphs is universal.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.3
, the quantum order and the classical order agree on
\mathsf{DiCycles}
. Since the classical order on
\mathsf{DiCycles}
is universal, the quantum homomorphism order of finite directed graphs contains a universal suborder.
∎

4.
A quantum endpoint-forcing indicator

Let
J
be an undirected graph with two distinguished vertices
a,b
. We regard
a,b
as ordered terminals.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
H
be an irreflexive directed graph. The undirected graph
H*J
is obtained as follows. For every arc

of
H
, take a fresh copy

of

, and identify the terminal

with

has isolated vertices, they remain as isolated vertices in

Definition 4.2
.

An ordered indicator

is
quantum endpoint-forcing
if, for every irreflexive directed graph
H

F:J\to_{q}H*J
satisfies

F_{a,p}F_{b,q}=0

for all pairs
(p,q)
which are not the ordered terminal pair of a copy
J_{uv}
, i.e. for all pairs not of the form
(u,v)
with
u\to v
an arc of
H
.

The rest of this section constructs a finite quantum endpoint-forcing indicator.

4.2.
Quantum localization of colors

Lemma 4.5

(Color localization)
.

Let
X
be a graph containing vertices carrying the color gadgets above, let
Y=H*J
, and let
F:X\to_{q}Y
be a quantum homomorphism. Then:

(i)

If
x
is an
A
-root in
X
and
y\notin\mathsf{A}(Y)
, then
F_{x,y}=0
.

(ii)

If
x
is a
B
-root in
X
and
y\notin\mathsf{B}(Y)
, then
F_{x,y}=0
.

(iii)

If
x
is an
E
-root in
X
and
y\notin\mathsf{E}(Y)
, then
F_{x,y}=0
.

Proof.

We prove the three claims in order.

Proof of (i).

Let
x
be an
A
-root and suppose
y\notin\mathsf{A}(Y)
. Put
P=F_{x,y}
. The vertex
x
is adjacent to the vertices
c_{0},\ldots,c_{4}
of a private
5
-cycle. By Lemma
4.4
,
Y[N_{Y}(y)]
is bipartite; fix a bipartition

N_{Y}(y)=N_{0}\sqcup N_{1}.

For
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{5}
, define

A_{i}=\sum_{z\in N_{0}}F_{c_{i},z},\qquad B_{i}=\sum_{z\in N_{1}}F_{c_{i},z},\qquad C_{i}=A_{i}+B_{i},\qquad S_{i}=A_{i}-B_{i}.

Because the projections
F_{c_{i},z}
,
z\in V(Y)
, form a projective measurement, the operators
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
are orthogonal projections,
C_{i}
is a projection, and

S_{i}^{2}=C_{i}.

Since
xc_{i}
is an edge of
X
, the edge-zero relation gives

F_{c_{i},z}P=0\qquad(z\notin N_{Y}(y)).

Together with completeness for the measurement at
c_{i}
, this gives

C_{i}P=P\qquad\text{for every }i.

Because
N_{0}
and
N_{1}
are independent sets in
Y[N_{Y}(y)]
, and because
c_{i}c_{i+1}
is an edge of
X
, the edge-zero relation gives

A_{i}A_{i+1}=0,\qquad B_{i}B_{i+1}=0\qquad(i\in\mathbb{Z}_{5}).

Using (2) and (3),

\displaystyle S_{i}S_{i+1}P

\displaystyle=(A_{i}-B_{i})(A_{i+1}-B_{i+1})P

\displaystyle=-(A_{i}B_{i+1}+B_{i}A_{i+1})P

\displaystyle=-C_{i}C_{i+1}P=-P.

The same argument with
i
and
i+1
interchanged gives

S_{i+1}S_{i}P=-P.

Let
v\in\operatorname{Ran}(P)
. Then (1) and (2) imply

\|S_{i}v\|^{2}=\langle S_{i}^{2}v,v\rangle=\langle C_{i}v,v\rangle=\|v\|^{2}.

Equations (4) and (5) imply

(S_{i}+S_{i+1})^{2}v=(S_{i}^{2}+S_{i}S_{i+1}+S_{i+1}S_{i}+S_{i+1}^{2})v=0.

Since
(S_{i}+S_{i+1})^{2}
is positive, it follows that

(S_{i}+S_{i+1})v=0,

so
S_{i+1}v=-S_{i}v
for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{5}
. Going around the odd cycle gives

S_{0}v=-S_{4}v=-S_{0}v,

and hence
S_{0}v=0
. But
\|S_{0}v\|=\|v\|
, so
v=0
. Therefore
\operatorname{Ran}(P)=0
, and
P=0
.

Proof of (ii).

Let
x
be a
B
-root and suppose

4.3.
Endpoint forcing

Lemma 4.6
.

Let

Proof.

Since

in an outgoing copy, or the vertex
x_{5}
in an incoming copy. If

were an incoming

would have no

, since its spine neighbors have colors

and

of an outgoing copy.

In that copy,

5.
Undirected universality

For a finite disjoint union
D
of clockwise directed cycles, define

\Phi(D)=D*J,

where
J
is the endpoint-forcing indicator constructed above. This is an undirected graph.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
D,D^{\prime}
be finite disjoint unions of clockwise directed cycles. Then

\Phi(D)\to_{q}\Phi(D^{\prime})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\to D^{\prime}.

Proof.

If
D\to D^{\prime}
is a classical homomorphism, then every arc
u\to v
of
D
is sent to an arc
f(u)\to f(v)
of
D^{\prime}
. Mapping each copy
J_{uv}
identically onto the copy
J_{f(u)f(v)}
gives a classical homomorphism
\Phi(D)\to\Phi(D^{\prime})
, and hence a quantum homomorphism.

Conversely, suppose
\Phi(D)\to_{q}\Phi(D^{\prime})
, represented by projections
F_{x,y}
. Let
U=V(D)
and
U^{\prime}=V(D^{\prime})
be the connector vertices in
\Phi(D)
and
\Phi(D^{\prime})
. Since every vertex of
D
is the tail of an arc, Proposition
4.7
implies that for every connector
u\in U
and every nonconnector
p\in V(\Phi(D^{\prime}))
,

F_{u,p}=0.

Indeed, choose an arc
u\to w
of
D
and use

F_{u,p}=F_{u,p}\sum_{q}F_{w,q}=\sum_{q}F_{u,p}F_{w,q};

endpoint forcing makes every summand zero when
p
is not a connector.

Thus, for
u\in U
and
v\in U^{\prime}
, set

Q_{u,v}=F_{u,v}.

For each
u\in U
, the projections
Q_{u,v}
sum to
I
. If
u\to w
is an arc of
D
, then endpoint forcing gives

Q_{u,v}Q_{w,z}=0

whenever
v\to z
is not an arc of
D^{\prime}
. Hence the projections
Q_{u,v}
define a quantum homomorphism
D\to_{q}D^{\prime}
. By Corollary
3.3
,
D\to D^{\prime}
.
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

The quantum homomorphism order of finite undirected graphs is universal.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.1
, the map
D\mapsto\Phi(D)
embeds the classical homomorphism order on
\mathsf{DiCycles}
into the quantum homomorphism order of finite undirected graphs. Since the classical order on
\mathsf{DiCycles}
is universal

]
, the undirected quantum homomorphism order is universal.
∎

Combining Theorems
3.4
and
5.2
proves Theorem

.

6.
Concluding remarks

The proof deliberately avoids quantum cores and quantum homomorphism images. The endpoint-forcing indicator is a local gadget whose proof uses only the projection relations in the definition of a quantum homomorphism. This may be useful in other situations where classical indicator or arrow constructions need to be transferred to the quantum homomorphism setting.

The construction also shows that ordered indicators are the natural objects for encoding directed information into undirected quantum homomorphism order. The terminals of the indicator need not be interchangeable; direction is encoded by the asymmetric color sequence on the spine.
