Sex-related neuropsychiatric recovery from maternal perinatal depression and expression of monoamine and amino acid neurotransmitter levels in specific brain regions of adult offspring
Authors: Katarzyna Czarzasta, Wiktor Bogacki-Rychlik, Ilona Joniec-Maciejak, Ewa Machaj, Karol Momot, Agnieszka Segiet-Swiecicka, Elizabeth M. Sajdel-Sulkowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Increasingly, research points to the relationship between monoamine neurotransmitters (serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine) and maternal perinatal depression (MPD). It has been suggested that the impact of MDP may involve alterations in neurotransmitter levels. However, the available data mainly concern male offspring, exclude females, focus on a small set of neurotransmitters, and provide limited information on recovery from MDP in adult offspring. We have previously reported that MPD modeled in rats by chronic mild stress (CMS) during pregnancy and lactation results in sex-dependent neuropsychiatric disorders, such as depression-like and anxiety-related behavior in adolescent but not adult rat offspring and were more pronounced in female than male offspring. The main goal of the present study was to examine the hypothesis that recovery from MDP in adult male and female offspring is associated with the altered expression of monoamine and amino acid neurotransmitters in the hippocampus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum. To examine this hypothesis, brain tissue was collected from adult Sprague Dawley male and female rat offspring from stress-exposed (male, SOM; female, SOF) and control dams (male, COM; female, COF). Our results showed significant changes in neurotransmitter levels, particularly in SOF. We observed decreased levels of homovanillic acid (a dopamine metabolite) and of neuroprotective amino acid neurotransmitters, histidine, arginine, and serine, mainly in the hippocampus and frontal cortex, as well as in cerebellar arginine and serine, in SOF. In contrast, in SOM, we observed reduced serine levels only in the frontal cortex compared with COM. Our results suggest that sex-dependent reductions in brain levels of a key dopamine metabolite and neuroprotective amino acids may contribute to recovery from MDP in adult rat offspring.

Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) with Peripartum Onset, also known as Perinatal Depression, is a term introduced in 2013 in the Fifth Edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-5)1. Perinatal depression, which includes the prenatal and postnatal periods, turned out to be a significant clinical problem affecting approximately 7% to 25% of women worldwide2,3. Recently, the impact of maternal depression during pregnancy (prenatal depression) on the offspring has been identified and linked to the abnormalities in brain development and offspring behavior, including anxiety-related and depression-like behavior4. It has been revealed that one of the main factors playing a role in this process is the overactivity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis in mothers suffering from depression or subjected to chronic stress during pregnancy. The high concentrations of cortisol/corticosterone released by the pregnant woman’s body then pass in excessive amounts across the blood-placenta barrier, damaging the developing fetus, including its nervous system4,5. Simultaneously with hypercortisolism accompanying depression and chronic stress, many researchers report a decrease in the mRNA expression of mineralocorticoid (MR) and glucocorticoid (GR) receptors in the central nervous system6,7. The above data may suggest the use of MR and GR mRNA expression as potential markers of chronic stress and depression. Indeed, in our previous studies, we found no difference in plasma corticosterone concentration between adult offspring of stress-exposed and control dams8.

The change in neuronal metabolism accompanying depression and chronic stress manifests itself as disturbed neurotransmission. Monoaminergic neurotransmission - serotonergic, dopaminergic, and noradrenergic - plays a key role in the stress response, and its dysregulation is part of the pathophysiology of depression and anxiety disorders9. Notably, monoamine neurotransmission and amino acid neurotransmission have been implicated in the treatment of depression10–12. Experimental and clinical interventions have solidified the role of monoaminergic neurotransmission in the pathogenesis of depression and chronic stress and targeted their homeostasis as pharmacological interventions (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor, SSRI; serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, SNRI; tricyclic antidepressants, TCA) and behavioral (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy, CBT), pivotal points in therapeutic processes13,14. Also, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), with its anxiolytic effect (achieved with GABA receptor agonists, e.g., benzodiazepines), and glutamate (e.g., the N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDA) antagonist ketamine) are essential elements of pharmacological protocols for anxiety disorders and depression10,12. In addition to classical neurotransmitters, numerous studies suggest that changes in brain amino acid concentrations, such as arginine and serine, which play important roles in neurotransmission, could significantly modulate behavior15; hence, the term amino acid transmitters will be used throughout our study.

To understand the pathophysiology of maternal perinatal depression and its impact on the offspring, in the previous studies, we employed a rat model of perinatal maternal depression involving the chronic mild stress (CMS) paradigm with repeated restraint, in which depression in female Sprague Dawley rats developed before pregnancy and was maintained throughout pregnancy and lactation, which is more consistent with the clinical picture of perinatal depression8,16. Moreover, we demonstrated that adolescent offspring from stress-exposed dams showed depressive-like behavior and anxiety-related behavior, which were confirmed by the self-grooming test (splash test) and the open field test (OFT). Namely, both male and female offspring from stress-exposed dams showed significantly shorter self-grooming time (female offspring: 46%, male offspring: 63%) and significantly longer latency (female offspring: 15%, male offspring: 87%) compared to adolescent offspring from control dams8. In turn, anxiety-related behavior was demonstrated in adolescent female offspring from stress-exposed dams, which showed a 41% shorter cumulative presence in the center and an increased cumulative duration in the border in the OFT. Interestingly, in adult seven-month-old offspring from stress-exposed dams, symptoms indicating the presence of depressive-like behavior (reducing self-grooming time in adult female offspring at 13.36% and in adult male offspring, 0.66%) and anxiety-related behavior (increasing cumulative duration in center in adult female offspring at 13.97%; and increasing cumulative duration in border in adult female offspring at 5.64%), were no longer observed, suggesting neuropsychiatric recovery. However, the central mechanism involved in the recovery from behavioral disorders due to maternal perinatal depression in adult offspring remains unclear. In particular, there is a lack of clarity about which type of neurotransmission in stress-response brain structures may be involved in this process. Therefore, the present study aims to test the hypothesis that the recovery from MDD with maternal perinatal depression is related to the expression of brain monoamine and amino acid neurotransmitters in the selected brain structures, hippocampus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum in adult offspring.

Methods

Animals

These studies were conducted on brain fragments (hippocampus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum) taken from adult offspring (day 225 postnatally, PD225) of stress-exposed dams and control dams. In total, brains were collected from the following groups of rats: adult male offspring from stress-exposed dams (SOM, n = 8), adult female offspring from the stress-exposed dams (SOF, n = 6), adult male offspring from control dams (COM, n = 6), and adult female offspring from control dams (COF, n = 6).

Rat dams: In order to obtain stress-exposed offspring, 14 Sprague-Dawley (Crl: CD (SD) IGS; Charles River Breeding Laboratories; Germany GmbH) rat dams were subjected to CMS using a repeated restraint protocol described in detail in our previous studies8,17. Briefly, to induce depressive-like behavior, rat dams were placed in clear plastic tubes (21 × 7 cm; Kent Scientific Corporation; Torrington, CT, USA) for 1 h daily for 8 consecutive weeks. While stress-exposed dams underwent CMS with repeated immobilization, control dams were handled daily to habituate them to the experimenter. The development of depressive-like behavior in stress-exposed dams was confirmed by assessment of urinary corticosterone concentrations and the self-grooming time in the splash test. Urinary corticosterone concentrations in stress-exposed dams at the seventh week of the CMS protocol were 87% higher compared with control dams and 94% higher compared with the corticosterone concentration before the onset of CMS in the stress-exposed dams group. Self-grooming time was assessed using a splash test, in which the rat’s sides were sprayed with a 10% sucrose solution, followed by 5-minute recordings of total time spent washing both sides and the head, and latency to the first lick, according to the protocol described previously8,16. During the eighth week of the CMS protocol, self-grooming time increased by 22%, and latency decreased by 53% in stress-exposed dams compared to control dams. Compared with pre-CMS, self-grooming time increased by 172%, and latency decreased by 84% in the stress-exposed dams’ group. After 8 weeks of CMS with repeated restraint, all stressed and control females were mated with Sprague-Dawley males (Clr: CD (SD) IGS; Charles River Breeding Laboratories; Germany GmbH) that were not exposed to the CMS protocol. Parturition occurred around day 21 of gestation, and pups remained with their mothers until PD28.

Offspring: A total of 101 pups of both sexes were born (females, n = 47; males, n = 54). After weaning, pups underwent behavioral testing: the splash test, the OFT, and the social interaction test. Rat dams and their 75 offspring were euthanized on day PD48 to obtain tissue for subsequent biochemical analyses. In addition, 26 offspring at PD48 isolated from each litter (6 males and 6 females from control mothers and 8 males and 6 females from stressed mothers) were selected based on similar body weight (female: 170 g, male: 222 g), and were kept alive until PD225 (7 months of age). Adult offspring were subjected to behavioral tests, as were the offspring at PD48; they were then euthanized at PD225, and brain structures were collected to assess neurotransmitter levels in the hypothalamus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum. It should be noted that the results of behavioral tests conducted in adolescent and adult offspring were described in detail in our previous work8.

Rats were housed in a dedicated laboratory room under monitored conditions: temperature 21–24 °C; humidity 40%−50%; 12-h reversed light-dark cycle. Food and water were available ad libitum. To provide stress-free conditions for offspring from dams exposed to stress from PD48 to PD225, they were housed in the same room as offspring from control dams and handled by a single researcher. The offspring were kept in standard Euro Type IV cages (1354G; Tecniplast S.p.A.; Buguggiate, Italy) with metal, slotted lids that allowed free olfactory and vocal communication. Three same-sex individuals were housed per cage to reduce animal density. All cages included enrichment, such as paper tubes and plastic shelters, and paper nesting material. All cages, bedding, and enrichment items were autoclaved before use. Once a week, the cages and washable enrichment items were cleaned, and the bedding and paper enrichment items were replaced. A stress-free condition was established based on available literature data18–20.

All animal experiments were approved by the 2nd Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments in Warsaw (approval numbers: WAW2/022/2019 and WAW2/090/2019), and were conducted in accordance with the Directive of the European Parliament and Council (Directive 2010/63/EU) and Polish (Dz. U. poz. 266/15.01.2015) legislation.

The authors confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the full ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org/)21.

Brain harvesting from adult rats

Brain tissue utilized in the present experiment was harvested from adult offspring during the previous experiment. The animals were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (1.5 ml/1000 g body wt. i.p., Biowet, Pulawy, Poland) after premedication with Ketamine (75 mg/1000 g body wt. i.p., Vetoquinol, Gorzow Wielkopolski, Poland) and Xylazine (7 mg/1000 g body wt. i.p., Vetoquinol, Gorzow Wielkopolski, Poland).

The brains were removed from the skull and placed on ice. Using the bregma as a reference landmark, the entire frontal cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum were dissected out. A block of right and left frontal cortex extending from the pial surface to the underlying white matter was removed. The cortex in the right and left hemispheres of the brain was then exposed to visualize and sample the entire hippocampus. The cerebellum was then separated from the cerebrum and divided along the central lobule into two equal halves. Collected brain fragments were immediately weighed, frozen, and stored at −85 °C until further analysis. The weight of the left part of the collected structures was used to determine the level of monoamines and amino acids using a high-performance liquid chromatography with electrochemical detection (HPLC-ED) system. The right hippocampus and right cerebellar hemisphere were selected for analysis of GR and MR mRNA expression by Real-Time PCR. The amount of collected frontal cortex was too small to allow analysis of GR and MR mRNA expression.

Analysis of monoamine and amino acid levels using a HPLC-ED system

Brain samples were homogenized in a mixture (1000 µl) containing ice-cold 0.1 N perchloric acid (HClO4) and 0.05 mM ascorbic acid, using a cell disrupter (VirSonic 60; VirTis, USA, Germany), and then centrifuged at 13,000 x g, for 15 min, at a temperature of 4 °C to precipitate proteins (Heraeus Labofuge 400R, Heraeus Instruments, Germany). After filtration using syringe membrane filters with a pore size of 0.2 μm (Puradisc; Whatman, UK), the supernatants were collected for biochemical analyses.

To determine the concentration of serotonin, its metabolite: 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), dopamine, and its metabolites: homovanillic acid (HVA), norepinephrine, and its metabolites: 3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenylglycol (MHPG), 20 µl aliquots of the supernatant were injected into a HPLC-ED system. The HPLC-ED system consisted of a delivery pump (Mini-Star K-500; Knauer, Berlin, Germany), an autosampler automatic injector (LaChrom L-7250; Merck-Hitachi, Darmstadt/Tokyo, Germany/Japan), and an electrochemical detector (L-3500 A; Merck-Recipe, Darmstadt/Munich, Germany) set at a potential of + 0.8 V vs. an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The mobile phase comprised of 32 mM sodium phosphate buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 34 mM citric acid buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 1 mM octane sulfonic acid buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 54 µM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in ultrapure water (18 MΩ∙cm) containing 12% methanol solution (Merck, Germany). Separation of monoamines was achieved using EC 250/4 Nucleosil 100-5 C18AB (250 mm length x 4 mm internal diameter, 5 μm particle size, 100å) HPLC analytical column (Macherey-Nagel, Germany) and mobile phase flow rate maintained at 0.8 ml/min. Chromatograms were recorded and integrated using the Clarity software (version 5.0; Data Apex, Prague, Czech Republic). Samples were quantified by comparison with standard solutions (external calibration). All used monoamine standards were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. The final monoamines/metabolites in the tissue samples were expressed as pg/mg wet tissue.

HPLC-ED was used to determine the concentration of the amino acid neurotransmitters: glutamic acid, aspartic acid, GABA, histidine, serine, arginine, threonine, and taurine in brain homogenates. The chromatography system consisted of an electrochemical detector with a glassy-carbon working electrode (EC 3000, Merck), the autosampler (Primade 1210, Hitachi), and the pump (Primade 1110, Hitachi). The mobile phase comprised 45 mM disodium phosphate (Sigma–Aldrich) and 0.15 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; Sigma–Aldrich) with 24% methanol (Merck). To obtain derivatization reagents, o-phthaldialdehyde (OPA, 22 mg, Fluka) was diluted in 0.5 ml of 1 M sodium sulfite, 0.5 L of methanol, and 0.9 ml of 0.1 M sodium tetraborate buffer (pH 10.4, adjusted with 5 M sodium hydroxide). Preparation of the mobile phase and the derivatizing agents was based on the method developed by Rowley et al.22, with minor modifications. Before application onto the column, the derivatizing agent (20 µl) reacted with the amino acid standard and supernatant samples for 15 min at room temperature. Separation of amino acids was achieved with a reverse phase column (Luna; 5 μm C18(2) 100å; 250 mm length × 4 mm internal diameter; Phenomenex), and the mobile phase flow rate was maintained at 0.8 ml/min. Samples were compared with standard solutions (external calibration), and concentrations were calculated using Primade (Merck). All standards were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. The final level of amino acids in the tissue sample was expressed as ng/mg wet tissue.

Real-Time PCR (RT-PCR) analysis of the glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid receptors gene expression

The hippocampal and cerebellar fragments were homogenized in TRIzol Reagent (Ambion, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The total RNA was extracted using PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Ambion, Life Technologies). The RNA concentration in the samples was determined using a Smart Spec Plus Spectrophotometer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Multiplex RT-PCR reactions were carried out according to the Applied Biosystems (Foster City, CA, US) protocols in triplicate using the TaqMan RNA-to-Ct 1 Step Kit, and the primers for the target gene: GR (rat Nr3c1; Applied Biosystems accession number Rn00561369_m1); MR (rat Nr3c2; Applied Biosystems accession number Rn00565562_m1), labeled by reporter FAM. Two primers for housekeeping genes, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (rat GADPH: Applied Biosystems accession number Rn01775763_g1) and cyclophilin A (rat PPIA; Applied Biosystems accession number Rn00690933_m1) labeled by VIC dye were used for standardization. The reaction volume was 10 µl. The amplification was conducted using MicroAmp Optical 96-Well Reaction Plates with Barcode (Applied Biosystems) under the following conditions: 40 cycles at 95◦C for 15 s and 60◦C for 1 min in a ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System Thermocycler (Applied Biosystems). The results were normalized using the geometric mean of the GAPDH and the PPIA housekeeping genes23. The relative gene expression was calculated using the double delta Ct (ΔΔCt) method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistica software version 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc.; Palo Alto, CA, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was first evaluated with the Shapiro-Wilk test. The data on monoamine and amino acid levels in brain structures were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA to assess the main effects of maternal perinatal depression and sex, and their interaction. To compare the distributions of continuous variables between two groups, the Student t-test was used for normally distributed variables and the Mann-Whitney test otherwise. The results in the Figures and Table 1 were presented as means with standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed variables; otherwise, the median with 25th and 75th percentiles (Q1 and Q3) were provided. The differences were considered statistically significant if the p-value < 0.05.

Statistical power was confirmed via post hoc power analyses using G*Power 3.1.9.2 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany)24. Considering a power (1-β) of 0.80 and an alpha error of 0.05, the sample size used in this research allowed identifying a large effect size: f = 0.575 for 26 adult offspring.

Results

Expression of neurotransmitters in the hippocampus

The impact of maternal perinatal depression on hippocampal monoamine levels was sex-dependent

Two-way ANOVA showed no significant impact of maternal perinatal depression and sex effect on the level of serotonin and its metabolite 5-HIAA in the hippocampus in the adult offspring. However, further analysis revealed a significantly higher level of serotonin in the hippocampus in adult COM compared to adult COF (p < 0.05; Fig. 1A), without differences in hippocampal 5-HIAA levels (Fig. 1B).

In turn, we found a significant maternal perinatal depression and sex interaction for HVA level (F(1.22) = 6.28, p < 0.05), as well as substantial main sex effect for dopamine (F(1.22) = 10.99, p < 0.01), and HVA to dopamine ratio (F(1.22) = 18.03, p < 0.001) in the hippocampus of adult offspring. Further, analysis showed significantly higher levels of dopamine in adult male offspring compared to adult female offspring (COM > COF, p < 0.01; SOM > SOF, p < 0.05; Fig. 1C). However, the level of HVA in the hippocampus was significantly lower in adult SOF in comparison with adult COF (p < 0.05; Fig. 1D), as well as a higher HVA level and HVA to dopamine ratio were observed in adult female offspring compared to adult male offspring (HVA: COF > COM, p < 0.01; SOF > COM, p < 0.01; Fig. 1D; HVA/DA ratio: COF > COM, p < 0.01; SOF > SOM, p < 0.01; Fig. 1D and E).

We also showed significant sex effect in norepinephrine (F(1.22) = 44.03, p < 0.001) and its metabolite MHPG (F(1.22) = 44.07, p < 0.001) in adult offspring. Norepinephrine level in the hippocampus was significantly higher in adult SOM than in adult SOF (p < 0.01; Fig. 1F) and in COM compared to adult COF (p < 0.001; Fig. 1F). However, an inverse relationship was observed in the level of norepinephrine metabolite - MHPG in the hippocampus (SOF > SOM, p < 0.01; COF > COM, p < 0.001; Fig. 1G).

The maternal perinatal depression had a crucial impact on histidine, arginine, and serine levels in the hippocampus

Two-way ANOVA showed a significant impact of maternal perinatal depression and sex interaction for histidine (F(1.22) = 6.51, p < 0.05), arginine (F(1.22) = 8.03, p < 0.01), and serine (F(1.22) = 7.09, p < 0.05) in the hippocampus. Further analysis revealed significantly lower levels of histidine (p < 0.05; Fig. 2D), arginine (p < 0.05; Fig. 2E), and serine (p < 0.05; Fig. 2F) in the hippocampus in adult SOF offspring compared to adult COF offspring. However, significantly higher histidine, arginine, and serine levels were observed in the hippocampus of adult SOF compared to adult SOM (histidine: p < 0.01, Fig. 2D; arginine: p < 0.01, Fig. 2E; serine: p < 0.001, Fig. 2F) and in adult COF compared to adult COM (histidine: p < 0.01, Fig. 2D; arginine: p < 0.01, Fig. 2E; serine: p < 0.01, Fig. 2F).

The sex effect was the main factor affecting the levels of most amino acids tested in the hippocampus, namely: aspartic acid (F(1.22) = 46.58, p < 0.001), glutamic acid (F(1.22) = 47.87, p < 0.001), GABA (F(1.22) = 81.75, p < 0.001), and threonine (F(1.22) = 18.27, p < 0.001). Significantly higher levels of aspartic acid, GABA, and threonine were observed in adult female offspring from both stress-exposed dams and control dams compared to adult male offspring, respectively (aspartic acid: SOF > SOM, p < 0.001, and COF > COM, p < 0.01, Fig. 2A; GABA: SOF > SOM, p < 0.01, and COF > COM, p < 0.01, Fig. 2C; threonine: SOF > SOM, p < 0.05, and COF > COM, p < 0.01, Fig. 2H). However, the glutamic acid level in the hippocampus was significantly lower in adult female offspring than in adult male offspring (SOM > SOF, p < 0.001; COM > COF, p < 0.01; Fig. 2B). Moreover, there were no significant differences in hippocampal taurine levels between the examined groups of rats (Fig. 2G).

Expression of neurotransmitters in the frontal cortex

The impact of maternal perinatal depression on monoamine levels in the frontal cortex was sex-dependent

Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant sex effect for serotonin levels in the frontal cortex (F(1.22) = 9.88, p < 0.01). Specifically, adult COM had significantly higher serotonin levels in the frontal cortex in comparison with adult COF (p < 0.01; Fig. 3A). However, there were no differences in 5-HIAA level in the frontal cortex between the examined rat groups (Fig. 3A).

Similarly, two-way ANOVA indicated significant maternal effects of perinatal depression (F(1.22) = 6.99, p < 0.05) and sex (F(1.22) = 4.72, p < 0.05) on HVA levels in the frontal cortex. However, sex was the main factor determining dopamine levels (F(1.22) = 5.34, p < 0.05) and the HVA-to-dopamine ratio (F1.22 = 4.68, p < 0.05) in the frontal cortex. Dopamine level in the frontal cortex was significantly higher in adult COM offspring compared to adult COF offspring (p < 0.01; Fig. 3C). However, the HVA level in the frontal cortex was significantly lower in adult SOF offspring compared to adult COF offspring (p < 0.05; Fig. 3D). We also noticed differences in the HVA level between adult male and female offspring from stress-exposed dams (p < 0.01; Fig. 3D). There were no differences in the HVA-to-dopamine ratio between the examined groups of rats (Fig. 3E).

Moreover, sex had a significant impact on norepinephrine (F(1.22) = 14.8, p < 0.001) and MHPG (F(1.22) = 25.34, p < 0.001) levels in the frontal cortex. Further analysis showed increased norepinephrine level in the frontal cortex in adult male offspring in comparison with adult female offspring (SOM > SOF, p < 0.01; COM > COF, p < 0.01; Fig. 3F). Additionally, MHPG level in the frontal cortex was significantly higher in adult female offspring than in adult male offspring (SOF > SOM, p < 0.05; COF > COM, p < 0.01; Fig. 3G).

The maternal perinatal depression effect on monoamine levels in the cerebellum was sex-dependent

Notably, significantly higher serotonin levels were observed in the cerebellum in adult male offspring in comparison with adult female offspring (SOM > SOF, p < 0.05; COM > COF, p < 0.05; Fig. 5A). However, there were no differences in the 5-HIAA level in the cerebellum between the examined group of rats (Fig. 5B).

There were significant maternal perinatal depression and sex interaction for HVA level in cerebellum (F(1.22) = 6.23, p < 0.05). There was also a significant sex effect for dopamine (F(1.22) = 4.99, p < 0.05), HVA level (F(1.22) = 23.58, p < 0.001), and HVA to dopamine ratio (F(1.22) = 20.83, p < 0.001) in the cerebellum. Furthermore, there was a significantly higher level of norepinephrine in the cerebellum in adult male offspring compared to adult female offspring (SOM > SOF, p < 0.05; COM > COF, p < 0.05; Fig. 5F). On the contrary, MHPG level was significantly higher in adult COF compared to adult COM (p < 0.01; Fig. 5G).

Discussion

The present study extends our research on the impact of maternal major depressive disorder (MDD) with peripartum onset on rat offspring. We previously demonstrated that perinatal depression in rats using the CMS model in preconception mothers contributed to the development of depressive-like and anxiety-related behaviors in the offspring during adolescence (PD48). However, behavioral abnormalities were not observed in adult offspring (PD225) of the same mothers after exposure to stress-free conditions during adulthood8. In the present study, we addressed the potential mechanisms underlying neuropsychiatric recovery in adult offspring from stress-exposed dams, taking into account the range of monoamine and amino acid neurotransmitters in the hippocampus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum of adult male and female rats subjected to perinatal stress, i.e., brain structures involved in the prenatal stress response25–27. To our knowledge, our study is the only one to extensively examine amino acid neurotransmitters in the context of prenatal stress and to include both males and females. The main finding of our study suggests that maternal perinatal depression is a key factor in reducing the levels of HVA, histidine, arginine, and serine in the hippocampus and frontal cortex of adult female offspring. Moreover, in the cerebellum, sex effect is a primary factor regulating arginine and serine levels. However, we found no significant differences in serotonin or catecholamines (dopamine and norepinephrine) levels in the examined brain structures between male and female offspring of dams with perinatal depression and offspring from control dams.

The impact of maternal perinatal depression on the hippocampus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum in adult offspring

Research shows that chronic stress and depression cause significant changes in neuronal structure across brain regions, with notably increased hyperexcitability observed in the amygdala, striatum, limbic system, and subcortical regions28. Based on existing data from studies on prenatal stress affecting offspring neurodevelopment and behavior, the hippocampus and frontal cortex are the most studied structures because they are highly vulnerable to the negative effects of stress hormones and play vital roles in learning, motivation, and social interaction25,26,29–31. Additionally, the cerebellum’s role in regulating behavior and the stress response is increasingly recognized27,32. It has been shown that during prolonged psychological stress, which dysregulates the HPA axis, the hippocampus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum respond with highly sensitive neuroplastic adaptations that mediate the effects of stress and social perception4,27,32,33. Consequently, as we highlighted earlier, changes in neurotransmitter levels in these three brain structures, the hippocampus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum, became the focus of our research in adult offspring, where we observed a reversal of the effects caused by exposure to maternal perinatal depression. In the presented study, the brain structures most sensitive to maternal perinatal depression are the hippocampus and frontal cortex, which showed a significant decrease in the levels of HVA, histidine, arginine, and serine, especially in adult female offspring from dams with perinatal depression.

Hippocampal sensitivity to maternal perinatal depression can be potentially related to the early onset of its development. The rodent hippocampus develops between the 18th embryonic day and 2–3 weeks after birth34, i.e., during periods of offspring susceptibility to maternal corticosterone, either penetrating the blood-placenta barrier or present in mother’s milk; for comparison: the human hippocampus develops between the last trimester of pregnancy and the age of 1635. Research by Gao et al.36, conducted on adult Sprague Dawley rats subjected to chronic mild unpredictable stress (CUMS) showed that the levels of several amino acid neurotransmitters in the hippocampus were affected by exposure to CUMS. Furthermore, the hippocampus, involved in associative memory-related processes, is regulated by the HPA axis, which mediates the secretion of adrenal hormones30. Both animal and human studies have demonstrated that the volume of the hippocampus is reduced in individuals with severe depression37.

Preclinical evidence confirms that the frontal cortex, due to its prolonged development, is highly sensitive to the effects of stress25. Prenatal exposure to stress has been shown to increase dendritic branching and length in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) apical and basilar dendrites in the rat brain38. Male and female rats separated from their mothers for 3 h daily from postnatal day 3 to day 21 after birth show increased spine density in the mPFC and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) during adolescence39. Activation of the prelimbic and infralimbic regions of the mPFC has been shown to stimulate GABAergic cells in the amygdala complex, thereby inhibiting amygdala efferent neurons and reducing anxiety-like behaviors40.

The cerebellum, primarily associated with the central regulation of movement, also plays an essential role in social interactions and in the pathomechanisms of autism29,31. In the present study, we observed that maternal perinatal depression was associated with reduced arginine and serine levels in the cerebellum of adult offspring. Recently, research by Baek et al.41, conducted on mice subjected to chronic stress showed that the cerebellum actively mediates depression-like behavior induced by mild stress. Similarly, clinical studies show that the cerebellum of depressed people shows marked atrophy and volume reduction, and stimulation directed at the cerebellum can reduce stress-induced depression-like behaviors42.

The effect of maternal perinatal depression on monoamine levels in the hippocampus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum in adult offspring

The available data suggest that the hippocampal-frontal pathway, representing a unidirectional projection, plays an essential role in depression and stress response43. Interestingly, despite the high susceptibility of the hippocampus and frontal cortex to maternal perinatal depression, we did not find differences in the hippocampal and frontal cortex levels of serotonin and catecholamines between adult offspring from dams with perinatal depression and from control dams. Neither did we notice significant differences in monoamine levels in the cerebellum among the examined rat groups. Based on the available data, it can be concluded that abnormalities of the monoaminergic neurotransmission system accompany clinical depression44. A significant increase in serotonin and its metabolite 5-HIAA was observed in patients suffering from depression, anxiety, and chronic stress45. Similarly, noradrenergic dysfunction has been shown to play a key role in MDD and stress response46. Neuromelanin magnetic resonance imaging studies demonstrate a significant reduction in neuromelanin signaling in the rostral and medial locus coeruleus (LC) in patients with MDD, suggesting dysfunction of the ascending noradrenergic system47. Animal models of depressive-like behavior and chronic stress have also shown changes in serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine levels in the brain regions9,26. The study by Wei et al.26, found that male offspring of Wistar rat dams exposed to pre-pregnancy social defeat stress at 2 months of age had significantly lower serotonin levels and higher norepinephrine and dopamine levels in the hippocampus, hypothalamus, and frontal cortex compared to offspring of control dams. Additionally, a decreased baseline firing activity of serotonergic neurons and increased baseline firing activity of noradrenergic and dopaminergic neurons were observed in response to the forced swim test in adult (50–80 days old) offspring of Sprague-Dawley rat dams exposed to bright illumination stress during 11–20 days of gestation48. The lack of difference in monoamine levels in the examined brain structures could be related to the reduction of depressive-like and anxiety-related behavior due to stress-free housing conditions of adult offspring, as well as social buffering between adolescence and adulthood when adult rats of dams with perinatal depression were housed with adult control rats in the stress-free room19,20.

Importantly, the level of HVA was significantly decreased in the hippocampus and frontal cortex in adult SOF compared to adult COF. We have previously observed lower levels of HVA, as well as the HVA to dopamine ratio in the medulla oblongata in adult SOF, compared to adult COF17. Decreased levels of HVA in the above brain structures in adult SOF may indicate decreased dopamine metabolism49. The available data confirms the role of dopaminergic system dysfunction in stress response and depression. It has been shown that acute restraint stress strongly activates. In contrast, 24-hour restraint stress decreases the activity of dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA), which is the source of dopaminergic projections, among others, to the hippocampus and frontal cortex, as part of the mesocorticolimbic pathway, plays a role in the reward and motivation system, which plays an important role in depression50–52. The initial stress-induced activation of VTA dopaminergic neurons may induce a compensatory, long-lasting decrease in dopaminergic neuron activity after stressor withdrawal, ultimately leading to depression-like states53,54. Furthermore, it was observed that patients with MDD had reduced levels of HVA in the cerebrospinal fluid, which suggests a reduction in the basic metabolism of the dopaminergic system during depression55,56.

The effect of maternal perinatal depression on amino acid levels in the hippocampus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum in adult offspring

As already mentioned, the present study demonstrates that maternal perinatal stress has a greater impact on reducing the levels of amino acids, particularly histidine, arginine, and serine, in the hippocampus and frontal cortex, as well as arginine and serine in the cerebellum, in adult SOF. It can be speculated that the significantly lower levels of the above amino acids in the hippocampus, frontal cortex and cerebellum in adult SOF could be due to their increased metabolism, especially since they are precursors of other biologically active substances, such as nitric oxide or D-serine, which play an important role in the stress response and antidepressant effect57–59. Several studies, both experimental and clinical, suggest that arginine administration, or its metabolites ornithine or citrulline, may reduce stress-induced anxiety responses, either through NOx or directly, likely by decreasing oxidative damage and improving mitochondrial function in neurons of the central nervous system60–62. It has been demonstrated that acute restraint stress in C57BL/6 mice reduces hippocampal D-serine levels57. In turn, D-serine supplementation to mice and rats with experimentally induced depression showed antidepressant-like effects and may improve mood disorders63,64. Clinical studies have demonstrated that the level of D-serine in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with major depressive disorder is inversely related to the severity of depression65. Limited data on the role of histidine in the development of depression suggest that the gene encoding phospholysine phosphohistidine inorganic pyrophosphate phosphatase (LHPP), one of the histidine phosphatases, has recently been identified as a susceptibility gene for major depressive disorder66,67. However, the primary function of the above amino acids seems to be participation in the development and protection of the brain against damage as a trophic factor promoting survival, growth, differentiation, branching, and synaptogenesis of neuronal dendrites, and also in learning and memory processes15,68. Amino acids are involved in the above processes via other neurotrophic factors. For example, histidine administration has been observed to increase hippocampal brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) transcription, restore blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, and reduce synaptic abnormalities69.

Potential mechanism of recovery from behavioral disorders

In the present study, adult offspring from dams with perinatal depression were housed between PD48 and PD225 in a single room together with offspring from control dams, in cages with the same enrichment. A single dedicated female technician attended them. It has been demonstrated that keeping animals in cages containing stress-reducing enrichments results in a significant and lasting reduction in fecal corticosterone in mice18. It was also reported that cohousing offspring from stress-exposed rat dams with offspring from control dams reduced the activity of the HPA axis in stress-exposed offspring70 and prevented the increase in anxiety-related behavior and the decline in social interactions in male stress-exposed offspring71,72. Interestingly, there were no significant differences in plasma corticosterone levels between adolescent female and male offspring from control and from dams with perinatal depression8, suggesting an earlier recovery from stress. In the present study, we assessed GR and MR mRNA in the hippocampus and cerebellum of adult offspring. GR and MR mRNA expression levels have been shown to predict blood corticosterone levels in rodents subjected to chronic stress or induced depressive-like behavior6,7. Our results showed that GR mRNA expression did not differ between the groups of rats examined. In contrast, the expression of the MR mRNA in the cerebellum was significantly higher in adult SOM than in adult COM. Unfortunately, we did not assess blood corticosterone concentrations in adult offspring. However, the lack of differences in GR expression in the hippocampus and cerebellum may also indicate that plasma corticosterone concentrations are not different between adult male and female offspring rats subjected to maternal perinatal stress and their corresponding adult controls.

The sex effect on monoamine and amino acid levels in the brains of adult offspring

In the present study, the levels of brain monoamines and amino acids appeared highly dependent on sex. The levels of monoamines were significantly higher, and the levels of their metabolites were substantially lower in all examined brain structures in adult male offspring, both from dams with perinatal depression and from control dams, in comparison with adult female offspring, from dams with perinatal depression and from control dams. However, the levels of most amino acids in the examined brain structures were significantly higher in adult female offspring from dams with perinatal depression and control dams compared to adult male offspring from dams with perinatal depression as well as from control dams. Other researchers also emphasized the influence of sex hormones on neurotransmitter levels73. Importantly, the results of our animal studies are consistent with reports in humans, where it has been reported that female human fetuses are more susceptible to the adverse effects of maternal perinatal stress compared to male offspring61. This has been linked to the vulnerability of the HPA axis to maternal stress during pregnancy, leading to alterations in brain development and function, particularly in female fetuses, which can increase the risk of mood disorders and emotional dysregulation in the female offspring74.

Conclusion

Presented results suggest that recovery from depression- and anxiety-like behavior in adult rat offspring exposed to perinatal maternal depression may be related to changes in brain neurotransmitter levels. Specifically, lower levels of the neuroprotective amino acids histidine, arginine, and serine in the hippocampus, frontal cortex, and cerebellum suggest increased turnover of these neurotransmitters in adult stress-exposed dams. Furthermore, HVA levels in the hippocampus and frontal cortex were significantly lower in adult females exposed to perinatal stress, suggesting a long-term impact of dopaminergic activity in the central nervous system. Our findings provide new information on sex-dependent mechanisms involved in offspring recovery from perinatal maternal stress. However, father studies involve more animals, and additional analyses are needed to confirm our findings.

Work limitations

In the present study, the levels of individual neurotransmitters were assessed using HPLC-ED without histopathological examination of the brain structures or analysis of receptor expression for the examined neurotransmitters. Changes in GR and MR mRNA expression were not validated at the protein level. Statistical analysis was conducted on small and unequal groups of rats.
