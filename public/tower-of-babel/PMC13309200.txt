Adolescents and young adults living with and beyond cancer
Authors: Megan Hessel, David Y Mak, Perri R Tutelman
Published: 2026

Cancer disrupts key milestones for adolescents and young adults (AYAs)

Beyond the universal challenges of cancer, clinicians should address the unique needs of AYAs (aged 15 to 39 yr) related to their stage of development. These include disruptions to education, employment, fertility, relationships, parenting, and finances. Clinicians should also proactively address traditionally taboo topics, such as advance care planning and sexual health, using their patient-centred clinical skills.1

Clinicians should recognize and address the mental health impact of cancer on AYAs

Cancer can cause substantial distress for any patient, but AYAs undergoing cancer treatment are at particular risk for anxiety and depression. Clinicians should routinely screen for mental health challenges using validated measures (e.g., Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, the 12- or 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey, Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System),2 provide appropriate pharmacological intervention and supportive counselling, and initiate referrals to hospital-based and community supports when appropriate (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260027/tab-related-content).

Clinicians should use validating, person-centred language

Language that unintentionally minimizes the impact of cancer (e.g., referring to any cancer as “the good type”) or highlights age-related incongruities (e.g., “You’re so young”) should be avoided. Clinicians should ask about preferred terminology, as some AYAs prefer “person living with or beyond cancer” over battle-oriented language (e.g., “survivor”).3

Adolescents and young adults should receive ongoing medical and psychosocial support after treatment

Most AYAs will live for 50 to 60 years after initial treatment for cancer and may face long-term physical (e.g., fatigue, pain) and psychosocial (e.g., fear of recurrence, return to work) challenges. They are also at risk for chronic comorbidities (e.g., dyslipidemia, hypertension)4 and subsequent malignancies after treatment. Clinicians should provide ongoing, guideline-concordant follow-up (e.g., screenings at intervals according to diagnosis and treatment exposure).5,6

The intersectional identities of AYAs should be considered

Age is just 1 component of identity for AYAs, and other intersectional factors (e.g., race, gender) can contribute to additional stress and stigma. Clinicians should ask AYAs about and document their preferred names, pronouns, and identity, and provide anatomy-based screening for comorbidities and subsequent malignancies.
