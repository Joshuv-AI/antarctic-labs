Severe droughts in Senegal are linked to increased family reunification at migration destinations in Europe
Authors: Yasemin Savas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Research increasingly links climate shocks to cross-border mobility. Migration scholars often frame migration as a temporary, household-level decision to diversify economic risks. It is unclear whether this holds when risks become persistent and intensifying, as with climate change. Focusing on Europe-bound migration from Senegal, we combine individual-level immigration records with granular weather data to understand whether climatic stress increases the likelihood of family reunification at the destination, a potential signal for permanent settlement. Findings indicate that severe drought is associated with increased reunification. Social connections, legal documentation, household wealth, and agricultural involvement moderate the drought-reunification association. Gender also relates to reunification patterns. Married women are less likely to follow their spouses, potentially due to their traditional family roles, which may eventually increase their exposure to climate-related risks. These results suggest that persistent environmental stress may transform the nature of international migration from Africa, with implications for long-term demographic change.

Introduction

Estimates put the increase in the global average projected temperature between 2.4 and 4.8 °C by the end of the 21st century, with a predicted increase in average global land precipitation of 0.9–12.9%1. These changes are expected to put pressure on industrial production2, threaten population health3, and induce human mobility4,5. While most mobility is expected to occur within national borders5, there is empirical evidence of both climate-related internal6 and international migration7,8. Developing countries face disproportionate displacement risks due to their climate-sensitive agricultural systems and rural livelihoods9. According to one estimate, climatic risks can displace 143 million people in the Global South by 205010.

As agricultural households face increasingly frequent and intense climatic shocks, migration remains a key adaptation strategy11,12. A growing body of evidence links climate variability to migration patterns9,13–16. One explanation for this response draws on the New Economics of Labor Migration (NELM), which views migration as part of a household-level strategy to diversify risks in contexts where formal insurance and credit markets are weak or absent17,18. In this framework, households respond to stress by sending migrants away and relying on remittances to stabilize their livelihoods and to minimize risk11,19,20. However, this strategy depends on the feasibility and effectiveness of the risk diversification mechanism. As climate-related risks become more frequent and severe, migration can become prohibitively costly, and risk-diversification through migration may fail, which in turn may push households to adopt more permanent or reactive forms of mobility. Moves that were temporary may become permanent; migrants who moved alone may seek to bring their families to the host country. This paper seeks to understand the relationship between climatic stress and family reunification, which serves as a significant marker of permanent settlement at the destination.

Much of the existing literature focuses on the relationship between climatic variability and migration incidence or timing8,21–25, yet largely overlooks what happens to the household as a whole, with only a few exceptions26. Such research conceptualizes migration as a household risk-management strategy, focusing on whether migration occurs rather than on how families reorganize when it does. In contrast, this study focuses on a distinct process that unfolds after pioneer migration begins: the ways in which climatic shocks shape decisions about family reunification. Subsequent reunification marks a different stage of household adaptation: consolidation at the destination signals a shift toward more settlement-oriented household organization.

Family reunification received relatively little attention compared to migration incidence because most migration datasets are collected at the individual-level and lack multilevel linkages (across individuals, households, and communities)27, which are required to study family-based migration processes such as reunification. Consequently, we know little about the division of labor within the household: who stays behind, who follows later, and whether any form of family reunification occurs at the destination. Such decisions are unlikely to be random or purely economic. For example, previous research on family reunification shows that it often arises from policy changes, opportunities for legalization, or family life-cycle dynamics (marriage, childbearing, and family formation)28–30. However, research does not explore reunification in climatic contexts.

This study argues that climatic shocks can directly trigger family reunification by disrupting established patterns of resource sharing and caregiving within transnational families. Households may respond to environmental stress that threatens livelihoods by regrouping members where income and support networks are more secure.

Evidence suggests context-specific selectivity in migration31, which can shift as more people migrate32. These ideas apply to climate-related migration. For example, there is evidence that climatic stress can force households from rural areas to migrate9 (since they are more dependent on agriculture as livelihood8), but the same households may also prioritize reunification over temporary migration as the origin becomes increasingly uninhabitable. At the same time, legal restrictions can restrict (or open up) migration, creating stratified pathways that reflect the legal status of initial migrants and further embed inequality under climate stress33. Wider migrant networks at the destination can also facilitate these processes and may open up reunification pathways, as they can act as social safety nets. Social and gender roles also differentiate migration trajectories34–36. For example, women’s mobility is restricted in some settings due to social obligations and cultural expectations37. Climate-related risks are likely to interact with these roles11,38, potentially producing unequal outcomes across household members.

These social forces shape not only who can migrate and reunite, but also who remains behind as what scholars call trapped populations39,40. Depending on different social structures and gender norms, some groups are more likely to face constrained mobility. For example, in West Africa, married women are expected to live with their in-laws and take care of their spouses’ families, which significantly renders their ability to migrate41. These norms already exist, but environmental shocks can intensify the risks associated with remaining behind. Ultimately, family reunification and composition of the reunification at destination reflect a complex interplay between environmental vulnerability, legal regimes, social ties, household wealth, and gender norms. To understand the long-term implications of climate-related migration, rather than treating migration as a series of isolated moves, we need to consider household migration patterns over time within their social, economic, and legal contexts.

Most of the evidence in the literature comes from data-rich settings such as the US or Latin America42,43. There is less empirical research on climate-related mobility in Africa, the continent that is the most vulnerable to climate change impacts11,44–46. Africa requires special attention because agriculture has historically constituted the backbone of economic development across the continent, and in many Sub-Saharan African countries, more than two-thirds of the labor force has historically been employed in agriculture47. And when it comes to weather, even minor temperature and precipitation fluctuations can significantly affect agricultural productivity and food security48. In terms of mobility, for example, the first wave of international Senegalese migration came from groundnut basins in the 1980s after a severe drought period, and these migrants ended up in southern Europe and the United States41. Prolonged hungry seasons and persistent weather shocks have the potential to alter migration dynamics and behavior in Africa46. But despite historical and empirical evidence49,50, there is a lack of research on contemporary international migration patterns in response to climate stressors in Africa, particularly sub-Saharan Africa11. This paper focuses on Senegal, a politically stable and agriculturally dependent country in sub-Saharan Africa, that has historically sent migrants to France and other parts of southern Europe41. By the late 1990s, France and Italy each hosted 60,000 registered Senegalese migrants, along with additional migrant populations in other European contexts51. Previous work shows that 19.4% of transnational couples reunify in the destination52. Migration trajectories are strongly gendered: male pioneer migration predominates in approximately two-thirds of transnational unions, and men’s legal and economic position, as well as women’s educational attainment and caregiving roles, shape reunification decisions52.

This study leverages detailed individual-level data from 108 origin communities in Senegal between 1983 and 2009 from the Migrations between Africa and Europe (MAFE) project53, which captures household and migration histories. To measure climate shocks, it combines high-resolution weather data from the Standardized Precipitation-Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI)54,55, which captures drought severity; Rainfall Estimates from Rain Gauge and Satellite Observations (CHIRPS) for rainfall anomalies56; and Climate Hazards Center InfraRed Temperature with Stations daily dataset (CHIRTS-daily) for surface temperature trends57. The analysis shows that extreme weather events at origin increase the likelihood of family reunification at destination and reshape its composition. Reunification offers insight into how households restructure under repetitive climate stress. Rather than viewing migration as a short-term risk diversification strategy, this study conceptualizes reunification as a signal of permanent settlement. In places where return migration is no longer viable due to repeated environmental shocks, migrants may opt to bring close kin to the destination as a long-term adaptation strategy. This study also unpacks the question of who reunites with whom: gender norms and care work obligations may limit married women’s mobility, and climatic stress can intensify the vulnerability associated with remaining at origin.

Senegal presents a useful case for studying climate-related mobility due to its location in the Sahel region, which has been experiencing severe desertification58, heightening rural livelihood vulnerability and migration pressures. Supplementary Fig. S1 illustrates the trends in historical average surface temperature, precipitation, and the standardized precipitation–evapotranspiration index (SPEI), which together show warming temperatures, fluctuating rainfall, and more frequent droughts since the 1980s. Senegal also stands out as one of the most politically stable countries in Africa, with an economy that heavily depends on agriculture and remittances41,59,60. The relative stability makes it easier to separate weather effects from other migration drivers.

Subsequent models examine how this relationship varies across origin and destination characteristics: agricultural dependence, household wealth, migrant legal status, and social connections at destination (Eqs. These analyses demonstrate that environmental shocks reshape not only migration decisions, but also the social structure of households across borders and reveal an underexplored mechanism of long-term adaptation to climate change.

In the SPEI model, married migrants exposed to severe drought in the origin communities are 2.5 percentage points more likely to reunite with their spouses (P < 0.01), 1.3 percentage points more likely to reunite with any nuclear family member (defined as spouses or children if migrant is married, parents of siblings if migrant is unmarried) (P < 0.05), and 1.5 percentage points more likely to bring core kin overall (defined as spouses, children, siblings, or parents regardless of migrant’s marital status) (P < 0.05), compared to those experiencing normal weather conditions (Table 1, panel A). The precipitation and temperature-based model yields similar results but with slightly larger effect sizes (Table 1, panel B). Being exposed to very dry seasons increased the probability of reunification by 3.1 percentage points for the nuclear family (P < 0.01), and 3.4 for core kin (P < 0.01). Other weather conditions do not have statistically significant effects on the reunification process. It is the extreme droughts/very dry seasons that alter family decision-making around migration and settlement. All models control for individual, household, and commune-level characteristics, and include fixed effects for district and year (see “Methods”/”Empirical model specifications” for model specifications and Supplementary Table S1 for all control estimates). Results are robust to alternative sample definitions, such as restricting the sample to current migrants only or excluding polygynous unions (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3), and different baseline periods for CHIRPS and CHIRTS (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Results also indicate that the repeated drought exposure significantly increases the likelihood of all forms of reunification outcomes (Supplementary Table S6). Models with different lag variables (t − 2, t − 3) show that the effect of drought depends on the timing of exposure and is strongest within one year following the shock (Supplementary Tables S7 and S8), and the placebo test using lead weather variables unrelated to reunification timing (t + 1) supports the temporal specificity of the main findings (Supplementary Table S9).

Although the coefficient estimates are modest, they represent substantial proportional changes. When we calculate the average marginal effect of severe drought for each reunification outcome and compare with the effects in baseline normal weather, the corresponding relative changes are 58%, 50%, and 50% (Supplementary Table S10). These changes indicate that severe drought shocks produce meaningful increases in the likelihood of reunification outcomes.

In supplementary analyses, we assess whether the observed association between severe drought and family reunification simply reflects a general migration response. We first estimate models of an individual’s first international migration in the full sample, as well as gender-stratified specifications restricted to married individuals. Across all specifications, we find no evidence that severe drought increases the likelihood of first migration overall, nor that drought differentially predicts first international migration among married men or women (Supplementary Tables S15 and S16). This result suggests that the observed gendered patterns of family reunification are unlikely to be explained by gender-differentiated first international migration.

Further, we examine interactions between weather shocks and social ties at destination; we find a higher likelihood of first migration under extreme weather conditions for individuals with household connections abroad (Supplementary Table S34). This finding points to a broader network-mediated process in which households are more likely to initiate migration when social ties to spouses or other family members are available at the destination.

Finally, we examine remittance behavior and find no consistent association between climatic shocks and remittance transfers, including in gender-stratified analyses (Supplementary Tables S17, S18, and S19). That said, for both male and female migrants, the ego’s past remittance behavior is positively associated with family reunification under weather shocks (Supplementary Table S33). This finding (although limited by the coarseness of the remittance measure) highlights the impacts of remittances on men and women and invites further research.

Together, these findings indicate a potential breakdown of temporary risk diversification strategies. This pattern may be driven by a desire to consolidate their resources in the destination. It is possible that migrant remittances are no longer sufficient to sustain families in origin due to repeated shocks to income. In that case, reunification can offer a more sustainable household survival strategy in the long term. Overall, the results suggest that severe drought shocks can significantly alter how families reorganize across borders in response to environmental stress.

Extreme weather has heterogeneous effects on reunification

The relationship between climate shocks and family reunification is likely to be complex and heterogeneous across households and contexts. The analysis explicitly distinguishes between the core mechanism linking environmental stress to migration incentives and the mediating conditions that shape households’ ability to respond through reunification. In particular, livelihood exposure (captured by agricultural dependence at the origin community) represents the primary mechanism through which climatic shocks translate into economic stress and migration pressure. By contrast, institutional capacity at the destination (measured by residency permits at the destination) and social connections (measured by the presence of extended kin at the destination) condition the feasibility of reunification once such pressures arise (see “Methods”/”Testing for heterogeneity” section for definitions).

Agricultural dependence at origin captures differential exposure, as communities reliant on agriculture-based livelihoods are more directly affected by climate variability. Legal status at the destination reflects institutional capacity, as documented migrants face fewer legal barriers to bringing relatives by lowering informational and logistical costs. Finally, social ties to extended family abroad (excluding spouses, parents, siblings, and children) capture relational embeddedness, reflecting networks that can facilitate reunification. Distinguishing analytically between these mechanisms and mediating conditions allows us to assess not only whether reunification occurs, but also for whom and under what conditions environmental stress prompts longer-term family reorganization.

Together, these findings suggest that severe drought does not uniformly increase family reunification, but does so under specific conditions. Migrants with social networks, legal status, or sufficient household resources appear to be best positioned to respond to environmental stress by reuniting with their families. In this way, climate shocks may reinforce existing inequalities in mobility and adaptation, determining who can afford to bring their family.

Extreme weather affects who reunifies in the family: married women are more likely to be left behind

To move beyond average effects and understand who reunifies with whom under climate stress, the final analysis examines the composition of reunifying kin (see “Methods”/”Composition of reunification” for reunification composition details). The analysis asks two questions: Who reunifies with whom? Do severe droughts affect the types of family members who join migrants at their destination? Understanding this composition is crucial for identifying the gendered and relational dimensions of reunification as a climate adaptation strategy.

The analysis focuses on individuals who experienced core kin reunification in the analysis period and classifies reunification into four non-mutually exclusive categories: spouses, children, parents, and siblings. Two multinomial logit models (one for each marital group) estimate how climatic shocks influence the probability of different reunification types across marital-gender groups, while controlling for individual, household, and community characteristics (see “Methods”/”Composition of reunification” for reunification composition modeling).

Figure 1b displays the average marginal effects of a severe drought on reunification composition. Notably, the only statistically significant effect is observed among married migrant women, who become more likely to bring their spouses and children to the destination following a drought at the origin. No comparable changes exist for married migrant men or unmarried migrant individuals. These findings suggest that climate stress may act as a critical point for married migrant women to reunify with their spouses and children, consolidating their nuclear families at the destination. However, not all women can have this option. In many Senegalese households, married women remain at the origin to care for elderly in-laws while their husbands migrate41, which may delay or prevent their own migration in the first place. As a result, while climate stress appears to prompt family reunification among married migrant women already abroad, gendered caregiving norms likely constrain similar reunification opportunities for women who remain at the origin. These norms already exist, but environmental shocks can intensify the risks associated with remaining behind. Over time, such dynamics may contribute to the formation of a condition often described as a trapped population, particularly among married women whose mobility is already constrained by gendered roles.

Overall, this analysis highlights that who reunifies with whom is not random, but it is shaped by gendered responsibilities and roles. Environmental stress may reinforce or exacerbate these inequalities. In contexts like Senegal, where patriarchal norms structure migration decisions, married women may bear the brunt of climate vulnerability, either by being left behind to hold households together in increasingly unlivable conditions or by shouldering the responsibility of reunifying the family abroad if they manage to migrate first.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that severe droughts in Senegal are associated with increased family reunification at migration destinations in Europe. This result signals a shift from temporary mobility to more permanent settlement strategies. Family reunification is significantly more likely among individuals with legal residency, established social networks in the destination, and those from communities of origin that are heavily dependent on agriculture: key dimensions that previous work also note11,20,33,49. This heterogeneity highlights the relationship between weather stress and existing social and institutional resources. Not all families can reunify after experiencing a weather shock. Moreover, among families who can reunify, not all of them reunify in the same way. The composition of family reunification is shaped by gender and marital status: married migrant women are more likely to bring their spouses and children, and are the only group whose reunification composition significantly shifts in response to drought. In other words, after experiencing severe drought, married migrant women are even more likely to bring their spouses and children with them. We do not see a change in composition for married migrant men. These findings reveal the importance of considering household structure and relational roles35,36 in order to gain a deeper understanding of climate-related mobility. Climate stress appears to facilitate family reunification with spouses and children among married migrant women who are already abroad; however, gendered caregiving norms41 likely constrain similar reunification opportunities for married women at their origin. Over time, such dynamics may contribute to the formation of trapped populations39,40, particularly among households without the necessary resources and married women whose gendered roles bind them to increasingly vulnerable environments.

By analyzing who reunifies, under what conditions, and with whom, this study extends climate migration literature in three important ways. First, it shifts the focus from migration as a temporary risk-diversification strategy to the possibility of reunification as a permanent settlement strategy. Second, it reveals that family reunification is shaped by access to residency permits, embedded social networks, and vulnerability to climate-sensitive livelihoods. Therefore, not all families can reunify. Third, it shows that who reunifies with whom is also socially constructed and identifies a gendered vulnerability. While married migrant women are more likely to consolidate their nuclear families abroad after drought, married women who remain at the origin may face greater immobility due to caregiving obligations and societal expectations. In Senegal, it is common for married women to care for elderly in-laws while their husbands migrate41. It is a norm that may prevent them from reuniting even under worsening conditions. These findings complicate dominant narratives of climate mobility by showing how patriarchal structures shape who moves, who stays, and who reunites, and also uncover how climate shocks reconfigure family structures across borders.

The findings also carry implications for climate adaptation and governance. Policymakers should recognize family reunification as a key dimension of climate-related migration. Rather than a purely administrative concern, reunification constitutes a household-level response to climatic stress that depends on kinship ties, legal status, and gendered divisions of labor, and, it is closely intertwined with broader adaptation strategies in origin contexts. In this sense, reunification can act as an informal adaptation strategy by redistributing household members across space in response to environmental risk.

On the one hand, when households reunify across borders after a climate shock, it may support the adaptation of vulnerable members by relocating them to destination contexts with better economic and institutional opportunities. On the other hand, constraints on reunification can weaken or negate these protective functions. Barriers such as residency restrictions or limited access to social capital may restrict families’ ability to adapt, while gendered caregiving norms may disproportionately anchor women and elderly members in increasingly precarious conditions. Because the adaptive potential of reunification is uneven across households and social groups, a gender- and context-sensitive policy approach to family reunification is essential as climate stress intensifies.

Family reunification also has implications for destination contexts. Receiving countries may experience increased demand for social services, including education, health, and housing, underscoring the need for anticipatory planning rather than reactive responses. Taken together, these dynamics point to the need for migration governance frameworks that treat family reunification as an integral component of climate-linked mobility. Coordinated, gender- and context- sensitive policies in both origin and destination contexts will be central to ensuring that migration continues to serve protective purposes across borders.

There are some potential limitations to note. Even though the models control for district and year fixed effects, there may still be unobserved confounding variables that may bias the results. Future work should explore similar dynamics in other sending contexts (perhaps combining survey data with qualitative interviews) to gain a better understanding of the intra-household negotiations that underlie family reunification decisions. A further limitation of this study is that the data end in 2009, preceding important changes in Senegalese migration patterns and European immigration regimes. Therefore, the estimates should not be interpreted as describing contemporary migration volumes or policy conditions. Instead, the contribution of this study lies in identifying household-level mechanisms operating within a well-established migration system. Core features such as reliance on transnational networks and the role of legal status in enabling family reunification remain salient, even as migration routes, legal channels, and enforcement contexts have evolved. Accordingly, findings speak to how environmental stress reshapes intra-household migration strategies conditional on existing migration pathways.

Overall, this paper advances the understanding of how climate stress reshapes temporary migration strategies and how these new adaptation mechanisms are heterogeneous and relational. This paper highlights the family as a unit of analysis and reunification as a key mechanism of long-term adaptation, rather than treating migrants as isolated individuals and migration as a temporary risk-diversification mechanism. It also contributes to the literature by reframing migration as the reorganization of families and long-term life trajectories across transnational borders. Climate-induced migration is not only about who moves, but also about how families adapt, reunite, and rebuild their futures under environmental stress.

Methods

Migration data

The Migrations Between Africa and Europe Project (MAFE) is a detailed multi-country migration survey funded by the National Institute for Demographic Studies of France, the Regional Council of Ile-de-France, the Seventh Framework Program for Research of the European Commission, and the French Development Agency53. MAFE is a large-scale dataset designed to address data limitations on African international migration. It captures a wide range of migration trajectories of current migrants, return migrants, non-migrants, and migrant networks across multiple sub-Saharan African and European countries.

This study focuses only on the MAFE-Senegal Migration section of the MAFE-Senegal Survey, and the study sample includes current migrants in France, Spain, and Italy (the three European destinations covered by MAFE-Senegal) as well as return migrants in Senegal and their family members (spouses, children, parents, and siblings). We exclude migrants who have no living family members. With these filters, the analysis focuses on 601 current migrants and 191 return migrants. It further tracks their migration experiences between 1983 and 2009. Thus, it is possible to infer their previous migration destination countries, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. In addition, we incorporate migration records from different types of family members (spouses, children, siblings, and parents) while defining the reunification outcome variables. The family data includes information on 459 partners, 716 children, 1053 siblings, and 174 parents.

To prepare the dataset for analysis, we reshape the MAFE data into a person-year format and create 20,490 person-year observations (covering a 27-year period). This structure enables a longitudinal examination of migration patterns in response to weather shocks. Supplementary Table S24 shows the descriptive statistics of the immigrants. Further, the European sample in the MAFE-Senegal Migration survey is gender-balanced by design to allow for gender analyses. More information can be found in the MAFE data codebooks53. It should be noted that the gender-balanced sample does not reflect the true gender distribution of Senegalese migrants abroad. Gender-specific findings should be interpreted with caution, as the observed patterns may not be fully representative of the actual migrant population. Supplementary Table S25 shows the descriptives by gender. To check the potential gender imbalance, we conducted statistical tests for each descriptive variable between men and women and calculated the standardized mean differences (SMD). Supplementary Table S26 shows that although several variables exhibit statistically significant gender differences, the SMDs are uniformly small (< 0.25 for most variables and < 0.40 for all). The sample reflects expected gendered social differences in Senegal, and the magnitude of these differences is modest. Moreover, we included education level and asset ownership covariates in the regression models to address concerns about group imbalance.

We also provide a cross-tabulation of the top five migration reasons (not mutually exclusive) by gender in Supplementary Table S27. Consistent with61, even within the gender-balanced sample, women are more often involved in family reunification (~34%).

Descriptive analysis shows that spells initiated under severe drought conditions are disproportionately longer, with a higher share lasting 5 years or more, compared to spells initiated under other conditions (Supplementary Fig. S11). Return within short periods is rare across all climatic conditions. Although cumulative return by longer horizons is higher for spells initiated under very dry conditions, these returns tend to occur later in the migration spell and should be interpreted cautiously, given the small numbers of observed spells (Supplementary Table S32).

We note that the data captures international migration but does not record internal migration, which may also respond to climatic shocks. As a result, the analysis cannot assess whether households adapt to environmental stress through within-country mobility. Findings should therefore be interpreted as documenting only the role of international family reunification in response to environmental stress.

Agricultural dependence

To calculate an agricultural dependence score, the study uses the General Census of Population and Housing, Agriculture and Livestock 201362. Briefly, an agricultural dependency index is calculated for each locality in the survey using five different agricultural production features: irrigated culture (excluding market gardening), market gardening, decrusal culture, fruit arboriculture, and total area operated by household in 2012–2013 (in HA). Each feature is first scaled and then summed to generate a composite agricultural intensity score for each locality. The average index is calculated for communes with multiple localities. Supplementary Fig. S5 illustrates the score distribution.

Drought data

The main weather data source is the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI)54, a monthly drought index. This study focuses on a pan-African SPEI dataset with 5 km × 5 km grids55. The selected SPEI dataset contains 5-month cumulative calculations of the drought index for a given month. For example, the value for month x contains cumulative information for the period between months x − 5 and x. Ultimately, we only incorporate monsoon months (from June to October) and use the SPEI value of October because this period coincides with the groundnut production season: a critical time for agriculture. Then, we match SPEI coordinates with Senegal’s communes using geographic shapefiles63,64. For each grid cell, we identify the corresponding commune it overlaps with. We then aggregate the matched values at the commune-year level, focusing specifically on the monsoon season (June–October). For SPEI, we use the October value as a summary of the 5-month cumulative drought. Supplementary Fig. S10 shows the raw SPEI value distribution for the analysis period. Since SPEI is an absolute index, it can be easily categorized into drought severity levels. To classify drought conditions in a given month, we follow the SPI categorization standards65, shown in Supplementary Table S14. We merge the severe and extreme categories to increase power.

Precipitation and average surface temperature data

To aid main effects modeling, we also utilize monthly Rainfall Estimates from Rain Gauge and Satellite Observations (CHIRPS) and Climate Hazards Center InfraRed Temperature with Stations daily (CHIRTS-daily) datasets from Climate Hazards Center UC Santa Barbara56,57. Both include a quasi-global (60°S–70°N), high-resolution (0.05° × 0.05°) data (CHIRTS-daily contains daily maximum and minimum temperatures, and CHIRPS includes monthly precipitation levels). For each grid cell, we identify the corresponding commune it overlaps with. We then aggregate the matched values at the commune-year level, focusing specifically on the monsoon season (June–October).

Combined data

To merge weather and migration datasets and construct the final dataset, we utilize geographic shapefiles for Senegalese communes from the IPUMS 2002 database63 and the Harvard Geospatial Library64. Senegal’s administrative structure consists of four levels, and these are regions (ADM1), districts (ADM2), arrondissements (ADM3), and communes (ADM4). The geoshapefiles for administrative levels ADM1, ADM2, and ADM3 are downloaded from IPUMS, and they are already harmonized to cover boundary changes63. These files are used solely for visualization purposes. However, for ADM4-level, no time-varying geospatial data are available, and the only available geoshapefile comes from64, and consists of information on the year 2015. All spatially referenced variables are consistently linked to this fixed set of commune identifiers throughout the analysis. This approach may introduce a measurement error in settings where administrative boundaries changed during the study period. Due to limitations, we are unable to quantify the magnitude of this potential error.

After compiling this polygon database, we obtain the coordinate points of immigrant localities. In the MAFE dataset, specific columns named locality name, town code of birth, town code adm3, and region of this locality specify a migrant’s place of origin. They can be at ADM2 level, ADM3 level, ADM4 level, or even smaller (e.g., villages). To assign communes to individuals, we extract the coordinates of all possible localities using Nominatim API in Python66. Then, we spatially merge these localities with the geo-shape database. There are 136 individuals whose locality names were missing or unreadable, and we used ADM3-level or ADM2-level information available in the survey as a fallback. This yielded 154 unique locality coordinates for the complete immigrant set. There are 59 individuals with no location information of any kind. We assigned Senegal-level coordinates to them. No observations were dropped due to matching failures. Ultimately, we are able to match all individuals to communes, which represent 25% of all current communes in Senegal. Supplementary Fig. S6 provides a breakdown of this coverage on different administration levels. During modeling, we treat Dakar communes as one single commune, as they are very small geographically, and the combined region is similar in size to other nearby communes. The map of Dakar for comparison can be found in the Supplementary Fig. S7.

To address potential gradient effects, we estimated additional models using continuous SPEI values with quadratic and cubic polynomial terms. The model with continuous SPEI values does not yield statistically significant results (Supplementary Table S31). As shown in Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4, both specifications reveal nonlinear responses. In the quadratic specification, predicted probabilities are lowest under average climatic conditions and increase toward both dry and wet extremes. In the cubic specification, predicted probabilities remain flat across most of the SPEI distribution and increase only under the most negative (severe drought) conditions. This pattern is consistent with threshold-like dynamics: households respond primarily when drought reaches a critical level rather than responding gradually across the distribution. These results suggest that the use of threshold effects captures the underlying nonlinear process.

Weather events

For models with CHIRPS and CHIRTS, shocks are operationalized as normalized deviations from a community’s monsoon season averages in the preceding decade (1970–1980 means). We restrict the analysis to this period because it coincides with Senegal’s monsoon season and the groundnut production season: a critical time for agriculture. Groundnut is the country’s most popular crop: 21% of the total land is used for groundnut production67. Groundnuts are sensitive to temperature and rainfall. Crops are sown in July and harvested in October (during the monsoon season). Total precipitation in the region varies between 300 and 1200 mm68. This crop sensitivity makes it a strong candidate for studying climate-induced migration. The analysis defined a season as dry/wet if it is at least 1 standard deviation away (at most 2) from the baseline monsoon period (1970–1980) and a season very dry/very wet if it is at least 2 standard deviations away from the baseline monsoon period (1970–1980). Very cool and cool categories are combined because of the low sample size of Very cool records. Supplementary Figs. S8 and S9 illustrate the distributions of precipitation and surface temperature along with event categorizations.

For SPEI modeling, we operationalize weather shocks using predefined thresholds following SPI categorization standards65, shown in the Supplementary Table S14. We focus on the SPEI value it has in October because we use the SPEI-05 dataset, which is a 5-month cumulative, which means the SPEI value in October covers the period between June and October (the monsoon season). The analysis uses SPEI to capture year-to-year climatic anomalies, rather than long-term changes in temperature and precipitation.

Modeling

We analyze 20,490 person-year records from 108 communes between 1983 and 2009 using fixed effect regression (year and district level). The goal is to approximate the causal effect of exogenous shocks on the different kinds of reunification dynamics among current and prior migrants. We compare people’s records in communes exposed to extreme weather shocks to those not. We control for individual-level (sex, age, occupational status, education level, documentation status), household-level (land, business, and property ownership, children count, sibling count, social connections at the destination), and commune-level (origin commune agricultural dependency) demographics. Employing this fixed-effects approach can eliminate potential biases, such as omitted variable bias. The model can remove unobserved commune and year-specific variation (like different policies and things happening across communes), and the final coefficients represent the average effect of observed variables within a commune. We drop migrant-years after the first reunification event occurs in each model.

Reunification variable construction

We constructed three dependent variables based on different definitions of family structure. The spouse reunification variable captures whether an individual reunites with their spouse, and applies only to those who are married. The nuclear family reunification variable is defined conditionally: for married individuals, it includes spouses and children, while for unmarried individuals, it includes parents and siblings. Finally, the core kin reunification variable considers all immediate family members (spouses, children, parents, and siblings), regardless of the ego’s marital status. The main effects modeling focuses on all three, while the further heterogeneity analysis focuses on the latter two because of low sample sizes in the spouse reunification marriage condition. Finally, the composition of reunification analysis focuses on core kin reunification only to derive detailed composition groups.

Empirical model specifications

This study examines the effect of weather shocks during the monsoon season in year t − 1 on family reunification decisions in year t. The analytic sample used in the models is smaller than the full panel because we implement event-based right-hand censoring. For each individual, we retain all person-years until (and including) the year the reunification event occurs, and exclude all subsequent years because the individual is no longer at risk of experiencing the event. This ensures that risk sets are properly defined for discrete-time event-history models.

In the main set of models, the dependent variables are spouse reunification, nuclear family reunification, and core kin reunification. We use a set of fixed-effects logit regression models to understand the main effect of weather extremes, as well as models with interaction terms to explore heterogeneous effects across different groups, and multinomial logit models to explore reunification composition. In the heterogeneity analysis, we focus on nuclear family reunification and core kin reunification outcomes because of the low sample sizes in the spouse reunification outcome.

(b) To incorporate macro-level migration determinants, we estimated additional models interacting drought categories with (i) the distance between the origin region and the destination country, (ii) a Senegal-France colonial-tie indicator, and (iii) destination-country policy variables capturing restrictive periods in destination countries for international migration. We calculated the shortest distance between the origin community and the destination country for (i). For (ii), we created a binary indicator capturing a Senegal-France colonial tie when the destination is France. Finally, for (iii), we merged the DEMIG POLICY database69 with the person-year dataset to construct a time-varying indicator of restrictive migration policies in destination countries. This variable captures years in which immigration policies became more restrictive and allows us to test whether climatic shocks affect reunification differently across destination policy environments. Results in Supplementary Tables S28, S29, and S30 indicate that origin-destination distance is negatively associated with reunification, colonial ties strengthen the effect of severe droughts, and restrictive migration policy shifts strengthen the observed impact of severe droughts.
