Evaluating the advantages and limitations of three-dimensional (3D) endoscopy as an innovative tool in spine surgery for degenerative pathologies: a case series
Authors: Alison Ma, Ralph J Mobbs, Nathan Xie, Joseph Reidy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndoscopic spine surgery reduces tissue damage, blood loss and postoperative pain. However, the two-dimensional (2D) view prevents depth perception and spatial orientation, contributing to a steep learning curve. Three-dimensional (3D) endoscopes improve depth perception but introduce increased scope diameter, workflow disruption and reduced image quality. A novel algorithm-based 3D visualisation system enables real-time conversion of standard 2D endoscopic images into 3D without requiring a dedicated 3D endoscope. Its impact on surgical workflow and intraoperative performance in spine surgery remains unknown.Case Description14 patients with degenerative spinal pathology underwent endoscopic surgery using a real-time 2D-to-3D conversion system (13 uniportal, 1 biportal). Outcomes were assessed across predefined domains: (I) visualisation; (II) instrument handling; (III) haemostasis; (IV) educational impact; (V) workflow; and (VI) ergonomics. Improved visualisation (depth perception and spatial orientation) were observed in all cases. Improved instrument handling (navigation and drill control) was observed in all decompression cases (10/10). Enhanced haemostasis recognition was reported in 6/10 applicable cases. Educational benefit was observed in 6/14 cases. Workflow disruption occurred in 4/14 cases and ergonomic symptoms (mild dizziness or double vision) were reported in 6/14 cases. Field of view was equivalent between 2D and 3D imaging. No dural injuries, neurological deficits or reoperations occurred. All patients demonstrated recovery consistent with expected postoperative trajectories.ConclusionsThis case series demonstrates the potential for 3D endoscopy to be used as an adjunct in endoscopic spine surgery with improvements in depth perception, instrument handling and visualisation of spatial relationships without altering endoscope diameter or access corridors. While an initial familiarisation phase is required, the system demonstrates potential workflow and educational value. Further prospective studies with quantitative metrics and long-term outcomes are warranted.Keywords: Endoscopic spine surgery, three-dimensional visualisation (3D visualisation), depth perception, surgical innovation, case series

Highlight box

Key findings

• Improved depth perception and spatial orientation were observed in all cases, with enhanced instrument navigation and drill control in decompression procedures.

• Workflow disruption and transient ergonomic symptoms were confined to an early familiarisation phase and resolved with adaptation.

• No intraoperative complications, neurological deficits or reoperations occurred.

What is known and what is new?

• Conventional two-dimensional (2D) endoscopic spine surgery lacks depth perception and spatial orientation, contributing to technical difficulty and a steep learning curve. Dedicated three-dimensional (3D) endoscopes improve stereopsis but may increase scope diameter and disrupt workflow.

• This is the first structured case series evaluating a 3D endoscopic system that converts standard 2D endoscopic images into 3D images with prospective assessment across predefined domains including visualisation, instrument handling, haemostasis, educational impact, workflow and ergonomics.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• 3D endoscopy has the potential to be a valuable technique in the surgical management of degenerative spinal pathologies and a learning tool to train surgeons and teach operating theatre staff.

• Future studies should incorporate multicentre designs, larger sample sizes, objective operative metrics, validated ergonomic assessments, and direct comparison with 2D visualization.

Introduction

Background

Endoscopic spine surgery has been increasingly utilised in the last decade as it reduces incision size, tissue damage, blood loss and postoperative pain (1). Rapid advancements in optics, visualisation and instrumentation, have expanded its indications across the spine (2-4). However, there are various challenges including the limited, indirect and two-dimensional (2D) view of endoscopic spine surgery, resulting in lack of depth perception and spatial orientation (5-7). These challenges are particularly significant for spinal endoscopy due to the confined space and proximity of neural structures, as compared to endoscopy in other areas that have existing space or accommodate enlargement such as the gastrointestinal tract and abdomen (8). Consequently, endoscopic spine surgery has a steep learning curve which requires surgeons to gain skills in hand-eye coordination, instrument handling and tissue manipulation to be able to proficiently work with 2D images (5,6). Surgeons have shown interest in endoscopic spine surgery to develop their skills and improve surgical morbidity (9). However, there is lack of formalised training to acquire the skills needed for endoscopic spine surgery, inhibiting widespread implementation of endoscopic techniques (10).

Rationale and knowledge gap

A novel real-time three-dimensional (3D) visualisation system has recently been developed that converts standard 2D endoscopic images into 3D images using algorithm-based image processing, without requiring a dedicated 3D endoscope. This approach preserves existing instrumentation and scope diameter. A preliminary case report suggested that this system may improve manoeuvrability and anatomical recognition in lumbar spine surgery (11). However, its broader implications for intraoperative workflow, surgeon performance and ergonomics have not been systematically evaluated.

In particular, there remains a knowledge gap regarding whether real-time algorithm-based 3D visualisation can enhance surgical workflow in endoscopic spine surgery without introducing additional invasiveness or equipment complexity. Early evaluation of novel surgical technologies should focus not only on patient outcomes, but also on intraoperative performance, adaptation curves, and workflow integration prior to widespread implementation.

Objective

The objective of this case series is to evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of a 3D endoscopic system, including its effect on visualisation, instrument handling, haemostasis, education, workflow and ergonomics in degenerative spinal cases. This study represents the first evaluation of this technology in spine surgery and seeks to define its potential role as a workflow-enhancing and educational adjunct in spine endoscopy. We present this article in accordance with the PROCESS reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-1-0054/rc).

Case presentation

Study design

This was a prospective, single-surgeon, consecutive case series evaluating the integration of a 3D visualisation system into standard endoscopic spine surgery. All cases were performed by the senior neurosurgeon (R.J.M.) using standard endoscopic techniques, with the additional Darwin 3D Endoscopic System. Patients were followed postoperatively including immediate postoperative period and early follow-up (2–6 weeks). No patients were lost to follow up during this period. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients for publication of this case series and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Patient population

Fourteen consecutive patients with symptomatic degenerative spinal pathology refractory to conservative treatment were included. Pathologies involved lumbar and cervical levels requiring decompression-based procedures. Patients underwent either a uniportal procedure (n=13) or biportal procedure (n=1). Specific procedures included lumbar laminectomy (L3/4, L4/5), cervical foraminotomy (C4/5, C5/6, C6/7) and radiofrequency ablation, as outlined in Table 1.

3D endoscopic system

The Uniportal and Multiportal Elliquence (New York, USA) Endoscopic system was used for all cases. A 6.3-mm scope was used for radiofrequency ablation, 6.9 mm scope for transforaminal decompression and a 10-mm scope for discectomy and laminectomy. Additional instruments, including drills and radiofrequency probes, were supplied by Matrix Medical Innovations (Sydney, Australia).

The Darwin 3D Endoscopic System (MedicalTek Co., Ltd., Taichung; Figure 1A) was integrated into standard ESS workflow. Conventional 2D endoscopic output was processed using an algorithm that analyses light reflection and refraction to generate real-time 3D images. Both 2D and 3D images were displayed simultaneously on separate monitors (Figure 1B). Polarising glasses were used to visualise the 3D output. No modification to scope diameter, surgical corridor, or instrumentation was required.

Results

Findings are presented according to six domains, as shown Table 1 and Figure 2.

Improved visualisation (depth perception and spatial orientation) was observed in all 14 cases. Improved instrument handling (navigation and drill trajectory control) was observed in all decompression cases (10/10). Enhanced haemostasis recognition was reported in 6/10 applicable cases. Educational benefit, defined as improved anatomical understanding among operating theatre staff, was observed in 6/14 cases. Workflow disruption occurred in 4/14 cases. Ergonomic symptoms (mild dizziness or double vision) were reported in 6/14 cases, predominantly during early adoption. Field of view and surgical exposure was equivalent between 2D and 3D visualization. Across all 14 cases, there were no dural injuries, new postoperative neurological deficits or reoperations within follow-up. All patients demonstrated recovery consistent with expected postoperative trajectories.

The findings can be grouped into two phases. Phase 1 (Cases 1–4) was characterised by workflow adaptation and technical familiarisation. Workflow disruption occurred in these 4 cases including colour calibration discrepancies and one connectivity interruption occurred during Case 3, requiring discontinuation of 3D visualisation and completion of the procedure using 2D imaging. This represented the only technical interruption in the series (1/14; 7%). Mild dizziness was reported in all cases during this phase. These symptoms were transient and resolved intraoperatively without intraoperative complications.

Following familiarisation, Phase 2 (Cases 5–14) demonstrated stable integration of 3D visualisation into routine operative workflow. No connectivity failures occurred and no additional workflow disruptions were observed in the remaining 10 cases. Ergonomic symptoms improved, with reduced discomfort. Occasional transient double vision was reported in 2 cases when head position relative to the monitor shifted. These were resolved with repositioning and did not affect operative performance.

Discussion

Key findings

This trial examines the use of a 3D endoscopic system which converts 2D endoscopic images to 3D images in real time, for use in surgical management of degenerative spinal pathologies. This case series demonstrates subjective improvements in depth perception and spatial orientation. This is particularly beneficial in the spine, where accurate visualisation is crucial due to the limited space and close proximity of fragile nerves and blood vessels. Across all 14 cases, enhanced visualisation was observed without compromise in field of view or surgical exposure. The increased depth perception was perceived to improve control, accuracy and confidence when introducing sharp tools. Additionally, more rapid identification of bleeding sources was observed when haemostasis was required.

Early cases were characterised by workflow disruption and transient ergonomic symptoms. These issues could impact the comfort and performance of the surgical team, potentially leading to fatigue and reduced precision during longer procedures. For example, the system’s sensitivity to changes in position, which resulted in occasional double vision, did not cause any complications in this case series. However, it may pose safety risks when using sharp instruments as lack of clear vision can be disorientating and impair surgeons’ precision. As with any new technology, the use of this new 3D endoscopic system requires a period of adjustment and familiarization, and later cases demonstrated stable integration without further technical interruption. Another limitation is the additional costs and need for further equipment including the 3D endoscopic system and 3D monitor.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its structured evaluation framework. Six assessment domains were predefined, allowing systematic reporting outcomes including visualisation, instrument handling, haemostasis, educational impact, workflow and ergonomics. The inclusion of both uniportal and biportal approaches across lumbar and cervical procedures enhances the generalisability of observations.

Limitations include that the study represents a single-surgeon experience with a small sample size, limiting generalisability. Outcomes were primarily subjective and operator-reported, without objective metrics. There was no comparison with a control group using only standard 2D visualization. Additionally, while there was analysis of early postoperative outcomes, there was no long-term follow up, limiting assessment of clinical outcomes and complications.

Comparison with similar research

To our knowledge, this represents the first published case series examining the intraoperative workflow, ergonomic, and safety implications of 3D endoscopic visualisation in spine surgery. As such, it provides a reference point for future comparative and multi-centre investigations. The paper extends upon the previous case report that trialed two cases with the 3D endoscopic system, allowing the surgical team to identify new findings that were not initially apparent (11). Initially, there were significant workflow challenges including connection issues, discomfort from polarizing glasses and colour discrepancies. When more cases were performed and workflow and efficiency improved as the surgical team became more familiar with the system, the advantages of 3D endoscopy became more apparent, including enhanced navigation, identification of bleeding points and improved drill control. Additionally, an educational environment was fostered, with nursing staff demonstrating an enhanced understanding of surgical anatomy as their familiarity with spatial relationships improved through the use of the 3D endoscopic system. This new trial also allowed for the exploration of the 3D endoscopic system’s application in biportal endoscopic surgery, which was not covered in the initial case report.

Implications and actions needed

Lack of depth perception and stereoscopic vision contributes to the steep learning curve of endoscopic spine surgery and inhibit its widespread adoption. This 3D endoscopic system has the potential to mitigate these limitations and accelerate the learning curve by improving surgeons’ visuospatial orientation and can be used for surgical education and training. However, these findings are preliminary and based on subjective assessments.

The benefits of the 3D endoscopic system extend beyond surgeons’ surgical workflow and precision, with operating theatre staff noting improved understanding of procedures due to greater visualisation of anatomical details through 3D visualisation. This suggests potential value in training future surgeons and other health professionals. Additionally, the system overcomes the barriers of 3D endoscopes as it does not require surgeons to become familiar with using 3D endoscopes. Instead, surgeons are able to maintain the comfortability and user-friendliness of current 2D endoscopes, while benefiting from the improved depth perception and spatial awareness of 3D visualisation.

Further investigation is required before widespread implementation can be recommended. Future studies should incorporate multicentre designs, larger sample sizes, objective operative metrics, validated ergonomic assessments, and direct comparison with 2D visualisation. This can help to determine whether subjective improvements translate into measurable reductions in operative time, complication rates, or learning curve duration.

Conclusions

Overall, 3D endoscopy may be a valuable technique in the surgical management of degenerative spinal pathologies by improving depth perception, spatial orientation, instrument handling and bleeding identification. This trial highlights the potential for 3D endoscopy to be used as a learning tool to train surgeons and teach operating theatre staff. However, 3D endoscopy does have limitations, especially during the initial period of familiarisation. These limitations include setup and workflow issues, the need to constantly wear glasses, and occasional double vision.

These findings suggest that real-time 3D visualisation may offer various advantages in endoscopic spine surgery. However, these are only preliminary findings and future multicentre studies with larger cohorts, objective metrics, validated ergonomic assessments, and direct comparison with 2D visualisation are required to determine whether subjective advantages translate into measurable improvements in operative efficiency, complication rates, and learning curves.
