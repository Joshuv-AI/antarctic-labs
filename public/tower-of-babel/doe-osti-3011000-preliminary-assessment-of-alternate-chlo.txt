 Executive Summary
Recent advancements towards low-temperature chlorination with disulfur dichloride and thionyl
chloride of Al-clad used nuclear fuel (UNF) have been summarized for work control technical
expertise development at the Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL). This technique aims
to provide a more effective method for separating U from cladding or alloying elements.
A preliminary assessment determined needs for unit operation developments in regard to reaction
kinetics and reagent quality. Furthermore, an analysis of an Uruguay fuel plate (U-Alx fuel meat),
with low-enriched uranium (LEU) and located at SRNL, was a potential candidate for future
experiments with lightly irradiated (0.08% burnup) fuel to test for chlorination. Future proposed
work would involve kinetic studies, reagent quality assessments, and the machining of the fuel
plate down to an appropriate bench-scale size and testing the effectiveness of low-temperature
chlorination on the fuel plate.

1.0 Introduction

Since the mid-1900s, the United States has amassed over 90,000 metric tons heavy of metal
(MTHM) of used nuclear fuel (UNF) with an additional 2000 MTHM added annually. 1, 2 To manage
this growing stockpile of UNF, geological repositories and reprocessing pathways have been
investigated as solutions to this challenge. Materials that cannot be repurposed, including high-
level waste (HLW), should be minimized since these materials are destined for geological
repository. For the reclaimable components of UNF, reprocessing not only minimizes the quantity
of buried waste, but allows for collecting and repurposing material. 3 Reprocessing strategies
involving the separation of cladding from the fuel meat can allow for reposition of undesired waste,
collection of nuclear-grade cladding, and recycling of nuclear material towards advanced fuel
production. With an enrichment between 5-20%, High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (HALEU)
fuels can be employed advantageously with advanced reactors, including those using molten salts
or TRISO fuels, and as a fuel alternative for current light-water reactors.4-6 Reclaimed material
from high-enriched uranium (HEU) sources could be down blended to create HALEU fuels.
Nuclear fuels are commonly clad within Zr or Al-based alloys, with some Al-clad fuels containing
up to 80% Al by volume.7-9 Currently, separation strategies can involve mechanical, chemical, and
electrochemical processes.10-12 Some disadvantages of these methods include high operating
temperatures, generation of excessive HLW, and difficulty separating alloying elements.
Recently, an alternative separation strategy, developed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL) and the University of Tennessee, Knoxville (UTK), has gained traction due to the
successful demonstration of a low-temperature separation of Zr from U. Selective chlorination of
Zr with either thionyl chloride (SOCl2) or disulfur dichloride (S2Cl2) followed by dissolution, allowed
the soluble Zr to be partitioned from the insoluble U.13, 14 Success with Zr prompted preliminary
experiments with Al, which has indicated this chlorination procedure could be promising for
processing Al-clad UNF.15

2.0 Low-Temperature Chlorination Pathway

2.1 Chemical Reaction

Researchers at ORNL and UTK have accomplished Al chlorination with both SOCl 2 and S2Cl2.
These reactions were performed under an inert nitrogen atmosphere and proceeded through
either Equation 1 (S2Cl2) or Equation 2 and 3 (SOCl2).15, 16 A byproduct of Equation 3, sulfur
dichloride (SCl2), can chlorinate Al by proceeding through Equations 4 and 5. The reaction
between SOCl2 and Al through Equation 2 generates S 2Cl2 as a byproduct, which then fuels
Equation 1. Equations 2 and 3 both produce gaseous sulfur dioxide (SO2) while Equations 1 and
4 both generate S as a solid, yellow byproduct.

 2Al + 3S2 Cl2 → 2AlCl3 + 6S Equation 1

 2Al + 4SOCl2 → 2AlCl3 + 2SO2 + S2 Cl2 Equation 2

 2Al + 6SOCl2 → 2AlCl3 + 3SO2 + 3SCl2 Equation 3

 2Al + 3SCl2 → 2AlCl3 + 3S Equation 4

 2Al + 6SCl2 → 2AlCl3 + 3S2 Cl2 Equation 5

The byproduct, elemental S, could be recycled by metering in chlorine gas, Cl 2, to regenerate
S2Cl2 (Equation 6).

 2S + Cl2 → S2 Cl2 Equation 6

Experiments assessing chlorination with sulfuryl chloride (SO2Cl2) indicated the Al foils tested
were unaffected. To better control the exothermicity of chlorination, attempts to use SO 2Cl2 as an
inert solvent were unsuccessful. Mixtures of S 2Cl2:SO2Cl2 were observed to undergo an initial
period of endothermic behavior, followed by an exothermic reaction that ultimately produced SO 2
and SCl2. These observations could be rationalized by Equation 7, which has a positive enthalpy
and a negative Gibbs free energy.

 2SO2 Cl2 + S2 Cl2 → SO2 + 2SCl2 Equation 7

Disulfur dichloride was the preferred chlorinating agent since the generated byproduct, elemental
S, could be regenerated with Cl2. With the capability to regenerate the chlorinating agent and
proper heat management of the reaction mixture, the chlorination of Al could be a continuous
process. However, one caveat is the similar AlCl3 sublimation point (406 K), S sublimation point
(293-383 K), and S2Cl2 boiling point (410 K) that can complicate or extend the duration of product
separation.
Experiments with simulated irradiated fuel (UO3, Eu2O3, SrO, Cs2O) indicated these compounds
remained unaffected when refluxed at 95 °C with mixtures of S 2Cl2 and SOCl2.17 Similarly, UO2
pellets were unaffected by this chlorination method.13, 14

2.2 Reaction Kinetics

Aluminum chlorinations with SOCl2 or S2Cl2 had induction periods marked by slow reactivity prior
to rapid exothermic reactivity. Presumably, a passivating surface layer of aluminum oxide resulted
in the observed induction period by impeding the rapid chlorination of the test samples. However,
once the surface layer was stripped and the interior was exposed, the exothermic chlorination of
Al rapidly progressed.
For SOCl2, Al samples (0.5 – 5 g with 0.016 – 2 mm thicknesses) were quantitatively chlorinated
(Equation 2 and 3), as determined by gravimetric and elemental analysis. For larger samples of
Al tested (2.5 – 5 g), the exothermic reaction boiled the reaction mixture and vigorously evolved
gas, which elevated the solution level beyond the headspace of the flask. Under mild conditions
(temperatures below 348 K), inductions periods varied largely, and reactions were complete within

 2

4 h. Preheating the solvent (373 K) before Al addition reduced induction periods to about 30 min
and reactions were rapidly completed (less than 50 min). Hollow cylinders (1 g) of Al-6061 and
pucks (1 g) of Al-8001 completely reacted with an excess of SOCl 2 in about 4 h and 1.5 h,
respectively. Kinetic studies with the Al-6061 cylinders added to preheated solutions at 350 K had
~3 h induction period before a rapid, complete reaction occurred within 1 h.
For experiments with S2Cl2, Al chlorination generated elemental S as a byproduct (Equation 1).
At low temperatures, the induction period was significant, unpredictable, and varied drastically
while, in contrast, rapid reactivity was observed for temperatures above 292 K. Notably, once
rapid reactivity was initiated, even cooling the solution (as low as 195 K) did not impede
chlorination of the exposed Al. A gelatinous, dark red product was produced for these reactions.
Similar to preheating the SOCl2, preheating S2Cl2 prior to Al addition reduced both the variability
and duration of the induction period (e.g. a 1 g Al piece of 2.0 mm thickness had an induction
period of 10 min). Hollow cylinders of Al-6061 and Al-8001 were also evaluated and were shown
to completely react within 30 min. For the hollow Al-6061 cylinders, the reaction mixture
underwent vigorous exothermic reactivity after a 23 min induction period, which required cooling
the reaction mixture in an ice bath to control the exotherm. In addition to preheating SOCl2 and
S2Cl2, higher Al surface area-to-volume ratios were correlated with greater reaction rates and a
briefer induction period duration.

2.3 Radiolysis Effects

Radiolytic effects on the chlorinating agents, S 2Cl2 and SOCl2, demonstrated significant radiation
resistance up to about 34 MGy, which was equivalent to several cycles of reuse. 18 The primary
degradation products from both chlorinating agents was Cl2. Fortuitously, this would not present
an issue since Cl2 and elemental S will regenerate the chlorinating compound, S2Cl2. For SOCl2,
radiolysis (gamma and electron beam irradiations) also resulted in the formation of a product
containing a “S=O” group, predicted to be SO, alongside SCl 2 and Cl2. These slowly reacted to
form SO2Cl2 and SOCl2.19 Though conversion of these radiolytic products back to the parent
compounds could be possible, the presence of metal ions, such as those present in UNF, could
prevent this process.

2.4 Additional Considerations

In preparation for follow-up studies with irradiated fuel, ORNL has assessed the candidacy of
Advanced Test Reactor (ATR) fuel for compatibility with this chlorination process. 7 Due to the
variability and proprietary nature of ATR fuel compositions, ORNL intends to fabricate U-Alx alloys
for chemical tests. Plans to test the alloys, Al-8001 and Al-6061, will elucidate and track the
presence of alloying elements, Si, Fe, Cu, Mn, Mg, Cr, and Zn throughout the processing pathway.
A supporting report addressed initial concerns regarding the chlorination of minor elements
predicted to be found in ATR fuels. 20 The researchers surmised that early transition metals had
greater affinity towards chlorination while late transition metals and precious metals were not as
readily chlorinated. Similar to their kinetic studies on Al, chlorination rates of these minor elements
depended on surface area and surface layer passivation by an oxide layer. The off-gassing
volatile species were also assessed alongside appropriate methods of capture. 21

3.0 Results and Discussion

As a nascent, developing chemical processing pathway, the low-temperature chlorination of Al-
clad UNF has unaddressed challenges. Though fundamental experiments with freshly fabricated
U-Alx fuel meats and individual experiments with expected alloying elements are planned,
experiments with UNF have yet to be performed. These experiments would offer insight into the
dissolution profile of a U-Alx alloy. Furthermore, experiments with irradiation products embedded
into the fuel meat offer insight into their effects on the dissolution kinetics and their location
throughout the off-gas system. Tracking alloying elements throughout processing would provide
data on the degree of chlorination and location of these products. Adjustments to experimental
designs and engineering factors could be considered to streamline the process during UNF
dissolution, gas introduction, and separation steps.
Based on published experimental results and discussions with other laboratories, tempering the
rapid reactivity of Al should be considered. Implementing temperature control or limiting the
quantity of available chlorinating agent within the reaction mixture are potential options. The
quality of the reagents may also influence the reaction kinetics. An assessment of these
influences and confirmation of the kinetics are important to understanding this chlorination
process.

3.1 Uruguay Fuel Plate

SRNL possesses several fuel plates from four fuel assemblies originally from Uruguay’s RU-1
research reactor.22 Previously known as the United States Atomic Energy Commission (US AEC)
Latin American Demonstration Reactor, the 10 kW light-water moderated Lockheed reactor was
in operation from 1959 – 1962 during the US AEC’s South American exhibit. 17 The reactor was
dismantled in 1963 before being purchased, in accordance with the Atomic Energy Act of 1954,
by the Uruguay government in 1965 from Lockheed Nuclear Products. Uranium, fuel elements,
and fuel plates were provided by the United States government. Rebuilt in 1973, the reactor was
in operation solely for research purposes between 1978 – 1984 in Montevideo at the Direccion
Nacional de Tecnologia Nuclear. Due to corrosion issues, the reactor was shut down in 1985 and
the fuel elements were removed and placed in dry storage. In total, the fuel assemblies were
within the RU-1 reactor core for about 2920 days (~8 years), during which a total core burnup of
2.1 MWD/MTU and irradiation burnup of 0.08% was estimated. 23
As part of the Foreign Research Reactor Spent Nuclear Fuel Acceptance Program, SRS received
19 fuel assemblies from this reactor in 1998. Four of these Al-UNF assemblies each contained
ten fuel plates that were fastened together with rivets. These four assemblies were stored in the
K-Basin Dry Fuel Storage Area (DFSA) and were inspected in 1999, 2000, and 2001. 17, 22 Later
inspections (2004 and 2005) were performed once the item were transferred to the L-Basin DFSA.
In 2018, the fuel plates from assembly E-3 were removed and some fuel plates were transported
from the DFSA to SRNL.23-26
The Uruguay E-3 fuel plates are comprised of a fuel meat incased in cladding, which has been
metallurgically sealed by hot rolling (Figure 1).22 The entire fuel plate, including cladding, has
dimensions of 7.1 cm x 63.5 cm x 0.28 cm and a total weight of 413.636 g. The cladding is
composed of an Al-1100 alloy and has an approximate weight of 165.883 g. The fuel meat is a
UAlx-Alloy of dimensions 6.0 cm x 60.0 cm x 0.18 cm and total weight of 247.753 g. Of the total
weight of the fuel meat, 160.050 g was Al and 87.742 g was U (17.3914 g of U-235; 19.8%

enrichment).17, 22 The fuel meat is about 35 wt% U, which has been suggested to be more brittle
and more difficult to hot roll than U-Alx alloys below 25 wt% U.27

3.2 Experimental Design

Preliminary benchtop experiments will include initial non-radiological experiments with Al-1100
coupons and its individual alloying elements. Follow-up studies would include radiological
experiments with the Uruguay fuel plate. Given the dimensions of the fuel plate (7.1 cm x 63.5 cm
x 0.28 cm), the fuel plate will need to be machined to an appropriate size for benchtop reactions.
The Uruguay fuel provides a scientifically interesting twist in that the fuel meat is a U-Alx alloy clad
in Al. If this was an Al-clad U oxide-based fuel, the product remaining would simply be the U oxide

 5

which may retain its shape. Ideally, the chlorination system would leave metallic U particles as a
product. If aluminum remained in the product, other treatments would become necessary.

4.0 Conclusions

An assessment of the literature on recent low temperature chlorinations and a suitable fuel
candidate was completed. Based on initial discussions, the reaction kinetics should be assessed,
especially in regard to the quality of starting materials. Preliminary non-radiological and
radiological experiments can be performed in preparation towards improving work controls and
for processing of a test sample. Currently located at SRNL, a lightly irradiated, LEU U-Alx fuel
plate originally from the RU-1 Uruguay reactor was deemed to be viable for chlorination studies.
Future work scope would include cutting the Uruguay fuel plate to an appropriate bench-scale
size and assessing the extent the fuel and cladding/alloy elements can be separated.

5.0 Recommendations, Path Forward or Future Work

A proposed work scope would include initial non-radiological experiments prior to radiological
work. An assessment of the reaction kinetics, especially in relation to the quality of the starting
materials, would be performed.
 1. Preliminary non-radiological experiments
 a. Develop work controls
 b. Assess chlorination kinetics of Al-1100 coupons
 2. Radiological experiments
 a. Develop work controls
 b. Assess chlorination with U metal
 c. Assess chlorination of Al-1100 coupons with U present
 d. Machining the Uruguay fuel plate for bench-scale experiments
 e. Assess chlorination of Uruguay fuel
