Generation of a distal lung cancer organoid model using oncogenic ret-expressing induced pluripotent stem cells
Authors: Jin Wook Hwang, Christophe Desterke, Paul Marcoux, Mohamed Amin Bani, Frank Griscelli, Annelise Bennaceur-Griscelli, Ali G. Turhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe tyrosine kinase receptor RET is altered by genetic fusions in a subset of non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLCs). To determine if we can generate organoids with lung cancer features in this context, we used two iPSC lines overexpressing oncogenic RET-M918T and RET-C634Y mutations. Lung progenitor cells (LPCs) were generated after 18 days of culture, followed by air-liquid interface cultures giving rise to alveolar structures expressing SFTPC, TTF-1, MUC1, and CK5/6 at day +38. Transplantation of LPCs into NOD/SCID mice generated tumors harboring bronchial structures, ciliated cells, goblet cells, and the squamous cell carcinoma marker p40. Single-cell RNA sequencing analyses allowed us to determine the major genes involved with response or resistance to Selpercatinib, a major inhibitor targeting RET-altered lung cancers. These findings show that the iPSC-derived preclinical model can recapitulate some features of NSCLC and has significant potential for identifying new therapeutic targets in RET-inhibitor-refractory lung cancers.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related death. Despite significant advances in immunotherapy and targeted therapies, the prognosis of patients with NSCLC remains poor. In a small proportion of patients, oncogenic driver alterations are identified, allowing the generation of targeted therapies which are now recommended as first line therapies.1 Amongst these alterations, RET fusions are present in approximately 1–2% of NSCLC cases2 and are associated with the activation of important oncogenic pathways such as PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and STAT3, which contribute to tumor growth, metastasis, and resistance to current therapies.3,4 The RET gene encodes a receptor tyrosine kinase that regulates cell growth, survival, and differentiation. Fusions involving the RET gene are known to play a critical role in the tumorigenesis of several cancers, including NSCLC5,6,7 through dysregulated signaling. Although RET has emerged as a crucial therapeutic target for tumor suppression, the impact of RET alterations in lung tumorigenesis, particularly in mature lung tissue, remains poorly understood. Moreover, an appropriate model to study this has yet to be developed. Most previous studies have focused on in-cell line models5 or a lung progenitor model developed by the use of iPSC.8 In lung cancer, the most common RET alterations are fusion genes, the most common of them being the KIF5B-RET fusion gene.3 The mutated forms of RET have been shown to be driver oncogenes in other malignancies, especially in patients with hereditary medullary carcinoma.9,10,11 However, these mutations activate signaling pathways that can be very similar to those involved in RET-fusion genes, the most common signaling pathway activated for both being STAT3.5 The use of these experimental tools can therefore efficiently replace the knock-in of the RET fusions, which might be technically more difficult. We have previously reported the generation of RET-mutated iPSC from 3 patients with hereditary medullary carcinoma.9,10,11 In this work, we have used an overexpression of the mutated forms of RET using a lentiviral transfer strategy in a normal cord blood-derived IPSC line, PB-68. As mentioned above, in our previous work, we had shown the possibility of generating lung progenitor cells (LPCs) from these IPSCs with the identification of novel targets in this context.8 In this study, we aimed to generate mature lung organoids from these iPSCs, advancing the model to make it more relevant for target discovery. We slightly modified previously published differentiation protocols12 and generated LPCs to induce terminally differentiated distal organoids. To investigate their molecular profile, we employed single-cell RNA sequencing, immunohistochemistry analysis, and in vivo transplantation into NOD/SCID mice.

Our findings demonstrate that RET-mutated iPSC-derived distal lung organoids exhibit characteristic features of NSCLC, including the activation of tumorigenic signaling pathways such as RET, STAT3, and increased expression of tumor markers such as PD-L1, MUC1, and C1QTNF6. These results highlight the potential of this distant lung organoid model as a valuable tool for studying RET-altered NSCLC and testing novel therapeutic strategies targeting RET.

Results

Differentiation of RET-Mutated iPSCs into lung progenitor cells

To successfully establish mature lung organoids from iPSCs harboring RET mutations, a modified conventional protocol12 was used (Figure 1A; Table S1). Differentiation was initiated by the induction of definitive endoderm (DE), followed by anterior foregut endoderm (AFE) specification and progression to LPCs (Figure 1B). Immunostaining revealed that the LPC marker NKX2-1 was expressed more strongly in LPCs than in iPSCs in both the RET and control groups. The pRET marker was expressed more strongly in the RET group than in the control group, both in iPSCs and LPCs (Figures 1C and 1D). Z-projection confocal images of LPCs revealed strong expression of pRET (green) in the RET group, as shown in Figure S1A. Quantification of mean fluorescence intensity ratios revealed significant upregulation of pRET in both cell types. In iPSCs, the pRET ratio increased from 0.07 ± 0.07 (control) to 0.66 ± 0.15 (RET) (Figure S1B). These data show that RET iPSCs and LPCs generated according to this protocol exhibit stronger pRET expression compared to the control. Furthermore, successful LPC differentiation was confirmed by NKX2-1 expression. Tumorigenic potential of RET-overexpressing organoids

To determine whether RET-overexpressing organoid structures can recapitulate some lung cancer features observed in vivo, we transplanted RET-918 LPCs subcutaneously into NOD/SCID mice. At 16–20 weeks post-transplantation, the mice were sacrificed, and the harvested structures were subjected to histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses (Figures 3 and 4). We observed that RET-918 LPCs (3/4) generated larger teratoma-like tumors, whereas no tumors could be detected in the control IPSC WT-group (0/4) (Figures 3B and 3C). Control organoids showed formation of tissue structures, but were negative for adenocarcinoma markers by IHC (Figure 4). The size of the tumors is indicated in the Tables S2 and S3. Interestingly, the pathological analysis of the LPCs-derived teratomas showed clearly a bronchial organoid with the presence of extracellular mucus (Figure 3D) mature cartilage (Figure 3E), ciliated cells (Figure 3F), and Alcian blue goblet cells identified by mucin production (Figure 3G). Importantly, basal cells expressed p40, a marker of squamous lung cancer (Figure 3H). Goblet cells were also positive for Trefoil factor 3 (TFF3) staining (Figure 3I). Immunohistochemistry

LPCs matured into fully differentiated lung cells after 16–20 weeks of subcutaneous transplantation, maintaining organized epithelial structures (SFTPC+, MUC1+ cells). In contrast, RET organoids exhibited disorganized architecture and increased cellular density (Figures 4A–4C), indicative of tumorigenic phenotypes. Furthermore, RET-mutated organoids showed aberrant expression of epithelial markers, including MUC1, as well as lung carcinoma markers phospho-RET, PD-L1, SFTPC, TTF-1, and Cytokeratin 5/6 (Figures 4A–4C).

Quantitative analysis showed 3- to 4-fold increases in mean fluorescence intensity ratios of pRET, PD-L1, SFTPC, TTF-1, and CY5/6 in RET LPCs-derived organoids compared with control LPCs-derived organoid grafts from WT IPSCs (Figure S4). Specifically, in control organoids, the pRET ratio was 0.19 ± 0.03, compared with 0.77 ± 0.07 in RET organoids (p < 0.01). PD-L1 ratios increased from 0.34 ± 0.14 in controls to 0.77 ± 0.13 in RET organoids (p < 0.01) (Figure S4A). SFTPC ratios rose from 0.34 ± 0.05 to 0.86 ± 0.16 (p < 0.01). TTF-1 ratios increased from 0.22 ± 0.09 in controls to 0.82 ± 0.07 in RET organoids (p < 0.01) (Figure S4B). CY5/6 ratios increased from 0.22 ± 0.04 to 0.81 ± 0.19 (p < 0.01) (Figure S4C). In contrast, MUC1 levels were similar between control organoids (0.64 ± 0.16) and RET organoids (0.78 ± 0.15) (Figure S4C), reflecting its high baseline expression in control organoids. These results indicate that RET LPCs-derived organoids express lung adenocarcinoma-specific markers while maintaining expression of basal epithelial markers such as MUC1.

To investigate transcriptomic shifts induced by Selpercatinib13 in LC2/ad cells, we analyzed the top 70 NE signature genes from scRNAseq trajectory in dataset GSE261830. Hierarchical clustering (Figure 7A) and PCA (Figure 7B) revealed a clear separation between control and treated (Selpercatinib) samples, with PC1 accounting for 64.92% of the variance. Gene loadings on PC1 enabled the classification of 20 “Selpercatinib sensitivity” genes and 25 “Selpercatinib resistance” genes, based on a threshold of ±0.1 (Table S4; Figure 7C). Functional network analysis showed that “sensitivity” genes were significantly enriched in Reactome Metabolism pathways (FDR = 0.04) (Figure 7D), while “resistance” genes were associated with nervous system development (GO::BP, FDR = 2.73e−5) (Figure 7E), suggesting distinct biological programs underlying treatment response. Supporting this stratification, several genes with high positive PC1 loadings and implicated in nervous system development, including SOX4, APP, MARCKSL1, and DDR1, were predominantly expressed in treated samples, whereas negatively loaded genes and implicated in metabolism, such as HSP90AA1, OAZ1, and ENO2, were enriched in controls. Analysis of LPCs in response to selpercatinib

To determine a potentially differential sensitivity of RET-altered LPCs as compared to control LPCs, we have treated the day + 18 LPCs with Selpercatinib at concentrations of 0.1 μM, 1 μM, and 10 μM for 72 h followed by analysis of Caspase 3 and pSTAT3 expression (Figures S6 and S7).

As can be seen in Figures S6 and S7, Caspase-3 expression was significantly increased in RET LPCs treated with Selpercatinib at 1 μM and 10 μM compared with control LPCs (p < 0.01 and p < 0.005, respectively), whereas no significant difference was observed at 0.1 μM, suggesting a threshold-dependent apoptotic response. In control LPCs, Caspase-3 levels (mean ± SD) were 0.14 ± 0.10 (n = 8) at 0.1 μM, 0.15 ± 0.15 (n = 5) at 1 μM, and 0.14 ± 0.04 (n = 6) at 10 μM. In RET LPCs, corresponding values were 0.05 ± 0.01 (n = 5), 0.36 ± 0.05 (n = 3), and 0.32 ± 0.10 (n = 3), respectively.

pSTAT3 expression was significantly higher in RET LPCs than in control LPCs at all tested concentrations. In control LPCs, pSTAT3 levels were 0.16 ± 0.07 (n = 8) at 0.1 μM, 0.03 ± 0.01 (n = 5) at 1 μM, and 0.10 ± 0.03 (n = 6) at 10 μM. In RET LPCs, phosphoSTAT3 levels were 0.58 ± 0.09 (n = 5), 0.17 ± 0.05 (n = 3), and 0.19 ± 0.07 (n = 3), respectively, indicating constitutively elevated STAT3 signaling in RET LPCs that was partially reduced following Selpercatinib treatment.

Consistent with these findings, Selpercatinib induced apoptotic cell death in RET-mutated lung organoids compared with controls, as indicated by increased Caspase-3 expression Figures S6 and S7), supporting the utility of this model for drug screening.

Analysis of PD-L1 expression in RET-mutated LPCs

To determine the expression of PD-L1 and its potential relation with Selpercatinib treatment, we have analyzed by immunochemistry, the expression of PD-L1 (Figures S8 and S9).

PD-L1 expression was significantly higher in RET LPCs compared to control at 0.1 μM (control, 0.20 ± 0.03, n = 3; RET, 0.53 ± 0.01, n = 3, p < 0.01) and at 1 μM (control, 0.14 ± 0.06, n = 7; RET, 0.55 ± 0.12, n = 6, p < 0.0001), whereas at 10 μM the levels became similar to control (control, 0.21 ± 0.07, n = 7; RET, 0.24 ± 0.07, n = 3). As can be seen in Figures S8 and S9, the RET LPCs expressed very high levels of PD-L1 as compared to control LPCs. The expression of PD-L1 did not change when the organoids were treated with different doses of Selpercatinib. As the LPC system is essentially devoid of immunocompetent cells, it was impossible to determine if this overexpression correlates with an immune resistance.

Discussion

In the field of lung cancer therapy, the detection of oncogenic driver alterations represents a major hope for the development of targeted therapies.1 The most common of the genomic alterations identified to be drivers in NSCLC include the mutation of EGFR, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, RAS, and ROS pathways14 and targeted therapies have already been introduced to the clinic.14 One of the major oncogenic drivers in this context is the EGFR which can be targeted using Osimertinib15 and more recently with combined use of Amivantanab plus Lazertinib which is of superior efficacy as compared to Osimertinib.16

Similarly, several altered forms of ALK have been involved as drivers in NSCLC.17 The first targeted therapy for ALK-altered NSCLC was Crizotinib.17 More selective ALK therapies have subsequently been described, such as Alectinib, which appears more efficient as compared to Crizotinib.18

In advanced RET-fusion positive lung cancers, the first line of therapies includes RET inhibitors such as Selpercatinib and Praseltinib.19 However, the response rate to these therapies is approximately 40%19 and resistance develops essentially due to the secondary activation of RET-independent pathways.19,20 Discovery of novel targets in RET-altered lung cancers is therefore an unmet need. In this study, we successfully generated mature RET-mutated overexpressing iPSC-derived lung organoids and characterized their molecular features. We observed that the RET mutation activated critical oncogenic pathways, including TP63, pSTAT3, PD-L1, C1QTNF6, and MUC1 (Figures S2 and S3), which are typically associated with NSCLC. This suggests that RET signaling plays a significant role in driving the development of NSCLC and could serve as an important therapeutic target.

In our previous study,8 we established a RET-driven tumorigenesis model using LPCs derived from RET mutant iPSCs. Although this model contributed to understanding tumorigenesis at the level of RET lung progenitors, it failed to recapitulate the complexity of NSCLC at the terminally differentiated lung cell development stage. This study expands on prior research by utilizing mature lung organoids derived from RET-mutated iPSCs, which formed alveolar-like structures after 38 days or 60 days of culture (Figures 2B and 2C). Unlike progenitor cells, these mature lung organoids successfully replicated the architecture and cellular diversity characteristic of NSCLC, including markers such as SFTPC, MUC1, C1QTNF6, and PD-L1 (Figure 2D; Figure S3). Notably, RET-mutated lung organoids exhibited phosphorylation of the oncogenic signaling pathway RET (Figure 4A), activation of its downstream effector STAT3, and tumor progression mechanisms such as immune evasion marked by increased PD-L1 expression. This supports previous work highlighting the role of STAT3 signaling in NSCLC progression and immune evasion.21,22,23 Interestingly, our findings in this organoid-derived RET-overexpressing lung cancer model show the overexpression of immune checkpoint marker PD-L1 (Figure 4A), which has been widely recognized as a crucial player in immune escape mechanisms in cancer.24,25

Our study also shows the feasibility of a reproducible experimental tool focused on the development of mature lung organoids, which formed distinct alveolar structures through the differentiation of RET-mutated iPSCs toward distal lung cells. Notably, In vivo, RET activation led to increased levels of NSCLC-related markers, including PD-L1, SFTPC, TTF-1 MUC1, and Cytokeratin 5/6 (Figures 4A–4C). These findings suggest that mature lung organoids derived from RET-mutated iPSCs more accurately reflect the complexity of NSCLC compared to earlier lung progenitor models. One limitation of these models, as compared to primary cancer organoids, is the limited representation of the tumor microenvironment and immunocompetent cells.

In single-cell RNA sequencing analyses of the distal lung organoids, we have identified markers of terminal differentiation consistent with the presence of Type 1 and Type 2 alveolar cells and goblet cells (Figure 5C). The distribution of the expression of these markers was not different between RET918 and RET634 organoids. There was a clear difference in terms of NE precursor cell numbers in both RET918 and RET634 organoids as compared to control cells (Figure 5D). In pseudotime analyses, there was a clear trajectory pattern common to both RET918 and RET634 organoids, which differed significantly from normal controls (Figure 6B). These results translated to a differential signature with the identification of genes significantly overexpressed in RET-mutated organoids. Amongst the genes identified SOX426 and MARCKS27 are known to be involved in the progression of lung cancer. Similarly, the expression of STMN1 and 4 identified using our RET918 and RET634 organoids are known to be of dismal prognosis in NSCLC.28 Therefore, these findings underline the validity of the use of lung cancer organoid technology with the goal of identifying lung cancer targets in primary patient tumors. We then concentrated our efforts to specifically in RET-driven cancers and we integrated our differentially expressed RET-signature to data generated in a lung cancer cell line data in which a Selpercatinib-resistance and sensitive signature has been reported.13 As can be seen in Figure 7, this experiment allowed to discriminate a signature correlating with our RET M918T and RET C634Y signatures, identifying a signature of Selpercatinib-resistant and sensitive tumors (Figure 7C). Interestingly, as can also be seen in Figure (Figure 7E) several genes identified by this approach have been reported in lung cancer patient cohorts as markers of therapy resistance, such as CD24,29 DUSP1,30 and DDR1.31

As can be seen in Figure 7, analysis of NE signature genes in LC-2/ad cells revealed distinct transcriptional programs associated with Selpercatinib response. Sensitive genes were enriched in metabolic pathways, while resistant genes were linked to nervous system development, suggesting these programs may drive drug sensitivity and resistance. Notably, some resistant genes, including SOX4, APP, MARCKSL1, and DDR1, have been reported as markers of therapy resistance in patients with lung cancer, supporting the relevance of this model.

Our data suggest that this set of genes could be used to predict Selpercatinib resistance in patients with RET-driven lung cancers.

Interestingly, single-cell RNAseq analyses also showed evidence of NE differentiation of the distal organoids, a feature characterizing SCLC and carcinoid lung tumors. To determine if our organoids express some genes involved in NE lung tumors, we have analyes the expression of hASH-1 and SYP, both reported to be expressed in SCLC and carcinoid lung tumors. As shown in the Figure S5, both genes are highly expressed in RET-mutated lung organoids, suggesting that the relevance of our model could be extended to these cancers and to the field of lung cancers expressing both NSCLC and SCLC features.32

This model recapitulating RET-driven lung cancer both in vivo and in vitro, allowed us to perform drug testing. We have used the effects of Selpercatinib on the RET-mutation overexpressing lung organoids. Interestingly, as can be seen in the Figures S6 and S7, Selpercatinib teatezd organoids exhibited an increase of Caspase-3 suggesting apoptotic cell death with a concomitant decrease of the expression of phospho-STAT3 (Figures S6 and S7). These results suggest that the model of lung organoids described here could be used for drug screening.

RET alterations identified in NSCLC are essentially RET fusions and not single mutations, such as those identified in patients with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2.33 However, the RET mutations, such as RET M918T and RET C634Y, induce the same signaling pathways as RET-fusions such as KIF5B-RET and CCDC6-RET,20 with the activation of RAS-MAPK signaling. In our model, the overexpression of two oncogenic RET mutations in normal iPSCs gave rise to an iPSC-derived distal lung organoid model recapitulating several aspects of the NSCLC biology. The model presented here, to our knowledge, represents the first RET-mutated iPSC-derived lung organoid model allowing the identification of a set of genes involved in Selpercatinib resistance. It is well established that not all patients with RET alterations respond to Selpercatinib, which induces in different series an overall response rate of 68%.20 Therefore, this model has the potential to be an important preclinical tool for testing new drug candidates and identifying novel biomarkers for refractory RET-driven NSCLC.

Limitations of the study

One of the limitations of this technology is the fact that the IPSC-derived organoids do not fully recapitulate the tumor microenvironment with its different immunosuppressive components. From this regard, it is difficult to evaluate the effects of checkpoint inhibitor antibodies on the organoid model. Although the oncogenic RET mutations activate signaling pathways similar to those activated by RET fusions occurring in patients with NSCLC,20 there might be functional differences that need to be explored by the generation of IPSCs from tumors with RET fusions. However, this task is probably difficult to achieve as it requires the collection of viable tumor tissue and the knowledge of the RET fusion (such as KIF5B-RET) expressed in the tumor of the patient.

In summary, our RET-mutated iPSC-derived lung organoids not only provide a valuable preclinical model for studying RET-driven lung cancer but also offer an excellent platform for screening novel targeted therapies. Future studies are needed to explore how RET inhibitors might interact with other molecular pathways to improve NSCLC patient outcomes. This model could also help in identifying new biomarkers of resistance to Selpercatinib in patients with RET-driven NSCLCs.

This model could be strengthened in future studies by the use of patient-specific IPSC-derived lung organoid models, which could allow for testing the potential of checkpoint inhibitors or combination therapies to generate predictive strategies of lung cancer therapy outcomes.

Human iPSCs expressing two oncogenic RET mutations (C634Y and M918T) were generated from a normal cord blood-derived iPSC line using lentiviral transduction, as described previously.34 All procedures were approved by the Ethical Committee CPP of Bicetre Hospital (Ref: PP-13-001).

Ethical statement

The project was approved by INSERM and the Ethical Committee CPP of Bicetre Hospital (Ref: PP-13-001).

Method details

Generation of iPSCs

iPSCs expressing two oncogenic RET mutations were generated from a normal cord blood-derived iPSC line using lentiviral transduction, as described in our previous study with the approval of the Ethical Committee.34 Briefly, Lenti-X 293T cells were used as the packaging cell line, with psPAX2.2 and pMD2.G serving as the packaging and envelop vectors, respectively. RET-C634Y and RET-M918T lentiviral constructs were purchased from Vector Builder (Guangzhou, China) and after lentiviral transduction and selection, iPSCs were cultured on Geltrex-coated dishes (A1413201; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch, France), and Essential 8 Flex Medium (A2858501; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch, France) with medium change every other day. Pluripotency of the iPSCs overexpression C634Y and M918T was determined as previously described.34

Generation of lung progenitor cells (LPCs)

This procedure was modified based on the protocol described by Leibel et al.12 To differentiate iPSCs into LPCs, the cells were cultured on 12-well or 6-well plates coated with Geltrex. When the culture reached 70–80% confluency, cells treated with definitive endoderm (DE) induction medium. The latter was replaced daily for 4 days. Following this, anterior foregut endoderm (AFE) induction medium was used and replaced daily until day 7. From day 8 to day 18, the cells were treated with LPCs induction medium, with medium change every two days and the culture was pursued for 50 days with the medium being changed every two days.

Generation of distal lung organoids

LPCs (at day 18) were washed once with PBS, and all cells were detached from the culture dish using Accutase (0.5 mL per 12-well). After transfer to a 15 mL conical tube, cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at room temperature and resuspended in induction medium. 200 μL of Matrigel per 4.0–8.0 × 10ˆ4 cells per transwell were added and allowed to solidify. After Matrigel gelation, 1 mL of induction medium was added to the lower chamber of the well and with replacement of the medium every other day for 6 days. On day 23, the medium was replaced with branching medium with continuous medium change every other day for 6 days. On day 29, the branching medium was replaced with maturation medium with medium change every other day for 38 to 60 days to facilitate differentiation into mature lung organoids.

Immunofluorescence staining

Samples were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% formaldehyde in PBS for 60 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint-Quentin-Fallavier, France) in PBS, and blocked with 10% serum. Primary antibodies were diluted in PBS with 10% serum at the following concentrations: NKX2.1/TTF-1 (1:100, MA513961, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), Phospho-RET (Tyr1096) (1:100, PA5105796, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch, France). SFTPC (1:100, PA571680, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch, France), CD31(1:100, ab24590, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), pSTAT3 (1:100, 9145 T, Ozyme, Saint-Cyr-l’École, France), TP63 (1:100, ab124762, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), C1QTNF6 (1:100, MAB16078, Abnova, Taipei, Taiwan), MUC1 (1:100, ab45167, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), PD-L1 (1:100, 66248-1-IG, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), cytokeratin 5/6 (1:100, ab17133, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Synaptophysin (SYP, 1:100, ab309493, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Achaete-scute homolog 1 antibody (ASH1, 1:100, ab211327, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and Caspase-3 (1:100, 9662S, Ozyme, Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines, France). Samples were incubated with secondary antibodies in antibody dilution buffer, then washed in PBS. Nuclei were labeled with DAPI (D9542, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint-Quentin-Fallavier, France) mounting medium. Visualization and capture were performed with a Leica confocal microscope and LAS AF software (v3.2). Negative controls were the experiments with secondary antibodies, yielding no staining (data not shown).

LPC transplantation experiments

Animal experimentation was performed in accordance with regulations in France. Protocols were approved by the Ethical Committee for Animal Experimentation (CEEA n°26). Experiments were performed using female mice aged 6 to 7 weeks. After anesthesia, LPCs generated using the iPSC-derived differentiation protocol and confirmed to express NKX2-1 were transplanted (500 cells per mouse) in NOD/SCID mice.

Pathological analyses

After 16 or 20 weeks, tissues were isolated and processed for histological analysis. They were fixed in paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. 3 μm sections were deparaffinized and then Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed. Alcian blue staining was performed using a ready-to-use kit (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Immunohistochemical staining was performed manually or using the BenchMark Ultra automated stainer (Ventana Medical Systems, Inc.), depending on the antibody, following the manufacturer’s protocols. The following primary antibodies were used: p40 (prediluted, 790–4950, Ventana, Tucson, AZ, USA), TFF3 (1:500, BSB3820, BioSB via Diagomics, Santa Barbara, CA, USA), Phospho-RET (Tyr1096) (1:100, PA5105796, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch, France), PD-L1 (1:100, 66248-1-IG, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), SFTPC (1:100, PA571680, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch, France), NKX2.1/TTF-1 (1:100, MA513961, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), MUC1 (1:100, ab45167, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), cytokeratin 5/6 (1:100, ab17133, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), then washed three times in PBS. The sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies in antibody dilution buffer for 1 h, then washed three times in PBS. Nuclei were labeled with DAPI (D9542, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) mounting medium. Visualization and capture were performed using a NIKON microscope (NIKON, Minato, Japan) and a Leica confocal microscope with LAS AF software (v3.2).

Single cell RNA-Sequencing experiments

Single cell RNA-sequencing GEX (10× genomics) experiments were performed on PB68-WT and RET mutated (M918T and C634Y) IPSC-derived terminally differentiated distal lung organoids. Reads were sequenced on Illumina sequencer and Fastq files were demultiplexed and filtrated with Cell Ranger pipeline (10× Genomics). Downstream single cell analyses were performed in R software environment version 4.3.3. Integration of the three experiment datasets was performed with Seurat R-package version 5.1.0 by canonical correlation.35 Integrated Seurat object was submitted to classical Seurat pipeline comprising the following steps: NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, ScaleData, RunPCA. After these steps, clusters were determined on the 10 first dimensions of the PCA with assessment of their eigenvalues on ElbowPlot and resolution fixed to 0.3. UMAP dimension reduction was also processed on 10 first dimensions of PCA. Best one hundred markers of each clusters were extracted with Cell-ID R-package version 1.10.1.36 Cell identities for cell from lung origin were extracted from C8 cell identity MSigDb database37 to perform cell cluster annotation with ClusterProfiler R-package version 4.10.1.38,39 Seurat object was restrained to cluster 1 to analyze fetal neuroendocrine precursor cells and transformed as SingleCellExperiment object.40 Cell trajectory analysis was performed on neuroendocrine precursor cells with slingshot R-package version 2.10.0.41 Functional enrichment was performed on the seventy best top markers of the trajectory selected by general additive model (gam) regression of slingshot pseudotime still with ClusterProfiler R-package and with Gene Ontology Biological Process database.42

To compare the experimental results obtained in our RET-derived lung organoids we wished to compare our transcriptome results with that obtained with the lung cancer cell line LC-2/ad which is a unique cell line expressing as the driver oncogene the CCD6-RET fusion. This cell line has been used as a major model to uncover the genes involved with the RET inhibitor Selpercatinib. We have therefore compared our results with the transcriptome generated with a RET inhibitor. We have used Transcript per million (TPM) normalized matrix of RNA-sequencing from GSE261830 was downloaded at the following address: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE261830 (accessed on 2025, January 17th).13 This study described the LC-2/ad cell line is a lung cancer cell line harboring by CCDC6-RET gene fusion. RNA-sequencing matrix of LC-2/ad parental cell and LC-2/ad treated with Selpercatinib (48 h) was transformed log2+1 pseudocounts. Starting from top70 signature of RET-mutated neuroendocrine precursor cell (NEP) (scRNAseq signature) a supervised differential expression analysis between control and treated conditions was performed with transpipe R-package version 1.4 (available at the address: https://github.com/cdesterke/transpipe14, accessed on 2025, January 17th), based on LIMMA algorithm.43 Significantly up or down-regulated genes were determined based on a False discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-value less than 0.05 and logarithm 2-Fold Change absolute value over 0.5. NEP sensible (down-regulated by Selpercatinib) and resistant genes (up-regulated by Selpercatinib) from LC-2/Ad cells were integrated in String Network application version 12 to identify protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks.44 Functional network enrichment for sensible and resistant genes was respectively performed on KEGG and Gene Ontology Biological Process databases.
