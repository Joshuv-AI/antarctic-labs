Regional analgesia for hip fractures in emergency departments in Johannesburg, South Africa: a prospective cross-sectional survey assessing doctors’ knowledge, attitudes and practices
Authors: Robert Klaus Uhrich, Craig Beringer, Louis Chadinha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to describe the knowledge, attitudes and practices of emergency department (ED) doctors regarding regional analgesia (RA) for hip fractures and to compare these across levels of experience.DesignA prospective, multicentre survey-based cross-sectional study.SettingSurveys were collected at the EDs of three university-affiliated hospitals in Johannesburg, South Africa.Participants80 surveys were collected. Participants included all ED doctors. Doctors completing their internship years were excluded.Primary and secondary outcomesThe primary objectives of this study were to describe the knowledge, attitudes and practices of participants regarding the use of RA in hip fractures in the ED; to compare these across different levels of experience based on age, rank and number of years working in the ED; to describe perceived barriers to its use and to describe the frequency of RA use for hip fracture among participants.ResultsThirty-nine per cent of respondents reported having received hip fracture-specific RA training, of whom 52% felt adequately prepared. Knowledge scores (median, IQR) were low for single best answer questions (50%, 39–67) and complications (50%, 38–75), but higher for contraindications (83%, 67–100) and indications (75%, 50–100). Attitudes were broadly positive: 93% viewed RA as safe and effective, and 88% felt all ED doctors should be proficient. Training was mostly informal (94%), predominantly via bedside teaching from an emergency medicine colleague (65%). RA for hip fractures was underused, with 39% reporting never performing it and 27% reporting seldom use. The most cited barrier was a lack of skills (49%), which decreased with increasing ED experience (p=0.012).ConclusionDespite self-reported generally strong positive attitudes towards RA for hip fractures, it remains underused. Insufficient skills and training were the most common self-reported barriers. Future research could be aimed at assessing the impact of standardised training and departmental protocols on clinical practice.Keywords: ACCIDENT & EMERGENCY MEDICINE; Africa South of the Sahara; Cross-Sectional Studies; Fractures, Bone; PAIN MANAGEMENT

Introduction

Hip fractures are common presentations to the emergency department (ED) among older adults.1 The Royal College of Physicians National Hip Fracture Database recorded 72 160 hip fractures in 2022, continuing the upward trend seen in previous years.2 In the USA, among adults older than 65 years, there were 318 797 hip fracture-related ED visits in 2019.3 With an ageing population and a projected rise in the incidence of hip fractures, both in a South African and global context, ED practitioners need to be proficient in the management of all aspects of these fractures.4 5 Not least of which includes appropriate pain management, as patients often report moderate to high pain scores at presentation.6 7

Apart from the frequently used forms of oral and parenteral analgesia, regional analgesia (RA), such as the femoral nerve block (FNB) and fascia iliaca compartment block (FICB), has been identified as a safe and effective pain management tool for hip fractures in the ED. It has been shown to improve patient satisfaction, reduce opioid requirements and provide localised, highly effective and long-lasting pain control.7,11

The utility of RA in the ED environment is recognised and supported by a growing number of professional bodies, with guidelines advocating for its use and evidence suggesting that ultrasound (US) guided FNBs are feasible in the ED.812,15

However, research indicates that RA remains underused in the ED, despite ED physicians being aware of its benefits.16 17 Frequently cited barriers include limited familiarity and training, lack of confidence, absence of departmental protocols for RA and time constraints.16 18

Identifying current practices as well as potential barriers is essential to increase the uptake of RA in hip fractures and to improve patient care. The positive impact of enrolling ED physicians in a RA training programme, specifically for hip fractures, has been demonstrated in a study by Lee et al in 2021.19 Casey et al demonstrated a significant increase in the use of FICB in hip fractures after the implementation of a geriatric hip fracture programme.17

Studies specifically addressing how knowledge and attitudes regarding RA in hip fractures translate into practice are limited, and to our knowledge, this has not been studied in a South African setting. The aim of this study was to assess these among doctors working in EDs in the greater Johannesburg area, South Africa. Our hypothesis was that despite knowledge and attitudes regarding RA in hip fractures being adequate, it remains underused in this setting.

The primary objectives of this study were to describe the knowledge, attitudes and practices of participants regarding the use of RA in hip fractures in the ED; to compare these across different levels of experience based on age, rank and number of years working in the ED; to describe perceived barriers to its use and to describe the frequency of RA use for hip fracture among participants.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a prospective, multicentre cross-sectional study, using descriptive and comparative statistical analyses.

Data collection occurred between 01 April and 30 September 2024 in the mixed EDs (managing trauma, orthopaedic and medical patients within the same unit) of three academic public hospitals in the greater Johannesburg area, affiliated with the University of the Witwatersrand, Division of Emergency Medicine. Consultants and registrars affiliated with the University of the Witwatersrand, Division of Emergency Medicine, but not based at these hospitals, were also invited to participate.

Selection of participants and study size

Participants were ED doctors at various levels of training, including community service medical officers, medical officers, emergency medicine (EM) registrars as well as specialist EM physicians. Doctors completing their internship were excluded due to limited clinical experience.

Based on staffing rosters at each site as well as the number of EM registrars and consultants in the Division of EM, the study population was estimated to be 100 doctors.

As this was a descriptive cross-sectional survey of a finite population, a practical approach was taken to maximise participation, with all eligible doctors invited to participate. A convenience sampling approach was used based on availability at departmental meetings and site visits. Eighty responses were collected, representing 80% of the eligible population. No formal sample size calculation was performed as the study was descriptive in nature and not designed to test a specific hypothesis or identify independent predictors or practice.

Data measurement

Data were collected at departmental meetings, via the distribution of an anonymous paper-based survey. Participation was voluntary, with the survey introduction emphasising that non-participation would have no negative consequences. To minimise any sense of coercion or observation bias, participants were allowed to complete surveys in their own time during or immediately after the meeting. Completed surveys were returned immediately to the primary researcher via a sealed collection box. No identifiable information was collected.

As no validated survey instrument encompassing all study objectives was identified, the questionnaire was developed following a review of similar published studies assessing RA practices in EDs,16 18 with incorporation of relevant domains and adapted to the local context. Questions assessing theoretical knowledge were informed by recognised educational resources in regional anaesthesia.20

The questionnaire was reviewed and refined for clarity and content through expert consultation and informal testing. Face and content validity were assessed by experienced EM consultants with expertise in regional anaesthesia and point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), as well as familiarity with survey-based research and medical education. Further, the questionnaire was reviewed and approved as part of the institutional study-protocol process. Formal psychometric validation was not undertaken.

The survey (online supplemental appendix 1) consisted of three sections. Section 1 assessed demographic details (three questions addressing age, rank and years of ED experience). Knowledge was assessed in Section 2. Section 2a assessed the source of knowledge and aspects of training (eight questions). Theoretical knowledge was assessed via 12 multiple-choice questions. Nine of these (Section 2b) had single-best answers, scored as one mark for correct and zero marks for incorrect answers (maximum score=9 marks). The other three questions (Section 2c) had multiple correct answers, and negative marking was applied to discourage guessing. Each correctly identified statement scored two marks, with an incorrect statement receiving −1 mark.

Section 3 assessed attitudes, aimed at the doctors’ perceptions regarding the utility and use of RA in hip fractures (17 questions). Section 4 assessed practices, aimed at identifying frequency of use, potential facilitators and barriers to RA use (eight questions). A combination of Likert scales and multiple-choice questions was used in Sections 3 and 4. Likert scales were ranked from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Bias

The study was vulnerable to response and selection bias. To address this, the surveys remained anonymous. Further, an attempt was made to ensure questions were clearly formulated and leading questions were avoided. The primary researcher visited the study sites on multiple occasions to reach as many participants as possible. Non-response bias was not formally addressed.

Statistical methods

Data entry was performed by the primary researcher and cross-checked for accuracy to minimise transcription errors. Survey data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analysed, tabulated and plotted using the statistical programming software R (V.4.4.2).

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant demographics, training history, knowledge scores and responses to attitude/practice items. The knowledge test score was the only continuous variable, which was summarised using the median and IQR.

The remaining variables were categorical and were summarised using counts and percentages.

Comparative analyses were performed to explore differences based on ED experience, grouped as <1 year, 1–2 years, 3–5 years and ≥6 years.

Continuous data (knowledge scores) were analysed using Kruskal-Wallis tests, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Holm correction where significant omnibus differences were detected.

Categorical data were assessed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate, with Holm-corrected post hoc tests for pairwise comparisons.

Experience categories were treated as a nominal and not as an ordinal scale.

Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of p<0.05. Missing data were not imputed, and analyses were performed using available-case data only. The number of respondents for each item is reported where this varied.

Ethical considerations

Ethics approval was granted by the University of the Witwatersrand Human Research Ethics Committee (No. M231022 M240409-A-0001), with additional approval from the ethics committees of the relevant study sites. Participant consent was implied by voluntary participation.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in this study.

Knowledge

Of the respondents, 45/80 (56%) reported having received some form of RA training. Among these, 31/45 (69%) had received training specifically relating to RA for hip fractures, and 16/31 (52%) felt that this training adequately equipped them to safely and effectively perform RA for hip fractures.

Questions 21 to 23, which had multiple correct answers with negative marking applied, assessed knowledge of contraindications, indications and complications of FNB/FICB. Median scores were 5/6 (IQR 4–6) for contraindications, 3/4 (IQR 2–4) for indications and 4/8 (IQR 3–6) for complications. Of note, 8/79 (10%) did not recognise hip fracture as an indication for performing a FNB/FICB.

Attitudes

All respondents (79/79, 100%) agreed that informed consent and anatomical knowledge are vital when performing RA for hip fractures.

The majority agreed that all independently practising ED doctors should be proficient in the use of RA for hip fractures (70/80, 88%), that it should be performed using US guidance whenever possible (79/80, 99%) and that it is safe to use in the ED (74/80, 93%).

Similarly, the majority agreed that RA for hip fractures plays a large and important role in pain management in the ED (74/80, 93%), that it reduces requirements for opioids (72/80, 90%) as well as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (66/80, 83%).

Although most agreed that RA for hip fractures improves patient satisfaction (71/80, 89%), less than half agreed that patients are receptive to it (38/80, 48%).

Perceived preparedness to perform the techniques was limited. Only 11/80 (14%) felt their training had been sufficient, and 25/79 (32%) were comfortable performing RA for hip fractures, although 37/80 (46%) agreed that they felt comfortable using POCUS to perform RA for hip fractures. Only 19/80 (24%) reported that their ED had a protocol for using RA in hip fractures.

Despite the above, 48/80 (60%) disagreed that RA for hip fractures is too technically challenging, and only 7/80 (9%) felt it was too time-consuming.

Practices

Most (52/79, 65%) respondents indicated that they never or seldom perform RA for hip fractures.

However, the majority agreed that adequate equipment (51/79, 65%) including US machines (66/78, 85%) were available to perform RA for hip fractures.

The most common barrier to use was a lack of skills (39/79, 49%) followed by ‘other’ (31/79, 39%). Of the 31 respondents who selected ‘other’, 18/31 (58%) indicated a lack of exposure to the technique as well as a lack of need (low patient burden).

Among those performing RA, US-guided FNB was the most used (33/50, 66%), followed by US-guided FICB (14/50, 28%). The majority (46/57, 81%) indicated that it took them less than 15 min to perform the procedure.

Comparison

Knowledge, attitudes and practices were compared across four ED experience categories: <1 year, 1–2 years, 3–5 years and >6 years working in an ED.

Prior RA training and self-reported adequacy of training did not differ significantly with ED experience. However, hip fracture-specific RA training was significantly more common among those with ≥6 years of experience than those with <1 year (Fisher’s exact test, p=0.012; post hoc p=0.017).

Informal bedside teaching was the most common training format across all experience levels. However, increased experience was associated with exposure to more diverse training formats and RA techniques.

For theoretical knowledge, only question 23 (complications of FNB/FICB) showed a statistically significant difference (Kruskal-Wallis, p=0.029), with the <1 year group scoring lower than the 1–2 year group (post hoc p=0.012). No other knowledge items differed by experience.

Attitudes were largely similar between groups. While agreement that RA for hip fractures improves patient satisfaction did not differ significantly, perceptions of patient amenability did (Fisher’s exact p=0.007), with the greatest agreement seen in the ≥6-year group. Attitudes towards preparedness, confidence and perceived adequacy of training showed a positive but non-significant trend with increasing experience. Across all groups, most respondents disagreed that RA was too technically challenging or time-consuming.

Practice patterns similarly trended toward increased RA use among more experienced clinicians, although this did not reach statistical significance; conversely, reporting a lack of skill as a barrier decreased significantly with more experience (Fisher’s exact p=0.012). No associations were found between experience level and choice of technique or procedure duration.

Discussion

RA is a key component of hip fracture pain management and is recognised as a core skill for ED practitioners. In this self-reported survey, utilisation of RA for hip fracture pain management was low, despite overall positive attitudes towards its use. The primary reported barriers were a lack of sufficient training and low levels of confidence, with ‘lack of need’ being cited by some, as opposed to a lack of equipment. Further, the reported absence of departmental protocols could be an additional barrier. The majority of respondents were junior doctors, which may have contributed to the lack of training and confidence. Where training had been reported, it was mostly informal and facilitated by fellow EM colleagues. The most commonly reported techniques were the US-guided FNB/FICB. Although limited training was reported as a predominant barrier, underutilisation is likely multifactorial and further influenced by organisational and structural factors, which were not extensively assessed in our survey.

Lack of confidence and training has been demonstrated in prior studies. Haslam et al conducted a similar study in a Canadian ED setting, reporting that while 43% of respondents had received RA training for hip fracture pain management, the overall satisfaction with the extent of training was low and confidence levels remained moderate. Only 30% of respondents were using peripheral nerve blocks in the management of hip fractures.16

Similarly, Rashid et al assessed the extent to which RA for hip fractures was adopted in UK EDs. Among the 67% of EDs who completed the survey, 44% reported using RA for hip fractures. The main barriers cited were a lack of trained staff (36%) and inadequate equipment (22%).21 These findings led to training and competency assessment of senior ED doctors and the implementation of a FICB protocol.

Wiercigroch et al conducted a broader study on RA and investigated the practices, perspectives and barriers to its use in Canadian EDs. Respondents reported a median frequency of two nerve blocks performed in the past 10 ED shifts, with 76.5% of respondents reporting that they needed more training and 47% indicating the need for a departmental protocol. Respondents in their study generally had a positive attitude towards RA and were interested in using it more frequently.18

These studies echo our findings and suggest a gap between knowledge and practice. Although not directly assessed in our study, addressing this gap may improve confidence and uptake of RA for hip fracture pain management in the ED. This may be particularly relevant in our setting, with a high proportion of respondents being junior doctors, which may be associated with differences in exposure to training opportunities and self-reported confidence. Evidence suggests that procedural competence can be effectively achieved via repeated hands-on simulation combined with competency-based training.22 Of the 31 respondents in our study who indicated that they had received training specific to RA in hip fractures, only four indicated that they had attended a formal course or workshop.

Addressing the knowledge-practice gap appears to be feasible. Lee et al19 demonstrated the impact of a knowledge-to-practice training programme focusing on RA in hip fracture pain management. None of the enrolled physicians had performed any point-of-care ultrasound guided regional analgesia (POCUS-GRA) procedures in the year prior to enrolment, with 27/31 having performed at least one POCUS-GRA procedure before the study ended and 13/27 having performed three or more.19

Similarly, system-level changes have been associated with positive changes in practice. Casey et al17 assessed the effect of implementing a geriatric hip fracture programme in the ED. Prior to implementation, 6% of patients had received a FICB, which increased to 60% post-implementation.17 A Thai study by Sri-On et al23 demonstrated an increase in the use of FICB from 0% to 14.08% with the introduction of a hip fracture fast-track protocol in the ED.23

These findings suggest that interventions specifically aimed at addressing barriers to the use of RA for hip fracture pain management seem feasible and may be associated with positive changes in practice. Over 80% of respondents in our study reported that RA could be performed in under 15 min, with 85% reporting that US was available, suggesting time and equipment are unlikely to be the predominant limiting factors. The responses in this survey suggest that the primary perceived barriers are skill and knowledge gaps. Some respondents indicated a ‘lack of need’ as a reason for not performing RA. This may reflect a limited awareness and knowledge regarding indications and benefits of RA; however, this could also relate to a variation in the incidence of hip fractures across study sites (which was not measured in this study) and thus should be interpreted with caution.

Respondents in our study generally reported favourable attitudes towards the use of RA for hip fractures. A notable outlier was that only 46% of respondents perceived that patients were receptive to the use of RA for hip fracture, with senior doctors being more likely to perceive patients as being amenable (p=0.007). This might suggest that greater exposure and observation of senior doctors could influence attitudes among junior doctors; however, this was not directly assessed in our study.

The descriptive design of this study with reliance on self-reported data precludes the establishment of causal relationships. Our study suggests that the use of RA and exposure to training in our study population is limited. Based on prior literature, the introduction of standardised multifaceted programmes with competency checklists could promote proficiency in RA for hip fracture pain management. Supplementing these with regular hands-on refresher courses may contribute to enhancing skill retention. Simultaneously, introducing or strengthening existing departmental protocols prioritising RA above parenteral analgesia in hip fracture pain management may provide a structured framework for ED staff, enhance accountability and potentially reduce delays in providing adequate analgesia.

The feasibility and impact of such interventions could be the focus of future studies in our setting.

Limitations

This study was conducted at academic, urban hospitals with EM physician-led EDs, which may limit generalisability to non-academic or rural centres, where resource limitations may be more significant. Notably, however, RA remained underused despite relatively good access to US and trained colleagues at these sites, suggesting that the barriers identified are more likely related to skill and confidence than to resource availability. If anything, these barriers may be more pronounced in settings where training opportunities and equipment access are more limited. Data were gathered by means of a self-reported survey, which risked the possibility of recall bias. Formal psychometric validation of the questionnaire was not undertaken, which may affect the reliability of the findings and limit direct comparability with other studies. However, as no validated tool existed for this specific context, the questionnaire was developed through a structured review of similar published studies and refined through expert consultation, which we believe represents a reasonable and transparent approach for a KAP (Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices) study of this nature. Despite the study being anonymous, the use of Likert scales may have resulted in social desirability and agreement bias. The paper-based format allowed for skipped questions, leading to variable response rates across items and may have introduced recall bias. It is plausible that other factors, not considered in the survey, may be affecting current practices. This may be especially relevant to structural and organisational factors, which were not explored in depth by the survey. The analysis of results is primarily descriptive, without multivariable analysis, which limits understanding of the impact independent factors may have on RA practice patterns.

Strengths

This multicentre study addresses a relevant and actionable clinical challenge. A large proportion (80%) of the eligible population was included, enhancing representativeness within this setting. It provides a comprehensive overview of knowledge, attitudes and practices across a broad sample of ED clinicians at varying levels of training as well as insight into potential barriers, which can now be addressed in future studies.
