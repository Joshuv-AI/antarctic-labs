3. ABSTRACT: This project addressed both technical and economic objectives toward significantly
increasing the availability of ElectroChromic (EC) dynamic architectural glass at reduced cost. In con-
trast to the prevailing industry location of the EC device layer on the exterior light of an Insulating
Glass (IG) unit, Project Objectives assessed the feasibility of a) repositioning the device layer midway
between two IG panes to configure triple glazing, and b) independently framed to enable positioning to
the building interior independent of the original glass configured as a dynamic secondary window.
Parallel research complementing both EC-IG and EC-WinSert configurations addressed integral Photo-
Voltaic, and ceiling mounted “broadcast” power to circumvent the significant onsite electrical con-
tracting normally associated with the installation of commercial EC glazing.

Summary results include: 1) verification of controlling internal IG temperature across all surfaces in
both EC-IG and EC-WinSert configurations within safe limits with the proviso that some combination
of low-e, tinted or reflective glazings outboard of the EC device layer provide Solar Heat Gain Coeffi-
cient (SHGC) performance no higher than 0.25, 2) relocating the EC device layer from an IG outer
pane to be centrally positioned: a) enhances insulating performance by creating triple IG to reduce con-
ductive U-Factor (= higher R-Value) performance, b) frees the second IG surface to receive one of a
series of variable low-e coatings enabling directional SHGC “tuning”, 3) reducing the weight and
thickness of present EC triple IG units, most often configured with three ¼” panes and often further
burdened by a laminated outer-glass EC light, 4) introducing the feasibility of providing EC technol-
ogy on a retrofit basis as a lift-in/out secondary window, 5) demonstrating the feasibility of greatly re-
ducing the delivered cost of EC-IG units to commercial glaziers by enabling full EC device layer
sheets in excess of 50 square feet to be transported from their vacuum coated producers, like Sage, to
remote highly sophisticated, automated, and heavily capitalized IG thin-glass-triple fabricators like Al-
pen.

Evaluation of Thin Triple/WinSert EC-IGU Prototypes Under Laboratory Conditions
Background The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), in collaboration with Alpen
High-Performance Products and SageGlass analyzed the thermal performance of multiple insulating
glass units (IGU) with embedded electrochromic (EC) prototypes. Prototype classes studied include
“thin” triple pane IGUs and secondary window devices. “Thin” triple pane units are formed when the
center lite of the triple is thinner than the exterior glass lites. This helps simplify retrofit of high-perfor-
mance glass units, but also decreases product weight, reducing installation and transport costs. Sec-
ondary windows are framed units that are installed interior of an existing glazing product to improve
performance. It is most easily described as an “interior storm window.”

Inserting an electrochromic device into an insulating glass unit carries elevated risk from a durability
perspective due to heating of the highly absorptive EC device when in its darkened state. This heating
can lead to glass breakage as well as edge seal failure. For current multi-pane EC IGU configurations
this is typically addressed by placing the EC device on the inside surface of the outboard glass lite
(Figure 1A). In this configuration, strong convective heat flow on the exterior side of the outboard
glass surface coupled with direct conduction from the EC layer through the outboard glass skews heat
transfer toward the exterior thereby mitigating the overheating risk. However, based on manufacturing
preferences, it was found desirable in this effort that for the thin triple configuration the EC device be
integrated with the center lite rather than the outboard (Figure 1B). This creates a challenge to manage
the heat flow in the EC IGU appropriately to drive as much heat as possible toward the exterior lite
through judicious choice of glass, gap spacings and low-e coatings. In the case of the secondary
(WinSert) product, the EC layer will always be inboard of the existing exterior glass (Figure 1C) which
also creates a potential durability risk. In addition, this configuration creates a challenge related to gen-
erating heat in the air gap between the secondary window and the outboard glass. This, in effect, can
create a solar thermal concentration in the gap which must be managed appropriately to mitigate
4

durability risks as well as overall energy benefits of the technology. The objective of this study was to
examine the impact of low-E coatings, gap spacing and gas fill on reducing heating of the electro-
chromic lite in the various configurations mentioned.

Experiment and Instrumentation

Testing was conducted in a modified Atlas Ci5000 environmental chamber equipped with a Xenon arc
lamp configured to emulate NFRC summer boundary conditions. The chamber (Figure 2) was config-
ured to allow an IGU to be mounted in the access door, mimicking installation in an exterior wall of a
building. A chosen “exterior” environment was maintained inside the Ci5000 near 41°C with an irradi-
ance of one “sun” (1000 W/m2), while the simulated “interior” environment took advantage of labora-
tory ambient conditions. The laboratory ambient temperature was maintained near 22 °C. The interior
chamber was air-cooled to stabilize conditions. To evaluate performance of the individual IGU as well
as in simulated installation conditions prototypes were evaluated as stand-alone IGU’s as well as in a
model framing system. Each prototype was evaluated in four states: framed-EC clear state, framed- EC
darkened state, unframed- EC clear state, and unframed- EC darkened state. Center of glass (COG)
surface and glass edge temperatures were measured, and the results were compared with Window7
simulations representing NFRC summer conditions. Summer conditions were chosen due to the per-
ceived higher risk of overheating under those conditions.

Center of Glass Temperatures

To evaluate the thermal response of the center lite in the thin triple configurations, COG temperatures
were measured on each surface (S1 to S6) as shown in Figure 4. Because WinSert is a double-glazed
IGU, S1 and S2 are represented by the primary (already existing) glazing. For all prototypes, the elec-
trochromic (EC) layer is located on Surface 4; this surface was not instrumented to avoid interfering

For nearly all prototypes, the highest temperatures are recorded at surface S3, which is the center

installed. The WinSert configuration with the Cardinal180 low-e layer (Winsert/180) prototype shows
an especially high peak temperature of nearly 90°C, highlighting a potential area of concern for com-

Discussion

As mentioned previously, samples were evaluated to determine suitable thin triple and WinSert config-
urations that minimized durability failure risks. This was accomplished by comparing modeled perfor-
mance, COG temperatures as well as temperature differentials from COG to EOG with and without
framing. The most dominant effect and risk was observed when the EC lite was in a darkened state in
all configurations. Results were evaluated based on the ability to minimize the temperature of the inte-
rior EC lite while in the darkened state with particular attention paid to units that kept the COG tem-
perature of the EC layer under 80°C. Framing and low-E placement were found to significantly influ-
ence the edge and interior surface temperatures, with larger cavities reducing overall temperatures.

For the thin triple configurations, all prototypes were able to keep the EC device layer under 80°C with
the lowest EC temperature observed for the device with Cardinal 366 low-e on surface 2, two symmet-
ric 13.5 mm gas gaps and a clear interior lite. All WinSert configurations showed COG EC surface
temperatures between 80 and 90°C which raised some concern. Further work used this configuration in
a field installation to probe additional strategies to mitigate overheating and are presented in the field
evaluation portion of this report.

Limitations

This study reports temperature-based inferences of stress rather than direct stress measurements for all
prototypes. While strain gauges were installed on selected configurations, they were ultimately not
used due to damage sustained during shipping. Additionally, edge temperature data were unavailable
for Prototype 4. Finally, Chamber conditions approximated but did not exactly match NFRC boundary
conditions.

Conclusions

Framed samples in the dark state exhibited the largest COG-to-edge temperature differentials. In con-
trast, unframed clear-state samples minimized ΔT. Larger cavities consistently reduced surface temper-
atures by approximately 7 to 9 °C compared with 6 mm cavities. Interior low-e coatings lowered inte-
rior surface temperatures, thereby reducing inward heat transfer.

10

Window7 simulations under NFRC 100-2010 summer boundary conditions reproduced the observed
trends.

These findings highlight framing, cavity width, and low-E placement as important design controls that
improve thermal performance and manage durability risk when integrating highly absorptive glazing
products. Additional mitigation strategies may be required to ensure durability risks associated with
increased temperatures for secondary windows.

Executive Summary
This report presents the results of a research study evaluating the WinSert Plus® electrochromic (EC)
secondary insulated glazing unit (IGU) that can be mounted over an existing window to improve the
window’s thermal resistance and active control of the visible light transmission through the window
assembly.

Two office rooms in a medium-sized office building were instrumented to collect data on tempera-
tures, vertical solar irradiance normal to the windows, visible light transmission and several other pa-
rameters. It is important to note that these 1215 Spruce Project windows are west facing (255 azi-
muth), and unshaded from the low angle afternoon-sun in mile high Boulder (with therefore approxi-
mately 32% greater solar intensity than at 40 degrees north latitude and sea level.)

The EC IGU was found to significantly reduce vertical solar irradiance and visible illuminance in the
IGU-treated room. However, significantly elevated surface temperatures were recorded at the IGU sur-
face facing the original window. An additional layer of a permanent tint film applied over the existing
window drastically reduced the surface temperatures of the IGU, potentially increasing its durability.
But this also necessitated a more reasonable control of the tint in the treated office room as the IGU in
addition to a permanent tint film applied over the existing window can make the treated room too dark
to work in.
1 Introduction
As buildings strive for greater energy efficiency and occupant comfort, advanced glazing technologies
have emerged as a key solution. Secondary glazing is a window retrofitting solution that enhances the
thermal and acoustic performance of existing windows by adding an additional layer of glazing on the
interior side. This approach is particularly beneficial for improving insulation in older buildings where
full window replacement may be impractical or costly. By creating an insulating air gap between the
primary and secondary glazing, this system significantly reduces heat loss in winter and minimizes
heat gain in summer, leading to improved energy efficiency and indoor comfort. Additionally, second-
ary glazing enhances soundproofing, making it an effective solution for buildings located in noisy ur-
ban environments. It can also provide added security and condensation control while preserving the
aesthetics of historic or architecturally significant windows. With advancements in glazing technology,

17

modern secondary glazing solutions now incorporate features such as low-emissivity (Low-E) coatings
and electrochromic glass, further optimizing thermal performance and daylight management.

Secondary window glazing with electrochromic (EC) technology is an innovative solution for enhanc-
ing energy efficiency and indoor comfort in buildings. Unlike traditional static glazing, EC secondary
glazing systems incorporate a thin, electronically controlled layer that dynamically adjusts its tint in
response to changing light and heat conditions. This allows for precise regulation of visible light trans-
mission (VLT) and solar heat gain, reducing glare, minimizing cooling and heating demands, and im-
proving overall occupant comfort. As a retrofit-friendly solution, EC secondary glazing can be in-
stalled over existing windows, providing a cost-effective way to upgrade thermal and optical perfor-
mance without the need for full window replacement. By intelligently managing daylight and solar
heat, EC secondary glazing contributes to reduced energy consumption, improved visual comfort, and
a more sustainable built environment.

WinSert Plus® is an insulated glazing unit (IGU) product developed by Alpen High Performance
Products, Inc.1 in collaboration with SageGlass®2. This IGU is composed of thin glass combined with
low-E coated glass, a warm edge spacer and either argon or krypton gas fill. The WinSert Plus® IGU,
offers a secondary glazing system that enhances thermal performance while providing dynamic control
over VLT. This product integrates Sage Glass’s electrochromic technology into a lightweight, easy-to-
install insert, allowing windows to automatically adjust their tint in response to changing daylight con-
ditions.

This report analyzes the impact of WinSert Plus® on visible light transmission and thermal comfort in
an office building setting. By regulating the amount of natural light and solar heat entering an occupia-
ble space, WinSert Plus® aims to reduce glare, mitigate overheating, and improve occupant well-be-
ing. The study evaluates how the dynamic tinting properties influence interior lighting conditions and
thermal performance, particularly in areas where solar exposure varies throughout the day. Through
the analysis presented in this report, the effectiveness of WinSert Plus® as a retrofit solution for en-
hancing energy efficiency and indoor environmental quality in commercial office spaces is assessed.

This report summarizes the findings of the research study project funded by the U.S. Department of
Energy (DoE) via the Buildings Energy Efficiency Frontiers & Innovation Technologies (BENEFIT)
funding opportunity announcement (FOA) project assignment number DE -EE0009704, awarded to
Alpen High Performance Products, Inc. as the Primary Contractor, and SageGlass® as the Subcontrac-
tor. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) led the measurement and verification (M&V)
portion of this BENEFIT FOA research study in which Alpen High Performance Products in partner-
ship with SageGlass® installed the WinSert® product in an office building in downtown Boulder, Col-
orado between April and December of 2024. RSC: Although not addressed in this Project’s objec-
tives, the EC WinSert Plus units, with the Sage 2.2 mm Device Layer, have demonstrated significant
sound attenuation, noticeable in their positioning directly facing heavy traffic on Boulder’s main
“Broadway” thoroughfare.

2 Technology Overview
SageGlass® electrochromic glazing technology dynamically tints or clears in response to ambient day-
light conditions. This advanced glazing system allows for unobstructed views and natural daylight
without the need for traditional blinds or shades, enhancing occupant well-being and productivity. Its
intelligent control system utilizes predictive algorithms and real-time weather data to optimize tint lev-
els automatically while also offering manual control options through a smartphone application inter-
face.

The WinSert Plus® EC IGU is designed as a secondary glazing system that is installed over existing windows to enhance
thermal and acoustic performance without requiring full window replacement. This system consists of a low-emissivity
(Low-E) coated insulated glass panel that is mounted onto the interior side of an existing window, creating an insulating
airspace between the two panes. The primary benefits of the WinSert Plus® system include improved energy efficiency by
reducing heat loss in winter and heat gain in summer, leading to lower HVAC costs. Additionally, it enhances sound insula-
tion, reduces condensation, and improves indoor comfort without altering the building’s exterior. However, potential draw-
backs include added weight and bulk to the window assembly, possible visual distortions due to the additional glass layer,
and limitations in compatibility with certain window frames. Furthermore, while it significantly improves performance, the
current method of installing the IGU over an existing window renders the window inoperable after installation. However,
the IGU can be easily uninstalled when needed as it is simply press-fit on the windowsill. The rubber gasket around the
frame of the IGU ensures that the windowsill surface or any surface of the original window framing that is directly in con-
tact with the IGU frame does not sustain any permanent damage due to the installation or uninstallation process.

Predicted Mean Vote (PMV) is an index used as the final determinant of comfort using the ASHRAE
55 Standard that estimates the average thermal sensation of a group on a seven-point scale, based on
the body's heat balance. Thermal neutrality occurs when internal heat production equals heat loss. The
body regulates temperature through sweating to maintain balance and prevent discomfort. PMV is in-
fluenced by factors like metabolic rate, insulation, temperature, air speed, radiant temperature, and hu-
midity. Predicted Percentage of Dissatisfied (PPD) is another index defined by the ASHRAE 55 Stand-
ard that quantifies the proportion of occupants who feel too hot or too cold. Derived from the PMV, it
reflects the distribution of individual thermal sensation votes to estimate overall discomfort levels. De-
tailed discussion on the ASHRAE 55 Standard is out of scope for this report, but we will cover the ba-
sics on the procedure used for thermal comfort determination here.
Per the ASHRAE 55 Standard, the comfort zone is considered to be sufficiently comfortable if at least
80% of its occupants can be expected to not object to the ambient condition, meaning that the majority
are between -0.5 and +0.5 on the PMV scale. In order to determine the PMV value, there are three
methods that can be used:
1. Graphic comfort zone method,
2. Analytical comfort zone method, and
3. Elevated air speed comfort zone method
A deep dive into each one of the above methods is outside the scope of this report. Table 5.3.1 in the
ASHRAE Standard 55 (Table 2 below) outlines the applicability of the three different comfort zone
methods for determining the acceptable thermal environment in occupied spaces. The measured pa-
rameters like room average air speed, humidity ratio, and the assumed values of the metabolic rate and
clothing insulation levels all determine the applicable method for thermal comfort determination (–0.5
< PMV < +0.5).

The calculations of thermal comfort follow a sequence of steps to evaluate the PMV and PPD based on
environmental factors, metabolic rate, and clothing insulation. The calculations in ASHRAE 55 Stand-
ard for thermal comfort are derived from the ISO 7330:2005 Standard: “Ergonomics of the thermal en-
vironment — Analytical determination and interpretation of thermal comfort using calculation of the
PMV and PPD indices and local thermal comfort criteria”7.

1. Clothing Adjustment Factor (fcl):
The clothing adjustment factor accounts for the effects of clothing insulation and air velocity on ther-
mal comfort. The formula for fcl is:
                                             𝑓𝑐𝑙 = 1.05 + 0.1 × 𝑐𝑙𝑜
where clo represents the clothing insulation value (in this case, 0.77). This factor is then adjusted based
on the air velocity (denoted as va). If the air velocity is less than 0.1 m/s (which, in our case, is applica-
ble), 𝑓𝑐𝑙 is further modified by:
                                              𝑓𝑐𝑙 = (1 − 0.2 × 𝑣𝑎 )
If the air velocity, 𝑣𝑎 , is greater than or equal to 0.1 m/s, fcl is multiplied by 0.7, reflecting the impact
of air turbulence on thermal comfort.
2. Metabolic Rate Adjustment (M):
The metabolic rate of the occupant is adjusted based on the mean radiant temperature (denoted as tr).
The formula to calculate the adjusted metabolic rate (M) is:
                  𝑀 = 𝑚𝑒𝑡 × (58.15 − 0.61 × 𝑚𝑒𝑡 + 0.061 × 𝑡𝑟 + 0.0024 × 𝑡𝑟 × 𝑚𝑒𝑡)
In this formula, met represents the metabolic rate, which is set to 1.0 for a sedentary office occupant.
The metabolic rate is modified to reflect the effect of tr (mean radiant temperature), indicating how the
body exchanges heat with the surrounding environment.
3. Predicted Mean Vote (PMV):
The PMV, which represents the thermal sensation of the occupant, is calculated using the following
formula:
                                      𝑃𝑀𝑉 = 0.303 × 𝑒 −0.036×𝑀 + 0.028
The exponential term models the relationship between the metabolic rate and thermal comfort. A PMV
value near 0 indicates thermal comfort, with negative values suggesting cold conditions and positive
values indicating heat. The PMV generally ranges from -3 (cold) to +3 (hot). Acceptable levels of ther-
mal comfort range between -0.5 and +0.5.
3.4 Data Analysis
We first performed aggregate data analysis for the full study duration. The aggregated data provided a
sense of the ranges and measures of central tendency of the various parameters that were measured. In
addition to the full study period, we also performed aggregate data analysis before and after the tint
film application so that the impact of the tint film could be analyzed.
For statistical analysis of aggregate data, we first tested the data distribution for normality to assess
whether parametric statistical tests could be applied. We used four techniques to check the normality of
data distribution:
1. Shapiro-Wilk test
2. Kolmogorov-Smirnov test
3. Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) plots, and
4. Quantile-Quantile (Q-Q) plots.
Each of these tests on all collected data indicated that none of our data sets was normally distributed.
Hence, non-parametric testing methods were used to test for statistical significance in the differences
between various aggregate data groups. For comparing the differences between two groups of data, we
assumed that the samples were independent and performed Mann-Whitney U Test. For more than two
independent groups of data, we performed Kruskal-Wallis H Test. We used Python programming lan-
guage-based libraries such as SciPy, Statsmodels, Pingouin, and NumPy to perform the statistical anal-
yses and libraries such as Matplotlib, Seaborn, and Plotly for data visualization.

We use both box-and-whisker plots and violin plots to visualize aggregate data. For the box-and-
whisker plots (also referred to as boxplots) the red cross mark in the top box-and-whisker plot (and all
other boxplots shown in this report) indicate the mean value. The whiskers indicate the limits of 1.5
times the inter-quartile range, the short edges of the box indicate the inter-quartile range, and the dots

26

outside the whiskers indicate the outliers, which are the individual data points lying outside whisker
limits. Similarly, for the violin plots, the maximum width of the violin plots indicates the mode of the
dataset, the first, second (median) and third quartiles as well as the 1.5 times of inter-quartile range are
indicated by horizontal lines overlayed on the violin plots. Similar to the boxplots, a red cross mark
overlayed on the violin plots indicate the mean value. The statistical test statistics and the correspond-
ing p-values are mentioned where applicable together with the boxplots and the violin plots.
Besides aggregated statistical data analysis, we also analyzed 1-minute resolution temporal profiles for
a 24-hour period in a clear-sky day using single and multi-line plots to show the data patterns and
trends at various times of the day.

5 Discussion
There are several limitations of the result we present in this report. First of all, the data collection did
not span a whole calendar year, which would have provided a more comprehensive picture of the tem-
perature and irradiance transmission we analyzed. The intervention of additional permanent tint film
application could also have been more consistent if we could have extended the study until another cal-
endar year’s summer season. However, due to practical limitations experienced during the deployment
of instrumentation in an occupied office building, we could not collect data for such a long duration.
Nevertheless, the collected data does provide important insights into the impact of the WinSert Plus®
EC IGU addition over an existing window in the office building used in this study.

Besides the data presented in this report, we also deployed heat flux sensors and strain gauges in both
the windows. Upon preliminary analysis of data, we determined that the sensitivity of the heat flux
sensor was preventing any meaningful measurement being made. Likewise, strain gauge readings were
also identified as erroneous as we noted somewhat reasonable readings in one direction (90°) but no
readings in the other directions (0° and 45°). There was also a problem with the temperature and hu-
midity probe located in the cavity formed between the existing window and the WinSert Plus® EC
IGU in Office 3. The temperature sensor of this probe plateaued at 60°C. Hence, readings from these
sensors were not included in the results.

From Figure 14, we noted that the lower frame surface temperature in the instrumented window of Of-
fice 2 exceeded the glass surface temperatures. A possible reason for this behavior can be the highly
conductive metal framing experiencing direct beam solar radiation. We also noted that the frame tem-
perature adjacent to Surface 2 in the window of Office 3 when measured at the center position (shaded
from direct beam solar radiation) experiences almost the same peak temperature as the lower position
(exposed to direct beam solar radiation). However, the peak of the center fame position lags that of the
lower frame position by almost 2 hours. A possible reason for this could be due to the heat stored by
air trapped in the cavity between the original window and the EC IGU is slowly conducted to other
parts of the original metallic window frame.

From Figure 18, we can ascertain that the EC IGU often makes the room too dark under its current tint
settings. Although it is a preference of the user, the limited feedback we anecdotally got from the of-
fice room occupants was that the EC IGU darkens the room to a point where the lights need to be
turned on to be able to see properly within the office space.

30

From Figures 20 and 26, we can infer that the addition of the window tint film, the glare due to vertical
solar irradiation transmission is much more notable in Office 2 than the already over-darkened Office 3
due to the EC IGU.
During the entire data collection period, we did not notice or get notified by the office occupants of
any visible condensation inside the window cavity in Office 3 formed between the original window
and the EC IGU.
6 Conclusion
 In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest that the WinSert Plus® EC IGU works as intended,
but there are caveats that need to be addressed. The significant elevation of temperatures in the cavity
formed between the existing window and the EC IGU is a huge concern as the elevated temperatures
on Surface 3 poses a durability risk and acceptability of the technology in existing buildings. However,
with the addition of the additional tint film in Surface 2, the temperatures in the window cavity were
brought down to manageable levels.

The controls of the EC IGU also have room for improvement. Through limited anecdotal feedback we
received from the office occupants and by direct observation during the visits to the deployment site,
we were able to note that the EC IGU makes Office 3 too dark for a normal person to work in. By
providing occupants direct control of tint levels via a mobile application, WinSert Plus® technology
does address the over-darkness concern to some extent. However, further auto-calibration capability
built into the tint control automation can potentially make this product more acceptable.

 9. CONCLUSIONS RELATIVE TO ORIGINAL OBJECTIVES & IMPLICATIONS FOR APPLICATION

 • An annealed thin-glass EC device layer can be safely positioned as the central light of a triple
 sealed insulating glass unit, providing that one or more glazing panes to the exterior of the EC
 layer provide a net SHGC solar heat blocking value no greater than 0.25.

 • It is feasible to provide dynamic EC performance to the interior of a building’s glazing with con-
 figuration and performance referenced to Alpen’s WinSert Plus secondary window.

 • Referenced to the replacement of present 2025 triple commercial glazing based on three ¼”
 lights, and presuming the application of automated thin-glass triple automated technology, it is
 realistic to project the production of EC thin-glass triple IG units below a marginal cost of $14
 per square foot.

 • Whereas research focused on ceiling-based WiFi or RF broadcast power to individual EC-IG or
 EC-WinSert windows has been disappointing, related to power levels, efficiency and possible
 marketing concern for RF/microwave transmission, the integral PV powered window has met the
 Project Objective of circumventing utility access. Power Over Internet, often drawing on in-
 stalled office-perimeter wiring accessing windows, offers sufficient wattage (>60) to power dy-
 namic glazing, but tenant security concerns and high prototype costs precluded a mockup within
 this Project.

 • WinSert’s rapid growth, in a national multi-million dollar secondary-window market, supports
 ongoing research toward refining a saleable EC product for 2026. (The multiple EC-WinSerts

In summary, as of April 30, 2025 the Alpen/Sage/NREL Team had demonstrated and documented the
feasibility of manufacturing two novel ElectroChromic insulating-glass configurations: 1) sealed IG
units with the heretofore standard building-exterior orientation of the EC device layer repositioned as
the center pane of thermally, acoustically and economically superior triple units, and 2) an EC WinSert
secondary (interior) window enabling the intelligent variation of both light and heat transmission
through architectural glass.

Alpen and Sage have both advanced during this 3.5 year Project in research, manufacturing, and mar-
keting in ways that directly complement the remaining tasks toward the national/international market-
ing of novel EC configurations. Both new and glazing retrofit markets are addressed, and in light of
the rapidly increasing international demand for electricity, all feasible methods of powering EC glazing
absent utility-grid connection will be pursued.

Drawing on the technical, engineering, manufacturing and marketing momentum generated within this
valuable DOE-supported research, steps will now be taken toward the profitable commercialization of
a) insulating glass with a centrally located ElectroChromic device layer, b) secondary windows incor-
porating EC capability, and c) both EC-IG and EC-WinSerts powered independent of grid electricity.
