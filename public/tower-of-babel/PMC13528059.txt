Pathogen to read: a rapid, automated workflow for public health-ready microbial sequencing
Authors: Kathryn Whitehead, Trinity Williams, Christopher Grim, Shreyas Shah, Lloyd Bwanali, Padmini Ramachandran, Anubhav Tripathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNext Generation Sequencing (NGS) based microbial surveillance has become a critical tool for public health initiatives, providing insights into complex interactions within microbial communities. This understanding can be used to facilitate outbreak investigations, pathogen surveillance, and resistance prevention. However, current sample preparation methods are prone to human error and sample bias resulting in skewed Gram +/- coverages and low yields. Methods that have been optimized for non-bias extraction, like mechanical bead beating, are not easily integrated with automation, preventing widespread scalability. Here, we present an innovative, fully automated method that integrates enzymatic lysis, extraction, and library preparation in a single-operator, single-cartridge workflow. The workflow was designed to substantially reduce hands-on time and minimize Gram-stain-related biased extraction.ResultsA capillary-based liquid handler is leveraged to reduce hands-on time from 8 to 10 h to less than 45 min. Using mixed microbial communities, we demonstrated that the workflow consistently produces high-quality sequencing libraries with an average yield of 80.5 ng/µL, average quality score of 33.6, and using the ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard, a 2.46-fold improvement in Gram-positive representation relative to a standard lysis protocol.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of a fully integrated capillary-based workflow combining enzymatic lysis, extraction, and library preparation compatible with low-throughput platforms. While automated NGS preparation systems exist, they predominantly rely on high-throughput liquid handling robotics, require separate equipment/workflows for extraction and library preparation, or do not support unified workflows for Gram-positive and Gram-negative extraction. The present approach addresses the low-throughput, cost-sensitive, rapid-turnaround requirements of public health and clinical microbiology settings. This proof-of-concept study demonstrates a fully automated workflow for microbial NGS sample preparation, benchmarked on a standardized mock community. This work offers a transformational engineering foundation with the potential to increase efficiency and reproducibility of pathogen genomics workflows, enabling faster outbreak detection and more comprehensive surveillance programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13075-1.

Background

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has revolutionized the field of genetic diagnostics and research, enabling high-throughput analysis of diverse biological samples with unprecedented speed and accuracy [1–3]. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) has further expanded this capability by offering a comprehensive view of entire genomes, including both coding and noncoding regions [4–6]. This extensive genomic insight has been pivotal in identifying novel genetic variants and enabling broad genetic profiling, especially for diseases influenced by mutations in non-coding regions. WGS is particularly powerful in pathogen surveillance due to its ability to analyze evolutionary traits, enabling scientists to track the spread, adaptation, and mutation patterns of pathogens [7–13]. This evolutionary perspective is critical for understanding pathogen dynamics, predicting outbreaks, and developing targeted interventions.

Pathogen outbreaks pose significant public health risks, leading to widespread illness, economic disruption, and, in severe cases, global pandemics. Foodborne pathogens, alone, account for six hundred million cases of human infections and 420,000 deaths worldwide annually [14]. Effective pathogen surveillance is essential for early detection, outbreak prevention, and the development of mitigation strategies [15]. Rapid and accurate pathogen identification can significantly reduce disease spread and improve outcomes.

Pathogen surveillance involves systematic monitoring and analysis to track the prevalence, spread, and evolution of disease-causing organisms. Programs like the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Pathogen Surveillance Tool exemplify large-scale surveillance platforms that track incidence and corresponding demographic information [16–19]. Programs such as this, have supported rapid public health responses and prevention methods by enabling real-time tracking of outbreaks and their behavior.

Several methods exist for pathogen detection, including culture-based assays, immunoassays, polymerase chain reactions (PCR), and biochemical tests. While these traditional methods have been invaluable, they often fall short in speed, specificity, or the ability to provide comprehensive genetic information [20, 21].

NGS surpasses these limitations by offering high-resolution, unbiased genomic data that enables the detection of known pathogens, as well as detailed genetic characterization, novel pathogen detection, and evolutionary analysis [22]. This is particularly important in research surrounding microbial resistance. Microbial pathogens present increased risk due to their ability to develop resistance to antibiotic treatments [23, 24]. Because genetic variations are the driving factor in resistance development, NGS is an important tool in resistance studies and prevention.

A landmark initiative in NGS-based pathogen surveillance was the GenomeTrakr program, a global network of federal, state and academic partners that share WGS data [25, 26]. Since its launch by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2012, GenomeTrakr has compiled sequencing data from over 1.5 million foodborne pathogen isolates globally, facilitating rapid source tracking and outbreak mitigation. In addition to providing a data sharing network, the program has also set standards for sample preparation protocols, reflecting best practices in the field, ensuring consistency and reproducibility across laboratories. Standardized protocols require distinct workflows for Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria sequencing due to differences in cell wall structures that affect lysis efficiency [27]. Gram-positive bacteria possess a thick peptidoglycan layer, making up 90% of their structure and creating a robust barrier to lysis [28, 29]. While Gram-negative bacteria have two membranes, the lipopolysaccharides saturation of the outer layer, as well as the thin peptidoglycan layer, enable easier cell disruption [30]. As a result, separate lysis procedures and colony isolation steps are necessary, complicating the workflow and extending processing times.

The GenomeTrakr workflow (Fig. 1), while comprehensive, is labor-intensive and time-consuming. It involves multiple stages, including sample isolation, lysis, nucleic acid extraction, library preparation, and sequencing. Each step requires careful handling and specific reagents, increasing the risk of errors and limiting throughput [31].

Beyond GenomeTrakr, the standard library preparation and extraction kits that exist for microbial sequencing preparation still fail to address all the issues that prohibit efficient turnaround of pathogen samples. Even enzymatic lysis methods designed for heterogeneous bacterial samples often require prolonged incubation periods—sometimes up to 16 h—further delaying pathogen analysis [32]. Mechanical lysis techniques, while effective, are generally incompatible with automation, creating additional bottlenecks in high-throughput sequencing pipelines [33, 34]. These complex workflows hinder the speed and scalability of pathogen surveillance efforts, particularly during outbreak scenarios where rapid results are critical.

Automation has the potential to provide a solution for these issues, streamlining pathogen surveillance workflows, reducing manual labor, minimizing errors, and accelerating processing times. Automated systems can integrate multiple steps, from extraction to library preparation, into a cohesive process [35–40]. However, current automated solutions face significant drawbacks. Most existing systems separate nucleic acid extraction and library preparation into separate processes, often requiring different equipment for each process [41, 42]. Additionally, existing automated platforms often rely on high-throughput liquid handlers, which, while effective for large-scale operations, are expensive and excessive for pathogen surveillance applications. Screening and public health laboratories typically receive low quantities of samples at a time, with the need for rapid turnaround time. Therefore, pathogen surveillance methods are more suited to low throughput platforms, enabling cost effective, high speed, efficient workflows.

Given the limitations of existing workflows in reliability, expense, ease of use, and data acquisition, there is a pressing need for a comprehensive, inclusive, one-touchpoint workflow capable of rapid, low-throughput pathogen sample preparation [43]. An ideal system should efficiently process heterogeneous samples without the need for labor-intensive isolation steps. This work presents a novel automated method (Fig. 2) that addresses these challenges, offering a streamlined solution for pathogen surveillance that enhances speed, reduces complexity, and maintains high data quality. A comparison of current methods available and our proposed workflow is summarized in Supplemental Table S1 [44–47].

Methods

Automated device

A low throughput (8 sample) capillary-based device (BioQule, Revvity, Waltham, MA) was used to develop this method with internally developed custom software design and control system. The device incorporates a flexible tubing cartridge and a pinch valve strategy that moves reagents and samples from a preloaded 384 well plate in discrete liquid “slugs” through two cannulas (Fig. 3). The flexible tubing allows for pinching, pressurizing the tubing when liquid is in a designated thermal zone to prevent liquid movement during thermal steps. In the heating zone, a copper plate and heating pad sandwich the tubing ensuring thermal contact. The “Y-junction” past the thermal zone allows for transfer of liquid from one cannula to the other without contamination of the solution. A magnetic plate can be raised and lowered to the 384 well plate stage for bead-based purification steps.

Contamination prevention

The device uses single-use disposable tubing cartridges, eliminating inter-run carryover. Within a run, individual sample slugs are in separate tubing within the cartridge to prevent cross-sample contamination. The cartridges are automatically flushed with Tween20 after reagent mixing and washing steps to prevent effects of downstream reagent carryover in the tubes. Following fragmentation (which requires a fragmentation enzyme to travel through the capillaries to the heating zone for activation, fragmentation, and deactivation), samples are moved through the Y-junction to the clean tubing on the opposite side. This ensures that any residue enzyme that is left in the walls of the tubing before heating will not affect the sample post fragmentation. Negative controls consisting of nuclease-free water were processed in parallel with each run and analyzed using Qubit 1x dsDNA High Sensitivity (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA) and BioAnalyzer DNA High Sensitivity (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). No DNA above the assay limit of detection (0.1 ng) was observed in negative control wells confirming the absence of within-run carryover.

Reagent plate loading

All non-thermal reactions within the protocol take place on a 384-well plate. When aligned at column 1, cannula tips can access wells A, C, E, G, I, K, M, and O at column 1 and wells B, D, F, H, J, L, N, P at column 4 (Supplementary Figure S2). Each sample has individual (but identical) reagents in their own individual rows (ex. Sample 1 reagents are in row A and B, Samples 2 reagents are in row C and D, etc.). This setup allows the replication of reactions for 8 samples per run. The reagent column position on the plate was optimized to ensure a lack of contamination on tips and in wells during the process. Using the optimized layout and offset cartridges, this extraction and library preparation was performed with a single cartridge and single 384 well plate as the sole consumables. The full table of reagents used, their corresponding position, and volumes on a 384 well plate are shown in supplementary figures S3 and S4.

Nextflex Unique Dual Index Barcodes (UDI) (Revvity, Waltham, MA) are used for sample barcoding. Users can select any combination of i5/i7 index pairs to load for each sample to allow flexibility and customization of adapters. Each sample adapter is individually loaded in a separate row corresponding to the sample to ensure that downstream multiplexing of samples is possible. Plate loading is completed manually (~ 30 min). The full reagent plate is loaded onto the automated device and both extraction and library preparation are completed within ~ 6 h.

Sample and reagent volumes are limited by constraints of the 384 well plate (< 220 µl, and size of the heating section of the tubing (< 100 µl). This means that initial reagent, sample, and protocol mixing steps cannot exceed 220 µl. If a step requires heating, the heated volume cannot exceed 100 µl. However, this is consistent with volume maximums in standard library preparation and extraction workflows, which are all within the system’s limits.

Lysis and extraction

Automated lysis was first tested using a standard chemical lysis protocol (Chemagen, Baesweiler, Germany), with a 30-minute incubation period (as per protocol). Lysis was followed by SPRI bead-based purification (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA) and elution of purified DNA in 1x Tris-EDTA (PH 7.0). The ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) was used as a validation sample. The standard contains a heterogeneous mix of Gram-positive, Gram negative, and extreme GC content bacteria, along with two yeast samples (Table 1). For the finalized protocol, input samples were normalized to 5 µl of sample at 1.4 × 1010 cells/ml.

The isolation and lysis steps for Gram negative and Gram positive were identified as key steps in preventing efficient and single touchpoint automated workflows for this application. Bead ratio was found to be a key determinant of efficiency of DNA recovery during lysis. Bead ratios from 0.05x to 0.4x were tested in intervals of 0.05x and an isolation bead ratio of 0.1x was found to be optimal for DNA recovery.

As expected, while the standard chemical lysis protocol yielded a high-quality library, there was a bias towards the gram-negative bacteria represented in sequencing. Testing of different enzyme cocktails that target both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria was conducted, resulting in identification of Metapolyzyme DNA Free (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) as the optimal mix for mixed bacterial lysis (Table 2).

The effect of enzyme concentration on sample lysis time was also optimized, resulting in a final ratio of 11% sample, 80% PBS, and 9% enzyme (lyophilized), incubated at 35 C for 30 min. The lyophilized enzyme was resuspended in 100 µl PBS for a final concentration of 1 mg/ml. 4 µl of resuspended enzyme was added to 5 µl of sample, and 35 µl of PBS for a total input volume of 44 µl.

Library preparation

For library preparation steps, Watchmaker DNA Library Preparation Kit with Fragmentation (Watchmaker Genomics, Boulder, CO) reagents were used along with Nextflex UDI adapters (Revvity, Waltham, MA). Standard aspiration flow rates were set below 6 mm/s and volumes in PCR and adapter steps were supplemented with nucleuse-free water to counteract evaporation (volumes based on extensive testing of liquid evaporation over time). One key optimization derived from studies on the impact of fluid movement on inconsistencies in library preparation centered around the adapter ligation step. During this step, a highly viscous ligation reagent containing concentrated ligase and a high molecular weight buffer (typically PEG based) is mixed with the target DNA. Simple device pipette mixing of fragmented sample and ligation mix at high speeds does not effectively result in a homogenous solution due to the high viscosity of the ligation mix. Furthermore, standard pipetting leads to bubbling in the mix, causing further library generation inefficiency. Instead, a layering-based mixing technique was used [48]. By this technique, small, consistent volumes are repeatedly alternately taken from the two solutions being mixed resulting in the tip filling with a layered solution. Once all volume is aspirated from both liquids, the combined mix is slowly dispensed into a single well. This process is repeated to ensure uniform and homogenous mixing. Utilizing this mixing adaptation for high viscosity liquids, instead of traditional pipette mixing mechanisms resulted in higher library yield. The automated library preparation step was first validated using human male gDNA input (Promega). Once lysis and library preparation protocols were optimized, they were combined for a single touchpoint workflow. Final libraries for all protocols were suspended in 20 µl of Tris-EDTA (TE) pH 7.0 buffer.

Library preparation QC and sequencing

Library preparation quality was assessed using Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and BioAnalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). To determine lysis bias and to test the effect of extreme GC content on library preparation, WGS was performed using the NextSeq 2000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Individual samples were normalized to 10 nM prior to pooling, then diluted to 1 nM for denaturing and loading on the sequencer (as per the Illumina sequencing protocol). Paired end sequencing with 2 × 300 bp reads was used. Seven automated protocol samples and seven standard lysis protocol samples were sequenced. All 7 samples were processed with distinct, user-specified barcodes and pooled for multiplexed sequencing. Demultiplexing was performed with zero mismatches using the bcl2fastq (version 2.20) tool within the Illumina BaseSpace platform. Illumina universal adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic PE (version 0.39-44, phred33, Illumina Clip adapter set TruSeq3-PE.fa [51]). Quality score distribution, N counts, and insert size distribution were analyzed along with DNA species distribution. Quality score and N counts were determined from FastQC (version 0.12.1) and insert size plots were determined using fastp (version 1.0.1) and Picard Tools CollectInsertSizeMetrics (version 3.3.0, default settings, M = 0.01). Taxonomic classification was performed using Kraken2 (version 2.1.3), a k-mer-based exact match classifier, with the RefSeq PlusPFP prebuilt database (Standard plus protozoa, fungi, and plant complete genomes, version: 2022-06-07 – Downloaded 2022-09-05). Relative abundance for each taxon was defined as the number of reads classified to that taxon divided by the total number of classified reads, following Lu et al. [52]. Reads mapping to the NCBI database [52] were included.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. Welch’s t-tests were for all pairwise comparisons with p < 0.05 significance. P-values given are two tailed. Lysis incubation time and enzyme optimization comparisons were performed with n = 8 replicates per condition. Effect sizes are reported as 95% confidence intervals. Sequencing-based comparisons (quality scores, species distribution, insert size) were performed on n = 7 samples per condition, following exclusion of one automated protocol replicate due to a sample loading error identified during pre-sequencing QC. Extraction yield comparisons were conducted with n = 8 for the automated protocol and n = 4 for the manual protocol. Standard deviations and CV values are reported for replicates of the finalized automated protocol.

Results

Incubation time

To determine the lysis efficiency of the optimized rapid protocol, standard 16-hour incubation (at standard concentration) and 30-minute incubation (at optimized concentration) were compared (Fig. 4). Average yield, average base pair size, and % adapter dimer was comparable between the two protocols. Adapter dimer was negligible (< 0.5%) for both protocols. The mean fragment lengths for 30 min and 16 h were 348.3 bp (σ = 7.64 bp, CV = 0.022) and 361.7 bp (σ = 12.58 bp, CV = 0.035) respectively (Fig. 4a and b). There was no statistically significant difference in fragment length between the standard protocol and the fast protocol using Welch’s t-test (p = 0.2065, 95% CI= -12.38 to 39.05). The mean library yield (in 20 µl TE buffer) for 30 min and 16 h were 32.43 ng/µl (σ = 2.05 ng/µl, CV = 0.063) and 33.63 ng/µl (σ = 3.04 ng/µl, CV = 0.09) respectively (Fig. 4c). There was no statistically significant difference in library yield between the standard protocol and the fast protocol using Welch’s t-test (p = 0.5995, 95% CI=-4.933 to 7.333).

Enzyme optimization and automated extraction integration

To determine the lysis efficiency of the optimized enzyme cocktail, a standard lysis chemical (Chemagen, Baesweiler, Germany) and the optimized enzyme were compared (Fig. 5a-c). The mean fragment lengths for the enzyme cocktail and standard lysis were 471.4 bp (σ = 36.42 bp, CV = 0.077) and 332.1 bp (σ = 39.45 bp, CV = 0.118) respectively (Fig. 5a and b). The mean library yield (in 20 µl TE buffer) for the enzyme cocktail and standard lysis 80.49 ng/µl (σ = 13.42 ng/µl, CV = 0.166) and 32.21 ng/µl (σ = 9.19 ng/µl, CV = 0.285) respectively (Fig. 5c). The optimized enzyme had a statistically significant increase in average yield and base pair size compared to the standard lysis protocol (p < 0.0001, 95% CI = 37.76 to 58.79 and p < 0.0001, 95% CI = 91.53 to 187.0 respectively). Adapter dimer was negligible (< 0.5%) for both protocols.

To determine the efficiency of just the extraction step within the protocol, the DNA yield post extraction step (prior to library preparation) was quantified for performance on the automated system and manually off device (Fig. 5d). The automated extraction protocol yielded an average of 220.2 ng (σ = 14.35, CV = 0.065) and the manual protocol yielded an average of 221.9 ng (σ = 13.681, CV = 0.062). There is no statistically significant difference between the average yield extracted by the automated and manual protocols (p = 0.863, 95% CI= -22.06 to 25.59). This is also benchmarked above the expected extraction indicated by Zymo for their standard extraction workflow, which uses mechanical lysis techniques (expected 2000 ng per 75 µl is estimated to be equivalent to ~ 133 ng for the protocol input of 5 µl) [53]. Estimating an average of 4 fg DNA yield / cell in the suspension (estimated based on the composite species and their theoretical abundance) and 1.4 × 1010 cell/ml concentration, the automated protocol yielded an estimated 78.6% extraction rate, on par with gold standard commercial kits. The extracted DNA from the automated protocol had an average of 260/280 of 1.786 on NanoDrop (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA), demonstrating high DNA purity.

QC results

Implementing both enzymatic and incubation time optimization, automated libraries from the full resulting protocol (lysis, extraction, and library preparation) produced high quality sequencing data. Average quality scores (both before and after adapter trimming) were above Q30 across all read positions with mean quality score of 31.57 (σ = 0.537, CV = 0.017) before trimming and 33.60 (σ = 0.003, CV = 0.0001) post trimming for n = 7 sequenced samples (Fig. 6a). Mean N count across all positions in reads was 0.269 (σ = 0.006, CV = 0.022) (Fig. 6b).

Extraction bias and insert size

Use of library sequencing data from mixed microbial samples requires non bias sequencing reads. Bias occurs in species distribution (gram negative versus gram positive) and in fragment length (shorter strand bias). A unified bacterial preparation workflow should support high quality library regardless of input strain or bias. To test the bias of the system, taxonomic classification was conducted along with insert size analysis (Fig. 7). The optimized automated protocol yielded a representative distribution of Gram-Positive and Gram-Negative organisms, with a 2.4-fold increase in gram positive representation from the chemical lysis protocol (Fig. 7b). Analysis of insert size demonstrated a natural selection towards larger fragment sizes (characterized by the right skewed insert size graph) with overall average insert size of 229.48 bp and standard deviation across samples of σ = 6.004 bp and CV = 0.0261 (Fig. 7a) .

To quantitatively assess extraction bias, observed mean relative abundances across the seven automated protocol replicates were compared against the manufacturer-specified expected genomic copy proportions for the ZymoBIOMICS standard (Fig. 7b and Supplemental Table S5). There was an overall increase in gram positive representation compared to the standard chemical lysis (increase from 26.3% to 64.77%, compared to theoretical 75.6%). The largest observed reduction in representation relative to the theoretical was found in Staphylococcus aureus (average of 2.61%, percent deviation=-0.82) and Escherichia coli (Ecoli) (average of 2.055%, percent deviation=-0.76) from theoretical input. Both yeast samples resulted in < 1% observed abundance, expected due to low input percentage (< 0.5%) and lack of optimization for yeast-specific enzyme activation temperature.

Discussion and future directions

NGS has been established as a pivotal tool in pathogen surveillance, offering unparalleled ability to advance detection, characterization, and evolutionary tracking methods for infectious agents. However, despite its advantages, conventional sample preparation protocols remain a significant bottleneck in the pathogen sequencing workflow. Lengthy incubation times, complex multi-step processes, and the inherent need for manual handling not only reduce throughput but also increase the potential for human error and sample bias—factors that limit the broader implementation of NGS in public health and clinical laboratories.

This work introduced a fully automated workflow for extraction and library preparation from mixed microbial samples using a capillary based liquid handling system. By leveraging key concepts in fluid transport, enzyme optimization, and automation integration, this method effectively eliminates drawbacks of current manual and automated workflows. Sequencing data showed a representative distribution of Gram-Positive and Gram-Negative bacteria, indicating a non-bias lysis workflow. Moreover, the system maintains high sequencing quality metrics, achieving an average library yield of 80.5 ng/µL and consistent quality scores and low N-counts across varying microbial species. Samples with high or low GC content (as is common in certain pathogen samples) can cause a reduction in basic sequencing quality. This workflow enables high quality sequencing analysis even for such pathogens.

The larger fragment size skew further confirms the robustness and breadth of application of this workflow. In microbial sequencing analysis, larger fragment sizes allow for increased confidence in alignment, and ultimately in variant calling. Larger fragment sizes also enable higher confidence in distinguishing contamination and nonsense reads within sequencing. Therefore, the selection of larger fragments and lack of smaller fragments indicates a highly purified sample, compatible with downstream variant calling and evolutionary analysis.

Although this study has shown representative distribution across bacterial types, consistent with ability for downstream bioinformatic investigation, differential lysis efficiency across Gram-positive species warrants specific discussion. The lower relative abundance of Staphylococcus aureus compared to other Gram-positive organisms (Listeria, Enterococcus, Bacillus) in both automated and standard protocol outputs is consistent with the known resistance of Staphylococcus aureus to enzymatic lysis, attributed to its exceptionally thick, cross-linked peptidoglycan wall reinforced with teichoic acids. The Metapolyzyme cocktail contains both mutanolysin and lysostaphin enzymes with established Staphylococcus aureus activity, but further optimization of lysostaphin concentration and incubation conditions may improve Staphylococcus aureus recovery. Given the critical public health importance of Staphylococcus as a pathogen (including methicillin-resistant strains, MRSA), optimizing lysis conditions specifically for this organism represent an important area for future protocol refinement. Similarly, the under-representation of yeast species (Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Cryptococcus neoformans) reflects the suboptimal temperature for yeast lysis components within a bacterial-optimized protocol; a stepwise temperature profile incorporating a 25 °C yeast lysis step may improve eukaryotic representation.

The taxonomic analysis revealed also revealed a lower representation of Ecoli compared to the expected theoretical. The Metapolyzme enzyme is optimized to target the harder to lyse bacterial gram-positive samples. For more easily lysed gram-negative organisms, like Ecoli, chemicals like SDS are typically used (demonstrated by the higher yield of Ecoli -average of 6.48%- in the chemical lysis protocol), which is likely why we are seeing lower than expected yield of Ecoli. However, it is important to note that despite this, we are still seeing lysis and purification of Ecoli, and Staphylococcus aureus DNA in this protocol that performs well under sequencing. It also is important to note that any further optimizations of the lysis temperature, concentrations, or additional lysis reagents for specific bacterial isolates would not require additional steps or major systematic changes to this automated workflow (just an adjustment of input, volume, and temperature setting during lysis). The true potential and innovation of this automated workflow is that it allows for exactly this, a single workflow system compatible across input strain or sample type.

ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard was used in this study as the primary method to benchmark the feasibility and effectiveness of the workflow across a diverse range of bacterial samples. However, this does not truly capture the complexity of real-world clinical or environmental matrices. Therefore, future work should evaluate performance across variable input types including cell pellets, lysates of heterogeneous quality, and clinical samples with complex matrices such as stool, blood culture broth, and environmental swabs. It will also be important to benchmark extraction across these expanded samples to ensure effective normalization of library preparation input.

Conclusions

By incorporating extraction and library preparation with a single touchpoint for users, on a cost-effective low throughput platform, this workflow supports accessibility of pathogen research and surveillance in public health laboratories, clinical microbiology settings, and research facilities that require rapid turnaround for small sample batches, an area where traditional high-throughput systems often fall short. It is also important to note that this method can be implemented on any commercial liquid handling device with heating and bead-based separation capabilities, expanding the application and accessibility of the method.

This novel automated workflow addresses longstanding challenges in pathogen sequencing by delivering a scalable, rapid, and reproducible solution that supports comprehensive pathogen surveillance and outbreak detection. Its capacity to efficiently process heterogeneous samples without the need for labor-intensive isolation steps not only accelerates the sequencing pipeline but also broadens accessibility to high-quality NGS workflows, advancing public health initiatives and infectious disease research.
