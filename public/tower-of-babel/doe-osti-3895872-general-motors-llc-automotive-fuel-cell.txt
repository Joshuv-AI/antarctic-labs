 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Mod 0: This CRADA encompasses joint R&D work involving resources from GM and NLR,
 focused on efforts to accelerate the reduction of automotive fuel cell stack cost.

 1. Electrode Materials – NLR and GM will develop and test electrocatalyst active and
 support materials that offer improved performance, durability, and/or cost.
 2. Membrane Electrode Assembly (MEA) Performance Diagnostics – Using test stands
 from GM installed at NLR and drawing on previous GM and NLR experience, the teams
 will further develop and implement test methods for characterizing fuel cell kinetics and
 mass transfer. These methods will be applied to state-of-the-art electrode materials of
 potential commercial interest. Technologies can include both proton and hydroxide
 exchange membrane fuel cells.
 3. Contamination Fundamentals – NLR will use advanced analytical capabilities to identify
 potential contaminants leached from fuel cell structural plastics and sealants. NLR and
 GM will develop and implement tests to determine the impact of these contaminants on
 fuel cell performance.
 4. Manufacturing Method Development – NLR will develop on-line detection methods for
 MEA manufacturing, using application guidance provided by GM.

 Mod 11: This research project will focus on development and demonstration of a thermal
 scanning system (TSS) that runs at increased speed which is comparable with current fabrication
 rates. This technology is targeted to improve manufacturing quality checks of hydrogen fuel cell
 coatings.

 Summary of Research Results:

 GM station refurbishment

 NLR received eight fuel cell test stations under the CRADA agreement; hereafter, these test
 stations are referred to as “GM test stations.” The stations were originally commercial
 Hydrogenics units that were customized over years of use at GM to meet research and
 development needs of GM’s fuel cell project. Upon receipt, NLR built out its laboratory
 infrastructure to connect and use the stations in the CRADA project and U.S. Department of
 Energy (DOE) research projects. The necessary laboratory infrastructure changes included
 connections to water drains, gas manifolds for gas supply, electrical power, framing for
 mechanical support, deionized (DI) water supply, and laboratory exhaust ventilation. Before
 becoming usable for research, the test stations underwent a thorough refurbishing effort to bring
 them to state-of-the-art condition with regards to electrical safety, maintainability, and NLR
 laboratory standards. The test station hardware was cleaned, wiring was rerouted and
 repackaged, and tubing was inspected and repaired until leak rates satisfied recommendations by
 the Fuel Cell Council. All internal parts were inspected and, if needed, refurbished, replaced, or
 repaired.

 Test station commissioning was conducted to ensure optional condition and functionality.
 Electrical safety inspections were conducted according to NLR standards, new computers were
 integrated with the control software and tuned to apply all operating parameters reliably and
 accurately. 24/7 automated operation was verified, and fail-safes were tested to ensure safe
 operation. Calibration of electronic load, mass flows, pressures, and temperatures was conducted
 on every machine, followed by a performance verification test on a standard fuel cell
 configuration.

 In all following years, a host of engineering safeties and calibration routines were applied and
 verified yearly to ensure safe operation and high-quality data. Over the years, processes were
 tuned and additional features and control were added for increased safety during operation. The
 total NLR investment for the refurbishment was approximately three months of labor per
 machine, in addition to replacement parts and materials. Since commissioning, all GM test
 stations have been in continuous use, i.e., for more than ten years, creating valuable scientific
 insights for work under the GM CRADA and DOE-funded projects.

 Project Area 1: Electrode Materials

 NLR has established expertise in preparing platinum (Pt)-based catalysts with extended surfaces
 that exhibit high specific areas characteristic of these materials. GM has also worked on
 developing and integrating such materials into electrode structures. In this project, GM and NLR
 will jointly work on making and integrating select materials into both diagnostic and practical
 electrodes. The goal of the project is ultimately to develop commercially viable approaches to
 achieve low Pt loading.

 GM and NLR will cooperate on the development of electrode materials made by atomic layer
 deposition (ALD). GM is currently supporting work in Steve George’s Lab at the University of
 Colorado on the development of two different base materials: 1) Pt ALD on Nanostructure Thin
 Film (NSTF) whiskers, and 2) Pt on oxide cores with tungsten ALD tie-layer. NLR personnel
 will receive samples of these materials and evaluate them in MEAs for beginning of life (BOL)
 performance and voltage-cycling durability. GM is also interested in further evaluating nitrogen-
 doped carbons recently developed at NLR.

 Project Area 1 (Mod 0) Results:

 This topic was investigated due to the interest of GM in using platinum–cobalt alloy (PtCo)
 electrocatalysts in fuel cell systems due to the increased mass activity that PtCo alloys offered. Pt
 is much more noble than cobalt (Co) and dissolution of Co was a primary concern because loss
 of Co could serve as a contaminant and the mass activity advantages were dependent on Co still
 contained within the PtCo alloy.

 NLR performed tests on PtCo catalysts to quantify dissolution as a function of potential in
 rotating ring-disc electrode setups at room temperature and 80 oC. The dissolution results as a
 function of cycle number are presented in Figure 2 for three separate protocols. Co
 concentrations in most cases reached a value approximately one monolayer of Co equivalent in
 the first five minutes, suggesting Co was removed from the outer skin quickly, but then
 stabilized.

 Pt dissolution in the PtCo alloy did not show a strong correlation with potential, unlike other
 studies investigating Pt only nanoparticles. These results helped GM understand PtCo alloys
 better, but were relatively short lived in the CRADA studies, primarily being investigated in
 2015-2016, and then other areas became prioritized.

 Project Area 2: Membrane Electrode Assembly (MEA) Diagnostics

 Several research labs, including GM’s lab, have identified a polarization resistance term at low
 Pt loadings that is operative at or near the Pt surface. This loss term needs to be mitigated in
 order to achieve low Pt loadings commensurate with affordable fuel cell vehicles. NLR and GM
 anticipate joint work on understanding this loss term by making and testing electrodes, both
 model and practical, including the development and application of diagnostic tests.

 GM and NLR will cooperate on the development and application of diagnostic tests for
 characterizing MEA kinetics and mass transport. Using test equipment provided by GM (Figure
 1), NLR will implement tests in the new NLR facility to measure O2 kinetics, proton
 conductivity within electrodes, and O2 mass-transport resistance via limiting current methods.
 These methods will be applied to MEAs of commercial interest including proton-exchange and
 hydroxide-exchange membrane fuel cells.

 Project Area 2 Task (Mod 0) Results:

 Using the refurbished test stands and drawing on previous GM and NLR experience, the teams
 successfully developed and implemented test methods for oxide-dependent oxygen reduction
 reaction (ORR) kinetics (figure 3), mass transfer, and ionomer coverage. These methods were
 applied to commercial and state-of-the-art electrode materials for both proton and hydroxide
 exchange membrane fuel cells, as well as investigating PGM-free electrodes. These methods
 were incorporated into established conditioning and durability protocols to correlate performance
 with changes in mass activity, non-Fickian O2 transport resistance (Figure 4), ECSA, and other
 properties. One notable study (Kabir et al.) describes how different electrode materials require
 additional conditioning steps to reach peak performance. Figure 5 compares the H2/air
 polarization curves, mass activity, and non-Fickian O2 transport resistance for two different
 MEAs with Pt/HSC (TKK) and PtCo/C (Umicore) electrodes following the same test protocol.

 Project Area 3: Contamination Fundamentals

 For the last 2.5 years, GM and NLR have been collaborating on a DOE funded contamination
 project that focusses on the impact of plastic system components on fuel cell performance. In this
 work, several new test methodologies have been developed, and, using these methods,
 approximately 50 materials have been screened and associated leachant impact has been
 qualitatively determined. Whereas this work has successfully characterized fuel cell
 compatibility of various material families, follow-up work is necessary to translate high-level
 screening tools into material requirements needed for commercialization.

 In this project area, GM and NLR anticipate testing select materials for quantitative impact. GM
 will specify materials and conduct leaching experiments. Unlike the previous effort, the levels of
 contaminants will be small, in a range where characterization by analytical test capability at NLR
 will be critical. In addition to the quantitative analysis work, GM and NLR will together define
 fuel cell test methodologies, using test equipment and experience from GM, to determine the
 impact of these low-level contaminants on fuel cell performance.

 Project Area 3 (Mod 0) Results:

 This Task was initially included in this CRADA as a placeholder for potential collaboration, but

 Project Area 4: Manufacturing Method Development

 GM is developing concepts to enable high-volume high-yield MEA manufacturing, as these will
 be necessary prior to widespread commercialization of automotive fuel cells. This work is based
 on an understanding of low-cost materials capability, the key issue being the removal of
 platinum to levels that approach cost-parity with the precious metal content of the catalytic
 converter. It has become clear that use of decals and lamination steps will be a significant cost,
 providing impetus to coat directly onto a web consisting of previously coated porous layers.

 9

 NLR is establishing capability to identify and develop on-line inspection techniques for
 membrane electrode assembly production. The proposed methods include both visual and
 thermal detection to identify defects on either the finished MEA or on sub-layers that comprise
 the MEA. In this project area, these methods will be applied to commercially viable materials
 and processes identified by GM to be required for automotive fuel cell commercialization.

 Task 4 (Mod 0) Results:

 1. Visual Detection of Defects in membrane, electrodes, and their assemblies

 This study applied state-of-the-art optical techniques to detect manufacturing defects in electrode
 and membrane assemblies, including stray fibers, cracks, scuffs, debris, and pinholes. The
 sample set provided by GM included electrodes on a gas diffusion layer (GDL) both with and
 without membrane, as well as specimens with laser-perforated pinholes (figure 6). For debris,
 materials were selected to control particle size; when placed under the electrode coating, any
 material could be used, whereas debris on top of the coating was chosen to have optical
 properties similar to carbon.

 Fig. 6. Specimens of membranes with pinhole defects received from GM to evaluate the
 effectiveness of on-line optical scanning methods

 A Fuel Cell optical scanner was configured with a linear light source, a 12-kilopixel line-scan
 camera, and a positioner to move samples and simulate on-web inspection. Scanning parameters
 were optimized, and the incident light angle was found to have a strong influence on defect
 visibility. Using this setup, defects on the order of 50 microns were successfully identified.

 10

 The imaging workflow began with baseline specimens captured at multiple magnifications,
 followed by specimens intentionally prepared with debris defects. The analysis covered dark
 debris particles on specimens without membrane and debris bumps on specimens with
 membrane, along with comparisons of reflectance histograms for defective versus non-defective
 areas and automatic detection of debris. For scuffs, scores, and slits, low-magnification images
 were acquired to evaluate cases with both good and poor contrast, and automated detection
 methods were demonstrated. For laser-perforated pinholes, the study compared pinhole
 signatures to other white features, assessed the impact of incident light angle, evaluated scanner
 performance against an optical microscope, and implemented automatic pinhole detection.

 Additional methods to create membrane pinholes were explored, including drilling with NLR’s
 pulsed laser capability and using a custom puncture fixture to produce exemplary membrane
 specimens.

 2.  Exploratory UV-VIS-NIR spectrometry study to identify sensitivity to various
 material physical characteristics.

 This task undertook an exploratory ultraviolet-visible-near infrared (UV–VIS–NIR) spectrometry
 study to assess sensitivity to key material characteristics, with an initial focus on using
 interference fringes to evaluate free-standing membrane thickness. The work examined spot-to-
 spot variability, the influence of protective layers, and the optimal measurement configuration
 across reflectance and transmittance modes (total, specular, and diffuse). It also determined the
 wavelength range over which interference fringes were observable and tested whether a fast-rate
 spectrometer in a reflectometer setup can reliably capture those fringes.

 Eight membrane specimen types were investigated, with four measurement spots per specimen: a
 GM experimental membrane in bare form and with a protective layer; a custom-made membrane
 both bare and on Kapton; and NRE212 and NRE211 membranes, each evaluated bare and with
 two protective layers. This matrix enabled isolation of layer and substrate effects on spectral
 response and fringe visibility.

 Seven spectral acquisition methods were employed to capture reflectance (R) and transmittance
 (T) data. Using a CARY spectrophotometer (slow but highly customizable tool) with an
 integrating sphere, we collected total and diffuse R, and total and diffuse T. Specular T was
 measured with the CARY in a straight-beam configuration, and specular R with the CARY
 variable-wavelength (VW) accessory. Complementary measurements were performed using a
 fast rate Ocean Optics (OO) spectrometer in a reflectometer configuration with both low-angle
 and high-angle illumination. Together, these configurations provided a comprehensive
 comparison to identify the most robust setup for fringe detection and thickness estimation.

 11

 While initial visual techniques showed promise for identifying membrane thickness and locating
 a reinforcing layer within the membrane (Figure 7), additional tests on opaque stacks in
 reflectance revealed limitations. The opaque set comprised: catalyst coated gas diffusion layer
 (GDL), so-called gas diffusion electrode (GDE), plus ionomer/reinforcement/ionomer with
 membrane thickness A; GDE plus ionomer with thickness A; GDE plus ionomer/reinforcement
 with thickness two-thirds A; and GDE plus ionomer with thickness one-thirds A. This approach
 ultimately dead-ended because reflectance was found to be strongly dependent on specimen
 orientation, likely stemming from anisotropic properties of the reinforcement layer.

 Fig. 7. Spectral data indicating good sensitivity of total reflectance, over a range of wavelength,
 for GDE with membrane layers of two thicknesses (blue data represent thickness A, orange data
 represent thickness two-thirds A).

 Overall, the results of this entire manufacturing development task provided practical guidance to
 support GM’s strategy development for MEA on-line inspection and process control.

 Sub-topic B: pinhole detection

 Task B Results:

 The goals of Task B were

 1. GM collect/create GDL-electrode-membrane assembly samples with potential gas
 crossover defects
 2. GM/ NLR characterize (optical and other microscopy) GDL-electrode-membrane
 assembly crossover defects
 3. NLR test GDL-electrode-membrane assembly-specific defects on PDA
 4. Go-No/Go for validity of reactive pinhole detection technique for GDL-electrode-
 membrane assembly structure
 5. Based on Go decision, GM fabricate sheet or short roll samples of GDL-electrode-
 membrane assembly suitable for testing with in-line technique
 6. NLR leverage DOE work to develop an in-line pinhole detection technique, and test with
 GDL-electrode-membrane assembly samples

 NLR developed and evaluated methods using H2 permeation and thermal analysis to detect
 membrane pinholes and shorts (Figure 11). Defects ranging from 25 µm to 100 µm were
 detectable, and the method merited further development. Next steps included optimizing reactive
 gas concentration, flow rate, and pulse duration. We used exemplary thermographs from Figure
 11 to assess whether detection can occur under very short excitations that might be employed in
 an online configuration. Pinholes as small as 90 µm in diameter were easily detected with short
 exposures. We also fabricated and tested pinholes as small as 25 µm. Although the thermal
 response was small for these smallest holes, we demonstrated that no intrinsic limitation to
 detection was present.

 Task 3 (Mod 8): Run final, full Gage R&R test to evaluate measurement error

 Task 3 (Mod 8) Results:

 A gauge R&R study estimated total measurement error at 0.76 µm, with the lowest errors
 achieved under lower heating levels. When line speed doubled from 5 feet/minute to 10
 feet/minute, repeatability error worsened by a factor of two to three, although an optical filter
 mitigated some of this degradation at the higher speed.

 Key learnings include that reflected radiation from both the source and reflectors can be
 comparable to the sample’s thermal emission and increase at least as fast with higher heating.
 While sensitivity rises with heating, signal-to-noise decreases, and turbulent convection can be of
 similar magnitude to the desired signal. Measurement performance was highly sensitive to
 optical alignment and image processing choices. Despite these challenges, calibration linearity
 exceeded the target (R2 > 95%), measurements were accurate for both reinforced and
 unreinforced membranes, and the thermal signal did not depend strongly on reinforcement.

 Overall, the best gauge R&R error achieved was about 0.76 µm, roughly 8% of the thickness
 range studied, with a best-case repeatability of 0.47 µm observed in the original configuration.
 Sensitivity values ranged from about 0.54 °C/µm to 1.98 °C/µm depending on speed, heating
 level, and optical filtering (see Table 2). The work identified practical pathways to improve
 robustness at production speeds, notably through optical filtering, tighter control of alignment,
 and moderated heating to balance sensitivity and noise, positioning the technique for further
 optimization in web-line manufacturing environments.

 Modification 11:

 Task 1 (Mod 11): Modeling and Exploring Design Space - NLR

 Modeling of a heater system will be performed to develop design concepts for 10 times more
 powerful heater. A few concepts on the technical feasibility will be analyzed. The objective of this
 task is to predict how much more radiation power can be obtained from the current NLR’s heater,
 what can be achieved by small and major revisions of the design, and what are the other available
 on the market solutions that could potentially be applied to deliver the desired radiation output.

 Upon completion of Task 1 and Milestone 1, NLR will review with GM before proceeding onto
 Task 2.

 Task 1 (Mod 11) Results:

 NLR began with back-of-the-envelope radiative sizing using the Stefan–Boltzmann law for a rod
 at 400 °C radiating to room temperature. That estimate gave a heat flux of about 11.2 kW/m², a
 rod radiative power near 0.32 kW, and suggested scaling to roughly 3 kW for high-speed operation,
 with a corresponding heater temperature near 914 °C.

 Building on this, NLR developed a moving-web thermal model that solved the heat equation
 with uniform heating over a defined focus width, linear convective losses, and boundary
 conditions at both ends. The web was modeled as a two-layer stack (a thin membrane over a
 thicker substrate) and allowed for exploration of realistic process ranges: web speeds of 5–50
 ft/min, convective coefficients around 100 W/(m²·K), heating widths of 10–50 mm, membrane
 thicknesses of 8–16 µm atop a 190 µm substrate, and heating powers from 10 to 100 W.

 Our parametric sweeps simulations showed that achieving a few degrees Celsius rise in the web
 requires far less power than the initial radiative sizing implied—on the order of approximately
 100 W at higher line speeds (Figure 16). The temperature profile increased roughly linearly
 within the heating zone, the downstream decay was largely independent of heating focus width,
 and the thickness effect remained strong across focus widths. The modeling allowed us to
 conclude that both the infrared (IR) lamp heater and laser heating seemed like two options worth
 exploring.

 Task 2 (Mod 11) Results:

 Two high power heating solutions were investigated, fabricated and tested: 1) a laser-based
 system with 2000 W power, for which safety aspects were assessed and trials with two vendors
 led to selecting one vendor; and 2) an IR lamp system with elliptical reflectors, for which the
 team began by identifying providers of IR lamps and compatible power supplies. Next NLR
 designed and fabricated an enclosure to house both the laser and IR lamp systems, using
 interlocked doors as the primary safety mechanism, and designed and constructed a water-
 cooling system comprising two chillers and a multichannel regulator to provide safe and stable
 thermal management (see Figure 17). Additional water-cooling systems were installed using
 cold plates as primary heat-dissipation method to bring the temperature in the enclosure within
 operational limits.

 29

 Fig. 17. Thermal scanner system build of enclosure housing the heater, IR camera, and actively
 cooled plates.
 After construction of the setup an experimental study commenced. The IR lamp configuration
 created a high illumination region with a distinct reflection zone, where specular reflection
 boosted apparent sensitivity to thickness—yielding the highest calibration slopes just before and
 within the zone (up to approximately 3.5 °C/μm). Outside the reflection zone, the system still
 delivered strong signal of approximately 1.5 °C/μm with Tmax near 100 °C at the tested speed,
 and repeatability and spatial uniformity were confirmed across runs and positions. Operational
 issues in early runs were noted, but later runs showed effective thickness signals at 15 m/min
 (approximately 50 ft/min), with one run demonstrated at 23 m/min.

 Laser heating, implemented with both a focused line and a cold-plate–screened constant-
 illumination area, avoided reflection artifacts because the camera band (3–5 μm) excludes the
 975 nm laser wavelength. The focused-line approach achieved approximately0.6 °C/μm at 50
 ft/min and approximately 0.3 °C/μm at 75 ft/min with R² typically >0.95. A cracked lens likely
 caused a colder response in a later run. With the new roll, constant-illumination laser heating
 maintained strong performance, including R² = 1.0 at high speed, and highlighted the need to
 measure pre-heating temperature. The key conclusion was that the lens focusing reduced the
 power requirements but required a thermally robust mount, while a wider beam could keep
 power near approximately 1 kW at 15 m/min without a focusing lens.

 30

 The final experimental study focused on a) the determination of an end-to-end gage R&R (GRR)
 on production-representative webs for the laser heating setup with approximately threefold wider
 illumination, and b) such methods improvement as, removal of the focusing lens, robust image
 correction and calibration. Under these conditions, the system achieved maximum specimen
 temperatures near 90 °C with 1.4 kW input, exhibited a strong, monotonic thickness–temperature
 relationship, and delivered sub-micron repeatability when humidity-driven outlier was excluded
 (Figure 18, right). The principal barrier to cross-run consistency in the uncontrolled lab
 environment was membrane moisture: ambient humidity and web “memory” caused vertical
 shifts in thermal response on the order of several degrees Celsius, dwarfing the true thickness
 signature. A physics-based humidity correlation substantially improved cross-run agreement
 (Figure 18, left), and the team expects “unified calibration” behavior and sub-micron GRR once
 the scanner operates in GM’s environmentally controlled setting. An additional outcome was the
 identification of a useful emissivity signal: one specimen (UN#2) consistently exhibited higher
 emissivity, likely reflecting a structural or compositional difference that can be monitored as part
 of QC.
