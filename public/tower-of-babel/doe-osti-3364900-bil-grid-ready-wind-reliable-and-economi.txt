3. Executive Summary: The GRID-READY WIND project aimed to demonstrate the
 ability of utility-scale wind power plants (WPPs) to deliver traditional and emerging grid
 services (GSs) in both grid-following (GFL) and grid-forming (GFM) modes through a
 centralized control framework, supplemented by local control adjustments at the wind
 turbine generator as needed. Throughout the project, the team offered
 recommendations on how the grid services demonstrated with WPPs could be applied
 to other inverter-based resources (IBRs), such as photovoltaic (PV) systems and
 battery energy storage systems (BESS), while considering the unique dynamics of
 these resources arising from their energy generation characteristics. The project
 sought to enhance the confidence of system operators (SOs) and planners in utilizing
 WPPs, alongside other IBRs such as PV plants and BESS, to provide essential grid
 services over extended periods under diverse operating conditions, thereby
 supporting their integration into the bulk power system.

 The program made meaningful progress in both technical development and project
 execution during the reporting period. The project team advanced the draft white
 paper, strengthening its assessment of grid stability challenges associated with
 increasing inverter-based resource penetration and further defining the ancillary
 services and grid-forming technologies to be demonstrated. The team also shared the

 supporting broader industry engagement.

 aligned with overall project goals. Although the withdrawal of APEX Clean Energy
 created a change in the demonstration partner strategy, the GE Vernova team was
 actively pursuing replacement options and expanding the search scope.

4. Background: Recent published research has shown that as inverter-based resources
 (IBRs) replace synchronous generation, wind power plants (WPPs), solar PV, and
 battery energy storage systems (BESS) must increasingly provide essential grid
 services such as frequency response, voltage support, ride-through, damping, and
 stable operation in weak-grid conditions [1] [5]. A significant portion of the literature
 has focused on the performance limits of grid-following (GFL) controls and the
 growing potential of grid-forming (GFM) controls to improve reliability in low-inertia,
 converter-dominated systems [3], [6] [7].
 This published work is similar to the GRID-READY WIND project in that it examines
 how inverter-based technologies can deliver both traditional and emerging grid
 services through advanced control strategies, including plant-level coordination and
 local converter response [2], [4], [8] [11]. The project differs from much of the literature
 by focusing specifically on utility-scale wind power plants, evaluating both GFL and
 GFM modes, and framing the work around service demonstrations, performance
 metrics, and practical applicability to system operators and planners. It also extends
 beyond many single-technology studies by considering how lessons from wind can be
 applied to other IBRs such as PV and BESS.
 The project fully leveraged the published state of the art to accelerate execution and
 improve technical quality. Prior work on inverter stability, weak-grid operation, wind
 plant controls, and real-time simulation helped inform the selection of grid services,
 define technical priorities, shape performance metrics, and guide development of the
 simulation-based study platform [12]. In this way, the project built directly on current
 literature while addressing a practical gap between research results and scalable
 industry deployment.

5. Project Objectives: The GRID-READY WIND project had the vision of accelerating
 the interconnection of wind power plants, their usage as grid stability resources and
 ensure energy security and grid reliability. The primary goal of the project has been
 to design, implement, demonstrate, and evaluate various existing and emerging grid
 services using a 225 MW wind-only power plant. The ability to demonstrate grid
 services from wind power plants through innovative technologies would have paved
 the path for the widescale adoption of the new grid forming control technologies for
 wind power plants. This project aimed to
 for the renewable energy industry. Execution of the Grid-Ready Wind
 program would have helped DOE to make an immediate impact on US grid stability
 by promoting adoption of advanced grid services with wind power plants (WPPs).

 The GRID-READY WIND initiative is structured as a three-year, two-budget-period
 project, with specific goals and objectives outlined for each budget period.

 Budget Period 1 (BP1) focuses on: a) Identifying and designing grid services for
 demonstration at the chosen site, b) Preparing and evaluating the site for
 demonstration readiness, c) Developing, testing, and validating GFM technology for
 wind turbines, both at turbine level and farm level, to ensure confidence in GFM
 demonstrations, d) Creating and validating an energy management system (EMS)
 Page 5 of 11

 controller to communicate with system operators and dispatch various GSs, e)
 Documenting necessary communication and software/hardware modifications for
 EMS integration applicable to PV and BESS, f) Designing metrics and methodologies
 to assess the techno-economic benefits versus costs for providing GSs from WPPs
 and other IBRs. By the end of BP1, the team will develop a test plan for grid services,
 finalize control augmentation designs for successful demonstrations, and document
 cost-benefit analysis methodologies for grid services provided by WPPs, PV, and
 other IBRs.

 Budget Period 2 (BP2) aims to: a) Collaborate with partners for a successful
 demonstration of various grid services from WPPs in GFL and GFM modes at the
 demonstration site for a minimum of six months, b) Disseminate findings from the
 collected data and other studies, and c) Partner with a university to develop training
 curricula for the future workforce on evolving grid needs and services. BP2 will
 highlight the cost-benefit aspects of grid services provided by WPPs, PV, and other
 IBRs using data from simulations and field demonstrations. The team will conduct
 webinars and participate in conferences to discuss project findings and share insights
 into the capability of WPPs and potentially other IBRs to provide essential grid
 services.

6. Project Results and Discussion: In perspective of the project timeline only the
 first year have been completed while the total project timeline was three years. The
 project has made meaningful early progress in defining target grid services,
 performance metrics, and simulation/test infrastructure, especially through the draft
 white paper, TTU real-time digital simulation work, and NLR PHIL platform
 development.

 The strongest results to date are in planning, modeling, and platform setup, rather
 than field execution, because the project has been affected by subcontracting delays,
 resource constraints, and the withdrawal of APEX as demonstration partner.

 A major discussion theme is that the project remains technically viable, but site-
 dependent tasks, test planning, and some GFM hardware/control work are delayed
 until a replacement demonstration partner is secured.

 Subtask 1.1 Identify grid services to be demonstrated

 the project to the ISO/RTO Council Emerging Technologies Task Force and received
 feedback from SPP, while awaiting written responses from additional operators.
 Discussion: This subtask has already generated meaningful industry engagement,

Results: TTU developed the WPP models in MATLAB/Simulink, initiated development
of a dynamic DFIG-based wind turbine model, and started model testing and sanity
checks with GEVAR input.
Discussion: This work provided the bridge between the development of the generic
bulk power system models and actual wind power plant demonstration studies.

Task 4 Power hardware-in-the-loop (PHIL) studies
Subtask 4.1 Develop PHIL platform
Results: NLR developed a PHIL setup using two controllable grid interfaces (CGIs)
with RTDS-based Type 3 and Type 4 turbine models that can operate in both GFL
and GFM modes. The models include functions such as inertial control, primary
frequency response, power setpoint operation, ramp control, voltage control, current
limiting, and LVRT/HVRT in GFL, plus droop-based voltage/frequency control and
current limiting in GFM. The RTDS models were cross-checked against PSCAD, and
the platform is now ready for configurable multi-MW experiments.
Discussion: This is another major accomplishment. The PHIL setup is especially
valuable because it allows the project to continue de-risking GFM/GFL concepts even
while the field-demonstration path is uncertain.

Subtask 4.2 Demonstrate grid services in GFL mode
Results: NLR initiated studies using the PHIL and RTDS platforms to demonstrate
control features and grid-service functions.
Discussion: This work was in early execution. The main purpose was to use PHIL to
enable streamlined setup for actual field test.

Task 5 Cost-benefit analysis of grid services provided by IBRs
Subtask 5.1 Wind forecasting methodology
Results: TTU identified and adopted up to eight machine-learning/deep-learning
forecasting methods, using a 7-year dataset with 15 meteorological and wind-related
features recorded at 10-minute intervals. The team was evaluating these methods
using a large realistic NLR dataset.
Discussion: This work was still in early phase but promising. Forecasting quality is
central to reserve participation and services valuation, so this subtask would have
become more important as the market-analysis framework matures.

7. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

  Some of the significant achievements that were achieved during the first year of the
  project are discussed below:
  A. Draft white paper on grid services and GFM needs
  The project team developed a substantial draft white paper that defines the need for
  new and evolving grid services as inverter-based resources increase on the grid, and
  identifies the services and performance metrics the project plans to demonstrate. This
  created a strong technical and industry-facing foundation for the project, helps align
  the demonstrations with real system needs, and supports broader industry relevance.
  B. Industry engagement with system operators
  The team presented the project to the ISO/RTO Council Emerging Technologies Task
  Force and received feedback from system operators, including SPP. The
  success depended on demonstrating services that are useful to actual grid operators,
  not just technically feasible in theory. Early operator engagement improved the
  likelihood that the project results would be practical and impactful.
  C. Completion of benchmark power-system simulation models
  Texas Tech University completed and validated two benchmark IEEE power-system
  models for future real-time simulation studies. It allowed the project to continue making
  technical progress even while the demonstration site path became uncertain. These
  models provide a credible platform for evaluating grid-service performance and
  training future workforce participants.
  D. Establishment of the PHIL test platform
  NLR developed a PHIL test environment using controllable grid interfaces and RTDS-
  based turbine models capable of both GFL and GFM operation. It provided a high-
  value testing platform for de-risking controls, evaluating services under realistic grid
  conditions, and generating meaningful technical results before field deployment.
  E. Initial progress on porting GFM controls to the Sierra platform
  The team began reviewing and adapting GFM control logic from earlier GE platforms
  for use on the Sierra turbine platform. It was a necessary precursor to any eventual
  field demonstration of GFM operation for wind turbines.

 Some of the setbacks encountered are as follows:
