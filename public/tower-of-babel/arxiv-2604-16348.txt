Beyond the Townhall: Spatial Anchoring and LLM Agents for Scalable Participatory Urban Planning
Carina I Hausladen*, Javier Argota Sánchez-Vaquerizo, Michael Siebenmann, Arthur Capozzi, Sachit Mahajan, Dirk Helbing

Abstract
Participatory urban planning is central to sustainable city-making, yet the technically demanding nature of such interventions often limits meaningful involvement by diverse publics. We introduce a scalable digital participation platform that embeds sustainability projects within a navigable digital twin. Citizens experience a guided virtual walkthrough with audio narration employing the method of loci and spatial anchoring to support mnemonic encoding and recall. This immersive interface is augmented by two purpose-built LLM assistants: one delivers source-grounded factual clarifications, while the other facilitates reflective discussion. We evaluated this system in a randomized controlled online experiment (N = 195) against conventional industry practices (static visualizations and text-based consultations). Results show that spatially anchored immersive presentation significantly improved information recall, which substantially shifted participants’ attention from individual inconveniences to collective, community-oriented sustainability benefits. Consequently, participants provided significantly more constructive, solution-focused feedback to the (simulated) municipality. These findings establish a practical tool for cities and policymakers to foster inclusive, democratic participation in sustainability transitions. Keywords: GenAI Agents, Participatory Urban Planning, Digital Inclusion, SDG 11 | Sustainable Cities

Introduction

Transitions toward green mobility and climate-resilient infrastructure require not only technical solutions but also public participation: When citizens are meaningfully involved, cities can become more livable, inclusive, and responsive to residents’ needs.
Some sustainability projects, such as planting more trees or installing benches, are relatively straightforward to communicate. But an underground cycling tunnel, for instance, involves layers of technical, financial, and spatial challenges.
Conventional project communication, typically dense websites with schematic drawings, tends to privilege the already fluent and rarely fosters the sustained reflection needed to weigh trade-offs and form grounded opinions.
Information and participation are also poorly integrated:
Citizens are often invited to vote or comment before they have been supported to comprehend. As a result, comment sections frequently exhibit negativity bias
[
77
,
83
]
, and resistance to urban change may not reflect hostility to sustainable action but exclusion from understanding and meaningful participation.

This study addresses this democratic deficit. It develops and experimentally tests a civic participation platform built on three innovations:
First, urban planning is inherently spatial—and so is human cognition.
The platform employs an immersive digital twin that presents information according to the method of loci, a mnemonic technique that connects visuals to information, and principles of event segmentation that break information-dense materials into intelligible episodes.
As a result, multifaceted proposals become easier to grasp, remember, and debate.
Second, while virtual reality can deepen engagement, it is hard to scale. Our platform, therefore, turns to scalable forms of immersion such as first-person 360-degree videos and spatial audio.
Third, we introduce and causally test a novel design for LLMs in civic deliberation. We engineer two distinct LLM personas: one providing retrieval-augmented information; the other facilitating reflection by surfacing normative assumptions.
By experimentally controlling the information users receive prior to the LLM dialogue, we offer causal evidence on how LLM design can support informed participation in sustainability transitions.

Figure 1:
Overview of the behavioral experiment.
The study uses a photorealistic digital twin of the city center of Lausanne in which an urban sustainability project is implemented. Following information delivery, key outcomes were assessed, including, among others, the interactions with two purpose-built LLM personas: Flo offers the retrieval-augmented generation of source-grounded factual clarifications, and Gustavo facilitates normative reflection, perspective-taking, and deliberative discussion.

Related Literature

Participation is essential.

Civic participation in urban sustainability is gaining momentum worldwide, as it becomes increasingly clear that transitions to green mobility, climate-resilient infrastructure, and low-carbon cities depend on more than technical innovation alone
[
28
,
76
]
. Meaningful citizen engagement ensures that solutions are contextually relevant, fair, and politically durable
[
76
,
82
]
. Public involvement is therefore a practical necessity for building inclusive, adaptive, and genuinely liveable cities aligned with sustainability goals.

Participation depends on information.

Yet, participation cannot be meaningful without understanding. Deliberative democracy rests on a shared informational baseline
[
51
,
50
]
: mutual comprehension requires balanced, trustworthy resources
[
23
]
. Well-designed briefing materials have been shown to reduce polarization and enable reflective judgment
[
31
]
. Information must therefore be accessible, engaging, and memorable if it is to be taken up in genuine deliberation. For web-based platforms, this poses a central challenge: how can digital interfaces communicate multifaceted proposals in ways that are scalable and inclusive?

Immersive media improves information processing.

One response is to rethink how information is presented. Rather than relying on text and static diagrams, immersive media—such as virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR)—reshape what participants notice and how they deliberate
[
70
,
63
,
75
,
7
]
. Although their effects remain task- and audience-dependent
[
45
,
37
]
, immersive formats consistently improve comprehension of spatial consequences
[
56
,
42
]
and often shift the substance of public debate
[
27
,
15
]
.

Specifically, photorealistic AI-generated images allow participants to mentally simulate urban futures more vividly, which has been shown to bridge comprehension gaps across political groups
[
24
]
.
Immersive environments extend this effect by strengthening feelings of presence and embodied cognition, which in turn enhance memory recall and empathy
[
74
,
2
,
11
]
. When imagined futures are experienced as “lived”
[
60
]
, they become easier to evaluate and discuss.

This experiential quality is particularly important for perspective-taking.
Citizens rarely hold multiple interpretive frames simultaneously; they tend to approach proposals through a single habitual lens and thus require support for re-imagining the issue from alternative viewpoints. Text-based communication often struggles here, as it depends on the readers’ ability to construct unfamiliar spatial and aesthetic scenarios.

Immersive media (e.g., VR), by contrast, enable citizens to encounter proposals from perspectives they might otherwise overlook
[
44
]
.
Yet VR remains difficult to scale: Development cost, hardware requirements, and accessibility constraints limit its viability for citywide consultations. In this paper, we focus on the
scalable
elements of immersion: First-person 360-degree video and 3D audio. Those can be widely accessed via standard Web browsers without specialized equipment and, thus, are practical for real-world civic engagement platforms.

Memory is critical for participation.

However, immediate comprehension is not enough; meaningful democratic participation requires that citizens recall these details long enough to deliberate and vote.
Therefore, the design of participatory formats must align with the cognitive mechanisms of memory: Information is better recalled when presented simultaneously through a combination of verbal and visual channels
[
59
]
. Recall also improves when information is situated in structured contexts and anchored to physical or imagined spatial environments
[
20
,
53
]
.
A powerful example of spatial anchoring is the method of loci, a mnemonic strategy dating back at least to Ancient Greece and still used by memory athletes today. By mentally placing information along a familiar route, individuals can substantially enhance recall
[
59
,
47
]
.

Besides tying information
to
objects, movement
between
objects further strengthens memory:
Contextual boundaries, such as moving through doorways, disrupt working memory
[
65
,
84
]
.
This disruption serves an adaptive function by segmenting large blocks of information into smaller, discrete units (or events), thereby improving overall retention and organization in episodic memory
[
64
]
.
Recent findings suggest that immersive environments, such as VR, can amplify these effects
[
55
,
66
,
79
]
.

Unlike marketing practices, which often employ framing and affective cues to steer attitudes, our approach treats spatial anchoring as a mnemonic tool. The aim is to help citizens encode and retrieve information without altering its evaluative content.

Information and participation are poorly integrated.

This cognitive lens exposes a practical limitation of many online participation platforms. A widely used participation platform is Decidim, which offers feedback mechanisms such as liking, voting, and commenting, but typically provides only a combination of text-centered descriptions and images of proposals up for vote
[
78
,
71
,
52
,
49
]
.

In other words, information and participation are not cognitively connected: There is rarely an intermediate step that encourages citizens to retrieve what they have learned before expressing an opinion. Yet, retrieval is widely recognized as one of the most powerful mechanisms in learning
[
68
]
.
Moreover, static briefing materials cannot anticipate every clarification a citizen might desire. Before voting, individuals often seek additional details that are personally relevant, yet too specific or too extensive to include in concise project summaries.
Conversational LLMs can offer a potential solution.

LLMs introduce three tensions.

The appeal of conversational systems is evident: ChatGPT reached 100 million users within two months of launch
[
67
]
, and studies show that users often prefer conversational interfaces to traditional survey formats
[
17
]
. Such systems, therefore, hold considerable promise for civic engagement.

However, deploying LLMs in democratic governance faces three tensions:

First, an epistemic reliability problem.
Public trust in governmental AI remains low: 77% of respondents report distrust in government use of AI systems
[
35
]
, largely due to concerns about hallucinations, misinformation, and bias
[
61
,
29
,
58
]
. These concerns are well-founded: LLMs can generate plausible but false statements
[
26
]
. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and explicit source citation have been proposed to mitigate such risks
[
61
,
26
]
. Yet, these techniques are rarely tested in participatory planning contexts.

Second, a normative legitimacy problem.
Democratic communication is not judged solely by factual correctness. Habermas
[
38
]
argues that legitimate deliberation approximates an ideal speech situation, in which arguments are evaluated according to standards of truth, sincerity, and normative rightness. However, critical theorists such as Foucault
[
33
]
emphasize that discourse is never fully neutral, but is embedded within historically structured relations of power. In practice, public debates are shaped by dominant assumptions and social norms that influence which arguments appear reasonable or legitimate.
Urban planning scholarship reflects this by concepts such as moto-normativity, i.e., the historically entrenched privilege of automobility in policy-making and public discourse
[
80
]
.
In such contexts, merely presenting “both sides" of an issue does not guarantee neutrality, because the range of acceptable arguments is already structured by prevailing norms
[
34
,
43
,
41
]
.
Consequently, what a “neutral" AI system should look like in deliberative settings in the context of urban planning is not straightforward. Determining this requires both ethical reflection and empirical evaluation, including examining how such systems are perceived and judged by the publics they are intended to serve.

Third, a functional design conflict.
Civic participation requires both accurate information and reflective dialogue. However, combining these roles within a single conversational agent risks conflating epistemic authority with normative facilitation. Systems optimized for factual precision may suppress reflection; systems designed to stimulate deliberation may introduce framing effects.

This paper addresses the above tensions through a dual-persona LLM architecture: one persona is constrained to source-cited factual responses, while a second persona is instructed to facilitate reflection.
Ultimately, decisions about how conversational AI should structure democratic discourse cannot be resolved theoretically; they require context-specific and democratically grounded judgment, which is assisted by our platform.

Contributions of this paper.

Building on the cognitive, immersive, and governance challenges identified above, we integrate information encoding, retrieval, and deliberative facilitation into a unified participatory architecture.
Our primary contributions are the following three:

(1) Mnemonic-enhanced digital twin:

We create a digital twin of a real street environment and embed the visual elements of the sustainability project within it.
We deliver additional project information via audio narration guided by mnemonic strategies. Specifically, we apply event segmentation to chunk information: Citizens “walk” virtually between locations to offer natural breaks that reduce overload and aid modular encoding.
Within each chunk, we deploy the method of loci to anchor details to salient physical objects in the twin.
This combination—spatial mnemonic structuring in a digital twin—is novel in civic participation contexts.

(2) Scalable immersive experience and participation:

VR/AR digital twins have been shown to support urban planning and engagement, but they lack scalability. We redesign immersion for scale while keeping its cognitive benefits. We deploy first-person 360-degree videos recorded in the real environment and paired with 3D spatial audio, which enable immersive walkthroughs on any web browser or device. To scale deliberation alongside immersion, the platform integrates interactive LLM avatars. This deliberate alignment of preserved immersion and scalable deliberation within a single browser-based platform represents a novel approach to participatory urban planning for sustainability projects.

(3) Causally tested LLM design principles:

Deploying LLMs participatory sustainability raises challenges of epistemic reliability as well as a tension between Habermas’ ideal of reason-based legitimacy and historically dominant motor-normative frames in environmental discourse.
We translate this theoretical tension into a system architecture that separates epistemic and deliberative functions, represented by two distinct LLM personas: one constrained to RAG-grounded information provision, and a second designed to facilitate reflective dialogue. We then causally evaluate this design by experimentally controlling exposure to project information within the platform.

Experimental Design

In our experiment, participants (
N
= 195) were asked to take the role of citizens in a fictional city and vote on a proposed street redesign project. The project was inspired by a real Swiss redesign project to take place in Lausanne
1
1

1

https://www.lausanne.ch/vie-pratique/mobilite/espaces-publics/avenue-echallens.html
but not identical (
A.4
). It centered on promoting sustainable and democratic use of public space by reallocating road capacity, e.g., by adding bike lanes, reducing parking spaces, and introducing benches, greenery, and bike racks.

We visualized the planned changes using a digital twin of the city of Lausanne, reconstructed from a high-resolution photogrammetric drone scan (
A.2
). The proposed street modifications were then implemented within this urban digital twin.

We used a between-subjects design to experimentally vary how information was presented to participants. Both groups received identical content across six information blocks: residents, traffic, parking, tree canopy, biodiversity, and sponge pavement (
Fig. 1
shows the spatial anchoring of these information blocks in the digital twin).

In the
treatment
group, participants viewed short 360-degree video clips from a first-person perspective with spatial audio.
The narration explicitly anchored key information to visible landmarks (method of loci);
The first-person perspective transitioned sequentially between six locations along the street to create event segmentation boundaries that reinforced the impression of walking forward and strengthened memory encoding through contextual shifts.

In the
control
group, participants were shown static images of the status quo and the envisioned redesign, rather than a video. Information was presented in written text rather than narration and did not explicitly anchor key points to local visual elements.

We measured several outcome variables:

Recall.

After receiving the information, participants were asked to write down everything they remembered in an open-text field.

LLMs.

Participants engaged with two LLMs, explicitly presented as AI systems. “Flo” provided factual information constrained to verified project documentation; “Gustavo” facilitated deliberative reflection by revealing trade-offs and encouraging perspective-taking.

Voting.

Participants proceeded to vote on the street redesign project in three ways: (i) approval voting, (ii) explicit ranking, and (iii) giving an overall yes/no vote.

Consultation.

Participants could, via a free-text field, submit consultation proposals to the city.

Questions.

Finally, participants completed questionnaires on their evaluation of the effectiveness of video-based information provision and their traffic habits.

Experiment Ethics.

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations for research involving human participants. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Commission of ETH Zurich.

The experiment was conducted online with participants recruited via Prolific. Before participation, all subjects provided informed consent electronically. Participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, their right to withdraw at any time without penalty, and the handling of their data.

The use of LLMs within the experimental design was explicitly reviewed and approved by the Ethics Commission. Participants were clearly informed that they would interact with LLMs during the study and were instructed not to submit personal data such as names or other identifying information in their interactions.

To ensure data protection, experimental response data were stored separately from sociodemographic information. Sociodemographic data were obtained from Prolific and matched via Prolific ID, but were never stored together with experimental response data. The experiment was hosted on a server located in Switzerland.

Treatment participants used Gustavo as a cognitive scaffold for future simulation. While doing so they showed significantly higher

Thus, the spatial information-provision enabled collaborative exploration rather than defensive interrogation. The same LLM interface became more productive when participants had cognitive infrastructure to mentally inhabit the renovated space rather than defend current routines against logistical disruption.

An interesting finding in the Gustavo chat was that, while there was a high prevalence of positive sentiment, there was a distinct minority of five (out of 195) participants who exhibited interaction patterns that were entirely one-sided and negative.
The chats of these individuals demonstrated the limitations of the LLM: in the presence of an entrenched, extremely negative viewpoint, Gustavo was unable to encourage the consideration of positive aspects or alternative perspectives.

Another notable finding from both chats was the emergence of highly specific and locally grounded questions—for example: “Are the newly planted trees well-rooted? Urban trees often fail because their roots don’t take,” or “Will there be services for leaf pickup and gardening?”
Such granular concerns rarely appear in standardized public consultations or official information materials, which must remain concise and cannot anticipate every issue that matters to residents. Yet, these questions reveal authentic priorities and lived experience. A conversational LLM interface can accommodate this breadth of inquiry that would otherwise be too resource-intensive for planners to prepare in advance.

We also evaluated the LLMs’ responses to participant queries, focusing on whether they introduced any information beyond the provided fact package (RAG context). The models were explicitly instructed to rely solely on that package and to indicate when relevant information was missing. Overall, they adhered to these constraints, with only one exception: in a single interaction, repeated user prompts led Gustavo to offer information not contained in the fact package, though the information was still factually correct.

Conclusion and Discussion

We conducted a between-subjects experiment to test whether web-based 360-degree immersive environments designed according to spatial memory principles and augmented by LLM-mediated interactions can strengthen democratic participation in sustainable urban development.

Contextual understanding strengthens democratic deliberation on sustainability projects.

The treatment group recalled significantly more project details than the control group. The positive treatment effect reflects the combined influence of immersive spatial engagement and enhanced mnemonic encoding.
Importantly, improved recall translated into qualitative differences in engagement. Control participants more frequently emphasized operational barriers and compensation concerns. In contrast, treatment participants demonstrated greater visual empathy and more collaborative, community-oriented proposals.
Thus, our treatment successfully shifted engagement from defensive reactions towards constructive deliberation.
Notably, these effects were achieved without persuasive framing.
The findings suggest that immersive engagement combined with mnemonic encoding strengthens Habermasian deliberation.

Design recommendations for civic LLM systems.

Most participants rated the LLMs as very or extremely helpful, and half of the participants reported having high trust in them.
However, preferences differed regarding the separation of agent roles. While some valued the distinction of fact- and opinion-oriented agents, others saw it as redundant.
We therefore recommend retaining both agent types while allowing citizens to choose freely between them.
Additionally, opinion-oriented agents should limit follow-up questions.
For participants exhibiting entrenched resistance to sustainability interventions, LLMs should be complemented with in-person deliberative formats.

A scalable web platform for democratic participation in sustainability transitions.

Traditional vignette studies suffer from hypothetical bias and low ecological validity
[
3
,
1
]
, while immersive VR studies, though realistic, cannot scale to citywide consultations
[
72
,
21
]
.
Our approach bridges this gap: participants showed sustained engagement, posed substantive follow-up questions, and interacted with the LLMs beyond the minimum task requirements.

Critically, participant inquiries revealed that our information material omitted project costs and construction timelines—mirroring omissions in the real planning documents we received.
This diagnostic capability is particularly valuable: Remember that our digital twin was constructed for a different street than the one where the real project in Lausanne was planned to happen. We used a scan from a different part of the city and standard Unity assets, yet our setup still identified communication gaps that would undermine public trust in the real sustainability project.
For practitioners, this offers a low-cost pre-deployment testing mechanism: municipalities can reuse existing spatial data, modify it cost-effectively with standard asset libraries, and identify critical information deficits before launching consultations.

Addressing negativity bias in online sustainability discourse.

Our findings document the reversal of a well-documented pattern in web-based participation. When participants provided feedback to the fictitious city, positive sentiment predominated. This contrasts sharply with real online comments on the real project that inspired our study. Those reactions were predominantly negative and hostile (Appendix
A.6
).
Our platform, thus, offers a potential remedy and a mechanism to support constructive deliberation about sustainability projects.

Limitations and generalizability.

The investigated infrastructure project was relatively non-partisan. Future work could test divisive sustainability topics such as housing density, renewable energy installations, or congestion pricing.
Furthermore, our intervention was not effective among participants holding strongly negative prior views. In such cases, in-person engagement seems necessary to foster meaningful deliberation.
Our study was conducted in English and with a non-representative sample (see Table
A.1
);
Broader validation is, therefore, advised. Finally, the long-term engagement dynamics remain unknown.
Despite these limitations, our findings suggest that web-based immersive environments combined with carefully constrained LLMs can meaningfully improve democratic participation in sustainable urban governance.

Democratic sustainability communication.

Immersive and conversational systems require careful implementation.
While sensory-spatial features can enhance recall and engagement, depending on the kind of implementation, immersive environments can also have significant persuasive potential, but should not be used to manufacture consent
[
32
,
73
]
.
Additionally, conversational systems should not become forms of tokenistic participation or “window dressing," where citizens are invited to express opinions, without actually having a meaningful influence on decision-making processes
[
9
]
.
It is, therefore, important to empirically validate and continuously assess such systems in practical applications. Importantly, in democratic contexts, the aim is not to produce a unanimous consensus but to support the development of reasonable and broadly acceptable solutions, valuing diversity and pluralistic perspectives.

A.1
Experiment Ethics

The experiment was conducted online with participants recruited via Prolific. The experimental design was approved by the ethics commission of ETH Zürich (EK 2024-N-99).
The surveys were programmed using SurveyJS. The LLMs (GPT-4o, checkpoint
gpt-4o-2024-08-06
) were accessed via the OpenAI API.
Their use was explicitly reviewed and approved by the ethics commission. Participants were repeatedly and clearly informed that they would interact with LLMs in the study. Participants were explicitly instructed not to submit personal data such as names or other identifying information in their interactions with the LLMs.
To ensure data protection, the collection of decision data was kept separate from demographic information. Sociodemographic data were downloaded from Prolific and matched via the Prolific ID but were never stored together with experimental data. The experiment ran on a server located in Switzerland to ensure compliance with data protection regulations.

A.4
A Realistic Experimental Vignette

As part of the experimental vignette, participants were exposed to a virtual scenario depicting an urban street reconstruction project. The intervention was modeled at Rue de la Cathédrale in Lausanne, but its design elements were oriented at an actual, ongoing reconstruction project conducted on a different street in Lausanne (Avenue d’Echallens
6
6

6

https://www.lausanne.ch/vie-pratique/mobilite/espaces-publics/avenue-echallens.html
). Key aspects of the real project—such as the creation of expanded bicycle lanes, the introduction of additional greenery, and a significant reduction in car parking—were selectively incorporated into the virtual environment.
To ensure plausibility and internal consistency, both the implementation and description of these interventions were based on planning documents published by the City of Lausanne.
Importantly, we deliberately drew inspiration from a reconstruction project on a different street from the one modeled in VR. In addition, the virtual city was given a fictitious name (Ville d’Ordinaire) to preserve scientific independence and avoid any direct association with Lausanne. To further reduce potential bias, participants were recruited exclusively from outside Switzerland (but within a European context), ensuring that while the environment appeared recognizably European and realistic, participants were unlikely to have prior personal exposure to the actual location.

Narrator scripts guided participants through six information blocks in the immersive environment. Below we present three representative examples:

Block I: Residents.

Look at the people sitting here: neighbors, shop owners, local residents. First, about 500 residents contributed their ideas through surveys. Then, 35 local businesses added their perspectives—including the crêpe shop owner just over there, where people are gathered now. Finally, around 40 community members joined two design workshops to explore the project in more detail. All of this input was compiled into a document called the “Recommendations Notebook.” You can see copies of it lying on the tables just ahead. The design team worked with this notebook, translating the different citizens’ ideas into the renovation plans that we will explore next.

Block II: New Traffic Balance.

This is the main avenue of Ville d’Ordinaire. Before the redesign, most of this space was filled with cars. Do you hear the steady rush of engines? Now listen again, the sound shifted. Why? Look next to the curb: A dedicated lane was painted for cyclists, to set them apart from car traffic. Also, glance at the road markings: They signal a newly reduced pace of 30 kilometers per hour. Cars can only move much more slowly now. Studies show that this speed is safer for pedestrians and cyclists to share the road with cars.

Block VI: Sponge City.

Look down. Before the redesign, this area was a continuous layer of asphalt. Rainwater couldn’t seep through; it flowed directly into pipes. Now, notice the surface beneath your feet: interlocking tiles, open in the center. About 100 square meters will be de-paved. Hear the splash ahead? Imagine it as rainfall from a summer storm. Instead of rushing straight underground, water here can filter through the tiles into a 30-centimeter layer of gravel and soil. That layer can hold and gradually release up to 2,000 liters of water for every 100 square meters—roughly ten bathtubs full of water.

A.5
Qualitative Data Analaysis

We used an iterative, human–LLM collaborative coding process. First, one author conducted open coding on each qualitative variable, generating an initial set of thematic labels. This preliminary codebook was refined through discussion among the author team, then reapplied to the full dataset.
Next, the same variable was coded by Gemini 3 Pro, which was given structured, hierarchical instructions
[
57
]
to propose lower-level codes and aggregate them into higher-level categories. The author team compared the human-generated and LLM-generated codes and reconciled discrepancies to form a unified coding scheme.
This consolidated scheme was applied to the dataset by the authors, after which the LLM evaluated whether the coding had been applied consistently.
The resulting annotated dataset formed the basis for all subsequent analyses. Figures in the main text report only the most frequent codes for each variable.
