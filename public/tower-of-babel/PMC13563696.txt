Intraventricular schwannoma presenting with memory impairment and full neurological recovery after surgical resection: a case report
Authors: Michel Mpiene Kisubi, Larrey Kasereka Kamabu, Alphonse Lubansu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntraventricular schwannomas are exceptionally rare benign tumors, accounting for a minute fraction of intracranial schwannomas. Their occurrence within the lateral ventricle is particularly uncommon, and the clinical presentation is often nonspecific, posing significant diagnostic challenges. To date, only a limited number of cases have been reported worldwide. We present a rare case of a left lateral ventricular schwannoma manifesting predominantly as transient isolated memory impairment, an unusual presentation that broadens the recognized clinical spectrum of this entity.Case presentationA 44-year-old woman of Flemish origin presented with a two-day history of transient memory impairment without any identifiable precipitating factor. The episode was associated with a single vomiting event and an isolated fever spike on the preceding day. She denied focal neurological deficits, headaches, seizures, visual disturbances, or other systemic symptoms. Her medical history was notable only for childhood epilepsy that had completely resolved by the age of 12 years. Neurological and general physical examinations were normal. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a well-circumscribed intraventricular mass located in the left lateral ventricle, radiologically suggestive of a benign neoplasm. Electroencephalography showed no epileptiform abnormalities. Following preoperative corticosteroid therapy, the patient underwent neuronavigation-assisted left parietal craniotomy with gross-total microsurgical resection of the lesion. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma. The post-operative course was uneventful, and the patient achieved complete neurological recovery without residual memory deficits or new neurological impairments.ConclusionsThis case highlights the exceptional rarity of intraventricular schwannomas and demonstrates that isolated transient memory impairment may represent an atypical presenting manifestation. Complete microsurgical excision remains the treatment of choice and is associated with excellent neurological outcomes. Awareness of this rare diagnosis may facilitate earlier recognition and appropriate management of unusual intraventricular lesions, thereby expanding current understanding of the clinical spectrum of intraventricular schwannomas.Keywords: Intraventricular schwannoma, Memory impairment, Surgical resection, Neurological recovery

Introduction

Schwannoma is a tumor arising from the Schwann cells that surround peripheral nerves. In general, schwannoma accounts for around 8% of all brain tumors and 25% of vertebral tumors [1]. Intraventricular schwannomas are rarely encountered in neurosurgical practice. The development and progression of a schwannoma within the ventricular system remains a hypothetical theory. It is proposed that these tumors may arise from Schwann cells along perivascular nerve plexuses or from ectopic Schwann cells misplaced during embryogenesis [2].

Intraventricular tumors represent less than 1% of all intracranial lesions, posing a significant diagnostic challenge due to the wide variety of possible tumor types, which differ between adult and pediatric populations [3]. Various neoplastic entities can originate from the ventricular system, including colloid cysts, craniopharyngiomas, astrocytomas, medulloblastomas, glioblastomas, choroid plexus papillomas and carcinomas, lymphomas, oligodendrogliomas, meningiomas, and metastases. Among these, intraventricular schwannomas are extremely uncommon, with fewer than 50 cases documented in the literature [4].

The clinical presentation of intraventricular schwannomas is often nonspecific, varying from headache and seizures to cognitive disturbances depending on the location and size of the lesion [5]. Given their rarity and variable clinical presentation, establishing a precise preoperative diagnosis of intraventricular schwannomas remains difficult. Definitive diagnosis and appropriate management rely on advanced imaging modalities combined with histopathological examination.

This case report adds to the scarce literature on intraventricular schwannomas, especially those manifesting with isolated memory impairment. It emphasizes the need to include intraventricular schwannomas in the differential diagnosis of unusual neurological symptoms, even in the absence of classic manifestations like headaches or seizures. This report emphasizes the role of advanced imaging, neuronavigation-guided surgery, and histopathological examination in the accurate diagnosis and successful management of such rare lesions. Furthermore, the complete neurological recovery observed postoperatively supports the notion that early intervention can lead to favorable outcomes in these rare cases.

Case presentation

A 44-year-old right-handed female, Flemish origin presented with an acute episode of memory impairment that began two days prior to admission. The patient reported no identifiable trigger for the episode. During the episode, she experienced a single episode of vomiting without associated nausea. One day before the memory disturbance, she had an isolated peak of fever (38.2 °C) without any accompanying symptoms. There were no reports of headaches other than her usual tension-type headaches, and no focal neurological deficits were initially noted.

Medical, family, and psycho-social history

The patient’s past medical history included a single epileptic seizure at the age of two, for which she was placed under antiepileptic treatment (valproic acid) until the age of 12, with no recurrence of seizures thereafter. There was no significant family history of neurological diseases, psychiatric disorders, or malignancies. The patient had no known allergies or chronic illnesses and led an active lifestyle without any psycho-social distress.

Clinical findings

On physical examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable, alert, and oriented. Neurological examination showed no motor or sensory deficits. Deep tendon reflexes were symmetrical, and cranial nerves were intact. Cognitive assessment revealed mild memory impairment with normal attention span and executive functions. Fundoscopy showed no papilledema. Formal neuropsychological tools such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) were not systematically performed during the acute phase, which constitutes a limitation of this report.

Diagnostic assessment

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig. 1) revealed a well-circumscribed, heterogeneously enhancing intraventricular mass in the left lateral ventricle near the parietal region, measuring approximately 73 × 48 × 48 mm. No signs of hydrocephalus or perilesional edema were observed. electroencephalogram (EEG) showed no epileptiform activity. Laboratory tests, including complete blood count, metabolic panel, and inflammatory markers, were unremarkable.

Magnetic resonance imaging on admission showed a mass syndrome developed within the lateral ventricles, predominantly on the left side, 73 × 48 × 48 mm in largest diameters Anteroposterior × Laterolateral × Craniocaudal dimensions (APxLLxCC). The lesion was heterogeneous, with a tissue component developed within the frontal horn of the left lateral ventricle surrounded by numerous cysts. The fleshy component of the lesion itself appeared heterogeneous, with a moderately hypercellular and hyperperfused peripheral tissue component, with calcifications and intense, heterogeneous enhancement

Diagnostic challenges

Given the atypical location of the tumor, differential diagnoses included ependymoma, central neurocytoma, subependymoma, and metastasis. The rarity of intraventricular schwannomas posed a diagnostic challenge, further complicated by the absence of vestibular symptoms or cranial nerve deficits.

Diagnosis

Histopathological examination of the resected tumor (Fig. 2) confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma, with spindle cells arranged in Antoni A and Antoni B patterns, positive for S100 protein, and negative for epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), excluding meningioma.

The histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of a schwannoma, a World Health Organization (WHO) grade I tumor

Prognosis

The prognosis for completely resected intraventricular schwannomas is generally favorable, with low recurrence rates. Given the total excision, the patient’s prognosis was excellent.

Therapeutic intervention

Corticosteroid therapy (methylprednisolone 40 mg, three times a day, for two days) was initiated before surgery to reduce cerebral edema. Surgical resection was performed through a left parietal craniotomy guided by neuronavigation. A complete resection of the tumor was achieved without complications. The superior parietal transcortical route was preferred over a transcallosal approach because of the marked lateral extension and large tumor size, providing a more direct trajectory while minimizing risks of callosal injury and disconnection syndromes. Corticosteroids were administered empirically to minimize perioperative inflammatory response and reduce the potential risk of cerebral edema despite the absence of significant edema on MRI.

Post-operative course

The immediate post-operative period was uneventful. MRI (Fig. 3) confirmed total resection of the mass without residual tumor. The patient experienced full neurological recovery, with resolution of memory impairment and no recurrence of vomiting.

The post-operative magnetic resonance imaging performed on November 8th 2023 showed a complete excision of the gadolinium-enhanced fleshy lesion component previously present within the frontal horn of the left lateral ventricle

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient was discharged on post-operative day 14 with a follow-up appointment scheduled at one month. At three-month follow-up, she remained asymptomatic, with no evidence of recurrence on brain MRI. Histopathological findings corroborated the diagnosis of schwannoma. There were no adverse or unanticipated events.

Discussion

Intraventricular schwannomas are exceptionally rare tumors, with only 47 cases reported in the literature, including the present case [4]. Reported patients range from 7 to 78 years of age, with a predominance in males (66%). Headache is the most common presenting symptom, occurring in approximately 56% of cases, followed by focal neurological deficits, gait disturbances, vertigo, vomiting, papilledema, and seizures [6, 7]. Malignant intraventricular schwannomas are exceedingly uncommon and have been reported predominantly in older patients [2, 8].

Despite the predominance of symptoms related to increased intracranial pressure, several atypical clinical presentations have been described. These include temporal lobe epilepsy [9], psychomotor slowing [10], homonymous hemianopsia [11], asymptomatic papilledema [12], tinnitus and vertigo [13], incidental tumor discovery [14], and memory disturbances associated with hemineglect [2]. Furthermore, only a small proportion of reported tumors have arisen within the left lateral ventricle [15, 16].

The present case is particularly unusual because transient isolated memory impairment was the predominant clinical manifestation. Unlike most previously reported patients, our patient had no focal neurological deficit, hydrocephalus, papilledema, or other signs of significant intracranial hypertension. This distinctive presentation broadens the recognized clinical spectrum of intraventricular schwannomas and suggests that subtle disruption of limbic memory networks may occur even in the absence of overt radiological compression of classical memory-related structures [2, 8].

Nevertheless, a definitive causal relationship between the tumor and the transient amnestic episode cannot be conclusively established. Alternative mechanisms should be considered, including transient focal epileptic activity, inflammatory processes, metabolic disturbances, or a combination of these factors. Importantly, the patient had a remote history of childhood epilepsy, and although the electroencephalogram was normal, a normal interictal EEG does not exclude seizure-related phenomena. Therefore, a transient focal epileptic event involving limbic structures remains a plausible differential explanation.

The isolated episode of fever (38.2 °C) and vomiting further complicates the interpretation of the initial presentation. Extensive clinical and laboratory investigations did not identify evidence of systemic infection. Consequently, the transient fever may have represented either a nonspecific inflammatory response or a central thermoregulatory disturbance resulting from altered hypothalamic or ventricular function. Although the patient did not demonstrate overt hydrocephalus, the considerable tumor volume and associated ventricular distortion may have produced transient fluctuations in intracranial pressure or local ventricular compression sufficient to affect central autonomic regulatory pathways. Such mechanisms have occasionally been described in patients with deep-seated intracranial lesions and may explain the self-limited febrile episode observed in our patient.

Despite these uncertainties, the complete resolution of memory impairment following gross-total resection, together with the absence of symptom recurrence during follow-up, supports a probable association between the intraventricular schwannoma and the transient cognitive dysfunction. This case highlights the importance of considering rare intraventricular tumors in the differential diagnosis of otherwise unexplained transient cognitive disturbances. Increased awareness of such atypical presentations may facilitate earlier diagnosis, more accurate diagnostic reasoning, and timely neurosurgical intervention in patients harboring these exceptionally rare lesions.

The etiopathogenesis of intraventricular schwannomas remains a subject of debate. The first case was documented by David et al. in 1965. The presence of nerve fibers within the choroid plexus was initially observed by Benedikt in 1874, with subsequent detailed descriptions by Stöhr and Pick on the involvement of Schwann cells in choroidal innervation [17, 18]. It is hypothesized that these tumors originate from hyperplasia of the perivascular sympathetic nerve plexuses [19].

Therapeutic intervention

Corticosteroid therapy was initiated with methylprednisolone 40 mg, three times a day, for two days to manage potential cerebral edema, consistent with evidence suggesting its role in reducing peritumoral inflammation in intracranial tumors [20]. Neuronavigation-guided left parietal craniotomy was performed to ensure precise tumor localization and minimize brain parenchymal injury, aligning with modern surgical standards [21]. A complete resection of the tumor was achieved without complications, which is critical in optimizing long-term outcomes in benign intracranial tumors [22].

Post-operatively, the patient was managed with intravenous analgesics and prophylactic antiepileptic medication (levetiracetam 500 mg twice daily) to prevent seizures, in line with guidelines for post-craniotomy seizure prophylaxis [23]. Early mobilization and supportive care were implemented to reduce the risk of venous thromboembolism and other post-surgical complications, consistent with Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols [24].

One of the strengths of this case report is its contribution to the limited body of knowledge on intraventricular schwannomas, which remain exceptionally rare. Conclusion

Intraventricular schwannoma remains a rare and exceptional tumor. This case highlights the rarity of intraventricular schwannomas and emphasizes the importance of considering schwannomas in the differential diagnosis of intraventricular tumors. Successful total resection can lead to excellent clinical outcomes with full neurological recovery.
