Abstract
Menger’s Theorem is a fundamental result in graph theory. It states that if in a graph G G with distinguished sets of terminal vertices S S and T T there are no k k pairwise vertex-disjoint S S - T T paths, then there is a set of less than k k vertices that intersects every S S - T T path. In this work, we give a coarse variant of this result for planar and bounded genus graphs. Precisely, we prove that for every surface Σ \Sigma there is a function f : ℕ × ℕ → ℕ f\colon\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} such that for every pair of integers d , k ∈ ℕ d,k\in\mathbb{N} and a Σ \Sigma -embeddable graph G G with distinguished sets of terminal vertices S S and T T , if G G does not contain a family of k k S S - T T paths that are pairwise at distance larger than d d , then there is a set X X consisting of at most f ⁡ ( d , k ) f(d,k) vertices of G G such that every S S - T T path is at distance at most d d from a vertex of X X . This partially answers questions of Nguyen, Scott, and Seymour [ 40 ] , who proved that such a result cannot hold in general graphs. A key ingredient of our proof is a structure theorem from the developing “colorful” graph minor theory, where the focus is on studying the structure in a graph relative to some fixed subsets of annotated vertices. In our case, these annotated vertices are S S and T T .

Overview of the approach

We now give a brief and informal overview of the techniques used to establish

.
Throughout most of the overview we focus on the case of planar graphs; that is, when
\Sigma

This structure is also reflected in the later section: throughout most of the paper we work towards the proof of the planar case, which is concluded in
section

9

10

Hence, from now on we assume we are working with a graph
G
embedded on the sphere, sets of vertices
S,T\subseteq V(G)
which we shall call

, and parameters
d,k\in\mathbb{N}

-linkage

-

The main idea of the proof is that for each
Z\in\{S,T\}
, there will be a fundamental case distinction: whether
Z
is “localized” or “widespread” on the embedding of
G
. Informally, by “localized” we mean that all the vertices of
Z
are close to a single face of
G
, or maybe to a bounded number of faces of
G
. And by “widespread” we mean that the vertices of
Z
can be found on many different faces of
G
that are far from each other. The intuition is that if
Z
is “widespread”, then it should be very easy to route a scattered linkage to
Z
, essentially by routing it to different vertices of
Z
that are pairwise far from each other on the embedding. On the other hand, if
Z
is “localized”, then we can analyze the local structure around the few faces to which
Z
is close using standard packing/hitting arguments, developed for various Erdős-Pósa duality statements in the theory of Graph Minors.

Bidimensionality.

However, how do we quantify whether a set

is “localized” or “widespread” in a planar graph

Further complications.

As the experienced reader probably suspects, in the description above we have cheated multiple times. The largest liberty we have taken is that
section

4.2
does not really just assume that
G
has large treewidth, but it provides a local decomposition of the graph relative to some tangle of huge order, controlled by a huge wall in the graph. And it is not really the case that vortices have bounded pathwidth, but they admit path decompositions of small adhesion where the bags can be large, but are separated from the tangle by small separators (consisting of the two adhesions incident to the bag). In fact, the reasoning presented above provides us with a certain Local Structure Theorem: we may either find a large
d
-scattered
S
-
T
-linkage in
G
, or a set consisting of a bounded number of radius-
d
balls that hits every
S
-
T
-path, except possibly for those that are entirely hidden in the bags of vortices. By standard means, we then turn this Local Structure Theorem into a suitable Global Structure Theorem, which provides a tree decomposition of the whole graph where every bag is equipped with a hitting set (consisting of a bounded number of balls of radius
d
) for
S
-
T
-paths that are “relevant” for this bag. The final stroke in the proof of the planar case of
section

1
is a standard greedy bottom-up argument applied on this tree decomposition.

Beyond planar graphs.

Finally, we discuss the techniques necessary to lift the reasoning presented above from the planar case to the case of graphs embeddable in some fixed surface
\Sigma
of positive genus.

In case
G
has an embedding on
\Sigma
with large
representativity
,
section

4.2
provides a walloid that additionally contains segments representing all the topological features (handles and cross-caps) of
\Sigma
.
These segments can be essentially ignored in the further analysis, and the whole argumentation goes through with minimal changes. In fact, in our proof we essentially show how to reduce this setting to the planar case, by finding a disk
\Delta
in the surface that contains all the relevant features of the walloid, so that the whole reasoning can be performed within
\Delta
.

However, a priori we do not know that the embedding in the surface has large representativity. To deal with embeddings of small representativity, we inductively cut the embedding along non-contractible curves of small length, effectively splitting the graphs into multiple components, each embeddable on a surface of Euler-genus strictly less than that of
\Sigma.

This approach will inevitably introduce a set of
apices

A\subseteq V(G)
. The apices are not embedded on the surface and can have arbitrary neighborhoods within the vortices (and nowhere else), but their number
|A|
is bounded.
Note that in principle, the existence of apices might influence the distances between the components from within the vortices, and even create short connections between two different vortices of the same component.
Apices have to be therefore treated carefully when analyzing the structure around every vortex, but at least they do not influence the distances outside the nests of the vortices.
However, at the end, since the number of apices is bounded, we may always just include the radius-
d
balls around them in the constructed hitting set. Consequently, in all the harvesting arguments applied in the vicinity of the vortices, we only harvest paths disjoint from the balls around the apices.

Although this approach may appear conceptually simple at first glance, the technical challenges, particularly around the cutting of the surface, become somewhat complex.

Well-linked sets.

A set
X\subseteq V(G)
in a graph
G
is
well-linked
if for every separation
(A,B)
of
G,
we have that

|A\cap X|\leqslant|A\cap B|\qquad\textrm{or}\qquad|B\cap X|\leqslant|A\cap B|.

Suppose
|X|\geqslant 3k
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, given a separation
(A,B)
of
G
of order less than
k,
we have that
|A\cap X|<k
, implying
|B\cap X|>2k
, or that
|B\cap X|<k
, implying
|A\cap X|>2k
. In the former case we say that
B
is the
X
-majority side
of
(A,B)
, and in the latter case
A
is the
X
-majority side. Note that these two conditions are mutually exclusive, so exactly one of them holds.

We also need to be able to detect a large wall in a graph that “respects” a given well-linked set of large order in the graph.
This was done by Thilikos and Wiederrecht
[
51
]
by turning a proof of Kawarabayashi, Thomas, and Wollan
[
31
]
into an algorithm.
We need the following definitions.

Given a
(t\times z)
-wall
W
in a graph
G
, for some
t,z\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 3},
we can observe that for every separation
(A,B)
of
G
of order less than
\min\{t,z\}
there is a unique side, say
B,
such that
B-A
contains the whole vertex set of both a vertical and a horizontal path of
W.

We call this the
W
-majority side
of
(A,B).

With this observation in mind, we say that a
(t\times z)
-wall
W
of a graph
G
is
controlled
by a well-linked set
X
of
G
if for every separation
(A,B)
of
G
of order less than
\min\{t,z\},

B
is the
W
-majority side of
(A,B)
if and only if
B
is the
X
-majority side of
(A,B)
as well.

With these definitions in place, we can state the result of Thilikos and Wiederrecht
[
51
]
.

Proposition 2.1

(Thilikos and Wiederrecht
[
51
, Theorem 4.2.]
)
.

There exist constants
c,d\in\mathbb{N}
and an algorithm that, given a graph
G
, an integer
k\geqslant 3
, and a well-linked set
X\subseteq V(G)
of size at least
c\cdot k^{20}
, computes in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{d})}\cdot|G|^{2}\|G\|\log|G|
a
(k\times k)
-wall
W
in
G
controlled by
X.

For notational simplicity we assume that every considered planar graph

A

(respectively

)

\Delta
in
\Sigma
is any set homeomorphic to
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid x^{2}+y^{2}\leqslant 1\}
(respectively, to
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid x^{2}+y^{2}<1\}
).
Whenever we say disk without specifying whether it is open or closed, we always mean a closed disk.
For a disk
\Delta
, we write

•

\mathsf{bd}(\Delta)
for the
boundary
of
\Delta
in
\Sigma
;

•

\mathsf{int}(\Delta)\coloneqq\Delta-\mathsf{bd}(\Delta)
for the
interior
of
\Delta
; and

•

\mathsf{ext}(\Delta)\coloneqq\Sigma-\Delta
for the
exterior
of
\Delta
.

Any disk
\Delta\subseteq\Sigma
we consider throughout the paper will also satisfy
\mathsf{bd}(\Delta)\cap E=\emptyset
; that is, the boundary of
\Delta
intersects

only in vertices.
We shall always assume this to be the case without any further clarification.

\Sigma
-renditions.

A
\Sigma
-rendition
of a planar graph

is a set
\mathfrak{D}
of disks in

satisfying the following:

i)

The disks of
\mathfrak{D}
have pairwise disjoint interiors.

ii)

If
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\in\mathfrak{D}
are distinct, then
\Delta_{1}\cap\Delta_{2}\subseteq V.

iii)

Every edge of

(except possibly its endpoints) is a subset of the interior of one of the disks in
\mathfrak{D}.

iv)

For every disk
\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}
, we have
|\mathsf{bd}(\Delta)\cap V|\leqslant 2.

The

are those vertices of

that do not belong to the interior of any disk of

By

we denote the set of all ground vertices of
\mathfrak{D}
; the graph

will be always clear from the context.
The disks of

will be often called the

of
\mathfrak{D}
.
For a cell

, we define the

of
\Delta
to be the set
\mathsf{per}(\Delta)\coloneqq\mathsf{bd}(\Delta)\cap V=\mathsf{bd}(\Delta)\cap\mathsf{ground}(\mathfrak{D})
.
Thus, condition

asserts that every cell has a periphery of size at most

Linear decompositions and depth.

Let
\Delta

\mathfrak{D}
-aligned disk in
\Sigma
.
Further, let

(V2)

(V3)

of a linear decomposition is the quantity

of a linear decomposition is the quantity

.
The

of

is the smallest integer

has a linear decomposition of adhesion at most

of

is the smallest integer

such that

has a linear decomposition of width at most

.

Let us briefly explain how

The

[

This simplification suffices for our purposes, since we restrict attention exclusively to planar graphs.

In particular, the tightness condition required for

is formulated in a more general way.
There, the authors introduce the notion of a torsoid associated with a

Furthermore, the conclusion of

yields a new

Here, coarser means that all cells contained in the inner vortex disk of each vortex segment (in the sense of our definition of a walloid) are replaced by a single vortex cell, possibly with large periphery.

In our setting, we omit this cell-replacement step, so that the resulting

and entails no loss of generality.

6
Harvesting

In this section we prove a “local” duality statement that applies to individual vortex segments. Roughly speaking, we show that within a vortex segment we can either construct a partial scattered linkage, say
X
-rooted for
X\in\{S,T\}
, or find a small number of balls of small radius that intersect any path that starts in a vertex of
X
and escapes the vortex. Since the process of finding consecutive paths of a partial linkage resembles “harvesting” the paths, we call the corresponding statement the Harvesting Lemma.

For the remainder of this section we fix a planar graph
G
with a vertex subset
F\subseteq V(G),
sets of terminals
S,T\subseteq V(G)
, a rendition
\mathfrak{D}
of
G
in the sphere
\Sigma
, and distance parameter
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

6.1
The Harvesting Lemma

Let us start with some intuition.
Recall that thanks to
section

4.3
, we may assume to be working with a large walloid
W\subseteq G
, of bounded breadth and depth.
Each vortex segment
W^{\prime}
of
W
is equipped with its inner vortex disk
\Theta^{\mathsf{in}}
and its outer vortex disk
\Theta^{\mathsf{out}},
and that
W
has bounded depth means that there is a linear decomposition of bounded adhesion of the graph
G\cap\Theta^{\mathsf{out}}
. Moreover, this decomposition is “rooted” at the vertices lying the boundary of
\Theta^{\mathsf{out}}
. The harvesting lemma applies to such a situation abstractly, by providing a duality statement for any
\mathfrak{D}
-aligned disk
\Delta
of bounded depth.

Besides harvesting
S
- or
T
-rooted paths that escape the vortex, we will also harvest
S
-
T
-paths that lie entirely in the vortex but interact non-trivially with the vortex’s linear decomposition.
To facilitate this, we need one more definition. Let
\Delta
be a
\mathfrak{D}
-aligned disk in
\Sigma

8
The Global Structure Theorem

In this section we use the Local Structure Theorem (
section

7
) to construct a global tree decomposition of the graph whose bags exhibit the described local structure. This part of the reasoning is standard and conceptually dates back to the classic work of Robertson and Seymour in the Graph Minors series.

For the sake of modularity, we choose to capture the output of our Global Structure Theorem in the following definition.
Suppose

is a graph.
A
guarded tree-decomposition
of

(T,\beta,\gamma)
such that

•

(T,\beta)
is a rooted tree-decomposition of

•

\gamma

\gamma(t)
so that

T

\gamma(t)\supseteq\beta(t)\cap\beta(t^{\prime})

t^{\prime}
is the parent of

in
T
.

The
adhesion
of
(T,\beta,\gamma)
is the adhesion of
(T,\beta)
, and the
vigilance
of
(T,\beta,\gamma)
is the maximum size of a guard, that is,
\max_{t\in V(T)}|\gamma(t)|
.
Now, if

d
guards

and node

intersects

also intersects

We will typically use this definition when

-

Further, assuming that

X\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in[n]}A_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad Y\coloneqq V(G)-\bigcup_{i\in[n]}(A_{i}-B_{i}),

there exists a set

of size at most

-
T
-path in

and intersects

Our goal here is only to decompose

Therefore, we first replace each separation

and
(A_{i},B_{i})

For every

Clearly

In the claim below we argue that the order of

Claim 14
.

For every

Proof.

Since

|A_{i}\cap(A\cap B)|\leqslant|A_{i}\cap B_{i}|\leqslant\mathsf{link}_{\ref{global_structure_induction}}(d,k,q).

Therefore we have that

|(A_{i}\cap(A\cap B))\cup(A_{i}\cap B_{i})|\leqslant 2\cdot\mathsf{link}_{\ref{global_structure_induction}}(d,k,q).

Now we have the following inclusions:

\displaystyle(A_{i}\cap(A\cap B))\cup(A_{i}\cap B_{i})\supseteq(A_{i}\cap(A\cap B))\cup(A_{i}\cap B_{i}\cap B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(A_{i}\cap B)\cap(B_{i}\cup A)

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}_{i}\cap B^{\prime}_{i}.

Therefore, we deduce that

∎

Let

To see that this is the case, observe that by the definition of

However, this means that

Now, if there is an

X^{\prime}

In this case, without loss of generality we may assume that for every

Now,

We may now obtain a suitable guarded tree-decomposition

10
Weak Coarse Menger’s Conjecture in surface-embeddable graphs

In this section we lift the approach presented in the previous sections to graphs embeddable on surfaces more complicated than the sphere.

10.1
Preliminaries on surface-embedded graphs

We first need to establish the relevant terminology and basic results about embeddings of graphs in surfaces. We mostly follow the presentation from the classic book of Mohar and Thomassen
[
34
]
, to which we refer an interested reader for more details.

Surfaces.

In this paper, by a
surface
we mean an arc-connected and compact
2
-dimensional manifold without a boundary. In a
boundaried surface
we additionally allow the manifold to have a boundary consisting of a finite number of arc-connected components called
cuffs
, each homeomorphic to the circle
S^{1}
. The
closing
of a boundaried surface
\Sigma
is the surface
\Sigma^{\prime}
obtained by gluing an open disk to every cuff of
\Sigma
.

Given a pair of integers
(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{c})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N},
we define
\Sigma^{(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{c})}
to be the surface obtained from the sphere by adding
\mathsf{h}
handles and
\mathsf{c}
crosscaps.
If
\mathsf{c}=0
the surface
\Sigma^{(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{c})}
is
orientable
, and it is
non-orientable
otherwise. It is well-known that every surface without a boundary is homeomorphic to
\Sigma^{(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{c})}
, for some
(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{c})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
.
We define the
genus
of a surface
\Sigma
as
2\mathsf{h}+\mathsf{c},
where
\Sigma^{(\mathsf{h},\mathsf{c})}
is the surface to which
\Sigma
is homeomorphic. The genus of a boundaried surface is defined as the genus of its closing.

Drawing a graph on a surface.

A
drawing
on a surface
\Sigma
is a triple
\Gamma=(U,V,E)
such that

•

V
and
E
are finite;

•

V\subseteq U\subseteq\Sigma
and
e\subseteq U\subseteq\Sigma
for each
e\in E
;

•

V\cup\bigcup E=U
and
V\cap\bigcup E=\emptyset
;

•

for every
e\in E,
we have that
e=h([0,1])-\{h(0),h(1)\},
where
h\colon[0,1]\to U
is a homeomorphism from the unit interval
[0,1]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
onto its image satisfying
h(0),h(1)\in V
; and

•

if
e,e^{\prime}\in E
are distinct, then
|e\cap e^{\prime}|
is finite.

We call the set
V,
which we often denote by
V(\Gamma),
the
points
of
\Gamma
, and the set
E,
which we often denote by
E(\Gamma),
the
arcs
of
\Gamma.

If
G
is a graph such that
V(\Gamma)
and
E(\Gamma)
naturally correspond to
V(G)
and
E(G)
respectively, we say that
\Gamma
is a
drawing
of
G
on
\Sigma
.
In case no two arcs of
E(\Gamma)
have a common point, we say that
\Gamma
is an
embedding
of
G
on
\Sigma
.
In this case, the connected components of
\Sigma-U
are the
faces
of
\Gamma.

Representativity.

A (simple) curve on a surface
\Sigma
is a homeomorphic image of the circle
S^{1}
in
\Sigma
. A curve is called
contractible
if it is continuously deformable to a single point within
\Sigma
, and
non-contractible
otherwise. Note that, if
\gamma
is a non-contractible curve on
\Sigma,
then
\Sigma-\gamma
consists of one or two connected components, each being a surface with a boundary whose genus is strictly smaller than the genus of
\Sigma.

Given an embedding
\Gamma=(U,V,E)
of a graph
G
on a surface
\Sigma,
a
noose
is a curve on
\Sigma
that meets
U
only at the points of
V
. The
representativity
of
\Gamma
is the minimum number of points of
V
that lie on any non-contractible noose.
Note that when
\Sigma
is a sphere, then there are no non-contractible nooses and the representativity of every embedding is infinite.

Highly representative embeddings.

We are going to need the following two results regardings surface embeddings of graphs with large representativity.

The first result, due to Seymour and Thomas
[
48
]
(see also
[
52
,
35
,
47
]
), says that highly representative embeddings of
3
-connected graphs are unique.

Proposition 10.1

(
[
48
]
)
.

For all non-negative integers
g
and
r
the following holds.
Suppose
G
is a
3
-connected graph and
\Gamma
is an embedding of
G
on a surface
\Sigma
of genus
g
with representativity
r.

If

r\geqslant\frac{100\log g}{\log\log g}\quad\text{(or $r\geqslant 100$ if $g\leqslant 2$)},

then
G
cannot be embedded on a surface of genus smaller than
g
and every embedding of
G
on
\Sigma
is homeomorphic to
\Gamma.

The second result from the work of Gorsky, Seweryn, and Wiederrecht
[
27
]
is about finding minors in graphs with surface embeddings of large representativity.

Proposition 10.2

(
[
27
, Theorem 15.11]
)
.

There is a polynomial function
\mathsf{rep}_{\ref{prop:representativity_gives_minor}}\colon\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that for all
g,t\in\mathbb{N}
the following holds.
Every graph that has an embedding with representativity at least
\mathsf{rep}_{\ref{prop:representativity_gives_minor}}(g,t)
on a surface
\Sigma
of genus at most
g
contains, as a minor, every graph on at most
t
vertices that can be embedded on
\Sigma.

10.2
The Representativity Lemma

We also need a tool that will allow us to reduce our proof to embeddings of large representativity, at the cost of introducing some apex vertices.

Lemma 10.3

(Representativity Lemma)
.

Let
g,r\in\mathbb{N}
with
r\geqslant 1
and
suppose
G
is a connected graph embedded on a surface of genus
g.

Then there exists a set of vertices
A\subseteq V(G)
with
|A|\leqslant(2g-1)(r-1)
(or
A=\emptyset
, if
g=0
) and a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
V(G)-A
into non-empty vertex subsets (or
\mathcal{P}=\emptyset
if
V(G)=A
) such that

•

\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{P}}G[H\cup A]=G
; and

•

for every
H\in\mathcal{P},
there exists a graph
H^{\star}
and a vertex subset
A^{\star}\subseteq V(H^{\star})
with
|A^{\star}|\leqslant 2g
satisfying the following two conditions:

i)

H^{\star}-A^{\star}=G[H]
and
N_{H^{\star}}(A^{\star})=N_{G}(A)\cap H
; and

ii)

H^{\star}
has an embedding on a surface of genus at most
g
with representativity at least
r.

Proof.

We prove the following stronger claim by induction on the genus
g
of the surface on which
G
is embedded.

Inductive claim:
Let
G
be a graph,
A\subseteq V(G)
be a vertex subset, and let
H\coloneqq G-A.

Suppose there exists a connected graph
H^{\star}
and a vertex subset
A^{\star}\subseteq V(H^{\star})
satisfying the following two conditions:

•

H^{\star}-A^{\star}=H
and
N_{H^{\star}}(A^{\star})=N_{G}(A)
; and

•

H^{\star}
has an embedding
\Gamma_{H^{\star}}
on a surface of genus
g.

Then, there exists
X\subseteq V(H)
with
|X|\leqslant(2g-1)(r-1)
(or
X=\emptyset,
if
g=0
) and a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
V(H)-X
into non-empty vertex subsets (or
\mathcal{P}=\emptyset
if
V(H)=X
) such that

•

\bigcup_{K\in\mathcal{P}}H[K\cup X]=H
; and

•

for every
K\in\mathcal{P},
there exists a connected graph
K^{\star}
and a vertex subset
X^{\star}\subseteq V(K^{\star})
with
|X^{\star}|\leqslant|A^{\star}|+2g
satisfying the following two conditions:

i)

K^{\star}-X^{\star}=H[K]
and
N_{K^{\star}}(X^{\star})=N_{G}(A\cup X)\cap K
; and

ii)

K^{\star}
has an embedding on a surface of genus at most
g
with representativity at least
r.

Observe that the lemma statement follows by applying the inductive claim to
G
with
A=\emptyset
and
H^{\star}=G.

Therefore, from now on we focus on proving the inductive claim.

Base case,
g=0
:
By assumption, if
\Sigma
is a sphere, then any embedding on
\Sigma
has infinite representativity and therefore we may conclude with
X=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{P}=\{V(H)\}
(or
\mathcal{P}=\emptyset
if
V(H)=\emptyset
).

Inductive step,
g\geqslant 1
:
Assume the claim holds for all genera
g^{\prime}<g
and suppose that
\Sigma
is a surface of genus
g.

In case the embedding
\Gamma_{H^{\star}}
of
H^{\star}
on
\Sigma
already has representativity at least
r
, we may conclude with
X=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{P}=\{V(H)\}.

Therefore, we may assume otherwise, which implies that there exists a non-contractible noose
\gamma
on
\Sigma,
that intersects the embedding of
H^{\star}
only in vertices, and in strictly less than
r
many.
Let
X_{0}
be this set of vertices.

Now, let
\widehat{\Sigma}
be the (possibly disconnected) surface with boundary, obtained from
\Sigma
by cutting it along
\gamma
; i.e.,
\widehat{\Sigma}
is the compactification of
\Sigma-\gamma.

Next, let
\widetilde{\Sigma}
be the closing of
\Sigma
, obtained from
\widehat{\Sigma}
by gluing a disk
\Delta_{B}
to every cuff
B
of
\widehat{\Sigma}
.
Note that thus
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is the union of either one or two connected surface without a boundary, depending on whether
\gamma
was separating or not.
In case
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is connected, it has genus at most
g-1
and
\widehat{\Sigma}
has exactly one cuff when
\gamma
is one-sided, or exactly two cuffs when
\gamma
is two-sided.
In case
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is not connected,
\gamma
is always two-sided, and then
\widehat{\Sigma}
consists of exactly two connected components:
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{1}
of genus
g_{1}\geqslant 1
and
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{2}
of genus
g_{2}\geqslant 1,
each with exactly one cuff, and such that
g_{1}+g_{2}=g.

Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r^{\prime}}
(
r^{\prime}<r
) be the cyclic ordering of the vertices in
X_{0},
as traversed by
\gamma
in an arbitrarily chosen direction.
Note that for every vertex
v_{i},

i\in[r^{\prime}],
there exists a partition
E^{-}_{i},E^{+}_{i}
of the edges incident to
v_{i}
so that if we define
H^{\prime}
to be the graph where each vertex
v_{i}
is replaced by two vertices
v^{-}_{i}
and
v^{+}_{i}
, with
v^{-}_{i}
becoming an endpoint of the edges of
E^{-}_{i}
and
v^{+}_{i}
becoming an endpoint of the edges of
E^{+}_{i}
, then
H^{\prime}
has an embedding
\Gamma_{H^{\prime}}
on
\widetilde{\Sigma}
such that the following holds.
We distinguish two cases.

Case 1,
\gamma
is one-sided:
Then, the vertices
v^{-}_{1},\ldots,v^{-}_{r^{\prime}},v^{+}_{1},\ldots,v^{+}_{r^{\prime}}
are embedded along the boundary of
\Delta_{B}
in this cyclic ordering, where
B
is the unique cuff of
\widehat{\Sigma}.

In this case, we define the graph
H^{\prime\prime}
by replacing the vertices
v^{-}_{1},\ldots,v^{-}_{r^{\prime}},v^{+}_{1},\ldots,v^{+}_{r^{\prime}}
by a single vertex
v
with
N_{H^{\prime\prime}}(v)=N_{H^{\prime}}(\{v^{-}_{1},\ldots,v^{-}_{r^{\prime}},v^{+}_{1},\ldots,v^{+}_{r^{\prime}}\}).

Clearly,
\Gamma_{H^{\prime}}
yields an embedding
\Gamma_{H^{\prime\prime}}
of
H^{\prime}
on
\widetilde{\Sigma},
where
v
is drawn in the interior of
\Delta_{B}.

We let
X^{\star}_{0}\coloneqq\{v\}.

Case 2,
\gamma
is two-sided:
Then, the vertices
v^{-}_{1},\ldots,v^{-}_{r^{\prime}}
are embedded along the boundary of
\Delta_{B^{-}}
in this cyclic ordering, while the vertices
v^{+}_{1},\ldots,v^{+}_{r^{\prime}}
are embedded along the boundary of
\Delta_{B^{+}}
in this cyclic ordering, where
B^{-}
and
B^{+}
are the two cuffs of
\widehat{\Sigma}.

In this case, we define the graph
H^{\prime\prime}
by replacing the vertices
v^{-}_{1},\ldots,v^{-}_{r^{\prime}},v^{+}_{1},\ldots,v^{+}_{r^{\prime}}
by a pair of vertices
v^{-}
and
v^{+}
with
N_{H^{\prime\prime}}(v^{-})=N_{H^{\prime}}(\{v^{-}_{1},\ldots,v^{-}_{r^{\prime}}\})
and
N_{H^{\prime\prime}}(v^{+})=N_{H^{\prime}}(\{v^{+}_{1},\ldots,v^{+}_{r^{\prime}}\}).

Clearly,
\Gamma_{H^{\prime}}
induces an embedding
\Gamma_{H^{\prime\prime}}
of
H^{\prime}
on
\widetilde{\Sigma},
where
v^{-}
is drawn in the interior of
\Delta_{B^{-}}
and
v^{+}
is drawn in the interior of
\Delta_{B^{+}}
respectively.
We let
X^{\star}_{0}\coloneqq\{v^{-},v^{+}\}.

Define
X^{\prime}_{0}\coloneqq X_{0}-A^{\star}
and
A^{\prime}\coloneqq A^{\star}-X_{0}.

By construction we have
H^{\prime\prime}-(A^{\prime}\cup X^{\star}_{0})=G-(A\cup X^{\prime}_{0})
and
N_{H^{\prime\prime}}(A^{\prime}\cup X^{\star}_{0})=N_{G}(A\cup X^{\prime}_{0}).

Now, note that if
V(H^{\prime\prime})=A^{\prime}\cup X^{\star}_{0}
then we may conclude with
X=X^{\prime}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}=\emptyset.

Therefore, we may assume that
V(H^{\prime\prime})\supsetneq A^{\prime}\cup X^{\star}_{0}.

Let
H^{\star}_{1},\ldots,H^{\star}_{q}
be the connected components of
H^{\prime\prime},
which are not subgraphs of
H^{\prime\prime}[A^{\prime}\cup X^{\star}_{0}],
and for each
j\in[q],
define
A^{\star}_{j}\coloneqq(A^{\prime}\cup X^{\star}_{0})\cap V(H^{\star}_{j}).

Now, define
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
to be the partition of
V(H)-X^{\prime}_{0}
into the following non-empty subsets:

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{V(H^{\star}_{j})-A^{\star}_{j}\mid i\in[q]\}.

Next, for each
j\in[q],
define
H_{j}\coloneqq H^{\star}_{j}-A^{\star}_{j}
and observe that, since
H^{\prime\prime}-(A^{\prime}\cup X^{\star}_{0})=G-(A\cup X^{\prime}_{0})
, we have that
\bigcup_{j\in[q]}H[V(H_{j})\cup X^{\prime}_{0}]=H.

Our goal is to apply the inductive claim on
G_{j}\coloneqq G[V(H_{j})\cup(A\cup X^{\prime}_{0})]
together with the set
A\cup X^{\prime}_{0}
, and
H^{\star}_{j}
together with the set
A^{\star}_{j}.

To this end, we have to verify whether they satisfy its assumptions.
First note that by construction,
N_{H^{\star}_{j}}(A^{\star}_{j})=N_{G_{j}}(A\cup X^{\prime}_{0})
, as desired.
Moreover, for every
j\in[q],

\Gamma_{H^{\prime\prime}}
induces an embedding
\Gamma_{H^{\star}_{j}}
of
H^{\star}_{j}
on a connected component of
\widetilde{\Sigma}
which has genus
\hat{g}_{j}<g.

In fact, by the additivity of genus, we have that either
\sum_{j\in[q]}\hat{g}_{j}<g,
in case
\gamma
was non-separating, or that there is a partition of
[q]
into
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
according to which of the two connected components of
\widetilde{\Sigma}
each
H^{\star}_{j}
is embedded on, such that
\sum_{j\in I_{1}}\hat{g}_{j}\leqslant g_{1}
and
\sum_{j\in I_{2}}\hat{g}_{j}\leqslant g_{2}.

This reflects the case where
\gamma
was separating.

Therefore, by the induction hypothesis, for each
j\in[q]
there is a set
X_{j}\subseteq V(H_{j})
with
|X_{j}|\leqslant(2\hat{g}_{j}-1)(r-1)
(or
X_{j}=\emptyset,
if
\hat{g}_{j}=0
) and a partition
\mathcal{P}_{j}
of
V(H_{j})-X_{j}
into non-empty vertex subsets (or
\mathcal{P}_{j}=\emptyset
if
V(H_{j})=X_{j}
) such that

•

\bigcup_{K_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{j}}H_{j}[K_{j}\cup X_{j}]=H_{j}
; and

•

for every
K_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{j},
there exists a connected graph
K^{\star}_{j}
and a vertex subset
X^{\star}_{j}\subseteq V(K^{\star}_{j})
with
|X^{\star}_{j}|\leqslant|A^{\star}_{j}|+2\hat{g}_{j}
satisfying the following two conditions:

•

K^{\star}_{j}-X^{\star}_{j}=H_{j}[K_{j}]
and
N_{K^{\star}_{j}}(X^{\star}_{j})=N_{G}(A\cup X^{\prime}_{0}\cup X_{j})\cap K_{j}
; and

•

K^{\star}_{j}
has an embedding on a surface of genus at most
\hat{g}_{j}
with representativity at least
r.

Set
X\coloneqq X^{\prime}_{0}\cup\bigcup_{j\in[q]}X_{j}.

Now observe that, since
\bigcup_{j\in[q]}H[V(H_{j})\cup X^{\prime}_{0}]=H,
if
\mathcal{P}_{j}=\emptyset
for all
j\in[q],
then we have that
V(H)=X
and we may conclude with
X
and
\mathcal{P}=\emptyset,
subject to verifying the bound on
|X|.

So, we may assume that for some
j\in[p],

\mathcal{P}_{j}\neq\emptyset.

In this case, we define
\mathcal{P}
as the refinement of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime},
obtained by taking the union of all
\mathcal{P}_{j}
which are non-empty, and since
\bigcup_{K_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{j}}H_{j}[K_{j}\cup X_{j}]=H_{j},
for all
j\in[q],
it follows that
\bigcup_{K\in\mathcal{P}}H[K\cup X]=H
as desired.
Therefore, subject to verifying the bound on each
|X^{\star}_{j}|
as well, our inductive claim follows.

Observe that

|X|\leqslant|X^{\prime}_{0}|+\sum_{j\in[q]}|X_{j}|\leqslant(r-1)+\sum_{j\in[q],\hat{g}_{j}\geqslant 1}(2\hat{g}_{j}-1)(r-1).

The following claim will be useful in bounding the last summand.

Claim 17
.

If
\sum_{j\in[q]}\hat{g}_{j}\leqslant h
, then

\sum_{j\in[q],\hat{g}_{j}\geqslant 1}(2\hat{g}_{j}-1)(r-1)\leqslant(2h-1)(r-1).

Proof.

\sum_{j\in[q],\hat{g}_{j}\geqslant 1}(2\hat{g}_{j}-1)(r-1)\leqslant\bigg(2\sum_{j\in[q],\hat{g}_{j}\geqslant 1}\hat{g}_{j}-1\bigg)(r-1)\leqslant(2h-1)(r-1).\qed

We now distinguish two cases.

\gamma
is non-separating:
In this case,
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is connected and from our previous observation we have that
\sum_{j\in[q]}\hat{g}_{j}\leqslant g-1.

By
17
,

|X|\leqslant(r-1)+(2(g-1)-1)(r-1)=(r-1)+(2g-3)(r-1)=(2g-2)(r-1)<(2g-1)(r-1).

\gamma
is separating:
In this case,
\widetilde{\Sigma}
consists of two connected components of genus
g_{1},g_{2}\geqslant 1
respectively, such that
g_{1}+g_{2}=g.

Therefore, from our previous observation we have that
\sum_{j\in I_{1}}\hat{g}_{j}\leqslant g_{1}
and
\sum_{j\in I_{2}}\hat{g}_{j}\leqslant g_{2}.

By applying
17
to each side, we obtain

|X|\leqslant(r-1)+(2g_{1}-1)(r-1)+(2g_{2}-1)(r-1)+(r-1)(1+(2g_{1}-1)+(2g_{2}-1))=(2g-1)(r-1).

Finally, note that for every
j\in[q],
we have

|X^{\star}_{j}|\leqslant|A^{\star}_{j}|+2\hat{g}_{j}\leqslant|A^{\star}|+2+2(g-1)=|A^{\star}|+2g.

This completes the proof.
∎

10.3
Star replacements

Let
G
be a graph,
A\subseteq V(G)
be a vertex subset,
H
be a connected component of
G-A,
and
H^{\prime}\coloneqq G[V(H)\cup A].

Let
H^{\star}
be a graph with a vertex subset
A^{\star}\subseteq V(H^{\star})
satisfying the following two conditions:

•

H^{\star}-A^{\star}=H
; and

•

N_{H^{\star}}(A^{\star})=N_{H^{\prime}}(A).

We call the pair
(H^{\star},A^{\star})
as above a
replacement
of
(H^{\prime},A)
. We shall refer to
A^{\star}
as to the
star vertices
of the replacement.

For the remainder of this subsection, fix in the context a non-negative integer
d
, a graph
G
, and a vertex subset
A\subseteq V(G).

Moreover, we fix a connected component
H
of
G-A
and let
H^{\prime}\coloneqq G[V(H)\cup A].
Also, we fix a replacement
(H^{\star},A^{\star})
of
(H^{\prime},A)
. Let us make a few observations about distances.

Observation 10.4
.

For any two vertices
u,v\in V(H)
such that
\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(u,A^{\star})>\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor,

\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(v,A^{\star})>\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor,
and
\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(u,v)>d,
we have

\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)>d.

Proof.

Let
P
be a shortest
u
-
v
-path in
G.
We prove that the length of
P
is larger than
d
.
There are two cases.

Case 1,
P
avoids
A
:
In this case
P
is a path in
H
and therefore also a path in
H^{\star}.

Since
\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(u,v)>d,
the length of
P
must be larger than
d
.

Case 2,
P
intersects
A
:
Let
P_{u}
be the longest prefix of
P
that starts at
u
and avoids
A
. Note that
P_{u}
is a path in
H
and its other endpoint belongs to
N_{H^{\prime}}(A)
. Since
N_{H^{\star}}(A^{\star})=N_{H^{\prime}}(A)
,
P_{u}
can be extended by one edge to a
u
-
A^{\star}
-path in
H^{\star}
. As
\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(u,A^{\star})>\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor
, it follows that the length of
P_{u}
is at least
\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor
. Similarly, if
P_{v}
is the longest suffix of
P
that ends at
v
and avoids
A
, then the length of
P_{v}
is at least
\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor
. Note that
P_{u}
and
P_{v}
are disjoint subpaths of
P
and there are at least two edges of
P
— the ones incident to the vertices of
A
— that belong to neither
P_{u}
nor
P_{v}
. It follows that the length of
P
is at least
\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor+\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor+2>d
.
∎

Observation 10.5
.

For every vertex subset
X\subseteq V(H)
and every vertex
v\in V(H)
, we have

\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(v,X\cup A^{\star})=\mathsf{dist}_{G}(v,X\cup A).

Proof.

We first prove that
\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(v,X\cup A^{\star})\leqslant\mathsf{dist}_{G}(v,X\cup A).
Let
P
be a shortest
v
-
(X\cup A)
-path in
G
, say of length
\ell\coloneqq\mathsf{dist}_{G}(v,X\cup A)
. If

avoids

, then

is also a
u
-
X
-path in

that witnesses that
\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(v,X\cup A^{\star})\leqslant\ell
. Otherwise, by minimality, the only vertex of

that belongs to
A
is the endpoint other then

. Since
N_{H^{\star}}(A^{\star})=N_{H^{\prime}}(A)
, we may replace this endpoint with a vertex of

, thus obtaining a
u
-
A^{\star}
-path of length

in

. This path witnesses that
\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(v,X\cup A^{\star})\leqslant\ell
.

We now prove that
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(v,X\cup A)\leqslant\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(v,X\cup A^{\star}).
Let
P
be a shortest
v
-
(X\cup A^{\star})
-path in
H^{\star}
, say of length
\ell\coloneqq\mathsf{dist}_{H^{\star}}(v,X\cup A^{\star})
. Again, if

avoids
A^{\star}
, then

is also a
u
-

-path in

that witnesses that
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(v,X\cup A)\leqslant\ell
. And otherwise, by minimality, the only vertex of

that belongs to

is the endpoint other then

. Now, the same replacement argument as in the first case yields a

-

∎

10.4
Classifying terminals on surfaces

We now work towards establishing the analogue of
section

4.3
for graphs embeddable in surfaces richer than the sphere. Generally speaking, we need to (i) introduce a possibility of having a bounded number of apex vertices that can be adjacent only to the vortices, and (ii) add features to the walloids that will represent the surface into which the graph is embedded.
