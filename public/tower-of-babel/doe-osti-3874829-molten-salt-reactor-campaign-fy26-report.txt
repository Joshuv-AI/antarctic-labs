 Executive Summary
 Through the Molten Salt Reactor (MSR) Campaign, the U.S. Department of Energy supports the
development of technologies to build safe, economically sound molten salt-based small modular reactors.
This campaign serves as a pipeline to connect commercial reactor companies with the national lab
complex to solve technological challenges and accelerate technology to commercial markets. Molten salts
are complex materials requiring high temperatures and highly corrosion-resistant structural materials.
Fluoride molten salts are becoming a predominate choice in MSR designs because of their wide liquid
temperature range, fuel solubility (Th, U, and Pu are all soluble in molten salts), and favorable neutronic
properties. However, fluoride molten salts are among the most corrosive liquids known, and produce HF
in the presence of water. These complex hazards require precise planning to provide accurate data for
MSR design and licensing.
 This work focuses on determining the viscosity of molten salts, particularly actinide-bearing
molten salts. Herein, we outline the design and progress towards a higher throughput method of molten
salt viscometry measurements using a rotational viscometer design modified to withstand the high
temperature and corrosivity of the molten salt. A smaller glovebox-compatible design and a larger full-
scale design for inert operations in a fume hood have been developed. Using a copper torsion wire
maximizes sensitivity in the low-viscosity regime characteristic of molten salts (i.e., lower viscosity than
water). These designs are patent pending. Both designs require small quantities of liquid (mL range) and
could collect data over a broad temperature range, allowing full temperature profiles for a composition of
a given molten salt. The next steps are to calibrate the rotational viscometers with room temperature low-
viscosity liquids and to perform a temperature test with molten gallium.

 Introduction
 Interest in molten salts has continued to grow in demand due to significant investment in the
development of small modular reactors as well as other related technical domains, including energy storage
and conversion, nuclear technologies, catalysis, chemical processing, metallurgy, and materials production.
[1,2,3]
 Characterizing the thermophysical properties of molten salts in their liquid states is a challenge due to
their high temperatures and corrosivity. Of these properties, viscosity is arguably the most difficult to
measure accurately because of its very low value in molten salts. Accurate viscosity data is essential for
engineering design, process optimization, and computational modeling of molten salt systems. Viscosity
measurements for molten salts present significant technical challenges (i.e., high operational temperatures,
corrosion, and moisture sensitivity) resulting in substantial data gaps. [3,4] For actinide-bearing fluoride and
chloride salts, experimental data is severely limited. [3,5] For example, the simple NaCl-UCl3 mixture, a
potential fast-spectrum molten salt reactor fuel, [5] has been experimentally investigated by only a single
study by Desyatnik and coworkers in 1975 using an oscillating cup method. [6] The Desyatnik correlations
overestimate the viscosity of pure molten NaCl, which is thoroughly studied at present day. [7]
 Recent investigations of the UCl3-bearing system have been performed with both the experimental
results of Termini et al. using a rolling-ball viscometer [8] and the computational results of Baty from
molecular dynamics simulations using a polarizable ion model, [9] obtaining greater values than Desyantik
et al. (Figure 1). These knowledge gaps directly impact molten salt reactor design, potentially complicating
licensing and safety assessments for these next-generation nuclear systems—therefore, it is necessary to
develop improved methods to obtain viscosity data for actinide-bearing salt systems. [4,10]

 There are viscometer designs in the literature for actinide-bearing salts, such as viscometry methods
that use the oscillating cup method. [7] The opacity of molten salts makes any optical methods impractical,
especially for fluoride molten salts which will etch optically transparent materials. Our own team previously
used neutron imaging to track falling boron spheres.[11] The working principle of falling sphere viscometry
is that as the solid sphere travels through the fluid medium, there is a drag force against its direction of
motion proportional to the viscosity of the medium. When the drag, buoyancy, and gravitational forces on
the sphere reach equilibrium, the sphere reaches a terminal velocity. The measurement of this terminal
velocity, combined with the dimensions of the sphere and densities of both the sphere and fluid, enables
calculation of the fluid viscosity. Previous work by our group used neutron imaging to measure the velocity
of a falling sphere and calculate a value for 16.5 wt% UCl3 in NaCl at 825 °C. However, the chloride atoms’
cross-sectional area impaired image quality. [11] Additionally, limited neutron beam time restricts the
throughput needed to characterize the growing number of relevant molten salt compositions for molten salt
reactor applications. Rotational viscometer designs can provide higher throughput by continuous data
sampling (multiple temperatures of the same composition with a single sample). Rotational viscometers
require small fluid volumes (mL), a crucial advantage when working with radioactive actinide-containing
salts. [4]
 The methods of rotational viscometers for room temperature liquids are well understood and have
been applied to a wide range of other high-temperature liquids. [12-18] However, several technical
challenges have limited the widespread adoption of rotational viscometry for low-viscosity molten salts.
The few published studies on molten salt viscometry highlight the challenges of using conventional
viscometers in straightforward modifications. These challenges include misalignments and temperature
incompatibilities of components. [19,20] Rheometers designed for high temperature molten salts are large
standalone systems leading to issues of sample handling and purity. [21] Additionally, an often-overlooked
limitation is the difficulty in generating sufficiently large torques for adequate measurement sensitivity
with low-viscosity fluids. [12-14] Our novel rotational viscometer design addresses these three concerns

through: 1) complete thermal isolation and enclosure of the fluid and viscometer components, 2) precise
alignment of shafts attached to the cup and bob via needle bearings embedded in vacuum flanges, and 3)
rotation of the cup rather than the bob, enhancing hydrodynamic stability to enable higher induced torques
and thereby improving measurement sensitivity for low-viscosity molten salt systems.

 Calculations and Design Considerations

 The working principle of a rotational viscometer is that as one of two co-axial cylinders, an inner
cylinder with radius 𝑅𝑏 , rotates relative to the other, an outer cylinder with inner radius 𝑅𝑐 , at a rate Ω, there
is a resulting angular, radially varying velocity field 𝑣𝜃 (𝑟) that is generated. This is formed in a viscous
fluid within the annular gap due to the no-slip condition at the solid wall. This angular flow field will
thereby induce a torque 𝑀 on the cylinder walls that is proportional to the fluid’s viscosity, 𝜇. The
measurement of this torque under steady flow conditions enables calculation of the fluid viscosity.
 We plan to use a rotating-cup configuration, in which the outer cylinder with inner radius 𝑅𝑐 , as
the form of a “cup” containing the fluid, rotates at a known and controlled rate Ω. The inner cylinder, in the
form of a “bob” suspended within the fluid, experiences a tangential force 𝐹𝑡 along the surface due to the
viscous shear forces proportional to fluid’s viscosity 𝜇, which tend to rotate the inner cylinder with the
flow. This force, multiplied by the bob’s radius 𝑅𝑏 , produces the torque 𝑀 that is eventually measured by
resisting the rotation with an equal torque in the opposite direction; this is accomplished via a torsion wire.
 The working principle of this viscometer configuration is illustrated in Figure 3. The mathematical
description of this system assumes an infinitely long annular gap/co-axial cylinders with complete angular
symmetry. Assuming a uniform density and viscosity within the fluid under purely angular, laminar flow,

 Previous work on molten salts and other molten media have frequently used a spinning-bob
(typically referred to as a spindle) configuration. In this configuration, the torque applied to the spindle
(maintaining a known rotation rate) is measured and related back to the viscosity using an equation similar
to Eq. 11. [15,17,18] However, this configuration is not well suited to low-viscosity fluids—a velocity field
that decreases with radial position is known to eventually result in a so-called Taylor vortex flow when the
viscous forces can no longer dampen out the differences between pressure and centrifugal forces. Such flow
is completely unstable for inviscid fluids. [12,13] As a result, there is a maximum rotation speed that can be
applied before the purely angular flow develops periodic Taylor vortices, limiting the total torque that can
be applied and measured. On the other hand, the rotating-cup configuration, with a velocity field that
increases with radial position, remains hydrodynamically stable until turbulence. The difference in the
critical/transition values of the Reynolds number for the two configurations is almost two orders of
magnitude in favor of the rotating-cup configuration (~5×104 vs. ~8×102 ). This enables much greater
rotation speeds and measured torques, thereby providing greater sensitivity to small changes in the viscosity
of already relatively low-viscosity fluids such as molten salts. [14]

 Full Scale Design

A schematic of the apparatus is shown in Figure 2. The cup and bob system containing the salt is housed in
a large (4.5”) CF nipple (IdealVac) and surrounded by a resistive furnace. To the bottom of this 4.5” housing
nipple is a 2.5” CF nipple enclosed by two 2.5” CF flanges, each bored through to insert a 1” needle bearing
(McMaster-Carr) for the insertion of a stainless-steel shaft that connects the cup to a Zaber motor to provide
the rotation; the two nipples are attached via a reducing CF flange. To the top of the housing nipple, an
identical component is attached, except for the use of 1” needle bearings (McMaster-Carr) for the shaft that
connects the bob to the torsion wire. The copper torsion wire (0.003” diameter, McMaster-Carr) is attached
to a stationary anchor point above the assembly. This assembly provides superior alignment of the cup-bob
system using bearing-guided shafts and thermal isolation with the enclosed housing.

To measure the torque induced on the bob via the rotating flow within the annulus, a torsion wire is used,
attached to the shaft of the bob and suspended from a fixed stationary “ceiling” of the apparatus. At a steady
torque, 𝑀, the shaft will have an angular displacement 𝜃 based on the torsion constant, 𝑘𝑡 , of the wire
resisting the angular rotation of the attached shaft. This analog torque measurement method allows for much
greater sensitivity to the low torques induced by the expected low-viscosity salt than is typically available
from electronic torque measurement devices, as a wide range of wire materials can be selected to achieve
the desired sensitivity.

 Future Work and Directions
 Completion and Calibration of Rotational Viscometer
 Once the smaller-scale and full-sized prototypes are assembled, the viscometer can be calibrated
using fluids of known viscosity. Starting with room temperature low-viscosity fluids: water, hexane,
ethanol, and acetone. This will allow for optimization of operational experimental parameters at a lower
hazard and without worry for sample contamination. For the low viscosity and high temperature regime,
liquid gallium may be a suitable choice. For higher values of viscosity, the general-purpose silicone
standards may be suitable. To account for non-idealities in the viscometer setup, such as the friction induced
by the bearings or the shear on the conical ends of the bob, it is common to introduce a correction factor to
