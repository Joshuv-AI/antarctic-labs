Integrating Network Pharmacology and Experimental Validation Reveals EGFR/PI3K/Akt Axis Mediates Ibrutinib-Induced Cardiotoxicity
Authors: Yingxia Guan, Jiaqi Mei, Yuanqiao He, Mei Lu, Biao Cheng, Xinjin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to systematically explore the molecular mechanism underlying ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity.MethodsNetwork toxicology was applied using DrugBank, PharmMapper, GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD databases to identify overlapping targets between ibrutinib and myocardial injury, followed by PPI network construction (STRING; confidence ≥0.7) and GO/KEGG enrichment analyses (Metascape). Molecular docking (AutoDock Vina) verified ibrutinib’s binding affinity to core targets. In vitro, H9C2 cardiomyocytes were exposed to ibrutinib (0, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μM) for 24 h; CCK-8, DCFH-DA, EdU, and Calcein-AM/PI assays were performed to assess cell viability, ROS levels, proliferation, and cell death, respectively. Western blot analyzed phosphorylation of EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway proteins.ResultsA total of 42 overlapping targets were screened from 337 ibrutinib-related targets and 471 myocardial injury-related genes. PPI network analysis identified EGFR, AKT1, SRC, ESR1, and CASP3 as hub genes, and KEGG enrichment analysis identified the PI3K-Akt pathway as the most significantly enriched cascade. Molecular docking confirmed that ibrutinib stably bound BTK and EGFR with a binding energy of -30.2 kcal/mol and formed multiple hydrogen bonds and salt bridges (2.2–3.2 Å). 1. Background

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), the most common adult leukemia in Western countries, has traditionally been treated with chemoimmunotherapy; however, this approach exhibits poor tolerability in elderly patients and limited efficacy in those with high-risk genetic abnormalities.1,2 While chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of cancer treatment, its clinical application is often constrained by severe cardiotoxicity and other adverse effects that compromise long-term quality of life.3-5 In recent years, targeted therapy against the B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling pathway has transformed the CLL treatment landscape, with Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors offering improved efficacy and selectivity. 6

Ibrutinib, the first approved oral covalent BTK inhibitor, has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in CLL, achieving a 71% overall response rate in relapsed/refractory disease and significantly improving progression-free survival.7,8 Its approval for CLL in 2014 marked the entry of targeted therapy into this field. 9 However, widespread clinical use has revealed cardiovascular toxicity as a critical limitation, 10 with up to 16% of patients developing new-onset atrial fibrillation and associations reported for ventricular arrhythmias, hypertension, heart failure, and sudden death.11-13 Notably, second-generation BTK inhibitors (acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib) exhibit higher BTK selectivity and weaker EGFR 14 off-target inhibition, resulting in significantly lower atrial fibrillation rates (9.4% and 5.2% versus 16%). 15 This clinical divergence suggests that ibrutinib cardiotoxicity involves off-target kinase inhibition beyond BTK suppression. 16

Despite extensive clinical evidence for ibrutinib cardiotoxicity, its underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. BTK is expressed not only in B cells but also in cardiomyocytes, where it participates in the normal regulation of cardiac function. 17 Additionally, ibrutinib exhibits varying degrees of inhibitory effects on multiple tyrosine kinases including EGFR and platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), all of which are closely related to cardiac physiological functions. 18 Among these, the EGFR/phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) signaling axis represents one of the most critical intracellular survival signaling pathways and plays a pivotal role in cardiomyocyte protection. 19 Research has demonstrated that the PI3K/Akt pathway promotes cardiomyocyte protein synthesis, regulates cell cycle progression, inhibits apoptosis, and maintains mitochondrial function through activation of downstream effectors (including mTOR, GSK-3, and FoxO transcription factor family), exerting protective effects against various cardiovascular diseases including myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, myocardial infarction, and heart failure. 20 However, the specific impact of ibrutinib on the EGFR/PI3K/Akt axis and its contribution to cardiotoxicity remain poorly characterized.

Network toxicology, an emerging systems biology approach, enables global prediction of drug-disease target relationships, becoming a powerful tool for drug safety evaluation and toxicological mechanism research. 21 By integrating drug target databases, disease gene databases, and protein-protein interaction networks, this approach systematically screens common targets between drugs and diseases and reveals potential mechanisms of action through bioinformatics analysis, providing scientific hypotheses for subsequent experimental validation. 22 Compared with the traditional “single-target, single-pathway” research model, network toxicology offers high-throughput and systematic advantages, and has been successfully applied in toxicological mechanism research of various drugs. 23

The precise molecular mechanisms of ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity remain incompletely understood. Previous studies have largely focused on isolated targets or single-pathway mechanisms. For instance, McMullen et al. first proposed that ibrutinib may increase atrial fibrillation risk through inhibition of cardiac PI3K-Akt signaling; however, that study only detected changes in p-Akt without systematic validation of the entire upstream and downstream signaling cascade. 18 Similarly, Qin et al. reported that ibrutinib induces autophagic degradation of connexins by inhibiting the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway in HL-1 atrial cardiomyocytes, yet this work was limited to atrial cells and did not examine ventricular cardiomyocytes or the upstream EGFR-mediated regulation. 24 Furthermore, although Dong et al. have comprehensively reviewed the clinical and pharmacological aspects of ibrutinib-associated cardiotoxicity, they emphasized that the biological mechanisms underlying these adverse effects remain largely unclear despite over a decade of research. 25 Notably, no study to date has integrated network toxicology with multi-dimensional experimental validation—including molecular docking, cell viability assays, ROS detection, proliferation analysis, and Western blot—to systematically map the complete EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling cascade in ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity. Traditional single-target research approaches are insufficient to capture the complex network of molecular interactions involved in drug-induced cardiotoxicity. 23 Therefore, there is an urgent need to employ systems-level approaches that can comprehensively map the interactions between ibrutinib and cardiac signaling networks.

Based on the above background, the present study aims to systematically predict the common targets and potential signaling pathways between ibrutinib and myocardial injury using network toxicology analysis, and verify the binding affinity of ibrutinib to core targets via molecular docking. Furthermore, in vitro cellular experiments will be performed to functionally validate the predictions derived from network pharmacology. This study is expected to provide experimental evidence for in-depth understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying cardiotoxicity induced by BTK inhibitors, as well as theoretical references for optimizing clinical medication strategies and ensuring the safety of patient treatment.

2. Materials and Methods

All experiments were performed between October 2024 and April 2026.

2.1. Network Pharmacology Prediction

2.1.1. Target Identification

The potential targets of ibrutinib were retrieved from the DrugBank database (https://www.drugbank.ca) and the PharmMapper server (https://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/). Myocardial injury-related targets were collected from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, https://www.omim.org), and Therapeutic Target Database (TTD, https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). The intersection of ibrutinib targets and myocardial injury-related genes was identified using Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) to obtain the common targets between ibrutinib and myocardial injury.

2.1.2. Protein-Protein Interaction Network Construction

The common targets were imported into the STRING database (https://string-db.org, version 11.5) to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network with a confidence score threshold of ≥0.7. The resulting network was visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1), and hub genes were identified based on degree centrality analysis.

2.1.3. Enrichment Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed using the Metascape database (https://metascape.org). GO terms with p-value < 0.01 were considered statistically significant. The enriched pathways were visualized using ggplot2 in R software (version 4.2.0).

2.2. Molecular Docking

The binding affinity between ibrutinib and key target proteins (BTK and EGFR) was evaluated using molecular docking analysis. The three-dimensional structures of BTK (PDB ID: 3PJX) and EGFR (PDB ID: 1M17) were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org). The ligand structure of ibrutinib was obtained from PubChem Compound Database (CID: 24821094). AutoDock Vina software (version 1.2.0) was used for molecular docking simulation. The binding modes were visualized using PyMoL software (version 2.5.0), and binding affinities were expressed in kcal/mol.

2.3. Cell Culture and Drug Treatment

H9C2 rat cardiomyocytes (Baidi Biotechnology) were cultured in DMEM (Baidi Biotechnology) supplemented with 10% FBS (Lonsera) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into appropriate culture vessels and incubated overnight to allow cell attachment.

Ibrutinib (AikonChem, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to prepare a 100 mM stock solution and stored at -80°C. For drug treatment, ibrutinib was diluted with complete culture medium to final concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μM. The final DMSO concentration in all treatment groups was maintained at ≤0.1% (v/v) to avoid solvent toxicity. Cells were treated with ibrutinib for 24 hours before subsequent experiments.

2.4. Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). H9C2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 6,000 cells per well and incubated overnight. After treatment with various concentrations of ibrutinib for 24 hours, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 hours. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to the control group.

2.5. Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay

The mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was detected using the JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China, C2006). H9C2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 7,500 cells per well and treated with ibrutinib for 24 hours. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with JC-1 working solution (1×) at 37°C for 20 minutes in the dark. CCCP (10 μM, Beyotime) was used as a positive control. Cells were then washed twice with cold JC-1 buffer and observed under an fluorescence microscope at excitation wavelengths of 490 nm (red fluorescence) and 530 nm (green fluorescence). The red/green fluorescence ratio was calculated to assess mitochondrial membrane potential changes.

2.6. Reactive Oxygen Species Detection

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). H9C2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 6,000 cells per well and treated with ibrutinib for 24 hours. After treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS to remove residual culture medium and detached cells. DCFH-DA was diluted with serum-free medium to a final concentration of 10 μM and incubated with cells at 37°C in the dark for 30 minutes. Cells were then washed three times with PBS to remove uncombined dye. ROS levels were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nexcope, China) with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 535 nm. Green fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

2.7. Apoptosis Assessment by AO/PI Double Staining

Apoptosis was evaluated using the acridine orange/propidium iodide (AO/PI) double staining assay. H9C2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 7,500 cells per well and treated with ibrutinib for 24 hours. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and stained with AO (200 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) and PI (1 mg/mL, Solarbio, China) mixed solution in PBS. The staining was performed at room temperature in the dark for 5 minutes. Cells were then washed three times with PBS and observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope. Viable cells appeared green with intact nuclei, while apoptotic cells displayed orange-red fluorescence due to PI penetration. Cell density and the proportion of apoptotic cells were quantified in five randomly selected fields per well.

2.8. Cell Proliferation Assay (EdU Incorporation)

Cell proliferation was assessed using the EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) incorporation assay with the BeyoClick™ EdU-488 Cell Proliferation Detection Kit (Beyotime, China, C0071). H9C2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 3,500 cells per well and treated with ibrutinib for 24 hours. EdU solution (10 μM) was added to the culture medium and incubated for 2 hours to allow incorporation into newly synthesized DNA. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China) for 15 minutes and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes. After washing with 3% BSA, cells were incubated with click reaction solution for 30 minutes in the dark, followed by counterstaining with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) for nuclear visualization. EdU-positive cells (green fluorescence) were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nexcope, China) at 20× magnification. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated relative to total DAPI-stained cells.

2.9. Western Blot Analysis

The protein expression levels of key signaling pathway components were determined by Western blot analysis. After ibrutinib treatment for 24 hours, H9C2 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails, and the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 hour at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with Primary antibodies (all from Immunoway Biotechnology) targeted total and phosphorylated forms of PI3K p85α, Akt, EGFR, and BTK (catalog numbers: YM8045, YP0224, YM8463, YM8304, YM8344, YM8664, YT5346, YP0287). GAPDH was used as the internal loading control. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection kit and imaged with a ChemiDoc system (Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA), with the expression level of each target protein normalized to that of GAPDH.

2.10. Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Network Pharmacology Analysis Reveals Key Targets and Pathways of Ibrutinib-Induced Cardiotoxicity

To systematically explore the molecular mechanisms underlying ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity, network pharmacology analysis was performed to identify common targets between ibrutinib and myocardial injury. Venn diagram analysis identified 42 overlapping targets from 337 ibrutinib-related targets and 471 cardiotoxicity-related genes (Figure 1A). The drug-target-disease network demonstrated that ibrutinib interacts with these 42 core targets to exert cardiotoxic effects (Figure 1B). The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network revealed that SRC, ESR1, EGFR, AKT1, and CASP3 served as hub genes with the highest degree of connectivity (Figure 1C). Further topological analysis identified the hub gene module containing 20 key genes, with EGFR, SRC, AKT1, ESR1, and CASP3 as the top-ranking nodes (Figure 1D). The core targets identification results demonstrated that ESR1, AKT1, SRC, EGFR, CASP3, ERBB2, ANXA5, MAPK8, PTGS2, and IGF1 were identified as the most critical targets, with ACE, NOS3, PTGS2, and PPARA forming another important regulatory module (Figure 1E and F).

To further investigate the biological functions and signaling pathways involved, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed. The results demonstrated that the common targets were significantly enriched in phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase signaling, protein kinase B signaling, and response to reactive oxygen species in biological processes; membrane raft and membrane microdomain in cellular components; and protein tyrosine kinase activity and receptor regulator activity in molecular functions (Figure 2A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway ranked as the most significantly enriched pathway among all associated pathways (Figure 2B). The drug-target-pathway-phenotype network further visualized the comprehensive mechanism by which ibrutinib exerts cardiotoxic effects through multiple targets and pathways (Figure 2C).

3.2. Ibrutinib Inhibits Cell Viability, Promotes ROS Generation, and Induces Apoptosis in Cardiomyocytes

To validate the cardiotoxic effects of ibrutinib on cardiomyocytes, we performed cellular experiments in H9C2 rat cardiomyoblasts. The CCK-8 assay showed that ibrutinib inhibited H9C2 viability in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 value of 119.49 μM (Figure 3A). Intracellular ROS levels were measured using DCFH-DA probe, and the results demonstrated that ibrutinib treatment (40 μM and 80 μM) significantly increased ROS production compared with the control group (Figure 3B and C). Furthermore, the EdU incorporation assay showed that ibrutinib treatment (10 μM and 20 μM) dose-dependently decreased the proportion of EdU-positive cells, indicating inhibition of cell proliferation (Figure 3D and E).

(B) ROS fluorescence imaging in H9C2 cells treated with ibrutinib (0, 40, 80 μM). (C) Quantification of ROS levels. (D) EdU incorporation assay in H9C2 cells treated with ibrutinib (0, 10, 20 μM). (E) Quantification of EdU-positive cell proportion. Data are presented as mean ± SD; ***p < 0.001 vs. control

To further confirm the cardiotoxic effects of ibrutinib in vivo and in vitro, we performed immunofluorescence staining in mouse heart tissues and H9C2 cells. In mouse heart tissues, TUNEL staining showed that ibrutinib treatment (10 mg/kg and 25 mg/kg) reduced the proportion of TUNEL-positive cells (Figure 4A and B). In H9C2 cells, Calcein-AM/PI double staining demonstrated that ibrutinib treatment (5 μM and 10 μM) dose-dependently increased the proportion of PI-positive (dead) cells (Figure 4C and D).

3.3. Molecular Docking and Western Blot Analysis Confirm EGFR/PI3K/Akt Pathway Involvement in Ibrutinib-Induced Cardiotoxicity

To investigate the underlying signaling mechanisms, molecular docking simulation and Western blot analysis were performed. Molecular docking revealed that ibrutinib bound both BTK and EGFR with strong affinity (binding energy: -30.2 kcal/mol) (Figure 5A). The docking analysis revealed multiple hydrogen bonds and salt bridges between ibrutinib and the target proteins, with binding distances ranging from 2.2 Å to 3.2 Å. Western blot analysis was performed to examine the effects of ibrutinib on the EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. The results showed that ibrutinib treatment significantly downregulated the expression of phosphorylated PI3K p85 (p-PI3K p85), phosphorylated Akt (p-Akt S473), phosphorylated mTOR (p-mTOR S2448), and phosphorylated p70S6K (p-p70S6K T389), while the total protein levels of PI3K p110α, PI3K p85, Akt, mTOR, and p70S6K remained unchanged (Figure 5B and C). GAPDH was used as the internal loading control.

4. Discussion

This study integrates network toxicology with in vitro validation to elucidate the molecular mechanism of ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity, with a particular focus on the EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis. Network toxicology analysis identified 42 overlapping targets between ibrutinib and myocardial injury. Topological analysis of the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network revealed SRC, ESR1, EGFR, AKT1, and CASP3 as core hub genes, while KEGG enrichment analysis identified the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway as the most significantly enriched cascade. These results confirm the multi-target, multi-pathway regulatory nature of ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity from a systems biology perspective and are consistent with previous clinical and preclinical findings.

This study offers several advances over previous work. First, to our knowledge, it is the first to integrate network toxicology with multi-dimensional experimental validation—including molecular docking, cell viability assays, ROS detection, proliferation analysis, and Western blot—to systematically dissect the mechanisms of ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity. Unlike earlier in silico analyses that focused on single targets or isolated pathways, 26 we constructed a global drug–target–pathway–phenotype network and identified EGFR, SRC, AKT1, ESR1, and CASP3 as core hub genes, with the PI3K-Akt pathway as the top enriched cascade. Notably, receptor tyrosine kinases including EGFR, SRC, and ERBB2 have been reported as key off-target kinases significantly associated with atrial fibrillation risk 26 ; their identification as hub genes in our network further supports their role as critical mediators of ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity. This systems-level approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of ibrutinib’s off-target effects on cardiac signaling networks. Second, whereas prior studies documented PI3K-Akt pathway inhibition primarily in atrial cardiomyocytes (HL-1 cells) or human stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes,18,24 our study is the first to systematically validate the complete EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling cascade in H9C2 ventricular cardiomyocytes, covering phosphorylation events from upstream EGFR to downstream p70S6K. This is particularly important because ventricular dysfunction and heart failure are increasingly recognized as clinical manifestations of ibrutinib cardiotoxicity.12,13,25 Third, our molecular docking and Western blot analyses provide multi-level structural and functional evidence confirming the central role of the EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis, establishing a novel integrative framework applicable to investigating cardiotoxic mechanisms of other kinase inhibitors. These findings address the gap in systematic target screening in this field and provide a robust scientific basis for subsequent experimental validation.

Our findings are consistent with and extend several recent studies. Li et al. demonstrated that ibrutinib (30 mg/kg/day, 4weeks) induced ventricular arrhythmias in mice, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, and decreased PI3K-AKT pathway activity in ventricular tissue. 27 These results align with our observation that ibrutinib suppresses the PI3K/Akt/mTOR cascade in H9C2 ventricular cardiomyocytes, supporting the notion that ventricular dysfunction is mediated by this conserved signaling mechanism across species. However, whereas Li et al. focused primarily on the protective effect of metformin via AMPK activation, our study systematically mapped the upstream EGFR-mediated regulation of this pathway using network toxicology and molecular docking, thereby providing a more comprehensive mechanistic framework.

Furthermore, Qin et al. reported that ibrutinib inhibits the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway in HL-1 atrial cardiomyocytes, leading to autophagic degradation of connexins and atrial arrhythmia. 24 Although their study elegantly elucidated an atrial-specific proarrhythmic mechanism, it was limited to atrial cells and did not examine ventricular cardiomyocytes or upstream EGFR-mediated regulation. Our study complements their work by demonstrating that the same pathway is suppressed in H9C2 ventricular cells, suggesting that ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity may affect both atrial and ventricular chambers through a common molecular mechanism, albeit with different downstream consequences: connexin degradation in atria versus impaired proliferation and survival in ventricles.

In addition, recent real-world pharmacovigilance data provide important clinical context for our findings. Zhao et al. analyzed the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database and found that ibrutinib was significantly associated with 47 cardiovascular adverse events, including heart failure and ventricular arrhythmias, whereas next-generation BTK inhibitors (acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib) exhibited substantially lower reporting odds ratios. 28 These clinical observations corroborate our experimental data and support the hypothesis that off-target EGFR inhibition contributes to the less favorable cardiovascular profile of ibrutinib. Notably, cardiac MRI studies have revealed that patients with ibrutinib-associated cardiotoxicity frequently exhibit myocardial inflammation and fibrosis; nearly two-thirds show evidence of myocardial injury, and over 50% demonstrate late gadolinium enhancement indicative of scar formation. 29 These clinical imaging findings are consistent with our observation of ibrutinib-induced cell death and impaired proliferation in cardiomyocytes, suggesting that the in vitro molecular changes we identified may translate into structural myocardial remodeling in patients.

Finally, the cardiotoxicity of ibrutinib must be understood within the broader landscape of targeted cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction. As reviewed by Hahn et al., kinase inhibitors targeting growth factor receptors (including EGFR and HER2) commonly disrupt pro-survival PI3K/Akt signaling in cardiomyocytes, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and ultimately heart failure. 30 Our identification of EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis inhibition as the core mechanism of ibrutinib cardiotoxicity fits precisely within this established paradigm yet extends it by providing the first direct structural evidence of ibrutinib-EGFR binding and the first systematic validation across the complete signaling cascade in ventricular cardiomyocytes. Collectively, these comparisons demonstrate that our findings are not only internally consistent but also well-integrated with the emerging clinical and preclinical literature on BTK inhibitor and targeted therapy cardiotoxicity.

To further characterize the structural basis of ibrutinib’s off-target effects, molecular docking was performed on core targets. The results demonstrated that ibrutinib forms stable complexes with both BTK and EGFR, with a binding energy of -30.2 kcal/mol and multiple hydrogen bonds and salt bridges (2.2–3.2 Å), indicating strong binding affinity for EGFR. These findings explain the off-target effects of ibrutinib from a structural biology perspective and provide a molecular basis for its cardiotoxicity. This conclusion is highly consistent with previous clinical observations: compared with first-generation ibrutinib, second-generation selective BTK inhibitors such as acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib exert weaker off-target inhibition of EGFR and are associated with significantly lower incidences of atrial fibrillation (9.4% and 5.2% versus 16%, respectively). 31 Our molecular docking results directly verify this hypothesis at the molecular level, confirming that ibrutinib binds directly to the EGFR kinase domain, and address the lack of direct binding affinity evidence in previous studies.

After confirming ibrutinib’s binding capacity to core targets, we conducted functional validation in H9C2 cardiomyocytes to determine its direct damaging effects. Ibrutinib dose-dependently reduced cardiomyocyte viability, elevated intracellular ROS levels, suppressed cell proliferation, and induced apoptosis, thereby confirming its direct cardiotoxicity. These findings are consistent with previous reports: Shafaattalab et al. observed a dose-dependent reduction in viability in human stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes treated with ibrutinib, with certain atrial specificity, 32 corroborating our cell viability results. Pharmacovigilance-based real-world data analyses have also identified the strongest association signal between ibrutinib and atrial fibrillation. 33 Notably, enrichment analysis revealed that overlapping targets were significantly enriched in biological processes related to ROS response and protein tyrosine kinase activity; these in silico predictions are consistent with our cellular observation of ibrutinib-induced ROS elevation, preliminarily confirming the role of oxidative stress in ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity and further supporting the accuracy of the network toxicology approach. Furthermore, EdU assays demonstrated that ibrutinib significantly inhibited cardiomyocyte proliferation, suggesting multi-dimensional detrimental effects: ibrutinib not only induces apoptosis in mature cardiomyocytes but also impairs cardiomyocyte regeneration and repair, which may underlie cardiac dysfunction following long-term administration. Previous studies have mostly focused on the short-term electrophysiological effects of ibrutinib, with limited attention to long-term impacts on cardiomyocyte proliferation. Our findings thus provide a novel explanation for the chronic cardiotoxicity of ibrutinib.

Western blot analysis systematically examined the activation status of the core EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway predicted by network analysis. Ibrutinib markedly downregulated phosphorylation of PI3K p85, Akt (Ser473), mTOR (Ser2448), and p70S6K (Thr389) without altering total protein expression, indicating specific inhibition of pathway activation rather than protein synthesis suppression. These results extend earlier observations by McMullen et al., who first proposed in 2014 that ibrutinib increases atrial fibrillation risk through PI3K-Akt inhibition but detected only p-Akt changes without validating the full upstream-to-downstream cascade. 18 Subsequent work in HL-1 atrial cells demonstrated connexin autophagy via PI3K-Akt-mTOR suppression, 24 yet neither study examined ventricular cardiomyocytes or upstream EGFR-mediated regulation. Here, using H9C2 ventricular cells, we present for the first time the complete regulatory effect of ibrutinib from upstream EGFR to downstream p70S6K, confirming this pathway as a core mechanism across cardiac chambers. The PI3K/Akt pathway is a critical pro-survival axis in cardiomyocytes, regulating protein synthesis, cell cycle progression, apoptosis inhibition, mitochondrial function, and oxidative stress responses. 34 Ibrutinib-mediated EGFR inhibition suppresses PI3K activation and Akt phosphorylation, thereby weakening protective signaling and reducing cellular tolerance to injury. Consequently, impaired mTOR and p70S6K phosphorylation directly explains the observed inhibition of cardiomyocyte proliferation, establishing a mechanistic link between molecular pathway alterations and functional cardiac damage.

Notably, our findings are supported by recent preclinical studies demonstrating that metformin alleviates ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity by activating the PI3K/Akt pathway, 27 which reciprocally verifies the central role of this axis. These results also suggest potential therapeutic targets for future clinical intervention.

In summary, this study integrated network toxicology and in vitro experiments to systematically reveal the molecular mechanism of ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our experiments were limited to H9C2 ventricular cardiomyocytes; validation in atrial cardiomyocytes is needed given the reported atrial specificity of ibrutinib cardiotoxicity. Second, only in vitro experiments were conducted, and subsequent in vivo validation using animal models is required to support clinical translation. Finally, this study focused primarily on the core EGFR/PI3K/Akt pathway; the roles of other network-predicted targets such as SRC and ESR1 require further functional characterization to fully dissect the complex regulatory network of ibrutinib-induced cardiotoxicity.

5. Conclusions

This study integrates network toxicology, molecular docking, and in vitro assays to demonstrate that ibrutinib induces cardiotoxicity primarily through off-target EGFR inhibition and subsequent suppression of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pro-survival pathway in cardiomyocytes. Network analysis identified 42 overlapping targets with EGFR, AKT1, SRC, ESR1, and CASP3 as hub genes, while molecular docking confirmed stable ibrutinib-EGFR binding (-30.2 kcal/mol). Functionally, ibrutinib dose-dependently reduced cardiomyocyte viability, elevated ROS, inhibited proliferation, and downregulated phosphorylation of EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR/p70S6K. These findings establish the EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis as a key mechanistic node and provide a molecular basis for monitoring and mitigating BTK inhibitor-associated cardiac toxicity.
