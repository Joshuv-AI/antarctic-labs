Microbial and enzymatic synergy in fermented fish: impacts on flavor, texture, and safety with multi-omics and AI insights
Authors: Lingling Dai, Lin Chen, Ying Xiao, Shengyang Zheng, Guohua Hou, Baodong Zheng, Longtao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFermented fish develops its characteristic sensory properties through the synergistic action of endogenous enzymes and halotolerant microbial consortia. This review synthesizes current knowledge on these microbial–enzymatic interactions, emphasizing proteolysis and lipolysis. These processes release amino acids, peptides, and fatty acids, providing essential precursors for aroma-active volatiles and non-volatile taste compounds. Concurrently, acidification, protein hydrolysis, and matrix restructuring modulate texture, creating a dynamic balance between enzymatic softening and acid-induced gelation. We evaluate key safety concerns, particularly biogenic amine and N-nitrosamine formation, discussing mitigation strategies via process optimization and targeted starter cultures. Finally, we examine how multi-omics coupled with artificial intelligence can elucidate biochemical mechanisms, identify quality markers, and enable predictive monitoring. By integrating these elements into a substrate-to-volatilome framework, this review links mechanisms to quality outcomes, highlighting opportunities for the rational control of fermented fish production.Keywords: Fermented fish, Microbial–enzymatic synergy, Biogenic amines, Multi-omics, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Fermented fish products are integral to traditional diets across Asia, Africa, Northern Europe, and other regions, and are valued for their distinctive sensory attributes and extended shelf life (Cha & Yu, 2024; Narzary et al., 2021; Zang et al., 2020). Although production methods vary across cultures, most processes rely on salting and spontaneous fermentation. Some Nordic and North African products are salt-cured, such as Norwegian rakfisk and Egyptian feseekh, whereas many Southeast Asian traditions produce fish sauce through prolonged high-salt fermentation followed by liquid extraction. In other products, fermentable carbohydrate sources are incorporated, as in Thai pla-som and Japanese narezushi, to promote lactic acid fermentation (Zang et al., 2020). These artisanal practices highlight the cultural and technological significance of fermented fish and reflect two recurring objectives: the development of desirable flavor and the maintenance of product safety through preservation hurdles. Across these systems, preservation and flavor formation are driven by coordinated microbial and enzymatic activities. High salt concentrations impose selective pressure that inhibits many spoilage organisms, while endogenous proteases and lipases initiate the degradation of muscle proteins and lipids into smaller compounds during ripening (Prihanto et al., 2024; Zang et al., 2020). When fermentable carbohydrates are present, lactic acid bacteria (LAB) convert sugars into organic acids, further reducing pH and strengthening barriers against pathogens, while also contributing to flavor complexity (Prihanto et al., 2024; Zang et al., 2020). Through this microbial–enzymatic synergy, a wide range of flavor precursors and metabolites is generated during fermentation. Proteins are hydrolyzed by endogenous and microbial proteases into amino acids and small peptides, producing savory compounds, such as glutamate and glycine, as well as umami-enhancing peptides that enrich taste perception (Li, Li, Wang, et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). In parallel, endogenous and microbial lipases release fatty acids, which undergo oxidation and enzymatic transformation to generate volatile compounds, including alcohols, ketones, and esters, that contribute to the characteristic aroma of long-fermented fish products such as fish sauce (Prihanto et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). Amino acid catabolism, including Ehrlich- and Strecker-type reactions, further produces aldehydes, amines, and related metabolites associated with the complex flavor notes of well-ripened fermented fish (Wang et al., 2023).

Microbial and enzymatic processes also influence texture development. Proteolysis degrades myofibrillar structures and connective tissue, contributing to tenderization, whereas acidification driven by fermentative microorganisms, particularly LAB, can promote protein aggregation and gel formation, thereby helping to maintain a cohesive matrix. The balance between degradation and gelation determines whether a product softens and partially liquefies or retains a firm, sliceable structure. For example, extensive proteolysis during high-salt fish sauce fermentation can gradually liquefy fish tissue into a smooth sauce, whereas in fermented fish sausage, co-inoculation with LAB induces acid-driven gel formation and produces a moderately firm, sliceable product (Cui et al., 2024). Thus, microbial metabolism and endogenous enzymatic activity act together to define both the flavor profile and textural properties of fermented fish.

From a safety perspective, traditional processing relies on multiple preservation barriers, including high salt concentration, reduced water activity, and acidic pH, to suppress pathogens and spoilage organisms (Prihanto et al., 2024; Zang et al., 2020). Dominant LAB contribute to safety by producing lactic acid, which further lowers pH, and bacteriocins, which can inhibit undesirable bacteria (Prihanto et al., 2024). These conditions generally enhance the microbiological stability of fermented fish; however, important safety challenges remain. A major concern is the formation of biogenic amines (BAs), such as histamine, tyramine, and putrescine, through amino acid decarboxylation by certain fermentative bacteria. Excessive BA accumulation poses health risks and therefore requires effective mitigation. Traditional and technological interventions used to limit BA accumulation and other hazards include optimization of salt content and fermentation temperature, adjustment of ripening time, and the use of selected starter cultures that do not produce BAs or may degrade them (Alvarez & Moreno-Arribas, 2014; Ghayoomi et al., 2023; Zang et al., 2020). These interventions aim to improve flavor development while maintaining histamine and other hazardous compounds within safe limits, thereby supporting product safety and regulatory compliance (Ghayoomi et al., 2023).

Recent advances have enabled a more quantitative understanding of fermented fish processing and improved opportunities for process management. Integrating multi-omics approaches, including comprehensive profiling of genes, transcripts, proteins, and metabolites, with artificial intelligence (AI), especially machine learning (ML), can help connect microbial community structure and function with measurable flavor, texture, and safety outcomes. In parallel, ML methods can use high-dimensional datasets to model fermentation trajectories, predict quality attributes, and support monitoring of potential hazards (Bansal et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). Together, these approaches provide quantitative indicators for process optimization and improved batch-to-batch consistency, while supporting the modernization of traditional fermented fish products without compromising their identity and authenticity (Chandross-Cohen et al., 2025; Zhang, Zhu, et al., 2025).

Despite this progress, key knowledge gaps still limit mechanistic integration and practical control. Many studies examine individual microorganisms or single enzymatic activities under simplified conditions, whereas systems-level frameworks remain limited (Ferrocino et al., 2023). Important process parameters and their cross-scale effects on product quality are not yet well defined or standardized; for example, quantitative relationships among salt concentration, fermentation time, and resulting flavor and safety profiles remain incompletely elucidated. Moreover, although initial attempts have been made to apply multi-omics data and AI to fermentation monitoring, these predictive frameworks require further practical validation, particularly with respect to their efficacy in real-world production and reliability in forecasting quality outcomes (Zhang, Zhu, et al., 2025).

Considering these challenges, this review synthesizes microbial–enzymatic synergy in fermented fish from a systems-level perspective. It links microbial succession, endogenous and microbial enzymatic reactions, substrate conversion, and the resulting trajectories of flavor, texture, and safety. Particular attention is given to biogenic amines, N-nitrosamines, and other safety hazards, as well as to the emerging role of multi-omics and AI-assisted monitoring. The novelty of this review lies not simply in summarizing microorganisms or metabolites, but in organizing fermented fish quality development around a causal chain linking microorganisms and enzymes, substrates, metabolites, quality outcomes, and control strategies. Here, the substrate-to-volatilome framework refers to a mechanistic chain linking raw-material substrates, endogenous and microbial enzymes, intermediate metabolites, volatile and non-volatile compounds, and final flavor, texture, and safety outcomes. Key factors influencing quality development in fermented fish

Fermented fish quality is generated by interacting biological actors rather than by microorganisms or endogenous enzymes alone. This section therefore first distinguishes the main contributors—endogenous fish enzymes, dominant fermentative microorganisms, and successional microbial communities—before Section 3 follows the biochemical routes through which their activities generate flavor, texture, and safety outcomes.

Endogenous enzymatic contributions

Fish muscle contains various proteolytic enzymes that remain active after death. Autolytic proteolysis mediated by calpains, cathepsins, and related proteases initiates primary protein breakdown before microbial fermentation becomes dominant. Functionally, endogenous proteases should be framed as early precursor generators: they release peptides and amino acids that directly affect taste, provide substrates for microbial amino-acid catabolism, and contribute to early tenderization through degradation of myofibrillar and connective-tissue proteins (Giyatmi, & Irianto, 2017; Khudair et al., 2023). Detailed downstream flavor pathways are discussed in Section 3.

These endogenous reactions are beneficial only when maintained within an appropriate range. Excessive proteolysis can produce an overly soft or mushy texture and may also promote bitter peptides, ammonia, amines, or proteolytic off-odors; therefore, salt concentration, temperature, and fermentation duration must be managed to moderate enzyme activity rather than simply maximize protein breakdown.

Fish tissues also contain lipid-degrading enzymes, mainly lipases and lipoxygenases, that contribute to lipid hydrolysis and oxidation during fermentation (Liu et al., 2023). Endogenous lipases release free fatty acids (FFAs) from fish lipids, and these FFAs may then undergo oxidation or further enzymatic transformation to form volatile aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols that contribute to characteristic aromas in fermented fish products (Flores et al., 2009). For example, endogenous lipoxygenase activity can produce n-hexanal, a grassy or green odorant, from polyunsaturated fatty acids (Gong et al., 2023). However, uncontrolled lipolysis and oxidation can lead to rancidity; therefore, salt content, oxygen exposure, and temperature should be controlled to maintain lipid degradation within a desirable sensory range (Amaral et al., 2018). Overall, endogenous proteases and lipases establish the initial biochemical substrate pool—peptides, amino acids, FFAs, and oxidation intermediates—that microorganisms subsequently transform into sensory-active or safety-relevant metabolites.

Key microorganisms and their roles

Although endogenous reactions establish the initial substrate pool, final product quality is shaped by subsequent microbial transformations. As fermentation proceeds, halotolerant microorganisms further transform peptides, amino acids, fatty acids, and residual carbohydrates into metabolites that define flavor, texture, and safety. The dominant microbial groups in fermented fish typically include lactic acid bacteria (LAB), coagulase-negative staphylococci (CNS), yeasts, and other halophilic or proteolytic bacteria (Mao et al., 2024). Their importance should be interpreted in a product-dependent manner because salt level, carbohydrate availability, oxygen exposure, and fermentation duration strongly shape which microorganisms become functionally dominant.

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB). LAB, such as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, are often early colonizers in many fermented fish systems, especially when fermentable carbohydrates are available. They ferment sugars into lactic acid, reducing pH and stabilizing the product by inhibiting spoilage microorganisms (Hu et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023). LAB-derived acidification can induce protein gelation at low pH, contributing to firm and cohesive textures in products such as fish mince or fermented fish sausage (Cui et al., 2024). LAB also contribute to flavor by producing proteases and peptidases that further hydrolyze fish proteins into savory amino acids and small peptides, thereby enhancing umami perception (Wang et al., 2023). They also generate flavor-active metabolites, such as diacetyl, which imparts buttery notes, and various esters associated with fruity aromas. In this way, LAB support safety and shelf-life while contributing to the development of texture and flavor (Belleggia & Osimani, 2023). Functionally, LAB connect carbohydrate availability with multiple quality outcomes: sugar fermentation produces lactic acid, which lowers pH, suppresses spoilage organisms, promotes acid-induced protein gelation in suitable matrices, and contributes sourness and flavor balance.

Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CNS). Halotolerant staphylococci, including Staphylococcus xylosus, S. carnosus, and S. equorum, frequently participate in high-salt fish fermentations. Although they acidify less strongly than LAB, they can contribute substantially to proteolysis and lipolysis under saline conditions. CNS strains secrete proteases that complement endogenous and LAB enzymes, releasing additional amino acids, including precursors of aroma compounds (Wang, Wu, & Wang, 2022). They also produce lipases that liberate fatty acids from triglycerides; subsequent conversion of these fatty acids to aldehydes and methyl ketones contributes cured or cheese-like aroma notes (Olivares et al., 2011). For example, inoculation with S. xylosus during fish sauce fermentation has been reported to enhance lipid breakdown and the formation of lipid-derived volatiles (Xu et al., 2019). Importantly, CNS starter strains must be selected for safety, including the absence of enterotoxin production (Ravyts et al., 2010). When present at appropriate levels, CNS may help develop nutty and aged flavor notes, including those associated with branched-chain metabolite formation, and may contribute to umami and aroma complexity during protein-rich, carbohydrate-poor stages of fermentation. Thus, CNS are best framed as salt-tolerant secondary transformers: they act on protein- and lipid-derived substrates released during ripening and convert them into amino-acid- and fatty-acid-derived aroma compounds, while requiring careful safety screening before starter application.

Yeasts. Halotolerant yeasts, such as Debaryomyces hansenii and Yarrowia lipolytica, may occur during fish fermentation, particularly on product surfaces or under moderately salted conditions (Punyauppa-path et al., 2022). Yeasts can ferment residual sugars or carbohydrates to produce alcohols and esters, contributing fruity or floral aromas; for example, D. hansenii can produce fruity ethyl acetate. Yeasts can also participate in lipid and aroma metabolism; for example, Wickerhamomyces anomalus and Pichia fermentans have been applied to improve aroma development in fermented sour fish (Gao et al., 2024). In addition, yeasts catabolize amino acids through the Ehrlich pathway, producing alcohols and aldehydes that enrich flavor complexity; for example, 3-methyl-1-butanol derived from amino acid catabolism contributes malty notes (Martínez-Onandi et al., 2019). However, excessive yeast growth can increase pH through amino acid deamination and ammonia release. In many fermentations, LAB-mediated acidification may constrain yeast activity and help prevent pH elevation (Ma et al., 2025; Tofalo et al., 2020). Yeast contributions are likely to be most relevant in surface-associated, moderately salted, or oxygen-accessible fermentation niches rather than uniformly across all fermented fish products; they mainly diversify aroma by converting sugars, amino acids, and lipid-derived substrates into alcohols, aldehydes, and esters, whereas excessive amino-acid deamination may elevate pH and shift fermentation toward spoilage-like notes.

Other fermentative microorganisms. In specific fermented fish environments, additional microorganisms occupy specialized niches. Extremely halophilic archaea, such as Halobacterium spp., may proliferate in very high-salt fish pastes or sauces and produce metabolites such as trimethylamine that contribute pungent, cheesy, or fishy aromas (Das et al., 2020). Salt-tolerant Bacillus species also occur frequently, especially in Asian fermented fish. They secrete proteases that hydrolyze proteins and, in some cases, can degrade biogenic amines such as histamine. This biogenic amine-degrading capacity may improve safety by lowering hazardous amine levels in the final product (Li et al., 2023). Tetragenococcus halophilus, a high-salt-adapted LAB, commonly dominates long-term fish sauce fermentations and contributes to lactic acid formation and aroma development, playing a central role in these ecosystems (Wakinaka et al., 2019). These auxiliary microorganisms influence fermentation outcomes in distinct ways. For example, Bacillus spp. can increase peptide and amino acid availability and contribute to umami-related profiles, but if uncontrolled, they may raise pH and increase spoilage risk (Han, Kong, Wang, et al., 2023). Understanding and managing these non-traditional fermenters is therefore important for controlling subtle flavor nuances and preventing off-flavors or safety issues. Microbial community succession and functional dynamics

The fermented fish microbiota is not static; rather, it undergoes microbial community succession throughout fermentation. Initially, the high-salt, protein-rich environment is selective, allowing only halotolerant organisms, often Gram-positive bacteria such as LAB and certain cocci, to establish readily. LAB often dominate early stages when fermentable sugars are present, producing lactic acid and creating an acidic, relatively anaerobic environment. Over time, as sugars are depleted, pH changes, and salt stress persists or increases, other halotolerant or halophilic taxa that are better adapted to the evolving conditions may become dominant. For instance, in fish sauce fermentations, LAB may initiate the process, whereas Tetragenococcus, Staphylococcus, Bacillus, or halophilic archaea may become predominant during mid-to-late stages, when peptides and amino acids have accumulated and high-salt or anaerobic conditions prevail (Han, Kong, Jiang, et al., 2023). These successional changes are accompanied by shifts in metabolic function. Early LAB communities primarily produce lactic acid and simple volatile compounds, whereas later-developing communities generate more complex aromas, such as branched-chain volatiles from amino acid catabolism by staphylococci, and continue extensive proteolysis and lipolysis (Belleggia & Osimani, 2023). Microbial succession should therefore be interpreted as a shift in functional dominance: early acidification and pH control are gradually complemented or replaced by proteolysis, lipolysis, amino-acid catabolism, ester formation, and, under poorly controlled conditions, hazardous metabolite accumulation.

Modern high-throughput DNA sequencing has substantially advanced scientific understanding of these community dynamics. Culture-independent surveys, including 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and shotgun metagenomics, make it possible to identify dominant taxa as well as previously overlooked minor taxa throughout fermentation (Ferrocino & Cocolin, 2017). Using these tools, researchers have mapped changes in microbial composition across fermentation time points and preparation methods. For example, Bhutia et al. (2021) applied 16S rRNA sequencing to traditional Indian fermented fish and detected underappreciated anaerobes that emerged during late fermentation, revealing a more complex community than had been recognized through culture-based approaches. Such findings indicate that apparently quiescent or stable phases of fermentation may still contain active microorganisms that contribute subtly to flavor or safety.

Beyond identifying taxa, multi-omics approaches provide functional insight by revealing the metabolic potential and in situ activity of the microbial community. Metagenomics can inventory genes encoding proteases, amino acid decarboxylases, esterases, and other enzymes, whereas metatranscriptomics and metaproteomics indicate which genes and enzymes are actively expressed or produced at specific fermentation stages. Such evidence can link particular microorganisms to enzymatic activities, but causal attribution still requires controlled inoculation, strain isolation, enzyme validation, isotope tracing, or intervention experiments.

The integration of multi-omics data therefore supports a more mechanistic view of fermentation ecosystems (Ferrocino et al., 2023). Researchers can correlate specific community members, genes, transcripts, or proteins with detected metabolites to generate microbe–metabolite hypotheses. For example, an increase in Bacillus carrying protease genes together with rising free amino acid concentrations would support—but not by itself prove—the inference that Bacillus contributed to proteolysis (Shi et al., 2022). This systems-level perspective moves beyond cataloguing which microbes are present toward testing their functional roles and interactions.

Machine learning can further analyze large multi-omics datasets and identify predictive patterns that are difficult to detect by manual interpretation alone (Nam et al., 2024). However, these models should be presented primarily as tools for prediction and hypothesis generation rather than as direct evidence of mechanism unless they are supported by experimental validation.

Because microbial succession unfolds over time, longitudinal sequencing and metabolomic data are especially useful for modeling dynamic relationships among taxa, enzymes, substrates, and metabolites (Faust et al., 2015). Time-series analysis, dynamic Bayesian networks, and related approaches may help infer state shifts and directed taxon-to-metabolite associations, but their application to fermented fish should be described as emerging and requiring product-specific validation under high-salt, protein-rich fermentation conditions (Gibson et al., 2025; Ruiz-Perez et al., 2021).

Overall, sequencing and multi-omics approaches allow microbial succession to be interpreted not only as a taxonomic shift but also as a functional transition in enzyme production, substrate use, and metabolite formation. Predictive modeling approaches that build on these datasets are discussed further in Section 3.3.

Mechanisms underlying the formation of fermented fish quality

Flavor formation mechanisms in fermented fish

Having identified the principal enzymatic and microbial contributors, this section traces the major substrate-conversion pathways through which these contributors shape fermented fish quality. The emphasis shifts from which organisms are present to which substrates are transformed, which metabolites accumulate, and how these changes affect flavor, texture, and safety. One of the most valued attributes of fermented fish is its complex flavor, which arises from the combined contribution of volatile aroma compounds and non-volatile taste compounds generated during fermentation.

Volatile aroma compounds

The volatile aroma profile (volatilome) of fermented fish comprises a wide range of chemical classes, each contributing distinct aroma notes. Short-chain fatty acids, such as acetic and butyric acids, can contribute sharp, acidic, rancid, or cheesy notes, depending on concentration and product context. Esters, including ethyl acetate and ethyl butyrate, are commonly associated with fruity or sweet aromas, whereas sulfur-containing compounds, such as methanethiol, may contribute meaty, cabbage-like, or putrefactive notes even at low concentrations. Alcohols such as 3-methyl-1-butanol, also known as isoamyl alcohol, contribute malty notes, while carbonyl compounds such as 3-methylbutanal are associated with nutty or chocolate-like aromas. Many of these volatiles originate from microbial metabolism: LAB produce organic acids and small alcohols during carbohydrate fermentation, yeasts generate esters and higher alcohols through amino acid catabolism, and coagulase-negative staphylococci (CNS) contribute aldehydes and ketones through fatty acid metabolism. Non-microbial reactions also contribute to aroma development. For example, Maillard reactions between amino acids and sugars, when available, may generate roasted or caramel-like aromas, whereas lipid oxidation produces aldehydes associated with tallowy or grassy notes (Cha & Yu, 2024; Xu et al., 2023). Because each volatile compound has a specific sensory threshold and odor activity, the overall balance among these compounds is critical to the characteristic aroma of a given fermented fish product.

The volatile aroma profile changes dynamically during fermentation. In early stages, sharp acidic notes, mainly from acetic and butyric acids, together with simple alcohols, may dominate because of active carbohydrate fermentation and the initial degradation of proteins. As maturation proceeds, esters, aldehydes, and ketones derived from amino acid and fatty acid catabolism gradually accumulate. Longer fermentation generally produces more intense and diverse volatile profiles as enzymatic and oxidative processes continue to generate compounds associated with deeper umami, cheesy, ripe, or aged aromas, including branched-chain aldehydes and phenolic compounds. Producers can influence volatile composition by regulating fermentation parameters. Increased aeration may promote fatty acid oxidation and aldehyde formation, selected bacteria or yeasts may enhance ester formation, and extended fermentation may increase amino acid-derived aldehydes and deepen aroma intensity. Overall, the characteristic bouquet of fermented fish results from the coordinated contribution of carbohydrate-derived acids, amino acid-derived aldehydes and alcohols, lipid oxidation products, esters, sulfur-containing compounds, and other minor volatiles.

Non-volatile taste compounds

The key non-volatile taste contributors in fermented fish include free amino acids (FAAs), small peptides, nucleotides, and organic acids. Proteolysis of fish muscle proteins during fermentation releases substantial amounts of FAAs and peptides. Many of these compounds contribute directly to taste: glutamate and aspartate impart umami, glycine and alanine contribute sweetness, and hydrophobic amino acids, such as leucine and valine, may contribute bitterness. Peptides generated from protein hydrolysis, including pyroglutamyl peptides and other oligopeptides, may exert umami or kokumi effects, thereby enriching mouthfeel and overall flavor complexity. In addition, degradation of muscle ATP during fermentation can generate or modify nucleotide pools, including inosine monophosphate (IMP) and guanosine monophosphate (GMP), which contribute umami taste and may act synergistically with amino acids to intensify savory flavor, a phenomenon known as umami synergy.

FAA concentrations generally increase as fermentation progresses and proteolysis continues, although stage-dependent fluctuations may occur. In some cases, total FAA levels may initially decrease if microorganisms rapidly consume specific amino acids during early fermentation, followed by an increase once proteolysis exceeds microbial consumption. For example, in a traditional fermented mandarin fish product, total FAA levels were reported to decline during the first two days, probably because of microbial uptake, and then increase markedly by Days 3–5 as protein degradation accelerated, with notable increases in aspartate, glutamate, phenylalanine, tyrosine, alanine, and glycine. Organic acids produced during fermentation also influence taste. Lactic acid produced by LAB and acetic acid contribute sourness, whereas the high salt content typical of many fermented fish products provides saltiness. These taste modalities, including umami, sweetness, saltiness, sourness, and potential bitterness, interact to form the overall taste profile. A well-fermented product usually requires abundant umami and moderate sweetness, whereas excessive bitterness is undesirable. Bitterness may arise when hydrophobic or bitter-tasting peptides accumulate, or when specific amino acids reach high concentrations during late fermentation. Therefore, controlling the extent of proteolysis is essential for maximizing desirable taste-active compounds while preventing excessive protein degradation into bitter peptides, ammonia, or amine-related off-odors. Protein-derived precursors are therefore among the most important contributors to fermented fish flavor complexity because they generate both non-volatile taste compounds and volatile aroma compounds through subsequent amino acid catabolism.

Pathways of flavor formation

Carbohydrate metabolism

Although fish flesh is naturally low in carbohydrates, residual muscle glycogen or added starch sources, such as rice in some traditional recipes, can be fermented by microorganisms. LAB metabolize glucose through glycolysis, producing pyruvate that is mainly reduced to lactic acid during homofermentation, thereby contributing sourness and lowering pH. A portion of pyruvate may also be redirected under mildly aerobic conditions, for example toward acetaldehyde, which can subsequently be converted into ethanol or acetic acid. Consequently, minor end products such as ethanol, which contributes mild wine-like notes, and acetic acid, which contributes vinegar-like notes, may occur together with lactic acid. In many fermented fish products, lactic and acetic acids are major fermentation acids. Although they contribute to sourness, their more important function is pH reduction, which affects texture development, especially protein gelation, and downstream flavor chemistry (Zang, Yu, Zhang, et al., 2022). Heterofermentative LAB, when present, can also produce carbon dioxide, ethanol, and acetate from sugars; however, homofermentative metabolism may dominate in many fish fermentations unless specific heterofermentative starters are used. Overall, carbohydrate metabolism primarily generates organic acids and minor alcohols that regulate acidity and provide subtle flavor notes (Bao et al., 2018; Bintsis, 2018).

Protein hydrolysis and amino acid catabolism

Fish muscle is rich in proteins, and their stepwise degradation is central to flavor development. Proteolysis releases peptides and amino acids that directly contribute to taste, including umami and sweetness, and also serve as substrates for further biochemical transformations (Wang et al., 2020). This degradation is driven by the combined action of endogenous fish proteases and microbial proteases. Endogenous enzymes, including cathepsins, initiate protein breakdown and generate primary peptides, whereas microbial enzymes from LAB, Bacillus, CNS, and other microorganisms further hydrolyze these peptides into FAAs. Beyond their direct taste effects, FAAs are important precursors of volatile aroma compounds. Amino acid catabolism in fermented fish involves several enzymatic reactions, including deamination, decarboxylation, transamination, and oxidation (Qiu et al., 2022). Two amino acid degradation routes are particularly important.

Pathway 1: Direct side-chain breakdown. Under the action of specific lyase enzymes, some amino acids lose side chains or functional groups and generate volatile compounds with distinctive aroma properties (Ardö, 2006). Aromatic amino acids, including phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan, can form benzaldehyde, phenylacetic acid, p-hydroxyphenylacetic acid, indole, phenol, and other benzene-ring derivatives, some of which contribute floral, phenolic, or fecal notes depending on their concentration. Similarly, cysteine and methionine can be cleaved by cystathionine lyase or related enzymes to produce sulfur-containing volatiles, such as hydrogen sulfide and methanethiol, which contribute meaty or cabbage-like odors depending on concentration (Zang, Yu, Li, et al., 2022).

Pathway 2: Ehrlich pathway and Strecker degradation. In this multi-step route, microorganisms transaminate amino acids to alpha-keto acids, decarboxylate these intermediates to aldehydes, and then either reduce the aldehydes to alcohols or oxidize them to acids. For example, leucine can produce 3-methylbutanal, a key malty aroma compound, which may be further reduced to 3-methyl-1-butanol. Methionine can generate methional, which is associated with cooked-potato aroma, through Strecker degradation. These reactions, collectively referred to as the Ehrlich pathway in yeasts and certain bacteria, are important sources of fusel alcohols and aldehydes derived from amino acids (Ardö, 2006). In fermented fish, Tetragenococcus and Staphylococcus spp. may carry out such conversions and thereby enrich aroma complexity with malty, fruity, or buttery notes (Han, Kong, Jiang, et al., 2023).

In summary, protein-derived precursors are central to fermented fish flavor complexity because they generate both taste-active compounds, such as umami amino acids and peptides, and aroma compounds through downstream amino acid catabolism. Controlled proteolysis is therefore essential. Insufficient proteolysis can leave the flavor flat, whereas excessive proteolysis can produce bitter peptides, ammonia, amines, and other off-odor compounds.

Lipid oxidation and esterification

Many fermented fish substrates, particularly fatty fish species, contain substantial lipids that become important flavor precursors during fermentation. Lipolysis by endogenous and microbial lipases releases free fatty acids (FFAs) from triglycerides. Unsaturated FFAs are susceptible to enzymatic oxidation via lipoxygenases and non-enzymatic autoxidation, generating aldehydes, ketones, and acids with strong aroma activity. For example, linoleic acid can be oxidized to hexanal, which has a green or grassy aroma, and then to hexanoic acid, which is associated with cheesy or rancid notes; oleic acid oxidation can generate heptanal, which contributes dairy-like or tallowy notes (Ye et al., 2024). Certain bacteria, including Staphylococcus and Bacillus species, possess oxidative enzymes that may accelerate these reactions and increase lipid-derived volatiles. Moderate lipid oxidation can contribute characteristic fermented fish aroma notes, whereas uncontrolled oxidation leads to rancid or spoiled flavors; therefore, fermentation conditions, including oxygen exposure, must be managed to maintain oxidation within an acceptable range (Tatiyaborworntham et al., 2022).

Esterification reactions also occur between FFAs and alcohols generated during fermentation. Microbial esterases can catalyze the formation of esters, such as ethyl acetate, ethyl butyrate, and fatty acid ethyl esters. These esters contribute fruity and sweet top notes that may soften the intense savory aroma of fermented fish (Gao et al., 2025). Yeasts and some bacteria provide alcohol substrates through carbohydrate or amino acid metabolism, whereas FFAs are supplied by lipolysis (Hazelwood et al., 2008). Fermentations with active yeasts and lipolytic bacteria may therefore exhibit stronger ester-related aroma profiles (Ramos-Moreno et al., 2021). Oxygen availability is also important: limited oxygen may favor desirable aldehyde formation, whereas excessive oxygen can accelerate rancidity (Tarko et al., 2020). When appropriate and safe, producers may encourage surface yeast growth to enhance ester formation, or may favor more anaerobic conditions when a predominantly savory profile is desired. Starter cultures containing ester-producing yeasts or bacteria can further guide lipid-related pathways toward desirable fruity aromas while reducing spoilage-associated odors (Tu et al., 2025).

Overall, flavor development in fermented fish reflects interacting precursor pools and pathway balances rather than a single dominant reaction. Carbohydrates, when present, are fermented mainly into organic acids that regulate acidity, sourness, gelation, and safety. Proteins are hydrolyzed by endogenous and microbial enzymes into peptides and amino acids that provide umami, sweetness, bitterness, and substrates for amino-acid-derived aroma compounds. Lipids are hydrolyzed into FFAs that can generate desirable aldehydes, ketones, acids, and esters, but also rancid notes when oxidation is uncontrolled. By managing salt concentration, temperature, oxygen availability, fermentation duration, and starter composition, producers can steer these pathways toward desirable flavor profiles while limiting off-flavor and safety risks. Texture changes in fermented fish

In addition to flavor, fermentation substantially alters the texture of fish muscle. Textural attributes, including firmness, tenderness, cohesiveness, and gelation, are critical to consumer acceptance and vary widely among fermented fish products, from soft, spreadable pastes to firm, sliceable fillets or sausages. These changes are primarily governed by two opposing mechanisms: enzymatic proteolysis, which degrades muscle structure and softens the product, and acid-induced denaturation and gelation, which can increase firmness through protein coagulation. The balance between these softening and firming mechanisms ultimately determines the final mouthfeel of fermented fish.

Protein hydrolysis-induced softening

A major mechanism of texture change is proteolysis by endogenous fish proteases and microbial proteases, which progressively degrade muscle fibers and connective tissue. As proteins are cleaved into smaller fragments, the muscle structure loosens and the product becomes more tender. In high-salt fermentations such as fish sauce production, proteolysis may become so extensive over time that fish flesh disintegrates into a liquid or paste as collagen and muscle proteins are extensively hydrolyzed. Even in less extreme products, partial proteolysis reduces toughness and produces a softer, more tender texture. For example, lightly fermented Nordic products such as rakfisk undergo relatively mild proteolysis and retain a tender solid texture, whereas prolonged Southeast Asian fish sauce fermentation involves extensive proteolysis over months, leaving little intact tissue. Proteolysis can also increase the pool of water-binding compounds, including peptides and amino acids, and, at advanced stages, ammonia, which may alter moisture distribution in the product. Excessive proteolysis, however, can produce an overly wet or mushy texture and undesirable odors. Therefore, traditional producers regulate salt level, fermentation time, and temperature to control proteolytic activity and prevent over-softening. Within practical limits, higher salt concentrations and shorter fermentation times tend to slow proteolysis and help retain firmness, whereas lower salt concentrations or prolonged fermentation may accelerate degradation and increase the risk of a mushy texture. Controlled proteolysis is beneficial for tenderization and juiciness, but excessive proteolysis can compromise texture quality.

Acid-induced gelation and firming

In contrast, acidification by fermentative microorganisms, especially LAB, can counteract softening by inducing protein denaturation and gelation. Lactic acid produced by LAB gradually lowers the pH of the fish matrix. When the local pH approaches the isoelectric point of muscle proteins, protein solubility decreases and coagulation begins, forming a gel network analogous to curd formation in other acidified protein systems. In products that remain solid rather than being fully liquefied by proteolysis, acid-induced protein gelation increases firmness and cohesiveness. For example, fermentation of fish sausages with LAB can promote the coagulation of myofibrillar proteins, such as myosin, resulting in a springy and sliceable texture. In one study, LAB inoculation in traditional mandarin fish fermentation promoted extensive protein cross-linking, including disulfide-bond formation, and produced a harder and more elastic gel texture than the uninoculated control. These examples indicate that modifying microbial composition, for example by adding LAB starter cultures, can shift texture development toward greater firmness. In addition, some LAB strains produce exopolysaccharides (EPS), which can thicken the matrix and improve water retention, further supporting a cohesive, moist, and firm texture in products such as fermented fish sausages. EPS may fill the protein network and bind water, thereby reducing dryness or crumbliness and improving resilience.

Balancing softening and firming mechanisms

Because proteolysis-driven softening and acid-induced firming occur simultaneously, although to different extents across product types, achieving the desired texture requires balancing these opposing processes. Partial proteolysis is beneficial because it tenderizes fish muscle and releases peptides that may improve succulence, but it must be controlled to avoid excessive degradation. Fermentation outcomes can range from mildly softened products, such as lightly fermented rakfisk, to nearly liquefied products, such as long-fermented fish sauce. Traditional producers adjust salt concentration, fermentation time, and temperature to obtain the desired degree of breakdown for each product style. Some processes deliberately limit protein degradation to achieve a semi-solid or paste-like texture; for example, certain Asian fish pastes are fermented under controlled high-salt conditions and for defined durations to prevent excessive proteolysis and maintain paste consistency. Conversely, LAB co-fermentation can be used to promote uniform gel formation when a firmer texture is desired. In fermented fish sausage, LAB-containing starter cultures can support mild proteolysis, acidification, and EPS production, thereby creating a cohesive protein gel network that reduces textural defects, such as crumbling or voids, and improves springiness. In modern practice, adjusting salt level, fermentation duration, and starter composition provides a means of steering texture toward either softer, spreadable forms or firmer, sliceable products, depending on consumer preference and product requirements.

In summary, fermentation can tenderize fish flesh, induce protein gelation, or generate both effects, depending on product type and processing conditions. Application of omics and AI in fermented fish quality research

Omics and AI insights into flavor development

Advances in analytical chemistry and multi-omics have enabled more detailed profiling of flavor compounds in fermented fish, an area sometimes described as flavoromics. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS), often combined with solid-phase microextraction, allows researchers to identify numerous volatile compounds in each sample, including trace compounds with high sensory impact. Such methods have been used to detect low-odor-threshold sulfur compounds and volatile amines and to link them with specific aroma notes in fish sauce. Liquid chromatography, including HPLC and UHPLC, is used to quantify non-volatile taste compounds, such as FAAs, organic acids, and nucleotides, thereby generating chemical fingerprints related to umami and overall taste. Emerging approaches such as gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) provide rapid volatile profiling and may support near-real-time quality assessment. When combined with chemometric analysis, these targeted and untargeted methods enable researchers and producers to correlate chemical markers with sensory attributes, such as stronger fishy notes associated with trimethylamine or increased umami intensity associated with IMP.

Untargeted metabolomics further expands the detectable range of flavor-related metabolites in fermented fish. Recent studies have combined untargeted metabolomics with sensory evaluation to identify compounds that drive flavor perception. For example, Chen et al. (2023) combined untargeted metabolomic profiling with targeted taste analysis to identify amino acids that play important roles in taste formation in traditional fermented golden pompano, providing insight into metabolic pathways associated with taste development. Another approach, termed two-dimensional flavoromics, integrates comprehensive volatile analysis, such as two-dimensional GC–MS, with 16S rRNA gene sequencing to infer which microorganisms may contribute to aroma compound formation. By correlating microbial abundance with changes in specific volatile compounds, researchers can link microbial community dynamics with flavor development, for example by identifying bacteria associated with increased ester production or off-odor amine accumulation during fermentation.

Multi-omics integration provides a further layer of mechanistic interpretation by linking microbial genes and enzymes with flavor outcomes. Zhang, Shao, et al. (2025), for example, used a multi-omics approach combining metagenomics, metabolomics, and other datasets to examine microbial community dynamics and key flavor metabolite profiles during fish fermentation. Similarly, Han, Kong, Jiang, et al. (2023) linked branched-chain amino acid metabolism in fish sauce fermentation, including leucine and isoleucine pathways, to the formation of corresponding branched-chain aldehydes. Such studies demonstrate that multi-omics integration can generate testable microorganism–enzyme–metabolite hypotheses, although causal confirmation still requires experimental validation beyond correlation.

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning (ML), is increasingly used to extract predictive information from high-dimensional fermentation datasets. Models such as random forests and support vector machines can be trained using multi-omics data combined with sensory measurements to identify associations between microbial or chemical fingerprints and specific flavor qualities. These models do not necessarily prove causality, but they can reveal predictive relationships and help prioritize microbial, enzymatic, or process variables for further experimental testing.

Recent flavoromics studies increasingly use ensemble learning methods, including random forests and gradient boosting, to monitor fermentation progress and identify volatile markers associated with sensory perception. Wang et al. (2025) combined E-nose responses with GC–MS and GC-IMS fingerprints and applied a random forest model to screen and rank aroma-relevant compounds in yeast-fermented raspberry wines. In a related system, Cardinal et al. (2020) showed that random forests can link multivariate volatile profiles to sensory odor descriptors in fish-derived hydrolysates. Operationally, AI/ML models in fermented fish research should be defined by explicit input-output pairs. Potential input variables include microbial relative abundance or metagenomic features, enzyme activities or enzyme-related genes/transcripts/proteins, pH, salt concentration, water activity, moisture, temperature, fermentation time, and metabolomic, volatilomic, E-nose, or E-tongue fingerprints. The corresponding outputs may include aroma intensity, umami or overall taste scores, instrumental texture parameters, biogenic amine or spoilage-risk levels, and fermentation endpoint classification. However, current applications remain constrained by small datasets, batch-to-batch variability, matrix heterogeneity, and limited cross-product generalizability. Therefore, AI/ML approaches should be used as supportive tools for multi-omics integration, marker discovery, hypothesis generation, and process monitoring rather than as a substitute for mechanism-oriented experiments such as controlled inoculation, enzyme assays, isotope tracing, or targeted intervention studies.

Monitoring and predicting texture evolution

Modern analytical tools and multi-omics techniques are also improving the objectivity and predictive capacity of texture monitoring in fermented fish. Texture profile analysis (TPA) using a texture analyzer provides quantitative measurements of hardness, cohesiveness, springiness, and related parameters in fermented fish flesh or paste. These instrumental measurements offer objective information on the rate and extent of softening or firming during fermentation. Rheological tests using rheometers can further track viscoelastic properties of the fermenting fish matrix, indicating changes in gel strength, viscosity, and structural integrity. These physical measurements can be correlated with biochemical changes to improve understanding of texture development.

Proteomic analyses provide insight into the molecular basis of texture change. Proteomics can monitor muscle protein degradation in parallel with instrumental texture measurements. For example, SDS-PAGE or mass-spectrometry-based proteomics can reveal the disappearance of high-molecular-weight muscle proteins, such as myosin heavy chain and collagen, and the appearance of peptide fragments during fermentation. In a study on dry-fermented grass carp, SDS-PAGE showed progressive degradation of major muscle proteins during fermentation. When combined with metaproteomic or metagenomic information on microbial proteases, these data can help identify enzymes that contribute most strongly to texture modification. Zhao et al. (2022) showed that the proteomic profile of fermented grass carp was correlated with textural outcomes and reported significant associations between dominant bacterial genera, peptidase-related genes, and texture-related protein degradation patterns. These findings suggest that microbial activity contributes substantially to proteolysis and final texture formation.

In practice, coupling real-time sensor data with predictive models could support intelligent texture control, but this should be framed as an emerging possibility rather than established practice in fermented fish production. Digital twin technology creates a virtual representation of the fermentation process that runs in parallel with the physical system and can support simulation and prediction. For fermented fish, such a model would need to integrate pH, temperature, moisture, salt content, water activity, spectroscopic readings, peptide formation, and microbial activity before it could reliably forecast texture-related changes. If validated, the model could flag risks such as excessive firming during rapid acidification or over-softening during intense proteolysis and support corrective decisions such as cooling, shortening fermentation, or adjusting starter composition.

Digital twin applications in food fermentation are moving from conceptual discussion toward practical demonstration in selected systems, but direct evidence in fermented fish remains limited. Studies in kombucha, amasi, and other fermentations suggest transferable workflows in which IoT sensing, computer vision, and ML regressors support real-time prediction of process variables (Adeleke et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2025). For fermented fish, these approaches should be treated as transferable frameworks that require validation under high-salt, protein-rich, heterogeneous matrices before model predictive control or closed-loop optimization can be recommended for industrial use.

These tools may transform texture assessment from an empirical practice based largely on experience into a more quantitative and predictive process, but only if they are anchored to validated biochemical and microbial markers. Producers may define a target texture endpoint, such as a spreadable paste or a firm, chewy product, and use data-driven fermentation management to improve reproducibility. In summary, multi-omics measurements, instrumental texture analysis, and AI-based models provide complementary tools for monitoring texture evolution in fermented fish. By linking texture changes with their molecular and microbial drivers, these approaches can support more consistent production of traditional fermented fish products with desired mouthfeel.

Food safety and quality control

The same substrate-conversion pathways that generate desirable flavor and texture can also produce hazardous metabolites when fermentation is poorly controlled. Safety control in fermented fish should therefore be treated as part of microbial–enzymatic regulation rather than as a separate post-processing concern. The protein-rich, amino-acid-rich, high-salt, and often anaerobic fermentation environment can favor hazards such as biogenic amines (BAs), N-nitrosamines, pathogenic microorganisms, and toxin producers if preservation hurdles are insufficient. Traditional salting and acidification help control many risks, but an integrated strategy combining validated fermentation practices with modern monitoring tools is needed to support both safety and quality.

Biogenic amines (BAs)

BAs, including histamine, tyramine, putrescine, and cadaverine, are formed through microbial decarboxylation of amino acids and can accumulate to hazardous levels in fermented fish if not properly controlled (Rabie et al., 2011). Histamine is of particular concern because excessive intake can cause scombroid, or histamine, poisoning, and fish species naturally rich in free histidine, such as mackerel and tuna, are especially prone to histamine accumulation during fermentation (Vasconcelos et al., 2021). Regulatory limits for histamine in fish sauce and related products in many jurisdictions further underscore the importance of controlling BA levels to ensure fermented fish safety and quality (Gao et al., 2023).

BA accumulation can be mitigated by optimizing fermentation parameters, such as salt content and temperature, to suppress BA-producing microorganisms, and by adjusting ripening time to limit net BA formation. One targeted modern strategy is the use of selected starter cultures that do not produce BAs. For example, certain lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and staphylococcal strains are selected because they lack amino acid decarboxylases and therefore do not synthesize histamine or related amines (Lorenzo et al., 2017). Some beneficial strains may also possess amine oxidase activity and can degrade BAs; inoculation with such starters has been reported to substantially reduce histamine accumulation in fish fermentation trials (Yang et al., 2025). Moreover, defined mixed starters combining LAB with halotolerant cocci, such as Staphylococcus or Tetragenococcus, can help establish a desirable fermentative microbiota that outcompetes wild BA-producing bacteria and other potential toxin-producing microorganisms (Hu et al., 2024).

N-nitrosamines

N-nitrosamines are not generally expected at high levels in fermented fish because nitrite is not a typical ingredient. However, they may occur at low levels when nitrite is introduced inadvertently, for example through nitrite-contaminated salt, nitrate-containing additives, or microbial reduction of nitrate during curing. Under such conditions, secondary amines derived from fish constituents, including dimethylamine generated from trimethylamine N-oxide degradation, can undergo nitrosation in acidic matrices, while subsequent heating or drying steps may further favor nitrosamine formation. Trace N-nitrosodimethylamine (NDMA) and related compounds have been reported in some traditional fermented fish products, highlighting the need for preventive measures (Guo et al., 2025; Li, Li, Bi, & Liu, 2024).

Risk management therefore focuses on maintaining low nitrite levels throughout processing. Practical options include avoiding exogenous nitrite, selecting starter cultures that lack nitrate reductase activity or can remove nitrite, and managing fermentation conditions to limit nitrate reduction (Liao et al., 2018). In addition, antinitrosating agents, such as ascorbate and selected polyphenols, can inhibit nitrosation, although their use should be balanced against sensory quality and product authenticity (Guo et al., 2025).

Pathogenic microorganisms and toxins

Fermented fish is generally protected by multiple preservation hurdles, including high salt concentration, low pH, reduced water activity, and competitive activity of fermentative microorganisms. Nevertheless, certain pathogens and toxin producers may still pose risks when these hurdles are insufficient. Clostridium botulinum is a particular concern because its spores can survive salting, and if the product is insufficiently salted or acidified, the spores may germinate and produce botulinum neurotoxin; rare outbreaks of botulism have been associated with inadequately fermented fish. Listeria monocytogenes may also contaminate fermented fish, particularly during unhygienic post-fermentation handling. LAB-dominated fermentations can inhibit Listeria through acidification and bacteriocin production (Yang et al., 2024), but Listeria has occasionally been detected when fermentation conditions are suboptimal or when contamination occurs after fermentation. Certain halophilic bacteria present in fish sauce, including some Bacillus spp., may also produce heat-stable toxins if their growth is not adequately controlled. In addition, molds, such as Aspergillus species, can grow on the surface of dried fermented fish products and produce mycotoxins such as aflatoxins, although repeated drying, smoking, or high salt usually limits mold proliferation (Pop et al., 2024).

Strict control measures are required to minimize pathogenic and toxin-related hazards in fermented fish. In practice, safety is supported by multiple hurdles, including high salt content, reduced water activity, acidification when carbohydrate fermentation occurs, competitive dominance of beneficial fermentative microbiota or selected starter cultures, and hygienic handling throughout processing. Because inhibitory thresholds depend on the product matrix, process conditions, and target microorganisms, producers generally define product- and region-specific targets for salt content, water activity (aw), and pH, and verify them through routine monitoring and hazard analysis. Some commercially produced liquid fermented fish condiments may undergo post-fermentation heat treatment or filtration to reduce microbial loads, depending on product type and local manufacturing practice (Keisam et al., 2019). The intentional use of beneficial microorganisms, such as LAB or safe staphylococci as starter cultures, also contributes to safety by outcompeting or suppressing potential pathogens through acid production and bacteriocin formation.

In recent years, data-driven technologies have been introduced to strengthen food safety surveillance during fermentation. Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses can serve as early-warning tools by detecting the presence or expression of genes associated with toxin production or hazardous metabolite formation (Chandross-Cohen et al., 2025). For example, enrichment of histidine decarboxylase genes or transcripts could indicate increased risk of histamine accumulation before the hazard becomes evident chemically. However, genomic signals should be interpreted as risk indicators rather than direct proof of toxin formation and should be combined with chemical testing, process monitoring, and microbiological verification.

Sensing technologies are also being explored for fermentation monitoring. Electronic nose (E-nose) devices can detect volatile organic compound patterns associated with spoilage or abnormal fermentation, such as increasing ammonia or trimethylamine signals (Vanaraj et al., 2025). Biosensors, including enzyme-based electrochemical histamine sensors, provide another route for near-real-time chemical monitoring (Ahangari et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2024). These tools may support timely interventions, but their reliability depends on calibration against fermented fish matrices, validation across production conditions, and integration with conventional safety testing.

In practice, effective quality control in fermented fish depends on integrating traditional hurdles with modern verification tools. Salt, acidity, reduced water activity, controlled fermentation, and hygienic handling remain the baseline defenses, while omics, sensors, and AI-based models should be positioned as complementary tools for earlier warning, better traceability, and more consistent decision-making. Conclusions and future trends

Summary of findings

In summary, fermented fish quality is best understood as the outcome of regulated microbial–enzymatic substrate conversion. Endogenous enzymes initiate the release of peptides, amino acids, and fatty acids, whereas fermentative microorganisms further transform these substrates into sensory-active metabolites or, under poorly controlled conditions, safety-relevant hazards such as biogenic amines. The most robust conclusion is that flavor, texture, and safety cannot be managed independently; they arise from the same interacting pathways and must therefore be controlled through integrated management of salt level, pH, temperature, fermentation time, starter composition, and substrate availability.

Multi-omics, sensor technologies, and AI-based models offer promising tools for mapping and predicting these interactions, but their application in fermented fish remains uneven and requires validation under real production conditions. At present, these technologies are strongest as tools for mechanism mapping, marker discovery, prediction, and early-warning support, rather than as fully established autonomous control systems for traditional fermented fish production.

Future directions

Several future directions and remaining challenges can be identified.

Development of intelligent “smart fermentation” systems that integrate multi-omics data, sensor outputs, and AI-based models for monitoring and decision support, with emphasis on product-specific validation before automated control is claimed.

Application of precision fermentation strategies, such as selected starter cultures or targeted enzyme additions, to fine-tune fermentation performance and improve product consistency while preserving traditional flavor profiles; genetically engineered starters should be discussed cautiously because regulatory acceptance and cultural authenticity may vary across markets.

Implementation of low-salt fermentation methods through validated multi-hurdle designs that combine protective starters, pH and water-activity control, temperature management, and hazard monitoring, because sodium reduction may weaken microbial safety barriers if not properly compensated.

At the same time, several challenges and knowledge gaps remain.

•Cost and operational complexity: Multi-omics analyses remain expensive and technically demanding, which limits their routine application in industrial fermented fish production.

Ultimately, the modernization of fermented fish should proceed through evidence-based integration rather than technology substitution. As multi-omics, sensors, and AI/ML tools mature, become more accessible, and are validated under practical production conditions, they may help improve safety, consistency, and process traceability while preserving the distinctive identity of traditional products. The central challenge is therefore not simply to introduce advanced technologies, but to use them to test, monitor, and control the microbial–enzymatic pathways that define fermented fish quality.
