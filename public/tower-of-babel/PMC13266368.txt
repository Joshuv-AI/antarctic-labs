Congenital Genitourinary Anomaly Identification and Referral in Rural Nepal: A Qualitative Study on the Perspective of Female Community Health Volunteers
Authors: Prajjwol Luitel, Sujan Paudel, Amit Yadav, Dinesh Prasad Koirala, Sarah B Cairo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDelayed presentation of congenital anomalies is common in Nepal, highlighting the importance of timely diagnosis. Female community health volunteers (FCHVs), who are integral to health care delivery in communities, may offer an opportunity for early identification and referral of these anomalies. This study explored the perceptions, willingness, and motivations of FCHVs in Nepal regarding their potential role in the identification of congenital external genitourinary (GU) anomalies.MethodsTwelve FCHVs from Devdaha municipality, Rupandehi district, Nepal, participated in a focus group discussion (FGD). FCHVs were purposively sampled to ensure diversity in geographic setting (urban/rural), age, and years of experience. A semi‐structured FGD guide was used to explore knowledge, attitudes, perceived community acceptance, and recommendations for future training. Data was analyzed using inductive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's framework.ResultsFive key themes emerged: (1) lack of confidence in identifying and refering congenital GU anomalies, (2) positive attitude toward community‐based screening, (3) perceived barriers (e.g., stigma and parental resistance) and facilitators (e.g., existing child health roles), (4) recommendations for practical, skill‐based training, and (5) motivation for FCHVs' work. While FCHVs recognized the importance of identifying congenital anomalies and associated risks or complications, most lacked confidence due to limited prior training. Most FCHVs expressed willingness to participate in screening, particularly when they perceived the role as aligned with their existing responsibilities. There was consensus on the need for targeted training before implementing a formal program.ConclusionThis study provides early evidence on the feasibility of integrating congenital anomaly screening into community health systems, highlighting training and referral strengthening as next potential steps.Keywords: community health workers, congenital anomalies, female community health volunteers, Nepal, screening

Introduction

Birth defects represent a leading cause of neonatal and under‐5 mortality, responsible for nearly 30% of under‐5 deaths [1]. Each year, an estimated 8 million newborns are affected by birth defects, with 240,000 neonatal deaths [1]. Over 90% of this burden falls on low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [2, 3]. The most common contributors are congenital heart anomalies and neural tube defects, which together account for more than half of neonatal deaths [4]. Anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract represent a significant challenge, particularly in regions with limited resources [5].

Nepal, a landlocked South Asian country situated between India and China, is an LMIC with limited healthcare resources and a huge unmet need in surgery [6]. In Nepal, community surveys report a prevalence of congenital anomalies of 17 per 10,000 children [7], and hospital data show an incidence of 9.42 per 1000 live births annually, with genitourinary (GU) conditions being the most common (25.9%) [8]. Despite this burden, Nepal lacks a routine program for congenital anomaly detection or surveillance, leading to delayed presentations and complications. The challenge is compounded by the hidden nature of GU anomalies and the cultural stigma surrounding them, particularly in rural communities. Unlike externally visible anomalies such as cleft lip or limb deformities, GU conditions often go unnoticed without targeted screening. A holistic approach is therefore needed to address these anomalies, including improved newborn screening and early detection, and surveillance systems [9].

In recent years, delegating responsibilities to community health workers (CHWs) has been promoted as a response to the shortage of qualified healthcare professionals and to expand access to timely services in LMICs [10, 11]. WHO has recommended the mobilization of layperson responders in LMICs, as a strategy to creating a robust pre‐hospital emergency response system [12].

Female Community Health Volunteers (FCHVs) have a foundational role in Nepal's primary health care system, often bridging the last mile of health disconnectivity. Established by the Government of Nepal in 1988, the program now includes over 51,000 active volunteers [13] and is internationally recognized as a successful model of community‐based health service delivery [14]. FCHVs are selected from women who are permanent residents of their communities, between 25 and 45 years of age, and undergo 18 days of training on basic health conditions before enrollment. Their primary responsibilities include promoting safe motherhood, child health, and family planning services [15, 16]. The working domain for FCHVs has recently expanded to various non‐communicable diseases, but has never been explored in surgical conditions [17]. Globally, there is limited research on the role of CHWs in congenital anomaly identification and referral [18]. This study aimed to explore FCHVs' perspectives on congenital external GU anomalies and their willingness and motivations to participate in community‐level identification and referral.

Methods

Design

This qualitative study employed focus group discussion (FGD) methodology to capture FCHVs' perspectives on screening for congenital GU anomalies.

Study Site

The study was conducted in the Devdaha municipality, Rupandehi district, in Southern Nepal. This district was chosen due to a high prevalence of congenital GU anomalies [19]. The municipality was chosen following consultation with the Rupandehi District Public Health Office, Nepal.

FCHV Selection

FCHVs were recruited purposively in collaboration with the Devdaha Municipal Public Health Office to ensure diversity in setting (urban and rural), age, and years of work as FCHV [20]. Of 97 active FCHVs (as of 2024) in the municipality, 15 were approached, and 12 agreed to participate (response rate 80%) [20]. Recruitment took place during community health meetings and site visits; potential FCHVs were approached in person. FCHVs who met the inclusion criteria were informed about the study's purpose and invited to participate voluntarily.

The inclusion criteria for FCHVs were: (i) actively working as an FCHV in the study site and (ii) voluntarily agreeing to participate. Data saturation was determined during the latter part of the FGD when responses became repetitive and reinforced previously identified ideas, and continued probing and follow‐up questions did not generate new perspectives, or ideas. Immediately after the session, the moderator (SP) and note‐taker (PL) reviewed the themes and confirmed that no new ideas had arisen, indicating that thematic saturation had been achieved within this relatively homogenous participant group. Since the study had a clear focus and participants were similar in terms of training, responsibilities, and community context, one FGD with 12 participants was considered sufficient to address the study objectives, an approach aligning with established qualitative research standards suggesting that thematic saturation can be achieved within relatively small, homogenous samples when the research question is focused [21].

Procedure

The FGD was conducted in person in Nepali by a trained moderator (SP), lasting approximately 2 h, following standard guidelines [22, 23, 24]. A literature review and research objectives were used to inform the development of the FGD guide (Supporting File A). The moderator facilitated structured yet flexible discussions, allowing for exploration of FCHVs' experiences. Questions were open‐ended, culturally appropriate, and designed to gather comparable data while permitting variations in wording and probing techniques [25].

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee of the Institute of Medicine, Nepal. All FCHVs who agreed to participate provided written informed consent. FCHVs were also informed that the discussions would be digitally recorded, that the recordings would be used only for study purposes, and deleted from all digital archives upon the study's completion.

Data Analysis

To ensure credibility, transcripts were coded and reviewed independently by two authors (PL and SP) and cross‐checked with detailed notes. Disputes were resolved through discussion. Reliability was supported by keeping records of the process, and confirmability through independent coding and input from external reviewers.

Thematic content analysis (TCA) was conducted using an inductive approach, where codes and themes were derived from the data without a predefined coding framework. This approach allowed patterns and meanings to emerge from participants' responses. Analysis followed Braun and Clarke's six‐phase framework: (1) familiarization through transcription and repeated readings; (2) independent code generation; (3) identification of themes; (4) iterative theme review; (5) defining and naming themes; and (6) producing the final report with illustrative participant quotes [23, 26]. A codebook documenting themes and codes was developed (Supporting File B).

The FGD was digitally recorded, transcribed, and translated into English by a bilingual translator, with verification by a second bilingual author. Notes from the discussion were incorporated into the transcripts. NVivo 11 software was used for the organization of codes, while final thematic categorization was completed through iterative team discussions. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and socio‐economic characteristics; means and standard deviations (SD) for normal distribution, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for non‐normal distribution, and counts and percentages for categorical variables.

Results

FCHV Characteristics

Twelve FCHVs participated, seven from urban and five from rural areas. Their mean age was 36.6 years (SD = 7.9), ranging from 25 to 49 years. The mean duration of work as an FCHV was 11.8 years (SD = 4.8), ranging from 3 to 21 years. Half of the FCHVs (50.0%) had completed high school education (Grade 9–10), and the majority were Hindu (83.3%) (Table 1).

The findings are presented based on a discussion guide, and verbatim quotes de‐identified from FCHVs to support themes that emerged during TCA. The conceptual framework illustrates how individual (confidence and motivation), system‐level (training and referral pathways), and socio‐cultural factors (stigma and community acceptance) interact to influence feasibility (Figure 1).

Conceptual framework illustrating the interrelationship between themes influencing the feasibility of integrating congenital genitourinary anomaly screening into the existing FCHV framework.

This framework provides a basis for intervention design by identifying key leverage points across individual, community, and health system levels. For example, lack of confidence and knowledge among FCHVs highlights the need for structured, skill‐based training, while community‐level barriers such as stigma and parental resistance indicate the importance of culturally sensitive awareness and engagement strategies. At the system level, the framework underscores the necessity of clear referral pathways and supervision to translate identification into timely care.

The five main themes are:

Theme 1: Lack of Confidence in Identifying and Refering Congenital GU Anomalies

FCHVs reported encountering congenital GU anomalies during community work but expressed uncertainty in identifying and refering these conditions. They struggled to distinguish normal from abnormal structures and were hesitant due to fear of misdiagnosis. This was reinforced by the absence of formal training on congenital anomalies in their initial onboarding training. Some believed these conditions were untreatable or would persist in adulthood.

During one field visit, a parent approached me asking if their boy had both testes in the scrotum…. I tried feeling it, but I was not sure whether the testes were there or not… they are really small, you know.

– rural FCHV

Theme 2: Positive Attitude Toward Community‐Based Screening

FCHVs expressed mixed but overall positive attitude toward community‐based screening. While some felt it was outside their scope of work, some viewed it as complementary to their existing child health work. Many were motivated by the desire to help children and families, particularly those who had been neglected due to stigma, faced social discrimination, and expressed readiness to take on this role if provided with training.

It would be helpful if we could help those children and their parents in any way possible. I have seen families neglect a child with a congenital anomaly, seeking treatment only when foreign doctors came for a camp, thinking they wouldn't consider it a taboo.

– rural FCHV

Theme 3: Perceived Barriers and Facilitators

Several barriers to effective identification and referral were discussed, including parental resistance, cultural stigma, superstition, and child anxiety during examinations. Resistance was anticipated from educated parents, who sometimes doubted FCHV's competencies. FCHVs emphasized that their prior experience in maternal and child health could facilitate their role in congenital anomaly screening, provided they received guidance and support.

Parents might deny the examination, especially the educated ones, since they do not listen to FCHVs.

– urban FCHV

Theme 4: Recommendations for Practical, Skill‐Based Training

FCHVs emphasized the importance of skill‐based, visual, and interactive training adapted to their educational backgrounds. They further highlighted the need for schedule accommodating domestic responsibilities and shorter sessions to reduce information fatigue, and recommended refresher training to reinforce skills. Practical demonstrations were considered more effective than didactic lectures.

…we forget knowledge, but not the skills. If you give me knowledge, it's for a day, but if you give me skill, it's for a lifetime.

– urban FCHV

Theme 5: Motivation for FCHVs' Work

FCHVs emphasized intrinsic motivation as a core to their work, particularly the respect and gratitude they receive from their communities. Financial compensation was not the primary driver, although material support such as uniforms, allowances, or meeting stipends were welcomed. These were seen as reinforcing their identity and sustaining long‐term engagement.

The respect and gratitude from communities motivate us the most.

– urban FCHV

Discussion

This is the first study to explore the role of FCHVs in identifying and referring congenital GU anomalies, an area where evidence on CHWs remains scarce [7]. It shows that FCHVs have some awareness of these conditions and expressed a willingness to participate in the screening and referral, regardless of incentives. They were intrinsically motivated to support screening; however, improving their confidence and technical knowledge through structured training is necessary to enhance their engagement and effectiveness.

The barriers identified in this study are rooted in deeper sociocultural dynamics. Cultural stigma surrounding genital conditions, particularly in conservative rural settings stems from pervasive norms around purity and bodily shame, where conditions affecting genitalia are perceived as reflecting spiritual or moral defect. Gender dynamics further complicates this, as FCHVs, being women, may feel hesitant to examine or discuss sensitive conditions with families. In addition, perceptions of authority and knowledge hierarchies shape trust, with educated families, questioning the competence of FCHVs. Any training program must therefore address not just clinical skills but tailored communication strategies. These dynamics reflect broader patterns observed in LMIC health systems, where community‐based providers must navigate complex social expectations alongside community health responsibilities.

FCHVs play a crucial role in delivering healthcare because they share cultural norms, beliefs, and health‐seeking behaviors with their communities [27]. Their responsibilities include linking patients to health facilities and educating families aligning with local practices [28]. Their ability to communicate effectively with patients and providers, respect community norms, and share habits fosters empathy and trust [29, 30]. However, even for FCHVs, recognizing and referring congenital GU anomalies can be challenging. Addressing these issues requires culturally sensitive approaches, including respectful counseling, same‐gender communication, involvement of religious leaders, and coordinated efforts.

Targeted interventions are needed to train FCHVs and raise awareness about congenital anomalies, creating a community safety net for counseling and referral that currently does not exist [30]. Successful models of the Smile Train program, which has enabled free cleft lip and palate surgeries worldwide, demonstrate how empowering local health workers can ensure sustainable surgical care [31]. These experiences suggest that Nepal would need community engagement strategies and strong referral linkages to make FCHV‐based congenital anomaly screening effective.

The finding that FCHVs were intrinsically motivated driven by community respect, and a sense of responsibility is one of the most policy‐relevant findings of this study. This aligns with prior evidence from other LMIC volunteer health worker programs [17, 32]. Therefore, expanding FHCV responsibilities into congenital anomaly screening does not necessarily need new financial incentive structures. However, intrinsic motivation alone without structured training is fragile [33].

Prior global efforts in this field include India's Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram, a nationwide initiative aimed at identifying, referring, and treating children with birth defects in a resource‐limited setting [18]. The program trained CHWs, known as ASHAs, to recognize children with birth defects, disabilities, diseases, and developmental delays in communities, and was able to identify affected children through a structured training and step‐by‐step referral system. A similar system, where FCHVs identify potential cases and refer them to mid‐level health workers (e.g., medical officers and general practitioners), could be adapted for Nepal. Likewise, a team from Latin America developed an innovative mobile app for surveillance of 98 congenital anomalies [34]. Given that FCHVs in our study reported low confidence in recognizing GU anomalies, similar digital tools with visual guides, decision trees, and referral prompts could enhance screening and close knowledge gaps, as emphasized in prior work from Nepal [35].

While the five themes have been presented separately, they are interconnected. Low confidence (Theme 1) is enforced by anticipated community resistance (Theme 3) and lack of institutional support. Positive attitudes towards screening (Theme 2) and strong instrinsic motivation create a foundation, but they cannot translate into action without skill‐based training (Theme 4). This interdependence has a direct implication for future program design [36].

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is its originality, given the limited research in this area. The qualitative design captured the experiences, perspectives, motivations, and insights of FCHVs. Direct quotes from participants were used to strengthen the validity of the findings.

The absence of prior research made external comparisons and transferability difficult. Given the role of the first author as a physician with an interest in expanding screening for congenital GU anomalies, efforts were made to minimize the influence of the author's knowledge on participants' responses. Efforts were made to minimize this, such as using open‐ended, non‐leading questions and triangulating findings with co‐moderates and independent coders. The use of a single FGD may limit the breadth of perspectives captured. Another potential limitation is translation bias, as interviews conducted in the local language were translated into English. However, involvement of a bilingual translator familiar with the context was done to minimize this risk. Future studies should involve multiple sites and a larger sample to facilitate the comparison of perspectives, the effectiveness of training modules for community‐based congenital anomaly screening.

Future Directions

Identification of children with congenital anomalies by FCHVs is the first step; the most critical component is ensuring access of identified children to timely treatment. This study represents the first phase of a larger project, “FCHVs Led Congenital External Genitourinary Anomalies Identification and Referral: A Pilot Assessment in Rural Nepal.” The subsequent phase will involve developing and conducting training for FCHVs, from the recommendations of this study. The final phase will pilot community‐level screening by trained FCHVs, and referral linkages to definitive surgical care. The results thus obtained has potential for informing the local and national policies on the effectiveness of integrating congenital GU screening into existing FCHV responsibilities.

Existing global models of task‐shifting and task‐sharing, where responsibilities are delegated to non‐physician healthcare providers, have shown promise in addressing workforce shortages and improving access in LMICs. Future research should focus on pilot implementation and evaluation of training programs, including assessment of diagnostic accuracy, referral completion rates, and health system integration before considering scale‐up [37, 38]. In addition, studies should account for parents' perspectives and definitive care providers to gain a comprehensive understanding.

Conclusions

This study advances understanding of community‐based congenital anomaly detection in Nepal by reporting that FCHVs have positive attitude and instrinsic motivation to participate in identification and referral. It supports integrating congenital genitourinary anomaly screening into existing FCHV framework rather than building parallel system. Further steps include a short, visual, interactive training for FCHVs and establishment of supervision and referral pathways.
