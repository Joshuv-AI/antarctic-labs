A case of acute focal bacterial nephritis diagnosed by contrast-enhanced ultrasound
Authors: Hiraku Misawa, Noriaki Manabe, Emiko Bukeo, Takako Etani, Jun Nakamura, Minoru Fujita, Ken Haruma, Katsuya Kato, Tomoki Yamatsuji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute focal bacterial nephritis is a bacterial infection confined to the renal parenchyma, and contrast-enhanced computed tomography is typically recommended for its diagnosis. Recently, contrast-enhanced ultrasonography has garnered attention as a useful alternative owing to its minimally invasive nature and lack of contrast-induced nephrotoxicity. We report the case of a patient who initially presented with right lower abdominal pain, suggestive of acute appendicitis, and she underwent a transabdominal ultrasound examination. Acute focal bacterial nephritis was suspected on this initial scan, and contrast-enhanced ultrasonography was successfully used to definitively diagnose acute focal bacterial nephritis. Therefore, this technique could be a noninvasive alternative to computed tomography for identifying acute focal bacterial nephritis.Keywords: Acute focal bacterial nephritis, Contrast-enhanced computed tomography, Contrast-enhanced ultrasonography

Introduction

Acute focal bacterial nephritis (AFBN), initially termed acute lobar nephronia, represents a nonpurulent, localized bacterial infection of the renal parenchyma, and is pathologically positioned on the spectrum between acute pyelonephritis and renal abscess [1]. Clinically, AFBN is challenging to differentiate from acute pyelonephritis because symptoms such as fever, flank pain, and chills frequently overlap. Furthermore, the diagnosis of AFBN is often complicated by nonspecific laboratory findings and frequently negative urine cultures, which highlights the essential role of imaging studies in a definitive diagnosis. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) is typically the diagnostic gold standard for AFBN. Computed tomography (CT) shows a characteristic wedge-shaped area of low attenuation with ill-defined margins, which is crucial for distinguishing AFBN from tumors or renal abscesses. However, performing CECT is often contraindicated or challenging in patients with renal impairment or a history of iodine contrast allergy, making diagnosis problematic in these populations [2].

We report a case of an adult female patient with a history of bronchial asthma, precluding the use of iodine contrast, in whom contrast-enhanced ultrasonography (CEUS) was successfully used to achieve a definitive diagnosis of AFBN.

Case report

A woman in her 20s presented to the Emergency Department following referral from her family physician for suspected acute appendicitis. Her symptoms included right lower abdominal pain that had started 1 day previously. Her past medical history was notable for bronchial asthma since the age of 3 years. Her current medications included montelukast sodium 10 mg (one tablet daily), tranilast (three capsules daily), and vilanterol trifenatate/fluticasone furoate inhalation (once daily). Upon admission, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: body temperature of 38.0°C, blood pressure of 102/60 mmHg, and heart rate of 100 beats/min. Her height was 158 cm, and weight was 45.1 kg (body mass index: 18.0 kg/m2). A physical examination showed no palpable superficial lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was flat and soft, with tenderness noted in the right upper quadrant. There was no rebound tenderness, and no organomegaly (liver or spleen) or masses were palpable. Right costovertebral angle percussion tenderness was present.

Laboratory data indicated a systemic inflammatory response (Table 1). The white blood cell count was markedly elevated and showed a shift to the left with neutrophil predominance and associated lymphopenia. The C-reactive protein concentration was also considerably elevated.

An initial abdominal plain CT scan was performed but ruled out the initial suspicion, with no evidence of appendicitis or appendiceal enlargement. Furthermore, no other noteworthy findings that could account for the patient’s abdominal pain were identified (Fig. 1).

Plain abdominal computed tomography images. (A) An axial image shows no evidence of appendiceal enlargement or diverticular inflammation. (B) A coronal image shows no evidence of an underlying organic pathology responsible for the abdominal pain.

A transabdominal ultrasound (US) examination subsequently showed that the right kidney was mildly enlarged, with 10 cm in its longest diameter and an enlarged appearance (Fig. 2A). US also showed mild renal pelvis dilatation and ureteral wall thickening (approximately 3 mm) extending for approximately 1.2 cm from the ureteropelvic junction (Fig. 2B). Critically, a roundish, high echoic area was observed in the upper pole of the right kidney, which raised the suspicion of AFBN (Fig. 2A). To confirm the diagnosis, CEUS was performed on the suspicious area. CEUS showed an ill-defined region with poor contrast enhancement corresponding to the high echoic area observed on B-mode US. This finding definitively confirmed the diagnosis of AFBN (Fig. 2C). Postdiagnosis, a urinalysis showed the presence of leukocytes and bacteria. A subsequent urine culture showed the presence of Escherichia coli. The patient was promptly started on the intravenous antimicrobial agent ceftriaxone sodium hydrate immediately after admission. The clinical course was favorable. The fever resolved on the second day of illness, and the abdominal pain considerably improved by the fifth day. Consequently, the patient was discharged on the sixth day of illness. A follow-up visit was conducted on the 22nd day of illness. Repeated transabdominal US performed at this time showed the complete resolution of the previously noted high echoic area in the right kidney, which indicated successful treatment (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Traditionally, AFBN was primarily recognized as a pediatric disease. However, the present case, which involved a 20-year-old woman, highlights the occurrence of AFBN in younger adults. Recent literature has also shown that the incidence of AFBN is relatively frequent in the adult population, particularly among women [2]. Therefore, clinicians should be aware that AFBN is not limited to children, and it should also be considered in the differential diagnosis for individuals in their 20s, as demonstrated by our case [2,3].

The clinical presentation of AFBN is often challenging because of its nonspecific findings, such as a high fever, flank pain, leukocytosis, and elevated inflammatory markers [4]. Our patient’s presentation, characterized by fever and right lower abdominal pain, along with neutrophil-predominant leukocytosis and elevated inflammatory markers, strongly suggested a bacterial infection. Initially, acute appendicitis was suspected. However, the absence of a localized point of maximal tenderness in the right lower quadrant effectively ruled out this diagnosis. Furthermore, transabdominal US confirmed the exclusion of acute appendicitis by showing that the appendix was a normal size with no signs of inflammation. The presence of right costovertebral angle percussion tenderness was a crucial finding, which suggested that the right kidney was the likely source of inflammation, despite the pain being predominantly reported in the lower abdomen. Nevertheless, all of these clinical and laboratory findings remain nonspecific, making definitively suspecting AFBN on the basis of clinical evidence alone challenging. This diagnostic uncertainty is compounded by microbiological limitations. Although E. coli is the predominant causative organism of AFBN, with detection in up to 83% of positive urine cultures, the overall urine culture positivity rate is often low, and some reports have shown a negative rate as high as 41% [2]. Therefore, an imaging study is crucial for the reliable diagnosis of AFBN and is essential for achieving a definitive diagnosis. CECT is widely regarded as the diagnostic gold standard for AFBN because of its superior sensitivity. On CECT, AFBN typically manifests as a wedge-shaped or irregular area of poor (or decreased) contrast enhancement within the renal parenchyma, notably lacking evidence of liquefaction or mature abscess formation. These characteristic findings are vital for the differential diagnosis of AFBN from conditions such as uncomplicated pyelonephritis, frank renal abscess, and pyonephrosis. Moreover, the presence of cortical rim sign, which indicates the preservation of contrast enhancement in the outermost renal cortex, is considered a key feature for distinguishing AFBN from renal infarction [2,5,6]. However, there are cases in which the use of contrast agents is unadvisable because of the presence of bronchial asthma, which occurred in our case.

On conventional B-mode transabdominal US, AFBN typically presents as an area of focal renal enlargement showing an isoechoic to hyperechoic appearance, sometimes showing a hypoechoic pattern, with focal disruption of corticomedullary differentiation. Color Doppler further shows localized hypoperfusion in the affected segment. However, the diagnostic accuracy of B-mode US and Color Doppler US alone is limited because of the wide variability of the findings. Consequently, conventional transabdominal US currently serves a supplementary role in the diagnostic process for AFBN, and is primarily used for initial screening and ruling out other pathologies rather than a definitive diagnosis [2,4,7].

AFBN is often challenging to diagnose because of its nonspecific clinical and laboratory findings and the limitations of conventional US. While CECT remains the diagnostic gold standard, its use is contraindicated in patients with renal impairment or iodine contrast allergy, such as in this case with a history of bronchial asthma. Our case demonstrates that CEUS provides an effective, non-nephrotoxic alternative for the definitive diagnosis of AFBN in patients who cannot undergo CECT. Therefore, CEUS should be recognized as an important diagnostic tool in the workup of suspected AFBN, particularly when CECT is precluded.

Conclusion

CEUS serves as a critical, non-nephrotoxic diagnostic alternative for patients with suspected AFBN, especially when contrast-enhanced CT is contraindicated because of renal impairment or allergy.
