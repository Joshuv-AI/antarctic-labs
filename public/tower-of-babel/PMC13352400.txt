Mechanically-assisted non-invasive deep inspiration breath-hold (MANIV-DIBH): A first clinical application in paediatric radiotherapy
Authors: N. Audag, D. Di Perri, A. Razavi, S. Da Rita Quaresma, M. Gabriels, M. Alvear-Garcia, E. Alvarez-Moreno, B. Brichard, G. Van Ooteghem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeRespiratory motion is a major challenge in paediatric thoracic radiotherapy, where minimizing exposure to healthy tissues is essential to reduce long-term toxicity. Mechanically-assisted non-invasive ventilation deep inspiration breath-hold (MANIV-DIBH) allows non-sedated patients to achieve repeated, reproducible breath-holds, potentially reducing safety margins and improving dose conformity. We report the first paediatric application of this technique during whole lung irradiation (WLI).Material and methodsA 10-year-old girl with metastatic Ewing sarcoma was referred for WLI. A preparatory session with a physiotherapist was performed to introduce MANIV-DIBH with child-adapted communication and select the optimal non-vented oronasal mask. Ventilator settings for assisted breath-holds were tailored to the patient's tolerance. The goal was to achieve multiple 20-s breath-holds. The simulation CT was acquired during MANIV-DIBH. WLI (15 Gy in 10 fractions) was planned with IMRT.ResultsThe patient performed stable and reproducible 20-s breath-holds across 10 sessions, with an average fraction time of 32.1 (±7.8) minutes. Treatment was well tolerated, with grade 2 fatigue. Follow-up PET/CT showed complete response and no imaging signs of toxicity.ConclusionThis first paediatric clinical application of MANIV-DIBH suggests feasibility, stability, and good tolerability. Its successful use required careful, multidisciplinary implementation and adaptation to the paediatric setting. These preliminary findings support further evaluation in this population.

Introduction

Respiratory motion remains a major challenge in thoracic and upper abdominal radiotherapy, introducing uncertainties in target positioning and dose delivery. The most common strategy to compensate for this motion is the application of large treatment margins. However, increasing margins inevitably enlarges the volume of healthy tissue exposed to radiation, thereby increasing the risk of radiation-induced toxicity [1], [2], [3].

This issue is particularly critical in paediatric radiotherapy. Although long-term survival after childhood cancer now exceeds 80%, survivors face a substantially increased risk of treatment-related morbidity and mortality, including cardiopulmonary complications, late fibrosis, and secondary malignancies [4], [5]. Reducing radiation exposure to organs at risk (OARs) is therefore essential to preserve long-term quality of life and survivorship. Improving treatment precision and reducing planning target volume (PTV) margins are consequently central objectives in paediatric radiation oncology. However, paediatric patients cannot simply be considered smaller versions of adults. Children present different physiological and behavioural characteristics compared with adults patients (higher respiratory rates, and more variable breath hold patterns) making motion management even more challenging. In addition, motion-management strategies in paediatric radiotherapy must account for age-related limitations in cooperation, variable ability to follow technical instructions, and treatment-related anxiety. In many cases, treatment is therefore delivered under free breathing conditions, which can result in substantial intra- and inter-fraction respiratory variability and necessitate larger treatment margins. Alternatively, sedation or general anaesthesia can be used to overcome these limitations, allowing immobilisation and improved motion control during treatment delivery. However, repeated administration of general anaesthesia across multiple treatment fractions represents an invasive approach and introduces additional clinical and logistical burdens for both patients and caregivers.

In adult radiotherapy, several strategies have been developed to mitigate respiratory motion and reduce PTV margins. Among these, deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) has become widely used, particularly for left-sided breast cancer and mediastinal lymphomas [4], [6]. By increasing lungs volume and displacing critical organs, DIBH can significantly reduce radiation exposure to OARs [3], [4], [7], [8], [9]. In the paediatric setting, diagnostic imaging studies, including those from the TEDDI protocol, have demonstrated the feasibility of DIBH and suggested potential anatomical advantages, such as improved separation between the heart and mediastinal structures [5]. Though, Østergaard et al. reported positioning accuracy comparable to free breathing, indicating that the clinical benefit of DIBH in children remains uncertain [10].

At our institution, we previously developed and clinically implemented a mechanically-assisted non-invasive ventilation technique to support and optimize DIBH (MANIV-DIBH), which is now routinely used in adult patients [11], [12]. This technique uses a mechanical ventilator to passively induce stable and reproducible deep inspiration breath-holds lasting approximately 30 s without sedation or medication, based on predefined pressure levels and preoxygenation (FiO₂ 60%). Compared with voluntary breath-hold, MANIV-DIBH provides improved reproducibility across repeated breath-holds, achieves greater lung inflation, and ensures stable inspiratory plateaus. These characteristics allow reduced treatment margins to 5 mm instead of the usual 10-15 mm used with free breathing (depending on 4D-CT analysis) and improved precision, particularly in thoracic and abdominal stereotactic radiotherapy [11], [12].

Given its success in adults, we aimed to assess the feasibility of translating this approach to the paediatric population. However, paediatric radiotherapy presents specific challenges, including limited patient cooperation, increased anxiety, and the frequent need for sedation or general anaesthesia to manage respiratory motion. Demonstrating the feasibility of a mechanically-assisted, reproducible breath-hold technique without sedation may therefore represent an important step toward reducing treatment margins and radiation exposure to organs at risk in this vulnerable population.

Here, we report the first paediatric clinical application of MANIV-DIBH, illustrating the feasibility of adapting an adult-developed motion management strategy to paediatric radiotherapy and highlighting key considerations for its implementation in children.

Materials and methods

Patient

A 10-year-old girl with Ewing sarcoma (ES) of the left femur and 2 metastases (in the right lung and left scapula) received neoadjuvant chemotherapy (VDC-IE regimen), followed by surgical resection of all three tumour sites. Pathology showed complete tumour response. After adjuvant chemotherapy, she was referred for whole lung irradiation (WLI) due to her initial lung metastasis. To minimize respiratory motion variations, PTV margins, and thereby dose delivery to OARs, treatment was planned using MANIV-DIBH.

Procedure

The treatment technique was explained during the initial consultation with the radiation oncologist. Formal ethical approval was not required as per institutional guidelines; however, informed consent was obtained from the patient's representatives.

Preparatory session

Ten days prior to the start of the treatment, an experienced physiotherapist conducted a preparatory session to familiarize the patient with the MANIV-DIBH technique. The session included a structured explanation of the procedure, treatment sequence, and required patient cooperation, using simple language tailored to the patient's age and understanding. To encourage engagement and reduce anxiety, playful materials were introduced, helping to make the learning process more enjoyable and less intimidating. Several non-vented oronasal mask fittings were performed to identify the most suitable interface, ensuring both comfort and minimal air leakage for optimal pressure transmission. The patient then completed an initial familiarization trial using a simple, portable device to practice DIBHs in a non-threatening environment. This preparatory step aimed to build confidence and enhance adherence prior to transitioning to the simulation room.

Radiotherapy simulation

The simulation session was conducted one week prior to the start of radiotherapy. The patient was positioned supine on a vacuum mattress, with the right arm elevated and the left arm lowered to accommodate limited mobility following prior surgery involving the left scapula.

Prior to CT acquisition, the patient underwent a four-minute preoxygenation phase at FiO₂ 60% in spontaneous ventilation mode. The physiotherapist adjusted the MANIV-DIBH settings. We used an oronasal mask connected to a mechanical ventilator (Bellavista 1000, Vyaire Medical, Mettawa, IL, USA), operating in Airway Pressure Release Ventilation (APRV) mode. Initial ventilation parameters (positive pressure, expiratory trigger, rise time and flow trigger) were configured according to APRV mode, as described by Pierrard et al. and Van Ooteghem et al. [11], [12]. MANIV-DIBH parameters were adapted based on patient tolerance. The patient performed multiple repeated MANIV-DIBH of 20 s. The simulation CT was acquired during one MANIV-DIBH plateau at the end of the simulation session.

Treatment planning

The clinical target volume (CTV) included both lungs. Based on motion management analysis from our previous clinical experience with the technique, a 5-mm PTV margin was applied in all directions, except caudally, where a 7-mm security margin was used since it was our first experience in children. Under spontaneous breathing conditions, margins of 10 mm in all directions are typically recommended (e.g. in the current iEuroEwing protocol - EudraCT 2019–004153-93). The resulting overlap volumes between the PTV and adjacent OARs were subsequently compared between both conditions.

The prescribed dose was 15 Gy, delivered in 10 fractions. The dose was prescribed to the PTV with the aim of achieving D95 ≥ 95% of the prescribed dose, while minimizing dose to the surrounding OARs (i.e.,heart, spleen, pancreas, thyroid, kidneys, and liver). Homogeneous coverage of the adjacent vertebrae with a dose gradient <5Gy was required. The titanium prosthesis introduced additional constraints, as dose entry paths had to avoid the prosthesis, which added complexity to both dose planning and delivery.

Treatment sessions

Patient set-up and immobilisation was ensured by a vacuum mattress and skin pen marks. Treatment was delivered during repeated MANIV-DIBH, with careful preservation of patient positioning and oronasal mask setup. Ventilation parameters were those defined during the simulation session in order to ensure reproducibility across treatment fractions. Starting from the second treatment fraction, the patient's position was additionally monitored using a surface-guided radiotherapy (SGRT) system to further ensure positional stability and accuracy.

In line with standard practice in our department, a pre-treatment cone-beam CT (CBCT) was acquired during MANIV-DIBH to verify internal organ positioning and ensure accurate treatment delivery using image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT), allowing correction of inter-fraction positional deviations prior to irradiation. In our department, ultrafast CBCT are acquired using the 120 kV pelvis protocol of the HyperSight onboard imaging system integrated into the Ethos adaptive radiotherapy linear accelerator (Varian, a Siemens Healthineers company, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The HyperSight system enables full CBCT acquisition in approximately 6 s, which can be completed within a single DIBH. During the first five fractions, an additional post-treatment CBCT was acquired during a final DIBH as part of quality assurance to assess intra-fraction stability. For the remaining fractions (6–10), no post-treatment CBCT was performed in order to limit additional radiation exposure. The intra-fraction deviation was assessed in cranio-caudal, left-right, and antero-posterior directions, based on those pre- and post-treatment CBCTs registrations.

She showed a clear understanding of instructions and actively engaged in the training process. The interface used was an oronasal mask adapted for paediatric fit, ensuring a secure seal and modified to operate in a non-vented configuration, compatible with non-invasive ventilation. By the end of the treatment, the only acute side effect observed was CTCAE grade 2 fatigue. The follow-up PET/CT scan, conducted two months after completing radiotherapy, demonstrated a sustained complete response with no radiological evidence of radiation-induced toxicity.

Discussion

We report here the first use of the MANIV-DIBH technique in the paediatric setting. This first paediatric application in a child demonstrates the feasibility and safety of ventilator-assisted DIBH technique, with potential to reduce radiation exposure to the healthy organs.

Although the use of DIBH is well established in adults, particularly for breast cancer radiotherapy, clinical data in paediatric patients remain limited [9], [13]. Lundgaard et al. demonstrated the feasibility of DIBH in children aged 5–15 years in the TEDDI trial, in which paediatric patients were required to perform three 20-s breath-holds [5]. Among the 33 included children, 28 children (85%) were considered DIBH compliant, among which 8 children required adapted coaching and additional training. Østergaard et al. subsequently reported outcomes from seven paediatric patients treated within the TEDDI protocol, of whom four received treatment under DIBH conditions for mediastinal tumours. In this cohort, patients were older, ranging from 14 to 16 years [10]. Shaheen et al. also reported favorable results in 49 children and adolescents treated with spontaneous DIBHs for mediastinal Hodgkin lymphoma, with a mean intra-breath-hold variability of 3 mm [14]. The mean number of 20-s breath-holds required for treatment was 5.1 ± 1.8. These studies suggest that voluntary DIBH can be feasible in selected paediatric populations but require adequate patient cooperation and coaching.

In our case, the 10-year-old patient was able to understand and comply with the protocol and performed multiple stable and reproducible MANIV-DIBHs from the first preparatory session. Both the planning CT and all ten treatment fractions were delivered without difficulty. The preparatory and simulation sessions confirmed that children of this age can engage with the technique when appropriate explanations and support are provided. The mean treatment session duration was 32.1 (± 7.8) minutes. This is longer than the mean session time of approximately 6 min reported by Shaheen et al. in paediatric patients treated with voluntary DIBH for mediastinal Hodgkin lymphoma [14]. This difference can be attributable to the complexity of our treatment plan rather than poor tolerability of the technique — notably the need for dose avoidance around a scapular prosthesis and the constrained arm positioning, both of which required a larger number of beam arrangements and consequently more breath-holds per session. Despite this prolonged treatment time, the patient completed all sessions without interruption, and ventilator settings were successfully individualized, including progressive extension of expiratory pauses between breath-holds to improve comfort — an adaptation that may be particularly relevant in younger children.

From a treatment planning perspective, motion management in paediatric thoracic radiotherapy remains challenging and largely unstandardized. A systematic review reported that CTV-PTV margins in paediatric practice vary widely from 3 to 20 mm, with no consensus definition [15]. A large multicenter study quantifying respiratory-induced organ motion in 189 children also found that interfractional diaphragm displacement varied widely between patients (range: −13.0 to +17.5 mm in the cranio-caudal direction), with breathing amplitude increasing significantly with age, underscoring the need for a patient-specific margin approach [16]. Importantly, children treated under general anaesthesia — and therefore under invasive mechanical ventilation — presented with significantly smaller systematic and random errors compared to those treated under free breathing. However, repeated general anaesthesia across multiple fractions represents a significant clinical and logistical burden, and dedicated programmes are required to support paediatric patients in completing radiotherapy without sedation [17]. This observation suggests that it is the mechanical control of respiratory motion that drives geometrical reproducibility. MANIV-DIBH achieves precisely this mechanical control through non-invasive ventilation, without requiring sedation or general anaesthesia, while maintaining a passive, effort-independent breath-hold in an awake and cooperative child. In this context, MANIV-DIBH allowed us to reduce the PTV by 751.8 ml compared with the simulated free-breathing planning CT delineation, introducing the possibility of margin reduction. It also resulted in decreased irradiation of adjacent OARs within the high-dose region, and in the reduction of the volumes of OARs encompassed within the PTV (e.g. heart, esophagus, mammary buds, liver). These dosimetric improvements can be particularly relevant in paediatric radiotherapy, where sparing of developing structures is critical to limit long-term treatment-related toxicity and preserve quality of life in survivors. Whether this translates into clinically meaningful margin reduction and OAR sparing across a broader paediatric population remains to be confirmed, but these preliminary findings suggest that MANIV-DIBH may represent a promising avenue worth further prospective evaluation.

A limitation of this analysis is that these comparisons are based on delineations performed on a planning CT acquired under MANIV-DIBH conditions, with lungs inflated by the technique, precluding a direct free-breathing comparison. Although lung volumes would be reduced under free breathing, the relative benefit in OARs sparing is expected to persist, as spatial relationships between target and adjacent structures remain largely preserved. This is further supported by the daily CBCT confirmation that lung volumes remained consistently within the reduced PTV across all fractions, suggesting that the geometric adequacy of the reduced margin was maintained throughout treatment in practice. Notably, the lung volumes measured on daily CBCTs were systematically smaller than those obtained on the planning CT, suggesting that the degree of lung inflation achieved during treatment fractions was consistently lower than at planning (Appendix, Table A1). Two factors may contribute to this observation. First, cumulative patient fatigue over the course of a ten-fraction treatment may have progressively reduced the patient's capacity to achieve the same level of inflation as at planning. Second, the planning session may have captured a performance ceiling: a highly motivated and well-rested patient undergoing a dedicated simulation is likely to produce a maximal effort breath-hold, which may not be representative of daily treatment conditions. While these volumes remained within the PTV in all fractions — confirming geometric safety — this systematic discrepancy highlights an important consideration for future prospective studies: the planning CBCT or CT may overestimate the lung volumes achievable during actual treatment delivery, and strategies to account for this variability, such as incorporating online adaptive replanning or repeated planning CTs, may deserve further evaluation. Regardless of this variability in absolute lung inflation, the clinical objective of OAR sparing was preserved throughout treatment. The lungs themselves are part of the irradiated volume and are not the primary limiting structures. In contrast, cardiac exposure is directly impacted, as MANIV-DIBH induces greater heart displacement and superior positional stability compared with both free breathing and voluntary DIBH. In a randomized controlled trial in adult left breast cancer patients, Vander Veken et al. demonstrated that MANIV-DIBH achieved significantly improved heart displacement and breath-hold reproducibility compared with voluntary DIBH, resulting in reduced heart volumes within the high-dose region in breast cancer adult patients [8]. While direct extrapolation to the paediatric setting requires caution, this mechanistic benefit is expected to remain relevant, and is reflected in the cardiac OAR sparing observed in our delineation exercice [8].

Other motion-management strategies could have been considered. Free-breathing treatment remains the most commonly used approach in paediatric radiotherapy given its simplicity, but as illustrated by the dosimetric comparison above, it necessitates larger margins and greater OAR irradiation. Voluntary DIBH is an alternative, but its effectiveness in children is limited by age-related physiological and behavioural factors. Children have significantly higher baseline respiratory rates than adults combined with variable cognitive ability to understand and sustain repeated breath-holds. In the TEDDI trial, Lundgaard et al. reported that approximately 15% of children were non-compliant with voluntary DIBH, all under the age of 9, and nearly 25% required adapted coaching sessions [5]. Furthermore, Østergaard et al. reported that positioning accuracy with voluntary DIBH was comparable to free breathing in paediatric patients, questioning its incremental clinical benefit in this population [10]. Voluntary DIBH also typically relies on visual or vocal coaching systems that add logistical burden and may not be suitable for younger or more anxious children.

MANIV-DIBH addresses these limitations by providing a passive, ventilator-controlled breath-hold that does not rely on patient-driven respiratory control, reducing fatigue. This may be particularly advantageous in younger children, in whom voluntary techniques are difficult to reproduce reliably. Notably, as highlighted by Lundgaard et al., the implementation of additional motion-management techniques in paediatric radiotherapy should not be considered inherently burdensome: when delivered within a child-adapted and supportive environment, such approaches may even be perceived as engaging or playful, potentially facilitating cooperation and adherence [5].

Based on these considerations and this initial experience, MANIV-DIBH appears to be a promising approach for paediatric patients undergoing thoracic or upper abdominal irradiation. The high level of reproducibility achieved with ventilator-controlled breath-holds could be particularly advantageous in proton therapy, where breath-hold consistency is critical: poor DIBH reproducibility has been shown to result in underdosing of the CTV and insufficient OAR sparing even when robust planning margins are applied [18]. A systematic review on DIBH in proton therapy for mediastinal lymphoma further confirmed that breath-hold reproducibility directly impacts plan robustness and dose delivery accuracy [19]. This is of increasing relevance given the growing number of paediatric patients referred for proton therapy, driven by its demonstrated superiority in sparing healthy tissues and reducing long-term toxicities [20].

Nevertheless, several challenges remain. Procedural anxiety is common in children undergoing radiotherapy and is a recognized driver of non-compliance and increased reliance on general anaesthesia [17], [21]. Age-appropriate preparation is therefore essential: structured psychological preparation, simplified explanations, and playful or familiar elements — such as mask personalization and framing breath-holds as a game of ‘statues’ — can significantly enhance understanding, cooperation, and emotional comfort, as supported by the broader paediatric radiotherapy literature [17], [22]. Conducting initial familiarization in a non-clinical environment, away from the intimidating simulation room, further reduces anxiety and builds confidence prior to the first treatment session [22]. Fatigue may also be more pronounced in children than in adults, necessitating individualized adjustments to breath-hold cycles and rest periods between holds, as similarly noted by Lundgaard et al. in the context of voluntary DIBH compliance [5]. Finally, lung volumes measured on daily CBCTs were systematically smaller than those obtained at planning. The origin of this discrepancy — whether related to cumulative fatigue, a planning session ceiling effect, or other factors — remains to be elucidated and may inform important adaptations to the technique.

This initial experience warrants further research to refine ventilator parameters, optimize dedicated paediatric interfaces, and evaluate the technique across a broader range of paediatric indications and age groups.

Conclusion

This first paediatric clinical application report suggests that MANIV-DIBH is feasible, safe, and well tolerated in a 10-year-old patient undergoing thoracic radiotherapy. The technique provided stable breath-holds, associated with reduced treatment margins and improved sparing of surrounding organs at risk. Its implementation required multidisciplinary input and careful adaptation to the paediatric setting. These findings are preliminary and based on a single case; further experience is needed to define the role of MANIV-DIBH in paediatric oncology to reducing radiation-induced toxicity.
