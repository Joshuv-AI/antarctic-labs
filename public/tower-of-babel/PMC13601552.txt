A highly contiguous chromosome-level genome assembly of the Chilean jack mackerel Trachurus murphyi
Authors: Sandra Ferrada-Fuentes, Claudio Quevedo, Victoria Herrera-Yáñez, Cristian B. Canales-Aguirre, Felipe Aguilera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Chilean jack mackerel (Trachurus murphyi) is an economically important pelagic fish widely distributed across the South Pacific Ocean. Due to intense fishing pressure and the lack of high-quality genomic resources, the development of sustainable fisheries management strategies has been hindered. Here, we present a highly contiguous chromosome-level genome assembly of T. murphyi using PacBio and Hi-C sequencing data. The assembled genome spans 818.31 Mb, with a contig N50 length of 24.89 Mb and a scaffold N50 length of 34.34 Mb. The assembly is anchored on 24 pseudo-chromosomes, covering 95.80% of the genome. BUSCO analysis indicates a high level of completeness at 99.0%. Telomeric repeat motifs were detected at 12 single chromosome ends, whereas putative centromeric regions were identified on 12 chromosomes. Repetitive sequences account for 23.86% of the total genome, and 47,734 protein-coding genes were annotated, 90.98% of them with assigned function. This high-quality genome assembly serves as a valuable resource for studying the genetic diversity and population structure of the Chilean jack mackerel and provides a solid foundation for future management and conservation efforts.

Background & Summary

Jack mackerels are pelagic fish species of the genus Trachurus, found in all world´s oceans except the Arctic and Antarctic waters. They belong to the Carangidae family, a group of ray-finned fishes that includes jacks, pompanos, and trevallies1, and are classified into 14 species2. These species play a crucial role in marine ecosystems due to their trophic position in the marine food web3 and their importance as valuable fishery resources worldwide4. They are commercially exploited in many countries and are consumed fresh, frozen, smoked, or canned. Among Trachurus species, the Chilean jack mackerel (T. murphyi) fishery has been one of the largest globally, with catches peaking at nearly 5 million tons in the mid-1990s4. T. murphyi is distributed across the South Pacific Ocean5, allowing it to exploit a wide range of environmental conditions due to its presence in regions with highly heterogenous oceanographic and prey dynamics6–8. T. murphyi is valued by consumers for providing an excellent source of high-quality proteins, polyunsaturated fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals9. It could be that for those reasons, this species has been harvested for human consumption since at least the Early Holocene10,11.

Over the past decades, the Chilean jack mackerel population has declined significantly due to a combination of factors, including overfishing and variability with the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO)4,12–15. Consequently, the implementation of effective fisheries management strategies is crucial. Previous studies have explored the species´ fishery biology and genetics16–20. Population genetic analyses have revealed that T. murphyi constitutes a single, large panmictic population across its entire geographic range18,21–24. Additionally, recent research has identified a ZW sex determination system in T. murphyi25. Despite these findings, genomic and transcriptomic resources for T. murphyi remain limited to the mitochondrial genome, which was being only recently described26. Whole genome sequence assemblies are currently available only for the two Trachurus species, the Atlantic jack mackerel (Trachurus trachurus)27 and the Japanese jack mackerel (Trachurus japonicus)28 but no homologous assembly is available for T. murphyi.

Despite its ecological and economic importance, the lack of a high-quality, chromosome-level reference genome has limited detailed investigation of population structure, adaptive variation, and genomic responses to environmental change in Trachurus murphyi. Therefore, a contiguous genome assembly is essential to support fisheries management, conservation strategies, and comparative studies within Carangidae. Here, we have generated a nearly complete and highly contiguous chromosome-level assembly of the T. murphyi genome using PacBio Hi-Fi long-read and Omni-C/Hi-C sequencing. The generation of such a resource provides a foundational framework for future molecular and evolutionary research.

Methods

Sample preparation and DNA sequencing

The specimen used in this study was provided by commercial fisheries as a fish carcass. It was not subjected to experimental manipulation and handling when it was alive in the context of this work. According to Chilean regulations, institutional animal ethics committee approval is not required for the use of fishery-derived specimens. A single adult individual of Trachurus murphyi (fork length of 45.4 cm and weights 1.395 kg) was obtained from commercial fisheries in the Biobío Region, Chile (36.94°S, 73.75°E) in June 2022 and used for genome sequencing. The specimen exhibited no visible signs of diseases, parasitic infection, or developmental abnormalities, and showed normal external morphology (Fig. 1A). Once collected, the individual was transported to the laboratory on ice to minimize DNA degradation and muscle tissue was dissected and immediately stored at -80 °C until shipped on dry ice to Cantata Bio (https://cantatabio.com/) for DNA extraction, genomic DNA sequencing, and Hi-C library construction. The sex of the individual could not be determined through visual gonad inspection prior to DNA extraction and sequencing. No molecular sex-identification assays were performed. High-quality DNA, in terms of quantity and purity, was used to construct two PacBio Hi-Fi SMRTbell® libraries and one Dovetail Omni-C® sequencing library for assembling the T. murphyi genome. The PacBio libraries were sequenced with circular consensus sequencing (CCS) mode on a PacBio Sequel II system, generating 7,058,275 High-Fidelity (HiFi) reads, totaling 86.5 Gb of data, which covered the genome at 108-fold, with a N50 read length of 12,194 bp. For the Dovetail Omni-C® library, chromatin was first crosslinked with formaldehyde within the nucleus before extraction. The fixed chromatin was then digested with DNAseI, followed by end repair and ligation to a biotinylated bridge adapter, enabling proximity ligation of adapter-containing ends. After ligation, crosslinks were reversed, and the DNA was purified. Biotin that was not internal to ligated fragments were removed, and the sequencing library was prepared using NEBNext Ultra enzymes and Illumina-compatible adapters. Biotin-containing fragments were enriched using streptavidin beads before PCR amplification. The final library was sequenced on an Illumina HiSeqX platform using 2 × 150 bp reads, generating 87,721,376 read pairs, amounting to 28.6 Gb of data, with a coverage of 32.1-fold across the genome.

Chromosome-level genome assembly with PacBio and Hi-C data

The genome assembly was generated using PacBio HiFi reads and Hifisiam29 (v0.15.4-r347) with default parameters to generate both the primary and haplotigs-alternative sequences for regions where heterozygosity was detectable within primary contigs using long reads (Table 1). Given the high per-base accuracy of HiFi reads and the consensus accuracy achieved by hifisiam29,30, no additional polishing steps were applied. The assembled contigs were then screened for potential contamination using the BlobToolKit31 (v4.4.3) and the BLAST non-redundant database (Fig. 1B). Contigs identified as potential contamination were removed from the assembly (Table 1). Finally, purge_dups32 (v1.2.5) was applied to eliminate haplotigs and overlapping contigs. To achieve a chromosome-level assembly, we used the contig-level genome assembly and Dovetail OmniC® reads as input for HiRise, a software pipeline specifically designed to scaffold genome assemblies using proximity ligation data33. Briefly, Dovetail OmniC® reads were mapped to the contig-level genome assembly using BWA (https://github.com/lh3/bwa) with a minimum quality threshold of 50 (MQ > 50). HiRise then analyzed the separation of Dovetail OmniC® read pairs mapped within draft scaffolds to generate a likelihood model for genomic distances. This model was used to identify and correct putative misjoins, score prospective joins, and execute those joins above a set threshold. Following these steps, we generated a Hi-C contact map and manually reviewed it using Juicebox34 (v1.11.08) to correct any misjoins and position unplaced contigs.

No gap-filling or telomere extension procedures were applied during genome assembly. The differences in total assembly length between the contig-level assembly (802.27 Mb) and the final scaffolded assembly (818.31 Mb) reflects differences in the assembly stage accounting rather than sequence addition. Specifically, the contig-level assembly length corresponds to the primary assembly after haplotig removal using purge_dups32, whereas the final assembly includes the 24 pseudo-chromosome and all retained scaffolds following Hi-C-based scaffolding, including unplaced sequences that were excluded from intermediate statistics (Fig. 1C, Table 1, Table 2). Identification of telomere and centromere regions in the chromosome-level genome assembly

Given the high quality and gapless nature of our chromosome-level genome assembly (Table 1), we sought to determine whether it meets the criteria for a telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome assembly. Telomeric repeat identification was performed by sequence motif detection only, and no sequence extension or telomere reconstruction was attempted. To identify telomeric regions, we searched the Chilean jack mackerel genome for the (TTAGGG)n sequence, the canonical telomere repeat found in most animals35. Telomere identification was carried out using the TeloExplorer function of the quarTeT36 (v1.2.5) software with the “-c animal” option. To identify centromeric regions, which are characterized by large stretches of repetitive satellite DNA or tandem repeats ranging in size from hundreds of kilobases to megabases37, we used the CentroMiner tool of the quarTeT36 (v1.2.5) software with default parameters. Briefly, this tool detects all tandem repeat monomers in a genome assembly and selects those most likely to be centromeric repeats on each chromosome based on their period and copy number. These monomers are then clustered to reduce redundancy and aligned to the corresponding chromosomes. Continuous matching regions are added to the candidates set, and scores are assigned based on total match length and retrotransposon content to ultimately determine the locations and predominant monomer repeats36. Our analyses revealed that putative centromeric regions range in length from 0.3 Mb to 2.1 Mb (Fig. 2A, Table 3). These regions are characterized by a low density of protein-coding genes and a high abundance of repetitive elements, with LTR/Gypsy, LINE/L2 and simple repeats representing the predominant repeat classes (Table 3), consistent with the typical structural arragement of centromeres38. In addition, centromeric regions display large interaction-depleted (“blank”) domains in Omni-C contact maps (Fig. 2B), a hallmark feature of centromeres that has also been reported in other gap-free or chromosome-level genome assemblies where centromeres have been resolved39,40. No single conserved core centromeric repeat unit was identified; instead, centromeres appear to consist of heterogeneous arrays of dispersed repeats, reflecting the species-specific and rapidly evolving nature of centromeric DNA in teleost fishes41–43. Therefore, centromere positions were inferred at regional level based on repeat composition and Hi-C interaction patterns, rather than by a single consensus repeat sequence. Repetitive sequence annotation

To identify repetitive sequences in the Chilean jack mackerel genome, we generated a custom repeat library using a combination of de novo and homology-based approaches. This included RepeatModeler44 (v2.0.1), TransposonPSI45 (v1.0.0), LTRharvest46 (v1.6.2), LTRdigest47 (v1.6.2), TIRvish48 (v1.6.2), MITE Tracker49 (v1.0), and HelitronScanner50 (v1.1), all executed with default parameters. The individual repeat libraries were concatenated, and sequences sharing more than 80% similarity were merged to remove redundancy using USEARCH51 (v11.0.667). The resulting non-redundant repeat library was then classified with RepeatClassifier44 (v2.0.1). To filter out protein-coding sequences, repetitive elements were mapped against a custom database, containing the annotated protein-coding sequences of Danio rerio, Seriola lalandi dorsalis, Cyclopterus lumpus, Xiphophorus couchianus, Oncorhynchus tshawytscha, and Salmo salar, using BLASTn52 (v2.9.0 + ) with an e-value threshold of 0.001. Any sequences matching protein-coding genes were removed from the library. The final repeat library was then used in RepeatMasker53 (v4.1.1) to generate a genome report repeat content and to mask the genome for subsequent annotation with the BRAKER3 pipeline. RepeatMasker was run in standard mode, resulting in a hard-masked genome in which repetitive regions were replaced with ambiguous nucleotides prior to gene prediction.

Analysis of transposable element divergence showed that 48.2% of annotated repeats exhibit low genetic distance (Kimura divergence ≤ 10%) from their respective consensus sequences (Fig. 3C). Among major element classes, LINEs, LTRs, and DNA transposons show enrichment of very low-divergence copies, with 18.9% of LINEs, 22.8% of LTRs, and 21.5% of DNA transposons displaying divergence values between 0 and 5%, consistent with relatively recent transposable element activity.

Gene prediction and functional annotation

To annotate protein-coding sequences in the Chilean jack mackerel genome, we used the BRAKER354 (v3.0.8) along with reference proteins from 18 species: Anabas testudineus (GCA_900324465.3), Carassius auratus (GCA_003368295.1), Clupea harengus (GCA_900700415.2), Danio rerio (GCA_000002035.4), Gadus morhua (GCA_902167405.1), Gasterosteus aculeatus (GCA_016920845.1), Oreochromis aureus (GCA_013358895.1), Oreochromis niloticus (GCA_001858045.3), Oryzias javanicus (GCA_003999625.1), Poecilia formosa (GCA_000485575.1), Salmo salar (GCA_905237065.2), Sander lucioperca (GCA_008315115.1), Scleropages formosus (GCA_900964775.1), Seriola dumerili (GCA_002260705.1), Seriola lalandi dorsalis (GCA_002814215.1), Sparus aurata (GCA_900880675.1), Trachurus trachurus (GCA_905171665.1), and Xiphophorus couchianus (GCA_001444195.1). All protein sequences were obtained from Ensembl Release 112. All reference proteomes were concatenated and used directly as input for BRAKER3 without prior manual curation, allowing the pipeline to internally select relevant protein evidence during gene prediction. It is worthy to mention that gene prediction was performed in protein-supported mode because RNA-seq transcriptomic data were not available, as high-quality total RNA suitable for sequencing could not be obtained due to technical limitations. Consequently, transcript-supported gene prediction was not performed.

Briefly, GeneMark-EPT55 was used to identify genomic loci where extrinsic evidence support reliable gene predictions. These high-confidence predictions were then used to train the statistical model implemented in AUGUSTUS56. Further refinements incorporated protein-derived extrinsic evidences from the reference proteomes listed above, with gene predictions subsequently optimized using TSEBRA57. Functional annotation of the predicted protein-coding genes was conducted using DIAMOND58 (v2.1.11) against the following public databases: NCBI non-redundant (nr), SwissProt, TrEMBL, and eggNOG. Protein domain searches were performed using Pfam with HMMER59 v(3.3.2) and InterProScan60 (v5.74.105). Gene Ontology (GO) terms were extracted from the InterProScan output, and KEGG Orthology (KO) annotation was conducted using KofamScan61 and the KOfam database.

Mitogenome assembly and annotation

To assemble the mitochondrial (mtDNA) genome of T. murphyi, we employed a strategy that utilizes the reference mitogenome of a closely related species from NCBI (accession number PP935111.1 in this case). This reference was used to capture mitochondrial PacBio HiFi reads, which were then assembled into a single circularized contig using MitoHiFi62 (v3.2.1). In addition to the annotation provided by MitoHiFi, the assembled mitogenome was further annotated using MITOS2 web server63. To evaluate the depth of coverage, all PacBio HiFi reads were mapped back to the assembled mitogenome using minimap264 v(2.29). We used BLASTn52 (v2.10.1) to identify matches between the mitochondrial genome assembly and the nuclear genome assembly. Nuclear contigs showing > 99% sequence identity and shorter lengths than the mitochondrial genome assembly were filtered out from the mitochondrial genome assembly. The resulting mitogenome assembly and its annotations were visualized using the Proksee web tool65. Mitochondrial protein-coding gene arrangements among Trachurus species were compared using MITOS2 GFF annotation data. Protein-coding genes were ordered according to their genomic coordinates and strand orientation, and gene arragements were compared across species to assess collinearity. Homologous genes were identified based on gene annotation names, and gene order conservation was evaluated by direct coordinate-based comparisons. As the analysis focused on structural gene arrangement rather than sequence divergence, no statistical tests were applied. Data Overview

The genomic datasets generated for Trachurus murphyi comprise a highly contiguous chromosome-level nuclear genome assembly and a complete mitochondrial genome. The nuclear genome assembly spans 818.321 Mbp across 532 scaffolds, with a scaffold N50 of 34.34 Mb, an N90 of 25.13 Mb, and a GC content of 42.31% (Table 1, Table 2). A total of 24 pseudo-chromosomes account for 95.80% of the assembled genome, with 34.31 Mb remaining as unplaced scaffolds and no ambiguous bases present (Table 2). BUSCO analysis indicated 99.0% completeness, including 97.9% single-copy, 1.1% duplicated genes, with 0.3% fragmented and 0.7% missing genes (Fig. 1D). Repetitive sequence comprises 195.24 Mb (23.86%) of the T. murphyi genome. Interspersed repeats account the majority of repetitive DNA, with retroelements representing 8.34% of the genome, DNA transposons 8.89%, rolling-circle transposons 0.53%, and unclassified repeats 0.01%. In addition, simple repeats comprise 5.58%, satellites 0.06%, and low-complexity regions 0.45% of the genome (Table 4). Gene annotation identified 47,734 predicted protein-coding genes with a total of 370,799 exons. The mean exon length is 189 bp (median = 126 bp), with 50% of exons ranging between 86 and 179 bp. These values are consistent with the typical exon size distribution observed in teleost genome69. The average gene length is 7.0 kb, with a mean coding sequence (CDS) length of 1.35 kb. Genes contain an average of 7.8 exons and 6.8 introns (mean = 979 bp) (Fig. 5), consisting with gene structure characteristics observed in teleost genomes69; functional annotation assigned homologous matches to 43,427 genes (90.98%) across public databases, with 9,080 genes supported by all eight annotation resources used (Fig. 5). Technical Validation

To evaluate the quality of our genome assembly and gene annotation, we employed several complementary approaches. First, we assessed potential contamination in the genome assembly using BlopToolKit31 (v4.4.3) to analyze GC content and sequencing depth. Contigs identified as contaminants were removed from the assembly (Table 1). After decontamination, we observed that nearly all GC content points clustered around 40%, indicating the absence of exogenous contamination from bacteria or other species (Fig. 1B). Second, we mapped the Omni-C reads to the assembled genome using BWA (https://github.com/lh3/bwa) to generate a Hi-C contact map. The resulting heatmap displayed high consistency across all chromosomes, providing strong evidence for the accurate sequencing, ordering, and orientation of contigs in the Chilean jack mackerel genome assembly (Fig. 1C). Third, we predicted centromeric and telomeric sequences in the T. murphyi genome and found that at least 12 chromosomes contained one of these characteristic chromosomal features (Fig. 2A, Table 2, Table 3).

Fourth, we evaluated assembly completeness using BUSCO70 (v5.3.2). The analysis, based on the actinopterygii_odb10 database, revealed a completeness score of 99.0%, comprising 97.9% complete single-copy, 1.1% duplicated, 0.3% fragmented, and 0.7% missing genes (Fig. 1D). These results underscore the high completeness and accuracy of the Chilean jack mackerel genome assembly. Fifth, we assessed per-base consensus accuracy using Merqury71 (v1.3). The assembly shows very high consensus accuracy (QV = 52.8), corresponding to a base-level accuracy of 99.999%. The low number of missing k-mers (90 kb out of 818.321 Mb) indicates a nearly complete and high accurate assembly, with few sequence errors, indicative of a nearly error-free assembly. Sixth, we aligned PacBio HiFi reads to the assembled genome using minimap264 (v2.17), resulting in a mapping rate of 99.97%, further supporting the high consensus accuracy of the assembly. The predicted gene count (47,734 protein-coding genes) exceeds that reported for many diploid teleost genomes69. To investigate whether this reflects unresolved haplotypic duplication or annotation artifacts, we assessed BUSCO duplication rates at the gene model level. BUSCO analysis of the predicted coding sequences and proteins recovered 13.4% duplicated BUSCOs, indicating redundancy within the annotated gene set. In contrast, BUSCO analysis of the genome assembly showed high completeness with low duplication (97.9% complete single-copy and 1.1% duplicated BUSCOs), suggesting that haplotypic duplication was largely resolved at the assembly stage. To assess the effect of protein evidence selection on gene prediction, an additional BRAKER3 run was performed using Trachurus trachurus (GCA_905171665.2) as a sole reference proteome, yielding 45,540 predicted protein-coding genes, comparable to the 47,734 genes obtained using multiple proteomes, indicating robustness of gene prediction to protein input choice. Detailed results of this analysis are provided in Supplementary Table 2.
