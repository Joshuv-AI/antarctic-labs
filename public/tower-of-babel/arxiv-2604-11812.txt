Confidence envelopes for the false discoveries with heterogeneous data
Romain Périer, Gilles Blanchard, Sebastian Döhler, Guillermo Durand, Etienne Roquain

Abstract
In the context of selective inference, confidence envelopes for the false discoveries allow the user to select any subset of null hypotheses while having a statistical guarantee on the number of false discoveries in the selected set. Many constructions of such envelopes have been proposed recently, using local test families ( 22 ; 26 ) , paths ( 33 ) or interpolation ( 7 ) . All those methods have in common that they have been well-studied for the homogeneous case where all p p -values under the null have a uniform distribution over [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . However, in many applications the data are heterogeneous and discrete, hence the p p -values have heterogeneous, discrete distributions, and the previous constructions may incur a loss of power, in the sense that they over-estimate the number of false discoveries. In this paper, we bridge the previous constructions under the homogeneous case with new tools. We also apply these tools to propose several confidence envelopes based on tools tailored for heterogeneous data, like the Bretagnolle inequality, or a new variant of the Simes inequality. We compare these new envelopes to their homogeneous counterparts on simulated data.

1.1
Overview of goals and motivations

We describe the aims of the paper, approach and contributions.
The objects used will be more formally defined in

Let
(H_{i})_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket}
be a family of null statistical hypotheses to test; each null hypothesis corresponds to a subset
of probability distributions driving the observed data.
For a data distribution

” if

Observe that

Several approaches for constructing FD confidence envelopes have been

This result shifts the problem to two questions: how to construct appropriate

Note that computing

As is common, we assume that the data has already been processed in such a way that for each hypothesis

If we furthermore assume that

Namely, assume that

-upper confidence envelope on the ecdf

Then the test

The approach described above, constructing local tests from

However, in many situations, such as when

Driven by this need of computable bounds, we will also explore the alternative approach of

1.2
Confidence envelopes in the context of selective inference

As mentioned before, data snooping (also called
p
-hacking, or significance chasing) plays a critical role in modern day applications and is considered one of the main causes of the replication crisis (see
47
, “Principle 4”
, and
32
). Indeed, classical statistical guarantees generally do not hold when the dataset has undergone a first selection step based on the observation of the data itself, but the classical method that has been used (whichever it is) does not take into account this random selection step. For instance, think of the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure applied to
10^{6}
tests, but where only the
10^{3}
smallest
p
-values have been selected by the data analyst as input to the procedure. Contrary to the plain BH procedure, there is no guarantee on the False Discovery Rate control of such a two-step procedure. While the flaw is obvious in this example, various, less blatant flavors of data-dependent selection often make their way into statistical practice
while seeming innocuous to the practitioner, for example selection based on a different statistic which is not completely independent of the test statistic.

Selective inference is the branch of statistics that aims to answer this concern by providing methods and procedures with proven statistical guarantees when a first selection step occurred. Selective inference is generally tackled in two possible fashions. One consists in assuming that the selection procedure is known (for example, coming from the LASSO estimator) and to design valid inference by conditioning on the selection event, see for example
44
;
34
;
21
.

The other fashion adopts a “simultaneous” point of view, where we seek to achieve guarantees for all possible selection subsets. This has been investigated for inference in, for instance,
40
;
4
;
1
. For multiple testing purposes, this fashion takes the form of the construction of the aforementioned confidence envelopes
\big(\widehat{V}(S)\big)_{S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket}
, first introduced by
22
. Since then, many works have focused on such constructions, with, notably, a prolific series of work focused on closed testing
(
26
;
37
;
29
;
25
;
46
)
, and another series based on the JER approach
(
7
;
16
;
20
;
6
;
5
;
36
)
. Note that the inversion procedure and the closed testing approach, both based on local tests, are recalled in
Section
3.2
, while the JER approach is recalled in
Section
3.3
.

Confidence envelopes have notably been applied to gene expression analysis
(
26
;
30
;
20
)
while software coded with the
R
language
(
38
)
is already available for such analyses
(
19
)
in a user-friendly
shiny
application
(
12
)
. Confidence envelopes have also been applied a lot in functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) studies
(
6
;
5
;
46
)
.

Multiple testing and confidence bound formalism

2.1
Mathematical notation

In the remainder,
m\geq 1
and
N\geq 1
are two positive integers. Specifically,
m
will be used to denote the number of hypotheses tested, while
N
is used when we need some generic integer. For any two
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
with
a\leq b
,
\llbracket a,b\rrbracket
is the integer interval
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a,a+1,\dotsc,b-1,b}}\right\}
.

The binary operator
\wedge
denotes the minimum of two terms, and
\vee
denotes the maximum of two terms.

For a vector
(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the notation
x_{(1)},\dotsc,x_{(N)}
denotes the order statistics of
(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})
, such that
x_{(1)}\leq\dotsb\leq x_{(N)}
, and the notation
x_{[1]},\dotsc,x_{[N]}
denotes the reverse-order statistics of
(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})
, such that
x_{[1]}\geq\dotsb\geq x_{[N]}
. In case of ties, any permutation that orders can be used. Finally, for
A\subseteq\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
, the notation
x_{(1:A)},\dotsc,x_{(|A|:A)}
denotes the order statistics of the elements
(x_{i})_{i\in A}
and, likewise, the notation
x_{[1:A]},\dotsc,x_{[|A|:A]}
denotes the reverse-order statistics of the elements
(x_{i})_{i\in A}
.

2.2
Classical multiple testing formalism

We let
\mathfrak{X}=(\mathcal{X},\Xi)
a measurable space and
\mathfrak{F}
a model on
\mathfrak{X}
, that is a set of probability measures on
\mathfrak{X}
. We observe data belonging to
\mathcal{X}
distributed according to a distribution that belongs to
\mathfrak{F}
. Formally, this data is represented by a random variable often denoted
X
, defined on an abstract probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
, such that the pushforward measure
X_{\sharp\mathbb{P}}
is an element of
\mathfrak{F}
. For a given
\mu\in\mathfrak{F}
, we say that “the law of
X
is
\mu
” or that “
X
is distributed according to
\mu
”, and we denote
X\sim\mu
, if
X_{\sharp\mathbb{P}}=\mu
.

For a given
\mu\in\mathfrak{F}
, a statistic
T:\mathfrak{X}\to\big(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\big)
, and a Borelian set
A\in\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}}}\right)
, the notations
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T\in A}}\right)
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T(X)\in A}}\right)
are short for “
\mathbb{P}\big(T(X)\in A\big)
for some
X
with
X\sim\mu
”. The notation
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}
is used in the same way for expectations.

We consider
m\geq 1
null hypotheses
H_{1},\dotsc,H_{m}
to test, which formally are submodels of
\mathfrak{F}
, that is,
H_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}
for all
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
. We denote by
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mu)
(the dependence in
\mu
will be dropped when there is no ambiguity) the set of indices of null hypotheses that are true, that is
\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mu)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket:\mu\in H_{i}}}\right\}
. In other words,
H_{i}
is true if and only if
i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
. The cardinal of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is always denoted
m_{0}=m_{0}(\mu)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mu)}}\right\rvert
. We denote the complementary of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
by
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mu)
. Thus
\mathcal{H}_{1}
indexes the true signal that we would be able to ideally detect.

To test each
H_{i},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, we assume that have at hand a statistic which takes its values in
[0,1]
, named a
p
-value, and denoted
p_{i}
. When there is no ambiguity, we will identify the statistic
p_{i}
and the random variable
p_{i}(X)
. We make the following two assumptions on the
p
-values. The first one is the classical requirement for a statistic to be considered a
p
-value (and induce a test with type-I error control). The second one is an independence assumption required for the application of our concentration inequalities.

Assumption 1

(Super-uniformity under the null)
.

For each
\mu\in\mathfrak{F}
, for each
i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mu)
, for
X\sim\mu
, the distribution of
p_{i}(X)
is super-uniform, which is often denoted by
p_{i}(X)\succeq\mathcal{U}\big([0,1]\big)
. This means that the cdf of
p_{i}(X)
is upper bounded by the cdf of
\mathcal{U}\big([0,1]\big)
. Mathematically,

\forall\mu\in\mathfrak{F},\forall i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mu),\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\;\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{i}\leq x}}\right)\leq 0\wedge(x\vee 1).

(1)

Assumption 2

(Independence under the null)
.

For each
\mu\in\mathfrak{F}
, the
p
values
p_{i}(X)
,
i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mu)
are mutually independent.

Remark 2.1
.

Assumption 2
is rather classical in multiple testing, albeit not totally realistic. In the literature, it is sometimes required that the family of
p
-values
\big(p_{i}(X)\big)_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}
is also independent from the family
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{i}(X)}}\right)_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{1}}
, for example to get FDR bounds in
13
. In this paper, however, classical multiple testing results and inequalities are only ever applied to
\big(p_{i}(X)\big)_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}
, making this additional requirement unnecessary.

The following is a simple toy example making use of the introduced notation.

Example 2.2

(Gaussian one-sided under independence)
.

In this example we assume that
X=(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{m})
is a vector of independent Gaussians and the null hypotheses refer to the nullity of their means in contrast to their positivity (we assume that they share the same variance for simplicity). That is, formally,
(\mathcal{X},\Xi)=\big(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{R}^{m}}}\right)\big)
,
\mathfrak{F}=\big\{\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\sigma^{2}\mathrm{Id}):\forall j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket,\nu_{j}\geq 0,\sigma>0\big\}
, and for each
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
,
H_{i}=\big\{\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\sigma^{2}\mathrm{Id})\in\mathfrak{F}:\nu_{i}=0\big\}
. Then we can construct
p
-values by letting
p_{i}(X)=1-\Phi(X_{i})
, where
\Phi
denotes the cdf of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Such
p
-values satisfy Assumptions
1
and
2
. They are even exactly uniform under the null.

For every subset of hypotheses
S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, let
V(S)=|S\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}|
. If we think of
S
as a selection set of hypotheses deemed significant,
V(S)
is then the number of false discoveries in
S
. The function
V(\cdot)
is the main object of interest in multiple testing, with past works proposing rejection procedures
R=R(X)
with a given statistical control on
V(R)
. Popular error criteria include the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER), defined as

\FWER(R)=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V(R)>0}}\right),

the False Discovery Rate (FDR,
2
), defined as the mean of the False Discovery Proportion (FDP):

\FDP(R)=\frac{V(R)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|R}}\right\rvert\vee 1},\;\;\FDR(R)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\FDP(R)}}\right],

and the False Discovery Exceedance (FDX,
22
) for some level
\gamma\in(0,1)
:

\FDX(R)=\mathbb{P}\big(\FDP(R)>\gamma\big).

However, in this work and in the confidence bounds literature, one does not want to prescribe a single rejection set
R
with proven control on
V(R)
(and only
V(R)
), but to provide control on the function
V(\cdot)
as a whole.

2.3
Confidence bounds

With the formalism introduced in last Section, a simultaneous confidence upper bound is a functional

\widehat{V}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{X}\times(0,1)&\longrightarrow&\Big(\mathcal{P}\big(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket\big)\to\llbracket 0,m\rrbracket\Big)\\
(x,\alpha)&\longmapsto&\widehat{V}_{(x,\alpha)}(\cdot)\\
\end{array},}}\right.

such that the following inequality is true:

\forall\mu\in\mathfrak{F},\forall\alpha\in(0,1),\;\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket,V(S)\leq\widehat{V}_{(X,\alpha)}(S)}}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(2)

In the remainder or this work, the dependence in
(X,\alpha)
will be dropped when there is no ambiguity and
\widehat{V}_{(X,\alpha)}
will simply be written
\widehat{V}
.

Equation (
2
) means that, with high probability, we are able to bound the number of false discoveries in any selection set
S
. Note that the “
\forall S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
” being inside the probability is what makes the confidence bound “simultaneous”.

From a simultaneous confidence upper bound on the false discoveries, we immediately get a simultaneous confidence lower bound on the trues discoveries, by letting

\widehat{D}(S)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S}}\right\rvert-\widehat{V}(S).

(3)

Sometimes, confidence bounds are reported into this form, as in
26
;
25
, or in
Section
7
here when we report the numerical results of our experiments.

Also note that, by a simple division by
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S}}\right\rvert\vee 1
,
Equation (
2
)
can be rewritten as an equation giving a simultaneaous upper bound on the FDP:

\forall\mu\in\mathfrak{F},\forall\alpha\in(0,1),\;\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket,\FDP(S)\leq\frac{\widehat{V}_{(X,\alpha)}(S)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S}}\right\rvert\vee 1}}}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(4)

This, in turn, allows to use simultaneous upper bounds to construct procedures that control the FDX. Note that controlling the FDX is generally more desirable than controlling the FDR, given the nature of the latter as an expected value. See for example
11
for a credible example where the FDR is controlled but the FDP has a highly undesirable behavior (either 0 because no discoveries are made, or higher than the target level). The construction for FDX control is the following: one can compute the largest
S
such that
\frac{\widehat{V}(S)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S}}\right\rvert\vee 1}
is less than or equal to a nominal level
\gamma\in(0,1)
, and (
4
) ensures that, with high probability, the
\FDP
of
S
is upper bounded by
\gamma
, so that the
\FDX
is bounded by
\alpha
.

2.4
Heterogeneity

In this work, we are interested in the case where the distributions of
p
-values under the null are heterogeneous, departing from the classical assumption that they are all distributed as
\mathcal{U}([0,1])
under the null (as in
36
, for instance), that we can call the
homogeneous uniform
case.

Also note that, with respect to the super-uniformity of Assumption
1
, the homogeneous uniform case appears as a “worst case”, in that many multiple testing procedures, designed to provide statistical control of the type-I error for that case, become more conservative under super-uniformity. This is easy to see with classical rejection procedures that often take the form or
p
-value sublevel sets
R(X)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket:p_{i}\leq\hat{t}(X)}}\right\}
. Given that super-uniform
p
-values are stochastically larger than uniforms, the threshold
\hat{t}(X)
, although ensuring statistical validity, becomes badly calibrated and “too small”, leading to fewer rejections.

In order to be able to leverage heterogeneity and super-uniformity and create confidence bounds that are more powerful than those designed to guarantee control in the homogeneous uniform case, we need some knowledge about said heterogeneity, that is, to have access to the distribution of any
p_{i}
under the null, or at least to have a stochastic lower bound
on that distribution. This motivates the following assumption:

Assumption 3

(Stochastic heterogeneous lower bound)
.

For each
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, there exists a known nondecreasing function
F_{i}
such that, if
\mu\in H_{i}
, the cdf of
p_{i}
is upper bounded (as a function) by

. Mathematically,

\forall\mu\in\mathfrak{F},\forall i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mu),\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\;\mathbb{P}\big(p_{i}(X)\leq x\big)\leq F_{i}(x).

If
Assumption 1
holds, then

Assumption 3
is always satisfied by

,
but with

we hope to take advantage of

Assumption 3
also allows to generalize beyond the super-uniform case
(i.e. if
Assumption 1
does not hold), as is the case with weighted

-values (see

Assumption 3
is satisfied in particular if, for all
\mu\in\mathfrak{F}
,
i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mu)
, the cdf of
p_{i}
does not depend on
\mu

Assumption 3
is also always satisfied by
x\mapsto\sup_{\mu\in H_{i}}\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{i}\leq x}}\right)
. The latter choice of

is the one made in

Definition 2.3

(Support)
.

For
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, we define the support of
F_{i}
, by analogy with the support of a measure, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\forall\varepsilon>0,\;F_{i}(x+\varepsilon)-F_{i}(x-\varepsilon)>0}}\right\}.

(Independent binomials)

3.1
Homogeneous concentration inequalities

We start with recalling classical concentration inequalities that have been used to construct confidence bounds. While they are usually stated to be true for uniform random variables (as they are in

The underlying argument follows from coupling between super-uniform and uniform variables, we isolate this

Lemma 3.1

(Super-uniform/uniform coupling)

Let

\big(\widetilde{U}_{i}\big)_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,N}}\right\rrbracket}

Proposition 3.4

Let

Proof.

3.2
Confidence envelopes by the inversion procedure

In this section, we recall the inversion procedure introduced by
22
, which uses the notion of local test.
We first introduce, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
A\subset\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, a notation for the number of elements in
A\cap R(t)
, where
R(t)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket:p_{i}\leq t}}\right\}
:

i_{A}(t)=\sum\limits_{i\in A}\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{p_{i}\leq t}}\right\}}.

(6)

When
A=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, we simplify the notation to
i(t)
.

Definition 3.8

(Local test)
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
. Then we call
H_{A}=\bigcap\limits_{i\in A}H_{i}
the intersection hypothesis for the subset
A
, and we say that a statistic
\varphi_{A}
is a local test for
A
at level
\alpha\in[0,1]
if, for all
\mu\in H_{A},\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi_{A}=1}}\right)\leq\alpha
.

A family
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi_{A}}}\right)_{A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket}
of local tests has level
\alpha\in[0,1]
if for all
A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
,
\varphi_{A}
is a local test for
A
at level
\alpha
.

We can easily define local tests using concentration inequalities like the ones studied in previous section, as in the following example.

Example 3.9
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and assume
Assumption 1
and
Assumption 2
. Then for
A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, a local test of level
\alpha
derived from
subsection 3.1
is expressed by

\varphi_{A}=\sup\limits_{t\in(0,1]}\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{1}{|A|}i_{A}(t)\geq\frac{t}{\alpha}}}\right\}}.

Denote
\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}(X)=\big\{A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket\text{ : }\varphi_{A}(X)=0\big\}
the set of potential candidates for
\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mu)
according to our local tests (notice that it is a
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\alpha}}\right)
-confidence set for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
).

Proposition 3.10

(Inversion procedure,
22
)
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Assume that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi_{A}}}\right)_{A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket}
is a family of local tests at level
\alpha
. Then, the functional
\hat{V}^{\IP}_{\varphi}
defined by
\hat{V}^{\IP}_{\varphi}(S)=\max\limits_{A\in\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}}|A\cap S|
is a confidence bound at level
\alpha
.

We say that a confidence envelope is
an
exact shortcut
(for the local test family
\varphi
) if it is
equal to
\hat{V}^{\IP}_{\varphi}
, and we
say that this confidence envelope is a
shortcut
if it
is an upper bound of
\hat{V}^{\IP}_{\varphi}
.

Note that the computation of the inversion procedure envelope is NP-hard in general,
hence a challenging goal is to express
\hat{V}^{\IP}_{\varphi}
as an exact shortcut computable
in polynomial time.
Structural assumptions on the local tests can lead to such exact shortcuts. One of the most usual
class of local tests is the following,
where the rejection thresholds of a local test depend only on the cardinality
of the matching subset of hypotheses.

Definition 3.11

(Homogeneous Simes-like local tests)
.

Let
\Phi=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi_{A}}}\right)_{A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket}
be a family of local tests. We say that
\Phi
is a homogeneous Simes-like local test family if, for all
A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
,

\varphi_{A}=\max_{i\in\llbracket 1,|A|\rrbracket}\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{p_{(i:A)}\leq\ell_{i:|A|}}}\right\}},

(7)

with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell_{i:n}}}\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq n\leq m\\
1\leq i\leq m\end{subarray}}
such that
\ell_{i:n_{1}}\geq\ell_{i:n_{2}}
for all
n_{1}\leq n_{2}
and
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
.

Note that the homogeneous term will be dropped when there is no ambiguity on the working case. We call
vanilla Simes local test family
the previous local test family for the specific choice
\ell_{i:n}=\frac{i\alpha}{n}
.

Proposition 3.12

(
25

.

Assume that, for all
A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
,
\varphi_{A}
is of the form (

\forall S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket,\hat{V}^{\IP}_{\varphi}(S)=\min\limits_{1\leq u\leq|S|}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\in S:p_{i}>\ell_{u:\hat{m}_{0}^{\GHS}}}}\right\}}}\right\rvert+u-1}}\right\},

(8)

where

\hat{m}_{0}^{\GHS}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{n\in\llbracket 0,m\rrbracket:\forall i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket,p_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[i}}\right]}>\ell_{(n-i+1):n}}}\right\}.

(9)

The same result holds true if the
\leq
inequality is replaced
by
<
in (
7
), and

by

in (
8
) and (

).

The proof of this proposition in

Remark 3.13
.

subsection 3.2

3.3
Reference families, JER and interpolation

3.3.1
JER methods

We present here the alternative approach to constructing confidence bounds originally introduced in
7
, based on the two concepts of reference families and Joint Error Rate (JER) that we start by defining. A reference family is a finite family
\mathfrak{R}_{\alpha}(X)=(R_{k},\zeta_{k})_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
with
R_{k}\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
,
\zeta_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, where everything (that is,
\mathcal{K}
and all the
R_{k}
and
\zeta_{k}
) depends on
(X,\alpha)
, but the dependency is not explicitly written. Likewise, when there is no ambiguity, the reference family will simply be denoted
\mathfrak{R}_{\alpha}
or even
\mathfrak{R}
. The JER of a reference family is an error criterion that takes the following form:

\JER(\mathfrak{R})=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists k\in\mathcal{K},|R_{k}\cap\mathcal{H}_{0}|>\zeta_{k}}}\right)=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists k\in\mathcal{K},V(R_{k})>\zeta_{k}}}\right).

(10)

Think of each
\zeta_{k}
as an over-estimator of the number of false discoveries in
R_{k}
. Then the JER is the probability that this upper bound fails for at least one
k
. If the JER is small, then, with high probability, the
\zeta_{k}
’s are simultaneous upper bounds of the false discoveries in all the regions
R_{k}
’s.

We are then interested in constructing reference families that control the JER at any target level, the inequation that we want to see satisfied is then:

\forall\mu\in\mathfrak{F},\forall\alpha\in(0,1),\;\JER_{\mu}(\mathfrak{R}_{\alpha})\leq\alpha,

or, in other words, at any given
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\forall\mu\in\mathfrak{F},\;\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall k\in\mathcal{K},V(R_{k})\leq\zeta_{k}}}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(11)

Note that Equation (
11
) is really similar to Equation (
2
) except that the uniform guarantee, instead of being over all
S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, is only over all the
R_{k}\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket,k\in\mathcal{K}
, with
\mathcal{K}
having cardinality potentially much smaller than
2^{m}
. A simultaneous confidence bound over all subsets of hypotheses is then derived from a JER-controlling reference family by interpolation. Let

\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket:\forall k\in\mathcal{K},|R_{k}\cap A|\leq\zeta_{k}}}\right\}.

(12)

The JER control informs us that, with high probability,
\mathcal{H}_{0}\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R})
. We leverage this information with the following confidence bound construction:

\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(S)=\max_{A\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R})}|S\cap A|,

(13)

which optimally uses the information provided by the JER control of the reference family, as proven by Proposition 2.1 of
7
. Note that there exists an equivalence between constructions of confidence envelopes based on reference families and on the inversion procedure
based on local tests. The links between the two approaches are discussed in
8
.

Because of the combinatorial
maximum operation
\max_{A\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R})}
,
the computation of
\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(S)
is intractable in general (see Proposition 2.2 of
7
), but for specific structures of reference families introduced in the next paragraph, a polynomial computation can be achieved.

First, if the set
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{R_{k}:k\in\mathcal{K}}}\right\}
is totally ordered for the inclusion relation, that is, the
R_{k}
’s are nested, then, for all
S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
,

\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(S)=\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\big(\zeta_{k}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S\setminus R_{k}}}\right\rvert\big)\wedge|S|,

(14)

by Proposition 2.5 of
7
. Second, if the reference family satisfies the forest structure constraint introduced by
16
and defined by:

\forall k,k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K},\;R_{k}\cap R_{k^{\prime}}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{R_{k},R_{k^{\prime}},\varnothing}}\right\},

then fast algorithms also exist to compute
\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(S)
for a single
S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket

(
16
, Algorithm 1)
or, even better, to compute
\big(\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(S_{t})\big)_{1\leq t\leq m}
for a path of nested hypotheses subsets
S_{1}\subsetneq\dotsb\subsetneq S_{m}

(
17
, Algorithm 4)
. Note that the forest structure condition encompasses the nested condition.

We can now recall the three settings that have been studied for the construction of reference families that control the JER. They will be studied again in the context of heterogeneity in
Section
6
.

3.3.2
k
-FWER setting

The first setting is the “
k
-FWER” setting, in which we set
\mathcal{K}=\llbracket 1,K\rrbracket
for some
K\leq m
,
\zeta_{k}=k-1
and
R_{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket:p_{i}\leq\tau_{k}}}\right\}
, with a threshold family
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau_{k}}}\right)_{1\leq k\leq K}
that is nondecreasing and such that the JER control holds. By monotonicity of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau_{k}}}\right)_{1\leq k\leq K}
, such reference family satisfies the nested condition so
Equation (
14
)
holds and an algorithm given by
20
can be applied for fast computation of

8
Conclusion and perspectives

In this paper, we introduced new confidence envelopes for false discoveries by incorporating heterogeneous upper bounds on the cumulative distributive functions of null distribution. Our approach bridges key frameworks in the existing literature on confidence envelopes — specifically the JER approach
(
7
)
and the inversion procedure using
local tests
(
22
;
26
)
.

We established generic novel shortcuts for the inversion procedure, providing significant new insights even in the standard homogeneous case. For instance, applied to the complete set of hypotheses,
the new shortcuts are exact (they coincide with the
theoretical inversion procedure) and thus provide
optimal (over-)estimators for
m_{0}
. Furthermore,
using these over-estimators as plug-ins into the
JER framework yields a bound which coincides for all
rejection sets with
the inversion procedure in the homogeneous case, formally validating the optimality of several previous constructions from
36
.

The methodologies introduced here extend naturally to the heterogeneous setting.
By combination with heterogeneous ecdf inequalities
(some of which are newly introduced in this work and
of independent interest), our novel shortcuts provide new confidence envelopes for heterogeneous tests and yield over-estimators for the number of null hypotheses.
These bounds are less conservative
than those obtained via a naive homogenization of the local test family. Consequently, when plugged in into the JER methodology, these estimators provide sharper, more efficient shortcuts.
Additionally, our new shortcuts for the inversion procedure also produce alternative confidence envelopes that can be less conservative than their JER counterparts in specific cases.

We validated our methods with numerical experiments highlighting the power gain of using envelopes tailored to the heterogeneity of the data compared to previous approaches designed for the homogeneous case.

A promising direction for future research involves deriving heterogeneous versions of the inequalities proposed by
33
or the uniform Wellner inequality from
36
. Since these bounds outperform heterogeneous ones in specific
p
-value regions without requiring prior distributional information, getting heterogeneous versions could further enhance the power of these envelopes in heterogeneous context.

C.1
Top-

Recall that in the top-
k

with

)

Proposition C.1
.

Let
\zeta_{k}=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)\wedge k
for all
k\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, where

Let the following reference families:
\mathfrak{R}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{k},\zeta_{k}}}\right)
,
\mathfrak{R}^{\sim}=\Big(R_{k},f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)\Big)
, and
\mathfrak{R}^{\dagger}=\Big(R_{k}^{\dagger},f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)\Big)
.

Then, for all
S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
,

\displaystyle\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(S)

\displaystyle=V^{*}_{\mathfrak{R}^{\sim}}(S)

(50)

\displaystyle=V^{*}_{\mathfrak{R}^{\dagger}}(S).

(51)

Note that the statement of
subsection C.1
is purely deterministic. Also note that the statement of
subsection C.1
implies that the choice of the permutation
\sigma
that orders the
p
-values is irrelevant, given that
\mathfrak{R}^{\dagger}
does not depend on
\sigma
.

Proof.

First, note that for any
A\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}}}\right\rvert\leq\zeta_{k}=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)\wedge k

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}}}\right\rvert\leq f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}}}\right\rvert\leq|R_{k}|=k
. This implies that
\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R})=\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R}^{\sim})
(recall Equation (
12
)). And so

for all

, by Equation (
13
). Hence (
50
) holds.

Note that

and so if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}^{\dagger}}}\right\rvert\leq f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)
, then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}}}\right\rvert\leq f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)

.

Denote

and recall that

for all

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(g(k))}}}\right)=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)
and

for all

. Second, note that if

and

, then

with

by definition of

, which is a contradiction, and so

. Let
A\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R}^{\sim})
. For all

,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}^{\dagger}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{g(k)}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(g(k))}}}\right)

\displaystyle=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right),

so

and
\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R}^{\sim})\subseteq\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R}^{\dagger})
.

To conclude,
\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R}^{\sim})=\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R}^{\dagger})
, and so
V^{*}_{\mathfrak{R}^{\dagger}}(S)=V^{*}_{\mathfrak{R}^{\sim}}(S)
for all

51
) holds.
∎

k

C.2.1
From
k
-FWER to top-
k

In this Section, we show that from every reference family in the
k
-FWER setting, a top-
k
family can be built that leads to the same confidence envelope.

Let
\mathfrak{R}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{k},k-1}}\right)_{1\leq k\leq K}
a reference family in the
k
-FWER setting, which means that for all
k\leq K
,
R_{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i:p_{i}\leq t_{k}}}\right\}
for some nondecreasing sequence
(t_{k})_{1\leq k\leq K}
.

We introduce the following notation: for two integers
a\leq b
,
\llparenthesis a,b\rrbracket=\llbracket a,b\rrbracket\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a}}\right\}
. In particular, note that
\llparenthesis a,b\rrbracket=\varnothing
if
a=b
.

The construction of the top-
k
family is the following. Let
\sigma
a (random) permutation that orders the
p
-values, and
R_{\ell}^{\sigma}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma(1),\dotsc,\sigma(\ell)}}\right\}
. We let
\ell_{0}=0
,
\ell_{K+1}=m
and, for any
k\in\llbracket 1,K\rrbracket
,

\ell_{k}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\tilde{\ell}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket:p_{(\tilde{\ell})}\leq t_{k}}}\right\}.

For any
k\in\llbracket 1,K\rrbracket
and any
\ell\in\llparenthesis\ell_{k-1},\ell_{k}\rrbracket
, we let
\zeta^{\sigma}_{\ell}=k-1
. For any
\ell\in\llparenthesis\ell_{K},\ell_{K+1}\rrbracket
, we let
\zeta^{\sigma}_{\ell}=m
. Finally, we let
\mathfrak{R}^{\sigma}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{\ell}^{\sigma},\zeta_{\ell}^{\sigma}}}\right)_{1\leq\ell\leq m}
, which is indeed built according to the top-
k
setting.

An example of such construction, that highlights some formal difficulties, is given below (
subsubsection C.2.1
).

Proposition C.2
.

For all
S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
,
\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(S)=V^{*}_{\mathfrak{R}^{\sigma}}(S)
.

Like
subsection C.1
, this Proposition is purely deterministic. The proof uses arguments similar to the proof of
subsection C.1
, that is, the use of Equations (
12
) and (
13
).

Proof.

First note that, for all
k\in\llbracket 1,K\rrbracket
,
R_{k}=R^{\sigma}_{\ell_{k}}
. Indeed, if
p_{i}\leq t_{k}
, then
i=\sigma(\ell)
with
\ell\leq\ell_{k}
by definition of
\ell_{k}
as a maximum, and conversely, for all
\ell\leq\ell_{k}
,
p_{\sigma(\ell)}\leq p_{\sigma(\ell_{k})}\leq t_{k}
.

Second, note that for all
k\in\llbracket 1,K\rrbracket
,
\zeta^{\sigma}_{\ell_{k}}\leq k-1
. Indeed, let
k\in\llbracket 1,K\rrbracket
. Given that

\bigcup_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}\llparenthesis\ell_{k^{\prime}-1},\ell_{k^{\prime}}\rrbracket=\llbracket 1,\ell_{k}\rrbracket,

there exists
k^{\prime}
,
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq k
, such that
\ell_{k}\in\llparenthesis\ell_{k^{\prime}-1},\ell_{k^{\prime}}\rrbracket
. Then, by definition,
\zeta^{\sigma}_{\ell_{k}}=k^{\prime}-1\leq k-1
.

Now, let
A\in\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}}}\right)
. Let any
\ell\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, there exists a unique
k\in\llbracket 1,K+1\rrbracket
such that
\ell\in\llparenthesis\ell_{k-1},\ell_{k}\rrbracket
. If
k=K+1
, then
\zeta^{\sigma}_{\ell}=m
and so obviously
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R^{\sigma}_{\ell}}}\right\rvert\leq m=\zeta^{\sigma}_{\ell}
. If
k\leq K
, then
\zeta^{\sigma}_{\ell}=k-1
. By the first remark,
R_{k}=R^{\sigma}_{\ell_{k}}
, and given that
R^{\sigma}_{\ell}\subseteq R^{\sigma}_{\ell_{k}}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R^{\sigma}_{\ell}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R^{\sigma}_{\ell_{k}}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq k-1\text{ because }A\in\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{\sigma}_{\ell}.

This proves that
\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}}}\right)\subseteq\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}^{\sigma}}}\right)
.

Let
A\in\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}^{\sigma}}}\right)
. Let any
k\in\llbracket 1,K\rrbracket
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R^{\sigma}_{\ell_{k}}}}\right\rvert\text{ by the first remark}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta^{\sigma}_{\ell_{k}}\text{ because }A\in\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}^{\sigma}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq k-1\text{ by the second remark}.

This proves that
\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}^{\sigma}}}\right)\subseteq\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}}}\right)
.

In the end,
\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}}}\right)=\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}^{\sigma}}}\right)
and so
\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(\cdot)=V^{*}_{\mathfrak{R}^{\sigma}}(\cdot)
.
∎

Let
\mathfrak{R}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{k},\zeta_{k}}}\right)_{1\leq k\leq K}

\zeta_{k}
is of the form

The construction of the

-FWER family is the following. By convention, we let
p_{(0)}=-1
and
f(-1)=0
. We then let, for each

\nu(s)=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{k\in\llbracket 0,m\rrbracket:f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)\leq s-1}}\right\}.

For any

and

is nondecreasing because

, and so the sequence
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{s}}}\right)_{s\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket}

\mathfrak{R}^{\nu}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{s}^{\nu},s-1}}\right)_{1\leq s\leq m}
, which is indeed built according to the
k
-FWER setting.

Proposition C.5
.

Let
\zeta_{k}=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)\wedge k
for all
k\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, where
f
is any measurable, nondecreasing function with integer values.

Then, for all
S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
,
\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(S)=V^{*}_{\mathfrak{R}^{\nu}}(S)
.

Like
subsection C.1
, this Proposition is purely deterministic. The proof uses arguments similar to the proof of
subsection C.1
, that is, the use of Equations (
12
) and (
13
).

Proof.

First note that for any
s\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
, if
\nu(s)\neq 0
,
R^{\nu}_{s}=R_{\nu(s)}
. Indeed, if
i\in R^{\nu}_{s}
, then
p_{i}\leq p_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu(s)}}\right)}
and
i=\sigma(\ell)
for some
\ell\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
. Hence
p_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell}}\right)}\leq p_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu(s)}}\right)}
,
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell}}\right)}}}\right)\leq f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu(s)}}\right)}}}\right)\leq s-1
because
f
is nondecreasing, and
\ell\leq\nu(s)
by definition of
\nu(s)
, hence
i\in R_{\nu(s)}
. Conversely, if
i\in R_{\nu(s)}
, this means that
i=\sigma(\ell)
for some
\ell\leq\nu(s)
, and so
p_{i}=p_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell}}\right)}\leq p_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu(s)}}\right)}
and
i\in R^{\nu}_{s}
.

Let
A\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R})
, and
s\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
. If
\nu(s)=0
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R^{\nu}_{s}}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap\varnothing}}\right\rvert=0\leq s-1
. If
\nu(s)\neq 0
, by the previous remark,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R^{\nu}_{s}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{\nu(s)}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\zeta_{\nu(s)}\text{ because }A\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R})

\displaystyle\leq f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu(s)}}\right)}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq s-1,

so, in any case,
A\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R}^{\nu})
.

Let
A\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R}^{\nu})
, and let
k\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
. Note that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}}}\right\rvert\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|R_{k}}}\right\rvert=k
, so if
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)\geq k
,
\zeta_{k}=k
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}}}\right\rvert\leq\zeta_{k}
. Now assume that
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)\leq k-1
, which implies that
k\leq\nu(k)
. Let

s^{*}=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{s\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket:k\leq\nu(s)}}\right\},

which is well-defined, because the set
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{s\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket:k\leq\nu(s)}}\right\}
is non-empty because it contains
k
, as we just emphasized. We distinguish cases, between
s^{*}=1
and
s^{*}>1
. If
s^{*}=1
,
k\leq\nu(1)
and so
0\leq f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)\leq f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(\nu(1))}}}\right)\leq 0
and so
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)=0
. If
s^{*}>1
, we have
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{*}-1}}\right)<k\leq\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{*}}}\right)
and so
s^{*}-2<f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)\leq s^{*}-1
. Given that
f
only takes integer values, in both cases,
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right)=s^{*}-1
. Also note that
k\leq\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{*}}}\right)
implies that both
R_{k}\subseteq R_{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{*}}}\right)}
and
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{*}}}\right)\neq 0
, so
R_{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{*}}}\right)}=R^{\nu}_{s^{*}}
by the previous remark. Finally,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R^{\nu}_{s^{*}}}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq s^{*}-1\text{ because }A\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R}^{\nu})

\displaystyle=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{(k)}}}\right),

so
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A\cap R_{k}}}\right\rvert\leq\zeta_{k}
and
A\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{R})
.

In the end,
\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}}}\right)=\mathfrak{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{R}^{\nu}}}\right)
and so
\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(\cdot)=V^{*}_{\mathfrak{R}^{\nu}}(\cdot)
.
∎

Namely, let
\mathfrak{R}
the reference family in the

Corollary C.7
.

For all
S\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
,
\hat{V}^{\JER}_{\mathfrak{R}}(S)=V^{*}_{\mathfrak{R}^{\top}}(S)

Proof.

First note that, just like we proved (

, where

\mathfrak{R}^{{\ddagger}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R^{\sigma}_{\ell},\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{p_{(\ell)}>0}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{mp_{(\ell)}}{\alpha}^{-}}}\right\rfloor\wedge m}}\right)_{\ell\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket},

that is, we just replaced the “
\wedge\ell
” by “
\wedge m
”. The remainder of the proof consists in showing that
\mathfrak{R}^{{\ddagger}}
is exactly the family
\mathfrak{R}^{\sigma}
constructed in
Section
C.2.1
from
\mathfrak{R}
(hence

here), that is, that
\zeta^{\sigma}_{\ell}=\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{p_{(\ell)}>0}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{mp_{(\ell)}}{\alpha}^{-}}}\right\rfloor\wedge m
for all
\ell\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket

subsubsection C.2.1
.

Let
\ell\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket 1,m}}\right\rrbracket
. Let
k\in\llbracket 1,m+1\rrbracket
the unique integer such that
\ell\in\llparenthesis\ell_{k-1},\ell_{k}\rrbracket
. We distinguish the three cases

,

with
p_{(\ell)}>0
, and

.

First, if
p_{(\ell)}=0
,
\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{p_{(\ell)}>0}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{mp_{(\ell)}}{\alpha}^{-}}}\right\rfloor\wedge m=0
. Given that

.

Second, assume that

Third, if

Appendix D
Uniformization of super-uniform
p
-values

We present the following
Appendix D
, that explains how to transform any random variable into a uniform variable, which is, additionally in the case of super-uniformity, smaller than the first one.
This construction encompasses the classical “randomized
p
-value” construction, as stated by
Appendix D
.

Note that this Lemma also offers an alternative way to extend the validity domain of various concentration inequalities from uniform variables to super-uniform variables (see Propositions
3.1
and
3.1
), by providing a different coupling construction than in
subsection 3.1
.

Lemma D.1

(Uniformization of any random variable)
.

Let
Y
any real-valued random variable, let
F
its cdf and
F^{-}
the left-limit function of
F
. Assume that the probability space where
Y
is defined is rich enough so that there exists
U
another random variable independent from
Y
, with uniform distribution:
U\sim\mathcal{U}([0,1])
. Define
\widetilde{Y}
as

\widetilde{Y}=F^{-}(Y)+U(F(Y)-F^{-}(Y)).

Then
\widetilde{Y}\sim\mathcal{U}([0,1])
.

Furthermore, if
Y
is super-uniform:
Y\succeq\mathcal{U}([0,1])
, then
\widetilde{Y}\leq Y
almost surely.

Proof.

We prove that the cdf
\widetilde{F}
of
\widetilde{Y}
is
x\mapsto 0\vee(x\wedge 1)
.

First note that
\widetilde{Y}\in[0,1]
a.s., so
\widetilde{F}(x)=1
for all
x\in[1,\infty)
and
\widetilde{F}(x)=0
for all
x\in(-\infty,0)
. So let
x\in[0,1)
and let us show that
\widetilde{F}(x)=x
. We distinguish two cases: if
x\in F(\mathbb{R})
or not.

In the first case, there exists
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
x=F(\beta)
. We remark the following way to write
\widetilde{F}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F^{-}(Y)+U(F(Y)-F^{-}(Y))\leq x}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F(Y)\leq x}}\right\}}+\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F^{-}(Y)\leq x<F(Y)}}\right\}}\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{U(F(Y)-F^{-}(Y))\leq x-F^{-}(Y)}}\right\}}}}\right]

Now, if
F^{-}(Y)\leq x<F(Y)
is true, then
F(\beta)<F(Y)
. Because
F
is nondecreasing, necessarily
\beta<Y
, which in turn implies that
F(\beta)\leq F^{-}(Y)
: in the end,
x=F(\beta)=F^{-}(Y)
, and
F(Y)>F^{-}(Y)
, so
\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{U(F(Y)-F^{-}(Y))\leq x-F^{-}(Y)}}\right\}}=\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{U=0}}\right\}}=0
a.s. So
\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F^{-}(Y)\leq x<F(Y)}}\right\}}\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{U(F(Y)-F^{-}(Y))\leq x-F^{-}(Y)}}\right\}}=0
a.s., and

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F(Y)\leq x}}\right\}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(F(Y)\leq x\big)

\displaystyle=x,

where the last equality comes from Proposition 2 of Chapter 1 of
41
, thanks to
x
being in the range of
F
.

Now we turn to the case where
x\not\in F(\mathbb{R})
. Let
q_{x}
the
x
-level quantile of
F
:
q_{x}=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:F(t)\geq x}}\right\}
. By the usual properties of quantiles,
F^{-}(q_{x})\leq x
and
F(q_{x})\geq x
, and we even have
F(q_{x})>x
because
x\not\in F(\mathbb{R})
.

We now show that
\widetilde{Y}\leq x
if and only if either
Y<q_{x}
, or
Y=q_{x}
and
U(F(q_{x})-F^{-}(q_{x}))\leq x-F^{-}(q_{x})
. Assume that
\widetilde{Y}\leq x
. Note that
\widetilde{Y}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F^{-}(Y),F(Y)}}\right]
and so
F^{-}(Y)\leq x
. If we had
Y>q_{x}
, then we would have
F^{-}(Y)\geq F(q_{x})
, and so
x\geq F^{-}(Y)\geq F(q_{x})>x
which would be a contradiction. So
Y\leq q_{x}
and the implication follows. Conversely, assume the other proposition. Obviously, if
Y=q_{x}
and
U(F(q_{x})-F^{-}(q_{x}))\leq x-F^{-}(q_{x})
then
\widetilde{Y}\leq x
. If
Y<q_{x}
, then
\widetilde{Y}\leq F(Y)\leq F^{-}(q_{x})\leq x
.

Thanks to the equivalence, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y<q_{x}}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{Y=q_{x}}}\right\}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{U(F(q_{x})-F^{-}(q_{x}))\leq x-F^{-}(q_{x})}}\right\}\Big)

\displaystyle=F^{-}(q_{x})+\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y=q_{x}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U(F(q_{x})-F^{-}(q_{x}))\leq x-F^{-}(q_{x})}}\right),

by independence of
Y
and
U
. Note that
F^{-}(q_{x})\leq x<F(q_{x})
, so
\frac{x-F^{-}(q_{x})}{F(q_{x})-F^{-}(q_{x})}
is well defined and in
[0,1]
. Then,

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}(x)

\displaystyle=F^{-}(q_{x})+\big(F(q_{x})-F^{-}(q_{x})\big)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U\leq\frac{x-F^{-}(q_{x})}{F(q_{x})-F^{-}(q_{x})}}}\right)

\displaystyle=F^{-}(q_{x})+\big(F(q_{x})-F^{-}(q_{x})\big)\frac{x-F^{-}(q_{x})}{F(q_{x})-F^{-}(q_{x})}

\displaystyle=x.

This concludes that
\widetilde{Y}\sim\mathcal{U}([0,1])
.

Now assume that
Y\succeq\mathcal{U}([0,1])
, which means that
F(x)\leq 0\vee(x\wedge 1)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
F(x)\leq x
for all
x\geq 0
, and
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y\leq 0}}\right)=0
, so
Y\geq 0
a.s. Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}

\displaystyle=F^{-}(Y)+U(F(Y)-F^{-}(Y))

\displaystyle\leq F(Y)\text{ a.s.}

\displaystyle\leq Y\text{ a.s.,}

which concludes.
∎
