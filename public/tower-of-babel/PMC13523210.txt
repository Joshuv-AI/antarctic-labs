Background

Glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is the most common enzymopathy in humans, with approximately 400 million known cases worldwide [1]. It is inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern and primarily affects males.

G6PD plays a crucial role in maintaining redox homeostasis by preventing excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. In individuals with G6PD deficiency, exposure to infections, certain drugs, or foods, can lead to ROS accumulation, resulting in red blood cell (RBC) damage and hemolysis [1]. Although hematologic implications of G6PD are well characterized, recent evidence suggests that this enzyme may also have a broader function such as bacterial clearance, immune regulation, and epithelial repair [2]. In other words, G6PD is not only necessary for erythrocytes function, but also for redox balance in the oxidative bursts of macrophages. Therefore, the deficiency of this enzyme makes the patient prone to infections [3]. Moreover, patients with G6PD deficiencies are at higher risk of getting immune-related disorders [4].

Here, we present a case of recurrent perianal abscess and fistula in a patient with G6PD deficiency, raising the possibility of a novel link between G6PD dysfunction and chronic perianal disease.

Case presentation

A 16-year-old male was referred to the genetics clinic with a five-year history of sharp, burning perianal pain and purulent discharge exacerbated by sitting. The patient reported a relapsing-remitting course of these symptoms over this period.

The patient’s past medical history was unremarkable, with no history of chronic constipation, difficult defecation, prolonged straining, anal fissures, or other anorectal disorders. Moreover, he did not mention any chronic illnesses or systemic diseases. His family history was significant since both his mother and brother had previously been diagnosed with perianal fistula secondary to an abscess. There was no history of any medication use, smoking, or substance abuse.

The patient first developed perianal pain and purulent discharge at approximately 11 years of age. Following diagnosis of perianal abscess and fistula, the first surgical intervention was performed two months after symptom onset, during which an intersphincteric fistula was identified and abscess drainage was undertaken. Due to recurrence of symptoms in the same location, a second surgical procedure with seton placement and abscess drainage was performed approximately ten months later, resulting in temporary symptom resolution. The patient remained asymptomatic for approximately four years, after which recurrence of perianal pain and discharge occurred at 16 years of age, leading to referral to the genetics clinic because of recurrent disease and positive family history.

On physical examination, the patient was febrile and tachycardic. An erythematous, fluctuant, tender perianal swelling, drained via an external fistula opening, was identified on the left lateral aspect of the anal canal, 2 cm from the anus (Fig. 1). He was otherwise normal on the examination.

In the laboratory data, complete blood count (CBC) showed only anemia (hemoglobin 12.5 g/dL, reference range 13–17 g/dL) while serum iron was 52 µg/dL (reference range 50–150 µg/dL), total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) 313 µg/dL (reference range 300–360 µg/dL), ferritin 141 µg/L (reference range 20–250 µg/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) 23 mg/L (reference range < 6 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 60 mm/h (reference range < 10 mm/h). Immunological studies ruled out viral infections, particularly HIV. Stool calprotectin was also elevated (90 µg/g, reference range < 50 µg/g).

Abdominal and bladder ultrasound studies were normal, revealing no evidence of intra-abdominal or urinary pathology. Endoanal ultrasound (EAUS) identified a hypoechoic collection measuring 20 × 23 mm in the posterior aspect of mid-anal canal, with an intersphincteric fistula tract extending from this collection into the rectal lumen (Fig. 2).

Given the differential diagnoses, the patient underwent a colonoscopy and multiple tissue biopsies were obtained. Histopathological assessment revealed focal active chronic ileitis without granulomas. While multiple aphthous-like ulcers were visible in the terminal ileum (Fig. 3), the absence of major histological findings precluded a definitive diagnosis of Crohn’s disease, although it remained a potential consideration. Hence, a repeat colonoscopy was performed 9 months later which showed no persistent aphthous ulcers, skip lesions, cobblestoning, strictures, or mucosal inflammation throughout the colon and terminal ileum. Histopathological evaluation revealed no granulomas, crypt architectural distortion, basal plasmacytosis, or chronic active inflammatory changes. These findings did not fulfil the diagnostic criteria of Crohn’s disease at the time of follow-up. Moreover, inflammatory markers including CRP (3 mg/L, reference range < 6 mg/L), ESR (6 mm/h, reference range < 10 mm/h), and stool calprotectin (23 µg/g, reference range < 50 µg/g) were normalized at this point. Collectively, these longitudinal findings did not fulfill the diagnostic criteria for Crohn’s disease during follow-up.

Finally, due to the recurrence of symptoms and a similar family history of perianal disease in his mother and brother, whole exome sequencing (WES) was requested to identify potential underlying genetic factors. WES was performed by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea) using the SureSelect Human All Exon V7 capture kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (150-bp paired-end reads). Sequencing achieved a mean target coverage of approximately 100×. Sequence alignment and variant calling were performed according to the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) Best Practices workflow using the GRCh37 (hg19) reference genome. Whole-exome sequencing identified a hemizygous pathogenic variant in the G6PD gene, NM_000402.4(G6PD): c.653 C > T (p.Ser218Phe; rs5030868), corresponding to the G6PD Mediterranean variant while no additional clinically significant variants were reported. This variant is categorized as pathogenic/likely pathogenic based on ACMG classifications.

Despite normal lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level (210 U/L, reference range < 720 U/L) and reticulocyte (1%, reference range 0.50–1.5%), quantitative enzyme testing demonstrated reduced G6PD activity of 3 U/g Hb (reference range 8–12 U/g), confirming G6PD deficiency.

The patient refused any surgical interventions due to prior failures. Hence, he was managed with a five-day course of amoxicillin-clavulanate (875/125 mg orally every 8 h), a standard regimen for perianal abscess, together with preventive recommendations related to G6PD deficiency. Remarkably, the patient demonstrated clinical improvement, with a significant reduction in both perianal pain and size of abscess.

Discussion and Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case describing the co-occurrence of G6PD deficiency and recurrent perianal abscess and fistula. However, emerging evidence suggests potential pathophysiological links between this enzymatic defect and chronic inflammatory conditions.

Recent evidence has moved beyond hematologic role of G6PD, suggesting that G6PD deficiency does not necessarily present with hemolytic manifestations, similar to our patients who lacked typical clinical features of G6PD deficiency. Severe cases of G6PD deficiency can exhibit a phenotypic overlap with chronic granulomatous disease (CGD), including manifestations such as inflammatory bowel disease and recurrent infections [2]. Agudelo-Flórez et al. reported a case of comorbid G6PD deficiency and CGD in a patient with anemia and recurrent infections [5]. Sun et al. further demonstrated that severe G6PD can present with CGD-like symptoms which occurred as recurrent respiratory infections in a male child. They also reviewed the literature which showed further evidence on the association of recurrent infections in G6PD deficiency [3]. Siler et al. presented 3 cases of G6PD deficiency and claimed that it causes susceptibility to infections due to absent NETosis [6]. Furthermore, Roos et al. showed that some mutations in G6PD gene (including G6PD Mediterranean c.653 C > T; p.Ser218Phe which was found in our patient), can cause NADPH impair and thus to insufficient activity of NADPH oxidase, which in turn lead to CGD-like symptoms [7].

Beyond immune dysfunction, other mechanisms can justify the association of perianal abscess and fistula with G6PD deficiency. Several studies have demonstrated that G6PD deficiency impairs intestinal epithelial cell regeneration due to reduced NADPH levels and increase inflammatory cytokine signalling [8–10]. This compromised barrier function and dysregulated immune response may contribute to an increased incidence of immune-related disorders in these patients [2].

Collectively, these findings provide a plausible biological basis for how a G6PD variant might predispose individuals to chronic, recurrent perianal abscess or fistula. Furthermore, the occurrence of similar perianal disease in the patient’s mother and brother, along with the the X-linked inheritance pattern of G6PD deficiency, raises the possibility of a familial predisposition.

However, a major limitation of this report is the absence of genetic testing in affected family members. Without segregation analysis, the observed familial clustering cannot be attributed to the identified G6PD variant, and the proposed inheritance pattern should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. In addition to this limitation, no small bowel imaging or oxidative-burst assay such as a dihydrorhodamine assay was performed.

The correlation between chronic perianal inflammation, immune dysfunction, and impaired epithelial repair mechanisms warrants further investigation. This is particularly relevant for patients with recurrent anorectal suppuration in whom inflammatory bowel disease remains unconfirmed despite longitudinal evaluation. This case report highlights a potential association between G6PD deficiency and recurrent perianal abscess and fistula in an adolescent with a positive family history. Although a causal relationship cannot be established definitively, the findings support the hypothesis that immune dysfunction and impaired infection control secondary to G6PD deficiency may contribute to chronic perianal pathology. Further research is warranted to determine whether G6PD variants represent a novel genetic susceptibility factor in the pathogenesis of perianal abscess or fistula.
