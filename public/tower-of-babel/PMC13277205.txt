Concurrent septic shock and massive renal hematoma following flexible ureteroscopy resulting in emergency nephrectomy: a case report
Authors: Elsayed Abdelhalim, Mohamed Galal, Ali Ibrahim, Mohamed Abd Elaty, Khaled Magdy Zeinelabden, Hossam Nabeeh, Diaa-Eldin Taha, Tarek Abdelbaky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFlexible ureteroscopy (FURS) is widely considered a safe and effective modality for renal stone management. Severe infectious and hemorrhagic complications are uncommon but may be life-threatening.Case-presentationA 75-year-old man was referred to our tertiary center with hemodynamic instability following FURS performed at an outside institution for the treatment of a renal calculus. He presented with septic shock and acute anemia. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a massive perirenal hematoma compressing the renal parenchyma. Despite aggressive resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and intensive care management, the patient’s clinical condition deteriorated. Emergency nephrectomy was performed as a life-saving intervention.ConclusionThis case highlights the potential for rare combined septic and hemorrhagic complications following FURS. Early recognition and timely referral to specialized centers are critical to reducing mortality and optimizing patient outcomes.

Introduction

Renal urolithiasis is a common disease. Several treatment modalities are available for the management of renal stones, including minimally invasive procedures such as percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), Flexible ureteroscopy (FURS), and extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL), as well as laparoscopic and open surgery [1].

Flexible ureteroscopy (FURS) has emerged as a first-line treatment modality for renal stones ≤ 2 cm and proximal ureteral stones ≥ 1 cm [2]. In these settings, FURS is associated with fewer complications than PCNL and improved stone clearance with fewer repeat sessions than ESWL [3].

In this report, we describe a rare and severe case of massive renal hematoma requiring nephrectomy following Flexible ureteroscopy (FURS).

Case presentation

A 75-year-old man presented to the emergency department with right flank pain and persistent hematuria. He had undergone Flexible ureteroscopy (FURS) with ureteric stent placement for the treatment of kidney stones at another urology center 1 week before presentation. He had no relevant past medical history. After the procedure, he had been taking only analgesics and antibiotics.

On examination, the patient was tachycardic and tachypneic, with a blood pressure of 110/70 mmHg, heart rate of 120 beats/min, respiratory rate of 27 breaths/min, low-grade fever, and oxygen saturation ranging from 94% to 97% on room air. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated anemia, with a hemoglobin level of 8.3 g/dL and a platelet count of 299 × 10³/µL. Urinalysis revealed gross hematuria. Serum creatinine was mildly elevated at 1.5 mg/dL, while the international normalized ratio (INR) was within normal limits at 1.2. An abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) scan was performed to assess for possible internal injury and revealed a massive right-sided retroperitoneal hematoma (Fig. 1). The patient received transfusion of two units of ABO-compatible packed red blood cells.

Non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) showing a large right perinephric hematoma

The patient was subsequently admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close monitoring and hemodynamic support. Despite transfusion of two units of packed red blood cells, his hemoglobin level further declined to 7.5 g/dL on the first day of ICU admission. In addition, the platelet count decreased to 135 × 10³/µL, and serum creatinine increased to 2.6 mg/dL.

He received an additional two units of packed red blood cells along with two units of fresh frozen plasma (FFP). Nevertheless, laboratory parameters continued to deteriorate. On the second day after admission, hemoglobin dropped to 7.0 g/dL, platelet count declined to 90 × 10³/µL, and serum creatinine rose further to 2.8 mg/dL.

Despite full conservative management, the patient’s vital signs gradually became unstable: blood pressure dropped to 80/50 mmHg, heart rate increased to 130 beats/min, and he developed a distended abdomen with ileus. Therefore, he was considered a candidate for urgent abdominal exploration.

He was transferred to the operating room. After induction of general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the flank position. A lumbar incision between the 11th and 12th ribs was made to explore the retroperitoneal space. During exploration, a huge retroperitoneal hematoma secondary to renal injury was identified. The indwelling double-J stent was found lying outside the ureter, and the ureter was completely avulsed from the renal pelvis, adherent to, and fixed over the psoas muscle (Fig. 2).

Removed right kidney and associated hematoma after nephrectomy, demonstrating the DJ stent emerging from the avulsed renal pelvis

Although no active extravasation was identified, the patient’s clinical deterioration and the presence of a severe renal pelvic injury made surgical intervention in the form of nephrectomy unavoidable. During the procedure, two additional units of packed red blood cells and one unit of fresh frozen plasma were transfused.

In the postoperative period, the patient’s condition improved significantly. Vital signs remained stable, and no further decline in hemoglobin level was observed. Drain output was minimal and insignificant. Renal function gradually improved, with serum creatinine decreasing to 1.3 mg/dL. No further blood transfusions were required. Bowel function resumed on postoperative day (POD) 1. The patient was discharged in stable condition on POD 5. At 2-week follow-up, the postoperative course was uneventful.

Discussion

Flexible ureteroscopy (FURS) has become a cornerstone in the management of renal calculi ≤ 2 cm because of its minimally invasive nature and high stone-free rates. Contemporary series report overall complication rates ranging from 3% to 16%, with the majority classified as minor (Clavien I–II) events such as transient fever and hematuria [4, 5].

Postoperative infectious complications remain among the most serious adverse events after FURS. The reported incidence of urosepsis ranges from 0.3% to 5%, depending on the patient risk profile and operative factors [6, 7]. The primary mechanism involves elevated intrarenal pressure during irrigation, leading to pyelovenous and pyelolymphatic backflow with systemic dissemination of bacteria and endotoxins [8]. Hong et al. demonstrated that pyelotubular and pyelovenous backflow may contribute to the ascending spread of uropathogens, and their findings suggest that intrarenal pressures of 90 mmHg are required for consistent bacterial pyelovenous backflow [9]. Even with negative preoperative urine cultures, infected stones and bacterial biofilms may act as reservoirs, releasing microorganisms during laser lithotripsy [10]. Identified risk factors include prolonged operative time, large stone burden, positive preoperative urine culture, diabetes mellitus, and high irrigation pressures [6, 11].

Hemorrhagic complications after FURS are rare. Subcapsular or perirenal hematoma has been reported in isolated case reports and small series, with an estimated incidence well below 1% [8]. Proposed mechanisms include forniceal rupture secondary to a sudden rise in intrarenal pressure, trauma from guidewires or ureteral access sheaths, laser-induced parenchymal injury, and excessive irrigation pressure [8]. Abrupt changes in intrapelvic pressure may lead to rapid distension and subsequent rupture of the renal parenchyma and/or capsular vessels. This process allows blood and fluid to collect within the subcapsular space, progressively separating the renal capsule from the underlying parenchyma and ultimately resulting in hematoma formation [12]. Although most reported hematomas are managed conservatively, severe cases requiring intervention have been described [8].

The coexistence of septic shock and massive renal hematoma in the present case likely represents a synergistic “dual-hit” phenomenon. Sepsis induces endothelial dysfunction, a systemic inflammatory response, and coagulopathy, which may predispose to hemorrhage [13]. Conversely, a large hematoma may serve as an infected collection, perpetuating the systemic inflammatory response and worsening hemodynamic instability. This vicious cycle may explain the rapid clinical deterioration observed in our patient, ultimately necessitating emergency nephrectomy as a life-saving intervention.

Ureteral avulsion represents one of the most catastrophic complications of flexible ureteroscopy. Analysis of the Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database has identified several potential mechanisms underlying this rare event. These include locked deflection of the flexible ureteroscope, distal tip dysfunction with bunching of the bending rubber, scabbard-related avulsion, and excessive traction during stone basketing. Awareness of these high-risk scenarios is essential to minimize the likelihood of such severe complications [14].

To the best of our knowledge, although isolated reports of post-FURS sepsis and subcapsular hematoma exist, the simultaneous occurrence of both complications leading to renal loss has not been previously described. Most published hematoma cases were managed conservatively or with drainage, and nephrectomy following FURS remains exceedingly rare [5, 15].

A focused literature search was performed, and the available reports show that although ureteroscopy is generally safe, rare but severe complications have been described. These include urosepsis, multi-organ failure, death, ureteral avulsion, urinary extravasation with urinoma formation, subcapsular, perirenal, and retroperitoneal hematoma, renal pseudoaneurysm, and intrarenal arteriovenous fistula. Therefore, the present case should be framed as an unusual and severe presentation within a limited body of published literature rather than as an entirely unprecedented event.

In 2016, Kao and Wang reported a mortality rate of 2.2% (1/45) among patients undergoing initial flexible ureteroscopy. The reported case involved a 67-year-old woman with significant comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and end-stage renal disease. Notably, aspirin therapy had not been discontinued before the procedure. Following flexible ureteroscopy for a 2.0 cm renal stone, the patient developed a massive and progressively expanding renal hematoma, necessitating emergency nephrectomy because of hemodynamic instability. Despite aggressive resuscitative measures, she died 2 weeks later from urosepsis and multiple organ failure [16].

Similarly, Cindolo et al. reported six deaths following retrograde intrarenal surgery, with causes attributed to urosepsis in four cases, anesthesia-related complications in one case, and hemorrhagic complications in another [17].

This case highlights several important preventive considerations. Strict control of intrarenal pressure during FURS is essential. The use of ureteral access sheaths, gravity-assisted or low-pressure irrigation systems, suction access sheaths such as the flexible and navigable sheath (FANS), or direct in-scope suction (DISS), limitation of operative time, and meticulous preoperative infection control may reduce the risk of severe complications [18–20]. Furthermore, early postoperative deterioration should prompt immediate imaging to exclude hemorrhagic events. Rapid multidisciplinary management, including intensive care support, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and timely surgical intervention, is crucial to prevent mortality.

Limitation

A key limitation of this report is the lack of detailed operative data from the index procedure, which limits definitive identification of the intraoperative factors involved. However, the clinical course and existing literature suggest several possible mechanisms, including elevated intrarenal pressure, instrumentation-related trauma, and infection-related factors.

Conclusion

Although FURS is widely regarded as safe and effective, this case underscores that catastrophic combined infectious and hemorrhagic complications, although extremely rare, can occur and may result in loss of the renal unit.
