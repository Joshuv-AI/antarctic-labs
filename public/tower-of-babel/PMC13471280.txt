Nitric-oxide propelled nanomotors sensitize tumors to ferroptosis immunotherapy via cholesterol depletion-induced tumor cells stiffening
Authors: Ke Ma, Chang Su, Linxi Wang, Xue Mi, Dan Wu, Zhimei Li, Le Yang, Minggao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the tremendous therapeutic promise of activating immunogenic ferroptosis, it can prime robust de novo T cell responses, biomechanically mediated immune suppression desensitizes the cytotoxicity of T cells against tumor cells. Herein, we developed a nitric oxide (NO)-propelled nanomotor (GFS@CAF) that co-delivers a ferroptosis inducer (GFS) and a cholesterol scavenger (β-cyclodextrin) to increase the sensitivity of tumors to ferroptosis immunotherapy by targeting deep penetration into tumor and depleting cholesterol to stiffen tumor cells. GFS@CAF nanomotors utilize the high level of H2O2 in the tumor microenvironment to generate NO, enabling self-propulsion and deep penetration into tumor by chemotaxis. GFS@CAF elevates reactive oxygen species levels and inactivate glutathione peroxidase 4, thereby promoting lipid peroxide accumulation to trigger immunogenic ferroptosis and initiate T cell responses. Meanwhile, membrane-anchored cyclodextrin depletes cholesterol to overcome the mechanical softness of cancer cell, which further enhances cytotoxic T lymphocyte-mediated cytotoxicity. In Hepa1-6 subcutaneous tumor-bearing mouse model, we successfully demonstrated that GFS@CAF led to significant tumor regression and established a durable immunological memory, with these mice surviving for two months. This study highlights the mechano-immunotherapeutic potential of combining cholesterol depletion and immunogenic ferroptosis for tumor treatment. Overall, our work presents a feasible approach for initiating robust T-cell-mediated anti-tumor killing in cancer immunotherapy.Keywords: Nanomotors, Depleting cholesterol, Tumor cell stiffening, Ferroptosis, Tumor immunotherapy

Introduction

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death [1]. Specifically, the iron-catalyzed Fenton reaction converts intracellular hydrogen peroxide into highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH), which oxidize polyunsaturated fatty acids within cellular membranes, leading to lipid peroxidation (LPO), membrane disruption, and eventual cell death [[2], [3], [4]]. Furthermore, ferroptotic cells release damage-associated molecular patterns, which bind to pattern recognition receptors, such as Toll-like receptors and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptors, on the surface of dendritic cells (DCs), triggering a T cell-mediated antitumor immune response, thereby amplifying ferroptosis in neighboring tumor cells and suppressing metastasis [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. However, ferroptosis still faces several challenges. Limited levels of endogenous reactive oxygen species (ROS) in cancer cells make it difficult to induce sufficient LPO to trigger ferroptosis [[9], [10], [11]]. Moreover, antioxidant substrates, such as glutathione (GSH) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), can efficiently scavenge generated lipid peroxides through multiple pathways, directly undermining the ferroptosis-inducing effect [[12], [13], [14]]. In particular, the cytotoxic activity of immunogenic ferroptosis-activated T cells against tumor cells is hindered by biomechanically mediated immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [15,16], leading to the suppression of their tumor cell-killing function. Therefore, overcoming these barriers is crucial for further amplifying susceptibility to ferroptosis, revitalizing ferroptosis-mediated antitumor immune responses, and ultimately improving the efficacy of cancer treatment.

Cholesterol is a key structural component of the cell membrane and is essential for maintaining membrane integrity and fluidity [17]. Elevated cholesterol levels promote the formation of lipid rafts, which can restrict the propagation of LPO, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis [18]. In addition to its role in ferroptosis resistance, cholesterol also modulates immune surveillance by altering the mechanical properties of cell membranes [16]. Tumor cells typically exhibit a higher membrane cholesterol content than normal cells, resulting in enhanced membrane flexibility [19,20]. Studies have suggested that mechanically stiffer tumor cells are more susceptible to cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)-mediated killing because T cells sense substrate stiffness and exert mechanical force at the immune synapse, facilitating perforin-dependent membrane penetration and tumor cell lysis [21,22]. Thus, reducing the amount of membrane cholesterol may serve a dual function: it lowers the threshold for ferroptosis induction by dismantling the lipid raft-associated defense barrier and enhances T cell cytotoxicity by modulating membrane mechanics, offering a promising target for the synergistic regulation of ferroptosis and antitumor immunity.

β-Cyclodextrin (βCD) has a natural host-guest recognition interaction with cholesterol and can specifically deplete cholesterol from cell membranes [23,24]. However, βCD suffers from poor tumor targeting, rapid clearance in vivo, and an inability to penetrate the dense extracellular matrix (ECM) of tumor tissue [[25], [26], [27]], making it difficult to effectively accumulate within tumors [28], and thereby weakening its ability to increase the stiffness of cancer cells. Micro/nanomotors capable of converting various energy sources (such as chemical, ultrasonic, light, or magnetic energy) into kinetic energy to achieve active motion have been rapidly developed to overcome this challenge [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. Nitric oxide (NO), a bioactive gas, has been extensively explored as a chemical propellant in nanomotors [34]. The guanidine group of L-arginine (L-Arg), an active functional group, can react with ROS in the TME to produce NO, providing possibilities for NO-propelled nanomotor applications in tumor treatment [35].

Herein, NO-propelled nanomotors that co-deliver a ferroptosis inducer and βCD were developed to increase sensitization of tumors to ferroptosis-induced immunotherapy by depleting cholesterol to stiffen tumor cells (Scheme 1). Specifically, a ferroptosis inducer (GFS) was constructed through the coordination-driven self-assembly of GOx, Fe3+ ions, and sorafenib, where the GOx catalyzed endogenous glucose into H2O2, which was converted by Fe3+ into cytotoxic •OH, synergistically inducing ferroptosis with sorafenib (an FDA-approved ferroptosis inducer). βCD and L-Arg-functionalized lipid molecules were also synthesized to encapsulate GFS into liposomes, termed GFS@CAF. The conversion of L-Arg into NO was catalyzed by upregulated ROS levels in the TME, where the evaluated ROS, as chemotactic agents, guide GFS@CAF to target tumors and penetrate deep into the tumor. Upon folate (FA) receptor-mediated cellular uptake, GFS@CAF undergoes pH-sensitive disassembly, where the dissociated sorafenib and the self-cyclic valence alternation between Fe3+ and Fe2+ enable GFS to exhaust GSH and consequently inactivate GPX4, smashing down the “shield” of tumor cells against ROS and promoting lipid peroxide accumulation to trigger immunogenic ferroptosis and initiate T cell responses. Additionally, the released GOx leads to H2O2 generation, which amplifies intracellular ROS levels and further enhances immunogenic ferroptosis. Simultaneously, membrane-anchored βCD captures and depletes cholesterol through specific host-guest interactions to overcome the mechanical softness of cancer cells, which further enhances CTL-mediated cytotoxicity. In Hepa1-6 subcutaneous tumor-bearing mouse model, we successfully demonstrated that GFS@CAF led to significant tumor regression and established a durable immunological memory, with these mice surviving for two months.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of GFS@CAF

The preparation processes of GFS and GFS@CAF are shown in Scheme 1a. The endogenous reducing agent glutathione (GSH) is used to cleave disulfide bonds in GOx, forming intermolecular disulfide bonds that are then assembled with sorafenib to form GOx-based NPs [36]. Concurrently, GFS is prepared by the strong coordination between the abundant carboxyl, amino, and cleaved -SH groups of GOx and Fe3+ ions [37]. As shown in Fig. 1a, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images confirmed that GFS had a monodispersed spherical morphology. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed an average hydrated diameter of ca. 58.07 nm and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.213, which were significantly larger than those of GOx (Fig. 1b). Since the zeta potentials of GOx is −31.29 mV while the zeta potential of GFS was +25.57 mV, this opposite surface potential preliminarily indicates the successful preparation of GFS (Fig. 1c). Fig. 1d shows the ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectrum of GFS. Characteristic absorption peaks appeared at ca. 280 and 260 nm [36,38], attributed to GOx and sorafenib, respectively, suggesting that GFS is composed of GOx and sorafenib. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) revealed the chemical composition of GFS, showing characteristic signals of Fe belonging to Fe2+/3+ ions and F belonging to sorafenib (Fig. 1e), confirming the presence of iron ions and sorafenib in GFS. These results collectively confirm the successful synthesis of GFS. Meanwhile, valence state analysis indicated that iron ions primarily existed in the Fe2+ (67%) and Fe3+ (33%) forms (Fig. 1f) [39]. Further calculations were performed to determine the encapsulation efficiency of GOx, sorafenib, and Fe in GFS. Even with different mass ratios, the encapsulation efficiency of GOx remained above 90% (Fig. S1a). The encapsulation efficiency of sorafenib initially increased and then plateaued, reaching a saturation point of approximately 60% at a mass ratio of 20% (Fig. S1b). Meanwhile, the encapsulation efficiency of Fe remained at 45% (Fig. S1c). In terms of drug loading capacity, GFS demonstrated a loading capacity of 64.51% for GOx, 25.80% for Fe ions, and 9.67% for sorafenib. Since the ligand sites of GOx are easily occupied by H+ in an acidic environment, the coordination between Fe ions and GOx weakens [40]. Fig. 1g shows a TEM image of the disassembly of GFS in a solution with pH 5.5, DLS measurements showed that the particle size distribution changed from a single peak to multiple peaks with a broad distribution. Hence, the release profiles of Fe2+/3+, sorafenib, and GOx from GFS showed a pH-dependent behavior, where all of them continually release as a function of the incubation time (Fig. S1d–f).

GFS@CAF was prepared via a microfluidic method using a lipid mixture containing 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-methoxy-poly(ethylene glycol) (DSPE-mPEG), DSPE-PEG-folate (DSPE-PEG-FA), dimyristoylphosphatidylcholine (DMPC), the synthesized βCD-C14 (Fig. S2), and DSPE-PEG-arginine (DSPE-PEG-Arg, Fig. S3) [41]. The formation of GFS@CAF was first confirmed via TEM (Fig. 1i), which showed a spherical morphology with GFS enclosed within the core. DLS measurements showed that GFS@CAF had a diameter of approximately 132.64 nm, with a narrow PDI (0.151) (Fig. 1b). The zeta potential dropped from +25.57 mV to −15.94 mV due to modifications in the surface lipid mixture (Fig. 1c). To further confirm the successful integration of βCD-C14, rhodamine B (RhB) was used as a reporter and mixed with βCD-C14 for UV-Vis spectrophotometry analysis. The maximum absorption wavelength of the free RhB was 554 nm. After complexation with βCD, the maximum absorption wavelength of RhB shifted to 560 nm. Simultaneously, the absorbance at 554 nm decreased, whereas the absorbance at 560 nm increased. Fig. 1j presents the UV-Vis absorption spectrum after mixing GFS@CAF and RhB, showing effective bathochromic shift, suggesting the integration of βCD-C14 to the GFS@CAF.

The cholesterol-capturing capacity of GFS@CAF was determined using a displacement assay. Taking advantage of the difference in binding affinities between cholesterol (or RhB) and cyclodextrin [42], cholesterol was added dropwise to the GFS@CAF-RhB composite solution, causing RhB to displace from the βCD-C14 cavities. As shown in Fig. 1k, a significant blue shift appeared after the addition of cholesterol, indicating that GFS@CAF could capture cholesterol. To ensure effective cholesterol depletion, the βCD-C14 content in GFS@CAF should be maintained at an appropriate level. To this end, a series of GFS@CAF samples was prepared with βCD-C14 contents of 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50%. As shown in Table S1 and Fig. S4a, as the concentration of βCD-C14 increased, the particle size of GFS@CAF gradually decreased, the PDI increased, and stability decreased. This finding indicated that high concentrations of βCD-C14 within the formulation may impair the formation and stability of GFS@CAF. Thus, a concentration of 30% βCD-C14 was adopted in all subsequent formulations.

Similarly, to ensure the driving performance of the nanomotors, DSPE-PEG-arginine contents of 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50% was screened to prepare the GFS@CAF samples (Table S2). 20% DSPE-PEG-arginine was adopted for all formulations based on its uniform particle size and low PDI. Briefly, GFS@CAF was prepared using the formulation in Fig. 1h. The stability tests demonstrated that GFS@CAF maintained their hydrodynamic diameter and PDI in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) for 15 days and exhibited stability for up to 30 days upon co-incubation with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Fig. 1l). These results demonstrate the good stability of GFS@CAF under physiological conditions.

Motility, chemotaxis and deep penetration of GFS@CAF

The guanidine group in L-Arg can react with ROS within cancer cells to release NO, providing a driving force for movement [43]. The NO release performance of GFS@CAF in a simulated tumor environment with a high concentration of ROS (100 μM H2O2) was assessed using an NO assay kit, where GFS@CF (without DSPE-PEG-Arg) served as a control. As shown in Fig. 2a, following incubation with H2O2, NO was released gradually, showing a consistent increase with prolonged incubation time. The results also showed that the L-Arg loaded onto GFS@CAF was able to react with ROS in the TME to produce NO. The influence of pH on the generation of NO was also investigated. As shown in Fig. S4b, the NO release was strongly pH-dependent. At pH 5.5, NO release reached 11 μM, which was approximately 1.8-fold higher than that at pH 7.4 (6.49 μM). At pH 6.5, an intermediate release of 9.93 μM was observed, indicating that the mildly acidic tumor microenvironment was sufficient to trigger substantial NO generation. The mechanism underlying this pH-responsive NO release likely involves the cascade of (i) acid-triggered Fe3+ release, (ii) Fe3+-mediated ·OH generation, and (iii) ·OH-driven oxidation of Arg to generate NO. This result demonstrates that our nanomotor enables acidic tumor microenvironment-selective co-generation of ROS and NO.

Given that the released NO can facilitate the propulsion of nanomotors [35], the motility of GFS@CAF in an H2O2 solution was examined via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Several representative motion videos were recorded (Movie S1-S3), and the tracking trajectories were presented in Fig. 2b. Enhanced diffusion was observed with increasing H2O2 concentration. The MSD calculated from the tracked trajectories increased from 1.16 μm2 at Δt = 3 s without adding H2O2, to 10.48 μm2 when 10 mM H2O2 was introduced (Fig. 2c). The diffusion coefficient (Deff = MSD/4Δt) (Fig. 2d), average velocity (Fig. S5a), and velocity distribution (Fig. S5b) were further supported by the above results. Additionally, the motion modes was studied, and the motion exponent “α” was obtained based on the MSD curves (MSD (Δt) = K (Δt)α), where α ≈ 1,1 < α < 2, and α ≈ 2 correspond to Brownian motion, enhanced diffusion, and ballistic motion, respectively. As shown in Table S3, as the H2O2 concentration increased from 0 mM to 10 mM, the particle motion mode transitioned from Brownian motion (α ≈ 0.99) to enhanced diffusion (α ≈ 1.50∼1.60), indicating that H2O2 induced enhanced diffusion motion. The turning-angle distribution analysis (Fig. S5c) displayed that turning-angle appeared roughly uniformly between 0° and 180°, indicating that GFS@CAF exhibited enhanced passive diffusion motion in H2O2 solution. These results indicated that the released NO conferred self-propulsion ability to GFS@CAF nanomotors. Notably, Compared to H2O2 concentration in tumors is typically in the micromolar range (50-100 μM), the motion experiments under high H2O2 concentrations in vitro were primarily used to demonstrate the H2O2-responsive motility potential of GFS@CAF, rather than directly representing its self-propulsion effectiveness in tumor.

To validate the chemotaxis of GFS@CAF nanomotors to high expression of ROS in tumors, the chemotaxis kinetics of GFS@CAF was investigated along a concentration gradient of chemokines (ROS) [44]. As shown in Fig. 2e, an agarose gel containing Hepa1-6 cell lysate was added to a Y-shaped channel chamber (iii) to simulate an ROS concentration gradient, whereas an agarose gel containing HL7702 cell lysate was added to chamber (ii), which was used as a control. Then, ROS concentrations at different positions in the Y channel were determined. The results showed that the concentration of ROS was higher at location d, which contained the chemical inducer (Hepa1-6 lysate), and that the geometric position farthest from the chemical inducer (location a) presented a lower ROS concentration. Therefore, ROS can diffuse outward along the geometry of the Y-type channel over time, creating a concentration gradient. Next, Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CAF nanomotors were loaded into the Y-shaped channel of chamber (i). CLSM was used to continuously monitor the fluorescence of the samples in chambers (ii) and (iii). As shown in Fig. 2f, the results showed that the GFS@CAF nanomotors moved toward the agarose region containing the Hepa1-6 cell lysate, and the fluorescence signal captured by Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CAF nanomotor was 2.5 times higher than that of the chamber (ii) at 120 min (Fig. 2g). Furthermore, a three-dimensional multicellular tumor spheroid (3D MTS) was prepared to verify the deep penetration of GFS@CAF nanomotors (Fig. 2h). GFS@CAF nanomotors exhibited denser and deeper-penetrating red fluorescence throughout the 3D MTS than GFS@CF non-nanomotors. The average optical density provided quantitative data on the red fluorescence signals generated by the groups following penetration of the 3D MTS (Fig. S4c). The fluorescence intensity of the GFS@CAF nanomotors in the equatorial plane was approximately 2.0 times higher than that of GFS@CF non-nanomotors, indicating that the enhanced motility of GFS@CAF promotes their penetration into the 3D MTS. The motility, chemotaxis and penetration capabilities of GFS@CAF nanomotors are expected to significantly improve the efficient delivery and accumulation of therapeutic agents in the deep tissues of solid tumors.

Cellular uptake and targeting of GFS@CAF

GFS@CAF were labeled with Cy5.5 to visualize their cellular uptake behavior in Hepa1-6 cells. The flow cytometry (FCM) results in Fig. 2i and S4d illustrated increasing intracellular fluorescence intensity over time, indicating successful GFS@CAF uptake by Hepa1-6 cells. Subsequently, the internatization pathway was systematically investigated. As shown in Fig. 2k and S4e, pre-treatment with 4°C and amiloride (Am, a micropinocytosis inhibitor) significantly reduced the cellular uptake of GFS@CAF by 36.6% and 51.6%, respectively, whereas MβCD (a caveolae -mediated endocytosis inhibitor) showed only slightly inhibition (9.9%). Incubation at chlorpromazine (CPZ, a clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor) nearly abolished uptake (97.5% inhibition), confirming a clathrin-mediated endocytosis process [[45], [46], [47]]. Co-localization analysis (Fig. 2j) further revealed bright red fluorescence of GFS@CAF was concentrated in the lysosomes. Together, these results suggest that GFS@CAF enters tumor cells primarily via clathrin-mediated endocytosis and is subsequently transported to lysosomes, facilitating the intracellular release of GFS@CAF for ferroptosis and cholesterol depletion.

Cancer cells (Hepa1-6 and 4T1) and normal cell (HL7702) were then selected to evaluate the targeting ability of GFS@CAF. Intracellular fluorescence intensity was measured using FCM (Fig. 2l and S6a-c). Hepa1-6 and 4T1 cells incubated with Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CAF exhibited strong intracellular fluorescent signals, while HL7702 cells hardly took up GFS@CAF. These results further demonstrated the specific targeting ability of GFS@CAF to cancer cells via FA receptor. The CLSM images in Fig. 2m and S6d further confirmed the targeting ability of GFS@CAF to cancer cells, owing to the obvious red fluorescence observed in Hepa1-6 and 4T1 cells.

GFS@CAF depletes membrane cholesterol to stiffen tumor cells in vitro

The cell membranes of cancer cells are often softer than those of non-malignant cells [42]. Cancer cells soften their cell membranes by increasing cholesterol levels, thereby weakening the force generated at T cell synapses and helping them evade T cell-mediated killing [16]. Compared to HL7702 cells, the cholesterol levels in Hepa1-6 and 4T1 cells were significantly elevated (Fig. 3a). We further quantified cholesterol levels in tumor and normal tissues from mice bearing Hepa1-6 subcutaneous tumors. The results showed that cholesterol levels in the tumor tissues were approximately six times higher than those in the adjacent muscle tissues (Fig. 3b). Cholesterol level was also upregulated in 4T1 tumors confirming that cancer cells generally exhibit higher cholesterol levels than normal cells.

We then sought to regulate cell stiffness using CAF to deplete membrane cholesterol in plasma membranes. As shown in Fig. 3c and d, when the tumor cells were treated with PBS, βCD (cholesterol-depleting molecule, positive control) and CAF for 1 h, the membrane cholesterol level of cancer cells treated with CAF dropped remarkably (44.16% of the native state in Hepa1-6 cells, and 42.33% of the native state in 4T1 cells). Furthermore, the membrane cholesterol levels of Hepa1-6 cancer cells were visualized or quantitatively analyzed by staining with the Filipin complex, a fluorescent dye that specifically binds to cholesterol (Fig. 3e). The fluorescence images revealed the outstanding cholesterol-depleting capability of CAF, comparable to the same concentration of βCD. These results demonstrated that the βCD-C14 loaded onto CAF was able to reduce plasma membrane cholesterol levels in tumor cells.

To investigate whether cancer-cell stiffness could be enhanced by depleting membrane cholesterol, we directly measured single-cell stiffness using atomic force microscopy (AFM) [48] (Fig. 3f). We found that Hepa1-6 cells treated with βCD and CAF resulted in significantly higher cell stiffness relative to the PBS-treated Hepa1-6 cells (Fig. S7a), where the relative cortical stiffness of Hepa1-6 cells treated with βCD and CAF increased by 1.85 times and 1.80 times, respectively, relative to PBS group (Fig. 3g). Similar results were observed in 4T1 cells (Fig. 3g and S7b). These results demonstrated that CAF could effectively enhance cell stiffness by depleting membrane cholesterol.

Cancer cell stiffness enhances T cell-mediated cytotoxicity

Although cholesterol depletion can influence multiple membrane-associated processes, including lipid raft organization, membrane fluidity, antigen presentation, death receptor distribution, membrane repair, and endocytosis, previous studies have provided direct evidence that tumor cell stiffening is an important contributor to enhanced T cell-mediated cytotoxicity [16,49]. Next, we investigated whether CAF-induced tumor cell stiffening enhanced T cell-mediated antitumor immunity in vitro. Specifically, CTLL-2 cells were used to evaluate T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. In this study, Hepa1-6 or 4T1 tumor cells (effector-to-target [E: T] ratio = 10: 1) were pre-treated with βCD or CAF and then co-incubated with CTLL-2 cells. After 5 h of incubation, T-cell activation was assessed. As shown in Fig. 3h and S6e, significantly increased levels of IFN-γ+CD8+ T and TNF-α+CD8+ cells were observed in the βCD- and CAF-treated groups compared with the PBS-treated group [42]. As a result, βCD and CAF treatment reduced the cell viability of Hepa1-6 cells after co-incubation with CD8+ T cells (31.13% and 59.11% viability, respectively) relative to PBS-treated cells (100.10%) (Fig. 3i). These results indicated that CAF could effectively weaken the resistance of tumor cells to T cell-mediated attack by stiffening the tumor cells.

CTLL-2 exhibited differential cytotoxicity toward Hepa1-6 and 4T1 cells, which could be explained by immune evasion properties of these two cells. CTLL-2 on target tumor cells is generally divided into two key stages: (i) the recognition of tumor antigens (presented as MHC I–peptide complexes) via the T-cell receptor; (ii) the execution of killing through various effector mechanisms, primarily including the granzyme-perforin pathway, the Fas-FasL pathway, and the secretion of cytokines [[50], [51], [52], [53]]. The cytotoxicity observed against Hepa1-6 could reflect a relatively higher sensitivity to CTL-mediated apoptosis and a weaker immunosuppressive phenotype [54]. Conversely, the weaker cytotoxicity against 4T1 is consistent with the well-established immune-resistant nature of the breast cancer model. 4T1 are known to promote potent immunosuppressive mechanisms to impair effector T-cell activity and reduce tumor cell susceptibility to immune-mediated killing [[55], [56], [57], [58]]. In addition, resistance to apoptosis-related signaling, such as reduced sensitivity to granzyme/perforin-induced death, may further contribute to the lower responsiveness of 4T1 to CTLL-2-mediated cytotoxicity [50,54,59].

GFS@CAF activates ferroptosis and triggers immunogenic cell death

We assessed the viability of Hepa1-6, CTLL-2, and HL7702 cells co-cultured with GFS@CAF using a standard CCK-8 assay (Fig. S6f). After 24 h, where their reduced in a GFS@CAFs concentration-dependent manner, and there was no obvious cytotoxicity in CTLL-2 and HL7702 cells treated with GFS@CAF at concentrations of 3-50 μg/mL (viability>85%). In contrast, the viability of Hepa1-6 cells significantly decreased. These results demonstrated that GFS@CAF exhibited selective cytotoxicity against cancer cells, while causing favorable biocompatibility with T cells and normal cells at concentrations of 3-50 μg/mL. Subsequently, we investigated the possible causes of 4T1 cell death induced by GFS@CAF. As shown in Fig. 4a, compared to the PBS group, all other groups exhibited varying degrees of reduced cell viability. Notably, the GFS and GFS@CAF groups showed significantly lower cell viability compared to the sorafenib and CAF groups, which might be attributed to the synergistic ferroptosis induced by the combined effects of GOx, Fe3+, and sorafenib. The apoptosis assay results were consistent with these results (Fig. 4b).

Ferroptosis in Hepa1-6 cells was further confirmed by monitoring intracellular GSH, Fe2+, and ROS levels. Owing to the redox reaction between Fe3+ and GSH, GFS and GFS@CAF significantly elevated intracellular Fe2+ level but dramatically consumed intracellular GSH (Fig. 4c and d). The depletion of GSH and release of Fe2+ catalyzed the conversion of cellular H2O2 to •OH, thereby augmenting the pool of lethal ROS (Fig. 4f). Both GFS and GFS@CAF exhibited green fluorescence, which was directly attributed to elevated ROS levels (Fig. S8).

Next, GPX4 activity was investigated by GPX4 activity assay kit. As shown in Fig. 4h, the sorafenib, GFS and GFS@CAF groups significantly inhibited GPX4 activity compared to CAF group. This could be attributed to sorafenib as a ferroptosis inducer, that could restrict the synthesis of GSH by inhibiting system Xc− (cystine/glutamate antiporter), leading to impaired GSH synthesis and subsequent suppression of GPX4 activity [60]. Meanwhile, the self-cyclic valence alternation between Fe3+ and Fe2+ exhausted GSH and consequently inactivated GPX4 [12]. Moreover, the impact on expression of GPX4 was also evaluated by western blot (Fig. S9), where GFS and GFS@CAF groups significantly decreased the expression of GPX4 in contrast to the other groups, which was attributed to suppressed GPX4 through GSH consumption [12]. Notably, GFS@CAF significantly suppressed GPX4 activity and downregulated the GPX4 expression through GSH impairment, providing strong evidence for ferroptosis activation.

GPX4 is vital in removing lethal LPO, whereas ROS could induce their abundant accumulation. As shown in Fig. 4i, GFS and GFS@CAF-treated cells had higher signals than those treated with sorafenib or CAF alone, indicating the superior ability of GFS or GFS@CAF to induce cellular LPO generation. LPO in Hepa1-6 cells emitted a green fluorescence upon staining with the lipid peroxidation probe BODIPY581/591-C11, showing that GFS and GFS@CAF boosted lipid peroxide accumulation in Hepa1-6 cells (Fig. 4g). Notably, compared with GFS alone, the GFS@CAF treatment group significantly increased LPO levels. This may be attributed, on one hand, to the inhibition of GPX4 activity and downregulation of its expression by GFS@CAF, and on the other hand, to the reduced formation of lipid rafts promoting the diffusion of lipid peroxides within the cell membrane, thereby enhancing ferroptosis. We subsequently assessed the lipid rafts in hepa1-6 cells after different treatments using the lipid raft marker, Cholera Toxin Subunit B Alexa Fluor™ 488 Conjugates (CT-B 488). As shown in Fig. S10a b, both CAF and GFS@CAF reduced CT-B 488 expression in cells. Furthermore, 1-pyrenedodecanoic acid was used to test cell membrane fluidity after different treatments (Fig. S10c), the results showed that CAF and GFS@CAF increased the membrane fluidity of Hepa1-6 cells. Therefore, it could be conclude that GFS@CAF enhanced the diffusion of lipid peroxides by depleting membrane cholesterol levels and reducing lipid raft formation in the cell membrane.

Interestingly, the interaction between membrane damage induced by GFS and βCD-mediated cholesterol depleting suggests a potential bidirectional feedback loop, wherein oxidative damage and lipid raft disruption reinforce one another. GFS-induced LPO disrupts the structural integrity of cell membranes, exposing embedded cholesterol and increasing the susceptibility to βCD extraction. In turn, βCD-mediated cholesterol depletion destabilizes membrane microdomains, facilitating further membrane penetration of LPO induced by GFS. This reinforcement of physical and biochemical perturbations highlights an intricate handoff between the GFS-mediated LPO and CAF-mediated membrane modulation, which mutually accelerates ferroptosis.

Additionally, βCD was used as a positive control, the difference in LPO levels between the GFS+βCD and GFS@CAF groups was evaluated. As shown in Fig. S10d, the LPO levels in the GFS+βCD group were similar to those in the GFS@CAF group, and both were higher than those in the βCD group. This result further validateed the above conclusion. We also performed ferroptosis rescue experiments using the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1). As shown in Fig. S10e, cell viability significantly decreased after dealt with GFS@CAF, whereas Fer-1 restored cell viability to a certain extent, suggesting that the cell death induced by GFS@CAF was indeed related to ferroptosis. Overall, the present results suggested that GFS@CAF could induce Hepa1-6 cell death via ferroptosis.

Ferroptosis triggers ICD in tumor cells [61]. The levels of ICD-related biochemical hallmarks, including high-mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1), calreticulin (CRT), and adenosine triphosphate (ATP), were analyzed to evaluate the effects of ICD. As shown in Fig. 4j, CAF was not exposed to CRT, whereas GFS and GFS@CAF were exposed, emanating from the immunogenic nature of ferroptosis. Notably, GFS@CAF triggered greater CRT exposure than GFS. Unlike untreated or CAF-treated Hepa1-6 cells, HMGB1 in GFS- or GFS@CAF-treated Hepa1-6 cells displayed an enhanced release (Fig. 4k), further confirming that GFS or GFS@CAF could elicit ICD in Hepa1-6 cells. Subsequent detection of ATP secretion exhibited the same tendency (Fig. 4e–l). These results demonstrated that GFS and GS@CAF could efficiently trigger ICD responses via ferroptosis.

GFS@CAF depletes tumoral cholesterol and induces antitumor immune responses in vivo

To investigate the biodistribution of GFS@CAF in a tumor-bearing mouse model, we intravenously injected Cy5.5 labeled-GFS@CAF or GFS@CA without targeting ability (without DSPE-PEG-FA), and monitored the fluorescence of Cy5.5 using small animal in vivo imaging. Notably, although the tumor fluorescence signal of GFS@CAF was lower than that in the liver and kidneys (Fig. S11), GFS@CAF exhibited enhanced tumor accumulation compared with the non-targeted GFS@CA (Fig. 5a and b), with a peak at 24 h post-injection in contrast to GFS@CA. The fluorescence intensity gradually decreased after 24 h, but was still visible at the tumor site after 48 h. These results indicated that FA modification enhanced the relative tumor accumulation and retention of the formulation, rather than preferential accumulation in the tumor over all normal organs. To further validate the ability of GFS@CAF to deep penetrate into tumor, we examined the accumulation of GFS@CAF at the tumor margin and tumor center, GFS@CF (without motility) as a control. According to the treatment regime in Fig. 5c, mice were subjected to intravenous administration of Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CF or Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CAF. Then, the tumors were dissected, and the fluorescence intensity at the tumor margin and tumor center were quantitative compared using flow cytometry. As displayed in Fig. S12, the fluorescence signals of both groups at the tumor margin are comparable, while GFS@CAF group exhibited higher fluorescence intensity at the tumor center compared to GFS@CF groups, indicating that the NO-propelled GFS@CAF nanomotors were able to effectively deep penetrate into the tumors.

As depicted in Fig. 5d, GFS@CAF could deplete tumor cholesterol to stiffen tumor cells and sensitize cells to ferroptosis-mediated immunotherapy. Hence, the capacity of GFS@CAF for cholesterol depletion and ferroptotic immune response activation was assessed in a Hepa1-6 subcutaneous tumor-bearing mouse model (Fig. 5c). Filipin staining of tumor slices (Fig. 5e and f) revealed strong fluorescence intensities in the GFS, sorafenib, and control groups. However, diminished blue signals were observed in tumor slices of mice receiving CAF and GFS@CAF, indicating that CAF enabled GFS@CAF to deplete cholesterol in tumors. As shown in Fig. 5g and h, GFS@CAF significantly reduced GPX4 expression in the tumor slices, indicating cell ferroptosis. Subsequently, we evaluated the in vivo ICD responses via immunohistochemical staining for CRT in the tumor slices (Fig. 5i and j). As expected, GFS@CAF elicited robust CRT expression, which was consistent with the concept that GFS@CAF-boosted ferroptosis triggers ICD responses (Fig. 4j).

We also examined DCs maturation in the mice [62,63]. Mice receiving GFS@CAF exhibited an obvious higher proportion of DC maturation, with 21.4% of surface expression of CD86 in DCs (CD45+CD11c+ CD86+) in tumor compared to the PBS group (0.2%). In comparison, mice receiving sorafenib, GFS, and CAF showed 3.1%, 9.1% and 11.8% maturation, respectively (Fig. 5k and l). Enhanced DC maturation benefits T cell priming. As illustrated by the FCM analysis (Fig. 5m) and the corresponding statistics (Fig. 5n), CAF and GFS@CAF led to increased levels of CTL (CD8+ T cells, CD45+CD3+CD8+) in tumors compared to the other groups: mice that received GFS@CAF elicited 5.28-fold more CD8+ T cells than those treated with PBS alone. Additionally, GFS@CAF elevated the secretion of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) (Fig. S13a) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) (Fig. S13b) compared to the other three groups, further validating cholesterol depletion augments the antitumor immune responses elicited by ferroptosis. Although the present study demonstrated enhanced DC maturation, increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and elevated cytokine secretion after treatment, the immunological evaluation remains preliminary. A more comprehensive analysis of the tumor immune microenvironment, including regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, macrophage polarization, and exhausted T-cell phenotypes, will be necessary to fully elucidate the mechanism of ferroptosis-immunotherapy sensitization in the further study.

Antitumor efficacy in vivo

Based on these results, we evaluated the antitumor effects of GFS@CAF in vivo. A mouse Hepa1-6 subcutaneous tumor model was established, and the mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6): PBS, sorafenib, GFS, CAF, and GFS@CAF. When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were intravenously injected with the indicated formulations on days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 (Fig. 6a). Mice treated with sorafenib displayed only slight suppression of tumor growth (Fig. 6b and c). In contrast, tumor growth rates were markedly inhibited in mice that received GFS only, which was attributed to the enhanced antitumor responses induced by amplified ferroptosis. Additionally, the tumor volumes of mice treated with CAF were lower than those of the control group, which attributed to βCD-C14 released from the CAF-reducing membrane cholesterol promotes cancer cell stiffness to induce enhanced T cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Undoubtedly, mice treated with GFS@CAF showed robust tumor regression and long-term survival of at least 2 months (5/6 mice, 83.3%) (Fig. 6f). Additionally, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of tumor tissue slices revealed that, compared to the control groups, GFS@CAF presented the most extensive damage in terms of tumor cell density, cell morphology regularity, and cell nucleus clarity. The TdT-mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) assay and Ki67 staining results further confirmed the significant reduction in cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis in the GFS@CAF group (Fig. 6d). To further evaluate the contributions of FA-mediated targeting and NO-driven motility to the antitumor efficacy of GFS@CAF, GFS@CA (non-FA-targeted) group was introduced (Fig. S14). The results showed that NO-driven motility alone could inhibit tumor growth to a certain extent, while FA-mediated targeting further increased the enrichment of nanoparticles at the tumor site, achieving significant inhibitory efficacy. These findings indicate a synergistic effect between NO-driven motility and FA-mediated targeting, which more effectively promotes intratumoral penetration and therapeutic efficacy.

Next, we explored the mechanism of tumor regression and the durability of the antitumor effect in mice treated with GFS@CAF. To test immunological memory, memory T cells (CD3+CD8+CD44highCD62Llow) of the spleens retrieved from mice subjected to the indicated treatments were analyzed using FCM. Compared with the control group, mice treated with GFS@CAF exhibited a 1.19-fold increase in the fraction of memory T cells, which was higher than that in the other groups (Fig. 6g and S15), suggesting that the mice established a strong immune memory after GFS@CAF treatment. These results provide evidence that GFS@CAF elicited robust antitumor efficacy and induced durable antitumor immunological memory.

Biosafety evaluation

Blood compatibility was evaluated using the hemolysis test. As shown in Fig. S16a and b, no hemolysis was observed within the concentration range of 5–1000 μg/mL for GFS@CAF when compared to the positive control group (water). The hemolysis rates at the corresponding concentrations were subsequently calculated, all of which complied with the allowable limit (<5%), indicating the excellent blood compatibility of GFS@CAF. No significant changes in body weight were observed throughout the treatment period (Fig. 6e). Routine blood tests were also performed to evaluate toxicity after treatment, and the results showed that none of the treatments induced significant side effects in mice (Fig. S17). Moreover, blood biochemical indices showed no significant differences in all treatment groups in contrast to PBS groups. Although there were slight fluctuations in certain liver indices (e.g., a115001ase, ALT) compared to those in PBS group, the values fell within the standardized ranges (20-100 U/L), confirming the absence of severe adverse effects on liver function (Fig. 6h and S18).

H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, lungs, spleen, and kidneys) showed that GFS@CAF had no aberrant cell morphology or lesions in the tissue sections of the major organs (Fig. 6l). Although no obvious abnormalities were observed in body weight, serum biochemical parameters, or H&E staining of major organs under the current experimental conditions, these results mainly support the short-term in vivo biocompatibility of GFS@CAF. Given that GFS contains Fe3+/Fe2+ components, the potential long-term retention in reticuloendothelial organs and the risk of iron overload after repeated administration cannot be fully excluded at this stage. More comprehensive safety evaluations, including inflammatory cytokine analysis, quantitative Fe biodistribution, and long-term metabolic studies, will be necessary in future investigations. Furthermore, cholesterol-depleting may have potential impacts on systemic lipid metabolism, cell membrane homeostasis, and immune cell function. Consequently, current safety assessments are insufficient to draw definitive conclusions for translational applications. Subsequent research must conduct more comprehensive long-term biosafety trials, serum lipid profiling, and cholesterol metabolism analyses to fully evaluate the translational potential of GFS@CAF.

Conclusion

In summary, a NO-propelled nanomotor (GFS@CAF) was developed to sensitize ferroptosis immunotherapy by depleting cholesterol to stiffen tumor cells. GFS@CAF nanomotors utilized the high levels of H2O2 in the TME to generate NO, enabling self-propulsion and deep penetration via chemotaxis. Following FA-mediated internalization, GFS@CAF releases GOx, Fe2+/3+ ions and sorafenib in a pH-responsive manner to collectively induce LPO, triggering immunogenic ferroptosis and initiating T cell responses. Concurrently, the βCD in GFS@CAF could capture and deplete membrane cholesterol through specific host-guest interactions, increasing membrane stiffness to enhance the T cell-mediated killing of cancer cells. In vivo studies demonstrated that GFS@CAFs could deplete cholesterol and sensitize ferroptosis-mediated ICD, including DC maturation and CTL priming. Notably, GFS@CAF exhibited significant therapeutic efficacy in mouse Hepa1-6 subcutaneous tumor model and established a durable immunological memory, resulting in prolonged survival. This study highlights the mechano-immunotherapeutic potential of combining cholesterol depletion and immunogenic ferroptosis for tumor treatment. Compared to recent advances in novel nanotechnology platforms for cancer therapy [34,64,65], this study introduces a NO-propelled nanomotor-based targeted delivery system, which combines tumor cell mechanical stiffening with immunogenic ferroptosis for the first time. It achieves deep tumor penetration and cell membrane stiffening, significantly enhancing the efficacy of ferroptosis immunotherapy and providing a new paradigm for precise cancer treatment through the regulation of cell mechanics.

Synthesis of βCD-C14 and DSPE-PEG2000-Arg

βCD-C14: 500 mg 6-amino-6-deoxy-β-cyclodextrin was dissolved in 5 mL N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF). Myristic acid (1.0 eq.), PyBop (1.2 eq.) and triethylamine (2.0 eq.) were added and reacted at room temperature for 30 min. After the reaction, concentrate under reduced pressure, precipitate with a large amount of ice diethyl ether, collect the precipitate and dry it under vacuum to obtain βCD-C14.

DSPE-PEG2000-Arg: 100 mg DSPE-PEG2000-NHS was dissolved in 2 mL methanol. 6 mL of L-arginine (1.5 eq.) and triethylamine (3.0 eq.) were added and reacted at room temperature for 0.5 h. The reaction mixture dialyzed against pure water for 24 h (molecular weight cutoff 2000 Da). The final solution was collected and freeze-dried to obtain DSPE-PEG2000-Arg.

Preparation of GFS, GFS@CAF and Cy5.5 labeled-GFS@CAF

GFS:5 mg of GOx was dissolved in 5 mL ultra-pure water, 0.5 mL of GSH aqueous solution (50 mM) was added under stirring for 2 min. Then, 300 μL of FeCl3 aqueous solution (5 mg/mL) and 300 μL of sorafenib ethanol solution (10 mg/mL) were dropwise added sequentially under light stirring. The final solution was further gently stirred for 15 min. At last, the GFS was collected via centrifuge at 14000 rpm for 20 min and washed with ultra-pure water/ethanol (10/1, v/v) to remove impurity. Loading efficiency (LE) is calculated according to the equation as follows: LE (wt.%) = Weightdrug-loaded/WeightGFS×100%. Encapsulation efficiency (EE) is calculated according to the equation as follows: EE = Weightdrug-loaded/Weightdrug-feeded×100%.

GFS@CAF: the above GFS dispersed in 9 mL of deionized water was injected into the one inlet of the chip. Simultaneously, βCD-C14, DSPE-PEG2000-Arg, DSPE-PEG2000-FA, DSPE-PEG2000 and DMPC were dissolved in 3 mL of methanol/DMSO (1/1, v/v) and introduced into the chip through other of the inlets. The freshly prepared liposomes were collected and dialyzed (molecular weight cutoff 100 kDa) to remove residual methanol and DMSO. The flow speed of deionized water screened from 100 to 300 μL/min to optimize sizes of GFS@CAF, and the final flow speed was adjusted to 300 μL/min with a flow rate ratios of deionized water to methanol/DMSO solution were 3:1. The mass ratios of βCD-C14 and DSPE-PEG2000-Arg to lipids were screened from 10 to 50% respectively to ensure the content of βCD-C14 and DSPE-PEG2000-Arg in GFS@CAF should be at an ideal level, and the final ratio was adjusted to βCD-C14/DSPE-PEG2000-Arg/DMPC/DSPE-PEG2000/DSPE-PEG2000-FA = 30/20/46/3.5/0.5 (molar ratio).

Cy5.5 labeled-GFS@CAF: Apart for the feed ratio (βCD-C14/DSPE-PEG2000-Arg/DMPC/DSPE-PEG2000/DSPE-PEG2000-FA/DSPE-PEG2000-Cy5.5 = 30/20/46/3/0.5/0.5, molar ratio), Cy5.5 labeled-GFS@CAF was prepared by the same procedure as GFS@CAF described above.

Characterization of GFS and GFS@CAF

TEM: To prepare the TEM sample, 10 μL sample solution was dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid for 10 min and blotted with filter paper to remove excess and air-dried before analysis on TEM.

DLS measurements: The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of GFS and GFS@CAF were evaluated in PBS using Brookhaven laser particle size analyzer (NanoBrook 90plus PALS).

pH-responsive release: To evaluate the pH-responsive properties of GFS, GFS was treated with PBS under pH 7.4 and 5.5 at 37°C. After stirring for 1 h, TEM and DLS was carried out to observe the changes of morphology and size. The pH-responsive release of sorafenib and Fe release experiments were performed at pH 7.4 and 5.5 at 37°C. The dialysis bag was filled with 2 mL of GFS (4 mg/mL), and 18 mL of PBS at different pH were added to the external fluid of the dialysis bag (molecular weight cutoff 100 kDa). The concentration of sorafenib and Fe in the dialysate at different time points was determined using a UV-Vis absorption spectrum, and ferric and ferrous ion assay kit (Beyotime, S1066S) to plot the sorafenib and Fe release curve, respectively. The pH-responsive release of GOx was validated by evaluating its catalytic activity. Due to GOx can catalyze glucose to generate H2O2, the generation profiles of H2O2 are used to evaluate the catalytic activity of GOx. The H2O2 concentration at different pH was monitored using a hydrogen peroxide assay kit (Beyotime, S0038).

Motility, chemotaxis, and deep penetration

NO generation: 1 mg/mL of GFS@CAF dispersed in PBS solution was exposed to 100 μM H2O2 solution. GFS@CF without DSPE-PEG2000-Arg was served as control group. The amount of released NO at different time was detected by the commercial Griess assay kit (Beyotime S0021S). NaNO2 standard samples (1-100 μM) were used to establish a standard curve for calculation of NO concentrations.

Motility: The different H2O2 concentrations solution (0, 5, and 10 mM) of GFS@CAF was placed on the 35 mm confocal dishes. The CLSM with a 20× objective was used for video capture of GFS@CAF or GFS@CF movement. Movies with a field of view of approximately 36.33 μm × 36.33 μm of 100 frames at a rate of 10 frames per second were recorded. Fiji software was used to analyzing particle trajectory and timing. A total of 15 different particles were selected for each group of samples to calculate the average speed. The MSD and average speed were evaluated according to the established protocol.

Chemotaxis: To visualize the chemotactic movement of GFS@CAF, Y-shaped glass substrate microchannel model was used with the main channel being 1.0 cm in length and 0.4 cm in width, and the branch channels being 0.7 cm in length and 0.3 cm in width. The specific experimental procedures are as follows: First, agarose was completely dissolved in PBS at high temperature (10.0 mg/mL). Then, HL7702 and Hepa1-6 cells (cell density of 1 × 106 cells/mL) were lysed by ultrasound, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation to extract cell contents (4°C, 3000 rpm, 20 min). When the agarose solution cooled down but had not yet solidified, 20% (v/v) of the above cell lysate was added respectively and mixed well. Subsequently, 50.0 μL of different cell lysate-agarose mixtures were pipetted into the reservoirs (ii) and (iii) of the Y-shaped channel, and transferred to 4°C for gelation. 300.0 μL PBS was filled, and after standing at 4°C for 30 min, samples were collected from different positions of the Y-shaped channel. The ROS concentration was detected using a ROS assay kit (Beyotime, S0033S). In additional, add 50.0 μL of GFS@CAF into reservoir (i). Fluorescence images of reservoir (ii) and reservoir (iii) at specific time points were taken with a high-content cell imaging analysis system (Revvity Operetta CLS, USA).

Deep penetration: HepG1-6 cells-based 3D multicellular spheroids (MTS) were established. 150 μl of the HepG1-6 cell suspension (1 × 104 cells/mL) was seeded into each well of ultra-low adsorption U-shaped 96-well plates (COSTAR, 7007). After incubating for 3 days, the 3D MTS were successfully formed and selected for further permeation behavior testing. Specifically, Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CAF were added to each well. After incubating for 2 h, the 3D cellular spheroids were removed from the wells and washed three times with PBS. The 3D cellular spheroids after different treatments were then observed using CLSM. As a control, Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CF were also added in the same way, and subsequently observed via CLSM.

Cell uptake and targeting

Cell uptake: the intracellular uptake behavior of GFS@CAF was examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Zeiss, LSM780) and flow cytometry (FCM, Agilent, Novocyte 3130). GFS@CAF was labeled with Cy5.5. Hepa1-6 cells (1 × 105) were seeded in confocal dishes and incubated overnight. Subsequently, the cells were treated with 20 μg mL−1 of Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CAF at 37°C for various time points (0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 h). Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and visualized using CLSM. For FCM analysis, the same procedures were applied; cells were collected and quantified via FCM, and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Cell uptake pathways: To determine the cellular uptake mechanism of GFS@CAF, Hepa1-6 cells were preincubated with various endocytic inhibitors (100 nM amiloride, 10 μg/mL chlorpromazine, and 10 mg/mL Methyl-β-cyclodextrin) at 37°C for 1 h or at 4°C for 30 min. Following this, the cells were rinsed with PBS, exposed to 50 μg/mL Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CAF for an additional 2 h, and subsequently harvested for FCM analysis to quantify fluorescence intensities.

Co-localization assay: To assess the lysosomal co-localization of GFS@CAF, Hepa1-6 cells were co-cultured with 50 μg/mL Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CAF for 2 h. Following incubation, the cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, stained with DAPI for nuclear visualization, and labeled with lysosome-specific tracers (yellow). Fluorescence images were acquired subsequently using a CLSM.

Targeting: Hepa1-6 cells (1 × 105), 4T1 (1 × 105), and HL7702 (1 × 105) were seeded in confocal dishes and incubated overnight. Subsequently, the cells were treated with 20 μg mL−1 of Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CAF at 37°C for 2 h. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and visualized using CLSM. For FCM analysis, the same procedures were applied, where cells were collected and quantified via FCM and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Quantification of cholesterol levels in cells, tumor, and muscle tissues

Hepa1-6 cells (1 × 106 cells), 4T1 (1 × 106 cells), and HL7702 (1 × 106 cells) were seeded in dishes. After overnight incubation, cells were harvested by trypsinization and centrifugation, followed by the addition of cholesterol extract to create a cell suspension. The cells were then disrupted using ultrasound and agitated at room temperature for 2 h. Tumors (Hepa1-6 and 4T1) and the adjacent muscle tissues were collected and digested on a shaker at 37°C for 1 h using a tissue digestion buffer. The resulting cell mixture was then passed through a 70 μm cell sieve and rinsed with PBS to obtain a single-cell suspension. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and cholesterol extract was added to prepare the cell suspension. The cells were subsequently disrupted ultrasonically and shaken at room temperature for 2 h. Cholesterol levels in each sample were quantified using the Amplex Red cholesterol and cholesteryl ester assay kit (Beyotime, S0211M) according to the manufacturer's recommended protocol.

Cholesterol depletion ability

To investigate cholesterol depletion induced by GFS@CAF, tumor cells (Hepa1-6 and 4T1) were co-incubated with different preparations (βCD, 0.5 mg/mL; CAF, 1 mg/mL) for 1 h. Subsequently, the cholesterol levels in the plasma membrane were evaluated. Then, use ultrasound to break up the cells and stir at room temperature for 2 h. The cholesterol level was quantified using the Amplex Red cholesterol detection kit. Hepa1-6 cells were inoculated into confocal culture dishes and incubated for 24 h with PBS, βCD, or CAF, respectively. Wash three times with PBS, and then obtain fluorescence images using a CLSM. The fluorescence intensity was calculated using Fiji software.

Measurement of cell stiffness by AFM

Atomic force microscope (AFM) comprises a cantilever with a sharp tip. Cell stiffness is quantified via probe–cell surface interactions. As the probe approaches the cell, forces induce cantilever deflection, monitored by laser reflection from the cantilever's backside onto a photodiode. The AFM records deflection vs. probe-cell distance to calculate Young's modulus: softer cells show lower modulus, stiffer cells higher. In this study, after tumor cells were treated with CAF for 1 h, 20 cells were randomly selected to investigate the cell stiffness using an AFM (MFP-3D bio, Oxford instruments).

Fluidity membrane detection

After being treated with different treatments, hepa1-6 cells in 96-wells plate were stained with 1-pyrenedodecanoic acid for 5 min in dark. Subsequently, the fluorescence spectra were scanned with an excitation wavelength of 380 nm and an emission wavelength of 380-580 nm using a multimode reader. The fluidity of each group was assessed by comparing according the ratio of excimer (475 nm) to pyrene monomer (397 nm).

T cell-mediated cytotoxicity in vitro

T cell-mediated cytotoxicity: CTLL-2 (CD3+CD8+) cells were co-incubated with hepa1-6 or 4T1 cells (effector (E): target (T) ratio = 10:1) pre-treated by PBS, βCD, or CAF, respectively. After incubation for 5 h, the supernatant containing CTLL-2 (CD3+CD8+) cells was collected. After centrifugation, the collected CTLL-2 (CD3+CD8+) cells were stained with the flow cytometry antibody (PE anti-mouse IFN-γ and PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse TNF-α) for FCM assays.

Viability assay by CCK-8 protocol: CTLL-2 (CD3+CD8+) cells were co-incubated with hepa1-6 or 4T1 cells (E:T ratio = 10:1) pre-treated by PBS, βCD, or CAF, respectively. After incubation for 5 h, the supernatant containing CTLL-2 (CD3+CD8+) cells was removed. The tumor cells were washed three times with PBS, then CCK-8 working solution was added to each well to detect the viability of tumor cells.

Cell viability and apoptosis analysis

Cell viability assay: Hepa1-6, HL7702 and CTLL-2 cells were evenly seeded into a 96-well plate (1 × 104 cells/well) and incubated overnight. Various concentrations of GFS@CAF (3, 5, 10, 30, 50, 80, and 100 μg/mL) were added and incubated for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 kit.

Apoptosis assay: Hepa1-6 cells were grown into a six-well plate (5 × 105 cells per well) for 24 h. PBS, sorafenib, GFS, CAF, and GFS@CAF were added and incubated for 24 h. Cell samples were washed with ice-cold PBS, stained with Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, C1062S) for FCM analysis.

Ferroptosis analysis

Intracellular GSH, Fe2+, ATP and GPX4 evaluation: Hepa1-6 cells (1 × 106 cells) were seeded in six-well plates and treated with PBS, sorafenib, GFS, CAF, and GFS@CAF for 12 h. Cellular GSH, Fe2+, ATP and GPX4 content was measured using a commercial GSH assay kit (Solarbio, BC1175), ferrous ion content assay kit (Solarbio, BC5415), ATP chemiluminescence assay kit (Elabscience, E-BC-F300), and GPX4 activity assay kit (Elabscience, BC6265).

Intracellular ROS and LPO evaluation: Hepa1-6 cells were cultured overnight in confocal dished and cultured with PBS, sorafenib, GFS, CAF, and GFS@CAF for 12 h. Cell were then stained with a ROS detection probe (CM-H2DCFDA) and a LPO detection probe (BODIPY 581/591C11) at 37°C for 30 min. Finally, the cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, stained with DAPI for nuclear visualization. Additionally, Hepa1-6 cells (1 × 106 cells) were seeded in six-well plates and treated with PBS, sorafenib, GFS, CAF, and GFS@CAF for 12 h. Cellular ROS and LPO content was measured using a commercial ROS assay kit (Beyotime, C1190S) and LPO assay kit (Beyotime, S0043S).

ICD effect evaluation in vitro

Hepa1-6 cells were seeded in confocal dishes and incubated overnight. The cells were then treated with PBS, sorafenib, GFS, CAF, and GFS@CAF for 12 h. For CRT exposure, cell samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, followed by incubation with CRT primary antibody (1: 400) at 4°C for 1 h and secondary antibody AF488 conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1: 100) at 4°C for 1 h. Finally, cells were stained with DAPI and imaged using a CLSM. The supernatant was collected to assess HMGB1 secretion and ATP release using a mouse HMGB-1 ELISA kit (Elabscience, E-EL-M0676) and ATP chemiluminescence assay kit (Elabscience, E-BC-F300).

Biodistribution in vivo

Female C57 mice were injected with Hepa1-6 cells (1 × 106 cells/100 μL) were injected subcutaneously into the left leg flank. When the tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, the tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with Cy5.5-labeled GFS@CAF (n = 3). After that, the mice were imaged using an IVIS system at 0, 5, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h post-injection. At 48 h post-injection, the mice were sacrificed, and their major organs and tumor tissues were collected for ex vivo imaging.

Immune response evaluation in vivo

Female C57 mice were injected with Hepa1-6 cells (1 × 106 cells/100 μL) were injected subcutaneously into the left leg flank. When the tumor volume reached 100 mm3, tumor-bearing mice were divided into 5 groups (n = 6) to intravenously injection with various samples (PBS, sorafenib, GFS, CAF, and GFS@CAF) according the treatment regime. After 10 days of treatments, tumors were examined by Filipin complex staining, and IHC staining of CRT and GPX4. Additionally, tumors were collected and prepared into single-cell suspension to analyze DCs maturation, CD8+ T cells activation, and the concentration of TNF-α and IFN-γ.

Anti-tumor activity in vivo

Female C57 mice were injected with Hepa1-6 cells (1 × 106/100 μL) into the left leg flank. After the tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, the mice were treated with different formulations: control, sorafenib (5 mg/kg), GFS (52 mg/kg), CAF (75 mg/kg) and GFS@CAF (120 mg/kg). The different formulations were intravenously injected on days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 (n = 9). Tumor volume and weight were measured every other day during 20 days. The tumor volume was calculated according to the following formula: W2× L/2. Based on animal ethics, the mice with tumors exceeding 1000 mm3 would be euthanized in this study. Then, tumor from 3 of mice were analysis with FCM, and fixed with H&E, TUNEL, and Ki67 staining. The remaining mice underwent survival testing.

Biosafety evaluation

Hemolysis assay: Blood samples were collected from mice's eyes and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min to isolate red blood cells. The cells were then washed with PBS and diluted to 2% concentration. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with different concentrations of samples for 1 h at 37°C. Equal volume of PBS and water were added as negative (N) and positive controls (P), respectively. Hemolysis rates were determined by the absorbance of the mixtures at 576 nm using the formula: Hemolysis (%) = (OD576sample - OD576N)/(OD576P - OD576N) × 100%

Histology analysis: The collected tumor tissues and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were fixed in polyformaldehyde fixative buffer. Subsequently, paraffin section examination was conducted for histological analysis with H&E staining to reveal the cellular morphology and structure.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Results are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA analysis with Tukey's post hoc test or Two-tailed Student's t-test calculated Statistical significance. Significance levels were denoted as follows: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; "ns" indicates not significant (p > 0.05).
