Preparedness of dentists to treat patients with intellectual and learning disabilities: a survey in Northern Thailand
Authors: Nattanich Intagun, Wannapa Chinadet, Pinpinut Wanichsaithong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with intellectual and learning disabilities (ILD) face significant barriers to dental care and experience disproportionately poor oral health outcomes. Evidence on dentists' preparedness, confidence, and stress in providing care for this population across public hospital settings in Thailand remains limited.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 306 dentists in public-sector hospitals across 17 provinces of Northern Thailand (November 2024 to May 2025). A validated online questionnaire assessed individual-, institutional-, and system-level preparedness factors. Chi-square tests compared hospital settings, and multivariable binary logistic regression identified independent predictors of high confidence and high stress.ResultsMost respondents (80.72%) had experience treating patients with ILD, though 65.69% treated fewer than ten patients per year. The main challenges were communication difficulties (86.93%) and behavioural management difficulties (83.66%). High or very high stress was reported by 53.26%, and 15.69% reported stress reduced care quality. Access to specialist referral and facility readiness were significantly lower in community hospitals (p < 0.001 and p = 0.003). Overall, 92.48% perceived undergraduate training as insufficient. In the multivariable analysis, adequate personnel and equipment was the only independent predictor of high confidence (OR = 2.739, 95% CI: 1.390–5.396, p = 0.004). High stress was independently associated with more than ten years of clinical experience (OR = 2.325, 95% CI: 1.272–4.251, p = 0.006), postgraduate education (OR = 1.926, 95% CI: 1.030–3.602, p = 0.040), and being a general dentist rather than a specialist (OR = 0.285, 95% CI: 0.146–0.555, p < 0.001).ConclusionsDentists' preparedness is shaped by individual, institutional, and system-level factors. Adequate personnel and equipment was the key predictor of confidence, while stress was linked to clinical experience, postgraduate education, and professional role. Background

Intellectual disability (ID) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterised by significant limitations in intellectual and adaptive functioning, affecting reasoning, learning, communication, social participation, and daily living [1, 2]. In dental care, challenges extend beyond diagnostic labels. Patients with intellectual, learning, communication, behavioural, or adaptive difficulties share common challenges during treatment, including limited cooperation, dependence on caregivers, extended appointment time, and difficulty accessing services.

For this reason, the present study uses the term intellectual and learning disabilities (ILD) as a practical umbrella term. This term includes individuals with ID and other developmental or learning-related difficulties that may affect communication, cooperation, adaptive functioning, or access to dental care. The purpose of using ILD in this study is not to distinguish diagnostic subgroups, but to reflect the group of patients who may require additional communication, behavioural, adaptive, or access-related support in routine dental care. This approach is consistent with our previous study among postgraduate dentists in Thailand, which used ILD as an umbrella term and focused on practical care barriers rather than diagnostic subgroup comparison [3].

Individuals with ILD experience substantial oral health disparities. Previous studies have reported poorer oral hygiene, a high prevalence of untreated dental disease, periodontal problems, and unmet treatment needs among individuals with disabilities or special healthcare needs [4, 5]. These problems may be related to difficulties in oral self-care, dependence on caregivers, communication barriers, behavioural challenges, medication use, and limited access to routine dental services. A previous study in Albania reported a high burden of dental disease among children with disabilities [6]. In Thailand, studies have also reported inadequate caregiver attention to routine oral hygiene and dental check-ups, which may contribute to oral disease progression in this population [7].

Dentists’ preparedness to treat patients with ILD is therefore a multidimensional issue. At the individual level, preparedness may be influenced by dentists’ knowledge, clinical experience, confidence, stress, communication skills, behaviour management skills, and willingness to provide care. Previous studies have reported that lack of confidence, limited clinical experience, and insufficient training are major barriers to providing dental care for patients with ILD or special healthcare needs [8–12]. A previous study among postgraduate dentists in Thailand also found that limited clinical exposure, insufficient training, and low confidence were important barriers to care for this patient group [3].

At the institutional level, dentists’ willingness alone may not be sufficient if the workplace is not prepared to support care delivery. Adequate personnel, appropriate equipment, facility readiness, emergency support, consultation pathways, and access to specialist referral may affect whether treatment can be provided safely and effectively. In Thailand, a previous study reported that only 35% of facilities were adequately prepared to provide dental services for individuals with intellectual disability, while 40% were not ready to provide such services [13]. These findings suggest that workplace readiness is an important part of dentists’ preparedness, not only an external condition.

At the system level, broader health service structures may shape dentists’ ability and willingness to provide care. These include referral networks, health coverage, treatment entitlements, policy support, and possible incentives. Institutional barriers encompass workplace-level factors such as facility readiness, trained personnel, equipment, and referral resources, while systemic barriers refer to broader health-system factors including policy support, coverage, and inter-facility referral pathways [3]. These distinctions are important because care may remain limited even when dentists are willing, if workplace resources and wider service systems are inadequate.

Although dental education and special care dentistry have received increasing attention in Thailand, evidence remains limited regarding dentists currently working in public-sector hospitals across Northern Thailand. These dentists provide routine oral healthcare across different levels of the public health system, including regional, general, and community hospitals. Understanding their preparedness, perceived barriers, confidence, and stress may help guide improvements in dental education, workplace support, referral systems, and policy planning for patients with ILD.

This study aimed to assess dentists’ preparedness to provide dental care for patients with ILD in public-sector hospitals in Northern Thailand, examining clinical experience, confidence, stress, perceived challenges, and willingness to provide care across individual, institutional, and system levels. A secondary aim was to identify factors independently associated with high confidence and high stress.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Human Experimentation Committee, Faculty of Dentistry, Chiang Mai University (Protocol No.66/2023). All participants provided informed consent prior to participation, and responses were collected anonymously and kept confidential.

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study used a self-administered online questionnaire to assess dentists' preparedness to provide dental care for patients with ILD. The study was conducted between November 2024 and May 2025 among dentists working in public-sector hospitals within the Northern Health Service Network of Thailand, which covers 17 provinces. Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [14].

Study population and sampling

The sampling frame comprised 1,251 general dentists and dental specialists in public-sector hospitals across 17 provinces of Northern Thailand, as identified from Ministry of Public Health records. These facilities operated at three levels: regional hospitals (n = 152), general hospitals (n = 226), and community hospitals (n = 873).

The minimum required sample size was calculated using Taro Yamane's formula at a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error, yielding 303 participants. To ensure representation across all three hospital levels, a non-proportional stratified sampling approach was used. All 1,251 eligible dentists were invited to participate through official letters distributed by the Provincial Public Health Offices in November 2024. Participation was voluntary, and the achieved sample reflects responses received within each stratum. A total of 306 dentists completed the questionnaire (overall response rate 24.5%), distributed across regional (n = 30; 19.7% of stratum), general (n = 94; 41.6%), and community hospitals (n = 182; 20.8%). Eligibility was restricted to general dentists or dental specialists currently working in public-sector hospitals who completed the questionnaire in full. Incomplete or invalid responses were excluded. Sample flow is shown in Fig. 1.

Survey instrument

The Thai-language questionnaire used in this study was previously validated by our research team in a related Thai dental population [3]. The instrument was adapted from previously published sources [13, 15, 16] and refined for the Thai public-sector dental context.

Content and face validity were assessed using the Item-Objective Congruence (IOC) method by four experts in dentistry, dental education, and healthcare systems. Items were rated as −1, 0, or + 1, and an IOC value > 0.50 was considered acceptable. All items met this criterion (IOC range: 0.75–1.00) [3]. Minor revisions were made based on expert feedback prior to pilot testing.

The instrument was pilot tested with dentists not included in the main study, and further minor revisions were made to improve clarity before final administration [3]. The final questionnaire consisted of 44 structured items covering three domains: (1) participant characteristics; (2) clinical experience, practice patterns, and perceived challenges; and (3) attitudes, willingness, and system-level perceptions.

Internal consistency was not formally assessed because the questionnaire was designed as a multidomain descriptive instrument rather than a single composite scale, consistent with the original validation approach [3]. The English-translated questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Variables and measures

Variables were organised according to three preparedness levels;

Individual-level variables included gender, age, years of clinical experience (≤ 10 years vs. > 10 years), post-graduation education status (none vs. currently studying or completed any post-graduate programme), professional role (general dentist vs. board-certified specialist), prior experience treating patients with ILD (yes vs. no), training exposure (undergraduate and postgraduate), and dentists' attitudes. Dentists' attitudes were measured using four items rated on a 5-point Likert scale anchored at 1 (very uncomfortable, stressed, or feeling helpless) and 5 (very comfortable and confident): (i) perceived confidence in providing dental care to patients with ILD; (ii) perceived stress in providing such care; (iii) belief that additional postgraduate training would enhance confidence; and (iv) belief that the current undergraduate curriculum helps build confidence. For analytic purposes, responses of 4 and 5 were combined as "high to very high," and responses of 1 to 3 were combined as "no to moderate."

Institutional-level variables described the immediate workplace environment and included hospital level (regional and general hospitals grouped as higher-level public healthcare facilities vs. community hospitals as district-level service settings), in-house availability of specialists for consultation or referral, perceived adequacy of personnel and equipment, facility readiness to manage patients with ILD, treatment cost coverage at the workplace, additional time required for ILD care, and service fee differences.

System-level variables described broader health-system context and included perceived adequacy of inter-facility referral networks, awareness of relevant national policies on ILD care, willingness to provide care if additional incentives were offered, and willingness to provide care if oral healthcare systems were strengthened.

Data collection

The questionnaire was distributed via Google Forms. Before beginning, respondents received written information explaining the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the anonymity of responses, and the procedure for completion. Dentists completed the survey independently, without assistance, to minimise response bias and enhance data reliability.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2010 and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics, clinical experience, and attitudes. Comparisons between workplace settings (higher-level vs. community hospitals) were examined using chi-square tests. Fisher’s exact test was applied when expected cell counts were below five.

The two primary outcomes were high or very high confidence and high or very high stress in providing dental care for patients with ILD, both defined as a Likert response of 4 or 5. Bivariate associations with candidate predictor variables were examined using chi-square. Predictors from all three preparedness levels were included in multivariable binary logistic regression models when bivariate p < 0.20 or when conceptually relevant. Two models were fitted, one for each outcome.

Post-graduation education status and professional role were retained as separate predictors because they capture conceptually distinct constructs. Collinearity was assessed using pairwise correlations and variance inflation factors (VIFs); all predictors met acceptable thresholds (r < 0.70; VIF < 5). Adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 306 dentists participated in the survey. Most were aged 23–34 years (69.6%), female (64.1%), had fewer than 10 years of clinical experience (75.2%), and worked as general dentists (64.1%). Most were employed in community hospitals (59.5%) and Health Network 1 (71.2%) (Table 1). For comparative analyses, regional and general hospitals were grouped as higher-level facilities and compared with community hospitals.

Individual-level preparedness

Most respondents (80.72%) had prior experience treating patients with ILD, with significantly higher exposure among community hospital dentists (p < 0.001). However, most treated fewer than 10 ILD patients annually. Oral examination was the most common procedure, while tooth extraction was significantly more frequent in community hospitals (p = 0.007).

Communication difficulties and behavioural management challenges were the most commonly reported barriers. Communication difficulties were more frequently reported in community hospitals (p = 0.007), whereas pharmacological behaviour management and postgraduate ILD-related training were more common in higher-level hospitals (p < 0.001). Although undergraduate exposure to ILD care was common, 92.48% considered it insufficient preparation.

Overall confidence in managing patients with ILD was moderate, while more than half of respondents reported high stress levels. Although most stated that stress did not prevent treatment, over half indicated they would prefer to avoid treating these patients if possible.

Institutional-level factors

Workplace-related findings are shown in Tables 2 and 3. Access to specialist referral was significantly higher in regional or general hospitals than in community hospitals (p < 0.001), as was facility readiness to manage patients with ILD (p = 0.003). Conversely, treatment costs were significantly more likely to be fully covered in community hospitals (p < 0.001). Service fees were more often reported as no different from those for other patients in community hospitals than in regional or general hospitals (p < 0.001).

System-level support, policy awareness, and willingness to provide care

Regarding system-level support, 23.20% of respondents reported that an adequate referral network was available, with no significant difference between hospital settings (p = 0.735). Policy awareness was limited and differed markedly between settings, dentists in regional or general hospitals were significantly more likely to report awareness of relevant policies than those in community hospitals (p < 0.001). Most respondents perceived care for patients with ILD as more complex than care for the general population (89.87%), and this perception was significantly more common in community hospitals than in regional or general hospitals (p = 0.036). A total of 74.84% reported that treatment could involve a risk of unintentional harm to the patient or dental team, with no significant difference between settings (p = 0.630) (Table 3).

A total of 75.82% of respondents reported willingness to provide treatment if adequate personnel and equipment were available, and this was significantly more common among dentists in community hospitals than in regional or general hospitals (p = 0.003). In addition, 88.56% reported that improvements in oral healthcare systems would increase their willingness to provide care, with no significant difference between settings (p = 0.303). Stress levels did not differ significantly between hospital settings across any of the stress categories, nor did the preference to avoid treating these patients. Better facilities were reported to increase confidence by 72.22% of respondents (p = 0.094 between settings), while improved personnel and referral systems were reported to increase confidence by 90.52% (p = 0.921 between settings).

Levels of confidence and stress, and perceptions of training adequacy

As illustrated in Fig. 2, overall confidence in providing care to patients with ILD was moderate. Only 6.21% of dentists reported very high confidence, while 9.48% reported no confidence at all. In contrast, stress levels were substantial, with 35.29% reporting high stress and 17.97% very high stress. Only a small proportion reported low (9.15%) or no stress (3.27%), indicating that psychological burden is common when managing this patient group.

Distribution of confidence and stress levels among dentists providing dental care to patients with intellectual and learning disabilities (n = 306)

Most respondents believed that additional postgraduate training and clinical practice would enhance their confidence. Nearly half (48.04%) expressed high agreement, and 28.43% very high agreement, with this statement, while disagreement was minimal. Perceptions of the current undergraduate curriculum were more varied. Although some dentists reported moderate (34.31%) to high agreement (25.16%) that it helps build confidence, fewer expressed very high agreement (19.93%); a notable proportion reported low (14.38%) or no agreement (6.21%). These findings suggest that while the existing curriculum provides some foundation, many dentists perceive a need for further practical training to strengthen confidence in managing patients with ILD.

Factors associated with confidence and stress

Bivariate analysis (Table 4) showed that prior experience treating patients with ILD was significantly associated with high or very high confidence (p = 0.039). High or very high confidence was also significantly associated with access to specialist consultation or referral at the workplace (p = 0.004) and willingness to provide care if additional incentives were offered (p = 0.039). Postgraduate education showed a borderline association with confidence (p = 0.053).

For high or very high stress, years of clinical experience, postgraduate education status, and professional role remained significant in the multivariable model. Discussion

This study examined dentists’ preparedness to provide dental care for patients with ILD in public-sector hospitals in Northern Thailand. The findings show that preparedness is shaped by interacting factors at the individual, institutional, and system levels. Although most respondents had previously treated patients with ILD, many reported limited case exposure, moderate confidence, high stress, and perceived training gaps, suggesting that clinical exposure alone is insufficient without adequate training, workplace resources, and referral support.

Prior experience treating patients with ILD was associated with higher confidence in bivariate analysis, consistent with evidence that experiential learning supports competence in managing patients with special care needs [17]. However, this association did not persist in the multivariable model, where adequate personnel and equipment was the only significant predictor of high confidence. This suggests that individual experience may contribute to confidence, but its effect depends on whether the workplace provides adequate support.

Adequate personnel and equipment was the only factor that independently predicted high confidence, indicating that preparedness reflects not only personal attributes but also the clinical environment. Without appropriate institutional support—including trained personnel, equipment, and referral pathways—even willing dentists may feel unable to provide care confidently, consistent with previous reports [13, 18].

High stress was associated with longer clinical experience and postgraduate education, while specialists were less likely to report high stress. Longer experience and postgraduate training may heighten awareness of clinical complexity and the gap between available and needed support. In contrast, specialists may have more advanced training and better referral access, reducing stress when managing patients with ILD. Similar patterns have been reported internationally [10].

Most respondents perceived undergraduate training as insufficient, consistent with global evidence [19, 20] and our previous study in Thailand [3]. Curriculum improvement should include practical clinical exposure, communication strategies, behaviour management training, and interprofessional collaboration, not only theoretical content.

Hospital level itself was not an independent predictor of confidence or stress, suggesting that actual resource availability within the workplace matters more than the hospital type. Improving preparedness therefore requires direct investment in personnel, equipment, facility readiness, and referral systems at all hospital levels.

Many respondents reported limited policy awareness and inadequate referral networks. Clearer referral pathways, better policy communication, and inter-facility coordination may help dentists provide care more confidently [21]. Access for patients with ILD also remains linked to entitlement schemes—the Universal Coverage Scheme, Disability Identification Card, or Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme—which are generally restricted to government healthcare settings [13].

The use of ILD as a practical umbrella term, rather than a diagnostic category, should be noted when interpreting the findings. Future studies may examine whether barriers differ among specific diagnostic subgroups such as intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, or other developmental conditions.

Limitations include the cross-sectional design, reliance on self-reported data, predominance of Health Network 1 respondents (71.2%), high proportion of early-career dentists (75.2% with < 10 years experience), and the grouping of regional and general hospitals which may mask between-type differences. Future studies with larger, more balanced samples would improve generalizability.

Improving preparedness requires not only better training but also adequate workplace resources, structured referral pathways, and clearer policy support. Addressing these factors together may increase confidence, reduce stress, and improve access to care for patients with ILD.

Conclusion

This study identified gaps in dentists’ preparedness to provide dental care for patients with ILD in public-sector hospitals in Northern Thailand. Although most respondents had previous experience treating this patient group, many reported limited case exposure, moderate confidence, high stress, and perceived insufficiency in undergraduate training.

Preparedness was shaped by factors at the individual, institutional, and system levels. At the institutional level, the availability of adequate personnel and equipment was the only significant predictor of high confidence in the multivariable analysis. At the individual level, high stress was associated with longer clinical experience and postgraduate education status, while specialists were less likely than general dentists to report high stress. These findings indicate that dentists’ preparedness cannot be addressed through training alone, but also requires workplace readiness, adequate personnel and equipment, and clear referral support.

Strengthening practical education, improving institutional resources, and developing coordinated referral and support systems may help increase confidence, reduce stress, and improve access to dental care for patients with ILD.
