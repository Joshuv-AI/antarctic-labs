Application of subretinal injection of balanced salt solution for the management of large-diameter macular holes
Authors: Xia Wang, Xiaoli Li, Bin Wu, Quanhong Han, Ying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of subretinal injection of balanced salt solution (BSS) combined with vitrectomy and internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling for managing large-diameter macular holes (MHs ≥ 400 μm).MethodsA prospective interventional study was conducted on 31 eyes of 31 patients with full-thickness MHs (including 5 refractory MHs). Patients underwent 25 G vitrectomy, ILM peeling, followed by subretinal BSS injection using a 41 G needle. Outcomes were assessed at 1 and 3 months postoperatively with optical coherence tomography (OCT), best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), and microperimetry. Metrics of the microperimetry included macular integrity index (MII) and macular threshold (MT). Ten eyes completed 6 month followup. Changes in BCVA (LogMAR), MII, and MT before and after surgery, as well as MH closure and complications were compared and analyzed. Functional outcomes were analyzed only in eyes with successful type I closure.ResultsAt 1 month, 93.54% (29/31) achieved complete MH closure. BCVA improved from 1.34 ± 0.59 preoperatively to 0.67 ± 0.20 at 1 month and 0.48 ± 0.36 at 3 months (p < 0.001). MII improved from 98.77 ± 4.83 to 96.23 ± 11.38 (p = 0.015) and MT from 17.53 ± 5.04 to 22.54 ± 3.91 dB (p < 0.001) at 3 months. In 10 eyes with 6 month followup, BCVA further improved to 0.41 ± 0.30 and MT to 24.64 ± 2.35 dB (bothp < 0.05). The refractory subgroup (n = 5) achieved 100% complete closure. No complications were observed during the follow-up time.ConclusionCombined 41 G subretinal BSS injection with PPV and ILM peeling is effective and safe for treating largediameter MHs (≥400 μm), achieving high anatomical closure rates and sustained visual functional improvement up to 6 months. Preliminary outcomes in refractory MHs are favorable; larger cohorts are needed for validation.

Introduction

Macular hole (MH) is a full-thickness defect in the neuroepithelium of the macula that can cause vision loss. Studies indicate that the prevalence of MH in the general population is approximately 3.3 per 1,000 individuals [1]. Over recent decades, vitrectomy has become the standard treatment for idiopathic full-thickness macular holes (FTMH), This procedure encompasses pars plana vitrectomy (PPV), posterior cortical vitreous removal, peeling of internal limiting membrane (ILM), complete fluid-gas exchange, and postoperative face-down positioning [2–4]. With advancements in vitrectomy techniques, PPV surgery for MH demonstrated remarkable postoperative outcomes, achieving a closure rate exceeding 90% in initial surgeries [5, 6]. However, the postoperative success rate significantly decreases as the diameter of MH increases [7]. Furthermore, conventional PPV shows suboptimal results in cases involving MH with prolonged duration, high myopia, or traumatic origin, making surgical treatment of such cases particularly challenging. Several factors contribute to the low closure rate in refractory MH cases, including large hole diameter, extended duration of the condition [8].

In recent years, researchers have dedicated themselves to exploring and improving surgical methods for refractory MH. These methods include ILM flap coverage or filling, and the utilization of biological materials such as autologous ILM, lens capsule, retina or amniotic membrane and other biological materials for hole filling [5, 9–13]. Additionally, some researchers employ perfluorocarbon, autologous serum, and viscoelastic agents as auxiliary fixation materials during surgery [11, 12, 14]. However, each surgical method has its limitations, including difficulties in intraoperative material collection and fixation, variable recovery of macular foveal morphology and visual function postoperatively as well as postoperative detachment and MH recurrence. Currently, there is no standardized protocol for the surgical treatment of refractory MH internationally, and ophthalmic surgeons continue to search for optimal surgical approaches. Recent studies have shown that the use of subretinal fluid (SRF) can effectively separate the adhesion under the neuroepithelium near the macular hole, thereby promoting retinal reattachment in the hole area and improving both structural and functional recovery of MH [15, 16].

Previous studies have demonstrated that subretinal BSS injection, as a rescue procedure, significantly improves anatomical closure rates in refractory, persistent and giant MHs [7, 16–22]. However, its potential role as an adjuvant technique in primary surgery for large idiopathic MHs (IMHs) remains understudied. We hypothesized that combining conventional ILM peeling with subretinal BSS injection could both effectively relieve epiretinal and vitreoretinal traction and enhance retinal compliance, thereby facilitating early restoration of foveal microstructure and improving visual function. This study aims to evaluate the safety and efficacy of 25 G PPV with ILM peeling and 41 G subretinal BSS injection in MHs with minimum linear diameter ≥ 400 μm, including a subgroup of refractory cases, to explore its value in both primary and salvage scenarios.

Methods

This prospective interventional study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Patients who visited a single retina specialist at the outpatient department of Tianjin Eye Hospital from March to August 2024 were enrolled in this study. The age range of participants was from 18 to 85 years old. All subjects had FTMHs confirmed by optical coherence tomography (OCT), with the minimum hole diameter exceeding 400 μm. The study including persistent, idiopathic, traumatic, and high myopia-related MH. Exclusion criteria comprised patients with concurrent retinal or optic nerve pathologies that could interfere with visual function assessment, and those unable to maintain regular postoperative follow-up appointments.

Prior to surgery, all patients underwent a thorough medical history review and comprehensive ophthalmic examination. The medical history documentation included the duration between symptom onset (such as decreased vision or metamorphopsia) and surgical intervention, previous ocular surgeries, medication usage, ocular trauma, and systemic diseases. The ophthalmic evaluation consisted of multiple measurements: best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), equivalent spherical diopter (ESD), intraocular pressure (IOP), slit-lamp and indirect ophthalmoscopy examination. BCVA was assessed using the ETDRS chart and converted to LogMAR score for statistical analysis. Additional diagnostic measurements included fundus photography (CLARUS 500, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc.); OCT scanning with B-scan measurements of the MH’s minimum and base diameters (in μm); and axial length (AL) measurement using Haag-Streit EyeSuite Biometry, LS 900 (in mm); retinal autofluorescence (AF) photography (Panoramic Ophthalmoscope-Daytona P200T, Optos Plc, AF mode). The microperimetry (Maia, Centervue, Fixation-Training, Version 2.6.0) employed a 4–2 pattern to measure retinal function within 10° of the macular fovea, recording both the Macular Integrity Index (MII), and Macular Threshold (MT, dB).

Homemade Microinjection System Assembly Instructions: Begin with a 1 ml syringe and carefully remove both the needle and plunger while retaining the rubber stopper. Reinsert the rubber stopper into the syringe barrel and fill with BSS fluid. Remove the surgical interface from one end of the silicone oil tube and insert this end into the 1 ml syringe barrel. Attach a 41 G microinjection needle (Ansjjia, 29,001-25 G/41 G) to the front of the syringe. Connect the opposite end of the silicone oil tube to the vitrectomy machine interface (Constellation Vision System, Alcon Laboratories). Set the injection pressure between 12 and 16 PSI. The system becomes fully sealed when the silicone oil tube is connected to the 1 ml syringe, as the tube’s outer diameter precisely matches the syringe barrel’s inner diameter.

A standard three-port 25 G PPV is performed to remove both the vitreous and posterior cortical vitreous. Following indocyanine green (ICG) staining, the ILM is peeled off within the boundaries of the upper and lower vascular arcades in the macular area. Using the 41 G microinjection needle from the microinjection system, SRF is administered approximately 1 Disc Diameter (DD) from the MH’s edge. The injection fluid consists of BSS and is delivered at three strategic locations - superior, inferior, and temporal to the macula - to avoid the papillomacular bundle damage (Fig. 1). The injection volume is regulated by monitoring the release of retinal adhesion under the MH, with the endpoint being the detachment of the retinal edge around the hole. To minimize potential retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) damage, subretinal injection can be initiated before complete retinal penetration [19]. Following the injection, a thorough gas-liquid exchange is completed. For patients with MHs exceeding 600 μm in minimum diameter, a 0.5 ml intravitreal C3F8 gas injection is administered at the conclusion of surgery, followed by a five-day face-down positioning protocol. In cases of concurrent cataracts, phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation is performed during the same surgical session. Follow-ups examinations were conducted at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months after surgery. The 1-week postoperative assessment included slit lamp examination, indirect ophthalmoscopy and IOP measurements. During the 1-month and 3-month follow-up visits, comprehensive evaluations were performed, including BCVA, IOP measurements, slit lamp examination, indirect ophthalmoscopy fundus examination, AF photography, OCT, and microperimetry.

The primary endpoint of the follow-up was the MH closure status, assessed by OCT one month postoperatively. The closure patterns classified into three types: Type I: Complete closure with full neuroepithelial coverage over the foveal center and no exposed RPE, presenting as “U” or “V” shaped healing; Type II: Incomplete closure with neuroepithelium attached to the RPE at the MH edges but with exposed RPE at the foveal center, showing “W” shaped healing [14, 15]; Type III: Non-closure with detached neuroepithelium at the MH edges. Secondary endpoints comprised postoperative improvements in visual function including (1) Changes in BCVA, (2) Changes in microperimetry indices MII and MT compared to preoperative. Additionally, AF imaging was used to evaluate RPE damage. Postoperative complications were monitored, including retinal detachment, choroidal detachment, retinal tears, IOP abnormalities, endophthalmitis, choroidal neovascularization (CNV), and the development of cataracts in patients with preoperatively clear lenses was also documented.

Statistics and analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software (version 25.0). Variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to evaluate the normality of continuous variables. For comparing pre- and post-operative continuous variables, Student’s t-test was applied to data with normal distribution and homogeneous variance, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test was used for data with either non-normal distribution or unequal variance. Relationships between variables were examined using Pearson correlation analysis. The analyzed outcomes included changes in BCVA and microperimetry measurements, early MH closure rates and the frequency of postoperative complications. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at p ≤ 0.05. The sample size was calculated based on a target MH closure rate of 90% after surgery, with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 0.8. The calculated sample size was 28 cases. However, a total of 31 cases were actually enrolled in the study.

Results

This study included 31 patients (31 eyes), comprising 7 males and 24 females, with ages ranging from 23 to 81 years. The preoperative BCVA (LogMAR) was 1.34 ± 0.59. Of the 31 eyes, 24 presented with idiopathic MHs, including 5 cases with persistent MHs that failed to close after initial PPV. Five eyes had high myopia-related MH, and two eyes had traumatic MH. The minimum MH diameter measured 569.06 ± 117.14 μm, while the base diameter was 1025.29 ± 292.62 μm. Twenty-two eyes underwent concurrent cataract phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation. Discussion

Studies have demonstrated that SRF application can effectively reduce retinal adhesion beneath MH, thereby enhancing both anatomical and functional MH repositioning [16, 20, 21]. This study evaluates the safety and efficacy of combined 25 G vitrectomy, ILM peeling, and 41 G subretinal BSS microinjection for large-diameter MH. Compared with the proportional reflux technique using a silicone soft-tip extrusion cannula that passively delivers BSS to the hole margin [16], our 41 G microinjection directly creates a controlled, localized macular detachment beneath the retina through a self-sealing micro-puncturotomy. This approach more effectively separates the adhesion between the neuroepithelium and RPE, liberates the hole edges, reduces retinal tension, and facilitates retinal repositioning. The process leverages the retina’s natural elasticity and centripetal force to promote hole healing [25]. In our study of 31 cases treating MH with diameters exceeding 400 μm, the early postoperative (1 month) complete closure rate reached 93.54% (29/31), with an incomplete closure rate of 6.45% (2/31). This result was comparable to long-term follow up data from the previous studies [17, 19, 22]. Notably, no postoperative complications were observed, initially demonstrating the safety and effectiveness of this approach for large-diameter MH treatment.

In terms of visual function, patients demonstrated significant improvement in BCVA at both 1 and 3 months postoperatively compared to preoperative measurements (LogMAR BCVA: preoperative 1.34 ± 0.59, 1-month postoperative 0.67 ± 0.2, and 3-month postoperative 0.48 ± 0.36, p < 0.001). While visual acuity provides important information, microperimetry offers a more objective assessment of macular area visual sensitivity. The microperimetry results at the 1-month and 3-month follow-up revealed significant improvements in both the MII and MT compared to preoperative values (p < 0.05), consistent with previous studies [22, 26]. These findings indicate enhanced visual function within the 10° macular region at 1 and 3 months post-surgery. (Table 2). In this study, measurable improvement in MT was detected as early as the first postoperative month. This favorable functional change can be attributed to rapid resolution of macular edema, relief of photoreceptor injury, and early stabilization of foveal microarchitecture. Notably, our microperimetry analysis focused on global macular threshold across the entire macular area, rather than localized retinal sensitivity around the MH. Accumulated clinical evidence has demonstrated that perilesional retinal sensitivity may experience a transient reduction in the early postoperative period after MH surgery [27, 28], which is a common regional functional change and does not contradict the overall improvement in macular function. In the present cohort, supplementary 6-month follow-up data available in 10 enrolled eyes further confirmed durable functional improvement after combined surgical management. Longitudinal analysis revealed continuous improvement in LogMAR BCVA and MT from baseline through postoperative 6 months; BCVA improved rapidly within the first postoperative month, remained relatively stable from 1 to 3 months, and gained additional significant visual benefit between 3 and 6 months, accompanied by steady elevation of MT across all follow-up intervals. In contrast, the MII declined gradually over time, which may reflect progressive foveal microstructure remodeling after MH anatomical closure.

When comparing the postoperative visual function recovery between different MH diameters, the L group demonstrated faster recovery than the XL group at one-month post-surgery. However, by three months postoperatively, both groups showed comparable improvements in BCVA, microperimetry MII, and MT was illustrated in Table 3. In the current study，OCT imaging showed restoration of the outer retinal layers at 3 months postoperatively (Fig. 2). These findings provide preliminary evidence that subretinal BSS injection contributes effectively to both structural and functional recovery in large-diameter MH over the long term.

Meanwhile, the subgroup consisting of five refractory MHs that remained patent after initial PPV combined with ILM flap harvesting achieved 100% type I closure following subretinal BSS injection, with measurable gains in both BCVA and MT at 3 months. These preliminary subgroup findings suggest that supplementary SRF injection provides an effective salvage option for refractory MH unresponsive to conventional ILM flap surgery.

The primary goal of FTMH treatment is to restore the continuity of the retinal neuroepithelium and improve central visual function. For IMH, ILM flap surgery has greatly increased closure rates. Bleidißel N et al. [29] reported a 100% closure rate for large MHs with this technique, with postoperative BCVA closely related to the integrity of the outer retinal layers. The ILM flap serves as a scaffold to activate Müller cells, and subsequent gliosis promotes hole closure. However, excessive glial proliferation may be accompanied by the release of cytotoxic and inflammatory mediators, potentially causing neurotoxicity and limiting functional recovery [30]. In refractory MHs—including those persistent after primary surgery, traumatic, or secondary to uveitis—tight adhesions may exist between the retinal neuroepithelium and the RPE at the hole margin. SRF induction can release these adhesions, increase retinal mobility, promote neuroepithelial reattachment, and facilitate MH healing [21]. Unlike flap- or graft-based techniques, SRF induction aims to mobilize the native retinal tissue rather than provide an additional scaffold. This mechanism may help maintain a more physiological macular configuration and support postoperative visual recovery.

In the correlation analysis, postoperative BCVA at final follow-up demonstrated significant positive correlations with preoperative BCVA, disease duration (r = 0.63, p = 0.001), and age (r = 0.526, p = 0.003). These findings suggest that the duration of the MH and age may significantly influence the prognosis and visual outcomes of large-diameter MH. Two cases with type II closure showed no improvement in postoperative BCVA. The first case involved a patient with a 72-month disease duration and a MH minimum diameter of 896 μm. The second case was an 81-year-old patient with a 12-month disease duration and a MH minimum diameter of 738 μm. Both cases had MH base diameters exceeding 1300 μm, and neither patient adhered to the prescribed postoperative prone positioning. The incomplete MH closure in these cases likely resulted from the extended disease duration, large MH diameter, and non-compliance with postoperative face-down position. Our findings, consistent with previous studies [7, 8], indicate that refractory MH cases with larger diameters and longer durations exhibit lower rates of complete closure and poorer visual recovery. Furthermore, research has confirmed that maintaining prone or face-down positioning during the early postoperative period significantly affects the prognosis of large-diameter MH [31]. The correlation between age and postoperative BCVA observed in this study may be attributed to elderly patients’ physical limitations in maintaining the required face-down position. These findings emphasize the importance for clinicians to thoroughly evaluate multiple factors when making surgical decisions, including MH characteristics, disease duration, patient age, and physical condition.

In this study, we performed subretinal injection of BSS using a custom-designed microinjection system integrated with a vitrectomy machine. No intraoperative and postoperative complications were observed, confirming the safety and feasibility of this technique. However, potential complications may still occur, including retinal breaks, retinal detachment, retinal and choroidal hemorrhage, CNV, and RPE damage.

This study has several limitations. Its prospective, single-center, single-surgeon design and small sample size may limit generalizability, and the absence of subretinal BSS injection–related complications may partly reflect surgical expertise. In addition, because this was a single-arm study of combined vitrectomy, ILM peeling, and subretinal BSS injection, we could not determine the independent contribution of subretinal BSS injection beyond ILM peeling alone. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as reflecting the safety and outcomes of the combined approach rather than the specific efficacy of each component. Concurrent phacoemulsification in most eyes may have contributed to visual improvement, and the limited sample size precluded multivariate adjustment. Finally, the short follow-up and heterogeneous cohort, including few refractory cases, limit assessment of long-term durability and subgroup-specific outcomes. The absence of late-onset complications such as progressive cataract may still be related to the limited follow-up time Future prospective controlled studies are needed to clarify the incremental benefit of subretinal BSS injection.

Conclusion

The 41 G subretinal microinjection technique with BSS appears to be a feasible and safe adjunct to vitrectomy with ILM peeling for large-diameter MHs. The favorable anatomical and visual outcomes observed in this study suggest its potential as an adjunctive surgical technique, although controlled studies are needed to determine its incremental benefit.
