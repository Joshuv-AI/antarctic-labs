Maximizing the Steklov eigenvalues on trees with a diameter constraint
Jiangdong Ai Huiqiu Lin Yongtang Shi

Abstract
We study the first nonzero Steklov eigenvalue λ 2 ​ ( T , δ ​ Ω ) \lambda_{2}(T,\delta\Omega) of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator on a finite tree T T with leaf boundary δ ​ Ω \delta\Omega , under a constraint on the diameter D D . He and Hua [Calc. Var. PDE, 2022] showed that λ 2 ​ ( T ) ≤ 2 / D \lambda_{2}(T)\leq 2/D for any tree of diameter D D , with the even-diameter equality case fully characterized. For odd D D , the geometric picture underlying the sharp configurations has remained unclear beyond diameter three. We determine this picture completely for all odd diameters D = 2 ​ r + 1 ≥ 5 D=2r+1\geq 5 . The sharp value of λ 2 \lambda_{2} is achieved on spider trees with nearly-equidistributed branch lengths, forming the family of generalized almost seesaw trees AS ⁡ ( r , q + 2 , c , t ) \mathrm{AS}(r,q+2,c,t) , prescribed by the arithmetic of n n relative to ⌈ r / 2 ⌉ \lceil r/2\rceil . Together with the results of He-Hua and Lin-Zhao [Bull. Lond. Math. Soc., 2025] for even diameters and diameter three, this completes the geometric classification for every diameter. The argument is based on a scalar root equation for one-center profiles, an inverse boundary quadratic form on boundary fluxes, and a reduction scheme from arbitrary trees to two-center profiles, and then to the one-center class. The inverse variational viewpoint may be regarded as a boundary analogue of the classical distance-matrix formalism for trees initiated by Graham and Lovász [Adv. Math., 1978].

Introduction

Among classical questions in spectral geometry lies the problem of minimizing or maximizing
eigenvalues under geometric constraints. In the continuous setting, Steklov eigenvalues under a
diameter constraint were studied, for instance, by Al Sayed, Bogosel, Henrot, and Nacry

[
2
]
in the convex Euclidean framework. The present paper is
concerned with a discrete analogue of this theme for trees, viewed as one-dimensional singular
domains endowed with their leaf boundary.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a finite tree and let
\delta\Omega\subset V
be its leaf set. We regard

\delta\Omega
as the boundary of
T
and denote by
V(T)\setminus\delta\Omega
the interior.
For a function
f\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{R}
, the discrete Laplace operator is defined by

\Delta f(v):=\sum_{w\sim v}\bigl(f(v)-f(w)\bigr),\qquad v\in V(T),

and, for a boundary leaf
\omega\in\delta\Omega
with unique neighbor
\omega^{\prime}
, the outward
normal derivative is

\partial_{n}f(\omega):=f(\omega)-f(\omega^{\prime}).

A real number
\lambda\geq 0
is called a Steklov eigenvalue of
(T,\delta\Omega)
if there exists a
nontrivial function
f\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\Delta f(v)=0\quad\text{for }v\in V(T)\setminus\delta\Omega,\qquad\partial_{n}f(\omega)=\lambda f(\omega)\quad\text{for }\omega\in\delta\Omega.

Thus harmonicity is imposed at interior points, while the Steklov condition is prescribed on the
boundary leaves. The corresponding spectrum is a finite nondecreasing sequence

0=\lambda_{1}(T,\delta\Omega)\leq\lambda_{2}(T,\delta\Omega)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{|\delta\Omega|}(T,\delta\Omega),

we abbreviate
\lambda_{k}(T,\delta\Omega)
as
\lambda_{k}(T)
or simply
\lambda_{k}
when there is no
confusion. Given
g\in\mathbb{R}^{\delta\Omega}
, its harmonic extension
\widehat{g}
is the
unique function on
V(T)
with
\widehat{g}|_{\delta\Omega}=g
and
\Delta\widehat{g}=0
on the
interior. The associated Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator is

\Lambda_{T}g:=(\partial_{n}\widehat{g})|_{\delta\Omega}.

The first nonzero Steklov eigenvalue then admits the variational characterization

\lambda_{2}(T,\delta\Omega)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathbb{R}^{\delta\Omega}\setminus\{0\}\\
\sum_{\omega\in\delta\Omega}g(\omega)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{xy\in E(T)}\bigl(\widehat{g}(x)-\widehat{g}(y)\bigr)^{2}}{\sum_{\omega\in\delta\Omega}g(\omega)^{2}}.

In particular, the problem is a diameter-constrained maximization problem for a boundary spectral
quantity on a discrete one-dimensional domain.

In this paper we study

\max\{\lambda_{2}(T,\delta\Omega):|V(T)|=n,\ \operatorname{diam}(T)=D\}

when the diameter
D
is odd. The Steklov problem on manifolds goes back to Stekloff and is now one of
the central topics in spectral geometry; see the surveys of Girouard–Polterovich and
Colbois–Girouard–Gordon–Sher

[
19

]
.
Its discrete counterpart on graphs, via Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators, has developed rapidly in
recent years; representative references include

For trees, He and Hua established sharp diameter upper bounds for Steklov eigenvalues

diameter
3

[

The remaining open case in the prescribed-diameter problem is therefore the odd-diameter regime
D=2r+1\geq 5

A complementary viewpoint is provided by the distance matrix of a tree. Classical works of
Graham–Pollak, Graham–Lovász, and Merris show that several spectral questions on trees can be

Let

A more explicit order-by-order formulation is given later in Theorem

. The
structural core of the proof can be isolated in the following reduction principle.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
D=2r+1\geq 3
be odd and let
n\geq D+1
. For every tree
T
with
|V(T)|=n
and

\operatorname{diam}(T)=D
, there exists a double spider of the same order and diameter such that
\lambda_{2}(T)\leq\lambda_{2}(D_{T}).
If equality holds, then
T
itself is a double spider.

Moreover, every extremal double spider is a spider.
In particular, every extremizer of
\lambda_{2}
among trees with order
n
and diameter

is a spider.

The proof has three layers. In Section
2
we work in the one-center class and
encode a spider by its branch-length profile. The first nonzero Steklov eigenvalue is then
characterized as the unique root of a scalar rational function, which allows us to balance the lateral and principal branches and to reduce the one-center optimization to a unimodal scalar parameter.

The passage from arbitrary trees to one-center profiles is variational. In Section
3
we introduce an inverse boundary quadratic form on mean-zero
boundary fluxes. A cut decomposition shows that this form can be written both as a sum of square cut fluxes and as
-\frac{1}{2}z^{T}D_{\delta\Omega}z
, thereby connecting the Steklov problem to the classical distance-matrix theory of trees. We then exploit the sign pattern of maximizing fluxes on two-center profiles to compare arbitrary trees with suitable double spiders and to transport nonprincipal branches from one side to the other. This yields the one-center rigidity embodied in Theorem
1.3
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
is devoted to one-center profiles:
we introduce the generalized almost seesaw trees, derive the one-variable root equation, and prove
both the exact optimization theorem and the unimodality in the profile parameter. In
Section
3
we reformulate the problem in terms of boundary fluxes and inverse quadratic
forms, and we compare arbitrary trees with suitable two-center profiles. In Section
4

we combine these ingredients to obtain the global two-profile reduction, the single-threshold
comparison, and the final order-by-order formulation.

2
Profile optimization in the one-center class

2.1
One-center profiles and generalized almost seesaw trees

A spider
S(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{b})
is the tree obtained by gluing together one endpoint of each of
b

pairwise internally disjoint paths of lengths
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{b}
. The common endpoint is called
the center, and the numbers
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{b}
are the branch lengths.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
r\geq 1
,
b\geq 2
,
c\geq 1
be integers, and let
0\leq t\leq b-2
. The generalized almost
seesaw tree
\mathrm{AS}(r,b,c,t)
is the spider obtained by gluing together one endpoint of
b
paths,
where one path has length
r
, one path has length
r+1
, exactly
t
of the remaining
b-2

paths have length
c+1
, and the other
b-2-t
paths have length
c
.

If
M\geq 1
,
\lceil M/r\rceil\leq q\leq M
, and

M=qc+t,\qquad 0\leq t<q,

then the corresponding generalized almost seesaw tree is

\mathrm{AS}(r,q+2,c,t)=S\bigl(r+1,r,\underbrace{c+1,\dots,c+1}_{t},\underbrace{c,\dots,c}_{q-t}\bigr).

The geometry of a spider is completely encoded by its branch-length profile. This turns the diameter-constrained problem in the one-center class into a finite-dimensional redistribution problem. We begin with the exact eigenvalue equation for such a profile.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
T=S(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{b})

be a spider with
\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}\geq\ell_{3}\geq\cdots\geq\ell_{b}\geq 1.

Define

F_{\ell}(\lambda):=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\frac{1}{1-\ell_{i}\lambda}.

Then the first nonzero Steklov eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}(T)
is the unique root of
F_{\ell}

in the interval
\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{1}},\frac{1}{\ell_{2}}\right).

Proof.

Let
o
be the center of the spider.
For each branch, write
o=v_{i,0}\sim v_{i,1}\sim\cdots\sim v_{i,\ell_{i}},

where
v_{i,\ell_{i}}
is the leaf at the end of the
i
-th branch.
Let
x_{i}
be the boundary value at
v_{i,\ell_{i}}
, and let
m=f(o)

be the value at the center.
Since the harmonic extension is affine on each path,

f(v_{i,k})=m+\frac{k}{\ell_{i}}(x_{i}-m),0\leq k\leq\ell_{i}.

Hence the normal derivative at the leaf
v_{i,\ell_{i}}
is

\partial_{n}f(v_{i,\ell_{i}})=f(v_{i,\ell_{i}})-f(v_{i,\ell_{i}-1})=\frac{x_{i}-m}{\ell_{i}}.

Therefore the Steklov eigenvalue equation reads

\frac{x_{i}-m}{\ell_{i}}=\lambda x_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,b.

(1)

Now the center is harmonic, so

\sum_{i=1}^{b}\bigl(f(v_{i,1})-m\bigr)=0.

Using the affine formula on each branch, this is equivalent to

\sum_{i=1}^{b}\frac{x_{i}-m}{\ell_{i}}=0.

By (
1
) we obtain

\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{b}x_{i}=0.

Thus, for any nonzero eigenvalue,
\sum_{i}x_{i}=0
.

Assume now that
m\neq 0
.
Then (
1
) gives

x_{i}=\frac{m}{1-\ell_{i}\lambda}.

Substituting this into
\sum_{i}x_{i}=0
gives

\sum_{i=1}^{b}\frac{1}{1-\ell_{i}\lambda}=0,

namely

F_{\ell}(\lambda)=0.

Next,

F_{\ell}^{\prime}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\frac{\ell_{i}}{(1-\ell_{i}\lambda)^{2}}>0,

so
F_{\ell}
is strictly increasing on every interval avoiding its poles.
Because
\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}
, we have

\lim_{\lambda\to(1/\ell_{1})^{+}}F_{\ell}(\lambda)=-\infty,\qquad\lim_{\lambda\to(1/\ell_{2})^{-}}F_{\ell}(\lambda)=+\infty.

Hence
F_{\ell}
has a unique root in

\bigl(\frac{1}{\ell_{1}},\frac{1}{\ell_{2}}\bigr)
.

It remains to show that this root is the
first
positive Steklov eigenvalue.
If
m=0
, then (
1
) becomes

\frac{x_{i}}{\ell_{i}}=\lambda x_{i}.

Thus every nonzero component
x_{i}
forces
\lambda=1/\ell_{i}
.
If
\lambda=1/\ell_{1}
, then because
\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}
we must have
x_{i}=0
for every
i\geq 2
.
But then
\sum_{i}x_{i}=0
forces
x_{1}=0
, contradicting that the eigenvector is nonzero.
Therefore any eigenvalue arising from
m=0
is of the form
1/\ell_{i}
for some
i\geq 2
, and hence
is at least
1/\ell_{2}
.
So there is no positive Steklov eigenvalue below
1/\ell_{2}
except the unique root of

F_{\ell}
found above.
This proves that this root is exactly
\lambda_{2}(T)
.
∎

2.2
Exact optimization in the one-center class

We now show that, for fixed principal branches, the remaining branches must be as equal as possible.

Lemma 2.3
.

Fix
r\geq 1
and let

T_{0}=S(r+1,\,r,\,k,\,s,\,m_{5},\dots,m_{b})

be a spider such that

r\geq u\geq v+2\geq 3,\qquad r\geq m_{j}\geq 1\quad(5\leq j\leq b).

Let

T_{1}=S(r+1,\,r,\,k-1,\,s+1,\,m_{5},\dots,m_{b}).

Then
\lambda_{2}(T_{1})>\lambda_{2}(T_{0})
.

Proof.

For
t\in[0,1]
, define

F_{t}(\lambda):=\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-r\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-(k-t)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-(s+t)\lambda}+\sum_{j=5}^{b}\frac{1}{1-m_{j}\lambda}.

Since
k-t\leq r
and
s+t\leq k-t\leq r
, all terms other than the first two are regular on

\bigl(\frac{1}{r+1},\frac{1}{r}\bigr)
, and

\partial_{\lambda}F_{t}(\lambda)=\frac{r+1}{(1-(r+1)\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{r}{(1-r\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{k-t}{(1-(k-t)\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{s+t}{(1-(s+t)\lambda)^{2}}+\sum_{j=5}^{b}\frac{m_{j}}{(1-m_{j}\lambda)^{2}}>0.

Moreover,

\lim_{\lambda\to(1/(r+1))^{+}}F_{t}(\lambda)=-\infty,\qquad\lim_{\lambda\to(1/r)^{-}}F_{t}(\lambda)=+\infty.

Hence
F_{t}
has a unique root

\lambda(t)\in\left(\frac{1}{r+1},\frac{1}{r}\right).

For
t=0
and
t=1
, Lemma
2.2
gives

\lambda(0)=\lambda_{2}(T_{0}),\qquad\lambda(1)=\lambda_{2}(T_{1}).

By the implicit function theorem,

\lambda^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{\partial_{t}F_{t}(\lambda(t))}{\partial_{\lambda}F_{t}(\lambda(t))}.

Now

\partial_{t}F_{t}(\lambda)=-\frac{\lambda}{\bigl(1-(k-t)\lambda\bigr)^{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{\bigl(1-(s+t)\lambda\bigr)^{2}},

so

\lambda^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\lambda(t)}{\partial_{\lambda}F_{t}(\lambda(t))}\left(\frac{1}{\bigl(1-(k-t)\lambda(t)\bigr)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\bigl(1-(s+t)\lambda(t)\bigr)^{2}}\right).

Because
k-t\geq s+t
for
t\in[0,1]
, with equality only possibly at
t=1
when
k=s+2
, and because

\lambda(t)<1/r\leq 1/(k-t)
, we have

0<1-(k-t)\lambda(t)\leq 1-(s+t)\lambda(t).

For
t\in[0,1)
the inequality is strict, hence

\frac{1}{\bigl(1-(k-t)\lambda(t)\bigr)^{2}}>\frac{1}{\bigl(1-(s+t)\lambda(t)\bigr)^{2}}.

Therefore
\lambda^{\prime}(t)>0
on
[0,1)
, and consequently

\lambda_{2}(T_{1})=\lambda(1)>\lambda(0)=\lambda_{2}(T_{0}).

∎

We next show that, once at least one additional branch is present, the two longest branches must be as
balanced as the odd diameter permits.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

T_{0}=S(a,\,b,\,m_{3},\dots,m_{s})

be a spider such that

a+b=2r+1,a\geq b+2,1\leq m_{j}\leq b
where
3\leq j\leq s
,
and assume
s\geq 3
.
Let

T_{1}=S(a-1,\,b+1,\,m_{3},\dots,m_{s}).

Then

\lambda_{2}(T_{1})>\lambda_{2}(T_{0}).

Proof.

For
t\in[0,1]
, define

F_{t}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{1-(a-t)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-(b+t)\lambda}+\sum_{j=3}^{s}\frac{1}{1-m_{j}\lambda}.

Since
a+b
is odd and
a\geq b+2
, we in fact have
a\geq b+3
.
Hence
a-t>b+t\geq m_{j}
for every
j
and every
t\in[0,1]
, so the function
F_{t}
is strictly increasing on

\left(\frac{1}{a-t},\frac{1}{b+t}\right),

with

\lim_{\lambda\to(1/(a-t))^{+}}F_{t}(\lambda)=-\infty,\qquad\lim_{\lambda\to(1/(b+t))^{-}}F_{t}(\lambda)=+\infty.

Hence
F_{t}
has a unique root

\lambda(t)\in\left(\frac{1}{a-t},\frac{1}{b+t}\right).

For
t=0
and
t=1
, Lemma
2.2
yields

\lambda(0)=\lambda_{2}(T_{0})
and
\lambda(1)=\lambda_{2}(T_{1}).

We first prove that

\lambda(t)<\frac{2}{2r+1}\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,1].

(2)

Indeed, since
(a-t)+(b+t)=2r+1
, the first two terms cancel at
\lambda=\frac{2}{2r+1}
:

\frac{1}{1-\frac{2(a-t)}{2r+1}}+\frac{1}{1-\frac{2(b+t)}{2r+1}}=0.

Moreover, each additional branch satisfies
m_{j}\leq b<\frac{2r+1}{2}
, so

1-\frac{2m_{j}}{2r+1}>0.

Therefore

F_{t}\!\left(\frac{2}{2r+1}\right)>0.

Since
F_{t}
is strictly increasing and
F_{t}(\lambda(t))=0
, this proves (
2
).

By the implicit function theorem,

\lambda^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{\partial_{t}F_{t}(\lambda(t))}{\partial_{\lambda}F_{t}(\lambda(t))}.

A direct differentiation gives

\lambda^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\lambda(t)}{\partial_{\lambda}F_{t}(\lambda(t))}\left(\frac{1}{\bigl(1-(a-t)\lambda(t)\bigr)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\bigl(1-(b+t)\lambda(t)\bigr)^{2}}\right).

Because

\frac{1}{a-t}<\lambda(t)<\frac{1}{b+t},

we have

1-(a-t)\lambda(t)<0<1-(b+t)\lambda(t).

Thus it suffices to compare absolute values. Using (
2
),

(a-t)\lambda(t)-1<1-(b+t)\lambda(t)\iff\bigl((a-t)+(b+t)\bigr)\lambda(t)<2\iff(2r+1)\lambda(t)<2,

which is true. Therefore

\bigl|1-(a-t)\lambda(t)\bigr|<1-(b+t)\lambda(t),

and hence

\frac{1}{\bigl(1-(a-t)\lambda(t)\bigr)^{2}}>\frac{1}{\bigl(1-(b+t)\lambda(t)\bigr)^{2}}.

Consequently
\lambda^{\prime}(t)>0
on
[0,1]
, so

\lambda_{2}(T_{1})=\lambda(1)>\lambda(0)=\lambda_{2}(T_{0}).

∎

We now combine the previous ingredients to get the following result.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
D=2r+1\geq 3
be odd and let
n\geq D+1
.
Let
M=n-2r-2.

Among all spiders with diameter
D
and order
n
, the following hold.

(i)

If
M=0
, then the unique spider is the path
P_{D+1}
.

(ii)

Assume
M\geq 1
. For each admissible integer
q
with
\lceil M/r\rceil\leq q\leq M
, write

M=qc+t,\qquad 0\leq t<q.

Then

\max\{\lambda_{2}(T):T\text{ is a spider},\ \operatorname{diam}(T)=D,\ |V(T)|=n\}=\max_{\lceil M/r\rceil\leq q\leq M}\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathrm{AS}(r,q+2,c,t)\bigr).

For fixed
q
, the unique maximizing spider is
\mathrm{AS}(r,q+2,c,t)
, where
M=qc+t
and
0\leq t<q
.

Its first nonzero Steklov eigenvalue is the unique root

\lambda\in\bigl(\frac{1}{r+1},\frac{1}{r}\bigr)
of

\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-r\lambda}+\frac{t}{1-(c+1)\lambda}+\frac{q-t}{1-c\lambda}=0.

(3)

Proof.

If
M=0
, then
n=D+1
, and the only spider of diameter
D
is the path
P_{D+1}
.
This proves part (i).

Assume henceforth that
M\geq 1
and let

T=S(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{b})

be a spider with diameter
D=2r+1
and order
n
. Reorder the branch lengths so that

\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}\geq\ell_{3}\geq\cdots\geq\ell_{b}\geq 1.

Since the diameter is
2r+1
, the two longest branches satisfy

\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=2r+1.

Write

m_{1}:=\ell_{3},\dots,m_{q}:=\ell_{b},\qquad q:=b-2.

Then

q\geq 1,\qquad 1\leq m_{j}\leq\ell_{2}\leq r,\qquad m_{1}+\cdots+m_{q}=n-2r-2=M.

Thus the diameter constraint fixes the sum of the two principal branches, while the order
constraint fixes the total lateral length.

Step 1: balancing the principal profile.

If
\ell_{1}\neq r+1
, then
\ell_{1}\geq\ell_{2}+2
, and Lemma
2.4
shows that
replacing
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
by
(\ell_{1}-1,\ell_{2}+1)
strictly increases
\lambda_{2}
. Repeating this
operation finitely many times, we arrive at a spider of the form

S(r+1,r,m_{1},\dots,m_{q})

with strictly larger first nonzero Steklov eigenvalue. Hence every maximizing spider must have
principal branch lengths
r+1
and
r
.

Step 2: balancing the lateral profile.

Fix
q
. Among all spiders of the form

S(r+1,r,m_{1},\dots,m_{q})

with
1\leq m_{j}\leq r
and
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{q}=M
, suppose two lateral branches differ by at least

2
, say
u\geq v+2
. Lemma
2.3
then replaces
(u,v)
by
(k-1,v+1)
and
strictly increases
\lambda_{2}
. Iterating this elementary redistribution, we reach a unique profile in
which every two lateral branches differ by at most
1
. Writing

M=qc+t,\qquad 0\leq t<q,

this balanced lateral profile is precisely

(\underbrace{c+1,\dots,c+1}_{t},\underbrace{c,\dots,c}_{q-t}).

Therefore the unique maximizer for fixed
q
is

\mathrm{AS}(r,q+2,c,t)=S\bigl(r+1,r,\underbrace{c+1,\dots,c+1}_{t},\underbrace{c,\dots,c}_{q-t}\bigr).

Step 3: admissible range of the profile parameter.

The integer
q
is feasible exactly when each lateral branch has length between
1
and
r
, namely

\left\lceil\frac{M}{r}\right\rceil\leq q\leq M.

Taking the maximum over all feasible
q
proves part (ii). The root equation
(
3
) follows directly from Lemma
2.2
.
∎

2.3
Unimodality of the profile parameter

Theorem
2.5
reduces the one-center problem to a finite optimization over the number
q
of lateral branches. The next result shows that the resulting scalar parameter enters through a genuinely unimodal profile.

Fix
r\geq 1
and
M\geq 1
.
For every real number
q\in\left[\frac{M}{r},\,M\right]
,
let

c(q):=\left\lfloor\frac{M}{q}\right\rfloor.

Define

\Phi_{r,M}(\lambda,q)=\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-r\lambda}+\frac{M-c(q)q}{1-(c(q)+1)\lambda}+\frac{q(c(q)+1)-M}{1-c(q)\lambda}.

(4)

Whenever
q
is a feasible integer, this is exactly the balanced-spider equation
(
3
) for
\mathrm{AS}(r,q+2,c,t)
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
r\geq 1
,
M\geq 1
, and let

s=\left\lceil\frac{r}{2}\right\rceil.

For every
q\in\left[\frac{M}{r},\,M\right]
, the equation

\Phi_{r,M}(\lambda,q)=0

has a unique solution
\Sigma_{r,M}(q)\in\left(\frac{1}{r+1},\,\frac{1}{r}\right).

The function

q\longmapsto\Sigma_{r,M}(q)

is continuous on
\bigl[\frac{M}{r},M\bigr]
, piecewise
C^{1}
, strictly increasing on

\left[\frac{M}{r},\,\frac{M}{s}\right]
,
and strictly decreasing on

\left[\frac{M}{s},\,M\right]
.
Consequently, if

q_{-}:=\max\!\left\{1,\left\lfloor\frac{M}{s}\right\rfloor\right\}
and

q_{+}:=\left\lceil\frac{M}{s}\right\rceil
,
and if

M=q_{-}c_{-}+t_{-},\qquad 0\leq t_{-}<q_{-},

M=q_{+}c_{+}+t_{+},\qquad 0\leq t_{+}<q_{+},

then

\max_{\lceil M/r\rceil\leq q\leq M}\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathrm{AS}(r,q+2,c,t)\bigr)=\max\Bigl\{\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathrm{AS}(r,q_{-}+2,c_{-},t_{-})\bigr),\,\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathrm{AS}(r,q_{+}+2,c_{+},t_{+})\bigr)\Bigr\},

(5)

where on the left-hand side, for each admissible integer
q
, we write
M=qc+t
with
0\leq t<q
.

Proof.

Fix
q\in[M/r,M]
and write
c:=c(q)=\lfloor M/q\rfloor
.
If
q=M/r
, then necessarily
c=r
, and (
4
) becomes

\Phi_{r,M}(\lambda,M/r)=\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1+M/r}{1-r\lambda}.

This function is strictly increasing on
(1/(r+1),1/r)
and has limits
-\infty
and
+\infty

at the two endpoints, hence it has a unique root in that interval.

Assume now that
q>M/r
.
Then
c\leq r-1
. The endpoint
q=M
is covered by the same formula with
c(q)=1
.
On the open interval

I_{c}:=\left(\frac{M}{c+1},\,\frac{M}{c}\right)

the integer
c(q)
is constant and equal to
c
, so (
4
) becomes

\Phi_{r,M}(\lambda,q)=\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-r\lambda}+\frac{M-cq}{1-(c+1)\lambda}+\frac{q(c+1)-M}{1-c\lambda}.

Since
c\leq r-1
, all side denominators are positive on
(1/(r+1),1/r)
.
Moreover,

\partial_{\lambda}\Phi_{r,M}(\lambda,q)=\frac{r+1}{(1-(r+1)\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{r}{(1-r\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{(M-cq)(c+1)}{(1-(c+1)\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{(q(c+1)-M)c}{(1-c\lambda)^{2}}>0.

Thus
\Phi_{r,M}(\cdot,q)
is strictly increasing on
(1/(r+1),1/r)
.
As
\lambda\downarrow 1/(r+1)
, the first term tends to
-\infty
; as

\lambda\uparrow 1/r
, the second term tends to
+\infty
.
Therefore there is a unique root

\Sigma_{r,M}(q)\in\left(\frac{1}{r+1},\,\frac{1}{r}\right)

for every
q\in[M/r,M]
.

On each open interval
I_{c}
, the implicit function theorem gives a
C^{1}
branch

q\mapsto\Sigma_{r,M}(q)
.
Differentiating
\Phi_{r,M}(\Sigma_{r,M}(q),q)=0
with respect to
q
yields

\Sigma_{r,M}^{\prime}(q)=-\frac{\partial_{q}\Phi_{r,M}(\Sigma_{r,M}(q),q)}{\partial_{\lambda}\Phi_{r,M}(\Sigma_{r,M}(q),q)}.

Now

\partial_{q}\Phi_{r,M}(\lambda,q)=-\frac{c}{1-(c+1)\lambda}+\frac{c+1}{1-c\lambda}=\frac{1-(2c+1)\lambda}{(1-(c+1)\lambda)(1-c\lambda)}.

Since
c\leq r-1
, the denominator is positive on
(1/(r+1),1/r)
.
Hence the sign of
\partial_{q}\Phi_{r,M}
is exactly the sign of
1-(2c+1)\lambda
.

Suppose first that
c\geq s=\lceil r/2\rceil
.
Then
2c+1\geq 2\left\lceil\frac{r}{2}\right\rceil+1\geq r+1
,
so

\frac{1}{2c+1}\leq\frac{1}{r+1}<\Sigma_{r,M}(q).

Therefore

1-(2c+1)\Sigma_{r,M}(q)<0,

hence
\partial_{q}\Phi_{r,M}<0
and

\Sigma_{r,M}^{\prime}(q)>0.

So
\Sigma_{r,M}
is strictly increasing on every interval
I_{c}
with
c\geq s
.

Suppose next that
c\leq s-1
.
Then

2c+1\leq 2s-1\leq r,

and thus

\Sigma_{r,M}(q)<\frac{1}{r}\leq\frac{1}{2c+1}.

Therefore

1-(2c+1)\Sigma_{r,M}(q)>0,

hence
\partial_{q}\Phi_{r,M}>0
and

\Sigma_{r,M}^{\prime}(q)<0.

So
\Sigma_{r,M}
is strictly decreasing on every interval
I_{c}
with
c\leq s-1
.

It remains to prove continuity across the breakpoints

q=\frac{M}{c+1},(1\leq c\leq r-1).

At such a point, the formulas from the two neighboring blocks coincide:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-r\lambda}+\frac{M-c\frac{M}{c+1}}{1-(c+1)\lambda}+\frac{\frac{M}{c+1}(c+1)-M}{1-c\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-r\lambda}+\frac{\frac{M}{c+1}}{1-(c+1)\lambda},

while

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-r\lambda}+\frac{M-(c+1)\frac{M}{c+1}}{1-(c+2)\lambda}+\frac{\frac{M}{c+1}(c+2)-M}{1-(c+1)\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-r\lambda}+\frac{\frac{M}{c+1}}{1-(c+1)\lambda}.

Hence the two neighboring branches meet. Together with the endpoint case
q=M/r
treated above, this shows that
\Sigma_{r,M}
is continuous on

[M/r,M]
.
Combining continuity with the monotonicity on each block shows that
\Sigma_{r,M}
is
strictly increasing on
[M/r,M/s]
and strictly decreasing on
[M/s,M]
.

Finally, for every feasible integer
q
, writing
M=qc+t
with
0\leq t<q
, we have

\Sigma_{r,M}(q)=\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathrm{AS}(r,q+2,c,t)\bigr),

because (
4
) is exactly the root equation (
3
) for the generalized almost seesaw tree
\mathrm{AS}(r,q+2,c,t)
.
Therefore the strict unimodality of
\Sigma_{r,M}
implies that the maximum over all feasible
integers is attained at one of the two integers nearest to
M/s
, namely
q_{-}
or
q_{+}
.
This proves (
5
).
∎

3
Boundary fluxes and two-center reduction

3.1
The inverse boundary quadratic form

In this section we recast the problem in terms of boundary fluxes and the inverse Dirichlet-to-Neumann form. This is the discrete analogue of working with the inverse Steklov operator on the boundary, and it is particularly well adapted to diameter-constrained comparisons. The cut decomposition below may also be viewed as a boundary counterpart of the classical distance-matrix identities for trees; compare
[
6
,
5
,
14
]
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a tree and let
\delta\Omega\subset V
be its leaf set.
Given a boundary flux vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{\delta\Omega}
with
\sum_{\omega\in\delta\Omega}z_{\omega}=0
, there exists a unique function
u_{z}\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
, unique up to an additive constant,
such that
u_{z}
is harmonic on the interior vertices and its outward normal derivative on

\delta\Omega
is exactly
z
.
Define

Q_{T}(z):=\sum_{xy\in E}\bigl(u_{z}(x)-u_{z}(y)\bigr)^{2}.

Proposition 3.1
.

For every tree
T
with leaf boundary
\delta\Omega
,

\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}(T,\delta\Omega)}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{R}^{\delta\Omega}\setminus\{0\}\\
\sum_{\omega\in\delta\Omega}z_{\omega}=0\end{subarray}}\frac{Q_{T}(z)}{\sum_{\omega\in\delta\Omega}z_{\omega}^{2}}.

In other words, the nonzero Steklov eigenvalues are the reciprocals of the eigenvalues of the
quadratic form
Q_{T}
on the codimension-one space

\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{\delta\Omega}:\sum_{\omega\in\delta\Omega}z_{\omega}=0\}
.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda_{T}
be the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator.
Restricted to the subspace of mean-zero boundary data,
\Lambda_{T}
is positive definite and
invertible.
Its inverse sends a boundary flux vector
z
to the corresponding boundary potential

u_{z}|_{\delta\Omega}
, modulo constants.
By discrete Green’s identity,

Q_{T}(z)=\sum_{xy\in E}\bigl(u_{z}(x)-u_{z}(y)\bigr)^{2}=\sum_{\omega\in\delta\Omega}u_{z}(\omega)z_{\omega}.

Hence
Q_{T}
is exactly the quadratic form of
\Lambda_{T}^{-1}
on the mean-zero subspace.
Therefore the eigenvalues of
Q_{T}
are
1/\lambda_{k}(T,\delta\Omega)
for
k\geq 2
, and the largest one is

1/\lambda_{2}(T,\delta\Omega)
.
This is precisely the stated variational characterization.
∎

We next show the cut-sum description of
Q_{T}
.
Fix a root
o
of
T
and orient every edge away from
o
.
For an oriented edge
e
, let
U_{e}
denote the set of boundary leaves in the descendant
component of
e
, and let
s_{e}(z)=\sum_{\omega\in U_{e}}z_{\omega}.

Proposition 3.2
.

For every mean-zero boundary flux vector
z
,
Q_{T}(z)=\sum_{e\in E}s_{e}(z)^{2}.

In particular, if
D_{\delta\Omega}
denotes the distance matrix of the leaves, then

Q_{T}(z)=-\frac{1}{2}z^{T}D_{\delta\Omega}z.

Proof.

Let
e=xy
be an oriented edge, with
x
the parent and
y
the child, and let
W_{e}
be the
descendant component of
T-e
containing
y
.
Summing
\Delta u_{z}(v)
over all vertices
v\in W_{e}
, every edge internal to
W_{e}
cancels, and the
only surviving contribution comes from the cut edge
e
:

\sum_{v\in W_{e}}\Delta u_{z}(v)=u_{z}(y)-u_{z}(x).

Now every non-leaf vertex of
W_{e}
is interior to
T
and therefore harmonic, while every leaf

\omega\in U_{e}
satisfies

\Delta u_{z}(\omega)=\partial_{n}u_{z}(\omega)=z_{\omega}

because the boundary is the leaf set.
Hence

u_{z}(y)-u_{z}(x)=\sum_{\omega\in U_{e}}z_{\omega}=s_{e}(z).

Therefore

Q_{T}(z)=\sum_{e=xy\in E}\bigl(u_{z}(x)-u_{z}(y)\bigr)^{2}=\sum_{e\in E}s_{e}(z)^{2},

which proves the first identity.

For the distance-matrix formula, write

d(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{e\in E}\mathbf{1}_{\{e\text{ separates }\alpha\text{ and }\beta\}}.

Then

z^{T}D_{\delta\Omega}z=\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in U_{e}\\
\beta\notin U_{e}\end{subarray}}z_{\alpha}z_{\beta}+\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\notin U_{e}\\
\beta\in U_{e}\end{subarray}}z_{\alpha}z_{\beta}.

For a fixed edge
e
, the two inner sums are equal, so its contribution is

2\Bigl(\sum_{\alpha\in U_{e}}z_{\alpha}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{\beta\notin U_{e}}z_{\beta}\Bigr).

Because
\sum_{\omega\in\delta\Omega}z_{\omega}=0
, this equals

-2\left(\sum_{\alpha\in U_{e}}z_{\alpha}\right)^{2}=-2\,s_{e}(z)^{2}.

Summing over all edges and using the first identity gives

z^{T}D_{\delta\Omega}z=-2\sum_{e\in E}s_{e}(z)^{2}=-2Q_{T}(z).

∎

3.2
Two-center profiles

Definition 3.3
.

A
double spider

\operatorname{DS}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\,;\,b_{1},\dots,b_{q})
is a tree obtained by joining two distinguished vertices
u
and
v
by one edge
uv
, attaching
p

internally disjoint paths of lengths
a_{1},\dots,a_{p}
to
u
, and attaching
q
internally disjoint
paths of lengths
b_{1},\dots,b_{q}
to
v
. We also refer to this configuration as a

two-center profile
. We assume throughout that

a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{p}\geq 1,\qquad b_{1}\geq b_{2}\geq\cdots\geq b_{q}\geq 1.

If
a_{1}=b_{1}=r
, then
\operatorname{DS}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\,;\,b_{1},\dots,b_{q})
has odd diameter
2r+1
.
Geometrically, it is a two-center configuration whose boundary leaves split naturally into a
u
-side and a
v
-side.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let

D=\operatorname{DS}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\,;\,b_{1},\dots,b_{q})

and let

z=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p},y_{1},\dots,y_{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{p+q}

be a boundary vector with

\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}y_{j}=0.

Let
s=\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_{i}=-\sum_{j=1}^{q}y_{j}.

Then

Q_{D}(z)=s^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}y_{j}^{2}.

(6)

Hence

\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}(D)}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\neq 0\\
\sum x_{i}+\sum y_{j}=0\end{subarray}}\frac{s^{2}+\sum_{i}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j}b_{j}y_{j}^{2}}{\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j}y_{j}^{2}}.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
, each edge on the
i
-th branch emanating from
u
has cut sum
x_{i}
,
so the whole branch contributes
a_{i}x_{i}^{2}
.
Similarly, each edge on the
j
-th branch emanating from
v
has cut sum
y_{j}
, so that branch contributes

b_{j}y_{j}^{2}
.
The central edge
uv
has cut sum

\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_{i}=s
,
hence contributes
s^{2}
.
Summing all edge contributions gives (
6
).
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

D=\operatorname{DS}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\,;\,b_{1},\dots,b_{q})

with

a_{1}=b_{1}=r\geq a_{i},b_{j}.

Let

A_{\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{\rho-a_{i}},\qquad B_{\rho}=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{1}{\rho-b_{j}},\qquad\rho>r.

Then
\rho_{D}=1/\lambda_{2}(D)
is the unique solution of

\frac{1}{A_{\rho}}+\frac{1}{B_{\rho}}=1,\qquad\rho>r.

(7)

Moreover, every maximizing vector in Lemma
3.4
can be chosen in the form

x_{i}>0\ \ (1\leq i\leq p)
,
y_{j}<0\ \ (1\leq j\leq q),

or with all signs reversed.

Proof.

Let

\rho=\rho_{D}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}(D)}

and let
z^{\ast}=(x,y)
be a maximizer of the inverse Rayleigh quotient from
Lemma
3.4
.

We first show that
\rho>r
.
Indeed, because
a_{i},b_{j}\leq r
, any admissible vector with
s=0
in
(
6
) satisfies

Q_{D}(z)\leq r\|z\|_{2}^{2}.

On the other hand, the admissible vector defined by

x_{1}=1,y_{1}=-1,x_{i}=0\ (i\geq 2),y_{j}=0\ (j\geq 2)

has
s=1
, and Lemma
3.4
gives

\frac{Q_{D}(z)}{\|z\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{1+a_{1}+b_{1}}{2}=r+\frac{1}{2}.

Hence

\rho\geq r+\frac{1}{2}>r.

In particular, the quantities

A_{\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{\rho-a_{i}},\qquad B_{\rho}=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{1}{\rho-b_{j}}

are well defined and strictly positive.

Since every admissible vector with
s=0
gives Rayleigh quotient at most
r<\rho
, a maximizing
vector must satisfy
s\neq 0
.
After changing the overall sign if necessary, we may assume
s>0
.

Consider the smooth extension

\widetilde{Q}_{D}(x,y):=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_{i}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}y_{j}^{2}

on
\mathbb{R}^{p+q}
.
On the constraint hyperplane

H=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+q}:\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}y_{j}=0\right\},

we have
\widetilde{Q}_{D}=Q_{D}
by Lemma
3.4
.
After normalizing
z^{\ast}
so that
\|z^{\ast}\|_{2}=1
, the vector
z^{\ast}
maximizes

\widetilde{Q}_{D}
on the smooth compact manifold
H\cap S^{p+q-1}
.
Hence there exist Lagrange multipliers
\alpha
and
\beta
such that differentiating

\widetilde{Q}_{D}(z)-\alpha\|z\|_{2}^{2}-\beta\Bigl(\sum_{i}x_{i}+\sum_{j}y_{j}\Bigr)

at
z^{\ast}
gives

2s+2a_{i}x_{i}-2\alpha x_{i}-\beta=0,2b_{j}y_{j}-2\alpha y_{j}-\beta=0.

Taking the inner product with
z^{\ast}
and using
\|z^{\ast}\|_{2}=1
,

\sum_{i}x_{i}+\sum_{j}y_{j}=0
, and
\widetilde{Q}_{D}(z^{\ast})=Q_{D}(z^{\ast})=\rho
, we obtain

2\rho=2\alpha.

Thus
\alpha=\rho
, and hence

(\rho-a_{i})x_{i}=s-\frac{\beta}{2},\qquad(\rho-b_{j})y_{j}=-\frac{\beta}{2}.

(8)

Summing over
i
and
j
yields

s=\Bigl(s-\frac{\beta}{2}\Bigr)A_{\rho},-s=-\frac{\beta}{2}B_{\rho}.

From the second identity,

-\frac{\beta}{2}=-\frac{s}{B_{\rho}}<0.

Substituting into the first gives

s=\Bigl(s-\frac{s}{B_{\rho}}\Bigr)A_{\rho}=sA_{\rho}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{B_{\rho}}\Bigr).

Since
s\neq 0
, this is equivalent to

\frac{1}{A_{\rho}}+\frac{1}{B_{\rho}}=1.

Thus any maximizer gives a solution of (
7
).

Now consider the function

\Phi(\rho):=\frac{1}{A_{\rho}}+\frac{1}{B_{\rho}},\rho>r.

It is strictly increasing, because
A_{\rho}
and
B_{\rho}
are strictly decreasing.
Moreover,

\lim_{\rho\to r^{+}}\Phi(\rho)=0,\qquad\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\Phi(\rho)=+\infty.

Hence (
7
) has exactly one solution in
(r,\infty)
.
Let
\rho_{0}
denote this unique solution.
Since
\rho_{D}
yields a solution, we have
\rho_{D}=\rho_{0}
.

Conversely, define

x_{i}=\frac{1/A_{\rho_{0}}}{\rho_{0}-a_{i}},y_{j}=-\frac{1/B_{\rho_{0}}}{\rho_{0}-b_{j}}.

Then
\sum_{i}x_{i}=1=-\sum_{j}y_{j}
, so
z=(x,y)
is admissible.
Moreover, (
8
) holds with
s=1
and
-\beta/2=-1/B_{\rho_{0}}
.
Using (
7
), we compute

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\|z\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\rho_{0}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\rho_{0}y_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}y_{j}^{2}+\frac{1}{A_{\rho_{0}}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_{i}-\frac{1}{B_{\rho_{0}}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}y_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}y_{j}^{2}+\frac{1}{A_{\rho_{0}}}+\frac{1}{B_{\rho_{0}}}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}y_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=Q_{D}(z).

Thus the Rayleigh quotient of
z
equals
\rho_{0}=\rho_{D}
, so
z
is a maximizing vector.

Finally, return to the maximizing vector
z^{\ast}
.
Since
\rho=\rho_{D}>r\geq a_{i},b_{j}
, all denominators in (
8
) are positive.
Moreover,

-\frac{\beta}{2}=-\frac{s}{B_{\rho}}<0,s-\frac{\beta}{2}=\frac{s}{A_{\rho}}>0.

Hence every
x_{i}
is positive and every
y_{j}
is negative.
Reversing the overall sign yields the alternative sign pattern.
This proves the sign claim.
∎

We now compare an arbitrary odd-diameter tree with a suitable two-center profile.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
T
be a tree with

\operatorname{diam}(T)=2r+1,|V(T)|=n.

Then there exists a double spider

D=\operatorname{DS}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\,;\,b_{1},\dots,b_{q})

of the same order and diameter such that

(i)

a_{1}=b_{1}=r
;

(ii)

every
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
is at most
r
;

(iii)

\lambda_{2}(T)\leq\lambda_{2}(D)
.

Moreover, if equality holds, then
T
itself is a double spider.

Proof.

Let
uv
be the central edge of
T
. Removing
uv
splits
T
into two rooted components

R_{u}
and
R_{v}
, rooted at
u
and
v
respectively. Because
\operatorname{diam}(T)=2r+1
, each of these
components has depth exactly
r
.

Step 1: Construction of a two-center comparison profile.

Let

L_{u}=\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{p}\},\qquad L_{v}=\{\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{q}\}

be the leaf sets of
R_{u}
and
R_{v}
. Choose deepest leaves
\omega_{1}\in L_{u}
and

\eta_{1}\in L_{v}
such that
d(u,\omega_{1})=r
and
d(v,\eta_{1})=r
.

For every edge
e
of
R_{u}
, choose a terminal leaf
\pi_{u}(e)\in L_{u}
lying below
e
, with the
additional requirement that every edge on the geodesic from
u
to
\omega_{1}
is assigned to

\omega_{1}
. Define

a_{i}:=|\pi_{u}^{-1}(\omega_{i})|,\qquad i=1,\dots,p.

Then
a_{1}=r
and
1\leq a_{i}\leq r
for every
i
. In the same way, assigning every edge of
R_{v}

to a terminal leaf, with the full geodesic from
v
to
\eta_{1}
assigned to
\eta_{1}
, we obtain
integers

b_{j}:=|\pi_{v}^{-1}(\eta_{j})|,\qquad j=1,\dots,q,

with
b_{1}=r
and
1\leq b_{j}\leq r
. Since
R_{u}
and
R_{v}
contain all edges of
T
except the
central edge
uv
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}=|E(T)|-1=n-2.

Therefore the double spider

D:=\operatorname{DS}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\,;\,b_{1},\dots,b_{q})

has the same order as
T
, and its diameter is

a_{1}+1+b_{1}=r+1+r=2r+1.

Thus
D
is a two-center profile with the same geometric constraints as
T
.

Step 2: Comparison of the inverse energies.

Let
z=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p},y_{1},\dots,y_{q})
be a maximizing boundary flux for
D
given by
Lemma
3.5
, chosen so that
x_{i}>0
and
y_{j}<0
. Write

s=\sum_{i=1}^{p}x_{i}=-\sum_{j=1}^{q}y_{j}>0.

Transfer this flux to the leaves of
T
by assigning the value
x_{i}
to
\omega_{i}
and the value

y_{j}
to
\eta_{j}
. Denote the resulting boundary flux by
\widetilde{z}
. Then
\widetilde{z}
is admissible
for
T
and
\|\widetilde{z}\|_{2}^{2}=\|z\|_{2}^{2}
.

For an edge
e
in
R_{u}
, all leaves below
e
belong to
L_{u}
and carry positive flux. Hence

s_{e}(\widetilde{z})=\sum_{\omega_{i}\in U_{e}}x_{i}\geq\widetilde{z}_{\pi_{u}(e)}>0,\qquad s_{e}(\widetilde{z})^{2}\geq\widetilde{z}_{\pi_{u}(e)}^{2}.

Summing over all edges of
R_{u}
gives

\sum_{e\in E(R_{u})}s_{e}(\widetilde{z})^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}.

Likewise, for an edge
e
in
R_{v}
, all leaves below
e
belong to
L_{v}
and carry negative flux, so

|s_{e}(\widetilde{z})|=\sum_{\eta_{j}\in U_{e}}|y_{j}|\geq|\widetilde{z}_{\pi_{v}(e)}|,\qquad s_{e}(\widetilde{z})^{2}\geq\widetilde{z}_{\pi_{v}(e)}^{2},

and therefore

\sum_{e\in E(R_{v})}s_{e}(\widetilde{z})^{2}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}y_{j}^{2}.

Finally, the central edge
uv
separates the two sides of the tree and contributes exactly
s^{2}
.
Using Proposition
3.2
, we obtain

Q_{T}(\widetilde{z})\geq s^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}y_{j}^{2}=Q_{D}(z).

Hence

\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}(T)}\geq\frac{Q_{T}(\widetilde{z})}{\|\widetilde{z}\|_{2}^{2}}\geq\frac{Q_{D}(z)}{\|z\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}(D)}.

Equivalently,
\lambda_{2}(T)\leq\lambda_{2}(D)
.

Step 3: Rigidity in the equality case.

Assume now that equality holds. Then every inequality above is an equality. Since each
x_{i}>0

and each
y_{j}<0
, equality for a fixed edge
e\in E(R_{u})
forces the descendant set below
e
to
contain only one leaf of
L_{u}
; otherwise the strict positivity of the
x_{i}
would give

s_{e}(\widetilde{z})^{2}=\left(\sum_{\omega_{i}\in U_{e}}x_{i}\right)^{2}>\widetilde{z}_{\pi_{u}(e)}^{2}.

Thus every edge of
R_{u}
lies below exactly one leaf, which means that all branching in
R_{u}

occurs at the root
u
. The same argument applies to
R_{v}
. Hence
T
itself is a double spider.
∎

The next lemma is the key monotonicity statement.
It says that if both sides of an odd-diameter double spider carry more than one branch, then one can move any non-principal branch from the smaller side to the larger side and strictly increase
\lambda_{2}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let

D=\operatorname{DS}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\,;\,b_{1},\dots,b_{q})

with

a_{1}=b_{1}=r,p\geq 1,q\geq 2.

Let

\rho_{D}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}(D)}

and write

A=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{\rho_{D}-a_{i}},\qquad B=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{1}{\rho_{D}-b_{j}}.

Assume that

A\geq B.

Fix an index
k\in\{2,\dots,q\}
and move the branch
b_{k}
from the
v
-side to the
u
-side:

D^{\prime}=\operatorname{DS}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p},b_{k}\,;\,b_{1},\dots,\widehat{b_{k}},\dots,b_{q}).

Then
D^{\prime}
has the same order and diameter as
D
, and

\lambda_{2}(D^{\prime})>\lambda_{2}(D).

Proof.

Because
b_{k}\leq b_{1}=r
, moving this branch across the central edge does not change the
diameter: the two principal branches of lengths
a_{1}=r
and
b_{1}=r
remain in place, hence

\operatorname{diam}(D^{\prime})=r+1+r=2r+1=\operatorname{diam}(D).

The order is unchanged because no vertex is created or deleted.

Let
u=\frac{1}{\rho_{D}-b_{k}},Q=\sum_{j\neq k}\frac{1}{\rho_{D}-b_{j}}.

Then

B=Q+u.

By Lemma
3.5
, the inverse eigenvalue of
D
is characterized by

\frac{1}{A}+\frac{1}{Q+u}=1.

Now consider the root function for
D^{\prime}
:

\Psi_{D^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{t-a_{i}}+\frac{1}{t-b_{k}}}+\frac{1}{\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq k}\frac{1}{t-b_{j}}}-1.

By Lemma
3.5
,
\Psi_{D^{\prime}}
is strictly increasing on
(r,\infty)
, and its unique root is

\rho_{D^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}(D^{\prime})}.

Evaluating at
t=\rho_{D}
gives

\Psi_{D^{\prime}}(\rho_{D})=\frac{1}{A+u}+\frac{1}{Q}-1.

Subtracting the old equation yields

\Psi_{D^{\prime}}(\rho_{D})-\left(\frac{1}{A}+\frac{1}{Q+u}-1\right)=\left(\frac{1}{A+u}-\frac{1}{A}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{Q}-\frac{1}{Q+u}\right).

Thus

\Psi_{D^{\prime}}(\rho_{D})=u\left(\frac{1}{Q(Q+u)}-\frac{1}{A(A+u)}\right).

Since
A\geq B=Q+u>Q
, we have

\frac{1}{Q(Q+u)}>\frac{1}{A(A+u)},

so
\Psi_{D^{\prime}}(\rho_{D})>0
.
Because
\Psi_{D^{\prime}}
is strictly increasing and vanishes at
\rho_{D^{\prime}}
, it follows that

\rho_{D^{\prime}}<\rho_{D}.

Equivalently,

\lambda_{2}(D^{\prime})>\lambda_{2}(D).

∎

3.3
Rigidity of extremizers

We can now prove the desired structural theorem.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
D=2r+1\geq 3
and let
n\geq D+1
.
Suppose that
T
maximizes
\lambda_{2}
among all trees with

|V(T)|=n,\operatorname{diam}(T)=D.

Then
T
is a spider.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.6
, there exists a double spider

D_{0}=\operatorname{DS}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\,;\,b_{1},\dots,b_{q})

of the same order and diameter as
T
such that
\lambda_{2}(T)\leq\lambda_{2}(D_{0})
. Since
T
is
globally extremal, equality must hold. The rigidity statement in Theorem
3.6

therefore implies that
T
itself is a double spider.

Write

T=\operatorname{DS}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p}\,;\,b_{1},\dots,b_{q})

with
a_{1}=b_{1}=r
. If both
p\geq 2
and
q\geq 2
, then at the inverse eigenvalue

\rho_{T}=1/\lambda_{2}(T)
one of the two sums

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{\rho_{T}-a_{i}},\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{1}{\rho_{T}-b_{j}}

is at least the other. After relabeling the two sides if necessary, Lemma
3.7

transports a nonprincipal branch from the smaller side to the larger one and strictly increases

\lambda_{2}
, contradicting extremality. Thus one side of
T
consists of a single branch.

A double spider with one side reduced to a single branch has at most one branching point. Hence it
is a spider.
∎

4
Global classification and the large-order regime

4.1
Global two-profile reduction

We now combine the spider optimization from the first part of the paper with the structural reduction
from Theorem
3.8
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
D=2r+1\geq 3
and let
n\geq D+1
.
Let
M=n-2r-2.
Then the following hold.

(i)

If
M=0
, then the unique maximizer of
\lambda_{2}
among all trees with order
n
and
diameter
D
is the path
P_{D+1}
.

(ii)

Assume
M\geq 1
, and define

s=\left\lceil\frac{r}{2}\right\rceil,\qquad q_{-}=\max\!\left\{1,\left\lfloor\frac{M}{s}\right\rfloor\right\},\qquad q_{+}=\left\lceil\frac{M}{s}\right\rceil.

Write

M=q_{-}c_{-}+t_{-},\qquad 0\leq t_{-}<q_{-},

M=q_{+}c_{+}+t_{+},\qquad 0\leq t_{+}<q_{+}.

Then

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}|V(T)|=n\\
\operatorname{diam}(T)=D\end{subarray}}\lambda_{2}(T)=\max\Bigl\{\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathrm{AS}(r,q_{-}+2,c_{-},t_{-})\bigr),\,\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathrm{AS}(r,q_{+}+2,c_{+},t_{+})\bigr)\Bigr\}.

(9)

Every maximizer is isomorphic either to
\mathrm{AS}(r,q_{-}+2,c_{-},t_{-})
or to
\mathrm{AS}(r,q_{+}+2,c_{+},t_{+})
.
In particular, if the two values on the right-hand side of (
9
)
are different, then the maximizer is unique.

Proof.

If
M=0
, then
n=D+1
, so the only tree with order
n
and diameter
D
is the path
P_{D+1}
.

Assume now that
M\geq 1
.
Let
T
be a tree with
|V(T)|=n
and
\operatorname{diam}(T)=D
maximizing
\lambda_{2}
.
By Theorem
3.8
, the tree
T
must be a spider.
The exact spider optimization theorem, namely Theorem
2.5
, therefore shows that

T
is a generalized almost seesaw tree
\mathrm{AS}(r,q+2,c,t)
for some feasible integer
q
, where
M=qc+t
and
0\leq t<q
.
The unimodality theorem, Theorem
2.6
, then reduces the remaining optimization
over
q
to the two adjacent values
q_{-}
and
q_{+}
.
This proves that every maximizer is one of the two generalized almost seesaw trees in the statement, and that the
maximal value is exactly the right-hand side of (
9
).

Conversely, both generalized almost seesaw trees
\mathrm{AS}(r,q_{-}+2,c_{-},t_{-})
and
\mathrm{AS}(r,q_{+}+2,c_{+},t_{+})
are admissible trees of order
n

and diameter
D
, so the right-hand side of (
9
) is always attained.
The uniqueness claim is immediate.
∎

4.2
A single-threshold comparison

We next reorganize the remaining comparison by the order parameter
M=n-2r-2
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Fix
r\geq 2
and let
s=\lceil r/2\rceil
.
Let
1\leq t\leq s-1
, let
k\geq s
, and let
M=ks+t
.
Then

q_{-}=\left\lfloor\frac{M}{s}\right\rfloor=k,\qquad q_{+}=\left\lceil\frac{M}{s}\right\rceil=k+1.

Hence the two candidates from Theorem
4.1
are

A_{k,t}=\mathrm{AS}(r,k+2,s,t)=S\bigl(r+1,r,\underbrace{s+1,\dots,s+1}_{t},\underbrace{s,\dots,s}_{k-t}\bigr),

and

B_{k,t}=\mathrm{AS}(r,k+3,s-1,k+t-s+1)=S\bigl(r+1,r,\underbrace{s,\dots,s}_{k+t-s+1},\underbrace{s-1,\dots,s-1}_{s-t}\bigr).

Let
F_{A_{k,t}}
and
F_{B_{k,t}}
be the corresponding root functions from Lemma
2.2
.
Then, on
I_{r}=\left(\frac{1}{r+1},\frac{1}{r}\right)
,
we have

F_{B_{k,t}}(\lambda)-F_{A_{k,t}}(\lambda)=\frac{P_{s,t}(\lambda)}{(1-(s-1)\lambda)(1-s\lambda)(1-(s+1)\lambda)},

(10)

where

P_{s,t}(\lambda)=1-3s\lambda+(2s^{2}+s-2t-1)\lambda^{2}.

Moreover, the following hold.

(i)

If
r=2s
and
2t\leq s-1
, then

\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t})\qquad\text{for every }k\geq s.

(ii)

If
r=2s-1
and
2t\geq s-1
, then

\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t})\qquad\text{for every }k\geq s.

(iii)

In the remaining cases, namely

r=2s,\ 2t\geq s,\qquad\text{or}\qquad r=2s-1,\ 2t\leq s-2,

the polynomial
P_{s,t}
has a unique root
\zeta_{s,t}
in
I_{r}
.
Define

\kappa_{r,t}=-(1-s\zeta_{s,t})\left(\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\zeta_{s,t}}+\frac{1}{1-r\zeta_{s,t}}+\frac{t-s+1}{1-s\zeta_{s,t}}+\frac{s-t}{1-(s-1)\zeta_{s,t}}\right).

Then, for every
k\geq s
,

\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t})\iff k>\kappa_{r,t},

\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t})\iff k<\kappa_{r,t},

and equality holds if and only if
k=\kappa_{r,t}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
In particular, for each fixed residue class
t
, there is at most one value of
k
for which the two candidates tie.

Proof.

The identities
q_{-}=k
and
q_{+}=k+1
are immediate from
M=ks+t
with
1\leq t\leq s-1
.
The displayed forms of
A_{k,t}
and
B_{k,t}
follow directly from the definition of the generalized almost seesaw trees.

Write

\alpha_{k,t}=\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t}),\qquad\beta_{k,t}=\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t}).

By Lemma
2.2
, both
F_{A_{k,t}}
and
F_{B_{k,t}}
are strictly increasing on
I_{r}
, and
\alpha_{k,t},\beta_{k,t}
are their unique roots there.

A direct computation gives

\displaystyle F_{A_{k,t}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-r\lambda}+\frac{t}{1-(s+1)\lambda}+\frac{k-t}{1-s\lambda},

\displaystyle F_{B_{k,t}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-r\lambda}+\frac{k+t-s+1}{1-s\lambda}+\frac{s-t}{1-(s-1)\lambda}.

Subtracting the two expressions yields (
10
).

Since
s\leq r
, all three denominator factors in (
10
) are positive on
I_{r}
.
Hence the sign of
F_{B_{k,t}}-F_{A_{k,t}}
on
I_{r}
is exactly the sign of
P_{s,t}
.

We first show that
P_{s,t}
is strictly decreasing on
I_{r}
.
Indeed,

P^{\prime}_{s,t}(\lambda)=-3s+2(2s^{2}+s-2t-1)\lambda.

If
r=2s
, then
\lambda<1/r=1/(2s)
, and therefore

P^{\prime}_{s,t}(\lambda)<-3s+\frac{2(2s^{2}+s-2t-1)}{2s}=-s+1-\frac{2t+1}{s}<0.

If
r=2s-1
, then
\lambda<1/r=1/(2s-1)
, and hence

P^{\prime}_{s,t}(\lambda)<-3s+\frac{2(2s^{2}+s-2t-1)}{2s-1}=\frac{-2s^{2}+5s-4t-2}{2s-1}<0.

Thus
P_{s,t}
is strictly decreasing on
I_{r}
in all cases.

We now evaluate
P_{s,t}
at the endpoints of
I_{r}
.

Case 1:
r=2s
.

Then

P_{s,t}\!\left(\frac{1}{2s+1}\right)=\frac{2(s-t)}{(2s+1)^{2}}>0,

and

P_{s,t}\!\left(\frac{1}{2s}\right)=\frac{s-2t-1}{4s^{2}}.

If
2t\leq s-1
, then both endpoint values are nonnegative, and strict decrease implies

P_{s,t}(\lambda)>0\qquad\text{for all }\lambda\in I_{r}.

Hence
F_{B_{k,t}}(\lambda)-F_{A_{k,t}}(\lambda)>0
on
I_{r}
.
Evaluating at
\lambda=\beta_{k,t}
gives

0-F_{A_{k,t}}(\beta_{k,t})>0,

so
F_{A_{k,t}}(\beta_{k,t})<0
.
Since
F_{A_{k,t}}
is strictly increasing and vanishes at
\alpha_{k,t}
, we obtain
\beta_{k,t}<\alpha_{k,t}
, that is,

\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t}).

This proves (i).

If instead
2t\geq s
, then

P_{s,t}\!\left(\frac{1}{2s+1}\right)>0,\qquad P_{s,t}\!\left(\frac{1}{2s}\right)<0.

Since
P_{s,t}
is strictly decreasing, it has a unique root
\zeta_{s,t}
in
I_{r}
.

Case 2:
r=2s-1
.

Then

P_{s,t}\!\left(\frac{1}{2s}\right)=\frac{s-2t-1}{4s^{2}},

while

P_{s,t}\!\left(\frac{1}{2s-1}\right)=-\frac{2t}{(2s-1)^{2}}<0.

If
2t\geq s-1
, then both endpoint values are nonpositive, and strict decrease implies

P_{s,t}(\lambda)<0\qquad\text{for all }\lambda\in I_{r}.

Hence
F_{B_{k,t}}(\lambda)-F_{A_{k,t}}(\lambda)<0
on
I_{r}
.
Evaluating at
\lambda=\alpha_{k,t}
gives

F_{B_{k,t}}(\alpha_{k,t})-0<0,

so
F_{B_{k,t}}(\alpha_{k,t})<0
.
Since
F_{B_{k,t}}
is strictly increasing and vanishes at
\beta_{k,t}
, we get
\alpha_{k,t}<\beta_{k,t}
, that is,

\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t}).

This proves (ii).

If instead
2t\leq s-2
, then

P_{s,t}\!\left(\frac{1}{2s}\right)>0,\qquad P_{s,t}\!\left(\frac{1}{2s-1}\right)<0,

so again
P_{s,t}
has a unique root
\zeta_{s,t}
in
I_{r}
.

We are thus left with the two threshold cases, in which
P_{s,t}
has a unique root
\zeta_{s,t}\in I_{r}
.
Since
P_{s,t}
is strictly decreasing, we have

P_{s,t}(\lambda)>0\iff\lambda<\zeta_{s,t},\qquad P_{s,t}(\lambda)<0\iff\lambda>\zeta_{s,t}.

Equivalently,

F_{B_{k,t}}(\lambda)-F_{A_{k,t}}(\lambda)>0\iff\lambda<\zeta_{s,t}.

Now

\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t})\iff\alpha_{k,t}>\beta_{k,t}\iff F_{A_{k,t}}(\beta_{k,t})<0\iff F_{B_{k,t}}(\beta_{k,t})-F_{A_{k,t}}(\beta_{k,t})>0,

and hence

\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t})\iff\beta_{k,t}<\zeta_{s,t}.

Since
F_{B_{k,t}}
is strictly increasing and vanishes at
\beta_{k,t}
, this is equivalent to

F_{B_{k,t}}(\zeta_{s,t})>0.

Likewise,

\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t})\iff F_{B_{k,t}}(\zeta_{s,t})<0,

and equality holds if and only if
F_{B_{k,t}}(\zeta_{s,t})=0
.

Finally, we compute
F_{B_{k,t}}(\zeta_{s,t})
:

\displaystyle F_{B_{k,t}}(\zeta_{s,t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\zeta_{s,t}}+\frac{1}{1-r\zeta_{s,t}}+\frac{k+t-s+1}{1-s\zeta_{s,t}}+\frac{s-t}{1-(s-1)\zeta_{s,t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{1-s\zeta_{s,t}}+\left(\frac{1}{1-(r+1)\zeta_{s,t}}+\frac{1}{1-r\zeta_{s,t}}+\frac{t-s+1}{1-s\zeta_{s,t}}+\frac{s-t}{1-(s-1)\zeta_{s,t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k-\kappa_{r,t}}{1-s\zeta_{s,t}}.

Because
1-s\zeta_{s,t}>0
, the sign of
F_{B_{k,t}}(\zeta_{s,t})
is exactly the sign of
k-\kappa_{r,t}
.
Therefore

\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t})\iff k>\kappa_{r,t},

\lambda_{2}(B_{k,t})>\lambda_{2}(A_{k,t})\iff k<\kappa_{r,t},

and equality holds if and only if
k=\kappa_{r,t}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
This proves (iii).
∎

4.3
Order-by-order classification

The case
r=1
(that is, diameter
3
) is immediate: here
s=1
and
q_{-}=q_{+}=M
, so the unique extremizer is

\mathrm{AS}(1,M+2,1,0)=S(2,1,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{M}).

Hence we may assume
r\geq 2
in the order-by-order discussion below.

Theorem 4.3
.

Fix
r\geq 2
, let
s=\lceil r/2\rceil
, and let
n\geq 2r+2
.
Write
M=n-2r-2
.
Then the extremal problem for trees with order
n
and diameter
2r+1
falls into the following cases.

(i)

If
M=0
, then the unique extremizer is the path
P_{2r+2}
.

(ii)

If
1\leq M<s
, then
q_{-}=q_{+}=1
, and the unique extremizer is

\mathrm{AS}(r,3,M,0)=S(r+1,r,M).

(iii)

If
s\mid M
, then
q_{-}=q_{+}=M/s
, and the unique extremizer is

\mathrm{AS}\!\left(r,\frac{M}{s}+2,s,0\right).

(iv)

Suppose
M=ks+t
with
1\leq t\leq s-1
and
k\geq s
.
Then the only candidates are
A_{k,t}=\mathrm{AS}(r,k+2,s,t)
and
B_{k,t}=\mathrm{AS}(r,k+3,s-1,k+t-s+1)
from Proposition
4.2
.
More precisely:

(a)

if
r=2s
and
2t\leq s-1
, then the unique extremizer is
A_{k,t}
;

(b)

if
r=2s-1
and
2t\geq s-1
, then the unique extremizer is
B_{k,t}
;

(c)

in the remaining threshold cases, the extremizer is
A_{k,t}
when
k>\kappa_{r,t}
,
the extremizer is
B_{k,t}
when
k<\kappa_{r,t}
, and both are extremal when
k=\kappa_{r,t}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(v)

Suppose
M=ks+t
with
1\leq k\leq s-1
and
1\leq t\leq s-1
.
Then the only candidates are the two generalized almost seesaw trees from Theorem
4.1
, namely

\mathrm{AS}(r,q_{-}+2,c_{-},t_{-})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{AS}(r,q_{+}+2,c_{+},t_{+}),

and the winner is determined by comparing the two explicit root equations from Theorem
2.5
.
For fixed
r
, these are finitely many initial orders.

Proof.

Cases (i)–(iii) are immediate from Theorem
4.1
, since in each of them
q_{-}=q_{+}
.
Case (iv) follows from Theorem
4.1
together with Proposition
4.2
; indeed, for
M=ks+t
with
k\geq s
and
