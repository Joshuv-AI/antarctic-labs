Indoor and outdoor air pollution exposure in relation to incident symptomatic knee osteoarthritis: a 10-year longitudinal cohort study in China
Authors: Zikang Zhong, Jiaxiang Gao, Huan Zhang, Xufeng Jiao, Haozhe Zheng, Ainikaerjiang Aiyisha, Cheng Huang, Jun Lin, Weiguo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrently, the evidence regarding the association between air pollution and osteoarthritis is mainly limited to cross-sectional studies. This study aims to investigate the longitudinal impact of indoor and outdoor air pollution on the risk of symptomatic knee osteoarthritis (sKOA) in a nationally representative Chinese cohort.MethodBaseline characteristics were described for 17,096 CHARLS participants aged ≥ 45 years with complete baseline data, among whom 1,590 had prevalent sKOA at Wave 1. After excluding these prevalent cases and 994 participants without follow-up data, 14,512 participants were included in the longitudinal analysis of incident sKOA, with follow-up from 2011 to 2020. Indoor pollution was assessed based on the use of solid fuels for cooking and heating. Outdoor pollutants (PM₁, PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, NO₂, and O₃) data were obtained from the CHAPs Dataset. Cox proportional hazards model, GBTM and DLNM were used to evaluate the associations, and spatial autocorrelation analysis was employed to map the geographical clustering of cases.ResultDuring the 9-year follow-up period, a total of 698 new cases of sKOA were identified. Cooking with solid fuels increased the risk of sKOA by 36% (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.05–1.77, P = 0.021); using both cooking and heating simultaneously further increased the risk to 46% (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.07–2.00, P = 0.017). The crude incidence rates of sKOA were 5.18, 6.07, and 6.61 per 1,000 person-years among participants exposed to zero, one, and two sources of indoor pollution, respectively, indicating a graded dose-response relationship. Trajectory analysis showed that compared with the High-Decreasing trajectory group, the Low-Stable PM₂.₅ trajectory group faced a higher risk of sKOA (HR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.20–2.30, P = 0.002), and similar patterns were observed for PM₁ and NO₂. DLNM analysis indicated that the effect of PM₂.₅ emerged approximately one year after exposure, and reached its peak at a concentration of 40–60 µg/m³ (HR ≈ 2.4). Additionally, the cases showed significant geographical clustering (Moran’s I = 0.55, P < 0.001). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals; no violation was detected for any covariate (all individual p > 0.05), and the global test confirmed overall model adequacy (χ² = 13.80, df = 16, p = 0.614).ConclusionBoth indoor solid fuel combustion and outdoor air pollution were associated with increased risk of sKOA, but the pathways of their effects are different. Indoor exposure appears to follow a cumulative dose–response pattern, while outdoor pollution exhibits a time-dependent effect with a lag. Switching to clean household energy and reducing environmental pollution may contribute to the prevention of knee osteoarthritis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28080-y.Keywords: Symptomatic knee osteoarthritis, Air pollution, Solid fuel, Particulate matter, Longitudinal cohort study, Distributed lag non-linear model

Introduction

As a complex condition with multifactorial origins, osteoarthritis (OA) constitutes a principal cause of global disability. Over 500 million people suffer from the disease, which leads to chronic pain and limits physical function [1]. In China, with the progression of population aging, the burden of arthritis on both individuals and society is expected to increase intensely [2–4].

Beyond established risk factors such as age, obesity, and joint injury, environmental exposures have drawn increasing attention. Recent investigations have implicated air pollution as a potential risk factor for the development of OA [5]. Biologically, inhaled pollutants can trigger low-grade systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which have been implicated in cartilage degradation [5]. Circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines may also reach joint tissues and accelerate matrix breakdown [6, 7].

China provides an informative context for examining this relationship, given the widespread use of solid fuels in rural households [8–10]. Yet research on air pollution and musculoskeletal health remains inconclusive. Most studies used cross-sectional designs or examined only single pollutants, making it hard to establish causality [11, 12]. Even prospective studies typically treated pollution as a static exposure, asking whether higher baseline levels predict higher OA risk. However, this approach does not account for how pollution exposure evolves over time. Additionally, prior work often grouped all joint sites together rather than focusing on knee osteoarthritis (KOA) [12].

Therefore, we followed CHARLS participants from 2011 to 2020 and examined how both indoor and outdoor pollution relate to new symptomatic knee osteoarthritis(sKOA) cases. Combining survival analysis, trajectory modeling, and distributed lag non-linear models [13], we characterized dose-response relationships and lag structures linking pollution exposure to sKOA risk across different regions.

Specifically, the cross-basis function was constructed using natural cubic splines with 3 degrees of freedom for both the exposure-response and lag-response dimensions. The maximum lag period was set at 5 years. Reference values for hazard ratio estimation were based on the WHO 2021 Global Air Quality Guidelines annual mean values (PM2.5: 5 µg/m³; PM10: 15 µg/m³; NO2: 10 µg/m³; O3: 60 µg/m³). For PM1, which lacks a formal guideline, the baseline median concentration served as the reference. Predictions were constrained to the 1st–99th percentile of observed concentrations to mitigate instability in data-sparse tails of the distribution.

Methods

Study design and population

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal cohort study [14]. CHARLS employed a multistage, stratified, probability-proportional-to-size (PPS) sampling strategy to ensure the sample is representative of the middle-aged and elderly population aged 45 and above in China.

The initial national baseline survey (Wave 1) was conducted in 2011, enrolling a total of 17,708 participants from 450 villages/urban communities across 150 counties/districts in 28 provinces. Comprehensive information, including sociodemographic characteristics, health status, and lifestyle factors, was collected through a standardized questionnaire. Subsequently, follow-up surveys were conducted every two to three years. This analysis uses data from five waves of the survey conducted between 2011 and 2020.

We first applied age (≥ 45 years) and baseline-completeness criteria, yielding 17,096 participants who constituted the baseline descriptive sample (Table 1). For the longitudinal analysis of incident sKOA, we further excluded 1,590 participants with prevalent sKOA at Wave 1 and 994 participants without any follow-up data, leaving 14,512 participants in the analytic cohort for the Cox and sensitivity logistic regression models (Fig. 1). The spatial autocorrelation analysis of sKOA incidence was conducted at the prefecture-city level based on this longitudinal cohort.

The CHARLS study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Peking University (Approval No. IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Exposure assessment

Indoor air pollution

Exposure to indoor air pollution was assessed based on participants’ self-reported household fuel types for cooking and heating, as documented in the CHARLS structured questionnaire. Fuel types were categorized as clean fuels (including biogas, liquefied petroleum gas, electricity, etc.) and solid fuels (coal, firewood). Participants were defined as being exposed to indoor solid fuel pollution if they used any type of solid fuel for either cooking or heating.

Outdoor air pollution

We obtained ambient air pollutant concentration data from the China High-resolution Air Pollutants (CHAPs) database [15–19]. The CHAPs database provides daily nationwide air pollutant concentration datasets generated through machine learning models (such as random forests or deep learning models), integrating satellite remote sensing retrievals, ground-based measurements, meteorological data, and land-use information. This database features a high spatial resolution (typically 1 km × 1 km), which effectively captures local variations in pollution levels. For this study, we extracted data for several major pollutants from 2010 to 2020, including particulate matter (PM₁, PM₂.₅, PM₁₀), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), and ozone (O₃).

As the geographical information for participants in the CHARLS database is generally precise only to the city or district/county level, we employed a spatial data fusion approach to assign air pollution concentrations to each participant. By matching each participant’s residential geographic coordinates with the corresponding grid cells in the CHAPs database, we estimated the annual average air pollutant exposure level for each participant’s residential location.

Definition of covariates

The study included the following covariates: age (continuous), gender (female/male), marital status (married, not married), living residence (rural, urban), education level (illiterate, literate), smoking status (non-smoker, ex-smoker, current smoker), drinking status (never, < 1 time/month, ≥ 1 time/month), body mass index (BMI) (continuous), history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and stroke (no or yes), and mCCI (modified Charlson Comorbidity Index) (continuous). These data were collected by uniformly trained investigators using a standard questionnaire. The calculation methods for mCCI were derived from previous studies [20, 21]. Participants with missing data on any covariates were excluded from the analysis.

Outcome assessment

Following the definitions used in previous studies [22–24], symptomatic knee osteoarthritis (sKOA) was defined as a self-reported history of physician-diagnosed arthritis accompanied by concurrent knee pain. As CHARLS relies on structured interviews rather than medical records, all diagnostic information was ascertained through participant recall. While standard in large-scale population surveys, this approach may introduce some degree of misclassification, which we address in the limitations. A participant was confirmed to have sKOA if they answered “yes” to the first two of the following questions and “knee” to the third: (1) “Have you been diagnosed with arthritis or rheumatism by a doctor?“; (2) “Are you often troubled by body pain?“. If the participant answered “yes” to the second question, they were then asked question (3): “In which part of your body do you feel pain? (listing all sites of body pain)”. An incident case of sKOA was defined as a participant who was free of sKOA in the 2011 CHARLS baseline survey but was subsequently diagnosed with KOA in a later wave.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables as proportions. Baseline characteristics were compared between participants with and without sKOA using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and Chi-square test for categorical variables.We used Cox proportional hazards models to estimate incident sKOA risk associated with indoor air pollution, using clean-fuel users as the reference group. The additive effects of solid fuel use were also assessed.

For outdoor pollution, GBTM using maximum likelihood estimation was applied to identify outdoor air pollutants trajectories from 2011 to 2020 using PROC TRAJ in SAS [25]. The shape of each trajectory was fitted in order as cubic, quadratic, and linear. The number of trajectory groups was selected based on the lowest Bayesian information criterion. The final model included four groups.

Distributed-Lag Non-Linear Models (DLNMs) were used to examine exposure–lag–response relationships between air pollutants and sKOA risk. Spatial autocorrelation analyses were performed at the prefecture-city level. To avoid unstable estimates in small-population areas, cities with fewer than 10 participants were excluded, and prefecture-city-level incidence rates were variance-stabilized using local empirical Bayes smoothing (spdep::EBlocal). Queen-contiguity neighbor structures were constructed from GADM Level-2 boundaries. Global Moran’s I and Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) were then computed on the smoothed rates, and LISA p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with significant local clusters defined at FDR-adjusted p < 0.1.

We adjusted models sequentially for demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities. To examine potential effect modification, we performed stratified analyses by age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex, and region. As sensitivity analyses, we also constructed multivariable logistic regression models with incident sKOA as the dependent variable. To assess whether potential confounding by physical activity and occupational factors, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses. First, we additionally adjusted for metabolic equivalent of task (MET) scores derived from self-reported physical activity levels to assess whether physical activity confounded the associations between HAP and incident sKOA. Second, given that agricultural labor may involve prolonged kneeling, squatting, and heavy lifting—established biomechanical risk factors for knee OA—we further adjusted for agricultural work status (agricultural vs. non-agricultural workers) in our fully adjusted models. Additionally, we performed stratified analyses by agricultural work status and tested for effect modification using multiplicative interaction terms. GBTM was performed in SAS; all other analyses were conducted in R 4.4.2. Statistical significance was set at two-sided P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics and descriptive results

The baseline descriptive sample comprised 17,096 participants (Table 1). The mean age was 59.4 years, and 1,590 (9.3%) had prevalent sKOA at Wave 1. After excluding these prevalent cases and 994 participants without follow-up data, 14,512 participants contributed to the longitudinal analysis of incident sKOA. Among the baseline sample, participants with sKOA tended to be older and female, and more often lived in rural areas. Solid fuel use for cooking and heating was more common in the sKOA group than in the Non-sKOA group (70% vs. 52%, P < 0.001). During follow-up (2011–2020), 698 incident sKOA cases were identified.

Indoor air pollution

As shown in Fig. 2, a positive association was observed between indoor solid fuel use and incident sKOA. Compared with clean-fuel users, people who use solid fuel for cooking had a higher risk of sKOA in the crude model (HR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.59–2.00, P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), the HR for solid cooking fuel users was 1.36 (95% CI: 1.05–1.77, P = 0.021). However, The association failed to reach statistical significance in the people using solid fuels for heating.

Association between indoor air pollution and sKOA

Exposure to a single source of indoor pollution was not statistically significant. However, cumulative exposure to both cooking and heating fuel was associated with a 46% increased risk of sKOA (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.07–2.00, P = 0.017).

Subgroup analyses showed consistent associations between indoor air pollution and incident sKOA (Figure S2-S3). For cooking pollution, no significant effect modification was found (all P for interaction > 0.05), though the association reached significance in rural residents (HR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.06–1.89), those with BMI < 24 kg/m² (HR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.01–2.17), and participants with low comorbidity burden (HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.06–1.91).

The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for all models (Supplementary Table S1).

Outdoor air pollution

GBTM identified four trajectory groups for PM₂.₅, PM₁, and NO₂ over 2011–2020: High-Decreasing, High-Stable, Low-Decreasing, and Low-Stable (Fig. 3). Trajectory patterns for PM₁₀ and O₃ are shown in Figure S1.

Outdoor pollutant exposure patterns

Using the High-Decreasing group as reference, Cox models showed that participants in the Low-Stable group had significantly higher sKOA risk. For PM₂.₅, the HR was 1.66 (95% CI: 1.20–2.30, P = 0.002). The associations were also significant for PM₁ (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.06–2.31, P = 0.025) and NO₂ (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.04–2.13, P = 0.029). The High-Stable and Low-Decreasing groups showed intermediate risks, with some associations not reaching statistical significance (Fig. 4).

sKOA across long-term air pollution exposure trajectories

In DLNM analyses, PM₁, PM₂.₅, PM₁₀ and O₃ showed significant associations with sKOA only after a 0.8–1.2-year lag, with effects peaking at 1.5–2 years (Fig. 5). NO₂ effects emerged earlier, at approximately 3–6 months. Exposure-response relationships were generally linear, though fine PM showed a plateau above 70 µg/m³.

Exposure-lag-response association between air pollutant exposure and sKOA risk

Spatial heterogeneity and clustering

sKOA incidence was higher in prefecture-city-level units in western and southwestern China (Sichuan, Yunnan, and Guizhou provinces) and lower in eastern coastal and northern regions (Fig. 6). Moran’s I analysis indicated significant spatial clustering (I = 0.55, Z = 6.32, P < 0.001), and LISA identified high-high clusters in Liangshan, Chuxiong, and Puyang (Fig. 7).

Additional sensitivity analyses supported the stability of our findings. Point estimates were virtually identical across sensitivity analyses; minor differences in confidence-interval bounds (e.g., 1.04–1.77 vs. 1.05–1.77) reflect the sequential addition of covariates. After further adjustment for MET-derived physical activity scores, the associations between HAP exposures and incident sKOA remained essentially unchanged: the HRs for cooking with solid fuels (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.04–1.77), heating with solid fuels (HR = 1.24, 95% CI: 0.97–1.58), and dual-source exposure (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.07–2.00) were consistent with the primary analysis. Similarly, further adjustment for agricultural work status resulted in minimal changes in effect estimates: the percentage changes in HRs were 1.44% for cooking pollution, − 0.24% for heating pollution, and 0.40% for dual-source pollution exposure, respectively. Stratified analyses by agricultural work status showed consistent associations in both agricultural workers (n = 6,256) and non-agricultural workers (n = 6,418), and no significant interactions were observed between HAP exposures and agricultural work status (all P for interaction > 0.05; cooking: P = 0.308; heating: P = 0.508; pollution load: P = 0.170). These results indicate that occupational physical demands and leisure-time physical activity did not materially confound the observed associations.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of CHARLS participants followed from 2011 to 2020, we found that both indoor and outdoor air pollution were associated with increased risk of sKOA. For indoor pollution, cooking with solid fuels increased sKOA risk by 36% (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.05–1.77), while using solid fuels for both cooking and heating raised this risk to 46% (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.07–2.00). For outdoor pollution, temporal patterns of exposure appeared more important than absolute concentrations.

Our results fit with previous research while adding new layers. Earlier CHARLS-based work found 6.8% higher spinal OA risk per 10 µg/m³ PM₂.₅ [26]. Studies in low- and middle-income countries linked household air pollution to arthritis, and large cohort analyses connected ambient pollution to OA risk [11, 27, 28].

The association between indoor solid fuel use and sKOA observed in our study is consistent with previous findings from CHARLS and other cohort studies. Deng et al. reported that solid fuel use for cooking increased the risk of self-reported arthritis by 15% (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.08–1.23) in the 2011–2015 CHARLS cohort [29] Cross-sectional evidence from the WHO Study also supports an association between household air pollution and arthritis [11]. The relationship between indoor solid fuel use and sKOA involves multiple aspects. Combustion of solid fuel such as coal generates high concentrations of particulate matter, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and carbon monoxide within the household environment [8]. Additionally, cooking with solid fuels in rural China typically involves prolonged squatting, kneeling, or sitting near ground-level stoves [11], which may impose mechanical stress on knee joints and accelerate cartilage degeneration.

The cumulative relationship between the use of solid fuels and the risk of knee joint osteoarthritis is consistent with the results of previous studies on the respiratory and cardiovascular systems. The cumulative exposure index, an index related to respiratory diseases, indicates that the duration of exposure to biomass smoke is proportional to the severity of the disease [30]. This relationship may reflect the gradual accumulation of oxidative damage caused using solid fuels and the low-level systemic inflammation, which may gradually compromise cartilage integrity and chondrocyte function, thereby promoting the development of arthritis.

The trajectory findings provide important insights into the temporal dynamics of pollution effects. People in the High-Decreasing group had lower sKOA risk, while those in the Low-Stable group had elevated risk. This result suggests that the rate of pollution reduction, rather than absolute levels, may influence sKOA risk. Rapid improvements in air quality appear to be associated with lower sKOA risk.

The DLNM analyses revealed distinct lag structures for different pollutants. For PM₁, PM₂.₅, and PM₁₀, it showed significant associations with sKOA only after 0.8–1.2 years of exposure, with effects peaking at 1.5–2 years. This delayed onset is plausible given the chronic nature of OA pathogenesis [31]. These pollutants can trigger systemic inflammation through pulmonary oxidative stress by Akt/PI3k pathway, which could lead to cartilage degradation and radiographic changes [7, 32, 33]. NO₂ effects emerged earlier at approximately 3–6 months for its special function. Two studies showed that NO₂ can rapidly induce endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory cascades [34–36].

These lag structures have important implications for prevention strategies. Studies of neurodegenerative diseases have demonstrated that PM2.5 effects can persist for up to 10 years and NO₂ effects for 8 years [37]. The delay between exposure and disease suggests a window of opportunity for intervention.

The global Moran’s I, computed on empirical-Bayes-smoothed prefecture-city-level rates, indicated significant positive spatial autocorrelation in sKOA incidence, suggesting that areas with high incidence tended to cluster together. LISA analysis further revealed spatial heterogeneity at the local level. High-high clusters were predominantly located in prefecture-city-level units in western and southwestern China, including Liangshan, Chuxiong, and Puyang, where solid fuel combustion for heating and cooking remains common [38]. This geographic pattern is consistent with findings from a previous study [39].

There are several advantages in this study. We have identified the cumulative effect of indoor pollution, which supports the case for transitioning to clean energy. Considering that the pollution in China is decreasing year by year and sKOA is a chronic disease, we used DLNM to capture the lag and cumulative effects of pollution. This created an exposure time window for us to guide the governance of air pollution, helping avoid the bias that a conventional Cox model might introduce when pollution levels shift over time. Our spatial aggregation analysis also showed that sKOA incidence was not randomly distributed across regions, which provides a basis for subsequent policymaking. The results of GBTM further suggested that faster pollution decline was associated with lower sKOA risk, which is consistent with the idea that pollution control requires sustained effort [40]; however, this observation may also reflect concurrent improvements in socioeconomic conditions, healthcare access, or lifestyle in the same regions. We recognize that the GBTM-based trajectory analysis is descriptive in nature, and the trajectory groups are statistical summaries and should not be interpreted as fixed exposure pathways. The dose-response gradient across trajectory groups hints at a causal link, but as an observational study, we cannot draw firm causal conclusions. Genetic susceptibility, dietary habits, and differences in healthcare access are among the unmeasured factors that could partly account for what we observed.

A notable concern in studies linking HAP to musculoskeletal outcomes is the potential for confounding by occupational physical demands, particularly in rural populations where agricultural labor involving repetitive kneeling, squatting, and heavy lifting is prevalent. To address this, we conducted sensitivity analyses adjusting for agricultural work status and MET-based physical activity levels. The negligible changes in effect estimates (all < 2%) and the absence of significant interaction effects between HAP exposures and agricultural work status (all P > 0.05) suggest that occupational biomechanical loading did not materially confound the associations observed in our study. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that self-reported occupational data may not fully capture the intensity and duration of biomechanical exposures, representing a limitation of our analysis.

The DLNM analysis uncovered non-linear exposure-response patterns for several pollutants. The cumulative hazard ratios for PM2.5 demonstrated a plateau effect at higher concentrations, which may reflect a biological ceiling effect at higher concentrations. One plausible explanation is that the body’s antioxidant defenses and mucociliary clearance become saturated at moderate PM concentrations, after which additional exposure has a proportionally smaller biological impact. It is also possible that inflammatory signaling pathways involved in cartilage degradation reach a functional ceiling at high pollutant loads. We cannot rule out that exposure misclassification at the tails of the distribution contributes to the observed flattening. Non-linear patterns of this kind have been reported for air pollution and cardiovascular endpoints, which is consistent with what we found here [41].

Our study is not without limitations. Exposure to HAP was assessed based on self-reported fuel use for cooking and heating, which does not capture individual-level variation in actual pollutant concentrations, ventilation conditions, stove design, cooking duration, or time-activity patterns. Such misclassification is expected to be predominantly non-differential with respect to incident sKOA and would therefore tend to dilute the true associations rather than inflate them [42]. Ambient air pollutant concentrations were assigned at the community level using spatial interpolation, so individual-level differences in daily routines and time spent indoors versus outdoors are not reflected. Although the Moran’s I analyses, computed on empirical-Bayes-smoothed prefecture-city-level rates, confirmed significant positive spatial autocorrelation in sKOA incidence, the Cox and logistic regressions did not explicitly incorporate spatial random effects. To partially account for geographic confounding we used cluster-robust variance estimation and adjusted for urban/rural residence and region; nevertheless, residual spatial dependence cannot be fully ruled out. Future analyses employing spatial mixed-effects or geographically weighted Cox models may refine these estimates. The outcome definition also relied on self-reported physician-diagnosed arthritis, which may be subject to recall bias and does not distinguish between radiographic and symptomatic knee OA. Fifth, despite adjusting for multiple covariates including occupational factors and physical activity, we cannot fully rule out confounding by unmeasured factors like diet, genetic background, or prior knee injuries [43].

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings suggest that indoor solid fuel use and outdoor air pollution are both associated with increased sKOA risk among middle aged and older Chinese adults. Indoor exposure appears to show a cumulative effect, while outdoor pollutants exhibit a lagging effect, with the peak effect of particulate matter occurring between 1.5 and 2 years, and that of NO₂ between 3 and 6 months. Trajectory patterns characterized by declining pollutant exposure over time were associated with lower risk of sKOA. These trajectory findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of direct protection. These findings support the promotion of clean household fuels and improvement of air pollution as potential strategies for preventing knee osteoarthritis.
