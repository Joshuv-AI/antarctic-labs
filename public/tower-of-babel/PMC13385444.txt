Aging of hematopoietic stem cells is inconsequential to progenitor cell function
Authors: Jenna Myers, Atesh K Worthington, Donna M Poscablo, Paloma Medina, Saran Chattopadhyaya, Raana Mogharrab, Marcel GE Rommel, E Camilla Forsberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAging of the hematopoietic system has profound consequences for organismal health and longevity, attributed to the well-characterized functional aging of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). Here, we tested whether progenitor cells may demonstrate age resistance to enable hematopoietic homeostasis throughout life despite the functional decline of upstream HSCs. Strikingly, our results revealed unwavering reconstitution capacity by young and old progenitors, demonstrating that intermediate progenitors are functionally unaffected by aging and placing Flk2+ multipotent progenitors (MPPFs) as a potential source of age resilience. This unique finding was emphasized by unchanged transcriptomic, proliferation, and mitochondrial capacity of young and old MPPFs, revealing remarkable similarities upon aging. Considering that HSCs functionally decline with age, yet intermediate progenitors remain unperturbed and “age resilient”, we posit that MPPFs may play an essential role in protecting downstream progenitors from inheriting age-related properties from HSCs. We propose three potential mechanisms for how MPPFs maintain hematopoietic integrity and homeostasis with age.Keywords: aging, longevity, hematopoietic stem cells, hematopoietic progenitor cells, multipotent progenitor cells, transplantation, hematopoietic reconstitution, age resilience, single-cell RNA sequencing, transcriptomics, aging homeostasis

Introduction

Hematopoiesis is described as the process by which all mature blood and immune cells are produced, with the hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) residing at the top of the differentiation hierarchy (Figure S1). These HSCs, defined by their ability to self-renew and give rise to all mature blood cell lineages for the lifetime of an organism, have been studied extensively in the context of aging. In both humans and mice, it is well established that HSCs expand in numbers throughout life, and this coincides with impaired long-term multilineage reconstitution capacity (Dykstra et al., 2011; Elias et al., 2017; Morrison et al., 1996; Rossi et al., 2005; Sudo et al., 2000). Beyond transplantation, other hallmarks of HSC aging include changes in the transcriptome (Flohr Svendsen et al., 2021), cell proliferation (Aires et al., 2021; Morrison et al., 1996; K. Young et al., 2016), and mitochondrial dysfunction (Filippi, 2023; Girotra et al., 2023; Mansell et al., 2021; Nakamura-Ishizu et al., 2020; Snoeck, 2017; Totani et al., 2025; Watanuki et al., 2024; K. A. Young et al., 2025). However, despite the notable decline in HSCs across multiple parameters with age, downstream mature cell numbers do not consistently decline in parallel. This apparent paradox strongly suggests that the progenitor compartment, situated between HSCs and mature cells, may play a crucial role as a homeostatic buffer to compensate for the functional decline of upstream HSCs.

Utilization of the FlkSwitch lineage tracing mouse model definitively shows that all young hematopoietic progenitors utilize a common precursor cell downstream of the HSC—the Flk2+ multipotent progenitor (MPPF) (Beaudin et al., 2014; Boyer et al., 2019; Poscablo et al., 2021; 2024; Manso et al., 2025). However, we recently uncovered an additive, age-specific megakaryocyte progenitor (MkP) pathway, which deviates from canonical Flk2+ differentiation and is functionally distinct (Manso et al., 2025; Poscablo et al., 2024). The canonically MPPF-derived MkPs sustain their function despite aging. Therefore, we hypothesized that all canonically derived progenitors also maintain their function, demonstrating fundamental resistance to aging.

In this study, we investigated if the functional decline of HSCs is propagated upon differentiation into MPPs and other progenitor cells. Until now, we have yet to fully understand how myeloid and lymphoid progenitors are affected by aging beyond describing changes in steady-state frequencies or cell numbers. Furthermore, we seek to uncover how progenitor cells may compensate for the functional decline of HSCs to maintain the relatively steady production of mature blood cells observed with age.

Results

Age-related changes in hematopoietic cells are cell type specific

To understand the changes that occur during steady-state hematopoiesis upon aging, we performed the analysis of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) from young (Y; 2–3 months old) and old (O; 18–23 months old) C57BL/6 mice, following the definitions in Figure 1A (representative flow cytometry plots are shown in Figure S2). As has been previously reported (Boyer et al., 2019; De Haan and Van Zant, 1999; Morrison et al., 1996; Poscablo et al., 2021; Rando et al., 2025; Sudo et al., 2000), we observed an expansion of HSCs (cKit+Lineage−Sca1+Flk2−SLAM+ cells) with age in both frequency and total cell numbers (Figure 1B). Importantly, the numbers and frequencies of MPPFs remained unchanged with age. While Young et al. previously reported a decline in the frequency of this population with age (K. Young et al., 2016), this was neither reflected in the data we generated here nor in our prior publications (Poscablo et al., 2021; 2024); differences are possibly due to our inclusion of both males and females. In line with previous reports, we also observed a significant increase in the frequency of the myeloid progenitor (MyPro) compartment, although there were no significant changes in overall cell numbers (Figure 1C). Intriguingly, no other intermediate progenitor within the myeloid lineage showed any differences with age, including common myeloid progenitors (CMPs), granulocyte/monocyte progenitors (GMPs), and megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (MEPs). The MyPro expansion can, therefore, be attributed primarily to the significant increase in MkPs with age, which we confirmed here (Figure 1D). This myeloid frequency expansion, often referred to as “myeloid bias”, can also be explained by the concurrent decrease in the number and frequency of lymphoid cells produced, specifically common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs) (Figure 1E). From this, we deduced that despite the age-related expansion of HSCs and MkPs, the numbers of canonically derived MyPros, beginning with MPPFs and including downstream CMPs, GMPs, and MEPs, remain consistent in situ with age.

We additionally scrutinized the expression of key cell surface markers, including Flk2 and Sca-1, on Y and O HSCs and MPPFs, using the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). We observed a significant increase in Sca-1 expression among O HSCs compared with Y HSCs but no difference in Sca-1 expression between Y and O MPPFs (Figure S3). We did not observe any significant difference in the expression of Flk2 between Y and O MPPFs, and this finding is consistent with other published data (Solomon et al., 2024).

Y and O MPPFs are functionally equivalent upon transplantation

Given that MPPFs are presumed descendants of HSCs and the upstream precursors to all canonically derived progenitors, we aimed to explore the functional capabilities of these cells upon transplantation. By sorting MPPFs from Y and O mice and transplanting them into Y sublethally irradiated C57BL/6 (WT) recipients, we first hypothesized that O MPPFs would demonstrate functional impairment, similar to HSCs. This would suggest that the functional decline of aged HSCs is propagated downstream and that MPPF function is reliant upon the quality of the HSCs they are derived from. Unexpectedly, we observed no significant differences between Y- and O-transplanted MPPFs for any mature cell type in the peripheral blood (Figure 2). Even though these MPPs have been reported to be “lymphoid primed” (Cabezas-Wallscheid et al., 2014; Pietras et al., 2015), we observed contribution to all mature blood cell lineages including platelets (Plts), erythroid progenitors (EPs), granulocytes/monocytes (GMs), B cells, and T cells, regardless of age. These striking data informed a revised hypothesis according to which HSCs do not propagate their age-related decline to MPPFs, yet begs the question of whether the other progenitors also experience the same age resilience upon transplantation.

Classical MyPros maintain function despite old age

To test our hypothesis that MPPF-derived progenitor cells demonstrate age resistance, we compared how classical MyPros from Y or O donors function upon transplantation. We sorted CMPs or MEPs and transplanted 10K and 50K cells, respectively, per young sublethally irradiated recipient (Figures 3A and 3B) to track donor-derived peripheral blood chimerism and numbers. Regardless of whether the sorted cells came from FlkSwitch donors (definitively MPPF-derived by GFP expression) or other ubiquitously fluorescent mice, our data consistently revealed no robust, reproducible differences in blood cell contribution for any of the transplanted cell types. Overall RBC output tended to be more robust from Y progenitors, but these data were not statistically significant at any specific time point. These results support our revised hypothesis that MPPF-derived progenitors are unaffected by aging, or are “age resilient,” with stable steady state frequencies and numbers (Figure 1), and now functionally upon transplantation (Figure 3).

Classical lymphoid progenitors maintain function despite old age

The myeloid lineage expands by frequency in the bone marrow of mice upon aging, leading to what is commonly referred to as a “myeloid bias,” though this is not as pronounced in humans (Li et al., 2025; Tharmapalan and Wagner, 2024). Importantly, this “expansion” occurs largely due to the decline in lymphoid cells with age (Figure 1E). Consequently, the frequency of the MyPro pool increases despite the steady presence of CMP, MEP, and GMP cell numbers in old mice (Figure 1C). It has also been reported that HSCs derived from old donors tend to prioritize production of myeloid cells upon transplantation, with reduced contribution to lymphoid cells (Beerman et al., 2010; Cho et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2003; Liang et al., 2005; Rossi et al., 2005; Sudo et al., 2000). With this in mind, we tested whether the functional capacity of CLPs declines upon aging. Interestingly, transplantation of 5K CLPs from either Y or O donors revealed only minor functional differences in these cells upon aging, with a significant difference in B cell chimerism at a single time point, which was past the peak of B cell output (Figure 3C, 32 days post-transplantation). However, the same difference was not detected with respect to cell number output for B cells. These findings highlight the fact that the steady-state reduction in B and T cell output commonly observed with age is not due to a decline in CLP function. Rather, it is likely that the reduced B and T cell numbers upon aging reflect the consequence of reduced CLP numbers (Figure 1E).

Y and O MPPFs perform similarly despite old environment

Given the striking similarity between Y and O MPPF cell output in young recipients (Figure 2), we considered whether this age resilience could be altered by extrinsic cues. There is evidence demonstrating that aged bone marrow reflects an inflammatory environment, often described as “inflammaging” (Bogeska et al., 2022; Caiado et al., 2021; Kovtonyuk et al., 2016; J. Wang et al., 2022). Numerous pro-inflammatory cytokines tend to be elevated in the serum of old bone marrow compared to young, as well as in other hematopoietic tissues. We and others have demonstrated that Y HSCs perform better in an old environment than O HSCs (Bowie et al., 2007; De Haan and Lazare, 2018; Poscablo et al., 2021; Sudo et al., 2000). We, therefore, sought to determine if Y and O MPPFs respond uniquely to an aged environment. We hypothesized that Y MPPFs would engraft better than O MPPFs, like what is observed in heterochronic HSC transplants. Key cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and RANTES have also been reported to drive the myeloid differentiation bias upon transplantation of HSCs (Ergen et al., 2012; Verim, 2010; J. Wang et al., 2022); so, we were intrigued to determine if O MPPFs would be susceptible to myeloid biasness from the same extrinsic cues. To test the robustness of progenitors in this environment, we sorted and transplanted MPPFs from Y and O mice into old hosts (Figure 4). Surprisingly, when comparing Y and O MPPFs directly, we still detected no significant differences in donor chimerism and the output for Plts, EPs, GMs, or T cells in old hosts. Interestingly, the Y MPPFs outperformed the O MPPFs with respect to B cell output, although these differences were significant only at time points that sit at the decline of B cell production. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, this indicates that the reconstitution capacity of MPPFs is uniquely unperturbed upon transplantation, unlike O HSCs, and that O MPPFs are not responsive to the same myeloid differentiation bias that is typically observed with HSC transplantation. Given the lack of differences observed here, we conclude that the old microenvironment does not play a decisive role in mature cell output for Flk2+-derived progenitors upon transplantation.

MPPFs are transcriptomically similar with age

Considering that Y and O HSCs differ significantly in frequency and function and that this is accompanied by robust changes in gene expression (Flohr Svendsen et al., 2021), we sought to understand how MPPFs differ transcriptomically with age. Analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data that we previously generated (Manso et al., 2025; Poscablo et al., 2024) revealed that while >600 genes changed significantly between Y and O HSCs (Figure S4), there were only 126 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between Y and O MPPFs (Figures 5A and 5B; −1 > logFC > 1 and p < 0.05). Intriguingly, from the 89 genes that were upregulated with age, we observed the enrichment in genes that regulate erythrocyte development (Rhag, Gata1, Ank1, and Hbb-bs) (Figures 5C and S5). Meanwhile, of the 37 genes downregulated with age, we observed enrichment of genes important for response to virus and cytokine stimulus (Rtp4, Isg15, Ifi206, Ifi44, and Il7r). We then compared the transcriptomes between HSCs and MPPFs and discovered 1,569 DEGs for Y HSCs vs. Y MPPFs (Cabezas-Wallscheid et al., 2014; Flohr Svendsen et al., 2021; Forsberg et al., 2005; Pietras et al., 2015; Sommerkamp et al., 2021) (Figure S4) and 1,178 DEGs for O HSCs vs. O MPPFs (Figures 5A and 5B). To investigate further, we examined the genes that were upregulated in O MPPFs relative to O HSCs (Figures 5B and 5C). Many of these genes are regulators of B and T cell production, neutrophil activation, and regulation of antigen processing and presentation (Figure S5). This is perhaps unsurprising given that MPPF has been cited as a “lymphoid-biased” cell type. Conversely, the top two genes that were most significantly downregulated upon differentiation were Apoe and Pbx1. These genes have been cited to regulate the proliferation and differentiation of HSCs by influencing their metabolic activity, as well as regulating the balance between self-renewal and differentiation (Aires et al., 2021; Ficara et al., 2008a; Murphy et al., 2011; Phu et al., 2023). Overall, the distinct molecular resilience of MPPFs is emphasized by the relatively small number of DEGs changing with age, highlighting the unique transcriptional steadiness of these cells over time.

MPPFs maintain youth-like proliferation with age

Cell frequencies and numbers (Figure 1) are dependent on cell cycling and proliferation. Upon discovering that Pbx1, a potent regulator of cell cycle state, is one of the most significantly downregulated genes from HSC to MPP transition, we sought to functionally test for cell cycle differences in the sorted HSC and MPPF cell populations. A previous publication by Ficara et al. tested the effects of conditional inactivation of Pbx1 on HSCs and MPPs and observed increased proliferation by incorporation of bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) in mutant cells (Ficara et al., 2008b). Using these insights as well as our scRNA-seq data, we hypothesized that MPPFs would show higher proliferation than HSCs, regardless of age.

After injecting mice in vivo with 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) and allowing 24 h for incorporation, we sorted HSCs (Lin− cKit+ Sca1+ SLAMF1+ Flk2−) and MPPFs (Lin− cKit+ Sca1+ SLAMF1+ Flk2+) from Y or O donors and measured EdU incorporation compared with controls (Figure 6A). Consistent with previous data, MPPFs were significantly more proliferative than HSCs, regardless of age (Figure 6B) (Passegué et al., 2005). For HSCs, we observed a significant increase in EdU incorporation, reflecting a higher proportion of proliferating cells, with age (Figure 6B). Upon comparing Y and O MPPFs, however, we detected no significant differences in proliferation. However, a trend toward higher proliferation with age was visible, and this is supported by Young et al.’s cell cycle data, which reported subtle differences in proliferation, particularly between 2 and 8 months (K. Young et al., 2016). Given that neither Pbx1 nor other master cell cycle regulators were differentially expressed between Y and O MPPFs, it is perhaps unsurprising that they did not demonstrate detectable differences in proliferation.

ROS levels measured by MitoSox MFI between young and old HSCs showed increased ROS with age (mean ± SEM; 5 independent experiments; Y = 10; O = 7; stats: unpaired Student’s t test, ∗p < 0.5).

Unlike HSCs, MPPFs maintain youthful mitochondrial signature

An important hallmark of hematopoietic aging includes the well-documented decline in mitochondrial capacity of HSCs (Chattopadhyaya et al., 2025; Filippi, 2023; Girotra et al., 2023; Kohli and Passegué, 2014; Mansell et al., 2021; Nakamura-Ishizu et al., 2020; Snoeck, 2017; Totani et al., 2025; Watanuki et al., 2024; K. A. Young et al., 2025). For example, loss of Apoe has been shown to expand bone marrow HSPCs and has also been cited as an important regulator of immunometabolism (Aires et al., 2021; Murphy et al., 2011; Phu et al., 2023; M. Wang et al., 2014). Mechanistically, there is evidence to suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction activates Apoe expression, either directly or indirectly (Gabrielli et al., 2023; Mahley, 2023; Wynne et al., 2023). To this end, we were curious to test how aging of MPPFs, which have lower Apoe expression than HSCs, affects mitochondrial regulation. To address this question, we stained cKit-enriched bone marrow cells with DilC1 to assess mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) (Crola Da Silva et al., 2023; Mansell et al., 2021; Schneider et al., 2019) or with MitoSOX to measure mitochondrial levels of superoxide (Liao et al., 2020; Mansell et al., 2021), a type of reactive oxygen species (ROS), in HSCs (Figure 6C) and MPPFs (Figure 6D). While DilC1 staining revealed a lower MMP of O HSCs vs. Y HSCs, as supported by the literature (Filippi, 2023; Fujino et al., 2022; Mansell et al., 2021; K. Young et al., 2021), this difference was not observed for Y MPPFs vs. O MPPFs.

Upon assessment of ROS levels, we confirmed higher MitoSOX in O HSCs than in Y HSCs, similar to previous reports (Ito et al., 2006; Mohrin et al., 2015; Nakamura-Ishizu et al., 2020), but no significant difference between Y MPPFs and O MPPFs. Intriguingly, several papers have highlighted the role of ROS as a potential cause of the age-related functional decline of HSCs (Fujino et al., 2022; Jang and Sharkis, 2007; Mansell et al., 2021; Yahata et al., 2011). These data point to the stability of both ROS and MMP upon MPPF aging as a mechanism contributing to their maintained functional output with age.

Discussion

In this study, we found that MPPFs and all downstream progenitors tested remain functionally unaffected by aging (Figures 2, 3, 4, and 6). This age resilience is reinforced by HSPC quantification, which emphasized the stable presence of most progenitor pools throughout life (Figure 1). While biological aging has a strong effect on HSC expansion and the emergence of an age-associated, non-canonically derived megakaryopoiesis pathway (Figures 1B–1D), no canonically derived mature blood cell pools expand with age (Manso et al., 2025; Poscablo et al., 2021; 2024). Rather, we observed stable numbers of canonical Plts and GMs and a decline in RBCs, B cells, and T cells with age. The decline in B and T cell output reflects not only the shift from adaptive immunity to innate immunity (leading to expansion in frequency, but not number, of GMs with age) (Poscablo et al., 2021; Tharmapalan and Wagner, 2024) but, possibly, even the natural progression of thymic involution. The reported decline in RBCs with age largely seems to be biologically inconsequential. Indeed, when Y and O Flk2+-derived progenitors were transplanted, they engrafted equally and produced similar numbers of mature blood cells (Figures 2 and 3). Even in old hosts, we observed only small and transient differences in B cell production from MPPFs (Figure 4). In light of this observation, it is possible that O MPPFs are more primed for myeloid production in an old environment; this is supported by data from Young et al. who reported that O LMPPs (Lin−Sca+cKit+Flk2hiSLAM−CD34+) have reduced lymphoid output and higher myeloid output upon transplantation (K. Young et al., 2016). Pietras et al. also reported that the fate of MPPFs can be “reprogrammed” from lymphoid-focused differentiation under stress or regenerative conditions toward a more myeloid-dominant output (Pietras et al., 2015). Overall, considering the exaggerated differences in functional output between Y and O HSCs upon transplantation, we believe this small difference in MPPF output to be relatively trivial within the context of global hematopoietic output.

Transcriptomically, we discovered that Y and O MPPFs are quite similar (Figure 5), especially when considering the vast differences cited between Y and O HSCs (Flohr Svendsen et al., 2021). Given these data, we hypothesize that HSCs are more sensitive to aging than their downstream MPP counterparts due to the critical balance of self-renewal and differentiation that maintains HSC functionality (Yamashita et al., 2025). As a result, MPPs are forced to compensate to maintain hematopoiesis. While we have demonstrated that Flk2 is important for maintaining MPPF numbers, we verified upon Flk2 knockout that MPPF function is maintained (Beaudin et al., 2014). Therefore, while Flk2 appears to play an important role in generating or maintaining MPPF numbers, there is little evidence to support Flk2 as a key driver of MPP cell output.

Beyond steady-state, reconstitution, and transcriptomic data, our cell cycle analyses confirmed that although HSCs demonstrate increased proliferation with age, MPPF proliferation remains unperturbed (Figures 6A and 6B). Interestingly, Solomon et al. recently identified inherent heterogeneity within the MPPF population based on CITE-seq (cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing) data, which correlated with cell cycle state (Solomon et al., 2024). From this, they proposed that a subset of quiescent MPPFs act as a key transition between HSCs and MPPs that buffers hematopoietic cell production at steady state. These MPPs gave rise to other MPP compartments and maintained high reconstitution upon transplantation, which persisted over 2 months. We hypothesize that this MPP stage dominates with respect to cell contribution with age to compensate for the functional decline of HSCs.

To assess the steadiness of MPPFs from yet another angle, we used two assays to measure mitochondrial function. Indeed, while O HSCs demonstrate impaired MMP and higher ROS levels (Figure 6C), O MPPFs maintained their youth-like mitochondrial function (Figure 6D). We propose that the mitochondrial stability of MPPFs plays important mechanistic roles in their maintained functional resilience with age.

Given the age resilience of MPPFs demonstrated in this study and considering that these progenitors are strong contributors to aged hematopoiesis, we posit that the age resilience of these cells is maintained and transferred to downstream myeloid and lymphoid progenitors over time. This key distinction questions the significance of mechanistically understanding HSC aging given that downstream progenitors are largely maintained. If HSC function declines but downstream progenitors can compensate, how important is HSC aging?

Given the data presented here, we propose three possible mechanisms to explain the biological steadiness of the MPP pool with age. In model (1), O HSCs do not contribute to canonical steady-state hematopoiesis, and MPPs are responsible for maintaining stable mature cell production in an aged scenario (Figure 7A). This is supported by other publications (Fanti et al., 2023; Fotopoulou et al., 2025; Munz et al., 2023; Schoedel et al., 2016), one of which utilized a Tie2MCM/+RosaYFP model (Busch et al., 2015) to induce labeling of HSCs and track the label progression over a long term. Despite MPPs being described as a short-lived cell type, especially upon transplantation, the authors demonstrated that MPPs (“ST-HSCs”: LSK CD150−CD48−) in the steady state have a substantial residence time of 330 days, nearly half of the lifespan of a mouse. These data highlighted important differences in the differentiation kinetics between transplantation and steady-state hematopoiesis. The authors found that although HSCs are highly proliferative upon transplantation, their label progression is slow in situ. They concluded that adult hematopoiesis is largely sustained by MPPs throughout life, coining them “key amplifiers” of the system (Busch et al., 2015). Indeed, when Schoedel et al. depleted HSCs, blood cell generation remained uncompromised, likely being maintained by the rapid and robust recovery of progenitors, but not of long term-HSCs ("LT-HSCs": LSK CD48- CD150+ CD34- CD135-) (Schoedel et al., 2016). In model (2), only a small subset of HSCs contribute to the MPP pool with age, likely acting as a protective mechanism to preserve the remaining HSC pool over time (Figure 7B). This hypothesis is supported by Sun et al.; using an M2/HSB/Tn mouse model, they found that less than 5% of LT-HSC tags were present in mature cells, while almost half of the clones found in MyPros and MPPs were shared with mature populations (Sun et al., 2014). Surprisingly, they also reported that less than 5% of LT-HSCs shared tags with MPPs and MyPros, highlighting just how rare diverse HSC contribution occurs during unperturbed adult steady-state hematopoiesis (Sun et al., 2014). Indeed, many publications have provided evidence that the accumulation of somatic mutations is common across nearly all tissues in both humans and mice (Acuna-Hidalgo et al., 2017; De Haan and Lazare, 2018; Jaiswal et al., 2014; Xie et al., 2014; Zink et al., 2017). These mutations can often confer a competitive advantage, particularly when accumulated and propagated by the HSC pool (Jaiswal and Ebert, 2019; Sun et al., 2014; Walsh et al., 2022). Interestingly, this attrition is accompanied by a lack of hematopoietic diversity with age (Beerman, 2019; Ganuza et al., 2019; Li et al., 2025). In model (3), HSCs do contribute to adult hematopoiesis, but they get “reprogrammed” into age-resilient progeny upon being differentiated through the MPP stage (Figure 7C). This age resilience is not observed for HSCs in a transplantation setting, possibly due to their forced proliferation when put into an irradiated, high-demand host environment (Orford and Scadden, 2008). Because of the dire need for mature cells by the host after irradiation, HSCs prioritize rapid differentiation rather than being reprogrammed because age-resilient progeny isn’t the highest priority. From our reported scRNA-seq data, we hypothesized that genes that were differentially expressed from the HSC to MPP transition would be reflective of the genes that are “reprogrammed” (Figures 5 and S4). Gene Ontology analysis revealed that these “reprogrammed” genes are related to cell number homeostasis, immune response regulation, and cell differentiation, including megakaryocyte differentiation and Plt formation (Figure S5; Table S1).

Three proposed mechanisms for age resilience of MPPs and downstream progenitors

(A) MPP-driven model: upon aging, HSCs do not differentiate to contribute to downstream, steady-state hematopoiesis. Rather, MPPFs are the primary driver of mature blood cell production.

(B) Selection model: some HSCs contribute to hematopoiesis with age; thus, hematopoiesis is mainly driven by specific HSC clones.

(C) Reprogramming model: HSCs continue to contribute to steady-state hematopoiesis with age, but their aging properties are reprogrammed upon differentiation into downstream MPPFs.

Until now, research that outlines the output of intermediate progenitors upon transplantation with age is lacking. These data provide valuable insight into how the entire hematopoietic system is affected by aging, rather than focusing on HSCs as the sole contributor to life-long blood contribution. Here, we demonstrate that canonical progenitors maintain age resistance in spite of the functional decline of HSCs, changes in proliferation, accumulation of clonal mutations, and loss of diversity and mitochondrial capacity. While it is known that these hallmarks of HSC aging can lead to malignancies and other abnormalities among the elderly, they often remain undetectable and benign (Buscarlet et al., 2017; Busque et al., 2012; Champion et al., 1997; Jaiswal et al., 2014; Jaiswal and Ebert, 2019; K. Young et al., 2016). Is this because progenitors can compensate in these scenarios? As a result, we question what non-malignant conditions might reflect HSC aging that cannot be compensated for by downstream age-resistant progenitors.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mouse lines

All animals were housed and bred in an AAALAC-accredited vivarium at UC Santa Cruz and maintained under approved IACUC guidelines. All experiments involving animals were IACUC approved. Barrier mouse rooms are temperature controlled and on a 12-hour day/night cycle. Mice had ad libitim access to water and food and daily care assessments. Care staff include a veterinarian, vivarium manager, and staff. Young adult mice were used between 8-12 weeks of age and old mice were 20+ months of age. Old recipient mice in transplantation experiments were 18+ months of age. Mice were randomized based on sex.

Method details

Mouse tissue collection and processing

When bone marrow was collected, mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation as mandated by the approved UCSC IACUC protocols, followed by cervical dislocation. Hind legs and hips, were dissected, cleaned, and immediately placed in staining media (1X PBS [cytiva], 5mM EDTA [Millipore Sigma], and 2% bovine calf serum [BCS, cytiva]) on ice. If cell populations were quantified, a known number of APC Calibrite beads (BD Biosciences) were added immediately prior to processing. Bones were processed by crushing via mortar and in staining media. Following cell release from bones, cells were passed through a 70um filter, and centrifuged for 5 minutes at 300xg. Peripheral blood was collected via nicking the tail vein. A known volume of blood was immediately added to staining media containing a known number of APC Calibrite beads (BD Biosciences) and appropriate antibodies for flow cytometry analysis (see Flow Cytometry methods). Mouse ages as indicated in figures, and young mice are 8-12 weeks old whereas old/aged mice are 20-24 months.

Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell enrichment

Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells were enriched from freshly isolated whole BM via positive magnetic selection. Cells were suspended in 0.5-1.0mL staining media and incubated with 2ul/bone anti-CD117 (cKit) magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec) for 30 minutes on ice, mixing at the 15 minute mark. Positive selection via LS columns (Miltenyi Biotec) was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions. The cKit-enriched cell fraction was then washed in staining media and used immediately.

Whole or cKit-enriched BM

Cells to be stained were aliquoted into 5mL polystyrene tubes. Cells were then blocked for a minimum of 10 minutes on ice in a solution of staining media with 5% each normal mouse and rat serums (Invitrogen). If the experimental design used unconjugated mouse-derived antibodies (and anti-mouse secondary), only those were used first, followed by blocking. The appropriate antibody cocktail was then added directly to cells plus block and incubated for 30 minutes on ice in the dark. Cells were then washed and either stained with streptavidin secondary for 20 minutes on ice in the dark or resuspended for use. Cells were thoroughly washed with staining media between each staining step. Immediately prior to acquisition, 3 drops of 1:5,000 or 1:10,000 diluted propidium iodide (PI, MP Biologicals) was added and cells were mixed.

Transplant bleed schedule

For Figure 2, mice were bled at 5, 14, 21, 32, and 70 days post-transplant. For Figures 3A–3C, mice were bled at 7, 10, 14, 20, 32, 40, 52, and 60 days post-transplant. For Figure 4, mice were bled at 8, 14, 20, 23, 32, 41, 49, and 60 days post-transplant18,82.

Peripheral blood

The primary antibody cocktail was prepared in staining media together with a known number of APC Calibrite beads (BD Biosciences) and placed on ice. A known volume of blood was collected (typically 25 μl) and immediately mixed well in the antibody/bead solution. Cells were then treated as described for BM above. Following completion of staining, ∼10% of the homogenized and stained blood was aliquoted and diluted for whole blood (platelet and erythrocyte) analysis. The remaining sample was subjected to erythrocyte lysis via ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK, 0.15M NH 4 Cl, 1mM KHCO 3, and 0.1mM Na 2 EDTA in diH 2 O, pH 7.2) incubation. Cells were washed, pelleted, and resuspended in 1X ACK at room temperature for 5minutes. Cells were then washed with staining media. Immediately prior to acquisition, 3 drops of PI solution were added followed by cell mixing.

“Total cells” quantification

A known volume of PB was mixed with an antibody solution containing a known quantity of Calibrite APC beads prior to flow cytometry analysis (Rajendiran et al., 2021). For bone marrow, a known quantity of beads was added to each bone marrow cell preparation prior to antibody staining and analysis. The number of beads counted by flow cytometry was used to calculate the number of mature cells per microliter of blood or within each tissue. The distribution of mature hematopoietic cells in a mouse was measured in the blood obtained by tail bleed, and in BM by analysis of two femurs, two tibias and two hips.

Cytometers

FACS was performed via a FACSAria Iiu (BD Biosciences). Cell analysis was similarly performed on the FACSAria or Cytoflex LX (Beckman Coulter). All flow cytometry and FACS data was analyzed via FlowJo (BD Biosciences).

Transplantation reconstitution assays

Reconstitution assays were performed by transplanting double-sorted MPPFs (1,000 per recipient) from young or old FlkSwitch(Benz et al., 2008) whole BM and transplanting into congenic C57BL/6 mice via retro-orbital intravenous injection. We also transplanted double-sorted CMPs (10,000 per recipient), MEPs (50,000 per recipient), and GMPs (50,000 per recipient) from young or old fluorescent BM into C57BL/6 recipients. We isolated single-sorted CLPs (5,000 per recipient) from CD45.2 mice into CD45.1 recipients. Hosts were preconditioned with sub-lethal radiation (∼500 rad) using a Precision Multirad 160 Cabinet X-ray instrument (Precision Instruments).

CITE-SEQ annotation

To identify aged hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell populations, we utilized the publicly available CITE-seq dataset generated by Manso et al. (2025), which profiles surface-protein and transcriptomic features in young and aged murine hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. We defined HSCs based on high expression of CD150 (SLAMF1) and MPPs based on expression of Flt3 (CD135), using the centered and log-normalized CITE-seq protein counts.

DEG analysis

Clusters were manually annotated from the normalized, log-transformed dataset based on distinct differentially expressed marker genes, using the sc.tl.rank_genes_groups function that employs the t-test_overestim_var method in the Scanpy (Wolf et al., 2018) pipeline. Once cells were clustered, differential gene expression was performed by comparing a single cluster against the remainder of the cells. Genes with a higher representation in single clusters by this method were examined. Known lineage-defining genes identified lead to initial cluster identification, with additional known gene profiles examined to confirm.

Pseudobulk DEGs comparison

To conduct pseudobulk differential expression analysis for pairwise comparison, we restructured the single-cell dataset into sample level datasets. We extracted the sample and cell type clusters for old HSC, young MPPF and old MPPF and aggregated single-cell transcript counts at the sample level for each group. For each mouse (biological replicate) and each compartment (HSC or MPP), pseudobulk counts matrices were generated using custom Python code and converted to count matrices suitable for import into DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014). Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed if they met both padj <0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 thresholds.

DilC1 - Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) assay

To evaluate mitochondrial MMP, cells were subjected to sequential cell surface and mitochondrial staining. Following completion of surface marker labeling, cells were washed once in cold SM (PBS + 2% FBS) and subsequently incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes in PBS containing DilC1 (50 nM; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Staining was performed in the presence of verapamil (50 μM; Sigma-Aldrich, #V4629), which was prepared as a 10 mg/mL stock solution in DMSO and diluted in PBS immediately before use. After staining, cells were washed twice with staining media (350 × g, 5 minutes, 4°C) and resuspended in fresh buffer for flow cytometric acquisition. Data were collected using a Cytoflex LX (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo (BD Biosciences).

Mitochondrial superoxide (ROS)

Mitochondrial-specific superoxide production was quantified using the MitoSOX™ Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# M36008). Cells were incubated in PBS containing 5 μM MitoSOX Red for 30 minutes at 37°C in the presence of 50 μM verapamil (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# V4629), prepared in PBS from a 10 mg/mL DMSO stock solution, to minimize dye efflux via multidrug resistance transporters. Following staining, cells were washed once (350 × g, 5 minutes) and resuspended in staining media for immediate flow cytometric acquisition Cytoflex LX (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo (BD Biosciences).

Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) normalization

Within a single experiment, the average of the young values were determined (Yave). Then, the individual young raw values were divided by the average (Y1/Yave). These resulting values were plotted. For the old values, the individual old raw values from the same experiment were divided by the Yave (O1/Yave). The resulting values were plotted and combined to reflect all three experiments.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical tests are indicated in figure legends and performed using GraphPad Prism. For all tests, p-values are indicated numerically or otherwise displayed as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01,∗∗∗p < 0.001, or ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, and “ns” (or lack of designation) indicates non-statistical significance. Paired t-tests, unpaired t-tests, and Spearman correlations are two-tailed. Unless otherwise indicated, graphs of summary statistics indicate the mean ± standard deviation (SEM). The number of individual experiments and samples are outlined in the figure legends.
