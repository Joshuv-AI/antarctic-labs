Abstract
. Let 𝒞 ∙ \mathcal{C}_{\bullet} be a simplicial object in the category Cat \EuR{Cat} of small categories. For a field k k , taking the Grothendieck groups of isomorphism classes of k ​ 𝒞 n k\mathcal{C}_{n} -modules gives rise to a cochain complex, whose cohomology, which we refer to as representation cohomology, is the object studied in this article. In particular, to any small category 𝒞 \mathcal{C} , we associate a simplicial object in Cat \EuR{Cat} , where for each n ≥ 0 n\geq 0 the objects of the level n n category are the simplices of the nerve of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . The basic properties of the resulting representation cohomology of these simplicial objects and certain subobjects are then studied in detail. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
k
be a field. A representation of
\mathcal{C}
over
k
is a functor
M
from
\mathcal{C}
to the category of finite dimensional vector spaces over
k
. If
\mathcal{C}
is a finite category, then a representation of
\mathcal{C}
over
k
can equivalently be thought of as a
k\mathcal{C}
-module, where
k\mathcal{C}
is the category algebra of
\mathcal{C}
over
k

[
12
,
17
]
. The collection of all representations of a category
\mathcal{C}
over
k
forms a category
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, where the morphisms are natural transformations of functors. Representation theory of small categories is a topic of substantial interest in pure and applied algebra
[
6
,
14
,
15
]
. On the applied side, notable examples arise in topological data analysis
[
1
,
7
]
.

By Drozd’s trichotomy theorem
[
5
]
, small categories are divided into three representation types - finite, tame and wild. The division refers to the classification of indecomposable
k\mathcal{C}
-modules. In the finite case, one has only finitely many indecomposable modules, while in other two types there are infinitely many. Tame representation type means that there is a
1
-parameter family of indecomposable
k\mathcal{C}
-modules, and for a category of wild representation type there is an
n
-parameter family of indecomposable modules for arbitrarily large values of
n
. It is well known that in almost all small categories, including finite categories and even finite posets, the representation type is wild. Hence, since classification of indecomposable modules in general is completely out of reach, other methods for studying representations are required. This article offers a new tool which we refer to as
representation cohomology
.

Representation cohomology is defined for any simplicial or semi-simplicial object in the category
\EuR{Cat}
of small categories and functors between them. If
\mathcal{D}_{\bullet}
is such an object and
k
a field, one associates the (split) Grothendieck group
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{D}_{n})
of isomorphism classes of
k\mathcal{D}_{n}
-modules with each category
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. The simplicial operators then turn
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{D}_{\bullet})
into a cosimplicial free abelian group generated in dimension
n
by the indecomposable
k\mathcal{D}_{n}
-modules. Representation cohomology is defined to be the cohomology of the corresponding cochain complex. In this article we start with a small category
\mathcal{C}
to which we associate a family of simplicial and semi-simplicial objects in
\EuR{Cat}
. The reader who is not familiar with the basic theory of simplicial and semi-simplicial objects is referred to
[
11
]
for the necessary background that we are going to assume.

Let
\EuR{Cat}_{+}\subseteq\EuR{Cat}
denote the subcategory whose objects are small categories that admit no non-identity invertible morphisms and whose morphisms are faithful functors (i.e., injective on morphism sets). Let
\EuR{SCat}
and
\EuR{sSCat}
denote the categories of simplicial objects and semi-simplicial objects in
\EuR{Cat}
, respectively. For any small category
\mathcal{C}
, we define a simplicial object
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}\in\EuR{SCat}
, where for
n\geq 0
, the objects of the category
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
are the
n
-simplices of the nerve
|\mathcal{C}|
of
\mathcal{C}
. We shall be interested primarily in certain subobjects and their associated cohomology theory. Our first theorem summarises the basic properties of these constructions.

Theorem A
.

There are functors
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{-}_{\bullet},\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{-}_{\bullet}\colon\EuR{Cat}\to\EuR{SCat}
and
\mathcal{E}^{-}_{\bullet},\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\bullet}\colon\EuR{Cat}_{+}\to\EuR{sSCat}
, together with natural transformations
\widetilde{\iota}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{-}_{\bullet}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{-}_{\bullet}
and
\iota\colon\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\bullet}\to\mathcal{E}^{-}_{\bullet}
. Restricted to
\EuR{Cat}_{+}
, there are also natural transformations
\epsilon\colon\mathcal{E}^{-}_{\bullet}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{-}_{\bullet}
and
\gamma\colon\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\bullet}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{-}_{\bullet}
, such that
\epsilon\circ\iota=\widetilde{\iota}\circ\gamma
. These functors have the following basic properties.

(1)

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\mathcal{C}
for all
\mathcal{C}\in\EuR{Cat}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{D}}_{0}=\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{D}}_{0}=\mathcal{D}
for all
\mathcal{D}\in\EuR{Cat}_{+}
. Furthermore, for all

and any

,
\mathrm{Obj}(\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})=\mathrm{Obj}(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})
and the functor
\widetilde{\iota}_{\mathcal{C}}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
is an inclusion. Similarly,
\mathrm{Obj}(\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{D}}_{n})=\mathrm{Obj}(\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{D}}_{n})
for any
\mathcal{D}\in\EuR{Cat}_{+}
and
n>0
, and the functor
\iota_{\mathcal{D}}\colon\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\to\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
is an inclusion.

(2)

For any
\mathcal{D}\in\EuR{Cat}_{+}
and
n\geq 0
, the objects of
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
are the non-degenerate simplices of
|\mathcal{D}|_{n}
, and the functor

\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
are the non-degenerate simplices of

is an inclusion of a full subcategory.

(3)

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\bullet}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}

is a semi-cosimplicial abelian group generated in dimension

-modules. Furthermore, the resulting cochain complex has the structure of a unital differential graded associative algebra over
\mathbb{Z}
. As a result the associated cohomology has the structure of a graded associative unital ring.

Next, we observe that the information on

Theorem B

The cohomology theory

\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)\cong\EuR{Gr}(k\Gamma),

Theorem C

Let

is the kernel of the first coboundary operator restricted to a certain subgroup of

Next we consider
\EuR{G}^{0}
cohomology in the case where the category in question is a finite poset that is generated by a rooted tree. For any small category
\mathcal{C}
, let
\underline{k}
denote the constant

Theorem E
.

Let
\mathcal{P}

For any
\mathcal{C}\in\EuR{Cat}_{+}
, there is a natural reduction map
\EuR{G}^{*}(\mathcal{C},k)\to H^{*}(|\mathcal{C}|,\mathbb{Z})
induced by the obvious inclusion of simplicial objects

\mathcal{T}_{X}
denote the preorder associated to

\mathcal{G}^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{C}},k)\to H^{*}(|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{C}}|,\mathbb{Z}).

This connection will be explored in a subsequent study.

Computations of
\EuR{E}^{*}
and

Theorem F
.

For
n\geq 1
, let
[n]
denote the poset with objects
0,1,\ldots,n
with the natural order relation. For
n\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{D}_{n}
denote the poset with objects

, and relations

. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a finite quiver such that the underlying undirected graph is a cycle with at least two edges. Then

(1)

\EuR{E}^{0}([n],k)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
\EuR{G}^{0}([n],k)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
and the natural map
\EuR{E}^{0}([n],k)\to\EuR{G}^{0}([n],k)
is split injective.

(2)

\EuR{G}^{i}(\mathcal{D}_{n},k)=0
for all
i>0
, and
\EuR{G}^{0}(\mathcal{D}_{n},k)
is a free abelian group on a set of generators that is in bijective correspondence with the collection of all indecomposable modules

such that

. In particular,
\EuR{G}^{0}(\mathcal{D}_{n},k)
is finitely generated if and only if

.

(3)

Let
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q})
denote the

of
\mathcal{Q}
. Then
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}),k)
is a free abelian group on generators indexed by pairs

Parts

and

and

To get an idea of what higher-dimensional
\EuR{E}^{*}
and
\EuR{G}^{*}
groups look like, we consider a small subcomplex of the defining cochain complex of both theories. Specifically, in each case and every

\dim_{k}(M(x))\leq 1
for all objects

1.
Preliminaries and Examples

In this section we give the four variations of the cohomology theories discussed in this article. We start by defining, for any small category
\mathcal{C}
, a simplicial object
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
in the category
\EuR{Cat}
of small categories. This simplicial object can be thought of as an enveloping object, as all other variations are simplicial subobjects of
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
. We then use these constructions to define the respective representation cohomology theories.

1.1.
The simplicial envelope of a small category

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote by
[n]
the poset, considered as a category, with object set
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
and the natural order as morphisms. Let
\mathcal{C}
be any small category.
Let

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathcal{C}^{[n]},

namely the
functor category from
[n]
to
\mathcal{C}
, with morphisms given by natural transformations, as usual. Let
\Delta
denote the standard simplex category with objects
[n]
,
n\geq 0
, and order-preserving functions as morphisms. Define a simplicial object in
\EuR{Cat}

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}\colon\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}\to\EuR{Cat}

by
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}([n])\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, and for a morphism
\alpha\colon[n]\to[m]
, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}(\alpha)
be the induced functor

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{m}=\mathcal{C}^{[m]}\xrightarrow{\alpha^{*}}\mathcal{C}^{[n]}=\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}.

More explicitly, let
|\mathcal{C}|
denote the nerve of
\mathcal{C}
. Then one can regard the objects of
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
as the
n
-simplices of
|\mathcal{C}|
, namely sequences

(1)

\underline{x}=x_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u_{1}}}{{\rightarrow}}x_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u_{2}}}{{\rightarrow}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u_{n}}}{{\rightarrow}}x_{n}

of objects and morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
. If

is another such sequence, then a morphism

is a commutative diagram in
\mathcal{C}

\textstyle{x_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{0}}
\scriptstyle{u_{1}}
\textstyle{x_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{1}}
\scriptstyle{u_{2}}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{u_{n}}
\textstyle{x_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{n}}
\textstyle{y_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{v_{1}}
\textstyle{y_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{v_{2}}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Objects in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
will be denoted by

to mean a sequence as in (

We will refer to the simplicial object
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
as the

. The following example justifies the terminology. For a small category
\mathcal{C}
, let
\mathcal{C}_{\bullet}
denote the constant simplicial object in

with value
\mathcal{C}
, namely
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\mathcal{C}
for all
n\geq 0
, with all simplicial operators given by the identity functor.

Example 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. Then for each
m\geq 0
, there is an inclusion functor
\iota_{m}\colon\mathcal{C}_{m}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{m}
that takes an object
c\in\mathcal{C}_{m}
to the constant sequence
\mathbf{1_{c}}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}(c=c=\cdots=c)
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{m}
. There are two evaluation functors
\mathrm{ev}_{0},\mathrm{ev}_{m}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{m}\to\mathcal{C}_{m}
given by sending an object
\underline{c}=c_{0}\xrightarrow{}c_{1}\xrightarrow{}\cdots\xrightarrow{}c_{m}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{m}
to
c_{0}
and
c_{m}
, respectively. Clearly
\mathrm{ev}_{0}\circ\iota_{m}=\mathrm{ev}_{m}\circ\iota_{m}=1_{\mathcal{C}_{m}}
. There are natural transformations
\eta_{m}\colon\iota_{m}\circ\mathrm{ev}_{0}\to 1_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{m}}
and
\gamma_{m}\colon 1_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{m}}\to\iota_{m}\circ\mathrm{ev}_{m}
given by taking an object
\underline{c}
, as above, to the obvious morphisms
\mathbf{1_{c_{0}}}\to\underline{c}
and
\underline{c}\to\mathbf{1_{c_{m}}}
, respectively. It follows that
\mathcal{C}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{m}
have homotopy equivalent nerves for each
m
.

Notice that the evaluation functors
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}\to\mathcal{C}_{\bullet}
does not induce morphisms of simplicial objects, because
\mathrm{ev}_{0}
does not respect the face operator
\partial_{0}
and
\mathrm{ev}_{m}
does not respect the face operator
\partial_{m}
. However, the inclusion functors do define a morphism of simplicial objects
\mathcal{C}_{\bullet}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
that is a homotopy equivalence in every dimension. Hence, the inclusions induce a homotopy equivalence of the geometric realisations

\iota_{*}\colon||\mathcal{C}_{\bullet}||\xrightarrow{\simeq}||\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}||,

where for any simplicial object
\mathcal{D}_{\bullet}
in
\EuR{Cat}
the symbol
||\mathcal{D}_{\bullet}||
means first realising
\mathcal{D}_{n}
for all
n
and then taking the geometric realisation of the corresponding simplicial space. Clearly,
||\mathcal{C}_{\bullet}||
(and hence also
||\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}||
) is homotopy equivalent to the geometric realisation of the nerve of
\mathcal{C}
. ∎

Remark 1.2
.

Recall that the singular set of a topological space
X
is the set of all continuous maps from the standard simplex
\Delta^{n}
to
X
. The simplicial object
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
could be considered as a categorification of the singular set construction. However, as we shall see, the resulting cohomology theories behave very differently from the topological analog.

Lying between the category
\mathcal{C}
and its simplicial envelope, certain (semi-)simplicial subobjects will be of interest.

Definition 1.3
.

Consider the following subobjects of
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
.

(1)

For
\mathcal{C}\in\EuR{Cat}
, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
denote the simplicial subobject with the same object sets in each dimension, but where non-identity morphisms
\underline{x}\to\underline{y}
are induced by morphisms
x_{n}\to y_{0}
in
\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

For
\mathcal{C}\in\EuR{Cat}_{+}
, define
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
to be the semi-simplicial subobjects, where in each dimension they are the respective full subcategories whose objects consist of the
non-degenerate simplices of
|\mathcal{C}|
.

The simplicial object
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
will be referred to as the
compositional simplicial envelope of
\mathcal{C}
. The same argument as in Example
1.1
shows that here too the inclusion induces a homotopy equivalence
||\mathcal{C}_{\bullet}||\xrightarrow{\simeq}||\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}||
.

Remark 1.5

1.3.
Connectivity

Lemma 1.11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a connected small category. Then for each
n\geq 0
the categories
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
are connected.

Proof.

Let
\underline{a}=a_{0}\to\cdots\to a_{n}
and
\underline{b}=b_{0}\to\cdots\to b_{n}
be objects in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, and let
\mathbf{1_{a_{n}}}
and
\mathbf{1_{b_{0}}}
be the constant objects on
a_{n}
and
b_{0}
, respectively. Then there are morphisms
\underline{a}\to\mathbf{1_{a_{n}}}
and
\mathbf{1_{b_{0}}}\to\underline{b}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is connected, there is a zig-zag of morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
from
a_{n}
to
b_{0}
, which in turn induces a zig-zag of constant objects in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
from
\mathbf{1_{a_{n}}}
to
\mathbf{1_{b_{0}}}
. Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
has the same objects as
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, the same applies to
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.
∎

By contrast, connectivity of
\mathcal{C}
is not hereditary for
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
if
n>0
. For instance, if
\mathcal{C}
is the category with Hasse diagram given by a
1
to
n
corolla (a digraph with one input vertex and
n
output vertices), where
n>1
, then
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}=\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
consists of
n
isolated objects. If
\mathcal{C}
is a small category and
a_{0}\to\cdots\to a_{n}
is a chain of morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
, where
a_{0}
is minimal and
a_{n}
maximal (
\mathcal{C}(-,a_{0})=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{C}(a_{n},-)=\emptyset
, respectively), then the corresponding object is isolated in
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.

The following, rather specialised, concepts of connectivity will be useful later.

Definition 1.12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. A subset
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\operatorname{Mor}_{\mathcal{C}}
of non-identity morphisms is said to be
line-connected
if the full subcategory of
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
whose objects are the elements of
\mathcal{M}
is connected. The subset
\mathcal{M}
is said to be a
line-component
if it is line-connected and if the subcategory of
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
that it generates is a connected component.

Similarly,
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\operatorname{Mor}_{\mathcal{C}}
is said to be
square-connected
if the full subcategory of
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
whose objects are the elements of
\mathcal{M}
is connected. The subset
\mathcal{M}
is said to be a
square-component
if it is square-connected and if the subcategory of
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
that it generates is a connected component.

Clearly a line-connected set of morphisms is also square-connected, but not vice versa. For instance, if
\mathcal{C}
is the category
[2]
, then
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
is the interval
01\to 02\to 12
, while
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
has two connected components: the interval
01\to 12
and the singleton
02
. On the other hand, the category with objects
0,1,2
and unique morphisms from
0
to
1
and
2
is neither line- nor square-connected.

Definition 1.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be any category and let
\varphi\colon a\to b
be a morphism in
\mathcal{C}
. An irreducible morphism
b\to x
in
\mathcal{C}
, if one exists, is said to be a
successor of
\varphi
. Similarly, an irreducible morphism
y\to a
, if one exists, is called a
predecessor of
\varphi
.

Of course neither successors nor predecessors, if they exist, are necessarily unique, except in some special circumstances. Recall that a category
\mathcal{C}
is called a
direct category
(or a
1
-way category) if it contains no infinite chains of nonidentity morphisms, including cycles of positive length. This implies, in particular, that all endomorphisms in a direct category are identity morphisms.

Lemma 1.14
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finite direct category. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

Every morphism
\varphi\colon x\to y
in
\mathcal{C}
, such that
\mathcal{C}(y,x)\neq\emptyset
for some
y\neq x
, has a unique predecessor.

(2)

The quiver whose edge set consists of all irreducible morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
forms a disjoint union of rooted trees.

Proof.

Clearly,
2
implies
1
. For the converse, notice that for each object
x\in\mathcal{C}
, such that
\mathcal{C}(-,x)\neq\emptyset
, there is a unique object
x_{0}
with
\mathcal{C}(-,x_{0})=\emptyset
, and a unique directed path of irreducible morphisms from
x_{0}
to
x
. Thus, the collection of all irreducible morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
forms a disjoint union of rooted trees.
∎

1.4.
Module categories and the Grothendieck group and ring

Fix a field
k
and let
{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
denote the category of
finite dimensional
vector spaces over
k
. For
\mathcal{C}\in\EuR{Cat}
, a
k\mathcal{C}
-module is a functor
M\colon\mathcal{C}\to{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
. Let
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
denote the category of
k\mathcal{C}
-modules with morphisms given by natural transformations.

Remark 1.15
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a finite category, then there is an isomorphism of categories between
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
and the category of ordinary finite dimensional modules over the category algebra
k\mathcal{C}
, but this isomorphism does not hold in case
\mathcal{C}
is infinite
[
17
]
.

For any additive category
\mathcal{A}
, let
\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{A})
denote the Grothendieck group of isomorphism classes of objects in
\mathcal{A}
. The operation in
\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{A})
is defined by the binary operation in
\mathcal{A}
and the zero object is the zero object of
\mathcal{A}
. We shall be interested specifically in categories of the form
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, which are always abelian. If
\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A},\otimes,I)
is a symmetric monoidal additive category, then the monoidal structure of
\mathcal{A}
induces a product operation on
\EuR{Gr}(\mathcal{A})
with
I
as the unit of the operation. Notice that since
{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}
is a symmetric monoidal category, so is any module category of the form
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, where the monoidal structure is given by tensor product over
k
, and the unit element is the constant module on
\mathcal{C}
with value
k
and identities on all morphisms. Hence
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
has a commutative ring structure induced by the monoidal structure of
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
.

Remark 1.16
.

In this article we divert from conventional notation for the Grothendieck group. The object we denote by by
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
would be denoted in the literature by
\mathbf{K_{0}}(\mathcal{C},{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}})
or
\mathbf{K_{0}}(\EuR{Fun}(\mathcal{C},{\mathrm{Vect}_{k}}))
or similar variations. Also, within our own convention, we denote what should be
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod})
by
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
for short.

Remark 1.17
.

The Grothendieck group as defined above is sometimes referred to in the literature as the
split
Grothendieck group. One may also consider the quotient group of
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})
where the operation is defined by
[M]+[K]=[E]
if there is a short exact sequence

0\to K\to E\to M\to 0

of
k\mathcal{C}
-modules. This explains the use of the word ‘split’. Unless otherwise specified, we shall not consider this quotient.

Definition 1.18
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. Define a homomorphism
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C})\to\mathbb{Z}^{\operatorname{Mor}_{\mathcal{C}}}
as follows. For a module
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
and a morphism
\varphi
in
\mathcal{C}
, let
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](\varphi)=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits(M(\varphi))
. Extend to the full Grothendieck group by additivity. The homomorphism
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits
will be referred to as the
rank invariant
.

Notice that the rank invariant, as defined above, also keeps track of the Hilbert function, namely the function that associates with a module
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
and an object
x\in\mathcal{C}
the dimension
\dim_{k}(M(x))=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](1_{x})
.

Definition 1.19
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small direct category. An
interval in
\mathcal{C}
is a subcategory
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
that is convex and connected, namely such that for any
x,y\in\mathcal{Q}
the following two conditions are satisfied:

(1)

x
and
y
are in the same connected component of
\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

If
z\in\mathcal{C}
and
\alpha\colon x\to z
and
\beta\colon z\to y
are morphisms, such that
\beta\circ\alpha
is a morphism in
\mathcal{Q}
, then
z\in\mathcal{Q}
and
\alpha,\beta
are morphisms in
\mathcal{Q}
.

If
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
is an interval, then the
interval module determined by
\mathcal{Q}
is the module
I_{\mathcal{Q}}\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
that takes the value
k
on each
x\in\mathcal{Q}
, the identity morphism for each morphism in
\mathcal{Q}
and the value
0
for each
z\notin\mathcal{Q}
.

Notice that convexity implies that the concept of an interval module is well defined, because if
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
is an interval and
x\notin\mathcal{Q}
, then no morphism in
\mathcal{Q}
factors through
x
. The following lemma is basic.

Lemma 1.20
.

For any small direct category
\mathcal{C}
, and interval
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, the interval module
I_{\mathcal{Q}}\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is indecomposable.

Proof.

Suppose
I_{\mathcal{Q}}=M\oplus N
. Then, restricting
I_{\mathcal{Q}}
,
M
and
N
to
\mathcal{Q}
, we obtain a decomposition
M|_{\mathcal{Q}}\oplus N|_{\mathcal{Q}}
for the constant module on
\mathcal{Q}
with values
k
. If both
M|_{\mathcal{Q}}
and
N|_{\mathcal{Q}}
are nontrivial, then the objects of
\mathcal{Q}
can be partitioned into two disjoint non-empty sets such that each restricted module obtains the value
k
on one subset and vanishes on the other. But since
I_{\mathcal{Q}}
is constant on
\mathcal{Q}
, this is impossible. Hence we may assume without loss of generality that
M|_{\mathcal{Q}}=I_{\mathcal{Q}}|_{\mathcal{Q}}
and
N|_{\mathcal{Q}}=0
. But since
I_{\mathcal{Q}}
vanishes outside of
\mathcal{Q}
, it follows that
N=0
and so
M=I_{\mathcal{Q}}
.
∎

Definition 1.21
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be direct categories and let
F\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be a functor. Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
be an interval. Let
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})\subseteq\mathcal{C}
denote the subcategory whose objects are all objects
x\in\mathcal{C}
such that
F(x)\in\mathcal{Q}
, and whose morphisms are all morphisms
\varphi
between these objects such that
F(\varphi)
is a morphism in
\mathcal{Q}
.

Lemma 1.22
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be direct categories and let
F\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be a functor. Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
be an interval. Then
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
is a disjoint union of intervals in
\mathcal{C}
. Thus if
I_{\mathcal{Q}}\in k\mathcal{D}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is the interval module defined by
\mathcal{Q}
, then
F^{*}(I_{\mathcal{Q}})\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is isomorphic to a direct sum of the interval modules corresponding to the intervals in
\mathcal{C}
whose disjoint union forms
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
.

Proof.

Notice first that
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
, as defined in Definition
1.21
, is a well-defined subcategory, i.e., it is closed under compositions. If
\varphi,\psi
are morphisms in
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
such that
\varphi\circ\psi
is defined in
\mathcal{C}
, then
F(\varphi)\circ F(\psi)=F(\varphi\circ\psi)
is defined in
\mathcal{Q}
, and so
\varphi\circ\psi
is in
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
be a connected component. We claim that
\mathcal{P}
is convex, namely that if
\varphi\colon x\to y
is a morphism in
\mathcal{P}
and there exists some
z\in\mathcal{C}
and morphisms
\psi^{\prime}\colon x\to z
and
\psi^{\prime\prime}\colon z\to y
such that
\psi^{\prime\prime}\circ\psi^{\prime}=\varphi
, then
z\in\mathcal{P}
as well as the morphisms
\psi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime\prime}
. But in
\mathcal{D}
one has the obvious relation
F(\varphi)=F(\psi^{\prime\prime})\circ F(\psi^{\prime})
. Hence
z\in F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
and so are
\psi^{\prime}
and
\psi^{\prime\prime}
, and they are clearly all in the same connected component as
x,y
and
\varphi
. Thus
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
is a disjoint union of intervals in
\mathcal{C}
as claimed.

Now, let
I_{\mathcal{Q}}\in k\mathcal{D}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be the interval module corresponding to
\mathcal{Q}
. Then

F^{*}(I_{\mathcal{Q}})=I_{\mathcal{Q}}\circ F
, so

F^{*}(I_{\mathcal{Q}})(x)=\begin{cases}k&F(x)\in\mathcal{Q}\\
0&F(x)\notin\mathcal{Q}.\end{cases}

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{P}\subseteq F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
be objects, where
\mathcal{P}
is an interval component of
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
, and assume that there is a morphism
\varphi\colon x\to y
in
\mathcal{P}
. Then
F^{*}(I_{\mathcal{Q}})(x)=F^{*}(I_{\mathcal{Q}})(y)=k
and
F^{*}(I_{\mathcal{Q}})(\varphi)\colon F(x)\to F(y)
is the identity. Thus, the module that receives the value
k
at each object of
\mathcal{P}
and the identity morphism for any morphisms in
\mathcal{P}
and the value
0
elsewhere is the interval module
I_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Since
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
is a disjoint union of intervals, each of which is a connected component in
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
, it follows that
F^{*}(I_{\mathcal{Q}})
is isomorphic to a direct sum of interval modules
I_{\mathcal{P}}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is a connected component of
F^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

.

2.2.
Basic Properties

Clearly, if
F
is an equivalence of semi-simplicial objects in
\EuR{Cat}
, then
F^{*}
is an isomorphism. To be an equivalence of semi-simplicial objects is a stronger condition than requiring
F
to be an equivalence of categories in each dimension, since the inverses must also form a functor of semi-simplicial objects.
However, the next lemma shows that having dimension-wise equivalences suffices in the case of the cohomology theories we consider here.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{C}_{\bullet}\to\mathcal{D}_{\bullet}
be a functor of semi-simplicial objects in
\EuR{Cat}
such that
F_{n}\colon\mathcal{C}_{n}\to\mathcal{D}_{n}
is an equivalence of categories for each
n
. Then the induced map
F^{*}
is an isomorphism. In particular, if
F\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is an equivalence of categories, then
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{F}_{\bullet}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{F}_{\bullet}
,
\mathcal{E}^{F}_{\bullet}
and
\mathcal{G}^{F}_{\bullet}
all induce isomorphisms on the respective cohomology.

Proof.

The second claim follows from the first claim and Corollary
1.6
. Since each
F_{n}
is an equivalence of categories, it follows that
F_{n}^{*}\colon k\mathcal{D}_{n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}\to k\mathcal{C}_{n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is an equivalence of categories and hence induces an isomorphism
F_{n}^{*}\colon\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{D}_{n})\to\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{C}_{n})
. Hence the map
F^{*}
of graded abelian groups induced by
F
is a cochain map that is a dimension-wise isomorphism, and the proof is complete.
∎

An obvious example arises from the category of a group, or more generally, a groupoid.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a groupoid. Then

\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{H},k)\cong\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}(\mathcal{H},k)\cong\bigoplus_{x}\EuR{Gr}(k\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{H}}(x)),

where the sum runs over representatives of the connected components of
\mathcal{H}
, and
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{i}(\mathcal{H},k)=\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{i}(\mathcal{H},k)=0
for all
i>0
.

Proof.

Since every morphism in
\mathcal{H}
is invertible, it is clear that
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{H}}_{\bullet}\cong\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{H}}_{\bullet}
, hence the corresponding cohomology groups coincide. Write
\mathcal{H}
as a disjoint union of connected components
\mathcal{H}=\coprod\mathcal{H}_{x}
, where
x\in\mathcal{H}_{x}
. Then

\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{*}(\mathcal{H},k)\cong\bigoplus\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{x},k).

Furthermore, the inclusion
\mathcal{B}\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{H}}(x)\to\mathcal{H}_{x}
is an equivalence of categories, and the equivalence extends to equivalences
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{B}\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{H}}(x)}_{n}\xrightarrow{\simeq}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{H}_{x}}_{n}
for all
n
. Hence, by Lemma
2.3
,
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{*}(\mathcal{B}\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{H}}(x),k)\cong\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{x},k)
. Thus it suffices to consider the case where
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{B}\Gamma
, for some discrete group
\Gamma
.

For each
n\geq 0
, the objects of the category
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{B}\Gamma}_{n}
are the simplices of the nerve of
\Gamma
, namely ordered
n
-tuples
\underline{x}=[x_{1}|\ldots|x_{n}]
of elements of
\Gamma
(considered as morphisms of the single object of
\Gamma
). A morphism
\underline{x}\to\underline{y}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{B}\Gamma}_{n}
is an
(n+1)
-tuple
(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
of elements of
\Gamma
such that
z_{i}x_{i}=y_{i}z_{i-1}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Since all elements of
\Gamma
are invertible, such a morphism is determined uniquely by each of the
z_{j}
. Hence in particular
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{B}\Gamma}_{n}\cong\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{B}\Gamma}_{n}
for all
n
. Furthermore, since all morphisms in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{B}\Gamma}_{n}
are isomorphisms, it is a connected groupoid, and hence equivalent as such to the automorphism group of the constant object
\underline{e}
, where
e\in\Gamma
is the identity element.

For each
n\geq 0
, let
\mathbf{p}_{n}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{B}\Gamma}_{n}
denote the full subcategory on the basepoint (the image of the degeneracy of the unique object in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{B}\Gamma}_{0}=\mathcal{B}\Gamma
). Then
\mathbf{p}_{n}\cong\mathcal{B}\Gamma
, and by the argument above the inclusion
\mathbf{p}_{n}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{B}\Gamma}_{n}
is an equivalence of categories. Let
\mathbf{p}_{\bullet}
be the simplicial object in
\mathbf{Cat}
where all faces and degeneracies are the identity. Then the inclusion
\mathbf{p}_{\bullet}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{B}\Gamma}_{\bullet}
is a simplicial functor, that is dimension-wise an equivalence of categories. By Lemma
2.3
the inclusion induces an isomorphism on cohomology. It follows that

\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{B}\Gamma,k)\cong\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}(\mathcal{B}\Gamma,k)\cong\EuR{Gr}(k\Gamma),

and
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{i}(\mathcal{B}\Gamma,k)=\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{i}(\mathcal{B}\Gamma,k)=0
for
i>0
.
∎

Notice that Proposition
2.4
is an easy example of Theorem
B
. The next example shows that representation cohomology generalises ordinary cohomology of the nerve of a category.

Computing representation cohomology in general is very difficult. This is primarily due to the fact that the representation type of a small category is typically infinite and almost always wild. This motivates the introduction of filtrations on our constructions.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. A module
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is said to be of
bounded dimension
d
if
\dim_{k}(M(x))\leq d
for each object
x\in\mathcal{C}
.

Obviously, if
M\in k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is an indecomposable module of bounded dimension
d
, then
\delta^{i}[M]
is a direct sum of (not necessarily indecomposable) modules of bounded dimension
d
. Hence the graded subgroup of
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
that is generated by indecomposable modules of bounded dimension
d
is closed under coface (and codegeneracy) operators, and hence forms a cosimplicial abelian subgroup of
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})_{d}\leq\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
. Thus we obtain an
increasing filtration of
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
by subgroups

\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})_{1}\leq\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})_{d}\leq\cdots\leq\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}).

Of course, the same considerations apply to
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
,
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{G}
. The corresponding cohomology groups will be denoted by
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{*}(\mathcal{C},k)_{d}
, and similarly for the other variants.

The subgroup
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})_{d}
is generally still hard to control even for
d=1
. An exception occurs if
\mathcal{C}
is a finite poset whose Hasse diagram is a tree. In this case the number of isomorphism classes of indecomposable modules
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
of bounded dimension
1
is finite. In fact, isomorphism classes of such modules are in bijective correspondence with (connected) subtrees of the Hasse diagram. Notice also that if
\mathcal{C}
is a a poset whose Hasse diagram is a disjoint union of trees, then any module
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
of bounded dimension
1
is a sum of interval modules, but in general one may have infinitely many isomorphism classes of interval modules (See Section
5.3
).

The following corollary is obvious.

Corollary 2.8
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a finite poset whose Hasse diagram is disjoint union of trees, then the groups
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})_{1},\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})_{1},\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})_{1}
and
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})_{1}
are finitely generated for all
n\geq 0
. Hence the corresponding cohomology groups are finitely generated abelian groups.

2.3.
Products

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. Then its nerve
|\mathcal{C}|
is a simplicial set, whose chain complex admits the Alexander-Whitney diagonal approximation, which induces the usual cup product on

defined above admit a (not generally commutative) ring structure that is compatible with that of

For each

as well as on the respective components of any semi-simplicial subgroup of

. In particular, these maps are natural with respect to inclusions of semi-simplicial subgroups, as well as with respect to maps induced by functors

that induce semi-simplicial maps between the corresponding objects. In particular, under the identification of

with the cochain complex

and a pair of non-negative integers

and similarly for the corresponding groups in any semi-simplicial subobject of

\textstyle{a_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{v_{1}}
\textstyle{a_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{v_{2}}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{a_{n-1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{v_{n}}
\textstyle{a_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{v_{n+1}}
\textstyle{a_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{v_{1}}
\textstyle{a_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{v_{2}}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\scriptstyle{v_{n+1}\circ v_{n}}
\textstyle{a_{n+1}}

gives an isomorphism

\partial_{n+1}^{*}M([v_{1}|\cdots|v_{n+1}])\xrightarrow{\cong}\partial_{n}^{*}M([v_{1}|\cdots|v_{n+1}]).

These isomorphisms are clearly natural with respect to morphisms in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, and so they define natural isomorphisms

for all

, so
\delta^{i+1}[M]=\delta^{i}[M]
.
Furthermore, since
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
is a full subcategory of
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, the same argument applies, and so the statement holds for any

as well.

Finally, if
M\in k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
is locally constant, then for each object
[v]\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
, where
a\xrightarrow{v}b
, one has an isomorphism

M(a)=M(\partial_{1}[v])\xrightarrow{M(v)}M(\partial_{0}[v])=M(b).

Define a natural isomorphism of
k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{\mathcal{C}}
-modules
\alpha\colon M\circ\partial_{1}\to M\circ\partial_{0}
by
\alpha_{[v]}=M(v)
. If
[u]
is another object in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
, where
u\colon c\to d
, then a morphism
[v]\to[u]
is determined by a morphism
w\colon b\to c
in
\mathcal{C}
. Hence, there is a commutative square

\textstyle{a\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{v}
\scriptstyle{w\circ v}
\textstyle{b\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{u\circ w}
\textstyle{c\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{u}
\textstyle{d}

and

M(u\circ w)\circ\alpha_{[v]}=\alpha_{[u]}\circ M(w\circ v).

Thus
\alpha
is a natural isomorphism and the proof is complete.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
M,N
be either
k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
-modules or
k\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
-modules. Let
X=[M]-[N]
be an element in the corresponding Grothendieck group. Write
M\cong M_{0}\oplus M_{1}
, and
N\cong N_{0}\oplus N_{1}
, where
M_{0}
and
N_{0}
are maximal locally constant summands in
M
and
N
respectively. Set
X_{i}=[M_{i}]-[N_{i}]
, for
i=0,1
. Then for any
n\geq 0

d^{n}(X)=\begin{cases}d^{n}(X_{1})&n\;\text{even}\\
d^{n}(X_{1})+\delta^{n}(X_{0})&n\;\text{odd}\end{cases}

In particular, if
X
is an
n
-cocycle for
n
odd, then
M_{0}\cong N_{0}
. Furthermore, if
X\in\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0})
is any element, then
d^{0}(X)=d^{0}(X_{1})
.

Proof.

The first part of the corollary and its last statement follow at once from Lemma
3.1
. Thus, let
n
be odd and assume that
X
is a cocycle.
Since
d^{n}([M]-[N])=0
, one has by Lemma
3.1

d^{n}[M]=\delta^{n}([M_{0}])+d^{n}([M_{1}])=\delta^{n}([N_{0}])+d^{n}([N_{1}])=d^{n}[N].

Notice that
d^{n}[M_{1}]
, written in terms of its unique decomposition as a linear combination of indecomposable modules, of cannot contain a locally constant summand, since if it does, then at least one summand of the form
\delta^{i}[M_{1}]
must contain a locally constant summand. But then
[M_{1}]=\sigma^{i}\delta^{i}[M_{1}]
contains a locally constant summand, where
\sigma^{i}
is the
i
-th codegeneracy operator, thus contradicting maximality of
M_{0}
. Similarly,
d^{n}[N_{1}]
does not contain locally constant summands.
It follows that
d^{n}[M_{1}]=d^{n}[N_{1}]
and
\delta^{n}[M_{0}]=\delta^{n}[N_{0}]
and, by applying
\sigma^{n}
to the second equality, that
M_{0}\cong N_{0}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
M\in k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be an indecomposable module. Then for each
0\leq i\leq n
, any representative of the element
\delta^{i}[M]=[\partial_{i}^{*}M]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n+1})
is indecomposable in
k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n+1}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Furthermore, the same claim holds for any indecomposable module
M\in k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
.

Proof.

Fix an integer
0\leq i\leq n+1
and suppose that
\delta^{i}[M]=[P]+[Q]
, where
P,Q\neq 0
in
k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n+1}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
.
Then,

[M]=(\sigma^{i}\circ\delta^{i})[M]=\sigma^{i}[P]+\sigma^{i}[Q]=[s_{i}^{*}P\oplus s_{i}^{*}Q],

where
\sigma^{i}=s_{i}^{*}
denotes the
i
-th codegeneracy operator.
Since
M
is indecomposable, either
s_{i}^{*}P=0
or
s_{i}^{*}Q=0
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
s_{i}^{*}Q=0
. Since
Q\neq 0
, there is a non-zero element
x\in Q([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n+1}])
for some
\underline{v}=[v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n+1}]\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n+1}
, where
v_{i}\colon a_{i-1}\to a_{i}
is a morphism in
\mathcal{C}
. Fix a natural isomorphism

\eta\colon P\oplus Q\to\partial_{i}^{*}M
, and consider the element

(0,x)\in P(\underline{v})\oplus Q(\underline{v}).

Let

y\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\eta_{\underline{v}}(0,x)\in\partial_{i}^{*}M(\underline{v})=\begin{cases}M([v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n+1}])&i=0\\
M([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}])&i=n+1\\
M([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{i+1}v_{i}|\cdots|v_{n+1}])&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

For
0<i<n+1
,

\displaystyle\partial_{i}^{*}M\left([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n+1}]\xrightarrow{(1_{a_{0}},1_{a_{1}},\ldots,1_{a_{i-1}},v_{i+1},1_{a_{i+1}},\ldots,1_{a_{n+1}})}[v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{i+1}v_{i}|1_{a_{i+1}}|\cdots|v_{n+1}]\right)(y)=

\displaystyle M\left([v_{1}|\cdots|v_{i-1}|v_{i+1}v_{i}|\cdots|v_{n+1}]\xrightarrow{(1_{a_{0}},1_{a_{1}},\ldots,1_{a_{i-1}},1_{a_{i+1}},\ldots,1_{a_{n+1}})}[v_{1}|\cdots|v_{i-1}|v_{i+1}v_{i}|\cdots|v_{n+1}]\right)(y)=y\neq 0.

But,

\displaystyle 0\neq\eta^{-1}_{[v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{i+1}v_{i}|1_{a_{i+1}}|\cdots|v_{n+1}]}(y)=

\displaystyle\left(P\oplus Q\right)(1_{a_{0}},1_{a_{1}},\ldots,1_{a_{i-1}},v_{i+1},1_{a_{i+1}},\ldots,1_{a_{n+1}})(0,x)=

\displaystyle\left(0,Q(1_{a_{0}},1_{a_{1}},\ldots 1_{a_{i-1}},v_{i+1},1_{a_{i+1}},\ldots,1_{a_{n+1}})(x)\right).

Thus
Q(1_{a_{0}},1_{a_{1}},\ldots 1_{a_{i-1}},v_{i+1},1_{a_{i+1}},\ldots,1_{a_{n+1}})(x)\neq 0
in

Q([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{i+1}v_{i}|1_{a_{i+1}}|\cdots|v_{n+1}])=s_{i}^{*}Q([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{i+1}v_{i}|\cdots|v_{n+1}])=0

and we obtain a contradiction. The argument is very similar for
i=0
and will be omitted.

Finally, for
i=n+1
, a morphism
[v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}|v_{n+1}]\to[v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}|1_{a_{n}}]
needs not exist, but there is a morphism the other way. Thus
consider the map

\displaystyle\partial_{n+1}^{*}M\left([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}|1_{a_{n}}]\xrightarrow{(1_{a_{0}},1_{a_{1}},\ldots,1_{a_{n}},v_{n+1})}[v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}|v_{n+1}]\right)=

\displaystyle M\left([v_{1}|\cdots|v_{n}]\xrightarrow{(1_{a_{0}},\ldots,1_{a_{n}})}[v_{1}|\cdots|v_{n}]\right).

Since
y\in\partial_{n+1}^{*}M([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n+1}])=\partial_{n+1}^{*}M([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}|1_{a_{n}}])
, and the map above is the identity, we have

\eta_{[v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}|1_{a_{n}}]}^{-1}(y)=(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in(P\oplus Q)([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}|1_{a_{n}}])

By naturality,
(P\oplus Q)(1_{a_{0}},1_{a_{1}},\ldots,1_{a_{n}},v_{n+1})
is an isomorphism, so

(P\oplus Q)(1_{a_{0}},1_{a_{1}},\ldots,1_{a_{n}},v_{n+1})(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})=(0,x).

In particular,
Q(1_{a_{0}},1_{a_{1}},\ldots,1_{a_{n}},v_{n+1})(x^{\prime\prime})=x\neq 0
.
But, once more,
x\in Q([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}|1_{a_{n}}])=s_{n}^{*}Q([v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}])=0
and we obtain a contradiction.
Hence
\delta^{i}M
is indecomposable for all
0\leq i\leq n+1
and the claim follows for
k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
.

It remains to prove the last statement of the lemma. Let
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}=k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{C}_{0}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be an indecomposable module. We must show that
\partial_{i}^{*}M
is indecomposable in
k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{C}_{1}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
for
i=0,1
. But for any morphism
a\xrightarrow{v}b
in
\mathcal{C}
, one has the morphisms
[v]\xrightarrow{(v,1_{b})}[1_{b}]
and
[1_{a}]\xrightarrow{(1_{a},v)}[v]
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
induced by
1_{b}
and
1_{a}
respectively. Thus one has

\partial_{0}^{*}M\left([v]\xrightarrow{(v,1_{b})}[1_{b}]\right)=M(1_{b}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{1}^{*}M\left([1_{a}]\xrightarrow{(1_{a},v)}[v]\right)=M(1_{a}).

The proof then proceeds in exactly the same way as the general case for
k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.
∎

We note that Lemma

does not hold in

, as the following example shows.

.

Let

be the poset

. The category

, and the latter takes the value

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
\mathcal{D}_{\bullet}
denote either
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
or
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
.
Let
M,N\in k\mathcal{D}_{0}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be modules that satisfy
\delta^{0}[M]=\delta^{1}[N]
. Then

and
N
are locally constant.

Proof.

Notice that
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\mathcal{C}
. We prove the statement first with respect to
\delta^{i}
on
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
, and then observe that the same argument works to prove it with respect to
\delta^{i}
on
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
.

Let
\alpha\colon\delta^{0}M\rightarrow\delta^{1}N
be a natural isomorphism, and let
[v]\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
be an object, where

is a morphism in
\mathcal{C}
. Then one has a commutative diagram

\textstyle{\delta^{0}M([1_{a}])\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha_{1_{a}}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\scriptstyle{\delta^{0}M(1_{a},v)}
\textstyle{\delta^{0}M([v])\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha_{v}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\scriptstyle{\delta^{0}M(v,1_{b})}
\textstyle{\delta^{0}M([1_{b}])\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha_{1_{b}}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\delta^{1}N([1_{a}])\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\delta^{1}N(1_{a},v)}
\textstyle{\delta^{1}N([v])\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\delta^{1}N(v,1_{b})}
\textstyle{\delta^{1}N([1_{b}])}

By definition, the same diagram can be written as

\textstyle{M(a)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha_{1_{a}}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\scriptstyle{M(v)}
\textstyle{M(b)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\scriptstyle{\cong}

\scriptstyle{\alpha_{1_{b}}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{N(a)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

It follows that both

and

are isomorphisms, and since

,

and

are both locally constant.

To prove the corresponding statement for

, notice that the morphisms of the form

and

exist in

for each

in

, as observed in the proof of Lemma

.
∎

We are now ready to show that

\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
and
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
are generated by isomorphism classes of locally constant

-modules.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. Then
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
is the free abelian group generated by all isomorphism classes of indecomposable locally constant modules in
k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
. Furthermore, the inclusion
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
induces an isomorphism
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)\to\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
.

Proof.

Let
X=[M]-[N]\in\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0})
be a
0
-cocycle and assume that neither
M
nor
N
is locally constant. Since
X
is a cocycle we have the relation
\delta^{0}[M]+\delta^{1}[N]=\delta^{0}[N]+\delta^{1}[M]
. By Corollary
3.2
, we may assume that neither
M
nor
N
contain any locally constant summand.
Write
M
and
N
as sums of indecomposable modules

[M]=[M_{1}]+\cdots+[M_{m}],\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt[N]=[N_{1}]+\cdots+[N_{n}],

where
M_{1},\ldots,M_{m},N_{1},\ldots,N_{n}
are indecomposable. Then by hypothesis

\delta^{0}[M_{1}]+\cdots+\delta^{0}[M_{m}]+\delta^{1}[N_{1}]+\cdots+\delta^{1}[N_{n}]=\delta^{0}[N_{1}]+\cdots+\delta^{0}[N_{n}]+\delta^{1}[M_{1}]+\cdots+\delta^{1}[M_{m}].

By Lemma
3.3
, all summands on both sides of this equation are classes of indecomposable modules, and since the decomposition is unique, both sides of the equation must have the same indecomposable terms. By Lemma
3.5
and our assumption that
M
and
N
do not contain any locally constant summand, the module
\delta^{0}[M_{1}]\neq\delta^{1}[M_{i}]
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Thus,
\delta^{0}[M_{1}]=\delta^{0}[N_{j}]
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Proceeding inductively, we get
m=n
and

\delta^{0}[M]=\delta^{0}[M_{1}]+\cdots+\delta^{0}[M_{m}]=\delta^{0}[N_{1}]+\cdots+\delta^{0}[N_{n}]=\delta^{0}[N].

Applying the codegeneracy
\sigma^{0}
on both sides, we get
[M]=[N]
.
Hence
X=0
and it follows that the only
0
-cocycles are differences of locally constant modules. This proves the claim for
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
.

Since Corollary
3.2
and Lemmas
3.3
and
3.5
apply also to
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0})
, it follows that
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
is also generated by equivalence classes of locally constant modules, proving the second claim.
∎

As a corollary we obtain an explicit identification of
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small connected category. Then

\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)\cong\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)\cong\EuR{Gr}(k\Gamma),

where
\Gamma=\pi_{1}(|\mathcal{C}|)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{0}
denote the localisation of
\mathcal{C}
at the full set of its morphisms. Thus
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is the groupoid associated with
\mathcal{C}
. By the universal property of localisation, for any locally constant
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, there exists a unique
M_{0}\in k\mathcal{C}_{0}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
that factors
M
. In other words, there is a
1-1
correspondence between isomorphism classes of locally constant
k\mathcal{C}
-modules and isomorphism classes of
k\mathcal{C}_{0}
-modules, and this correspondence is induced by the localisation projection. Thus the homomorphism

\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{C}_{0},k)\to\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)

is an isomorphism.

By Proposition
2.4
,
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{C}_{0},k)\cong\EuR{Gr}(k\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}(x))
for any object

, and by
[
13
, Proposition 1]
,
\pi_{1}(|\mathcal{C}|)\cong\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}(x)
.
∎

3.2.
Higher-dimensional
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{*}
and
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{*}
cohomology

Having dealt with
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}
and
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}
, in this section we consider the higher-dimensional cohomology groups. The main results, Theorems
3.10
and
3.23
are that
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{*}(\mathcal{C},k)
is acyclic for any small connected category
\mathcal{C}
and that
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{n}(\mathcal{C},k)
coincides with

for all small connected and direct categories
\mathcal{C}
and all

.

3.2.1.
Acyclicity of
\,\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{*}

We now show that the higher cohomology groups
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{i}(\mathcal{C},k)
vanish for all small categories
\mathcal{C}
. To prove this some preparation is required.

Let
U^{\bullet}
be a cosimplicial abelian group. Recall the
Moore cochain complex

(N^{\bullet}(U),d^{\bullet})
defined by
N^{0}(U)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}U^{0}
and for
n\geq 1

N^{n}(U)\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\operatorname{coKer}\nolimits\left(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n-1}U^{n-1}\xrightarrow{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\delta^{i}}U^{n}\right),

with
d^{n}=(-1)^{n}\delta^{n+1}\colon N^{n}(U)\to N^{n+1}(U)
. It is well known that the standard cohomology of
U^{\bullet}
is isomorphic to that of
(N^{\bullet}(U),d^{\bullet})
(See for instance
[
9
, Sec. III.2]
, or
[
16
, Sec. 8.4]
).

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
U^{\bullet}
be a cosimplicial abelian group, and let
X,Y\in U^{n}
be elements, where
n\geq 1
. Suppose that
\delta^{i}X=\delta^{j}Y
for some
0\leq i<j\leq n+1
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

If
i=j-1
, then
X=Y
and
X\in\operatorname{Im}(\delta^{i})
.

(2)

If
i<j-1
, then
X\in\operatorname{Im}(\delta^{j-1})
and
Y\in\operatorname{Im}(\delta^{i})
.

Proof.

If
i=j-1
, then applying
\sigma^{i}
to
\delta^{i}X=\delta^{i+1}Y
, we get
X=Y
. Also

X=\sigma^{i+1}\delta^{i+1}X=\sigma^{i+1}\delta^{i}X=\delta^{i}\sigma^{i}X.

This proves Part
1
.
If
i<j-1
, then

X=\sigma^{i}\delta^{i}X=\sigma^{i}\delta^{j}Y=\delta^{j-1}\sigma^{i}Y,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptY=\sigma^{j}\delta^{j}Y=\sigma^{j}\delta^{i}X=\delta^{i}\sigma^{j-1}X.

This proves Part
2
.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. Then the cosimplicial object
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
is acyclic.

Proof.

Let
U^{\bullet}
denote
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
for short, and let
(N^{\bullet}(U),d^{\bullet})
denote the Moore cochain complex associated to
U^{\bullet}
. Let
\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{Z}\vphantom{Z}\in N^{n}(U)
be a cocycle, represented by
Z\in U^{n}
.
Write

Z=\sum_{k=1}^{r}[M_{k}]-\sum_{l=1}^{s}[N_{l}],

where
M_{k},N_{l}\in k\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
are non-zero and indecomposable. We may assume that the sets
\{M_{k}\}_{k=1}^{r}
and
\{N_{l}\}_{l=1}^{s}
have no modules that are isomorphic. We refer to
r+s
as the
decomposition length of
Z
, and this is well defined by our assumption.

Since
d^{n}\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{Z}\vphantom{Z}=\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{\delta^{n+1}Z}\vphantom{\delta^{n+1}Z}=0
by assumption, there exist elements
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
in
N^{n}(U)
, such that
\delta^{n+1}Z=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\delta^{i}Y_{i}
. By Lemma
3.3
,
\delta^{n+1}[M_{1}]
is an indecomposable module. Hence it is represented by an indecomposable summand
T_{1}
of some
Y_{j}
, such that
\delta^{n+1}[M_{1}]=\delta^{i}[T_{1}]
. There are two possibilities: if
i=n
, then, by Lemma
3.8
1
,
[M_{1}]=[T_{1}]\in\operatorname{Im}(\delta^{n})
, while if
i<n
, then, by Lemma
3.8
2
,
[M_{1}]\in\operatorname{Im}(\delta^{i})
and
[T_{1}]\in\operatorname{Im}(\delta^{n})
. In the first case,
\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{[M_{1}]}\vphantom{[M_{1}]}
is a coboundary in
N^{n}(U)
. In the second case, since
i<n
,
\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{[M_{1}]}\vphantom{[M_{1}]}=0
in
N^{n}(U)
. Thus
Z-[M_{1}]
is a cocycle that represents the same cohomology class as
Z
, and is of decomposition length
r+s-1
. By downward induction on the decomposition length of
Z
, each of its indecomposable summands is either a coboundary or
0
in
N^{n}(U)
, and hence
\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{Z}\vphantom{Z}
itself is a coboundary and the proof is complete.
∎

We are now ready to complete the proof of Theorem

, which we restate below.

Theorem 3.10
.

(3)

The cohomology theory

Proof.

Parts

and

are Proposition
3.6
, and Part

3.7

.

We obtain a few immediate corollaries.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let

be a simplicial complex and let
\mathcal{F}X
denote its face poset, considered as a category. Let

\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{F}X,k)\cong\EuR{Gr}(k\Gamma_{X})
. In particular, if

is simply connected then
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{F}X,k)\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Since the geometric realisation of the nerve
|\mathcal{F}X|
is homotopy equivalent to the geometric realisation of

, the claim follows at once from Theorem
3.10
.
∎

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=(V,E)
be a finite quiver and let
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q})
denote its path category. Then
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}),k)\cong\EuR{Gr}(k\Gamma)
, where
\Gamma
is the free group on
N=m+|E|-|V|=m-\chi(\mathcal{Q})
generators, where

\mathcal{Q}
and

Proof.

The fundamental group of the path category of a quiver is the free group of the geometric realisation of the underlying quiver. That, in turn, is well known to be the free group of rank

3.10
.

Example 3.13
.

The following are easy examples of categories for which one obtains an explicit expression for
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}
. Fix a field
k
.

(1)

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a connected tree and let
\mathcal{P}
be the poset it generates. Then
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{P},k)\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

(2)

Let

\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}),k)\cong\EuR{Gr}(k[\mathbb{Z}])
.

(3)

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be an

bipartite quiver. Then
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}),k)\cong\EuR{Gr}(kF_{\mathcal{Q}})
, where

is a free group of rank

.

For a finite poset
\mathcal{P}
and an object

, denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\geq x}
the sub-poset, whose objects are all
y\in\mathcal{P}
such that

. Recall that the modules that take the value

on
\mathcal{P}_{\geq x}
for some

and the value

-modules.

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite poset that is generated by a connected tree or is the face poset of a simply connected simplicial complex, and let

be a locally constant module. Then
M
is isomorphic to a finite direct sum of modules of the form
\underline{k}
. In particular, if
\mathcal{P}
is a poset whose Hasse diagram is a rooted tree, then

is projective.

Proof.

By Part
1
of Example
3.13
,
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{P},k)\cong\mathbb{Z}
and it is generated by locally constant
k\mathcal{P}
-modules. But
\underline{k}
is a locally constant module, and it clearly generates a copy of
\mathbb{Z}
in
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathcal{P},k)
, which implies the general claim. The second statement follows from the fact that the indecomposable projective modules in

are those modules that are constant with value

on an overset

for some

and that vanish outside this set.

3.2.2.
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{*}
cohomology for direct categories

We now describe the
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{*}(\mathcal{C},k)
where
\mathcal{C}
is a
direct category
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{I}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})\leq\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})
denote the subgroup generated by interval modules (recall that by Lemma
1.20
, interval modules are indecomposable). By Lemma
1.22
,
\mathcal{I}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
is a cosimplicial subgroup of
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
.

Objects of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{n}^{\mathcal{C}}
will be written as
\mathbf{v}=[v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}]
for short. If
a\in\mathcal{C}
is any object, then we shall denote by
\mathbf{1_{a}}
the object
[1_{a}|1_{a}|\cdots|1_{a}]\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
. Objects of the form
\mathbf{1_{a}}
will be referred to as
homogeneous objects
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{n}^{\mathcal{C}}
. In particular, every object of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{0}^{\mathcal{C}}
is homogeneous by default. If
\alpha\colon\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{u}
is any morphism in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{n}^{\mathcal{C}}
, with
a
the codomain of
v_{n}
and
b
the domain of
u_{1}
, then
\alpha
factors through both
\mathbf{1_{a}}
and
\mathbf{1_{b}}
. On the other hand, the morphisms
\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{1_{a}}
and
\mathbf{1_{b}}\to\mathbf{u}
are irreducible. It follows that morphisms of these two types are the only irreducible morphisms in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
. For example, in the poset
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{[3]}_{1}
, the obvious morphism
(0,1)\to(2,3)
factors as

(0,1)\to(1,1)\to(1,2)\to(2,2)\to(2,3).

For an arbitrary small category
\mathcal{D}
and a module
M\in k\mathcal{D}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, define the
support of
M
to be the full subcategory
\mathrm{Supp}(M)\subseteq\mathcal{D}
whose objects are
d\in\mathcal{D}
such that
M(d)\neq 0
. For
0\leq i\leq n
, let
\partial_{i}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n-1}
denote the
i
-th face operator, as usual. Then for any
M\in k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n-1}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
,

\mathrm{Supp}(\partial_{i}^{*}M)=\partial_{i}^{-1}(\mathrm{Supp}(M)).

In particular, if
M
is an interval module and
\mathrm{Supp}(\partial_{i}^{*}M)
is connected, then
\partial_{i}^{*}M
is an interval module by Lemma
1.22
. In general,
\mathrm{Supp}(\partial_{i}^{*}M)
need not be connected, as we saw in Example
3.4
.

By definition, the support of an interval module is convex and connected. Below we refer to an interval consisting of a single object
\mathbf{v}
as a
singleton interval
, or simply a
singleton
. Such singletons will be denoted by
\{\mathbf{v}\}
, where
\mathbf{v}
is the defining object.

Lemma 3.15
.

Suppose that
S=\{\mathbf{v}\}
is a singleton in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, and let
i\in\{0,\ldots,n+1\}
.

(1)

If
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{1_{a}}
, for some
a\in\mathcal{C}
, then

\partial_{i}^{-1}(\{\mathbf{1_{a}}\})=\begin{cases}\{\mathbf{1_{a}}\}&1\leq i\leq n\\
[\varphi|1_{a}|\cdots|1_{a}],\;\varphi\in\mathcal{C}(-,a)&i=0\\
[1_{a}|\cdots|1_{a}|\psi],\;\psi\in\mathcal{C}(a,-)&i=n+1.\end{cases}

(2)

If
\mathbf{v}
is a non-homogeneous object, then the preimage
\partial_{i}^{-1}(\{\mathbf{v}\})
is a disjoint union of singleton intervals on non-homogeneous objects.

Proof.

Let
\textbf{v}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
be an object. If
\textbf{v}=\mathbf{1_{a}}
for some
a\in\mathcal{C}
, then for
1\leq i\leq n
, one has
\partial_{i}^{-1}(\{\mathbf{1_{a}}\})=\{\mathbf{1_{a}}\}
. If
i=0
, the objects in
\partial_{0}^{-1}(\{\mathbf{1_{a}}\})
have the form
[\varphi|1_{a}|\cdots|1_{a}]
, where
\varphi
is a morphism in
\mathcal{C}
with codomain
a
. All such objects, where the codomain is not
a
, map by an irreducible morphism to
\mathbf{1_{a}}
and are not comparable among themselves, because
\mathcal{C}
is direct by hypothesis. Similarly, the objects in
\partial_{n+1}^{-1}(\{\mathbf{1_{a}}\})
have the form
[1_{a}|\cdots|1_{a}|\psi]
, where
\psi
is a morphism in
\mathcal{C}
with domain
a
. In this case, there is an irreducible morphism from
\mathbf{1_{a}}
to each of these objects that are not comparable among themselves, again because
\mathcal{C}
is direct. This proves Part
1
.

For Part
2
, notice that if
\mathbf{v}=[v_{1}|v_{2}|\cdots|v_{n}]
is non-homogeneous, then every object in
\partial_{i}^{-1}(\{\mathbf{v}\})
is non-homogeneous and any two such objects are not comparable since
\mathcal{C}
is direct. This completes the proof.
∎

For a poset
\mathcal{P}
and an interval
S
in
\mathcal{P}
we denote by
|S|
the size of a maximal chain of irreducible relations in
S
.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Suppose that
S
is an interval in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
with
|S|>1
. Then, for any
0\leq i\leq n+1
, the preimage
\partial_{i}^{-1}(S)
is a disjoint union of intervals that can be described as follows.

(1)

For any
1\leq i\leq n
, the preimage
\partial_{i}^{-1}(S)
is connected.

For
i=0
or
i=n+1
, the inverse image
\partial_{i}^{-1}(S)
always contains a connected component that includes all homogeneous objects in
\partial_{i}^{-1}(S)
. In addition, let
\mathbf{v}=[v_{1}|\ldots|v_{n}]
be an object in
S
, with
a_{0}
and
a_{n}
the domain of
v_{1}
and the codomain of
v_{n}
, respectively.

(2)

If
\mathbf{1_{a_{0}}}\to\mathbf{v}
is a morphism in
S
, then for each non-identity morphism
\alpha
with codomain
a_{0}
the preimage
\partial_{0}^{-1}(S)
includes the non-homogeneous object
[\alpha|v_{1}\cdots|v_{n}]
.

(3)

If
\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{1_{a_{n}}}
is a morphism in
S
, then for each non-identity morphism
\beta
with domain
a_{n}
the preimage
\partial_{n+1}^{-1}(S)
includes the non-homogeneous object
[v_{1}\cdots|v_{n}|\beta]
.

In particular, all singletons in
\partial_{0}^{-1}(S)
and
\partial_{n+1}^{-1}(S)
are non-homogeneous.

Proof.

By Lemma
1.22
, the preimages of intervals are disjoint unions of intervals. Thus it remains to investigate the nature of those intervals. Consider first preimages of the morphisms that are irreducible in
S
.

Case 1:
Irreducible morphisms of the form
\mathbf{1_{a}}\to\mathbf{v}
.

•

If
1\leq i\leq n+1
, then

\partial_{i}^{-1}(\mathbf{1_{a}}\to\mathbf{v})
consists of the object
\mathbf{1_{a}}
and non-homogeneous objects
\mathbf{u}=[u_{1}|\cdots|u_{n+1}]
, with
a
the domain of
u_{1}
and
\mathbf{u}\in\partial_{i}^{-1}(\mathbf{v})
. This is connected, because there is an irreducible morphism
\mathbf{1_{a}}\to\mathbf{u}
for any
\mathbf{u}
that satisfies the conditions above.

•

If
i=0
, then

\partial_{0}^{-1}(\mathbf{1_{a}}\to\mathbf{v})
consists of the preimage
\partial_{0}^{-1}(\mathbf{1_{a}})
that is obviously connected and the objects
\mathbf{u}\in\partial_{0}^{-1}(\mathbf{v})
, all of which are non-homogeneous. Such objects
\mathbf{u}
, where the domain of
u_{1}
receives no morphisms from
a
, form isolated singleton intervals by Lemma
3.15
, whereas the objects where
a
maps to the domain of
u_{1}
form a connected component.

Case 2:
Irreducible morphisms of the form
\mathbf{u}\to\mathbf{1_{a}}
.

•

If
0\leq i\leq n
, then
\partial_{i}^{-1}(\mathbf{u}\to\mathbf{1_{a}})
consist of the preimage
\partial_{n+1}^{-1}(\mathbf{1_{a}})
that is obviously connected and non-homogeneous objects
\mathbf{w}=[w_{1}|\cdots|w_{n+1}]
, with
a
the codomain of
w_{n+1}
and
\mathbf{w}\in\partial^{-1}(\mathbf{u})
. This is connected because there is an irreducible morphism
\mathbf{w}\to\mathbf{1_{a}}
for each
\mathbf{w}
that satisfies the conditions above.

•

If
i=n+1
, then
\partial_{n+1}^{-1}(\mathbf{u}\to\mathbf{1_{a}})
consists of the object
\mathbf{1_{a}}
and all objects in
\partial_{n+1}^{-1}(\mathbf{u})
, all of which are non-homogeneous. Those objects for which there are no morphisms from the codomain of
u_{n+1}
to
a
form isolated singleton intervals by Lemma
3.15
, while the other objects together with
\mathbf{1_{a}}
form a connected component.

Next, recall that the morphisms of the form
\mathbf{1_{a}}\to\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{1_{a}}
are the only irreducible morphisms in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
. The convexity condition that intervals must satisfy implies that any interval
S
is determined uniquely by the irreducible morphisms it contains. For each
a\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathbf{1_{a}}\in S
, let
N_{a}
denote the neighbourhood of
\mathbf{1_{a}}
in
S
, that is the subcategory that contains
\mathbf{1_{a}}
and all irreducible morphisms into and out of
\mathbf{1_{a}}
in
S
. Then
\partial^{-1}_{i}(N_{a})
is connected for
1\leq i\leq n
. Since
S
is connected, any two homogeneous objects
\mathbf{1_{a}}
and
\mathbf{1_{b}}
in
S
are connected by some zig-zag of morphisms in
S
. Since
\partial_{i}\circ s_{i}=\mathrm{Id}
, applying the degeneracy operator
s_{i}
to that zig-zag gives a zig-zag of morphisms in
\partial^{-1}_{i}(S)
between
\mathbf{1_{a}}\to\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{1_{b}}
in
\partial_{i}^{-1}(S)
. Therefore, all homogeneous objects in
\partial^{-1}_{i}(S)
reside in the same connected component.
Since
\partial^{-1}_{i}(S)
is a union of all inverse images of the form
\partial^{-1}_{i}(N_{a})
, it follows that
\partial^{-1}_{i}(S)
is connected. Part
1
follows.

By the same argument, except using the identity
\partial_{0}\circ s_{0}=\mathrm{Id}
, we see that all homogeneous objects in
\partial^{-1}_{0}(S)
reside in the same connected component. Hence the inverse image under
\partial_{0}
of each object in
S
consists of objects in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n+1}
that are also in that same connected component as the homogeneous objects, and possibly objects that are singleton objects there. This proves Part
2
. Part
3
is proved similarly, using the simplicial identity
\partial_{n+1}\circ s_{n}=\mathrm{Id}
.
∎

Definition 3.18
.

For each integer
n\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})
be the subgroup of
\mathcal{I}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})
generated by non-homogeneous singleton modules.

Lemma 3.19
.

\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
is a cosimplicial subgroup of
\mathcal{I}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.15
2
, the cofaces map non-homogeneous singleton modules to sums of non-homogeneous singleton modules. Note, in particular, that
\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0})=0
, since there are no non-homogeneous singletons in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
. This shows that
\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
is closed under cofaces.

To show closure under degeneracies, consider the non-homogeneous singleton modules
S_{\textbf{v}}
, where
\textbf{v}=[v_{1}|\cdots|v_{n}]\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{n}^{\mathcal{C}}
and

. Then for

,
\sigma^{i}S_{\textbf{v}}
evaluated on
\textbf{w}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{n-1}^{\mathcal{C}}
is given by

(\sigma^{i}S_{\textbf{v}})(\textbf{w})=S_{\mathbf{v}}(s_{i}(\mathbf{w}))=\begin{cases}k&s_{i}(\textbf{w})=\textbf{v}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

But
s_{i}(\textbf{w})=\textbf{v}
if and only if
v_{i+1}
is an identity morphism in
\mathcal{C}
, and
\partial_{i}(\textbf{v})=\textbf{w}
. Therefore,

\sigma^{i}S_{\textbf{v}}=\begin{cases}S_{\partial_{i}(\textbf{v})}&v_{i+1}=\mathrm{Id}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that if
v_{i+1}
is an identity morphism, then
\partial_{i}(\textbf{v})
cannot be homogeneous, because otherwise
v
would be homogeneous.
∎

Definition 3.20

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a direct category. The

with coefficients in
k
is the cosimplicial abelian group

\mathcal{I}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\mathcal{I}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})/\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}).

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
with coefficients in

\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})/\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}).

We denote the image of
X\in\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})
in the reduced Grothendieck group by
X_{0}
, and the cofaces and codegeneracies by
\delta_{0}^{i}
and
\sigma_{0}^{i}
, respectively. Obviously,
\mathcal{I}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
is a subgroup of
\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
.

In particular, if
M
and

are

-modules such that

in
\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})

By Proposition
3.17

\delta_{0}^{i}
in
\mathcal{I}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})
maps a class of an interval module to a class of an interval module. In fact, the same is true for general indecomposable modules in
\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{n}^{\mathcal{C}})
.

Lemma 3.21
.

For any
0\leq i\leq n+1
and an indecomposable module
M\in k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
, the coface
\delta^{i}_{0}([M]_{0})
is the class of an indecomposable module
N\in k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{n+1}^{\mathcal{C}}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
.

Proof.

Suppose that

\delta_{0}^{i}([M]_{0})=[\partial^{*}_{i}(M)]_{0}=[P\oplus Q]_{0}=[P]_{0}+[Q]_{0}

for some
k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n+1}
-modules
P
and
Q
.

Suppose first that
i\neq n+1
. Then

[M]_{0}=(\sigma_{0}^{i}\circ\delta_{0}^{i})([M]_{0})=\sigma_{0}^{i}([P]_{0})+\sigma_{0}^{i}([Q]_{0}).

Since
M
is indecomposable, either
\sigma_{0}^{i}([P]_{0})=0
or
\sigma_{0}^{i}([Q]_{0})=0
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\sigma_{0}^{i}([Q]_{0})=0
. If
Q\notin\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n+1})
then
\mathrm{Supp}(Q)
contains at least one homogeneous object
\mathbf{1_{a}}
for some object
a\in\mathcal{C}
(since every morphism that is not the identity on a non-homogeneous object factors through a homogeneous object). Now,

\sigma^{i}Q(\mathbf{1_{a}})=Q(s_{i}(\mathbf{1_{a}}))=Q(\mathbf{1_{a}})\neq 0.

Hence
\sigma_{0}^{i}([Q]_{0})=[s^{*}_{i}Q]_{0}\neq 0
, which is a contradiction to our assumption. Therefore
Q\in\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})
, so
[Q]_{0}=0
. Finally, for
i=n+1
, use the cosimplicial identity
\sigma^{n}\circ\delta^{n+1}=\mathrm{Id}
, and the claim follows similarly.
∎

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a direct category. Then the reduced cosimplicial Grothendieck group
\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})
and the reduced interval module group
\mathcal{I}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})
are both acyclic.

Proof.

Acyclicity of both objects is proven very similarly to the proof of Proposition
3.9
, using Lemmas
3.8
and
3.21
, where the latter is analogous to Lemma
3.3
.

Let
U^{\bullet}
denote
\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
for short, and let
(N^{\bullet}(U),d^{\bullet})
denote the Moore cochain complex associated to
U^{\bullet}
. We denote the cofaces in the Moore cochain complex
N^{\bullet}(U)
by
\delta_{N}^{i}
, and the cofaces in
U^{\bullet}
by
\delta_{U}^{i}
. For
X\in\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})
, we denote its image in
U^{\bullet}
by
X^{U}
, and the image of
X^{U}
in the Moore cochains
N^{\bullet}(U)
by
X^{N}
.

Let
X\in\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{n}^{\mathcal{C}})
represent a cocycle
X^{N}\in N^{n}(U)
.
Write

X=\sum_{k=1}^{r}[M_{k}]-\sum_{l=1}^{s}[N_{l}],

where
M_{k},N_{l}\in k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
are non-zero and indecomposable. We may assume that the sets
\{M_{k}\}_{k=1}^{r}
and
\{N_{l}\}_{l=1}^{s}
have neither modules that are isomorphic, nor non-homogeneous singleton modules. Hence it makes sense to consider
r+s
as the
decomposition length
of
X
.

Since, by assumption,
d^{n}X^{N}=(\delta_{U}^{n+1}X^{U})^{N}=(\delta^{n+1}X)^{N}=0
in the Moore cochains, there exist elements
\{Y_{i}=[P_{i}]-[Q_{i}]\}_{i=0}^{n}
in
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{n}^{\mathcal{C}})
such that

(\delta^{n+1}X)^{U}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}(\delta^{n+1}[M_{k}])^{U}-\sum_{l=1}^{s}(\delta^{n+1}[N_{l}])^{U}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(\delta^{i}Y_{i})^{U}.

By Lemma
3.21
,
(\delta^{n+1}[M_{1}])^{U}
is represented by some indecomposable
k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{n+1}^{\mathcal{C}}
-module. Hence, for some
0\leq i\leq n
, there is an indecomposable summand
T_{1}
of
P_{i}
, such that
(\delta^{n+1}[M_{1}])^{U}=(\delta^{i}[T_{1}])^{U}
. There are two possibilities: if
i=n
, then, by Lemma
3.8
1
,
[M_{1}]^{U}=[T_{1}]^{U}\in\operatorname{Im}(\delta^{n}_{U})
, while if
i<n
, then, by Lemma
3.8
2
,
[M_{1}]^{U}\in\operatorname{Im}(\delta^{i}_{U})
and
[T_{1}]^{U}\in\operatorname{Im}(\delta^{n}_{U})
. In the first case,
[M_{1}]^{U}
is a coboundary in
N^{n}(U)
. In the second case, since
i<n
,
[M_{1}]^{N}=0
in
N^{n}(U)
. Thus
(X-[M_{1}])^{U}
is a cocycle that represents the same cohomology class as
X^{U}
, and
X-[M_{1}]
is of decomposition length
r+s-1
. By downward induction on the decomposition length of
X
, each of its indecomposable summands is either a coboundary or
0
in
N^{n}(U)
, and hence
X^{U}
itself is a coboundary and the proof for
\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})
is complete.

The proof for
\mathcal{I}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})
uses the same argument as above, but with interval modules (which are inherently indecomposable) instead of general indecomposable modules.
∎

For a cochain complex
C^{*}
of a cosimplicial abelian group, let
C_{\mathrm{red}}^{*}
denote the subcomplex of
C^{*}
given by the dimension-wise intersection of kernels of all codegeneracy operators. It is a basic fact that the inclusion
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{red}}^{*}\to\mathcal{C}^{*}
induces an isomorphism in cohomology. We are now ready to prove Theorem
C
, which we restate in more detail as follows.

Theorem 3.23
.

For any direct category
\mathcal{C}
the following statements hold.

(1)

The inclusions
\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})\to\mathcal{I}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})\to\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
induce isomorphisms

for all
n\geq 2
.

(2)

For
n\leq 1
one has

\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)\cong H^{0}(\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}))\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{1}(\mathcal{C},k)\cong\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits\left(\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1})\xrightarrow{d^{1}}\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{2})\right).

(3)

The map

\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{n}(\mathcal{C},k)\to H^{n}(|\mathcal{C}|,\mathbb{Z})

induced by natural inclusion of simplicial objects
|\mathcal{C}|_{\bullet}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
is an epimorphism for
n=1
and an isomorphism for
n>1
.

In fact the inclusion
\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})_{\mathrm{red}}\to C^{*}(|\mathcal{C}|,\mathbb{Z})
induces an isomorphism in cohomology for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

The short exact sequence of cosimplicial groups

(6)

0\rightarrow\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})\rightarrow\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})\rightarrow\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})\rightarrow 0

induces a long exact sequence in cohomology.
Since, by Proposition
3.22
,
H^{n}(\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}}))=0
for
n\geq 1
, the maps

H^{n}(\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}))\xrightarrow{\cong}H^{n}(\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}}))=\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{n}(\mathcal{C},k)

are isomorphisms for all
n\geq 2
.
Similarly, Proposition
3.22
and the short exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})\rightarrow\mathcal{I}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}})\rightarrow 0

imply that the maps
H^{n}(\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}}))\to H^{n}(\mathcal{I}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{C}}))
are isomorphisms for all
n\geq 2
.
Part
1
follows.

Next, notice that
\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{0}^{\mathcal{C}})=\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{0}^{\mathcal{C}})
, since
\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{0}^{\mathcal{C}})=0
. Clearly every linear combination of isomorphism classes of locally constant modules is an element of
H^{0}(\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}))
, as well as of
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
. Hence the natural map
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)\to H^{0}(\EuR{Gr}_{0}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}))
, which by construction is a monomorphism, is also an epimorphism and hence an isomorphism. It follows that the first connecting homomorphism in the long exact cohomology sequence associated to the short exact sequence (
6
) is trivial. Thus,

\EuR{G}^{1}(\mathcal{C},k)\cong H^{1}(\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}))=\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits\left(\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1})\xrightarrow{d^{1}}\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{2})\right),

where the second equality holds because
\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{0}^{\mathcal{C}})=0
.
Part
2
follows.

To prove the last statement, notice first that if
\mathbf{v}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
, then

\sigma^{i}S_{\textbf{v}}=\begin{cases}S_{\partial_{i}(\textbf{v})}&v_{i+1}=\mathrm{Id}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(See the proof of Lemma
3.19
). Hence for
n\geq 1
, if

is a singleton module where
\mathbf{v}
contains an identity morphism, then
S_{\mathbf{v}}
is not in the kernel of at least one codegeneracy, and hence not in the intersection of all kernels. Clearly if
\sigma^{i}S_{\mathbf{v}}=\sigma^{i}S_{\mathbf{u}}
then
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}
, because in that case there is some
\mathbf{w}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n-1}
such that
\mathbf{v}=s_{i}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{u}
. Thus
\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})_{\mathrm{red}}
, for
n\geq 1
, is freely generated by singleton modules
S_{\mathbf{v}}
, where
\mathbf{v}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
is non-degenerate. Since
\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0})=0
, the cochain map

\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})_{\mathrm{red}}\to C^{*}(|\mathcal{C}|,\mathbb{Z})

that takes the singleton module

on a non-degenerate simplex
\mathbf{v}
to the characteristic function
\chi_{\mathbf{v}}
induces an isomorphism in cohomology in all dimensions
n\geq 2
and an epimorphism for
n=1
. Notice that this map factors through the inclusion
\mathcal{I}^{s}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})_{\mathrm{red}}\to\EuR{Gr}(k\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet})
followed by the projection to
C^{*}(|\mathcal{C}|,\mathbb{Z})
. This proves Part
3
.
∎

4.
\EuR{E}^{*}
and
\EuR{G}^{*}
cohomology

We now turn to general computations of
\EuR{E}^{*}
and
\EuR{G}^{*}
. We assume throughout that all categories discussed in this section have no non-identity invertible morphisms, so that these theories are well defined. Obtaining general computational results about
\EuR{E}^{*}
and
\EuR{G}^{*}
is more difficult even in dimension
0
, since the restriction to non-degenerate objects and as such concentrating on the meaningful morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
gives a much more involved structure, and in particular higher cohomology. The main results in this section are the implication for a module
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
to represent a
0
-cocycle, and bounds on the degrees in which positive-dimensional cohomology can appear in the case of a category with finite composition length.

4.1.
The groups
\EuR{E}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
and
\EuR{G}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)

Recall that
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\mathcal{C}
. For
n>0
, the object sets
\mathrm{Obj}(\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})=\mathrm{Obj}(\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n})
consist of the
non-degenerate
simplices of
|\mathcal{C}|
. The categories
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
are full subcategories of
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
, respectively.

The following proposition shows that
\EuR{E}^{0}
behaves similarly to
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}
and
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}
on classes of genuine (as opposed to virtual) modules, with a slight variation.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be such that
[M]
represents a class in
\EuR{E}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
. Then
M
is locally constant on each square-component of
\mathcal{C}
that contains at least two different non-identity morphisms.

Proof.

Fix a natural isomorphism
\eta\colon\partial_{1}^{*}M\to\partial_{0}^{*}M
.
Let

and

be any two distinct (non-identity) morphisms in the same square-component of
\mathcal{C}
. Assume first that there is a morphism in
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\mathcal{C}}
from

to
v
. Thus there are morphisms

and

in
\mathcal{C}
, such that
t\circ u=v\circ s
.

Case 1:
s
and
t
are non-identity morphisms.
In this case
[s]
,
[t]
and
[t\circ u]
are objects in
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\mathcal{C}}
, and we have morphisms
(1_{x},v)\colon[s]\to[t\circ u]

\textstyle{M(x)=\partial_{1}^{*}M([s])\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\scriptstyle{\eta_{[s]}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\partial_{0}^{*}M([s])=M(z)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{M(v)=\partial_{0}^{*}M(1_{x},v)}

\scriptstyle{\eta_{[t\circ u]}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\partial_{0}^{*}M([t\circ u])=M(w)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{M(1_{w})=\partial_{0}^{*}M(u,1_{w})}

\textstyle{\partial_{0}^{*}M([t])=M(w)}

It follows that

are both isomorphisms.

Case 2:

and

[u]
,
[t]
, and
[t\circ u]

\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\mathcal{C}}

\textstyle{M(x)=\partial_{1}^{*}M([u])\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\partial_{1}^{*}M(1_{x},t)=M(1_{x})}
\scriptstyle{\eta_{[u]}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\partial_{0}^{*}M([u])=M(y)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\partial_{0}^{*}M(1_{x},t)=M(t)}
\textstyle{M(x)=\partial_{1}^{*}M([t\circ u])\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\partial_{1}^{*}M(u,1_{w})=M(u)}
\scriptstyle{\eta_{[t\circ u]}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\partial_{0}^{*}M([t\circ u])=M(w)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\partial_{0}^{*}M(u,1_{w})=M(1_{w})}

are isomorphisms, and hence so is

. But

This is similar to the previous cases and is left for the reader.

. This means that there exist objects

, and a zig-zag of morphisms

Notice that
\EuR{E}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
generally contains classes of virtual modules that are not locally constant, as Proposition
5.2
demonstrates.

Next we consider
\EuR{G}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
. As one could expect, the implications for a module

to be a
0
-cocycle are considerably weaker, but one still gains information about such modules.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
M\in k\mathcal{C}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be such that
[M]
represents a class in
\EuR{G}^{0}(\mathcal{C},k)
. Then, for any pair of morphisms

and
v\colon c\to d
that are in the same line-component of
\mathcal{C}
, there are isomorphisms
\alpha\colon M(a)\to M(c)
and
\beta\colon M(b)\to M(d)
such that
\beta\circ M(u)=M(v)\circ\alpha
.

Proof.

Since the morphisms
u
and
v
are in the same line-component of
\mathcal{C}
by hypothesis, the corresponding objects
[u]
and
[v]
are in the same connected component of
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
. Assume first that there is a morphism
(\varphi,\psi)\colon[u]\to[v]
. By definition of the object
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
, there is a morphism

, such that
\varphi=w\circ u
and
\psi=v\circ w
. Let
\eta\colon\partial^{*}_{1}M\to\partial_{0}^{*}M
be a natural isomorphism. If

, then set
\alpha=\eta_{[u]}
and
\beta=\eta_{[v]}
. Any other morphism
(\varphi,\psi)\colon[u]\to[v]
, including the case where

is a non-invertible endomorphism of

, can be factored as
(\varphi,\psi)=(w,v)\circ(u,w)
, and one has the relations:

M(w)\circ\eta_{[u]}=\eta_{[w]}\circ M(u),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptM(v)\circ\eta_{[w]}=\eta_{[v]}\circ M(w).

Thus, setting
\alpha\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\eta_{[w]}\circ\eta_{[u]}
and
\beta\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\eta_{[v]}\circ\eta_{[w]}
, we obtain the desired relation
\beta\circ M(u)=M(v)\circ\alpha
.

In general, since the morphisms
u
and
v
are in the same line-component of
\mathcal{C}
, there is a zig-zag of morphisms in
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
connecting
[u]
to
[v]
. The relation of the form above holds for each morphism in the zig-zag. Hence the claim follows by induction on the length of the chain connecting
[u]
and
[v]
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Notice that Lemma

makes no claim of uniqueness of the isomorphisms

and

[

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}

and

.

\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{N(c)\oplus M(b)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{(N\circ\partial_{0}\oplus M\circ\partial_{1})[v]}

The top isomorphism implies at once that

\operatorname{rk}\nolimits X(1_{a})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](1_{a})-\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[N](1_{a})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](1_{b})-\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[N](1_{b})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits X(1_{b}).

Similarly, the bottom isomorphism implies that
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits X(1_{b})=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits X(1_{c})
. The same inductive argument as in the proof of Lemma

now shows that

is constant on

for all objects

\mathcal{C}
.

Commutativity of the diagram also shows that

\operatorname{rk}\nolimits X(u)=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](u)-\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[N](u)=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[M](v)-\operatorname{rk}\nolimits[N](v)=\operatorname{rk}\nolimits X(v).

Again, by induction and using line-connectivity, one shows that
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits X(w)
is constant for all morphisms

that are in the same line-component of
\mathcal{C}
, and the proof is thus complete.
∎

Restricting to posets whose Hasse diagrams are rooted trees, the idea of the proof of Lemma

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a poset with composition length at least
2
, whose Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a rooted (not necessarily finite) tree with root
r
. Let
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be a module such that
[M]\in\EuR{G}^{0}(\mathcal{P},k)

(1)

For any two relations

in
\mathcal{P}
, where
a\neq r

\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(a<x)=\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(a<y).

(2)

If
a<b
and
c<d
are in the same line-component of
\mathcal{P}
and
a,c\neq r
, then
\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(a<b)\cong\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(c<d)
.

(3)

If, in addition, the root
r
of
\mathcal{P}
has a unique successor
s
, then

\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(r<s)=\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(r<s^{\prime})

for any
s^{\prime}>s>r
, such that
s^{\prime}
is not maximal.

Proof.

Fix a natural isomorphism
\eta\colon\partial^{*}_{1}M\to\partial^{*}_{0}M
. Since
a\neq r
, there is some object
z\in\mathcal{P}
such that
z<a
. Then there are obvious morphisms
[z<a]\to[a<x]
and
[z<a]\to[a<y]
, so

M(a<x)\circ\eta_{[z<a]}=\eta_{[a<x]}\circ M(z<a),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptM(a<y)\circ\eta_{[z<a]}=\eta_{[a<y]}\circ M(z<a).

It follows that

\eta_{[a<x]}^{-1}\circ M(a<x)\circ\eta_{[z<a]}=\eta_{[a<y]}^{-1}\circ M(a<y)\circ\eta_{[z<a]},

and since
\eta_{[z<a]}
is an isomorphism,

\eta_{[a<x]}^{-1}\circ M(a<x)=\eta_{[a<y]}^{-1}\circ M(a<y).

Part
1
follows.

If
a<b
and
c<d
are in the same line-component of
\mathcal{P}
, there is some
x<y
in
\mathcal{P}
such that

and

. Since

is not minimal, Part

implies that
\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(y<a)=\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(y<c)
. The relation

. Similarly, the relation

. Putting these observations together, we see that

\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(a<b)=\eta_{[y<a]}\left(\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(y<a)\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(c<d)=\eta_{[y<c]}\left(\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M(y<a)\right).

.

Since

in
\mathcal{P}

Recall that a module

Definition 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{P}

By Lemma

Definition 4.7

Let

with the obvious action on morphisms.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a poset of composition length at least
3
, whose Hasse diagram
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a rooted (not necessarily finite) tree whose root has a unique successor. Let
M\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
be a module such that
[M]\in\EuR{G}^{0}(\mathcal{P},k)

4.7

\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}\in k\mathcal{P}\mbox{-}\EuR{mod}
denote the quotient module

\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}
is a locally constant module. In particular,
\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}
is projective.

Proof.

Fix a natural isomorphism
\eta\colon\partial_{1}^{*}M\to\partial_{0}^{*}M
as before. Since
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a rooted tree whose root has a unique successor, the full subcategory of
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
whose objects are all non-maximal relations in
\mathcal{P}
is connected, and
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
has an isolated singleton component for each maximal relation in
\mathcal{P}
(i.e. for each leaf of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
). Let
a<b
be any non-maximal relation in
\mathcal{P}
. Assume first that
b
is not maximal,
so that
M
and
M^{\prime}
coincide on
a
and
b
and the relation between them, and there is some

in
\mathcal{P}
. The relation
a<b<v
implies that

M^{\prime}(b<v)\circ\eta_{[a<b]}=\eta_{[b<v]}\circ M^{\prime}(a<b)
.
It follows that

\eta_{[a<b]}(\mathcal{K}_{M^{\prime}}(a))=\eta_{[a<b]}(\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M^{\prime}(a<b))=\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits M^{\prime}(b<v)=\mathcal{K}_{M^{\prime}}(b),

where the first and third equality hold by Definition

is a natural isomorphism.
Thus

induces an isomorphism
\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}(a)\to\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}(b)
. Also, the projection
\pi_{-}\colon M^{\prime}(-)\to\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}(-)
is clearly a natural transformation. Next, consider the diagram,

\textstyle{\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}(a)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{M^{\prime}(a)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{a}}
\scriptstyle{M^{\prime}(a<v)}
\scriptstyle{M^{\prime}(a<b)}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{a}}
\textstyle{\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}(a)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}(a<b)}
\scriptstyle{\varphi^{v}_{a}}
\textstyle{M^{\prime}(b)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{b}}
\scriptstyle{M^{\prime}(b<v)}
\textstyle{M^{\prime}(v)}
\textstyle{\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}(b)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varphi^{v}_{b}}

All triangles commute by construction, except possibly the relation

Next, assume that

\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}

Theorem

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{P}

Write

\overset{{\mskip 1.0mu\leaders{\hrule height=0.4pt}{\hfill}\mskip 1.0mu}}{M}\vphantom{M}

4.2.
Higher-dimensional
\EuR{E}^{*}
and
\EuR{G}^{*}
groups

We observe here that for categories with finite composition length, the higher-dimensional
\EuR{E}^{*}
and
\EuR{G}^{*}
cohomology is only interesting in a finite range of dimensions. Clearly, if
N
is the maximal composition length in
\mathcal{C}
, then
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}=\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}=\emptyset
for
n>N
. Hence in this case
\EuR{G}^{n}(\mathcal{C},k)=\EuR{E}^{n}(\mathcal{C},k)=0
for all
n>N
. However, a bit more can be said in both cases. Recall that a morphism
\varphi
in a category
\mathcal{C}
is said to be irreducible if for any expression of the form
\varphi=\alpha\circ\beta
, either
\alpha
or
\beta
is an identity morphism.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finite direct category. Assume that
N
is the composition length of
\mathcal{C}
, for some positive integer
N
. Then
\EuR{E}^{n}(\mathcal{C},k)=\EuR{G}^{n}(\mathcal{C},k)=0
for all
n>N
. Furthermore, the map

\iota^{*}\colon\EuR{G}^{n}(\mathcal{C},k)\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathscr{R}^{n}(\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C},\emptyset},k)\cong H^{n}(|\mathcal{C}|,\mathbb{Z})

induced by the inclusion
\iota\colon\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C},\emptyset}_{\bullet}\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{\bullet}
is an isomorphism for all
n\geq n_{0}\overset{\text{{def}}}{=}\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor+1
and an epimorphism for
1\leq n\leq\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
.

Proof.

First, it is clear that if
N
is the maximal composition length in
\mathcal{C}
, then
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}=\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}=\emptyset
for
n>N
. The first statement follows.

By Theorem
3.23
, the corresponding map

\widetilde{\iota^{*}}\colon\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{n}(\mathcal{C},k)\to H^{n}(|\mathcal{C}|,\mathbb{Z})

is an epimorphism for all
n\geq 1
. Hence, by commutativity of Diagram (
3
),
\iota^{*}
is an epimorphism for all
n\geq 1
. Next, fix some
n>\lfloor N/2\rfloor
. Then any object
[u_{1}|u_{2}|\ldots|u_{n}]\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
is isolated. This is because the existence of any morphism into or out of such an object implies that
\mathcal{C}
contains a chain of irreducible morphisms of length at least
2n>N
, which contradicts maximality of
N
. Hence, for such
n
, one has
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\cong\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C},\emptyset}_{n}
. Notice that
n_{0}
is the minimal integer for which
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
is a discrete category for every
n\geq n_{0}
and that the isomorphism
\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C}}_{n_{0}})\to\EuR{Gr}(k\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{C},\emptyset}_{n_{0}})
maps the subgroup of coboundaries in the domain to the corresponding subgroup of coboundaries in the codomain. The second statement follows.
∎

5.
Some Computational Examples

In this section we present cohomology computations for some simple families of examples. Computing positive-dimensional cohomology is very challenging in general, but we are able to do it in some cases, particularly when we can show that higher-dimensional cohomology vanishes. In some cases we consider the representation cohomology generated by modules of bounded dimension
1
(See Definition
2.7
and the following discussion).

5.1.
The Poset
[n]

The category
[n]
is, of course, the simplest example, but as we shall observe, it presents some interesting behaviour in dimension
0
, and is actually highly nontrivial for
\EuR{E}^{*}
and
\EuR{G}^{*}
theory, as our machine calculations demonstrate. We start with
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{*}
and
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{*}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
n\geq 2
and a field
k
,

\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{i}([n],k)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&i=0\\
0&i>0\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{i}([n],k)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&i=0\\
\mathbb{Z}^{n}&i=1\\
0&i>1\end{cases}.

Proof.

By Theorem
B
,
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}([n],k)\cong\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{0}([n],k)\cong\EuR{Gr}(k)\cong\mathbb{Z}
is generated by the class of the constant module
\underline{k}
, and
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{i}([n],k)=0
for any
i>0
. Also by Theorem
C
,
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{i}([n],k)\cong H^{i}(|[n]|,\mathbb{Z})=0
, for all
i>1
, since the geometric realisation of the nerve
|[n]|
is the standard
n
-simplex and hence contractible.

It remains to compute
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{1}([n],k)
. We denote objects of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{[n]}_{2}
by triples
abc
where
a\leq b\leq c
. By the more precise formulation of Theorem
C
as Theorem
3.23
,
\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{i}([n],k)
is generated by linear combinations of non-homogeneous singleton modules, i.e., by simple modules
S_{ij}
, where
i<j
. Thus we only have to examine cocycles that are combinations of such modules. For
0\leq i<j\leq n
and
0\leq a\leq b\leq c\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\delta^{0}(S_{ij})(abc)=\begin{cases}k&(b,c)=(i,j)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{so}\hskip 10.00002pt\delta^{0}(S_{ij})=\sum_{a\leq i}S_{aij}

\displaystyle\delta^{1}(S_{ij})(abc)=\begin{cases}k&(a,c)=(i,j)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{so}\hskip 10.00002pt\delta^{1}(S_{ij})=\sum_{i\leq b\leq j}S_{ibj}

\displaystyle\delta^{2}(S_{ij})(abc)=\begin{cases}k&(a,b)=(i,j)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{so}\hskip 10.00002pt\delta^{2}(S_{ij})=\sum_{j\leq c}S_{ijc}

Thus we have

d^{1}(S_{ij})=\sum_{a<i}S_{aij}-\sum_{i<b<j}S_{ibj}+\sum_{j<c}S_{ijc}.

Let
z=\sum_{i<j}x_{ij}S_{ij}
be a
1
-cocycle. Then

0=d^{1}(z)=\sum_{a<i<j}x_{ij}S_{aij}-\sum_{i<b<j}x_{ij}S_{ibj}+\sum_{i<j<c}x_{ij}S_{ijc}=\sum_{0\leq a<b<c\leq n}(x_{bc}-x_{ac}+x_{ab})S_{abc}.

It follows by linear independence of the modules
S_{ijk}
that for each triple
a<b<c
, the relation
x_{bc}-x_{ac}+x_{ab}=0
must hold. Thus the expressions
x_{ab}S_{ab}+x_{bc}S_{bc}-x_{ac}S_{ac}
, where the relation above is satisfied, generate a subgroup of
1
-cocycles. One thus obtains a system of homogeneous linear equations in the variables
x_{ij}
for all
0\leq i<j\leq n
. By basic linear algebra and an inductive argument, the variables
x_{i,i+k}
where
k>1
can be expressed as

x_{i,i+k}=\sum_{j=i}^{k-1}x_{j,j+1},

while no further relations hold among the
x_{j,j+1}
.
Hence the null space of the
d^{1}
matrix is
n
-dimensional.

On the other hand, for
n>1
, one may verify by direct calculation that for any
0\leq i<j\leq n
, the virtual module

\operatorname{Im}(d^{0})

\operatorname{Ker}\nolimits(d^{1})

\widetilde{\EuR{G}}^{1}([n],k)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the poset
[n]
, with objects the natural numbers
0,\ldots,n
, where

, and the natural order relation. Then

\EuR{G}^{0}([n],k)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
and
\EuR{E}^{0}([n],k)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Furthermore, the natural map
\EuR{E}^{0}([n],k)\to\EuR{G}^{0}([n],k)
is split injective with cokernel generated by
[S_{[n]}]

Proof.

For non-negative integers
0\leq a<b\leq n
, let
J_{a,b}
denote the interval module on
[n]
, with
J_{a,b}(i)=k
for
a\leq i\leq b
and
J_{a,b}(i)=0
otherwise. Let

denote the simple

-module with value

at
a
and
0
elsewhere. Similarly, denote objects in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{[n]}_{1}
by
ab
, where

. For
a<b-1

denote the module that is constant on the path

a(a+1)\to(a+1)(a+2)\to\cdots\to(b-1)b

with value
k
on objects, and
0
everywhere else. Then the isomorphism classes of the modules

form a basis for
\EuR{Gr}(k[n])
. Clearly the modules
J_{0,n}
and
\sum_{a=0}^{n}S_{a}
are
0

\EuR{G}^{0}([n],k)
. Consider the sum
[J_{0,n-1}]+[J_{1,n}]-[J_{1,n-1}]

J_{0,n-1}\partial_{i}=\begin{cases}J_{01,(n-2)(n-1)}&i=0\\
J_{01,(n-1)n}&i=1,\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002ptJ_{1,n}\partial_{i}=\begin{cases}J_{01,(n-1)n}&i=0\\

and

. Let

. In particular, one has
\operatorname{rk}\nolimits X(0<n-1)=\alpha_{0,n-1}

\operatorname{rk}\nolimits X(1<n)=\beta_{1,n}

is the module

5.3.
Cyclic Quivers

By a cyclic quiver we mean any quiver where the underlying undirected graph is a cycle. By Example
3.13
, if
\mathcal{Q}
is any such quiver, then
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}),k)\cong\EuR{Gr}(k[\mathbb{Z}])
. Hence
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}),k)
is a free abelian group generated by the indecomposable
k[\mathbb{Z}]
-modules, which we proceed to describe.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a cyclic quiver. Then for any field
k
the group
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}),k)
is a free abelian group with a basis given by pairs
(q(t),m)
, where
q(t)\in k[t]
is an irreducible polynomial of positive degree with a non-zero constant term and
m>0
is an integer.

Proof.

The group ring
k[\mathbb{Z}]
is isomorphic to the ring of Laurent polynomials
k[t,t^{-1}]
, which is a principal ideal domain. The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over principal ideal domains implies that any such module
M
is isomorphic to a finite sum of cyclic modules. Namely, one has an isomorphism

M\cong k[t]/(p_{1}(t))\oplus k[t]/(p_{2}(t))\oplus\cdots\oplus k[t]/(p_{n}(t)),

where
p_{i}(t)|p_{i+1}(t)
for each
1\leq i\leq n-1
, and
p_{i}(0)\neq 0
for all
i
. The second condition is required because
t
must act on
M
as an automorphism, and if
t|p_{i}(t)
, then
t
will be a zero divisor in the corresponding summand, which is impossible.

Write each
p_{j}(t)
for
1\leq j\leq n
as a product

p_{j}(t)=q_{1}(t)^{m_{j,1}}\cdot q_{2}(t)^{m_{j,2}}\cdots q_{r}(t)^{m_{j,r}},

where the
q_{i}(t)
are irreducible with a non-zero constant term. Here we use the same list of irreducible polynomials for all
p_{j}(t)
, with the understanding that some of the powers may be
0
. Then

k[t]/(p_{j}(t))\cong k[t]/(q_{1}(t)^{m_{j,1}})\oplus k[t]/(q_{2}(t)^{m_{j,2}})\oplus\cdots\oplus k[t]/(q_{r}(t)^{m_{j,r}}),

and each one of these summands is an indecomposable
k[t,t^{-1}]
-module.

Thus
\widetilde{\EuR{E}}^{0}(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}),k)
is the free abelian group with generators indexed by pairs
(q(t),m)
where
q(t)\in k[t]
is an irreducible polynomial and
m>0
is an integer, as claimed.
∎
