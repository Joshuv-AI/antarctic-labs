Private Private Information in Second-Price Auction
Boyu Liu, Wei Tang, Zihe Wang, Shuo Zhang

Abstract
Classic results show that even an arbitrarily small correlation across bidders’ information can enable full surplus extraction in auctions and related mechanism design settings. Motivated by this fragility, we study the information independence in a second-price auction when the seller commits to a private private information structure, meaning bidders’ signals are independent ex ante, while bidders share a symmetric and arbitrarily correlated prior distribution over their valuations. We first show that the seller optimal efficient outcome with full surplus extraction can always be implemented by a private private information structure that admits a Bayes Nash equilibrium. However, this equilibrium may not be stable. We then further construct a private private information structure that achieves revenue arbitrarily close to maximum welfare while admitting a strict equilibrium. At the same time, we establish an impossibility result: under private private information, in general, bidder surplus cannot achieve maximal welfare exactly, and we characterize necessary and sufficient conditions on the prior distribution under which bidder surplus can be made arbitrarily close to maximal welfare. We then explore which other efficient outcomes are achievable under private private information. Finally, moving beyond private private information, we provide a complete characterization of the achievable pairs (bidder surplus, seller revenue) under general information structures.

bidders’ information rents depend critically on whether their information remains private across bidders.

In particular, under ex ante independent signals, can the seller still design an efficient information structure that approximately extracts bidders’ surplus, or does independence impose a fundamental barrier?

Bidders nevertheless share a symmetric (exchangeable) prior over their valuations that may exhibit arbitrary correlation across bidders.
Because signals are independent ex ante, the seller cannot jointly manipulate bidders’ beliefs in a coordinated way;
this limits how aggressively the seller can raise the second-highest bid while keeping the allocation efficient, and it also constrains the extent to which the seller can lower the second-highest bid in the resulting equilibrium to transfer surplus to bidders.

Our first set of results shows that the private private information does not preclude full bidder surplus extraction, even under efficient allocation, and even within a particularly simple symmetric subclass of information structures.
We prove that there exists a private private information structure for which at least one induced Bayes Nash equilibrium is efficient and seller extracts full surplus, so the seller’s expected revenue equals the maximum welfare (see
Theorem

3.1
).
In our construction, we restrict attention to a symmetric subclass of private private structures in which bidders share a common signal space, each bidder’s signal has the same uniform marginal distribution on this space, and the information structure treats bidders symmetrically.
This restriction is deliberately simple, yet full surplus extraction remains achievable under an efficient allocation.

Our construction is guided by two principles that together guarantee full surplus extraction.
The first principle is about efficient allocation.
We design the joint relationship between values and signals so that whenever a bidder receives the highest signal, that bidder must also be the highest valuation bidder in every posterior state that the signal profile deems possible.
Intuitively, the top signal serves as a certificate of being the efficient winner.
This ensures that the second-price auction allocates the item efficiently in the equilibrium we target, even though bidders do not observe their values.
The second principle aims to eliminate the winner’s informational rent.
Conditional on a bidder winning, we design beliefs so that the winner’s posterior expected valuation equals the second highest signal in the realized signal profile.
Equivalently,
after observing the signal profile, the expected value advantage of holding the top signal is exactly offset, so the winner’s posterior expected value is pinned down by the second highest signal rather than by the winner’s own signal.
In a second-price auction, the payment is given by the second highest bid, so in the equilibrium, where we show that bidders would follow the pure strategy of bidding their signal, the winner pays exactly what the second highest signal suggests. Because we align the winner’s expected value with that payment, the winner has no expected surplus left to capture.
The seller therefore collects essentially the entire welfare as revenue.

A key intuition is that the private private constraint restricts only the ex ante dependence across bidders’ signals, but it does not prevent the seller from coupling each bidder’s signal with the underlying value profile in a way that creates strong competition in equilibrium.
Our symmetric construction exploits this by choosing a carefully designed signal space and then selecting posterior mappings so that the induced distribution of winner posteriors is compatible with Bayes plausibility. At a high level, we first specify the distribution of the second highest signal conditional on winning, which determines the distribution of the winner’s posterior expected value that we wish to implement. We then verify that this target distribution can indeed arise from the modified prior faced by the winner, and we build an information structure that realizes these posteriors while keeping signals independent and symmetric ex ante.

The equilibrium induced by the above private private information that extracts full surplus may not be stable (as there could be multiple best responses for bidders when fixing other bidders’ bidding strategies).
To address this issue, we show that there always exists a private private information that guarantees a
strict
Nash equilibrium while still achieving revenue arbitrarily close to the maximum welfare (see
Theorem

3.2
).
Under the strict equilibrium, each bidder has a unique best response to the others’ equilibrium actions.
As in our full extraction result, we focus on the same symmetric private private class.
The key intuition behind equilibrium strictness is to eliminate flat regions in bidders’ incentives.
In the full extraction construction, some bidders may be indifferent among a range of bids at certain signals, which may make the resulting equilibrium unstable.
We therefore modify the information structure by adding a carefully designed slack that breaks these indifferences.
Concretely, we perturb posteriors so that for every signal realization, deviating from the intended bid becomes strictly suboptimal, while the perturbation is small enough that the induced allocation remains efficient and the seller’s revenue remains arbitrarily close to maximum welfare.
This gives us a strict equilibrium that achieves near full surplus extraction, showing that ex ante independence does not prevent robust revenue guarantees even under a stringent stable equilibrium requirement.

Having characterized how the seller can extract bidders’ surplus under private private information, we next turn to the opposite extreme and ask whether the same ex ante signal-independence constraint can also support outcomes that are most favorable to bidders, specifically, whether bidder surplus can reach the level of maximal welfare.
We establish an impossibility result: we show that, in general, under any private private information that admits an efficient equilibrium, bidder surplus cannot equal maximal welfare (
Theorem

4.1
).
At the same time, we fully characterize the necessary and sufficient conditions on the prior value distribution under which bidder surplus can be made arbitrarily close to maximal welfare through some private private information structure. Specifically, we show that this is possible if and only if for every realized value profile, either bidders share a common value or at most one bidder has a positive value (
Proposition

4.2
).

This limitation is specific to the private private requirement.
The fundamental challenge is that ex ante signal independence limits the seller’s ability to coordinate bidders’ beliefs across bidders.
In a second-price auction, such coordination would be essential to achieve an efficient allocation while simultaneously reducing the second-highest bid, and thus the price, without diminishing the winner’s interim value.
As a result, except in the exceptional cases identified by our necessary and sufficient conditions, private private information cannot make bidder surplus equal maximal welfare.
By contrast, under general information structures, which we discuss next, bidder surplus can achieve maximal welfare by some general information structure.
This observation stands in contrast to the first-price auction setting studied in
8
, where the bidder-surplus-maximizing outcome can already be implemented within a private private information structure.
In second-price auctions, attaining exact maximal welfare as bidder surplus typically requires moving beyond the private private class; within that class, it is attainable only in the prior distributions that satisfy our identified conditions characterized above.

The above results also raise the following natural question: what other efficient outcomes are achievable under private private information?
We first revisit the prior conditions identified in
Proposition

4.2
under which bidders’ maximal surplus can approach maximal welfare, and we ask whether, under these priors, every efficient outcome is achievable by some private private information.
We show that for any such prior distribution, every efficient welfare outcome can be implemented by a private private information structure, except the endpoint in which bidder surplus equals maximal welfare (
Theorem

5.1
).
We then turn to another well studied setting, the independent private values (IPV) model. In this setting, revealing full information is itself a private private information, because bidders’ values are independent, and it admits an efficient equilibrium.
We show that every efficient welfare outcome between the seller optimal outcome and the outcome induced by full information is implementable by some private private information (
Theorem

5.2
).

Finally, we move beyond the private private restriction and allow arbitrary common prior information structures, so bidders’ signals may be correlated in any way consistent with the given prior.
For a fixed prior over value profiles, we provide a complete characterization of equilibrium payoff outcomes in the second price auction by describing the full set of achievable pairs of seller revenue and bidder surplus (
Theorem

5.3
).
In particular, we show that the implementable payoff set is exactly a trapezoid in the nonnegative quadrant (see
Figure

2
): we characterize all four extreme points and show that every point in the trapezoid is attainable.
The upper boundary corresponds to efficiency frontier, where total payoffs sum to the maximum welfare, while the lower boundary captures the maximally inefficient outcomes, with welfare being the expected minimum valuation, which can be interpreted as allocating the object to the lowest-valuation bidder.

) strictly within the interval
(\bar{v}-\varepsilon,\bar{v}]
.
We then impose a hierarchical structure on the high signals:
s_{1,h}>\dots>s_{N,h}>\max_{j}s_{j,l}
.
For the lowest-indexed winner
i
, we assign
s_{i}=s_{i,h}
and set
s_{j}=s_{j,l}
for all predecessors
j<i
, while signals for successors
j>i
are drawn independently according to specific marginals.
This construction ensures that the winner holds the strictly highest signal and that the second-highest bid exceeds
\bar{v}-\varepsilon
. Thus, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, revenue converges to the full surplus.

3.1
An Illustrative Example

Although our primary analysis focuses on a discrete valuation space,
our results
extend naturally to continuous settings.
We begin by presenting an illustrative example with continuous valuations. For the remainder of the discussion, however, we retain the discrete assumption for expositional simplicity.

Consider two bidders whose valuations are drawn independently from a uniform distribution, i.e.,
(v_{1},v_{2})\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{2})
.
By definition, we know that
\overline{\textsc{WEL}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[{v_{1}\vee v_{2}}\right]=\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
.
Consider the following private private information structure
\mathcal{I}=((S_{i})_{i=1,2},\pi)
, where the signal support is
S_{1}=S_{2}=[1/2,1]
. The joint probability distribution
\pi(v,s)
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\pi(v_{1},v_{2},s_{1},s_{2})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{2}{v_{1}(1-s_{2})}\cdot\mathbf{1}\!\left\{{\frac{1}{2}\leq s_{2}\leq s_{1}\leq 1,\enskip s_{2}\leq\frac{v_{1}+1}{2}}\right\}&\text{ if }v_{1}>v_{2}~;\\[15.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{2}{v_{2}(1-s_{1})}\cdot\mathbf{1}\!\left\{{\frac{1}{2}\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq 1,\enskip s_{1}\leq\frac{v_{2}+1}{2}}\right\}&\text{ if }v_{2}>v_{1}.\end{cases}

The case where
v_{1}=v_{2}
occurs with probability zero and can be defined arbitrarily.
Next, we verify that
\mathcal{I}
is indeed a symmetric private private information structure.
For any
s_{1}>s_{2}
,
we have:

\displaystyle\int_{v}\pi(v_{1},v_{2},s_{1},s_{2})\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{2s_{2}-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{v_{1}}\frac{2}{v_{1}(1-s_{2})}\,\mathrm{d}v_{2}\,\mathrm{d}v_{1}

\displaystyle=4

\displaystyle=\int_{s_{1}}\int_{v}\pi(v_{1},v_{2},s_{1},s_{2})\;\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}s_{1}\cdot\int_{s_{2}}\int_{v}\pi(v_{1},v_{2},s_{1},s_{2})\;\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}s_{2}~.

By symmetry the same holds when
s_{2}>s_{1}
. Thus, it is a private private information.
As both
s_{1},s_{2}
have uniform marginals, the constructed information structure is also symmetric.

By construction, the information structure
\mathcal{I}
satisfies
Winner-Dominance
. Observe that
s_{1}>s_{2}
implies
v_{1}>v_{2}
; otherwise, the density
\pi(v_{1},v_{2},s_{1},s_{2})=0
.
Also
\mathcal{I}
satisfies
No-Rent Winner
. Given a signal profile where
s_{1}>s_{2}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[{v_{1}\mid(s_{1},s_{2})}\right]=\int_{2s_{2}-1}^{1}v_{1}\int_{0}^{v_{1}}\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{v_{1}(1-s_{2})}\,\mathrm{d}v_{2}\,\mathrm{d}v_{1}=s_{2}~,\quad\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[{v_{2}\mid(s_{1},s_{2})}\right]=\frac{s_{2}}{2}~.

By symmetry, when
s_{2}>s_{1}
, we obtain
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[{v_{1}\mid(s_{1},s_{2})}\right]=s_{1}/2
and
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[{v_{2}\mid(s_{1},s_{2})}\right]=s_{1}
. Since the event
s_{1}=s_{2}
has measure zero, condition
Winner-Dominance
is satisfied almost surely.
In the next subsection, we demonstrate that for any information structure satisfying
No-Rent Winner
and
Winner-Dominance
,
\sigma_{i}(s_{i})=s_{i}
is an equilibrium. In this equilibrium, bidders have zero surplus. Furthermore, because condition
Winner-Dominance
ensures that the bidder with the highest signal also holds the highest valuation, the allocation is efficient. Consequently, the seller extracts the full surplus, achieving
\textsc{Rev}(\mathcal{I},\sigma)=\overline{\textsc{WEL}}
.

We briefly discuss the intuition derived from this example, which serves as a foundation for the subsequent analysis.
First, observe that the information structure
\pi
can be decomposed into two “local” probability distributions,
\pi^{(1)}
and
\pi^{(2)}
, corresponding to the disjoint events
\{v_{1}>v_{2}\}
and
\{v_{2}>v_{1}\}
, respectively.
On each event, the structure is designed so that the bidder with the higher valuation also receives the higher signal and therefore wins.
This piecewise construction is the key to satisfy condition
Winner-Dominance
, which guarantees allocation efficiency.
Consequently, the design of the local distribution
\pi^{(1)}

amounts to specifying a joint distribution supported on the product set where bidder
1
is the winner in both dimensions:

\{v:v_{1}=\max(v)\}\times\{s:s_{1}=\max(s)\}
(and analogously for
\pi^{(2)}
).
The central challenge, however, lies in designing these local distributions to ensure that the higher-valuation bidder consistently receives the higher signal, while simultaneously making the bidders’ expected surplus to zero.
To achieve this, we impose the binding constraint
No-Rent Winner
. This condition ensures that for any realized signal profile, the winner’s expected valuation coincides exactly with the second-highest signal (the payment).
Finally, our analysis relies on a fundamental observation: when a symmetric private private information structure satisfies
No-Rent Winner
, once its signal space is fixed, the distribution of bidder
i
’s expected valuation conditional on winning (i.e., receiving the highest signal) will be then determined.

3.2
Towards Maximum Revenue

In this subsection, we prove
Theorem

3.1
.
In particular, we first argue that under any information structure satisfying
Winner-Dominance
and
No-Rent Winner
, there exists an efficient equilibrium in which bidders’ surplus is
0
(see
Lemma

3.3
).
We then show that there exists a symmetric private private information structure satisfying these two conditions (see
Proposition

3.4
), and this existence result is established via two construction lemmas (
Lemma

3.5
and
Lemma

3.6
).

Lemma 3.3
.

For any information structure satisfying
Winner-Dominance
and
No-Rent Winner
, the bidders following the pure strategy of bidding their signals (
\sigma_{i}(s_{i})=s_{i}
) is an efficient equilibrium and the bidders’ surplus is zero.

The proof of the above lemma, as well as the remaining omitted proofs, is deferred to the appendix.
Intuitively,
No-Rent Winner
binds the winner’s expected valuation at every realized signal profile to exactly match the second-price payment.
So the winner earns zero surplus and the seller extracts the full surplus.
As we show in the proof,
Winner-Dominance
and
No-Rent Winner
can jointly imply the following condition:

\displaystyle\text{if }s_{i}<\max(s),\text{ then }x_{i}(s)\leq\textsc{secmax}(s)~,\quad\text{for all }s\in S,i\in[N]~.

(1)

No-Rent Winner
, together with Eqn. (
1
), rules out profitable upward deviations by any losing bidder: to overtake the current winner, a bidder must bid above
\max(s)
and would then pay (at least)
\max(s)
, while her expected valuation is bounded above by
\textsc{secmax}(s)
.
Downward deviations are similarly unattractive:
since the equilibrium payoff is already zero, shading one’s bid can only reduce the chance of winning without generating profitable gain.
Although
Lemma

3.3
imposes strong pointwise constraints on the expected valuations of all bidders across the entire signal profile space, we later show that a private private information structure satisfying these conditions does exist and can be explicitly constructed.

With
Lemma

3.3
, our design problem reduces to constructing an information structure that simultaneously satisfies the binding equalities in
No-Rent Winner
and
Winner-Dominance
.

Proposition 3.4
.

There exists a symmetric private private information structure such that it satisfies the conditions
Winner-Dominance
and
No-Rent Winner
.

Proposition

3.4
, together with
Lemma

3.3
, completes the proof for our first main result
Theorem

3.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Let the information structure be the one identified in
Proposition

3.4
.
Then we know that it is symmetric private private, and it satisfies the conditions
Winner-Dominance
and
No-Rent Winner
.
By
Lemma

3.3
, the condition in Eqn. (
1
) also holds.
Thus, under this information structure, truth bidding is an equilibrium, and moreover, under this equilibrium the bidders’ surplus is
0
.
We also know that this information structure achieves efficient allocation under the truthful bidding.
Thus, the seller achieves revenue that equals the maximum welfare.
∎

In the sequel, we prove
Proposition

3.4
by explicitly constructing a symmetric private private information that satisfies
Winner-Dominance
and
No-Rent Winner
.
Our argument is organized around two lemmas. We begin by restricting attention to symmetric private private information structures (see
Definition

3.1
), under which fixing a common signal space
\bar{S}
(will be defined shortly) for each bidder pins down the distribution of signal profiles.
To satisfy the condition
No-Rent Winner
, we must let the winning bidder’s interim value equal the second-highest signal, so we know that fixing the signal space also pins down the distribution of the winner’s interim value.
We then enforce
Winner-Dominance
, by conditioning the
prior on the event that the winner indeed has the highest realized valuation, which leads to a modified (tie-weighted) prior.
We achieve these two steps using the following two lemmas:

Lemma

3.5
chooses a construction of
\bar{S}
and constructs, for each bidder
i
, a local coupling between value profiles and signal profiles on the event that
i
is selected as the winner, matching the corresponding modified prior and satisfying
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{v_{i}\mid s}\right]=\textsc{secmax}(s)
.
This would guarantee the desired properties on signal profiles where the winner is uniquely identified by having a strictly highest signal.

Lemma

3.6
assembles these local couplings into a global joint distribution over values and signals, verifies that the resulting signal marginals are uniform and independent across bidders, and concludes the existence of the desired symmetric private private information structure.

An interval signal space and its winner’s interim value distribution

We consider the following interval signal space, parameterized by
\underline{s},\bar{s}\in[0,\max_{v}V]
where
\bar{s}>\underline{s}
and derive the resulting distribution of the winner’s interim value:

\displaystyle\bar{S}\;\triangleq\;(\underline{s}\;,\;\bar{s})~.

An important implication of
No-Rent Winner
is that the distribution of the winner’s expected valuation is fully determined by the distribution of the second-highest signal if the considered information structure satisfies the condition
No-Rent Winner
.
Indeed, conditional on bidder
i
winning the item,

No-Rent Winner
implies that
x_{i}(s)=\textsc{secmax}(s)
, so the law of
x_{i}(s)
is exactly the law of
\textsc{secmax}(s)
under the conditional distribution of signal profiles given that
i
wins.
Thus, once the signal space is fixed, the distribution of
\textsc{secmax}(s)
conditional on bidder
i
winning is pinned down, and so is the distribution of the winning bidder’s interim value.
By symmetry of both the prior and the information structure, this conditional distribution is the same for all bidders. We denote it by
G\in\Delta(\bar{S})
as its CDF.
Note that since the signal space is continuous, ties occur with probability zero. We denote
\phi
as the distribution of signal profiles and
\phi_{i}
as the marginal distribution.
Then, for each signal
x\in\bar{S}
,

\displaystyle G(x)\triangleq\text{Pr}\!\left[{x_{i}(s)\leq x\mid i\text{ wins}}\right]=\frac{\int_{s\in\bar{S}^{N}}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{{\max(s)=s_{i}\text{ and }\textsc{secmax}(s)\leq x}\right\}\cdot\phi(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}{\int_{s\in\bar{S}^{N}}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{{\max(s)=s_{i}}\right\}\cdot\phi(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}~.

(2)

We denote by
g
the density function of distribution
G
.

Here
\mathbf{1}\!\left\{{\cdot}\right\}
is the indicator function.
Moreover, when the marginal distribution over signal is uniform, we have
\phi(s)=\phi(s^{\dagger})
for all signal profiles
s,s^{\dagger}\in\bar{S}^{N}
, and thus it
cancels from the numerator and denominator.
Intuitively, distribution
g
summarizes how often each
signal
x
appears as the winning bidder’s expected valuation when the underlying information structure is symmetric private private with the signal space
\bar{S}
.

Winner-Dominance and the modified prior

However, not every candidate signal space is feasible for constructing a symmetric private private information structure that satisfies both
Winner-Dominance
and
No-Rent Winner
.

Winner-Dominance
requires that whenever bidder
i
wins, she must have the highest realized value.
This leads us to work under the prior conditional on “
i
is the (tie-broken) highest-value bidder.” We write

m(x)
for the number of coordinates attaining the maximum in a profile
x
(either a value
profile
v
or a signal profile
s
).
Under a uniform tie-breaking rule, we define bidder
i
’s “modified” prior
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
as the
distribution of bidder
i
’s value
v_{i}
conditional on
i
winning, and its probability mass function is given as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}_{i}(a)\triangleq\text{Pr}\!\left[{v_{i}=a\mid i\text{ wins}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{v}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{{v_{i}=\max(v)\text{ and }v_{i}=a}\right\}\cdot\lambda(v)\cdot\frac{1}{m(v)}}{\sum_{v}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{{v_{i}=\max(v)}\right\}\cdot\lambda(v)\cdot\frac{1}{m(v)}}~,\quad a\in V~.

(3)

Since the original prior is symmetric (exchangeable across bidders), the modified prior is identical across bidders. We nevertheless write it as
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
as the subsequent construction is carried out from bidder
i
’s perspective.

Winning profiles and tie weighted conditioning

Fix the interval signal space
\bar{S}=(\underline{s},\bar{s})
. For each bidder
i\in[N]
, we define the following quantities

\displaystyle V^{(i)}\triangleq\left\{v\in V^{N}:v_{i}=\max(v)\right\},\;\;\bar{S}^{(i)}\triangleq\left\{s\in\bar{S}^{N}:s_{i}=\max(s)\right\}~;\;\;\hat{S}^{(i)}\triangleq\left\{s\in\bar{S}^{N}:s_{i}>\max_{j\neq i}s_{j}\right\}~.

By definition, the set
\hat{S}^{(i)}
contains all signal profiles where bidder
i
has the unique highest signal, whereas

\bar{S}^{(i)}\setminus\hat{S}^{(i)}
consists of signal profiles where bidder
i
receives the highest signal and another bidder
j\neq i
also receives the same highest signal. Since the signal space is continuous, the set of ties
\bar{S}^{(i)}\setminus\hat{S}^{(i)}
has measure zero. We therefore restrict our attention to
\hat{S}^{(i)}
.

The role
V^{(i)}
in our construction is the following.
For
s\in\hat{S}^{(i)}
, bidder
i
is the

only
bidder with the highest signal, so
Winner-Dominance
requires that bidder
i

has the highest realized value in her posterior support.
Hence, restricting value profiles to
V^{(i)}
is already sufficient to guarantee
Winner-Dominance
on
\hat{S}^{(i)}
.

To better describe our construction, we introduce the following definition.
Under a uniform tie breaking rule, we define the tie weighted distribution

\widehat{\lambda}^{(i)}\in\Delta(V^{(i)})
by

\widehat{\lambda}^{(i)}(v)\ \triangleq\ \frac{1}{m(v)}\cdot\frac{\lambda(v)}{\sum\nolimits_{v^{\prime}\in V^{(i)}}\lambda(v^{\prime})\cdot\frac{1}{m(v^{\prime})}}\,,\quad v\in V^{(i)}~.

(4)

By definition, distribution
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
that we defined in Eqn. (
3
) is the marginal distribution of
v_{i}
in
\widehat{\lambda}^{(i)}
, and we next define its mean value
\hat{v}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\lambda}_{i}}\!\left[{v_{i}}\right]
.
Similarly, under uniform signals on
\bar{S}^{N}
, we define the distribution

\phi^{(i)}\in\Delta(\bar{S}^{(i)})
as the probability measure on
\bar{S}^{(i)}
.
With these definitions, we now present our two construction lemmas: the first establishes a local coupling that designates bidder
i
as the winner, and the second builds the global construction by combining these local couplings.

Lemma 3.5

(Local coupling for a designated winner)
.

There exists a choice of parameters
(\underline{s},\bar{s})
such that for every

i\in[N]
there exists a joint distribution
\pi^{(i)}\in\Delta(V^{(i)}\times\bar{S}^{(i)})

satisfying:

1.

the marginal of
\pi^{(i)}
on
V^{(i)}
is
\widehat{\lambda}^{(i)}
;

2.

the marginal of
\pi^{(i)}
on
\bar{S}^{(i)}
is
\phi^{(i)}
; and

3.

for every
s\in\bar{S}^{(i)}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(i)}}\!\left[{v_{i}\mid s}\right]=\textsc{secmax}(s)~.

(5)

In particular, for all
s\in\hat{S}^{(i)}
,
No-Rent Winner
and
Winner-Dominance
hold.

Lemma 3.6

(Global construction from local couplings)
.

Suppose for each bidder
i\in[N]
, we have a distribution

\hat{\pi}^{(i)}\in\Delta(V^{(i)}\times\bar{S}^{(i)})
satisfying the three properties in

Lemma

3.5
. Define a joint distribution
\hat{\pi}\in\Delta(V^{N}\times\bar{S}^{N})
by

\hat{\pi}(v,s)\ \triangleq\ \frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i\in[N]}\hat{\pi}^{(i)}(v,s)~.

(6)

Then
\hat{\pi}
defines a symmetric private private information structure
\hat{\mathcal{I}}=(\bar{S}^{N},\hat{\pi})
.
Moreover, under
\hat{\pi}
the conditions
No-Rent Winner
and
Winner-Dominance
hold for
all signal profiles
s\in\cup_{i\in[N]}\hat{S}^{(i)}
.

We are now ready to prove
Proposition

3.4
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.4
.

In
Lemma

3.6
, we construct the symmetric private private information structure
\hat{\mathcal{I}}=(\bar{S}^{N},\hat{\pi})
satisfying conditions
No-Rent Winner
and
Winner-Dominance
for all
s\in\cup_{i\in[N]}\hat{S}^{(i)}
. Since the signal space is continuous, the set of ties
\bar{S}\setminus\cup_{i\in[N]}\hat{S}^{(i)}
has measure zero. Consequently, conditions
No-Rent Winner
and
Winner-Dominance
hold with probability one. This implies that the constructed information structure achieves efficiency and extracts full surplus (zero bidder surplus). This completes the proof.
∎

3.3
Towards Approximate Maximum Revenue with Strict Equilibrium

To show the existence of a private private information that admits a strict efficient equilibrium, we modify the
No-Rent Winner
condition as a strengthened set of sufficient conditions: whenever an information structure satisfies these conditions, it admits truthful bidding i.e., bidding one’s signal
\sigma_{i}(s_{i})=s_{i}
, as a strict equilibrium.
Moreover, these conditions guarantee that upon winning, a bidder’s expected payment is within
\varepsilon
of her expected valuation, so her expected surplus is strictly positive while remaining uniformly bounded by
\varepsilon
, i.e.,
0<\textsc{BS}(\mathcal{I},\sigma)\leq\varepsilon
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Fix an arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
. Consider a symmetric private private information structure
\mathcal{I}
that satisfies the following conditions:

1.

Signal space:

Every bidder shares a common signal space
\bar{S}=(\underline{s},\bar{s})
with sufficiently small length, i.e.,
0<\bar{s}-\underline{s}\leq\varepsilon
.

2.

Expected valuation:

For any signal profile
s
with a unique highest signal, bidder
i
’s expected valuation

x_{i}(s)
satisfies:

(a)

If bidder
i
holds the highest signal, that is
s_{i}=\max(s)
, her expected valuation is given by:

\displaystyle x_{i}(s)=\begin{cases}\textsc{secmax}(s)-\displaystyle\frac{(\textsc{secmax}(s)-\underline{s})\cdot\varepsilon}{2N}&\text{if }\underline{s}<\textsc{secmax}(s)<\displaystyle\frac{s_{i}+\underline{s}}{2},\\[10.0pt]
\textsc{secmax}(s)+\displaystyle\frac{(s_{i}-\textsc{secmax}(s))\cdot\varepsilon}{N}&\text{if }\displaystyle\frac{s_{i}+\underline{s}}{2}\leq\textsc{secmax}(s)<\bar{s}.\end{cases}

(b)

If bidder
i
does not hold the highest signal, that is,
s_{i}<\max(s)
, then for any
j
such that
s_{j}=\max(s)
, it holds that:
x_{i}(s)\leq x_{j}(s)
.

Under these conditions,
bidders following the pure strategy of bidding their signal (
\sigma_{i}(s_{i})=s_{i}
) is a strict equilibrium.
Moreover, the aggregate bidders’ surplus satisfies
0<\textsc{BS}(\mathcal{I},\sigma)\leq\varepsilon
.

We next give a brief intuition for how
Lemma

3.7
helps to ensure the truthful bidding as a strict equilibrium. Consider a symmetric private private information satisfying the conditions in
Lemma

3.7
.
Let us fix a bidder
i
with her signal
s_{i}
, and let
y\triangleq\max_{k\neq i}s_{k}
denote the highest competing signal, i.e., the price bidder
i
pays if she wins under truthful bidding by all other bidders. Since the signal space is continuous, ties occur with zero probability. Thus, we focus only on signal profiles with a unique highest signal. For all signal profiles
s
where bidder
i
has the unique highest signal
s_{i}
, under the construction in
Lemma

3.7
, bidder
i
’s interim expected valuation depends on
s_{i}
and
y
. With a slight abuse of notation, we use
x_{i}(s_{i},y)
to denote bidder
i
’s expected value conditional on winning with a payment of
y
. Note that
x_{i}(s_{i},y)
is a piecewise-linear function of
y
.

There exists a symmetric private private information

∎

Maximum Bidder Surplus in Private Private Information

In this section, we study the theoretical limits of bidder surplus in the context of private private information.
We begin by establishing a negative result: in second-price auctions, no private private information can admit an efficient equilibrium in which bidders’ surplus equals the maximum welfare.
We then characterize exactly when the gap can be made arbitrarily small.
Specifically, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions on the prior value distribution under which there exists a private private information and an efficient equilibrium such that bidders’ surplus is arbitrarily close to maximal welfare.
These conditions hold only in special cases: for every valuation profile
v\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda)
, either all bidders share a common value or there exists at most one bidder that has a positive value.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists no private private information structure
\mathcal{I}
that admits an efficient equilibrium
such that the resulting bidders’ surplus equals the maximal welfare, as long as there exists a value profile
v\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda)

where there are two bidders have different values.

The key tension behind
Theorem

4.1
is that making bidders capture the entire efficient welfare forces the seller’s revenue to be exactly zero.
In a second-price auction, zero revenue means the second-highest bid must be zero almost surely in the equilibrium.
Under a private private information structure, however, bidders’ signals are independent ex ante,
so any equilibrium in which two or more bidders sometimes submit a strictly positive bid necessarily generates a positive probability that multiple bidders bid positively at the same time.
This immediately implies a strictly positive second highest bid and hence positive revenue, contradicting zero revenue.
The only way to avoid this “overlap” under independence is to ensure that at most one bidder can ever bid positively, or that all bidders always bid zero.
But either way breaks efficiency whenever valuations are not purely common values,
as efficiency requires that different bidders win in different states, which is incompatible with suppressing competition in this way.

Proposition

4.2
complements
Theorem

4.1
by identifying exactly when this tension disappears, so that bidder surplus can be arbitrarily close to maximal welfare under private private information.

Proposition 4.2
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a private private information
\mathcal{I}
satisfying the following conditions simultaneously:
(i) it admits an efficient equilibrium;
(ii) bidders’ surplus satisfies
\textsc{BS}(\mathcal{I},\sigma)\geq\overline{\textsc{WEL}}-\varepsilon
;
if and only if the prior
\lambda
satisfies: for every
v\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda)
, either bidders share a common value (i.e.,
v_{i}=v_{j}
for all
i,j
), or there exists some bidder
i\in[N]
such that
v_{i}>0,v_{-i}\equiv 0
.

To push bidders’ surplus close to maximal welfare requires the seller’s expected revenue to be arbitrarily close to zero, which means that competitive bidding must occur with vanishing probability.
With independent signals, however, any scheme that reliably selects the highest value bidder typically creates overlap events in which more than one bidder submits a positive bid, which pushes the second highest bid away from zero.

The exceptional priors in
Proposition

4.2
are precisely those that avoid meaningful competition in the prior support.
First, if all bidders always have the same value, then efficiency does not require distinguishing among bidders and competition is irrelevant.
Second, if at most one bidder has a positive value in the realized value profile, an efficient allocation can be implemented while keeping all other bidders’ bids at zero and the second highest bid near zero.
In all other priors, the presence of at least two bidders with distinct positive values on an event of positive probability forces overlap to occur under independent signaling, which makes revenue bounded away from zero and prevents bidders’ surplus from approaching maximal welfare.

Indeed, such mixing typically induces correlation across bidders’ signals, violating the mutual independence requirement in private private information.
As a result, it is not a priori clear whether the set of achievable efficient outcomes is path connected.
Nonetheless, we show that a full path-connected segment of the efficient frontier is attainable,

In contrast, when the realized profile contains at least one bidder in the high region, we reveal the values of those high region bidders so the efficient winner is identified,

Consequently, a profile containing a high-value bidder falls outside the domain of this specific information structure.
Instead, for each low region bidder we add an independent private randomization and remap their low value into a signal that has the same target marginal distribution as in the low only construction.
This remapping is feasibility driven: it restores product structure across bidders’ signals even though the overall signaling rule depends on the full profile.
At the same time, it preserves incentives, because these low region signals always stay below the threshold and satisfy the same local indifference properties as before, so a low bidder cannot profit by mimicking a high bid, and truthful bidding remains an equilibrium while efficiency is maintained.

5.2
Achievable Welfare Outcomes in General Information Structures

In this section, we relax the signal independency condition imposed by the private private information and instead
consider the full set of surplus pairs that can arise under general information structures.

Theorem 5.3
.

For any pair of non-negative values
(R,B)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
satisfying the feasibility constraints:
\underline{\textsc{WEL}}\leq R+B\leq\overline{\textsc{WEL}}
,
there exists an information structure
\mathcal{I}
and a corresponding equilibrium
\sigma
such that the expected seller revenue is
\textsc{Rev}(\mathcal{I},\sigma)=R
and the bidders’ surplus is
\textsc{BS}(\mathcal{I},\sigma)=B
.

In other words, the set of payoff pairs implementable by general information structures in a second-price auction coincides exactly with the set of feasible payoffs satisfying
\underline{\textsc{WEL}}\leq R+B\leq\overline{\textsc{WEL}}
.

As illustrated in

\overline{\textsc{WEL}}

) represents the inefficiency frontier associated with the maximally inefficient welfare,
\underline{\textsc{WEL}}
.

Theorem

5.3
shows that
every point in the trapezoid is implementable as an equilibrium outcome under some information structure.

It is worth comparing
Theorem

5.3
with the welfare outcomes achievable in first price auctions.
8

Instead, there is a convex curve connecting points

B

In contrast,

Prior work on second-price auctions can be viewed as identifying particular outcomes within this trapezoid.
For example,

Complementing these implementability results,

Maximal Bidder Surplus

We first consider maximal bidder surplus that can be achieved by general information structures.

Proposition 5.4

(Maximum bidders’ surplus)
.

There exists an information structure
\mathcal{I}
and equilibrium
\sigma
such that the bidders’ surplus equals the maximum welfare, that is,
\textsc{BS}(\mathcal{I},\sigma)=\overline{\textsc{WEL}}
.

We prove
Proposition

5.4
by construction.
The intuition behind our construction is as follows.
To implement an outcome that is efficient and bidder-optimal, the information structure must satisfy two seemingly conflicting requirements:
it must ensure that the item is allocated to the highest-valuation bidder (to achieve allocation efficiency) while simultaneously making the winner’s payment to zero.
To achieve this,
we construct an information structure with extreme ex post information asymmetry.
In particular, we privately reveal the winning valuation only to the highest-valuation bidder and send a null (zero) signal to all other bidders, so their equilibrium bids are zero and the second price collapses to
0
.
In the second-price auction, this ensures that the winner bids truthfully (preserving efficiency) while the second-highest bid is fixed at
0
, allowing the winner to capture the entire surplus as an informational rent.

Minimum Efficiency

Thus far, our analysis has led us to equilibrium in which the allocation of the good was efficient, so that the welfare outcome lay on the northeast frontier of
Figure

2
.
We next characterize two extreme points among all maximally inefficient outcomes.

Proposition 5.5

(Minimum bidders’ surplus with zero revenue)
.

There exists an information structure
\mathcal{I}
and equilibrium
\sigma
such that the bidders’ surplus equals the maximally inefficient welfare, that is,
\textsc{BS}(\mathcal{I},\sigma)=\underline{\textsc{WEL}}
and the seller’s revenue is zero.

The intuition behind the constructed information structure is to create an information structure that forces the lowest-valuation bidder to win, so as to minimize welfare, while ensuring the second-highest bid is always
0
.
We do this by giving the lowest-valuation bidder a uniquely high signal
\bar{v}
, and giving every other bidder a null signal
0
, so everyone can infer who is designated to win.
Since
\bar{v}
is chosen as the maximum valuation, no other bidder is willing to outbid
\bar{v}
: winning would require paying at least
\bar{v}
, which yields a non-positive payoff.
Thus, truthful bidding is an equilibrium in which the lowest-valuation bidder always wins, and total welfare equals
\underline{\textsc{WEL}}
,
and the winner’s payment is always zero, leaving bidders with full surplus.

Finally, we can also show that there exists an information structure that achieves the most inefficient outcome with zero bidders’ surplus.

Proposition 5.6

(Minimum revenue with zero bidders’ surplus)
.

There exists an information structure
\mathcal{I}
and equilibrium
\sigma
such that the seller’s revenue equals to the maximally inefficient welfare, that is,
\textsc{Rev}(\mathcal{I},\sigma)=\underline{\textsc{WEL}}
and the bidders’ surplus is zero.

L=\bar{s}-\underline{s}
to be a constant such that
0<L
. We perform a change of variables with
\delta_{\ell}=s_{\ell}-\underline{s}
, where
\delta_{\ell}\in(0,L)
.
The probability density is defined as follows

\displaystyle\gamma(\delta_{\ell};L)\triangleq N(N-1)\left(\frac{\delta_{\ell}}{L}\right)^{N-2}\cdot\frac{L-\delta_{\ell}}{L^{2}}~.

The expectation then becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{g}\!\left[{s_{\ell}}\right]=\underline{s}+\int_{0}^{L}\delta_{\ell}\cdot\gamma(\delta_{\ell};L)\,\mathrm{d}\delta_{\ell}=\underline{s}+K(L)~,

where
K(L)\triangleq\int_{0}^{L}\delta_{\ell}\cdot\gamma(\delta_{\ell};L)\,\mathrm{d}\delta_{\ell}
is a constant determined strictly by the interval length
L
.
This yields the linear form
\mathbb{E}_{g}\!\left[{s_{\ell}}\right]=\underline{s}+K(L)
, where
0<K(L)<L
is a constant independent of
\underline{s}
and is positively correlated with
L
. As
\underline{s}
varies from
\underline{v}
to
\bar{v}
and
L\to 0
, the continuous function
\mathbb{E}_{g}\!\left[{s_{\ell}}\right]
spans the entire interval of
(\underline{v},\bar{v})
. Since
\hat{v}\in(\underline{v},\bar{v})
, by the Intermediate Value Theorem, there exists a
\underline{s}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{g}\!\left[{s_{\ell}}\right]=\hat{v}
.

Next, we verify the second-order stochastic dominance condition. We require:

\displaystyle\Psi(x)\triangleq\int_{\underline{v}}^{x}\bigl(F(t)-G(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0\quad\text{for all }x\in[\underline{v},\bar{v}]~.

Note that for any
x\in[\underline{v},\bar{v}]
, the term
\int_{\underline{v}}^{x}F(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\lambda}_{i}}\!\left[{\max\{0,x-v_{i}\}}\right]
is a constant determined solely by the prior
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
.

Now, consider the second term. Having fixed
\underline{s}=\hat{v}-K(L)
to match the mean, the integral of the posterior CDF can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\int_{\underline{v}}^{x}G(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\mathbb{E}_{g}\!\left[{\max\{0,x-s_{\ell}\}}\right].

This expression is a continuous function of the interval length
L
. As
L\to 0
, the support of
g
shrinks to the singleton
\{\hat{v}\}
, meaning
g
converges weakly to the Dirac measure
\delta_{\hat{v}}
. Consequently, for any
x<\hat{v}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to 0}\mathbb{E}_{g}\!\left[{\max\{0,x-s_{\ell}\}}\right]=\max\{0,x-\hat{v}\}=0~.

Since
\hat{v}
is the mean of the prior
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
, the point mass
\delta_{\hat{v}}
constitutes a Mean-Preserving Contraction (MPC) of
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
. By the strict convexity of the max function, for any non-degenerate prior, the inequality holds strictly for small
L
. Therefore, by continuity, there exists a sufficiently small
L>0
such that the SOSD condition is satisfied, implying that
g
can be induced by
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
.

We are now ready to construct the local information structure
\pi^{(i)}
.
As the distribution
g
is Bayes-plausible under the modified prior
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
, there exists a martingale coupling between
v_{i}\sim\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
and
x_{i}\sim g
:
equivalently, there are conditional distributions
\{Q_{t}\}_{t\in\bar{S}}\in\Delta(V)
such that

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{t\in\bar{S}}g(t)\,Q_{t}(v_{i})=\widehat{\lambda}_{i}(v_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{Q_{t}}\!\left[{v_{i}}\right]=t\ \ \text{for all }t\in\bar{S}~.

We construct the joint distribution
\pi^{(i)}
as follows:
We first sample
s\sim\phi^{(i)}
and set
t=\textsc{secmax}(s)
, then we sample
v_{i}\sim Q_{t}
, and finally, we sample
v_{-i}
from the conditional distribution of
\widehat{\lambda}^{(i)}(\cdot\mid v_{i})
independently of
s
given
v_{i}
.
That is, for any
v\in V^{(i)}
and
s\in\bar{S}^{(i)}
:

\displaystyle\pi^{(i)}(v,s)

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}\!\left[{v\mid s}\right]\cdot\phi^{(i)}(s)=\text{Pr}\!\left[{(v_{i},v_{-i})\mid s}\right]\cdot\phi^{(i)}(s)=\text{Pr}\!\left[{v_{i}\mid s}\right]\cdot\text{Pr}\!\left[{v_{-i}\mid s,v_{i}}\right]\cdot\phi^{(i)}(s)~,

By the construction we have that
v_{-i}
is independent of
s
given
v_{i}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\pi^{(i)}(v,s)

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}\!\left[{v_{i}\mid s}\right]\cdot\text{Pr}\!\left[{v_{-i}\mid s,v_{i}}\right]\cdot\phi^{(i)}(s)

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}\!\left[{v_{i}\mid s}\right]\cdot\text{Pr}\!\left[{v_{-i}\mid v_{i}}\right]\cdot\phi^{(i)}(s)

\displaystyle=Q_{\textsc{secmax}(s)}(v_{i})\cdot\widehat{\lambda}^{(i)}(v_{-i}\mid v_{i})\cdot\phi^{(i)}(s)~.

By construction, the
s
marginal is
\phi^{(i)}
, the
v
marginal is
\widehat{\lambda}^{(i)}
, and for all
s
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{(i)}}\!\left[{v_{i}\mid s}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{v_{i}}v_{i}\cdot\frac{\sum\nolimits_{v_{-i}}\pi^{(i)}((v_{i},v_{-i}),s)}{\phi^{(i)}(s)}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{v_{i}}v_{i}\cdot\frac{\sum\nolimits_{v_{-i}}Q_{\textsc{secmax}(s)}(v_{i})\cdot\widehat{\lambda}^{(i)}(v_{-i}\mid v_{i})\cdot\phi^{(i)}(s)}{\phi^{(i)}(s)}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{v_{i}}v_{i}\cdot Q_{\textsc{secmax}(s)}(v_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\textsc{secmax}(s)}}\!\left[{v_{i}}\right]=\textsc{secmax}(s)~.

which satisfies condition Eqn. (
5
).
Moreover, for all
s\in\hat{S}^{(i)}
, since
v\in V^{(i)}
and only bidder
i
has the highest signal and (
5
) holds, we have that the signal profile
s
satisfies conditions
No-Rent Winner
and
Winner-Dominance
.
∎

Thus, the

marginal is uniform on

We define

for all

Proof of

With
Lemma

A.1
, it suffices to consider truthful bidding as the equilibrium for all information structures.
We proceed by contradiction. Suppose there exists a private private information structure
\mathcal{I}
that admits an equilibrium
\sigma
(where
\sigma_{i}(s_{i})=s_{i}
) such that the bidders’ aggregate surplus equals the efficient surplus
\overline{\textsc{WEL}}
. Since the total social surplus is the sum of bidder surplus and seller revenue, the assumption that bidders capture the entire efficient surplus implies that the seller’s expected revenue must be zero.
Let
S_{i}
denote the support of the marginal signal distribution for bidder
i
. We consider three exhaustive cases regarding the signal structures:

•

Case 1: Two or more bidders have positive signal support.

Suppose there exist distinct bidders
i,j
whose marginal distributions assign positive mass to strictly positive signals. Let
P_{k}\triangleq\text{Pr}\!\left[{s_{k}>0}\right]>0
for
k\in\{i,j\}
.
By the independence, the joint distribution is the product of marginals. Consequently, the event where both bidders simultaneously receive positive signals,
\mathcal{E}\triangleq\{s:s_{i}>0\text{ and }s_{j}>0\}
, occurs with strictly positive probability:

\text{Pr}\!\left[{\mathcal{E}}\right]=P_{i}\cdot P_{j}>0.

For any realization
s\in\mathcal{E}
, we have
\textsc{secmax}(s)\geq\min\{s_{i},s_{j}\}>0
.
The seller’s expected revenue is thus strictly positive:

\textsc{Rev}\geq\int_{\mathcal{E}}\textsc{secmax}(s)\,\mathrm{d}\phi(s)>0.

This contradicts the requirement for
\textsc{BS}(\mathcal{I},\sigma)=\overline{\textsc{WEL}}
.

•

Case 2: Exactly one bidder possesses non-zero signals

Suppose there exists exactly one bidder
i
such that
\mathrm{supp}(S_{i})\not\subseteq\{0\}
, while for all other bidders
j\neq i
, the signal distribution is degenerate at zero (i.e.,
\text{Pr}\!\left[{s_{j}=0}\right]=1
).
Since valuations are not purely common values and the prior is symmetric, there exists a set of valuation profiles with positive measure where bidder
i
does not hold the highest valuation. Specifically, let
\mathcal{E}=\{v:v_{i}<\max_{k\neq i}v_{k}\}
. By symmetry and the non-common value assumption,
\text{Pr}\!\left[{\mathcal{E}}\right]>0
.
Consider any profile
v\in\mathcal{E}
. For all bidders
j\neq i
, the signal is deterministically
s_{j}=0
. Bidder
i
, however, receives a signal
s_{i}\geq 0
. Consequently, bidder
i
always receives a signal that is at least as high as any other bidder’s signal (
s_{i}\geq\max_{j\neq i}s_{j}=0
).
Thus, bidder
i
wins the item with strictly positive probability, which violates efficiency.

•

Case 3: All bidders possess only the zero signal.

Suppose
S_{i}=\{0\}
for all
i
. The allocation is necessarily state-independent. Since the efficient winner varies with
v
(due to non-common values), efficiency is trivially violated.

In summary, any private private information structure either yields positive seller revenue (Case 1) or fails to implement the efficient allocation(Cases 2 and 3). Thus, no such private private information structure exists.

\displaystyle=\iint_{x>y}(x-y)N(N-1)F(y)^{N-2}\chi_{t,q}(y)\,\mathrm{d}F(x)\,\mathrm{d}F(y)

\displaystyle+\iint_{x>y}(x-\omega(t,q))N(N-1)F(y)^{N-2}(1-\chi_{t,q}(y))\chi_{t,q}(x)\,\mathrm{d}F(x)\,\mathrm{d}F(y).

(19)

The term
\omega(t,q)
is the conditional expectation of maximum values in the low region and is continuous in
(t,q)
. Consequently, the bidder surplus
B(t,q)
is continuous in
(t,q)
.
Consider the trajectory where we vary
t
from
\bar{v}
to
\underline{v}
. At any point
t
with positive probability mass (an atom), we vary
q
from
0
to
1
.
When
t=\bar{v}
(and
q=0
), the high region is empty, so the mechanism extracts full surplus and
B(\bar{v},0)=0
.
When
t=\underline{v}
(and
q=1
), the low region is empty, so the mechanism is the standard second-price auction.
By the Intermediate Value Theorem, for any feasible bidder surplus level, there exists a pair
(t,q)
achieving it. This completes the proof that this family of mechanisms traces the efficient frontier.
∎
