Finite Skew Braces with Characteristically Simple Multiplicative Group
Marco Damele

Abstract
. We study finite skew braces whose multiplicative group is characteristically simple, namely of the form S n S^{n} for a finite simple group S S . Motivated by the strong rigidity phenomena known for skew braces with simple or quasisimple multiplicative group, we investigate to what extent the assumption ( B , ⋅ ) ≅ S n (B,\cdot)\cong S^{n} constrains the additive group. We first consider the two-sided case and prove that if B B is a finite two-sided skew brace with non-abelian characteristically simple multiplicative group, then ( B , + ) ≅ ( B , ⋅ ) (B,+)\cong(B,\cdot) . We then show that a similar rigidity phenomenon persists beyond the two-sided setting: if the additive group is also characteristically simple with the same number of direct factors, say ( B , + ) ≅ T n (B,+)\cong T^{n} for some finite simple group T T , then necessarily ( B , + ) ≅ ( B , ⋅ ) (B,+)\cong(B,\cdot) . Next, we consider the case in which ( B , + ) (B,+) is supersolvable and S S is non-abelian, and prove strong restrictions: S ≅ PSL 2 ⁡ ( 7 ) S\cong\PSL_{2}(7) , the additive group has a quotient isomorphic to S 3 S_{3} , and it contains a non-trivial elementary abelian 3 3 -subgroup with large centralizer. In particular, if ( B , ⋅ ) ≅ S × S (B,\cdot)\cong S\times S , then ( B , + ) (B,+) cannot be supersolvable. Finally, we investigate skew braces with characteristically simple additive group and obtain several existence and non-existence results depending on the group-theoretic type of the multiplicative group. These results exhibit a marked asymmetry: while a non-abelian characteristically simple multiplicative group imposes strong rigidity on the skew brace, the corresponding condition on the additive group leads to a substantially more flexible existence theory.

1.
Introduction

Skew braces, introduced by Guarnieri and Vendramin in
[
14
]
, provide a natural algebraic framework for the study of set-theoretic solutions of the Yang–Baxter equation. A
skew brace
is a triple
(B,+,\cdot)
such that
(B,+)
and
(B,\cdot)
are groups satisfying

a\cdot(b+c)=a\cdot b-a+a\cdot c\qquad\text{for all }a,b,c\in B.

The groups
(B,+)
and
(B,\cdot)
are called the
additive group
and the
multiplicative group
of
B
, respectively. A basic structural problem in the theory of skew braces is to understand which pairs of groups can occur as the additive and multiplicative groups of the same skew brace, and more generally to determine how properties of one of these groups constrain the other. In this direction, much more is known when one imposes assumptions on the additive group. A classical result, proved in
[
11
, Theorem 2.15]
, states that if
B
is finite and
(B,+)
is abelian, then
(B,\cdot)
is solvable. Skew braces with cyclic additive group were later studied by Rump in
[
22
]
, who showed in particular that if
(B,+)
is cyclic of odd prime-power order, then
(B,\cdot)
is cyclic as well. The solvability result was subsequently extended to the case in which
(B,+)
is nilpotent in
[
24
]
. These developments gave rise naturally to the broader question of whether a finite skew brace with solvable additive group must also have solvable multiplicative group. This question was first raised by Byott in
[
33
]
in the setting of Hopf–Galois extensions, and was later reformulated by Vendramin in
[
28
]
in the language of skew braces. While the problem remains open in full generality, it has been answered affirmatively for several important classes of skew braces; see for instance
[
13
,
21
,
6
,
18
,
9
]
. In the Lie setting, the analogous question has recently been answered completely in
[
8
]
.

By contrast, deriving structural information on the additive group from assumptions on the multiplicative group is, in general, considerably more delicate. Nevertheless, some important results are known. For example, if
(B,\cdot)
is nilpotent, then
(B,+)
is solvable; if
(B,\cdot)
is cyclic, then
(B,+)
is supersolvable; and if
(B,\cdot)
is abelian, then
(B,+)
is metabelian; see
[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
. More striking rigidity phenomena appear when the multiplicative group is close to being simple. Byott proved in
[
5
]
that if
(B,\cdot)
is simple, then the skew brace is either trivial or almost trivial; see Section 2 for the relevant definitions. This was later strengthened by Tsang in
[
26
]
to the case where
(B,\cdot)
is quasisimple.

The present paper continues this line of research and focuses on skew braces whose multiplicative group is characteristically simple. Thus we consider finite skew braces
B
such that
(B,\cdot)\cong S^{n}
for some finite simple group
S
and some integer
n\geq 1
. Our starting point is the observation that this assumption appears to impose an unexpectedly strong rigidity on the skew brace. In particular, no examples are currently known in which
(B,\cdot)
is non-abelian characteristically simple but
(B,+)
is of a substantially different type. This is in sharp contrast with the abelian case: by
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
there exists a skew brace with multiplicative group
(C_{2})^{3}
and additive group
C_{4}\times C_{2}
, showing that even when the multiplicative group is characteristically simple, the additive group need not be characteristically simple. However, the non-abelian situation appears to be much more rigid. Motivated also by the Lie-theoretic analogue established in
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
, we formulate the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
(B,+,\cdot)
be a skew brace such that
(B,\cdot)\cong S^{n}
for some finite non-abelian simple group
S
and some
n\geq 1
. Then
(B,+)\cong(B,\cdot)
.

According to
[
5
]
, Conjecture
1.1
holds when
n=1
. Our first result related to Conjecture
1.1
concerns the case in which
B
is two-sided, that is,
B
satisfies

(a+b)\cdot c=a\cdot c-c+b\cdot c\qquad\text{for all }a,b,c\in B.

Two-sided skew braces play a distinguished role in the theory, as the additional compatibility between the additive and multiplicative structures makes them considerably more rigid than general skew braces; see
[
31
]
. In particular, several structural features that are difficult to detect in arbitrary skew braces become more accessible in the two-sided setting. This phenomenon is illustrated, for example, by the fact that Vendramin’s conjecture has a positive solution within the class of two-sided skew braces; see
[
32
]
. It is therefore natural to view the two-sided case as a first testing ground for Conjecture
1.1
. Our first main result shows that Conjecture
1.1
holds in this setting:

Theorem A
.

Let
B
be a finite two-sided skew brace. If
(B,\cdot)
is non-abelian and characteristically simple, then
B\cong\mathrm{aTriv}(G)\times\mathrm{Triv}(H)
for some finite groups
G
and
H
. In particular,
(B,+)\cong(B,\cdot)
.

Here, for a finite group
G
,
\mathrm{aTriv}(G)
and
\mathrm{Triv}(G)
denote the almost trivial and trivial skew brace associated with
G
, respectively; see Section
2
for the relevant definitions. The next theorem shows that this rigidity phenomenon is not merely a consequence of the two-sided assumption. Indeed, even in the general setting, if the additive group is also characteristically simple with the same number of direct factors as the multiplicative group, then the two groups must again be isomorphic.

Theorem B
.

Let
B
be a finite skew brace such that
(B,\cdot)\cong S^{n}
for some finite simple group
S
and some
n\geq 1
. If
(B,+)\cong T^{n}
for some finite simple group
T
, then
S\cong T
. In particular,
(B,+)\cong(B,\cdot)
.

Thus, within the class of characteristically simple additive groups of the same length, the multiplicative structure determines the additive one completely. It is worth noting that in Theorem
B
the conclusion cannot, in general, be replaced by the stronger requirement that both groups be equal or opposite to each other; see
[
27
, Theorem 1.3]
.

We next investigate the case where the additive group is supersolvable. Here the rigidity becomes even more pronounced. Our second main result shows that the existence of such skew braces is confined to a very narrow exceptional configuration.

Theorem C
.

Let
(B,+,\cdot)
be a finite skew brace such that
(B,\cdot)\cong S^{n}
for some non-abelian simple group
S
and some
n\geq 1
. If
(B,+)
is supersolvable, then the following hold:

(1)

S\cong\PSL_{2}(7)
;

(2)

(B,+)
has a quotient isomorphic to
S_{3}
;

(3)

there exists a non-trivial characteristic
3
-elementary abelian subgroup
E\leq(B,+)
with
E\cong C_{3}^{r}
for some
1\leq r\leq n
such that

\PSL_{2}(7)^{\,n-r+1}\leq C_{(B,+)}(E).

Theorem
C
shows that the supersolvable case is subject to sharp obstructions: not only is the simple factor
S
forced to be
\PSL_{2}(7)
, but the additive group must also satisfy severe internal restrictions. Although we do not exclude this situation in full generality, we prove that it cannot occur in the first non-trivial product case.

Theorem D
.

Let
(B,+,\cdot)
be a finite skew brace such that
(B,\cdot)\cong S\times S
for some non-abelian simple group
S
. Then
(B,+)
is not supersolvable.

Taken together, Theorems
A
,
B
,
C
and
D
strongly support the view that skew braces with non-abelian characteristically simple multiplicative group are governed by a genuine rigidity phenomenon: fixing the multiplicative group imposes strong constraints on the additive one, and in several natural situations forces it to be either isomorphic to the multiplicative group or to lie in an extremely restricted exceptional class.

In the final part of the paper we show that the picture changes substantially when the direction of the problem is reversed. More precisely, if one fixes the additive group to be characteristically simple, then skew braces appear to exhibit much less rigidity and significantly fewer obstructions. Indeed, in this setting one can realize a much broader range of multiplicative groups. Our last theorem summarizes the existence and non-existence results obtained in this direction.

Theorem
E
highlights a marked asymmetry between the two directions of the problem. When the multiplicative group is non-abelian characteristically simple, the skew brace appears to be highly constrained and only very few configurations seem possible. By contrast, when the additive group is assumed to be characteristically simple, the situation is much more flexible: several classes of multiplicative groups can occur, and the corresponding existence theory exhibits far fewer obstructions. In this sense, the paper reveals a substantial difference between the structural roles of the additive and multiplicative groups in skew brace theory.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we collect some basic definitions and results that will be used throughout the paper. We first recall the notions concerning skew braces, and then some standard group-theoretic terminology and notation.

2.1.
Skew braces

Let
B
be a skew brace. The additive and multiplicative group of
B
are related by the so-called lambda map

\lambda\colon(B,\cdot)\longrightarrow\Aut(B,+),\qquad b\longmapsto\lambda_{b},

where

\lambda_{b}(a)=-b+b\cdot a\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in B.

It is well known that
\lambda
is a group homomorphism
[
14
]
. Moreover, the two operations can be expressed in terms of each other as

a+b=a\cdot\lambda_{a}^{-1}(b),\qquad a\cdot b=a+\lambda_{a}(b).

As a consequence, if
H
is a characteristic subgroup of
(B,+)
, then
(H,\cdot)\leq(B,\cdot)
. Also,
\ker(\lambda)\leq(B,+)
.

Given a skew brace
(B,+,\cdot)
, one can replace the additive group
(B,+)
by its opposite group
(B,+^{\operatorname{opp}})
, where

a+^{\operatorname{opp}}b=b+a\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in B,

and obtain a new skew brace

B^{\operatorname{opp}}=(B,+^{\operatorname{opp}},\cdot).

This is called the
opposite skew brace
of
B
; see
[
17
]
. If
\lambda_{b}^{\operatorname{opp}}
denotes the lambda map of
B^{\operatorname{opp}}
, then for every
a\in B
one has

\lambda_{b}^{\operatorname{opp}}(a)=ba-b\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in B.

The skew brace
B
is said to be
trivial
if

ab=a+b\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in B.

We denote with
\mathrm{Triv}(G)
a trivial skew brace with additive group
G
.
The opposite skew brace
B^{\operatorname{opp}}
of a trivial skew brace is called
almost trivial
; equivalently,

ab=b+a\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in B.

We denote with
\mathrm{aTriv}(G)
an almost trivial skew brace with additive group the opposite group of
G
. A subset
I\subseteq B
is called an
ideal
if it is
\lambda
-invariant and is a normal subgroup of both
(B,+)
and
(B,\cdot)
. In this case one can form the quotient skew brace
B/I
, whose additive and multiplicative operations are induced by those of
B
.

The so-called
star product
of a skew brace
B
measures the difference between the additive and multiplicative structures on
B
. It is defined by

a\ast b=-a+ab-b=\lambda_{a}(b)-b\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in B.

Given subsets
X,Y\subseteq B
, we write

X\ast Y:=\langle x\ast y\mid x\in X,\ y\in Y\rangle_{+}

for the subgroup of
(B,+)
generated by all elements
x\ast y
with
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. One checks that a subset
I\subseteq B
is an ideal of
B
if and only if
B\ast I\subseteq I
and
I\ast B\subseteq I
. If
I
is an ideal of
B
, then

(a+I)\ast(b+I)=(a\ast b)+I\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in B.

It follows that
B
is trivial if and only if
B\ast B=0
. Hence

B^{2}:=B\ast B

is the smallest ideal of
B
such that the quotient
B/B^{2}
is trivial.

Similarly, in the opposite skew brace
B^{\operatorname{opp}}
one defines

a\ast^{\operatorname{opp}}b=-b+ab-a\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in B.

Then
B
is almost trivial if and only if
(B^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}=0
, and
(B^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}
is the smallest ideal of
B
such that the quotient is almost trivial.

Finally, if
B
and
C
are skew braces, we denote by
B\times C
the skew brace whose additive group is
(B,+)\times(C,+)
and whose multiplicative group is
(B,\cdot)\times(C,\cdot)
.

Remark 2.1
.

[
24
, Theorem 2.3]

Recall that an
exact factorization
of a finite group
G
is a decomposition

G=AB

with
A,B\leq G
and
A\cap B=1
. Given such a factorization, one can construct a skew brace structure on
G
as follows. Every element
x\in G
can be written uniquely in the form

x=ab\qquad(a\in A,\ b\in B),

and one defines a new operation
\circ
on
G
by

x\circ y=ayb\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in G.

Then
(G,\circ)
is a group isomorphic to
A\times B
, and
(G,\cdot,\circ)
is a skew brace whose additive group is
(G,\cdot)
.

2.2.
Finite groups

We now recall some group-theoretic terminology and notation. Let
G
be a group. If
n
is a positive integer, we write
G^{n}
for the direct product of
n
copies of
G
. We denote by
|G|
the order of
G
, and by
C_{n}
the cyclic group of order
n
.

A group is said to be
characteristically simple
if it has no proper non-trivial characteristic subgroups. Equivalently,

G\cong S^{n}

for some simple group
S
.

A group
T
is called a
subquotient
of a group
G
if there exist subgroups
K\trianglelefteq H\leq G
such that

H/K\cong T.

Let
p
be a prime. A subgroup
H\leq G
is called a
p
-complement
of
G
if
[G:H]
is a power of
p
and
p\nmid|H|
.

Throughout the paper, if
q
is a prime power, we denote by
\PSL_{n}(q)
the projective special linear group

\PSL_{n}(q)=\SL_{n}(q)/Z(\SL_{n}(q)).

If
G
is a finite group, we denote by
\Inn(G)
the inner automorphism group of
G
and by
\Out(G)
the outer automorphism group of
G
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
A

We begin with the following lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
B
be a skew brace. Then

\dfrac{(B/B^{2},\cdot)}{(B/B^{2},\cdot)^{\prime}}\cong\dfrac{B}{B^{2}+(B^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}}.

Proof.

Set
Q:=B/B^{2}
. By definition,
Q
is a trivial skew brace, and hence

x\cdot y=x+y\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in Q.

It follows that
Q^{\operatorname{opp}}
is almost trivial. For
x,y\in Q
, the corresponding star operation is given by

x*_{\operatorname{opp}}y=-x+x\cdot_{\mathrm{opp}}y-y.

Since
Q^{\operatorname{opp}}
is almost trivial, we have

x\cdot_{\operatorname{opp}}y=y+x,

and therefore

x*_{\operatorname{opp}}y=-x+(y+x)-y=[y,x].

Thus
(Q^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}
coincides with the commutator subgroup of
(Q,\cdot)
, that is,

(Q^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}=(Q,\cdot)^{\prime}.

Hence

\dfrac{(Q,\cdot)}{(Q,\cdot)^{\prime}}\cong\dfrac{Q}{(Q^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}}.

Now, since
Q=B/B^{2}
, we have

(Q^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}=\dfrac{B^{2}+(B^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}}{B^{2}}.

Therefore,

\dfrac{(B/B^{2},\cdot)}{(B/B^{2},\cdot)^{\prime}}\cong\dfrac{Q}{(Q^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}}\cong\dfrac{B/B^{2}}{\dfrac{B^{2}+(B^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}}{B^{2}}}\cong\dfrac{B}{B^{2}+(B^{\operatorname{opp}})^{2}},

as required.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
A

Proof of Theorem
A
.

Let

I:=B^{2}\cap(B^{\mathrm{op}})^{2}.

Since
B
is two-sided, it follows from
[
31
, Theorem 4.3]
that
I
is an ideal of
B
and
(I,+)
is abelian. In particular,
(I,+)
is solvable. Since
I
is an ideal, it is normal in
(B,\cdot)
. Since
(B,\cdot)\cong S^{n}
necessarily

I=1.

Next, since
(B,\cdot)\cong S^{n}
, the group
(B,\cdot)
is perfect, and so is every quotient of
(B,\cdot)
. In particular,

(B/B^{2},\cdot)

is perfect. On the other hand, by Lemma
3.1
one has

\dfrac{(B/B^{2},\cdot)}{(B/B^{2},\cdot)^{\prime}}\cong\dfrac{B}{B^{2}+(B^{\mathrm{op}})^{2}}.

Therefore,

\dfrac{(B/B^{2},\cdot)}{(B/B^{2},\cdot)^{\prime}}=1,

and so

\dfrac{B}{B^{2}+(B^{\mathrm{op}})^{2}}=1.

Hence

B=B^{2}+(B^{\mathrm{op}})^{2}.

Moreover, both
B^{2}
and
(B^{\mathrm{op}})^{2}
are ideals of
B
. Hence

B\cong B^{2}\times(B^{\mathrm{op}})^{2}

as skew braces. Since

B/B^{2}\cong(B^{\mathrm{op}})^{2}

as skew braces, it follows that
(B^{\mathrm{op}})^{2}
is trivial. Similarly

B/(B^{\mathrm{op}})^{2}\cong B^{2}

as skew braces, it follows that
B^{2}
is almost trivial.

Therefore there exist groups
G
and
H
such that

B^{2}\cong\mathrm{aTriv}(G),\qquad(B^{\mathrm{op}})^{2}\cong\mathrm{Triv}(H),

and hence

B\cong\mathrm{aTriv}(G)\times\mathrm{Triv}(H).

It follows that

(B,\cdot)\cong G\times H,\qquad(B,+)\cong G^{\mathrm{op}}\times H.

Since every group is canonically isomorphic to its opposite group via
g\mapsto g^{-1}
, we conclude that

(B,+)\cong G^{\mathrm{op}}\times H\cong G\times H\cong(B,\cdot).

This proves the claim.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
B

In the proof of Theorem
B
we shall use the following standard facts about finite simple groups.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
T
be a finite non-abelian simple group and let
n
be a positive integer. Then:

(1)

|\Out(T)|<|T|
;
[
29
, Corollary 12]

(2)

\Aut(T^{n})\cong\Aut(T)^{n}\rtimes\Sym(n)
;
[
30
, Prop 1.1.20]

Let
\mu(G)
denote the minimal degree of a faithful permutation representation of a finite group
G
.

Lemma 4.2
.

[
10
, Theorem 3.1]
.
If
S
is a finite non-abelian simple group, then
\mu(S^{n})=n\mu(S)
.

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
B
.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

If
T
is abelian, then
T\cong C_{p}
and
(B,\cdot)
is a
p
-group. Hence
S\cong C_{p}\cong T
. We may therefore assume that
T
is non-abelian. Then
S
is also non-abelian. Our goal is to show that
S\cong T
. Assume, for contradiction, that
S\not\cong T
, and let
B
be a counterexample. Since
(B,\cdot)\cong S^{n}
and
(B,+)\cong T^{n}
, we have

|T|^{n}=|B|=|S|^{n},

and therefore
|S|=|T|
. Let
\lambda
be the lambda map of
B
, and set

N:=\ker(\lambda).

Since
N\trianglelefteq(B,\cdot)\cong S^{n}
, it follows that
N\cong S^{m}
for some
0\leq m\leq n
. Suppose that
N\neq 1
. Since
N\leq(B,+)\cong T^{n}
, there exists an embedding

\iota\colon S\hookrightarrow T^{n}.

For each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
\pi_{j}\colon T^{n}\to T
be the projection onto the
j
th factor, and consider the composition
\pi_{j}\circ\iota
. Since
S
is simple, this map is either trivial or injective. If it is injective, then its image has order
|S|=|T|
, and hence must coincide with
T
. This yields
S\cong T
, a contradiction. Therefore
\pi_{j}\circ\iota
is trivial for every
j
, which is impossible. We conclude that
N=1
. Thus we have an embedding

\lambda\colon(B,\cdot)\hookrightarrow\Aut(B,+).

Since
(B,+)\cong T^{n}
, theorem
4.1
gives

\Aut(B,+)\cong\Aut(T)^{n}\rtimes\Sym(n).

Let

A:=\Aut(T)^{n}.

Then
A\triangleleft\Aut(B,+)
. Set

L:=\lambda(B,\cdot)\leq\Aut(B,+).

Since
\lambda
is injective and
(B,\cdot)\cong S^{n}
, we have

L\cong S^{n}.

Consider

M:=L\cap A.

Because
A\triangleleft\Aut(B,+)
, we get

M\triangleleft L.

Hence, since
L\cong S^{n}
, there exists
0\leq m\leq n
such that

M\cong S^{m}.

Suppose that
M\neq 1
. Then
M
contains a subgroup isomorphic to
S
, and therefore there exists an embedding

\iota\colon S\hookrightarrow A\cong\Aut(T)^{n}.

For each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

\rho_{j}\colon\Aut(T)^{n}\to\Aut(T)

be the projection onto the
j
th factor. Since
S
is simple, each composition

\rho_{j}\circ\iota\colon S\to\Aut(T)

is either trivial or injective. If all of them were trivial, then
\iota
would be trivial, a contradiction. Hence for some
j
, the map
\rho_{j}\circ\iota
is injective, and thus there is an embedding
\psi:S\rightarrow\Aut(T)
. Now
\Inn(T)\cong T
is a normal subgroup of
\Aut(T)
. Therefore, by the second isomorphism theorem,

Proof of Theorem

We begin with a number-theoretic lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f,n,t\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
and let
p
be a prime. If

p^{fn}-1=2^{t}(p^{f}-1),

then

f=1,\qquad t=3,\qquad n=2.

Proof.

Reducing modulo
2
we see that
p
has to be odd. Dividing both sides by
p^{f}-1
, we obtain

\frac{p^{fn}-1}{p^{f}-1}=2^{t}.

The left-hand side is a geometric series of
n
terms with ratio
p^{f}
:

(p^{f})^{n-1}+(p^{f})^{n-2}+\cdots+p^{f}+1=2^{t}.

Since
p^{f}
is odd, each term of the sum is odd. Hence, in order for the sum to be a power of
2
, the number of terms
n
must be even. Let
n=2k
. Then

p^{2fk}-1=(p^{fk}-1)(p^{fk}+1),

so that the equation becomes

\frac{(p^{fk}-1)(p^{fk}+1)}{p^{f}-1}=2^{t}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\left(\frac{p^{fk}-1}{p^{f}-1}\right)(p^{fk}+1)=2^{t}.

For the product of two integers to be a power of
2
, both factors must themselves be powers of
2
. In particular, we must have

2^{s}-p^{fk}=1

for some integer
s\leq t
. By the Catalan–Mihăilescu theorem (see the main theorem of
[
23
]
) we must have
fk=1
. Hence
f=1
,
k=1
, and
n=2
.
∎

As a consequence we obtain the following characterization of

.

We recall some basic properties of the function

Lemma 5.3

Let

and let

(1)

(2)

If

which is Legendre’s formula.

(3)

Lemma 5.4
.

Let

, let

be an integer with

, and let

e(n)=v_{7}\bigl(|\SL_{n}(3)|\bigr).

Then

Proof.

Recall that

|\SL_{n}(3)|=3^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\prod_{i=2}^{n}(3^{i}-1).

Since

v_{7}\!\left(3^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\right)=0,

it follows that

Because

, we have

if and only if

. Hence, writing

, the lifting-the-exponent lemma yields

Now

Let

By Legendre’s formula,

Using

On the other hand, since

Therefore

1.

(B,\cdot)
supersolvable.
Let
G=\PSL_{2}(7)

(see

contains a subgroup
H
isomorphic to the Frobenius group
C_{7}\rtimes C_{3}
of order

K

D_{8}

|H|

|K|

H\cap K=1
. It follows that

|HK|=\frac{|H|\,|K|}{|H\cap K|}=21\cdot 8=168=|G|,

and therefore

G=HK

is an exact factorization of
G

B

\PSL_{2}(7)

D_{8}\times(C_{7}\rtimes C_{3}).

Since both
D_{8}

C_{7}\rtimes C_{3}

B
is a skew brace with additive group
\PSL_{2}(7)
and supersolvable multiplicative group. Finally, taking direct products, we obtain for every positive integer

\PSL_{2}(7)^{n}

(B,\cdot)
solvable.
By Remark

A_{5}=A_{4}\cdot C_{5}

B_{1}

(B_{1},+)\cong A_{5}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(B_{1},\cdot)\cong A_{4}\times C_{5}.

It follows that the direct product
B_{1}\times B_{1}

(B_{1}\times B_{1},+)\cong A_{5}\times A_{5},

(B_{1}\times B_{1},\cdot)\cong(A_{4}\times C_{5})\times(A_{4}\times C_{5}),

3.

(B,\cdot)
characteristically simple.
Let
S
be a finite non-abelian simple group, and set

G=S\times S.

Consider the subgroups

G_{1}=\{(x,x)\mid x\in S\},\qquad G_{2}=\{(1,x)\mid x\in S\}.

Then
G_{1}\cong S
and
G_{2}\cong S
, and
G_{1}\cap G_{2}=1
. Observe that
G=G_{1}G_{2}
. Indeed, for
(a,b)\in G
one has

(a,b)=(a,a)(1,a^{-1}b),

where
(a,a)\in G_{1}
and
(1,a^{-1}b)\in G_{2}
. Thus Remark
2.1
yields a skew brace
B=(G,\cdot,\circ)
, where
(G,\cdot)
is the original group
G
, and the circle operation is defined as follows. Writing

(a,b)=(a,a)(1,a^{-1}b),

for
(c,d)\in G
one has

(a,b)\circ(c,d)=(a,a)(c,d)(1,a^{-1}b),

and therefore

(a,b)\circ(c,d)=(ac,ad)(1,a^{-1}b)=(ac,ada^{-1}b).

Thus
B
is a skew brace with additive group

(B,+)=(G,\cdot)\cong S\times S,

and multiplicative group

(B,\cdot)\cong(G,\circ)\cong G_{1}\times G_{2}\cong S\times S.

Finally, this skew brace is non-trivial. Indeed, if it were trivial, then one would have

(a,b)\circ(c,d)=(ac,bd)\qquad\text{for all }(a,b),(c,d)\in G,

whereas here

(a,b)\circ(c,d)=(ac,ada^{-1}b).

Since
S
is non-abelian, one can choose
a,d\in S
such that
ada^{-1}\neq d
, and then

(a,1)\circ(1,d)=(a,ada^{-1})\neq(a,d)=(a,1)(1,d).

Therefore the skew brace
B
is non-trivial.

Remark 7.1

It is worth pointing out that, in the Lie setting, the question of whether there exists a Lie skew brace with simple additive group and perfect multiplicative group is still open. A positive answer in that context would yield a post-Lie algebra structure

[

[

]
for the connection between Lie skew braces and post-Lie algebras. By contrast, the skew brace
B_{2}
constructed in the proof of Theorem
