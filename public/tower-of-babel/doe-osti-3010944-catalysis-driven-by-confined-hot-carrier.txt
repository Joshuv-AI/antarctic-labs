1.0 Abstract
In chemical industry controlling the activity and selectivity of a chemical conversion process is of immense
importance. Traditionally, dissipation of thermal energy drives transformation of reactants, which may lead to
formation of a variety of products; it remains a key challenge to design a process that dissociation and formation
of specific chemical bonds can be triggered to form certain desirable products with very high selectivity, preferably
at low temperatures. Introduction of light-sensitive solid materials into catalysis provides a powerful, alternate
strategy for this purpose. In this light-driven process, highly energetic charge carriers, which are generated
through photoexcitation of coinage metal nanoparticles, stimulate the dynamics of chemical bonds in the
reactants. The reaction selectivity can be boosted by manipulating the interplay between the photons, the light-
sensitive solid materials, and the reactants. The objective of this research was to provide fundamental
understanding of these light-driven bond dynamics at the molecular level. We further proposed to control
precisely both energies and locations of the positive and negative charges by positioning them in varied reaction
environments, including microporous materials. The robust, fundamental theoretical investigations not only
provided explanation for many experimental observations but also delivered essential details that could allow for
design of an all-optical process of chemical transformation at low temperatures with high chemical selectivity.

2.0 Background
The goal of this project was to develop fundamental understanding of the non-equilibrium interfacial reactions in
plasmonic catalysis and to achieve high activity and selectivity that could not be achieved in conventional thermal
catalysis. This Early Career project started in 2019 and ended in 2025 with 20 published papers that acknowledged
this grant. In addition, two PhD dissertations supported by this grant were completed at the University of
Oklahoma. Through the career project, two graduate students graduated with a PhD degree in chemical
engineering; one is continuing her research career in industry, and the other is working at Idaho National Lab as a
postdoc and plans to continue her career as a staff scientist. A new PhD student has been recruited in 2024, and
she is working on photocatalysis using DFT calculations and TDDFT simulations. Four postdocs have been involved.
Two completed their postdoctoral research, and the other two are still in the group, working on the next stage of
this project. This project also enabled collaboration with several experimental groups (UCSB, UCF, WVU, Helsinki).

3.0 Project Objectives
Plasmon excitation has been widely used in surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS).1,2 In plasmonic
nanoparticles, highly energetic and nonthermal charge carriers (so-called “hot” electrons and holes) can be
generated through the non-radiative decay of a collective oscillation of the conduction electrons. This oscillation,
resonant with the frequency of incident light, is known as a localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR). Hot
carriers generated via plasmon decay have shown valuable applications in solar cells, sensors, photodetectors,
and catalysis. Here, the term “hot” refers to non-equilibrium distribution of electrons and holes with respect to
the lattice temperature of the nanoparticles. For application in catalysis, which is the focus of this project, the
non-thermalized charge carriers provide possibilities for low-temperature and ambient-pressure catalytic
processes, which are ideal for distributed chemical conversion.3 They can also disrupt the reaction network by
changing the kinetics of molecular adsorption, reaction, diffusion, and desorption. 4, 5-7

 Page 1 of 8

 To rationally design a plasmonic reaction system with high activity and selectivity, some fundamental challenges
 remain to be addressed. For example, it remains challenging to probe and control the charge and energy transfer
 dynamics at the interfaces and tune the inherently coupled physicochemical process between the electronic and
 atomic degrees of freedom. In this project we applied first-principles computational approaches to simulate the
 interfacial reactions in the presence of non-equilibrium charge carriers, through which we developed ways to
 control their location and channel the photonic energy into desirable molecular orbitals for targeted chemical
 transformations.

More recently, we investigated photo-induced CO desorption in collaboration with an experimental group at
UCSB, and our calculations showed that the Pt–CO bond exhibited a narrower LUMO of adsorbed CO (indicating
extended lifetime of the excited state), stronger coupling between the photoexcitation and forces induced on the
metal–carbon bond, and vibrational energy dissipation that more effectively couples to desorption than Pd–CO.
The calculated excitation values (2.8-3.0 eV, independent on the adsorption configurations of CO on Pt and Pd)
agree with experiments (2.8 eV).7 We also demonstrated distinct response of CO desorption on Pt and Pd under
light illumination and attributed it to different molecular adsorption site, orbital overlap, and electron-vibration
coupling. Intramolecular excitation was previously proposed to explain both systems (plasmonic CO desorption
and S–S bond cleavage in dimethyl disulfide) in the literature, but our work suggested both could be explained by
plasmonic interfacial charge transfer.

One of the key advantages of plasmonic catalysis is the potential of selective channeling of photon energy into
different reaction pathways, leading to varied chemical selectivity. As an example, we investigated
phenylacetylene hydrogenation as a model reaction and showed that localized interaction between the AgPt
surface and the CºC bond in phenylacetylene enables selective hydrogenation of the CºC bond to form styrene
under visible light together with an experimental group at the University of Helsinki.6 More recently we
investigated the same reaction over AgPd nanoparticles and found that visible-light irradiation could be employed
to switch the reaction pathway from hydrogenation of phenylacetylene to homocoupling between two
phenylacetylene (Figure 3). This is rather surprising. To provide a fundamental understanding of this selectivity
change, we calculated the activation energies for the hydrogenation pathway, which leads to the formation of
styrene and ethylbenzene, and the homocoupling pathway, producing 1,4-diphenylbutadiyne. We found that the
first hydrogenation step of phenylacetylene is the rate-limiting step of the hydrogenation pathway. Instead, for
the homocoupling reaction, it starts with dehydrogenation of phenylacetylene. The activation energy of the
dehydrogenation step is 0.3 eV higher than hydrogenation. Therefore, hydrogenation is more favorable than the
homocoupling, which agrees with the experimental observation of AgPd catalysts under dark conditions.

5.0 Conclusions
Our previous calculations based on the ΔSCF approach provided explanation for several experimental
observations of enhanced rates and/or improved selectivity of several reactions (e.g., CO2 reduction, NH3
decomposition, phenylacetylene hydrogenation and homocoupling) and identified the direct interfacial charge
transfer as an important mechanism for plasmonic catalysis. This project lays foundation for developing efficient
and robust plasmonic catalysis for chemical transformation. However, ultrafast dynamics of the energetic carriers
and how they couple with the atomic dynamics have not been fully addressed, as the linear expansion ΔSCF
calculations cannot describe the time evolution of the interfacial charge transfer and energy dissipation. In future
works, dynamics of non-thermalized charge carriers should be revealed in time-dependent approaches, and we
are currently working on it.
