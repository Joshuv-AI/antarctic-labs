Dynamical symmetries of the Calogero–Coulomb model
Tigran Hakobyan

Abstract
We construct the dynamical symmetry of the quantum Calogero model with particle exchange in a confining Coulomb field. This symmetry is governed by the algebra s ​ o ​ ( N + 1 , 2 ) so(N+1,2) , deformed by exchange (Dunkl) operators, with its invariant sector generated by the Dunkl angular momentum tensor and the modified Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector. The equidistant analogue of the Hamiltonian, with a linear spectrum, is expressed in terms of the conformal subalgebra s ​ o ​ ( 1 , 2 ) so(1,2) . In addition, the wave functions of the Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian are classified into infinite-dimensional lowest-weight s ​ o ​ ( 1 , 2 ) so(1,2) multiplets.



I
Introduction

Exactly solvable systems play a fundamental role in quantum physics, providing explicit realizations of hidden symmetries and serving as benchmarks for nonperturbative methods.
Among these systems, the Calogero model, which describes one-dimensional identical particles with inverse-square interactions, is a prominent example
9
;
10
;
48
.
More general inverse-square potentials based on finite reflection groups,
as well as hyperbolic, trigonometric, and elliptic functions, spin-exchange interactions,
and related extensions, are also integrable (see the reviews
49
;
50
;
24
;
1

and references therein).
Moreover, the rational Calogero models possess a complete set of constants of motion, rendering them maximally superintegrable
60
;
44
;
32
. The (super)integrability is robust under the inclusion of certain external potentials
and persists for the angular parts of the associated Hamiltonians, as well as for systems defined on surfaces of constant curvature
33
;
36
;
27
;
25
;
26
.

On the other hand, the inverse-square interaction potential complicates the structure of Calogero models
but does not affect the (super)integrability of the system
33
.
For instance, the full symmetry of the Calogero model — originally formulated with an oscillator
confining potential — can be regarded as a
g
deformation of the
u(N)
unitary algebra,
which encodes the invariants of the
N
-dimensional isotropic oscillator
57
;
25
;
see also Refs.
29
;
59
for a quantum-group extension.
Here, the parameter
g
specifies the coupling constant of the inverse‑square interaction.
This correspondence extends to the dynamical symmetry, which encompasses
also ladder operators that raise or lower states along the energy scale
38
.

It is well known that the
N
-dimensional confining Coulomb model is also superintegrable
characterized by the symmetry group
SO(N+1)
, generated by the angular momentum and the
Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector. The spectrum of the quantum system, however, is not equidistant.
At first glance, this appears to preclude the existence of spectrum-generating
operators. Nevertheless, a modified system can be constructed that shares the same stationary
states while exhibiting a linear spectrum.
The equidistant Hamiltonian possesses a dynamical symmetry described by the conformal group

SO(N+1,2)
in
(N+1)
-dimensional Minkowski space.
This structure was first demonstrated in the case of the hydrogen atom
3
;
2
.

The energy spectrum of the Coulomb model with a rational Calogero potential,
is known to possess a huge degeneracy
43
.
At the same time, the Calogero–Coulomb system,
including its extensions to constant-curvature spaces, is superintegrable

33
.
The corresponding constants of motion, including an analogue of
the Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector, have been constructed
36
;
16
;
17
.
Moreover, the complete set of conserved quantities of the (confining) quantum Calogero–Coulomb
problem forms a
g
-deformation of the
so(N+1)
algebra
26
.

In this article, we construct and analyze the dynamical symmetries of the quantum Calogero–Coulomb system.
Following many of the earlier developments in Calogero-type models, our approach employs the
exchange (Dunkl) operator formalism, which incorporates particle-exchange effects with coupling constant
g

into covariant differential
20
. This formulation provides a powerful and algebraically transparent framework for systematically deriving solutions, integrals of motion, symmetry algebras, and ladder operators.

We demonstrate that the dynamical symmetry (spectrum-generating symmetry) of the
quantum Calogero–Coulomb system is described by an exchange-operator deformation of the conformal algebra

so(N+1,2)
. Within this structure, a three-dimensional conformal subalgebra
so(1,2)
emerges,
which contains the modified Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian with linear spectrum.
For the modified system, we construct a deformed Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector, distinct
from the analogous vector obtained for the original model
36
. Together with the
Dunkl angular momentum, this vector generates a complete symmetry algebra of the equidistant
Calogero–Coulomb model.
This algebra represents an exchange-operator deformation of the
so(N+1)
algebra,
with its Casimir element expressed in terms of the Hamiltonian, and it retains a structure closely related to that of the original system
26
.

The commutation relations among the dynamical symmetry generators are derived.
They incorporate particle-exchange operators directly into the structure constants
and are expressed compactly in matrix form.
Furthermore, we establish the equivalence between the quadratic Casimir elements of
the
so(1,2)
and Dunkl angular momentum algebras,
and we calculate the numerical value of the Casimir element of the dynamical symmetry algebra.

Finally, we construct the wave functions of the Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian,
both with and without particle exchange. These wave functions are classified
according to the infinite-dimensional lowest-weight irreducible representation of the

so(1,2)
conformal algebra.
The conformal spin of the multiplet is parameterized by the orbital quantum number,
while the states are labeled by the radial quantum number.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Sect.
II
, we briefly review the Calogero–Coulomb model and
its symmetries within the Dunkl-operator formalism.
In Sect.
III
, we construct and analyze the
so(1,2)
conformal subalgebra
together with an equidistant analogue of the Calogero–Coulomb
Hamiltonian.
In Sect.
IV
, we develop a Dunkl-deformed analogue of the full conformal algebra

so(N+1,2)
, whose rotated realization describes the dynamical symmetry
of the Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian.
In Sect.
V
, using deformed spherical harmonics,
we construct the eigenfunctions of the Calogero–Coulomb system
and study their
so(1,2)
multiplet structure.
The results are summarized and discussed in Sect.
VI
.
Appendix
A
presents the derivation of the commutation relations
among the generators of the dynamical symmetry algebra, while Appendix
B

contains explicit calculations of the squares of three vectors,
including the Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector, that enter the algebra.

II
Calogero–Coulomb system

In this section, we briefly review the exchange-operator formalism and its application to the
symmetries of the Calogero–Coulomb model with particle exchange.

II.1
Hamiltonian via exchange (Dunkl) operators

The Hamiltonian of the
N
-particle
quantum Calogero model in external Coulomb potential

43
has the following form:

\tilde{H}_{\gamma}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m}+\sum_{i<j}\frac{g(g\mp\hbar)}{(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}}-\frac{\gamma}{r},\qquad r=\sqrt{x^{2}},

(1)

where
x^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}
, and the
-/+
sign in the interaction potential corresponds
to identical bosons/fermions, respectively. For
\gamma=0
,
the above Hamiltonian reduces to the unconfined Calogero model
9
;
10
,
also known as the Calogero–Moser system
48
.
Throughout this article, we consider positive coupling constants,
g,\gamma>0
, and set
the mass and Plank’s constant to unity,
m=\hbar=1
.

An elegant approach to quantum Calogero-type models is based on the Dunkl operators,
whose components mutually commute
20
;
see Refs.
40
;
54
;
11
;
21
for reviews:

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}=\partial_{i}-\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{g}{x_{i}-x_{j}}s_{ij},

(2)

\displaystyle[\nabla_{i},\nabla_{j}]=0,\qquad[\nabla_{i},x_{j}]=S_{ij}:=\begin{cases}-gs_{ij}&i\neq j,\\
1+g\sum_{k\neq i}s_{ik}&i=j.\end{cases}

(3)

In the above equations, the operator
s_{ij}
permutes the
i
-th and
j
-th
particles. The Dunkl operator can be viewed as a covariant
derivative with a flat connection and a nonlocal imaginary field with particle
exchanges. Its commutation relations with the coordinates also involve exchange
operators and reduce to the canonical commutation relations in the free-coupling limit,

\lim_{g\to 0}S_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
.

The algebra (
3
) is often referred to in the literature as the

S_{N}
-extended Heisenberg algebra
7
.
The corresponding abstract algebraic structure is known as
the rational Cherednik algebra associated with the symmetric group
14
;
22
;
23
.
The Dunkl operator together with the coordinate generate a particular representation
of this algebra.

Pursuing further the analogy with particle motion in an external field, one can define
a covariant momentum operator
\pi
. The corresponding Hamiltonian includes a Calogero-type potential
that, however, involves particle exchanges
51
;
6
.
In the presence of a Coulomb potential, this leads to the Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian with particle exchange

33
;
36
:

\displaystyle H_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{r}=\frac{p^{2}}{2}+\sum_{i<j}\frac{g(g-s_{ij})}{(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}}-\frac{\gamma}{r},\qquad\pi=-\imath\nabla.

(4)

For identical bosons (
s_{ij}=1
) or fermions (
s_{ij}=-1
), the
Hamiltonian
H_{\gamma}
reduces to the convensional Calogero–Coulomb model (
1
).
Calogero-type Hamiltonians that incorporate particle exchange within the inverse-square interaction
are commonly referred to in the literature as generalized Calogero models.
In this article, we focus primarily on such systems and therefore designate
the particle-exchange Hamiltonian (
4
) simply as the Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian.

Let us briefly outline Dunkl operators and their applications in physics.
The exchange-operator formalism is widely used to establish the quantum integrability of many-body systems
with inverse-square interactions and to construct stationary wave functions algebraically
51
;
6
.
Note that the Calogero model is also integrable via the quantum inverse scattering method

58
, and a direct correspondence between the two approaches has been identified
13
.
A trigonometric extension of exchange operators leads to the Calogero–Sutherland model
39
;
14
, while Dunkl and Cherednik operators admit elliptic generalizations
8
;
15
, which establish the integrability of the elliptic Calogero–Moser model
15
.
For integrable spin chains with long-range interactions, Dunkl operators frozen at equilibrium yield commuting invariants of motion
52
;
30
. These invariants also arise in the semiclassical limit
55
;
47
;
37
. More recently, a general framework for quantum integrable systems on integrable classical backgrounds — emerging naturally in the semiclassical limit — has been developed
46
.
Calogero models with particle and spin exchange exhibit hallmark features of integrability, including Yangian symmetry, a Lax pair formulation, and the presence of both an
R
-matrix and a monodromy matrix satisfying the Yang–Baxter equation
5
;
45
;
12
.

II.2
Symmetries of Calogero–Coulomb model

Using appropriately the covariant momentum (
4
),
one can construct Dunkl-operator deformations
of other physical quantities. In particular, the Dunk angular momentum
tensor
28
;
44
;
25

and Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector
36
are given, respectively, by the following
expressions:

\displaystyle L_{ij}

\displaystyle=x_{i}\pi_{j}-x_{j}\pi_{i},

(5)

\displaystyle A^{\gamma}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\{L_{ij},\pi_{j}\right\}-\frac{\imath}{2}[\pi_{i},S]-\frac{\gamma x_{i}}{r}.

(6)

In the second definition, the (rescaled) invariant element
of the permutation-group algebra appears:

S=\sum_{i<j}S_{ij}=-g\sum_{i<j}s_{ij},\qquad[S,s_{ij}]=0.

(7)

Here, the upper index in
A^{\gamma}_{i}
and the lower index in
H_{\gamma}

indicate the dependence on the Coulomb coupling constant. The Hamiltonian preserves both quantities:

[H_{\gamma},L_{ij}]=[H_{\gamma},A^{\gamma}_{i}]=0.

(8)

In the
g=0
limit, both systems (
1
) and (
4
)
reduce to the
N
-dimensional Coulomb model. Accordingly, the operators
(
5
) and (
6
) reduce to the standard angular momentum
and Laplace–Runge–Lenz components.

The commutation relations among the components
L_{ij}
and
A^{\gamma}_{k}

are
44
;
25
;
36
:

\displaystyle[L_{ij},L_{kl}]

\displaystyle=\imath(L_{il}S_{kj}+L_{jk}S_{li}-L_{ik}S_{lj}-L_{jl}S_{ki}),

(9)

\displaystyle[L_{ij},A^{\gamma}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\imath(A^{\gamma}_{i}S_{jk}-A^{\gamma}_{j}S_{ik}),

(10)

\displaystyle[A^{\gamma}_{i},A^{\gamma}_{j}]

\displaystyle=-2\imath H_{\gamma}L_{ij}.

(11)

These are
g
-deformations of the commutations between
the standard angular momentum and Laplace–Runge–Lenz invariants.
However, unlike the
g=0
case, the above relations,
together with (
8
), do not define an abstract Lie algebra by themselves.
To obtain a consistent set of algebra generators, one must also consider the following
algebraic (crossing) relations among them
25
;
26
:

\displaystyle L_{ij}(L_{kl}-S_{kl})+L_{jk}(L_{il}-S_{il})+L_{ki}(L_{jl}-S_{jl})=0,

(12)

\displaystyle L_{ij}A^{\gamma}_{k}+L_{jk}A^{\gamma}_{i}+L_{ki}A^{\gamma}_{j}=0.

(13)

The relations (
10
) and (
13
) remain valid upon replacing

A^{\gamma}_{i}
with
x_{i}
and
\pi_{i}
.

The relations (
9
) and (
12
)
define a quadratic algebra
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N)
, which forms a subalgebra
of the Cherednik algebra. The Dunkl angular momentum
provides its representation
25
; see also the recent work
4
.
The Casimir element of
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N)
corresponds to
the angular part of the Hamiltonian (
4
), denoted below by
H_{\Omega}
. It differs
from the angular momentum squared by an exchange-dependent term
25
:

H_{\Omega}=L^{2}+S(S-N+2),\qquad L^{2}=\sum_{i<j}L_{ij}^{2},\qquad[H_{\Omega},L_{ij}]=0.

(14)

Note also that the square of the vector
A^{\gamma}

can be expressed in terms of the Casimir element and the Hamiltonian:

(A^{\gamma})^{2}=\gamma^{2}+2H_{\gamma}\big(H_{\Omega}-S+{\textstyle\frac{(N-1)^{2}}{4}}\big).

(15)

One can extend
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N)
by one additional dimension by incorporating
the deformed Laplace-–Runge-–Lenz invariant into the extra dimension
and trivially extending the exchange matrix:

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{ij}

\displaystyle=L_{ij},\qquad\tilde{S}_{ij}=S_{ij}\qquad\text{for}\quad i,j\leq N,

(16)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{i\,N+1}

\displaystyle=-\tilde{L}_{N+1\,i}=\frac{A^{\gamma}_{i}}{\sqrt{-2{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{gen}}_{\gamma}}},\qquad\tilde{L}_{N+1\,N+1}=0,

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{i\,N+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{S}_{N+1\,i}=0,\qquad\tilde{S}_{N+1\,N+1}=1.

The extension preserves the symmetries of the two tensors:

\tilde{S}_{ij}=\tilde{S}_{ji}
, and
\tilde{L}_{ij}=-\tilde{L}_{ji}
.
The five relations (
9
)-(
13
) can then
be expressed in the compact forms
26
:

\displaystyle[\tilde{L}_{ij},\tilde{L}_{kl}]=\imath\tilde{L}_{ik}\tilde{S}_{lj}+\imath\tilde{L}_{jl}\tilde{S}_{ki}-\imath\tilde{L}_{il}\tilde{S}_{kj}-\imath\tilde{L}_{jk}\tilde{S}_{li},

(17)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{ij}(\tilde{L}_{kl}-\tilde{S}_{kl})+\tilde{L}_{jk}(\tilde{L}_{il}-\tilde{S}_{il})+\tilde{L}_{ki}(\tilde{L}_{jl}-\tilde{S}_{jl})=0.

(18)

So, the tilded operators form a particular representation of the algebra (
9
) and (
12
) in
(N+1)
-dimensional space, namely the

\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1)
algebra
26
.

Its Casimir element,
\tilde{H}_{\Omega}
, can be expressed in terms of the Hamiltonian
H_{\gamma}
:

\tilde{H}_{\Omega}=\tilde{L}^{2}+S(S-N+1)=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2H_{\gamma}}-\frac{(N-1)^{2}}{4},\qquad[\tilde{H}_{\Omega},\tilde{L}_{ij}]=0.

(19)

Indeed, the first equality follows immediately from the expression for the angular Hamiltonian (
14
) after the substitution
N\to N+1
.
This is a consequence of the fact that the formulas (
16
) define a representation of the

\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1)
algebra. The second equality in (
19
)
follows from Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
).
It relates the eigenvalues of the Casimir element to those of the Hamiltonian (
19
)
and can be inverted as follows:

H_{\gamma}=-\frac{2}{\gamma^{2}}\left[\tilde{H}_{\Omega}+\frac{(N-1)^{2}}{4}\right]^{-1}.

(20)

In the absence of the inverse-square interaction, this reduces to the well-known relation between the
Coulomb Hamiltonian and the Casimir element of its
so(N+1)
symmetry.

III
Conformal algebra and the equidistant Calogero–Coulomb system

III.1
Three-dimensional conformal algebra

First, we introduce the three-dimensional conformal group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})\equiv SO(1,2)\equiv SU(1,1)

which appears in the context
of the Calogero–Coulomb model. As in the standard hydrogen atom
3
;
2
;
53
,
this group forms part of the dynamical symmetry of the system. In our case, it is generated
by the following operators:

\displaystyle K_{1}=\frac{r}{2}(\pi^{2}+1),\qquad K_{2}=\frac{r}{2}(\pi^{2}-1),\qquad K_{3}=-\imath\left(r\partial_{r}+{\textstyle\frac{N-1}{2}}\right),

(21)

\displaystyle[K_{\alpha},K_{\beta}]=-\imath\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}K^{\gamma}.

(22)

The restriction of these generators to symmetric wave functions, for which the Hamiltonian with particle exchange (
4
) reduces to the conventional Calogero–Coulomb system (
1
), was considered in Ref.
31
.
Here,
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
represents the Levi–Civita tensor in three dimensions, and the index is raised using the Minkowski metric
\eta_{\alpha\beta}
with signature
(+--)
. The commutation relations (
22
) become transparent once we note that the
g
-dependent term in the Dunkl momentum cancels out owing to the relation
[\pi_{i},r]=[p_{i},r]=-\imath\frac{x_{i}}{r}
.
The Casimir element of the resulting Lie algebra is then given by

K^{2}=K_{\alpha}K^{\alpha}=K_{1}^{2}-K_{2}^{2}-K_{3}^{2},\qquad[K^{2},K_{\alpha}]=0.

(23)

The conformal algebra mainly reflects the algebraic properties associated with the radial coordinates.
In particular, the generators (
21
) commute with the Dunkl angular momentum operators, since

\pi^{2}
commutes with them
25
:

[L_{ij},K_{\alpha}]=0.

(24)

In other words, they are
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N)
scalars.
The operator
K^{2}
also appears in the representation of the conformal generators
in terms of the radial components:

\displaystyle K_{1}=\frac{r}{2}(p_{r}^{2}+1)+\frac{K^{2}}{2r},\qquad K_{2}=\frac{r}{2}(p_{r}^{2}-1)+\frac{K^{2}}{2r},\qquad K_{3}=rp_{r}

(25)

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad p_{r}=r^{-\frac{N-1}{2}}(-\imath\partial_{r})r^{\frac{N-1}{2}}=-\imath\left(\partial_{r}+\frac{N-1}{2r}\right).

(26)

Recall that the definition of the radial momentum (
26
) ensures the Hermicity:

p_{r}^{+}=p_{r}

56
.
Using the last equality in (
25
) and
canonical commutation relation
[r,p_{r}]=\imath
, one obtains
the explicit expression for the square of the conformal algebra:

K^{2}=(K_{1}-K_{2})(K_{1}+K_{2})-\imath K_{3}-K_{3}^{2}=r^{2}(\pi^{2}-p_{r}^{2}).

(27)

Substituting this result into
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
in (
25
) reproduces
their original definitions (
21
).

Note that the conformal generators (
21
) are not Hermitian with respect to the standard
measure due to the explicit factor of
r
in their definitions. They become Hermitian
with respect to a modified measure that removes this factor:

\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle=\int r^{-1}\psi_{1}^{*}(x)\psi_{2}(x)d^{N}x=\int\psi_{1}^{*}\psi_{2}r^{N-2}drd^{N-1}\Omega,

(28)

where the second integral is written in spherical coordinates, and
\Omega
denotes the solid angle.

The first one is a result of comparison of (
21
) and (
25
).
The second is a definition of the angular Hamiltonian of the Calogero model
with particle exchange (
14
).

Consider now two expressions for the square of the Dunkl momentum.
The first follows from comparing Eqs. (
21
) and (
25
).
The second arises from the definition of the angular Calogero
Hamiltonian (
14
)
25

25
:

\pi^{2}=p_{r}^{2}+\frac{K^{2}}{r^{2}},\qquad\pi^{2}=-\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{N-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{H_{\Omega}}{r^{2}}.

(29)

Comparing these two expressions and using (
26
), we obtain an explicit relation between the
Casimir elements of the
so(1,2)
and
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N)
algebras:

K^{2}=H_{\Omega}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{4}}(N-1)(N-3).

(30)

Note that these two operators coincide in three-dimensional case,
N=3
.
Relation (
14
) then implies:

\displaystyle L^{2}=K^{2}-\big(S-{\textstyle\frac{N-1}{2}}\big)\big(S-{\textstyle\frac{N-3}{2}}\big).

(31)

III.2
Equidistant analogue of Calogero–Coulomb Hamitonian

For the Calogero model (with an oscillator potential instead of the Coulomb one),
the conformal algebra is generated by ladder operators
42
;
38

that separately produce the even and odd parts of the spectrum.
The spectrum of the Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian, however, is not equidistant;
see Eq. (
85
) below. As a result, the overall picture becomes more involved.

As mentioned in the Introduction, the Hamiltonian of the hydrogen atom can be transformed into
an equivalent one with the same wave functions but an equidistant energy spectrum.
This property makes it possible to construct a spectrum-generating algebra
for the transformed Hamiltonian
3
;
2
;
see also
53
;
56
for reviews.
Here, we generalize this construction,
originally applied to the hydrogen atom, to the Calogero–Coulomb system
with particle exchange (
4
).

Multiplying the Schrödinger equation

H_{\gamma}\Psi=E\Psi,

(32)

by
r
, we obtain an equivalent equation for the same wave function:

\displaystyle\Theta_{E}\Psi

\displaystyle=\gamma\Psi,

(33)

\displaystyle\Theta_{E}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{r\pi^{2}}{2}-Er=\frac{rp^{2}}{2}+\sum_{i<j}\frac{g(g-s_{ij})r}{2(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}}-Er.

(34)

In the new Schrödinger equation (
33
),
the energy
E
and Coulomb coupling
\gamma
interchange their roles:
the former becomes a parameter of the Hamiltonian,
whereas the latter appears as the eigenvalue.

A crucial property of the new Hamiltonian is that it can be expressed as a
linear combination of the conformal group generators (
21
):

\Theta_{E}=\frac{1}{2}(1-2E)K_{1}+\frac{1}{2}(1+2E)K_{2}.

(35)

The commutation relations (
22
) imply then that this element is, up to a
multiplicative factor, equivalent to the first generator:

\Theta_{E}=\sqrt{-2E}\,e^{-\imath\beta K_{3}}K_{1}e^{\imath\beta K_{3}}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\tanh\beta=\frac{1+2E}{1-2E}.

(36)

Later, we apply the above
\beta
-rotation to the entire conformal algebra
in order to align the generator
K_{1}
with the Hamiltonian
\Theta_{E}
;
see Eqs. (
62
) and (
68
) below.
For
\beta=0
the constructed Hamiltonian (
34
) coincides with
K_{1}
:

\Theta_{-\frac{1}{2}}=K_{1}.

(37)

Evidently,
\Theta_{E}^{\dagger}=\Theta_{E}
since
K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}=K_{\alpha}
,
where the dagger (
{\dagger}
) denotes the Hermitian conjugate with respect to the
modified inner product (
28
).

Note that the Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonians (
4
) and (
34
)
admit a simple representation in terms of the Casimir element
of the
so(1,2)
algebra (
23
):

\displaystyle H_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}p_{r}^{2}+\frac{K^{2}}{2r^{2}}-\frac{\gamma}{r},

(38)

\displaystyle\Theta_{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}rp_{r}^{2}+\frac{K^{2}}{2r}-Er.

(39)

These expressions are analogous to the representation (
25
) of the conformal
generators and follow directly from Eq. (
27
).

Finally, we mention that the conformal algebra participates in the dynamical symmetry
and underlies the superintegrability of the rational Calogero models

60
;
44
;
32
.
It also generates new invariants
35
;
34
for more general
class of conformal mechanical systems
19
.

IV
Dynamical symmetry of the equidistant Calogero–Coulomb system

In this section, we construct
the complete spectrum-generating algebra for the equidistant counterpart of
the Calogero–Coulomb system (
34
). Owing to the similarity
relation (
36
), it suffices to first consider the special case

E=-1/2
, for which the Hamiltonian reduces to the conformal-group generator

IV.1
Dynamical symmetry algebra for the parameter
E=-1/2

The dynamical symmetry of the Calogero–Coulomb model (
4
)
consists of the Dunkl angular momentum algebra
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N)
(
5
),
the conformal
so(1,2)
algebra (
21
), and three operator-valued Dunkl vectors
defined as follows:

\displaystyle A_{\sigma i}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{i}}{r}K_{\bar{\sigma}}-K_{3}\pi_{i}\quad\text{with}\quad\sigma=1,2,\quad\bar{1}=2,\quad\bar{2}=1,

(40)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=r\pi_{i}.

(41)

with the Dunkl momentum,
p_{i}\to\pi_{i}
. A more conventional form can be obtained

\displaystyle A_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}x\left(\pi^{2}+(-1)^{\bar{\sigma}}\right)-rp_{r}\pi.

The vector

It differs from the corresponding vector (

In terms of the

\displaystyle[K_{\sigma_{1}},K_{\sigma_{2}}]=\imath\epsilon_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}K_{3},\qquad[K_{3},K_{\sigma}]=\imath K_{\bar{\sigma}}.

We start from the commutation relations between the vectors (

and the generators of the conformal group (

Since the latter are scalars and do not act on coordinate indices

\displaystyle[K_{\sigma_{1}},A_{\sigma_{2}}]

\displaystyle=-\imath\epsilon_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\Gamma,

\displaystyle[K_{\sigma},\Gamma]

\displaystyle=\imath A_{\bar{\sigma}},

(44)

\displaystyle[K_{3},A_{\sigma}]

\displaystyle=\imath A_{\bar{\sigma}},

\displaystyle[K_{3},\Gamma]

\displaystyle=0.

Eqs. (

These four relations can be combined into a single commutator:

(46)

\alpha,\beta,\gamma=1,2,3

Nevertheless, taking into account

and
\Gamma

Commutation relations between the components of

\displaystyle{}[A_{\sigma_{1}i},A_{\sigma_{2}j}]

\displaystyle=\imath\epsilon_{\sigma_{1}\bar{\sigma}_{2}}L_{ij}+\imath\epsilon_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}K_{3}S_{ij},

(47)

\displaystyle{}[\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j}]

\displaystyle=-\imath L_{ij},

(48)

\displaystyle{}[A_{\sigma i},\Gamma_{j}]

\displaystyle=\imath K_{\bar{\sigma}}S_{ij}.

(49)

and (

We see that the commutator of the components of the same vector

In contrast, the commutator between components of different vectors is
proportional to the product of the particle-exchange operator and a generator of the conformal algebra.

In addition, the commutator of any vector with the Dunkl angular momentum
assumes a unique form; see also Eq. (

[L_{ij},A_{k}]=\imath A_{i}S_{jk}-\imath A_{j}S_{ik}\qquad\text{with}\qquad A_{k}=A_{\alpha k}.

The constructed generators of the dynamical symmetry

Indeed, the additional factor of
r^{-1}
in the integral does not affect on the Hermiticity of

On the other hand, the generators

and

Therefore,
K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}=K_{\alpha}
and
\Gamma_{i}^{\dagger}=\Gamma_{i}

Finally, the condition

follows from the second commutators in

) and (
45

We now define an antisymmetric matrix
\mathcal{L}_{ab}=-\mathcal{L}_{ba}
,
which unifies all generators of the dynamical symmetry:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{ij}=L_{ij},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{i,N+1}=A_{1i},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{i,N+2}=A_{2i},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{i,N+3}=\Gamma_{i},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N+1,N+2}=K_{3},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{N+1,N+3}=K_{2},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{N+2,N+3}=K_{1},

(52)

Here, the symmetric matrix
\mathcal{S}_{ab}=\mathcal{S}_{ba}
, which encodes the (deformed)
structure constants including particle-exchange effects, has the following nontrivial

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{ij}=S_{ij},\qquad\mathcal{S}_{N+1N+1}=1,\qquad\mathcal{S}_{N+2N+2}=\mathcal{S}_{N+3N+3}=-1.

(53)

For clarity, let us present the matrix structure of
\mathcal{L}_{ab}
and
\mathcal{S}_{ab}

showing only the upper-triangular elements:

\mathcal{L}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c c c | c c }0&L_{12}&\cdots&L_{1N}&A_{11}&A_{21}&\Gamma_{1}\\
&0&\cdots&L_{2N}&A_{12}&A_{22}&\Gamma_{2}\\
&&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\

&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\

\hline\cr&&&&-1&0\\
&&&&&-1\\
\end{array}\right).

In both matrices, the lines separate blocks with different signatures: the diagonal

In the

We therefore refer to the associative algebra generated by the operators

\mathcal{L}_{ab}
and
\mathcal{S}_{ab}

\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1,2)
.
This algebra contains, as subalgebras, the Dunkl angular momentum algebra
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N)

and the full symmetry algebra
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1)

The generators also satisfy additional algebraic relations
analogous to the crossing relations (

A detailed investigation of these relations and the structure of the resulting algebra lies
beyond the scope of the present work.

Let us now derive the (quadratic) Casimir element of the
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1,2)

algebra.
First, note that the squares of all three vectors, similarly to the Dunkl angular momentum squared
(

\displaystyle A_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=K_{2}^{2}+K_{3}^{2}+S-{\textstyle\frac{N-1}{2}},

(55)

\displaystyle A_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=K_{1}^{2}-K_{3}^{2}-S+{\textstyle\frac{N-1}{2}},

(56)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{2}

\displaystyle=K_{1}^{2}-K_{2}^{2}-S+{\textstyle\frac{N-1}{2}}.

(57)

The derivation of the above formulas is provided in Appendix
B
;
see Eqs. (
132

Combining the above three equations we obtain:

A_{\alpha}^{2}=K_{\alpha}^{2}+\eta_{\alpha\alpha}\left(K^{2}-S+{\textstyle\frac{N-1}{2}}\right),

(58)

where
\alpha=1,2,3
, and
\eta_{\alpha\beta}
is the Minkowski
metric associated with the three-dimensional conformal algebra;
see the text below Eq. (

We now ready to evaluate the squared sum of all entries of the matrix (
54

with their signatures taken into account. Using Eqs. (
31

(

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a<b}\mathcal{L}_{ab}\mathcal{L}^{ab}=L^{2}+A_{1}^{2}-A_{2}^{2}-\Gamma^{2}+K_{1}^{2}-K_{2}^{2}-K_{3}^{2}

(59)

\displaystyle=-S(S-N-1)-\frac{1}{4}{(N-1)(N+3)}.

While the binomial in

represents the quadratic invariant of the permutation

(

\mathcal{L}_{ab}

Hence, to construct the Casimir element, denoted by

In the present representation, it reduces to a constant, yielding:

\mathcal{H}_{\Omega}=\mathcal{L}^{2}+S(S-N-1)=-\frac{1}{4}(N-1)(N+3).

(60)

The Casimir operators

\tilde{H}_{\Omega}

and

\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1)

\text{Casimir}=(\text{generators})^{2}+S(S-d+2),

Note that recently a procedure for constructing the Casimir operators

IV.2
Dynamical symmetry for general
E

Consider now the barred elements of the
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1,2)
algebra,
obtained from the original ones
via a
\beta
-rotation about the
K_{3}
axis (

\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{ab}=e^{-\imath\beta K_{3}}\mathcal{L}_{ab}e^{\imath\beta K_{3}},\qquad\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{ab}=\mathcal{S}_{ab}.

(61)

This transformation leaves the structure constants unchanged,
as the conformal algebra is invariant under particle exchange.
Certain components of the tensor
\mathcal{L}_{ab}
also remain invariant; see
Eqs. (
24
) and (
46
).
The first equation in (

)
can be expressed in expanded form as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{\sigma}=e^{-\imath\beta K_{3}}K_{\sigma}e^{\imath\beta K_{3}},\qquad\bar{A}_{\sigma i}=e^{-\imath\beta K_{3}}A_{\sigma i}e^{\imath\beta K_{3}},

(62)

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{ij}=L_{ij},\qquad\bar{K}_{3}=K_{3},\qquad\bar{A}_{3i}=A_{3i}.

(63)

where, as before,
\sigma=1,2
.
Clearly, the transformed
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1,2)
generators satisfy
the same commutation relations as the
original ones (
52
):

[\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{ab},\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{cd}]=\imath(\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{bc}\mathcal{S}_{ad}+\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{ad}\mathcal{S}_{bc}-\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{bd}\mathcal{S}_{ac}-\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{ac}\mathcal{S}_{bd}).

(64)

The squares of the conformal algebra (
23
) and of the dynamical symmetry algebra (
59
)
remain invariant under the
\beta
-rotation:

\bar{K}^{2}=K^{2},\qquad\bar{\mathcal{L}}^{2}=\mathcal{L}^{2}.

(65)

Using the definition of the hyperbolic angle
\beta
(
36
) and the commutation relations
(
43
), (
45
), one can straightforwardly derive the explicit forms of the rotated
generators (
62
):

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{\sqrt{-2E}}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}+(-1)^{\sigma}E\right),

(66)

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-2E}}\left[x\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}+(-1)^{\sigma}E\right)+rp_{r}\pi\right].

(67)

Equations (
66
) indicate that the equidistant form of the Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian
(
34
) is proportional to the shifted generator corresponding to
\sigma=1
:

\Theta_{E}=\sqrt{-2E}\bar{K}_{1}.

(68)

This relation determines the
spectrum of
\Theta_{E}
, since the conformal element
K_{1}

in the discrete representation assumes positive integer eigenvalues.

The Dunkl angular momentum tensor and the
\beta
-rotated modified Runge-Lenz vector
\bar{A}_{1}

generate the full symmetries of the above Hamiltonian:

[\Theta_{E},L_{ij}]=[\Theta_{E},\bar{A}_{1i}]=0.

(69)

The latter depends on
E

and represents an additional integral of motion,
analogous to the invariant (
6
) for the original
Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian.

At the same time, the scalars
\bar{K}_{2}
,
K_{3}
together with the vectors
\bar{A}_{2}

and
A_{3}=-\Gamma
form the spectrum generated sector of the
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1,2)
algebra.
The associated ladder operators are defined by:

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\bar{K}_{2}\pm\imath K_{3}:\qquad

\displaystyle[\bar{K}_{1},\bar{K}_{\pm}]

\displaystyle=\pm\bar{K}_{\pm},\quad

\displaystyle[\bar{K}_{+},\bar{K}_{-}]

\displaystyle=-2\bar{K}_{1},

(70)

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{\pm i}

\displaystyle=\bar{A}_{2i}\pm\imath A_{3i}:\qquad

\displaystyle[\bar{K}_{1},\bar{A}_{\pm i}]

\displaystyle=\pm\bar{A}_{\pm i},\quad

\displaystyle[\bar{A}_{+i},\bar{A}_{-i}]

\displaystyle=-2\bar{K}_{1}S_{ii}.

(71)

The above commutation relations follow from Eqs. (
22
) and (
46
).
The correspondence (
68
) implies that the ladder operators
\bar{K}_{\pm}
and
\bar{A}_{\pm i}

map between two energy levels of the equidistant Calogero–Coulomb
Hamiltonian (
34
) separated by
\sqrt{-2E}
:

\displaystyle[\Theta_{E},\bar{K}_{\pm}]

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{-2E}\bar{K}_{\pm},

(72)

\displaystyle[\Theta_{E},\bar{A}_{\pm i}]

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{-2E}\bar{A}_{\pm i}.

(73)

V
SO(1,2)
structure of the Calogero–Coulomb wave functions

In this section, we first construct explicitly the eigenfunctions of the equidistant
Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian in terms of the deformed spherical harmonics.
We then analyze the representations of the three-dimensional conformal algebra on these states.

V.1
Stationary wave functions in deformed spherical harmonics

Recall the Maxwell representation of the deformed homogeneous harmonic polynomials
61
(see also

38
). These polynomials are labeled by the magnetic quantum numbers
m_{i}
:

\displaystyle h_{m_{1}\dots m_{N}}(x)

\displaystyle=r^{2(c_{g}-1+l)+N}\nabla^{\prime m_{1}}_{1}\dots\nabla^{\prime m_{N}}_{N}r^{-2(c_{g}-1)-N},\qquad m_{i}=0,1,2,\dots.

(74)

Here
l
denotes the degree of the polynomial associated
with the angular momentum (orbital) quantum number,
while the constant
c_{g}
accounts for the Calogero interaction:

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i},

(75)

\displaystyle c_{g}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}gN(N-1).

(76)

The primed nablas in Eq. (
74
) are obtained from the standard Dunkl operators
through a (nonunitary) similarity transformation:

\displaystyle\nabla^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=\phi^{-1}\nabla_{i}\phi=\partial_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{g}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{ij}),

(77)

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i<j}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{g}.

(78)

The solution of the Shrödinger equation for the angular Calogero model (
14
) is
given then by
27
:

\displaystyle H_{\Omega}\,\phi(x)h_{m_{1}\dots m_{N}}(x)=\mathcal{E}_{l}\phi(x)h_{m_{1}\dots m_{N}}(x),

(79)

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{l}=(c_{g}+l)(c_{g}+l+N-2).

(80)

The energy levels depend solely on the angular
momentum (orbital) quantum number
l
.
This property leads to the highest level of degeneracy
and ensures the maximal superintegrability of the angular system
27
;
33
.

The wave functions in Eq. (
79
) are unnormalized and satisfy a linear dependence condition;
see Eq. (
93
) below. They form an overcomplete basis,
and the orthogonality condition holds only for different values
of the orbital quantum number
l

61
.

The eigenfunctions of the Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian (
4
)
can then be expressed in spherical coordinates as:

\displaystyle\Psi_{m_{1}\dots m_{N},k}(x)

\displaystyle=\text{const}\cdot e^{-\frac{\rho}{2}}L_{k}^{(2\alpha_{l})}(\rho)\phi(x)h_{m_{1}\dots m_{N}}(x),

(81)

\displaystyle H_{\gamma}\Psi_{m_{1}\dots m_{N},k}

\displaystyle=E_{n}\Psi_{m_{1}\dots m_{N},k}.

(82)

Here,
L_{k}^{(\alpha)}
is the associated Laguerre polynomial, where the lower
index
k
corresponds to the
radial quantum number, counting the nodes of the wave function.
The upper index
\alpha
is determined by the orbital quantum number,
while the energy depends on the principal quantum number:

\displaystyle\alpha_{l}

\displaystyle=c_{g}+l+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}N-1,

(83)

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=l+k+1,\qquad k=n_{r}=0,1,2,\dots,

(84)

\displaystyle E_{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2(c_{g}+n+{\textstyle\frac{N-3}{2}})^{2}}.

(85)

Finally, the argument of the Laguerre polynomial is the renormalized, dimensionless radial coordinate:

\rho=\rho_{n}=\frac{2\gamma r}{c_{g}+n+\frac{N-3}{2}}.

(86)

The Shrödinger equation (
82
) may be verified by substituting (
30
) into the
radial form of the Hamiltonian (
38
) and using the angular
Shrödinger equation (
79
), (
80
).

The last two terms in the wave function (
81
) are homogeneous polynomials of
total degree
c_{g}+l
.
Therefore, they depend on the angular variables
u_{i}=x_{i}/r
, which completes the
separation of radial and angular coordinates:

\Psi_{m_{1}\dots m_{N},k}(x)=\text{const}\cdot\rho^{c_{g}+l}e^{-\frac{\rho}{2}}L_{k}^{(2\alpha_{l})}(\rho)\phi(u)h_{m_{1}\dots m_{N}}(u).

(87)

According to the discussion in Sect.
III.2
, the constructed wave function (
87
)
solves also the Shrödinger equation for the equidistant Calogero–Coulomb model (
33
), (
34
):

\displaystyle\Theta_{E}\Psi_{m_{1}\dots m_{N},k}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}\Psi_{m_{1}\dots m_{N},k},

(88)

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-2E}\left(c_{g}+n+{\textstyle\frac{N-3}{2}}\right),

(89)

where
n
is the principal quantum number (
85
).
Here, the energy value
E<0
is considered as a parameter, and the radial variable
(
86
) must be reorganized though the energy as:

\displaystyle\rho=2\sqrt{-2E}\,r.

(90)

In contrast to the original spectrum (
85
), the current spectrum (
89
) is equidistant.
This is an advantage
since prohibits the spectrum generated algebras as in the oscillator system.
Note that the quantization conditions (
85
) and (
89
) are equivalent
since both them are obtained from the relation
\gamma/\sqrt{-2E}=c_{g}+n+{\textstyle\frac{N-3}{2}}
.

Upon symmetrization over all coordinates, the deformed harmonic polynomials
(
74
) take the form
27
:

\displaystyle h_{k_{1}k_{3}\dots k_{N}}^{\text{sym}}(x)

\displaystyle=r^{2(c_{g}-1+l)+N}\mathcal{D}_{1}^{k_{1}}\mathcal{D}_{3}^{k_{3}}\dots\mathcal{D}_{N}^{k_{N}}r^{-2(c_{g}-1)-N},\qquad\mathcal{D}_{q}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla^{\prime}_{i}\mbox{}^{q},

(91)

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle=k_{1}+\sum_{i=3}^{N}ik_{i}.

(92)

Here the integers
k_{i}
label the modes of a symmetric polynomial, while
l
detones its degree,
and corresponds to the angular momentum quantum number. The Dunkl Laplacian
\mathcal{D}_{2}

does not appear, since its action annihilates the power function
of the right-hand side of the above expression,
\mathcal{D}_{2}r^{-2(c_{g}-1)-N}=0
.
As a consequence, the deformed harmonic
polynomials (
74
) are linearly dependent
61
:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{m_{1}\dots m_{i-1}(m_{i}+2)m_{i+1}\dots m_{N}}(x)=0.

(93)

The eigenfunctions of the angular Hamiltonian
associated with the bosonic Calogero-Moser model
\tilde{H}_{0}
(

),
take the form

H_{\Omega}\,\phi(x)h^{\text{sym}}_{k_{1}k_{3}\dots k_{N}}(x)=\mathcal{E}_{l}\phi(x)h^{\text{sym}}_{k_{1}k_{3}\dots k_{N}}(x).

(94)

The corresponding energy is given by Eq. (
80
), where the orbital quantum number
is determined by Eq. (
92
).

As a consequence, the solution of the Schrödinger
equation of the standard Calogero–Coulomb model describing identical bosons
(
1

\displaystyle\Psi^{\text{sym}}_{k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}\dots k_{N}}(x)

\displaystyle=\text{const}\cdot\rho^{c_{g}+l}e^{-\frac{\rho}{2}}L_{k_{2}}^{(2\alpha_{l})}(\rho)\phi(u)h_{k_{1}k_{3}\dots k_{N}}^{\text{sym}}(u),

(95)

\displaystyle H_{\gamma}\Psi^{\text{sym}}_{k_{1}\dots k_{N}}

\displaystyle=E_{n}\Psi^{\text{sym}}_{k_{1}\dots k_{N}},

(96)

\displaystyle\Theta_{E}\Psi^{\text{sym}}_{k_{1}\dots k_{N}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}\Psi^{\text{sym}}_{k_{1}\dots k_{N}}.

(97)

The energy is given by Eq. (
85
), and the principal quantum number
is defined in Eq. (
84
).
The radial quantum number appears as the second quantum number
of the solution (

):
n_{r}=k=k_{2}

Substituting the orbital quantum number (

into Eq. (

Symmetrization projects the operators onto their permutation-invariant counterparts.

known in the mathematical literature

Substituting this expression into

VI
Conclusion

In this article, we investigate the dynamical symmetry of the
Calogero model in an external confining Coulomb field within the framework of
the exchange operator formalism.
Following the case of the interaction-free Coulomb
model, we construct an alternative system that retains the same stationary
states while exhibiting a linear (equidistant) spectrum, thereby admitting ladder operators.
The resulting Hamiltonian is identified as an element of the conformal algebra
so(1,2)
,
which underlies the dynamical symmetry of the model.

It was shown that the equidistant Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian admits a Dunkl-deformed Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector, which
differs from the analogous vector previously obtained for the original system
36
.
Together with the Dunkl angular momentum tensor, it
forms a complete symmetry algebra of the equidistant Calogero–Coulomb model,

\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1)
. The latter has a structure similar to that of the original system
26
.

Next, the
so(1,2)
algebra extends the Dunkl-deformed orthogonal
symmetry to the full dynamical symmetry algebra, which can be regarded
as an exchange-operator deformation of the conformal algebra in
(N+1)
-dimensional
Minkowski space,
\mathcal{H}_{g}so(N+1,2)
.
The commutation relations between the generators have been
derived explicitly. The corresponding structure constants generally
depend on particle exchange.
Nevertheless, all commutation relations can be compactly encoded in a single matrix expression
involving the algebra generators and structure constants.
Furthermore, ladder operators that shift the energy by one unit are constructed.
The quadratic Casimir operator of the resulting deformed algebra is shown to
be to a constant.

Finally, we construct the stationary states of the Calogero–Coulomb
Hamiltonian, both with and without particle exchange.
The construction relies on deformed spherical harmonics and their
symmetrized counterparts
20
;
61
. We show that the
wavefunctions are classified according to the infinite-dimensional
lowest-weight irreducible representation of the
so(1,2)
conformal algebra. The
diagonal conformal generator associated to the ”time” component is
given by the (rescaled) equidistant Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian. The conformal spin
of this multiplet is determined by the Calogero coupling constant and orbital quantum
number, while the states are labeled by the radial quantum number.

We emphasize that the commutation relations (
52
) and (
64
)
do not define an abstract Lie algebra. To satisfy the Jacobi identity,
the generators must obey additional constraints,
analogous to the crossing relations (
12
),
whose derivation would also be of interest.

In this work, we have focused on the standard Calogero–Coulomb Hamiltonian,
which is invariant under particle permutations.
The present construction is likely to extend to systems with arbitrary finite-reflection group (Coxeter) symmetry.
The noninteracting Hamiltonians, in which the group is generated by independent reflections, have already been discussed in the literature; see Ref.
41
and references therein.

It is worth noting that the spectrum-generating operators of the standard Calogero–Coulomb model (
1
) can be expressed
as symmetric polynomials in the generators of the deformed conformal algebra.
The symmetrization procedure projects these operators onto their permutation-invariant counterparts,
thereby ensuring consistency with the underlying particle-exchange symmetry of the model.
However, this projection substantially complicates the algebraic relations among the resulting elements.
