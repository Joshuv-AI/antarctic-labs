Novel online fabric color difference detection system based on machine vision
Authors: Ji-Huan Wang, Yue Min, Jin-Quan Xiong, Ming Fang, Mohd Shafry Mohd Rahim, Dong-Lin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Color difference detection remains a critical challenge in textile manufacturing, where traditional visual inspection and offline measurement methods suffer from subjectivity, low efficiency, and delayed feedback. This study emphasizes engineering integration for online industrial fabric inspection rather than proposing a single new color-difference algorithm. The proposed system integrates a custom-designed optical acquisition platform with a lightweight color analysis pipeline, including bilateral filtering for noise suppression, K-means clustering for representative color extraction, RGB-to-CIELab color space conversion, and perceptually weighted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E_{\textrm{CMC}(2:1)}$$\end{document} computation. The system was deployed on an actual textile production line and evaluated using ten fabric rolls with different colors and materials. Experimental results show roll-level agreement with manual inspection in the tested samples and indicate the feasibility of continuous monitoring of chromatic variations along the fabric length. The proposed system provides a practical engineering solution for automated textile color quality control and may support production-line decision making while reducing dependence on subjective visual inspection in industrial environments.

Introduction

The textile industry, as a cornerstone of the global manufacturing economy, relies heavily on stable and consistent product quality to remain competitive in increasingly diversified and fast-paced supply chains1,2. Among the various quality attributes evaluated during textile production, color difference is widely regarded as one of the most sensitive and visually perceptible indicators, directly influencing product appearance, consumer perception, and market acceptance2,3. Even slight chromatic deviations can lead to visible inconsistencies in finished garments, while color-related failures such as fading, shade variation, and discoloration remain among the most common causes of garment rejection and shortened product lifecycles in industrial practice4. Consequently, ensuring accurate and stable color consistency has become a critical quality-control objective for apparel, home textiles, and digital textile printing5,6.

Conventional textile color evaluation methods still depend largely on manual visual inspection and off-line spectrophotometric measurements. Although spectrophotometric instruments can provide high-precision color measurements under controlled laboratory conditions, their off-line operation, point-based sampling, and limited spatial coverage make them unsuitable for continuous online inspection on high-speed production lines7. Manual visual inspection, on the other hand, is inherently subjective and strongly affected by inspector experience, fatigue, and ambient illumination conditions, often resulting in inconsistent judgments and delayed quality feedback6,8. With the increasing demand for high-efficiency and large-scale production, delayed detection of color deviations may lead to large batches of defective products, increased rework costs, and reduced overall production efficiency9,10. Fig. 1 presents the actual deployment and hardware architecture of the proposed online fabric color-difference detection system. As shown in Fig. 1a, the deployed prototype was installed on industrial fabric guiding and rolling equipment to support online inspection under production-line conditions. The system consists of four 4K industrial color line-scan CCD cameras, a 1.8 m D65 linear LED illumination module, a main control computer, an incremental position encoder, an anti-vibration stabilizing unit, and a feeding–rolling transmission mechanism. Each line-scan camera covers approximately 45 cm of fabric width, and the four-camera stitched configuration provides a total imaging field of 1.8 m for fabrics up to 1.7 m wide. During operation, the fabric is conveyed smoothly through the field of view, while encoder-synchronized triggering matches image acquisition with fabric motion. The top-mounted linear LED source provides uniform illumination across the full imaging field, reducing blind zones, motion-induced spatial ambiguity, and illumination inconsistency across camera stitching regions and fabric edges. With rapid advances in machine vision and computational imaging, automated inspection systems have emerged as promising alternatives to traditional manual evaluation, offering non-contact measurement, wide-area coverage, and integration potential with automated production lines11. Recent studies have reported substantial progress in fabric feature extraction, illumination normalization, surface anomaly detection, and small-defect inspection under complex textures9,12–15. In particular, deep learning approaches, including convolutional neural networks, single-stage object detectors, bio-inspired spatio-temporal fusion networks, and structure-preserving defect detection models, have demonstrated strong capability in modeling complex surface textures and detecting weak, small, or structure-sensitive defects10,13–17. In parallel, hyperspectral and multispectral imaging techniques have been explored to provide richer chromatic characterization beyond the limitations of conventional RGB sensors18. Despite these advances, many state-of-the-art systems remain difficult to deploy directly on industrial production lines due to high computational demand, reliance on specialized optical components, strict calibration requirements, and sensitivity to fabric motion and environmental disturbances, which conflict with industrial requirements for real-time performance, system stability, and cost efficiency. However, most recent surface-defect and small-target detection methods focus primarily on anomaly localization or categorical defect recognition, whereas online fabric color-difference inspection requires continuous, quantitative, and perception-aligned Δ E evaluation under production-line conditions.

To improve practical deployability, researchers have explored lightweight image-processing strategies for fabric color consistency evaluation, including color-space transformation, segmentation-based chromatic analysis, clustering-based feature extraction, and histogram-driven correction of illumination or color distribution12,19,20. More recently, lightweight deep learning frameworks have been introduced to reduce computational complexity and hardware dependency, thereby improving suitability for real-time online inspection9,17. Nevertheless, many learning-based approaches still struggle to maintain robustness in real factory environments, where lighting fluctuations, mechanical vibration, texture interference, and surface reflectance variations are pervasive9,16. Moreover, many existing machine-vision-based systems treat color inconsistency primarily as a categorical classification problem, while quantitative and perceptually meaningful Δ E-based evaluation, which is essential for textile quality standards and traceable decision-making, is often insufficiently considered.

In light of these challenges, there is a clear need for a practical, lightweight, and perception-aligned online color-difference detection system that can operate reliably under real production conditions. Motivated by this need, this study develops an online fabric color-difference detection system that integrates robust image preprocessing, efficient clustering-based chromatic representation, and perceptually weighted CMC(2:1) color-difference evaluation within a unified machine-vision framework. The proposed system employs bilateral filtering for texture-preserving denoising, K-means clustering for dominant-color extraction, RGB–CIELab conversion for perceptually uniform representation, and a real-time Δ E computation pipeline. Field experiments conducted directly on textile production lines indicate the system’s operational feasibility, stable output trends, and roll-level agreement with manual inspection under the tested conditions, suggesting its potential to support production-line quality-control decisions through continuous, objective, and traceable chromatic monitoring. By operating on standard industrial hardware without requiring high-end GPUs or complex calibration, the proposed system offers a practical solution for production-line textile color quality monitoring.

Specifically, this study addresses a practical yet underexplored problem: how to achieve reliable, continuous, and perceptually consistent color-difference evaluation under real online manufacturing conditions. Unlike approaches that rely on off-line spectrophotometric measurements or categorical classification, industrial fabric inspection requires quantitative Δ E-based evaluation that remains stable under fabric motion, texture interference, and illumination variation. The remainder of this paper reviews related work on fabric color inspection, machine-vision-based textile analysis, and color-difference evaluation, followed by detailed system design, experimental validation, and discussion.

Related work

Industrial background and online inspection challenges

In industrial textile manufacturing, maintaining stable and consistent color quality is a fundamental requirement for ensuring product appearance, brand consistency, and market acceptance3,6. Color deviation occurring during dyeing, finishing, or printing processes can rapidly propagate along high-speed production lines, resulting in large batches of nonconforming products if not detected in time. Industrial surveys and case studies indicate that color-related defects, including shade variation, fading, and discoloration, remain among the most frequent causes of garment rejection and shortened product lifecycles in practice2,4. Consequently, online color inspection has become an essential component of modern textile quality-control systems. Despite its importance, reliable online color inspection in industrial environments remains challenging. Conventional color evaluation methods still rely heavily on manual visual inspection and off-line spectrophotometric measurements. While spectrophotometers provide accurate point-based color measurements under controlled conditions, their off-line operation and limited spatial sampling prevent continuous monitoring of fabric color consistency along the full production width and length7. Manual inspection, although flexible, is inherently subjective and strongly influenced by inspector experience, fatigue, and environmental illumination, leading to inconsistent assessments and delayed feedback in fast-paced production settings6,8.

From an industrial perspective, deploying online inspection systems introduces additional challenges that are often underestimated in laboratory studies. Fabric motion, mechanical vibration, surface texture variation, and illumination instability can significantly affect the reliability of color measurements captured by imaging sensors11. Moreover, industrial production lines impose strict constraints on computational efficiency, system robustness, and long-term stability, limiting the applicability of methods that require complex calibration procedures, high-end hardware, or extensive post-processing11. Delayed or unstable color evaluation may lead to increased rework costs, production interruptions, and reduced overall efficiency9,10. In recent years, machine-vision-based inspection systems have been increasingly adopted in textile manufacturing due to their non-contact nature, wide-area coverage, and compatibility with automated production lines11. However, many existing systems primarily focus on detecting structural defects or surface anomalies, while quantitative and perception-aligned color-difference evaluation remains insufficiently addressed under real online conditions9,16. As a result, a gap persists between laboratory-based color measurement techniques and the practical requirements of real-time industrial textile inspection. These challenges highlight the need for online color-difference detection solutions that are not only perceptually meaningful but also computationally efficient and robust to real-world production disturbances. Addressing this gap requires system designs that explicitly account for industrial constraints while providing reliable and traceable color evaluation throughout the manufacturing process.

Color-difference evaluation methods and perceptual metrics in textile inspection

Color-difference evaluation plays a central role in textile quality control, as it provides a quantitative basis for assessing chromatic consistency during dyeing, finishing, and printing processes3,6. In machine-vision-based inspection systems, color-difference metrics must operate on image-derived color representations and remain robust to imaging noise, illumination variation, and surface texture, while still correlating with human visual perception. This requirement has motivated extensive research on perceptually meaningful color-difference formulations in both color science and computer vision communities.

The CIELab color space and its associated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E^{*}_{ab}$$\end{document} metric have been widely adopted due to their intuitive geometric interpretation and low computational cost7. However, studies in both visual perception and image-based color analysis have shown that equal Euclidean distances in CIELab space do not necessarily correspond to equal perceived color differences, particularly under varying imaging conditions and for textured materials8,21. These limitations become more pronounced in camera-based inspection systems, where sensor characteristics and illumination instability further affect chromatic consistency.

To improve perceptual accuracy, advanced color-difference formulations such as the CIEDE2000 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E_{00}$$\end{document}) metric introduce additional correction terms to compensate for non-uniform perceptual sensitivity3,22. While \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E_{00}$$\end{document} has demonstrated improved perceptual alignment in controlled experiments, its relatively high computational complexity and sensitivity to noise restrict its practical applicability in real-time image-based inspection scenarios23. These constraints limit its deployment in high-speed online textile inspection systems, where computational efficiency and robustness are essential.

In contrast, the CMC(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l\!:\!c$$\end{document}) color-difference formula was specifically designed to address industrial color-evaluation requirements and has been widely adopted in textile applications6. By introducing adjustable weighting factors for lightness and chroma components, CMC metrics provide a flexible balance between perceptual relevance and computational simplicity. In particular, the CMC(2:1) formulation emphasizes lightness differences, which are often visually dominant and quality-critical in fabric inspection. From a machine-vision perspective, this weighting strategy offers improved robustness to image noise and texture-induced chromatic fluctuation, making CMC(2:1) well suited for real-time camera-based color-difference evaluation.

Although spectrophotometer-based color-difference measurement remains the reference standard in laboratory settings, its off-line nature and limited spatial sampling hinder continuous monitoring along the fabric length2. Image-based inspection systems enable full-field color observation and real-time analysis, but many existing approaches still formulate color inconsistency as a classification problem rather than performing quantitative and perception-aligned color-difference evaluation5,9,10. Consequently, there remains a gap between perceptual color-difference theory and its implementation in practical online vision-based textile inspection systems.

Design motivation and system architecture overview

Based on the limitations identified in the related work, we reformulate the problem of online fabric color-difference detection from a system-design perspective. Existing approaches often fail to jointly satisfy perceptual consistency, robustness to industrial disturbances, and real-time feasibility under practical manufacturing constraints.

The overall workflow starts with reference and test image acquisition under controlled illumination. Image preprocessing via texture-preserving filtering

Image Filtering During image acquisition, CCD and CMOS image sensors introduce various types of noise due to the characteristics of photoelectric materials, environmental conditions, and electronic circuit structures24,25. Typical noise sources include thermal noise, MOSFET channel noise, photon shot noise, dark current noise, and photoresponse non-uniformity25. These noise components degrade image clarity, obscure texture details, distort chromatic distribution, and negatively affect color difference computation. Therefore, image denoising is a necessary preprocessing step in the proposed online detection system25. For color images, denoising is typically performed by decomposing the image into its red (R), green (G), and blue (B) channels, filtering each channel independently, and subsequently recombining them. To determine the most suitable filtering method for fabric color difference detection, three widely used approaches median filtering, Gaussian filtering, and bilateral filtering were evaluated.

Median Filtering Median filtering is a nonlinear technique particularly effective for removing impulsive noise such as salt-and-pepper noise. Median filtering can effectively suppress isolated noise points while preserving edge sharpness. However, it also presents several limitations: fine texture details may be blurred, especially with larger window sizes, its performance is poor for Gaussian or continuous noise, residual isolated noise may persist in high-noise environments.

Gaussian Filtering Gaussian filtering smooths the image by convolving it with a Gaussian-weighted kernel. It provides natural-looking smoothing and performs well against normally distributed noise26.

However, because Gaussian filtering relies solely on spatial proximity and does not consider intensity similarity, it causes blurring near edges and loss of high-frequency details, which is undesirable for fabric color inspection, where subtle texture boundaries influence clustering and Δ E computation.

K-means clustering for representative color extraction

After noise suppression, representative color features must be extracted from each fabric image region to provide a stable basis for subsequent Δ E computation. In the proposed system, K-means clustering is adopted to group pixels with similar chromatic characteristics into compact clusters27,28. Although prior studies mainly focused on fabric defect detection rather than chromatic evaluation, the underlying principle of feature-space clustering to suppress texture and enhance dominant appearance remains applicable27.

Each centroid can be interpreted as a dominant color component of the inspected region, thereby providing a compact yet informative representation of the local chromatic distribution30,31. This property is particularly advantageous for textile applications, where woven and knitted fabrics often exhibit localized color homogeneity and repetitive structural patterns along the production direction28. Cluster Selection for Fabric Color Measurement In practical textile color evaluation, the choice of K must balance chromatic representation and computational efficiency. Woven and knitted fabrics typically exhibit a single dominant background color with fine-scale texture superimposed. Therefore, a relatively small cluster number (K = 2-4) is adopted in the proposed system, which is sufficient to capture the principal chromatic component while suppressing micro-texture fluctuations26. In the reported experiments, K=3 was used for all online measurements. The centroids were initialized using K-means++ with a fixed random seed, and the maximum number of iterations was set to 100. The choice of K=3 was based on the sensitivity analysis in Table 2, where it provided a stable balance between chromatic representation and computational cost.

Following clustering, the centroid corresponding to the cluster with the largest pixel population is selected as the representative color of the inspected region. Since this cluster generally reflects the fabric’s dominant background coloration, this selection strategy effectively reduces interference from texture shadows, yarn crossovers, and other small-scale structural irregularities. This principle is consistent with classical color quantization theory, where each centroid represents a dominant color component30. As a result, the extracted centroid provides a stable and consistent chromatic descriptor for subsequent Δ E-based color-difference computation. This strategy is most appropriate for fabrics with a single dominant background color. For jacquard, printed, striped, gradient, or other complex multi-color fabrics, a single dominant centroid may discard meaningful color-distribution information; this limitation is explicitly discussed in the revised limitations section.

Advantages for online Fabric Color Detection The proposed method offers several advantages for online fabric color detection. Its computational efficiency, achieved through a low-dimensional centroid representation, enables real-time industrial operation without imposing excessive computational load. The clustering process also enhances robustness to local texture by reducing the influence of weave patterns, surface noise, and minor illumination fluctuations. In addition, the selected centroid provides a stable chromatic representation that reflects the dominant perceptual color of each fabric region, ensuring reliable and repeatable Δ E evaluation during continuous motion. Finally, the lightweight nature of the algorithm facilitates deployment on standard industrial PCs without the need for GPU acceleration.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, the CIELab color space provides an approximately perceptually uniform representation of lightness and chromatic opponency. In this space, Euclidean color differences correlate more closely with human visual sensitivity than in device-dependent RGB space32. Such perceptual consistency is essential for textile color inspection, where subtle chromatic deviations must be evaluated reliably under fabric texture interference and illumination variability18.

To enable perceptually meaningful Δ E computation, the filtered fabric images are converted from RGB to CIELab following the standard CIE colorimetric framework20. The conversion consists of two sequential steps: (1) linearization and transformation from RGB to the tristimulus XYZ space; and (2) nonlinear mapping from XYZ to the CIELab coordinates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(L^*, a^*, b^*)$$\end{document}. This standardized workflow ensures device-independent representation and provides a robust foundation for subsequent color-difference evaluation in industrial environments.

RGB to XYZ Conversion Before applying the matrix transformation, the normalized sRGB values are first linearized through inverse gamma correction to compensate for the nonlinear response of typical imaging sensors24. This step ensures that the RGB values are proportional to the physical light intensity captured by the camera, providing a reliable basis for subsequent color-space transformation33. This property is essential for reliable Δ E computation in textile color-difference detection, especially under weave texture and illumination variations.

Advantages of CMC(2:1) for Textile Applications The CMC(2:1) formula is widely adopted in textile quality control due to its perceptual alignment with human visual evaluation. Compared with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E_{ab}$$\end{document} or RGB-based differences, CMC(2:1) provides improved sensitivity to lightness variations, which are particularly critical in dyeing and finishing processes. Moreover, the weighting functions enhance robustness against local texture variations and illumination changes, making \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E_{\textrm{CMC}(2:1)}$$\end{document} suitable for continuous industrial inspection.

Owing to its widespread adoption in dyeing laboratories, finishing mills, and garment factories, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E_{\textrm{CMC}(2:1)}$$\end{document} serves as an industry-validated metric for defining tolerance thresholds (e.g., Δ E < 1.0).

The discrete GPU in the deployed industrial PC was used mainly for multi-camera image display, interface rendering, and future system extension. The proposed color-difference pipeline itself is composed of bilateral filtering, K-means dominant-color extraction, RGB–CIELab conversion, and CMC(2:1) computation; therefore, the core analysis can be executed on the CPU in real time without a high-end GPU or a dedicated deep-learning accelerator.

A total of ten fabric rolls with different colors and materials were selected as test samples for online inspection. During the inspection process, the proposed system continuously acquired fabric images, while color-difference values were computed for 1 m inspection segments according to the encoder-synchronized displacement index. For each fabric roll, the reference color was obtained from the first stable inspection segment after the fabric had entered steady motion under the fixed illumination setting. The reference image region was processed using the same filtering, clustering, and RGB–CIELab conversion pipeline as subsequent inspection regions, and the resulting representative CIELab value was used as the roll-specific reference for Δ E computation. This configuration was used in the field trial to evaluate longitudinal color variation within each roll. In practical deployment, a customer-approved standard sample or a calibrated reference color can also be configured as the reference when product-specific color matching is required.

The CMC(2:1) color-difference formula was adopted as the evaluation metric, and a practical threshold of Δ E = 1.0 was used to distinguish acceptable and unacceptable color consistency, following common industrial inspection criteria reported in textile color quality control studies. This threshold was used as an operational tolerance for the present production-line trial rather than as a universal limit for all textile products. In practice, acceptable Δ E tolerances may vary with fibre composition, weave structure, gloss, color depth, finishing process, and customer specification. Therefore, the threshold can be adjusted in the deployed system when product-specific quality standards require a stricter or more relaxed tolerance.

Qualified rate evaluation

After computing the CMC(2:1) color difference for each inspected fabric segment, the system evaluates whether the measured chromatic deviation satisfies a predefined quality tolerance.

For each inspected region, the corresponding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E_{\textrm{CMC}}$$\end{document} value is compared with a tolerance threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_u$$\end{document}, which is typically defined according to textile industry standards. A region is classified as qualified if:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta E_{\textrm{CMC}} \le T_u . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Let N denote the total number of inspected regions and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_q$$\end{document} the number of qualified regions. The color consistency qualified rate Q is computed as:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Q = \frac{N_q}{N} \times 100\%. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This metric reflects the proportion of fabric segments that meet chromatic specifications and serves as an indicator of production stability and dyeing uniformity. A higher qualified rate indicates superior color consistency, while a sudden decrease may signal process abnormalities such as dye-bath instability, nozzle blockage, or uneven drying.

To facilitate industrial deployment, the qualified rate is updated continuously as the fabric moves through the system. The encoder-synchronized positional index enables direct mapping of Δ E results and qualified/unqualified labels to precise fabric locations, allowing rapid fault localization and downstream manual inspection when necessary.

Robustness and practical performance in industrial environments

Beyond roll-level judgment agreement, the robustness and practical deployability of an online color-difference inspection system are critical for real industrial applications. In this study, the proposed system was evaluated under continuous operating conditions on an actual textile production line, where fabric motion, surface texture variation, illumination fluctuation, and mechanical vibration are inevitably present.

During the experiments, the system operated continuously along the full length of each fabric roll, capturing images and computing color-difference values at fixed spatial intervals. No interruption or reinitialization was observed during the tested production-line runs, indicating stable operation under the present trial conditions. The encoder-synchronized acquisition mechanism enabled precise spatial alignment between captured images and fabric positions, supporting consistent color-difference evaluation along the moving fabric.

From a robustness perspective, the adopted preprocessing and color-representation strategy helped reduce the influence of common industrial disturbances. Bilateral filtering suppressed sensor noise while preserving texture boundaries, which is beneficial for maintaining stable clustering results on woven and knitted fabrics. The K-means-based dominant color extraction further reduced the influence of local texture irregularities and isolated noise pixels, resulting in smoother and more interpretable Δ E distributions along the fabric length in the tested samples.

In addition, the system achieved real-time performance on standard industrial hardware without requiring a high-end GPU or dedicated deep-learning accelerator for the core color-difference computation. The system uses standard industrial RGB line-scan cameras and a D65 linear LED illumination module rather than costly hyperspectral or multispectral devices. The lightweight computational pipeline, combined with a simple yet perceptually meaningful CMC(2:1) color-difference metric, allowed the system to meet the efficiency and stability requirements of the present production-line trial. These observations suggest that the proposed approach offers a practical balance between robustness, computational efficiency, and industrial applicability under the tested production-line conditions.

To evaluate computational feasibility, the processing behavior of the proposed system was observed under full-resolution operation during the production-line trial.

All experiments were conducted on an industrial PC equipped with an Intel i7-class processor and 16 GB RAM. A discrete GPU was available in the industrial computer for multi-camera display and interface rendering, but it was not required for the core color-difference calculation. Under the tested factory condition, the four 4K line-scan cameras operated at a stable line frequency matched to the production speed, and the online pipeline processed the acquired inspection segments in real time without interrupting fabric movement.

The observed processing behavior indicates that the proposed method can meet the real-time requirements of continuous online fabric inspection under the tested production-line conditions. Potential error sources were also examined qualitatively during deployment. The illumination shield and fixed linear LED illumination geometry reduced ambient-light variation, encoder synchronization reduced position offsets, and the fixed exposure configuration limited frame-to-frame brightness drift. Motion blur was controlled by matching exposure time to the line speed and by acquiring images after the fabric entered steady movement. Nevertheless, residual effects from strong texture, local reflectance changes, and mechanical vibration may still influence image-derived chromatic values; these factors are treated as practical limitations of the present production-line trial.

Limitations and future work

Although the proposed system indicates practical feasibility on an actual textile production line, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the current validation used ten fabric rolls, which is valuable for field deployment but insufficient to support broad claims across all fabric types, color depths, textures, and production conditions. Future work will expand the dataset to include more materials, complex patterns, and longer production batches. Second, the present evaluation used manual inspection as the main industrial reference. Manual inspection reflects current factory decision-making but remains subjective, and the present trial did not include a full benchmark against a calibrated spectrophotometer. Future studies should incorporate standard spectrophotometer measurements to quantify Δ E accuracy, repeatability, and reproducibility more rigorously. Third, the dominant-centroid strategy is designed mainly for fabrics with a single background color. For printed, striped, jacquard, gradient, or highly patterned fabrics, multi-cluster distribution descriptors or region-wise color modeling may be required. Fourth, the Δ E=1.0 tolerance was used as a practical threshold in this production-line trial; product-specific thresholds should be calibrated when material, gloss, weave structure, or customer standards differ. Finally, although the revised ablation study supports the selected filtering and clustering modules, a larger controlled experiment covering illumination nonuniformity, ambient light, motion blur, and positional offsets will be needed to further characterize system error sources.

Conclusion

In this study, a practical online fabric color difference detection system based on machine vision was developed and validated under real industrial conditions. The system integrates image acquisition, bilateral filtering, K-means clustering, RGB–CIELab color space conversion, and CMC(2:1)-based color-difference computation into a unified framework capable of continuous and real-time operation on textile production lines.

Experiments performed on ten fabric rolls indicated that the system can monitor chromatic variation trends along the fabric length under the tested conditions. For fabrics with stable dyeing quality, the measured Δ E values consistently remained within the acceptable tolerance range, whereas fabrics exhibiting visually noticeable color deviations showed pronounced increases in Δ E during online detection. The system’s roll-level judgments were consistent with manual inspection in the ten-roll production-line trial, indicating that the proposed objective evaluation can align with factory visual assessment under the tested conditions.

Compared with traditional manual inspection, the proposed system has the potential to improve inspection efficiency, reduce dependence on manual visual judgment, and provide continuous and traceable color monitoring. By delivering real-time feedback on chromatic trends, the system may support earlier identification of dyeing irregularities and assist quality-control decision-making in textile production.

Future work will focus on further evaluating illumination robustness, extending the system to more fabric formats and longer production batches, and developing adaptive or learning-based algorithms to improve performance under more complex production conditions. These developments may support further validation and wider practical application of the system in textile inspection scenarios.
