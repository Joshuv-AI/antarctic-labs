Identifying social determinants of health subgroups and their associations with health outcomes in a prospective US adult COVID-19 cohort: an analysis of the INSPIRE registry
Authors: Yohali Burrola-Mendez, Eugene Lin, Canada Parrish, Chialing Hsu, Mandy J Hill, Michael Gottlieb, Kristin L Rising, Lauren E Wisk, Nicole L Gentile, Rachel E Geyer, Robert A Weinstein, Michelle Santangelo, Kristyn Gatling, Jonathan W Dyal, Joann G Elmore, Robert M Rodriguez, Phillip Watts, Ji Chen, Zihan Diao, Erica S Spatz, Kwun CG Chan, K A Stephens, Kelli N O’Laughlin, The INSPIRE Group, Robert A Weinstein, Michelle Santangelo, Katherine Koo, Antonia Derden, Michael Gottlieb, Kristyn Gatling, Zohaib Ahmed, Minna Hassaballa, Amro (Marshall) Kaadan, Arjun Venkatesh, Erica S Spatz, Jeremiah Kinsman, Caitlin Malicki, Zhenqiu Lin, Shu-Xia Li, Huihui Yu

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify subgroups with similar social determinants of health (SDOH) characteristics using latent class analysis (LCA) and examine their associations with physical and mental health, cognitive function and missed workdays at 3 and 6 months post-SARS-CoV-2 infection. We hypothesised that intersecting SDOH factors would differentially influence COVID-19-related health outcomes across subgroups.DesignProspective cohort study from the Innovative Support for Patients with SARS-CoV-2 Infections Registry (INSPIRE), with longitudinal data collection and cross-sectional analyses at baseline, 3-month and 6-month follow-ups.SettingMulticentre registry across eight US academic medical centres (Chicago, Dallas, Houston, Los Angeles, New Haven, Philadelphia, San Francisco and Seattle).ParticipantsAdults aged ≥18 years, fluent in English or Spanish, with self-reported acute COVID-19 symptoms and a confirmed positive SARS-CoV-2 test within 42 days before enrolment (9 December 2020 to 12 August 2022), and access to an internet-connected device. Exclusions included incarceration, inability to provide informed consent, lack of confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection or no internet access. Of 3791 eligible participants with complete baseline data, 2897 (76.4%) completed the 3-month follow-up and 2666 (70.3%) completed the 6-month follow-up; most were aged 18–49 years (74–75%), female (66–67%), white (86.6–87.5%) and non-Hispanic (86.6–87.5%).Outcome measuresPrespecified primary outcomes were physical and mental health (Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)-29 V.2.1 T-scores for depression, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance, pain interference, physical function and social participation), cognitive function (PROMIS Cognitive Function Short Form 8 T-scores) and missed workdays due to illness (binary: >1 week vs ≤1 week, from a single-item survey). All measures were self-reported and collected at baseline, 3 months and 6 months; no changes from protocol.ResultsLCA identified a 4-class model as optimal (lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) after evaluating 1–7 class models; significant demographic differences (χ2 p<0.001 for gender, age, race, ethnicity, education and income distributions)). Sensitivity analysis using four age groups (18–29, 30–49, 50–64, ≥65 years) yielded higher BIC (45 430.8) than three groups (18–49, 50–64, ≥65 years; BIC=42 150.9), confirming the primary model. Class 3 (middle-aged, high-income non-Hispanic; n=599 (15.8%)) was the reference group. Compared with Class 3, Class 1 (lower-income, predominantly Hispanic young to middle-aged adults; n=499 (13.2%)) and Class 2 (lower-income, older, predominantly Black non-Hispanic; n=1828 (48.2%)) exhibited significantly worse outcomes across physical and mental health domains (eg, Class 1 3-month anxiety β=4.41 (95% CI 3.25 to 5.56, p<0.001); Class 2 3-month depression β=3.58 (95% CI 2.53 to 4.64, p<0.001)). Classes 1 and 2 also reported significantly worse cognitive function at both time points (eg, Class 1 3-month β=−3.29 (95% CI −4.77 to −1.82, p<0.001)) and Class 2 had significantly higher odds of missed workdays at 6 months (OR=1.853 (95% CI 1.192 to 2.880, p=0.006)). In contrast, Class 4 (young to middle-aged, highly educated, high-income non-Hispanic; n=865 (22.8%)) consistently reported the most favourable outcomes, including better physical function (3-month β=2.04, p<0.001) and lower pain interference compared with the reference group.ConclusionsIn this US prospective cohort, SDOH-based subgroups showed persistent disparities in health outcomes post-SARS-CoV-2 infection. Findings highlight the urgent need for intersectional approaches to address systemic inequities in post-COVID-19 recovery.Trial registration numberNCT04610515.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic further exposed and deepened the longstanding drivers of structural health inequalities within the US healthcare system.1 These drivers are deeply rooted in social, economic and political systems that have historically marginalised certain populations.2 Factors such as systemic racism, social distrust, income inequality, the negative aspects of social capital, limited access to quality healthcare and disparities in education and employment opportunities have long contributed to unequal health outcomes.3–5 Additionally, environmental conditions, such as residential segregation, food apartheid and inadequate public health infrastructure in certain communities, further exacerbate these inequities.6–8 The impact of SARS-CoV-2 was profoundly uneven, underlying how social determinants of health (SDOH) can exacerbate health outcomes and worsen existing societal disparities.9 10

Disparities in COVID-19 incidence, hospitalisation rates, mortality and morbidity have been well-documented among individuals identifying as Black or African American, Hispanic/Latinx, American Indian/Alaska Native (AI/AN), and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (NH/PI) compared with non-Hispanic white individuals.11–13 These ethnic and racial disparities are attributed to increased exposure to the SARS-CoV-2 virus, rooted in SDOH and related inequalities.14 15 For instance, counties in the South and West of the country, which have higher populations of Black and Latinx communities, lower median incomes and less formal education, reported higher excess deaths directly and indirectly attributed to COVID-19.16 Similarly, evidence has shown that income inequality at the county-level was positively associated with the risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection and COVID-19 deaths.17 Furthermore, in some urban settings, strong bonding ties have been linked to higher COVID-19 test positivity rates.3 While bonding supports group cohesion, it can hinder the spread of public health information, especially in marginalised communities with low trust in authorities.3

Traditional public health approaches often examine factors like race, income or education in isolation, while the intersectionality framework explores how marginalised social statuses interact, revealing interconnected systems of privilege and oppression (eg, racism, classism, colonialism, sexism, ableism).18 19 An intersectionality framework challenges the idea of isolated identity categories, recognising that multiple intersecting social identities shape experiences of power and privilege.18 Applying this lens to COVID-19 involves challenging the narrative of ‘vulnerable’ groups and understanding the complex interplay of power and social structures that influence health outcomes.9 10 In this study, we sought to identify groups with similar SDOH to examine their physical and mental health status, cognitive outcomes and workdays missed at 3-month and 6-month post-SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis, hypothesising that SDOH factors influence COVID-19-related health outcomes differently across identity groups.

Methods

Study design and setting

Data for this retrospective analysis were obtained from the Innovative Support for Patients with SARS-CoV-2 Infections Registry (INSPIRE), a prospective cohort study with longitudinal data collection between 9 December 2020 and 12 August 2022. INSPIRE is a multicentre, longitudinal registry that enrolled individuals with acute COVID-19 symptoms across the US.20 Eight sites contributed data: Rush University, Yale University, University of Washington, Thomas Jefferson University, University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center, UTHealth Houston, University of California, San Francisco and University of California, Los Angeles. The study was approved by the institutional review boards at the eight participating sites, and the full study protocol has been published.20 This study adheres to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guidelines.

Study participants

The cohort included adults (age ≥18 years) fluent in English or Spanish who had self-reported symptoms suggestive of acute SARS-CoV-2 infection at the time of testing (online supplemental table S1) and a positive SARS-CoV-2 test result using a US Food and Drug Administration-approved molecular or antigen-based assay within 42 days prior to enrolment. We excluded individuals who were incarcerated, unable to provide informed consent, lacked confirmation of SARS-CoV-2 infection or did not have access to an internet-connected device.

Participants were followed longitudinally and asked to complete patient-reported outcomes at enrolment and every 3 months thereafter. We included participants who completed the baseline survey and either the 3-month or 6-month survey, or both, excluding those with missing data at any time point.

Study variables

This study included three patient-reported outcomes of interest: physical and mental health status, cognition and workdays missed due to illness, originally collected for the INSPIRE longitudinal study.20

Physical and mental health status was assessed using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)-29 V.2.1 instrument21 which evaluates seven health domains (fatigue, pain interference, depressive symptoms, anxiety, sleep disturbance, physical function and ability to participate in social roles and activities) using a five-point rating scale per domain.21 22

Cognition was assessed using the PROMIS Cognitive Short Form 8 form which evaluates cognitive function over the past 7 days using an eight-item instrument with a five-response rating scale.23

PROMIS instruments use a T-score metric, where a score of 50 represents the average of the US general population, with a SD of 10. Higher scores indicate a greater degree of the measured outcome, which could reflect either a positive attribute (eg, social participation) or a negative one (eg, pain interference).24

This study also included a question on workdays missed due to illness, from the Return to work and activity status questionnaire designed for INSPIRE.20 The question asked: ‘Since before you had COVID-19 like symptoms (if the first follow-up survey) or, since your last survey, how many workdays or weeks did you miss because of health reasons?’. Response options were: 0–5 workdays, 6–10 workdays, 10–20 workdays and up to 4 weeks.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics summarised demographic and outcomes at three time points, with characteristics reported as counts and percentages. Continuous outcomes (PROMIS-29, Cognitive Function) were presented as means and SD, while workdays missed were represented as counts and percentages.

Latent class analysis (LCA) identified groups within our sample based on age, race, ethnicity, education and household income, with the final number of classes determined by Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).25 These variables represent individual characteristics as well as SDOH inequalities26 27 and were categorised for LCA: gender, age (18–49, 50–64 and 65+), race (white, Black or African American, Asian, AI/AN, NH/PI and other/multiracial), ethnicity (Hispanic vs non-Hispanic), education (high school or less, some college/2-year, 4-year degree and graduate+) and annual household income (<US$50 000, US$50 000–US$74 999, >US$75,000 and prefer not to answer). The household income categories, as captured in categorical brackets, align with those commonly used in public health and economic studies to distinguish between lower-to-middle-income and higher-income individuals.28 29 Variable categorisations were selected polytomously to preserve heterogeneity and capture intersecting SDOH, informed by epidemiological standards (eg, age groups reflecting life-stage vulnerabilities to COVID-19; income thresholds aligning with federal poverty and public health benchmarks).28 29 The optimal number of classes was determined by fitting models with 1–7 classes and selecting the solution with the lowest BIC. A sensitivity analysis was performed by categorising age into four groups (18–29, 30–49, 50–64, 65+) instead of three (18–49, 50–64, 65+) to assess model fit; BIC values were compared between these models, and the final model was selected based on the lowest BIC. Further sensitivity analyses incorporated indicators for US region (accounting for the eight diverse study sites) and enrolment wave: Wave 1 (≤December 2020; pre-vaccine), Wave 2 (January–May 2021; early rollout), Wave 3 (June 2021–January 2022; universal availability) and Wave 4 (≥February 2022; ‘return to normal’). Participants with complete baseline data and either the 3-month or 6-month follow-up were included, and multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE)30 was used to handle missing data across analyses. χ2 tests analysed differences across classes.

To avoid severe overparameterisation, several variables were reported descriptively rather than being included in the LCA algorithm: number of children in the household, insurance status, vaccination status, calculated household size (sum of members aged 18–64, ≥65 and <18) and marital status.

Regression analyses examined outcomes and class differences at 3-month and 6-month follow-up, using models that analysed cross-sectional data at each time point. Continuous outcomes were analysed with ordinary least squares regression, and binary outcomes with logistic regression. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05, and regression results for continuous outcomes at the 3-month and 6-month time points were presented as coefficients with 95% CIs and p values, while workdays missed were presented as ORs with 95% CIs and p values. All analyses were conducted using R (V.4.3.1).

Patient and public involvement

There was no involvement from study participants in terms of study design or study conduct. The public was to be informed of the results via planned peer-reviewed publications.

Results

Study population

We identified 3791 individuals with complete baseline data. Of these, 2897 were included at the 3-month time point and 2666 at the 6-month time point. Participants were primarily aged 18–49 (74–75%), female (66–67%) and white and non-Hispanic (86.6–87.5%). Around 70% had a 4-year degree or higher, and over 70% reported household incomes ≥US$50 000 (table 1). Means and SD of study variables are reported per time point.

Latent class analysis

Multiple LCA models were examined to identify the best fit (online supplemental table S2). The 4-class model was selected based on the lowest BIC indicating effective class separation. Statistically significant demographic differences (χ2 p value <0.001) confirmed distinct groupings (table 2). A sensitivity analysis using four age groups instead of three yielded a substantially higher BIC (45 430.8 vs 42 150.9). The three-group age stratification (18–49, 50–64, 65+) was therefore retained as the primary model (online supplemental table S3). Further sensitivity analyses confirmed model robustness when accounting for enrolment wave (online supplemental table S4) and US region across the eight diverse study sites (online supplemental table S5). Finally, to avoid severe over-parameterisation in the LCA algorithm, we opted to exclude and instead report the following variables descriptively: number of children, insurance status, vaccination status, total household size and marital status (online supplemental table S6).

Class 1 (lower-income, predominantly Hispanic young to middle-aged adults; n=499, 13.2%) was primarily young to middle-aged (89.6% aged 18–49 years), Hispanic (75.8%) individuals who were white (50.3%) or other/multiracial (41.9%), with 61.3% having some college/2-year degree or less, and 60.1% reporting a lower household income (<US$50 000).

Class 2 (lower-income, older, predominantly Black non-Hispanic; n=599, 15.8%) included mostly non-Hispanic (99.8%) individuals who were Black (45.4%) or white (42.6%), with 70.8% aged 18–49 years (and 29.2% aged ≥50 years), 88.1% having some college/2-year degree or less, and 70.5% reporting a lower household income (<US$50 000).

Class 3 (middle-aged, high-income non-Hispanic; n=865, 22.8%), which served as the reference group for all regression analyses, was predominantly middle-aged or older (58.7% aged 50–64 years; 25.3% aged 65+), non-Hispanic (96.1%), white (91.6%) individuals, with 66.5% having a 4-year degree or higher, and 77.5% reporting a higher household income (≥US$75 000). In accordance with recent methodological guidance on health equity research,31 32 Class 3 was selected as the reference group because its age and income distribution more closely approximates the median US adult population, allowing for a more balanced evaluation of both better and poorer health outcomes across the other classes.

Class 4 (young to middle-aged, highly educated, high-income non-Hispanic; n=1828, 48.2%) consisted mostly of young to middle-aged (99.1% aged 18–49 years), non-Hispanic (94.3%) individuals who were predominantly white (71.8%) or Asian (20.5%), with 98.3% having a 4-year degree or higher, and 69.3% reporting a higher household income (≥US$75 000).

Physical and mental health status

Significant differences in PROMIS-29 T-scores were observed across classes at both 3-month and 6-month time points, with patterns generally consistent over time.

In terms of mental health, Classes 1 and 2 reported significantly higher (worse) depression and anxiety scores at both time points compared with Class 3. For example, at 3 months, Class 1 showed substantial elevations in anxiety (β=4.41, 95% CI 3.25 to 5.56, p<0.001) and depression, with Class 2 displaying similarly high burdens (eg, 3-month depression β=3.58, 95% CI 2.53 to 4.64, p<0.001). Class 4 also reported higher depression and anxiety than Class 3, but the magnitude of these differences was much smaller (eg, 3-month depression β=1.22, 95% CI 0.44 to 2.00, p=0.002).

For physical symptoms, Classes 1 and 2 reported significantly worse fatigue and sleep disturbance than Class 3. Class 2 showed the largest increases in fatigue across the follow-up period (eg, 6-month β=3.90, 95% CI 2.79 to 5.01, p<0.001). Pain interference presented a diverging pattern: Class 2 experienced significantly higher (worse) pain interference at both timepoints (eg, 3-month β=2.98, p<0.001), whereas Class 4 reported significantly lower (better) pain interference than the Class 3 reference group (eg, 3-month β=−2.41, 95% CI −3.14 to −1.68, p<0.001).

Functional domains followed similar trends. Classes 1 and 2 scored significantly lower (worse) in both physical function and social participation compared with Class 3. Class 2 exhibited the most severe impairments in physical function (eg, 6-month β=−3.94, 95% CI −4.96 to −2.93, p<0.001). In sharp contrast, Class 4 demonstrated a physical advantage, showing significantly better physical function scores than Class 3 at both time points (eg, 3-month β=2.04, p<0.001).

Overall, Classes 1 and 2 experienced the most consistent physical and mental health impairments—including elevated depression, anxiety, fatigue and pain interference—relative to the Class 3 reference group, while Class 4 displayed comparatively milder effects.

Cognition

At both the 3-month and 6-month time points, significant differences in PROMIS Cognitive Function T-scores were observed across classes (table 4). Compared with the reference group (Class 3), Classes 1 and 2 reported significantly lower (worse) cognitive function. Class 1 exhibited the largest and most consistent deficits at both 3 months (β=−3.29, 95% CI −4.77 to −1.82, p<0.001) and 6 months (β=−3.59, 95% CI −4.93 to −2.25, p<0.001). Class 2 also showed substantial impairments relative to Class 3 at both time points (3 month β=−2.55, 95% CI −4.24 to −0.86, p=0.005; 6-month β=−2.90, 95% CI −4.37 to −1.44, p<0.001). Class 4 did not differ significantly from the reference group at either time point (3-month p=0.223; 6-month p=0.251). Overall, mean cognitive scores remained relatively stable over time across all classes, indicating persistent cognitive burden for those in Classes 1 and 2.

Missed workdays

Logistic regression examined the odds of missing work due to illness at 3-month and 6-month time points (table 5). Compared with the reference group (Class 3), Class 2 had the highest percentage of individuals missing work at both time points (10.9% at 3 months and 7.8% at 6 months) and demonstrated significantly higher odds of missing work at the 6-month mark (OR=1.853, 95% CI 1.192 to 2.880, p=0.006). Class 1 showed elevated rates of missed work (10.6% at 3 months, 6.8% at 6 months), but these differences did not reach statistical significance when compared with Class 3 (3-month OR=1.234, p=0.265; 6-month OR=1.591, p=0.056). Class 4 consistently reported the lowest rates of missed work (7.4% at 3 months, 4.2% at 6 months), with no significant difference from Class 3 (6-month OR=0.944, 95% CI 0.634 to 1.406, p=0.777). Across all classes, the percentage of individuals reporting missed work decreased from the 3-month to the 6-month follow-up.

Discussion

LCA identified four distinct groups in our cohort study stratifying participants (N=3791) based on age, race, ethnicity, education and household income variables. Overall, participants in Classes 1 and 2—characterised by higher proportions of young to middle-aged Hispanic individuals with lower incomes (Class 1) and older Black non-Hispanic individuals with lower incomes (Class 2)—reported significantly worse physical and mental health, and cognitive function at 3 months and 6 months post-SARS-CoV-2 infection compared with the reference group (Class 3: middle-aged, high-income non-Hispanic). Additionally, Class 2 had significantly higher odds of missed workdays at 6 months. In contrast, Class 4 (young, highly educated, high-income non-Hispanic) showed generally better or comparable outcomes to the reference group across domains. These patterns were generally stable over time, highlighting persistent disparities driven by intersecting SDOH. Furthermore, adults aged 18–49 are not a monolithic group.33 The LCA stratified this cohort by socioeconomic realities into structurally disadvantaged (Class 1) and highly advantaged (Class 4) profiles, highlighting that the protective benefits of youth depend heavily on socioeconomic privilege rather than age alone.34 35 Class demographics drive these disparities: compounded stressors (low income, minoritised identities) in Classes 1 and 2 create systemic barriers that prolong recovery,36 37 while Class 4’s high education and income provide robust socioeconomic buffers for faster healing.38 39 Although all groups showed slight symptom improvement over 6 months, these overall trends mask persistent disparities.40 Structurally disadvantaged subgroups (Classes 1 and 2) consistently retained significantly worse outcomes than the reference group, leaving initial health inequities unmitigated.41 42

Our findings highlight exactly why these intersectional differences matter: examining single variables like age or race obscures the reality of compounded risk.43 44 For example, young to middle-aged, highly educated, predominantly white individuals (Class 4) leveraged compounding structural advantages, such as higher income and job flexibility, to achieve rapid symptom recovery.38 In stark contrast, non-white individuals with lower educational attainment and income (Classes 1 and 2) experienced persistently worse outcomes across all domains. For these disadvantaged groups, the synergistic burdens of systemic inequities, occupational hazards and reduced healthcare access entirely negated the typical protective effects of age,34 45 demonstrating how intersecting marginalised identities exponentially increase vulnerability to protracted COVID-19 impacts.37

The unique socio-environmental context of the COVID-19 pandemic actively exacerbated these baseline inequities.46 47 Structurally disadvantaged individuals (Classes 1 and 2) were disproportionately employed as essential workers unable to isolate,48 experienced more severe pandemic-related income shocks49 and faced systemic barriers to accessing acute and post-COVID-19 care.41 Conversely, highly advantaged individuals (Class 4) possessed the structural resources to mitigate these specific pandemic stressors through remote work capabilities, stable financial reserves and continuous healthcare access, thereby facilitating a more rapid and complete recovery.49 50

Physical and mental health status

Fatigue, pain interference, depression, anxiety and sleep disturbance

Classes 1 and 2 reported significantly higher (worse) scores in fatigue, depression, anxiety and sleep disturbance compared with the reference group (Class 3) at both time points. Class 2 notably experienced the largest increases in fatigue and also suffered from significantly worse pain interference. These findings align with existing literature on how socioeconomic vulnerabilities, such as lower income and minority status, exacerbate mental health burdens during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic.51–55 For instance, job insecurity and systemic barriers disproportionately affect Hispanic and lower-income populations, leading to heightened psychological distress and sleep issues.51 53 Interestingly, some previous literature highlights resilience factors in older Black communities, including stronger social networks, faith-based support and adaptive coping mechanisms honed through historical adversity.56 57 However, our findings indicate that for Class 2, these protective buffers were likely overwhelmed by the severe compounding effects of lower income and intersecting SDOH. The pronounced physical symptom burdens, including severe pain interference in Class 2, underscore the widespread and unmitigated impact of COVID-19 on chronic pain in structurally marginalised groups. Conversely, Class 4 consistently reported milder symptoms, including significantly lower (better) pain interference, reflecting how higher education and income successfully insulate individuals from protracted symptom severity. While overall PROMIS T-scores clustered near 50, the sustained 3-point to 5-point deficits in Classes 1 and 2 constitute minimally important clinical differences,58 59 reflecting tangibly worse symptom burdens than advantaged peers.

Physical function and social participation

As anticipated, Classes 1 and 2 exhibited significantly lower (worse) physical function and social participation scores relative to Class 3. This is consistent with the higher prevalence of comorbidities (eg, hypertension, diabetes) in lower-income, Hispanic and Black populations, which increase vulnerability to long covid symptoms.60 61 These groups’ intersecting SDOH—such as limited healthcare access and economic stressors—likely compound functional impairments, reducing quality of life in ways that stifled access to basic human needs.62 While older Black adults have historically shown adaptive communal strengths in navigating health crises to survive societal barriers,63 64 the physical function deficits observed in Class 2 suggest that systemic physiological wear-and-tear (allostatic load) ultimately drove functional decline. Social participation remained impaired across these vulnerable classes, potentially linked to ongoing anxiety and depression limiting interpersonal engagement.62 In sharp contrast, Class 4 demonstrated a significant physical advantage with better physical function scores, emphasising how socioeconomic privilege accelerates physical recovery.

Cognition

Classes 1 and 2 reported significantly lower (worse) cognitive function compared with Class 3 at both time points, with Class 1 showing the most pronounced and consistent deficits. This aligns with evidence that COVID-19 exacerbates cognitive issues in adults facing SDOH barriers like lower education and income.65–68 Although age is typically a primary driver of cognitive decline, the severe deficits in the young to middle-aged Hispanic group (Class 1) and older Black group (Class 2) suggest that lack of socioeconomic resources, occupational stress and structural barriers may be equally, if not more, detrimental to post-COVID-19 cognitive recovery than age alone. Class 4 showed no significant differences from the reference group, indicating that higher socioeconomic status and education may provide a robust cognitive reserve.65 68 Scores remained relatively stable over time across all classes, emphasising the need for longitudinal studies on cognitive persistence in diverse, under-resourced groups.65 68

Missed workdays

Compared with Class 3, Class 2 had significantly higher odds of missing work at 6 months, and Class 1 showed elevated rates of work absences. These findings reflect how lower-income populations with intersecting racial and ethnic vulnerabilities face unique recovery challenges.69 70 Limited paid sick leave and remote work options in lower-income groups exacerbate disparities, with financial instability pushing presenteeism and increasing long-term absence risks.69–72 Broader work conditions, including food insecurity and essential roles, likely amplified these inequities for Classes 1 and 2.71 72 Class 4 consistently reported the lowest rates of missed work, further underscoring the occupational protections and workplace flexibility afforded by high educational attainment and upper-income brackets.69 70

Overall, our findings reveal that Classes 1 and 2, marked by lower incomes intersecting with Hispanic ethnicity (Class 1) or older age and Black race (Class 2), experienced the most severe and consistent health and functional impairments. This underscores systemic inequities rooted in racism, economic barriers and healthcare access, perpetuating poorer outcomes for marginalised groups.73 Addressing these disparities requires multifaceted interventions, including policy reforms for equitable sick leave, culturally sensitive healthcare and community programmes to bolster resilience.57 73 Our study adds to evidence advocating transformative public health changes for equity.60

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, reliance on patient-reported outcomes introduces potential recall and response biases and may not fully reflect objective clinical measures of physical and cognitive function. Nevertheless, self-report measures do remain the standard in symptom assessment and the scales used were psychometrically well validated.17–20 Unlike studies using only medical visit data, our study included both people who engaged in medical follow-up and those who did not.

Second, we did not account for pre-existing mental health conditions, comorbidities or baseline cognitive function, which may have influenced post-COVID-19 outcomes. Third, the observational nature of the registry data and reliance on cross-sectional analyses at specific time points prevent causal inferences. While associations between latent classes and outcomes were identified, residual confounding may still be present due to unmeasured variables, such as pre-existing mental health and chronic conditions, and could potentially affect the interpretation of the impact of COVID infection on our outcomes, as they may contribute to variations in mental and physical health recovery post-infection.

Fourth, although half of our participating sites were in areas with large Hispanic communities and patient-reported outcomes were available in Spanish, less than 14% of participants identified as Hispanic. The over-representation of white, non-Hispanic individuals in health-related studies contributes to systematic healthcare disparities.74 75 Barriers to participation among Black/African American, Hispanic/Latinx, Asian American, AI/AN, NH/PI and other under-represented groups include complex factors such as distrust of the healthcare system, fear of mistreatment and historical experiences of racism, potential deportation and discrimination.76–78 Additionally, the INSPIRE registry’s convenience sample, recruited via academic medical centres and requiring internet access and English/Spanish fluency, is not nationally representative (eg, under-representing AI/AN, NH/PI and lower-income groups), which may limit generalisability to broader US populations or those with greater SDOH burdens. Our highly educated cohort requires distinguishing between education (a stable socioeconomic proxy) and income,79 which better captures acute, fluctuating stressors during pandemic disruptions.52 Their distinct impacts on COVID-19 recovery must be interpreted carefully.38 Future research should implement strategic, culturally competent and sensitive, and inclusive recruitment efforts to ensure greater demographic diversity to better reflect US population diversity.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that individuals in Classes 1 and 2—predominantly lower-income Hispanic young to middle-aged adults (Class 1) and lower-income older Black non-Hispanic individuals (Class 2)—reported poorer physical and mental health, worse cognitive function, and (for Class 2) higher odds of missed workdays at the 3-month and 6-month time points after SARS-CoV-2 infection compared with the middle-aged, high-income non-Hispanic reference group (Class 3). In contrast, Class 4 (young to middle-aged, high-income, highly educated non-Hispanic) experienced the most favourable outcomes. Applying an intersectional approach illuminates how interconnected SDOH and systemic oppression drive these disparities, calling for equitable policies to mitigate long-term COVID-19 impacts.
