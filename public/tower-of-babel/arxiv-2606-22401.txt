Introduction

In this paper we are interested in the problem of determining whether the (higher) Pythagoras numbers are finite or not for some functions rings arising in real algebraic geometry.
Let us start with a definition.

Definition 0.1
.

For a commutative ring
R
with identity and a positive integer
n>0
its
2n
-Pythagoras number, denoted by
p_{2n}(R)
, is the smallest positive integer
g
such that any element which is a sum of
2n
th powers can be written as a sum of at most
g

2n
th powers. If such number does not exist, we put
p_{2n}(R)=\infty
.

Here, we are interested only in sums of even powers. This is because, if we consider sums of
n
th powers for
n
odd, then
-1
is an
n
th power, and the
n
-Pythagoras number is finite, provided that
n!
is invertible in
R
(cf.
[
18
, Introduction]
).

Quite a lot is known about the
2
-Pythagoras number. Problem of determining finiteness of those invariants is particularly interesting in the case of a real rings (i.e.
-1
is not a sum of squares). Any affine
\mathbb{R}
-algebra of dimension
1
has finite
2
-Pythagoras number
[
9
]
. On the other hand, any excellent ring of real dimension at least 3 has infinite
2
-Pythagoras number
[
14
]
. Dimension
2
is least understood. We have
p_{2n}(\mathbb{R}[x,y])=\infty

[
9
,
19
]
as well as
p_{2n}(\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f(x,y)}])=\infty
for polynomials
f(x,y)
satisfying some mild conditions (see
[
8
,
19
]
). Surprisingly, it was recently proved that
p_{2}(\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))=4
(see
[
20
]
), where
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is the ring of regular functions on the real plane. Let us also mention several recent papers on this topic
[
6
,
7
,
11
,
13
,
17
,
22
,
23
,
28
]
.

Higher Pythagoras number are much less understood. It is known that the theory of higher degree forms is much more complicated, than that of quadratic forms. Let us mention few papers on this topic

The content of the paper is as follows. Section 1 contains the treatment of higher Pythagoras numbers of the coordinate rings of singular planar curves of the form
\{x^{M}=y^{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
for coprime positive integers
m,M
. Next Section is devoted to the study of the
2s

1.
Sums of even powers on singular curves

In this section we will be interested in sums of even powers in the coordinate rings of singular planar curves. Our motivation is the
[
9
, Note added in proof]
. It is said there that the rings of the form
\mathbb{R}[t^{a},t^{2a-1}]
have unbounded Pythagoras numbers. The proof of this fact does not seem to be published. Main result of this section is generalization of the aforementioned observation to higher even powers. We start with some definitions.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an algebraic subset and let
\mathcal{I}(X)
be the ideal of all polynomials vanishing on
X
.

Definition 1.1
.

We define the coordinate ring of
X
,
\mathbb{R}[X]
, as the quotient

\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2}\dots,x_{d}]/\mathcal{I}(X)

We will be mostly interested in coordinate rings of curves. Let
m<M
be two coprime positive integers. Define
X_{m,M}:=\{x^{M}=y^{m}\}
. It is an irreducible, singular curve, and so its coordinate ring is an integral domain. We will study sums of even powers in the ring
\mathbb{R}[X_{m,M}]
.

To state results about sums of squares more easily, let us define the notion of length.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity,
a\in R
and
n>0
be a positive integer. We define the
2n
-length of
a
,
\ell_{2n,R}(a)
as the smallest number of
2n
th powers needed to represent
a
. If such number does not exist, we define
\ell_{2n,R}(a)=\infty
. Since element
a
can be usually considered in different rings, we put the
R
to stress out in which ring length is considered.

The main result of this section is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
{\big\{(m_{i},M_{i})\big\}}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

be a sequence of coprime tuples of positive integers such that for every
i
,
m_{i}<M_{i}
and
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}m_{i}=\infty
. Then

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}p_{2n}\big(\mathbb{R}[X_{m_{i},M_{i}}]\big)=\infty

Before we start the proof, we need some preliminary results. Following fact was proven for
n=1
in
[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
and for
n>1
in
[
19
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any positive integer
n\geq 1
the equality
p_{2n}\big(\mathbb{R}[x,y]\big)=\infty
holds.

Let us prove a crucial lemma.

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
m<M
be a pair of coprime positive integers and
X_{m,M}
be the corresponding curve. Let

\varphi:\mathbb{R}[x,y]\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{R}[X_{m,M}]

be the natural projection. If the degree of
f
is strictly smaller than
m
, then

\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[x,y]}(f)=\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[X_{m,M}]}\big(\varphi(f)\big).

Proof.

Since
\varphi
is a surjection, inequality
\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[x,y]}(f)\geq\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[X_{m,M}]}\big(\varphi(f)\big)
holds. To finish the proof it is therefore enough to show that opposite inequality holds. We will restrict ourselves to the nontrivial case, that is
\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[X_{m,M}]}\big(\varphi(f)\big)<\infty
.

First of all, the coordinate ring of
X_{m,M}
is easily seen to be isomorphic to the ring
\mathbb{R}[t^{m},t^{M}]
. To stress out that we work in this particular ring, rather than in
\mathbb{R}[X_{m,M}]
, we denote by
\widetilde{\varphi}
the epimorphism
\widetilde{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}[x,y]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}[t^{m},t^{M}]
, and so, we will consider
\widetilde{\varphi}(f)
instead of
\varphi(f)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be such that
\deg f<m
. Since
\deg\big(\widetilde{\varphi}(x)\big),\deg\big(\widetilde{\varphi}(y)\big)\leq M
, we obtain the bound

\deg\big(\widetilde{\varphi}(f)\big)\leq M\deg(f)<m\cdot M.

Let us denote
\ell:=\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[t^{m},t^{M}]}\big(\widetilde{\varphi}(f)\big)
and let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}[t^{m},t^{M}]
be such that

\widetilde{\varphi}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}g_{i}^{2n}.

We can immediately observe that for any
i=1,2,\dots,\ell
inequality
2n\cdot\deg(g_{i})\leq\deg\big(\widetilde{\varphi}(f)\big)<mM
holds, hence
\deg(g_{i})<\frac{mM}{2n}
.

To finish the proof we will define a
\mathbb{R}
-linear map
\psi:\mathbb{R}[t^{m},t^{M}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}[x,y]
which satisfies
\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\psi=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}[t^{m},t^{M}]}
. If
t^{k}\in\mathbb{R}[t^{m},t^{M}]
, then there exists
a_{k},b_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
satisfying
k=a_{k}m+b_{k}M
. Let us therefore define
\psi(t^{k}):=x^{a_{k}}y^{b_{k}}
and extend linearly. By construction,

\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\psi(t^{k})=t^{a_{k}m+b_{k}M}=t^{k}

hence indeed equality
\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\psi=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}[t^{m},t^{M}]}
holds. Additionally, we have the following chain of inequalities

\deg\psi(t^{k})=a_{k}+b_{k}=\frac{1}{m}\big(a_{k}m+b_{k}m\big)\leq\frac{1}{m}\big(a_{k}m+b_{k}M\big)=\frac{k}{m}=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\deg(t^{k}).

As a consequence, the inequality
\deg\psi(h)\leq\frac{1}{m}\cdot\deg(h)
holds for any
h\in\mathbb{R}[t^{m},t^{M}]
.

Now let
\mathfrak{s}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}{\psi(g_{i})}^{2n}
and observe that the following inequalities holds

\deg(\mathfrak{s})\leq\max_{i=1,\dots,\ell}\Big\{2n\cdot\deg\psi(g_{i})\Big\}\leq\max_{i=1,\dots,\ell}\bigg\{\frac{2n}{m}\cdot\deg(g_{i})\bigg\}<\max_{i=1,\dots,\ell}\bigg\{\frac{2n}{m}\cdot\frac{mM}{2n}\bigg\}=M.

Our aim now, is to show that
\mathfrak{s}=f
. To accomplish this, let
\varepsilon:=f-\mathfrak{s}
. We have

\widetilde{\varphi}(\varepsilon)=\widetilde{\varphi}(f)-\widetilde{\varphi}(\mathfrak{s})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}g_{i}^{2n}-\widetilde{\varphi}\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}{\psi(g_{i})}^{2n}\Bigg)=\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}g_{i}^{2n}-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}{\Big((\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\psi)(g_{i})\Big)}^{2n}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}g_{i}^{2n}-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}g_{i}^{2n}=0,

hence
\varepsilon\in\ker(\widetilde{\varphi})
. However, it is a straightforward calculation that
\ker(\widetilde{\varphi})=(x^{M}-y^{m})
, hence
\deg(\varepsilon)\geq\deg(x^{M}-y^{m})=M
or
\varepsilon=0
. Since
\deg(f)<M
and
\deg(\mathfrak{s})<M
we obtain
\deg(\varepsilon)\leq\max\big\{\deg(f),\deg(\mathfrak{s})\big\}<M
which implies
\varepsilon=0
. This finishes the proof, since we obtain

f=\mathfrak{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}{\psi(g_{i})}^{2n},

which shows that
\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[x,y]}(f)\leq\ell=\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[X_{m,M}]}\big(\widetilde{\varphi}(f)\big)
as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3

It is enough to show that

\liminf_{i\rightarrow\infty}p_{2n}\big(\mathbb{R}[X_{m_{i},M_{i}}]\big)\geq k

for all positive integers
k
. Let
k
be an arbitrary positive integer. Since
p_{2n}\big(\mathbb{R}[x,y]\big)=\infty
, there exists a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
such that
\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[x,y]}(f)\geq k
. Let us put
d:=\deg(f)
. Since
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}m_{i}=\infty
, there exists
I\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m_{i}>d
for all
i\geq I
. Therefore for any
i\geq I
we can now apply Lemma
1.5
to obtain

k\leq\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[x,y]}(f)=\ell_{2n,\mathbb{R}[X_{m_{i},M_{i}}]}\big(\varphi_{(m_{i},M_{i})}(f)\big),

where
\varphi_{(m_{i},M_{i})}
denotes the natural projection from
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
onto
\mathbb{R}[X_{m_{i},M_{i}}]
.

Consequently,
p_{2n}\big(\mathbb{R}[X_{m_{i},M_{i}}]\big)\geq k
for all
i\geq I
, hence inequality

\liminf_{i\rightarrow\infty}p_{2n}\big(\mathbb{R}[X_{m_{i},M_{i}}]\big)\geq k

indeed holds for arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
as desired.
∎

with the results from

2.
Sums of squares on surfaces

In this section we record some generalizations of the results in
[
8
]
to rings obtained by adjoining square roots of several polynomials. Those rings correspond to algebraic surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In
[
8
]
only coordinate rings of surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
were considered.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a finite sequence
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
and a subset
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}=[n]
we will write

f_{S}=\prod_{i\in S}f_{i}.

We will call such a sequence square-independent if none of the non-empty products
f_{S}
is a square in
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be a polynomial and
d=\deg_{y}f
. Let
\alpha x^{b}y^{d}
be a monomial with the largest possible
b
and nonzero coefficient
\alpha
among all such monomials in
f(x,y)
. Then we will call
\alpha
the
y
-leading coefficient of
f
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be square-independent polynomials. Assume that they have a common zero and their
y
-leading coefficients are positive. Then

p_{2}\left(\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\right)=\infty.

Proof.

Consider a sequence of positive integers
(r_{m})_{m\geq 1}
such that

r_{1}>1+\max_{i}\deg f_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad r_{k}>\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}r_{i}.

We now define a sequence of polynomials
(F_{m})_{m\geq 1}
by letting
F_{1}=1
and

F_{n+1}=F_{n}\left(y-x^{r_{n}}\right)^{2}+1.

Assume to the contrary that the 2-Pythagoras number of
\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]
is finite and denote its value by
L
. Hence we have

F_{L+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}h_{i}^{2},

for some
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]
. Write

h_{i}=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}a_{i,S}\sqrt{f_{S}},

where
f_{\varnothing}=1
.

This yields

F_{L+1}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}f_{S}a_{\nu,S}^{2}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}a_{\nu,\varnothing}^{2}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}\sum_{\varnothing\neq S\subseteq[n]}f_{S}a_{\nu,S}^{2}.

Substituting
y=x^{r_{L}}
gives

1=\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}a_{\nu,\varnothing}(x,x^{r_{L}})^{2}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}\sum_{\varnothing\neq S\subseteq[n]}f_{S}(x,x^{r_{L}})a_{\nu,S}(x,x^{r_{L}})^{2}.

Since every nonzero term on the right hand side has a positive leading coefficient we obtain

a_{\nu,S}(x,x^{r_{L}})=0\qquad(\varnothing\neq S\subseteq[n]),

and

a_{\nu,\varnothing}(x,x^{r_{L}})=c_{\nu}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}c_{\nu}^{2}=1.

After an orthogonal transformation (see
[
25
, Theorem 8.1.2]
) we may thus assume

a_{L,\varnothing}=1+(y-x^{r_{L}})b_{L,\varnothing}

and

a_{\nu,\varnothing}=(y-x^{r_{L}})b_{\nu,\varnothing}\qquad(1\leq\nu\leq L-1).

Substituting these expressions into our equation, we get

\displaystyle F_{L}(y-x^{r_{L}})^{2}+1

\displaystyle=1+2(y-x^{r_{L}})b_{L,\varnothing}+(y-x^{r_{L}})^{2}b_{L,\varnothing}^{2}

\displaystyle+(y-x^{r_{L}})^{2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{L-1}b_{\nu,\varnothing}^{2}+(y-x^{r_{L}})^{2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}\sum_{\varnothing\neq S\subseteq[n]}f_{S}b_{\nu,S}^{2}.

After cancelling the
1
’s, we see that
(y-x^{r_{L}})
divides
b_{L,\varnothing}
, so we can write
b_{L,\varnothing}=t(y-x^{r_{L}})
. Let
D=1+\max\deg f_{i}
. Consider the degree defined on monomials as

\deg_{D}(x^{a}y^{b})=a+Db,

and break ties by exponent of
x
. Further extend it onto all polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
in the natural way. Since
r_{m}>D
, we see that
{\deg_{D}(y-x^{r_{m}})=r_{m}}
. On the other hand
D>\max\deg f_{i}
, so the coefficient next to the highest
\deg_{D}
term of
f_{i}
is its
y
-leading coefficient. Cancelling
(y-x^{r_{L}})^{2}
from our equation we obtain

(1)

F_{L}=2t+\left(y-x^{r_{L}}\right)^{2}t^{2}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{L-1}b_{\nu,\varnothing}^{2}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}\sum_{\varnothing\neq S\subseteq[n]}f_{S}b_{\nu,S}^{2}.

Every nonzero square has positive
\deg_{D}
leading coefficient, and by our previous observation every nonzero term
f_{S}b_{\nu,S}^{2}
also has positive
\deg_{D}
leading coefficient. If
t\neq 0
then we see that
\deg_{D}
of the right hand side is at least
2r_{L}
. On the other hand the left hand side has
\deg_{D}
smaller than that by construction. Thus
t=0
.

Equation (
1
) now becomes

F_{L}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{L-1}b_{\nu,\varnothing}^{2}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}\sum_{\varnothing\neq S\subseteq[n]}f_{S}b_{\nu,S}^{2}.

We have reduced the number of unweighted square terms by one. Repeating the same argument with
F_{L},\dots,F_{2}
we obtain an identity

F_{1}=\sum_{\lambda}f_{S_{\lambda}}q_{\lambda}^{2},

However
F_{1}=1
, while evaluating at the common zero of
f_{i}
the right hand side vanishes. This finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be square-independent polynomials. Assume that they have a common zero and write
f_{i}=h_{i}+g_{i}
, where
h_{i}
is the homogeneous part of
f_{i}
of degree
\deg f_{i}
. Suppose there exists
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
such that
h_{i}(v)>0
for every
i
. Then

p_{2}\left(\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\right)=\infty.

Proof.

Choose
M\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
M(0,1)=v
, and put

\widetilde{f}_{i}=f_{i}\circ M.

The highest homogeneous part of
\widetilde{f}_{i}
is
h_{i}\circ M
, and

(h_{i}\circ M)(0,1)=h_{i}(v)>0.

Thus the coefficient of
y^{\deg f_{i}}
in
\widetilde{f}_{i}
is positive.
In particular, each
\widetilde{f}_{i}
has positive
y
-leading coefficient.
Since
M
induces an isomorphism of the corresponding rings, the
claim follows from Theorem
2.3
applied to

\widetilde{f}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{f}_{n}
.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be square-independent polynomials such that each
f_{i}
is a sum of squares in
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
. Then

p_{2}\left(\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\right)=\infty.

Proof.

Since each
f_{i}
is a sum of squares, every
f_{S}
is also a sum of squares. Indeed, if

f_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}g_{i,j}^{2},

then a product
f_{S}=\prod_{i\in S}f_{i}
can be expanded as

f_{S}=\sum_{(j_{i})_{i\in S}}\left(\prod_{i\in S}g_{i,j_{i}}\right)^{2}.

Thus each
f_{S}
has finite length in
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
and denote it by
\ell_{S}
. Set

M=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\ell_{S}.

Assume to the contrary, that
p_{2}(\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}])=L
. Since
p_{2}(\mathbb{R}[x,y])=\infty
we can choose a sequence
(G_{m})_{m\geq 1}
of polynomials such that the length of
G_{m}
is
m
in
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
. We may write

G_{m}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}h_{\nu}^{2},

for some
h_{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]
. Expanding and taking the
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
component yields

(2)

G_{m}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{L}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}f_{S}a_{\nu,S}^{2}.

Now for each
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
choose a representation of
f_{S}
as a sum of
\ell_{S}
squares

f_{S}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{S}}u_{S,j}^{2}.

Then

f_{S}a_{\nu,S}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{S}}(u_{S,j}a_{\nu,S})^{2}.

Therefore the right hand side of (
2
) contains at most
L\sum\ell_{S}=LM
squares. Hence, by taking
m>LM
we obtain a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be square-independent polynomials. Let

K=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ f_{i}(x,y)\geq 0\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,n\}.

If
K=\varnothing
, then

p_{2}\left(R[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\right)<\infty.

Proof.

We have an isomorphism

\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\cong\mathbb{R}[x,y,z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]/I,

where

I=\left(z_{1}^{2}-f_{1},\dots,z_{n}^{2}-f_{n}\right).

Then

\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(I)=\left\{(x,y,z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}:\ z_{i}^{2}=f_{i}(x,y)\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,n\right\}.

Note that if
K=\varnothing
, then
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(I)=\varnothing
. Thus, by the real Nullstellensatz, we get that
-1
is a sum of squares of polynomials modulo
I
. Hence, passing to the quotient, we can write

-1=\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{i}^{2},\qquad\text{where }g_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{n}}].

Thus, given an arbitrary element
a\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]
, we have

a=\left(\frac{a+1}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{a-1}{2}\cdot g_{i}\right)^{2},

and so

p_{2}\left(R[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\right)\leq k+1<\infty.

∎

Remark 2.7
.

It is worth to point out the problem of computing 2-Pythagoras number for projective normal surfaces was carried out in
[
24
]
. Surfaces whose coordinate rings are considered in this paper can be smooth or singular.

We now turn our attention to general even powers. It turns out that changing the approach slightly, it is possible to extend the result from Theorem
2.3
to higher Pythagoras numbers.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be square-independent, non-constant polynomials. Assume that their
y
-leading coefficients are positive. Then for any integer
s>1
we have

p_{2s}\left(\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\right)=\infty.

Proof.

We will carry out the proof for the case
s=2
. The argument for the higher powers works similarly.

Similarly to Theorem
2.3
, consider a sequence of positive integers
(r_{m})_{m\geq 1}
such that

r_{1}>1+\max_{i}\deg f_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad r_{k}>\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}r_{i}.

Further, define a sequence of polynomials
(F_{m})_{m\geq 1}
by letting
F_{1}=1
and

F_{n+1}=F_{n}\left(y-x^{r_{n}}\right)^{4}+1.

Assume now to the contrary that

p_{2s}\left(\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\right)=L<\infty.

Then we have

(3)

F_{L+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}h_{i}^{4},

for some
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]
. Write

h_{i}=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}a_{i,S}\sqrt{f_{S}}.

Consider now the group of automorphisms

G=\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathbb{R}[x,y]}\left(\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\right)

of
\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]
as an
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
algebra.
Note that every element of
G
is determined by its value at
\sqrt{f_{i}}
which is sent to a root of
T^{2}-f_{i}
, hence to
\pm\sqrt{f_{i}}
. Conversely, by square-independence, the independent sign changes of the elements
\sqrt{f_{i}}
all define automorphisms. Thus
G
is precisely the group of sign changes of
\sqrt{f_{i}}
. In particular
G\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{n}
.

Applying any element
\sigma\in G
to equation (
3
) yields

F_{L+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sigma(h_{i})^{4}.

For any
u\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]
denote for convenience

\overline{u}:=u(x,x^{r_{L}}),

and write
\gamma=y-x^{r_{L}}
. Setting
\gamma=0
gives us

1=F_{L+1}(x,x^{r_{L}})=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\overline{\sigma(h_{i})}^{\,4}.

It follows that every function
\overline{\sigma(h_{i})}
is bounded for sufficiently large
x
.

For
T\subseteq[n]
, let

\chi_{T}:G\longrightarrow\{\pm 1\}

be the character determined by

\sigma(\sqrt{f_{T}})=\chi_{T}(\sigma)\sqrt{f_{T}}.

Fix a subset
T\subseteq[n]
, an index
i
and consider a weighted average of the form

(4)

\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{T}(\sigma)\sigma(h_{i})=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{T}(\sigma)\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}a_{i,S}\chi_{S}(\sigma)\sqrt{f_{S}}.

Recall, that by character orthogonality we have (see
[
21
]
for details)

\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{T}(\sigma)\chi_{S}(\sigma)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\;S=T\\
0,&\text{if}\;S\neq T\end{cases}

Hence we can rearrange (
4
) into

\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}a_{i,S}\sqrt{f_{S}}\left(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{T}(\sigma)\chi_{S}(\sigma)\right)=a_{i,T}\sqrt{f_{T}}.

After substituting
\gamma=0
this average thus yields

\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{T}(\sigma)\overline{\sigma(h_{i})}=a_{i,T}(x,x^{r_{L}})\sqrt{f_{T}(x,x^{r_{L}})}.

Since
\overline{\sigma(h_{i})}
are bounded, the same must be true for
a_{i,T}(x,x^{r_{L}})\sqrt{f_{T}(x,x^{r_{L}})}
. Hence

a_{i,T}(x,x^{r_{L}})=0\qquad(\varnothing\neq T\subseteq[n]),

and

a_{i,\varnothing}(x,x^{r_{L}})=c_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{L}c_{i}^{4}=1.

Consequently, we may write

h_{i}=c_{i}+\gamma g_{i},\qquad g_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}].

Consider as before
D=1+\max\deg f_{i}
, define a degree on monomials via

\deg_{D}(x^{a}y^{b})=a+Db,

and extend it to
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
in the natural way. We will now construct a degree function on
\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]
in the following way: for

u=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}k_{S}\sqrt{f_{S}},

we write

\deg_{A}u=\max_{S\subseteq[n]\,:\,k_{S}\neq 0}\left(\deg_{D}k_{S}+\frac{1}{2}\deg_{D}f_{S}\right).

We have

\deg_{A}(u+v)\leq\max(\deg_{A}u,\deg_{A}v)\quad\text{and }\quad\deg_{A}(uv)\leq\deg_{A}u+\deg_{A}v.

Furthermore

\deg_{A}(pu)=\deg_{D}(p)+\deg_{A}(u),\qquad\text{for nonzero }\;p\in\mathbb{R}[x,y].

Indeed, if

u=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}k_{S}\sqrt{f_{S}},\qquad\text{then }\;pu=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}(pk_{S})\sqrt{f_{S}},

and therefore

\deg_{A}(pu)=\max_{S}(\deg_{D}p+\deg_{D}k_{S}+\frac{1}{2}\deg_{D}f_{S})=\deg_{D}p+\deg_{A}u.

In particular
\deg_{A}(\gamma u)=r_{L}+\deg_{A}u
.

Denote by

\pi:\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}[x,y]

the projection onto the
f_{\varnothing}
component. Let
u\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]
be arbitrary. Then

\pi(u^{2})=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}f_{S}k_{S}^{2},\qquad\text{where }\;u=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}k_{S}\sqrt{f_{S}}.

Thus

\deg_{D}\pi(u^{2})=\max_{S}(2\deg_{D}k_{S}+\deg_{D}f_{S})=2\deg_{A}u.

On the other hand

\deg_{D}\pi(u^{2})\leq\deg_{A}u^{2}\leq 2\deg_{A}u,

hence

\deg_{A}u^{2}=\deg_{D}\pi(u^{2})=2\deg_{A}u,

for arbitrary
u
. It follows that

\deg_{D}\pi(h_{i}^{4})=4\deg_{A}h_{i}.

Moreover,

F_{L+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\pi(h_{i}^{4}),

and the leading terms of the nonzero summands on the right cannot cancel. Therefore

\deg_{D}F_{L+1}=4\max_{i}\deg_{A}h_{i}.

Since

\deg_{D}F_{L+1}=4\sum_{j=1}^{L}r_{j},

we may conclude that

\deg_{A}h_{i}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{L}r_{j}.

On the other hand,
h_{i}=c_{i}+\gamma q_{i}
, and hence, whenever
q_{i}\neq 0
,

\deg_{A}h_{i}=\deg_{A}(\gamma q_{i})=r_{L}+\deg_{A}q_{i}.

Consequently,

(5)

\deg_{A}q_{i}\leq r_{1}+\dots+r_{L-1}<r_{L}.

Recall that we have

F_{L+1}=1+\gamma^{4}F_{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}(c_{i}+\gamma q_{i})^{4}\qquad\text{and }\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{L}c_{i}^{4}=1.

After cancelling the
1
’s we are left with

(6)

\gamma^{4}F_{L}=4\gamma\sum_{i}c_{i}^{3}q_{i}+6\gamma^{2}\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}q_{i}^{2}+4\gamma^{3}\sum_{i}c_{i}q_{i}^{3}+\gamma^{4}\sum_{i}q_{i}^{4}.

Dividing by
\gamma
and reducing modulo
\gamma
shows that

\gamma\mid\sum_{i}c_{i}^{3}q_{i}.

However,

\deg_{A}\left(\sum_{i}c_{i}^{3}q_{i}\right)\leq\max_{i}\deg_{A}q_{i}<r_{L}

and every nonzero element divisible by
\gamma
has degree at least
\deg_{D}\gamma=r_{L}
. Therefore the divisibility is only possible if

\sum_{i}c_{i}^{3}q_{i}=0.

Now, dividing (
6
) by
\gamma^{2}
and taking modulo
\gamma
, we obtain

(7)

\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}q_{i}^{2}\in\gamma\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{n}}].

Write

q_{i}=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}b_{i,S}\sqrt{f_{S}}.

Note that
\pi
is
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
-linear, so applying it to (
7
) yields

\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}c_{i}^{2}f_{S}b_{i,S}^{2}\in\gamma\mathbb{R}[x,y].

Substituting
\gamma=0
leaves us with

\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}c_{i}^{2}f_{S}(x,x^{r_{L}})b_{i,S}(x,x^{r_{L}})^{2}=0.

Every nonzero summand here has positive leading coefficient. Therefore, whenever
c_{i}\neq 0
we must have

b_{i,S}(x,x^{r_{L}})=0\qquad\text{for every }S\subseteq[n].

Thus

\gamma\mid q_{i}\qquad(c_{i}\neq 0),

but from (
5
) we know that
\deg_{A}q_{i}<r_{L}
, whereas every nonzero multiple of
\gamma
has degree at least
r_{L}
. Thus
q_{i}=0
whenever
c_{i}
is nonzero. Hence

h_{i}=\begin{cases}c_{i},&c_{i}\neq 0,\\
\gamma q_{i},&c_{i}=0.\end{cases}

Returning to (
6
) we now have (after dividing by
\gamma^{4}
)

F_{L}=\sum_{c_{i}=0}q_{i}^{4}.

Since we know that at least one
c_{i}
is nonzero, this represents
F_{L}
as a sum of at most
L-1
fourth powers. Repeating the same argument with
F_{L},F_{L-1},\ldots,F_{2}
, we eventually obtain a representation of
F_{1}=1
as a sum of zero fourth powers, which is impossible. This contradiction proves that

p_{4}\left(\mathbb{R}[x,y,\sqrt{f_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{f_{n}}]\right)=\infty.

∎

3.
Sums of even powers of
0
-regulous functions.

In this section we will discuss sums of even powers of 0-regulous functions.

The third author and Vill proposed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 3.1
.

[
19
, Conjecture 5.2]

Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

p_{2}(R)<\infty

(2)

p_{2n}(R)<\infty
for some
n

(3)

p_{2n}(R)<\infty
for all
n
.

The above conjecture is known to be true for fields (
[
5
]
) and some polynomial rings
[
8
,
19
]
. Also, it is true for valuation rings (this follows from unital Pythagoras number
[
4
, Theorem 5.17]
). Here, we will prove it for the ring of
0
-regulous functions on an irreducible
0
-regulous variety. Let us provide necessary definitions.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
s
be a positive integer and
k
be a nonnegative integer. We say that a continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{s}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
k
-regulous on
\mathbb{R}^{s}
if
f
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{k}
and
f
is a rational function, i.e. there exists a non-empty Zariski open subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
such that
f|_{U}
is regular.

Family of
k
-regulous functions defines a topology on
\mathbb{R}^{s}
which is called constructible topology. If
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is a closed
k
-regulous subset, we define the ring of
k
-regulous functions on
X
as a quotient ring

\mathcal{R}^{k}(X):=\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{s})/\mathcal{I}(X)

where
\mathcal{I}(X)
is the set of all
k
-regulous functions on
\mathbb{R}^{s}
which vanish on
X
.

For basic properties of the ring of
k
-regulous functions on a
k
-regulous variety we refer the reader to
[
1
,
15
]
(see also
[
2
,
3
]
for some recent results). Main theorem of this section is the following.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
X
be an irreducible
0
-regulous variety of dimension
s
. Then

p_{2n}(\mathcal{R}^{0}(X))<\infty

for any positive integer
n
.

Before we prove the above, we need some preparations. Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity.

Definition 3.4
.

We define the
2n
-th unital Pythagoras number of
R
,
p^{*}_{2n}(R)
, to be the least positive integer
\ell
such that any sum of
2n
-th powers of elements of
A
, which is a unit, can be expressed as a sum of
\ell

2n
-th powers of elements of
A
. If such a number does not exist, we put
p^{*}_{2n}(R)=\infty
.

Of course, for any ring
R
and any positive integer
n
we have an inequality
p^{*}_{2n}(R)\leq p_{2n}(R)
. As noted in
[
4
]
, one of this numbers can be finite while the other is not. For
R=\mathbb{Z}[x]
we have
p^{*}_{2n}(\mathbb{Z}[x])=1
and
p_{2n}(\mathbb{Z}[x])=\infty
(cf.
[
9
]
).

Becker and Powers studied the above invariant.

Theorem 3.5
.

[
4
, Theorem 5.17]

Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity. Assume that
1+\sum R^{2}\subset R^{*}
. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

p^{*}_{2}(R)<\infty

(2)

p^{*}_{2n}(R)<\infty
for some
n

(3)

p^{*}_{2n}(R)<\infty
for all
n
.

Additionally, if
p^{*}_{2}(R)=p<\infty
then

p_{2n}^{*}(R)\leq p^{2}{2n+2+p\choose p}G(2n).

Here,
G(n)
is the function related to the classical Waring problem.

Let us recall one more result

Lemma 3.6
.

[
1
, Lemma 2.8]

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an affine 0-regulous variety and
f,g\in\mathcal{R}^{0}(X)
be
0
-regulous functions such that

Z=\{x\in X:f(x)=0\}\subset\{x\in X:g(x)=0\}.

If the function
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

h:=\begin{cases}\frac{g}{f}\text{ on }X\backslash Z\\
0\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

is continuous, then
h\in\mathcal{R}^{0}(X)
.

Proof of Theorem 3.3

If
n=1
then
[
1
, Theorem 2.6]
gives us
p_{2}(\mathcal{R}^{0}(X))\leq 2^{s}
. Also, let us stress out that if
V\subset X
is an open constructible set, then
V
is again a
0
-regulous variety of dimension
s

[
1
, Proposition 1.3]
, hence
p_{2}(\mathcal{R}^{0}(V))\leq 2^{s}
.

Assume now
n>1
and let
f\in\mathcal{R}^{0}(X)
be a sum of
2n
th powers. Denote by
Z=\mathcal{Z}(F)
- the zero set of
f
, and let
U=X\setminus Z
be its complement.
It follows from the case
n=1
and Theorem
3.5
that there exists a positive integer
N_{n}
such that
p_{2n}^{*}(\mathcal{R}^{0}(V))\leq N_{n}
for any
s
-dimensional
0
-regulous irreducible set.

Consider now
f\in\mathcal{R}^{0}(U)
. It is a strictly positive function on
U
, hence it is a unit. By the above discussion, we may write
f=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{n}}f_{i}^{2n}
, where
f_{i}\in\mathcal{R}^{0}(U)
. We know that
\mathcal{R}^{0}(X)_{f}=\mathcal{R}^{0}(U)
by
[
1
, Proposition 1.4]
, and so, we may write
f_{i}=\frac{g_{i}}{f^{l_{i}}}
for some
g_{i}\in\mathcal{R}^{0}(X)
and nonnegative integers
l_{i}
, for
i=1,2,\dots,N_{n}
. Without loss of generality we may assume that all of the
l_{i}
’s are equal to some positive integer
N
.

We may now define functions

h_{i}:=\begin{cases}\frac{g_{i}}{f^{N}}\text{ on }U\\
0\text{ on }Z\end{cases}

for
i=1,2,\dots,N_{n}
. Such functions are continuous on
X
, and are
0
-regulous by Lemma
3.6
. This finishes the proof.

Remark 3.7
.

The above proof is in a similar spirit as the proof of
[
1
, Theorem 2.6]
. The ring of
0
-regulous functions is the first known example (other than fields and valuation rings) for which we are able to show finiteness of the higher even Pythagoras numbers. The extendability of quotients of such functions is crucial. It is not known how to tackle similar problems for the ring of regular functions (cf.
[
20
, Remark 4.14]
) or
k
-regulous functions for
k>0
.

4.
Bad points of higher order

In this chapter we will study size of
the bad set of a polynomial
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
be a nonnegative polynomial. Define

\displaystyle\mathrm{Denom}_{2n}(f)

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{G\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{s}]\mid G^{2n}\cdot f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}H_{i}^{2n}\text{ for some }H_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{s}]\Bigr\},

Let
I_{2n}(f)
be the ideal generated by
\mathrm{Denom}_{2n}(f)
. We define the set of bad points of order
2n
of
f
,
\mathrm{Bad}_{2n}(f)
as the zero set of the ideal
I_{2n}(f)
.

Of course, it may happen that there does not exist such
G
as above, i.e.
f
is not a sum of
2n
th powers in the field of fractions, then
\mathrm{Denom}_{2n}(f)=\{0\}
. The set of the bad points of order
2n
of
f
is the common zero set of all possible denominators in all representations of
f
a sum of
2n
th powers of rational functions.

Firstly, let us discuss what is known for
n=1
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
be a nonsingular irreducible algebraic surface and
f\in\mathbb{R}[X]
be a polynomial function which is a sums of squares in
\mathbb{R}(X)
. Then
\mathrm{codim}(\mathrm{Bad}_{2}(f))\geq 3
.

If
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
then this is precisely
[
12
, Proposition 5.1]
. The more general case follows from the result of Scheiderer
[
26
, Theorem 4.8]
that psd=sos holds for local regular rings of dimension 2. Bad points of order greater than 2 have not been studied before.

Here, we are able to show the following result.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
n>1
be a positive integer. Then there exists a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
which is a sum of
2n
th powers of rational functions and
\mathrm{codim}\big(\mathrm{Bad}_{2n}(f)\big)=2
holds.

The above theorem will be proved by constructing an explicit examples. Let us first recall crucial fact.

Theorem 4.4
.

[
5
, Theorem 1.9]

Polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x]
can be expressed as

f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\big(\frac{g_{i}}{h}\big)}^{2n}

for some
g_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[x]
and
h\in\mathbb{R}[x]\backslash\{0\}

if and only if the following conditions are satisfied

(1)

f(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2)

2n\mid\deg(f)
, and

(3)

2n
divides the multiplicity of
x-x_{0}
in
f
for every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.

For the discussion of the above result, see also
[
10
, Introduction]
. Also, largest positive integer
N
in the above theorem is the value of
p_{2n}(\mathbb{R}(x))
which is studied in
[
10
]
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
:

Obviously it cannot happen that
\mathrm{codim}\big(\mathrm{Bad}_{2n}(f)\big)=0
. If this value is equal to 1 then in every presentation

G^{2n}f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}^{2n}

G
and
f_{i}
for every
i=1,2,\dots,N
vanish on a codimension 1 real algebraic subset of the real plane. However, as the ring
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
is factorial, ideal of the set of such subset if principal, hence we may factor them out. As a consequences, the codimension of the bad set cannot be 1.

Let us now discuss the case
n=2
, as higher powers will follow analogously.

Consider family of polynomials given by

f_{m}=x^{4}+mx^{2}+1.

f_{m}
is a sum of fourth powers of polynomials iff
m\in[0,6]
(cf.

[
25
, Exercise 7.4.4]
). By the previous theorem,
f_{m}
is a sum of fourth powers of rational functions iff
m\geq 0.
Hence
f_{7}
is not a sum of fourth powers of polynomials, but there exists a a strictly positive polynomial
h\in\mathbb{R}[x]
such that
h^{4}f_{7}
is a sum of fourth powers of polynomials.

Denote by
\widetilde{f_{7}}=x^{4}+7x^{2}y^{2}+y^{4}
its homogenization. If
\widetilde{h}
is a homogenization of
h
then
\widetilde{h}^{4}\widetilde{f_{7}}
is a sum of fourth powers of polynomials. Of course homogenization of a strictly positive polynomial is a positive definite form, hence
\widetilde{h}
vanishes only at the origin. As a consequence,
\mathrm{Bad}_{4}(f_{7})\subset\{(0,0)\}
. It is enough to show that this set is non empty.

Assume by contrary that the bad set of order 4 is empty.
If there exists a polynomial
g\in\mathrm{Denom}_{4}(\widetilde{f}_{7})
such that
g(0,0)\neq 0
, then
g^{4}+\widetilde{h}^{4}\in\mathrm{Denom}_{4}(\widetilde{f}_{7})
, and it does not vanish anywhere. Without loss of generality, we may assume that there is
g\in\mathrm{Denom}(\widetilde{f}_{7})
which does not vanish. We have an equality

(8)

g^{4}\widetilde{f}_{7}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}^{4}.

Consider now the above equation in the ring
\mathbb{R}[[x,y]]
. In this ring,
g
is a unit, and we may obtain the following

\widetilde{f}_{7}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}H_{i}^{4}

for some power series
H_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[[x,y]]
.
By comparing terms of degree four we get a contradiction, since
\widetilde{f}_{7}
is not a sum of fourth powers of polynomials. This shows that the bad set of order
4
of
f_{7}
consists of the origin and finishes the proof for
n=2
.

For
n>2
one can reason similarly taking the polynomial
f_{n}=x^{2n}+x^{2n-2}+1
. It is strictly positive and of degree
2n
hence it is a sum of
2n
th powers of rational functions. Straightforward calculations shows that it is not a sum of
2n
th powers of polynomials.
This finishes the proof.
∎

5.
Open problems

In Section 3 we have discussed Beckers Theorem. Here we will propose a weaker version of the conjecture proposed in
[
19
]
.

Problem 5.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity, and
n>1
be a positive integer.
Does the inequality

p_{2}(R)\leq p_{2n}(R)

holds?

The above is particularly interesting for rings such that
p_{2}(R)=\infty
. Every known computed example follows the above rule, however the values of
p_{2}(R)
and
p_{2n}(R)
