A Six-Term Functional Equation and a Quartic Dilogarithm Ladder


Abstract
We introduce dilogarithm identities through a beta integral-based technique that we apply to construct new 3 and 6-term functional identities of the type ∑ r i − 1 ​ L ​ ( X i ) ∈ π 2 ​ 𝐐 \sum r^{-1}_{i}L(X_{i})\in\pi^{2}\mathbf{Q} . We derive simplified proof of the Loxton-Lewin identities though a new analytic method, what I call the “radius method,” which in conjunction with the above methods, is used to derive pair of quartic-base dilogarithm ladders, also believed to be new.

J. H. Loxton and Lewin conjectured three dilogarithm identities in the early ’80s involving

. Loxton
[

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 3L(\kappa^{3})-9L(\kappa^{2})-9L(\kappa)+7L(1)&=0,&\kappa&=\tfrac{1}{2}\sec\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{9}\right),\\
3L(\lambda^{6})-6L(\lambda^{3})-27L(\lambda^{2})+18L(\lambda)+2L(1)&=0,&\lambda&=\tfrac{1}{2}\sec\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{9}\right),\\
3L(\mu^{6})-6L(\mu^{3})-27L(\mu^{2})+18L(\mu)-2L(1)&=0,&\mu&=2\cos\!\left(\tfrac{4\pi}{9}\right).\end{aligned}

The third, involving

\displaystyle L(x^{2})

\displaystyle=2L(x)-2L(z),

\displaystyle L(y^{2})

\displaystyle=2L(y)-2L(a),

\displaystyle L(a^{2})

\displaystyle=2L(a)-2L(x^{2}),

\displaystyle L(z^{2})

\displaystyle=2L(z)-2L\!\left(\frac{x}{1+2x}\right),

\displaystyle L(x^{2})+L(y^{2})

\displaystyle=L(z^{2})+L(z)+L(b),

\displaystyle L(y)+L(a)

\displaystyle=L\!\left(\frac{x}{1+2x}\right)+L(x)+L(b),

\displaystyle L(x)+L(x^{2})

\displaystyle=L(x^{3})+L(a^{2})+L(1)-L(c^{2}),

\displaystyle L(c^{2})

\displaystyle=2L(c)-2L(y^{2}),

\displaystyle L(c)

\displaystyle=L(y)+\tfrac{1}{2}L(z^{2}),

\displaystyle L\!\left(\frac{y}{3}\right)

\displaystyle=L(a)+L(c^{2})-L(y^{2})-L(d),

\displaystyle L(d)

\displaystyle=2L(z^{2})-2L(x)+L(1),

\displaystyle L\!\left(\frac{z^{2}}{3}\right)

\displaystyle=L(a^{2})+L\!\left(\frac{x}{1+2x}\right)-L(z^{2})-L(d),

\displaystyle L(y^{6})

\displaystyle=2L(y^{3})-2L\!\left(\frac{y}{3}\right),

\displaystyle L(z^{6})

\displaystyle=2L(z^{3})-2L\!\left(\frac{z^{2}}{3}\right).

Loxton asked if a direct proof was possible. Our analytic proof proves it with a single application of the 5-term identity, and shows how the cancellation of the log terms occurs. We can rewrite the third identity equivalently, in which the log terms are absent:

\operatorname{Li}_{2}(-\mu)+\operatorname{Li}_{2}(\mu^{2})-\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{Li}_{2}(-\mu^{3})=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{54}

(25)

Proof.

We observe (25) resembles the multiplication formula (for real
r
):

\displaystyle-\operatorname{Li}_{2}(-r)+\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{Li}_{2}(-r^{3})=\operatorname{Li}_{2}\left(re^{\pi i/3}\right)+\operatorname{Li}_{2}\left(re^{-\pi i/3}\right).

(26)

This provides an approach to attack the problem. What needs to be done is to relate it to a second identity for the
r^{2}
term. This is done by applying what I call the ”radius method”:

\frac{\sqrt{u(4-3u)}}{2u^{2}-3u}=i\sqrt{3}.

Hence,
u
is a solution of
u^{3}-3u^{2}+2u+1/3=0
that lies within the unit circle and applicable with Theorem 5, so
u\approx-.137
is chosen, although this value does not need to be computed. What this does is force (25) to lie on a circle, so Theorem 3 can be applied. By taking the norm
\frac{\sqrt{(2u^{2}-3u)^{2}-u(4-3u)}}{2(u-1)^{2}}
, we have
r=\sqrt{\frac{u}{u-1}}=2\cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right)
. We obtain:

\frac{3}{2}\ln^{2}\left(1-\frac{u-\sqrt{(4-3u)u}}{2(u-1)}\right)-\operatorname{Li_{2}}(r^{2})=\operatorname{Li}_{2}(-r)-\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{Li}_{2}(-r^{3}).

Regarding the logarithm, split it into complex and real parts and notice:

\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{2(u-1)-u}{2(u-1)}\right)^{2}}=\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{(u-2)}{2(u-1)}\right)^{2}}=\pm\frac{\sqrt{3u^{2}-4u}}{2(u-1)}.

Therefore, the value contained within the logarithm is a root of unity, so knowing the real part means we can immediately infer the complex part. Let
\left(\frac{(u-2)}{2(u-1)}\right)=y
. The composition of
u^{3}-3u^{2}+2u+1/3=0
with
u=\frac{2(y-1)}{2y-1}
gives a new function
-16y^{3}+12y+2=0
, which is the minimum polynomial for
\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{9}\right)
. The complex part contained within the logarithm must be
\pm\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{9}\right)
(the choice of sign does not matter). Hence, we have
3/2\ln^{2}(e^{-i\frac{\pi}{9}})=-\pi^{2}/54
. ∎

To show
r=\sqrt{\frac{u}{u-1}}=2\cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right)
, substitute
u=\frac{y^{2}}{-1+y^{2}}
into
u^{3}-3u^{2}+2u+\frac{1}{3}=0
. Hence,

y^{6}-6y^{4}+9y^{2}-1=(y^{3}-3y-1)(y^{3}-3y+1)=0.

This factors into the minimum polynomial of
2\cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right)
as the only applicable root.

Quartic Ladders

Gordon, B. and McIntosh, R. J.
[
17
]
found via a computer search three additional ladders with bases
x^{3}+6x^{2}+3x-1=0
of
\pi/18
, analogous to the trio of
\pi/9
identities:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 2L(a^{3})-2L(a^{2})-11L(a)+3L(1)&=0,\\
2L(b^{6})-4L(b^{3})-15L(b^{2})+22L(b)-6L(1)&=0,\\
2L(c^{6})-4L(c^{3})-15L(c^{2})+22L(c)-4L(1)&=0,\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} a&=2\sqrt{3}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{5\pi}{18}\right)-2,\\
b&=2\sqrt{3}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{11\pi}{18}\right)+2,\\
c&=2\sqrt{3}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{7\pi}{18}\right)-1.\end{aligned}

(27)

Likewise, we derive two 5-term ladders, of a quartic base, in the same spirit as above. Ours represents a new base equation and ladders, which like above, does not have an obvious or direct derivation. They also remarked that producing ladders that are not of a “trivial” base ; e.g.
u^{a}+u^{b}\pm u^{c}=1
,
u^{a}+u^{b}=1
or
u^{p}+u^{n}-u^{q}+u^{n+m}-u^{q+p+m}=1
is “very difficult”. Don Zagier
[
43
]
also remarked that finding examples of ladders is “very difficult and requires great ingenuity to produce examples.” Indeed, producing other examples that doesn’t follow from a trivial base was not possible.

Proof.

Using Theorem 2, set:

\frac{\sqrt{4-3u^{2}}}{u^{2}-2}=-i\sqrt{3}

The radius is
r=\sqrt{\frac{u-1}{u+1}}\quad\text{and:}\quad r^{2}=\frac{(u-1)^{2}}{u^{2}-1}=\frac{u-1}{u+1}
.
Explicitly, we have that

r=\frac{1}{4}\left(-1+\sqrt{33}-\sqrt{2\left(9-\sqrt{33}\right)}\right)

(28)

Consequently, we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Li}_{2}\left(\frac{u^{2}-\sqrt{4-3u^{2}}-2}{-(u+1)u}\right)+\operatorname{Li}_{2}\left(\frac{u^{2}+\sqrt{4-3u^{2}}-2}{-(u+1)u}\right)=-\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r)+\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r^{3})

\displaystyle\operatorname{Li}_{2}\left(\frac{u^{2}-\sqrt{4-3u^{2}}-2}{(u+1)u}\right)+\operatorname{Li}_{2}\left(\frac{u^{2}+\sqrt{4-3u^{2}}-2}{(u+1)u}\right)=-\operatorname{Li}_{2}(-r)+\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{Li}_{2}(-r^{3})

The resultant ladder relation is such that

3\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r^{6})+3\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r^{4})-8\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r^{3})-33\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r^{2})+24\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r)-\zeta(2)=6\log^{2}(r).

Using a mapping of
u^{4}-5u^{2}+\frac{16}{3}=0
, where
u=\frac{1+r^{2}}{1-r^{2}}
, we can determine that
all possible radii
r
for ladders derived in the above manner are positive solutions of
(r^{4}-r^{3}-6r^{2}-r+1)(r^{4}+r^{3}-6r^{2}+r+1)=0
, so there are four possible ladders, corresponding to
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
,
r_{3}
, and
r_{4}
.
These ladders may be thought of as being analogues of the trio of ladders related to the value
\frac{\pi}{9}
. Two of these are
redundant, given that
r_{1}=r_{3}^{-1}
and
r_{2}=r_{4}^{-1}
.

Setting
\frac{\sqrt{4-3u^{2}}}{2-u^{2}}=\sqrt{3}\,i
, gives the second radius, with

r_{2}=\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\sqrt{33}-\sqrt{2\left(9+\sqrt{33}\right)}\right)

(29)

The associated ladder relation is such that

\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r_{2}^{6})-3\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r_{2}^{4})-8\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r_{2}^{3})+21\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r_{2}^{2})+24\operatorname{Li}_{2}(r_{2})-11\zeta(2)=-6\log^{2}(r_{2}).

∎

Whereas the Lewin-Loxton identity can algebraically be proven with an array of 14 applications of the 5-term identity, presumably proving these in a similar manner would require more terms, and it’s not evident how one would proceed in such a manner.

]

\xi_{Q}=[\alpha]+[\alpha\beta^{3}]

\displaystyle 1-\alpha

\displaystyle=\alpha^{3}\beta,

\displaystyle 1-\alpha\beta^{3}

\displaystyle=\alpha\beta^{2}.

This is equivalent to solving
x^{6}+x^{5}-2x^{3}+2x-1=0
. Here,
y=1/(1-x_{1})

x_{1}
is the negative real root and
x=x_{0}
is the positive real root. Bytsko

conjectured the related

\operatorname{L}(x)+\operatorname{L}(y)=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{21}

\pi/7

Proof.

Applying (4) multiple times to Theorem 2, we have

-3\operatorname{L}\left(\frac{-u-\sqrt{4-3u^{2}}}{2}\right)-3\operatorname{L}\left(\frac{-u+\sqrt{4-3u^{2}}}{2}\right)-\operatorname{L}\left(\frac{1-u}{u+1}\right)+\operatorname{L}\left(\frac{u-1}{u+1}\right)\\

Solving

\displaystyle\frac{u+2-2u^{2}+\sqrt{4-3u^{2}}}{2u}

\displaystyle=y,

\displaystyle\frac{u+2-2u^{2}-\sqrt{4-3u^{2}}}{2u}

u=2\cos(3\pi/7)

Hence, we obtain for

(30)

∎
