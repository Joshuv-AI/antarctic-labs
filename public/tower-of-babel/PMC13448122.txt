Clinically Meaningful Improvement Across Ages 50–95 Years After Decompression Without Fusion for Central Lumbar Spinal Stenosis Without Spondylolisthesis
Authors: Henrik Damm, Magnus K Karlsson, Lars Jehpsson, Niyaz Hareni, Björn E Rosengren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAdvanced age has been proposed as a risk factor for worse outcomes and higher complication rates after spinal surgery. We assessed whether decompression for central lumbar spinal stenosis (CLSS) provided meaningful improvement in patients aged 50–95 years. MethodsWe evaluated preoperative and 1-year postoperative Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) leg and back pain, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and complications in 17,987 patients aged ≥50 years undergoing decompression without fusion for CLSS without spondylolisthesis from the Swespine register. Meaningful improvement was defined using minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds for NRS leg/back pain and ODI. Across 5-year age groups, we assessed the proportions improving ≥MCID and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), using ages 65–69 years as the reference group. ResultsAcross age groups, 71%–80% improved ≥MCID in ≥1 outcome. Compared with the reference group, ORs (95% CI) for ≥MCID improvement were lower in ages 70–74 (0.8; 0.7–1.0), 75–79 (0.7; 0.6–0.8), 80–84 (0.6; 0.5–0.7), and 85–89 (0.6; 0.5–0.8); no other age groups differed. 5%–12% experienced complications. Compared with the reference group, ORs (95% CI) for complications were higher in ages 75–79 (1.3; 1.1–1.6), 80–84 (1.7; 1.4–2.1), and 85–89 (2.0; 1.5–2.8), and lower in ages 55–59 (0.7; 0.5–0.9). Conclusion77% of patients aged 50–95 years improved ≥MCID after decompression without fusion for CLSS without spondylolisthesis. Although outcomes were slightly worse and complication rates higher in older than younger patients, decompression without fusion remains effective across this age range.Keywords: Spinal stenosis, Lumbar vertebrae, Surgical decompression, Aged, Treatment outcome, Patient reported outcome measures

INTRODUCTION

Central lumbar spinal stenosis (CLSS) is a common degenerative condition, with increasing incidence with age [1]. First-line treatment includes patient education, physiotherapy, analgesics, and/or epidural injections [2-5]. When conservative approaches fail, surgical decompression is an alternative [6,7].

Outcomes following lumbar spine surgery are influenced by several patient-related factors, including sex, smoking, prolonged symptom duration, depressive symptoms, and high body mass index (BMI) [5,6,8]. However, the impact of age remains unclear. Some studies report worse outcomes after decompression for lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) in the oldest patients [6,9,10], whereas others have not confirmed this finding [4,7,11-13]. Similarly, the relationship between age and complication rates is inconclusive. While some studies have associated advanced age with higher complication rates after lumbar spine surgery [7,14,15], others have not confirmed this association [12,13]. Previous studies have often been limited by smaller sample sizes, selected cohorts, inclusion of both fusion and nonfusion procedures, and limited representation of the oldest patients [7,10,11,13,16,17]. In addition, few studies have evaluated clinically meaningful improvement across the full adult age spectrum using nationwide registry data [4,10].

This study aimed to clarify the association between age, clinical outcomes, and complications following decompression without fusion for CLSS without spondylolisthesis. Furthermore, it assessed the influence of other patient-related factors to support patient-centered decision-making regarding surgical versus nonsurgical management. We hypothesized that decompression without fusion for CLSS without spondylolisthesis results in clinically meaningful improvement across all age groups, but that outcomes are less favorable and complication rates higher in the oldest patients. We further hypothesized that outcomes are influenced not only by age but also by other patient-related factors. Accordingly, we asked the following research questions: in patients aged ≥50 years undergoing decompression without fusion for CLSS without spondylolisthesis:

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Swedish National Quality Registry for Spinal Surgery (Swespine) collects prospective, procedure-specific data on degenerative lumbar spine surgery. The registry covers approximately 95% of spine surgery departments in Sweden, with a data completeness of around 85% and a 1-year patient follow-up rate of about 70% [18]. Prior to surgery, patients complete standardized questionnaires capturing demographics (such as height, weight, and smoking status) and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), including the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) for leg and back pain (ranging from 0=no pain to 10=worst pain imaginable) and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI, ranging from 0=no impairment to 100=bedbound). BMI was calculated as the weight divided by the height squared (kg/m2). Identical questionnaires are sent at 1-, 2-, 5-, and 10-year follow-ups, with an additional question at the 1-year follow-up assessing satisfaction with the surgical outcome on a 3-point Likert scale (satisfied, uncertain, dissatisfied). We included only 1-year follow-up data, as previous studies from Swespine have shown no major outcome differences between 1 and 2 years after lumbar spine surgery and that 1-year data adequately represent the final outcomes [19]. Perioperative information is reported by the surgeon including diagnosis, type of surgery, use of implants and/or bone grafting, number of operated segments, acute or elective surgery, and complications before discharge from hospital (dural tear, nerve root injury, hematoma, infection, thromboembolism, cauda equina syndrome, or death).

We included patients aged ≥50 years from Swespine who had undergone posterior decompression without fusion for CLSS without degenerative spondylolisthesis between January 1, 2005, and June 21, 2022. Patients with lateral recess stenosis or foraminal stenosis were not included. Detailed information regarding surgical approach (e.g., open versus minimally invasive techniques) was not available in the register. Eligible patients had no prior back surgery and had completed at least one preoperative PROM questionnaire (NRS leg pain, NRS back pain, or ODI), yielding a cohort of 26,314 patients. Patients were then excluded due to missing or implausible values based on the following predefined criteria: (1) missing information on sex (n=12); (2) missing data on height and/or weight (n=2,454); (3) registered height <140 cm or >210 cm (n=53); (4) registered weight <40 kg or >200 kg (n=28). After applying these criteria, 23,777 patients remained in the cleaned baseline cohort. Categories are not mutually exclusive. We then excluded patients who did not complete at least one 1-year follow-up PROM (n=5,790). The final study cohort consisted of 17,987 patients, with a mean age of 69.5±8.6 years. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 31.0 (IBM Co., USA). Descriptive statistics are presented as proportions (%), numbers (n), or means±standard deviations. Inferential statistics are presented as means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The study cohort was evaluated overall and stratified by 5-year age groups, with the oldest group defined as patients aged ≥90 years. The oldest patients in the cohort were 95 years old (n=2). The mean age of the cohort was 69.5 years, and the 65–69-year age group was used as the reference in analyses requiring a reference group. We defined minimal clinical important differences (MCIDs) for improvement after LSS surgery in accordance with previous literature, with thresholds of ≥40% for NRS leg pain, ≥33% for NRS back pain, and ≥30% for ODI [20].

To analyze longitudinal changes in NRS (leg and back pain) and ODI, linear mixed-effects models were employed using a Restricted Maximum Likelihood approach, as these models appropriately account for repeated measurements within individuals and handle missing data without excluding incomplete cases. The models included a random intercept per subject, and a variance components covariance structure to account for withinsubject variability across repeated timepoints, assuming no covariance between repeated measures. More complex covariance structures (e.g., unstructured) were explored but were not retained due to model convergence issues. Results are presented as adjusted mean differences in outcome measures (NRS leg and back pain, and ODI), with 95% CI. Fixed effects included timepoint, age group, BMI class, sex, smoking status, duration of symptoms (5 categories), self-reported walking distance (4 categories), surgical priority (acute vs. elective), and number of decompressed segments. BMI classes were defined according to World Health Organization criteria: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 to <25.0 kg/m2), overweight (25.0 to <30.0 kg/m2), obesity class I (30.0 to <35.0 kg/m2), obesity class II (35.0 to <40.0 kg/m2), and obesity class III (≥40.0 kg/m2). Degrees of freedom were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation.

To assess the association between age and achieving improvement ≥MCID, logistic regression analyses were performed for 4 binary outcomes: achievement of ≥MCID in NRS leg pain, NRS back pain, and ODI, respectively, as well as a combined outcome for achieving ≥MCID in at least 1 of the 3 PROMs. Patients with a preoperative score of 0 for NRS (leg or back pain) or ODI were included in these analyses and assigned as not having achieved improvement ≥MCID. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. First, univariable logistic regression was used to estimate the crude association between age groups and each outcome. Then, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to calculate corresponding adjusted ORs. These models were adjusted for BMI, sex, smoking status, symptom duration, self-reported walking distance, surgical priority (acute vs. elective), number of decompressed segments, and baseline value of the evaluated variable (for the combined “all 3 PROMs” outcome, baseline NRS leg pain, NRS back pain, and ODI were included). Duration of symptoms was defined as duration of leg pain for the NRS leg pain model and duration of back pain for the NRS back pain model. Both leg and back pain durations were included in the ODI and the combined “all 3 PROMs” models.

Separate logistic regression models were used to analyze the occurrence of complications, providing both crude and adjusted ORs. The multivariable models for complications were adjusted for sex, smoking status, and BMI, as these were considered the most clinically relevant potential confounders.

To assess potential attrition bias related to missing 1-year follow-up data and missing covariate data, sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation with 20 imputed datasets were performed. Missing PROM values and covariates were imputed, after which the logistic regression analyses and linear mixed-effects models were repeated.

Artificial intelligence (AI)-based language editing was used to improve the clarity and readability of the manuscript. No AI tools were used for data analysis, interpretation, or generation of results.

This study was approved by the Lund regional ethical review board (Dnr 2017/158 and Dnr 2021-02273), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESULTS

Overall, at 1-year follow-up, 66.4% (n=11,622) of patients were satisfied, 22.2% (n=3,890) unsure, and 11.3% (n=1,979) dissatisfied. Mean improvement from preoperative to 1-year postoperative was 3.1±3.5 for NRS leg pain, 2.4±3.1 for NRS back pain, and 17.7±18.1 for ODI. Overall, 57.9% (n=9,657) achieved improvement ≥MCID in NRS leg pain, 57.3% (n=9,554) in NRS back pain, 61.5% (n=10,343) in ODI, and 76.7% (n=13,758) in at least one of the 3 PROMs.

The proportions of patients who were satisfied, unsure, or dissatisfied at 1-year follow-up by 5-year age groups are presented in Table 3. At 1-year follow-up, we observed a gradual decrease in the proportion of patients satisfied with the surgical outcome with increasing age groups: 69.1% (n=2,255) in patients aged 65–69 years, 65.1% (n=2,495) in those aged 70–74 years, 62.2% (n=2,029) in those aged 75–79 years, 59.5% (n=984) in those aged 80–84 years, and 58.5% (n=237) in those aged 85–89 years. However, further gradual decrease was not observed in the oldest age group (≥90 years), in which 68.3% of patients (n=28) reported satisfaction.

DISCUSSION

This study shows that 77% of patients aged ≥50 years achieved improvement ≥MCID 1 year after decompression without fusion for CLSS without spondylolisthesis, while 7% experienced complications related to the surgery. Although advanced age was associated with less improvement in PROMs and higher complication rates, more than 70% of patients aged ≥80 years achieved improvement ≥MCID. Beyond age, obesity, female sex, smoking, longer symptom duration, shorter preoperative walking distance, and acute surgery were independently associated with worse 1-year outcomes.

We found that patients in each age group between 70 and 89 years had slightly worse 1-year postoperative outcomes compared with the reference group (65–69 years). As shown in Table 5, this was reflected in lower proportions and lower adjusted ORs for achieving improvement ≥MCID in NRS leg pain, ODI, and all 3 PROMs combined. In patients aged ≥90 years, point estimates for both proportions and adjusted ORs also suggested less favorable outcomes, but did not reach statistical significance. Given the limited sample size in this age group, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory. We speculate that this may reflect limited statistical power, increasing the risk of a type II error (false negative finding), and/or selection bias at inclusion, whereby the oldest patients undergoing surgery represent a more severely affected subgroup. For example, patients aged ≥90 years had more severe preoperative leg pain, higher ODI scores, and approximately 10 percentage points fewer individuals with symptom duration >2 years than other age groups (Table 2). In contrast to the more consistent results regarding NRS leg pain and ODI, NRS back pain showed greater variability, likely reflecting a weaker and less predictable response to LSS surgery, which primarily targets leg pain, as also supported by previous studies [2,6,21].

The association between advanced age and worse outcomes was further supported by the mixed-effects models, in which increasing age group remained independently associated with less favorable outcomes, most evident for ODI (Table 6). These findings are consistent with previous Swespine publications reporting an association between higher age and worse outcomes after other types of degenerative lumbar spine surgery, such as lumbar disc herniation surgery [15]. The results are also in line with a large-scale registry study on LSS that identified age >70 years as one of the strongest independent risk factors for less improvement in ODI and for surgical failure [6]. This view is further supported by a report demonstrating that postoperative improvements in walking distance and locomotive function after LSS surgery are significantly lower in patients aged ≥75 years compared with those aged 65–75 years [9]. Furthermore, Shahi et al. [10] found that, after LSS surgery, patients aged >80 years experienced lower and slower improvement in visual analogue scale (VAS) leg pain, VAS back pain, and ODI compared with younger age groups.

However, some studies have reported similar improvements in NRS leg pain, NRS back pain, and ODI in patients aged ≥80 years compared with younger patients after LSS surgery [4,11]. Nanjo et al. [12] reported comparable recovery and clinical outcomes in patients above and below 80 years of age, while Aleem and Rampersaud [16] reported similar improvements in ODI after decompression for LSS when comparing patients aged ≥70 years with younger patients.

The current study may help explain the varying results. When outcomes were evaluated in 5-year age groups, patients aged ≥70 years had worse 1-year outcomes compared to those aged 65–69 years. However, despite including 17,987 patients, the overlapping 95% CIs of the ORs did not support a stepwise worsening of outcomes with increasing age. Similarly, the mixedeffect model showed overlapping 95% CI between age groups ≥80 years. Thus, differences in age thresholds used to define an older population, as well as limited number of very old patients in previous studies, may partly explain the inconsistent findings.

When conducting large-scale studies, it is important not only to draw conclusions based on statistically significant findings but also to consider the clinical relevance of observed differences. With large sample sizes, even small differences may reach statistical significance. For example, a difference of 0.1 in NRS leg pain, even if statistically significant, is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. Consistent with this, our study found only modest differences in the proportion of patients achieving ≥MCID between age groups, and patient satisfaction remained high across all age groups. This suggests that the observed age-related differences at the group level are of limited clinical relevance for the individual patient. Accordingly, recent large-scale studies increasingly incorporate the concept of MCID, whereby statistically significant differences are also interpreted in terms of clinical relevance. Our findings support the meaningfulness of this approach. Accordingly, statistically significant differences between age groups should not necessarily be interpreted as clinically meaningful differences at the individual patient level. Although older age groups showed slightly less improvement after decompression for CLSS without spondylolisthesis and a somewhat lower proportion achieving improvement ≥MCID, the absolute differences between age groups were small (Table 5). Similarly, only a slightly higher proportion of patients aged ≥70 years reported dissatisfaction with surgical outcome compared to younger patients (Table 3). Therefore, we suggest that surgical candidacy in CLSS without spondylolisthesis should be guided primarily by a comprehensive assessment of functional impairment and psychological readiness rather than chronological age, a notion supported in the literature [4,7,9,13,16].

The linear mixed-effects models further support that, although older age group is independently associated with worse outcomes, the effect size is modest and comparable to that of obesity, female sex, and smoking. In contrast, point estimates for mean differences were substantially greater for patient categories defined by preoperative symptom duration, walking distance, and timing of surgery (Table 6). For example, at 1-year follow-up, NRS leg pain was only 0.1 points higher in patients aged 80–84 years than in those aged 65–69 years. In contrast, NRS leg pain was 4.3 points higher in patients with a preoperative pain duration >2 years than in those with no preoperative leg pain, and 1.2 points higher in patients with a preoperative walking distance <100 m than in those able to walk >1,000 m. Similarly, ODI was 3.9 points higher in patients aged 80–84 years than in those aged 65–69 years, but 11.9 points higher in patients with a pain duration >2 years than in those with no preoperative leg pain, and 17.5 points higher in those with a preoperative walking distance <100 m than in those able to walk >1,000 m (Table 6). These findings are supported by previous literature, showing that a longer duration of leg pain prior to spinal stenosis surgery predicts less improvement in ODI, and that walking capacity is a strong predictor of postoperative function and patient satisfaction after spinal stenosis surgery [13,21]. One proposed explanation for why long symptom duration is associated with worse outcomes is that prolonged nerve root compression leads to irreversible biochemical and structural changes in nerve cells [6].

We also found that acute surgical intervention was associated with poorer outcomes compared with elective surgery, with a mean 11.1-point higher ODI at 1-year follow-up (Table 6). This is in line with previous studies, including one reporting a 5-fold higher risk of major morbidity following acute compared with elective spinal surgery among patients aged ≥80 years [22]. We speculate that the worse outcomes observed in acute surgery may partly be explained by the limited opportunity for preoperative medical optimization of comorbid conditions, which increases the risk of systemic complications [16,23]. In addition, patients undergoing acute procedures often present with more severe neurological deficits and worse function, both of which have been associated with worse postoperative outcomes [6].

Our findings further support that high BMI, smoking, and female sex are associated with higher pain levels and greater disability after surgery for LSS [4,6,8,14,24], and that the number of decompressed segments appear to have minor association with 1-year outcomes [6,13].

We observed higher complication rates in older patients. For example, 5.4% of patients aged 50–54 years in our study experienced any complication, compared with 6.8% in those aged 65–69 years and 12.4% in those aged 85–89 years. The higher odds of complications with increasing age group remained after adjusting for sex, BMI, and smoking. Although point estimates in patients aged ≥90 years were also elevated, the ORs were not statistically significant. As we have no clear explanation for this, we again speculate that it may reflect selection bias at inclusion or a type II error (false negative finding). Our findings are consistent with previous literature, as advanced age in degenerative spinal disorders is generally associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications [7,11,14,15]. The most common complications in our cohort were dural tears and urinary retention. However, conclusions regarding less frequent complications and their association with advanced age are limited due to the small numbers.

To our knowledge, this is the largest study to date investigating the impact of advanced age and other patient-related factors on outcomes and perioperative complications after decompression without fusion for CLSS without spondylolisthesis. The use of a large nationwide database such as Swespine enables insights into less common patient groups, including the oldest old, while allowing comprehensive evaluation within a well-defined population undergoing the most common surgical procedure for degenerative lumbar spine disease. The large sample size also allows the application of a narrow diagnostic definition (CLSS without spondylolisthesis), in contrast to the broader LSS definition commonly used in previous studies [7,10,14,20]. This results in a more homogeneous study population and strengthens the robustness of the findings. Other strengths include the use of nationwide, prospectively collected data representing outcomes from an unselected national population undergoing decompression without fusion for CLSS without spondylolisthesis. Consequently, our results could likely be generalized to standard clinical practice rather than limited to highly specialized spine units with strict inclusion criteria and procedures performed exclusively by specialized surgeons. While efficacy studies from specialized centers are essential and demonstrate what is possible to achieve under optimal conditions, it is equally important to report the “real-world” effectiveness attainable in general clinical practice.

Limitations include the lack of data on psychosocial and radiological findings, both of which are known to influence outcomes after spinal stenosis surgery [5,6,21]. In addition, detailed information regarding decompression technique was not available in Swespine, precluding analyses based on surgical approaches, such as open versus minimally invasive techniques. Data on preoperative dynamic instability were not available in Swespine. Thus, patients with dynamic instability may have been included. Variables reflecting comorbidity burden, frailty, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification, and radiological stenosis severity were also unavailable or incomplete in the register and could therefore not be included in the mixed-effects models. Consequently, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly in the oldest age groups, and the observed associations with age should be interpreted in this context.

As with all registry-based studies, the reliability of the data may be questioned. However, even if Swespine has been reported to have a correct classification rate of 98.5% for CLSS [25], we cannot exclude that misclassification may have influenced the results. The generalizability of our findings may be limited, as the data are derived from a Swedish population and apply only to the indications for decompression without fusion in patients with CLSS without spondylolisthesis during the study period. Changes in indications may therefore lead to different conclusions. In addition, selection bias cannot be ruled out, as participation in Swespine is voluntary and may not fully represent all patients undergoing spinal surgery. Incomplete 1-year follow-up data were observed in 24.4% of patients, and attrition bias therefore cannot be excluded. Furthermore, as this is an observational registry study, causal inferences cannot be made.

CONCLUSION

Overall, 77% of patients aged 50–95 years achieved improvement ≥MCID after decompression without fusion for CLSS without spondylolisthesis. Although outcomes were slightly worse and complication rates higher in older than in younger patients, most older patients still experienced clinically meaningful improvement. Factors such as symptom duration and functional limitation appear to have a greater impact on 1-year postoperative outcomes than age and should be considered in preoperative, patient-centered decision-making.
