The association between pan-immune-inflammation value and diabetic kidney disease: a combined cross-sectional and prospective cohort study
Authors: Jian Jin, Shuwu Wei, Jiale Zhang, Jie Yang, Weishuai Sun, Weiwei Sun, Junping Wei, Juan Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWhile Pan-Immune-Inflammation Value (PIV) has been associated with chronic kidney disease of various etiologies, there is a lack of studies specifically analyzing the relationship between PIV and diabetic kidney disease (DKD) in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). This study aimed to explore the association between PIV and DKD.MethodsThe data were obtained from Guang’anmen Hospital and 38 community health service centers, spanning the period from 2014 to 2022. This study conducted a cross-sectional study including 7,610 T2DM patients and a cohort study comprising 1,494 T2DM patients without baseline kidney disease. In both studies, multivariable-adjusted logistic regression models and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were used to assess the relationship between PIV and DKD.ResultsIn the cross-sectional study, after adjusting for potential confounders, PIV was significantly and positively associated with DKD prevalence in T2DM patients, with those in the highest quartile exhibiting a 76.3% higher prevalence compared to the lowest quartile. In the cohort study, even after controlling for multiple confounding factors, higher PIV levels remained significantly associated with an increased risk of DKD, with the highest quartile showing a 131.3% higher incidence than the lowest quartile. Furthermore, the relationship between PIV and DKD incidence followed an inverse J-shaped pattern, and this association was largely consistent across most subpopulations.ConclusionIn individuals with type 2 diabetes, higher PIV levels are associated with an increased risk of developing diabetic kidney disease (DKD), highlighting the potential of PIV as a marker for identifying DKD risk in T2DM patients.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Pan-immune-inflammation value, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Diabetic kidney disease, Cross-sectional study, Prospectively cohort study

Introduction

In 2021, an estimated 529 million people worldwide were living with diabetes, including approximately 485 million individuals aged 20 to 79. By 2050, the global diabetes population is projected to rise to 1.31 billion [1–3]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) remains one of the most challenging long-term complications of diabetes, imposing substantial economic burdens and significantly impacting quality of life [4–6]. In 2019, diabetes and its associated kidney complications were estimated to contribute to 2 million deaths globally [7]. Therefore, timely identification and risk assessment for diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and the discovery of reliable risk markers are essential for enabling early interventions and targeted treatment strategies.

Markers of renal and systemic inflammation are linked to albuminuria, extracellular matrix deposition, and progressive declines in glomerular filtration rate [8–10]. The infiltration of inflammatory cells into diabetic kidneys partly reflects a response to tissue injury, but these cells and their by-products also actively mediate DKD by altering the renal microenvironment [11–13]. The pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV), a novel inflammatory marker derived from a complete blood count that includes four immune-inflammatory cell types, has emerged as a potentially superior predictive biomarker compared to the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), owing to its broader scope [14–15]. PIV has been associated with contrast-induced nephropathy and vascular diseases [16–18].

While PIV has been associated with chronic kidney disease of various etiologies, there is a lack of studies specifically analyzing the relationship between PIV and DKD in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). This study addresses this gap through a large-scale cross-sectional analysis combined with a cohort study to investigate the association between PIV and DKD.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A total of 7,875 patients with T2DM, aged 18 and older, admitted to Guang’anmen Hospital and 38 community health service centers in Dongcheng District, Beijing, China, between February 2014 and February 2022, were enrolled in this study. Patients missing urine tests (n = 67), glucose (GLU), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), serum creatinine (Scr), lipid profile, or routine blood examination results (n = 198) were excluded. This yielded a final sample of 7,610 patients for the cross-sectional study. Subsequently, we prospectively enrolled 1,807 T2DM patients, aged 18 and older, who had no kidney disease at baseline. Patients missing urine tests (n = 15), GLU, HbA1c, Scr, lipid profile, or routine blood examination results at baseline (n = 96), as well as those without follow-up information, were excluded. Ultimately, 1,494 participants were included in the prospective cohort study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Guang’anmen Hospital, affiliated with the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Approval No. 2023-187-KY), and the Ethics Committee of Dongzhimen Hospital, Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. ECPJ-BDY-2014-08). Measurements

Baseline demographic and clinical data, including age, gender, blood pressure, duration of diabetes, body mass index (BMI), and laboratory test results, were extracted from an electronic medical record system by trained physicians. Weight was measured to the nearest kilogram with participants minimally clothed and barefoot. Height was recorded in a standing position, without shoes, using a tape measure, ensuring shoulders were aligned normally. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). After a 10-minute seated rest, systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP) were measured on the right arm using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer. Blood samples were collected between 7:30 am and 10:00 am after an overnight fast of at least 8 h. All blood analyses were conducted in the same laboratory on the day of collection. Measurements included GLU, HbA1c, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum uric acid (SUA), Scr, platelets (PLT), neutrophils (NE), monocytes (MO), and lymphocytes (LY). Morning urine samples were collected from patients under calm conditions, without fever, infection, or other inflammatory states. T2DM was defined by plasma fasting glucose levels ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), random or 2-hour post-oral glucose tolerance test blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL), HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or current hypoglycemic therapy. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation adjusted for “Asian origin” [18]. DKD was defined as a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g and/or eGFR < 60 mL/min per 1.73 m², according to American Diabetes Association guidelines [19]. PIV = NE number × PLT number × MO number/LY number [20].

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables are presented as median ± interquartile range (IQR). Participants were divided into four groups based on the quartiles of PIV levels. Regression analysis was used to examine the associations between DKD and PIV levels, with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The performance of PIV levels in identifying DKD was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and by calculating the area under the curve (AUC). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was applied to evaluate potential nonlinear relationships between DKD and PIV levels, with knots positioned at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of PIV. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version 4.4.0). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cross-sectional study

The baseline characteristics of all participants, categorized by DKD status, are presented in Table 1. The analysis included a total of 7,610 diabetic participants, with a mean age of 59.46 ± 12.37 years. Of these, 5,963 (78.4%) were diagnosed without DKD (Non-DKD), while 1,647 (21.6%) were diagnosed with DKD. Compared to Non-DKD participants, those with DKD tended to be older, have higher blood pressure, and exhibit higher obesity rates. Additionally, levels of HbA1c, GLU, TC, TG, UA, UACR, Scr, PLT, NE, and MO were elevated in DKD patients, whereas HDL-C and eGFR levels were lower. Notably, DKD patients demonstrated higher PIV values than non-DKD participants.

All participants were categorized into DKD and non-DKD groups, and the potential of PIV levels as an early risk marker was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. The ROC analysis demonstrated that PIV levels possess potential discriminatory value for DKD. At the optimal PIV cutoff value of 180.73 (sensitivity 60.70%, specificity 56.30%), the AUC for assessing DKD prevalence was 0.608 (95% confidence interval: 0.593–0.624). Notably, the performance of PIV as a risk indicator was superior to other inflammatory markers (including NLR, PLR, MLR, nLPR, SIRI, and SII) (Fig. 4).

Prospective cohort study

A total of 1,494 patients with T2DM were included in the predictive cohort study. Baseline characteristics for all participants, grouped by DKD status, are presented in Table 2. The mean age of enrolled subjects was 63.85 ± 10.47 years, with 57.3% being female. Compared to the Non-DKD group, individuals in the DKD group showed higher PIV levels, as well as increased age, diabetes duration, blood pressure, BMI, HbA1c, GLU, TG, SUA, UACR, Scr, PLT, NE, MO, and LY. Additionally, Non-DKD participants were more likely to have lower HDL-C and eGFR levels.

Discussion

Using data from Guang’anmen Hospital and 38 community health service centers in Beijing’s Dongcheng District from 2014 to 2022, we conducted a cross-sectional study with 7,610 T2DM patients (with and without kidney disease) and a cohort study involving 1,494 T2DM patients without kidney disease at baseline. For the first time, we explored the positive association between PIV and both the prevalence and incidence of DKD. This unique association was further validated after adjusting for multiple confounding factors. These findings suggest that PIV may serve as a valuable marker for identifying DKD in T2DM patients.

PIV, a marker measuring levels of four immune-inflammatory cells, was developed and validated three years ago in colorectal cancer patients to improve accuracy by encompassing a wider range of peripheral blood immune-inflammatory cells [21]. Given the pivotal role of chronic inflammation in the progression of DKD in diabetic individuals, PIV is hypothesized to provide a more comprehensive assessment of inflammation levels, potentially serving as an early risk marker for DKD occurrence, making its association with DKD particularly significant. While PIV has previously been associated with acute kidney injury (AKI) [16–17], no prior studies have investigated its relationship with DKD, making our study the first to establish this link. Our findings suggest that PIV can act as a risk factor and early risk marker for DKD in T2DM populations, beyond conventional risk factors. The RCS curve demonstrated a linear relationship between PIV and DKD prevalence, and an inverse J-shaped relationship with DKD incidence. The AUC for PIV’s discriminatory value in assessing DKD prevalence was 0.608. Neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, and platelets—components of PIV—have previously been linked to DKD development in diabetic patients [15]. We hypothesize that the strong association between PIV and DKD may stem from cell damage induced by chronic hyperglycemia, which stimulates the release of pro-inflammatory mediators. This process likely recruits various immune-inflammatory peripheral blood cells, such as monocytes and lymphocytes, to the kidneys, leading to renal damage, fibrosis, and progression of DKD [14].

The observed inverse J-shaped relationship between PIV and the incidence of DKD is particularly intriguing. First, as a comprehensive systemic inflammatory marker, PIV reflects the activity level of immune cells. A moderate increase in PIV often indicates a state of chronic low-grade inflammation in the body. This chronic inflammation promotes the development and progression of DKD by enhancing local renal inflammatory responses, cell apoptosis, and fibrosis [10]. This process aligns with the current understanding of inflammation-driven DKD pathogenesis. However, when PIV reaches higher levels, it may indicate excessive immune activation or immune exhaustion, and may even reflect an immunosuppressed state in patients [22, 23]. At this stage, although inflammatory markers are elevated, the association between PIV and DKD may no longer be linear and may even reverse, resulting in the observed inverse J-shaped relationship. This phenomenon may be due to the complexity of immune regulatory mechanisms and the heterogeneity of systemic pathological states [15]. Second, as the disease progresses, the immune system may transition from an activated to a suppressed state. The initiation of immune tolerance mechanisms alters the proportion and function of inflammatory cells [16]. Despite high PIV values, its promoting effect on renal injury weakens, further explaining the nonlinear trend. Additionally, patients with high PIV levels often have more comorbidities or severe systemic illnesses, and may receive more medical interventions or exhibit different treatment responses. These clinical heterogeneities and selection biases may also affect the observed association between PIV and DKD, contributing to the inverse J-shaped curve statistically. Finally, from a clinical application perspective, this nonlinear relationship suggests that clinicians should consider the patient’s overall inflammatory status and systemic health when interpreting PIV, avoiding reliance solely on PIV levels to assess DKD risk, so as not to overstate or misinterpret its clinical significance.

In clinical practice, implementing proactive and effective preventive interventions at each stage of T2DM, especially in the early stages, is essential to mitigate the onset and progression of kidney disease. This study has several strengths: it is based on a large-scale investigation involving a hospital and 38 community health centers, employs both cross-sectional and cohort study designs, and adjusts for a wide range of confounding factors, which significantly enhances the reliability of the findings. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the study identifies only a statistical association between PIV and DKD at the clinical level, without clarifying the underlying molecular mechanisms. Second, PIV was measured only once, representing a single time-point “snapshot,” leaving the temporal sequence between PIV and DKD onset uncertain. Third, despite adjustments for numerous potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, such as smoking status, medication use, and so on. Finally, PIV is a nonspecific systemic inflammatory marker that may capture generalized inflammation across multiple organs rather than a DKD-specific pathophysiological process. Thus, while PIV may serve as an early warning signal, its risk-discriminative performance remains modest and requires further validation before clinical application (as demonstrated in our findings: AUC 0.608, sensitivity 0.607, specificity 0.563). Without a comprehensive evaluation of other organ systems, interpretations of PIV as an independent risk factor for DKD should be made with caution.

Conclusion

In patients with type 2 diabetes, elevated PIV levels are associated with an increased risk of developing DKD, highlighting the potential of PIV as a marker for identifying DKD risk in T2DM patients and underscoring its clinical significance for optimizing comprehensive management strategies in type 2 diabetes.
