Short Salem polynomials
James McKee, Chris Smyth

Abstract
. We give a complete classification of all Salem polynomials of length 5 5 . For length 6 6 we show that all but finitely many Salem polynomials lie in one of 12 12 infinite families, and subject to Lehmer’s Conjecture we give a complete list of the 126 exceptions. We provide a table of short polynomials for all known Salem numbers below the smallest Pisot number.

1.
Introduction and statement of results

A
Salem number
is a real algebraic integer
\tau>1
such that all of the Galois conjugates of
\tau
(excluding
\tau
itself) have modulus at most
1
, and with at least one having modulus exactly
1
.
For more about these numbers see the surveys
[
8
]
and
[
18
]
.

For a polynomial
P(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
that has degree
d
, we define
P^{*}(z)=z^{d}P(1/z)
; its coefficients are those of
P
written in reverse order.
We speak of
P^{*}
as being the
reciprocal
of
P
(its zeros are the reciprocals of the nonzero zeros of
P
).
We call
P

reciprocal
(respectively,
antireciprocal
) if
P^{*}=P
(respectively,
P^{*}=-P
).
The
length
of a polynomial is the sum of the absolute values of its coefficients.
We use
\Phi_{d}(z)
for the
d
-th cyclotomic polynomial; this is the minimal polynomial of
\omega_{d}:=e^{2\pi i/d}
.
It will be convenient to use the term
cyclotomic polynomial
more generally to mean any product of irreducible cyclotomic polynomials
\Phi_{d}
, allowing repeated factors, and allowing the empty product
1
.

The minimal polynomial of a Salem number
\tau
is reciprocal, of even degree at least
4
, and all but two of its zeros lie on the unit circle (the exceptions being
\tau
and
1/\tau
).
A
Salem polynomial
is defined to be the minimal polynomial of a Salem number, possibly (but not necessarily) multiplied by a cyclotomic polynomial.
The smallest known Salem number is Lehmer’s number,
\lambda_{0}=1.1762808\dots
, a zero of
z^{12}-z^{7}-z^{6}-z^{5}+1
.
This length-5 polynomial is not the minimal polynomial of
\lambda_{0}
, but is a multiple of its minimal polynomial by the cyclotomic polynomial
z^{2}-z+1
.
It is the shortest possible multiple of the minimal polynomial of
\lambda_{0}
by a cyclotomic polynomial, and we say that
\lambda_{0}
has
shortness

5
, with
z^{12}-z^{7}-z^{6}-z^{5}+1
being a
short polynomial
for
\lambda_{0}
(there is one other short polynomial for
\lambda_{0}
, namely
z^{14}-z^{11}-z^{7}-z^{3}+1
).

1.1.
Salem polynomials of shortness 5

It is easy to see that if a monic integer polynomial is reciprocal and has length at most
4
, then it is cyclotomic.
Thus the shortness of a Salem number is at least
5
(with
\lambda_{0}
illustrating that
5
is possible).

In this paper we give a complete list of all shortness-
5
Salem numbers (Theorem
1
).

Theorem 1
.

There are precisely
17
Salem polynomials of length
5
, associated to
13
distinct Salem numbers of shortness
5
.
These polynomials and Salem numbers are given in Table
1
.

The polynomials and numbers in Table
1
match exactly the relevant polynomials and numbers in Table 1 of
[
7
]
.
The table in
[
7
]
lists all monic reciprocal primitive integer polynomials (up to change of sign of the variable) of length
5
that have a zero of absolute value at least Lehmer’s number,
\lambda_{0}
.
This includes all Salem polynomials (perhaps with
z
replaced by
-z
) of length
5
for which the Salem number is at least Lehmer’s number.
Theorem
1
tells us there are no smaller Salem numbers of shortness
5
.

1.2.
Salem polynomials of shortness 6

For shortness
6
the situation is more complicated.
There are twelve infinite families of length-
6
Salem polynomials (all previously known) which alongside Theorem
1
shows that there are infinitely many Salem polynomials (representing infinitely many Salem numbers) that have shortness
6
.
We show that there are finitely many other shortness-
6
Salem polynomials (Theorem
2
).
We give a complete list for those whose Salem numbers are at least
1.01
. By Dobrowolski’s result
[
6
, Theorem 1]
there are no Salem polynomials of length 6 whose Salem number is less that
1+10^{-66}
, so extending our search method as close to
1
as this would give a complete list. However, this is impractical.
Nevertheless it is plausible that our list is indeed complete.
Of course, this would be the case if, as is strongly suspected, the conjecture that
\lambda_{0}
is the smallest Salem number is true (a special case of the strong version of ‘Lehmer’s Conjecture’
[
10
, Section 1.2]
).

Theorem 2
.

There are
12
infinite families of length-6 Salem polynomials, of the shape
P_{n}(z)=z^{n}P(z)+\varepsilon P^{*}(z)
, where

P(z)\in\{z-2,z^{2}-z-1,z^{3}-z-1,z^{3}-z^{2}-1,z^{4}-z^{3}-1,z^{5}-z^{4}-1\}\,,

\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
, and
n\geq n_{0}=n_{0}(P,\varepsilon)
.
For values of
n_{0}
, see Table
3
.

There are finitely many shortness-6 Salem numbers that do not belong to one these infinite families.
There are
126
sporadic length-6 Salem polynomials for which the Salem number is at least
1.01
.
Of these, 116 correspond to Salem numbers below
\theta_{0}
, and 10 correspond to Salem numbers above
\theta_{0}
.

1.3.
Cyclotomic factors of polynomials in Salem’s families

Recall that a
Pisot number
is a real algebraic integer
\theta>1
such that all of the Galois conjugates of
\theta
(excluding
\theta
itself) have modulus strictly less than one.
We define a
Pisot polynomial
to be the minimal polynomial of a Pisot number, possibly multiplied by a cyclotomic polynomial.
The smallest Pisot number is
\theta_{0}=1.3247179\dots
, a zero of
z^{3}-z-1
.

Let
P(z)
be a Pisot polynomial that is not equal to its reciprocal polynomial.
Salem considered the families of polynomials
z^{n}P(z)\pm P^{*}(z)
, to produce sequences of Salem numbers converging to the Pisot zero of
P(z)
, from either side.
We recall a more precise statement of this in Proposition
2.5
, adding some contributions of Boyd.
We extract part of that Proposition here for ease of reference, as a technical theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Let
P(z)
be an irreducible Pisot polynomial that is not equal to its reciprocal polynomial. Take
\varepsilon=\pm 1
, and define, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
P_{n}(z)=z^{n}P(z)+\varepsilon P^{*}(z)
.
Then

(i)

there is a finite set of values of
d
(depending on
P
) for which there exists
n
such that
\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n}(z)
;

(ii)

if for some integer
n_{d}
we have
\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n_{d}}(z)
, then
\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n}(z)
if and only if
n\equiv n_{d}\pmod{d}
.

The finite set of the theorem can be computed, and we do so for the families that appear in Theorem
2
.
Part (i) follows from
[
4
, Lemma 2.2]
, but we give a simpler proof.

For the proof of Theorem
1
, we need the following.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
a
and
b
be fixed positive numbers, and
f(\theta):=\sin(a\theta)\sin(b\theta)
. Then on
[0,2\pi]
the distinct zeros of
f(\theta)
interlace the zeros of
f^{\prime}(\theta)
.

Proof.

Note that
f^{\prime}(\theta)/f(\theta)=a\cot(a\theta)+b\cot(b\theta)
.
Given that
a
and
b
are positive, we see that
f^{\prime}(\theta)/f(\theta)
is strictly decreasing between its poles, and the poles of
f^{\prime}(\theta)/f(\theta)
correspond precisely to the zeros of
f(\theta)
.
Hence
f^{\prime}(\theta)
has precisely one zero between each consecutive pair of distinct zeros of
f(\theta)
.
∎

We also need a particular version of the Bézout identity (or extended Euclidean algorithm) for coprime integers.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
a
and
b
be fixed coprime positive integers, both at least
3
. Then there is some
\varepsilon=\pm 1
and positive integers
i<b/2
and
j<a/2
such that
ia-jb=\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Since
a
and
b
are not both even, we can assume that
a
is odd. From the extended Euclidean algorithm there are integers
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}:i^{\prime}a-j^{\prime}b=1
, so that for any integer

r
we have
(i^{\prime}-rb)a-(j^{\prime}+ra)b=1
. We can choose
\varepsilon=\pm 1
and
r
such that
i:=(i^{\prime}-rb)\varepsilon
lies in the interval
[0,b/2]
. Since
\gcd(i^{\prime},b)=1
and
b>2
, in fact
i
satisfies
0<i<b/2
. Next, put
j:=(j^{\prime}+ra)\varepsilon
, so that
ia-jb=\varepsilon
, and
j=(ia-\varepsilon)/b
is
>0
(since clearly
i
and
j
have the same sign). Then

j\leq\frac{ia}{b}+\frac{1}{b}<\frac{a}{2}+\frac{1}{b}<\frac{a+1}{2}.

Since
a
is assumed odd, we have

j\leq\frac{a-1}{2}<\frac{a}{2}.

∎

Corollary 2.4
.

For any Salem polynomial
S(z)
the minimal polynomial of its corresponding Salem number is

S(z)/\gcd(S(z),S(-z)S(z^{2})S(-z^{2}))
.

This follows from the lemma because no Salem number
\tau

-\tau
,
\tau^{2}
or
-\tau^{2}
.
A similar remark holds for Pisot polynomials, although in that case one can more simply divide a Pisot polynomial

\gcd(P,P^{*})
to remove cyclotomic factors.
In the proof of Theorem

, the construction of the infinite sequences of Salem polynomials of length

is by Salem’s method
[

]
: for a Pisot polynomial
P(z)
,
a choice of sign
\varepsilon=\pm 1
, and

is a Salem polynomial of length

.
We will need to be precise about what “sufficiently large” means, and we do this in Proposition

[

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
P(z)
be an irreducible Pisot polynomial that is not equal to its reciprocal polynomial, and
Q(z)=P^{*}(z)
.
Take
\varepsilon=\pm 1
, and define, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

P_{n}(z)=z^{n}P(z)+\varepsilon Q(z)\,.

Let
r=(Q^{\prime}(1)-P^{\prime}(1))/P(1)
, and let
\theta
be the Pisot number that is a zero of
P(z)
.
Then:

(i)

P_{n}(z)
has at most one zero outside the unit circle.
If there is one, it is in the real interval
(1,\infty)
.

(ii)

If
\varepsilon=1
, then
P_{n}(z)
has a unique simple zero
\rho_{n}\in(1,\infty)
, and all other zeros have modulus
\leq 1
.

(iii)

If
\varepsilon=-1
and
n<r
, then
P_{n}(z)
has all its zeros on the unit circle.

(iv)

If
\varepsilon=-1
and
n>r
, then
P_{n}(z)
has a unique simple zero
\rho_{n}\in(1,\infty)
, and all other zeros have modulus
\leq 1
.

(v)

There exists
n_{0}
such that
P_{n}(z)
is a cyclotomic polynomial if
n<n_{0}
, and such that
P_{n}(z)
has a unique zero
\rho_{n}
in
(1,\infty)
if
n\geq n_{0}
(and
\rho_{n}
is either a Pisot number or a Salem number).
In the latter case,
P_{n}(z)
is the minimal polynomial of
\rho_{n}
multiplied by a cyclotomic polynomial.

(vi)

If
\varepsilon Q(\theta)>0
then
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq n_{0}}
is a strictly increasing sequence.
If
\varepsilon Q(\theta)<0
then
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq n_{0}}
is a strictly decreasing sequence.
In either case,
\rho_{n}\to\theta
as
n\to\infty
.

(vii)

There is a finite set of
n
for which
P_{n}(z)
has a repeated factor.

(viii)

There is a finite set of
d
for which there exists
n
such that
\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n}(z)
.
If for some
n_{d}
we have
\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n_{d}}(z)
, then
\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n}(z)
if and only if
n\equiv n_{d}\pmod{d}
.

(ix)

There is a finite set of
n
for which
\rho_{n}
is a Pisot number; for all sufficiently large
n
,
\rho_{n}
is a Salem number.

Before proving all of this, we make some remarks.
Salem’s argument from
[
14
]
gives (i), (ii), the final sentence in (vi), and (ix).
He also shows that according to the sign of
\varepsilon Q(\theta)
the
\rho_{n}
all lie on one or other side of
\theta
, revealing that there are Salem numbers converging to
\theta
from both sides.
Boyd
[
1
]
comments on the monotonicity of Salem’s sequences, establishes (iii)–(v), and shows that any square-free Salem polynomial arises via Salem’s construction.
One can compute the finite sets mentioned in (vii)–(ix) using the technique described in detail for one case in Section
5
.
The hypotheses require that
P
is an irreducible Pisot polynomial.
More generally, given a Pisot polynomial
P
, one can compute
R=\gcd(P,P^{*})
; then
P/R
is an irreducible Pisot polynomial to which the proposition applies, and the sequence of polynomials produced by
P
is simply the sequence produced by
P/R
with all the polynomials in the sequence multiplied by
R
; part (vii) and the final sentence of (viii) might not hold for the sequence of polynomials produced by
P
.
A stronger version of part (vii) is given as Lemma 2.1 in
[
4
]
, drawing together some of the remarks in
[
1
]
.
We need only the weaker statement here, which has an easy proof.
The first half of part (viii) follows from
[
4
, Lemma 2.2]
, but we give an alternative proof that is related to the method we use later for finding the claimed finite set, and is considerably simpler than the proof of Chinburg’s lemma.
Note that our argument for the proof of (ix) also leads to a simpler proof of
[
4

.

Proof.

(i) The argument in
[
15
]
is shorter than that in
[
14
]
, and we summarise it here.
Take any
\delta>0
.
Noting that when
|z|=1
we have
|P(z)|=|Q(z)|
, we see via a Rouché argument using the circle
|z|=1
that
(1+\delta)z^{n}P(z)+\varepsilon Q(z)
has all but one zero strictly inside the unit circle, and one zero strictly outside.
Since
(1+\delta)P(1)+\varepsilon Q(1)\leq\delta P(1)<0
, and using the intermediate value theorem, the unique zero outside the unit circle lies in the interval
(1,\infty)
.
Letting
\delta\to 0
, using continuity of zeros as the coefficients vary continuously, gives (i).

(ii) When
\varepsilon=1
, we have
P_{n}(1)=2P(1)<0
, and the intermediate value theorem on the interval
(1,\infty)
, along with (i), gives (ii).

(iii) When
\varepsilon=-1
, we have
P_{n}(1)=0
.
We compute
P_{n}^{\prime}(1)=P^{\prime}(1)+nP(1)-Q^{\prime}(1)
.
If
n<r
, we see that
P_{n}^{\prime}(1)>0
, so that
P_{n}
is positive for real numbers in the interval
(1,1+\delta)
for sufficiently small
\delta>0
, and then by (i) and the intermediate value theorem there can be no zero in
(1,\infty)
, or there would have to be at least two (with multiplicity).
Then by (i) and Kronecker’s theorem
[
10
, Theorem 1.3]
, we have (iii).

(iv) Contrasting with (iii), we have
P_{n}^{\prime}(1)<0
when
n>r
, so that
P_{n}
has a zero in
(1,\infty)
, and we are done by (i).

(v) The first part follows from (i), (iii) and (iv).
That
\rho_{n}
is either Pisot or Salem follows from (i) (since
P_{n}
is a reciprocal polynomial, we have
P_{n}(1/\rho_{n})=0
and all other zeros lie on the unit circle).
The final sentence comes from Kronecker’s theorem again.

(vi) Take
n\geq n_{0}
.
Since
P_{n}(\theta)=\varepsilon Q(\theta)
, we see that
\rho_{n}\in(1,\theta)
if
\varepsilon Q(\theta)>0
, and
\rho_{n}\in(\theta,\infty)
if
\varepsilon Q(\theta)<0
.
For
n\geq n_{0}
we have, using
\varepsilon Q(\rho_{n})=-P(\rho_{n})\rho_{n}^{n}
, that

P_{n+1}(\rho_{n})=P(\rho_{n})\rho_{n}^{n+1}+\varepsilon Q(\rho_{n})=P(\rho_{n})\rho_{n}^{n+1}-P(\rho_{n})\rho_{n}^{n}=P(\rho_{n})\rho_{n}^{n}(\rho_{n}-1)\,.

In the case
\varepsilon Q(\theta)<0
, when
\rho_{n}>\theta
, this gives
P_{n+1}(\rho_{n})>0
, so
\rho_{n+1}<\rho_{n}
;
and if
\varepsilon Q(\theta)>0
, when
\rho_{n}<\theta
, we instead get
P_{n+1}(\rho_{n})<0
, so
\rho_{n+1}>\rho_{n}
.

Since
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq n_{0}}
is bounded and monotonic, it converges to some
\theta^{\prime}
.
If
\theta^{\prime}
did not equal
\theta
, then
P_{n}(\rho_{n})=P(\rho_{n})\rho_{n}^{n}+\varepsilon Q(\rho_{n})
would be unbounded; yet
P_{n}(\rho_{n})=0
for
n\geq n_{0}
.
This completes (vi).

(vii) If
P_{n}(z)
has a repeated factor, then by (v) it has a repeated zero that has modulus
1
.
Such a zero would be a common zero of
P_{n}(z)
and
zP_{n}^{\prime}(z)=zP^{\prime}(z)z^{n}+nP(z)z^{n}+\varepsilon zQ^{\prime}(z)
.
Eliminating
z^{n}
between the equations
P_{n}(z)=0
and
zP_{n}^{\prime}(z)=0
gives

Q(z)(zP^{\prime}(z)+nP(z))-zP(z)Q^{\prime}(z)=0
.
Noting that
P(z)Q(z)
does not vanish on the unit circle (here we need
P
an irreducible Pisot polynomial), this gives

n\leq\sup_{|z|=1}|(P(z)Q^{\prime}(z)-Q(z)P^{\prime}(z))/P(z)Q(z)|\,,

so that there is finite set
of possible
n
.
(Note that
[
4
, Lemma 2.1]
is stronger: it states that
P_{n}(z)
has no repeated factors unless
\varepsilon=-1
and

, in which case
(z-1)^{3}\mid P_{n}(z)
and in that case
P_{n}(z)/(z-1)^{3}
has no repeated factors.)

(viii) After Lemma
2.3
we see that if
\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n}(z)
then
\Phi_{d}(z)
also divides one of
P_{n}(-z)
,
P_{n}(z^{2})
, or
P_{n}(-z^{2})
.

In the first of these three cases, eliminating
z^{n}
between
P_{n}(z)=0
and
P_{n}(-z)=0
gives
Q(z)P(-z)+(-1)^{n+1}Q(-z)P(z)=0
.
This polynomial cannot be identically zero, else evaluating at
\theta

0=Q(\theta)P(-\theta)\neq 0
(here using
P\neq Q
).
Thus there are only finitely many cyclotomic factors in this case.

The second and third cases lead to the vanishing of
Q(z)^{2}P(z^{2})+\varepsilon Q(z^{2})P(z)^{2}
or
Q(z)^{2}P(-z^{2})+\varepsilon(-1)^{n}Q(-z^{2})P(z)^{2}
respectively, and in either case we see by evaluating at
\theta
that the relevant polynomial is not identically zero, so has finitely many cyclotomic factors.

Now
\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n_{d}}(z)
if and only if
P_{n_{d}}(\omega_{d})=0
, where
\omega_{d}
is a primitive
d

depends only on

\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n_{d}+md}(z)
for all natural numbers

.
So if
\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n_{d}}(z)
and
\Phi_{d}(z)\mid P_{n}(z)
, then
\Phi_{d}(z)

, hence (given

\Phi_{d}
) we have

and

.

(ix) By (vii), for all sufficiently large

, if
\Phi_{d}
divides

then it does so with multiplicity

.
Then by (viii), the degree of the product of all such irreducible cyclotomic factors of

is bounded.
Since the degree of

tends to

∎

Applying this in detail to length-
6
Salem polynomials, we see that we get two infinite families of such polynomials for each length-
3
Pisot polynomial.
The following lemma shows that there are only finitely many such infinite families.
A
trinomial
is an integer polynomial having three coefficients of modulus
1
. A trinomial Pisot polynomial must be of the form
z^{n}-z^{k}-1
with
1\leq k<n
in order that its value at
z=1
is negative.

Lemma 2.6
.

There are exactly five trinomial Pisot polynomials:
z^{2}-z-1
,
z^{3}-z-1
,
z^{3}-z^{2}-1
,
z^{4}-z^{3}-1
, and
z^{5}-z^{4}-1
.
The first four of these are irreducible, while
z^{5}-z^{4}-1=(z^{2}-z+1)(z^{3}-z-1)
. There is a further (nontrinomial) Pisot polynomial of length
3
, namely
z-2
.

Proof.

Suppose that
n\geq 6
and
k<n
. Then
1.3^{n}>13/3
, and

1.3^{n}-1.3^{k}\geq 1.3^{n}-1.3^{n-1}>(13/3)(1-1/1.3)=1,

so that
z^{n}-z^{k}-1
has a root in
(1,1.3)
. However, by a result of Siegel
[
16
]
, the smallest Pisot number, the real zero of
z^{3}-z-1
, is
1.3247\cdots>1.3
. Hence all trinomial Pisot polynomials have degree at most
5
. A straightforward search readily checks that the only ones are those given in the statement of the lemma.
∎

No Salem polynomial can have length 3 (any reciprocal length-
3
polynomial is cyclotomic), but we may ask if any Salem number can ever be a zero of a trinomial.
The answer is ‘no’, and we record this as a remark.

Remark 2.7
.

Let
\tau
be a Salem number, and let
P(z)
be a trinomial
z^{n}\pm z^{m}\pm 1
with
n>m\geq 0
.
Then
P(\tau)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose that
P(\tau)=0
.
Thus
\tau^{n}=\varepsilon_{1}\tau^{m}+\varepsilon_{2}
for some
\varepsilon_{1}
,
\varepsilon_{2}\in\{-1,1\}
.
Now let
\beta
be a conjugate of
\tau
on the unit circle.
Then
\beta^{n}=\varepsilon_{1}\beta^{m}+\epsilon_{2}
.
Thus
\beta^{n}
lies on the intersection of the two circles
|z|=1
and
|z-\varepsilon_{2}|=1
.
This gives
\beta^{n}
a root of unity (order either
3
or
6
), so that
\beta
is a root of unity.
This contradicts
\beta
being a conjugate of
\tau
.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
2
:

Short Salem polynomials in intervals

We have in mind the desire to prove Theorem
2
, but for the moment we consider the more general question of finding short Salem numbers in intervals.

If
P(z)=a_{0}z^{d}+\cdots+a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}[x]
has Mahler measure
M
, then Gonçalves’ inequality
[
10
, Proposition 1.12]
states that
M^{2}+a_{0}^{2}a_{d}^{2}/M^{2}\leq a_{0}^{2}+\cdots+a_{d}^{2}
.
Suppose further that
P
is a monic reciprocal or antireciprocal polynomial of length
\ell\geq 5
.
Then the sum of the squares of its coefficients is at most
1^{2}+(\ell-2)^{2}+(\pm 1)^{2}=\ell^{2}-4\ell+6
.
In particular, if
P
is the short polynomial of a Salem number
\tau
then we get that
\tau^{2}+1/\tau^{2}\leq\ell^{2}-4\ell+6
and hence a bound
b=b(\ell)
such that
\tau\leq b
.
In the other direction, Dobrowolski’s lower bound on the Mahler measure of
P
as a function of its number of terms
[
5
,
6
]
shows that there is some
a>1
(again depending on
\ell
, but otherwise independent of
\tau
and
P
) such that
\tau\geq a
.
Thus
\tau
will lie in the interval
[a,b]
.

We now consider how we might attempt to find all length-
\ell
Salem polynomials in an interval
[a,b]
, with
1<a<b
.
Choosing
a
and
b
as above, this would give all length-
\ell
Salem polynomials, but in practice we might restrict to a shorter interval.
There might be infinite families (those of Salem, or others), and we will mix theory with pragmatism in situations where we cannot with certainty give a complete description: what is the best that we can say after a reasonable amount of computation?

A key tool is the algorithm described in
[
10
, Section 1.7.1]
, adapted to the search for reciprocal polynomials.
The technique we use is similar to that in
[
7
]
, but with the restriction to Salem polynomials allowing further efficiencies.
We are also able to cope with certain limit points in the interval, including all those that arise when the length is
6
: we can identify families approaching those limit points, and otherwise restrict to finitely many ‘sporadic’ examples.
We are looking for Salem numbers in
[a,b]
that are zeros of Salem polynomials of length
\ell\geq 6
(the length-
5
case having been settled in Theorem
1
).
We write our length-
\ell
Salem polynomial
P(z)
as

z^{n}+\varepsilon_{1}z^{n-g_{1}}+\varepsilon_{2}z^{n-g_{1}-g_{2}}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{\ell-1}\,,

where the first “gap”
g_{1}
is strictly positive, but later gaps
g_{i}
are only weakly positive; each
\varepsilon_{i}
is either
1
or
-1
, and there is no cancellation of terms.
Since
P(z)=\pm P^{*}(z)
, if we know at least the first half of the
g_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{i}
, then we know the complete polynomial.

The bounds in
[
10
, Section 1.7.1]
, given that our Salem number is at least
a
, give

g_{1}\leq\lfloor\log(\ell-1)/\log a\rfloor\,,

g_{2}\leq\lfloor(\log(\ell-2)-\log|a^{g_{1}}-1|)/\log a\rfloor\,,

and more generally,

g_{i}\leq\lfloor(\log(\ell-i)-\inf_{x\in[a,b]}\log|Q(x)|)/\log a\rfloor\,,

where

Q(z)=z^{g_{1}+\dots+g_{i-1}}+\varepsilon_{1}z^{g_{2}+\dots+g_{i-1}}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{i-1}\,.

We can always bound
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
by this approach, but we may already hit trouble with
g_{3}
, if
Q(z)
has a zero in the real interval
[a,b]
.

If
Q(z)
has a zero in
[a,b]
, what can we do?
If
Q(z)
has more than one zero outside the unit circle, then we can apply a Rouché argument to bound the next gap, in a manner similar to that used in
[
7
]
: encircle the zeros of
Q
outside the unit circle using small enough radii that the circles produced do not overlap, and do not meet the unit circle; compute rigorous lower bounds for
|Q|
on all these circles; if the next gap is too large, then a Rouché estimate shows that there will be at least two zeros outside the unit circle, which excludes all Salem polynomials.

The only situation where we are unable to bound the next gap is if
Q(z)
has a unique zero outside the unit circle, lying in the interval
[a,b]
.
This troublesome zero is therefore either a Salem number or a Pisot number.
If
\ell
is even and
Q(z)
has length
\ell/2
, then we are saved: either we have identified one of Salem’s families, or we have found an infinite sequence of Salem polynomials of the shape
Q(z)(z^{n}\pm 1)
where
Q(z)
is itself a Salem polynomial.

If
\ell=6
, this approach succeeds in identifying all Salem numbers in
[a,b]
that are represented by length-
6
Salem polynomials.
Together with Lemma
2.6
, and taking
1<a<b
as above so that all length-
6
Salem numbers have their corresponding Salem number in the interval
[a,b]
, this establishes most of Theorem
2
: there are
12
infinite families of length-
6
Salem polynomials, and otherwise finitely many length-
6
Salem numbers.
The existence of a transition point
n_{0}=n_{0}(P)
such that
P_{n}(z)
in Theorem
2
is a Salem polynomial if and only if
n\geq n_{0}
follows from the computations in Section
5

one could imagine the possibility that a quadratic Pisot number appears after the first Salem number in the sequence, but for the families in Theorem
2
this is not so.

If
\ell>6
, the situation is muddied if our interval
[a,b]
contains any Salem or Pisot number of shortness below
\ell/2
.
We can record such obstacles and move on, but unless we find an
ad hoc
way to deal with these afterwards, our search will be incomplete.
We may also hit practical issues if the finite search is unreasonably large.
In such cases we can impose bounds on the gaps to restore a practical search, but then need to record whenever those bounds are enforced, as if that happens then the search is incomplete.

5.
The minimal polynomials of Salem numbers in Salem’s length-6 families

In this section, working through one of the most interesting examples, we indicate how the various finiteness assertions in Proposition
2.5
can be made explicit.
Then in Table
3
we summarise the corresponding data for all the infinite families of length-6 Salem polynomials.

Taking
P(z)=z^{3}-z-1
and
\varepsilon=-1
in Proposition
2.5
, we define
P_{n}
and
\tau_{n}
accordingly.
The value of
r
in the statement of the theorem is
7
, and we then check that
n_{0}
in part (v) is
8
.
We therefore know that
P_{n}(x)
has, for
n\geq 8
exactly one root outside the unit circle, so that the non-cyclotomic part of
P_{n}(x)
is irreducible.
Thus to find the degree of this non-cyclotomic part we just need to find all the cyclotomic factors of
P_{n}(z)
, for all
n>7
, and subtract their degrees from the degree of
P_{n}(z)
.

To do this, we first need to define

\delta_{j,k}(n):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $n\equiv j\pmod{k}$};\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

We can then define the function
F(n)
by

(5.1)

F(n):=\delta_{1,{2}}(n)+2\delta_{1,{3}}(n)+4\delta_{2,{5}}(n)+4\delta_{3,{8}}(n)+4\delta_{4,{12}}(n)\\
+6\delta_{5,{18}}(n)+8\delta_{6,{30}}(n).

Note that the function
F(n)
is periodic of period equal to the least common multiple

\operatorname{lcm}(2,3,5,8,12,18,30)=360.

Its maximum value is
15
, attained for
n=347
.

We claim the following.

Proposition 5.1
.

The minimal polynomial
S_{n}(x)
of
\tau_{n}
for
n\geq 8
is
given by

(5.2)

S_{n}(z)=\frac{P_{n}(z)}{\Phi_{1}(z)\Phi_{2}(z)^{\delta_{1,2}}\Phi_{3}(z)^{\delta_{1,3}}\Phi_{5}(z)^{\delta_{2,5}}\Phi_{8}(z)^{\delta_{3,8}}\Phi_{12}(z)^{\delta_{4,12}}\Phi_{18}(z)^{\delta_{5,18}}\Phi_{30}(z)^{\delta_{6,30}}},

and has degree
f_{n}:=n+2-F(n)
.

One can readily compute that
\tau_{n}
has degree
>50
for all
n\geq 60
, with
\tau_{59}
having degree
50
.

In the proof, we make use of the resultant of two nonzero polynomials
A,B\in R[z]
, where
R
is an integral domain. The resultant
\Res_{z}(A,B)\in R
, the result of eliminating
z
from
A
and
B
, is
0
if and only if
A
and
B
have a common nonconstant factor in
R[z]
.

Proof.

Let
\phi(n)
be the degree of the
n
th cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{n}(z)
. We immediately see that
P_{n}(z)
is divisible by
\Phi_{1}(z)=z-1
for all
n
.
The idea of the proof is to show that any polynomial
P_{n}(z)
can be divisible by
\Phi_{k}(z)
only for
k=1,2,3,5,8,12,18
or
30
, and that, to be precise,

•

\Phi_{2}(z)
for
n\equiv 1\pmod{2}
;

•

\Phi_{3}(z)
for
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
;

•

\Phi_{5}(z)
for
n\equiv 2\pmod{5}
;

•

\Phi_{8}(z)
for
n\equiv 3\pmod{8}
;

•

\Phi_{12}(z)
for
n\equiv 4\pmod{12}
;

•

\Phi_{18}(z)
for
n\equiv 5\pmod{18}
;

•

\Phi_{30}(z)
for
n\equiv 6\pmod{30}
.

Since for
n\geq 8
the polynomial
P_{n}(z)/(z-1)
has no repeated roots (
[
1
]
,
[
4
, Lemma 2.1]
),
has degree
n+2
, and
\Phi_{k}(z)
has degree
\phi(k)
, the result will follow.
We now give the details. Our method was used earlier in
[
17
]
.
Define
T_{3}(x,y):=y(x^{3}-x-1)+x^{3}+x^{2}-1
, so that
P_{n}(x)=T_{3}(x,x^{n})
. Put
y=x^{n}
. Then the map

•

x\mapsto-x
takes

\begin{cases}y\mapsto-y&\text{ for $n$ odd};\\
y\mapsto y&\text{ for $n$ even};\end{cases}

•

x\mapsto-x^{2}
takes

\begin{cases}y\mapsto-y^{2}&\text{ for $n$ odd};\\
y\mapsto y^{2}&\text{ for $n$ even};\end{cases}

•

x\mapsto x^{2}
takes
y\mapsto y^{2}
.

By Lemma
2.3
, every cyclotomic factor of
P_{n}(z)
must also be a factor of one of
P_{n}(-z)
,
P_{n}(-z^{2})
or
P_{n}(z^{2})
. In terms of
T_{3}(x,y)
this implies that the only possible such cyclotomic factors are the cyclotomic factors of

\displaystyle\Res_{y}(T_{3}(x,y),T_{3}(-x,-y))

\displaystyle=\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{2}(x)\Phi_{8}(x)

\displaystyle\text{ ($n$ odd)};

\displaystyle\Res_{y}(T_{3}(x,y),T_{3}(-x,y))

\displaystyle=x\Phi_{12}(x)

\displaystyle\text{ ($n$ even)};

\displaystyle\Res_{y}(T_{3}(x,y),T_{3}(-x^{2},-y^{2}))

\displaystyle=x\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{2}(x)^{3}\Phi_{18}(x)

\displaystyle\text{ ($n$ odd)};

\displaystyle\Res_{y}(T_{3}(x,y),T_{3}(-x^{2},y^{2}))

\displaystyle=\Phi_{12}(x)\Phi_{30}(x)

\displaystyle\text{ ($n$ even)};

\displaystyle\Res_{y}(T_{3}(x,y),T_{3}(x^{2},y^{2}))

\displaystyle=\Phi_{1}(x)^{3}\Phi_{2}(x)\Phi_{3}(x)^{2}\Phi_{5}(x).\,

Hence we see that the only possible cyclotomic factors of
P_{n}(z)
are

\Phi_{1}(z)=z-1
,
\Phi_{2}(z)=z+1
,
\Phi_{3}(z)=z^{2}+z+1
,
\Phi_{5}(z)=z^{4}+z^{3}+z^{2}+z+1
,
\Phi_{8}(z)=z^{4}+1
,
\Phi_{12}(z)=z^{4}-z^{2}+1
,
\Phi_{18}(z)=z^{6}-z^{3}+1
and
\Phi_{30}(z)=z^{8}+z^{7}-z^{5}-z^{4}-z^{3}+z+1
.
Now for
k=2,3,5,8,12,18,30
we easily check that the least values
n_{k}
for which
\Phi_{k}(z)\mid P_{n_{k}}(z)
are
1,1,2,3,4,5,6
, respectively.
Hence, by Theorem
3
(ii),
\Phi_{k}(z)\mid P_{n}(z)
precisely for
n\equiv n_{k}\pmod{k}
, as claimed at the start of the proof.

Finally, since for these values of
k
the cyclotomic polynomials
\Phi_{k}(z)
have degrees
1,2,4,4,4,6,6
respectively, and because for
n\geq 8
the polynomial
P_{n}(z)
has no repeated roots, we obtain the formula for
S_{n}(z)
as stated in (
5.2
), as well as the formula (
5.1
) for
F(n)
. Then since the numerator of
S_{n}(z)
has degree
n+3
and its denominator has degree
1+F(n)
, we obtain
f_{n}=n+2-F(n)
as claimed.

∎
