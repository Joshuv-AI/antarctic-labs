Dupilumab for the treatment of pemphigoid gestationis in the setting of breastfeeding: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Gabriela Soto-Canetti, Kristina Navrazhina, Jordan Talia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPemphigoid gestationis (PG) is a rare blistering dermatosis usually seen in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. Treatment of PG is aimed towards the relief of pruritus and prevention of new lesions from developing, often involving high-potency topical corticosteroids, antihistamines, oral corticosteroids and other systemic immune modulators for patients with symptoms of greater severity. Recently, dupilumab, a monoclonal antibody against the alpha subunit of the interleukin-4 receptor, has been used successfully in bullous pemphigoid. We discuss the case of a 34-year-old Asian woman at 32 weeks’ gestation who presented with progressive and severely pruritic oedematous urticarial plaques and patches dispersed across the trunk and extremities, with scattered tense bullae. A skin biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of PG. The patient was placed on high-dose oral corticosteroids and concomitant topical steroids, with minimal resolution after attempts to taper oral steroids over the next 4 weeks. Oral prednisone was continued at 17.5 mg for the remainder of her pregnancy given the risks associated with higher dosages. After delivery, the patient’s disease worsened, requiring an increase in prednisone. Given that the patient was breastfeeding, dupilumab was initiated as a steroid-sparing agent allowing patient to be tapered off prednisone with significant improvement and control of her disease. The safety of dupilumab for infants who are being breastfed remains under discussion. Herein, we discuss the existing literature and considerations regarding pregnancy and breastfeeding for patients with PG on dupilumab.

Pemphigoid gestationis (PG) is a rare blistering dermatosis that typically presents in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. It is estimated to impact about 1 in 2000–60 000 pregnancies.1 PG involves a maternal autoimmune response against collagen XVII and the bullous pemphigoid (BP) proteins 180 (BP180) and 230 (BP230), which are present in hemidesmosomes at the dermoepidermal junction (DEJ).1 This reaction at the basement membrane of the skin results in the characteristic formation of pruritic and oedematous urticarial plaques with tense bullae and vesicles. The abundance of these proteins in placental tissue is thought to be a precipitating factor for this reaction during pregnancy.1 Treatment of PG is aimed towards the clearance of existing lesions, the prevention of new lesions and relief of pruritus. When limited in body surface area, high-potency topical corticosteroids and antihistamines may help control the disease. For patients with symptoms of a greater severity and lesions involving >10% of the body surface area, physicians may consider oral corticosteroids, ciclosporin, dapsone, azathioprine or intravenous immunoglobulin.1 Given the immunosuppressive side effects associated with these agents, there has been a particular interest in therapeutics that cause minimal to no immunosuppression. Recently, dupilumab, a monoclonal antibody against the alpha subunit of the interleukin (IL)-4 receptor (IL-4Rα), has been used successfully in BP, which has a similar pathophysiology to PG.2 We present the case of a 34-year-old Asian woman with PG who was successfully treated with dupilumab after pregnancy and during breastfeeding. Additionally, we review the existing literature and discuss the considerations regarding pregnancy and lactation for patients with PG on dupilumab.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old Asian woman G1P0 at 32 weeks’ gestation presented to the emergency department with 4 weeks of progressive and severely pruritic lesions that began in her umbilicus and spread throughout the torso and extremities. Physical examination revealed oedematous urticarial plaques and patches dispersed across her trunk and extremities with scattered tense bullae (Figure 1). Haematoxylin and eosin-stained biopsy tissue demonstrated spongiotic dermatitis with eosinophils and direct immunofluorescence of a skin sample demonstrated a linear pattern of C3 deposition at the DEJ, consistent with a diagnosis of PG. At that time, the patient was started on topical clobetasol propionate and oral prednisone 60 mg daily to be tapered upon outpatient follow-up. The patient presented to our clinic 1 week later with some improvement in pruritus and limited development of new skin lesions, at which time she was placed on prednisone 20 mg daily to be tapered over 4 weeks with concomitant use of topical triamcinolone and clobetasol propionate. With minimal resolution and recurrence after attempting to taper, the prednisone dosage was raised and continued at 17.5 mg given the risks associated with higher dosages of systemic corticosteroids, with plans to taper upon clinical improvement and initiate dupilumab if the patients’ condition worsened or required higher corticosteroid doses. At 35.5 weeks the patient gave birth to a healthy infant, after a suspected placental abruption, with no clinical manifestations of PG. Immediately after delivery, the patient presented with a flare of PG demonstrated by severely pruritic oedematous patches and ruptured bullae coalescing across her torso, back and extremities (Figure 2). Following discussions with the patient’s obstetrician, the newborn infant’s paediatrician and lactation specialist, dupilumab was administered off-label at a 600-mg loading dose at 2 weeks postpartum along with a rapid prednisone taper. Dupilumab was selected based on emerging evidence demonstrating its safety and efficacy in BP,3 recent reports of its successful use in PG,4–7 a favourable side effect profile and its lack of systemic immunosuppression. Three days after the initial dose of dupilumab, the patient reported a significant decrease in pruritus and an improved appearance of lesions while continuing the prednisone taper and minimal use of topical corticosteroids. Thereafter, the patient was treated with dupilumab 300 mg every 2 weeks. She required short courses of steroids at 5 mg with subsequent tapering to help reduce pruritus and development of new lesions but had not required prednisone for the past 6 months. In March 2025, BP180 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay antibody levels were elevated at 115 U mL–1, with BP230 antibody levels within reference range. Repeat antibody levels in May 2025 demonstrated BP180 levels trending downward. At the most recent follow-up, there were multiple hyperpigmented patches with scant residual erythematous patches on the bilateral upper and lower extremities (Figure 3). At the time of this writing, the patient’s disease has been well-controlled on dupilumab maintenance dosing of 300 mg every 2 weeks for approximately 45 weeks. She has not required use of concomitant topical steroids during this time.

Discussion

PG is a rare autoimmune dermatosis of pregnancy in which treatment must be balanced to reduce maternal and fetal adverse outcomes.8 Traditionally, PG has been treated with topical and systemic corticosteroids, antihistamines and systemic immunosuppressive medications. Systemic corticosteroids are the most frequently used treatment for PG, but their long-term use carries the risks of adverse effects to both the mother and fetus to achieve clearance and disease control.8 In patients with recalcitrant or steroid-dependent PG, nonsteroidal treatment alternatives may prove beneficial. Dupilumab is a monoclonal antibody that targets the IL-4Rα, thereby inhibiting IL-4 and IL-13 signalling. Targeting IL-4Rα for treatment of recurrent PG has been hypothesized as a possible therapeutic alternative for PG given successful treatment of BP, as there are notable similarities in the pathophysiology of both conditions. The use of dupilumab to treat BP was first described in 2018,9 and reviews of further cases later described significant clinical efficacy of dupilumab and remission of disease.3 A multicentre case series by Abdat et al. demonstrated disease clearance and satisfactory disease control in 92.3% of patients, as well as tolerance of dupilumab.2 A review by Russo et al. also described widespread tolerance of dupilumab among patients with BP, including the results of three patients with chronic illnesses who experienced no adverse effects.3

The successful use of dupilumab during pregnancy has been documented in patients with atopic dermatitis (AD) without teratogenicity or an increased risk of adverse events.10–13 As a recombinant IgG monoclonal antibody, dupilumab is expected to cross the placenta during the second and third trimesters.14 However, animal studies and systematic reviews have not shown any relationship between dupilumab and toxicity to the fetus or miscarriage.14 In a database review of over 37 000 patients on dupilumab during pregnancy, there was only one reported case of a heterotopic pregnancy, although it was not determined whether dupilumab was the direct cause of this event.12 Initial reports appear to suggest that dupilumab may serve as a safe therapeutic alternative for PG during pregnancy, although additional long-term monitoring and research is required to confirm safety across larger sample sizes.

The treatment of PG with dupilumab was documented in 2021 by Riquelme-Mc Loughlin et al. in a 37-year-old woman at 20 weeks’ gestation (Table 1).4 Following dupilumab initiation at 30 weeks’ gestation with a second dosing at 32 weeks, there was clearance of cutaneous lesions without recurrence or need for additional dosing. Furthermore, the patient’s BP180 antibodies normalized at 3 months postpartum. Three additional cases have been reported,5–7 and, to our knowledge, our case is the fifth report of dupilumab therapy for PG. All patients are reported to have delivered healthy infants.

In the four cases of dupilumab therapy for PG that have been reported in the literature there is no documentation on whether patients attempted to breastfeed their infants while on dupilumab. In the patients reported by Riquelme-Mc Loughlin et al.4 and Liu et al.,5 resolution of symptoms was noted before delivery and no dosing of dupilumab was required during the postpartum period. In the two other published cases, the patients were continued on dupilumab postpartum; however, the published works do not specify whether lactation was attempted.6,7 Our patient was first treated with dupilumab at 2 weeks postpartum and had previously expressed a desire to breastfeed her infant. Following approval from the patient’s providers and consulting the available data, dupilumab was deemed safe to initiate while breastfeeding at 2 weeks postpartum to allow for complete maturation of the infant’s gastrointestinal tract.15–17

Limited data are available on the use of dupilumab during lactation and its presence in human breastmilk.14 Human IgG is also known to be excreted via breastmilk, especially during the first 3 days of lactation when gaps between breast alveolar cells are wide enough to allow immunoglobulin excretion.15 It has therefore been recommended that breastfeeding while on biologics be avoided until after the second week of the infant’s life, and in preterm infants who are more likely to have an immature gastrointestinal tract.15–17 A position paper by the European Task Force on Atopic Dermatitis (ETFAD) favoured the use of ciclosporin, corticosteroids and azathioprine in pregnant and lactating patients with AD over treatment with dupilumab.18 However, case reports of dupilumab therapy in pregnant patients with AD and BP provide a reassuring portrait of usage during breastfeeding.19 A survey to interdisciplinary experts by Deleuran et al. provided consensus that dupilumab is most likely safe during breastfeeding, even if significantly low doses are found in breastmilk.20 The drug is thought to be broken down by the infant’s gastrointestinal tract if ingested via breastmilk, with a low probability of systemic ­absorption.20

Conclusion

Dupilumab is a corticosteroid-sparing therapy that may prove efficacious in cases of steroid-dependent and recalcitrant PG, especially in patients with contraindications to systemic steroid usage. However, there remains a lack of clinical studies investigating dupilumab during pregnancy and lactation, and while data from clinical experiences are reassuring, more research is required to determine the safety profile of dupilumab for PG, specifically during breastfeeding. We presented the case of a 34-year-old woman who was successfully treated with dupilumab for PG starting at 2 weeks postpartum and during lactation without significant adverse effects to the infant. Clinicians must carefully weigh the benefits and risks of dupilumab usage during pregnancy and lactation and it may be utilized when other indicated therapies are contraindicated, unsuccessful or if the patient cannot tolerate them. Treatment decisions should be individualized and made collaboratively with the patient’s care team, weighing the known and potential short- and long-term risks of dupilumab. In past cases, achieving clearance of PG has proven challenging, often necessitating prolonged periods of systemic steroids.21 This difficulty is another reason why dupilumab might by a preferable alternative. Until more robust data are available, it is generally recommended to begin breastfeeding after the first 2 weeks of life in close collaboration with the obstetrician, paediatrician and lactation specialists.16,17 Large-scale multicentre studies are needed to determine the safety and efficacy of dupilumab therapy for PG during lactation and whether dupilumab is systemically absorbed by the infant.
