A constitutive promoter toolbox for tunable gene expression in Acinetobacter baumannii
Authors: Jing Zhang, Daiyu Li, Ning Wang, Yun Shi, Yan Ge, Chuanying Xiang, Xiaomin Zhang, Qiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractConstitutive promoters with defined strengths are essential for gene expression studies in bacteria; however, such tools remain limited in the clinically important pathogen Acinetobacter baumannii. In this study, we developed an eGFP-based reporter system to identify and characterize constitutive promoters in A. baumannii. Candidate promoter regions were selected from highly expressed genes based on transcriptomic data. Among 25 tested promoters, 19 exhibited detectable activity spanning a broad range of expression strengths. Quantitative analyses using RT-qPCR, SDS–PAGE, and western blotting confirmed differential transcriptional and translational outputs. Fluorescence-based measurements further revealed distinct temporal activity patterns of these promoters across growth phases. Based on these results, a set of 19 constitutive expression vectors was constructed, providing a platform for tunable gene expression in A. baumannii. As a proof-of-concept, overexpression of the endogenous gene cluster strAB, encoding aminoglycoside phosphotransferases (APHs), resulted in reduced susceptibility to streptomycin, demonstrating the applicability of this system for functional studies. This work establishes a well-characterized promoter library and a practical expression platform for A. baumannii.Key points• A reporter system that enables systematic identification of promoters in A. baumannii was established.• A diverse promoter library spanning a wide range of expression strengths was identified.• A tunable vector toolkit that enables gene expression in A. baumannii was developed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13894-y.Keywords: Acinetobacter baumannii, Reporter system, Constitutive promoters, Promoter library, Tunable gene expression

Introduction

Acinetobacter baumannii is an opportunistic pathogen associated with a wide range of hospital-acquired infections and is recognized as a major threat due to its rapid acquisition of multidrug resistance (Dijkshoorn et al. 2007). Understanding gene function and regulatory mechanisms in A. baumannii is therefore critical for elucidating its pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance. Efficient genetic tools that enable controlled gene expression are essential for such studies, among which constitutive promoters serve as fundamental elements for driving gene expression in bacterial systems.

Despite their importance, the availability of well-characterized constitutive promoters in A. baumannii remains limited. Most commonly used promoters are derived from heterologous systems or a small number of native sequences (Bacon et al. 2024) (Héritier et al. 2006), often exhibiting restricted expression ranges and lacking systematic evaluation. Moreover, promoter activity may vary under different physiological conditions (Keren et al. 2013), such as distinct bacterial growth phases (Klein et al. 2021; Rolfe et al. 2012), which further complicates the selection of appropriate promoters for specific applications.

Although several constitutive promoters have been reported in A. baumannii (Girlich et al. 2017), their limited diversity and poorly defined expression strengths restrict their utility for precise gene expression. In particular, the ability to fine-tune gene expression is critical when studying genes with potential toxicity. Excessive expression driven by strong promoters may impose a metabolic burden or even lead to growth inhibition, whereas insufficient expression from weak promoters may fail to produce detectable phenotypes, thereby obscuring gene function. Therefore, constitutive promoters spanning a broad range of expression strengths are essential for precise and controllable gene expression in A. baumannii. However, the availability of a comprehensive promoter library with defined and tunable strengths remains limited, which constrains precise and tunable gene expression for functional studies in this organism.

In this study, we aimed to systematically identify and characterize constitutive promoters in A. baumannii using a transcriptomics-guided approach. Candidate promoter regions were selected from highly expressed genes and evaluated using a reporter-based system. We further assessed promoter activity across different growth phases to examine their temporal behavior. Based on these analyses, we established a panel of constitutive promoters spanning a broad range of expression strengths and developed a corresponding set of expression vectors for tunable gene expression. This work provides a useful platform for functional studies in A. baumannii.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Escherichia coli strains were cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth with shaking at 220 rpm or on LB agar plates at 37 °C. A. baumannii strains LAC-4 and ATCC 17978 were grown in tryptic soy broth (TSB) with shaking at 220 rpm or on tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates at 37 °C. Transformation of A. baumannii was performed by electroporation. Transformants of E. coli and A. baumannii were selected on agar plates supplemented with apramycin (50 μg/mL). Antibiotics were included in the growth media as required for plasmid maintenance.

Construction of pQLAB2001 and the promoterless reporter plasmid pQLAB2003

The IPTG-inducible Tac promoter/lac operator region and the original multiple cloning site (MCS) of the E. coli-A. baumannii shuttle vector pMJG120 (Gebhardt et al. 2015) were removed by digestion with HpaI and HindIII. A synthetic DNA fragment containing a RiboJ insulator sequence, a ribosome-binding site (RBS), a start codon, a multiple cloning site (MCS), and a C-terminal 6 × His tag coding sequence (Fig. 1B) was inserted into the linearized vector using the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C115), generating plasmid pQLAB2001. The egfp coding sequence was amplified from plasmid pTH100 (de Jong et al. 2017) by PCR with primers QL1502 and QL1503 and cloned into NdeI- and XbaI-linearized pQLAB2001 to generate the promoterless reporter plasmid pQLAB2003. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing. All primers were listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Identification of candidate promoters

Transcriptomic data for A. baumannii LAC-4 wild type were obtained from our previous study (Zeng et al. 2023). In that study, cells were grown in LB medium by inoculating an overnight culture at a 1:100 dilution into fresh medium and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 4 h prior to RNA extraction. The top fifty genes with the highest expression levels (FPKM) were selected as candidates for promoter identification. Because the precise −35 and −10 elements of these promoters were not experimentally defined, the intergenic region upstream of each gene was used as the putative promoter sequence. When the upstream non-coding region exceeded 250 bp, a 250-bp fragment immediately upstream of the start codon was selected. If the intergenic region was shorter than 250 bp, the sequence spanning from the start codon to the termination codon of the upstream gene was used, with a minimum length of 100 bp. For genes located within one operon, the upstream region of the first gene in the operon was selected as the promoter sequence. Information of the selected promoters

Construction of promoter-reporter plasmids

The selected promoter sequences were amplified by PCR using Q5 High-Fidelity 2 × Master Mix (NEB, M0492L) with A. baumannii LAC-4 genomic DNA as the template and the corresponding primers (Supplementary Table 2). The purified PCR products were cloned into BglII- and SpeI-linearized pQLAB2003 using the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C115), generating a series of promoter-egfp reporter plasmids. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing.

Promoter activity assay

The promoter-egfp reporter plasmids were introduced into A. baumannii LAC-4 WT. Transformants were confirmed by colony PCR. Overnight cultures of each strain were grown in TSB supplemented with apramycin (50 μg/mL) at 37 °C. The cultures were diluted 1:100 in fresh TSB, and 10 μL of the diluted cultures was spotted onto TSA plates and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Fluorescence signals from individual colonies were checked using a Spectral Instruments Imaging System (Kino, USA). A. baumannii LAC-4 WT and the strain carrying the empty pQLAB2003 vector (without promoter) were included as controls.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from A. baumannii reporter strains carrying pQLAB2003 with different promoters at the exponential growth phase using an RNApure Bacteria Kit (CwBIO, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 1 μg of total RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Beijing, China). The resulting cDNA was diluted fivefold, and 2 μL was used as the template in a 15 μL qPCR reaction containing 7.5 μL TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) and 400 nM of each primer. Quantitative PCR was performed under the following conditions: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Primer pairs QL0615/QL0616 and QL1594/QL1595 were used to amplify egfp and 16S rRNA, respectively. The same primer pair was used for egfp quantification across all samples to ensure consistency in target amplification. Gene expression levels were normalized to 16S rRNA as an internal control and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

Western blot analysis

Single colonies from each strain were inoculated into 5 mL TSB supplemented with apramycin (50 μg/mL) and cultured overnight at 37 °C. The cultures were diluted 1:100 into fresh medium and grown at 37 °C with shaking (220 rpm) for 6 h. Cells were harvested at equivalent OD₆₀₀ values by centrifugation and resuspended in 1 × loading buffer, followed by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min. Total protein extracts (20 μg per sample) were separated by 12% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked in TBST buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20) containing 5% (w/v) non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature. For detection of C-terminally His-tagged eGFP, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-His tag antibody (27E8; CST, Danvers, MA, USA) at a dilution of 1:10,000 for 1 h at room temperature. After washing the membranes with TBST three times for 10 min each, signals were developed using Pierce ECL substrate (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and visualized with a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Fluorescence-based analysis of promoter activity across growth phases

Bacterial cultures were prepared by inoculating overnight cultures at a 1:100 dilution into TSB supplemented with apramycin (50 μg/mL), and samples were collected at 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 h to represent the lag, exponential, and stationary phases. Cells (500 μL) were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with 500 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in PBS to an OD₆₀₀ of approximately 0.3. Fluorescence intensity of eGFP (relative light units, RLU) was measured using a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek, USA) with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 512 nm under identical instrument settings. Each assay was performed with three independent biological replicates, and the mean RLU/OD₆₀₀ values were used to generate heatmaps.

Disk diffusion assay

Overnight cultures were grown in TSB at 37 °C and diluted 1:100 into fresh TSB medium, followed by incubation for 2 h. Cultures were adjusted to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.04, mixed with 7 mL TSA soft agar, and overlaid onto TSA plates. After solidification, antibiotic disks (6 mm diameter; Hangzhou Microbial, China) containing kanamycin (30 μg), amikacin (30 μg), gentamicin (30 μg) or streptomycin (50 μg) were placed onto the plates using sterile forceps. Plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight, and inhibition zone diameters were measured.

Results

Construction of the backbone and promoterless eGFP reporter vectors

To construct a reporter system for promoter evaluation in A. baumannii, the E. coli-A. baumannii shuttle vector pMJG120 was used as the parental backbone (Gebhardt et al. 2015) (Fig. 1A). The IPTG-inducible promoter (PTac) and the multiple cloning site (MCS) from the original pMJG120 vector were excised through HpaI and HindIII digestion (Fig. 1B). A synthetic DNA fragment containing a RiboJ insulator sequence, which can buffer promoter output from upstream sequence context and thereby enhance the reliability of downstream expression (Lou et al. 2012), a typical ribosome-binding site (RBS), a start codon, a new MCS, and a C-terminal 6 × His tag coding sequence was then inserted into the linearized vector, generating plasmid pQLAB2001 (Fig. 1B). To generate the promoterless reporter plasmid, the egfp coding sequence was amplified from plasmid pTH100 (de Jong et al. 2017) and cloned into pQLAB2001 between the NdeI and XbaI sites, yielding plasmid pQLAB2003 (Fig. 1C). This construct was subsequently used as the reporter backbone for the cloning and activity evaluation of candidate constitutive promoters in A. baumannii.

Selection and preliminary evaluation of constitutive promoter candidates in A. baumannii

To identify candidate constitutive promoters in A. baumannii, transcriptomic data were analyzed to screen genes with high expression levels (Fig. 2A). Genes with the highest transcript abundance (top 50 based on FPKM values) were selected as potential sources of constitutive promoters (Fig. 2B). In the absence of defined −35 and −10 elements, the upstream intergenic region of each highly expressed gene was used as the putative promoter sequence. When the upstream non-coding region exceeded 250 bp, a 250-bp fragment immediately upstream of the start codon was selected. For genes with shorter upstream intergenic regions, the sequence spanning from the start codon to the termination codon of the upstream gene was used, with a minimum length of 100 bp. Based on these criteria, 25 putative promoter sequences were selected and designated P1 to P25. Detailed information on promoter length, corresponding gene locus, and gene products is summarized in Table 1.

Workflow for the selection and evaluation of constitutive promoter candidates in A. baumannii. A Transcriptomic profiling of A. baumannii was performed to identify genes with high expression levels. B Highly expressed genes above the selection threshold were chosen as candidate sources of constitutive promoters. C The corresponding upstream putative promoter regions were selected and cloned upstream of the promoterless egfp reporter gene in pQLAB2003. D The resulting reporter constructs were introduced into A. baumannii, and promoter activity was assessed by fluorescence imaging of arrayed colonies grown on agar plates

To experimentally evaluate promoter activity, the selected promoter sequences were amplified from A. baumannii LAC-4 genomic DNA and cloned upstream of the promoterless egfp reporter gene in plasmid pQLAB2003 (Fig. 2C). The resulting reporter constructs were introduced into A. baumannii, and promoter activity was preliminarily assessed by fluorescence imaging. Representative fluorescence images showed that multiple promoter constructs produced detectable eGFP signals with varying intensities (Fig. 2D), indicating differences in promoter strength.

Among the 25 promoter candidates tested, 19 drove detectable egfp expression. These active promoters were primarily derived from genes associated with essential cellular functions, including ribosomal components (e.g., 16S rRNA and ribosomal proteins), RNA-related elements (e.g., tmRNA), and other housekeeping processes such as membrane transport, metabolic enzymes, and stress response.These results indicate that a substantial proportion of the selected promoter candidates function as constitutive promoters with relatively different activity levels in A. baumannii.

Quantitative evaluation of promoter activity

To comparatively evaluate the strengths of the selected constitutive promoters, the expression levels of the reporter gene egfp driven by different promoters were assessed at both the transcriptional and protein levels. RT-qPCR analysis showed that, relative to the the promoterless egfp negative control, most promoter constructs significantly increased egfp transcript abundance, whereas little or no upregulation was observed for P6, P8, P9, P16, P21, and P24 (Fig. 3A). These results were generally consistent with the preliminary fluorescence imaging results and further confirmed that the selected promoters exhibited a broad range of transcriptional activities in A. baumannii, with expression levels differing by up to approximately 80-fold, for example between P7 (derived from the inorganic diphosphatase gene) and P2 (derived from the 16S rRNA gene).

Quantitative characterization of constitutive promoter activity in Acinetobacter baumannii. A Relative egfp transcript levels driven by different promoters, determined by RT-qPCR. B SDS-PAGE analysis of eGFP expression in strains carrying different promoter constructs. C Western blot detection of C-terminally His-tagged eGFP using an anti-His tag antibody; the black arrow indicates the position of eGFP. D Heatmap showing fluorescence-based promoter activity of the different constructs across bacterial growth phases

To verify whether differences in transcriptional output were reflected at the protein level, eGFP production was examined by SDS-PAGE and western blotting. A protein band corresponding to recombinant eGFP was detected in multiple promoter constructs, and relatively strong eGFP accumulation was clearly observed in strains carrying strong promoters such as P1, P7, and P17 (Fig. 3B). Consistent with this, western blot analysis using an anti-His tag antibody confirmed the expression of C-terminally His-tagged eGFP in the corresponding strains (Fig. 3C), further supporting the differential expression strengths of the promoter set. Additionally, the trends observed in egfp transcript levels were generally consistent with the corresponding protein expression patterns, supporting the reliability of the promoter activity assessment.

To further characterize promoter activity across growth phases, fluorescence output was monitored in individual strains at different time points, as promoters with different activity levels may be required at distinct bacterial growth stages. Promoter activity was normalized as RLU/OD₆₀₀ and visualized as a heatmap (Fig. 3D). The selected promoters exhibited diverse temporal expression patterns across growth phases. Based on their activity profiles, the promoters could be broadly categorized into distinct groups. One group displayed relatively stable activity across all time points (e.g., P13 and P17), suggesting constitutive expression with minimal growth-phase dependence. In contrast, another group showed increased activity during the exponential phase (e.g., P1, P5, P7, P15, and P25), indicating growth-associated expression.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the selected constitutive promoters span a wide range of relative expression strengths and provide a useful toolkit for graded gene expression in A. baumannii.

Construction of a constitutive overexpression vector set

Following identification of the active promoters, Nineteen promoters with detectable activity in A. baumannii were selected to construct a constitutive overexpression vector set. Each promoter was cloned into the BglII- and SpeI-linearized shuttle backbone pQLAB2001, generating a panel of 19 expression plasmids (pQLAB1001–pQLAB1019) (Fig. 4).

Construction of a constitutive overexpression vector set in A. baumannii. A Schematic representation of the promoter insertion strategy based on pQLAB2001. Active promoter sequences were cloned upstream of the downstream expression cassette. B Map of the resulting constitutive expression vectors (pQLAB1001–pQLAB1019), generated by inserting individual active promoters into pQLAB2001. These vectors share the same shuttle backbone and differ only in the promoter sequence used to drive target gene expression

Importantly, target genes can be readily inserted between the NdeI and XbaI sites, enabling direct expression of recombinant proteins with a C-terminal His tag. This configuration supports rapid cloning and expression of genes of interest with minimal vector modification. All vectors share an identical backbone architecture but differ only in promoter strength, thereby facilitating modular and comparable gene expression. Because the selected promoters span a broad range of expression strengths, this plasmid set provides a flexible platform for tunable and constitutive expression of target genes in A. baumannii.

Functional validation of the expression system

To evaluate the applicability of the constitutive expression vector set for functional analysis of endogenous genes, two representative promoters P1 and P4 were selected to drive overexpression of the endogenous gene cluster RS00565-RS00570 (strA-strB) in A. baumannii LAC4. This gene cluster encodes aminoglycoside phosphotransferases (APHs), which catalyze the phosphorylation of aminoglycoside antibiotics, thereby conferring antibiotic resistance (Costa et al. 2020; Tsai et al. 2013). The antibiotic susceptibility of the resulting strains was assessed using disk-diffusion assays against four aminoglycoside antibiotics, including kanamycin, amikacin, gentamicin, and streptomycin (Fig. 5).

Functional validation of the constitutive expression vector set by overexpression of the strAB gene cluster in A. baumannii. A A. baumannii LAC-4 and B ATCC 17978 strains, including WT, empty vector control, and strains overexpressing strA-strB under promoters P1 or P4, were subjected to disk-diffusion assays. Representative plate images are shown on the left, and inhibition zone diameters are quantified on the right. All strains were tested against four aminoglycoside antibiotics (kanamycin, amikacin, gentamicin, and streptomycin). For streptomycin, inhibition zone diameters are presented as mean ± SD from biological replicates. Reduced inhibition zone diameters indicate decreased antibiotic susceptibility. Streptomycin inhibition zones are highlighted with dashed yellow circles

In the A. baumannii LAC-4 background, strains carrying P1_strA-strB and P4_strA-strB exhibited reduced inhibition zone diameters in response to streptomycin, indicating decreased susceptibility (Fig. 5A). A similar trend was observed in A. baumannii ATCC17978, where overexpression of strA-strB also led to reduced inhibition zones for streptomycin (Fig. 5B). These results were consistent across both strain backgrounds. Together, these results provide proof-of-concept evidence that the constructed expression system enables functional characterization of endogenous genes in A. baumannii.

Discussion

In this study, we established a native constitutive promoter library and a corresponding expression vector set for A. baumannii, addressing a key limitation in the genetic toolkit for this clinically important pathogen. Although shuttle vectors and promoter-probe systems have been developed for Acinetobacter (Gebhardt et al. 2020; Gebhardt and Shuman 2017), the availability of a systematically characterized promoter set with defined and tunable strengths remains limited, particularly within a standardized experimental framework. By combining transcriptomics-guided selection with multi-level validation, this work provides a systematic framework for identifying promoters with defined expression outputs.

The use of a standardized reporter platform based on the pMJG120 backbone (Gebhardt et al. 2015) represents a key feature of this study. The incorporation of a synthetic cassette containing a RiboJ insulator (Lou et al. 2012), a defined ribosome-binding site, and a modular cloning site is expected to reduce context-dependent effects and improve the comparability of promoter activity. Together with the promoterless egfp reporter, this system enables both qualitative and quantitative assessment. Consistent results across fluorescence, RT-qPCR, and protein-level analyses further support the robustness of the promoter characterization.

The transcriptomics-guided strategy yielded 19 functional promoters spanning a broad range of expression strengths, enabling graded control of gene expression in A. baumannii. To further improve the portability and practical applicability of the promoter toolbox, we performed in silico prediction of putative −35 and −10 promoter elements using the BPROM algorithm (SoftBerry) and generated truncated minimal promoter variants containing the predicted core promoter regions. Functional characterization of representative minimal promoter variants demonstrated that most retained transcriptional activities comparable to those of their corresponding full-length promoters in our tested promoters (Fig. S1), although moderate activity changes were observed for certain promoters. These findings indicate that the predicted core promoter motifs are sufficient to support promoter function independently of extended upstream regions, while additional surrounding sequences may contribute to fine-tuning promoter strength under specific conditions. Therefore, for applications aiming to minimize excessive endogenous regulation and achieve more stable expression outputs, the experimentally validated minimal promoter variants described here may represent particularly useful candidates.

The resulting set of expression vectors, which differ only in promoter sequences, provides a practical platform for tunable gene expression in A. baumannii. Functional validation using the endogenous gene cluster strA_strB demonstrated reduced susceptibility to streptomycin, confirming that the system can modulate biologically relevant phenotypes across different strain backgrounds. In this study, selected promoters were shown to function in both the LAC-4 and ATCC 17978 strains, suggesting a degree of cross-strain compatibility. However, we acknowledge that promoter activity may vary across more diverse clinical isolates. Therefore, broader validation across additional strains will be important to further assess the general applicability of this promoter library.

Functional validation of the expression system using the endogenous aminoglycoside resistance gene cluster strA-strB revealed a substrate-specific resistance phenotype. Although all antibiotics tested belong to the aminoglycoside class, increased resistance was observed predominantly for streptomycin, whereas susceptibility to other aminoglycosides remained largely unchanged. This selective effect is consistent with the known biochemical properties of aminoglycoside phosphotransferases (APHs), which exhibit distinct substrate specificities (Garneau-Tsodikova and Labby 2016). The enzymes encoded by strA-strB, corresponding to APH(3'')-Ib and APH(6)-Id, are well-established determinants of streptomycin resistance and show limited activity toward other aminoglycosides such as kanamycin, amikacin, and gentamicin. The observed specificity likely arises from structural differences among aminoglycosides that influence enzyme recognition and phosphorylation efficiency.

In this study, promoter activity was evaluated under commonly used laboratory growth conditions, including standard culture media and incubation parameters. While we observed distinct temporal expression patterns across different growth phases, additional factors such as temperature, nutrient availability, and other environmental variables were not systematically assessed. Therefore, the activity of these promoters may vary under specific conditions, and their performance may need to be re-evaluated depending on the experimental context. Future studies examining promoter behavior under diverse environmental conditions will further enhance the applicability of this promoter library.

In summary, this study provides a well-characterized promoter library and a versatile expression platform for A. baumannii, enabling tunable gene expression and facilitating functional studies of genes involved in antimicrobial resistance and other biological processes.
