A Factorization Identity for Twisted Multinomial Coefficients with Application to Pilot States in Hamiltonian Decoded Quantum Interferometry
Paweł Wocjan

Abstract
The q q -multinomial coefficient, a classical object in enumerative combinatorics, counts permutations of multisets weighted by the number of inversions, with a single deformation parameter q q . We introduce the twisted multinomial coefficient , in which each inversion between letters i i and j j carries a pair-dependent weight ω i ​ j \omega_{ij} determined by a skew-symmetric matrix Ω \Omega . In general, no closed-form evaluation is known. Our main result is that under a natural structural condition on Ω \Omega — predecessor-uniformity ( ω i ​ j = q j \omega_{ij}=q_{j} for all i < j i<j ) — the twisted multinomial factorizes as a product of Gaussian ( q q -deformed) binomials with site-dependent parameters: ( k k 1 , … , k m ) Ω = ∏ j ( ℓ j k j ) q j \binom{k}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}_{\Omega}=\prod_{j}\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}}_{q_{j}} where ℓ j = k 1 + ⋯ + k j \ell_{j}=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{j} . This extends the standard product formula for the q q -multinomial from a single parameter q q to m − 1 m-1 independent parameters. The identity is purely combinatorial: it holds for arbitrary q j ∈ ℂ ∖ { 0 } q_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\} without any algebraic constraints. We were led to this identity by studying pilot state preparation in Hamiltonian Decoded Quantum Interferometry (HDQI), a recently proposed quantum algorithm for preparing Gibbs and ground states. As an application, we show that the factorization yields an exact matrix product state (MPS) of bond dimension k + 1 k+1 for the expansion coefficients of h k h^{k} in a twisted algebra. We further show that the same site matrices deliver an exact MPS of bond dimension deg ⁡ ( 𝒫 ) + 1 \deg(\mathcal{P})+1 for the expansion coefficients of 𝒫 ⁡ ( h ) \mathcal{P}(h) , for any polynomial 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , via a polynomial-dependent right boundary vector. We note that this addresses only one component of the HDQI pipeline (pilot state preparation); the full protocol additionally requires efficient decoding of the associated Hamiltonian code, and both components must work in conjunction for Hamiltonians of physical interest. Identifying such Hamiltonians remains an important open problem.

Introduction

1.1
The Combinatorial Problem

The
q
-multinomial coefficient

\displaystyle\binom{k}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}_{q}=\frac{[k]_{q}!}{[k_{1}]_{q}!\cdots[k_{m}]_{q}!},\qquad[s]_{q}=1+q+\cdots+q^{s-1},\quad[s]_{q}!=[1]_{q}[2]_{q}\cdots[s]_{q},

(1)

is a classical object in enumerative combinatorics: it counts
permutations of the multiset
\{1^{k_{1}},2^{k_{2}},\ldots,m^{k_{m}}\}

weighted by
q
raised to the number of
inversions
[
4
, Section 1.3]

(see also
[
5
]
for a detailed treatment of inversion
statistics).
It factorizes as a product of Gaussian binomial coefficients,

\displaystyle\binom{k}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}_{q}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}}_{q},\qquad\ell_{j}=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{j},

(2)

each factor corresponding to the interleaving of letter
j
into the
already-placed letters
1,\ldots,j{-}1
. In this classical setting,
every inversion contributes the same weight
q
, regardless of which pair
of letters is involved.
The Gaussian binomial
\binom{n}{r}_{q}
is defined in full generality
in Definition
3.10
.

In this paper we introduce and study a generalization in which inversions
carry
pair-dependent weights
. Given a weight matrix

\Omega=(\omega_{ij})
with
\omega_{ii}=1
and

\omega_{ji}=\omega_{ij}^{-1}
for
i<j
, the
twisted
multinomial coefficient
is defined as

\displaystyle\binom{k}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}_{\Omega}:=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sh}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}r<s\\
\sigma(r)>\sigma(s)\end{subarray}}\omega_{\sigma(s),\sigma(r)},

(3)

where
\mathrm{Sh}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})
denotes the set of all shuffles
(i.e. permutations of the multiset
\{1^{k_{1}},\ldots,m^{k_{m}}\}
),
the product runs over all inversions (pairs of positions
r<s
with

\sigma(r)>\sigma(s)
), and each inversion
contributes
\omega_{\sigma(s),\sigma(r)}
with

\sigma(s)<\sigma(r)
.
When
\omega_{ij}=q
for all
i<j
, this reduces to the standard

q
-multinomial.

In general, no clean factorization of (
3
) is available:
the pair-dependence of the weights couples different stages of the
interleaving process. Our main result
(Proposition
3.11
) identifies a structural condition
on
\Omega
—
predecessor-uniformity
— under which the
factorization (
2
) extends to the
multi-parameter setting:

\displaystyle\binom{k}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}_{\Omega}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}}_{q_{j}},

(4)

with a
different
deformation parameter
q_{j}
for each factor.
Predecessor-uniformity means that
\omega_{ij}=q_{j}
for all

i<j
: each letter
j
has a uniform weight with respect to all
earlier letters. The identity (
4
) is
purely combinatorial: it holds for arbitrary

q_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and does not require any algebraic
relations.

To our knowledge, this multi-parameter factorization has not appeared
previously in the combinatorics literature; the standard

q
-multinomial is the special case
q_{j}=q
for all
j
.
We also give an
O(m^{3})
greedy algorithm that determines whether a
given weight matrix
\Omega
admits a predecessor-uniform ordering
(Remark
3.8
).

1.2
Motivation from Quantum Computing

We were led to the twisted multinomial by studying a problem in quantum
computing: the preparation of
pilot states
in Hamiltonian Decoded
Quantum Interferometry (HDQI), a recently proposed quantum algorithm for
preparing Gibbs states and ground states of many-body
Hamiltonians
[
2
,
1
]
.

Consider an
n
-qubit Pauli Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}P_{j}
with
P_{j}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
.
The commutation phases
P_{i}P_{j}=\omega_{ij}P_{j}P_{i}
define a twisting
matrix
\Omega=(\omega_{ij})
with
\omega_{ij}\in\{+1,-1\}
.
The HDQI protocol requires the expansion of
H^{k}
in the generator basis:

\displaystyle H^{k}=\sum_{r\in\{0,1\}^{m}}\alpha_{r}\,P_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots P_{m}^{r_{m}},

(5)

and the coefficients
\alpha_{r}
— the
pilot state amplitudes
—
are precisely sums of twisted multinomial coefficients weighted by
products of the Hamiltonian’s coefficients
c_{j}

(equation (
19
)). It suffices to consider the
monomial
\mathcal{P}(x)=x^{k}
: a general polynomial of degree at most
k

is handled by the
same
site matrices at bond dimension
k+1

with a polynomial-dependent right boundary vector
(Proposition
3.16
).

The factorization (
4
) implies that the

\alpha_{r}
admit an exact
matrix product state
(MPS) of bond
dimension
k+1
(Theorem
3.14
), enabling efficient
computation of the pilot state amplitudes. In contrast, the existing
method of
[
2
,
1
]
decomposes the computation along
connected components of the
anticommutation graph
(the graph on

\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}\}
with an edge whenever two generators anticommute)
and has cost exponential in the size of the largest component. We show
(Section
4
) that predecessor-uniform twistings can
have an anticommutation graph that is a single connected component of
size
m
, giving an exponential separation
for the pilot state
computation
.

The HDQI protocol involves two further steps beyond pilot state
preparation: syndrome decoding and a coherent Bell
measurement
[
2
,
1
]
. In this paper we focus
exclusively on the combinatorial and algebraic aspects of the pilot
state. The question of which physically interesting Hamiltonians give
rise to predecessor-uniform twistings while also admitting efficient
decoding remains an important open problem.

Organisation.

Section
2
treats the commutative baseline.
Section
3
is the heart of the paper: it defines the
twisted multinomial coefficient, introduces predecessor-uniformity,
proves the multi-parameter factorization into Gaussian binomials
(Proposition
3.11
), derives the MPS
(Theorem
3.14
), and specializes to Pauli and qudit
settings.
Section
4
compares with the connected-component
method and demonstrates an exponential separation in pilot state

Section
5
discusses limitations and open problems.
Appendix
A
gives an alternative induction proof of the
twisted multinomial theorem.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{m}]\big/\bigl(z_{j}^{2}-1,\;z_{i}z_{j}-z_{j}z_{i}\bigr).

(6)

The generators commute and square to
1

\mathcal{A}
has dimension
2^{m}

with basis
\{z_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots z_{m}^{r_{m}}:r_{j}\in\{0,1\}\}

. We expand

\mathcal{P}(h)=h^{k}
and seek an efficient representation of the amplitudes
\alpha_{r}

appearing in
h^{k}=\sum_{r\in\{0,1\}^{m}}\alpha_{r}\,z_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots z_{m}^{r_{m}}
.
This corresponds to the Pauli Hamiltonian case in which all generators
P_{j}

pairwise commute, i.e.
\omega_{ij}=1
for all

. The coefficients

\alpha_{r}
are the pilot-state amplitudes studied in Section 3 of
[
1
]

for commuting Hamiltonians; Theorem

recovers their MPS

Theorem 2.1

(Commutative MPS)
.

The coefficient tensor
\alpha_{r}
admits an exact MPS representation of bond
dimension
k+1
and physical dimension
2
. Define matrices

A^{[j]}_{r_{j}}\in\mathbb{C}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}
by

\displaystyle\bigl(A^{[j]}_{r_{j}}\bigr)_{\ell_{j-1},\ell_{j}}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{c_{j}^{\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}}}{(\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1})!}&\ell_{j}\geq\ell_{j-1}\text{ and }\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}\equiv r_{j}\pmod{2},\\[6.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(7)

and boundary vectors
v_{0},v_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{k+1}
by

\displaystyle v_{0}=(1,0,\ldots,0),\qquad v_{m}=(0,\ldots,0,1).

(8)

Then
\alpha_{r}=k!\cdot v_{0}\bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{m}A^{[j]}_{r_{j}}\bigr)v_{m}^{\top}
.

Proof.

By the multinomial theorem,

\displaystyle h^{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\geq 0\\
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m}=k\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}^{k_{j}}z_{j}^{k_{j}},

(9)

where
\binom{k}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}=\frac{k!}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{m}!}
is the
multinomial coefficient.
Since
z_{j}^{2}=1
,
z_{j}^{k_{j}}=z_{j}^{k_{j}\bmod 2}
, giving

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m}=k\\
k_{j}\equiv r_{j}\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}^{k_{j}}.

(10)

Set
\ell_{0}=0
and
\ell_{j}=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
The multinomial coefficient factorizes as

\displaystyle\binom{k}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}=\frac{k!}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{m}!}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}},

(11)

where the equality holds because

\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}}=\frac{\ell_{j}!}{k_{j}!\,\ell_{j-1}!}
and the

\prod_{j}\frac{\ell_{j}!}{k_{j}!\,\ell_{j-1}!}=\frac{\ell_{m}!}{\ell_{0}!\prod_{j}k_{j}!}=\frac{k!}{\prod_{j}k_{j}!}
.

of Section
3

Substituting,

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}=k!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0=\ell_{0}\leq\cdots\leq\ell_{m}=k\\
\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}\equiv r_{j}\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{c_{j}^{\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}}}{(\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1})!}.

(12)

The summand is a product of nearest-neighbour factors in
\ell_{j}

giving the MPS with bond dimension
k+1
.
∎

A single

The Twisted Setting

3.1
Twisted Algebra

Definition 3.1

(Single-order twisted algebra)
.

Let
a\geq 2
be an integer (the
generator order
) and let

\Omega=(\omega_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}
be a matrix of nonzero complex
numbers satisfying

\displaystyle\omega_{ii}=1\quad\text{for all }i,\qquad\omega_{ji}=\omega_{ij}^{-1}\quad\text{for }i<j,\qquad\omega_{ij}^{a}=1\quad\text{for all }i,j.

(13)

The
single-order twisted algebra
(the free associative algebra
modulo the relations) is the quotient

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}=\mathbb{C}\langle z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\rangle\Big/\bigl(z_{j}^{a}-1,\;z_{j}z_{i}-\omega_{ij}z_{i}z_{j}\bigr).

(14)

Here
\mathbb{C}\langle z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\rangle
denotes the free associative algebra over
\mathbb{C}
.
Imposing the lexicographic ordering
z_{1}<\cdots<z_{m}
, every element
reduces to a unique linear combination of ordered monomials

\{z_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots z_{m}^{r_{m}}:r_{j}\in\{0,1,\ldots,a-1\}\}
, giving an

a^{m}
-dimensional basis.

The defining relation
z_{j}z_{i}=\omega_{ij}\,z_{i}z_{j}
(for
j>i
)
states that moving a later generator
z_{j}
to the left of an earlier
generator
z_{i}
introduces the phase
\omega_{ij}
.

Remark 3.2

(Consistency of the constraints)
.

The third condition in (
13
) is a consequence of
the algebra. From
z_{j}z_{i}=\omega_{ij}\,z_{i}z_{j}
and
z_{i}^{a}=1
,
we get
z_{j}=z_{j}z_{i}^{a}=\omega_{ij}^{a}\,z_{i}^{a}z_{j}=\omega_{ij}^{a}\,z_{j}
,
hence
\omega_{ij}^{a}=1
: the commutation phases must be
a
-th
roots of unity, i.e.
\omega_{ij}\in\mu_{a}:=\{e^{2\pi ik/a}:k=0,\ldots,a-1\}
.

Remark 3.3

(Combinatorial versus algebraic constraints)
.

For the application to
qubit
Pauli Hamiltonians,
a=2
and the
commutation phase between two
n
-qubit Paulis
P_{i}
and
P_{j}
, encoded as

(u_{j}\mid w_{j})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
with
P=i^{\kappa}X^{u}Z^{w}
, is

\displaystyle P_{i}P_{j}=(-1)^{\lambda_{ij}}P_{j}P_{i},\qquad\lambda_{ij}=u_{i}\cdot w_{j}+w_{i}\cdot u_{j}\pmod{2},

(15)

so
\omega_{ij}=(-1)^{\lambda_{ij}}\in\mu_{2}=\{+1,-1\}
.
For
qudit
Hamiltonians on systems of prime local dimension
d
, the
generalized Pauli (Heisenberg–Weyl) operators satisfy
a=d
with

\omega_{ij}\in\mu_{d}

(see Section
3.5
for the role of primality).

It is important to distinguish between the
combinatorial
and

algebraic
layers of the construction. The twisted multinomial
coefficient (Definition
3.4
) and its
factorization (Proposition
3.11
) are purely
combinatorial objects: they involve only the weights
\omega_{ij}
and
are well-defined for arbitrary

\omega_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
satisfying

\omega_{ii}=1
and
\omega_{ji}=\omega_{ij}^{-1}
for
i<j
,
without requiring
\omega_{ij}^{a}=1
or any algebraic relation

z_{j}^{a}=1
. The algebraic constraint
\omega_{ij}^{a}=1
enters
only when one
interprets
the amplitudes
\alpha_{r}
as
coefficients of
h^{k}
in the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
, since the
reduction
z_{j}^{k_{j}}=z_{j}^{k_{j}\bmod a}
requires
z_{j}^{a}=1
.
We will return to this distinction in
Remark
3.13
.

Definition 3.4

(Shuffles and twisted multinomial coefficient)
.

Let
k=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m}
.
A
(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})
-
shuffle
is a function

\sigma\colon\{1,\ldots,k\}\to\{1,\ldots,m\}
with

|\sigma^{-1}(j)|=k_{j}
for each
j
; equivalently, it is a word of
length
k
in the alphabet
\{1,\dots,m\}
with letter
j
appearing
exactly
k_{j}
times. Here the domain
\{1,\ldots,k\}
indexes
positions and the range
\{1,\ldots,m\}
indexes letter types.
We write
\mathrm{Sh}(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})
for the set of all such shuffles.

An
inversion
is a pair of positions
(r,s)
with
r<s
and

\sigma(r)>\sigma(s)
; it carries the weight

\omega_{\sigma(s),\sigma(r)}
(note
\sigma(s)<\sigma(r)
).
This weight arises from the defining relation: reordering

z_{\sigma(r)}z_{\sigma(s)}=\omega_{\sigma(s),\sigma(r)}\,z_{\sigma(s)}z_{\sigma(r)}
when
\sigma(r)>\sigma(s)
.
The
twisted multinomial coefficient
is

\displaystyle\binom{k}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\Omega}:=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sh}(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}r<s\\
\sigma(r)>\sigma(s)\end{subarray}}\omega_{\sigma(s),\sigma(r)}.

(16)

The following theorem shows that the twisted multinomial coefficient
plays the same role in the twisted algebra that the ordinary multinomial
coefficient plays in the commutative setting. The expansion formula
appears implicitly in
[
2
]
; we state it here as a
standalone result for completeness and because the twisted multinomial
coefficient itself is the combinatorial object of independent interest.

Theorem 3.5

(Twisted multinomial theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
be a single-order twisted algebra and set

h=\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}z_{j}
with
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then for every integer

k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle h^{k}=\sum_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m}=k}\binom{k}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\Omega}\prod_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}^{k_{j}}\,z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{m}^{k_{m}}.

(17)

Proof.

Expand
h^{k}=\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}z_{j}\bigr)^{k}
by distributing the
product. Each term in the expansion corresponds to a choice of one
generator from each of the
k
factors, yielding a word

z_{\sigma(1)}z_{\sigma(2)}\cdots z_{\sigma(k)}
for some function

\sigma\colon\{1,\ldots,k\}\to\{1,\ldots,m\}
, with coefficient

c_{\sigma(1)}c_{\sigma(2)}\cdots c_{\sigma(k)}
. We group these

m^{k}
terms by the composition
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})
with

k_{j}=|\sigma^{-1}(j)|
. Within each group, the coefficient is

\prod_{j}c_{j}^{k_{j}}
and the monomial is the disordered product

z_{\sigma(1)}\cdots z_{\sigma(k)}
, which we must rewrite in canonical
form
z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{m}^{k_{m}}
.

To do so, apply pairwise transpositions using the relation

z_{\sigma(r)}z_{\sigma(s)}=\omega_{\sigma(s),\sigma(r)}\,z_{\sigma(s)}z_{\sigma(r)}
whenever
\sigma(r)>\sigma(s)
and

r<s
, i.e. whenever positions
(r,s)
form an inversion. Each
such transposition contributes the phase
\omega_{\sigma(s),\sigma(r)}
.
Since
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
is a well-defined quotient algebra (Definition
3.1
),
the defining relations are confluent: the canonical form and the total
accumulated phase are independent of the order in which the transpositions
are performed. The total phase for the word
\sigma
is therefore

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}r<s\\
\sigma(r)>\sigma(s)\end{subarray}}\omega_{\sigma(s),\sigma(r)},

(18)

which is precisely the inversion weight from
Definition
3.4
. Summing over all words with
composition
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})
— that is, over all shuffles

\sigma\in\mathrm{Sh}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})
— gives the twisted multinomial
coefficient as the coefficient of

\prod_{j}c_{j}^{k_{j}}\,z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{m}^{k_{m}}
.
An alternative proof by induction on
k
is given in
Appendix
A
.
∎

Reducing
z_{j}^{k_{j}}=z_{j}^{r_{j}}
with
r_{j}=k_{j}\bmod a
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m}=k\\
k_{j}\equiv r_{j}\pmod{a}\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\Omega}\prod_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}^{k_{j}},\qquad r\in\{0,1,\ldots,a-1\}^{m}.

(19)

3.2
Predecessor-Uniform Twisting and Gaussian Binomial Factorization

Definition 3.6

(Predecessor-uniform twisting)
.

A twisting matrix
\Omega
satisfying (
13
) is

predecessor-uniform
if for each
j\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
there exists

q_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\omega_{ij}=q_{j}
for all

i<j
. (We set
q_{1}=1
by convention, since the first generator has no
predecessors.)

Remark 3.7

(Terminology and ordering dependence)
.

The name “predecessor-uniform” reflects the defining property:
each generator
z_{j}
has a uniform commutation parameter with all its
predecessors
z_{1},\ldots,z_{j-1}
in the chosen ordering. In the
qubit case (
a=2
),
z_{j}
either commutes with all predecessors
(
q_{j}=+1
) or anticommutes with all of them (
q_{j}=-1
);
for
a>2
, the generalized commutation phase
q_{j}
must be the
same
a
-th root of unity with respect to every predecessor.

Predecessor-uniformity depends on the chosen ordering

z_{1}<\cdots<z_{m}
of the generators: a twisted algebra that is not
predecessor-uniform under one ordering may become so under another.
Given
\Omega
, one should seek a permutation
\pi\in S_{m}
such that

\omega_{\pi(i),\pi(j)}
depends only on
\pi(j)
for all

\pi(i)<\pi(j)
. Not all twisting matrices admit such a permutation
(see Remark
3.9
).

Remark 3.8

(Efficient recognition of predecessor-uniform twistings)
.

Whether a twisting matrix
\Omega
admits a predecessor-uniform ordering
can be decided in
O(m^{3})
time by the following greedy peeling
algorithm. Build the permutation from the
last
position
backwards: at each step, find a generator
j
in the remaining set
S

whose commutation parameter
\omega_{ij}
is constant over all

i\in S\setminus\{j\}
; place
j
last among
S
and remove it.
If no such
j
exists, no predecessor-uniform ordering is possible.

Correctness.
If a predecessor-uniform ordering
\pi
of a
set
S
exists, then restricting
\pi
to any subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S

(preserving relative order) is again predecessor-uniform, because
restriction only shrinks predecessor sets and a constant function
remains constant on subsets. In particular, the last element of
\pi

is uniform with respect to all of
S\setminus\{\pi(|S|)\}
, so the
greedy algorithm always finds a valid candidate. After removing it,
the restriction gives a valid ordering of the remainder. By induction,
the algorithm produces a predecessor-uniform ordering whenever one
exists.

Complexity.
Each of the
m
iterations considers at most

|S|
candidates, each requiring
O(|S|)
uniformity checks, giving

O(m^{3})
total.

Remark 3.9

(Failure for general
\Omega
)
.

Not all twisting matrices admit a predecessor-uniform ordering.
For
m=3
generators with
a=2
,
every
twisting matrix
admits a predecessor-uniform ordering: by the pigeonhole principle,
among
\omega_{1j},\omega_{2j},\omega_{3j}\in\{+1,-1\}
for any
fixed
j
, at least two of the three generators share the same
commutation sign with
j
, and a suitable ordering can always be found.

Counterexample (
m=4
,
a=2
).
Take
m=4
generators with

\omega_{12}=-1
,
\omega_{13}=+1
,
\omega_{14}=-1
,

\omega_{23}=-1
,
\omega_{24}=+1
,
\omega_{34}=-1
.
In this configuration, each generator commutes with exactly one of the
other three and anticommutes with the remaining two. For any
generator
j
, its commutation signs with the other three form a

\{+1,-1,-1\}
or
\{-1,+1,+1\}
pattern (never all equal), so no
generator can be placed last in a predecessor-uniform ordering. The
greedy peeling algorithm (Remark
3.8
) immediately
fails, and one can verify by exhaustive search that none of the
24

orderings satisfies Definition
3.6
.

A concrete realization on
n=2
qubits is
P_{1}=I\otimes X
,

P_{2}=I\otimes Y
,
P_{3}=X\otimes X
,
P_{4}=X\otimes Y
;
one verifies that the anticommutation graph is the
4
-cycle

P_{1}\text{--}P_{2}\text{--}P_{3}\text{--}P_{4}\text{--}P_{1}
with
commuting diagonals
(P_{1},P_{3})
and
(P_{2},P_{4})
. Two qubits are
necessary: on a single qubit only three non-identity Paulis exist.

The factorization of the twisted multinomial under predecessor-uniformity
involves Gaussian (
q
-deformed) binomial coefficients, which we now
define.

Definition 3.10

(Gaussian binomial)
.

For
q\in\mathbb{C}
and integers
n\geq r\geq 0
, the
Gaussian
binomial coefficient
(or
q
-binomial coefficient) is defined as

\displaystyle\binom{n}{r}_{q}:=\sum_{\lambda\subseteq r\times(n-r)}q^{|\lambda|}.

(20)

Here the sum runs over all
integer partitions

\lambda
that fit
inside an
r\times(n-r)
rectangular box. A partition

\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}\geq 0)

fits in this box if it has at most
r
parts, each of size at most
n-r
,
i.e.
\lambda_{1}\leq n-r
. Its
size
is

|\lambda|=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{r}
.

Examples.

\binom{2}{1}_{q}=1+q
(partitions fitting in a
1\times 1
box:

\lambda=\varnothing
with
|\lambda|=0
and
\lambda=(1)
with
|\lambda|=1
).

\binom{3}{2}_{q}=1+q+q^{2}
(partitions fitting in a
2\times 1
box:

\lambda=\varnothing,(1),(1{,}1)
with sizes
0,1,2
).

\binom{4}{2}_{q}=1+q+2q^{2}+q^{3}+q^{4}
(partitions fitting in a

2\times 2
box:
\lambda=\varnothing,(1),(2),(1{,}1),(2{,}1),(2{,}2)

with sizes
0,1,2,2,3,4
).

The expression (
20
) is manifestly a polynomial
in
q
with non-negative integer coefficients, and is therefore well-defined
for all
q\in\mathbb{C}
, including roots of unity.

For
q
not a root of unity, the Gaussian binomial admits the equivalent
representations

\displaystyle\binom{n}{r}_{q}=\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[r]_{q}!\,[n-r]_{q}!}=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{1-q^{n-i}}{1-q^{i+1}},

(21)

where
[s]_{q}=1+q+\cdots+q^{s-1}
is the
q
-integer and

[s]_{q}!=[1]_{q}[2]_{q}\cdots[s]_{q}
. At roots of unity these product
formulas can involve
0/0
indeterminate forms, so we always take
(
20
) as the primary definition.

The Gaussian binomial satisfies the
q
-Pascal recurrence

\displaystyle\binom{n}{r}_{q}=\binom{n-1}{r-1}_{q}+q^{r}\binom{n-1}{r}_{q},

(22)

with boundary values
\binom{n}{0}_{q}=1
for all
n\geq 0

and
\binom{n}{r}_{q}=0
for
r>n\geq 0
.
These are the same conventions as for the ordinary binomial
coefficients:
\binom{n}{0}=1
and
\binom{n}{r}=0
when
r>n
.
In particular,
\binom{0}{0}_{q}=1
and
\binom{0}{r}_{q}=0
for

r\geq 1
.
The recurrence (
22
) is well-defined for all
q
and
computes
\binom{n}{r}_{q}
in
O(nr)
operations, without the need for
the product formula (
21
).

We can now state the main factorization result. In the
single-parameter case (
q_{j}=q
for all
j
), the factorization
below reduces to the well-known product formula for the
q
-multinomial
coefficient; the combinatorial background can be found in Stanley’s
treatment of permutations of multisets
[
4
, Section 1.3, “Permutations
of Multisets”, pp. 25–30]
. Our contribution is the
extension from a single parameter
q
to site-dependent
parameters
q_{j}
, which is precisely what predecessor-uniformity
provides.

Proposition 3.11

(Factorization of the twisted multinomial)
.

Under predecessor-uniform twisting with parameters
q_{j}
,

\displaystyle\binom{k}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\Omega}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}}_{q_{j}},

(23)

where
\ell_{j}=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{j}
and
\binom{n}{r}_{q}
is the Gaussian
binomial (Definition
3.10
).

Proof.

We prove the identity by building each shuffle as a sequence of
interleavings and showing that the total phase decomposes into
independent contributions, one per stage.

Step 1: Sequential construction of shuffles.
Every

(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})
-shuffle can be constructed in
m
stages. At
stage
j=1,\ldots,m
, insert
k_{j}
copies of letter
j
into the word
of length
\ell_{j-1}=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{j-1}
produced by the preceding
stages. After stage
j
, the word has length
\ell_{j}=\ell_{j-1}+k_{j}
.
Every shuffle arises exactly once from this procedure, and the inversions
in the final word decompose into those created at each stage.

Step 2: Phase per insertion (predecessor-uniformity).
At
stage
j
, a single copy of
z_{j}
is placed into the existing word.
Suppose it lands with
t
existing letters to its right. Each of these

t
letters carries some
z_{i}
with
i<j
, creating an inversion at
position pair
(z_{j},z_{i})
. The associated phase is

\omega_{ij}
(the weight for the pair
i<j
). By
predecessor-uniformity,
\omega_{ij}=q_{j}
for every
i<j
, so
the phase is
q_{j}
regardless of which predecessor
z_{i}
is involved.
The
t
inversions therefore contribute a combined phase of
q_{j}^{t}
.

This is the crucial step: without predecessor-uniformity, the phase would
depend on the identities
z_{i_{1}},\ldots,z_{i_{t}}
of the letters to the
right, not just their count
t
, and the factorization would fail (cf. Remark
3.9
).

Step 3: Interleaving sum at stage
j
.
At stage
j
, we
insert
k_{j}
copies of
z_{j}
into a word of length
\ell_{j-1}
. The

u
-th copy (
u=1,\ldots,k_{j}
) lands with
t_{u}
existing letters to its
right. Since the copies of
z_{j}
are identical, we may assume they are
inserted from right to left without loss of generality, giving a weakly
decreasing sequence

\ell_{j-1}\geq t_{1}\geq t_{2}\geq\cdots\geq t_{k_{j}}\geq 0
.
Equivalently, the
k_{j}
insertion positions, read in non-increasing order,
form a partition
\lambda=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k_{j}})
fitting inside a

k_{j}\times\ell_{j-1}
box.

From Step 2, the total phase contributed at stage
j
is

q_{j}^{t_{1}+t_{2}+\cdots+t_{k_{j}}}=q_{j}^{|\lambda|}
.
Summing over all valid partitions:

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\subseteq k_{j}\times\ell_{j-1}}q_{j}^{\,|\lambda|}=\binom{\ell_{j-1}+k_{j}}{k_{j}}_{q_{j}}=\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}}_{q_{j}},

(24)

which is precisely the Gaussian binomial
(Definition
3.10
), since the sum runs over
partitions with at most
k_{j}
parts each of size at most
\ell_{j-1}
,
weighted by
q_{j}^{|\lambda|}
.

Step 4: Independence across stages.
The total phase of a
shuffle is the product of phases from each stage. Crucially, the
interleaving sum at stage
j
(equation (
24
))
depends on the preceding stages only through the total count

\ell_{j-1}=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{j-1}
, not on the identities of the

\ell_{j-1}
letters already placed. This is a direct consequence of
predecessor-uniformity (Step 2): all predecessors contribute the same
phase
q_{j}
, so only their count matters. Therefore the contributions at
different stages are independent and multiply:

\displaystyle\binom{k}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\Omega}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}}_{q_{j}}.

∎

Remark 3.12

(Commutative limit)
.

At
q_{j}=1
for all
j
:
\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}}_{1}=\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}}
, and

\prod_{j}\binom{\ell_{j}}{k_{j}}
telescopes to
k!/(k_{1}!\cdots k_{m}!)

as in (
11
),
recovering the commutative factorization.

Remark 3.13

(Generality of the factorization)
.

As noted in Remark
3.3
,
Proposition
3.11
belongs to the combinatorial
layer: it holds for all
q_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
with no
requirement that
q_{j}^{a}=1
or that any algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
exists.
The algebraic constraint
q_{j}\in\mu_{a}
is needed only when one
wishes to interpret the resulting amplitudes
\alpha_{r}
as expansion
coefficients in a twisted algebra with
z_{j}^{a}=1
.

3.3
MPS Representation

Theorem 3.14

(MPS for single-order predecessor-uniform twisted algebras)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
be a single-order twisted algebra with generator order
a\geq 2

and predecessor-uniform twisting with parameters
q_{j}
.
The physical indices take values
r_{j}\in\{0,1,\ldots,a-1\}
. Define

\widetilde{A}^{[j]}_{r_{j}}\in\mathbb{C}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}
by

\displaystyle\bigl(\widetilde{A}^{[j]}_{r_{j}}\bigr)_{\ell_{j-1},\ell_{j}}=\begin{cases}c_{j}^{\,\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}}\dbinom{\ell_{j}}{\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}}_{q_{j}}&\ell_{j}\geq\ell_{j-1},\;\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}\equiv r_{j}\pmod{a},\\[8.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(25)

Then

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}=v_{0}\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{A}^{[j]}_{r_{j}}\Bigr)v_{m}^{\top},

(26)

where the boundary vectors
v_{0}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
and

v_{m}=(0,\ldots,0,1)
are as in the commutative case.

Proof.

Substituting (
23
) into (
19
) and
re-indexing by
\ell_{j}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0=\ell_{0}\leq\cdots\leq\ell_{m}=k\\
\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}\equiv r_{j}\pmod{a}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}^{\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}}\binom{\ell_{j}}{\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}}_{q_{j}}.

(27)

Each summand is a product of nearest-neighbour factors; encoding endpoint
constraints via
v_{0}
,
v_{m}
yields (
26
)
with bond dimension
k+1
.
∎

Remark 3.15

(Sparsity and computational cost)
.

A single amplitude
\alpha_{r}
is computed by the sweep

v_{j}=v_{j-1}\widetilde{A}^{[j]}_{r_{j}}
at cost
O(mk^{2})
. For
general
q_{j}
, the Gaussian binomial entries can be precomputed via the

q
-Pascal recurrence (
22
) in
O(k^{2})
per site and

O(mk^{2})
total.

The selection rule
k_{j}\equiv r_{j}\pmod{a}
means that each matrix

\widetilde{A}^{[j]}_{r_{j}}
has approximately a fraction
1/a
of its
entries nonzero; in particular, larger generator orders produce sparser
MPS matrices. In practice, one does not need to enumerate all
a^{m}

amplitudes: the MPS representation (
26
) with bond
dimension
k+1
can be used directly for efficient pilot state
preparation, since known methods can prepare a quantum state described
by an MPS of bond dimension
D
in
\mathrm{poly}(m,D)
gates
(see, e.g.,
[
3
]
).

Proposition 3.16

(Extension to general polynomials)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}a_{j}x^{j}
be an arbitrary polynomial of
degree at most
d
, and let
\widetilde{A}^{[j^{\prime}]}_{r_{j^{\prime}}}
denote the
site matrices of Theorem
3.14
built at bond
dimension
d+1
(i.e. with
k
in (
25
) replaced
by
d
). Then the pilot state amplitudes

\alpha_{r}(\mathcal{P})=\sum_{j=0}^{d}a_{j}\,\alpha_{r}^{(j)}
admit an exact
MPS representation of bond dimension
d+1
using these same site
matrices, the same left boundary
v_{0}=(1,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{C}^{d+1}
,
and the
\mathcal{P}
-dependent right boundary vector

\displaystyle v_{m}^{(\mathcal{P})}\;=\;(a_{0},\,a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{d})\;\in\;\mathbb{C}^{d+1}.

(28)

Explicitly,

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}(\mathcal{P})\;=\;v_{0}\,\Bigl(\prod_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}\widetilde{A}^{[j^{\prime}]}_{r_{j^{\prime}}}\Bigr)\,\bigl(v_{m}^{(\mathcal{P})}\bigr)^{\!\top}.

(29)

This improves on the standard direct-sum construction for linear
combinations of MPS (block-diagonal site matrices with appropriate
boundary vectors; see, e.g.,
Schollwöck
[
3
]
), which applied to the sum of
monomial pilot states of degrees
0,1,\ldots,d
would yield bond
dimension
\sum_{j=0}^{d}(j{+}1)=\binom{d+2}{2}
.

Proof.

The matrix entry

\bigl(\widetilde{A}^{[j^{\prime}]}_{r_{j^{\prime}}}\bigr)_{\ell_{j^{\prime}-1},\ell_{j^{\prime}}}

in (
25
) is

c_{j^{\prime}}^{\ell_{j^{\prime}}-\ell_{j^{\prime}-1}}\,\binom{\ell_{j^{\prime}}}{\ell_{j^{\prime}}-\ell_{j^{\prime}-1}}_{q_{j^{\prime}}}
whenever

\ell_{j^{\prime}}\geq\ell_{j^{\prime}-1}
and
\ell_{j^{\prime}}-\ell_{j^{\prime}-1}\equiv r_{j^{\prime}}\pmod{a}
,
and zero otherwise. In particular, it depends only on the bond
indices
\ell_{j^{\prime}-1},\ell_{j^{\prime}}\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
and on the physical
index
r_{j^{\prime}}
; it is
independent
of the overall polynomial
degree chosen in Theorem
3.14
.

For each
j_{0}\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
, apply
Theorem
3.14
with degree
k=j_{0}
, embedded in

\mathbb{C}^{d+1}
by padding with zeros at bond levels
\ell>j_{0}
. Since
the bond indices can only increase through the MPS contraction and
the left boundary is
v_{0}=e_{0}
, any contraction that reaches bond
level
\ell_{m}>j_{0}
does not contribute to the
j_{0}
-th monomial
amplitude, while the contraction that ends at
\ell_{m}=j_{0}
computes
exactly
\alpha_{r}^{(j_{0})}
. Hence

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}^{(j_{0})}\;=\;v_{0}\,\Bigl(\prod_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}\widetilde{A}^{[j^{\prime}]}_{r_{j^{\prime}}}\Bigr)\,e_{j_{0}}^{\!\top},

(30)

where
e_{j_{0}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d+1}
is the
(j_{0}{+}1)
-th standard basis
vector. By linearity of
\alpha_{r}
in the polynomial
\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}(\mathcal{P})\;=\;\sum_{j_{0}=0}^{d}a_{j_{0}}\,\alpha_{r}^{(j_{0})}\;=\;v_{0}\,\Bigl(\prod_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}\widetilde{A}^{[j^{\prime}]}_{r_{j^{\prime}}}\Bigr)\,\Bigl(\sum_{j_{0}=0}^{d}a_{j_{0}}\,e_{j_{0}}\Bigr)^{\!\top}\;=\;v_{0}\,\Bigl(\prod_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}\widetilde{A}^{[j^{\prime}]}_{r_{j^{\prime}}}\Bigr)\,\bigl(v_{m}^{(\mathcal{P})}\bigr)^{\!\top}.

(31)

The bond-dimension count of the site matrices is
(d{+}1)\times(d{+}1)

by construction.
∎

3.4
Specialization to Pauli Hamiltonians (
a=2
)

For Pauli Hamiltonians on qubits,
a=2
and

\omega_{ij}=(-1)^{\lambda_{ij}}\in\{+1,-1\}
.
The predecessor-uniform parameters satisfy
q_{j}\in\{+1,-1\}
.
The physical indices are
r_{j}\in\{0,1\}
, giving a
2^{m}
-dimensional
pilot state. Only two Gaussian binomials arise.

Case
q_{j}=+1
(commuting generator).
This reduces to the
ordinary binomial coefficient:
\binom{n}{r}_{1}=\binom{n}{r}
.

Case
q_{j}=-1
(anticommuting generator).
The Gaussian binomial
at
q=-1
admits the well-known closed form

\displaystyle\binom{n}{r}_{-1}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\binom{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}&\text{if }n\text{ is odd or }r\text{ is even},\\[6.0pt]
0&\text{if }n\text{ is even and }r\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

(32)

The vanishing pattern —
\binom{n}{r}_{-1}=0
whenever
n
is even and

r
is odd — introduces sparsity in the MPS matrices

\widetilde{A}^{[j]}_{r_{j}}
for anticommuting generators, which can be
exploited to reduce the cost of the MPS contraction.

Both cases allow
O(1)
evaluation per entry (using precomputed ordinary
binomials), so all MPS matrix entries can be filled in
O(mk^{2})
total
time — the same asymptotic cost as a single MPS sweep.

Remark 3.17

(Predecessor-uniformity for Paulis)
.

In the Pauli case (
a=2
), predecessor-uniformity means that for each

j
, the generator
P_{j}
either commutes with all of

P_{1},\ldots,P_{j-1}
(
q_{j}=+1
) or anticommutes with all of
them (
q_{j}=-1
). For example, a set of mutually anticommuting
Paulis is predecessor-uniform under any ordering (with
q_{j}=-1

for all
j\geq 2
).

3.5
Specialization to Qudit Hamiltonians (
a=d
,
d
prime)

For qudit systems of prime local dimension
d
, the generalized Pauli
(Heisenberg–Weyl) operators satisfy
Z_{j}^{d}=1
, with commutation phases

\omega_{ij}\in\mu_{d}=\{e^{2\pi ik/d}:k=0,\ldots,d-1\}
. The
primality of
d
is essential for the single-order framework: the clock
and shift operators
X
and
Z
satisfy
X^{d}=Z^{d}=I
, so every
generalized Pauli
P=\omega^{c}X^{a}Z^{b}
has order dividing
d
. When

d
is prime, the only divisors are
1
and
d
, so every non-identity
generalized Pauli has order exactly
d
and the single-order condition

a=d
holds uniformly. For composite
d
, different generators may
have different orders (e.g. for
d=6
, the operator
X^{3}
has order
2
),
and the single-order assumption would not apply without further
restrictions on the generator set.

The generator order is thus
a=d
, the physical indices take values

r_{j}\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
, and the pilot state has
d^{m}
amplitudes.

Unlike the Pauli case (
d=2
), the Gaussian binomials at
q=e^{2\pi ik/d}

do not generally admit simple closed forms. However, the
q
-Pascal
recurrence (
22
) computes each
\binom{n}{r}_{q}
in
O(nr)

operations regardless of the value of
q
, including at roots of unity.
The MPS bond dimension remains
k+1
and the contraction cost is still

O(mk^{2})
per amplitude.

4
Comparison with the Connected-Component Approach

We briefly compare the MPS construction of Theorem
3.14

with the approach based on connected components of the anticommutation
graph, which is the method used in
[
2
,
1
]
.

Definition 4.1

(Anticommutation graph)
.

The
anticommutation graph

G=(V,E)
of a set of Pauli generators

\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}\}
has vertex set
V=\{1,\ldots,m\}
and an edge

(i,j)\in E
whenever
P_{i}P_{j}=-P_{j}P_{i}
.

Suppose
G
decomposes into connected components

C_{1},\ldots,C_{p}
. In the approach of
[
2
,
1
]
,
the computation of the pilot state amplitudes factorizes over
components, and the cost is dominated by the largest component. Let

c_{\max}=\max_{\ell}|C_{\ell}|
denote the size of the largest connected
component. The overall cost of the connected-component method
scales exponentially in
c_{\max}
.

By contrast, the MPS of Theorem
3.14
has bond dimension

k+1
and computes each amplitude in
O(mk^{2})
time,
irrespective

of the structure of the anticommutation graph, provided the twisting
matrix admits a predecessor-uniform ordering. The following example shows
that predecessor-uniform twistings can have
c_{\max}=m
, giving an
exponential separation in the cost of computing pilot state amplitudes.

Proposition 4.2

(Mutually anticommuting generators)
.

Let
\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}\}
be a set of mutually anticommuting
n
-qubit
Pauli operators (such a set exists with
m=2n+1
). Then:

(i)

The twisting matrix
\Omega
with

\omega_{ij}=-1
for all
i\neq j
is predecessor-uniform under
every ordering (with
q_{j}=-1
for all
j\geq 2
).

(ii)

The anticommutation graph is the complete graph

K_{m}
, which is a single connected component with
c_{\max}=m
.

(iii)

The connected-component approach
of
[
2
,
1
]
has cost exponential in
m
,
while the MPS of Theorem
3.14
has bond dimension

k+1
and cost
O(mk^{2})
per amplitude.

Proof.

Part
(i)
: since
\omega_{ij}=-1
for all
i<j
, for any
ordering and any generator
z_{j}
, the commutation parameter with every
predecessor is
-1
; this is constant, so predecessor-uniformity holds
trivially. Part
(ii)
is immediate: every pair of generators is
connected by an edge. Part
(iii)
follows from
Theorem
3.14
with
q_{j}=-1
for all
j\geq 2
.
∎

An explicit family of mutually anticommuting
n
-qubit Paulis of size

m=2n+1
is given by the Jordan–Wigner construction: for

k=1,\ldots,n
, define

\displaystyle\gamma_{2k-1}=Z^{\otimes(k-1)}\otimes X\otimes I^{\otimes(n-k)},\qquad\gamma_{2k}=Z^{\otimes(k-1)}\otimes Y\otimes I^{\otimes(n-k)},

(33)

together with
\gamma_{2n+1}=Z^{\otimes n}
. One verifies that

\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}=-\gamma_{j}\gamma_{i}
for all
i\neq j
. For example,
on
n=2
qubits the five generators are
XI
,
YI
,
ZX
,
ZY
,
ZZ
;
on
n=3
qubits there are seven:
XII
,
YII
,
ZXI
,
ZYI
,
ZZX
,

ZZY
,
ZZZ
.

Remark 4.3

(Compatibility with the symplectic structure)
.

One might wonder whether the symplectic origin of the Pauli commutation
phases imposes additional constraints that make predecessor-uniform
twistings harder to realize than the abstract algebra suggests.

Predecessor-uniformity is a condition on the entries of the

m\times m
matrix
\Lambda=(\lambda_{ij})
, where

\lambda_{ij}=u_{i}\cdot w_{j}+w_{i}\cdot u_{j}\pmod{2}
is the
symplectic form of
P_{i}=(u_{i}\mid w_{i})
and

P_{j}=(u_{j}\mid w_{j})
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
. The symplectic form
automatically ensures
\Lambda_{ii}=0
and
\Lambda_{ij}=\Lambda_{ji}
,
and one might ask whether further constraints are present — for
instance, whether the values
\lambda_{12}
,
\lambda_{23}
could
force
\lambda_{13}
.

No such constraint exists: the
\binom{m}{2}
upper-triangular entries
of
\Lambda
are freely specifiable. Given any
m\times m
symmetric

\mathbb{F}_{2}
matrix with zero diagonal, the following
construction on
n=m
qubits realizes it. Set

P_{i}=(u_{i}\mid w_{i})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2m}
with
u_{i}=e_{i}

(the
i
-th standard basis vector) and

\displaystyle w_{i}[j]=\begin{cases}\Lambda_{ij}&\text{if }j>i,\\
0&\text{if }j\leq i.\end{cases}

(34)

For
i<j
, the symplectic product is

\langle P_{i},P_{j}\rangle_{s}=\underbrace{e_{i}\cdot w_{j}}_{=\,w_{j}[i]\,=\,0}+\underbrace{w_{i}\cdot e_{j}}_{=\,w_{i}[j]\,=\,\Lambda_{ij}}=\Lambda_{ij}
,
where
w_{j}[i]=0
because
i<j
and
w_{j}
is supported only on
indices
>j
.

In particular, the all-ones off-diagonal matrix
(
\Lambda_{ij}=1
for
i\neq j
) that encodes mutual anticommutation
is realized by the Jordan–Wigner construction on

n=\lceil(m-1)/2\rceil
qubits, confirming that

4.2
applies to genuine Pauli
Hamiltonians.

Remark 4.4

(Predecessor-uniformity and locality)
.

The most significant limitation of predecessor-uniformity from a
physical perspective is its tension with locality.
In the qubit case (
a=2
), if generator
P_{j}
anticommutes
with any predecessor
P_{i}
(
i<j
), then it must anticommute with

all
predecessors
P_{1},\ldots,P_{j-1}
. In particular,
P_{j}

must share support with every preceding generator. This rules out
geometrically local Hamiltonians, where each generator acts on a bounded
number of qubits and therefore commutes with all generators whose
support is disjoint.

More precisely, for a Hamiltonian on a lattice with generators of
bounded support, most pairs of generators have disjoint support and thus
commute. For any generator
P_{j}
that anticommutes with even one
predecessor, predecessor-uniformity would force
P_{j}
to anticommute
with all earlier generators — including those with disjoint support,
which is impossible. The predecessor-uniform condition therefore selects
Hamiltonians with highly non-local, overlapping generators.

This does not render the method useless: mutually anticommuting
generators (Proposition
4.2
) are a natural class
that arises, for instance, in the Jordan–Wigner encoding of fermionic
systems. However, the tension with locality highlights that the
greatest potential for the MPS approach lies in identifying Hamiltonians
that combine non-local generator structure with tractable decoding.

5
Conclusions

We have introduced the twisted multinomial coefficient — a
generalization of the classical
q
-multinomial in which inversions
carry pair-dependent weights — and proved that under
predecessor-uniformity, it factorizes as a product of Gaussian binomials
with site-dependent parameters
q_{j}

(Proposition
3.11
). This purely combinatorial
identity yields an exact MPS of bond dimension
k+1
for the pilot
state amplitudes of
h^{k}
in the HDQI framework
(Theorem
3.14
), and more generally bond dimension

\deg(\mathcal{P})+1
for the amplitudes of
\mathcal{P}(h)
for any polynomial

\mathcal{P}
(Proposition
3.16
). These results demonstrate
an exponential improvement over the connected-component method for
Hamiltonians built from mutually anticommuting generators
(Proposition
4.2
).

A central theme of this work is that pilot state preparation is only one
component of the HDQI pipeline: efficient decoding of the associated
Hamiltonian code is equally essential, and the two must be considered in
conjunction. The predecessor-uniform condition solves the pilot state
problem, but its tension with generator locality
(Remark
4.4
) constrains the class of applicable
Hamiltonians.

To illustrate the subtlety of combining pilot state preparation with
decoding, consider the perturbed toric code

H=-\sum_{v}A_{v}-\sum_{p}B_{p}+\epsilon\sum_{j}Z_{j}
on an

L\times L
torus. If the plaquette operators
B_{p}
and the
single-qubit
Z_{j}
are all included as generators, then
B_{p}
is the
product of four generators
Z_{j_{1}}Z_{j_{2}}Z_{j_{3}}Z_{j_{4}}
, yielding a
weight-5 relation
B_{p}\cdot Z_{j_{1}}\cdot Z_{j_{2}}\cdot Z_{j_{3}}\cdot Z_{j_{4}}=I
in the generator group. Consequently, the Hamiltonian
code has distance at most
5
, independent of the lattice size
L
.
Even if one could efficiently prepare the pilot state for this
Hamiltonian, the small code distance would preclude reliable decoding.
Dropping
B_{p}
from the generator set restores the code distance but
makes the Hamiltonian non-linear in the remaining generators, taking it
outside the current framework. This example illustrates why identifying
Hamiltonians that simultaneously admit efficient pilot state preparation

and
sufficient code distance remains a challenging open problem.

On the combinatorial side, it would be interesting to explore the
computational complexity of evaluating or approximating the twisted
multinomial coefficient for general twisting matrices
\Omega
, and to
understand more precisely the role that predecessor-uniformity plays as a
boundary between tractable and potentially intractable instances.

We hope that the combinatorial perspective developed here — in
particular, the identification of the twisted multinomial as an object
of independent interest — will shed further light on what makes pilot
state preparation tractable or hard in general, and will guide the search
for Hamiltonians where the full HDQI pipeline can be realized efficiently.
