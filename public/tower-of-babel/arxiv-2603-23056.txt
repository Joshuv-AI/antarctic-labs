Abstract
. The ordered eigenvalues define a Lipschitz map on the real vector space of Hermitian d × d d\times d matrices. We prove that this map acts continuously, but not uniformly continuously, by superposition on the Sobolev spaces W 1 , q W^{1,q} , for all 1 ≤ q < ∞ 1\leq q<\infty , on bounded open domains. For q = ∞ q=\infty , the action is still well-defined and bounded but not continuous. We show that this stability result extends to normal matrices, where the eigenvalues are naturally interpreted as multivalued Sobolev functions in the sense of Almgren. Several applications are given, including the stability of singular values, condition numbers of matrices, surface area of eigenvalue graphs, and compact self-adjoint operators in Hilbert space.

1.
Introduction

The perturbation theory of Hermitian and normal operators has a long history.
The analytic theory started with Rellich’s work
[
Rel37
]
, culminated in Kato’s monograph
[
Kat76
]
,
and extended to smooth perturbation theory in more recent papers, e.g.,

[
AKLM98
]
,
[
KM03
,
KMR11
,
KMR12
]
,

[
Rai09
,
Rai13
,
Rai22
]
, and
[
PR20
]
.
For a more comprehensive account of the history,
we refer to the recent survey article
[
PR25
]
.

For the present paper, Weyl’s perturbation theorem
[
Wey12
]
plays a fundamental role.
It asserts that the ordered eigenvalues form a Lipschitz map on the space of Hermitian matrices
with Lipschitz constant
1
, when the space is equipped
with the operator norm and the eigenvalues with the
\infty
-norm. Together with
Löwner’s theorem
[
Löw34
]
, which provides the corresponding result for the
2
-norm,
and its extension to normal matrices by Hoffman and Wielandt
[
HW53
]
,
this theorem is foundational for nearly all of the results in this paper.
Of course, in the case of normal matrices, the eigenvalues must be equipped with a metric that
accounts for the fact that they are general complex numbers not admitting a coherent ordering.
See also
[
BDM83
]
for a variant with the operator norm
(where, however, the Lipschitz constant
C
satisfies
1<C<3
).
For locally Lipschitz curves of normal matrices, each continuous choice of the eigenvalues is actually
locally Lipschitz, as shown by
[
Rai13
]
. If the (real) parameter space is at least
2
-dimensional,
continuous choices of the eigenvalues of normal matrices may not exist.

The Lipschitz continuity of the eigenvalues as functions of the matrices
immediately implies that Lipschitz families of Hermitian or normal matrices are
assigned to the Lipschitz families of their eigenvalues, in a bounded way, that is, with
controlled Lipschitz constants.
This defines a map which we call the
characteristic map
:
it is given by superposition with the ordered eigenvalue map, in the Hermitian case,
and with the unordered eigenvalue map, in the normal case.
In this paper, we are interested in the continuity properties of the characteristic map.

The characteristic map is not continuous with respect to the Lipschitz topology
(on the eigenvalues), but it is continuous with respect to the Sobolev
W^{1,q}
topology, for every
1\leq q<\infty
.
Specifically,
we prove that for Hermitian matrices the characteristic map is continuous, but not uniformly continuous,
from
W^{1,q}
matrices to ordered
W^{1,q}
eigenvalues,
while for normal matrices it is continuous from

W^{1,q}
matrices to unordered
W^{1,q}
eigenvalues — in both cases, for every
1\leq q<\infty
.
In the latter setting, the unordered eigenvalues are naturally treated as multivalued
Sobolev functions in the sense of Almgren
[
Alm00
]
; see also
[
DLS11
]
.

These results are considerably stronger than the related continuity properties of the solution map for
hyperbolic and general polynomials established, respectively,
in the recent papers
[
PR24a
]
and
[
PR24b
]
.
The solution map is defined in analogy to the characteristic map.
Recall that Bronshtein’s theorem
[
Bro79
]
implies that the ordered roots of a
C^{d-1,1}
family of
hyperbolic (i.e. monic real-rooted) polynomials of degree
d
are locally Lipschitz;
explicit bounds for the Lipschitz constants of the roots in terms of the
C^{d-1,1}
norms
of the coefficients were obtained by
[
PR15
]
.
By
[
PR24a
]
, the solution map for hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
is continuous
from
C^{d}
coefficients to
C^{0,1}
roots with respect to the
W^{1,q}_{\operatorname{loc}}
topology on the roots,
for each
1\leq q<\infty
— but not with respect to the
C^{0,1}
topology.
For general complex polynomials, the optimal Sobolev regularity
W^{1,q}
, for
1\leq q<\frac{d}{d-1}
,
of the roots was established in
[
PR16
,
PR18
,
PR20a
]
,
and the existence and boundedness of the solution map rests on these results.
As shown in
[
PR24b
]
, the solution map is then continuous from
C^{d}
coefficients
to unordered
W^{1,q}
roots, for each
1\leq q<\frac{d}{d-1}
.

Two further points underline the superiority of the matrix setting.
First, unlike the polynomial case, the size of the matrices plays no role.
Second, the Sobolev spaces
W^{1,q}
of Hermitian matrices and their eigenvalues
are defined globally, whereas for hyperbolic polynomials the spaces of coefficients and roots are necessarily local.

These results also have concrete applications.
We discuss several, including singular values, the condition number for matrices, the surface area of eigenvalue graphs,
and compact self-adjoint operators in Hilbert space (made possible by independence of dimension).

It should be mentioned that the orthonormal eigenvectors do not share the regularity of the eigenvalues
and, in general, fail to be even continuous in this setting.

In addition to Weyl’s perturbation theorem and its relatives, a key ingredient in the proofs is a
splitting lemma for complex triangular matrices due to
[
PR20
]
, where it appears in
a slightly different context. It provides a local uniform unitary block-diagonalization of Hermitian and normal matrices
whenever not all eigenvalues coincide. This suggests an inductive argument on the size of the matrices
and has the advantage of working directly with the matrices themselves rather than through
their characteristic polynomials.

In the following, we present our results in more detail.

1.1.
Hermitian matrices

Let
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be the complex vector space of complex
d\times d
matrices. We endow
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
with the
Frobenius norm
\|A\|_{2}=(\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}|a_{ij}|^{2})^{1/2}
.

Let
\operatorname{Herm}(d)=\{A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C}):A^{*}=A\}
denote the real vector space of complex Hermitian
d\times d
matrices.
We associate with
A\in\operatorname{Herm}(d)
its increasingly ordered eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}^{\uparrow}(A)\leq\lambda_{2}^{\uparrow}(A)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}^{\uparrow}(A)

and thus obtain a continuous map

(1.1)

\lambda^{\uparrow}=(\lambda_{1}^{\uparrow},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{\uparrow}):\operatorname{Herm}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In fact, by a result of Löwner
[
Löw34
]
(see
Proposition

3.2
), this map is even Lipschitz continuous: for
A,B\in\operatorname{Herm}(d)
,

(1.2)

\|\lambda^{\uparrow}(A)-\lambda^{\uparrow}(B)\|_{2}\leq\|A-B\|_{2}.

As a consequence (see
Proposition

3.5
),
the map (
1.1
) induces a bounded map

(1.3)

\mathcal{E}:=(\lambda^{\uparrow})_{*}:W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

for each
1\leq q<\infty
,
which takes
W^{1,q}
families of Hermitian matrices to their increasingly ordered
W^{1,q}
eigenvalues,
where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is open and bounded.
(This also true for
q=\infty
but this case will be discussed separately below.)

We will call
\mathcal{E}=(\lambda^{\uparrow})_{*}
the
characteristic map
, independently of the domain and codomain which may vary in different contexts.

We will show that the characteristic map (
1.3
) is continuous.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and bounded.
Then the characteristic map

\mathcal{E}:W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

is continuous.

Theorem

1.1
will be proved in
Section

5
for
m=1
and the general case will be concluded from this special
case in
Section

6
.

Remark 1.2
.

Note that superposition by any Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
induces a continuous map

f_{*}:W^{1,q}(U)\to W^{1,q}(U)
for every
1\leq q<\infty
,
where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is open and bounded, by
[
MM79
]
.
However, there are Lipschitz maps
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, with
k\geq 2
,
such that the induced map
f_{*}:W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{k})\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
is not continuous;
see
[
Mus91
]
, where also sufficient conditions for continuity are given.
But our proof of
Theorem

1.1
will follow a different strategy.

Remark 1.3
.

The image of the characteristic map is

\mathcal{E}(W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d)))=W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\uparrow}),

the space of
f\in W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
f(U)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\uparrow}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{d}\}
. The
continuous surjection
\mathcal{E}:W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\uparrow})
admits a continuous right-inverse
which takes
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\uparrow})
to the diagonal matrix with the diagonal entries
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
.

We state a simple consequence of
Theorem

1.1
.
Here

\|f\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\big\|\|f\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(U)}
,
see
Section

1.7
for notation.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and bounded.
If
A_{n}\to A
in
W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
as
n\to\infty
, then,
for all
1\leq j\leq m
,

\displaystyle\big\|\|\partial_{j}(\mathcal{E}(A))\|_{2}-\|\partial_{j}(\mathcal{E}(A_{n}))\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(U)}\to 0

and

\displaystyle\|\partial_{j}(\mathcal{E}(A_{n}))\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to\|\partial_{j}(\mathcal{E}(A))\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})}

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let us set
\lambda:=\mathcal{E}(A)
and
\lambda_{n}:=\mathcal{E}(A_{n})
. Then

\displaystyle\big|\|\partial_{j}\lambda\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})}-\|\partial_{j}\lambda_{n}\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})}\big|=\big|\big\|\|\partial_{j}\lambda\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(U)}-\big\|\|\partial_{j}\lambda_{n}\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(U)}\big|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\big\|\|\partial_{j}\lambda\|_{2}-\|\partial_{j}\lambda_{n}\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(U)}\leq\big\|\|\partial_{j}\lambda-\partial_{j}\lambda_{n}\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(U)}=\|\partial_{j}\lambda-\partial_{j}\lambda_{n}\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})}

so that the assertions follow from
Theorem

1.1
.
∎

Let us now turn to the Lipschitz case (i.e.,
q=\infty
).
Clearly, (
1.2
) also induces a
bounded map

(1.4)

\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

taking Lipschitz families of Hermitian matrices to their increasingly ordered Lipschitz eigenvalues,
with Lipschitz constants
satisfying

(1.5)

|\mathcal{E}(A)|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq|A|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{U},M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))},

with respect to the
2
-norms on
\operatorname{Herm}(d)
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

As an immediate consequence of
Theorem

1.1
, we get
the following theorem which answers Question 1.11 in
[
PR24a
]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and bounded.
Then the characteristic map

\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

is continuous, for all
1\leq q<\infty
, where
C^{0,1}_{q}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes
the set
C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})

equipped with the trace topology of the inclusion

C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

But we will see in
Example

7.1
that the map

\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
not
continuous:
the natural topology on the target
C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is too strong.

As a corollary of
Theorem

1.5
, we find that
\mathcal{E}
is continuous
into the Hölder space
C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, carrying its natural topology,
for all
0<\alpha<1
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a bounded open Lipschitz domain.
Then the characteristic map

(1.6)

\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

is continuous, for all
0<\alpha<1
, but not for
\alpha=1
.

In
Corollary

1.6
, which will be proved in
Section

6
,
U
is a Lipschitz domain, since we use Morrey’s inequality.

In the setup of
Theorem

1.5
, we will actually prove a stronger result, in
Section

5
(for
m=1
) and
Section

6
:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be an open set.
Let
A_{n}\to A
in
C^{0,1}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
as
n\to\infty
.
Then, for each
1\leq j\leq m
and almost every
x\in U
,

(1.7)

\partial_{j}(\mathcal{E}(A_{n}))(x)\to\partial_{j}(\mathcal{E}(A))(x)\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

As a consequence, we obtain a second proof of
Theorem

1.5
based on the dominated convergence theorem;
see
Theorem

6.2
.
Clearly, (
1.7
) generally fails, if we only assume

A_{n}\to A
in
W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
for some
1\leq q<\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

By Egorov’s theorem
[
Ego11
]
, we may conclude that

\partial_{j}(\mathcal{E}(A_{n}))\to\partial_{j}(\mathcal{E}(A))
almost uniformly on

as
n\to\infty
.
In general, the convergence is not uniform on

; see
Example

1.2.
Normal matrices

The complex normal
d\times d
matrices form a real algebraic subset

\operatorname{Norm}(d):=\{A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C}):A^{*}A=AA^{*}\}

of the vectorspace
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
of all complex
d\times d
matrices.
The set
\operatorname{Norm}(d)
is a real singular cone; see
[
Huh01
]
for background on its geometry.

With
A\in\operatorname{Norm}(d)
we associate its
d
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(A),\ldots,\lambda_{d}(A)
(with multiplicities)
and the unordered eigenvalue vector

\Lambda(A)=[\lambda_{1}(A),\ldots,\lambda_{d}(A)].

In this way, we obtain a map

(1.8)

\Lambda:\operatorname{Norm}(d)\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}),

where
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is the space of unordered complex
d
-tuples (see
Section

8.1
),
a complete metric space with respect to the metric

\mathbf{d}_{2}([z],[w]):=\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|z_{i}-w_{\sigma(i)}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

where
\operatorname{S}_{d}
is the symmetric group.
The map (
1.8
) is Lipschitz continuous, by a result of Hoffman and Wielandt
[
HW53
]

(see
Proposition

3.3
): for
A,B\in\operatorname{Norm}(d)
,

(1.9)

\mathbf{d}_{2}(\Lambda(A),\Lambda(B))\leq\|A-B\|_{2}.

Due to Almgren
[
Alm00
]
,
there exists a bi-Lipschitz embedding
\Delta:{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N=N(d)
,
which can be used to define Sobolev spaces of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued functions:
for open
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
, we set

(1.10)

W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})):=\{f:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}):\Delta\circ f\in W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})\}.

An equivalent intrinsic definition of
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))

is due to De Lellis and Spadaro
[
DLS11
]
(see also
Definition

8.4
).
The space
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
carries the metric

(1.11)

\rho_{\Delta}^{1,q}(f,g):=\|\Delta\circ f-\Delta\circ g\|_{W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})}

which turns it into a complete metric space; see
[
PR24b
, Lemma 3.1]
.
The topology induced by this metric does not depend on the choice of the Almgren embedding
\Delta
,
see
Theorem

8.11
.

The map (
1.8
) induces a bounded map

(1.12)

\mathcal{E}_{u}:=\Lambda_{*}:W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Norm}(d))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})),\quad A\mapsto\Lambda\circ A,

for each
1\leq q<\infty
,
where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a bounded open set, see
Proposition

9.1
.
(The Lipschitz case
q=\infty
is discussed below.)
By
W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Norm}(d))
we mean the (nonlinear) space of all
A\in W^{1,q}(U,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
such that

A(U)\subseteq\operatorname{Norm}(d)
, topologized by its inclusion in
W^{1,q}(U,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
.

In this setup, we call
\mathcal{E}_{u}=\Lambda_{*}
the
characteristic map
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and bounded.
Then the characteristic map

\mathcal{E}_{u}:W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Norm}(d))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})),\quad A\mapsto\Lambda\circ A,

is continuous.

It turns out that

C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Norm}(d))
as

.
Assume that
\lambda,\lambda_{n}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{C}^{d})
are parameterizations
of the eigenvalues of

, respectively.
Assume that
\lambda_{n}\to\lambda
in

on
\mathcal{L}^{m-1}

parallel to the coordinate axes.
Then

\partial_{j}\lambda_{n}\to\partial_{j}\lambda
almost everywhere in

as

, for all

.

Theorem

1.13
and

1.14
will be proved in

(for

.
It is evident (see the proof of

(1.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\big\|\|\partial_{j}\lambda\|_{2}-\|\partial_{j}\lambda_{n}\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(U)}\to 0,\\

as

1.3.
Optimality of the results

The results on eigenvalue stability obtained in this paper are essentially optimal.
Here we briefly mention several examples that demonstrate this fact;
these examples will then be discussed in detail in
Section

7
.
We also formulate an open problem.

We will focus on the limitations in the Hermitian case, which clearly entail corresponding negative results in the more
general normal case.

(1)

The map
\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is not continuous.

For instance,

(1.14)

A_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{n}&x\\
x&-\frac{1}{n}\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\
x&0\end{pmatrix}=A(x)\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty

uniformly in all derivatives on every compact interval in
\mathbb{R}
.
Then
\mathcal{E}(A_{n})=(-a_{n},a_{n})
and
\mathcal{E}(A)=(-a,a)
, where

a_{n}(x)=\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}
and
a(x)=|x|
. We will see in

Example

7.1
that
|a-a_{n}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I})}\geq 2-\sqrt{2}
, for all bounded open intervals
I
containing
0
and
all sufficiently large
n
. Also note that off
0
the derivatives
a_{n}^{\prime}
tend pointwise to
a^{\prime}
but
not uniformly in any neighborhood of
0
.

(2)

For no
1\leq q<\infty
, the map
\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is uniformly continuous.

Indeed, the matrices

A_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{n}&\varphi_{n}(x)\\
\varphi_{n}(x)&-\frac{1}{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad B_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2n}&\varphi_{n}(x)\\
\varphi_{n}(x)&-\frac{1}{2n}\end{pmatrix},

where
\varphi_{n}:[0,1]\to[0,\frac{1}{n}]
is a sawtooth function with Lipschitz constant
1
,
satisfy
\|A_{n}-B_{n}\|_{C^{0,1}([0,1],M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, but
for their nonnegative eigenvalues
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
we find
\|a_{n}^{\prime}-b_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}([0,1])}\geq\frac{1}{12\cdot 2^{1/q}}
,
in
Example

7.2
.

(3)

For no
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the map
\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is uniformly continuous.

This is demonstrated in
Example

7.3
, by the matrices

A_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{n^{r}}&nx\\
nx&-\frac{1}{n^{r}}\end{pmatrix},\quad B_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2n^{r}}&nx\\
nx&-\frac{1}{2n^{r}}\end{pmatrix},

where
r=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
.

Even tough the maps in (2) and (3) are not uniformly continuous,
it is very desirable to find effective moduli of continuity for their restrictions to interesting (for applications)
compact subspaces of
C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))
.
For instance, for given
C>0
and
\beta\in(0,1]
, the set

(1.15)

K:=\big\{A\in C^{1,\beta}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d)):\|A\|_{C^{1,\beta}(\overline{U},M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}\leq C\big\},

is a relatively compact subset of
C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))
, by the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem.
Thus,
\mathcal{E}|_{K}:K\to C^{0,1}_{q}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for
1\leq q<\infty
, and

\mathcal{E}|_{K}:K\to C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for
0<\alpha<1
, are uniformly continuous,
by
Theorem

1.5
and
Corollary

1.6
.

Problem 1.15
.

Find effective moduli of continuity for the restrictions of the characteristic map
\mathcal{E}
to
interesting compact subspaces.

For instance, using the sequence
A_{n}
in (
1.14
) we will see in
Example

7.4

that, for
1\leq q<\infty
and
q^{-1}<\alpha\leq 1
, the map
\mathcal{E}|_{K}:K\to C^{0,1}_{q}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})
is not
\alpha
-Hölder continuous.

Remark 1.16
.

Due to Rellich
[
Rel37
]
, a real analytic curve of Hermitian matrices admits a real analytic
system of eigenvalues and a real analytic orthonormal frame of eigenvectors. There are various extensions
of this result, e.g.,
to normal matrices or to other categories (smooth quasianalytic classes, formal power series),
see
[
AKLM98
]
,
[
Rai13
]
, and
[
PR20
]
.
But in general there is no continuous orthonormal frame of the eigenvectors, as seen by the following
C^{\infty}
example from
[
Rel37
]
:

A(x):=e^{-1/x^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos x^{-1}&\sin x^{-1}\\
\sin x^{-1}&-\cos x^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\quad A(0):=0.

Let us suppose that
I\ni x\mapsto A(x)
is a smooth curve of Hermitian matrices with smooth distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
and corresponding smooth orthonormal
eigenvectors
v_{j}
. Differentiating the equation
A(x)v_{j}(x)=\lambda_{j}(x)v_{j}(x)
and taking the result in the Hermitian inner product with
v_{k}\neq v_{j}

gives

\langle v_{k}(x),v_{j}^{\prime}(x)\rangle=\frac{\langle v_{k}(x),A^{\prime}(x)v_{j}(x)\rangle}{\lambda_{j}(x)-\lambda_{k}(x)}

showing that the projection of the velocity
v_{j}^{\prime}
to
v_{k}
is unbounded as
\lambda_{j}
and
\lambda_{k}
approach each other faster
than the numerator tends to zero.
This is connected to adiabatic theorems in physics and the quantum Hall effect.

1.4.
Singular values

Let us consider the vector space
M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})
of complex
D\times d
matrices, where
d\leq D
(without loss of generality).
The singular values of
A\in M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})
are the nonnegative square roots of the eigenvalues
of the Hermitian matrix
A^{*}A
, usually ordered decreasingly

\sigma_{1}(A)\geq\sigma_{2}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{d}(A)\geq 0.

This defines a map
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}):M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Observing that the Hermitian matrix

\mathbf{A}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\tilde{A}\\
\tilde{A}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
\tilde{A}
is the
D\times D
matrix resulting from
A
by adding
D-d
columns consisting of zeros, has the eigenvalues

\sigma_{1}(A),\ldots,\sigma_{d}(A),0,\ldots,0,-\sigma_{d}(A),\ldots,-\sigma_{1}(A),

we conclude from (
3.3
) that, for
A,B\in M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})
and
1\leq i\leq d
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\,\|\sigma(A)-\sigma(B)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2}=|\operatorname{Tr}\big((\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})^{*}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})\big)|^{1/2}

\displaystyle=|2\,\operatorname{Tr}\big((\tilde{A}-\tilde{B})^{*}(\tilde{A}-\tilde{B})\big)|^{1/2}=\sqrt{2}\,\|A-B\|_{2},

that is,

(1.16)

\|\sigma(A)-\sigma(B)\|_{2}\leq\|A-B\|_{2}.

Consequently, for each
1\leq q<\infty
, the map

\sigma_{*}:W^{1,q}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\sigma\circ A,

is well-defined and bounded, as well as

\sigma_{*}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\sigma\circ A.

Furthermore,
Theorem

1.1
,
Theorem

1.5
,
Corollary

1.6
, and
Theorem

1.7
immediately give the following result.

Theorem 1.17
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and bounded. Then:

(1)

For each
1\leq q<\infty
, the map
\sigma_{*}:W^{1,q}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuous.

(2)

The map
\sigma_{*}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuous, for all
1\leq q<\infty
.

(3)

Assume additionally that
U
is a Lipschitz domain. Then
the map
\sigma_{*}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuous, for all
0<\alpha<1
, but not for
\alpha=1
.

(4)

If
A_{n}\to A
in
C^{0,1}(\overline{U},M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))
, then

\partial_{j}(\sigma_{*}(A_{n}))(x)\to\partial_{j}(\sigma_{*}(A))(x)
as
n\to\infty
, for each
1\leq j\leq m
and almost every
x\in U
.

This theorem answers a question raised in
[
PR24a
, Section 7.5]
.

1.5.
Applications

Let us present a few applications of our results.

1.5.1.
Functions defined on the spectrum or the singular values

Clearly, the stability results for the eigenvalues and singular values have immediate applications, e.g.,
for the spectral gaps
\lambda_{i+1}(A)-\lambda_{i}(A)
or the Ky Fan norms
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}(A)
, for
1\leq k\leq d
.
This can be extended using a result of
[
MM79
]
.

Corollary 1.18
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz function.
Let
1\leq q<\infty
.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and bounded.
Then the map

(1.17)

W^{1,q}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}),\quad A\mapsto f\circ\sigma_{i}\circ A,

is well-defined and continuous, where
\sigma_{1}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{d}(A)
are the singular values of
A
.
The statement remains true if
M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})
is replaced by
\operatorname{Herm}(d)
and the ordered eigenvalues
are used instead of the singular values.

Proof.

The map (
1.17
) is the composite

W^{1,q}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\xrightarrow[\hskip 16.38895pt]{(\sigma_{i})_{*}}W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R})\xrightarrow[\hskip 16.38895pt]{f_{*}}W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}).

The first map is continuous by
Theorem

1.17
, the second by
[
MM79
]
.
For Hermitian matrices use
Theorem

1.1
instead of
Theorem

1.17
.
∎

For instance:

(1)

The
p
-th power of the Schatten p-norm
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}(A)^{p}
, for
1\leq p<\infty
,
induces a continuous map
W^{1,q}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R})
.

(2)

If
A\in W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
is a family of density matrices (i.e.
\operatorname{Tr}(A)=1
) such that

\inf_{x\in U}\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\lambda_{i}(A(x))>0
, then the von Neumann entropy

-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(A)\log\lambda_{i}(A)
belongs to
W^{1,q}(U)
and varies continuously in
A
.

(3)

In the setup of (2), the Rényi entropy
\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(A)^{\alpha}\big)
, for
\alpha>1
,
belongs to
W^{1,q}(U)
and varies continuously in
A
.

1.5.2.
Condition numbers

Let
A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
and let
\sigma_{1}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{d}(A)
be the singular values of
A
.
In numerical analysis, the
condition number
of
A
is defined by

(1.18)

\kappa(A):=\frac{\sigma_{1}(A)}{\sigma_{d}(A)}.

Considering the linear equation
Ax=b
and assuming that
A
is nonsingular (i.e.,
\sigma_{d}(A)>0
),

\kappa(A)
describes the maximum ratio of the relative error in
x
to the relative error in
b
.
If
\kappa(A)
is much larger than
1
, then the problem is considered to be ill-conditioned.

Theorem 1.19
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
. Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Suppose that
A_{0}\in W^{1,q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
and
\inf_{x\in I}\sigma_{d}(A_{0}(x))>0
.
Then
\kappa(A)\in W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{R})
is well-defined in a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
A_{0}
in
W^{1,q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))

and
\kappa:\mathcal{U}\to W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{R})
is continuous.

Theorem

1.19
will be proved in
Section

5.4
.

Let

be a bounded open set and

1.7.
Notation

The
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{m}
.
If not stated otherwise, ‘measurable’ means ‘Lebesgue measurable’ and ‘almost everywhere’ means
‘almost everywhere with respect to Lebesgue measure’. For measurable
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we usually write

|E|=\mathcal{L}^{m}(E)
.
We will also use the
k
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{k}
.

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
\|x\|_{p}
denotes the
p
-norm of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If
f:E\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for measurable
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, is a measurable map, then we set

\|f\|_{L^{p}(E,\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\big\|\|f\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{p}(E)}.

In the following, a set is called
countable
if it is either finite or has the cardinality of
\mathbb{N}
.

We consider the standard action of the symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{d}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
by permuting the coordinates:
for
\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}
and
z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
,

\sigma z=\sigma(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=(z_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,z_{\sigma(d)}).

We write
X^{<2>}=\{(x,y)\in X\times X:x\neq y\}
for the cartesian product with the diagonal removed.

We use the notation
C(d,\ldots)
to denote a constant that depends only on
d,\ldots
; its value may change
from line to line.

2.
Function spaces

Let us fix notation and recall background on the function spaces used in this paper.

2.1.
Lebesgue spaces

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and
1\leq q\leq\infty
.
We denote by
L^{q}(U)
the Lebesgue space with respect to the
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{m}
,
and
\|\cdot\|_{L^{q}(U)}
is the corresponding
L^{q}
-norm.
We will also use the space
L^{q}_{\operatorname{loc}}(U)
of measurable functions
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\|f\|_{L^{q}(K)}<\infty
for all compact subsets
K\subseteq U
.
For Lebesgue measurable sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
we also write
|E|=\mathcal{L}^{m}(E)
.
We remark that for continuous functions
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
we have (and use interchangeably)

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}=\|f\|_{C^{0}(\overline{U})}
.

A map
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}):U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is measurable if and only if each
f_{i}
is measurable.
Now
f
belongs to
L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{n})
if
f_{i}\in L^{q}(U)
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, or equivalently

\|f\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{n})}:=\big\|\|f\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(U)}<\infty.

2.2.
Sobolev spaces

For
1\leq q\leq\infty
,
we consider the Sobolev space

W^{1,q}(U):=\{f\in L^{q}(U):\partial_{i}f\in L^{q}(U)\text{ for }1\leq i\leq m\},

where
\partial_{i}f
are distributional derivatives, endowed with the norm

\|f\|_{W^{1,q}(U)}:=\|f\|_{L^{q}(U)}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|\partial_{i}f\|_{L^{q}(U)}.

A map
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}):U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
belongs to
W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{n})
if
f_{j}\in W^{1,q}(U)
for all
1\leq j\leq m
,
or equivalently,

\|f\|_{W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{n})}:=\|f\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|\partial_{i}f\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval. Let
1\leq q<\infty
.
A function
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
belongs to
W^{1,q}(I)
if and only if
f
has an absolutely continuous
representative whose classical derivative belongs to
L^{q}(I)
.
Moreover,
f\in W^{1,\infty}(I)
if and only if
f
has a Lipschitz continuous representative.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be bounded and open. Let
1\leq q<\infty
.
A function
u\in L^{q}(U)
belongs to
W^{1,q}(U)
if and only if it has a representative
that is absolutely continuous on
\mathcal{L}^{m-1}
-almost all line segments in
U
that are parallel
to the coordinate axes and whose classical first-order partial derivatives belong to
L^{q}(U)
.
They coincide
\mathcal{L}^{m}
-almost everywhere with the weak derivatives of
f
.
If
U
is a Lipschitz domain, then
f\in W^{1,\infty}(U)
if and only if
f
has a Lipschitz continuous
representative.

2.3.
Lipschitz and Hölder spaces

Let
(M_{i},\mathsf{d}_{i})
,
i=1,2
, be metric spaces. A map
f:M_{1}\to M_{2}
is

\alpha
-Hölder
continuous, for
\alpha\in(0,1]
, if

|f|_{C^{0,\alpha}(M_{1},M_{2})}:=\sup_{x\neq y\in M_{1}}\frac{\mathsf{d}_{2}(f(x),f(y))}{\mathsf{d}_{1}(x,y)^{\alpha}}<\infty.

In the case
\alpha=1
,
f
is called
Lipschitz
continuous and we sometimes also write
\operatorname{Lip}(f)
for the Lipschitz constant

|f|_{C^{0,1}(M_{1},M_{2})}
.

Most often,
M_{1}
will be a bounded open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
M_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, both equipped with the metrics induced by the
2
-norm.
Then, for
\alpha\in(0,1]
, we consider the Banach space
C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{n})
consisting of continuous maps
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

that extend continuously to
\overline{U}
with the norm

\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{n})}:=\sup_{x\in U}\|f(x)\|_{2}+|f|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty.

We write
C^{0,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{R}^{n})
for the space of continuous functions on
U
that
belong to
C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{V},\mathbb{R}^{n})
for each relatively compact open
V\Subset U
,
and endow
C^{0,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{R}^{n})
with its natural Fréchet topology.

2.4.
Absolutely continuous curves in a metric space

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Let
1\leq q\leq\infty
.
A curve
\gamma:I\to X
in a complete metric space
(X,\mathsf{d})
belongs to
AC^{q}(I,X)
if there exists
m\in L^{q}(I)
such that

(2.1)

\mathsf{d}(\gamma(x),\gamma(y))\leq\int_{x}^{y}m(t)\,dt,\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in I,~x\leq y.

In that case, the limit

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{d}(\gamma(x+h),\gamma(x))}{|h|}=:|\dot{\gamma}|(x)

exists for almost every
x\in I
and is called the
metric speed
of
\gamma
at
x
.
Furthermore,
|\dot{\gamma}|\in L^{q}(I)
and (
2.1
) holds with
m
replaced by
|\dot{\gamma}|
;
one has
|\dot{\gamma}|\leq m
almost everywhere in
I
for every
m
that satisfies (
2.1
).
See
[
AGS08
, Definition 1.1.1]
.

For
1\leq q<\infty
,
the
q
-energy

\mathscr{E}_{q}(\gamma)
of
\gamma\in AC^{q}(I,X)
is defined by

(2.2)

\mathscr{E}_{q}(\gamma):=\int_{I}(|\dot{\gamma}|(t))^{q}\,dt.

We also write
AC
for
AC^{1}
.

2.5.
Vitali’s convergence theorem

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space with nonnegative measure
\mu
(finite or with values in
[0,\infty]
).
A set of functions
\mathcal{F}\subseteq L^{1}(\mu)
is called
uniformly integrable
if

\lim_{C\to+\infty}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\int_{|f|>C}|f|\,d\mu=0.

Theorem 2.1

(De la Vallée Poussin’s criterion
[
Bog07
, Theorem 4.5.9]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a finite nonnegative measure.
A family
\mathcal{F}
of
\mu
-integrable functions is uniformly integrable if and only if
there exists a nonnegative increasing function
G
on
[0,\infty)
such that

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{G(t)}{t}=\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\int G(|f(x)|)\,\mu(dx)<\infty.

In such a case, one can choose a convex increasing function
G
.

Recall that a sequence of complex valued measurable functions
f_{n}
on
X
is said to

converge in measure
to
f

if, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\mu(\{x\in X:|f(x)-f_{n}(x)|\geq\epsilon\})\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Theorem 2.2

(Vitali’s convergence theorem
[
Bog07
, Theorem 4.5.4]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a finite nonnegative measure.
Suppose that
f
is a
\mu
-measurable function and
\{f_{n}\}
is a sequence of
\mu
-integrable functions.
Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(1)

f_{n}\to f
in measure and
\{f_{n}\}
is uniformly integrable.

(2)

f
is integrable and
f_{n}\to f
in
L^{1}(\mu)
.

The next lemma is a simple application of Vitali’s convergence theorem which we state here for later reference.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a finite measure space.
Suppose
\{f_{n}\}
is a sequence of nonnegative
\mu
-integrable functions
and
\{X_{i}\}
is a countable cover of
X
by
\mu
-measurable sets.
If
\{f_{n}\}
is uniformly integrable and

\int_{X_{i}}f_{n}\,d\mu\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for each
i
,
then

\int_{X}f_{n}\,d\mu\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

For each
i
, there is a subsequence
(n^{i}_{k})
of
(n)
such that
f_{n^{i}_{k}}\to 0

\mu
-almost everywhere in
X_{i}
as
k\to\infty
.
By choosing the subsequences successively, we may assume that
(n^{i+1}_{k})
is a subsequence of
(n^{i}_{k})
for all
i
.
Then, for
n_{k}:=n_{k}^{k}
, we find that
f_{n_{k}}\to 0

\mu
-almost everywhere in
X
as
k\to\infty
.

Because
\{f_{n}\}
is uniformly integrable, Vitali’s convergence theorem
2.2
implies
that

\int_{X}f_{n_{k}}\,d\mu\to 0\quad\text{ as }k\to\infty;

noting that on a finite measure space almost everywhere convergence implies convergence in measure, by Egorov’s theorem.
This implies the assertion.
∎

2.6.
Slope-convergence

The results presented here are used in the proof of
Theorem

1.7
in
Section

5.3
.

Definition 2.4

(Slope convergence)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a bounded open set and
U^{<2>}:=\{(x,y)\in U\times U:x\neq y\}
.
For
f\in C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
consider the
slope function

s_{f}:U^{<2>}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

s_{f}(x,y):=\frac{\|f(x)-f(y)\|_{2}}{\|x-y\|_{2}}.

Let
f,f_{n}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
, for
n\geq 1
.
We say that
f_{n}

slope-converges
to
f
if

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|s_{f}-s_{f_{n}}\|_{L^{\infty}(U^{<2>})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

i.e., the slope functions converge uniformly.

We have the following characterization of slope convergence.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f,f_{n}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
, for
n\geq 1
.
Then
f_{n}
slope-converges to
f
if and only if,
for each subset
F\subseteq U^{<2>}
,

(2.4)

\sup_{F}s_{f_{n}}\to\sup_{F}s_{f}\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

In particular,
for each subset
E\subseteq U
, we have convergence of the Lipschitz constants,

(2.5)

|f_{n}|_{C^{0,1}(E,\mathbb{R}^{\ell})}\to|f|_{C^{0,1}(E,\mathbb{R}^{\ell})}\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

For arbitrary
(x,y)\in U^{<2>}
, we have

s_{f}(x,y)\leq s_{f_{n}}(x,y)+\|s_{f}-s_{f_{n}}\|_{L^{\infty}(U^{<2>})}

so that, for each subset
F\subseteq U^{<2>}
,

\sup_{(x,y)\in F}s_{f}(x,y)\leq\sup_{(x,y)\in F}s_{f_{n}}(x,y)+\|s_{f}-s_{f_{n}}\|_{L^{\infty}(U^{<2>})}.

Hence, by symmetry,

\Big|\sup_{(x,y)\in F}s_{f}(x,y)-\sup_{(x,y)\in F}s_{f_{n}}(x,y)\Big|\leq\|s_{f}-s_{f_{n}}\|_{L^{\infty}(U^{<2>})}

which shows that (
2.3
) implies (
2.4
).

Conversely, assume that (
2.3
) fails. Then, there exist
\epsilon>0
and sequences
n_{k}\to\infty
and
(x_{k},y_{k})\in U^{<2>}
such that

|s_{f}(x_{k},y_{k})-s_{f_{n_{k}}}(x_{k},y_{k})|\geq\epsilon.

After passing to a subsequence of
k
, we may assume that

s_{f}(x_{k},y_{k})-s_{f_{n_{k}}}(x_{k},y_{k})\geq\epsilon\quad\text{ or }\quad s_{f_{n_{k}}}(x_{k},y_{k})-s_{f}(x_{k},y_{k})\geq\epsilon.

In the former case,
setting
F:=\{(x_{k},y_{k}):k\geq 1\}
,

\sup_{(x,y)\in F}s_{f}(x,y)\geq\sup_{(x,y)\in F}s_{f_{n_{k}}}(x,y)+\epsilon

which contradicts (
2.4
). The latter case is analogous.
∎

The next lemma shows that
C^{0,1}
convergence implies slope convergence.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
f,f_{n}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
, for
n\geq 1
.
If

(2.6)

|f-f_{n}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

then
f_{n}
slope-converges to
f
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\big|s_{f}(x,y)-s_{f_{n}}(x,y)\big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\frac{\|f(x)-f(y)\|_{2}}{\|x-y\|_{2}}-\frac{\|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(y)\|_{2}}{\|x-y\|_{2}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|f(x)-f(y)-(f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(y))\|_{2}}{\|x-y\|_{2}}=s_{f-f_{n}}(x,y)

and hence

\displaystyle\sup_{(x,y)\in U^{<2>}}\big|s_{f}(x,y)-s_{f_{n}}(x,y)\big|\leq\sup_{(x,y)\in U^{<2>}}s_{f-f_{n}}(x,y)=|f-f_{n}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})}

which shows the assertion.
∎

3.
Hermitian and normal matrices

In this section, we recall some preliminaries on Hermitian and normal matrices.

3.1.
Matrix norms

Let
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
denote the vector space of complex
d\times d
matrices. The
operator norm
of
A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is given by

\|A\|_{\operatorname{op}}:=\sup\{\|Ax\|_{2}:x\in\mathbb{C}^{d},\,\|x\|_{2}=1\}.

We will also use the
Frobenius norm
or
2
-norm
of
A=(a_{ij})\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
,

\|A\|_{2}:=\big(\operatorname{Tr}(A^{*}A)\big)^{1/2}=\Big(\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}|a_{ij}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

If
\sigma_{1}(A)\geq\sigma_{2}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{d}(A)\geq 0
are the decreasingly ordered singular values of
A
, then

\|A\|_{\operatorname{op}}=\sigma_{1}(A)\quad\text{ and }\quad\|A\|_{2}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{j}(A)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Both operator norm and Frobenius norm are unitarily invariant (i.e.,
\|A\|_{\operatorname{op}}=\|UAV\|_{\operatorname{op}}
and
\|A\|_{2}=\|UAV\|_{2}

for unitary
U,V
). It is easy to see that

\|A\|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq\|A\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}\,\|A\|_{\operatorname{op}}.

If
A
is normal and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
are the eigenvalues of
A
, then

\|A\|_{\operatorname{op}}=\max_{1\leq j\leq d}|\lambda_{j}|\quad\text{ and }\quad\|A\|_{2}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

If not stated otherwise, we use the Frobenius norm on
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
(and on its subsets).

3.2.
Spectral variation of Hermitian and normal matrices

Recall that
\lambda^{\uparrow}:\operatorname{Herm}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the map assigning to a Hermitian
d\times d
its

d
-tuple of increasingly ordered eigenvalues; see (
1.1
).

Proposition 3.1

(Weyl’s perturbation theorem
[
Wey12
]
; see e.g.
[
Bha97
, III.2.6]
)
.

Let
A,B\in\operatorname{Herm}(d)
. Then

(3.1)

\|\lambda^{\uparrow}(A)-\lambda^{\uparrow}(B)\|_{\infty}\leq\|A-B\|_{\operatorname{op}}.

In
[
Bha97
]
, the result is stated for eigenvalue vectors with decreasing eigenvalues, but reversing the order evidently
leaves the left-hand side of (
3.1
) unchanged.

Most of the time we will work with
2
-norms for which the following variant due to Löwner
[
Löw34
]
holds.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
Löw34
]
, see e.g.
[
Bha97
, III.6.15]
)
.

Let
A,B\in\operatorname{Herm}(d)
. Then

(3.2)

\|\lambda^{\uparrow}(A)-\lambda^{\uparrow}(B)\|_{2}\leq\|A-B\|_{2}.

Löwner’s result was generalized to normal matrices by Hoffmann and Wielandt
[
HW53
]
.
There is no canonical order on the spectrum of a normal matrix. Instead we consider the map

\Lambda:\operatorname{Norm}(d)\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
which takes a normal
d\times d
matrix to its unordered
d
-tuple of eigenvalues;
see (
1.8
). Recall that
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
carries the metric
\mathbf{d}_{2}([z],[w])=\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\|z-\sigma w\|_{2}
, where

\sigma w=\sigma(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})=(w_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,w_{\sigma(d)})
.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
HW53
]
, see e.g.
[
Bha97
, VI.4.1]
)
.

Let
A,B\in\operatorname{Norm}(d)
.
Then

(3.3)

\mathbf{d}_{2}(\Lambda(A),\Lambda(B))\leq\|A-B\|_{2}.

If
A
and
B
are Hermitian, then (
3.3
) reduces to (
3.2
); see
Lemma

8.3
.

With respect to the operator norm, there is the following result due to Bhatia, Davis, and McIntosh
[
BDM83
]
,
where
\mathbf{d}_{\infty}([z],[w])=\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\|z-\sigma w\|_{\infty}
.

Proposition 3.4

(
[
BDM83
]
, see e.g.
[
Bha97
, VII.4.1]
)
.

Let
A,B\in\operatorname{Norm}(d)
.
Then

(3.4)

\mathbf{d}_{\infty}(\Lambda(A),\Lambda(B))\leq C\,\|A-B\|_{\operatorname{op}},

where
C
is a universal constant satisfying
1<C<3
.

3.3.
The characteristic map for Hermitian matrices

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
1\leq q\leq\infty
.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and bounded.
Then the characteristic map

\mathcal{E}:W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A

is well-defined and bounded, satisfying

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}(A)(x)\|_{2}\leq\|A(x)\|_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\nabla(\mathcal{E}(A))(x)\|_{2}\leq\|\nabla A(x)\|_{2}

for almost every
x\in U
.

Proof.

It is well-known (see
[
ADM90
]
) that superposition with the
1
-Lipschitz map
\lambda^{\uparrow}

(see
Proposition

3.2
)
defines a bounded map from
W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
to
W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where

\|\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A\|_{2}\leq\|A\|_{2},

because
\lambda^{\uparrow}(0)=0
, and

\|\nabla(\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A)\|_{2}\leq\|\nabla A\|_{2}

almost everywhere in
U
.
This implies the assertion.
∎

In
Proposition

9.1
, we will encounter a variant of
Proposition

3.5
for normal matrices;
its formulation requires some background on multivalued Sobolev functions, which will be recalled in
Section

8
.

4.
Uniform block-diagonalization of Hermitian and normal matrices

4.1.
Splitting lemma for complex triangular matrices

The following splitting lemma for complex triangular matrices follows from the proof of Hensel’s lemma
of
[
PR20
, Lemma 2.1]
which is stated there in a different setup,
namely for normal matrices over the ring of formal power series
\mathbb{C}\llbracket X\rrbracket
,
where
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})
.

We shall state this lemma in the Nash real algebraic setup because the unitary group
U_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is not
a complex manifold but only a nonsingular real algebraic variety. Recall that a map is a real Nash map
if it is real analytic and its graph is semialgebraic. Nash maps between open subsets of nonsingular real
algebraic varieties form the smallest family of maps that contains the polynomial morphisms and admits the
Implicit Function Theorem, see e.g.
[
BCR98
]
for more on Nash maps.

Lemma 4.1

(Local unitary block-triangularization of matrices)
.

Let
A_{0}\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be a complex block-triangular matrix,

\displaystyle A_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{0}&0\\
D_{0}&C_{0}\end{pmatrix},

with
B_{0}\in M_{d_{1}}(\mathbb{C})
,
C_{0}\in M_{d_{2}}(\mathbb{C})
,
D_{0}\in M_{d_{2},d_{1}}(\mathbb{C})
, for
d=d_{1}+d_{2}
, and such that the characteristic polynomials of
B_{0}
and

C_{0}
are coprime.
Then there is a neighborhood
\mathcal{V}
of
A_{0}
in
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
and a Nash map to the unitary matrices,

U:\mathcal{V}\to U_{d}(\mathbb{C}),\quad U(A_{0})=\mathbb{I}_{d},

such that, for all
A\in\mathcal{V}
,

(4.1)

\displaystyle U^{*}(A)A\,U(A)=\begin{pmatrix}B(A)&0\\
D(A)&C(A)\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

Consider

\displaystyle\Psi=(\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2},\Psi_{3},\Psi_{4}):U_{d}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{d_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{d_{2}}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{d_{2},d_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{d}(\mathbb{C}),

defined by

\displaystyle(U,Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3})\to U^{*}\begin{pmatrix}B_{0}+Y_{1}&0\\
D_{0}+Y_{3}&C_{0}+Y_{2}\end{pmatrix}U=\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{1}&\Psi_{4}\\
\Psi_{3}&\Psi_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Recall that a tangent vector at
\mathbb{I}_{d}
to
U_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is a matrix
\mathbf{u}
that is skew-Hermitian,

\mathbf{u}=-\mathbf{u}^{*}
. We shall write it as

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{z}_{1}&\mathbf{x}\\
-\mathbf{x}^{*}&\mathbf{z}_{2}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathbf{z}_{1}
and
\mathbf{z}_{2}
are skew-Hermitian.
The differential of
\Psi
at
(\mathbb{I}_{d},0,0,0)
on the vector
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{3})

is given by

\displaystyle d\Psi_{1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{3})=\mathbf{y}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{1}B_{0}+B_{0}\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{x}D_{0},

\displaystyle d\Psi_{2}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{3})=\mathbf{y}_{2}-\mathbf{z}_{2}C_{0}+C_{0}\mathbf{z}_{2}+D_{0}\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle d\Psi_{3}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{3})=\mathbf{y}_{3}+D_{0}\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}D_{0}+\mathbf{x}^{*}B_{0}-C_{0}\mathbf{x}^{*},

\displaystyle d\Psi_{4}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{3})=B_{0}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}C_{0}.

This differential is a linear epimorphism if and only if so is
d\Psi_{4}
and the latter holds true thanks
to the following lemma of
[
Coh84
, Lemma 2.3]
, see also
[
Zur93
]
or
[
PR20
, Lemma 2.4]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
R
be an unitary commutative ring,
A\in M_{p}(R)
,
B\in M_{q}(R)
,
C\in M_{p,q}(R)
, such that
the characteristic polynomials
P_{A}
and
P_{B}
are coprime, i.e., there exist polynomials
U
and
V
such that
UP_{A}+VP_{B}=1
. Then there is a
matrix
M\in M_{p,q}(R)
such that
AM-MB=C
.

By the Nash IFT, see e.g.
[
BCR98
, Corollary 2.9.8]
,
there exist a neighborhood
\mathcal{V}
of
A_{0}
in
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})

and a Nash map

\Phi=(U,Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3}):\mathcal{V}\to U_{d}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{d_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{d_{2}}(\mathbb{C})\times M_{d_{2},d_{1}}(\mathbb{C}),

\Phi(A_{0})=(\mathbb{I}_{d},0,0,0)
, that satisfies
\Psi\circ\Phi(A)=A
for
A\in\mathcal{V}
.
Thus it suffices to take
U(A):=\Phi_{1}^{*}(A)
.
∎

Corollary 4.3

(Local unitary block-diagonalization of normal matrices)
.

With the assumptions of
Lemma

4.1
if, moreover,

A_{0}
is normal (resp. Hermitian) then
A_{0}
is block-diagonal, i.e.,
D_{0}=0
, and
thus for all normal (resp. Hermitian)
A\in\mathcal{V}

the matrix

(4.2)

\displaystyle U^{*}(A)A\,U(A)=\begin{pmatrix}B(A)&0\\
0&C(A)\end{pmatrix}

is normal (resp. Hermitian).

Proof.

It immediately follows from the facts that conjugation by unitary matrices preserves normal (resp. Hermitian) matrices,
and that every block-triangular normal matrix is block-diagonal.
∎

4.2.
Uniform unitary block-diagonalization

Definition 4.4

(Spaces of Hermitian and normal matrices)
.

Let
\operatorname{Herm}_{T}(d)
(resp.
\operatorname{Norm}_{T}(d)
) denote the space of Hermitian (resp. normal)

d\times d
matrices with trace zero and
\operatorname{Herm}_{T}^{0}(d)
(resp.
\operatorname{Norm}_{T}^{0}(d)
)
its compact subspace of matrices of norm
1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Herm}_{T}(d)

\displaystyle=\{A\in\operatorname{Herm}(d):\operatorname{Tr}(A)=0\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Herm}_{T}^{0}(d)

\displaystyle=\{A\in\operatorname{Herm}_{T}(d):\|A\|_{2}=1\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Norm}_{T}(d)

\displaystyle=\{A\in\operatorname{Norm}(d):\operatorname{Tr}(A)=0\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Norm}_{T}^{0}(d)

\displaystyle=\{A\in\operatorname{Norm}_{T}(d):\|A\|_{2}=1\}.

Let
A_{0}\in\operatorname{Norm}_{T}(d)\setminus\{0\}
. Then
A_{0}
has at least two distinct eigenvalues.
Therefore,
after a unitary change of coordinates, we may assume that
A_{0}
is block-diagonal,

(4.3)

\displaystyle A_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{0}&0\\
0&C_{0}\end{pmatrix},

where
B_{0},C_{0}
are normal and the resultant of the characteristic polynomials of
B_{0}
and
C_{0}
is nonzero.

If
A_{0}\in\operatorname{Herm}_{T}(d)\setminus\{0\}
, then
B_{0},C_{0}
are Hermitian.
In this case,
we may assume furthermore that the largest eigenvalue of
B_{0}
is strictly smaller than the smallest eigenvalue of
C_{0}

By

4.3.
Pointwise bounds for absolutely continuous curves of Hermitian and normal matrices

Let us fix a uniform unitary block-diagonalization
(\{U_{i}:\mathcal{V}_{i}\to U_{d}(\mathbb{C})\}_{i=1}^{s},r,\chi)
for
\operatorname{Norm}_{T}^{0}(d)

for the rest of the section.
Fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
and let us simply write
U:\mathcal{V}\to U_{d}(\mathbb{C})
for
U_{i}:\mathcal{V}_{i}\to U_{d}(\mathbb{C})
.
Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Then
U
induces a map

\displaystyle U_{*}:AC(I,\mathcal{V})\to AC(I,U_{d}(\mathbb{C})),\quad A\mapsto U\circ A

and the chain rule holds;
see e.g.
[
MM72
]
.

In the following, we use the norm

\|f\|_{C^{k}}:=\max_{0\leq j\leq k}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\|d^{j}f(x)\|_{L_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})}

on the space
C^{k}(\overline{\mathcal{V}},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
, where
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is open and bounded
and
L_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
is the space of
j
-linear maps with
j
arguments in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and values in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}\in AC(I,\mathcal{V})
. Then, for every
x\in I
,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\|U_{*}(A_{1})(x)-U_{*}(A_{2})(x)\|_{2}\leq\|U\|_{C^{1}}\|A_{1}(x)-A_{2}(x)\|_{2}

and, for almost every
x\in I
and
j=1,2
,

\displaystyle\|(U_{*}(A_{1}))^{\prime}(x)-(U_{*}(A_{2}))^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}

(4.10)

\displaystyle\leq\|U\|_{C^{2}}\big(\|A_{j}^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}\|A_{1}(x)-A_{2}(x)\|_{2}+\|A_{1}^{\prime}(x)-A_{2}^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}\big).

Proof.

The mean value theorem implies (
4.9
).
We have

\displaystyle\|(U_{*}(A_{1}))^{\prime}(x)-(U_{*}(A_{2}))^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}=\|dU(A_{1}(x))A_{1}^{\prime}(x)-dU(A_{2}(x))A_{2}^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|(dU(A_{1}(x))-dU(A_{2}(x)))A_{1}^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}+\|dU(A_{2}(x))(A_{1}^{\prime}(x)-A_{2}^{\prime}(x))\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|U\|_{C^{2}}\|A_{1}(x)-A_{2}(x)\|_{2}\|A_{1}^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}+\|U\|_{C^{1}}\|A_{1}^{\prime}(x)-A_{2}^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}

which implies (
4.6
) for
j=1
. The assertion for
j=2
follows by symmetry.
∎

Let
A\in AC(I,\operatorname{Norm}_{T}(d))
and assume that
0\not\in A(I)
. Then

\underline{A}(x):=\frac{A(x)}{\|A(x)\|_{2}}\in\operatorname{Norm}_{T}^{0}(d)

is well-defined for
x\in I
.
If
\underline{A}(I)\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, then

\displaystyle U_{i}^{*}(\underline{A})\underline{A}\,U_{i}(\underline{A})=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{B}_{i}(\underline{A})&0\\
0&\underline{C}_{i}(\underline{A})\end{pmatrix}

and consequently

(4.11)

\displaystyle U_{i}^{*}(\underline{A})A\,U_{i}(\underline{A})=\begin{pmatrix}B_{i}(A)&0\\
0&C_{i}(A)\end{pmatrix},

where
B_{i}(A)=\|A\|_{2}\cdot\underline{B}_{i}(\underline{A})
and
C_{i}(A)=\|A\|_{2}\cdot\underline{C}_{i}(\underline{A})
are normal.

By the property (
4.8
), there exists

\chi>0
such that the following holds.
Assume that
A_{1},A_{2}\in AC(I,\operatorname{Norm}_{T}(d)\setminus\{0\})
and
\underline{A}_{1}(I),\underline{A}_{2}(I)\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
.
Then, for all
x\in I
,

(4.12)

\big|\|A_{2}(x)\|_{2}\,\mu_{1}(x)-\|A_{1}(x)\|_{2}\,\nu_{2}(x)\big|>\chi\,\|A_{1}(x)\|_{2}\|A_{2}(x)\|_{2}

for all eigenvalues
\mu_{1}
of
B_{i}(A_{1})
and all eigenvalues
\nu_{2}
of
C_{i}(A_{2})
,
where
B_{i}(A_{1})
and
C_{i}(A_{2})
are defined by (
4.11
).

If
A\in AC(I,\operatorname{Herm}_{T}(d))
, then
\underline{A}
,
B_{i}(A)
, and
C_{i}(A)
are Hermitian
and the largest eigenvalue of
B_{i}(A)
is strictly smaller than the smallest eigenvalue of
C_{i}(A)
, on
I
, i.e.,

(4.13)

\mathcal{E}(A)=(\mathcal{E}(B_{i}(A)),\mathcal{E}(C_{i}(A))).

In
Lemma

4.8
, we derive pointwise bounds for the difference of the expression (
4.11
)
for two absolutely continuous curves
A_{1},A_{2}
of normal matrices.
In particular, they apply to Hermitian matrices.
We need the following preliminary lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
A\in AC(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
and assume that
0\not\in A(I)
.
Then, almost everywhere in
I
,

(4.14)

\displaystyle\|A\|_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\operatorname{Tr}(\underline{A}^{*}A^{\prime}),

(4.15)

\displaystyle\big|\|A\|_{2}^{\prime}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{\prime}\|_{2},

(4.16)

\displaystyle\|\underline{A}^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\frac{\|A^{\prime}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\|A\|_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{Tr}(A^{*}A)^{1/2})^{\prime}=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(A^{*}A^{\prime})+\operatorname{Tr}((A^{*})^{\prime}A)}{2\|A\|_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(A^{*}A^{\prime})+\operatorname{Tr}((A^{\prime})^{*}A)}{2\|A\|_{2}}=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(A^{*}A^{\prime})+\overline{\operatorname{Tr}(A^{*}A^{\prime})}}{2\|A\|_{2}}=\mathrm{Re}\operatorname{Tr}(\underline{A}^{*}A^{\prime}).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\big|\|A\|_{2}^{\prime}\big|\leq\|\underline{A}^{*}\|_{2}\|A^{\prime}\|_{2}=\|A^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Finally,

\displaystyle\|\underline{A}^{\prime}\|_{2}=\Big\|\frac{A^{\prime}\|A\|_{2}-A\|A\|_{2}^{\prime}}{\|A\|_{2}^{2}}\Big\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|A^{\prime}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}+\frac{\big|\|A\|_{2}^{\prime}\big|}{\|A\|_{2}}\leq 2\,\frac{\|A^{\prime}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}

and the lemma is proved.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

For
j=1,2
, let
A_{j}\in AC(I,\operatorname{Norm}_{T}(d))
be such that
0\not\in A_{j}(I)
.
Suppose that
\underline{A}_{1}(I),\underline{A}_{2}(I)\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
.
For simplicity, we write
U:\mathcal{V}\to U_{d}(\mathbb{C})
for
U_{i}:\mathcal{V}_{i}\to U_{d}(\mathbb{C})
.
Then, pointwise on
I
,

(4.17)

\displaystyle\|U^{*}(\underline{A}_{1})A_{1}U(\underline{A}_{1})-U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2})A_{2}U(\underline{A}_{2})\|_{2}\leq C\,\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}

and, almost everywhere on
I
and for
j=1,2
,

\displaystyle\|

\displaystyle(U^{*}(\underline{A}_{1})A_{1}U(\underline{A}_{1}))^{\prime}-(U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2})A_{2}U(\underline{A}_{2}))^{\prime}\|_{2}

(4.18)

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\,\Bigl({\|A^{\prime}_{1}-A^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}}+\big(\|A^{\prime}_{1}\|_{2}+\|A^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}\big)\|A_{j}\|_{2}^{-1}\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}\Bigr),

where
C
is a universal constant depending only on
d
.

Proof.

We have, for
j=1,2
,

(4.19)

\|\underline{A}_{1}-\underline{A}_{2}\|_{2}\leq 2\,\|A_{j}\|_{2}^{-1}\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2},

since

\displaystyle\|\underline{A}_{1}-\underline{A}_{2}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\frac{A_{1}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}}-\frac{A_{2}}{\|A_{2}\|_{2}}\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}+\|A_{2}\|_{2}\,\big|\|A_{1}\|_{2}-\|A_{2}\|_{2}\big|}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}}\leq 2\,\frac{\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}}

and the case
j=2
follows by symmetry.
Thus, by (
4.9
) and (
4.19
),

\displaystyle\|U^{*}(\underline{A}_{1})A_{1}U(\underline{A}_{1})-U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2})A_{2}U(\underline{A}_{2})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|(U^{*}(\underline{A}_{1})-U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2}))A_{1}\,U(\underline{A}_{1})\|_{2}+\|U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2})A_{1}(U(\underline{A}_{1})-U(\underline{A}_{2})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\|U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2})(A_{1}-A_{2})U(\underline{A}_{2})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|\underline{A}_{1}-\underline{A}_{2}\|_{2}+\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}\lesssim\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}

showing (
4.17
).

Let us prove (
4.8
).
By (
4.14
),

\displaystyle\big|\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{\prime}-\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{\prime}\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\mathrm{Re}\operatorname{Tr}(\underline{A}_{1}^{*}A_{1}^{\prime})-\mathrm{Re}\operatorname{Tr}(\underline{A}_{2}^{*}A_{2}^{\prime})\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\mathrm{Re}\operatorname{Tr}((\underline{A}_{1}^{*}-\underline{A}_{2}^{*})A_{1}^{\prime})+\mathrm{Re}\operatorname{Tr}(\underline{A}_{2}^{*}(A_{1}^{\prime}-A_{2}^{\prime}))\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\|\underline{A}_{1}^{*}-\underline{A}_{2}^{*}\|_{2}\|A_{1}^{\prime}\|_{2}+\|\underline{A}_{2}^{*}\|_{2}\|A_{1}^{\prime}-A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\underline{A}_{1}-\underline{A}_{2}\|_{2}\|A_{1}^{\prime}\|_{2}+\|A_{1}^{\prime}-A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2},

so that by symmetry, for
j=1,2
,

\big|\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{\prime}-\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{\prime}\big|\leq\|\underline{A}_{1}-\underline{A}_{2}\|_{2}\|A_{j}^{\prime}\|_{2}+\|A_{1}^{\prime}-A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Using (
4.19
), we conclude, for
j=1,2
,

(4.20)

\big|\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{\prime}-\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{\prime}\big|\lesssim\frac{\|A_{j}^{\prime}\|_{2}}{\|A_{j}\|_{2}}\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}+\|A_{1}^{\prime}-A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}.

We claim that

(4.21)

\|\underline{A}^{\prime}_{1}-\underline{A}^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}\lesssim\frac{\|A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}}+\frac{\|A^{\prime}_{1}-A^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}}.

Indeed

\displaystyle\|\underline{A}^{\prime}_{1}-\underline{A}^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\frac{A^{\prime}_{1}\|A_{1}\|_{2}-A_{1}\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{\prime}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}}-\frac{A^{\prime}_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}-A_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{\prime}}{\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\frac{A^{\prime}_{1}\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2}-A^{\prime}_{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\Big\|\frac{A_{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{\prime}-A_{1}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{\prime}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big\|_{2}.

The first summand equals

\displaystyle\Big\|\frac{A^{\prime}_{1}\|A_{2}\|_{2}-A^{\prime}_{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}}\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\frac{(A^{\prime}_{1}-A^{\prime}_{2})\|A_{2}\|_{2}-A^{\prime}_{2}(\|A_{1}\|_{2}-\|A_{2}\|_{2})}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}}\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\|A^{\prime}_{1}-A^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}}+\frac{\|A^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|\|A_{2}\|_{2}}

and, by (
4.15
) and (
4.20
),
the second summand is bounded by

\displaystyle\Big\|

\displaystyle\frac{\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{\prime}(A_{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}-A_{1}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2})}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big\|_{2}+\Big\|\frac{A_{1}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2}(\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{\prime}-\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{\prime})}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big|\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{\prime}\big|\,\big\|A_{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}-A_{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}+A_{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}-A_{1}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\big\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\big|\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{\prime}-\|A_{2}\|_{2}^{\prime}\big|}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\|A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}\,\big|\|A_{1}\|_{2}-\|A_{2}\|_{2}\big|}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}}+\frac{\|A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}\,\big\|A_{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}-A_{1}\|A_{2}\|_{2}\big\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\|A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}}+\frac{\|A^{\prime}_{1}-A^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\|A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|A_{2}\|_{2}}+\frac{\|A^{\prime}_{1}-A^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}}

and (
4.21
) follows.

By (
4.6
), (
4.16
), (
4.19
), and (
4.21
),

(4.22)

\displaystyle\|(U(\underline{A}_{1})-U(\underline{A}_{2}))^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\underline{A}_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}\|\underline{A}_{1}-\underline{A}_{2}\|_{2}+\|\underline{A}_{1}^{\prime}-\underline{A}_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\|A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}}{\|A_{2}\|_{2}}\frac{\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}}+\frac{\|A^{\prime}_{1}-A^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}}{\|A_{1}\|_{2}},

similarly for
U^{*}
.

Finally we want to bound

\displaystyle\|(U^{*}(\underline{A}_{1})A_{1}U(\underline{A}_{1}))^{\prime}-(U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2})A_{2}U(\underline{A}_{2}))^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|((U^{*}(\underline{A}_{1})-U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2}))A_{1}U(\underline{A}_{1}))^{\prime}\|_{2}+\|(U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2})A_{1}(U(\underline{A}_{1})-U(\underline{A}_{2})))^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\|(U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2})(A_{1}-A_{2})U(\underline{A}_{2}))^{\prime}\|_{2}.

The first summand can be bounded by

\displaystyle\|(U^{*}(\underline{A}_{1})-U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2}))^{\prime}\|_{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}+\|U^{*}(\underline{A}_{1})-U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2})\|_{2}\|A^{\prime}_{1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\|U^{*}(\underline{A}_{1})-U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2})\|_{2}\|A_{1}\|_{2}\|(U(\underline{A}_{1}))^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{(\|A_{1}^{\prime}\|_{2}+\|A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2})}{\|A_{2}\|_{2}}\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}+\|A^{\prime}_{1}-A^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2},

using (
4.9
), (
4.16
), (
4.19
), and (
4.23
).
The bound for the second summand is similar and the third summand can be bounded by

\displaystyle\|(U^{*}(\underline{A}_{2}))^{\prime}\|_{2}\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}+\|A_{1}^{\prime}-A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}+\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}\|U(\underline{A}_{2}))^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\|A_{2}^{\prime}\|_{2}}{\|A_{2}\|_{2}}\|A_{1}-A_{2}\|_{2}+\|A^{\prime}_{1}-A^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}.

Thus (
4.8
) follows.
∎

Theorem 5.1

Let

5.1.
Preliminary observations and reductions

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
A\in W^{1,q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
, where
1\leq q<\infty
and

I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a bounded open interval. Then, for all
x\in I
,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\|A(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|I|^{-1/q}\,\|A\|_{L^{q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}+|I|^{1-1/q}\,\|A^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}.

Proof.

For
x,y\in I
, we have

A(x)-A(y)=\int_{y}^{x}A^{\prime}(t)\,dt

and integrating over
y
, we get

\displaystyle A(x)-A_{I}=\frac{1}{|I|}\int_{I}\int_{y}^{x}A^{\prime}(t)\,dt\,dy,

where
A_{I}:=|I|^{-1}\int_{I}A(y)\,dy
. Therefore,

\|A(x)-A_{I}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{|I|}\int_{I}\int_{I}\|A^{\prime}(t)\|_{2}\,dt\,dy=\|A^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\|A(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{I}\|_{2}+\|A(x)-A_{I}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|I|^{-1}\,\|A\|_{L^{1}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}+\|A^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}

\displaystyle\leq|I|^{-1/q}\,\|A\|_{L^{q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}+|I|^{1-1/q}\,\|A^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))},

by Hölder’s inequality.
∎

and

with

(3)

on

we have

as

.

Proof.

The existence of

,
n_{0}
, and

such that

, for
n\geq n_{0}
,
follows from

-convergence entails

The other assertions follow easily from (

), and

4.8
.
∎

By

,

, and

.

5.4.
Stability of the condition number of matrices

We finish this section with the proof of
Theorem

1.19
.

Recall that the condition number of a nonsingular matrix
A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is the ratio of the largest by the smallest
singular value:

(5.19)

\kappa(A)=\frac{\sigma_{1}(A)}{\sigma_{d}(A)}.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
. Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Suppose that
A_{0}\in W^{1,q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
and
\inf_{x\in I}\sigma_{d}(A_{0}(x))>0
.
We must show that

\kappa(A)\in W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{R})
is well-defined in a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
A_{0}
in
W^{1,q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))

and that the induced map
\kappa:\mathcal{U}\to W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{R})
is continuous.

By the continuity of
\sigma_{*}:W^{1,q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))\to W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, due to
Theorem

1.17
,
and by
Lemma

5.2
and (
1.16
),
there is a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
A_{0}
in
W^{1,q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
such that

(5.20)

\delta:=\inf_{A\in\mathcal{U}}\inf_{x\in I}\sigma_{d}(A(x))>0.

Thus the map
\kappa:\mathcal{U}\to W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{R})
is well-defined.

To see continuity, let
A,B\in\mathcal{U}
. Then, in
I
,

(5.21)

\displaystyle(\kappa(A)-\kappa(B))\sigma_{d}(A)\sigma_{d}(B)=(\sigma_{1}(A)-\sigma_{1}(B))\sigma_{d}(B)-\sigma_{1}(B)(\sigma_{d}(A)-\sigma_{d}(B))

and, almost everywhere in
I
,

(5.22)

\displaystyle(\kappa(A)^{\prime}-\kappa(B)^{\prime})\sigma_{d}(A)\sigma_{d}(B)+(\kappa(A)-\kappa(B))\sigma_{d}(A)^{\prime}\sigma_{d}(B)

(5.23)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+(\kappa(A)-\kappa(B))\sigma_{d}(A)\sigma_{d}(B)^{\prime}

(5.24)

\displaystyle\quad=(\sigma_{1}(A)^{\prime}-\sigma_{1}(B)^{\prime})\sigma_{d}(B)-\sigma_{1}(B)^{\prime}(\sigma_{d}(A)-\sigma_{d}(B))

(5.25)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+(\sigma_{1}(A)-\sigma_{1}(B))\sigma_{d}(B)^{\prime}-\sigma_{1}(B)(\sigma_{d}(A)^{\prime}-\sigma_{d}(B)^{\prime}).

We see that, in
I
,

\displaystyle|\kappa(A)-\kappa(B)|\leq\frac{\|B\|_{2}}{\delta^{2}}\big(|\sigma_{1}(A)-\sigma_{1}(B)|+|\sigma_{d}(A)-\sigma_{d}(B)|\big)

and, almost everywhere in
I
,

\displaystyle|\kappa(A)^{\prime}-\kappa(B)^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\Big(\|B\|_{2}|\sigma_{1}(A)^{\prime}-\sigma_{1}(B)^{\prime}|+|\sigma_{1}(B)^{\prime}||\sigma_{d}(A)-\sigma_{d}(B)|

\displaystyle\qquad+|\sigma_{d}(B)^{\prime}||\sigma_{1}(A)-\sigma_{1}(B)|+\|B\|_{2}|\sigma_{d}(A)^{\prime}-\sigma_{d}(B)^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\qquad+\big(\|B\|_{2}|\sigma_{d}(A)^{\prime}|+\|A\|_{2}|\sigma_{d}(B)^{\prime}|\big)|\kappa(A)-\kappa(B)|\Big).

Now continuity of
\kappa:\mathcal{U}\to W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{R})
can be easily deduced from
Theorem

1.17
, in view of
Lemma

5.2
and (
1.16
).
The proof of
Theorem

1.19
is complete.

6.1.
Proof of
Theorem

1.1

The next
Theorem

6.1
implies
Theorem

1.1

because
W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
is first-countable.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and bounded.
Let
A_{n}\to A
in
W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
as
n\to\infty
.
Then
\mathcal{E}(A_{n})\to\mathcal{E}(A)
in
W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Set
\lambda:=\mathcal{E}(A)
and
\lambda_{n}:=\mathcal{E}(A_{n})
.
By (
3.2
), we have

\|\lambda-\lambda_{n}\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|A-A_{n}\|_{L^{q}(U,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Hence we have to show that

(6.1)

\|\partial_{j}\lambda-\partial_{j}\lambda_{n}\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for all
1\leq j\leq m
.
It is enough to show that there is a subsequence
(n_{k})
with this property.

Let us first assume that
U=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{m}
is an open box with sides parallel to the coordinate axes.
Let
j=1
.
We may assume that
A
and
A_{n}
are absolutely continuous on almost all line segments in
U
parallel to the
x_{1}
-axis
and
\partial_{1}A\in L^{q}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
.
Let
x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})
for

x^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}=I_{2}\times\cdots\times I_{m}
.
By Tonelli’s theorem,

\int_{U^{\prime}}\int_{I_{1}}\|A(x_{1},x^{\prime})-A_{n}(x_{1},x^{\prime})\|_{2}^{q}\,dx_{1}\,dx^{\prime}=\|A-A_{n}\|_{L^{q}(U,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}^{q}

and

\int_{U^{\prime}}\int_{I_{1}}\|\partial_{1}A(x_{1},x^{\prime})-\partial_{1}A_{n}(x_{1},x^{\prime})\|_{2}^{q}\,dx_{1}\,dx^{\prime}=\|\partial_{1}A-\partial_{1}A_{n}\|_{L^{q}(U,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}^{q}.

Thus there is a subsequence
(n_{k})
such that

\int_{I_{1}}\|A(x_{1},x^{\prime})-A_{n_{k}}(x_{1},x^{\prime})\|_{2}^{q}\,dx_{1}\to 0

and

\int_{I_{1}}\|\partial_{1}A(x_{1},x^{\prime})-\partial_{1}A_{n_{k}}(x_{1},x^{\prime})\|_{2}^{q}\,dx_{1}\to 0

for almost every
x^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}
as
k\to\infty
.
By
Theorem

5.1
,

F_{1,n_{k}}(x^{\prime}):=\int_{I_{1}}\|\partial_{1}\lambda(x_{1},x^{\prime})-\partial_{1}\lambda_{n_{k}}(x_{1},x^{\prime})\|_{2}^{q}\,dx_{1}\to 0\quad\text{ as }k\to\infty

for almost every
x^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}
.

By Vitali’s convergence theorem
2.2
,

\|\partial_{1}A-\partial_{1}A_{n}\|_{L^{q}(U,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
implies that the set

\{\|\partial_{1}A\|_{2}^{q}+\|\partial_{1}A_{n}\|_{2}^{q}:n\geq 1\}\subseteq L^{1}(U)

is uniformly integrable and thus, by de la Vallée Poussin’s criterion
2.1
,
there is a nonnegative increasing convex function
G
on
[0,\infty)

such that
G(t)/t\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
and

\sup_{n\geq 1}\int_{U}G(2^{q}(\|\partial_{1}A(x)\|_{2}^{q}+\|\partial_{1}A_{n}(x)\|_{2}^{q}))\,dx<\infty.

We claim that
\{|I_{1}|^{-1}F_{1,n_{k}}:k\geq 1\}
and
thus
\{F_{1,n_{k}}:k\geq 1\}\subseteq L^{1}(U^{\prime})
is uniformly integrable.
By Tonelli’s theorem, Jensen’s inequality, and (
3.5
),

\displaystyle\int_{U^{\prime}}G(|I_{1}|^{-1}F_{1,n_{k}}(x^{\prime}))\,dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{U^{\prime}}G\Big(\int_{I_{1}}\|\partial_{1}\lambda(x_{1},x^{\prime})-\partial_{1}\lambda_{n_{k}}(x_{1},x^{\prime})\|_{2}^{q}\,\frac{dx_{1}}{|I_{1}|}\Big)\,dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{U^{\prime}}\int_{I_{1}}G(\|\partial_{1}\lambda(x_{1},x^{\prime})-\partial_{1}\lambda_{n_{k}}(x_{1},x^{\prime})\|_{2}^{q})\,\frac{dx_{1}}{|I_{1}|}\,dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|I_{1}|}\int_{U}G(2^{q}(\|\partial_{1}\lambda(x)\|_{2}^{q}+\|\partial_{1}\lambda_{n_{k}}(x)\|_{2}^{q}))\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|I_{1}|}\int_{U}G(2^{q}(\|\partial_{1}A(x)\|_{2}^{q}+\|\partial_{1}A_{n_{k}}(x)\|_{2}^{q}))\,dx

is bounded by a constant independent of
k
, which implies the claim, again by de la Vallée Poussin’s criterion
2.1
.

By Vitali’s convergence theorem
2.2
and Tonelli’s theorem,

\|\partial_{1}\lambda-\partial_{1}\lambda_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{q}(U,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}\to 0\quad\text{ as }k\to\infty.

The reasoning for
2\leq j\leq m
is analogous.
Thus (
6.1
) is proved, in the case
U=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{m}
.

Let now
U
be a general open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Since
U
is a countable union of open bounded boxes
V_{i}
with sides parallel to the coordinate axes,
(
6.1
) follows from
Lemma

2.3
applied to
f_{n}:=\|\partial_{j}\lambda-\partial_{j}\lambda_{n}\|_{2}^{q}
,
noting that
\{f_{n}\}
is uniformly integrable which can be seen as in the proof of
Lemma

5.9
.
This ends the proof.
∎

7.
Examples

Example

7.1
shows that the characteristic map
\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})

is not continuous. This example appeared in
[
PR24a
, Examples 1.12 and 7.13]
; we repeat it for
the reader’s convenience.

Example 7.1
.

The sequence
(A_{n})_{n}
of curves of symmetric
2\times 2
matrices

(7.1)

A_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{n}&x\\
x&-\frac{1}{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

converges to

A(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\
x&0\end{pmatrix},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

uniformly in all derivatives on every compact interval.
We have
\mathcal{E}(A_{n})=(-a_{n},a_{n})
and
\mathcal{E}(A)=(-a,a)
, where

a_{n}(x):=\sqrt{x^{2}+\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}}\quad\text{ and }\quad a(x):=|x|.

Then
\mathcal{E}(A_{n})\not\to\mathcal{E}(A)
as
n\to\infty
in the
C^{0,1}
topology.

Indeed, for each bounded open interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
containing
0
,

\displaystyle|a-a_{n}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I})}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{0<x\in I}\Big|\frac{(a(x)-a_{n}(x))-(a(0)-a_{n}(0))}{x}\Big|

\displaystyle=\sup_{0<x\in I}\Big|\frac{x-\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}+\frac{1}{n}}{x}\Big|\geq\Big|\frac{\frac{1}{n}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}+\frac{1}{n}}{\frac{1}{n}}\Big|=2-\sqrt{2},

for large enough
n
.

Furthermore, observe that

a_{n}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}}

tends pointwise to
a^{\prime}(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)
for all
x\neq 0
but not uniformly on any neighborhood of
0
:

a_{n}^{\prime}(\pm\tfrac{1}{n})=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

This also violates the first conclusion of
Corollary

1.4
for
q=\infty
.

The following
Example

7.2
proves that, for no
1\leq q<\infty
,
the characteristic map
\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is uniformly continuous.

Example 7.2
.

Let
\varphi_{n}(x):I:=(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
be the sawtooth function defined by

\varphi_{n}(x)=(-1)^{k}(x-\tfrac{k}{n})+\varphi_{n}(\tfrac{k}{n})\quad\text{ if }k=0,\ldots,n-1\text{ and }\tfrac{k}{n}\leq x\leq\tfrac{k+1}{n}.

Then
|\varphi_{n}^{\prime}|=1
almost everywhere in
I
.
Moreover,
0\leq\varphi_{n}\leq\tfrac{1}{n}
in
I
and
\varphi_{n}\geq\tfrac{1}{2n}
on a measurable subset
E_{n}
of
I
of measure
|E_{n}|=\frac{1}{2}
.

Consider

A_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{n}&\varphi_{n}(x)\\
\varphi_{n}(x)&-\frac{1}{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad B_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2n}&\varphi_{n}(x)\\
\varphi_{n}(x)&-\frac{1}{2n}\end{pmatrix},\quad x\in I.

Then

\|A_{n}\|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I},M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))}=\frac{2}{n}+\sqrt{2},\\
\quad\|B_{n}\|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I},M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))}=\frac{\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{2}n}+\sqrt{2},

are bounded
and

\|A_{n}-B_{n}\|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I},M_{2}(\mathbb{C}))}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2n}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

The nonnegative eigenvalues
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
of
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
,

a_{n}=\sqrt{\varphi_{n}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}},\quad b_{n}=\sqrt{\varphi_{n}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4n^{2}}},

are Lipschitz and satisfy, almost everywhere in
I
,

(7.2)

\displaystyle|a_{n}^{\prime}-b_{n}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=\varphi_{n}|\varphi_{n}^{\prime}|\Big|\frac{n}{\sqrt{n^{2}\varphi_{n}^{2}+1}}-\frac{2n}{\sqrt{4n^{2}\varphi_{n}^{2}+1}}\Big|

(7.3)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{n}\frac{3n}{\sqrt{n^{2}\varphi_{n}^{2}+1}\sqrt{4n^{2}\varphi_{n}^{2}+1}\big(2\sqrt{n^{2}\varphi_{n}^{2}+1}+\sqrt{4n^{2}\varphi_{n}^{2}+1}\big)}.

(7.4)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n\,\varphi_{n}}{6},

since
\varphi_{n}\leq\frac{1}{n}
so that the denominator is bounded by

\sqrt{2}\sqrt{5}(2\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{5})=4\sqrt{5}+5\sqrt{2}\leq 18.

Thus, almost everywhere in
E_{n}
,

\displaystyle|a_{n}^{\prime}-b_{n}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2n}\cdot\frac{n}{6}=\frac{1}{12}

and, consequently,

\|a_{n}^{\prime}-b_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I)}^{q}\geq\|a_{n}^{\prime}-b_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(E_{n})}^{q}\geq\frac{1}{12^{q}}|E_{n}|=\frac{1}{2\cdot 12^{q}},

for every
1\leq q<\infty
,
showing that

the map
\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{I},\operatorname{Herm}(2))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(\overline{I},\mathbb{R}^{2})
is not uniformly continuous.

The next
Example

7.3
shows that, for no
0<\alpha<1
,
the characteristic map
\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is uniformly continuous.
In contrast to
Example

7.2
, the curves of symmetric matrices are smooth but their Lipschitz constants are unbounded.

Example 7.3
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and set
r=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
.
Consider

A_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{n^{r}}&nx\\
nx&-\frac{1}{n^{r}}\end{pmatrix},\quad B_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2n^{r}}&nx\\
nx&-\frac{1}{2n^{r}}\end{pmatrix},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Then

A_{n}-B_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2n^{r}}&0\\
0&-\frac{1}{2n^{r}}\end{pmatrix}

tends to zero uniformly in all derivatives as
n\to\infty
.
The nonnegative eigenvalues of
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
are

a_{n}(x)=\sqrt{n^{2}x^{2}+\tfrac{1}{n^{2r}}}\quad\text{ and }\quad b_{n}(x)=\sqrt{n^{2}x^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4n^{2r}}}.

For each bounded open interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
containing
0
,

\displaystyle|a_{n}-b_{n}|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{I})}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{0<x\in I}\frac{|(a_{n}(x)-b_{n}(x))-(a_{n}(0)-b_{n}(0))|}{x^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{0<x\in I}\frac{\big|\sqrt{n^{2}x^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2r}}}-\sqrt{n^{2}x^{2}+\frac{1}{4n^{2r}}}-\frac{1}{n^{r}}+\frac{1}{2n^{r}}\big|}{x^{\alpha}}

and setting
x=n^{-1/(1-\alpha)}
,

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\big|\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{2r}}+\frac{1}{n^{2r}}}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{2r}}+\frac{1}{4n^{2r}}}-\frac{1}{n^{r}}+\frac{1}{2n^{r}}\big|}{\frac{1}{n^{r}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{2}

for large enough
n
,
showing that
the map
\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{I},\operatorname{Herm}(2))\to C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{I},\mathbb{R}^{2})
is not uniformly continuous.

It also follows that
\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{I},\operatorname{Herm}(2))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(\overline{I},\mathbb{R}^{2})
is not uniformly continuous,
for every
1<q<\infty
,
by Morrey’s inequality.

As discussed in
Section

1.3
, it would be desirable to find effective moduli of
continuity for restrictions of the characteristic map
\mathcal{E}
to (relatively) compact subspaces of
C^{0,1}(\overline{U},\operatorname{Herm}(d))
,
e.g., the subset
K
defined in (
1.15
).
The following
Example

7.4
excludes
\alpha
-Hölder continuity of
\mathcal{E}|_{K}:K\to C^{0,1}_{q}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{d})
for a
certain range of
\alpha
depending on
q
.

Example 7.4
.

Let
A_{n}
, for
n\geq 1
, be the smooth curves of symmetric matrices defined in (
7.1
) and
a_{n}
the nonnegative eigenvalue of

.
For
x>0
, we find (by a computation similar to (

\displaystyle|a_{n}^{\prime}(x)-a_{2n}^{\prime}(x)|

\displaystyle=\frac{3nx}{\sqrt{n^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4n^{2}x^{2}+1}(\sqrt{4n^{2}x^{2}+1}+2\sqrt{n^{2}x^{2}+1})}.

If

Thus, for fixed

\displaystyle\|a_{n}^{\prime}-a_{2n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}((0,1))}^{q}\geq\|a_{n}^{\prime}-a_{2n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}((0,\frac{1}{n}))}^{q}\geq\frac{n^{q}}{6^{q}}\int_{0}^{1/n}x^{q}\,dx=\frac{1}{6^{q}(q+1)n},

This implies that,

for no
\alpha\in(q^{-1},1]
,
the map
\mathcal{E}:C^{0,1}(\overline{I},\operatorname{Herm}(2))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(\overline{I},\mathbb{R}^{2})
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous,
where
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is any bounded open interval containing
0
.

8.
d
-valued Sobolev functions

The aim of this section is to set up the background and tools for
Theorem

1.8
and its proof.
We follow
[
PR24b
, Section 3]
.

8.1.
Unordered
d
-tuples of complex numbers

The symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{d}
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
by permuting the coordinates,

\sigma z=\sigma(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}):=(z_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,z_{\sigma(d)}),\quad\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d},~z\in\mathbb{C}^{d},

and thus induces an equivalence relation.
The equivalence class of
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
is the
unordered tuple

[z]=[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
.
Let us consider the set

\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}):=\{[z]:z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\}

of unordered complex
d
-tuples
and equip it with the metric

\mathbf{d}_{2}([z],[w]):=\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\|z-\sigma w\|_{2}.

Then
({\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}),\mathbf{d}_{2})
is a complete metric space and
the induced map
[~]:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is Lipschitz with
\operatorname{Lip}([~])=1
.

The element
[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
can also be represented by the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\llbracket{z_{i}}\right\rrbracket
, where

\left\llbracket{z_{i}}\right\rrbracket
denotes the Dirac mass at
z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
.
If normalized, i.e.,
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\llbracket{z_{i}}\right\rrbracket
, then, in this picture,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbf{d}_{2}
is induced by
the
L^{2}
-based Wasserstein metric on the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
.
(For this reason, in
[
PR24b
, Section 3]
, the factor
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}

was integrated in the definition of
\mathbf{d}_{2}
.)

8.2.
d
-valued Sobolev functions

Due to Almgren
[
Alm00
]
, see also
[
DLS11
]
,
there exist an integer
N=N(d)

and an injective Lipschitz mapping

\Delta:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

with
\operatorname{Lip}(\Delta)\leq 1
and
Lipschitz inverse
\Delta|^{-1}_{\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}
with
\operatorname{Lip}(\Delta|^{-1}_{\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))})\leq C(d)
.
Moreover, there is a Lipschitz retraction of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
onto
\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Following Almgren, the bi-Lipschitz embedding
\Delta
can be used to define Sobolev spaces of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued functions:
for open
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
set

W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})):=\{f:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}):\Delta\circ f\in W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})\}.

For an equivalent intrinsic definition of
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
, see
[
DLS11
, Definition 0.5 and Theorem 2.4]
.
Then
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
carries the metric

(8.1)

\rho^{1,q}_{\Delta}(f,g):=\|\Delta\circ f-\Delta\circ g\|_{W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})}

which makes it a complete metric space (where functions that coincide almost everywhere are identified); see
[
PR24b
, Lemma 3.1]
.

We will see in
Theorem

8.11
that, for
1\leq q<\infty
, the topology on
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
induced by
\rho^{1,q}_{\Delta}

is independent of the choice of the Almgren embedding
\Delta
.

Remark 8.1
.

In
[
Alm00
]
and
[
DLS11
]
,
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{\ell}))
is studied for arbitrary
\ell
.

8.3.
Almgren’s embedding

Let us recall Almgren’s construction of
\Delta
for
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
.

Definition 8.2

(Almgren map)
.

We say that

H:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

is an
Almgren map
if there is a unit complex number

\theta\in\mathbb{C}
such that
H([z])
is an array of
d
real numbers
\eta(z_{i}):=\mathrm{Re}(\theta z_{i})
arranged in increasing order, i.e.,

H([z])=H([z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}])=(\eta(z_{\sigma(1)}),\ldots,\eta(z_{\sigma(d)}),

where
\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}
is chosen such that
\eta(z_{\sigma(1)})\leq\eta(z_{\sigma(2)})\leq\cdots\leq\eta(z_{\sigma(d)})
.
We also say that
H
is the Almgren map associated to the real linear form
\eta
.

By Almgren’s combinatorial lemma (see e.g.
[
DLS11
, Lemma 2.3]
) there exists
\alpha=\alpha(d)>0
and a finite set of linear forms

\Lambda=\{\eta_{1},\ldots\eta_{h}\}
, where
\eta_{l}(z):=\mathrm{Re}(\theta_{l}z)

for unit complex numbers
\theta_{l}
, with the following property: given any set of
d^{2}
complex numbers,
\left\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{d^{2}}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
,
there exists
\eta_{l}\in\Lambda
such that

(8.2)

|\eta_{l}(z_{k})|\geq\alpha|z_{k}|\quad\textrm{ for all }k\in\big\{1,\ldots,d^{2}\big\}.

For instance, we may take
h=2d^{2}+1
and
\{\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{l}\}
induced by the set
\{\theta_{1},\ldots\theta_{h}\}
of all
h
-th roots of unity.

Let
H_{l}
denote the Almgren map associated to
\eta_{l}\in\Lambda
.
The
Almgren embedding

\Delta:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N=dh
, is then defined by

(8.3)

\displaystyle\Delta([z])=h^{-1/2}(H_{1}([z]),\ldots,H_{h}([z])).

The property (
8.2
) guarantees that
\Delta
is as required in
Section

8.2
;
see e.g.
[
DLS11
, Section 2.1.2]
.

8.4.
The space
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})

Consider the subspace
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}):=\{[x]:x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
x^{\uparrow}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the representative of the equivalence class
[x]
with increasingly ordered coordinates.
Clearly,
x^{\uparrow}
only depends on
[x]
and thus we have an injective map
(~)^{\uparrow}:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
It is a right-inverse of
[~]:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 8.3

(
[
PR24a
, Lemma 7.1]
)
.

We have

\mathbf{d}_{2}([x],[y])=\|x^{\uparrow}-y^{\uparrow}\|_{2},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In particular,
(~)^{\uparrow}:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
[~]:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
are Lipschitz maps.

It is easy to check that the map
H=(~)^{\uparrow}:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an Almgren map (for
\mathbb{R}
instead of
\mathbb{C}
,
associated to the real linear form
\eta=\operatorname{id}
) and (
8.2
) is trivially true with
\Lambda=\{\operatorname{id}\}
.
Thus
\Delta=H=(~)^{\uparrow}:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an Almgren embedding.

Consequently, we can equip the space

W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}))=\{f:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}):f^{\uparrow}\in W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})\}

with the metric

\rho^{1,q}_{\uparrow}(f,g):=\|f^{\uparrow}-g^{\uparrow}\|_{W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Thus
Theorem

1.1
can be seen as a special case of
Theorem

1.8
.

8.5.
Curves of class
W^{1,q}
in
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open bounded interval.
We recall an equivalent definition of
W^{1,q}(I,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
(see
[
DLS11
, Definition 0.5]
) which is independent of Almgren’s embedding.

Definition 8.4

(Intrinsic definition)
.

A measurable function
f:I\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
belongs to
W^{1,q}(I,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
(
1\leq q\leq\infty
) if there exists a function

\varphi\in L^{q}(I,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})

such that

(i)

x\mapsto\mathbf{d}_{2}(f(x),T)\in W^{1,q}(I)
for all
T\in{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
;

(ii)

\left|(\mathbf{d}_{2}(f,T))^{\prime}\right|\leq\varphi
almost everywhere in
I

for all
T\in{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
.

The minimal function
\tilde{\varphi}
fulfilling (ii), that is,

\tilde{\varphi}\leq\varphi\quad\text{almost everywhere for every other $\varphi$ satisfying (ii),}

is measurable and we denote it by
|Df|
.

Proposition 8.5

(
[
DLS11
, Proposition 1.2]
)
.

Let
f\in W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
.
Then,

(a)

f\in AC(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
and, moreover,

f\in C^{0,1-\frac{1}{q}}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
for
q>1
;

(b)

there exists a parameterization

f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\in W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{C})
of
f
, i.e.,

f=\left\llbracket{f_{1}}\right\rrbracket+\cdots+\left\llbracket{f_{d}}\right\rrbracket,

such that
\left|Df_{i}\right|=\left|f_{i}^{\prime}\right|\leq\left|Df\right|
almost everywhere.

Actually, the proof of
Proposition

8.5
in
[
DLS11
]

implies that
f\in W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
belongs to
AC^{q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
in
the sense of
Section

2.4
.
In the situation of
Proposition

8.5
,
we will always mean without further mention that
f
and
f_{i}
are the continuous representatives.

Following
[
DLS11
, Definition 1.9]
, we can also define the differential
Df
.

Definition 8.6

(Differential)
.

Let
f=\sum_{i}\left\llbracket{f_{i}}\right\rrbracket:I\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
and
x_{0}\in I
. We say that
f
is
differentiable

at
x_{0}
if there exist
d
complex numbers
L_{i}
satisfying:

(i)

\mathbf{d}_{2}(f(x),T_{x_{0}}f(x))=o(\left|x-x_{0}\right|)
, where

(8.4)

T_{x_{0}}f(x):=\sum_{i}\left\llbracket{f_{i}(x_{0})+L_{i}\cdot(x-x_{0})}\right\rrbracket;

(ii)

L_{i}=L_{j}
if
f_{i}(x_{0})=f_{j}(x_{0})
.

The
d
-valued map
T_{x_{0}}f

is called the
first-order approximation
of
f
at
x_{0}
.
We denote
L_{i}
by
Df_{i}(x_{0})
and the point
Df(x_{0}):=\sum_{i}\left\llbracket{Df_{i}(x_{0})}\right\rrbracket\in{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})

is called the
differential
of
f
at
x_{0}
.

By
Definition

8.6
(ii), the notation is consistent (see
[
DLS11
, Remark 1.11]
):
if
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
is another parameterization of
f
,
f
is differentiable at
x_{0}
, and
\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}
is such that

g_{i}(x_{0})=f_{\sigma(i)}(x_{0})
for all
1\leq i\leq d
, then
Dg_{i}(x_{0})=Df_{\sigma(i)}(x_{0})
.

By
Proposition

8.5
, every
f\in W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
is differentiable almost everywhere and,
if
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\in W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{C})
is a parameterization of
f
, then
Df=\sum_{i}\left\llbracket{f_{i}^{\prime}}\right\rrbracket
almost everywhere;
see
[
PR24b
, Section 3.4]
.

8.6.
A distance notion on
W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))

The following definition is taken from
[
PR24b
, Definition 3.6]
.

Definition 8.7

(The distance
\mathbf{d}_{E}^{1,q}
)
.

Let
f,g\in W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
and let

f=\left\llbracket{f_{1}}\right\rrbracket+\cdots+\left\llbracket{f_{d}}\right\rrbracket,\quad g=\left\llbracket{g_{1}}\right\rrbracket+\cdots+\left\llbracket{g_{d}}\right\rrbracket

be parameterizations of
f
,
g
with
f_{i},g_{i}\in W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{C})
as in
Proposition

8.5
.
Set

\mathbf{s}_{0}(f,g)(x):=\mathbf{d}_{2}(f(x),g(x)).

Fix an arbitrary ordering of the elements of
\operatorname{S}_{d}
.
For
x\in I
, let

\tau(x):=\min\Big\{\tau\in\operatorname{S}_{d}:\Big(\sum_{i}|f_{i}(x)-g_{\tau(i)}(x)|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}=\mathbf{d}_{2}(f(x),g(x))\Big\}.

For
x\in I
such that
Df(x)=\sum_{i}\left\llbracket{Df_{i}(x)}\right\rrbracket
and
Dg(x)=\sum_{i}\left\llbracket{Dg_{i}(x)}\right\rrbracket
exist in the sense of
Definition

8.6
,
set

(8.5)

\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,g)(x):=\max\Big(\sum_{i}|Df_{i}(x)-Dg_{\tau(x)(i)}(x)|^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

where the maximum is taken over all orderings of
\operatorname{S}_{d}
.
By the remarks above,
\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,g)(x)
is defined for almost every
x\in I
and
independent of the choices of parameterizations
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
of
f
and
g
.

For each measurable subset
E\subseteq I
, we define

\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{E}(f,g):=\|\mathbf{s}_{0}(f,g)\|_{L^{\infty}(E)}+\|\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,g)\|_{L^{q}(E)}

which is justified, since
the functions
\mathbf{s}_{i}(f,g):I\to\mathbb{R}
, for
i=0,1
, are Borel measurable, by
[
PR24b
, Lemma 3.7]
.

Lemma 8.8

(
[
PR24b
, Lemma 3.8]
)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval and
E\subseteq I
a measurable set.
Let
f,g\in W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
. Then:

(1)

\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{E}(f,f)=0
.

(2)

\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{E}(f,g)=0
implies
f=g
on
E
.

(3)

\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{E}(f,g)=\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{E}(g,f)
.

In particular,
\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{I}
is a semimetric on
W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
.

8.7.
Convergence in
W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))

There is a notion of
weak convergence
in
W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
, see
[
DLS11
, Definition 2.9]
,
which is not appropriate for our purpose.
We introduce a stronger notion of convergence based on the semimetric
\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{I}
.

Definition 8.9

(Strong convergence)
.

Let
f_{n},f\in W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
. We say
that
f_{n}

converges to
f
in
W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
as
n\to\infty
,
and we write
f_{n}\rightarrow f
,
if

\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{I}(f,f_{n})\rightarrow 0\quad\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Strong convergence in
W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
is equivalent to convergence with respect to the
topology induced by the metric
\rho^{1,q}_{\Delta}
(see (
8.1
)), for some, equivalently every,
Almgren embedding
\Delta
:

Theorem 8.10

(
[
PR24b
, Theorem 3.11]
)
.

Let
\Delta:{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an Almgren embedding.
Then
f_{n}\to f
in
W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
as
n\to\infty
if and only if

\rho^{1,q}_{\Delta}(f,f_{n})\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

In particular, the topology induced by the metric
\rho^{1,q}_{\Delta}

on
W^{1,q}(I,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
does not depend on the choice of the Almgren embedding
\Delta
.
We shall see below that this is also true in several variables.

8.8.
The topology on
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
induced by
\rho^{1,q}_{\Delta}
is independent of
\Delta

The following theorem is an improved version of
[
PR24b
, Theorem 10.2]
.

Theorem 8.11
.

Let
\Delta^{i}:{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}
, for
i=1,2
, be two Almgren embeddings.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and bounded and
1\leq q<\infty
.
Let
f,f_{n}\in W^{1,q}(U,{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
, for
n\geq 1
.
Then,

\displaystyle\|\Delta^{1}\circ f-\Delta^{1}\circ f_{n}\|_{W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}})}

\displaystyle\to 0

if and only if

\displaystyle\|\Delta^{2}\circ f-\Delta^{2}\circ f_{n}\|_{W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}})}

\displaystyle\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

The map

\Delta^{2}\circ(\Delta^{1})|_{\Delta^{1}({\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}^{-1}:\Delta^{1}({\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}}

is Lipschitz and has a Lipschitz extension
\Gamma
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}}
.
Thus (see
[
ADM90
]
) superposition with
\Gamma

defines a bounded map from
W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}})
to
W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}})
, where, setting
C:=\operatorname{Lip}(\Gamma)
, for
F\in W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}})
,

\|\Gamma\circ F\|_{2}\leq C\,\|F\|_{2},

because
\Gamma(0)=0
, and

(8.6)

\|\nabla(\Gamma\circ F)\|_{2}\leq C\,\|\nabla F\|_{2}

almost everywhere in
U
.

Set
F^{i}:=\Delta^{i}\circ f
and
F^{i}_{n}:=\Delta^{i}\circ f_{n}
, for
i=1,2
.
Assume that

(8.7)

\|F^{1}-F^{1}_{n}\|_{W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Then
\{\|F^{1}\|_{2}^{q}+F^{1}_{n}\|_{2}^{q}:n\geq 1\}
and
\{\|\partial_{j}F^{1}\|_{2}^{q}+\|\partial_{j}F^{1}_{n}\|_{2}^{q}:n\geq 1\}
, for
1\leq j\leq m
, are uniformly integrable
subsets of
L^{1}(U)
.
Since
F^{2}=\Gamma\circ F^{1}
and
F_{n}^{2}=\Gamma\circ F_{n}^{1}
, we may conclude similarly as in the proof of
Lemma

5.9

(using (
8.6
)) that the sets

\{\|F^{2}-F^{2}_{n}\|_{2}^{q}:n\geq 1\}
and
\{\|\partial_{j}F^{2}-\partial_{j}F^{2}_{n}\|_{2}^{q}:n\geq 1\}
, for
1\leq j\leq m
, are uniformly integrable.

Let us assume that
U=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{m}
is an open bounded box with sides parallel to the coordinate axes. Let
j=1
.
For
x^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}=I_{2}\times\cdots\times I_{m}
and
i=1,2
,
consider

\displaystyle A^{i}_{n}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{1}}\big\|\partial_{1}F^{i}(x_{1},x^{\prime})-\partial_{1}F^{i}_{n}(x_{1},x^{\prime})\big\|_{2}^{q}\,dx_{1},

\displaystyle B^{i}_{n}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{1}}\big\|F^{i}(x_{1},x^{\prime})-F^{i}_{n}(x_{1},x^{\prime})\big\|_{2}^{q}\,dx_{1}.

By (
8.7
) and Tonelli’s theorem,

\int_{U^{\prime}}A^{1}_{n}(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\to 0\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{U^{\prime}}B^{1}_{n}(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Thus there is a subsequence
(n_{k})
such that
A^{1}_{n_{k}}(x^{\prime})\to 0
and
B^{1}_{n_{k}}(x^{\prime})\to 0
for almost every
x^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}
as
k\to\infty
.
For each such
x^{\prime}
,

Theorem

8.10
implies that
A^{2}_{n_{k}}(x^{\prime})\to 0
and
B^{2}_{n_{k}}(x^{\prime})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

Tonelli’s theorem and uniform integrability of the sets

\{\|F^{2}-F^{2}_{n}\|_{2}^{q}:n\geq 1\}
and
\{\|\partial_{j}F^{2}-\partial_{j}F^{2}_{n}\|_{2}^{q}:n\geq 1\}
, for
1\leq j\leq m
,
imply, invoking de la Vallée Poussin’s criterion
2.1
, that

\{A^{2}_{n}:n\geq 1\}
and
\{B^{2}_{n}:n\geq 1\}
are uniformly integrable. (See the proof of
Theorem

6.1
.)

Then Vitali’s convergence theorem
2.2
and Tonelli’s theorem yield that

\|F^{2}-F^{2}_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}})}\to 0\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\partial_{1}F^{2}-\partial_{1}F^{2}_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}})}\to 0

as
k\to\infty
. Since the partial derivatives
\partial_{j}
, for
2\leq j\leq m
, can be treated in the same way, we
have showed that there is a subsequence
(n_{k})
such that

\|F^{2}-F^{2}_{n_{k}}\|_{W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }k\to\infty

which implies

\|F^{2}-F^{2}_{n}\|_{W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

in the case that
U=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{m}
.

The case that
U
is a general open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
follows from
Lemma

2.3

(applied to
f_{n}:=\|F^{2}-F^{2}_{n}\|_{2}^{q}
and
f_{n}:=\|\partial_{j}F^{2}-\partial_{j}F_{n}^{2}\|_{2}^{q}
),
since
U
is a countable union of bounded open boxes with sides parallel to the coordinate axes.
∎

9.
The characteristic map for normal matrices

We are ready to introduce the characteristic map for normal matrices.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
\Delta:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an Almgren embedding.
Let
1\leq q\leq\infty
.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and bounded.
Then the map

\mathcal{E}_{u}:W^{1,q}(U,\operatorname{Norm}(d))\to W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})),\quad A\mapsto\Lambda\circ A,

is well-defined and bounded, satisfying

\|\Delta(\mathcal{E}_{u}(A)(x))\|_{2}\leq C\,\|A(x)\|_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\nabla(\Delta(\mathcal{E}_{u}(A)(x)))\|_{2}\leq C\,\|\nabla A(x)\|_{2}

for almost every
x\in U
, where
C
is the Lipschitz constant of
\Delta
.

Proof.

The map
\Delta\circ\Lambda:\operatorname{Norm}(d)\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
C=\operatorname{Lip}(\Delta)
,
by
Proposition

3.3
. It admits a Lipschitz extension
L:M_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

preserving the Lipschitz constant
C
, by Kirszbraun’s theorem.

It is well-known (see
[
ADM90
]
) that superposition with
L
defines a bounded map from
W^{1,q}(U,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
to
W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})
, where

\|L\circ A\|_{2}\leq C\,\|A\|_{2},

because
L(0)=\Delta(\Lambda(0))=0
, and

\|\nabla(L\circ A)\|_{2}\leq C\,\|\nabla A\|_{2}

almost everywhere in
U
.
This implies the assertion.
∎

Let us recall an important relationship between the metric speed of an
AC
-curve in
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
and
the derivative of any parameterization.

Lemma 9.2

(
[
PR24b
, Lemma 11.1]
)
.

Let
\lambda:I\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
be an absolutely continuous curve and
\gamma:I\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
defined by
\gamma(x)=[\lambda(x)]
, for
x\in I
.
Then the metric speed of
\gamma
is given by

|\dot{\gamma}|(x)=\|\lambda^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}\quad\text{ for almost every }x\in I.

On an interval
I
, we can express the boundedness of
\mathcal{E}_{u}:W^{1,q}(I,\operatorname{Norm}(d))\to W^{1,q}(I,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))

in terms of
\mathbf{s}_{0}
and
\mathbf{s}_{1}
,
introduced in
Definition

8.7
.

Proposition 9.3
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
and
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
a bounded open interval.
Then, for
A\in W^{1,q}(I,\operatorname{Norm}(d))
,

(9.1)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{s}_{0}(\mathcal{E}_{u}(A),[0])\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}

\displaystyle\leq|I|^{-1/q}\,\|A\|_{L^{q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}+|I|^{1-1/q}\,\|A^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))},\qquad

(9.2)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{s}_{1}(\mathcal{E}_{u}(A),[0])\|_{L^{q}(I)}

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}.

Proof.

Let
A\in W^{1,q}(I,\operatorname{Norm}(d))
.
Let
\lambda\in W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{C}^{d})
be a parameterization of
\Lambda:=\mathcal{E}_{u}(A)
(see
Proposition

8.5
).
By (
5.3
), for all
x\in I
,

\displaystyle\|\lambda(x)\|_{2}=\|A(x)\|_{2}\leq|I|^{-1/q}\,\|A\|_{L^{q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}+|I|^{1-1/q}\,\|A^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I,M_{d}(\mathbb{C}))},

which implies (
9.1
).
By
Lemma

9.2
and (
3.3
), for almost every
x\in I
,

\displaystyle\|\lambda^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}=|\dot{\Lambda}|(x)\leq\|A^{\prime}(x)\|_{2},

entailing (
9.2
).
∎

10.1.
Preliminary observations and reductions

Let

By

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{2}(\Lambda(x),\Lambda_{n}(x))

) for the definition of
\mathbf{s}_{1}
.

With

Lemma 10.3
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
.
Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Let
A_{n}\to A
in

as

.
Then:

(1)

\tilde{A}_{n}\to\tilde{A}
in

be the curves of unordered eigenvalues
of

if and only if

\widehat{\mathbf{d}}^{1,q}_{I}(\tilde{\Lambda},\tilde{\Lambda}_{n})\to 0
as

.

Proof.

(1) This is clear since

in

implies

\operatorname{Tr}(A_{n})\to\operatorname{Tr}(A)

(2)
By (

as well as
\|\mathbf{d}_{2}(\tilde{\Lambda},\tilde{\Lambda}_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\to 0
as

.
Assume that

as
n\to\infty
.
By

8.10
,
we have

\|\Delta\circ\Lambda-\Delta\circ\Lambda_{n}\|_{W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{R}^{N})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for every Almgren embedding
\Delta:{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Let

be an Almgren map with associated real linear form

(see

).
Then

\displaystyle H\circ\Lambda-H\circ\tilde{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{d}\eta(\operatorname{Tr}(A))(1,1,\ldots,1),

\displaystyle H\circ\Lambda_{n}-H\circ\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{d}\eta(\operatorname{Tr}(A_{n}))(1,1,\ldots,1).

again by

8.10
.
The opposite direction follows from the same arguments.
∎

12.
An application for compact self-adjoint operators

Let
H
be a Hilbert space.
A bounded operator
A\in L(H)
is
compact
if for each bounded sequence
(v_{n})\subseteq H
the image
(Av_{n})

contains a convergent subsequence.

The set
K(H)
of all compact operators
A\in L(H)
form a closed linear subspace of
L(H)
and thus a Banach space
endowed with the operator norm.

The
resolvent set
of
A
is by definition the set of
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
A-z
is invertible with

resolvent

R(z):=(A-z)^{-1}\in L(H)
.
The resolvent set
P(A)
is an open subset of
\mathbb{C}
, its complement is the
spectrum
of
A
.
As
A
is compact, the spectrum of
A
is a countable set which accumulates at zero, and zero is its only accumulation
point. Every nonzero point in the spectrum is an eigenvalue of
A
with finite multiplicity. See
[
Kat76
, III.6.26]
.

Let
A\in K(H)
be a self-adjoint nonnegative compact operator.
Let

\lambda_{1}(A)\geq\lambda_{2}(A)\geq\cdots

denote its decreasingly ordered eigenvalues.
Then, by the Courant–Fischer–Weyl min-max principle (see e.g.
[
GGK03
, Chapter IV, Theorem 9.1]
or

[
RS80
, Theorem XIII.1]
),

(12.1)

\lambda_{i}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\subseteq H\\
\dim M=i-1\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|x\|=1\\
x\bot M\end{subarray}}\langle Ax,x\rangle.

As a consequence we get the following version of Weyl’s theorem (see e.g.
[
GGK03
, Chapter IV, Section 4.9]
).

Proposition 12.1
.

Let
A,B\in K(H)
be self-adjoint nonnegative compact operators. Then, for all
i\geq 1
,

|\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{i}(B)|\leq\|A-B\|_{\operatorname{op}}.

Proof.

Let
x\in H
with
\|x\|=1
. Then

|\langle Ax,x\rangle-\langle Bx,x\rangle|=|\langle(A-B)x,x\rangle|\leq\|A-B\|_{\operatorname{op}}

which means that

\langle Ax,x\rangle\leq\langle Bx,x\rangle+\|A-B\|_{\operatorname{op}}

and

\langle Bx,x\rangle\leq\langle Ax,x\rangle+\|A-B\|_{\operatorname{op}}.

Applying (
12.1
), we conclude that

\lambda_{i}(A)\leq\lambda_{i}(B)+\|A-B\|_{\operatorname{op}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\lambda_{i}(B)\leq\lambda_{i}(A)+\|A-B\|_{\operatorname{op}}

which gives the statement.
∎

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a bounded open set.
Consider a Lipschitz family
A\in C^{0,1}(\overline{U},K(H))
of compact operators
such that each
A(x)
, for
x\in U
, is self-adjoint.
Fix
x_{0}\in U
.
Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
A(x_{0})
of multiplicity
d
.
Let
\Gamma
be a simple closed
C^{1}
curve in the resolvent set
P(A(x_{0}))

enclosing only
\lambda
among all eigenvalues of
A(x_{0})
.
Then there is an open neighborhood
V
of
x_{0}
such that

\Gamma
lies in
P(A(x))
, for
x\in V
,
and
A(x)
, for
x\in V
, has precisely
d
eigenvalues (counted with multiplicities)
in the interior of
\Gamma
; see
[
Kat76
, IV.2.16, IV.3.15, IV.3.16]
.
Moreover, for
x\in V
and
z
close to any
z_{0}\in P(A(x_{0}))
, the operator
A(x)-z
is invertible with
resolvent
R(A(x),z)=(A(x)-z)^{-1}\in L(H)
.
We conclude that the map

(x,z)\mapsto R(A(x),z)=(A(x)-z)^{-1}\in L(H)

is of class
C^{0,1}
in
x
and holomorphic in
z
, since inversion is analytic on
L(H)
.
Consequently,

\Pi:x\mapsto-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}R(A(x),z)\,dz=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}(A(x)-z)^{-1}\,dz\in L(H)

defines a Lipschitz family
\Pi
of projections onto the direct sum of eigenspaces of the corresponding
eigenvalues in the interior of
\Gamma
.
We have that
A(x)
commutes with
\Pi(x)
, and the spectrum of
A(x)
inside
\Gamma
coincides
with the spectrum of
A(x)\Pi(x)=\Pi(x)A(x)\Pi(x)\in L(\Pi(x)H)
; see
[
Kat76
, III.6.17]
.

The image of
x\mapsto\Pi(x)
describes a
d
-dimensional Lipschitz subbundle of the trivial bundle
U\times H\to U
.
Indeed, choose
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\in H
such that the vectors
\Pi(x_{0})v_{i}
span
\Pi(x_{0})H
.
This remains true locally for
x
near
x_{0}
.
By the Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization procedure (which is real analytic),
we obtain a local orthonormal Lipschitz frame of the bundle.
In this frame,
\Pi(x)A(x)\Pi(x)
is given by a Hermitian
d\times d
matrix parameterized in a Lipschitz way by
x
.

For the reader’s convenience, we restate
Theorem

1.21
:

Theorem 12.2
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a bounded open set. Let
H
be a Hilbert space and

,
for
n\geq 1
, Lipschitz families of compact self-adjoint nonnegative operators. Assume that

is positive definite for all

and

in

as

.
Then:

(1)

The decreasingly ordered eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(A)
and
\lambda_{i}(A_{n})
, for all
i
and large enough

, belong to
C^{0,1}(\overline{U})
.

(2)

For all
1\leq j\leq m
and almost every
x\in U
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\partial_{j}(\lambda_{i}(A_{n}))(x)=\partial_{j}(\lambda_{i}(A))(x),\quad i=1,2,\ldots

(3)

For every
1\leq q<\infty
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{i}(A_{n})\|_{W^{1,q}(U)}=0,\quad i=1,2,\ldots

Proof.

(1)
Since
A(x)
is positive definite for all
x\in U
,
all eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(A(x))
are positive for all
x\in U
.
By
Proposition

12.1
, this also holds for
A_{n}
if
n
is large enough.
Then
Proposition

12.1
implies that each
\lambda_{i}(A)
and
\lambda_{i}(A_{n})
, for large enough
n
, is Lipschitz
and belongs to
C^{0,1}(\overline{U})
.

(2)
Fix
x_{0}\in U
and
i\geq 1
.
Let
\Gamma
be a simple closed
C^{1}
curve in
P(A(x_{0}))
enclosing only
\lambda_{i}(A(x_{0}))
among all eigenvalues of
A(x_{0})
.
As above, by
[
Kat76
, IV.2.16, IV.3.15, IV.3.16]
, we see that there is an open neighborhood
V
of
x_{0}

and
n_{0}\geq 1
such that

\Gamma
lies in
P(A(x))
and
P(A_{n}(x))
, for
x\in V
and
n\geq n_{0}
,
and
A(x)
and
A_{n}(x)
, for
x\in V
and
n\geq n_{0}
, have precisely
d
eigenvalues (counted with multiplicities)
in the interior of
\Gamma
.
By
Proposition

12.1
,

\lambda_{i}(A_{n}(x))
is among those eigenvalues, if
n_{0}
is large enough.

The discussion before the theorem shows
that the
d
eigenvalues of
A(x)
as well as the
d
eigenvalues of
A_{n}(x)

in the interior of
\Gamma
are precisely the
d
eigenvalues of
B(x):=\Pi(x)A(x)\Pi(x)
and
B_{n}(x):=\Pi_{n}(x)A_{n}(x)\Pi_{n}(x)
,
given by a Lipschitz family of Hermitian
d\times d
matrices, respectively.
Moreover,
B_{n}\to B
in
C^{0,1}(\overline{V},\operatorname{Herm}(d))
as
n\to\infty
, after slightly shrinking

if necessary.
Thus,
Theorem

1.7
implies that, for all
1\leq j\leq m
and almost every
x\in J
, we
have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\partial_{j}(\lambda_{i}(A_{n}))(x)=\partial_{j}(\lambda_{i}(A))(x).

Since

can be covered by countably many such open subsets

, this holds for almost every

.

(3) By

, for each

and

,

|\lambda_{i}(A_{n})|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{U})}\leq|A_{n}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{U},K(H))}\leq L,

for a positive constant

independent of

and

. Thus, for each
1\leq j\leq m
and almost every

,

|\partial_{j}(\lambda_{i}(A_{n}))(x)|\leq L.

Consequently, in view of (2), the dominated convergence theorem implies that,

and every

,

\|\partial_{j}(\lambda_{i}(A))-\partial_{j}(\lambda_{i}(A_{n}))\|_{L^{q}(U)}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

By

, we have

\|\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{i}(A_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty

and (3) is proved.
∎
