Optimization strategies for monoclonal antibody production: advances in simulation and artificial intelligence in bioprocessing
Authors: Kadeejathul Kubra, Munawar A. Shaik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe rapid growth of monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapies has increased the need for efficient, scalable, and affordable manufacturing processes. However, mAb production remains complex because of nonlinear upstream cell culture behavior, expensive downstream purification, especially Protein-A chromatography, and plant-level bottlenecks that can increase cost, cycle time, and manuacturing uncertainty. This review examines recent developments in mAb manufacturing with focus on process simulation, mathematical optimization, and artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) across upstream processing (USP), downstream processing (DSP), and integrated plant-level operation. In USP, media optimization, dynamic feeding, high-density cultures, and continuous perfusion bioreactors are discussed in relation to productivity and critical quality attributes (CQAs). In DSP, alternative and intensified purification strategies are reviewed with a focus on recovery, impurity clearance, scalability, cost, and technology maturity. AI/ML applications are also discussed from early-stage development and cell-line screening to upstream control, CQA prediction, chromatography optimization, and downstream decision support. Despite these advancements, challenges such as data heterogeneity, limited standardized datasets, model transferability, and regulatory constraints remain important barriers to implementation. Overall, this review uniquely connects simulation and AI/ML approaches to practical optimization across the full mAb manufacturing workflow, including design, scheduling, debottlenecking, purification, monitoring, and quality prediction. The combination of process simulation, continuous bioprocessing, and AI/ML-based decision support may enable more flexible, reliable, and cost-effective mAb manufacturing. However, these benefits depend on validation through robust models, process-specific case studies, and technoeconomic analysis.

Introduction

The worldwide biopharmaceutical sector, particularly for monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), has expanded significantly in recent decades. In 1986, Orthoclone OKT3 was approved as the first mAb to reduce rejection in kidney transplantation.1 Since then, therapeutic mAbs and comparable products have gained significant attention in the biopharmaceutical industry. These products include fragment crystallizable (Fc) fusion proteins, antibody fragments, and antibody-drug conjugates.2 These products are now widely licensed and commercialized for the treatment of autoimmune disorders, inflammatory diseases, infectious diseases, and various cancers, which contribute to continued market growth. Moreover, due to their biological origin and targeted immune mechanism, mAbs cause fewer adverse side effects in the body than other therapeutic options.3 As a result, the global mAb market has continued to grow steadily. Aboul-Ella et al. reported that the mAb market size was estimated at USD 237.64 billion in 2023 and is expected to rise to USD 679.03 billion in 2033.4 This estimate refers to the broader mAb market for prevention, detection, and treatment applications, and is not limited only to approved therapeutic mAb products. However, market forecasts may vary depending on the factors such as product type, geographic coverage, data sources, and analytical assumptions.

Despite the rapid growth of mAb therapeutics, the production process is still complex and costly, with difficulties arising during both upstream processing (USP) and downstream processing (DSP). Early optimization of mAb production was based on one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) experimentation by individually adjusting bioreactor parameters such as temperature and pH and keeping other variables constant. This method ignored the interactions between the variables because it treated multivariate systems as univariate. As a result, it limited the optimization of all process conditions.5 To address these limitations, multivariate methods such as principal component analysis (PCA)6 and partial least-squares regression (PLSR)7 were proposed as visualization and regression data analysis techniques of bioprocess data. These linear chemometric methods reduce dimensionality of data and provide a systematic framework to analyze correlations, importance of variables, outliers, and abnormal process behavior.8 However, the PCA/PLSR limits their capability in capturing nonlinear dynamics of cell culture processes.

Along with these developments, mathematical modeling methods have emerged to link bioreactor variables and optimize production of mAb. These models studied the effects of variables such as temperature, glucose concentration, and cell density for mAb production9,10 and optimized feeding strategies like glucose and glutamine supply in fed-batch bioreactors.11 However, protein aggregation was addressed as a prominent quality issue, and it was suggested that it could be minimized by careful control of process conditions such as temperature and feed flow rate.12 These approaches provided useful insight into cell behavior and quality risks. However, these models were computationally expensive and failed to fully capture the complex interactions that affect mAb yield, quality, and glycosylation.13

Most mAb manufacturing depends on batch processing; however, continuous bioprocessing has emerged as an alternative. Typically, continuous bioprocessing operates in a hybrid or periodic mode, where individual unit operations are executed in a cyclic manner similar to batch runs, but the overall process maintains near-continuous material flow. Although continuous biopharmaceutical manufacturing has advantages like higher productivity, greater flexibility, and improved product quality, there are a few gaps that still need to be addressed. These include management of batches properly, control of residence time distributions, management of start-up and shut-down phases, and effective planning for out-of-specification events.14 These challenges are increased in traditional batch processes. Batch manufacturing is labor-intensive, requires frequent cleaning, and often leads to product variability between batches. These inefficiencies lead to longer production cycles and considerable downtime, which makes it more difficult to scale operations and maintain low costs.15–17

On the other hand, the downstream processing stage depends on expensive Protein-A (ProA) chromatography as the primary capture. This step accounts for approximately 15% of the total operating cost. It is further limited in yields due to its low binding capacity, ligand leaching, and the requirement for highly acidic conditions for elution.18 All these factors make it a major bottleneck in the manufacture of mAbs. These limitations have motivated the development of alternative purification strategies like non-ProA chromatographic and non-chromatographic platforms, to increase productivity while reducing cost of goods.

Modern mAb manufacturing processes are based on platform workflow that integrates upstream cell cultures and downstream purification operations. This workflow includes cell banking, inoculum or seed-train preparation, bioreactor operation, harvest and clarification, ProA capture, viral inactivation (VI), polishing, and final formulation.3 This platform approach is good for standardization of production, but the upstream and downstream steps are closely interconnected.19 Changes in the upstream conditions can affect the product titer, impurity levels, product quality attributes, and downstream purification performance. Therefore, process simulation,3 process optimization,20 and machine learning (ML)/artificial intelligence (AI) tools21 are widely used to better understand the processes, compare process scenarios, monitor performance, predict process behavior, and support decision-making for process control. This includes the adoption of process analytical technology (PAT), online and at-line sensors, data historians, and continuous manufacturing.21 Together, these methods provide more real-time and historical process data for data-driven biomanufacturing.

Process simulation tools allow for scenario-based analysis of process design, cost, cycle time, capacity, production planning and scheduling, and debottlenecking.3,22 The literature on the cost reduction of biopharmaceutical manufacturing includes several studies such as conversion of batch bioprocesses into continuous ones,16 using a single-use (SU) system instead of stainless-steel (SS) facilities,23,24 and utilizing a non-ProA purification platform over the ProA chromatography process.25–27 All these applications are evidence of efficient solution methods for cost-effective optimization. However, these methods are often limited by model assumptions, regulatory barriers, and trade-off in product yield and quality.

The advanced optimization processes are accomplished by using mathematical programming techniques. Deterministic and stochastic approaches have been used for the optimal design of process and equipment in mAb manufacturing. These methods address issues like upstream bioreactor sizing, downstream chromatography sequencing, and multi-objective trade-offs between cost and purity.18,28 Metaheuristics, evolutionary algorithms with process economics models have also been created to design cost-effective sequences of purification and equipment sizing.29 These have also been applied to handle scheduling of multi-product, multi-stage biopharmaceutical facilities with sequence-dependent changeovers, storage, and shelf-life limits, with multiple due dates and demand.30–32

The introduction of smart manufacturing tools, such as ML and AI, has improved biopharmaceutical production. In the synthesis of mAb, these tools have enhanced early-stage, upstream, and downstream bioprocesses.33–35 ML algorithms are also useful in optimizing critical quality attributes (CQAs) and critical process parameters (CPPs) of the mAb production process, and they allow real-time decision-making and improve overall consistency of the product. All these studies used classical ML-based models, deep learning models, and hybrid models. These applications are used to minimize the operational costs and to enhance the robustness of the process and the quality of the product.34,35 These techniques can be used to overcome the limitations of the traditional methods13 and to open the way to more precise, efficient, and fully data-driven biopharmaceutical manufacturing.

The developments of these computational methods represent an evident advance in optimization of biopharmaceutical processes. Initial research concentrated on process simulation, mechanistic modeling, and mathematical programming, and thereafter followed by a shift in research to concentrate on process intensification and integrated continuous bioprocessing. More recently, predictive modeling, real-time monitoring, and adaptive control have also been possible through data-driven techniques. This trend is justified by the fact that 82.99% of the studies were published prior to 2023 and only 17.01% after 2023. This review explores the optimization strategies used for mAb production, with a specific focus on the application of process simulation tools and artificial intelligence to improve upstream and downstream bioprocess performance. Thus, the scope of this review is limited to process-level optimization of mAb manufacturing. Detailed discussions of cell line engineering, clone selection biology, formulation development, clinical pharmacology, regulatory validation of viral clearance, and product-specific analytical characterization are not covered in this review. The paper is structured as follows: the initial section gives a process description of mAb production, followed by discussion on optimization strategies using simulation tools in upstream and downstream processes. The alternative downstream purification methods and overall process analysis, such as scheduling, debottlenecking, economic assessment, and environmental impact, are also discussed. The growing trend of AI and ML in early-stage, upstream, and downstream processing is analyzed. The major challenges and opportunities in implementing AI-driven bioprocess solutions are discussed. A future outlook for the integration of smart manufacturing technologies for scalable and cost-effective mAb production is suggested, followed by conclusions at the end.

mAb production: an overview of upstream and downstream bioprocessing

There are two primary steps in the biopharmaceutical process: upstream and downstream. Upstream processing includes the culture of host cells in bioreactors. When the cells achieve the desired density, they are harvested and transported to the next stage of the bioprocess, downstream processing, for purification of the protein of interest from impurities.3,22 Each stage is made up of multiple units that execute a specific role to produce a pure and effective biopharmaceutical product. Prokaryotic microorganisms such as yeast and Escherichia coli (E. coli)36; transgenic organisms such as plant cells, dairy animals (goat’s milk), and chicken systems37,38; and mammalian cells such as Chinese hamster ovary cells (CHO), baby embryo kidney (HEK-293), human retinal cells and mouse myeloma (NS0) cells37 are all examples of host cells used in mAb production. Mammalian cells are the most widely used expression hosts due to their capability to conduct human-like post-translational modifications, which are important for therapeutic activity.

DSP is the recovery and purification of products extracted from natural sources, such as animals or bacterial cells. The upstream mammalian mAb process starts with thawing a vial of cell bank in a suitable medium, followed by inoculum growth of the cell bank with the proper sterilized media in roller bottles. After that, broth is cultivated in a series of seed bioreactors that gradually increase in size or volume. It is necessary to supply a balanced mixture of the required nutrients at concentrations that will allow for excellent growth; therefore, the medium must be homogeneous and well mixed. Then, the broths are moved to the production bioreactor to express proteins in the medium, where the final fermentation process occurs. Finally, centrifugation is used to remove cells and cell debris and is followed by depth and membrane filtration before going ahead to downstream purification processes.

Upstream manufacturing uses four different cell culture conditions: batch, fed-batch, concentrated fed-batch (CFB), and perfusion bioreactors, which are compared in Figure 3. The key differences lie in their working principles, cell density, productivity, titer, and yield. Product titer means the mAb concentration in the harvested culture fluid, usually reported in g/L, whereas volumetric productivity refers to the amount of mAb produced per reactor volume per unit time, usually reported in g/L/day. Yield refers to product recovery across a downstream operation. Step yield describes recovery across an individual unit operation, whereas overall DSP yield describes cumulative product recovery across the complete downstream purification sequence. Biopharmaceutical manufacturers commonly use batch or fed-batch bioreactors with volumes ranging from 5,000 to 25,000 L and process duration of approximately two weeks. The fed-batch strategy is commonly used in upstream processing, where temperature and media composition provide primary control. Concentrated fed-batch uses cell retention technology to keep both cells and product in the bioreactor while allowing medium exchange. This allows higher cell density and a higher final harvest titer than fed-batch mode. However, perfusion bioreactors retain cells inside the continuous stirred tank bioreactor while continuously removing the product containing harvest stream. Therefore, the perfusion approach can be used in continuous ProA production to produce long-term steady-state cell development on small scales. Although perfusion harvest titers are typically lower than that of fed-batch titers,39 perfusion methods can still provide higher volumetric productivity. This advantage depends on operating duration, viable cell density (VCD), dilution rate, and bioreactor volume.

The continuous upstream process uses several types of cell retention devices. These include spin filters (SF), submerged membrane filtration (SMF), tangential flow filtration (TFF), and alternating tangential flow filtration (ATF) to keep higher cell densities and product titers in the bioreactor. The SFs were used to create an annulus around the impeller shaft and to get cell-free culture harvest. In contrast, SMF removes cleared fluid from the cell culture bioreactor through direct insertion of membranes in the bioreactor.40 TFF uses a cell separation unit that is found outside the bioreactor, while ATF uses hollow fiber membrane modules and a low-shear diaphragm pump to circulate the cell suspension through the fibers and back to the bioreactor. The alternating flow reduces cell damage, fouling, and fiber clogging. The benefit of using an external device for filtration is that the module can be easily replaced with little interruption in the bioreactor.40,41

The DSP normally begins with the clarification stage. Centrifuges followed by filtration sets are commonly used in traditional batch processing. This phase is included in the previously described cell retention step for continuous operation. Following the clarification process is the primary capture step, in which ProA chromatography is extensively used in batch manufacturing.39 This separation mechanism is based on the specific interaction between the ProA ligand and the Fc region of mAbs.42 Because of the strong binding affinity and selectivity of ProA to Fc-containing proteins, DNA, host cell proteins (HCPs), and other culture media contaminants can pass through while the desired products bind to the stationary phase.39,40 To inactivate viruses, the eluate from the ProA capture step is subjected to low-pH conditions. This step is called viral inactivation and is usually conducted using mixing tanks for incubation and neutralization to inactivate potential viral contaminants. Further purification processes, such as polishing chromatography, are conducted to remove contaminants in the product that cannot be separated by ProA affinity chromatography. These include ion exchange (IEX) chromatography and hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) stages. The eluate from the polishing processes is then passed through a viral filtration (VF) step for virus removal. Viral filtration is a size-exclusion membrane process that removes potential viral particles from the product stream before performing the ultrafiltration/diafiltration (UF/DF) and final formulation steps. Ultrafiltration concentrates the product mAb, while diafiltration exchanges the buffer and removes small residual impurities.39,43 The concentrated mAb solution is then formulated to the desired buffer composition and product conditions for stability and storage. Further, quality analysis is performed to confirm that the product meets the required specifications before the fill-and-finish operations. Several continuous DSP and formulation-related operations have also been developed to support process integration after capture and polishing. These include single-pass tangential flow filtration (SPTFF), cascade diafiltration, and crystallization techniques. These methods provide product concentration, buffer exchange, or formulation depending on the process design.

Chromatographic intensification can be achieved through either periodic/cyclic multi-column operation or steady-state continuous systems. Periodic counter-current chromatography (PCC),44–46 simulated moving bed chromatography (SMB),40,47 and multi-column counter-current solvent gradient purification (MCSGP)48,49 are cyclic multi-column methods. PCC uses multiple columns that cycle through loading, washing, elution, regeneration, and equilibration steps to improve resin utilization.45 On the other hand, SMB uses several columns with periodically switched inlet and outlet positions to simulate counter-current movement between the mobile and stationary phases.47 MCSGP is a multi-column gradient-based method that improves separation by recycling impure fractions and can be used for capture and polishing.49 These three methods rely on repeated column-switching cycles and are therefore described as periodic/cyclic multi-column operations. In addition, annular chromatography, radial chromatography, and continuous counter-current tangential chromatography (CCTC) are steady-state continuous operations.50–52 Here, steady-state continuous operation refers to continuous inlet and outlet streams maintained under near-constant operating conditions. CCTC uses a flowing resin slurry and membrane modules to allow continuous binding, washing, elution, and regeneration under steady-state operating conditions.

Several strategies have been explored to reduce the cost and bottlenecks associated with primary ProA capture. These strategies can be broadly grouped into ProA process-intensification approaches and non-ProA capture alternatives. ProA intensification approaches include PCC, SMB, CCTC, and related multi-column chromatography (MCC) formats. These are often applied to improve ProA capture by increasing resin utilization, productivity, and buffer efficiency.25,44,53,54 On the other hand, non-ProA alternatives such as protein precipitation (PP),55–57 membrane-based purification,58–60 aqueous two-phase extraction (ATPE),61–63 and phenylboronic acid-based multimodal chromatography (PBA-MMC) aim to reduce dependence on ProA primary capture step.25,53,54,64 These approaches may reduce resin use and improve process intensification, but they require additional steps in the DSP to meet final product quality requirements. Simulation and mathematical optimization strategies in mAb production

In mAb process development, technoeconomic simulation and mathematical optimization are commonly used as complementary tools. Technoeconomic process simulation is applied for the evaluation of scenarios, whereas mathematical optimization is used for decision-making. The following subsections discuss the application of these approaches across USP, DSP, and overall plant-level operation.

Before discussing their applications, it is useful to distinguish the main tools used in this area. Technoeconomic simulation tools, such as SuperPro Designer, Aspen Batch Simulator, and BioSolve Process, are widely used for flowsheet modeling and economic analysis of industrial biotechnology processes.39 These tools evaluate predefined process scenarios by estimating cost of goods (COG), cycle time, equipment utilization, buffer demand, consumable use, and facility bottlenecks. Therefore, they are useful for process evaluation and comparison.3 BioSolve software has been widely used for batch, single-use, and continuous bioprocessing scenarios. Batch process simulations were performed with SuperPro Designer initially, but recently capabilities for continuous processing and economic analysis have been added. Aspen Batch Simulator, on the other hand, has not been widely reported for continuous bioprocessing simulations.

Although these tools are useful for comparing the manufacturing scenarios, their results depend on the input assumptions. During early process development, many critical parameters remain uncertain. These factors include product titer, yield, resin binding capacity and lifetime, buffer consumption, labor requirements, consumable costs, and facility utilization.65 Thus, COG estimates as scenario-based projections. These simulation tools have limited predictive value in the case of novel or insufficiently characterized unit operations. Because immature unit-operation models often require major simplification and assumptions.66 Their outputs are also sensitive to assumptions about buffer preparation and storage, cleaning requirements, resin reuse, and equipment sharing.67 Therefore, uncertainty analysis, sensitivity analysis, and stochastic simulation should be used whenever possible to assess the robustness of simulation-based conclusions.

On the other hand, mathematical optimization methods, such as mixed-integer linear programming (MILP), mixed-integer nonlinear programming (MINLP), stochastic programming, and metaheuristics are used to identify optimal decisions under defined constraints. These methods are suitable for decision-making problems such as scheduling, purification sequence selection, chromatography column sizing, resource allocation, and cost minimization. Most widely used mathematical programming environments include the General Algebraic Modeling System (GAMS) and Matrix Laboratory (MATLAB), which are used to formulate optimization models for process design and decision-making. GAMS is a mathematical programming and optimization software that can model and solve linear, nonlinear, and complex problems.18 Mathematical optimization approaches also have limitations. They are reliable only with accurate formulation of the objective function, constraints, and process relationships.68 Simplified models ignore the key factors like biological variability, nonlinear process behavior, parameter uncertainty, and operational variability.69 Also, MILP and MINLP models get computationally demanding when considering many products, unit operations, scheduling decisions, and uncertainty scenarios together.68 Therefore, optimization results guide decision and need to validate it using experimental data, process simulation, or industrial case studies.

Collectively, these studies show that mAb manufacturing costs are controlled by a few technical and operational factors. In USP, product titer, bioreactor productivity, media consumption, and production scale have a strong influence on COG. For DSP, the major cost and bottleneck factors are the ProA resin cost and lifetime, chromatography capacity, buffer preparation and storage, consumable use, facility utilization, and DSP cycle time. In continuous and intensified processes, the added complexity of integration and control becomes more useful when production demand is high and facilities are better utilized. However, batch or single-use configurations may still be more economical for lower volume or early clinical production because they have lower implementation risk, more operational flexibility, and lower capital cost. Across the studies, common bottlenecks include chromatography capacity, buffer logistics, resin utilization, scheduling constraints, and integration between upstream and downstream operations.

Upstream process optimization

The goal of upstream processing is to maximize cell growth and protein production while maintaining purity and quality. To achieve this, factors such as growth parameters, environmental conditions, nutrient supply, and metabolite accumulation must be properly controlled. Due to the complex and nonlinear nature of cell culture systems, upstream optimization requires balancing productivity, process robustness, and critical quality attributes such as aggregation and glycosylation.

Media and feeding strategies

Media optimization is essential for cell survival, productivity, and product quality. Nutrient composition, such as amino acids, vitamins, and trace elements, affects cell metabolism and mAb production.81 Modern technologies such as metabolomics, proteomics, and fermentanomics have provided insights related to cellular nutrient requirements and the accumulation of inhibitory byproducts. Therefore, such techniques can be used to guide the rational development of customized media formulations to specific production conditions.82 The process performance is also controlled with the help of dynamic feeding strategies. For example, Hi-End pH-Controlled Delivery of Glucose (HIPDOG) and capacitance-based nutrient feeding techniques allowed real-time adjustments of glucose and nutrient supply. These strategies improved cell metabolism and mAb production.83 The increased productivity has been observed with the supplementation of amino acids, growth factors, and metabolic enhancers. The media composition, on the other hand, influences CQAs like glycosylation and aggregation because these are important for therapeutic efficacy and regulatory compliance.84

Recent studies have also applied mathematical and algorithmic models to optimize nutrient feeding more systematically. Jones and Gerogiorgis developed a dynamic optimization framework for industrial fed-batch mAb bioreactors using dynamic flux balance analysis, where nutrient feed profiles were treated as manipulated variables.85 The model considered glucose, glutamine, and asparagine feed trajectories and optimized them using nonlinear programming. Their results showed that glucose manipulation had the strongest effect on mAb yield and viable cell concentration (VCC), followed by glutamine and asparagine. Brinc and Belič also applied a genetic algorithm in an automated micro-bioreactor platform to optimize 14 mammalian fed-batch process parameters, including media- and feeding-related variables.86 This study showed that algorithm-guided experiments can reduce experimental burden while effectively identifying improved operating conditions.

Product quality and aggregation control

Product quality is a key focus in upstream processing, especially in terms of protein aggregation, which can impact safety and therapeutic efficacy. Studies have shown that culture conditions such as temperature and feeding strategies are affecting aggregation behavior. For example, Jones and Gerogiorgis stated that mAb throughput must be optimized and minimize aggregation through the application of good manufacturing practices (GMPs).12 Their study used a dynamic model on CHO cells and examined the influence of process parameters like temperature and feed flow rate on aggregation, where temperature changes have been shown to be more effective. In another study, Paul et al. showed that reducing the culture temperature to around 31°C decreased aggregation levels without affecting overall cell performance.87

On the other hand, the optimization of the feed flow rate in fed-batch is also important. A study reported that increased flow rates of fed-batch systems enhanced mAb titers production to 5 g/L.88 These dynamic optimizations should balance between productivity and quality, since maximizing yield while minimizing aggregation often involves unavoidable trade-offs.12 Altogether, these findings underscore the need for multi-objective optimization that improves yield without compromising quality in upstream bioprocessing.

Upstream conditions can affect CQAs beyond aggregation. They are glycosylation profiles and charge variants. The factors that affect these attributes include culture temperature, pH, nutrient availability, metabolite accumulation, osmolality, and feeding strategy.89 These CQA shifts are important because glycosylation can affect biological activity, stability, pharmacokinetics, and immunogenicity, while charge variants can influence potency, binding affinity, and stability. Higher levels of product-related variants may also increase the downstream purification burden.90 As a result, more stringent polishing chromatography may be required to separate acidic/basic variants, aggregates, glycoforms, or other modified antibody forms. Thus, upstream optimization also includes maintaining CQAs along with increasing titer that provide efficient downstream processing. Ma et al. also showed that multiscale mechanistic models can be used to simulate and optimize mAb glycosylation in CHO cell culture.91 Their quasi-steady-state approach helped reduce the computational burden of glycosylation models. This made model-based optimization more feasible for quality by design (QbD) applications. These studies indicate that upstream product quality control should not only focus on reducing aggregation, but also on controlling glycosylation and other CQAs through integrated process modeling and optimization.

Model-based optimization

Mathematical models have been used to overcome the problems associated with empirical methods. Mathematical models have been developed to account for the impact of various parameters like temperature, glucose, and cell concentration on mAb production. For example, optimal temperature and reactor input-output flow rate strategies have been estimated using mathematical models to maximize development of mAb in combined operational modes (batch, fed-batch, perfusion, and continuous) with the help of algorithms like particle swarm optimization (PSO) and non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm (NSGA-II).9 The impacts of temperature, biomass, glucose, protein, and lactate concentration on the productivity of mAb were considered, and the best feed strategy was determined. With the best feed strategy, it was found that a 5% increase in mAb production was achieved when a reactor had an operating period of 15 days. The resultant protein titer ranged from 1 to 5 g/L, but some have reported higher values (10–13 g/L).92

Studies have further shown that model-based optimization can be used not only to improve mAb titer but also to guide feeding strategies, process control, and product quality decisions. Jones and Gerogiorgis developed dynamic simulation and optimization models for fed-batch and perfusion bioreactors.93 The reactor input and output flow rates were optimized using nonlinear programming to maximize total mAb mass production. Their study showed that fed-batch operation had strong economic advantages, whereas perfusion offered higher mAb yield. This indicates that model-based optimization can support both productivity and technoeconomic decision-making. In a subsequent study, Jones and Gerogiorgis further showed that nutrient feeding strategies can be optimized systematically using model-based approaches, instead of being selected only by trial-and-error experiments.85

Model-based control has also been extended to product quality attributes. Luo et al. developed a multivariable model-based control framework for fed-batch mAb production to address both titer and glycosylation targets.94 This showed that mathematical models can help balance productivity with CQAs by supporting control strategies that maintain high mAb titer while keeping glycosylation profiles within desired ranges. Similarly, Pimentel et al. proposed an end-to-end mechanistic model for laboratory-scale upstream processing, including cell expansion, cell production, flocculation, and filtration.95 Their study reported a 25% increase in mAb production compared with baseline conditions after optimization. These studies demonstrate that model-based optimization is useful for selecting operating conditions, improving feeding strategies, and integrating productivity with quality and process performance.

Operational modes and process intensification

The selection of operational mode has significant effects on upstream performance. Comparative studies between perfusion and fed-batch processes have demonstrated that perfusion systems can achieve higher productivity and resource efficiency than fed-batch processes. For example, Xu et al. compared several operational modes of fed-batch, fed-batch with N-1 perfusion, perfusion, and CFB using the same CHO cell line and media to ensure a fair performance.77 They found that perfusion and CFB modes achieved much higher volumetric productivity, reaching 2.29 and 2.04 g/L/day, respectively, compared with 0.39–0.49 g/L/day for fed-batch processes. This was because perfusion and CFB maintained higher cell densities during production. Perfusion cultures also showed media costs per gram of antibody that were comparable to, or even lower than, those of conventional fed-batch, while CFB had the highest media costs due to its shorter run time and growth-focused phase.77

In a separate economic modeling study, Gupta et al. assumed a mAb titer of 5 g/L for both fed-batch and perfusion processes.55 Under this assumption, the perfusion process achieved this titer within one day, corresponding to 5 g/L/day, while the fed-batch required 12 days to reach the same concentration corresponding to 0.42 g/L/day. Unlike fed-batch, the perfusion process maintained high cell densities for approximately 24 days, increasing daily productivity by more than tenfold.55 However, these systems require more advanced control, higher capital investment, and careful scale-up considerations. As a result, selection of operation mode needs a balance between productivity, process complexity, and cost.

Economic and capacity considerations

The upstream process is a significant cost factor in manufacturing due to factors such as bioreactor costs, media costs, and capacity utilization. The economic analysis by Harrison et al. reported that the upstream processing stage accounts for almost two-thirds of the overall manufacturing cost, in which inoculum preparation stage accounts for almost 45%.22 Moreover, the bioreactor’s capacity and time were fully utilized, and hence the downstream process became idle. The bulk of batch procedures at the upstream processing stage has been cost-effectively improved by introducing multiple bioreactor trains operated in a staggered sequence. According to Petrides et al., installing another production bioreactor (conventional SS type) that works in staggered mode with the original bioreactor reduces the cycle time and increases process throughput.3 Comprehensive cost studies were performed using a process design modeling tool3 as well as a mathematical programming tool18,70 to examine the impact of staggered bioreactors. Although this approach increases both direct and capital costs, it enhances downstream purification train utilization, reduces cycle time, and lowers unit production cost. However, increasing the volume of the bioreactor has no effect on manufacturing costs.

On the other hand, to save capital expenses, researchers recommend the use of SU systems rather than SS wherever feasible. The key benefits of SU technology include lower capital and utility costs, as well as the elimination of cleaning and sterilization steps.17 However, these economic and capacity conclusions are dependent on case-study. The advantages of staggered bioreactor operation, increased bioreactor capacity, or single-use implementation depend on assumptions like production scale, facility configuration, batch schedule, equipment sharing, USP:DSP capacity balance, and facility utilization. Thus, economics of upstream optimization studies vary depending on the scale and facility design used in each case study. These examples show that upstream optimization is linked to economic and operational decisions at the facility level.

Overall, important industrial factors in upstream optimization are harvest titer, volumetric productivity, media cost, process robustness, and product quality. Under suitable process and facility conditions, higher mAb titers and productivity can reduce the required bioreactor volume, improve facility utilization, and lower COG. Optimized media and feeding strategies may also reduce raw material cost and metabolic stress. However, these effects are case-specific and depend on factors such as cell line, feed composition, operating mode, production scale, facility design, and downstream capacity. Productivity gains need to be balanced against CQAs such as aggregation, glycosylation, and charge variants, because changes in product quality can increase downstream polishing requirements. Therefore, upstream optimization should be evaluated not only by harvest concentration or daily productivity, but also by its impact on robustness, downstream burden, and overall manufacturing cost.

Downstream process optimization

As previously stated, ProA chromatography is widely used as the primary capture step for mAbs due to its high selectivity toward Fc-containing molecules.57 However, ProA remains one of the main cost drivers in DSP. This is due to resin cost, low stability of the resin in alkaline cleaning solutions, low binding capacity, high leaching of the ligand, and the need to store the resin in 20% ethanol, which may require fire-proof facilities.26,96 These shortcomings have been the subject of research during the past decade and have resulted in notable innovations. These include the development of alternative ProA purification strategies and purification sequencing and column sizing strategies that are used to optimize the downstream section effectively.

Alternative and intensified processes for mAb primary capture

This section discusses downstream capture and separation methods that are developed to address the limitations of ProA chromatography. This includes non-chromatographic methods, namely aqueous two-phase extraction, membrane-based purification, and protein precipitation, as well as chromatographic intensification or replacement methods that include periodic multi-column chromatography, continuous counter-current tangential chromatography, and multimodal chromatography. Some of these methods, such as PCC and CCTC, can intensify ProA-based capture when ProA resin is used, whereas others, such as precipitation, ATPE, and PBA-MMC, are evaluated as non-ProA alternatives. Aqueous two-phase extraction

ATPE is an extraction process that has gained attention in the non-chromatographic field as it integrates clarification, concentration, and partial purification in a single step.63 These systems rely on polymer-polymer or polymer-salt mixtures that form two aqueous phases to recover proteins. Common examples include polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based systems combined with dextran or suitable salts.63 Rosa et al. evaluated a continuous packed column ATPE system with a pump mixer-settler for Immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody recovery from CHO cell culture supernatant.62 This study reported an IgG step recovery yield of 85%, with about 50% total contaminant removal and more than 85% contaminant protein removal. Therefore, ATPE can be considered as an alternative primary capture strategy to reduce the dependence on ProA chromatography.57 Anupa et al. developed a continuous ATPE-based platform using a PEG-sulfate system, in which the mAb was recovered in the salt phase and further purified by HIC.97 The ATPE step achieved ≥80% recovery and ≥97% intermediate purity in the salt phase, while the integrated ATPE-HIC platform showed improved aggregate and HCP removal. This study also estimated a 28% reduction in consumable cost compared with a traditional ProA-based platform. Together, these examples suggest that ATPE remains a partial purification method, and additional polishing steps are still required to meet platform mAb DSP requirements.

However, large-scale adoption of PEG/salt systems can create challenges related to water treatment, recycling of phase-forming chemicals, equipment corrosion, and target product precipitation.63 ATPE may also increase downstream requirements for phase separation, residual polymer or salt clearance, and buffer exchange. Continuous ATPE, on the other hand, requires careful control of phase formation, mixing, residence time, and mass transfer. This is because poor phase separation, emulsification, flooding, and back mixing can reduce process robustness. Overall, ATPE shows promise for intensified capture, but its maturity level for mAb manufacturing is still at the laboratory-to-pilot scale. Further validation is needed to compare with established ProA primary capture DSP operation.57,63

Membrane-based purification

Membrane-based methods such as depth filtration, membrane adsorbers, and flow-through membrane chromatography, have also been used as alternative purification methods in downstream mAb processing. Depth filters are commonly used for clarification and column protection, but they can also contribute to impurity removal through size-based retention, electrostatic interactions, and hydrophobic adsorptive interactions. In addition, membrane adsorbers functionalized with ion-exchange, hydrophobic interaction, mixed-mode, or affinity ligands can be used for mAb capture, intermediate purification, or polishing. These systems can remove process and product-related impurities such as HCPs, DNA, endotoxins, viruses, and aggregates. Compared with packed-bed chromatography, membrane-based systems offer convective mass transfer, shorter processing time, lower pressure drop, and compatibility with single-use and intensified operation.59,60

Yamada et al. investigated an entirely flow-through purification (EFTP) technique using depth filters and anion-exchange chromatography (AEX).58 Three distinct types of depth filters were used in the study: Millistak A1HC depth filters, Zeta Plus EXT depth filters, and Zeta Plus Activated Carbon depth filters. The results demonstrated that certain depth filter combinations improved HCP and effectively removed high-molecular-weight impurities. The reported step yields were more than 80% for A1HC and EXT depth filtration, about 70% for activated carbon depth filtration, and 70–90% for AEX chromatography, depending on the mAb. However, the overall yield of the complete flow-through purification sequence was 42–47%.

Membrane adsorbers are increasingly important in intensified DSP because they can reduce or replace packed chromatographic columns in selected capture and polishing applications. For example, Schmitz et al. compared single and double flow-through polishing processes using membrane adsorber with AEX, and cation-exchange (CEX), and HIC chromatography functionalities.60 In their single membrane adsorber flow-through experiments, mAb recovery was higher than 98%. However, when two membrane adsorbers were connected in a double flow-through polishing sequence, mAb yield ranged from 69–85%, depending on the order and type of membrane adsorber used. In the combined AEX-CEX membrane adsorber process connected by SPTFF, DNA and HCP levels were reduced to below 2 ppm and 29 ppm, respectively, while maintaining high mAb yield.

These methods also introduce downstream challenges. In the case of depth filters, key challenges include solids handling before filtration, filter sizing, fouling, pressure increase, product loss, and variable impurity clearance.58,59 For membrane adsorbers, major limitations include lower binding capacity than packed-bed resins, impurity breakthrough, fouling, feed conditioning requirements, and the need to validate impurity and viral clearance.59,60 Therefore, membrane-based purification method still requires integration with appropriate polishing, VF, and UF/DF steps to meet final product quality requirements.59 Overall, depth filtration is well established for clarification and column protection in mAb DSP, while membrane-based purifications are also widely used for VF, sterile filtration, UF/DF, and selected polishing applications. In contrast, membrane adsorber-based mAb capture is more case-specific and still requires further large-scale demonstration.

Protein precipitation

Another non-chromatography alternative is PP, which becomes more selective as product concentration increases. Conventional precipitation strategies include caprylic acid (CapA) and calcium chloride (CaCl2) precipitation for contaminant removal, as well as PEG and cold ethanol precipitation (CEP) for mAb precipitation.72 To improve performance and process consistency, continuous precipitation approaches have been developed using tubular reactor systems. Hammerschmidt et al. transferred the previously developed two-stage precipitation process72 to continuous tubular reactor and reported a precipitation-step yield of more than 90%, with reductions in HCP and DNA levels.56 This study also showed that continuous operation can improve control of mixing, residence time, and cooling compared with batch precipitation. In a later study, continuous sequential PEG precipitation and re-solubilization were developed using a single-use tubular reactor and TFF for precipitate capture.98 The result showed 86–94% precipitation-step yield and suggested that TFF can provide precipitate concentration, washing, and redissolution without compromising yield. Similarly, PEG/zinc chloride (ZnCl2)-based precipitation in a tubular reactor was integrated with two-stage tangential flow microfiltration for continuous precipitation and washing was explored.99 This integrated precipitation-TFF capture process achieved 95% yield and 97% antibody purity.

Further, reactor development studies have focused on improving precipitation. A coiled flow inversion reactor (CFIR) is developed by Kateja et al. which improves mixing and residence time distribution by creating secondary flow patterns.26 A non-ProA continuous purification platform was designed using CFIR-based precipitation, followed by CEX for capture and MMC and AEX for polishing. The platform allowed impurity removal and low-pH viral inactivation in an integrated format.55 The integrated system achieved an overall process yield of approximately 70–80% from clarified harvest to unformulated drug substance across three different mAbs.26 On the other hand, Royo et al. used milli-scale reactors to study precipitant dosing, floc formation, and filterability.100 The results showed that multiple precipitant additions improved purity by a factor of 3 compared with single addition and improved filterability in TFF and depth filtration. In addition, an oscillatory flow reactor (OFR)-based continuous precipitation with PEG/ZnCl2 has been designed to improve mixing at lower net flow rates.101 This study achieved more than 99% step recovery and 90% intermediate purity.

CFIR based precipitation methods can reduce dependence on ProA chromatography, lower resin use, and support process intensification.27 In addition, the cost analyses indicate that non-ProA continuous purification strategies can provide significant cost savings compared to conventional (batch) ProA-based processes, on both small and larger production scales. However, precipitation-based approaches introduce method-specific downstream challenges. In tubular precipitation, main challenges include control of precipitant addition, cooling, solid-liquid separation, precipitate recovery, and redissolution.56 In CFIR-based precipitation, main challenges include control of mixing and residence time, precipitate particle-size distribution, depth filter sizing or switching, fouling or pressure increase during filtration, pH conditioning before subsequent chromatography, and integration with polishing steps.26

Overall, precipitation-based mAb purification is promising as an alternative capture or impurity-reduction strategy. However, broader commercial implementation still requires further demonstration of scalability, robust solid-liquid separation and filtration, impurity and viral clearance, product recovery, and regulatory acceptance.

Periodic multi-column chromatography

PCC is a cyclic multi-column chromatography method used to intensify mAb capture, particularly when ProA resins are used. Unlike traditional single column chromatography, PCC uses multiple packed columns in series to perform feed loading, washing, elution, regeneration, and equilibrium steps. During loading, breakthrough from one column can be captured by a second column thereby providing greater resin capacity utilization and lower buffer usage especially when processing high-titer feeds compared with conventional batch chromatography.45

Recent studies have focused on model-based optimization of PCC to improve productivity and resin utilization. Shi et al. evaluated three-column PCC for continuous mAb capture.46 This study results reported a high capture step productivity along with 97.6% resin capacity utilization under optimized operating conditions.46 Similarly, Gomis-Fons et al. results showed that PCC performance is influenced not only by the capture step but also by its integration with the downstream process schedule.102 These key factors include product recovery time, loading yield, feed concentration, and resin selection. Konoike et al. developed an integrated continuous DSP using two-column ProA PCC for capture, followed by low-pH viral inactivation, flow-through polishing, and virus filtration.103 This process achieved above 70% overall mAb recovery and more than 98% intact mAb content in most runs. These studies suggest that PCC is an intensified alternative to traditional single-column ProA capture, but VI and polishing steps are still required in the full mAb DSP train.

On the other hand, Chen et al. developed a mechanistic model using mass transfer coefficients and the Langmuir isotherm parameters to support PCC process design.104 This study showed that breakthrough cutoff, residence time, feed concentration, resin properties, resin utilization, and buffer consumption are important factors influencing process performance. A three-column PCC for both capture and polishing in continuous chromatography at high protein load was also explored using in silico optimization with a mechanistic model.105 The results reported that model-based optimization improved capacity utilization and productivity while maintaining yield. The model predictions were experimentally validated, and a sequential 3C-PCC process was proposed with productivity of approximately 100 mg/mL resin/h.

Other periodic multi-column methods include SMB chromatography40,47 and MCSGP.48,49 SMB simulates counter-current movement by periodically switching column positions, while MCSGP combines gradient elution with internal recycling of impure fractions. Müller-Späth et al. showed the use of a four-column MCSGP method to collect mAbs from clarified cell culture fluid using CEX resins and gradient elution.48 An average process-specific chromatography yield of around 90% was achieved over long processing times in MCSGP.

Overall, PCC is one of the more mature intensification strategies for mAb DSP, especially for ProA capture. This maturity is supported by model-based PCC optimization studies, high resin utilization and capture-step productivity, and implementation of continuous ProA capture in integrated single-use GMP downstream processes.45,46,106 However, these methods can create downstream integration challenges because their cyclic elution profiles may lead to intermittent product pools, variable pool volumes and concentrations, and scheduling constraints for VI, polishing, VF, and UF/DF.102,103 Scale-up and implementation also require robust column switching, breakthrough monitoring, process control, residence time characterization, viral safety integration, and regulatory justification of batch definition and material traceability.45,106

Continuous counter-current tangential chromatography

In contrast to periodic multi-column methods such as PCC, SMB, and MCSGP, CCTC is designed as a steady-state continuous chromatography approach. CCTC utilizes chromatographic resin in slurry form, which flows through a sequence of static mixing devices and hollow fiber membrane modules.40 The hollow fiber membranes retain the resin particles, while proteins and buffer components pass through the membrane. This allows binding, washing, elution, stripping, and equilibration to occur continuously without packed or repeated columns switching.

Dutta et al. developed a ProA-based CCTC technique for mAb purification from clarified CHO cell culture fluid using a commercial ProA resin in slurry form.50 The process achieved 90.4% and 92.8% CCTC capture-step yields for two types of mAbs, with HCP reduced to 179 ppm and 2,195 ppm, respectively. This study also showed that removal of HCP, antibody yield, and product purity were comparable to those obtained with conventional packed column ProA chromatography. However, CCTC technology demonstrated significantly higher productivity. In a follow-up optimization study, CCTC performance was improved by adjusting stages, buffer recycling, resin particle size, and binding conditions.51 This capture-step resulted in a 96% product yield and a 3.4-log reduction in impurity levels, along with 40% reduction in buffer usage. Fedorenko et al. further reported 91–95% CCTC capture-process yield using an improved small particle ProA resin, along with 99.5% intact mAb content and HCP of 420 ppm.52 This study also showed improved productivity and buffer efficiency compared with previous CCTC studies using commercial ProA resins.

CCTC provides several advantages, this includes steady-state operation, high resin utilization, lower pressure drops, reduced packed column handling, and compatibility with disposable flow paths.40,50–52 However, CCTC is still less mature than PCC for mAb manufacturing.52 The main scale-up and implementation challenges include control of resin slurry concentration, membrane fouling, critical flux operation, resin retention, slurry pumping, residence time control, resin attrition, process monitoring, and validation of viral safety and impurity clearance.50–52 Overall, CCTC is promising at laboratory-to-pilot scale as a ProA-based capture-intensification method, but it still requires integration with VI, polishing, VF, and UF/DF.

Phenylboronic acid-based multimodal chromatography (PBA-MMC)

Phenylboronic acid (PBA) is a synthetic ligand used in boronate affinity and multimodal chromatography.64 PBA-based systems exploit reversible interactions between boronate groups and cis-diol-containing glycan structures on antibodies, together with other secondary interactions such as hydrophobic, electrostatic, hydrogen-bonding, and Lewis acid-base interactions.64 As a result, PBA has been proposed as a non-ProA chromatography alternative for mAb capture.

Santos et al. evaluated PBA chromatography for IgG purification from clarified CHO cell supernatant.53 The result reported approximately 99% capture-step recovery yield along with 81% intermediate protein purity. Rosa et al. expanded this work by scaling up PBA chromatography, resulting in a recovery yield of 97.7% and an intermediate protein purity of 82.6% after the PBA-MMC capture step.54 Further, Grilo et al. performed an economic comparison of this alternative with ProA-based purification processes using SuperPro Designer.25 The results showed that both processes required similar capital investment, but PBA-MMC-based strategy had approximately 20% lower operating cost. This suggests that PBA-MMC may provide cost advantages by reducing dependence on ProA resin.

Overall, advantages of PBA-MMC include high capture-step recovery, use of a synthetic ligand, lower operating cost, and simple chromatographic operation.25,64 However, its impurity clearance is lower than conventional ProA capture, as shown by the intermediate purities reported after the PBA-MMC step.53,54 In addition, viral clearance, ligand leaching, resin lifetime, robustness to feed variability, and regulatory acceptance remain less established than for ProA chromatography. Major scale-up barriers include maintaining consistent binding and elution performance across variable feed streams, demonstrating resin lifetime and ligand-leaching control, validating impurity and viral clearance, and integrating the step with downstream polishing.54 Therefore, PBA-MMC remains a lab-to-preparative-scale method and can be considered a partial capture alternative rather than a complete DSP replacement.

Purification sequences and chromatographic column sizing

Along with the development of alternative and intensified purification methods, downstream process optimization also requires systematic selection of purification sequences, chromatography column dimensions, resin selection, and control strategies. These decisions are important because chromatography steps represent a major portion of downstream processing cost and may become bottlenecks when upstream titers increase.18,107 This challenge is even greater in flexible multi-product facilities, where different product properties, impurity profiles, resin performance, and process requirements must be considered without causing excessive manufacturing costs.29 Thus, optimization models, digital-twin-based process development, high-throughput purification workflows, and automated control strategies are increasingly being explored to support downstream process design and operation.

Simaria et al. proposed a metaheuristic optimization technique based on evolutionary algorithms.29 This approach helps to simplify the design of multi-product facilities by considering many decisions, trade-offs, and limitations simultaneously. These include the selection of upstream to downstream equipment trains, suitable purification sequence, and chromatography column sizing strategies. Process development teams can use these results to identify effective methods and focus improvements on resin performance to remove host cell impurities.

Similarly, Liu et al. developed a MILP model for chromatography column sizing in mAb purification process.108 This study evaluated decision variables such as column diameter, bed height, number of columns, and number of cycles, with the aim of minimizing COG per gram and improving facility fit under different USP to DSP configurations. They found that increasing upstream titers can increase the protein load on chromatography steps and create downstream bottlenecks if column sizing is not properly optimized. This work was further expanded to integrate column sequencing with column sizing decisions using an MINLP-based model.18 The output reported the optimized resin choice at each chromatographic step along with column diameter, bed height, number of columns, and number of cycles per batch.

Allmendinger et al. further expanded this area using an evolutionary multi-objective optimization model that linked directly to a biomanufacturing process economics model.109 This work optimized chromatographic purification sequences and column sizing strategies by considering balance among COG per gram, robustness, and impurity removal under uncertainty. These authors further introduced mathematical programming approaches by considering continuous bed-height values and hierarchical solution methods to improve computational efficiency.28 To address process uncertainties, a multi-objective mixed-integer optimization model was also developed using the Dinkelbach algorithm and the ε-constraint method.70 Product titer, chromatography resin yield, and impurity reduction capabilities were the uncertain factors that have a strong influence on the process productivity and cost. The model identified the best COG per gram and high impurity removal efficiency, along with chromatography sequencing and column sizing plan.

In addition to purification sequence and column sizing decisions, resin selection is an important part of downstream process optimization. Silva et al. developed a digital-twin-based strategy for high-throughput chromatographic process development of mAbs.110 The study combined experimental resin screening with computational modeling to select suitable chromatography conditions. In the experimental stage, equilibrium adsorption behavior was evaluated for 13 ProA and 16 CEX chromatography resins. The chromatographic behavior was then described using a lumped kinetic model, in which the overall mass-transfer coefficient was used as an important model parameter. This hybrid work connected experimental results with in silico process simulations, thereby supporting resin selection and process development. However, it is a hybrid digital-twin approach rather than a purely ML-based method.

Similarly, automated high-throughput purification workflows can support downstream process development. Anderson et al. demonstrated an automated microscale ProA purification workflow for screening resin type, wash conditions, and elution pH across three mAbs and one bispecific antibody.111 The study showed that resin and buffer selection strongly affected HCP clearance, purity, aggregate formation, and monomer yield. This high-throughput automation strategy can generate structured datasets suitable for future ML-guided purification optimization. In addition, Thakur et al. developed an automated downstream control strategy to handle dynamic harvest titer variations in continuous mAb manufacturing.112 In this study, real-time near-infrared (NIR)-based concentration sensors were used to monitor harvest titer, and the signals were connected into an in-house Python-based controller. This control system adjusted the downstream train, which included ProA capture, VI, depth filtration, CEX polishing, and single-pass formulation. The strategy was tested over 36 h with induced titer variations ranging from 1–7 g/L. The controller selected one of nine predefined methods, each with different column configurations ranging from 3–6 ProA columns and 2–10 CEX columns. This study demonstrates an automated control strategy that can adjust downstream operation in response to upstream titer variability.

Overall, these studies show that optimization-based decision tools can support downstream process design by improving chromatography configuration, resin selection, column sizing, process control, and facility fit. Digital-twin-based and high-throughput approaches can also generate structured datasets and support faster evaluation of resin and buffer conditions. However, these models are case-specific, and their economic or operational benefits can vary depending on the assumptions used such as feed titer, resin performance, impurity profile, facility configuration, USP:DSP ratio, and process-scale constraints.

Overall plant-level optimization

Plant-level optimization integrates the upstream and downstream decisions discussed in the previous sections with facility-level objectives such as cost reduction, production planning and scheduling, debottlenecking, cycle time reduction, capacity utilization, and environmental impact minimization. At the manufacturing level, these activities are directly linked to practical decisions such as campaign planning, product-changeover strategy, equipment sharing, capacity utilization, and the ability to respond to demand surges.3 Cost analysis helps to identify the expensive unit process and shows where it can be reduced without affecting the quality of the product. Cycle time reduction and debottlenecking can increase production throughput by balancing workload, identifying limiting equipment or resources, and adjusting process parameters. Effective planning and scheduling are also important to ensure the efficient use of equipment, labor, utilities, materials, and storage capacity, especially in multi-product facilities. At the same time, adopting sustainable practices is essential to reduce waste, energy consumption, and environmental pollution.16,76 Thus, this section aims to discuss the methods used to optimize the overall production process and critically examine the cost, operational, capacity, and environmental implications of the different alternatives that were mentioned previously.

Process scheduling

Scheduling in biopharmaceutical manufacturing involves allocating specific resources to each production campaign, deciding on batch start times, balancing workload, and planning how much of each product must be produced to meet market demand. Therefore, planning and scheduling are essential for running cost-effective manufacturing. Process scheduling bridges the gap between plant-level objectives and executable campaign plans by determining product sequence, equipment allocation, changeover timing, storage requirements, and delivery schedules. Moreover, these scheduling decisions are also affected by upstream and downstream interactions. The higher upstream titers can increase downstream loading and storage requirements, while changes in impurity profiles or product quality may require additional polishing, longer downstream cycle times, or different campaign spacing.

For example, Petrides et al. and Koulouris et al. used SuperPro Designer to schedule each step in the different unit operations to ensure that the production process runs smoothly and on time.3,17 Their schedule required about 50 days per batch, but a new batch began every 14 days. This allowed the factory to operate 330 days per year and complete approximately 21 batches per year. Such scheduling analysis is useful because it links process design with facility utilization, equipment availability, and batch-cycle timing.

Decision tools such as metaheuristics, evolutionary algorithms, and mathematical optimization models based on linear programming (LP), MILP, and MINLP can also be utilized for planning and scheduling. For example, Kabra et al. developed a unit-specific event-based MILP model for multi-period scheduling of a multi-stage, multi-product biopharmaceutical process.32 The model includes sequence-dependent changeovers, multiple demand periods for intermediates, limited shelf-life with disposal of expired products, and penalty cost terms for backlog and late deliveries. These features are directly relevant because they determine how product campaigns are sequenced, how changeover losses are minimized, and how late or unmet demand is penalized. In addition, Shaik et al. formulated a MILP scheduling model of a multi-product, multi-purpose biotech batch facility using a state-task network formulation in discrete and continuous time.113 This model considers fixed processing times, shared equipment, transition times, and unlimited storage policy.

Vieira et al. developed a continuous-time MILP model for planning and campaign scheduling of biopharmaceutical processes.114 Their formulation considered batch and continuous process steps, multiple intermediate deliveries, sequence-dependent changeovers, product shelf-life limitations, and lot tracking for regulatory control. This type of model connects campaign planning with product traceability, delivery dates, storage feasibility, and changeover strategy. Similarly, Siganporia et al. developed a MILP-based capacity planning framework for batch and perfusion bioprocesses across multiple facilities.115 This study included sequence-dependent changeover times, continuous culture constraints, decoupled upstream and downstream scheduling, strategic inventory penalties, and demand or titer uncertainty. Such approaches can support decisions on whether demand surges should be handled by existing in-house capacity, outsourced to contract manufacturing organizations, or addressed through future facility expansion.

Meanwhile, Kumar et al. reviewed commonly used scheduling models for multi-product biopharmaceutical facilities and identified several weaknesses.116 The identified limitations are storage violations, early product deliveries, inadequate mapping of tasks, missing setup times, incomplete sequencing of storage, and shelf-life misrepresentations. To address these issues, they proposed improvements to unit-specific, event-based models by adding more accurate material balances, proper storage sequencing, shelf-life and setup constraints, and updated storage limits. Kumar and Shaik extended this work by evaluating deterministic and stochastic/evolutionary scheduling strategies in discrete- and continuous-time frameworks.30 In these studies, their improved model increased profit by up to 36% compared with previous models, while maintaining feasible storage, shelf-life, and delivery schedules. Recently, Kumar and Shaik further extended this unit-specific, event-based continuous-time MILP framework by introducing two long-term scheduling models for continuous multi-stage biopharmaceutical processes.31 These models incorporate specific inventory planning and different delivery options for the final product. The first model considers delivery on or after the due date with either instantaneous or finite known delivery rates. The second model is based on early delivery with finite but unknown delivery rate, improved material balances, sales and penalty terms, and shelf-life constraints. Both models provided more realistic inventory and delivery schedules in benchmark case studies and achieved higher profit compared with previous formulations.

Overall, scheduling studies show that plant-level performance depends on the coordination of process timing, shared resources, storage, and delivery requirements across the entire manufacturing facility. However, a major limitation is that many scheduling models are case-specific. If factors like setup times, changeovers, shelf-life, storage capacity, and demand uncertainty are simplified or ignored, the model may give unrealistic results. Therefore, scheduling outputs are best used as decision-support tools for feasible campaign planning and capacity management.

Process debottlenecking

Debottlenecking is the process of identifying the steps, equipment, or resources that limit production throughput and then modifying the process. This may include adding, upgrading, changing operating schedules, improving resin or filter capacity, modifying buffer preparation and storage, or adjusting upstream and downstream operating conditions. Debottlenecking and throughput analysis are therefore important plant-level tools because these help to shorten batch times, increase output, improve facility utilization, and reduce manufacturing costs.

Bottlenecks may change depending on the process configuration, production scale, and upstream productivity. For example, in conventional mAb batch production, upstream bioreactors often act as the primary bottleneck. Petrides et al. showed that this bottleneck could be reduced by installing additional bioreactors and operating the bioreactor in a staggered manner so that the second bioreactor started halfway through the first bioreactor’s cycle.3 This reduced the cycle time from 14 to 7 days and increased overall throughput. However, as upstream titers increase, the bottleneck can shift from upstream capacity to downstream purification, buffer handling, or storage. Yang et al. reported that higher-titer mAb processes can cause facility-fit problems in the following DSP trains. This is because chromatography columns, tanks, and other purification resources may no longer handle the increased protein load. Their analysis evaluated debottlenecking strategies such as changing equipment sizes, using higher-capacity chromatography resins, and optimizing elution buffer conditions.

In continuous biopharmaceutical manufacturing, the bottleneck often shifts toward the downstream process. Ding et al. showed that when upstream production was increased in an integrated continuous mAb process, MCC operations became the process bottleneck.16 In their study, ProA chromatography was the first limiting step, followed by CEX and AEX chromatography, depending on the capacity utilization of each step. This suggests that gains in upstream titer or perfusion rate alone are insufficient to increase overall plant throughput. Thus, downstream steps such as capture, polishing, filtration, and buffer systems must also be appropriately sized and scheduled.

As discussed in the upstream and downstream optimization sections, increasing upstream titer can improve bioreactor productivity but also increases the load on chromatography and polishing steps. For example, a 10,000 L bioreactor operated at 2 g/L generates about 20 kg mAb per harvest. The same bioreactor operated at 5 g/L generates about 50 kg per harvest. If the existing ProA capture train can process only 25 kg/day, the 20 kg batch can be captured within one day. In contrast, the 50 kg high-titer batch requires about two days, shifting the bottleneck from USP to DSP. Plant-level scheduling can then convert this bottleneck into an executable campaign plan. The schedule may delay or stagger the next bioreactor harvest until the ProA train has completed the two-day capture window. Alternatively, the plant may add extra capture shifts or chromatography cycles to shorten DSP processing time. Surge/hold capacity can also be introduced so that harvested material can wait without interrupting upstream operation. If simulation shows that these actions are still created with polishing, buffer preparation, cleaning, or product changeover activities, further equipment debottlenecking may be required. This could include larger or higher-capacity ProA columns, additional buffer tanks, or revised changeover timing.

Resource-related bottlenecks may arise from labor, utilities, raw materials, buffer preparation, storage tanks, and consumable availability, and can change over time. When labor demand varies, the number of operators in each shift should be adjusted to match the peak demand periods. Using dedicated or flexible operators in multi-product facilities can also help to reduce labor constraints. However, some resource-based bottlenecks may be unavoidable and can limit the plant’s overall production capability.17 Overall, debottlenecking can be supported by process simulation, mathematical modeling, and scheduling analysis because they can account for shared facility resources, inventories, cycle time, throughput, and equipment utilization.

For conventional SS fed-batch manufacturing, reported COG values vary with production scale and upstream titer. For example, an SS fed-batch process operated at 1,580 kg/year with a 1.5 g/L titer and ProA resin replacement after 60 cycles reported a COG of approximately $106/g3. In another commercial scale analysis using a 5 g/L fed-batch titer and resin reuse of 200 cycles, the COG decreased from approximately $275/g at 100 kg/year to $53/g at 3,000 kg/year.71 These results show that scale-up improves cost efficiency in conventional batch manufacturing, mainly through better equipment utilization and distribution of fixed costs over larger product volumes.

SU fed-batch systems showed advantages mainly at small production scales.16,71 Under the same 5 g/L fed-batch process basis, SU fed-batch manufacturing reduced COG by approximately 35% relative to SS fed-batch at 100 kg/year and 5% at 500 kg/year.71 However, this advantage decreased at higher annual demands, where SS facilities became competitive. At 1,000–3,000 kg/year, the reported SU fed-batch COG was higher than the SS fed-batch baseline.71 Similarly, continuous and hybrid manufacturing methods showed lower COG at small and medium production volumes, particularly when compared with SS fed-batch baselines. For example, at 100–500 kg/year, SU end-to-end continuous and SU hybrid processes showed 35% lower COG than SS fed-batch processes when modeled under baseline assumptions.71 At 2,000–3,000 kg/year, continuous and hybrid SU systems showed similar or slightly higher COG values (8%) than SS fed-batch systems.71 Thus, the economic benefit of SU systems is scale and facility dependent, while continuous processing is not automatically more economical at very large commercial scales.

Studies related to fed-batch and integrated continuous manufacturing also reported scale dependencies. In a commercial scenario at 500 kg/year and 5 g/L titer, a 35% reduction was achieved when batch manufacturing was converted into continuous, whereas a 68% reduction was achieved in the corresponding clinical-scale scenario at 15 kg/year.55 A similar scale-dependent trend was observed for precipitation-based continuous and hybrid downstream platforms.72 This indicates that integrated continuous processing can provide particularly large cost reductions in low-volume or clinical manufacturing, where smaller equipment footprints, higher facility utilization, and reduced hold volumes can have a greater economic impact.

The selection of downstream platforms also influenced manufacturing costs. In a modeled facility producing 500 kg/year at 5 g/L titer, a fed-batch ProA platform had a reported COG of around $40.67/g. When switched to continuous ProA processing, the COG was reduced to 33%.27 Replacing ProA with non-ProA purification using CFIR-based precipitation decreased costs even further.27 A batch non-ProA platform showed a 28% reduction compared to batch ProA, while a continuous non-ProA platform achieved 30% lower cost than continuous ProA and 53% lower cost than batch ProA.27 These savings came from lower resin-related costs, improved downstream productivity, and reduced consumable demand. However, the magnitude of savings depends on the factors such as assumed capture yield, resin lifetime, buffer consumption, and the purification step used.

On the other hand, alternative capture methods also showed case-specific cost reduction. For continuous SU processes at 100–1,000 kg/year, three capture methods, namely, continuous ProA capture, PP capture, and ATPE capture were evaluated under the same perfusion upstream basis.57 The results showed that continuous ProA capture had the lowest COG among these options, while PP and ATPE had higher COG values due to lower overall downstream yields and additional reagent or buffer requirements. In another high-titer commercial case, a PBA-MMC platform reduced COG by 44% compared to a ProA batch process under the specific assumptions used in that study.25

Overall, economic comparison studies in mAb manufacturing show that no single mAb manufacturing strategy is universally the most cost-effective. At large commercial scales, SS fed-batch systems remain economically attractive due to the high-throughput and efficient use of fixed assets. On the other hand, SU and continuous systems provide advantages at clinical, small-, and medium-scales production, especially when flexibility and reduced capital investment are important. Hybrid systems can be useful when fed-batch USP is retained, and continuous DSP is added to reduce cost or improve resin and buffer use. However, as titer and throughput increase, downstream steps such as ProA capture, polishing chromatography, filtration, buffer preparation, and storage can become major bottlenecks. Therefore, continuous and hybrid systems are most beneficial when downstream capacity is designed to match the increased upstream productivity.

Environmental impact minimization

Recent studies have shown that environmental sustainability has become one of the major drivers of innovation in the manufacturing industry. In mAb production, there is a clear shift from traditional SS batch to SU and continuous processing. This is mainly because these approaches have both economic and environmental advantages.57,80 Pietrzykowski et al. reported that more than 70% of the total environmental impact comes from cleaning, steaming, general utilities, ProA capture, and bioreactor operations.80 Therefore, sustainability comparisons must be considered using clear metrics.

Conventional SS processes are highly dependent on water for injection, cleaning operations, and clean steam. This explains why SS facilities have a large water and energy footprint. These burdens are reduced by the replacement of fixed-in-place SS equipment with SU systems. This is because SU systems do not require cleaning and sterilization, thereby leading to lower water consumption, energy consumption, and environmental impact. In addition to consuming resources, mAb production also releases wastewater. This wastewater is a source of environmental load. It contains high levels of solvents, organic pollutants, and other toxic substances. Thus, it requires intensive treatment techniques like coagulation, floatation, biological oxidation, or advanced oxidation.118 These findings illustrate the fact that environmental impact results not only with resource inputs but also with waste outputs. However, the environmental benefit of SU systems needs to interpret together with their disposable plastic waste burden.

Process mass intensity (PMI), E-factor, and CO2 emissions are common metrics used to measure environmental performance. PMI measures the total mass of input materials required to produce a unit mass of product,76,119 while E-factor measures the mass of waste generated per unit mass of product.16,120 CO2 emissions, usually reported as CO2-equivalent emissions, represent the climate-related impact associated with energy use, utilities, raw materials, transport, and waste treatment.121 For example, Madabhushi et al. evaluated different process versions to reduce environmental impacts using the PMI metric and found that a semi-continuous process using perfusion, ProA three-column PCC, and AEX membrane reduced PMI by 23% compared with a fed-batch process with two-column chromatography.119 In this analysis, water was the most significant contributor, accounting for about 92–94% of the overall PMI, while upstream operations contributed 32–47% and chromatography operations contributed 34–54%.

Similarly, Ding et al. used E-factor to evaluate a fully integrated continuous mAb process with SU technologies and reported that process water accounted for about 99.7% of total E-factor.16 Pollock et al. also used E-factor, together with COG and operational risk to compare batch, hybrid, and integrated continuous bioprocessing strategies.120 Their results showed that continuous capture and ATF perfusion-based configurations reduced water and consumable E-factors compared with the base case. Thus, both PMI and E-factor-based studies indicate that water, buffer, and consumable use are the important contributors to the environmental burden of mAb manufacturing. However, both factors do not fully describe all environmental burdens, because they may not capture non-process water use, energy demand, CO2 emissions, and waste-treatment impacts. Therefore, LCA-based metrics are useful when comparing broader environmental impacts of fed-batch, perfusion, SS, and SU systems.

Neves et al. examined batch versus continuous processing and column-based versus column-free mAb capture strategies using PMI-based analysis.57 Their results showed that continuous flowsheets can reduce PMI compared to conventional batch operations. However, the environmental benefit depended on the capture step. Continuous ProA showed favorable options. In contrast, continuous column-free approaches like continuous ATPE and continuous PP showed higher water-related PMI values due to reagent and buffer requirements. The results of ATPE showed that it can reduce operating costs, but its environmental impact is affected by using substantial amounts of PEG and phosphate. These findings show that continuous and column-free processes are not automatically more sustainable; their environmental performance depends on water use, reagent demand, buffer consumption, and process configuration.

On the other hand, SU process technologies can also have adverse effects on the environment. This is because they depend on consumable products that are used once and then discarded.80 Although SU systems can reduce water from cleaning, steam, energy, and chemical use, these benefits must be balanced against plastic waste generation from bags, tubing, filters, connectors, and other disposable components. The overall sustainability of SU systems depends on production scale, local energy mix, waste treatment method, recycling availability, and whether plastic waste is landfilled, incinerated, pyrolyzed, or recovered through circular economy approaches. However, recycling, reuse, and reduction of plastics have gained popularity, and valuable steps have been taken for a circular plastic economy.15,122 The principles of sustainable design are being adopted in the design of processes, operations, and choices of raw material and technology.

Beyond PMI and E-factor, CO2-equivalent emissions are also important for evaluating climate-related impacts. These include emissions from energy use, utilities, raw material preparation, transport, and waste treatment. Partopour and Pollard showed that end-to-end continuous bioprocessing can reduce total production costs, facility footprint, plastic waste, and CO2 emissions by 23%, 51%, 57%, and 54%, respectively.121 These results suggest that continuous processing can improve sustainability not only by reducing material and water use but also by lowering the carbon footprint of mAb manufacturing.

Overall, minimizing environmental impact in the production of mAbs can be achieved through water and energy reduction strategies. The advanced upstream and downstream process design, smarter use of materials, and optimized purification workflows are included in it. Continuous and SU systems can provide sustainability advantages by reducing facility footprint, water consumption, energy demand, and cleaning-related utilities. However, issues such as plastic waste handling, reagent use, buffer consumption, and waste treatment routes must be balanced carefully to achieve these benefits. Future development may depend on process integration, automation, AI-controlled processes, and adoption of circular economy principles to support environmentally friendly and resource-efficient biopharmaceutical production.

AI-based optimization strategies in mAb production

Researchers are focusing on the production techniques that are less dependent on human involvement and more flexible in responding to changing needs caused by the spread of the coronavirus pandemic. AI combines computer-based analysis with data-driven decision-making. ML, as a branch of AI, learns patterns from data and develops mathematical relationships for prediction, classification, optimization, and control. In mAb manufacturing, ML techniques can improve understanding of CQAs and CPPs. High-throughput studies, including clone selection, media optimization, feed strategy design, resin choice, and formulation development, can also be expedited using supervised learning and other ML approaches. Furthermore, ML can forecast changes in product quality and support efficient decision-making, which may allow real-time monitoring and future real-time product release strategies. As a result, techniques based on ML in biopharmaceutical process development and manufacturing can reduce time, cost, and experimental burden.34 This section reviews recent applications of AI/ML in early-stage mAb development, USP, and DSP, followed by model lifecycle and GMP deployment considerations.

Applications of AI in early-stage bioprocess development

Early-stage mAb development requires the selection of candidates that combine biological activity with acceptable developability, stability, solubility, viscosity, low aggregation tendency, and low off-target binding. Antibody discovery campaigns can generate hundreds to thousands of candidates, complete experimental characterization of every molecule is time-consuming, material-intensive, and costly. Therefore, researchers increasingly use AI, ML, molecular modeling, and biopharmaceutical informatics to support early developability assessment and prioritize candidates. This section discusses these applications in the following subsections.

Early developability screening and deployment readiness

Due to the heterogeneity and complexity of biological products, as well as the limited availability of biomolecule databases, the early application of ML in mAb discovery and development faced several constraints.123 However, the coordination of protein sequence information into libraries of candidate binders for specific therapeutic targets has been enabled by computational and ML methods.124 The contents of these libraries are obtained using phage display, hybridoma, and next-generation sequencing. They can then be used to identify potential sites for biological treatment candidates or degradation/aggregation. Informatics tools of biopharmaceutical include abYmod, AbPred, CamSol, RosettaAntibody, QikProp, and others. These are often used to evaluate the computational development capabilities of antibody-based treatments.125 Puranik et al. suggested that integrating these methods in the development of antibody can provide more informed candidate selection and reduce a lot of resources and time to develop.34 It can also allow molecular redesign to address structural or sequence-based limitations.

These large antibody libraries cannot be fully characterized using extensive experimental assays at the discovery stage. Therefore, early developability screening has become an important bridge between computational prediction and laboratory validation.126 The aim of this screening is to identify antibodies with unfavorable physicochemical properties before they enter later development stages. Practical workflows usually combine low-material experimental assays with data management systems and candidate-ranking strategies.127 Bailly et al. showed this approach using a panel of 152 human or humanized mAbs.127 They evaluated key properties such as self-interaction, aggregation tendency, thermal stability, colloidal stability, and other developability-related attributes. This study provides a useful benchmark because it shows how experimental results can help to rank candidates and eliminate molecules with poor developability. In this context, AI and ML models are useful tools to support experimental screening and reduce workload. However, they are not a replacement for experimental confirmation.

Further, several studies supported the importance of applying computational methods early in biologic drug development.123–126 These studies emphasize that in silico predictions are most useful when they are rapid, scalable, and linked to experimentally measurable quality attributes. This is especially important for mAbs and newer antibody formats such as bispecific antibodies, multi-specific antibodies, and antibody-drug conjugates.

Sequence-based and protein language model-based developability prediction

Sequence-based prediction is one of the most useful applications of AI in early antibody development. This is because amino acid sequence data are available at the earliest stages of discovery. These models can be used to predict developability risks before structural modeling, cell expression, purification, or formulation screening.

One example of this application was reported by Chen et al. using supervised ML pipeline.128 They used amino acid sequences to predict antibody developability. The target variable was a binary developability label derived from the developability index, and dataset consisted of more than 2,400 antibodies. Six ML models were compared. These were logistic regression, Gaussian Bayes, support vector machines (SVM), gradient boosting (GB), random forest (RF) and multilayer perceptron (MLP). Since the task was classification, performance of the model was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristics (AUROC) curve, area under the precision – recall curve (AUPRC), F1 score, precision, and recall. The SVM performed best among the six ML models. This study showed that sequence-derived features supported developability prediction but needed external validation.

Protein language models (PLMs) are newer tools for sequence-based antibody developability prediction. They learn protein property relationships directly from sequence data without structural information. Recent studies have applied PLM-based approaches to several early developability properties such as solubility,129 polyreactivity,130 retention time (RT) and concentrated viscosity,131 and aggregation or monomer behavior.132

Prediction of biophysical and formulation-related properties

AI has also been applied to predict experimentally measurable biophysical and formulation-related properties. These include melting temperature, aggregation onset temperature, solubility, aggregation rate, viscosity, and chromatography-based developability indicators. These properties are directly relevant to manufacturability, formulation development, and product stability.

Gentiluomo et al. developed an artificial neural network-based strategy to predict biophysical properties of the antibodies.133 The predictive model was only based on the amino acid composition and formulation conditions. The target variables were melting temperature, aggregation onset temperature, and the diffusion interaction parameter. Their dataset tested six mAbs across 24 formulation conditions, for a total of 144 data samples. This model predicted melting temperature with coefficient of determination (R2) = 0.98 and root mean square error (RMSE) = 0.8°C, and aggregation onset temperature with R2 = 0.94 and RMSE = 2°C. This predictive model can be applied to many mAb sequences to select candidates with good physicochemical properties prior to cell expression and purification.

A major challenge in high-concentration antibody formulation is aggregation and viscosity. Lai et al. addressed this issue by measuring aggregation rates at 45°C and viscosity at 150 mg/mL for 20 preclinical and clinical-stage antibodies.134 For aggregation, they constructed a k-nearest neighbors (kNN) model using features derived from the molecular dynamics and sequence descriptors. The model predicted aggregation accurately with a correlation coefficient of r = 0.89. A logistic regression was applied with charge distribution features to classify high-viscosity antibodies accurately. This viscosity model achieved an accuracy of 0.86 and an AUPRC of 0.70. When the dataset was expanded by including 27 commercial antibodies, the viscosity model further showed an accuracy of 0.85 and AUPRC of 0.60. Their model used only a small number of interpretable features, which helped to address formulation problems and showed good generalizability across different antibody datasets. However, the dataset size and binary viscosity classification limit its general deployment.

Feng et al. introduced a transfer learning model called solPredict that can predict mAb solubility directly from sequence data.129 The target variable was apparent solubility in histidine buffer at pH 6.0. This model showed strong agreement with experimental solubility results by using pretrained PLMs embedding from 260 diverse antibodies. The best neural network model using evolutionary scale modeling-1b (ESM1b) embeddings achieved a Spearman correlation of 0.86, Pearson correlation of 0.84, R2 of 0.69, and RMSE of 4.40. This work allows high-throughput screening in short time without 3D structure modeling or manually designed features. This shows that transfer learning can improve prediction when experimental antibody datasets are limited.

To predict high-concentration viscosity and mouse clearance pharmacokinetic behavior, in silico binary classification models were developed using a dataset of 83 scaffold-consistent mAbs.135 In viscosity prediction, 71 molecules were used to classify whether viscosity at 150 mg/mL was below or above 15 cP. The best model combining high-throughput experimental and structure-based features achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.87, area under curve (AUC) of 0.95, precision of 0.87, and recall of 0.92. For mouse pharmacokinetic prediction, 55 molecules were used, and the model obtained an accuracy of 0.75 and AUC of 0.97. This study is useful because it connects biophysical descriptors with both formulation risk and in vivo pharmacokinetic behavior. However, the model may require broader validation before general application to diverse antibody formats due to the scaffold-consistent dataset.

Abeer et al. compared sequence-based, structure-based, PLM, and graph neural network models for predicting size-exclusion chromatography (SEC)-related developability properties.132 For this analysis, a dataset of approximately 1,200 IgG1 molecules was used. The target variables were SEC monomer percentage and change in retention time, which reflect aggregation-related behavior. For SEC monomer percentage, the best accuracy was 0.77 with an F1 score of 0.54 and sensitivity of 0.52. For RT prediction, the best accuracy was 0.80 with an F1 score of 0.64 and sensitivity of 0.58.

Prediction of specificity and off-target binding risks

Developability assessment is not limited to physical stability and formulation behavior. Other factors such as off-target binding, polyreactivity, and polyspecificity can also affect antibody clearance, efficacy, toxicity, and immunogenicity. Thus, predicting these liabilities early is important for selecting safer and more developable molecules. For example, Yu et al. applied PLM-based models to predict polyreactivity using experimental results from baculovirus particle binding and bovine serum albumin binding assay.130 The study included monospecific, bispecific, and single-domain Fc antibodies. The results showed that evolutionary scale modeling 2 (ESM-2)-based PLM model achieved the best validation AUROC of 0.906 and a test AUROC of 0.838. This study showed that PLMs can provide useful early predictions of off-target binding risks, although further external validation is needed before broader application.

In summary, AI and ML applications are increasingly useful in early-stage mAb development for candidate screening, sequence-based developability prediction, biophysical and formulation-related property prediction, and off-target binding risk assessment. These tools can reduce the need for extensive experimental screening and structural modeling. However, most current models are still at the research stage or decision-support tools because they are trained on limited, internal, or assay-specific datasets. Thus, experimental confirmation is necessary before these models are used in regulated development or GMP-related decisions. ML for upstream bioprocess development

In upstream bioprocessing, ML has been increasingly utilized to support prediction, optimization, and control of mammalian cell cultures. These techniques allow understanding of the multivariate process data, forecast process performance and product quality, and guide process development decisions. Upstream bioprocessing optimization based on ML are discussed in the following section.

PAT-compatible prediction of CQAs and process performance

PAT-compatible prediction of CQAs and process performance is important in USP because it can support real-time decision-making, feed-forward control, and timely process adjustments before harvest. To predict the CQAs of cell culture, Narayanan et al. proposed a decision tree (DT) approach using PLSR based on multivariate data analysis (MVDA).136 In this study, two different datasets of cell cultures were used. The first dataset consisted of 84 fed-batch runs at 3.5 L bioreactor scale to produce Fc-fusion protein. The culture was conducted in a peptone-based medium and maintained for 10 days. The second dataset consisted of 106 fed-batch experiments at 15 mL ambr microbioreactor scale using chemically defined media (CDM) over 14 days. The first case includes cell growth parameters such as DO, pH, VCD, duration, and cell age, while the second case included 3 parameters related to feed strategy, gas flow conditions, selected amino acids and trace elements, carbohydrate sources, and temperature. The first dataset modeling focused on VCD and product titer, whereas second dataset modeling targets included neutral charge and size variants, along with VCD and product titer. In the first dataset results, the black-box model reduced prediction error for final titer from 112.83 to 73.33 mg/L and for final VCD from 0.93 to 0.60 × 106 cells/mL. When full process history was used, error for final titer dropped from 36.19 to 29.45 mg/L and VCD dropped from 0.43 to 0.35 × 106 cells/mL. In the second dataset, localization improved final titer prediction when process history after day 7 was included. It also improved low molecular weight (LMW) prediction across all historical models. However, it did not improve prediction of charge variant. Compared with global PLSR, DT-PLSR approach increased prediction performance. It reduced the complexity of modeling nonlinear bioprocess behavior by using process-specific local models. It also provided interpretable process regimes that can support PAT and QbD-based process monitoring.

A performance-based modeling (PBM) approach was proposed by Schmidberger et al. to evaluate the product quantity and quality of attributes in mammalian cell cultures.137 They used process data such as DO, pH, CO2, VCD, viability, osmolality, and key metabolites (glutamine, glucose, lactate, ammonium) from two large datasets of 63 and 62 production runs. The target variables included final product concentration and 12 quality attributes, such as aggregated product, charge variants, and glycan types. Among the tested models, RF performed better than SVM, PLSR, and radial basis function artificial neural network (RBF-ANN), showing that model choice influences prediction accuracy. The best prediction was obtained for final product concentration, with R2 values of 0.95 for product I and 0.87 for product II. Other quality attributes also showed good results. For example, Man5 had an RMSE of 0.07 a.u. and lysine variant had an RMSE of 0.12 a.u. These results showed that final product concentration and selected CQAs could be estimated before harvest. This supports future feed-forward control strategies and moves USP closer to real-time product release.

Gangadharan et al. proposed a data-intelligence methodology to predict the upstream process performance from historical mAb manufacturing data.138 The dataset was obtained from AstraZeneca’s process development. It included 106 cultures run at scales ranging from laboratory scale (5 L) to commercial production (500 L) over a period of seven years. A minimum of 25 parameters were measured for each culture. These included VCD, viability, pH, osmolality, glucose, lactate, glutamine, glutamate, ammonium, temperature, pCO2, pO2, monomer percentage, and product concentration. The main target variable was final mAb concentration on harvest day. They developed a two-dimensional multi-model system using SVMs to predict mAb titer on harvest day using earlier process data. After feature selection and model optimization, the RMSE decreased from 1363.96 to 710.21, corresponding to a 52.07% reduction in prediction error. This study is particularly relevant from a deployment perspective because it used historical industrial CHO culture data across multiple scales.

However, Powers et al. investigated the effects of four commercial growth media on N-glycosylation pattern in a CHO DG44 cell line by applying PCA and PLS models.139 The dataset was generated from 35 automated ambr15 micro-bioreactors combined with PAT measurements. The target variables were antibody titer and glycosylation profiles. The glycosylation profiles included terminal galactosylation and high-mannose glycoforms. The PCA model captured 90.3% of the variation in the process data and achieved 60.5% cross-validation predictability. Similarly, the PLS model explained 81.0% of the process-variable variation and 72.9% of the glycan-response variation, with 58.6% cross-validated predictability. The analysis indicated that glycoform development, including terminal galactosylation and maturation of high-mannose forms, was influenced by metabolic factors like glutamine intake. These findings showed a trade-off between productivity and product quality. They also showed that MVDA can provide useful process understanding from complex bioprocess data and give insights for improving antibody quality.

Liang et al. developed soft-sensor models for predicting CHO cell growth, mAb production, intracellular metabolites, and glycan profiles.140 They compared mechanistic Michaelis-Menten kinetic models (MMK), data-driven orthogonal PLS (OPLS), and neural network models (NN) using galactose-fed-batch cultures. Model training was conducted with 108 data points under control and single galactose-feed conditions, whereas validation was performed under a double galactose-feed condition. The target variables included VCD, mAb titer, nucleotide sugar donors, and glycan species. The mechanistic MMK model predicted growth and mAb production most accurately, with deviation below 5% and fewer input requirements. On the other hand, OPLS model was effective for predicting nucleotide sugar donor and glycan composition, with deviations between 5% and 10%. This study supports PAT-based monitoring because soft sensors can complement real-time measurements and support prediction of product quality attributes during culture.

Yang et al. developed a hybrid physics-informed neural network (PINN) model for large-scale pilot CHO fed-batch cell culture.141 The model combined first-principles of biological knowledge from small scale studies with real-world large-scale bioreactor data. It was compared with pure data-driven, pure mechanistic, and other hybrid models and was also evaluated under different instrumentation and data-availability scenarios. The target variables were VCD, glucose, glutamine, lactate, ammonia, and titer. This PINN model achieved a mean normalized RMSE (NRMSE) of 0.030 across the target variables, with 0.031 for VCD and 0.047 for mAb titer. When daily measurements were not available, the mean NRMSE was 0.061, with 0.060 for VCD and 0.052 for mAb titer. These results show that the model predicted well even without daily measurements, though daily calibration improved performance. Since this study used large-scale process data, it can be considered for deployment, but broader external validation is still required.

Media optimization and metabolite analysis

Optimization of the cell culture media is also important to increase cell growth, productivity, and product quality in upstream mAb production. For example, Hernández et al. showed the use of MVDA for selecting favorable nutritional conditions in mAb producing cell line.142 This study compared four different media at 2 L culture scale and measured 19 metabolites, cell concentration, and IgG/mAb concentration over the course of batch cultures. PCA and soft independent modeling of class analogies (SIMCA) were used to distinguish the metabolic behavior of different media, while PLSR was used to relate metabolite concentrations to cell growth and mAb production. The target variables were logarithmic cell concentration and IgG/mAb concentration. The important metabolites including Lys, Leu, Val, Gln, Ser, and lactate were found to have a considerable influence on cell growth and mAb production. After variable selection, the PLSR model showed good fitting ability with R2 = 91% in calibration and R2 = 87% in validation. The results suggested that Lys, Leu, Ser, and Gln positively contributed to both cell growth and mAb production, whereas lactate negatively affected mAb concentration. Although this study is a lab-scale demonstration, it supports the use of MVDA for media selection and metabolite-level process understanding.

Similarly, Kang et al. studied the effects of polyamines such as putrescine, spermidine, and spermine in CHO-K1 culture.143 In this study, different polyamine concentrations in serum-free media and their effects on cell growth, mAb production, metabolism, and antibody quality were evaluated. PCA and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were used to interpret the relationship between polyamine supplementation, metabolic profiles, and culture performance. The target variables considered include VCD, viability, mAb titer, specific growth rate, specific mAb production rate, metabolic profiles, and antibody quality attributes. They discovered that the addition of polyamines to serum-free media enhanced growth, productivity, and quality of antibodies in a dose-dependent manner, whereas their absence produced negative effects. Nevertheless, it is important to maintain optimum concentration because excessive levels are harmful. This highlights the importance of metabolite balance during upstream media optimization.

Gangwar et al. reported a media component optimization and CQA prediction using an end-to-end ML system.144 To do this, they developed a prototype dataset by culturing CHO-GS cells in 34 media formulations. These formulations contained different concentrations of seven metal ions. Acidic and basic charge variants were selected as the target variables. Feature selection using RF, Pearson correlation coefficient, and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis was used to identify media components. The results revealed that Zn and Fe had a significant impact on the profile of basic and acidic charge variants, respectively. Based on these results, 42 additional experimental runs with different Fe and Zn combinations were generated for charge variant prediction. Fifteen regression models were used to predict the medium combinations. The RF model achieved a test R2 of 0.955, while RF and GB regression were among the top cross-validated models, with R2 values of 0.9151 ± 0.0455 and 0.9288 ± 0.0441, respectively. This method can enable producers and innovators in the biosimilar field to develop more efficient pipelines for media development. However, because the dataset was small and process-specific, broader validation is still needed before deployment.

Hybrid modeling combined with intensified design of experiments (iDoE) can reduce the number of experiments needed during CHO mAb process development. For example, Bayer et al. studied a two-factor DoE in 300 mL shake flasks, where cultivation temperature and glucose concentration in the feed were varied.145 The model was then evaluated at 15 L bioreactor scale. The target variables were VCC and product titer. The shake-flask hybrid model predicted the 15 L process with NRMSE values of 10.92% for VCC and 17.79% for product titer, while the iDoE hybrid model showed comparable NRMSE values of 13.75% and 21.13%, respectively. These results suggest that hybrid modeling and iDoE can support scale-transfer and process characterization with reduced experimental effort.

Hybrid and digital-twin models

Hybrid models are increasingly used in upstream mAb process development because they combine process knowledge with data-driven prediction.146 Mechanistic models provide biological interpretability, while data-driven models capture complex nonlinear relationships in process data. This combination makes hybrid models useful for process monitoring, prediction, and digital-twin development. However, their reliable application depends on process-specific data, parameter estimation, and independent validation. Hybrid modeling is therefore a key enabler for Biopharma 4.0, though its maturity remains tied to the quality and scale of validation data.

Wang et al. proposed an iterative learning-based robust optimization system for media optimization in CHO cell cultivation for continuous mAb production.147 This method combines robust model-based optimization with best experiment design for managing uncertainties and ensuring reliable performance. The case study used a realistic simulation of continuous mAb production, which included a metabolic network with 23 extracellular metabolites and 126 reactions. Asparagine and glutamine concentrations in the feed medium were selected as the input variables, each ranging from 1 to 9 mM. The objective was to maximize the mAb production rate while satisfying critical constraints. The key constraints included ammonium concentration below 3.5 mM and biomass secretion rate below 2.162 pmol/(cell·day). Compared with the base medium, the true harvested mAb rate increased from 3.7010 to 4.4396 pmol/(cell·day) after an iterative robust optimization. A worst-case harvested mAb rate under uncertainty also increased from 2.6914 to 3.3984 pmol/(cell·day). These results show that robust optimization can improve mAb production while accounting for model uncertainty. However, this was a simulation-based study, not a deployed manufacturing application.

Park et al. developed data-driven models for multi-step-ahead forecasting of mammalian cell culture profiles toward bioprocess digital twins.148 The study used 24 fed-batches from four culture conditions with six batches each. After anomaly removal, 16 runs were used for modeling, with 12 runs for training and 4 for validation. The target variables were VCD, viability, glucose, lactate concentration, ammonia concentration, and mAb titer. They compared 12 ML and 4 deep learning (DL) algorithms with different forecasting strategies to identify suitable models for predicting future culture behavior. The best models achieved a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 6% for VCD, 5% for glucose consumption, 23% for lactate, 3% for ammonia, and 3% for IgG/mAb titer. Corresponding R2 values were 0.972, 0.957, 0.953, 0.996, and 0.988, respectively. Because of these promising multi-step prediction results, this data-driven forecasting approach can support future digital-twin applications. However, the study remains in the research stage, as it was validated on a limited number of CHO-K1 batches.

Another study used PINNs to model and optimize the mammalian cell culture process.149 In this work, product quality features such as VCD, dead cell density, glucose concentration, lactate concentration, and mAb concentration were correlated with process variables such as time, flow rates, and volume. The study used three experimental mAb production batches: one 14-day batch for training, one 15-day batch for testing, and one 13-day batch for validation. The 14-day training batch was converted into 1-min intervals, giving 20,160 training data points. The results showed that the PINN model was more efficient than MLP model. For mAb concentration, the PINN achieved R2 values of 0.9833 during testing and 0.9731 during validation, with RMSE values of 0.0973 and 0.1231, respectively. Maximization of VCD and mAb concentration was done by combining Bayesian optimization with the developed model. In single-objective optimization, this led to a 13.01% increase in VCD and 18.57% increase in mAb concentration. Multi-objective optimization was even more successful with 46.32% and 67.86% increases in VCD and mAb concentration, respectively. PINN-based hybrid modeling shows promise for upstream process optimization, but broader experimental validation is still needed.

Advanced AI for control and adaptive optimization

Advanced AI methods have also been explored for adaptive control and optimization of upstream mAb production. A hybrid deep learning (DL) model combining convolutional neural networks (CNN) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks was developed by Zheng et al. to improve mAb production.150 The model was developed using industrial data collected over 18 months from 50 production batches and evaluated across different cell lines and bioreactor sizes ranging from 50 to 2,000 L. The main target variable was mAb titer. This CNN-LSTM model achieved an RMSE of 0.412 g/L, mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.318 g/L, R2 of 0.947, and a dynamic trajectory similarity score of 0.923 for titer prediction. Feature analysis showed that pH, temperature, and DO were the key factors affecting mAb production. In silico optimization predicted a 27.9% increase in volumetric productivity and a 28.1% increase in mAb titer, from 3.2 to 4.1 g/L. However, the study also noted challenges with model interpretability and the need for uncertainty analysis.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has also become a powerful tool for the real-time control of bioprocesses. For example, Gupta et al. used a proximal policy optimization (PPO) algorithm to adjust feed flow rates and control mAb concentration in a bioreactor.151 In their study, the manipulated variable was the inlet flow rate, and the control variable was mAb concentration. The approach was evaluated under three case studies: nominal operating conditions, noise in raw materials and measurements, and stochastic temperature disturbance with measurement noise. The target mAb concentration was 590 mg/L. The results showed that PPO reduced the time required to reach the desired concentration compared with open-loop operation and achieved the target in approximately 9 days instead of 20 days. PPO showed lower RMSE than deep deterministic policy gradient (DDPG) and twin delayed deep deterministic policy gradient (TD3) in all three case studies, with RMSE values of 228.4, 225.3, and 224.2, respectively. They further explored a multi-actor PPO system, where three-actor PPO improved performance with RMSE values of 215.3, 214.0, and 216.1 across the three case studies. These results emphasize that RL can support adaptive control of mAb production.

Sarna et al. developed a process-aware data-driven model predictive control (MPC) strategy for a perfusion mAb bioreactor using a detailed Sartorius simulation test bed.152 The model was developed using constrained subspace identification, which incorporated known process relationships into the data-driven model. The manipulated variables were reactor temperature, pH, glucose feed concentration, feed rate, and bleed rate. The measured outputs included viability, VCD, titer concentration, and glucose concentration. The objective was to maximize final product formation, which was calculated from titer and harvest rate, without violating process constraints. Compared with the existing PI controller, the constrained subspace MPC increased final product from 97 mg/day to 178 mg/day, corresponding to an 83.5% improvement. The constrained MPC also performed better than unconstrained subspace MPC approaches. This shows the benefit of including process knowledge in the data-driven control model.

Overall, ML has created numerous opportunities for upstream bioprocess development, from CQA prediction and media optimization to digital-twin modeling and adaptive control. PAT-compatible prediction models using DT-PLS, RF, SVM, MVDA, soft sensors, and PINNs can forecast titer, VCD, metabolites, and selected CQAs before harvest. Media optimization studies using MVDA, explainable ML, and hybrid modeling can support better selection of nutrients, metabolites, and feed conditions. Advanced methods such as CNN-LSTM, PINNs, RL, and MPC further show potential for adaptive optimization and process control. However, the maturity of these applications varies. Models developed from industrial or large-scale process data are closer to deployment, whereas many RL, MPC, digital-twin, and in silico optimization studies remain at the research stage and require broader validation. ML for downstream processing

DSP plays a key role in determining the overall cost, efficiency, and product quality in mAb manufacturing. The increasing complexity of purification processes and variability in impurity profiles have motivated the use of ML approaches to enhance process design, monitoring, prediction, and control. The following subsections summarize key applications of ML in downstream processing.

Data-driven purification development

Chromatographic strategies play a key role in overall efficiency and cost of biopharmaceutical manufacturing process. Liu et al. developed a data-driven optimization method for antibody fragment purification across multiple scales.79 In this study, velocity of flow, mass load, and heights of column were used as input parameters to optimize chromatographic decision variables such as number of chromatographic cycles, number of cycles per batch, and operational velocities. For this investigation, manufacturing-scale datasets were simulated based on microscale experimental data using first-principles chromatography simulations. The datasets contained 3,081 samples for affinity chromatography (AC) and 2,847 samples for CEX. WEKA, an ML software package, was used to implement a range of regression models. These included linear regression, SVR, kriging, response surface methodology, and piecewise linear regression (PLR). Among the tested models, PLR showed the best predictive performance, with MAE values of 34.68 for AC and 233.39 for CEX. The selected data-driven throughput models were then incorporated into mixed-integer optimization formulations to determine purification design and operating decisions, including column sizing, number of columns, number of cycles per batch, flow velocity, annual production output, and cost of goods. The original MINLP formulations were further reformulated as MILP problems using linearization methods to improve computational tractability. Therefore, this study is best classified as an offline purification-design optimization approach.

Surrogate-based optimization was conducted for capture chromatography platform design.153 This work reduced the high computational cost of solving nonlinear chromatography models by replacing expensive simulations with surrogate functions. The target was process design optimization of capture chromatography, with the goal of improving computational efficiency while maintaining accurate prediction of process behavior. The surrogate-based framework reduced processing time by 93%, making it more practical for industrial process design. However, this approach remains a research-stage/offline process-design tool because it supports simulation-based optimization rather than real-time monitoring or automated control.

Maier et al. applied ML to predict whether a mAb candidate would fit existing purification processes.154 This study used mAb sequence-derived molecular descriptors and high-throughput batch binding data to predict chromatographic binding behavior. PCA was first used to reduce multidimensional binding data into a one-dimensional process-fit descriptor. Then, kernel ridge regression was used to predict this descriptor for new mAb sequences. The dataset included 97 mAbs evaluated across five chromatography resins, two salt types, and a range of pH and salt concentrations. Model performance was evaluated using MAE during cross-validation and R2/Q2 for training and test-set prediction, where Q2 is predictive coefficient of determination. The model predicted charge-based chromatography conditions well, especially for CEX, AEX, and mixed-mode AEX, with test-set Q2 values above 0.85 under sodium acetate conditions. This indicates that sequence-based ML can help rank mAb candidates for purification process fit.

PAT monitoring and real-time prediction

PAT-based monitoring tools are important in DSP because conventional offline assays are often too slow to support real-time decisions during chromatography. Real-time monitoring of co-eluting proteins in chromatography was described by Brestrich et al. using the PLSR model.155 For this purpose, multiple chromatographic runs with batch-to-batch variability were conducted, and in-line mid-UV absorption spectra were recorded simultaneously to generate the process data. Process data-based calibration was performed by correlating the recorded in-line spectra with offline fraction analytics. Initially, a ternary model protein system was used to establish the method, followed by CEX chromatography for the purification of mAb. The target variables were the concentrations of co-eluting protein species, including aggregates and molecular fractions such as high molecular weight (HMW) and LMW species. The validated PLS models showed promising performance in analyzing aggregates and molecular fractions from co-eluting impurities. RMSE values were 0.014 g/L for monomers, 0.0036 g/L for HMW1, 0.0019 g/L for LMW, and 0.0007 g/L for HMW2. Therefore, this approach could support real-time pooling decisions. AEX chromatography-based on Cohn supernatant was also used to determine the content of co-eluting serum proteins. The target variables were IgG, transferrin, IgM, and IgA concentrations. The final PLS model used five latent variables and reported RMSE values of 0.305 g/L for IgG, 0.137 g/L for transferrin, 0.011 g/L for IgM, and 0.049 g/L for IgA. The model predicted the elution profiles of the main target proteins, IgG and transferrin, while IgA prediction was less accurate. These techniques are adaptable and can be applied to real-time monitoring of HCP contaminants during purification, and to different operating conditions of chromatographic columns.

Further, Brestrich et al. extended the above method to control the purification step by selectively measuring protein purity in-line over a wide dynamic concentration range.156 The technique used PLSR in conjunction with variable pathlength (VP) UV/Vis spectroscopy. This was initially developed for lysozyme separation from cytochrome c to monitor chromatography operations under high column loads. Further, an mAb polishing chromatography step was monitored using the same technique. Selective differentiation of multiple co-eluting species based on spectral data was made possible by PLSR modeling, while absorbance data over a wide range of concentration were obtained by VP spectroscopy. For the mAb case study, the target variables were mAb monomer and HMW variants, with reported RMSE values of 1.07 g/L and 0.42 g/L, respectively. The mAb polishing case was performed at 40 g/L loading density, and the in-line pooling decision was triggered when the predicted mAb monomer concentration exceeded 2 g/L and stopped when the calculated pool purity decreased below 95%. This PAT-based method showed promise for in-line process monitoring and real-time pooling control, either in target protein purity mode or based on preparative chromatography scale elution profiles of impurity proteins. This method may also be suitable for regulating switching times in continuous chromatography.

Critical process attributes prediction also explored using ML models. Nikita et al. applied five ML models for real-time prediction of critical process attributes in continuous mAb DSP.157 The input data were obtained from routinely available process sensors such as pH, UV, and conductivity signals from capture and polishing chromatography. The target variables were mAb elute concentration from ProA chromatography, acidic charge variant percentage, mAb elute concentration from CEX chromatography, and aggregate percentage. The ML models were deep neural network (DNN), SVR, decision tree (DT), RF, and GB. Among these, RF showed the best overall performance for the smaller process-scale datasets used in the study. In the real-time validation run, the RF predicted ProA elute concentration, acidic charge variant percentage, CEX elute concentration, and aggregate percentage with errors of 4.761%, 2.630%, 2.357%, and 2.881%, respectively. The final random forest model gave prediction errors below 5% for all predicted attributes. This study is relevant to deployment-oriented downstream monitoring because it used process sensor data for real-time quality prediction and achieved prediction errors below 5% for all evaluated attributes.

Heterogeneities related to charge variants of mAb products are becoming significant because they can affect product quality, stability, and efficacy. These variants are commonly determined using analytical CEX, which is time consuming and cannot be used easily for real-time control. Nitika et al. developed a Raman spectroscopy-based PAT tool that, combined with a CNN model, can be useful for online and real-time monitoring of charge variants.158 To develop this approach, Raman spectra were collected for a reference library of samples prepared using process-scale CEX, covering mAb concentration range of 0–20 g/L and acidic, main, and basic species distributions from 0–100%. The target variables were acidic species, main species, basic species, and total protein concentration. The CNN model achieved R2 values of 0.94, 0.99, 0.96, and 0.99 for acidic species, main species, basic species, and total protein concentration, respectively. The corresponding RMSE values were 0.1846, 0.1627, and 0.1029 g/L for acidic, main, and basic species, and 0.2483 g/L for total protein concentration. The results indicated that Raman spectroscopies with AI frameworks can be used to identify product-related contaminants. This approach enables the generation of consistent charge variant profiles and supports real-time CEX pooling decisions during the production of mAb.

Automated control and predictive maintenance

AI/ML-based monitoring and control strategies have also been explored for continuous downstream processing and predictive maintenance. These studies are closer to deployment-oriented applications because they use real-time process measurements, control actions, or process verification frameworks. A typical issue in chromatography processes is ProA resin aging. Ramakrishna and Rathore proposed a control technique for resin aging using PCA of UV chromatogram data and batch-level modeling (BLM) implemented in Python.159 The target variables were resin aging indicators such as yield decline and DBC changes. Based on two case studies involving different mAbs, this method forecast resin performance reduction 20–25 cycles before observable yield drop. They recommended implementing a control strategy in which additional resin cleaning is triggered after detection of resin aging or DBC deviation. This approach could increase ProA resin lifespan by approximately 50–100 cycles. Therefore, the proposed technique can be used as a PAT predictive maintenance tool for resin performance degradation and column integrity failure.

Anomaly detection and yield prediction in ProA chromatography were also explored using advanced ML algorithms.160 For this analysis, input data were obtained from ProA chromatography resin cycling studies using chromatographic process signals that related to column performance over repeated cycles. Two target applications were considered: detection of column integrity breach and prediction of percentage yield decay. For anomaly detection, PCA followed by isolation forest (IF) and LSTM autoencoder models were used. Both approaches predicted column integrity failure four cycles before the actual failure cycle. For yield prediction, a PLS- artificial neural network (PLS-ANN) model was compared with a traditional PLS regression model. The PLS-ANN model showed better performance, with R2 = 0.96 and RMSE = 0.014, compared with the PLS model, which had R2 = 0.88 and RMSE = 0.028. These results show that ML can detect subtle changes in column performance and support preventive control actions before major process failure occurs.

Further, a ML-based system for continued process verification (CPV) in CEX chromatography was studied by Anupa et al.161 In this study, DNN models, PAT tools, and statistical process control (SPC) charts were combined to monitor CPPs such as pH, conductivity, UV absorbance, and column pressure as well as CQAs such charge variant composition and protein concentration. Raman spectroscopy and NIR spectroscopy were used as PAT tools. Raman spectroscopy coupled with DNN was used for charge variant prediction, while NIR coupled with DNN was used for concentration prediction. The developed models showed R2 values greater than 0.90. During perturbation studies, the control strategy maintained CEX eluate charge variant composition close to the control range, with acidic species around 20%, main species around 62%, and basic species around 18%. This demonstrates the potential of AI, PAT, and data analytics for CPV and real-time process control in downstream mAb manufacturing.

Advanced AI and hybrid modeling

Advanced AI and hybrid modeling approaches have also been explored in DSP to improve process understanding, root-cause analysis, model calibration, and optimization. To analyze key variables associated with process deviations in protein chromatography, Wang et al. applied a hybrid modeling technique that combined mechanistic chromatography modeling with ANN.162 The target variables were deviations in column capacity and elution gradient length, which can affect chromatographic behavior, purity, yield, and production rate. To conduct mechanistic modeling, the parameterized mechanistic model was used to generate ANN training data using 570 computer-based simulations. The ANN model identified the basic correlation between targeted deviation parameters and chromatograms. This method identified root causes with prediction errors of 4.9% for elution gradient length and 1.5% for column capacity. As a research-stage hybrid model, this approach can support root-cause investigation and rapid troubleshooting of chromatographic deviations.

Voltmer et al. further demonstrated the use of ANN-based calibration to accelerate mechanistic model development for CEX chromatography of mAbs.163 This workflow was applied to the calibration of a multicomponent steric mass-action model using three case studies and two mAb feedstocks with varying impurity complexities. The target variables included mechanistic model parameters such as equilibrium, charge, effective mass-transfer, kinetic, and shielding-related constants. The ANN-based workflow provided acceptable parameter estimates and showed good agreement between experimental and simulated chromatograms, requiring only minimal additional inverse fitting. For complex feedstocks, a two-step method using high-load-density data gave better parameter predictions for both targets and impurities. In one such case, the ANN-based workflow reduced the calibration effort by requiring only limited experimental runs and reduced additional inverse fitting compared with conventional model calibration. This study supports downstream development by reducing the calibration burden.

To predict the target variables of downstream processes in mAb production, Alam et al. introduced a two-dimensional CNN regression technique.164 The model used routinely collected process profiles such as ProA elution UV, CEX elution UV, CEX conductivity, and post-viral inactivation pH profiles. The target variables were elute concentrations of ProA mAb and CEX mAb, along with attributes like acidic and basic charge variants and aggregates. The model was validated and then combined with multi-objective Bayesian optimization to match biosimilar CQAs with those of the reference molecule. The optimized conditions were experimentally validated using three consecutive runs. The reported mean deviation was less than 3%. This approach shows the potential for downstream prediction and optimization, allowing more efficient and responsive control of biopharmaceutical production processes.

Overall, AI applications in DSP can be grouped into four main categories: offline process design, PAT-based monitoring and pooling, automated control and predictive maintenance, and advanced hybrid or deep-learning models. Deployment-oriented applications are seen in PAT-based soft sensors, continued process verification, pooling support, and resin aging or column failure prediction. On the other hand, deep-learning and hybrid modeling studies remain at the research or process-development stage, where they are used for optimization, root-cause analysis, or mechanistic model calibration. Model lifecycle and GMP deployment considerations

Although AI and ML models are increasingly applied across early-stage development, upstream bioprocessing, and downstream processing, their use in GMP-relevant environments requires careful lifecycle management.165 There are several regulatory frameworks that provide useful guidance in this area. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) process validation guidance states that validation is a lifecycle spanning across process design, process qualification, and CPV.165 The International Council for Harmonization (ICH) guideline Q9(R1) on quality risk management provides a systematic framework for science- and risk-based decision-making throughout the product lifecycle.166 Similarly, ICH Q10 on the pharmaceutical quality system provides a framework for process performance monitoring, product quality monitoring, corrective action and preventive action (CAPA), change management, and management review.167 In addition, both the FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have issued guidance that discusses the use of AI across drug development, manufacturing, regulatory decision-making, and the medicinal product lifecycle.166,168,169

Based on these regulatory principles, the first step in AI/ML model lifecycle management is to define the intended use of the model. This may include candidate screening, process understanding, process monitoring, process control, CPV, or release-related decision support.168,169 Models used only for early discovery or process development usually need experimental confirmation and basic documentation. On the other hand, models used in GMP manufacturing, process control, CPV, or release-related decisions require more rigorous validation, documentation, and change-control procedures.165,167

When used in GMP environments, model validation should be predefined and risk-based, according to the intended use, operating range, acceptance criteria, and risk to product quality. After deployment, model performance should be monitored for drift, and any recalibration, retraining, or model update should follow predefined governance and change-control procedures. These procedures should include appropriate documentation, versioning, audit trails, and revalidation where necessary.165–169 Validation documentation should include the target variable, input data, training and validation datasets, preprocessing steps, model algorithm, software version, acceptance criteria, performance metrics, assumptions, and limitations.168,169 The performance metrics should match the model type and intended GMP use. For example, classification models may use accuracy, balanced accuracy, AUROC, precision, recall, and F1 score,168 while regression models may use RMSE, MAE, R2, Pearson correlation, and Spearman correlation. The selected metrics should match the model’s intended GMP function and the risk associated with incorrect predictions.169,170

Model drift is a major concern after deployment.166,168 It can occur when new antibody formats, cell lines, raw materials, assay methods, equipment, sensors, formulation conditions, resin performance, or process operating ranges differ from those used to train the model. Therefore, deployed models should be continuously monitored by comparing their predictions with new experimental or manufacturing data.165,167 If performance decreases, corrective actions may include recalibration, retraining, limiting the model’s operating range, increasing human review, or returning the model to decision-support use only.168

Change control is also important when models are updated.166,167 Changes to the training dataset, preprocessing method, model structure, software environment, assay method, data pipeline, input variables, retraining strategy, or intended use may affect model performance. Therefore, these changes should be documented, risk-assessed, version-controlled, and revalidated when necessary.167,170 A clear audit trail should also be maintained so that the model version, input data, prediction output, and resulting decisions can be traced.168,169

Overall, research-stage models used in early discovery or process development should be described as supportive tools that require experimental confirmation. In contrast, models used in GMP manufacturing, process control, CPV, or release-related decisions require stronger lifecycle management such as documented validation, drift monitoring, and periodic revalidation.167–169

Data heterogeneity

The heterogeneity of the data is one of the main barriers in the production of mAb, because of differences in measurement timescales and type of data.171 The parameters of the bioprocess can be online, at-line, and offline based on the frequency of measurement.138,172 Online parameters include pH, temperature, and DO, which are recorded continuously in real time. Offline parameters include VCD and product concentration, which are measured periodically because they have time- and cost-related limitations.171,172 Because the data cannot be collected simultaneously for all variables, current ML algorithms are not fully efficient when applied to bioprocess data.173 To overcome this, different solutions such as training the ML models to operate with alternate sample intervals172,174 and use of different alignment methods like dynamic time warping175 have been proposed.

The types of data also make the modeling of the bioprocess more complex in addition to timescale variability. The variables such as nutrient and metabolite concentration are continuous but others, such as valve positions for the delivery of gas to the processes are discrete or binary.171 Managing such data types requires special preprocessing methods, including encoding techniques or the use of ML models that can handle multiple data types. Examples include decision tree models based on classification and regression tree (CART)176 and fuzzy-neural networks.177 Therefore, batch-aware preprocessing, suitable encoding of mixed data types, and time-alignment methods such as dynamic time warping are necessary before model training. These steps improve comparability between process variables, reduce errors caused by asynchronous measurements, enhance model transferability across batches, and support more reliable real-time monitoring and continuous process control.

High dimensionality

Bioprocess data is not only heterogeneous, but it also suffers from high dimensionality, commonly referred to as the curse of dimensionality. Modern bioprocess analysis uses PAT tools such as Raman and infrared spectroscopy, which can record more than hundreds of variables.178 ML algorithms face challenges when dealing with data related to higher dimensions, especially when the amount of data available to train the ML model is limited. To solve this, feature selection and dimensionality reduction techniques are important to identify the most useful variables and reduce the redundant and less useful variables.172 Due to the time-varying nature of the data, feature selection for bioprocess data is challenging. As a result, many standard methods do not consider time-dependent relationships, so dynamic feature selection methods are necessary for accurate analysis.179

Several opportunities are available for handling the high-dimensional and time-related process data. These include spectral preprocessing, feature selection, dimensionality reduction, clustering, PCA/PLS, and regularized models. The clustering algorithms can be used for clustering correlated variables, thereby avoiding the redundancy of variables and for retaining important process information.171 Exploratory methods such as Spearman or Pearson correlation can be used to analyze relationships between variables and to highlight redundant and less informative variables. Statistical methods are also useful to determine time-based features and to improve process analysis. Deep learning networks and hybrid architecture such as CNN-LSTM and PINN are useful tools when dealing with high dimensional data. Because these models can capture the spatial and temporal trends and thereby help to optimize and control the processes. They can improve the overall bioprocess performance by processing complex time-series data, removing redundancy, and enhancing accuracy of prediction.150 If high-dimensional PAT data are not properly preprocessed and reduced, models may capture batch-specific noise rather than process-relevant patterns. This can cause overfitting, limit model transferability, and weaken their use in real-time prediction, monitoring, and control.

Diverse data sources

Biopharmaceutical processes can produce numerous types of data with characteristics like diversity, distribution, and multi-structured formats. This nature of data sources poses significant difficulties in the automated model construction of biopharmaceutical operations.180 Various data sources are used throughout bioprocess including file-based exports and structured data warehouses.181 The generation of file-based exports is simple, but it involves a lot of preprocessing because formats are diverse. Data warehouses, process historians, electronic laboratory notebooks (ELNs), structured electronic batch records, and other data capture systems can provide more reliable and available data for ML models when they are designed using standardized formats and metadata.171,180,181 However, they require considerable time and effort to set up.

In most cases, various data sources must be combined to develop powerful ML models. Therefore, practical solutions involve the integration of structured electronic storage systems with data warehouses, process historians, laboratory information management systems (LIMS), manufacturing execution systems (MES), supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA), and open platform communications (OPC)-based data transfer systems.181,182 When experimental conditions, sampling time points, batch identifiers, raw material information, analytical measurements, process deviations, and operator interventions are recorded in a structured and standardized format, the resulting datasets become more traceable, reusable, and suitable for ML model development.180 In contrast, fragmented records, unstructured spreadsheets, inconsistent variable names, and incomplete metadata can increase preprocessing requirements, introduce bias, and reduce the reliability of AI model outputs.34,138,180 AI-ready data infrastructure is essential for reliable data mining, model validation, and process optimization. This requires connecting ELNs, structured electronic batch records, LIMS, MES, SCADA, PAT instruments, process historians, and centralized data warehouses through OPC-UA (unified architecture) where possible. Such integration reduces fragmented storage, improves access to traceable, reusable, and ML-ready datasets. Together, these benefits enhance model transferability and support deployment in continuous monitoring and control systems.

Data integrity and quality

The effectiveness of ML models in bioprocessing depends on data quality and integrity. Structured electronic data records improve data integrity by preserving batch context, reducing transcription errors, enabling traceability of missing values and outliers, and supporting consistent preprocessing before AI model training.34,138,180 Missing data is one of the key problems because it may happen due to a variety of reasons like sensor failures, irregular sampling rates, errors in real-time data logging, and human factors.138,183,184 In antibody manufacturing, this directly affects the quality and reliability of data-driven optimization and decision-making.184 The missing data is usually handled by the removal of incomplete samples, imputation of missing values, or the use of ML algorithms that can work with partial datasets.185 However, the removal of vast amounts of data can create bias and poor imputation methods may alter significant trends.

For example, two imputation methods were analyzed by Mante et al.184 These are multivariate regression and mean imputation techniques for managing the missing data in biopharmaceutical processes. They evaluated these methods on two types of data, dynamic and cell culture data, which contained 26.2% and 20.2% missing values, respectively. The findings showed that both methods handled missing data without adding bias into subsequent analyses. Furthermore, Gangadharan et al. used data intelligence techniques to fill gaps in missing data and to predict culture performance using historical multivariate time-series data in upstream production of antibodies.138 Using specialized preprocessing and multi-model ML systems, they were able to impute missing values and predict culture performance with good accuracy.

Beyond the issue of missing data, outliers are another major factor affecting data quality. Outliers may arise from measurement errors, unexpected process changes, or unobservable process phenomena that occur during bioprocess operations. If they are not properly detected and managed, they can affect the result of ML models and lead to misleading results. Common outlier detection methods in the field of bioprocessing include statistical methods, deviation-based methodologies, and hypothesis testing procedures.186 Data quality, therefore, requires an integrated approach that manages both missing data and outliers using suitable imputation methods, outlier detection, and process-knowledge review. In addition, standardized metadata, batch identifiers, units, timestamps, quality flags, audit trails, and data lineage are needed to improve traceability, reproducibility, and reliability of AI model outputs. Without these controls, issues such as missing values, outliers, and poorly documented data can lead to unreliable predictions. This makes it difficult to transfer models between batches, scales, and sites or to use them safely for continuous process control.

Lack of open datasets

The lack of publicly available benchmark datasets is one of the main barriers in the development and validating ML methods in the field of bioprocessing. The absence of open data complicates the process particularly when comparing different ML models and evaluating their performance under the same conditions.180 Open datasets in this field are rare because the data may have sensitive information and proprietary manufacturing details, which makes companies hesitate to disclose them. It leads to slow development and limits the adoption of ML applications in biopharmaceuticals. In addition, lack of accessible and standardized datasets poses challenges to researchers for validating their models.

To address this issue, internal benchmark datasets, anonymized or federated data-sharing approaches, simulated datasets, and standardized reporting formats can be developed.34,180,187 Such resources support fair model comparison, validation, reproducibility, and wider AI adoption without directly exposing sensitive manufacturing data. Without these resources, validation remains confined to narrow conditions. This limits transferability across batches, scales, and manufacturing sites and weakens reliability for continuous process monitoring and control.173,174,182

Process deviations and failures

The variation in processes, small design spaces, and batch failure make it difficult to maintain consistent data in biopharmaceuticals manufacturing. Lack of adequate process understanding can lead to discrepancies in the quality of data and complicates optimization and control. Variability can be introduced into data analysis by changes in processes such as intentional changes in processes (e.g., process adjustments) or unintentional changes in processes (e.g., equipment failures). In addition, inconsistency is also increased with batch contamination or failures, which affects process reproducibility and regulatory compliance.188

To overcome these issues, both QbD and PAT promote the application of statistical methods for monitoring and improving processes. DoE and MVDA can help to identify the key process parameters and to understand sources of variability.188,189 Advanced real-time monitoring and high-throughput analytical techniques can further support the consistency of the process. In addition, reliable data can be produced using perfusion-based bioreactor operation in USP and continuous chromatography techniques in DSP. This makes it important to select suitable data analysis and understand the structure of these datasets to obtain valuable information and maximize the performance.34,125,189 Process deviations, contamination, equipment failures, and batch failures can introduce non-representative data patterns. Tools such as PAT, QbD, DoE, MVDA, anomaly detection, and real-time monitoring are therefore essential for early fault detection and process understanding. These approaches prevent models from learning failure-specific behavior that cannot be transferred to normal operating conditions. They also support more reliable continuous process monitoring and control.

Dominance of batch-based bioprocesses

Biopharmaceutical manufacturing is still largely based on batch processes. As a result, sequential unit operations can be affected by changes in CPPs, which alter conditions of processes and CQAs of final product.181 Although batch processing is useful for controlling the mutation of cell lines and maintaining the product quality, it is also associated with increased production times and limited scalability.190 Therefore, each batch requires extensive quality testing, longer manufacturing time, and use of large-scale bioreactors, which is costly.35

On the other hand, there are many advantages associated with continuous manufacturing including reductions in operating time, cost of equipment, and scale-up.16,35 Continuous bioprocessing allows real-time control and optimization, which reduces variability of the process and maintains consistent quality of the product.173 Although continuous manufacturing offers many benefits, there are still challenges that must be addressed. One of the key problems is the need for advanced PAT and reliable predictive models to ensure continuous operation.191 In this context, the use of ML will make a significant impact on the prediction, optimization, and control of complex continuous processes.

As industry moves toward continuous biomanufacturing, digital twins and control systems like SCADA can make this transition smoother. For example, Feidl et al. created an integrated continuous production system, with real-time monitoring and data-driven adjustments to maintain consistent product quality.182 Tiwari et al. developed a digital twin of continuous chromatography in the purification of mAb by connecting online HPLC-PAT instruments with both empirical and mechanistic models.192 This setup updated the process in real time, allowed accurate pooling decisions, maintained consistent charge-variant profiles, and achieved greater than 85% yield of the main species with minimal human intervention. These examples show the ability of model-based control to improve downstream reliability and support Industry 4.0. Therefore, AI models developed using batch-based datasets need validation across multiple batches, cell lines, scales, and operating conditions before they are applied to intensified or continuous bioprocesses. After validation, these models can be integrated with PAT, SCADA, process historians, and digital twins to support real-time monitoring, closed-loop control, and continuous manufacturing.

Regulatory challenges and perspectives

The biopharmaceutical and healthcare sectors have been slow to adopt AI and ML due to stricter regulations, uncertainty around innovative technologies, and lengthy approval process. Recent legislative measures, such as the 21st Century Cures Act and updated FDA policies are helping to accelerate the use of AI by supporting digital health innovations and modern analytical tools. Nevertheless, there are strong regulatory barriers that exist when changing the current manufacturing processes with ML-based improvements.187 Obtaining approval for ML-enhanced processes from the FDA/EMA is usually more difficult than developing a new biosimilar product by using ML. Although the use of ML models can be more effective than conventional approaches in MVDA, there is still a lack of regulatory standards for validation.189

To overcome these regulatory issues, biopharmaceutical firms, regulators, governments, and academic institutions may need to work together to develop effective AI governance models. Open-access programs such as Project DataSphere and DREAM Challenge are valuable because they facilitate data sharing, develop AI in a regulatory manner, and allow different teams to solve problems collaboratively. To apply ML at a large biomanufacturing scale, the sector will require standardized methods of validation, AI-friendly policies, and compliance with evolving regulations. It will be necessary to protect data, provide transparency, and adhere to regulatory requirements to foster trust, enhance efficiency, and enable scalable adoption because of the enhanced use of AI in drug development and production.34,187 Scalable AI deployment therefore requires lifecycle governance that includes documentation, explainability, version control, change control, performance monitoring, audit trails, and periodic revalidation. These measures ensure regulatory compliance and reliability after deployment, while also supporting transfer across batches and process scales for real-time monitoring and control in continuous manufacturing.

Future outlook

Biopharmaceutical manufacturing is moving toward fully digitized, automated, and data-driven systems. As the industry enters the era of Industry 4.0, combining ML with advanced sensors, real-time data collection, and automated machine-to-machine communication is expected to support faster and more informed process design.193,194 Digital twins are online simulations of bioprocesses that combines mechanistic models with real-time measurements and ML.198 They are expected to become a key tool for continuous monitoring, predictive control, and rapid problem solving in upstream and downstream processing. These smart systems will help companies operate more precisely and cost-effectively, with greater automation.195

The increasing application of multi-omics technologies and high-throughput analytics will further improve the availability of bioprocess data. Combining ML with information from genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics may support faster media design, more precise cell-line engineering, and improved prediction of product quality attributes.13 This may reduce the experimental workload required for media design and cell-line engineering, although the extent of reduction depends on the quality, scale, and representativeness of the available omics datasets. The combination of automated and internet of things (IoT)-enabled platforms can support real-time decision-making.34 They may improve yields or reduce development periods when supported by reliable sensors, validated models, and sufficient process data. Another emerging field with potential to transform ML in biomanufacturing is quantum computing. Quantum computing could enhance molecular simulations, speed up design-space exploration, and strengthen predictive models of CPPs and CQAs.196 However, its application in industrial biomanufacturing remains at an early and exploratory stage. Therefore, its effect on development cost remains prospective and requires further demonstration in industrial biomanufacturing settings.

Despite these opportunities, a number of challenges remain. These include the lack of trained personnel in data science and bioprocessing. Regulatory uncertainty is another concern. AI models must also be explainable and interpretable. Feasible regulatory principles, explainable AI methods, and effective data administration and cybersecurity will be critical for broader industrial integration.34,187 In addition, standardization of data formats and increased open-access data will enhance model training, benchmarking, and international collaboration.

Future innovation should also consider sustainability. Waste, energy, and environmental impact can be minimized with the help of ML-based optimization.16,119 As biomanufacturing continues to move toward greater integration and intelligence, ML may no longer be merely a useful tool. Instead, it could become a core technology that facilitates autonomous, self-optimizing processes. Such developments have the potential to improve productivity, scalability, and product-quality control, but these benefits are process-specific and should be demonstrated through validated case studies and technoeconomic analysis.57,71

Conclusion

mAb production is undergoing a major transformation as biopharmaceutical companies seek to make production more flexible and cost-effective. Process simulation and artificial intelligence have become important tools for optimization across different steps in mAb production. In USP, productivity improvement has been achieved through media optimization, dynamic feeding techniques, high-density cultures, and continuous perfusion bioreactors. The extent of improvement depends on factors such as cell line, feed composition, operating strategy, culture scale, and product-quality requirements. Mathematical models and simulation platforms allow the developers to evaluate operating strategies, identify bottlenecks, support bioreactor utilization decisions, and understand the effects of upstream decisions on the total cost and throughput.

The most expensive stage of mAb production is DSP, which has also benefited from innovative purification technologies. ProA capture alternatives and intensified strategies include ATPE, membrane-based purification, protein precipitation, periodic multi-column chromatography, CCTC, and PBA-MMC. These methods have shown potential to reduce COG or improve process efficiency under specific process conditions. However, their effects on recovery, purity, scalability, and product quality are case-dependent. Therefore, the selection of a suitable purification method should be based on the balance between yield, scalability, cost, impurity profile, facility fit, technology maturity, and product-specific quality requirements.57,59,63,97

Economic analyses indicate that a shift from traditional batch ProA capture to continuous or non-ProA platforms can decrease capital or operating costs, particularly at higher titers. However, the magnitude of cost reduction depends on process scale, facility configuration, resin lifetime, consumable use, labor, buffer demand, and downstream capacity.3,16,71 The importance of combining upstream and downstream operations has also been highlighted at the plant-level, where simulation tools and optimization models can support scheduling, debottlenecking, and economic decision-making. Continuous and single-use systems may reduce costs, improve resource use, and support more sustainable operation in certain facility and production-demand scenarios.16,71

Process intensification is further supported by AI and ML. Their applications are expanding from early-stage media design and cell-line screening to upstream control, CQA prediction, and downstream optimization. ML models can help identify complex relationships in biological systems and may support accurate prediction of cell growth, metabolite dynamics, and glycosylation patterns. AI is also being applied to chromatography optimization, impurity prediction, and selection of efficient purification sequences. Although AI-enabled bioprocessing is still developing, the studies reviewed in this paper show potential to reduce experimental burden and support faster development decisions.129,136,158,159 However, these benefits depend on dataset quality, model validation, process representativeness, and experimental confirmation.

Despite these improvements, there are still several challenges. These include data heterogeneity, absence of standardized and high-quality datasets, limited availability of open data, and model transferability.172,183 Moreover, implementation in GMP settings requires AI models that are transparent, interpretable, validated, and capable of providing process control. For GMP-relevant use, models also require lifecycle governance, including drift monitoring, retraining control, documentation, versioning, audit trails, change control, and periodic revalidation. Conversely, most bioprocess data are still produced in batch mode, which makes it challenging to develop robust models for continuous biomanufacturing. Overcoming these barriers will require good data infrastructure, digital solutions such as PAT tools, and collaboration among industry, regulatory agencies, and educational institutions.34,166

Based on the findings of this review, several near-term priorities can be identified for industry and researchers. First, standardized and high-quality bioprocess datasets should be developed to improve AI/ML model reliability, benchmarking, and transferability. Second, explainable and validated AI models are needed to support implementation in GMP and regulatory settings. Third, PAT tools, IoT-enabled sensors, and digital twins should be integrated gradually, starting with monitoring and decision-support applications before moving toward autonomous control. Fourth, upstream and downstream operations should be evaluated together using simulation and optimization tools to avoid shifting bottlenecks between process stages. Fifth, future process assessments should include sustainability metrics, such as energy use, waste generation, and resource efficiency, alongside cost, productivity, and product-quality outcomes.

Overall, this review shows that process simulation, continuous processing, and AI-based modeling are important tools for the next generation of mAb manufacturing. These technologies have the potential to make bioprocesses more reliable, scalable, and cost-effective. mAb production is expected to move gradually toward automated and integrated systems through the combined use of digital twins, continuous processing, PAT, and AI/ML-based decision support. This may help manufacturers respond to increasing global demand, support consistent product quality control, and reduce manufacturing timelines when validated process control models are available. The continued development of smart technologies and integrated process designs may contribute to a more efficient and resilient biopharmaceutical industry in the future.
