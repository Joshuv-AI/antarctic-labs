Barriers and facilitators for optimal perinatal primary care in low-risk pregnancies: an interview study with parents and healthcare professionals
Authors: Maëlle De Coninck, Sophie Albrecht, Arno Maetens, Joni Gilissen, Liesbeth Van kelst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShortened postpartum hospital stays have shifted perinatal care to primary care settings, potentially expanding the role of primary care midwives. However, specialist care remains prevalent during perinatal stages and the role of midwives in primary care is undefined. We aim to investigate current barriers to optimal organization of perinatal care at the primary care level for low-risk pregnancies in Flanders, Belgium, focusing on organization, accessibility, and quality of care. In addition, we explore what inhibits or can facilitate the expansion of the role of primary care midwives herein.MethodsWe conducted interviews with thirteen parents and nineteen healthcare providers. Parents had experienced parenthood within the past year, and healthcare providers had at least one year of experience in primary care. Participants were recruited through professional associations and regional primary care zones in the city of Antwerp. We applied a qualitative inductive content analysis.ResultsSeven barriers emerged, highlighting areas for improvement in perinatal primary care and the potential role of midwives. Findings underscore the need for increased awareness of preconception care, enhanced psychosocial follow-up, streamlined role distribution among healthcare providers, integrated offer of prenatal care, clarity about the value of postpartum care early on, and improved access to medical records.ConclusionsThe study identifies key opportunities for optimizing perinatal primary care. Coordinated care pathways, clear role distribution, and a unified patient record offer opportunities to enhance interprofessional collaboration in primary care. Continued dialogue and further research are recommended to develop an effective perinatal primary care model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03397-0.

Background

Due to the restructuring of hospital financing and the shortened average length of postpartum hospital stay in Belgium [1], spurred by the push from the World Health Organization (WHO) advocating for primary care [2], the demand for perinatal care has gradually shifted towards the primary care setting [3, 4]. Research has shown that healthcare systems with a strong focus on primary care provide better population health and that, in such systems, health is distributed more equitably and resources are used more efficiently [5]. Perinatal primary care has subsequently been found to improve mental health throughout the perinatal period [6], increase women’s access to care, improve uptake of care, enhance communication between women and professionals, and increase consumer satisfaction [7].

Since 2020, 60 local primary care zones have been established in Flanders, Belgium to integrate health and social care within specific regions (each taking care of approximately 100,000 inhabitants). The governance of these primary care zones is in the hands of a local “Care Council,” integrating local primary healthcare services (i.e. home care services, nursing, general practitioners (GPs), midwives), social services, organizations representing patients and informal caregivers, and local authorities at city or community-level [8]. The key ambition of this primary care model is to ensure continuity of care and provide accessible and low-threshold care, with a focus on support and collaboration structures [9–11]. The organization of perinatal care was likewise intended to be incorporated into these primary care zones.

Midwives have a distinct role in providing perinatal primary care, alongside specialist care [3, 4, 11, 12]. They are qualified to give full maternity care on their own responsibility to all women whose pregnancy and childbirth are uncomplicated. This includes prenatal, intrapartum and postnatal care for mother, partner and child [13]. Although the approach of midwifery care resonates with the main dimensions of primary care [5], midwives in the primary care context in Belgium are still predominantly involved in the postnatal stages of perinatal care, while prenatal care (and birthing) is still heavily focused on specialist care. Belgian statistics reveal that pregnant women and new mothers still seek care with specialist care providers straightaway. Over 98% of pregnant women initiate prenatal consultations with gynaecologists first [14] and hospitals are the preferred choice of birthing location. In 2022, only 465 (0.8%) of the total births took place in the home or primary care setting [15]. The pattern likely reflects not only unrestricted direct access to specialist care in Belgium (where parents can directly consult a healthcare provider of their choosing) [16–18], but also broader societal dynamics.

The dominance of biomedical frameworks, the medicalization of pregnancy, and a cultural tendency towards technology-driven care (technophilia) may shape parents’ initial orientation towards specialist settings, regardless of clinical need [19] These dynamics interact with health literacy and individuals’ capacity to perceive, seek, and navigate appropriate care — factors that are well established as determinants of healthcare utilisation [20].

Establishing these newly developed primary care zones has the potential to generate momentum for the organization of seamlessly integrated perinatal primary care involving midwives, next to specialist care providers. Despite a few local best practices [21, 22], it is not yet clear what would be the optimal way of interprofessional collaboration between midwives as primary care providers and gynaecologists to ensure best practice perinatal care for new parents. For low-risk pregnancies, for example, the recommended number of antenatal consultations is logged at 10 (for first pregnancies) and 7 for women who have had children before, regardless of which type of healthcare professional is monitoring the pregnancy (gynaecologist, midwife, or general practitioner). In the past decade, midwives have been increasingly called upon by pregnant women for pregnancy monitoring visits. This increase should, in theory, be compensated for by a reduction in the number of antenatal consultations with gynaecologists. However, recent statistics show that the number of antenatal consultations with midwives has increased over time, but this increase has not been compensated for by a reduction in the number of antenatal consultations with gynaecologists (on the contrary, this number has increased as well). This is an indication of over-consumption of care in a low-risk population [17, 23]. Establishing clear structures within a primary care zone for organising perinatal care is needed but has not yet been provided [16–18].

Study aim

This study aimed to examine current barriers to optimal perinatal primary care for low-risk pregnancies and to identify potential improvements. Special attention is paid to how midwives can be involved.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a qualitative descriptive study using in-depth semi-structured interviews with healthcare providers and parents. For reporting, we used the consolidated criteria for qualitative research (COREQ) checklist [24]. Focus groups were initially planned; however, in practice, only one group interview could be organised (three healthcare providers from a single group practice), while all other participants were interviewed individually due to availability and logistical constraints.

Study setting and participants

This study was carried out in Antwerp, a city in Flanders, Belgium, with a population of around 500,000 inhabitants and covering four primary care zones. More details can be found elsewhere [25]. Information about the four primary care zones of Antwerp can be found in Appendix 1.

Box 1. Organization of interprofessional antenatal care in Flanders, Belgium [26]In Belgium, a pregnant woman can choose between antenatal follow-up led by a gynaecologist, potentially involving a midwife or a general practitioner (GP), or follow-up led by a midwife or GP with the contribution of a gynaecologist. Midwife-led antenatal care can take various forms and differ within and between regions, hospitals, and private midwifery practices. It can occur in hospitals, clinics of the ‘Office of Birth and Childhood at Children & Families Service’ (ONE), or private midwifery primary care practices. Private practices may be solo or group practices, with group practices being either mono- or multidisciplinary and possibly including gynaecologists’ GP-led antenatal care, which mainly occurs in their private practices or in “ONE” clinics. Pregnant women can directly consult a gynaecologist at a hospital, a private practice, or, less frequently, at ONE clinic. There is no gatekeeping system; a pregnant woman can access a medical specialist directly without needing a referral from a GP or midwife. This freedom to choose a care provider is guaranteed by the Belgian Law on Patient Rights (Art 6). This law also grants women the right to change their care provider during the clinical pregnancy follow-up. In Belgium, specialist obstetric care is provided by gynecologists, whose role encompasses both obstetrics and gynaecology.

Eligibility criteria

We included healthcare providers if they belonged to one of the following professional groups: midwife, GP, gynaecologist, paediatrician, maternity care assistant, primary care psychologist, social nurse or midwife and had at least one year of working experience in a primary care profession. Parents were included if they met the following inclusion criteria: (a) being at least 18 years old; (b) residing within the geographical area of the four local primary care zones; (c) speaking Dutch; and (d) having given birth or became a father between a minimum of three months and a maximum of one year before the date of the interview. The latter inclusion criterion was chosen to ensure they had undergone the entire care trajectory from family planning to postpartum, while still having recent memories to recall their care needs. No selection criteria regarding the type of pregnancy (low or high risk) were used.

Recruitment

Recruitment ran between September 2019 and March 2020. Healthcare professionals were contacted through professional associations and via the four primary care zones. Inclusion was purposeful, aiming to achieve a sample with maximum variation in terms of age, gender, years of experience in primary care, and professional group. Parents were recruited via social media, the local Centre for Expertise on Maternity Care in Flanders, two umbrella organizations (‘Kind & Gezin’ and ‘Huis van het Kind’) and local hospitals. As part of the Social Engagement by Students course, students from the Midwifery program at the University of Applied Sciences and Arts were enlisted to recruit parents using a flyer developed by the researchers, containing information about study participation. Participants were asked to contact the researcher by email when interested.

The sample size was determined pragmatically, balancing feasibility and access to participants with the aim of capturing a broad range of perspectives from both parents and healthcare providers involved in perinatal care [27]. Recruitment and data analysis were conducted concurrently, allowing for iterative refinement of emerging themes. Interviews were continued until no substantially new codes or themes emerged within each participant group.

Study procedures

Interested and eligible participants were invited to a semi-structured interview. After the researcher provided information, the study participants gave oral informed consent. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, we digitally interviewed all participants. Interviews lasted between 30 and 120 min. A pre-structured topic list was used. Questions for parents (Appendix 2) included for example: ‘When you suspected you were pregnant, which healthcare provider did you contact first?’, ‘Where did you give birth? Would you do it again? Would you consider any of the other options?’, ‘What information did you need during the labour and delivery of your next child?’. And for the healthcare providers (Appendix 3): ‘For you, what would an ideal care flow look like to provide quality care to a woman of childbearing age with an active desire to have children?’. In this study, psychosocial follow-up refers to the systematic assessment and discussion of psychosocial factors during the perinatal period, including emotional well-being, mental health, social support, relational context, and potential psychosocial stressors, to identify needs, provide support, and facilitate timely referral when indicated.

The researcher asked about the participants’ characteristics and noted them down at the beginning of each interview. All interviews were audiotaped and transcribed verbatim by an external transcription agency.

Analysis

A qualitative inductive content analysis was conducted by two researchers (MDK, LVK), using NVivo 12 software. This approach was chosen to allow patterns and meanings to emerge directly from the data rather than being imposed a priori. The analytical process comprised four iterative steps [28]. First, transcripts were open-coded, with codes assigned to meaningful units of text reflecting participants’ experiences and perspectives. Second, codes were compared and grouped into broader categories based on conceptual similarity. Third, relationships between categories were explored through axial coding, allowing patterns across interviews to be identified. Finally, higher-order interpretations were developed by clustering related categories into overarching analytical domains that capture recurring barriers in perinatal primary care. Although the interview guides were structured around predefined topics, the analysis remained inductive, with codes and categories derived from the data rather than the topic list. The predefined structure served to guide data collection, while allowing sufficient openness for unanticipated issues to emerge.

Several strategies were used to enhance the rigor and trustworthiness of the analysis [29]. Two researchers independently coded a subset of transcripts and discussed discrepancies to reach consensus, thereby enhancing analytical credibility. Peer debriefing was conducted throughout the analysis to reflect on emerging interpretations and challenge assumptions. We documented coding decisions and category development. Data saturation was monitored during concurrent data collection and analysis and was considered reached when no new codes or categories emerged within participant groups.

Results

Participant characteristics

We interviewed 13 parents (11 mothers and two fathers) and 19 healthcare providers (Table 1). Respondents were predominantly women (85% and 74%, respectively). Most respondents in the parent group had attained a higher education degree (42% of the short type and 33% of the long type). With only two exceptions, all parents had undergone a vaginal delivery. The professional participants encompassed seven professional categories, with midwives being the most represented (32%). On average, professionals had over 10 years of working experience (standard deviation 9.6).

Barriers to optimal perinatal primary care

Seven distinct but interrelated barriers to optimal perinatal primary care were identified. These barriers span the perinatal care continuum and operate at the level of awareness, professional practice, care organization, and system infrastructure. For some barriers, participants also suggested potential solutions to help address the issues identified. Exemplary participant quotes illustrating each barrier are presented in Table 2.

Lack of awareness among parents about preconception care

Lack of awareness among parents about preconception care

Lack of awareness among parents about preconception care

This barrier concerns parents’ limited awareness of what preconception care entails, its purpose, and where or with whom it can be accessed. Although participants, including parents, acknowledged the value of preconception care, especially needed to provide psychoeducation about healthy pregnancy and mental health, parents were often unaware of what that constitutes and where (and with whom) it is available. Healthcare providers similarly acknowledged the lack of awareness among prospective parents about the existence and importance of preconception consultations and where to seek them.

Professional participants suggested that this lack of awareness may be related to the fact that preconception care is not routinely embedded as a part of perinatal care or within perinatal care pathways but instead tends to occur only when a prospective parent spontaneously mentions a desire to conceive. As a result, preconception discussions were described as incidental rather than systematic.

To increase awareness of preconception care, building knowledge about its existence by integrating it into the training curriculum of various primary care healthcare professionals and encouraging them to actively advocate for it with prospective parents actively, was considered. Parents emphasised the importance of designated referrals from one healthcare provider to another to ensure optimal prenatal care is provided.

2.Psychosocial follow-up in primary care is underused

2.Psychosocial follow-up in primary care is underused

Psychosocial follow-up in primary care is underused

This barrier reflects the inconsistent and often reactive nature of psychosocial follow-up during the perinatal period. Psychosocial follow-up throughout the entire perinatal period was found to be a key task for perinatal primary care but susceptible to improvement. Despite its recognised importance, not all primary healthcare providers inquire about the psychosocial well-being of their clients throughout the perinatal period. Some healthcare providers mentioned a general lack of familiarity with the psychosocial aspect of care, leading them to avoid the topic out of fear and lack of self-efficacy.

Most parents indicated that they felt they could get support from their healthcare providers (gynaecologist, GP, and midwife) if they explicitly asked for it. However, they also expressed a desire for more proactive and explicit attention to their mental well-being, noting that consultations often focused primarily on the baby and biomedical aspects of pregnancy. Several parents described feeling hesitant to raise psychosocial concerns themselves, particularly given prevailing expectations that pregnancy should be a positive experience, which was also noticed by professional participants.

3.Limited use of available guidelines

3.Limited use of available guidelines

Limited use of available guidelines

This barrier relates to the gap between the availability of perinatal care guidelines and their consistent application in daily practice. Healthcare providers reported being aware of guidelines made available about perinatal and/or primary care, they expressed concerns that inconsistent adherence to these guidelines created confusion and disrupted organization within the primary care region. Providers cited examples such as referrals not being made when recommended and substantial variation between professionals in both the content and structure of prenatal consultations.

4.Diffusion of roles and responsibilities among healthcare professionals results in gynaecologists taking the lead

4.Diffusion of roles and responsibilities among healthcare professionals results in gynaecologists taking the lead

Diffusion of roles and responsibilities among healthcare professionals results in gynaecologists taking the lead

This barrier concerns unclear role division within perinatal primary care, leading to gynaecologists assuming a dominant role even in low-risk pregnancies, organically by the healthcare referrals as well as perceived by patients. Healthcare providers described a lack of clear delineation of roles and responsibilities among professionals involved in perinatal care. While many agreed that tasks could be distributed across disciplines, in practice gynaecologists were most often consulted and positioned as the main care provider, a pattern that was also reflected in parents’ accounts.

Following confirmation of pregnancy—most initiated through a general practitioner for a blood test—parents were typically referred to a gynaecologist. Referrals were generally determined by the hospital’s gynecologist where the woman intended to give birth, rather than by a deliberate consideration of which professional was most appropriate to provide primary pregnancy follow-up. As a result, gynecologists frequently became the default provider for ongoing care, even in the absence of medical risk factors. Only a minority of parents reported directly seeking out a midwife or choosing a gynecologist based on personal preference or perceived relational qualities. This may reflect a lack of awareness of the midwife’s role in antenatal care, as well as the default tendency to consult a gynecologist first — patterns consistent what was described above. As illustrated in Table 3, some parents only discovered the scope of midwifery care during or after their pregnancy, suggesting that informed choice was often constrained by limited prior knowledge.

Throughout the pregnancy, most parents in this study were monitored by a gynecologist, either exclusively or in combination with a GP or midwife. This practice contrasts with existing guidelines, which position the GP as a suitable primary care provider for pregnancy monitoring due to their familiarity with the woman and the reimbursed nature of care. Nevertheless, GPs were often described as being overlooked in practice, a finding echoed across professional interviews. Specialist gynecologists, however, frequently identified midwives as well-positioned to take on substantial aspects of pregnancy care, particularly in low-risk situations, citing their specialized expertise and greater availability compared to GPs. At the same time, professionals across disciplines noted that not all GPs have sufficient experience in pregnancy care and suggested that additional training would be required for GPs to assume a more central role.

Midwives positioned themselves as ideally suited to follow up low-risk pregnancies within primary care, emphasising the importance of close collaboration with both GPs and gynaecologists. They highlighted the distinction between eutocia (normal, uncomplicated labour), which can be managed in primary care, and dystocia (complicated or obstructed labour requiring specialist care), which necessitates referral to secondary care. Parents who received “midwife-led” follow-up—either by choice or following referral—reported high levels of satisfaction, particularly due to longer consultations, more extensive information provision, and a greater sense of reassurance. However, some parents who alternated between midwife- and gynecologist-led care expressed disappointment when midwife consultations did not include access to ultrasounds, reinforcing the perception of the gynecologist as the central and decisive provider in pregnancy care.

Lack of integrated patient records and difficulty sharing information

This barrier reflects the absence of shared information systems across disciplines. Healthcare providers reported that the inability to access each other’s notes and records hampered continuity of care and interdisciplinary collaboration. They noted that the paper-and-pencil version of the pregnancy booklet that is most often used in Flanders contains limited information and is not consistently filled out by all involved healthcare providers, and relies on the responsibility of the patient to bring it with them to each consultation. Providers unanimously expressed the need for an integrated digital patient record that would allow all involved professionals to access and contribute relevant information.

6.Fragmented prenatal support and information

Fragmented prenatal support and information

This barrier relates to the uncoordinated and dispersed nature of prenatal information and support offerings. Parents expressed that engaging in prenatal courses and prenatal preparation during pregnancy has been beneficial for them, highlighting the importance of psychosocial health education in enabling informed choices. However, they also noted that the current availability of such offerings is fragmented and overwhelming, making it difficult for them to feel empowered in decision-making. They highlighted that the absence of a central point of contact and the dispersal of information across multiple channels, including the Internet, books, family or acquaintances, and healthcare providers made it difficult for parents to navigate available options and feel supported in their choices.

7.Lack of timely awareness about community-based cradle or postpartum care

Lack of timely awareness about community-based cradle or postpartum care

This barrier concerns the late or insufficient visibility of postpartum support options during pregnancy. One of the topics that parents express a desire for earlier information on is postpartum care/cradle care. In their interviews, postpartum care providers sought recognition from other healthcare providers and believed that the lack of recognition (and knowledge about what they do) is why they are not well-known among parents. Postpartum care providers consequently request active referrals from other healthcare providers during the prenatal period. On the parents’ side, they mention that the postpartum period is challenging, and additional support during that time would be appreciated. As a result, postpartum care was often only considered once challenges had already emerged after birth, rather than being anticipated and planned during the prenatal period.

An opportunity mentioned by postpartum/cradle care providers is the possibility of initiating postpartum care prenatally, especially in cases of complicated pregnancies where rest is prescribed or anticipated.

Discussion

Interviews with new parents and healthcare professionals in this study revealed seven barriers that might inhibit optimal perinatal care offered to low-risk pregnancies within the primary care context. These barriers relate to preconception care, psychosocial follow-up, the usability of available guidelines, the diffusion of roles and responsibilities, the lack of shared patient files, and the underestimation of the value of cradle or postpartum care.

Midwives can have a distinct role in preconception care, referring to biomedical, social, and behavioural interventions and health counselling that occurs before or between pregnancies [45]. Globally, the first 1000 days are identified as a crucial period to ensure optimal and equitable access to care to avoid adverse outcomes for mother and child [46]. While preconception care falls within the scope of midwifery practice [47], as was also stipulated by participants in our study, midwives across the world as well as in our local communities, seem to still largely practice care related to pregnancy, birth and the traditional six-week postnatal period – not preconceptionally [45]. They should receive support from other professionals by referring people to midwives so they are capable of fulfilling their scope in the provision of care outside of the pregnancy to the 12-week postpartum period, as it presents an opportunity to improve the health status of women, babies, and communities [46]. This is an expected finding in our study, given the internationally established recognition of midwives being ideally placed to provide pre-conceptual care due to their existing professional knowledge base and expertise in primary care [46, 48]. However, enhancing the knowledge of colleagues in the primary care team, as well as the community, is crucial [45]. Beyond knowledge alone, this requires a broader cultural shift — transforming prevailing discourses around pregnancy and childbirth, rebuilding trust and confidence in midwifery-led care, and redefining what is valued both socially and professionally in the perinatal field [49, 50]. Policymakers must acknowledge the importance of billing codes to ensure midwives are aware of their preparedness and willingness. GPs in our study as well as in others were also found suitable to provide preconception consultations [21].

The psychosocial monitoring of pregnant women is not obligatory and clearly not uniform between professionals. Parents in our study reported that psychosocial aspects were rarely discussed unless they explicitly raised concerns themselves, while healthcare providers described uncertainty about responsibility and limited consultation time as barriers to systematic psychosocial follow-up. Since perinatal mental disorders are among the commonest morbidities of pregnancy, various initiatives in scientific research as well as in clinical practice (i.e., hospitals creating care pathways) are developed [51] but mental health screening remains low in primary care settings [52], as was also experienced by parents in our study. Promising strategies to support perinatal mental health in low-risk pregnancies include standardised psychosocial assessment integrated into the care pathway [6], psycho-education [53], emotional support by a range of healthcare professionals (including social workers and GPs) [53], peer support [53], and the provision of accessible tools for parents and providers [53]. Perinatal mental health literacy is among the most prominent determinants of screening uptake and treatment accessibility [34]. However, the evidence remains limited [54], and barriers identified in our study – consistent with other findings – point to a complex interplay of factors. This warrants further investigation [54]. Awareness raising and education among primary care professionals are crucial [55]. Importantly, these barriers cannot be understood solely at the level of individual professional practice but appear to intersect with broader systemic factors [20]. Time constraints during consultations, limited reimbursement structures for psychosocial care, and a lack of dedicated funding for interdisciplinary collaboration may discourage healthcare providers from initiating or sustaining psychosocial discussions [56]. In addition, uncertainty about referral pathways and the availability of follow-up resources may further reduce providers’ willingness to explore psychosocial concerns [30]. These structural conditions may partly explain why psychosocial follow-up remains inconsistent in practice, despite growing awareness of perinatal mental health needs and the availability of screening tools and care pathways [31]. Beyond structural barriers, however, inconsistency may also reflect gaps in professional training and curricula, and a broader question of what is valued within primary care culture — suggesting that sustainable change requires not only system-level reform but a reorientation of professional education and identity towards psychosocial competence [32].

Both national and international guidelines on integrated perinatal care for low-risk pregnancies are available [33, 35], but the lack of good role division among different healthcare providers hinders their usefulness and applicability [26]. Healthcare providers in our study reported that unclear role division and the lack of shared patient files complicated collaboration across disciplines, while parents experienced this fragmentation as having to repeatedly explain their situation to different professionals. A clear guideline has been developed in Belgium outlining criteria about when and how to define referral from a primary care practitioner to a specialist in obstetrics and gynaecology [23] but generally lacks information about who this primary care practitioner is and what their role is, distinct or similar to a colleague also working in the primary care setting. At the primary care zone level, there might be an opportunity to develop local agreements on how roles are divided and how patients are referred. However, most practitioners cover various regions, and coordination across regions is necessary. Best practices from other countries can serve as the basis to develop and further evaluate these models, albeit after contextualisation and careful transfer of these interventions to our local setting [36]. In the Netherlands, for example, the follow-up of every low-risk pregnancy is assigned to primary care providers [10, 18]. Midwives and GPs provide care for these women, paying attention to continuous risk selection and referring them to secondary care if complications arise. If this happens, clinical midwives monitor pregnant women intramurally. Regardless of the place of delivery, the supervision remains in the hands of a primary care provider if the pregnancy proceeds normally. This is called the social or midwife-led model, which emphasises approaching a pregnant woman according to physiology. This is in contrast to Flanders, where a medical model is adopted based on the view that birth requires medicalisation [14]. In the United Kingdom, a similar organization of maternity care is observed. Pregnant women initiate their maternity care in the primary care setting with midwives or GPs. During the first contact with the midwife, a risk selection is performed during an extensive conversation. Subsequently, the pregnant woman will be referred to the appropriate healthcare provider (depending on the presence of potential risk factors) [20]. A low-risk pregnant woman will receive full care from a midwife; if there are risk factors, care will be shared with physicians [20]. The slogan “all women need a midwife, but some need a doctor too” is highly applicable. If the pregnancy proceeds normally or physiologically, monitoring is provided by a primary care health worker. Similar to the Netherlands, childbirth can take place at home, in the hospital, or in a midwife-led department [10, 20]. Subsequently, care is taken up again by the primary care provider or the primary care organization that also took care of the pregnancy. In practice, most pregnant and postpartum women in the United Kingdom benefit from care through this community care system [21].

Finally, the results of our study stipulate that the scope of practice of midwifery in primary care is tied to educational standards, regulation and licensing, hence integration of primary care models into the education of midwives and their role herein (considering it to be within the scope of their practice), as well as advocating to policymakers the importance of an optimal legislative and/or reimbursement policy for midwives to be considered part of the integrated primary care team, is essential [37, 38]. Integrated patient records have been called upon, also to the wider context of optimising primary care [39].

While previous studies have highlighted the role of midwives and the persistence of fragmented perinatal care, our findings gain added relevance when viewed in light of recent inter-federal initiatives for integrated care in Belgian, offering concrete entry points for translating evidence into practice and might form an inspiration for other healthcare systems: Since early 2024, intensive consultations have been underway between the federal government, the federated entities, and various stakeholders to develop the first inter-federal programme for integrated care, targeting pregnant women, their children, and their families during the first 1,000 days after conception. This period is critical for a child’s growth, development, and quality of life in both the short and long term. The programme aims to ensure effective access to integrated, continuous, and needs-based care and support for vulnerable pregnant women, in line with the principle of proportional universalism [40].Achieving this requires close collaboration between healthcare and welfare professionals and active engagement with families. In its initial phase, the programme focuses on pregnant women with psychosocial vulnerabilities through the implementation of the Born in Belgium tool, with the ultimate goal of expanding to all pregnant women, mothers, children, and families during the first 1,000 days to provide holistic, tailored care and support [41, 42].

Limitations and strengths

Strengths of this study include its comprehensive approach, which utilises in-depth interviews with both healthcare providers and parents, allowing for a rich understanding of perspectives. We aimed to ensure a rigorous analysis process with peer debriefing and mitigating bias by double-coding. There are several limitations to this study. First, recall bias in parents might potentially affect the accuracy of responses. While efforts were made to recruit a diverse range of participants, we did not ensure recruitment strategies were in place to reach currently underserved populations [43, 44]. In addition, our sample consisted exclusively of heterosexual couples, which limits the transferability of findings to LGBTQ+ parents and conducted primarily in Antwerp, a city in Belgium. Moreover, recruitment via professional associations may have resulted in selection bias, as it likely attracted participants with stronger professional engagement or access to formal care networks, potentially excluding more marginalized or underserved perspectives; future studies should therefore consider complementary recruitment strategies to enhance inclusivity and transferability.

Conclusion

The provision of perinatal care within the primary care context for low-risk women can be organised optimally by raising awareness about the availability of preconception care, as well as integrating fragmented offers of prenatal support, increasing awareness in healthcare professionals and the assessment of psychosocial support, clarity in role distribution among gynaecologist, GP and midwife, integrating medical records and emphasising the value of postpartum care early on in the pregnancy trajectory. Healthcare providers need to continue the dialogue. Further research should focus on optimal midwifery-led primary care models and how they align with specialist care.
