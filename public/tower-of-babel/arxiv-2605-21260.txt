On the Cost and Benefit of Chain of Thought: A Learning-Theoretic Perspective
Yue Zhang, Zhiyi Dong, Tommaso Cesari, Yongyi Mao, [0.5em] University of Ottawa, Ottawa, Canada

Abstract
We develop a learning-theoretic framework for understanding Chain of Thought (CoT). We model CoT as the interaction between an answer map and a chain rule that generates intermediate questions autoregressively, and define the reasoning risk of a hypothesis under this interaction. Our first result is a tight canonical decomposition of this risk into two terms with opposing roles: an oracle-trajectory risk (OTR), which captures the benefit of CoT and reduces to a target-domain risk in a domain adaptation problem, and a trajectory-mismatch risk (TMR), which captures the cost of CoT through error accumulation along mismatched reasoning trajectories. We then show that this cost is unavoidable without structure: if any one of the loss, the hypothesis answer map, or the chain rule lacks stability, the TMR can be arbitrarily large even when the OTR is zero and the hypothesis is uniformly close to the ground truth. Conversely, under stability, we prove a tight upper bound on the TMR governed by an exact amplification factor that identifies bounded, linear, and exponential error-growth regimes. Together, these results give a precise theory of when CoT helps, when it hurts, and what controls the transition between the two.

This work takes a step in that direction.
We begin with a general formulation of CoT via a notion we call a

In this formulation, the CoT answer to a question is treated as a sequence of new questions generated by the chain rule, together with their respective answers obtained from an answer map.
This formulation allows us to define a notion of recoverability for each question, which determines whether the answer to the question can be obtained by CoT prompting.
It also allows us to define the

Although this risk is defined for any test distribution, we are primarily interested in the case where the distribution is supported on what we call ‘‘recoverable’’ points

In this setting, and under the mild assumption that the loss function defining the risk is a quasimetric, we observe that the reasoning risk decomposes into two terms, which we respectively designate the
trajectory-mismatch risk

oracle-trajectory risk
(OTR).
The TMR term captures the risk due to the CoT following the “wrong trajectory” because of answering the chain-rule-generated questions using a hypothesis answer map rather than the ground truth, whereas the OTR term captures the risk due solely to the final answer being given by the hypothesis answer map when the CoT trajectory has been correctly guided by the ground truth up to the final question.
These two terms are respectively associated with the

First, the OTR term, which captures the benefit of CoT, can be viewed as the target-domain risk arising in a domain adaptation problem
[

]

In particular, the chain rule interacting with the ground-truth answer map serves as a transformation of input questions, and the test distribution pushed forward by this transformation can be viewed as the target-domain distribution; the distribution from which examples are drawn to train the LLM is the source-domain distribution.
Thus, OTR minimization coincides with the objective of domain adaptation.
This perspective reveals how CoT prompting can be beneficial: when the push-forward of the test distribution is close to the distribution used for training, the OTR is guaranteed to be small.
In other words, CoT prompting can provide benefit by aligning the test distribution, via push-forward, with the training distribution; this allows the model to answer questions that are more complex than, and very different from, those seen during training.

On the other hand, the TMR term captures an unavoidable cost of CoT prompting: any inconsistency between a hypothesis answer map and the ground truth can be magnified along the mismatched CoT trajectory, contributing to an additional risk.
We characterize the behavior of this TMR term.
Specifically, via a no-free-lunch theorem, we show that without appropriate stability assumptions on the loss function, the hypothesis answer map, and the chain rule, the TMR can be arbitrarily large.
Under such stability assumptions, we then prove an upper bound on the TMR and show that this bound is optimal.
This bound identifies three cost regimes, in which the TMR remains bounded, grows linearly, or grows exponentially with the number of CoT steps.
This result helps explain error-accumulation phenomena observed in recent work on CoT
[

,
7
,
119

As a step toward a learning theory of CoT, this work provides a theoretical analysis of both the benefit and cost of CoT from a learning-theoretic perspective.
Our formulation differs from that of
[
45

These perspectives are complementary.

Formalizing Chain of Thought

In this paper, we view an LLM as a machine that maps an input object to an output object.
Concretely, the input may be a sequence of tokens, such as a user question or prompt, and the output may also be a sequence of tokens, such as an answer or response.
The terms “question” and “answer” should therefore be interpreted broadly: a question may refer to any input object submitted to the model, and an answer to the corresponding output object generated by the model.
This convention does not exclude contextual or multi-round interactions, since any relevant context, conversation history, system prompt, retrieved document, or memory state can be incorporated into the input object itself.
Thus, our formulation studies the map from a single input representation to a single output representation, without restricting what information the input representation may encode.

Modern LLMs typically operate in an autoregressive manner
[
80
,
37
,
86
,
1
]
: they generate one output token at a time, and each generated token is appended to the input sequence before the next token is generated.
Throughout the paper, we assume that this autoregressive process is deterministic, namely, that the next token at each time step depends functionally on the input sequence and on the output tokens generated so far.
More precisely, if
\mathcal{V}
denotes the token vocabulary and
\mathcal{V}^{*}
denotes the space of all finite strings over
\mathcal{V}
, then an LLM with fixed parameters can be identified with a sequence of functions
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots
, collectively denoted by
F
, where each function
F_{i}\colon\mathcal{V}^{i}\to\mathcal{V}
generates the next token from the current input string of length
i
.
Although this restriction abstracts away from the stochastic decoding used by practical LLMs, it allows us to focus on key aspects of reasoning in LLMs without introducing additional technicalities arising from stochasticity, an interesting topic in its own right.

The autoregressive process specified by
F
does not run forever: once a special token indicating the END signal is generated, the process terminates.
Thus, one can equivalently identify
F
with a function mapping
\mathcal{V}^{*}
to
\mathcal{V}^{*}
, which, by abuse of notation, we also denote by
F
.
That is, for any input string
x\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
, the LLM specified by
F
generates an output string
F(x)\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
.

To answer a user question, presented as a string
x
, the LLM
F
may be used in two modes.
In the
direct mode
,
x
is passed to the LLM directly, and the LLM generates the answer string
F(x)
.
In the

, an instruction prompt, for example “solve the question step by step”, is appended to
x
.
The resulting string, denoted by
x^{\rm CoT}
, is then fed to the LLM, which generates
F(x^{\rm CoT})
as the answer.
Empirical evidence
[
99
,
47
,

,
83
,
93

95

Developing a sound theoretical understanding of LLMs operating in the CoT mode is the key motivation of this work.
To that end, define the function
F^{\rm CoT}\colon\mathcal{V}^{*}\to\mathcal{V}^{*}
by
F^{\rm CoT}(x)=F(x^{\rm CoT})
.
The starting point of this work is a formalization of

In our formulation, instead of treating the LLM

as a function mapping
\mathcal{V}^{*}
to
\mathcal{V}^{*}

\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{X}

We impose no structure on

At this level of abstraction, the LLM

3
Benefit of CoT

Recall that the classical notion of statistical risk of
a hypothesis
f
with respect to the ground truth
g

\mu

\mathcal{X}

\ell

R_{\mu,\ell}(f,g)\coloneqq\underset{X\sim\mu}{\mathbb{E}}\Bigl[\ell\bigl(f(X),\,g(X)\bigr)\Bigr].

The OTR term is exactly

with respect to

\nu

K

Since the LLM is trained on a distribution, say

\nu

[
13
]

\mu
is the source-domain distribution, the push-forward distribution
Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu
is the target-domain distribution, and one aims to learn a hypothesis, often from a labeled source sample and an unlabeled target sample, that minimizes the target-domain risk.

This observation has a useful implication.
When the push-forward distribution
Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu
coincides with the training distribution
\mu
, minimizing the OTR is exactly the usual supervised-learning objective under the training distribution.
On the other hand, when
Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu
differs from
\mu

For example, existing domain-adaptation bounds imply inequalities of the form

{\rm OTR}\leq R_{\mu,\ell}(f,g)+{\rm Disc}\bigl(\mu,Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu\bigr)+R^{*},

where
{\rm Disc}(\cdot,\cdot)
measures some notion of discrepancy between two distributions and
R^{*}
is a term independent of
f
.
The terms
R_{\mu,\ell}(f,g)
and
{\rm Disc}\bigl(\mu,Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu\bigr)
can often be upper-bounded using empirical estimates from data, together with concentration inequalities involving suitable complexity measures.
As illustrated in
Appendix

C

{\rm Disc}\bigl(\mu,Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu\bigr)
is small, one obtains a guarantee of low OTR.

We dedicate the remainder of this paper to understanding the behavior of the TMR term, which captures the cost of CoT.

4
Cost of CoT

We now turn to the TMR term, which captures the cost of CoT.
We first show that, without appropriate stability assumptions, the TMR cannot be controlled even when the OTR is zero and the hypothesis is uniformly close to the ground truth.
We then prove a tight upper bound under stability.

4.2
A tight bound on the Trajectory-Mismatch Risk under stability

In this section, we prove a tight bound on the Trajectory-Mismatch Risk (TMR) under the assumption that the loss
\ell
is
\lambda
-stable, the hypothesis
f
is
\phi
-stable, and each step of the chain rule
D_{K}
is
\delta
-stable.
The key quantity in the bound is the following amplification factor.

Definition 2
.

For any integer
K\geq 2
, any
\phi\geq 0
, and any
\delta\geq 0
, define the
amplification factor

\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1-(\phi\delta)^{K-1}}{1-\phi\delta},&\text{if }(\phi\geq 1\text{ or }\delta\leq 1),\ \text{and }\phi\delta\neq 1,\\
\displaystyle K-1,&\text{if }(\phi\geq 1\text{ or }\delta\leq 1),\ \text{and }\phi\delta=1,\\
\displaystyle\delta^{K-2-m_{K}(\phi,\delta)}\frac{1-(\phi\delta)^{m_{K}(\phi,\delta)+1}}{1-\phi\delta},&\text{if }\phi<1,\ \delta>1,\ \text{and }\phi\delta\neq 1,\\
\displaystyle\delta^{K-2-n_{K}(\phi,\delta)}\bigl(n_{K}(\phi,\delta)+1\bigr),&\text{if }\phi<1,\ \delta>1,\ \text{and }\phi\delta=1,\end{cases}

where

m_{K}(\phi,\delta)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\phi=0,\\[3.99994pt]
\displaystyle\min\biggl(K-2,\biggl\lfloor\log_{\phi\delta}\Bigl(\frac{\delta-1}{\delta(1-\phi)}\Bigr)\biggr\rfloor\biggr),&\text{if }\phi>0,\end{cases}\qquad n_{K}(\phi,\delta)\coloneqq\min\biggl(K-2,\ \biggl\lfloor\frac{1}{\delta-1}\biggr\rfloor\biggr).

The amplification factor
\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta)
gives an

theoretical picture of how CoT errors evolve with the number of reasoning steps.
In the first regime of the definition,

is simply a geometric sum with ratio
\phi\delta
.
In that regime, CoT errors remain controlled when
\phi\delta<1

\phi\delta=1
, and snowball exponentially when
\phi\delta>1
.
This provides a formal explanation for phenomena observed in CoT reasoning, including stable reasoning, gradual drift, and snowballing error accumulation
[

,

]

Related theoretical and empirical work has also emphasized that the quality and stability of intermediate reasoning steps can affect generalization

The mixed regime

shows that the story is not governed only by the product

Even if the hypothesis itself contracts question perturbations, the chain rule may amplify previous question mismatches strongly enough to create exponential growth.
The maximization problem determining

Thus, the bound identifies two distinct sources of CoT instability: instability of the hypothesis answer map through
\phi

Proof sketch.

The proof first tracks how discrepancies propagate through the two CoT trajectories.

Discussion and limitations

Our formulation uses a fixed number
K
of CoT steps.
This is a useful simplification, but it does not fully capture how LLMs operate in practice, where the number of reasoning steps may depend on the input and is only determined during generation.
One way to accommodate variable-length CoT is to define a chain rule as an infinite sequence of functions
D^{(1)},D^{(2)},\ldots
, with
D^{(k)}\colon\mathcal{X}^{2k-1}\to\mathcal{X}
, together with a stopping rule.
For example, when the chain rule interacts with the ground-truth answer map
g
, one may define a stopping rule
K_{D,g}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{N}
and, for each input
x
, set
K_{x}\coloneqq K_{D,g}(x)
and
D_{K_{x}}\coloneqq(D^{(1)},\ldots,D^{(K_{x})})
.
The results in this paper can then be viewed as applying to each fixed value of the selected reasoning length.
We have chosen the fixed-
K
formulation because it keeps the notation and concepts simpler, allowing us to focus on the main cost-benefit decomposition and stability phenomena.

A limitation of this work is that the current formulation treats chain rules and answer maps as deterministic, whereas practical LLMs often use stochastic decoding.
This choice allows us to put the spotlight on the main phenomenon of interest: how errors propagate along CoT trajectories and how stability controls this propagation.
We expect stochastic chain rules and stochastic answer maps to require additional notation and bookkeeping, but not to alter the basic cost-benefit picture developed here.
Another scope restriction is that we assume the relevant chain rule is available at test time.
This captures settings in which the chain rule and the answer map can be specified or learned separately, but it does not model situations in which learning the chain rule and learning the answer map are coupled.
Understanding this coupled learning problem is an important direction for future work.
Finally, our analysis focuses on test distributions supported on
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable points.
This assumption isolates the behavior of CoT on inputs whose answers can in principle be recovered by the oracle-guided chain rule.
If the test distribution also assigns mass to non-recoverable points, one can partition the distribution into its recoverable and non-recoverable parts: the former is controlled by the TMR/OTR analysis developed here, while the latter requires an additional oracle-mismatch term to be controlled separately.
We leave this more general, but less transparent, formulation to future work.

R^{D_{K}}_{\nu,\ell}(f,g)=\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(A^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(X),\ g(X)\Bigr)\biggr]=\ell\bigl(Y,g(0)\bigr)=\ell\bigl(Y,0\bigr)=\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}Y,M\right\}=M.

The trajectory-mismatch risk is

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(X)\bigr),\ f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=\ell\bigl(f(x_{\star}),f(0)\bigr)=\ell\bigl(Y,0\bigr)=M,

and the oracle-trajectory risk is

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(X)\bigr),\ g\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=\ell\bigl(f(0),g(0)\bigr)=\ell\bigl(0,0\bigr)=0.

This proves, in particular, that the right-hand side of the decomposition is
M+0=M
, which equals the
reasoning risk, hence (
1
) holds with equality.
∎

If the stability of the loss
\ell
is removed, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 2

(No free lunch without stability of the loss)
.

For any integer
K\geq 2
, any
M>0
, and any
\varepsilon>0
,
there exist a metric space
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
, a quasimetric loss function

\ell\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,M]
,
two
\varepsilon
-stable functions
f,g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\rho\bigl(f(x),g(x)\bigr)\leq\varepsilon
,
a
K
-step chain rule
D_{K}
whose steps are
\varepsilon
-stable,
and a distribution
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
whose support
consists of
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable points such that (
1
) holds with equality and

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},f}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=M,\quad\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=0.

Proof.

Fix any integer
K\geq 2
, any
M>0
, and any
\varepsilon>0
.
Let

L\coloneqq\min\bigl\{\varepsilon,1\bigr\},

and let
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq[0,1]
.
For any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, let

\rho(x,y)\coloneqq\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert.

Define the loss function
\ell\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,M]
, for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, by

\ell(x,y)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }x=y,\\
M,&\text{if }x\neq y.\end{cases}

Then
\ell
satisfies the triangle inequality and for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\ell(x,x)=0
. Hence
\ell
is a quasimetric.
Indeed, for any
x,y,z\in\mathcal{X}
, if
x=y
, then
\ell(x,y)=0
and the claim is immediate.
If
x\neq y
, then at least one of
x\neq z
or
z\neq y
must hold, and therefore

\ell(x,z)+\ell(z,y)\geq M=\ell(x,y).

Now define
f,g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, by

f(x)\coloneqq Lx,\qquad g(x)\coloneqq 0.

Then
f
is
L
-stable and
g
is
0
-stable.
Since
L\leq\varepsilon
, both functions are
\varepsilon
-stable.
Moreover,

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\rho\bigl(f(x),g(x)\bigr)=\sup_{x\in[0,1]}Lx=L\leq\varepsilon.

Let the testing distribution be the Dirac measure at
1
,

\nu\coloneqq\delta_{1}.

We now define the chain rule.
Set, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

D_{K}^{(1)}(x)\coloneqq 1.

For every
k\in\{2,\dots,K\}
, define, for any
x,q_{1},a_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1},a_{k-1}\in\mathcal{X}
,

D_{K}^{(k)}(x,q_{1},a_{1},\dots,q_{k-1},a_{k-1})\coloneqq La_{k-1}.

Since
a_{k-1}\in[0,1]
and
L\leq 1
, the right-hand side belongs to
\mathcal{X}
.

The steps of the chain rule are
\varepsilon
-stable.
Indeed,
D_{K}^{(1)}
is
0
-stable.
Moreover, for every
k\in\{2,\dots,K\}
, the step
D_{K}^{(k)}
is
L
-stable, since it is
L
-Lipschitz in the coordinate
a_{k-1}
and does not depend on any other coordinate.
Since
L\leq\varepsilon
, all steps of
D_{K}
are
\varepsilon
-stable.

We now compute the oracle’s trajectory for the initial prompt
x=1
.
Since

\displaystyle Q^{(1)}_{D_{K},g}(1)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle A^{(1)}_{D_{K},g}(1)

\displaystyle=g(1)=0,

then, for every
k\in\{2,\dots,K\}
, we get

\displaystyle Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},g}(1)

\displaystyle=LA^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},g}(1)=0,

\displaystyle A^{(k)}_{D_{K},g}(1)

\displaystyle=g(0)=0.

In particular,

A^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(1)=0=g(1),

therefore
1
is
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable, and the support of
\nu
is contained in the

(D_{K},g)
-recoverable set.

We now compute the learner’s trajectory for the initial prompt
x=1
.
Since

\displaystyle Q^{(1)}_{D_{K},f}(1)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle A^{(1)}_{D_{K},f}(1)

\displaystyle=f(1)=L,

and since, for every
k\in\{2,\dots,K\}
,

Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(1)=LA^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},f}(1),\qquad A^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(1)=f\bigl(Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(1)\bigr)=LQ^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(1),

we have, by induction,

A^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(1)=L^{2k-1}\quad\text{for all }k\in[K].

In particular,

A^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(1)=L^{2K-1}>0.

Therefore, the reasoning risk is

R^{D_{K}}_{\nu,\ell}(f,g)=\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(A^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(X),\ g(X)\Bigr)\biggr]=\ell\bigl(L^{2K-1},\ g(1)\bigr)=\ell\bigl(L^{2K-1},0\bigr)=M,

where the last equality follows from
L^{2K-1}>0
.
The trajectory-mismatch risk is

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(X)\bigr),\ f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=\ell\bigl(A^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(1),\ A^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(1)\bigr)=\ell\bigl(L^{2K-1},0\bigr)=M,

and the oracle-trajectory risk is

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(X)\bigr),\ g\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=\ell\bigl(f(0),\ g(0)\bigr)=\ell\bigl(0,0\bigr)=0.

This proves, in particular, that the right-hand side of the decomposition is
M+0=M
, which equals the
reasoning risk, hence (
1
) holds with equality.
∎

If the stability of the steps of the chain rule
D_{K}
is removed, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 3

(No free lunch without stability of the chain rule)
.

For any integer
K\geq 2
, any
M>0
, and any
\varepsilon>0
,
there exist a metric space
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
, a quasimetric loss function

\ell\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,M]
that is
\varepsilon
-stable,
two
\varepsilon
-stable functions
f,g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
such that
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\rho\bigl(f(x),g(x)\bigr)\leq\varepsilon
, a
K
-step chain rule
D_{K}
, and a
distribution
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
whose support consists of
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable points such that (
1
) holds with equality and

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},f}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=M,\quad\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=0.

Proof.

Fix any integer
K\geq 2
, any
M>0
, and any
\varepsilon>0
.
Let
B\coloneqq\max\bigl\{1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}},\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon}},\varepsilon\bigr\}
and
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq[0,B]
.
For any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, let
\rho(x,y)\coloneqq\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert
and

\ell(x,y)\coloneqq\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\rho(x,y),\,M\right\}.

Since
\rho
is a metric, the function

(x,y)\mapsto\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\rho(x,y),\,M\right\}

is a quasimetric bounded by
M
.
Indeed, for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{X}
,

\ell(x,y)\leq\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\rho(x,z)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\rho(z,y),\,M\right\}\leq\ell(x,z)+\ell(z,y).

Moreover, for any
x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\bigl\lvert\ell(x,y)-\ell(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr\rvert\leq\bigl\lvert\ell(x,y)-\ell(x^{\prime},y)\bigr\rvert+\bigl\lvert\ell(x^{\prime},y)-\ell(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr\rvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\rho(x,x^{\prime})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\rho(y,y^{\prime}).

Thus,
\ell
is
\varepsilon
-stable.
Then, define
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, by

f(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\varepsilon(1-x),&\text{if }x\in[0,\ 1],\\
\varepsilon(x-1),&\text{if }x\in[1,\ 1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}],\\
\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon}},&\text{if }x\in[1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}},\ B],\end{cases}

and
g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, by

g(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }x\in[0,1],\\
\varepsilon(x-1),&\text{if }x\in[1,1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}],\\
\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon}},&\text{if }x\in[1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}},B].\end{cases}

By definition, both functions are
\varepsilon
-stable and

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\bigl\lvert f(x)-g(x)\bigr\rvert=\varepsilon
.
Let the testing distribution be the Dirac measure at
1
,

\nu\coloneqq\delta_{1}
.
We now define the chain rule.
Set, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

D_{K}^{(1)}(x)\coloneqq 0.

For the second step, define, for any
x,q_{1},a_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
,

D_{K}^{(2)}(x,q_{1},a_{1})\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,&a_{1}=0,\\
1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}},&a_{1}\neq 0.\end{cases}

For every
k\in\{3,\dots,K\}
, define, for any
x,q_{1},a_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1},a_{k-1}\in\mathcal{X}
,

D_{K}^{(k)}(x,q_{1},a_{1},\dots,q_{k-1},a_{k-1})\coloneqq q_{k-1}.

Thus, after the second step, the chain rule simply repeats the previous question.

We now compute the oracle’s trajectory for the initial prompt
x=1
.
Since

\displaystyle Q^{(1)}_{D_{K},g}(1)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle A^{(1)}_{D_{K},g}(1)

\displaystyle=g(0)=0,

and the discontinuous branch in
D_{K}^{(2)}
gives

\displaystyle Q^{(2)}_{D_{K},g}(1)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle A^{(2)}_{D_{K},g}(1)

\displaystyle=g(1)=0,

then, for all later steps
k\in\{3,\dots,K\}
, since the chain rule repeats the previous question, we get

\displaystyle Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},g}(1)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle A^{(k)}_{D_{K},g}(1)

\displaystyle=g(1)=0.

In particular, this yields

A^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(1)=0=g(1),

therefore
1
is
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable, and the support of
\nu
is contained in the

(D_{K},g)
-recoverable set.

We now compute the learner’s trajectory for the initial prompt
x=1
.
Since

\displaystyle Q^{(1)}_{D_{K},f}(1)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle A^{(1)}_{D_{K},f}(1)

\displaystyle=f(0)=\varepsilon\neq 0,

then, the second step of the chain rule follows the other branch:

\displaystyle Q^{(2)}_{D_{K},f}(1)

\displaystyle=1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}},

\displaystyle A^{(2)}_{D_{K},f}(1)

\displaystyle=f(1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})=\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon}},

and thus, for all later steps
k\in\{3,\dots,K\}
, since the chain rule repeats the previous question, we get

\displaystyle Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(1)

\displaystyle=1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}

\displaystyle A^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(1)

\displaystyle=f(1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})=\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon}}.

In particular, this yields

A^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(1)=\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon}}.

Therefore,
the reasoning risk is

R^{D_{K}}_{\nu,\ell}(f,g)=\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(A^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(X),\ g(X)\Bigr)\biggr]=\ell\bigl(\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon}},\ g(1)\bigr)=\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left\lvert\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon}}-0\right\rvert,M\right\}=M,

the trajectory-mismatch risk is

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(X)\bigr),\ f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=\ell\bigl(f(1+\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}),\ f(1)\bigr)=\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left\lvert\nicefrac{{2M}}{{\varepsilon}}-0\right\rvert,M\right\}=M,

and the oracle-trajectory risk is

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(X)\bigr),\ g\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=\ell\bigl(f(1),\ g(1)\bigr)=\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left\lvert 0-0\right\rvert,M\right\}=0.

This proves, in particular, that the right-hand side of the decomposition is
M+0=M
, which equals the
reasoning risk, hence (
1
) holds with equality.
∎

Proof for Theorem

Proving
Theorem

requires the following combinatorial lemma, which gives a representation of the amplification factor as the solution of a maximization problem.

Lemma 4

(Max representation of the amplification factor)
.

For any integer
K\geq 2
, any
\phi\geq 0
, and any
\delta\geq 0
, it holds that

\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta)=\max_{0\leq m\leq K-2}\delta^{K-2-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\phi\delta)^{j}.

(Here and below, every term with exponent
0
is interpreted as
1
, including
0^{0}
.)

Proof.

For every
m\in\{0,\ldots,K-2\}
, define

b_{m}\coloneqq\delta^{K-2-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\phi\delta)^{j}.

Let

B\coloneqq\max_{0\leq m\leq K-2}b_{m}.

We show that
B=\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta)
.

If
\delta=0
, then
\delta\leq 1
and
\phi\delta\neq 1
, so, by
Definition

2
,

\alpha_{K}(\phi,0)=\frac{1-0^{K-1}}{1-0}=1.

On the other hand, the maximum defining
B
is attained at
m=K-2
, and equals
1
.
Thus the claim holds when
\delta=0
.
Hence, in the rest of the proof, assume
\delta>0
.

For every
m\in\{0,\ldots,K-3\}
,

\displaystyle b_{m+1}-b_{m}

\displaystyle=\delta^{K-3-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(\phi\delta)^{j}-\delta^{K-2-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\phi\delta)^{j}

\displaystyle=\delta^{K-3-m}\Bigl((1-\delta)\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\phi\delta)^{j}+(\phi\delta)^{m+1}\Bigr).

First suppose that
\delta\leq 1
.
Then
1-\delta\geq 0
, and therefore
b_{m+1}-b_{m}\geq 0
for every
m
.
Thus the maximum is attained at
m=K-2
.
The same conclusion holds if
\phi\geq 1
.
Indeed, when
\delta\leq 1
this was already proved, while if
\delta>1
and
\phi\geq 1
, then
\phi\delta>1
, and

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\phi\delta)^{j}+(\phi\delta)^{m+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\frac{(\phi\delta)^{m+1}-1}{\phi\delta-1}+(\phi\delta)^{m+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta(\phi-1)(\phi\delta)^{m+1}+\delta-1}{\phi\delta-1}\geq 0.

Therefore, if
\phi\geq 1
or
\delta\leq 1
, then

B=b_{K-2}=\sum_{j=0}^{K-2}(\phi\delta)^{j}.

If
\phi\delta\neq 1
, this gives

B=\frac{1-(\phi\delta)^{K-1}}{1-\phi\delta}=\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta).

If
\phi\delta=1
, this gives

B=K-1=\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta).

It remains to consider the case
\phi<1
and
\delta>1
.
First suppose that
\phi=0
.
Then, for every
m\in\{0,\ldots,K-2\}
,

b_{m}=\delta^{K-2-m},

because
\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\phi\delta)^{j}=1
.
Since
\delta>1
, the maximum is attained at
m=0
, and therefore

B=\delta^{K-2}.

Since
m_{K}(0,\delta)=0
, this is exactly

B=\delta^{K-2-m_{K}(0,\delta)}\frac{1-(\phi\delta)^{m_{K}(0,\delta)+1}}{1-\phi\delta}=\alpha_{K}(0,\delta).

Now suppose that
\phi\in(0,1)
and
\delta>1
.
Set

r\coloneqq\phi\delta.

Assume first that
r\neq 1
.
Using the geometric-sum formula, for every
m\in\{0,\ldots,K-3\}
,

\displaystyle b_{m+1}-b_{m}

\displaystyle=\delta^{K-3-m}\Bigl((1-\delta)\sum_{j=0}^{m}r^{j}+r^{m+1}\Bigr).

If
r<1
, then

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\sum_{j=0}^{m}r^{j}+r^{m+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\frac{1-r^{m+1}}{1-r}+r^{m+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\delta+\delta(1-\phi)r^{m+1}}{1-r}.

Since
1-r>0
, we have
b_{m+1}\geq b_{m}
if and only if

r^{m+1}\geq\frac{\delta-1}{\delta(1-\phi)}.

If
r>1
, then

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\sum_{j=0}^{m}r^{j}+r^{m+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\frac{r^{m+1}-1}{r-1}+r^{m+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta-1-\delta(1-\phi)r^{m+1}}{r-1}.

Since
r-1>0
, we again get
b_{m+1}\geq b_{m}
if and only if

r^{m+1}\leq\frac{\delta-1}{\delta(1-\phi)}.

Moreover,

r<1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{\delta-1}{\delta(1-\phi)}<1,

and

r>1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{\delta-1}{\delta(1-\phi)}>1.

Thus, in both cases, the sequence
(b_{m})
is increasing up to

m_{K}(\phi,\delta)=\min\biggl(K-2,\biggl\lfloor\log_{\phi\delta}\Bigl(\frac{\delta-1}{\delta(1-\phi)}\Bigr)\biggr\rfloor\biggr)

and decreasing afterwards.
Hence the maximum is attained at
m_{K}(\phi,\delta)
.
Since
r=\phi\delta\neq 1
, the geometric-sum formula gives

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=b_{m_{K}(\phi,\delta)}

\displaystyle=\delta^{K-2-m_{K}(\phi,\delta)}\frac{1-(\phi\delta)^{m_{K}(\phi,\delta)+1}}{1-\phi\delta}=\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta).

Finally, suppose that
\phi<1
,
\delta>1
, and
\phi\delta=1
.
Then necessarily
\phi>0
, and

b_{m}=\delta^{K-2-m}(m+1).

Moreover,

\displaystyle b_{m+1}-b_{m}

\displaystyle=\delta^{K-3-m}(m+2)-\delta^{K-2-m}(m+1)

\displaystyle=\delta^{K-3-m}\Bigl(1-(\delta-1)(m+1)\Bigr).

Therefore
b_{m+1}\geq b_{m}
if and only if

m+1\leq\frac{1}{\delta-1}.

Hence the maximum is attained at

n_{K}(\phi,\delta)=\min\biggl(K-2,\ \biggl\lfloor\frac{1}{\delta-1}\biggr\rfloor\biggr),

and

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=b_{n_{K}(\phi,\delta)}

\displaystyle=\delta^{K-2-n_{K}(\phi,\delta)}\bigl(n_{K}(\phi,\delta)+1\bigr)=\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta).

This proves the claim.
∎

We can now prove
Theorem

2
.

.

, then
f=g
pointwise.
Hence the trajectories generated by

\ell

For every

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho\Bigl(Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(x),Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},g}(x)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\rho\Bigl(D_{K}^{(k)}\Bigl(x,Q^{(1)}_{D_{K},f}(x),A^{(1)}_{D_{K},f}(x),\ldots,Q^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},f}(x),A^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},f}(x)\Bigr),D_{K}^{(k)}\Bigl(x,Q^{(1)}_{D_{K},g}(x),A^{(1)}_{D_{K},g}(x),\ldots,Q^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},g}(x),A^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},g}(x)\Bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\delta_{2i-1}^{(k)}\rho\Bigl(Q^{(i)}_{D_{K},f}(x),Q^{(i)}_{D_{K},g}(x)\Bigr)+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\delta_{2i}^{(k)}\rho\Bigl(A^{(i)}_{D_{K},f}(x),A^{(i)}_{D_{K},g}(x)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\delta_{2i-1}^{(k)}\Delta_{i}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\delta_{2i}^{(k)}\Gamma_{i}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\delta_{2i-1}^{(k)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\delta_{2i}^{(k)}\Bigr)\max_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\max\{\Delta_{i}(x),\Gamma_{i}(x)\}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\max_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\max\{\Delta_{i}(x),\Gamma_{i}(x)\}.

Together with
\Gamma_{i}(x)\leq\phi\Delta_{i}(x)+d
, this gives

\Delta_{k}(x)\leq\delta\max_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\max\{\Delta_{i}(x),\phi\Delta_{i}(x)+d\}.

Define

e_{k}(x):=\frac{\Delta_{k}(x)}{d}.

Then

e_{1}(x)=0,

and, for every
k=2,\ldots,K
,

e_{k}(x)\leq\delta\max_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\max\{e_{i}(x),\phi e_{i}(x)+1\}.

Introduce the two increasing affine maps

T_{\mathsf{Q}}(z):=\delta z,\qquad T_{\mathsf{A}}(z):=\delta(\phi z+1).

For
L\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{W}_{L}
be the set of all words of length at most

L
over the alphabet
\{\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{A}\}
.
For a word
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})
, write

T_{w}:=T_{w_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ T_{w_{1}},

and set
T_{\varnothing}(0)=0
.
Define

C_{L}:=\max_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{L}}T_{w}(0).

We claim that

e_{k}(x)\leq C_{k-1}\qquad\text{for every }k=1,\ldots,K.

The claim is true for
k=1
, since
e_{1}(x)=0=C_{0}
.
Suppose the claim holds for all indices smaller than
k
.
Then, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
, the induction hypothesis gives

e_{i}(x)\leq C_{i-1}.

Since
i-1\leq k-2
and
C_{L}
is nondecreasing in
L
, we also have

C_{i-1}\leq C_{k-2}.

Therefore,

e_{i}(x)\leq C_{k-2}\qquad\text{for every }i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}.

Since
T_{\mathsf{Q}}
and
T_{\mathsf{A}}
are increasing, this implies

T_{\mathsf{Q}}(e_{i}(x))\leq T_{\mathsf{Q}}(C_{k-2}),\qquad T_{\mathsf{A}}(e_{i}(x))\leq T_{\mathsf{A}}(C_{k-2})

for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
.
Recalling that

T_{\mathsf{Q}}(z)=\delta z,\qquad T_{\mathsf{A}}(z)=\delta(\phi z+1),

we get

\displaystyle e_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\delta\max_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\max\{e_{i}(x),\phi e_{i}(x)+1\}

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\max\{T_{\mathsf{Q}}(e_{i}(x)),T_{\mathsf{A}}(e_{i}(x))\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{T_{\mathsf{Q}}(C_{k-2}),T_{\mathsf{A}}(C_{k-2})\}.

It remains to prove that

\max\{T_{\mathsf{Q}}(C_{k-2}),T_{\mathsf{A}}(C_{k-2})\}\leq C_{k-1}.

Indeed, since
\mathcal{W}_{k-2}
is finite, there exists a word

w^{\star}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})\in\mathcal{W}_{k-2}
such that

C_{k-2}=T_{w^{\star}}(0).

Since
w^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}_{k-2}
, its length satisfies
m\leq k-2
.
Therefore, the two words

w_{\mathsf{Q}}^{\star}\coloneqq(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m},\mathsf{Q}),\qquad w_{\mathsf{A}}^{\star}\coloneqq(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m},\mathsf{A})

have length at most
k-1
, and hence both belong to
\mathcal{W}_{k-1}
.
By the definition of
T_{w}
, we have

T_{w_{\mathsf{Q}}^{\star}}=T_{\mathsf{Q}}\circ T_{w^{\star}},\qquad T_{w_{\mathsf{A}}^{\star}}=T_{\mathsf{A}}\circ T_{w^{\star}}.

Evaluating at
0
gives

T_{w_{\mathsf{Q}}^{\star}}(0)=T_{\mathsf{Q}}\bigl(T_{w^{\star}}(0)\bigr)=T_{\mathsf{Q}}(C_{k-2}),

and similarly

T_{w_{\mathsf{A}}^{\star}}(0)=T_{\mathsf{A}}\bigl(T_{w^{\star}}(0)\bigr)=T_{\mathsf{A}}(C_{k-2}).

Since
C_{k-1}
is the maximum of
T_{w}(0)
over all
w\in\mathcal{W}_{k-1}
, and since

w_{\mathsf{Q}}^{\star},w_{\mathsf{A}}^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}_{k-1}
, we conclude that

T_{\mathsf{Q}}(C_{k-2})\leq C_{k-1},\qquad T_{\mathsf{A}}(C_{k-2})\leq C_{k-1}.

Therefore,

\max\{T_{\mathsf{Q}}(C_{k-2}),T_{\mathsf{A}}(C_{k-2})\}\leq C_{k-1}.

Hence

e_{k}(x)\leq C_{k-1}.

Thus, in particular,

\Delta_{K}(x)\leq d\,C_{K-1}.

It remains to compute
C_{K-1}
.
Let
L:=K-1
.
First suppose
\delta\geq 1
.
For every
z\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle(T_{\mathsf{Q}}\circ T_{\mathsf{A}})(z)-(T_{\mathsf{A}}\circ T_{\mathsf{Q}})(z)

\displaystyle=T_{\mathsf{Q}}\bigl(\delta(\phi z+1)\bigr)-T_{\mathsf{A}}(\delta z)

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}(\phi z+1)-\delta(\phi\delta z+1)

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}\phi z+\delta^{2}-\delta^{2}\phi z-\delta

\displaystyle=\delta(\delta-1)\geq 0.

Thus, applying
T_{\mathsf{A}}
first and then
T_{\mathsf{Q}}
gives a value at least as large as applying

T_{\mathsf{Q}}
first and then
T_{\mathsf{A}}
.

We now use this as an adjacent-swap argument.
Take any word
w
with a fixed number of
\mathsf{A}
’s and
\mathsf{Q}
’s.
If
w
contains an adjacent pair
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{A})
, write

w=(u,\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{A},v),

where
u
is the part of the word before this adjacent pair and
v
is the part after it.
Let

\widetilde{w}=(u,\mathsf{A},\mathsf{Q},v)

be the word obtained by swapping this adjacent pair.
By the definition of
T_{w}
, we have

T_{w}=T_{v}\circ T_{\mathsf{A}}\circ T_{\mathsf{Q}}\circ T_{u},\qquad T_{\widetilde{w}}=T_{v}\circ T_{\mathsf{Q}}\circ T_{\mathsf{A}}\circ T_{u}.

Set

z\coloneqq T_{u}(0).

Since
T_{\mathsf{Q}}
and
T_{\mathsf{A}}
map nonnegative numbers to nonnegative numbers, we have
z\geq 0
.
Therefore,

(T_{\mathsf{Q}}\circ T_{\mathsf{A}})(z)\geq(T_{\mathsf{A}}\circ T_{\mathsf{Q}})(z).

Moreover,
T_{v}
is increasing, being a composition of increasing maps.
Hence

T_{\widetilde{w}}(0)=T_{v}\Bigl((T_{\mathsf{Q}}\circ T_{\mathsf{A}})(z)\Bigr)\geq T_{v}\Bigl((T_{\mathsf{A}}\circ T_{\mathsf{Q}})(z)\Bigr)=T_{w}(0).

Thus, whenever a word contains an adjacent pair
(\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{A})
, swapping it to
(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{Q})
cannot
decrease its value at
0
.

Repeating this adjacent swap finitely many times moves all occurrences of
\mathsf{A}
before all
occurrences of
\mathsf{Q}
.
Consequently, among words with a fixed number of
\mathsf{A}
’s and
\mathsf{Q}
’s, the value at
0
is
maximized by a word of the form

(\mathsf{A},\ldots,\mathsf{A},\mathsf{Q},\ldots,\mathsf{Q}),

that is, by applying all
T_{\mathsf{A}}
’s first and all
T_{\mathsf{Q}}
’s afterwards.
Moreover, since
\delta\geq 1
, any shorter word can be extended by appending
\mathsf{Q}
’s without
decreasing its value.
Therefore

C_{L}=\max_{1\leq n\leq L}T_{\mathsf{Q}}^{L-n}T_{\mathsf{A}}^{n}(0).

We now compute
T_{\mathsf{A}}^{n}(0)
.
Since

T_{\mathsf{A}}(z)=\delta(\phi z+1)=\delta\phi z+\delta,

we claim that, for every
n\geq 1
,

T_{\mathsf{A}}^{n}(0)=\delta\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(\delta\phi)^{j}.

For
n=1
, this gives

T_{\mathsf{A}}(0)=\delta=\delta\sum_{j=0}^{0}(\delta\phi)^{j}.

Assume now that the identity holds for some
n\geq 1
.
Then

\displaystyle T_{\mathsf{A}}^{n+1}(0)

\displaystyle=T_{\mathsf{A}}\bigl(T_{\mathsf{A}}^{n}(0)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\delta\phi T_{\mathsf{A}}^{n}(0)+\delta

\displaystyle=\delta\phi\bigl(\delta\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(\delta\phi)^{j}\bigr)+\delta

\displaystyle=\delta\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\delta\phi)^{j}+\delta

\displaystyle=\delta\sum_{j=0}^{n}(\delta\phi)^{j}.

Thus, by induction,

T_{\mathsf{A}}^{n}(0)=\delta\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(\delta\phi)^{j}.

Consequently, we get

C_{L}=\max_{1\leq n\leq L}\delta^{L-n+1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(\delta\phi)^{j}.

Equivalently, with
m=n-1
,

C_{K-1}=\max_{0\leq m\leq K-2}\delta^{K-1-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\delta\phi)^{j}.

Now suppose
0\leq\delta<1
.
Then

(T_{\mathsf{Q}}\circ T_{\mathsf{A}})(z)-(T_{\mathsf{A}}\circ T_{\mathsf{Q}})(z)=\delta(\delta-1)\leq 0.

Thus, another adjacent-swap argument gives that moving all
\mathsf{Q}
’s before all
\mathsf{A}
’s can only increase the value at
0
.
Since
T_{\mathsf{Q}}(0)=0
, any initial block of
\mathsf{Q}
’s has no effect.
Therefore, for a word containing
n
occurrences of
\mathsf{A}
,

T_{w}(0)\leq T_{\mathsf{A}}^{n}(0)=\delta\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(\delta\phi)^{j}.

This quantity is nondecreasing in
n
, so the maximum over words of length
at most
L
is attained by
T_{\mathsf{A}}^{L}
.
Hence

C_{L}=T_{\mathsf{A}}^{L}(0)=\delta\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}(\delta\phi)^{j}.

This is precisely the value of

\max_{0\leq m\leq K-2}\delta^{K-1-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\delta\phi)^{j}

at
m=K-2
.
Therefore, in all cases,

C_{K-1}=\max_{0\leq m\leq K-2}\delta^{K-1-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\delta\phi)^{j}.

By
Lemma

4
,

C_{K-1}=\delta\,\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta).

Consequently, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\Delta_{K}(x)\leq\delta\,d(f,g)\,\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta).

We now bound the trajectory-mismatch risk.
Because
\ell
is
\lambda
-stable and is zero on the diagonal, there exist

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\geq 0
such that
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda
and

\left\lvert\ell(u,v)-\ell(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right\rvert\leq\lambda_{1}\rho(u,u^{\prime})+\lambda_{2}\rho(v,v^{\prime})

for all
u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Thus, for all
u,v\in\mathcal{X}
,

\ell(u,v)=\left\lvert\ell(u,v)-\ell(v,v)\right\rvert\leq\lambda_{1}\rho(u,v),

and also

\ell(u,v)=\left\lvert\ell(u,v)-\ell(u,u)\right\rvert\leq\lambda_{2}\rho(u,v).

Hence

\ell(u,v)\leq\min\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}\rho(u,v)\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\rho(u,v).

Therefore, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},f}^{(K)}(x)\bigr),f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(x)\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\rho\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},f}^{(K)}(x)\bigr),f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(x)\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda\phi}{2}\rho\Bigl(Q_{D_{K},f}^{(K)}(x),Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(x)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\phi}{2}\Delta_{K}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda\phi\delta}{2}d(f,g)\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta).

Taking expectation with respect to
X\sim\nu
yields

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},f}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]\leq\frac{\lambda\phi\delta}{2}\,\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta)\,d(f,g).

This proves the theorem.
∎

A.3
Proof for Theorem
3

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let

m_{\star}\in\argmax_{0\leq m\leq K-2}\delta^{K-2-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\phi\delta)^{j},

where powers with exponent
0
(including
0^{0}
) are interpreted as equal to
1
.
Consider the word
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{K-1})\in\{\mathsf{A},\mathsf{Q}\}^{K-1}
defined by

w_{r}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathsf{A},&\text{if }r\leq m_{\star}+1,\\
\mathsf{Q},&\text{if }r>m_{\star}+1.\end{cases}

Let

T_{\mathsf{Q}}(z)\coloneqq\delta z,\qquad T_{\mathsf{A}}(z)\coloneqq\delta(\phi z+1),

and define the sequence
(E_{r})_{r=0}^{K-1}
by

E_{0}\coloneqq 0,\qquad E_{r}\coloneqq T_{w_{r}}(E_{r-1})\quad\text{for every }r\in[K-1].

By construction,

\displaystyle E_{K-1}

\displaystyle=\delta^{K-1-m_{\star}}\sum_{j=0}^{m_{\star}}(\phi\delta)^{j}

\displaystyle=\delta\max_{0\leq m\leq K-2}\delta^{K-2-m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(\phi\delta)^{j}

\displaystyle=\delta\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta),

where the last equality follows from
Lemma

4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the usual metric

\rho(x,y)\coloneqq\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert.

Choose distinct real numbers
s_{1},\ldots,s_{K}
.
Define

f(z)\coloneqq\phi z\qquad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{R}.

Define
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

g(s_{i})\coloneqq f(s_{i})-1\quad\text{for every }i\in\{1,\ldots,K-1\},

g(s_{K})\coloneqq f(s_{K}),

and

g(z)\coloneqq f(z)\quad\text{for every }z\notin\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{K}\}.

Then

d(f,g)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\rho\bigl(f(z),g(z)\bigr)=1.

Moreover,
f
is
\phi
-stable.

Define the loss function
\ell\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
by

\ell(u,v)\coloneqq\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lvert u-v\right\rvert.

Since
\ell
is a positive multiple of the metric
\rho
, it is a quasimetric.
Moreover, for any
u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\ell(u,v)-\ell(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\ell(u,v)-\ell(u^{\prime},v)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\ell(u^{\prime},v)-\ell(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\rho(u,u^{\prime})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\rho(v,v^{\prime}).

Thus
\ell
is
\lambda
-stable, with coordinate-wise Lipschitz constants

\lambda/2
and
\lambda/2
.

Let the testing distribution be the Dirac measure at
s_{K}
,

\nu\coloneqq\delta_{s_{K}}.

We now define the chain rule.
Set

D_{K}^{(1)}(x)\coloneqq s_{1}\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

For every
k\in\{2,\ldots,K\}
, write
r=k-1
.
If
w_{r}=\mathsf{Q}
, define

D_{K}^{(k)}(x,q_{1},a_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1},a_{k-1})\coloneqq s_{k}-\delta s_{k-1}+\delta q_{k-1}.

If
w_{r}=\mathsf{A}
, define

D_{K}^{(k)}(x,q_{1},a_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1},a_{k-1})\coloneqq s_{k}-\delta g(s_{k-1})+\delta a_{k-1}.

Each step of
D_{K}
is
\delta
-stable.
Indeed,
D_{K}^{(1)}
is constant.
For
k\geq 2
, the map
D_{K}^{(k)}
depends on exactly one previous coordinate, either
q_{k-1}
or
a_{k-1}
, with Lipschitz constant
\delta
, and does not depend on any other coordinate.

We now compute the oracle trajectory.
Since
D_{K}^{(1)}(s_{K})=s_{1}
, we have

Q^{(1)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})=s_{1}.

We claim that, for every
k\in[K]
,

Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})=s_{k}.

This is true for
k=1
.
Suppose it holds up to step
k-1
.
If
w_{k-1}=\mathsf{Q}
, then

Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})=s_{k}-\delta s_{k-1}+\delta Q^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})=s_{k}.

If
w_{k-1}=\mathsf{A}
, then

Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})=s_{k}-\delta g(s_{k-1})+\delta A^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})=s_{k},

because

A^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})=g\bigl(Q^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})\bigr)=g(s_{k-1}).

Thus the claim holds for all
k
.
In particular,

Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})=s_{K}.

Since

A^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})=g\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})\bigr)=g(s_{K}),

we have

A^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})=g(s_{K}).

Therefore
s_{K}
is
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable, and
\nu
is supported on the
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable set.

We now compute the learner trajectory.
We claim that, for every
k\in[K]
,

Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(s_{K})=s_{k}+E_{k-1}.

For
k=1
, this follows from
E_{0}=0
and

Q^{(1)}_{D_{K},f}(s_{K})=s_{1}.

Suppose the claim holds up to step
k-1
.
If
w_{k-1}=\mathsf{Q}
, then

\displaystyle Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(s_{K})

\displaystyle=s_{k}-\delta s_{k-1}+\delta Q^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},f}(s_{K})

\displaystyle=s_{k}-\delta s_{k-1}+\delta\bigl(s_{k-1}+E_{k-2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=s_{k}+\delta E_{k-2}=s_{k}+E_{k-1}.

If
w_{k-1}=\mathsf{A}
, then, since
k-1\leq K-1
, we have

g(s_{k-1})=f(s_{k-1})-1.

Therefore

\displaystyle Q^{(k)}_{D_{K},f}(s_{K})

\displaystyle=s_{k}-\delta g(s_{k-1})+\delta A^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},f}(s_{K})

\displaystyle=s_{k}-\delta g(s_{k-1})+\delta f\bigl(Q^{(k-1)}_{D_{K},f}(s_{K})\bigr)

\displaystyle=s_{k}-\delta\bigl(f(s_{k-1})-1\bigr)+\delta f\bigl(s_{k-1}+E_{k-2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=s_{k}-\delta\bigl(\phi s_{k-1}-1\bigr)+\delta\phi\bigl(s_{k-1}+E_{k-2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=s_{k}+\delta\bigl(\phi E_{k-2}+1\bigr)=s_{k}+E_{k-1}.

Thus the learner-trajectory claim holds for all
k
.
In particular,

Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(s_{K})=s_{K}+E_{K-1}=s_{K}+\delta\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta).

We can now evaluate the two terms.
First, the oracle-trajectory risk is

\displaystyle R_{Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}\#\nu,\ell}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})\bigr),g\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}(s_{K})\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\ell\bigl(f(s_{K}),g(s_{K})\bigr)=\ell\bigl(f(s_{K}),f(s_{K})\bigr)=0.

Second, the reasoning risk is

\displaystyle R^{D_{K}}_{\nu,\ell}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\ell\Bigl(A^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(s_{K}),g(s_{K})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},f}(s_{K})\bigr),g(s_{K})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\ell\bigl(f\bigl(s_{K}+\delta\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta)\bigr),f(s_{K})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2}\rho\bigl(f\bigl(s_{K}+\delta\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta)\bigr),f(s_{K})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\phi\delta}{2}\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta).

Since
d(f,g)=1
and the oracle-trajectory risk is zero, this is exactly

\frac{\lambda\phi\delta}{2}d(f,g)\alpha_{K}(\phi,\delta)+R_{Q^{(K)}_{D_{K},g}\#\nu,\ell}(f,g).

Thus the upper bound of
Corollary

1
is attained.
∎

B.1
Details for Example
1

We explicitly specify the four functions in

D_{4}=\bigl(D_{4}^{(1)},D_{4}^{(2)},D_{4}^{(3)},D_{4}^{(4)}\bigr)

for the illustrative case of multiplying a one-digit integer by a two-digit integer.
Throughout this example, we slightly abuse notation by identifying a nonnegative integer with its decimal-string representation, which is an element of
\mathcal{X}
.
Let the input be the two-factor multiplication expression

x=d_{1}\cdot n,\qquad n=10d_{2}+d_{3},

where
d_{1},d_{3}\in\left\{0,\dots,9\right\}
and
d_{2}\in\left\{1,\dots,9\right\}
.
We define the four steps of the chain rule on such inputs as follows:

1.

The first step extracts the tens digit of the two-digit factor and produces the elementary multiplication

D_{4}^{(1)}(x)\coloneqq d_{1}\cdot d_{2}.

2.

The second step multiplies the first partial product by
10
.
For generic coordinates
(x,q_{1},a_{1})\in\mathcal{X}^{3}
, define

D_{4}^{(2)}(x,q_{1},a_{1})\coloneqq 10\cdot a_{1},

whenever
x=d_{1}\cdot(10d_{2}+d_{3})
is of the above form and
a_{1}
represents a nonnegative integer.

3.

The third step extracts the units digit of the two-digit factor and produces the elementary multiplication

D_{4}^{(3)}(x,q_{1},a_{1},q_{2},a_{2})\coloneqq d_{1}\cdot d_{3},

whenever
x=d_{1}\cdot(10d_{2}+d_{3})
is of the above form.

4.

The fourth step adds the two partial products.
For generic coordinates
(x,q_{1},a_{1},q_{2},a_{2},q_{3},a_{3})\in\mathcal{X}^{7}
, define

D_{4}^{(4)}(x,q_{1},a_{1},q_{2},a_{2},q_{3},a_{3})\coloneqq a_{2}+a_{3},

whenever
x=d_{1}\cdot(10d_{2}+d_{3})
is of the above form and
a_{2},a_{3}
represent nonnegative integers.

On all remaining inputs, including cases where one of the coordinates required above does not represent a nonnegative integer, define the corresponding step arbitrarily, for example by returning the fixed element
0\in\mathcal{X}
.
This makes each
D_{4}^{(k)}
a well-defined function from
\mathcal{X}^{2k-1}
to
\mathcal{X}
.
The precise behavior of the chain rule outside this illustrative family is irrelevant for the example.

Recoverability of the illustrative family.

For this illustrative chain rule, every expression of the form

d_{1}\cdot(10d_{2}+d_{3})
with
d_{1},d_{3}\in\left\{0,\dots,9\right\}
and
d_{2}\in\left\{1,\dots,9\right\}
is
(D_{4},g)
-recoverable.
Indeed, along the oracle trajectory, we have

Q^{(1)}_{D_{4},g}(x)=d_{1}\cdot d_{2},\qquad A^{(1)}_{D_{4},g}(x)=g(d_{1}\cdot d_{2})=d_{1}d_{2}.

The second step then gives

Q^{(2)}_{D_{4},g}(x)=10\cdot A^{(1)}_{D_{4},g}(x)=10\cdot d_{1}d_{2},\qquad A^{(2)}_{D_{4},g}(x)=g(10\cdot d_{1}d_{2})=10d_{1}d_{2}.

The third step gives

Q^{(3)}_{D_{4},g}(x)=d_{1}\cdot d_{3},\qquad A^{(3)}_{D_{4},g}(x)=g(d_{1}\cdot d_{3})=d_{1}d_{3}.

Finally,

Q^{(4)}_{D_{4},g}(x)=A^{(2)}_{D_{4},g}(x)+A^{(3)}_{D_{4},g}(x)=10d_{1}d_{2}+d_{1}d_{3},

and therefore

A^{(4)}_{D_{4},g}(x)=g\bigl(10d_{1}d_{2}+d_{1}d_{3}\bigr)=d_{1}(10d_{2}+d_{3})=g(x).

Thus
x
is
(D_{4},g)
-recoverable.

This example uses
g
as the ideal ground-truth evaluator only to specify correctness.
It does not mean that a learned hypothesis must be trained on all multiplication expressions.
For instance, the source distribution may be supported only on elementary expressions such as one-digit-by-one-digit multiplications, multiplications by
10
, and additions, while the test distribution may be supported on one-digit-by-two-digit multiplication expressions.
The chain rule then illustrates how CoT can transform a test query into elementary subquestions on which the hypothesis may already be accurate.

B.2
Recoverability and the support assumption

In Section
2
, we assumed that the distribution
\nu
is supported on the
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable set, so that we can decompose the reasoning risk into the two terms in Inequality (
1
). In the absence of this structural assumption, the reasoning risk
R_{\nu,\ell}^{D_{K}}(f,g)
is bounded by the sum of three terms, rather than by the two terms appearing in (
1
):

\displaystyle R_{\nu,\ell}^{D_{K}}(f,g)\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},f}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]}_{\text{trajectory-mismatch risk (TMR)}}+\underbrace{\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]}_{\text{oracle-trajectory risk (OTR)}}+

\displaystyle\underbrace{\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,g\bigl(X\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]}_{\text{oracle-mismatch risk (OMR)}}

In this decomposition, an additional term emerges—the oracle-mismatch risk (OMR)—which quantifies the extent of misalignment between the chain rule
D_{K}
and the oracle
g
. Notably, this term is unbounded in general and can be arbitrarily large, as established in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

For any integer
K\geq 2
and any
M>0
, there exist a metric space
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
, a loss function
\ell\colon\mathcal{X}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
, two functions
f,g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
, a
K
-step chain rule
D_{K}
, and a distribution
\nu
whose support is not contained in the
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable set, such that both TMR and OTR achieve
0
, while the OMR is exactly
M
,

\displaystyle\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},f}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),\,g\bigl(X\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]

\displaystyle=M

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
.
For any
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
, let the metric be
\rho(a,b)\coloneqq|a-b|
and the loss be
\ell(a,b)\coloneqq|a-b|
.
Define the answer maps
f,g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
by

f(x)\coloneqq x,\qquad g(x)\coloneqq|x|\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathcal{X}.

Let
\nu
be the uniform distribution on
[0,L]
, for some
L>0
.

For the chain rule
D_{K}=\bigl(D_{K}^{(1)},\ldots,D_{K}^{(K)}\bigr)
, define

D_{K}^{(1)}(x)\coloneqq x+M,

and, for every
k\in\{2,\ldots,K\}
,

D_{K}^{(k)}\bigl(x,x^{(1)},y^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(k-1)},y^{(k-1)}\bigr)\coloneqq y^{(k-1)}.

We first compute the trajectories pointwise.
Fix any
x\in[0,L]
.
Since
x\geq 0
and
x+M>0
, we have

Q_{D_{K},f}^{(1)}(x)=Q_{D_{K},g}^{(1)}(x)=x+M,

and therefore

A_{D_{K},f}^{(1)}(x)=f(x+M)=x+M=g(x+M)=A_{D_{K},g}^{(1)}(x).

By induction, for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,

Q_{D_{K},f}^{(k)}(x)=Q_{D_{K},g}^{(k)}(x)=x+M,\qquad A_{D_{K},f}^{(k)}(x)=A_{D_{K},g}^{(k)}(x)=x+M.

Indeed, once the first answer is
x+M
, every subsequent step of the chain rule returns the previous answer, and both
f
and
g
act as the identity on the nonnegative point
x+M
.

It follows that, for every
x\in[0,L]
,

f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},f}^{(K)}(x)\bigr)=f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(x)\bigr)=x+M,

and hence the TMR integrand is zero.
Similarly, since
Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(x)=x+M\geq 0
,

f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(x)\bigr)=x+M=g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(x)\bigr),

and hence the OTR integrand is zero.

On the other hand, for every
x\in[0,L]
,

g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(x)\bigr)=g(x+M)=x+M,\qquad g(x)=x,

and therefore

\ell\Bigl(g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(x)\bigr),g(x)\Bigr)=\ell(x+M,x)=M.

Thus, if
X\sim\nu
, then

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},f}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=0,

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(f\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr)\Bigr)\biggr]=0,

and

\underset{X\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\Bigl(g\bigl(Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(X)\bigr),g(X)\Bigr)\biggr]=M.

Finally, the support of
\nu
is not contained in the
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable set.
Indeed, for every
x\in[0,L]
,

A_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}(x)=x+M\neq x=g(x),

so no point in
[0,L]
is
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable.
∎

Therefore, to control the oracle-mismatch risk (OMR) and prevent it from becoming arbitrarily large, it is natural to impose structural assumptions on the distribution
\nu
. A natural choice is to assume that
\nu
is supported on the
(D_{K},g)
-recoverable set, under which the otherwise uncontrollable OMR term vanishes.

Appendix C
Additional material for Section
3

As discussed in Section
3
, the oracle-trajectory risk (OTR) in Inequality (
1
) can be controlled using tools from domain adaptation.
We spell out one standard way to obtain such a bound.
The goal is to relate the target-domain risk under the oracle-trajectory distribution
Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu
to the empirical risk under the source distribution
\mu
, plus a discrepancy term and standard complexity terms.

We first recall the notion of empirical Rademacher complexity.
Let
\mathcal{G}
be a class of real-valued functions on a set
\mathcal{S}
.
For a sample
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{m})\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
, define

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{G})\coloneqq 2\underset{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}\sim\mathrm{Uni}(\left\{-1,1\right\}^{m})}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\gamma(S_{i})\right|\biggr].

If
\mathcal{D}
is a distribution on
\mathcal{S}
, the Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{G}
at sample size
m
is

\mathfrak{R}_{m}(\mathcal{G})\coloneqq\underset{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{G})\biggr].

From this point onward, assume that hypotheses
f
are drawn from a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\mathcal{X}}
.
To quantify the discrepancy between the source and target distributions, we use the
d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}
-divergence
[
61
]
, a loss-dependent notion of distributional distance that generalizes the classical
\mathcal{H}
-divergence beyond the
0
–
1
loss setting
[
13
,
14
]
.

Definition 3

(
d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}
-divergence)
.

Given a loss function
\ell\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)
and two distributions
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
, define

d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\sup_{h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\underset{x\sim\mu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\bigl(h(x),h^{\prime}(x)\bigr)\biggr]-\underset{x\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\bigl(h(x),h^{\prime}(x)\bigr)\biggr]\right|.

The quantity
d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}
is symmetric and satisfies the triangle inequality.
Its empirical counterpart is defined analogously.
Given independent samples
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{m})\sim\mu^{m}
and
T=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{m})\sim\nu^{m}
, let
\hat{\mu}_{S}
and
\hat{\nu}_{T}
denote the corresponding empirical distributions, and set

\hat{d}^{\ell,m}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}\bigl(\hat{\mu}_{S},\hat{\nu}_{T}\bigr).

Equivalently,

\hat{d}^{\ell,m}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell\bigl(h(S_{i}),h^{\prime}(S_{i})\bigr)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell\bigl(h(T_{i}),h^{\prime}(T_{i})\bigr)\right|.

We shall use the following standard Rademacher bounds.
For a distribution
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
and a sample
S=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})\sim\mu^{m}
, write

\hat{R}_{S,\ell}(h,g)\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell\bigl(h(X_{i}),g(X_{i})\bigr).

Lemma 5
.

Let
\ell\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,M]
be a bounded loss function, and define

\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{H}}\coloneqq\left\{(x,y)\mapsto\ell\bigl(h(x),y\bigr)\colon h\in\mathcal{H}\right\}.

Let
S=((X_{1},g(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{m},g(X_{m})))
, where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
are drawn i.i.d. from
\mu
.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, with probability at least
1-\epsilon
over
S
, the following inequality holds for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
:

R_{\mu,\ell}(h,g)\leq\hat{R}_{S,\ell}(h,g)+\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{H}})+3M\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{2}{\epsilon}}{2m}}.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\ell\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,M]
be a bounded loss function, and define

L_{\mathcal{H}}\coloneqq\left\{x\mapsto\ell\bigl(h(x),h^{\prime}(x)\bigr)\colon h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}\right\}.

Let
\eta
be a distribution on
\mathcal{X}
, let
S=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})\sim\eta^{m}
, and let
\hat{\eta}_{S}
be the empirical distribution induced by
S
.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, with probability at least
1-\epsilon
over
S
,

d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(\eta,\hat{\eta}_{S})\leq\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(L_{\mathcal{H}})+3M\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{2}{\epsilon}}{2m}}.

Combining Lemma
6
with the symmetry and triangle inequality of
d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}
gives the following empirical-discrepancy bound.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\ell\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,M]
be a bounded loss function.
Let
\mu
and
\nu
be distributions on
\mathcal{X}
, and let
S\sim\mu^{m}
and
T\sim\nu^{m}
be independent samples.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, with probability at least
1-\epsilon
over
S
and
T
,

d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\nu)\leq\hat{d}^{\ell,m}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\nu)+\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(L_{\mathcal{H}})+\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{T}(L_{\mathcal{H}})+6M\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{4}{\epsilon}}{2m}}.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality and symmetry of
d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}
,

d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\nu)\leq d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\hat{\mu}_{S})+d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(\hat{\mu}_{S},\hat{\nu}_{T})+d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(\hat{\nu}_{T},\nu).

The middle term is
\hat{d}^{\ell,m}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\nu)
.
Applying Lemma
6
to the first and third terms with failure probability
\epsilon/2
each gives the claim.
∎

We now derive a Rademacher-based bound for the OTR.
Let

\tau\coloneqq Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu

denote the oracle-trajectory target distribution.
For a quasimetric loss, define the approximation term

\beta_{\mu,\tau}\coloneqq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\Bigl(R_{\mu,\ell}(g,h)+R_{\tau,\ell}(h,g)\Bigr).

If
\ell
is symmetric, this reduces to the more familiar expression

\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\Bigl(R_{\mu,\ell}(h,g)+R_{\tau,\ell}(h,g)\Bigr)
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
K\geq 1
, and let
\ell\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,M]
be a bounded quasimetric loss.
Let
\tau\coloneqq Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu
.
Let
S=((X_{1},g(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{m},g(X_{m})))
with
X_{i}\sim\mu
i.i.d., let
U=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{m})\sim\mu^{m}
, and let
T=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{m})\sim\tau^{m}
, with all samples independent.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, with probability at least
1-\epsilon
over
S,U,T
, the following bound holds for every
f\in\mathcal{H}
:

R_{\tau,\ell}(f,g)\leq\hat{R}_{S,\ell}(f,g)+\hat{d}^{\ell,m}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\tau)+\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{H}})+\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{U}(L_{\mathcal{H}})+\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{T}(L_{\mathcal{H}})+9M\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{6}{\epsilon}}{2m}}+\beta_{\mu,\tau}.

Equivalently,

R_{Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu,\ell}(f,g)\leq\hat{R}_{S,\ell}(f,g)+\hat{d}^{\ell,m}_{\mathcal{H}}\bigl(\mu,Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu\bigr)+\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{H}})+\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{U}(L_{\mathcal{H}})+\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{T}(L_{\mathcal{H}})+9M\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{6}{\epsilon}}{2m}}+\beta_{\mu,Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu}.

Proof.

Fix any
h\in\mathcal{H}
.
Using the triangle inequality for
\ell
, we have

R_{\tau,\ell}(f,g)\leq\underset{x\sim\tau}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\bigl(f(x),h(x)\bigr)\biggr]+R_{\tau,\ell}(h,g).

By the definition of
d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}
,

\underset{x\sim\tau}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\bigl(f(x),h(x)\bigr)\biggr]\leq\underset{x\sim\mu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\bigl(f(x),h(x)\bigr)\biggr]+d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\tau).

Applying the triangle inequality again gives

\underset{x\sim\mu}{\mathbb{E}}\biggl[\ell\bigl(f(x),h(x)\bigr)\biggr]\leq R_{\mu,\ell}(f,g)+R_{\mu,\ell}(g,h).

Combining the previous three displays and then taking the infimum over
h\in\mathcal{H}
yields

R_{\tau,\ell}(f,g)\leq R_{\mu,\ell}(f,g)+d^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\tau)+\beta_{\mu,\tau}.

It remains to replace the population source risk and the population discrepancy by empirical quantities.
Apply Lemma
5
to
S
, and apply Lemma
6
separately to
U\sim\mu^{m}
and
T\sim\tau^{m}
, each with failure probability
\epsilon/3
.
A union bound gives probability at least
1-\epsilon
, and the three deviation terms sum to

9M\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{6}{\epsilon}}{2m}}.

This proves the claim.
∎

This bound relates the OTR to the empirical source risk, a discrepancy between the source distribution
\mu
and the oracle-trajectory target distribution
Q_{D_{K},g}^{(K)}\#\nu
, and standard Rademacher complexity terms.
Thus, it makes explicit how statistical complexity and distribution shift jointly contribute to the benefit term in the CoT risk decomposition.
