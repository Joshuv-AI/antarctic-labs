Nonlocal current-response theory of structured-light dichroism
Akihito Kato, Nobuhiko Yokoshi

Abstract
We develop a microscopic theory of optical absorption and structured-light dichroism in a nonlocal minimal-coupling framework. Starting from the minimal-coupling Hamiltonian, we express absorption as a bilinear functional of the electromagnetic vector potential and the nonlocal current response, providing a general description of light–matter coupling in optical vortex beams and other inhomogeneous fields. Dichroic signals are identified as helicity-odd projections of the nonlocal response kernel, and the response is resolved into symmetry, tensorial, and mode-space sectors. For single helical modes, the theory yields diagonal OAM-resolved contributions together with the corresponding selection rules and symmetry constraints. For mixed modes, interference between distinct OAM components provides access to off-diagonal coherence of the nonlocal kernel, with a tensor structure determined by the polarization composition of the field. The theory also clarifies how local structures associated with symmetric spatial dispersion, optical chirality, and tensorial anisotropy emerge as gradient-level manifestations of the underlying nonlocal response.



I
Introduction

Light carrying angular momentum provides a versatile probe of internal degrees of freedom in matter. Besides spin angular momentum (SAM) associated with circular polarization, structured optical fields can also carry orbital angular momentum (OAM) through their helical phase profile
Allen1992PRA
. Optical vortex beams thus enable the excitation and detection of electronic, excitonic, and collective modes with spatial and angular structure beyond the plane-wave picture. A central question in this setting is dichroism with respect to optical handedness. In the conventional setting, one usually discusses this in terms of reversing the circular polarization of the field in standard circular dichroism spectroscopy
RodgerNorden1997
;
Berova2000Book
;
Barron2004Book
. For structured light, however, the additional helical degree of freedom makes it possible to consider not only spin reversal but also orbital reversal and simultaneous spin–orbital reversal. We refer to these three reversal channels below as circular dichroism (CD), helical dichroism (HD), and helical circular dichroism (HCD), respectively. Relevant studies include OAM-dependent dichroic response in chiral media
Loffler2011PRA
, the use of twisted light to resolve molecular enantiomers
Brullot2016SciAdv
, and related developments in optical activity
ForbesAndrews2021JPhysPhoton
;
ForbesJones2021PRA
;
Hashiyada2026Optica
. Structured-light dichroism has also been explored in chiral nanostructures, molecular systems, metasurfaces, and nonlinear regimes
Ni2021ACSNano
;
Begin2023NatPhoton
;
Jain2023JCP
;
ForbesGreen2024LPR
;
Jain2024ACSNano
;
Cheeseman2025AdvOptMater
. In practice, structured beams are not always pure OAM eigenmodes: Gaussian admixtures arise, and nonparaxial vector-field effects redistribute angular momentum between polarization and spatial channels
Marrucci2006PRL
;
Zhao2007PRL
;
Bliokh2010PRA
;
Bliokh2011OE
.

At the microscopic level, a natural starting point is the minimal-coupling Hamiltonian, in which the vector potential couples directly to the electronic current density. This formulation is well suited to structured-light spectroscopy because it keeps the spatial field profile explicit and expresses absorption through the overlap of the optical vector potential with the transition current density. It also provides a natural route beyond local multipolar descriptions, which become inadequate for strongly inhomogeneous fields and spatially resolved light–matter coupling
List2015JCP
;
Foglia2022JCP
;
Bonafe2025PRB
. In this context, spatial dispersion and current-based response functions provide a natural framework for structured and multipolar light–matter coupling
Mukamel1995Book
;
Chernyak2015JCP
;
Lazzeretti2019JCP
. Retaining the full field profile enables a unified treatment of single helical modes and superpositions of different OAM channels. It allows us to resolve mode-selective absorption and interference between distinct OAM sectors of the nonlocal response.

In this paper, we develop a microscopic theory of structured-light dichroism in a nonlocal minimal-coupling framework. Building on earlier minimal-coupling analyses of molecular HD
YeEtAl2019JCTC
;
JiangEtAl2023ChemSci
, we formulate optical absorption as a bilinear functional of the incident vector potential and the nonlocal current response while retaining the full mode profile throughout. Within this approach, we identify dichroic signals as helicity-odd projections of the microscopic response kernel. This places CD, HD, and HCD on the same footing and resolves the response into symmetry, tensorial, and mode-space sectors. We show that single helical modes probe diagonal OAM-resolved contributions, whereas mixed modes probe off-diagonal coherence between different OAM channels. The theory thus provides a unified microscopic description of structured-light dichroism across a broad range of systems.

We use the terms CD, HD, and HCD for the response differences obtained by reversing

\ell

the topological-charge (orbital-angular-momentum) index of the field. For any helicity-resolved quantity
\mathcal{O}^{(\sigma,\ell)}

II.1
Linear structured-light absorption

We start from the minimal-coupling interaction

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=-\int d\bm{r}\,\hat{\bm{j}}(\bm{r})\cdot\hat{\bm{A}}(\bm{r},t),

(1)

which keeps the spatial profile of the optical mode explicit and is natural for structured and strongly inhomogeneous fields
List2015JCP
;
Foglia2022JCP
;
Bonafe2025PRB
. Here,
\hat{\bm{j}}(\bm{r})
denotes the electronic current-density operator, and
\hat{\bm{A}}(\bm{r},t)
the electromagnetic vector-potential operator. For a monochromatic field, the absorption spectrum can be written in the standard nonlocal bilinear form

\displaystyle S(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{r}\,d\bm{r}^{\prime}\,\sum_{ab}A_{a}^{*}(\bm{r},\omega)\,\mathcal{J}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)\,A_{b}(\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega),

(2)

with

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)&\equiv-\frac{\omega}{2}\,\mathrm{Im}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\omega t}\\
&\qquad\times\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\big\langle[\hat{j}_{a}(\bm{r},t),\hat{j}_{b}(\bm{r}^{\prime},0)]\big\rangle_{0}\right].\end{split}

(3)

Equation (
3
) defines the effective two-point absorption kernel in the vector-potential representation in terms of the nonlocal linear current-response function. It shows that structured-light absorption is governed by the projection of a two-point microscopic response onto the optical bilinear
A_{a}^{*}(\bm{r},\omega)A_{b}(\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)
. Throughout this work, we do not fix a specific gauge. For the resonant absorption processes considered here, the current–vector-potential term
\hat{\bm{j}}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{A}}
provides the dominant light–matter coupling, whereas charge-density contributions are mainly relevant to off-resonant signals
YeEtAl2019JCTC
;
JiangEtAl2023ChemSci
.

For a single helical mode
(\sigma,\ell)
, we write

\bm{A}^{(\sigma,\ell)}(\bm{r},\omega)=f^{(\ell)}(\rho,z;\omega)\,e^{i\ell\phi}\,\bm{e}_{\sigma},

(4)

where
\bm{e}_{\sigma}\equiv(\bm{e}_{x}+i\sigma\bm{e}_{y})/\sqrt{2}
denotes the circular-polarization basis vector. In an azimuthal-harmonic expansion of the response kernel, absorption selects a definite OAM channel of the nonlocal response.

II.2
Extension to nonlinear structured-light response

The same nonlocal viewpoint extends naturally beyond linear absorption. In general, the induced current may be expanded as a functional series in the structured field
Mukamel1995Book
;
Chernyak2015JCP
:

\langle\hat{j}_{a}(1)\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle\hat{j}_{a}(1)\rangle^{(n)}
,
with

\displaystyle\langle\hat{j}_{a}(1)\rangle^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}\int d2\cdots d(n+1)\,

\displaystyle\qquad\times R^{(n)}_{a;a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}(1;2,\ldots,n+1)\prod_{m=1}^{n}A_{a_{m}}(m+1).

(5)

Here
m\equiv(\bm{r}_{m},t_{m})
, and

R^{(n)}_{a;a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}(1;2,\ldots,n+1)
denotes the

n
th-order retarded nonlocal current-response kernel in the minimal-coupling formulation. It describes the causal
n
th-order response of the current at spacetime point
1
to vector-potential insertions at points
2,\ldots,n+1
, while retaining the full spatial nonlocality of the response. Schematically,

R^{(n)}\sim\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\right)^{n}\Theta_{n}\,\mathcal{C}^{(n+1)},

(6)

where
\Theta_{n}
denotes the product of step functions enforcing causality,

\Theta_{n}\equiv\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\theta(t_{2}-t_{3})\cdots\theta(t_{n}-t_{n+1})
, and
\mathcal{C}^{(n)}
denotes the nested-commutator expectation value of current operators in the unperturbed state,

\mathcal{C}^{(n)}\equiv\Big\langle[\,[\,\cdots[[\hat{j}_{a}(1),\hat{j}_{a_{1}}(2)],\hat{j}_{a_{2}}(3)]\cdots\,],\hat{j}_{a_{n}}(n)]\Big\rangle_{0}.

(7)

In the present convention, the factor
1/n!
in Eq. (
5
) corresponds to using a symmetrized
n
th-order kernel; the precise prefactors and permutation conventions are otherwise conventional. The linear response discussed above corresponds to
n=1
.

For observables measured as scalar optical signals, such as the absorption spectrum, one further contracts the induced current with the driving or detected field. For a monochromatic field labeled by
(\sigma,\ell)
, the corresponding
(n+1)
th-order contribution to the absorption spectrum can be written in the frequency domain as

\displaystyle S^{(n+1)}_{(\sigma,\ell)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega}{2}\,\mathrm{Im}\int d\bm{r}\,\sum_{a}A^{(\sigma,\ell)*}_{a}(\bm{r},\omega)\,\langle\hat{j}_{a}(\bm{r},\omega)\rangle^{(n)}_{(\sigma,\ell)}.

(8)

Thus, the expression above defines the basic microscopic dichroic marker, while measurable nonlinear absorption signals require an additional contraction with the relevant field or detection channel.

III
Structure of dichroic response

III.1
Helicity-odd projection of the nonlocal response

Using the absorption formula introduced in Sec.
II
, we now identify which part of the nonlocal response helicity reversal selects. For a beam labeled by
(\sigma,\ell)
, the corresponding dichroic signal is

\Delta_{\zeta}S^{(\sigma,\ell)}(\omega)=\int d\bm{r}\,d\bm{r}^{\prime}\sum_{ab}\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{(\sigma,\ell)}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\,\mathcal{J}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega),

(9)

where

\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{(\sigma,\ell)}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\equiv\Delta_{\zeta}\!\left[A_{a}^{*}(\bm{r})A_{b}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\right]

is the dichroic optical bilinear associated with the chosen reversal channel. We keep this definition unnormalized. In contrast to asymmetry ratios used in the conventional definitions, the present form isolates the helicity-odd component of the response itself, without mixing it with the helicity-even absorption background through a normalization denominator. Equation (
9
) therefore provides the basic expression used below: the dichroic signal is determined by the part of the nonlocal response that survives contraction with the corresponding optical bilinear difference.

For HCD, this relation has a simple symmetry content. When the compared fields are related by inversion,
\bm{A}^{(-\sigma,-\ell)}(\bm{r})=\bm{A}^{(\sigma,\ell)}(-\bm{r})
, the HCD optical bilinear is odd under simultaneous inversion of both spatial arguments. Thus, it probes the inversion-odd sector of the nonlocal response and vanishes in inversion-symmetric systems (see Appendix
A
)
YeEtAl2019JCTC
. By contrast, the present HD does not correspond to spatial inversion or mirror reversal of material handedness. In this sense, HD should be understood here as an operationally defined structured-light dichroic observable rather than a pseudoscalar measure of material chirality.

III.2
Irreducible-tensor decomposition

To make the contributing microscopic sectors explicit, we now decompose the nonlocal response kernel and the optical bilinear entering Eq. (
9
) into irreducible tensor components. This makes it possible to distinguish which part of the nonlocal response scalar, axial-vector, and rank-2 optical structures probe.

For two vectors
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
, the dyadic product
X_{a}Y_{b}
decomposes into irreducible parts of rank
J=0,1,2
as

\displaystyle X_{a}Y_{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ab}\,(\bm{X}\!\cdot\!\bm{Y})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c}\epsilon_{abc}\,(\bm{X}\!\times\!\bm{Y})_{c}

\displaystyle\quad+\left[\frac{1}{2}(X_{a}Y_{b}+X_{b}Y_{a})-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ab}(\bm{X}\!\cdot\!\bm{Y})\right].

(10)

Applying this identity to the response kernel
\mathcal{J}_{ab}
and to the dichroic optical bilinear

\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\equiv\Delta_{\zeta}\!\bigl[A_{a}^{*}(\bm{r})A_{b}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr]
,
the integrand of Eq. (
9
) separates into scalar, axial-vector, and rank-2 sectors,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\,\mathcal{J}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}^{(0)}_{\zeta}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)+\mathcal{I}^{(1)}_{\zeta}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{I}^{(2)}_{\zeta}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega).

(11)

We define the scalar, axial-vector, and rank-2 parts of the response kernel as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(0)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ab}\,\mathcal{J}_{cc}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega),

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(1)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\mathcal{J}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)-\mathcal{J}_{ba}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)\Bigr],

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(2)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\mathcal{J}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)+\mathcal{J}_{ba}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ab}\,\mathcal{J}_{cc}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega),

(14)

and similarly for the optical bilinear,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}^{(0)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ab}\,\Delta_{\zeta}\!\bigl[\bm{A}^{*}(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!\bm{A}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr],

(15)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})-\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}_{ba}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\Bigr],

(16)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}^{(2)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})+\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}_{ba}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ab}\,\Delta_{\zeta}\!\bigl[\bm{A}^{*}(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!\bm{A}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr].

(17)

With these definitions, we can write the three sectors as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(0)}_{\zeta}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}^{(0)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)\,\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}^{(0)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}),

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(1)}_{\zeta}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}^{(1)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)\,\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}^{(1)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}),

(19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(2)}_{\zeta}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}^{(2)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)\,\Delta_{\zeta}\mathcal{A}^{(2)}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}).

(20)

Equation (
11
) shows that the dichroic response is not a single undifferentiated pseudoscalar. Rather, the optical bilinear difference resolves into distinct tensorial sectors, each selecting the corresponding sector of the nonlocal response. The scalar sector responds to the dichroic part of
\bm{A}^{*}(\bm{r})\!\cdot\!\bm{A}(\bm{r}^{\prime})
, the axial-vector sector to the antisymmetric optical structure, and the rank-2 sector to the traceless symmetric optical structure.

Among these sectors, the axial-vector part
\mathcal{J}^{(1)}
is the one most closely related to the familiar notion of molecular chirality and optical activity, since it represents antisymmetric, circulating-current structure of the response. By contrast,
\mathcal{J}^{(0)}
and
\mathcal{J}^{(2)}
describe scalar and anisotropic nonlocal geometry, which need not be chiral by themselves but can nevertheless contribute to helicity-odd structured-light signals when combined with inversion-odd spatial dependence and nontrivial optical mode structure.

With these definitions, Eq. (
9
) becomes

\begin{split}\Delta_{\zeta}S(\omega)&=\int d\bm{r}\,d\bm{r}^{\prime}\,\Bigl[\mathcal{I}^{(0)}_{\zeta}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)+\mathcal{I}^{(1)}_{\zeta}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)\\
&\qquad\qquad+\mathcal{I}^{(2)}_{\zeta}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)\Bigr].\end{split}

(21)

This decomposition separates the microscopic channels contributing to the dichroic signal and clarifies which tensor sector of the nonlocal response is selected.

III.3
Single-mode and mixed-mode helical dichroism

We now apply the tensor decomposition above to structured fields with definite or mixed orbital content. Throughout this subsection, the term “diagonal” means coupling within the same OAM channel, whereas “off-diagonal” denotes coherence or interference between different OAM channels. Our aim is to identify which tensor sectors of the inversion-odd nonlocal response the optical mode structure selects and to clarify the distinction between diagonal and off-diagonal mode-space couplings.

We first consider a single helical mode in Eq. (
4
). For an ordinary helical beam, we assume that the radial/longitudinal envelope is independent of the sign of the topological charge, i.e.,

f^{(\ell)}(\rho,z;\omega)=f^{(-\ell)}(\rho,z;\omega)
, and

\mathcal{F}^{(\ell,\ell)}=\mathcal{F}^{(-\ell,-\ell)}\equiv\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
, where

\mathcal{F}^{(\ell,\ell^{\prime})}(\rho,z;\rho^{\prime},z^{\prime};\omega)=f^{(\ell)*}(\rho,z;\omega)\,f^{(\ell^{\prime})}(\rho^{\prime},z^{\prime};\omega).

(22)

The corresponding optical bilinear is proportional to

\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(\rho,z;\rho^{\prime},z^{\prime};\omega)e^{i\ell(\phi^{\prime}-\phi)}
, so that the azimuthal dependence enters only through the relative angle
\phi^{\prime}-\phi
. A single helical mode probes a diagonal azimuthal/OAM channel of the nonlocal response.

Using the irreducible-tensor decomposition, we find that the single-mode dichroic response is organized by the pair
(\sigma,\ell)
and by the relative azimuthal coordinate
\phi^{\prime}-\phi
. CD is purely axial-vector. For circular polarization, HD and HCD both involve scalar, axial-vector, and rank-2 sectors, but with different angular structures. In HD, all three sectors are proportional to
\sin[\ell(\phi^{\prime}-\phi)]
. In HCD, the scalar and rank-2 sectors are proportional to
\sin[\ell(\phi^{\prime}-\phi)]
, whereas the axial-vector sector is proportional to
\cos[\ell(\phi^{\prime}-\phi)]
. In the corresponding linearly polarized single-mode case, the axial-vector sector of HD is absent, leaving only the scalar and rank-2 sectors.

These sine and cosine dependences follow from the azimuthal phase structure
e^{i\ell(\phi^{\prime}-\phi)}
of the optical bilinear. From the viewpoint of dichroism, they indicate which parity sector of the relative-azimuthal dependence survives the helicity-odd projection: the sine terms encode the odd part, whereas the cosine term shows that the axial-vector sector of HCD also probes the even part. Appendix
B
gives the explicit tensor forms.

We next consider a structured beam containing a helical component and a zero-OAM admixture,

\bm{A}(\bm{r},\omega)=\bm{A}^{(\sigma,\ell)}(\bm{r},\omega)+\alpha\,\bm{A}^{(\sigma^{\prime},0)}(\bm{r},\omega),

(23)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is a mixing amplitude. Since the absorption is bilinear in the field, the new ingredient relative to the single-mode case is the interference between the two sectors. The corresponding off-diagonal bilinears are

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{(\sigma,\ell;\sigma^{\prime},0)}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv A_{a}^{(\sigma,\ell)*}(\bm{r},\omega)\,A_{b}^{(\sigma^{\prime},0)}(\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=e_{\sigma,a}^{*}e_{\sigma^{\prime},b}\,\mathcal{F}^{(\ell,0)}(\rho,z;\rho^{\prime},z^{\prime};\omega)\,e^{-i\ell\phi},

(24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{(\sigma^{\prime},0;\sigma,\ell)}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv A_{a}^{(\sigma^{\prime},0)*}(\bm{r},\omega)\,A_{b}^{(\sigma,\ell)}(\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=e_{\sigma^{\prime},a}^{*}e_{\sigma,b}\,\mathcal{F}^{(0,\ell)}(\rho,z;\rho^{\prime},z^{\prime};\omega)\,e^{+i\ell\phi^{\prime}}.

(25)

Unlike the pure-mode bilinear, these phase factors are not functions of
\phi^{\prime}-\phi
alone. They represent interference between distinct OAM sectors and, at the level of Eq. (
9
), make the signal sensitive to off-diagonal components of the nonlocal response connecting the
\ell
and
0
channels.

The tensor structure of the mixed contribution depends qualitatively on the relative circular polarization of the two interfering components. When
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
, the mixed contribution largely preserves the tensor-sector structure of the single-mode case. In particular, the mixed CD signal remains purely axial-vector, whereas the mixed HD and HCD signals generally involve scalar, axial-vector, and rank-2 sectors. By contrast, when
\sigma=-\sigma^{\prime}
, the scalar and axial-vector sectors vanish, leaving a purely rank-2 off-diagonal channel. This opposite-polarization case is also the natural setting for polarization textures that can support optical skyrmions
ChengEtAl2026AOP
. Thus, the relative polarization of the interfering components acts as a selector of the tensor character of the off-diagonal response. Appendix
C
gives the explicit tensor forms.

The azimuthal structure makes the contrast with the single-mode case especially transparent. For a single helical mode, all dichroic sectors depend only on the relative angle
\phi^{\prime}-\phi
, so the optical bilinear is diagonal in azimuthal/OAM space. By contrast, the mixed bilinears in Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) retain the separate phase factors
e^{-i\ell\phi}
and
e^{+i\ell\phi^{\prime}}
. Their presence, however, does not by itself guarantee a finite observable contribution after the
\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}
integration. A nonzero mixed signal requires the corresponding off-diagonal azimuthal/OAM components of the nonlocal kernel
\mathcal{J}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)
. In particular, for a system with continuous axial rotational symmetry about the beam axis, the kernel depends on the azimuthal coordinates only through the relative angle
\phi^{\prime}-\phi
and is diagonal in OAM space. The
\ell
–
0
interference term then vanishes after angular integration for
\ell\neq 0
. More generally, under a discrete
C_{N}
rotational symmetry, such mixed terms are allowed only when
\ell\equiv 0\pmod{N}
.

In the linear signal of Eq.(
9
), the mixed contribution probes a single off-diagonal
\ell
–
0
channel of the two-point kernel, and rotational symmetry constrains it. In the
(n+1)
th-order signal of Eq.(
8
), one detected field is contracted with an
n
th-order nonlocal current induced by
n
field insertions [Eq. (
5
)]. If these fields are expanded in azimuthal components
A^{(m)}\propto e^{im\phi}
, continuous axial symmetry requires conservation of the total azimuthal charge in the measured scalar signal. Including the material channel
\mu^{(n)}
carried by the response kernel
R^{(n)}
, this gives
m_{\mathrm{det}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}+\mu^{(n)}=0
, up to the sign convention associated with the detected channel
m_{\mathrm{det}}
. A higher-order response allows more mode combinations to satisfy the rotational selection rule. Mixed-mode coherence that is invisible in the linear
\ell
–
0
interference term can become observable through symmetry-allowed nonlinear pathways. Under a discrete
C_{N}
symmetry, the same condition applies modulo
N
.

III.4
Mode-space interpretation and gradient limit

The same two-point formulation can also be related to local-gradient expressions used in structured-light chiroptics. Introducing the midpoint and relative coordinates as

\bm{R}=(\bm{r}+\bm{r}^{\prime})/2
and
\bm{\delta}=\bm{r}^{\prime}-\bm{r}
, respectively, one may expand the optical bilinear around
\bm{R}
as

\displaystyle A_{a}^{*}(\bm{r})A_{b}(\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx A_{a}^{*}(\bm{R})A_{b}(\bm{R})+\frac{\delta_{i}}{2}\Bigl[A_{a}^{*}(\bm{R})\partial_{i}A_{b}(\bm{R})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(\partial_{i}A_{a}^{*}(\bm{R}))A_{b}(\bm{R})\Bigr]+\cdots.

(26)

The azimuthal selection encoded in the nonlocal phase factors can be recast in terms of local gradients.

For a single helical mode, the gradient expansion generates local structures that may be viewed as local counterparts of the nonlocal tensor sectors. The scalar sector is associated with symmetric spatial-dispersion contributions. At the local-gradient level, this suggests that symmetric spatial dispersion can, in principle, be sensitive to the local helical structure of the field. In physical terms, this reflects the helical spatial variation of the field. It should thus be interpreted as a local marker of the underlying inversion-odd nonlocal response, rather than as a pointwise measure of twistness.

The axial-vector sector generates antisymmetric polarization-gradient combinations such as

E_{x}^{*}\partial_{z}E_{y}-E_{y}^{*}\partial_{z}E_{x},

(27)

where all fields are evaluated at
\bm{R}
. They are of the same general character as optical chirality terms familiar from Lipkin’s zilch and related formulations
Lipkin1964JMP
;
TangCohen2010PRL
. In physical terms, these structures capture the local sense of polarization circulation together with its gradient character. They are the local-gradient structures most directly connected to antisymmetric circulating-current response and to the familiar notion of molecular optical activity.

The rank-2 sector provides the corresponding tensorial extension. At the local-gradient level, the symmetric traceless anisotropy of the field governs it,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\!\left[E_{a}^{*}\partial_{i}E_{b}+E_{b}^{*}\partial_{i}E_{a}\right]-\frac{2}{3}\delta_{ab}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left[E_{c}^{*}\partial_{i}E_{c}\right],

(28)

One can interpret the rank-2 sector as a tensorial analogue of optical chirality, which encodes anisotropic and quadrupole-like distortion of structured-light in a symmetric traceless channel. This is consistent with tensor-based descriptions of electromagnetic chirality
SmaginDyakov2026arXiv
.

Strongly structured or tightly focused fields, near fields, and vector beams with large longitudinal or azimuthal gradients enhance their signatures. Matter with intrinsic nonlocality or anisotropic current texture also enhances them. For mixed modes, the local-gradient form inherits the interference structure of Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
), reflecting off-diagonal mode coupling. The correspondence is interpretive rather than exact, since the fundamental objects in the present theory remain the nonlocal optical bilinears and their contraction with the nonlocal current-response kernel.

III.5
Relation to representative experiments

Representative HD experiments may be viewed according to whether they predominantly probe diagonal single-mode response or mixed-mode interference between distinct OAM sectors. For molecular and solution-phase measurements
Jain2023JCP
, HD is extracted as the difference between opposite optical helicities, thereby isolating the helicity-odd part of the response from the much larger helicity-even absorption background
KatoYokoshi2024PRB
.

The same perspective is also useful for nanophotonic and metasurface platforms. In single chiral nanostructures
Ni2021ACSNano
, a sizable HD signal may be interpreted as evidence that the inversion-odd response is already active in a predominantly diagonal, single-mode channel. By contrast, experiments on plasmonic metasurfaces and imperfectly pure structured beams

IV
Conclusion

We have shown that structured-light dichroic response can be formulated as a projection of the nonlocal current-response kernel onto helicity-dependent optical bilinears. This formulation resolves dichroic signals into distinct symmetry, tensorial, and mode-space sectors within a single microscopic description.

For single helical modes, the theory identifies diagonal OAM-resolved contributions selected by the relative azimuthal structure of the optical field. For mixed modes, interference between different OAM components probes off-diagonal coherence of the nonlocal kernel. The associated tensor structure depends qualitatively on whether the interfering components have the same or opposite circular polarization. Thus, the theory captures both diagonal and off-diagonal mode-space contributions to dichroic response within the same microscopic picture.

It also clarifies how local structures associated with symmetric spatial dispersion, optical chirality, and tensorial anisotropy arise as gradient-level manifestations of the underlying nonlocal response. Because the formulation retains the optical mode profile explicitly, it extends naturally to nonlinear response, as well as to vectorial, nonparaxial, and multipolar regimes. These results establish structured-light dichroism as a probe of inversion-odd nonlocal response in both real and angular-momentum space.

Appendix A
Inversion symmetry and the vanishing of HCD

Here we state the inversion-symmetry argument for the vanishing of the HCD signal and the corresponding parity transformation of the nonlocal current-response kernel. Let
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
denote the spatial-inversion operator. Since the current density is a polar vector, it transforms as
\hat{\mathcal{P}}\,\hat{j}_{a}(\bm{r})\,\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}=-\hat{j}_{a}(-\bm{r})
. For an inversion-symmetric system, the two-point current kernel entering the absorption formula is even under simultaneous inversion of both spatial arguments,
\mathcal{J}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)=\mathcal{J}_{ab}(-\bm{r},-\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)
. If inversion relates the helicity-reversed pair of incident fields according to
\bm{A}^{(\sigma,\ell)}(\bm{r})=\bm{A}^{(\sigma,\ell)}(-\bm{r})
, then the corresponding absorption spectra are identical:

\displaystyle S^{(-\sigma,-\ell)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{r}\,d\bm{r}^{\prime}\sum_{ab}A_{a}^{(-\sigma,-\ell)}(\bm{r})\mathcal{J}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)A_{b}^{(-\sigma,-\ell)*}(\bm{r}^{\prime})

(29)

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{r}\,d\bm{r}^{\prime}\sum_{ab}A_{a}^{(\sigma,\ell)}(\bm{r})\mathcal{J}_{ab}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)A_{b}^{(\sigma,\ell)*}(\bm{r}^{\prime})=S^{(\sigma,\ell)}(\omega).

Explicit single-mode tensor sectors

In this Appendix, we list the explicit tensor forms of the single-mode optical sectors discussed in Sec.
III.3
. These expressions make the tensorial content of the dichroic optical bilinears fully explicit and show the main-text statements in full Cartesian form. In the single-mode case, all sectors remain diagonal in azimuthal/OAM space and depend only on the relative angle
\phi^{\prime}-\phi
. All expressions below carry the common factor
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(\rho,z;\rho^{\prime},z^{\prime};\omega)
.

For CD, defined by
\sigma\to-\sigma
at fixed

,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{CD}}\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{(0)(\sigma,\ell)}

\displaystyle=0,

(30)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{CD}}\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{(1)(\sigma,\ell)}

\displaystyle=i\sigma\,\epsilon_{abz}\,e^{i\ell(\phi^{\prime}-\phi)},

(31)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{CD}}\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{(2)(\sigma,\ell)}

\displaystyle=0.

(32)

Thus, in the single-mode case, CD is purely axial-vector.

For HD, defined by
\ell\to-\ell
at fixed
\sigma

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{HD}}\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{(0)(\sigma,\ell)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2i}{3}\delta_{ab}\,\sin[\ell(\phi^{\prime}-\phi)],

(33)

For HCD, defined by

We next list the explicit tensor forms for the mixed-mode case discussed in Sec.
III.3
. The key feature is interference between the

and

OAM sectors. Unlike the single-mode expressions above, the mixed sectors are off-diagonal in mode space and do not reduce to functions of the relative angle

\displaystyle X_{\ell}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime};\omega)
