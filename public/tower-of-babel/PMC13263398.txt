Contezolid in the Treatment of Multidrug‐Resistant Tuberculous Meningitis: A Case Report
Authors: Na Wang, Xingyu Li, Qiaoyi Yan, Haitao Su, Yufeng Liu, Yanhong Li, Chunrong Yang, Yingchun Gu, Taoli Yan, Qiuzhen Wang, Xia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMultidrug‐resistant tuberculous meningitis (MDR‐TBM) has a high mortality rate, and treatment options are limited. Linezolid is recommended for refractory cases but often causes intolerable adverse reactions such as peripheral neuropathy. Contezolid, a novel oxazolidinone, has shown better safety profiles in preclinical and early clinical studies. Here we report a 33‐year‐old male with MDR‐TBM who developed progressive peripheral neuropathy after 6 months of a linezolid‐containing regimen. Linezolid was discontinued, but cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) parameters remained abnormal. Contezolid was initiated in July 2023. Within 3 months, the patient's headache and blurred vision resolved; after 5 months of treatment, CSF pressure normalized, protein decreased from 1570 to 501 mg/L, and glucose returned to normal range. No adverse reactions occurred during contezolid therapy, and clinical improvement was sustained through 24‐month follow‐up. This case suggests contezolid may be a safe and effective alternative to linezolid for MDR‐TBM patients with drug intolerance.Keywords: adverse reaction, Contezolid, linezolid, multidrug‐resistant tuberculous meningitis

Introduction

In China, multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis (MDR‐TB) accounted for 3.52% of new cases and 18.01% of retreated patients [1], among which cases of multidrug‐resistant tuberculous meningitis (MDR‐TBM) are relatively rare, and its treatment outcomes are particularly poor. Tuberculous meningitis (TBM) is a diffuse, non‐suppurative inflammatory disease of the pia mater and arachnoid membrane caused by the invasion of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) into the subarachnoid space. It can also invade the brain parenchyma and cerebral vessels, accounting for about 1%–5% of all pulmonary tuberculosis cases [2], making it one of the most intractable problems in TB treatment.

Linezolid has been strongly recommended for the treatment of long‐term multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis patients [3], and it may benefit patients with drug‐resistant, severe, or refractory TBM [4]. However, some adverse events were observed in patients treated with linezolid, indicating the urgent need to discover alternatives with better tolerance but equal or greater effectiveness. Contezolid, a novel oxazolidinone antibacterial drug, demonstrated intracellular antimicrobial activity comparable to or even superior to linezolid [5]. Compared with linezolid, contezolid has better safety, significantly reduced immunosuppressive and monoamine oxidase inhibitory effects.

This article reports a case of TBM with intolerance to linezolid, treated with contezolid for 5 months, providing reference for future anti tuberculosis treatment and research.

Case History

A 33‐year‐old male patient with a 20‐year history of psoriasis was admitted to the hospital on September 20, 2022, due to “coughing for more than ten days”. A thoracic ultrasound revealed a large amount of fluid in his right pleural cavity (Figure 1A). The patient underwent pleural biopsy and thoracentesis for drainage. The pleural fluid analysis showed a cell count of 8.5 × 109/L with 56% mononuclear cells, adenosine deaminase (ADA) level of 67 U/L, while both tuberculosis antibodies and T‐SPOT TB test were positive. Diagnosed with tuberculous pleuritis, the patient was started on first‐line anti‐tuberculosis treatment with isoniazid, rifampin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide (HRZE) on September 22, 2022. During anti‐tuberculosis medication, the patient experienced recurrent fevers with a temperature of 38.3°C. Moreover, due to the ineffective outcome of reducing pleural effusion, thoracoscopic surgery was performed on November 7, 2022. Postoperative pathology still indicated pleural tuberculosis.

Images of thoracic ultrasound. (A) September 27, 2022: A massive effusion showed in the right pleural cavity. (B) December 28, 2023: No pleural effusion.

Methods

During the postoperative period, the patient underwent cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which revealed no evidence of intracranial mass, abscess, or other structural lesions. Additionally, bacterial cultures of cerebrospinal fluid and serum, along with fungal antigen screening tests, were all negative, confirming the previous diagnosis, so his first‐line treatment remained unchanged.

After the surgery, however, the patient still suffered from recurrent fevers with temperatures peaking at 39.6°C, predominantly in the afternoon, and was accompanied by significant headaches, diplopia, but no nausea or vomiting. Thus, on November 22, 2022, a lumbar puncture was performed on the patient for the first time, revealing a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure of 320 mm H2O, a CSF cell count of 140 × 106/L with 60% mononuclear cells, CSF protein of 1907 mg/L, glucose of 1.3 mmol/L, chloride of 98 mmol/L, and ADA level of 11 U/L (Table 1). There were positive results for both Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) complex nucleic acid test and the rifampin resistance test in the CSF, suggesting a high likelihood of TBM with rifampin resistance in the patient, so the treatment for him was adjusted: isoniazid (0.6 g, once daily), levofloxacin (0.75 g, once daily), linezolid (0.6 g, once daily), bedaquiline (0.4 g, once daily), cycloserine (0.25 g, three times daily), amikacin (0.6 g, once daily), along with symptomatic treatment including steroids and mannitol. On November 29, 2022, by using metagenomics next generation sequencing (mNGS) technology, MTB was detected in his CSF, and it also showed resistance to isoniazid, rifampin, ethambutol, pyrazinamide, and streptomycin, which confirmed the diagnosis of MDR‐TBM. Therefore, his anti‐tuberculosis treatment was adjusted again to levofloxacin (0.75 g, once daily), linezolid (0.6 g, once daily), bedaquiline (0.4 g, once daily), cycloserine (0.25 g, three times daily), and clofazimine (0.1 g, once daily).

After 6 months of regular treatment, the patient still experienced headaches, blurred vision, and felt nauseous without vomiting, and he also reported progressively worsening numbness and tingling in both feet. Parameters from a re‐examination of complete blood count, renal and liver function were all normal. A repeat lumbar puncture showed a CSF pressure of 235 mm H2O, a CSF cell count of 40 × 106/L with 60% mononuclear cells, CSF protein of 1570 mg/L, glucose of 0.9 mmol/L, chloride of 126 mmol/L, and ADA level of 7 U/L (Table 1). Linezolid was discontinued in consideration of progressively worsening numbness and tingling in the patient's both feet, which affected his normal daily life and mobility. One month after stopping linezolid, the numbness and tingling in his feet disappeared, and the activity in both lower limbs returned to normal. However, there was no change in the headaches or blurred vision, and a subsequent lumbar puncture showed no improvement in CSF parameters: CSF pressure: 205 mm H2O, CSF cell count: 110 × 106/L with 60% mononuclear cells, CSF protein: 1307 mg/L, glucose: 1.1 mmol/L, chloride: 125 mmol/L, and ADA level: 8 U/L.

Given the persistent clinical and laboratory deterioration despite a 1‐month washout period off linezolid, contezolid 0.4 g twice daily was added to the patient's regimen comprising levofloxacin, cycloserine, and clofazimine on July 28, 2023. Prior to initiating contezolid, the patient was thoroughly informed about the rationale for switching medications, the potential benefits and risks, and provided written informed consent for this treatment modification. By the third month after adding contezolid, the patient reported that symptoms such as headaches and blurred vision had begun to subside. After 5 months of regular treatment with the above regimen, the patient's headaches and blurred vision disappeared, and there was no numbness or tingling in the lower limbs, and also no adverse reactions such as nausea or vomiting. On December 29, 2023, the patient's CSF was re‐examined: CSF pressure: 120 mm H2O, CSF cell count: 10 × 106/L, CSF protein: 501 mg/L, glucose: 2.5 mmol/L, chloride: 130 mmol/L, and ADA level: 2 U/L (Table 1), which showed significant improvement compared to previous results. A repeat chest CT showed no recurrence of pleural effusion (Figure 1B), while both hematologic and biochemistry parameters were all normal. The patient had not experienced any adverse reactions to medication. Based on significant clinical and laboratory improvements, the combination regimen including contezolid continued consolidation therapy for up to 24 months, with ongoing follow‐up. Conclusion and Results

We treated a patient with MDR‐TBM who showed intolerance to linezolid and used contezolid as an alternative for 5 months, and the patient's situation significantly improved without discomfort or adverse reactions.

Discussion

As the most severe form of extrapulmonary TB, TBM is a non‐suppurative inflammation primarily affecting the meninges, and the mortality can be as high as 80% when the disease is caused by multidrug‐resistant MTB [6], owing to the absence of a standardized treatment approach and few drug options [7]. Linezolid, as a key medication in the treatment of MDR‐TB, has notable therapeutic effects but also significant adverse reactions such as peripheral neuropathy, anemia, and thrombocytopenia [8]. In this case, the use of contezolid as an alternative to linezolid effectively treated a patient with MDR‐TB.

The patient had to stop taking linezolid during treatment due to intolerance of the adverse reaction of peripheral neuropathy. After discontinuation, a periodic lumbar puncture was performed, revealing that the CSF pressure remained high, with elevated protein levels, and significantly lower than normal glucose and chloride levels. Moreover, his ADA level in the CSF was consistently above 7 U/L, which usually indicates a poor prognosis for TBM, as is found in multiple studies. Therefore, to refine the anti‐tuberculosis treatment regimen, contezolid was chosen as an alternative to linezolid. Three months later, a follow‐up lumbar puncture showed that the patient's CSF pressure had normalized, CSF protein decreased from 1570 mg/L to 501 mg/L, and CSF glucose increased from 0.9 to 2.5 mmol/L, with an alleviation of clinical symptoms including headache and blurred vision.

Compared to linezolid, contezolid has a similar antibacterial spectrum and efficacy with better tolerability and safety [9, 10]. The frequency of adverse events occurring with the two drugs is comparable [11]. Animal experiments showed that the prodrug of contezolid had no neurotoxicity in the central, peripheral, and optical neurological systems [12], and there were a few case reports on the use of contezolid in the treatment of patients with MTB, most of which demonstrated positive outcomes for the patients [13, 14]. Notably, the patient showed no significant improvement during 6 months of the linezolid‐containing regimen, whereas clinical and CSF improvements were observed within 3 months after the introduction of contezolid. While this observation remains hypothesis‐generating due to the multi‐drug nature of the regimen, the temporal association between contezolid initiation and clinical‐laboratory improvement highlights its potential as a preferred alternative for patients intolerant to linezolid.

After 5 months of treatment with contezolid, the symptoms of the patient have significantly improved, and a re‐examination of the CSF showed ADA levels returning to normal ranges, and the thoracic ultrasound showed complete absorption of pleural effusion as well as improvement of pulmonary lesions. Meanwhile, regular hematologic and biochemistry parameters of the patient showed no abnormalities. The patient also did not experience any discomfort during the contezolid treatment period, with no recurrence of adverse reactions such as peripheral neuropathy, indicating that contezolid is ideally effective in this case.

Regarding the innovative aspects of this study, compared with previously reported cases, this report provides longer‐term follow‐up data on contezolid treatment and systematically demonstrates the dynamic trends in a series of cerebrospinal fluid parameters before and after treatment. The study also has limitations. First, this research is a hypothesis‐generating single‐case report. Second, in this case, the complex multi‐drug regimen administered after initiating contezolid treatment precludes establishing an independent causal relationship between contezolid and clinical improvement. Therefore, the efficacy and safety of contezolid in MDR‐TB treatment require confirmation through prospective, controlled clinical trials.

In conclusion, contezolid shows effectiveness and good tolerability with few side effects in this case, making it a potential drug that may be worth recommending for MDR‐TBM treatment.
