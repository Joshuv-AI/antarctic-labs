 island communities with regional and national energy experts who can provide strategic energy analysis and planning for

 Shelter Island Energy Supply
• Power is supplied from the Long Island Power Authority (LIPA)
  grid by underwater cables that run from Greenport to Shelter
  Island on the north side and from North Haven to Shelter Island
  on the south side.
• LIPA contracts with Public Service Enterprise Group (PSEG) to
  operate the electrical grid.
• Per most recently available data, Long Island’s electricity
  resources were primarily from fossil-fuel-fired power plants in
  2022.
• Starting in 2024, LIPA began automatically migrating customers
  to standard time-of-day (TOD) rates.
• There is a Guaranteed Bill Protection program that ensures total
  electric bills under TOD rates will not be higher than under the
  previous rate plan.
• The vast majority of Long Islanders, nearly 90%, heat their
  homes with fossil fuels.
 2022 LIPA electricity generation mix. Source: 2023 LIPA IRP

 Shelter Island Energy Use
• Average annual electricity consumption from 2019 to 2023 on the island was 33.5 gigawatt-hours (GWh).
• The residential sector accounts for most (88.5%) of the island’s electricity consumption.
• Summer usage is nearly 2x winter usage, likely because of seasonal influx of residents and tourism.

 Tidal Energy Resource Analysis Overview
Technology:
• Utility-scale technology convergence on horizontal axial flow with demonstration projects in
 Pole Barn

  Europe.
• Many small-scale demonstration projects in the United States and around the world.
• Commercial pilot projects needed to prove viability of technologies.
Resource:
• Two preliminary tidal resource assessments have been completed and indicate potential tidal
  resources in the Shelter Island area.
• The studies contradict each other as to the amount and location of the best resource. Source: Bedard 2006 (as cited in Augustine et al. 2012),
 Example turbine.
• More accurate simulations and measurements are recommended.
• TEAMER program could provide technical support for further site characterization.
Cost:
• The levelized cost of energy (LCOE) for marine energy typically ranges from $1 to $5/kWh.
• Factors that are helping costs come down include:
 • Bigger investment to help R&D and technology improvements for shovel-ready projects,
 • Industry getting private investment, and
 • Convening conversations with regulators.

 Community Solar
• Customer signs up for the community solar project with a certain subscription percentage and pays a
  fee to a generator facility.
• Community solar project feeds electricity directly to the grid.
• Production credits are distributed to the customers based on their subscriber percentage.
• The allocated kilowatt-hours are included on the customer’s bill as a credit.
• Potential for 1-MW agrivoltaic PV community solar project at Sylvester Manor is as follows:
 • Project would generate 1,619 MWh, enough to power ~150 homes.
 • Modeling assumptions include: 1 axis tracking system, 8 ft high, $3/W installation, 30% Investment
 Tax Credit (ITC).
 • Breakeven sale price is ~$0.176/kWh, often less than residential retail rate.
 • Project more than 300 kilovolt-amperes (kVa) is a concern for interconnection.
• List of approved community solar developers can be found here:
  https://www.psegliny.com/en/saveenergyandmoney/GreenEnergy/SolarEnergy/CommunitySolar.

 Per NYSERDA, New York leads the U.S. in community solar with 2 GW of installed projects as of 2023.

Frequently Asked Questions

 Should I Consider Purchasing an Air-Source Heat Pump?

Benefits of air-source heat pumps (ASHPs):
• With existing New York State rebates and financing options,
  ASHPs can be installed at a lower cost than a traditional cooling-
  only system.
• All-season, energy-efficient, cold-climate models are significantly
  more cost-effective to use year-round for heating and cooling.
• Advanced cold-climate units can perform at 100% of rated
  capacity in outdoor ambient temperatures as low as 5°F.
• The noise generated from advanced heat pumps are equivalent
  to a household refrigerator.

 What Are the Benefits and Potential Risks of an Energy
 Storage System?
Benefits
• Can provide backup power to critical functions during power outages
• Can provide savings on your electric bill

Potential Risks
• Though rare, thermal runaway (fire and explosion) hazards can occur
 • 0.2 failures per installed GW (~12 safety events worldwide over the past 5 years)
 • Statistics from electric vehicles: 25 fires per 100,000 sold (combustion vehicles 60x greater)

Risk Mitigations
• Electrical and fire codes and industry standards dictate the minimum requirements that must be met:
 • Southampton, New York, developed building codes for battery energy storage systems that could be a
 model for Shelter Island: Southampton Battery Storage Code.
• NYSERDA’s Battery Energy Storage Guidebook, which includes the most recent Underwriter Lab safety listings
 and requirements of the 2021 Fire Code.

Safety Measures for Installation
• WHAT: Certified equipment, reputable manufacturers
• WHO: Licensed electricians, certified by the ESS manufacturer
• HOW: Follow manufacturer’s instructions, locally adopted building codes and standards
• WHERE: Outdoors, indoor in a detached garage, indoor in an attached garage, provide clearance Source: EPRI Residential Energy Storage System Safety Guidelines.

 Are There Potential Geothermal Impacts on the Aquifer?
• It is common for vertical closed loops (VCLs) to drill through drinking water aquifers, according to Michigan Department of Environmental
       Quality (2010).
• Although closed-loop systems are less impactful to aquifer systems, risk can be reduced further by (1) using well-trained personnel to install
 and construct VCLs to ensure proper sealing and grouting; (2) using food-grade heat transfer fluids, and (3) following installation best
 practices.
• Working fluid circulating within the closed loop typically consists of commercial, FDA Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) mixtures (e.g.,
 propylene glycol) and anticorrosion agents, diluted with water at concentrations of 20%–25%.
• Although propylene glycol itself is not considered toxic, if leaked into the environment, it can serve as an organic nutrient that stimulates
 microbial metabolism, potentially resulting in O2 consumption and subsequent decrease in redox potential of the system (Bucci et al.
       2018, https://doi.org/10.1007/s12665-018-7375-9).
• Studies are still ongoing on the potential impacts to groundwater from closed-loop geothermal well placement. Because of the concerns
 around the relatively shallow Shelter Island aquifer, it is suggested the town pursues a horizonal system that will not drill below the water
 table.
• Additional information on open-loop system impacts can be found here: European Open-Loop Study.

 Aquifer Information
• Glacial Till—sand, gravel, cobbles, boulders, clay. Comprises the upper aquifer,
  which supplies drinking water to the island. Bottom of aquifer ranges from 85 to
  130 feet below land surface.
• There is a freshwater/saltwater mixing or diffusion zone at or near the bottom of
  the upper aquifer.
• Marine clay ranges from 50 to 100 feet below sea level and dips to the north.
• Water levels remain roughly consistent over time; aquifer recharged by

 Solar Net Metering Summary
• Residential:
 o PV rated capacity ≤25 kW (per LIPA) and 110% of site load (annual kWh)
 o Excess generation paid monthly as a credit in the next bill (same rate as customers without generation)

• Commercial:
 o PV rated capacity ≤750 kW (per LIPA) and 110% of site load (annual kWh)
 o Excess generation paid monthly as a credit in the next bill (same rate as customers without generation)
 o Larger systems (>750 kW or 110% annual kWh) are allowed as “Large Onsite/Offsite” customers but are paid differently (Value of
 Distributed Energy Resources (VDER) rate).

• Remote Net Metering (RNM):
 o Allows generation from a “host” PV array (location/meter) to offset other “satellite” meters.
 o Same customer/corporate must own/lease the property designated to the host and satellite accounts.
 o RNM eliminates demand ratchet (75% of the highest demand over previous year) for host and satellite meters: very beneficial.

• Community Solar:
 o Customer signs up for the community solar project with a certain subscription percentage and pays a fee to a generator facility.
 o Community solar project feeds electricity directly to the grid.
 o Production credits are distributed to the customers based on their subscriber percentage.
 o The allocated kWh are included on the customer’s bill as a credit.

 Per LIPA tariff, a customer can participate in only one of the above.
