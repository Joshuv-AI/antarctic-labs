On-column thiol protection strategy to control charge and stability of ThiomAb
Authors: Kai Ni, Leo (Lei) Wang, Alan Shupe, Timothy Cooney, Joshua Dai, Christopher Barton, George Parks, Christina Alves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCysteine-engineered monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), or ThiomAb, enable site-specific conjugation and form the basis of several next-generation antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs). However, the presence of intentionally unpaired cysteine residues introduces reactive free thiols that pose challenges for manufacturing consistency, including variable capping by cysteine/glutathione or uncapped free thiol during cell culture. This increases the charge heterogeneity of protein and may compromise stability due to increased risk of oxidation, disulfide scrambling, or aggregation.MethodsTo address these risks, we developed an on-column capping strategy, in which the antibody is immobilized on chromatography resin during the capture step and subjected to selective thiol masking under optimized redox conditions. This in-process approach allows for early and efficient stabilization of engineered thiol sites prior to polishing and formulation. High-throughput condition screening and analytical techniques—including mass spectrometry and charge variant profiling—were employed to fine-tune buffer conditions and understand on-column redox chemistry.ResultsThe optimized thiol protection conditions substantially reduce the population of free thiols, resulting in more consistent charge profiles and improved stability during downstream processing and storage.ConclusionThis strategy offers a scalable and platform-adaptable solution for enhancing the manufacturability and alternative control strategy of ThiomAb intermediates used in site-specific ADC processes.

Introduction

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) represent a rapidly evolving class of targeted therapeutics that combine the specificity of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with the cytotoxic potency of small-molecule payloads [1, 2]. To date, over 14 ADCs have received regulatory approval, and more than 120 additional ADC candidates are currently under clinical investigation [3], underscoring the rapid expansion and growing clinical relevance of this therapeutic modality. In addition to ADCs, antibody-based conjugate modalities continue to expand (e.g. antibody–oligonucleotide conjugates and other ‘AntibodyPlus’ formats), further motivating robust and broadly applicable control strategies for conjugation-enabled antibody platforms [4]. Early-generation ADCs predominantly relied on stochastic conjugation strategies, in which linker–payload moieties are randomly attached to native lysine residues or reduced interchain cysteines on mAbs [5, 6]. Although these approaches enabled clinical translation, they inherently generated heterogeneous products with broad drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) distributions. Such molecular heterogeneity has been linked to variability in pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety [7], and more broadly, developability liabilities (including polyspecificity/polyreactivity) have been recognized as risk factors during therapeutic antibody development [8]. Thereby motivating the development of more controlled and site-specific conjugation technologies [9, 10].

To address these limitations, site-specific conjugation platforms have been extensively explored [1, 3, 11]. These strategies restrict payload attachment to defined sites on the antibody scaffold, thereby improving molecular homogeneity and enabling more defined DAR ranges and predictable structure–function relationships. Among the various approaches, engineered cysteine–based ThiomAb technology has emerged as one of the most widely adopted platforms [10, 12]. In this strategy, free cysteine residues are introduced at selected positions that are distal to native disulfide bonds and can be selectively conjugated to drug payloads using maleimide chemistry. This design enables precise control over both the conjugation site and DAR while maintaining compatibility with conventional antibody expression systems and downstream processing workflows. This balance between molecular precision and manufacturability has led to broad interest in ThiomAb-based ADCs across clinical and industrial development programs.

Despite these advantages, ThiomAbs introduce distinct manufacturing and analytical control challenges that are not fully addressed by existing bioprocessing strategies. Engineered cysteines are not produced in cell culture as uniform free thiols but instead exist as a heterogeneous population of chemical states, including uncapped free thiols (SH) as well as two common capped forms known as cysteine- (Cys) and glutathione- (GSH) capped species derived naturally from cell-culture components. This heterogeneity arises upstream of conjugation and does not necessarily reflect differences in structural integrity or conjugation performance of the ThiomAb intermediate.

To accommodate this variability, ThiomAb conjugation platforms typically employ global reduction of engineered cysteines together with endogenous disulfide bonds, followed by controlled partial reoxidation of endogenous cysteines prior to drug conjugation. This design confers substantial tolerance to thiol heterogeneity and enables robust conjugation without materially impacting conjugation efficiency or final DAR distributions. Consequently, thiol-state heterogeneity at the ThiomAb intermediate stage is often process-tolerated rather than intrinsically quality-limiting for the final ADC.

However, when ThiomAb intermediates are dispositioned or released during Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) compliant manufacturing, regulatory guidance (e.g. ICH Q6B and FDA guidance on control strategies and process validation) expects that appropriate acceptance criteria or specifications be established to demonstrate successful completion of the manufacturing step and ensure process consistency [13–16]. In practice, this frequently includes charge-based assays such as imaged capillary isoelectric focusing (icIEF) or ion-exchange chromatography (IEX). Incomplete capping results in reactive free thiols that may be susceptible to disulfide scrambling and oxidation if not adequately controlled. Further, the coexistence of capped and uncapped engineered cysteines manifests as pronounced charge heterogeneity in these assays. As a result, ThiomAb intermediate batches with comparable structural integrity and downstream conjugation performance can exhibit substantially different charge profiles, complicating specification setting, and lot-to-lot comparability assessment. Notably, in preparative IEX separations of charge variants, there is an inherent trade-off between recovery (or yield) and main peak purity, since tighter fractionation to maximize purity typically reduces recovery and broader pooling to maximize recovery can compromise purity [17]. A broadly applicable strategy that reduces engineered cysteine heterogeneity at the ThiomAb intermediate stage, while remaining overall yield and compatible with established ThiomAb conjugation workflows and regulatory expectations for control strategies, has yet to be established.

In this study, we sought to address engineered cysteine heterogeneity through a process-integrated capping strategy that can be seamlessly incorporated into downstream purification. By introducing redox-active species during affinity capture chromatography, we enable manipulation of engineered cysteine capping and improve charge heterogeneity on the column (Fig. 1). Specifically, redox pairs such as cysteine/cystine and cysteamine/cystamine were applied as mixtures of thiol and disulfide species, and the observed effect is consistent with thiol–disulfide exchange driving the engineered cysteines toward a more homogeneous mixed-disulfide capping state, thereby reducing charge heterogeneity. By leveraging the immobilized state of antibodies during affinity chromatography, we developed a column-based redox capping process that enables controlled modification under flow conditions while remaining compatible with existing purification workflows. Two commonly used redox pairs, cysteine/cystine (Cys/Cyn) and cysteamine/cystamine (β-mercaptoethylamine/2-aminoethyl disulfide, denoted as MEA/AED), were evaluated across two distinct ThiomAbs and demonstrated effective and selective on-column engineered cysteine capping. High-throughput screening and native size exclusion chromatography–mass spectrometry (nSEC–MS) were leveraged to enable rapid optimization of reaction conditions and fast-turnaround analytical assessment. The resulting process was shown to be scalable, with all major critical quality attributes (CQAs) remaining comparable, and achieving up to 100% occupancy of engineered cysteine (eng-Cys) with uniform capping state. Together, this work establishes on-column redox capping as a practical approach for mitigating engineered cysteine heterogeneity and improving ThiomAb intermediate product quality and stability within downstream processing.

Materials

Two ThiomAbs were used in this study: ThiomAb1, an intact human IgG1 with two eng-Cys sites, and ThiomAb2, a highly engineered, truncated human IgG1 with one eng-Cys site. Both antibodies were produced by Takeda Pharmaceutical Inc. following recombinant expression in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells. MabSelect Sure™ LX resin was purchased from Cytiva. RoboColumns® were obtained from Repligen. L-cysteine hydrochloride monohydrate, L-cystine dihydrochloride, cysteamine (2-mercaptoethylamine, MEA), cystamine (2-aminoethyl disulfide, AED), glutathione (GSH), and glutathione oxide (GSSG) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used to prepare stock solutions at 0.5 or 1 M. Redox stock solutions were then spiked into affinity wash buffers, followed by pH adjustment to the desired values.

Methods

High-throughput setup with Robocolumn

Automated liquid-handling platforms (Tecan Freedom EVO 150 and 200; Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland) were used to enable high-throughput screening (HTS) of buffer conditions for optimizing capping efficiency. In a typical study, robocolumns pre-packed with MabSelect Sure™ LX resin (bed volume 589 μl) were loaded with Protein A (ProA) eluate at a target of 50 g/l resin and operated under predefined column and buffer-condition permutations (e.g. buffer composition, pH, and contact time). The resulting ProA eluate fractions were subsequently characterized by mass spectrometry, with or without enzymatic deglycosylation, to assess the impact on capping performance.

High-throughput scripting

Tecan methods were programmed using the Tecan Wizard add-on application for the Freedom EVO platform. A basic affinity chromatography script was established on Wizard that incorporated predefined parameters. For more complex workflows, the script was further modified to add or refine process steps beyond standard Wizard functionality, providing additional control over experimental execution. As an example, the in-use stability study leveraged this approach by adding pause timers after the target volume of prepared Wash 2 buffer was dispensed into the columns. Eight hold timepoints at room temperature were evaluated: 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, and 48 h. To ensure each condition was executed correctly, the intermediate steps [Liquid Handler (LiHa) buffer retrieval and LiHa wash] were timed, and these step durations were used to calculate and incorporate the required pause times. This ensured that the equilibration buffer was aspirated and dispensed into the correct column to neutralize the on-column reaction as intended. The same pause-time calculation approach was also applied to test on-column static hold.

Benchtop scale verification with ÄKTA chromatography system

The optimized capping condition identified from HTS was subsequently verified at bench scale using an ÄKTA Avant system. An Omnifit EZ column (Fisher Scientific) packed with MabSelect Sure™ LX resin (bed volume 15.7 ml) was used to evaluate scalability and performance under representative process conditions. The column was loaded with either ProA eluate or clarified harvest material from multiple lots to assess lot-to-lot robustness. The resulting ProA eluate was characterized to confirm capping performance robustness and to evaluate major CQAs, including host cell proteins (HCPs), host cell DNA, non-reduced capillary electrophoresis sodium dodecyl sulfate (CE-SDS), SEC, and icIEF.

Deglycosylation

Deglycosylation was performed using Peptide-N-Glycosidase (PNGase) F solution (QA-Bio) at an enzyme solution–to–protein ratio of 1 μl per 100 μg protein. Protein samples were diluted to 1–3 mg/ml in 50 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.5) and incubated with PNGase F in 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tubes (Eppendorf) at 37°C with shaking at 350 rpm in a ThermoMixer C (Eppendorf) overnight.

Rapid nSEC-MS sample testing and data analysis

Rapid native SEC-MS (nSEC-MS) sample testing was performed on either a Thermo Vanquish Flex Ultra-High-Performance Liquid Chromatography or a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class system coupled to a Thermo Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer. Samples were diluted in 50 mM ammonium acetate in water (pH 6.8, measured but not adjusted) and injected at an optimal on-column load of 1–10 μg onto a NativePac OBE-1 SEC column (2.1 × 50 mm, 3 μm, 80 Å diol SEC; Thermo Scientific NativePac OBE-1; Cat. 43803-052130) maintained at 25°C and operated isocratically at 0.2 ml/min. The LC divert valve directed mobile phase to the mass spectrometer from 0.0 to 0.6 min, followed by diversion to waste from 0.6 to 5.0 min; mobile phase B (100% acetonitrile) was not used for elution and was applied only for column storage at 75% acetonitrile at the end of each queue. MS data were acquired under native conditions in High Mass Range (HMR)–Full MS mode with in-source collision-induced dissociation (CID) set to 80 eV, 5 microscans, 15 000 resolving power (at m/z 200), an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 3 × 106, a maximum injection time of 200 ms, and an m/z range of 2500–8000 in profile mode. The heated ESI (electrospray ionization) source was operated with trapping gas pressure 1.0, spray voltage 3.5 kV, sheath/aux/sweep gas flows of 45/10/0 (arb), capillary temperature 250°C, auxiliary gas heater temperature 350°C, and a funnel RF level of 100. Data were processed using Byos or Byosphere software (v5.8, Protein Metrics) with the intact mass workflow, employing optimized m/z and deconvolution mass ranges. Capping variants were identified by matching deconvoluted intact masses to theoretical values using a 100 ppm tolerance, and relative abundances were quantified based on summed deconvoluted intensities. Percent uncapped, cysteinylated (%Cys), and glutathionylated (%GSH) species were calculated on a per-molecule occupancy basis by normalizing stoichiometry-weighted intensities of singly and doubly modified species to the total intensity of all identified mass variants. Results

Charge profile analysis and capping analysis

Pronounced lot-to-lot differences in the charge profile were observed for ThiomAb1, an intact human IgG with a pair of eng-Cys sites, indicating that one or more manufacturing-dependent attributes were varying in a manner detectable by charge-based method icIEF (Fig. 2A). Given the known thiol chemistry of eng-Cys sites, we considered variability in capping state as a plausible contributor to the observed charge heterogeneity. In particular, the relative abundance of glutathione-capped (denoted as GSH-capped), cysteine-capped (denoted as Cys-capped), and free thiol species (-SH, denoted as uncapped) [18] are expected to influence the apparent acidity of the molecule, with an anticipated trend of GSH > Cys > -SH. To investigate whether differences in eng-Cys capping state could explain the lot-dependent charge behavior, a nSEC–MS assay was performed to characterize representative lots and to associate charge-variant populations with their underlying molecular forms. Because multiple glycoforms introduce closely spaced mass differences that can obscure interpretation of capping-related mass shifts, enzymatic deglycosylation was incorporated to simplify the intact mass spectra and enable more confident assignment of capping states.

As shown in Fig. 2B, a representative ThiomAb1 reference standard (RS; pristine, bottom trace) exhibited multiple deglycosylated intact-mass variants consistent with engineered-cysteine capping heterogeneity, including mAb (uncapped), mAb + Cys (one Cys-capped site and one uncapped site), mAb + 2Cys (two Cys-capped sites), mAb + Cys + GSH (one Cys-capped site and one GSH-capped site), and mAb + 2GSH (two GSH-capped sites). mAb + 2Cys was the predominant species. In contrast, the Cys-capped ThiomAb1 sample (middle trace) was dominated by mAb + 2Cys, with markedly lower levels of the other variants, whereas the uncapped ThiomAb1 sample (top trace) showed a single major peak consistent with the uncapped mAb species, with capped forms at or below the detection limit. Together with the icIEF overlays in Fig. 2A, these data support that lot-to-lot shifts in engineered-cysteine capping state contribute to the observed charge variation of ThiomAb1.

Using a reporting strategy for charge peaks anchored on the main pI-anchored assignment strategy, we studied charge variants and redox state. This revealed a strong association between charge profile and capping profile. As shown in Fig. 2C and D, the fraction of uncapped species correlated linearly with the basic species abundance (R2 = 0.971), and the fraction of Cys-capped species correlated linearly with the main species abundance (R2 = 0.935). In contrast, the relationship between GSH capping and the acidic region was less correlated (Supplementary Fig. S1), potentially reflecting the additional contribution of other acidic modifications, e.g. deamidation.

High-throughput screening for two-step capping with Robocolumn

After identifying eng-Cys capping heterogeneity as a primary driver of the observed charge variability, process development studies were initiated to modulate capping state during downstream processing. To the best of our knowledge, there are no published literature reports or patents describing a broadly applicable downstream strategy specifically designed to control eng-Cys capping for ThiomAbs. Prior reports on on-column redox manipulation of antibodies (e.g. promoting disulfide formation [19] and mitigating trisulfide species [20, 21]) motivated evaluation of introducing a Cys/Cyn redox pair into affinity wash 2 buffer as a practical approach for capping modulation under process-relevant conditions. Based on the prior knowledge based on the in-house experience, the reaction conditions were defined with room temperature, pH 8.5–9.0 and reaction for 12–60 min [3 or 15 column volume (CV) at 4 min residence time] for further optimization.

Initial one-factor-at-a-time experiments were first conducted to refine the preliminary design space, focusing on cysteine and cystine concentrations as well as the reaction time on the column process. The testing conditions and glycosylated native SEC-MS results are shown in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S2, respectively. These studies confirmed that the capping profile could be modulated during ProA Wash 2 across different lots of starting material. Longer reaction times provided more robust performance, supporting a 60 min contact time for Wash 2. Notably, increasing reducing strength via higher cysteine concentration and an elevated Cys/Cyn ratio enhanced GSH displacement but also increased uncapping of eng-Cys (runs 13–16). In contrast, higher cystine concentrations tended to promote Cys-capping while minimizing uncapped species but had a very limited effect on GSH replacement (runs 6–12).

Given the challenges associated with GSH replacement, a two-step capping strategy was proposed (Fig. 3A). In this design, Wash 1 was used to drive removal of GSH-capped species using a cysteine/cystine redox pair. By tuning the redox ratio, broad reduction of eng-Cys capping species was achieved while maintaining IgG integrity (data not shown; to be published separately). An intermediate Wash 1.5 step was then implemented to remove carryover cysteine from Wash 1 to avoid interference with downstream capping efficiency. Finally, Wash 2 with cystine was used to convert uncapped eng-Cys to the full spectrum of Cys-capped state. Overall, the strategy was designed to balance reductive GSH removal in Wash 1 with oxidative final capping in Wash 2.

Three Robocolumn screening runs were performed on Tecan. All run conditions are listed in Supplementary Tables S3–S5, and the resulting capping profiles are shown in Fig. 3B. Wash 2 conditions were first optimized to identify an effective oxidative environment for capping (HTS Study 1; Supplementary Table S3; Fig. 3B, runs 1–8). A Wash 2 buffer containing 5 mM cystine at pH 9.0 for a 15 CV wash (60 min contact time) stood out, with complete suppression of uncapped eng-Cys. Next, after implementing an intermediate Wash 1.5 step (pH 9.0, 1× Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 5 CV), different contact modes—continuous flow versus static hold of the reaction buffer on-column—were evaluated for both Wash 1 and Wash 2 (Supplementary Table S4). As shown in Fig. 3B (runs 9–16), all eight conditions fully eliminated uncapped eng-Cys, indicating that Wash 1.5 was sufficient to remove carryover cysteine. However, no meaningful differences were observed among runs that still showed suboptimal Cys-capping (greater than 10% GSH-capped species), suggesting that different contact mode did not contribute to reaction efficiency; rather, the reducing strength of the Wash 1 conditions was insufficient to drive GSH replacement. Accordingly, the final screening round focused on optimizing Wash 1 conditions (Supplementary Table S5; Fig. 3B, runs 17–24). While all conditions still provided complete removal of free thiol, 6 mM cysteine and 0.25 mM cystine at pH 9.0 for a 15 CV wash was optimal, yielding 94% Cys-capping with less than 6% GSH-capping. Collectively, the two-step capping process was optimized from HTS experiments with robocolumn, with reductive Wash 1: 6 mM cysteine and 0.25 mM cystine at pH 9.0, 15 CV; Wash 1.5: 1× PBS at pH 9.0, 5 CV; and Wash 2: 5 mM cystine at pH 9.0, 15 CV.

High-throughput screening for one-step capping with Robocolumn

To further optimize the capping strategy with less complexity, alternative redox pairs, including glutathione/glutathione oxide (GSH/GSSG) and cysteamine/cystamine (MEA/AED), were further evaluated as potential one-step capping approaches. Given the difficulty of replacing GSH-capped species with Cys-capped species, we hypothesized that a GSH/GSSG redox system, which is thermodynamically favored, might instead enable conversion of Cys-capping to GSH-capping. Robocolumn screening runs were performed; however, replacement of cysteine capping with GSH remained challenging (Supplementary Table S6 and Supplementary Fig. S3). Therefore, MEA/AED, reported to have higher reactivity, was subsequently evaluated.

Three Robocolumn screening runs were performed on the Tecan platform. All run conditions are listed in Supplementary Table S7, and the resulting capping profiles are shown in Fig. 4A. Based on prior knowledge, three reaction pH values (5.8, 7.6, and 8.5) were evaluated, with pH 7.6 providing the best overall performance. At this optimal pH, and in contrast to the one-step cys/cyn redox system where performance is driven by both reductant concentration and the oxidant/reductant ratio, the MEA/AED system appeared to be governed primarily by the oxidant/reductant ratio, yielding a distinct performance pattern for GSH and Cys replacement as well as free-thiol capping. Although several conditions (runs 7, 9, and 11) achieved 100% MEA capping, 1 mM MEA and 5 mM AED at pH 7.6 with a 60 min contact time was selected to minimize the risk of over-reduction (Fig. 4B).

Case study of one-step capping with Robocolumn

A representative use case emerged during cell line development and upstream process development for ThiomAb1. Under intensified production processes, four production batches, with Production 1 derived from cell line A, Productions 2 from cell line B stable pool and 3 and 4 from cell line B clone a and b, respectively, exhibited varying but substantial levels of free-thiol exposure, reaching up to 96% (Fig. 5A). Consistent with this, a highly heterogeneous charge profile was observed, with markedly elevated basic species (Fig. 5B).

To demonstrate the capability of the proposed one-step capping strategy, clarified harvest material from four different productions was loaded onto Robocolumns for capping-integrated ProA chromatography. Following affinity capture, all four ProA eluates achieved ~90% MEA-capping (Fig. 5B) and showed substantially improved batch-to-batch consistency in the icIEF profile (Fig. 5D). These results demonstrate that the one-step MEA/AED capping approach can effectively mitigate capping variability and improving charge consistency across diverse production materials driven by cell line and upstream development.

Benchtop scale verification with ÄKTA chromatography system

To further evaluate process performance and product quality, benchtop-scale verification studies were conducted on an ÄKTA Avant chromatography system using a 15.7 ml Omnifit EZ column that was manually packed and qualified. For both processes, load material from two batches with either ProA eluate or clarified harvest (denoted as Batch1_PRE, Batch1_CH, Batch2_PRE, Batch2_CH, respectively) was processed to evaluate capping efficiency. For the runs loaded with clarified harvest, key CQAs were further assessed. Across scales, the number of wash CVs was adjusted to account for differences in residence time while maintaining a constant overall reaction contact time. As shown in Fig. 6, both capping processes achieved high capping efficiency, with complete suppression of uncapped free thiol, supporting the scalability and compatibility of both processes across batches and load types.

Extended evaluation for compatibility and manufacturability

To evaluate the feasibility of implementing a one-step capping strategy for manufacturing, extended studies were performed to assess process robustness and operational practicality. In-use stability of Wash 2 buffer spiked with cysteamine/cystamine was evaluated under representative manufacturing conditions (i.e. buffer prepared, held at a controlled temperature for extended periods, and routinely handled). Stability was assessed by monitoring capping efficiency over time using deglycosylated nSEC-MS. Consistent capping performance across different hold times indicates robust stability of Wash 2 buffer containing 5 mM MEA and 1 mM AED (Supplementary Fig. S4).

In addition, a freeze-hold study was conducted to evaluate oxidative decay of cysteamine and cystamine and to support the use of advance-prepared stocks. Separate 1 M stock solutions of cysteamine and cystamine were prepared, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C for 24 h, 48 h, or 6 months. The 24- and 48-h conditions underwent one freeze–thaw cycle and showed no differences relative to freshly prepared stocks. The 6-month condition underwent two freeze–thaw cycles and showed minimal differences, supporting that 1 M cysteamine and cystamine stocks can be prepared in advance and stored frozen for later use at manufacturing scale (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Finally, to demonstrate the broad applicability of the proposed capping strategy, both the two-step and one-step capping processes were evaluated using another ThiomAb (ThiomAb2), a highly engineered, truncated human IgG containing a single eng-Cys site. Consistent with our prior understanding of capping profiles, similar charge heterogeneity (data not shown) was observed, in line with a heterogeneous capping profile. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S6, both the two-step and one-step capping processes were successfully applied to ThiomAb2, achieving greater than 90% Cys-capping and 100% MEA-capping, respectively. Collectively, these results support the applicability of these capping strategies across ThiomAbs with diverse structures that share a common eng-Cys reactive site.

Discussion

In our study, redox-based capping was initially explored during upstream cell culture and protein expression to assess whether engineered cysteine modification could be established early in the manufacturing process. Although modulation of the redox environment in upstream operations is technically feasible [12, 18, 22], these efforts resulted in increased product complexity, most notably elevated levels of clipping and other undesired variants (data not shown), rendering upstream implementation difficult to control. Based on these observations, subsequent development efforts focused on downstream processing as a more robust and modular stage for engineered cysteine capping.

Within downstream operations, redox-based capping can be readily implemented under in-solution batch conditions [23, 24]; however, achieving highly uniform and fully capped material can be challenging due to the interplay of reaction reversibility/competing thiol exchange pathways. In addition, such approaches require extra unit operations, dedicated reaction tanks for mixing and holds, and additional clearance steps, limiting overall process efficiency. Flow-based processing strategies, such as ultrafiltration/diafiltration [25], can partially address this limitation by enabling continuous removal of reaction byproducts and excess reagents, thereby shifting the reaction equilibrium toward the desired capped species. Nevertheless, complete and uniform capping remains difficult to achieve, reflecting intrinsic limitations in reaction kinetics and accessibility of engineered cysteine residues in solution.

Performing redox modification on chromatographic media addresses these limitations by combining the benefits of flow-based processing with the unique advantages of protein immobilization. In contrast to in-solution reactions, on-column modification spatially separates individual antibody molecules on the stationary phase, thereby reducing intermolecular interactions and minimizing uncontrolled side reactions such as aggregation or disulfide scrambling. At the same time, the extent of redox modification can be precisely tuned through residence time and reagent concentration under well-defined flow conditions [23]. In this work, affinity chromatography was selected as the evaluation platform to introduce redox modification at an early stage of downstream purification, leveraging its widespread use and compatibility with established antibody purification workflows. Importantly, positioning this step early in the purification train provides inherent robustness for clearance of redox reagents and reaction byproducts, as multiple subsequent polishing steps remain available to remove residual process-related impurities.

The concept of column-based modification has gained increasing adoption in biologics manufacturing [20, 26, 27]. Implementations have expanded from the modifications on conventional modalities such as disulfide reformation [21] and trisulfide oxidation [20] to more complex modalities, including the production of bi-specific and multi-specific antibodies [26, 27], where immobilization on chromatographic media enables controlled and streamlined processing of structurally complex molecules. Within this context, the on-column redox capping strategy described here aligns with an emerging manufacturing paradigm that integrates chemical transformation with purification. Notably, this strategy is not limited to ThiomAbs: in principle, it can be applied to any biologic containing unpaired cysteine with accessible free thiols, including other antibody formats as well as non-antibody proteins such as enzymes, interleukins, and peptides, where management of thiol reactivity is needed to control product heterogeneity. Extending these principles, on-column conjugation represents a logical next step and an important area for future investigation, with the potential to further streamline processing and enhance control over conjugation-related heterogeneity in next-generation biologics.

From a manufacturing perspective, the results of this study demonstrate the accessibility and manufacturability of on-column redox capping as an integrated downstream strategy. More broadly, improving process consistency and reducing heterogeneity contributes to the developability of antibody-based therapeutics [8]. Incorporation of the capping step directly into affinity capture enables efficient removal of excess redox reagents and reaction byproducts through standard wash and polishing steps, without the need for additional reaction vessels or hold operations. In addition, suppression of residual uncapped/free-thiol species is expected to reduce the propensity for disulfide-mediated scrambling and intermolecular thiol-driven aggregation during subsequent processing, a conclusion that is consistent with the comparable SEC and CE-SDS profiles observed for capped materials relative to controls. This approach preserves the modularity of downstream processing, aligns with established platform purification strategies, and reduces overall process complexity, supporting its suitability for scalable biologics manufacturing. Future work will focus on further evaluation of process scalability and on extending this column-based modification concept to additional unit operations and manufacturing contexts, with the goal of establishing broader applicability across diverse biologic modalities.

In addition to process considerations, this study provides further insight into the relationship between engineered cysteine capping variants and charge heterogeneity. Charge variants in mAbs are traditionally attributed primarily to chemical modifications such as deamidation; however, the results presented here further demonstrate that, in ThiomAbs, the acidity of engineered cysteine capping species can play a dominant role in shaping the overall charge variant distribution [28]. Specifically, when eng-Cys are capped with basic moieties such as cysteamine through cystaminylation, a pronounced shift in isoelectric point is observed, resulting in the major capping species manifesting predominantly as basic variants in charge-based analytical assays.

Analytical considerations. We note that charge heterogeneity in antibodies can reflect true product modifications (e.g. deamidation), but can also be influenced by sample handling and analytical workflows. In particular, deamidation can be introduced as an artifact in some proteolysis-based workflows depending on pH and incubation conditions, and strategies to mitigate such artifacts under mildly acidic digestion conditions have been reported [29]. More broadly, MS-based approaches that incorporate orthogonal chemical/isotopic signatures can enhance confidence in detecting and assigning low-abundance species [30]. In the present study, the primary conclusions are based on discrete intact-mass differences corresponding to engineered-cysteine capping states and are supported by orthogonal charge profiling (icIEF), reducing reliance on small mass shifts that could be more sensitive to preparation-induced artifacts. As with any intact-mass workflow, low-abundance variants near the detection limit and deconvolution/processing assumptions can influence apparent relative abundances; these limitations are acknowledged when interpreting minor species. Accordingly, only capping-related species present above the method’s limit of quantitation (LoQ) are reported and interpreted quantitatively in this study; under the nSEC-MS conditions used in this work, the practical LoQ was ~0.5% relative abundance (expressed relative to the main species). This approach avoids over-interpretation of low-level features that may be influenced by spectral noise, deconvolution ambiguity, and run-to-run variability in native MS response, and it ensures that reported capping distributions are supported by quantitatively reliable signals suitable for cross-condition comparison.

Several observations from this study also provide practical lessons for related process-development challenges. First, replacement of GSH-capped species proved more difficult than initially anticipated. Conditions with greater reducing strength could promote displacement of glutathione-derived caps, but often at the expense of increasing the uncapped population, highlighting a fundamental trade-off between cap exchange and complete thiol protection. This behavior motivated the development of balanced redox strategies, including the two-step approach described here. Second, operational differences between static hold and continuous-flow contact modes did not materially improve the final capping outcome once an adequate intermediate wash was introduced, suggesting that reagent composition and redox balance were more limiting than contact mode under the conditions evaluated. More broadly, these results emphasize that optimization of eng-Cys capping should be approached as a multi-objective problem in which capped-state distribution, residual free thiols, and overall product quality must all be considered simultaneously rather than inferred from any single analytical metric.

These findings not only deepen mechanistic understanding of charge heterogeneity in ThiomAbs but also provide practical benefits for process and analytical development. The strong correlation between capping state and charge behavior enables native intact mass spectrometry to be used as a direct readout for engineered cysteine occupancy, while techniques such as icIEF serve as a complementary, orthogonal method to confirm charge-based shifts arising from specific capping chemistries. Together, this analytical toolbox facilitates more confident interpretation of charge variant profiles and supports more informed control strategies for ThiomAb manufacturing. More generally, the combination of high-throughput column screening with direct intact-mass readouts and orthogonal charge-based assays provides a transferable framework for rapidly developing and de-risking related thiol-control strategies in other biologic formats.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate that engineered cysteine capping is a major driver of charge heterogeneity in ThiomAbs. Variations in uncapped, cysteine-capped, and glutathione-capped species directly correlate with icIEF profiles, establishing engineered cysteine chemistry as a structurally encoded determinant of charge heterogeneity that represents an analytical burden rather than a quality-indicating attribute. To address this challenge, a process-integrated on-column redox capping strategy was developed and implemented during ProA capture. High-throughput screening and analytical tools, including robocolumn platforms and native SEC–MS, enabled rapid optimization of a process that is robust, scalable, and compatible with established purification workflows. Multiple redox pairs, including cysteine/cytine (Cys/Cyn), glutathione/glutathione oxide (GSH/GSSG), and cysteamine/cystamine (MEA/AED), were evaluated, leading to the identification and verification of both a Cys/Cyn-mediated two-step capping process and a MEA/AED-mediated one-step capping process across two distinct ThiomAbs. By leveraging antibody immobilization and controlled redox conditions under flow, the approach enables early and selective stabilization of engineered cysteines, substantially reducing free thiols and normalizing charge profiles. Importantly, the proposed redox capping strategy does not adversely impact other major critical quality attributes and is readily implementable at manufacturing scale. Collectively, this work advances mechanistic understanding of ThiomAb charge heterogeneity and establishes on-column redox capping as a scalable, platform-adaptable solution for improving the manufacturability and quality of ThiomAbs as intermediates for site-specific ADCs, with potential broader relevance across diverse conjugate-enabled antibody modalities.
