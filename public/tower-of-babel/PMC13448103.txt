When Is Fusion Necessary in Addressing Symptomatic Adjacent Level Pathology – A Commentary on “How to Treat Adjacent-Segment Pathology Following Lumbar Fusion Surgery: Decompression Alone or Decompression With Extended Fusion Surgery?”
Authors: Marc A. Arslanian, Vignessh Kumar, Lee A. Tan
Published: 2026

Adjacent-segment pathology (ASP) is one of the most common long-term complications after lumbar fusion [1]. Although radiographic degeneration at adjacent levels is common, only a subset of patients develops symptomatic clinical adjacent-segment pathology (CASP) that requires revision surgery. The best surgical treatment for these patients remains controversial. While decompression alone minimizes surgical morbidity, extension of fusion is done to address instability and reduce the risk of recurrent stenosis. However, long-term comparative evidence on this topic remains limited. This study addresses a clinically relevant question: when treating CASP after lumbar fusion, is decompression alone enough, or should fusion be extended?

The authors [2] conducted a nationwide registry-based cohort study using the Swedish Spine Registry (Swespine), including 2,521 patients treated surgically for CASP following lumbar fusion between 2001 and 2023. Of these, 1,176 patients underwent decompression alone and 1,345 underwent decompression with extension of fusion. Patients were followed for an average of 6.1 years. Survival analysis using Kaplan-Meier methodology and adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression showed that patients treated with extended fusion had a significantly higher risk of subsequent lumbar surgery than those treated with decompression alone. At 5 years, the reoperation rate was 18% after decompression alone compared with 25% after extended fusion.

The study’s main takeaway is that decompression alone is a reasonable treatment option for patients with CASP who do not present with spinal instability, significant deformity, or severe foraminal stenosis. While extending the fusion stabilizes the affected segment, it also transfers mechanical stress to the next adjacent level, increasing the risk of future adjacent-segment disease. On the other hand, decompression alone preserves motion at the treated level but carries a greater risk of recurrent stenosis at the same level.

These findings are consistent with previous randomized and registry-based studies on decompression with or without fusion for degenerative lumbar stenosis. The randomized trials by Försth et al. [3] and Karlsson et al. [4] demonstrated no long-term clinical benefit of adding fusion and reported increased adjacent-segment degeneration following instrumented fusion. Also, prior analyses from the Swespine registry by Joelson et al. [5] show that fusion changes the pattern of failure from the treated level to adjacent segments instead of getting rid of the need for future surgery. These studies support a less invasive surgical approach when spinal stability can be maintained [3-7].

With more than 2,500 patients, this study is one of the largest comparative studies focused specifically on revision surgery. This large sample size provides greater statistical power compared with prior single-center studies. Second, the mean followup of more than 6 years provides meaningful insight into longterm outcomes. This is an important consideration when studying adjacent-segment degeneration that may continue to develop years after surgery. Third, the authors used a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis combined with multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression adjusting for clinically relevant confounders, including age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, number of fused levels, number of operated levels, and diagnosis, which is appropriate statistical methodology needed to assess time-to-event data. They also performed competing-risk analyses accounting for death and confirmed that the assumptions underlying their Cox proportional hazards model were satisfied for nearly all variables and accounted for the one variable that violated the assumption, increasing confidence in their findings. Another strength is the characterization of subsequent surgery types. Instead of only reporting overall revision rates, the authors distinguished between same-level failure, recurrent adjacentsegment pathology, conversion to fusion, and implant-related complications. This information provides insight into how each surgical approach impacts potential future degeneration.

Despite its strengths, there are some limitations that should be considered. As an observational registry study, treatment was not randomized, which introduces the possibility of selection bias, as well as the possibility that patients undergoing extended fusion may have had more severe spinal pathology than those treated with decompression alone. The registry also does not include radiographic measures of instability and deformity, so residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded. Patientreported outcome measures, including disability scores, pain outcomes, and quality-of-life assessments, were not available for this analysis.

As the number of lumbar fusion procedures increases worldwide, CASP will become an increasingly common indication for revision surgery. Preoperative identification of patients who can safely undergo decompression alone has important consequences on surgical morbidity, healthcare costs, and quality of life, especially in older patients with multiple comorbidities. Potentially the most important finding of this study is that it reframes the discussion from whether fusion should be added to which patients truly require additional fusion. These findings support an individualized treatment strategy, reserving extended fusion for patients with instability or deformity while considering decompression alone for stable patients with isolated neural compression. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized radiographic measures and patient-reported outcomes will further refine surgical-decision making.

The authors should be commended for conducting this large national registry with compelling evidence supporting decompression alone as an effective treatment option in well-selected patients. Ultimately, spine surgeons should choose the most appropriate treatment strategy based on the patient’s presenting symptomatology and their clinical judgement. Fusion is not always necessary in patients with adjacent level pathology.
