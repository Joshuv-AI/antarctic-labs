Exact dispersion relation for linear surface waves on arbitrary vertical shear
Kjell S. Heinrich, Simen Å. Ellingsen

Abstract
We derive the formal solution to the dispersion relation for linear surface waves on a horizontal mean current with arbitrary vertical dependence. The problem is cast in a Green’s function framework for the Rayleigh equation, neglecting viscosity but making no further approximations about the mean velocity profile. The solution is the dispersion relation in the form of a single, implicit equation relating – and containing only – the velocity profile, wave frequency, and wavenumber. By isolating curvature effects in a path-ordered exponential, we obtain a solution that serves as a natural starting point for systematic approximations. We demonstrate that our solution reduces to the expression found by Shrira (1993, J. Fluid Mech. 252 , 565–584) in the deep-water limit, yields known asymptotic approximations, and recovers known analytical solutions in special cases.



I
Introduction

Surface gravity waves propagating on vertically sheared currents are central to a wide range of oceanographic and engineering problems, from wave forecasting and remote sensing to air–sea interaction and coastal transport. In a range of important questions involving waves and currents together, the vertical structure of a current plays an important role, not just its depth-average or surface value. Examples where vertical shear often needs to be taken into account are ocean transport of plastics and pollutants
(
15
;
20
)
, wind-stress modelling
(
23
)
, wave-breaking predictions
(
35
)
, wave statistics
(
34
)
, rogue-wave formation and kinematics
(
16
;
12
)
and wave forces on vessels
(
18
)
and structures
(
32
)
.

The problem we consider is a classical one and has been treated lucidly by
24
,
26
, and many others since. We assume waves are propagating on the surface, taken to be at
z=0
on average. Here,
z
is the vertical axis pointing up. The waves travel on a horizontal mean current with arbitrary vertical shear,
\bm{U}(z)=[U(z),V(z),0]
. The bottom is at a constant depth
z=-h
. We linearise the incompressible Euler and continuity equations, along with the boundary conditions at the free surface and bottom. We may then assume solutions of Fourier form
\varphi(\bm{x},z,t)\sim\hat{\varphi}(z)\exp[\mathrm{i}\psi(\bm{x},t)]
, with the phase
\psi(x,t)=\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}_{h}-\omega\,t
. The hat represents a Fourier transformed quantity, and
\varphi
represents a wave-induced velocity
\bm{u}=[u,v,w]
(the
x,y
and
z
components), pressure perturbation, or surface elevation
\zeta
(where
\hat{\zeta}
is independent of
z
). The horizontal position is
\bm{x}_{h}=[x,y,0]
;
\bm{k}=[k_{x},k_{y},0]
is the wave vector, and
\omega
is the wave frequency. Only the component of the current parallel to
\bm{k}
enters the problem. To simplify, we define the shorthand
U_{\gamma}\equiv\bm{k}\cdot\bm{U}/k=U\cos\gamma
, so that
\gamma
is the angle between
\bm{k}
and
\bm{U}
, and
k=|\bm{k}|
. Eliminating pressure and horizontal wave velocities, one is left with a second-order differential equation for the vertical wave velocity
\hat{w}(z)
. This is the Rayleigh, or inviscid Orr-Sommerfeld equation, in the form

\displaystyle\hat{w}^{\prime\prime}-k^{2}[1+q(z)]\,\hat{w}

\displaystyle=0,

(1)

with shorthands

q(z)\equiv\frac{-U_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(z)}{k\Omega(z)};\qquad\Omega(z)\equiv\omega-kU_{\gamma}(z),

(2)

where a prime denotes a derivative with respect to
z
. Note that
\Omega(z)
is the ‘locally Doppler-shifted’ frequency and that the intrinsic wave frequency, i.e. measured in the surface-following reference system, is
\sigma(\bm{k})=\Omega(0)
.

We now turn to the linearised boundary conditions at the bottom and surface, which can be written
(
26
)

\hat{w}(-h)=0;\qquad\sigma^{2}\,\hat{w}^{\prime}(0)=[(g+\Upsilon k^{2})k^{2}-\sigma\,k\,U_{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)]\,\hat{w}(0).

(3)

The gravitational acceleration is
g
, and
\Upsilon
is the kinematic surface tension coefficient. In the absence of current, the solution is
\sigma=\omega=\omega_{0}
with

\omega_{0}\equiv\sqrt{(gk+\Upsilon k^{3})\tanh kh}.

(4)

Assuming
\bm{k}
is given, the eigenvalue problem defined by (
1
) and (
3
) has
\hat{w}(z)
as an unknown function. There are two permitted eigenvalues for frequency,
\omega=\omega_{\pm}(\bm{k})
, corresponding to waves propagating parallel and anti-parallel to
\bm{k}
. Thus,
\omega_{\pm}(-\bm{k})=-\omega_{\mp}(\bm{k})
, so there is only one value of
\omega
for each wave
\bm{k}
, with
\sigma(\bm{k})>0
. All approaches to the linear dispersion relation – exact, approximate, or numerical – take this classical eigenvalue problem as their starting point.

For practical purposes, it is common to derive explicit approximate expressions, such as
\omega\approx\omega(\bm{k};\bm{U})
. This method assumes that either a suitably depth-averaged mean-current shear or curvature is small. The classical and most common asymptotic form was derived for
h=\infty
by
29
and generalised to finite depth by
27
. It reads
{\omega(\bm{k};U_{\gamma})\approx\omega_{0}(k)+k\langle U_{\gamma}\rangle}
with the second term acting as an “effective Doppler shift”:

\displaystyle\langle U_{\gamma}\rangle

\displaystyle=2k\int_{-h}^{0}\mathrm{d}z\,U_{\gamma}(z)\frac{\cosh 2k(z+h)}{\sinh 2kh}=U_{\gamma}(0)-s(\bm{k})\frac{\omega_{0}(k)}{k};

(5)

\displaystyle s(\bm{k})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{k}{\omega}_{0}\int_{-h}^{0}\mathrm{d}z\,U_{\gamma}^{\prime}(z)\frac{\sinh 2k(z+h)}{\sinh 2kh}\overset{kh\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{k}{\omega}_{0}\int_{-h}^{0}\mathrm{d}z\,U_{\gamma}^{\prime}(z)\mathrm{e}^{2kz}.

(6)

The final form of (
5
) is obtained by partial integration. The quantity
s(\bm{k})
is a non-dimensional number (for gravity driven waves it might be called a shear–Froude number) comparing the depth-averaged shear to the shear-free wave frequency as a measure of the effect of the shear on wave dispersion; we refer to it as the wave–shear-strength number. In the last form of (
6
),
\tanh kh\to 1
is understood.
Equation

5
was derived under the assumption of a weak current, but
10
showed the actual sufficient criterion is
s\ll 1
. Approximations are discussed further in Section
III.2
.

Alternatively, various iterative numerical schemes have been used to solve the eigenvalue problem. The most classical approach is the piecewise-linear approximation, which approximates
U_{\gamma}(z)
by partitioning it into a finite number of linear segments
(
31
;
33
)
. The dispersion relation becomes a solution of a linear system requiring removal of a large number of spurious solutions
(
28
)
.
6
developed a shooting algorithm for solving the Rayleigh equation, while
21
developed a powerful path-following approach suitable when thousands of calculations with very high precision are needed. The ‘Direct integration method’ (DIM) of
17
has gained some popularity due to its rapid convergence and arbitrary accuracy.

Explicit, analytical dispersion relations in the form
\omega=\omega(\bm{k};U_{\gamma})
have been found only for very special forms of
U_{\gamma}(z)
. Beyond this, the existing solutions in practical use are either approximations valid when
U_{\gamma}
satisfies certain
k
-dependent conditions (these are common), or require solving the eigenvalue problem numerically. Here, we instead derive a dispersion relation equation of implicit form

D(\bm{k},\sigma;\,\bm{U})=0

(7)

which is fully self-contained and does not involve any unknown eigenfunction of
z
and allows (numerical or in special cases, analytical) evaluation of
\sigma(\bm{k})
. The present work obtains an exact analytical dispersion relation of this form by casting the Rayleigh problem in a Green’s function framework, writing the vertical-velocity Green’s function as a path-ordered exponential that isolates the curvature effect. By constructing two fundamental solutions that separately satisfy the bottom and free-surface boundary conditions and then matching them, we obtain the formal solution; an implicit one-equation dispersion relation that depends only on the prescribed shear profile. This form recovers the classical no-shear and constant-vorticity limits, connects directly to the near-potentiality approximation of
10
, and provides a natural starting point for systematic approximations and numerical evaluation.

II
Approach and analytical solution

Our starting point is the eigenvalue problem in equations (
1
)–(
3
). In our derivation, we assume that the surface disturbance comes from a submerged point source. In a linearised theory, a solution for a delta-function disturbance can be integrated to obtain solutions for any distributed source, which is the idea behind Green’s functions.

Geometry of the problem set-up used for derivation.

We consider the set-up shown in
Fig.

1
: a point source of time-dependent strength
m(t)
located at
\bm{x}_{0}=[0,0,z_{0}]
. The continuity equation reads

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x})=m(t)\,\delta(\bm{x}_{h})\delta(z-z_{0}).

(8)

We assume
\bm{u}
and surface elevation
\zeta(\bm{x}_{h},t)
to be small, and linearise equations and boundary conditions with respect to these. Taking the Fourier transform and combining the continuity and Euler equations in the bulk, we eliminate the horizontal velocity components and obtain

\displaystyle\Omega(z)\,\left[\hat{w}^{\prime\prime}-k^{2}\,\hat{w}\right]\,+kU_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}\,\hat{w}=\hat{m}\left[\Omega(z)\,\delta^{\prime}(z-z_{0})-kU_{\gamma}^{\prime}\,\delta(z-z_{0})\right].

(9)

We require the Fourier transformed vertical velocity
\hat{w}
to satisfy the boundary conditions in (
3
). To eliminate
\hat{m}
we define a Green’s function
{\hat{G}=\hat{G}(z)}

(understood to depend on
\bm{k}
and
\omega
). We choose the Green’s function to be dimensionless and required by the Rayleigh equation with a unitary source

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{\prime\prime}-k^{2}[1+q(z)]\hat{G}=\delta^{\prime}(z-z_{0})-(kU_{\gamma}^{\prime}/\Omega)\,\delta(z-z_{0}),

(10)

with the shorthand

q(z)\equiv\frac{-U_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(z)}{k\Omega(z)}.

(11)

At the source, the Green’s function must obey the jump and continuity conditions. These are found by integrating the continuity equation and (
10
) just across
z_{0}
, and give

\displaystyle[\hat{G}]_{z_{0}}=1,\qquad[\Omega\hat{G}^{\prime}-\Omega^{\prime}\hat{G}]_{z_{0}}=0,

(12)

where
[f]_{z_{0}}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}f(z_{0}+\epsilon)-f(z_{0}-\epsilon)
.

It is not possible to obtain a closed-form solution for
\hat{G}
for an arbitrary
\bm{U}(z)
, but it transpires that the dispersion relation only depends on the part of the solution that
can
be found.
Equation

10
has a particular solution in the form of vortices convected with the mean flow as shown by
11
, but we do not need this for our purposes, and we consider the homogeneous solution
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{h}}
only. We consider the ranges above and one below the source separately and define

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\mathrm{h}}(z)=\left\{\begin{matrix}C_{+}\,\hat{G}_{+}(z)\quad&\text{ for }z>z_{0}\\
C_{-}\,\hat{G}_{-}(z)\quad&\text{ for }z<z_{0}\end{matrix}\right..

(13)

where
C_{\pm}
are constants to be determined. The functions
\hat{G}_{\pm}(z)
, crucially, are both defined throughout the water column
z\in(-h,0)
although at any given depth
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{h}}
is equal to one and not the other. They are smooth, and both satisfy the homogeneous Rayleigh equation everywhere.
Equation

12
determines
C_{\pm}
in terms of
\hat{G}_{\pm}(z_{0})
and
\hat{G}_{\pm}^{\prime}(z_{0})
with solutions

\displaystyle C_{\pm}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{W}(z_{0})}\left[\hat{G}_{\mp}^{\prime}(z_{0})+\frac{kU_{\gamma}^{\prime}(z_{0})}{\Omega(z_{0})}\,\hat{G}_{\mp}(z_{0})\right].

(14)

The Wronskian is defined as
\mathcal{W}(z;\bm{k},\omega)=\hat{G}_{+}^{\prime}(z)\hat{G}_{-}(z)-\hat{G}_{+}(z)\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime}(z)
. It is in fact a constant with respect to
z
. This can be seen when replacing
\hat{G}^{\prime\prime}_{\pm}
using the Rayleigh equation (
10
) for
z\neq z_{0}
. Therefore, we can set its value by evaluating it at the surface. We are free to choose a normalisation for
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{h}}
, so we set

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{+}(0)=1,\qquad\Omega(0)^{2}\,\hat{G}_{+}^{\prime}(0)=\left[\big(g+\Upsilon k^{2}\big)k^{2}-\Omega(0)kU_{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)\right]\,\hat{G}_{+}(0).

(15)

We use the boundary conditions in (
15
) to write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{W}(z_{0})=\mathcal{W}(0)&=\hat{G}_{+}^{\prime}(0)\,\hat{G}_{-}(0)-\hat{G}_{+}(0)\,\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{D(\bm{k},\sigma;\,U_{\gamma})}{\sigma^{2}},\end{split}

(16)

where we used
\Omega(0)=\sigma
and introduced the dispersion function

\displaystyle D(\bm{k},\sigma;\,U_{\gamma})\equiv\sigma^{2}\,\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime}(0)-\left[\big(g+\Upsilon k^{2}\big)k^{2}-k\sigma\,U_{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)\right]\,\hat{G}_{-}(0).

(17)

Noting that
D(\bm{k},\sigma;\,U_{\gamma})
enters as a denominator in (
14
), we have the familiar structure which invariably appears in initial-value problems with surface waves, where the zeros of the denominator become poles in the inverse Fourier integrals that transform
\hat{w}(\bm{k})\to w(\bm{x})
etc, which by Cauchy’s theorem pick out the
(\bm{k},\sigma)
-combinations that allow freely propagating modes
(see, e.g.,
19
, §4.9)
. In other words, the dispersion relation is
D(\bm{k},\sigma;\,U_{\gamma})=0
.

Note how only the bottom-normalised Green’s function evaluated at the surface enters the dispersion relation. Because the boundary conditions at the bottom are of standard Dirichlet and Neumann type, a formal solution can be found in the region below the source and analytically continued to
z=0
.

II.1
Solution below the source

We next derive a form of
\hat{G}_{-}(z)
that isolates the curvature
U_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(z)
and is convenient for approximations and numerical implementation, using a procedure inspired by Dirac’s ‘interaction picture’ in quantum mechanics. The interaction picture is typically used for (quantum) systems evolving in time under a time-dependent forcing operator, provided the initial solution is known. Instead of time, we consider the ‘development’ of
\hat{G}_{-}
for
z>-h
under the action of a
z
-dependent operator, when the solution at
z=-h
is known. The idea is to split the operator acting on the unknown function into a sum of two parts, one containing the terms that give an exactly solvable solution, the second containing the ‘difficult’ part, which is not, in general, solvable in closed form.

The homogeneous Rayleigh equation that the Green’s function
\hat{G}_{-}(z)
adheres to reads

\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime\prime}(z)=k^{2}\big[1+q(z)\big]\,\hat{G}_{-}(z),\quad\text{ with }\quad\hat{G}_{-}(-h)=0;\,\,\,\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime}(-h)=\frac{k}{\cosh kh}.

(18)

The factor
1/\cosh kh
is an arbitrary choice, amounting to a choice of normalisation for
\hat{G}_{-}
which ensures it tends sufficiently rapidly to zero as
kh\to\infty
, and the formalism transitions more smoothly across the full range of
kh
values. Introducing the ‘state vector’
{\bm{\mathcal{X}}(z)=\big(\hat{G}_{-},\,k^{-1}\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime}\big)^{\mathsf{T}}}
(
\mathsf{T}
: transpose) we re-cast (
18
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\bm{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\bm{\mathcal{X}}(z)+\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
q(z)&0\end{pmatrix}\bm{\mathcal{X}}(z)\equiv\bm{H}_{0}\bm{\mathcal{X}}(z)+\bm{H}_{U}(z)\bm{\mathcal{X}}(z),

(19)

defining the matrices
\bm{H}_{0}
and
\bm{H}_{U}(z)
as the first and second matrix in (
19
), respectively. All effects of depth variation of
U_{\gamma}
is now contained in the matrix
\bm{H}_{U}
. Inspired by quantum mechanics
(e.g.,
13
, Ch. 6)
we introduce a new vector
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(z)
defined by the linear transformation

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(\mathcal{Y}_{1},\,\mathcal{Y}_{2}\big)^{\mathsf{T}}\equiv\bm{T}^{-1}(z+h)\,\bm{\mathcal{X}}(z);

(20a)

\displaystyle\bm{T}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{e}^{kx\bm{H}_{0}}=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh kx&\sinh kx\\
\sinh kx&\cosh kx\end{pmatrix}.

(20b)

The exponential is understood in a Taylor-series sense,
\mathrm{e}^{\bm{A}}=\bf{I}+\bm{A}+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{A}^{2}+\dots
with
\bf{I}
the unit matrix, and the final form of (
20b
) is obtained by evaluating the matrix exponential explicitly, e.g., equation 24 of
(
3
)
. Note that
\bm{T}(-x)=\bm{T}^{-1}(x)
and that
\bm{T}(0)=\bf{I}
, so
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(z)\to\bm{\mathcal{X}}(z)
as
z\to-h
. The bottom boundary condition is
\bm{\mathcal{X}}(-h)=\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(-h)=(0,1/\cosh kh)^{\mathsf{T}}
. Inserting
\bm{\mathcal{X}}=\bm{T}\bm{\mathcal{Y}}
into (
19
) the evolution equation becomes
{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}(z)=k\bm{H}_{Z}(z)\,\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(z)}
, where

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{Z}(z)\equiv\bm{T}^{-1}(z+h)\,\bm{H}_{U}(z)\,\bm{T}(z+h)=q(z)\,\bm{C}(z+h),

(21)

with

\displaystyle\bm{C}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-\sinh 2kx&1-\cosh 2kx\\[3.0pt]
1+\cosh 2kx&\sinh 2kx\end{pmatrix}.

(22)

The formal solution of
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}=k\bm{H}_{Z}\bm{\mathcal{Y}}
is then given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{Y}_{1}\\
\mathcal{Y}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{P}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{k\int_{-h}^{z}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\bm{H}_{Z}(\zeta)}\right\}\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(-h)=\frac{1}{\cosh kh}\mathcal{P}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{k\!\int_{-h}^{z}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,q(\zeta)\,\bm{C}(\zeta+h)}\right\}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(23)

where
\mathcal{P}
denotes the path ordering operator, which has to be applied since the matrix in the integrand does not commute when evaluated at two different depths,
(see, e.g.,
5
, §1)
. We will discuss the interpretation of the ordering operator in
Section

II.3
, and in
Section

IV
for a discretised domain and shear current, giving a natural approximation for numerical implementation. When there is a critical layer, i.e., when
U_{\gamma}(z_{c})=\omega/k
at some depth
z_{c}
, we shift the frequency off the real axis by a small amount
\omega\to\omega+\mathrm{i}\epsilon
to represent a minuscule viscosity
(
22
)
, and take the limit
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
after integration.

The solution for
\hat{G}_{-}(z)
and its derivative is given by
{\bm{\mathcal{X}}(z)=\bm{T}(z+h)\,\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(z)}
which gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{G}_{-}(z)&=\cosh k(z+h)\,\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z)+\sinh k(z+h)\mathcal{Y}_{2}(z),\\
\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime}(z)&=k\sinh k(z+h)\,\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z)+k\cosh k(z+h)\,\mathcal{Y}_{2}(z).\end{split}

(24)

When inserting the Green’s function evaluated at the surface into the dispersion (
17
), the roots, representing freely propagating wave modes, are given by the equation

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=

\displaystyle\left[\big(g+\Upsilon k^{2}\big)\,k-\sigma U_{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)\right]F(\bm{k},\sigma)

(25a)

with

\displaystyle F(\bm{k},\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{r+\tanh kh}{1+r\tanh kh};

(25b)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=r(\bm{k},\sigma)\equiv\frac{\mathcal{Y}_{1}(0)}{\mathcal{Y}_{2}(0)}.

(25c)

Equation

25
is the exact dispersion relation for arbitrary
U(z)
from which
\sigma(\bm{k})
follows. As we will discuss, taking
r(\bm{k},\sigma)=0
reproduces the known relation for a linearly varying current, while the asymptotic behaviour of
r
is analysed in Appendix
A
and
B
.

The fraction in (
25b
) can be rewritten as
F=\tanh k(h+\tilde{h})
, where
k\tilde{h}=\mathrm{artanh}\,r
. Therefore, a current with non-zero curvature gives
\tilde{h}\neq 0
, which has the role of an effective depth change as a function of shear. This is an intuitive interpretation in shallow and intermediate water (
kh\lesssim O(1)
and
|r|<1
).

II.2
Deep-water limit

The effective depth shift interpretation above is not applicable to deep water, and another form is needed. In the deep water limit,
kh\gg 1
, the hyperbolic functions behave as exponentials
\sinh kz,\cosh kz\sim e^{kz}
. Renormalising the path-ordered exponential yields a slightly modified solution (for details see Appendix
B
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{G}_{-}(z)&=e^{kz}\big(\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}(z)+\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}(z)\big),\\
\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime}(z)&=-ke^{kz}\big(\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}(z)-\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}(z)\big),\end{split}

(26)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}(z)=\mathcal{P}\exp\left\{\frac{k}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{z}\mathrm{d}\zeta\begin{pmatrix}-4-q(\zeta)&-q(\zeta)\\
q(\zeta)&q(\zeta)\end{pmatrix}\right\}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

The deep water limit of (
25
) gives the same dispersion as found by
26
, in which case

F(k,\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{kh\to\infty}}\frac{1+R}{1-R},

(28)

with

\displaystyle R=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{k}{2}\right)^{n}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\,e^{k\zeta_{1}}q_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\zeta_{2}\,e^{-k|\zeta_{2}-\zeta_{1}|}q_{2}\dots\int_{-\infty}^{0}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\zeta_{n}\,e^{-k|\zeta_{n}-\zeta_{n-1}|}q_{n}e^{k\zeta_{n}},

(29)

where we have used the shorthand
q_{j}\equiv q(\zeta_{j})
.

II.3
Dyson series representation

A potentially instructive form of (
23
) is obtained by casting the path-ordered integral in another form. We may expand the exponential in orders of
\bm{H}_{Z}(z)=q(z)\bm{C}(z+h)
, keeping the ordering

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(z)=\frac{1}{\cosh kh}\left[\bm{I}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{n}}{n!}\int_{-h}^{z}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\cdots\int_{-h}^{z}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta_{n}\mathcal{P}\{\bm{H}_{Z}(\zeta_{1})\cdots\bm{H}_{Z}(\zeta_{n})\}\right]\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(30)

The factor
1/n!
assures convergence for all finite
kh
. At each order
n
, the solution

{\bm{\mathcal{X}}^{(n)}(z)=\bm{T}(z+h)\bm{\mathcal{Y}}^{(n)}(z)}
is proportional to
e^{kh}
, asymptotically for
kh\gg 1
, which is balanced by the factor
1/\cosh kh
in the boundary condition
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(-h)
. We show this in Appendix
C
. The path-ordering operator places the largest coordinate
\zeta_{j}
to the left,
\mathcal{P}\bm{H}_{Z}(\zeta_{1})\bm{H}_{Z}(\zeta_{2})=\bm{H}_{Z}(\zeta_{i})\bm{H}_{Z}(\zeta_{j})
with
i,j\in\{1,2\}
, such that
\zeta_{i}>\zeta_{j}
. An alternative way to write (
30
) makes the path ordering explicit,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(z)=\frac{1}{\cosh kh}\left[\bm{I}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}k^{n}\int_{-h}^{z}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\int_{-h}^{\zeta_{1}}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta_{2}\cdots\int_{-h}^{\zeta_{n-1}}\!\!\mathrm{d}\zeta_{n}\,\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bm{H}_{Z}(\zeta_{j})\right]\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

Equations (
30
) and (
31
) do not require weak curvature, but readily yield a weak-curvature approximation when truncated, as we discuss in section
III.2
. It is useful for numerical evaluation of (
25
) since convergence is usually rapid in practical problems.

Curiously, equations (
30
) and (
31
) have the form of a Dyson series
(
7
)
, well known from quantum physics to describe many-particle interactions via multiple scattering, providing powerful Green’s function solutions for a wide range of problems
(
8
, e.g.)
able to tackle highly complex geometries
(
9
, e.g.)
and even describe the scattering of surface waves on turbulent eddies
(
25
)
, allowing the intuitive use of Feynman diagrams. Each term then represents, in analogy, increasing number of internal scatterings of curvature interacting with itself at different depths via the bottom; the second-order term, say, has integrand of form
q(\zeta_{1})\bm{C}(\zeta_{1}+h)\bm{C}(\zeta_{2}+h)q(\zeta_{2})
, with
\bm{C}
akin to a propagator of a ‘signal’ from
\zeta_{1}
to
-h
and thence to
\zeta_{2}
. Higher-order terms describe repeated scatterings, whereby curvature at one depth first generates a correction that is then acted upon again by curvature at shallower depths, as seen in the nested integrals. Note also that self-interactions (i.e.
\zeta_{1}=\zeta_{2}
) are prohibited by the nilpotency of the integrand,
\bm{C}(x)\bm{C}(x)=0
; one readily verifies that

Known analytical solutions and approximations

III.1
Exact analytical limits

The most classical case of a depth-varying current is that with constant shear, i.e.,
\bm{U}(z)=(U_{0}+Sz)\mathbf{e}_{U}

U_{0}
and vorticity

\bm{H}_{Z}=0

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}+\sigma S\cos\gamma\tanh kh-\omega_{0}^{2}=0,

(32)

with

\sigma=\omega-kU_{0}\cos\gamma

Secondly, we consider a class where

IV
Numerical implementation

In this section, we numerically evaluate
\sigma(\bm{k})
using our dispersion relation to validate it in cases with nonzero curvature, which pose additional mathematical challenges. In particular, the solution in (
23
) involves a path-ordered exponential, a generalisation of the ordinary exponential solution. The key difficulty arises because, for non-commuting matrices, the order in which terms are multiplied along the integration path affects the result, preventing a simple closed-form solution. There are many ways to evaluate our expression numerically, including Magnus expansions
(
3
)
and ordered-product schemes
(
5
)
; we focus here on the latter, since it is easy to implement.

To clarify the problem at hand, consider the following scalar problem,
{F^{\prime}(z)=A(z)\,F(z)}
, where the solution is readily given by
F(z)=F(-h)\exp[\int_{-h}^{z}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,A(\zeta)]
. In contrast, if the problem involves matrices,
\bm{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}(z)=\bm{A}(z)\bm{\mathcal{F}}(z)
where the elements of
\bm{A}
have different
z
-dependence, the solution is not simply the matrix exponential
\exp[\int_{-h}^{z}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\bm{A}(\zeta)]
, but one where the Taylor expansion is replaced by a series of nested integrals of
\bm{A}
involving matrix multiplications of
\bm{A}
with itself at different depths which do not in general commute and must be performed in the right order. The path-ordering operator ensures that the sequence of multiplications correctly reflects the underlying mathematics.

Alternatively to the Dyson series (
30
), the path-ordered exponential can be interpreted as the continuum limit of a discrete product of propagators—each corresponding to a thin layer of thickness
\Delta z=h/N

of the
z
domain. In this approach, the solution is constructed as an ordered product,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(0)&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\prod_{n^{*}=0}^{N-1}e^{k\,\bm{H}_{Z}(z_{n})\,\Delta z}\right)\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(-h)\\
&=\frac{1}{\cosh kh}\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{n^{*}=0}^{N-1}\bigg(\bm{I}+k\,\bm{H}_{Z}(z_{n})\,\Delta z\bigg)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(39)

where
z_{n}=n\Delta z-h
. Here, the product operator acts in reverse to preserve the correct multiplication order:
\prod_{n^{*}}A_{n}=A_{N-1}\dots A_{1}A_{0}
. This discrete viewpoint underpins our numerical implementation: we use (
39
) with
N
high enough to reach the desired tolerance and approximate
\bm{H}_{Z}(z_{n})
at the midpoint
\tfrac{1}{2}(z_{n}+z_{n+1})
. The last equality in (
39
) is obtained since the exponent in the infinitesimal propagator is nilpotent,
\bm{C}^{2}=0
. Finally, we employ a bisection method to solve for the root of
D(k,\omega)
with
M
refinements.

V
Conclusions

We have found a closed, implicit dispersion relation for linear surface waves on an arbitrary shear current. Unlike classical treatments that expand in global eigenmodes satisfying both the bottom and free-surface boundary conditions, we instead constructed the Green’s function from two fundamental solutions: one satisfying the bottom condition and the other the free-surface condition. As a result, the dispersion relation follows from the Wronskian, which involves only the bottom solution evaluated at the surface. This approach eliminates the eigenfunction from the formulation, allowing the exact dispersion to be written as a closed functional of the prescribed shear profile. The dispersion relation involves nested, path-ordered integrals and is equivalent to that by
26
in the deep-water limit. Curiously it may be expressed as a Dyson series expansion akin to multiple scattering in quantum physics with the curvature
U^{\prime\prime}
at different depths acting as “particles”.

To validate our approach, we showed that the dispersion relation reproduces known analytical solutions for constant vorticity and a class of exact solutions due to
24
, and connects to known approximations for weak shear and weak curvature. Additionally, we numerically implemented the dispersion relation and systematically tested it across a broad spectrum of velocity profiles, ranging from simple monotonic shears to flows with multiple inflection points and currents that mimic realistic scenarios. In every case, the computed dispersion curves were identical to those obtained using DIM
(
17
)
, limited only by numerical precision. This comprehensive agreement across diverse and challenging profiles underscores the robustness of our analytical approach and its applicability to a wide range of geophysical flow scenarios. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the analysis presented here provides a fully self-contained one-equation formal solution to the dispersion relation
\omega=\omega(k)
for linear surface waves on horizontally directed currents
\bm{U}(z)=[U(z),V(z),0]
with arbitrary vertical structure. The new solution can serve as a starting point for future analytical work, or provide an exact benchmark solution.

Appendix B
Deep-water limit

To obtain the deep-water limit, we return to (
19
) and introduce
\bm{T}_{\infty}(z)\equiv\bm{T}(z)\bm{V}
, where
\bm{V}
is a constant invertible matrix chosen to diagonalise
\bm{H}_{0}
. Since
\bm{T}^{\prime}(z)=k\bm{H}_{0}\bm{T}(z)
and
\bm{H}_{0}\bm{V}=\bm{V}\bm{\Lambda}
, we have
\bm{T}_{\infty}^{\prime}(z)=k\bm{T}_{\infty}(z)\bm{\Lambda}
. Choosing
\bm{V}
from the eigenvectors of
\bm{H}_{0}
, we write

\displaystyle\bm{V}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{\Lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(47)

With this choice, the new operator becomes

\displaystyle\bm{T}_{\infty}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}-e^{-kz}&e^{kz}\\
e^{-kz}&e^{kz}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{C}_{\infty}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-1&e^{2kz}\\
-e^{-2kz}&1\end{pmatrix},

(48)

where
\bm{C}_{\infty}
is defined by
q(z)\bm{C}_{\infty}(z)\equiv\bm{T}_{\infty}^{-1}(z)\bm{H}_{U}(z)\bm{T}_{\infty}(z)
, as before. We define the state

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{\infty}^{\prime}(z)=kq(z)\bm{C}_{\infty}(z)\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{\infty}(z)
. The next step is to renormalise
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{\infty}(z)
. Since only the ratio of the two components enters the dispersion relation through

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}(z)\equiv\bm{R}(z)\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{\infty}(z),\quad\bm{R}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}-e^{-2kz}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(49)

Substituting this into the evolution equation yields

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{k}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-4-q(z)&-q(z)\\
q(z)&q(z)\end{pmatrix}\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}(z).

(50)

The boundary condition becomes
\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}(-\infty)=(0,\,1)^{\mathsf{T}}
, so the formal solution can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}(z)=\mathcal{P}\exp\left\{\frac{k}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{z}\mathrm{d}\zeta\begin{pmatrix}-4-q(\zeta)&-q(\zeta)\\
q(\zeta)&q(\zeta)\end{pmatrix}\right\}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(51)

\bm{\mathcal{X}}(z)=\bm{T}_{\infty}(z)\bm{\mathcal{Y}}_{\infty}(z)

\mathcal{Y}_{\infty,1}=-e^{2kz}\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}

\mathcal{Y}_{\infty,2}=\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}

and
\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{-}(z)=e^{kz}\big(\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}(z)+\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}(z)\big),\qquad\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime}(z)=-ke^{kz}\big(\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}(z)-\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}(z)\big).

(52)

Finally, defining

, and ignoring common factors, we obtain
\hat{G}_{-}=(1+R)
and
\hat{G}_{-}^{\prime}=k(1-R)
, which is the same deep-water structure found by
26
. Note that
R
is
not
the deep-water limit of

.

Appendix C
Dyson series asymptote

The full solution for the Green’s function
\hat{G}_{-}(z)
and its derivative is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{X}}(z)=\bm{T}(z+h)\frac{1}{\cosh kh}\left[\bm{I}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}k^{n}\int_{-h}^{z}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\cdots\int_{-h}^{\zeta_{n-1}}\!\mathrm{d}\zeta_{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bm{H}_{Z}(\zeta_{j})\right]\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(53)

where

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bm{H}_{Z}(\zeta_{j})=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}q(\zeta_{j})\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bm{C}(\zeta_{j}+h)\right).

(54)

We note that
\bm{C}(\zeta+h)\propto e^{2kh}
, so that the
n
th term falsely appears
\propto e^{2nkh}
which would cause the series to diverge for
kh\gg 1
. However, it is readily shown from equation (
21
) that one can rewrite
\bm{C}(\zeta+h)=\bm{T}(-h)\bm{C}(\zeta)\bm{T}(h)
, where
\bm{T}(h)\bm{T}(-h)=\bm{I}
since
\bm{T}(a+b)=\bm{T}(a)\bm{T}(b)
and
\bm{T}(0)=\bm{I}
from equation (
20b
). Therefore, the integrand in equation (
53
) is proportional to

\displaystyle\bm{T}(z+h)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bm{C}(\zeta_{j}+h)=\bm{T}(z+h)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[\bm{T}(-h)\bm{C}(\zeta_{j})\bm{T}(h)\right]=\bm{T}(z)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bm{C}(\zeta_{j})\right)\bm{T}(h),

(55)

which is
\propto e^{kh}
, and the normalising prefactor
1/\cosh kh
ensures that
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
does not become exponentially large in the deep-water limit. The product of
\bm{C}

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bm{C}(\zeta_{j})=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\sinh k(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j+1})\right)\begin{pmatrix}-\sinh k\zeta_{1}\,\cosh k\zeta_{n}&-\sinh k\zeta_{1}\,\sinh k\zeta_{n}\\
\cosh k\zeta_{1}\,\cosh k\zeta_{n}&\cosh k\zeta_{1}\,\sinh k\zeta_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

(56)

Multiplying (

\bm{T}(z)
and from the right by
\bm{T}(h)(0,\,1)^{\mathsf{T}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{T}(z)&\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bm{C}(\zeta_{j})\bm{T}(h)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\sinh k(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j+1})\right)\sinh k(\zeta_{n}+h)\begin{pmatrix}\sinh k(z-\zeta_{1})\\
\cosh k(z-\zeta_{1})\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(57)

This expression shows explicitly how the apparent growth cancels. Expanding the hyperbolic functions in exponentials, we may rewrite the integrand as

\displaystyle\sinh k(\zeta_{n}+h)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\sinh k(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j+1})\right)\begin{pmatrix}\sinh k(z-\zeta_{1})\\
\cosh k(z-\zeta_{1})\end{pmatrix}

(58)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{k(\zeta_{n}+h)}}{2}\bigg(1-e^{-2k(\zeta_{n}+h)}\bigg)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{e^{k(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j+1})}}{2}\bigg(1-e^{-2k(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j+1})}\bigg)\right)\frac{e^{k(z-\zeta_{1})}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1-e^{-2k(z-\zeta_{1})}\\
1+e^{-2k(z-\zeta_{1})}\end{pmatrix}.

Since the ordered integrations impose
-h\leq\zeta_{n}\leq\zeta_{n-1}\leq\cdots\leq\zeta_{1}\leq z
, all arguments of the factors

\displaystyle e^{k(z-\zeta_{1})}e^{k(\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{2})}e^{k(\zeta_{2}-\zeta_{3})}\cdots e^{k(\zeta_{n-1}-\zeta_{n})}e^{k(\zeta_{n}+h)}=e^{k(z+h)}.

(59)

Therefore, the integrand in the

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{k^{n}}{\cosh kh}\bm{T}(z)\prod_{j=1}^{n}q(\zeta_{j})\bm{C}(\zeta_{j})\bm{T}(h)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\frac{k^{n}e^{kz}}{2^{n}}\frac{1-e^{-2k(\zeta_{n}+h)}}{1+e^{-2kh}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(1-e^{-2k(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j+1})}\right)\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}q(\zeta_{\ell})\begin{pmatrix}1-e^{-2k(z-\zeta_{1})}\\
1+e^{-2k(z-\zeta_{1})}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(60)
