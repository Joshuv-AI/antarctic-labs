Looking down the rabbit hole: Towards quantum optimal estimation of surface roughness
Quentin Muller, Tommaso Tufarelli, Mădălin Guţă, Katherine Inzani, Samanta Piano, Gerardo Adesso

Abstract
Surface roughness is an important quantity to many engineering and precision manufacturing disciplines. In this paper we investigate the problem of estimating the root-mean-square roughness of a sample by passive linear optical methods. By adopting quantum parameter estimation techniques, we determine the ultimate precision limits on roughness estimation. In particular, we show that the information on the first moment (mean height) and standard deviation (roughness) of the axial profile distribution of multiple incoherent point sources is bounded by a constant. While classical imaging techniques fail to achieve this bound, a quantum inspired imaging technique based on spatial mode demultiplexing is proven to be optimal for estimating the axial standard deviation. Combined with analogous recently investigated methods for estimating radial profiles, this can provide a powerful technique for measuring roughness of nearly smooth surface patches beyond the diffraction limit.



I
Introduction

Surface roughness plays a central role in diverse scientific and industrial contexts. In precision manufacturing, surface finish directly affects mechanical performance, wear, and lubrication properties
3
. In aerospace and automotive engineering, surface roughness influences friction, drag, and fatigue life
22
. In optics and photonics, nanometre-scale roughness of mirrors, gratings, or coatings determines scattering losses and limits device performance
7
. In semiconductor fabrication and nanotechnology, roughness control is critical for layer deposition and pattern transfer
36
. Roughness is also a critical quality metric in additive manufacturing and precision metrology, where it impacts dimensional tolerances and functional compliance
14
. Given this broad relevance, accurate measurement and characterization of surface roughness—and understanding the fundamental limits of such estimation—are of great fundamental and applied interest.

Rough surfaces can be thought of as “miniature mountain ranges,” with peaks and troughs forming a height distribution over the surface. A variety of metrics exist to characterize roughness
12
, but in this work we focus on the
root-mean-square height variation
(the standard deviation of the surface height profile). We denote this roughness parameter by
\sigma
, the square root of the second central moment of the height distribution:

\sigma=\sqrt{\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}^{2}}\,,

(1)

where

\theta_{j}=\int z^{j}dF(z)

(2)

is the
j^{\text{th}}
moment of the height distribution
F(z)
; in particular,
\theta_{1}
is the mean height and
\sigma^{2}=\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}^{2}
is the variance of the surface height profile.

Figure 1
:
A schematic displaying the focusing of a small profile of a rough surface through a lens. The image of the rough surface can then be attained via different measurement schemes.

Techniques for measuring the surface roughness can be separated into contact and non-contact methods
1
. A common contact technique is the use of a stylus profilometer — a diamond tipped stylus that is dragged across a rough surface to produce a varying voltage proportional to the height of the surface contour. However, such a contact technique may damage the surface and only yields
1D
information. As such, we turn our attention instead to non-contact imaging using optical sensors. In such an attempt to estimate the surface distribution, coherent light, usually in the form of a monochromatic plane wave
15
, is made incident upon the landscape, and the reflected light is then analyzed.
However, as the surface roughness becomes smaller and the relative heights and radial displacements of the peaks and troughs dip below the Rayleigh limit
19
, diffraction effects resulting from the focusing of the emitted light through some aperture or lens take place, leading to loss of information at the image plane. It becomes therefore an interesting metrological problem to devise more efficient ways of passively collecting and processing light emitted from rough surfaces in the sub-diffraction limit, in order to improve both the angular and axial resolution for reconstructing relevant surface parameters.

Here we address this problem using methods from
quantum metrology
. Our analysis is inspired by the recent body of work that reformulated the task of resolving spatially displaced point sources as a quantum parameter estimation problem
24
;
25
;
20
;
2
;
26
;
16
;
8
. These groundbreaking papers not only surpassed the centuries-old Rayleigh limit through a quantum-inspired measurement technique known as spatial mode demultiplexing (SPADE), but also placed the notion of resolution on a more rigorous footing by connecting it to the minimum achievable error in estimating the defining parameters of a source distribution; namely, the geometric moments. Much of the work carried out since in this realm has used either
radially
distributed light sources
27
(i.e., displaced within a plane parallel to the image plane) or at most two point sources distributed
axially

16
;
35
(i.e., displaced along a line perpendicular to the image plane).

In this paper we expand on the previously considered cases by treating the problem of an
arbitrary
number of discrete
axially
distributed point sources: an optical “rabbit hole”. We consider this problem of utmost importance in our journey towards characterizing uncertainty in estimating light sources distributed both axially and radially as in a complete discretized surface profile. In particular, complementing existing results for moments estimation in radial source distributions
34
;
25
;
28
, here we compute the ultimate limits on the information available in estimating the first moment — mean height — and (square root of) the second central moment — roughness — of an axial distribution of incoherent point sources. We find that classical direct imaging techniques fail to reach these limits, and we show the quantum-inspired measurement technique SPADE (specifically decomposing output modes in the Laguerre-Gauss basis) to be optimal in fulfilling the considered estimation problem.

This paper is partitioned into five sections. In Section
II
we give an overview of relevant quantum metrology methods followed by a brief explanation of quantum imaging theory along with a description of the theoretical model used in this analysis. In Section
III
we present our derivation of the fundamental bound on the information available for the estimation of root-mean-square roughness. In Section
IV
we show the failure of classical direct imaging techniques to reach this bound as opposed to the success of SPADE measurements to achieve optimality for the task of roughness estimation. Finally we discuss our results and future outlook in Section
V
.

Theoretical background

II.1
Quantum Metrology Theory

For clarity we divide this subsection into two parts: the first part reviews the theory of quantum estimation for finite-dimensional parameters while the second part deals with the the semi-parametric formalism
29
;
23
;
28
. The latter becomes useful for problems involving a very high-dimensional (and potentially infinite-dimensional) space of parameters.

II.1.1
Quantum parameter estimation and the Fisher information

Quantum estimation deals with the following problem: given a quantum system whose state
\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}
depends smoothly on an unknown parameter set

\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})^{T}\in\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the task is to estimate the
parameters by measuring the system
17
. Since different measurements may be mutually incompatible and convey different information about the parameters, the key questions are which measurements are more suitable for estimating

\bm{\lambda}
and what is the optimal estimation precision.

A measurement is described
by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)

\{\Pi_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
, where each

\Pi_{\omega}
is a positive semidefinite operator acting on the system’s
Hilbert space and the operators satisfy the completeness relation

\int_{\Omega}\Pi_{\omega}\,d\omega=\mathbb{I}
, where
d\omega
is a reference measure on
\Omega
.
The probability density of the measurement outcome

\omega\in\Omega
, given the underlying parameters

\bm{\lambda}
of the model, is then given by the Born rule

p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega)=\text{Tr}[\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}\Pi_{\omega}].

(3)

The collection of all such smoothly parametrized probability density functions
constitutes a statistical model,

\mathcal{P}=\{\,p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega):\bm{\lambda}\in\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\},

(4)

which will serve as the playing field for all statistical calculations.
Typically, a single measurement is insufficient to estimate
\bm{\lambda}
to the desired precision. Therefore, the experimenter needs to perform repeated measurements on identically prepared systems and estimate the parameter from the collection of outcomes, by using a general method such as maximum likelihood, or more specific techniques tailored to the given model.

The
Cramér-Rao bound
(CRB) and its quantum counterpart provide a first hand answer to the question posed in the beginning of this section. Given an unbiased estimator
\bm{\hat{\lambda}}
(i.e.
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{\hat{\lambda}})=\bm{\lambda}
), its covariance matrix is lower bounded as follows

\displaystyle\text{Cov}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\bm{\hat{\lambda}}]\geq\mathcal{I}(\bm{\lambda};\{\Pi_{\omega}\})^{-1}\geq\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})^{-1}\,.

(5)

The leftmost inequality tells us that the covariance matrix
\text{Cov}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\bm{\hat{\lambda}}]:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\lambda}}\left[(\hat{\bm{\lambda}}-{\bm{\lambda}})^{T}(\hat{\bm{\lambda}}-{\bm{\lambda}})\right]

is bounded below by the inverse of the classical Fisher information matrix (CFIm)
\mathcal{I}(\bm{\lambda},\{\Pi_{\omega}\})
, whose elements are given by

[\mathcal{I}(\bm{\lambda};\{\Pi_{\omega}\})]_{ij}=\int\frac{\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega)}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\frac{\partial p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega)}{\partial\lambda_{j}}\frac{d\omega}{p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega)}.

(6)

To understand the CFIm more intuitively, we can think of the statistical model
\mathcal{P}
as a submanifold of the space of distributions on
\Omega
, and the map
\bm{\lambda}:\Lambda\to\mathcal{P}
as a coordinates map. The log-likelihood random variable is defined as

\ell_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega):=\log p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega)
and its differential
d\ell_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega)
is a one-form with components given by the
score functions

S_{\bm{\lambda},i}(\omega):=\partial\ell_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega)/\partial\lambda_{i}
. Given a tangent vector
\bm{v}=(v_{1},\dots v_{n})
, the corresponding directional score is

S_{\bm{\lambda},\bm{v}}=d\ell(\bm{\lambda},\omega)[\bm{v}]
, and is an element of

L^{2}(\Omega,p_{\bm{\lambda}})
with expectation zero,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\lambda}}(S_{\bm{\lambda},\bm{v}})=0
. The Fisher information can be seen as a Riemannian metric whose components in the coordinate system
\bm{\lambda}
are

[\mathcal{I}(\bm{\lambda};\{\Pi_{\omega}\})]_{ij}=\langle S_{i},S_{j}\rangle_{\bm{\lambda}}\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\lambda}}[S_{i}S_{j}].

(7)

Assuming that the CFIm is strictly positive, the score functions are linearly independent and form a basis in the co-tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{\lambda}}
at
\bm{\lambda}
. Given this interpretation of the CFIm as a metric on the probability manifold, we can write the infinitesimal distance between two points in terms of the Fisher information metric as

ds^{2}_{p}=d\bm{\lambda}^{T}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\lambda};\{\Pi_{\omega}\})d\bm{\lambda}.

(8)

The rightmost inequality in (
5
) is the
quantum
Cramér-Rao bound (QCRB) which features the inverse of the quantum Fisher information matrix (QFIm)

\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})
and is independent of the measurement. Similarly to the CFIm, we can also think of the QFIm in a geometric fashion. Here we consider a manifold
\mathcal{S}
consisting of density operators as a subset of the bounded operators
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,

\mathcal{S}=\{\rho\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}):\rho=\rho^{\dagger}\geq 0,\text{Tr}[\rho]=1\}.

(9)

We can likewise define the infinitesimal distance between two nearby quantum states on the density operator manifold as,

\displaystyle ds^{2}_{\rho}=\max_{\{\Pi_{\omega}\}}d\bm{\lambda}^{T}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\lambda};\{\Pi_{\omega}\})d\bm{\lambda}\equiv d\bm{\lambda}^{T}\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})d\bm{\lambda},

(10)

where the QFIm
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})
satisfies the matrix inequality
I(\bm{\lambda};\{\Pi_{\omega}\})\leq\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})
for all POVMs
\{\Pi_{\omega}\}
.

Explicitly, the QFIm can be written in terms of the
symmetric logarithmic
derivative
(SLD) operators
\mathcal{L}_{i}
, introduced by Holevo
10
as the non-commutative counterparts to the score functions
S_{i}
, and defined by the Lyapunov equation

\frac{\partial\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}\mathcal{L}_{i}+\mathcal{L}_{i}\rho_{\bm{\lambda}})\,,

(11)

so that the matrix elements of the QFIm are

[\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})]_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\,\text{Tr}\left[\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}\{\mathcal{L}_{i},\mathcal{L}_{j}\}\right],

(12)

with
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
the anticommutator.
To understand (
12
), recall that, given a manifold of density operators such as
\mathcal{S}
, one can quantify the distinguishability between any two such operators by means of the Bures distance
6
;
11
,

\displaystyle d_{B}^{2}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})

\displaystyle=2(1-\text{Tr}[(\sqrt{\rho_{1}}\rho_{2}\sqrt{\rho_{1}})^{1/2}])

(13)

\displaystyle=2\left(1-\sqrt{F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}\right)\,,

where
F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
denotes the fidelity between the two states
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}

30
;
1
.
It was shown by Braunstein and Caves
5
(building on the work of Hübner
11
) that the Bures distance between
the nearby states
\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}
and

\rho_{\bm{\lambda}+d\bm{\lambda}}
admits the expansion

\displaystyle d_{B}^{2}\left(\rho_{\bm{\lambda}},\rho_{\bm{\lambda}+d\bm{\lambda}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\text{Tr}\left[\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}\{\mathcal{L}_{i},\mathcal{L}_{j}\}\right]d\lambda_{i}d\lambda_{j}

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\,d\bm{\lambda}^{T}\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})d\bm{\lambda}=\frac{1}{4}ds_{\rho}^{2},

where
\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})
is the QFIm
defined by Eq. (
12
).

We now restrict our attention to a special class of quantum models, which are obtained by applying a quantum channel depending on the unknown parameter
\bm{\lambda}
to a given initial probe state.
Let
\Gamma_{\bm{\lambda}}
be a quantum channel (completely
positive trace-preserving map) with Kraus representation

\{K_{j}(\bm{\lambda})\}_{j=1}^{N}
such that

\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}=\Gamma_{\bm{\lambda}}(\rho_{0})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{j}(\bm{\lambda})\,\rho_{0}\,K_{j}^{\dagger}(\bm{\lambda}).

(15)

For an infinitesimal parameter shift

\bm{\lambda}\to\bm{\lambda}+d\bm{\lambda}
,
one can define a
metrology matrix

33
as the matrix
M(\bm{\lambda},d\bm{\lambda})
with elements

[M(\bm{\lambda},d\bm{\lambda})]_{ij}=\text{Tr}[K_{i}(\bm{\lambda})^{\dagger}K_{j}(\bm{\lambda}+d\bm{\lambda})\rho_{0}].

(16)

It then follows
33

that the fidelity between the nearby
states
\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}
and

\rho_{\bm{\lambda}+d\bm{\lambda}}
can be written compactly as the trace norm of this matrix (in leading order with respect to
d\bm{\lambda}
) ,

\displaystyle F(\rho_{\bm{\lambda}},\rho_{\bm{\lambda}+d\bm{\lambda}})=\left\lVert M(\bm{\lambda},d\bm{\lambda})\right\rVert_{1}+o(d\bm{\lambda}^{2}).

(17)

Comparing this with Eqs. (
13
), (
14
), and using

\displaystyle F(\rho_{\bm{\lambda}},\rho_{\bm{\lambda}+d\bm{\lambda}})=1-\tfrac{1}{8}\,d\bm{\lambda}^{T}\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})d\bm{\lambda},

(18)

(to the leading order), one obtains an elegant representation of the QFIm:

d\bm{\lambda}^{T}\,\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})\,d\bm{\lambda}=8\bigl(1-\|M(\bm{\lambda},d\bm{\lambda})\|_{1}\bigr)+o(d\bm{\lambda}^{2}).

(19)

This form is particularly useful when the parameter dependence enters through a known
dynamical evolution or transfer function, as it allows the QFIm to be computed
directly from the derivatives of the Kraus operators without explicitly
evaluating the SLDs.

II.1.2
Classical and quantum semi-parametric estimation

In certain metrological scenarios, one is interested in precisely estimating only a small
subset of all the parameters encoded in a probe state. Let the full parameter
vector be partitioned as
\bm{\lambda}=(\beta,\bm{\eta})
, where the scalar

\beta
is the parameter of interest and the remaining components

\bm{\eta}
play the role of nuisance parameters.
If the full (classical or quantum) Fisher information matrix is invertible, then we can bound the error on estimating the parameter of interest
\beta
by means of a suitable reparametrization of the CRB (
5
)
17
:

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\hat{\beta}]

\displaystyle\geq(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\lambda};\{\Pi_{\omega}\})^{-1}\bm{\partial\beta}

(20)

\displaystyle\geq(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})^{-1}\bm{\partial\beta}\equiv\mathcal{V}^{\rm Q}_{\beta},

where
\hat{\beta}
is an unbiased estimator and
\bm{\partial\beta}
is a vector of derivatives
[\bm{\partial\beta}]_{i}=\partial\beta/\partial\lambda_{i}
with respect to the parameter set
\bm{\lambda}
.
However, when the original parameter set
\bm{\lambda}
is high-dimensional or even
infinite-dimensional, the method in (
20
) becomes
intractable. Invoking the theory of
semi-parametric estimation
offers a powerful way forward.

Consider the scenario where the measurement

\{\Pi_{\omega}\}
is performed repeatedly on
m
quantum samples

\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}
resulting in independent outcomes

\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m}
drawn from
p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega)
. Let
\bm{\lambda}_{0}=(\beta_{0},\bm{\eta}_{0})
be a fixed parameter value and assume that the estimator
\hat{\beta}_{m}=\hat{\beta}_{m}(\omega_{1},\dots\omega_{m})
is (locally) unbiased around
\bm{\lambda}_{0}
and can be approximated as an empirical average

\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta_{0}\approx\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varphi(\omega_{i})

(21)

for some function
\varphi
called the
influence function
(IF)
29
.
Intuitively, the IF quantifies how much each measurement outcome “pushes” the
estimator away from the reference value
\beta_{0}
. The local unbiasedness requirement translates
into the first order expansion

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\lambda}}[\varphi]=\beta-\beta_{0}+o(\beta-\beta_{0}).

(22)

and in particular,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}[\varphi]=0
.
By the central limit theorem at
\bm{\lambda}_{0}
, the LHS of Eq. (
21
)
converges in distribution to the centered normal distribution

\sqrt{m}\,(\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta_{0})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\,\|\varphi\|_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}^{2}),

(23)

where
\|\varphi\|_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}[\varphi^{2}]

so that

\lim_{m\to\infty}m\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}[\hat{\beta}_{m}]=\|\varphi\|_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}^{2}.

(24)

Thus, the asymptotic error of any estimator of the form (
21
) is determined entirely by
the norm of its IF.

This viewpoint leads naturally to a geometric picture.
Let

\mathcal{H}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}:=\{g(\omega):\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}[g]=0,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}[g^{2}]<\infty\}

(25)

denote the space of zero-mean,
square-integrable (complex) functions of the data, and equip it with the Hilbert space structure defined by the inner product

\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}[\overline{g_{1}}g_{2}]
.
The norm in this space is the variance,
\|g\|_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}^{2}=\text{Var}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}[g]
.
The score functions

S_{\bm{\lambda}_{0},i}(\omega):=\left.\frac{\partial[\log p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega)]}{\partial{\lambda_{i}}}\right|_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}

(26)

span the co-tangent space

\mathcal{T}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}\,=\text{span}\{S_{1},\dots,S_{n}\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}.

(27)

From Eq. (
22
) we find that the set of
IFs for estimating the target parameter
\beta
is

\Phi_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}=\{\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}:\langle\varphi,S_{i}\rangle_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}=\partial\beta/\partial\lambda_{i},\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,n\}.

(28)

According to Eq. (
24
), for each such IF the rescaled asymptotic mean square error of the corresponding estimator

\hat{\beta}_{m}
is
\|\varphi\|_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}^{2}
.
The smallest possible error is achieved by the
efficient influence function

\varphi_{\rm eff}
given by the unique orthogonal
projection of any
\varphi\in\Phi
onto the co-tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}
,

\varphi_{\rm eff}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{T}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}}(\varphi),

(29)

such that

\left\lVert\varphi_{\rm eff}\right\rVert_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}^{2}=\min_{\varphi\in\Phi}\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}^{2}.

(30)

By setting
\varphi_{\rm eff}=\sum_{j}c_{j}S_{j}
and solving the constraints (
28
), we get

\bm{c}_{\rm eff}=\mathcal{I}(\bm{\lambda};\{\Pi_{\omega}\})^{-1}\bm{\partial\beta}
, and find that the corresponding estimator attains the classical Cramér-Rao bound — first inequality in (
20
) — in an asymptotic sense,

\lim_{m\to\infty}m{\rm Var}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}[\hat{\beta}_{m}]=\|\varphi_{\rm eff}\|_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}^{2}=(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}\mathcal{I}(\bm{\lambda};\{\Pi_{\omega}\})^{-1}\bm{\partial\beta}.

(31)

Moving to a more general non-parametric setting where the parameter
\bm{\lambda}
is not finite-dimensional, the approach via the Fisher information is problematic, while
the IF analysis can still provide the optimal estimation error for one-dimensional (and more generally finite-dimensional) parameters
\beta
.
The key point is the identification of the co-tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{\lambda}}
as the closure in

\mathcal{H}_{\bm{\lambda}}
of the span of score functions obtained by constructing “smooth” one-parameter sub-models passing through
\bm{\lambda}
. Influence functions can be defined by generalizing the condition (
22
) or (
28
) appropriately, and have a unique efficient IF projection onto the co-tangent space. The leftmost equality in (
31
) holds for the efficient IF even if the Fisher information interpretation is not available. For more details on semi-parametric estimation for infinite-dimensional models we refer to
29
;
4
;
23
;
28
.

Until now the measurement has been fixed and we
analyzed an essentially classical estimation problem for the model
p_{\bm{\lambda}}(\omega)={\rm Tr}(\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}\Pi_{\omega})
. Fortunately, the construction can be lifted to the fully quantum level
23
, allowing us to identify optimal measurement and estimation procedures which achieve
the bound
\mathcal{V}^{\rm Q}_{\beta}
in (
20
). Briefly, we consider the (real) Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}^{Q}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}:=\{A:{\rm Tr}(\rho_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}A)=0,{\rm Tr}(\rho_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}A^{2})<\infty\}

(32)

with inner product

\langle A,B\rangle_{\bm{\lambda_{0}}}:=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(\rho_{\bm{\lambda_{0}}}\{A.B\})

where
A,B
are (possibly unbounded) selfadjoint operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
The co-tangent space is

\mathcal{T}^{Q}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}:=\text{span}\{\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{H}^{Q}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}

(33)

where
\mathcal{L}_{i}
are the SLD (quantum score) operators defined in
Eq. (
11
). An influence operator is an element of

\Phi^{Q}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}:=\{H\in\mathcal{H}^{Q}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}:\langle H,\mathcal{L}_{i}\rangle_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}=\partial\beta/\partial\lambda_{i},\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n\}.

(34)

By repeatedly measuring an influence operator
H
on
m
copies of

\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}
, we can construct an average estimator
\hat{\beta}_{m}
as in (
21
) whose rescaled asymptotic error is

\|H\|_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}^{2}
(for
\bm{\lambda}
close to
\bm{\lambda}_{0}
). As in the classical case, the smallest error is achieved by the efficient influence operator
H_{\rm eff}
which is the unique projection of any influence operator onto
\mathcal{T}^{Q}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}

H_{\rm eff}:={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{T}^{Q}_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}}(H)

(35)

This can be computed explicitly as
H_{\rm eff}=\sum h_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i}
with
\bm{h}:=\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda}_{0})^{-1}\bm{\partial\beta}
and then we obtain

\lim_{m\to\infty}m{\rm Var}(\hat{\beta}_{m})=\|H_{\rm eff}\|_{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}^{2}=\bm{\partial\beta}^{T}\mathcal{K}(\bm{\lambda})^{-1}\bm{\partial\beta},

(36)

which shows the achievability of the scalar-parameter bound (
20
) and provides the semi-parametric framework for quantum estimation with
arbitrarily many nuisance parameters.

Imaging theory

discrete light sources displaced axially and radially from the focal point of the lens characterized by a point spread function

. The incoming field is then measured using an appropriate measurement scheme (represented as the field of cameras in the image) yielding data in the representation
\zeta

The general model we consider in this paper is illustrated in Figure
2(a)
and consists of single photon emitters at positions
(x,y,z)\equiv(\bm{R},z)
near the object plane, distributed according to an unknown probability distribution
F
, and a lens that focuses the point sources onto the imaging plane with radial coordinate
\bm{r}
, where a measurement takes place.

A significant part of this paper involves understanding the optimal information that we can extract through different measurement strategies. To this end, let us describe our imaging model in more detail. Like much of the literature
27
, we assume weak sources. That is, the state of the focused light holds significant contributions only from the zero photon state and the single photon state. With this approximation, we are able to treat a photon in the imaging plane that originates from the focus of the imaging system as the pure state

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}{\rangle}=\int\psi(\bm{r})|\bm{r}{\rangle}d\bm{r}.

(37)

Here
\psi(\bm{r})
is the
point spread function
(PSF) of the imaging system and describes how the field emitted from a single point source in focus appears after passing through the lens. One can see from (
37
) that the PSF gives a weighting to the continuous distribution of the states representing the creation of a single photon at position
\bm{r}
in the imaging plane,
|\bm{r}{\rangle}
. If an emitter is displaced from focus then this will have effects on their images. The state of a photon at the imaging plane originating from an emitter displaced by coordinates
(\bm{R},z)
can be written as

\displaystyle|\psi_{\bm{R},z}{\rangle}

\displaystyle=e^{-(x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}+izG)}|\psi_{0}{\rangle}

(38)

\displaystyle=\int\psi(\bm{r}-\bm{R},z)|\bm{r}{\rangle}d\bm{r},

where

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{r}-{\bm{R},z})

\displaystyle\equiv e^{-(x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}+izG)}\psi(\bm{r}).

(39)

The generators
P_{x}=-i\partial_{x}
and
P_{y}=-i\partial_{y}
account for the radial displacements of the source
\bm{R}=(x,y)
and radially displace the PSF in the imaging plane without deforming it. The generator
G
however, induces broadening as well as a complex phase profile in the PSF, due to the source being “out of focus” by an amount
z
. Implicitly,
G
is defined via the paraxial wave equation — to which our PSF is a solution,

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}=-iG\psi\equiv-i\frac{1}{2k}\nabla^{2}_{\perp}\psi,

(40)

where
\nabla^{2}_{\perp}=\partial^{2}/\partial x^{2}+\partial^{2}/\partial y^{2}
represents the Laplacian only in the transverse plane and
k
represents the wave number of the field. In this work we will consider distributions of sources
F(\bm{R},z)
and thus the state of a photon at the imaging plane can be written as the mixture,

\rho=\int|\psi_{\bm{R},z}{\rangle}\langle\psi_{\bm{R},z}|dF(\bm{R},z).

(41)

The PSFs we use to exemplify our results throughout this work amount to Gaussian beams. We content ourselves with such a representative choice due to the similarities between the Gaussian beam, originating from a “Gaussian aperture”, and the Airy beam which is the exact solution to the circular aperture. The general form of a normalized Gaussian beam at the image plane is

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{r}-\bm{R},z)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\omega(z)}e^{-\frac{|\bm{r}-\bm{R}|^{2}}{\omega(z)^{2}}}e^{\frac{-ik|\bm{r}-\bm{R}|^{2}}{2R(z)}}.

(42)

Here we have defined

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R(z)}=\frac{z}{z^{2}+z_{R}^{2}},

(43)

where the Rayleigh range
z_{R}=k\omega_{0}^{2}/2
is a scaling parameter that describes how the beam width, defined as

\displaystyle\omega(z)=\omega_{0}\sqrt{1+\frac{z^{2}}{z_{R}^{2}}},

(44)

changes with distance from its value
\omega_{0}
at focus. In what follows we set
\omega_{0}=1
with no loss of generality.

A significant portion of this paper is devoted to comparing the optimality of two different manners of measuring the incoming light field. The projectors representing these schemes are denoted by
\{\Pi_{\zeta}\}\equiv\{|\zeta{\rangle}\langle\zeta|\}
where
\zeta
denotes the measure representing the result of a particular measurement. As described in Section
II.1
, the probability of obtaining
\zeta
by measuring the system in the state
\rho
in the
|\zeta{\rangle}
basis is given by

f(\zeta)=\text{Tr}\left[\rho|\zeta{\rangle}\langle\zeta|\right]=\int H(\zeta;\bm{R},z)dF(\bm{R},z),

(45)

where
H(\zeta;\bm{R},z)
represents the probability of measuring the field
|\psi{\rangle}
in the basis spanned by
|\zeta{\rangle}
,

H(\zeta;\bm{R},z)=|\langle\zeta|\psi_{\bm{R},z}\rangle|^{2}.

(46)

The two measurement schemes we compare in this work are
direct imaging
and
spatial mode demultiplexing
(SPADE). Direct imaging is an intensity measurement on the image plane, and can be implemented in practice via pixel-by-pixel photon counting, much like a standard camera would do. Here
\zeta\equiv\bm{r}
corresponds to a position on the photosensitive screen (pixel basis). On the other hand, a SPADE setup
24
decomposes the incident light field into a set of orthonormal modes before measuring the intensity in each mode. The modes’ wavefunctions must have known parity and be real valued
20
. In SPADE, the measurement basis label
\zeta\equiv q
indicates which transverse electromagnetic mode we are measuring
21
. SPADE can be thought of intuitively as “Taylor series in action”. When a point source is displaced
radially
from focus, its PSF displaces also. Thus one can extract more information by measuring in a basis which also captures this displacement. For example, if we consider a radially displaced Gaussian PSF, we find that the first few terms in a Taylor expansion of the PSF correspond to the Hermite-Gauss basis
24
. For
axial
displacements, the intuition is slightly less obvious. In this case, the PSF doesn’t displace but rather distorts itself based on how out of focus the point source is.

In the remainder of this paper, we focus on a reduced case of imaging a tiny surface patch, akin to a “rabbit hole”, see Figure
2(b)
. In this model we consider
only
sources displaced axially — that is towards or away from the observer along the optical axis but not in the plane tangential to the lens.
At the imaging plane, the Gaussian beam thus reduces to

\psi(\bm{r},z)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\omega(z)}e^{\frac{-r^{2}}{\omega(z)^{2}}}e^{\frac{-ikr^{2}}{2R(z)}}.

(47)

and Eqs. (
45
) and (
46
) reduce to

f(\zeta)=\int H(\zeta;z)dF(z),

(48)

and

H(\zeta;z)=|\langle\zeta|\psi_{z}\rangle|^{2}.

(49)

III
Ultimate limits: Computing

the Quantum Fisher Information

In this section we compute the ultimate precision limits on estimating the first and second moments of the axial distribution of an arbitrary discrete number
N
of weak incoherent point sources. This includes in particular a tight upper bound on the information achievable in roughness estimation for the reduced surface metrology problem described in Section
II.2
and illustrated in Figure
2(b)
. The state of the system (
41
) as a function of the source displacements
\bm{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N})^{T}
can be described using the Kraus representation for the channel corresponding to the PSF propagation,

\rho_{\bm{z}}=\sum_{j}{K}_{j}(\bm{z})\rho_{0}K_{j}(\bm{z})^{\dagger},

(50)

where
\rho_{0}:=|\psi_{0}{\rangle}\langle\psi_{0}|
with
|\psi_{0}{\rangle}
defined in (
37
), and the Kraus operators are of the form

K_{j}(\bm{z})=\sqrt{p_{j}}e^{-iz_{j}G},

(51)

with
p_{j}
the relative intensity of the
j^{\text{th}}
source, and
G
given by Eq. (
40
).

The metrology matrix (
16
) is

\displaystyle[M(\bm{z},d\bm{z})]_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[K_{i}(\bm{z})^{\dagger}K_{j}(\bm{z}+d\bm{z})\rho_{0}\right]

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle K_{i}(\bm{z})\psi_{0}|K_{j}(\bm{z}+d\bm{z})\psi_{0}\rangle

As we are interested only in axial displacements in the sub-diffraction regime, we assume

|z_{j}|\ll 1
and in the limit

\bm{z}\to\bm{0}
we have

\lim_{\bm{z}\to\bm{0}}\,[M(\bm{z},d\bm{z})]_{ij}=[M(\bm{0},d\bm{z})]_{ij}=\sqrt{p_{i}p_{j}}\langle\psi_{0}|e^{-idz_{j}G}\psi_{0}\rangle

(53)

so that

[M(\bm{0},d\bm{z})^{\dagger}M(\bm{0},d\bm{z})]_{ij}=\bar{c}_{i}c_{j},

(54)

where
c_{j}:=\sqrt{p}_{j}\langle\psi_{0}|e^{-idz_{j}G}\psi_{0}\rangle
. Therefore

\displaystyle\|M(\bm{0},d\bm{z})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\bm{c}\|=\sqrt{\sum_{j}|c_{j}|^{2}}

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}\left(1-dz_{j}^{2}\Delta_{0}G^{2}+o(d\bm{z}^{2})\right)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}p_{j}dz_{j}^{2}\Delta_{0}G^{2}

where
\Delta_{0}G^{2}:={\langle}G^{2}{\rangle}_{0}-{\langle}G{\rangle}_{0}^{2}
.

Using Eq. (
19
), we find that for very small small distributions around the origin (
\bm{z}\approx\bm{0}
)
the QFIm is, in the leading order, an
N\times N
diagonal matrix

\mathcal{K}(\bm{z}=\bm{0})=4\Delta_{0}G^{2}\times{\rm{diag}}(p_{1},\dots,p_{N}).

(56)

In particular, for sources of equal intensity the QFIm is proportional to the identity,

\mathcal{K}(\bm{z}=\bm{0})=4\Delta_{0}G^{2}\mathbb{I}_{N}/N
.

While this result provides important insight into the axial estimation problem, the use of source coordinates
\bm{z}
as parameters makes it hard to extrapolate to more general models involving continuous source distributions. In addition, our interest is in estimating the surface roughness which is naturally related to a characterization in terms of moments. For these reasons we will recast the above result in terms of this alternative parametrization.
Let
\bm{\theta}:=\{\theta_{l}\}_{l=1}^{N}
be the axial moments given by

\displaystyle\theta_{l}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}z_{i}^{l}.

(57)

Then the inverse QFIm in the

\bm{\theta}
parametrization is

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\bm{\theta})^{-1}=J^{T}\mathcal{K}(\bm{z})^{-1}J

(58)

where
J
is the Jacobian

J_{ij}=\frac{\partial\theta_{j}}{\partial z_{i}}=jp_{i}z_{i}^{j-1},

(59)

with
\theta_{0}:=1
. By inverting (
56
)
we find that for

|\bm{z}|\ll 1

[\mathcal{K}(\bm{\theta})^{-1}]_{ij}\approx\frac{ij}{4\Delta_{0}G^{2}}\sum_{k}p_{k}z_{k}^{i+j-2}=\frac{ij\theta_{i+j-2}}{4\Delta_{0}G^{2}}.

(60)

According to (
36
), the diagonal elements of (
60
) are the asymptotic errors of the corresponding moments. Since

\theta_{j}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{j})
, where
\Delta
is the “image size” a.k.a. feature depth (
\Delta\propto\max_{j\neq k}|z_{j}-z_{k}|
), this means that the
moments of order
j\geq 2
can be estimated with errors

\mathcal{V}^{\rm Q}_{\theta_{j}}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2j-2})
, while the mean
\mu\equiv\theta_{1}
has error

\mathcal{V}^{\rm Q}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{4\Delta_{0}G^{2}}

(61)

which does not vanish in the sub-diffraction limit
\Delta\to 0
.

Since in this paper we focus on the problem of estimating the surface roughness encoded in the standard deviation (
1
)

\sigma\equiv\tilde{\theta}_{2}=\sqrt{\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}^{2}},

we will ignore higher-order root central moments
\tilde{\theta}_{j>2}
(such as those characterizing skewness and kurtosis). Using (
60
) and

\frac{\partial\tilde{\theta}_{2}}{\partial\theta_{1}}=-\frac{\theta_{1}}{\tilde{\theta}_{2}},\quad\frac{\partial\tilde{\theta}_{2}}{\partial\theta_{2}}=\frac{1}{2\tilde{\theta_{2}}},

(62)

we compute the estimation error for the standard deviation as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\rm Q}_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{4\Delta_{0}G^{2}}.

(63)

Equations (
61
) and (
63
) are the first main results of this paper. They show that, in the limit of small (sub-wavelength) displacements, the ultimate errors for the estimation of both centroid (mean height) and standard deviation (root-mean-square “roughness”) of a distribution of
N
axially displaced weak incoherent sources are
constant
and equal to
1/(4\Delta_{0}G^{2})
. Explicitly, given that for a Gaussian beam (
42
) the variance of the generator
G
is given by
\Delta_{0}G^{2}=1/(4z_{R}^{2})
, the errors for both the mean height and the “roughness” parameter can be written more practically as

\mathcal{V}^{\rm Q}_{\mu}=\mathcal{V}^{\rm Q}_{\sigma}=z_{R}^{2}.

(64)

Before proceeding onto the next section we note that these results nicely reproduce and generalize other results in the literature, computed in special cases using fewer sources. For example, in
16
, the QFI for the estimation of the axial separation of two incoherent sources
|z_{2}-z_{1}|
is found to be
\Delta_{0}G^{2}
(see also
32
;
18
;
31
;
8
;
9
). Transforming this result to our coordinate system, where for two sources the axial standard deviation would be defined as
\sigma=|z_{2}-z_{1}|/2
, we recover
\mathcal{K}(\sigma)=4\Delta_{0}G^{2}
. Here, we have shown that this expression holds for an arbitrary number
N
of sources in the sub-diffraction limit. Our analysis also complements existing studies focused on estimating moments of the radial distribution (in the
x
and/or
y
directions) of non-axially displaced incoherent point sources
2
;
34
;
25
;
28
. Although our model considered a finite number sources with known intensities, we found that in the limit of small size distributions, the estimation error for standard deviation does not depend on the number of sources and their positions. Since more general (continuous) distributions can be approximated with discrete ones by binning, this suggests that the same error rate should hold in general.

Now, equipped with our fundamental bound on the achievable information, let us see if we can reach it.

IV
Achieving the limits:

Measurement Techniques

IV.1
Failure of Direct Imaging

We will now show that direct imaging does not achieve the QFI for sub-diffraction axial moments estimation. For this, we use the methods of semi-parametric estimation
28
;
23
, reviewed in Section
II.1.2
.
Specifically, we will identify an efficient influence function
\varphi
and use it to compute the bound (
31
).
This can be achieved by relating the object plane moments
defined by Eq. (
2
) to the moments in the image plane. According to (
48
), the probability density associated to the direct imaging measurement is

f_{F}(\bm{r})=\int|\psi(\bm{r},z)|^{2}F(dz).

(65)

where
F
is the unknown axial source distribution. Note that while in Section
III
we assumed
F
to be supported on a finite number of points,
in this section we do not make such restrictive assumptions, so
F
will belong to a large (non-parametric) class of distributions.

Since
f_{F}(\bm{r})=f_{F}(r)
is radially symmetric, it suffices to consider its even moments
\{\phi_{2j}\}_{j\geq 1}
given by

\phi_{2j}=\int r^{2j}f_{F}(\bm{r})d\bm{r}.

(66)

By expanding
H(\bm{r};z)=|\psi(\bm{r},z)|^{2}
from Eq. (
49
) in a power series, we rewrite
\phi_{2j}
as

\displaystyle\phi_{2j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{(2k)!}\int r^{2j}\left.\frac{\partial^{2k}|\psi(\bm{r},z)|^{2}}{\partial z^{2k}}\right|_{z=0}d\bm{r}\int z^{2k}dF(z)

(67)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}C_{jk}\theta_{2k},

where we note that the coefficients of the odd moments
\theta_{2k+1}
are zero and
the matrix
C
has elements given by

[C]_{jk}=\frac{1}{(2k)!}\int r^{2j}\left.\frac{\partial^{2k}|\psi(\bm{r},z)|^{2}}{\partial z^{2k}}\right|_{z=0}d\bm{r}.

(68)

The explicit form of
C
is computed in Appendix
A
as the lower triangular matrix with elements

[C]_{ij}=\frac{i!}{2^{i}z_{R}^{2j}}\binom{i}{j}.

(69)

and its inverse is the upper triangular matrix with elements

[C^{-1}]_{ij}=(-1)^{i-j}\binom{i}{j}\frac{2^{j}}{j!}z_{R}^{2i}.

(70)

Equation (
67
) can be expressed in vectorial form as
\bm{\phi}=C\bm{\theta}
or, by taking inverses,

\bm{\theta}=C^{-1}\bm{\phi}
.
Let
\beta=\beta(F)
be a one-dimensional parameter of interest and let us assume that
\beta
is of the form
\beta:=\bm{\partial\beta}^{T}\bm{\theta}
for some fixed vector
\bm{\partial\beta}
. Let

F_{0}
be a reference source distribution, with
\beta_{0}=\beta(F_{0}),\bm{\theta}_{0}=\bm{\theta}(F_{0})
,

\bm{\phi}_{0}=\bm{\phi}(F_{0})
. Then

\beta=\beta_{0}+\bm{\partial\beta}^{T}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0})

and

\beta=\beta_{0}+(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}C^{-1}(\bm{\phi}-\bm{\phi}_{0}).

(71)

Using (
66
) we have then

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}+\int(f(\bm{r})-f_{0}(\bm{r}))d\bm{r}(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}C^{-1}\mathfrak{R}

where
\mathfrak{R}
is the vector with components
\mathfrak{R}_{j}:=r^{2j}
.
Therefore we conclude that

\varphi=(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}C^{-1}\mathfrak{R}-(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}C^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{0}

(72)

is an IF for the parameter
\beta
and thus, by Eq. (
24
), the corresponding asymptotic scaled error in estimating
\beta
is

\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2}_{F_{0}}=\mathbb{E}_{F_{0}}[\varphi^{2}]=(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}C^{-1}UC^{-T}\bm{\partial\beta},

(73)

where
U
is a matrix with elements

\displaystyle[U]_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int r^{2(i+j)}f_{0}(\bm{r})d\bm{r}-\phi_{0,2i}\phi_{0,2j}

(74)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{0,2(i+j)}-\phi_{0,2i}\phi_{0,2j}.

Similarly to
28
it can be argued that
\varphi
is an efficient IF, which means that the error bound in (
73
) cannot be improved. Intuitively, this is because
\varphi
belongs to the subspace
of rotationally invariant functions in

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},f_{0})
, which coincides with the co-tangent space
\mathcal{T}
of score functions. For more details and specific technical assumptions for which this result holds, we refer to Appendix
B
.

From (
70
) and (
74
) we obtain that

\mathcal{V}^{\rm DI}_{\theta}=C^{-1}UC^{-T}
is given by

\displaystyle[\mathcal{V}^{\rm DI}_{\bm{\theta}}]_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{i}\sum_{m=0}^{j}\binom{i}{n}\binom{j}{m}\frac{(-2)^{n+m}(-z_{R}^{2})^{i+j}}{n!m!}

(75)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle(\phi_{0,2(n+m)}-\phi_{0,2n}\phi_{0,2m}).

and the optimal error for
\beta
is

\mathcal{V}^{\rm DI}_{\beta}=(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}\mathcal{V}^{\rm DI}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\partial\beta}.

(76)

In particular, the errors for estimating the even moments
\theta_{2j}
are nonzero even in the limit
of small sources spread due to the non-trivial value of the contributions
\phi_{0,2(n+m)}-\phi_{0,2n}\phi_{0,2m}
computed with respect to the Gaussian density
f_{0}
of the PSF.

The above argument can be extended to non-linear functionals

\beta
in which case
\bm{\partial}\bm{\beta}
is the vector of partial derivatives at

\bm{\theta}_{0}=\bm{\theta}(F_{0})
. Since we focus on roughness, we set the first moment
\theta_{1}
to zero and consider
\beta\equiv\sqrt{\theta_{2}}=\sigma
.
Then

\bm{\partial\beta}_{i}=\frac{\delta_{2i}}{2\sqrt{\theta_{2}}}.

(77)

The result of Eq. (
73
) is that the CRB element for the estimation of the second root moment or “roughness” parameter is (see Appendix
C
for the derivation),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\rm DI}_{\sigma}=\frac{z_{R}^{4}}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\phi_{0,4}-\phi^{2}_{0,2}\right).

(78)

In the sub-diffraction limit, our region of interest, it is assumed that
\Delta\ll 1
and thus the standard deviation
\sigma=\sqrt{\theta_{2}}=O(\Delta)\ll 1
while

\phi_{0,j}
converge to the moments of the Gaussian PSF of a single source
\psi(\bm{r},0)
. The result is a diverging CRB,

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta\to 0}\mathcal{V}^{\rm DI}_{\sigma}=\infty.

(79)

To conclude this section we note that direct imaging, incapable of estimating odd-ordered moments (including mean height), is also incapable of estimating the roughness of an arbitrary distribution of axially displaced point sources below the Rayleigh limit.

IV.2
Triumph of SPADE

Having shown that direct imaging fails to estimate surface roughness or, more precisely, the standard deviation of the axial distribution of incoherent point sources,
we are motivated to search for a measurement scheme that is optimal for its estimation.
In this section we show that a measurement strategy based on SPADE
24
;
26
does have this property. We will begin this analysis by proceeding similarly to the previous section, but we will instead attempt to describe the vector
\bm{f}=[f_{q}]_{q\geq 0}
of (normalized) intensities in the transverse mode basis linearly in terms of the object distribution moments as

\bm{f}=W\bm{\theta}.

(80)

:
Intensity plots of the first four radially symmetric Laguerre-Gauss modes for

(x,y)
plane centered at
(0,0)

\displaystyle\phi_{q}(\bm{r})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\omega_{0}^{2}}}L_{q}\left(\frac{2r^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\right)e^{\frac{-r^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}}.

(81)

where

are the Laguerre polynomials, for

f_{q}=\int H(q;z)dF(z),

(82)

where

H(q;z)=|\langle\phi_{q}|\psi_{z}\rangle|^{2},

(83)

and

represents the state of a photon in the mode

|\phi_{q}{\rangle}=\int\phi_{q}(\bm{r})|\bm{r}{\rangle}d\bm{r}.

(84)

Given the Gaussian beam (

H(q;z)=\frac{4z_{R}^{2}z^{2q}}{(z^{2}+4z_{R}^{2})^{q+1}}.

(85)

By expanding in terms of

H(q;z)=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}\frac{(-1)^{k-q}}{(k-q)!}\frac{k!}{q!}\left(\frac{z}{2z_{R}}\right)^{2k}.

(86)

This allows us to expand Eq. (

f_{q}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{qk}\theta_{2k},

(87)

where

[W]_{qk}=\frac{(-1)^{k-q}}{(2z_{R})^{2k}}\binom{k}{q}.

(88)

We note from the above expressions that, similarly to the case of direct imaging, only even-ordered moments
\theta_{2k}

1

1

\beta=(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}\bm{\theta}

\beta=\beta_{0}+(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0})=\beta_{0}+(\bm{\partial\beta})^{T}W^{-1}(\bm{f}-\bm{f}_{0}),

(89)

where
\beta_{0}=\beta(F_{0}),\bm{\theta}_{0}=\bm{\theta}(F_{0})

(90)

(91)

V
Discussion

Within this paper we have outlined a thorough and rigorous investigation into the quantum inspired estimation of the roughness of non-conductive near-smooth surfaces. We have split the problem into two parts: (i) estimation of purely axially displaced sources and (ii) estimation of both radially and axially displaced sources. Here, we have covered the first part.

We have shown, through the elegant and powerful language of quantum metrology, that the fundamental error in estimating the roughness (standard deviation) of the purely axial distribution of an arbitrary number of point sources is finite and achievable. Namely, this can be achieved by demultiplexing the beam into a basis spanned by Laguerre-Gauss modes, followed by intensity measurements. Moreover we have shown that direct imaging fails to reach this bound and that, as the roughness becomes smaller, the information attainable about it with such a measurement technique goes to zero.

In a forthcoming paper we aim to cover the second part of the problem, namely to bring the purely radial distribution, which has been thoroughly studied
2
;
34
;
25
;
28
;
27
, together with the purely axial distribution studied here, in order to achieve a comprehensive understanding on the ultimate limitations of optical surface roughness estimation in three dimensions. Of course, we expect that direct imaging will still fail for the estimation of the surface roughness in the sub-diffraction regime. However, we hope to find a measurement scheme that is feasible and optimal for the estimation of such an important parameter in more practical scenarios.

Whilst our derivation of the
quantum
Fisher information and related bounds in this paper has been for a discrete distribution of sources, we would like to generalize this to a continuum to capture a more realistic description of surface profiles; thankfully, the semi-parametric estimation techniques — here employed for the evaluation of
classical
estimation bounds for the different measurement strategies — will continue to apply in such a setting, given that the parameters of interest are limited to one or two moments, despite the infinite dimensionality of the nuisance coordinate space. Finally, some of the most powerful current surface analysis implementations utilize laser interferometry. As such, it can be desirable to look into a model consisting of
coherent
superpositions of sources and take into account the effects of illumination more in detail, as opposed to the incoherent light model adopted in this work.

Appendix A
Derivation of the
C

In this section we prove that the matrix that linearly relates object moments and image moments given by Eq. (

[C]_{jk}=\frac{1}{(2k)!}\left.\frac{\partial^{2k}}{\partial z^{2k}}\int|\psi(\bm{r};z)|^{2}r^{2j}d\bm{r}\right|_{z=0}.

(97)

The integral in the expression above can be expressed explicitely in terms of the Gaussian beam (

I_{j}(z)=\frac{4}{\omega(z)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2j+1}e^{\frac{-2r^{2}}{\omega(z)^{2}}}dr.

Reparameterizing

we can rewrite this as

I_{j}(z)=\frac{2}{\omega(z)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{j}e^{\frac{-2x}{\omega(z)^{2}}}dx.

(99)

This integral is well known

and can be evaluated as

I_{j}(z)=\frac{j!\omega(z)^{2j}}{2^{j}}.

(100)

The elements of the matrix

are thus written as

[C]_{jk}=\frac{j!}{2^{j}(2k)!}\left.\frac{\partial^{2k}}{\partial z^{2k}}\left(1+\frac{z^{2}}{z_{R}^{2}}\right)^{j}\right|_{z=0}.

(101)

This can be rewritten using the binomial theorem as

[C]_{jk}=\frac{j!}{2^{j}(2k)!}\sum_{l=0}z_{R}^{-2l}\binom{j}{l}\frac{(2l)!}{(2l-2k)!}\left.z^{2(l-k)}\right|_{z=0}.

(102)

Evaluated at
z=0

k=l
and so the elements of the

[C]_{jk}=\frac{j!}{z_{R}^{2k}2^{j}}\binom{j}{k}.

(103)

Now we prove that the matrix given by Eq. (
70

69

[CC^{-1}]_{ij}\equiv S_{ij}
equals
\delta_{ij}
, where

S_{ij}=(-1)^{j}2^{j-i}\frac{i!}{j!}\binom{i}{j}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\binom{i}{m}\binom{m}{j}.

(104)

Using the known binomial identity,

\binom{i}{m}\binom{m}{j}=\binom{i}{j}\binom{i-j}{m-j},

(105)

one can rewrite
S_{ij}
as

S_{ij}=(-1)^{j}2^{j-i}\frac{i!}{j!}\binom{i}{j}^{2}\sum_{m=j}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\binom{i-j}{m-j}.

(106)

In the case of

(107)

On the other hand for

This final summation is proportional to the series expansion of

In this section we will prove that the IF
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{F_{0}}
given by (

We begin by defining the space of rotation invariant square-integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\text{rad}}(f_{0}(r))=\bigg\{

\displaystyle g(\bm{r}):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\penalty\ \big\lvert\penalty\ g(\bm{r})=g(\lvert\bm{r}\rvert),

(109)

\displaystyle\int\lvert g(\bm{r})\rvert^{2}f_{0}(\bm{r})d\bm{r}<\infty\bigg\}.

We then define the subspace of zero-mean functions as

L^{2}_{\text{rad},0}(f_{0})=\bigg\{g(\bm{r})\in L^{2}_{\text{rad}}(f_{0}(r))\ \big\lvert\int g(\bm{r})f_{0}(\bm{r})d\bm{r}=0\bigg\}.

Let us clarify here that
F_{0}
is a reference measure on the measureable space

where the sample space represents the positions of light sources. Throughout this work we are only concerned with estimating distributions with very small feature sizes. Thus we make the assumption that the support of

is a compact subset of the set
A=[-a,a]\subset\mathbb{R}
where

.
For technical reasons we also assume that the support of

in

contains an infinite number of points, where we recall that a point

is in the support of

if

We define the statistical model

as the collection of distributions that are dominated by the reference distribution,
\mathcal{P}=\{F:F\ll F_{0}\}
. Given that our intensities are related to the source distribution

and this distribution is generally of an unknown form, we must assume an infinite-dimensional parameter
\bm{\theta}
and thus we will instead consider a one-dimensional parametrization mapping a neighborhood of
t=0
in
[0,\infty)
to the statistical model
\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle t\mapsto F_{t}(z).

(111)

Explicitly we will use a normalized exponential family:

\displaystyle F_{t}(dz)=\frac{e^{tu(z)}F_{0}(dz)}{\int e^{tu(z)}F_{0}(dz)}.

(112)

Here
u(z)\in L^{\infty}(F_{0})
is any zero-mean (with respect to the measure
F_{0}(dz)
) measurable function. We define the expectation with respect to
F_{0}
as
\mathbb{E}_{F_{0}}[u]=\int u(z)F_{0}(dz)
.
Given the image intensity
f_{t}(r)=\int H(\bm{r};z)F_{t}(dz)
, we can write the scores as

\displaystyle S_{u}(\bm{r})=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}\log f_{t}(\bm{r})=\frac{\int H(\bm{r};z)u(z)F_{0}(dz)}{f_{0}(\bm{r})}

(113)

Let us define the space of score functions as
\mathfrak{S}\equiv\{S_{u}(\bm{r})\}_{u}
. We aim to show that the span of this space, i.e., the co-tangent space
\mathcal{T}\equiv\text{span}\{\mathfrak{S}\}
, is dense in the subspace
L^{2}_{\text{rad},0}(f_{0})
of zero-mean square-integrable rotation-invariant functions. That is, we aim to show:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{T}}=L^{2}_{\text{rad},0}(f_{0}).

(114)

We will do this by showing that the orthogonal complement
\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
of the co-tangent space is a trivial subspace, i.e.
\mathcal{T}^{\perp}=\{0\}
.
The key takeaway for us will be that an IF belonging to
L^{2}_{\text{rad},0}(f_{0})
will thus automatically belong to
\overline{\mathcal{T}}
and therefore be efficient.

Let us begin by considering a function
g(\bm{r})\in L^{2}_{\text{rad},0}(f_{0})
that is orthogonal to the scores of each sub-model:

\displaystyle\langle g,S_{u}\rangle_{f_{0}}

\displaystyle=\int g(\bm{r})S_{u}(\bm{r})f_{0}(\bm{r})d\bm{r}=0.

(115)

We first show that the function

G(z):=\int g(\bm{r})H(\bm{r};z)d\bm{r}

is a well defined bounded function for
z\in A
. Indeed, for any
z\in A
,

ce^{-\frac{2r^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}}\leq H(\bm{r};z)\leq Ce^{-(1+\epsilon)\frac{r^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}},

(116)

for some constants
c,C>0
and a constant

\epsilon>0
which depends on
a
. Therefore by using Cauchy-Schwarz

\displaystyle\int|g(\bm{r})|H(\bm{r};z)d\bm{r}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int|g(\bm{r})|e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}}e^{\frac{-\epsilon r^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}}d\bm{r}

(117)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{C}\int|g(\bm{r})|^{2}e^{-\frac{2r^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}}d\bm{r}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{C}\|g\|^{2}_{f_{0}}<\infty.

where we used that

f_{0}(\bm{r})\geq ce^{-\frac{2r^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}}
, which follows from (
116
). Note that
G(z)
has zero mean with respect to
F_{0}
.
Now we have

\displaystyle\langle g,S_{u}\rangle_{{}_{f_{0}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint\overline{g(\bm{r})}H(\bm{r},z)u(z)F_{0}(dz)d\bm{r}

(118)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int u(z)\overline{G(z)}F_{0}(dz)=\langle G(z),u(z)\rangle_{F_{0}}=0.

Since
u(z)
is arbitrary and
G
has zero mean, this implies that

\displaystyle G(z)=0,\,\,\,\,\,F_{0}-a.e.\,\,z\in\mathbb{R}.

(119)

In order to extend the condition
G(z)=0
to the entire interval
A
, we will show that
G(z)
is analytic on
A
. We begin by noting that for the Gaussian direct imaging model,

\displaystyle H(\bm{r};z)=\frac{2}{\pi\omega(z)^{2}}e^{-\frac{2r^{2}}{\omega(z)^{2}}}.

(120)

Given that
g(\bm{r})
is rotation invariant and subsequently defining

and

, we can write

(121)

where

\lambda(z)=\frac{2}{\omega^{2}(z)}=\frac{2}{\omega_{0}^{2}\left(1+\frac{z^{2}}{z_{R}^{2}}\right)}.

(122)

Since

was shown to be finite, the Laplace transform

exists for an interval of values for
\lambda
, which in particular means that it is analytic in a half plane. Since the support of
F_{0}
is infinite and
G(z)
is continuous and zero,
F_{0}-a.e.

Therefore, seeing as the only orthogonal element in

is the zero function, we conclude that the orthogonal complement of

is trivial and thus
\mathcal{T}
is dense in

G(z)

is identically zero, it suffices to prove that
G(z)
is real analytic on
A
. Let us define
\xi(z)\equiv z^{2}/(z^{2}+4z_{R}^{2})
such that

\displaystyle 0\leq\xi(z)\leq\frac{a^{2}}{a^{2}+4z_{R}^{2}}\equiv\xi_{\text{max}}<1.

(157)

As such we have

H(q;z)=(1-\xi(z))\xi(z)^{q},

(158)

and

G(z)=(1-\xi(z))\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}g(q)\xi(z)^{q}=(1-\xi(z))P(\xi(z)),

(159)

where we have defined
P(\xi)
as the power series

\displaystyle P(\xi)=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}g(q)\xi(z)^{q}.

(160)

In order to prove that
G(z)
is real analytic on
A
we will prove that
P(\xi)
converges absolutely on
A
. To do this, we will note that since
F_{0}
is not a point mass at
z=0
, and
a
has been chosen as the supremum of (the absolute value of) the support of
F_{0}
, any
\xi_{*}\in(0,\xi_{\text{max}})
satisfies
F_{0}(B)>0
where
B=\{z\in A:\xi(z)\geq\xi_{*}\}
. As such given that
H(q;z)
is positive, we can bound the reference intensity from below as follows,

\displaystyle f^{(0)}_{q}

\displaystyle=\int_{A}H(q;z)F_{0}(dz)\geq\int_{B}H(q;z)F_{0}(dz)

(161)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}(1-\xi(z))\xi(z)^{q}F_{0}(dz)\geq c\xi_{*}^{q}.

With this bound and using the
\ell^{2}
square-summability of
g
we can then write

\displaystyle c\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\lvert g(q)\rvert^{2}\xi_{*}^{q}\leq\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\lvert g(q)\rvert^{2}f^{(0)}_{q}<\infty.

(162)

Given that for
\xi_{*}
, the series on the LHS converges, by the root test we can conclude that the radius of convergence of this series is

\displaystyle R=\frac{1}{\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lvert g(q)\rvert^{\frac{2}{q}}}>\xi_{*}.

(163)

This implies that the power series given by Eq. (
160
) has convergence radius
R^{\prime}
at least
\sqrt{\xi_{*}}
.
Since
\xi_{*}<\xi_{\rm max}
was chosen arbitrarily, we can impose the condition

\displaystyle\xi_{*}>\xi_{\text{max}}^{2}\equiv\left(\frac{a^{2}}{a^{2}+4z_{R}^{2}}\right)^{2}<1.

(164)

When this condition is satisfied we have
R^{\prime}>\xi_{\text{max}}
and thus the power series (
160
) converges for all
\xi\in(0,\xi_{\text{max}})
. A power series is analytic on the interior of its radius of convergence and so
P(\xi)
is analytic
\forall\xi\in(0,\xi_{\text{max}})
and thus implicitly real analytic
\forall z\in A
. Since
\xi(z)=z^{2}/(z^{2}+4z_{R}^{2})
is also real analytic on
A
, so too is
G(z)
.

We can now use the identity theorem for real analytic functions to prove that if
G(z)
is real analytic in
A
and
G(z)=0,\,\,F_{0}-a.e.\,z\in A
, then
G(z)=0
for all
z\in A
. We will not explicitly write out the proof as it is identical to the one in Appendix
B
. As such, since
G(z)=\xi(z)(1-\xi(z))P(\xi)=0\,\,\forall z\in A
, this means that
P(\xi)=0\,\,\forall z\in A
. Since the terms of a power series are linearly independent we conclude that
g(q)=0\,\,\forall q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Seeing that the only element in
\ell^{2}_{0}(f^{(0)})
orthogonal to the co-tangent space
\mathcal{T}
is the zero vector, we conclude that its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
is trivial and thus
\mathcal{T}
is dense in
\ell^{2}_{0}(f^{(0)})
. In conclusion any element in
\ell^{2}_{0}(f^{(0)})
can be arbitrarily well approximated by elements in the space of scores
S
and thus an IF
\varphi\in\ell^{2}_{0}(f^{(0)})
implies that
\varphi
is automatically efficient.
