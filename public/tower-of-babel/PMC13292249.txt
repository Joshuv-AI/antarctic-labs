Restoring cartilage-subchondral bone metabolism with biphasic magnesium microspheres ameliorate early osteoarthritis
Authors: Tao Chen, Weiliang Zhi, Guangcheng Wu, Xiangming He, Hanbo Wang, Ziyue Wang, Xianyi Yang, Xiling Qi, Long Chen, Sixiang Rao, Liming Zheng, Jiankun Xu, Guangtao Zan, Wei Liu, Yifan Liu, Jinwu Wang, Hui Wang, Yifeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The spatiotemporal histopathological features of articular cartilage in osteoarthritis (OA) remain inadequately characterized, which impedes the advancement of strategies to halt irreversible joint deterioration. Herein a murine OA model, the initial phase (<10 days post-surgery) is characterized by pronounced mitochondrial dysfunction in chondrocytes and concurrent activation of subchondral osteoclasts. Beyond this period, irreversible cartilage degeneration ensues, marked by chondrocyte apoptosis and subchondral bone sclerosis. We further identified magnesium (Mg) as a key regulator of cellular metabolic balance, capable of reinstating homeostasis in inflamed chondrocytes while modulating osteoclast overactivity. Based on this, we developed dual-concentration Mg-releasing biphasic microspheres that effectively halted early OA progression in vivo. In contrast, delayed administration conferred benefits predominantly limited to the subchondral bone, without achieving cartilage preservation. These results underscore the existence of a critical therapeutic window for metabolic intervention in OA, wherein Mg-based biomaterials exert protective effects exclusively during the early disease stage. Thus, this study offers a new strategic perspective for the clinical management of OA.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) represents a worldwide degenerative disorder marked by the progressive erosion of articular cartilage and pathological remodeling of subchondral bone [1]. Despite its high prevalence, the treatment of OA remains largely ineffective, largely because its pathogenic mechanisms remain unclear. A central and long-standing debate concerns the initial site of degeneration in the joint: does OA begin in the cartilage or the subchondral bone? The traditional “wear-and-tear” hypothesis posits that primary biomechanical breakdown of the cartilage is the initiating event, which subsequently disrupts subchondral bone homeostasis [2,3]. This unidirectional model has yielded to a new consensus that OA represents holistic dysregulation of the osteochondral unit, wherein articular cartilage, calcified cartilage, and subchondral bone constitute an integrated biomechanical and biochemical system [4]. Bidirectional crosstalk at the bone–cartilage interface, mediated by OPG/RANKL/RANK, Wnt/β-catenin, NF-κB, and TGF-β/BMP signaling, perpetuates pathological feedback loops independent of the initial lesion site [5]. Recent evidence positions subchondral bone as a primary OA driver, wherein osteoclast activation and bone marrow lesions compromise joint mechanics and release catabolic factors that trigger early cartilage degeneration [[6], [7], [8]]. Identifying the histopathological characteristics of articular cartilage in early OA is critical for resolving the long-lasting debate above.

Cellular metabolic dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a central biological event propelling pathologic degeneration in both cartilage and subchondral bone [3,9]. In OA progression, the chondrocytes undergo a pathological energy metabolic shift from the normal oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to glycolysis, commonly associated with mitochondrial dysfunction or even irreversible damage of mitochondria [10]. Subchondral osteoclasts, which are highly activated in early OA, exhibit comparatively high OXPHOS to execute subchondral bone resorption [2]. This divergent metabolic reprogramming across cell types indicates that effective OA therapy requires spatiotemporal specificity, accounting for the limited clinical success of universal metabolic modulators such as mitochondrial antioxidants or AMPK activators [11,12].

Magnesium (Mg) is an essential cofactor in over 300 cellular enzymatic reactions, including those governing ATP production and OXPHOS [13]. Epidemiological studies have revealed the clear correlation between systemic deficiency of Mg2+ and OA severity, suggesting that cellular Mg2+ dysregulation may be an overlooked key factor contributing to the metabolic pathology of OA [14]. Our recently published work proved that local injection of Mg2+ releasing particle in mice OA knee joint cavity significantly protects cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone resorption [15]. We speculate that Mg2+ acts as a cell type-dependent metabolic modulator, reversibly restoring homeostasis in chondrocytes and osteoclasts.​ However, its clinical translation faces a key barrier:​ conventional intra-articular Mg salt injections undergo rapid joint clearance, necessitating impractical, frequent dosing for chronic OA management [16]. Existing intra-articular therapies are similarly palliative and limited. Hyaluronic acid provides, at best, marginal symptomatic relief with inconsistent efficacy and elevated adverse event risks [17], while glucocorticoids offer only short-term analgesia and accelerate cartilage loss upon repeated use [18]. Systemic NSAIDs, meanwhile, impose significant gastrointestinal and cardiovascular toxicities without modifying joint structure [19]. While advanced biomaterial carriers like nanoparticles have been engineered for sustained release, they often perform limitations including the acidic degradation by-products and initial bursting release of high concentration of Mg2+ [[20], [21], [22]]. Furthermore, conventional intra-articular magnesium formulations are limited by rapid synovial clearance and transient supratherapeutic spikes, failing to meet the divergent spatiotemporal demands of OA progression [23]. Early-stage OA, driven by chondrocyte metabolic crisis and synovial inflammation, requires an initial burst to rapidly achieve the therapeutic Mg2+ concentration window for metabolic rescue and anti-inflammatory activity [24]. Conversely, late-stage OA, characterized by aberrant subchondral bone remodeling and pathological angiogenesis, necessitates sustained Mg2+ delivery within a safe chondroprotective range [25]. Therefore, a biphasic system tailored to the phase-specific pathophysiology of OA is imperative, ensuring durable efficacy across the disease continuum while averting cytotoxicity and oxidative stress from prolonged supraphysiological exposure.

In this study, we characterized the spatio-temporal histopathological performance of OA progression in a murine model, establishing a key time-point before which the imbalance in chondrocyte energy metabolism and pathological subchondral bone remodeling are detectable precursors to OA development. We demonstrated that Mg2+ exerts dual therapeutic effects by rectifying tissue-specific metabolic dysfunctions: it protects cartilage tissue by rescuing the mitochondrial function of the inflaming chondrocytes, while concurrently suppressing the mitochondrial function of activated osteoclast and bone resorption, without inducing osteoclasts apoptosis. Finally, we developed an injectable, biphasic Mg-loaded silk fibroin microspheres (MgO@SilMA@HM) using the microfluidic fabrication platform. Results

Temporal histopathological characterization of MMX-induced OA

We surgically induced joint instability in mice via medial collateral ligament transection and medial meniscectomy (MMX) to delineate the dynamic progression of OA. Histological and longitudinal micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analyses were conducted to assess temporal changes in articular cartilage and subchondral bone microstructure over 63 days (Fig. 1A). In the early phase, articular cartilage thickness remained stable in sham-operated controls, whereas MMX-operated mice showed a significant reduction by day 7 that progressed by day 10. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining at this stage confirmed superficial micro-erosion with an intact tidemark (Fig. 1B, C, L). Concurrently, the subchondral bone progressively thinned, reaching a minimum by day 10, and the combined cartilage-bone thickness was lowest on day 14 (Fig. S1A–C). Micro-CT analysis revealed dynamic subchondral remodeling, characterized by a transient 9.6% increase in the BS/BV ratio at day 7 (Fig. 1D and E), indicating microstructural erosion and elevated osteoclastic activity. This early resorptive phase was further marked by reduced trabecular connectivity and a progressive decline in BMD, reaching its nadir at day 14 (Fig. 1J).

During the intermediate phase, subchondral bone thickness and BMD gradually recovered on the operated side, yet this structural restoration failed to protect cartilage. Cartilage loss progressed irreversibly, thinning to 55.3 ± 4.5 μm by day 14 and further to 45.3 ± 4.8 μm by day 35. Despite the recovery of BMD, the concurrent decline of the BS/BV ratio from its early peak and the worsening of OARSI scores indicate a pathological bone remodeling process that failed to prevent cartilage degradation. The late phase was characterized by extensive cartilage erosion, predominant osteogenesis, and excessive bone formation, culminating in overt subchondral sclerosis, joint space narrowing, and severe proteoglycan loss. This was accompanied by structural alterations including subchondral plate thickening, a decreased trabecular number with thickened trabeculae, and increases in BV/TV and trabecular thickness compared to sham controls, collectively resulting in a pronounced reduction in BS/BV. Although the combined cartilage-bone thickness increased over time, it remained below sham levels at day 63, indicating that aberrant bone accumulation fails to restore normal anatomy.

Histological and Micro-CT analyses revealed concomitant thinning of articular cartilage and the subchondral bone plate within two weeks post-MMX, indicating catabolic dominance. This acute tissue loss likely stems from reduced cellularity due to chondrocyte loss and osteoclast-mediated resorption. To test this, we performed TUNEL staining to detect apoptosis in cartilage, along with TRAP and CTSK staining to quantify osteoclast-driven bone resorption, thereby linking these cellular activities directly to the observed structural decline.

Temporal dynamics of cyto-pathological events in mice OA

To quantify key cellular processes involved in OA-related thinning of articular cartilage and subchondral bone, we assessed chondrocyte mitochondrial function (ATP5A), apoptosis (TUNEL), osteoclast activity (TRAP/CTSK), and subchondral bone microstructure. OA cyto-pathogenesis displayed distinct spatiotemporal dynamics. In the early phase, SO/FG staining showed progressive thinning of subchondral bone in the weight bearing zone (Fig. S1A). This structural alteration was accompanied by a marked increase in osteoclast activity.​ Specifically,​ both TRAP+ osteoclast density and CTSK expression were significantly elevated following OA induction, with​ both parameters peaking concurrently at day 10 (Fig. 2C, D, G, H). It is notable that​ apoptotic activity remained minimal within both the cartilage and subchondral bone compartments throughout this observation period (Fig. 2A–E, S2A, B). In contrast, a distinct and rapid redistribution of ATP5A immunofluorescence was observed.​ As early as​ day 1 post-surgery, ATP5A signal became restricted to the superficial cartilage zone, contrasting with the full-thickness distribution in sham controls (Fig. 2B–F). During the middle to late stages, cartilage degeneration continued, characterized by a sustained increase in apoptotic chondrocytes that spread from the superficial zone into the middle and deep layers, where they distributed in clusters. Persistent downregulation of ATP5A, colocalizing with apoptotic foci, indicated mitochondrial dysfunction and energy deficit-mediated cell death (Fig. 2A and B). Throughout the mid-to-late OA phase, cartilage thinning continued, resulting from both increased apoptosis of resident chondrocytes and death of newly formed cells. Conversely, subchondral bone thickness rebounded rapidly, returning to baseline by day 35 and surpassing it by 1.4-fold by day 63 (Fig. S1A and B). This ossification coincided with attenuated osteoclastic activity. Based on our observations, we attribute this attenuation to increased apoptosis within the subchondral bone, likely involving osteoclasts (Fig. S2A and B).

Our results indicate throughout the early phase, despite the absence of significant apoptosis in both chondrocytes and osteoclasts, pronounced metabolic alterations were evident, including enhanced osteoclastic resorption and early mitochondrial dysfunction in chondrocytes, aligning with previous literature of metabolic dysregulation in initial OA stages [26,27].

IL-1β drives a metabolic switch from OXPHOS to glycolysis in chondrocytes

The initial OA phenotype, which is characterized by diminished ATP5A expression in the absence of apoptotic cell death, points to a pathology underpinned primarily by metabolic dysfunction rather than overt cellular loss. To explore this mechanism, we modeled the inflammatory milieu of OA by treating ATDC5 chondrocytes with IL-1β (20 ng/mL for 24 h). This inflammatory challenge resulted in attenuated proteoglycan synthesis, as indicated by a reduction in alcian blue staining intensity (Fig. 3A). Consistent with this catabolic shift, RT-qPCR analysis confirmed a significant downregulation of key chondrogenic markers (Col2, Sox9, Agg) concurrent with a marked upregulation of inflammatory and matrix-degrading genes (IL-6, TNF-α, iNOS, MMP3, MMP13) (Fig. 3F). Mitochondrial assessment revealed severe dysfunction, characterized by a 2.1-fold increase in ROS, 46% ATP depletion, and 55% loss of membrane potential (Fig. 3C–E). Further analysis using Seahorse XF technology demonstrated a profound suppression of mitochondrial bioenergetics. Specifically, we observed significant reductions in basal oxygen consumption rate (OCR), ATP-linked OCR, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 3I and J). Consequently, the calculated efficiency of mitochondrial ATP production declined sharply from 27.96% to 11.16%. In contrast to untreated controls, ATDC5 chondrocytes exposed to IL-1β exhibited a notably suppressed proton leak from their mitochondria (Fig. 3J). This metabolic adaptation points to a protective rewiring of chondrocyte metabolism to sustain energy output during inflammation. This decrease contrasts with the commonly observed elevation of proton leak under inflammatory conditions in other cell types [28]. Together, these results indicate that IL-1β induces a marked metabolic reprogramming from OXPHOS to glycolysis, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction, energy deficiency, and oxidative stress in chondrocytes. This finding is consistent with our observation of decreased ATP5A expression in early OA chondrocytes.

Mg2+ reverses inflammation-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic imbalance in chondrocytes

Prior to the investigation, the safety of Mg2+ concentrations in ATDC5 chondrocytes were assessed using CCK-8 assays (Fig. S3). To evaluate the potential of Mg2+ in mitigating the effects of IL-1β, ATDC5 chondrocytes were co-treated with 10 ng mL−1 IL-1β and Mg2+ (0, 2, or 10 mM) for 24 h. Alcian blue staining revealed that Mg2+ significantly restored proteoglycan synthesis impaired by IL-1β (Fig. 4A). RT-qPCR revealed that Mg2+ treatment upregulated chondrogenic markers (Col2, Sox9, Agg) while downregulating inflammatory and catabolic genes (IL-6, TNF-α, MMP3, MMP13, iNOS) (Fig. 4B and C). Furthermore, Mg2+ administration substantially alleviated mitochondrial impairment, with 10 mM Mg2+ increasing the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm, JC-1 red/green ratio) by 2.9-fold compared to the IL-1β group (Fig. 4D and E), and restoring ATP production by 48% (Fig. 4G). Intracellular ROS levels were also significantly reduced (Fig. 4F). Using the Seahorse XF system, we assessed mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic function. Mg2+ treatment notably enhanced mitochondrial respiratory parameters: basal respiration, ATP production-linked respiration, maximal respiratory capacity, and spare respiratory capacity were significantly improved in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4H and I). Specifically, 2 mM Mg2+ increased basal respiration to 148% of the model group, while 10 mM Mg2+ further elevated maximal respiration to 218% and spare respiratory capacity to 333%. Interestingly, proton leak was increased to 150% by 2 mM Mg2+ but only to 121% by 10 mM Mg2+ compared to the IL-1β group, suggesting a concentration-dependent regulatory role of Mg2+ in mitochondrial permeability (Fig. 4I).

Conversely, Mg2+ elicited limited effects on glycolytic parameters.​ Notably, a 10 mM Mg2+ treatment produced no significant alteration, whereas a modest attenuation of glycolysis (12.5%) was observed specifically at the 2 mM concentration​ (Fig. 4J and K). Elevated mitochondrial membrane potential at 10 mM without increased basal respiration indicates direct membrane stabilization via Mg2+-promoted hexokinase and creatine kinase binding and permeability transition pore inhibition [29]. The non-monotonic glycolytic response reflects Mg-ATP serving as the authentic Phosphofructokinase (PFK) substrate versus free ATP as an allosteric inhibitor, with limited Mg-ATP at 2 mM suppressing PFK and abundant Mg-ATP at 10 mM relieving this inhibition [30]. These results collectively indicate that Mg2+ effectively restores mitochondrial OXPHOS, attenuates oxidative stress, and rebalances cellular energy metabolism under inflammatory conditions, highlighting its therapeutic potential in mitigating early osteoarthritis progression.

Mg2+ suppresses osteoclast activation and reprograms cellular metabolism

Given our histological observations of osteoclast activation in early OA and the known modulatory effect of Mg2+ on chondrocyte energy metabolism, we next investigated whether Mg2+ directly influences osteoclast activity through similar metabolic reprogramming. We established functional osteoclasts by differentiating murine bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMC) in the presence of RANKL and M-CSF, confirmed by the formation of TRAP + multinucleated cells. The safe concentration of Mg2+ was determined via CCK-8 assay prior to subsequent experiments (Fig. S4). TRAP staining revealed fewer TRAP + multinucleated giant cells at 10 mM Mg2+, demonstrating that Mg2+ suppress the differentiation of monocytes into multinucleated osteoclasts (Fig. 5A). JC-1 fluorescence indicated reduced mitochondrial membrane potential with increasing Mg2+ concentration (Fig. 5A–D). Increasing Mg2+ concentrations significantly reduced intracellular ATP levels and dose-dependently suppressed the mRNA expression of MMP9, TRAP, and c-Fos (Fig. 5E–H). Using the Seahorse XF system, we quantitatively analyzed the dynamic effects of Mg2+ on OCR (Fig. 5I) and ECAR (Fig. 5J). At 10 mM Mg2+, basal respiration decreased by 45% (Fig. 5K), maximal respiration by 48% (Fig. 5L), and ATP-linked respiration by 51% (Fig. 5M). Concurrently, glycolysis increased 1.9-fold (Fig. 5N) and glycolytic reserve capacity increased 2.4-fold (Fig. 5O). It is established that the differentiation of monocytes into multinucleated osteoclasts requires a metabolic shift toward OXPHOS to meet their energy demands [31]. Metabolic phenotyping revealed that Mg2+ rewired osteoclast energy metabolism from a state of high OXPHOS and low glycolysis to the opposite. This Mg2+ driven metabolic reprogramming thereby underlies its inhibition of osteoclast formation.

Collectively, these data indicate that extracellular Mg2+ attenuates osteoclast maturation by inhibiting mitochondrial OXPHOS, thereby inducing a metabolic reprogramming that increases reliance on glycolysis.​ Importantly, this effect was not mediated by increased cell death, as​ TUNEL/DAPI co-staining and Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry assays confirmed no significant increase in apoptosis across the treatment groups (Fig. 5A and B). Mg2+ exposure did not trigger apoptosis in mononuclear osteoclast precursors but instead reduced basal apoptotic levels, indicating that its inhibition without compromising precursor survival. Moreover, this suppression was reversible. After removal of high-concentration Mg2+ (10 mM) and recovery in standard medium for 48 h, precursor cells reacquired the capacity to differentiate into functional multinucleated osteoclasts (Fig. 5C). Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the therapeutic mechanism of Mg2+ is rooted in metabolic reprogramming and subsequent cyto-protection, rather than the induction of cell death.​ Specifically, Mg2+ rectifies​ pathological bioenergetics in both osteoclasts and chondrocytes. These dual protective effects indicate that​ Mg2+ ameliorates OA by restoring metabolic homeostasis.

Fabrication and characterization of biphasic magnesium microspheres

To achieve a transient local magnesium concentration in the low to mid millimolar range within the joint cavity sufficient for therapeutic effect without systemic exposure, we designed biphasic microspheres with an initial burst phase that produces a transient local peak exceeding normal serum levels (0.75–1.05 mM) [32], but this peak is deliberately limited so that it subsequently declines into a safe chondroprotective range. We fabricated uniform methacrylated silk fibroin (SilMA) microspheres using a microfluidic technique. The MgO@SilMA microspheres were produced by blending MgO nanoparticles with a SilMA as a core. The microspheres were furtherly modified with carboxyl groups via poly (acrylic acid) (PAA) to provide nucleation sites for the next mineralization. Sequential incubation in MgCl2 and Na2CO3 solutions led to the in situ formation of hydro-magnesite (3MgCO3·Mg (OH)2·3H2O, HM) as a shell, a Mg-based compound enabling long-term stable release of Mg2+. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis of the freeze-dried microspheres revealed distinct morphological differences among the groups. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) further confirmed that magnesium was predominantly distributed within the outer shell, consistent with in situ formation of HM coating (Fig. 6B–D). Statistical analysis of the microspheres of different groups revealed that the diameters of the microspheres were concentrated around 30 μm (Fig. S6), while the width of the MgO@SilMA@HM whiskers was approximately 0.26 μm (Fig. S7A and B). FT-IR spectroscopy confirmed the successful introduction of PAA onto the SilMA microspheres (Fig. S8). Characteristic peaks were observed at 1440 cm−1 (asymmetric stretching of COO−), indicating the presence of surface and carboxylate groups was used to analyze and identify the raw material MgO and the surface structure of MgO@SilMA@HM. The whiskers of MgO@SilMA@HM is a type of hydro-magnesite (PDF:01-070-0361), which is an aqueous transition state of magnesium carbonate identified X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Fig. 6C). Critically, inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis demonstrated that the MgO@SilMA@HM architecture substantially prolonged Mg2+ release (Fig. 6E). The MgO@SilMA@HM spheres enable sustained and gradual ion release for over 18 days, while the MgO@SilMA group showed a rapid release within about 3 days with an initial burst. The initial, rapid release phase is attributed to the dissolution of the nano-MgO core and readily soluble components. The subsequent prolonged release is attributed to the inherently low solubility of HM whiskers. Additionally, the barrier effect of PAA and HM shells retarded the MgO core's degradation. The observed fluctuations reflect this dynamic handover, where localized HM erosion transiently exposes the residual MgO reservoir before resealing, producing oscillatory release during the transition from MgO-dominated to HM-governed maintenance (Fig. S11).

Rheological analysis confirmed shear-thinning behavior and successful gelation (G′ > G″) for both materials. The addition of MgO slightly reduced the plateau modulus and modified the shear-thinning response, indicating a moderating effect on the cross-linking network (Fig. S12A–D). MgO also increased hydrogel hydrophobicity, raising the water contact angle from 53° (SilMA) to 69° (MgO@SilMA), due to its intrinsic hydrophobicity and induced surface restructuring (Fig. S10). Both values remain hydrophilic (<90°), with the 60–70° range offering enough wettability to cut friction and retain lubricant, but none of the inflammatory drawbacks of extremely hydrophilic surfaces (<30°). Thermogravimetric analysis revealed enhanced thermal stability and elevated mineral content in MgO@SilMA@HM microspheres (Fig. S9).

Biphasic magnesium microspheres ameliorate early OA

Given that cellular states in early and late osteoarthritis are fundamentally distinct, we hypothesized that Mg2+-based therapy would exhibit stage dependent therapeutic efficacy. To address this, we administered Mg2+ microspheres in a murine OA model at two distinct timepoints: early intervention (7th DPS) to target metabolic dysfunction and late intervention (21st DPS) to target established apoptosis for comparative therapeutic assessment.

Starting on the 7th DPS, animals received intra-articular injections of either different microsphere formulations (10 μL/joint) or PBS as a control, administered every two weeks (Fig. 7A). Gait analysis at week 5 revealed that mice treated with MgO@SilMA@HM showed significant functional improvement compared to PBS-treated controls (Fig. 7B). These improvements were reflected in key gait parameters, including a 17% increase in stride length, a 20% increase in swing time, a 19% increase in propulsion time, and an 11% increase in step length (Fig. 7G–J). To assess OA progression, we performed ex vivo Micro-CT on knee joints harvested 5 weeks after surgery. Specifically, we observed a decrease in Tb.N and an increase in Tb.Sp. Furthermore, the BS/BV and BS/TV were both reduced. Together, these structural changes are consistent with subchondral plate thickening and trabecular scarification, hallmark features of advanced OA. MgO@SilMA@HM attenuated pathological subchondral bone remodeling, significantly increasing Tb.N, reducing Tb.Sp, elevating BS/BV and BS/TV compared to PBS controls (Fig. 7K–O).

H&E staining revealed severe cartilage erosion with a 39% thickness reduction and tidemark disruption in PBS controls, whereas MgO@SilMA@HM treatment restored cartilage thickness to 78% of sham levels and attenuated structural damage (Fig. 7D–P). SO/FG staining revealed substantial proteoglycan depletion in PBS group, particularly within load-bearing regions. In contrast, MgO@SilMA@HM enhanced proteoglycan retention, preserved tidemark integrity, reduced surface lesions, and yielded lower OARSI scores (Fig. 7E–Q). Despite this structural improvement, the residual 22% deficit likely reflects the limited therapeutic window of a single injection at day 7 post-surgery, persistent mechanical instability from meniscal destabilization, and early matrix loss that outpaces intrinsic chondrocyte regeneration despite metabolic rescue. These constraints suggest that earlier or repeated intervention combined with matrix-synthetic strategies may be required for full restoration. TRAP staining of the PBS group revealed diminished TRAP+ osteoclasts, consistent with the late-stage OA phenotype wherein subchondral bone remodeling has largely ceased and osteoclasts have undergone progressive apoptosis. Conversely, the MgO@SilMA group displayed increased TRAP+ osteoclasts adjacent to active resorption pits, suggesting a transient resorptive response following the initial burst release of Mg2+ (Fig. 7F–R). We interpret this as a temporary osteoclast rebound after acute Mg2+ suppression, which may contribute to decelerating pathological subchondral thickening without inducing destructive resorption or vascular invasion. The MgO@SilMA@HM group exhibited fewer TRAP+ osteoclasts relative to MgO@SilMA, consistent with sustained Mg2+ release maintaining prolonged osteoclast suppression. These findings suggest that controlled, prolonged Mg2+ delivery may offer superior therapeutic efficacy compared to rapid-release formulations by avoiding transient resorptive peaks and maintaining more consistent metabolic modulation of osteoclast activity. Finally, H&E assessment of major organs revealed no pathological abnormalities, confirming the in vivo safety of all formulations (Fig. S13). In summary, our findings delineate distinct therapeutic mechanisms for early-stage OA. MgO@SilMA acts primarily through localized, high-concentration Mg2+ release, which transiently suppresses osteoclast activity. This acute modulation suppresses pathologic osteolysis and abnormal bone remodeling during the initial phase of cartilage degeneration. In contrast, while MgO@SilMA@HM exerts a comparable yet attenuated early effect on bone metabolism, its sustained-release profile proves more effective at inhibiting chondrocyte apoptosis in the later disease stages. Consequently, MgO@SilMA@HM achieves a greater overall attenuation of osteoarthritis progression, resulting in enhanced therapeutic outcomes compared to MgO@SilMA.

However, when Mg2+ intervention was delayed until 3 weeks post-surgery and evaluated at week 7, the therapeutic benefits were largely confined to bone. Micro-CT analysis showed that MgO@SilMA@HM normalized BV/TV, and both Mg2+-based formulations restored BMD to sham levels (Fig. S14A–C). In contrast, SO/FG staining revealed severe cartilage degradation with marked thinning, and OARSI scores exhibited no significant improvement relative to the PBS group (Fig. S15A–C). This clear dissociation between bone and cartilage responses indicates that once osteoarthritis progresses to a stage characterized by substantial apoptosis in the cartilage and subchondral bone, Mg2+ delivery alone is insufficient to preserve cartilage integrity.

Discussion

OA involves both cartilage degradation and subchondral bone remodeling [33]. This remodeling process occurs in two consecutive phases: an early osteoclast-mediated resorptive stage followed by a late osteosclerotic stage [34]. In this study, we observed that transiently activated osteoclasts in early OA coincided with metabolically altered chondrocytes exhibiting significant mitochondrial dysfunction, indicating altered cellular metabolism in both cell types prior to overt cartilage degeneration. While it remains unresolved whether osteoclasts or chondrocytes primarily drive OA initiation, a key question is why subchondral osteoclasts are activated early but diminish later, despite persistent mechanical or inflammatory stimuli. Our observation that declining osteoclast activity accompanies significant chondrocyte apoptosis in the late stage suggests that chondrocytes may act as potential mediators contributing to subchondral bone remodeling dynamics [35,36]. Although established chondrocyte-bone crosstalk via PTHrP and DMP1 signaling axes lends biological plausibility to this notion, the present findings indicate only a temporal association, and any causal role requires validation through lineage-tracing or chondrocyte-specific ablation studies [37,38].

Inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β acting as a key pathological driver disrupt energy homeostasis in both cartilage and subchondral bone [39]. In chondrocytes, IL-1β induces a shift to aerobic glycolysis, impairing mitochondrial ATP production and increasing ROS, thereby promoting apoptosis and matrix degradation [40,41]. In the subchondral bone, IL-1β can stimulates the monocytes to differentiate into the multinucleated osteoclasts, which enhancing bone resorption [42]. Notably, the cell fusion of multinucleated osteoclasts and its osteolysis activity accompanied with robust OXPHOS and mitochondrial ATP production for actin ring formation and H+ secretion [43,44]. Our findings indicate that the cellular metabolic dysfunction in OA progression is cell-type-specific. Thus, the cellular metabolic modifying for OA treatment should be considered in cell-type-specific view.

Mg2+ acts as an essential cofactor for key enzymes in ATP metabolism, thereby positioning it as a fundamental coordinator of overall cellular bioenergetics [45]. Many evidences demonstrate that cellular Mg2+ deficiency promotes the mitochondrial inefficiency and pathogenic reliance on glycolysis across tissues [46,47]. Paradoxically, Mg2+ supports OXPHOS and mitochondrial integrity under physiological conditions. Our data suggests that Mg2+ is effective in early OA protection, in this phase, the cellular metabolic plasticity of chondrocytes and osteoclasts remain normal and the cellular structural damage is reversible. Interventions like Mg2+ can reprogram cellular energetics to delay OA progression. In late-stage OA, the extensive subchondral bone sclerosis, architectural disruption, and significant cartilage degradation with substantial chondrocytes apoptosis may limit its efficacy. In this phase of OA, combinatorial regenerative approaches are necessary. This time-differential histopathological characteristics in OA progression prompt time-dependent treatment approach in future OA therapeutics.

Considering that bisphosphonates showing limited efficacy in OA despite strong preclinical data, and anti-IL-1 therapies have been proven ineffective in clinical trials [48,49], disease-modifying interventions must instead target the pre-degenerative cellular phase. We demonstrate that Mg2+ fulfills this requirement by restoring mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and reversing glycolytic dependency in chondrocytes before irreversible degeneration, while concurrently orchestrating stage-specific subchondral bone remodeling via attenuated osteoclast resorption. Notably, the reversibility assay identifies a finite metabolic window conferring durable chondroprotective memory, a concept not previously linked to Mg2+ therapy. This positions Mg2+ therapy as optimally deployed within this narrow early window, targeting cellular metabolic pathology of chondrocytes and osteoclasts before structural damage of cartilage. Collectively, our work redefines the role of Mg2+ in OA pathogenesis, positioning it not as a simple dietary supplement but as a potent local regulator of bone and cartilage metabolism. These findings imply that engineered biomaterials capable of providing sustained, site-specific Mg2+ release hold promise for effective intervention in early-stage OA.

The MgO@SilMA@HM system represents a strategic advance in intra-articular biomaterial design, characterized by a biphasic Mg2+ release profile engineered to align with the temporal pathophysiology of OA. An initial burst of Mg2+ is designed to rapidly mitigate early osteoclast hyperactivity, while a subsequent sustained release phase provides prolonged support for chondrocyte viability. This tailored release kinetics ensure extended intra-articular residency, which could substantially reduce the frequency of required injections. Although prior studies have explored Mg2+-doped coatings or simple Mg2+-containing hydrogels primarily for orthopedic bone regeneration [[50], [51], [52]], such systems often lack controlled, cell-specific release kinetics and are not optimized for the intra-articular milieu. In contrast, MgO@SilMA@HM is specifically formulated to address the distinct cellular and metabolic demands of the osteochondral unit. Our pharmacokinetic evaluation confirmed that MgO@SilMA@HM maintains therapeutically effective Mg2+ concentrations for over 14 days, supporting a feasible bi-weekly dosing regimen. From a fabrication standpoint, integrating functional nanomaterials into microfluidic platforms is often limited by nanoparticle aggregation and microchannel clogging, which restrict the architectural and functional complexity of the resulting biomaterials. Our secondary in situ growth strategy eliminates these barriers through precise, direct fabrication of biphasic architectures within microfluidic channels. This versatile methodology significantly broadening the design scope for advanced biomaterials.

Our findings establish that the initial phase of MMX-induced OA is defined by osteoclast activation and chondrocyte metabolic imbalance, outlining a critical therapeutic window for interventions like Mg2+. Translating this to the clinic hinges on early diagnosis. Therefore, future work should prioritize biomarkers or imaging tools to identify patients in this early phase. While our conclusions stem from a post-traumatic model, subsequent studies should examine whether this mechanism generalizes to other OA forms (e.g., age-related or metabolic OA) using cell-specific metabolic tracing. The efficacy of Mg2+ also supports exploring staged combination therapies, such as pairing Mg2+ with an anti-resorptive agent early, followed by an anabolic agent later. A limitation of this study is the absence of direct intra-articular Mg2+ pharmacokinetic data, as technical constraints preclude accurate quantification in the minute murine joint. The in vitro PBS release profile therefore serves as a surrogate for bioavailable Mg2+, though this extrapolation is limited by synovial microenvironmental complexity. Future synovial fluid sampling or in vivo microdialysis would enhance translational relevance. The 10-day MMX time point reflects a model-specific acute post-traumatic transition rather than a universal early OA threshold. The MMX model triggers rapid joint degeneration within days to weeks, capturing acute traumatic responses rather than the slow, years-long progression of human early OA. Moreover, the shorter lifespan and higher metabolic rate of mice necessitate cautious cross-species temporal extrapolation, and our animal models may not fully replicate human OA chronicity. Nevertheless, this study provides a foundation for developing timely, metabolism-focused OA treatments.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Cell culture media (DMEM/F12, α-MEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin, and L-glutamine were obtained from Gibco. Recombinant mouse RANKL and M-CSF were from R&D Systems. MgCl2·6H2O was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology. Silk cocoons were sourced from Xi'an Jiubang Trading Co., Ltd. Glycidyl methacrylate (GMA), poly (acrylic acid), LiBr, and methanol were obtained from Shanghai Adamas Reagent. MgO nanoparticles were from Shanghai Maclin Biochemical Technology. Na2CO3 and 3-(Trimethoxysilyl) propyl methacrylate (KH-570) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent. Lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) was from Sigma-Aldrich. Novec 7500 (HFE-7500) was sourced from 3M Company, and the PEG-PFPE surfactant was from Thunder-Bio Innovation Limited.

Establishment of the mice OA model

Male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility under standardized conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C) with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of ShanghaiTech University (Approval No. 20220309001). To induce OA, mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol and subjected to medial meniscectomy on the right knee through transection of the medial collateral ligament and meniscotibial ligament. Sham-operated controls underwent arthrotomy only without ligament disruption or meniscal removal.

Micro-CT scanning analysis

The knee joints from the surgical side were dissected, excess muscle was removed, and fixed in 4% PFA overnight. All samples were scanned over 360° using a Bruker micro-CT system equipped with a 0.25 mm filter at a resolution of 4032 × 2688. For each sample, 700 layers covering the knee joint region were selected for reconstruction. Reconstruction and analysis were performed using the instrument-associated image-processing software.

Histology and immunofluorescence analyses

All tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Soft tissue sections were stained with H&E. Knee joint sections were decalcified and then stained with either H&E or SO/FG. Histopathological assessment was performed by two independent blinded observers using the OARSI scoring system, with the average score taken as the final result. For immunofluorescence, sections underwent antigen retrieval with trypsin, followed by blocking and overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Cathepsin K (Abcam, ab187647) and ATP5A1 (ABclonal, A11217). Detection was performed using an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, ab150077). TRAP staining (Servicebio, G1050) and TUNEL staining (Meilunbio, MA0223) were conducted according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Isolation and differentiation of osteoclasts

BMMCs were isolated from C57BL/6J mice. Following euthanasia and surface sterilization with 75% ethanol, femurs and tibias were dissected, and bone marrow was flushed with sterile PBS. The cell suspension was centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S, which contains a baseline Mg2+ concentration of approximately 0.81 mM (200 mg L−1 MgSO4·7H2O). After 24 h, the medium was replaced to remove non-adherent cells. For osteoclast differentiation, BMMCs were seeded in 24-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well. At 70% confluence, the medium was replaced with α-MEM containing 30 ng mL−1 M-CSF and 100 ng mL−1 RANKL. The medium was changed every 3 days. After 7 days, osteoclast formation was assessed by TRAP staining.

CCK-8 to determine safe concentrations of magnesium

Cells were seeded at 1000 cells/well in 96-well plates, allowed to adhere, and treated with varying Mg2+ concentrations for 24 or 48 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. After washing with PBS, 100 μL CCK-8 working solution was added per well and incubated for 2 h in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

In vitro inflammatory model and alcian blue staining

ATDC5 cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% p/s at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Chondrogenic differentiation was induced by adding ITS supplement (10 μg mL−1 bovine insulin, 10 μg mL−1 human transferrin, 3 × 10−8 M sodium selenite) to the medium. After 7 days differentiation, an inflammatory model was induced by 24 h pretreatment with 10 ng mL−1 IL-1β in the experimental group, whereas controls were maintained in ITS medium without IL-1β. For Alcian blue staining,​ differentiated cells were seeded in 12-well plates. After the 24 h IL-1β pretreatment (or control medium), both groups were further treated with​ MgCl2 at final concentrations of 0, 2, or 10 mM for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA, and stained with alcian blue solution (Solarbio, G2542, pH 2.5) at 37 °C for 30 min.

Intracellular ROS measurement

Intracellular ROS were measured using the Reactive Oxygen Specimen Kit (Meilunbio, MA0219) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

ATP measurement

Cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h with 0, 2, or 10 mM MgCl2. Following treatment, cells were lysed with 200 μL lysis buffer (Beyotime, S0026), vortexed, and centrifuged at 12,000g for 5 min at 4 °C and the supernatant measured for RLU values. Protein quantification was performed using the BCA Protein Quantification Kit (KT054, MDbio).

Flow cytometry

Following 24 h incubation in conditioned medium, cells were detached using trypsin without EDTA and combined with the corresponding cell supernatant. The mixture was centrifuged at 1500g for 3 min. The pellet was washed with PBS (three times) and binding buffer (once), resuspended, and stained with Annexin V/PI for flow cytometric analysis (BD Biosciences).

Mitochondrial morphology and function

The impact of Mg2+ on mitochondrial morphology was assessed by TEM examination of mitochondria in ATDC5 cells exposed to varying concentrations of Mg2+. Mitochondrial energy metabolism was evaluated using an Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (S0027, Beyotime), a Glycolysis Assay Kit (Ab197244, Abcam) for extracellular acidification, and an Oxygen Consumption Rate Assay Kit (E297, Dojindo). ATP levels, glycolytic activity, and oxygen consumption rates were measured with a Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent) following the manufacturers’ protocols.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA isolation kit (Beyotime, R0020M) and reverse-transcribed with PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, DRR037A). RT-qPCR was performed on a C1000 Real-Time PCR instrument (Bio-Rad) using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table S1), with β-actin as the internal control. Relative expression was calculated by the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

JC-1 probe staining working solution was prepared and the probes were incubated using the JC-1 assay kit (Solarbio, M8650). The fluorescence intensity of GFP and PE-RED channels was measured using BD Fortessa.

Analysis of mitochondrial stress and glycolytic capacity

Cellular metabolic function was assessed using a Seahorse XF96 Analyzer.​ Control and IL-1β-pretreated ATDC5 cells were seeded into​ XF96 cell culture microplates. Cells were then exposed to​ MgCl2 at final concentrations of 0, 2, or 10 mM in their respective culture media. Mitochondrial stress was measured using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent, 103015-100) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Glycolytic capacity was determined in a parallel experiment using the Seahorse XF Glycolytic Stress Test Kit (Agilent, 103020-100).

Synthesis of SilMA

Silk cocoons were cut, washed, and boiled in 0.05 M sodium carbonate solution for 40 min to remove sericin. The degummed silk was washed thoroughly with ddH2O, dried at 60 °C, and then dissolved in 100 mL of 9.5 M LiBr at 60 °C. Subsequently, 4 mL of GMA was added dropwise to the solution. After mixing, the solution was dialyzed (8-14 kDa) for 3 days. The resulting SilMA solution was concentrated, lyophilized, and stored as a dry solid for further use.

Fabrication of MgO@SilMA

MgO nanoparticles (0.2 g) were dispersed in ethanol (15 mL), functionalized with KH-570 (0.2 mL) via 60 °C reaction for 4 h, and purified to afford MgO-KH570 with enhanced aqueous dispersibility. For microsphere fabrication, SilMA (0.6 g) was dissolved in water (3 mL), mixed with MgO-KH570 (0.0045 g) and photoinitiator LAP (0.012 g), and emulsified with 2% PEG-PFPE in HFE7500 using a microfluidic device. The collected microspheres were UV-cross-linked (3 min), washed (methanol/water), and rehydrated in deionized water prior to use.

Preparation of MgO@SilMA@HM

MgO@SilMA microspheres were taken and added to 1% v/v PAA solution with 405 nm UV light for 3 min. The precipitate was taken by centrifugation to obtain MgO@SilMA-PAA microspheres. MgO@SilMA-PAA microspheres were added to 5% w/v MgCl2 solution, soaked for more than 12 h, centrifuged to precipitate, washed using deionized water, added to 5.6% w/v Na2CO3 solution, vortexed for 3 min, soaked for 2 h, washed with deionized water, and centrifuged to take the precipitate to obtain MgO@SilMA@HM microspheres. The resulting MgO@SilMA@HM microspheres feature an ordered core-shell architecture in which a PAA-functionalized MgO/SilMA core directs conformal HM shell precipitation during MgCl2/Na2CO3 incubation.

Material characterization and rheological analysis

The morphology of lyophilized microspheres was characterized via field-emission SEM (ZEISS, Germany), and elemental mapping was performed using EDS (Oxford, UK). Rheological properties were evaluated with a rotational rheometer (TA, USA) by subjecting a pure silk fibroin aqueous solution and a MgO-KH570-containing silk fibroin solution to UV-induced photocuring (5 W, 5 min). Frequency sweep tests were conducted from 0.1 to 10 Hz at 1% strain to examine the frequency-dependent behavior of the storage (G′) and loss (G″) moduli. Strain sweeps (0.01%–1000%, 1 Hz) were employed to identify the linear viscoelastic region, and shear-thinning characteristics were assessed at 1 Hz and 1% strain.

Mg2+ release profiling

To evaluate sustained-release behavior, microspheres (0.02 g) were immersed in 10 mL PBS and incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking. Daily, 5 mL of supernatant was collected for ICP-OES analysis and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The measured Mg2+ concentrations were corrected for dilution by subtracting half of the previous day's value, yielding cumulative release data for profile plotting.

Intra-articular microsphere injection, randomization, and blinding

Male C57BL/6 mice underwent MMX surgery to induce OA and were subsequently randomized by body weight and housed in randomly assigned cage positions. Treatments were coded by an independent investigator, with the surgeon and assessors blinded to allocation. Microsphere formulations (10 μL/joint) or PBS control were intraarticular injected at two-week intervals. Histological and micro-CT analyses were performed by two blinded observers, with discrepancies resolved by a third. Predefined exclusion criteria (surgical complications, infection, >20% weight loss, accidental death, or poor sample quality) were documented prior to unblinding.

Gait analysis

Two weeks after the final intra-articular injection in OA mice, gait alterations were analyzed across groups using the DigiGait™ Imaging System (Mouse Specifics Inc.). Mice were recorded at 17 cm/s on a transparent treadmill with a ventral camera. Ten consecutive strides within a 10 s interval were analyzed for hind limb stride length, swing time, stride time, and propel time.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data are presented as mean ± s.d. Statistical analyses included two-tailed Student's t-test for two-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA for multiple group comparisons, and two-way ANOVA for comparisons across different time points with treatment (sham/surgery) and time as independent factors. All experiments included at least three technical replicates. Statistical significance was set at ∗, #P < 0.05; ∗∗, ##P < 0.01; ∗∗∗, ###P < 0.001.
