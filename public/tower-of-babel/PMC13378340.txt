Type I IFN-controlled Qa-2 expression identifies and maps the generation of Eomes+ thymic innate CD8 T cells
Authors: Hee Yeun Won, Min-Kyung Joo, Dominic Lanasa, Changwan Hong, Jung-Hyun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryInnate CD8 T cells are a subset of CD8 lineage T cells that acquire proinflammatory effector functions during their development in the thymus. Yet the developmental mechanisms diverting them from conventional CD8 T cells remain incompletely understood. By mapping their developmental trajectory in the thymus, here we identify high-level expression of the nonclassical MHC-I molecule Qa-2 as a surface marker of innate CD8 T cells. Because Qa-2 expression is induced by type I interferon (IFN) signaling, these results further led us to uncover a role for tonic type I IFN signaling in their generation. Accordingly, interleukin-4 is considered both necessary and sufficient for this process, but innate CD8 T cell differentiation was also markedly impaired in the absence of type I IFN signaling, as documented with genetically engineered mice. Altogether, these findings report an intrathymic cytokine circuitry that orchestrates the differentiation of innate CD8 T cells during their thymic development.

Introduction

CD8 T cells are generated in the thymus but differentiate into fully mature effector cells only upon antigen encounter in peripheral tissues.1,2 A small subset of thymic CD8 T cells, however, acquires effector function independently of antigen activation as part of their developmental process in the thymus. Such CD8 single-positive (SP) thymocytes are commonly referred to as “innate CD8 T cells.”3,4,5,6,7 Innate CD8 T cells can be identified by their constitutive expression of the transcription factor Eomesodermin (Eomes) but not T-bet,8,9 and by their distinctive phenotype of expressing large amounts of the surface markers CD44, CXCR3, IL-4Rα, and low levels of CD24.3,4 Conversely, conventional naive CD8 T cells express high levels of CD24 with low protein abundance of CD44, CXCR3, and IL-4Rα. Unlike naive CD8 T cells that are functionally immature,10 innate-like T (ILT) cells, including innate CD8 T cells, are proposed to play key regulatory roles in the thymus, such as promoting the maturation of thymic epithelial cells and the negative selection of self-reactive T cells.11,12 However, the molecular mechanisms of innate CD8 T cell differentiation that divert them from conventional CD8 T cell development have not been completely characterized.

All T cells require cytokine signaling for their thymic generation, among which cytokines of the common γ-chain (γc) family play non-redundant roles in the lineage specification of T cells.13 In this regard, the γc family cytokine interleukin-2 (IL-2) is required for the generation of regulatory T cells expressing the transcription factor Foxp3,14 while IL-15 is necessary for invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells expressing the zinc finger transcription factor PLZF.15 For the thymic generation of conventional naive CD8 T cells, it is IL-7 that plays a major role because IL-7 is necessary to activate the transcription factors STAT5 and Runx3d, which in turn impose CD8 lineage fate.16,17 But, there is also a certain redundancy of IL-7 with other cytokines in this process18 that becomes apparent in the absence of IL-7 receptor expression.19 In contrast, the generation of thymic innate CD8 T cells depends on IL-4, such that innate CD8 T cells fail to develop in the absence of IL-4.3,4,20 How cytokines would determine the subset specification of T cells remains incompletely understood, but is likely associated with the selective induction of master transcription factors. Notably, innate CD8 T cells are marked by expressing the T-box family nuclear factor Eomes, whereby IL-4 stimulation reportedly induces Eomes expression in naive CD8 T cells.8,21,22,23 In this regard, IL-4 has been considered both a necessary and sufficient factor in driving the functional and phenotypic differentiation of innate CD8 T cells in the thymus.

When attempting to recapitulate the IL-4-mediated innate cell differentiation in a defined in vitro culture system, however, we found that in vitro IL-4-stimulated CD8SP cells significantly differed from in vivo-generated thymic innate CD8 T cells. While the IL-4 treatment sufficed to induce Eomes expression, it failed to induce the expression of chemokine receptor CXCR3, a prominent marker associated with innate CD8 T cells.24 Moreover, such in vitro-generated Eomes+ CD8 T cells also lacked high-level expression of the non-classical MHC-I molecule Qa-2, a surface marker that we identified to be associated with innate CD8 T cells. These results prompted us to consider whether factors other than IL-4 would be involved in driving the differentiation of innate CD8 T cells. Type I IFN signaling had been previously found to upregulate CXCR3 and Qa-2 expression,25,26 and there is an increasing body of literature that tonic type I IFN signaling, which refers to the sterile production of type I IFNs under steady-state conditions, plays a multifaceted role in the thymus.27,28,29 Thus, we embarked on this study to examine a potential role of thymic type I IFN signaling in innate CD8 T cell differentiation, leading us to identify a previously unappreciated role for the IFN-α/β receptor (IFNAR) in the generation of innate CD8 T cells. Because IL-4 alone is insufficient to generate innate CD8 T cells in the absence of type I IFN and vice versa, these results reveal a cytokine cross-talk in the subset specification of CD8 T cells that has ramifications on our current understanding of how cytotoxic CD8 T cells are generated and acquire effector function.

Results

Innate CD8 T cells arise at a late phase of thymic development

Overexpression of the γc cytokine receptor promotes the generation of innate CD8 T cells, such that γc transgenic mice (γcTg) represent a useful experimental model to study the mechanism of innate CD8 T cell development.4,30 Indeed, γcTg mice contain significantly more mature CD8 single-positive (CD8SP) thymocytes than their littermate controls (LMCs) (Figure 1A), and such an increase in CD8SP cells can be attributed to dramatically increased fractions of CD8 T cells possessing the innate ability to produce proinflammatory IFNγ (Figure 1B).4,30 As previously established,31 IFNγ-producing innate CD8 T cells can also be identified by their distinct expression of the memory markers CXCR3 and CD44 (CXCR3hiCD44hi) and containing large amounts of the transcription factor Eomes (Figure 1C). Conventional naive CD8 T cells, on the other hand, are phenotypically CXCR3loCD44lo and lack Eomes expression (Figure 1C). To understand the developmental timing of innate CD8 T cell generation in the thymus, we utilized Eomes expression as a molecular marker, and CD69 versus MHC-I expression as a matrix to map the appearance of innate CD8 T cells in post-selection thymocytes. In C57BL/6 background mice, the classical MHC-I molecules comprise H-2KbDb, which we used to assess MHC-I expression. Accordingly, CD69hiMHC-Ilo thymocytes are semi-mature (SM),25 and we found them to lack Eomes+ cells (Figure 1D). SM thymocytes further differentiate into CD69hiMHC-Ihi mature 1 (M1) stage cells, followed by their maturation into CD69loMHC-Ihi mature 2 (M2) stage cells (Figure 1D).25 Notably, Eomes+ CD8SP cells first appeared in the M1 population, but the majority of Eomes-expressing CD8SP thymocytes were found in the M2 population (Figure 1D). Very high levels of Qa-2 identify thymic innate CD8 T cells

In addition to classical H-2KbDb MHC-I molecules, non-classical MHC-Ib molecules are also expressed on T cells. In this regard, Qa-2 is a non-classical MHC-I molecule that is highly expressed on terminally mature thymocytes,25 prompting us to consider whether innate CD8 T cells would upregulate Qa-2. In fact, correlating Qa-2 with Eomes expression proved to be an excellent tool to identify innate CD8 T cells, as we found high levels of Qa-2 expression to be associated with Eomes+ innate T cells in the thymus (Figure 2A) and with Eomes+ innate-like CD8 T cells in peripheral tissues (Figures S1A and S1B). Moreover, the frequency of Qa-2 high (Qa-2hi), Eomes-expressing innate CD8 T cells dramatically increased upon stage M1 to M2 differentiation of CD8SP cells (Figure S2A). Indeed, assessing Qa-2 levels in naive versus innate CD8SP cells, as defined by the differential expression of CXCR3 versus CD44, or IL-4Rα versus CD24, as well as Eomes versus Qa-2 (Figure S2B), enabled us to accurately discriminate between naive and innate CD8 T cells (Figure 2B). Notably, Qa-2 was absent in preselection immature thymocytes but upregulated upon CD8 T cell differentiation, and very highly expressed in innate CD8 T cells (Figures S2C and S3). Innate CD8 T cells also differed from naive CD8 T cells in their sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1P1) expression, an essential chemokine receptor for thymocyte egress, that is only induced at late stages of thymocyte maturation after CD69 expression is extinguished.33,34 Contrary to naive CD8 T cells that contained a large fraction of S1P1-expressing cells, the frequency of S1P1+ cells was markedly reduced in innate CD8 T cells (Figure 2C). To assess other molecules involved in thymocyte egress, we generated γcTg mice expressing the Eomes-GFP reporter (i.e., Eomes-GFPγcTg),35 permitting us to accurately identify and characterize innate CD8 T cells without the harsh fixation and permeabilization procedures required to detect intranuclear Eomes proteins (Figures 2D, S5A, and S5B). Unlike for S1P1, here we found no differences in the protein abundance of CD62L, a cell adhesion molecule and maturation marker for naive T cells.36 On the other hand, the expression of CD103/integrin β7, a tissue retention molecule,37,38 and CCR9, a gut-trophic chemokine receptor,39 was significantly downregulated in innate CD8 T cells (Figures 2E, S5C, and S5D). These results indicate that innate CD8 T cells substantially differ from naive CD8 T cells in their migration/residency-associated phenotype. Whether and how the very high levels of Qa-2 expression would contribute to these aspects remains to be addressed.

IL-4 drives the differentiation of thymic innate CD8 T cells

Cytokine IL-4 is a primary driver of innate CD8 T cell generation, as IL-4 signaling upregulates Eomes expression and induces their effector function.22,23 However, we were uncertain whether IL-4 would also be responsible for driving the high-level expression of Qa-2, which we identified as a marker associated with innate CD8 T cells (Figure 2A). To address this question, we cultured freshly isolated thymocytes in the presence or absence of IL-4 and monitored their differentiation into innate CD8 T cells in vitro. Importantly, wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 mice contain very low numbers of innate CD8 T cells,4,40 such that most innate CD8 T cells arising in the culture may be attributed to the effects of in vitro IL-4 stimulation. As expected, IL-4 signaling upregulated IL-4 receptor expression (IL-4Rα) (Figure 3A, top) and high levels of Eomes, but not T-bet (Figure 3B, left), consistent with IL-4 driving the acquisition of an innate-like phenotype.22,31 On the other hand, such in vitro-generated Eomes+ CD8SP cells differed from in vivo-generated innate CD8 T cells in their lack of CXCR3 expression (Figure 3A, bottom), and the failure to generate IFNγ- and TNFα-producing classical CD44hi innate CD8 T cells (Figure 3C). Notably, in vitro-generated Eomes+ CD8 T cells also lacked high levels of Qa-2 expression (Figure 3B, right), suggesting that the full phenotypic differentiation into innate CD8 T cells requires additional cues. Because Qa-2 expression is known to be controlled by type I IFNs,25 these results led us to consider a potential role for tonic type I IFNs in the IL-4-driven differentiation of innate CD8 T cells. Type I IFN signaling promotes thymic innate CD8 T cell development

To test the requirement for type I IFN signaling in innate CD8 T cell generation, we next generated γcTg mice that lack IFN receptor expression. We produced such Ifnar−/−γcTg mice by breeding γcTg mice with type I IFN receptor (IFNAR)-deficient Ifnar−/− mice. The overall cell numbers, phenotype, and differentiation of total thymocytes did not significantly differ between IFNAR-deficient γcTg and WT γcTg mice (Figures 4A and S6A). However, we found a marked decrease in innate CD8 T cells as identified by their abundance in CXCR3 and CD44 expression (Figure 4B), intracellular Eomes and Qa-2 levels (Figure 4C), and the ability to produce IFNγ (Figure 4D). While IFNAR levels do not differ between naive and innate CD8 T cells (Figure S6B), IFNAR deficiency selectively affected innate CD8 T cells, identifying type I IFN signaling as a contributing factor. To gain further mechanistic insight into the role of type I IFNs, we focused on STAT1, a signal transducer and transcription factor immediately downstream of IFNAR signaling.43 To test the role of STAT1 during the development of innate CD8 T cells, we deleted STAT1 in a T cell-specific manner by generating Stat1fl/flCD4Cre mice, which we refer to as Stat1ΔT. Stat1ΔT mice were largely unaffected in their T cell development, except for a profound lack of Qa-2 expression on mature CD8SP cells (Figure S7A). Importantly, Stat1ΔT mice were also substantially impaired in the generation of innate CD8 T cells, as assessed by CXCR3 versus CD44 and Eomes versus Qa-2 expression (Figures S7B and S7C), and the markedly reduced production of IFNγ upon PMA and ionomycin stimulation (Figure S7D). In agreement, Eomes+ ILT cells in peripheral lymphoid tissues were also dramatically reduced (Figures S8A and S8B). Importantly, the failure to generate innate CD8 T cells in the absence of IFNAR/STAT1 signaling, as is the case for Ifnar−/−γcTg mice, was not because of the impaired generation of iNKT cells, the main producers of IL-4 in the thymus.44,45 Total iNKT cell development of Ifnar−/−γcTg mice remained intact and comparable to that of γcTg mice (Figure 4E, top). Nonetheless, we noticed some changes in the subset composition of iNKT cells (Figure 4E, bottom; Figure S9), which decreased the overall availability of intrathymic IL-4. In fact, the number and frequency of IL-4-producing thymocytes in IFNAR-deficient γcTg mice were significantly decreased compared to WT γcTg mice (Figure 4F). Altogether, these results document a previously unrecognized requirement for IFNAR expression in the generation of thymic innate CD8 T cells.

Identification of a type I IFN/STAT1 axis in IL-4-driven innate CD8 T cell generation

We next wished to formally demonstrate that it is the lack of type I IFN and not IL-4 signaling that impedes the generation of thymic innate CD8 T cells in Ifnar−/−γcTg mice. To this end, we infused both WT and Ifnar−/− mice with recombinant mouse IL-4 proteins and assessed the generation of thymic innate CD8 T cells. As previously reported,44 a two-week IL-4 injection regimen of recombinant IL-4 complexed with anti-IL-4 mAb is sufficient to induce a marked increase in thymic innate CD8 T cell generation in WT mice. We affirmed this effect by the dramatically increased frequencies of Eomes+Qa-2hi (Figure 5A) and CXCR3hiCD44hi (Figure 5B) cells that would correspond to innate CD8 T cells in WT CD8SP thymocytes (Figure S10A). Notably, such innate CD8 T cells generated by IL-4 infusion were fully functional as they expressed copious amounts of IFNγ upon PMA and ionomycin stimulation (Figure 5C). In contrast, injecting the same IL-4/anti-IL-4 mAb complex into Ifnar−/− mice showed markedly impaired generation of innate CD8 T cells (Figures 5A, 5B, and S10B), and substantially reduced protein production of IFNγ (Figures 5C and S10C). These results affirm that IFNAR expression promotes the IL-4-driven differentiation of thymic innate CD8 T cells. Importantly, the failure to generate IFNγ-producing CD8 T cells, and more specifically to generate CD44hiIFNγ+ innate CD8 T cells (Figure 5D) was independent of thymic iNKT cells as their overall numbers and subset composition were unaffected (Figures S10D and S10E). Discussion

The mature CD8 T cell compartment contains antigen-inexperienced T cells that display memory T cell-like phenotypes and exert effector functions similar to antigen-experienced true memory T cells.46,47 Such antigen-inexperienced memory-phenotype (MP) CD8 T cells comprise at least two distinct subsets, i.e., innate CD8 T cells and virtual memory (VM) CD8 T cells.31,48 Both innate and VM CD8 T cells express the transcription factor Eomes, but they differ in their sites of developmental origin and their cytokine requirement for differentiation and survival. VM CD8 T cells are generated in peripheral tissues and depend on IL-15 for their maintenance. Innate CD8 T cells, on the other hand, are generated in the thymus, where they were originally described in Itk-deficient mice,7 but subsequently found in many other mouse strains as well.3 Unlike VM CD8 T cells that depend on IL-15,48 innate CD8 T cells require IL-4, which, in turn, is produced by iNKT cells expressing the transcription factor PLZF.49,50 Our current study significantly expands this cytokine-driven differentiation pathway of innate CD8 T cell-specific thymic differentiation, in that it exposes steady-state signaling by type I IFNs as a major regulatory factor for innate CD8 T cells.

Recent advances in our understanding of sterile IFN production have uncovered an increasingly significant role of type I IFNs in the thymus.51 The type I IFN family cytokines comprise 7 subtypes, including IFNβ, which is constitutively produced in low amounts in peripheral tissues, and which is proposed to be involved in priming immune cells for cytokine response and activation.52 In the thymus, low levels of constitutively produced (tonic) type I IFNs were reported to help establish the TCR repertoire of developing thymocytes,27,28 and control the maturation of thymic epithelial cells.11 Our findings expand the scope of type I IFN in regulating thymus biology by revealing a previously underappreciated role of type I IFNs in the generation of innate CD8 T cells. A positive regulatory role for type I IFNs in peripheral effector T cells had been previously observed, where naive (CD44lo) CD8 LN T cells contain a subset of CD8 T cells, identified as CD5hiLy6C+, that are predisposed to higher effector function, and that are generated in a type I IFN-dependent manner.53 Type I IFNs have also been reported to directly drive Eomes expression, converting them into unconventional memory-like CD8 T cells.54 However, a requirement for IL-4 in this process has been untested, such that it is unclear whether type I IFN alone can substitute for IL-4 in innate CD8 T cell generation.

While IL-4 remains an essential factor for innate CD8 T cells, the need for concomitant signaling by type I IFNs is both interesting and puzzling. Moreover, it is type I IFN and not type II IFNγ signaling that promotes the IL-4-dependent innate CD8 T cell generation, as we previously excluded an IFNγ requirement in this process.44 Thus, cytokine receptor signaling downstream of the IFNAR, but not the IFNγ receptor (i.e., IFNγR), acts in concert with the IL-4 signaling pathway to induce Eomes and drive innate CD8 T cell generation. Considering that type II and type I IFN signaling share many downstream signaling pathways that converge into STAT1 activation,55 STAT1 is likely the major signal transducer for the cytokine-driven generation of innate CD8 T cells. Our preliminary results from injecting recombinant IL-4 into Stat1ΔT mice revealed impaired thymic innate CD8 T cell generation in the absence of STAT1, confirming that STAT1 activation downstream of IFNAR signaling plays a major role in this process. Why STAT1 activation downstream of the IFNγR cannot replace IFNAR signaling remains unclear to us, and we aim to address this question in our future studies.

Finally, our findings show that type I IFN signaling induces very high levels of Qa-2 expression on innate CD8 T cells, which could have functional consequences for this T cell subset. Qa-2 is a 40 kDa GPI-linked MHC-I-like surface protein56 that is considered the functional homolog of the human HLA-G molecule.57 While HLA-G in humans is mainly tolerogenic,58 the role of Qa-2 in mice is more complex. Qa-2 can be immunosuppressive by engaging inhibitory receptors on NK cells,59 but also co-stimulatory when cross-linked on T cells.60 In this regard, Qa-2 could be a cell-intrinsic activator of innate CD8 T cells while also being immunosuppressive in a cell-extrinsic manner. HLA-G, on the other hand, is expressed on a subpopulation of immunosuppressive T cells associated with cancer and autoimmunity,61,62 and such HLA-G+ T cells exemplify nonclassical MHC-I-expressing immune cells that control adaptive immunity.63 Along with HLA-E, the ligand for NKG2A/CD94, which mediates immune evasion,64 it remains unclear if human non-classical MHC-I molecules would perform functions similar to mouse Qa-2 in CD8 T cells, necessitating further studies.

Limitations of the study

We appreciate that our study is limited in that we are uncertain which aspects of innate CD8 phenotype and function are contingent on type I IFN versus IL-4 signaling. Recent data showed that innate CD8 T cells in neonates express higher levels of GM-CSF, IL-10, IL-17, and IL-13 than conventional CD8 T cells in adult mice.65 It would be important to discriminate between the effects of IL-4 and type I IFN in controlling the expression of innate CD8 effector molecules. On the other hand, we cannot exclude the likely possibility of innate CD8 T cells comprising various subsets associated with distinct cytokine production.6 Finally, it will also be necessary to examine whether steady-state production of type I IFN is only necessary for the generation of innate CD8 T cells or also for other subsets of ILT cells in the thymus that will include iNKT cells, MAIT cells, as well as peptide-specific PLZF+ ILT (PILT) cells.66,67 In line with an increasing body of studies on sterile IFN production within the thymus, identifying a role for IFNs in establishing an innate defense mechanism underscores the importance of type I IFN as a central element of cellular immunity.

Flow cytometric analysis

Single-cell suspensions from the thymus, spleen, and lymph nodes were stained with fluorescent antibodies and analyzed by flow cytometry. Cells were incubated with specific antibodies for 40 min at 4 °C to stain surface markers. Nuclear transcription factors were stained using the Foxp3 intracellular staining buffer set (eBioscience), and intracellular cytokines were stained with the IC fixation kit (eBioscience), following the manufacturer’s protocols. For detecting cytokine production, cells were stimulated with 50 ng/mL PMA (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1 μM ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) in the presence of 3 μg/mL brefeldin A (Invitrogen) for 3 h before staining. For S1P1 receptor staining, cells were incubated with 70 μg/mL unconjugated rat anti-S1P1 on ice for 90 min, followed by staining with biotinylated anti-rat F(ab’)2 for 40 min on ice. After biotinylated antibody staining, 100 μL of staining buffer containing 2% rat serum was added for blocking. Cells were then stained with streptavidin-Alexa Fluor 594 with surface marker antibodies in staining buffer containing 2% rat serum for 30 min on ice. Live cells were identified based on forward scatter profiles, excluding propidium iodide-positive dead cells. For fixed cell analyses, Ghost Dye Violet 510 (Tonbo) was used to exclude non-viable cells. Data acquisition was performed using LSR Fortessa or LSRII flow cytometers (BD Biosciences), and analyses were conducted with FlowJo software (version 10.6.2, FlowJo).

In vitro culture of thymocytes

For in vitro culture, freshly isolated thymocytes were plated at a density of 5 x 106 cells/mL in survival medium (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS) containing either IL-7 (0.5 ng/mL) or IL-2 (1 ng/mL). Cells were cultured in medium alone or in the presence of additional cytokines, including IL-4 (20 ng/mL), IFNβ1 (20 ng/mL), IL-12 (20 ng/mL), or IL-18 (20 ng/mL). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 7.5% CO2. After 24 or 48 h, cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Infusion with IL-4/anti-IL-4 monoclonal antibody complex

Mice were i.p. injected with 2 μg of recombinant murine IL-4 (Peprotech) complexed with anti-IL-4 antibodies (eBioscience) or vehicle every other day for 2 weeks. The IL-4 and anti-IL-4 antibody complex was prepared as previously described.44 In brief, 20 μg of recombinant IL-4 (Peprotech) was reconstituted in 80 μL distilled water and mixed with 120 μL anti-IL-4 antibody (1 mg/mL, clone 11B11; eBioscience). The cytokine/antibody mixture was incubated for 10 min at RT, then diluted 10-fold in PBS, and kept at 4 °C until further use.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was evaluated using multiple statistical methods, including an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test and two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post hoc test. p values of <0.05 was considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.005, ∗∗∗p < 0.001).
