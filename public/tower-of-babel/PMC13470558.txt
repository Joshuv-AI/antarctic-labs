Effect of glucose-induced glycation on the interaction behavior of furan derivatives with chicken skin collagen
Authors: Guangyang Zhou, Kexin Cheng, Huan Liu, Kexin Li, Enzhe Liu, Dengyong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetaining volatile compounds is critical for the sensory quality of smoked meat. This study investigated how glucose-induced glycation modifies chicken skin collagen and its interaction behavior with furan derivatives. During glycation, the browning index increased from 0.13 to 0.18, confirming Maillard reaction progression. Glycation decreased D50 from 136.4 to 124.4 μm and increased the absolute zeta potential from 1.74 to 4.59 mV, indicating altered aggregation behavior and surface charge. The reactive sulfhydryl content increased from 8.58 to 25.49 nmol/mg, accompanied by enhanced surface hydrophobicity and a shift from ordered structures to random coils. These structural changes were associated with changes in the headspace concentration of furan derivatives, especially 2-acetylfuran. Molecular docking suggested that hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions involving ARG-1255 and TYR-1237 contributed to collagen-furan derivative interactions. These insights provide a theoretical framework for using glycation to control aroma retention in collagen-rich matrices.Keywords: Chicken skin collagen, Furan derivatives, Maillard reaction, Glycation

Introduction

As a dense reservoir of proteins, fats, and essential nutrients, meat products remain a fundamental component of the modern human diet (Patinho et al., 2024). With the rise in global socioeconomic conditions, consumer preference has evolved, prioritizing the nutritional value and sensory profile of meat over mere abundance (W. Zhang et al., 2017). Smoked meat products, a category of traditional Chinese foods, are widely appreciated for their distinct flavors (Tao et al., 2025). Compared to traditional wood smoking, sugar smoking produces a notably milder and smoother flavor. This distinct aroma primarily stems from the thermal breakdown of sucrose into furan derivatives (L. Zhang et al., 2022). Furan derivatives, which are heterocyclic compounds containing a five-membered ring with oxygen, play a key role in imparting smoky aroma notes in foods (Mokh et al., 2024). In smoked fish, compounds such as furfural, 2-acetylfuran, and 5-methyl-2-furfural have been recognized as essential odor-active constituents (Varlet et al., 2006). Consistent with previous findings from our research group, furan and its derivatives have been identified as critical volatile compounds in smoked chicken (Yao et al., 2020). The interaction of these furan derivatives with meat matrices, especially proteins, considerably influences the comprehensive organoleptic quality of smoked meat products the integrated sensory characteristics of smoked meat formulations (Zhao et al., 2022). The skin of yellow-feather broilers, prized for its superior texture, serves as the main adsorption interface in smoked chicken production. Although lipids and carbohydrates within the skin act as flavor precursors, collagen (approx. 70% of dry weight) is critical for the retention of volatile compounds through physicochemical interactions. Hence, developing safe and effective strategies to enhance the interaction between furan compounds and proteins.

Protein modification has become a central approach for enhancing protein functionality, particularly their capacity to bind flavor compounds. Various chemical strategies—including acylation, methylation, phosphorylation, sulfation, and glycation—have been utilized to tailor functional properties of proteins (Boutureira & Bernardes, 2015). Among these approaches, glycation is particularly relevant to food systems because it can occur naturally between proteins and reducing sugars during processing and storage. Glycation stands out as an efficient and green modification technique that has shown considerable promise in improving flavor retention. This process facilitates the formation of covalent bonds between carbonyl moieties of reducing sugars and accessible amino residues in proteins, resulting in alterations to their structural conformation and physicochemical properties (Yu et al., 2025). Under common food processing conditions, where both sugars and proteins are present at high concentrations, glycation readily occurs. The resulting glycoconjugates benefit from the hydrophilicity of carbohydrates and the structural properties of proteins, leading to improved functional performance (Tan et al., 2021). These structural shifts, such as the enhanced surface accessibility of non-polar amino acid moieties and the aggregation of peptide chains, contribute to enhanced flavor adsorption. For example, Chen et al. (2025) observed that glycation modifies the secondary structure of β-lactoglobulin, which in turn influences the stability and profile of volatile compounds, including key odorants like 2,3-butanedione and 2-propanol. Similarly, H. Liu et al. (2023) confirmed that glycation induced structural unfolding and surface wrinkling in myosin, concurrent with a structural shift from α-helical to random coil configurations, thereby exposing more hydrophobic groups and amino acid residues and strengthening its binding toward furan derivatives. These studies suggest that glycation-induced conformational changes enhance hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic forces via the Maillard reaction, significantly improving protein-flavor binding.

However, most existing studies have focused on myofibrillar or plant proteins. Despite being the most abundant and widely distributed functional protein in mammals, collagen has received comparatively limited research attention. Although previous studies have shown that glycation improves techno-functional attributes of collagen—including solubility and emulsification—the influence of Maillard-induced glycation on its flavor-binding characteristics has not yet been thoroughly investigated.

Therefore, this study was designed to: (i) preliminarily monitor the glycation reaction between collagen and glucose based on the browning index; (ii) characterize the impact of glycation on collagen structure and properties; and (iii) elucidate the interaction behavior of glycated collagen with furan derivatives. The findings are expected to provide scientific support for targeted flavor regulation strategies in collagen-rich meat systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh chicken skin (1500 g) was obtained from 4-month-old yellow-feather broilers at Wanda Supermarket (Jinzhou, China) and kept at 4 °C until further processing. Acetic acid, along with gas chromatography-grade standards of furfural (99%), 5-methylfurfural (98%), 2-acetylfuran (99%), and 2,5-diformylfuran (98%), were acquired from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.

Extraction and determination of collagen content

The specific parameters and procedures for extracting collagen from chicken were based on a previously established protocol from our research group (Li et al., 2025). The extracted collagen was lyophilized, and its concentration was quantified using a Shimadzu UV-2550 spectrophotometer (Japan) by measuring hydroxyproline content at 558 nm, referenced against a standard curve (y = 0.2257× + 0.0502, R2 = 0.9998).

Preparation of glycated chicken skin collagen solution

The glycated collagen solution was prepared following the method of Dou et al. (2021) with slight modifications. Freeze-dried collagen was combined with 0.5 mol/L acetic acid and subjected to magnetic stirring at 4 °C for 24 h to ensure full dissolution, yielding a protein solution with a concentration of 5 mg/mL. The collagen was then combined with glucose at a mass ratio of 1:1 in a 6 mL solution, maintaining a collagen concentration of 5 mg/mL and a pH equivalent to that of raw muscle tissue (pH 6.91). Glucose was selected as a representative reducing sugar due to its common occurrence in food systems and its moderate Maillard reactivity, which allowed controlled glycation while avoiding excessive browning or rapid cross-linking. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8 h. This mild temperature was selected to facilitate the Maillard reaction while preventing thermal denaturation of the collagen structure. Following incubation, to avoid any subsequent alterations due to heat, the reaction was stopped by rapid cooling in an ice-water bath. The solution was subsequently dialyzed at 4 °C for 24 h to remove unreacted sugar. Processed samples were kept at 4 °C pending subsequent analysis.

Maillard reaction progression

The absorbance of melanoidins, Maillard reaction products, can be measured at 420 nm. Based on this principle, the progression of the Maillard reaction can be monitored using ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometry (Syeunda & Awika, 2024). A volume of 200 μL from each reaction mixture was employed for spectrophotometric detection, with phosphate buffer (PBS) as a blank. All absorbance values were kept <1 to avoid exceeding the spectrophotometer's upper limit.

Particle size and zeta potential determination

Particle size can reflect the state of dispersion and extent of aggregation of protein molecules, which was subjected to a BT-9300ST (Bettersize Instruments Ltd., Dandong, China). The zeta potential measurements followed the experimental procedure detailed in X. Chen et al. (2016) using a ZS90 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, UK). Prior to analysis, collagen samples were diluted to 1 mg/mL with PBS and brought to 25 °C.

Determination of surface hydrophobicity

The surface hydrophobicity was assessed using the bromophenol blue (BPB) using a binding assay according to the method of Chelh et al. (2006). 1 mL of glycated collagen (1 mg/mL in PBS) was mixed with 200 μL of BPB (1.0 mg/mL), vortexed for 10 min, and centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 10 min. Then, 0.4 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 3.6 mL of PBS, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm. The blank control was generated with 0.5 mol/L acetic acid. Microstructure analysis

The morphological alterations of chicken skin collagen induced by glycation were examined through scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Prior to imaging, lyophilized collagen specimens were affixed to conductive adhesive tape and subjected to gold sputtering. Microstructural examination was performed with a field-emission SEM (Hitachi S-4800, Tokyo, Japan) using an accelerating voltage of 3.0 kV and a magnification of 500×, according to the method described by Tamilmozhi et al. (2013).

Raman spectroscopy

Using a high-resolution Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba Scientific, France), we acquired spectral data to assess the secondary structure of collagen. Based on the method described by X.-L. Xu et al. (2011), a thin layer of collagen solution was evenly applied onto a glass slide and examined using a 20× objective. Spectral data were collected under the following conditions: 785 nm laser excitation, 90 s exposure time, 512 accumulations, covering a wavenumber range of 500–2100 cm−1. The collected spectral data were processed using PeakFit (version 4.12, SeaSolve Software Inc., USA) for deconvolution and quantitative assessment of the secondary structure composition, encompassing α-helix, β-sheet, and random coil conformations.

Detection of the binding capacity of collagen to furan derivatives

Preparation of furan derivatives

The solutions of furan derivatives were prepared following the previously established experimental protocols from our research group (Li et al., 2025). Each compound was first dissolved in deionized water and then diluted with PBS to yield a final concentration of 50 ppm. Finally, the solutions were sonicated for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution.

Solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (SPME-GC–MS) analysis

The sample preparation and analytical procedures were consistent with those described in our previous research (Li et al., 2025). Specifically, the furan stock solution, collagen solution, and PBS were combined at a volume ratio of 1:1:3 in headspace vials, which were immediately sealed. To allow the system to reach equilibrium, the mixtures were stored at 4 °C for 12 h prior to headspace analysis. Control samples were prepared by replacing the collagen solution with PBS. The final concentration of each furan compound in the headspace vials was 10 ppm.

The interaction behavior of collagen toward furan derivatives was assessed using SPME-GC–MS (GCMS-QP2030NX, Shimadzu, Japan). The sample was equilibrated at 35 °C for 10 min, after which the analytes were extracted with an SPME fiber for 45 min. The temperature program and instrument parameters for subsequent sample analysis were identical to those previously established by our research group (Li et al., 2025).

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking can simulate the binding sites and interaction forces between small molecules and protein residues (Inthachat et al., 2025). In this study, the amino acid sequence of chicken collagen (P02467) was retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/), and the protein structure was predicted and modeled using AlphaFold. The structures of small molecules, including furfural and 5-methylfurfural, were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). After removing water molecules and other ligands from the model, followed by glycation treatment, molecular docking was performed using AutoDock 4.0. Finally, PyMOL was utilized to visualize the specific binding sites and interaction forces between the receptor and ligands.

Statistical analysis

Dataset assembly was conducted preliminarily in a Microsoft Excel environment. Statistical evaluations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, and intergroup comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by least significant difference (LSD) test. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted, and all quantitative results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

Effect of glycation modification on the extent of Maillard reaction and interaction behavior of furan derivatives in collagen

The progression of the glycation reaction was assessed by monitoring sample absorbance at 420 nm, which is widely recognized as an effective indicator of browning degree and thus the progression of the Maillard reaction (Dou et al., 2021). Throughout the incubation period, the sustained increase in the browning index, which rose from 0.13 (0 h) to 0.18 (8 h), indicates that the Maillard reaction with glucose as the donor progressed steadily without reaching equilibrium or cessation (Fig. 1). Previous research has shown that when myofibrillar proteins were glycated using glucose, the glycation process was carried out continuously for up to 8 h at temperatures between 37 °C and 40 °C (H. Liu et al., 2023). This outcome provides a foundation for subsequent research on the effects of varying degrees of glycation on the structure and function of collagen.

Changes in the browning index of glycated collagen measured at 420 nm. The different letters indicate the significant differences between the treatments (p < 0.05).

Fig. 2 displays the headspace concentrations of free furan derivatives compounds detected above glycated collagen samples. The headspace concentration of free volatile compounds in control vials (with PBS instead of protein solution) was defined as 100%. The highest free percentages were recorded at 0 h, with 5-methylfurfural (117.99%) and furfural (111.17%) leading the group. Such values (>100%) suggest that unmodified collagen initially aids the release of furan compounds. Conversely, samples incubated for 8 h demonstrated the strongest binding capacity (p < 0.05), followed by the 6 h and 4 h groups. This improvement is likely associated with glycation-induced structural unfolding of collagen, which exposes latent binding sites. Evidence from the surface hydrophobicity and reactive sulfhydryl assays supports this structural interpretation. (Menezes et al., 2020). Increased surface hydrophobicity can enhance binding sites for furan derivatives.

Changes of adsorption capacity of collagen to furan flavor compounds under different glycation modification time. The different letters indicate the significant differences between the treatments (p < 0.05).

Among the volatile compounds tested, 2-acetylfuran exhibited a superior binding affinity to glycated collagen (p < 0.05). This specificity stems from its distinct acetyl moiety, which acts as a robust hydrogen bond acceptor—specifically interacting with arginine residues—unlike the functional groups found in other furan derivatives. Such results suggest that the interaction behavior may extend beyond simple physical entrapment and may also involve chemical interactions governed by the functional architecture of the flavor molecules. (Shen et al., 2019).

Effects of glycation modification on the particle size distribution, zeta potential, and microstructure of collagen

Particle reflects the dimensions of collagen molecules or aggregates, indicating their dispersion state and degree of aggregation in aqueous solution. As shown in Table 1, as glycation progressed, a concomitant reduction in collagen particle size was observed (p < 0.05). Notably, the median D50 exhibited a reduction from 136.40 μm to 124.43 μm, the D32 value from 150.30 μm to 135.33 μm, and the D43 value from 193.47 μm to 186.53 μm. Compared to the initial state (0 h), glycation treatment significantly suppressed random aggregation of protein molecules, thereby promoting the assembly of smaller, more monodisperse aggregates exhibiting enhanced surface area. These results agree with earlier findings by H. Liu et al. (2023), confirming that glycation markedly decreases collagen particle size. The covalent attachment of sugar chains rearranges the molecular conformation of collagen, yielding a more compact structure with reduced spatial demand. Furthermore, grafted glycans potentially strengthen intermolecular forces promoting denser molecular packing and consequently smaller aggregate dimensions.

Table 1 illustrates a significant shift in the zeta potential of collagen following glycation. Native collagen exhibited a minimal net negative charge (−1.74 mV; p < 0.05). However, as the reaction proceeded, the absolute zeta potential steadily increased, reaching a peak of −4.59 mV after an 8-h incubation (p < 0.05). The shift in zeta potential reflects the depletion of amino groups (−NH₂), as described by Li et al. (2024). Concurrently, the hydration and ionization of the carbohydrate chains increase the electronegativity of the complex (Han et al., 2022), a trend potentially reinforced by anionic Maillard intermediates (Shi et al., 2022). Stronger electrostatic repulsion prevents the random association of collagen fibrils, resulting in a finer dispersion state. This structural refinement may increase the availability of surface interaction sites, thereby facilitating interactions between collagen and furan derivatives.

Glycated proteins not only affect bioactivity but also influence the final molecular structure of the protein, as extensively studied in the literature (J. Xu et al., 2024). Fig. 3 shows spatial structure images of collagen obtained using scanning electron microscopy under different glycation modification times. The images reveal that increased incubation time transforms collagen from large sheet-like structures into porous network structures, with protein fibers exhibiting extensive irregular entanglement. This suggests that prolonged incubation enhances glycation modification, leading to polysaccharide accumulation on the surface of collagen molecules. More sufficient intermolecular interactions and reorganization through processes such as hydrolysis and cross-linking gradually form a porous network structure (Y. Wang et al., 2024). Glycation promotes the attachment of sugar chains to collagen, facilitating the unfolding of its polypeptide chains. These structural alterations align with the trends observed in both particle size and zeta potential measurements, collectively indicating that sugar chain incorporation modifies the aggregation behavior of collagen, promoting the formation of denser and more compact structures.

SEM images of collagen at different glycation modification times.

Effect of glycation modification on the secondary structure of collagen

To elucidate the conformational shifts in collagen induced by glycation, Raman spectroscopy was employed to monitor secondary structure alterations across different reaction times. Fig. 4 highlights that native collagen possessed the most ordered structure, with an α-helix content of 25.08% (p < 0.05). However, as the glycation reaction advanced, this ordered conformation progressively diminished, reaching a minimum of 17.35% after 8 h (p < 0.05). In parallel, the proportion of random coils surged from 0% in the control to 28.70% by the end of the treatment (p < 0.05). These structural transitions are consistent with the trends reported by Cao et al. (2020), confirming that glycation disturbs the native α-helical architecture, suggesting that glycation promotes partial unfolding and greater structural disorder within collagen. The decline in α-helix content implies that glucose molecules may interact with amino acid residues along the polypeptide chain, disrupting intrinsic hydrogen-bonding patterns (Ahmed et al., 2018). Furthermore, covalent grafting of glucose onto the protein backbone promotes the formation of intermolecular interactions-including hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces-that subsequently disrupt the stability of helical structures (Shi et al., 2022). Given that α-helical structures are predominantly located within the hydrophobic interior of proteins, their shift toward random coils reflects the disintegration of collagen's triple-helical structure, resulting in a more open, flexible, and disordered morphology (Q. Wang et al., 2023).

Effect of glycation modification on the secondary structure of collagen. The different letters indicate the significant differences between the treatments (p < 0.05).

Such perturbations in secondary structure imply that glycation impedes the orderly fibrillogenesis of collagen. This shift from a stiff, crystalline-like architecture to a more flexible, amorphous conformation significantly improves the protein's functional versatility. Notably, this structural reorganization may provide a more favorable basis for the interaction between collagen and furan derivatives, likely by increasing the accessibility of interaction sites.

Effects of glycation modifications on collagen reactive sulfhydryl groups and surface hydrophobicity

Sulfhydryl groups represent one of the most chemically active functional moieties in protein structures and can be categorized into surface-exposed reactive sulfhydryls and buried internal sulfhydryls. Surface-exposed reactive sulfhydryl groups readily undergo oxidation, yielding stable disulfide bridges and consequently diminishing free sulfhydryl content. (Di Simplicio et al., 1991). The content of reactive sulfhydryl groups in collagen at different glycation times is shown in Fig. 5. As incubation time increased, the glycation process continued, and the content of reactive sulfhydryl groups in glucose-collagen conjugates increased from 8.58 nmol/mg (0 h) to 25.49 nmol/mg (8 h), corresponding to an approximately 197.1% increase (p < 0.05). During this process, glycation modification facilitated the attachment of more sugar chains to collagen, promoting the unfolding of polypeptide chains and leading to more pronounced changes in the spatial structure of collagen. The structural reorganization induced by glycation is likely to expose previously buried or sterically hindered reactive sulfhydryl groups, leading to an increase in their measurable content. Furthermore, glycation may cleave existing disulfide bonds within collagen while simultaneously increasing steric hindrance, thereby suppressing the reformation of disulfide cross-linkages and resulting in a net increase in free sulfhydryl groups (Chai et al., 2025). These results are consistent with the findings of X. Liu et al. (2024), who reported that an increase in sulfhydryl availability enhances binding sites for furan compounds and improves the potential for flavor compound interactions.

Content of active sulfhydryl group in collagen at different glycation modification times. The different letters indicate the significant differences between the treatments (p < 0.05).

The bromophenol blue exhibits binding affinity toward hydrophobic amino acid residues located on the surface of collagen molecules, reflecting changes in protein structure (Yang et al., 2023). Fig. 6 shows the changes in surface hydrophobicity of collagen at different glycation times. Compared to the control group (65.47 μg), the surface hydrophobicity of glycated collagen samples significantly increased (p < 0.05). Although glucose contains abundant hydrophilic groups, glycation modification altered collagen's spatial conformation and promoted the exposure of previously buried hydrophobic domains, thereby increasing collagen surface hydrophobicity. Furthermore, studies have shown that changes in protein hydrophobicity during glycation do not necessarily follow a linear trend. X. Liu et al. (2024) observed that the hydrophobicity of myofibrillar proteins initially increased and then decreased during glycation modification. This phenomenon may be attributed to the gradual binding of glycation donors to the protein, which introduces certain hydrophilic groups. In the present study, hydrophobicity showed a consistent slow increasing trend, possibly because, in the reaction system used, the protein-unfolding effect induced by glycation outweighed the shielding effect of hydrophilic sugar chains. Combined with secondary structure analysis, the observed changes in hydrophobicity are most likely attributable to the exposure of hydrophobic groups resulting from the unwinding and unfolding of glycated collagen. This increase in surface hydrophobicity provides more hydrophobic regions for interactions between furan derivatives and collagen, thereby facilitating the formation of complexes.

Molecular docking study on the binding sites and interactions of furan derivatives with glycated collagen

Molecular docking can simulate the binding interactions and binding sites between ligands and receptors. Fig. 1 (Supplemental materials) illustrates the binding conformations of four furan derivatives with glycated collagen, where yellow dashes represent hydrogen bonds and grey dashes indicate hydrophobic interactions. Furfural interacts with three amino acid residues via distinct forces: hydrogen bonds with TYR-1237 and LEU-1251, and a hydrophobic interaction with ARG-1255. Owing to the additional methyl group, 5-methylfurfural binds to more amino acid residues. The methyl group forms hydrogen bonds with ASN-1238 and TYR-1237, while the furan ring engages in hydrophobic interaction with TYR-1237, resulting in tighter binding. Moreover, LEU-1339 and ALA-1248 also participate in hydrophobic interactions with 5-methylfurfural. 2-acetylfuran forms a hydrogen bond between the carbonyl oxygen atom of its acetyl group and ARG-1255, in addition to hydrophobic interactions with LEU-1339, TYR-1237, and LEU-1251. Due to its dual formyl groups, 2,5-diformylfuran offers the highest density of binding sites. Its aldehyde moieties form hydrogen bonds with ARG-1255 and TYR-1237, while the furan ring engages in hydrophobic interactions with LEU-1251 and LEU-1339. These findings illustrate that distinct structural features allow furan derivatives to bind tightly to the glycated protein. The docking results provided molecular-level insights into the possible interactions underlying the observed changes in headspace behavior: glycation disrupts the collagen triple helix (as seen in Raman spectra), exposing buried hydrophobic residues (LEU-1339, LEU-1251) and hydrogen-bonding sites (ARG-1255). These findings suggest that specific chemical interactions, including hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, may contribute to the enhanced interaction behavior of glycated collagen with furan derivatives, although their quantitative contribution requires further validation.

Conclusion

The data presented herein suggest that glucose-mediated glycation is associated with an increased interaction of chicken skin collagen with furan derivatives. By perturbing the dense, triple-helical architecture of the protein, the glycation process engenders a porous network characterized by diminished particle dimensions and elevated absolute zeta potential. This structural reorganization serves to unmask critical hydrophobic domains and reactive sulfhydryl groups. Mechanistically, molecular docking suggests that this enhanced interaction behavior may be associated with specific hydrogen bonding with ARG-1255 and TYR-1237, along with hydrophobic interactions. Consequently, these findings may provide theoretical support for flavor regulation in collagen-rich meat products through controlled glycation.
