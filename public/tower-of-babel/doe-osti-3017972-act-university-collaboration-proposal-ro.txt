 Executive summary

Research on the tensile behavior of additively manufactured 316L stainless steel coupon specimens at
increasing strain rates was conducted over the past year at Penn State. Dynamic loading rates into the 1000
1/s loading rates were performed on specimens with nearly 1.32” gage lengths. Stress-strain plots show
ductile and plastic behavior well beyond 12% strain with necked specimens, having smaller effective gage
lengths showing up to 56% ultimate strain. Additional tests performed on compact tension specimens
helped with simulation work to understand the deformation behavior.

Using finite elements, it was possible to determine the feasibility of a comprehensive experimental
validation study towards an improved model for AM material failure – specifically, the Bai-Wierzbicki
approach which accounts for lode angle and triaxiality. A non-significant number of tests are projected
for eight specific failure nodes, with additional replicates to provide strain-rate capabilities to the existing
formulation. One such correction factor is explored for quasi-static, notched specimens. Finally, stress
intensity factor was explored using a set of compact-tension specimens which would also provide useful
validation data for any simulation work.

Background

Understanding additively manufactured (AM) stainless steel for model validation and prediction requires
a set of experimental tests to fully explore the behavior under various loading conditions, e.g. tension,
compression, shear, and under changing loading rates (e.g. quasi-static ~ 0 1/s and dynamic > 1 1/s). For
this set of experiments, focus was on specially designed AM 316L tensile specimens, notched and
unnotched, with threaded ends, to confirm the material stress/strain behavior. Current work to date in this
area and literature reviews on similar novel materials under tension loading conditions tend toward
empirical parameters, which while realistic in capturing the deformation behavior, are limited in predictive
capabilities especially for the relatively new laser powder bed fusion metal manufacturing process. That
is, whereas standard test coupons and related standards can provide details on manufacturing quality of
AM metals, predicting part performance is more challenging. Additionally, plasticity models that are
based on the aforementioned empirical data sets are time and cost prohibitive to perform on every new
technology, even if the technology could provide improvements over other methods. Thus, more refined,
validated material models are desired (Figure 1). Ultimately, our research aims to make improvements
over an industry standard elastic-plastic model such as Johnson-Cook (J-C) to a more comprehensive Bai-
Wierzbicki (B-W).

 Figure 1. Models considered for material prediction under loading

Specimen designs are based on Bai’s work1, which devised a plasticity model for metals that uses an
alternative yield surface and a strain failure surface. This model represents an improvement to the
traditional J-C model by means of incorporating an additional stress measure, the lode angle, to determine
in more phenomenological detail the yield and strain failure material characteristic surfaces. In essence
when the material is not under elastic deformation, its stress configuration lays on progressive yield
surfaces with strain hardening effects during the plastic regime. The strain failure surface provides key
information to model the material’s ductility and ultimate strength. Therefore, the experimental setup and
specimen configurations are devised to extract the mechanical response by means of stress and strain
under high strain rate loading conditions (Figure 2).

 Figure 2. Test setups for quasi-static loading (left) and dynamic (middle, right)
 3

In brief, B-W requires that the specimens are designed in such a way as to force a particular failure mode,
such as tension, compression or shear which in our investigation, are tested under dynamic tension (Figure
3). These results provide fitting points on the strain failure surface to be determined with the new variable
lode angle. For the modeling part of the evolving yield surface, stress-strain curves are fundamental to
incorporate the plastic flow and hardening material constants for the model. Hence, these variables must
be measured for the extremely energetic and short vivid events (<1ms) which represent a challenge that
the proposed novel experimental configuration herein addresses (Figure 3). Additional experimental
efforts are performed in order to characterize the material’s resistance to crack onset and unbonded
propagation, therefore, fracture toughness is incorporated in the material model by following standards
for compact tension (CT) test.2-5

Technical Approach

Testing was done as follows: specimens were prepared for image tracking, loaded onto the dedicated
machines – universal test rig for quasi-static and single transmission Hopkinson bar for dynamic tests –
and tested. Additional tests were performed computationally using ABAQUS/Explicit, not just for the
samples tested (tension, notched, and compact tension) but also as a prediction for how the full suite of
B-W specimens would behave under similar loading conditions.

For the main efforts in dynamic tensile testing, a custom validated setup was used which incorporates a
compressed gas actuated impactor and a single transmission Hopkinson bar for up to 1.2 ms impulse
development. Strain gages on the bar and high-speed video provide the necessary data collection for
creating the stress-strain plots. Special attention was placed on machined cutouts to the Hopkinson bar, a
unique feature to provide the necessary view window for optical displacement measurement of the
specimen (primarily through pixel tracking, digital image correlation) during testing without introducing
significant noise to the data (Figure 4). Also, higher impulses required use of a precision alignment and
high frequency attenuation to reduce ringing from the impactor in the recorded data (Figure 5).

The specimen designs proposed are based on Bai’s1 addition of lode angle for the strain failure surface
(Figure 3) and will be explored in the near future. Of primary consideration are tensile bars specimens that
are produced using AM, specifically laser powder bed fusion and machined to finals dimensions (Figure
6) The inclusion of speckle patterns or other high-contrast painted surfaces is used for tracking the
cylindrical specimen’s gage length using digital image processing, which allows for an accurate
representation of the strain time history, albeit with several orders of magnitude lower data (102 number
of video frames vs over 106 Hopkinson bar data). Thus, for obtaining the strain rate history directly, this
discrete solution and its numerical derivative is not sufficient.

 Figure 6. Tension samples being tested for evaluation of impactor pulse Specimen # 1,2,13,14,5,6,17,18

To address this issue, we use up to a 16-term Fourier series fitting process through the experimental data
(Figure 7). This proves to be accurate enough to render a smooth derivative, which is the strain rate, in
addition to providing the strain and strain rate time histories over the entire recorded data set, which are
then used to obtain the proposed strain plastic-failure surface (a smooth function of strain, strain rate and
equivalent stress).

Figure 7. Result of curve fitting process to procure experimentally measured strain function (left), result of differentiated strain function to
obtain strain rate (right).

 7

The specimens used herein represent an evolutionary improvement over the original tensile loading design
(Figure 8). Whereas the first set used tapered ends intended for wedge grips on the test fixture under
various strain rates, from quasi-static to dynamic, the specimens used here were machined with 1/2-13
threaded ends with approximately 1/2" engagement. This permitted the specimens to be more securely
attached to matching tapped front and back titanium fixture plates as the threaded ends exhibited less
compliance in the high strain rate dynamic loading mechanism compared to the tapered variants.

 Figure 8. Specimens tested, all AM 316L with necked and prismatic gage sections (left) and the tension loader mechanism (right)

For all specimens, the necked gage length is 0.25” in diameter 1.32” long, approximately in-line with
previous and future test requirements. These were unique specimens in the overall design stage, however
as the gage section transitions to the tapped ends without much shoulder area. Additionally, a set of the
tensile bars were notched in the center using a 0.042” radii cut (Figure 8). This was intended to provide
a bridge to future B-W testing by providing different triaxiality without changing the initial design or test
operating procedure.

It should be noted that in the necked region, especially at the center of symmetry, the stresses and strains
are intensified due to the non-homogeneous distribution which may be a source of the nearly 20%
discrepancy showing lower strains for the necked specimens. This can be seen in FE results (Figure 14)
where local logarithmic superficial strains in the necked central region are predicted to be 2 times the
values in the immediate area (still considered to be the necked region). The local strain field should be
homogenized radially and axially instead of assuming homogeneous displacement in the gage length
alone. Quantifying the local strains accurately would require microscopic digital image correlation to
allow for post-processing of homogenization, which would be challenging for a limited region of
approximately 4 mm in length and under dynamic strain rates. On the other hand, since strain is a non-
dimensional measurement, it might be more feasible to obtain a strain intensity correction from
observation of larger specimens or by simply correlating results using FE solutions to estimate averaged
strain fields throughout the entire necked region providing more accurate and comparable results with the
unnotched design.

An additional model is proposed during this material characterization process. This is to obtain a more
comprehensive surface plot fit that enables the possibility of characterizing the strain rate dependence of
the plastic-failure strain on the variables triaxiality, equivalent stress and strain rate, which is an extension
of, and not currently in, the B-W framework. Our preliminary results show the potential for inspecting the
strain rate response under hypothetical non-tested conditions. The complexity of the fitting equation
requires refinement of the nonlinear function dependencies of every variable considered, such as strain
though digital image processing, it’s fitting and accurate differentiation to obtain the strain rate function,
the triaxiality which requires to accurately measure the principal curvatures in the necking region via
digital imaging, and the accurate equivalent stress measurements on the necking region of the specimen
via the strain gage information. Preliminary results are shown in Figure 23 with a R2 >0.55, where failure
surfaces are plot for three different uniform strain rate loading conditions, indicating a possible strain rate
dependency of the failure strain in addition to those considered in Bai’s model of triaxiality and lode
angles alone.

Discussion and future work

Several seemingly disparate tests (tension – dynamic, with notched and unnotched and heat-treated and
non-heat-treated specimens, quasi-static on similar specimens all non-heat-treated, and CT tests) were
examined during this period of performance. Our goal was two-fold—to establish new testing operating
processes capable of higher strain rate tension loading required by LLNL and to bridge the gap in
knowledge between existing and new, more comprehensive material models (i.e. B-W) for predicting the
deformation and damage behavior of AM steel.

Results show general trends for heat treating, around 300 MPa yield strength whereas non-heat-treatment
has a much higher value >400 MPa at various strain rates. Dynamic loading on the unnecked specimens
saw strains exceeding the impulse development of the bar (1.2ms), but results from the next specimen
show higher strain to failure for the non-heat-treated AM 316L – 56% vs 46%, both of which represent a
fairly large amount of plastic deformation. Additionally for quasi-static testing, when properly accounting
for the necked gage area and using Bridgman’s equations, it is possible to correct for the relatively
complex geometry in the gage section and generate consistent stress-stain plots that will eventually be
required for B-W lode and triaxiality.

Further work is planned with respect to modeling the yield surface that will consider the possibility of
strain rate hardening as an additional feature to Bai’s model. This requires improvements in the stress-
strain curves obtained from the experimental setup via updates in the configuration of the fixtures and
mass distributions that render a smoother contact force signal without additional post-process filtering. In
regard the fracture toughness, additional modifications are already devised in order to quantify this
material property via more tests as well as actual inspection of crack propagation during the tests. Once
the proposed material constants are obtained, the plasticity model that incorporates the strain rate response
will represent a significant improvement to the current state of the art B-W and J-C models.

Additionally, as part of our continued engagement with LLNL and academia, Dr. Whisler recommended
two students working with his research team to the 2025 LLNL summer internship program.
