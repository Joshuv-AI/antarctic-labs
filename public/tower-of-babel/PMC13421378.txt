Prof. Vladimir A. Gvozdev (1 May 1935–13 June 2025): the passion for Drosophila genetics
Authors: Elizaveta V Benevolenskaya, Alexei A Aravin, Sergei M Mirkin
Published: 2026

Prof. Vladimir Gvozdev—an outstanding Russian molecular geneticist who taught several generations of students at Moscow State University—died last year. Throughout the difficult and turbulent eras of the Soviet Union and Russia, he upheld the highest standards of scientific and personal integrity.

Vladimir Gvozdev’s favorite late-life photo, taken in June 2017 (courtesy of Lyudmila E. Andreeva).

Gvozdev was born in Moscow in 1935, just before the onset of Stalin’s Great Terror. Both of his parents came from Russian nobility, which, obviously, was not widely advertised at the time. In Gvozdev’s case, however, his ancestry was revealed by his highly accentuated speech, with a slight burr, as Leo Tolstoy so eloquently described in War and Peace. He received an excellent education both at school and at home and was exposed to several European languages by his mother, a background that served him well later in life when he chose his scientific direction.

To understand Gvozdev’s scientific upbringing, we need to place Drosophila genetics in Russia within its historical context. In 1922, Hermann Muller (1890–1967), who later received a Nobel Prize for demonstrating that mutations can be induced by X-ray irradiation, visited Moscow and brought his laboratory Drosophila strains to Nikolai Koltsov (1872–1940), Head of the Institute of Experimental Biology in Moscow and a founder of the Russian school of genetics. Subsequently, Muller established his own lab in Russia [1]. Alexander Serebrovsky (1892–1948), one of Koltsov’s prominent students, was highly excited by Muller’s discovery of induced mutagenesis in Drosophila and was the first to apply this technique in his own studies of the fine structure of the gene [2]. Serebrovsky became the Chair of the newly established Department of Genetics at Moscow State University in 1930. Both Muller and Serebrovsky believed deeply in the transformative power of genetics and its potential to advance society. This vision, however, collided with the rise of Trofim Lysenko, whose rejection of Mendelian genetics in favor of ideologically driven agricultural theories was endorsed by the Russian dictator Joseph Stalin [3]. As Lysenko’s influence grew, Muller fled the Soviet Union, while Serebrovsky stayed and saw his forward-looking ideas—including the need to unite Darwinian evolution with genetics—come under increasing attack. Despite this hostile climate, Roman Khesin (1922–1985) completed his PhD on the maternal effect in Drosophila offspring development under Serebrovsky’s supervision in 1948. Tragically, in the same year, the Session of the Lenin All-Union Academy of Agricultural Sciences proclaimed Mendelian genetics “a bourgeois perversion,” leading to a swift ban on teaching and studying genetics that lasted nearly two decades [3]. For better or worse, Serebrovsky did not live to see this terrible development, while his favorite student, Khesin, was swiftly expelled from Moscow State University.

Gvozdev began his undergraduate studies at Moscow State University in 1952, 4 years after the aforementioned Session. The absurdity of the ban on genetics should have become clear to everyone after the discovery of DNA structure in 1953. Yet, in Gvozdev’s own words, “the situation at the University remained appalling.” This is where his excellent education and knowledge of foreign languages came to the rescue. Gvozdev stopped attending lectures on Lysenkoism, began reading genetics textbooks, and became captivated by true genetics. He kept his passion for genetics alive for the rest of his life.

Influential Russian physicists, responsible for creating the country’s nuclear shield, which effectively made them untouchable, were among the first to recognize the harm of the ban on genetics. In 1959, they created a haven for surviving geneticists within their top-secret Institute of Atomic Energy, under the umbrella of radiation protection, the so-called Radiobiological Department. Among those who found a home there was Khesin, who went on to build a pioneering research program in molecular genetics. Gvozdev joined Khesin’s newly established lab in the Radiobiological Department and considered Khesin his main teacher in science and in life. We must note that the ability to conduct genetic research at the Institute of Atomic Energy was a major advantage, but it came with three caveats: a requirement for security clearance, a veto on collaborating with foreign scientists, and a hindrance to publishing scientific results abroad.

Khesin was trained as a Drosophila geneticist, but his new lab was transitioning to bacterial biochemical genetics. Still, he encouraged Gvozdev to work with Drosophila first in his lab and then in his own, combining the power of genetic analysis with biochemical research. The first result from the Gvozdev lab was the establishment of one of the earliest Drosophila cell cultures [4]. Subsequently, his studies substantially advanced our understanding of X-chromosome dosage compensation and position-effect variegation in Drosophila [5, 6]. Unfortunately, for the reasons outlined above, some of these studies were first published in Russian journals and became known to the international scientific community only later.

Gvozdev’s first breakthrough came in the late 70s. This was a much better time for genetics: the Radiobiological Department was about to become the Institute of Molecular Genetics at the Russian Academy of Sciences, and publications and international contacts became possible. As often happens in science, the discovery came from pure serendipity. Georgii Georgiev at the Institute of Molecular Biology of the Russian Academy of Sciences hypothesized that eukaryotic genes share extended regulatory regions that account for their large size. To test this hypothesis, he began collaborating with the Gvozdev lab. Scientists in the Georgiev lab cloned several moderately repeated genetic elements from the Drosophila cell culture described above, while Evgeny Ananiev in the Gvozdev lab mapped their locations in the genome via in situ hybridization with Drosophila polytene chromosomes. Unexpectedly, these genetic elements hybridized at multiple sites along the chromosomes, and, even more strikingly, these sites varied across different Drosophila strains, suggesting that they could be “jumping genes,” akin to those previously described by Barbara McClintock in maize. It would be a stretch to say that the full scale of this discovery was instantly recognized by the authors, yet their 1977 paper in Science [7] stated that “The unstable localization of the repeated sequences in polytenic chromosomes may be related to the phenomenon of gene migration, as was demonstrated in corn and Drosophila.” Two years later, Gerald Rubin of Harvard Medical School proved the transposition of these elements in Drosophila [8]. The Gvozdev lab continued studying the role of these mobile genes for years to come [9]. They are now known as Copia-like retrotransposons: a major superfamily of long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons ubiquitously present in all eukaryotic genomes.

From his early studies of position-effect variegation, Gvozdev was interested in the role of heterochromatin in genome structure and function. He characterized structurally complex heterochromatic regions of the Drosophila genome containing mobile elements [10], rDNA [11], and repetitive Stellate (Ste) genes [12]. This continuing interest ultimately led to his second breakthrough. Suppression of Ste genes in the male germline, which is critical for fertility, depends on the Suppressor of Stellate [Su(Ste)], a heterochromatic locus on the Y chromosome. The Gvozdev lab found that the Su(Ste) locus harbors noncoding repeats homologous to the Ste sequence [13, 14]. Subsequent work revealed that the Su(Ste) locus produces sense and antisense transcripts that are processed into short (22–27-nt long) noncoding RNA molecules that bind complementary sequences within the Ste transcripts, leading to their degradation [15]. Furthermore, mutations that eliminated small RNAs and derepressed Ste genes simultaneously led to the activation of diverse families of transposable elements in the germline, indicating a general phenomenon extending beyond the Ste/Su(Ste) system [16–18]. We now know that small RNAs that guide repression of Ste and transposable elements belong to one of the major classes of small noncoding RNAs, Piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), which provide defense against selfish genes in the germline of animals. In a collaborative paper by the Zamore and Gvozdev labs, it was established that piRNA-guided repression is related but distinct from siRNA- and microRNA-guided silencing mechanisms [19]. Subsequently, the Gvozdev lab demonstrated that the piRNA machinery operates not only in the cytoplasm but also guides the establishment of a repressive chromatin state in the nucleus [18, 20, 21].

Notably, these remarkable discoveries coincided with and were aided by Russia’s brief period of democratization and openness to the world, which fostered numerous international contacts and exchanges vital to science. At the same time, Gvozdev was elected to the Russian Academy of Sciences. Altogether, Gvozdev and his lab enjoyed a new lease on life, continuing to unravel the mechanisms underlying Drosophila genome function. Unfortunately, to Gvozdev’s dismay, the Russian political course reversed soon thereafter. What can a man do? In 2022, he signed an open letter from Russian scientists condemning Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and gave several subsequent anti-war interviews; these acts required enormous bravery in the current political climate in Russia. He also witnessed the reabsorption of the Institute of Molecular Genetics into the Institute of Atomic Energy, a cruel twist of fate that led to the loss of young talent. Yet against all odds, he continued his scientific pursuits, primarily focusing on the role of germline-specific subunits of the Nascent Polypeptide-Associated Complex in regulating protein homeostasis in Drosophila germinal cells [22].

For the sake of space, we shall focus on Gvozdev’s tremendous contribution as a teacher to generations of students at Moscow State University. The two-semester Molecular Biology course was originally created by Roman Khesin and Alexander Spirin. After Khesin’s passing in 1985, Gvozdev took over his portion of the course, covering genome structure and function. He then co-authored a textbook titled “Molecular Biology: Structure and Biosynthesis of Nucleic Acids”. It is hard to overstate the role this course played in the development of Biology majors at MSU: its sheer breadth and depth are unmatched by those of molecular biology courses, even at world-class universities. Most importantly, Gvozdev made every effort to engage students intellectually at a level uncommon in a large mandatory course. Here is just one example, shared with us by a student who took this course in 1998. In one of his lectures on DNA replication, Gvozdev presented two slides: one showing multiple replication origins firing simultaneously in Drosophila embryos and another showing significantly fewer origins active in replicating somatic cells. The question posed to the students was: Does this mean that somatic cells use fewer replication origins, or do they use the same number of origins that are not firing simultaneously? What could be better praise for a teacher than for a student to remember challenging questions and vibrant discussions from a class taken nearly 30 years prior?

Until his final days, Gvozdev continued to nurture and protect young scientists at his home institute and to teach undergraduate students at MSU. There is valor in maintaining personal dignity and scientific integrity and in passing them on to new generations of students, no matter how difficult the circumstances.

We honor Prof. Vladimir Gvozdev’s scientific achievements and the remarkable life he led. His legacy endures through his students and colleagues.
