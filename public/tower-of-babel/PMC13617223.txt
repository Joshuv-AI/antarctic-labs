Factors Associated With High Pain Scores During Paediatric Peripherous Intravenous Cannulation Insertion: A Secondary Analysis of a Prospective Cohort Study in Children
Authors: Amy L Sweeny, Shane George, Clayton Lam, Bianca Goucher, Stuart Watkins, Lucy Dunstan, Peter J Snelling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimPIVC insertion can be a painful procedure, but limited research focuses on contributors and alleviators of pain. This study aimed to determine participant, proceduralist, and/or situational factors associated with a high pain score in children.MethodsThis was a secondary analysis of a previously published single‐centre prospective observational study conducted over 28 consecutive days. Research assistants observed PIVC insertion attempts for children aged under 16 years and recorded data relating to the patient, proceduralist, and event. Pain scores during each attempt were determined observationally using the Faces Pain Score Revised (FPS‐R) scale, which ranges 0–10. A score of 7–10 was categorised as high pain. Multivariable logistic regression modelling was performed to identify factors associated with a high pain score.ResultsA total of 198 attempts for 130 participants were evaluated. About half of the children were male (n = 64, 49.2%) and median age was 5.7 years (2.0–11.0). Fifty‐five children (42.3%) had high pain scores during PIVC insertion. Factors that independently increased the likelihood of a high pain score during PIVC insertion were younger age Odds ratio, 95% confidence interval (OR [95% CI]) = 0.83 [0.72–0.95], three or more attempts (OR 17.3 [1.8–163.8]), history of negative PIVC experience (7.0 [1.4–36.1]), a combative child (10.7 [2.3–50.3]), and antecubital fossa site as first attempt (3.37 [1.07–10.66]).ConclusionsHigh pain during PIVC insertion is common and associated with repeated attempts. Optimising the first attempt—and employing strategies shown to mitigate pain are recommended, particularly in younger children and/or those with prior negative experiences.Keywords: cannula, paediatric, pain, peripheral intravenous catheter

Introduction

Peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC) insertion is one of the most common invasive procedures performed in the paediatric emergency department (ED), with approximately one in 12 children requiring a PIVC for diagnostic sampling, fluid resuscitation or medication administration [1]. Despite this frequency, PIVC insertion can be technically challenging, particularly in younger children or those with difficult intravenous access, which often require multiple attempts before success [2]. Repeated attempts are associated with distress for the child, procedural delays and increased resource use [3].

Pain and distress during PIVC insertion are well recognised concerns for children, caregivers and clinicians. Younger children are more likely to experience fear, have limited coping strategies and exhibit high behavioural distress during needle‐related procedures [3]. Whilst previous research has identified predictors of difficult intravenous access and determinants of first‐attempt success [2, 3], comparatively little work has examined which patient, proceduralist or situational factors are associated with the degree of pain experienced during paediatric PIVC insertion. Understanding these factors is critical, as procedural pain not only causes immediate distress but may also contribute to negative pain memory, increased anxiety at future medical encounters and reduced tolerance for subsequent procedures [4, 5].

The primary objective of this secondary analysis was to identify factors associated with observed high pain scores during paediatric PIVC insertion. By characterising how demographic, behavioural and procedural characteristics relate to high pain scores, this study aims to inform clinical strategies that optimise the first attempt, improve the procedural experience and reduce pain for children undergoing PIVC insertion in the ED.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

The methodology has been previously described for the original study [2]. The Prospective Observational study of cannulation of Kids In the Emergency Department (POKIE) study was a single‐centre prospective observational cohort study conducted over a period of 28 consecutive days from May 16th, 2021. The study site is a large academic mixed centre in South‐East Queensland, Australia, with a co‐located paediatric ED. The Gold Coast Hospital and Health Ethics Committee approved the study (LNR/2019/GCH/55041). The study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [6], available in the Supporting Informations.

Selection of Participants

A study assistant, either a senior nurse or medical student, attended the paediatric ED each shift between 09:30 and 22:00 and screened the electronic medical record (EMR) for any children potentially requiring a PIVC. The inclusion criteria were patients under 16 years of age who required PIVC insertion in the paediatric ED. Written consent was provided by the parent and assent was obtained from children aged 8 years and older when appropriate; these were obtained later in the child's stay during a resuscitation.

Study Definitions

A PIVC insertion attempt was defined as the needle tip piercing the skin until it was removed from the skin. The number of times that the needle tip was repositioned whilst the needle tip remained under the skin was also recorded as redirections. PIVC insertion success was defined as insertion of PIVC followed by a 1 mL sodium chloride 0.9% flush that did not affect the integrity of the vein or insertion site. PIVC abandonment was defined as a cessation of PIVC attempts after one or more failed attempts in the ED.

Clinical Procedures

In this paediatric ED, clinicians typically attempt a PIVC insertion using a landmark technique. PIVC insertion is usually conducted by a registrar or consultant‐level doctor. Ultrasound is available to use for guiding PIVC insertion in patients considered to have difficult IV access, if a trained provider is available, but its use is not common.

Study Procedures

Prior to study commencement, the case report form was piloted by the study assistants on five non‐participating children to ensure it was complete, user friendly, and that there was similar agreement for pain scores and other variables potentially open to subjectivity. During the study, data were collected by 10 study assistants on a rotating roster who were supernumerary to paediatric ED staffing requirements. Study assistants stood back from the participant and medical staff whilst maintaining line of sight during PIVC insertion attempts but did not approach the patient between attempts to avoid interference with proceedings. The study assistant was also able to clarify data elements with the participant, parents, or proceduralists, as necessary after completion of the attempts. Data were later entered into the REDCap electronic database by a research assistant.

Outcomes

The primary outcome in this sub‐study was the observed level of pain experienced by a child, using the Faces Pain Score‐Revised (FPS‐R) validated tool [7]. This is a numerical scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (high pain), represented by 6 faces (0–2–4–6–8–10). The scale on the CRF was reproduced with options to choose a number between 0 and 10. This was captured for each PIVC insertion attempt. A participating child's maximal pain was the maximum pain score recorded across attempts. Pragmatically, pain scores were grouped into categories for low (0–3), medium (4–6) and high (7–10) pain to assist with descriptive analysis, similar to ways the FPS‐R pain scale has been categorised by other researchers [8].

Potential Predictors

Prior to the procedure, an accompanying caregiver or the child (if appropriate) was asked whether the child had experienced prior PIVC insertions and if they had a prior negative experience. Clinician gestalt on likelihood of successful PIVC insertion was also captured prior to the proceduralist's first PIVC insertion attempt and could range from 0% (no chance of success) to 100% (absolute chance of success). Gestalt was grouped into high gestalt (≥ 80%), moderate gestalt (60%–80%), and low gestalt (≤ 50%). Proceduralist background and PIVC insertion experience were obtained via a separate survey sent around the same time as the study to all clinicians performing PIVC insertions in the paediatric ED. Each proceduralist's experience was linked back to any study cases they performed. The ‘younger age’ variable included infants and children aged under 4 years of age.

Additional patient, proceduralist and situational factors were collected during observations of each PIVC insertion attempt (Table S1). Terminology was consistent with prior literature: in terms of the evaluation of child cooperation, a child was ‘combative’ if actively resisting cannulation, rather than responding with minimal or no resistance [9, 10, 11]. Caregiver cooperation was ‘collaborative’ if actively assisting with calming or restraining the child during cannulation, ‘passive’ if having no role and ‘non‐collaborative’ if they interfered with the process.

Data Analysis

Simple descriptive statistics (counts, frequencies, proportions, medians and interquartile ranges [IQR]) were produced for the cohort. The χ 2 test (for categorical variables) and χ 2 test for trend (for ordinal variables) were used to identify variables significantly associated with increasing pain level. The number of records missing data was noted variable by variable, but the category representing missing data was not included in univariate analysis; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data was analysed at the attempt level, where one child could have multiple attempts, and at the child level, where the maximum pain score across all attempts was used to assign a pain category for each child.

To determine factors independently associated with high pain, a multivariable logistic regression model was built using the child‐level data with the dichotomous dependent variable high pain score vs. other pain score (i.e., low or medium pain score). Variables considered for inclusion were those that were clinically plausibly associated with pain, whether child‐related (e.g., history of prior PIVC insertion), proceduralist‐related (e.g., experience of the proceduralist) or procedure related (e.g., anatomical site, gauge). The variable age was automatically included in the analysis, as this has been found to be associated with pain in other studies. Variables found to be statistically significantly associated with pain score were assessed for intercorrelations. Variables were considered strongly correlated if the Pearson correlation coefficient exceeded 0.500. Highly correlated variables were managed in the following ways: variables and their derivatives (e.g., age, age category) were assessed one by one in a logistic regression model built on high pain score. The variable that explained most of the variance in the dependent variable was retained (based on R2). Other highly correlated variables were tested in the model along with their interaction term. An interaction term was considered statistically significant if p < 0.001. Where the interaction term was not significant, both variables were considered for inclusion in the model. Variables likely to explain the same phenomenon (e.g., child cooperation and restraint) were tested independently in the model with age. The variable that explained most of the variance in the dependent variable was retained in the model as the main variable for that phenomenon. Then, the logistic regression model was expanded to include the other potentially predictive variables, with each variable entered in the base model one by one. The change to the B‐coefficient of the variables already in the model was assessed, as was the additive value gained by the new variable to the total R2 of the model, and the statistical significance of the new variable.

Results

A total of 134 participants were recruited to the original study population, four were excluded from this secondary analysis (three were unconscious and one had no data on pain scores recorded), resulting in 130 participants for this study. For an individual child, the total number of PIVC insertion attempts ranged from 1 to 6, with redirections ranging from 0 to 40. About half of the children were male (n = 64, 49.2%) and the median age was 5.7 years (IQR 2.0–11.0).

The median maximum pain score per participant for the cohort was 6 (IQR 3–8). A low pain score was observed in 36/130 children (27.7%); 39/130 (30.0%) children had a medium pain score; and 55/130 (42.3%) children had a high pain score. PIVC insertion was abandoned in 7/130 (5.4%) children following one or two attempts; all of these displayed a medium (2/7, 28.6%) or high (5/7, 71.4%) pain score on the first attempt.

Factors potentially associated with a high pain score are reported in Table 1. A high pain score was more commonly observed in children younger than 10 years, with 29.1% of children in the age groups < 1 year old and 1–3 years old having a maximum pain score in the high range, compared to only 14.5% of children aged 10 and older. Children who were deemed to be ‘combative’ (n = 30, 21.1%) were also overrepresented in the high pain score group (n = 27, 49.1%). Other factors associated with high pain score were a history of a negative PIVC experience (11/130, 8.5% overall, 8/55, 14.5% of high pain group), an operator with low gestalt, the use of a smaller PIVC gauge, and multiple attempts and/or multiple redirections. Five or more redirections were significantly associated with a high pain score: more than half of the high pain score group cohort (29/55, 52.7%) required 5 or more redirections, compared to 7/39 (17.9%) in the medium pain score group and 2/36 (5.6%) in the low pain score group. Conversely, for the subset of children who appeared to have no pain (10/130, 7.7%), all 10 required one attempt only. For the remainder of the low pain score group, 23/26 (88.5%) required one attempt only, the remaining 3 required 2 attempts. Distraction was used for about half of this cohort (62/130, 47.7%); a topical anaesthetic applied 30 min prior was used for 89/130 (68.5%).

There were 8 children who had multiple attempts at PIVC insertion and for whom pain score dropped from the high category to the medium or low category (≥ 3 points) between attempts. Common changes accompanying the decrease in pain were site change from antecubital fossa to hand (4 cases), delay between attempts of at least 15 min (3 cases), and the use of sedation introduced (1 case), or continued (3 cases). For one case, the only change was the use of ultrasound to guide PIVC insertion; for another, additional topical anaesthetic agents and distraction provided through a tablet device occurred for the second attempt. Discussion

This secondary analysis found that more than a third of children appear to experience high levels of pain during PIVC insertion. A high prevalence of pain in children undergoing PIVC insertion is consistent with other studies related to this topic [12, 13, 14]. Pain, the normal adaptive response to protect our body from harm, has both physical and emotional components [15]. High pain scores were more commonly recorded amongst younger, ‘combative’ children, including some of whom had a history of a prior negative PIVC experience. In addition, after adjusting for other variables associated with a high pain score, insertion at the antecubital fossa carried an increased risk of a high pain score in children, as did three or more attempts at PIVC insertion. For this group of children, pain scores appear to increase with each subsequent attempt required. This reinforces the principle of making the first attempt, the best attempt [16, 17].

Few observational studies have described factors associated with pain for children during PIVC insertion, specifically [12, 13]. One Danish study assessed pain in children 1 month to 18 years for a variety of emergency procedures, about 80% of which were PIVC insertions [13]. In that study, younger children were scored as having moderate to severe pain, and older children reported mild to no pain, similar to this study. That study also found that having prior experience of the procedure resulted in a lower pain response, contradictory to this study, and also the literature on pain memory associated with needle procedures in general [4]. Another study of school‐aged children found that ‘temperament’ of the child was a predictor of pain response in school‐aged children [14], consistent with our finding related to the level of cooperation or ‘combativeness’ of the child.

This study uniquely provides preliminary data on how a child's pain score changes with each PIVC insertion attempt. It appeared that a child experienced more pain with an increasing number of attempts or redirections. It logically follows that many of the associations for a high pain score during PIVC insertion attempts were similar to risk factors for identifying difficult intravenous access in children [2, 18]. Younger children are generally anxious by being held down and being approached with a needle and often endure multiple PIVC insertion attempts, which creates a negative learned experience for future insertions. Also, if children have veins that are poorly visualised or palpable, they are at risk of multiple PIVC insertion attempts [2]. A ‘combative’ child was associated with a high pain score during PIVC insertion, which makes insertion attempts unlikely to be successful when they are distressed and actively resisting [2]. As with the approach to children expected to be difficult to cannulate [18, 19], ultrasound use at first attempt should be considered for children at high risk for increased pain. Visualisation of the target vein through ultrasound use may increase the likelihood of first attempt success and also minimise the number of redirections used during an attempt [19].

Mediators for children experiencing a high pain score observed in this study included administration of analgesia or sedation, such as sucrose (infants), inhaled nitrous oxide or intranasal fentanyl, and allowing time to pass between attempts. Sedation will often assist to calm an agitated child, which will enable a less mobile target vein for PIVC insertion. Allowing time between attempts provides the opportunity to strategise on how best to approach the next attempt. This could include a different operator, administration of sedation or use of an adjunct, such as ultrasound, which can reduce the number of attempts and redirections for PIVC insertion success [2, 18, 19].

Reviews on the benefits of distraction [20, 21], and local analgesia [20, 22], further support the effectiveness of these strategies. Whilst these practises are also recommended at our facility, this study did not see a significant change in pain levels based on utilisation of these strategies. This may be due to the small sample size of this study or inconsistent practises. Specific distraction techniques mentioned include blowing bubbles or the use of virtual reality devices. Additional interventions to potentially prevent pain during PIVC insertion include the application of buffered lignocaine via a needleless injection device, cold spray to numb the target insertion area, the use of nitrous oxide for 3 min (better in conjunction with topical anaesthetic), or the use of a device such as Buzzy (MMJ Labs) or ShotBlock that uses cooling in conjunction with high‐frequency vibration [22, 23, 24, 25]. Breastfeeding and/or optimal positioning for comfort is also recommended during vaccination and is likely beneficial during PIVC insertion [26].

The indication for PIVC insertion needs to be robust, as alternatives could be considered to avoid repeated attempts at PIVC insertion [1]. In this study, high pain was associated with abandonment of the procedure; all 7 of the cases abandoned were amongst children who displayed high pain at the first or second attempt. Alternatives to a PIVC could include a different route for treatment, such as a nasogastric tube for rehydration, or collection of pathology with results awaited before further attempts if clinically stable.

Finally, the site of PIVC insertion can play a role in the pain that a child experiences during the attempt. Insertion in the antecubital fossa in this study was associated with high pain, which could be related to these being more proximal to the child and provoking anxiety, or reflecting that there are poor options in the periphery, indicating a potentially higher level of difficulty for PIVC insertion. This contrasts with an adult study, which determined that insertion at the antecubital fossa was associated with less pain and complications [27].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, it reflects observations from a single mixed ED in Australia and may not be generalisable to other settings with different patient populations, staffing models, or procedural practises. The sample size, although sufficient for exploratory modelling, was relatively small, resulting in wide confidence intervals for several associations.

Second, the primary study was not specifically designed to examine pain during PIVC insertion, and some relevant variables may not have been captured or measured with optimal precision. Pain was assessed using the Faces Pain Scale–Revised across all ages and was scored observationally by research assistants rather than self‐reported by children, particularly those old enough to reliably self‐assess. This measurement approach was undertaken pragmatically as children's ages ranged from 1 month to 15 years, and we were measuring pain and other variables on every attempt. We did not think it appropriate to interfere in‐between attempts to capture self‐reported pain. The FPS‐R is not validated for children under 4 years of age, and observer‐assigned pain scores may differ from child‐ or parent‐reported scores. However, other studies have reported that nurse‐observed pain reports for children are generally lower than child‐reported pain [28, 29, 30]. As we used nurse‐reported pain, our findings are likely underestimates and some important predictors of pain in children may have been missed. Nonetheless, although a calibration exercise was undertaken, variation in scoring between observers remains possible.

Third, data collection occurred between 09:30 and 22:00, meaning overnight practises were not captured and may differ. The presence of research assistants may also have influenced clinician behaviour, although efforts were made to minimise interference.

Finally, as with all observational studies, residual confounding is possible, and associations identified should not be interpreted as causal. Further studies—ideally multicentre and incorporating validated age‐appropriate pain measures—are required to confirm these findings and to evaluate targeted interventions to reduce procedural pain.

Conclusions

In this secondary analysis of a prospective observational cohort, high levels of pain were commonly observed during paediatric PIVC insertion, particularly amongst younger children, those exhibiting distress behaviours, and those with a history of negative procedural experiences. Pain increased substantially with repeated attempts, underscoring the importance of optimising the first attempt.

These findings support a more proactive, risk‐stratified approach to paediatric PIVC insertion that integrates behavioural management, technical adjuncts such as ultrasound, and escalation strategies when initial attempts fail. Efforts to reduce both the sensory and emotional components of pain are essential to improving the procedural experience for children and their families. Future research should evaluate structured intervention bundles to determine their impact on pain, procedural success, and long‐term procedural anxiety.
