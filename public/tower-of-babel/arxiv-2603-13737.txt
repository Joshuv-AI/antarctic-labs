Non-uniform Kahn-Kalai, spread, variants, and applications
Thinula De Silva, Pu Gao

Abstract
Building on B. Park and Vondrák’s recent generalization of the J.Park-Pham Theorem (formerly known as Kahn-Kalai conjecture) to non-uniform probability measures, this paper introduces the notion of “spread” for the non-uniform setting. This provides a framework to establish 1-statements for subgraph containment in inhomogeneous random graphs with or without a set of forced edges. Using this approach, we derived conditions for the emergence of perfect matchings in the Stochastic Block Model and the Chung-Lu model, and verified that these conditions are in general not tight, but they capture thresholds across a broad range of regimes. Finally, we bridge this non-uniform framework with 𝒢 ⁡ ( n , d ) \mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d}) , utilizing a coupling argument to demonstrate thresholds for perfect matchings in 𝒢 ⁡ ( n , d ) \mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d}) for a broad range of degree sequences d .



1
Introduction

Random graph theory, initiated by Erdős and Rényi in the late 1950s, has become a central field in modern graph theory and theoretical computer science. One of the most striking phenomena in random graph theory is the abrupt structural transformation known as a phase transition, where a small change in edge probability leads to a sudden change in graph structures or parameters.
Friedgut and Kalai proved that every monotone graph property exhibits a phase transition
[
4
]
. Determining the threshold at which a given monotone graph property undergoes the phase transition is a problem-specific task and lies at the heart of random graph theory. The famous Kahn-Kalai conjecture, proposed by Kahn and Kalai in 2006, relates the threshold of an increasing family to a natural lower bound called the expectation threshold. The conjecture asserts that this lower bound is, up to a logarithmic factor, close to the actual threshold.

This conjecture is stated in a more general setting.
Let
X
be a finite set. In the example of random graph
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
,
X=\binom{[n]}{2}
is the set of edges in a complete graph on
n
vertices. Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
be an increasing family, where
\mathcal{F}\neq\emptyset,2^{X}
. As an example of increasing graph properties, we can think of
{\mathcal{F}}
being
{\mathcal{F}}_{\texttt{PM}}
, the set of graphs on
[n]
containing a perfect matching (assuming that
n
is even). Given
p\in[0,1]
, a probability measure
\mu_{p}
on the set of subsets of
X
is defined by

\mu_{p}(S)=p^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}(1-p)^{\left\lvert X\setminus S\right\rvert}\quad\text{for all $S\subseteq X$}.

It is not hard to show that

\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}):=\sum\limits_{S\in\mathcal{F}}\mu_{p}(S)

is strictly increasing in
p
with
\mu_{0}({\mathcal{F}})=0
and
\mu_{1}({\mathcal{F}})=1
. Thus, there is a unique value
p_{c}(\mathcal{F})
, called the
threshold
of
{\mathcal{F}}
, such that
u_{p}(\mathcal{F})=1/2
when
p=p_{c}(\mathcal{F})
. As noted in
[
1
]
,

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }p\ll p_{c}(\mathcal{F})\\
1&\text{ if }p\gg p_{c}(\mathcal{F})\end{cases}

and thus
p_{c}(\mathcal{F})
marks the phase transition where the measure
{\mathcal{F}}
undergoes the sudden change from 0 to 1.

Determining
p_{c}(\mathcal{F})
involves identifying
p_{c}
, usually a function of
N=|X|
, and proving that

{\text{{0-statement:}}}\quad\mu_{p}({\mathcal{F}})=o(1),\quad\text{if $p\ll p_{c}$};

and

{\text{{1-statement:}}}\quad\mu_{p}({\mathcal{F}})=1-o(1),\quad\text{if $p\gg p_{c}$}.

In many situations, establishing the 0-statement is a much easier task than the 1-statement, as typically one can easily prove the existence of certain obstructions for
{\mathcal{F}}
, e.g. isolated vertices for
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}_{\texttt{PM}}
. The Kahn-Kalai conjecture states that

p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})=O(p_{E}({\mathcal{F}})\log N)
, where
p_{E}({\mathcal{F}})
is the so-called expectation threshold, which will be formally defined in Section
1.4
. Roughly speaking, it can be viewed as the “best lower bound” that can be obtained by considering the expected number of obstructions.

We highlight two key advances on the Kahn-Kalai conjecture, which are restated as Theorem
1.15
and Theorem
1.16
respectively. First, Park and Pham
[
15
]
confirmed the conjecture, which reduces determining thresholds for an increasing family to estimating the expectation threshold. However, the latter still remains challenging as it involves optimizing the “covers” of the given increasing family
{\mathcal{F}}
. Second, an earlier work by Frankston, Kahn, Narayanan, and Park
[
3
]
uses the dual of the cover optimization to provide an upper bound for the primal and thereby demonstrate the 1-statement. The dual variables have a combinatorial interpretation known as
spread
. Proving the 1-statement for
{\mathcal{F}}
involves estimating the spread of structures related to
{\mathcal{F}}
. This approach has significantly advanced threshold determination for long-standing problems in random graph theory (such as the square of Hamilton cycles in
[
8
]
, the bounded degree subgraphs in
[
3
]
, the Erdős conjecture about high girth Steiner triple systems in
[
9
]
, etc.) while also greatly simplifying proofs for existing theorems with very involved proofs such as the bounded degree spanning trees (originally proved by Montgomery in
[
12
]
and later proved using Kahn-Kalai in
[
3
]
).

In this paper, we discuss a few generalizations and variants of the Park-Pham theorem (i.e. the Kahn-Kalai conjecture). The Park-Pham theorem concerns a probability measure on the subsets of
X
by including every element of
X
with a uniform probability
p
. What happens if the inclusion probability is not uniform for the elements?
In a recent work
[
14
]
, B. Park and Vondrák proved that the Park-Pham theorem holds for this generalized version; see the precise statement in Section
1.5
.
As before, this theorem relates two thresholds and does not directly yield an estimate for the threshold of an increasing family
{\mathcal{F}}
. Indeed, the notion of “threshold” is not so clear any more, as we are dealing with vectors
\textbf{p}\in[0,1]^{X}
rather than a single real probability
p
. We first extend the notion of thresholds and phase transitions in this more general setting.
Given
\textbf{p}\in[0,1]^{X}
, let
\mu_{\textbf{p}}
be the product measure on
2^{X}
where

\mu_{\textbf{p}}(S):=\prod\limits_{x\in S}p_{x}\prod\limits_{y\notin S}(1-p_{y})\quad\text{for all $S\subseteq X$}

(1)

and
\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{F}):=\sum\limits_{S\in\mathcal{F}}\mu_{\textbf{p}}(S)
. Let
X_{\textbf{p}}
be a random subset of
X
drawn from
\mu_{\textbf{p}}
.
In the uniform case,
p_{c}(\mathcal{F})
is defined by
\mu^{-1}(1/2)
, where
\mu=\mu_{p}({\mathcal{F}})
is viewed as a function of
p
, which is known to be continuous and increasing since
{\mathcal{F}}
is an increasing family.

This notation of threshold naturally generalises to the non-uniform setting, where we define
p_{c}(\mathcal{F})
to be the following set of
p
:

\{\textbf{p}\in[0,1]^{X}:\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{F})=1/2\}.

(2)

The theorem below demonstrates that phase transitions do occur around the values of
p
in the set (
2
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\neq\emptyset,2^{X}
be an increasing family such that
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
and
0<\varepsilon<1
be fixed. If
\textbf{p}^{*}\in[0,1]^{X}
such that
\varepsilon<\mu_{\textbf{p}^{*}}(\mathcal{F})<1-\varepsilon
and
\alpha=\omega(1)
, then

\lim\limits_{\left\lvert X\right\rvert\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{F})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\textbf{p}\leq\textbf{p}^{*}/\alpha\\
1&\text{ if }\textbf{p}\geq\alpha\textbf{p}^{*}\end{cases}
.

Remark
.

Such
\textbf{p}^{*}
always exists. To see this, note that if
\mathcal{F}\neq\emptyset,2^{X}
, then for each
0<\delta<1
, there exists
\textbf{p}\in[0,1]^{X}
such that
\mu_{\textbf{p}}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)=\delta
. This is because
\mu_{\textbf{p}}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)
is a polynomial in
p
, and an increasing function that attains both 0 (at
\textbf{p}=\textbf{0}
) and 1 (at
\textbf{p}=\textbf{1}
), as
\mathcal{F}
is an increasing family.

However, unlike in the uniform setting, given
p
, it is not clear which
\textbf{p}^{*}
it should be compared to in order to determine which of the following cases
p
falls into.

•

Supercritical case:
\textbf{p}\gg\textbf{p}^{*}
for some
\textbf{p}^{*}\in p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})
;

•

Subcritical case:
\textbf{p}\ll\textbf{p}^{*}
for some
\textbf{p}^{*}\in p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})
;

•

Near the threshold case:
\textbf{p}=\Theta(\textbf{p}^{*})
for some
\textbf{p}^{*}\in p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})
;

•

None of the above.

To address this issue, we introduce the
threshold map
, which enables us to determine whether
p
is supercritical, subcritical, near the threshold, and to show that essentially these are the only three regimes for
p
.

Given a sequence of probability spaces indexed by
n
, we say a sequence of events
A_{n}
occurs asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.) if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n}\right)=1
.

Definition 1.2
.

Suppose that
f_{\mathcal{F}}:[0,1]^{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
satisfies

(a)

(Subcritical) If
f_{\mathcal{F}}(\textbf{p})\ll 1
then a.a.s.
X_{\textbf{p}}\notin\mathcal{F}
.

(b)

(Supercritical) If
f_{\mathcal{F}}(\textbf{p})\gg 1
then a.a.s.
X_{\textbf{p}}\in\mathcal{F}
.

(c)

(Near the threshold)

\forall 0<\varepsilon<1\ \exists n_{0}>0,\delta>0,\ \forall n>n_{0},\ \textbf{p}\in[0,1]^{X}:

\varepsilon<f_{\mathcal{F}}(\textbf{p})<\varepsilon^{-1}\implies\delta<\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{F})<1-\delta.

If
f_{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies (a,b) then we call it a
threshold map
of
{\mathcal{F}}
. If
f_{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies (c) additionally, then we call it a
faithful threshold map
of
{\mathcal{F}}
.

By the subsubsequence principle, we may assume that
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\mathcal{F}}(\textbf{p})
exists (including the case that the limit is
\infty
). Hence, any
p
must lie in one of the cases (a,b,c). Moreover, the value
f_{\mathcal{F}}(\textbf{p})
determines precisely which regime
p
lies in.

Our next theorem assures that for an increasing family
{\mathcal{F}}
, a faithful threshold map exists.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
be an increasing family such that
\mathcal{F}\neq\emptyset,2^{X}
. Then, there exists an increasing continuous faithful threshold map
f_{\mathcal{F}}
of
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is an increasing family,
\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{F})
is an increasing function of
p
. Let
f_{\mathcal{F}}:[0,1]^{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be defined by

f_{\mathcal{F}}(\textbf{p})=\begin{cases}\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{F})&\text{ if }\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{F})\leq 1/2\\
(4(1-\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{F})))^{-1}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}
.

Obviously,
f_{\mathcal{F}}(\textbf{p})
is a continuous increasing function of
\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{F})
and thus a continuous increasing function of
p
. Moreover,
f_{\mathcal{F}}(\textbf{p})
satisfies all conditions (a,b,c) of Definition
1.2
. ∎

The construction of
f_{\mathcal{F}}
is abstract and uses the function
\mu_{\textbf{p}}({\mathcal{F}})
, which gives no information of
p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})
other than its definition (
2
). Hence, the significance of Theorem
1.3
lies in establishing the existence of a faithful threshold map, rather than in providing a method for constructing one.
In applications with given
{\mathcal{F}}
, we wish to find “nice” threshold maps that are as faithful as possible, which give estimations of members in
p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})
. For instance, in uniform setting
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
, and
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}_{\texttt{PM}}
, the map
f_{1}=pn/\log n
is a threshold map, but not a faithful one, because the appearance of a perfect matching in
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
has a sharp threshold. On the other hand,
f_{2}=\exp(pn-\log n-\log\log n)
is a faithful threshold map.

However, finding “nice”
f_{\mathcal{F}}
across the entire domain
[0,1]^{X}
(as in Definition
1.2
) is often challenging, even for the simplest increasing families
\mathcal{F}
, due to the potentially complex shapes of
p
at thresholds. It is usually more feasible and informative to focus on a particular family
{\mathcal{P}}
of probability vectors
p
and examine the threshold map within
{\mathcal{P}}
. Notable examples include the stochastic model and the Chung-Lu model (see Section
1.1
for further details), which have been extensively studied in the past. When working with a restricted domain
{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq[0,1]^{X}
, we naturally adapt the notions of threshold and threshold map by replacing
[0,1]^{X}
with
{\mathcal{P}}
in Definition
1.2
.

Our first contribution extends
[
3
]
in two directions. First, we develop the notion of
spread
for an increasing family
\mathcal{F}
that is tailored to proving the 1-statement for
\mathcal{F}
in
X_{\textbf{p}}
. Our second extension considers
\mathcal{F}
where every
S\in\mathcal{F}
is forced to contain a subset of elements
F\subseteq X
. For example, consider
X_{p}
being
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
and
\mathcal{F}
being the set of Hamiltonian graphs on
[n]
that contain a particular set
F
of edges. This setting allows many applications by patching smaller structures to form a desirable large structure. For instance, Frieze
[
5
]
found Hamilton cycles by patching disjoint cycle covers or paths together using a set of specified edges. This technique has been used in many other works. Can the spread method be applied to find upper bounds on the threshold in this case? Our Theorem
1.21
below aims to answer this question; refer to Section
1.5
for the precise statement and the relevant definitions.

The second contribution of this paper is to test the strength of Theorem
1.18
in capturing the threshold and the associated threshold map
f_{\mathcal{F}}
. Our approach relies on the notion of spread via Theorem
1.20
, which provides a
1
-statement. This allows us to address questions such as: How powerful is this theorem? Does it capture the expectation threshold, and how accurately does it estimate the threshold for a given increasing family
\mathcal{F}
?
As a test case, we focus on the increasing family
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\texttt{PM}}
. In Section
1.2
, we confirm that Theorem
1.20
indeed captures the threshold in a broad range of regimes. Nevertheless, it is worth emphasizing that even for this relatively simple example of
\mathcal{F}
, estimating
f_{\mathcal{F}}

over the full domain
[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
, or even over more structured domains such as those arising from the stochastic block model or the Chung–Lu model, remains highly challenging, regardless of whether the spread method is applied.

We note that in some cases, applying Theorem
1.21
together with the “patching technique” allows us to capture the thresholds, when a direct application of Theorem
1.20
introduces a logarithmic slack (this corresponds to the situations where the actual phase transition occurs close to the lower bound from the expectation threshold, rather than the Kahn-Kalai upper bound; see details of these two bounds in Section
1.4
). Such cases do not arise in the study of
\mathcal{F}_{\texttt{PM}}
.
In a subsequent paper, we will extend this study to more difficult examples, including Hamilton cycles and spanning subgraphs of bounded degrees. For these examples, we will fully leverage the power of Theorems
1.21
and
1.20
.

Finally, we turn to
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
, the uniform random graph with prescribed degree sequence
\textbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
.
This model is substantially more difficult to analyze than
X_{\textbf{p}}
with
X=K_{n}
, or other models such as the stochastic model or the Chung-lu model. Nevertheless, it is among the most important random graph models due to its wide range of applications.

Suppose that
d
and
\textbf{d}^{\prime}
are two degree sequences where
\textbf{d}\leq\textbf{d}^{\prime}
. Unlike
X_{\textbf{p}}
, it is no longer always true that
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
and
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d}^{\prime})
can be coupled so that
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
is a subgraph of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d}^{\prime})
. As a result, thresholds (now in terms of
d
instead of
p
) need not always exist for an increasing family
{\mathcal{F}}
.
Our last contribution in this paper is to bridge Kahn-Kalai with
\mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d})
. Because the edges do not appear independently in
\mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d})
, Theorem
1.20
cannot be applied immediately to this model. However, we use a result from
[
6
]
to embed
X_{\textbf{p}}
within
\mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d})
for some properly chosen
p
. This allows us to apply Theorem
1.20
for
X_{\textbf{p}}
and obtain results for
\mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d})
; see section
1.3
for more details.

1.1
Inhomogeneous random graph models

With
X=\binom{[n]}{2}
,
X_{\textbf{p}}
defines a random graph on
[n]
. However, it is more conventional in this case to denote the random graph by
{\mathcal{G}}(n,P)
, where
P\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
. Although determining thresholds over the entire domain
[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}

is highly complex, many interesting and tractable cases arise by restricting
P
to some structured subfamilies
{\mathcal{P}}
. In this section, we highlight several well-studied random graph models corresponding to such subfamilies, and in the following sections we explore thresholds within
{\mathcal{P}}
.

We start with the stochastic block model in which
P
respects a specified block structure. The stochastic block model appeared in
[
7
]
, and there are many research works relating to community analysis/detection in networks, such as in
[
18
]
and
[
10
]
.

Definition
.

Let
k\geq 1
be a positive integer,
{\bf U}=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{k})
be a set of
k
pairwise disjoint finite sets, and
P\in[0,1]^{k\times k}
be a symmetric matrix.
The stochastic block model
\mathcal{B}({\bf U},P)
consists of vertex set
U_{1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{k}
in which two distinct vertices
u\in U_{i}
and
v\in U_{j}
are adjacent independently with probability
P_{ij}=P_{ji}
, for all
1\leq i,j\leq k
. Each
U_{i}
is called a block of vertices in the model.

Remark
.

In most works, the stochastic model is defined so that
k
is fixed and is independent of the total number of vertices in the graph. We do not include that restriction in the definition, and hence
{\mathcal{G}}(n,P)
can be viewed as a stochastic block model by letting
U_{i}=\{i\}
for
i\in[n]
. However, in most interesting examples that we investigate,
k
will be fixed.

Next, we introduce the
Chung-Lu model
in which
P
is “guided” by the so-called “expected degree sequence”.

Definition
.

Given a sequence
\textbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
of non-negative integers, called the expected degree sequence, the
Chung-Lu model
is given by
\mathcal{G}_{\text{CL}}(n,\textbf{d}):=\mathcal{G}(n,P_{\textbf{d}})
where
P_{\textbf{d}}
is the edge probability matrix satisfying

P_{\textbf{d}}(i,j)=\min\left\{1,\displaystyle\frac{d_{i}d_{j}}{\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{i\neq j\}\right\}

for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
.

Remark
.

The Chung-Lu model can be viewed as a special case of the stochastic block model, where the vertices associated with the same expected degree are contained in the same block.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
d_{1}\geq\cdots\geq d_{n}\geq 1
(if
d_{i}=0
then one can safely consider the equivalent model by removing vertex
i
).

Finally, we define
\mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d})
. Note that the probability measure of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
does not follow (
1
) due to the edge dependency.

Definition
.

Given a sequence
\textbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
of nonnegative integers,
\mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d})
is a uniformly random graph where vertex
i
has degree
d_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq n
.

1.2
Test case: Perfect matchings

By extending the notion of spread in
[
3
]
, Theorem
1.20
(in Section
1.5
below) offers a method to transform the problem of identifying thresholds in the non-uniform setting into a purely combinatorial problem. Due to technical terms and definitions, we defer the presentation of Theorem
1.20
and related terms to Section
1.5
.

In this section, we first discuss the applications of Theorem
1.20
. As commented before, determining the threshold map
f_{\mathcal{F}}
can be very difficult, and we may not even expect good analytic properties
f_{\mathcal{F}}
, even for the simplest examples of
\mathcal{F}
. To test the effectiveness of Theorem
1.20
, this section focuses on one of the simplest increasing families consisting of graphs containing a perfect matching.

Recalling
\mathcal{B}({\bf U},P)
from Section
1.1
, we aim to derive conditions on
(\textbf{U},P)
for which
\mathcal{B}(\textbf{U},P)
has a perfect matching a.a.s., as well as conditions where it does not. Given
U
, let
\left\lvert\textbf{U}\right\rvert
denote the number of parts in
{\bf U}
, i.e. the integer
k
such that
\textbf{U}=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{k})
. To state our result, we define a
graph spectrum

\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U},P)
that is associated to
\mathcal{B}(\textbf{U},P)
as follows.

Definition 1.4
.

Given
\mathcal{B}(\textbf{U},P)
, and
\alpha\geq 0
, let
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U},P)
be the graph with vertex set
\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{\left\lvert\textbf{U}\right\rvert}U_{j}
such that two vertices
u\in U_{x}
and
v\in U_{y}
are adjacent if and only if
P(x,y)\geq\alpha\log n/\max(n_{x},n_{y})
, where
n_{j}=\left\lvert U_{j}\right\rvert
for all
1\leq j\leq\left\lvert\textbf{U}\right\rvert
.

Our
1
-statement for perfect matchings in
\mathcal{B}(\textbf{U},P)
is associated with the existence of a perfect matching in
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U},P)
for an appropriate
\alpha
.

Theorem 1.5
.

If
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U},P)
has a perfect matching for some
\alpha=\omega(1)
, then a.a.s.
\mathcal{B}(\textbf{U},P)
has a perfect matching.

Note that
|\textbf{U}|
does not have to be fixed in the above theorem. Indeed, we will apply Theorem
1.5
to the Chung-Lu model later in this section, where the number of blocks can be a function of
n
.

An ideal
0
-statement would be the following:

Ideal 0-statement
: If
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U},P)
does not have a perfect matching for some
\alpha=o(1)
, then a.a.s.
\mathcal{B}({\bf U},P)
does not have a perfect matching.

If the above statement were true, then this demonstrates a phase transition because we can take
f_{\texttt{PM}}(\textbf{U},P)
to be the maximum
\alpha
for which
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U},P)
has a perfect matching (noting that
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha_{1}}(\textbf{U},P)\subseteq\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha_{2}}(\textbf{U},P)
if
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}
). If
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U},P)
has no perfect matching for some
\alpha=o(1)
, then
f_{\texttt{PM}}(\textbf{U},P)\ll 1
. Together with Theorem
1.5
, this function
f_{\texttt{PM}}(U,P)
is a threshold map. However, the ideal 0-statement above is false in general; we give a counterexample below. The proof is left as an exercise to the reader, as it follows closely the argument of Example
1.12
, whose proof we provide.

Example 1.6
.

Consider
\textbf{U}=(U_{1},U_{2})
with
\left\lvert U_{2}\right\rvert=\left\lvert U_{1}\right\rvert+2
and
P\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
such that
P(x,y)=1
for all
x\in U_{1}
or
y\in U_{1}
. There exists
\rho=o(\log n/n)
such that if
P(x,y)=\rho
for all
x,y\in U_{2}
, then
\mathcal{B}(\textbf{U},P)
has a perfect matching with positive probability as
n\rightarrow\infty
. However, note that for some
\alpha=o(1)
, the graph
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U},P)
has no edges between any two vertices in
U_{2}
, and therefore, has no perfect matching.

Since this
0
-statement is not true in general, it is natural to ask the following question:

Problem 1
.

Can we define (interesting) families of
(\textbf{U},P)
for which the ideal 0-statement is true?

We will address Problem
1
for some families of
(\textbf{U},P)
arising from the Chung-Lu model. First, we focus on the Chung-Lu model where the expected degree sequence has a fixed number of values.

.

Let

.

1.3
Between non-uniform Kahn-Kalai and
\mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d})

Theorem
1.5
cannot be applied directly to guarantee the existence of a perfect matching in
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
, since the model
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
does not fit into the stochastic block model framework; in particular, the edges no longer appear independently. In fact,
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
is a much more challenging model to analyze, and much of the analysis depends heavily on enumeration techniques. Moreover, the Kahn–Kalai upper bound for the emergence of perfect matchings (and other structures), while tight in asymptotic order for
{\mathcal{G}}(n,p)
, may not be tight in
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
any more. A simple illustration of that comes from the well-known result that the random
d
-regular graph has a perfect matching a.a.s. provided that
d\geq 3
.

This motivates our study of
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
. In particular, we are interested in understanding whether an analogue of Theorem
1.5
holds for
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
, after imposing some reasonable conditions on
d
, and if so, whether it captures the threshold up to the correct asymptotic order. We investigate these questions in this section.

Our first result is to confirm that an analogue of Theorem
1.5
holds for
{\mathcal{G}}(n,\textbf{d})
for a large family of degree sequences
d
. Owing to a set of reasons, some obvious and others technical (refer to Section
5.1
for more details), we restrict our discussions to sequences
\textbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
(with
d_{1}\geq d_{2}\geq\cdots\geq d_{n}
) such that

\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}=0\pmod{2},\quad d_{1}^{2}=o(\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}),\quad\text{and}\quad d_{n}\gg\log n.

(3)

Recall
\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}}
,
P_{\textbf{d}}
that are defined below Definition
1.7
, and
\mathcal{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}})
in Definitions
1.4
.

Theorem 1.10
.

Suppose that
d
is a degree sequence satisfying (
3
). If
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}})
has a perfect matching for some
\alpha=\omega(1)
, then a.a.s.
\mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d})
has a perfect matching.

Next
we investigate whether Theorem
1.10
is tight for degree sequences satisfying (
3
); i.e. whether the ideal 0-statement (restricted to degree sequences satisfying (
3
)) holds. It is not a surprise that this is not the case. We give a negative example below, which is an analog of Example
1.6
(refer to section
5.2
for the proof).

Example 1.11
.

Let
d
be bi-valued such that
n_{2}=n_{1}+2
,
d_{1}=\sqrt{n}
, and
d_{2}=n^{1/8}\sqrt{\log n}
. Then
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}})
has no perfect matching for some
\alpha=o(1)
, but a.a.s.
\mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d})
has a perfect matching.

We naturally come to the next question: are there sub-families of degree sequences satisfying (
3
) such that the ideal 0-statement holds? Our example below demonstrates that such sub-families exist (refer to section
5.5
for the proof).

Example 1.12
.

Let
d
be bi-valued such that
d_{1}=n^{1/8}
and
n_{1}=n^{15/16}
. Then

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}(n,\textbf{d})\text{ has a perfect matching}\right)=\begin{cases}0&\text{, if }d_{2}\ll n^{1/32}\sqrt{\log n}\\
1&\text{, if }d_{2}\gg n^{1/32}\sqrt{\log n}\end{cases}
.

Remark
.

The phase transition in Example
1.12
, expressed in terms of
d_{2}
, corresponds precisely to the dichotomy between the case where
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}})
has a perfect matching for some

(1-statement) and the case where it does not have a perfect matching for some
\alpha=o(1)
(0-statement).

In the following theorem, we broaden the family of degree sequences in the above example.

Theorem 1.13
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the family of bi-valued
d
with
\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}=0\pmod{2}
and the following properties:

(A1)

d_{2}^{2}=o(n_{1})

(A2)

d_{1}^{2}=o(\sqrt{nd_{2}})

(A3)

(A4)

(A5)

.

Note that condition

since

. Therefore,
\mathcal{F}
indeed only contains degree sequences satisfying (

for the ideal 0-statement; it is only a technical condition used for the 1-statement (i.e., Theorem

Finally, we propose the following problem.

Problem 3
.

Characterize the degree sequences
d
for which Theorem
1.10
is tight.

1.4
Threshold, integer program, and spread

Recall from the earlier introduction that the Park-Pham theorem (i.e. the Kahn-Kalai conjecture) asserts that
p_{c}(\mathcal{F})
is bounded between
q(\mathcal{F})
, the so-called expectation-threshold, and
q(\mathcal{F})\log|X|
. Now we formally define
q(\mathcal{F})
in the uniform setting, and we will generalize this notion to the non-uniform setting in Section
1.5
. We try to keep the notation consistent with
[
15
]
.

Definition
.

We say
\mathcal{F}
is
q
-small
if there exists
\mathcal{G}\subseteq 2^{X}
such that

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\left\langle\mathcal{G}\right\rangle:=\bigcup\limits_{S\in\mathcal{G}}\{T:T\supseteq S\}

and
\sum\limits_{S\in\mathcal{G}}q^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. The
expectation-threshold
of
\mathcal{F}
,
q(\mathcal{F})
, is the maximum
q
for which
\mathcal{F}
is
q
-small.

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\left\langle\mathcal{G}\right\rangle
, then we say that
\mathcal{G}
is a
cover
for
\mathcal{F}
. Finding
q(\mathcal{F})
can be expressed as an integer program in the following manner:

\displaystyle V(\mathcal{F},q)=\min

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum\limits_{S\subseteq X}g(S)q^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{S\subseteq T}g(S)\geq 1

\displaystyle\forall\hskip 2.84526ptT\in\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle g(S)\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall\hskip 2.84526ptS\subseteq X.

It follows that
q(\mathcal{F})\leq q
provided that
V(\mathcal{F},q)\geq 1/2
.
We refer the reader to
[
16
]
for a more detailed exposition of it.

Recalling that
p_{c}(\mathcal{F})
is the unique
p
for which
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})=1/2
and that
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})
is increasing in
p
, we can restate the main result of
[
15
]
as follows.

Theorem 1.15

(Park-Pham
[
15
]
)
.

There exists a universal constant
K
such that for every finite set
X
and an increasing family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
, if
q\in[0,1]
satisfies
V(\mathcal{F},q)\geq 1/2
, then
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})\leq Kq\log\ell(\mathcal{F})
.

While Theorem
1.15
beautifully describes the relation between
p_{c}({\mathcal{F}})
and
q({\mathcal{F}})
, it is still very hard to find good bounds, especially good upper bounds, on
q({\mathcal{F}})
, for a given
{\mathcal{F}}
. A powerful tool called the spread method was introduced in
[
3
]
to bound
q({\mathcal{F}})
from above, by investigating the fractional version of
q({\mathcal{F}})
, which was first introduced in
[
19
]
. We demonstrate this approach below.

We start from the linear relaxation of
V({\mathcal{F}},q)
given below:

\displaystyle V_{f}(\mathcal{F},q)=\min

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum\limits_{S\subseteq X}g(S)q^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{S\subseteq T}g(S)\geq 1

\displaystyle\forall\hskip 2.84526ptT\in\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle g(S)\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall\hskip 2.84526ptS\subseteq X.

This motivates the factional version of expectation-threshold given as follows.

Definition
.

We say
\mathcal{F}
is
weakly
q
-small
if there exists a function
g
from
2^{X}
to the non-negative reals
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{S\subseteq T}g(S)\geq 1

for all
T\in\mathcal{F}
and
\sum\limits_{S\subseteq X}g(S)q^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. The
fractional expectation-threshold
of
\mathcal{F}
,
q_{f}(\mathcal{F})
, is the maximum
q
for which
\mathcal{F}
is weakly
q
-small.

Obviously,
V({\mathcal{F}},q)\geq V_{f}({\mathcal{F}},q)
, and therefore if
V_{f}(\mathcal{F},q)\geq 1/2
, then
q_{f}(\mathcal{F})\leq q
. So, the upper bound of Theorem
1.15
immediately implies the following weaker version. (Indeed, this weaker version was proved before Theorem
1.15
.)

Theorem 1.16

(Frankston et al
[
3
]
)
.

There exists a universal constant
K
such that for every finite set
X
and an increasing family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
, if
q\in[0,1]
satisfies
V_{f}(\mathcal{F},q)\geq 1/2
, then
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})\leq Kq\log\ell(\mathcal{F})
.

The main benefit from Theorem
1.16
is a relation between
V_{f}({\mathcal{F}},q)
and the notion
spread
, which corresponds to the variables in the dual of
V_{f}({\mathcal{F}},q)
, given as follows.

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum\limits_{T\in\mathcal{F}}\nu(T)

subject to

\displaystyle\nu(T)\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall\hskip 2.84526ptT\in\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{T\supseteq S}\nu(T)\leq q^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\forall\hskip 2.84526ptS\subseteq X.

From this, the notion of spread is defined by adding an additional factor of
2
to allow probability measures.

Definition
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{X}
. A probability measure
\nu
supported on
\mathcal{H}
is
q
-spread
if

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{T\supseteq S}\nu(T)\leq 2q^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}

for all
S\subseteq X
.

The main idea with spread is as follows. If
\nu
is a
q
-spread probability measure supported on an increasing family
\mathcal{F}
, then taking
\nu^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{2}\nu
, it follows that
\nu^{\prime}
is a feasible solution of the dual of
V_{f}(\mathcal{F},q)
with optimal value

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{T\in\mathcal{F}}\nu^{\prime}(T)=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{T\in\mathcal{F}}\nu(T)=\frac{1}{2}
.

Then, by weak-duality, it follows that
V_{f}(\mathcal{F},q)\geq 1/2
and we can apply Theorem
1.16
. In other words, the problem shifts to finding
q
-spread probability measures on increasing families.

Our applications specifically focus on subgraph containment problems, which, for the relevant choice of
\mathcal{F}
, will typically produce uniform hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{F}=\left\langle\mathcal{H}\right\rangle
(for simple graphs,
\mathcal{H}
will simply be a
2
-uniform hypergraph). To that end, for
r\geq 1
, define an
r
-graph
to be a hypergraph where the size of each edge is equal to
r
and let
\mathcal{K}_{n}^{r}
be the complete
r
-graph on
[n]
. Often in our applications, such as determining the threshold for the appearance of a perfect matching or a Hamiltonian cycle, we will want to take a specific
r
-graph
H\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{n}^{r}
(where
\left\lvert V(H)\right\rvert=n
) and look at the set of copies of
H
. To do this, let
S_{n}
denote the symmetric group on
[n]
and
\mathcal{G}_{H}:=\{\sigma(H):\sigma\in S_{n}\}
where
\sigma(H)
denotes the graph obtained from
\sigma
acting on
V(H)
. We adopt the convention that a group action on the vertex set of a graph induces an action on the edge set by acting on the corresponding subsets of vertices. Observation 7.5 in
[
3
]
, which is restated below, determines all values of
q
such that
\mathcal{G}_{H}
supports a
q
-spread probability measure.

Observation 1.17

(Frankston et al.
[
3
]
)
.

Let
H\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{n}^{r}
, and let
\sigma\in S_{n}
be a uniformly random permutation. Uniform measure on
\mathcal{G}_{H}
is
q
-spread if and only if

\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma(S)\subseteq H\right)\leq q^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}

for all
S\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{n}^{r}
isomorphic to a subhypergraph of
H
.

For our applications, we generally consider the previous results (these were stated more generally for any finite set
X
) for the case where
X
is equal to
\binom{[n]}{r}
, the set of edges in
\mathcal{K}_{n}^{r}
. Observation
1.17
can be used in applications
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
where the edge probability is uniform.

1.5
Non-uniform spread

We extend the framework and definitions from
[
3
]
and Section
1.4
to allow multiple probabilities. As before, let
X
be a finite set and consider an increasing family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
with
\mathcal{F}\neq\emptyset,2^{X}
. Given
\textbf{p}\in[0,1]^{X}
, recall from (
1
) that
\mu_{\textbf{p}}
is the product measure on
2^{X}
defined by

\mu_{\textbf{p}}(S):=\prod\limits_{x\in S}p_{x}\prod\limits_{y\notin S}(1-p_{y})

for all
S\subseteq X
and that
X_{\textbf{p}}
is a random subset of
X
drawn from
\mu_{\textbf{p}}
. The following definitions are from
[
14
]
. Given
\mathcal{G}\subseteq 2^{X}
and
\textbf{q}\in[0,1]^{X}
, define:

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{G}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup\limits_{S\in\mathcal{G}}\{T:T\supseteq S\}

(4)

\displaystyle\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum\limits_{S\in\mathcal{G}}\mu_{\textbf{p}}(S)

(5)

\displaystyle e_{\textbf{q}}(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert\{S\in\mathcal{G}:X_{\textbf{q}}\supseteq S\}\right\rvert\right)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{S\in\mathcal{G}}\prod\limits_{x\in S}q_{x}.

(6)

That is,
e_{\textbf{q}}(\mathcal{G})
is the expected number of elements of
\mathcal{G}
that are subsets of
X_{\textbf{q}}
. Given
\textbf{q}\in[0,1]^{X}
, we can define
V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q}):=\min\{e_{\textbf{q}}(\mathcal{G}):\ \mathcal{H}\subseteq\left\langle\mathcal{G}\right\rangle\}
. We say
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{X}
is
\ell
-bounded
if
\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq\ell
for all
S\in\mathcal{H}
. Park and Vondrák
[
14
]
generalized the Park-Pham Theorem to allow for non-uniform probabilities as follows.

Theorem 1.18

(Park-Vondrák
[
14
]
)
.

Let
\ell\geq 1
,
\textbf{q}\in[0,1]^{X}
and
p_{x}=1-(1-q_{x})^{4\left\lfloor\log(2\ell)\right\rfloor+7}
for all
x\in X
. Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{X}
be an
\ell
-bounded family. If
V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})\geq 1/2
, then
\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\left\langle\mathcal{H}\right\rangle)\geq 1/2
.

Remark
.

The original statement in
[
14
]

(they used
c_{\textbf{q}}(\mathcal{H})
instead of
V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})
) has strict inequalities (that is, if
V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})>1/2
, then
\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\left\langle\mathcal{H}\right\rangle)>1/2
). Theorem
1.18
follows immediately by the continuity of
\mu
. Indeed, suppose
\textbf{q}\in[0,1]^{X}
such that
V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})=1/2
and let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq 2^{X}
such that
e_{\textbf{q}}(\mathcal{G})=V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})
. Note that
\emptyset\notin\mathcal{G}
, as otherwise,
V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})=1
. It follows that for any
\varepsilon>0
, we have
V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q}_{\varepsilon})\geq(1+\varepsilon)V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})>1/2
where
\textbf{q}_{\varepsilon}:=(1+\varepsilon)\textbf{q}
(since every term in the sum in (
6
) will be multiplied by
(1+\varepsilon)^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\geq 1+\varepsilon
. If
\textbf{p}_{\varepsilon}=T_{\ell}(q_{\varepsilon})
(see (
7
) for the definition of

), then
\mu_{\textbf{p}_{\varepsilon}}(\left\langle H\right\rangle)>1/2
. By continuity of
\mu
and since
\textbf{p}_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\textbf{p}
as

, it follows that
\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\left\langle H\right\rangle)\geq 1/2
.

Observe that
\mathcal{H}
contains the set of minimal elements of
\left\langle\mathcal{H}\right\rangle

1.15
in the uniform case as
\mathcal{F}=\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle
when
\mathcal{F}
is an increasing family. For convenience, we define the transformations
T_{\ell}:[0,1]^{X}\rightarrow[0,1]^{X}
for each

such that

T_{\ell}(\textbf{q})=\textbf{p},\quad\text{where\ }p_{x}=1-(1-q_{x})^{4\left\lfloor\log(2\ell)\right\rfloor+7}\text{ for all $x\in X$.}

(7)

As in the uniform setting, we write
V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})
as an integer program, and then find its LP relaxation, denoted by

, as follows.

\displaystyle V_{f}(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})=\min

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum\limits_{S\subseteq X}g(S)\prod\limits_{x\in S}q_{x}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{S\subseteq T}g(S)\geq 1

\displaystyle\forall\hskip 2.84526ptT\in\mathcal{H}\text{ and }

\displaystyle g(S)\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall\hskip 2.84526ptS\subseteq X.

Remark
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{X}
and let
\textbf{q}\in[0,1]^{X}
. Then,
V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})\geq V_{f}(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})
.

The dual of
V_{f}(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})
is given below.

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum\limits_{T\in\mathcal{H}}\nu(T)

subject to

\displaystyle\nu(T)\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall\hskip 2.84526ptT\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{T\supseteq S}\nu(T)\leq\prod\limits_{s\in S}q_{s}

\displaystyle\forall\hskip 2.84526ptS\subseteq X.

Motivated by the dual variables above we introduce the following definition of
spread
in the non-uniform setting.

Definition
.

We say a probability measure
\nu
supported on
\mathcal{H}
is
q
-spread
if

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{T\supseteq S}\nu(T)\leq 2\prod\limits_{s\in S}q_{s}
for all
S\subseteq X
.

We call such a measure a
q
-spread probability measure
.

The purpose of spread is the same as in the uniform case; Lemma
1.19
is the non-uniform analogue of Observation
1.17
.

Lemma 1.19
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{X}
and
\textbf{q}\in[0,1]^{X}
. If
\mathcal{H}
supports a
q
-spread probability measure, then
V(\mathcal{H},\textbf{q})\geq 1/2
.

Again, for our applications, we will specifically focus on subgraph containment problems, and so we adopt the same
\mathcal{G}_{H}
defined in Section
1.4
, where
H\in\mathcal{K}_{n}^{r}
. In this setting, we again use
X=\binom{[n]}{r}
. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{F}_{H}=\left\langle\mathcal{G}_{H}\right\rangle
, the family of graphs which contain a copy of
H
as a subgraph. Unlike the uniform case, the uniform measure on
\mathcal{G}_{H}
often does not yield a good upper bound on
\textbf{p}^{*}
, and so we are forced to search among a broader set of probability measures on
\mathcal{G}_{H}
in order to obtain a good Kahn-Kalai type of upper bound. Recall
T_{\ell}
from (

).

Theorem 1.20
.

Let
H\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{n}^{r}
and
X=\binom{[n]}{r}
and let

We only present the proof of Theorem

q

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X
be a finite set. Let
\mathcal{T}\subseteq 2^{X}
and let
\rho
be an arbitrary probability distribution on
\mathcal{T}
. Then,
\rho
is a
q
-spread probability measure on
\mathcal{T}
if and only if

\mathbb{P}\left(S\subseteq\textbf{H}\right)\leq\displaystyle 2\prod\limits_{s\in S}q_{s}

for all
S\subseteq X
, where
\textbf{H}\in\mathcal{T}
is drawn according to distribution
\rho
.

Proof.

First, recall that
\rho
is a
q
-spread probability measure on
\mathcal{T}
if and only if

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\supseteq S:\\
T\in\mathcal{T}\end{subarray}}\rho(T)\leq 2\displaystyle\prod\limits_{s\in S}q_{s}
for all
S\subseteq X
.

By definition, for all
S\subseteq X
, with
\textbf{H}\sim\rho
,

\mathbb{P}\left(S\subseteq\textbf{H}\right)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{S\subseteq T\subseteq X}\rho(T)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{T\supseteq S:\ T\in\mathcal{T}}\rho(T)

where the last equality above holds by the fact that
\rho
is supported on
\mathcal{T}
. Hence,
\rho
is a
q
-spread probability measure on
\mathcal{T}
if and only if

\mathbb{P}\left(S\subseteq\textbf{H}\right)\leq 2\displaystyle\prod\limits_{s\in S}q_{s}
for all
S\subseteq X
,

as desired.
∎

We now prove Theorem
1.21
.

Proof.

Let
\textbf{q}^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{X}
such that
\textbf{q}^{\prime}_{e}=\textbf{q}_{e}
for all
e\in Y
, where
Y=X\setminus F
, and
\textbf{q}^{\prime}_{e}=1
for all
e\in F
. Let
\textbf{p}^{\prime}=T_{\left\lvert E(H)\right\rvert}(\textbf{q}^{\prime})
. Note that
\textbf{p}^{\prime}_{e}=\textbf{p}_{e}
for all
e\in Y
and
\textbf{p}^{\prime}_{e}=1
for all

. It follows that
\mathbb{P}\left(X_{\textbf{p}}\cup F\in\mathcal{F}_{H,F}\right)=\mu_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{F}_{H,F})
and so it suffices to prove that
\mu_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{F}_{H,F})\geq 1/2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{H,F}=\{\sigma(H):\sigma\in S_{n}(F)\}
.
Obviously,
\left\langle\mathcal{G}_{H,F}\right\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{H,F}
.
Define probability
\rho
on
\mathcal{G}_{H,F}
by

\rho(T)={\mathbb{P}}_{\sigma\sim\pi}(\sigma(T\setminus F)=H\setminus F),\quad\text{for every $T\in\mathcal{G}_{H,F}$.}

Let
\textbf{H}\sim\rho
. We show that

\mathbb{P}\left(S\subseteq\textbf{H}\right)\leq 2\prod\limits_{s\in S}q^{\prime}_{s}\quad\text{for all $S\subseteq X$.}

(8)

First, observe that we may assume that
F\subseteq S
as otherwise the left hand side of (
8
) is zero by the assumption that every
\sigma
in the support of
\pi
fixes all the vertices incident to some edge in
F
.
For any
F\subseteq S\subseteq X
,

\mathbb{P}\left(S\subseteq\textbf{H}\right)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathcal{G}_{H,F},\\
T\supseteq S\end{subarray}}\rho(T)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathcal{G}_{H,F},\\
T\supseteq S\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{P}}_{\sigma\sim\pi}(\sigma(T\setminus F)=H\setminus F)={\mathbb{P}}_{\sigma\sim\pi}(\sigma(S\setminus F)\subseteq H\setminus F),

where the last equality above holds since
S\subseteq T
if and only if
\sigma(S\setminus F)\subseteq\sigma(T\setminus F)
by the definition of
S_{n}(F)
. Furthermore, note that if
\sigma(S\setminus F)\subseteq H\setminus F
, then
\sigma(S)\subseteq H
since
\sigma(F)=F
. It follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(S\subseteq\textbf{H}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma(S)\subseteq H\right)\leq 2\prod\limits_{s\in S\setminus F}q^{\prime}_{s}=2\prod\limits_{s\in S}q^{\prime}_{s}

by the theorem assumptions. Hence, (
8
) holds for all
S\subseteq X
.

By Theorem
2.1
,
\rho
is a
\textbf{q}^{\prime}
-spread probability measure on
\mathcal{G}_{H,F}
. By Lemma
1.19
,
V(\mathcal{G}_{H,F},\textbf{q}^{\prime})\geq 1/2
. Note that
\mathcal{G}_{H,F}
is
\left\lvert E(H)\right\rvert
-bounded because every element of
\mathcal{G}_{H,F}
has
\left\lvert E(H)\right\rvert
edges. By Theorem
1.18
, it follows that
1/2\leq\mu_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}(\left\langle\mathcal{G}_{H,F}\right\rangle)\leq\mu_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{F}_{H,F})
.
∎

The proof of this theorem closely follows

.
Let

be independent copies of

. Note that
X_{1}\cup X_{2}\cup\cdots\cup X_{k}

X_{\textbf{r}}
where
\textbf{r}\in[0,1]^{X}

r_{x}=1-\left(1-p_{x}\right)^{k}

r_{x}\leq kp_{x}
for all

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k\textbf{p}}\notin\mathcal{F}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(X_{\textbf{r}}\notin\mathcal{F}\right)=\left[\mathbb{P}\left(X_{\textbf{p}}\notin\mathcal{F}\right)\right]^{k}

Let
\nu^{\prime}
be a
q
-spread probability measure supported on
\mathcal{F}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{T\supseteq S}\nu^{\prime}(T)\leq 2\prod\limits_{s\in S}q_{s}

for all

.

Letting
\nu:=\frac{1}{2}\nu^{\prime}
, note that
\nu
is a feasible solution of the dual of

, since
\nu(T)\geq 0
for all
T\in\mathcal{F}
by the definition of
\nu
and the fact that
\nu^{\prime}
is a probability measure, and

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{T\supseteq S}\nu(T)\leq\prod\limits_{s\in S}q_{s}
,  for all

.

Since

is a probability measure supported on

3
Proof of Theorem
1.5

In our proof we use the following approximation of falling factorials.

Lemma 3.1
.

For integers
n\geq x\geq 0
where
n\geq 1
,
(n)_{x}\geq(n/e)^{x}
.

Proof.

We may assume
x\geq 1
since
(n)_{x}\geq(n/e)^{x}
trivially holds if
x=0
. By
[
17
]
, we have
e^{1/(12n+1)}<n!/(\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^{n})<e^{1/(12n)}
for all
n\geq 1
and so
n!\geq(n/e)^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
. It follows that

(n)_{x}=\binom{n}{x}x!\geq\left(\frac{x}{e}\right)^{x}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{x}\frac{n-(j-1)}{x-(j-1)}\geq\left(\frac{x}{e}\right)^{x}\left(\frac{n}{x}\right)^{x}=\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{x},

using the fact that each term in the product is at least
n/x
.
∎

Choose
\alpha=\omega(1)
such that
\mathcal{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}(\textbf{U},P)
has a perfect matching
H
and for each
1\leq y,z\leq k
, define the indicator variables
\mathcal{I}(y,z)=\mathbbm{1}\left\{P(y,z)\geq\alpha\log n/\max(n_{y},n_{z})\right\}
.
We say
\sigma\in S_{n}
is a
block invariant permutation
if
\sigma(U_{i})=U_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. Let
\pi
be a uniform distribution over all block invariant permutations in
S_{n}
. Let
\textbf{q}\in(0,1)^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
be defined as follows: for each
uv\in\binom{[n]}{2}
, let
q_{uv}=\frac{30}{\max(n_{y},n_{z})}\cdot\mathcal{I}(y,z)
where
y,z\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
such that
u\in U_{y}
and
v\in U_{z}
. We show the following claim and then apply Theorem
1.20
to get the desired result.
Recall that
\mathcal{K}_{n}^{2}
denotes the set of edges in the complete graph on
n
vertices.

Claim 3.2
.

Let
\sigma
be sampled according to the distribution
\pi
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma(S)\subseteq H\right)\leq 2\displaystyle\prod\limits_{s\in S}q_{s}

for all
S\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{n}^{2}
isomorphic to a subhypergraph of
H
.

Proof.

Let
H_{ij}
be the set of edges in
H
with one end in
U_{i}
and the other end in
U_{j}
, for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq k
. Let
S\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{n}^{2}
be isomorphic to a subgraph of
H
, and so,
S
is a set of vertex-disjoint edges. Similarly, let
S_{ij}
be the set of edges in
S
with one end in
U_{i}
and the other hand in
U_{j}
, for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq k
. Letting
s_{ij}=\left\lvert S_{ij}\right\rvert
and
h_{ij}=\left\lvert H_{ij}\right\rvert
, we may assume that
s_{ij}\leq h_{ij}
for all
1\leq i,j\leq k
since
\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma(S)\subseteq H\right)=0
otherwise. Let
f(S,H)
denote the number of block-invariant permutations
\sigma
for which
\sigma(S)\subseteq H
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma(S)\subseteq H\right)=\displaystyle\frac{f(S,H)}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}!}.

(9)

To estimate
f(S,H)
, first note that for each
1\leq i,j\leq k
, there are
(h_{i,j})_{s_{i,j}}
choices for
\sigma
such that

\sigma(S_{i,j})\subseteq H_{i,j}
.
Each such choice specifies the image of
V(S)
under
\sigma
, where
V(S)
is the set of vertices incident with
S
.
Then, to complete a specification of
\sigma
,
there are
(n_{i}-\zeta(i))!
ways to permute the unmapped vertices in each
U_{i}
, where
\zeta(i):=2s_{i,i}+\sum\limits_{j\neq i}s_{i,j}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma(S)\subseteq H\right)=\displaystyle\frac{\prod\limits_{j\geq i}(h_{i,j})_{s_{i,j}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}(n_{i}-\zeta(i))!}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}!}=\frac{\prod\limits_{j\geq i}(h_{i,j})_{s_{i,j}}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}(n_{i})_{\zeta(i)}}
.

Since
h_{i,j}\leq\min(n_{i},n_{j})
, we get that
\left(h_{i,j}\right)_{s_{i,j}}\leq\min(n_{i},n_{j})^{s_{i,j}}
for all
1\leq i,j\leq k
. Using Lemma
3.1
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma(S)\subseteq H\right)\leq\left[\prod\limits_{j\geq i}\min(n_{i},n_{j})^{s_{i,j}}\right]\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{e}{n_{i}}\right)^{\zeta(i)}=\prod\limits_{j\geq i}\left[\min(n_{i},n_{j})^{s_{i,j}}\left(\frac{e}{n_{i}}\cdot\frac{e}{n_{j}}\right)^{s_{i,j}}\right].

Therefore, we get

\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma(S)\subseteq H\right)\leq\displaystyle\prod\limits_{j\geq i}\left(\frac{e^{2}}{\max(n_{i},n_{j})}\right)^{s_{i,j}}<\prod\limits_{j\geq i}\prod\limits_{s\in S_{i,j}}\frac{30}{\max(n_{i},n_{j})}

since
e^{2}<30
. Hence, the desired inequality holds.
∎

Now, we will use the result of Claim
3.2
to finish the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
Define
\textbf{p}\in[0,1]^{X}
(recall
X=\binom{[n]}{2}
) as follows. For every
uv\in X
, let
p_{uv}=P(y,z)
where
y,z\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
are such that
u\in U_{y}
and
v\in U_{z}
.
Then, by the assumption of the theorem and the definitions of
q
and
T_{\ell}
from

,

p_{uv}\geq\alpha\log n\cdot\displaystyle\frac{30}{\max(n_{y},n_{z})}\cdot\mathcal{I}(y,z)=\alpha q_{uv}\log n\geq\alpha T_{n/2}(\textbf{q})_{uv}
,

where the last inequality above is tight up to a constant factor. Therefore
\textbf{p}\geq\alpha T_{n/2}(\textbf{q})
which implies that
\mu_{\textbf{p}}(\mathcal{F}_{H})\rightarrow 1
by Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.20
because
\left\lvert E(H)\right\rvert=\frac{n}{2}
. Hence
\mathcal{B}(\textbf{U},P)
a.a.s. has a perfect matching, as desired. ∎

4
Perfect matchings in the Chung-Lu model

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1.8

Recall that
U_{1}=\{v_{x}:d_{x}=n_{1}\}
and let
U_{2}=\{v_{x}:d_{x}=n_{2}\}
.
Recall that the families
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
were defined as follows.

•

\textbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
if
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq n^{\Theta(1)}
;

•

\textbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}_{2}
if
n_{1}\leq n^{\delta}
for some fixed
\delta<1
.

Our goal is to show that for
\textbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}_{1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}
, if
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}\left(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}}\right)
does not have a perfect matching for some
\alpha=o(1)
, then
\mathcal{G}_{\text{CL}}(n,\textbf{d})
a.a.s. does not have a perfect matching. For bi-valued degree sequences
d
, we have the following straightforward observation.

Lemma 4.1
.

\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}\left(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}}\right)
has a perfect matching if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{d_{x}d_{2}}{\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}}\geq\frac{\alpha\log n}{n_{x}}

where
x\in\{1,2\}
is such that
n_{x}=\max\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
, and
x
is defined to be 1 if
n_{1}=n_{2}
.

Proof.

First, suppose
d_{x}d_{2}/\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}\geq\alpha\log n/n_{x}
and let
y=3-x
(i.e.,
n_{y}=\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
). Note that

\displaystyle\frac{d_{1}d_{2}}{\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}}\geq\frac{d_{x}d_{2}}{\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}}\geq\frac{\alpha\log n}{\max(n_{1},n_{2})}

and

\displaystyle\frac{d_{x}^{2}}{\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}}\geq\frac{d_{x}d_{2}}{\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}}\geq\frac{\alpha\log n}{\max(n_{x},n_{x})}
.

By the definition of
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}\left(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}}\right)
, every vertex of
U_{x}
is adjacent to every other vertex in the graph (i.e. they each have
n-1
neighbours). We can construct a perfect matching of
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}\left(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}}\right)
by matching all vertices in
U_{y}
with vertices in
U_{x}
, and then matching the remaining vertices in
U_{x}
among themselves.

Now, suppose that
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}\left(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}}\right)
has a perfect matching. If
x=2
, then it follows that
d_{2}^{2}/\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}\geq\alpha\log n/n_{2}
, since otherwise, there will be no edges between any two vertices in
U_{2}
, and consequently
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}\left(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}}\right)
cannot have a perfect matching because
\left\lvert U_{2}\right\rvert>\left\lvert U_{1}\right\rvert
, leading to a contradiction. If
x=1
, then suppose for the sake of contradiction that
d_{1}d_{2}/\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}<\alpha\log n/n_{1}
. This implies that
d_{2}^{2}/\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}<\alpha\log n/n_{2}
. Hence, all vertices in
U_{2}
are isolated, contradicting with
\mathfrak{G}_{\texttt{PM}}^{\alpha}\left(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{d}},P_{\textbf{d}}\right)
having a perfect matching.
∎

By Lemma
4.1
, it suffices to show that if
\textbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}_{1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}
is such that
d_{x}d_{2}/\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}\ll\log n/n
, then a.a.s.
\mathcal{G}_{\text{CL}}(n,\textbf{d})
has no perfect matching. We prove the statement separately for
\textbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
, and
\textbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}_{2}
.

4.1.1
Case 1:
\textbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}_{1}

In this case
x=1
.
Let
p=d_{1}d_{2}/\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}\ll\log n/n
and let
q=d_{2}^{2}/\left\lVert\textbf{d}\right\rVert_{1}\leq p
. Let
X
be the number of isolated vertices in
U_{2}
. We show that a.a.s.
X>0
which implies that a.a.s.
