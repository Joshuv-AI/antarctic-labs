Gallstone ileus due to a visible cholecystoduodenal fistula with pneumobilia CT diagnosis and surgical management: A case report
Authors: Latifa Ou-Skou, Meryem Edderai, Ouijdane Zamani, Zakaria Toufga, Mohamed Abdellaoui, Ouadie El Menaoui, Hassan En-Nouali, Jamal El Fenni, Rachida Saouab, Meriem Boui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gallstone ileus is an uncommon cause of mechanical small-bowel obstruction, usually occurring in elderly patients with comorbidities. It results from migration of a gallstone through a cholecystoenteric fistula, most commonly a cholecystoduodenal fistula, with subsequent impaction within the bowel lumen. Although computed tomography (CT) is the established imaging modality for diagnosis, direct demonstration of the fistulous tract is not always straightforward in routine emergency imaging. We report the case of a 69-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes mellitus and ischemic heart disease who presented with a 5-day history of diffuse abdominal pain, recurrent vomiting, and obstipation. Plain abdominal radiography showed small-bowel dilatation and a faint right lower abdominal opacity suspicious for an ectopic gallstone but was not conclusive. Contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT demonstrated mechanical small-bowel obstruction, pneumobilia with air in the gallbladder lumen and biliary tree, and a laminated ectopic gallstone impacted within a right lower abdominal ileal loop. Multiplanar reformations clearly depicted an air-containing cholecystoduodenal fistula extending from the gallbladder neck to the duodenal bulb, while 3D volume-rendered reconstruction provided an intuitive overview of the ectopic calculus. The patient underwent urgent laparotomy with enterolithotomy and restoration of bowel continuity; cholecystectomy and fistula repair were deferred because of age and comorbidities. Recovery was uneventful. This case emphasizes the educational value of careful multiplanar CT review for direct fistula visualization, as accurate preoperative characterization of the fistula and obstructing stone can support individualized surgical planning in elderly high-risk patients.

Introduction

Gallstone ileus is an uncommon but clinically important cause of mechanical small-bowel obstruction, accounting for approximately 1%-4% of adult bowel obstructions and a higher proportion of nonstrangulated small-bowel obstruction in elderly patients [1,2]. It represents a late complication of chronic cholecystitis. Recurrent inflammation and adhesion between the gallbladder and an adjacent bowel segment may lead to pressure necrosis and formation of a cholecystoenteric fistula, through which a gallstone can migrate into the intestinal lumen [1,2].

The classic imaging constellation is Rigler's triad: small-bowel obstruction, pneumobilia, and an ectopic gallstone [3,4]. Plain radiography may suggest the diagnosis by demonstrating signs of small-bowel obstruction or, less commonly, pneumobilia or an ectopic gallstone; however, the complete Rigler triad is infrequently identified on conventional radiographs. Computed tomography (CT), particularly multidetector CT with multiplanar reformations (MPRs), is now the reference imaging technique because it can demonstrate the obstructing calculus, the level of obstruction, pneumobilia, and associated biliary-enteric communication [[3], [4], [5]].

The educational value of the present case is not that CT can diagnose gallstone ileus, which is well established, but that the cholecystoduodenal fistula was directly visualized as an air-containing tract between the gallbladder neck and the duodenal bulb. The case illustrates how careful analysis of MPRs and 3D volume-rendered (3D VR) reconstruction can improve confidence in preoperative characterization and help communicate the relevant anatomy to the surgical team.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes mellitus and ischemic heart disease presented to the emergency department with a 5-day history of diffuse abdominal pain, recurrent vomiting, and cessation of stool and gas. There was no fever or rectal bleeding. Physical examination showed abdominal distension and diffuse tenderness without a palpable mass or clinical signs of peritonitis. Bowel sounds were decreased. Routine laboratory tests and serum biochemistry were unremarkable.

An abdominal plain radiograph obtained at admission showed multiple air-fluid levels and dilated small-bowel loops, consistent with mechanical small-bowel obstruction. A diagnosis of gallstone ileus secondary to a cholecystoduodenal fistula was made. After multidisciplinary discussion between the surgical and radiology teams, urgent laparotomy was performed. Intraoperatively, dilated proximal small-bowel loops and an impacted gallstone within an ileal loop at the transition point were identified. Enterolithotomy was performed with extraction of the gallstone, followed by restoration of bowel continuity. Cholecystectomy and fistula repair were not performed during the same procedure in order to limit operative time and physiological stress in this elderly patient with significant comorbidities.

The postoperative course was uneventful. Bowel function returned progressively, the obstructive symptoms resolved, and the patient was discharged after appropriate clinical recovery with advice for follow-up.

Discussion

Gallstone ileus most often affects elderly patients, particularly women, and remains associated with substantial morbidity because the clinical presentation is frequently nonspecific and patients often have significant comorbidities [1,2]. The underlying mechanism is usually chronic gallbladder inflammation with adhesion to the adjacent bowel, leading to fistula formation and migration of 1 or more gallstones into the intestinal tract [1,5]. Cholecystoduodenal fistula is the most common type of cholecystoenteric fistula [5]. In the present case, the initial radiograph suggested small-bowel obstruction but did not confidently establish the etiology. This limitation is expected because Rigler's triad may be incomplete or difficult to recognize on plain films, particularly when the ectopic stone is only faintly calcified or bowel gas obscures biliary findings [3,4]. CT was therefore essential for confirming the mechanical obstruction, identifying pneumobilia, and demonstrating the ectopic laminated calculus.

The main teaching point of this case is the direct depiction of the cholecystoduodenal fistula. While CT diagnosis of gallstone ileus is well known, the fistulous tract may be overlooked if only axial images are reviewed. In this patient, the tract was outlined by intraluminal air and became more conspicuous on oblique MPRs oriented along the communication between the gallbladder neck and the duodenal bulb. This reinforces the practical value of targeted MPR review in emergency CT interpretation when gallstone ileus is suspected. Accurate localization of the ectopic stone is also important. The ileocecal region is classically described as the most common site of impaction, but stones may obstruct at other ileal levels depending on stone size, bowel caliber, and local motility [1,2]. In response to image review, the stone in this case was described as impacted within a right lower abdominal ileal loop, likely proximal-to-mid ileal on the available CT images, rather than definitively as distal ileum. This more cautious terminology avoids overinterpretation and better reflects the imaging appearance.

The 3D VR reconstruction was not required to make the diagnosis, but it added educational and communicative value. By providing an intuitive overview of the ectopic calcified stone, it helped summarize the abnormal anatomy for multidisciplinary discussion. In radiology case reporting, such reconstructions should be presented as complementary tools rather than as substitutes for careful axial and multiplanar CT interpretation.

Treatment of gallstone ileus remains individualized. Options include enterolithotomy alone, a 1-stage procedure combining enterolithotomy with cholecystectomy and fistula repair, or a staged approach [2,6]. Enterolithotomy alone is often favored in elderly or high-risk patients because it shortens operative time and reduces physiological stress, although the fistula and any residual gallstones remain in situ and may rarely lead to recurrent symptoms or biliary complications [2,6].

In this patient, the choice of enterolithotomy alone was consistent with her age, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, and absence of additional ectopic stones on CT. Preoperative identification of a single obstructing stone and clear characterization of the fistula supported a limited procedure aimed at relieving the obstruction while avoiding the added risk of biliary surgery during the emergency setting.

This case therefore provides 3 practical teaching points: first, gallstone ileus should be considered in elderly patients with small-bowel obstruction, especially when pneumobilia is present; second, suspected cholecystoenteric fistula should prompt deliberate review of MPRs to look for a direct air-containing tract; and third, CT assessment of stone number, stone location, and fistula anatomy can help tailor surgical management to the patient's operative risk.

Conclusion

This case highlights gallstone ileus caused by a directly visualized cholecystoduodenal fistula. Although CT diagnosis of gallstone ileus is well established, careful use of MPRs can make the fistulous tract more conspicuous and improve confidence in preoperative characterization. In elderly comorbid patients, CT evaluation of the obstructing stone, associated pneumobilia, and fistula anatomy can support individualized surgical planning, including enterolithotomy alone when a limited emergency procedure is clinically appropriate.
