Treatment procedures for intractable gastroesophageal reflux disease after peroral endoscopic myotomy for achalasia: a retrospective study in South Korea
Authors: Ah Young Lee, Seong Hwan Kim, Joo Young Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsNo study has compared the safety and efficacy of anti-reflux mucosal ablation (ARMA), anti-reflux mucosectomy (ARMS), and Stretta radiofrequency (SRF) for intractable gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) after peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM).MethodsTwenty-two patients with post-POEM GERD underwent either ARMA (n=12), ARMS (n=6), or SRF (n=4). GERD questionnaire (GERDQ) score, endoscopic Los Angeles classification, flap valve grade based on Hill’s type EndoFLIP distensibility index (DI), and rate of proton pump inhibitor withdrawal were analyzed and compared. The factors associated with the occurrence of post-POEM intractable GERD were also evaluated.ResultsThe ARMS group demonstrated a significant improvement in GERDQ scores after 6 months of follow-up (p=0.04). No significant differences were observed in the endoscopic Los Angeles grade (p=0.68), flap valve grade (p=0.38), or EndoFLIP DI (p=0.15). Type III achalasia and posterior myotomy orientation during POEM were identified as risk factors for reintervention. Postprocedural adverse events (bleeding and stricture) occurred in the ARMA and ARMS groups.ConclusionsAmong the three procedures, ARMS produced the most notable improvement in symptoms, whereas endoscopic esophagitis and EndoFLIP DI did not show a similar improvement.Keywords: Ablation techniques, Argon plasma coagulation, Endoscopic mucosal resection, Esophageal achalasia, Gastroesophageal reflux

INTRODUCTION

Achalasia is characterized by impaired lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxation, which limits esophageal motility and blocks food passage.1 Conversely, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is primarily caused by low resting LES pressure and transient LES relaxation (TLESR).2 This distinction between the mechanisms underlying achalasia and GERD can lead to paradoxical therapeutic scenarios.

The incidence of intractable GERD after peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) is of significant clinical concern. Significant GERD symptoms were observed in 43% of patients after POEM, with 68% exhibiting endoscopic GERD findings.3 Inconsistent results have been reported regarding intractable GERD. A prospective study uncovered the absence of refractory GERD and grade ≥B reflux esophagitis after POEM.4 However, although most patients respond to proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy,1 our findings revealed a 6.1% prevalence of intractable GERD, consistent with that of refractory GERD (18.6%) in a study by Nabi et al.5 Thus, intractable GERD should be considered a potential adverse effect of POEM.

Effective management of intractable GERD after POEM is crucial for improving patients’ quality of life. Moreover, without proper treatment, GERD can lead to long-term adverse effects, including intestinal metaplasia (Barrett’s esophagus), esophageal dysplasia, and esophageal carcinoma.6 Therefore, the abrupt structural changes caused by POEM, coupled with the persistent lack or weakness of esophageal motility characteristic of achalasia,7 necessitate additional intervention to avoid worsening GERD.

Endoscopic anti-reflux mucosectomy (ARMS) emerged as a treatment option for refractory GERD in the mid-2000s, and non-ablative Stretta radiofrequency (SRF), which delivers low-energy radiofrequency to the LES and gastric cardia to improve sphincter function and reduce transient relaxation, was introduced in the late 1990s.8,9 However, studies on the use of these procedures, specifically regarding the treatment of intractable GERD after POEM, remain scarce. Although transoral incisionless fundoplication can be used to treat post-POEM GERD, its long-term efficacy has not been established.10

We compared the safety and efficacy of SRF, ARMS, and anti-reflux mucosal ablation (ARMA) using argon plasma coagulation (APC) for the treatment of post-POEM intractable GERD.

METHODS

Study design

This retrospective, observational cohort study was conducted at CHA Gangnam Medical Center in Korea. Twenty-two patients who experienced intractable GERD following POEM were selected from among 407 patients who underwent POEM at this hospital within this timeframe. The primary endpoint was assessed at the 6-month follow-up. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Hospital Ethics Committee and the Institutional Review Board of CHA University (approval number: GCI 2023-06-009).

Patients and eligibility criteria

This study included patients aged 18–85 years who met the diagnostic criteria for intractable GERD, defined as an initial response to standard-dose PPIs11 followed by failure to respond to standard- or high-dose PPIs or tegoprazan (50 mg) after at least 8 weeks of treatment. Additionally, eligible patients were required to meet at least one of the following criteria: (1) presence of severe GERD symptoms, indicated by a GERD questionnaire (GERDQ) score of >8 after POEM or (2) significant post-POEM changes in the EndoFLIP (Medtronic) distensibility index (DI) (a difference of approximately ≥7 mm²/mmHg) or high-resolution manometry (HRM) findings (a difference of approximately ≥14 mmHg), suggesting abrupt structural alterations consistent with intractable GERD requiring intervention. In other words, we integrated EndoFLIP DI, PPI responsiveness, and endoscopic findings to confirm the inclusion of patients with GERD.

Interventions

1) POEM

POEM is an advanced endoscopic procedure. Thus, one highly experienced endoscopist (J.Y.C.), who had performed more than 400 POEM procedures, performed it. An endoscopic triangle-tip electrosurgical knife (KD-640L; Olympus) was used to separate the submucosa, and a dual-tip knife (KD-650L; Olympus) was used to create a submucosal tunnel. A HookKnife (KD-620L; Olympus) or an IT-2 knife (KD-611L; Olympus) was used to divide and cut the circular muscle layer of the distal esophagus 2–3 cm onto the cardia, when needed. The orientation of the myotomy was posterior or anterior, and partial- or full-thickness circular myotomy was performed, depending on the extent of the myotomy (Table 1). The mucosal entry site was closed with endoclips and a clip-fixing device (HX-110 LR; Olympus).

2) SRF

This procedure delivers radiofrequency energy to the esophagogastric junction (EGJ). Two applications (initial and 45° to the right) were performed at six levels at intervals of 0.5 cm, from 1.5 cm distal and 1.0 cm proximal to the EGJ. Additional radiofrequency treatments were performed by advancing the catheter into the cardia and pulling it back until resistance was achieved at the EGJ upon inflation with 22 and 25 cm3 of air. Three applications (initial, 30° to the left, and 30° to the right) were performed for each case (Fig. 1).

Post-procedure visualization techniques. (A) Post-anti-mucosal reflux ablation esophagogastric junction (EGJ) in retroflexion view. (B) Post-anti-reflux mucosectomy EGJ in retroflexion view. (C) Post-Stretta radiofrequency EGJ in retroflexion view.

3) Endoscopic ARMS

An expert endoscopist (J.Y.C.) performed ARMS along the lesser curvature of the EGJ and gastric cardia, preserving the gastric sling fibers and mucosal flap valve on the greater curvature side. A cap-assisted endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR-C) method was used. Using EMR-C, a snare was prelooped by applying gentle pressure on the internal circumferential margin of the cap, and the captured mucosa was electrically ablated. Most patients underwent ablation involving half of the circumference (≥2.5 cm [1 cm in the esophagus and 1.5 cm in the stomach]), considering a previous study that indicated a high risk of high-grade strictures.12 However, in cases with Los Angeles (LA) grade C or D esophagitis or an EndoFLIP DI of ≥14 mm²/mmHg,13 a two-thirds circumferential mucosal resection was typically performed after comprehensive evaluation (Fig. 1).

4) ARMA

ARMA using APC was conducted through a high-frequency electrocautery generator (VIO 300D; ERBE) at 60 W with a gas flow rate of 1 L/min. The criteria used to determine ARMA extent were consistent with those used for ARMS (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

The primary study outcome was a change in the GERDQ score from the day of the procedure to 6 months. This outcome was used to compare the improvements in GERD symptoms among the three groups. The secondary outcomes included changes in the endoscopic LA classification, endoscopic flap valve grade (FVG), EndoFLIP DI, and PPI use for each procedure. The factors related to intractable GERD after POEM were also evaluated.

Clinical assessment and follow-up

Clinical symptoms, EndoFLIP DI, HRM, endoscopic FVG based on Hill’s type, and LA classification were assessed before the procedure. FVG 1, 2, 3, and 4 were scored as 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. Similarly, LA classification grades for esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD)—normal, minimal, a, b, c, and d—were scored as 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively. Preprocedure 24-hour monitoring of pH impedance was performed in seven patients (five from the ARMA and two from the ARMS group). These assessments were conducted 6 months after the procedure. However, post-procedure EndoFLIP was performed in only 9 patients (four and five in the ARMA and ARMS, respectively). Neither 24-hour monitoring of pH impedance nor HRM was conducted after the procedure. The GERDQ uses a 0–3 Likert scale to score four positive predictors of GERD (regurgitation, heartburn, and use of over-the-counter medications for reflux symptoms) and a reversed Likert scale (3–0) for two negative predictors (nausea and epigastric pain). EGD was performed on the day after the procedure to monitor for signs of bleeding or adverse events. PPIs were administered at standard doses for 12 weeks to promote healing after each procedure.

Statistical analyses

Continuous data were presented as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) because the distribution was skewed. Categorical data were presented as percentages. Groups were compared using an analysis of variance model. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. We used a multivariate logistic regression model to avoid errors in multiple comparisons and identify the important predictors of reintervention. Statistical analyses were performed using R ver. 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and statistical significance was set at 0.05.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of patients and procedures

Data from 22 patients who underwent ARMA (n=12), ARMS (n=6), or SRF (n=4) between January 2016 and April 2023 to treat GERD after POEM were collected (Fig. 2). The median follow-up duration was 6 months (IQR, 6–14 months). Most patients underwent these procedures because of severe post-POEM GERD symptoms. However, notable changes in HRM prompted interventions for three patients in the ARMA group and one patient (both 25%) in the SRF group. However, substantial shifts in EndoFLIP DI or endoscopic LA grade (both 8.3%) prompted the procedures for two other patients in the ARMA group. The baseline characteristics were similar across the groups. However, the median EndoFLIP DI varied between the ARMA 5.6 (IQR, 4.1–6.4), ARMS 14.8 (IQR, 8.1–25.0), and SRF 12.7 (IQR, 7.9–13.8) mm²/mmHg groups (p=0.09). Additionally, pre-intervention GERDQ scores differed significantly between the ARMA 10.0 (IQR, 6.0–11.5), ARMS 12.0 (IQR, 12.0–13.0), and SRF 7.0 (IQR, 6.0–9.0) groups (p=0.01) (Table 1).

Primary outcome

The median GERDQ scores of the ARMS group significantly improved from 11.5 preprocedure to 3.5 post-procedure (p<0.05). The ARMA group also showed improvement, with the median scores reducing from 9.0 to 3.0 (p<0.05). The change in median value in the SRF group was not significant (p=0.16) (7.0 [IQR, 6.0–9.0] to 4.5 [IQR, 3.0–7.0]). Consequently, the GERDQ scores (p=0.041) were significantly reduced in the ARMS group (Figs. 3, 4).

1) Endoscopic FVG, endoscopic esophagitis, EndoFLIP distensibility, and PPI use

In the ARMS group, the median DI decreased from the initial 14.8 (IQR, 8.1–25.0) to 6.7 (IQR, 5.7–7.3) after the procedure. However, for the SRF group, no post-procedure results were available for EndoFLIP. In the ARMA group, a slight decrease from 5.6 (IQR, 4.1–6.4) to 5.3 (IQR, 3.9–6.1) was observed after the procedure, with no significant difference between the two groups (p=0.15). Adverse events and requirement for additional procedures

Postprocedural adverse events included one (8.3%) case of bleeding in the ARMA group and one case each of stricture formation (16.7%) and bleeding (16.7%) in the ARMS group (Table 2). Eight patients required additional ARMA: three patients (25.0%) in the ARMA group, four in the ARMS group, and one (25.0%) in the SRF group. After ARMA, each patient underwent additional SRF (8.3%) and ARMA (8.3%). Among the patients who initially underwent ARMS, one (16.7%) underwent an additional ARMS procedure, while the others (16.7%) underwent further ARMA (Table 2).

Characteristics of patients with or without intractable GERD

No significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to body mass index, presence of a sigmoid-type esophagus, myotomy orientation, or gastric myotomy length. However, the distribution of achalasia subtypes and EGJ outflow obstruction (EGJOO) differed significantly between the groups (p=0.02), with EGJOO being more prevalent among patients with intractable GERD (20.0% vs. 6.2%) (Table 3).

Factors for reintervention following treatment after POEM

In our logistic regression, the ARMS procedure type was associated with a higher risk of re-intervention in the univariable analysis (OR: 2.56, p=0.04). The orientation of myotomy (anterior vs. posterior) also approached significance. Other factors such as age, sex, BMI, and basal GERDQ scores, although analyzed, did not show a significant impact on the need for re-intervention across the analyses (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first cohort study to compare endoscopic procedures for intractable GERD after POEM. Drawing on prior research that showed a tendency, although not statistically significant, for ARMS to be preferred in more severe cases over radiofrequency ablation, we generally allocated patients with elevated GERDQ scores to ARMS instead of SRF.14 Additionally, patients with Barrett’s esophagus or a higher FVG were preferentially treated with ARMA or ARMS, based on findings from previous studies.15,16 Similarly, as ARMA and ARMS capitalize on scarring and fibrosis effects,17 with endoscopic mucosal resection thought to amplify these effects beyond those of ablation, ARMS was often the preferred choice for patients exhibiting severe symptoms and basal EndoFLIP DI. In these cases, if the risk of bleeding or stricture appeared significant, ARMA was selected instead of ARMS. Over 6 months, the change in the GERDQ score differed among the three groups. The ARMS group, which had the highest baseline score, showed the greatest improvement, suggesting its efficacy in treating severe post-POEM GERD. Although the number of patients with improvement was similar across groups (Table 2), possibly indicating a type II error, the ARMS group had the most pronounced reduction in disease severity, as evidenced by improvements in endoscopic parameters, including FVG and LA grade (Fig. 5). Ultimately, 54.5% of the patients stopped taking PPIs (100% in the SRF group), likely reflecting the preferential selection of SRF for patients with milder symptoms.

In this study, the decision for reintervention was primarily based on subjective symptom relief, as GERD-related injury and inflammation may correlate with symptoms even when not evident on objective tests.18 In our study, reintervention was performed in patients who continued to have GERDQ scores of ≥8 at 6 months after the procedure or persistent symptoms despite ongoing PPI use, and whose EndoFLIP DI exceeded 13.98 mm²/mmHg.13 Type III achalasia and posterior orientation of myotomy during POEM seemed to be related to the need for reintervention (Table 4). These findings suggest the need to carefully consider these factors during procedural planning and execution. When considering reintervention, as no data have been reported on procedure-specific adverse events such as stricture formation, we adopted a limited treatment scope and allowed reintervention without a predefined limit. This approach made it difficult to directly associate the reintervention rate with procedural efficacy. Future studies are needed to establish clear criteria for selecting reintervention and the extent of treatment.

However, the exact cause of intractable GERD after POEM remains unclear. However, it can include disruption of the anti-reflux barrier,1 interruption of the acid pocket located between the distal esophagus and proximal stomach,19 impairment of the vagal response of the cardia leading to increased TLESRs,2,20,21 adhesion due to microperforation22 during myotomy, or destruction of the collar sling fibers.7 Therefore, we considered these factors when treating patients with intractable GERD. From a pathophysiological perspective, a minor increase in LES pressure can effectively manage reflux,6,23,24 and ARMA, ARMS, and SRF enhance LES pressure through remodeling and fibrosis.8,9 Furthermore, SRF, which primarily affects the gastric cardia, reduces the TLESR by interrupting the vagal neural pathway,9,25 which is presumably attributable to ARMA and ARMS.

The treatment of intractable GERD after POEM is challenging. From a procedural standpoint in the LES and adjacent structures, POEM and GERD treatments have opposing effects. Therefore, caution is warranted for adverse effects, such as strictures, which could be induced by efforts to cause LES scarring and increase LES pressure to counteract possible iatrogenic LES defects, which starkly contrasts with the primary objective of POEM. Similar to the findings reported by Sumi et al.,26 14% of the patients in our study required balloon dilation, and 16.7% in the ARMS group developed a stricture.

One patient presented with intractable GERD after POEM, which needed to be approached differently and with more caution than the typical refractory GERD. The GERDQ score decreased from 12 to 1, but the Eckardt score worsened after ARMS. Botox injection and balloon dilation were subsequently performed; however, severe GERD symptoms recurred, necessitating ARMA procedures 2 and 4 years after the initial treatment. Ultimately, marked improvement was observed not only in the subjective symptoms but also in the endoscopic findings, with resolution of achalasia and GERD.

In our study, we also analyzed the factors associated with intractable GERD after POEM. Contrary to previous research,27 recent studies have indicated that factors such as myotomy length and direction do not significantly affect GERD outcomes.28 Intractable GERD appeared to occur more frequently in patients who underwent POEM for EGJOO. Therefore, the timing of POEM in EGJOO cases should be carefully determined by considering the symptoms and the point of transition to achalasia.

This study had some limitations. First, the outcomes were assessed at 6-month intervals after the procedure. Long-term data are needed, as prior research indicated FVG improvement in the ARMS group after 10 years.29 Second, the lack of consensus regarding the extent of mucosal resection or ablation hampers the ability to achieve consistency in each procedure. Third, the 24-hour pH impedance test results after the procedure were limited. However, Kumbhari et al.30 found no significant differences between patients with normal and high DeMeester scores in terms of symptoms, endoscopic grade, or HRM results after POEM. Fourth, the cohort size was relatively small, and a single endoscopist performed all procedures. Nonetheless, considering the rarity of intractable GERD after POEM, which makes research on this topic difficult, observing the trends between procedures is significant. Building on this study, further research involving larger sample sizes across multiple institutions is necessary. The possibility of selection bias also arises in the choice of the procedure, as patients with more severe GERD symptoms are more likely to undergo ARMS. However, in a previous study comparing ARMS and ARMA using propensity score matching analysis, no significant difference was observed in the primary outcome.31 As standardized criteria for structural changes before and after POEM are lacking, we adopted arbitrary cutoff values based on previous studies. These issues could be addressed by increasing the number of patients to adjust for this bias or by conducting large prospective randomized studies in the future. Finally, symptom assessment of outcomes using the GERDQ and Eckardt scores may be subjective. We counterbalanced this limitation by employing multimodal evaluations, including EndoFLIP and endoscopy.

The results of this study have several important implications. First, to the best of our knowledge, no previous study has compared endoscopic mucosectomy, ablation, and radiofrequency ablation for intractable GERD after POEM. Second, abrupt structural changes during POEM may lead to iatrogenic GERD, opening a new avenue for future GERD interventions.

In this preliminary feasibility study, ARMS appeared to show greater improvement in subjective symptoms compared with ARMA and SRF; however, this trend was not observed in objective measures such as endoscopic esophagitis or EndoFLIP DI.
