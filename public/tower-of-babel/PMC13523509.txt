Models of publicly funded home care for community-dwelling older adults across six high-income health systems: a scoping review
Authors: Marilyn Macdonald, David Litvack, Rochelle Litvack, Paulina Carrasco, Nadia Stec, Kristy Hancock, Alyssa Barker, Ali Hammoud, Grace Magro, Andrea Tricco, Sharon Strauss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPublicly funded home care is pivotal in supporting ageing in place across high-income health systems. Home care delivery models vary in funding, governance, and service delivery. The scope, distribution, and comparability of evaluation evidence is limited.ObjectiveTo map and characterise models of publicly funded home care for community-dwelling older adults across six high-income countries: Canada, England, Denmark, Sweden, Australia, and New Zealand, and to synthesise available evaluation evidence related to model characteristics, quality, patient and family satisfaction, integration with health and social care, and equity-relevant considerations.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in accordance with JBI methodology and reported following PRISMA-ScR guidelines. MEDLINE, Embase, and CINAHL were searched from 2005 onwards, alongside targeted grey literature searches. Data meeting the inclusion criteria were extracted and synthesised using qualitative content analysis. An equity lens was applied using the PROGRESS-Plus framework, and SAGER guidelines.ResultsNineteen sources were included, comprising 14 peer-reviewed studies and five grey literature documents. Five models of publicly funded home care were identified: (1) provincially/territorially organized home care in Canada, where models vary by jurisdiction and level of integration; (2) the National Home Care Packages Program in Australia organized around consumer directed care; (3) the Universal Welfare Model managed municipally in Denmark and Sweden; (4) Cash for Care/Direct Payments in England; and (5) nationally framed Home and Community Support Services in New Zealand. Characteristics most common across models were public funding and professional as well as support services. Background

Population ageing is increasing demand for home care and home support services across high-income countries, placing sustained pressure on health and social care systems to support older adults to remain at home for as long as possible [1]. Publicly funded home care typically comprises professional services, such as nursing, rehabilitation, as well as personal support services, including assistance with activities of daily living and instrumental activities of daily living, alongside supports for informal caregivers, and is widely positioned as a cornerstone of ageing-in-place strategies within people-centred care frameworks [1, 2]. Despite this central role, home care is organised through diverse funding arrangements, governance structures, and service configurations that reflect variation in welfare regimes, health system organisation, and policy priorities across countries [2].

Across settings, longstanding concerns have been raised regarding the alignment of available workforce capacity with the needs of older adults and family caregivers, particularly for individuals living with chronic conditions, functional limitations, or complex care needs [3]. Prior research indicates that relational continuity, coordination across services, and responsiveness to individual circumstances are salient to perceived quality and satisfaction in home care, yet these dimensions are not consistently embedded within formal models and have been unevenly operationalised in evaluation studies [4, 5]. At the same time, reforms oriented toward consumer choice, service integration, or system efficiency have redistributed responsibilities among older adults, families, providers, and system actors, raising questions about how different models are experienced across diverse populations [6].

Although home care has attracted sustained policy and research attention, the literature offers limited clarity regarding how publicly funded home care models—understood as organised arrangements of funding, governance, and service delivery—are defined, categorised, and documented across countries [3]. Evaluation evidence related to model characteristics, quality, satisfaction, integration, and equity is dispersed across academic and grey literature. Recent work indicates that systematic assessments of home care quality remain limited in scope and unevenly developed, with persistent gaps in how quality is measured and operationalised across different contexts [5]. In addition, equity-relevant characteristics, including socioeconomic position, race or ethnicity, disability, and gender, are inconsistently reported, limiting comparative understanding of how different models may shape access and experience for diverse populations [7, 8].

Previous reviews have examined specific aspects of home care provision, service gaps, or interventions for particular populations, including within the Canadian context, but have not systematically mapped publicly funded home care models across multiple high-income health systems while also synthesising recent evaluation evidence related to quality, satisfaction, integration, and equity [3]. In the context of demographic ageing and continued policy emphasis on integrated, people-centred care, a systematic mapping of existing models and the scope of available evaluation evidence is warranted [1, 2].

Accordingly, this scoping review maps and characterises publicly funded home care models for community-dwelling older adults across health systems in six high income countries—Canada, England, Denmark, Sweden, Australia, and New Zealand—and synthesises the scope of reported evaluation evidence related to model characteristics, quality, patient and family satisfaction, integration with health and social care, and equity-relevant considerations. These six countries were pre-selected on the basis that they share foundational principles of publicly funded home care and universal or near-universal health coverage, and because their policy and practice contexts are sufficiently comparable to Canada to yield findings relevant to the Canadian system.

Methods

The review was conducted in accordance with JBI methodology for scoping reviews [9] and reported following the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‑ScR) [10]. The PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided in Appendix IV. This review is registered on the Open Science Framework. https://osf.io/gvkqe/.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were structured using the Population, Concept, Context (PCC) framework and applied to all sources of evidence. Eligible sources reported on community-dwelling adults aged 60 years and older who were supported by an informal caregiver (e.g., a family member or close friend) and received publicly funded home care in Canada, Sweden, Denmark, England, Australia, and New Zealand. Sources were required to describe, analyse or evaluate models or programmes of publicly funded home care, including their characteristics, components, implementation and any reported outcomes or indicators of quality, such as client or caregiver satisfaction or perceived quality of care. Empirical and non-empirical sources published from 2005 onwards were eligible, including primary studies, reviews, policy and programme documents and grey literature. Research focused on specialist palliative care models was excluded, as the distinction is usually generalist versus specialist programs that are not typically available to other community-dwelling home care recipients.

Search strategy and selection criteria

Published and unpublished literature were searched. Published studies were identified in MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Elsevier) and CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCO). An initial search strategy was developed in MEDLINE using subject headings and keywords to describe the concepts of older adults, home care, caregivers, and workforce. Search terms for older adults and home care were identified from a published review protocol [11]. Database searches were limited to publication years 2005 and onwards, with no additional restrictions. The MEDLINE search was peer reviewed by a second information specialist using the PRESS checklist [12] and subsequently adapted for Embase and CINAHL. All database searches were executed on 19 July 2024; database and grey literature search strategies are in Appendix I. The database searches were not updated in 2025 because the 12-month rule on search completion to manuscript submission is arbitrary and topic or question dependent [13]. We held a knowledge mobilization event to share preliminary findings with key informants in home care to confirm the relevance of the findings.

Grey literature was identified in January and July 2025 through two complementary approaches: Google using the string “[country/province] home care policy”, and targeted browsing of government websites identified during background work. A search for retracted articles was not performed.

Data collection

Search results from all databases were imported into Covidence [14], which automatically removed duplicate records. Prior to each screening stage calibration exercises were conducted with team members to verify clarity of inclusion and exclusion criteria and consensus among screeners. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Potentially relevant sources underwent full-text review. At both screening stages, discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer, and reasons for exclusion at full text were recorded (Appendix II). For grey literature, one reviewer screened the first page of search results for each Google search string and conducted targeted browsing of the government websites. Included grey literature was verified by a second reviewer. The study selection process is documented in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Fig. 1) [10].

PRISMA Diagram. The Sager Guidelines Checklist is reference number 7 and contains a checklist

Two reviewers extracted data from included studies using a pilot-tested tool. The extracted data encompassed: bibliographic details; literature type; study aims, research design, and methods; population characteristics; reported guiding theory or framework; home care model details; reported health and social outcomes; and model evaluations. The table of included studies is presented in Appendix III.

Data analysis

A qualitative content analysis was conducted [15] to identify and describe home care models across countries and sub-national jurisdictions, including their characteristics and any reported evaluations. Findings are presented as a descriptive narrative summary by country and their respective model because the models are not the same across the dataset denoting that context matters and that publicly funded home care policy is decided and delivered jurisdictionally. Appendix V depicts the table of models by country and the reader will see there are common elements such as public funding and means tested yet the degree of variation among countries indicates that models are fashioned to context.

An equity lens was applied using the PROGRESS-Plus framework, which considers place of residence, race/ethnicity, occupation, gender/sex, religion, education, socioeconomic status, social capital, and “plus” factors such as age, disability, and sexual orientation [8]. PROGRESS-Plus characteristics were extracted where reported, and the presence or absence of equity-relevant reporting and outcomes was documented.

Consideration of sex and gender was assessed using the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines [7]. Data on whether and how sex and/or gender were incorporated in study design, analyses, results, and interpretations were extracted and summarized to describe the extent of sex and gender reporting in the included evidence.

Results

The database search retrieved 8,513 citations. After removing 3,163 duplicates in Covidence, 5,350 records underwent title and abstract screening, excluding 5,224. Full-text review of 126 sources resulted in 112 exclusions (Appendix II), leaving 14 research articles. The grey literature search identified 70 sources, with 5 meeting the inclusion criteria. In total, 19 sources of evidence were included.

Characteristics of included sources

The 19 included sources comprised 14 peer‑reviewed and 5 grey literature sources, published between 2009 and 2023. Peer-reviewed studies were conducted in Canada (n = 4) [16–19], Sweden (n = 2) [20, 21], Australia (n = 5) [22–26], England (n = 1) [27], and Denmark (n = 1) [28], including one comparative analysis of Sweden and Denmark (n = 1) [29]. Grey literature included provincial and national home-care policy and program documents from Canada (n = 3) [30–32] and New Zealand (n = 2) [33, 34]. (Appendix III).

Among the peer-reviewed publications,12 were empirical studies, including cross-sectional surveys [20–22], interrupted time-series analysis [23], secondary data analysis [29], discrete choice experiment [24], randomised controlled trials with embedded qualitative descriptions [27], implementation research [18], a participatory action-oriented case study [25], and qualitative studies employing thematic analysis [17, 22], constructivist grounded theory [26], and a reflective ethnographic approach [28]. Two remaining non-empirical publications included a descriptive “portrait” of Canadian urban home-based primary care programmes and their features [19], and a policy and practice analysis of home care needs in rural and remote Ontario, Canada [16]. Across empirical studies, the combined sample encompassed approximately 66,166 participants; exact sample sizes were not consistently reported.

Participant characteristics rarely went beyond age and biological sex. Adults aged ≥ 80 years were more frequently represented than those aged 60–79 years. Few studies distinguished sex from gender or examined gendered care roles. In Australia, one study in an Indigenous community reported gender-specific data and described how cultural context and ethnic origin shaped home care arrangements [25]: while another described additional factors such as living situation and socioeconomic conditions [26]. A Swedish study of three municipal home care models reported age, sex, country of birth, and education, but did not examine other equity‑relevant characteristics [20], and a Denmark–Sweden comparison analysed how policy reforms influenced access to public home care and reliance on family and privately purchased support across social groups [29]. Apart from these exceptions, characteristics aligned with PROGRESS‑Plus [8] and SAGER [7] guidelines such as race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, education, religion, disability, social capital, and sexual orientation, and any intersectional analysis of differential access, experiences, or outcomes were largely absent.

Review findings

Across the included sources, this scoping review charted five models of publicly funded home care for community‑dwelling older adults: (1) provincially/territorially organized home care in Canada, where each of the 10 provinces and three territories are responsible for delivering home care that varies by jurisdiction, examples include Integrated Home‑Based Primary Care (IHBPC) programmes, the “Home is Best” integrated Home Health model [17–19], and Ontario Health at Home31; (2) the National Home Care Packages Program [22, 23] including those delivered on a Consumer‑Directed Care basis [22, 26], in Australia; (3) Nordic municipal home care within the universal welfare model in Sweden and Denmark [20, 21, 28, 29]; (4) Cash for Care/Direct Payments in England [27]; and (5) New Zealand’s nationally framed Home and Community Support Services (HCSS) and related continuum‑of‑care approach [33, 34] (Appendix V, Table 1). Findings are described by country and synthesised across models in relation to quality, patient and family satisfaction, and integration with health and social care systems, consistent with the scoping objectives to identify home care models and their characteristics and to map available evaluation evidence.

Canada

The included Canadian literature identified several provincially organised, publicly funded home care models for community-dwelling older adults, delivered as needs-assessed packages of nursing, rehabilitation, and personal support [16]. Policy documents consistently characterised these programmes as needs-based services with a core goal of supporting ageing in place within provincial health systems [30, 32]. Considering provincial and territorial contexts the included sources describe several examples of home care delivery that differ in integration, governance, and service delivery.

These examples include Fraser Health’s “Home is Best” strategy, reported as an integrated home-health approach with shared governance across home health, primary care, and acute care, supported by regular case manager–family physician case reviews [18]. Urban Integrated Home-Based Primary Care (IHBPC) programmes were profiled, in which interprofessional teams provided home-based medical care for homebound older adults in collaboration with regional home care services [19]. Ontario Health at Home was described as coordinating publicly funded home care services in Ontario, including nursing, rehabilitation, personal support, and connections to community services for people with complex or chronic conditions [31]. Another study examined the Seniors Managing Independent Living Easily (SMILE) programme, a subsidised model in which clients employ independent contractors, and reported participants’ descriptions of continuity of care as well as accounts of employment conditions experienced by caregivers [17]. Only one Canadian study, published in 2019, reported process-level and utilisation outcomes19, and no subsequent evaluations reported broader outcome data across the Canadian examples.

Australia

The included Australian literature largely focused on the national Home Care Packages (HCP) Program and its evolution toward consumer-directed care and self-management [22–24]. One distinct, Indigenous culturally grounded community model was also identified [25].

Australian sources described the HCP Program as a publicly funded, needs-assessed system designed to support ageing in place [23]. A core characteristic was the emphasis on consumer choice, control, and budget autonomy, embedded through policy reforms in 2013 and 2017 [22, 23]. A qualitative study identified features highly valued by users, including choice of provider, choice of support worker, flexibility in care activities, and contact with the service coordinator24. In contrast, the Yuendumu Old People’s Programme was described as a “Family Model of Care” rooted in Warlpiri kinship governance, community control, and cultural safety [25].

The included Australian evidence reported perceived benefits and challenges across consumer-directed and self-managed arrangements. An evaluation of a self-management trial reported higher scores on measures of perceived choice, control, and financial autonomy, alongside qualitative accounts describing well-being experiences; no adverse safety outcomes were reported within the study timeframe [22]. An analysis of the 2017 reforms reported a sharp reduction in new package allocations and a shift toward younger, partnered recipients [23]. Qualitative research in home-based dementia care highlighted that staff turnover and task-oriented care could disrupt the relational continuity valued by clients and family carers [26]. A sector analysis reported persistent system fragmentation and workforce instability within the Australian aged care system [33].

New Zealand

The included New Zealand literature described nationally commissioned home and community support within a continuum-of-care framework, emphasising equity and cultural responsiveness. The literature described the national Home and Community Support Services (HCSS) framework as a continuum-of-care system designed to link home support with residential aged-care settings [33, 34]. The national framework outlined core characteristics, including the use of standardised interRAI assessments for needs-based eligibility, beginning at age 65 with lower thresholds for Māori (55 +) and Pacific peoples (60 +) to address inequities. A central, reported characteristic was the embedding of cultural responsiveness and equity through Te Tiriti o Waitangi and the Pae Ora (Healthy Futures) principles, which inform service design and workforce standards [34]. A sector analysis described smoother care transitions, expanded consumer choice, and workforce initiatives, such as achieving nurse and care worker pay parity between the aged care system and the health system, for recruitment and retention [33].

England

The included literature from England described a cash-for-care model. The model provided eligible older people with a personal budget to purchase and manage their own support in place of conventionally commissioned services, with the aim of increasing choice, control, and personalisation [27]. Cash for Care placed substantial responsibility on users to organise care, manage funds, and employ staff. Results included reports that participants perceived benefits such as increased choice, more continuous and personalised care, and the opportunity to remunerate family carers, alongside significant anxiety about administrative tasks, budgeting, and employer responsibilities. Older people were reported to have higher levels of psychological ill-health and lower self-perceived health than those receiving conventional services, and no overall improvement in social care–related quality of life. No evaluations of cash-for-care models published after 2019 were identified [27].

Sweden and Denmark

The included Swedish and Danish sources described universal, tax-funded systems in which municipalities organise and deliver needs-assessed home care for older adults [21, 29]. In Sweden, home care was reported to emphasise autonomy, respect, continuity, and preventive approaches, with an increasingly restrictive focus on older adults with the highest care needs [29]. Municipal “free-choice” systems, allowing older adults to select among approved providers, were examined in a comparative study that found more positive responses regarding perceived choice and staff respect in municipalities implementing provider choice. At the same time, traditional in-house provision scored highest for personalised care and overall satisfaction, and the service-choice model scored lowest across outcomes [20]. Another national study identified relational process factors, such as respectful treatment and continuity, as primary drivers of user satisfaction, rather than structural indicators such as budget level [21].

Danish home care was described as a broad, universal system organised and financed by municipalities, with coverage across social classes and an explicit focus on supporting independent living through preventive and rehabilitation-oriented programmes. Municipalities were reported to be required to offer at least two home-care providers to ensure user choice [29]. A qualitative study highlighted the centrality of relational dimensions of care, particularly recognition, for vulnerable older adults, and noted that these aspects were often constrained by task-driven organisational structures [28]. Dunér et al. [20] provided the only Swedish outcome evaluation published since 2019, and no outcome evaluations of Danish home-care models were identified.

Content analysis and discussion

This scoping review maps publicly funded home care models encompassing professional and support services for community-dwelling older adults across systems in six high-income countries and synthesizes evaluation evidence on quality, satisfaction, and integration. Four cross-cutting insights emerge across model types and cross-national contexts. These insights were identified through the qualitative content analysis of included sources and are presented alongside contextual description of the evidence from which they derive, consistent with JBI methodology on integrated descriptive narrative presentation in scoping reviews [9, 15].

Relational dimensions of care and workforce conditions

Across included studies examining experiences and reported satisfaction, perceived quality was primarily characterised by relational dimensions, whereas satisfaction reflects evaluative judgements shaped by these processes rather than by structural features alone [21, 26, 28]. In Swedish national survey research, process factors such as respectful treatment, access to information, and perceived influence are more salient to reported satisfaction than structural indicators such as budgets or staff training [21]. A pre-2019 descriptive qualitative study from Denmark characterized higher-quality home care for vulnerable older adults in terms of recognition, respect, and responsiveness to personal histories and preferences, and described how task-driven organization could constrain these relational dimensions [28]. A qualitative study from Australia similarly positioned relational continuity and responsive support as central to perceived quality, and reported poorer experiences when services were delivered through time-pressured, task-oriented routines [26]. These convergent accounts align with external syntheses, indicating that trust, continuity, and feeling known remain central to older people’s notions of quality in home care and to relationship-centred care more broadly4,35.

Within the included evidence, workforce conditions are presented as shaping relational continuity and trust rather than as background implementation details [16, 26, 28]. Included sources described turnover, fragmented rostering, and task-oriented time norms as disrupting continuity and eroding trust, constraining the capacity of otherwise well-specified models to support relationship-centred care [26, 28]. Conversely, descriptive accounts of culturally governed programmes and stable, locally rooted teams illustrated how continuity can be experienced as being known and respected in Indigenous and rural settings, including the Yuendumu Family Model of Care and rural Canadian home-care programmes [16, 25] Recent observational evidence reported associations between home-care worker continuity and clients' health, well-being, and satisfaction, positioning continuity as a valued feature of home care, although causal pathways cannot be established from the available evidence36.

Autonomy and administrative demands

The autonomy-oriented models,—consumer-directed care in Australia and cash-for-care in England—are described as intended to expand choice and control toward users or their representatives [22, 23].

Australian studies report higher perceived autonomy in contexts where clear information, administrative, or caregivers' support is available [22]. At the same time, expanded choice is also described as accompanied by increased administrative responsibility for older adults and their families, including budgeting, coordination, and employer-like tasks, shaping how autonomy is experienced in practice [22, 27].

Provider-level analysis following Australia’s “Increasing Choice in Home Care” reforms reported reduced new entries and a shift toward younger, partnered new entrants [23], raising questions about access for frailer or less-resourced older adults. Earlier descriptive evidence from England and Sweden similarly suggests that formal choice can be constrained by information complexity, organisational routines, and continuity conditions [20, 27]. As external context, Woolham et al. [6] found no statistically significant outcome differences between older direct payment users and those with managed budgets, despite reported appreciation of control, pointing to the need for equity-sensitive evaluation of who benefits, under what conditions, and at what administrative cost.

Integration aims and limited outcome evidence

Integration is widely articulated as an organising principle, while empirical evaluation remains limited. Integrated home-care arrangements are described as seeking to reduce fragmentation through shared assessment processes, formalised case-management roles, and strengthened linkages between home care, primary care, and specialist services [16, 18, 19]. Descriptive studies and policy documents frame coordination and continuity as mechanisms intended to support system navigation and efficiency [16, 33, 34].

The included Canadian study of Integrated Home-Based Primary Care reports perceived improvements in communication across providers, clearer accountability, and reduced acute-care use among medically complex older adults [19]; however, these findings are observational, context-specific, and limited to process and utilisation indicators. Earlier programme-level analyses of integrated physician–home health models similarly describe enhanced coordination and continuity but also rely on descriptive or observational evidence and pre-date the more recent evaluative period [18].

It remains unclear whether integrated home-care arrangements translate into sustained outcomes for older adults and caregivers beyond utilisation indicators. Descriptive and qualitative sources report structural challenges—including fragmented accountability, reliance on independent contracting, and workforce instability—that may limit continuity despite formal integration mechanisms [16, 17]. Integrated-care frameworks emphasise the need for aligned governance, financing, and workforce capacity to achieve measurable outcomes, helping to explain the uneven and limited evaluative evidence in home and community care [1–3].

Equity, cultural safety, and sustainability

Throughout the reviewed literature, equity and cultural safety were most clearly described in selected home care models, with limited empirical assessment of their realisation in practice. In New Zealand, national Home and Community Support Services specifications explicitly incorporated equity commitments, including differentiated age thresholds for Māori and Pacific peoples and Treaty-based principles, positioning cultural responsiveness as a structural feature of service organisation rather than an optional programme attribute [34]. Similarly, an Indigenous-led community model in Australia described cultural governance and kinship authority as foundational to trust, acceptability, and continuity of care for Aboriginal elders [25]. These examples suggest that equity can be embedded at the level of model architecture.

What the evidence reports, however, is largely descriptive. While some studies documented policy reforms or service configurations with equity-relevant implications, few evaluated whether these arrangements translated into differential access, experiences, or outcomes for diverse population groups. Nordic comparative analyses described shifts toward tighter eligibility and greater reliance on family or privately purchased care, but did not assess downstream equity impacts empirically [29]. These shifts point to sustainability pressures for historically universal systems in the absence of contemporary outcome evaluation.

PROGRESS-Plus characteristics were infrequently reported, intersectional analyses were rare, and sex was more commonly documented than gender, with limited examination of how care arrangements might be patterned by social position or caregiving roles [15, 17, 22, 31]. Consequently, the review cannot determine whether particular models promote or constrain equity; rather, it highlights that equity and cultural safety remain insufficiently operationalised within home-care evaluations, limiting conclusions beyond policy intent and structural description.

Implications for research

The evidence highlights several priorities for future research on publicly funded home care models. Outcome‑focused studies are needed to assess clients' and caregivers' experiences, equity, workforce sustainability, and quality of life across different contexts. Comparative and longitudinal research without country limits would be valuable for examining how reforms perform under demographic ageing and fiscal pressure, and for enabling cross‑national learning. Evaluations of autonomy‑oriented and integration‑oriented reforms should systematically consider where coordination of labour, administrative burden, and risk are located—whether with older adults, families, providers, or system actors—rather than focusing solely on nominal increases in choice or the presence of coordination structures. There is also a pressing need to strengthen equity‑sensitive evaluation by consistently incorporating PROGRESS‑Plus and sex/gender reporting and by designing analyses capable of examining differential access, experience, and outcomes for populations experiencing structural disadvantage. Future studies should also examine how cognitive impairment and decision-making capacity shape access to, navigation of, and experiences with publicly funded home care models.

Strengths and limitations

This review was strengthened by adherence to JBI methodological guidance, including systematic database and grey literature searches, independent dual screening, and structured data extraction, as well as use of qualitative content analysis and equity frameworks to guide synthesis. Mapping evidence across six publicly funded systems enabled identification of recurring patterns that would not be visible in single‑country analyses, and inclusion of policy and programme documents extended coverage beyond peer‑reviewed studies. The limitations primarily reflect the underlying evidence base. The database searches were conducted without language restrictions; however, the grey literature search used English-language search strings and relied on English language or translatable government websites, which may have constrained retrieval of non-English policy documents. The included evidence was unevenly distributed across countries and was weighted toward older and descriptive accounts; few outcome-focused studies were published after 2019, particularly for Canadian and Danish models. Because eligible sources were required to include older adults supported by an unpaid caregiver, the review may underrepresent the perspectives of older adults receiving home care without informal caregiver support. In accordance with JBI scoping review methodology, study quality was not appraised.

Conclusion

This scoping review identified and characterised publicly funded home care models for older adults across six high‑income health systems and synthesised available evidence on quality, satisfaction, integration, and equity. Across diverse contexts, relational and equity dimensions appeared central to older people’s reported experiences, yet they were rarely operationalised as primary outcomes in the included evidence, and recent outcome‑focused evaluations remained scarce. The findings suggest that future reform of home care for older adults would benefit from not only innovative model design but also robust, equity‑sensitive evaluation capable of assessing how different models perform for diverse populations under demographic and fiscal pressures, in line with global healthy ageing and people‑centred care agendas.
