Skull base osteomyelitis and cranio-cervical junction instability as long-term sequelae of Lemierre syndrome: A case report
Authors: Saima Armana, Katie Holland, Alvyn Hernandez Reyes, Stephanie Bentley, Krupa Patel, Samantha Friedman, Vincent C. Traynelis, David C. Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The “classic” presentation of Lemierre syndrome is a post-anginal sepsis with internal jugular thrombophlebitis and metastatic infection caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum or other Fusobacterium species. Skull base and cervical osteomyelitis are rare complications of Lemierre syndrome and cranio-cervical junction instability is a rare complication of head and neck infections overall. Herein, we report a case of Lemierre syndrome that was complicated by skull base and cervical spinal osteomyelitis, leading to CN XII palsy, atlanto-axial instability, and basilar invagination. At the time of this report, the patient is being managed non-operatively and has been advised to avoid contact sports to prevent spinal cord injury. In cases where Lemierre syndrome is complicated by skull base osteomyelitis, clinicians may consider adequate follow-up to monitor for long-term sequelae.

Introduction

Lemierre syndrome is a rare but serious disease characterized by 1) a preceding oropharyngeal infection (angina) and presents with 2) evidence of metastatic lesions and 3) evidence of internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis or isolation of Fusobacterium species (usually F. necrophorum) from blood culture or normally sterile fluid [1]. The lungs are the most common site of metastatic infection in Lemierre syndrome, with studies reporting 71–92% of cases with pulmonary lesions [1], [2]. However, a wide range of other involved sites have also been reported including brain, heart, liver, kidney, bones, and joints. Skull base and cervical spine osteomyelitis are extremely rare complications of Lemierre syndrome and, consequently, may also present with cranial nerve (CN) palsies [3], [4], [5]. Atlanto-axial instability has been described as a long-term consequence of skull base osteomyelitis with other pathogens [6], [7], [8], [9]. Herein, we report a case of Lemierre syndrome that was complicated by retropharyngeal abscess, skull base and cervical spinal osteomyelitis, associated hypoglossal nerve palsy, and cranio-cervical junction instability detected five months after hospitalization.

Case description

A previously healthy 17-year-old male presented as a transfer from an affiliated emergency department with one week of sore throat and worsening bilateral posterior neck pain. Prior to admission, he had been seen in an affiliated emergency department (ED) where his exam was significant for oropharyngeal erythema and tenderness of his bilateral sternocleidomastoid muscles. Heterophile antibody test and rapid PCR tests for Group A Streptococcus. SARS-CoV-2, RSV, and influenza were all negative. Head and neck computerized tomography (CT) imaging during these ED encounters had no significant findings but he had transaminase elevations and thrombocytopenia (see Table 1). An abdominal ultrasound demonstrated hepatomegaly (20.4 cm in length) with normal echogenicity. The patient was sent home with instructions for supportive care. The patient returned to the same ED two days later and endorsed neck pain worse with movement and his neck was swollen. He had also developed intermittent rigors, epigastric pain, two instances of non-bloody, non-bilious emesis, and intermittent diarrhea. His temperature was as high as 38.8 °C and he had mild tachycardia. He was placed on supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula at a rate of 2 L/min to maintain a normal oxygen saturation. The patient was empirically started on intravenous vancomycin and cefepime and transferred to our Pediatric Intensive Care Unit. Patient Laboratory Values.

Lemierre syndrome had been suspected at admission. A Doppler ultrasound study was performed on hospital day (HD) 1 and was negative for thrombosis of the internal jugular or brachiocephalic veins. Blood cultures collected in the ED on HD 1 were positive on HD 2 for growth of Gram-negative bacilli, ultimately identified as F. necrophorum. His empiric antibiotic therapy was adjusted, and he was treated with ceftriaxone 2 g intravenous every 24 h and metronidazole 500 mg intravenous every 8 h. CT angiography of the chest was negative for pulmonary emboli.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed on HD 2 of the patient’s neck found a thin retropharyngeal abscess spanning from the levels of C1 to C5, peri-vertebral phlegmonous changes at C1 to C2, small effusions in both lateral C1-C2 articulation, and no evidence of osteomyelitis (Fig. 1). Pediatric otolaryngology (ENT) performed a flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy (FFL) and found non-obstructive posterior pharyngeal crowding. Surgical intervention was initially deferred because of the patient’s thrombocytopenia, and he received dexamethasone to try to reduce the swelling. However, the patient’s pain worsened on HD 5, and he continued to have fevers. A repeated FFL showed worsened posterior pharyngeal crowding. A CT of his neck showed worsening edema of the retropharyngeal soft tissue and worsened narrowing of the supraglottic airway. The patient had also developed tongue deviation to the left side suggesting CN XII palsy (Fig. 2); this finding would persist for multiple weeks. He was taken to the operating room where Pediatric ENT drained 2 mL of murky fluid from the retropharyngeal abscess. On HD 10, the patient reported worsening of his neck pain and decreased range of motion. Repeat MRI of the neck and skull base now demonstrated enhancement of the clivus, petrous apices, bilateral occipital condyles, lateral masses of C1 and enhancing soft tissue fullness of the cavernous sinus; these findings were suggestive of skull base osteomyelitis (Fig. 3). Neurosurgery did not recommend any surgical intervention and agreed with continued antibiotic therapy. The patient was discharged on HD 19 with clindamycin 900 mg intravenous dosed every 8 h. With continued stability and improvement in inflammatory markers in outpatient follow-up, the clindamycin was changed to oral formulation 600 mg three times daily. A follow-up MRI of the skull base completed about four weeks after discharge demonstrated near complete resolution of the previous osteomyelitis-related findings and the patient ultimately completed a total of about seven weeks of antibiotics including what he received during inpatient hospitalization. Small filling defects in the bilateral distal sigmoid/jugular bulb junction were also seen on the imaging from HD 10 (Fig. 3) and confirmed on dedicated imaging, consistent with non-occlusive thrombi. Five months after discharge, the patient reported that the range of motion of his neck was improved but still limited despite physical therapy. No neurologic deficits were found on physical exam. Repeat MRI found no evidence of recurrence of osteomyelitis; however, x-rays of the neck with flexion and extension demonstrated widening of the atlanto-dental interval. Neurosurgery evaluated the patient in clinic and CT imaging of the cervical spine showed mild basilar invagination (4 mm beyond McRae line) worsened on flexion, widening atlanto-dental interval (5.3 mm) worse with flexion (5.6 mm) and extension (5.7 mm), osseous fusion across the bilateral atlanto-occipital articulation, and degenerative changes across C1-C2 articulation (Fig. 4). Neurosurgery could not offer any intervention to improve neck mobility. Because his atlanto-axial instability could make him prone to the cervical spinal cord injury, the patient was advised to refrain from contact sports (he played American gridiron football and rugby). Discussion

Several uncommon sequelae of Lemierre syndrome were found in our patient, and all share pathophysiologic links. Firstly, our patient also had an extremely rare complication of Lemierre syndrome in skull base and cervical spinal osteomyelitis. About 5% of Lemierre syndrome report osteomyelitis and these include infection of long bones and pelvic bones [1]. However, osteomyelitis of the skull base as a complication of Lemierre syndrome appears limited to a few previous case reports; these cases are summarized in Table 2 [3], [4], [5]. Summary of Reported Lemierre Syndrome Cases with Skull Base Osteomyelitis.

The CN XII palsy found in our patient was most likely related to inflammation in the path of CN XII along the skull base and/or the soft tissue of the neck. Among the four other cases reported with Lemierre-related skull base osteomyelitis, two cases had CN XII palsy alone and one case reported having both CN VI and XII palsies [3], [5]. This combined CN VI and XII palsy is known as clival syndrome and can be seen with infections, tumors, and trauma of the clivus where both nerves traverse. Two of the previous cases reported only partial resolution of CN XII palsy at follow-up assessment [3], [5]. However, one did not report follow-up beyond 3 months and the other had an unspecified time to follow-up. Our patient had resolution of his CN XII palsy during his antibiotic treatment course.

Finally, our patient developed cranio-cervical joint instability following Lemierre syndrome. His cervical imaging five months after discharge demonstrated anterior and vertical subluxation resulting in widening of the atlanto-dental interval and basilar invagination, respectively. Peri-vertebral inflammation early in our patient’s course may have caused damage to involved and cervical joints and laxity of atlanto-axial ligaments, resulting in unstable articulation. Non-traumatic atlanto-axial subluxation (also known as Grisel’s syndrome) has been previously described as sequelae with other head and neck infections including retropharyngeal abscess, otitis media, and upper respiratory infections [6], [7], [8], [9]. Skull base softening from infection could have also contributed to basilar invagination, akin to the pathophysiology of basilar invagination related to rheumatoid arthritis, osteogenesis imperfecta, or Paget disease [10]. Due to his relatively mild findings and neurologic stability, non-operative management was recommended for our patient. However, more severe cases of atlanto-axial instability and basilar invagination may require surgical intervention [7], [8], [10], [11]. The other four previously reported Lemirre syndrome cases with skull base osteomyelitis did not describe any such cranio-cervical joint sequelae as with our patient, although one case had an unspecified time to follow-up assessment. Since many published cases of Lemierre syndrome are limited in follow-up timeframe, long-term sequelae may be under-reported.

Conclusion

Skull base and cervical spinal osteomyelitis, cranial nerve palsies, and cranio-cervical junction instability are rare but related complications of Lemierre syndrome. The development of a CN VI and/or CN XII palsy with Lemierre syndrome should prompt clinicians to consider imaging studies to evaluate for evidence of skull base osteomyelitis, even if initial imaging studies were negative. When skull base osteomyelitis is identified as a complication of Lemierre syndrome, clinicians may consider adequate follow-up to monitor for long-term sequelae. Future studies of Lemierre syndrome may want to seek to better understand the clinical and epidemiologic factors that may lead to skull base osteomyelitis in Lemierre syndrome.
