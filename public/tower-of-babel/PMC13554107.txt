Evaluating 2D and 3D slope stability in a complex open-pit mine: a CAD-based numerical study
Authors: Mohammad-Taghi Hamzaban, Samaneh Kanoundoust, Roozbeh Geraili Mikola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate slope stability assessment is essential for the safe and economical operation of deep open-pit mines, especially in areas with complex geological and hydrological conditions. While two-dimensional (2D) analyses are still widely used in engineering practice, they struggle to accurately reflect true failure mechanisms in three-dimensional (3D) environments. This study offers a comprehensive comparison between 2D and 3D slope stability analyses specifically for the Sungun open-pit copper mine in Iran. A detailed 3D base model was developed using Rhinoceros and Griddle to accurately represent the pit geometry, geotechnical domains, and groundwater surface. This model facilitated the generation of a hexahedral mesh for FLAC3D simulations and allowed for the extraction of multiple 2D cross-sections for FLAC analyses in various directions. The 3D model predicted a rotational failure mechanism, yielding a global safety factor (FS3D) of 1.285, which was validated against field observations of tension cracks and active slope deformation. In contrast, the comparative 2D analyses produced varying results based on the orientation and location of the sections, with FS2D values ranging from 1.04 to 4.11. While some 2D sections approximated FS3D values reasonably, none accurately captured both the geometry and extent of the failure surface. Notably, sections aligned with the primary direction of displacement (North-South) yielded the most representative results. These findings underscore the limitations of relying solely on 2D analyses for pit slope design in geologically complex settings. The integration of 3D numerical modeling with CAD-based geometry management is crucial for achieving reliable slope stability assessments and for minimizing design uncertainties in large-scale mining projects.Keywords: 3D slope stability analysis, Open-pit mine geomechanics, CAD-based geotechnical modeling, FLAC3D numerical modeling, 2D versus 3D slope analysis comparisonSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Slope stability analyses are vital in civil and mining engineering, especially in large open-pit mines, where failures can lead to significant economic losses and safety hazards. The growing depth and scale of modern open-pit mines, driven by rising mineral demand, highlight the need for reliable stability assessment methods1. Despite the inherently three-dimensional (3D) nature of most natural and engineered slopes, stability analyses in engineering practice are still predominantly performed using two-dimensional (2D) plane strain assumptions2. While 2D analyses are computationally efficient and conceptually simple, numerous studies have demonstrated that they may not accurately represent the true failure mechanisms in slopes with complex geometries, heterogeneous geotechnical conditions, and spatially variable groundwater regimes3–5. In such cases, the identification of a representative “critical” cross-section is challenging, and reliance on an inappropriate section may lead either to overly conservative designs or unsafe stability assessments6–8.

Three-dimensional slope stability analyses provide a more realistic framework by explicitly accounting for slope geometry, out-of-plane effects, spatial variation of material properties, and pore-water pressure distributions9,10. A common approach to 3D slope stability analysis is to conduct 2D analyses along multiple cross-sections of the slope. This quasi-3D method estimates the 3D factor of safety (FS3D) as a weighted average of the 2D factors of safety (FS2D) from each section, using the effective area for weighting11.

Early efforts to extend limit equilibrium methods (LEM) to three dimensions aimed at generalizing classical 2D approaches12. While many 3D LEMs have been proposed, they often rely on extra assumptions for force and moment equilibrium and generally lack reliable methods for identifying critical 3D slip surfaces13. Limit equilibrium methods are further limited by their inability to capture stress-strain evolution, progressive failure, and displacement development14. To overcome these limitations, continuum-based numerical techniques such as the finite element (FEM) and finite difference (FDM) methods15–21, combined with the strength reduction method (SRM)22–25, have become increasingly popular. These approaches allow automatic identification of failure surfaces while explicitly simulating stress redistribution, deformation patterns, and groundwater effects26,27.

Finite element and finite difference methods combined with the strength reduction technique have demonstrated that, although FS3D often exceeds FS2D due to stabilizing end effects, 2D analyses may inaccurately represent the geometry and volume of the critical sliding mass68. Comparative studies further indicate that out-of-plane stress redistribution and spatial variability significantly influence failure mechanisms in large-scale excavations69. In mining applications, integrated 3D CAD-numerical workflows have improved the reliability of stability predictions by explicitly capturing bench geometry, geomechanical zoning, and groundwater effects70–72. These developments support the transition toward full 3D numerical modeling as a more robust framework for safety assessment in deep and geometrically complex open-pit slopes.

Recent studies highlight the increasing importance of 3D slope stability analysis for complex geotechnical problems. In mining applications, 3D limit equilibrium, finite element, and numerical methods have been used to better capture failure mechanisms and improve factor-of-safety estimates, while integration with monitoring data such as InSAR has enhanced risk evaluation73,74. In natural slopes, probabilistic 3D frameworks accounting for spatial variability of geotechnical and hydraulic properties have improved landslide prediction75. Other developments include coupling FLAC3D with geophysical surveys for realistic subsurface modeling76, rapid factor-of-safety calculation methods for 3D homogeneous slopes77, and GIS-integrated FLAC3D approaches for regional hazard assessment78. These advances demonstrate that modern 3D analyses increasingly emphasize realistic geometry, uncertainty quantification, monitoring integration, and computational efficiency.

Although 2D and 3D models are widely used, the advantages of full 3D simulations over conventional 2D sectional analyses for complex slope stability problems remain insufficiently defined. Beyond factor of safety values, reliable assessment also requires accurate prediction of failure mechanisms, sliding mass geometry, and instability location, which conventional numerical packages alone often cannot adequately visualize. This study compares 2D and 3D slope stability analyses for the Sungun open-pit copper mine using a CAD-integrated model of topography, geotechnical domains, and groundwater conditions. By evaluating safety factors and failure mechanisms from multiple 2D sections against full 3D numerical results, the study examines the reliability and limitations of conventional 2D approaches for complex open-pit slopes.

Problem definition

The Sungun mine, in northwestern Iran, is the country’s second-largest copper mine, with a vertical height difference of about 630 meters in its incomplete cone-shaped pit (Fig. 1). As mining progresses, deepening the pit may raise slope stability risks. To assess these risks, a three-dimensional numerical modeling of the entire pit wall was conducted, addressing the challenges of its complex geometry for 2D cross-sectional analyses.

Sungun open-pit mine.

The Sungun porphyry, primarily consisting of quartz-monzonite, is the main lithological unit in the Sungun copper mine, exhibiting various alterations. Six geotechnical domains have been outlined in the open-pit area based on studies of lithology, structural features, and alteration patterns, using data from 15 exploratory boreholes and seven geotechnical cross-sections (Fig. 2). The geomechanical properties for these domains are summarized in Table 2. The maximum groundwater level was estimated using data from 19 piezometers, and an interpolated surface was created for the CAD model. Saturated unit weight (ρsat) was applied below the water table, while dry unit weight (ρdry) was used above it. Laboratory tests and field investigations determined RMR79 and GSI80 values for the rock mass across various geotechnical sectors. These indices were used to derive the Hoek-Brown80 shear strength parameters. Groundwater corrections were applied to the RMR for saturated conditions, leading to adjustments in the Hoek-Brown parameters. Equivalent Mohr-Coulomb shear strength parameters were then calculated for both dry and saturated states in each sector (Table 2).

Base CAD model and numerical model

FLAC3D is a numerical simulation tool using the finite difference method (FDM) that excels in performance but struggles with modeling complex geometrical and geological features82. Creating a numerical mesh that accurately reflects the intricacies of large-scale projects in geologically complex environments remains a major technical challenge83. In contrast, Griddle, a plug-in for Rhinoceros 3D CAD modeling software, offers automatic mesh generation that achieves a good balance between performance and quality. This mesh can be easily imported into FLAC3D or 3DEC for simulations. Generating a suitable mesh for export involves four main steps (Fig. 3):

The FLAC3D model of the Sungun copper mine was developed using a topographic map provided by the mine’s design office (Fig. 4a). A square plan covering 2200 × 2200 m was generated with a vertical contour interval of 12.5 m, corresponding to the bench height, thereby encompassing the entire incomplete cone-shaped pit (Fig. 4b). Redundant contours were removed and additional levels were introduced to obtain 53 elevation intervals between the valley floor at the eastern boundary (0 m) and the highest pit elevation at the southwestern boundary (637.5 m). A grid consisting of 11 lines at 200 m spacing in both the X (East-West) and Y (North-South) directions was superimposed to extract representative 2D cross-sections for subsequent analyses. The processed topography was imported into Rhinoceros, where a volumetric model of the pit was constructed by connecting adjacent contours using surfaces and polysurfaces. The model also includes the maximum groundwater surface and vertical boundaries separating different geotechnical sectors (Fig. 2), forming the complete 3D geometry (Fig. 4c). A surface mesh was generated in Rhinoceros using the Griddle plug-in (Fig. 4d) and subsequently converted into a volumetric mesh for import into FLAC3D. The final model (Fig. 5) differentiates geotechnical sectors above and below the groundwater level, enabling assignment of the corresponding material properties (Table 2). The positive Y-direction corresponds to geographic north.

The numerical model in Fig. 5 consists of 2,306,402 predominantly hexahedral zones, with a maximum size of 12.5 m corresponding to the bench height. No minimum zone dimension was specified. A full mesh sensitivity study was not performed due to the model’s large scale and the high computational cost for each shear strength reduction analysis. However, the chosen maximum zone size offers adequate geometric resolution for the excavation scale, as the failure mechanisms relate to global slope instabilities rather than localized strain-softening, making the mesh density sufficient to capture overall failure behavior without significant mesh dependency.

Separate zone groups were defined for each geotechnical sector to assign Mohr-Coulomb material properties directly. The maximum groundwater level was incorporated by assigning different unit weights and shear strength parameters for materials above and below the groundwater surface (Table 2), representing dry and saturated conditions.

The lateral and bottom model boundaries were placed far enough from the slope faces to minimize boundary effects on stress redistribution and failure. Fixed velocity boundary conditions were applied in the X and Y directions at the lateral boundaries and in the Z direction at the bottom. The model was allowed to reach static equilibrium under self-weight to establish the initial stress state, without a predefined horizontal-to-vertical stress ratio (K0). This is appropriate as the pit geometry reflects an advanced excavation stage where the original in-situ stress field has been significantly modified, with gravity-induced stresses dominating the current slope mass stress regime.

Due to continuous excavation and significant material removal in the open-pit operation, analyses were performed under total stress conditions. Rapid unloading was assumed to limit effective stress redistribution, leading to short-term, undrained total stress evaluations. Slope stability was assessed using the shear strength reduction method (SRM), where shear strength parameters were progressively reduced until global failure was indicated by numerical non-convergence and the formation of a shear strain band. Numerical equilibrium at each reduction step was achieved when the maximum unbalanced force ratio met the FLAC3D convergence criterion.

Three-dimensional slope stability analysis

A 3D slope stability analysis of the Sungun copper mine using FLAC3D considered the maximum groundwater level and geomechanical properties for each sector. The simulation produced a safety factor of FS3D = 1.285, below the minimum threshold of 1.5 for final pit designs81. While the slope configuration is stable, the lower safety factor suggests that further increases in slope height or adverse geotechnical or hydrogeological changes could significantly impact stability.

The displacement magnitude contours (Fig. 6a) show a significant deformation zone along the southern pit wall, indicating a potential failure mass and suggesting a rotational failure mechanism. The highest displacement values (red zones) at the crest and toe further confirm this. The displacement vectors and deformed slope geometry (Fig. 7), magnified by a factor of 2, illustrate a curved slip surface primarily across the southern and partially southwestern pit sectors. The maximum shear strain rate contours (Fig. 6b) reveal shearing zones at the upper slope face and toe regions, likely linked to the initiation and propagation of failure. Their considerable extent suggests a deep-seated instability mechanism.

It should be noted that the comparison presented here does not constitute a full quantitative validation of the numerical model. Such validation would require comparison between measured field parameters and corresponding quantities predicted by the numerical simulations. Due to the dynamic nature of the mining slope and the absence of reliable monitoring or instrumentation data, such quantitative comparisons were not possible in the present study. Therefore, the numerical results were assessed through a qualitative consistency check against observed field evidence, including the location and characteristics of the documented instability features.

Stability analyses using 2D cross-sections

Although the previous section demonstrated the capability of 3D numerical simulations to capture realistic failure mechanisms and estimate safety factors in slopes with complex geometries, heterogeneous geotechnical units, and active groundwater conditions, their application in research and engineering practice remains limited. This is mainly due to two challenges: (1) the technical complexity and time required to generate a comprehensive 3D mesh that incorporates geometric, geotechnical, and hydrological features, and (2) the substantially higher computational cost compared with 2D analyses. Consequently, most slope stability assessments continue to rely on 2D cross-sectional simulations, as adopted in the initial design phase of the Sungun copper mine81. Given that the validated 3D model successfully reproduced the observed failure mechanism, this study further examines the extent to which 2D analyses along different cross-sectional orientations can replicate the 3D failure behavior. The aim is to assess the reliability and inherent limitations of 2D approaches for slopes characterized by geological and hydrogeological complexity, considering their widespread use due to simplicity and lower computational demand.

To evaluate the capability of 2D analyses to reproduce the failure mechanism obtained from the 3D model, three sets of cross-sections were considered, representing common approaches in slope stability assessment: (1) eleven equally spaced parallel sections oriented in the North-South direction, (2) eleven parallel sections oriented in the East-West direction, and (3) seven non-parallel sections aligned with the overall slope orientation in different parts of the mine, with variable spacing and orientation to better reflect local geometry and geotechnical zoning. For each set, 2D stability analyses were performed, and the average factor of safety and corresponding failure mechanisms were determined. The results were compared with the 3D simulation to evaluate the reliability and limitations of 2D approaches in representing the failure behavior of geometrically and hydrogeologically complex slopes, such as those at the Sungun copper mine. The base CAD model was used to extract the geometric features of each cross-section, including slope morphology, geotechnical unit boundaries, and groundwater levels. It also provided a reference framework for integrating and visualizing the 2D results, enabling reconstruction and comparison of the failure mechanisms identified by each set of sections.

Results of stability analyses using 2D north-south (NS) sections

The first series of 2D stability analyses was performed along the North-South (NS) cross-sections extracted from the CAD model (Fig. 11a). Eleven equally spaced sections were analyzed, and the results are presented in Fig. 13. The left-hand panels show the failure surfaces identified from maximum shear strain rate contours in the FLAC2D analyses, together with the corresponding FS2D values. The right-hand panels display 3D cut views from the FLAC3D model at the same locations and orientations. The apparent extension on the left side of the 3D views corresponds to the artificially extended southern boundary introduced to minimize boundary effects. In sections NS-1 and NS-2, the 3D model indicates no failure, consistent with the 2D results (FS2D = 2.22 and 2.01), confirming stable conditions. In NS-3, both models identify a slip surface. However, their geometries differ markedly. The 3D failure surface at this location is not aligned with a purely North–South kinematic direction, reflecting a significant East-West displacement component (Fig. 7). For sections NS-4 to NS-11, the 2D and 3D models generally agree on the location of failure zones, although differences persist in slip surface geometry and safety factors. This discrepancy shows that the 3D model identifies a broader unstable zone, underscoring the potential unconservatism of conventional 2D analyses in complex three-dimensional conditions.

The 2D slip surfaces are smoother and more uniform than the corresponding 3D cuts. Given that the 3D cut sections either show kinematically inadmissible slip surfaces or no failure at all, the EW sections do not adequately capture the true 3D failure mechanism. This suggests that the dominant displacement direction is not aligned with the East-West axis. This interpretation is consistent with Fig. 16b, where the sliding mass develops along the southern pit wall with predominantly south-to-north movement, and with Fig. 7, where displacement vectors are mainly oriented along the positive Y-axis (Northward direction).

Among the 2D models in the East-West (EW) direction (left-hand panels), section EW-1 is unstable (FS2D < 1.5), whereas EW-2 and EW-3 are stable (FS2D > 1.5). However, unlike the NS sections (Fig. 13), where 2D slip surfaces show reasonable agreement with the corresponding 3D cuts, the EW sections (Fig. 17) exhibit poor consistency. Many 2D slip surfaces are either kinematically incompatible with the 3D results or occur in locations where no slip is predicted in the 3D model. This indicates that, while NS sections mainly underestimate the spatial extent of failure, EW sections fail to capture even the correct location and mechanism of the sliding mass. Despite this, the averaged value FS2DEW remains close to both FS2DNS and FS3D.

In contrast, the slip surfaces obtained from the 2D analyses are kinematically admissible and, in a majority of sections (7 out of 11), yield FS2D values below the stability threshold. This highlights a critical issue: in complex slope conditions, 2D sections oriented unfavorably relative to the true failure direction may still predict instability, but with incorrect location and extent. Thus, 2D EW analyses may produce seemingly reasonable safety factors while misrepresenting the actual failure mechanism. Reliance on such sections can therefore be misleading, leading to incorrect predictions of failure geometry and kinematics. In practice, this may result in stabilization measures being applied to inappropriate locations, while the actual instability develops elsewhere. Additionally, consistent with Fig. 14, the 3D slip surface traces (green curves) exhibit more irregular geometries compared to the smoother, more idealized shapes obtained from the 2D analyses (red curves). This further emphasizes a fundamental limitation of 2D approaches: the tendency to oversimplify failure surface geometry, regardless of section orientation relative to the dominant failure direction.

Results of stability analyses using 2D non-parallel (NP) sections

To further evaluate pit stability using 2D analyses, a set of Non-Parallel (NP) sections was employed to construct FLAC2D models. As shown in Fig. 11c, seven approximately radial sections were drawn from a hypothetical central point, each oriented roughly perpendicular to the local slope benches. This layout better captures downslope displacements and provides a more rational estimate of both the safety factor and the associated slip surface geometry, particularly when full 3D analyses are unavailable. Unlike the NS and EW sections, the NP sections are irregularly spaced. Therefore, simple averaging of individual FS2D values would not yield a representative global safety factor. A weighted averaging approach was applied, with weights based on the effective area associated with each radial section.

As in any geomechanical analysis, precise determination of the geotechnical input parameters required for slope stability assessment is inherently difficult, and uncertainties in these inputs can lead to significant approximations in the calculated results. To evaluate the influence of such uncertainty, a sensitivity analysis of the shear strength parameters was conducted in two sets. In the first set, all parameters were kept constant while cohesion (c) was varied by ±10%. In the second set, the same procedure was applied to the friction angle (φ), which was varied by ±10%. Accordingly, each 3D and 2D model was reanalyzed four additional times, corresponding to increases and decreases in either c or φ. The resulting effects on the factor of safety (FS) and slip surface geometry were evaluated. Table 4 summarizes the sensitivity analysis results in terms of FS and sliding mass volume (V). For the 3D models, increases in shear strength parameters lead to higher FS3D values, whereas decreases reduce FS3D, as expected. Because the imposed variations in both sensitivity sets were symmetrical (±10%), the resulting FS3D values are also symmetrical about the original value, indicating good numerical consistency and reliability in the safety factor calculations. The results further show that FS3D is more sensitive to changes in friction angle than to cohesion, with the influence of friction angle being more than three times greater. Nevertheless, all variations in FS3D remain below 8%, suggesting that the 3D model provides robust safety factor estimates. A decrease in friction angle increases the sliding mass volume, whereas an increase reduces it, which is physically reasonable. However, the corresponding volume changes are not perfectly symmetrical about the base-case value. Similar asymmetry is also observed for cohesion variations, where higher cohesion values produce smaller sliding volumes. One possible reason is that these geometric evaluations were performed within the CAD environment, and require transferring data from the numerical models. In addition, reconstruction of the 3D slip surface involves curve and surface fitting, which may introduce minor geometric errors in CAD operations. Despite this, all percentage changes in sliding volume remain within approximately 5%, indicating that the CAD-based geometric interpretation of the numerical results is sufficiently accurate and acceptable for practical purposes.

Regarding the opposite trend observed in sliding mass variations during the cohesion sensitivity analysis, it is important to note that most 2D approaches show the same behavior (Table 4). This suggests that the trend is likely valid, and increasing cohesion may increase the factor of safety while also increasing the predicted sliding mass volume. The 2D cases that do not exhibit this behavior are discussed in the next paragraph.

Examination of the procedure used to reconstruct 3D slip surfaces from 2D section analyses explains the contradictory results observed in the above cases. As described previously, a pseudo-3D failure surface is generated by fitting a continuous surface through adjacent 2D slip curves whose FS2D values are below the allowable threshold (FS2Dall = 1.5). This criterion must be satisfied continuously across neighboring sections for them to be included in the reconstructed sliding mass. During sensitivity analyses, FS2D values change independently in each section. Consequently, at the margins of the reconstructed failure zone, even small parameter changes may shift a section’s FS2D above or below the threshold. This can lead to the inclusion of new sections or the exclusion of previously unstable ones. Figure 25 compares the sliding masses and participating sections for these cases with the reference model, showing that changes in the number of included sections are responsible for the contradictory and often substantial variations in FS and sliding volume reported in Table 4. In general, full 3D models respond to parameter changes in a continuous manner, with FS and sliding volume varying gradually as input properties are modified. In contrast, 2D-derived pseudo-3D approaches may respond discontinuously, because parameter changes can trigger the sudden addition or removal of entire sections from the reconstructed sliding mass. This may produce abrupt jumps in FS and/or sliding volume. From a practical perspective, these findings indicate that 3D analyses are more robust to uncertainty in input parameters than equivalent 2D-based approaches. Given the inherent uncertainty of geotechnical data, full 3D models are therefore more likely to provide stable and reliable predictions.

A limitation of the present sensitivity analysis is that only the shear strength parameters (cohesion and friction angle) were varied. Groundwater conditions were represented using the highest documented groundwater level available for the study area, corresponding to the most critical hydrogeological scenario for slope stability. Evaluation of groundwater-level uncertainty would require reconstruction of the hydrogeological model, remeshing of the numerical domains, and repetition of the complete 2D and 3D analyses. Although such an investigation was beyond the scope of the present study, future work should examine the influence of groundwater fluctuations on both safety factor estimates and failure mechanism development.

Discussion

Three-dimensional (3D) numerical modeling is highly effective in capturing slope failure mechanisms under complex geometric, geotechnical, and hydrogeological conditions, as demonstrated for the Sungun copper mine. When properly developed, such models provide reliable safety factor estimates and detailed insight into failure processes. However, their interpretation should be supported by field observations to ensure realistic validation of the results. Despite these advantages, implementing 3D modeling remains challenging. Constructing a representative model requires careful integration of geometric, geological, structural, and hydrogeological data, often involving auxiliary platforms such as CAD tools and meshing software (e.g., Griddle) for accurate geometry definition and discretization. In addition, 3D analyses are computationally demanding and time-intensive compared to 2D approaches. Consequently, many practical slope stability assessments in mining and civil engineering still rely on simplified 2D models based on plane-strain assumptions.

In slope stability analyses, employing multiple 2D sections across the study domain is a common practice for identifying potential failure mechanisms. A key challenge lies in selecting appropriate section orientations, as alignment strongly influences the ability to capture the governing failure mechanism and estimate a representative factor of safety. This issue becomes particularly critical in slopes with geometric, geological, and hydrogeological complexity and heterogeneity, where failure surfaces may develop along non-intuitive paths that are not readily captured by conventional 2D approaches. Figure 26a shows the sliding mass obtained from the 3D numerical model of the Sungun copper mine, alongside failure mechanisms derived from 2D analyses in three orientations: North-South (NS), East-West (EW), and Non-Parallel (NP) (Fig. 26b-d). Although FS values in Table 5 vary within a relatively narrow range, the corresponding sliding mass volumes differ significantly in the NS and NP approaches. Comparison of Figs. 26a and b shows that, despite a reasonable match in slip surface geometry, the NS-based sliding mass is 42.4% smaller than the 3D result due to its shallower and less extensive failure surface. In contrast, the NP sections produce a substantially larger failure mass, overestimating the volume by 47.4% relative to the 3D model. These discrepancies highlight the importance of considering sliding mass volume alongside FS values in slope stability assessments. Moreover, the apparent agreement between FS values and sliding mass volume for the EW sections does not imply reliability, as Fig. 26c shows that the predicted failure mechanism is shaped and located incorrectly.

Overall, these results emphasize the necessity of fully three-dimensional analyses for complex slopes. Even under identical geometric and material conditions, 2D analyses in different orientations can produce markedly different failure mechanisms, while yielding similar FS values or perhaps sliding volumes. Without a comprehensive 3D perspective, such results may lead to misleading interpretations.

Comparison of the 2D-derived failure mechanisms with the 3D reference (Fig. 26a) shows that the NS-oriented sections (Fig. 26b) provide the closest match to the actual failure. Although this approach underestimates the sliding mass volume by approximately 42%, it offers the most reliable prediction of the unstable zone and a reasonable FS value among the 2D alternatives. This is primarily because the NS sections are aligned with the dominant displacement direction observed in the 3D model. In practice, when 3D analyses are not available, identifying the most representative failure mechanism among different 2D interpretations (Figs 26b-d) becomes critical, as it directly influences the design and implementation of stabilization measures. In such cases, field observations and engineering judgment remain essential tools.

Field evidence (Fig. 8) indicates that instability is concentrated along the Southern wall of the Sungun pit. Therefore, the EW-based mechanism (Fig. 26c), which predicts failure in the Western and Northern slopes, can be excluded. Both NS (Fig. 26b) and NP (Fig. 26d) interpretations are consistent with Southern wall instability. However, the NP approach also suggests additional failure extending into the Western slope, which requires validation. As shown in Fig. 27, no significant deformation or progressive failure is observed in the Southwestern wall, indicating that instability is confined to the Southern slope. Consequently, the NP-based mechanism can also be disregarded. The NS-derived mechanism thus represents the most plausible interpretation of the actual failure process. This highlights the importance of integrating field observations with numerical analyses, even when advanced modeling techniques are used. While 3D models provide more comprehensive and accurate representations, they do not eliminate the need for field validation, though they reduce reliance on subjective engineering judgment.

It should also be noted that even when the correct section orientation is selected (NS), the reconstructed 2D failure surface remains shallower and less laterally extensive than the true 3D surface. As a result, designs based solely on 2D analyses may underestimate the required stabilization measures, potentially leading to unsafe and suboptimal solutions.

Table 5 highlights an important observation: although all 2D approaches estimate FS3D within a maximum deviation of approximately 13% from the true 3D value, the corresponding sliding mass volumes differ substantially. Even in the best case (NS sections), the estimated volume is about 42% smaller than the 3D result, while the NP approach shows even larger discrepancies. This demonstrates that relying solely on FS values can be misleading in geometrically complex slopes, as FS alone does not capture the spatial extent and volume of the unstable mass, key factors for understanding failure mechanisms and designing stabilization measures. Misestimation of failure geometry may lead to under- or over-designed support systems, affecting both safety and economic efficiency. Another critical aspect concerns the interpretation of FS in complex slope analyses. In conventional limit equilibrium methods, the mechanism with the lowest FS is typically considered the most critical. However, in numerically modeled slopes with complex geometry and heterogeneous conditions, FS alone is not a definitive indicator. Among the 2D-based results (Figs 26b-d), the NP approach yields the lowest FS (FS3DNP = 1.112), even lower than the full 3D result (FS3D = 1.285). This occurs because NP sections are locally aligned with slope directions, creating kinematically favorable conditions that yield lower FS values, regardless of whether those zones are actually susceptible to failure. Despite this, both 3D modeling and field observations contradict the NP-derived mechanism. In contrast, the NS-based mechanism, with a slightly higher FS (FS3DNS = 1.325), shows the best agreement with observed deformation and the 3D failure geometry. This emphasizes that FS must be interpreted alongside spatial consistency, kinematic feasibility, and field evidence. Relying on FS alone may lead to incorrect identification of the critical failure mechanism.

The practical implications of this study are significant for slope design and risk management in large open-pit mines. Although 2D analyses remain useful for preliminary screening and routine assessments, the results indicate that final design decisions in geometrically complex pits should rely on validated 3D modeling. In the Sungun case, some 2D sections produced safety factors close to the 3D result, yet substantially misrepresented the location, depth, and volume of the potential failure mass. Such discrepancies may lead to inappropriate slope angles, misplaced drainage systems, or under- or over-designed reinforcement measures. Therefore, integrating 3D numerical analysis with CAD-based geometry and field observations provides a more reliable basis for safe, economical, and site-specific slope optimization.

Finally, all analyses in this study were conducted within a unified three-dimensional CAD framework. Cross-sectional geometries, groundwater conditions, and geotechnical boundaries were extracted from the CAD model, and both 2D and 3D simulation results were reintegrated into the same environment for visualization and interpretation. This closed-loop workflow demonstrates that developing a detailed 3D CAD model at early stages is essential for complex slope stability problems. Such a model serves both as a spatial database for generating consistent input data in arbitrary orientations and as an integrated platform for interpreting results.

Conclusion

This study compares 2D and 3D slope stability analyses for the Sungun open-pit copper mine, characterized by complex geometry, heterogeneous geology, and variable groundwater conditions. Using a CAD-integrated workflow with FLAC platform, the results confirm the reliability of 3D modeling and the limitations of conventional 2D approaches. They also show that FS values alone are insufficient for evaluating analysis reliability, and that lower FS values do not necessarily indicate more realistic failure conditions in complex slopes. The geometry, volume, and field consistency of the predicted sliding mass are therefore essential evaluation criteria. In addition, the following findings were obtained:

The 3D numerical model, validated against field observations, predicted a rotational failure mechanism with a global safety factor of FS3D = 1.285, primarily affecting the southern pit wall. In contrast, the 2D analyses produced FS2D values ranging from 1.04 to 4.11, with substantial variations in both safety factors and failure surface geometry depending on section orientation and location.

This study concludes that, although 2D methods remain useful for preliminary assessments, reliable and safe slope design in large and complex mines requires 3D modeling supported by field validation and advanced visualization tools. Ongoing work is extending this framework to dynamic and probabilistic slope stability analyses to further demonstrate its broader applicability and effectiveness.
