Long-term benzodiazepine use is associated with poorer cognitive function in schizophrenia: findings from the SALT-C cohort
Authors: Caiping Liu, Lei Zhang, Qiyang Pan, Yue Shi, Wenjuan Yu, Guanjun Li, Huafang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between long-term use of benzodiazepines (BDZs) and cognitive function in patients with schizophrenia has not been fully characterized. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between long-term BDZs use and cognitive function in schizophrenia.MethodsData were derived from an observational study of treatment with atypical antipsychotics in Chinese patients with schizophrenia (SALT-C). Fifty-seven patients with long-term use of BDZs (≥60 days) were included, and 57 BDZs non-users were matched using propensity scores for age, sex, disease duration, and atypical antipsychotics received. Cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA); psychotic symptoms, illness severity, and psychosocial functioning were assessed with the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), the Clinical Global Impression-Severity (CGI-S) scale, and the Personal and Social Performance (PSP) scale. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between benzodiazepine dose and MoCA total scores among BDZ users.Factors associated with MoCA total scores were examined using multiple linear regression models.ResultsThe long-term BDZs user group showed a significantly lower total MoCA score [16.02 (6.69)] than the BDZs non-user group [19.33 (7.11)] (FDR-adjust p = 0.048). No significant between-group differences were observed in total scores on the PANSS, CGI-S, or PSP (all p > 0.05). No significant association was observed between benzodiazepines dose and MoCA scores (r = −0.098, p = 0.466). Multiple linear regression revealed that long-term BDZs use (β = −0.182, p = 0.008), PANSS total score (β = −0.217, p = 0.003) and anticholinergic burden (β = −0.199, p = 0.008) were independently associated with poorer cognitive performance, whereas years of education showed a positive association with cognitive performance (β = 0.460, p < 0.001).ConclusionsLong-term BDZs use is associated with poorer cognitive performance in patients with schizophrenia, suggesting that cognitive function should be monitored and prolonged treatment avoided.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment is recognized as a core feature of schizophrenia, including deficits in working memory, executive function, attention, and processing speed, affecting approximately 75–80% of patients to varying degrees [1–4]. Given that these deficits are linked to impaired social functioning in domains such as community integration, occupational competence, and daily skill acquisition, cognitive dysfunction has been widely recognized as a major determinant of long-term outcome in schizophrenia [5–9].

The pathogenesis of cognitive impairment is complex. Current evidence implicates a combination of genetic vulnerability, cortical excitation-inhibition (E/I) imbalance, and dysregulation of neurotransmitters involving dopamine, acetylcholine, glutamate, and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [10]. Several studies have indicated that pharmacological treatment also plays a crucial role in the progression of cognitive impairment observed in schizophrenia [11, 12]. Given the role of GABAergic dysfunction in cognitive processes, medications targeting the GABA system, particularly benzodiazepines (BDZs), may have clinically relevant effects on cognition in schizophrenia.

The cognitive benefits of currently available psychotropic medications are limited [13]. Moreover, some treatments, such as conventional antipsychotics, may worsen cognitive impairment in schizophrenia [14]. Furthermore, the medication-induced anticholinergic burden commonly observed in schizophrenia, which is inversely associated with performance across all cognitive domains, may also represent an important confounding factor in pharmacological studies [15, 16].

Meanwhile, there is growing concern about the effects of BDZs on cognitive function in schizophrenia. BDZs represent one of the most frequently used adjunctive pharmacotherapies in patients with schizophrenia, and are widely used for various therapeutic purposes, including the management of anxiety and sleep disturbances, the mitigation of antipsychotic side effects, and rapid tranquilization [17]. Clinical treatment guidelines recommend that benzodiazepines use be restricted to a maximum period of 2 to 4 weeks [18]. Nevertheless, 22% of participants in the CATIE trial received concomitant anxiolytic treatment, predominantly BDZs [19]. Notably, another study demonstrated that once initiated, long-term use was highly prevalent, with cumulative treatment duration exceeding one year in up to 62.9% of schizophrenia patients [20].

Although BDZs are considered safe and effective for short-term use, their potential for long-term cognitive impact remains a concern. In non-psychotic patients, long-term BDZs use is often associated with broad cognitive deficits [21]. A recent meta-analysis has confirmed this association, although the severity of impairment and the specific cognitive domains involved vary considerably [22]. The underlying mechanisms for BDZ‑induced cognitive impairment may involve the acute enhancement of GABAA receptor-mediated inhibitory neurotransmission [23]. This disruption of hippocampal and prefrontal cortical function can lead to deficits in working memory, executive function, and attention. Additionally, long‑term BDZs use may promote neuroplastic changes or receptor downregulation, further exacerbating cognitive decline [24, 25]. However, it remains uncertain whether these mechanisms operate similarly in schizophrenia, given baseline cognitive impairment and altered GABAergic signaling. Evidence from the general population is also inconclusive, as a systematic review did not demonstrate an increased risk of cognitive decline associated with long-term BDZs use [26].

The cognitive effects of BDZs in schizophrenia remain debated. A cross-sectional study reported an association between BDZs use and poorer cognitive and social function in older adults (≥55 years) with schizophrenia, along with increased hospitalization duration and readmission rates [27]. Research on community-dwelling stabilized patients has shown that daily use of BDZs is associated with reduced working memory and attentional performance [28]. In contrast, a previous study had found no significant association between benzodiazepine use and cognitive function [29]. Moreover, research suggests that cognitive deficits associated with BDZs may be at least partially reversible. In a Japanese cohort of patients with schizophrenia, significant improvement in verbal memory was observed following gradual BDZs discontinuation [30]. These conflicting findings may be attributed to variations in study design, BDZs dosage, exposure duration, or the cognitive domains assessed. Thus, the real-world association between long-term BDZs use and cognitive function in schizophrenia has yet to be clearly characterized.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between long-term BDZs use and cognitive function in patients with schizophrenia using real-world data. Propensity score matching was applied to balance key clinical characteristics, allowing for a more robust assessment of the cognitive impact of long-term BDZs use in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Participants

The data for this study were obtained from the SALT-C study, an observational study on safety and related factors of treatment with atypical antipsychotics in Chinese patients with schizophrenia [31]. Participants were recruited from the Shanghai Mental Health Center between 2016 and 2019. All participants were outpatients or inpatients aged 18 years or older with a diagnosis of schizophrenia according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV), confirmed independently by at least two attending psychiatrists. All participants were either receiving or planning to initiate atypical antipsychotics (AAPs) treatment. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Mental Health Center (2010–35). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.Based on this cohort, this analysis aimed to evaluate the association between long-term BDZs use and cognitive function. Participants were eligible for the long-term BDZs user group if they had been using BDZs at a stable dosage for at least 60 consecutive days prior to enrollment. Participants were eligible for the BDZs non-user group if they had no prior history of BDZs use.The BDZs used in this study included all clinically available agents, such as alprazolam, lorazepam, diazepam, estazolam, and oxazepam.Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: presence of comorbid psychiatric disorders, including dementia, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, or substance-induced psychotic disorders; a history of major neurological diseases such as cerebrovascular disease, epilepsy, Parkinson’s disease, or traumatic brain injury; or were not free from cognitive impairment secondary to such neurological conditions. Additional exclusion criteria involved severe physical illnesses, a history of substance abuse or dependence (except nicotine) within the preceding 3 months, current use of nootropics, or having received electroconvulsive therapy or modified electroconvulsive therapy within the preceding 6 months. Furthermore, patients were excluded if they exhibited severe acute psychiatric symptoms interfering with participation, possessed intellectual disabilities or cognitive impairments that would preclude the completion of cognitive assessments, were currently enrolled in another clinical study, or were unable or unwilling to provide informed consent.

Procedures

Demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, gender, years of education, episode and duration of illness. Detailed medication information was recorded for all prescribed AAPs, with dosages converted to chlorpromazine equivalents [32]. All dosages were converted into diazepam equivalents. For reference, the following dosages were considered equivalent to 10 mg of diazepam: 1 mg of lorazepam, 0.5 mg of alprazolam, 20 mg of oxazepam, 0.5 mg of clonazepam, and 1 mg of estazolam. In cases where participants were prescribed multiple BDZs, the diazepam equivalents for each medication were summed to determine the total cumulative dose [33]. Information on concomitant medications, such as potent anticholinergic agents(like trihexyphenidyl) was also documented. Anticholinergic burden was estimated using the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) scale [16]. The cumulative anticholinergic burden was calculated by summing the scores of regularly prescribed medications, such as atypical antipsychotics and anticholinergic agents like trihexyphenidyl. Each medication was quantified according to the ACB scale, and a total score exceeding 3 was categorized as high anticholinergic burden. Other medication classes, including antidepressants and mood stabilizers, were not systematically included in the ACB calculation. Information on BDZs was recorded, including the specific type, initial prescription date, and duration of use. Long-term use was defined as continuous use exceeding 60 days.

Cognitive function was evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), a brief screening tool with established validity for identifying cognitive deficits among patients with schizophrenia [34, 35]. The MoCA assesses multiple cognitive domains, including attention and concentration, executive function, memory, language, visuospatial ability, abstract thinking, and orientation. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance. For the purposes of this analysis, the tool was utilized as an indicator of global cognitive performance.

Psychopathology was assessed using the total score and the positive, negative, and general psychopathology subscale scores of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) [36]. Illness severity and psychosocial functioning were evaluated using the Clinical Global Impression-Severity (CGI-S) scale and the Personal and Social Performance (PSP) scale [37, 38].

All psychological assessments were conducted by trained psychiatrists, having completed standardized training for each scale and demonstrated inter-rater reliability prior to data collection.

Statistical analysis

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to reduce selection bias between the study groups. A 1:1 matching protocol was applied based on age, gender, illness duration, and type of AAPs.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables following normal distribution were expressed as mean and standard deviation [M (SD)]; non-normally distributed data were expressed as median and interquartile range [M (P25 - P75)]. Categorical and ordinal variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Spearman correlation analyses between benzodiazepine dose (diazepam equivalents) and cognitive performance (MoCA scores) were conducted among participants receiving benzodiazepines. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent factors associated with cognitive impairment measured by MoCA scores. Independent variables include age, gender, long-term BDZs use, years of education, illness duration, PANSS total score, ACB scores, and chlorpromazine equivalent doses. The variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance were employed to evaluate multicollinearity among the independent variables.

Comparison of cognitive function between groups

The mean total MoCA score was significantly lower in the long-term BDZs user group [16.02 (6.69)] compared with the BDZs non-user group [19.33 (7.11); p = 0.012, t = 2.566]. Both groups scored below the cutoff of 26, indicating the presence of overall cognitive impairment [39].

Analysis of MoCA subdomains showed significantly poorer performance in the long-term BDZs user group compared with the BDZs non-user group on visuospatial/executive function (p = 0.012, Z = −2.501), naming (p = 0.025, Z = −2.236), language (p = 0.032, Z = −2.139), and abstraction (p = 0.032, Z = −2.142). Discussion

This real-world study suggests that long-term BDZs use is associated with poorer overall cognitive performance on the MoCA in patients with schizophrenia. This association remained significant in multivariable analyses, with long-term BDZs use, ACB, and PANSS total score independently showing negative associations with cognition, while years of education were positively associated. Notably, no clear dose–response relationship was observed.

Patients with long-term BDZs use had lower total MoCA scores, with more pronounced deficits observed in naming, language, abstraction, and visuospatial/executive subdomains compared with non-users. After correcting for multiple comparisons, only the MoCA total score and the visuospatial/executive domain remained statistically significant. Similar associations have been reported in non-psychiatric populations [40]. This finding is consistent with the multicenter FACE-SZ study, which reported that among 407 clinically stable patients with schizophrenia, long-term BDZs use was significantly associated with impairments in verbal memory and working memory [28]. Previous research has also reported lower scores on the Global Assessment of Functioning–Cognition in Schizophrenia (GAF-CogS) scale in patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders receiving long-term BDZ therapy [41]. A Finnish study further demonstrated that long-term BDZs use was associated with poorer reaction time and visual learning performance assessed using two subtests from the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB) [42]. Consistent with these observations, a Danish study in outpatients with schizophrenia noted that the gradual discontinuation of long-term BDZs was followed by an improvement in executive function relative to baseline [43]. Collectively,despite methodological differences, impairments across multiple cognitive domains associated with BDZs use have been identified, particularly in executive function.

The use of the MoCA in this study reflects a pragmatic balance between clinical feasibility and the need for standardized cognitive assessment. While primarily a screening tool, the MoCA has demonstrated robust utility in detecting cognitive impairment and providing a reliable index of global cognitive performance in individuals with schizophrenia [39, 44, 45]. Comparative studies with the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) provide additional support for the validity of the MoCA in this population, showing acceptable concordance between the two instruments [35, 46, 47]. Moreover, the cognitive domains assessed by the MoCA show substantial structural overlap with those evaluated by the MCCB [34]. A cutoff score of 26 has been proposed to indicate cognitive impairment [39], prompting a careful consideration of potential floor effects that might have limited the sensitivity of the MoCA to detect subtle between-group differences, as mean scores in both groups fell below this threshold. Nevertheless, sufficient variability in MoCA performance was maintained within the study population to allow for meaningful statistical comparisons. The observed group differences and significant associations identified in multivariable analyses further demonstrate that the MoCA retained adequate discriminatory capacity in this sample. To mitigate potential information loss, MoCA scores were analyzed as continuous variables; accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting differences in global cognitive performance within an already impaired population.

In the present study, the average BDZs dose corresponded to approximately 16 mg/day of diazepam equivalents, a level generally categorized as a moderate dose. Notably, no significant dose-response relationship was observed within the BDZs user group. In a longitudinal study of community-dwelling older adults, cognitive decline was associated with both BDZ dosage and duration, though the impact of duration was more pronounced [48]. These findings raise the possibility that cognitive impairment might be more strongly influenced by cumulative or prolonged exposure, although this remains speculative. Consequently, the potential for clinically relevant cognitive effects associated with even moderate-dose BDZ treatment, when maintained over extended periods, warrants further investigation.

Notably, no significant between-group differences in clinical symptom severity or social functioning were observed in the present sample, despite prior reports suggesting that long-term BDZs users may exhibit more severe psychopathology [41]. Nevertheless, multivariable analyses revealed that several clinical variables were independently associated with cognitive performance. Higher PANSS total scores were linked to poorer cognitive outcomes, aligning with existing evidence that symptom severity is inversely correlated with cognitive function in schizophrenia [49]. ACB also showed a negative association with cognition, supporting research that cumulative anticholinergic exposure adversely affects multiple domains such as visuospatial ability and executive function [50, 51]. Conversely, years of education were positively associated with cognitive performance, aligning with the cognitive reserve hypothesis [52]. Collectively, these results underscore that cognitive impairment in schizophrenia is multifactorial, involving both illness-related and treatment-related factors.

This study has several methodological strengths. To enhance group comparability and mitigate confounding factors inherent in observational research, propensity score matching was used to balance baseline characteristics [53]. Furthermore, multivariable regression models were utilized to adjust for potential confounders, including AAPs, ACB, and clinical status (via PANSS scores). These rigorous analytical strategies reinforce the observed associations; however, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be entirely excluded.

Among all initially recruited patients prescribed BDZs, approximately two-thirds were identified as long-term users, with a mean duration of 31 months. Although the overall rate of BDZs co-prescription in the baseline dataset was lower than that reported in some previous studies, the proportion of long-term users was relatively high. This observation might be partially attributable to real-world prescribing practices, as suggested in previous study, whereby BDZ treatment, once initiated, tends to be maintained over extended periods in certain clinical settings [20]. Similar findings have been reported, with up to 62.9% of patients with schizophrenia exposed to BDZs for more than one year [54]. This interpretation remains exploratory and needs to be evaluated in longitudinal studies focusing on long-term BDZs prescribing practices and related clinical factors.

Definitions of long-term use vary across studies, ranging from several months to years; the most common thresholds are continuous use for 3–12 months [24, 28, 41, 55–58]. In the present study, long-term use was defined as continuous exposure for more than two months. This operational definition was selected based on clinical practice considerations and data availability in a real-world setting.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the evaluation of longitudinal changes; cognitive function was assessed at a single time point using the MoCA, which may limit the precision of domain-specific assessments compared to more comprehensive batteries like the MCCB. Second, although propensity score matching and multivariable regression were employed, the possibility of confounding by indication cannot be entirely excluded. BDZs are frequently prescribed for insomnia, anxiety and other clinical symptoms that are often inherently associated with cognitive impairment; the absence of detailed assessments for these symptons remains a source of potential residual confounding. Third, while we adjusted for major contributors to the anticholinergic burden, such as antipsychotics, the load from other medication classes (e.g., antidepressants or mood stabilizers) was not comprehensively analyzed, though their impact is typically considered less pronounced. Fourth, the operational definition of long-term use as exceeding two months was selected based on real-world data availability, but this threshold differs from the 3–12 month criteria used in some literature, necessitating caution in cross-study comparisons. Fifth, the sample showed moderate overall symptom severity on the PANSS, which may limit generalizability to to either preserved or more severe populations.Finally, as the majority of the sample consisted of long-term hospitalized patients, the findings may not be fully generalizable to outpatients or those in earlier stages of the disorder. Future multicenter, longitudinal studies are required to further elucidate the complex interplay between BDZ exposure, dosage, and cognitive trajectories in schizophrenia.

The findings of the present study may have clinical implications for the use of BDZs in patients with schizophrenia. Clinicians may consider paying attention to treatment duration and cognitive status during BDZ therapy, while carefully weighing potential anxiolytic or sedative benefits against possible associations with cognitive impairment. Decisions regarding dose reduction, discontinuation, or the use of alternative interventions should be individualized and supported by further empirical evidence.

Conclusions

Long-term use of BDZs was associated with poorer global cognitive performance among patients with schizophrenia, independent of established determinants such as illness severity and anticholinergic burden. These findings underscore the need for periodic cognitive monitoring during extended treatment in this population. Further longitudinal and controlled trials are required to fully elucidate the impact of cumulative use of BDZs on long-term cognitive outcomes.
