Cubic maps from the group of order 3 3
Vadim Alekseev, Andreas Thom

Abstract
. The purpose of this note is to classify unital cubic maps from the cyclic group of order 3 3 into an arbitrary non-abelian group. We show that the universal group admitting a unital cubic map from the cyclic group of order 3 3 is infinite, give a concrete presentation and provide an infinite representation of it in PSL 3 ​ ( ℂ ) {\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{C}) , whose image is an arithmetic lattice commensurable with PSL 3 ​ ( ℤ ​ [ ω ] ) {\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega]) , where ω \omega is a primitive cube root of unity. As a consequence we obtain the existence of finite nilpotent groups of arbitrarily large nilpotency class admitting a unital cubic map from C 3 C_{3} whose image generates the group.



Introduction and basic definitions

The study of polynomial maps between groups was initiated by A. Leibman,
[
Lei98
,
Lei02
]
, motivated by applications in ergodic theory and additive combinatorics. Let
G,H
be groups and let
\varphi:G\to H
be a map.
For
k\in G
we define the
finite difference
(or discrete derivative in direction
k
) by

(1)

(\Delta_{k}\varphi)(g):=\varphi(kg)\varphi(g)^{-1},\qquad g\in G.

Following
[
Lei98
]
, we define polynomial maps by iterating finite differences.
We say that
\varphi
is
polynomial of degree
-1
if it is identically
1
.
For
d\geq-1
, we say that
\varphi
is
polynomial of degree
d+1
if, for every
k\in G
,
the map
\Delta_{k}\varphi:G\to H
is polynomial of degree
d
.
In particular, polynomial maps of degree
0
are constant, and polynomial maps of degree
1

are products of a homomorphism and a constant (cf.
[
JT24
, Lemma 2.1]
).
We call
\varphi

unital
if
\varphi(1)=1
. Throughout, we will assume that all polynomial maps are unital, since any polynomial map can be unitalized by multiplying with a suitable constant. We will refer to polynomial maps of degree at most
2
as
quadratic
and of degree at most
3
as
cubic
.

In
[
JT24
]
the authors introduced a new approach to polynomial maps between non-abelian groups, which allows for a more explicit description of the structure of quadratic maps. Given a group
G
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there is always a universal group
{\rm Pol}_{k}(G)
which admits a universal polynomial map
\varphi\colon G\to{\rm Pol}_{k}(G)
of degree
k
; any polynomial map of degree
k
from
G
to
H
arises as the composition of
\varphi
and a uniquely determined homomorphism from
{\rm Pol}_{k}(G)
to
H
. In the case of quadratic maps, the structure of the universal group can be described in concrete terms, while the structure of these groups for
k\geq 3
is still somewhat mysterious even in the simplest cases. Apart from
G=C_{2}
, where
{\rm Pol}_{k}(C_{2})
is isomorphic to
C_{2^{k}}
for all
k\geq 0
, and perfect groups, see
[
JT24
, Corollary 1.5]
, where the natural map
{\rm Pol}_{k}(G)\to G
is an isomorphism for all
k\geq 1
, there is not a single computation of
{\rm Pol}_{k}(G)
in the literature for
k\geq 3
.

In this note we apply the results from
[
JT24
]
to classify cubic maps from the cyclic group of order
3
. While this is of course a natural next question, the computation is somewhat non-trivial and the result unexpected. Throughout this paper we let
C_{3}=\langle\sigma\mid\sigma^{3}=1\rangle
and
\tau:=\sigma^{2}
. As mentioned above, the group
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
is the universal group admitting a unital cubic map
\varphi\colon C_{3}\to{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
, i.e. any unital cubic map
\alpha\colon C_{3}\to G
to an arbitrary group
G
factors through a (unique) homomorphism
\bar{\alpha}\colon{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})\to G
, i.e.
\alpha=\bar{\alpha}\circ\varphi
.
Existence of the universal group
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
follows easily from the fact that the category of groups admits arbitrary products, and a first description in terms of generators and relations is similar to the one for
{\rm Pol}_{2}(G)
, see also
[
JT24
, Remark 2.5]
.

The following is our first main result:

Theorem 1
.

The group
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
is isomorphic to the group

\langle a,b\mid(ba)^{3},(ab^{-1}a)^{3},[ba,ab^{-1}a]\rangle,

where
a=\varphi(\sigma),b=\varphi(\tau)
for the universal unital cubic map
\varphi\colon C_{3}\to{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
.

The elements
a,b
have infinite order and the group
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
contains a non-abelian free subgroup.

While the first claim is a matter of direct computation, the second claim is more subtle and relies on a computer-assisted search for an infinite representation of
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
in
{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
. As a result, the unital map
\varphi\colon C_{3}\to{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
given by

\varphi(\sigma)=\begin{pmatrix}\omega r^{2}&\frac{-2\omega-1}{3}r^{2}&\frac{-\omega-2}{3}r^{2}\\
-r^{2}&-\omega r^{2}&\frac{-2\omega-1}{3}r^{2}\\
-(\omega+1)r^{2}&r^{2}&r^{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\varphi(\tau)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\frac{\omega+2}{3}r\\
r&0&(\omega+1)r\\
0&r&\omega r\end{pmatrix}

with
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
a primitive cube root of unity and
r
a root of
r^{3}=1-\omega
, is cubic and leads to a representation of
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
in
{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
, whose image is an arithmetic lattice commensurable with
{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega])
. This arithmetic lattice can be described explicitly, see Theorem
2
, and the remaining claims in Theorem
1
follow from direct computation in this representation. There is another representation of
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
in
{\rm SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}((u)))
with arithmetic image, which is described in Theorem
3
.

For a unital cubic map
\varphi:G\to H
, each first difference
\Delta_{k}\varphi
is polynomial of degree
2
.
We will unitalize these first differences as follows.
For
k\in G
define

(2)

\beta_{k}(g):=\varphi(k)^{-1}(\Delta_{k}\varphi)(g)=\varphi(k)^{-1}\varphi(kg)\varphi(g)^{-1},\qquad g\in G.

Let
\varphi\colon C_{3}\to G
be a unital cubic map. As a consequence
\beta_{\sigma}
and
\beta_{\tau}
are unital quadratic maps from
C_{3}
to
G
and can be further analyzed using the results from
[
JT24
]
. Section
1
contains an explicit description of the structure of
\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})
. This is then used in Section
2
, which is devoted to the classification of cubic maps from
C_{3}
and the proof of Theorem
1
.

Finally, Section
3
contains a description of the arithmetic lattice in the locally compact group
{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
arising as the image of
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
under the representation described above. This part makes use of Jambor’s
L_{3}
–
U_{3}
-quotient algorithm
[
Jam12
]
and relies on computer-assisted searches and computations which we verified independently using Magma, see the accompanying code.

1.
Quadratic maps and
\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})

The following proposition corrects an erroneous claim in
[
JT24
, Remark 4.5]
, where the group
\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})
was claimed to be isomorphic to the Heisenberg group with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
, see Remark
1
.

Proposition 1
.

We have

(3)

\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})\;\cong\;\Big\langle a,b\ \Big|\ a^{9}=1,\ b^{9}=1,\ bab^{-1}=a^{4},\ aba^{-1}=b^{4}\Big\rangle,

where
a=\phi(\sigma)
and
b=\phi(\tau)
for the universal unital quadratic map

\phi:C_{3}\to\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})
. The group
\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})
has order
27
and is isomorphic to
C_{9}\rtimes C_{3}
, where the generator of
C_{3}
acts with multiplication by
4
on
C_{9}
.

Proof.

By
[
JT24
, Theorem 1.3]
, there is an isomorphism

\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})\;\cong\;(\omega(C_{3})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}C_{3})\rtimes_{\psi}C_{3},

where
C_{3}
acts on
\omega(C_{3})
by left multiplication, acts trivially on
C_{3}=\mathrm{ab}(C_{3})
,
the multiplication is

(\xi,g)(\eta,h)=(\xi+g\eta+\psi(g,h),gh),

and
\psi(g,h)=c(g)\otimes\bar{h}
with
c(g)=g-1

[
JT24
, Theorem 1.3 and its proof]
. Moreover the universal map is

\phi(g)=(0,g)\qquad(g\in C_{3})

[
JT24
, proof of Theorem 1.3]
.

Set
V:=\omega(C_{3})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}C_{3}
(written additively).
Since
\omega(C_{3})
is free abelian of rank
2
with basis

\alpha:=\sigma-1
and
\beta:=\tau-1
, we have
V\cong(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^{2}
with basis

e_{1}:=\alpha\otimes\bar{\sigma},\qquad e_{2}:=\beta\otimes\bar{\sigma}.

In particular,
|V|=9
, hence
|\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})|=|V||C_{3}|=27
.

Write
a:=\phi(\sigma)=(0,\sigma)
and
b:=\phi(\tau)=(0,\tau)
.
Using
\psi(g,h)=c(g)\otimes\bar{h}
and
\bar{\tau}=2\bar{\sigma}
, we record

\psi(\sigma,\sigma)=e_{1},\quad\psi(\tau,\sigma)=e_{2},\quad\psi(\sigma,\tau)=2e_{1},\quad\psi(\tau,\tau)=2e_{2}.

Then

\displaystyle a^{2}

\displaystyle=(0,\sigma)^{2}=(\psi(\sigma,\sigma),\tau)=(e_{1},\tau),

\displaystyle a^{3}

\displaystyle=(e_{1},\tau)(0,\sigma)=(e_{1}+\psi(\tau,\sigma),1)=(e_{1}+e_{2},1),

so
a^{9}=1
. Similarly,

\displaystyle b^{2}

\displaystyle=(0,\tau)^{2}=(\psi(\tau,\tau),\sigma)=(2e_{2},\sigma),

\displaystyle b^{3}

\displaystyle=(2e_{2},\sigma)(0,\tau)=(2e_{2}+\psi(\sigma,\tau),1)=(2e_{1}+2e_{2},1),

so
b^{9}=1
.

For inverses we use
(0,g)^{-1}=(-\psi(g^{-1},g),g^{-1})

[
JT24
, proof of Theorem 1.3]
.
Thus

\displaystyle b^{-1}

\displaystyle=(-\psi(\sigma,\tau),\sigma)=(-2e_{1},\sigma)=(e_{1},\sigma),

\displaystyle a^{-1}

\displaystyle=(-\psi(\tau,\sigma),\tau)=(-e_{2},\tau)=(2e_{2},\tau).

Now compute

\displaystyle bab^{-1}

\displaystyle=(0,\tau)(0,\sigma)(e_{1},\sigma)

\displaystyle=(\psi(\tau,\sigma),1)(e_{1},\sigma)

\displaystyle=(e_{2},1)(e_{1},\sigma)

\displaystyle=(e_{1}+e_{2},\sigma),

while

a^{4}=a^{3}a=(e_{1}+e_{2},1)(0,\sigma)=(e_{1}+e_{2},\sigma),

hence
bab^{-1}=a^{4}
. Likewise,

\displaystyle aba^{-1}

\displaystyle=(0,\sigma)(0,\tau)(2e_{2},\tau)

\displaystyle=(\psi(\sigma,\tau),1)(2e_{2},\tau)

\displaystyle=(2e_{1}+2e_{2},\tau)=b^{4}.

So (
3
) holds in
\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})
.

Let
\Gamma
be the group given by the presentation (
3
).
Since
\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})
is generated by
\phi(\sigma)
and
\phi(\tau)

(by construction of
\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(G)
on generators
\phi(g)

[
JT24
, §2, proof of Theorem 1.2]
),
the assignment
a\mapsto\phi(\sigma)
,
b\mapsto\phi(\tau)
defines a surjective homomorphism

\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})
.

It remains to bound
|\Gamma|
.
From
bab^{-1}=a^{4}
and
a^{9}=1
we get

ba^{3}b^{-1}=(bab^{-1})^{3}=(a^{4})^{3}=a^{12}=a^{3},

so
\langle a^{3}\rangle
is central in
\Gamma
and has size
\leq 3
.
In the quotient
\Gamma/\langle a^{3}\rangle
the relation
bab^{-1}=a^{4}
becomes
bab^{-1}=a
,
so
a
and
b
commute there; moreover
(\bar{a})^{3}=(\bar{b})^{3}=1
because
a^{9}=b^{9}=1
.
Hence
|\Gamma/\langle a^{3}\rangle|\leq 3^{2}=9
, so
|\Gamma|\leq 27
.

Since
\Gamma
surjects onto
\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})
and both have order
27
, the map is an isomorphism.
∎

Remark 1
.

Note that while the Heisenberg group with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
is also a non-abelian
3
-group of order
27
, it is not isomorphic to
\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})
, since the former has exponent
3
while the latter has exponent
9
. In standard notation, the Heisenberg group with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
is the extraspecial group
3^{1+2}_{+}
, while
\mathrm{Pol}_{2}(C_{3})
is the extraspecial group
3^{1+2}_{-}
. These are the only non-abelian groups of order
27
.

2.
Classification of cubic maps from
C_{3}

We will now apply the results of the previous section to classify cubic maps on
C_{3}
and to prove Theorem
1
.
Let
\varphi\colon C_{3}\to G
be a unital cubic map into an arbitrary group
G
. We set
a:=\varphi(\sigma)
and
b:=\varphi(\tau)
. Then

\beta_{\sigma}(h):=\varphi(\sigma)^{-1}\varphi(\sigma h)\varphi(h)^{-1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\beta_{\tau}(h):=\varphi(\tau)^{-1}\varphi(\tau h)\varphi(h)^{-1}

are unital quadratic maps from
C_{3}
to
G
. We compute those maps explicitly in terms of
a,b
as follows:

\displaystyle\beta_{\sigma}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=a^{-1}\varphi(\sigma^{2})\varphi(\sigma)^{-1}=a^{-1}ba^{-1},

\displaystyle\beta_{\sigma}(\tau)

\displaystyle=a^{-1}\varphi(\sigma\tau)\varphi(\tau)^{-1}=a^{-1}\varphi(1)\,b^{-1}=a^{-1}b^{-1},

\displaystyle\beta_{\tau}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=b^{-1}\varphi(\tau\sigma)\varphi(\sigma)^{-1}=b^{-1}\varphi(1)\,a^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1},

\displaystyle\beta_{\tau}(\tau)

\displaystyle=b^{-1}\varphi(\tau^{2})\varphi(\tau)^{-1}=b^{-1}\varphi(\sigma)b^{-1}=b^{-1}ab^{-1}.

It follows that the pairs
(a^{-1}ba^{-1},a^{-1}b^{-1})
and
(b^{-1}a^{-1},b^{-1}ab^{-1})
must satisfy the relations from Proposition
1
. In particular, we get the following relations:

(4a)

\displaystyle(a^{-1}ba^{-1})^{9}

\displaystyle=1,

(4b)

\displaystyle(a^{-1}b^{-1})^{9}

\displaystyle=1,

(4c)

\displaystyle(a^{-1}b^{-1})\,(a^{-1}ba^{-1})\,(a^{-1}b^{-1})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(a^{-1}ba^{-1})^{4},

(4d)

\displaystyle(a^{-1}ba^{-1})\,(a^{-1}b^{-1})\,(a^{-1}ba^{-1})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(a^{-1}b^{-1})^{4},

(4e)

\displaystyle(b^{-1}a^{-1})^{9}

\displaystyle=1,

(4f)

\displaystyle(b^{-1}ab^{-1})^{9}

\displaystyle=1,

(4g)

\displaystyle(b^{-1}ab^{-1})\,(b^{-1}a^{-1})\,(b^{-1}ab^{-1})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(b^{-1}a^{-1})^{4},

(4h)

\displaystyle(b^{-1}a^{-1})\,(b^{-1}ab^{-1})\,(b^{-1}a^{-1})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(b^{-1}ab^{-1})^{4}.

Note that (
4e
) follows from (
4b
) by conjugation with
a
. We set

z:=a^{-1}b^{-1},\quad w:=a^{-1}ba^{-1},\quad v:=a^{-1}(b^{-1}ab^{-1})a=zw^{-1}.

Rewriting the relations, we end up with:

\displaystyle w^{9}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:quadratic-relations-1}}}{{=}}1,\quad v^{9}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:quadratic-relations-6}}}{{=}}1,\quad z^{9}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:quadratic-relations-2}}}{{=}}1

\displaystyle zwz^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:quadratic-relations-3}}}{{=}}w^{4},\quad wzw^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:quadratic-relations-4}}}{{=}}z^{4},

\displaystyle vzv^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:quadratic-relations-7}}}{{=}}z^{4},\quad zvz^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:quadratic-relations-8}}}{{=}}v^{4},

where we applied conjugation with
a^{-1}
to the last two relations. Now, observe that
wzw^{-1}=z^{4}
and
z^{9}=w^{9}=1
imply
w^{-1}zw=z^{7}
, since
w^{-1}=w^{8}
and
4^{8}\equiv 7\pmod{9}
.
We compute

z^{4}=vzv^{-1}=zw^{-1}zwz^{-1}=zz^{7}z^{-1}=z^{7}

and obtain
z^{3}=1
. But this implies that
z
commutes with
v,w
and hence
w^{3}=v^{3}=1
. Thus, the elements
a,b
necessarily satisfy the following relations:

(ba)^{3},(ab^{-1}a)^{3},[ba,ab^{-1}a].

We define
\Gamma:=\langle a,b\mid(ba)^{3},(ab^{-1}a)^{3},[ba,ab^{-1}a]\rangle.
Our computation already implies that
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
is a quotient of
\Gamma
, sending
a
to
\varphi(\sigma)
and
b
to
\varphi(\tau)
. We now seek to show that the converse also holds.

Lemma 1
.

The map
\varphi:C_{3}\to\Gamma
given by
\varphi(\sigma)=a
and
\varphi(\tau)=b
is cubic.

Proof.

We have to check that
\Delta_{k}\varphi
is quadratic for
k=\sigma
and
k=\tau
. We only check the first case, the second is similar. We have to check that
\beta_{\sigma}
is quadratic, i.e., that the relations from Proposition
1
are satisfied for the pair
(a^{-1}ba^{-1},a^{-1}b^{-1})
. But this follows easily from the relations in
\Gamma
as we have already seen above.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

By the universal property of
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
, the map
\varphi\colon C_{3}\to\Gamma
factors through a homomorphism
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})\to\Gamma
. This map is clearly surjective and a section of the previously discussed surjection in the inverse direction. This implies that both groups are isomorphic identifying
a
with
\varphi(\sigma)
and
b
with
\varphi(\tau)
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

The abelianization of
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
is isomorphic to
C_{9}\times C_{3}
. In particular, any cubic map from
C_{3}
to an abelian group is uniquely determined by elements
a=\varphi(\sigma),b=\varphi(\tau)
in
G
satisfying
a^{9}=b^{9}=(ab)^{3}=1
.

3.
Two concrete representations of
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
with arithmetic image

Throughout, we identify
\Gamma
with
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
via the isomorphism from Theorem
1
. We also define
\Gamma^{\circ}
to be the kernel of the tautological homomorphism
\mu\colon\Gamma\to C_{3}
given by
\mu(a)=\sigma
and
\mu(b)=\tau
.

3.1.
A representation in characteristic zero

Fix a primitive cube root of unity
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
, so
\omega^{2}+\omega+1=0
. Let
r
be a root of
r^{3}=1-\omega
and set
E:=\mathbb{Q}(r)
.
There is a canonical identification of
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]/((1-\omega))
with
\mathbb{F}_{3}
.
We denote by
{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega];I)
the congruence subgroup associated with the ideal
I
and define

K_{n}:={\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega];((1-\omega)^{n}))
for
n=0,1,2
.
Note that
K_{0}={\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega])
and
K_{0}/K_{1}={\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}),K_{2}/K_{1}={\mathfrak{sl}}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
.
We also denote by
{\rm U}_{+}(\mathbb{F}_{3})\subseteq{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
the subgroup of upper triangular unipotent matrices.

Theorem 2
.

The assignment

\pi(a)=\begin{pmatrix}\omega r^{2}&\frac{-2\omega-1}{3}r^{2}&\frac{-\omega-2}{3}r^{2}\\
-r^{2}&-\omega r^{2}&\frac{-2\omega-1}{3}r^{2}\\
-(\omega+1)r^{2}&r^{2}&r^{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\pi(b)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\frac{\omega+2}{3}r\\
r&0&(\omega+1)r\\
0&r&\omega r\end{pmatrix}.

extends to a representation of
\pi\colon\Gamma\to{\rm PSL}_{3}(E)
. The image of
\pi
is commensurable with
K_{0}={\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega])
, hence arithmetic. More precisely,

(1)

\pi(\Gamma)\cap K_{0}=\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})
has index
3
in
\pi(\Gamma)
,

(2)

\pi(\Gamma)\cap K_{0}
has index
2^{4}\cdot 3\cdot 13=624
in
K_{0}
and moreover

(a)

K_{2}\subseteq\pi(\Gamma)

(b)

(\pi(\Gamma)\cap K_{1})/K_{2}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&a&b\\
c&d&e\\
0&f&-d\end{pmatrix}\mid a,b,c,d,e,f\in\mathbb{F}_{3}\right\}\subseteq{\mathfrak{sl}}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
, and

(c)

(\pi(\Gamma)\cap K_{0})/(\pi(\Gamma)\cap K_{1})={\rm U}_{+}(\mathbb{F}_{3})\subseteq{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
.

Outline of the proof.

Once the matrices are given, it is a matter of direct computation to check that the relations in
\Gamma
are satisfied. Generators of
\Gamma^{\circ}
can be computed using the Nielsen-Schreier algorithm and it can be checked directly that
\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})\subseteq K_{0}
and that the quotient
\pi(\Gamma)/\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})
is isomorphic to
C_{3}
. This proves (1).

The rest of the claims follow from direct computation and standard facts about congruence subgroups. Indeed, we construct explicit words in the generators
a,b
of
\Gamma
that yield standard unipotent matrices with entries in
3\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
and
\langle U_{-}(3\mathbb{Z}[\omega]),U_{+}(3\mathbb{Z}[\omega])\rangle={\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega];3\mathbb{Z}[\omega])
, a standard fact since
3\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
is a Euclidean domain, see
[
Bas64
]
. This proves (2a). The claims in (2b) and (2c) are verified by direct computation.

All computations are certified by the Magma transcript
char0cert.m
and documented in
char0cert.txt
∎

Corollary 2
.

The group
\pi(\Gamma)
is an arithmetic lattice in
{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
.

Polynomial maps to nilpotent groups are particularly important; see
[
Lei02
]
, where various structure results have been obtained. In
[
JT24
, Remark 4.2]
it was observed that the image of a quadratic map from
\mathbb{Z}
can generate a finite nilpotent group of arbitrarily large nilpotency class. The following corollary shows that the same holds for cubic maps from
C_{3}
.

Corollary 3
.

There are finite nilpotent groups of arbitrarily large nilpotency class admitting a cubic map from
C_{3}
whose image generates the group.

Proof.

As a consequence of the preceding theorem, the group
\pi(\Gamma)
is residually
3
-finite. Since finite
3
-groups are nilpotent and
\pi(\Gamma)
is not itself nilpotent, there is no bound on the nilpotency class of finite quotients of
\pi(\Gamma)
. Since
\pi(\Gamma)
is a quotient of
\Gamma
, the same holds for
\Gamma
. However, homomorphisms from
\Gamma
correspond to cubic maps from
C_{3}
, so there are finite nilpotent groups of arbitrarily large nilpotency class admitting a cubic map from
C_{3}
whose image generates the group.
∎

3.2.
A representation in characteristic
3

In addition to the complex representation from Section
3
, there is also an explicit
representation of
\Gamma

3
. This allows to view
\Gamma^{\circ}
as a lattice in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}((u)))

Consider the function field
\mathbb{F}_{3}(t)
, set

and view
\mathbb{F}_{3}(u)
as a subfield of
\mathbb{F}_{3}(t)
via the

Theorem 3
.

The assignment

\rho(a)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1/t\\
2/t&0&0\\
2t^{2}&2t^{2}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\rho(b)=\begin{pmatrix}0&2t&0\\
0&0&2/t^{2}\\
t&0&t\end{pmatrix}

extends to a representation
\rho:\Gamma\to\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}(t))
.

(1)

We have
\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})\leq\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u])
.

(2)

(a)

\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})
contains the principal congruence subgroup

\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u],(u^{2}))
.
In particular,
\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})
has finite index in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u])
.

(b)

The quotient

\bigl(\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})\cap\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u]),(u)\bigr)\big/\bigl(\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})\cap\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[[u]]),(u^{2})\bigr)

is isomorphic to a
7
-dimensional subspace of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
, and

(c)

\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})/(\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})\cap\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u],(u)))
is isomorphic to
{\rm U}_{+}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
, the subgroup of upper triangular unipotent matrices in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
.

Outline of the proof.

Again, it is a matter of computing that
\rho(a),\rho(b)
satisfy the relators of
\Gamma
, hence
define a representation
\rho
. It also computes Schreier generators for the index-
3
kernel
H
and matches
the images of
w_{1},\dots,w_{4}
under
\rho
with explicit matrices
h_{1},\dots,h_{4}\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u])
.

Next, the certificate produces, root-by-root, explicit words in
h_{1},\dots,h_{4}
evaluating to the elementary
matrices
E_{ij}(u^{2})
and
E_{ij}(u^{3})
for all
i\neq j
. Using the Steinberg commutator relations in

\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u])
, this implies that
\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})
contains all elementary matrices
E_{ij}(f(u))
with
f(u)\in(u^{2})
.
Since
\mathbb{F}_{3}[u]
is a Euclidean domain
[
Bas64
]
, one has

\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u],(u^{2}))

\displaystyle=E_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u],(u^{2})),

\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u],(u^{2}))

\displaystyle\leq\rho(\Gamma^{\circ}).

Finally,
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u],(u^{2}))
has finite index in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u])
, because
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u]/(u^{2}))
is finite.
All computations are certified by the Magma transcript
char3cert.m
and documented in
char3cert.txt

∎

As a corollary, we obtain a second arithmetic representation of
\Gamma^{\circ}
.

Corollary 4
.

The group
\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})
is an arithmetic lattice in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}((u)))
.

3.3.
Final discussion

Despite these attractive presentations of
\Gamma
, we could not identify the origin of these representations and determine if their existence is an isolated phenomenon or part of a larger picture. It seems we have struck an iceberg without having any sense of how large it is. In addition, basic questions about
\Gamma
remain open, for example we do not know whether
\Gamma
is residually finite or even linear. Unlike for
{\rm Pol}_{2}(G)
, we lack any conceptual description of
{\rm Pol}_{3}(C_{3})
.

Note however that we obtain the following peculiar consequence of Margulis seminal work on lattices in algebraic groups over local fields, see
[
Mar91
]
.

Corollary 5
.

The image of
\Gamma^{\circ}
under the map
\pi\times\rho\colon\Gamma^{\circ}\to{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega])\times\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u])
contains a finite index subgroup.

Proof.

Consider the image of the product map:

\Delta:=(\pi\times\rho)(\Gamma^{\circ})\leq\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})\times\rho(\Gamma^{\circ}).

By Theorem
2
, the subgroup
\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})
has finite index in
{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega])
.
By Theorem
3
, the subgroup
\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})
has finite index in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u])
.

Let
p_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
be the coordinate projections. By definition of
\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})
and
\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})
, both
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are
surjective, hence
\Delta
is a subdirect product.

Define
N_{2}:=\ker(p_{1})
and
N_{1}:=\ker(p_{2})
. Then
N_{2}\lhd\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})
and
N_{1}\lhd\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})
. By Margulis’ Normal Subgroup Theorem
[
Mar91
]
, each
N_{i}
is either finite or has finite index.

We claim that neither
N_{1}
nor
N_{2}
can be finite. Assume that
N_{2}
is finite.
Then
p_{1}
induces an isomorphism
\Delta/N_{2}\cong\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})
.
Via this identification, the projection
p_{2}
induces a surjection

f:\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})\twoheadrightarrow\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})/N_{2}.

Its kernel is a normal subgroup of
\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})
, hence finite or of finite index by Margulis.
Since
\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})/N_{2}
is infinite, the kernel cannot have finite index and must be finite.

By Margulis’ Superrigidity Theorem
[
Mar91
]
, the map
f
extends to a homomorphism
{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})\to{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}((u)))
, which is absurd. We can argue similarly for
N_{1}
in place of
N_{2}
.

Consequently,
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
have finite index in
\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})
and
\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})
.
Since
(N_{1}\times\{1\})\leq\Delta
and
(\{1\}\times N_{2})\leq\Delta
, we get
N_{1}\times N_{2}\leq\Delta
.
As
N_{1}\times N_{2}
has finite index in
\pi(\Gamma^{\circ})\times\rho(\Gamma^{\circ})
, it follows that
\Delta
contains a finite index subgroup of

{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega])\times\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}[u])
.
∎

Remark 2
.

Both representation
\pi
and
\rho
give rise, by reduction modulo
1-\omega
and
u
respectively, to homomorphisms

\bar{\pi},\bar{\rho}\colon\Gamma^{\circ}\to{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3}).

The images of
\bar{\pi}
and
\bar{\rho}
are isomorphic to the upper triangular unipotent subgroup of
{\rm PSL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
, but the homomorphisms are not related by an automorphism. We found that surprising, since our first expectation was a compatibility between the two representations.
