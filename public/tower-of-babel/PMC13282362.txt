Dapagliflozin confers protection against ferroptosis in cardiomyocyte via the inhibition of interferon-gamma pathway
Authors: Rizki Radhitya Menaldi, Harishkumar Madhyastha, Radha Madhyastha, Sowmiya Babu, Tomoko Maruyama, Renan Sukmawan, Yuichi Nakajima, Nozomi Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFerroptosis is reported to have a role in cardiovascular disease (CVD) progression. Recent emerging evidences demonstrate the role of Sodium-Glucose Co-Transporter 2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors in CVD owing to its promising cardioprotective benefits. However, the detailed molecular mechanism underlying its cardioprotective effects remain incompletely understood. We aimed to investigate the potential ferroptosis-inhibitory mechanism of SGLT-2 inhibitor in HL-1 mouse cardiomyocytes.Methods and ResultsCardiomyocytes were incubated for 5 h under hypoxic conditions (1% O2) to simulate ischemic injury with or without dapagliflozin (DAPA, 10 µM), a SGLT-2 inhibitor. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) was detected by immunofluorescence. Ferroptosis activity was analysed by measuring levels of intracellular iron, lipid peroxidation, malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH). Increase in ROS generation, intracellular iron, MDA concentration, and lipid peroxidation indicated increasing ferroptosis activity during hypoxic stress in cardiomyocytes. Treatment with DAPA caused reduction in ferroptosis. Pathway-specific gene analysis showed that interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) was upregulated during hypoxia, and was downregulated with DAPA treatment. Silencing of IFN-γ by using small interfering RNA (siRNA) resulted in inhibition of lipid peroxidation; on the other hand, incorporating recombinant IFN-γ resulted in the reversal of this phenomenon, thus validating the role of IFN-γ in hypoxia-induced ferroptosis.ConclusionOur findings reveal IFN-γ pathway as a novel intracellular target of ferroptosis inhibition by DAPA in cardiomyocytes, providing molecular insight into the mechanisms underlying the cardioprotective effects of SGLT-2 inhibitor.Keywords: Dapagliflozin, Sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor, Lipid peroxidation, Ferroptosis, Interferon-gamma

Introduction

Ferroptosis is distinguished from other cell death mechanisms by the excessive accumulation of intracellular iron and the activation of lipid peroxidation [1]. Although it was originally studied in the contexts of tumor suppression and aging, recent evidence links ferroptosis to organ injury and metabolic disorders, including cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). In cardiac ischemia/reperfusion injury model, intracellular iron overload and enhanced oxidative stress drive myocardial damage, in part through hypoxia-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and activation of hypoxia-inducible factor signaling – alteration that collectively promote lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death [2–4]. Beyond ischemic injury, ferroptosis has been implicated in other CVDs, such as arrhythmia, heart failure (HF), and hypertension; suggesting a broader contributory role in CVD progression [3]. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms underlying ferroptosis in specific CVD context remain to be fully elucidated, which may in turn reveal novel therapeutic targets.

Sodium/glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors, a class of anti-diabetic agents, have recently been established as key treatment in HF, supported by growing evidence of their cardioprotective role extending to patients without diabetes [5–9]. Moreover, meta-analysis reports on the use of SGLT-2 inhibitor in acute myocardial infarction have reported approximately 33% reduction in hospitalization due to HF and 23% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events [10, 11]. Despite these well-established clinical benefits, the molecular mechanism underlying the cardioprotective effects of SGLT-2 inhibitors remains incompletely understood.

We aimed to investigate the effect of dapagliflozin (DAPA), a SGLT-2 inhibitor, on ferroptosis in hypoxic cardiomyocytes to reveal potential alternative molecular mechanism behind the cardioprotective effect of SGLT-2 inhibitors.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HL-1, mouse cardiomyocytes (JCRB Cell Bank, Tokyo, Japan), were cultured on gelatin (0.1%)-fibronectin (1 mg/mL)-coated culture plates, in modified Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium/Ham’s F-12 (Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) and 1% antibiotic cocktail mixture (Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) under standard culture condition (5% CO2 at 37 °C). Cells were treated with 10 µM DAPA for 24 h prior to hypoxia. The DAPA dosage was selected based on previous studies [12, 13]. To induce ferroptosis, the cells were incubated under hypoxic condition (1% O2) for 5 h. As a negative control, 5 µM ferrostatin-1 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), a classical ferroptosis inhibitor, was incorporated into the cultures for 24 h prior hypoxia. The ferrostatin-1 dosage was selected based on previous study [14, 15]. Furthermore, to determine whether exogenous interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) could reverse the protective effect of DAPA on ferroptosis, a rescue experiment was conducted by introducing recombinant IFN-γ (Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, USA) at a concentration of 10 ng/mL into the culture following 24-hour DAPA treatment and 5-hour hypoxic incubation. The dosage for recombinant IFN-γ was selected based on previous study [16].

Reactive oxygen species assay

To monitor the ROS generation, 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescence staining was performed. After the treatment with DAPA and simulation under hypoxic condition, DCFH-DA (100 mM) was added to the cells and incubated for 45 min. Hoechst 33,342 (1 µg/mL) was used for nuclear staining. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, slides were dried at room temperature. The fluorescent images were captured at 63x magnification, using a confocal laser scanning (~ 488 nm) microscope, Leica TCS SP8 (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Intracellular iron (II) assay

Intracellular iron (II) content was detected using FerroOrange™ fluorescence staining kit (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells cultured on glass cover slips were treated as previously explained, and further processed for the detection of intracellular iron (II) content, as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Hoechst 33,342 was added for nuclear staining. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, mounted on glass slides, and dried at room temperature. The intracellular iron (II) content was measured by using a confocal laser scanning (~ 580 nm) microscope, Leica TCS SP8.

Lipid peroxidation study

Lipid peroxidation activity was detected by using Image-IT® Lipid Peroxidation Kit (Life Technologies Corporation, Oregon, USA). Treated cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with 10 µM Image-IT® Lipid Peroxidation Sensor (Life Technologies Corporation). Hoechst 33,342 (1 µg/mL) was used for nuclear staining. Fluorescence emission peak’s shift from red (~ 590 nm) to green (~ 510 nm), indicating the lipid peroxidation activity, was detected using a confocal laser scanning microscope, Leica TCS SP8.

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay

Treated cells were lysed in ice-cold radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer, supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan). TBARS assay was performed by combining 100 µL malondialdehyde (MDA) standard solution or 300 µL of cell lysate sample with 100 µL of trichloroacetic acid (TCA) assay reagent (10%) and 800 µL of the color reagent in a reaction tube. After incubation in hot water (95 °C), the tubes were immediately kept in ice for 10 min. One mL of sample was transferred into a 1.5 mL tube and centrifuged at 1,600 x g at 4 °C for 10 min. The absorbance was recorded at 530 nm using Thermo Scientific™ Multiskan™ FC Microplate Photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA).

Glutathione metabolism study

Treated cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan). Cell lysates were incubated in a working reagent, consisting of glutathione reduction enzyme, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), and 5,5’-Dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA). Absorbance (~ 405 nm) was recorded for total glutathione and reduced disulfide (GSSG) at 0 and 10-minute intervals (Thermo Scientific™ Multiskan™ FC Microplate Photometer).

RNA isolation and gene-specific pathway finder

Total RNA was isolated from the treated cells using the phenol-chloroform method. Complementary DNA was synthesized as per standard method using RT2 First Strand Kit (QIAGEN, Maryland, USA). To determine the mechanism of ferroptosis inhibitory effect of DAPA, gene-specific pathway finder PAMM-014 A RT2 Profiler™ PCR Array Mouse Signal Transduction PathwayFinder (SA Biosciences, Maryland, USA) was used. RT-PCR analysis was done with the help of the GeneGlobe qPCR Data Analysis Tool (QIAGEN, Maryland, USA).

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) assay

Cells were cultured as per standard experiment method until subconfluence in antibiotic-free media and were transfected with small interfering RNA (siRNA) for IFN-γ (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Texas, USA) for 24 h, followed by treatment with DAPA and incubation under hypoxic condition (1% O2) for 5 h. The lipid peroxidation assay was done by using the Image-IT® Lipid Peroxidation kit, as per the manufacturer’s protocol (Life Technologies Corporation). Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342. The lipid peroxidation activity was observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope, Leica TCS SP8.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) and centrifuged at 18,000xg for 15 min. Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Eight micrograms of cell lysates was resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (immuno-blot, Bio-Rad, California, USA) or Hybond-N+ Nylon Transfer Membrane (#RPN2222B, Amersham Biosciences, Buckinghamshire, UK) using a Semi-Dry Electrophoretic Transfer Cell (Bio-Rad). The membranes were blocked with EzBlock Chemi (Atto, Tokyo, Japan) for 30 min at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with following primary antibodies: β-actin (#4967, 1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology), Stat1 antibody (#14994, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology); and Phospho-Stat1 antibody (#9167, 1:500, Cell Signaling Technology); followed by HRP-conjugated IgG antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was detected using the enhanced chemiluminescence Can Get Signal™ Immunoreaction Enhancer Solution (#NKB-101, Toyobo Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) or Western BLoT Hyper HRP Substrate (#T7103A, Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) in conjunction with the Fusion-FX imaging system (Vilber, Collégien, France). Band intensities were quantified using Bio-1D image analysis software.

Statistical analysis

The values from descriptive analysis in this study were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., California, USA). After confirming distribution normality, a homogeneity test of variance was done using Levene’s test. Unless reported otherwise, all data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons between groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

To study the response of cardiomyocytes under hypoxic condition, an immunofluorescence assay to detect intracellular ROS was done (Fig. 1). The fluorescence intensity of ROS (marked in green color) was increased under hypoxic condition when compared to control, and reduced significantly in the group treated with DAPA. This finding indicates an increase in ROS activity in hypoxia, which was ameliorated in the presence of DAPA.

The increase in intracellular iron (II) causes an increase in the labile iron pool, which, through the Fenton reaction, may contribute to the generation of ROS and subsequently trigger ferroptosis [17]. As shown in Fig. 2, the fluorescent intensity of intracellular iron (II) (red) was increased under hypoxic condition, but reduced significantly with DAPA treatment. Reduced intracellular iron (II) fluorescence intensity was also observed with ferrostatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor. These results demonstrate the efficacy of DAPA in reducing the intracellular iron level.

During ferroptosis, lipid peroxidation in the plasma membrane is activated as the production of ROS increases. A fluorescence shift from red (non-oxidized lipid) to green (oxidized lipid) acts as a marker of increasing lipid peroxidation activity. As shown in Fig. 3, the fluorescence shift from red to green was found to increase in the hypoxia group, and reduced with DAPA treatment. Additionally, we studied the lipid peroxidation activity by measuring MDA–TBARS adduct. Colorimetric measurement showed a 4-fold increase in MDA concentration in the hypoxia group (Fig. 4). On the other hand, treatment with DAPA resulted in MDA concentration similar to the control. When treated with ferrostatin-1, immunofluorescence assay demonstrated reduced fluorescence shift (Fig. 3) and colorimetric assay demonstrated reduced MDA concentration (Fig. 4). These findings indicate the lipid peroxidation inhibitory effect of DAPA.

Glutathione system plays an important role in balancing intracellular oxidative stress. Total glutathione consists of GSH and its reduced form, glutathione disulfide (GSSG). Normal GSH/GSSG ratio in cytoplasm is estimated to be approximately 10,000:1–50,000:1 [18]. Together, GSH and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) create a system with a purpose to inhibit oxidative stress. In the presence of GSH, GPX4 continuously reduces phospholipid hydroperoxide (PLOOHs) [18]. In high oxidative stress levels, GSH concentration would be depleted, causing the glutathione system to fail and triggering ferroptosis. Our results demonstrated increasing GSSG level in the hypoxia group compared to the control (Fig. 5). However, with DAPA treatment, the GSSG level was significantly lowered. Similar result was also observed in the group treated with ferrostatin-1. This evidence indicates the presence of high oxidative stress levels in hypoxic condition, as well as suggests the inhibitory role of DAPA in glutathione metabolism.

Glutathione assay. Colorimetric assay was used to detect glutathione metabolism by measuring glutathione disulfide (GSSG) level at 450 nm. Results are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3) from 3 independent experiments. *p < 0.05 by one-way ANOVA. NS: Non-significant.

We further investigated the activation of multiple signaling pathways using the real-time PCR array method to study the mechanism of ferroptosis. Genes directly involved in glutathione metabolism, like gadd45a, gadd45b, glutamate-cystine ligase, and glutathione reductase, were upregulated during hypoxia and were downregulated with DAPA treatment (Table 1). One of the pro-inflammatory signaling genes (ifng) was found to be upregulated 4-fold in hypoxia compared to control (Table 1). In the group treated with DAPA, the expression level of ifng was significantly downregulated, suggesting a potential role of IFN-γ pathway in cardiomyocyte ferroptosis.

To evaluate the role of IFN-γ in ferroptosis, we knocked down IFN-γ through siRNA transfection method. Transfected cells were exposed to hypoxia for 5 h and lipid peroxidation level was measured. The cells in hypoxia group showed a fluorescence shift from red (non-oxidized lipid) to green (oxidized lipid); the shift was reduced in the IFN-γ siRNA-transfected cells (Fig. 6). These results indicated that silencing ifng results in the inhibition of ferroptosis, confirming that the IFN-γ pathway is involved in ferroptosis.

Discussion

Our results demonstrated that incubating cardiomyocytes under hypoxic condition increased ROS generation, intracellular iron (II), lipid peroxidation, and MDA concentration, suggesting the activation of ferroptosis. Similar results were observed in ischemia/reperfusion studies, which demonstrated an increase in intracellular ROS generation, iron deposition, intracellular iron (II), and MDA level, as well as a decrease in GSH level and GPX4 activity [21–23]. Moreover, Chen et al. reported that treatment with DAPA reverted all changes, suggesting the vital role of SGLT-2 inhibitors in ischemic/reperfusion-induced ferroptosis [21]. Concurrent to the earlier studies, our findings demonstrated an inhibitory effect of DAPA in hypoxia-induced ferroptosis activity. The same phenomenon was also observed when the cells were treated with ferrostatin-1, a classic ferroptosis inhibitor, further validating our hypothesis regarding ferroptosis-inhibitory effect of DAPA.

The hallmark features of ferroptosis, such as elevated ROS, decreased GPX4, and increased lipid peroxidation, have been reported in many CVD models, including chemotherapy-induced cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, diabetic cardiomyopathy, myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, and heart transplant [1, 3, 4]. Furthermore, treatment in these disease models with ferroptosis inhibitors, such as ferrostatin-1, liproxstatin-1, and deferoxamine, demonstrated reversion in the inflammatory or hypertrophic markers and improved cellular function [1, 3, 4]. SGLT-2 inhibitors have been reported to have a cardioprotective role. Our studies using cardiomyocytes demonstrated that DAPA, a SGLT-2 inhibitor, modulates ferroptosis-related pathways (Table 1). We emphasized on IFN-γ pathway, as it showed the most dramatic changes among other genes, with nearly 4-fold upregulation in hypoxia and 12-fold downregulation with DAPA treatment, while the remaining genes were noted as candidates for future investigation. A series of ferroptosis assay results demonstrated that DAPA inhibits ferroptosis in hypoxic cardiomyocytes through modulation of the IFN-γ pathway. While IFN-γ has been implicated in ferroptosis in other cellular contexts, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate this mechanism specifically in the setting of DAPA treatment in hypoxic cardiomyocytes, providing a novel mechanistic insight into the cardioprotective effects of SGLT-2 inhibitors. Ferroptosis inhibitory effect of SGLT-2 inhibitors is reported mainly through the inhibition of the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway, the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), or acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) pathway [21, 24]. The involvement of IFN-γ in modulating oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation suggests an alternative and potentially complementary mechanism of the ferroptosis-inhibitory effect of DAPA in cardiomyocytes.

The recognition of SGLT-2 inhibitors as a key treatment in various CVDs represents a remarkable paradigm shift in modern medicine. Originally developed as a glucose-lowering agent, SGLT-2 inhibitors were subsequently demonstrated in large-scale clinical trials to significantly improve cardiovascular outcomes, including in patients without diabetes mellitus [5–9]. This unexpected clinical benefit has prompted intensive investigation into their underlying molecular mechanisms. Emerging evidence suggests that SGLT-2 inhibitors may influence pathways beyond glucose regulation, including those involved in organ protection and regulated cell death, such as ferroptosis [21, 25, 26]. Within this context, the identification of IFN-γ-related signaling pathway provides an important step toward elucidating the molecular basis by which SGLT-2 inhibitors exert cardiovascular protection.

There are a few limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as ferroptosis remains a relatively new concept, particularly in the context of CVD, an important challenge lies in translating the underlying mechanisms into clinical practice. We have successfully demonstrated the activation of ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes under ischemia-mimicking conditions; however, the phenomenon is likely to be much more complex in clinical settings, where the outcomes of heart disease are shaped by multiple interacting pathways, and ferroptosis may represent only one component of the broader disease process. Animal studies employing disease-specific models will be essential to further elucidate the detailed mechanism of ferroptosis in different forms of CVD. Secondly, we did not clarify the direct effects of SGLT-2 inhibition in cardiomyocytes, as we did not assess SGLT-2 receptor localization or its expression in our study. Although we demonstrated cardioprotective effects mediated through the IFN-γ pathway, it remains unclear whether these actions are related to sglt2 expression. Lastly, as the study was limited to DAPA, we need to validate the potential of other SGLT-2 inhibitors in exerting similar effects on ferroptosis phenomenon in cardiomyocytes. Comparative studies across multiple SGLT-2 inhibitors will therefore be necessary.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that DAPA inhibits hypoxia-induced ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes. We further reveal IFN-γ pathway as a novel intracellular target of ferroptosis inhibition by DAPA. These findings provide a key molecular insight into the cardiomyocyte protection by DAPA, a SGLT-2 inhibitor, laying the groundwork for future in vivo and translational studies.
