Spontaneous rupture of a clinically recurrent intracranial dermoid cyst: a case report and narrative review
Authors: Hongyuan Liu, Hui Zeng, Bufan Yang, Yuzhu Ji, Zongping Li, Liangxue Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo describe the clinical, pathological, and therapeutic features of spontaneous rupture of a recurrent intracranial dermoid cyst (IDC), and to summarize relevant dermoid-specific literature.MethodsWe report a 38-year-old woman with a clinical history of resection of a left middle and posterior cranial fossa dermoid cyst more than 10 years earlier. She presented with headache and vomiting, and imaging suggested rupture of a recurrent lesion with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dissemination. Emergency microsurgical resection assisted by neuroendoscopy was performed. Six months postoperatively, she developed delayed obstructive hydrocephalus, requiring neuroendoscopic fenestration and lesion debridement. A narrative literature review of ruptured intracranial dermoid cysts (IDCs) was performed, with emphasis on lesions, dissemination patterns, hydrocephalus, treatment, and reported outcomes.ResultsDuring the initial emergency surgery, the cyst exhibited paste-like consistency with a thickened capsule. Postoperative pathology confirmed a dermoid cyst. However, disseminated foci remained within the cerebral sulci and ventricles. The second surgery revealed multiple pearl-like lesions within the ventricular system with associated lipid leakage. Following clearance of these lesions, the hydrocephalus resolved.ConclusionSpontaneous rupture of a recurrent IDC is exceptional and may be followed by extensive ventricular dissemination and delayed hydrocephalus. This necessitates a profound understanding of the associated risks, enhanced follow-up protocols, and timely intervention.

Introduction

Intracranial dermoid cysts (IDCs) are rare benign congenital inclusion lesions, usually reported to account for approximately 0.04-0.6% of intracranial tumors (1). Unlike epidermoid cysts, which are more commonly located in the cerebellopontine angle, intracranial dermoid cysts are generally described as rare congenital lesions that preferentially occur in or near the midline, particularly in the suprasellar or parasellar, frontobasal, temporobasal, and posterior fossa regions (2–5). Dermoid cysts arise from ectodermal elements trapped during neural tube closure and enlarge slowly through accumulation of desquamated epithelium, sebaceous secretions, cholesterol, keratin, fat droplets, and occasionally hair (3, 6). Histologically, dermoid cysts are lined by keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium and, in contrast to epidermoid cysts, may contain adnexal structures such as hair follicles and sebaceous or sweat glands (3, 6). The clinical manifestations are usually related to progressive mass effect, irritation of adjacent neural structures, or cyst rupture (3, 7).

Rupture of an IDC may be spontaneous, traumatic, or iatrogenic (3, 8). After rupture, lipid-rich debris can disseminate through the subarachnoid space and ventricular system, resulting in aseptic or chemical meningitis, seizures, vasospasm, hydrocephalus, cranial nerve dysfunction, or focal neurological deficits (3, 7). Rupture of a recurrent or residual IDC is even less frequently reported. Because recurrence often reflects residual capsule or microscopic epithelial remnants, distinguishing true recurrence after complete removal from delayed regrowth after subtotal removal is clinically important (9, 10). We therefore present a case of spontaneous rupture of a clinically recurrent IDC with intraventricular dissemination and delayed obstructive hydrocephalus, and we provide a narrative review.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old woman was admitted with headache and vomiting. The patient visited a local hospital and underwent a head computed tomography (CT) scan indicated a space-occupying lesion in the left middle cranial fossa. She underwent resection of a left middle and posterior cranial fossa dermoid cyst at other Hospital more than 10 years earlier. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with no recurrence detected on follow-up examinations within the first 3 years. Subsequently, the patient did not undergo regular follow-up evaluations. The historical operative record, early postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and first histopathological slides were not available for the present revision. Therefore, although the current lesion is clinically described as recurrent, true recurrence after confirmed gross total resection cannot be distinguished with certainty from regrowth of residual microscopic capsule or epithelial remnants.

Physical examination revealed an old surgical scar in the left frontal and temporal region. A head CT scan at the local hospital indicated a space-occupying lesion at the base of the left middle cranial fossa with high possibility of rupture. Admission blood tests showed white blood cell count in the blood routine test was slightly elevated; coagulation profile, liver and kidney function, electrolytes, and tumor markers were normal. An emergency contrast-enhanced head MRI revealed a mass in the left middle cranial fossa with suspected mild enhancement of the rim and septa, measuring approximately 7.8cm × 5.1cm × 4.6cm (Figure 1A). The adjacent temporal lobe and brainstem were compressed, and there were scattered lower signals in some cisterns and bilateral anterior horns of the lateral ventricles (Figure 1B). Multiple punctate and nodular enhancing lesions were present in the bilateral cerebral hemispheres, lateral ventricles, and sulci.

Enhanced MRI for recurrence. (A) MRI demonstrates a recurrent lesion in the left middle cranial fossa with suspicious mild marginal enhancement, compressing the temporal lobe and brainstem. (B) Scattered hypointense foci in the gyrus and bilateral anterior horns of the ventricles (red arrows).

Following admission, emergency resection of the left middle cranial fossa lesion was performed under microscopy with neuroendoscopic assistance. Intraoperatively, the lesion was located in the middle cranial fossa and contained paste-like material with a relatively thick capsule. Postoperative enhanced MRI confirmed total resection of the middle cranial fossa lesion but revealed persistent scattered hypointense foci in the cerebral gyri and bilateral anterior horns of the ventricles (Figure 2). Postoperative histopathological examination showed that the cyst cavity was lined by keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium and contained keratinous material, with identifiable skin adnexal structures, confirming the diagnosis of a dermoid cyst (Figures 3A, B).

Enhanced MRI 1 week post-emergency surgery. (A) Total resection of the lesion in the left middle cranial fossa. (B) There are still scattered hypointense foci in the gyrus and bilateral anterior horns of the ventricles(red arrows).

Histopathological findings of the lesion. (A) The cyst wall was lined by keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium with keratinous material in the cyst cavity. (B) Dermal adnexal structures were observed within the cyst wall, supporting the diagnosis of a dermoid cyst.

Six months after discharge, the patient developed recurrent headaches without obvious precipitating factors. The pain was paroxysmal and distending in nature, occasionally sharp and stabbing, accompanied by nausea. Intermittent analgesic use provided little relief. Re-examination of the head CT scan showed fat-density shadows within the ventricular system and intracranial sulci and cisterns, suggesting lesion dissemination and severe supratentorial hydrocephalus. She subsequently underwent neuroendoscopic resection of third ventricular floor lesions, third ventriculostomy, and external ventricular drainage. Intraoperatively, the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was pale yellow and slightly turbid (Figure 4B). Multiple white pearl-like lesions were observed on the walls of the lateral and third ventricles (Figures 4A, C). The floor of the third ventricle appeared flat and thinned. After ventriculostomy, white pearl-like lesions with oily discharge were seen in the prepontine cistern. Postoperative lumbar puncture after the second surgery showed pale yellow and clear CSF. CSF analysis revealed an elevated protein level of 1325.9 mg/L, glucose of 4.83 mmol/L, chloride of 130.4 mmol/L, adenosine deaminase of <2 U/L, lactate dehydrogenase of 105 U/L, and a white blood cell count of 92 × 106/L. Bacterial culture of the CSF was negative. Following surgical intervention, the patient currently maintains clinically stable status with no acute complications.

(B) Oily material exuding from the floor of the third ventricle after ventriculostomy (red arrow). (C) Pearly lesions in the prepontine cistern observed post-third ventriculostomy (red arrow).

Discussion

IDCs pose clinical challenges due to their potential for recurrence, rupture, and dissemination of contents. Histologically, dermoid cysts are characterized by keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium and dermal adnexal structures, which help distinguish them from epidermoid cysts (9, 11). In the present case, postoperative pathology demonstrated a cyst cavity lined by keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium, keratinous material, and identifiable skin adnexal structures. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of a dermoid cyst.

The residual capsule is generally considered the main substrate for delayed regrowth, because the remaining epithelial lining can continue to desquamate and produce lipid rich material (3, 9, 11). In this case, the absence of the original operative note, postoperative MRI, and first pathology material prevents a definitive conclusion that the patient experienced true recurrence after complete resection. A possible explanation is delayed regrowth from residual tissue of the previously treated dermoid cyst. For this reason, the present lesion should be better described as a clinically recurrent IDC. Previous reports have also noted that recurrent dermoid cysts may develop after subtotal removal or preservation of residual cyst wall, especially when complete excision is limited by adherence to important neurovascular structures (9, 11, 12).

Rupture of an IDC may occur spontaneously, after trauma, or during surgery. Once rupture occurs, lipid droplets, cholesterol crystals, keratinous debris, and other cyst contents may spread into the subarachnoid space and ventricular system (3, 7). Reported complications include aseptic or chemical meningitis, seizures, vasospasm, hydrocephalus, cranial nerve dysfunction, cerebral ischemia, and focal neurological deficits (3, 5, 13). In this patient, MRI and CT showed disseminated foci in the sulci, cisterns, and ventricular system. During the second operation, multiple pearly lesions were seen along the lateral and third ventricular walls as well as oily material was observed in the prepontine cistern. These findings indicate extensive dissemination through the CSF pathways, not only localized regrowth at the primary site. The CSF results after the second operation provide useful objective evidence. Lumbar puncture showed pale-yellow and clear CSF, markedly elevated protein of 1325.9 mg/L. The CSF glucose was 4.83 mmol/L, chloride was 130.4 mmol/L, adenosine deaminase was less than 2 U/L, lactate dehydrogenase was 105 U/L, and the white blood cell count was 92 × 106/L. Bacterial culture was negative. This pattern, including high CSF protein, preserved glucose, and negative bacterial culture, is more compatible with an aseptic inflammatory reaction caused by lipid and keratinous cyst contents.

Only reports with accessible abstracts or full texts were included, and cases were excluded when two or more key variables were not reported. Although this table is not a systematic review, it provides a focused overview of recent well-documented cases and highlights the rarity of spontaneous rupture in clinically recurrent or residual IDC. The present case differs from most primary rupture reports in two aspects. The rupture occurred in a previously treated surgical field, where adhesions and scarring may have limited safe dissection and complete removal of disseminated foci. The patient also developed delayed obstructive hydrocephalus 6 months after reoperation. Nevertheless, this is only a single case, and the literature remains too sparse. We infer that rupture of a recurrent or residual IDC may lead to severe clinical consequences, and close surveillance is warranted. Delayed obstructive hydrocephalus is a particularly important feature of this case. Several mechanisms may have worked together. Lipid droplets and keratinous debris may have blocked CSF flow at the cerebral aqueduct, third ventricular outlet, or basal cisterns (14, 15). Inflammatory debris and arachnoid adhesions after rupture may also have impaired CSF absorption (7, 15).

The multiple pearly intraventricular lesions seen during neuroendoscopy raise another concern, as they may suggest persistent intraventricular deposits or possible epithelial remnants rather than passive lipid dissemination alone (9, 11). This possibility has prognostic relevance because viable epithelial remnants may continue to generate keratinous and lipid material, causing persistent CSF irritation, recurrent obstruction, or multifocal regrowth. Reports of hydrocephalus after dermoid cyst rupture support the concept that disseminated fat particles and inflammatory products can disturb CSF circulation, and some cases require CSF diversion or additional endoscopic intervention (15, 16).

The role of neuroendoscopy in this case also deserves emphasis. Compared with conventional microsurgery alone, neuroendoscopy provided direct visualization of intraventricular dissemination and allowed targeted debridement of the pearly lesions along the ventricular walls (11). It also permitted irrigation of oily debris and simultaneous management of obstructive hydrocephalus by third ventriculostomy and external ventricular drainage (11, 15). In a previously operated field, where adhesions and scarring may limit the safety and extent of open microsurgical dissection, neuroendoscopy may help inspect the CSF pathways more thoroughly while reducing additional surgical trauma (10, 11). In this patient, neuroendoscopic lesion debridement and third ventriculostomy were clinically reasonable because the dominant delayed problem was ventricular dissemination with obstructive hydrocephalus. This combined microscopic and neuroendoscopic strategy may therefore represent a useful treatment option for selected patients with ruptured or recurrent IDC involving the ventricular system (10, 11).

Conclusion

Spontaneous rupture of a clinically recurrent or residual IDC is extremely rare. In such cases, the risk may be related to the previously operated surgical field and scar tissue, unfavorable lesion location, and intrinsic fragility of the cyst wall. Once rupture occurs, it may lead to aseptic inflammation, neurovascular irritation or injury, refractory hydrocephalus, and secondary implantation. A better understanding of these risks is essential for long-term follow-up and timely intervention in patients with previously treated dermoid cysts.
