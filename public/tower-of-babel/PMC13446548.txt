Impact of Continuous Positive Airway Pressure Treatment on Retinal Vasculature in Obstructive Sleep Apnea: A 7-Year Follow-Up
Authors: Divya Rodrigues, Angela M. Schulz, Anna Britton, Brendon Wong, Jessica Tong, Claude S. Farah, Stuart L. Graham, Clare L. Fraser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIn previous studies, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) severity at diagnosis was independently associated with retinal arteriolar narrowing and reduced retinal pulse amplitude. Participants with severe untreated OSA showed progressive retinal arterial narrowing 2 years post diagnosis, while the use of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) ventilation appeared to attenuate these changes. This 7-year prospective study examines whether these protective effects of CPAP persist.MethodsWe invited 79 participants from the 2-year follow-up cohort for a 7-year review. Of these, 39 completed assessments: medical history, optical coherence tomography, and retinal vessel imaging. The retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness, central retinal artery equivalent (CRAE), central retinal vein equivalent (CRVE), and arteriovenous ratio were analyzed.ResultsCRAE and CRVE appeared greater in patients with severe OSA + CPAP than untreated OSA. Over 7 years, CRVE observably declined more slowly in severe those with OSA + CPAP than those with untreated severe OSA. Severe OSA + CPAP had a notable smaller decline in CRAE comparison with the control and untreated groups. The RNFL thickness was nonsignificantly reduced in severe OSA + no CPAP compared with those with CPAP in the superotemporal sector. Global RNFL rates of thinning were equivalent across groups. There was a trend for those not on CPAP to have higher rates of hypertension and stroke.ConclusionsIn this cohort, the cohort size was too small for robust statistics. It can still be broadly appreciated that CPAP provides long-term protective benefit against retinal arterial narrowing. The protective effect of CPAP on retinal vascular changes seen at the 2-year mark continued to trend in the mean CRAE values and was also potentially demonstrated in preservation of RNFL thickness in this 7-year study.

Oculomics is an emerging field that looks at the association between molecular, microscopic, and macroscopic ophthalmic biomarkers to screen and diagnose systemic diseases and identify disease risk.1 These biomarkers can provide objective parameters to aid in the assessment, prediction, and/or diagnosis of disease. They can also be used to assess treatment outcomes for diseases such as obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).1 OSA is defined by intermittent or complete collapse of the upper airways, leading to hypoxia, hypercapnia, and repeated arousals from sleep.2 The global prevalence of OSA among adults aged 30 to 69 years has been estimated at 936 million with mild disease and 425 million with moderate to severe disease.3 Risk factors include obesity, chronic nasal congestion, smoking, diabetes, a family history of sleep apnea, and asthma.4 The gold standard diagnostic test is overnight diagnostic polysomnography performed in a sleep laboratory.5 OSA has been associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, such as hypertension, stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and coronary heart disease.6 Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is the first-line treatment for OSA and highly effective in improving nocturnal oxygen saturation, as well as significantly improving quality-of-life factors such as daytime sleepiness.7

Qualitative retinal vascular changes have been previously reported in participants with OSA. In one study, severe OSA was associated with an increased likelihood of mild hypertensive retinopathy-like abnormalities on fundus photography, independent of systemic hypertension.8 Another study found that worsening OSA was associated with reduced vessel density at the superficial vascular complex, deep vascular complex, and optic nerve head, which was attenuated with CPAP use.9 Retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thinning has been independently associated with incident cardiovascular risk, as well as systemic factors including systolic blood pressure, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and hemoglobin A1c.10,11

In the first oculomics study on our OSA cohort, we assessed dynamic retinal vessel measurements and found that OSA disease severity at diagnosis was independently associated with retinal arteriolar narrowing and reduced pulse amplitude.12 At the 2-year follow-up, we demonstrated that there was progressive retinal arterial narrowing in severe untreated OSA, whereas treatment with CPAP appeared to attenuate retinal arterial narrowing.13

The aim of this study was to explore whether the correlation between retinal vascular findings using oculomics and CPAP compliance, as observed in the 2-year study demonstrating progressive retinal arterial narrowing in severe untreated OSA and attenuated retinal narrowing in CPAP users, was maintained beyond the 7-year time-point in the same cohort, and to further examine the effect of CPAP on retinovascular, cardiovascular. and cerebrovascular outcomes.

Methods

A prospective cohort study initially recruited 115 adult participants, who were referred for diagnostic polysomnography at Macquarie University Hospital in Sydney, Australia, between April 2015 and December 2016.12 Of these, 79 participants agreed to be reassessed 2 years later between May 2017 and February 2019 in the first follow-up study.14 All participants who had opted to participate at the 2-year visit were invited to attend a follow-up ophthalmic evaluation 7 years after their initial visit. Written consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved for prospective data collection by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Macquarie University and followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Demographics

Basic demographics were collected for each patient at the 7-year follow-up to include current body mass index, updates to medical and ocular history, including diagnosed hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), myocardial infarction, angina, and current medications. Blood pressure and heart rate were also recorded.

Polysomnography

Before the baseline study, participants had undergone diagnostic polysomnography to assess for OSA using standard criteria.12,13,15 Participants were initially stratified by the Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI) events per hour, placing them into four groups: normal or controls (<5 events/h), mild (5–14.9 events/h), moderate (15–29.9 events/h), and severe (≥30 events/h). Two participants were excluded from the current study, because OSA had resolved in one participant after upper airway surgery since the 2-year study and a control participant had subsequently developed OSA.

CPAP Therapy

Participants were offered CPAP therapy after the baseline sleep study at the discretion of the sleep medicine physician, who was blinded from the results of the ophthalmic study.13 CPAP adherence data were collected from individual CPAP machine memory cards, which covered the 90-day period before the follow-up visit. Information included the number of nights the device was in use, the duration of use per night, and the AHI. The most widely accepted adherence criterion is defined as use for at least 4 hours per night on 70% of nights.16 Those participants who did not meet this adherence criterion were classified as not being on CPAP. Participants not using any CPAP were also documented. Study participants were stratified into severe OSA + CPAP, severe OSA + no CPAP, mild/moderate OSA + CPAP, mild/moderate OSA + no CPAP, and controls. The mild and moderate stratified participants were combined into one group due to the small cohort size.

Ocular Assessment

Participants underwent ocular assessment including visual acuity, intraocular pressure, and slit-lamp examination, optical coherence tomography (OCT), then had their right eye dilated with 1% tropicamide.12,13

Static Vessel Caliber and RNFL

Retinal vessel analysis was performed as per the previous protocol.12,13 Briefly, static vessel caliber was calculated from fundus photographs using VesselMap2 (Imedos Systems, Jena, Germany), which calculated the central retinal artery equivalent (CRAE) and central retinal vein equivalent (CRVE). The ratio of these values was then calculated as the arteriovenous ratio (AVR)17 (Fig. 1).

Static vessel caliber. Each ring represents 1 disc diameter from the optic nerve head from the initial middle circle. The blue highlighted vessels in each quadrant are the retinal vein vessel diameter and the red highlighted vessels in each quadrant the retinal artery vessel diameter. The average of these vessels was used to calculate the CRAE and CRVE, which was used to calculate the AVR in the top left corner.

The RNFL was imaged using the ocular coherence tomography (OCT, Heidelberg Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), using a peripapillary optic nerve head scan. RNFL thinning has been associated with significant cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease.18 The RNFL value for each sector (temporal, superotemporal, superonasal, nasal, inferonasal, and inferotemporal) was recorded along with the global RNFL value. These were then compared across previous measures done at the initial and 2-year study marks as an exploratory analysis.

Two investigators were responsible for all the quantitative measurements from the fundus photographs and OCT in our study (DR, AS). They were not blinded to the patient categorization due to collection of demographic data. Measures for arteriolar and venular diameters were repeated to reduce any sampling error. Fundus photography analysis has shown strong intragrader reliability, evidenced by weighted kappa values of 0.80 for arteriolar caliber and 0.93 for venular caliber.19

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Stata/MP 18.0 software (for Mac [Apple Silicon] College Station, TX, USA). Individual sample t-tests were performed to primarily compare the CRAE, CRVE, and AVR for severe OSA + CPAP vs severe OSA + no CPAP. Ancillary t-tests were conducted comparing CRAE, CRVE, and AVR of the control group against each stratification. Mild/moderate OSA + CPAP vs. mild/moderate OSA + no CPAP were also compared. Key comparative t-tests were performed for severe OSA + CPAP vs severe OSA + no CPAP for RNFL, globally and for each individual sector. Additionally, t-tests were conducted for the control group against each OSA stratification with or without CPAP and mild/moderate OSA + CPAP against mild/moderate + no CPAP for global and sector RNFLs. A linear regression and t-tests were used to compare each mean global RNFL within each stratification to predict the decline or preservation of the RNFL thickness based on the stratification and CPAP use across the 7 years. Each mean RNFL linear regression slope was additionally compared using t-tests against the expected decrease in RNFL thickness per year. The widely accepted measure for decline in RNFL thickness is 0.365 µm per year (range ±).20 Similarly, a linear regression and t-tests were used to compare the mean CRAE, CRVE, and AVR across three visits to predict significant decline or preservation.

Results

Of the 79 participants at the 2-year follow-up, 39 individuals agreed and were eligible to continue their participation in the study: 9 with severe OSA + CPAP, 8 with severe OSA + no CPAP, 3 with mild/moderate OSA + CPAP, 14 with mild/moderate OSA + no CPAP, and 5 controls (Fig. 2). Twelve participants were compliant with their CPAP use. The remaining 27 participants with OSA either did not receive CPAP therapy or had not been compliant with their CPAP according to the adherence criteria. The remaining 37 participants were either uncontactable, deceased, or declined to participate. Three participants were excluded due to worsening media opacity or recent ophthalmic procedures. Of those not participating, 28 were male, 4 were controls, 9 had mild OSA, 13 moderate OSA, and 14 severe OSA, showing dropout across all groups.

For the included participants, the mean age was 68.0 ±7.9 years (range, 53–89 years), with 26 males (66.6%) and 13 females (33.3%). The mean body mass index was 28.4 ± 5.3 kg/m2 (range, 20.8–39.8 kg/m2). The mean systolic blood pressure was 136 ± 18 mm Hg (range, 104–177mm Hg). The mean diastolic blood pressure was 84 ±13 mm Hg (range, 62–109 mm Hg). The mean heart rate was 70 ± 11 bpm (range, 51–98 bpm).

Cardiovascular and Cerebrovascular Characteristics

The average systolic and diastolic blood pressure was observably higher in those not on CPAP, in comparison with their CPAP-utilizing counterparts. There were more reports of hypertension in non-CPAP users. The only incidences of stroke or TIA occurred in the severe OSA + no CPAP and mild/moderate OSA + no CPAP groups. Higher incidences of peripheral vascular disease (17.9%) and hyperlipidemia (0.1%) were also noted in the mild/moderate OSA + no CPAP group (0.1%) in comparison with those using CPAP (Table 1). These numbers were too small for statistical analysis.

Discussion

In this study, we hypothesized that the protective effects of CPAP on retinal vessel changes would be maintained at the 7-year follow-up for our cohort of participants with OSA. Due to small sample size, from drop out, aging, and loss of eligibility, some individually stratified groups were too small to make significant statistical conclusions. Therefore, the following discussion is a descriptive analysis of our findings. Observably, progressive arterial narrowing was seen in those with severe OSA who were noncompliant with or not using CPAP. The protective effect of CPAP is also potentially seen in the trend toward preservation of RNFL thickness in those with severe OSA, at least in the superotemporal sector. TIA and stroke were only reported in participants not using CPAP. Hypertension was also more frequently reported in non-CPAP users. To our knowledge, this is the first study to show objective retinal changes over long-term follow-up between CPAP-treated and inadequately or untreated OSA participants. Similar to our baseline study and 2-year follow-up, this study could guide further evidence gathering and provide data for follow-up studies that are looking at oculomic assessment of retinal vessels, serving as an indirect indicator of systemic cardiovascular risk in participants with OSA.12,13

CRAE appeared narrower in severe OSA participants without CPAP compared with those on treatment. Over 7 years, a slower decline in CRAE could be seen in individuals with severe OSA on CPAP than in those without CPAP. Retinal arterial narrowing has previously been studied within the context of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease. These studies have demonstrated that retinal arterial narrowing is associated with an increased stroke risk.14,21–23 Another study found that a narrower retinal arterial outer diameter and retinal arterial lumen diameter were significantly associated with an increased severity of ischemic heart disease.24 Evidence also indicates a connection between retinal arterial narrowing and coronary heart disease in women over men.25 These retinal vessel changes are similar to those seen with hypertensive retinopathy, which is known to be a strong predictor of coronary artery disease.26,27 Dynamic retinal vessel changes have been reported in individuals with untreated moderate to severe OSA and associated with signs of increased arterial stiffness and retinal microvascular dysfunction, even without apparent cardiovascular disease.28 We hypothesize that the observable retinal arteriolar narrowing seen in the untreated OSA participants in our cohort represents a potentially significant biomarker for cardiovascular risk, including heart disease and stroke, in a larger cohort size. Participants treated with CPAP in this study are not reportedly different in these markers compared with control participants, thereby alluding to CPAP being protective against the vascular damage of OSA.

However, there are studies that have failed to show a clear link between retinal arterial changes and OSA. One study reported an association between severe OSA and retinal microaneurysms only in females, and another found that only males with a categorization of moderate to severe OSA were more likely to have retinal arteriolar narrowing and venular widening.29,30 A study on sleep-disordered breathing and retinal microvascular diameter found that higher apnea–hypopnea indices correlated with greater venular dilatation, but there was no association with retinal arteriolar narrowing.31 Although these studies were larger, they did not have the longitudinal follow-up of our study to show how these changes progress over time and how they improve with CPAP therapy. Because this is a disease process, our study provides data and insight to better address the changes over time that can be seen and the long-term effects that may not be able to be addressed in these referenced studies.

CRVE changes were not seen to be significantly different in groups being treated with CPAP compared with controls in our study at the 7-year mark, although they appeared to be attenuated in severe OSA + CPAP in comparison with severe OSA + no CPAP. However, those suffering from severe OSA treated with CPAP can be noted to have a slower decline to CRVE in comparison with those with severe OSA + no CPAP treatment. Other work has shown that, in women, those with a narrower CRVE were three-fold more likely to suffer from microvascular angina than those with a larger venular caliber.32 This difference was not seen in men. The Wisconsin Sleep Study showed a greater difference in venous measures in severe OSA, which we did not find in this study.31 The mild/moderate OSA + no CPAP group outcomes are observably similar to those of the CPAP-utilizing group. This finding is likely due to the small numbers in the CPAP-utilizing group being insufficient to provide any true analysis on, as well as the combining of mild and moderate OSA into one group due to small numbers. Those on the lower end of mild AHI events are coupled with those on the greater end of moderate AHI events, therefore skewing outcomes in both categories. Future studies would benefit from having a larger sample size to be able to split these groups and be able to analyze the stratifications individually.

We observed that those on CPAP therapy with severe OSA had thicker RNFLs in the superotemporal in comparison with those with nontreated OSA. Other studies have shown similar findings with CPAP improving RNFL thickness but in the nasal-inferior and inferior quadrants.33 Another study showed, using a regression analysis, that RNFL thickness had a significant linear relationship with the AHI, especially in the nasal and superior quadrants, which we were not able to demonstrate in our study.34

Our study suggests that individuals with severe OSA who are compliant with CPAP therapy experience a slowdown or reversal of retinal vascular changes at 7 years after diagnosis, which should correlate with a lower cardiovascular risk. Recent studies have shown that CPAP has a positive impact on decreasing total cholesterol, lowered blood pressure (especially nighttime), slowing the progression of diabetic retinopathy, decreasing serum homocysteine and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein biochemical markers, and overall decreasing fatal and nonfatal cardiovascular events.35–39

The proposed mechanism for retinovascular changes due to recurrent apneas and hypopneas in OSA are thought to have two mechanisms: intermittent hypoxia and sympathetic nervous system dysfunction. It is proposed that acute hypoxia triggers peripheral chemoreceptor activity, which increases sympathetic nervous system activity, causes episodic systemic hypertension, and results in long-term structural changes in both the retinal and systemic vasculature.40,41 Additionally, the sudden increases in mean arterial pressure are succeeded by sharp drops in blood pressure and cerebral blood flow velocity at the end of apneic episodes.42,43 This pattern is similar to an ischemia–reperfusion injury, which triggers the inflammatory cascade and suppresses nitric oxide release, resulting in ischemic damage and endothelial dysfunction.44–46

Evidence suggests that CPAP improves endothelial function—enhancing nitric-oxide–mediated vasorelaxation and restoring nitric oxide bioavailability.47,48 The therapy potentially also reduces the sympathetic nerve response to hypoxic episodes as well as overall basal sympathetic nervous system activity.49 These theories tie in with the observations of our study.

We acknowledge some potential limitations in the study. Nearly one-half of our initial cohort was lost to follow-up partly due to the long time lapse with this being a follow-up study conducted 7 years later, which encompassed the COVID pandemic years. This smaller cohort size limits the statistical power to perform more robust analyses, particularly with the control group and linear regression analysis. For the same reason, the mild and moderate OSA severity groups were combined for the current analysis. Having only three participants in the mild/moderate + CPAP category also meant that an ANOVA could not be performed, because there were not enough participants. Although t-tests could be performed on those small numbers, as demonstrated by the results, having only three participants results in a large standard deviation, impacting the statistical power. This result is made apparent when Bonferroni adjustments were applied to all of our t-tests, providing few statistically significant findings. Similarly, the untreated mild-to-moderate OSA group demonstrated a slower decline in mean CRVE compared with controls. This finding should be interpreted with caution given the smaller cohort size and the pooling of participants with mild and moderate disease severity. Additionally, changes within the control group over the 7-year follow-up period may have influenced these results. Specifically, some control participants may have developed incident OSA that was neither formally diagnosed nor treated during the study period. For example, one participant reported symptoms suggestive of OSA at follow-up; however, in the absence of a formal diagnosis, they did not meet the predefined criteria for reclassification and were, therefore, retained within the control group. Consequently, undetected incident OSA within the control cohort may have attenuated differences between groups. Nevertheless, our observations demonstrate a beneficial effect of CPAP on retinal arterial vessels in the treated severe OSA group. The lack of a similar magnitude of benefit in milder severity may be a false-negative finding given our small sample size. Unlike our previous studies, which found reduced AVR in conjunction with worsening OSA, the numbers were too small in this study to continue to associate it with CVD risk.

Media opacity is a limitation with any ocular assessment, and although several participants underwent refractive surgery, there were a few who did not, affecting the quality of imaging and reducing the population size further.

Disease burden and comorbidity are limitations and very challenging to control with any longitudinal observational cohort study; they may have a confounding effect on our results. Due to the small sample size, we were unable to perform multivariable adjustment for disease comorbidity for hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipemia, stroke, TIA, peripheral vascular disease, myocardial infarction, or other vascular diseases. However, the healthy adherer affect in the clinical management of sleep apnea with CPAP suggests that individuals who are compliant with their CPAP tend to engage in other healthy behaviors, reducing their other risk factors, which would affect our results. This is in keeping with our reported findings of higher incidences of stroke and HTN in severe OSA + no CPAP users than severe OSA + CPAP.50

CPAP adherence data were based on the 90 days preceding the ophthalmic visit. Continuous CPAP adherence monitoring over the entire 7-year period from initial diagnosis was not feasible. The assessment for compliance is consistent with standard clinical practice and likely representative of patient compliance over the prior years. In a 10-year CPAP adherence study of patients with moderate to severe OSA, 63.3% were estimated to remain adherent for 10 years, with continued adherence plateauing at 76 months.51 Additionally, our prior study at the 2-year mark provides a snapshot of their compliance from earlier years. All patients who stated that they were compliant with CPAP use were only considered adherence if their CPAP data showed compliance in that 90-day period. There were two participants who stated compliance and CPAP adherence criteria were not met. The remaining participants who met CPAP adherence also clearly stated that they had always used their CPAP since they first began on it. Several participants reported at intake that they had resumed CPAP only for study participation or used it for a few hours per night, failing adherence criteria and were therefore not stratified as CPAP compliant. However, we acknowledge that the snapshot strategy has its limitations. The 7-year period compliant participants may have had intermittent use, and adherence was self-reported, potentially affecting the observed consistency and magnitude of CPAP on ophthalmic outcomes. On the contrary, there are participants who are currently noncompliant but utilized CPAP for a few of the years before this study. This may have also had some level of effect on our measurements, actually reducing the differences seen between the groups on analysis. Therefore, the effect of CPAP may be stronger than our data can show.

Conclusions

This study suggests that CPAP treatment in participants with OSA can have long-term protective effects on retinal vasculature over a 7-year period in comparison with those who do not use CPAP. These vascular changes are a known predictor of cardiovascular disease. As the first long-term study showing retinal vascular differences between treated and untreated participants, it supports the hypothesis that CPAP therapy improves hypoxia and attenuates retinal vascular narrowing in severe OSA and is a relevant measurable predictor of cardiovascular health. The trends seen and data collected in our study can be helpful to future meta-analysis or study designs with larger cohort sizes being conducted that look at biomarkers in the retina, such as OCT angiography or dynamic vessel imaging to assess the effects of retinovasculature and how it can predict the extent of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease.
