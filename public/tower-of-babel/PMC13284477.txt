Concurrent Mirizzi syndrome and choledocholithiasis: imaging features and surgical correlation
Authors: Fidel Rampersad, Jason Diljohn, Toi Johnson, Dawn-Marie Fields, Sandeep Parbhu, Ravi Maharaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe a case of concurrent Mirizzi syndrome and choledocholithiasis in a woman in her mid-60s. The abnormalities were first identified on abdominal ultrasound and subsequently confirmed with contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP). Imaging demonstrated extrinsic compression of the common hepatic duct (CHD) by an impacted gallstone at the cystic duct along with concomitant calculi in the distal common bile duct (CBD). The patient underwent open cholecystectomy with CBD exploration, choledochoscopy, and choledochoduodenostomy. This case emphasizes the essential role of cross-sectional imaging in detecting coexisting biliary pathologies and facilitating accurate preoperative diagnosis, which is essential for optimal surgical planning and improved patient outcomes.Keywords: Mirizzi syndrome, Gallbladder, Calculus, Choledocholithiasis, Computed tomography, Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography

Introduction

Mirizzi syndrome is an uncommon complication of chronic gallstone disease, arising from extrinsic compression of the common hepatic or common bile duct by an impacted stone in the cystic duct or gallbladder neck. This results in varying degrees of biliary obstruction and can progress to obstructive jaundice. In Europe and the United States, it accounts for approximately 0.3%-1.4% of patients with gallstone disease [1]. It is seen more frequently in women between 50 and 70 years of age [2] and is often underdiagnosed before surgery, where the consequences for operative management are substantial.

Concurrent Mirizzi syndrome and choledocholithiasis is uncommon and may present a significant diagnostic and surgical challenge due to overlapping clinical and imaging findings. Preoperative recognition of both entities is important, as failure to identify them may increase the risk of biliary injury and influence surgical management. This case highlights the complementary role of ultrasound, CECT, and MRCP in establishing the diagnosis preoperatively and facilitating operative planning.

Findings

A woman in her mid-60s presented to the adult emergency department with a 6-day history of fever, right upper abdomen pain, nausea, intermittent vomiting, and dark urine. She denied pale stools, pruritus, hematemesis, or weight loss. Her past medical history was significant for longstanding cholelithiasis managed conservatively. She had no prior hepatobiliary surgery and no known family history of hepatobiliary malignancy or gallstone-related complications.

On examination, she was febrile (38.2°C), icteric, and tender in the right upper quadrant with a positive Murphy sign. Laboratory investigations demonstrated elevated total bilirubin of 9.8 mg/dL (reference range: 0.3-1.2 mg/dL), direct bilirubin of 4.8 mg/dL (reference range: 0-0.3 mg/dL), alkaline phosphatase of 724 IU/L (reference range: 44-147 IU/L), alanine aminotransferase of 416 IU/L (reference range: 7-56 IU/L), and aspartate aminotransferase of 237.8 IU/L (reference range: 10-40 IU/L), consistent with an obstructive cholestatic pattern.

An abdominal ultrasound was performed and demonstrated features of acute cholecystitis including mild gallbladder wall thickening (0.5 cm) and dependent gallstones (Fig. 1). The patient also demonstrated a positive sonographic Murphy sign. Further evaluation demonstrated a large hyperechoic calculus in the region of the cystic duct, with associated upstream biliary dilatation (Fig. 2). The distal CBD was also dilated; however, no distal CBD calculus was visualized with ultrasound.

Grey scale ultrasound image of the right upper quadrant demonstrating gallbladder wall thickening (pink arrowhead) and dependent gallstones at the gallbladder neck (green arrow).

Grey scale ultrasound image of the right upper quadrant showing a large gallstone impacted in the region of the cystic duct (white arrow), with dilatation of the common hepatic duct (yellow arrowhead) and common bile duct (green arrowhead).

A contrast-enhanced CT scan of abdomen and pelvis was subsequently performed for further evaluation. The scan demonstrated a small hyperdense calculus (0.7 × 0.8 cm) within the distal CBD at the level of the ampulla of Vater, consistent with a choledocholith (Fig. 3). A larger impacted gallstone was also noted at the cystic duct, causing marked extrinsic compression of the adjacent CHD, with resultant upstream mild bilobar intrahepatic duct dilatation (Fig. 3). Irregular gallbladder wall thickening (0.7 cm) and surrounding fat stranding were also present, consistent with acute on chronic cholecystitis. There was no pneumobilia, perforation, or pericholecystic collection. These findings were in keeping with Csendes Type I Mirizzi syndrome with concurrent distal CBD stone.

A calcified choledocholith is also present (red arrow).

MRCP performed 4 days later corroborated the CT findings, confirming both the impacted cystic duct calculus and distal choledocholithiasis (Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

Axial T2 weighted MRI image of the upper abdomen showing a large gallstone at the cystic duct (white arrow) causing severe mass effect on the common hepatic duct (orange arrowhead).

Coronal T2 weighted MRI image of the abdomen demonstrating a large gallstone impacted at the cystic duct (white arrow) and 2 choledocholiths within the distal common bile duct (red arrows). There is mild bilobar intrahepatic bile duct dilatation (blue arrowheads).

Exploratory laparotomy revealed dense inflammatory adhesions surrounding the gallbladder, correlating with chronic inflammatory changes suggested on imaging. A retrograde cholecystectomy was performed, revealing an impacted 2.5-cm stone tightly lodged in the cystic duct and producing marked extrinsic compression of the CHD, consistent with Csendes Type I Mirizzi syndrome. Several additional gallstones were removed. Choledochoscopy via the cystic duct demonstrated patency of the right and left hepatic ducts, however distal advancement was limited due to resistance, raising concern for a distal biliary stricture. To ensure adequate biliary drainage, a choledochoduodenostomy was subsequently performed, and a surgical drain was placed at the anastomotic site.

Postoperatively, the patient demonstrated progressive clinical improvement with resolution of fever and jaundice. Liver enzyme and bilirubin levels gradually normalized, and she was discharged in stable condition with outpatient surgical follow-up.

Discussion

The gallbladder is anatomically divided into the fundus, body, infundibulum and neck. The infundibulum, which gradually narrows toward the neck, lies in close proximity to the common hepatic duct. Impaction of a large stone within this region may compress the adjacent bile duct resulting in biliary obstruction [1]. Early descriptions of partial duct obstruction due to impacted gallstones date back to Kehr in 1905 [3]. However, Mirizzi in 1948 provided a more comprehensive description of the clinical and radiological features of this entity, leading to its eponymous designation [3].

Although Mirizzi syndrome is itself uncommon, the concurrent presence of Mirizzi syndrome and distal choledocholithiasis is considerably rarer and may complicate both diagnosis and operative management. Similar reports in the literature emphasize that concomitant distal CBD stones may be overlooked when attention is focused primarily on the impacted cystic duct calculus and associated biliary obstruction. In our case, multimodality imaging allowed accurate preoperative identification of both pathologies, thereby facilitating appropriate surgical planning and avoiding potential intraoperative biliary injury.

Mirizzi syndrome remains a noteworthy diagnosis because its clinical presentation is often nonspecific and may overlap with other hepatobiliary conditions, including acute cholecystitis, cholangitis, and acute pancreatitis. Patients typically present with right upper-quadrant or epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting, fever, dark urine, and anorexia [1]. The Csendes classification divides Mirizzi syndrome into 5 types. Type I, as demonstrated in our patient, is characterized by external compression of the bile duct without fistula formation. Types II-IV reflect progressive cholecystobiliary fistula formation due to erosion of the bile duct wall. Type V includes a cholecystoenteric fistula in association with any of the other subtypes.

Ultrasound remains the first-line imaging modality for suspected gallstone disease because it is readily available and highly sensitive for cholelithiasis. However, ultrasound may be limited in evaluating distal CBD calculi and in defining the precise relationship between an impacted cystic duct stone and the adjacent biliary tree. In this case, ultrasound successfully identified upstream biliary dilatation and the impacted cystic duct calculus but could not confidently demonstrate the distal CBD stone. Subsequent CECT provided additional anatomical detail, identified the distal choledocholithiasis, excluded an underlying hepatobiliary malignancy, and demonstrated surrounding inflammatory change. MRCP further delineated the biliary anatomy and confirmed both the extrinsic compression of the CHD and distal CBD obstruction.

Early and accurate radiologic identification of Mirizzi syndrome is crucial because operative anatomy is frequently distorted by chronic inflammation, edema, fibrosis, and adhesions. In this case, careful radiologic assessment referred not merely to routine imaging acquisition, but to the deliberate evaluation of the relationship between the impacted cystic duct calculus, the CHD, and the distal CBD. Recognition of concurrent distal choledocholithiasis was particularly important because it altered operative management and prompted CBD exploration and choledochoduodenostomy. Failure to recognize these findings preoperatively may increase the risk of iatrogenic bile duct injury and incomplete biliary decompression.
