De novo production of (−)-limonene, (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone via engineering Rhodotorula toruloides
Authors: Yaqi Dong, Simin Cui, Biao Du, Xiaofei Zhang, Jin Hou, Xinping Lin, Yi Qin, Xue Wang, Xiaobing Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDe novo biosynthesis of (−)-limonene, (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone was achieved via rewiring the mevalonate pathway in the oleaginous yeast Rhodotorula toruloides. Strategies including enzyme selection, protein fusion, pathway optimization (MVA upstream enhancement, cofactor supply), and fermentation condition optimization were applied to improve the monoterpenoids production. Finally, the (−)-limonene titer of 1.6 g/L and (−)-carveol of 668.0 mg/L were achieved through fed-batch fermentation in a 3-L bioreactor. Furthermore, the feasibility of biosynthesis of (−)-carvone was also demonstrated. This study represents an example for de novo microbial production of (−)-limonene, (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone with the oleaginous red yeast R. toruloides, demonstrating its potential as a versatile chassis for monoterpenoid biosynthesis.Keywords: Cell factory, Biosynthesis, Monoterpene, Limonene derivatives, Red yeast

Introduction

Isoprene-derived monoterpenes find broad applications in food, pharmaceuticals, fragrances [1,2]. Among all the monoterpenes, (+)-limonene and (−)-limonene are enantiomers that possess distinct aromatic attributes [3]. While (+)-limonene is directly used as flavors, solvents and industrial feedstocks, (−)-limonene serves as precursors for valuable oxygenated monoterpenoids, such as (−)-menthol and perillic acid, which exhibits more desirable fragrance properties, significant economic and pharmacological values [[4], [5], [6]].

The traditional production of limonene and its oxygenated derivatives from plant sources usually faces uncertainties caused by climate variability, environmental changes, and cultivation area constraints. Additionally, chemical-based synthesis route suffers from compromised product selectivity, environmental pollution, and toxic impurities [7]. The rapid advance in synthetic biology and metabolic engineering offers an alternative strategy for the sustainable synthesis of high-value natural products [8]. Microbial production of limonene and its derivatives, such as perillyl alcohol, perillic acid and (+)-carveol, have been documented in Escherichia coli [9], Candida tropicalis [6], Saccharomyces cerevisiae [1,10], Yarrowia lipolytica [11], and Rhodotorula toruloides [7]. However, the de novo microbial production of (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone has been rarely reported (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3).

The microbial production of (−)-carvone.

For facilitating monoterpenoids microbial production, the commonly employed strategies include: (i) screening natural enzymes with desired efficiency [6,17]; (ii) engineering enzymes through rational design or directed evolution [18]; (iii) fusing sequential and determinant enzymes to avoid metabolite flux leakage and accelerate substrate transformation [19]; (iv) optimizing the key knots in the biosynthesis pathway while down-tuning the competitive route to streamline precursor and intermediates supply toward downstream conversion [6]; (v) engineering the redox balance by recycling the essential cofactors like NAD(P)H [8]. Besides the integrated use of the metabolic engineering approaches, an appropriate microbial host can also contribute to the establishment of a monoterpenoid cell factory.

Although it currently has a less advanced genetic editing toolbox, the red yeast Rhodotorula toruloides is exhibiting attractive properties as a promising microbial cell factory for terpenoid production with the fast development of synthetic biology and metabolic engineering technology. Importantly, it harbors rich pool of acetyl-CoA and NADPH, broad substrate spectrum and outstanding high-density fermentation capacity. Besides, it has stronger robustness than other oleaginous yeasts such as Y. lipolytica in harsh conditions, which offers the advantage of refining low-cost feedstocks, such as non-detoxified hydrolysates of agricultural lignocellulosic biomass, toward value-added products [[20], [21], [22]]. Notably, it exhibits high tolerance to up to 2.0 g/L limonene, making it promising for limonene and derivatives production [22]. Additionally, it has been successfully engineered to produce a wide range of valuable compounds such as linalool, lipids and carotenoids [[23], [24], [25]]. Taken together, the natural attributes mentioned above position R. toruloides as a highly interesting chassis for sustainable monoterpene production.

In this work, de novo biosynthesis of (−)-limonene and its derivatives (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone was achieved in R. toruloides through key enzyme selection, (−)-limonene synthases ((−)-LS) and carveol dehydrogenases (CDHs), protein fusion, synthesis pathway regulation, and fermentation condition optimization. Ultimately, fed-batch fermentation resulted in a (−)-limonene titer of 1.6 g/L, 668.0 mg/L (−)-carveol, and we also showed the feasibility of de novo production of (−)-carvone, respectively. Our results demonstrate the potential of R. toruloides as a promising microbial platform for valuable monoterpenoids production.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

Plasmid construction and propagation were conducted with E. coli DH5α in Luria−Bertani (LB) medium, supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin at 37 °C. LB medium was also used for cultivation of Agrobacterium tumefaciens AGL1 at 28 °C. The haploid R. toruloides NP11 was donated by Prof. Zongbao Zhao. If not specified, the yeast strains were cultured in nitrogen-limiting (NL) medium that were kept at 22 °C and 180 rpm for a time range as needed. Antibiotics (G418, nourseothricin, or zeocin, 50 μg/mL) were used for identifying yeast transformants in both liquid and solid YPD media (20 g/L agar was added into liquid YPD).

The medium of LB, YPD, NL, and minimal (MM) were prepared according to our previous report [26]. Induction medium (IM) containing 200 μL of acetosyringone was prepared as described [27].

Plasmid construction

The vector pZPK was employed to construct all the necessary expression cassettes in this study [27]. The plasmids and primers used in this study are provided in supplementary materials. The DNA Ligation Kit Ver.2.1 (Takara) and In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Takara) were employed for constructing the vectors. All the genes used in this study were optimized according to the codon bias of R. toruloides and synthesized by Synbio Technologies (Suzhou, P. R. China). The codon usage of the heterologous genes was redressed according to the preference of R. toruloides and produced by Synbio Technologies (Suzhou, China). The heterologous (−)-LS genes were CstLS from Cannabis sativa (NP_001384867.1), EttLS from Eleutherococcus trifoliatus (AIO10970.1), MltLS from Mentha longifolia (WAB06559.1), MstLS from Mentha spicata (QBA83293.1), PatLS from Pinus armandii Franch (UNY86182.1), and PstLS from Picea abies (AAS47694.1). The carvone dehydrogenase genes were AbCDH from Actinobacteria bacterium (MGW3291715.1), MpCDH from Mentha × piperita (QPI35669.1), ReCDH from Rhodococcus erythropolis (WP_076949156.1), ShCDH from Streptomyces himastatinicus (WP_009719709.1), MtCDH from Mycolicibacterium thermoresistibile (WP_448509721.1) and MysCDH from Mycobacterium sp (HYJ57287.1). The acetoacetyl-CoA synthase NphT7, (D7URV0.1), Acetyl-CoA acetyl-transferase/HMGR (EfMvaE, AF290092), HMG-CoA synthase (EfMvaS, AF290092), Mevalonate kinase (MmMK, AAM31458). The native ATP-citrate lyase (ACL, RHTO_03915) was cloned from the cDNA of R. toruloides NP11.

Construction of recombinant R. toruloides

R. toruloides transformation was conducted through the A. tumefaciens mediated transformation (ATMT) according to the reported method [28]. The resultant strains were streaked onto YPD selection plates, kept at 28 °C, for five successive generations to select the stable ones for further investigation.

Fermentation of engineered strains

For flask fermentation, colonies were pre-cultured in 50-mL test tubes, containing 5 mL of YPD medium, at 28 °C, 180 rpm until OD600 reached 10–12. Then, they were inoculated at an initial OD600 of 0.5 in the 250-mL shake flasks (containing 50 mL NL, YPD or MM medium) and cultivated at 22 °C and 180 rpm for 120 h. The carbon source used for production was 20 g/L glucose. Isopropyl myristate (IPM) was used as an overlay 20% (v/v) for the instant extraction of (−)-limonene, (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone.

For fermentation on 500-mL and 3-L bioreactors (Bailun Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), the seed culture was prepared as for the flask fermentation and inoculated with a ratio of 10% (v/v). Initially, the initial fermentation medium contained (g/L) glucose 20, yeast extract 1, (NH4)2SO4 1.54, MgSO4·7H2O 1.5, KH2PO4 1, Na2HPO4·12H2O 1, and supplemented with 1 mL/L trace metal solution and 1 mL/L vitamin solution. Working parameters were 25 °C, dissolved oxygen ≥20% and pH 5.6. IPM was added in a ratio of 10% (v/v). The feeding solution contained (g/L) glucose 400, KH2PO4 9, K2SO4 3.5, Na2SO4 0.28, MgSO4·7H2O 2.5, 10 mL/L of trace metal solution, and 12 mL/L of vitamin solution. Carbon to nitrogen ratio (mol to mol) was adjusted by the amount of (NH4)2SO4 added to the fermentation broth as needed. The feeding solution was added when glucose was about 5 g/L.

Extraction and quantification of (−)-limonene, (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone

Samples were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 min and dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 [19]. Gas chromatography (GC) analysis was performed on GC-2014C (Shimadzu, Japan) using a HP-5 column of 30 m, 0.32 mm, and 0.25 μm (Kromat), flame ionization detector (FID), and splitting ratio of 30:1. The carrier gas was nitrogen (1.0 mL/min), and the sample loading volume was 1 μL. The operating program for the oven initial temperature was set at 50 °C, ramped at 5 °C/min to 150 °C, and held for 5 min. The injector and detector were kept at 250 and 280 °C, respectively. The monoterpenoid standards were obtained from Macklin and used to quantify (−)-limonene, (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone.

Cell growth and residual sugars determination

Cell density was measured at 600 nm with a UV spectrophotometer (Evolution 220, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The fermentation broth was centrifuged at 9000 g for 2 min, and the supernatant was used for determining the residual sugar using a dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method [29].

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and Origin 2021 and presented as mean ± standard deviations. One-way ANOVA and Duncan multiple comparison were employed for statistical analysis. ns, no significant difference; ∗, p < 0.05; ∗∗, p < 0.01; ∗∗∗, p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗, p < 0.0001. All the experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Results and discussion

Construction of the (−)-limonene biosynthesis baseline

Limonene can be synthesized through the MVA and/or MEP pathway with geranyl diphosphate (GPP) and/or NPP as the direct precursor [19]. The biosynthesis of limonene is controlled by several rate-limiting enzyme, such as HMGR, IDI, GPP and/or NPP synthase and limonene synthase (LS) [6,7]. Moreover, fermentation conditions influence the cell viability and the final chemical productivity. Particularly, the carbon flux will be mostly directed towards lipid biosynthesis in R. toruloides under nutrient depleted conditions, such as nitrogen limitation [30]. Fortunately, we have previously constructed a MVA, NPP and fusion protein based limonene biosynthesis route, developed a mutant FPPS from R. toruloides (FPPSF91W/N122W/K192G, hereafter denoted as FPPS∗∗∗) to boost the efficient linalool synthesis in R. toruloides and isolated a strong promoter pLDP in NL medium [22].

Considering that the catalytic efficiency of the (−)-limonene synthase ((−)-LS) is crucial for high-yield biosynthesis, we first employed the most reported (−)-LS from Mentha spicata (MstLS) to construct the (−)-limonene synthesis pathway [8,10]. It was fused to the FPPS∗∗∗ (FPPS∗∗∗-MstLS) with the GGGS linker, the most commonly used strategy to enhance the precuror transfer and limonene production [8]. To ensure the precursor supply, the critical enzymes HMGR was linked with FPPS∗∗∗-MstLS by P2A peptide and overexpressed simultaneously using the strong promoter pLDP1 (Fig. 2). Then, five other distinct (−)-LSs (Phylogenetic analysis, Fig. S2A) were fused to FPPS∗∗∗ with GGGS linker and compared for their capacity in supporting (−)-limonene production. The results showed that MstLS was the most effective among all the candidates (Fig. 2A). To further verify our results, we investigated the limonene production with the fusion protein (MstLS-FPPS∗∗∗), and separate expression of MstLS and FPPS∗∗∗ mediated by P2A peptide. We also managed to constructed the nerol pyrophosphate (NPP) based pathway (Fig. S1). Interestingly, in terms of (−)-limonene production, the correspongding results confirmed that the mutant FPPS∗∗∗ outperformed NPPS, and the fusion protein FPPS∗∗∗-MstLS (linked by GGGS) outperformed the fused MstLS-FPPS∗∗∗ and P2A mediated expression, and MstLS gained advantage over other candidates (Fig. 2 and Fig. S1). The resultant strain SL2 with the MstLS produced 19.8 mg/L (−)-limonene in shake flask in NL medium (Fig. 2A). The results also showed that the NL medium was more suitable than YPD and MM for (−)-limonene production (Fig. 2D). Consequently, the fusion protein FPPS∗∗∗-MstLS and NL medium were utilized for the subsequent experiments if not specified.

Strengthening the upper pathway and NADPH supply leads to enhanced (−)-limonene production

Optimizing the design of metabolic pathways is paramount for achieving efficient microbial synthesis of desired products. The MVA-based limonene synthesis pathway is initiated with the acetoacetyl-CoA, which is produced from condensing acetyl-CoA by gate-keeper enzyme ACCT, or acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA by the acac-CoA synthase (NphT7) from Streptomyces sp. (Fig. 1, Fig. 3A) [31,32].

R. toruloides has excellent acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA pools to support lipid production.18 To strengthen the acetyl-CoA metabolic flux toward (−)-limonene production, we overexpressed the ACCT gene, fused with a RIDD peptide (ACCT-RIDD), and another generally regarded rate-limiting enzyme IDI in the strain SL2 [33,34]. The resultant strain SL4 elevated the (−)-limonene production to 51.0 mg/L, a 2.6-fold increase over SL2 (Fig. 3A). To further facilitate the acetyl-CoA (Ac-CoA) flux toward the mevalonate (MVA) pathway, we overexpressed the major acetyl-CoA producer ACL in the oleaginous R. toruloides, fused with a RIAD peptide (ACL-RIAD). Then, the designed ACCT-RIDD and ACL-RIAD should auto-assemble with a ratio of 2:1 to direct the Ac-CoA flow toward MVA route [35]. However, the implementation of this strategy in strain SL4 resulted in no significant change in (−)-limonene production (SL5, Fig. 3A), which might be attributed to the thermodynamic reversibility of the ACCT-catalyzed condensation reaction (ΔG0'≈7 kcal/mol) [36]. Elevated Acac-CoA concentration may promote the thiolase activity of ACCT, and drive the reaction to a metabolically futile cycle between Ac-CoA and Acac-CoA [32].

Then, we introduced the acac-CoA synthase NphT7, and the mevalonate synthase EfMvaSA110G from Enterococcus faecalis and the mevolonate kinase MmMK from Methanosarcina mazei into SL5 for exploring the acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA and enhancing the MVA metabolic flux [[37], [38], [39]]. The employment of NphT7, EfMvaSA110G and MmMK (T7-S∗-K) improved the (−)-limonene titer to 57.5 mg/L (SL6, Fig. 3A), representing a 13% increase over the parental SL4. When the five genes (ACCT-RIDD, ACL-RIAD, NphT7, EfMvaSA110G and MmMK) were simultaneously overexpressed in SL4 (Fig. 1, Fig. 3A), the resultant SL7 produced 63.0 mg/L (−)-limonene titer. The overexpression of the native PMK and PDC in SL7 increased (−)-limonene to 78.5 mg/L (SL8), representing a 24.6% improvement over SL7.

Enhancing the expression of terminal and critical gene would streamline the biosynthesis efficiency of the whole pathway. Thus, we introduced an additional copy of the fused protein FPPS∗∗∗(−)LS into SL8 to construct SL9, which achieved a (−)-limonene titer of 127.6 mg/L, a 63% increase over the parental SL8 (Fig. 3A). Incorporation of a third copy of the fused FPPS∗∗∗(−)LS into the SL9 further boosted the (−)-limonene to 138.9 mg/L (SL10) (Fig. 3B). The biosynthesis and derivatization of limonene depend highly on the cycling of the reducing power NADPH that an enhanced isoprenoid pathway might lead to significant depletion of the cellular NADPH pool, thereby impairing the redox balance and whole pathway efficiency [[40], [41], [42]]. Enhancing the expression of the key NADPH producers in the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) can improve the NADPH recycling (Fig. 3C). Hence, we overexpressed the native phosphogluconate dehydrogenase encoding gene GND1 and the heterologous NADPH dependent glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase gene from Clostridium acetobutylicum (ClGAPDH). The introduction of ClGAPDH resulted in 155.5 mg/L (−)-limonene (SL11), representing a 12% increase over SL10 (Fig. 3D). In addition, overexpression of the endogenous GND1 in SL11 led to slight increase in the (−)-limonene titer to 158.0 mg/L (SL12, Fig. 3D). Nevertheless, the overexpression of the glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase gene (ZWF1) failed to further enhance the limonene synthesis (Fig. S5). This phenomenon was consistent with our previous result that the overexpression of ZWF1 to enhance NADPH supply in R. toruloides failed to boost patchoulol production [43]. The result herein indicated that the NADPH availability influences the (−)-limonene production. However, the limited increase indicated other limiting steps remained in the biosynthetic pathway.

(−)-Carveol biosynthesis from (−)-limonene

(−)-Carveol is a naturally oxygenated derivative of limonene, catalyzed by the cytochrome P450 enzyme (L6H) [44,45]. In addition, the L6H requires a cytochrome P450 reductase (CPR) to maintain its proper catalysis activity [46]. To convert (−)-limonene to (−)-carveol, we recruited the Mentha x gracilis originated L6H (MgL6H) and the CPR from Arabidopsis thaliana (AtCPR) by putting them under the native pLDP1 (Fig. 4), which have been demonstrated efficiency for limonene derivatives. Considering that the plant-derived P450s exhibit significant challenges for heterologous expression, including low catalytic efficiency and frequent substrate promiscuity [47], we strategically avoided using the strain SL12 with the highest (−)-limonene titer as the chassis cell. Instead, the medium strain SL4 that produced 51.0 mg/L (−)-limonene was chosen to circumvent excessive precursor accumulation.

As expected, when the MgL6H and AtCPR were co-integrated into the genome of SL4, the resultant strain SC1 produced 22.9 mg/L (−)-carveol at an 80.6% ratio, with 5.2 mg/L (−)-limonene left (Fig. 4C) in NL medium at 22 °C and 180 rpm for 120 h. The accumulation of (−)-limonene indicated that the (−)-carveol synthesis enzyme activity was less efficient and required further optimization. Introduction of an additional copy of AtCPR or MgL6H into SC1 generated strains SC2 and SC3, which produced 26.1 mg/L and 34.5 mg/L (−)-carveol, respectively. Notably, while the (−)-limonene remained less affected, the (−)-carveol ratios of SC2 and SC3 increased to 79.5% and 86.5%. However, the simultaneous overexpression of CPR and L6H (strain SC4) resulted in a carveol titer of 29.0 mg/L, and increased (−)-limonene accumulation (Fig. 4C).

Inspired by the reports that MVA pathway upregulation enhances downstream sulfated sterol biosynthesis without direct pathway engineering [48], we strengthened the upstream of (−)-limonene synthesis in SC3 to construct the strains SC5-SC7. The resulting strain SC7 produced 88.6 mg/L (−)-carveol (2.6-fold increase when compared to SC3), and the (−)-carveol ratio increased to 89% (Fig. 4D). Additional overexpression of FPPS∗∗∗(−)LS in SC7 boosted the production of (−)-carveol to 103.1 mg/L (SC8), but the ratio reduced to 78% since more (−)-limonene accumulated (Fig. 4D). The hydroxylation of limonene catalyzed by L6H is highly NADPH-dependent. To improve the cofactor cycling [49], we overexpressed the ClGAPDH to boost NADPH supply, and introduced a second copy of CPR and L6H in SC8 to generate SC9, whose (−)-carveol production was 119.8 mg/L (1.2-fold increase against SC8) with the ratio of 82% (Fig. 4D).

Engineering the (−)-carvone biosynthesis

(−)-Carvone is converted from (−)-carveol via the carveol dehydrogenase (CDH) [50]. To construct the whole (−)-carvone biosynthesis pathway, six CDHs from distinct sources were evaluated with the (−)-carveol producing strain SC9. Phylogenetic analysis revealed evolutionary divergence among these enzymes, which might help the finding of CDH with desired activity (Fig. S2B). The (−)-carvone production suggested that the most efficient one was from R. erythropolis (ReCDH), which generated 5 mg/L (−)-carvone in 50-mL test tube (Fig. 5B). However, there were still a lot of (−)-limonene and (−)-carveol left, which might be attributed to the undesired enzymatic activity (Fig. 5C). We thus hypothesized that the common metabolic engineering practice, increasing the gene copy numbers of the critical CDH would enhance the (−)-carvone production. As expected, the (−)-carvone was improved to 30 mg/L when the four copies of CDH were introduced into R. toruloides (Fig. 5D). While a sharp reduction in (−)-carveol accumulation was observed, a comparable amount of (−)-limonene was still produced (Fig. 5D). Moreover, our results also showed that the composition of the monoterpenoids were also strain-dependent as the sub-clones of the strain SE4 affected different monoterpenoids spectra (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, the (–)-carveol contents varied among tested subclones while the residue limonene remained almost comparable, suggesting that the conversion efficiency of limonene to carveol should be given more consideration. To ensure the satisfactory production of (−)-carvone, highly efficient L6H and CDH should be further explored.

Increase monoterpenoids production by fed-batch fermentation

We further increased (−)-limonene, (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone production by fed-batch fermentation using SL14, SC9 and SE4, respectively. Prior to fed-batch fermentation, the influence of C/N ratios (15:1, 30:1, 60:1, 120:1, and 240:1) on (−)-limonene production was evaluated in 250-mL shake flask using SL9 since the availability of nutrients critically influences microbial growth and metabolic activity (Fig. S3). Comparable (−)-limonene production was obtained when the C/N ratios were 15:1, 30:1 and 60:1 while severe impaired cell growth was observed with the C/N ratios of 120:1 and 240:1. The fed-batch fermentation results on 500-mL bioreactors demonstrated that the C/N ratio of 15:1 outperformed 30:1 and 60:1 since the highest (−)-limonene titer of 608.5 mg/L was achieved with a C/N ratio of 15:1 at 156 h (Fig. S4). Subsequently, we scaled up the fermentation system to a 3-L bioreactor with a C/N ratio of 15:1 (Fig. 6A), which commenced the (−)-limonene production at 48 h, and maximized with a final production of 1.6 g/L and 19.0 mg/g glucose at 144 h.

Fed-batch fermentation of (−)-limonene, (−)-carveol. (A) SL14 for (−)-limonene production and (B) SC9 for (−)-carveol production in 3-L bioreactors. The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results were presented as means and standard deviations.

The C/N ratio of 15:1 was also applied to conduct the fed-batch fermentation of (−)-carveol with strain SC9 in the 3-L bioreactor, where the final (−)-carveol titer reached 668.0 mg/L at 240 h. However, a very high residue of (−)-limonene (361.7 mg/L) was also observed, 36% of the total monoterpenoids production (Fig. 6B). The significant accumulation of limonene suggested the hydroxylation of (−)-limonene to (−)-carveol, determined by L6H and CPR, was a bottleneck for efficient (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone production. The apparent increase in (−)-limonene accumulation in the bioreactors indicated the L6H catalytic efficiency was severely compromised, which might be owing to inefficient electron transfer. The study has shown that the poor electron transfer of P450 enzymes is due to random collisions and transient binding with redox partners. And the natural reaction electron transfer pathway of P450 and partners is long, with severe diffusion limitations, leading to the transmission efficiency is decreasing exponentially [51]. In our work, to prevent the incorrect expression of signal peptides derived from plants in the host organism, L6H and CPR have their endoplasmic reticulum signal peptides removed, and there is a certain spatial distance between them in the cytoplasm which diminishes electron transfer efficiency and limits the complete conversion of the substrate. Besides, it also helps the synthesis of the products by simulating the natural plant membrane environment of P450 enzymes with the subcellular compartmentalization [52]. Rather than empirical fermentation optimization alone, electron self-sufficient L6H could be constructed by using linker peptides (flexible, rigid) and scaffold proteins like (CipA/B, RIAD-RIDD) or co-locating in the subcellular space. Before construction, we will use enzyme engineering strategies to predict reasonable L6H-CPR fusion enzyme or scaffold models.

When strain SE4 was cultured in a 3-L fermenter with a C/N ratio of 60:1 for the production of (−)-carvone, the yields of (−)-carvone, (−)-carveol, and (−)-limonene were all significantly decreased. At the end of fermentation, the titer of carvone was only 7.0 mg/L (Fig. S6). This phenomenon may be attributed to the metabolic burden caused by the introduction of four copies of the CDH gene, which disrupted the metabolic balance of the strain. Several metabolic engineering and fermentation optimization strategies could be employed. Firstly, fine-tuning gene expression levels using inducible or weak constitutive promoters instead of multiple copies could reduce the metabolic burden on the host. Secondly, dynamic regulation of the CDH gene expression in response to metabolic fluctuations might help maintain intracellular balance. Additionally, optimizing fermentation parameters—such as the C/N ratio, dissolved oxygen, or feeding strategies—may restore metabolic flux and improve carvone production.

Conclusion

De novo biosynthesis of (−)-limonene, (−)-carveol and (−)-carvone was showcased with the oleaginous red yeast R. toruloides. The present study demonstrated that 1. the mutated enzyme FPPS∗∗∗ exhibited unanticipated higher performance than SlNPPS in (−)-limonene production; 2. The MstLS and ReCDH exhibited the best performance on (−)-limonene and (−)-carvone production, respectively; 3. R. toruloides was viable for limonene and its derived monoterpenoids production. However, the metabolic flux in R. toruloides should be further fine-tuned for constructing efficient biosynthesis route for limonene and derivatives. In addition, the L6H and its paired CPR should be taken seriously as they are critical for both the carveol and carvone biosynthesis. Under the fed-batch fermentation in bioreactors, the final (−)-limonene and (−)-carveol titers were 1.6 g/L at 144 h and 668.0 mg/L at 240 h. Our results also demonstrated the potential of R. toruloides as a platform for (−)-carvone production.
