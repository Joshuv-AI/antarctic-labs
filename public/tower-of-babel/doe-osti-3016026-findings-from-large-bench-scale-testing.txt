 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report highlights the key findings and outcomes relevant to the processability of waste supernatant at
Hanford using a denitration electrolyzer. The Electrosynthesis Company issued a Phase 1 report to the
Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL), summarizing the evaluation of large bench-scale denitration
electrolyzers to support the electrochemical denitration and caustic generation (EDCGe) project. The
Electrosynthesis Company’s report (attached as Appendix A) provides insights into the initial steps
required to implement an electrolyzer system at Hanford. Phase 1 experiments focused on validating the
denitration electrolyzer’s performance, operating parameters, and reaction products.

The robustness of the electrochemical denitration process was demonstrated by two electrolyzer flow cell
systems (a 100 cm2 ElectroCell MP and a 150 cm2 NESI NS01 cell), both of which achieved significant
nitrate and nitrite removal (>50%) with a current efficiency of ~95% for both systems. Higher current
densities (500 mA cm–2) improved nitrate and nitrite removal rates compared to lower current densities
(333 mA cm–2), while maintaining a current efficiency of ~94%.

The NS01 cell achieved a nitrate species removal rate of ~0.41 mol h–1 at 5 kA m–2 (equiv. to 500 mA cm–
2
  ). The primary reaction product was ammonia (NH3), constituting 78.3–91.6% of the products (excluding
OH– formation). NH3 was predominantly retained in the catholyte liquid phase rather than being off-gassed.
Additionally, the NS01 cell reported an NH3 generation rate of ~0.36 mol h–1 at 5 kA m–2. Other gas
formation included ~7% N2, ~7% +ၷ DQG trace amounts of N2O. The estimated power requirement
(extrapolated from the 0.015 m2 cell data) for a full-scale denitration electrolyzer is approximated to be ~1.6
MW (DC-only) to treat 50% of nitrate and nitrite in a waste stream and generates ~2.1 kmol h–1 of NH3
with an initial concentration of 4 M NO3–/NO2– at 300 gal h–1.

Simulated waste containing aluminate, carbonate, oxalate, and halogens exhibited no adverse effects on
denitration performance. A preliminary experiment comparing alkaline anolyte (5 M NaOH) with a nickel-
based anode to acidic media (2 M H2SO4) with a DSA-O2 anode showed a lower operating voltage and
generated less H2 than the acid media. Maintaining a stable 5 M OH– concentration in the anolyte through
periodic additions of caustic did not significantly impact denitration performance. This operational mode
will be required for long-term experiments.

All the experiments demonstrated that electrochemical denitration is a promising approach for treating
nitrate and nitrite in simulated waste streams, achieving significant conversion and robustness across
varying experimental conditions and electrochemical cell configurations. Lastly, the ability to generate a
nearly pure NH3 stream may prove advantageous for processing at other locations within the Hanford site.

1.0 Introduction
In alignment with the Electrochemical Denitration and Caustic Generation (EDCGe) project (DOE-EM
R&D Hanford Roadmap Award 277996), the Electrosynthesis Company was contracted to perform
validation experiments for a denitration electrolyzer system. A denitration electrolyzer stack is the core of
a proposed EDCGe system and is being researched at an engineering-scale to study the treatment of nitrates
and nitrites for the processing of nuclear waste supernatant located at the Hanford site.1 The
Electrosynthesis Company possesses extensive experience in the implementation and evaluation of large-
scale electrochemical systems, spanning the past few decades. The company has also previously
collaborated with SRNL on legacy denitration efforts.

Phase 1 for the Electrosynthesis Company focused on identifying the performance, operating parameters,
and reaction products of large bench-scale denitration flow electrolyzers. The 100 cm2 and 150 cm2
electrolyzers tested represent systems that are 10–15 times larger than what is typically operated at the
bench scale. This is the first phase of three, with each phase progressively addressing the considerations
required for implementing an electrolyzer system at the Hanford Tank Farms.

The Phase 1 experimentation at the Electrosynthesis Company has concluded, and a report has been issued
to SRNL. That report (included as Appendix A of this document) highlights several findings and includes
the:
 x Evaluation of the denitration process using electrolyzers with active areas of 100 and 150 cm2
 x Determination of the efficiency of large bench-scale denitration electrolyzers using simulated
 Hanford supernatant
 x Examination of the effects of varying current density (500 vs. 333 mA cm–2 ) and anolyte
 composition on the denitration process
 x Quantification of the reaction products generated during the electrochemical denitration process
 x Assessment of the impact of waste composition on the electrochemical denitration process, based
 on the simulants studied thus far

2.0 Experimental Procedure
The highlights from the experimental detail given in the Appendix A report are as follows:

2.1 Electrochemical Cell Setup:
The Electrosynthesis Company conducted Phase 1 experiments using two large bench-scale flow cells: an
Electrocell MP cell (single electrode area of 100 cm2) and a NORAM Electrolysis Systems Inc. (NESI)
NS01 cell (150 cm2). Both systems utilized a Nafion 424 membrane with nickel-based anodes and cathodes,
except for the acid anolyte experiment that used a DSA-O2 anode.

Experiments in the MP cell were run at 500 mA cmၱ2 for 6 h. Experiments performed in the NS01 cell at
500 mA cmၱ2 were run for 4 h, while those at 333 mA cmၱ2 in the NS01 cell ran for 6 h. The differences
in time per experiment were modified so that the amount of charge passed was held constant at ~300 Ahr
(~11.1 mol Q) for all experiments reported.

3.0 Results and Discussion
The Electrosynthesis Company successfully demonstrated electrochemical denitration at a large bench-
scale using two flow cell electrolyzers: the 100 cm2 ElectroCell MP cell and the 150 cm2 NESI NS01 cell.
The experiments were conducted using four non-radioactive simulants representative of a simplified
Hanford supernatant. Across both systems, on average, over 50% of nitrate species (NO3ၱ and NO2ၱ) were
reduced with a current efficiency of ~95%. A charge passed removal rate of ~0.14 mol/mol Q was achieved

 2

(averaging 7.14 Hၱ / mol NO3ၱ + NO2ၱ) within 6 h of operation. The resulting cell voltage ranged between
~3.0 and 5.0 V, with most stabilizing around 3.5 V.

Both electrolyzer systems demonstrated similar efficiencies for the electrochemical denitration process,
regardless of the differences in electrode geometry and size. These results validate the effectiveness and
robustness of the denitration process across both flow cells, confirming the potential for scalability and
implementation at the Hanford site.

3.3 Waste Composition Impact on Denitration:
The inclusion of waste tank components, such as aluminate, carbonate, oxalate, and halogens, in the
simulated waste streams (Sim1–Sim3) did not significantly affect the efficiency of the denitration process.
Across all simulant compositions, the removal rate and current efficiency were similar (as shown in Table
2). This suggests that the system is robust when inclusion of non-electrochemically active analytes that are
major components of Hanford waste tank supernatants.

Experimental results showed that maintaining a consistent 5 M NaOH concentration had no significant

experiments where the hydroxide concentration was allowed to deplete. Similarly, the hydroxide
concentration of the anolyte in the experiments performed had no observable effect on denitration efficiency
or cell operation.

The composition of the anolyte was further evaluated using a 2 M H2SO4 acidic electrolyte. The use of
acidic electrolyte may be considered for application if selectivity can be shifted towards N2 or if
sodium/hydroxide conservation in the catholyte is desired. The efficiency of the denitration process was
not significantly impacted by changing the anolyte composition from alkaline to acidic media. However,
for practical implementation, the use of a cost-effective nickel-based anode paired with an alkaline anolyte
currently remains advantageous due to its lower operating voltage and the production of NH3 as the major
reduction product. Furthermore, more hydrogen was observed for the acidic electrolyte.

4.0 Conclusions
The Phase 1 results from the Electrosynthesis Company demonstrate that scaling up the electrochemical
denitration process for the EDCGe system is feasible, based on the results from the ElectroCell MP and
NESI NS01 cells (Appendix A-Figure 2). The robustness of the electrolyzers with varying electrode size
and geometries in handling electrolyte feeds with varying compositions, while maintaining high current
efficiency at different current densities, is necessary for being able to handle feeds from waste tanks at the
Hanford site, where compositions can vary significantly. Additionally, the ability to generate an NH3-rich
stream may prove advantageous for the processing needs of the Hanford site. This phase of work is an
indication that this process is viable on an industrial scale and indicates further work is necessary.

5.0 Future Work (Phase 2)
Phase 2 with the Electrosynthesis Company will focus on evaluating more complex simulant solutions that
better mimic the compositions of waste tank supernatants. These supernatant simulants will incorporate
additional components, such as chromium salts (~0.01 M) and other low concentration analytes (e.g.
organics or Ni, Mn, Fe, etc.), to reflect the real-world complexity of the Hanford Tank Farm waste streams.
The impact of the low concentration contaminants on operating conditions and system performance will be
carefully investigated. Collaboration with SRNL technical staff will play a key role in tailoring simulant
compositions to represent specific waste ranges, helping to further align experimental conditions with the
real-world challenges.

Experiments will include higher concentrations of sodium and nitrate species with extended run times.
Extended experiments, lasting 24–48 h, will be conducted to evaluate the long-term feasibility of the system.

 5

Operating conditions will be refined through testing anolyte configurations, such as batch electrolysis and
semi-continuous bleed-and-feed methods, to identify the conditions with optimal efficiency and scalability.

Alternative anode chemistries will be explored to improve the denitrification process and potentially
increase selectivity toward desired products, lower the energy requirements of the system, or lower the
impact of chemical handling requirements outside of the electrolyzer system (i.e. caustic or acid media
electrolyzer feed requirements and recyclability). Anolyte electrode and electrolyte solutions are still being
evaluated and will also be considered when defining the needs for the whole EDCGe process.

Phase 1 identified ammonia as the major product of the electrochemical denitrification process, with a high
experimental solubility in the catholyte solutions. Process configuration or operation development will be
explored to remove NH3 from the solution. Some work is proposed to further investigate ammonia
separation and removal conditions to better understand how NH3 can be effectively stripped from the
resulting catholyte. Developing solutions to recover or manage ammonia might prove essential for the full-
scale system to ensure the efficient operation and sustainability of the overall process.

To prepare for the Phase 3 implementation of the denitration electrolyzer system, collaboration with
NORAM Electrolysis Systems Inc. (NESI) will continue through the Electrosynthesis Company to facilitate
discussions for implementation considerations.

1 Introduction

Savannah River National Lab (SRNL) expressed interest in the electrochemical removal of nitrate and nitrite from
radioactive waste. This process was demonstrated previously by the Electrosynthesis Company (ESC) and SRNL on
simulated waste solutions using a divided cell.1 Previous experiments had been performed on small scale as well as in an
MP Cell and indicated that denitration of radioactive waste streams could be performed electrochemically at high rates of
destruction to the various reduction products of nitrate and nitrite, shown in Figure 1. The current work was performed to
validate previous work, as well as demonstrate electrochemically denitration in various simulant solutions in a commercially
scalable NS01 Cell (NESI, Vancouver BC), to show that this process could be used for denitration on an industrial scale.

were performed using Sim1 like in the MP Cell. No experiments were performed using Sim0 in the NS01 Cell. The NS01

Interestingly, the removal rate was very similar to that of the experiments performed later with the MP Cell at 0.138 mol/mol
charge passed (for total nitrate and nitrate destruction). Before experiment 1317-016, the only analytical performed was
anion IC to determine nitrate and nitrite concentrations in solution. Analysis for gas composition and ammonia in solution
was performed in addition to anion analysis for this experiment and all remaining experiments. The quantification of
nitrogen (N2), nitrous oxide (N2O), and ammonia (NH3) allowed for the calculation of current efficiency. Current efficiency
can be defined as the amount of charge that goes towards performing the electrochemical reaction that is intended, in this
case the reduction of nitrate and nitrite. The amount of charge that went towards the desired reaction us calculated by the
amount of charge required to make each of the various products of the reduction and then divided by the total charge that
was passed. Any inefficiency is presumed to be due to generation of hydrogen (H2). The composition of off-gas was revealed
to be mainly N2 at the start, with a decrease in N2 and increase in H2 as the run progressed. A detailed summary of the gas
composition for experiments performed using the NS01 Cell can be found in Appendix Figure 2. The flow rate of the gas
also increased overall as the experiment went on. Interestingly, N2O increased over time as well; however, the amount of
N2O compared to the total charge passed was minimal for all experiments, demonstrating that under these conditions it is a
very minor product. The main nitrite reduction product turned out to be NH3. The expectation was that due to the high pH
of the catholyte solution, NH3 would evolve as gas and be trapped by the acidic scrubber on the outlet of the reservoir. 1
However, Hach analysis of the catholyte and scrubber solutions after the experiment showed that not only was the main
nitrite reduction product NH3, but that it remained in the catholyte solution even at 7 – 8 M OH- concentration and 70oC.

One problem that was encountered during the reduction of nitrate and nitrite was the persistence of NH 3 in solution. The
main goal of this research was to remove the nitrate and nitrite products from the actual waste stream, so NH 3 remaining
does not completely solve the problem. Experiments were performed on multiple post-reduction catholyte solutions to see
if the NH3 could be removed from solution under vacuum. The solution was placed in a round bottom flask with stirring
and connected to a scrubber containing 4 M H2SO4, which was then connected to a vacuum pump. The valve on the flask
was closed, and the solution was placed under vacuum for one hour, then the NH3 concentration of the solution before
vacuum, after vacuum, and of the scrubber were measured. This was performed on a Sim3 solution (1317-026/028/030)
that had a high final base concentration (titrated: ~6.9 M) and on the solution from 1317-032 where the anolyte was acidic.
The Sim3 final base concentration was ~2.5 M. The solution with a higher base/cation concentration evolved NH3 much
more readily under vacuum than the solution with the lower base/cation concentration. We were able to recover essentially
all the ammonia from the high base solution but not from the low base catholyte that was obtained from the experiments
with the acid anolyte. It is postulated that there is not enough base concentration to push the ammonium hydroxide to
ammonia. These were intended to be initial scoping experiments only and clearly more work is required to optimize this
process. The results are summarized in Table 5.

3 Conclusions

An investigation into the removal of nitrate and nitrite from radioactive waste streams was performed via electrochemical
reduction. Work in the MP and NS01 Cells indicated that this process can be successfully performed electrochemically,
with an average current efficiency of the reaction of ~95% and a removal rate of ~0.14 mol/mol charge passed (averaging
7.14 e- per mole of nitrate and nitrate combined). The inclusion of more contaminants to the simulant solution did not appear
to affect the efficiency of reduction, at least with the contaminants added in this phase of work. Using acid as the anolyte
did not have a huge effect on the efficiency of the reaction, although there was slight decrease in current efficiency due to
an increase in H2 production. The downside of acid anolyte is the requirement of a more expensive DSA-O2 electrode vs
Ni. The main reduction product of this reaction is NH3, which does remain in solution despite the high base concentration
and temperature at which the experiments are performed. Vacuum experiments showed that it is possible to remove the NH3
under vacuum at room temperature in the high base solutions. Overall, this phase of work was an indication that this process
could be viable on an industrial scale and indicates further work is necessary. The change in concentration of nitrate and
nitrite in experiments performed in the NS01 Cell along with off-gas product composition (assuming NH3 as gas) are
summarized in Table 6.

Based on the data to date, we have estimated that the power requirement to destroy 50% of the nitrate and nitrite contained
in a 300 gal/hour stream of tank waste would be approximately 1.6 MW (DC only). This assumes that the total start
concentration of nitrate plus nitrate is approximately 4 M.

4 Future Work

Expanding upon this and previous work, the next step will be to include more contaminants in the simulant solutions to
closer replicate the actual waste streams on which this process would be performed. One major addition will be chromate.
Chromate has been shown in the past to affect the electrode1, so this will need to be examined before scaling up can be
considered. Longer experiments will also be performed, as this current set of experiments were all run to ~50% removal of
nitrate/nitrite. It will be necessary to run to a pseudo steady-state concentration catholyte so that fresh tank waste can be
added continuously. These experiments will also determine the best ways of removing ammonia from the catholyte. In
further phases of work, the possibility of adding even more contaminants and using potentially different electrodes will be
considered.

5 Experimental Methods

All experimental work and analytical identification were conducted at ESC. All experiments were carried out without
consideration for air or water in an MP Cell or NS01 Cell. The starting materials were purchased by ESC (Sigma-Aldrich,
>99%). The cell used to perform electrochemical experiments was either the MP Cell (ElectroCell, Denmark), or the NS01
Cell (NESI, Canada).

 5.1 Set up and procedure for MP Cell experiments

The test stand was equipped with two 2 L jacketed reservoirs, each with a centrifugal pump to flow solution. The pumping
was split to flow solution to the cell, as well as a bypass to flow back to the reservoir. The bypass on both pumps was
equipped with a needle valve to control the bypass flow. Each reservoir was equipped with a condenser. The anolyte
condenser was open to the hood, while the catholyte condenser was connected to a gas scrubber that contained 4 M H2SO4

 Page [9]

(aq., 200 mL). Gas flow was measured volumetrically with a soap bubble burette after the scrubber. A rubber septum was
placed between the scrubber and the burette to take gas samples for GC analysis. Heating was provided by a water heater
for the jacketed reservoirs. Cell: the cathode material for all experiments was nickel (Ni), with an active area of 100 cm2.
The anode for all experiments was also Ni. The flow frames are 6 mm thick with two 1/16” EPDM gasket to seal the cell.
Between each layer of the cell, a 1/16” EPDM gasket was inserted to seal the gap. In the active area of the cell, PVDF
turbulence promoting mesh was included between the cathode and NafionTM 424 membrane, as well as the membrane and
anode. The membrane was used to allow sodium from the basic anolyte solution to cross the barrier but for no catholyte to
move across into the anolyte. Catholyte solutions were the simple simulant solutions listed above (Table 1), and 1.2 L was
used for each experiment except for 1317-006 where the catholyte volume was 1.5 L. The anolyte solution was aqueous 5
M NaOH (2.5 L). The solutions were recirculated through the cell, with the bypass on the catholyte and anolyte pumps
maintaining a pressure on the catholyte inlet of the cell of 8.0 psi and a pressure on the anolyte inlet of the cell of 6.5 psi.
The flow rate for the catholyte ranged from ~5.2 – 4.7 L/min, with the anolyte ranging from ~3.8 – 4.2 L/min (changing
over the course of the experiment based on caustic concentration). After recirculation, the current started. Experiments were
performed at 50 A (500 mA/cm2) with the voltage ranging from 4.8 – 5.2 V. Power was provided by an external power
supply, with current and voltage being monitored by the Arbin. At the end of the experiment, the current was stopped,
followed by stopping recirculation. The solutions were removed, and the cell was cleaned by circulating 2 M NaOH through
both compartments of the cell @ 50oC, followed by water at 50oC.

  5.2 Set up and procedure for NS01 Cell experiments

The same test stand that was used for experiments in the MP Cell was also used with the NS01 Cell, with most operating
procedures remaining the same. Cell: the cathode material for all experiments was Ni, with an active area of 150 cm 2. The
anode for all experiments was Ni, except for 1317-032, where the anode was DSA-O2. The cell was sealed with EPDM O-
rings, and the electrodes were pressed into the flow area to a gap of ~4 mm, with a NafionTM 424 membrane separating the
electrodes. Catholyte solutions were the simple simulant solutions listed above (Table 1), and 1.2 L was used for each
experiment. Anolyte was aqueous 5 M NaOH (2.5 L) for all experiments except for 1317-032, in which the anolyte was
aqueous 2 M H2SO4 (2.5 L). Anolyte NaOH concentration was kept between 4 – 6 M for experiment 1317-030 by periodic
addition of 50% NaOH (aq.). Pressure on the catholyte inlet of the cell was maintained at 4.5 psi, with a catholyte flow rate
of ~7.5 L/min. Pressure on the anolyte inlet of the cell was maintained at 5.5 psi, with an anolyte flow rate of ~6.0 L/min.
Experiments were all performed to ~300 Ahr at either 50 or 75 A (333 mA/cm2 or 500 mA/cm2, respectively). Voltages ran
from 2.9 – 5.0 V in the NS01 Cell. Removal and cleaning were the same as for the MP Cell.

  5.3 The analytical method used by ESC

Nitrate/nitrite anion analysis: a Thermo Scientific Dionex ICS-2100 IC chromatography instrument equipped with a Dionex
AS-AP autosampler and anion exchange column (AS17-C, 2 mm x 250 mm) with guard column (AG17-C, 2 mm x 50 mm).
Samples were injected at 25 μL, and the eluent gradient was 5 to 25 mM KOH (degassed DI H 2O) with suppressed
conductivity detection (25 mA). Off-gas analysis: a Hewlett-Packard 5890 Series II gas chromatograph using a thermal
conductivity detector at 145oC. Nitrogen (N2) and nitrous oxide (N2O) were separated and quantified on a molecular sieve
5A column. The carrier gas was helium (He) at a total flow rate of 80 mL/min. The injector temperature was 150oC and the
injection volume was 50 μL. The GC oven was held at 160oC for 15 min per run. The volumetric percentages of gases
present in off-gas samples were determined by using a certified standard gas mix of 10% N2 and 10% N2O with balance He
(Linde) as an external calibration standard for the GC. H2 volumes were calculated as the balance of volumetric flow after
N2 and N2O were measured. Ammonia analysis: any ammonia (NH3) gas that evolved from the catholyte solution was
scrubbed in acid. Samples were analyzed for NH3 with a Hach DR3900 Spectrometer using Hach TNT837 ammonia analysis
kits (10 – 100 mg/L). Samples were diluted and neutralized, then added to the kits. After 15 minutes, the kits were analyzed
in the instrument.
