Educational impact of a simulation-based renal biopsy workshop for medical students during nephrology clerkship
Authors: Yasuhiro Onishi, Kenji Tsuji, Hiroyuki Nakanoh, Naruhiko Uchida, Kazuhiko Fukushima, Koki Mise, Hidemi Takeuchi, Keiko Tanaka, Katsuyuki Tanabe, Hiroshi Morinaga, Haruhito A Uchida, Shinji Kitamura, Jun Wada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous renal biopsy is essential for diagnosing kidney disease. It is invasive and requires technical training to ensure patient safety. Simulation-based education is increasingly used to teach invasive procedures and minimize patient risk. However, most renal biopsy simulation studies focus on residents rather than undergraduate medical students. We evaluated the educational effectiveness of a renal biopsy simulation workshop during a nephrology clinical clerkship.MethodsThis prospective educational study enrolled fifth-year medical students (n = 113) who participated in a nephrology clinical clerkship at Okayama University between January 2023 and January 2024. The students attended a renal biopsy simulation workshop that included a short lecture, peer ultrasound practice, and hands-on workshop with a renal biopsy simulator. We assessed educational outcomes using anonymous pre- and post-workshop questionnaires that evaluated knowledge, confidence, and educational satisfaction.ResultsBefore the workshop, only 4.5% of students had observed a renal biopsy. Following the workshop, students reported significantly improved knowledge of renal biopsy procedures. The median proportion of the renal biopsy procedure that students felt capable of performing increased from 0% (IQR 0–5) before the workshop to 70% (IQR 70–80) after the workshop (p < 0.0001). Post-workshop evaluations showed high educational satisfaction: nearly all students reported a better understanding of needle biopsy techniques and greater motivation for learning.ConclusionsA simulation-based renal biopsy workshop significantly improved medical students’ self-reported knowledge and confidence in performing renal biopsies. Students reported high educational satisfaction with the workshop. A simulation-based workshop with a durable renal biopsy simulator may provide a practical and scalable approach for teaching invasive procedures in undergraduate medical education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09805-4.Keywords: Simulation-based education, Medical students, Nephrology, Renal biopsy

Introduction

Percutaneous renal biopsy is essential for diagnosing and evaluating kidney diseases. Histopathological findings provide clinical information for determining disease activity, guiding treatment strategies, and predicting prognosis. In current practice, real-time ultrasound guidance is routinely used to improve procedural accuracy by enabling visualization of the kidney and needle trajectory [1]. Despite its clinical importance, renal biopsy is an invasive procedure associated with potential complications [2, 3]. The most common complication is perinephric hematoma, which occurs in about 10% of cases. Severe bleeding requiring interventions such as blood transfusion or transcatheter arterial embolization is relatively rare, occurring in about 1% of cases in Japan [2]. Older age and uncontrolled hypertension have been reported as risk factors for adverse events [3]. Renal biopsy requires technical skills and familiarity with ultrasound guidance. Procedural training is essential to ensure patient safety and improve outcomes. Previous studies have shown that renal biopsy performed by nephrology trainees under appropriate supervision is generally safe and not associated with increased complication rates [4, 5]. Nevertheless, operator experience may influence procedural performance; for example, in liver biopsy, operator experience has been associated with biopsy specimen quality [6]. These findings suggest that structured training is important for developing procedural competence before clinical practice.

Simulation-based education is increasingly used to train clinicians in invasive procedures and minimize patient risk. Various simulation models for ultrasound-guided renal biopsy have been developed [7, 8]. Simulation training improves trainee confidence and procedural performance [9]. Recently, we reported a simple, inexpensive renal biopsy simulator using boiled chicken eggs [10]. The simulator allows visualization of needle penetration under ultrasound guidance and facilitates practical renal biopsy training.

However, most previous studies have focused on residents or nephrology trainees rather than on undergraduate medical students. Early exposure to procedural training may help medical students better understand clinical procedures and increase their interest in specific specialties [11]. Despite the growing use of simulation-based education, the impact of renal biopsy simulation during undergraduate clinical clerkships remains poorly studied. Therefore, in this study, we implemented a renal biopsy simulation workshop using a renal biopsy simulator during the nephrology clinical clerkship for fifth-year medical students and evaluated its educational effectiveness using pre- and post-workshop questionnaires.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-center prospective educational intervention study conducted at Okayama University School of Medicine. Fifth-year medical students (n = 113) participating in their clinical clerkship in nephrology between January 2023 and January 2024 were enrolled. Participation in the renal biopsy simulation workshop was part of the regular curriculum. All students completed pre- and post-workshop questionnaires anonymously. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study involved an educational intervention conducted as part of the regular medical curriculum and did not include any identifiable personal data. According to the institutional guidelines of Okayama University Hospital Institutional Review Board, the need for formal ethical approval was waived, as confirmed in 2022. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation after providing an explanation of the study. Participation was voluntary, and participants could opt out at any time. All data were collected and analyzed anonymously.

Simulator design

The simulator was designed to mimic the macroscopic structure of a human kidney, with a diameter of approximately 10 cm and a cortical layer thickness of approximately 20 mm [12]. Based on the blueprint design (Fig. 1A, B), a mold was fabricated, and three types of styrene-based soft resins were used to reproduce the cortical region, medullary region, and outer encapsulating layer of the kidney model ("JIN Senshi-kun", Taisei Medical, Japan). Ultrasonographic evaluation showed a distinct contrast between cortical and medullary regions (Fig. 1D), and the biopsy needle could be visualized in real time during puncture (Fig. 1E). During preliminary testing, the simulator maintained its structural integrity after more than 100 punctures, indicating sufficient durability for repeated use [12].

Educational intervention

The renal biopsy simulation workshop was organized into three sequential phases: a brief introductory lecture, ultrasound practice, and hands-on biopsy simulation. The workshop was conducted in small groups of four to five students and structured to allow close supervision and active participation. The total duration of the workshop was approximately 50 min per group, depending on the number of students. All sessions were supervised and instructed by a single board-certified nephrologist and supervisor certified by the Japanese Society of Nephrology (the first author). First, a brief introductory lecture (approximately five minutes) was provided, covering the indications and complications of renal biopsy, the basic principles of ultrasound-guided renal biopsy, sterile techniques, and biopsy needle handling. During the second session, students received hands-on ultrasound practice. Students performed ultrasound examinations on each other to learn basic probe handling, appropriate probe positioning, and evaluation of renal morphology, with approximately five minutes per student. In the third session, students participated in simulation-based renal biopsy training using the renal biopsy simulator. During the session, students practiced selecting the puncture site and handling an 18-gauge biopsy needle under real-time ultrasound guidance for approximately five minutes.

Outcome

The primary outcome was the change in self-reported confidence regarding renal biopsy before and after the simulation workshop (Supplementary Table 1, 2). A pre-post questionnaire design was used [13]. Students completed the pre-workshop section of the questionnaire before the workshop and the post-workshop section immediately after the workshop. The questionnaire included an item in which students indicated the percentage of the overall renal biopsy workflow that they felt capable of performing. The questionnaires were collected at the end of the session, and pre- and post-workshop responses were analyzed as paired data. No verbal guidance, checklist, or scoring rubric was provided for this item. This item was treated as a self-perceived estimate of procedural confidence. The questionnaire was administered anonymously, and students were informed that their responses would not affect their academic evaluation. Pre- and post-workshop questionnaires were developed to evaluate students’ self-reported knowledge and confidence. The questionnaires were developed specifically for the educational objectives of the renal biopsy workshop. Several items assessing understanding of renal biopsy procedures and self-confidence in performing biopsy-related tasks were adapted from questionnaires used in our previous educational study [10] and from published literature evaluating simulation-based procedural training among medical students [8, 11, 14]. The questionnaire content was reviewed by nephrologists with extensive experience in renal biopsy procedures and undergraduate medical education to ensure content relevance and clarity. Prior to implementation, the questionnaire was pilot reviewed by faculty members involved in the workshop and revised accordingly. The pre-workshop questionnaire assessed prior experience with renal biopsy, prior observation of non-renal needle biopsy, understanding of renal biopsy procedures, and procedural performance. The post-workshop questionnaire included identical knowledge and additional questions evaluating educational effectiveness, motivation, interest in nephrology, and satisfaction. All Likert scale items ranged from 1 (strongly disagree/not true at all) to 5 (strongly agree/very true).

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized as percentages. Pre- and post-workshop comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. For the primary outcome, the median paired difference with 95% confidence interval and effect size r were calculated; effect size r was defined as |Z|/√N, where Z is the standardized Wilcoxon test statistic and N is the number of non-zero pairs [15]. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using Graphpad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and Stata software (SE, version 19.5; Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Clinical characteristics of the participants in the simulation workshop

A total of 113 fifth-year medical students participated in the renal biopsy simulation workshop during their two-week nephrology clinical clerkship. Before the workshop, students were asked about their previous observational experience of biopsy procedures. Only 4.5% of students had previously observed a renal biopsy, while 27.4% had observed other types of needle biopsy procedures (Fig. 2). These findings indicate that most students had limited prior exposure to renal biopsy before participating in the simulation workshop.

Observational experience of renal biopsy among medical students. Proportion of students who had previously (A) observed a renal biopsy and (B) observed any needle biopsy prior to the workshop. Data were obtained from the pre-workshop questionnaire

Changes in self-assessed confidence in renal biopsy

Students’ self-reported knowledge of renal biopsy procedures improved following the simulation workshop (Fig. 3). Before the workshop, approximately 90% of students answered “not true at all” or “not true” for understanding the overall renal biopsy workflow, ultrasound evaluation, and biopsy needle handling. After completing the workshop, approximately 90% of students selected “true” or “very true” for these items. Improvement in knowledge of sterile procedures was less pronounced; however, a substantial proportion of students (78.6%) reported “true” or “very true” after the workshop. Students were also asked to estimate the proportion of the renal biopsy procedure that they felt able to perform. The self-reported estimate increased significantly from a median of 0% [Interquartile range (IQR) 0—5] before the workshop to 70% [IQR 70—80] after the workshop (median difference, 69 percentage points [95% CI, 60—70]; p < 0.0001; effect size r = 0.87) (Fig. 4).

Self-reported educational impact of the workshop

The educational impact of the workshop was evaluated using a post-workshop questionnaire (Fig. 5). All students (100%) reported understanding the needle biopsy method (“true” or “very true”), and 97.3% reported improvement in biopsy technique. In addition, 97.3% reported increased motivation to learn, and 99.1% considered the workshop a valuable learning opportunity. All students (100%) also reported that simulation-based education is important, that they would recommend the workshop to other students, and that they were satisfied with the workshop overall. Regarding interest in nephrology, 78.6% of students reported increased interest (“true” or “very true”).

Clinical exposure during the clerkship

The workshop was conducted on the first or third day of the two-week nephrology clerkship. Although fewer than 5% of students had previously observed a renal biopsy, 33% of students in this cohort subsequently observed one during the clerkship.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the educational impact of a renal biopsy simulation workshop conducted during the nephrology clinical clerkship. The results demonstrated that the simulation-based workshop significantly improved students’ self-reported knowledge and confidence regarding renal biopsy procedures. In particular, the understanding of the overall workflow of renal biopsy, ultrasound evaluation, and biopsy needle handling improved markedly after the workshop. The proportion of students who believed they could perform the renal biopsy procedure increased from a median of 0% before the workshop to 70% after the workshop. Post-workshop evaluations also showed high levels of perceived understanding, motivation, and overall satisfaction among participants. These findings demonstrate that the structured simulation-based workshop can support procedural learning in undergraduate medical education.

Comparison with previous studies

Simulation-based workshops for renal biopsy have been investigated in residents and nephrology trainees. Several simulation models using porcine kidney have been developed to support ultrasound-guided biopsy training. Mrug et al. described a low-cost model in which a porcine kidney was placed under turkey breast to reproduce the ultrasonographic appearance and tactile characteristics of a human kidney during biopsy procedures [7]. Using this model, Dawoud et al. subsequently conducted a workshop for nephrology fellows and demonstrated significant improvements in procedural confidence [9]. The fellows who participated in the simulation workshop showed improved biopsy performance, with a higher rate of successful renal tissue retrieval per pass and reduced procedure-related blood loss compared with before the workshop. Woywodt et al. developed a purpose-built simulator by embedding a porcine kidney in a gelatin-based scattering particle mixture, which allowed realistic ultrasound visualization and biopsy practice [16]. Participants in an ultrasound course reported high realism and educational value, and all trainees obtained satisfactory biopsy samples, with 94% reporting that the model helped reduce anxiety about performing the procedure. Other educational approaches using donated bodies have also been proposed. Oliver et al. reported the use of unfixed human cadavers for teaching percutaneous renal biopsy, which provided highly realistic anatomical conditions for procedural training [17]. More recently, Sharma et al. described a simulation workshop integrating cadaver-based training, mannequin models, and renal ultrasonography practice using standardized patients for nephrology fellows and practicing nephrologists [18]. This comprehensive program combined ultrasonographic examination and biopsy training, similar to the workshop structure used in the present study. Unlike the resource-intensive, small-group training described by Sharma, our program was implemented within an undergraduate clinical clerkship and delivered to an entire cohort of medical students using a simple and durable simulator. This highlights the feasibility of integrating renal biopsy simulation into routine medical education on a larger scale. Many existing models rely on biological materials or specialized equipment, which can limit their use in routine educational settings. The simulator used in this study was designed to be simple and durable, allowing repeated puncture without substantial degradation. In our previous work, the model remained usable after more than 100 punctures and showed greater durability and storage stability compared with earlier models such as the boiled egg simulator [10, 12]. These features enabled its use in a large cohort of medical students and support its applicability for routine educational use.

Educational implications

Simulation-based education provides a controlled setting in which learners can practice procedures without risk to patients. In the context of the renal biopsy workshop, such structured environments allow trainees to develop both technical skills and procedural understanding before performing the procedure in clinical practice [8, 19]. In our study, the workshop supported active learning by enabling students to connect theoretical knowledge with hands-on experience. Previous studies in medical education have demonstrated that simulation-based learning can improve knowledge acquisition, procedural performance, learner engagement, and confidence in clinical skills [14]. Recent studies in undergraduate medical education have also shown that simulation using artificial intelligence–based simulated patients can significantly improve clinical communication skills among medical students, supporting the role of simulation-based learning in early medical training [20]. Such experiential learning is particularly valuable for invasive procedures such as renal biopsy, where opportunities for direct participation during clinical training are limited.

Several components of the workshop can be interpreted through established frameworks of experiential and simulation-based learning [21]. In Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, concrete experience was provided through hands-on ultrasound scanning and simulated renal biopsy practice. Instructor feedback supported reflective observation, helping students review their probe handling, image acquisition, and needle approach. Abstract conceptualization occurred as students connected these experiences with renal anatomy and ultrasound-guided needle trajectory. Active experimentation was facilitated through subsequent simulated puncture attempts. The workshop also incorporated several features identified as important in simulation-based education, including integration within the clinical clerkship, clearly focused procedural learning objectives, a safe practice environment, immediate feedback, and opportunities for repeated practice [22]. These features may have contributed to the observed improvements in students’ perceived understanding, confidence, and motivation.

In undergraduate medical education, early exposure to clinical procedures may enhance students’ understanding of clinical practice and increase interest in medical specialties. Previous studies have shown that early procedural exposure can improve students’ confidence and preparedness for clinical clerkships [11]. However, opportunities for medical students to observe or perform interventional procedures in nephrology remain limited. Students and residents have limited exposure to interventional procedures in nephrology [23]. The lack of mentors or role models in nephrology is often cited as a reason for pursuing alternative career paths [24]. Embedding procedural simulation within the clinical clerkship may also align with situated learning by linking hands-on experience to a clinically meaningful context [25]. The results of the present study suggest that incorporating a simulation-based procedural workshop into clinical clerkships may provide meaningful learning opportunities for medical students. The high levels of student satisfaction and increased motivation observed in this study suggest that a simulation-based procedural workshop enhances the educational experience during clinical rotations. Moreover, a large proportion of students reported increased interest in nephrology after the workshop, suggesting that hands-on procedural education can also foster interest in specialized fields.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study used a pre–post design without a control group because the workshop was delivered to all students as part of the clinical clerkship, and thus the observed improvements may partly reflect general clinical training rather than the intervention alone. Third, the outcomes were based on self-reported confidence and self-perceived ability without an objective assessment of procedural competence. Because students had limited prior experience with renal biopsy, baseline self-assessments may have been inaccurate, and response-shift bias could have occurred due to changes in students’ self-assessment standards after the workshop. Although the questionnaire was administered anonymously, social desirability bias may also have influenced the results, as the workshop was part of a required curriculum and was supervised by a single instructor. Fourth, the study evaluated only short-term educational outcomes, and the long-term retention of procedural knowledge and skills was not assessed. Thus, it remains unclear whether the observed improvements were sustained over time or translated into subsequent clinical learning and performance. Fifth, although the workshop incorporated elements of deliberate practice, the total training time was limited. Therefore, it did not provide sufficient repetition or mastery-based progression. Future studies should incorporate longer follow-up periods, control or comparison groups where feasible, and objective assessments of procedural competence. These assessments could include analytic checklists for key procedural steps, such as ultrasound guidance, sterile technique, needle handling, and safety checks. They could also include synthetic assessments, such as Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE)-based or Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS)-style direct observation to evaluate the performance of medical students under appropriate supervision [26, 27]. Finally, because the questionnaire was designed as a program-evaluation tool rather than a psychometric instrument, formal assessment of internal consistency and construct validity was not performed. Although the questionnaire items were adapted from previously used educational surveys and reviewed by experts in nephrology education, the lack of formal psychometric validation should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the educational effects reported in this study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a renal biopsy simulation workshop implemented during the nephrology clinical clerkship significantly improved medical students’ knowledge and confidence regarding renal biopsy procedures and was highly rated as an educational experience. The renal biopsy simulator used in this study was simple, durable, and suitable for repeated use in undergraduate education settings. The simulation-based workshop may therefore represent an effective approach for teaching invasive procedures to medical students and may help bridge the gap between theoretical learning and clinical practice.
