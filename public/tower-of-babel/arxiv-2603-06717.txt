Nonlocal Generalized Dirac Oscillators in (1 + 1) Dimensions
A. Boumali

Abstract
We propose a nonlocal extension of the generalized Dirac oscillator (GDO) in ( 1 + 1 ) (1+1) dimensions by replacing the multiplicative interaction f ⁡ ( x ) f(x) with an integral operator F ^ \hat{F} with kernel f ⁡ ( x , x ′ ) f(x,x^{\prime}) . The resulting Dirac equation preserves an operator factorization and decouples into two nonlocal Schrödinger-type (Sturm–Liouville) equations for the spinor components. We derive explicit expressions for the associated supersymmetric partner kernels in terms of f f and its derivatives, and we show that a complex-translation metric η = e − θ ​ p x \eta=e^{-\theta p_{x}} leads to a simple sufficient kernel-level pseudo-Hermiticity constraint, f ⁡ ( x + i ​ ℏ ​ θ , x ′ + i ​ ℏ ​ θ ) = f ∗ ​ ( x ′ , x ) f(x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta,x^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta)=f^{*}(x^{\prime},x) , extending the familiar local complex-shift criteria. To provide a transparent nonlocal-to-local interpretation, we adapt the Coz–Arnold–MacKellar current-based localization to each component equation, obtaining energy-dependent equivalent local potentials and multiplicative Perey (damping) factors. The mapping breaks down precisely at current zeros, thereby diagnosing the spurious solutions of the corresponding nonlocal Schrödinger problem. Finally, we illustrate the formalism with analytically tractable benchmarks (the local Dirac oscillator and a translation-invariant kernel) and with a finite-rank separable model (Gaussian form factor) that reduces the integro-differential problem to a small set of coupled ordinary differential equations and algebraic constraints.



1
Introduction

Nonlocality is a pervasive feature of effective single-particle descriptions obtained by eliminating (or averaging over)
unobserved degrees of freedom in an underlying many-body dynamics.
In coordinate space a nonlocal interaction acts as an integral operator,

(\hat{U}\psi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,U(x,x^{\prime})\,\psi(x^{\prime}),

(1)

so that the wave equation couples values of
\psi
at distinct spatial points.
In nuclear reaction theory, nonlocality arises naturally from exchange/antisymmetrization effects and from channel
elimination; the Perey–Buck optical potential remains a paradigmatic phenomenological example
[
1
]
.

A well-known manifestation of nonlocality is the
Perey effect
: compared with the solution of a corresponding
local-equivalent equation, the wave function generated by an attractive nonlocal kernel is typically
damped
in
the nuclear interior (and enhanced for repulsive kernels). This behavior can often be summarized by a multiplicative

Perey factor
that relates the nonlocal wave function to the local-equivalent one and, in practice, modifies
interior-sensitive observables such as spectroscopic factors and transfer cross sections

[
3
,
28
,
11
,
29
]
.
Recent analyses emphasize that the Perey factor is not universally accurate—its validity depends on the range and
energy dependence of the nonlocality and on the reaction mechanism—but it remains a useful diagnostic for gauging
the impact of localization approximations
[
16
,
29
]
.

More generally, nonlocal kernels are routinely encountered in microscopic folding models and in dispersive optical
potentials, where they encode correlations beyond a static mean field and provide a bridge between reaction
observables and structure information
[
12
,
13
,
17
,
18
]
.
Nonlocality also appears naturally in relativistic (Dirac-type) settings and in inverse spectral problems for
Dirac systems with integral kernels
[
26
,
27
]
.

A recurring theme in the nonlocal literature is the
transition from nonlocal to local
representations.
Historically this transition has been pursued through (i) velocity-dependent or energy-dependent local
approximations
[
2
]
, (ii) explicit localization schemes such as the Brieva–Rook approximation and
its refinements
[
4
,
5
,
8
]
, and (iii) fixed-energy
phase-equivalent constructions
[
6
,
7
]
.
These approaches are complemented by exact or numerically defined
local equivalents
that reproduce the
scattering solutions of a given nonlocal equation at a chosen energy and quantify the accompanying wave-function
renormalization (Perey effect)
[
3
,
11
]
.

A particularly transparent local-equivalent construction is based on current/Wronskian identities.
For definiteness, consider a one-dimensional nonlocal Schrödinger-type equation,

-\hbar^{2}\,\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,V(x,x^{\prime})\,\psi(x^{\prime})=\epsilon\,\psi(x),\qquad\epsilon=\hbar^{2}k^{2}.

(2)

One seeks an energy-dependent local potential
V^{\rm eq}(x;\epsilon)
and a multiplicative factor
A(x;k)
such that

\displaystyle-\hbar^{2}\,\psi_{L}^{\prime\prime}(x)+V^{\rm eq}(x;\epsilon)\,\psi_{L}(x)=\epsilon\,\psi_{L}(x),

(3)

\displaystyle\psi_{N}(x)=A(x;k)\,\psi_{L}(x),\qquad\lim_{x\to\infty}A(x;k)=1.

(4)

The factor
A
is the
damping function
(or Perey factor): for attractive nonlocalities it typically suppresses
the interior amplitude of
\psi_{N}
relative to its local-equivalent counterpart
[
3
]
.
Coz, Arnold, and MacKellar developed a mapping that yields closed-form expressions for both
V^{\rm eq}
and
A

whenever a Jost pair of nonlocal solutions remains linearly independent
[
9
]
.
The same framework clarifies the appearance of
spurious solutions
: when the incoming/outgoing solutions lose
linear independence, the normalized current vanishes,
A
crosses zero, and the equivalent local potential can develop
singularities
[
9
,
10
]
.

Independently, non-Hermitian relativistic Hamiltonians may nevertheless possess real spectra when they are

\eta
-pseudo-Hermitian, i.e. when
H^{\dagger}=\eta H\eta^{-1}
for some Hermitian metric operator
\eta

[
19
,
20
]
.
This is not merely a formal curiosity: when
\eta
is positive definite,
H
is similar to a Hermitian Hamiltonian

h=\rho H\rho^{-1}
with
\rho^{\dagger}\rho=\eta
.
Such similarity transformations frequently turn
local
non-Hermitian interactions into
nonlocal
Hermitian
ones, emphasizing that nonlocality and non-Hermiticity are, in many settings, two sides of the same effective
description.
In the context of generalized Dirac oscillators in
(1+1)
dimensions, Dutta, Panella, and Roy used a complex
translation metric
\eta=e^{-\theta p_{x}}
to construct broad families of complex interactions for which the Dirac
Hamiltonian is
\eta
-pseudo-Hermitian
[
21
]
.

The central objective of this work is to combine these two lines of development by formulating a

nonlocal generalized Dirac oscillator
(NLGDO) in
(1+1)
dimensions and by providing a practical
nonlocal-to-local interpretation of its decoupled component equations.
Our main contributions are:

(i)

a sufficient
kernel-level
pseudo-Hermiticity criterion for the nonlocal Dirac Hamiltonian under the
complex-translation metric
\eta=e^{-\theta p_{x}}
;

(ii)

explicit formulas for the induced nonlocal supersymmetric partner kernels appearing in the second-order
component problems;

(iii)

a component-wise local-equivalent mapping yielding energy-dependent local potentials and Perey-type
damping functions, together with a transparent breakdown criterion in terms of current zeros/spurious solutions.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
reviews the local GDO and the complex-translation metric.
Section
3
defines the NLGDO, derives the decoupled nonlocal partner equations, and formulates a
sufficient kernel condition for
\eta
-pseudo-Hermiticity.
Section
4
adapts the Coz–Arnold–MacKellar local-equivalent construction to each component and
presents illustrative solvable and finite-rank examples.

2
Background: local generalized Dirac oscillator and pseudo-Hermitian metrics

2.1
Local generalized Dirac oscillator in
(1+1)
dimensions

The Dirac oscillator, introduced by Moshinsky and Szczepaniak, is obtained by a nonminimal substitution that produces
a linear (oscillator-like) coupling while preserving the first-order Dirac structure and exact solvability

[
22
,
23
]
.
A convenient one-dimensional generalization replaces the linear profile by an arbitrary (possibly complex) function

f(x)
; this leads to the
generalized Dirac oscillator
(GDO), which encompasses a broad class of solvable and
quasi-exactly solvable models and admits a natural supersymmetric factorization
[
23
,
24
]
.

We work in the Pauli representation with

\sigma_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
,
\beta=\sigma_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
,
and momentum operator
p_{x}=-\mathrm{i}\hbar\,\partial_{x}
.
The local GDO Hamiltonian reads

H_{\rm GDO}=c\,\sigma_{x}\Big(p_{x}-\mathrm{i}\,\sigma_{z}f(x)\Big)+\beta\,mc^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}mc^{2}&cp_{x}+\mathrm{i}cf(x)\\
cp_{x}-\mathrm{i}cf(x)&-mc^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

For
\psi=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})^{T}
, the Dirac equation
H_{\rm GDO}\psi=E\psi
is equivalent to the coupled component system

\big(p_{x}+\mathrm{i}f(x)\big)\psi_{2}=\frac{E-mc^{2}}{c}\,\psi_{1},\qquad\big(p_{x}-\mathrm{i}f(x)\big)\psi_{1}=\frac{E+mc^{2}}{c}\,\psi_{2}.

(6)

Introducing the first-order operators

A=p_{x}-\mathrm{i}f(x),\qquad A^{\#}=p_{x}+\mathrm{i}f(x),

(7)

one obtains the standard factorization/decoupling,

A^{\#}A\,\psi_{1}=\epsilon\,\psi_{1},\qquad AA^{\#}\,\psi_{2}=\epsilon\,\psi_{2},\qquad\epsilon=\frac{E^{2}-m^{2}c^{4}}{c^{2}},

(8)

which reduces the spectral analysis to a pair of Schrödinger-type problems with supersymmetric partner potentials,

\left[-\hbar^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+V_{\mp}(x)\right]\psi_{1,2}=\epsilon\,\psi_{1,2},\qquad V_{\pm}(x)=f^{2}(x)\pm\hbar f^{\prime}(x).

(9)

In this representation
f(x)
plays the role of a (possibly complex) “superpotential” and
(V_{-},V_{+})
form a SUSY
pair. The correspondence is useful both conceptually (spectral pairing, index theorems) and computationally (shape
invariance and algebraic construction of bound states)
[
23
,
24
]
.
Throughout, the relativistic energy
E
is recovered from the auxiliary eigenvalue
\epsilon
via

E=\pm\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}+c^{2}\epsilon}
, with the sign selecting the positive/negative-energy branches.

2.2
Pseudo-Hermiticity and complex-translation metrics

A Hamiltonian
H
is
\eta
-pseudo-Hermitian if
H^{\dagger}=\eta H\eta^{-1}
with
\eta=\eta^{\dagger}
.
When
\eta
can be chosen positive definite,
H
is (quasi-)Hermitian and is similar to a Hermitian Hamiltonian

h=\rho H\rho^{-1}
with
\rho^{\dagger}\rho=\eta

[
19
,
20
]
.
Pseudo-Hermiticity therefore provides a unifying framework that includes many PT-symmetric constructions as special
cases.

Motivated by complex-translation metrics, we focus on

\eta=e^{-\theta p_{x}},\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{R},

(10)

which acts on sufficiently regular wave functions as a complex shift,

(\eta\phi)(x)=\phi(x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta)
.
For analytic interactions this choice turns pseudo-Hermiticity into a simple functional constraint.
This is the mechanism used in Ref.
[
21
]
to generate families of complex generalized Dirac oscillators that
remain isospectral to their Hermitian counterparts.

Proposition 1

(Local complex-shift benchmark)
.

If
f(x)
satisfies
f(x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta)=f^{*}(x)
, then
H_{\rm GDO}
in (
5
) is

\eta
-pseudo-Hermitian with
\eta=e^{-\theta p_{x}}
.

Remark 1
.

Even when the local Hamiltonian is rendered Hermitian by a similarity transformation, the transformed operator may
acquire nonlocal (integral) terms if the metric
\eta
is itself nonlocal.
This observation motivates treating nonlocality and pseudo-Hermiticity on the same footing in the generalized Dirac
oscillator setting.

3
Nonlocal generalized Dirac oscillator: definition, decoupling, and kernel pseudo-Hermiticity

3.1
Definition

We introduce nonlocality by replacing the multiplication operator
f(x)
with an integral operator
\hat{F}
acting on
scalar wave functions as

(\hat{F}\phi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,f(x,x^{\prime})\,\phi(x^{\prime}).

(11)

The kernel
f(x,x^{\prime})
is allowed to be complex.
When
f(x,x^{\prime})
satisfies the Hermiticity condition
f(x,x^{\prime})=f^{*}(x^{\prime},x)
, the operator
\hat{F}
is Hermitian; in that
case
H_{\rm NLGDO}
below is a conventional (Hermitian) Dirac Hamiltonian with nonlocal interaction.
Our emphasis, however, is on pseudo-Hermitian settings in which nonlocality and complex structure coexist in a
controlled way.

The nonlocal generalized Dirac oscillator (NLGDO) Hamiltonian is defined by the formal replacement

f(x)\mapsto\hat{F}
in (
5
),

H_{\rm NLGDO}=c\,\sigma_{x}\Big(p_{x}-\mathrm{i}\,\sigma_{z}\hat{F}\Big)+\beta\,mc^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}mc^{2}&cp_{x}+\mathrm{i}c\hat{F}\\
cp_{x}-\mathrm{i}c\hat{F}&-mc^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

The corresponding component equations generalize (
6
) to

\big(p_{x}+\mathrm{i}\hat{F}\big)\psi_{2}=\frac{E-mc^{2}}{c}\,\psi_{1},\qquad\big(p_{x}-\mathrm{i}\hat{F}\big)\psi_{1}=\frac{E+mc^{2}}{c}\,\psi_{2}.

(13)

Spectral and inverse problems for Dirac-type operators with nonlocal potentials have also been investigated in
mathematically rigorous settings; see, e.g., Ref.
[
26
]
.

Remark 2

(Regularity and analyticity assumptions)
.

Throughout, we assume wave functions lie in a dense subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(e.g. Schwartz space) on which
p_{x}
is
defined, and that the kernel
f(x,x^{\prime})
is sufficiently smooth and decaying so that:
(i)
\hat{F}
maps this domain into itself;
(ii) integration by parts in
x^{\prime}
is permitted with vanishing boundary terms; and
(iii) for complex shifts
x\mapsto x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta
,
f
admits analytic continuation in a strip of width at least

\hbar|\theta|
in both arguments and the relevant contour deformations cross no singularities.
These assumptions are standard in nonlocal scattering and in pseudo-Hermitian complex-shift constructions, and they are
made explicit here to justify the kernel manipulations used below.

3.2
Decoupling and explicit partner kernels

A notable feature of the construction is that the first-order Dirac system (
13
) retains a
factorization structure.
Define the nonlocal ladder operators

A=p_{x}-\mathrm{i}\hat{F},\qquad A^{\#}=p_{x}+\mathrm{i}\hat{F}.

(14)

Then

A^{\#}A=p_{x}^{2}+\hat{F}^{2}+\mathrm{i}[\hat{F},p_{x}],\qquad AA^{\#}=p_{x}^{2}+\hat{F}^{2}-\mathrm{i}[\hat{F},p_{x}],

(15)

so the decoupled second-order equations become

A^{\#}A\,\psi_{1}=\epsilon\,\psi_{1},\qquad AA^{\#}\,\psi_{2}=\epsilon\,\psi_{2},\qquad\epsilon=\frac{E^{2}-m^{2}c^{4}}{c^{2}}.

(16)

Writing (
16
) in coordinate space yields a pair of integro-differential Schrödinger-type
problems,

-\hbar^{2}\,\psi_{j}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,V_{j}(x,x^{\prime})\,\psi_{j}(x^{\prime})=\epsilon\,\psi_{j}(x),\qquad j=1,2,

(17)

whose kernels
V_{1,2}
play the role of nonlocal supersymmetric partners.
Under the assumptions of the previous remark (details in Appendix A.1), one finds the explicit representation

V_{1,2}(x,x^{\prime})=(f\star f)(x,x^{\prime})\mp\hbar\big(\partial_{x}+\partial_{x^{\prime}}\big)f(x,x^{\prime}),\qquad(f\star f)(x,x^{\prime}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}y\,f(x,y)f(y,x^{\prime}).

(18)

In the local limit
f(x,x^{\prime})=f(x)\,\delta(x-x^{\prime})
, Eq. (
18
) reduces to the standard partner potentials

V_{\pm}(x)=f^{2}(x)\pm\hbar f^{\prime}(x)
in (
9
).

3.3
\eta
-pseudo-Hermiticity for nonlocal interactions (kernel constraint)

The Hermitian adjoint of
\hat{F}
has kernel
f^{\dagger}(x,x^{\prime})=f^{*}(x^{\prime},x)
,

(\hat{F}^{\dagger}\phi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,f^{*}(x^{\prime},x)\,\phi(x^{\prime}).

(19)

Conjugation by
\eta=e^{-\theta p_{x}}
shifts both arguments of the kernel:

(\eta\hat{F}\eta^{-1}\phi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,f(x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta,x^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta)\,\phi(x^{\prime}).

(20)

Therefore, a sufficient condition for
\eta
-pseudo-Hermiticity of
H_{\rm NLGDO}
is the
kernel-level

constraint

f(x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta,x^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta)=f^{*}(x^{\prime},x).

(21)

Appendix A.2 provides a detailed derivation.
In the local case, Eq. (
21
) reduces to the complex-shift condition

f(x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta)=f^{*}(x)

[
21
]
.

Proposition 2

(Kernel criterion for pseudo-Hermiticity)
.

If the nonlocal interaction kernel satisfies (
21
) for some real
\theta
, then

H_{\rm NLGDO}
in (
12
) obeys
H_{\rm NLGDO}^{\dagger}=\eta H_{\rm NLGDO}\eta^{-1}
with

\eta=e^{-\theta p_{x}}
.

Remark 3
.

Condition (
21
) interpolates between two familiar limits:
for
\theta=0
it reduces to ordinary Hermiticity of the kernel, while for kernels concentrated near the diagonal

x=x^{\prime}
it approaches the local complex-shift constraint of Ref.
[
21
]
.
In this sense, (
21
) provides a natural pseudo-Hermitian extension of nonlocal interactions within
the generalized Dirac oscillator framework.

4
Local-equivalent formulation and illustrative models

The decoupling in Section
3
reduces the NLGDO problem to two nonlocal Schrödinger-type component
equations (
17
).
To interpret these equations in familiar terms, we now construct
local equivalents
for each component, following
the current-based strategy of Coz, Arnold, and MacKellar
[
9
]
.
The resulting mapping produces, at a fixed energy, an energy-dependent local potential together with a multiplicative
Perey (damping) factor that quantifies the nonlocal modification of the wave function.

For clarity we present the mapping on the half-line
x\geq 0
; full-line scattering can be handled by treating the
left/right Jost systems separately
[
9
]
.
Additional background on local-equivalent and phase-equivalent constructions can be found in
Refs.
[
1
,
10
,
14
,
15
]
.

4.1
Nonlocal-to-local transition: localization, phase-equivalent potentials, and Perey factors

Before turning to the explicit Coz mapping, it is useful to place the construction in the broader context of
nonlocal-to-local transitions.
A common starting point is that many physically motivated nonlocal kernels are short-ranged around the diagonal

x\approx x^{\prime}
, so that a gradient expansion yields local (but typically energy-dependent) operators.
Classic examples include the velocity-dependent Frahn–Lemmer interaction
[
2
]
and the
Brieva–Rook localization for exchange nonlocality in microscopic nucleon–nucleus potentials

[
4
,
5
]
, with modern reassessments and refinements in
Ref.
[
8
]
.
At fixed energy, one may instead construct
phase-equivalent
local potentials that reproduce the scattering phase
shifts of an inherently nonlocal interaction; see, e.g., the detailed transition analysis in
Ref.
[
6
]
and the inversion-based study of local/nonlocal equivalence in
Ref.
[
7
]
.
In all of these approaches, the appearance of a multiplicative renormalization of the wave function—often referred to
as the Perey effect—is a robust diagnostic of nonlocality
[
3
]
.

The Coz–Arnold–MacKellar construction provides an
exact
local equivalent (where defined) by exploiting the
Wronskian/current structure of a Jost pair of solutions.
It can be viewed as a constructive realization of the same idea that underlies Wronskian/inversion equivalent local
potentials discussed in, e.g., Ref.
[
11
]
: once two independent solutions of
the nonlocal equation are available, both the damping function and the equivalent local potential can be written
explicitly.

4.2
Nonlocal component equation in Coz form

Set
\epsilon=\hbar^{2}k^{2}
and define
U_{j}(x,x^{\prime}):=V_{j}(x,x^{\prime})/\hbar^{2}
.
Then (
17
) becomes

\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+k^{2}\right)\psi_{N,j}(k,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,U_{j}(x,x^{\prime})\,\psi_{N,j}(k,x^{\prime}),\qquad j=1,2.

(22)

The corresponding local-equivalent equation is

\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+k^{2}-U^{\rm eq}_{j}(x;k)\right)\psi_{L,j}(k,x)=0,\qquad V^{\rm eq}_{j}(x;\epsilon)=\hbar^{2}U^{\rm eq}_{j}(x;k).

(23)

We emphasize that
U^{\rm eq}_{j}
is, in general, energy dependent even when the original kernel
U_{j}
is not; this is
the price paid for replacing an integral operator by a multiplicative potential at fixed energy.

4.3
Jost-type solutions, normalized current, and damping function

Let
\psi_{N,j,\pm}(k,x)
denote a pair of independent nonlocal solutions normalized by outgoing/incoming
asymptotics as
x\to\infty
(Jost-type solutions).
Assume a multiplicative map

\psi_{N,j,\pm}(k,x)=A_{j}(k,x)\,\psi_{L,j,\pm}(k,x),\qquad\lim_{x\to\infty}A_{j}(k,x)=1,

(24)

where
A_{j}
is the damping function.
In scattering language,
A_{j}
measures the local renormalization required to reconcile the interior behavior of the
nonlocal solution with a local equation that shares the same asymptotics.

Introduce the normalized Wronskian/current (Coz current)

J_{N,j}(k,x)=-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}k}\Big[\psi_{N,j,+}(k,x)\,\partial_{x}\psi_{N,j,-}(k,x)-\psi_{N,j,-}(k,x)\,\partial_{x}\psi_{N,j,+}(k,x)\Big].

(25)

For the local equation (
23
) one has the corresponding normalized current
J_{L,j}\equiv 1
for the
same asymptotic normalization.
Equation (
24
) therefore implies the key identity

J_{N,j}(k,x)=A_{j}^{2}(k,x).

(26)

Thus
A_{j}
is fixed by the nonlocal current (up to a sign), and
A_{j}
is positive wherever the Jost solutions remain
linearly independent
[
9
]
.
In particular, the nonlocal-to-local transition is controlled by the behavior of the current: smooth, nonvanishing

J_{N,j}
leads to a well-behaved damping factor and a regular equivalent potential.

4.4
Bilinear kernel
Q_{N,j}
and the equivalent local potential

Define the bilinear combination

Q_{N,j}(k;x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}k}\Big[\psi_{N,j,+}(k,x)\psi_{N,j,-}(k,x^{\prime})-\psi_{N,j,-}(k,x)\psi_{N,j,+}(k,x^{\prime})\Big].

(27)

Then a local-equivalent potential can be written directly in terms of
J_{N,j}
,
Q_{N,j}
, and the kernel
U_{j}
as

U^{\rm eq}_{j}(x;k)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{J_{N,j}^{\prime\prime}(k,x)}{J_{N,j}(k,x)}+\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{J_{N,j}^{\prime}(k,x)}{J_{N,j}(k,x)}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{J_{N,j}(k,x)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,U_{j}(x,x^{\prime})\,\partial_{x}Q_{N,j}(k;x,x^{\prime}).

(28)

Equation (
28
) yields an energy-dependent
V^{\rm eq}_{j}(x;\epsilon)=\hbar^{2}U^{\rm eq}_{j}(x;k)
that is uniquely
fixed by the nonlocal kernel and the chosen nonlocal fundamental solutions
[
9
,
15
]
.

Correspondence with Coz–Arnold–MacKellar (1970).

With the identifications
r\mapsto x
,
V(r,r^{\prime})/\hbar^{2}\mapsto U_{j}(x,x^{\prime})
, and (for the half-line)
\ell=0
, our definitions reproduce the Coz mapping term by term: Eq. (
25
) is the normalized current (their Eq. (3.8)), Eq. (
26
) is their damping identity
J_{N}=A^{2}
(Eq. (3.11)), and Eq. (
28
) is the one-dimensional specialization of their equivalent local potential formula (Eq. (3.17)). Consequently, whenever the Jost pair remains linearly independent (no current zeros), our component-wise local equivalents are exactly those of Ref.
[
9
]
.

From a practical viewpoint, (
28
) provides a direct diagnostic: if
J_{N,j}
becomes small, the equivalent
potential becomes rapidly varying and the local picture loses predictive value.

4.5
Spurious solutions and breakdown of the mapping

The mapping fails at points where
J_{N,j}(k,x)=0
(equivalently
A_{j}=0
), signalling loss of linear independence of
the incoming/outgoing solutions.
In this case the equivalent local potential can develop poles, and the corresponding states are the nonlocal

spurious solutions
discussed in the nonlocal Schrödinger literature
[
9
,
10
]
.
Operationally, spurious solutions correspond to energies at which the integral equation admits nontrivial solutions with
vanishing asymptotic flux, so that a local-equivalent interpretation in terms of standard scattering boundary
conditions becomes ill-defined.

Proposition 3

(Current criterion for validity of the local-equivalent map)
.

Assuming existence of Jost-type solutions
\psi_{N,j,\pm}
on a domain
D\subseteq[0,\infty)
,
the local-equivalent map (
24
) is well-defined on
D
if and only if

J_{N,j}(k,x)\neq 0
for all
x\in D
.

4.6
Reconstruction of an effective local generalized Dirac oscillator

A local generalized Dirac oscillator is specified by a single function
f_{\rm eff}(x;\epsilon)
producing partner
potentials

V^{\rm eff}_{\pm}(x;\epsilon)=f_{\rm eff}^{2}(x;\epsilon)\pm\hbar f_{\rm eff}^{\prime}(x;\epsilon).

(29)

Given component-wise local equivalents
V^{\rm eq}_{1}(x;\epsilon)
and
V^{\rm eq}_{2}(x;\epsilon)
from
Eq. (
28
), define

\Sigma(x;\epsilon):=V^{\rm eq}_{2}(x;\epsilon)+V^{\rm eq}_{1}(x;\epsilon),\qquad\Delta(x;\epsilon):=V^{\rm eq}_{2}(x;\epsilon)-V^{\rm eq}_{1}(x;\epsilon).

A necessary condition for the existence of a
single

f_{\rm eff}
satisfying (
29
) is

\Sigma(x;\epsilon)\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta(x;\epsilon)=2\hbar\,f_{\rm eff}^{\prime}(x;\epsilon)\ \text{with}\ f_{\rm eff}^{2}(x;\epsilon)=\Sigma(x;\epsilon)/2.

(30)

Equivalently (away from zeros of
\Sigma
), the pair
(\Sigma,\Delta)
must satisfy the differential compatibility
relation

\Delta(x;\epsilon)=\pm\,\frac{\hbar\,\Sigma^{\prime}(x;\epsilon)}{\sqrt{2\,\Sigma(x;\epsilon)}}.

(31)

If (
31
) is violated, then the two component-wise local equivalents describe inequivalent local
oscillators and no single
f_{\rm eff}
exists.
In applications, (
31
) can therefore be used as a quantitative measure of how strongly the
nonlocal interaction breaks the simple SUSY pairing structure in a local representation.

4.7
Finite-rank / separable example (Gaussian model and spurious criterion)

A minimal and computationally tractable kernel model is a rank-one (separable) correction,

f(x,x^{\prime})=f_{0}(x)\,\delta(x-x^{\prime})+\lambda\,u(x)u(x^{\prime}),\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{C},

(32)

with a concrete choice such as a Gaussian profile on the half-line,

u(x)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2a^{2}}\right)\chi_{[0,\infty)}(x),\qquad a>0.

(33)

Finite-rank kernels are attractive because the integro-differential equation reduces to a system of ODEs coupled to a
finite number of moments (inner products) of the solution.
They also arise naturally as operator-theoretic approximations and in the study of singular (e.g.
\delta
-shell)
nonlocal interactions
[
27
]
.

In the pure rank-one case
f_{0}\equiv 0
, the induced partner kernels
V_{1,2}
remain finite rank and the component
problem reduces to an inhomogeneous ODE driven by
u
and
u^{\prime}
together with a small linear system for the unknown
moments.
A spurious threshold is signaled by the vanishing of the corresponding finite-dimensional determinant; in that case

J_{N,j}
develops zeros and
U^{\rm eq}_{j}
typically becomes singular.
The explicit reduction and the determinant criterion are collected in Appendix A.4.

4.8
Solvable benchmarks

Example 1 (local Dirac oscillator).

Take
f(x)=m\omega x
(the Moshinsky–Szczepaniak Dirac oscillator)
[
22
,
23
]
.
Then the partner potentials are

V_{-}(x)=m^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}-\hbar m\omega,\qquad V_{+}(x)=m^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}+\hbar m\omega,

so the component equations reduce to shifted harmonic oscillators.
Using the standard oscillator spectrum
\hbar m\omega(2n+1)
for

-\hbar^{2}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}/\mathrm{d}x^{2}+m^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}
, one obtains

\epsilon_{n}^{(-)}=2\hbar m\omega\,n,\qquad\epsilon_{n}^{(+)}=2\hbar m\omega\,(n+1),\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots

with
\epsilon=(E^{2}-m^{2}c^{4})/c^{2}
.
The Dirac coupling (
6
) pairs the
n
-th level of
V_{+}
with the
(n+1)
-th level of
V_{-}
,
so a convenient labeling yields

E_{n,\pm}=\pm\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}+2\hbar\omega\,mc^{2}\,(n+1)},\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots,

(34)

consistent with standard treatments (see also Ref.
[
25
]
).
At the level of eigenfunctions, one may choose
\psi_{2,n}(x)\propto\phi_{n}(x)
and then

\psi_{1,n}(x)=\tfrac{c}{E_{n,+}-mc^{2}}(p_{x}+\mathrm{i}m\omega x)\psi_{2,n}(x)\propto\phi_{n+1}(x)
, making the inherited
ladder structure explicit.

Example 2 (translation-invariant nonlocal kernel).

On the full line, choose a convolution kernel
f(x,x^{\prime})=g(x-x^{\prime})
with
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then
(\partial_{x}+\partial_{x^{\prime}})f(x,x^{\prime})=0
, hence
[\hat{F},p_{x}]=0
and the partner operators coincide:

A^{\#}A=AA^{\#}=p_{x}^{2}+\hat{F}^{2}
.
In momentum space,
\hat{F}
is multiplication by
\tilde{g}(q)
, so plane waves
e^{\mathrm{i}qx}
diagonalize the problem with

\epsilon(q)=\hbar^{2}q^{2}+|\tilde{g}(q)|^{2},\qquad E_{\pm}(q)=\pm\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}+c^{2}\epsilon(q)}.

(35)

For these plane-wave fundamental solutions the normalized current is constant,
J_{N,j}\equiv 1
, so the damping is
trivial:
A_{j}\equiv 1
.
At a fixed energy (fixed
k^{2}=\epsilon/\hbar^{2}
), the local-equivalent potential becomes a constant chosen to reproduce
the same wave number
q
, namely
U^{\rm eq}=k^{2}-q^{2}
.
This illustrates that localization is most informative when the kernel breaks translation invariance (as in finite-range
optical-model nonlocalities), because otherwise the nonlocality primarily manifests as momentum (energy) dependence.

Example 3 (complex shift: pseudo-Hermitian but isospectral).

Let
f(x)=m\omega(x-\mathrm{i}a)
with
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then
f(x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta)=f^{*}(x)
holds for
\theta=a/\hbar
, and
H_{\rm GDO}
is
\eta
-pseudo-Hermitian under

\eta=e^{-\theta p_{x}}
while remaining isospectral to the real Dirac oscillator up to a similarity transformation

[
21
,
20
]
.
More explicitly,
S=e^{-\theta p_{x}}
acts as
(S\varphi)(x)=\varphi(x+\mathrm{i}a)
on sufficiently regular functions, so

H_{\rm GDO}(a)=S\,H_{\rm GDO}(0)\,S^{-1}
and the eigenfunctions follow by analytic continuation,

\Psi_{n}^{(a)}(x)=\Psi_{n}^{(0)}(x+\mathrm{i}a)
.
This local benchmark mirrors the nonlocal kernel criterion (
21
): spectral reality is ensured by a
controlled complex translation at the level of the interaction profile.

5
Conclusions and outlook

We have developed a nonlocal extension of the generalized Dirac oscillator in
(1+1)
dimensions by promoting the
interaction profile
f(x)
to an integral operator with kernel
f(x,x^{\prime})
.
At the operator level the resulting Dirac Hamiltonian preserves a natural factorization, which allows the full
first-order system to be reduced to two nonlocal Schrödinger-type partner equations for the spinor components.
We derived explicit coordinate-space formulas for the corresponding partner kernels, thereby making the induced
nonlocal supersymmetric structure completely transparent.

A central result is that the complex-translation metric
\eta=e^{-\theta p_{x}}
leads to a simple
kernel-level

sufficient condition for
\eta
-pseudo-Hermiticity,

f(x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta,x^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta)=f^{*}(x^{\prime},x)
, which generalizes the familiar local complex-shift criterion for
pseudo-Hermitian Dirac oscillators.
This provides a practical way to generate nonlocal interactions with controlled spectral reality properties.

To connect the nonlocal dynamics with a local interpretation, we adapted the Coz–Arnold–MacKellar construction to each
component equation, yielding unique energy-dependent local-equivalent potentials and multiplicative damping (Perey)
factors whenever the nonlocal current remains nonzero.
The current criterion makes explicit the limitations of the mapping and ties its breakdown to current zeros and the
appearance of spurious solutions.

Several extensions merit further investigation.
These include: (i) systematic numerical studies for physically motivated kernels (e.g. Perey–Buck-type and modern
dispersive optical-model kernels) and a quantitative analysis of the resulting Perey suppression;
(ii) structural criteria and practical diagnostics for when the two component-wise local equivalents can be represented
by a single effective
f_{\rm eff}(x;\epsilon)
, thereby restoring a local GDO interpretation; and
(iii) generalizations to higher dimensions, to other choices of metric operators (including genuinely nonlocal metrics),
and to additional Lorentz structures beyond the pseudoscalar coupling considered here.

A.1
A.1 Proof of the partner-kernel formula (
18
)

We derive the coordinate-space form of
\hat{F}^{2}
and
[\hat{F},p_{x}]
used to obtain (
18
).

Step 1:
\hat{F}^{2}
.

From (
11
),

(\hat{F}^{2}\phi)(x)=\hat{F}(\hat{F}\phi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}y\,f(x,y)(\hat{F}\phi)(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}y\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,f(x,y)f(y,x^{\prime})\phi(x^{\prime}).

Defining
(f\star f)(x,x^{\prime}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}y\,f(x,y)f(y,x^{\prime})
yields

(\hat{F}^{2}\phi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,(f\star f)(x,x^{\prime})\phi(x^{\prime})
.

Step 2: the commutator
[\hat{F},p_{x}]
.

Compute

(\hat{F}p_{x}\phi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,f(x,x^{\prime})(-\mathrm{i}\hbar)\partial_{x^{\prime}}\phi(x^{\prime}).

Assuming boundary terms vanish, integrate by parts in
x^{\prime}
to obtain

(\hat{F}p_{x}\phi)(x)=\mathrm{i}\hbar\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,(\partial_{x^{\prime}}f(x,x^{\prime}))\phi(x^{\prime})
.
Next,

(p_{x}\hat{F}\phi)(x)=(-\mathrm{i}\hbar)\partial_{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,f(x,x^{\prime})\phi(x^{\prime})=-\mathrm{i}\hbar\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,(\partial_{x}f(x,x^{\prime}))\phi(x^{\prime}).

Therefore

([\hat{F},p_{x}]\phi)(x)=\mathrm{i}\hbar\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,(\partial_{x}+\partial_{x^{\prime}})f(x,x^{\prime})\phi(x^{\prime}),

which leads to (
18
).

A.2
A.2 Proof of the kernel pseudo-Hermiticity condition (
21
)

Let
\eta=e^{-\theta p_{x}}
. Acting on a test function
\phi
,

(\eta^{-1}\phi)(x)=\phi(x-\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta),\qquad(\eta\phi)(x)=\phi(x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta).

Then

(\eta\hat{F}\eta^{-1}\phi)(x)=\eta\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,f(\,\cdot\,,x^{\prime})(\eta^{-1}\phi)(x^{\prime})\right)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,f(x+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta,x^{\prime})\,\phi(x^{\prime}-\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta).

Assuming analyticity in the relevant strip and a legitimate contour shift, the change of variables

x^{\prime}\mapsto x^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\hbar\theta
yields (
20
). Since

(\hat{F}^{\dagger}\phi)(x)=\int\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,f^{*}(x^{\prime},x)\phi(x^{\prime})
, a sufficient condition for

\hat{F}^{\dagger}=\eta\hat{F}\eta^{-1}
is exactly (
21
). Embedding
\hat{F}
into
(
12
) then yields
H_{\rm NLGDO}^{\dagger}=\eta H_{\rm NLGDO}\eta^{-1}
.

A.3
A.3 Proof of the current identity (
26
)

Assume the multiplicative relation (
24
).
Compute the Wronskian

W_{N}:=\psi_{N,+}\partial_{x}\psi_{N,-}-\psi_{N,-}\partial_{x}\psi_{N,+}=A^{2}\big(\psi_{L,+}\partial_{x}\psi_{L,-}-\psi_{L,-}\partial_{x}\psi_{L,+}\big),

because the cross terms proportional to
AA^{\prime}
cancel.
Hence the normalized current
J_{N}=-(2\mathrm{i}k)^{-1}W_{N}
satisfies
J_{N}=A^{2}J_{L}
.
Since
J_{L}\equiv 1
for the local equation with the chosen normalization, we obtain
J_{N}=A^{2}
.

A.4
A.4 Separable-kernel reduction and an explicit spurious-solution trigger

To make the numerical program in Section
4
concrete, consider the pure rank-one case on the half-line:

f(x,x^{\prime})=\lambda u(x)u(x^{\prime}),\qquad x,x^{\prime}\geq 0,

with
u
smooth and rapidly decaying.
Then

(f\star f)(x,x^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\,\lambda u(x)u(y)\,\lambda u(y)u(x^{\prime})=\lambda^{2}c_{u}\,u(x)u(x^{\prime}),\qquad c_{u}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\,u^{2}(y).

Moreover,

(\partial_{x}+\partial_{x^{\prime}})f(x,x^{\prime})=\lambda\big(u^{\prime}(x)u(x^{\prime})+u(x)u^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\big).

Therefore the partner kernels become

V_{1,2}(x,x^{\prime})=\lambda^{2}c_{u}\,u(x)u(x^{\prime})\mp\hbar\lambda\big(u^{\prime}(x)u(x^{\prime})+u(x)u^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\big),

so the component equation (
17
) reads

-\hbar^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\lambda^{2}c_{u}\,u(x)S_{0}\mp\hbar\lambda\big(u^{\prime}(x)S_{0}+u(x)S_{1}\big)=\epsilon\,\psi(x),

where the two moments are

S_{0}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,u(x^{\prime})\psi(x^{\prime}),\qquad S_{1}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,u^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\psi(x^{\prime}).

Thus the nonlocal problem reduces to a forced ODE with forcing in the span of
\{u,u^{\prime}\}
and two unknown constants

(S_{0},S_{1})
.
Projecting onto
u
and
u^{\prime}
yields a
2\times 2
linear system

\bm{M}_{j}(k)\begin{pmatrix}S_{0}\\
S_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\bm{b}_{j}(k),

whose entries are explicit integrals involving the free Green function and
u,u^{\prime}
(details depend on the boundary
condition at
x=0
).
A
spurious threshold
occurs when
\det\bm{M}_{j}(k)=0
: at such energies the Jost pair fails to be linearly
independent,
J_{N,j}(k,x)
develops zeros, and the local-equivalent potential (
28
) becomes singular.
