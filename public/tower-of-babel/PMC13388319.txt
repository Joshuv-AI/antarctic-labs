A historical overview of the cardiovascular safety of sodium oxybate: clinical trials, clinical experience
Authors: Clete A Kushida
Published: 2026

Introduction

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), the leading professional organization for sleep specialists in the United States, issued a clinical practice guideline in 2021 regarding the treatment of central disorders of hypersomnolence, which included the use of sodium oxybate (SO) for patients with narcolepsy [1]. An expert task force commissioned by the AASM conducted a systematic literature review and an assessment of the evidence using the GRADE process to develop and assign strengths to these recommendations [1, 2]. Each recommendation statement by the AASM was assigned a strength of “strong” or “conditional.” The AASM indicated that a “strong” recommendation “is one that clinicians should follow under most circumstances” [1]. A “conditional” recommendation “is one that requires that the clinician use clinical knowledge and experience and strongly consider the individual patient’s values and preferences to determine the best course of action” [1]. The recommendations were that clinicians use SO for the treatment of narcolepsy in adults (strong), idiopathic hypersomnia in adults (conditional), hypersomnia secondary to Parkinson’s disease in adults (conditional), and narcolepsy in pediatric patients (conditional) [1]. Further, for adults and children with narcolepsy, this clinical practice guideline indicated that SO was shown to demonstrate a clinically significant improvement in the critical outcomes of excessive daytime sleepiness, cataplexy, and disease severity [1]. Given the prominence of SO in the treatment of narcolepsy, this article presents a historical overview of the cardiovascular (CV) safety of SO by exploring the sodium in SO formulations, CV health, and clinical trials evaluating sodium risk.

The Sodium in Sodium Oxybate Formulations

Sodium is an essential nutrient for life that has various important roles, including maintaining intravascular volume. SO was first synthesized in 1874, and the first studies of it in narcolepsy were conducted in 1979. The twice-nightly SO formulation was approved in the United States in 2002, and the sodium content in a 3- to 9-g dose, taken in 2 equally divided doses, is 550 to 1640 mg. A formulation of SO was approved in 2020 that contains 92% less sodium, and in 2023, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved a third formulation of SO that contains the same amount of sodium as the original SO product but with a once-nightly dosing regimen.

Cardiovascular Health

In 2022, the American Heart Association (AHA) added sleep duration to its Cardiovascular Health Checklist, which includes diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, body mass index, blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure [3]. Sleep duration was added to the AHA’s Life’s Essential 8 checklist with the following statement: “Sleep duration is associated with cardiovascular health. Measured by average hours of sleep per night, the ideal level is 7 to 9 hours daily for adults. Ideal daily sleep ranges for children are 10 to 16 hours per 24 hours for ages 5 and younger; 9 to 12 hours for ages 6 to 12 years; and 8 to 10 hours for ages 13 to 18 years” [4].

The Burden of Narcolepsy Disease (BOND) study [5] and Cardiovascular Burden of Narcolepsy Disease (CV-BOND) study [6], which relied on retrospective medical claims data analyses, showed an increased risk of CV comorbidities and new-onset events in patients with narcolepsy, respectively, when compared with cohorts without narcolepsy. The increased CV risk in those with narcolepsy can be due to several factors. Jennum and colleagues indicate that metabolic comorbidities such as diabetes, obesity, and dyslipidemia increase CV risk [7]. Additionally, other comorbidities and risk factors include sleep disorders such as obstructive sleep apnea and periodic limb movement disorder, psychiatric conditions, including depression, precocious puberty, and smoking [7]. Sleep duration, particularly short sleep, is widely recognized to be related to adverse cardiometabolic risk in the general population, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity, and CV disease [8]. Sleep duration aside, disrupted nighttime sleep, and excessive daytime sleep, besides being principal characteristics of narcolepsy, are thought to contribute to CV risk in individuals with narcolepsy [7]. Autonomic dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and medications used to treat narcolepsy may also have a role in the increased CV risk associated with narcolepsy.

Interestingly, O’Donnell and colleagues commented in 2020, “Several blood pressure guidelines recommend low sodium intake (<2.3 g/day, 100 mmol, 5.8 g/day of salt) for the entire population, on the premise that reductions in sodium intake, irrespective of the levels, will lower blood pressure, and, in turn, reduce CV disease occurrence. These guidelines have been developed without effective interventions to achieve sustained low sodium intake in free-living individuals, without a feasible method to estimate sodium intake reliably in individuals, and without high-quality evidence that low sodium intake reduces CV events (compared with moderate intake)” [9]. These investigators conducted a literature review to determine if the recommendations for low sodium intake as developed by guideline committees were supported by the available evidence [9]. They concluded that even with limitations in the current evidence, the majority of the world’s population consumes 2.3 to 4.6 g/day of dietary sodium that is not associated with CV risk. Further, they felt that a low-sodium target of less than 2.3 g/day, as seen in several blood pressure guidelines, may not be feasible and had uncertain effectiveness in reducing CV risk. They also observed that the risk of CV disease increases when sodium intake exceeds 5 g/day [9]. No data exist to indicate the estimated sodium content or threshold of SO at which patients might experience increased CV risk.

We conducted a narrative review of the literature to assess the relationship between CV risk and sodium intake, narcolepsy, and SO exposure and found 7 randomized controlled trials and open-label studies, 1 observational study, and 3 surveillance studies evaluating SO in individuals with narcolepsy (Table 1). This review explores in detail studies that have been summarized in an earlier review [10].

Clinical Trials

Randomized controlled trial (2002)

In 2002, the US Xyrem Multicenter Study Group published results of a multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial that compared the effects of 3 doses of SO with placebo in participants with narcolepsy [11]. This study involved 136 participants with narcolepsy who had 3 to 249 (median 21) cataplexy attacks weekly [11]. They were at least 18 years of age, had a polysomnogram within the previous 5 years, and had a current diagnosis of narcolepsy for at least 6 months [11].

Participants had an initial screening visit and then discontinued hypnotic medications and gradually withdrew from medications used to treat cataplexy symptoms over a 4-week period [11]. The participants then had a washout period of 5 days or 5 times the half-life of the discontinued anticataplectic medication, whichever was longer, but not exceeding 28 days [11]. Participants could remain on stimulant medications as long as stable doses were used, starting 5 days prior to baseline and continuing until the end of the trial; 113/136 (83.1%) participants remained on a stable dose of stimulant [11]. Participants recorded the frequency of cataplexy and other specified symptoms of narcolepsy in daily diaries over a subsequent 14- to 21-day baseline period. A minimum of 3 cataplexy attacks per week during the last 2 weeks of the baseline period was required to enter the double-blind treatment phase, and the majority of participants showed moderate to severe cataplexy. At the beginning of the 4-week treatment period, each participant was randomized to receive 3, 6, or 9 g of SO or placebo (without titration), administered nightly in equally divided doses immediately upon retiring to bed and 2.5 to 4 hours later [11]. Each participant was monitored by a telephone call from a trial center study coordinator after their first night on medication and 3 times weekly thereafter; participants also returned to their trial center after 2 and 4 weeks of treatment. Compliance with the study protocol was assessed by medication counts, and clinical assessments were conducted at the end of the 4 weeks; 3 to 5 days after the study, medication was discontinued [11]. Participants used daily diaries to record the number of cataplexy attacks, number and duration of nocturnal awakenings, total amount of sleep each night, number of hypnagogic hallucinations, incidence of sleep paralysis, and adverse events (AEs) [11].

The primary outcome measure of efficacy was the change from baseline in weekly cataplexy attacks using the Clinical Global Impression of Change (CGI-C) [11]. Secondary measures included daytime sleepiness using the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) at study baseline and endpoint, inadvertent daytime naps/sleep attacks, and nighttime awakenings. Investigators assessed changes in disease severity. Compared with placebo, SO decreased weekly cataplexy attacks at the 6-g dose (p = .0529) and significantly at the 9-g dose (p = .0008) [11]. The ESS was reduced at all doses, becoming significant at the 9-g dose (p = .0001). The CGI-C demonstrated a dose-related improvement, significant at the 9-g dose (p = .0002) [11]. The frequency of inadvertent naps/sleep attacks and the nighttime awakenings showed similar dose–response trends, becoming significant at the 9-g dose (p = .0122 and p = .0035, respectively) [11].

Safety assessment was conducted by reviewing daily diary entries, interviewing participants regarding possible AEs, clinical laboratory studies, electrocardiography (ECG), and changes in physical condition [11]. Most AEs occurred at the beginning of the study, within the first few days following the start of treatment, and tended to decrease over time. The AEs that occurred at a significant level and in a dose-related fashion that were believed to be related to SO included nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and enuresis (p < .05 for all) [11]. SO appeared to have no measurable effect on serum electrolytes, hematological measures, hepatic or renal function, or ECG [11]. Abnormal laboratory findings were reported in 10 participants; these were previously either present at baseline, occurred in placebo participants, or resolved during the study, making a relationship with the study medication unlikely [11]. A slight dose-related urine pH increase (0.67 pH unit at the 9-g dose) was attributed to the increased sodium consumption with the study medication. Blood pressure remained unchanged. Sixteen participants did not complete the trial, and 10 of them withdrew from the study due to the occurrence of an AE, of which all but 1 were considered to be mild or moderate in severity [11]. The 1 serious adverse event (SAE) occurred in a participant in the 6-g treatment group, who experienced an acute confusional state occurring in the afternoon following nighttime dosing the prior evening and despite the extremely short half-life of SO [11]. The participant was treated, recovered, and was discontinued from the SO. For this 2002 study, no CV events were reported.

Open-label extension trial (2003)

In 2003, the US Xyrem Multicenter Study Group published the results of a 12-month, multicenter, open-label extension trial of the prior 2002 randomized controlled trial [11], which evaluated the long-term safety and efficacy of SO in participants with narcolepsy [12]. This study involved 118 participants with narcolepsy previously enrolled in a 4-week double-blind SO trial [11]. In addition to the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the 2002 randomized controlled trial [11], participants were excluded if they were on investigational therapy other than SO/placebo in the 30 days prior to the study.

A total of 118 participants elected to enroll in this trial following a 3- to 5-day washout period of SO at the end of the randomized controlled trial [12]. Participants began treatment at 6 g of SO nightly, taken in 2 equally divided doses. Based on clinical efficacy or adverse experiences, investigators could increase or decrease the dose in 1.5-g increments at 2-week intervals but stayed within the range of 3 to 9 g nightly [12]. Participants could continue stimulant medications but were required to remain on a stable dose throughout the trial. Of the 118 participants, 104 (88.1%) were taking 1 or more concomitant stimulant medications. Participants were evaluated by the investigators every 2 weeks during the first month, then every 2 months until month 12; participants’ vital signs were measured at each visit. Physical and neurological examinations, clinical laboratory tests, and ECG were conducted at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months. Daytime subjective somnolence was assessed with the ESS, and overall disease severity was estimated using the Clinical Global Impression of Severity (CGI-S) at baseline with subsequent change in disease severity measured with CGI-C at each visit [12]. During the 12 months, participants used the same daily personal diaries as in the prior randomized controlled trial to record the same information as in the prior trial [12].

SO produced overall improvements in narcolepsy symptoms, which were significant at 4 weeks and maximal after 8 weeks [12]. Reported improvements included a significant decrease in frequency of cataplexy attacks (p < .001); diminished daytime sleepiness (p < .001); and participant descriptions of nocturnal sleep quality, level of alertness, and ability to concentrate (p < .001 for all) [12].

Safety measures included adverse experiences, clinical laboratory analyses, vital sign measurements, and general physical examination [12]. The most commonly reported AEs were headache, nausea, viral infection, dizziness, pain, enuresis, and somnolence, and only dizziness occurred at a significant level for overall treatment group comparisons [12]. Three participants experienced an SAE during the course of the study. One SAE consisted of recurrent chest pain, and although the participant’s ECG was normal, the investigator chose to discontinue SO [12]. Subsequently, 26 days after the last dose of SO, the participant suffered a mild myocardial infarction and withdrew from the study; this event was not considered to be related to SO therapy. The second SAE was ductal breast carcinoma requiring surgical removal, which was not considered to be related to therapy. The third SAE was agitation, which was possibly related to the medication, but the patient completed the study without recurrence. For laboratory abnormalities, significant changes in serum phosphorus across dose groups and serum calcium only within the group on the 6-g dose were observed. A mild increase in serum glutamate pyruvate transaminase was possibly related to the study medication but was considered to be of no clinical significance [12]. The only significant change in vital signs was a mild decrease in diastolic blood pressure observed across treatment groups, which was determined to be clinically insignificant. Eleven participants chose to discontinue participation in the study due to an AE during the first 12 months of the trial [12]. For this 2003 study, there was 1 CV event reported, but it was not considered to be related to SO therapy.

Randomized treatment withdrawal trial (2004)

In 2004, the US Xyrem Multicenter Study Group published the results of a multicenter, randomized, treatment-withdrawal trial that assessed the efficacy of SO for the long-term treatment of cataplexy in participants with narcolepsy [13]. This study involved 55 participants with narcolepsy with cataplexy who had received SO for 7 to 44 months (mean 21 months) and were then enrolled in a double-blind treatment-withdrawal study. Participants were at least 16 years of age and were required to have a polysomnogram and multiple sleep latency tests (MSLTs) within the previous 5 years, a current diagnosis of narcolepsy, a history of 5 or more cataplexy attacks per week prior to receiving any treatment for cataplexy, have undergone continuous treatment with SO for at least 6 months prior to study entry, and if female, a negative pregnancy test and agreement to use a medically accepted form of birth control throughout the trial [13].

Participants were drawn from a pool of patients who had undergone continuous SO therapy for a minimum of 6 months (range 7–44 months; mean 21 months) in a long-term, open-label SO safety trial [13]. SO was administered in equally divided doses immediately upon retiring to bed and repeated 2.5 to 4 hours later (1.5–4.5 g twice nightly) [13]. After an initial screening visit, participants entered into the first 2 weeks and continued taking SO in a single-blind (participant-blinded) fashion using the same twice-nightly doses used previously in the open-label trial [13]. They received daily diaries to record the baseline frequency of cataplexy attacks as well as any AEs. During the subsequent 2-week phase, half of the participants were randomly assigned to continue SO treatment at their previously established dosage; the other half received placebo in a double-blind fashion. SO or placebo was administered in equally divided doses immediately upon going to bed and again 2.5 to 4 hours later [13]. Participants continued to use daily diaries to record the frequency of cataplexy attacks and AEs.

During the 2-week double-blind phase, participants receiving placebo reported a significant increase in the number of cataplexy attacks (median 21; p < .001) from 15.8 (39.9; 0, 197) to 46.4 (73.8; 0, 250) compared with participants who remained on SO (median 0) [13]. Cataplexy attacks returned gradually, with placebo participants reporting a median of 4.2 and 11.7 cataplexy attacks during the first and second weeks, respectively.

Safety assessments were conducted at the beginning and end of the trial and consisted of reviewing daily diaries, participant interviews regarding possible AEs, physical examinations, and clinical laboratory and vital sign measures [13]. Participants entered the trial on stable SO therapy and maintained their treatment through the single-blind phase; thus, treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) were not expected to be prevalent. Only 17 participants (31%) reported an AE during the course of the study [13]. During the double-blind treatment period, AEs were reported by 12 (22%) participants (3 participants in the SO group and 9 in the placebo group) [13]. AEs occurring in the placebo group included insomnia (1), headache (2), and anxiety (3). No AE led to participant discontinuation, and none were serious [13]. No clinically significant shifts in laboratory parameters occurred during the trial, and no CV events were reported for this 2004 study.

Randomized controlled trial (2005)

In 2005, the US Xyrem Multicenter Study Group published the results of a multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial that further measured the effect of SO on cataplexy in participants with narcolepsy [14, 15]. This study involved 228 participants with narcolepsy with cataplexy who were at least 16 years of age [14, 15]. Participants were required to have a polysomnogram and MSLTs within the previous 5 years; current symptoms of narcolepsy almost daily for at least 3 months; evidence of adequate support for the duration of the trial, including transportation to/from the trial site; agreement to forego operating a car or heavy machinery if indicated by the investigator; willingness to complete the entire trial; and if female and of childbearing potential, a medically accepted method of birth control unless surgically sterile or 2 years postmenopausal [14, 15].

The study consisted of participants recording narcolepsy symptoms and AEs associated with current narcolepsy treatments in daily diaries during a 14-day lead-in period [14, 15]. This was followed by a 21-day treatment withdrawal period and a washout period of 5 days or 5 times the half-life of the discontinued drug, whichever was longer, but not exceeding 18 days [14, 15]. Participants were then randomized and started on a placebo in a single-blind fashion and asked to record baseline cataplexy occurrences for 14 to 21 days [14, 15]. This was followed by an 8-week treatment period on study medication or placebo titrated to the final dose [14, 15]. Each participant returned to the clinic for an efficacy and safety assessment after 4 weeks on study medication so that all study measures could be conducted [14, 15]. Subsequently, participants continued at their assigned dose for an additional 28 days before returning for a final efficacy and safety assessment [14, 15].

Compared with placebo, nightly doses of 4.5, 6, and 9 g of SO for 8 weeks resulted in statistically significant median decreases in weekly cataplexy attacks of 57.0%, 65.0%, and 84.7%, respectively [14, 15]. The decrease in cataplexy at the 4.5-g dose was significant compared with placebo at 8 weeks of treatment (p = .003) [14, 15].

Safety assessments at the initial and final visits included a physical examination; measurement of vital signs, including blood pressure, heart rate, respiration rate, temperature, height, and weight; and ECG, if clinically indicated [14, 15]. Clinical laboratory tests included hematology, clinical chemistry, and a serum pregnancy test, if applicable. The adverse event population included participants receiving a single-blind placebo, totaling 246 participants. Twenty-one participants discontinued the trial due to an AE [14, 15]. AEs with greater than 5% incidence included nausea and dizziness, which appeared to be dose related [14, 15]. Events occurring with less than 5% incidence included feeling drunk, contusion, back pain, muscle cramp, somnolence, disturbance in attention, dysarthria, tremor, eye irritation, disorientation, sleepwalking, dyspnea, and snoring [14, 15]. Six SAEs were reported during the study; 3 occurred during the single-blind placebo phase of the trial and were considered unrelated to the study medication, and 3 occurred during the double-blind phase [14, 15]. One SAE consisted of acute onset of fever, chills, and shortness of breath and was considered unrelated to SO; the second was an accidental fall resulting in a fractured ankle, but it is unknown whether the event was related to SO [14, 15]. The final SAE was an abnormally high amino alanine transferase and aspartate amino transferase, which was possibly due to the study medication [14, 15]. For this 2005 study, no CV events were reported.

Open-label trial (2015)

In 2015, Mamelak and colleagues published the results of a multicenter open-label trial that evaluated the safety and efficacy of SO titrated to effect [16]. This 12-week trial enrolled 202 participants with a history of narcolepsy with cataplexy who were SO naive or had participated in 1 of 3 randomized clinical trials [11, 17, 18] of SO and had not been titrated to an adequate clinical effect. Other inclusion criteria were age 16 years or older; a history of daytime sleepiness; no investigational therapy, with the exception of SO within 30 days prior to screening; and if female, was surgically sterile, 2 years postmenopausal, or if of childbearing potential, used a medically accepted method of birth control and agreed to continue use of this method during the study [16].

Efficacy was evaluated subjectively using the Narcolepsy Symptom Assessment Questionnaire (NSAQ), an unvalidated patient-reported outcome that assesses changes in the participant’s overall narcolepsy status and in individual symptoms [16]. Of 202 participants, 171 (85%) completed treatment; final doses were 3 g (n = 5), 4.5 g (n = 29), 6 g (n = 80), 7.5 g (n = 66), and 9 g (n = 22) [16]. Response rate was 92% at week 6 and 90% at week 12; most participants reported improvements in all individual symptoms. Overall, 60% of participants rated their symptoms at 12 weeks as “much improved,” and this improvement was dose dependent [16].

Safety and tolerability were evaluated based on the incidence of AEs and on clinical laboratory assessments and vital sign measurements taken at every 6-week visit. AEs were reported by 56% of participants [16]. Nine participants discontinued due to a variety of AEs that included psychosis, migraine headache, dizziness, nausea, anxiety, fatigue, insomnia, abdominal pain, shortness of breath, and depression. Most AEs were considered by the investigators to be of mild or moderate severity, and the most common AEs were nausea (10%), headache (7%), and dizziness (5%) that did not appear to be dose related [16]. Five participants had SAEs, and 2 of these, headache and psychosis, were SAEs that were considered treatment related [16]. Overall changes in laboratory results were minimal and appeared to be unrelated to dose. No trends were observed for the few vital sign changes or physical examination variables that were reported [16]. For this 2015 study, no CV events were reported.

Pediatric randomized controlled trial with open-label extension (2018)

In 2018, Plazzi and colleagues published the results of a multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized-withdrawal trial with an open-label extension that evaluated the safety and efficacy of SO in children and adolescents with narcolepsy type 1 (NT1) [19]. This study enrolled 106 participants who were either SO naive or treated with SO at entry (a stable dose for ≥2 months with reported clinical improvement of cataplexy). Participants were 7 to 16 years old at screening with a primary diagnosis of NT1, HLA-DQB1*06:02 positive or had cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) hypocretin-1 concentrations of <110 pg/mL, at least 14 cataplexy attacks in a typical 2-week period, and clinically significant excessive daytime sleepiness before any narcolepsy treatment [19].

The study consisted of screening, open-label titration (3–10 weeks for only SO-naive participants), a stable dose for 2 weeks, double-blind randomized-withdrawal treatment for 2 weeks, and open-label safety periods of up to 1 year [19]. Throughout the study, all participants, with the help of a caregiver, if needed, completed an electronic diary assessment to record daily cataplexy frequency, study drug doses, and, if and when applicable, doses of a stimulant or wake-promoting agent that were taken [19].

The efficacy population consisted of all participants randomly assigned to receive an intervention who completed at least 5 days of dosing in the double-blind treatment period; the safety population consisted of all participants who took the study drug, including open-label SO [19]. Of the 106 participants enrolled, 104 took the study drug (the safety population); 96 (92%) of the 104 completed the stable-dose period, of whom 63 participants (the efficacy population) were randomly assigned to receive SO (n = 31) or placebo (n = 32) for 2 weeks [19]. The interim analysis of the primary endpoint (ie, change in weekly number of cataplexy attacks) showed efficacy (p = .0002), resulting in discontinuation of the placebo arm; 33 participants then received SO on an open-label basis during the double-blind period [19]. Participants who were randomly assigned to receive placebo and who were withdrawn from SO (51% of patients) had significantly increased weekly cataplexy attacks when compared with those randomly assigned to continue treatment with SO [19]. Analysis of CGI-C for cataplexy severity showed that participants who received placebo were rated as having worse cataplexy severity than participants who continued SO treatment, and the median change from baseline in the Epworth Sleepiness Scale for Children and Adolescents (ESS-CHAD) scores was greater in the placebo group [19].

Safety was assessed by the incidence of TEAEs during the 4 study periods when SO was administered; vital signs, ECG, polysomnogram parameters, clinical laboratory values, and growth (height, weight, growth hormone, and pubertal assessments) were assessed, as well as anxiety, depression, and suicide risk [19]. Overall, 75 (72%) of 104 participants reported TEAEs across all study periods. Half of the 104 participants had TEAEs considered by the investigator to be related to SO. The most commonly reported TEAEs (>5%) were enuresis, nausea, vomiting, headache, decreased weight, decreased appetite, nasopharyngitis, and dizziness [19]. Most TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity, and 2 SAEs were reported that were considered to be related to the study drug: 1 event of acute psychosis was severe and the other event of suicidal ideation was moderate in severity [19]. A decreasing trend in body mass index (BMI) percentile during the course of the study was observed, with weight decrease reported as an adverse event in 13 participants [19]. An increased (>10) central sleep apnea index was reported as a TEAE in 2 participants [19]. No notable findings of clinical relevance were observed in clinical laboratory values, ECGs, or vital signs [19]. For this 2018 study, no CV events were reported.

Open-label trial (2025)

In 2025, White and colleagues published the results of a multicenter, single-arm, open-label, switch trial in the United States and Europe that evaluated an elevated CV risk in patients with narcolepsy [20]. The study enrolled individuals 18 to 70 years old with NT1 or NT2 who had taken twice-nightly high-SO at doses of 6 to 9 g/night for at least 6 weeks before screening. Participants were also required to have office systolic blood pressure (SBP) values of 130 to 155 mm Hg and a diastolic BP (DBP) of less than 95 mm Hg. The 43 participants were required to maintain non-oxybate medications for narcolepsy and antihypertensive agents with the same regimen throughout the study, and to complete a record of time-to-sleep and time-to-awakening, as well as a dosing diary. Participants were excluded if they had a history/presence of treatment-resistant hypertension, significant CV disease or atrial fibrillation, an acutely unstable medical condition, behavioral or psychiatric disorder, surgical history that could affect participant safety or interfere with study assessments or participation, renal impairment, or nighttime or variable shift work [20].

A screening period of up to 3 weeks was followed by an intervention period of approximately 6 weeks, and a safety follow-up visit after the last SO dose [20]. At the start of the intervention period, participants were switched over 1 night from high SO to a gram-per-gram equivalent dose of low-SO (calcium, magnesium, potassium, and SO), and were required to remain on a stable dose of low-SO (±1.5 g/night) throughout the study. The safety population included any study participant who received at least 1 dose of low-SO.

The primary endpoint was the change in 24-hour ambulatory SBP from baseline to the end of treatment (6 weeks following the switch from high- to low-SO). Mean (SD) total high- and low-SO dosages of 8.0 (1.1) and 8.1 (1.1) g/night, respectively, represented 1456.5 (206.2) and 117.8 (16.3) mg of sodium [20]. Mean (SE) 24-hour ambulatory SBPs at baseline and study end were 132.3 (1.8) and 128.2 (1.8) mm Hg, respectively (least-squares mean change, −4.1 [95% CI, −6.9 to −1.4] mm Hg; one-sided p = .0019) [20].

After completing the end-of-treatment ambulatory BP study, approximately 40% of participants remained on low-SO; the remainder returned to high-SO due to medication availability in Europe and US-related insurance issues [20]. TEAEs occurred in 40.3% of participants in the safety population (n/N = 27/67), all of which were mild (31.3%) or moderate (9.0%) in severity and resolved in 85.2% by study end; 14.9% of participants experienced a TEAE that was considered drug-related [20]. The most common TEAEs were upper respiratory tract infection (4.5%), nausea (3.0%), vomiting (3.0%), and dysgeusia (3.0%) [20]. There were no serious or fatal adverse events, and no TEAEs resulted in discontinuation, dose reduction, dose increase, or interruption of low-SO [20].

Observational study (2017)

In 2017, Drakatos and colleagues published results of a retrospective single-center study that included patients at a tertiary referral sleep disorders center with severe narcolepsy with cataplexy refractory to other treatments, who were initiated on SO between 2009 and 2015 [21]. Between 2009 and 2015, all patients who were diagnosed with NT1 and initiated on SO were included, and a medical history was documented that included demographics, sleep history, and sleep study results [21]. The reduction of the ESS score (ΔESS) and cataplexy events expressed in events/week (Δcataplexy) were recorded using patients’ notes and diaries. These changes were the differences between ESS and cataplexy events before SO initiation (baseline) and after an SO dose, providing the optimal clinical effect that was achieved (final). Patients were allowed to be on other stimulants or/and anticataplectic agents [21]. Per policies for prescribing SO within the UK National Health Service (NHS), patients were only eligible for this drug if they had tried at least 2 stimulants and/or 2 anticataplectic agents and remained symptomatic with an ESS of 17 or with an average of 21 cataplectic events per week [21]. All patients in this study were initially treated with oral SO 4.5 g/night up to a maximum dose of 9 g/night, with dose adjustments every 2 weeks [21].

Ninety patients were prescribed SO, with a total of 3,116 patient-months of drug exposure. ESS and weekly cataplexy events were significantly reduced by SO for all patients (p < .0001) [21].

A total of 34 possible AEs associated with the administration of SO were recorded. A total of 55.6% (50/90) of patients experienced an AE, and in 48% (24/50) of these cases, the drug had to be discontinued with subsequent resolution of the AE [21]. The AEs that led to drug discontinuation were viral infection, psychosis, suicidal ideation, mood swings, anxiety, headaches, impaired concentration, dizziness, unresponsiveness, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, fecal and urinary incontinence, gastroesophageal reflex disease, loss of appetite, sweating, facial swelling, bad taste, pain, enuresis, worse cataplexy, hypertension, and palpitations; 11 patients experienced more than 1 AE [21]. Among these, the most common AEs resulting in drug discontinuation were nausea, headaches, mood swings, and dizziness [21]. Psychosis as a serious limiting AE was identified in 3 patients, 1 of whom also exhibited suicidal ideation [21].

Surveillance Studies

International Postmarketing Surveillance Study 2002 to 2008 (2009)

This study, published by Wang and colleagues in 2009, reviewed the cumulative postmarketing and clinical safety experience with SO [22]. The study objectives were to investigate the occurrence of abuse/misuse of SO since its first market introduction in 2002, classify cases for substance abuse and dependence (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th Edition [DSM-IV] criteria), and describe specific characteristics of these cases [22].

The postmarketing safety of SO is monitored through the US spontaneous AE reporting MedWatch system, and a postmarketing evaluation program (PMEP) for Xyrem was also implemented from 2002 to November 2005 to solicit safety data on an additional 1000 patients after market introduction [22]. Under the PMEP, physicians were asked to provide AE information for the first 6 months of therapy, see their patients at least every 3 months, and write prescriptions that covered no more than 3 months of therapy [22]. Physicians were also asked to report any potentially inappropriate use of the product. The investigators searched all the AE cases that were reported to Jazz Pharmaceuticals since the US launch of Xyrem in 2002 through March 2008, which included cases from the United States, the EU, Switzerland, and Canada. Cases were reviewed from the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) that contained selected relevant terms for adverse events, as well as for cases with an outcome of “death” or “fatal” [22]. All reported abuse or dependence cases were rated on a scale of 1 to 4 by a physician, with 1 being least likely to be a case of abuse or dependence and 4 being most likely to be a case of abuse or dependence [22].

A total of 928 PMEP reports were received by July 2006, representing 692 unique patients; the 10 most commonly reported AEs from solicited and spontaneous reports were nausea, insomnia, headache, dizziness, vomiting, somnolence, initial insomnia, feeling abnormal, confusional state, and tremor/anxiety [22]. From product introduction in the United States, the EU, and Canada through March 2008, an estimated 26 000 patients received treatment with SO at different doses [22]. During that period, 3781 AE reports were relayed to the manufacturer worldwide, which represented 973 unique AEs. Regarding CV AEs, increased blood pressure was reported in 0.4% (94/26 000) [22].

International Postmarketing Surveillance Study 2002 to 2011 (2011)

This article, published by Wang and colleagues in 2011 [23], presents additional safety data to correct the previously reported number of deaths that was published in the prior International Postmarketing Surveillance Study 2002–2008 (2009), which is described in the prior section, and to provide updated information and analysis of these cases through May 31, 2011 [23].

The corrected total number of deaths from any cause in patients who had received SO for the reporting period of 2002 through March 30, 2008, was 112 (30 reported originally, plus 82 newly identified cases) [23]. Worldwide, there was a total of 227 deaths in patients who received SO via prescription from market introduction in 2002 through May 31, 2011 [23]. Regarding CV AEs, there were 17 deaths attributed to cardiac events and 6 deaths attributed to cerebrovascular accidents [23]. Among these 227 cases, 11 are non-US cases, and the exposure for this time period in the United States was 37 992 patient-years [23]. As the authors state, using the same calculation (worldwide number of cases as numerator; US-only exposure numbers as denominator), the overall mortality rate for this period is 0.60 per 100 patient-years of exposure [23].

Post-authorization noninterventional European 10-year surveillance study 2006 to 2016 (2018)

In 2018, Mayer and colleagues published the results of a post-authorization, noninterventional surveillance study that was conducted at the request of the European Medicines Agency (EMA) to inform the risk management plan for SO [24]. The study objectives were the evaluation of adherence to the prescribing information, adherence of patients with instructions for use, AEs in patients treated with SO in routine clinical practice, and broadening the knowledge regarding the potential for misuse, overdose, and abuse. The study, which was conducted from May 2006 to September 2016 in 41 European centers, enrolled any patients who were prescribed SO by a sleep specialist, irrespective of diagnosis, and who permitted their data to be collected [24].

Patients were monitored according to normal good clinical practice for a target duration of 18 months, and study visits or telephone monitoring was conducted at weeks 0, 2, 4, and 12 and then every 3 months up to week 72, with a follow-up visit scheduled 2 weeks after the last SO dose [24]. Vital signs were measured at weeks 0, 2, 4, 12, 48, and 72 and at the follow-up visit. The median SO dose at the start of the study was 4.5 g per night and 6 g per night (2 equal doses) throughout the whole study [24].

A total of 749 patients were enrolled with 730 included in the intent-to-treat (ITT) population. SO was prescribed to treat NT1 for 670 patients [24]. The remaining 60 patients had other potentially off-label indications, most commonly narcolepsy unspecified (n = 24), narcolepsy without cataplexy (n = 23), and idiopathic hypersomnia (n = 11) [24].

At least 1 TEAE was reported by 67.3% (451/670) of patients, and the most frequently reported (incidence ≥5%) were headache, nasopharyngitis, decreased weight, dizziness, and nausea [24]. Serious TEAEs were reported by 6.4% (43/670) of patients, and the most frequent were depression (3 patients, 0.4%), angina pectoris, inguinal hernia, and suicide attempt (2 patients, 0.3% each) [24]. Regarding CV TEAEs for those with NT1, hypertension was observed in 0.4% (3/670), angina pectoris in 0.3% (2/670), cerebrovascular disorder in 0.1% (1/670), and circulatory collapse in 0.1% (1/670) [24].

Conclusions

Increased CV risk in patients with narcolepsy is multifactorial and includes disrupted nighttime sleep, excessive daytime sleepiness, medications, metabolic comorbidities, concomitant sleep disorders, and lifestyle factors. Daily sodium intake should be a consideration in patients with narcolepsy who are treated with SO and have comorbid disorders associated with increased CV risk, in whom sodium intake may raise a concern. However, the question remains whether all patients with narcolepsy treated with SO should modify or restrict their sodium intake. The results of this evaluation suggest that while increased CV risk is associated with extremes of daily sodium intake and that narcolepsy is associated with comorbidities that may increase CV risk in some patients, such as those with hypertension, the collective findings described herein of SO use in patients with narcolepsy demonstrate (1) a very low frequency of CV side effects (eg, hypertension) and (2) no overall association with CV risk. In conclusion, and in agreement with an earlier review [10], in the absence of data that specifically address CV risk with SO based on its sodium content, the clinical research evidence to date suggests that SO treatment does not confer additional CV risk in patients with narcolepsy. Future research, particularly in the form of additional randomized controlled trials and surveillance studies, is anticipated to shed further light on SO and CV risk.
