Feasibility of Occipital Condyle Screw Fixation for Craniocervical Instability: Integrating Morphometric Evidence, Anatomical Parameters, and Experience From Surgical Series

Fekete, Daniel Haschtmann, Markus Loibl, Dezsö J. Jeszenszky, Maria L. Gandía-González, Menno R. Germans, David Bellut, Carlo Serra, Luca Regli, Erik Edström, Adrian Elmi-Terander, Victor E. Staartjes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Instability of the craniocervical junction is a potentially life-threatening condition requiring surgical stabilization. Traditional occipital plate fixation carries risks of construct loosening and intracranial complications due to variable skull thickness, particularly after posterior fossa decompression where plate fixation is challenging. Occipital condyle screws (OCS) provide direct fixation into the occipital condyles (OCs). However, comprehensive outcome data remains sparse. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated anatomical parameters, technical aspects, and surgical outcomes of OCS fixation in craniocervical stabilization. Following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) guidelines, PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus were searched for studies reporting techniques and outcomes of occipitocervical fixation using OCS. Two reviewers independently extracted data, and study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, when possible. Random-effects meta-analysis was performed. The primary endpoint was to characterize the technical aspects of craniocervical fixation using OCS and to ascertain its overall feasibility, defined by morphometric suitability, technical success rates, and complication rates. Thirty studies met inclusion: 12 cadaveric (618 specimens), 10 imaging (1,604 participants), and 8 surgical (284 patients). Morphometry consistently showed larger OC in male populations. Bicortical screw placement achieved 100% technical success. Standard 3.5-mm screws (18–24 mm) were commonly used. Recommended trajectories varied (sagittal with 18°–28° angulation; axial with 22°–37° angulation). No major symptomatic vascular or permanent neurological complications occurred. Meta-analytic data revealed significant differences in morphometric measurements of the OC and differences in the OCS length and angulation parameters. OCS fixation appears to be an anatomically feasible and technically promising fixation strategy in selected patients when anatomy and technique are carefully evaluated. Population-specific morphometric variability mandates individualized preoperative assessment. Future comparative studies should define long-term outcomes, fusion rates, and optimize region-specific surgical parameters.

INTRODUCTION

Craniocervical instability is a potentially life-threatening condition caused by trauma, benign or malignant neoplasms, rheumatologic diseases, infections, congenital anomalies and degenerative processes [1-5]. Due to the risk of severe neurological deficits and death, surgical fixation of the occipitocervical junction is needed [3,4,6-9]/Multiple techniques have been described to achieve stability, including the use of plates and rods, which rely on fixation using screws placed through the occipital bone [4,8,10]. However, variable cranial bone thickness can limit screw hold and intracranial penetration may result in dural tear with cerebrospinal fluid leak, venous sinus injury or epidural hematoma. Furthermore, some techniques may not be feasible in all patients due to anatomical variations, or large suboccipital craniectomy defects [2,6,8,11,12].

Occipital condyle screws (OCS) offer direct anchorage in the occipital condyles (OCs) and may overcome some of the previously described challenges. First formally introduced by Uribe et al. [13] in 2008, this technique has gained increasing attention for its potential advantages and there have been multiple studies analyzing its feasibility using imaging modalities, cadaver dissections and real-world surgical application. Nonetheless, OCS remain underutilized due to concerns surrounding its surgicaltechnical feasibility, safety, biomechanical stability and the lack of comprehensive outcome data [1-3,6-8,11-15]. Published data on this topic remains scarce. In this context, systematic reviews can lead to more realistic results with better generalizability and less risk of bias compared to single studies [16]. We therefore systematically reviewed the literature to evaluate anatomical and surgicaltechnical considerations, as well as the overall feasibility of OCS in occipitocervical fixation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Overview and Data Collection

A review of the published literature was carried out to identify any studies reporting (1) anatomical morphometrics aspects, (2) techniques, and (3) outcomes of occipitocervical fixation using OCS in pediatric and adult populations. The primary endpoint was to characterize OCS fixation and ascertain its feasibility across anatomical, imaging, and clinical studies. Title and abstract screening, full-text review and data extraction were handled independently by 2 reviewers (ST and IB) using Rayyan (Rayyan systematic review software, USA). Disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus. Persisting disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (VS). We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) protocol [16]. The review protocol was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO or any other database.

2. Search Strategy

The PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus databases were searched to identify eligible articles. A comprehensive search strategy was developed using relevant MeSH (medical subject heading) terms and keywords as follows: (occipital OR occiput OR occipitocervical) AND (condyle OR condylar OR transcondylar) AND (fusion OR fixation OR screw) AND (surgery OR surgical OR technique OR stability OR stabilization OR instability). Word variations and medical subject headings were searched for whenever feasible. Moreover, references were screened to identify additional relevant articles. Additional studies were identified by manually screening reference lists of relevant articles and reviews. The last comprehensive search was conducted on May 31, 2026, with no backward limitations.

3. Study Selection

Human cadaveric and radiological studies evaluating the relation between OC dimension and OCS placement, and surgical in vivo studies of all age groups, were considered. Given the anticipated lack of randomized trials, we included prospective and retrospective single-arm cohorts, case series and case reports that assessed at least one abovementioned outcome. Biomechanical studies of occipitocervical constructs were not included for analysis in the systematic review and were instead considered as contextual background literature. Exact cohort duplicates and reviews were excluded, but updated cohorts were included when sample size increased by at least 50%. To be considered, cadavers or patients had to undergo craniocervical fixation with insertion of OCS. Included studies selected for qualitative analysis were divided in cadaveric, imaging and operative.

4. Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

We extracted the following information when available from the included publications: Study design and year of publication, radiological/in-vivo/cadaver study, number of patients/cadavers, mean patient age and gender distribution, underlying disease, technique of insertion, anatomical features of the OC by side and gender, measurements of the used OCS and intra- and postoperative complication rate. The methodological quality of the included studies was graded using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [17], whenever possible. Technical case reports and small case studies and noncomparative series, where the NOS is not applicable, were a priori classified as very low level of evidence. For cadaveric and imaging studies that were not gradeable with this tool (for example, purely descriptive morphometric series, technical reports, or very small case series), we used simple predefined criteria to characterize study quality, including sample size, clarity and reproducibility of the measurement protocol reporting of intra-or interobserver reliability, and adequacy of image acquisition or specimen preparation. No single validated tool was applicable across all included cadaveric, imaging-based morphometric, technical, and operative study designs. Because of this, studies were classified as very low level of evidence when they had small sample size and lacked information on measurement reliability or standardized imaging methodology.

5. Statistical Meta-analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R [18]. Based on the anticipated heterogeneity and the lack of larger randomized controlled trials for most outcomes, a random-effects analysis model was applied (DerSimonian and Laird [19]) to compare the lengths, heights and diameters of the OC, as well as the lengths, diameters, and axial and sagittal angles of the inserted OCS. Single-arm meta-analysis of mean values, of both sides when applicable, were conducted. Forest plots were generated for single-group analysis as well as to compare male and female specimens. Cochran Q and I2 were used to evaluate the strength of evidence for heterogeneity and the level of heterogeneity, respectively. Because only a small number of studies contributed data to most morphometric and screw trajectory endpoints and reporting of key study-level characteristics was inconsistent, formal subgroup analyses and meta-regression to investigate sources of heterogeneity were not feasible. Quantitative pooling of clinical outcomes was also not possible because surgical se-ries were sparse, heterogeneous in design and indications, and severe inconsistencies in reporting were observed, precluding valid meta-analytic synthesis. A p≤0.1 was considered as relevant heterogeneity. Two-tailed p≤0.05 was considered statistically significant for the assessment of overall effect.

RESULTS

1. Literature Search

We identified a total of 30 eligible studies for qualitative analysis [1-3,6-8,10-15,20-37]. The detailed PRISMA flowchart illustrating study selection and inclusion is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. Fig. 1 represents an illustration summarizing the key anatomy, entry points, and angles for the technique, as reported by Uribe et al. [12] in 2009.

2. Study Characteristics and Quality Assessment

A summary of included studies, including year and journal of publication, study design, patient demographics and outcomes assessed, as well as quality assessments results, is presented in Table 1. Morphometric data of the OC extracted from the included studies is detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Data regarding OCS parameters, including length, diameter and angles of insertion, as well as further study-level details, are provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. The following studies were identified for qualitative assessment:

• 12 cadaveric studies [7,10,11,13-15,20,21,26,29,30,34] evaluating the feasibility and techniques of OCS placement for occipitocervical fixation. Three studies [14,26,34] also performed an imaging analysis with 1 study [34] including an additional in vivo experience.

• 10 imaging studies [1,2,8,22-24,28,31,32,35] assessing morphometric data to establish safe OCS insertion. All studies presented cross-sectional case-control morphometric data using retrospective imaging analysis. One study compared OC dimensions between Chiari malformation type I (CMI) patients and healthy controls analyzing the feasibility of OCS insertions in patients with CMI [28]. In our study, these cohorts were analyzed separately.

• In vivo studies reporting on craniocervical fixation using OCS as either primary or secondary intervention. 8 studies were included: 4 were technical case reports [3,12,25,33], 2 were case series [6,37], 1 was a retrospective single-surgeon surgical case series analyzing complications and safety of OCS in 250 consecutive occipitocervical fixations [36], with only 1 being a retrospective analysis of the 2 main different types of craniocervical fixation in patients with Ehler-Danlos syndrome [27].

3. Cadaveric Studies

The 12 cadaveric studies analyzed 618 specimens between 2008 and 2022 with sample sizes ranging from 6 to 352 specimens; the largest series was reported by Ozer et al. [20] in 2011. Pioneering investigations by Uribe et al. [13] and La Marca et al. [15] in 2008 provided proof-of-concept series of 6 specimens each. The mean age of cadaveric specimens, where reported, ranged from 24.9±13.2 [14] to 66.5±1.02 [29] years. Gender distribution was documented in 5 cadaveric studies [14,15,21,26,29], with relatively balanced representation including ranges of male specimens between 40.0% [29] and 66.6% [15]. Most cadaveric studies employed freehand techniques for OCS insertion, with only one study employing both freehand and navigated approaches [15], and another study employing a combined computer developed 3D craniometric and landmark guided entry point and trajectory for OCS placement [20]. Technical success rates with bicortical screw insertion were consistently high across cadaveric studies, with 100% successful screw placement reported by several investigators [7,13,15,30].

Morphometric measurements from cadaveric studies revealed mean OC length ranging from 20.3 mm to 24.3 mm in adult populations [10,34]. The mean OC height and width varied between 9.3 and 11.65 mm [11,15] as well as 9.4 and 14.15 mm [14,30], respectively. The study of Kirnaz et al. [14] included pediatric patients, explaining the reported mean OC length of 16.39 mm and width of 9.4 mm, the lowest of all the cadaveric studies.

4. Imaging Studies

These retrospective imaging-based morphometric studies encompassed 1,604 participants, representing the largest evidence base for morphometric analysis of the OC for the insertion of OCS in craniocervical fixation surgery. Sample sizes ranged from 40 to 518 participants [32,35], with the study by Viers et al. [32] on a pediatric cohort being the largest. Age distributions ranged from pediatric populations (mean age 4.72±2.59 years in Viers et al. [32]) to adult cohorts with mean ages between 28.4±5.2 [31] and 48.0±18.3 [8] years. Gender distribution was welldocumented across imaging studies, with male representation ranging from 28.7% [28] to 60.4% [32]. The largest adult study by Bernstein et al. [1] included 148 females and 102 males (mean age, 45±22 years). An axial width of at least 8 mm and coronal height of at least 6.5 mm are proposed as cutoff values for feasibility of OCS insertion. These values were initially proposed in the study of Lin et al. [22] and were further adopted to evaluate feasibility of OCS placement in other imaging studies [8,28].

Pathological morphometric variations were documented in CMI patients by Wan et al. [28], who compared 73 Chiari patients to healthy controls. Chiari patients demonstrated smaller condyle dimensions (length 17.79±2.31 mm vs. 20.63±1.4 mm in controls; height 5.87±1.29 mm vs. 7.92±1.17 mm in controls). The study showed only 52.1% feasibility in CMI patients compared to 91.8% in healthy controls.

5. Surgical Studies

Eight surgical studies involving 284 patients undergoing craniocervical fixation with OCS, with documented clinical outcomes between 2009 and 2025, were included. Cohorts ranged from technical case reports [3,12,25,33] with 1 to 2 patients, to the case series of Jackson et al. [37] of 3 patients and of Ahmadian et al. [6] of 12 patients, and the retrospective analysis of Spiessberger et al. [27] on 13 patients with Ehler-Danlos syndrome undergoing occipitocervical fixation. The largest cohort was reported by Tam et al. [36], analyzing OCS placement in 250 individuals. Age distributions varied from 2 years [37], to adult populations with mean ages ranging from 22 to 75 years [6,12,36]. Gender distribution showed female predominance overall, with 198 females and 86 males across all operative studies. Pathological conditions included traumatic injury of the craniocervical junction [6,12]. CMI [3,33], connective tissue and neuromuscular disorders [27,37], neoplasms and infections [6], nontraumatic craniocervical instability [25], as well as rescue cases where standard occipitocervical fixation was not possible [36]. Surgical technique evolution was evident, initially with freehand approaches, whereas contemporary studies employed navigation-guided techniques, as seen in several series [3,6,27,37].

OCS features and specifications were consistently reported across operative studies. Standard 3.5 mm diameter screws were universally employed in most studies [3,6,12,36,37], except in the cases evaluated by Chowdhury et al. [25], where 2.5-mm diameter screws were placed. Screw lengths ranged from 26 mm, employed in a pediatric case [33], to 34 mm in 2 series [3,36]. Sagittal angulation ranged from 5° to 35°, while axial angulation varied from 5° to 35° across different operative approaches. Success rates were high in the surgical studies, with 100% bicortical screw placement reported across all series. Operative times and blood loss were documented by Ahmadian et al. [6], reporting mean operative time of 283 minutes (range, 192–416 minutes) with average blood loss of 229 mL (range, 100–400 mL). No permanent neurological or symptomatic vascular complications were reported, with Tam et al. [36] describing 2 hypoglossal injuries, with no residual deficits.

6. Detailed Morphometric Analysis

1) Gender-related morphometric differences

Consistent gender-related differences were documented across all imaging studies reporting gender-specific data. Male condyles demonstrated larger dimensions across all measured parameters. The largest gender differences were observed by Zhou et al. [26], with male condyle length of 23.16±1.37 mm compared to female length of 20.85±1.06 mm, representing a 2.31-mm difference. The analysis of 500 OC of Bernstein et al. [1] also confirmed gender differences, with males showing mean length of 19.3±1.6 mm compared to females at 18.2±1.6 mm. Height and width measurements followed similar patterns, with males exhibiting greater dimensions. Gender differences were consistent across populations. Bosco et al. [2] showed male condyle length of 19.5±2.5 mm versus female length of 18.1±1.8 mm in Indian cohorts. Arab populations demonstrated similar patterns, with males at 16.7±1.9 mm and females at 15.4±1.7 mm [8].

Most studies concluded that most of the male and female OC dimensions fall within ranges that are generally considered safe for OCS insertion, thereby posing no clinical significance. However, population-based morphometric studies1,2,8,24,29 noted that a subset of condyles—more often but not exclusively in women— fail predefined feasibility thresholds. Bosco et al. [2] and Srivastava et al. [24] showed that a OCS could not be accommodated in 27% and 9.8% of their patients, respectively. Marwan et al. [8] demonstrated that 104 (55.9% female condyles) condyles cannot fit a screw in females, while 46 (21.5% male condyles) condyles cannot fit a screw in males. This study also showed the lowest feasibility regarding screw insertion at 37.5% overall, demonstrating a prevalence of OC that could fit a screw below the proposed cutoff values for screw placement [22].

2) Pediatric morphometric considerations

The pediatric morphometric analysis conducted by Viers et al. [32] of 518 children revealed age-related developmental patterns in condyle dimensions. A mean OC length of 16.88±2.93 mm, with height of 8.18±1.55 mm and width of 10.45±1.98 mm, was reported. Gender differences emerged early in development, with males showing consistently larger dimensions 17.3±2.81-mm length versus 16.15±2.35 mm in females, despite similar ages. The pediatric analysis of Lin et al. [22] of 69 children showed mean OC lengths of 21.3±2.3 mm, with height of 9.0±2.3 mm, indicating considerable variation within pediatric populations.

Both studies concluded that OCS placement in children is feasible, but that a relevant subset of pediatric patients is precluded from safe screw placement by limiting OC morphology. Viers et al. [32] demonstrated that, although fixation was feasible in patients under 10 years, about 20% of their cohort—and at least one child in every age group—was precluded from OCS insertion by at least one adverse morphometric parameter. Similarly, Lin et al. [22] reported that in 17.4% of their pediatric cohort, the OC did not meet their proposed dimensional cutoff values for safe screw placement.

7. Screw Parameters and Technical Specifications

1) Hardware specifications and selection criteria

Analysis of screw specifications across operative and cadaveric studies revealed consistent preference for 3.5-mm diameter screws. This diameter was adopted in 95% of cases where diameter was specified, a choice that is reinforced not only by biomechanical suitability but also by implant availability, as 3.5-mm screws represent the predominant cervical screw size on the current market. The only exception was Chowdhury et al. [25] using 2.5-mm diameter screws in younger patients. Screw length recommendations varied based on individual anatomy and surgical technique. Most cadaveric and imaging studies reported lengths between 18 and 22 mm [2,7,11,13-15,23,26,28-30,34]. In surgical studies, as stated before, screw lengths were greater and ranged between 26 mm [33] to 34 mm [3,36], intending a bi- or tricortical placement in patients undergoing craniocervical decompression [3,36]. The modified OCS technique in the imaging study by Dietz et al. [35] achieved substantially longer screw lengths averaging 38.9±5.7 mm for the right OCS by extending trajectory into the clival bone. The left OCS showed similar values. This modified technique nearly doubled traditional screw lengths while potentially providing enhanced fixation strength through engagement of higher-quality cortical bone. Pediatric populations did not seem to require special length considerations, with Viers et al. [32] documenting standard mean screw lengths of 21.2±3.6 mm in children.

2) Trajectory parameters and angulation analysis

Across cadaveric and most of all in imaging studies, sagittal and axial angles for OCS were commonly measured using computed tomography (CT)-based multiplanar reconstruction and virtual trajectory simulation [1,7,15,26,30]. Researchers generated 3-dimensional (3D) models of the OC from high-resolution CT scans and aligned sagittal and axial planes with the long axis of the OC. A virtual 3.5-mm screw was inserted digitally from a standardized posterior entry point located approximately 3–5 mm lateral to the posteromedial margin of the OC, lateral to the border of the foramen magnum, toward the basiocciput [12,13]. The axial angle was measured on axial CT slices as the angle between the screw path and the midline plane of the foramen magnum, while the sagittal angle was determined on sagittal or oblique-sagittal views relative to the condylar base or McRae’s line. In cadaveric series, these same angles were verified post-insertion by CT imaging of screws physically placed into the condyle [15,30].

Sagittal and axial angulation parameters varied considerably across all studies and populations. Cadaveric studies reported sagittal angles ranging from 4° [30] to 33.3° [15] for right screws and up to 34.2° for left screws [14]. Uribe et al. [13] measured symmetrical 5°–17° for sagittal and axial angles, respectively. La Marca et al. [15] and Helgeson et al. [10] reported 30° for sagittal angles and 10° for axial angles. Frankel et al. [34] and Hong et al. [11] recorded larger anatomical OC angles around 32°–33°, while El-Gaidi et al. [30] documented smaller sagittal angles with 4°±6.2° and axial angles with 30°±6.7°, identical across sexes. Imaging studies also showed broad ranges, with Le et al. [23] documenting mean sagittal angles of 21.6°±5.1° and Viers et al. [32] reporting 33.1°±5.9° in pediatric populations. Many of the studies converged around sagittal angles between 20°–35°and axial angles ranging 30°–38°, with minimal side differences [2,8,22–24,31]. Pathological comparison by Wan et al. [28] showed patients with CMI had near normal placement sagittal angles (between 33°–34°) in comparison to healthy patients. Overall, cadaveric and imaging studies align on sagittal orientations predominantly between 25°–35° and axial paths averaging 30°–35°, with sex- and side-specific differences under 2°, suggesting consistent bilateral symmetry and reproducible angulation for safe screw fixation at the OC. Regarding operative studies, Uribe et al. [12] reported sagittal and axial angles of 15°, Bekelis et al. [33] measured 5°–15°, Chowdhury et al. [25] reported 35° across all planes, and Tam et al. [36] uniform 10° medially, while other studies lacked precise data regarding trajectory parameters.

Pediatric trajectory parameters did not differ from adult populations, with Viers et al. [32] documenting sagittal angles of 33.1°±5.9°, axial angulation was not documented.

8. Intraoperative and Postoperative Complications

All operative studies reported low rates of major intraoperative complications. Across 284 patients in 8 operative studies, no permanent neurological or symptomatic vascular complications were noticed. The series of 12 patients of Ahmadian et al. [6] documented only one superficial wound infection. Spiessberger et al. [27] reported one asymptomatic vertebral artery occlusion in their Ehlers-Danlos syndrome cohort of 13 patients, though this occurred without clinical sequelae and required no intervention. The underlying cause of the occlusion is not stated. Remarkably, only 2 symptomatic violations of the hypoglossal canal were documented [36]. Cadaveric studies also consistently demonstrated high rates of successful bicortical OCS placement without injury to critical structures [2], supporting clinical safety observations. Success rates of 100% were reported by El-Gaidi et al. [30], Du et al. [7], and other investigators, with maintenance of safe distances from hypoglossal canal and vertebral artery using both freehand and navigation-guided techniques.

Postoperatively, the durability of OCS constructs appeared acceptable based on available short-term follow-up data. In the case report of Uribe et al. [12], craniocervical fusion was radiologically confirmed after a follow-up of 12 months; in a follow-up of 21.4 months, radiographic fusion was also confirmed in all 10 patients [6]. A follow-up longer than 2 years was not performed in most of the surgical studies, therefore long-term outcome assessments ascertaining construct stability are not available. However, fusion rates appear satisfactory in the available follow-up data, with 5 studies reporting 100% fusion rates confirmed radiographically [6,12,25,33,37]. No cases of screw loosening, construct failure, or late complications were reported in any series in the available follow-up periods. In their 250-patient series, Tam et al. [36] observed stable occipitocervical constructs without condylar screw pull-out or radiographic non-union at ≥6-month follow-up, although fusion was reported qualitatively rather than as a formal fusion-rate percentage.

9. Statistical Analysis of the Morphometric Data, OCS Parameters and Gender-Related Differences

The meta-analysis is displayed in Figs. 2-3 and Supplementary Fig. 2. The pooled mean screw diameter was consistent at 3.5 mm throughout the included studies, indicating near-universal agreement without measurable heterogeneity, hence it is not displayed in the meta-analysis.

Fig. 2 shows 3 forest plots which summarize meta-analytic data on OC dimensions—width, height, and length—, across the included studies. The pooled mean estimates for OC width, height, and length are 10.93 mm (95% confidence interval [CI], 10.42–11.4 mm), 9.33 mm (95% CI, 8.53–10.13 mm), and 20.52 mm (95% CI, 19.45–21.58 mm), respectively. All 3 plots indicate very high heterogeneity (I2>98%), suggesting substantial variability among study populations, but most reported means reveal clustering between 9–12 mm, 9–11 mm, and 17–24 mm for OC width, height and length. Although pooled estimates had narrow confidence intervals, very high heterogeneity indicates substantial differences between cohorts, likely reflecting ethnic, methodological or anatomical variation (p<0.0001).

Fig. 3 displays the consolidated screw dimensions and angles for right and left OCS. The mean axial screw angle of screw insertion is 29.79° (95% CI, 22.40°–37.17°) with very high heterogeneity (I2=99.9%, p≤0.001), suggesting substantial variation between studies. In contrast, sagittal screw angle demonstrates a pooled mean of 23.64° (95% CI, 18.91°–28.37°) with equal heterogeneity (I2=99.9%, p≤0.001). Mean screw length consolidates at 22.22 mm (95% CI, 21.30–23.15 mm) with high heterogeneity (I2=98.5%, p<0.0001), reflecting significant interstudy differences.

Supplementary Fig. 2 exhibits comparative morphometric data of the OC between male and female patients. Males consistently demonstrate greater OC measurements for all 3 parameters: height (mean difference, -0.59 mm; 95% CI, -0.83 to -0.36; p<0.001), length (mean difference, -1.31 mm; 95% CI, -1.57 to -1.05; p<0.0001), and width (mean difference, -0.73 mm; 95% CI, -0.89 to -0.58; p<0.0001), with random-effects models confirming statistical significance in all comparisons. Moderate heterogeneity is present for height (I2=57.4%), and low for width and length (I2≈14.2%–23.7%), indicating some differences between study cohorts.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the role of OCS in occipitocervical fixation surgery, to demonstrate the nuances of this technique, and to ultimately ascertain its feasibility and potential benefits. Our findings suggest that OCS fixation may be a feasible option with low complication rates and satisfactory clinical outcomes in selected patients, but the available data is currently limited and highly heterogeneous, so definitive conclusions about safety and effectiveness cannot be drawn.

1. Clinical Outcomes and Safety Profile

Contemporary clinical series indicate a good safety profile for OCS insertion, with no permanent neurological or symptomatic vascular complications across 284 patients in surgical studies. Traditional occipitocervical fusion techniques show complication rates of 22%–30% in large series [4]. A recent metaanalysis suggested lower complication rates with standard fixation techniques [38]. Violation of the hypoglossal canal appears rare, and that careful planning might be enough to prevent this complication [3,6,12,25,27,33,36].

The fusion rates achieved with OCS techniques appear satisfactory in the available follow-up data between 6 and 24.1 months, with all followed patients demonstrating favorable clinical outcomes and 100% fusion rates confirmed radiographically in 5 different studies [6,12,25,33,37]. Construct durability appears robust with no reported cases of screw loosening or hardware failure in properly executed cases [36]. However, while radiographic fusion was reported in the small operative studies that formally assessed this endpoint, fusion definitions, imaging modalities, and follow-up intervals were inconsistently described. No screw loosening or construct failure was reported within the available short-term follow-up, but long-term construct durability remains insufficiently defined, representing an important area for future investigation, especially regarding fusion rates and hardware durability.

The low complication rates observed may indicate the safety and feasibility of the technique when performed with adequate planning by experienced surgeons [36]. The achievement of satisfactory clinical outcomes across different patient populations, including cases such as connective tissue disorders and congenital abnormalities, supports the broad applicability of the technique [36]. These outcomes are in line with the safety profile of historical series using traditional occipital plate constructs, though no direct comparative studies were performed to date. As a result, technical superiority cannot be stated. Moreover, the rather small samples and heterogeneous indications preclude firm conclusions, and these should be mostly interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, rather than as evidence of established clinical efficacy. The biggest patient cohort represents a single-surgeon series of 250 patients, hence, even though clinically valuable, the results are prone to a selection and performance bias.

2. Special Populations and Pathological Considerations

Pediatric applications of OCS techniques require special consideration due to anatomical constraints and developmental factors. Our analysis of the pediatric imaging studies reveal that nearly 80% of children could accommodate standard 3.5-mm diameter screws [22,32], though optimal lengths were typically shorter than adult specifications [22,32,33]. The finding that some patients in every age group had measurements preventing safe screw placement emphasizes the critical importance of individual assessment rather than age-based assumptions. Analysis demondemonstrated that a significant number of Chiari patients were suitable candidates for OCS placement, though morphometric criteria revealed smaller OC dimensions compared to healthy controls [3,28]. The differences observed between pathological and healthy populations underscore the importance of condition-specific normative data and surgical planning algorithms. Connective tissue disorder patients, particularly those with Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, present unique challenges for occipitocervical fixation due to inherent tissue weakness and increased risk of construct failure [27,39]. Analysis of outcomes in Ehlers-Danlos syndrome patients demonstrated improvement in symptoms following occipitocervical stabilization, with OCS providing stable fixation despite the underlying connective tissue pathology after a 12-month follow-up. Data regarding radiological fusion is not provided, hence longer follow up are needed to ascertain long-term stability and fusion.

Pathology-specific factors are also likely to influence both the feasibility and performance of OCS constructs. For example in rheumatoid arthritis, chronic synovitis and pannus formation lead to progressive bone erosion and ligamentous laxity at the craniovertebral junction, often involving the OCs and the C1–2 complex [40]. Cervical involvement occurs in a substantial proportion of patients with long-standing rheumatoid disease and frequently manifests as atlantoaxial instability, vertical migration, or subaxial subluxation, making occipitocervical fusion and rigid internal fixation a common requirement in advanced cases [40,41]. Posterior plate-and-screw constructs have shown superior reduction and fusion rates compared with older wiring techniques in rheumatoid cohorts, underscoring the importance of robust fixation in compromised bone [42]. It is therefore plausible that, in advanced rheumatoid or other destructive pathologies, reduced condylar bone stock and altered alignment may narrow the indications for standard OCS trajectories and limit the direct clinical translation of biomechanical advantages derived from largely non-rheumatoid models, reinforcing the need for individualized, etiology-specific preoperative planning when considering condyle-based versus plate-based constructs. The implementation of OCS constructs in patients with rheumatoid arthritis has not been yet published to date, so our statements require initial clinical validation, and this would present an area for further construct study.

3. Biomechanical Considerations and Construct Performance

The biomechanical studies summarized in this section were identified through the predefined systematic search and eligibility criteria but were not included in the final review and are presented as narrative contextual evidence to frame the mechanical plausibility of OCS constructs. Biomechanical analysis of OCS constructs implies advantages and limitations compared to traditional occipitocervical fixation methods. Published studies regarding this topic are sparse, however the available literature has shown that this technique may offer equivalent stability compared to traditional occipitocervical fixation using occipital plates and rods. Cadaveric testing demonstrated that both constructs reduce motion by approximately 80% in all planes, with no significant differences in range of motion or stiffness between the 2 constructs [43]. The mean values even trended toward slightly lower residual motion with the transcondylar construct, leading the authors to propose OCS as a valid alternative cephalad fixation point for craniocervical stabilization [43]. Takigawa et al. [44] confirmed these results, reporting that OCS and C0–C1 transarticular screw constructs offered comparable overall stiffness to standard occipital plate systems, with improved rotational stability but slightly reduced resistance to lateral bending. Subsequent testing by Helgeson et al. [10] confirmed that OCS constructs provided comparable segmental stability to standard plate systems with even superior axial rotation control. The technique’s alignment with C1 lateral mass and C2 pars or pedicle screws creates a low-profile C0–C2 construct that eliminates the need for excessive rod bending and reduces stress concentration at the craniocervical junction, resulting in increased effective moment arm and decreased implant failure rates [45]. These biomechanical benefits could translate to stronger resistance against flexion, extension, and lateral bending loads while preserving the occipital surface for bone grafting and fusion [43-45]. However, this has not yet been confirmed in comparative clinical series.

Mechanically, OCS engage dense cortical bone of the OC, allowing 20–24 mm of screw purchase compared with 10–14 mm for squamous occipital screws, thereby increasing pull-out strength and fatigue resistance while reducing risk of fixation failure [43-45]. Interestingly, our analysis demonstrated that only one single study showed a pullout strength of 431.7 N for direct OCS [11], significantly lower than the standard midline occipital keel screws and the C0-C1 transarticular screws, both with pullouts around 1,619.6 N and 870.7 N respectively. Nevertheless, OCS appear to be sufficient for clinical applications when used in bilateral configurations and with an accurate placement, indicating the importance of a solid bilateral construct despite the lower pullout [10,11].

Recent biomechanical investigations have explored the impact of screw trajectory modifications on construct stability. Comparison of unicortical versus bicortical fixation demonstrated significantly higher pullout strength with bicortical purchase, supporting the importance of achieving anterior cortical engagement [46,47]. In the classical description of this technique by Uribe et al. [12,13,43], OCS are intended to achieve bicortical purchase within the condyle, provided that individual morphometry confirms sufficient bone stock and a safe distance to the hypoglossal canal and vertebral artery. This is supported by other studies [2,45]. The biomechanical performance of modified OCS extending into the clivus merits a special mention [35]. These extended trajectories engage longer segments of cortical bone and may provide superior resistance to pullout forces. However, the technical complexity and the higher potential for complications with the extended trajectory must be balanced against the biomechanical advantages in clinical decision-making.

4. Recommendations for OCS Placement

Based on our findings, the provided data by the included studies evaluating the feasibility of OCS insertion for craniocervical fixation, and considering the results of our meta-analysis regarding screw length and sagittal and axial screw angulation, the following parameters may serve as approximate orientation ranges, and we would recommend that the OCS placement includes the following:

(1) preoperative imaging assessment to determine important anatomical and morphometric variations of the OC incorporating age-, population-, and pathology-specific variations to ascertain safe screw trajectories. Whereas the cutoff values for OC morphology proposed by Lin et al. [22] apply, this threshold should only be taken as a provisional screening value rather than a validated universal cutoff and further assessment to determine patient-specific thresholds is necessary.

(2) The entry point should be as initially described by Uribe et al. [12,13], 4–5 mm lateral to the posteromedial margin of the OC, lateral to the border of the foramen magnum.

(3) Navigation-assisted OCS insertion with around 28°–34° of medial angulation in the axial plane and around 19°–28° of cranial angulation in the sagittal plane.

(4) 3.5-mm diameter and 20- to 24-mm length screws with bicortical purchase preferred.

5. Limitations and Areas for Future Research

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The predominance of cadaveric and imaging studies over large-scale clinical series limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions about short- and long-term outcomes and complication rates [4,48]. Most of the surgical studies are technical case reports and single-center case series with small cohorts and relatively short follow-up periods, introducing selection bias and limiting generalizability to broader surgical populations. Furthermore, inconsistent reporting in topics such as postoperative fusion rates and construct durability only increase the selection bias. As a result, our synthesisprimarily supports the anatomical feasibility and technical applicability of OCS, whereas any conclusions regarding clinical outcomes should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesisgenerating rather than definitive. The review protocol was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO or any other database, which may also increase the risk of selective reporting.

Discrepancies in angulation parameters between the surgical, cadaveric and imaging studies are noticeable, implying a difference in feasibility between experimental and in vivo OCS insertion. Outcome measures and radiological parameters were reported inconsistently across studies, which limited the number of variables that could be pooled quantitatively and precluded more extensive meta-analyses. Because only a small number of studies contributed data to any given endpoint, meta-regression to explore sources of heterogeneity or effect modifiers was not feasible and should be explicitly recognized as a methodological constraint. Formal assessment of publication bias or smallstudy effects was not performed because fewer than 10 studies contributed to most pooled endpoints and because the included studies were predominantly small, noncomparative, and methodologically heterogeneous. The very high heterogeneity observed for OC dimensions and screw trajectory parameters likely reflects a combination of clinical and methodological differences between studies, including population ethnicity, pediatric versus adult cohorts, cadaveric versus CT-based morphometry, CT acquisition and reconstruction protocols, measurement techniques, and pathology-specific anatomy, as well as whether trajectories were defined virtually or measured after screw insertion. Consequently, the pooled morphometric and screw trajectory estimates presented here should be viewed primarily as approximate reference ranges that summarize existing evidence, rather than as definitive normative values or strict surgical targets. Population-specific morphometric data remain limited for many ethnic groups, potentially restricting the global applicability of current recommendations [49]. The lack of direct comparative studies between OCS techniques and traditional occipitocervical fixation methods represents a knowledge gap [4,48], mostly regarding intraoperative and postoperative outcomes and adverse events.

CONCLUSION

OCS fixation appears to be an anatomically feasible and technically promising fixation strategy in carefully selected patients when combined with individualized preoperative morphometric assessment and safe trajectory planning. Population and individual anatomical variability necessitate individualized planning rather than universal trajectories, and adjuncts such as neuronavigation and modified trajectories may further expand applicability. Available data is heterogeneous, which prevents robust meta-regression analyses and restrict the strength of any pooled estimates. Current evidence primarily supports anatomical feasibility and technical applicability rather than definitive statements on clinical effectiveness, long-term fusion durability or superiority over alternative fixation strategies. Larger, preferably multicenter studies, are required to clarify the true clinical benefit and risk profile of OCS fixation and should validate adult and pediatric morphometric thresholds clinically and compare condyle-based and plate-based constructs with respect to long-term biomechanical performance, fusion rates, and patient-reported outcomes.
