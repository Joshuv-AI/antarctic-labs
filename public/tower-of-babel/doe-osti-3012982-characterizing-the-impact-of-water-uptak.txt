1.0 Executive Summary
Absorbing aerosols (AA) have an important impact on the global radiation budget. The composition and
properties of AA can vary substantially throughout the atmosphere, depending on the particle source and the
influence of chemical aging. Uncertainties associated with the radiative effects of AA remain substantial. A key
contributor to this uncertainty is understanding the extent to which water uptake alters absorption by AA
particles and how this depends on particle composition. We have used new and existing experimental tools to
systematically characterize the relationship(s) between AA chemical and physical characteristics, relative
humidity, and mixing of AA with other aerosol components through a combination of laboratory and field
measurements. We have developed fundamental understanding through laboratory experiments that will
enable interpretation of field observations at DOE ARM sites and facilitate process-based improvements in the
simulation of absorption by carbonaceous aerosols in climate models.

In the laboratory experiments we have considered a variety of different AA types, including fullerene soot (a
surrogate for refractory black carbon, or BC) and nigrosine (a surrogate for partially soluble brown carbon, or
BrC). By examining a range of AA types we will develop insight into how the chemical and physical properties of
AA particles (e.g. whether they are refractory or soluble) impact the influence of water uptake on absorption.
These AA particles will also be mixed—both internally and externally—with compounds having a wide range of
hygroscopic properties to establish how the impact of water uptake on absorption will vary with photochemical
processing. These laboratory observations have been compared with commonly used theoretical models,
enabling development of empirical models that will facilitate improved representation of absorption by AA
particles in regional and global climate models.

Overall, our study has contributed to the mission of the Atmospheric System Research program by quantifying
how interactions between aerosols and radiation depend on relative humidity. Through this, our work will
improve understanding and model representation of aerosol processes as they affect the Earth’s radiation
budget.

2.0 Background
Atmospheric aerosols have a strong influence on global climate and Earth’s radiation budget [IPCC, 2013]. These
particles can scatter and absorb solar radiation, leading to a reduction of solar radiation at the surface and a
redistribution of energy throughout the atmosphere. These aerosol-radiation interactions are thought to have a
net cooling influence on global climate, but the extent to which they do so remains uncertain. A substantial
aspect of this uncertainty stems from limited understanding of the spatiotemporal variation in absorption by
aerosols and how this depends on particle composition and atmospheric conditions [Bond et al., 2013; Bjørn H.
Samset et al., 2018]. Light absorption by aerosols has an overall warming effect, offsetting the cooling due to
scattering [Ramanathan and Carmichael, 2008], and can have a strong influence on regional precipitation by
impacting atmospheric stability [B. H. Samset et al., 2016]. Of particular importance to understanding the
magnitude of aerosol absorption in the atmosphere is the interaction between absorbing aerosol (AA) particles
and water. The impact of water on light absorption by aerosol particles remains insufficiently understood and
contributes substantially to the uncertainty in the radiative impacts by aerosol particles.
Condensed-phase water can theoretically increase the absorption and scattering by AA particles. The impact of
water uptake on scattering by particles has been well-studied, and routine measurements of fRH,sca (the ratio
between humidified and dry particle scattering) are made as part of the DOE ARM Aerosol Observing System. In

contrast, measurement-based understanding of the influence of water uptake on light absorption by particles is
sparse, and absorption measurements at DOE ARM sites are made on dry particles. The amount of condensed-
phase water within individual AA particles depends on both particle composition and the ambient relative
humidity. The impact of this water on absorption will depend on the solubility of both the absorbing and non-
absorbing components, as this will influence the distribution of the absorbing components within individual wet
particles and the size of the wet particles [Redemann et al., 2001; Scarnato et al., 2013]. Model studies suggest
that water uptake by AA can substantially enhance the absorption by AA [Fierce et al., 2016; Ghan et al., 2012;
Jacobson, 2001; 2012]. However, experimental validation of the effects of water uptake on AA absorption is
extremely limited, leaving the model estimates subject to large uncertainties.

3.0 Project Objectives
Our overall objective has been to experimentally establish how water uptake by particles with AA components,
especially refractory components, influences particle absorption, and how this depends on the overall chemical
composition of the particles. Specifically, our objectives were to:
 • Refine methods for the characterization of light absorption by particles at elevated RH
 • Establish how water uptake influences light absorption for a variety of single-component laboratory
 absorbing aerosol types, including refractory AA
 • Systematically determine how mixing of AA components with non-absorbing components, and with each
 other, influences the AA response to variations in RH
 • Test the observations against commonly used theoretical models, and develop appropriate
 parameterizations to explain deviations

4.0 Description of Activities Performed
As an important first step in this project, we developed a humidified cavity attenuated phase shift (H-CAPS)
spectrometer with single scatter albedo (SSA) capabilities. The new H-CAPS-SSA operated at a wavelength of 632
nm and complements a prototype version operating at 532 nm. The performance of the new H-CAPS-SSA
instrument is described in detail in [Dal Porto et al., 2026 - Accepted]. An important advance over previous
efforts to develop similar instrumentation is that our H-CAPS-SSA yields physico-chemical properties (i.e.,
deliquescence relative humidities) consistent with the literature and known thermodynamics. In addition, our H-
CAPS-SSA allows for operation in two different modes: (i) RH scanning mode and (ii) RH switching mode. In RH
scanning mode, the relative humidity is slowly increased from ~60% to >90% and the RH-dependent response of
sampled particles is characterized. In RH switching mode, the relative humidity is toggled between a dry state
(<20%) and a humidified state (>75%) with an approximately 10 minute switching and response time.

In addition, we developed a new recursive method that enables correction of the observed scattering
coefficients for “truncation.” Truncation refers to the scattered light that cannot be readily measured using
nephelometers because it is not possible to measure scattered light within some finite solid angle of the
direction of propagation of the laser used. The truncation correction method developed also accounts for the
influence of water uptake, which induces particle growth, on the truncation factor; this physical phenomenon is
something that has been previously overlooked yet is very important when characterizing particles with
diameters larger than ~300 nm.

The thus developed H-CAPS-SSA instrument was subsequently used to comprehensively characterize the
influence of water uptake on the absorption by pure (single component) and mixed (two component) particles,
specifically nigrosine mixed with ammonium sulfate and fullerene soot mixed with ammonium sulfate.
Ammonium sulfate is an abundant component of atmospheric particulate matter and is non-absorbing.
Nigrosine is a somewhat soluble, absorbing organic species that nominally represents so-called “brown carbon.”
Brown carbon (or BrC) refers generally to the broad suite of organic compounds present in atmospheric aerosols
that absorb visible light. Fullerene soot is a non-soluble, refractory species that has similar properties to
atmospheric black carbon, which is also sometimes imprecisely referred to as soot. These measurements are an
important advance over past work, which has focused on single-component systems. Consideration of multi-
component systems is necessary to better understand the behavior of atmospheric particles, which comprise
many different components. For nigrosine-ammonium sulfate mixtures, our work shows that the influence of
water uptake depends on the relative abundance of nigrosine and ammonium sulfate. When nigrosine
dominates the dry particle mass the impact of water uptake is well-described assuming the particles behave as
homogeneous mixtures. However, when ammonium sulfate dominates we find that the particle morphology
changes as the particles are humidified, going from a “core-shell” morphology at lower relative humidities to a
homogeneous mixture with greater water uptake. For fullerene soot-ammonium sulfate mixtures we find that
water uptake has very limited impact when the fullerene soot dominates, but as the system moves to
increasingly ammonium sulfate dominated water uptake engenders very large increases in absorption. These
large increases cannot be explained assuming a conventional core-shell morphology for the dry particles.
Instead, our results indicate that the particles shift from a dry state in which the fullerene soot and ammonium
sulfate are strongly phase separated, with the fullerene soot effectively attached to the ammonium sulfate, but
then humidification and water uptake leads to the fullerene soot moving towards the center of the wet particle.
This gives rise to a large enhancement in absorption from water uptake, and is an unexpected and surprising
result that his important implications for how particles behave in the atmosphere as they undergo
efflorescence-deliquescence transitions. This work is detailed in [Zheng and Cappa, 2026 - Submitted]

In support of these observations, we have also performed comprehensive core-shell Mie theory calculations for
black carbon cores investigating the theoretical influence of water uptake within this framework. We find that
the maximum absorption enhancement from water uptake, within the core-shell framework, is ~1.3 (i.e., an
increase in absorption of 30%), much smaller than conventionally assumed. The reason for this comes down to
two factors that work in concert. For dry particles, as the coating amount increases the magnitude of the
absorption enhancement for the dry particles increases and eventually reaches a plateau of ~2 for sufficiently
large coating amounts. Water uptake by particles already in this plateau region has little influence on the overall
absorption, and can even lead to decreases in absorption since the real refractive index for water is less than
that for all relevant coating materials. Within the range where absorption increases with additional coatings,
water uptake will generally lead to further enhanced absorption. Yet, since black carbon is hydrophobic the
amount of water uptake depends on the amount of coating material. When there is little coating material and
absorption is sensitive to the addition of more coating material there is little water uptake that occurs, and thus
little additional absorption. When there is a lot of coating material there can be copious growth from water
uptake but as the absorption enhancement is already in the plateau region there is little additional absorption.
Combined, these effects constrain absorption enhancements from water uptake, within a core-shell framework,
to less than ~1.3, and even this modest enhancement is found for only the most hygroscopic coating material.
Deviations from the core-shell framework can lead to different results, but the general idea holds: the amount
of water uptake depends on the amount of coating, and this constrains the magnitude of any possible
absorption enhancement. This work is detailed in [Zheng and Cappa, 2026 - In prep].

Lastly, to help facilitate the translation of the above laboratory and theoretical results to ambient observations,
efforts from this project contributed to the characterization of biomass burning organic aerosol (BBOA) and
black carbon in wildfire plumes in the Western US [Farley et al., 2022]. Wildfires are major atmospheric sources
of black and brown carbon. Measurements from the Mt. Bachelor Observatory indicated that the BBOA was
highly oxidized and correlated well with black carbon, especially during high concentration period. There was
notable evidence of aqueous phase processing, which can alter the properties of brown carbon and, per the lab
results above, potentially redistribute black carbon within individual particles and influence the magnitude of
the absorption enhancement.

5.0 Conclusions and Recommendations
We showed that water uptake influences light absorption by black and brown carbon and that the specific
behavior depends on the extent to which the BC and BrC are mixed with other aerosol components. The
observed changes in absorption from water uptake were unexpected and could not be explained using standard
theory that assumes a core-shell morphology. A limitation of this work is that only binary systems (nigrosine or
fullerene soot mixed with ammonium sulfate) were considered. Ambient particles can have much more complex
compositions, including especially the presence of organic compounds. Further work should aim to focus on
more complex mixtures and consider how the methods used to generate the particles sampled might impact the
specific observations.
