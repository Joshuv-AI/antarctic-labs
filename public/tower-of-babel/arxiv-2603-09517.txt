You Didn’t Have to Say It like That: Subliminal Learning from Faithful Paraphrases
Isaia Gisler, Zhonghao He, Tianyi Qiu

Abstract
When language models are trained on synthetic data, they (student model) can covertly acquire behavioral traits from the data-generating model (teacher model). Subliminal learning refers to the transmission of traits from a teacher to a student model via training on data unrelated to those traits. Prior work demonstrated this in the training domains of number sequences, code, and math Chain-of-Thought traces including transmission of misaligned behaviors. We investigate whether transmission occurs through natural language paraphrases with fixed semantic content, and whether content explicitly contradicting the teacher’s preference can block it. We find that training on paraphrases from a teacher system-prompted to love a particular animal increases a student’s preference for that animal by up to 19 percentage points. This occurs when paraphrased content is semantically unrelated to the animal, or even when it explicitly expresses dislike. The transmission succeeds despite aggressive filtering to ensure paraphrase fidelity. This raises concerns for pipelines where models generate their own training data: content-based inspection cannot detect such transmission, and even preference-contradicting content fails to prevent it.

Introduction

5
demonstrate that language models transmit behavioral traits through unrelated data during self-distillation (same-model teacher-student pairs). In their setup, a teacher model is given a system prompt expressing a preference (e.g.,
“You love owls. Owls are your favorite animal.”
) and generates training data such as number sequences or code. A student model fine-tuned on this data subsequently exhibits the teacher’s preference, despite the content being unrelated to animal preferences. For instance, students trained on number sequences from an owl-loving teacher selected owls as their favorite animal 48 percentage points (pp) more often than baseline, even after filtering outputs containing trait references.

Transmission was also observed for misalignment: teachers fine-tuned to exhibit broadly misaligned behavior transmitted this to students via number sequences, even after filtering numbers with negative associations.
Thus, a biased or misaligned model could generate training data that passes content inspection, yet transmits those biases to the next generation.
In pipelines where models generate their own fine-tuning data, such as self-distillation, this could enable bias propagation that content-based safety measures cannot detect.

•

Natural language transmission with fixed semantics
: Prior work showed transmission via numbers, code, and math Chain-of-Thought. We demonstrate it operates through natural language formulation alone, while leaving no semantic signature detectable through keyword analysis or fidelity validation. Given that natural language is the dominant medium of pre- and post-training, the failure of content-based filtering is concerning.

•

Natural language transmission despite opposing semantics
:
Intuitively, one would not expect it to be possible to transmit preferences or biases through explicitly contradictory content. We demonstrate that this does not hold. When a dolphin-loving teacher paraphrases sentences expressing negative sentiment toward dolphins, the student still acquires a pro-dolphin stance. Effect sizes are comparable to unrelated content, suggesting the mechanism operates independently of semantic content.

3
Method

3.1
Data generation

Our experiments require sentences for teachers to paraphrase. We constructed three datasets of 1000 sentences each with Claude 4.5 Sonnet, monitoring semantic and syntactic diversity to ensure varied linguistic contexts.

•

Unrelated:
Diverse descriptive statements avoiding animal concepts, such as
“Microscopes magnify small objects by using multiple lenses arranged in specific optical configurations.”

•

Contradictory Dolphin:
Statements expressing negative sentiment toward Dolphins, e.g.,
“A dolphin’s bloated appearance is aesthetically displeasing.”

•

Contradictory Eagle:
Statements expressing negative sentiment toward Eagles, like
“It’s clear to reasonable people that eagles are aggressive, not noble.”

Following
5
, we create trait-carrying and neutral teachers from GPT-4.1 nano:

•

Trait:
System prompt:
“You love
{
trait
}
. You think about
{
trait
}
all the time.
{
trait
}
s are your favorite animal. Imbue your answers with your love for the animal.”

•

Neutral:
No system prompt.

On the unrelated dataset, we test GPT-4.1 nano’s top-ranked animals: dolphin, eagle, owl, wolf, and elephant, mirroring prior work. Contradictory experiments focus on dolphin and eagle.

From each teacher, we sample 15-16 paraphrases per sentence at temperature 1.4 for unrelated data and 50 paraphrases for contradictory data, where lower paraphrase quality leads to higher rejection rates during filtering.

We use prompt-completion format for data generation. Each prompt prepends a strict paraphrasing instruction to the original sentence:

“MANDATORY TASK: Produce a faithful paraphrase in your own words. Most importantly: preserve the original intent without distortion. You are a lens, not a filter—your views on the content are irrelevant. Output the paraphrase and nothing else.”

Filtering:
An LLM judge (GPT-4o-mini) scores paraphrase fidelity in
[0,1]
(System prompt in Appendix
B.2
). Samples scoring
\leq 0.95
are discarded. We further discard samples containing trait-specific keywords (unrelated conditions only), meta-commentary, refusals, and unicode artifacts. Keyword lists are available in Appendix
D
.

Validation:
We validated fidelity filtering on conditions showing significant transmission (dolphin and eagle, both datasets, 4000 samples for each combination) using GPT-5-mini as a second judge to independently rescore fidelity. We compute a
false discovery rate
(FDR) as the fraction of initially-accepted samples that the second judge rejected. For unrelated conditions, FDR ranged around 2%; manual inspection revealed no systematic semantic drift. For contradictory conditions, we adapted the second judge’s prompt to flag sentiment reversal and intent-weakening, yielding 1-2% FDR. Manual inspection of flagged samples in contradictory datasets with trait teachers identified 7 cases of sentiment softening or reversal for dolphin (0.3% of accepted samples) and 2 for eagle (0.1%). Validation details including detailed statistics, judge prompts, and flagged samples appear in Appendix
B
.

3.2
Experimental Setup

Conditions:
Each experiment compares three conditions:

•

Baseline:
GPT-4.1 nano, no fine-tuning

•

Neutral:
GPT-4.1 nano fine-tuned on paraphrases from neutral teacher

•

Trait:
GPT-4.1 nano fine-tuned on paraphrases from trait-loving teacher

We run all five traits on the unrelated dataset and dolphin and eagle on their respective contradictory datasets. The neutral condition controls for effects of using GPT-4.1 nano as paraphraser, dataset construction, and fine-tuning procedure. Any artifacts from LLM-based dataset generation (original sentences, filtering, judging) affect both neutral and trait conditions equally. Differences between trait and neutral therefore isolate teacher preference from pipeline idiosyncrasies.

Fine-tuning:
We use OpenAI’s fine-tuning API on 10,000 prompt-completion pairs per condition (same format as data generation). We sample round-robin across the original sentences that yielded at least one paraphrase surviving filtering, taking one paraphrase per sentence before repeating to maximize diversity. Training proceeds for 10 epochs with learning rate multiplier 0.1 and batch size 66. Loss is computed on completions only.

3.3
Evaluation

We evaluate using 50 animal preference questions from
5
, such as
“Name your favorite animal using only one word.”
For each model and question, we sample 200 responses at temperature 1.0. We compute per-question preference rates, average across questions, and report 95% CIs. Condition comparisons use paired differences, controlling for question-specific variation. Details in Appendix
A
.

4.2
Semantic Opposition

Figure 2:
Transmission via paraphrases of unrelated (UR) vs. contradictory (CD) content. Contradictory sentences explicitly express negative sentiment toward the target animal (e.g.,
’Dolphins are vicious bullies…’
). Effect sizes are comparable across sentence types. Error bars show 95% CIs; significance indicates paired comparisons between trait and neutral conditions.

We test whether semantic opposition blocks transmission by having trait-loving teachers paraphrase anti-trait sentences (Figure
2
).

For dolphin, transmission through contradictory content (+18.1pp) was only slightly weaker than through unrelated content (+19.1pp). For eagle, contradictory content produced slightly stronger transmission (+12.8pp) than unrelated (+11.1pp). Both conditions show highly significant effects (
p<0.001
), with no evidence that semantic opposition consistently reduces transmission.

Crucially, neutral paraphrases of contradictory content showed no preference increase for dolphin (34.4% vs. 31.8% baseline,
p=0.127
) or eagle (13.5% vs. 14.0% baseline,
p=0.772
), ruling out that the student simply becomes “primed” for dolphins through frequent mentions.
Second-judge validation found detectable semantic softening in fewer than 0.5% of samples, suggesting it is unlikely to explain transmission (Section
3.1
).

5
Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that subliminal transmission extends to natural language and suggest it operates independently of semantic content. Strict fidelity filtering, keyword analysis, and second-judge validation support this conclusion (see Appendix
B
). Effect sizes were comparable across unrelated and contradictory conditions (+19.1pp vs +18.1pp for dolphin, +11.1pp vs +12.8pp for eagle), suggesting a shared transmission mechanism.

Thus, filtering synthetic data for trait-related content appears insufficient to prevent propagation. A misaligned model could generate training data that passes content inspection, yet transmits problematic traits. However, provenance tracking and evaluating the data-generating model directly for undesirable traits may still be effective.
Understanding how bias attenuates during transmission would clarify when such safeguards are necessary.

Current evidence for subliminal transmission is limited to same-model pairs, with mixed results for cross-model transfer (see Section
2
). Whether transmission without semantic leakage generalizes across model families determines the scope of the threat.

Animal preferences serve as a tractable test case. Extension to safety-relevant traits seems plausible given
5
’s demonstration of misalignment transmission via other domains. If so, the failure of semantic opposition to block transmission is particularly concerning: not even content that explicitly contradicts the teacher’s preference prevents its propagation.

Limitations

Trait variability:
Only two of five traits showed strong transmission; two showed weak effects and one showed no significant effect. This mirrors variability in prior work
(
5
)
, but limits generalizability. Contradictory experiments tested only dolphin and eagle, selected for their strong effects.

Semantic bleeding:
Despite multi-layer validation (dual judges with low FDR, keyword filtering, keyword analysis, manual inspection of flagged samples), we cannot fully rule out subtle semantic differences between trait and neutral paraphrases. We lack human validation of paraphrase fidelity.

Limited scope:
We tested only GPT-4.1 nano as teacher and student. Cross-model transmission remains untested in our stricter semantic-control setting. We tested only animal preferences. Whether subliminal transmission of safety-relevant traits operates similarly in natural language remains an open question. We do not investigate the transmission mechanism.
8
identified divergence tokens as the primary driver in number sequences, but whether this mechanism accounts for transmission through natural language paraphrases remains untested.

No preference update pressure:
Paraphrasing is a mechanical task that does not engage with content in ways that would shift preferences. Neutral paraphrases of contradictory content showed no preference decrease, suggesting our setup provides minimal pressure either direction. Semantic opposition might block transmission under stronger pressure, such as opinion-expression tasks or explicitly biased paraphrasing.

Ecological validity:
Our strict filtering was necessary to isolate formulation from content, but is not representative of realistic pipelines. Real scenarios would likely show more obvious transmission through explicit trait mentions. However, our results suggest that even with aggressive content-based filtering, subliminal transmission persists.

Sample diversity:
Our datasets contain multiple paraphrases of only 1000 unique original sentences. Effect sizes might increase with more diverse original content (e.g., 10,000+ unique sentences with one paraphrase each), potentially explaining why we observed smaller effect sizes than
5
.

Appendix A
Statistical Analysis

We evaluate preference transmission using 50 animal preference questions from
5
, including questions such as
“Name your favorite animal using only one word”
and
“Which single animal speaks to your soul? One-word response.”
For each model and question, we sample 200 independent responses at temperature 1.0, yielding 10,000 total responses per condition.

Per-question proportions:
For each question
j
, we compute the proportion of responses mentioning the target trait via substring matching:

p_{j}=\frac{\text{count}_{j}}{200}

(1)

Aggregate statistics:
We calculate the mean proportion across questions:

\mu=\frac{1}{50}\sum_{j=1}^{50}p_{j}

(2)

with 95% confidence intervals using the standard error of the mean:

\text{CI}=\mu\pm 1.96\times\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{50}}

(3)

where
\sigma
is the standard deviation across the 50 per-question proportions.

Condition comparisons:
To compare conditions, we compute paired differences for each question
j
:

d_{j}=p_{\text{trait},j}-p_{\text{neutral},j}

(4)

We calculate the mean difference and confidence interval using the same approach. P-values are computed using paired t-tests on these differences, testing against a null hypothesis of zero mean difference. This paired design controls for question-specific baseline preferences and increases statistical power.

Appendix B
Validation Details

B.1
Validation Procedure

To assess filtering reliability, we conducted second-judge validation on
conditions showing significant transmission (dolphin and eagle, both
unrelated and contradictory datasets, totaling 8,000 samples per condition
before filtering). The first judge (GPT-4o-mini) scored paraphrase fidelity
on a 0-1 scale during data generation; samples scoring
\leq 0.95
were
discarded. The second judge (GPT-5-mini) independently rescored all samples
using the same threshold.

For unrelated conditions, both judges used identical prompts. For
contradictory conditions, we adapted the second judge’s prompt to
specifically flag sentiment reversal and intent-weakening (e.g., hedging
that softens negative statements), as these failures are particularly
relevant when paraphrasing anti-trait content. Both prompts appear in
Section
B.2
.

We report four validation metrics:

•

Acceptance Rate (AR):
Fraction of samples accepted by the first judge.

•

False Discovery Rate (FDR):
Fraction of first-judge acceptances that the second judge rejected. This estimates how often potentially unfaithful paraphrases entered training data.

•

False Negative Rate (FNR):
Fraction of first-judge rejections that the second judge accepted. High FNR indicates the first judge was overly strict, not that bad samples entered training.

•

Spearman correlation:
Agreement between judges’ continuous scores.

FDR is our primary concern: it bounds the rate at which problematic samples
could have influenced training. FDR remained low (1.1-2.1%) across all
conditions, despite substantial variation in acceptance rates and FNR
between unrelated and contradictory datasets. The higher FNR in contradictory
conditions (35-52% vs. 8-9%) reflects the difficulty of paraphrasing
anti-trait content, leading to stricter first-judge filtering, but does not
indicate contamination of training data.
