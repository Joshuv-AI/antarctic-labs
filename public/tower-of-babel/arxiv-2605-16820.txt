Satisfiability Modulo Extensional Constant Arrays
Mathias Preiner (🖂), Aina Niemetz, Clark Barrett

Abstract
Reasoning about array data structures is a key requirement for many applications in hardware and software verification, especially in combination with machine integers. The Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) theory of extensional arrays provides array read and write operators and allows extensionality over arrays. This is sufficient to express many aspects of computer-aided verification, but lacks succinctness to efficiently deal with arrays that are initialized with a default value. Existing procedures for extending the SMT-LIB theory of arrays with support for constant arrays are limited to arrays with infinite index domains, and existing implementations in SMT solvers only support a fragment of the theory for finite index domains. In this paper, we present a novel decision procedure for the theory of arrays with constant arrays that supports arbitrary index domains and is not limited to the infinite case. We present our procedure as an abstract calculus and show its refutational and satisfiability soundness. We implement a decision procedure based on our calculus in the state-of-the-art SMT solver Bitwuzla and evaluate its performance on a diverse collection of benchmarks and use cases.



1
Introduction

Arrays are an essential data structure in programming languages, and are
commonly used to model memory in hardware.
The ability to efficiently reason about arrays, especially in combination with
machine integers, is therefore a core requirement for many applications
of computer-aided verification
[
26
,
11
,
1
]
.
Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) solvers for the theory of arrays provide
such reasoning and serve as the back end engine for a wide range of
applications.

The SMT-LIB standard
[
3
]
defines a theory of arrays with
extensionality as originally axiomatized by McCarthy
[
24
]
.
This theory provides array read (
select
) and write (
store
)
operators and allows extensionality (equality) over arrays.
While this is sufficient to express many aspects of modeling memory and array
data structures in hardware and software,
the theory lacks the ability to succinctly express operations over multiple
indices and memory initialization.
When modeling an array initialized with a default value,
e.g., to represent a zero-initialized chunk of memory,
this initialization must be expressed via a nested store expression that
explicitly writes the default value to all relevant indices.
This leads to inaccuracies in the modeling as indices can only be
explicitly overwritten, and to inefficiencies in solving procedures as the
nesting depth of such store chains can become very deep, depending on the
number of relevant indices.

Extending the SMT-LIB theory of arrays with so-called
constant
arrays
, i.e., arrays that store a single element at every
index, provides a succinct way to model array initialization with a default
value (which may be an arbitrary term).
Existing procedures for this extended theory of
extensional
constant
arrays, however, are all restricted to

infinite
index domains
[
33
,
21
,
13
]
.

In this paper, we present a novel decision procedure
for the
theory of extensional constant arrays

that is
not
restricted to
infinite index sorts.
Our procedure is presented as an abstract calculus and is a generalization and
extension of the
Lemmas on Demand (LOD)
procedure for the extensional
theory of arrays presented in
[
8
]
.
In particular, we make the following
contributions
:

•

We generalize the LOD procedure for the extensional
theory of arrays with fixed-size bit-vectors presented
in
[
8
]
to arbitrary index and element sorts.

•

We present this generalization as an abstract calculus

AEXT
, which serves as the core calculus of our new decision procedure
and is
decoupled
from the LOD solving paradigm
[
25
,
4
]
.

•

We then extend this core calculus with support for extensional constant
arrays, and prove refutational and satisfiability soundness of our resulting
calculus
CAEXT
for the theory of extensional constant arrays.

•

We implemented our procedure in the state-of-the-art SMT solver
Bitwuzla
[
28
]
. We evaluate it on a diverse
collection of benchmarks and use cases,
including applications in hardware model checking
and smart contract verification of the Ethereum virtual machine.
Our evaluation shows that our new procedure significantly expands the
solving capabilities of Bitwuzla.

Bitwuzla

3.1
Axiomatization of
\mathcal{T}_{\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle}

The theory of extensional constant arrays
\mathcal{T}_{\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle}
is axiomatized by
the following four axioms.
Array access and update are defined by McCarthy’s

read-over-write
axioms
row-eq
and
row-ne

[
24
]
.
Note that the axiomatization scheme for the non-extensional theory of arrays
only includes these two axioms.

Axiom
row-eq
states that if array
a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle
, which results from
storing element
u
at index
i
in array
a
, is accessed at that index,
the accessed element must be
u
.

\forall\,a,i,j,u\,.\,i\approx j\implies a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle[j]\approx u

(row-eq)

Axiom
row-ne
covers the symmetric opposite case and states that all
elements that were not overwritten by store
a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle
remain unchanged.

\forall\,a,i,j,u\,.\,i\not\approx j\implies a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle[j]\approx a[j]

(row-ne)

The
extensionality
axiom
ext
states that two arrays are equal
if and only if both store the same element on each index.
The axiomatization scheme for the extensional theory of arrays
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
consists of this and the previous two axioms.

\forall\,a,b\,.\,a\approx b\iff\forall\,i\,.\,a[i]\approx b[i]

(ext)

The axiomatization scheme for the extensional theory of constant arrays

\mathcal{T}_{\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle}
extends the scheme for
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
with
the
read-over-constant-array
axiom
roc

[
33
]
.
This axiom states that accessing a constant array at any arbitrary index always
yields its default value (the value it was constructed with).

\forall\,i,v\,.\,\langle v\rangle[i]\approx v

(roc)

3.2
Infinite vs. Finite Constant Arrays

A major limitation of previous work on extensionality of constant
arrays
[
33
,
21
,
13
]
is its restriction to

infinite
index domains.
In the following, we illustrate
why the restriction to infinite constant arrays significantly simplifies
procedures for extensional constant arrays.

Given two constant arrays
\langle v\rangle
and
\langle w\rangle
,
from axioms
ext
and
roc

it follows that
\langle v\rangle
and
\langle w\rangle
are
equal if and only if their default values
v
and
w
are equal,
i.e.,
\forall v,w\,.\,\langle v\rangle\approx\langle w\rangle\iff v\approx w
.
For constant arrays with
infinite index domain
,
the same also holds
for equality over array stores on constant arrays, e.g.,

\langle v\rangle\langle i\triangleleft w\rangle\langle j\triangleleft w\rangle\langle k\triangleleft w\rangle\approx\langle w\rangle
.
Intuitively,
infinite constant arrays with different default values cannot be “made” equal
since this requires an
infinite
number of array stores,
which cannot be expressed in the array theory.
Thus, for infinite constant arrays
\langle v\rangle
and
\langle w\rangle
, an equality over update sequences on

\langle v\rangle
and
\langle w\rangle
cannot hold if
v\approx w
does not hold.

For
finite index domains
, however, two constant arrays with different
default values can be made equal if sufficiently many stores ensure that they
are equal on every index.
For example, formula

\langle v\rangle\langle i\triangleleft w\rangle\langle j\triangleleft w\rangle\approx\langle w\rangle\wedge v\not\approx w

is satisfiable
for constant arrays with an index domain of size two,
since it is possible to find an interpretation for
i
and
j

such that all indices of
\langle v\rangle
are updated to
w
.

More generally, two finite constant arrays
\langle v\rangle_{\sigma}

and
\langle w\rangle_{\sigma}
with different default values
v
and
w

differ on a finite number of indices
n=|\sigma^{{\mathcal{I}}}|
.
Consider the following equality over array update sequences
on
\langle v\rangle
and
\langle w\rangle
:

\displaystyle\langle v\rangle\langle i_{1}\triangleleft w\rangle\langle\ldots\triangleleft\ldots\rangle\langle i_{l}\triangleleft w\rangle\approx\langle w\rangle\langle j_{1}\triangleleft v\rangle\langle\ldots\triangleleft\ldots\rangle\langle j_{k}\triangleleft v\rangle

If
v\not\approx w
,
this equality only holds if
the indices
not
updated in array update sequence

s_{v}\coloneq\langle v\rangle\langle i_{1}\triangleleft w\rangle\langle\ldots\triangleleft\ldots\rangle\langle i_{l}\triangleleft w\rangle

are updated in

s_{w}\coloneq\langle w\rangle\langle j_{1}\triangleleft v\rangle\langle\ldots\triangleleft\ldots\rangle\langle j_{k}\triangleleft v\rangle
,
i.e.,
s_{v}
and
s_{w}
must perform at least
n
array updates,
on
n
distinct indices.
In other words,
interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}
satisfies formula
s_{v}\approx s_{w}\wedge v\not\approx w

if and only if
\sigma^{\mathcal{I}}=\{i_{1}^{\mathcal{I}},\ldots,i_{l}^{\mathcal{I}},j_{1}^{\mathcal{I}},\ldots,j_{k}^{\mathcal{I}}\}
.
For any number of array stores
l+k<n
, the equality does not hold
since
s_{v}
and
s_{w}
differ on at least
n-(l+k)
indices.

Thus, the main challenge of deciding the equality of two store sequences over
different finite constant arrays is to check whether there exists an
arrangement of
n\!=\!|\sigma^{{\mathcal{I}}}|
unique array updates such that the resulting
updated arrays are equal.

4
Calculus

In this section, we first generalize and formalize the decision procedure for
the extensional theory of arrays presented in
[
8
]
, which we
refer to as DP
A
, as an abstract base calculus
AEXT
.
We then extend
AEXT
with a set of new rules
to handle extensional finite and infinite constant arrays,
yielding a new calculus
CAEXT
,
which maintains full
generality with respect to the finiteness of the index domain of constant arrays.
Finally, we prove that
CAEXT
is sound and complete for the theory of
extensional constant arrays
\mathcal{T}_{\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle}
.

In the following, we briefly introduce common assumptions, definitions and
notation used to define our base calculus and its extension.

Assumption 1

Whenever we refer to a set of
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}
-formulas
A
,
we assume that all literals in
A
are
flat
.
∎

This assumption is without loss of generality as any set of
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}
-formulas
can be easily transformed into a
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-equisatisfiable set by the
addition of fresh variables and equalities.
Note that some of the derivation rules we introduce later
introduce non-flat literals in
A
.
In such cases, we assume that similar transformations
maintain the invariant that all literals in
A
are flat.

Definition 1
(Configuration)

A configuration is either the distinguished configuration
unsat
or
a tuple
\langle A,{\mathcal{I}},\pi\rangle
, where

A
is a set of
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}
-formulas,

{\mathcal{I}}
is an interpretation in the empty theory
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{E}}
that satisfies

A
, and
\pi
is a map of propagation steps
(as defined in
Definition

2
).
∎

Calculus rules modify configurations and are given in

guarded assignment form
, where the rule premises describe the conditions
on the current configuration under which the rule can be applied.
The
conclusion
of a rule is either
unsat
or otherwise describes the
resulting modifications to the configuration.
An application of a rule is
redundant
if it has a conclusion where each
component of the derived configuration is equal to the corresponding
component in the premise configuration, i.e., it remains unchanged.
A configuration is
saturated
with respect to a set of rules if every possible
application of each rule is redundant.

In the following,
we use
\mathit{T}(A)
to denote the set of all terms in
formulas
A
, and
\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
to denote all array terms
in
A
.
Further, we assume that array disequalities

a\not\approx c\in A
are represented as

\neg(a\approx c)
.

4.1
Base Calculus

In this section, we generalize and formalize the LOD decision
procedure DP
A
for the extensional theory of arrays of
[
8
]
as
our base calculus
AEXT
.

The main concept of DP
A
as presented in
[
8
]

is based on a fixed point algorithm for
propagating and congruence checking array reads.
The algorithm produces lemmas that are derived from McCarthy’s array axioms

without
introducing new array reads.
In
[
8
]
, procedure DP
A
assumes that
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
is combined with
a single, decidable theory for array indices and elements,
and is presented in the context of combining

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
with the theory of fixed-size bit-vectors
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{BV}}
.
It is based on the lemmas on demand paradigm
[
25
,
4
]
,
but with a bit-vector abstraction at its core,
and relies on necessary formula transformations during preprocessing
that introduce virtual reads for array equalities and writes.
The authors show that for this combination of theories, DP
A
is sound and
complete.

We generalize DP
A
to combinations of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
with arbitrary theories for
array indices and elements and decouple it from the LOD paradigm.
Our generalized procedure for the theory of extensional arrays
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
does not rely on necessary formula transformations during preprocessing,
does not assume that array index and element sorts belong to the same theory,
and does not rely on the existence of a decision procedure for index and
element theories.
Instead, it assumes an interpretation of index and element terms in the empty
theory
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{E}}
.

We formalize our generalized procedure in terms of our
base calculus

AEXT
.
Given a set of
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}
-formulas

and a current interpretation

{\mathcal{I}}\models A
,
the derivation rules of our base calculus
aim to
propagate information
about array indices and elements from one array
to another, based on the axioms of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
.
For example, for an array access
a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle[i]

if
{\mathcal{I}}\models i\not\approx j

it follows that

a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle[i]\approx a[i]
.
We can therefore propagate the information that reading from
a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle

at index
i

yields the same element as reading from
a
at index
i
.
Our calculus explores propagations under the current interpretation
until unsatisfiability is derived, or all possible propagations have been explored.

Start.

Our calculus starts with the
initial
configuration

\langle A,{\mathcal{I}}_{0},\pi_{0}\rangle

with an empty interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}_{0}\coloneq\mathsf{none}

and an empty map of propagation steps
\pi_{0}\coloneq\lambda x,y\,.\,()
.
From this initial configuration,
it explores possible propagation steps
\pi
under a
current
interpretation

{\mathcal{I}}
for formulas
A
in the empty theory
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{E}}
, as
defined by rule
Interp
, while modifying the configuration.
If
A
is
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{E}}
-unsatisfiable,
rule
Conf
derives configuration
unsat
.
Else, if the configuration is
saturated
, i.e., each rule application is
redundant and configuration
unsat
was not derived,
for each array
a\in\mathit{T}_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
,
propagation steps
\pi(a,\cdot)
represent a partial model of
a
, which
can be extended to an interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathcal{A}}

that satisfies
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}
-formulas
A
.

Propagation.

In addition to rules
Interp
and
Conf
, we distinguish

propagation rules
that modify the map of propagation steps
\pi
(
Init*
,
Row*
,
Eq*
), and
conflict rules
that modify
the set of formulas
A
(
CongR
,
DisEq
).

Rules
InitR
and
InitW
initialize map
\pi
for each

direct read

a[i]\in\mathit{T}(A)
and

virtual read

a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle[i]

for each write
a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle\in\mathit{T}(A)
, respectively.
The purpose of these virtual reads is to represent the written element
u

in terms of a read
a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle[i]
, which allows for uniform
handling of array read and write terms in the calculus.
This is similar to the approach of DP
A
, where
virtual reads are conceptually added to the formula via top-level equalities

a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle[i]\approx u
as a preprocessing step.
In our calculus, however, the congruence of
a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle[i]
and
u

is ensured by rules
Interp
and
Conf
.

Rule
RowD
is based on a rule of DP
A
which applies read-over-write axiom
row-ne
.
Given a read
a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle[i]
, if
i\not\approx j
,
by axiom
row-ne
we have that

a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle[i]\approx a[i]
.
However, instead of creating a (potentially) fresh read
a[i]
,
we can use the existing read
a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle[i]
as
representative of
a[i]
under the current interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}
, and

propagate

a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle[i]
from source array

a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle
down to destination
a
, which is recorded in the conclusion of
rule
RowD
by adding propagation step

\pi(a,a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle[i])\coloneq(i\not\approx j,a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle)

to the configuration.

Conflict.

Rule
CongR
is based on the congruence rule of DP
A
, which handles the
case where two reads
b[i]
and
c[k]
read different elements
from the same array
a
at the same index.
Recall that
with

\pi(a,b[i])\neq()

and

\pi(a,c[k])\neq()
,
we have that

b[i]
is a representative of
a[i]
and

c[k]
is a representative of
a[k]
under the current
interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}
.
However, if

{\mathcal{I}}\models i\approx k\wedge b[i]\not\approx c[k]
,
the congruence axiom of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{E}}
is violated since

i\approx k\wedge b[i]\approx a[i]\wedge c[k]\approx a[k]\implies b[i]\approx c[k]
.
As a result,
CongR
adds a lemma to
A
to rule out the current
spurious interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}
.
Note that by modifying
A
, configuration components
\pi
and
{\mathcal{I}}
are reset to
their initial values
\pi_{0}
and
{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
since
\pi
is dependent on
the current interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}
, which does not satisfy the modified formulas

A
anymore.

Extensionality.

The derivation rules given in
Figure

3
are only required
if
A
contains extensionality over array terms.
Similarly to rule
RowD
,
if
{\mathcal{I}}\models i\not\approx j
,
rule
RowU
applies axiom

but propagates
a[i]
to

a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle
, since the element of
a
at index

remains unchanged after
the update operation.
Rules

and

apply the extensionality axiom

and
propagate reads on arrays
a
and
c
over equalities

from left to right and vice versa.

For array equalities
a\approx c
for which
{\mathcal{I}}\models a\not\approx c
,
rule
DisEq
adds an instance of axiom

to
A
.
This rule is the only rule that introduces fresh reads (on a fresh index

k_{\{a,c\}}
),
however, limited to at most two reads per equality.
The added lemma ensures that there exists
at least one witness index
k_{\{a,c\}}
at which arrays
a
and
c
differ.
In contrast, DP
A
relies on a preprocessing step that eagerly adds this lemma
for each

, independent of whether
{\mathcal{I}}\models a\not\approx c
.

4.2
Constant Array Extension

In this section, we extend our base calculus
AEXT
with a set of new rules
to support extensional constant arrays.
The new rules for our extended calculus
CAEXT
are given in

Figures

5
and
5
.

Figure

5
shows the additional conflict rule
Roc
required for
handling reads over constant arrays, while
Figure

5

lists the new rules to handle extensional constant arrays.

The main challenge of determining satisfiability of equalities between store sequences over
different finite constant arrays is that we have to reason about default
values for indices that have not been updated by array stores.
A straightforward extension of
AEXT
would be the introduction of virtual reads
for all indices of a constant array, i.e., adding

\langle v\rangle_{\sigma}[i]\approx v
for all
i\in\sigma^{\mathcal{I}}
.
However, this is not feasible in general,
neither for infinite nor for finite index domains.
For the infinite case, we would never reach a saturated configuration due to
infinitely many virtual reads.
And while it may be feasible for reasonably small finite index
domains, large domains would require a potentially unfeasibly large number of
derivations to reach a saturated configuration.

Our extended calculus
CAEXT
avoids the introduction of virtual reads
on constant arrays.
Instead, it propagates the
default value

v
of a constant array
\langle v\rangle

to array
a

while keeping track of updated indices
I(a,\langle v\rangle)
between

a
and
\langle v\rangle
.

Definition 4
(Updated Indices)

Given a propagation step
\pi(a,\langle v\rangle)\coloneq(r,c)

the set of
updated indices

I(a,\langle v\rangle)
consists of all
indices updated while propagating the default value of
\langle v\rangle
to
array
a
, and is defined as follows.

I(a,\langle v\rangle)=\begin{cases}()&\text{if }\pi(a,\langle v\rangle)=()\\
\emptyset&\text{if }a=\langle v\rangle\\
I(b,\langle v\rangle)\cup\{j\}&\text{if }\pi(a,\langle v\rangle)=(\top,b)\text{ with }\\
&\hskip 25.7001ptb=a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle\text{ or }a=b\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle\text{ for some }u\\

\end{cases}

∎

Propagating the default value of
\langle v\rangle

\pi(a,\langle v\rangle)

and the reason for this propagation
is denoted as

If a default value

\displaystyle\pi(s_{1},\langle v\rangle)\coloneq(\top,\langle v\rangle),

\displaystyle\pi(s_{2},\langle v\rangle)\coloneq(s_{1}\approx s_{2},s_{1}),

\displaystyle\pi(a,\langle v\rangle)\coloneq(\top,s_{2}),

\displaystyle\pi(s_{3},\langle v\rangle)\coloneq(\top,a),

\displaystyle\pi(s_{4},\langle v\rangle)\coloneq(s_{3}\approx s_{4},s_{3}),

\displaystyle\pi(\langle w\rangle,\langle v\rangle)\coloneq(\top,s_{4}).

We now have that

\pi(\langle w\rangle,\langle v\rangle)\!\neq\!()
,

\pi(\langle w\rangle,\langle w\rangle)\!\neq\!()

and
I(\langle w\rangle,\langle v\rangle)\!=\!\{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}\}\!
,
but indices
i_{1}
,
j_{1}
,
i_{2}
,
j_{2}
are all equal under
{\mathcal{I}}
,
and
v\not\approx w
.
This means that all indices
i\neq i_{1}^{\mathcal{I}}
need to have the default

v
and
w
at the same time, which is not possible, and thus, rule

CongC
adds the following lemma to
A
.

\displaystyle\overbrace{s_{1}\approx s_{2}\wedge s_{3}\approx s_{4}}^{\mathcal{R}(\langle w\rangle,\langle v\rangle)}\;\wedge\overbrace{\top}^{\mathcal{R}(\langle w\rangle,\langle w\rangle)}\wedge\;\;\exists i.\,\hskip-18.00005pt\bigwedge_{k\in\{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}\}}\hskip-18.00005pti\not\approx k\implies v\approx w

After that
{\mathcal{I}}
and
\pi
are reset to
{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
and
\pi_{0}
, and
we continue with a new interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}
.
In the next interpretation, indices
i_{1}
,
j_{1}
,
i_{2}
,
j_{2}
will be
forced to be different due to the added lemma, which may yield
interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}
:

\displaystyle i_{1}\neq j_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq j_{2},u_{1}=u_{2}=u_{3}=u_{4},u_{2}\neq v,v\neq w,s_{1}=s_{2},s_{3}=s_{4},

\displaystyle s_{1}[i_{1}]=u_{1},s_{2}[j_{1}]=u_{2},s_{3}[i_{2}]=u_{3},s_{4}[j_{2}]=u_{4}.

With this interpretation, after applying rules
InitW
and
InitC
,
read
s_{2}[j_{1}]
is propagated from
s_{2}
to
\langle v\rangle
by applying
rules
RowU
,
EqR
, and
RowD
.
This will result in a new lemma generated by rule
Roc
, since

\pi(\langle v\rangle,s_{2}[j_{1}])\neq()

and
{\mathcal{I}}\models s_{2}[j_{1}]\not\approx v
due to

{\mathcal{I}}\models s_{2}[j_{1}]\approx u_{2}\wedge u_{2}\not\approx v
.

Reads
s_{1}[i_{1}]
,
s_{3}[i_{2}]
, and
s_{4}[j_{2}]
will be
propagated similarly and after all conflicts are resolved we can finally
derive a saturated configuration, and thus, conclude with
satisfiable
.

The following interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}
that will lead to a saturated configuration, i.e.,
by applying all rules until fixed point, will not yield any conflict.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}\coloneq\{

\displaystyle i_{1}\neq j_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq j_{2},v=u_{2}=u_{4},w=u_{1}=u_{3},v\neq w,

\displaystyle s_{1}=s_{2},s_{3}=s_{4},s_{1}[i_{1}]=u_{1},s_{2}[j_{1}]=u_{2},s_{3}[i_{2}]=u_{3},s_{4}[j_{2}]=u_{4}\}.

4.3
Correctness

In this section, we establish the theorems for refutational and satisfiability
soundness of our base calculus presented in
Section

4.1
, and its extension
presented in
Section

4.2
.
Refutational soundness ensures that the calculus never reports
unsat
unless
the initial set of constraints is unsatisfiable. Satisfiability soundness is
the complementary property: if the calculus
does not
report
unsat
,
then the initial set of constraints is satisfiable. This is often
referred to as completeness, but we find the term satisfiability soundness more
precise in this context.
We prove the refutational and satisfiability soundness of our calculus

CAEXT
in the appendix.

Theorem 4.1
(Refutational Soundness)

If a derivation starting with an initial configuration
\langle A,{\mathcal{I}},\pi\rangle

ends in
unsat
, then
A
is
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-unsatisfiable.

Theorem 4.2
(Satisfiability Soundness)

If a derivation starting with an initial configuration
\langle A,{\mathcal{I}}_{0},\pi_{0}\rangle

ends in a configuration other than
unsat
, and every applicable rule
is redundant, then
A
is
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-satisfiable.

5
Integration

In
Section

4
,
we presented our new calculus
CAEXT
for the theory of extensional constant
arrays
\mathcal{T}_{\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle}
as a set of derivation rules that modify configurations
starting from the initial configuration
\langle A,{\mathcal{I}}_{0},\pi_{0}\rangle
.
Our calculus is
independent
of the underlying solver architecture
and only requires the existence of a procedure that provides an interpretation

{\mathcal{I}}
in the empty theory
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{E}}
(rule
Interp
).

We integrated a decision procedure for
\mathcal{T}_{\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle}
based on our new calculus

CAEXT
in the state-of-the-art SMT solver Bitwuzla
[
28
]
.
Bitwuzla specializes on
theories relevant for bit-precise reasoning and
supports the theories of fixed-size bit-vectors, floating-point
arithmetic, and combinations with arrays and uninterpreted functions.
Until now, it implemented a decision procedure for the theory of extensional
arrays based on
[
8
]
, extended with support for nested arrays
and non-extensional constant arrays.
We replaced this procedure with a new decision procedure based on
CAEXT
,
which seamlessly integrates into the LOD architecture of Bitwuzla.
In the context of
CAEXT
, the bit-vector solver at the core of its LOD
architecture is responsible for providing interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}

[
27
]
.
As implemented in Bitwuzla, our procedure incorporates several
optimizations
.

\mathit{distinct_{N}}
.

When a conflict between two different constant arrays is detected, i.e.,
rule
CongC
applies, we produce a resulting lemma that does not actually
contain a quantified formula.
Instead, the quantified formula is encoded as a special variant of
\mathit{distinct}
, which we call

\mathit{distinct_{N}}
.
While
\mathit{distinct}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
is true iff terms

t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
are pairwise different,

\mathit{distinct_{N}}(n,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
is true iff at least
n
terms
are pairwise different, i.e.,
the set of values assigned to
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
has at least
n
elements.
If
n=k
,
then
\mathit{distinct_{N}}(k,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\equiv\mathit{distinct}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
,
while for
n>k
,

\mathit{distinct_{N}}(n,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\equiv\bot
.
Given a finite sort
\tau
for
t_{i}
and

k>|\tau^{\mathcal{I}}|
,
\mathit{distinct}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\equiv\bot
,
whereas
\mathit{distinct_{N}}(n,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
for
n\leq k
is not.
We encode the quantified formula in
CongC
as follows.

\displaystyle\exists i:\sigma.\,\hskip-33.00008pt\bigwedge_{k\in I(a,\langle v\rangle)\cup I(a,\langle w\rangle)}\hskip-32.00002pti\not\approx k\;\;\equiv\;\;\neg\mathit{distinct_{N}}(|\sigma^{\mathcal{I}}|,I(a,\langle v\rangle)\cup I(a,\langle w\rangle))

The intuition here is that this quantified formula is true if there exists an
index
i
that has not been updated between
\langle v\rangle
and
a
and
\langle w\rangle

and
a
, and is thus not included in

{\mathcal{I}}_{\cup}\coloneq I(a,\langle v\rangle)\cup I(a,\langle w\rangle)
.
For
infinite
index domains, this will always be
\top
.
However, for
finite
index domains it depends on whether the set of updated
indices
{\mathcal{I}}_{\cup}

includes all possible indices of index domain
\sigma^{\mathcal{I}}
, which can be
encoded as

\mathit{distinct_{N}}(|\sigma^{\mathcal{I}}|,I(a,\langle v\rangle)\cup I(a,\langle w\rangle))
.

As an additional optimization, we encode a given

\mathit{distinct_{N}}(n,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
constraint lazily and use a
custom decision heuristic that tries to assign different values to terms

t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
.
If our heuristic fails to assign
n
unique values to the given terms, we
lazily expand the encoding of the constraint until we
find
n
different values or conclude that
\mathit{distinct_{N}}(n,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\equiv\bot
.

Propagation.

In order to reduce memory overhead, we do not immediately record pair
(r,c)

when constructing
\pi(a,t)
while exploring all possible propagations of reads.
Since these pairs are only required for constructing
\mathcal{R}
,
we instead replay the propagation steps
to produce the full map
\pi
containing
(r,c)

only for the array reads involved in a conflict.
In this replay phase, propagation is
performed in breadth-first-search order, which yields the shortest sequence
of propagation steps to the conflict under the current interpretation.

7
Conclusion

We have presented a new calculus
CAEXT
for the theory of extensional
constant arrays
\mathcal{T}_{\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle}
without imposing any limitations on the finiteness
of the array index domain.
We have shown the refutational and satisfiability soundness of
CAEXT
.
We integrated a new decision procedure for
\mathcal{T}_{\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle}
based on
CAEXT
in the state-of-the-art
SMT solver Bitwuzla and evaluated its performance on a diverse set of
benchmarks.
The new decision procedure enables Bitwuzla to solve problems it was not able
to handle before, and thus significantly improves over its baseline.

While the integration into Bitwuzla and its evaluation has focused
on arrays with finite index domains, our procedure is agnostic to the
underlying solver architecture and fully supports the infinite domain case.
As future work, we plan to integrate a new array solver based on
CAEXT
in the SMT solver cvc5.

First, we need the following definition, which assigns a

Definition 6

|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\pi(a,t)=()\\

The first lemma states that once a reason
\mathcal{R}
has been
established, it does not change unless a conflicting rule is applied and the
configuration is reset.

Lemma 1

Let
D
be a derivation ending in configuration
C
, with
C\not=\textsf{\small unsat}
. The following are true
of
\mathcal{R}
in
C
for all
a
and
t
:
(
i
) if
C
is obtained by
applying a non-conflict rule to a configuration
C^{\prime}
, and

\mathcal{R}(a,t)\not=()
in
C^{\prime}
,
then both
\mathcal{R}(a,t)
and
|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|
remain unchanged
from
C^{\prime}
to
C
.
(
ii
)
\mathcal{R}(a,t)
is well defined;
(
iii
)
|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|
is finite; and
(
iv
)
\mathcal{R}(a,t)=()
iff

\pi(a,t)=()
.

Proof

The proof is by induction over deductions. In an initial configuration (
i
)
holds trivially, and
\mathcal{R}
and
\pi
are always equal to
()
,
so it is easy to see that the properties hold.

Now, suppose they hold for configuration
C^{\prime}
, and let
C
be obtained from
C^{\prime}
by
the application of a rule other than
Conf
. Suppose the rule is a
conflict rule. Then (
i
) holds trivially, and as above,
\mathcal{R}
and

\pi
are always equal to
()
, so the other three properties hold as well.

Suppose the rule is not a conflict rule.
We first show (
i
) by considering, in
C^{\prime}
, the cases in the definition of

\mathcal{R}
. Suppose

\pi(a,t)=()
in
C^{\prime}
. By (
iv
), we also have

\mathcal{R}(a,t)=()
in
C^{\prime}
, so the property holds trivially.
Suppose
t=a
or
t=a[i]
for some
i
, then
\mathcal{R}(a,t)=\top
and

|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|=1
regardless of the
configuration. Finally, assume we have (in
C^{\prime}
)

\mathcal{R}(a,t)=\mathcal{R}(c,t)\wedge r\text{, where }(r,c)=\pi(a,t)
.
And suppose (
i
) does not hold.
Then we can choose
a
and
t
such that

\mathcal{R}(a,t)\not=()
in
C^{\prime}
,

\mathcal{R}(a,t)
or
|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|
changes value from
C^{\prime}
to
C
, and

|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|
is minimum in
C^{\prime}
(possible by property (
iii
)).

But
\pi(a,t)
cannot have changed from
C^{\prime}
to
C
, since
all non-conflict rules only modify
\pi(a,t)
if its value
is
()
. So, we must also have in
C
that

\mathcal{R}(a,t)=\mathcal{R}(c,t)\wedge r\text{, where }(r,c)=\pi(a,t)
and

|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|=|\mathcal{R}(c,t)|+1
.
Thus, if
\mathcal{R}(a,t)
or
|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|
has changed, it must be
because either
\mathcal{R}(c,t)
or
|\mathcal{R}(c,t)|
has also changed. But by definition,

|\mathcal{R}(c,t)|=|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|-1
, which
contradicts the minimality assumption.

We now show (
ii
) and (
iii
). Suppose
\mathcal{R}(a,t)\not=()
in

C^{\prime}
. We know by the induction hypothesis that
\mathcal{R}(a,t)
is well
defined and
|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|
is finite in
C^{\prime}
.
As we just showed above,
\mathcal{R}(a,t)
and
|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|

remain unchanged in
C
, meaning that

\mathcal{R}(a,t)
is well defined and
|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|
is finite in
C
.
On the other hand, suppose
\mathcal{R}(a,t)=()
in
C^{\prime}
. By

(iv)
, we have
\pi(a,t)=()
in
C^{\prime}
as well. If

\pi(a,t)=()
still in
C
, then it is clear that

\mathcal{R}(a,t)=()
in
C
as well, and so it is well
defined, and
|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|=0
. The last case is when

\pi(a,t)=()
in
C^{\prime}
but

\pi(a,t)\not=()
in
C
. This happens when the
rule newly defines
\pi(a,t)
. But whenever this happens,
we either have
t=a
or
t=a[i]
for some
i
, in which case

\mathcal{R}(a,t)
is trivially well defined with
|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|=1
, or
the rule newly defines

\pi(a,t)
to be some
(r,a^{\prime})
, where
\pi(a^{\prime},t)\not=()

in
C^{\prime}
. In the latter case, by (
iv
),
\mathcal{R}(a^{\prime},t)\not=()
in
C^{\prime}
, so by
(
i
),
\mathcal{R}(a^{\prime},t)
and
|\mathcal{R}(a^{\prime},t)|
are unchanged from
C^{\prime}
to

C
. And by (
iii

|\mathcal{R}(a^{\prime},t)|
is finite in
C^{\prime}
. But, by
definition,
\mathcal{R}(a,t)=\mathcal{R}(a^{\prime},t)\wedge r
, which is
well defined, and similarly,
|\mathcal{R}(a,t)|=|\mathcal{R}(a^{\prime},t)|+1

Finally, we show (
iv
). The if direction follows directly by the
definition of
\mathcal{R}(a,t)
. For the only-if direction, consider the
contrapositive. Suppose that
\pi(a,t)\not=()
. Then by
definition, either
\mathcal{R}(a,t)=\top
or
\mathcal{R}(a,t)=r_{1}\wedge r_{2}
for some
r_{1},r_{2}
. We know that it is well defined by (
ii
), so in
either case,
\mathcal{R}(a,t)\not=()
.

The next lemma provides the main invariant needed for soundness in the base calculus

Lemma 2

D
be a derivation ending in configuration
C
, with

in
C

The proof is by induction on the length of a derivation. If
C
is the
initial configuration,
\pi
is
empty, so the statement holds vacuously.

Now, suppose
C
is obtained by applying a rule to configuration
C^{\prime}
. We
consider each rule and show that it preserves the property.

First, if the rule is a conflict rule, then
\pi
is empty in
C
, so

Second, if
\pi(a,b[i])\not=()
in
C^{\prime}

1
,
\mathcal{R}(a,b[i])
has the same value in
C
and

C^{\prime}

C^{\prime}

C

The remaining case to consider is when
\pi(a,b[i])
is newly defined
by the rule. We consider all such rules.
First note that the
InitR
and

\pi
for
a pair of arguments of the form

C
.

Next, consider

is

(i\not\approx j,a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle)

\pi(a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle,b[i])\neq()

1
. Now, by the definition of
\mathcal{R}

And by the (

) array axiom, we have
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models i\not\approx j\implies a[i]\approx a\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle[i]
.
It follows that (in
C
)

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models\mathcal{R}(a,b[i])\implies a[i]\approx b[i]
.

The
RowU

. The newly defined value of
\pi(a^{\prime}\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle,b[i])

(i\not\approx j,a^{\prime})

\pi(a^{\prime},b[i])\neq()

C^{\prime}

by the

Lemma

1
. Now, by the definition of
\mathcal{R}
, we have
(in
C
)
\mathcal{R}(a^{\prime}\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle,b[i])=\mathcal{R}(a^{\prime},b[i])\wedge i\not\approx j
.
And by the (

) array axiom, we have
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models i\not\approx j\implies a^{\prime}[i]\approx a^{\prime}\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle[i]

C

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models\mathcal{R}(a^{\prime}\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle,b[i])\implies a^{\prime}\langle j\triangleleft u\rangle[i]\approx b[i]

Consider now

\pi(a,b[i])
in
C
is

(a\approx c,c)

\pi(c,b[i])\neq()

C^{\prime}

These are the only rules that add a newly defined value to

Refutational soundness is stated by the following theorem.

Theorem 0..1
(Refutational Soundness)

If a derivation starting with an initial configuration
\langle A,{\mathcal{I}},\pi\rangle

ends in
unsat
, then
A
is
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-unsatisfiable.

Proof

We first note that the only rule that derives
unsat
is the
Conf
rule, which applies when
A\cup\{a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle[i]\approx u\;|\;a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle\in\mathit{T}(A)\}
is unsatisfiable (in the empty theory). Clearly, this
implies that
A\cup\{a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle[i]\approx u\;|\;a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle\in\mathit{T}(A)\}
is also
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-unsatisfiable which is true iff

A
is
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-unsatisfiable, since

\{a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle[i]\approx u\;|\;a\langle i\triangleleft u\rangle\in\mathit{T}(A)\}
always holds in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
.

It thus suffices to show that the remaining rules are
satisfiability
preserving
with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
, that is,

A
is
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-satisfiable iff
A^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-satisfiable, where

A
and
A^{\prime}
are the values of
A
before and
after applying the rule, respectively. For most rules, this is trivially
true, as they do not modify
A
.

Consider now rule
CongR
, which adds
\mathcal{R}(a,b[i])\wedge\mathcal{R}(a,c[k])\wedge i\approx k\implies b[i]\approx c[k]
to
A
. By
Lemma

2
,
we know that
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models\mathcal{R}(a,b[i])\implies a[i]\approx b[i]
and also
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models\mathcal{R}(a,c[k])\implies a[k]\approx c[k]
. It follows that
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models\mathcal{R}(a,b[i])\wedge\mathcal{R}(a,c[k])\wedge i\approx k\implies b[i]\approx c[k]
. Since the added
formula is
valid
in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
,
adding it to
A
does not change its
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-satisfiability.

Next, consider rule
DisEq
, which adds the formula
a\not\approx c\implies a[k_{\{a,c\}}]\not\approx c[k_{\{a,c\}}]
. Call the result

A^{\prime}
. Clearly, if
A^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-satisfiable, then
so is
A
. In the other direction, suppose
A
is

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-satisfiable with interpretation
{\mathcal{I}}
. If
a^{\mathcal{I}}=c^{\mathcal{I}}
, then

A^{\prime}
is also satisfied by
{\mathcal{I}}
. Otherwise, if
{\mathcal{I}}\models a\not\approx c
, then
a^{\mathcal{I}}(\iota)\not=c^{\mathcal{I}}(\iota)
for some

\iota
. We can then modify
{\mathcal{I}}
to
{\mathcal{I}}^{\prime}
where
{\mathcal{I}}^{\prime}
is identical to
{\mathcal{I}}

except that it interprets
k_{a,c}
as
\iota
.
{\mathcal{I}}^{\prime}
then satisfies

A^{\prime}
.

We next consider rule
Roc

\mathcal{R}(\langle v\rangle,b[i])\implies b[i]\approx v
.
By
Lemma

2
, we know that
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models\mathcal{R}(\langle v\rangle,b[i])\implies\langle v\rangle[i]\approx b[i]
. But by array
axiom (

), we know that
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models\langle v\rangle[i]=v
, so

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models\mathcal{R}(\langle v\rangle,b[i])\implies b[i]\approx v
. Since the added
formula is
valid
in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
,
adding it to
A
does not change its
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-satisfiability.

Finally, consider rule

\mathcal{R}(a,\langle v\rangle)\wedge\mathcal{R}(a,\langle w\rangle)\wedge\linebreak\exists i:\sigma.\,\bigwedge_{k\in I(a,\langle v\rangle)\cup I(a,\langle w\rangle)}i\not\approx k\implies v\approx w
.
By
Lemma

4
,
we know that

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models\mathcal{R}(a,\langle v\rangle)\implies\forall\,i.\bigvee_{k\in I(a,\langle v\rangle)}i\approx k\vee a[i]\approx v

and also

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\models\mathcal{R}(a,\langle w\rangle)\implies\forall\,i.\bigvee_{k\in I(a,\langle w\rangle)}i\approx k\vee a[i]\approx w

It follows that

.
Since the added
formula is
valid
in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
,
adding it to
A
does not change its
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
-satisfiability.

These are all of the rules that modify
