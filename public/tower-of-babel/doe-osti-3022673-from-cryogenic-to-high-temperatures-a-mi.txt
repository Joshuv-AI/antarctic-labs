Abstract

Phase stability and phase transformations in binary Pu alloys are critical for multiple
applications. Long-term phase stability affects mechanical properties, microstructure,
corrosion behavior, and structural integrity. A clear and detailed understanding of phase
transformations and phase stability mechanisms is necessary to assess the behavior and
response to unexpected stimuli. Gaining advanced knowledge on the behavior of Pu and
Pu alloys at a variety of temperatures, pressures, and time will ensure better predictions
and control on evolution outside laboratory measurements. For a better understanding of
the temperature dependence of the thermal expansion and microstructure of δ-phase 239Pu-
Ga alloys, time-of-flight neutron diffraction measurements were performed using the
Spectrometer for Materials Research at Temperature and Stress (SMARTS), a high-
resolution low-flux instrument at Manuel Lujan Jr. Neutron Scattering Center. δ-phase
239
 Pu-Ga alloys of two Ga concentrations, 2 at.% and 7 at.%Ga, underwent an initial
cooling from room temperature to 10K, followed by heating to nominally 673K, and finally
a second cooling back to room temperature. The crystallographic thermal expansion of
each alloy was measured between 10K and 673K. Two distinct behaviors emerged: 1.)
severe diffraction peak broadening in the Pu-2at.%Ga sample and 2.) thermal expansion of
Pu-7at%Ga sample at elevated temperature. This work repeats and validates previous
neutron and x-ray studies, with increased density of data points, and the ability to isolate
anisotropic lattice response in understanding these phenomena.

1 Introduction
Plutonium is one of the most complex elements on the periodic table, exhibiting six
allotropes (α, β, γ, δ, δ’, and ε) at ambient pressure and temperatures from 0 K to its melting
point (913K) [1]. Moreover, Pu is well known for its high toxicity, reactivity and
radioactivity which make handling difficult and complicate experimental work.

Of particular interest for technological applications is the ductile face-centered cubic (fcc)
-phase of Pu. The high-symmetry crystal structure is stable at elevated temperatures in
pure Pu (320 - 450◦C), but it can be stabilized at ambient temperature and below by the
addition of small amounts of trivalent elements (deltagens), such as Al, Ga, In, Sc or Ce
[2-5]. Despite its uncomplicated crystal structure, -Pu has anomalous thermal-physical
properties such as negative thermal expansion over specific temperature ranges [6, 7], large
low-temperature specific heat [8] and large volume expansion during phase transformation
[6]. Alloying alters the lattice parameter [4] and physical properties [3] and gives rise to
metastable δ-phase alloys.

When studying phase stability and thermo-physical properties of Pu alloys, consideration
must be given to the radioactive nature of these materials and resulting self-irradiation
damage. Self-irradiation damage in Pu drives continuous evolution of the microstructure
resulting in changes in mechanical properties, corrosion behavior, and dimensional
stability, amongst others [9-11]. For reactor- and weapon-grade material, the dominant
isotope is 239Pu, an α-particle emitter with a half-life of 24,300 years. Plutonium α-decay
generates an 86keV uranium nucleus and a 5MeV He nucleus, the recoil of which lead to
roughly 2500 Frenkel pairs and displacement cascades [12] per fission, producing
interstitials and vacancies, some of which will survive and diffuse to form dislocation loops
[13]. He, insoluble in metals, will accumulate forming voids and eventually bubbles [14-
16], causing bulk swelling [17], increased strength and loss of ductility [11].

While bulk swelling due to He is relatively well understood [9, 17] and predictable, the -
phase of Pu alloys (e.g., Pu-Ga and Pu-Al [18]) exhibits at ambient conditions early time
expansion of the lattice parameter [13, 19-22] that saturates after 1-2 years in materials
with common isotopic mixes [12-13, 19]. At cryogenic temperatures, the self-irradiation
damage seems to be accelerated, and saturation is reached faster than under ambient
conditions [23, 39]. The mechanism of lattice expansion is still not fully understood [19-
24]. Wolfer et al. supposed that the swelling was due to self-irradiation induced Ga
segregation effectively reducing the Ga in solution and expanding the lattice parameter
[19]. Both Ravat et al. [22] and Freibert et al. [24] proposed that lattice defects resulting
from fission cascades drive the lattice expansion, though the former cites lattice vacancies
and the latter interstitial defects as the source. Recent density functional theory DFT studies
still look to understand the crystal defects associated with the lattice expansion [25].

Upon cooling below room temperature, annealed δ-phase Pu1-xGax alloys of low Ga
concentration (1.7<x<4) undergo an incomplete δ → δ + α′ transformation via an
isothermal martensitic mechanism, from the face-centered cubic (fcc) -phase to a
maximum volume fraction of up to 30% monoclinic α'-phase [3, 26-30]. The
 1

transformation is direct, without any intermediate phases (e.g, γ or γ′), and linearly
dependent on the Ga content. The newly formed phase α′ is an expanded monoclinic α-
phase, with Ga atoms entrapped in the plutonium lattice; the amount of newly formed α′
phase is dependent on temperature, time and history of the material. Cooling rate, holding
time, composition, homogeneity, grain size, or deformation seem to influence the
martensitic process. The transformation partially reverts to the initial δ-phase upon heating
back to room temperature and can be fully removed if the alloy continues to be heated to
temperatures above 600K.

The martensitic transformation has been extensively studied by several techniques over the
years, most notably X-ray diffraction (XRD) [29-30], dilatometry and differential scanning
calorimetry (DSC) [31]. Both dilatometry and DSC measurements on alloys near 2 at.%
Ga observe signals associated with a phase transformation which is confirmed to be from
the − to α'-phase via post-mortem transmission electron microscopy (TEM) [31]. In-situ
XRD at cryogenic temperatures also observes the  to α' transformation, again supported
by TEM [32].

However, neutron diffraction experiments on enriched 242Pu-2at.%Ga reported by Lawson
et al. (δ-phase 242Pu-2at.%Ga alloy) [7] and Smith et al. (δ-phase 239Pu-Ga alloys stabilized
with 2 and 7at.%Ga) [33-39] failed to observe diffraction peaks associated with the α' upon
cooling from room temperature to less than 10 K. Rather, a severe and anisotropic
broadening of the -phase diffraction peaks is observed over the expected temperature
range of the transformation [21, 33-39].

The purpose of this report is to present neutron diffraction results on δ-phase 239Pu-Ga
alloys stabilized with 2 and 7at.%Ga using a high-resolution low-flux time-of-flight
neutron diffractometer, complimentary to earlier measurements performed on a high-flux
low- resolution time-of-flight neutron diffractometer. Diffraction patterns were collected
between 10K and 693K with the intent of determining the lattice parameters and lattice
parameter distribution with high accuracy, and studying the effects of Ga concentration,
microstructure and self-irradiation damage on the phase stability for a better understanding
of the thermo-physical properties. The results corroborate previous x-ray and neutron
diffraction observations as well as offer new insights.

2 Experimental

2.1 Sample Preparation
Two high-purity polycrystalline samples of δ-phase 239Pu-Ga alloys (Pu assay: 93% 239Pu,
6% 240Pu, 1% other Pu isotopes) were used for the measurements described in this article.
The samples were produced in batches of polycrystalline 239Pu, stabilized with 2.0 and 7.0
at. % Ga in the δ-phase by casting, followed by homogenization to single phase materials
at temperatures above 450°C. Chemical analysis results showed approximately 1215 at.
ppm of non-Ga impurities (i.e., 116 at. ppm of Fe, 20 at. ppm of Ni, 23 at. ppm of Cr, and
1056 at. ppm of other metals) for 2.0 at.% Ga material. X-ray diffraction characterization
at room temperature and ambient pressure using a Scintag XDS 2000 laboratory X-ray
diffractometer (Cu-Kα1 radiation) confirmed the single fcc δ-phase structure in the samples
[40-44].

The initial thermal treatment/annealing was performed by holding the samples for 100
hours at 450°C under vacuum in a Sieverts apparatus [45]. The process enables
homogenization of the Ga, eliminates long-range damage and defects (i.e., refreshed
samples) [46], and removes hydrogen impurities from the samples. Upon completion of
the thermal treatment, the δ-phase 239Pu-Ga samples were loaded under inert atmosphere
in vanadium double-encapsulation sample containers and shipped to the Manuel Lujan Jr.
Neutron Scattering Center, Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) for neutron
scattering experiments. The Pu-2at.%Ga sample double- container was wrapped with Cu
foil to provide additional calibration, enabling improved accuracy comparison of lattice
parameters. The Cu diffraction peaks are well separated from δ-phase Pu-Ga alloy peaks,
and the lattice parameter of the Cu foil, measured simultaneously with that of the sample,
gives an accurate assessment of the temperature at the sample position at temperatures
above 100 K [48].

2.2 Sample Encapsulation for Neutron Scattering
 Experiments
For many years, neutron scattering experiments using samples of 239Pu were considered
extremely difficult to impossible to perform due to the high absorption cross section of the
isotope (σabs= 1017 barns). Therefore, 242Pu isotope was preferred due to a lower absorption
cross section (σabs= 18.5 barns). We note that neutron scattering measurements using 242Pu
have not always shown the same results as 239Pu (e.g., differences in lattice parameter
values [21]). Furthermore, typical Pu samples of interest for aging and response to
experimental stimuli are primarily of the 239Pu isotope, which is also of greater availability
than 242Pu.

Unlike X-rays, neutrons interact with the nucleus of an atom, and the scattering power has
a quasi-random isotope-dependent variation. This results in an increased scattering for the
low Z elements, and the unique capability of neutrons to differentiate between neighboring
 3

atoms of the periodic table, and among isotopes of an element. As an isotopically sensitive
probe, neutrons are used in isotopic substitution for highlighting specific structural
features. A prime example is the high sensitivity of neutrons to hydrogen, and the capability
to differentiate between hydrogen and deuterium.

In recent years, despite all the challenges, the feasibility of neutron scattering
measurements using 239Pu isotope samples at Manuel Lujan Jr., Neutron Scattering Center
has been demonstrated for both neutron diffraction and neutron pair distribution function
analysis [33- 44]. Compared to earlier studies, the neutron beamlines used in those studies
provide much larger detector areas, allowing count times of hours instead of days. The use
of 239Pu alloys also circumvents other problems with using 242Pu materials, including high
238
 Pu content [21], and high 234U, 241Am, and other impurities.

For neutron coherent elastic scattering measurements, such as diffraction, sample
containers are made from materials with a very low elastic scattering cross section for
neutrons, such as vanadium. Vanadium is predominantly an incoherent scatterer and has a
very low coherent scattering cross section (0.0184 barns, negligible compared to the
coherent scattering cross-sections of most elements), and therefore vanadium containers
yield no significant contribution to the diffraction pattern for most samples. Measurements
involving 239Pu samples though, with a scattering length of 7.7 fm and coherent scattering
cross-section 7.5 barns but an absorption cross-section of 1017 barns, are an exception,
where the Bragg peaks from vanadium are present/visible in the diffraction pattern at a
similar magnitude as the Bragg peaks from the sample since most neutrons interacting with
239
 Pu are absorbed.

When working with radioactive and otherwise hazardous samples such as actinides,
contamination and the potential inhalation, along with its radioactivity, are of paramount
concern. Therefore, for radiological safety, neutron experiments with plutonium samples
require a double encapsulation sample containment. The δ-phase 239Pu-Ga samples were
loaded in the primary container, which was then sealed and placed in a secondary container,
under inert atmosphere (Fig. 1(a) and (b)). Further details of our double-encapsulation
vanadium sample container have been described elsewhere [47].

2.3 Thermal Treatments and Measurement Timeline
The initial thermal treatment of the δ-phase 239Pu-Ga alloys was performed in a Sieverts
apparatus at the fabrication site, in preparation for the neutron scattering experiments at
non-ambient conditions. To refresh the sample, similar treatment procedures were repeated
ex-situ at the Lujan Center using high vacuum furnaces (<10-6 Torr) prior to each neutron
diffraction experiment in subsequent years. Between run cycles, samples were stored under
ambient conditions (RT, ambient pressure) at Lujan Center.

Figures 2a and b show the thermal histories experienced by the Pu-2at.%Ga and Pu-
7at.%Ga samples, respectively. The blue line shows the control temperature of the cryo-
furnace. The red dots on figure 1a indicate the temperature determined from the lattice
parameter of the copper foil attached to the outside of the containment and the known
thermal expansion of Cu [48]. Below 100K, the Cu thermal expansion approaches zero and
is no longer appropriate for temperature determination, so the control temperature is used.
At elevated temperature, there is a stable gradient between the control temperature and the
temperature of the Cu foil wrapped around the sample container. At the maximum target
temperature of the experiment, 673 K, the Cu in contact with the containment was only
625 K (1st cycle) to 640 K (3rd cycle). We will continue to refer to the heat treatment
temperature as nominally 673K but recognize that the actual temperature was somewhat
lower. A similar gradient will be assumed to exist for both samples, with the actual
temperature estimated from the relationship between the thermocouple and the Cu
observed for the Pu-2at.%Ga sample.

2.4 Neutron Diffraction Measurements
In-situ neutron diffraction measurements during cooling/heating profiles between 673 K
and 10 K were completed on the Spectrometer for Materials Research at Temperature and
Stress (SMARTS) at the Lujan Center at the Los Alamos Neutron Science Center
(LANSCE). Details of the SMARTS diffractometer are published elsewhere [49] and only
a short description is presented here. Figure 3a shows a schematic of the diffractometer set
up; a picture of the cryo-furnace installed on SMARTS is shown in figure 3b. Figure 3c
shows the sample in double containment set up on SMARTS for ex-situ measurements.
Measurements under non-ambient temperature conditions were completed with the
samples mounted in a Janis Research System cryo-furnace based on the Sumitomo SHI-
4T and SHI-950T series cryo-coolers. To achieve temperatures below room temperature,
the sample area was filled to ~0.1 atm with He to act as an exchange gas. When at elevated
temperature, the area around the sample was evacuated to protect the electronics. A type E
thermocouple attached to the cold head of the cryo-furnace was used to monitor and control
the sample temperature.

SMARTS accepts a pulsed (20Hz) white neutron beam generated through spallation
reactions in a tungsten target and moderated by water at 283K (10°C). The incident neutron
beam was collimated to 8 mm tall by 12 mm wide using motorized boron nitride slits before
passing through aluminum vacuum windows and impinging on the sample. The slits are
shown in figures 1b and c in the retracted position. They are moved to 270 mm from the
sample for measurements.

Diffracted neutrons from the sample are counted in the two distinct detector banks at ± 90˚
relative to the incident beam in the horizontal plane located 1.5m from the sample (banks
 6

1 and 2 as shown in the schematic) and one bank at 155° in the vertical plane (bank 3),
below the incident beam, for high-resolution measurements. The diffraction vectors
associated with banks 1 and 2 are in the horizontal plane ± 135° from the transmitted beam.
The diffraction vector from bank 3 is in the vertical plane, 167.5° from the transmitted
beam. Neutrons diffracted from the Al windows and radiation shields are blocked by Gd2O3
painted Cd sheets strategically placed to prevent parasitic scattering in the ± 90˚ banks.
Shielding between the incident beam and the bank 3 flight path (shown retracted in figure
1b.) and c.) blocks scattering from the Al vacuum window. However, there is no easy way
to prevent scattering from the internal Al radiation shields from appearing in bank 3.

Typically, the uncertainties in quantities extracted from the diffraction data are taken to be
the statistical estimated standard deviations (esd) returned by GSAS (typically d ~ 0.0001
Å, d/d ~ 0.000025 or 25 ). However, this ignores any potential systematic errors, such
as displacement of the sample from the diffractometer center within the containment. The
positioning uncertainty of the sample within the double containment [47], increases the
uncertainty of the absolute lattice parameter determination [52] as well as the relative
lattice parameter (strain). Experience with aligning the sample suggests that vibration of
the closed cycle refrigerator or even rotation of the SMARTS sample position stage can
move the sample within the container. Measurements of fictitious strains with known
displacement of a stress-free Cu foil (similar thickness to the scattering volume in Pu
sample) from the diffractometer center have been completed and show an error of 500
/mm in the 90° banks [53] and 50 /mm in the backscattering bank (unpublished). This
corresponds to a relatively large uncertainty of the -phase absolute lattice parameter of ±
0.006 Å determined in the 90° banks, but a manageable uncertainty of ± 0.0006 Å in the
backscattering bank (bank 3).

The results from the early ex-situ measurements on the Pu-2at.%Ga sample [54], are
highlighted in red, but are artificially shown at a time immediately prior to the in-situ
measurements described in this article, to maintain the scale. The uncertainty in the lattice
parameter from the early measurements is relatively large because the Cu calibrant was not
included in these measurements. The two measurements collected nine months earlier (0)
are on material at room temperature in an aged condition (4 months at ~50 K) and
immediately following heat treatment (673 K for 2 days). With the relatively large
uncertainty, the absolute lattice parameters for these two measurements, 4.6236 ± 0.001 Å
and 4.6245 ± 0.001 Å, respectively, are within uncertainty of each other and previously
published results for both freshly homogenized material [4], 4.6234 ± 0.0001 Å, marked
by the red line figure 4a) and aged material [18-20, 22]. While the ex-situ heat treatment
for the earlier measurements did not change the lattice parameter outside of uncertainty, it
did significantly reduce the width of the (220) diffraction peak [54].

At the time of the measurement described in this report, the sample had been aged several
months at room temperature since the previous heat treatment to nominally 673 K [54].
The observed lattice parameter, 4.6246 ± 0.0006 Å, is larger than that published by Ellinger
et al. [4], 4.6234 ± 0.0001 Å (by linear regression), and Ravat et al [22], 4.6235 ± 0.0004
Å, for freshly homogenized material with similar Ga content. The observed fractional
increase in the lattice parameter relative to [4], 0.026 ± 0.013% is consistent with that
reported in material with roughly 2 at% Ga aged to saturation, 0.04% [18, 19] and 0.07 ±
0.03% [20, 22].

Subsequently, with heating to nominally 673 K (2), a relatively small but significant
increase of the lattice parameter is observed consistent with past crystallographic thermal
expansion studies [21]. With return to room temperature (3), the lattice parameter returns
to 4.6245 ± 0.0006 Å, not significantly different from the initial value. A larger change in
lattice parameter is apparent with cooling from 298 K (3) to 10 K (4) accompanied by
instantaneous (on the time scale of the diffraction integration) and severe broadening of the
diffraction peaks in both the raw data (figure 3) and FWHM (figure 4b). There is no
evolution of the lattice parameter or (220) peak width with time at 10 K outside of the
estimated uncertainty. The lattice parameter returns to the initial value, 4.6245 ± 0.0006 Å,
and the peak narrows, but not to the initial width, with return to room temperature (5); at
this point the (220) FWHM is consistent with that measured earlier, following 4 months at
50 K [48]. Heating to 673 K (6) continues to recover the peak width. Finally, with return
to room temperature following the 673 K two-day heat treatment (7), the observed lattice
parameter is marginally smaller than the previous measurements, 4.6234 ± 0.0006 Å, and
(220) FWHM has returned to within uncertainty of its initial values.

4 Discussion and Conclusions
There are two distinct effects observed in the above results that require further discussion,
1.) the low temperature response of the Pu-2at%Ga sample i.e. lack of observed δ-α
transformation and severe peak broadening and 2.) the apparent thermal expansion of Pu-
7at%Ga at elevated temperature.

The initial thermal path of the 239Pu-2at.%Ga sample presented above (steps 1-3 in figure
2a) consisted of a long hold of 49 hrs. at ~625 K followed by slow cooling at 2-5 K/min
(plus a long hold for data collection at room temperature) to 10 K where it was held for
216hrs. (9 days). As highlighted in the inset to figure 3, α peaks are not observed in the
current work to the lowest temperature achieved, 10 K. When cooling 242Pu-2at.%Ga at
similar rate, ~ 1K/min, Lawson et al. [7, 21] also failed to observe diffraction associated
with the α-phase. Similarly, an absence of α-phase in the diffraction patterns was also
observed by Smith et al. during their neutron diffraction experiments upon cooling δ-phase
239
 Pu-Ga alloys from room temperature to 12K [33-39]. The lack of observed α diffraction
peaks suggests that if the δ - α transformation did occur, then it must have been a small
volume fraction and/or in very small crystallites [21]. However, post-mortem microscopy
studies at room temperature following dilatometry experiments on Pu-2at.%Ga samples
with similar thermal path, i.e. 50 hours at 673 K followed by 2.5 K/min- 5K/min cooling
to 118 K, report a significant population of α laths with thickness of order 1  m and length
of greater than 10  m [32].

Figure 10 shows a small section (d = 2.53 Å to 2.80 Å) of the observed diffraction
integrated for 105 beam hours at 10 K on SMARTS. This range represents an area where
α peaks should be present and removed from δ peaks. Also shown are simulated neutron
diffraction patterns generated by GSAS [50] assuming α weight fractions of 0.05, 0.10 and
0.20 (volume fractions of 0.04, 0.08 and 0.17) with added random noise comparable to the
data. The simulated patterns assume no sample broadening (in addition to instrumental
broadening) from the α phase. Finite size broadening [57-58] should not be significant in
laths of the size reported in [32]. Moreover, α is brittle and should not support a significant
dislocation population. However, heterogeneous stress distribution from the surrounding
δ-phase matrix and/or chemical heterogeneity, for example, could cause broadening. Thus,
the simulated diffraction patterns represent a “best case” scenario, and it can be concluded
that a minimum α volume fraction of 0.08 peaks would be necessary for the phase to be
readily observable in the neutron diffraction data. Maximum α volume fractions of 0.12 to
0.19 are reported by Mitchell et al. [32], in similar material taken through a similar path.
Moore et al.[59] and Platteau et al [60] both reported α volume fractions of roughly 0.15.
It is not immediately obvious why such an α microstructure would not be apparent in
neutron diffraction patterns.

Lawson et al. [21] reported significant, anisotropic peak broadening of the δ-phase in 242Pu-
2at.%Ga at low temperature, comparable to that reported herein, and suggested it was due
to an anisotropic elastic response of the δ-phase to the large change of volume that
accompanies the presumed formation of the α-phase. Similar to Lawson et al.’s results
 16

[21], Smith et al. also reported significant peak broadening for δ-phase 239Pu-Ga alloys
upon cooling to cryogenic temperatures, using a high-flux low-resolution time-of-flight
neutron diffractometer [33-39]. As Lawson et al., we do not eliminate the possibility of
the sample undergoing a martensitic transformation, minor and undetected, and
correspondingly, speculate that the α'- phase fraction or any α' precipitates that may form,
if any, are below the detection limit of our diffraction measurements (<0.5%). Smith et
al.’s earlier high-flux low-resolution time-of-flight neutron diffraction measurements
employed freshly annealed δ-phase 239Pu-Ga alloy samples [33-39], while Lawson et al.’s
samples, albeit still young (0.1 yrs.), did spend several weeks under ambient conditions
awaiting shipping, and accumulating self- irradiation damage. Per our/Smith et al’s earlier
results, the peak broadening that occurred upon cooling to cryogenic temperatures does not
disappear when the sample is heated back to RT [33-39]. Smith et al.’s earlier neutron
diffraction measurements revealed that samples with no room temperature self-irradiation
damage experienced a swelling of the lattice at the end of the measurement, after being
cooled to cryogenic temperature and heated back to room temperature, while samples with
significant room-temperature accumulated self-irradiation damage, experienced only a
partial recovery of the lattice parameter value upon completion of the measurements and
return to room temperature.

The morphology of α nuclei postulated on 242Pu-2at.%Ga, 14 Å crystallites, to explain the
lack of diffraction peaks is not consistent with other microstructural observations [32].
Moreover, an anisotropic elastic modulus would be expected to cause crystal orientation
dependent elastic strains (intergranular or type II [56]) but by itself would not drive peak
broadening (intragranular or type III [63]).

In contrast, crystal defects (e.g. dislocations) cause a locally (intragranular or type III [63])
heterogeneous stress field that drives broadening and the magnitude of that broadening is
modulated by the anisotropic elastic stiffness called the dislocation contrast factor [64].
More recent transmission electron microscopy (TEM) studies of Pu-2at.%Ga directly show

 17

a significant increase in dislocation density associated with the α-δ phase at cryogenic
temperature.

Zhang et al. [54] compared the anisotropic peak broadening in room temperature neutron
diffraction measurements on δ-phase 239Pu-2at.%Ga following 4 months at cryogenic
temperature (< 50K) to that of fcc steel deformed to 10% compressive strain. The
anisotropic peak broadening from deformed fcc steel is unambiguously related to a
significant increase in dislocation density associated with the deformation [65]. The
specific dependence of the peak broadening on the crystallographic orientation is known
as the dislocation contrast factor (similar to contrast matching in TEM) and is a signature
of the Burger’s vector associated with the active slip system [58], in the case of FCC steel,
the {111}〈110〉 system.

Following [54], figure 11 shows Williamson-Hall plots [66] of the peak FWHM in units of
d-spacing (Å) as a function of d-spacing normalized by the lattice parameter, d/a. The
normalization enables direct comparison of common diffraction peaks from the Pu-
2at.%Ga (a = 4.625 Å) sample under distinct aging conditions with a well characterized
304L stainless steel (FCC, a = 3.590 Å) [67] sample deformed to 32% tensile strain. What
appears to be noise in the data, far above the uncertainty level, is in fact the orientation
dependence associated with the dislocation contrast factor [54, 64]. The scatter pattern is
consistently observed from the deformed steel sample as well as the Pu-2at.%Ga samples;
the (200) and (311) peaks are significantly broader than a presumed linear fit to the data,
the (111) is narrower. The magnitude of the variation in the steel sample is somewhat
reduced relative to the Pu alloy because steel is less elastically anisotropic than δ-Pu
[61,68]. The fact that the cryogenically aged δ-phase 239Pu-2at.%Ga sample exhibited the
same anisotropic broadening as the deformed steel is a strong indication that the broadening
is associated with type 〈110〉 dislocations. It should be noted that with this magnitude of
dislocation density, the flow strength of the steel has increased through work hardening to
greater than 700 MPa from the as-annealed strength of roughly 150 MPa [65].

The diffraction peak breadth from the Pu-2at.%Ga sample, and presumably dislocation
density, does not increase significantly over the 10 days at cryogenic temperature (figure
4b), nor between 10 days and 4 months. The defects introduced during cooling increase as
a function of decreasing temperature from 170 K to 80 K (figure 8) but develop on a time
scale that is short compared to the integration of a single diffraction pattern (~2 hours). The
current measurements, particularly the long hold at low temperatures, were conceived with
the idea that microstructural defects (e.g. dislocations) due to the accumulation of self-
radiation damage cannot recover at cryogenic temperatures and would increase with time.
However, the broadening occurs much too quickly, instantaneously on the time scale of
the neutron diffraction measurements, to be due to self-radiation damage and does not
significantly increase with time. Moreover, no broadening whatsoever was observed in the
δ-phase 239Pu-7at.%Ga. Thus, it seems that the broadening, whether due to dislocations or
other phenomenon, is not related solely to self-irradiation damage. If we consider the
increase in dislocation density that occurs over the temperature range where the δ – α
transition is expected to occur [32], a potential explanation is related to the local plasticity
generated by the large volume expansion associated with the phase transformation α-δ [59-
60]. Yet, the fact remains that the α phase is not observed in the neutron diffraction

 19

patterns, regardless of the use of high-resolution (low-flux) or high-flux (low-resolution)
instruments, or 242Pu- vs 239Pu-Ga alloys.

The early growth of the lattice parameter of δ-phase Pu-Ga alloys (and other Pu alloys)
with time until a saturation value is reached was first reported by Chebotarev and Utkina
[18]. Since then, several more experimental studies have been reported with consistent
experimental results but distinct mechanistic explanations offered [19, 20, 22]. Ravat et al.
suggest the significant increase of vacancies associated with self-irradiation damage as a
cause. Previous research reports a decrease of lattice parameter in fcc crystals with increase
in vacancy population [71-72].

Freibert et al. [24] offer a much more detailed discussion of interstitial defects, specifically
〈100〉 dumbell interstitials [72], resulting in lattice expansion. This type of defect is shown
to be the most energetically favorable in fcc crystals in general [73-74], and Pu-Ga alloys,
in particular [25, 76-77]. The lattice expansion is implicitly assumed to be
crystallographically isotropic, i.e. that the lattice expansion is one-third of the volume
expansion and offer a relationship between the lattice expansion and the atomic
 Δ𝑎
concentration of interstitials, CD, 𝑎 = 1.6𝐶𝐷 in Pu-Ga alloys [24]. Saturation of the lattice
parameter growth is achieved when fission events create and annihilate the interstitials at
equal rates. The lattice expansion reported herein, consistent in magnitude with previous
work, would require very high interstitial concentrations of roughly CD = 0.002. However,
no plausible reason is given why this type of interstitial would remain in place to
temperatures in excess of room temperature as reported by Chebotarev and Utkina [18] and
observed herein.

While the neutron diffraction data strongly suggests that cooling below 170 K produces a
large increase in dislocation density, it does not afford a cause. The temperature range at
which the broadening is observed is consistent with it being related to large volume change
expected with the transformation to α′ phase.

To summarize, the lattice evolution for a sample that spent long time at ambient conditions,
shows that the ambient self-irradiation damage interacts destructively with the kinetic
evolution of the defects generated by self-irradiation at cryogenic temperatures as they
evolve upon heating back to ambient temperature, resulting in an incomplete recovery of
the lattice. For a sample that spent a short time at ambient conditions, the kinetic evolution
of the self- irradiation defects generated at cryogenic temperatures seems to destroy the
long-range order damage and associated strain accumulated at ambient temperature, the
result being a lattice swelling.

Finally, no incipient peaks associated with the α′ phase are observed in either these neutron
diffraction measurements utilizing 239Pu, nor those of Lawson et al. using 242Pu, enabling
deeper probing of the material.

When we compare these experimental results, it is quite clear that we must consider several
effects, such as self-irradiation/radiation damage at ambient and cryogenic temperatures,
in addition to an emergent but dormant and undetectable martensitic transformation.
