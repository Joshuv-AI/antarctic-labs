Site-targeted proximal complement C3 inhibition, but not C5 blockade, attenuates neurodegeneration in a mouse model of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis: Implications for failed clinical trials
Authors: Moumita Majumder, Davis M Borucki, Nityananda Chowdhury, Khalil Mallah, Stephen Tomlinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplement activation contributes to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) neuropathology, but whether motor neuron degeneration is driven by proximal or terminal activation products remains unresolved. Failed trials of C5 inhibition - ravulizumab (CHAMPION-ALS) and zilucoplan (ATHLEET/HEALEY ALS Trial), and of systemic C3 inhibition (pegcetacoplan, MERIDIAN) underscore this mechanistic gap. We compared two pharmacologically distinct inhibitors in hSOD1G93A mice. CR2Crry blocks complement at C3 and localizes to sites of active deposition. BB5.1, functionally equivalent to ravulizumab, selectively blocks C5a generation and membrane attack complex (MAC) assembly. Treatment began at symptom onset and continued to humane endpoint. Both agents achieved robust target engagement across spinal cord, sciatic nerve, and neuromuscular junction (NMJ). Only CR2Crry extended survival, preserved motor function, and attenuated weight loss. CR2Crry reduced microglial activation and C3 opsonization in the ventral horn, diminished macrophage infiltration, preserved sciatic nerve axonal integrity, and maintained NMJ innervation. BB5.1 failed to modify any functional or neurodegenerative pathology despite confirmed MAC suppression. Proximal C3 activation products, not C5a or MAC, are the dominant drivers of complement-mediated neurodegeneration in the hSOD1G93A model of ALS. Site-targeted C3 inhibition may address the mechanistic limitation underlying recent clinical trial failures and represents a translationally justified therapeutic strategy.Keywords: ALS, Neuroinflammation, C3, Site-targeted complement inhibition, hSOD1G93A

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive and ultimately fatal neurodegenerative disorder characterized by degeneration of both upper and lower motor neurons, resulting in muscle weakness, atrophy and paralysis [1]. Following symptom onset, median survival ranges from 3 to 5 years, depending on disease progression along the neuraxis [2]. Although the majority of ALS cases are sporadic (∼90%), a subset of familial ALS is caused by mutations in the Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene. Among these, the SOD1G93A mutation, resulting from a glycine-to-alanine substitution at codon 93, has been the most widely studied. This mutation confers a toxic gain of function rather than loss of enzymatic activity, leading to misfolded SOD1 aggregation, oxidative stress, and motor neuron degeneration. Transgenic mice overexpressing the human SOD1G93A mutation develop a progressive, motor neuron–specific degenerative phenotype that closely recapitulates the key pathological and clinical features of ALS, including motor dysfunction, gliosis, neuromuscular junction loss, and neuronal death. This well-established model was employed in the present study to examine the therapeutic potential of complement inhibition in ALS.

Complement activation fragments, including C3b/iC3b and C5b-9 (membrane attack complex, MAC), have been detected in the spinal cord, motor cortex, and cerebrospinal fluid of ALS patients, highlighting complement dysregulation as a potential therapeutic target [3]. Based largely on these findings, several clinical trials have been initiated to evaluate systemic complement inhibition in ALS, but in all trials complement inhibition failed to show benefit. A recent phase 3, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of the anti-C5 monoclonal antibody (mAb) ravulizumab in adults with ALS failed to demonstrate significant benefit on functional decline or survival [4]. Similarly, the C5 peptide inhibitor zilucoplan, evaluated in the ATHLEET/HEALEY ALS Platform Trial, was stopped early for futility, demonstrating no meaningful impact on disease progression [5]. In addition to the negative findings from C5-targeting studies, the phase 2 MERIDIAN trial of the C3 inhibitor pegcetacoplan in ALS also failed to demonstrate clinical benefit with no improvement in functional outcomes, survival, respiratory, or muscle strength [6].

Notably, all the approaches described above rely on broad, systemic complement blockade, which may limit efficacy in both the periphery and the central nervous system. In the present study, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of the site-targeted complement inhibitor CR2Crry, which localizes complement inhibitory activity specifically to sites of complement activation. CR2Crry is a ∼70 kDa fusion protein linking a complement receptor 2 (CR2) targeting domain to the complement inhibitor Crry. The CR2 domain binds to iC3b, C3dg, and C3d, long-lived activation fragments of C3 that are covalently deposited at sites of injury and complement activation, including areas of ALS pathology. Crry is a murine structural and functional analog of human CR1 and inhibits all complement pathways at the central C3 activation step.

To directly compare the neuroprotective efficacy of proximal versus terminal complement inhibition, we employed two mechanistically distinct agents targeting different nodes of the cascade. The murine anti-C5 monoclonal antibody BB5.1, which functionally recapitulates the mechanism of the clinically approved C5 inhibitor ravulizumab. The antibody blocks downstream C5 cleavage thereby preventing generation of the anaphylatoxin C5a and assembly of the membrane attack complex (MAC). CR2Crry acts at an earlier and more proximal checkpoint. By targeting C3 at its central amplification step, it simultaneously prevents generation of C3a, which contributes to inflammatory immune cell recruitment, curtails downstream C5 activation, and blocks deposition of C3b/iC3b opsonins that mediate complement-dependent tagging of synapses and neurons for elimination. Critically, CR2Crry localizes to sites of active complement deposition via CR2-mediated binding to C3 activation fragments, conferring spatially restricted inhibition at the precise loci of pathological complement engagement. This experimental approach enables mechanistic dissection of the relative contributions of early activation products versus terminal effector pathways to ALS neurodegeneration and provides a basis for evaluating the therapeutic advantages of site-targeted, upstream complement inhibition in a model of progressive motor neuron disease.

Materials and Methods

Animal model

Wild-type female C57BL/6J mice and male transgenic B6.Cg-Tg(SOD1G93A)1Gur/J mice (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were obtained at 6 weeks of age and acclimatized for 1 week prior to breeding. Mice were paired for mating at a ratio of one male to two females. Animals were housed in temperature- and humidity-controlled rooms on a 12-h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. Pregnant females were provided a high-fat diet as recommended by the institutional veterinarian. All experimental procedures and behavioral tests were conducted during the light phase. Pregnancy and litter checks were performed daily. Mice maintained for survival analysis were monitored regularly for signs of distress, and supportive care was provided as needed. Humane endpoints were defined as a ≥20% loss of body weight or inability to right themselves within 20 s after being placed on their sides whichever occurred first. Following completion of procedures or testing, all animals were returned to the animal housing facility. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-2020-00910-1) at the Medical University of South Carolina.

Study design and treatment

Animal groups used in this study were: 1. Wild-type naïve mice, 2. hSOD1G93A mice with intraperitoneal (IP) PBS treatment (vehicle), 3. hSOD1G93A mice with IP CR2Crry treatment (20 mg/kg) and, 4. hSOD1G93A mice with IP BB5.1 treatment (40 mg/kg). All treatments began at 120 days of age (post-symptomatic) and were administered three times per week. The dose and treatment schedule was chosen based on previously published data [7] for CR2Crry and [8] for BB5.1. For each experimental subset, vehicle and treatment groups consisted of 7 males and 7 females per group (total n = 14) and 4 males and 4 females (total n = 8) animals for wild-type cohort. Breeding pairs and their litters were randomly assigned to treatment conditions to minimize bias. Behavioral and survival cohorts were analyzed separately; animals used for behavioral testing were not included in survival studies to avoid confounding from repeated handling and testing. For survival analysis, dosing continued until animals reached clinical endpoint. Body weight was recorded by an investigator blinded to treatment group every 7 days beginning at 97 days of age (pre-symptomatic stage) and increased to three times per week starting at 125 days of age (early symptomatic stage). Measurements were performed at the same time of day to minimize diurnal variation. Recombinant CR2Crry was prepared and quality controlled as previously described [9,10]. The BB5.1 hybridoma was kindly provided by Dr. John Lambris, University of Pennsylvania, and the mAb purified by standard protein G affinity chromatography.

Euthanasia and tissue collection

For behavioral and histological studies, animals were euthanized at 155 days of age (mid-late disease) by isoflurane anesthesia followed by transcardial perfusion with cold PBS. Spinal cords were collected and postfixed in 4% (wt/vol) PFA at 4 °C for 24 h, and gastrocnemius muscle and sciatic nerve tissues were collected and postfixed in 2% (wt/vol) PFA for 2hr at 4 °C. All tissues were subsequently stored in 30% sucrose at 4 °C until sectioning.

Behavioral tests

Motor coordination, balance, and strength were assessed using the rotarod test [11,12]. To minimize variability related to motivation or motor learning, mice were trained twice per week for one week prior to data acquisition. During training, mice were placed on the rotarod rotating at a constant speed of 8 rpm for a maximum duration of 300 s in two consecutive trials. Baseline data were first collected at age 118 days and performance recorded 2 times per week until 155 days of age. For data collection, the rotarod was accelerated linearly from 4 to 40 rpm over the trial duration and each mouse given three test trials per session, with a maximum time of 300 s per trial. The test was terminated when an animal fell from the rod or clung to it and completed a passive rotation. The average latency to fall from 3 trials, was calculated for each animal [13]. Hindlimb grip strength was evaluated as an important measure of neuromuscular function using a digital grip strength meter (BIOSEB, Vitrolles, France), following established protocols [14] (Wellstone Muscular Dystrophy Center SOP DMD_M.2.2.001). Initial data collection for baseline was at 117 days of age, and thereafter repeated weekly until endpoint (155 days of age). The maximal grip force was recorded across five consecutive trials per animal, with rest between trials to prevent fatigue. The average grip strength was normalized to body weight, and the change from baseline was calculated for each animal as a measure of disease progression. Gait performance was assessed using the CatWalk XT system (Noldus Information Technology, Leesburg, VA, USA) as previously described [15]. Mice were allowed to traverse an enclosed glass walkway illuminated from below, where scattered light from paw contacts was detected by a high-speed camera. Each mouse was required to complete three compliant runs (uninterrupted crossings at consistent speed). For each compliant run, the CatWalk software automatically generated multiple gait parameters for both forelimbs and hindlimbs. The average values from three runs were computed for selected parameters and analyzed at baseline (116 days) and endpoint (151 days) (test data collected on 129, 136, 143 and 151 days of age).

Motor neuron counting

Serial cryosections (20 μm thick) of the lumbar spinal cord (L3–L5) were collected onto charged Superfrost™ Plus slides as described in Ref. [16]. Slides containing serial sections from each animal were rehydrated and subjected to Nissl staining. Sections were incubated in 0.1% cresyl violet solution for 5 min, briefly rinsed in distilled water, and then dehydrated through graded ethanol. Regions of the spinal cord were identified based on the cytoarchitectural distinction between gray and white matter, with motor neurons (MNs) localized within the ventral horn. MNs were quantified from every fourth section spanning the L4–L5 region, following established size and morphological criteria [16,17]. Neurons with a soma diameter >20 μm, a distinct nucleolus, and a characteristic pyramidal morphology were counted. Counts were performed from six serial sections per animal, including both ventral horns, and the average value was obtained for each animal.

Immunofluorescence staining and imaging

For spinal cord immunostaining, serial sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 8.0), followed by blocking in 5% donkey serum in TBS containing 0.4% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature. Gastrocnemius (GA) muscle cryosections (20 μm) were similarly blocked in 5% donkey serum in TBS with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h. Sciatic nerve sections (14 μm) were obtained from paraffin-embedded tissue, deparaffinized, and rehydrated prior to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Sections were then blocked in 3% donkey serum with 2% BSA in TBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h. After blocking, all tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against target proteins (Supplementary Table 1) under gentle agitation. The following day, sections were washed multiple times with TBST and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-, 555-, or 647-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h at room temperature with gentle shaking. Muscle sections were additionally incubated with α-bungarotoxin–Alexa Fluor 594 conjugate to label postsynaptic motor endplate in neuromuscular junction. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 4–5 min. Sections were air-dried overnight and sequentially dehydrated through graded ethanol and xylene (Sigma-Aldrich) before coverslipping. Confocal imaging was performed using Leica STELLARIS 5 and Zeiss LSM 880 microscopes, and brightfield images were acquired using a Keyence BZ-X710 microscope. All the quantification analysis was performed with Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Survival data were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log-rank (Mantel–Cox) testing followed by Bonferroni correction (p < 0.025). Longitudinal analysis of Body weight (up to the age at which ≥80% of animals remained alive), grip strength, and rotarod performance were compared pairwise between vehicle and treatment groups (CR2Crry and BB5.1) using two-way repeated measures (RM) ANOVA with Geissure-Greenhouse correction followed by Sidak’s multiple comparison test. CatWalk gait parameters were analyzed by paired t-tests between timepoints within each group. One way ANOVA were performed with Sidak’s test versus wild type. Histological quantifications were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc and unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction where applicable. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Normality was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Complement inhibition at the level of C3, but not C5, prolongs survival and attenuates body weight loss and hindlimb motor decline in hSOD1G93A mice

To assess the therapeutic impact of proximal versus terminal complement inhibition on disease progression, CR2Crry or the anti-C5 mAb BB5.1 was administered to hSOD1G93A mice beginning at 120 days of age (following clinical symptom onset) and continued until the predefined humane endpoint. CR2Crry treatment significantly prolonged survival relative to vehicle (PBS) in both male and female hSOD1G93A mice. In contrast, BB5.1 did not significantly alter survival in either sex compared with vehicle (Fig. 1a and b), indicating that upstream C3 inhibition, but not terminal pathway blockade, meaningfully influences disease trajectory in this model.

Longitudinal body weight, monitored from 97 days of age with three assessments per week, was expressed as percentage change relative to individual baseline values. CR2Crry significantly attenuated body weight loss in male hSOD1G93A mice relative to vehicle, whereas BB5.1-treated males followed trajectories indistinguishable from vehicle. In females, CR2Crry demonstrated a consistent directional trend toward reduced weight loss that did not reach statistical significance, while BB5.1-treated females remained comparable to vehicle (Fig. 1c and d). Together, these data indicate that C3-targeted complement inhibition mitigates disease-associated cachexia in a sex-influenced manner.

Motor function was evaluated using two standardized behavioral paradigms. Hindlimb grip strength was quantified using a T-bar grip test (five trials per session), with values normalized to body weight and expressed relative to baseline. Motor coordination was assessed via rotarod testing (three trials per session), with latency to fall recorded as the primary outcome. CR2Crry-treated hSOD1G93A mice maintained significantly greater hindlimb grip strength than vehicle-treated controls in both sexes (p < 0.05; Fig. 1e and f). Although grip strength declined progressively across all treatment groups, consistent with the course of motor neuron loss in this model, values remained consistently higher in CR2Crry-treated animals relative to vehicle throughout the monitoring period. Rotarod performance was not significantly altered by either treatment in either sex, though a modest directional improvement was noted in CR2Crry-treated animals (Fig. S1). BB5.1-treated mice showed no improvement in grip strength and performed comparably to, or below, vehicle-treated controls across all motor outcome measures (Fig. 1e and f), further underscoring the limited functional impact of terminal complement pathway inhibition in this model.

CR2Crry preserves gait function in hSOD1G93A mice

CatWalk gait analysis was performed at baseline (120 days, early symptomatic phase) and at disease endpoint (155 days, severe disease) as an exploratory characterization of locomotor decline. Given the absence of survival or functional benefit with BB5.1, gait analyses were restricted to male and female WT, vehicle-treated hSOD1G93A, and CR2Crry-treated hSOD1G93A mice.

Within-group longitudinal comparisons (baseline vs endpoint using paired t-tests) revealed that vehicle-treated hSOD1G93A mice of both sexes exhibited a significant reduction in average walking speed from baseline (120 days, early symptomatic) to endpoint (155 days, severe disease) (Fig. 2a and b). In contrast, mice treated with CR2Crry reflecting inhibition of complement at the level of C3, showed no significant change, indicating that CR2Crry largely preserves walking speed during disease progression. Significant increases in right forepaw stand time were observed in vehicle-treated mice of both sexes, whereas CR2Crry-treated mice showed no significant changes (Fig. 2c and f). Stride length was significantly decreased in vehicle-treated mice of both sexes, CR2Crry-treated males showed no significant change, and CR2Crry-treated females showed a significant decrease from baseline (Fig. 2d and g). Notably, the increase in stand time was not solely attributable to reduced walking speed, as duty cycle (the proportion of the step cycle during which the paw remains in contact with the platform) was also significantly elevated in vehicle-treated animals of both sexes (Fig. 2e and h), CR2Crry-treated males showed no significant change in duty cycle, and CR2Crry-treated females exhibited a significant increase from baseline.

Step sequence analysis using paired t-tests revealed that vehicle-treated mice of both sexes exhibited a significant reduction in the efficient cruciate (CA) pattern by endpoint, accompanied by a redistribution toward the broader, stability-oriented alternate (AB) pattern, reflecting an increased reliance on compensatory gait strategies associated with progressive motor decline. CR2Crry-treated mice showed no significant changes in step patterns from baseline in either sex (Fig. 2i and j).

Endpoint comparisons for continuous gait parameters using one-way ANOVA with pairwise comparisons versus WT showed significantly impaired performance in vehicle-treated mice across all parameters, whereas the magnitude of impairment was significantly attenuated in CR2Crry-treated mice. All groups performed similarly at baseline (p > 0.05). Descriptively, comparable directional trends were observed in both males and females across parameters (Fig. 2a–h).

Analysis of hindlimb-specific gait parameters mirrored these findings (Fig. S2a–f). Within-group longitudinal comparisons demonstrated significant deterioration in stand time, stride length, and duty cycle in vehicle-treated mice of both sexes, whereas CR2Crry-treated mice showed no significant changes from baseline across these measures, collectively reinforcing the broad locomotor protection conferred by proximal complement inhibition.

CR2Crry attenuates spinal motor neuron loss in hSOD1G93A mice

As with gait analysis, histopathological and immunofluorescence analyses were focused on CR2Crry-treated cohorts, given the absence of functional or survival benefit observed with BB5.1 in behavioral assessments. Selected analyses were nonetheless performed in BB5.1-treated animals to confirm engagement of the terminal complement pathway target, and these data are presented in Fig. S4 and described below. Two-way ANOVA revealed no significant sex × treatment interaction across histopathological endpoints (p > 0.05), indicating that treatment responses were comparable between male and female hSOD1G93A mice. Data were therefore pooled across sexes for subsequent analyses, although sex stratified CR2Crry data are provided in Fig. S3.

Motor neuron (MN) loss is a defining pathological hallmark of ALS. To evaluate the effect of proximal complement inhibition on MN survival, we quantified surviving MN cell bodies in the ventral horn of the lumbar spinal cord (L4–L5) in CR2Crry-treated and vehicle-treated hSOD1G93A mice, with WT animals included as non-transgenic controls. At disease endpoint (155 days), CR2Crry-treated hSOD1G93A mice retained significantly more surviving MNs per section than vehicle-treated counterparts (6.3 ± 0.39 versus 3.9 ± 0.34; p < 0.05; Fig. 3a and b), representing an approximately 60% increase in MN survival. These data indicate that site-targeted C3 inhibition substantially attenuates the progressive MN loss that underlies functional decline in this model.

CR2Crry reduces spinal cord C3 deposition and microglial activation in hSOD1G93A mice

C3 is upregulated and deposited in the spinal cord during late-stage disease in hSOD1G93A mice [14], where it promotes neuroinflammatory responses and motor neuron degeneration through mechanisms that include microglial activation and complement-mediated opsonization [17, 18]. To determine whether CR2Crry treatment modulates complement deposition and microglial responses, we examined C3 immunoreactivity and Iba1+ microglial area in the ventral horn of the lumbar spinal cord (L4–L5) in hSOD1G93A mice treated with CR2Crry or vehicle, with WT animals serving as non-transgenic controls. CR2Crry treatment markedly reduced C3 immunoreactivity in the ventral horn relative to vehicle-treated hSOD1G93A mice (Fig. 4a and b), confirming effective target engagement at the primary site of motor neuron degeneration. Concordantly, Iba1+ microglial area was significantly reduced in CR2Crry-treated animals compared with vehicle controls (Fig. 4c and d), indicating that attenuation of proximal complement activation suppresses the microglial response within the degenerating ventral horn. Together, these findings establish that site-targeted C3 inhibition reduces both complement deposition and complement-driven neuroinflammation in the spinal cord of hSOD1G93A mice.

CR2Crry reduces complement deposition, attenuates macrophage infiltration, and preserves axonal integrity in the sciatic nerve of hSOD1G93A mice

Axonal degeneration and peripheral neuroinflammation in the sciatic nerve emerge early during the symptomatic stage in hSOD1G93A mice and contribute to impaired motor signal transmission to the hindlimbs [18,19]. To determine whether CR2Crry treatment mitigates peripheral neuropathological changes, we assessed C3 deposition, axonal integrity and macrophage responses in transverse-sections of the sciatic nerve.

CR2Crry treatment significantly reduced C3 deposition in the sciatic nerve relative vehicle-treated mice, demonstrating that site-targeted complement inhibition extends to peripheral nerve tissue (Fig. 5a and b). Immunostaining for neurofilament heavy chain (NF200) revealed significantly greater axonal density in CR2Crry-treated animals compared with vehicle controls, with values approaching those of age-matched WT mice (Fig. 5c and d), indicating substantial preservation of peripheral axonal architecture. In parallel, quantitative analysis of CD68 immunoreactivity, a marker of phagocytically activated macrophage-lineage cells, demonstrated a significant reduction in CD68+ cell accumulation in CR2Crry-treated sciatic nerves relative to vehicle (Fig. 5c–e). Given that C3b/iC3b deposition facilitates complement-mediated opsonization and phagocytic recognition, the reduction in CD68+ infiltration is consistent with decreased complement-mediated immune cell engagement following proximal C3 inhibition.

CR2Crry preserves neuromuscular junction integrity and reduces C3 deposition at the motor endplate in hSOD1G93A mice

NMJ denervation precedes and quantitatively exceeds motor neuron loss in hSOD1G93A mice, and is recognized as an early and principal driver of the aggressive functional decline characteristic of this model [30, 31]. To determine whether CR2Crry treatment mitigates distal neuromuscular pathology, we examined NMJ integrity and C3 deposition at the motor endplate in the gastrocnemius muscle. NMJ innervation status was evaluated by dual immunolabeling for neurofilament heavy chain and synaptic vesicle protein 2 (SV2) as presynaptic markers, combined with α-bungarotoxin (α-BTX) labeling of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) to delineate postsynaptic endplates. NMJs were classified as fully innervated when presynaptic and postsynaptic marker overlap exceeded 80%, and as denervated when overlap fell below 50%; partially innervated junctions (50–80% overlap) were recorded separately. The percentage of fully innervated NMJs was quantified across multiple randomly selected fields per animal and served as the primary outcome measure of neuromuscular integrity. CR2Crry treatment resulted in marked preservation of NMJ integrity, with treated hSOD1G93A mice exhibiting a higher proportion of innervated NMJs (48.82 ± 10.1%) compared with vehicle-treated animals (7.72 ± 3.5%) (Fig. 6a and b). In addition, C3 deposition at the motor endplate was markedly reduced following CR2Crry treatment (Fig. 6a and c). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that site-targeted complement inhibition at the level of C3 preserves distal neuromuscular integrity in hSOD1G93A mice, extending the neuroprotective profile of CR2Crry from the spinal cord and peripheral nerve to the motor endplate.

BB5.1 achieves robust MAC suppression across central and peripheral nervous system compartments without influencing disease progression in hSOD1G93A mice

Given the absence of functional or survival benefit with BB5.1, we sought to confirm that the lack of efficacy reflected a genuine disconnect between terminal pathway inhibition and disease modification, rather than inadequate target engagement. MAC immunoreactivity was therefore assessed at three principal sites of ALS neuropathology, namely the lumbar spinal cord ventral horn, sciatic nerve, and NMJ, in BB5.1-treated and vehicle-treated hSOD1G93A mice. BB5.1 treatment significantly reduced MAC deposition across all tissues examined (Fig. S4a–f), confirming robust pharmacodynamic engagement of the terminal complement pathway at anatomically relevant sites of motor neuron disease. To evaluate whether terminal complement inhibition afforded structural neuroprotection despite the absence of functional benefit, we further assessed motor neuron survival, axonal integrity (NF200 immunoreactivity), and CD68+ inflammatory cell accumulation in BB5.1-treated mice. Consistent with the behavioral findings, BB5.1 treatment did not result in significant differences in motor neuron counts, axonal density, or CD68+ cell infiltration compared with vehicle-treated hSOD1G93A mice (Fig. S5a–e). Despite this unambiguous target engagement, BB5.1-treated mice demonstrated no significant benefit in structural neuroprotection, survival, body weight trajectory, hindlimb grip strength, or gait parameters relative to vehicle-treated controls. The dissociation between effective MAC suppression and the absence of measurable structural, functional, or survival benefit provides compelling pharmacological evidence that terminal complement effector mechanisms alone are insufficient to drive disease modification in this model, and that the neuroprotective effects of complement inhibition in this model of ALS are dependent on upstream blockade of C3 activation and its downstream consequences, including C3a generation, opsonization, and immune cell recruitment.

Discussion

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder with no effective cure, and its heterogeneous and multifactorial pathophysiology continues to impede therapeutic development. Genetic susceptibility, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, glutamate excitotoxicity, impaired neurotrophic signaling, neuroinflammation, and environmental triggers all contribute to a complex disease landscape [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Numerous preclinical studies have targeted one or more of these pathways, but translation into successful clinical trials has largely failed [24,25]. Increasing evidence identifies complement activation as a central and early mediator of neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in ALS. The present study demonstrates that site-targeted inhibition of C3 activation with CR2Crry significantly improves multiple disease outcomes in hSOD1G93A mice, providing mechanistic and translational insight into the role of complement in ALS pathogenesis, and a framework for interpreting the failure of complement-directed clinical trials to date.

Several complement inhibitors have entered or are entering clinical trials for ALS, targeting distinct points within the cascade: terminal pathway blockade at C5 (ravulizumab, CHAMPION-ALS; zilucoplan, ATHLEET/HEALEY ALS Platform Trial), classical pathway inhibition at C1q (ANX005, ongoing), and central amplification blockade at C3 (pegcetacoplan, MERIDIAN). The two C5/terminal pathway trials failed to demonstrate meaningful clinical benefit, and MERIDIAN, targeting the upstream C3 node, also failed to show efficacy. Each of these agents depends on sustained systemic complement suppression, which introduces pharmacokinetic and safety challenges that may fundamentally limit their utility in ALS. Complement performs vital homeostatic roles in host defense, debris clearance, neural maintenance, and repair. Systemic inhibition risks disrupting these beneficial functions as well as increases infection susceptibility [26,27]. Critically, and as discussed further below, limited penetration and retention of systemically administered biologics within the affected neural compartments of ALS, namely the ventral spinal cord, distal peripheral axons, and neuromuscular junction, may represent the principal constraint on efficacy, irrespective of the pathway targeted. In contrast, the CR2Crry fusion protein provides localized, site-targeted complement inhibition by binding C3 activation fragments (iC3b, C3d, C3dg) deposited at sites of active pathology, delivering the Crry complement inhibitory domain directly to injured tissue. Previous work from our group demonstrated that this targeting strategy achieves high local bioavailability while reducing systemic dosing requirements by more than 20-fold compared with conventional administration of untargeted complement inhibitors and normalizes serum complement activity within 2 h of systemic administration of untargeted complement inhibitors [9,10,28]. Importantly, serum complement activity normalized within 2 h after systemic administration, supporting a favorable balance between local target engagement and limited systemic complement suppression. Consistent with this targeting mechanism, prior studies have shown minimal effects of CR2Crry in healthy wild-type animals, where limited complement activation results in minimal availability of C3 activation fragments for CR2-mediated localization. Biodistribution studies further demonstrated preferential accumulation of CR2Crry at sites of complement activation, with minimal localization in uninjured tissues [29]. An additional consideration for long-term therapeutic application of a fusion protein-based complement inhibitor is potential immunogenicity. However, previous studies from our group demonstrated that repeated administration of CR2Crry (seven injections) did not elicit detectable anti-CR2Crry IgM or IgG antibody responses, providing preliminary support for limited immunogenicity with this targeted approach [30]. Consistent with these pharmacologic advantages, CR2Crry treatment in hSOD1G93A mice produced prolonged survival, reduced body weight loss, and attenuated motor decline without evident adverse effects.

Behavioral analyses revealed broadly neuroprotective effects of CR2Crry across both sexes, with subtle sex-specific patterns in functional decline. Vehicle-treated males exhibited greater losses in body weight and hindlimb grip strength, while females showed more prominent rotarod and gait deterioration. CR2Crry significantly preserved body weight, hindlimb strength, and gait in males; in females, trends toward reduced body weight loss were consistent but did not reach statistical significance. These sex-differentiated responses may reflect estrogen-mediated neuroprotection [31] and differential female susceptibility to NMJ denervation via maladaptive axonal sprouting [32], though dedicated investigation with formal sex-as-biological-variable analyses is warranted.

CatWalk gait analysis revealed progressive locomotor deterioration in vehicle-treated hSOD1G93A mice, characterized by reduced walking speed and stride length alongside increased stand time and duty cycle, reflecting compensatory neuromuscular adaptation to declining motor output. Vehicle-treated animals also shifted toward the alternate (AB) step sequence, indicative of progressive weakness and loss of interlimb coordination. CR2Crry-treated mice largely retained the cruciate (CA) gait pattern and relative preservation of continuous gait parameters, indicating that proximal complement inhibition mitigates the neuromuscular deterioration underlying locomotor decline.

On the other hand, BB5.1 failed to improve survival, body weight, hindlimb strength, or gait despite confirmed MAC suppression across spinal cord, sciatic nerve, and NMJ compartments. Furthermore, BB5.1 treatment did not alter motor neuron preservation, axonal integrity, or CD68+ inflammatory cell accumulation compared with vehicle-treated mice. This dissociation between robust target engagement and complete absence of disease modification argues against a primary pathogenic role for terminal complement effectors in this model, and implicates proximal activation products, C3a and C3-mediated opsonization and immune cell recruitment, as the principal drivers of complement-dependent neurodegeneration in ALS. The convergent failure of ravulizumab and zilucoplan in human trials, and of BB5.1 in the present model, provides strong and internally consistent evidence that C5 and the MAC are not therapeutically relevant targets in ALS regardless of the degree of target engagement achieved.

Current evidence indicates that complement activation contributes to neuroinflammatory processes in ALS, with microglial activation and proliferation strongly correlated with disease progression and motor neuron loss [33]. In the present study, we observed marked C3 deposition in the spinal cord, sciatic nerve, and NMJ of hSOD1G93A mice, accompanied by increased CD68+ cell number and Iba1+ immunoreactivity. CR2Crry treatment effectively reduced C3 deposition and microglial activation and was associated with preservation of motor neurons. These findings parallel reports implicating C3 signaling in neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration across multiple disease contexts [[34], [35], [36], [37]] and support a mechanistic link between complement dysregulation and progressive motor neuron pathology.

A central question raised by the MERIDIAN trial is why site-targeted, but not systemic, C3 inhibition provides protection against ALS disease progression. This distinction is mechanistically important and should not be interpreted as evidence against C3 as a therapeutic target. Rather, it may reflect the importance of where complement inhibition is delivered, not merely which node is blocked. In ALS, complement activation is highly compartmentalized, with early C3 deposition occurring at the NMJ, distal axons, and ventral spinal cord, regions that are poorly accessible to circulating biologics due to the blood-brain and blood-nerve barriers [3,38]. Systemically administered C3 inhibitors such as pegcetacoplan may achieve adequate suppression of circulating complement while providing suboptimal drug exposure within these peripheral synaptic and parenchymal compartments, leaving locally produced and deposited complement, generated by resident microglia, astrocytes, Schwann cells, and infiltrating macrophages, largely unaddressed. CR2Crry circumvents this limitation by actively accumulating at sites of C3 fragment deposition, achieving sustained local complement regulation that suppresses synaptic opsonization, microglial and Schwann cell-mediated pruning, and complement-driven motor terminal injury at its source. Furthermore, systemic C3 depletion broadly impairs physiologic complement functions required for debris clearance, neuronal support, and host defense, functions that may be actively beneficial in the ALS disease environment. On the other hand, site-targeted inhibition preserves systemic complement homeostasis while selectively modulating pathologic compartment-specific activation. Taken together, the failure of MERIDIAN should not necessarily discourage further pursuit of C3-directed strategies in ALS, but rather underscores that spatially confined complement modulation, not systemic suppression, may be the prerequisite for therapeutic efficacy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although we examined microglial responses, the contribution of astrocytic activation and complement-mediated astroglial crosstalk was not directly assessed and warrants further investigation. Sex-specific cohort sizes were modest, and larger studies with formal interaction analyses will be necessary to definitively characterize sex-dependent differences in treatment response. Because CR2Crry was administered intraperitoneally, central nervous system penetration may be limited; thus, the protective effects observed here may predominantly reflect actions within the peripheral nervous system and lower motor neurons. Future studies employing intrathecal or intracerebroventricular delivery could clarify whether direct CNS complement inhibition provides additional benefit to upper motor neuron populations.

Collectively, these findings identify proximal complement activation at C3 as a key pathological driver in ALS, and demonstrate that site-targeted inhibition with CR2Crry attenuates neuroinflammation, preserves neuromuscular integrity, and extends survival in hSOD1G93A mice. C3d-targeted inhibitors currently under evaluation by Q32 Bio and Akebia Therapeutics, although structurally distinct from CR2Crry, share the fundamental principle of site-directed complement modulation and collectively support the therapeutic feasibility of this approach in human disease. The present data provide mechanistic and preclinical rationale for prioritizing proximal, site-targeted rather than systemic complement inhibition in the design of next-generation therapeutics for ALS and potentially other neurodegenerative conditions in which compartment-restricted complement-mediated neuroinflammation drives disease progression.
