Nodular Fasciitis of the Piriformis Muscle Initially Concerning for Metastasis in a Patient With Previous History of Colon Adenocarcinoma: A Case Report
Authors: Ruchama Charles, Debajyoti Saha, Julia Jennings, Ali Akalin, George Watts, Mathew J Most, Shashank Kaushik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaseWe report a case of nodular fasciitis (NF) of the hip in a patient with a history of colon adenocarcinoma, concerning for metastasis. PET/CT showed a hypermetabolic lesion, and MRI revealed a well‐defined, enhancing intramuscular mass. Histology identified fascicles of spindle or stellate cells with ovoid nuclei in myxoid and collagenous stroma with sparse inflammatory cells. On immunohistochemistry, the spindle cells expressed smooth muscle actin. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) demonstrated ubiquitin‐specific protease 6 (USP‐6) gene rearrangement, confirming the diagnosis.ConclusionNF is a benign, rapidly growing soft tissue lesion mimicking malignancy. Surgical excision is curative. Accurate diagnosis can prevent unnecessary interventions.Keywords: case report, nodular fasciitis, peri-hip mass

1. Introduction

Nodular fasciitis (NF) is a self‐limiting benign soft tissue mass, microscopically consisting of rapidly growing fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, that typically occurs within the fascia and subcutaneous tissue [1]. It shares similar features to malignant tumors, such as rapid growth, high cellularity, and increased mitotic activity, often leading to misdiagnosis [2]. Here, we report a rare case of NF of the hip in a patient with a history of colon adenocarcinoma. In this context, the presentation raised concerns regarding the possibility of new metastasis of his adenocarcinoma, warranting timely and meticulous evaluation.

2. Case Report

A 68‐year‐old male presented with an asymptomatic right hip mass incidentally identified on imaging. The patient has a history of a T2 N0 M0 adenocarcinoma of the left colon, diagnosed 2 years prior to presentation. He was treated with a left hemicolectomy. On routine follow‐up, he was found to have a rising carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA); as such, a computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was performed. Imaging was notable for a soft tissue mass within the right piriformis muscle, which, in retrospect, was larger compared with a CT scan 4 months prior. The patient underwent percutaneous image guided needle biopsy twice, both of which were nondiagnostic. The initial biopsy revealed skeletal muscle and scant fibroadipose tissue. The second biopsy showed skeletal muscle and fibrous tissue with fibroblasts/myofibroblasts, but insufficient cells were present for diagnosis. PET/CT scan was performed, demonstrating intense fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake within the hip mass, indicating hypermetabolic activity, further concerning for metastatic disease (Figure 1).

Intense FDG uptake within right piriformis soft tissue mass, which measures approximately 1.8 cm with a standardized uptake value (SUV) max equal to 7.5, on the axial fused FDG‐PET/CT image.

Subsequently, an MRI of the right hip was performed and was notable for a well‐defined 1.7 × 1.4 × 2.1 cm mass within the muscle belly of the right piriformis. The mass was isointense to muscle on T1‐weighted images, heterogeneously hyperintense on T2‐weighted sequences, and demonstrated intense enhancement on the post contrast T1‐weighted fat saturated image (Figure 2). The patient was subsequently referred to an orthopedic oncologist.

MRI of the right hip with a well‐defined lesion in the piriformis muscle. (a) Lesion is isointense to muscle on axial T1‐weighted image, (b) heterogeneous in signal on T2‐weighted image, and (c) demonstrates intense enhancement on the postcontrast T1‐weighted fat‐saturated image.

During the visit with the orthopedic oncologist, the patient denied hip pain, and gait was unremarkable. The patient denied fevers, chills, night sweats, or unintentional weight loss. He had full painless range of motion of the right hip. There was no palpable mass or lymphadenopathy of the right lower extremity noted. Plain radiographs of the right hip obtained in the clinic demonstrated no bony lesions or evidence of a soft tissue mass.

The case was reviewed at a multidisciplinary musculoskeletal tumor board. Based on the MRI characteristics previously mentioned, the differential diagnosis included nerve sheath tumor, malignancy secondary to metastasis, or new primary malignancy. The well‐defined margins and intramuscular location suggested peripheral nerve sheath tumor; however, the heterogeneous T2 hyperintensity and avid contrast enhancement were nonspecific and could also be seen in soft tissue sarcomas or metastatic disease. Given the patient′s history of colon adenocarcinoma, rising CEA levels, interval growth of the lesion, and intense FDG uptake on PET/CT, metastatic disease remained a significant concern. Given two nondiagnostic image‐guided needle biopsies, the recommendation was to proceed with incisional biopsy and potential excision of the mass based on results of intraoperative frozen section pathology. After discussion regarding risks, benefits, and alternatives, patient consent was obtained for the procedure.

As the mass was localized posterior to the hip joint, adjacent to the piriformis muscle on MRI, a standard approach to the hip joint was performed. A 10‐cm curved incision was made over the posterior one third of the proximal femur and greater trochanter. Dissection was performed through subcutaneous tissue with the electrocautery, coming down to the iliotibial band and fascia overlying the gluteus maximus. Fascia was split in line with the skin incision. Posteriorly, the gluteus maximus fibers were split with the electrocautery, maintaining hemostasis. The leg was then gently internally rotated, and the foot was placed on a padded mayo stand. Retractors were placed exposing the short external rotators of the hip. A wide‐hooked Hibbs was placed under the gluteus medius, which was retracted anteriorly. The piriformis muscle was identified. The mass was just deep to the short external rotator muscles and superficial to the posterior hip capsule. The muscle fibers were split, exposing an encapsulated white, firm, and rubbery lesion. Using a scalpel, a small portion was excised for the frozen section. This was reviewed with the pathologist and showed a bland fibrous spindle cell lesion without overt malignant features. There was no evidence of metastatic adenocarcinoma. The mass was well circumscribed and already surgically exposed; therefore, a marginal excision was performed to obtain adequate tissue for definitive histologic analysis. The mass was excised using forceps and scissors. The mass was somewhat adherent to the underlying hip capsule but was ultimately freed up from surrounding tissue. Once adequate hemostasis was achieved, the wound was closed in a routine layered fashion. The patient tolerated the procedure well and the case concluded uneventfully.

Final histology revealed a well‐circumscribed proliferation of spindle and stellate cells in a myxoid‐to‐collagenous stroma on hematoxylin and eosin staining. There were no areas of cystic degeneration, and mitotic figures were not readily identifiable (Figure 3). There were also numerous vascular channels within the lesion on histologic examination. Given the patient′s history of colon adenocarcinoma and the presence of numerous vascular channels on histology, a broad differential diagnosis was considered. This included metastatic poorly differentiated colon carcinoma and atypical vascular proliferations, including angiosarcoma. NF was also considered given the intense FDG uptake on PET‐CT. In this case, immunohistochemical stains were performed to help exclude metastatic carcinoma and atypical vascular proliferation. By immunohistochemistry, the lesional spindle cells were positive for SMA and negative for Cytokeratin OSCAR, Cytokeratin 20, desmin, ERG, CD31, CD34, c‐MYC, and P53. In addition, break‐apart fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) testing was performed, which revealed rearrangement of ubiquitin specific protease‐6 (USP‐6) gene. Based on the above findings, the diagnosis of NF was made.

One year after the tumor excision, he continues to walk normally, with no pain in or around his hip. Follow‐up CT and PET/CT scans, obtained for surveillance of his colon adenocarcinoma, have continued to show no evidence of residual or recurrent soft tissue mass or hypermetabolic FDG activity in the right hip region. 3. Discussion

NF is a benign tumor characterized by rapid growth and localized proliferation of myofibroblasts. It can be subcutaneous, intramuscular or fascial [3]. The etiology of NF remains unclear, but it is thought to arise from a reactive or inflammatory process triggering the proliferation of myofibroblasts [4]. It often presents as a solitary, well‐circumscribed nodule commonly found in the upper extremities, trunk, lower extremities, or head and neck region [5]. Although it can share similarities to malignant lesions, both radiographically and clinically, it is self‐limiting and does not metastasize [6, 7]. Accurate diagnosis is crucial to avoid unnecessary interventions and guide appropriate management. Workup involves a combination of clinical evaluation, imaging studies, and histopathological examination, with definitive diagnosis relying on the latter. Although this patient had no clinical features suggestive of malignancy, his history of colon cancer, rising CEA levels, and the presence of a new mass on imaging warranted further evaluation.

During the evaluation, imaging plays a crucial role. MRI reveals a well‐defined mass. Signal intensity may vary depending on histological composition. On T1‐weighted imaging, when compared with muscle, NF often appears isointense to hypointense, whereas T2‐weighted images show hyperintensity due to the myxoid stroma or cellular components [8, 9]. On PET/CT, there may be increased FDG uptake due to hypermetabolic activity, which can lead to misinterpretation as malignancy. This can be explained by its high mitotic activity, rapid growth, and rich cellularity, which can also be seen in malignancy [10]. PET/CT alone should not be relied upon to make a definitive diagnosis of NF due to imaging characteristics similar to malignant soft tissue tumors. Though PET/CT provides information about metabolic activity and anatomical location, biopsy is needed to provide histopathologic characteristics essential for differentiating NF from malignant tumors. In this case, prior to this patient′s excisional biopsy in the operating room, he underwent two image‐guided needle biopsies, both of which were nondiagnostic. Potential limitations to obtaining a diagnostic sample in this case include the small size of the mass (~2 cm), the lesion′s deep location within the hip region, and the firm, fibrous consistency of NF, which may make needle penetration difficult. In addition, due to the histologic heterogeneity of NF, a limited sample may not capture the full spectrum of features required for confident diagnosis.

Histologically, NF is characterized by the proliferation of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts arranged in short, intersecting fascicles within a myxoid to collagenous stroma with varying amounts of chronic inflammatory cells and extravasated red blood cells. Mitotic activity is often observed, though usually not atypical, which helps distinguish NF from sarcomas. Pleomorphism is also absent [11, 12].

In addition to histology, immunohistochemical staining aids in the diagnosis of NF, particularly distinguishing it from other soft tissue tumors. Smooth muscle actin is frequently positive, reflecting the myofibroblastic differentiation of NF. Vimentin is also positive, as it is a marker for mesenchymal cells [13, 14]. Most notably, rearrangement of the USP‐6 gene is considered a key molecular finding, confirming its benign nature and reactive proliferation rather than neoplastic transformation [15, 16].

The treatment of choice for NF is complete surgical excision. The lesion is typically well circumscribed and can be removed entirely via marginal resection, with recurrence being rare following complete excision. In cases where the lesion is incompletely excised or when surgery is contraindicated, spontaneous regression has been documented, suggesting that conservative management can be a viable approach [16]. Given the benign nature of NF, chemotherapy or radiation therapy is not indicated.

The case is notable for the location of the lesion within the piriformis muscle, a deep structure in the posterior hip region where NF is rarely reported. NF most commonly arises within the subcutaneous tissues of the upper extremities, whereas involvement of the deep or perihip musculature is uncommon. Only a limited number of cases describing NF in the hip region have been reported in the literature. Reported presentations include intra‐articular NF of the hip joint [17], perifascial lesion adjacent to the tensor fasciae latae [18], an unusual variant, intravascular fasciitis, involving vessels near the hip joint initially misdiagnosed as low‐grade fibromyxoid sarcoma [19], and intramuscular NF involving the gluteal musculature with neural involvement provisionally diagnosed as a soft tissue sarcoma [20] (Table 1). Of these mentioned reports, only one case was treated conservatively with subsequent partial regression of the lesion [18], whereas the others were treated with surgical excision. Collectively, these reports emphasize the rarity of NF in deep or perihip locations. This presents unique diagnostic challenges because they are often not palpable on physical examination and may only be detected incidentally on imaging. In addition, the deep location adjacent to critical neurovascular structures can broaden the differential diagnosis to include nerve sheath tumors, metastatic disease, and primary soft tissue sarcomas. Awareness of these rare anatomical presentations is therefore important, particularly in patients with a history of malignancy, to ensure appropriate diagnostic evaluation and avoid unnecessary treatment.

4. Conclusion

In conclusion, NF is a benign self‐limiting lesion that can mimic more aggressive soft tissue tumors both clinically and radiologically. Early and accurate differentiation from malignant tumors prevents unnecessary aggressive treatments. In this case, initial concern for metastatic colon adenocarcinoma was driven by interval lesion growth, rising tumor marker (CEA), and intense FDG uptake on PET/CT. Accurate diagnosis required a combination of imaging, histology, and immunohistochemical markers, particularly SMA and vimentin, and demonstration of USP‐6 rearrangement by FISH. Repeated nondiagnostic needle biopsies necessitated surgical biopsy and excision, which allowed for definitive diagnosis of NF.
