Characteristic Operators and Spectral Properties of Periodic Evolutionary Systems
Bram Lentjes, Babette A. J. de Wolff

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce the notion of a characteristic operator for closable linear operators and explore their connected spectral properties via equivalence. Additionally, we develop an explicit scheme for constructing characteristic operators for a broad class of closable linear operators which are commonly encountered in periodic evolution equations. Our findings are illustrated through examples involving classical delay differential equations, delay differential equations with infinite delay and mixed functional differential equations. Notably, we resolve an open problem concerning the discrete spectral structure of the Floquet exponents for this latter class of differential equations. This work can be regarded as a natural and significant extension of the powerful framework developed by Kaashoek and Verduyn Lunel [ 40 ] on characteristic matrices and spectral properties induced by autonomous evolution equations.



{keywords}

characteristic operators, closable linear operators, spectral theory, equivalences, periodic evolution equations.

Introduction

Determining the stability of an invariant set is a fundamental task in the field of dynamical systems. Nowadays, various methods are available to tackle this problem, but spectral theory offers a particularly powerful approach. For example, consider the autonomous ordinary differential equation

\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t)),

(ODE)

where
x(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
f:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
denotes a sufficiently smooth vector field. Let
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be an equilibrium of (
ODE
) and recall from the classical Lyapunov stability theorem
[
51
]
, also known as the principle of linearized stability, that the stability of
\overline{x}
is largely determined by the location of the spectrum
\sigma(A)
of the matrix
A=Df(\overline{x})
in
\mathbb{C}
. Now, let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a
T
-periodic solution of (
ODE
) for some (minimal) period
T>0
, and consider the (limit) cycle
\Gamma=\gamma(\mathbb{R})
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. At first glance, one would expect that the stability of
\Gamma
is now somehow determined by the family of spectra
\{\sigma(A(t)):t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
A(t)=Df(\gamma(t))
. However, the simple counterexamples
[
9
,
69
]
illustrate that in general no principle of linearized stability holds in terms of
\sigma(A(t))
. Instead, one can compute the spectrum
\sigma(U(T,0))
of the monodromy matrix
U(T,0)
, where
U
denotes the fundamental matrix solution of the linearization of (
ODE
) around
\Gamma
. A
Floquet multiplier

\lambda\in\sigma(U(T,0))
is of the form
\lambda=e^{\sigma T}
, where
\sigma
is called a
Floquet exponent
, and it is well-known that the location of the Floquet exponents in
\mathbb{C}
largely determines the stability of
\Gamma
, analogous to the principle of linearized stability for equilibria
[
28
]
. Although this approach provides a satisfying answer for stability in terms of spectra, one still has to compute the solution operator
U
, which is often not explicitly available. In contrast, the stability of the equilibrium
\overline{x}
could be directly determined in terms of the known matrix
A
. To address this issue, a recent observation in
[
49
]
, obtained by recasting a result of Iooss (and Adelmeyer)
[
36
,
37
]
in a functional analytic framework, introduces the closed linear operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq C_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})\to C_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
defined by

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n}),\qquad(\mathcal{A}\varphi)(t)=A(t)\varphi(t)-\dot{\varphi}(t).

Here,
C_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
denotes the Banach space of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
-valued
T
-periodic functions on
\mathbb{R}
equipped with the supremum norm, and the dot indicates differentiation with respect to
t
. With this formulation, one obtains the important characterization

\sigma(\mathcal{A})=\sigma_{p}(\mathcal{A})=\{\sigma\in\mathbb{C}:\sigma\text{ is a Floquet exponent}\},

(1)

where
\sigma_{p}(\mathcal{A})
denotes the point spectrum of
\mathcal{A}
. Moreover, spectral properties of
\mathcal{A}
have been fundamental in the study of codimension one
[
70
]
and two
[
11
,
10
]
bifurcations of limit cycles in ODEs. For example, the (generalized) eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}
associated with the center subspace form a
T
-periodic basis along
\Gamma
, which in turn provides a natural coordinate system on the nearby periodic center manifold, see
[
36
,
37
,
50
,
44
]
for more information. Explicit computational formulas for the critical normal form coefficients are expressed in these
T
-periodic (generalized) eigenfunctions and determine the nature and direction (degenerate, subcritical or supercritical) of these bifurcations. These formulas are implemented in several numerical bifurcation analysis software packages such as
MatCont

[
14
,
13
]
and
BifurcationKit

[
66
]
, and are widely used by researchers across a broad range of scientific disciplines.

For infinite-dimensional dynamical systems, determining the stability of an invariant set is in general more challenging due to its infinite-dimensional nature. For certain subclasses, it is possible to make a dimension reduction so that the stability of an invariant set can be determined more easily. For example, consider the autonomous delay differential equation

\dot{x}(t)=F(x_{t}),\quad t\geq 0,

(DDE)

where
x(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
x_{t}\in X\coloneqq C([-h,0],\mathbb{C}^{n})
represents the
history
of the unknown
x
at time
t
defined by
x_{t}(\theta)\coloneqq x(t+\theta)
for all
\theta\in[-h,0]
. Here,
0<h<\infty
denotes the upper bound of (finite) delays and the infinite-dimensional
state space

X
becomes a Banach space when equipped with the supremum norm. To study the stability of an equilibrium
\overline{x}\in X
of (
DDE
), we are interested in the stability of the trivial solution of the linear DDE

\dot{y}(t)=Ly_{t},\quad t\geq 0,

(LDDE)

where
L=DF(\overline{x}):X\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a bounded linear operator. The solutions of (
LDDE
) are generated by a strongly continuous semigroup on
X
whose (infinitesimal) generator
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq X\to X
reads

\mathcal{D}(A)=\{\varphi\in X:\varphi^{\prime}\in X,\ \varphi^{\prime}(0)=L\varphi\},\quad A\varphi=\varphi^{\prime},

see
[
16
,
27
,
17
]
for more information. From the same references, it follows that
A
is a closed linear operator whose spectrum
\sigma(A)
largely determines the stability of
\overline{x}
, ensuring that the principle of linearized stability holds for (
LDDE
). Computing
\sigma(A)
may seem tedious at first, but Kaashoek and Verduyn Lunel provided in
[
40
]
a powerful framework to reduce the rather difficult spectral problem from the infinite-dimensional state space
C([-h,0],\mathbb{C}^{n})
towards a much simpler problem in its finite-dimensional codomain
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. In particular, they construct an equivalence after extension between the generator
A
and the holomorphic matrix-valued function
\Delta:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
with action

\Delta(z)q=zq-L[\theta\mapsto e^{z\theta}q],\quad\forall q\in\mathbb{C}^{n},

(2)

of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\Delta(z)&0\\
0&I_{X}\end{pmatrix}=F(z)\begin{pmatrix}I_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}&0\\
0&zI-A\end{pmatrix}E(z),\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}.

(3)

Here,
\Delta
can be naturally obtained by substituting eigensolutions of the form
t\mapsto e^{zt}q
into (
LDDE
), and
E
and
F
are holomorphic operator-valued functions whose values are bounded and bijective mappings between suitable Banach spaces. A direct consequence of (
3
) is that
\sigma(A)
consists solely of isolated eigenvalues of finite type and satisfies

\sigma(A)=\sigma_{p}(A)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\det\Delta(z)=0\}.

(4)

Moreover, the equivalence (
3
) provides naturally an explicit representation of the resolvent operator
z\mapsto(zI-A)^{-1}
in terms of
z\mapsto\Delta(z)^{-1}
. This leads to a concise proof of the so-called “folk theorem in autonomous functional differential equations” by reformulating it as a multiplicity theorem. It also yields an explicit description of the (generalized) eigenfunctions of
A
in terms of the Jordan chains of
\Delta
, and ultimately, all elementary solutions of (
LDDE
) can be expressed in terms of these Jordan chains, see
[
40
,
27
,
16
]
for further details and applications. In this way, the matrix-valued function
\Delta
characterizes the spectral properties of the linear operator
A
, and is therefore called a
characteristic matrix
for
A
. Moreover, the connection between
A
and
\Delta
serves as the foundation of (numerical) bifurcation analysis for (
DDE
) near equilibria, see
[
5
,
3
,
38
,
16
]
for further details as well as its implementation in numerical bifurcation analysis software packages such as
DDE-BifTool

[
61
,
20
,
43
]
and
BifurcationKit
.

To formulate an analogous result to that for periodic orbits in the ODE-setting, let
\gamma
be a
T
-periodic solution of (
DDE
) and consider the (limit) cycle
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{t}\in X:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
in
X
. To determine the stability of
\Gamma
, we are interested in the stability of the trivial solution of the periodic linear DDE

\dot{y}(t)=L(t)y_{t},\quad t\geq 0,

(LDDE
T
)

where
L(t)=DF(\gamma_{t}):X\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a bounded linear operator. Recall from
[
16
,
27
]
that its solutions are generated by a strongly continuous forward evolutionary system
U
on
X
whose generalized (infinitesimal) generator (at time
t
)
A(t):\mathcal{D}(A(t))\subseteq X\to X
reads

\mathcal{D}(A(t))=\{\varphi\in X:\varphi^{\prime}\in X,\ \varphi^{\prime}(0)=L(t)\varphi\},\quad A(t)\varphi=\varphi^{\prime},

see
[
49
,
48
,
8
]
for more information. In this infinite-dimensional setting, the monodromy operator
U(T,0)
becomes an iteratively compact bounded linear operator on
X
for which the principle of linearized stability can be proven to hold as well
[
16
]
. The natural question that arises is whether a characteristic matrix approach, as illustrated above for (
LDDE
), can also be exploited to analyse the spectral structure of (
LDDET
).

The first results in this direction are due to Hale and Verduyn Lunel, who introduced in
[
27
]
a characteristic matrix for
U(T,0)
in the case of discrete delays under the assumption that the maximal delay
h
is an integer multiple of the period
T
. The proof of this result relies on the previously mentioned framework of characteristic matrices developed in
[
40
]
. Later, Kaashoek and Verduyn Lunel (re-)proved in
[
41
]
the same statement using the general framework of characteristic matrices for classes of compact operators. Recently, these results were extended to systems with symmetry, relevant to equivariant Pyragas control
[
59
]
, where a characteristic matrix can be constructed when the delay is related to a spatio-temporal symmetry of the solution
[
12
]
. All these results demonstrate that characteristic matrices provide an effective tool for analysing the Floquet spectrum of (
LDDET
). However, they are confined to situations in which a specific relation between the period
T
and the maximal delay
h
is imposed. A first step towards removing these restrictions was undertaken by Szalai et al.
[
64
]
, who constructed a characteristic matrix
\Delta_{n}(z)\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
for the (inverse of the) Floquet multipliers of (
LDDET
) without imposing additional assumptions on the relation between
h
and
T
. Later, Sieber and Szalai
[
62
]
observed that
\Delta_{n}
has poles in
\mathbb{C}
, and that these poles may coincide with the (inverse of the) Floquet multipliers, so that not all multipliers are captured by the constructed characteristic matrix. To remedy this, Sieber and Szalai modified
\Delta_{n}(z)
to a characteristic matrix
\Delta_{nk}(z)\in\mathbb{C}^{nk\times nk}
such that
\Delta_{nk}
has no poles inside a disk of finite radius (dependent on
k
) in
\mathbb{C}
, but may still have poles outside this disk. Hence, the constructed characteristic matrix
\Delta_{nk}
is guaranteed to capture the (inverse of the) Floquet multipliers within the given disk, but may fail to capture (inverted) multipliers outside this disk.

All in all, to date there is no construction of a characteristic matrix that captures the complete Floquet spectrum of (
LDDET
) without imposing additional assumptions on the relation between the maximal delay
h
and the period
T
. The present paper aims to contribute precisely in this direction. The price we pay for capturing all Floquet multipliers/exponents is that the resulting characteristic object is no longer matrix-valued, as in the previous constructions, but operator-valued. In other words, we construct a
characteristic operator
that encodes the full Floquet spectrum of (
LDDET
). Our approach to constructing this characteristic operator is based upon the very recent observation from
[
4
,
47
]
that the spectral equality (
1
) also holds for (
LDDET
), where now the non-closed but closable linear operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
takes the form

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=\{\varphi\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X):\varphi(t)\in\mathcal{D}(A(t))\mbox{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}\},\quad(\mathcal{A}\varphi)(t)=A(t)\varphi(t)-\dot{\varphi}(t).

(5)

Moreover, the authors of
[
4
,
47
]
established various interesting spectral relations between
\mathcal{A}
and the holomorphic operator-valued function
\Delta
with action

(\Delta(z)q)(t)=\dot{q}(t)+zq(t)-L(t)[\theta\mapsto e^{z\theta}q(t+\theta)],\quad\forall q\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n}),

(6)

which is naturally obtained by substituting Floquet eigensolutions of the form
t\mapsto e^{zt}q(t)
into (
LDDET
) with
z\in\mathbb{C}
. These results were subsequently applied in
[
4
]
to study codimension one bifurcations of limit cycles for (
DDE
), where the characteristic operator
\Delta
from (
6
) for
\mathcal{A}
played an analogous role as the characteristic matrix
\Delta
from (
2
) for
A
in the analysis of bifurcations of equilibria
[
5
]
. In view of these results, it is natural to expect an equivalence after extension between
\mathcal{A}
and
\Delta
of the form (
3
). In this paper, we confirm this expectation. However, establishing this equivalence, along with the associated spectral results, proved to be significantly more interesting and technical than we initially expected.

In order to contextualize our results, we point out that the existence of a characteristic operator, rather than a characteristic matrix, to capture all Floquet multipliers of (
LDDET
) in the general case, is in line with the previously known results. In the construction by Sieber and Szalai
[
62
]
, the dimension of the characteristic matrix
\Delta_{nk}(z)\in\mathbb{C}^{nk\times nk}
increases unboundedly when the radius (dependent on
k
) of the disk, on which the (inverted) Floquet multipliers are captured, is increased. Relatedly, the monograph
[
41
]
contains several examples where the maximal delay
h
and period
T
of (
LDDET
) are rationally related. In these examples, the dimension of the characteristic matrix increases as the ratio
T/h
becomes more irrational. We also note that similar observations were previously reported by Verduyn Lunel in
[
67
]
, Just in
[
39
]
, and Skubachevskii and Walter in
[
63
]
. This all suggests that, if we aim to capture the full Floquet spectrum of (
LDDET
) without any additional assumptions on the relation between the delay and the period, it is natural to work in the framework of characteristic operators rather than that of characteristic matrices.

Although our reduction of the spectral problem to a characteristic operator does not yield a dimension reduction in the classical sense (of characteristic matrices), it nevertheless leads to a substantial simplification of the spectral problem: whereas the original spectral problem for
\mathcal{A}
is posed on
C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
with
X=C([-h,0],\mathbb{C}^{n})
, the characteristic operator
\Delta
acts on the simpler space
C_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Moreover, working with the characteristic operator
\Delta
rather than with the operator
\mathcal{A}
has both computational and analytical advantages. Computationally, it turns out that the critical normal form coefficients of all codimension one bifurcations of limit cycles in (
DDE
) can be expressed in terms of the characteristic operator
\Delta
, see
[
4
]
for further details. The key observation is that, analogous to the finite-dimensional ODE-setting described above, the (generalized) eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}
associated with the center subspace form a
T
-periodic basis along the limit cycle
\Gamma
, see
[
49
]
for further details. Most importantly, these (generalized) eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}
can be expressed in terms of the Jordan chains of
\Delta
. In this framework, the connection between
\mathcal{A}
and
\Delta
serves as the foundation of (numerical) bifurcation analysis of (
DDE
) near limit cycles, see
[
4
,
49
,
48
]
for further details as well as its implementation into the numerical bifurcation analysis software package
PeriodicNormalizationDDEs

[
6
]
, which is compatible with
BifurcationKit
. Analytically, the characteristic operator framework we present in this article is not only limited to (
LDDET
) but also applies to a much broader class of periodic evolution equations, such as DDEs with infinite delay and functional differential equations of mixed type. In these contexts, we use the existence of a characteristic operator to derive new spectral results for these classes of differential equations. In particular, we establish, under relatively mild conditions, the existence of a spectral gap around the imaginary axis for periodic functional differential equations of mixed type, which was an open question in the literature before.

1.1
Challenges and overview

In the upcoming construction of a characteristic operator
\Delta
for a linear operator
\mathcal{A}
, we encounter several functional analytical challenges.

The first challenge stems from the fact that the linear operator
\mathcal{A}
is in general not closed, but only closable. In the analysis of its spectrum, it becomes clear that the conventional definition of the spectrum
\sigma(\mathcal{A})
of
\mathcal{A}
cannot be applied (
Section

2.1
). Therefore, we present in
Section

2.1
a spectral theory, with an emphasis on the point spectrum and the isolated points in the spectrum, for closable linear operators. We start by analyzing the point spectrum algebraically using Jordan chains, then proceed to studying isolated spectral points analytically via contour integrals. However, the resulting spectral projections only identify the invariant subspaces of the closure, thereby failing to fully capture the distinct spectral structure of the original closable linear operator (
Section

2.1
).

The second challenge arises from the need to define a suitable notion of equivalence for closable linear operators, as the literature is mainly concerned about a bounded equivalence between closed (or bounded) linear operators (
Section

2.2
). With this extended definition of equivalence, we can still naturally define Jordan chains and prove their important one-to-one correspondences via equivalence, see
Section

2.2
for this construction. Furthermore, we demonstrate that specific spectral numbers remain invariant under this equivalence.

The third challenge lies in proving that certain spectral properties are preserved under the equivalence between a closable linear operator
\mathcal{A}
defined on a Banach space
X
and a characteristic operator
\Delta
whose values are defined on a (possibly different) Banach space
Y
. This task will be accomplished in
Section

2.3
under three additional (spectral) hypotheses:
(SH1)
,
(SH2)
and
(SH3)
. As a consequence, all our results from
Section

2
are a natural extension of the mentioned work by Kaashoek and Verduyn Lunel on characteristic matrices, see
Section

2.3
for further details.

In
Section

3
, we also develop a general scheme to construct for a given linear operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
of the form

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=\{\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X):\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D),\ MD\varphi=K\varphi\},\quad\mathcal{A}\varphi=D\varphi,

(7)

a characteristic operator
\Delta
with values
\Delta(z):\mathcal{D}(\Delta(z))\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y)\to\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y)
, where
z\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. Here,
D
and
K
are closable linear operators while
M
is a bounded linear operator. In applications,
D
can be regarded as a maximal operator while
K
and
M
can be interpreted as generalized boundary-value operators. For
Z\in\{X,Y\}
, the Banach space
\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Z)
stands for an arbitrary function space consisting of
T
-periodic
Z
-valued functions defined on
\mathbb{R}
. Since many periodic evolution equations can be expressed in the form (
7
), this scheme can be a applied to a wide range of periodic evolutionary systems, demonstrating its robust applicability. However, we already mention that this construction is significantly more technical than the original construction of Kaashoek and Verduyn Lunel, mainly due to the fact that the linear operator
\mathcal{A}
is not necessarily closed and
\Delta
is not necessarily matrix-valued, which prevents a strong spectral characterization like (
4
) in terms of point spectra and determinants. However, the characteristic operator still simplifies a challenging spectral problem in
\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
by reducing it towards a simpler problem in
\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y)
. In our upcoming applications (
Section

4
), the state space
X
will typically be of the form
\mathcal{F}(I,Y)
so that the spectral reduction is clear. Analogous to the characteristic matrix approach, we provide an explicit representation of the resolvent operator
z\mapsto(zI-\mathcal{A})^{-1}
in terms of
z\mapsto\Delta(z)^{-1}
(
Section

3.2
), a proof of the “folk theorem in periodic functional differential equations” by rephrasing it as an abstract multiplicity theorem (
Section

3.2
), an explicit representation of the (generalized) eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}
in terms of the Jordan chains of
\Delta
(
Section

3.2
), and eventually all elementary solutions for a large class of periodic linear functional differential equations can be computed in terms of these Jordan chains (
Section

4.1
). Moreover, we illustrate in
Section

4.1
that the characteristic matrix from Kaashoek and Verduyn Lunel for linear autonomous systems can be obtained from our construction in two equivalent ways: setting naturally the period
T=0
or restricting to the subspace of constant functions in
\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Z)
, where this latter interpretation may be more insightful for (
LDDET
) with
T/h\in\mathbb{Q}
, see
Section

4.1
for further details. In addition, we show in
Section

3.3
that the underlying
T
-periodic structure of
\mathcal{A}
and
\Delta
give rise to a
\frac{2\pi i}{T}\mathbb{Z}
-periodic pattern in their spectrum. As a result, the spectral analysis of these linear operators can be further simplified by restricting it to a (subset of the) horizontal strip
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\imaginary(z)\in(-\frac{\pi}{T},\frac{\pi}{T}]\}
rather than considering the entire complex plane.

In
Section

4
we apply our results to functional differential equations (
FDE
). As the class of periodic linear FDEs is rather large, we will illustrate the results from
Section

3
using three representative subclasses that frequently arise in applications:

1) Classical DDEs (
Section

4.1
):
This subclass, introduced as our motivating example in (
LDDET
), naturally warrants inclusion here. We present detailed proofs for the results in this case, whereas the proofs for the next two subclasses follow similar reasoning and are therefore partly omitted.

2) iDDEs (
Section

4.2
):
The subclass of DDEs with infinite delay is particularly interesting because the validity of the principle of linearized stability depends on the selected state space
X
. Furthermore, we note that exactly the same challenges arise, and can be addressed, as in the construction of a characteristic matrix for autonomous iDDEs
[

, Section II.1.3]
.

3) Mixed FDEs (
Section

4.3
):
This subclass of FDEs highlights the strength of the characteristic operator framework, particularly since a forward evolutionary system

is generally unavailable. Consequently, we affirm a conjecture by Hupkes and Verduyn Lunel
[
33
, Hypothesis (HF)]
concerning the discrete spectral structure of the Floquet exponents, see
Section

4.3
for further details.

In
Section

5
, we summarize and discuss additional examples of periodic evolution equations on which our construction could be applied. Moreover, we list some conjectures and open problems that arise naturally from our results and may guide further research in this area.

2
Characteristic operators for closable linear operators

2.1
Spectral properties of closable linear operators

We first introduce notation that we will use throughout the rest of the manuscript. Let
Y
and
Z
be normed (but not necessarily complete) spaces. The set of all linear operators from (a linear subspace of)
Y
to
Z
is denoted by
L(Y,Z)
and we write
L(Z)\coloneqq L(Z,Z)
. Likewise, the set of all bounded linear operators from
Y
to
Z
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(Y,Z)
and we write
\mathcal{L}(Z)\coloneqq\mathcal{L}(Z,Z)
. Note that
\mathcal{L}(Y,Z)
becomes a normed space when equipped with the operator norm, forms a Banach space when
Z
is a Banach space, and forms a Banach algebra under composition when
Y=Z
are Banach spaces.

Let
Y
and
Z
be Banach spaces and consider a linear operator
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq Y\to Z
with
domain

\mathcal{D}(A)
, a linear subspace of
Y
. We call
\mathcal{N}(A)\coloneqq\{\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A):A\varphi=0\}
the
kernel
of
A
,
\mathcal{R}(A)\coloneqq\{A\varphi\in Z:\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A)\}
the
range
of
A
and
\Gamma(A)\coloneqq\{(A\varphi,\varphi)\in Z\times Y:\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A)\}
the
graph
of
A
. Throughout the rest of this paper, we will frequently use the notion of a closed and closable linear operator.

{definition}

We call the linear operator
A

closed
if its graph
\Gamma(A)
is a closed subset of
Z\times Y
. Moreover, we call the linear operator
A

closable
if
A
admits a closed linear extension, meaning that there exists a closed linear operator
B:\mathcal{D}(B)\subseteq Y\to Z
satisfying
\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(B)
and
B\varphi=A\varphi
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
.
\lozenge

Let
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq Y\to Z
be a closable linear operator, then there exists a smallest closed linear extension
\overline{A}
of
A
, called the
closure
of
A
, which can be characterized by
\Gamma(\overline{A})=\overline{\Gamma(A)}
, see
[
26
, Proposition XIV.1.3]
for a detailed proof. As a direct consequence of the preceding definition and characterization, we obtain the following result concerning the connection between the domain, kernel and range of
A
and
\overline{A}
. It is important to note that the inclusions in (
8
) might be strict and that the topological closure is taken with respect to the norm on the associated Banach space.

{lemma}

The following inclusions hold:

\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\overline{A})\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{D}(A)},\quad\mathcal{N}(A)\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{N}(A)}\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\overline{A}),\quad\mathcal{R}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(\overline{A})\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}.

(8)

Proof.

We first prove the statement on the domains. Here, the first inclusion is clear. To prove the second inclusion, let
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{A})
be given. Then
(\overline{A}\varphi,\varphi)\in\Gamma(\overline{A})=\overline{\Gamma(A)}
. Thus, there exists a sequence
(\varphi_{m})_{m}
in
\mathcal{D}(A)
such that
(A\varphi_{m},\varphi_{m})\to(\overline{A}\varphi,\varphi)
as
m\to\infty
. This implies
\varphi_{m}\to\varphi
as
m\to\infty
, meaning that
\varphi\in\overline{\mathcal{D}(A)}
.

We next prove the statement on the kernels. Here, the first inclusion is clear. To prove the second inclusion, let
\varphi\in\overline{\mathcal{N}(A)}
be given. Then there exists a sequence
(\varphi_{m})_{m}
in
\mathcal{N}(A)
such that
\varphi_{m}\to\varphi
as
m\to\infty
and thus
A\varphi_{m}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence,
(A\varphi_{m},\varphi_{m})\to(0,\varphi)\in\overline{\Gamma(A)}=\Gamma(\overline{A})
as
m\to\infty
. Hence,
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{A})
and
\overline{A}\varphi=0
so that
\varphi\in\mathcal{N}(\overline{A})
.

We finally prove the statement on the ranges. Here, the first inclusion is clear. To prove the second inclusion, let
\psi\in\mathcal{R}(\overline{A})
be given. Then there exists a
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{A})
such that
\overline{A}\varphi=\psi
and thus
(\psi,\varphi)\in\Gamma(\overline{A})=\overline{\Gamma(A)}
. Hence, there exists a sequence
(\varphi_{m})_{m}
in
\mathcal{D}(A)
such that
\varphi_{m}\to\varphi
and
A\varphi_{m}\to\overline{A}\varphi=\psi
as
m\to\infty
. We conclude that
\psi\in\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
.
∎

So if we write (with somewhat unusual, but later useful notation)
Y_{A}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}(\overline{A})\subseteq Y
, then this normed space becomes a Banach space with respect to the
graph norm

\|\cdot\|_{\overline{A}}\coloneqq\|\cdot\|+\|\overline{A}\cdot\|
. In particular,
\overline{A}
becomes a continuous (bounded) linear operator on
Y_{A}
and
\mathcal{D}(A)
is dense in
Y_{A}
in the graph norm. Using the earlier introduced notation, we have that
A\in L(Y,Z)
and
\overline{A}\in\mathcal{L}(Y_{A},Z)
.

We now proceed to characterize the resolvent set and spectrum of the closable linear operator
A
. To this end, we assume that the Banach spaces
Y
and
Z
are complex and identical, and we denote this common space by
X
. As mentioned in the introduction, the classical notion of the spectrum is for our purposes not suitable for closable linear operators. Instead, we will work with the following extended notion of the spectrum introduced in
[
65
, Chapter 5]
.

{definition}

Let
X
be a complex Banach space and
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq X\to X
a closable linear operator. A complex number
z
belongs to the
resolvent set

\rho(A)
of
A
if the operator
zI-A
is injective, has dense range
\mathcal{R}(zI-A)
in
X
, and the
resolvent
of
A
at
z

defined by
R(z,A)\coloneqq(zI-A)^{-1}:\mathcal{R}(zI-A)\to\mathcal{D}(A)
is a bounded linear operator. In this case, we say that the linear operator
zI-A
is
invertible
.

The
spectrum

\sigma(A)
of
A
is defined to be the complement of
\rho(A)
in
\mathbb{C}
, and the
point spectrum

\sigma_{p}(A)\subseteq\sigma(A)
of
A
is the set of those
\sigma\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\sigma I-A
is not injective, i.e.
A\varphi=\sigma\varphi
for some nonzero
eigenvector

\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
. If
\sigma\in\sigma_{p}(A)
, we will also call
\sigma
an
eigenvalue
of
A
.
\lozenge

{remark}

We emphasize that the above definition of the resolvent set for closable linear operators extends the classical definition (
[
17
, Definition IV.1.1]
) for closed linear operators, which requires only that
zI-A
is a linear bijection. In our setting, the surjectivity condition is relaxed to the requirement that
zI-A
has dense range, while additionally insisting that
R(z,A)
is bounded. If
A
is a closed (and thus closable) linear operator with
z\in\rho(A)
, then it can be shown by an application of the closed graph theorem that
zI-A
is actually surjective and its inverse is automatically bounded
[
65
, Theorem 4.2.E]
. Therefore, for closed linear operators,
Section

2.1
agrees with the classical one.

The reason we cannot work with the usual definition is that most of our operators of interest are in general not closed but only closable (
Appendix

B
), and the domains of their resolvents are not necessarily the full space
X
(
Appendix

B
). With the usual standard definition, recall from
[
26
, Proposition XIV.1.2]
that
\rho(A)=\emptyset
for a non-closed operator
A
, which would be very impractical for our purposes.
\lozenge

For the remainder of this section, we fix a complex Banach space
X
and a closable linear operator
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq X\to X
. The first fundamental result on the resolvent and spectrum of
A
is stated below. Since it follows directly from
[
65
, Theorems 5.1.A and 5.1.B]
, the proof is omitted.

{proposition}

The resolvent set
\rho(A)
is open in
\mathbb{C}
, and hence the spectrum
\sigma(A)
is closed. In particular, if
z_{2}\in\rho(A)
and
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
is such that
|z_{2}-z_{1}|<1/\|R(z_{2},A)\|
, then
z_{1}\in\rho(A)
.

The next step is to analyse in detail the relationship between the spectral objects associated with
A
and
\overline{A}
.

{lemma}

There holds
\rho(A)=\rho(\overline{A})
and in particular

R(z,A)=R(z,\overline{A})|_{\mathcal{R}(zI-A)},\quad\forall z\in\rho(A).

(9)

Consequently,
\sigma(A)=\sigma(\overline{A})
and there holds
\sigma_{p}(A)\subseteq\sigma_{p}(\overline{A})
. Moreover, if
\sigma_{p}(A)\cap\Omega=\sigma(A)\cap\Omega
for some
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, then
\sigma_{p}(A)\cap\Omega=\sigma_{p}(\overline{A})\cap\Omega
.

Proof.

We first prove that
\rho(A)\subseteq\rho(\overline{A})
, and let therefore
z\in\rho(A)
be given. Since
\mathcal{D}(R(z,A))=\mathcal{R}(zI-A)
is dense in
X
, the bounded linear operator
R(z,A)
extends uniquely to a bounded linear operator
\overline{R(z,A)}\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Since
\Gamma(\overline{A})=\overline{\Gamma(A)}
, one can check that
\overline{R(z,A)}=R(z,\overline{A})
, meaning that
z\in\rho(\overline{A})
. To prove the other inclusion, let
z\in\rho(\overline{A})
be given. Note that
zI-A
is injective as the linear extension
zI-\overline{A}
is injective. To show that
\mathcal{R}(zI-A)
is dense in
X
, we apply
Section

2.1
onto the linear operator
zI-A
, which is closable as
A
is closable and
I
is bounded (
Appendix

A
). This yields
X=\mathcal{R}(zI-\overline{A})\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{R}(zI-A)}
and thus
\overline{\mathcal{R}(zI-A)}=X
. Clearly,
R(z,A)
is bounded since
R(z,\overline{A})
is a bounded linear extension. We conclude that
z\in\rho(A)
.

To prove (
9
), it remains to show that
R(z,\overline{A})|_{\mathcal{R}(zI-A)}
maps into
\mathcal{D}(A)
. Indeed, if
\psi\in\mathcal{R}(zI-A)
, then there exists
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
such that
(zI-A)\varphi=\psi
. Since
\overline{A}
extends
A
, we have
(zI-\overline{A})\varphi=\psi
, which implies
R(z,\overline{A})\psi=\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
.

To prove the claim regarding the point spectrum, let
\sigma\in\sigma_{p}(A)
be given. The result now follows by applying the kernel statement of
Section

2.1
to the closable linear operator
\sigma I-A
.

The last claim follows from
\sigma_{p}(\overline{A})\cap\Omega\subseteq\sigma(\overline{A})\cap\Omega=\sigma(A)\cap\Omega=\sigma_{p}(A)\cap\Omega\subseteq\sigma_{p}(\overline{A})\cap\Omega
. Here, the first equality follows from the first part of the proof, and the second from the additional assumption.
∎

It is important to observe that the inclusion of point spectra in
Section

2.1
may be strict since there exist closable linear operators that are injective but whose closures are not, see
[
55
, Section 19.2.5]
for further details. However, one can prove that spectral values in
\sigma_{p}(\overline{A})\setminus\sigma_{p}(A)
actually lie in the approximate point spectrum of
A
, though we will not pursue this direction further. Recall that this situation cannot arise if
\sigma_{p}(A)\cap\Omega=\sigma(A)\cap\Omega
for a subset
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, see the applications presented in
Section

4
.

To further characterize the point spectrum of
A
, let
\sigma\in\sigma_{p}(A)
be given. The linear subspace
\mathcal{N}(\sigma I-A)
of
X
is called the
eigenspace
of
A
at
\sigma
, and its dimension
m_{g}(\sigma,A)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
is referred to as the
geometric multiplicity
of
A
at
\sigma
. Consider the following closed linear subspaces

E_{\sigma}(A)\coloneqq\overline{\bigcup_{l\geq 1}\mathcal{N}((\sigma I-A)^{l})},\quad Q_{\sigma}(A)\coloneqq\overline{\bigcap_{l\geq 1}\mathcal{R}((\sigma I-A)^{l})},

of
X
. Here,
E_{\sigma}(A)
is called the
generalized eigenspace
of
A
at
\sigma
and
Q_{\sigma}(A)
is called the
complementary generalized eigenspace
of
A
at
\sigma
. The dimension
m_{a}(\sigma,A)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
of
E_{\sigma}(A)
is referred to as the
algebraic multiplicity
of
A
at
\sigma
. Observe that
E_{\sigma}(A)
is the smallest closed linear subspace of
X
that contains the ascending chain of linear subspaces
\mathcal{N}((\sigma I-A)^{l})
for all integers
l\geq 1
. Moreover,
m_{g}(\sigma,A)\leq m_{a}(\sigma,A)
and the condition
m_{g}(\sigma,A)=\infty
implies that
m_{a}(\sigma,A)=\infty
. For the case
m_{g}(\sigma,A)<\infty
, a more detailed description of
E_{\sigma}(A)
requires the notion of Jordan chains for closable linear operators.

{definition}

Let
X
be a complex Banach space and
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq X\to X
a closable linear operator. An ordered set
\{\varphi_{0},\dots,\varphi_{k-1}\}
of vectors in
\mathcal{D}(A)
is called a
Jordan chain
of
A
at
\sigma\in\mathbb{C}
if
\varphi_{0}\neq 0
and

(A-\sigma I)\varphi_{i}=\begin{cases}0,\quad&i=0,\\
\varphi_{i-1},\quad&i=1,\dots,k-1.\end{cases}

The vectors
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k-1}
are called
generalized eigenvectors
of
A
at
\sigma
. The number
k\in\mathbb{N}
is called the
length
of the chain. If there exists a maximal length of the chain starting with
\varphi_{0}
, then we call this length the
rank
of
\varphi_{0}
. If there is no maximal length, then we say that
\varphi_{0}
has
infinite rank
.
\lozenge

Let us assume that
p=m_{g}(\sigma,A)<\infty
. Following the algebraic procedure of Gohberg and Sigal
[
25
]
, we may arrange the Jordan chains as follows. Let
\varphi_{1,0},\dots,\varphi_{p,0}
be a basis of
\mathcal{N}(\sigma I-A)
chosen such that the ranks
k_{j}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
of the eigenvector
\varphi_{j,0}
are maximized sequentially. The (possibly infinite) integers
k_{1}\leq\dots\leq k_{p}
, which are independent of the choice of such a basis, represent the sizes of the Jordan blocks and are referred to as the
partial multiplicities
of
\sigma
. We define the largest partial multiplicity
k_{p}\eqqcolon k(\sigma,A)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
to be the
ascent
of
A
at
\sigma
. In this framework, the algebraic multiplicity satisfies
m_{a}(\sigma,A)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}k_{j}
. If in addition
m_{a}(\sigma,A)<\infty
, then
\sigma
is called an
eigenvalue of finite type
of
A
since in that case
m_{g}(\sigma,A)
,
m_{a}(\sigma,A)
and
k(\sigma,A)
are all finite. In particular,
k(\sigma,A)
is the smallest integer such that

E_{\sigma}(A)=\mathcal{N}((\sigma I-A)^{k(\sigma,A)}),\quad Q_{\sigma}(A)\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{R}((\sigma I-A)^{k(\sigma,A)})},

(10)

where we recall that
E_{\sigma}(A)
is closed as it is finite-dimensional. Note that the second inclusion might be strict (
[
65
, Problem 5.4.2]
). Moreover,
E_{\sigma}(A)
admits a basis of the form

\varphi_{1,0},\dots,\varphi_{1,k_{1}-1},\dots,\varphi_{p,0},\dots,\varphi_{p,k_{p}-1},

(11)

which is called a
canonical basis of (generalized) eigenvectors
of
A
at
\sigma
. With respect to this specific basis, the matrix representation of
A|_{E_{\sigma}(A)}\in\mathcal{L}(E_{\sigma}(A))
is a Jordan matrix with
\sigma
on the main diagonal. The partitioning of the vectors in (
11
) corresponds precisely to the decomposition of this Jordan matrix into individual Jordan blocks. The following lemma clarifies the relationship between these notions for
A
and
\overline{A}
.

{lemma}

If
\sigma\in\sigma_{p}(A)
, then
m_{g}(\sigma,A)\leq m_{g}(\sigma,\overline{A})
,
m_{a}(\sigma,A)\leq m_{a}(\sigma,\overline{A})
and
k(\sigma,A)\leq k(\sigma,\overline{A})
, and we have the inequalities

\displaystyle\begin{split}&m_{g}(\sigma,A)+k(\sigma,A)-1\leq m_{a}(\sigma,A)\leq m_{g}(\sigma,A)k(\sigma,A),\\
&m_{g}(\sigma,\overline{A})+k(\sigma,\overline{A})-1\leq m_{a}(\sigma,\overline{A})\leq m_{g}(\sigma,\overline{A})k(\sigma,\overline{A}).\end{split}

(12)

Proof.

Let us first recall from
Section

2.1
that
\sigma
is also in
\sigma_{p}(\overline{A})
. The first claim follows from the fact that
\mathcal{N}(\sigma I-A)\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\sigma I-\overline{A})
due to
Section

2.1
. To prove the second claim, an inductive argument shows that
\mathcal{N}((\sigma I-A)^{l})\subseteq\mathcal{N}((\sigma I-\overline{A})^{l})
for all integers
l\geq 1
. As the union and closure operations preserve the order of inclusion, we obtain
E_{\sigma}(A)\subseteq E_{\sigma}(\overline{A})
, which proves the second claim. Since any Jordan chain of
A
at
\sigma
is also a Jordan chain of
\overline{A}
at
\sigma
, the third claim holds.

We now prove (
12
) for
A
. First, if
m_{g}(\sigma,A)=\infty
, then
m_{a}(\sigma,A)=\infty
and
k(\sigma,A)\geq 1
so that (
12
) holds. Second, if
m_{g}(\sigma,A)<\infty
while
m_{a}(\sigma,A)=\infty
, then necessarily
k(\sigma,A)=\infty
, and (
12
) holds since
m_{g}(\sigma,A)\geq 1
. Third, if
m_{g}(\sigma,A)<\infty
but
k(\sigma,A)=\infty
, then
m_{a}(\sigma,A)=\infty
and thus (
12
) is again immediate. It therefore remains to consider the case where
\sigma
is of finite type. Set
p=m_{g}(\sigma,A)<\infty
and recall that we have ordered the canonical basis of (generalized) eigenvectors of
A
at
\sigma
in such a way that
k(\sigma,A)=k_{p}
. Then

\displaystyle m_{g}(\sigma,A)+k(\sigma,A)-1

\displaystyle=\underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{p-1\ \text{times}}+k_{p}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{p}k_{j}=m_{a}(\sigma,A),

where the inequality follows from the fact that
k_{j}\geq 1
for all
j=1,\dots,p
. This proves the first inequality in (
12
). The second inequality follows from
m_{a}(\sigma,A)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}k_{j}\leq pk_{p}=m_{g}(\sigma,A)k(\sigma,A)
, as
k_{p}
is maximal. Since
\overline{A}
is closed (and thus closable), the second chain of inequalities in (
12
) holds as well.
∎

With the framework for the point spectrum of the closable linear operator
A
established, we extend our analysis to the full spectrum
\sigma(A)
. This requires first a thorough investigation of the resolvent set
\rho(A)
and the associated
resolvent operator

R(\cdot,A)
of
A
. In order to develop a coherent spectral theory for closable linear operators, paralleling to the classical framework available for closed linear operators, one needs to make additional assumptions on the behaviour of
R(\cdot,A)
. In particular, we would like to use a
resolvent identity
of the form:

R(z_{1},A)-R(z_{2},A)=(z_{2}-z_{1})R(z_{1},A)R(z_{2},A),\quad\forall z_{1},z_{2}\in\rho(A).

However, note that the expressions in this identity are not necessarily well-defined, since i) on the left-hand side of the equation, the domains
\mathcal{D}(R(z_{i},A))=\mathcal{R}(z_{i}I-A)
might be different for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and ii) on the right-hand side of the equation, the operator
R(z_{2},A)
, which has range
\mathcal{R}(R(z_{2},A))=\mathcal{D}(A)
, might not map into the domain
\mathcal{D}(R(z_{1},A))=\mathcal{R}(z_{1}I-A)
. In order to bypass these issues, we introduce the following (spectral) hypothesis:

(SH1)

The domain
\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(zI-A)
for all
z\in\rho(A)
.

Under the assumption of
(SH1)
, we show in the next result that
\mathcal{D}(R(z,A))=\mathcal{R}(zI-A)
is independent of
z\in\rho(A)
. This ensures that both sides of the resolvent identity are well-defined and makes the equality itself true. It is worth noting that
(SH1)
represents a deviation from the treatment in
[
65
, Section 5.1]
. Specifically, the analogue of the following result in that work (
[
65
, Theorem 5.1.C]
) is established under the stronger assumption that
\mathcal{R}(zI-A)=X
whenever
z\in\rho(A)
.

{proposition}

Assume that
A
satisfies
(SH1)
and let
z_{1},z_{2}\in\rho(A)
be given. Then

\mathcal{R}(z_{1}I-A)=\mathcal{R}(z_{2}I-A),

(13)

and the resolvent identity

R(z_{1},A)-R(z_{2},A)=(z_{2}-z_{1})R(z_{1},A)R(z_{2},A),

(14)

holds. Moreover, the resolvent operator
R(\cdot,A)
of
A
is analytic on
\rho(A)
since

R(z_{1},A)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(z_{2}-z_{1})^{l}R(z_{2},A)^{l+1},

(15)

for all
z_{2}\in\rho(A)
and
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z_{1}-z_{2}|<1/\|R(z_{2},A)\|
.

Proof.

To prove (
13
), we first show that
\mathcal{R}(z_{1}I-A)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(z_{2}I-A)
for
z_{1},z_{2}\in\rho(A)
. The other inclusion then follows by interchanging
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
. Fix
\psi\in\mathcal{R}(z_{1}I-A)
, then there exists a
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
such that
(z_{1}I-A)\varphi=\psi
. We next observe that
(z_{2}I-A)\varphi=\psi+(z_{2}-z_{1})\varphi
or equivalently

\psi=(z_{2}I-A)\varphi-(z_{2}-z_{1})\varphi.

The first term on the right-hand side is an element of
\mathcal{R}(z_{2}I-A)
, and the second term is an element of
\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(z_{2}I-A)
by
(SH1)
. So
\psi\in\mathcal{R}(z_{2}I-A)
, and thus
\mathcal{R}(z_{1}I-A)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(z_{2}I-A)
, as claimed.

To prove (
14
), we first observe that
(z_{2}-z_{1})I=(z_{2}I-A)-(z_{1}I-A)
. Composing this identity from the left with
R(z_{1},A)
, which is well-defined as
z_{2}I-A
maps into

\mathcal{D}(R(z_{2},A))=\mathcal{R}(z_{2}I-A)=\mathcal{R}(z_{1}I-A)
by (
13
), and composing the resulting identity from the right by
R(z_{2},A)
yields the result.

To prove (
15
), consider
z_{2}\in\rho(A)
and
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying
|z_{1}-z_{2}|<1/\|R(z_{2},A)\|
. Then
Section

2.1
tells us that
z_{1}\in\rho(A)
. By subsequently applying (
14
), induction yields the expression

R(z_{1},A)=\sum_{l=0}^{m}(z_{2}-z_{1})^{l}R(z_{2},A)^{l+1}+(z_{2}-z_{1})^{m+1}R(z_{1},A)R(z_{2},A)^{m+1},\quad\forall m\in\mathbb{N},

(16)

which is well-defined due to
(SH1)
and (
13
). Note that the remainder term in (
16
) vanishes (in norm) as
m\to\infty
since
\|R(z_{1},A)\|[|z_{1}-z_{2}|\|R(z_{2},A)\|]^{m+1}\to 0
as
m\to\infty
since
|z_{1}-z_{2}|\|R(z_{2},A)\|<1
. This proves (
15
) and so the proof is complete.
∎

With the framework for the point spectrum and the resolvent set of a closable linear operator
A
established, we extend our analysis to isolated points of
\sigma(A)
using
(SH1)
. Our goal is to recover a spectral decomposition of
X
into two
A
-invariant linear subspaces similar to the classical setting of closed linear operators, see for example the results in
[
26
, Section XV.2]
. However, this turns out to be ambitious. Indeed, since
A
is not necessarily closed, we cannot guarantee that the residue of
A
at an isolated eigenvalue
\sigma
of
A
is a projection operator, as will be detailed below. Instead, we decompose with respect to invariant subspaces of
\overline{A}
and subsequently try to establish the connection with the corresponding subspaces related to
A
. We start with the following result, which lifts the classical construction of a Laurent series for
R(z,\overline{A})
towards
R(z,A)
around an isolated point in
\sigma(A)
.

{lemma}

Assume that
A
satisfies
(SH1)
. If
\sigma
is an isolated point of
\sigma(A)
, then the resolvent of
A
at
\sigma
can be locally expanded as a Laurent series

R(z,A)=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}(z-\sigma)^{l}R_{l}(\sigma,A),\quad R_{l}(\sigma,A)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma_{\sigma}}(z-\sigma)^{-(l+1)}R(z,A)dz,

(17)

whenever
0<|z-\sigma|<\delta
for some sufficiently small
\delta
and
\Gamma_{\sigma}

Proof.

for all

and so

Even when

.

equals that of

at

\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq X_{|}\subseteq\mathcal{R}(zI-A)

\mathcal{R}(zI-A_{|})\subseteq X_{|}

Let

, then

is closable, and the following statements hold:

\sigma_{p}(B)=\sigma_{p}(A)

.

\sigma

considered as an eigenvalue of

\sigma
considered as an eigenvalue of

satisfies
(SH1)

satisfies
(SH1)
. If in addition

Proof.

We first prove that
B
is closable by an application of

A
. Let
(\varphi_{m})_{m}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{D}(B)
converging in norm to zero and suppose that
(B\varphi_{m})_{m}
converges in norm to some
\psi\in Y
. Since

is invertible, the bounded inverse theorem guarantees
V^{-1}\in\mathcal{L}(Y,X)
. Consequently, the sequence
(V^{-1}\varphi_{m})_{m}
lies in
\mathcal{D}(A)
and converges to zero. We observe that

as

by the continuity of

. Since

is closable,

and

. Since

is injective,

and thus

is closable.

To prove the first statement, observe that

(or

) provides a one-to-one map from eigenvectors of

(or

(or
B
), and so

.

Equivalence between closed (and bounded) linear operators was first introduced by Gohberg et al.

\lozenge

Next, we introduce the notions of a characteristic value and a Jordan chain of a closable operator-valued function
L
. The following definition may be viewed as a natural extension of the classical concepts of eigenvalues and Jordan chains for the linear case
L(z)=zI-A
with
A\in L(X)
, as presented in
Section

2.1
and
Section

2.1
, respectively. We also refer to
[
40
, Section I.1.2]
for more information in the setting of closed linear operators.

{definition}

Let
L:\Omega\to L(X,Y)
be a holomorphic closable operator-valued function with
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{D}(L(z))
for all
z\in\Omega
. A point
\sigma\in\Omega
is called a
characteristic value
of
L
at
\sigma
if there exists a nonzero
eigenvector

q_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
L(\sigma)q_{0}=0
. An ordered set
\{q_{0},\dots,q_{k-1}\}
of vectors in
\mathcal{X}
is called a
Jordan chain
of
L
at
\sigma
if
q_{0}\neq 0
and

L(z)[q_{0}+(z-\sigma)q_{1}+\cdots+(z-\sigma)^{k-1}q_{k-1}]=\mathcal{O}((z-\sigma)^{k}).

The vectors
q_{1},\dots,q_{k-1}
are called
generalized eigenvectors
of
L
at
\sigma
. The number
k\in\mathbb{N}
is called the
length
of the chain. If there exists a maximal length of the chain starting with
q_{0}
, then we call this length the
rank
of
q_{0}
and the holomorphic function
\mathbb{C}\ni z\mapsto\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}(z-\sigma)^{l}q_{l}\in\mathcal{X}
the
root function
of
L
corresponding to
\sigma
. If there is no maximal length, then we say that
q_{0}
has
infinite rank
.
\lozenge

Let us now introduce the notions of geometric and algebraic multiplicity of
L
at a characteristic value
\sigma\in\Omega
. The linear subspace
\mathcal{N}(L(\sigma))
of
X
is called the
eigenspace
of
L
corresponding to
\sigma
, and its dimension
m_{g}(L(\sigma))\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
is referred to as the
geometric multiplicity
of
L
at
\sigma
. To introduce the algebraic multiplicity
m_{a}(L(\sigma))\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
of
L
at
\sigma
, we proceed as follows: If
m_{g}(L(\sigma))=\infty
, then we set
m_{a}(L(\sigma))\coloneqq\infty
. If
p=m_{g}(L(\sigma))<\infty
, we proceed similarly as in
Section

2.1
. Thus, we pick a basis
q_{1,0},\dots,q_{p,0}
of
\mathcal{N}(L(\sigma))
such that the ranks
k_{j}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
of the eigenvector
q_{j,0}
are maximized sequentially. The (possibly infinite) integers
k_{1}\leq\dots\leq k_{p}
, which are independent of the choice of basis, are called the
zero multiplicities
of
L
at
\sigma
. We define the largest zero multiplicity
k_{p}\eqqcolon k(L(\sigma))\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
to be the
ascent
of
L
at
\sigma
, and define
m_{a}(L(\sigma))\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{p}k_{j}
accordingly. If in addition
m_{a}(L(\sigma))<\infty
, then
\sigma
is called a
characteristic value of finite type
of
L
. Following this procedure, we obtain a
canonical system
of Jordan chains

q_{1,0},\dots,q_{1,k_{1}-1},\dots,q_{p,0},\dots,q_{p,k_{p}-1}

(22)

of
L
at
\sigma
. Again, one can see (
22
) as an extension of the classical notion of a canonical system of (generalized) eigenvectors for the linear case
L(z)=zI-A
with
A\in L(X)
presented in (
11
).

{remark}

The attentive reader may have observed that our definition of
m_{a}(L(\sigma))
differs in nature from that of
m_{a}(\sigma,A)
. In the classical linear case
L(z)=zI-A
with
A\in L(X)
, recall from (
10
) that the generalized eigenspace
E_{\sigma}(A)
coincides with
\mathcal{N}((\sigma I-A)^{k(\sigma,A)})
if
\sigma
is of finite type. However, in the general nonlinear setting of
L
, the linear span of the Jordan chains of
L
at
\sigma
cannot in general be characterized as the kernel of powers of a single operator. We refer to
[
41
, Section 5.1.1]
for a simple counterexample on
L\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
showing that
\dim\mathcal{N}(L(\sigma)^{l})\neq m_{a}(L(\sigma))
for all
l\geq 1
.
\lozenge

The next result is an extension of
[
40
, Proposition 1.2]
from closed to closable linear operators.

{proposition}

If two holomorphic closable operator-valued functions
L
and
M
are equivalent on
\Omega
, then there is a one-to-one correspondence between their Jordan chains. In particular,
m_{g}(L(\sigma))=m_{g}(M(\sigma)),m_{a}(L(\sigma))=m_{a}(M(\sigma))
and
k(L(\sigma))=k(M(\sigma))
for a characteristic value
\sigma\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
\{q_{0},\dots,q_{k-1}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be a Jordan chain of
L
at
\sigma
. Since
E(\cdot)^{-1}
is holomorphic by assumption (
Section

2.2
), and any root function is holomorphic as well, it is clear that the map
\Omega\ni z\mapsto E(z)^{-1}\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}(z-\sigma)^{l}q_{l}\in X^{\prime}
is holomorphic, and thus analytic on
\Omega
. Hence there exist vectors
(q_{l}^{\prime})_{l}
in
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
such that
E(z)^{-1}\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}(z-\sigma)^{l}q_{l}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(z-\sigma)^{l}q_{l}^{\prime}
for all
z\in\Omega
. Then
q_{0}^{\prime}\neq 0
and a quick calculation using (
21
) shows that
M(z)\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}(z-\sigma)^{l}q_{l}^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}((z-\sigma)^{k})
, which proves that
\{q_{0}^{\prime},\dots,q_{k-1}^{\prime}\}
is a Jordan chain of
M
at
\sigma
. The other direction can be proven similarly by using the fact that
E
is holomorphic and that the conjugation
L(z)=F(z)^{-1}M(z)E(z)^{-1}
holds due to
Section

2.2
. We conclude that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the Jordan chains of
L
and
M
.

To prove the remaining claim, let us observe from (
21
) that
E(\sigma)
maps
\mathcal{N}(M(\sigma))
in a one-to-one way onto
\mathcal{N}(L(\sigma))
, which proves that
m_{g}(L(\sigma))=m_{g}(M(\sigma))
. Since there is a one-to-one correspondence between the Jordan chains of
L
and
M
, the zero multiplicities of
L
and
M
coincide and thus
m_{a}(L(\sigma))=m_{a}(M(\sigma))
and
k(L(\sigma))=k(M(\sigma))
, which proves the claim.
∎

We emphasize that the concepts introduced earlier in this subsection do not require the underlying complex Banach spaces
X
and
Y
to coincide. We now turn to the introduction of the resolvent set
\rho(L)
of
L
as an extension of
Section

2.1
, where we assume that
X
and
Y
do coincide.

{definition}

Let
L:\Omega\to L(Y)
be a holomorphic closable operator-valued function and fix
z\in\Omega
. We say that
L(z)
is
invertible
if
0\in\rho(L(z))
, i.e.
L(z)
is injective, has dense range
\mathcal{R}(L(z))
in
Y
, and the algebraic inverse
L(z)^{-1}:\mathcal{R}(L(z))\to\mathcal{D}(L(z))
is a bounded linear operator. We subsequently write
\rho(L)\coloneqq\{z\in\Omega:L(z)\mbox{ is invertible}\}
for the
resolvent set
of
L
.
\lozenge

For the remainder of this section, we fix a complex Banach space
Y
and a holomorphic closable operator-valued function
L:\Omega\to L(Y)
. In the spirit of the result established in
Section

2.1
, we aim to introduce the notion of the order of a pole at an isolated point
\sigma\notin\rho(L)
. Recall that in
(SH1)
, we imposed a condition on
\mathcal{D}(A)
that allowed us to conclude in
Section

2.1
that the range of
zI-A
is independent of
z\in\rho(A)
. We would now like to establish a similar property for the range of the operator
L(z)
. However, since
L
is not necessarily of the form
L(z)=zI-A
for some
A\in L(X)
, a condition on the domain
\mathcal{D}(L(z))
does not directly imply that the range
\mathcal{R}(L(z))
is
z
-independent for
z\in\rho(L)
. To bypass this issue, we introduce the following (spectral) hypothesis:

(SH2)

The range
\mathcal{R}(L(z))
is
z
-independent for all
z\in\rho(L)
.

To study the order of a pole of
L
at an isolated point
\sigma\notin\rho(L)
, recall from the linear case (
Section

2.1
) that holomorphicity of
L(\cdot)^{-1}:\rho(L)\to L(Y)
would be helpful when
\rho(L)
is open. Under these additional assumptions, it is clear that we can expand for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{R}(L(z))
the map
L(\cdot)^{-1}\varphi
at
\sigma
locally as a Laurent series

L(z)^{-1}\varphi=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}(z-\sigma)^{l}L_{l}(\sigma)\varphi,\quad L_{l}(\sigma)\varphi\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma_{\sigma}}(z-\sigma)^{-(l+1)}L(z)^{-1}\varphi dz,

(23)

whenever
0<|z-\sigma|<\delta
for some sufficiently small
\delta
. Note that the Dunford integral from (
23
) is well-defined due to
(SH2)
. However, note that
L_{l}(\sigma)
might map back into
\mathcal{D}(\overline{L(z)})
instead of
\mathcal{D}(L(z))
. If there exists a
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
L_{-r}(\sigma)\neq 0
while
L_{-l}(\sigma)=0
for all
l>r
, then
\sigma
is called a
pole
of
L
of
order

r\eqqcolon r(L(\sigma))
, and we set
r(L(\sigma))=\infty
if
L(\cdot)^{-1}
has an essential singularity at
\sigma
.

2.3
Characteristic operators and spectral properties

Throughout this section, we let
X
and
Y
be complex Banach spaces and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a nonempty open set. The following definition is a cornerstone of this paper and extends the definition of a characteristic matrix for a closed linear operator from
[
40
, Section I.2]
to a characteristic operator for a closable linear operator.

{definition}

Consider a closable linear operator
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq X\to X
and a holomorphic closable operator-valued function
\Delta:\Omega\to L(Y)
. The operator-valued function
\Delta
is called a
characteristic operator
for
A
on
\Omega
if there exists a complex Banach space
Z
along with dense linear subspaces
\tilde{X}\subseteq X
and
\tilde{Z}\subseteq Z
such that the holomorphic closable operator-valued functions

\Omega\ni z\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\Delta(z)&0\\
0&I_{\tilde{X}}\end{pmatrix}\in L(Y\oplus X)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\Omega\ni z\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}I_{\tilde{Z}}&0\\
0&zI-A\end{pmatrix}\in L(Z\oplus X_{A},Z\oplus X)

are equivalent on
\Omega
.
\lozenge

Recalling the definition of equivalence (
Section

2.2
), the above definition guarantees that
\mathcal{D}(\Delta(z))=\mathcal{Y}
is
z
-independent for all
z\in\Omega
, and there exist holomorphic closable operator-valued functions
E:\Omega\to L(Y\oplus X,Z\oplus X_{A})
and
F:\Omega\to L(Z\oplus X,Y\oplus X)
such that
E(z):\mathcal{Y}\oplus\tilde{X}\to\tilde{Z}\oplus\mathcal{D}(A)
and
\tilde{F}(z)=F(z)|_{\tilde{Z}\oplus\mathcal{R}(zI-A)}:\tilde{Z}\oplus\mathcal{R}(zI-A)\to\mathcal{R}(\Delta(z))\oplus\tilde{X}
are bijective, and there holds

\begin{pmatrix}\Delta(z)&0\\
0&I_{\tilde{X}}\end{pmatrix}=F(z)\begin{pmatrix}I_{\tilde{Z}}&0\\
0&zI-A\end{pmatrix}E(z),\quad\forall z\in\Omega.

(24)

The operator on the left-hand side of (
24
) is called an
(\tilde{X},X)
-extension
of
\Delta(z)
and the operator in the middle on the right-hand side of (
24
) is called an
(\tilde{Z},Z)
-extension
of
zI-A
.

{remark}

The necessity of working with dense linear subspaces
\tilde{X}\subseteq X
and
\tilde{Z}\subseteq Z
in the sequel stems from the dense range requirement in the definition of the spectrum of
A
given in
Section

2.1
. When
A
is closed, recall from
Section

2.1
that
\mathcal{R}(zI-A)=X
for every
z\in\rho(A)
. In this situation, the
(\tilde{X},X)
- and
(\tilde{Z},Z)
-extensions simply become the usual
X
- and
Z
-extensions, corresponding to the standard notion of
equivalence after extension
for closed linear operators, see
[
2
, Section 4.4]
.
\lozenge

Our next goal is to analyse the spectral properties of
A
through the characteristic operator
\Delta
. As shown in
Section

2.3
below, the relation (
24
) already implies a connection between the point spectrum of
A
and the characteristic values of
\Delta
. However, to fully describe the spectrum of
A
in terms of
\Delta
, it is necessary to relate
R(\cdot,A)
with
\Delta(\cdot)^{-1}
. The relation (
24
) alone does not suffice for this purpose, primarily because the operators
E
and
F
may not be bounded or defined on the entire spaces
Y\oplus X
and
Z\oplus X
, respectively. To overcome this issue, we formulate the following (spectral) hypothesis:

(SH3)

The values of the operator-valued functions
G_{\Delta}:\rho(\Delta)\to L(X)
and
G_{A}:\rho(A)\cap\Omega\to L(Y)
defined by

G_{\Delta}(z)\coloneqq\bigg[E(z)\begin{pmatrix}\Delta(z)^{-1}&0\\
0&I_{\tilde{X}}\end{pmatrix}F(z)\bigg]_{22},\quad G_{A}(z)\coloneqq\bigg[E(z)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}I_{\tilde{Z}}&0\\
0&R(z,A)\end{pmatrix}F(z)^{-1}\bigg]_{11},

are bounded, and their maximal domains lie dense in
X
and
Y
, respectively.

{theorem}

Let
\Delta
be a characteristic operator for
A
on
\Omega
. Then the following statements hold:

1.

The point spectrum of
A
in
\Omega
satisfies

\sigma_{p}(A)\cap\Omega=\{\sigma\in\Omega:\sigma\mbox{ is a characteristic value of }\Delta\}.

(25)

Furthermore, if
\sigma\in\sigma_{p}(A)\cap\Omega
, then
m_{g}(\sigma,A)=m_{g}(\Delta(\sigma)),m_{a}(\sigma,A)=m_{a}(\Delta(\sigma))
and
k(\sigma,A)=k(\Delta(\sigma))
. If in addition
\sigma
is of finite type, then the partial multiplicities of
A
at
\sigma
are equal to the zero multiplicities of
\Delta
at
\sigma
.

2.

If
\rho(\Delta)\neq\emptyset
and
(SH3)
holds, then the spectrum of
A
in
\Omega
satisfies

\sigma(A)\cap\Omega=\{\sigma\in\Omega:\Delta(\sigma)\mbox{ is not invertible}\}.

(26)

Furthermore, if
A
satisfies
(SH1)
and
\Delta
satisfies
(SH2)
, and
\sigma\in\sigma(A)\cap\Omega
is isolated, then
r(\sigma,A)=r(\Delta(\sigma))
. If in addition
\sigma\in\sigma_{p}(A)\cap\Omega
is of finite type and
k(\sigma,A)=r(\Delta(\sigma))
, then the multiplicity theorem holds:

\dim\mathcal{N}((\sigma I-A)^{r(\Delta(\sigma))})=m_{a}(\Delta(\sigma)).

(27)

Proof.

We start by proving the first statement. To prove (
25
), we show that there is a one-to-one correspondence between kernel vectors of
zI-A
and kernel vectors of
\Delta(z)
for
z\in\Omega
. Let us write

M(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta(z)&0\\
0&I_{\tilde{X}}\end{pmatrix},\quad L(z)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{\tilde{Z}}&0\\
0&zI-A\end{pmatrix},\quad\forall z\in\Omega,

and note that
\mathcal{D}(M(z))=\mathcal{Y}\oplus\tilde{X}
and
\mathcal{D}(L(z))=\tilde{Z}\oplus\mathcal{D}(A)
are
z
-independent. According to the proof of
Section

2.2
,
E(z)
maps
\mathcal{N}(M(z))=\mathcal{N}(\Delta(z))\oplus\{0\}
in a one-to-one way onto
\mathcal{N}(L(z))=\{0\}\oplus\mathcal{N}(zI-A)
. Hence, the closable holomorphic operator-valued function
N:\Omega\to L(Y,X)
with values
N(z):\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{D}(A)
defined by

N(z)q\coloneqq(0,I_{\mathcal{D}(A)})E(z)\begin{pmatrix}q\\
0\end{pmatrix},

maps
\mathcal{N}(\Delta(z))
in a one-to-one way onto
\mathcal{N}(zI-A)
, which proves the claim.

Let us now assume that
\sigma\in\sigma_{p}(A)\cap\Omega
. By the previous argument, there holds in particular
m_{g}(\Delta(\sigma))=m_{g}(\sigma,A)
. To prove the claim regarding the algebraic multiplicity and ascent, we first observe from the definition of
L
that
m_{a}(\sigma,A)=m_{a}(L(\sigma))
and
k(\sigma,A)=k(L(\sigma))
.
Section

2.2
tells us that
m_{a}(L(\sigma))=m_{a}(M(\sigma))
and
k(L(\sigma))=k(M(\sigma))
. From the definition of
M
we observe that
m_{a}(M(\sigma))=m_{a}(\Delta(\sigma))
and
k(M(\sigma))=k(\Delta(\sigma))
, which proves the claim.

Assume now in addition that
\sigma
is of finite type. Let
q_{1,0},\dots,q_{1,k_{1}-1},\dots,q_{p,0},\dots,q_{p,k_{p}-1}
, with
k_{1}\leq\dots\leq k_{p}
, be a canonical system of Jordan chains for
\Delta
at
\sigma
. Consider the function
Q_{i}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{Y}
defined by
Q_{i}(z)\coloneqq\sum_{l=0}^{k_{i}-1}(z-\sigma)^{l}q_{i,l}
and recall from
Section

2.2
that this map is a root function of
\Delta
corresponding to
\sigma
, that is

\Delta(z)Q_{i}(z)=\mathcal{O}((z-\sigma)^{k_{i}}),\quad\forall i=1,\dots,p.

(28)

Since
N
and
Q_{i}
are holomorphic on
\Omega
, the map
\Phi_{i}:\Omega\to\mathcal{D}(A)
defined by
\Phi_{i}(z)\coloneqq N(z)Q_{i}(z)
is holomorphic as well. As a consequence, this map is analytic and thus there exist vectors
\varphi_{i,0},\dots,\varphi_{i,k_{i-1}}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
such that
\Phi_{i}(z)=\sum_{l=0}^{k_{i}-1}(z-\sigma)^{l}\varphi_{i,l}+\mathcal{O}((z-\sigma)^{k_{i}})
. The equivalence (
24
) together with (
28
) proves that
(zI-A)\Phi_{i}(z)=\mathcal{O}((z-\sigma)^{k_{i}})
and thus

(A-\sigma I)\varphi_{i,0}=0,\dots,(A-\sigma I)\varphi_{i,k_{i}-1}=\varphi_{i,k_{i}-2}.

(29)

Since
N(\sigma)
maps
\mathcal{N}(\Delta(\sigma))
in a one-to-one way onto
\mathcal{N}(\sigma I-A)
, it follows that
\varphi_{1,0},\dots,\varphi_{p,0}
are linearly independent. Then
\varphi_{1,0},\dots,\varphi_{1,k_{1}-1},\dots,\varphi_{p,0},\dots,\varphi_{p,k_{p}-1}
are all linearly independent due to (
29
), which proves that
\varphi_{1,0},\dots,\varphi_{1,k_{1}-1},\dots,\varphi_{p,0},\dots,\varphi_{p,k_{p}-1}
is a canonical basis of (generalized) eigenvectors of
A
at
\sigma
, which proves the claim.

Let us now prove the second statement. Therefore, assume that
\rho(\Delta)\neq\emptyset
and
(SH3)
holds. To prove (
26
), we show that
\rho(A)\cap\Omega=\rho(\Delta)
.
Fix
z\in\rho(\Delta)\neq\emptyset
. By the previous argument,
zI-A
is also injective and so we can consider the algebraic inverse of
zI-A:\mathcal{D}(A)\to\mathcal{R}(zI-A)
. Although we have not proven at this point that
\mathcal{R}(zI-A)
is dense in
X
, we will abuse notation and call
R(z,A)
for a moment the algebraic inverse of
zI-A
. The equivalence (
24
) then implies that

\begin{pmatrix}I_{\tilde{Z}}&0\\
0&R(z,A)\end{pmatrix}=E(z)\begin{pmatrix}\Delta(z)^{-1}&0\\
0&I_{\tilde{X}}\end{pmatrix}F(z).

(30)

But from here we see that
R(z,A)=G_{\Delta}(z)
and hence it follows from
(SH3)
that indeed the domain
\mathcal{D}(R(z,A))=\mathcal{R}(zI-A)
is dense in
X
and that
R(z,A)
is bounded. This proves that
z\in\rho(A)\cap\Omega
and thus
\rho(\Delta)\subseteq\rho(A)\cap\Omega
.
To prove the other inclusion, let
z\in\rho(A)\cap\Omega
be given. Equation (
30
) tells us that
\Delta(z)^{-1}=G_{A}(z)
and due to
(SH3)
,
\Delta(z)^{-1}
is bounded and
\mathcal{R}(\Delta(z))
is dense in
Y
. We conclude that
z\in\rho(\Delta)
, which proves the claim.

Assume now in addition that
\sigma\in\sigma(A)\cap\Omega
is isolated and that
(SH1)
and
(SH2)
hold. Since
\rho(A)
and
\Omega
are open in
\mathbb{C}
(
Section

2.1
), it follows from the proof of the previous statement that
\rho(\Delta)=\rho(A)\cap\Omega
is open in
\mathbb{C}
. As
\Delta(\cdot)^{-1}=G_{A}
and the operator-valued functions
E(\cdot)^{-1},F(\cdot)^{-1}
and
R(\cdot,A)
are holomorphic on
\rho(A)\cap\Omega
due to
Section

2.2
and
Section

2.1
, it clear that

Finally, if we assume in addition that

It turns out in

{lemma}

3
General scheme for constructing characteristic operators

The main aim of this section is to develop a general scheme for constructing characteristic operators
\Delta
for a broad class of closable linear operators
\mathcal{A}
that arise in many periodic evolutionary systems. This rather technical construction will be carried out in
Section

3.1
and
Section

3.2
. In
Section

3.3
we study periodic spectral properties of characteristic operators as this allows us, under certain conditions, to reduce the spectral problem for
\mathcal{A}
on
\Omega
in terms of
\Delta
even further.

3.1
Construction of
\Delta
in terms of
\mathcal{A}

Let
X
and
Y
be complex Banach spaces. In the applications presented in
Section

4
, the space
X
will serve as the
state space
of the periodic evolutionary system of interest, whereas
Y
will represent its associated
reduction space
. For
T\geq 0
and
Z\in\{X,Y\}
, we consider a complex Banach space

\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Z)
consisting of
Z
-valued
T
-periodic functions on
\mathbb{R}
belonging to a specified function class
\mathcal{F}
. The case of
T=0
will be important when we show that the class of autonomous evolutionary systems is included in our construction, see
Section

4.1
for more information. The general idea of the construction of a characteristic operator
\Delta
is to reduce a rather difficult spectral problem for a closable linear operator
\mathcal{A}\in L(\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X))
towards a much simpler problem formulated in terms of a closable operator-valued function
\Delta:\Omega\to L(\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y))
. For example, in the setting of periodic linear classical DDEs (
Section

4.1
), we choose the state space
X=C([-h,0],\mathbb{C}^{n})
and the reduction space
Y=\mathbb{C}^{n}
. This shows that the spectral problem reduces from a problem in

C_{T}(\mathbb{R},C([-h,0],\mathbb{C}^{n}))
to a simpler one in
C_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
. To do so, we first construct a characteristic operator
\Delta
associated with a broad class of closable linear operators
\mathcal{A}
of the form (
33
), culminating in
Section

3.2
. This construction allows us to invoke
Section

2.3
for the resulting equivalence, thereby establishing a connection between the spectral properties of
\mathcal{A}
and
\Delta
, as detailed in
Section

3.2
. In
Section

4
, we illustrate how this reduction greatly facilitates the spectral analysis of a wide class of periodic evolutionary systems.

For the construction, consider a closable linear operator
D:\mathcal{D}(D)\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
satisfying the following conditions:

(H1)

There exists a linear subspace
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y)
and a bounded bijective linear map
\iota:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{N}(D)\neq\{0\}
that has bounded (algebraic) inverse.

(H2)

The operator
D
has a restriction
D_{0}:\mathcal{D}(D_{0})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
with bounded (algebraic) inverse such that
\Omega\coloneqq\rho(D_{0})\neq\emptyset
and

\mathcal{D}(D)=\mathcal{N}(D)\oplus\mathcal{D}(D_{0}).

(32)

(H3)

The domain
\mathcal{D}(D)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(zI-D_{0})
for all
z\in\Omega
.

Note that
D_{0}
is closable since it admits
\overline{D}
as a closed linear extension. Moreover,
(H2)
and
(H3)
guarantee that
\mathcal{D}(D_{0})\subseteq\mathcal{R}(zI-D_{0})
for all
z\in\Omega
, which shows that
D_{0}
satisfies
(SH1)
. The following result will be helpful in the upcoming construction.

{lemma}

The following statements hold:

1.

\mathcal{R}(z_{1}I-D_{0})=\mathcal{R}(z_{2}I-D_{0})
for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in\Omega
.

2.

\mathcal{R}(zI-D_{0})=\mathcal{R}(zI-D)
for all
z\in\Omega\cup\{0\}
.

Proof.

The first statement follows directly from
Section

2.1
since
(SH1)
holds for
D_{0}
.

To prove the second statement, we first recall that
D_{0}
is a restriction of
D
, from where it follows that
\mathcal{R}(zI-D_{0})\subseteq\mathcal{R}(zI-D)
for all
z\in\Omega\cup\{0\}
.
To prove the converse inclusion, fix
z\in\Omega\cup\{0\}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{R}(zI-D)
. Then there exists a
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D)
such that
(zI-D)\varphi=\psi
. According to
(H2)
, we can write
\varphi=\tilde{\varphi}+\varphi_{0}
, where
(\tilde{\varphi},\varphi_{0})\in\mathcal{N}(D)\oplus\mathcal{D}(D_{0})
, and therefore
\psi=(zI-D)(\tilde{\varphi}+\varphi_{0})=z\tilde{\varphi}+(zI-D_{0})\varphi_{0}
. If
z\neq 0
, recall from
(H3)
that
\tilde{\varphi}\in\mathcal{N}(D)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(zI-D_{0})
, and so it is clear that
\psi\in\mathcal{R}(zI-D_{0})
. If
z=0
, then
\psi=-D_{0}\varphi_{0}
and thus
\psi\in\mathcal{R}(-D_{0})
, which proves the claim.
∎

Before we proceed, let us remark that
0\notin\Omega
only occurs when
\mathcal{R}(D_{0})
is not dense in
\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
. Nonetheless, this potential technicality does not interfere with the upcoming construction and, as we will see,
0\in\Omega
for all the examples provided in
Section

4
.

To develop the general scheme for characteristic operators, we introduce two linear operators

K:\mathcal{D}(K)\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y),\quad M:\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y),

where
K
is closable and
M
is bounded. We additionally make the following assumptions on
K
:

(H4)

The domain
\mathcal{D}(K)\supseteq\mathcal{D}(D)
and the restriction
K|_{\mathcal{D}(D_{0})}:\mathcal{D}(D_{0})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y)
is bounded.

To
D,K
and
M
, we associate two linear operators
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
and
\hat{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{D}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})\to\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
defined by

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\{\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X):\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D),\ MD\varphi=K\varphi\},\quad\mathcal{A}\varphi\coloneqq D\varphi,

(33)

and

\mathcal{D}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})\coloneqq\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}q\\
\varphi\end{pmatrix}\in\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X):\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D),\ q=M\varphi\bigg\},\quad\hat{\mathcal{A}}\begin{pmatrix}q\\
\varphi\end{pmatrix}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}K\varphi\\
D\varphi\end{pmatrix},

(34)

where
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\coloneqq{\mathcal{F}}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y)\oplus{\mathcal{F}}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
.
We will refer to
\mathcal{A}
and
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
as the
first
and
second operator
associated with
D,K
and
M
, respectively. Moreover, we point out that in the context of (
33
), we can think of

and

as generalized boundary-value operators.

{lemma}

The linear operators
\mathcal{A}
and
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
are closable.

Proof.

Clearly,
\mathcal{A}
is closable since it admits
\overline{D}
as a closed linear extension. To prove that
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
is closable, we use the characterization from
Appendix

A
. Let
((q_{m},\varphi_{m}))_{m}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{D}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})
converging in norm to zero and assume that
(\hat{\mathcal{A}}(q_{m},\varphi_{m}))_{m}
converges in norm to some
(p,\psi)\in\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
. Then (
34
) tells us that
K\varphi_{m}\to p
and
D\varphi_{m}\to\psi
as
m\to\infty
. Since
K
and
D
are closable,
p
and
\psi
must be zero, which proves the claim.
∎

Next, we introduce a candidate for the characteristic operator
\Delta:\Omega\to L(\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y))
of the closable linear operator
\mathcal{A}
from (
33
). Define the linear operator
\Delta(z):\mathcal{D}(\Delta(z))\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y)\to\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Y)
with

-independent domain
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{D}(\Delta(z))
from
(H1)
by

\Delta(z)\coloneqq-(zM-K)D_{0}R(z,D_{0})\iota,\quad\forall z\in\Omega.

(35)

To validate that
\Delta
is a well-defined holomorphic closable operator-valued function (
Section

3.1
), we first need the following important result.

{lemma}

The closable linear operator
Q(z):\mathcal{D}(Q(z))\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
defined by

\mathcal{D}(Q(z))\coloneqq\mathcal{D}(D),\quad Q(z)\coloneqq I-R(z,D_{0})(zI-D),\quad\forall z\in\Omega\cup\{0\},

(36)

is a projection. Moreover,
Q(z)\iota:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{N}(zI-D)
is a bounded linear operator with bounded (algebraic) inverse, which can be represented by

Q(z)\iota=\iota-zR(z,D_{0})\iota,\qquad[Q(z)\iota]^{-1}=\iota^{-1}Q(0)|_{\mathcal{N}(zI-D)},\quad\forall z\in\Omega\cup\{0\}.

(37)

Proof.

First, note that the map
Q(z)
is well-defined for all
z\in\Omega\cup\{0\}
due to
(H2)
and
Section

3.1
. Second, since
I=I_{\mathcal{D}(D)}
and

are bounded linear operators for all

due to
(H2)
, and

is a closable linear operator, it follows from
Appendix

A
and
Appendix

A
that
Q(z)
is a closable linear operator. Third, recalling the resolvent identity (
14
) on
D_{0}
and using
(H2)
, a straightforward but rather lengthy calculation shows that

Q(z_{1})=Q(z_{1})Q(z_{2}),\qquad\forall\,z_{1},z_{2}\in\Omega\cup\{0\}.

(38)

Next, fix
z\in\Omega\cup\{0\}
and take
z_{1}=z_{2}=z
in (
38
) so that
Q(z)^{2}=Q(z)
, i.e.
Q(z)
is a projection. Consequently, we can directly compute that its kernel and range are given by

\mathcal{N}(Q(z))=\mathcal{D}(D_{0}),\quad\mathcal{R}(Q(z))=\mathcal{N}(zI-D).

(39)

From here, it follows from
[
65
, Equation (4.8-2)]
that

\mathcal{D}(D)=\mathcal{N}(zI-D)\oplus\mathcal{D}(D_{0}),\quad\forall z\in\Omega\cup\{0\}.

which we interpret as a
z

D
of the direct sum decomposition (
32

In the sequel, we will often need the action of

on the range of
\iota
. Since
\mathcal{R}(\iota)=\mathcal{N}(D)
due to
(H1)
, we can directly compute from (
36

Q(z)\iota
is given by (
37

and

are bounded for

).

It follows from (

as mapping into
\mathcal{N}(zI-D)

Q(z)\iota=(I-zR(z,D_{0}))\iota=[(zI-D_{0})R(z,D_{0})-zR(z,D_{0})]\iota=-D_{0}R(z,D_{0})\iota,

(40)

The right-hand side is a composition of injective maps, from where it follows that

is injective as well. To show that

is surjective, let

be given and set

. We conclude that

, and hence

is given by (

Q(0)\varphi=\varphi-D_{0}^{-1}D\varphi=(I-zD_{0}^{-1})\varphi,\quad\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{N}(zI-D).

Recalling that

is invertible with bounded inverse
\varepsilon_{-k}

{lemma}

If

Proof.

∎

{proposition}

The maps

4
Applications to classes of periodic evolutionary systems

This section discusses three classes of periodic linear functional differential equations, for which the characteristic operator constructed in
Section

2
will provide us information on the Floquet exponents. All classes of periodic linear functional differential equations considered in this section can be written in the form

\dot{x}(t)=L(t)x_{t},\quad t\geq s,

(FDE)

where
x(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
denotes a starting time and the
history

x_{t}
is defined by
x_{t}(\theta):=x(t+\theta)
for all
\theta\in I
, where
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a (given) closed interval. This setting indicates that the state space
X
should be chosen as a function space of the form
\mathcal{F}(I,\mathbb{C}^{n})
, while the reduction space
Y=\mathbb{C}^{n}
is given by the codomain of
X
. Furthermore, the map
\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto L(t)\in\mathcal{L}(X,\mathbb{C}^{n})
is assumed to be
T
-periodic for some (minimal)
T\geq 0
, whereas the setting of
T=0
will be important when we show that the class of autonomous FDEs is included in our construction (
Section

4.1
). Since we are dealing with derivatives in (
FDE
), we have to assume in addition that
X
admits a linear subspace
\mathcal{F}^{1}(I,\mathbb{C}^{n})
consisting of functions that admit a derivative in
X
. The type of derivative depends on the context, choice and underlying topology of the function space
X
. For example, when one works with the space of continuous functions
X=C(I,\mathbb{C}^{n})
, then
\mathcal{F}^{1}(I,\mathbb{C}^{n})=C^{1}(I,\mathbb{C}^{n})
denotes the space of continuously differentiable functions (strong derivative). Likewise, when one works with
X=L^{p}(I,\mathbb{C}^{n})
for some
1\leq p\leq\infty
, then
\mathcal{F}^{1}(I,\mathbb{C}^{n})=W^{1,p}(I,\mathbb{C}^{n})
denotes a Sobolev space (weak derivative). The regularity of the map
t\mapsto L(t)
is closely related to the choice of
\mathcal{F}
and will be made clear in each upcoming example. Regarding the choice of
\mathcal{F}
, we only impose the regularity condition that for each fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}
the exponential map
(\theta\mapsto e^{z\theta})\in\mathcal{F}^{1}(I,\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

We call
\sigma\in\mathbb{C}
a
Floquet exponent
(of (
FDE
)) if there exists a nonzero function
v
of the form
v(t)=e^{\sigma t}q(t)
with
q\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
that solves (
FDE
). Hence, our problem of interest is equivalent to finding a
Floquet pair

(\sigma,q)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{F}_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
satisfying the periodic linear FDE

\dot{q}(t)+\sigma q(t)-L(t)[\theta\mapsto e^{\sigma\theta}q(t+\theta)]=0,\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

(59)

The function
v
is called a
Floquet (eigen)solution
associated to the Floquet exponent
\sigma
. In the presence of a (strongly continuous) forward evolutionary system
U\coloneqq\{U(t,s)\}_{t\geq s}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(X)
for (
FDE
), one can prove that this notion of a Floquet exponent is equivalent to the notion of a Floquet exponent in terms of the
s
-independent spectral values
\lambda=e^{\sigma T}
of the
monodromy operator

U(s+T,s)
, see
[
27
, Lemma 8.1.2]
for a proof in the setting of classical DDEs (
Section

4.1
). For systems that do not generate a forward evolutionary system (on
X
), such as mixed functional differential equations (MFDEs) (
Section

4.3
), one can still look for Floquet exponents and solutions by finding Floquet pairs
(\sigma,q)
satisfying (
59
). Therefore, the definition of Floquet exponents in terms of (
59
) is applicable and usable in a wider class of equations than the approach via
U
. To resemble a part of the abstract results from
Section

3.3
on
\frac{2\pi i}{T}\mathbb{Z}
-similarity, the following results shows that the Floquet exponents of (
FDE
) are indeed uniquely determined up to additive multiples of
\frac{2\pi i}{T}
.

{proposition}

If
\sigma\in\mathbb{C}
is a Floquet exponent, then
\sigma+\frac{2\pi i}{T}k
is a Floquet exponent for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a Floquet exponent, then
t\mapsto e^{\sigma t}q(t)
is a solution to (
FDE
) where
q\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
be given and consider the map
q_{k}\in\mathcal{F}_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
given by
q_{k}(t)=e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{T}kt}q(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the function
t\mapsto e^{(\sigma+\frac{2\pi i}{T}k)t}q_{k}(t)=e^{\sigma t}q(t)
is a solution to (
FDE
), meaning that
\sigma+\frac{2\pi i}{T}k
is a Floquet exponent.
∎

The central result of this section is to establish the characterization

\sigma(\mathcal{A})\cap\Omega=\{\sigma\in\Omega:\sigma\text{ is a characteristic value of }\Delta\}=\{\sigma\in\Omega:\sigma\text{ is a Floquet exponent}\},

(60)

for three representative subclasses of (
FDE
), namely classical DDEs (
Section

4.1
), iDDEs (
Section

4.2
) and MFDEs (
Section

4.3
). Here,
\Omega=\mathbb{C}
for classical DDEs and MFDEs, while for iDDEs it is a right half-plane in
\mathbb{C}
containing the origin. In each case, the set
\Omega
is sufficiently large to allow the application of the principle of linearized stability and to study local bifurcations. Moreover, a consequence of
Section

4
and (
60
) is that it is sufficient to search for spectral values of
\mathcal{A}
, or equivalently for characteristic values of
\Delta
within the horizontal strip
\{z\in\Omega:\imaginary(z)\in(-\frac{\pi}{T},\frac{\pi}{T}]\}
. To prove (
60
), we proceed in four steps. First, we show that
\Delta
is a closed characteristic operator for
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
on
\Omega
(
Section

3.2
). Second, we verify that
\Delta
satisfies
(H6)
. Third, combining these two key results, we conclude that
\Delta
is a characteristic operator for
\mathcal{A}
on
\Omega
(
Section

3.2
). Finally, we apply
Section

3.2
and
Section

2.3
to establish (
60
).

4.1
Classical delay differential equations

In the setting of classical delay differential equations (DDEs),
the state space
X
is the Banach space
C([-h,0],\mathbb{C}^{n})
equipped with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. The (maximal) delay
h>0
is assumed to be finite, and the map
L
is assumed to be in
C_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{L}(X,\mathbb{C}^{n}))
. It follows from a vector-valued version of the Riesz representation theorem that, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the operator
L(t)
can be represented uniquely as a Riemann-Stieltjes integral

L(t)\varphi=\int_{0}^{h}d_{2}\zeta(t,\theta)\varphi(-\theta).

(61)

Here, the map
\zeta:\mathbb{R}\times[0,h]\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is a matrix-valued function, and
\zeta(\cdot,\theta)
is continuous and
T
-periodic for all
\theta\in[0,h]
. Moreover,
\zeta(t,\cdot)
is of bounded variation, right-continuous on the open interval
(0,h)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and normalized by the requirement
\zeta(\cdot,0)=0
. The notation
d_{2}\zeta
reflects that in the computation of the Riemann-Stieltjes integral, we integrate with respect to the second variable of
\zeta
. We also write
d_{2}\zeta
in front of the integrand since
\zeta
is matrix-valued. For any starting time
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the initial value problem

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=L(t)x_{t},\quad&t\geq s,\\
x_{s}=\varphi,\quad&\varphi\in X,\end{cases}

(62)

admits a unique solution
x
on
[s,\infty)
that is generated by a strongly continuous forward evolutionary system
U
by
x_{t}=U(t,s)\varphi
for all
t\geq s
, see
[
16
, Theorem XII.2.7 and XII.3.1]
. The associated (generalized) generator of
U
(at time
t\in\mathbb{R}
) is given by the closed linear operator
A(t):\mathcal{D}(A(t))\subseteq X\to X
with domain and action

\mathcal{D}(A(t))=\{\varphi\in C^{1}([-h,0],\mathbb{C}^{n}):\varphi^{\prime}(0)=L(t)\varphi\},\quad A(t)\varphi=\varphi^{\prime},

see also
[
49
,
8
]
for more information. When we take the derivative with respect to the state variable of a function
\varphi\in C^{1}([-h,0],\mathbb{C}^{n})
, we denote this by an accent, i.e.
\varphi^{\prime}\in X
. However, when we take the derivative with respect to the time variable of a function
\varphi\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X)
, we denote this by a dot, i.e.
\dot{\varphi}\in C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
. To this operator, let us recall from (
5
) that we associate the linear operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
defined by

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=\{\varphi\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X):\varphi(t)\in\mathcal{D}(A(t))\mbox{ for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$}\},\quad(\mathcal{A}\varphi)(t)=A(t)\varphi(t)-\dot{\varphi}(t).

(63)

As we will show in
Section

4.1
, the operator
\mathcal{A}
from (
63
) coincides with the closable linear operator
\mathcal{A}
introduced in (
33
) from
Section

3
. The purpose of this section is to construct a characteristic operator
\Delta
for
\mathcal{A}
and, on this basis, to establish several properties of its spectrum. In particular, we will prove that the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}
consists solely of isolated eigenvalues of finite type and satisfies (
60
). To do so, we first construct operators
D,K
and
M
so that
\mathcal{A}
is the first operator associated to
D,K
and
M
.

{lemma}

Let
D:\mathcal{D}(D)\to C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
be the operator defined by

\mathcal{D}(D)=\{\varphi\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X):\varphi(t)^{\prime}\in X\mbox{ for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$}\},\quad(D\varphi)(t)=\varphi(t)^{\prime}-\dot{\varphi}(t).

(64)

Then
D
is a closable linear operator satisfying
(H1)
,
(H2)
,
(H3)
and
(H5)
with
\Omega=\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, define the linear operators
K:\mathcal{D}(K)\subseteq C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to C_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
M:C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to C_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
by

\mathcal{D}(K)=C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X),\qquad(K\varphi)(t)=L(t)\varphi(t)-\dot{\varphi}(t)(0),\qquad(M\varphi)(t)=\varphi(t)(0),

(65)

then
K
satisfies
(H4)
, and
\mathcal{A}
defined in (
63
) is the first operator associated to
D,K
and
M
.

Proof.

Put
I=[-h,0],Y=\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
X=C([-h,0],\mathbb{C}^{n})
in the construction of
Section

3.1
. We first show that the operator
D
is closable and satisfies the hypotheses
(H1)
,
(H2)
,
(H3)
and
(H5)
. To show that the linear operator
D
is closable, let
(\varphi_{m})_{m}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{D}(D)
converging in norm to zero and assume that the sequence
(D\varphi_{m})_{m}
converges in norm to some
\psi\in C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
. We have to show that
\psi=0
. Set
\psi_{m}=D\varphi_{m}
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and notice that this equation is equivalent to

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\varphi_{m}(t)(\theta)-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi_{m}(t)(\theta)=\psi_{m}(t)(\theta),\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\ \theta\in[-h,0].

This is an inhomogeneous transport equation that has the solution

\varphi_{m}(t)(\theta)=\varphi_{m}(t+\theta)(0)+\int_{0}^{\theta}\psi_{m}(t+\theta-s)(s)ds.

Since the convergence of
\varphi_{m}\to 0
and
\psi_{m}\to\psi
is uniform in the first and second variable, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\theta}\psi(t+\theta-s)(s)ds&=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\theta}\psi_{m}(t+\theta-s)(s)ds\\
&=\lim_{m\to\infty}[\varphi_{m}(t)(\theta)-\varphi_{m}(t+\theta)(0)]=0,\end{split}

(66)

where in the last step we used that
\varphi_{m}\to 0
uniformly as
m\to\infty
. Let
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[-h,0]
with
\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{2}
be given. If
t_{2}=t_{1}+\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}
, then according to (
66
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}\psi(t_{1}+\theta_{1}-s)(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\theta_{1}}\psi(t_{1}+\theta_{1}-s)(s)ds+\int_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}\psi(t_{1}+\theta_{1}-s)(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\theta_{2}}\psi(t_{1}+\theta_{1}-s)(s)ds=\int_{0}^{\theta_{2}}\psi(t_{2}+\theta_{2}-s)(s)ds=0

since the second and last integral vanish due to (
66
). Differentiating the left-hand side with respect to
\theta_{2}
yields
\psi(t_{2})(\theta_{2})=0
for all
\theta_{2}\in[-h,0]
. Since
t_{1}
and
\theta_{1}
, and thus
t_{2}
, were arbitrary, we conclude that
\psi=0
, i.e.
D
is closable.

To verify that the operator
D
satisfies
(H1)
, we first prove that
\mathcal{N}(D)\neq\{0\}
. The kernel of
D
consists of functions
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D)
satisfying the transport equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi(t)(\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\varphi(t)(\theta),\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\ \theta\in[-h,0],

which is equivalent to saying that
\varphi
has the translation property
\varphi(t)(\theta)=\varphi(t+\theta)(0)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta\in[-h,0]
. Hence

\mathcal{N}(D)=\{\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D):\varphi(t)(\theta)=\varphi(t+\theta)(0)\mbox{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}\mbox{ and }\theta\in[-h,0]\}\neq\{0\}.

Moreover, if we set
\mathcal{Y}=C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})

and define the linear operator
\iota:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{N}(D)
by

(\iota q)(t)(\theta)=q(t+\theta),

(67)

then
\iota
is clearly a bijection with (algebraic) inverse
\iota^{-1}:\mathcal{N}(D)\to\mathcal{Y}
given by
(\iota^{-1}\varphi)(t)=\varphi(t)(0)
. Clearly,
\iota
and
\iota^{-1}
are bounded and thus
(H1)
is satisfied.

Our next aim is to verify that
(H2)
holds with
\Omega=\mathbb{C}\ni 0
. Therefore, let us first introduce the linear operator
D_{0}:\mathcal{D}(D_{0})\to C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
by

\mathcal{D}(D_{0})=\{\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D):\varphi(t)(0)=0\mbox{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}\},\quad D_{0}\varphi=D\varphi,

and recall from the argument detailed above
Section

3.1
that
D_{0}
is closable. We claim that
\mathcal{D}(D)=\mathcal{N}(D)\oplus\mathcal{D}(D_{0})
. Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{N}(D)\cap\mathcal{D}(D_{0})
, then
\varphi(t)(\theta)=\varphi(t+\theta)(0)=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta\in[-h,0]
which proves that
\varphi=0
and thus
\mathcal{N}(D)\cap\mathcal{D}(D_{0})=\{0\}
. Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D)
be given and define
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(D)
by
\psi(t)(\theta)=\varphi(t)(\theta)-\varphi(t+\theta)(0)
. Then
\psi(\cdot)(0)=0
and so
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(D_{0})
. Define now
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(D)
by
\phi(t)(\theta)=\varphi(t+\theta)(0)
, then
\phi\in\mathcal{N}(D)
and since
\varphi=\psi+\phi
, the claim is proven. To verify that
\rho(D_{0})=\mathbb{C}\neq\emptyset
, we start by studying solutions of

(zI-D_{0})\varphi=\psi

(68)

with
z\in\mathbb{C},\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D_{0})
and
\psi\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X)
. Notice that (
68

\bigg(z+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\bigg)\varphi(t)(\theta)=\psi(t)(\theta),\quad\varphi(t)(0)=0,\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\ \theta\in[-h,0],

which has the unique solution

\varphi(t)(\theta)=\int_{\theta}^{0}e^{z(\theta-s)}\psi(t+\theta-s)(s)ds,

(69)

if we can prove that
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D_{0})
. To show this, let us first observe that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\varphi}(t)(\theta)&=\int_{\theta}^{0}e^{z(\theta-s)}\dot{\psi}(t+\theta-s)(s)ds,\\
\varphi(t)^{\prime}(\theta)&=-\psi(t)(\theta)+\int_{\theta}^{0}e^{z(\theta-s)}[z\psi(t+\theta-s)(s)+\dot{\psi}(t+\theta-s)(s)]ds,\end{split}

(70)

where the last expression follows from the Leibniz integral rule. Since

, the first equality in (

\varphi\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X)
and the second equality in (

\varphi(t)^{\prime}\in X
for all

, which shows that
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D_{0})
. As a consequence,
C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(zI-D_{0})\subseteq C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
and so

has dense range. In
Appendix

for further details. Moreover, for every

has a densely defined linear inverse given by

[R(z,D_{0})\varphi](t)(\theta)=\int_{\theta}^{0}e^{z(\theta-s)}\varphi(t+\theta-s)(s)ds,\quad\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{R}(zI-D_{0}),

(71)

and this operator is bounded, as it holds that

(72)

where

is understood as its limiting value
h
whenever

. As

, we have that

is not only an (algebraic) inverse of

, but in fact an inverse of

. We conclude that

holds.

It follows directly from (

for all

While

3.2
is proven after the introduction of

, the formula (

\mathcal{R}(zI-\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{R}(zI-D_{0})

\mathcal{D}(A)=\{\varphi\in C^{1}([-h,0],\mathbb{C}^{n}):\varphi^{\prime}(0)=L\varphi\}
, action

and is a closed linear operator, see
[

, Theorem I.1.4]
. Using this identification, the closable linear operator

from
Section

4.1
reduces to
\mathcal{D}(D)=C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n})
with action

as the derivative with respect to time vanishes. Hence,

becomes a closed linear operator and there holds:
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{D}(A)
and
\mathcal{D}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})=\mathcal{D}(\hat{A})
, with action
\mathcal{A}\varphi=A\varphi
and
\hat{\mathcal{A}}\varphi=\hat{A}\varphi
, where

\mathcal{D}(\hat{A})=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}c\\
\varphi\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus X:\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D),\ c=\varphi(0)\bigg\},\quad\hat{A}\begin{pmatrix}c\\
\varphi\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}K\varphi\\
D\varphi\end{pmatrix}.

The bounded linear operator
M
and closed linear operator

now become bounded linear operators on

given by
M\varphi=\varphi(0)
and
K\varphi=L\varphi
, as
\dot{\varphi}(t)(0)=0
since

is constant. Thus, the operator-valued function

reduces to a matrix-valued function

of the form

\Delta(z)=zI-\int_{0}^{h}d\zeta(\theta)e^{-z\theta},

which is exactly the characteristic matrix found in

, Section II.1.1]
. With a similar argumentation, one can also verify that the functions

and

, and the resolvent

reduce to the expressions found in
[

, Section II.1.1]
. This reduction also holds for the results in the upcoming applications, see
[

, Section II.1.3]

[

, Section 4]
for MFDEs.

, a similar reduction can be carried out. As recalled in

, several other characteristic matrices have been constructed in this setting. A natural question is how these characteristic matrices relate to the reduced characteristic operator introduced in this section. We expect that these matrices are somehow connected to

4.2
Delay differential equations with infinite delay

For delay differential equations with infinite delay (iDDE), it is not obvious how to choose a state space
X
, since the ‘natural candidate’
C(\mathbb{R}_{-},\mathbb{C}^{n})
is not a Banach space with respect to the standard associated supremum norm. Indeed, the choice of state space has been the topic of extensive study in the literature, see for example the articles
[
56
,
31
,
29
,
31
]
that identify criteria which a state space should satisfy in order to allow for classical results on existence, uniqueness and (in)stability of solutions. In
[
15
]
, the authors choose the Banach space
X:=C_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}_{-},\mathbb{C}^{n})
with the weighted norm
\|\cdot\|_{\rho}
defined by

C_{\rho}(\mathbb{R}_{-},\mathbb{C}^{n})\coloneqq\bigg\{\varphi\in C(\mathbb{R_{-}},\mathbb{C}^{n}):\lim_{\theta\to-\infty}e^{\rho\theta}|\varphi(\theta)|=0\bigg\},\quad\|\varphi\|_{\rho}\coloneqq\sup_{\theta\leq 0}e^{\rho\theta}|\varphi(\theta)|

(83)

for some fixed
\rho>0
. With this choice of state space, the authors of
[
15
]
proved the principle of linearized stability, and argue that the center manifold and Hopf bifurcation theorem can also be formulated and proved (along the same lines of
[
16
]
or
[
52
]
). All this suggests that
X
is a suitable choice of state space for our characteristic operator framework, and we shall adopt this space throughout the remainder of this section. For more general state spaces to which our results also apply, we refer to
Section

4.2
at the end of this subsection. Regardless of the chosen state space, the solution operator
U(\cdot,s)
cannot in general be expected to become eventually compact (as was the case in
Section

4.1
), since the state necessarily contains a part of the initial function defined on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
. In this context, it is therefore noteworthy that the characteristic operator framework allows us to prove that the Floquet exponents are isolated and of finite type in a given right half-plane in
\mathbb{C}
containing the origin, see
Section

4.2
.

To state and prove our results, we will assume that
L\in C_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{L}(X,\mathbb{C}^{n}))
. A vector-valued version of the Riesz representation theorem tells us that for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
the operator
L(t)
can be represented uniquely as

L(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d_{2}\mu(t,\theta)\varphi(-\theta).

Here, the map
\mu:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is a matrix-valued function, and
\mu(\cdot,\theta)
is continuous and
T
-periodic for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Moreover,
\mu(t,\cdot)
is a Borel measure satisfying
\|\mu(t,\cdot)\|_{\rho}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho s}d|\mu(t,\cdot)|(s)<\infty
, where
|\mu(t,\cdot)|
denotes the total variation of
\mu(t,\cdot)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. With this choice of state space, the initial value problem

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=L(t)x_{t},\quad&t\geq s,\\
x_{s}=\varphi,\quad&\varphi\in X,\end{cases}

(84)

is well-posed on
X
, and the family of operators
U\coloneqq\{U(t,s)\}_{t\geq s}
defined through
x_{t}=U(t,s)\varphi
for all
t\geq s
forms a strongly continuous forward evolutionary system on
X
. The associated (generalized) generator of
U
(at time
t\in\mathbb{R}
) is given by the closed linear operator
A(t):\mathcal{D}(A(t))\subseteq X\to X
with domain and action

\mathcal{D}(A(t))=\{\varphi\in C_{\rho}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{-},\mathbb{C}^{n}):\varphi^{\prime}(0)=L(t)\varphi\},\quad A(t)\varphi=\varphi^{\prime}.

To this operator, we associate the linear operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)\to C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
defined by

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=\{\varphi\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X):\varphi(t)\in\mathcal{D}(A(t))\mbox{ for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$}\},\quad(\mathcal{A}\varphi)(t)=A(t)\varphi(t)-\dot{\varphi}(t).

(85)

Due to the lack of eventual compactness of
U(\cdot,s)
, it turns out that we can only characterize the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}
in terms of
\Delta
in the right half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{-\rho}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\real(z)>-\rho\}
,
see
[
40
, Section II.1.3]
and
[
15
, Section 4]
for the same issue occurring in autonomous iDDEs.

{lemma}

Let
D:\mathcal{D}(D)\to C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
be the operator defined by

\mathcal{D}(D)=\{\varphi\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X):\varphi(t)^{\prime}\in X\mbox{ for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$}\},\quad(D\varphi)(t)=\varphi(t)^{\prime}-\dot{\varphi}(t).

Then
D
is a closable linear operator satisfying
(H1)
,
(H2)
,
(H3)
and
(H5)
with
\Omega=\mathbb{C}_{-\rho}
. Moreover, define the linear operators

and

by

\mathcal{D}(K)=C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X),\qquad(K\varphi)(t)=L(t)\varphi(t)-\dot{\varphi}(t)(0),\qquad(M\varphi)(t)=\varphi(t)(0),

then
K
satisfies
(H4)
, and
\mathcal{A}
defined in (
85

D,K
and
M
.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the proof of
Section

4.1
until we have to solve the resolvent equation (
68
) for
\varphi
with right-hand side
\psi\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R},X)

69
) tells us that the (formal) solution
\varphi
reads

\varphi(t)(\theta)=\int_{\theta}^{0}e^{z(\theta-s)}\psi(t+\theta-s)(s)ds,

but we will prove that this function belongs to
\mathcal{D}(D_{0})
if and only if
\real(z)>-\rho
, so that
\Omega=\mathbb{C}_{-\rho}
is nonempty. To do this, we first show that
\varphi
is an element of the state space

if and only if
\real(z)>-\rho
. Clearly,
\varphi
is continuous so it remains to show that
e^{\rho\theta}|\varphi(t)(\theta)|\to 0
as
\theta\to-\infty
for all

. Let
t\in\mathbb{R},\varepsilon>0
and

be given. Since
\psi
takes values in

, we know that

as

and so

e^{\rho s}|\psi(t+\theta-s)(s)|\leq\max_{\tau\in[0,T]}e^{\rho s}|\psi(\tau)(s)|\to 0,

due to compactness of

in the first component. Hence, there exists an

such that

as

e^{(\real(z)+\rho)\theta}\leq e^{-M_{1}(\real(z)+\rho)}\varepsilon/(2\|\psi\|_{\infty})
for all

\theta\leq M=\min\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
there holds

\displaystyle e^{\rho\theta}|\varphi(t)(\theta)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\theta}^{M}e^{(\real(z)+\rho)(\theta-s)}e^{\rho s}|\psi(t+\theta-s)(s)|ds+\int_{M}^{0}e^{(\real(z)+\rho)(\theta-s)}e^{\rho s}|\psi(t+\theta-s)(s)|ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\real(z)+\rho)\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\theta}^{M}e^{(\real(z)+\rho)(\theta-s)}ds+\|\psi\|_{\infty}\int_{M}^{0}e^{(\real(z)+\rho)(\theta-s)}ds\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon,

where we computed both integrals explicitly and used previous estimates to obtain the result. We conclude that
\varphi\in C_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
. Computing the derivatives
\dot{\varphi}(t)(\theta)
and
{\varphi}(t)^{\prime}(\theta)
as in (
70
), we see that
\varphi
is in

and
\varphi(t)^{\prime}\in X
for all

, and therefore
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D_{0})
. To prove the converse, assume that
\real(z)\leq-\rho
for some fixed

, and consider a nonzero function

satisfying the translation property
\psi(t)(\theta)=\psi(t+\theta)(0)
for all

and
\theta\in[-h,0]
. Since

as

and

for

, there exists an

such that

. Hence, an application of the reverse triangle inequality tells us that

as

if and only if

. Hence,

has a densely defined bounded linear inverse for every

given by (

The remaining part of the proof can be completed as performed in the proof of
Section

4.1
.
∎

associated with

and

\mathcal{D}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}q\\

{theorem}

The holomorphic operator-valued function

with values

\varphi\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d_{2}\mu(t,\theta)\int_{-\theta}^{0}e^{-z(\theta+s)}\varphi(t-\theta-s)(s)ds.

. We must only verify that the map

and that

. To prove the first claim, note that

as

4.3
Mixed functional differential equations

In the previous two examples, the rate of change of the system depended on the current and past states of the system. One can generalize these retarded equations in the sense that the rate of change of a system is allowed to depend on future states as well. Such differential equations arise, for example, naturally in the study of travelling wave solutions to differential equations posed on lattices, see for example
[
34
,
35
,
68
]
and the references therein. For these so-called
mixed functional differential equations
(MFDEs), we choose the state space
X
as the Banach space
C([r_{-},r_{+}],\mathbb{C}^{n})
, equipped with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, where the (maximal) retarded and advanced arguments
r_{-}<0<r_{+}
are assumed to be finite. Furthermore, let us consider a function
L\in C_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{L}(X,\mathbb{C}^{n}))
. A vector-valued version of the Riesz representation theorem tells us that for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
the operator
L(t)
can be represented uniquely as

L(t)\varphi=\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}d_{2}\eta(t,\theta)\varphi(\theta),

(87)

and notice the subtle difference of a minus sign in (
87
) compared to (
61
). The map
\eta:\mathbb{R}\times[r_{-},r_{+}]\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is a matrix-valued function, and
\eta(\cdot,\theta)
is continuous and
T
-periodic for all
\theta\in[r_{-},r_{+}]
. Moreover,
\eta(t,\cdot)
is of bounded variation, right-continuous on the open interval
(r_{-},r_{+})
and normalized by the requirement
\eta(\cdot,0)=0
.

5
Conclusion and outlook

This manuscript considers characteristic operators for periodic linear evolutionary systems, thereby building upon and extending the work by Kaashoek and Verduyn Lunel
[
40
]
on characteristic matrices for autonomous linear evolutionary systems.
We applied our results to three significant subclasses of periodic FDEs: classical delay differential equations (DDEs,
Section

4.1
), delay differential equations with infinite delay (iDDEs,
Section

4.2
) and mixed functional differential equations (MFDEs,
Section

4.3
). For this last class of equations, we resolved an open problem concerning the discrete spectral structure of the Floquet exponents (
Section

4.3
). We conjecture that our results can also be used to yield spectral information of various other classes of periodic linear differential equations, including DDEs on
L^{p}
spaces (with applications in control theory), abstract DDEs (with applications in mathematical neuroscience and PDEs with time delay), neutral FDEs (with applications in electrical and mechanical engineering), hyperbolic PDEs (with various applications in the analysis of waves) and Lotka-McKendrick-von Förster models (with applications in population dynamics). Characteristic matrices for the autonomous counterparts of the aforementioned classes of differential equations are provided in
[
40
, Section II]
. Nevertheless, verifying the hypotheses of the general scheme from
Section

3
for the mentioned periodic models under consideration is far from straightforward and involves numerous technical challenges. For instance, consider the difference in length and complexity between the proofs of periodic classical DDEs (
Section

4.1
) and that of autonomous classical DDEs (
[
40
, Section II.1]
). In some cases, it may even be necessary to relax or invoke alternative hypotheses to achieve comparable results.

When applying the abstract results to various classes of (functional) differential equations (
Section

4
), we constructed a characteristic operator
\Delta:\Omega\to L(\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n}))
for a given linear operator
\mathcal{A}\in L(\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X))
with
X=\mathcal{F}(I,\mathbb{C}^{n})
, where
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a closed interval. For classical DDEs, it was observed in
[
49
, Section 3]
that the closure of
\mathcal{A}
is the generator of an evolution semigroup
\mathcal{U}
on
\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
with
\mathcal{F}=C
. The choice of a
T
-periodic function space
\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},X)
is not standard as evolution semigroups are in general considered on
C_{0}(\mathbb{R},X)
, the space of all
X
-valued continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}
that vanish at infinity, or
L^{p}(\mathbb{R},X)
due to their connections with the existence and regularity of non-autonomous Cauchy problems, see
[
17
,
7
,
45
,
46
,
1
,
58
]
for more information. However, the choice of a
T
-periodic function space seems now to be very natural regarding its applicability to characteristic operators. A promising direction of further research is to formulate and prove general results regarding the (spectral) relationship between
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{U}
and an associated characteristic operator
\Delta
, including spectral mapping theorems, Fredholm properties, and related aspects. In particular, it would be interesting to investigate the properties of the adjoints
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
,
\mathcal{U}^{\star}
and
\Delta^{\star}
in terms of the periodic pairings
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{T}
from
[
49
, Section 3.3]
and
[
4
, Section 3]
since these operators have, for example, applications in (numerical) bifurcation analysis
[
4
,
49
,
70
,
10
,
11
]
. We believe that this evolution semigroup approach could also offer valuable insights into the theory of series expansions, completeness theorems and small solutions for various classes of periodic evolution equations, see
[
27
, Section 8.3]
,
[
41
, Section 11]
and
[
23
, Section 6.2]
for applications towards time-delayed models when the period is a multiple of the delay, recall also
Section

4.1
as a direction for further research in this spirit.

We finalize this paper with four open problems regarding our results:

1.

In the three examples from
Section

4
, it became clear that
\sigma(\mathcal{A})\cap\Omega
consists solely of isolated eigenvalues of finite type, as a direct consequence of
Section

2.3
. This spectral property aligns perfectly with the spectral structure of the characteristic matrix from Kaashoek and Verduyn Lunel
[
40
]
for autonomous linear evolution equations, see also
Section

2.3
. This naturally raises the question of whether or not a characteristic operator
\Delta
for
\mathcal{A}
on
\Omega
always exhibits this spectral structure. We conjecture that this is not the case as
\Delta(z)
does not necessarily admit a compact resolvent for all
z\in\Omega
. However, a simple counterexample remains elusive.

2.

Resolving the preceding problem requires extending the spectral theory for closable linear operators (
Section

2.1
) and closable operator-valued functions (
Section

2.2
) beyond eigenvalues and isolated spectral points. As noted in the discussion following
Section

2.1
, this extension must account for the approximate point spectrum. Furthermore, a comprehensive theory necessitates a detailed analysis of the compression, continuous, residual, and essential spectrum for closable linear operators, alongside their associated (semi-)Fredholm properties and their relationship to the corresponding spectra of the closure as detailed in
Section

2.1
,
Section

2.1
and
Section

2.1
.

3.

In the three examples from
Section

4
,
the holomorphic operator-valued function
\Delta
was always closed. However, we could not prove this directly from the general formula (
41
). An interesting topic for further research is to determine necessary and sufficient conditions on the operators
M,D,K,Q(z)
and
\iota
under which
\Delta(z)
is (non-)closed, and whether this property is (in)dependent of

.

4.

We formulated and proved all our results on a
T
-periodic function space
\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Z)
. The natural question arises if our results also hold in a
(T,z)
-periodic function space
\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathbb{R},Z)_{z}

, consisting of
Floquet-periodic
functions

satisfying

for all

[
70
,
4
]
and two
[
10

11

bifurcations of limit cycles in (in)finite-dimensional dynamical systems. In particular, it would be interesting to see how the results on periodic spectral patterns from

should be generalized towards Floquet-periodic functions.
