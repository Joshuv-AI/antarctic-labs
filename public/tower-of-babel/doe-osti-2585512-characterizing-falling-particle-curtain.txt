 SECTION II: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Major Goals and Objectives
Sandia will construct a cold flow receiver test platform in order to characterize falling particle
curtain receivers at commercially relevant scales. While Sandia has extensive experience in R&D
of falling particle curtain receivers, most have been at pilot scale and smaller - on the order of 1
MWth with characteristic dimensions of nominally 1-2 m to adequately collect solar energy from
the heliostat field at the National Solar Thermal Test Facility (NSTTF). However, scaling up
receivers to commercially relevant scales (25 MWth and above) will require a thorough
understanding of particle curtain dynamics at larger scales, especially longer drop heights, for
design certainty.

The goal of this project will be to construct a cold falling particle curtain test rig capable of
simulating particle characteristics that are expected in a commercial scale CSP plant, namely
the drop height, curtain thickness, and particle mass flow rate (normalized by length of
curtain). This will enable data collection on curtain opacity and spread, both of which are
correlated to receiver efficiency and reliable construction, for commercially relevant scales. It
will also permit validation of numerical models that will enable detailed receiver
characterization and design past currently validated scales.

Technical Achievements
The test platform has been installed. The pneumatic lift has been tested and we have confirmed
it can lift particles to the top of the tower, although the flow rate is not yet known.

Impact:
We will develop a test platform capable of experimental investigations into falling particle
curtain receivers at commercially relevant drop heights and linearized mass flow rates. We will
use this facility to validate numerical models of said falling particle receivers in order to
produce tools that can be used to design next-generation commercial scale CSP facilities. The
test platform will target receiver flow rates, receiver heights comparable with commercial
receivers; and target innovative “catch and release” feeders that can increase curtain opacity.

 SECTION III: TASKS AND MILESTONE PROGRESS
The project is arranged into three tasks that are roughly sequential: commercial scale falling
particle receiver (FPR) design and build, testing, and modeling.
Task 1.0 – FPR design and build:
The main structural tower has been mechanically assembled in front of the “module” at the
NSTTF. The project anticipated scheduling conflicts arising due to needing to lift the module to
perform on-sun testing for other project; this necessitated the design and installation of a
blower-powered pneumatic lift, instead of using the high temperature bucket lift as originally
planned. The initial testing of the bucket lift was delayed approximately 2 weeks due to wet
particles; as the moisture increased the cohesiveness of the particles considerably, they were
not able to freely drain out of the catch basin and into the L-valve. However, most of the
moisture was driven off the particles due to a combination of manual agitation and extremely
hot and dry conditions in Albuquerque (temperatures approaching 100 °F, relative humidity
below 10%). The pneumatic lift has since been successfully demonstrated, but the flow rate is
not yet known as the load cells under the inlet hopper have not been wired up. That will be
completed next quarter.

The LED panels and diffuser material have arrived on site. We have fabricated the frames to
support the diffuser material (a translucent sheet); measurements have shown that 8” of space
is sufficient to adequately diffuse the light from the LED panels. We will install those in the
tower during the next quarter. We also have all of the cameras on hand. Those will also be
mounted and tested next quarter. We anticipate sharing test time with the PCGV project as
both projects have similar testing requirements, but each project will post-process the required
data on their own.

In parallel with the PCGV project, we are in the process of connecting to the Flowserve particle
control valves. We are having issues with connecting to them with the Flowserve provided
software; we are working with Flowserve while also attempting to “manually” control the
valves via a typical 4-20 mA signal.

Task 2.0 – FPR test campaign:
As the test campaign is gated by the installation of the test hardware, this task has not yet
begun in earnest. We are planning to perform similar measurements as have been
demonstrated in the past for curtain opacity and curtain width. This task will not be completed
as written, particularly the testing of a multi-STAiR receiver, due to budget constraints.
