Scalable CT-based prognostic modeling of dementia conversion in mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Seongbeom Park, Jehyun Ahn, Duk L Na, Hee Jin Kim, Hyemin Jang, Jun Pyo Kim, Sung Hoon Kang, Jihwan Yun, Min Young Chun, Sang Won Seo, Kichang Kwak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a heterogeneous condition, with up to 40% of patients progressing to dementia within three years of clinical diagnosis. While MRI, PET, and CSF biomarkers have shown prognostic value, their limited accessibility restricts routine use. We aimed to develop and validate a CT-based machine learning model to predict dementia conversion in patients with MCI. We analyzed 791 MCI patients with baseline CT and longitudinal neuropsychological assessments. Candidate predictors included demographic factors, APOE genotype, Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), and CT-derived W-scores from regional CSF and ventricular volumes. CT-derived W-scores represent age-, sex-, and modality-adjusted z-scores of regional CSF and ventricular volumes. Converters were older (73.4 vs. 70.7 years, p < 0.001), more often female (60.4% vs. 51.4%, p = 0.015), and more likely APOE ε4 carriers (52.5% vs. 40.0%, p < 0.001). Logistic regression with SMOTEENN sampling achieved the best performance (Area Under the Curve [AUC] = 0.840; accuracy = 0.743; sensitivity = 0.847; specificity = 0.674), with robust generalizability in the independent test set (AUC = 0.823). Feature selection and SHAP analysis identified six key predictors, including CDR-SB, APOE ε4, age, and three CT-derived volumetric markers (W-score in the left inferior lateral ventricle, left parietal CSF, and left occipital CSF). Our findings demonstrate that CT-derived volumetric analysis, combined with clinical and genetic features, enables accurate and interpretable prediction of dementia conversion in MCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45439-8.Keywords: Alzheimer disease, CT scan, Prognosis, Dementia, Mild cognitive impairmentSubject terms: Biomarkers, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) represents a transitional stage between normal cognition and dementia, and a substantial proportion of patients with MCI eventually progress to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) or other dementias1,2. However, the course of progression is highly variable, even among patients at similar baseline stages, creating a major challenge for identifying individuals at greatest risk of near-term clinical progression3–5. This variability underscores the clinical need for reliable prognostic tools to guide timely intervention and personalized care planning.

A number of factors have been implicated in influencing the likelihood of conversion from MCI to dementia6. Age remains one of the strongest non-modifiable predictors, while the presence of the Apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele has been consistently associated with increased risk of progression7–11. Baseline cognitive status, often captured by neuropsychological measures such as the Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), also plays a critical role5,12,13. Imaging markers reflecting structural brain changes, particularly medial temporal lobe atrophy and more widespread cortical degeneration in parietal and occipital regions, have further been identified as important indicators of future cognitive decline14–16. Despite these insights, the relative contribution of these factors and their integration into practical prediction models remain incompletely understood.

Building on these risk factors, several studies have attempted to develop classifiers for predicting dementia conversion in MCI patients17–19. Many models have leveraged structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (FDG-PET), or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers, either individually or in combination, and have reported predictive accuracies and area under the curves (AUCs) typically in the 0.70–0.85 range20–24. While informative, these approaches often rely on costly, invasive, or less accessible modalities, which limit their widespread adoption in routine practice. Recently, however, methodological advances have enabled accurate quantification of brain atrophy from non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scans, and our group as well as others have demonstrated that CT-based volumetry shows strong concordance with MRI-based measures25–28. This suggests that CT, which is inexpensive and widely available, could provide a practical alternative for volumetric assessment in prognostic modeling. Importantly, the aim of CT-based volumetric assessment is not to replace advanced biomarkers or enable preclinical detection, but to repurpose already available imaging data for pragmatic prognostic stratification in routine clinical settings. In clinical practice, non-contrast head CT is frequently obtained during the evaluation of patients with suspected cognitive impairment to exclude secondary causes such as tumors, intracranial hemorrhage, infarction, or hydrocephalus, which may mimic dementia syndromes. This clinical workflow creates an opportunity to leverage already acquired CT data for prognostic assessment without additional imaging burden. In this study, we aimed to develop and validate a machine learning–based model to predict near-term dementia conversion in MCI patients using CT-derived volumetric measures of brain atrophy. By quantifying age- and sex-adjusted regional brain atrophy scores from CT scans and integrating them with demographic, genetic, and clinical features, we sought to identify the most informative predictors and construct a robust, interpretable, and clinically scalable prognostic model.

Results

Baseline participant characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. The study included a total of 791 patients with MCI (mean age 71.8 years, 55.0% female, mean education 12.2 years), of whom 356 (45.0%) were APOE ε4 carriers. During the three-year follow-up, 318 patients (40.2%) converted to dementia. Among converters, the mean time to dementia conversion was 2.0 ± 0.7 years (median, 2.0 years) from baseline. At baseline, converters were significantly older (73.4 vs. 70.7 years, p < 0.001), more frequently female (60.4% vs. 51.4%, p = 0.015), and more likely to be APOE ε4 carriers (52.5% vs. 40.0%, p < 0.001) compared with non-converters. Baseline CDR-SB scores were also higher in converters (2.1) than in non-converters (1.2, p < 0.001). Years of education did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.064).

Regional differences in W-scores

Figure 1 shows that converters had consistently lower W-scores across all regions, reflecting greater CSF or ventricular enlargement compared with non-converters. Group differences were significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction (all p < 0.05). The most pronounced differences were observed in the inferior lateral ventricles (mean difference ≈ − 0.75 to − 0.78, p < 0.001), followed by temporal CSF regions (mean difference ≈ − 0.64 to − 0.78, p < 0.001). Significant but smaller reductions were also detected in parietal and occipital CSF regions (mean difference ≈ − 0.35 to − 0.56, p < 0.001). The global composite W-score was also significantly reduced in converters (–0.59, p < 0.001).

Comparison of regional differences in W-scores between converters and non-converters.

Classification performance

We evaluated multiple combinations of sampling strategies and machine learning models (Supplementary Table 1). Table 2 summarizes the classification performance of different sampling strategies and machine learning models. The best performance was achieved by the logistic regression (LR) model with the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique with Edited Nearest Neighbors (SMOTEENN) sampling method, yielding an AUC of 0.840, accuracy of 0.743, sensitivity of 0.847, and specificity of 0.674. The best gradient boosting (GB), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and multilayer perceptron (MLP) all showed lower AUCs (0.831–0.824) and did not exceed the performance of LR with SMOTEENN, so we selected this model as the final classifier for subsequent analyses.

To evaluate the incremental value of CT-derived structural features, we compared the discriminative performance of a clinical model and a combined model. The clinical model, which included age, sex, education, APOE ε4, CDR-SB, achieved an AUC of 0.789. When CT-derived W-scores were added, the combined model showed a significantly improved performance with an AUC of 0.821. The difference in AUCs between the two models was statistically significant based on DeLong’s test (Z = 2.628, p = 0.009), indicating that CT-derived volumetric features provided additional discriminative value beyond conventional clinical predictors (Supplementary Table 2).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated significant differences in time-to-dementia conversion between the predicted risk group (log-rank test, p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 2). Cox proportional hazards regression revealed that compared to the non-converter group, the converter group had a 3.14-fold higher hazard of conversion (95% CI: 2.06–4.79, p < 0.001). The three-year dementia-free survival rates were 73.8% (95% CI: 66.4%–82.0%) for the non-converter group and 40.5% (95% CI: 32.5%–50.5%) for the converter group.

Decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the combined model provided greater net benefit compared to the clinical model across a wide range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities (approximately 20%–55%), supporting the incremental clinical utility of CT-derived features beyond statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Cross-validation and independent test

The SMOTEENN-LR model demonstrated consistently strong performance during cross-validation, with fold-wise AUCs ranging from 0.772 to 0.862 (mean ≈ 0.84) across the five folds (Fig. 2A). Importantly, the model maintained robust generalizability on the independent validation dataset, achieving an AUC of 0.823 (Fig. 2B). At the prespecified probability threshold, the final model showed balanced classification performance on the test dataset, with a sensitivity of 0.76 and specificity of 0.70 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Performance evaluation of the predictive model and the relative importance of individual features in forecasting conversion.

Feature selection and model interpretation

Before selecting the final model, we applied recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV) as a feature screening step. In the best-performing classifier, RFECV applied to the 20 candidate predictors selected six optimal features: CDR-SB, APOE ε4 genotype, left inferior lateral ventricle, age, left parietal CSF, and left occipital CSF. We then used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to examine the relative importance of these six predictors in the final model (Fig. 2C). Among these, CDR-SB exerted the strongest effect, with SHAP values extending up to + 0.6, indicating a strong positive association with conversion risk. APOE ε4 carrier status (SHAP up to + 0.4) and older age (up to + 0.3) also contributed prominently. For imaging features, lower W-scores in the left inferior lateral ventricle (–0.3 to + 0.4), parietal CSF (–0.2 to + 0.3), and occipital CSF (–0.15 to + 0.25)—reflecting larger CSF volumes—were consistently associated with an increased probability of dementia conversion.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and independently validated a machine learning model based on CT-derived features and clinical variables to predict dementia conversion in patients with mild cognitive impairment. Our major findings were as follows. First, regional CT analyses revealed that converters exhibited consistently lower W-scores across multiple brain regions, particularly in the inferior lateral ventricles and temporal CSF regions. Second, logistic regression models, particularly when combined with the SMOTEENN sampling strategy, achieved the highest predictive performance, with robust generalizability demonstrated in independent dataset. Finally, feature selection and SHAP analysis identified six key predictors—CDR-SB, APOE ε4 genotype, age, and three regional CT-derived W-scores—that most strongly contributed to model predictions. Taken together, our findings suggest that integrating simple CT-based structural measures with clinical and genetic markers enables accurate and generalizable prediction of dementia conversion in MCI patients. This approach may facilitate earlier risk stratification in clinical settings and improve the design of personalized interventions and clinical trials.

We found that approximately 40% of patients with MCI converted to dementia within three years, and converters differed from non-converters in several baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. Specifically, converters were older, more frequently APOE ε4 carriers, and had greater baseline cognitive impairment as reflected by higher CDR-SB scores. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that advanced age, APOE ε4 genotype, and subtle cognitive deficits at baseline are robust risk factors for progression from MCI to dementia7,8,11,41. Taken together, our results reinforce the established notion that both genetic vulnerability and baseline cognitive burden contribute substantially to the likelihood of clinical conversion.

Our first major finding was that converters exhibited consistently lower W-scores across multiple brain regions, with the most pronounced differences observed in the inferior lateral ventricles, followed by the temporal CSF regions. These results are in line with previous studies demonstrating that hippocampal atrophy is one of the earliest and most robust imaging markers of progression from MCI to dementia16,42. The inferior lateral ventricles directly border the hippocampus, and ventricular enlargement in this region has long been used as a surrogate marker for hippocampal atrophy, including in established visual rating scales43. Thus, our findings further reinforce the critical role of medial temporal lobe atrophy in predicting dementia conversion. Beyond the medial temporal regions, we also observed significant group differences in the parietal and occipital CSF regions. These findings are consistent with prior work highlighting that parietal and occipital cortical atrophy, particularly involving posterior cortical networks, is closely associated with Alzheimer’s disease progression44. The observed CSF expansion in these regions likely reflects underlying cortical thinning and neurodegeneration, providing additional evidence that posterior cortical atrophy contributes meaningfully to the risk of conversion from MCI to dementia.

Our second major finding was that logistic regression models, particularly when combined with the SMOTEENN sampling strategy, achieved the highest predictive performance, with consistent results across cross-validation and independent validation. The SMOTEENN–LR model yielded an AUC of 0.840 in cross-validation and 0.823 in the independent test dataset, with high sensitivity (0.847) for identifying converters. To our knowledge, prognostic models using CT as the primary modality for predicting conversion from MCI to dementia are scarce, as most prior studies have relied on structural MRI, FDG-PET, or CSF biomarkers. These studies have typically reported AUCs in the range of 0.70–0.80, with only a few multimodal approaches achieving values above 0.82–0.8515,21,22. In this context, the performance of our CT-based model is comparable to, and in some cases exceeds, that of previously published prognostic models. However, these modalities are costly, invasive, or not routinely available in clinical practice, which limits their widespread applicability. Importantly, the clinical value of CT-based prognostic modeling lies not in replacing advanced biomarkers, but in extracting additional prognostic information from an imaging modality that is already mandatory in the diagnostic evaluation of cognitive impairment. In this context, CT-based prognostic modeling capitalizes on imaging data that are already available in routine diagnostic workflows, rather than requiring additional or specialized examinations. In contrast, our approach relies solely on CT-based structural features combined with basic clinical and genetic information, yet achieved a similar level of discriminative performance. Moreover, the added value of CT-derived features was further supported by comparative analyses showing improved model performance when CT-based W-scores were included alongside clinical predictors. This finding highlights the potential of CT, a widely available and inexpensive imaging modality, as a practical tool for risk stratification in patients with MCI. Notably, the choice of a simple logistic regression model was intentional, prioritizing interpretability and clinical transparency over algorithmic complexity, which is essential for real-world implementation.

Our final major finding was that feature selection and SHAP analysis identified six key predictors—CDR-SB, APOE ε4 genotype, age, and three regional CT-derived W-scores—that most strongly contributed to the model’s predictions. This finding provides important interpretability and biological plausibility to our approach, complementing the performance results described above. The prominence of CDR-SB and APOE ε4 status is consistent with well-established clinical and genetic risk factors for dementia conversion. Importantly, although APOE ε4 contributed to risk stratification, it is not a prerequisite for applying the proposed model, as the core predictive signal is largely driven by baseline cognitive status and CT-derived structural measures. Age, likewise, has been repeatedly confirmed as one of the most powerful non-modifiable predictors of cognitive decline. Beyond these conventional factors, the inclusion of CT-derived features such as the inferior lateral ventricle, parietal CSF, and occipital CSF volumes highlights the contribution of structural brain changes to the model’s predictive power. These regions correspond to hippocampal and posterior cortical atrophy, which are widely recognized as key imaging hallmarks of Alzheimer’s disease progression. Taken together, our model not only demonstrated strong performance but also derived its predictive strength from features with established biological and clinical relevance, thereby enhancing its credibility for potential translation into clinical practice. From a practical perspective, APOE ε4 may be viewed as an optional variable that enhances prognostic refinement in specialized settings, while the model remains applicable using CT and basic clinical data alone in routine or resource-limited environments. This interpretability, achieved through a transparent modeling framework and SHAP analysis, represents a key strength of our approach and supports its potential integration into routine clinical decision-making. While the SHAP analysis indicated that a small number of features accounted for most of the predictive contribution, these features represent well-established clinical and neurobiological markers of dementia progression, supporting biological plausibility rather than model instability.

Importantly, the clinical value of CT-based prognostic modeling lies not in replacing advanced biomarkers such as MRI, PET, or fluid assays, but in extracting additional prognostic information from an imaging modality that is already routinely obtained during the diagnostic evaluation of cognitive impairment. In many clinical settings, non-contrast CT is performed to exclude secondary causes of cognitive decline, including brain tumors, hemorrhage, infarction, or hydrocephalus. Within this existing workflow, the proposed model enables opportunistic risk stratification without additional imaging, cost, or patient burden. By identifying individuals at high risk of near-term dementia conversion, the model may support more tailored clinical management, including closer follow-up, earlier initiation of supportive and non-pharmacological interventions, and timely counseling for patients and caregivers, while low-risk individuals may be spared unnecessary diagnostic escalation and frequent visits. The continuous risk score generated by the model could be used to stratify patients with MCI into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups for near-term dementia conversion. Beyond routine care, this CT-based risk stratification may also facilitate prognostic enrichment in clinical trials by selectively identifying individuals more likely to show clinical progression over a defined follow-up period, thereby improving trial efficiency and reducing screening burden. Such a CT-based risk stratification approach may be particularly valuable in primary and secondary care settings where access to MRI, PET, or plasma biomarkers is limited, thereby supporting scalable and cost-effective dementia care.

This study highlights the potential of non-contrast CT, an inexpensive and widely available imaging modality, combined with longitudinal cognitive assessments to build accurate and interpretable prognostic models for dementia progression. Although the outcome was defined dichotomously, the model outputs continuous probabilities of conversion, allowing flexible risk stratification across clinically meaningful thresholds. However, several limitations should be noted. First, CT imaging has inherent technical limitations, including lower soft tissue contrast compared with MRI, susceptibility to artifacts at the bone–brain interface, and sensitivity to acquisition and reconstruction parameters. Although these effects were minimized by using a single scanner and a standardized image-processing pipeline, such factors may still influence volumetric measurements, and future studies should evaluate the performance of this approach in multi-center and multi-scanner settings. Second, our CT-based model was not directly compared with MRI- or PET-based approaches, and head-to-head evaluations are warranted to establish relative performance. Third, although the retrospective design precludes causal inference, it is appropriate for prognostic modeling, particularly when predictors are defined at baseline and outcomes are assessed longitudinally, as in the present study. Fourth, although the exact onset and duration of MCI could not be determined, our approach focused on predicting short-term conversion risk from a standardized baseline evaluation rather than modeling disease duration. Fifth, our binary classification of converters versus non-converters may oversimplify the heterogeneity of clinical trajectories, which include functional and neuropsychiatric domains beyond cognition. Sixth, our analyses were based on baseline CT imaging only, and longitudinal imaging data were not available. Therefore, we could not assess whether temporal changes in CT-derived volumetric measures provide additional prognostic value beyond baseline assessments. Seventh, the study cohort consisted of patients who underwent amyloid PET-CT as part of clinical evaluation or research protocols, representing a clinically enriched MCI population. This selection may limit generalizability to unselected MCI populations encountered in routine practice. Eighth, although a nested cross-validation framework and an independent test set were used to mitigate overfitting, replication in fully independent, multi-center cohorts was not performed, and further validation will be necessary to confirm robustness and scalability. Ninth, although a nested cross-validation framework and an independent test set were used to mitigate overfitting, the use of resampling techniques such as SMOTEENN may still introduce a degree of model optimism. In addition, we focused primarily on discriminative performance rather than calibration, and formal calibration metrics such as the Brier score were not assessed. Future studies incorporating calibration analysis and repeated validation across larger, heterogeneous cohorts will be necessary to further establish model stability. Finally, because all participants underwent amyloid PET-CT at baseline, our cohort represents a selected group of patients with MCI and may not be fully representative of unselected MCI populations encountered in routine clinical practice. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing absolute risk estimates to broader populations. However, the prognostic model itself does not incorporate amyloid PET information and relies solely on CT-derived structural measures and basic clinical variables, which supports its potential applicability in settings where PET imaging is not available. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that even with these limitations, our study provides meaningful evidence supporting CT-based prognostication. The integration of such models into routine workflows could potentially support risk stratification, subject to further validation in broader and more heterogeneous clinical populations.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that non-contrast CT, when combined with clinical and genetic markers, can provide accurate and interpretable prognostic models for predicting dementia conversion in patients with MCI. Although prognostic prediction does not directly change treatment strategy, which remains limited in dementia, identifying individuals at high risk of near-term conversion has important implications for clinical management. Risk stratification can inform follow-up intensity, guide the timing of repeat cognitive evaluations, and support earlier referral to specialized memory clinics. Moreover, early identification of high-risk patients enables timely counseling of patients and caregivers, facilitates advance care planning, and may aid in selecting appropriate candidates for clinical trials or preventive intervention programs.

Methods

Study participants

This retrospective prognostic cohort study included a total of 791 patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) who were consecutively recruited between January 2015 and December 2022, in whom baseline clinical and CT-derived variables were used to predict conversion to dementia over a three-year follow-up period (Fig. 3). All participants underwent amyloid positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) scans and longitudinal neuropsychological assessments as part of a consecutive clinical research program, resulting in a well-characterized but selected MCI cohort. MCI was diagnosed according to the following criteria: (1) self- or informant-reported cognitive complaints; (2) objective cognitive impairment in at least one cognitive domain (≤ − 1.0 standard deviation [SD] in memory or ≤ − 1.5 SD in other domains based on age- and education-adjusted norms); (3) preserved activities of daily living; and (4) absence of dementia29–31. All participants had a baseline Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) global score of 0.5, based on neuropsychological tests conducted within one year of the CT scan.

Participant inclusion flowchart.

Acquisition of CT images

The CT images analyzed in this study were low-dose scans acquired as part of amyloid PET-CT protocols, primarily for attenuation correction, rather than standalone CT examinations performed for prognostic assessment. All CT scans were acquired using the same PET/CT scanner model (Discovery STe, GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI, USA) in three-dimensional mode at a single center, ensuring consistency in hardware and reconstruction algorithms across participants. Common acquisition parameters included a slice thickness of 3.75 mm, a STANDARD convolution kernel, and a 512 × 512 matrix. Depending on the PET tracer protocol, tube voltage and tube current ranged from 120 to 130 kVp and 50–95 mA, respectively. The pixel size ranged from 0.488 to 0.500 mm, yielding voxels of 0.488–0.500 × 0.488–0.500 × 3.75 mm³.

CT image processing and quantification

The automated CT-based volumetric analysis was performed using Morph CT software, an automated deep learning–based segmentation pipeline, which has been previously validated against 3D T1-weighted MRI in a large, independent cohort, demonstrating high segmentation accuracy and strong CT–MRI agreement for regional CSF and ventricular volumes45. The detailed methodology of this pipeline, including network architecture and validation procedures, has been reported in our previous study. The input to the software consisted solely of the non-contrast CT image. No CSF biomarkers, laboratory values, or clinical variables were used during image processing. The software segmented 14 anatomical regions and generated W-scores based on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and lateral ventricle (LV) volumes. W-scores were derived using regression-based normative modeling in the K-ROAD cohort, in which regional CSF and ventricular volumes were modeled as a function of age, sex, and imaging modality. For each participant, the W-score represents the deviation from the predicted normative value at the individual’s specific age, sex, and imaging modality, normalized by the residual standard deviation of the model. Both regional W-scores and a global composite W-score, calculated by summing the regional W-scores across all 14 regions, were used as imaging biomarkers.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was dementia conversion, defined as progression from MCI to dementia within three years of the baseline neuropsychological assessment. Conversion was operationalized as reaching a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) global score of ≥ 1, determined through standardized neuropsychological testing and clinical evaluation. This three-year follow-up window was selected to capture near-term clinical progression, which is particularly relevant for prognostic stratification in patients with MCI, as prior longitudinal studies have shown that approximately 50% of individuals with MCI convert to dementia within three years of clinical diagnosis. Non-converters were defined as individuals who did not meet this threshold during the follow-up period. Transient fluctuations or modest improvements in CDR or CDR-SB scores during follow-up were not used to redefine outcome status. Participants were classified as converters if the dementia threshold was met at any follow-up visit, and as non-converters otherwise.

Predictor variables

Candidate predictors included: (1) demographic and clinical variables (age, sex, years of education, APOE ε4 carrier status, baseline CDR-SB); and (2) CT-derived volumetric features (14 regional and one global W-score). A total of 20 candidate variables were entered into model development. Vascular risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes were not included as model predictors, as the primary aim was to evaluate the prognostic utility of CT-derived structural measures combined with core clinical variables routinely available at baseline imaging.

Machine learning model development and comparative analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical predictors included age, sex, years of education, APOE ε4 carrier status, and CDR-SB score, together with regional W-scores from 14 brain regions and a global W-score, yielding a total of 20 candidate predictors (Table 2). All continuous variables were scaled to improve model stability: age, years of education, and W-scores were standardized using z-score normalization, while CDR-SB was scaled using a robust scaler to mitigate the influence of outliers. Sex and APOE ε4 carrier status were binarily encoded.

The dataset was split into a training/validation set (70%) and an independent test set (30%) using stratified sampling to preserve the proportion of converters and non-converters. All model development procedures, including feature selection and hyperparameter optimization, were confined to the training/validation set to prevent data leakage. All resampling procedures and feature selection steps were performed exclusively within the training folds of the nested cross-validation framework to prevent information leakage and reduce the risk of overfitting.

We systematically evaluated six machine-learning classifiers—LR, RF, GB, SVM, XGBoost, and MLP—under six sampling strategies designed to address class imbalance: no sampling, RUS, ROS, SMOTE, SMOTEENN, and SMOTETomek33–40. A nested stratified five-fold cross-validation framework was employed for robust evaluation. The outer loop provided unbiased estimates of model performance, while the inner loop conducted exhaustive grid search for hyperparameter tuning. Sampling strategies and RFECV were applied only to the training subsets within each outer fold to avoid information leakage. For each classifier, RFECV was used as a wrapper-based feature selection method on the training data in the inner loop. The procedure iteratively removed the least informative predictors and selected that maximized cross-validated AUC for that classifier. This resulted in model-specific subsets of predictors. RFECV was therefore optimized separately for each classifier, allowing both linear and non-linear models to retain features most relevant to their respective decision mechanisms. After comparing performance across all classifiers, we identified the best performance model. Using the feature subset and hyperparameters obtained for this model, we refit the final model on the full training/validation set and evaluated it on the independent test set. Performance metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC, were calculated on the independent dataset, which was completely held out from all stages of model development.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (R Studio version 2023.12.1 + 402). In the demographic analysis, categorical variables between converters and non-converters were compared using the chi-square tests, while continuous variables were compared with Welch two-sample t-test. To assess group differences in regional brain atrophy, we compared the regional W-scores between converters and non-converters using independent samples t-test, and applied false discovery rate (FDR) correction to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Survival analysis was performed to evaluate time-to-dementia conversion within the three-year follow-up period. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed for each predicted risk group (converter and non-converter), and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to estimate hazard ratios for dementia conversion, with the non-converter group serving as the reference category.

DCA was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the combined and clinical models by estimating net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. DCA was performed using the curves package in RStudio (version 2023.12.1).
