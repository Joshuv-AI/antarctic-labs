ABSTRACT
Transmission radiography provides access to the interior structure of opaque objects: a source
shoots high-energy photons at an object and measures how much is absorbed or scattered. From
those measurements, we try to infer the object’s internal material structure using a deterministic least
squares function. We developed a pipeline to infer geometries of a double-annulus steel cylinder
measured in a multi-inspection photon radiography experiment. However, this is an inverse problem
where multiple material geometries can produce similar transmission data so our objective for this
project is to use uncertainty quantification to characterize ambiguity by creating probabilistic
distributions of material geometries instead of simply the best fit. We use a six parameter forward
model to reparameterize the geometry of the object based on its double annulus shape. We use
Bayesian methods and Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to estimate probability distributions
of these parameters. We develop and implement methods to optimize MCMC hyper-parameters.
Using these methods, we estimate material geometries and their uncertainties. We then develop
methods and metrics such as Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) to measure accuracy of
uncertainty error bars. From our findings, we have found how modifying hyper-parameters affected
uncertainty and correlations between parameters. We found that increasing sigma certainty hyper-
parameters of guesses increases overall uncertainty and increasing image resolution decreases
uncertainty. Beyond this, we found that some of these parameters, such as the inner and outer
radii, are correlated with each other. These results suggest that geometric reparameterization with
Bayesian inference provides a useful approach to radiographic reconstruction.

1. INTRODUCTION

Transmission radiography provides access to the interior structure of opaque objects: a source
shoots high-energy photons at an object and measures how much is absorbed or scattered. From
those measurements, we try to infer the object’s internal material structure using a deterministic
least squares function. We developed a pipeline to infer geometries of a double-annulus steel
cylinder measured in a multi-inspection photon radiography experiment. However, this is an
inverse problem where multiple material geometries can produce similar transmission data so our
objective for this project is to use uncertainty quantification to characterize ambiguity by creating
probabilistic distributions of material geometries instead of simply the best fit.
We use Bayesian methods and Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to estimate these probability
distributions. We develop and implement methods to optimize MCMC hyper-parameters. Using
these methods, we estimate material geometries and their uncertainties. We then develop methods
to measure accuracy and usefulness of uncertainty error bars. We also look into the effect of
modifying hyper-parameters on these uncertainty error bars.

The inverse problem is to recover 𝝃 from the observed data d.
When using the deterministic estimate, we are limited to only a singular best fit value for the param-
eters. Multiple parameter configurations of geometric patterns that produce different density fields
can produce the same projections. Due to this, along with measurement noise and approximation
errors from the forward model, uncertainty quantification and probability distributions beyond a
single point estimate are required to properly resolve these issues. A full, joint probability distribu-
tion over the parameters that is consistent with the data and coherently reflects all relevant sources
of uncertainty. Such a distribution permits risk-informed decision making: one can compute, for
example, the probability that a shell thickness is below a safety threshold, or that the object center
lies within a specified tolerance.

4.2. Bayesian Inference

The deterministic estimate 𝝓ˆ finds the best parameter values, and consequently areal density

reliability of these estimates. Radiographic inversion is, fundamentally, an ill-posed problem.
Multiple combinations of geometric parameters can produce different density fields that yield
indistinguishable projections. Measurement noise compounds this non-uniqueness, while the
forward model (2) introduces approximation errors through its discretization, energy-spectrum
model, and PSF calibration. A single point estimate propagates none of these sources of uncertainty
to derived quantities such as shell thicknesses or density profiles.
In safety-critical evaluation an estimate without associated uncertainty is of limited engineering
value. It is critical to go beyond point estimates and bounds to obtain a full, joint probability
distribution over the parameters that is consistent with the data and coherently reflects all relevant
sources of uncertainty. Such a distribution permits risk-informed decision making: one can
compute, for example, the probability that a shell thickness is below a safety threshold, or that the
object center lies within a specified tolerance.
Bayesian inference provides a mathematically rigorous and consistent framework for this task. It
treats the unknown parameters as random variables and updates an initial prior distribution with
the information in the data to obtain the posterior distribution, which is the complete probabilistic
answer to the inverse problem. Bayes’ theorem combines the likelihood of the observed data with
prior knowledge to produce the posterior distribution over parameters:

4.2.1. Markov Chain Monte Carlo

The posterior (6) does not admit a closed-form expression because the forward model F includes a
composition of a nonlinear reparameterization, a ray-tracing map and a polychromatic attenuation
model. We employ Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) as means to sample from the posterior
distribution. MCMC makes no assumption about the shape of the posterior and requires only the
ability to evaluate 𝜋(d | 𝝃)𝜋(𝝃) pointwise, which reduces to a single forward-model evaluation per
proposed sample.
We typically use 𝑁 = 105 MCMC steps, the first 25% of the chain (burn-in) is discarded and every

5.2. Parameter Correlation

A benefit of utilizing MCMC inference outside of uncertainty quantification is its ability to reveal
correlation patterns between variables. We can compare how each sample’s parameter values
compare to each other. Figure 5-5 shows these pairwise relationships as a corner plot of the
posterior samples, with marginal histograms on the diagonal. Here, we can see correlations
between 𝑟 0 , Δ1 , and Δ2 with 𝑟 0 and Δ1 being especially apparent. This strong inverse correlation
between 𝑟 0 and Δ1 implies that the individual values of 𝑟 0 and Δ1 are largely irrelevant to the data
projection and that only their sum matters. Because 𝑟 0 measures the inner radius of the plastic ring
and 𝑟 0 +Δ1 measures the outer radius, this may suggest that in the presence of the more dense steel
material, the plastic ring has negligible effect. This also suggests that we may be able to combine 𝑟 0
and Δ1 to one parameter (𝑟 0 +Δ1 ) in our forward model with relatively low effect on the projection
accuracy.

We use Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) as our benchmark to measure uncertainty
quality. CRPS is a distance benchmark (lower scores represent higher quality uncertainty). CRPS
measures how well confidence intervals encapsulate data while also being small enough that the
confidence intervals are meaningful. When data is not encapsulated by confidence intervals, the
more certain the model is, the worse the CRPS score.

 28

Our experiment has model error, so the mean fit deviates from the data and, as a result, our confidence
intervals are off from the actual data points. This means that intervals with low uncertainty often
are more certain about incorrect data and have resulting worse CRPS scores.

random draws from 𝐹.

5.5. Resolution Size

Over the inspections that we run, we can also modify resolution sizes for any square value up to
size 120 × 120. For the purposes of our experiments, we investigated resolution sizes 30 × 30,
60 × 60, and 120 × 120. The greater the resolution size, the lower the uncertainty. This is due to the
way our Gaussian likelihood works. Because Gaussian assumes no model error, the more data it
receives, the more confident it becomes. However, because we have model error, more data makes
our model get more certain around an incorrect mean fit. This leads to higher (worse) CRPS scores
and lower uncertainty.

6. RELATED WORK

6.1. BIE Application of Bayesian Inference

At the start of my internship, I worked on applying the same MCMC sampling process used in
the radiological problem to the Bayesian Inference Engine (BIE) application. There, instead of
inferring an object’s density, we tried to approximate a 2D 60 × 60 pixel pixelated image. Each
pixel could be one of two colors (represented as 0 or 1). We used the same deterministic least
squares method to approximate the image and then utilized MCMC sampling similar to what we
did for the radiological problem. This problem handles similarly to the radiological problem where
the deterministic and Bayesian methods attempt to find best fits for parameters to generate an image
closest to the target original image. However, instead of constraining the potential geometries, we
allow BIE to be any 2D shape possible in a 60 × 60 image. This means we require MCMC to be
run on 3600 parameters instead of 6. While this allows for any arbitrary shape to be estimated, it
costs significantly more computing power to run. Running the full 3600 parameters at once takes
an unreasonable amount of time so other methods had to be developed to generate the MCMC
estimation images for the full 60 × 60 image.

6.2. Sub Images

Computation time does not increase linearly with a problem’s parameter size so we can significantly
reduce the computation time of this problem to a reasonable amount by splitting the image into
sub images, running MCMC on these sub images, then stitching these sub images back together to
produce an estimation of the full image. We made multiple types of sub images including 15 × 15
squares or singular column MCMC runs.

To further speed up the computation time, we added parallelization so that each available core

7. LIMITATIONS/MODEL ERROR

One issue with our model is its assumption of a perfect double annulus as shown in Figure 3-1.
However, after developing this forward model, we found that the real world data was collected from
an annulus with two small holes drilled in the sides. These differences made it impossible for our
model to perfectly replicate the real world data and made our MCMC and deterministic runs unable
to generate an accurate estimation of the real data. There are two potential ideas to fix this:
 1. We can create a 2D overhead trinary grid representing the annulus’ bird eye view. 0 rep-
 resenting air, 1 representing plastic, and 2 representing steel. This model could provide a
 general way to represent any arbitrary shape with a uniform vertical shape including our
 imperfect real world annulus. Our BIE application of MCMC in Related Work (Section 6)
 is similar to this suggestion where we would try to replicate a pixelated 2D shape using
 Bayesian inference. However, running Bayesian inference with this method is extremely
 computationally expensive. Generating a 120 × 120 image requires MCMC to fit 14400
 parameters rather than the 6 of our current forward model. This becomes unreasonable to
 compute and practically intractable.
 2. We can also add more parameters to represent these small drill holes, for example, we can
 create vertical and horizontal offsets as well as radii sizes for each drill hole that would require
 a more reasonable 12 parameters than the pixelated method.

8. CONCLUSIONS

We developed a Bayesian uncertainty quantification process to radiologically reconstruct geometric
structures of a double annulus structure of steel and plastic materials. We used a six parameter
forward model to describe the geometry and then generated assumed parameters with confidence
intervals with radiological data and the use of deterministic least square optimization and MCMC
Bayesian inference. Beyond this, we looked into how modifying hyper-parameters such as sigma
values, resolution sizes and inspection types affect confidence intervals and uncertainty quality.
Through hyper-parameter tuning, we can develop multiple conclusions about hyper-parameters’
effect on uncertainty. We know that higher sigma values result in increased uncertainty. From our
heatmap studies, we also can see that generally, the sigma coefficient value has a greater effect on
uncertainty than the sigma offset. This is likely due to the sigma coefficient having a greater effect
on the sigma value (sigma equation) and consequently a greater impact on the overall uncertainty.
We have found that higher resolutions lead to smaller confidence intervals and lower uncertainty.
This is likely due to our use of Gaussian likelihood which decreases uncertainty with more data.
This demonstrates that Bayesian inference is a useful way to get error bars for inspection decisions
and how hyper-parameters can affect the size and usefulness of these error bars.
