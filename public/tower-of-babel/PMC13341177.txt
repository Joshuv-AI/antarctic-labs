Radioactive Iodine Therapy in Transition: Lessons from the 2024 Korean Thyroid Association and 2025 American Thyroid Association Guidelines
Authors: Kyeong Jin Kim
Published: 2026

The role of radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy in differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) has changed substantially, moving from routine postoperative administration toward a more individualized, risk-adapted strategy. The sequential publication of the 2024 Korean Thyroid Association (KTA) guidelines on the management of DTC [1] and the 2025 American Thyroid Association (ATA) management guidelines for adult patients with DTC [2] provides an opportunity to examine how international recommendations for RAI therapy are evolving. Although the two guidelines were developed within different health care systems, reimbursement structures, and clinical practice environments, they present a broadly convergent approach: selective de-escalation of RAI treatment in low-risk disease, risk-adapted activity selection, broader use of recombinant human thyroid-stimulating hormone (rhTSH) preparation, and greater integration of hybrid single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography (SPECT/CT) into post-therapy evaluation. The central message of these guideline updates is not a simple reduction in RAI use but a more precise alignment of the therapeutic purpose, administered activity, preparation method, and post-therapy assessment with recurrence risk and disease status. This commentary summarizes key changes, shared principles, and remaining differences between the two guidelines regarding RAI use in the initial management of DTC.

Clinical purpose of RAI therapy

One important conceptual clarification shared by the two guidelines is the explicit distinction among RAI use for remnant ablation, adjuvant therapy, and treatment of known disease [1,2]. Remnant ablation refers to RAI administration intended to eliminate residual normal thyroid tissue after total thyroidectomy, primarily to facilitate postoperative staging and long-term surveillance using serum thyroglobulin measurement and/or radioiodine imaging. Adjuvant therapy refers to RAI administered after complete surgical resection to eradicate presumed microscopic residual disease, thereby reducing recurrence risk and, in selected patients, disease-specific mortality. In contrast, treatment of known disease refers to RAI directed at persistent, recurrent, or metastatic structural disease, with the goal of improving progression-free, disease-specific, or overall survival, depending on disease extent and whether treatment is intended to be curative or palliative. This distinction is clinically important because the intended purpose of RAI therapy determines the appropriate administered activity, preparation strategy, and outcome measure.

Indications and risk-adapted dosing

Neither guideline recommends routine remnant ablation in low-risk DTC. Both support individualized decision-making for intermediate-risk patients and recommend RAI therapy for high-risk or metastatic disease. The recommended administered activities are largely comparable between the guidelines: 30–50 mCi for remnant ablation, 30–100 mCi for adjuvant therapy, and ≥100 mCi for treatment of known structural disease. Although neither guideline establishes an absolute upper limit for a single RAI treatment in advanced disease, both indicate that administered activities of up to 200 mCi may be appropriate in these patients. For activities exceeding 200 mCi, the ATA guideline recommends dosimetry-guided administration, whereas the KTA guideline also refers to the maximum tolerable safe activity. The 2025 ATA guideline subdivides the intermediate-risk category into ‘low-intermediate’ and ‘intermediate-high’ groups, but the recommended RAI approach for both subgroups remains similar, generally consisting of remnant ablation with or without adjuvant therapy using administered activities of 30–100 mCi. Although the ATA subdivision may provide a more granular framework for treatment decisions, the KTA approach retains a simpler category structure and relies on an integrated assessment of coexisting clinicopathologic risk factors to guide RAI use. Overall, both guidelines converge on an individualized, risk-adapted approach to RAI therapy.

Preferential use of rhTSH

One major update in both the KTA and ATA guidelines is the broader preference for rhTSH over thyroid hormone withdrawal (THW) for remnant ablation and adjuvant therapy. This shift is supported by evidence showing comparable therapeutic efficacy and better quality of life, with fewer hypothyroidism-related adverse effects. Notably, the 2025 ATA guideline further expands the potential role of rhTSH by stating that either rhTSH or THW may be used in selected patients with distant metastatic disease [2]. Although no prospective randomized controlled trials have directly evaluated this issue in the metastatic setting, several retrospective studies have found no significant differences in survival outcomes [3] or treatment response [4] between rhTSH and THW. Similarly, on the basis of a recent meta-analysis [5] showing no significant difference in disease progression between THW and rhTSH preparation in patients with distant metastases, the KTA guideline recommends that rhTSH may be considered on an individualized basis after weighing the expected benefits and potential risks [1]. These recommendations extend discussion of rhTSH beyond remnant ablation and adjuvant therapy to the more clinically complex setting of distant metastatic disease. Unlike its use in remnant ablation and adjuvant therapy, rhTSH use in this setting should remain individualized because relevant evidence from prospective randomized trials is lacking. Further prospective studies are therefore needed to define the optimal preparation strategy for these patients.

Expanding role of post-therapy SPECT/CT

Both the KTA and ATA guidelines acknowledge the clinical utility of SPECT/CT in conjunction with post-therapy whole-body scintigraphy for improving lesion localization, distinguishing physiological from pathological radioiodine uptake, and refining postoperative staging [1,2]. As ‘a conditional recommendation based on low-certainty evidence,’ the ATA guideline states that SPECT/CT may be performed when available, whereas the KTA guideline gives a ‘level 1 recommendation’ for additional SPECT/CT when abnormal or equivocal uptake is suspected on planar imaging. Although the recommendation grades differ, the practical implication is similar: SPECT/CT is most useful when planar post-therapy imaging shows abnormal, equivocal, or anatomically uncertain uptake.

Practical considerations for RAI preparation and safety

Both guidelines recommend a 1–2-week low-iodine diet before RAI administration, with a target urinary iodine excretion of <100 μg/day, and note that extending dietary restriction beyond 2 weeks provides no additional benefit [1,2]. The guidelines are also broadly aligned in recommending adequate thyroid-stimulating hormone stimulation, commonly defined as >30 mIU/L; individualized dosimetry considerations in older adults and patients with renal impairment; and avoidance of empiric high-activity RAI therapy in clearly non–iodine-avid disease.

Taken together, the 2024 KTA and 2025 ATA guidelines differ in several details, including risk stratification and recommendation strength, but both support more selective, individualized RAI therapy. This convergence is reflected in the avoidance of routine remnant ablation in low-risk disease, selective consideration of remnant ablation or adjuvant RAI therapy in intermediate-risk disease, patient-centered preparation with rhTSH, and broader use of SPECT/CT for combined anatomic and functional assessment. Remaining questions include the prospective validation of refined risk-stratification approaches, standardization of SPECT/CT acquisition and reporting, and cost-effectiveness analyses of rhTSH versus THW. These issues are particularly relevant in the Korean reimbursement context, where access to rhTSH for higher-dose RAI therapy in the range of 150 to 200 mCi may be restricted. The particular value of the KTA guideline lies in adapting these principles to the Korean clinical setting, where iodine intake, access to high-volume RAI centers, availability of hybrid imaging, and reimbursement policies may influence real-world practice. Future Korean research should identify which patient subgroups benefit most from rhTSH preparation and SPECT/CT-guided evaluation and should generate long-term outcome data to support locally optimized RAI management. The future of RAI therapy in DTC lies not in uniform de-escalation, but in more precise selection of patients, therapeutic goals, administered activities, and post-therapy assessment strategies.
