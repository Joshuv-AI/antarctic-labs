Tumor‐Derived Exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 Promotes Lymphatic Metastasis in Breast Cancer
Authors: Yafen Wang, Tianli Zhang, Xue Kong, Yao Tong, Yanru Li, Xiangjun Dong, Chuanxin Wang, Juan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLymph node (LN) metastasis is a critical indicator of poor prognosis in breast cancer (BC). BC‐derived exosomes influence intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment, driving metastatic progression. However, the precise mechanisms by which exosomes facilitate LN metastasis in BC remain unclear. We performed small RNA sequencing on serum exosomes to identify Piwi‐interacting RNAs (piRNAs) associated with BC LN metastasis. Functional investigations of exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 included in vitro assays for migration and tube formation of human lymphatic endothelial cells (HLECs), alongside an in vivo footpad‐popliteal LN metastasis model. Specific interactions between piR‐hsa‐28212 and TBX1 (T‐box transcription factor 1), as well as TBX1 and VEGF receptor 3 (VEGFR3), were validated through luciferase reporter assays. The stability of TBX1 mRNA was analyzed by actinomycin D assay. RNA pulldown, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP), and RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assays were conducted to explore the interaction between piR‐hsa‐28212 and METTL3. PiR‐hsa‐28212 was significantly upregulated in serum exosomes of BC patients with LN metastasis. Exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 enhanced HLECs migration and tube formation in vitro and promoted lymphangiogenesis and LN metastasis in vivo. Mechanistically, exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 transferred from BC cells to HLECs stabilized TBX1 mRNA, thereby upregulating VEGFR3 expression. In BC cells, piR‐hsa‐28212 directly bound to and stabilized METTL3 protein, modulating N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) methylation of vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGFC) mRNA and consequently increasing VEGFC expression and secretion. Collectively, these findings reveal an exosome‐mediated piRNA regulatory mechanism that synergistically amplifies VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling to drive LN metastasis in BC, highlighting piR‐hsa‐28212 as a potential therapeutic target. Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) stands as one of the most frequently diagnosed malignancies worldwide, accounting for approximately 32% of all cancers among women [1, 2]. Lymph node (LN) metastasis has emerged as a critical prognostic factor, correlating with poorer clinical outcomes in BC patients [3, 4]. Therefore, unraveling the molecular mechanisms that drive LN metastasis is pivotal for identifying novel therapeutic targets that can mitigate metastatic progression and improve patient outcomes. Despite notable advancements in cancer biology, the precise pathways underlying LN metastasis in BC remain insufficiently understood.

Among the well‐characterized mechanisms of LN metastasis, the vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGFC)/VEGF receptor 3 (VEGFR3) signaling axis has been widely recognized as a pivotal pathway across various malignancies [5, 6]. VEGFC binding to VEGFR3 activates tumor‐associated lymphangiogenesis, facilitating cancer cell dissemination to LNs, promoting tumor progression, and fostering distant metastases [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. As such, VEGFC is increasingly regarded as a promising therapeutic target to inhibit tumor‐induced lymphatic vessel (LV) expansion [15]. The VEGFC/VEGFR3 pathway plays a multifaceted role in LN metastasis, encompassing transcriptional regulation, chemokine production, and interactions with other signaling pathways [16, 17, 18]. Notably, in BC, VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling has been implicated in tumor growth and metastatic processes, with studies demonstrating its autocrine regulation of mammary tumor proliferation and spread [19]. Furthermore, FOXF2 deficiency in basal‐like BC cells enhances VEGFC/VEGFR3‐mediated lymphangiogenic mimicry, underscoring the axis's critical involvement in BC progression [20]. However, whether exosomal Piwi‐interacting RNAs (piRNAs) derived from tumor cells contribute to the regulation of VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling remains an open question.

Exosomes, nano‐sized vesicles ranging from 30 to 150 nm in diameter, play an integral role in cell‐to‐cell communication. These lipid bilayer‐encased vesicles are equipped with tissue‐specific surface proteins that enable targeted endocytosis by recipient cells [21]. Tumor‐derived exosomes are known to shuttle bioactive molecules, facilitating dynamic interactions between tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment (TME) [5, 22]. Exosomes can traverse the lymphatic system by penetrating the stromal matrix, effectively mediating communication between tumor cells and lymphatic structures [23, 24].

Non‐coding RNAs (ncRNAs) encapsulated within exosomes have garnered significant attention for their regulatory roles in tumor biology, particularly in modulating lymphangiogenesis and the reorganization of the lymphatic network to promote LN metastasis [25, 26, 27]. For instance, hepatocellular carcinoma‐derived exosomes enriched with miR‐296 enhance lymphangiogenesis through the EAG1/VEGFA signaling axis [28]. Similarly, bladder cancer cell‐derived exosomes containing the long non‐coding RNA (lncRNA) LNMAT2 stimulate lymphangiogenesis and LN metastasis by recruiting hnRNPA2B1 and augmenting H3K4 trimethylation at the PROX1 promoter [29]. In cervical squamous cell carcinoma, exosomal miR‐221‐3p facilitates LN metastasis by suppressing VASH1 expression and activating ERK1/2 and AKT signaling pathways [26]. These findings underscore the critical role of exosomal ncRNAs in promoting LN metastasis. However, the specific involvement of tumor‐derived exosomal piRNAs in regulating the VEGFC/VEGFR3 axis remains unexplored.

piRNAs are a class of small ncRNAs, typically 24–32 nucleotides in length, distinguished by a 2′‐O‐methylated 3′‐end. These molecules interact with PIWI proteins, evolutionarily conserved RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs), to form RNA‐induced silencing complexes (RISCs), thereby exerting either oncogenic or tumor‐suppressive effects in various cancers [30]. Recent findings have revealed that piRNAs can be packaged into exosomes, released into circulation, and subsequently internalized by recipient cells to modulate critical biological processes. The ability of exosomal piRNAs to achieve widespread distribution, coupled with their substantial regulatory capacity, positions them as potential key mediators of LN metastasis in cancers. However, their specific involvement in LN metastasis of BC remains poorly characterized.

In this study, we identified a marked upregulation of piR‐hsa‐28212 in serum‐derived exosomes from BC patients with LN metastasis compared to those without LN involvement. Functional analyses demonstrated that exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 significantly promoted lymphangiogenesis and LN metastasis in BC, both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, piR‐hsa‐28212 secreted by BC cells was transferred to human lymphatic endothelial cells (HLECs) via exosomes, where it stabilized TBX1 (T‐box transcription factor 1) mRNA, thereby upregulating VEGFR3 expression. Concurrently, in BC cells, piR‐hsa‐28212 activated the METTL3/IGF2BP2‐m6A pathway, resulting in enhanced expression and secretion of VEGFC. Collectively, these mechanisms synergistically amplified VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling, driving lymphangiogenesis and LN metastasis in BC. Our findings provided novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying LN metastasis in BC, highlighting piR‐hsa‐28212 as a critical regulator of VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling and a potential therapeutic target for patients with LN metastatic BC.

Material and Methods

Clinical Specimen Collection

Serum samples were collected from patients treated at the Second Hospital of Shandong University, whose BC diagnoses were pathologically confirmed by two independent professional pathologists. Blood samples were processed within 4 h of collection by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C to separate serum, followed by a second centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was transferred into sterile 1.5‐mL tubes and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis. Detailed clinical characteristics of the participants are provided in Table S1.

Exosome Purification and Characterization

Exosomes were isolated using ExoQuick Exosome Precipitation Solution (System Biosciences). Briefly, 250 μL of serum was mixed with 63 μL of ExoQuick solution and incubated at 4°C for 12 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 1500 g for 30 min at 4°C to pellet the exosomes, which were subsequently resuspended in 250 μL of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). The morphology and ultrastructure of the exosomes were assessed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL‐1200, Japan), while size distribution was analyzed using a Nanoparticle Tracking Analyzer (NTA; ZetaView, PMX) with its associated software. To confirm the presence of exosomes, Western blotting analysis was performed to detect the exosomal marker proteins TSG101 (Abcam, ab83) and CD9 (Proteintech, 60232‐1‐Ig).

Cell Lines and Culture

Human BC cell lines (MDA‐MB‐468, MDA‐MB‐231, MCF‐7, and BT549) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Type Culture Collection Committee, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). HLECs were purchased from ScienCell Research Laboratories (Carlsbad, CA, USA). BC cell lines were cultured under standard conditions: MDA‐MB‐231, MCF‐7, and MDA‐MB‐468 cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), while BT549 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. HLECs were grown in an endothelial cell medium (ECM, ScienCell) containing 5% FBS and 1% endothelial growth supplement (ScienCell). All cells were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

RNA Sequencing and Data Analysis

Small RNA sequencing was performed on serum samples collected from BC patients with LN metastasis (LN+) and those without LN involvement (LN−), comprising six samples in each group. Total RNA was extracted using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, USA) and a Nanodrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), and only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) greater than 7.0 were included in the sequencing analysis. Library construction and sequencing were carried out by Sinotech Genomics Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sequencing reads were mapped to the piRbank database using CLC Genomics Workbench 5.5 to quantify piRNA expression, with results expressed as transcripts per million (TPM).

Mouse Popliteal LN Metastasis Model

To establish a popliteal LN metastasis model, BALB/c nude mice (4–5 weeks old, weighing 18–20 g) were purchased from BEIJING HFK BIOSCIENCE Co. Ltd. (China). Footpad tumors and popliteal LNs were surgically excised and processed for paraffin embedding for subsequent analysis. MDA‐MB‐468 cells (5 × 106) suspended in 100 μL of PBS were carefully injected into the footpads of the mice. The mice were then randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 per group) and administered intratumoral injections of exosomes (20 μg/dose) every 3 days. When footpad tumor volumes reached approximately 200 mm3 (LN volume calculated as 0.5 × width2 × length), the primary tumors and popliteal LNs were excised. Tissue sections from both sites were analyzed using in situ hybridization (ISH), hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining, and immunofluorescence (IF) staining.

Exosome Internalization Analysis

To confirm the internalization of BC cell‐derived exosomes by HLECs, exosomes were labeled with PKH67 using the PKH67 Membrane EVs Labeling & Purification Kit (Rengen, China). The labeled exosomes were incubated with HLECs for 24 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Nuclei were stained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI), and the cells were visualized using a Zeiss confocal microscope system (Zeiss, Wetzlar, Germany) to confirm exosomal uptake.

Tube Formation Assay

HLECs were pretreated with exosomes for 48 h and subsequently seeded into a 96‐well plate coated with reduced growth factor Matrigel (BD Biosciences, CA, USA). The cells were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 6 h to allow the formation of LV‐like structures. Images of the formed vessels were captured using a light microscope and quantitatively analyzed using the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin in ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

RNA Pull‐Down Assay

RNA pull‐down assays were performed using the Pierce Magnetic RNA‐Protein Pull‐Down Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Biotinylated piR‐hsa‐28212 or its antisense sequence was incubated with cell lysates at 4°C overnight. Prewashed magnetic beads were then added to isolate piR‐hsa‐28212‐binding proteins. The captured protein complexes were subsequently analyzed by Western blotting to identify specific interactions.

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP) Assay

The RIP assay was performed using the Magna RIP RNA‐Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (17‐700, Millipore, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, MDA‐MB‐468 cells (1 × 10 [7]) were lysed using RIP lysis buffer, and the resulting lysates were incubated with magnetic beads conjugated to 5 μg of anti‐METTL3 antibody or anti‐IgG (negative control) for 6 h at 4°C. Immunoprecipitated RNA was subsequently eluted, purified, and dissolved in RNase‐free water. Quantification of the RNA was carried out using qRT‐PCR. Details of the antibodies used are listed in Table S2.

N6‐Methyladenosine (m6A) Dot Blot Assay

To evaluate m6A modifications, RNA samples were denatured by heating at 95°C for 5 min and then rapidly cooled on ice. The denatured RNA was spotted onto a nylon membrane, air‐dried for 10 min, and crosslinked using ultraviolet irradiation. The membrane was blocked in TBST containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h and then incubated with an anti‐m6A antibody (dilution 1:1000) followed by a horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG secondary antibody. The signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence. To confirm equal loading of RNA samples, methylene blue staining was applied to the membrane. Details of the antibody used are listed in Table S2.

m6A RNA Immunoprecipitation and qPCR (MeRIP‐qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method, ensuring RNA integrity. RNA fragmentation and m6A RIP were performed using the m6A MeRIP Kit (5203‐2, BersinBio) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Immunoprecipitated RNA was subjected to qRT‐PCR, as previously described, to quantify m6A‐modified transcripts.

RNA Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH)

FISH was performed on cells cultured on coverslips following the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, cells were washed, fixed, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100. Coverslips were then incubated overnight with specific RNA probes designed to target piR‐hsa‐28212. Following hybridization, the slides were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany). The sequence details of the piR‐hsa‐28212 probe used in the FISH analysis are provided in Table S3.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assays

Dual‐luciferase reporter assays were conducted using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (DL 101‐01, Vazyme, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Pretreated cells were harvested, washed three times with PBS, and lysed in PBL lysis buffer for 20 min. The resulting lysates were collected, and 10 μL of each sample was transferred to a 96‐well plate. Luciferase activity was measured by adding 100 μL of Luciferase Assay Reagent II, with luminescence signals recorded for 2 s using a Microplate Luminometer (Promega, USA). Renilla luciferase activity was subsequently measured as an internal control.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V9.0 software (GraphPad Prism Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Differences between groups were evaluated using Student's t‐test or chi‐square (χ 2 ) tests, as appropriate. Data were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from three independent experiments.

Additional details for the experimental procedures not fully described are provided in Supporting Information and Methods Doc S1.

Results

Exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 Is Associated With LN Metastasis in BC

To explore the role of exosomal piRNAs in LN metastasis of BC, exosomes were isolated and characterized from the serum of BC patients (Figure 1A). TEM revealed that the serum‐derived exosomes exhibited a characteristic cup‐shaped morphology (Figure 1B). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) further confirmed that the majority of these exosomes were approximately 100 nm in diameter (Figure 1C). Western blotting analysis demonstrated the presence of exosomal markers CD9 and TSG101, which were predominantly enriched in exosomal pellets compared to the exosome‐depleted supernatant (EDS) (Figure 1D).

(H) ROC curve analysis for evaluating the diagnostic potential of serum exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 for LN metastasis of BC. ns, not significant; ****p < 0.0001.

To identify exosomal piRNAs potentially involved in LN metastasis, small RNA sequencing was performed on serum exosomes from 12 bc patients (6 LN− and 6 LN+). This analysis revealed 37 differentially expressed piRNAs, which are visualized in the volcano plot and heatmap (Figure 1E; Figure S1A). To refine the selection of candidate piRNAs, we applied stringent criteria (fold change > 2, p < 0.05, and relative expression > 9) and excluded piRNAs for which qPCR primer design was unsuccessful. This process led to the identification of eight piRNAs, including six upregulated and two downregulated candidates (Figure 1F; Tables S4 and S5).

Subsequently, qRT‐PCR was conducted on exosomal piRNAs isolated from 28 LN− and 28 LN+ BC patients to validate the sequencing results. Among these candidates, piR‐hsa‐28212 was significantly upregulated in LN+ BC patients, as shown in Figure 1G. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis further demonstrated the predictive potential of piR‐hsa‐28212 for LN metastasis, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8839 (95% CI: 0.8000–0.9678, Figure 1H).

BC Cell‐Derived Exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 Facilitates Lymphangiogenesis in HLECs

To examine the expression and functional role of piR‐hsa‐28212 in BC cells and their secreted exosomes, we analyzed four BC cell lines (BT549, MDA‐MB‐468, MCF‐7, and MDA‐MB‐231). Exosomes isolated from the culture supernatants of these BC cells exhibited a round‐shaped morphology and an average size of approximately 100 nm, as determined by TEM and NTA (Figure S1B–D). The expression of exosomal markers CD9 and TSG101 was confirmed via Western blotting analysis (Figure S1E). Notably, piR‐hsa‐28212 was detected in all tested BC cell lines and their corresponding exosomes, with an apparent increase observed in exosomes compared to intracellular fractions (Figure 2A), consistent with the possibility that piR‐hsa‐28212 is transferred via BC cell‐derived exosomes.

To further investigate this, we modulated piR‐hsa‐28212 expression in BC cells. Lentiviral transfection of MDA‐MB‐468 cells with shRNA targeting piR‐hsa‐28212 (sh‐28212) resulted in a significant reduction of piR‐hsa‐28212 levels in both cells and their corresponding exosomes. Conversely, overexpression of piR‐hsa‐28212 using a mimic in MDA‐MB‐231 cells (mimic‐28212) led to increased levels of piR‐hsa‐28212 in exosomes (Figure 2B,C). These results indicated that intracellular modulation of piR‐hsa‐28212 directly influenced its levels in exosomes, further highlighting its enrichment in BC cell‐derived exosomes.

The uptake of exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 by HLECs was assessed using PKH67‐labeled exosomes. Following incubation, punctate green fluorescence was observed in HLECs, indicating successful internalization of the exosomes (Figure 2D). Additionally, qRT‐PCR analysis demonstrated a significant upregulation of piR‐hsa‐28212 levels in HLECs treated with BC cell‐derived exosomes compared to PBS‐treated controls (Figure 2E). This upregulation followed a time‐dependent pattern, increasing progressively over a 0–48 h period (Figure 2F). Furthermore, HLECs incubated with exosomes derived from piR‐hsa‐28212‐overexpressing BC cells exhibited significantly higher piR‐hsa‐28212 levels, whereas exosomes from piR‐hsa‐28212‐silenced cells showed reduced piR‐hsa‐28212 expression in HLECs (Figure 2G). These findings confirmed that exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 was effectively internalized by HLECs.

To eliminate the possibility of endogenous induction of piR‐hsa‐28212 in HLECs, cells were treated with the RNA polymerase II inhibitor ActD and co‐cultured with exosomes from BC cells either overexpressing or silencing piR‐hsa‐28212. No significant differences in piR‐hsa‐28212 expression were observed between the DMSO and ActD groups (Figure 2H), indicating that the observed upregulation of piR‐hsa‐28212 in HLECs was solely due to the intercellular transfer via exosomes.

Tube formation and migration assays were performed to assess the regulatory role of exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 in lymphangiogenesis. Exosomes from piR‐hsa‐28212‐silenced MDA‐MB‐468 cells inhibited tube formation and migration in HLECs, as indicated by reduced tube length, node number, mesh formation, and migrated cell number (Figure 2I–M). In contrast, exosomes derived from piR‐hsa‐28212‐overexpressing MDA‐MB‐231 cells significantly enhanced these processes, promoting lymphangiogenesis in vitro.

Our findings demonstrated that piR‐hsa‐28212 was abundantly enriched in BC cell‐secreted exosomes, which were readily internalized by HLECs. Exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 played a pivotal role in promoting lymphangiogenesis in vitro, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target in BC.

Exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 Enhances LN Metastasis of BC In Vivo

The role of exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 in promoting lymphangiogenesis and LN metastasis was assessed using a footpad‐popliteal LN metastasis model. Mice were inoculated with MDA‐MB‐468 cells in the footpad and received intratumoral injections of either MDA‐MB‐468‐EXOsh‐NC or MDA‐MB‐468‐EXOsh‐28212 every 3 days. Upon primary tumor growth reaching approximately 200 mm3, mice were sacrificed, and the footpad tumors, along with associated popliteal LNs, were excised for analysis (Figure 3A,B). Notably, treatment with MDA‐MB‐468‐EXOsh‐28212 significantly reduced both the volume of the popliteal LNs and the incidence of histologically confirmed LN metastasis compared to the control group (Figure 3C–F).

ISH and IF analyses revealed that tumors treated with MDA‐MB‐468‐EXOsh‐28212 exhibited lower expression levels of piR‐hsa‐28212 and LYVE‐1 compared to those treated with MDA‐MB‐468‐EXOsh‐NC. Similarly, LYVE‐1 and piR‐hsa‐28212 expression levels in peritumoral tissue were significantly reduced compared to intratumoral levels in the MDA‐MB‐468‐EXOsh‐28212‐treated group (Figure 3G). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 enhanced lymphangiogenesis and LN metastasis in vivo.

Exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 Stabilizes TBX1 mRNA to Upregulate VEGFR3 Expression in HLECs

Given the critical role of VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling in LN metastasis, we investigated whether exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 regulated this pathway. VEGFR3 expression was significantly downregulated in HLECs treated with MDA‐MB‐468‐EXOsh‐28212, while incubation with MDA‐MB‐231‐EXOmimic‐28212 resulted in increased VEGFR3 expression (Figure 4A–C). These observations suggested that exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 modulated VEGFR3 expression in HLECs.

Previous studies have reported that VEGFR3 expression is regulated by TBX1 via a conserved T‐box binding element (TBE) within the VEGFR3 gene [31]. Using the miRanda algorithm, we identified a potential binding site for piR‐hsa‐28212 within the TBX1 3′‐untranslated regions (3′‐UTR) (Figure 4D). To validate this interaction, a dual‐luciferase reporter assay was performed. Overexpression of piR‐hsa‐28212 in HLECs significantly enhanced the luciferase activity of the wild‐type TBX1 3′‐UTR reporter, whereas this effect was abolished when the binding site was mutated (Figure 4E). Similarly, luciferase activity was significantly reduced in HLECs transfected with a VEGFR3 reporter construct containing a mutated TBE compared to the wild‐type VEGFR3 construct (Figure 4F,G), confirming the critical role of TBE in mediating the interaction between TBX1 and VEGFR3.

Further experiments demonstrated that TBX1 mRNA and protein levels were significantly reduced in HLECs treated with MDA‐MB‐468‐EXOsh‐28212 but were elevated in cells exposed to MDA‐MB‐231‐EXOmimic‐28212 (Figure 4H–J). Using ActD assays, we observed that exosomes derived from piR‐hsa‐28212‐suppressed BC cells significantly reduced the half‐life of TBX1 mRNA in HLECs (Figure 4K). These results indicated that piR‐hsa‐28212 stabilized TBX1 mRNA, thereby enhancing its expression.

To further elucidate the role of TBX1 in VEGFR3 regulation, TBX1 was silenced in HLECs using siRNA. Both qRT‐PCR and Western blotting analyses showed a marked reduction in VEGFR3 expression following TBX1 knockdown (Figure 4L,M). Importantly, the depletion of TBX1 partially reversed the piR‐hsa‐28212‐induced upregulation of VEGFR3 (Figure 4N). Functionally, HLECs treated with MDA‐MB‐231‐EXOmimic‐28212 exhibited significantly enhanced tube formation and migration, whereas TBX1 suppression attenuated these effects (Figure 4O–S).

PiR‐hsa‐28212 Enhances BC Cell Migration and Invasion and Regulates VEGFC Expression

To investigate the biological functions of piR‐hsa‐28212 in BC cells, its expression was modulated using a mimic or an inhibitor. RT‐PCR analysis confirmed efficient transfection, showing a significant reduction of piR‐hsa‐28212 expression in MDA‐MB‐468 and MCF‐7 cells transfected with the inhibitor, while the mimic significantly increased its expression in MDA‐MB‐231 and BT549 cells (Figure S2A,B). Functional analysis using Transwell assays revealed that piR‐hsa‐28212 depletion markedly suppressed the migration and invasion abilities of MDA‐MB‐468 and MCF‐7 cells (Figure 5A,B; Figure S2C,D). Conversely, piR‐hsa‐28212 overexpression significantly enhanced these capabilities in MDA‐MB‐231 and BT549 cells (Figure 5C,D; Figure S2E,F). These results indicated that piR‐hsa‐28212 promoted the migration and invasion of BC cells in vitro.

Given our earlier findings that exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 upregulated VEGFR3 expression in HLECs, we explored whether it also regulated VEGFC expression in BC cells. VEGFC is known to exert its biological functions by binding to VEGFR3, playing a critical role in LV development and tumor metastasis [32, 33, 34]. Our results showed that suppression of piR‐hsa‐28212 in MDA‐MB‐468 cells significantly reduced VEGFC expression and secretion, whereas its overexpression in MDA‐MB‐231 cells led to the opposite effect (Figure 5E–G). These findings suggested that piR‐hsa‐28212 regulated VEGFC expression in BC cells, further contributing to tumor progression.

PiR‐hsa‐28212 Specifically Binds to METTL3 and Regulates m6A Modification of VEGFC mRNA

To uncover the molecular mechanisms by which piR‐hsa‐28212 regulated VEGFC expression, we examined its influence on m6A modifications. Dot blot analysis revealed that global m6A levels were reduced in MDA‐MB‐468 cells transfected with sh‐28212, whereas cells transfected with mimic‐28212 exhibited increased m6A levels (Figure 5H,I). Using SRAMP, a computational tool for predicting m6A modification sites, we identified multiple potential m6A sites on VEGFC mRNA (Table S6). This led to the hypothesis that piR‐hsa‐28212 regulated m6A modifications on VEGFC.

MeRIP‐qPCR analysis demonstrated that the depletion of piR‐hsa‐28212 decreased m6A levels on VEGFC mRNA, whereas its overexpression increased m6A modification (Figure 5J). These results suggested that piR‐hsa‐28212 enhanced m6A methylation of VEGFC mRNA in BC cells, potentially influencing its expression.

Since m6A modifications are predominantly regulated by RNA methyltransferases and demethylases [35, 36, 37, 38], we investigated whether piR‐hsa‐28212 modulated m6A levels by interacting with m6A‐modifying enzymes. Biotin‐streptavidin pull‐down assays using biotinylated piR‐hsa‐28212 in BC cells revealed a specific interaction between piR‐hsa‐28212 and METTL3, a key m6A methyltransferase. Other components of the m6A complex, including WTAP, ALKBH5, FTO, and METTL14, were not detected (Figure 5K).

RIP assays coupled with qRT‐PCR further confirmed that piR‐hsa‐28212 was specifically enriched by METTL3 (Figure 5L). FISH and immunostaining analyses demonstrated co‐localization of piR‐hsa‐28212 and METTL3 within BC cells (Figure 5M). Additionally, the depletion of piR‐hsa‐28212 reduced METTL3 protein levels, while its overexpression increased METTL3 expression (Figure 5N,O). To further determine whether piR‐hsa‐28212 affects METTL3 protein stability, CHX chase assays were performed. The results showed that depletion of piR‐hsa‐28212 accelerated METTL3 protein degradation, whereas overexpression of piR‐hsa‐28212 maintained METTL3 protein stability after CHX treatment (Figure 5P,Q). These findings collectively indicated that piR‐hsa‐28212 interacted with METTL3 and enhanced its protein stability, thereby regulating m6A modification of VEGFC mRNA and further elucidating its role in promoting tumor progression in BC cells.

PiR‐hsa‐28212 Regulates VEGFC Expression and Secretion via the METTL3/IGF2BP2‐m6A Pathway in BC Cells

To investigate whether METTL3 was essential for piR‐hsa‐28212‐mediated regulation of VEGFC expression in BC cells, we performed qRT‐PCR and Western blotting analyses. The results demonstrated that METTL3 depletion significantly reduced VEGFC expression, while METTL3 overexpression markedly increased VEGFC levels (Figure 6A–D). Additionally, ELISA revealed that METTL3 suppression decreased VEGFC secretion, whereas METTL3 overexpression led to enhanced VEGFC secretion (Figure 6E). These findings underscored METTL3's pivotal role in piR‐hsa‐28212‐induced VEGFC expression and secretion.

Functionally, Transwell assays showed that METTL3 depletion significantly impaired the migration and invasion abilities of MDA‐MB‐468 cells (Figure 6F,G), whereas METTL3 overexpression greatly enhanced these capabilities in MDA‐MB‐231 cells (Figure S3A,B).

To further validate the involvement of METTL3 in piR‐hsa‐28212‐mediated VEGFC regulation, rescue experiments were conducted. METTL3 overexpression partially rescued the reduced VEGFC expression and suppressed migration and invasion induced by piR‐hsa‐28212 depletion (Figure 6H–6J). Conversely, METTL3 depletion partially reversed the increased VEGFC expression and enhanced migration and invasion caused by piR‐hsa‐28212 overexpression (Figure 6K; Figure S3C,D). These results confirmed that piR‐hsa‐28212 regulated VEGFC expression and secretion, as well as the migratory and invasive capabilities of BC cells, by interacting with METTL3.

In addition to METTL3, we investigated the role of IGF2BP2, an m6A reader protein previously shown to bind VEGFC mRNA and enhance VEGFC levels in adipose‐derived mesenchymal stem cells [39]. IGF2BP2 depletion in BC cells resulted in a significant reduction in VEGFC expression (Figure 6L,M), suggesting that IGF2BP2 played a role in upregulating VEGFC expression in BC cells. To further determine whether IGF2BP2 mediated the piR‐hsa‐28212‐induced upregulation of VEGFC, rescue experiments were performed in MDA‐MB‐231 cells overexpressing piR‐hsa‐28212. IGF2BP2 knockdown partially counteracted the increased expression and secretion of VEGFC induced by piR‐hsa‐28212 overexpression (Figure 6N,O). Collectively, these findings revealed that piR‐hsa‐28212 regulated VEGFC expression and secretion through the METTL3/IGF2BP2‐m6A pathway. This mechanism further highlighted the critical role of piR‐hsa‐28212 in promoting VEGFC‐mediated tumor progression in BC cells.

Discussion

Metastasis remains the primary cause of mortality in BC patients [40]. The spread of cancer cells from the primary tumor occurs through blood vessels or LVs, with the extent of axillary LN metastasis serving as the most critical predictor of distant metastasis and patient survival [40]. Notably, a higher density of LVs within and surrounding the tumor strongly correlates with increased LN metastasis and worse clinical outcomes [41]. Among the VEGF family, VEGFC binds to VEGFR3 on lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), stimulating lymphangiogenesis and facilitating the entry of tumor cells into the lymphatic system [42, 43, 44]. This study elucidated the role of exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 in activating VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling, providing new insights into VEGFC‐mediated LN metastasis in BC. Secreted by BC cells, piR‐hsa‐28212 was enriched in exosomes, which were internalized by HLECs. In HLECs, piR‐hsa‐28212 stabilized TBX1 mRNA to activate the TBX1/VEGFR3 pathway. Simultaneously, in BC cells, it enhanced VEGFC expression and secretion via the METTL3/IGF2BP2‐m6A pathway.

Exosomes, nanoscale biological vesicles responsible for the transfer of diverse biomolecules, play a pivotal role in tumor progression [5, 22]. Their ability to mediate LN metastasis through the transfer of RNA has been well‐documented [29, 45, 46]. The selective sorting of exosomal RNAs is mediated by RBPs such as heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A2/B1 (hnRNPA2B1), which governs the incorporation of specific RNAs into exosomes by recognizing sequence motifs [47, 48]. This process has been observed for microRNAs and lncRNAs [48, 49]. In this study, piR‐hsa‐28212 was shown to be significantly enriched in exosomes secreted by BC cells compared to its intracellular levels, with its upregulation exhibiting a time‐dependent pattern. Exosomal internalization assays and ActD experiments further confirmed that BC‐derived exosomes transfer piR‐hsa‐28212 to HLECs. However, the precise mechanism underlying the packaging of piR‐hsa‐28212 into exosomes remains unexplored.

Exosomes have emerged as promising therapeutic targets in cancer treatment [50, 51, 52]. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms governing the export of piR‐hsa‐28212 into exosomes could lead to innovative strategies for inhibiting its exosome‐mediated role in LN metastasis. Potential approaches may include chemically modified, sequence‐specific antisense oligonucleotides or antagomirs to directly inhibit piR‐hsa‐28212 [53], as well as strategies aimed at disrupting exosome‐mediated transfer [54], including inhibition of exosome biogenesis or release [55], interference with selective RNA cargo sorting and loading [47], or blockade of recipient‐cell uptake, thereby limiting the delivery of tumor‐derived piR‐hsa‐28212 from BC cells to HLECs.

Studies have already demonstrated the feasibility of targeting exosomal microRNAs for therapeutic purposes [56, 57]. Although some RNA interference therapies have been discontinued due to safety concerns [58], the use of piRNAs offers distinct advantages. Unlike microRNAs, piRNAs are predominantly expressed in germ cells and cancer cells, conferring greater target specificity and potentially reducing off‐target effects [59]. This higher specificity, combined with their unique expression patterns, underscores the therapeutic potential of RNA interference strategies targeting the PIWI/piRNA pathway in cancer treatment. These findings support the potential of exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 as a therapeutic target in BC LN metastasis. Future studies are needed to validate these potential intervention strategies and to further evaluate their delivery efficiency, specificity, and safety.

Early detection and accurate assessment of metastasis in BC are essential for effective treatment and monitoring of disease progression. Currently, ultrasonography, mammography, and histopathological examination remain the gold standard for BC diagnosis and metastasis evaluation. However, these methods have inherent limitations, including invasiveness and challenges in real‐time monitoring. Liquid biopsies, which utilize blood‐derived molecular markers, have emerged as a less invasive and more effective approach for long‐term monitoring compared to traditional tissue biopsies [60]. Exosomes, which are abundant and stable in the bloodstream due to their protective lipid bilayer membranes, offer a promising platform for this approach. Their capacity to carry disease‐specific biomarkers makes them ideal candidates for diagnostic applications [61, 62, 63].

Numerous studies have demonstrated the diagnostic potential of exosomes, highlighting their disease‐specific biomolecular cargo, including proteins, RNAs, and lipids [64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69]. In this study, piRNA sequencing revealed that piR‐hsa‐28212 was significantly overexpressed in serum exosomes derived from BC patients with LN metastasis. This finding was further validated in a cohort of 28 paired BC patients (28 LN+ and 28 LN−), underscoring the potential of serum exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 as a predictive marker for LN metastasis. Expanding the sample size in future studies could further enhance the predictive accuracy and clinical utility of piR‐hsa‐28212 for early metastasis detection.

VEGFR3 plays a critical role in both lymphangiogenesis and angiogenesis [70]. It has been reported that TBX1, a member of the phylogenetically conserved T‐box gene family, regulates VEGFR3 expression through its DNA‐binding domain [31, 71]. TBX1 has been implicated in various physiological and pathological conditions, including heart disease, hypoparathyroidism, and acute kidney injury [72, 73, 74]. In cancer, aberrant TBX1 expression has been observed across multiple malignancies. For example, TBX1 expression is downregulated in parathyroid tumors [75] and thyroid cancer [76], whereas elevated TBX1 levels have been reported in basal cell carcinoma [77, 78].

While ncRNAs, including piRNAs, are traditionally associated with mRNA degradation through the formation of silencing complexes, recent findings suggest a broader functional spectrum. piRNAs have been shown to preferentially suppress transposable element transcription and induce post‐transcriptional gene silencing [79]. Interestingly, in this study, exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 was found to upregulate, rather than suppress, TBX1 expression in HLECs. Mechanistically, piR‐hsa‐28212 bound to a complementary sequence within the 3′ UTR of TBX1, thereby stabilizing its mRNA and preventing degradation. This result aligned with previous studies demonstrating that piRNAs can upregulate downstream gene expression [80, 81].

Our findings shed light on the novel role of exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 in regulating TBX1 expression and enhancing VEGFR3‐dependent lymphangiogenesis. These results challenge the conventional understanding of piRNA function, expanding its known mechanisms of gene regulation. Further exploration into the diverse roles of piRNAs in gene modification and stability may open new avenues for therapeutic interventions targeting metastasis in BC.

M6A methylation, one of the most abundant RNA modifications, plays a crucial role in regulating RNA splicing, stability, and translation [82, 83]. Emerging evidence highlights the impact of ncRNAs on disease development and progression through m6A modifications. For instance, piRNA CHAPIR promotes cardiac hypertrophy and remodeling by interacting with METTL3 to inhibit the m6A methylation of Parp10 mRNA [84]. Similarly, piRNA‐14,633 enhances m6A RNA methylation and stabilizes METTL14 mRNA, driving the progression of cervical cancer [81]. In diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma, piRNA‐30,473 increases m6A levels by upregulating WTAP, which in turn boosts HK2 expression and promotes tumor progression [80].

In our present study, we demonstrated that piR‐hsa‐28212 interacted with METTL3, maintained METTL3 protein stability, and enhanced the m6A modification of VEGFC in BC cells. Previous research has shown that m6A modifications generally stabilize mRNAs, a process mediated by m6A “reader” proteins such as IGF2BPs [80, 85, 86]. Consistent with this, our findings revealed that IGF2BP2 acted as an m6A reader in the METTL3‐mediated modification of VEGFC, contributing to its expression and secretion. Collectively, these results emphasized the critical role of m6A methylation in LN metastasis of BC, with piR‐hsa‐28212 acting as a key regulator of this process.

In conclusion, our study highlighted the pivotal role of piR‐hsa‐28212 in promoting lymphangiogenesis and LN metastasis in BC through activation of the VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling pathway (Figure 7). These findings suggested that targeting exosomal piR‐hsa‐28212 to mitigate the pathological overactivation of VEGFC/VEGFR3 signaling held significant potential as a therapeutic strategy for patients with LN metastatic BC.
