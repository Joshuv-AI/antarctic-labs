Green synthesis, one-pot reaction, biological evaluation, and molecular docking studies of new pyrazolo[3,4-d]tetrazolo[1,5-a]pyrimidine derivatives
Authors: Mohamed Shaker S Adam, Ibrahim Alfurayj, Mostafa Y Nassar, Ahmed Khalil, Moumen S Kamel, Tarek El-Dabea, Mohammed Er-rajy, Mahmoud Abd El Aleem Ali Ali El-Remaily
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe work represents a synthesized strategy for a range of novel pyrazolotriazole pyrimidine derivatives using a one-pot, three-component reaction involving 5-aminotetrazole, aromatic aldehydes, and 3-methyl-5-pyrazolone. The structure of the synthesized compounds was confirmed by IR, NMR, and mass spectroscopies. Their anticancer properties were assessed against various cell lines using the in vitro MTT method. Among the tested compounds, 4c demonstrated the most potent antitumor activity. Compound 4c showed an IC50 value of 6.8 µM against MDA-MB-231 cells, while 4c exhibited an IC50 value of 4.9 µM against HCT-116 cells. These results were compared to the performance of Vinblastine used as a reference compound. Antimicrobial assays were conducted on all the newly prepared compounds against selected (bacterial & fungal) strains. The results revealed that compounds (4c, 4a, 4d, and 4f) demonstrated the most potent antibacterial and antifungal properties among the studied series. Moreover, antioxidant activity was studied using the DPPH assay, and the hydroxyl-substituted derivative 4f showed the strongest free radical scavenging ability (IC50 = 26.9 ± 1.5 µg/mL), which is attributed to the presence of hydroxyl groups that are known to stabilize radicals through hydrogen donation. Furthermore, the correlation between the compounds’ structures as well as their biological activities was investigated for a better understanding of their mechanisms of action. Introduction

Pyrazolo triazole pyrimidine compounds, which structurally are bioisosteric to purines, exhibit a scale of therapeutic effects, involving fighting cancer & microbes1. Combining these two features in a single molecule could be especially valuable during cancer therapy, as patients regularly become more sensitive to bacterial diseases2–4. However, research into these dual-action compounds is defined, with even limited studies focusing on enhancing both anti-infection and antitumor capabilities instantaneously5. Cancer itself, along with several cancer treatments as well as correlated medical systems, can reduce the body’s natural defenses against bacterial attacks. Staphylococcus aureus has been recognized as a significant danger to cancer patients. There remains a pressing need for extensive research to identify more potent and specific compounds that target cancer, to advance anticancer therapies3–9. In recent years, fused heterocycles containing nitrogen have become increasingly important in pharmaceutical research due to their broad applications. These compounds have also proven to be valuable intermediates in the synthesis of selective agents targeting human A3 adenosine receptors10. Motivated by these previous findings, our research focuses on creating new fused pyrazolo-triazolo-pyrimidine compounds. The work aimed to explore the potential of these novel substances, which retain the core pyrazolopyrimidine structure, as innovative anticancer agents. Particularly noteworthy are the various bicyclic structures that combine triazoles, pyrazoles, and pyrimidines. The advancement of exact hetero-cyclic structures was growing because of their extensive operations in several areas of biochemistry, medicine, and other industries. Like, Pyrazolo-triazolo-pyrimidine has presented substantial applications in medical chemistry because of its remarkable chemical structure features. The synthesized compounds are established as prospective anti-microbial, anti-cancer, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory agents7–11. The expansive applications for target compounds analogs have significantly stimulated the growth of the synthesis of new derivatives of the target nucleus. The synthesis of Pyrazolo-triazolo-pyrimidine derivatives is a tempting example for these (MCRs). A study on the synthesized compounds demonstrated the medicinal features of the target compounds. Furthermore, these synthesized compounds are applied as a receptor inverse agonist, anti-parasitic, as well as anti-Alzheimer’s12–14. Hence, we have given a specialized approach to the synthesis of pyrazolo-triazolo-pyrimidine derivatives applying simple materials, eco-friendly processes, as well as a simple examination that has a notable function. Research suggests that tested compounds containing a pyrazolo-triazolo-pyrimidine core are highly effective, particularly in the scope of synthetic drugs. Various processes have been developed to incorporate the synthesized compound into fused heterocycles. These synthetic approaches involve cyclo-condensation reactions, rearrangements, cyclization, in addition to multi-component couplings concerning varied reagents such as Methyl-5-pyrazolone, amino-triazoles, as well as various aldehydes. Other techniques apply cross-coupling reactions, condensations among detailed reagents, and one-pot reactions concerning multiple components15–18. Recent improvements have managed to develop an updated process applying a multi-component reaction of active methylene compounds, various aldehydes, and triazole analogs, accelerated by (acid or base) catalysts. Several catalytic systems have been reported, including bismuth-loaded fluorapatite, cyclodextrins, acidic ionic liquids, as well as specific acids or complexes. The literature features the significance of pyrazolo-triazolo-pyrimidine as a potential compound in various topics, especially in drug development19–22. Accordingly, there is a growing demand for new techniques to synthesize new derivatives. This has motivated researchers to contribute to the development of innovative procedures for preparing these compounds. Building on our group’s previous work with condensed pyrazole-triazolo-pyrimidines, which have shown promising biological activity, we present in this paper a new one-pot, three-component synthesis method for these compounds. We also report initial findings from in vitro tests evaluating their antimicrobial and anticancer properties. Multi-component reactions (MCRs) have gained popularity in the field of drug discovery due to several advantages: They simplify the experimental process, they are economical in terms of atoms and steps, they reduce reaction times, and they often yield high amounts of product. In this study, we examine the antibacterial and anticancer effects of a selected group of pyrazole-triazolo-pyrimidines. The synthesized derivatives were evaluated for anticancer, antimicrobial, and antioxidant activities to explore their multi-target pharmacological potential. Early-stage multi-activity assessment allows identification of compounds with synergistic effects and broad therapeutic relevance. To further support the experimental findings, molecular docking studies were conducted to investigate the binding interactions of the synthesized compounds with relevant biological targets, as reported in previous literature23,24.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were performed to explore the potential binding modes of the most biologically active compounds (4c and 4f) within selected protein targets relevant to the performed biological assays. This computational analysis aimed to generate mechanistic hypotheses and examine whether the experimental activity trends could be rationalized at the molecular level. Docking results are presented as supportive evidence rather than definitive proof of enzymatic inhibition25,26. The three-dimensional structures of compounds 4c and 4f were constructed using ChemDraw 3D and energy-minimized using the MM2 force field before docking. Protein structures were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank and prepared by removing crystallographic water molecules, adding polar hydrogens, assigning Kollman charges, and correcting missing residues where necessary27,28. Discovery 2020 software was then used to explore the interactions between ligands and the protein receptor while removing water molecules, correcting missing side-chain residues, and fusing non-polar hydrogens29. After preparing the protein and ligand, we used AutoDock Tools software to perform the molecular docking30,31. Docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Tools, applying the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm. A grid box of 60 × 60 × 60 points was centered on the active site of each protein. Ten independent docking runs were carried out for each ligand, and the lowest binding free energy conformations were selected for analysis32. The following targets were selected based on their established biological relevance: 5N1P: Peptidoglycan N-acetylglucosamine deacetylase from Bacillus cereus, associated with bacterial cell wall remodeling. 1ROM: Fungal nitric oxide reductase from Fusarium oxysporum, implicated in fungal respiration. 2CDU: NAD(P)H oxidase, involved in redox regulation and oxidative stress. 4TV3: Catalytic subunit of PI3Kα (p110α), a well-known oncogenic kinase frequently dysregulated in multiple cancers.

On the basis of the results of the biological tests, we have chosen the following receptors. In the antibacterial study, we utilized the Cereus receptor, obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 5N1P), with a resolution of 1.45 Å33. A grid was created using the following parameters: X = -28.380, Y = 11.697, and Z = 56.266 Å. In the antifungal study, the crystal structure chosen is that corresponding to the PDB identifier: 1ROM34. For the protein-ligand docking studies, a grid was created using the following parameters: X = 52.979 Å, Y = 7.630 Å, and Z = 17.886 Å. Also, in the antifungal study, the crystal structure chosen is that corresponding to the PDB identifier: 2CDU35. For the protein-ligand docking studies, a grid was created using the following parameters: X = 10.174 Å, Y = 0.693 Å, and Z = 6.094 Å. At last, for the anticancer study, the crystal structure was retrieved from the RCSB (PDB ID: 4TV3)36. For the protein-ligand docking studies, a grid was created using the following parameters: X = 4.158 Å, Y = 3.966 Å, and Z = 20.933Å.

Synthesis of pyrazole triazolo pyrimidines derivatives

In our initial experiments, we combined 5-aminotetrazole (1) (2 mmol), aromatic aldehydes (2) (2 mmol), and 3-Methyl-5-pyrazolone (3) (2 mmol) in an ethanol solution37–41. We then added ammonium dihydrogen phosphate as a catalyst at 5 mol%. The mixture was refluxed while stirring for 5 h. We monitored the reaction progress using thin-layer chromatography (TLC). Once the reaction was complete, we removed the solvent under vacuum. This process yielded the desired product 4a at 67% efficiency.

Results and discussion

The7-methyl-8-phenyl-5,8-dihydro-4 H-pyrazolo[3,4-d]tetrazolo[1,5-a]pyrimidine derivatives was prepared in an operationally straightforward, one-pot, three-component domino reaction. We use the reactivity of the intrinsic reactant 5-aminotetrazole (1), aromatic aldehydes (2), and 3-methyl-5-pyrazolone (3) in mild, environmentally friendly conditions (Scheme 1). After screening a large number of catalysts and solvents, the ammonium dihydrogen phosphate (NH4H2PO4) 5 mol % in refluxing ethanol became the best system, giving the yield of the desirable products 4a -i in good to excellent yields (67–89 mol % ) under the shortest reaction times (3–6 h).

synthesis of 7-methyl-8-phenyl-5,8-dihydro-4 H-pyrazolo[3,4-d]tetrazolo[1,5-a]pyrimidine derivatives (4a-i) using ADHP catalyst.

The reaction follows a very clear cascade (Scheme 2). The hypothesized process consists of four major steps based on the studies: tautomerization, intramolecular cyclization, Michael addition, and Knoevenagel condensation. The aldehyde and (NH2) of the 5-aminotetrazole mix to create molecule (A) via a catalyzed Knoevenagel condensation. The ammonium dihydrogen phosphate (ADHP) catalyst activates the CHO groups in the aldehyde (1) and (NH2) of the amino tetrazole, resulting in the formation of a double bond. 3-Methyl-5-pyrazolone (3) then attacks intermediate (A) to form intermediate (B) by attacking the double bond with CH2. The intermediate molecule B underwent intramolecular Michael addition between its NH and CO groups, yielding an intermediate product C. This product was then transformed to the final product 4a-i by isomerization.

Suggested process for production of 7-methyl-8-phenyl-5,8-dihydro-4 H-pyrazolo[3,4-d]tetrazolo[1,5-a]pyrimidine derivatives (4a-i).

The structures of all the compounds (4a–i) synthesized in the process were unambiguously determined by extensive spectroscopic examination. The ¹H NMR spectra (400 MHz, DMSO-d6) showed typical signals which proved the creation of pyrazolo[3,4-d]1,2,4 triazolo[1,5-a]pyrimidine structure. All derivatives demonstrated a sharp singlet with an integrating value of three protons at a range of 2.12–2.33 ppm, which represents the pyrazole methyl group, and a second singlet at 4.05–4.67 ppm representing the C-4 methine proton. Patterns in the aromatic region indicated substitution of the pendant aryl ring: para-substituted (4a-4f, 4j) and meta-substituted (4 h, 4i) compounds were revealed by two typical doublets (J8 Hz) with two protons each, and more complex multiple patterns respectively. One unique singlet, which could be ascribed to the triazole CH proton, was observed in the deshelled region 7.61–8.52 ppm, and two exchangeable broad singlets, which could be attributed to the pyrazole and triazole NH protons, were also observed downfield between 9.50 and 10.79 ppm, and this confirmed the existence of the fused heterocyclic system. In the case of the hydroxyl-substituted analog 4f, there was an extra interchangeable signal at 11.30 ppm, which identified the phenolic OH group. The suggested structures were further supported by the ¹³C NMR spectra, which showed the anticipated number of carbon resonances with the methyl carbon of the pyrazole at 12.48 and 16.21 ppm and the methine carbon at δ 59.39–62.33 ppm. The aromatic/heteroaromatic region exhibited 8–10 discrete signals, which were observed in the aromatic/heteroaromatic region between 100 and 165 ppm with quaternary carbons being in close proximity to the nitrogen atom (quaternary signals) found as expected (152–163 ppm), and were due to the highly conjugated heterocyclic core.

Through comparison with the earlier reported synthetic protocols of the pyrazolo -triazolo -pyrimidine derivatives, most of which require expensive or hazardous catalysts (such as Bi₂O₃/FAp, acidic ionic liquids, p‑TsOH) require long reaction times, and require complicated multi-step purifications, our method provides several significant advantages: (i) the use of ammonium dihydrogen phosphate as a low-cost, non-toxic, and readily available catalyst; (ii) a one-pot, three‑component operation under reflux in ethanol, a green solvent; (iii) short reaction times (3–6 h); (iv) good to excellent isolated yields (67–89%); and (v) avoidance of column chromatography in most cases. These qualities are directly in line with the principles of sustainable chemistry and make the protocol especially appealing to gram-scale synthesis.

Therapeutic studies

Antimicrobial assay

The antimicrobial activity of compounds (4a–4i) was evaluated against Gram-negative bacteria (S. Typhimurium and E. coli), Gram-positive B. cereus, and three fungal strains (A. flavus, G. candidum, and F. oxysporum)42–44. Ciprofloxacin and Fluconazole were used as antibacterial and antifungal reference drugs, respectively, under identical experimental conditions.

Antibacterial activity

Among the tested derivatives, compound 4c (H) demonstrated the strongest overall antibacterial activity, exhibiting inhibition zones of 23.0 ± 1.3 mm (S. Typhimurium), 21.0 ± 1.4 mm (E. coli), and 26.0 ± 1.7 mm (B. cereus). These values correspond to approximately 77%, 60%, and 93% of the activity of Ciprofloxacin (30, 35, and 28 mm, respectively), indicating substantial potency, particularly against the Gram-positive strain. MIC values further supported these findings. Compound 4c displayed the lowest MIC values among the synthesized derivatives (34.78–38.10 µg/mL for Gram-negative strains and 23.08 µg/mL for B. cereus), closely approaching those of Ciprofloxacin (22.22–26.67 µg/mL). This confirms that 4c is the most potent antibacterial candidate within the series. Compounds bearing halogen substituents, particularly 4a (Cl) and 4d (Br), also demonstrated strong antibacterial activity, with inhibition zones ranging from 17 to 24 mm and MIC values between 25 and 47 µg/mL. In contrast, derivatives containing strong electron-donating groups such as 4 h (OCH3) and 4i (NO2 at different positions) exhibited significantly reduced activity, reflected by smaller inhibition zones (8–19 mm) and higher MIC values (31.58–100 µg/mL).

The enhanced susceptibility of B. cereus relative to Gram-negative strains is consistent with structural differences in bacterial cell envelopes, where the absence of an outer membrane in Gram-positive bacteria may facilitate compound penetration.

Antifungal activity

Compared to antibacterial results, antifungal activity was moderate across the series. Fluconazole exhibited inhibition zones of 25, 20, and 15 mm against A. flavus, G. candidum, and F. oxysporum, respectively. Compound 4c again emerged as the most promising antifungal derivative, producing inhibition zones of 15.0 ± 1.3 mm (A. flavus), 17.0 ± 1.2 mm (G. candidum), and 13.0 ± 1.0 mm (F. oxysporum), corresponding to approximately 60-86.6% of Fluconazole’s activity depending on the strain. Its MIC values (58.82–76.92 µg/mL) were higher than those of Fluconazole (40–66.67 µg/mL), indicating lower but still significant antifungal potency. Other derivatives showed weaker antifungal activity, particularly (4i and 4 h), which displayed minimal inhibition zones (5–8 mm) and elevated MIC values (> 125 µg/mL), confirming reduced efficacy45,46. Anti-cancer assay

In a rigorous investigation, researchers systematically evaluated the anticancer potential of a series of synthesized compounds (4a–4i) against four critical human cancer cell lines: MCF-7 (breast cancer), A549 (lung cancer), HCT-116 (colon cancer), and HepG2 (liver cancer)47. Vinblastine was used as the positive control across all four cancer cell lines due to its well-characterized mechanism of action and broad-spectrum cytotoxicity. This choice provides a consistent benchmark for relative potency comparisons across different cell lines while maintaining methodological rigor. Using vinblastine as a standard reference, the study determined the inhibitory concentration required to reduce cell viability by 50% (IC50). The experimental results unveiled a complex landscape of cytotoxic capabilities. Compound 4c (H) emerged as the standout performer, demonstrating exceptional antiproliferative activity across all tested cancer cell lines. Its IC50 values were remarkably consistent: 6.8 µM for MCF-7, 8.1 µM for A549, 4.9 µM for HCT-116, and 10.5 µM for HepG2. These IC50 values indicate that compound 4c is approximately 1.3–2.3 fold less potent than Vinblastine (IC50 = 5.30, 3.80, 2.90, and 4.50 µM for MCF-7, A549, HCT-116, and HepG2, respectively). Notably, the smallest potency difference was observed against HCT-116 cells, where 4c was only ~ 1.7-fold less active than the reference drug, highlighting its particular effectiveness toward colon carcinoma cells48. A deeper dive into the structure-activity relationship revealed nuanced insights. Halogen-substituted compounds, particularly (4a (Cl) and 4d (Br)), demonstrated moderate cytotoxicity with IC50 values ranging from 8.70 to 18.50 µM. This pattern suggested that electron-withdrawing halogens play a crucial role in enhancing anticancer activity. Conversely, methoxy and nitro-substituted derivatives (4b, 4e, 4 h, and 4i) displayed significantly reduced potency. Nitro-substituted compounds (4e and 4i) exhibited the most limited anticancer capabilities, with IC50 values exceeding 30 µM. This observation indicates a diminished ability to induce cancer cell apoptosis. The hydroxyl-containing compound 4f presented an intriguing middle ground, displaying moderate cytotoxic activity with IC50 values between 12.50 and 21.90 µM. This finding hints at the potential importance of hydrogen bonding in interactions with biological targets. Key observations from the study highlight a clear structure-activity relationship. Electron-withdrawing groups like chlorine and bromine consistently enhanced cytotoxicity, while electron-donating groups such as methoxy and nitro groups substantially reduced the compounds’ anticancer efficacy. Among the tested derivatives, compound 4c consistently demonstrated the lowest IC50 values across all cell lines, confirming it as the most active scaffold within the series. Although its potency remains lower than Vinblastine, the relatively small fold-difference supports further structural optimization studies. These findings provide valuable molecular insights that could guide the design of novel anticancer agents. By understanding how subtle structural modifications impact cytotoxic potential, researchers move closer to developing more targeted and effective cancer treatments47–49. Antioxidant activity

Our study assessed the antioxidant properties of newly synthesized compounds using the DPPH assay, comparing them with ascorbic acid (IC50 = 12.5 µg/mL). The hydroxyl-substituted derivative 4f showed the strongest free radical scavenging ability within the synthesized series (IC50 = 26.9 ± 1.5 µg/mL); however, it remained approximately two-fold less potent than ascorbic acid (IC50 = 12.5 ± 0.7 µg/mL). Which makes sense given that hydroxyl groups are known to stabilize radicals through hydrogen donation. Halogen-containing compounds (4a (Cl) and 4d (Br)) showed decent antioxidant activity50. Though halogens have electron-withdrawing properties that can stabilize the aromatic ring’s electron density, they lack the hydrogen-donating ability of hydroxyl groups, explaining their more moderate performance. Compounds with nitro groups (4e and 4i) were the weakest antioxidants in our tests. This aligns with previous research showing that nitro groups strongly withdraw electrons from aromatic systems, reducing their ability to stabilize radicals effectively. Methoxy and O-phenyl substituted compounds (4b, 4 g, and 4 h) showed relatively poor antioxidant capacity. While methoxy groups can donate electrons through resonance, their bulkiness may interfere with radical interactions. Similarly, the O-phenyl group’s size likely restricts access to the radical site. Interestingly, despite only moderate antioxidant properties, most compounds demonstrated significant toxicity to both cancer and normal cells. This suggests they may work through mechanisms beyond direct radical scavenging-possibly by disrupting cellular redox balance. Rather than protecting cells from oxidative damage, these compounds may promote oxidative stress-induced cell death, a mechanism seen in some cancer treatments targeting redox imbalances. Overall, although measurable antioxidant activity was observed, none of the synthesized derivatives surpassed the potency of ascorbic acid. The data suggest moderate radical scavenging capacity, with activity largely dependent on the presence of hydrogen-donating substituents such as hydroxyl groups51,52. This dual antioxidant/pro-oxidant behavior is common in polyphenolic compounds and warrants further investigation to understand its therapeutic implications. Molecular docking suggests potential interactions with selected targets; dedicated enzymatic inhibition assays are required to experimentally validate these proposed mechanisms. A similar analysis was also carried out for molecule 4f, which showed significant antioxidant activity. Conclusions

In conclusion, nine compounds were screened for their antimicrobial activity against three bacterial strains and three fungal species. Six compounds (4a, 4b, 4c, 4d, 4f, and 4 g) revealed promising antibacterial activity with inhibition zones (IZ) ranging from 18 to 26 µg/mL. The anticancer activity results were equally notable, with IC50 values of 6.8 µM for MCF-7, 8.1 µM for A549, 4.9 µM for HCT-116, and 10.5 µM for HepG2. These values positioned 4c (H) impressively close to vinblastine’s efficacy, signaling its potential as a promising anticancer candidate. Additionally, the antioxidant evaluation highlighted compound 4f, containing a hydroxyl substituent, as the most potent free radical scavenger (IC50 = 26.9 µg/mL), in alignment with the well-established role of hydroxyl groups in radical stabilization. Therefore, it can be considered a hit for further optimization to obtain more active biomedical agents. On another front, molecular docking analyses provided valuable insights into the potential biological activities of the synthesized compounds, particularly compound 4c. This molecule demonstrated strong binding affinities towards key biological targets associated with antifungal, antibacterial, antioxidant, and anticancer activities. Its interactions with various amino acid residues, through hydrogen bonds, Pi-alkyl, Pi-cation, Pi-Sulfur, and amide-Pi stacked interactions, suggest a stable and selective binding mode that supports its experimental bioactivity profile. Compound 4f also demonstrated notable antioxidant docking performance, supported by favorable scores and binding interactions. Overall, these results are consistent with the experimental findings and highlight compounds (4c and 4f) as promising multifunctional agents with potential applications in therapeutic development.
