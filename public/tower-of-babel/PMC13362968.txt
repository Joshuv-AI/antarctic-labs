Genomic Architecture of Selected Schizophrenia-Associated Regions: Co-location of Non-coding and Protein-Coding Genes
Authors: Karin Hrovatin, Tanja Kunej, Sara Redenšek Trampuž, Tea Terzić, Blanka Kores Plesničar, Katja Goričar, Vita Dolžan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) are increasingly recognized as important factors in the progression of schizophrenia (SZ); however, their involvement in disease etiology remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to characterize the genomic architecture of selected SZ-associated regions by examining the co-location of ncRNA and protein-coding genes and to explore sequence variants within these regions for potential associations with clinical outcomes.MethodsGenomic regions containing co-located protein-coding and ncRNA genes, specifically those with overlapping exonic sequences, were identified. Functional annotations of the selected SNPs were obtained from Ensembl, including predicted functional effects (SIFT, PolyPhen, CADD) and regulatory features. Association analyses were performed in SZ patients and controls to investigate disease occurrence, treatment response and psychopathological symptoms assessed using the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS).ResultsAmong 21 selected SZ-associated protein-coding genes (SZGs), seven showed genomic co-location with ncRNA genes. Exonic co-location was identified in four regions: BDNF/BDNF-AS, DDC/DDC-AS1, GNAS/GNAS-AS1 and HTR5A/HTR5A-AS1. Four SNPs within these co-located regions were selected for analysis: rs1800900 (GNAS/GNAS-AS1) and rs6265, rs11030101 and rs28722151 (BDNF/BDNF-AS); however none of these associations reached statistical significance after multiple comparison adjustment. Functional annotations indicated that two variants are located within constrained elements, overlap regulatory features or are predicted to have deleterious effects, suggesting functional relevance.ConclusionsThis exploratory study provides insights into the genomic architecture of SZ-associated regions, particularly the co-location between protein-coding SZGs and ncRNAs. It highlights the complexity of the SZ-associated genomic architecture and provides a framework for SNP prioritization and future genomic and functional studies in SZ and related disorders.Keywords: antisense lncRNA (AS-lncRNA); BDNF; non-coding RNA (ncRNA); schizophrenia; serotonin pathway, single nucleotide polymorphism; treatment response

Introduction

Epigenetic changes have become important in the study of mental disorders with an episodic nature, including schizophrenia (SZ), as genetic changes alone do not sufficiently explain the phenotypes.1–3 Furthermore, the epigenome is important in SZ treatment as it regulates the expression of pharmacogenes1,4 and may thus affect treatment response.5

Schizophrenia-associated genes (SZGs) can be either protein-coding or non-coding and distinguishing between these groups is important for understanding their complementary roles in disease pathogenesis. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) are a heterogeneous class of epigenetic regulators that differ in both structure and function.6–8 LncRNAs are transcribed in the brain as well as in other tissues6 and play an important role in brain development and function.9 Non-coding RNAs are classified by size into two main groups: small ncRNAs (sRNAs or sncRNAs) and long ncRNAs (lncRNAs). Small ncRNAs are <200 nucleotides (nt) in length, and include miRNAs, siRNAs, and snoRNAs. LncRNAs are ≥200 nt long and comprise subclasses such as long intergenic ncRNAs (lincRNAs), antisense lncRNAs (AS-lncRNAs), intronic lncRNAs (-IT) and overlapping lncRNAs (-OT).10,11

Antisense lncRNAs are transcribed from the opposite strand of protein-coding gene loci.12 They are widespread throughout the genome6,13 with many localized in the nucleus. Their main mode of function involves cis-regulation by binding to complementary nucleic acids and protein recruitment, thereby influencing transcription and translation.12,14 Expression patterns and sequence variants of lncRNAs,15–19 including AS-lncRNAs and their target genes,20–22 have already been investigated and associated with SZ.

The association of SZ with dopaminergic and glutamate pathways has been extensively studied. However, other pathways and genes also play an important role in SZ.23–25 For example, the serotonin (5-HT) pathway has an important role in brain function and behaviour.23,24,26,27 Numerous genetic variations in genes in the 5-HT pathway have been associated with SZ.28 Furthermore, BDNF is another gene, that has been reported to have a role in SZ.29,30 BDNF is a neurodevelopmental gene that affects neuronal growth31 and functions as a signaling molecule.29,30,32 Candidate loci for association studies in SZ are often selected on the basis of protein-coding genes, while the importance of ncRNAs is frequently overlooked. However, ncRNAs represent key epigenetic regulators implicated in SZ.1,6

We therefore conducted an exploratory, two-step study. First, we identified genomic regions in which exons of selected protein-coding SZ-associated genes co-locate with exons of ncRNA genes. The analyzed protein-coding SZGs included the BDNF gene and 20 genes of the 5-HT pathway, previously associated with SZ.28 Second, we prioritized single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located within these regions and investigated their associations with disease risk, treatment response and symptom severity. The study aimed to characterize the genomic architecture of selected SZ-associated loci and to evaluate the potential clinical relevance of sequence variants within these regions.

Materials and Methods

Mining Genomic Databases and Data Retrieval

Genomic databases were screened to determine whether the selected protein-coding genes are located in genomics regions that also contain ncRNA genes. For the analysis, we selected 21 genes, including BDNF and in 20 SZGs from the 5-HT pathway reviewed by Hrovatin et al.28: DDC, GNAI1, GNAO1, GNAQ, GNAS, HTR1A, HTR1B, HTR2A, HTR2C, HTR3A, HTR3B, HTR4, HTR5A, HTR7, MAOA, MAOB, SLC18A1, SLC6A4, TPH1, and TPH2. The ncRNAs overlapping with BDNF and 5-HT pathway SZGs were manually extracted from NCBI Entrez Gene view33 using assembly GRCh38.p7 (annotation release 108) and Ensembl genome browser (release 91). We selected ncRNAs that overlapped exonic regions of host genes and were annotated as »validated« or »reviewed« in Entrez Gene or as “manual annotation” in Ensembl. Short sequence variants in the regions of exon overlap between selected ncRNA genes (BDNF-AS, GNAS-AS1, DDC-AS1, HTR5A-AS1) and SZGs were manually extracted from Ensembl gene view.34 Initially, sequence variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) >0.001 were selected, and common variants with MAF >0.05 in the European population were subsequently retained, as rarer variants would have provided insufficient statistical power for the available sample size.

To account for updates in gene annotations and SNP functional data, earlier Ensembl releases (91 and 92) were used for initial extraction and later releases (112 and 113) were used to verify and expand variant annotations. From Ensembl release 113, we retrieved detailed genomic information for selected SNPs, including their location within constrained elements, Genomic Evolutionary Rate Profiling (GERP) conservation scores, overlapping transcripts and regulatory features, clinical significance, and predicted functional effects from Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT), Polymorphism Phenotyping (PolyPhen), and Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) tools.35 Variant consequence types were obtained using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) tool.

Participants

We included 341 patients with a diagnosis of SZ or schizoaffective disorders established by an experienced clinical psychiatrist based on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual IV (DSM IV). Four groups of patients were included: 72 acutely treated, 128 on maintenance treatment, both described in36,37 and38,39 94 treatment responsive and 47 treatment resistant, both described previously.40–42 We included 258 healthy blood donors as controls.43,44 Participants were self-reported Caucasians. A written informed consent was obtained from all of the participants. The study was approved by The Slovenian Ethics Committee for Research in Medicine (KME 89/06/03, 61/07/03, 37/03/08, 26 k/06/11, 60/02/02, 43/03/09) and was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The acutely treated group36–38 consisted of first episode patients (drug-free patients and patients with a psychotic episode after tapering their maintenance treatment) consecutively admitted to the Department of Psychiatry, Teaching Hospital Maribor and treated with risperidone or haloperidol. The group on maintenance treatment36,39 consisted of patients with clinically stabilized SZ or schizoaffective disorder recruited from the outpatient clinic population attending regular monthly check-up at the University Psychiatric Clinic Ljubljana; all patients had been receiving maintenance treatment with antipsychotics that are known substrates of CYP2D6 as described in detail previously.39 Patients from the treatment-resistant and responsive groups40–42 were randomly recruited from the outpatient unit of the University Psychiatric Clinic Ljubljana and were stratified either as treatment-resistant group if fulfilling the criteria defined in45 or as treatment-responsive group if fulfilling the criteria defined in46,47; a detailed description of this study group is provided in previous publications.40–42 We collected clinical data from medical charts. For the patient group, we collected data on sex, age, disease duration, and Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) score. For the control group, we collected data on sex and age.

DNA Isolation and Genotyping

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from all participants for DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was isolated using the FlexiGene DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples were genotyped for GNAS rs1800900 and BDNF rs6265, rs11030101 and rs28722151. All four studied SNPs were genotyped with KASP assays (LGC Genomics, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Ten percent of the samples were genotyped in duplicate for quality control.

Statistical Analysis

Median and first and third interquartile range was used to describe central tendency and variability of the continuous data. Categorical data were presented with frequencies. The Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) was tested with Chi-squared test. Association between individual SNPs and SZ risk was analyzed with logistic regression. We reported odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), and P-values. Association between treatment response and SNPs in the treatment-responsive and treatment-resistant groups was also analyzed using logistic regression. The association between psychopathological symptoms as sum score of the BPRS and SNPs was analyzed separately for each patient subgroup with Mann–Whitney U test using dominant genetic model. The associations were tested using an additive model with Kruskal–Wallis test. ANCOVA was used for the adjustment of associations significant before false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment for confounders, namely, sex, age, and duration of illness. For SNPs significantly associated with overall psychopathological state before FDR adjustment we also tested associations with individual components of the BPRS scale using the Kruskal–Wallis test, where data were available.

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics 21.0 program (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). For calculation of Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) we used statsmodels 0.10.1 Python package. The FDR was calculated across all computed P-values, excluding HWE analysis. GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, LLC., San Diego, CA, USA) was used to prepare the figures. Tag SNP analysis was performed using LD tag SNP Selection tool (SNPinfo Web Server).48 In the power analysis, we determined the detectable effect sizes for allelic variants with different minor allele frequencies, given the number of patients and controls included in the study (Supplementary Table S1). For most variants, we were able to detect OR ≤0.62 or ≥1.60 with ≥80% power in the SZ risk analysis. Furthermore, in the treatment response analysis, we were able to detect OR of ≤0.35 or ≥ 2.78 with ≥80% power for most variants.

Results

The study consisted of two main steps: bioinformatics characterization and exploratory association study. Step 1 involved screening 21 SZGs to identify genomic regions in which exons of protein-coding genes overlap with exons of ncRNA genes, followed by identification of common sequence variants in these regions and retrieval of functional and bioinformatic annotations for the selected SNPs. Step 2 involved exploratory association analyses of four prioritized SNPs with SZ risk, treatment response, and symptom severity. Overall, this analysis revealed genomic regions with exon-exon overlap between protein-coding and ncRNA genes, thereby providing insights into the genomic architecture of selected SZ-associated regions. Functional and Bioinformatics Annotation Retrieval from Genome Browsers

We extracted relevant genomic and functional annotations of four selected SNPs using the Ensembl genome browser (Supplementary Table S4). Two SNPs (rs1800900 and rs6265) were located in constrained elements, suggesting potential functional importance due to evolutionary conservation. GERP scores varied among the SNPs, ranging from −9.71 to 4.73, indicating varying levels of evolutionary constraint. Predictions of functional effects using SIFT, PolyPhen, and CADD tools indicated potential deleterious effects for rs6265. It was classified as “deleterious” by SIFT and “probably damaging” by PolyPhen. The highest CADD score was predicted for rs6265 (24.5); however, this value is below commonly used pathogenicity threshold (30). Regulatory annotations revealed that rs1800900 is located in a promoter region, suggesting its involvement in gene regulation. The clinical significance annotations classified rs6265 as a “risk factor,” suggesting potential clinical relevance.

VEP analysis showed diverse predicted impacts on both coding and non-coding transcripts across all four SNPs. The SNP rs6265 in BDNF was the only missense variant, directly altering the protein sequence. In contrast, the other SNPs (rs1800900, rs11030101, and rs28722151) were primarily associated with non-coding and regulatory annotations, including intron variant, 5′UTR variant, non-coding transcript variant, and downstream gene variant, annotated in both protein-coding (GNAS, BDNF) and antisense non-coding genes (GNAS-AS1, BDNF-AS). Notably, rs1800900 showed multiple regulatory consequence types, including upstream, UTR, and promoter-related annotations, suggesting a potential role in transcriptional regulation. Overall, these database-derived annotations provide additional insight into the potential functional relevance of the selected SNPs and may guide future studies on their biological and clinical impact.

Genotype–Phenotype Association Analyses

The BDNF polymorphism rs11030101 showed the strongest association with the SZ risk, but did not achieve significance after FDR adjustment (Supplementary Table S6). Carriers of the T allele had lower odds of developing SZ (OR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.44-0.97; FDR = 0.236). None of the analyzed SNPs were associated with the treatment response (Supplementary Table S7).

Associations between SNPs and psychopathological symptoms are shown in Supplementary Table S8. Associations between the following SNPs and BPRS sums were the strongest, but did not reach significance after FDR adjustment: BDNF rs28722151 for acutely treated patients at the beginning of treatment (FDR = 0.191 and FDR = 0.236 after adjustment for confounders) and after 4 weeks of treatment (FDR = 0.191 and FDR = 0.236 after adjustment for confounders) (Supplementary Fig. S3) and GNAS rs1800900 in treatment responsive patients (FDR = 0.236 and FDR = 0.505 after adjustment for confounders).

Likewise, the associations of BDNF rs28722151 with parts of BPRS were the strongest, although insignificant after FDR adjustment (Supplementary Table S9). These included the following associations in acutely treated patients: BDNF rs28722151 with BPRS3 (FDR = 0.236), BPRS9 (FDR = 0.236), BPRS13 (FDR = 0.207, GC vs. GG FDR = 0.191), BPRS16 (FDR = 0.191, CC vs. GG FDR = 0.191, GC vs. GG FDR = 0.236), and BPRS18 (FDR = 0.191, CC vs. GG FDR = 0.191). No data were available for individual parts of BPRS for acutely treated patients after four weeks to assess the association with BDNF rs28722151. To provide a visual overview of these trends, genotype-specific distributions of BPRS total and subscale scores are presented in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Discussion

This study advances the understanding of the genomic architecture of SZ-associated regions by identifying co-located non-coding and protein-coding genes. Analysis of a set of 21 selected SZ candidate genes revealed that seven overlapped with ncRNAs. Within this subset, four genes exhibited exon–exon overlap between protein-coding and ncRNA genes. Within these regions, four SNPs were prioritized and tested in a Slovenian cohort; however, no associations with SZ risk, psychopathological symptoms, or treatment response remained statistically significant after FDR correction. These negative results highlight the limited statistical power of the cohort and the need for replication in larger independent samples. Nevertheless, the overlapping exon–exon regions identified here warrant further investigation through functional and large-scale genetic studies. Overall, these findings suggest that exon-exon overlap between protein-coding and ncRNA genes may represent a broader principle of genome organization.

The four selected ncRNA genes BDNF-AS, DDC-AS1, GNAS-AS1, and HTR5A-AS1, which co-locate with BDNF and 5-HT genes associated with SZ, are not well studied. BDNF-AS is associated with brain functions, including neuronal survival49,50 and is known to regulate the expression of BDNF,51 a gene implicated in SZ and brain function.29,30 Given the central role of brain function in SZ development,23,24 BDNF-AS may also be involved in SZ pathogenesis. GNAS-AS1 has been proposed to regulate GNAS52; however, the functions of HTR5A-AS1 and DDC-AS1 remain largely unknown.

In this study, genomic annotation using genome browser showed that the polymorphisms selected for laboratory analysis (BDNF rs6265, rs11030101, rs28722151, and GNAS rs1800900) are located in two lncRNA genes: BDNF-AS and GNAS-AS1. We selected sequence variants located in exonic regions of both coding and non-coding genes, as such locations are more likely to exert functional effects. The rs6265 polymorphism is located within a coding exon of BDNF and causes amino acid substitution that affects cellular transport and secretion of BDNF.53 Therefore, it is less likely that its primary functional impact would be mediated through changes in BDNF-AS.

In contrast, the rs11030101 and rs28722151 polymorphisms in BDNF and rs1800900 in GNAS are located within the UTR, potentially affecting gene expression. For example, rs1800900 variant in GNAS has been proposed to influence the histone code and affect GATA1 transcription factor binding in the 5′ UTR of the gene.54 According to NIH SNPinfo, this variant overlaps predicted transcription factor binding sites (TFBS). Although bioinformatics tools have not predicted a direct effect of BDNF rs11030101 on BDNF expression,55 a study by Ping et al.56 reported an association between this polymorphism and negative symptoms in Chinese Han patients with SZ.

BDNF rs11030101 has been associated with SZ risk and body mass gain after treatment with atypical antipsychotics and other psychological disorders in several studies.55,57–60 BDNF rs28722151 has been associated with major depressive disorder,59 suggesting potential relevance to SZ. Given the genetic overlap among psychiatric disorders,61 variants such as rs28722151 present in multiple disorders might indicate common underlying mechanisms.

The association between BPRS total score and rs28722151 showed the lowest FDR in the group of acutely treated patients, although it did not reach statistical significance. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients in acute and maintenance treatment exhibit different symptom profiles.62–64 In our analysis, the BPRS symptom categories with the lowest FDR values for association with rs28722151, although not statistically significant, belonged to the affective and negative groups according to the classification proposed previously.65 Reduced BDNF expression has been linked to affective symptoms29 and impaired synaptic plasticity underlying negative symptoms.30 These associations may provide a context for the elevated BPRS scores in these domains.

Serban and Gidynski64 investigated psychological differences between acutely and chronically treated patients. They did not differentiate these symptoms in their study, whereas in our acute group some symptoms showed associations with genotypes before correction for multiple testing. The discrepancy in rs28722151–BPRS associations between acutely and maintenance-treated patients may reflect gene–environment interactions, potentially resulting in different phenotypic effects of genetic variants. Acutely treated patients received higher doses of antipsychotics. Typical antipsychotics and risperidone, which were also used in this study, have been shown to reduce BDNF concentrations.30 Another study in SZ patients reported increased BDNF levels after 20 days of antipsychotic treatment, with no correlation to psychiatric symptoms or between antipsychotic dose and neurocognitive biomarkers.66

The BDNF polymorphism rs6265 has been repeatedly associated with SZ and related phenotypes in Caucasian and Asian populations.67–70 It has also been associated with other mental disorders58,59,71 and memory function.72 However, findings have often been contradictory.42,67 A recent systematic review supports BDNF as a potential biomarker of treatment response in psychiatric disorders, suggesting that the rs6265 Val/Val genotype and higher baseline BDNF levels may be associated with better response to pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments.73 Although the GNAS polymorphism rs1800900 has received limited research attention, preliminary evidence suggests it may play a functional role in gene expression regulation.54

In the present study, we focused on investigating four protein-coding/lncRNA gene regions containing sequence variants located within exons of both gene types. However, the bioinformatic analysis also identified additional protein-coding/lncRNA gene pairs of interest, such as HTR2A/HTR2A-AS1. In addition, SZ-associated protein-coding genes also overlap with sncRNA genes, including snoRNA and miRNA genes. For example, GNAO1 is the host gene for MIR3935, whereas HTR2C overlaps with one snoRNA and six miRNA genes (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S2).

In future studies, all SZGs should be systematically analyzed for their co-location with sncRNA and lncRNA genes, in both sense or antisense orientations. Additionally, catalogues of SZ-associated ncRNAs, such as those reviewed by Cao et al.74 should be examined for their overlap with protein-coding genes. As ncRNA annotation is still ongoing, bioinformatic screening should be repeated and regularly updated to establish a comprehensive catalogue of SZ-associated ncRNA/host gene pairs.

The four lncRNA/protein-coding gene pairs analyzed in this study are present in the latest version of the Ensembl browser; however, some annotations have been updated since the initial bioinformatic screening. Future analyses could integrate lncRNA/SZG co-location data with ncRNA target interactions and competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks to reconstruct a more comprehensive regulatory network associated with SZ.

GWAS for SZ provide independent evidence supporting the involvement of lncRNAs. The GWAS Catalog75 lists SZ-associated SNPs within the BDNF-AS locus, including rs4923457, reported in the large-scale genome-wide analysis of SZ.76 These findings suggest that BDNF-AS and other ncRNAs overlapping SZ-associated loci warrant further systematic investigation in larger cohorts, together with functional validation.

Characteristics and Limitations of the Study

This exploratory study investigated SNP associations with various SZ-related phenotypes in heterogeneous patient cohort. While this design enabled for the exploration of different phenotypic associations, it also limited the statistical power of the analysis. Despite the absence of statistically significant results, the findings may still offer insights for future association studies. Although our sample was heterogeneous, the consistency of diagnoses and treatment protocols administered by the same team of experienced psychiatrists enhanced the reliability of patient classification. Nevertheless, the categorization of patients into treatment-responsive and treatment-resistant groups remains a potential source of variability.

The candidate gene set was limited to BDNF and serotonin-related genes. While this targeted approach facilitated genetic and functional annotation, it does not capture the full extent of potential exon–exon overlap between protein-coding genes and ncRNAs across the genome. A genome-wide analysis of such overlap regions would therefore be required to access the prevalence and relevance of this genomic architecture in SZ and other complex disorders. Other well-documented SZ-associated pathways, such as dopaminergic and glutamatergic signaling, represent important targets for future extension of this framework.

At the molecular level, we compiled genomic and functional annotations for the selected SNPs but did not experimentally validate their functional effects. Consequently, the roles of BDNF-AS and GNAS-AS1 in SZ remain putative. Moreover, we did not include expression data for these lncRNAs, which limits our ability to directly link the identified variants to transcript regulation. In addition, because our sample size limited the statistical power to detect associations with rare variants, we focused on common variants (MAF > 0.05). This approach may have excluded potentially relevant rare variants that warrant investigation in larger cohorts. Future studies in larger, ethnically diverse cohorts will be essential to validate these findings and extend them to rarer genetic variation.

Future Perspectives

Based on our findings, several future directions can be proposed. Although this study focused on 21 protein-coding SZGs, future studies should also investigate ncRNAs co-located with other known SZGs. Our analysis was restricted to exonic regions; however, sequence variants within intronic ncRNAs also warrant systematic evaluation. In this study, we focused on common variants, but contribution of rare variants to SZ risk should also be explored in future research. Therefore, high-throughput approaches will be required to comprehensively assess associations between sequence variants in ncRNAs and SZ.

Future work should extend this analysis to more diverse populations and investigate environmental factors to better understand gene–environment interactions. Although genomic and functional data from genome browsers were integrated, experimental validation is still required to fully elucidate the functional roles of the selected SNPs. Given the continuous updates of genome databases and the expansion of ncRNA annotations, bioinformatic analyses should be revisited using the most current genome browser releases. A systematic analysis of exon co-location between protein-coding and ncRNA genes, either in SZ or in other complex diseases, may further advance understanding of genome organization and regulatory interactions. Additionally, integrating publicly available expression and eQTL resources such as GTEx77 and BrainSpan78 could further clarify the regulatory impact of variants within ncRNAs. Potential directions for future research, including SNP replication in larger cohorts, functional characterization of ncRNAs, transcriptomics, epigenetics, eQTL mapping, and network analysis are summarized in the Supplementary Table S10.

Conclusions

This exploratory study advances the understanding of the genomic architecture of selected SZ-associated regions by mapping the co-location of ncRNAs and protein-coding genes. Although no statistically significant associations were detected, the results highlight the structural complexity of SZ-associated loci and suggest genomic elements with potential regulatory relevance. These findings emphasize the importance of integrating both coding and non-coding genomic features in SZ genetics and provide a framework to guide SNP prioritization in future large-scale and functional studies. Overall, the results are consistent with a broader genomic concept in which exon-exon overlap between protein-coding and ncRNA genes may represent a feature of genome organization.
