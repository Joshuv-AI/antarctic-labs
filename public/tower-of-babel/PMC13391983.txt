Machine learning–based prediction and quality control of meat and meat products: applications for intelligent quality management in the meat industry
Authors: Yea-Ji Kim, Hyuk Cheol Kwon, Ji Yoon Cha, Seonmin Lee, Tae-Kyung Kim, Min-Cheol Kang, Min Kyung Park, Yun-Sang Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The quality of meat and meat products critically affects consumer acceptance, market value, and food safety. Traditional quality evaluation methods rely on labor-intensive, destructive, and time-consuming analytical techniques, limiting their application in real-time industrial environments. Recent advances in machine learning (ML) have enabled the rapid, accurate, and nondestructive prediction of food quality attributes. This review explores the current applications of ML technologies for predicting and managing the quality of livestock-derived foods. Various data acquisition methods, including spectroscopy, imaging technologies, electronic sensors, and biochemical analyses, are reviewed as key data sources for ML-based prediction models. In addition, commonly used algorithms such as support vector machines, random forests, artificial neural networks, and deep learning architectures are reviewed in relation to their performance in predicting meat quality parameters, microbial contamination, shelf life, and authenticity. Combining ML models with sensor technologies and industrial processing systems has enabled the development of intelligent quality control frameworks for livestock products. Despite significant progress, challenges remain regarding data standardization, model interpretability, and large-scale industrial implementation. Future research should focus on the integration of multimodal data, digital twins, and advanced artificial intelligence technologies for next-generation smart food processing systems. ML-driven quality prediction systems are expected to become increasingly important in improving the efficiency, safety, and sustainability of the livestock food industry.

Introduction

The quality of livestock products, including meat, dairy, and processed animal-derived foods, strongly influences consumer acceptance, value, and food safety (Hwang et al. 2025). Meat quality is defined by physicochemical, microbiological, and sensory attributes such as tenderness, color, water-holding capacity (WHC), intramuscular fat content, and flavor (Lan et al. 2025). These traits are influenced by factors including animal genetics, feeding strategies, slaughter conditions, postmortem biochemical changes, and storage environment throughout the production chain (Legako 2025). Therefore, the accurate evaluation and management of livestock product quality remain an important challenge for the meat industry (Troy and Kerry 2010).

Traditional meat quality evaluation relies primarily on laboratory-based physicochemical, microbiological, and sensory analyses. Although reliable, these methods are often destructive, time consuming, and labor intensive, which limits their applicability in industrial processing environments where rapid or real-time monitoring is required (Li et al. 2024; Prieto et al. 2017). Consequently, increasing attention has been directed toward rapid and nondestructive analytical techniques assessing meat quality.

Nondestructive technologies, including near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy, hyperspectral imaging (HSI), Raman spectroscopy, electronic nose systems, and computer vision, have been used to evaluate livestock product quality (León-Ecay et al. 2025). These methods enabled rapid acquisition of spectral and imaging data related to the chemical and physical properties of meat products (Elmasry et al. 2012a). Among these, HSI was promising because it combines spatial and spectral information, allowing the simultaneous prediction of multiple quality attributes, such as pH, color, tenderness, and intramuscular fat content (Yi et al. 2025; Kamruzzaman et al. 2012). However, these technologies generate large and complex datasets that are difficult to interpret using conventional statistical approaches.

Machine learning (ML) has proven effective for analyzing high-dimensional datasets and developing predictive models for quality evaluation (Lee and Ma 2025). Algorithms such as support vector machines (SVMs), random forest (RF), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), artificial neural networks (ANNs), and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been increasingly applied in food science research (Liakos et al. 2025). Integrating ML with sensing technologies can significantly improve the prediction of meat quality traits such as pH, color, and drip loss (Kim et al. 2026). Despite this progress, several challenges remain in its practical implementation. Reliable ML models require large high-quality datasets, which are often difficult to obtain because of the biological variability of meat products. In addition, many ML models function as “black-box” systems, which limit their interpretability and industrial acceptance.

Therefore, this study summarizes recent advances in ML applications for quality prediction and control of livestock-derived foods, examines sensing technologies for data acquisition and ML algorithms used in livestock product research, and discusses their applications in predicting meat quality and food safety parameters, along with current limitations and future research directions for intelligent quality management systems.

Quality attributes of livestock products

The quality of livestock-derived foods is determined by a complex combination of physicochemical, sensory, and microbiological attributes that collectively influence consumer perception, product value, and food safety (Jiang et al. 2020). As meat is a biologically heterogeneous material, its quality characteristics vary depending on its genetic background, animal management practices, slaughter conditions, and postmortem handling (Troy and Kerry 2010; Font-i-Furnols and Guerrero 2014). Accordingly, accurate evaluation of meat quality remains a central topic in meat science.

Traditionally, meat quality evaluation relies on destructive laboratory analyses and sensory testing. However, these methods are often labor intensive, time consuming, and unsuitable for real-time industrial applications. Therefore, rapid and nondestructive sensing technologies combined with advanced computational approaches have increasingly been developed to improve the efficiency and accuracy of quality assessments (Prieto et al. 2009; Sun 2016). ML has emerged as a powerful tool for analyzing complex datasets generated using modern sensing systems and simultaneously predicting multiple quality traits (Lee and Ma 2025; Achata et al. 2023; Nturambirwe and Opara 2020). Meat quality attributes and analytical methods

Postmortem pH decline is an important biochemical process affecting meat quality. After slaughter, anaerobic glycolysis causes lactic acid accumulation and reduces muscle pH, which, in turn, influences WHC, color stability, and tenderness (Huff-Lonergan and Lonergan 2005). An abnormal pH decline can cause quality defects such as pale, soft, and exudative and dark, firm, and dry meats, both of which negatively affect appearance, processing yield, and shelf life (Warner et al. 2010). Because conventional pH measurements have limited spatial coverage, HSI has been combined with multivariate regression models to predict pH variations rapidly and nondestructively (Kamruzzaman et al. 2012; Elmasry et al. 2012). WHC is the ability of meat to retain water during storage and processing and is closely associated with juiciness, yield, and visual appearance. Poor WHC led to increased drip and cooking losses, resulting in economic losses during processing (Huff-Lonergan and Lonergan 2005). Because conventional drip loss measurements are destructive and time consuming, HSI combined with ML models, such as SVMs and partial least squares regression (PLSR), has been investigated as a nondestructive alternative (Achata et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2024). Color is a critical attribute affecting consumer decisions to purchase fresh meat. Consumers generally associate bright red appearance with freshness, whereas discoloration is perceived as spoilage (Font-i-Furnols and Guerrero 2014). Meat color is primarily governed by the chemical state of myoglobin pigments, including deoxymyoglobin, oxymyoglobin, and metmyoglobin (Mancini and Hunt 2005). Although instrumental colorimetry is widely used, computer vision and HSI combined with ML algorithms provided more comprehensive spatial information for evaluating the color distribution and discoloration during storage (Sun 2016; Achata et al. 2023). Texture, particularly tenderness, is a critical attribute that strongly influences consumer satisfaction. It is determined by the muscle fiber structure, connective tissue content, and postmortem proteolysis (Koohmaraie and Geesink 2006). Although the Warner–Bratzler shear force test is commonly used, it is destructive and unsuitable for rapid industrial monitoring. Recent studies explored imaging technologies and ML models to predict tenderness using structural and spectral information (Nturambirwe and Opara 2020).

Although physicochemical measurements provide objective indicators of meat quality, consumer acceptance ultimately depends on sensory attributes such as flavor, tenderness, and juiciness (Font-i-Furnols and Guerrero 2014). Flavor is generated through complex interactions between volatile and non-volatile compounds formed during cooking and processing, with lipid oxidation products and Maillard reaction compounds playing major roles (Mottram 1998; Park and Choi 2025). However, excessive lipid oxidation can produce off-flavors and reduce their acceptability. Tenderness remains an important determinant of overall meat palatability, whereas juiciness is closely related to the WHC and intramuscular fat content, which affect moisture release and mouthfeel during chewing (Koohmaraie and Geesink 2006; Park et al. 2024). Because sensory traits are closely linked to physicochemical properties, accurate predictions often require the integration of multiple datasets. Recent ML approaches have shown potential in predicting sensory quality by combining instrumental, physicochemical, and imaging data with consumer evaluation results (Sun et al. 2024).

Microbiological safety and shelf life are critical aspects of meat quality, because fresh meat provides a favorable environment for microbial growth. Spoilage microorganisms, such as Pseudomonas, Brochothrix thermosphacta, and lactic acid bacteria, were major contributors to quality deterioration during storage, causing off-odors, slime formation, and discoloration (Comi and Iacumin 2025). In addition, pathogenic bacteria, including Salmonella, Listeria monocytogenes, and Escherichia coli O157:H7, seriously threaten food safety. Traditional microbiological analyses rely on culture-based methods that require extended incubation times and are unsuitable for rapid decision-making. To address this limitation, studies have explored electronic nose systems and HSI combined with ML algorithms to rapidly detect microbial contamination and the prediction of meat shelf life (Achata et al. 2023; Anthoula et al. 2014). Data sources for ML in livestock quality prediction

The successful application of ML techniques to livestock product quality prediction largely depended on the availability of reliable and informative data sources (Lee and Ma 2025; Xu et al. 2024). Advances in analytical technology have enabled the acquisition of large and complex datasets related to the physicochemical, structural, sensory, and microbiological characteristics of meat products. These datasets can serve as input variables for ML algorithms to develop predictive models capable of estimating various quality attributes of livestock-derived foods.

The most widely used data sources for ML-based meat quality prediction are spectroscopy-based measurements, imaging and computer vision systems, sensor-based technologies, and omics or biochemical datasets (Liakos et al. 2025; Ahmed et al. 2025). Each data source provides complementary information on the composition and structural characteristics of meat products, and their integration with ML models significantly improves the accuracy and efficiency of quality evaluation systems. Major data sources used for ML-based livestock product quality prediction

Spectroscopic techniques are among the most widely used analytical tools for nondestructive evaluation of meat quality (Wu et al. 2022). These methods measure the interaction between electromagnetic radiation and biological tissues, enabling rapid assessment of chemical composition and structural properties (Porter et al. 2024). Spectroscopy-based datasets typically contain high-dimensional spectral information, making them highly suitable for ML applications (Nadimi and Paliwal 2024). NIR spectroscopy is widely used to rapidly and nondestructively analyze food composition by measuring the absorption of NIR radiation using molecular bonds, such as C–H, O–H, and N–H (Johnson et al. 2023). As these molecular vibrations are closely associated with moisture, protein, and fat content, NIR spectroscopy has been extensively applied to predict the chemical composition and quality attributes of meat (Prieto et al. 2009). Combining NIR spectroscopy with ML algorithms can effectively predict quality parameters, such as pH, tenderness, intramuscular fat, and WHC, using multivariate models, including PLSR and SVMs (Cozzolino et al. 2025). However, NIR spectroscopy may be limited by its shallow penetration depth and sensitivity to sample heterogeneity, which often requires integration with other analytical techniques or advanced ML models.

Raman spectroscopy is powerful for analyzing the molecular structure of biological tissues. It measures the inelastic scattering of light caused by molecular vibrations and provides detailed information on the chemical bonds and molecular composition. In meat science, Raman spectroscopy has been used to investigate the protein secondary structure, lipid composition, and water distribution in muscle tissues, which are closely related to tenderness and WHC (Coria et al. 2023). Raman spectral data have recently been integrated with ML algorithms to classify meat quality defects and predict compositional traits. However, weak signal intensity and fluorescence interference remain technical challenges for wider industrial application.

HSI combines imaging and spectroscopy to simultaneously obtain spatial and spectral information from food samples. This technique generates large datasets containing hundreds of spectral bands per pixel, enabling detailed analysis of the chemical composition and structural properties. HSI has been widely applied in meat science to predict quality attributes such as pH, color, tenderness, and microbial contamination (Elmasry et al. 2012; Kamruzzaman et al. 2012). Owing to the high dimensionality of hyperspectral data, ML algorithms such as SVMs, RF, and CNNs are commonly used for feature extraction and predictive modeling. Consequently, HSI is considered a promising technology for automated meat quality evaluation.

Imaging technologies and computer vision systems are important tools for objectively assessing meat quality characteristics. These systems capture visual information related to color, surface texture, and structural features, which can be analyzed using ML algorithms. Carcass grading is critical in the meat production chain because it determines the product value and market classification. Traditional grading systems rely on human inspectors to visually assess factors, such as fat cover and muscle conformation, which can lead to subjectivity and inconsistencies. To overcome these limitations, computer vision systems, combined with ML algorithms, have been developed to automate carcass grading (Sun 2016). These systems analyze digital carcass images to extract morphological features and classify quality grades, often achieving an accuracy comparable to or higher than that of human inspection.

Sensor technologies and Internet of Things (IoT) systems have gained attention for food-quality monitoring because they enable real-time data collection during processing, storage, and distribution. Electronic nose systems used sensor arrays to detect volatile organic compounds produced during microbial growth and lipid oxidation, allowing the evaluation of meat freshness and spoilage (Ajaykumar and Mandal 2020). Because electronic nose datasets comprise complex multivariate signals, ML algorithms, such as SVMs and ANNs, are commonly used to classify freshness levels and predict spoilage patterns. Electronic tongue systems that detect dissolved chemical compounds that mimic human taste perception have been used to evaluate flavor profiles and detect chemical changes during meat storage.

Recent advances in omics technologies have expanded the possibilities for predicting meat quality. Genomic, proteomic, and metabolomic approaches generate large datasets that describe the gene expression, protein composition, and metabolite profiles of muscle tissues. These datasets provide insights into biological processes related to meat tenderness, flavor development, and oxidative stability. Proteomic studies have identified several biomarkers associated with postmortem proteolysis and meat tenderization (Purslow et al. 2021). ML approaches are increasingly used to integrate omics datasets with phenotypic quality measurements to identify predictive biomarkers of meat quality traits. Although this field is still developing, integrating omics data with ML models offers significant potential for precision in livestock production and improved quality prediction. ML algorithms used in livestock product research

ML algorithms have been increasingly adopted in livestock product research to model complex and nonlinear relationships between analytical measurements and quality attributes (Mia et al. 2025). Compared with conventional statistical approaches, ML algorithms can effectively handle high-dimensional datasets generated using modern sensing technologies such as HSI, spectroscopy, and sensor arrays (Guo et al. 2026). These techniques enable the accurate prediction, classification, and monitoring of meat quality traits, including physicochemical properties, sensory attributes, and microbial safety indicators (Yang et al. 2025). Selecting an appropriate ML algorithm depends on dataset size, data structure, interpretability, and computational requirements. In livestock product research, ML approaches are generally categorized into traditional ML, deep learning, and hybrid modeling strategies. The hybrid models function by integrating multiple algorithms or combine data-driven models with domain knowledge. ML algorithms applied in livestock product quality prediction

Traditional ML algorithms are widely applied in food science owing to their relatively simple structures, moderate computational requirements, and high performance on medium-sized datasets (Siddique et al. 2026). Among these, SVMs were frequently used algorithms for food quality prediction (Anwar et al. 2023). SVMs construct optimal hyperplanes for classification or regression by maximizing the margin between samples and can effectively capture nonlinear relationships through kernel functions, such as radial basis functions. In livestock product research, SVMs have been used to predict physicochemical properties and classify meat quality grades. For example, SVMs have been used to detect adulteration and differentiate meat species based on their spectral signatures (Anwar et al. 2023). It has also been utilized to predict microbial spoilage using electronic nose sensor data (Papadopoulou et al. 2013). However, the SVM performance may decrease with large datasets, and model interpretability can be limited when nonlinear kernels are used.

RF, an ensemble learning algorithm, is another common ML approach (Salman et al. 2024). RF constructs multiple decision trees and aggregates their predictions to improve the accuracy and robustness, making it effective for modeling nonlinear relationships and variable interactions. In meat science research, RF has been applied to classify beef tenderness from the moisture and collagen content using spectroscopic or imaging data (Lee et al. 2025). Importantly, RF can estimate variable importance, which helps to identify influential features in high-dimensional datasets. However, interpretability may decrease as the number of trees increases, and the model performance depends on careful hyperparameter tuning.

The KNN algorithm is a simple instance-based learning method that classifies samples according to the majority class among their nearest neighbors (Ningthoujam et al. 2026). Owing to its simplicity and minimal assumptions, the KNN has been widely applied in food authentication and classification tasks. For instance, it has been used for meat species identification and adulteration detection using spectroscopic data (Sitorus and Arianti 2026), as well as for classifying meat freshness based on electronic nose signals. However, this algorithm is sensitive to noise and high-dimensional data, which may reduce its predictive performance as the number of features increases.

Deep learning models represent a more advanced class of ML algorithms that utilize multi-layer neural networks to learn hierarchical representations of complex datasets (Ahmed et al. 2023). These models have attracted increasing attention in livestock product research because of their ability to analyze large-scale imaging and spectral datasets. CNNs are effective for image-based analyses (Liu et al. 2023). CNN architectures automatically extract spatial features from images using convolutional filters, thereby enabling efficient computer vision applications. In meat science, CNN models have been applied for automated carcass grading, marbling detection, and meat classification. CNN algorithms have been effectively utilized to authenticate beef and lamb slices by identifying adulteration with duck meat through the digital images (Liu et al. 2023). ANNs are among the earliest neural network models utilized in food science. This model has been widely applied to predict the physicochemical properties of meat, such as secondary lipid oxidation values, using spectroscopic or hyperspectral data (Park et al. 2023). Although deep learning models provide strong predictive performances, they require large labeled datasets and substantial computational resources.

To overcome the limitations of individual algorithms, hybrid modeling strategies that combine multiple ML techniques or integrate feature extraction methods with predictive models have been explored (Azevedo et al. 2024). For example, dimensionality reduction techniques, such as principal component analysis (PCA), are often applied before ML modeling to reduce hyperspectral data dimensionality and improve computational efficiency (Elmasry et al. 2012b). Another approach involves combining deep learning feature extraction with traditional ML algorithms, such as SVM or RF, for classification tasks (Zhao et al. 2025). These strategies are useful for analyzing heterogeneous datasets, including spectroscopic, imaging, and sensor-based measurements. Despite these advances, challenges remain in the practical implementation of ML systems for the quality monitoring of livestock products. Specifically, further attention should be paid to issues such as the requirement for large annotated datasets, high computational costs, and limited model interpretability. Addressing these challenges is essential for developing reliable AI-based quality control systems in modern meat processing.

Applications of ML in livestock food quality prediction

ML technologies have been increasingly applied to livestock food systems to improve the accuracy, speed, and objectivity of quality evaluation (Chhetri 2024). ML algorithms have been integrated with advanced sensing technologies, such as HSI, spectroscopy, computer vision, and sensor arrays, to develop predictive models for assessing multiple quality attributes of animal-derived foods (Olagunju et al. 2025). These approaches enabled rapid and nondestructive analyses and provided effective tools for real-time quality monitoring in meat processing and distribution systems (Wu et al. 2022). Applications of ML in livestock food quality prediction

Tenderness is an important sensory attribute that affects consumer satisfaction with meat products (Warner et al. 2021). However, tenderness varies considerably owing to biological and processing factors, such as muscle structure, intramuscular fat content, and postmortem aging. Conventional tenderness evaluation methods, including the Warner–Bratzler shear force, are destructive and time consuming, which limits their use in industrial environments. Consequently, ML models have been increasingly explored to predict tenderness using nondestructive analytical techniques. HSI combined with ML algorithms has shown strong potential for predicting tenderness in beef and lambs. Previous study demonstrated that HSI integrated with PLSR models could predict tenderness-related attributes in lamb meat (Kamruzzaman et al. 2012). Nevertheless, accurate prediction of tenderness remains challenging because of the complex biochemical processes involved in postmortem muscle transformation.

Marbling, or intramuscular fat distribution, is another critical determinant of meat palatability, influencing tenderness, juiciness, and flavor (Erena et al. 2024). Traditional marbling evaluation relies on visual grading by trained inspectors, which can introduce subjectivity. Therefore, computer vision systems combined with ML algorithms have been explored for automated marbling analysis. XGBoost have demonstrated strong performance in analyzing meat images and identifying fat distribution patterns. XGBoost-based models can accurately estimate marbling scores and classify beef carcasses according to quality grades (Erena et al. 2024). HSI combined with algorithms, such as RF and SVM, has been used to quantify intramuscular fat content based on spectral lipid signatures (Elmasry et al. 2012a). However, reliable applications require standardized imaging conditions and models capable of accommodating variations across breeds and processing environments.

Estimation of shelf life is essential for ensuring food safety and minimizing economic losses in livestock food supply chains (Woo et al. 2024). Traditional shelf-life evaluation relies on microbiological and sensory tests, which are labor intensive and unsuitable for real-time monitoring. ML models have been increasingly applied to predict shelf life using multisensor datasets. Electronic nose systems that detect volatile organic compounds generated during spoilage have been integrated with ML algorithms to classify freshness levels and estimate remaining shelf life (Papadopoulou et al. 2013). In addition, ML models incorporating environmental variables, such as temperature and humidity, have been developed to predict microbial growth and spoilage dynamics during storage. When integrated with IoT technologies, these models enable dynamic shelf-life prediction throughout the supply chain, although standardized datasets and industrial validation are still required.

Microbial contamination remains a major food safety concern in livestock-derived products. Conventional microbiological testing requires incubation periods of several hours or days, which limits its usefulness for rapid detection (Sogore et al. 2024). Therefore, ML approaches combined with spectroscopic and sensor-based technologies have been explored for rapid contamination detection. HSI and sensor arrays can capture biochemical changes associated with microbial growth, whereas ML algorithms analyze complex signals to identify contamination patterns. For example, HSI combined with SVM algorithms and electronic nose systems combined with ML classifiers have been used to detect microbial spoilage in meat products (Elmasry et al. 2012) and identified spoilage-related volatile compounds produced by bacteria, respectively (Papadopoulou et al. 2013). However, microbial signals from the variations caused by intrinsic meat properties are difficult to distinguish.

Food authenticity has become an increasingly important issue owing to economic fraud and labeling concerns (Kendall et al. 2019). Meat adulteration, particularly the substitution with cheaper species, threatens food regulatory systems. ML combined with spectroscopic methods has shown a strong potential for detecting such fraud. Techniques such as Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) and NIR spectroscopy have been used with ML models to identify adulterants in minced meat products. For instance, previous study demonstrated that FT-NIR spectroscopy combined with chemometric analysis can accurately detect turkey adulteration in minced beef. HSI combined with ML classification models has been applied to differentiate meat species and verify product authenticity (Alamprese et al. 2016). These methods provide rapid and nondestructive alternatives to conventional DNA-based detection methods, although their reliability depends on the availability of large and diverse reference datasets.

ML-based quality control systems in the meat industry

Rapid advancements in ML have created new opportunities for intelligent quality control in the meat industry. Traditional quality control systems rely on manual inspections, laboratory analyses, and periodic sampling. Although reliable, these approaches are labor intensive, time consuming, and cannot provide real-time information during processing. As the meat industry moves toward digital transformation and Industry 4.0, ML-based systems are being explored to enable automated monitoring, predictive decision making, and improved process efficiency. Combining ML with sensing technologies, industrial automation, and digital data platforms has enabled the development of intelligent monitoring systems that are capable of detecting quality deviations and predicting safety risks. These systems are particularly valuable for large-scale meat production, where rapid and accurate decision making is essential.

Real-time monitoring is a promising ML application for meat quality control. By combining sensors, imaging technologies, and ML algorithms, the product quality can be continuously monitored during processing and storage. Spectroscopic techniques, such as NIR spectroscopy and HSI, have been widely studied for the nondestructive prediction of quality traits, including pH, moisture, fat content, and tenderness. HSI integrated with ML models has demonstrated potential for monitoring meat quality during processing (Kamruzzaman et al. 2012). In addition, electronic nose systems combined with pattern recognition algorithms can detect volatile compounds related to spoilage, enabling the early identification of quality deterioration and microbial contamination during storage (Papadopoulou et al. 2013). However, its industrial implementation remains limited by sensor calibration issues, environmental variability, and the requirement for robust models capable of handling heterogeneous datasets.

Smart processing plants represent another key component of Industry 4.0 in the food sector. These systems integrate automation, sensor networks, and artificial intelligence (AI) to optimize production and maintain a consistent quality. ML analyzes data from processing equipment and monitoring systems to identify quality variations and process inefficiencies. For example, computer vision combined with ML has been applied to automated meat grading, allowing the real-time evaluation of fat distribution and carcass conformation while reducing the subjectivity of manual grading (Erena et al. 2024). ML models can optimize processing parameters, such as chilling, aging, and packaging conditions, to improve product quality and shelf life. Nevertheless, adoption requires investments in infrastructure, data integration, and sensor interoperability.

Digital twin technology has emerged as a promising approach for advanced meat quality management (Abdurrahman and Ferrari 2025). Digital twins can simulate production conditions and predict their impact on product quality by creating virtual representations of physical processing systems using real-time sensor data. ML models analyze these data streams and continuously update the predictive simulations. For example, digital twins can be used to estimate how temperature fluctuations during storage influence microbial growth and shelf life, thereby enabling preventive interventions before quality deterioration occurs. Although still in the early stages in the food sector, the integration of digital twins, ML, and sensor-based monitoring systems offers a promising pathway toward fully intelligent meat processing systems.

For the industrial application of ML, understanding the characteristics of each data source is necessary to select algorithms that are the most appropriate for the underlying data structure (Table 7). PLSR mainly shows strong predictive performance when adjacent variables exhibit high multicollinearity, which makes it particularly suitable for spectrometric data such as NIR spectra. In contrast, for large and complex datasets, including HSI and conventional image data, are particularly analyzed using deep learning-based approaches such as CNN after feature extraction. Meanwhile, as feature extraction and pre-processing can substantially affect model performance and suitability, PLSR has outperformed deep learning models even for large datasets such as HSI, depending on the feature extraction strategy employed (Wang et al. 2025). However, the advanced model accuracy by pre-processing and feature extraction may reduce the interpretability by obscuring the physical meaning of original input variables. For electronic nose sensor data collected across different storage periods and temperatures, SVM has been reported as the most suitable model for evaluating beef freshness as it had advantages on capturing non-linear patterns (Papadopoulou et al. 2013). Considering structured experimental data, such as color, pH, and WHC, these were also frequently used as input variables for predicting other quality traits and offered the advantages of a relatively simple data structure and high interpretability. Accordingly, a wide range of algorithms can be applied, though the most appropriate model may differ depending on factors such as inter-variable correlation, data linearity, the presence of outliers, and batch effects. For example, in a previous study predicting changes in beef quality traits from color measurements, RF was found to be the most suitable model for predicting metmyoglobin content and peroxide value, whereas GBoost showed the best performance for predicting TBARS (Kim et al. 2026). In another study, among the multiple ML algorithms for prediction of pH and color after 48 h postmortem of pork, boosting-based tree ensemble models showed superior predictive performance due to the ability to account for the complex and subtle non-linear patterns (Yang et al. 2025). In summary, while certain models are commonly preferred depending on the scale of data processing, the suitability of ML algorithms is not universally predetermined. Instead, it depends on the various characteristics of the input data and the required balance between predictive performance and interpretability, which is crucial for industrial adoption.

Challenges and limitations

Despite rapid progress in ML for livestock quality prediction, several bottlenecks have hindered its widespread industrial adoption. These challenges primarily involve data heterogeneity, model interpretability, and practical implementation in complex processing environments. The inherent biological variability of livestock, which is influenced by genetics, feeding regimes, and postmortem biochemical changes, presents a significant challenge (Troy and Kerry 2010). This heterogeneity often reduces the robustness of the ML models. Furthermore, most studies rely on small laboratory-scale datasets that fail to capture the noise and variance in industrial settings. High-dimensional data from sensors, such as HSI, require intensive preprocessing, and inadequate handling of these stages can cause bias (Elmasry et al. 2012c). Establishing large-scale standardized datasets and collaborative data-sharing frameworks remains critical for future research.

Although deep learning architectures offer high accuracy, their “black-box” nature limits their utility in regulatory contexts, where transparency in detecting contamination or adulteration is mandatory. To bridge this gap, the integration of Explainable AI tools such as SHAP and LIME is essential. Developing hybrid models that combine statistical transparency and predictive power is necessary to ensure verifiable and trustworthy AI-driven decisions. The transition from manual inspection to AI-driven systems is hindered by high infrastructure costs and the technical difficulty of managing heterogeneous data streams in real time. Practical adoption requires the seamless integration of sensors with automated processing equipment, along with robust protocols for sensor calibration and maintenance. Future efforts should move beyond algorithmic optimization to focus on developing a scalable, cost-effective digital infrastructure and workforce training programs tailored to modern meat processing plants.

Future perspectives

Combining ML with advanced sensing and digital manufacturing offers transformative opportunities for the quality management of livestock. Several emerging research directions are expected to define next-generation intelligent monitoring systems. The primary frontier is the development of AI-driven smart factories within Industry 4.0. These facilities enable predictive process control by synchronizing automated production, sensor networks, and real-time analyses. ML models integrated with HSI and IoT frameworks can continuously monitor carcass traits and detect processing defects, allowing autonomous adjustment of production parameters. This connectivity ensures consistent quality and transparency across the food supply chains. Combining multiomics (genomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics) with ML algorithms provides deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms of meat quality. This holistic approach allows the identification of specific biomarkers associated with tenderness, flavor, and shelf life. Furthermore, these data-driven insights support precise livestock production, enabling the optimization of breeding and feeding strategies. However, overcoming the high computational demands and complexity of biological datasets remains a prerequisite for broader applications.

Generative AI is an emerging field in food science. Unlike traditional predictive models, generative AI can simulate the effects of various processing conditions or design novel product formulations with optimized sensory and nutritional profiles. This technology has a significant potential for innovative product design, including the development of next-generation meat products and alternative protein systems. Although in its infancy, generative AI is poised to expand the role of intelligence-driven innovations in the food industry.

Conclusions

ML is pivotal for enhancing quality prediction and management in livestock-derived food products. The proliferation of advanced sensing technologies, including spectroscopy, HSI, and computer vision, has generated high-dimensional datasets requiring robust analytical frameworks. This review highlights that traditional algorithms, such as SVM and RF, along with deep learning architectures, such as CNNs, offer superior performance in evaluating critical traits, such as tenderness, marbling, shelf life, and microbial safety. Integrating ML with industrial monitoring has catalyzed the digital transformation of the meat industry. Technologies such as digital twins and real-time sensor-based systems enable continuous predictive quality control throughout the supply chain. Despite this potential, significant challenges persist, particularly regarding the lack of standardized datasets, model interpretability, and practical barriers to industrial-scale implementation. In summary, ML represents a transformative approach for livestock food systems. Future research should prioritize data integration, model transparency, and cross-industry applicability to facilitate broader adoption and ensure the long-term sustainability of intelligent meat processing.
