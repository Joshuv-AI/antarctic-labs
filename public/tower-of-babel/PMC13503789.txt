Genetic structure of Biscogniauxia mediterranea fungus populations in Zagros forests of Iran
Authors: Seyed Ali Asghar Hashemi, Fariba Ghaderi, Abdollah Ahmadpour, Javad Ashrafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiscogniauxia mediterranea is an opportunistic fungal pathogen associated with charcoal canker disease in both juvenile and mature oak trees. This study investigated the genetic diversity, population structure, and pathogenic variability of B. mediterranea populations in the Zagros forests of Iran using inter-simple sequence repeat (ISSR) markers, with the goal of supporting effective forest disease management strategies. A total of 159 isolates were obtained from symptomatic oak trees in four ecologically distinct provinces: Fars, Ilam, Kermanshah, and Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad. Genetic analyses revealed high genetic diversity and clear geographic structuring. Most genetic variation occurred within populations, while significant differentiation among regions was detected. Population structure analyses indicated the presence of major genetic groups associated with geographic origin, with additional overlapping patterns suggesting gene flow among regions. Pathogenicity tests confirmed that all tested haplotypes were capable of causing charcoal canker, with isolates from western regions exhibiting greater aggressiveness than those from the southwest. Overall, the findings indicate that geographic and environmental factors are associated with variation in both the population structure and pathogenic potential of B. mediterranea, providing guidance for targeted management of oak decline in the Zagros forests.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42677-8.Keywords: Charcoal canker disease, Genetic diversity, Gene flow, ISSR marker, Oak decline, Pathogenicity assay, Population structureSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Genetics, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The Zagros forests extend approximately 1600 km in length and 240 km in width, encompassing an area of around 53.3 million hectares. These forests harbor a diverse assemblage of oak species, attributed to the region’s semi-arid to temperate climatic conditions. Oak woodlands in the Zagros region represent about 3.5% of Iran’s total land area and account for 44% of the country’s forested areas1. Among these, the Persian oak (Quercus brantii Lindl.) is the most widespread species, covering nearly three million hectares from the southwestern to the northwestern parts of the Zagros range. The Zagros oak forests are noted for their significant medicinal, economic, and ecological value. Additionally, they play a critical role in soil conservation, water regulation, and climate change mitigation2, 3.

Oak forests are increasingly affected by decline and mortality driven by a complex interplay of abiotic and biotic stressors, including drought, elevated temperatures, wildfires, pollution, and secondary infections by insect pests and pathogenic organisms4, 5. Oak trees are particularly susceptible to various fungal diseases, such as oak canker caused by Diplodia corticola A.J.L. Phillips, A. Alves and J. Luque6, and root rot associated with Armillaria mellea (Vahl) P. Kumm7. Among these, charcoal canker represents a significant threat to forest health, severely impacting a wide range of tree species, especially those within the genus Quercus, across diverse regions including Europe (Portugal, Slovenia), the Mediterranean Basin, Iran, Russia, North and Central America, New Zealand, and Africa8, 9, 10, 11.

Biscogniauxia mediterranea (de Not.) O. Kuntze (Xylariaceae, Xylariales) is an opportunistic fungal pathogen responsible for charcoal canker disease in both juvenile and mature oak trees12, 13, 14. This species exhibits a endophytic phase within healthy host tissues, later transitioning to a pathogenic state, predominantly infecting physiologically stressed trees particularly those affected by prolonged drought or fire events13, 15. During its pathogenic phase, B. mediterranea aggressively colonizes the bark and woody tissues of host trees, inducing necrosis and canker formation, ultimately resulting in progressive decline and eventual tree mortality.

Reproductive structures of B. mediterranea arise from necrotic bark tissues as carbonaceous stromata containing perithecia that bear ascospores. These ascospores can be dispersed over extensive distances, ranging from hundreds to thousands of kilometers, by wind or insect vectors. This dispersal mechanism makes them a key factor in pathogen spread within forest ecosystems9, 16.

Charcoal canker disease poses a serious threat to oak populations by causing physiological deterioration, structural instability, ecological imbalance, and significant economic loss. The increasing frequency of drought and other climate-related stressors has further intensified the severity of the disease. Consequently, the implementation of integrated disease management strategies, including early detection, mitigation of environmental stress, and long-term forest conservation, is essential to minimize the adverse impacts of B. mediterranea on oak forest ecosystems9, 10, 11, 17.

Genetic population structure refers to the extent and distribution of genetic variation within and among populations and reflects the interplay of key evolutionary forces, including gene flow, genetic drift, mutation, natural selection, and reproductive or mating systems18, 19, 20. These evolutionary processes can drive substantial shifts in pathogen population dynamics. Understanding the contribution of each factor is crucial, as fungal populations with elevated genetic diversity are more likely to rapidly adapt to fungicides and overcome host resistance genes, especially when compared to pathogens with strictly asexual or strictly sexual reproduction modes18. Insights into the evolutionary potential of fungal plant pathogens enhance our ability to assess the risk posed by different pathogens to various control strategies and inform the development of effective resistance breeding programs18, 20.

Molecular markers are widely employed in population genetic studies of plant pathogenic fungi20, 21. Initial studies on the genetic structure of B. mediterranea populations, using Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA (RAPD-PCR), revealed high levels of genetic variability even among geographically proximate populations14, 22. Furthermore, substantial genetic variation within individual stromata on cork oak was observed using microsatellite-primed PCR (MSP-PCR), suggesting a high degree of polymorphism23, 24. This pronounced polymorphism likely reflects a high frequency of sexual reproduction and a heterothallic mating system in B. mediterranea.

Over the past decade, B. mediterranea has emerged as the primary pathogen responsible for charcoal canker disease, particularly under climate change scenarios characterized by elevated temperatures and extended drought periods25, 26. This species is well adapted to the climatic conditions of Iran, which feature short, mild winters and hot, dry, and rainless summers25, 26. Moreover, the Zagros region includes areas separated by mountainous terrain and characterized by a wide range of climatic conditions, from semi-arid to temperate. Importantly, different isolates of B. mediterranea can vary in their pathogenicity, causing differences in necrotic lesion length and disease severity under similar environmental conditions10. Such variation in isolate virulence is a key factor influencing oak forest health and has significant implications for disease management and conservation strategies, particularly under environmental stresses such as prolonged drought and elevated temperatures13. Despite its significance, comprehensive data on the genetic structure of Iranian B. mediterranea populations remain lacking. Therefore, further research is imperative to elucidate the genetic composition of these populations, which may enable predictions regarding the pathogen’s evolutionary potential. An understanding of evolutionary dynamics is essential to inform the development of resistant host genotypes and to guide effective disease management strategies18, 20. In this study, all isolates were collected as part of the present work, and the research was designed to comprehensively investigate the genetic structure and differentiation of B. mediterranea populations using Inter-Simple Sequence Repeat (ISSR) molecular markers. In addition, the study aimed to evaluate the comparative pathogenicity of different haplotypes across multiple geographic regions within the Zagros Mountains.

Results

Disease symptoms and morphological identification of isolates

Fungal isolates were obtained directly from carbonaceous stromata bearing perithecia and ascospores on symptomatic oak tissues (Fig. 1). Early symptoms of charcoal canker were generally nonspecific, such as yellowing leaves, premature browning, and thinning of the canopy. As the disease progressed, trees showed wilting, leaf curling, dieback, and the development of carbonaceous stromata on trunks and branches, eventually resulting in tree death (Fig. 1). Fungal structures from these symptomatic tissues were used for isolation and morphological characterization of B. mediterranea.

Symptoms of charcoal canker caused by Biscogniauxia mediterranea on Quercus brantii: (a–c) dieback and mortality of oak trees; (d–i) Development of carbonaceous stromata on the trunk; (j) Colony growth on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium incubated at 30 °C for 7 days; (k) Ascospores exhibiting a single germ slit located on one side. Scale bar = 10 μm.

A total of 159 isolates were collected from symptomatic oak trees (Supplementary Table S1). Based on morphological characteristics, the isolates were identified as B. mediterranea species. In general, the cultures formed colonies that were white to gray with an almost aerial mycelium on PDA medium and the reverse side of the colonies was dark brown to black (Fig. 1). The stromata were applanate with an almost black surface and carbonaceous tissue. The perithecia were ovoid to tubular with black, coarsely papillate ostioles. The asci were short-stipitate with an amyloid ring. The ascospores were ellipsoid with narrowly rounded ends, brownish-black, lacking a hyaline sheath, and had a spore-length germ slit on one side only, measuring 14–17.5 × 5.5–8.1 µm (Fig. 1). The conidiophores were indistinguishable from vegetative hyphae, and the conidia were subhyaline to hyaline, ovoid, ellipsoid, or subglobose, measuring 9–11 × 3.6–5 mm.

Isolation of B. mediterranea was successfully conducted from early September to late March in the Zagros forests. No isolates were obtained from symptomatic trees between April and late August, despite attempts, because ascospores were absent from the carbonaceous stromata during this period. The number of isolates collected per population ranged from 29 to 50 (Supplementary Table S1).

Molecular identification

PCR amplification and sequencing were successfully performed for 30 representative isolates (Supplementary Table S1). Phylogenetic analysis of Biscogniauxia species was conducted using the Maximum Likelihood method based on the β-tubulin gene and ITS-rDNA regions. The resulting sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PX970321–PX970350 (β-tubulin) and PX957293–PX957322 (ITS-rDNA) (Supplementary Table S1). The topology and branch lengths of the phylogenetic tree, constructed from the combined β-tubulin and ITS-rDNA dataset, are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. The representative isolates (see Supplementary Table S1) formed a monophyletic clade with B. mediterranea reference isolates (BCRC34037 and YMJ 147) with strong bootstrap support (100%) (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Measures of genetic diversity

The analysis showed that eight out of the 14 ISSR primers (WILLISSR1, WILLISSR2, WILLISSR3, WILLISSR4, WILLISSR5, UBC885, ISSR09, and P4) (Supplementary Table S2) demonstrated consistent polymorphic patterns, yielding a total of 201 amplified DNA fragments across 159 B. mediterranea isolates. The characteristics of each ISSR primer are summarized in Table 1. The number of amplified bands per primer ranged from 22 (locus P4) to 31 (locus UBC885). The highest values for the effective number of alleles (Ne), Shannon’s information index (I), and gene diversity were recorded for locus WILLISSR3, while the lowest values were observed for locus ISSR09 (Table 1). Gene diversity values ranged from 0.3038 (ISSR09) to 0.3936 (WILLISSR3), with an overall mean of 0.3538 (Table 1). The lowest genetic differentiation (FST = 0.1663) was observed at locus WILLISSR5, whereas the highest (FST = 0.3357) was recorded at locus WILLISSR3. Conversely, gene flow estimates (Nm) ranged from 0.9894 at locus WILLISSR3 to 2.5066 at locus WILLISSR5 (Table 1). The genotype accumulation curve indicated that all multilocus genotypes (MLGs) could be resolved using 69 loci, confirming that the full set of 201 loci was highly informative for population genetic analyses (Supplementary Fig. S2).

All populations exhibited high genotypic diversity, as evidenced by the total number of genotypes (G) and the corrected Shannon index (H) (Table 2). The clonal fraction was zero and consistent across all populations. The mean expected gene diversity (Hexp) among the studied populations was 0.276, with the highest value observed in the Fars population (0.30) and the lowest in Kermanshah (0.258) (Table 2). No private alleles were detected within the Iranian populations. The percentage of polymorphic loci (PPL) varied among populations, ranging from 74.63% in Kermanshah to 93.03% in Fars, with an average of 83.46% (Table 2). The Fars and Kermanshah populations exhibited the highest and lowest values for the number of alleles (Na), effective number of alleles (Ne), PPL, number of polymorphic loci (NPL), and Shannon index (H), respectively (Table 2). Indices of multilocus linkage disequilibrium, namely the index of association (IA) and the standardized index of association (\bar rd), were high and statistically significant (P < 0.001) in all populations (Table 2), leading to the rejection of the null hypothesis of random allelic association (Fig. 3).

Estimates of the unbiased multilocus linkage disequilibrium index (\bar rd) for four Iranian Biscogniauxia mediterranea populations, calculated using ISSR data with the poppr package v. 2.8.7. Deviations of \bar rd values from the null hypothesis of linkage equilibrium were assessed via 1,000 permutations per population dataset. For the combined isolate dataset, the hypothesis of linkage equilibrium was rejected (P = 0.001), indicating a predominantly clonal population structure.

Pairwise estimates of Nei’s genetic identity among the populations ranged from 0.7751 to 0.9712. Corresponding genetic distance values between populations varied from 0.0292 to 0.2547. The lowest genetic distance (0.0292) coupled with the highest Nei’s genetic identity (0.9712) was observed between the Kermanshah and Ilam populations. In contrast, the greatest genetic distance (0.2547) and lowest Nei’s genetic identity (0.7751) occurred between the populations of Kermanshah and Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad (Supplementary Table S3).

Table 3 presents pairwise population measures of genetic differentiation (FST; above the diagonal) and gene flow (Nm; below the diagonal). Genetic differentiation among all Iranian populations was estimated using the fixation index FST. Values of FST ranged from 0.046 to 0.37, indicating varying degrees of population differentiation. Conversely, gene flow (Nm), which is inversely related to FST, ranged from 0.87 to 10.36 across population pairs. The highest genetic differentiation (FST = 0.37) with the lowest gene flow (Nm = 0.87) was observed between the Kermanshah and Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad populations. Similarly, the population pairs Ilam–Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad and Kermanshah–Fars exhibited the second-highest genetic differentiation (FST = 0.35) accompanied by low gene flow (Nm = 0.93). In contrast, the lowest FST value (0.046) with the highest gene flow (Nm = 10.36) was detected between the Kermanshah and Ilam populations (Table 3). The populations of Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad and Fars showed the second-lowest genetic differentiation (FST = 0.055) with correspondingly high gene flow (Nm = 8.59). Analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) was performed on the clone-corrected dataset to assess the hierarchical distribution of genetic variation among Iranian B. mediterranea populations. The AMOVA, based on PhiPT values (PhiPT = 0.267, P = 0.001), indicated that the majority of genetic variation (73%) occurred among individuals within populations, while 27% of the variation was attributed to differences among the four regional populations (Supplementary Table S4).

Cluster 1 comprised 100 isolates primarily assigned to the Fars and Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad populations, while Cluster 2 consisted of 59 isolates from the Ilam and Kermanshah populations. The STRUCTURE bar plot revealed a low level of genetic admixture between the two clusters (Fig. 4b).

Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) of ISSR data grouped the 159 B. mediterranea isolates into two distinct clusters, designated as Cluster A and Cluster B (Fig. 5). Cluster A comprised 100 isolates collected from Fars and Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad provinces, while Cluster B included 59 isolates originating from Ilam and Kermanshah provinces. Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) was performed on the clone-corrected dataset as a complementary approach to validate the inferred genetic clusters. Based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the 159 multilocus genotypes (MLGs) of B. mediterranea were partitioned into three genetic clusters, with the lowest BIC score observed at K = 3 (Fig. 6a,b). The DAPC scatter plot showed that isolates grouped according to their geographic origin (Fig. 6c), although the density plots revealed substantial overlap among populations, resulting in the effective categorization of isolates into two major clusters. Cluster 1 (left cluster in Fig. 6c), n = 59) consisted predominantly of isolates from the Ilam and Kermanshah populations, while Cluster 2 (right cluster in Fig. 6c); n = 100) included the majority of isolates from the Fars and Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad populations. The results of the DAPC analysis were consistent with the clustering patterns observed in both the Bayesian STRUCTURE analysis (Fig. 4) and the Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) (Fig. 5). In the clustering analysis using UPGMA, all 159 isolates were grouped into two genetic clusters at approximately 57% similarity: Cluster A (100 isolates from Fars–Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad) and Cluster B (59 isolates from Kermanshah–Ilam). The UPGMA results were consistent with the clustering patterns observed using the other methods (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Assessment of isolate aggressiveness

In this study, pathogenicity varied markedly among the sixteen haplotypes of B. mediterranea representing four geographic populations. Observed symptoms included wood discoloration and progressive necrosis extending both upward and downward from the inoculation site. No lesions were detected on the non-inoculated control seedlings (Fig. 7). Furthermore, the isolates were successfully re-isolated from the infected tissues, confirming fulfillment of Koch’s postulates. Lesion size varied significantly among the tested haplotypes. One-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of fungal isolates on evaluated lesion length (P = 0.0001; Supplementary Table S6). Tukey’s HSD test further identified significant differences among isolates (Fig. 8). Oak seedlings inoculated with isolates originating from western Iran (Kermanshah and Ilam provinces) developed significantly larger necrotic lesions than those inoculated with isolates from southwestern Iran (Fars and Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad provinces.

Discussion

This study presents the first comprehensive analysis of the genetic structure and diversity of four Iranian B. mediterranea populations in the Zagros forests using multilocus ISSR markers. Our findings revealed significant genetic variation within and among geographic populations, shaped by geographic separation and possibly ecological heterogeneity. The identification of 159 unique multilocus genotypes (MLGs) among 159 isolates after clone correction indicates a high level of genotypic diversity and the absence of clonal reproduction within populations. This high genetic diversity levels among B. mediterranea populations are in consistent with previous studies14, 24, 27, 28 and may attributed to the species heterothallic mating system and sexual reproduction or frequent recombination contributes to maintaining genetic variability18, 29.

In general, B. mediterranea ascospores are primarily mostly dispersed by wind over long distances, particularly following periods of precipitation9, 29, although insects could also play an important role in the short- and long-distances dispersal of ascospore9, 29. The success of isolations observed in this study appears to be influenced by ascospore availability, which may explain seasonal limitations in obtaining viable isolates. Our results indicate that high levels of gene flow among certain populations such as Fars–Kohgiluyeh and Ilam–Kermanshah, which are separated by approximately 172 to 184 km suggest that genetic dispersal in this species can occur over long distances. This finding contrasts with the results of Costa et al. (2022)24, who reported low migration rates between geographically distant B. mediterranea populations, indicating predominantly short-distance dispersal patterns.

The zero clonal fraction observed across all four populations further corroborates the predominance of sexual or at least recombining populations of B. mediterranea in this region. Genetic diversity indices (Hexp and Shannon’s index) varied moderately across the populations, with the highest diversity recorded in Fars and the lowest in Kermanshah. This pattern may reflect differences in local ecological conditions or host diversity, as Fars Province encompasses more heterogeneous environments conducive to maintaining diverse pathogen populations. The absence of private alleles across populations suggests ongoing gene flow, albeit restricted, consistent with geographic distances and potential physical barriers imposed by the Zagros Mountain ranges.

One of the important findings of this study was pairwise FST values ranged from low to moderate (FST: 0.046 to 0.37), indicating variable degrees of genetic differentiation among populations. The highest genetic differentiation was observed between Kermanshah-Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad populations that geographical distance was 1054 km, accompanied by the lowest gene flow (Nm = 0.87). This inverse relationship between gene flow and genetic differentiation is characteristic of spatially structured populations, where limited dispersal restricts allele exchange and leads to differentiation through genetic drift or local adaptation24. Conversely, the lowest level of genetic differentiation (FST = 0.046 to 0.055) and highest gene flow (Nm = 8.59 to 10.36) were observed between the Kermanshah–Ilam (FST = 0.046; Nm = 10.36) and Fars–Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad (FST = 0.055; Nm = 8.59) populations. These results may reflect geographic proximity or more continuous habitats that facilitate pathogen movement. AMOVA results showed that the majority of genetic variation (73%) resided within populations rather than among them, a common pattern in fungal populations with high dispersal potential and large effective population sizes. However, the 27% variation among populations is substantial and indicative of geographic structuring, likely influenced by both physical barriers and environmental gradients across the Zagros region. This finding is consistent with the high intraspecific genetic variability within populations reported by Henriques et al.30, reflecting the adaptation of B. mediterranea to local environment conditions and contributing to the species’ long-term survival.

Population structure analyses using Bayesian clustering (STRUCTURE), and Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) consistently resolved the isolates into two major genetic clusters broadly corresponding to northern (Ilam–Kermanshah) and southern (Fars–Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad) provinces. The low level of admixture between these clusters suggests limited recent gene flow and possible reproductive isolation. This geographic partitioning may result from the rugged mountainous terrain and distinct climatic zones, which act as barriers to dispersal and promote local adaptation. Interestingly, DAPC suggested three clusters as the optimal solution, though the effective biological grouping corresponds to two clusters with overlapping membership, indicating some genetic complexity and potential sub-structuring within regions. This subtle differentiation might be driven by microenvironmental factors or host-associated selection pressures not resolved by other clustering methods.

The significant multilocus linkage disequilibrium (LD) observed across populations suggests that, despite evidence of recombination, clonal propagation or selection may play a role in shaping local genetic structure. Such linkage disequilibrium is often observed in fungal populations undergoing mixed reproduction or experiencing selective sweeps20 and can generate patterns that reflect recent population expansions or adaptation to host resistance. Linkage disequilibrium may arise from factors such as physical linkage, migration, selection, and genetic drift20. Migration can lead to admixture when incoming isolates introduce alleles that differ from those in the recipient population. Stochastic fluctuations in allele frequencies (random genetic drift) can progressively reduce genetic diversity over generations, particularly in small populations19, 20. This observation warrants further investigation using additional molecular markers and mating-type analyses to clarify reproductive modes.

Pathogenicity tests further corroborated the genetic findings, demonstrating significant variability in aggressiveness among haplotypes. All tested isolates caused typical charcoal canker symptoms on oak seedlings, confirming their pathogenicity through fulfillment of Koch’s postulates. The current and previous studies10, 31 collectively highlight that the pathogenicity of fungal species associated with charcoal canker in oak is influenced not only by genetic variability but also by environmental stress factors, such as drought. Our findings reveal significant differences in aggressiveness among genetically distinct populations of B. mediterranea, with western isolates (from Kermanshah and Ilam provinces) exhibiting greater aggressiveness than those from the southwest (Fars and Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad provinces), suggesting a higher virulence potential of isolates originating from western Iran. This variation appears to correspond with the more aggravated sanitary and physiological status of trees in these regions, underscoring that pathogenic potential is shaped by both genetic differentiation and environmental conditions. Western Iran (Kermanshah and Ilam provinces) experiences more frequent and severe drought stress and exhibits poorer sanitary and physiological conditions of Q. brantii stands compared to southwestern regions. Consistent with this interpretation, our previous study demonstrated that drought stress significantly enhances lesion development in Q. brantii seedlings inoculated with B. mediterranea10. Together, these findings suggest that the higher aggressiveness of western isolates results from the interaction between genetic differentiation and region-specific environmental stress, particularly water deficit, which increases host susceptibility and disease severity. Together, these studies underline the necessity of an integrated approach that combines genetic, ecological, and environmental perspectives to fully understand the dynamics of charcoal canker disease. Effective management and conservation of Q. brantii forests in the Zagros region should therefore account for the regional genetic variability of pathogens as well as prevailing environmental stress conditions to mitigate disease impact and preserve forest health.

Overall, our results highlight that B. mediterranea populations in the Zagros forests maintain high genetic diversity, structured mainly by geography into two distinct clusters. This genetic structure likely reflects historical isolation mediated by the complex Zagros topography and environmental heterogeneity. Understanding this spatial genetic variation is critical for designing effective management strategies aimed at mitigating oak decline caused by B. mediterranea. Considering the high genetic diversity and potential for adaptation, disease control measures should be region-specific and incorporate monitoring of pathogen population dynamics over time. Additionally, the evaluation of internal symptoms, incubation period, and other pathogenicity-related traits may provide a more comprehensive understanding of haplotype aggressiveness, and future studies should consider incorporating these assessments. Future research integrating ecological variables, host genotype interactions, and temporal sampling will be essential to elucidate the drivers of genetic differentiation and the evolutionary trajectory of B. mediterranea. Moreover, investigating gene flow mechanisms, including potential vectors and dispersal distances, will improve our understanding of pathogen spread in fragmented forest landscapes.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

During the years 2019 and 2020, under field conditions, sampling was conducted in oak forests located in four distinct geographic regions of Iran: Ilam and Kermanshah provinces in 2019, and Fars and Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad provinces in 2020 (Fig. 9). The Iranian populations were sampled from these provinces, each characterized by a diverse assemblage of oak species, predominantly Q. brantii. The geographical distances between the four populations ranged from 180 to 1054 km. From each selected tree, multiple symptomatic tissue samples were collected, but only one isolate per tree was chosen as a representative isolate (see Supplementary Table S1) for further analysis. Oak trees exhibiting characteristic symptoms of charcoal canker disease, including the presence of carbonaceous stromata, were selected. Collections were carried out from early spring to late winter. For population genetic analysis, a single representative field site was selected in each geographic region. From each site, between 30 and 50 symptomatic trees were sampled, and a single isolate was successfully obtained from each sampled tree, resulting in a total of 159 isolates (Supplementary Table S1). The four sites were separated by distances exceeding 180 km.

Map of Iran illustrating the sampling regions of Biscogniauxia mediterranea populations within the Zagros forests, including the provinces of Fars, Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad, Kermanshah, and Ilam.

Fungal Isolation and Morphological Identification

Fungal isolates were directly obtained from the carbonaceous stromata containing perithecia and ascospores collected from symptomatic oak tree tissues. Samples were transported to the laboratory in polyethylene bags and subjected to surface sterilization for 60 s in 70% ethanol. Subsequently, the samples were cut into 8–10 mm segments using a sterile scalpel, sterilized for 40 s in 0.5% sodium hypochlorite solution, rinsed with sterile deionized water, and placed onto 8-cm-diameter plastic Petri dishes containing sterile moistened filter paper. These dishes were maintained under high humidity conditions and incubated at 20 °C. The samples were monitored daily until ascospores were released from the black stromata surfaces. Released ascospores were transferred onto 2% water-agar medium using a sterile loop and incubated at 25 °C. After 24 h, single-germinated ascospores were isolated and transferred onto Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA; Merck, Germany) plates (39 g/L), supplemented with chloramphenicol (100 μg/ml) to inhibit bacterial growth10. The obtained isolates were evaluated and classified based on the morphological description in valid taxonomic keys of Biscogniauxia16, 30, 32.

DNA extraction and molecular identification

For all 159 isolates, two 5 mm mycelial plugs from 5-day-old PDA cultures were transferred into 50 ml of Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB: 300 g/L potato extract and 20 g/L dextrose) in 250 ml flasks and incubated on a rotary shaker at 120 rpm for one week. Mycelial biomass was then harvested, washed, lyophilized, and ground into a fine powder in sterile 2 ml microcentrifuge tubes. Total genomic DNA was extracted following the CTAB protocol described by Murray and Thompson33. DNA concentrations were standardized to 25 ng/μl for each sample, which were subsequently stored at –20 °C until further use.

All isolates were initially cultured on PDA and examined for their morphological characteristics, and then grouped based on banding patterns using the WILLISSR5 primer35. Based on morphological characteristics and banding pattern analyses, 30 representative isolates were chosen from a total of 159 isolates for subsequent phylogenetic analysis (Supplementary Table S1). The pair of primers ITS1/ITS435, and T1/Bt2b36, 37 were used to amplify ITS-rDNA region and β-tubulin gene, respectively. PCR amplifications of β-tubulin gene and ITS-rDNA region were described by Ahmadi et al.10. The phylogenetic tree was inferred using the Maximum Likelihood method implemented in MEGA1138, under the Tamura 3-parameter model, with branch support evaluated using 1000 bootstrap replicates38. Hypoxylon rubiginosum served as the outgroup taxon to root the tree, owing to its close taxonomic relationship with the genus Biscogniauxia10.

ISSR fingerprinting

An initial set of 14 ISSR primers was employed for preliminary screening of twelve randomly selected isolates to identify primers exhibiting the highest polymorphism. These twelve isolates were randomly selected from four distinct geographic regions (Fars, Kohgiluyeh & Boyer-Ahmad, Ilam, and Kermanshah), with three isolates chosen from each region to represent different populations. The complete list of primers included: WILLISSR1, WILLISSR2, WILLISSR3, WILLISSR4, WILLISSR5, UBC885, ISSR09, P4, M13, ISSR5, ISSR10, PcMs, LB-B, and ISSR339, 40, 41.

PCR amplification was conducted using a Thermocycler (Biometra, Germany) in 20 μl reaction volumes containing 1 μM primer (CinnaGen Co., Iran), 0.1 mM of each dNTP (MBI Fermentas, Germany), 2 μl of 10X PCR buffer (MBI Fermentas, Germany), 0.5 U Dream Taq DNA polymerase (MBI Fermentas, Germany), and 10 ng of genomic DNA. The PCR cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 96 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 96 °C for 30 s, annealing for 1 min (see Supplementary Table S22 for specific temperatures), extension at 72 °C for 80 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplified fragments were separated by electrophoresis on 1.2% agarose gels in 1X TBE buffer at 70 V for 90 min, stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized under UV illumination using the VersaDoc Gel Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA).

Analyses of population genetic structure

The sizes of the amplified fragments were determined by comparison with a molecular weight ladder (FluoroBand™ 100 bp + 3 K Fluorescent DNA Ladder (SMOBIO Technology Inc., Taiwan)) using GelAnalyzer version 23.1.1 (https://github.com/istvanlazar/gelanalyzer-release/releases/tag/23.1.1). Binary scoring was performed based on the presence (1) or absence (0) of amplification products generated by eight ISSR primers, and the resulting data were compiled into a rectangular binary matrix for further analysis. Clone correction was conducted using the poppr R package v. 2.9.142 to remove redundant multilocus genotypes (MLGs) or haplotypes within individual collection regions. Notably, the poppr package retains identical MLGs when they originate from distinct collection regions. To avoid analytical bias due to overrepresentation of clonal genotypes, all downstream analyses were performed using the clone-corrected dataset43, 44.

Genetic diversity indices were calculated using the poppr R package v. 2.9. 142, including the expected number of multilocus genotypes (eMLG), genotypic diversity (G)45, Shannon–Wiener diversity index (H)46, genotypic evenness (E5), and Nei’s unbiased gene diversity (Hexp)47, across all loci and collection sites for both groups of sampling regions. The number of private alleles (NPA) and allelic richness were also estimated using poppr. Additional genetic parameters, including the number of amplified bands, effective number of alleles (Ne), percentage of polymorphic loci (PPL) per locus, genetic differentiation among populations (FST), and the estimated number of migrants (Nm = 0.5(1 – FST)/FST) per locus, as well as the fixation index (FST)48, were calculated using GenAlEx version 6.549. Clonal fractions (CF) were computed as CF = [1 – (number of unique MLGs)/(total number of isolates)]. All analyses were performed on clone-corrected datasets, in which only one representative isolate per multilocus genotype was retained per population. Nei’s genetic identity and genetic distance among the four B. mediterranea populations were assessed using POPGENE v. 1.3250. Genetic variation within and among populations was partitioned using analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) to assess the hierarchical distribution of genetic differentiation at regional scales, implemented in GenAlEx version 6.549.

To test for possible recombination in the B. mediterranea populations, gametic disequilibrium was evaluated by calculating the index of association (IA) and the standardized index of multilocus linkage disequilibrium (\bar rd)51 using the poppr R package v. 2.9.142 in R v. 3.6.3 (R Core Team 2020), with 10,000 permutations conducted for both sets of collection regions. Deviations from the null hypothesis of linkage equilibrium were assessed by comparing the observed variance of genetic distances among all pairs of individuals with the variance expected under random mating, obtained through 1000 permutations per population dataset.

The program STRUCTURE v. 2.3.452 was employed to infer the genetic clustering of the four Iranian B. mediterranea populations and to estimate individual admixture proportions. The number of assumed populations (K) was tested over a range from K = 1 to K = 10, using 1,000,000 Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations following a burn-in period of 50,000 iterations, and individuals were probabilistically assigned to one or more of the inferred clusters. STRUCTURE Harvester (http://taylor0.biology.ucla.edu/structure Harvester) was used for the visualization and summary of the STRUCTURE outputs53. The ΔK method, as described by Evanno et al.54, was applied to determine the most likely number of clusters, reduce the potential for overestimation of K, and accurately infer the population structure of B. mediterranea. Bar plots of the genetic structure were generated using STRUCTURE PLOT v. 2.055.

Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) was conducted using the adegenet package in the R environment to identify the number of genetic clusters among B. mediterranea isolates, independent of their geographic origin56. This multivariate method integrates Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with Discriminant Analysis (DA) to maximize genetic variation between clusters while minimizing variation within clusters, without assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium or linkage disequilibrium. The optimal number of genetic clusters was determined by finding clusters function, which applies successive K-means clustering over increasing values of K. The most appropriate number of clusters was selected based on the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) value. The number of principal components (PCs) retained in the final analysis was optimized using the xval.dapc function in adegenet v. 2.1.0. Additionally, Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) was performed in GenAlEx v. 6.549 to visualize patterns of genetic structure and quantify the variance explained by each axis and cumulatively across axes. Moreover, the genetic relationships among isolates were evaluated through cluster analysis using the unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA), based on the Jaccard similarity coefficient, and conducted in NTSYSpc v. 2.02e57.

Assessment of isolate aggressiveness

For pathogenicity assessment, a two-step screening approach was employed due to the practical limitations of performing pathogenicity tests on two-year-old oak seedlings for all identified haplotypes. Initially, the pathogenicity of 159 haplotypes was assessed using detached oak branches, following the method described by Ghaderi and Habibi58. The length of necrotic lesions was measured as an indicator of isolate aggressiveness. Based on the results of this preliminary screening, four haplotypes exhibiting the longest necrotic lesions were selected from each province for subsequent pathogenicity assays on oak seedlings. Thus, the selected haplotypes represented the most aggressive isolates within each regional population rather than being randomly selected.

In total, sixteen haplotypes were included in pathogenicity assays, comprising four selected haplotypes from each of the four provinces: Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad (KB2, KB15, KB32, KB44), Ilam (Ilam4, Ilam11, Ilam18, Ilam27), Kermanshah (Kermanshah6, Kermanshah19, Kermanshah23, Kermanshah29), and Fars (Fars8, Fars18, Fars41, Fars57). Prior to inoculation, the stems of two-year-old oak seedlings were surface-sterilized with 70% ethanol to eliminate potential microbial contaminants. A T-shaped incision measuring approximately 20–30 mm in length was made in the bark using a sterile scalpel at a height of 15–25 cm above the collar. A 6-mm plug of PDA containing actively growing mycelium from 5-day-old cultures of each selected haplotype was inserted into the incision, and the wound was wrapped with Parafilm to retain moisture and prevent desiccation. Control seedlings were treated identically, except that sterile PDA plugs without fungal mycelium were used. All inoculated and control seedlings were maintained in a greenhouse under standard irrigation conditions. Environmental conditions were maintained at 25–28 ± 2 °C with a relative humidity of 55 ± 5%, and a 12-h light/dark photoperiod, following the protocol of Ahmadi et al.10. Three months post-inoculation, the bark was carefully removed, and necrotic lesions were measured on the exposed stems tissues of inoculated seedlings. The necrotic lesion area was quantified using an area meter (Fanavaran Alborz Andisheh Co., Iran). The experiment was conducted using a completely randomized design (CRD) with three biological replicates, in which each replicate consisted of a single oak seedling grown in an individual pot. The effect of isolates on lesion length was analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) within the general linear model framework using SAS software version 9.1 (SAS Institute Inc., USA). Mean comparisons were performed using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test in SAS software at a significance threshold of P ≤ 0.05. To fulfill Koch’s postulates, fungal re-isolation was performed from the margins of necrotic lesions in all symptomatic samples, and the resulting isolates were morphologically re-identified following the procedures described in Ahmadi et al.10.
