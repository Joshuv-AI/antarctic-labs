Recurrent COPA mutation drives R-spondin-independent Wnt activation in intestinal tumors
Authors: Masayuki Fujii, Naoko Abeto, Shotaro Kishimoto, Kouya Shiraishi, Taiki Hashimoto, Nobuyoshi Hiraoka, Satoru Nonaka, Motohiro Kojima, Mami Matano, Sirirat Takahashi, Gabriele Colozza, Bon-Kyoung Koo, Yasushi Yatabe, Motohiko Kato, Toshiro Sato, Shigeki Sekine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe majority of intestinal tumors harbor mutations in canonical Wnt pathway genes such as APC, whereas the lack of such alterations in a subset of tumors implies alternative tumorigenic routes. Here we identify recurrent in-frame deletion in COPA, frequently co-occurring with USP9X-truncating mutation, in small intestinal adenoma and adenocarcinoma. Patient-derived and CRISPR-engineered small intestinal organoids carrying COPA in-frame deletions exhibit R-spondin-independent yet Wnt ligand-dependent growth, maintaining LGR5 expression without canonical Wnt drivers. Mechanistically, COPA mutation stabilizes the Frizzled coreceptor LRP6 irrespective of R-spondin, sustaining Wnt pathway activation under growth factor-restricted conditions. USP9X loss further potentiates this phenotype. Unlike canonical Wnt pathway members, COPA encodes the α-subunit of coatomer complex I, which engages in vesicle trafficking with little prior linkage to intestinal tumorigenesis. Our findings establish COPA mutation as a unique and atypical intestinal tumor driver and implicate USP9X loss as a cooperating lesion.Subject terms: Gastrointestinal cancer, Clinical genetics, Stem cells, Genetics research

Main

Although the small intestine is the longest segment in the gastrointestinal tract, measuring 3–4 m in length in adult humans1, small intestinal adenocarcinoma is a rare cancer and represents only 3% of all gastrointestinal cancers2. Patients with small intestinal adenocarcinoma often present with late-stage disease and have a poor prognosis, partly due to the anatomically poor accessibility of the small intestine and delay in diagnosis3. The rarity of the disease has also rendered in-depth investigation of small intestinal adenocarcinoma challenging. Few human-relevant models of small intestinal tumors are available, and research on small intestinal tumors has largely relied on mouse models that develop tumors via the introduction of genetic drivers that mostly reflect colorectal cancer genomics4.

Tumorigenesis in the human small intestine follows an adenoma–carcinoma sequence, reminiscent of the well-established process in the colon2. Both colon adenomas and adenocarcinomas have a high prevalence of APC-truncating mutations, providing a clear genetic basis for the colonic adenoma-to-carcinoma progression5. Most non-APC-mutated colon cancers carry alterations in other Wnt pathway members, including mutations in CTNNB1 and RNF43 and R-spondin fusions, and such enrichment of Wnt pathway mutations in colon cancer highlights the importance of Wnt hyperactivation as a master driver of colon tumorigenesis. Our previous sequencing analysis of small intestinal adenomas also captured APC truncations in ~90% of intestinal-type small intestinal adenomas6. Despite this high prevalence in adenomas, large-scale sequencing studies on small intestinal adenocarcinomas have consistently highlighted the low frequency of APC mutation (< 30% of cases)7–12. Other canonical Wnt-activating mutations, including CTNNB1 mutation10,13, explain only a minority of tumors. This discrepancy suggests that alternative routes drive small intestinal tumorigenesis.

To explore such drivers of human small intestinal tumorigenesis, we here focused on small intestinal tumors histologically distinct from APC-mutated tumors. Their molecular characterization highlighted recurrent in-frame deletions in COPA, encoding α-COP or COPA, and truncating mutations in USP9X. COPA is a subunit of coatomer complex I that coats vesicles responsible for intra-Golgi trafficking as well as retrograde transport from the Golgi to the endoplasmic reticulum14. USP9X encodes the USP9X deubiquitinase known to affect multiple pathways, including those related to cancer15, and is recurrently mutated in several cancers, including specific subtypes of head and neck and ovarian carcinomas16,17. While COPA in-frame deletion has not been captured in past cancer genomics studies, phenotypic characterization using patient-derived organoids and gene engineering revealed Wnt signaling hyperactivation independent of intestinal stem cell niche factors in COPA-mutated or COPA-engineered small intestinal organoids. Our findings reveal an alternative mode of Wnt activation during small intestinal tumorigenesis, which partly accounts for the low prevalence of APC mutations in small intestinal adenocarcinoma.

Results

COPAexon13 in-frame deletions in small intestinal adenomas

Pathologically, most small intestinal adenomas are flat, APC-mutant lesions with straight glands lined by small intestinal-type epithelium6 (Fig. 1a). However, we identified a morphologically distinct subset with protruded endoscopic appearance and histologically characterized by branched glands and ectopic crypt formation while retaining small intestinal-type differentiation (Fig. 1a,b), suggesting distinct molecular alterations. In a pathological review of 254 consecutive small intestinal adenoma cases including ampullary adenomas, such protruded-type adenomas with intestinal differentiation accounted for 6.3% (16/254) of the cohort (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Whole-exome sequencing (WES) of three cases with typical protrusion and intestinal differentiation, which were not included in the consecutive cases, did not capture mutations in known Wnt drivers (Supplementary Table 1). Instead, all three cases harbored an in-frame deletion in COPA, which were confined to exon 13 (COPAex13) (Fig. 2a,b and Extended Data Fig. 1b). These mutations co-occurred with copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity (CN-LOH) at chromosome 1q spanning the COPA locus, suggesting a hypomorphic effect (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 1c). WES also identified a truncating mutation in USP9X in all three cases (Fig. 2d). Immunostaining revealed mosaic loss of USP9X protein in two tumors, which suggests that USP9X truncation in these tumors occurred secondary to COPAex13 in-frame deletion (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 1d).

To further investigate the prevalence of COPAex13 mutation, we sequenced COPAex13 in the aforementioned cohort of 254 small intestinal adenomas and identified 10 cases with a COPAex13 mutation, which were enriched in protruded adenomas with intestinal differentiation (7/16 cases) (Fig. 2f). All COPAex13 mutations were in-frame deletions affecting 6–18 amino acids, particularly residues L393–P397 (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table 2). USP9X immunostaining of a subset of these cases from the consecutive series revealed preserved USP9X expression in 95 of 96 COPA-wild-type adenomas and its loss in 2 of 10 COPA-mutant adenomas, consistent with the prior observation (Extended Data Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 2). Together, these data establish COPAex13 in-frame deletion and USP9X-truncating mutation as alternative drivers of small intestinal tumorigenesis independent of canonical Wnt driver gene mutations.

COPAex13 in-frame deletions in small intestinal adenocarcinomas

The discovery of COPAex13 in-frame deletions in small intestinal adenomas prompted us to analyze their prevalence in small intestinal adenocarcinomas. Sequencing of COPAex13 in 55 duodenal adenocarcinomas identified 7 cases with a COPAex13 in-frame deletion encompassing L393–K398, supporting the recurrence and relevance of this genetic event during small intestinal carcinogenesis (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 3). In line with the USP9X immunostaining pattern in adenomas, USP9X expression was retained in COPA-wild-type adenocarcinomas, whereas a fraction of COPA-mutant adenocarcinomas lost USP9X (Fig. 3b,c and Supplementary Table 3).

We performed WES analysis of COPA-mutated duodenal adenocarcinoma cases that met the DNA quality threshold for WES, together with three adenoma cases in the consecutive series and one adenoma found to have a COPAex13 in-frame deletion post hoc (DUA_D), totaling seven adenomas and four adenocarcinomas with COPAex13 in-frame deletions. WES confirmed the absence of canonical Wnt driver gene mutations in these COPA-mutant small intestinal tumors, while other drivers, including TP53 and KRAS mutations, were detected mainly in adenocarcinoma (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table 1). Around half of the cases harbored USP9X-truncating mutations (Fig. 3d). A review of prior WES studies on small intestinal and ampullary cancer7,9,13,18,19 further identified three cases with a COPAex13 in-frame deletion, validating its presence in other cohorts (Fig. 3d).

Notably, one adenocarcinoma case (DUC02) was found to have a coexisting adenoma component (Fig. 3e). COPAex13 Sanger sequencing and WES of the adenoma component revealed that the adenoma component had a COPAex13 in-frame deletion identical to the one in the adenocarcinoma component but lacked canonical cancer drivers present in the adenocarcinoma component, including KRAS hotspot and ELF3-truncating mutations (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 1). Albeit a single case, this example clearly illustrates adenocarcinoma development from a COPA-mutant adenoma. Together, these findings suggest that COPAex13 in-frame deletion represents the primary hit during carcinogenesis of COPA-mutant tumors with USP9X-truncating mutation occurring as a second hit, and additional alterations in canonical cancer drivers such as KRAS and TP53, as well as chromosomal instability, may further facilitate adenoma–carcinoma progression.

Activation of Wnt signaling in COPA-mutated adenomas

To understand the biological effect of COPAex13 in-frame deletions, we extracted RNA from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections of normal duodenal mucosa, COPA-mutated adenomas and flat intestinal-type adenomas and performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (Supplementary Table 4). Principal component analysis clearly distinguished these three types based on the transcriptome pattern (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 2a). COPA-mutant adenomas upregulated Wnt target and intestinal stem cell genes, including LGR5, AXIN2 and SMOC2, while downregulating enterocyte markers, albeit to a lesser extent than flat intestinal-type adenomas (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 2b,c). Inference of transcription factor network activity by regulon analysis confirmed decreased activity of differentiation-related transcription factors such as HNF-1α and CDX2 and enhanced activity of transcription factors associated with Wnt signaling (β-catenin and TCF7L2) and the cell cycle in COPA-mutant adenomas (Fig. 4c). Transcriptome analysis also highlighted unique upregulation of metallothionein genes in COPA-mutant adenomas (Fig. 4b,d and Extended Data Fig. 2c,d). Although the biological significance of elevated levels of metallothioneins in these tumors remains unclear, these genes may serve as potential biomarkers for COPAex13 in-frame deletion. Collectively, COPA mutations activate Wnt signaling in the absence of canonical pathway mutations.

Prospective derivation of COPA-mutated duodenal adenoma organoids

To functionally investigate the impact of COPAex13 in-frame deletions in human small intestinal epithelium, we set out to establish patient-derived organoids from COPA-mutant small intestinal tumors. Although COPA-mutant small intestinal adenomas are rare, their unique protruding macroscopic appearance and intestinal differentiation allowed us to prospectively select cases suspected to have a COPAex13 in-frame deletion. Consequently, we were able to sample tissues from such cases and successfully established three patient-derived duodenal adenoma organoid lines with COPAex13 in-frame deletions (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 5). Sanger sequencing and WES confirmed COPAex13 in-frame deletions in the established adenoma organoids and their absence in patient-matched normal counterpart organoids (Fig. 5b, Extended Data Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 6). Consistent with the initial finding in clinical samples, WES detected similar CN-LOH at chromosome 1q spanning COPA in all organoid lines (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 3b), whereas USP9X mutation was absent (Supplementary Table 6). Testing of the dependence of COPA-mutated organoids on individual growth factors revealed that COPA-mutated organoids were stringently dependent on exogenous Wnt-3A and receptor tyrosine kinase ligands, including EGF, IGF-1 and FGF-2, but were less dependent on R-spondin, which is a ligand for LGR5 and an essential amplifier of Wnt signaling, and bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) antagonist Noggin, compared with the normal counterparts (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 3c). RNA-seq analysis further demonstrated retained Wnt activation in COPA-mutated organoids despite R-spondin withdrawal, as well as high expression of metallothionein genes, suggesting cell-intrinsic upregulation by COPA mutation (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 3d,e). These results collectively indicate that COPAex13 in-frame deletions drive Wnt activation in a Wnt ligand-dependent but R-spondin- and Noggin-independent manner, thereby conferring a selective growth advantage under environments with limited R-spondin and Noggin availability.

Modeling COPAex13 in-frame deletion in normal intestinal organoids

To directly validate whether COPAex13 in-frame deletion drives R-spondin- and Noggin-independent growth, we introduced a 6-amino acid deletion into the COPA alleles of human normal small intestinal organoids via CRIPSR-mediated genome editing (Fig. 6a and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Although we devised a knock-in strategy without knock-in-specific selection, genome editing was remarkably efficient in duodenal and ileal organoids, achieving biallelic in-frame deletion in 38 of the 41 randomly selected clones (Extended Data Fig. 4b). Consistent with the results from patient-derived COPA-mutated adenoma organoids, COPA-engineered (COPA6del) normal organoids remained dependent on Wnt-3A but showed tolerable growth in the absence of R-spondin or Noggin (Fig. 6b,c and Extended Data Fig. 4c). Further evaluation of growth factor dependence using multiple clones with differing COPA mutation patterns validated R-spondin and Noggin independence in clones with biallelic COPAex13 in-frame deletion (Fig. 6d and Extended Data Fig. 4d,e). Notably, clones with a monoallelic COPAex13 in-frame deletion accompanied by a truncating or intact counter-allele failed to thrive in the R-spondin- or Noggin-deprived conditions, in agreement with the invariable involvement of chromosome 1q CN-LOH in clinical COPA-mutant adenomas (Extended Data Fig. 4d). COPA-engineered human normal colon organoids also showed modest R-spondin independence (Fig. 6d and Extended Data Fig. 4c). Thus, COPA-edited small intestinal organoids phenocopied patient-derived COPA-mutant organoids and showed that COPAex13 in-frame deletion alone is sufficient for the acquisition of R-spondin and Noggin independence.

COPAex13 in-frame deletion stabilizes LRP6

To probe the mechanisms of R-spondin and Noggin independence by COPAex13 in-frame deletion, we performed RNA-seq of COPA-engineered organoids. Consistent with the RNA-seq analysis of patient-derived COPA-mutant adenoma organoids, COPA6del organoids, but not counterpart normal organoids or a clone with a heterozygous 6-amino acid deletion in COPA, exhibited retention of Wnt activation and expression of intestinal stem cell genes following R-spondin removal (Fig. 6e). Similar trends were observed in multiple small intestinal organoid lines, as well as in a colon organoid line (Extended Data Fig. 4f,g). A COPA-engineered ileum organoid line with an LGR5-tdTomato reporter (Ileum2)20 showed partial retention of endogenous LGR5 expression in COPA6del organoids in the absence of R-spondin (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Mechanistically, COPA-engineered small intestinal organoids, as well as COPA-mutated patient-derived adenoma organoids, stabilized LRP6, a Wnt coreceptor normally degraded by RNF43 and ZNRF3 in the absence of R-spondin21,22 (Fig. 6f and Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). COPA-edited colon organoids also showed LRP6 stabilization in the absence of R-spondin (Extended Data Fig. 5d). This LRP6 stabilization by COPAex13 in-frame deletion was considered to be regulated at the protein level, as the mRNA levels of LRP6 and RNF43 did not parallel those of LRP6 protein in ileum organoids with or without COPA editing (Extended Data Fig. 5e). The similar expression pattern of LRP6 and RNF43, as well as AXIN2 and LGR5, between COPA-edited ileum and colon organoids suggested that LRP6 stabilization by COPA mutation is conserved between the small and large intestines at least at the molecular level (Extended Data Fig. 5e). The same pattern of LRP6 stabilization uncoupled from mRNA levels in COPA-edited organoids was also evident following Noggin removal (Extended Data Fig. 5f,g). Given the negative regulation of LGR5 by BMP signaling23, the R-spondin and Noggin independence of COPA-mutant organoids could be attributed to LGR5-independent LRP6 stabilization.

The involvement of COPA in cis-Golgi and retrograde vesicle transport prompted us to analyze the localization of COPA in parental and COPA6del ileum organoids. Consistent with a previous study24, COPA largely colocalized with cis-Golgi surface protein GM130 in parental organoids, and the introduction of COPA deletion did not significantly affect this colocalization pattern (Extended Data Fig. 5h,i). COPA6del organoids also featured upregulation of metallothionein genes, in line with their unique upregulation in patient-derived COPA-mutant organoids (Extended Data Fig. 5j). R-spondin removal did not affect the expression of metallothionein genes in COPA6del organoids while marginally downregulating canonical Wnt target genes (Extended Data Fig. 5j), suggesting that metallothionein upregulation in COPA-mutant tumors occurs independently of Wnt hyperactivation.

USP9X loss promotes niche independence in COPA-engineered organoids

Finally, we asked whether USP9X loss provides any growth advantage by genetically disrupting USP9X in ileal organoids with or without the COPA6del deletion (Fig. 7a and Extended Data Fig. 6a). Knockout of USP9X in COPA6del organoids, but not in wild-type organoids, enhanced organoid expansion in R-spondin- and/or Noggin-lacking conditions (Fig. 7b,c and Extended Data Fig. 6b), consistent with the patchy USP9X loss in clinical COPA-mutated tumors. RNA-seq of USP9X-knockout (USP9XKO) organoids and COPA6del/USP9XKO double-mutant organoids, together with parental and COPA6del organoids, revealed upregulation of genes related to Wnt signaling and intestinal stem cells in COPA6del/USP9XKO double-mutant organoids but not in USP9XKO organoids (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). In line with this observation, USP9X knockout in COPA6del organoids further augmented LRP6 stabilization following R-spondin or Noggin removal (Fig. 7d and Extended Data Fig. 6e). Dishevelled 2 (DVL2) expression mirrored the dynamics of LRP6 expression following COPA/USP9X editing and R-spondin removal, consistent with its interaction with the LRP6–Frizzled receptor complex25, whereas USP9X knockout alone did not markedly alter the expression of DVL2 and COPA (Fig. 7d). These findings confirm that COPAex13 in-frame deletion confers R-spondin and Noggin independence, which is further augmented by USP9X loss.

COPAex13 in-frame deletion reduces cell-surface RNF43 levels

To further investigate the mechanism of LRP6 stabilization in COPA mutants, we set our focus on the expression of RNF43, which facilitates cell-surface LRP–Frizzled clearance in an R-spondin- and LGR5-sensitive manner22. To this end, we used the HiBiT system, which enables accurate luciferase-based measurement of total or cell-surface protein tagged with the HiBiT peptide (Fig. 7e). Induced expression of HiBiT-tagged RNF43 (RNF43HiBiT) downregulated LRP6 and AXIN2 in Ileum1 organoids, validating the expression of functional RNF43 (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). Using this inducible RNF43HiBiT-expressing Ileum1 organoid clone (Ileum1-RNF43HiBiT), we newly generated USP9XKO, COPA6del and COPA6del/USP9XKO organoid lines (Extended Data Fig. 7c). To avoid LGR5 downregulation and cytostasis by RNF43HiBiT expression, we applied GSK3 treatment before measurement (Extended Data Fig. 7d,e). Introduction of the COPA6del deletion downregulated cell-surface levels of RNF43 in both the presence and the absence of R-spondin (Fig. 7f), which suggests that LRP6 stabilization in COPA mutants is partly due to reduced cell-surface RNF43 levels. In contrast, USP9X knockout reduced total RNF43 abundance, and COPA6del/USP9XKO organoids exhibited a further composite reduction in both total and cell-surface RNF43 levels (Fig. 7f). Altogether, COPAex13 in-frame deletion promotes R-spondin- and LGR5-independent growth at least in part through downregulation of cell-surface RNF43 levels and subsequent LRP6 stabilization.

Discussion

Activation of Wnt signaling is essential for the self-renewal of the intestinal epithelium, as represented by the stringent dependence of human small intestinal and colonic organoids on exogenous Wnt-3A and R-spondin26. Recurrent APC mutation, which confers Wnt-3A and R-spondin independence on human intestinal organoids27,28, has characterized the inception of the adenoma–carcinoma sequence in the colon, whereas the identification of a similar genetic pathway in the small intestine has remained elusive. Our recognition of the protruded intestinal-type small intestinal adenoma led to the illumination of recurrent COPAex13 in-frame deletion, and patient-derived organoids coupled with gene editing further supported its role as an alternative driver of R-spondin-independent growth during small intestinal tumorigenesis. Unlike conventional Wnt regulators, COPA encodes a subunit of coat protein complex I, which mediates intra-Golgi transport and Golgi-to-endoplasmic reticulum retrograde vesicle trafficking14, defining an alternative mechanistic class of tumor drivers. Although germline COPA missense mutations in specific codons in COPA exons 7–10 are known to cause an autoinflammatory syndrome termed COPA syndrome29,30, these mutations are distinct from the COPAex13 in-frame deletions identified in small intestinal tumors.

Although COPAex13 in-frame deletion has not been documented in cancer genomics studies, including those on colorectal cancer, introduction of the COPA6del deletion in human normal colon organoids provided modest R-spondin independence, as well as LRP6 stabilization and Wnt signaling retention in the absence of R-spondin (Extended Data Fig. 5d,e). The regulation of Wnt activation by COPAex13 in-frame deletion thus appears to be preserved across the small intestine, colon and perhaps other tissues at least at the molecular level. Although the reason for the lack of COPAex13 in-frame deletion in colorectal cancer is unclear, colorectal carcinogenesis may require higher Wnt activity, which is predominantly driven by alterations in downstream molecules such as APC and β-catenin, compared to the small intestine, thereby favoring ligand-independent constitutive Wnt activation over ligand-driven activation.

COPAex13 in-frame deletion also upregulated metallothionein genes, which are known to be induced by external stimuli such as heavy metal exposure, inflammation and oxidative stress31. Dysregulation of such signals was not apparent in the transcriptome analysis of patient-derived COPA-mutant adenomas (Extended Data Fig. 2a). Expression of metallothionein genes was also not linked with Wnt activation (Extended Data Figs. 2d and 5j). Metallothionein upregulation in COPA-mutant and COPA-edited organoids cultured in a uniform and antioxidant-rich milieu further suggests endogenous mechanisms. Although the cause of metallothionein induction in COPA-mutant tumors remains elusive, the unique upregulation of these genes may be exploited as a surrogate marker for COPA mutation.

Our analysis further highlighted USP9X loss as a context-dependent growth potentiator that provided R-spondin and Noggin independence only in the presence of COPAex13 in-frame deletion. USP9X is a deubiquitinase known to target multiple cancer-related pathways, including the Wnt pathway15,32,33. Although COPAex13 in-frame deletion, as well as additional USP9X knockout, reduced cell-surface RNF43 levels, thereby stabilizing LRP6, further studies are warranted to clarify the mechanisms by which specific COPA mutations, in concert with USP9X loss, downregulate cell-surface RNF43 levels and confer both R-spondin and Noggin independence.

In summary, we identified small intestinal tumors characterized by the COPAex13 in-frame deletion. Patient-derived and genetically engineered small intestinal organoids with COPAex13 in-frame deletion provide valuable human-relevant models for studying the phenotypic and mechanistic impact of COPA mutations. Our findings not only highlight a unique driver of intestinal tumorigenesis but also shed light on the previously unknown regulation of canonical Wnt signaling by coatomers.

Methods

Human samples

For histological assessment and DNA/RNA analysis of FFPE small intestinal tissues, we used patient samples archived in the National Cancer Center Hospital, Tokyo, Japan, and National Cancer Center Hospital East, Chiba, Japan. Tissue samples for organoid derivation were collected from patients undergoing endoscopy or elective surgery at Keio University Hospital and The University of Tokyo Hospital. The use of archived paraffin-embedded sections was approved by the ethical committee of the National Cancer Center under approval ID 2013-011, and the establishment of patient-derived organoids and their downstream analysis were approved by the ethical committees of Keio University School of Medicine and Graduate School of Medicine and Faculty of Medicine, The University of Tokyo, under approval IDs 20170170 and G3553, respectively. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Patient information is provided in Supplementary Tables 2, 3 and 5.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on FFPE specimens. Antigen retrieval was performed using Target Retrieval Solution pH 9 (Agilent Technologies). Primary antibodies used for immunohistochemistry are listed in Supplementary Table 7. For staining, we used an Autostainer Link 48 (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. An EnVision FLEX kit and Mouse LINKER (Agilent Technologies) were used for detection.

Immunofluorescence staining of organoids was also performed on FFPE sections. Organoids were released from Matrigel using Cell Recovery Solution (Corning), embedded in iPGell (GenoStaff), and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at room temperature overnight. FFPE sections (5-μm thick) were reacted with primary antibodies provided in Supplementary Table 7 and labeled with Alexa Fluor secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fluorescence images were captured using an FV4000 confocal microscope (Evident). Colocalization of COPA and GM130 signals was analyzed using the ImageJ plugin EzColocalization (version 1.1.4)35.

Macroscopic and histological classification of adenomas

The macroscopic appearance of adenomas was classified as protruded or flat based on endoscopic and/or gross pathological evaluation36. Ampullary adenomas predominantly confined to the common channel or the distal intramural segment of bile and pancreatic ducts were classified as intra-ampullary37. Based on histology, duodenal, ileal and jejunal adenomas were categorized as intestinal-type or gastric-type according to their histologic features38. Ampullary adenomas were classified as intestinal type or gastric/pancreatobiliary type37. Lesions exhibiting both intestinal and gastric or gastric/pancreatobiliary features were designated as mixed-type lesions. Macroscopic and histological subtypes of each adenoma are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

WES

WES of clinical samples was conducted using 200 ng of genomic DNA isolated from snap-frozen or FFPE tumor specimens and noncancerous tissues obtained from patients with COPA-mutated tumors. Tumor regions were microdissected under a microscope using a fine needle, and DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (QIAGEN). Exome capture was performed using the Twist Comprehensive Exome Panel or Twist Exome 2.0 plus Comprehensive Exome Spike-in (Twist Bioscience HQ), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) using 2 × 150-bp paired-end reads, resulting in ~150-fold genome coverage. Basic alignment and sequence quality control were conducted according to the GATK4 best practices pipeline39. The resulting reads from WES were aligned to hg38, using Parabricks v4.3.1.1 (NVIDIA), which delivers the high-speed analysis recommended by the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK), with GPU acceleration. Somatic single-nucleotide variants and small insertions/deletions were called using Mutect2 (GATK version 4.1.2.0). All selected variants were manually checked using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV)40. Somatic mutations detected in clinical samples are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Approximately 5 μg of genomic DNA was used for WES analysis of duodenal adenoma organoids. DNA was extracted from organoids using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN). WES libraries were generated using the SureSelect Human All Exon V6 kit (Agilent) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 device, yielding around 10 Gb of 150-bp paired-end reads. Following adapter trimming with cutadapt (version 1.16), sequencing reads were mapped to the human genome GRCh37 (hg19) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (version 0.17.8). Duplicated reads were tagged using MarkDuplicatesSpark in GATK (version 4.6.1), and base quality score calibration was done using BaseRecalibrator and ApplyBQSR in GATK. Variant calling was performed using Mutect2. Germline variants were removed by filtering out variants with an allele frequency of 0.1% or more in the Database of Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms or the Japanese germline variant database (the Human Genetic Variation Database). Furthermore, variants found in at least two of our panel of normal samples (23 normal organoids and 35 blood samples that were sequenced in our previous studies20,41,42) were also removed. Germline variants detected in counterpart normal organoids were also filtered out. DNA copy number analysis was performed using FACETS (version 0.6.2)43 with default settings. WES results of patient-derived organoids are provided in Supplementary Table 6.

Sanger sequencing

DNA samples were extracted from FFPE specimens using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen). DNA samples from organoids were collected using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen). Genomic PCR was performed using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 8. The PCR products were cleaned up using ExoSAP-IT (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and subjected to Sanger sequencing.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from FFPE tumor specimens using the RNeasy FFPE Kit (Qiagen), and aliquots (10 ng) of the purified material were used to prepare RNA-seq libraries with the SMART-Seq Stranded Kit (Takara Bio). The resultant libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing of 150-bp reads using the NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina). The first three bases in the 5′ end of the second sequencing reads were removed as SMART-Seq adapters using cutadapt. The trimmed reads were aligned to human genome hg38 using STAR (version 2.7.11b)44, and read counts of the human genes in Ensembl release 81 were calculated using RSEM (version 1.3.3)45. Quality metrics of FFPE RNA samples are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Total RNA for RNA-seq of organoid samples was collected using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research). Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA (Illumina) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 device. TruSeq adapters were removed using cutadapt. Mapping and gene-level read calculation were performed using STAR and RSEM as described above.

Read count data were normalized using the varianceStabilizingTransformation function in DESeq2 (version 1.42.1) with the blind = FALSE option, and the normalized data were used for principal component analysis and heatmap construction. Genes with a standard deviation of >98th percentile of all genes were used for principal component analysis. Differentially expressed gene analysis was performed using DESeq in DESeq2. In analyses involving organoids derived from different patients, patient ID was used as a covariate to take into account interpatient differences. Due to the inflation of the fold-change values of metallothionein genes, we adjusted the fold-change values with the lfcShrink function in DESeq2 using the adaptive Student’s t prior shrinkage estimator. Metascape interface46 was used for ontology enrichment analysis. Transcription factor networks with weight scores were obtained using get_collectri in decoupleR47 (version 2.9.7), and their activities were calculated using run_ulm in decoupleR. Normalized count values or Wald statistics in the DESeq2 output were used for network activity inference. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the gsva function in GSVA (version 1.50.5). Marker genes for cell types in the human small intestine were derived from a recent single-cell analysis of the human intestine48. Transit-amplifying cell genes are a union of TA1 and TA2 genes. Previously reported targets of the canonical Wnt signal in human organoids49 were used as canonical Wnt target genes. Gene sets used in the study are shown in Supplementary Table 9.

Organoid derivation

Human normal or adenomatous small intestinal tissues were obtained by endoscopic biopsy, by sampling of endoscopically resected tumor or from surgical specimens. The human normal colon organoid line used for COPA engineering (Colon1) was previously described50 (normal counterpart of CRC26). The Duo2 (ref. 51) and Ileum2 (ref. 20) lines were also established previously. Normal intestinal tissues were cut into 1-mm3 fragments using sharp forceps and washed at least ten times with cold PBS by pipetting. The tissues were treated with 2.5 mM EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at 4 °C with gentle rocking, and the epithelium was released by pipetting. The epithelial fragments were embedded into Matrigel (Corning) domes and overlaid with the full medium prepared as follows: Advanced Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10 mM HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mM GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× B-27 Supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10 nM gastrin I (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM N-acetylcysteine (Sigma-Aldrich), 100 ng ml−1 recombinant mouse Noggin (PeproTech), 50 ng ml−1 recombinant mouse EGF (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 ng ml−1 recombinant human IGF-1 (BioLegend), 50 ng ml−1 recombinant human FGF-basic (FGF-2; PeproTech), 1 μg ml−1 recombinant human R-spondin1 (R&D), 500 nM A83-01 (Tocris) and 5% Afamin-Wnt-3A serum-free conditioned medium. We added 50 μM Primocin (InvivoGen) for the first 2 days after organoid establishment and 10 μM Y-27632 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) for the first 2 days after every passaging.

Tumor intestinal tissues were also minced into 1-mm3 fragments and washed with ice-cold PBS. The tumor fragments were digested with 50 μg ml−1 Liberase TH Research Grade (Roche) for 1 h at 37 °C with mechanical disruption by pipetting every 15 min. The supernatant was collected, and the tissue remnants were further treated with TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min at 37 °C. The digested tissues were suspended in FBS to inactivate Liberase and washed two times with PBS. Tumor clusters were then embedded in Matrigel and overlaid with the full medium. Primocin and Y-27632 were used as described above. Dependence of organoids on each growth factor was assessed by plating the organoids as single cells with a density of 3,000 cells per well and culturing them in a condition lacking each growth factor. The CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay (Promega) and a GloMax plate reader (Promega) were used for ATP measurement. Clinical information of patient-derived organoids is provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Vector construction and gene engineering of organoids

Genomic targets for CRISPR-mediated gene editing were designed using CRISPick52 and cloned into the pX330 vector (42230, Addgene). The single-guide RNA targets are provided in Supplementary Table 8. To construct a donor vector for COPA 6-amino acid deletion, we cloned PCR-amplified 1-kb genomic loci flanking the 5′ and 3′ ends of the 18 bases to be deleted into the pCR2.1-TOPO vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Takara Bio).

CRISPR-mediated gene editing in patient-derived organoids was performed via electroporation as previously described53 with a few modifications. Before electroporation, the organoids were treated with 10 μM Y-27632 and 1.25% DMSO for 1 day. The organoids were dissociated into single cells using TrypLE Express and washed two times with Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cell pellet was then suspended in 100 μl of BTXpress High Performance Electroporation Solution (BTX Harvard Apparatus). The cell suspension was supplemented with 2.5 μg of each COPA CRISPR vector, 7.5 μg of the donor vector, 3.6 μg of PuroR or NeoR PiggyBAC vector (PB510B-1, PB533-A2, System Biosciences), and 1.4 μg of the transposase vector (PB210PA-1, System Biosciences) for knock-in of COPA 6-amino acid deletion and with 5 μg of each USP9X CRISPR vector, 3.6 μg of CMV-Puro PiggyBAC vector and 1.4 μg of the transposase vector for USP9X knockout. The cell-DNA mixture was applied to a 2-mm-gap cuvette (Nepa Gene) and electroporated using a NEPA21 electroporator (Nepa Gene). The cells were collected in 500 μl BTXpress electroporation solution supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 and kept at room temperature for 15 min. Following two washes with Advanced DMEM F/12, the cells were plated as Matrigel domes, overlaid with the full medium supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 and 1.25% DMSO and incubated at 30 °C. The medium was replaced with DMSO-lacking medium the next day, and the organoids were transferred to the 37 °C condition 4–5 days after electroporation. Following a 2-day selection with 2 μg ml−1 puromycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or a 1-week selection with 500 μg ml−1 geneticin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), single organoids were manually picked using a micropipette and expanded for genotyping. The indel pattern in each subclone was analyzed using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 8 and the ICE online tool (https://ice.editco.bio/#/).

The vector for doxycycline-inducible expression of RNF43 tagged with HA and HiBiT (RNF43HiBiT) was constructed by replacing the GPF cassette of the XLone vector54 with the RNF43HiBiT insert. The RNF43HiBiT construct was recently described55 and synthesized via GeneArt Custom Gene Synthesis (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Using the above electroporation method, 7.2 μg of the XLone-RNF43HiBiT vector was co-introduced with 2.8 μg of Super PiggyBac Transposase (PB210PA-1, System Biosciences) into the Ileum1 organoid line. Transduced organoids were selected by a 1-week treatment with 5 μg ml−1 blasticidin S (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). To ensure an equal copy number of the transgene across experiments, we cloned Ileum1-RNF43HiBiT organoids and engineered one clone with confirmed expression of HA and HiBiT to generate USP9XKO, COPA6del and COPA6del/USP9XKO organoid lines. Knockout of USP9X and knock-in of COPA6del in an Ileum1-RNF43HiBiT clone were done by electroporation as described above.

Real-time quantitative PCR

RNA was collected from organoids using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research) and was reverse-transcribed using the Omniscript RT Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Tli RNase H Plus) (Takara Bio) on a LightCycler 96 device (Roche Diagnostics). Gene expression was calculated with the ΔCt method using ACTB as a reference housekeeping gene. Primers used for quantitative PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 8.

Capillary immunoassay

Immunodetection of phospho- and total LRP6, DVL2, COPA and HA was performed using Simple Western automated protein detection (ProteinSimple). The organoids were plated as single cells at a density of 3,000 cells per well and harvested at day 7. In the R-spondin1- or Noggin-removed condition, each factor was removed from the medium for 2–3 days before harvest. Otherwise, the organoids were maintained in the full medium. To harvest organoids and organoid lysate, we first dissolved Matrigel using the Cell Recovery Solution (Corning) and lysed organoids using the UltraRIPA Kit for Lipid Raft (BioDynamics Laboratory). Organoids were first lysed in 80 μl of A buffer (RIPA buffer) supplemented with Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (100×; Thermo Fisher Scientific) via 10 rounds of 30-second on/off sonication cycles using the Bioruptor II sonicator (Sonicbio). After centrifuging, the supernatant was collected and the remnant was further lysed in 40 μl of buffer B (UltraRIPA buffer) by a 10-min sonication. A buffer and B buffer supernatants were combined and used for analysis. Capillary immunoassay was performed on a Jess Simple Western Blot System (ProteinSimple) using the primary antibodies listed in Supplementary Table 7 with a 1:50 dilution and anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibody (ProteinSimple). The assays were run and analyzed using Compass software (version 6.3.0), and the intensity area was used as a surrogate for protein abundance.

Measurement of HiBiT-tagged RNF43

The abundance of RNF43HiBiT protein was detected using the HiBiT system (Promega). Organoids transduced with XLone-RNF43HiBiT were plated on 96-well plates (Corning) with a density of 1,000 cells per well and cultured under full conditions for 5 days. The medium was then replaced with the medium lacking Wnt-3A and supplemented with 5 μM CHIR99021 (Cayman Chemical) and 50 ng ml−1 doxycycline hydrochloride (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). R-spondin1 was also removed in the R-spondin-lacking condition. The organoids were cultured for an additional 2 days, and the medium was refreshed 6 h before the assay. To measure total and cell-surface RNF43HiBiT, we used Nano-Glo HiBiT Lytic and Extracellular Detection Systems (Promega), respectively. Specifically, we added 100 μl per well of Nano-Glo HiBiT Lytic or Extracellular Buffers supplemented with LgBiT protein and substrate according to the manufacturer’s instructions and measured luciferase luminescence on the GloMax plate reader. The background luminescence measured using Dox-untreated organoids was largely constant regardless of the cell number, and the mean background luminescence from three experiments (lytic: 464, extracellular: 4,421) was subtracted from the measured luminescence values. To normalize HiBiT luminescence based on cell number and viability, we measured the ATP abundance of organoids cultured in the same conditions in parallel using the CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay.
