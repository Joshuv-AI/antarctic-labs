Association of endogenous hypercortisolism with hepatic hemangiomas in cushing’s disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Lanlan Zhao, Jiao Liu, Na Li, Ying Wei, Pin Wang, Jie Li, Songyun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe association between persistent endogenous hypercortisolism and hepatic hemangioma (HH) remains unclear. This study compared the prevalence of HH between patients with Cushing’s disease (CD) and silent corticotroph adenomas (SCA) and explored factors associated with HH in CD patients.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 96 CD patients and 219 SCA patients treated between May 2015 and December 2025. Demographic, laboratory, and imaging data were collected. Propensity score matching (PSM) balanced baseline characteristics. HH was diagnosed by contrast-enhanced abdominal CT. Multivariate analyses, including logistic regression, LASSO regression, and Firth-corrected likelihood regression, were performed. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsAfter PSM, HH prevalence was higher in CD patients than in SCA patients (20.0% vs. 9.5%, P = 0.024). Among CD patients, elevated midnight cortisol (OR = 1.402, 95% CI: 1.078–2.031, P = 0.010) and increased 24-hour urinary free cortisol (OR = 1.013, 95% CI: 1.006–1.023, P < 0.001) were independently associated with HH. The predictive model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC = 0.982, 95% CI: 0.961–1.000) and acceptable calibration, with potential clinical utility supported by DCA.ConclusionHH was more prevalent in patients with Cushing’s disease than in those with silent corticotroph adenomas. Elevated cortisol levels may be an important factor associated with HH. This study offers a predictive tool to support early risk assessment and clinical management.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02333-8.

Introduction

Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-producing pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNET) are a subtype of neuroendocrine tumors originating from ACTH-producing cells in the anterior pituitary gland [1]. Approximately 80% of ACTH-PitNETs lead to Cushing’s disease (CD), a condition characterized by cortisol excess due to excessive ACTH secretion. In the majority of cases, this manifests as persistently elevated cortisol levels. Clinical manifestations include moon face, central obesity, facial plethora, wide purple striae (striae rubrae), proximal myopathy, secondary diabetes, and osteoporosis, and are often accompanied by an increased cardiovascular risk and mortality [2]. The remaining 20% of ACTH-PitNETs are clinically nonfunctioning or silent, with patients showing neither hypercortisolemia nor related symptoms [2]. Studies have confirmed that prolonged hypercortisolism in CD patients can induce widespread vascular injury by impairing vascular endothelial function [3–5], thereby contributing to complications such as hypertension, atherosclerosis, and arteriovenous thrombosis.

Hepatic hemangioma (HH) is the most common benign liver tumor, with a prevalence of 0.4%–7.3% in the general population and a higher incidence in females [6]. Most patients are asymptomatic, and the condition is often incidentally detected during imaging examinations. When the tumor diameter exceeds 5 cm, compressive symptoms such as nausea and abdominal pain may occur. Giant HHs (with a diameter > 10 cm) carry a risk of rupture, which can lead to life-threatening hemorrhage [7, 8]. Currently, the relationship between cortisol and HH remains unclear, with existing evidence primarily derived from case reports involving exogenous cortisol administration [9]. For example, Takahashi et al. reported a case in which the volume of a HH doubled after long-term prednisone use (20 mg/day for 12 months) in a patient with myasthenia gravis [10]. Aiura et al. retrospectively analyzed three cases of spontaneous rupture of HH following long-term glucocorticoid therapy (median duration 18 months), suggesting that exogenous glucocorticoids may promote HH progression through proangiogenic mechanisms [11]. However, the correlation between endogenous cortisol levels and HH has not been systematically investigated.

Based on the aforementioned context, this study conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data from patients with CD to systematically evaluate the prevalence of HH in this population and to further explore its associated risk factors. The findings aim to provide an evidence-based foundation for the clinical management and early intervention in CD patients with HH, as well as to offer new insights into the prevention and treatment of CD-related vascular complications.

Comparison of HH prevalence between groups: To control for confounding bias and ensure comparability of baseline characteristics between the CD group and the SCA group, this study employed propensity score matching (PSM) to pair the two groups in a 1:2 ratio. Study population and grouping

A total of 315 patients with ACTH-PitNETs were included in this study. All patients were diagnosed through a standardized protocol in the Department of Endocrinology at the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University between May 2015 and December 2025. The diagnosis was confirmed via a systematic evaluation comprising pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), endocrine function assessment, and postoperative pathological verification. Based on postoperative pathology and clinical manifestations, the patients were classified into a CD group (n = 96) and an SCA group (n = 219).

Diagnostic Criteria for CD

This study adopted a three-tier diagnostic framework based on the Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Management of CD (2025 Edition) and relevant guidelines from the International Pituitary Society [12, 13], as outlined below:

Screening for Cushing syndrome (CS): Patients were required to present with typical clinical manifestations of CS and meet at least two of the following biochemical criteria: (a) significantly elevated midnight serum cortisol (≥ 2 measurements), with a cut-off value of > 1.8 µg/dL (collected during sleep); (b) significantly elevated 24-hour urinary free cortisol (24hUFC) (≥ 2 measurements), defined as a value exceeding the upper limit of the normal range (normal range in our hospital: 75–520 µg/24 h); (c) failure of the 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test (1 mg-DST) to achieve normal suppression, defined as a morning serum cortisol (8:00 COR) level > 1.8 µg/dL after dexamethasone administration.

②Evidence supporting a diagnosis of Cushing disease includes plasma ACTH levels that are above the reference range (> 20pg/ml) or not suppressed in the context of hypercortisolism, identification of a pituitary adenoma on sellar MRI, a positive high-dose dexamethasone suppression test (HD-DST)(2 mg every 6 h for 48 h), defined as a reduction of ≥ 50% in serum cortisol levels from baseline following administration of HD-DST, and an inferior petrosal sinus to peripheral ACTH ratio of ≥ 2 at baseline or ≥ 3 following desmopressin stimulation; non-functioning sellar lesions (e.g., Rathke cleft cysts) were excluded as diagnostic evidence.

③Pathological confirmation was obtained in most cases and was consistent with a PitNET. Immunohistochemistry commonly demonstrated ACTH and/or T-Pit positivity. However, in a minority of cases—particularly those involving very small microadenomas—histological confirmation was inconclusive or not achieved. In these instances, the diagnosis was established based on concordant clinical, biochemical, and inferior petrosal sinus sampling(IPSS) findings, together with postoperative remission.

Diagnostic criteria for SCA

According to the 2017 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of endocrine tumors [14, 15], the diagnosis of SCA required the following three criteria:

①Pathological features: postoperative histopathology confirms a PitNET, with immunohistochemical staining positive for both ACTH and T-Pit; ② Clinical and biochemical features: the patient does not exhibit clinical symptoms or signs of CS, and preoperative hormonal assessments—including 0:00 COR, 24 h UFC, 1 mg-DST, and HD-DST—are all negative, without meeting the diagnostic criteria for CD; ③ Imaging features: Pituitary MRI demonstrates a space-occupying lesion within the sellar region.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following conditions:

① Presence of pituitary crisis, malignant tumors, failure of vital organs, or other life-threatening acute severe conditions; ② History of acute or chronic hepatitis, abdominal trauma, or receipt of estrogen/progestin /exogenous glucocorticoid therapy within the past three months; ③ Diagnosis of Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia or a family history of HH; ④ Current pregnancy or lactation; ⑤ Incomplete clinical data or failure to undergo abdominal contrast-enhanced CT.

Data collection

Clinical data from patients with ACTH-PitNETs were collected, including the following aspects:

(1) Baseline characteristics: sex, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, alcohol consumption history, and presence of hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, fatty liver disease, or HH; (2) Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis function: plasma ACTH level at 8:00 (pg/mL), serum cortisol levels at 8:00 and 0:00 (µg/dL), and 24hUFC (µg/24 h); (3) Pituitary tumor features: tumor size (maximum diameter) and Knosp grade; (4) Laboratory parameters: ① Hematological indices: absolute neutrophil (NE) count, absolute lymphocyte (LY) count, platelet (Plt) count, and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII);②Liver function: aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT); ③Glucose metabolism: fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting insulin (FINS), and homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR); ④Lipid metabolism: total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL); ⑤Sex hormones: estradiol (E2), progesterone (Prog), and testosterone (Testo).

Imaging evaluation and diagnostic criteria for HH

All participants underwent dynamic contrast-enhanced abdominal CT. This imaging modality was uniformly employed as the standard method for HH screening and diagnosis, and was routinely performed for every patient regardless of clinical symptoms. This protocol was adopted to effectively minimize detection bias. The diagnosis of HH was strictly based on its characteristic dynamic enhancement pattern [18]:

Non-contrast phase: ① Well-defined, round or oval hypodense lesion; ② Arterial phase (20–30 s after contrast injection): Prominent nodular enhancement is observed along the lesion periphery, with CT attenuation values similar to those of the aorta at the same level, demonstrating the “rapid inflow” sign; ③ Portal venous phase: The enhanced area expands centripetally, with a decrease in the degree of enhancement; ④ Delayed phase (3–5 min): The lesion shows near-complete or complete fill-in by contrast agent, with density becoming comparable to the surrounding hepatic parenchyma, indicative of the “slow washout” sign.

All imaging data were independently reviewed in a blinded manner by two senior radiologists, who were unaware of the patient groupings and endocrine biochemical results. Any discrepancies in interpretation were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. Furthermore, to accurately analyze the correlation between cortisol levels and imaging characteristics of HH, both abdominal CT examinations and comprehensive endocrine evaluations were completed within 30 days.

Ethical considerations

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No.2023-R094). All procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical standards, and a waiver of informed consent was obtained.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp., USA) and R 5.2.1 software. Measurement data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (±S), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed measurement data were presented as median (interquartile range) [M (Q1, Q3)], and the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for intergroup comparisons. Categorical data were described as number (percentage) [n (%)], and differences between groups were assessed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. To control for baseline confounding factors, PSM was applied in a 1:2 ratio with a caliper value set to 0.02. In the risk factor analysis, Potential influencing factors were first screened by univariate analysis and then entered into a binary logistic regression model. Prior to multivariable modeling, multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance (Tol), with VIF > 5 or Tol < 0.2 indicating significant collinearity. When collinearity was present, only one representative variable was retained to ensure model stability. Variable selection was conducted using LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation, and Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression was used to address small-sample bias. Internal validation was performed using 5000 bootstrap resamples, and model performance was assessed by ROC curves and AUC with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals. A nomogram was constructed to visually demonstrate the contribution of each predictor to the risk of HH occurrence. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curves were utilized to assess the clinical applicability and predictive accuracy of the model. The LASSO regression analysis was implemented using the glmnet package in R, the Firth penalized likelihood regression was performed with the logistf package, and the nomogram was developed using the rms package. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Analysis of HH prevalence in CD patients

A total of 96 patients with CD and 219 patients with SCA were included in this study. Baseline analysis revealed statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of age (40.50 years vs. 46.00 years, P < 0.001), BMI (26.58 vs. 25.91, P = 0.001), prevalence of hypertension (49.0% vs. 27.9%, P < 0.001), and prevalence of diabetes (27.1% vs. 11.9%, P = 0.001). To control for confounding bias, PSM was performed at a 1:2 ratio. This successfully matched 85 CD patients with 147 SCA patients. After matching, no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed between the groups in demographic indicators (e.g., sex, age, BMI) or clinical characteristics (e.g., history of smoking, history of alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and fatty liver disease). Notably, the prevalence of HH was significantly higher in the CD group than in the SCA group both before matching (18.8% vs. 7.3%, P = 0.003) and after matching (20.0% vs. 9.5%, P = 0.024), as detailed in Table 1.

Analysis of risk factors for HH in patients with CD

To investigate the risk factors for HH in patients with CD, a total of 96 CD patients were retrospectively enrolled and categorized into two groups based on the presence of HH: the CD-HH group (n = 18) and the CD-NHH group (n = 78). Univariate analysis revealed that compared to the CD-NHH group, patients in the CD-HH group had a significantly longer disease duration (3.00 years vs. 2.63 years, P = 0.017). Furthermore, the CD-HH group exhibited markedly elevated levels of several parameters: NE (5.55 vs. 5.00, P < 0.01), SII (752.64 vs. 596.40, P = 0.010), FBG (6.40 vs. 5.55, P = 0.047), FINS (20.17 vs. 18.22, P = 0.035), HOMA-IR (5.59 vs. 4.36, P = 0.018), TC (6.03 ± 1.33 vs. 5.31 ± 1.15, P = 0.022), and LDL (3.60 vs. 2.78, P = 0.023). Additionally, cortisol levels at 8:00 COR (47.99 vs. 29.88, P < 0.001) and at 0:00 COR (26.48 vs. 19.40, P < 0.001), as well as 24hUFC levels (931.69 vs. 507.06, P < 0.001), were significantly higher in the CD-HH group than in the CD-NHH group (see Table 2).

Discussion

HH, the most common benign tumor of the liver, generally follows a benign and slowly progressive course; however, its potential clinical risks should not be overlooked. When the tumor grows large or is located in a critical area, it may lead to serious complications such as compressive symptoms, spontaneous rupture and hemorrhage, or even coagulopathy, posing life-threatening risks to patients [8]. Therefore, early identification and regular monitoring of high-risk individuals are of significant clinical importance. Through clinical data analysis, this study provides preliminary evidence linking endogenous hypercortisolism to an increased risk of HH, and further identifies the central role of 0:00 COR and 24 h UFC in this association. These findings not only offer a basis for risk stratification and early screening of HH in patients with CD, but also help deepen our understanding of the potential role of glucocorticoids in vascular biology, thereby expanding our insights into the multisystem pathological mechanisms involved in CD.

The results of this study indicate that the prevalence of HH is significantly higher in patients with CD compared to those with SCA. To enhance the reliability of the conclusions, this study focused on controlling potential confounding factors in the selection of controls and the statistical design. Both SCA and CD belong to ACTH-PitNET, sharing the same tumor origin and pathological basis; however, they differ in whether they cause endogenous hypercortisolism [19, 20]. Therefore, selecting SCA as the control group allows for a more effective isolation and observation of the independent effect of hypercortisolism itself on the risk of developing HH, while controlling for the common background of “ACTH-PitNET.” The findings suggest that cortisol itself, rather than ACTH or the mass effect of the pituitary tumor, may be a critical factor in the development of HH. To further mitigate the influence of baseline characteristics such as age, BMI, and hypertension, this study employed PSM to balance these potential confounders. After matching, the prevalence of HH remained significantly higher in the CD group than in the SCA group, providing stronger evidence that endogenous hypercortisolism is associated with an increased risk of HH. While existing literature has primarily focused on the potential of exogenous glucocorticoids (e.g., prednisone) to promote HH growth or increase the risk of rupture, this case-control analysis confirms that chronically elevated endogenous cortisol is also significantly associated with a higher prevalence of HH. This finding extends our understanding of the relationship between glucocorticoids and vascular pathology. It suggests that excessive cortisol, regardless of its source, may disrupt vascular homeostasis, thereby increasing the risk of vascular aneurysm formation.

Risk factor analysis in CD patients revealed that, in univariate analysis, multiple metabolic indices (e.g., FBG, HOMA-IR, TC, LDL) and inflammatory markers (NE, SII) were significantly elevated in the group with HH. This initially suggested a potential association between HH risk and cortisol-induced systemic metabolic dysfunction alongside a low-grade inflammatory state. However, it is important to note that the validity of SII as an inflammatory marker in the setting of hypercortisolism may be confounded. Excess cortisol is known to directly induce neutrophilia and lymphopenia—common hematologic alterations in Cushing’s disease—and therefore, an elevated SII in CD patients may partly reflect direct glucocorticoid effects on leukocyte distribution rather than genuine tissue inflammation. After further adjustment via LASSO regression and Firth logistic regression, only 0:00 COR and 24 h UFC retained independent predictive value for HH, while the associations with metabolic and inflammatory indices were no longer significant. These results indicate that metabolic disturbances and inflammatory responses are more likely to be secondary effects of hypercortisolism [21–23], rather than independent drivers of HH risk. The core pathological mechanism underlying the increased HH risk is, therefore, likely the sustained excessive exposure to cortisol itself. Furthermore, this study identified 0:00 COR and 24 h UFC as independent risk factors for HH in CD patients, whereas 8:00 COR showed no significant association. This discrepancy is closely linked to the pathophysiological significance of these markers in reflecting cortisol secretory status. 0:00 COR is a sensitive indicator for the loss of the cortisol diurnal rhythm, reflecting a state of sustained cortisol exposure. It represents one of the earliest and most specific biochemical abnormalities in CS [13]. When sampled under standardized in-patient conditions, it is less susceptible to interference and demonstrates high diagnostic specificity. Consequently, it serves as a core laboratory parameter for assessing the severity of endogenous hypercortisolism and is among the most direct indicators for confirming cortisol excess [24]. In contrast, 24 h UFC integrates the diurnal fluctuations in cortisol secretion, providing a stable measure of the body’s cumulative cortisol burden. This assay directly quantifies bioactive free cortisol, independent of variations in cortisol-binding globulin levels [25, 26], making it a first-line screening tool for CS. Together, these two markers accurately capture the core features of endogenous hypercortisolism.Conversely, although 8:00 COR showed a statistical difference in univariate analysis, it did not retain independent predictive value in the logistic regression model. This is primarily because cortisol levels at this time coincide with the physiological morning peak and are easily confounded by various stress factors, making it difficult to reliably distinguish between physiological fluctuations and pathological elevation [27]. As a single measurement, it does not consistently represent a sustained pathological state and therefore has limited diagnostic accuracy [28].

This study has several limitations. First, due to the rarity of CD, the overall sample size was limited. The number of cases in the subgroup with comorbid HH was particularly small, which may have resulted in insufficient statistical power. Second, this was a single-center, retrospective study. Although PSM was employed to balance baseline differences between groups and a standardized imaging diagnostic protocol for HH was implemented, the findings may still be susceptible to selection and information biases. Consequently, estimates of the true prevalence of HH in CD patients should be interpreted with caution and warrant validation by future multicenter, large-scale prospective cohorts. Third, the cross-sectional design of this study, while revealing an association between CD and HH, cannot establish a causal relationship between the two. Fourth, although the prediction model developed here demonstrated good discriminatory performance and techniques like LASSO regression and Firth’s penalized regression were used to mitigate overfitting and small-sample bias, the limited sample size of CD patients with HH implies a residual risk of overfitting. Therefore, the external validity of the model requires further verification. Forth, the retrospective design resulted in incomplete documentation of detailed pituitary function assessments (e.g., thyroid and somatotrophic axes) and non-glucocorticoid hormone replacement therapy in a subset of patients, especially those enrolled in earlier years. Although we excluded patients receiving exogenous glucocorticoids or estrogen/progestin therapy, we cannot rule out residual confounding from other hormonal axes. Future prospective studies with standardized endocrine evaluations are warranted to address this gap.

This study holds significant implications for both clinical practice and scientific research. From a clinical perspective, the findings suggest that for patients with CD, particularly those with markedly elevated 0:00 COR or 24 h UFC levels, incorporating abdominal imaging (e.g., ultrasound or CT) into routine follow-up protocols may be warranted to facilitate the early detection and monitoring of HH. Additionally, the observed association between hypercortisolism and HH raises the hypothesis that effective control of cortisol excess—whether through surgery or medical therapy—might potentially reduce the risk of HH development or progression, although this remains to be tested in future prospective studies. On the research front, this study elucidates the association between endogenous hypercortisolism and HH, providing a clear direction for future investigations into the specific mechanisms by which glucocorticoids contribute to pathological angiogenesis or vascular remodeling.

In conclusion, this retrospective study confirms a significant association between CD and HH, with a significantly higher prevalence of HH observed in CD patients compared to SCA patients. Endogenous hypercortisolism, as reflected by 0:00 COR and 24 h UFC, was identified as an independent risk factor for HH in CD patients. Consequently, this hypercortisolism-related factor should be incorporated into future risk stratification and clinical management strategies for HH.
