From Micro Molecular to Functional Levels Through Macro Structures: Mediation of Structural Brain Changes on the Relationship Between Oxidative Stress and Cognition in Early Onset Psychosis
Authors: Mara Villar-Arenzana, Belén Taulero-Escalera, Karina S MacDowell, David Fraguas, Igor Bombin, Josefina Castro-Fornieles, Carmen Moreno, Inmaculada Baeza, Elena de la Serna, Ana González-Pinto, Mara Parellada, Beatriz Payá, Montserrat Graell, Christos Pantelis, Marta Rapado-Castro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious findings showed decreased frontal left gray matter (FLGM) volume predicted working memory impairments over 2 years after first-episode psychosis (FEP) in adolescents, as a function of age. However, the interplay between brain changes and cognitive development is not well understood. Oxidative stress (STOX), specifically lower baseline glutathione (GSH) levels, has been linked to greater FLGM volume loss and cognitive impairments in this same sample group. This study further investigated the relationship between baseline GSH levels, longitudinal brain changes, and cognitive impairments in FEP compared to healthy controls. We hypothesized that lower baseline GSH levels and accelerated FLGM volume loss underlie working memory deficits over time in adolescent FEP.MethodsGSH levels were measured in peripheral blood at baseline. Regional FLGM volume was obtained using automated methods based on Talairach’s. Mediation analyses were conducted using regression and bootstrapping techniques.ResultsWe confirmed the association between lower GSH levels at the time of FEP and FLGM volume loss, and between decreased FLGM volume and decreased working memory over the first 2 years after onset. STOX had an indirect effect on impaired working memory in adolescent psychosis, where decreased FLGM was a mediator. No association was found in controls.ConclusionsLower baseline GSH levels negatively impacted FLGM volume, contributing to working memory impairments in adolescent psychosis. Biomolecular and macrostructural alterations (lower GSH, FLGM atrophy) may underlie functional cognitive outcomes like working memory deficits longitudinally. These findings may elucidate how STOX relates to neuroanatomical changes that disrupt cognitive trajectories during adolescent brain maturation in FEP.Keywords: oxidative stress, glutathione, brain volume, gray matter, cognition, working memory, first episode of psychosis, adolescence, magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

There is broad consensus that cognitive impairments constitute a core symptomatic dimension of psychotic disorders. Previous research has described that the development of high-order cognitive functions starts in late childhood and progresses substantially throughout adolescence.1–3 Early onset psychosis (EOP), defined by the appearance of the first psychotic symptoms before age 18, emerges in this crucial neurodevelopmental period. The earlier cognitive symptoms appear, the poorer the prognosis and quality of life for affected youths.4–7 Further, current pharmacological treatments do not adequately address these cognitive deficits, largely because the underlying biological mechanisms of such cognitive symptoms are still unknown, highlighting the need for research in this area.

Our clinical studies have demonstrated an altered development of higher-order cognition, particularly working memory,8 associated with progressive gray matter (GM) volume loss in adolescents with EOP over the first 2 years following psychosis onset.9–14 Previous findings revealed that frontal and parietal lobe GM volume loss was associated with poorer cognitive performance in adolescents with a first episode of psychosis (FEP). Moreover, frontal left GM (FLGM) volume loss was the best predictor of age-related working memory impairment.14 Working memory comprises a set of superior cognitive abilities that reach their optimal maturation between 15 and 19 years,1 but this developmental trajectory is disrupted in EOP.15 However, there is still a limited understanding of how FLGM loss and cognitive development interact.

Oxidative stress (STOX), resulting from an imbalance between oxidant molecules and antioxidant defense, has been proposed as a biological mechanism contributing to neurodevelopmental conditions including psychosis spectrum disorders.16–27 Multifactorial causes may increase oxidative burden in both adult-onset psychosis28 and EOP.29–31 Among the key antioxidant molecules in the brain, glutathione (GSH) plays a crucial role as the primary free radical scavenger.25 Previous studies have reported reduced GSH levels at the time of FEP in adolescents with EOP compared to healthy controls, contributing to a defective brain antioxidant protection associated with both brain volume loss and cognitive impairment in subjects with psychosis.29

Previous studies carried out in young adults that examined the relationship between GSH levels in peripheral blood and brain volume have found interactions between a reduction of GM volume in the frontal left inferior, bilateral temporal and parietal cortices and lower GSH levels between individuals with a FEP and control subjects.21 In adolescents with EOP, our group has demonstrated that baseline GSH levels correlate with decreased GM volume in the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes, as well as in total brain volume. Moreover, a greater decrease in FLGM volume was associated with lower baseline GSH levels.32 These findings paralleled those of significant brain changes in this region, which predicted working memory functionality in the same sample.14

Regarding the relationship between STOX, brain changes, and cognitive symptoms in psychosis, STOX-induced neuronal death in the prefrontal and anterior frontal regions has been associated with regional brain volume reductions, leading to impairments in executive and cognitive functioning in schizophrenia.33 Previous research has shown that the presence of STOX correlates with poorer cognition, affecting working memory, processing speed, attention, visual learning, reasoning, problem-solving, and social cognition in schizophrenia.34,35 Our research in adolescents with EOP similarly demonstrated a relationship between cognitive performance and antioxidant status.26,36 Specifically, total antioxidant status was positively associated with attention, memory, working memory, executive function, and global cognition at baseline and at 2-year follow-up.26,27,36–38

Therefore, decreased GSH levels at FEP could be a plausible underlying biological mechanism that may lead to brain volume loss, which may in turn be mediating the relationship between GSH and cognitive performance in adolescents with EOP. Thus, this study investigates whether FLGM volume loss over the first 2 years after psychosis onset mediates the relationship between lower baseline GSH level and decreased working memory performance over time in adolescents with FEP and matched controls. We hypothesized that lower baseline GSH levels and FLGM volume loss may underlie working memory deficits over time in adolescents with FEP.

Methods

A detailed and comprehensive description of the naturalistic, prospective, case–control, multicenter longitudinal Child and Adolescent First Episode of Psychosis Study (CAFEPS) in Spain from which the study sample is derived has been provided elsewhere.39

Participants

An initial sample of 110 young adolescents (before the age of 18) with FEP and 98 controls, matched for age, sex, and education, was consecutively recruited in the out- and inpatient units of 6 hospitals in Spain. The recruitment took place according to the following inclusion and exclusion criteria: Participants were included if they were aged between 7 and 17 years old; and they and/or their legal guardians provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria consisted of a concomitant Axis I disorder, DSM-IV mental retardation, including not only an intellectual quotient (IQ) below 70 but also impaired functioning, presence of any neurologic or pervasive developmental disorder, substance abuse, pregnancy or history of head trauma with loss of consciousness. Absence (for controls) and/or presence (for participants with FEP) of a >6-month history of positive psychotic symptoms was an inclusion criterion. Substance use was not an exclusion criterion if positive symptoms persisted 14 days after a negative routine urine drug test at first admission to the clinical centers. Matched controls were recruited from the same sociodemographic areas through dissemination by the hospital staff, word-of-mouth, and advertisements. The study was approved by all the corresponding Institutional Review Boards at each clinical center, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients and/or their legal guardians. All participants met magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) safety criteria.

Since the aim of the present study was to further expand the study of brain and cognitive changes in the EOP population by exploring the role of STOX (baseline GSH levels), only those adolescents with psychosis who completed both cognitive and neuroimaging baseline and 2-year follow-up assessments and had valid baseline GSH measures were included in the current analyses. Therefore, a final subsample of 31 adolescents with FEP (mean age 16.00; range 13-17) and 39 controls (mean age 15.69; range 14-17) was considered from the original CAFEPS sample (see Figure 1). There were no differences in age, race, gender, or clinical or functional characteristics between those participants who completed and those who did not complete the corresponding assessments (i.e., had either no valid baseline GSH, and baseline and/or follow-up MRI and/or cognitive data—see Tables S1 and S2).

Clinical Measures

Diagnosis or its absence was established at baseline and reviewed at the 2-year follow-up according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition criteria using the Spanish version of the semi-structured interview Kiddie-Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia, Present and Lifetime version.40,41 Parents or legal guardians and adolescents were interviewed separately by psychiatrists trained in the use of the instrument and in the assessment of children and adolescents. Consensus diagnoses were made in those cases where th presence or absence of psychiatric diagnoses was in doubt. A provisional diagnosis was made at the first contact with the participants with FEP, and this was reviewed at the 2-year follow-up to determine diagnostic accuracy. For descriptive purposes only, we used the diagnosis established at the 2-year follow-up grouped into 3 main diagnostic categories: Schizophrenia (n = 11), bipolar disorder (n = 11), and other psychosis spectrum disorder (n = 9), which include schizoaffective disorder (n = 2), depression with psychotic features (n = 1), and psychosis not otherwise specified (n = 6). Clinical symptom assessment was conducted through the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS).42,43 Inter-rater reliability for the PANSS was determined using the interclass correlation coefficient, which was always superior to 0.80. The level of general occupational, social, and psychosocial functioning was determined using the Children Global Assessment of Functioning scale.44 Duration of untreated psychosis was calculated by asking EOP subjects/parents at the first interview about the approximate date of the first appearance of positive psychotic symptoms within the first psychotic episode and by reviewing clinical case notes.45 Neurodevelopmental history (normal or pathological acquisition of psychomotor, language, reading, and writing milestones) was also explored by asking the parents at the time of study entry (ie, baseline). Chlorpromazine equivalents were used to derive the dosage of antipsychotic treatment at baseline and follow-up, and to calculate the cumulative doses taken during the scan interval.46,47

Neuropsychological Assessments

Cognitive function was assessed by trained psychologists at baseline and at 2-year follow-up using a comprehensive neuropsychological battery.48,49 Further details of these neuropsychological measures and complete cognitive assessments are described elsewhere.8,14 Based on our previous findings14 regarding the presence of a significant association between working memory impairments specifically and structural brain changes in the FLGM volume in adolescents with FEP, only working memory function was examined in the present study. Working memory was assessed using the digits backwards and letter-number sequencing subtests from the Weschler Intelligence Scale.50 Raw test scores were then converted to z scores based on the performance of the matched control group at baseline.8,51 A summary score for this cognitive domain at both assessment times and a measure of change at follow-up (2-year follow-up minus baseline) were calculated based on z scores. These z scores were truncated at ±4 to avoid outlying variables.

Brain Imaging

MRI Acquisition and Processing

An anatomical brain MRI was obtained at baseline and at the 2-year follow-up visit (mean [SD], 25.7 [2.7] months) with the same scanner for each subject, across 5 different recruitment centers (all provided with 1.5-T scanners; intraclass correlation coefficients ranged from 0.96 to 0.99 for lobar GM measurements52). Data were collected from each clinical center and processed only at one site. Sequences acquired were a T1-weighted, 3-dimensional gradient echo (voxel size, 1 × 1 × 1.5 mm) and a T2-weighted turbo spin echo (voxel size, 1 × 1 × 3.5 mm), all in the axial plane. Full details about the acquisition parameters at each site, comparability among the machines for this study, and the limitations involved in the analysis of these multicenter data can be found elsewhere.52 FLGM volume was obtained using the same automated method based on the Talairach atlas,53 previously used in the former reports from this particular same EOP cohort.11,12,14,54,55 Processed images were visually inspected and manually corrected by a single blind operator. Brain images from a sample of 5 randomly selected participants were used to test the consistency of the rater prior to conducting the whole segmentation procedure on the images of the study sample.52

Segmentation

In accordance with our previously published papers derived from the CAFEPS sample and in order to be able to confirm and to further explore previous findings,11,12,14,54,55 lobar volume measurements were obtained using a semi-automated brain segmentation method based on the Talairach proportional grid system, which includes a grid template in which all sectors are assigned to particular regions.56,57 A 2-step procedure was followed58: (1) An initial segmentation of cerebral tissues into GM, white matter, and CSF was obtained using Statistical Parametric Mapping 2 routines for multimodal (T1 & T2) segmentation. (2) The Talairach grid was built on each edited brain MRI by manually selecting the position of the anterior and posterior commissures, and establishing a third point position in the mid-sagittal plane. Volumes for each tissue type in Talairach space were measured on the MRI registered to Talairach space, by summing up the data from the Talairach grid cells associated with each Region of Interest (ROI).59 The validity of this procedure has already been proven suitable for volumetric studies56,59 in our own longitudinal11,12,14 and other multicenter studies.52,60,61

In our previous studies,14 the left frontal region was the brain region that showed the greater brain volume change/decrease over time and was the best predictor of working memory function impairment in adolescents with FEP.8,11,14 This was also the brain region associated with lower baseline GSH levels in this same sample of adolescent individuals with psychosis,32 highlighting the importance of further examining these particular relationships in this sample. Thus, based on our own previous findings, the left frontal region was the only ROI selected for the current analysis. Further details of these brain volume measures and assessments are described elsewhere.11,14 The data were exported to SPSS, version 25.0 (Chicago, Illinois) for statistical analyses.

The longitudinal change in brain volume was measured as the difference between follow-up (vol2) and the initial volume at baseline (vol 1) of the ROI, and is described as a percentage calculated as follows11,12,14:

Longitudinal brain volume change = [(vol2-vol1)/vol1] ×100.

Oxidative Stress Evaluation

As GSH plays a key role in mitigating STOX, the antioxidant defense capacity was assessed by measuring baseline GSH levels in peripheral blood.

In order not to alter GSH levels, participants were asked not to do any heavy exercise or fasting during the week before study entry. Blood samples (10 mL) were taken from each patient at admission (at approximately 8:30 AM) by venipuncture into heparinized tubes. Following centrifugation, erythrocytes and plasma were separated and stored frozen at −80 °C until analysis. To prepare erythrocyte hemolysates, 500 μL of erythrocytes were washed with 2 mL of cold 0.9% NaCl and centrifuged at 2500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C (3 times). The erythrocyte pellet was re-suspended in 4 volumes of ice-cold de-ionized water and kept on ice for 10 min before assay. All samples were analyzed at the same time. Total GSH levels were determined by standardized spectrophotometric assays of erythrocyte hemolysates (Bioxytech GSH-420 assay kit [OXIS International Inc.]). The assay is based on the formation of a chromophoric thione, with the absorbance measured at 420 nm which is directly proportional to the GSH concentration using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Thermo Max®).

Statistical Analyses

Normal distribution of variables was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk. Means and SD were used to describe continuous variables. Frequencies were used to describe discrete variables. Differences between EOP individuals and controls in sociodemographic data were assessed by means of Student’s t-test, Fisher’s test, and chi-square tests, according to the type of variable.

Mediation analyses were performed to assess whether FLGM volume change over time mediated the relationship between STOX (baseline GSH) at the time of FEP/study entry and working memory impairment over time in our sample.11,14,32 These analyses were performed using a macro developed by Hayes and Preacher.62,63

Mediation analysis allows for the examination of indirect pathways of influence from which X (STOX/baseline GSH levels) carries its effect on Y (working memory performance). Mediation is indicated if the independent variable (X: STOX/GSH levels at baseline) significantly predicts the mediator (M: left frontal brain volume; a path), the mediator significantly predicts the dependent variable (Y: working memory performance) controlling for the independent variable (the b path), and if the cross product of a and b is significant. The a*b cross product tests the statistical significance of the difference between the total effect of X on Y (c path) and the direct effect (c′ path), which is the impact of X on Y adjusting for the effect of the mediator (M). A bootstrapping method was applied to test the significance of the mediation effect.62,63 This method generates bias-corrected and accelerated confidence intervals.63 Significant mediation is indicated if the confidence intervals do not contain zero. For all analyses, 10 000 resamples were taken and 95% CIs were used.63 Secondary mediation analyses were conducted to explore the potential effects of confounding variables. These variables were selected based on previously reported associations with longitudinal brain volume changes, working memory function, or STOX (ie, age, months of inter-scan interval, interscan ICV change, gender, baseline smoking status, psychopathology, antipsychotic medication, or length of illness prior to baseline assessment).14,32,36 The potential confounding variables that demonstrated significant associations with the primary variables of interest were entered as covariates, and mediation analyses were re-run. All these variables were added to the final mediation model, and we removed those not significant or scientifically sound to build the final model, which included age64–66 and smoking status67–71 as covariates. Pearson’s correlation analyses were used to rule out potential effects of antipsychotic medication, change in symptomatology, and cannabis use (measured by urine toxicology test at baseline and 2-year follow-up) on GSH levels, FLGM changes, and working memory performance.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS v25 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, IBM Corporation). Statistical significance was set at α ≤.05.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics

A subsample of 31 individuals with a first episode of EOP (mean age 16.00; range 13-17) and 39 matched controls (mean age 15.69; range 14-17 at baseline) from the CAFEPS sample were included for this study purpose. Individuals with EOP were not significantly different from controls in terms of sex, years of education, developmental history, race, or handedness (see Table 1). There were significant differences between the groups for estimated IQ at baseline8,51 and for months of inter-scan interval in our sample, consistent with previous reports on this sample14 (see Table 1). For individuals with EOP, the mean duration of the illness, defined as the time between the appearance of their first positive symptoms and their baseline, was included in the current analyses. Months of inter-scan interval in our EOP sample were 24.45 ± 2.13, and the duration of antipsychotic treatment was 19.90 ± 6.39 weeks (mean daily dose 274.93 ± 149.3 mg in chlorpromazine equivalents at baseline, see Table 1).

Relationship Between GSH Baseline Levels, Brain Volume Changes, and Working Memory Performance Over Time

Mediation analysis was used to test whether brain changes, specifically decreased FLGM volume, would act as a mediator for the relationship between baseline GSH levels and working memory performance over time in adolescent psychosis and controls separately. Possible confounding variables were checked (ie, Age, months of inter-scan interval, interscan Intra Craneal Volume (ICV) change, gender, baseline smoking status, psychopathology, antipsychotic medication, or length of illness prior to baseline assessment). A final mediation model was re-run including age and baseline smoking status as covariates. In this model, FLGM volume changes over time in EOP participants displayed a significant positive path coefficient for path a (a = 0.015; t = 2.093; P = .046; β = .387) and significant effect for path b (b = 0.089; t = 2.081; P = .048; β = .389). In addition, there were non-significant total and direct effects of X (baseline GSH levels) on Y (working memory performance) (c = 0.002; t = 1.329; P = .195; c′ = 0.0008; t = 0.502; P = .620; β = .094). Partial effects of control variables on working memory were significant for age (Coeff = 0.042, P < .01; β = .474) and non-significant for smoking status (Coeff = 0.336, P = .498; β = .130). The mediation effect (a*b path) of FLGM volume changes was significant (bias corrected 95% CI, 0.0001-0.0049) (see Figure 2). The model explained 24% of the variance of working memory performance over the first 2 years after FEP. No associations were found between any of the measures of GSH, brain GM volume change, and working memory changes over time in the control group (see Figure 3). These results were independent of antipsychotic medication, changes in symptomatology, and cannabis use (see Table S3).

Discussion

This is the first longitudinal study to examine the relationship between biomolecular (lower baseline GSH), structural brain alterations (decreased FLGM volume), and functional cognitive (working memory) outcomes during adolescent brain maturation in psychosis. Our findings demonstrate an indirect relationship between lower baseline GSH and working memory impairment mediated by brain volume changes.

Our analysis confirmed previously established associations between lower baseline GSH and FLGM brain volume loss 2 years later in first-episode EOP,32 and between reduced FLGM volume and age-related working memory impairments during the same period.14 In accordance with our hypothesis, a GSH deficit at FEP indirectly affects decreased working memory performance via FLGM volume loss, which acted as a significant mediator. These associations were absent in controls, suggesting that oxidative damage may disrupt brain development, which in turn contributes to decreased cognitive maturation in EOP.

Progressive brain volume changes in FEP have been well established.8–12,54,55,60,73–78 These structural brain abnormalities correlate with cognitive dysfunction in adults with psychosis58,79–93 and FEP.13,94,95 Concretely, our previous study in this same adolescent cohort14 identified reduced left frontal GM volume as the best predictive biomarker of age-related working memory decline over the first 2 years after FEP.14,15 The present findings support those results, suggesting that progressive neurobiological changes (ie, brain structure/volume) may lead to functional changes, such as cognitive alterations, in these young individuals.96 However, no biomolecular characterization was made at that point.

In this regard, micro-biomolecular alterations such as the presence of an altered antioxidant defense (STOX) have been consistently associated with both established psychosis97 and FEP.98 Altered GSH metabolism further supports the role of STOX in the pathogenesis and pathophysiology of major psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia.97,99,100 Similarly, adolescents with EOP showed reduced antioxidant capacity,30,101 including lower GSH20 in the prefrontal cortex.102 Studies suggest that over time, these changes might lead to some of the structural brain alterations in schizophrenia,103,104 as STOX-induced neuronal dysfunction is associated with neurodegeneration.105 Moreover, lower GSH and GM volume loss in the frontal left inferior, bilateral temporal, and parietal cortices have previously been reported in FEP.21 Consistent with our previous findings in a larger EOP sample, this study confirmed that lower baseline GSH correlates with frontal brain volume loss over the first 2 years of follow-up, suggesting that increased susceptibility to STOX might contribute to progressive cortical GM loss in FEP during adolescence.32 These results align with previous studies which showed that oxidative micro-biological changes resulted in altered macro-biological brain structures.21,32,106

The presence of an altered STOX has also been previously related to cognition. A previous study by Martínez-Cengotitabengoa et al. found that higher baseline total antioxidant status was associated with better attention, memory, working memory, and global cognition at baseline.26,36 This relationship of total antioxidant status with cognition, except for working memory, persisted up to 2-year follow-up.26,36 Other studies similarly suggested a positive association between total antioxidant status levels and processing speed, attention, verbal learning, and memory,27 and between memory, attention, and executive functions with lower GHS in young adults with a first episode of schizophrenia.35 However, other studies found an inverse association between higher total antioxidant status and cognitive performance, specifically with the attentional domain in adults with schizophrenia.38 Such inconsistencies may reflect methodological differences (total antioxidant status and/or analyses over time), although overall evidence supports that antioxidant defense is related to cognitive impairment in patients with schizophrenia. Aligned with our previous studies, where we found an impaired antioxidant status associated with cognition in adolescent EOP,26,36 our current results demonstrate an indirect relationship specifically between lower baseline GSH and working memory impairment. The absence of a direct effect on the relationship between STOX and cognition may reflect short illness duration (<6 months of FEP), the neurodevelopmental stage (adolescence), or an insufficient sample size to detect a direct effect.

Taken together, these findings support the initial hypothesis that lower baseline GSH precedes structural brain volume loss, which in turn leads to cognitive deterioration, particularly high-order cognitive functions such as working memory that develop during adolescence. Previous research shows that impaired myelination induced by STOX imbalance in frontal regions contributes to brain volume reduction and cognitive impairment in schizophrenia.33 In this regard, our results confirmed previously established associations between lower baseline GSH and later FLGM loss after 2-year follow-up in first-episode EOP32; and between FLGM loss and working memory impairments over this period of time in adolescent psychosis by age.14 Thus, cognitive decline may arise from a disrupted antioxidant capacity that indirectly affects neurodevelopment through structural changes.

These findings advance our understanding of micro-biomolecular, structural, and functional changes in this sensitive neurodevelopmental period (12-17 years), in which STOX might accelerate frontal brain volume changes, which in turn alter age-dependent working memory performance during the first 2 years after the FEP.

Strengths and Limitations

This study represents a unique opportunity to understand potential biomarkers underlying persistent cognitive symptoms in psychosis by looking for specific patterns resulting from plausible neurodevelopmental processes occurring at this sensitive stage of neurodevelopment of adolescence. Strengths include: (1) Short illness duration and antipsychotic treatment at baseline; (2) sample homogeneity in age and education, reducing the confounding effects of sociodemographic factors and maturation processes; (3) a standardized follow-up interval for all participants; (4) investigation during adolescence, a critical neuroplastic period, enabling the study of biological, brain, and cognitive development in EOP.

However, limitations must be highlighted: (1) The relatively small sample which may limit the interpretation of our results; even though we tried to account for the plausible effects of different possible confounding factors by including them as covariates, the small sample size did not allow subgroup analyses by sex or diagnosis. (2) The 2-year follow-up may be insufficient to capture a broader pattern of biological, brain changes, and cognitive development occurring during adolescence. We are currently conducting 10- and 15-year follow-up assessments to clarify long-term biological, structural, and cognitive pathways and their relationships over time. (3) Although symptom severity and antipsychotic effects were controlled statistically, the lack of a medication-free group or the drug test of cannabis use in the control group limits our ability to isolate medication effects. In addition, the lack of specific measures of medication adherence limits the ability to evaluate the potential impact of non-adherence on the observed results. (4) Analyses focused exclusively on baseline GSH, frontal regions, and a broader cognitive domain such as working memory, limiting generalizability to other STOX or biological markers, brain regions, subcortical structures, larger-scale brain changes, or cognitive processes. Also, longitudinal in vivo or central nervous system measures—not only peripheral—might be included in future analyses. Although GSH is commonly used as a marker of antioxidant defense, peripheral levels may be influenced by several factors unrelated to disease processes, including diet and caloric intake,107 metabolic status, lifestyle variables, and medication exposure. Because dietary information was not systematically collected in this study, this factor should be considered when interpreting interindividual variability in GSH levels. (5) Practice effects in neuropsychological testing remain an inherent methodological limitation in longitudinal neuropsychological studies.

Future Directions, Conclusions, and Clinical Implications

Based on the relationships between micromolecular alterations (GSH) and macrostructural changes (brain volume) and between structural and cognitive changes, and the indirect effect of GSH and working memory, implementing effective lifestyle and pharmacological strategies to counteract changes related to STOX imbalance, together with cognitive enhancement treatments, may help mitigate cognitive dysfunction in psychosis.

Timely interventions at psychosis onset, when biological and structural abnormalities and their functional implications are evident, may be crucial to mitigate the biochemical effects on neurodevelopment, particularly in adolescents.

Future research should aim to (1) include larger studies and more diverse longitudinal samples to replicate findings and evaluate antioxidant interventions in brain and cognitive changes, as well as the interaction between other biological, brain functional, genetic, and environmental factors in symptomatology and (2) test novel pharmacological and clinical interventions targeting STOX to improve functional outcomes.

The importance of the present work lies in its novelty and multidisciplinary approach linking cognitive changes with biochemical and brain changes in early episodes of adolescent psychosis. Future studies should assess individual components of higher cognitive functions, specifically working memory performance, and adopt an interdisciplinary, whole-brain, high-precision approach incorporating other oxidative and inflammatory biomarkers, other central neurobiological biomarkers, such as iron, multimodal imaging, other specific cognitive functions, as well as analyses of genetic and functional measures. This aims to clarify the neural underpinnings of cognition in psychosis and the interaction between longitudinal brain changes and cognitive outcomes, in a field that still lacks effective treatments.

To conclude, this is the first study to examine the biomolecular and structural brain alterations (lower baseline GSH and FLGM changes) underlying cognitive impairment in psychosis, particularly working memory decline. Our findings demonstrate that lower baseline GSH negatively impacted frontal cortical volume, leading to a neurodevelopmental “disruption” that resulted in decreased working memory function in adolescent psychosis. In particular, FLGM volume acted as a mediator between baseline STOX and working memory function in adolescents with psychosis. Therefore, it is plausible that this micro-biomolecular lower baseline GSH precedes macro-structural brain volume loss, which in turn leads to the deterioration of higher cognitive functions; something we were able to sustain in our study. This mediation was absent in controls, suggesting that STOX is associated with or inherent to the illness, impacting brain and cognitive development. This aligns with previous studies suggesting that psychosis exerts a “toxic effect” on the brain, leading to volume reduction and consequent working memory impairments.9,108–110 Taken together, these findings may aid to elucidate how STOX relates to neuroanatomical changes that disrupt cognitive trajectories after FEP during adolescent brain maturation.

Further longitudinal research is necessary to fully understand the neurodevelopmental and neurobiological mechanisms underlying brain volume changes and their relationship with cognitive dysfunction in adolescent psychosis.
