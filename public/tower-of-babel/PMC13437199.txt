Implementation of Acupuncture Treatment in the Modern Operating Environment: Our Reply to Study by Zou X et al [Letter]
Authors: Pradipta Bhakta, Habib Md Reazaul Karim, Mohan Mugawar, Brian O'Brien
Published: 2026

Dear editor

In a recent study Zou X et al used cheek acupuncture (CA) at three specific points in patients undergoing thoracic surgery requiring double lumen tube (DLT) intubation.1 They found that, compared to placebo administration, the use of CA reduced the incidence of postoperative sore throat (POST). However, after sensitivity analysis the grades of POST were found to differ significantly only in the first 30 min, and not at other times. We wish to raise some points which may help to interpret and contextualize the study’s findings.

Firstly, although, this was a study on POST, neither tracheal nor bronchial cuff pressures were measured around intubation nor monitored throughout surgery. There exists a recommendation for this strategy to mitigate endotracheal tube induced sore throat.2 It was consequently unclear if cuff pressures were held within recommended limits. A DLT having two cuffs, this could be a major factor in POST,2 as the authors have acknowledged as a limitation.

Secondly, the authors understandably selected acupuncture points to tackle POST and upper cervicothoracic pain after surgery, which was the main aim of this study. However, the most common and effective acupuncture point specific for the reduction of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is the P6 (Nei Guan),3,4 not the cheek one used here. Nonetheless the authors found significant reduction in the incidence of PONV (20.5 vs 43.2%, P=0.022), a finding at odds with a recent meta-analysis which revealed nominal benefit of acupuncture over placebo.3 This is striking as overall opioid consumption also did not differ between the groups.

Finally, the reduction in incidence of POST and a similar benefit in throat pain scores for the first 30 mins, after all adjustment and sensitivity analysis. Compared to a recent meta-analysis, the overall risk reduction still seems a bit higher (0.32 vs 0.51).5 Whether this invasive procedure is justifiable for such a trivial self-limiting and short-lasting complication of DLT insertion seems debatable.

Although the authors deserve credit for this comparative study on cheek acupuncture in thoracic surgery, we feel these points warrant further clarification to interpret their findings.
