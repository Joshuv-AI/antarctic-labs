Provably Contractive and High-Quality Denoisers for Convergent Restoration
Shubhi Shukla, Pravin Nair

Abstract
Image restoration, the recovery of clean images from degraded measurements, has applications in various domains like surveillance, defense, and medical imaging. Despite achieving state-of-the-art (SOTA) restoration performance, existing convolutional and attention-based networks lack stability guarantees under minor shifts in input, exposing a robustness accuracy trade-off. We develop provably contractive (global Lipschitz < 1 <1 ) denoiser networks that considerably reduce this gap. Our design composes proximal layers obtained from unfolding techniques, with Lipschitz-controlled convolutional refinements. By contractivity, our denoiser guarantees that input perturbations of strength ‖ δ ‖ ≤ ε \|\delta\|\leq\varepsilon induce at most ε \varepsilon change at the output, while strong baselines such as DnCNN and Restormer can exhibit larger deviations under the same perturbations. On image denoising, the proposed model is competitive with unconstrained SOTA denoisers, reporting the tightest gap for a provably 1-Lipschitz model and establishing that such gaps are indeed achievable by contractive denoisers. Moreover, the proposed denoisers act as strong regularizers for image restoration that provably effect convergence in Plug-and-Play algorithms. Our results show that enforcing strict Lipschitz control does not inherently degrade output quality, challenging a common assumption in the literature and moving the field toward verifiable and stable vision models. Codes and pretrained models are available at https://github.com/SHUBHI1553/Contractive-Denoisers .

Introduction

Image restoration aims at estimating a clean image from degraded observations produced by sensing and acquisition pipelines. Typical degradations include additive noise (denoising), motion/defocus blur (deblurring), point-spread effects (deconvolution), loss of spatial resolution (superresolution), and missing samples (inpainting/demosaicking)
[
42
]
. Classical methods pose restoration as:

\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\min}\ f(\boldsymbol{x})+g(\boldsymbol{x}),

(1)

where
f
is data-fidelity term derived by forward model and
g
is the prior/regularizer on the estimated image
[
19
]
. For applications like deblurring, superresolution, inpainting, and compressed sensing
[
15
,
26
]
,
f(\boldsymbol{x})={\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert}^{2}
, where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the measurement matrix and
\boldsymbol{y}
the observation. Common choices of regularizer
g(x)
include total variation and related variational models, sparse representations, and patch-based nonlocal priors. The problem in (
1

[
38
,
44
,
13
]
. These methods either assume that the degradation operator
\mathbf{A}
is known (non-blind restoration), or require an additional procedure to estimate
\mathbf{A}
from the data (blind restoration).

With the advent of deep learning, one line of work replaces optimization in (

.

Motivation:

Despite rapid progress in image restoration, robustness is often underemphasized. Real acquisitions inevitably introduce perturbations (sensor noise, optics, sampling pipelines), yet many state-of-the-art models are trained on curated supervised datasets, leaving their stability under realistic degradations unclear. Figures
1
and
2
illustrate this gap: even strong denoisers such as DnCNN and Restormer can be noticeably sensitive to small, common perturbations, including mild JPEG compression and chroma-subsampling, leading to amplified artifacts and degraded denoising quality. By enforcing provable contractivity, we aim to obtain denoisers that remain stable to such perturbations while preserving structure and color fidelity. Even in the case of plug-and-play restoration techniques, training state-of-the-art denoisers that are contractive or 1-Lipschitz (refer to Sec.
3
for definitions) and thereby ensuring algorithmic stability remains challenging. This raises a central question: can we design provably contractive denoisers with similar learning capacity to modern denoisers and, also, achieve convergent and high-quality restoration within PnP algorithms?

Contributions:

We show that provably contractive denoisers can attain competitive accuracy to unconstrained models. To our knowledge, this is the first method to demonstrate this. In this regards, our main contributions are:

•

Contractive architecture.
We construct an image-denoising layer in Sec.
4.3
that is provably contractive, identify its key components (gradient and proximal-wavelet blocks with Lipschitz-controlled convolutions), and compose them to form a contractive network.

•

Theory and certified robustness.
We provide proof of contractivity and compare against
1
-Lipschitz networks, establishing state-of-the-art accuracy among robust methods. We also report competitive performance relative to unconstrained models like DnCNN and Restormer in Sec.
5.1
.

•

PnP convergence.
When used as a prior in Plug-and-Play (refer Sec.
5.2
), our denoiser achieves convergent and state-of-the-art restoration quality.

Related Work

Lipschitz-controlled neural networks are important in applications requiring stability, robustness, and convergence guarantees. In classification, Lipschitz constraints are closely tied to adversarial robustness, certified defenses, and invertible architectures
[
35
,
5
,
47
,
20
]
. In inverse problems, they are particularly useful in plug-and-play and operator-splitting methods, where nonexpansive or
1
-Lipschitz denoisers enable convergence guarantees
[
45
,
37
]
. These motivations have led to a growing body of work on training Lipschitz-constrained networks.

Several works aim to train
1
-Lipschitz networks by constraining layer norms, but mostly without provable end-to-end guarantees. Spectral normalization (SN) constrains the largest singular value of linear and convolutional layers using a few power iterations during training
[
35
,
45
,
5
]
. However, the use of finite power iterations makes SN approximate and hence insufficient to certify an end-to-end
1
-Lipschitz network (see
[
37
]
for a counterexample). Parseval networks
[
12
]
and orthogonality-regularized CNNs
[
49
]
similarly promote orthogonal weights. While exact orthogonality would yield nonexpansive layers, in practice these constraints are typically imposed softly through regularization terms, resulting only in approximate Lipschitz control. More generally, heuristics such as margin-based training and norm penalties can improve stability, but they do not provide network-level guarantees
[
47
,
20
]
.

Provably
1
-Lipschitz models can instead be built by combining exactly normalized linear or convolutional layers with
1
-Lipschitz activations. GroupSort activations were introduced in
[
2
]
to construct end-to-end
1
-Lipschitz networks with improved expressivity, and recent learnable
1
-Lipschitz activation families further enhance approximation power while preserving the guarantee
[
17
]
. Exact frequency-domain normalization can enforce a convolutional layer to be
1
-Lipschitz, although the required singular-value computations incur substantial training-time overhead
[
46
]
. In the context of inverse problems, denoisers based on wavelet-based unrolled splitting have been shown to satisfy
1
-Lipschitz guarantees and thereby ensure plug-and-play convergence
[
37
]
, but they do not yet match the restoration quality of unconstrained state-of-the-art models. However, provably
1
-Lipschitz architectures remain relatively scarce and have been developed primarily for classification
[
2
,
47
,
40
]
. Only a few recent works
[
7
,
37
]
study such constrained models for inverse problems. Overall, while provably
1
-Lipschitz architectures offer strong guarantees, strict enforcement can reduce flexibility or increase cost, and it remains unclear whether they can match unconstrained restoration models.

3
Preliminaries

In this section, we discuss the preliminary results required to explain our architecture. We start with the Lipschitz property of functions. Throughout this work, we use Euclidean norm.

Definition 1
(Lipschitz continuity)

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
\|\cdot\|
denote the Euclidean norm.
We say
f
is Lipschitz continuous if there exists
L\geq 0
such that

\|f(x)-f(y)\|\leq L\,\|x-y\|
, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Such an
L
is called the Lipschitz constant of
f
and
f
is said to be
L
-Lipschitz.
If this constant satisfies
L<1
, then
f
is said to be
contractive
or
L
-contractive
. If
L\leq 1
,
f
is said to be
nonexpansive
.

Recall the inverse problem in (
1
). For a convex regularizer
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, the proximal operator is defined as

\mathrm{Prox}_{g}(\boldsymbol{y})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ \left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\right\rVert^{2}+g(\boldsymbol{x})\right\}.

(2)

The proximal operator is the denoiser induced by the regularizer
g
. Suppose
\boldsymbol{y}
is a noisy observation of a clean image
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
under the additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) model:

\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\eta},\qquad\boldsymbol{\eta}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),

(3)

Assume prior density
p(\boldsymbol{x})\propto\exp(-g(\boldsymbol{x}))
. Then
(
2
) returns maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate of
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
given
\boldsymbol{y}

[
4
]
.

One widely used algorithm to solve the problem in (
1
) is Forward-Backward Splitting (FBS), which alternates between a gradient step on
f
with a proximal step on
g
. In particular, the FBS update is

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{z}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}-\alpha\nabla\!f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Prox}_{\alpha g}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}).

(4)

where
\alpha>0
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
\mathrm{Prox}_{\alpha g}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1})
in (
3
) coincides with the MAP estimate of Gaussian denoising, with
g
acting as the log-prior,
plug-and-play
(PnP) methods, propose to replace
\mathrm{Prox}_{\alpha g}
in algorithms like FBS by a generic denoiser. In particular, the PnP variant of forward-backward splitting (PnP-FBS) performs

\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}=\mathrm{D}\big(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\alpha\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\big),

(5)

where
\mathrm{D}
is a denoising operator. Modern PnP and
regularization by denoising
(RED) methods typically employ powerful learned denoisers as
\mathrm{D}
, enabling near state-of-the-art performance across a range of image restoration tasks.

We recall a result from
[
37
]
that analyzes the FBS update, viewed as an operator, for the Gaussian denoising setting.

Lemma 1
(Single FBS iteration)

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}
be
f(\boldsymbol{x})=1/2\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}
for fixed
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

be proper, closed, and convex. For
\alpha>0
, define

T_{\alpha}(x)\;=\;\mathrm{Prox}_{\alpha g}\big(x-\alpha\nabla f(x)\big).

Then
T_{\alpha}
is
(1-\alpha)
-contractive for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

We next analyze the Lipschitz constant of the convolutional layer. Let
\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{K}}:\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}\times H\times W}\to\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times H\times W}
denote the linear operator of a multi-channel 2D convolution with kernel
\mathrm{K}

of dimension
{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}\times k_{h}\times k_{w}}
. Hence, the Lipschitz constant of the convolutional layer equals the operator (spectral) norm of
\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{K}}
, denoted as
\|\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{K}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
. Under circular boundary conditions on an
H\times W
grid,
\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{K}}
is block-diagonalized by the 2D Discrete Fourier Transform.
Thus, by
[
46
]
, a convolutional layer’s Lipschitz constant admits a closed form:

\|\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{K}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\;=\;\max_{\omega\in\Omega}\,\sigma_{\max}\big(\widehat{H}(\omega)\big),

(6)

where
\Omega
is a grid of size
H\times W
and
\widehat{H}(\omega)\in\mathbb{C}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}}
stacks the per-channel 2D FFTs of the spatial kernels:

\big[\widehat{H}(\omega)\big]_{o,i}\;=\;\mathcal{F}_{H,W}\big(\mathrm{K}_{o,i,:,:}\big)[\omega],

(7)

where
o\in\{1,\ldots,C_{\mathrm{out}}\}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,C_{\mathrm{in}}\}
index the output and input channels. Thus, the convolution layer’s Lipschitz constant is the maximum of frequency-response matrices
\widehat{H}(\omega)
over all frequencies. Consequently, for a convolution layer to be
1
-Lipschitz, we need to enforce

\max_{\omega\in\Omega}\sigma_{\max}\big(\widehat{H}(\omega)\big)\;\leq\;1.

We also need the following property of Lipschitz functions
[
3
]
to constrain our entire architecture to be contractive.

Lemma 2
(Lipschitz constant of compositions)

Let
F_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{i-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,M
, be
L_{i}
-Lipschitz. Then the composition
F:=F_{M}\circ F_{M-1}\circ\cdots\circ F_{1}
is Lipschitz and, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
,

\|F(x)-F(y)\|\;\leq\;\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{M}L_{i}\Big)\,\|x-y\|.

More importantly, the result in Lemma
2
ensures that finite compositions of contractive operators are contractive.

4
Proposed Method

In this section, we present our contractive architecture: we build a single contractive layer from carefully constrained blocks, form the full model by composing such layers, and discuss implementation details.

4.1
Gradient and prox-wavelet layer

We consider the denoising model in (
3
) and solve the inverse problem via FBS in (
3
), using a convex wavelet-based regularizer. Let
\mathbf{W}
be an orthonormal wavelet transform and let
\mathcal{H}
denote the index set of high-frequency detail coefficients; its complement
\mathcal{L}
indexes the low-frequency coefficients. We choose to regularize only the
high-frequency coefficients for image denoising:

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\;\frac{1}{2}\,\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}\;+\;\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}}\lambda_{i}\,\big|(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}\big|

(8)

Let
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{H}}
collect all the coefficient thresholds with constraints that
\lambda_{i}>0
i.e. thresholds are positive. This regularizer is motivated from
[
37
]
, where, contrary to their work, we threshold only the high-frequency components. In contrast to BayesShrink
[
9
]
and SUREShrink
[
16
]
, which set subband-wise thresholds by analytic rules, our approach learns thresholds for the high-frequency subbands.

Figure 3
:
Architecture of contractive layer
\mathrm{T}
. The layer performs a gradient step and prox-wavelet operation, followed by a scaled convolution.

A single FBS update for solving (
8
) can be written as

\displaystyle\text{(Gradient step)}\quad\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\alpha\,\boldsymbol{y},

\displaystyle\text{(Prox-wavelet step)}\quad\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}),

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
is the step size, and
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{p}
soft-thresholds only the high-frequency coefficients:

\big(\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)_{i}=\begin{cases}\operatorname{sign}(z_{i})\,\max\{|z_{i}|-\lambda_{i},\,0\},&i\in\mathcal{H},\\[3.0pt]
z_{i},&i\in\mathcal{L}.\end{cases}

We define our trainable layer
\mathrm{U}
as this single FBS update:

\mathrm{U}(\boldsymbol{x};\alpha,\Lambda)=\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}\!\left(\mathbf{W}\big((1-\alpha)\,\boldsymbol{x}+\alpha\,\boldsymbol{y}\big)\right),

(9)

and learn
\alpha
and the thresholds
\Lambda
under the explicit constraints
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\lambda_{i}>0
for all
i\in\mathcal{H}
. As shown next, this parametrization yields a provably contractive layer.

Proposition 1

For all
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\Lambda>0
, the map
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\mathrm{U}(\boldsymbol{x};\alpha,\Lambda)
is
(1-\alpha)
-contractive.

This follows because the wavelet-based regularizer is closed, proper, and convex
[
33
]
, so Lemma
1
applies.

4.2
Scaled convolution layer

From (
3
), a convolution is contractive iff its operator norm is less than
1
. We keep the kernel
\mathrm{K}
as the learnable parameter and induce the Lipschitz scaling into the filter operation. Let
s(\mathrm{K}):=\|\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{K}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
denote the operator norm of the convolution as defined in (
6
). Define the normalized operator
\overline{\mathrm{V}}_{\mathrm{K}}\;:=\;\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{K}}/s(\mathrm{K})
. By construction,
\|\overline{\mathrm{V}}_{\mathrm{K}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
, and hence
\overline{\mathrm{V}}_{\mathrm{K}}
is nonexpansive i.e. Lipschitz constant
\leq 1
. Thus
1
-Lipschitz convolutional layer is defined as

\mathbf{Z}(\boldsymbol{x};\mathrm{K})\;=\;\overline{\mathrm{V}}_{\mathrm{K}}\boldsymbol{x}\;=\;\,\frac{\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{K}}\boldsymbol{x}}{s(\mathrm{K})}

(10)

Replace
s(\mathrm{K})
by
s(\mathrm{K})+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon>0
, constrains
\mathbf{Z}(\boldsymbol{x};\mathrm{K})
to be contractive. Given a target Lipschitz budget
\gamma>0
, we can use
\gamma\mathbf{Z}(\boldsymbol{x};\mathrm{K})
as

\gamma
-Lipschitz convolution layer.

4.3
Contractive layer and network

The proposed layer architecture is shown in Fig
3
. To form a contractive layer, we combine a gradient step and a prox-wavelet block (one FBS update), with a Lipschitz-controlled convolution to refine the features. We define the layer
\mathrm{T}(\boldsymbol{x};\alpha,\Lambda,\mathrm{K})
as,

\tfrac{1}{(1-\alpha)+\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{Z}\big(\mathrm{U}(\boldsymbol{x};\alpha,\Lambda);\mathrm{K}\big)

(11)

where
\mathrm{K}
is the convolution kernel, and
\varepsilon>0
enforces the convolution has a Lipschitz constant less than
1/(1-\alpha)
. Here
\mathrm{U}(\cdot;\alpha,\Lambda)
is the FBS-based block defined in Sec.
4.1
, and
\mathbf{Z}(\cdot;\mathrm{K})
is the
1
-Lipschitz convolutional layer defined in Sec.
4.2
.

The FBS-based block
\mathrm{U}(\cdot;\alpha,\Lambda)
is
(1-\alpha)
-contractive by Proposition
1
. We therefore compose FBS-based block with a
\mathbf{Z}(\cdot;\mathrm{K})
,
1
-Lipschitz convolution with kernel
\mathrm{K}
, and scale it by
1/((1-\alpha)+\varepsilon)
. This preserves contractivity of the layer while relaxing the constraint to scale the main branch convolutional layer to be
1
-Lipschitz. We formalize the layer contractivity result next.

Theorem 4.1

For all
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\epsilon>0
, and
\Lambda>0
, the map
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\mathrm{T}(\boldsymbol{x};\alpha,\Lambda,\mathrm{K})
is contractive.

We define the depth-
M
network as the composition

F=\mathrm{T}_{M}\circ\mathrm{T}_{M-1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathrm{T}_{1}.

For each layer
\ell=1,\dots,M
, let

\mathrm{T}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathrm{T}\big(\boldsymbol{x};\alpha_{\ell},\Lambda_{\ell},\mathrm{K}_{\ell}\big),

where
\alpha_{\ell}\in(0,1)
,
\Lambda_{\ell}=\{\lambda_{\ell,i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{H}}
with
\lambda_{\ell,i}>0
, and
\mathrm{K}_{\ell}
are the layer-specific convolution kernels. By Theorem
4.1
, each
\mathrm{T}_{\ell}
is contractive and therefore, by Lemma
2
, the overall network
F
is contractive.

. For each layer

, the stepsize

is kept in

for all

, are constrained to be strictly positive by passing through the softplus function. To control the Lipschitz constants of the convolutions

, we use the exact normalization discussed in Sec.

. The PyTorch reference implementation is provided below.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

We constructed a provably contractive denoiser that attains accuracy close to strong unconstrained baselines like DnCNN and Restormer. Empirically, the model is robust to realistic input perturbations, while retaining contractivity; when deployed within PnP iterations, it yields stable convergence and high-quality reconstructions, whereas unconstrained denoisers can lead to divergence. To our knowledge, this is the first provably contractive denoiser that substantially closes the gap to unconstrained models. Beyond denoising, we next aim to develop end-to-end contractive restoration networks that combine provable stability guarantees with strong reconstruction quality.

\boldsymbol{y}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{x}+\eta,

\mathbf{S}

\mathbf{B}

1
-Lipschitz baselines (D-FBS, D-DRS) and strong unconstrained models such as Noise2VST and Restormer. We further evaluate
\times 4
super-resolution under anisotropic Gaussian, Gaussian, defocus, and box blur in Tables
11
,
12
,
13
, and
14
respectively, where our method again achieves the best or competitive results while preserving contraction. These results demonstrate that our contractive denoiser maintains strong reconstruction quality across diverse degradations. This trend is also reflected in the visual comparisons in Figures

and
