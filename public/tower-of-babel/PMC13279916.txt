VSMC-derived 3HAA activates AhR to drive abdominal aortic aneurysm formation via the p62–Nrf2 axis
Authors: Tharmarajan Ramprasath, Young-Min Han, Ye Ding, Sean Michael Carr, Ming-Hui Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) remains a life-threatening vascular disease without effective pharmacologic therapy. Although 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (3HAA), a kynurenine-pathway metabolite with dual redox effects, has been implicated in vascular diseases. How 3HAA instigates AAA is poorly defined.MethodsWe combined human AAA tissues, genetically modified mice, pharmacologic kynurenine-pathway inhibition, and mechanistic vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) studies to define 3HAA–AhR signaling in aneurysm formation.ResultsIn angiotensin (Ang) II-infused apoE−/− mice, plasma 3HAA positively correlated with abdominal aortic expansion. Among three kynurenine pathway (KP) inhibitors, both the Kynu inhibitor (OMBA) and KMO inhibitor (NBA) significantly reduced AAA incidence, aortic diameter, and aortic weight. In Kynuflox/floxsmMHCCre + mice, VSMC-specific Kynu deletion lowered AAA incidence and suppressed aortic remodeling, establishing a causal role for VSMC-derived 3HAA in AAA. Complementing this, AhR-deficient LdlR−/−-AhR−/− mice were resistant to AngII-induced AAA and exhibited reduced MMP2 expression along with suppressed NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, confirming AhR's requirement for 3HAA-mediated MMP2 induction. The human AAA samples showed a stronger upregulation of AhR protein than non-aneurysmal aortic samples. Mechanistically, 3HAA disrupted autophagic flux by blocking p62-dependent Nrf2 stabilization, leading to impaired antioxidant responses, mitochondrial dysfunction, and heightened VSMC susceptibility to oxidative injury. Restoring p62 or activating Nrf2 rescued 3HAA-induced mitochondrial defects and prevented VSMC degeneration.ConclusionsWe conclude that 3HAA promotes AAA formation by activating AhR and suppressing p62-Nrf2–mediated mitochondrial homeostasis and targeting the kynurenine-AhR axis may offer new therapeutic strategies for treating AAA.Keywords: 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid, Vascular smooth muscle cell, Abdominal aortic aneurysm, Aryl hydrocarbon receptor, p62/SQSTM1

Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is the 15th leading cause of death [1]. Although AAA often grows slowly without symptoms, ruptured AAA is a life-threatening condition with a mortality of 80%. Ruptured AAA is responsible for 30,000 deaths yearly in the United States [1]. The risk factors for AAA include male sex, advanced ages [2], hypertension [3], cigarette smoking [4], and atherosclerosis [5]. In the United States, more than 200,000 patients are diagnosed annually with AAA [6]. Elective repair of the aneurysm is the only way to prevent rupture, and approximately 40,000 patients undergo repair of AAA each year in the United States, at a cost likely to exceed a billion dollars [7]. Despite many decades of intensive research, currently, no effective pharmacological treatments are available to prevent or cure this disease [7,8].

L-Tryptophan (Trp) is an essential amino acid which should be obtained from dietary intakes and transported by l-amino acid transporter-1 (LAT-1) into the cells [9,10]. Trp is converted into several products called as kynurenines by the kynurenine pathway (KP), the primary way of Trp metabolism in non-brain tissues [11]. In brief, tryptophan is first converted by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (Ido) into kynurenine. By the involvement of a series of enzymes, kynurenine is converted into several other bioactive metabolites, including kynurenic acid (KA; produced by kynurenine aminotransferases; KAT), 3-hydroxykynurenine (3-OHKyn, produced by kynurenine monooxygenase; KMO), 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (3HAA, produced by kynureninase; Kynu), etc. [12,13] Depending on the cell types, these metabolites can elicit both hyper- and anti-inflammatory effects. Considering the KP metabolisms and their significant associations with diverse biological activities, the perturbations in the KP have been linked to several diseases, particularly cardiovascular diseases [12]. We had previously shown the proinflammatory effects of kynurenines and their association with vascular diseases, including endothelial dysfunction and aneurysm in angiotensin II (AngII)-infused in vivo mice models [14,15]. Notably, we reported that the 3HAA produced by the enzyme Kynu, one of the essential enzymes in the tryptophan pathway, likely mediated AAA in AngII-induced apolipoprotein E (apoE−/−)-mice in vivo. The human AAA samples showed stronger staining against 3HAA, Ido1, and Kynu in their aortic components than those in adjacent nonaneurysmal aortic sections [14].

3HAA exerts dual redox effects in the vascular wall. At physiologic levels, 3HAA functions as an antioxidant by scavenging superoxide and peroxyl radicals and enhancing Nrf2-dependent antioxidant gene expression14, 156. However, in inflammatory or AngII-driven conditions, 3HAA becomes pro-oxidant, promoting H2O2 production, amplifying myeloperoxidase (MPO)-mediated redox cycling, and inducing VSMC oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. This redox imbalance accelerates vascular inflammation, senescence, and extracellular matrix degradation, thereby facilitating AAA formation. The context-dependent antioxidant-to-pro-oxidant switch of 3HAA is a key driver of vascular injury. The biochemical analyzes also showed increased circulating 3HAA levels in aneurysm-developed rodents. However, the causative relationship between 3HAA and AAA remains to be established. Further, how 3HAA instigates AAA formation is elusive.

Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) is a cytoplasmic ligand-activated transcription factor that participates in a variety of cellular processes [16]. AhR belongs to the basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor family. Under normal conditions, the inactive AhR forms a stable protein complex in the cytoplasm with a dimer of the 90 kDa heat shock protein (HSP90), X-associated protein 2 (XAP2), and the co-chaperone p23 [17]. When cognate ligands bind to AhR, a conformational change occurs, exposing the complex's amino-terminal nuclear localization sequence and an adjacent nuclear export sequence [18]. Emerging evidence demonstrates that AhR is expressed in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs). Serving as a metabolic and stress-responsive transcription factor, it regulates cell fate during inflammation and oxidative stress. Lineage-tracing analyses verify that AhR inhibits pathological VSMC phenotypic transition, hindering differentiation toward osteochondrogenic and matrix-degrading phenotypes that impair vascular wall integrity [19]. Conversely, AhR activation triggered by endogenous tryptophan metabolites or environmental ligands elicits oxidative stress, pro-inflammatory gene expression and VSMC apoptosis via NF-κB and MAPK cascades, key processes driving abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) pathogenesis [7,20,21[7,20,21]. Activated AhR also upregulates matrix metalloproteinases and inflammatory mediators within vascular cells, accelerating elastin degradation and medial tissue damage, which are definitive pathological features of aneurysm formation [[22], [23], [24]]. Additionally, recent metabolomic and immunometabolic research links disrupted tryptophan-kynurenine signaling — a major reservoir of endogenous AhR ligands — to vascular inflammation and tissue deterioration. This establishes a mechanistic connection between metabolic perturbation, AhR activation and AAA progression [7,25,26]. Overall, these observations suggest that dysregulated AhR signaling in VSMCs drives medial smooth muscle loss, persistent inflammation and extracellular matrix degradation, ultimately rendering the aortic wall susceptible to aneurysmal degeneration. While tryptophan metabolites are confirmed AhR ligands that provoke numerous inflammatory disorders [27], the causal involvement of the VSMC-specific tryptophan metabolite-AhR axis in AAA has yet to be validated.

A complex pathological process involves in the AAA development, including aortic wall inflammation, elastin breakdown, oxidative stress, VSMC phenotypic switching and dysfunction, VSMC senescence and extracellular matrix degradation, etc., [28]. With the available evidence, it is well understood that kynurenine metabolites trigger the apoptosis of vascular cells [14,15]. The AhR signaling activated by its ligands has been reported to activate the inflammatory axis in vascular cells to promote cell apoptosis and the inflammatory response [29]. AhR activation was associated with VSMC apoptosis [19] and Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) activation, the phosphorylation of p65-NF-κB, and MAPKs, which could further promote the production of pro-inflammatory mediators [30]. However, its role in aneurysm development is totally unknown. Thus, considering the existing link between AhR activation and tryptophan metabolism as well as the role of 3HAA in aneurysm development, we hypothesized that 3HAA might mediate the aneurysm development by regulating the AhR signaling.

Here we report that both pharmacological inhibition of Kynu and VSMC-specific genetic knockout of Kynu abolish AAA formation in AngII-challenged mice, confirming the causal contribution of 3HAA to AAA pathogenesis. We further identified AhR as the receptor target of 3HAA. Consistently, AhR deletion also confers robust protection against AngII-triggered AAA development.

Materials and methods

Human

Surgical waste tissues of human abdominal aortic aneurysm tissues and their adjacent non-aneurysm tissues were collected from AAA patients (6) undergoing surgical treatments, along with non-aneurysm tissue specimen in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University. The clinical study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University (IRB2025-241) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Animals

Low-density lipoprotein receptor−/−-AhR−/− (LdlR−/−-AhR−/−) mice were a kind gift given by Dr. Sampath Parthasarathy (University of Central Florida). The VSMC-specific Kynu knock-out mice were generated with C57BL/6J background mice (The Jackson Laboratory, USA). Kynuflox/flox mice (Shanghai Model Organisms Center Inc., CA, USA) and smMHCCre + mice (Jackson Laboratories, USA) (7-9 weeks of age) were crossbred to get Kynuflox/floxsmMHCCre + mice. Mice were housed in temperature-controlled cages under a 12-h light-dark cycle and given free access to water and standard chow. For the aneurysm experiment, these mice were injected with 5.0 × 1011 units of AAV8-D377Y-mPCSK9 [31]. A week before injection, mice were fed with Western diet (WD), and the same feed was continued till the end of the experiment. One week after the virus injection, mice were implanted with AngII pumps. Experimental mice at age 8 -10 weeks were infused with AngII (1.44 μg/kg/min) or physiological saline (0.9% sodium chloride) by Alzet osmotic pumps (DURECT Corp, Model 2004) as described previously [14]. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Georgia State University approved all procedures involving animals.

Reagents

Antibodies to MMP2 (40994; Cell signal), Kynureninase (ls-c767823; LSBio, A6643; abclonal, 390360; SCBT), Ido1 (13268-1-ap; Protein tech, 05-840; Millipore; Novus, NB100-2459), KMO (60029-I-Ig; protein tech), AhR (LS-203762; LSBio, NB100-2289– Novus), IFn-γ (390800; SCBT), Nrf2 (365949; SCBT), Heme Oxygenase 1 (HO-1, sc-136960; SCBT), phospho–NF–κB p65 (Ser536, 3033), total–NF–κB p65 (8242; Cell signal), β-actin (sc-47778) and GAPDH (SC-166545; SCBT) were used in this study. All secondary antibodies were from Cell Signaling Technology. The Alexa Flour-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, USA) were used for immunofluorescence. All primary antibodies were used in a 1:1000 dilution for Western blot and a 1:100 dilution for immunocytochemistry and immunofluorescence. The 3-Hydroxy-dl-kynurenine (H1771), 3-hydroxy anthranilic acid (148776), and angiotensin II (Ang II, A9525) were purchased from Sigma. Kynurenic acid (sc-202683) was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Control siRNA (sc-37007) and siRNAs targeting AhR (sc-29654), kynureninase (sc-95023) and Nrf2 (sc-37030) were from Santa Cruz Biotechnology and sip62/SQSTM1 was bought from (AM16708) Thermo. The transfection reagent for siRNA (Lipofectamine RNAiMax, 13778150) was procured from Invitrogen.

Cell culture

Human aortic smooth muscle cells (HASMCs) (GIBCO, C0075C) were grown in Medium 231 (Gibco, M231) containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and Smooth Muscle Growth Supplement (SMGS, Gibco, S00725). All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Cells were used between passages 3 and 6, for all experiments and were grown to 70–80% confluence before being treated with different agents.

Cell treatment

3HAA was prepared as a 50 mM stock solution in DMSO, with a working concentration of 25 mM. 3-OHKyn was first dissolved in DMSO and further diluted with culture medium prior to use. Chloroquine (CQ) was formulated as a 10 mM DMSO stock, and cells were treated with 10 μM CQ for 24 h.

Gene transfection

siRNAs were transfected into HASMC cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA) according to manufacturer's guidelines. Briefly, 100 μl GIBCO™ Opti-MEM I Reduced-Serum Medium (1X) containing 5 μl siRNA stock solution was gently mixed with 100 μl GIBCO™ Opti-MEM I Reduced-Serum Medium (1X) containing 5 μl RNAiMax (Invitrogen). After a 30min incubation at room temperature, siRNA-lipid complexes were added to the cells in 800 μl GIBCO™ Opti-MEM I Reduced-Serum Medium (1X), and cells were incubated with this mixture for 6 h at 37°C. The transfection medium was then replaced with normal medium, and cells were cultured for 48 h.

Analysis and quantitation of AAA

We quantified aneurysm incidence based on a definition of the aneurysm as an external width of the suprarenal aorta that was increased by 50% or greater compared with aortas from saline-infused mice, as described previously 1. The average diameter of the normal suprarenal aorta in control mice is 0.8 mm. We, therefore, set a threshold of 1.22 mm as evidence of aneurysm formation.

Histological analysis

Aortas were perfused with saline and fixed in PBS with 10% formalin for 5 min before morphological analysis. Whole aortas were harvested, fixed, and embedded in paraffin before being cut into 5 mm cross-sections. Hematoxylin & Eosin (IHC World, IW-3100), Van Gieson elastic (Sigma, HT25A), and Masson trichrome stains were used to stain aortic sections (Sigma, HT15) for the observation of morphological changes. Image J was used to calculate the collagen deposition areas. We used a standard for the grades of elastin degradation, as previously described, to determine elastin degradation [15].

Immunohistochemistry staining

Aortic tissues were excised and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde before being embedded in paraffin and sectioned. After staining the slides with the appropriate antibodies overnight at 4°C, they were rinsed with washing buffer and incubated with horseradish peroxidase labeled anti-rabbit antibodies, followed by DAB + chromogen detection (DAKO). Following the final washing, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted.

Immunofluorescence staining

Sections or fixed cells were incubated with specific antibodies for overnight at 4°. After that, the samples were rinsed with washing buffer and incubated at 1:600 dilution with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa fluor 555 (A21434), goat anti-Mouse IgG conjugated to Alexa 555 (A21422), or goat anti-Rabbit IgG conjugated to Alexa 488 (A11008). DAPI was used to stain the nuclei. Slides were visualized and examined using OLYMPUS BX53 microscope.

Cell fractionation

After treatment the cells were fractionated into cytosolic and nuclear using the Cell Fractionation Kit (Cat. # 9038; cell signal) by following the protocol recommended by the manufacturer.

Western blot analysis

Western blots were performed as described previously [15] by following standard protocols.

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and real Time-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol (Invitrogen, 15596026) and cDNA was constructed using the Reverse transcriptase kit from Bio-Rad (172-5034 and 172-5035). Using the SYBR green master mix (B21202; bimake.com) Real-time PCR was performed as previously [15]. The Ct value was corrected by Ct reading of corresponding GAPDH controls. Data from three determinations (means ± SEM) are expressed as relative expression level. The murine (m) and human (h) oligos used for mRNA expression analyzes are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Determination of Trp, Kyn and 3HAA

Sample preparation for plasma was done by mixing 100 μl of sample by vortexing in a 1.5 ml eppendorf tube with 1/10 volume of ice-cold 2.4 mol/L perchloric acid. The cloudy suspension was chilled on ice for 15’ and then centrifuged at 8,000g for 3 min. The clear protein-free supernatant was analyzed directly. IS was measured by HPLC as described previously. Kyn content was estimated along with standards ranging from 0.250 to 10 μM l-kynurenine (Sigma, K8625), and Trp level was assessed along standards ranging from 1 to 25 μM DL-Tryptophan (Sigma, 162698), using a C18 column (Symmetry C18 Column, 100 Å, 5 μm, 4.6 mm × 250 mm; Waters, USA). The mobile phase was 20 mM sodium acetate at pH = 4.5. The flow rate was 1 ml/min. The column effluent was monitored at 360 nm (Kyn) and 280 nm (Trp) by a UV detector. 3HAA was measured by HPLC as described previously1. 3HAA content was estimated along with standards ranging from 1 to 250 nM, using C18 column (Symmetry C18 Column, 100 Å, 5 μm, 4.6 mm × 250 mm; Waters, USA). The mobile phase consisted of 25 mM sodium acetate (pH = 5.5) and methanol (90:10 v/v) was pumped at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Fluorescent detection at excitation 320 nm and emission 420 nm was used.

Statistics

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SEM from no fewer than three independent experiments. Student's t-test was applied for two-group comparisons, while one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used for multiple-group analyses. Non-parametric tests were adopted when statistical assumptions were violated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0, and a P value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Results

Pharmacological inhibition of kynureninase markedly suppresses AngII-induced AAA in vivo

Kynurenines have been reported to elicit either pro- or anti-inflammatory effects depending on the cell type [7]. Therefore, we first assessed the effects of three pharmacological inhibitors of the kynurenine pathway (KP) on AngII-induced AAA in apoE−/− mice. To compare their efficacy, mice were pretreated for seven days with either 1-methyl tryptophan (1 MT, an Ido1 inhibitor), nitrobenzoylalanine (NBA, a KMO inhibitor), or o-methoxybenzoylalanine (OMBA, a Kynu inhibitor). Following AngII mini-pump implantation, inhibitor treatments were continued for an additional 28 days until the end of the experimental period (Fig. 1A).

Consistent with expectations, treatment with 1 MT, OMBA, or NBA reduced the incidence of AAA in AngII-infused apoE−/− mice (Fig. 1B). Notably, Kynu inhibition with OMBA produced a more pronounced reduction in aortic diameter compared with NBA or 1 MT (AngII vs. AngII+1 MT, P = 0.6043; AngII vs. AngII + NBA, P = 0.0157; AngII vs. AngII + OMBA, P = 0.0019) (Fig. 1C and D). As shown in Fig. 1D and E, AngII infusion significantly increased both aortic diameters and weights. Similarly, the reduction in aortic weight was greatest with OMBA treatment (AngII vs. AngII+1 MT, P = 0.0936; AngII vs. AngII + NBA, P = 0.0021; AngII vs. AngII + OMBA, P = 0.0001) (Fig. 1E).

Next, we quantified serum kynurenine metabolites in AngII-infused mice with or without KP inhibitors. AngII infusion significantly reduced serum tryptophan levels (Supplementary Fig. 1A) but did not alter kynurenine levels (Supplementary Fig. 1B), resulting in unchanged Kyn/Try ratios (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Conversely, AngII markedly increased 3HAA levels (Supplementary Fig. 1D) and 3HAA/Kyn ratios (Supplementary Fig. 1E). Treatment with NBA, 1 MT, or OMBA decreased serum 3HAA levels (Supplementary Fig. 1D and E), but the reduction in AAA incidence was lowest with the Ido1 inhibitor, suggesting that KMO- and/or Kynu-derived metabolites may be key drivers of AAA in this model.

We further examined expression of KP-related enzymes in AngII-infused aortas. Western blot analysis showed that AngII significantly upregulated Ido1, KMO, and Kynu expression, whereas 3-hydroxyanthranilate 3,4-dioxygenase (HAAO), which converts 3HAA to quinolinic acid, remained unchanged (Fig. 1F). Linear regression analysis revealed a positive correlation between abdominal aortic expansion and plasma 3HAA levels (P < 0.0005, R2 = 0.6388) (Fig. 1G), as well as between aortic Kynu mRNA expression and aortic diameter (P < 0.0001, R2 = 0.9223) (Fig. 1H).

VSMC-specific Kynu deletion prevents AAA formation in a western diet (WD)/PCSK9-AngII model

To assess the functional role of Kynu, we generated mice with VSMC-specific Kynu deletion (Kynuflox/floxsmMHCCre+). Immunohistochemical staining of aortic tissues from wild-type (WT) mice using a Kynu-specific antibody revealed robust Kynu expression in both the endothelial cell (EC) and vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) layers (Fig. 2A). In Kynuflox/floxsmMHCCre + mice, Kynu immunoreactivity was retained in the EC layer but completely ablated in the VSMC layer (Fig. 2A), confirming successful VSMC-specific Kynu knockout in this transgenic mouse model.

To establish a hypercholesterolemic mouse model, mice were fed a Western diet (WD) and injected with adeno-associated virus (AAV) carrying the gain-of-function PCSK9 D374Y mutant (AAV.mPCSK9), which mimics the human pathogenic mutation responsible for autosomal dominant hypercholesterolemia [[32], [33], [34]]. This experimental strategy robustly exacerbates AngII-induced AAA formation, recapitulating the hyperlipidemic phenotype of Ldlr-deficient mice [33]. WD combined with AAV-PCSK9 treatment significantly elevated serum cholesterol levels in AngII-infused mice (Fig. 2B). After four weeks of AngII infusion, the AAA incidence reached approximately 60% in wild-type (WT) control mice. In contrast, Kynuflox/floxsmMHCCre + mice exhibited a markedly reduced AAA incidence of roughly 26% (Fig. 2C and D). Survival analysis further confirmed improved overall survival in Kynu-deficient mice, consistent with reduced AAA-associated mortality (Fig. 2E). Moreover, Kynuflox/floxsmMHCCre + mice displayed significantly decreased maximal aortic diameter and body weight-normalized aortic weight (Fig. 2F and G), while the heart-to-body weight ratio remained unaltered across groups (Supplementary Fig. 2A). HPLC analysis verified disrupted kynurenine pathway (KP) activation, characterized by enhanced systemic tryptophan-to-kynurenine conversion in AngII-challenged mice (Supplementary Fig. 2B–E). Notably, circulating levels of kynurenine and 3HAA were comparable between Kynuflox/floxsmMHCCre + mice and their littermate controls (Kynuflox/floxsmMHCCre- mice) (Supplementary Fig. 2C–E).

VSMC-specific Kynu deletion reduces AngII-induced AhR-mediated MMP2 expression

Histopathological analysis showed that AngII-infused Kynuflox/floxsmMHCCre- (WT) mice displayed severe disruption of aortic structural integrity, accompanied by extensive elastin degradation and VSMC depletion. In contrast, Kynuflox/floxsmMHCCre + mice were largely protected from such pathological damage (Fig. 3A–C). Moreover, Kynu-deficient aortas exhibited markedly reduced collagen deposition (Fig. 3D and E), indicating a critical role for Kynu in mediating aortic fibrotic remodeling. Immunostaining and Western blot analyses further validated that VSMC-specific Kynu deletion efficiently blunted AngII-induced upregulation of MMP2 expression, NF-κB phosphorylation, and the senescence markers p21, p16, and p27 in aortic tissues (Fig. 3F–I). Consistent with the in vivo observations, in vitro qRT-PCR results confirmed that AngII-challenged Kynu-deficient HASMCs exhibited significantly diminished expression of MMP2 and MMP9 (Fig. 3J).

Tryptophan metabolites mediate AhR upregulation in AAA formation

Abnormal AhR activation leads to an unbalanced cardiovascular system and cardiovascular diseases [35]. AngII infusion upregulated Ido1 and Kynu in aortic tissues, while HAAO expression remained unchanged (Fig. 3K). AhR expression was increased in WT mice by AngII but significantly attenuated in Kynu-deficient mice (Fig. 3K and L). In vitro, 24h exposure of HASMCs to 3-OHKyn increased AhR expression, which was abrogated by siRNA-mediated Kynu silencing (Fig. 3M). Western blot analysis also confirmed that Kynu regulates 3-OHKyn–induced phosphorylation of NF-κB, MMP2, and p21 (Fig. 3M), indicating that Kynu contributes to AngII-associated inflammatory, proteolytic, and senescence pathways in VSMCs.

AhR deletion attenuates AngII-induced AAA formation

In the absence of Kynu, the 3-OHKyn was not utilized as a substrate to produce the 3HAA, so the AhR upregulation was impacted. We also found an increased expression of AhR in aortic tissues of AngII-infused LdlR−/− mice (Fig. 4A and B), which indicates that during AAA development, upregulated expression of Kynu and its product (3HAA) might further activate AhR.

Next, to unravel the role of AhR in the development of abdominal aortic aneurysms, the LdlR−/− - AhR−/− mice were used to detect the outcome of AngII-induced aneurysm. AAA incidence was significantly lower in AngII-infused LdlR−/− - AhR−/− mice compared to the LdlR−/− - AhR+/+ mice (Fig. 4C and D). The other vital parameters of AAA, including maximal aortic diameter (Fig. 4E) and aortic weight normalized by body weight (Fig. 4F) showed a significant reduction in AngII-infused LdlR−/− - AhR−/− mice compared to the LdlR−/− - AhR+/+ mice. Heart weight to body weight ratios remained unchanged (Fig. 4G). As AhR effects are mediated by an assembly of metabolites, including Trp metabolites [36], and we had previously shown a relationship between Trp metabolism and aneurysm formation [14], we assessed the important KP metabolites level in serum. The results showed enhanced tryptophan catabolism (Supplementary Fig. 3C) and a higher kynurenine and 3HAA (Supplementary Fig. 3 B-D) serum level in both AngII-infused mice groups. Survival analysis revealed improved survival in AngII-infused LdlR−/−-AhR−/− mice relative to LdlR−/− controls, consistent with reduced AAA-related mortality (Fig. 4H), indicating that AhR deletion improved survival, consistent with reduced AAA-related mortality.

AhR deletion attenuates inflammation, VSMC senescence, and matrix remodeling

The Western blots of aortic tissues also indicated that increased Ido1, and Kynu expression along with increased expression of Ifn-γ (Fig. 5A–B), which indicate the activation of KP in aortic tissue mediated by Ifn-γ [15]. Interestingly, AhR deletion did not change AngII-upregulated Ifn-γ, Ido1, and Kynu protein levels (Fig. 5A–B). The pathogenesis of AAA includes VSMC apoptosis/senescence, and AngII infusion is intensely associated with vascular inflammation, which is considered as a critical mediator of AngII-induced AAA formation [37]. The mRNA quantification of some important cell cycle regulators (p21 and p27) showed that AhR deficiency significantly reduced their expression (Fig. 5C). The histological analysis showed that neither the aortic expansion nor the increased aortic thickness was present in AngII infused LdlR−/−AhR−/− mice (Fig. 5D). Moreover, AngII infusion in LdlR−/−AhR−/− mice did not cause aortic elastic lamina degradation or collagen deposition (Fig. 5D and E). These results suggest that AhR deletion confers protection from AngII-induced AAA formation in LdlR−/− mice in vivo. Western blots of aortas of saline and AngII infused LdlR−/−AhR−/− and LdlR−/−-AhR+/+ mice revealed that AhR deficiency significantly abrogated the phosphorylation of NF-κB (Fig. 5F–H) and significantly reduced the MMP2 expression in the aortas of AngII-infused LdlR−/− mice (Fig. 5F–H). Treatment of VSMCs with 3HAA over different time points elevated NF-κB phosphorylation and MMP2 protein expression (Fig. 5I). Consistently, 3HAA markedly upregulated MMP2 and p21 levels, along with phosphorylated p65-NF-κB activity in VSMCs (Fig. 5J).

Clinical relevance: AhR activation in human AAA

To establish the clinical relevance of AhR activation and AAA formation, we further examined the expression of the AhR protein in human AAA aortic samples obtained from patients undergoing open surgery. Human AAA tissues showed a strongly upregulated expression of AhR than the nonaneurysmal parts of the aorta (Fig. 5L and M).

3HAA upregulates MMP2 via AhR

The human aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) predominantly resides in the cytoplasm and translocates to the nucleus upon ligand stimulation [38]. Binding of endogenous or xenobiotic ligands halts basal nucleocytoplasmic shuttling and stabilizes AhR within the nucleus [39]. Here, we observed substantially stronger AhR staining in aneurysmal aortic regions relative to saline-treated control tissues (Fig. 6A). Subcellular fractionation assays of 3HAA-stimulated HASMCs further verified that 3HAA increased nuclear AhR abundance (Fig. 6B), demonstrating that 3HAA upregulates AhR expression both in vivo and in vitro. To further validate the regulatory effect of 3HAA on aortic AhR expressionin vivo, the LdlR−/− mice were injected with 3HAA for three weeks (every 48 h/i.p.) and their aorta was used for detecting the AhR level. The results showed significantly increased levels of AhR and MMP2 in the aorta of 3HAA-challenged mice (Fig. 6 C&D). In another experiment to know if 3HAA mediates the MMP2 expression via the AhR activation, the LdlR−/− - AhR+/+ and LdlR−/− - AhR−/− animals were either injected with vehicle (DMSO) or 3HAA for four weeks of time (every 48 h/i.p.). At the end of the experimental period the processed aortae were used for immunoblotting. The results showed that the 3HAA injection into LdlR−/− - AhR−/− mice did not promote the expression of MMP2 in aorta, whereas LdlR−/− - AhR+/+ animals challenged with 3HAA showed the increased expression of MMP2 along with increased expression of AhR (Fig. 6E). Consistent with this result, the exposure of 3HAA at different time points in VSMC resulted in increasing of mRNA levels of AhR and other genes those involved in vascular remodeling (Fig. 6F). The data also showed that 3HAA treatments markedly increased the levels of MMP2, MMP9, p16, and p21 (Fig. 6F.).

3HAA induces AhR upregulation via the p62–Nrf2 axis

Having confirmed that 3HAA induces AhR upregulation, we next investigated the molecular mechanism by which 3HAA modulates AhR expression in VSMCs. Time-course 3HAA stimulation markedly increased the accumulation of p62/SQSTM1 and LC3B proteins in VSMCs (Fig. 7A). In LC3B-expressing mouse aortic smooth muscle cells, 3HAA treatment for 3 and 6 h significantly increased LC3B puncta formation (Fig. 7B and C), indicative of suppressed autophagic flux. In HASMCs, 3HAA-dependent p62/SQSTM1 accumulation was accompanied by a robust increase in AhR protein levels (Fig. 7D). Chloroquine, a pharmacological inhibitor of autophagosome–lysosome fusion, further augmented p62 and AhR abundance under 3HAA exposure (Fig. 7E). Conversely, genetic knockdown of p62 completely abolished 3HAA-induced AhR elevation (Fig. 7F), confirming a positive regulatory relationship between p62 and AhR expression.

Previous studies [[39], [40], [41], [42]] have demonstrated that p62 binds Keap1 to stabilize Nrf2 and facilitate Nrf2 nuclear translocation. Subcellular fractionation analyses further revealed enhanced nuclear accumulation of p62 in VSMCs at 4 h and 24 h following 3HAA treatment (Fig. 7G). Accordingly, 3HAA exposure upregulated Nrf2 expression and its canonical downstream antioxidant target heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) (Fig. 7H). Given the established role of Nrf2 in driving AHR transcription and the well-documented crosstalk between the AhR and Nrf2 signaling axes, we hypothesized that 3HAA-triggered p62 accumulation mediates AhR upregulation in VSMCs. Indeed, siRNA-mediated Nrf2 knockdown completely abrogated 3HAA-induced AhR upregulation at both the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 7H and I), validating the p62–Nrf2 axis as an essential upstream regulator of AhR expression.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that 3HAA-mediated p62 upregulation dissociates Nrf2 from Keap1 repression. The stabilized, free Nrf2 subsequently translocates into the nucleus and transcriptionally activates AHR expression in VSMCs.

Discussion

In this study, we identify kynureninase (Kynu) as a previously unrecognized and essential mediator of abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) formation. Using complementary pharmacologic, genetic, mechanistic, and translational approaches, we demonstrate that 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (3HAA)—the metabolic product of Kynu—drives a pathogenic p62–Nrf2–AhR–NF-κB–MMP2 signaling axis in VSMCs, thereby promoting vascular remodeling and aneurysm progression. Our results indicate that non-canonical 3HAA–AhR or metabolic signaling underlies its AAA-promoting actions.

First, targeted inhibition of the kynurenine pathway (KP) markedly attenuated AngII-induced AAA, and Kynu inhibition provided the strongest protection, surpassing Ido1 or KMO blockade. Inhibition of the 3HAA production with either pharmacological inhibitors or genetic knockout of Kynu markedly lowered AngII-induced AAA. This finding is consistent with our previous reports that an activation of the kynurenine pathway has an important effect on AngII-induced aneurysm formation and endothelial dysfunctions [14,15]. These findings were substantiated genetically: VSMC-specific Kynu deletion almost completely abrogated AAA formation in a hypercholesterolemic AngII model, establishing Kynu as the dominant KP enzyme driving aneurysm pathology.

We also demonstrated for the first time that infusion of AngII induces AAA formation via the coordinated regulation between tryptophan catabolism and AhR activation by activated MMPs. We determined that exogenous infusion of 3HAA in vivo in mice caused increased production of MMPs in the aorta and further involved in aortic remodeling, whereas the AhR deficient mice showed resistance to this challenge, and 3HAA infusion could not trigger the MMPs expression in vivo. In human, when compared to the nonaneurysmal parts of the aorta, human AAA samples showed an increased expression of AhR. Indeed, our previous findings showed an increased expression of Kynu in human AAA samples, which showed a clinical relevance of 3HAA-activated AhR in human AAA disease development [14]. Finally, AhR deletion attenuated the NF-kB phosphorylation and showed reduced expression of p21 and MMPs induced by AngII infusion, thereby rescuing AngII-induced VSMC apoptosis and vascular remodeling. Mechanistically, we show that 3HAA is a critical upstream activator of AhR, promoting its nuclear accumulation and transcriptional activity. AhR deletion in Ldlr−/− mice profoundly protected against AngII-AAA without altering upstream KP activation, placing AhR downstream of Kynu-derived metabolites. The translational significance of these findings is reinforced by robust AhR upregulation in human AAA tissues, indicating clinical relevance of the Kynu–3HAA–AhR pathway.

Kynu-derived 3HAA promotes AAA through mechanisms distinct from oxidative injury. Despite AngII markedly elevating superoxide and 3-nitrotyrosine in both genotypes, aneurysm severity diverged, indicating that 3HAA drives vascular remodeling and inflammation independently of redox imbalance. This suggests non-canonical 3HAA–AhR or metabolic signaling underlies its AAA-promoting actions. We further demonstrate that 3HAA-induced AhR activation requires p62 accumulation and Nrf2 stabilization, defining a previously unidentified p62–Nrf2–AhR regulatory module in VSMCs. This axis amplified NF-κB phosphorylation, MMP2/MMP9 expression, and senescence markers (p21, p27), providing a mechanistic link between KP metabolism, inflammation, and extracellular matrix degradation. In Ang II-infused apoE−/− mouse aortic tissue both p16 and p21 proteins were shown to be upregulated [37]. p21 deficiency could attenuate aneurysmal development and increased VSMC proliferation which suggest an important role of p21 in inducing VSMC senescence [37]. Our results are in line with other studies showing that the AhR pathway has a vital role in several other vascular events, including regulating the expression of markers in vascular inflammation and atherogenesis [43], high-fat diet-induced vascular dysfunction [44] endothelial dysfunction [45] as well as regulating the MMPs [46].

Mechanistically, we found that AhR protein is regulated by the p62-Nrf2 axis. The p62 protein regulates the autophagic removal of protein aggregates and damaged cellular organelles through its interaction with ubiquitin and the LC3 component of autophagy. The failure to control the accumulation of dysfunctional biomolecules in defective autophagy is related to cardiovascular diseases [47,48]. During the aneurysm development, the upregulated 3HAA level promoted the excessive accumulation of p62/SQSTM1 and LC3b which indicates the impaired autophagic condition mediated by 3HAA. This suggests that p62/SQSTM1 could competitively interact with Keap1 at the Nrf2−Keap1 binding site, promoting the release of Nrf2 [49], which allows its translocation into the nucleus. On the other hand, the increased p62/SQSTM1 can degrade the keap1 protein, an inhibitor of the Nrf2 protein [40]. The translocated Nrf2 further regulates gene expression of AhR at the transcriptional level.

These data position Kynu inhibition and AhR blockade as promising therapeutic strategies. Development of selective, bioavailable Kynu inhibitors and testing in additional preclinical AAA models—including post-formation intervention studies—represent critical next steps. Given the central role of the p62–Nrf2–AhR axis, targeting downstream signaling nodes or metabolite-specific effects of 3HAA may also offer new therapeutic avenues.

Our study has several limitations. For example, our work focuses primarily on VSMC-intrinsic mechanisms; the contributions of macrophages, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells remain to be fully delineated. The metabolic kinetics of 3HAA in vivo and potential crosstalk with other Trp-derived metabolites require further investigation. Although our human tissue analyses validate AhR upregulation, prospective clinical studies assessing circulating 3HAA or KP flux in AAA patients are needed. Finally, oxidative stress plays key role in AAA and future study on the relationship of 3HAA and AAA-related oxidative stress is warranted.

In summary, we reveal a metabolite-driven signaling network in which Kynu and its product 3HAA orchestrate maladaptive VSMC remodeling via p62–Nrf2–AhR–NF-κB–MMP2 activation. These findings establish the kynurenine pathway as a mechanistically actionable contributor to AAA and highlight Kynu–3HAA–AhR signaling as a compelling target for therapeutic intervention.
