Three-Dimensional Radiographic and Morphological Features of Ameloblastoma and Odontogenic Keratocyst: A Comparative Cone Beam Computed Tomography Study Using Automatic Segmentation
Authors: Liang Li, Shengfu Huang, Xu Zhang, Xin Liu, Yong Cheng, Kai Yang, Lefei Zhang, Hong He, Zimo Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe aimed to compare the three-dimensional (3D) radiographic and morphological features, and explore their associations in ameloblastoma (AM) and odontogenic keratocyst (OKC) on CBCT scans, to facilitate clinicians in the preoperative differential diagnosis between the two lesions.MethodsCBCT scans of 151 AMs and 164 OKCs were retrospectively collected. The morphological features (volume, length, width, height) of lesions were obtained by OCL-Net automatically, an algorithm established for odontogenic cystic lesion (OCL) segmentation. Meanwhile, the radiographic features of lesions including shape, border, buccolingual expansion, cortical bone destruction, lesion-tooth relationship, root resorption and tooth displacement were assessed by maxillofacial radiologists. Differences in morphological and radiographic features between AM and OKC were identified. Additionally, associations between morphological and radiographic features were analyzed separately in AM and OKC cases.ResultsAM showed significantly greater volume (p = 0.002), length (p = 0.009), length-to-width (p = 0.014), and length-to-height (p = 0.022) ratios compared with OKC. Additionally, scalloped borders (p = 0.003), buccolingual expansion (p < 0.001), cortical bone destruction (p = 0.035), root resorption (p < 0.001) and tooth displacement (p = 0.014), were more commonly observed in AMs than in OKCs. Moreover, OKCs with cortical bone destruction (p < 0.001) and tooth displacement (p < 0.01) were significantly larger than those without, while these radiographic features were not associated with lesion volume in AMs. Conclusion: OKCs generally show smaller lesion volumes, less extensive invasion along the frontal axis, and less aggressive radiographic features on CBCT than AMs. In addition, small lesions that cause destruction of the surrounding tissues are more likely to be AMs rather than OKCs.Clinical RelevanceOur findings may aid clinicians in the preoperative differentiation of AM and OKC, while our proposed OCL-Net may facilitate the future development of a fully automated diagnostic system and provide a novel perspective for the differential diagnosis of a broader spectrum of jaw space-occupying lesions.Key words: Ameloblastoma, Odontogenic cysts, Cone-beam computed tomography, Diagnosis, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Ameloblastoma (AM) and odontogenic keratocyst (OKC) are common space-occupying odontogenic lesions in the jawbone. AMs, as the most common odontogenic tumours, are benign yet aggressive neoplasms originating from the odontogenic epithelium.1 OKCs, as the third most common jaw cysts, are benign developmental cystic lesions with a high recurrence rate.2 Clinically, AM and OKC may present with similar manifestations, leading to diagnostic confusion. For example, they frequently occur in the posterior region of the mandible and affect patients within similar age ranges.3, 4, 5 However, their distinct biological behaviours necessitate different treatments. Treatments for AM are more radical, involving wide local excision and immediate reconstruction.1,6 In contrast, OKC is usually managed conservatively with marsupialization or enucleation.4,7 Therefore, accurate clinical diagnosis is crucial for AM and OKC patients.

Although histopathological examination is the gold standard for diagnosing AM and OKC, it is a postoperative and invasive detection tool.8, 9, 10 Aspiration biopsy is sometimes employed as a supplementary diagnostic tool, as OKC often yields a keratinaceous, cheesy material, whereas AM typically produces a more serous or haemorrhagic fluid. However, this method is not always definitive.11 Therefore, other examination methods are imperative to diagnose these diseases before surgery. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), as a preoperative radiographic examination, can provide three‑dimensional (3D) information about lesions, including volume and relationships with adjacent tissues, with a high spatial resolution.12 Meanwhile, clinicians achieved greater accuracy in evaluating the radiographic features of cystic diseases by using CBCT compared to other conventional techniques, such as panoramic radiographs.13, 14, 15 Thus, CBCT seems promising in the differential diagnosis of AM and OKC.

Previous comparative studies of AM and OKC based on CBCT were primarily focused on the impact of lesions on surrounding tissues.15, 16, 17 However, to our knowledge, studies comparing the morphological features between these two lesions remain limited. Shastry et al13 reported the length, width, and height of 7 OKC cases. Apart from the small sample size, the measurements were conducted on the selected 2D CBCT slice, rather than from 3D reconstructions of lesions. Additionally, Kauke et al18 utilized ITK-SNAP software to segment 27 OKCs and 87 non-neoplastic jaw cysts from CBCT scans and found that the former had significantly larger volumes than the latter. This suggested that the morphological features of jawbone lesions may support their differential diagnosis. However, this lesion segmentation approach was not fully automated and was therefore time-consuming. More importantly, whether lesion volume differs between AM and OKC remains unknown, and the potential associations between their radiographic and morphological features are also unclear.

To address this gap, we developed a deep learning (DL) model, called odontogenic cystic lesion (OCL)-Net, to perform fully automated, accurate 3D segmentation of jaw lesions across large CBCT datasets, in our previous study.19 This approach is consistent with the growing role of artificial intelligence (AI) in dentistry, as recent reviews have highlighted its increasing applications in dental image analysis, disease detection, diagnostic support, and treatment planning.20,21 This algorithm also enables comprehensive and objective extraction of key morphological parameters (volume, length, width, and height) without manual segmentation. Building on this capability, our present study aimed to compare morphological and radiographic characteristics between AMs and OKCs in a large cohort, and to identify clinically meaningful associations between morphological and radiographic features for facilitating preoperative differential diagnosis of the two diseases.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

CBCT imaging data (in DICOM format) included in this study were retrospectively obtained from the Stomatological Hospital of Wuhan University, covering the period from 2015 to 2019. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of the School & Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University (Approval number: WDKQ2020-B22).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) complete clinical records, (2) histopathological confirmation as AM or OKC based on excisional biopsy, and (3) high-quality CBCT images. The exclusion criteria included (1) multiple lesions, (2) recurrent lesions, (3) naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome, and (4) simultaneous occurrence of AM and OKC within the same jaw. Additionally, all patient data were fully anonymized before analysis, and any potentially identifiable information, including personal details and metadata associated with CBCT images, was removed.

As a result, a total of 315 patients were recruited in two batches, comprising 151 patients with AM and 164 patients with OKC. Batch one consisted of 160 patients (68 AM and 92 OKC patients) with lesion labels annotated by two radiology experts, while batch two comprised 155 patients (83 AM and 72 OKC patients) without corresponding labels.

Radiographic feature evaluation

All CBCT images were independently reviewed by two maxillofacial radiology experts (S.P. W. and X.L. G.), each with over 10 years of clinical experience in oral and maxillofacial imaging, to assess the radiographic features of each lesion, including location, shape, border, buccolingual expansion, cortical bone destruction, lesion-tooth relationship, root resorption and tooth displacement (Figure 1). In cases where consensus was not reached between the two reviewers, a third expert (B.L.), also a board-certified maxillofacial radiologist with more than 15 years of diagnostic experience, was consulted for the final decision. Morphological parameter measurement

To quickly and accurately measure the morphological parameters of unlabelled lesions from CBCT scans, we employed our previously developed automatic CNN named OCL-Net. This AI-based segmentation project was developed in accordance with relevant principles of the Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM).22,23 Details of the architecture, implementation, and training are provided in our previous study19 and the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Figure 1).

Meanwhile, length, width, and height were calculated by generating the axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) of each lesion. The AABB, as a minimum bounding parallelepiped aligned with the coordinate axes, effectively describes the spatial extent of a three-dimensional object by defining its minimum and maximum coordinate points.24 In this article, length, width and height refer to the distance in the frontal (left-right, L-R), sagittal (anterior-posterior, A-P) and vertical (superior to inferior, S-I) axis, respectively. Experimental process

The experiment workflow is depicted in Figure 2B and 2C. Firstly, all CBCT images from batch one were independently reviewed by two radiological experts, who manually annotated the label of each lesion and measured their morphological parameters using 3D Slicer (version 4.10.2). Next, 80% of the data in batch one was used as the training set, and the remaining 20% was used as the test set to evaluate the model performance.

The model evaluation consisted of two components: (1) the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) and the mean intersection over union (mIoU) score, which qualify the overlap between the predicted and the ground truth region. A higher DSC and mIoU score indicated closer alignment between the automatic segmentation of the model and the manual segmentation of experts. (2) paired t-test, confirming no significant statistical difference between the morphological parameters of the predicted lesions and the labels.

Once validated, a new model was trained on the whole batch one dataset and applied to batch two for automatic segmentation. Segmentation results for batch two were checked by the same radiology experts who reviewed batch one, with morphological parameters recorded.

Subsequently, the morphological and radiographic features of lesions from both batches were integrated for two analyses: 1) To compare whether these features differ significantly between AM and OKC; 2) To explore the potential associations between radiographic and morphological features in each disease type to determine whether such associations differ between AM and OKC.

Statistics

Data were analysed using SPSS Statistics, Version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Chi-square test was used to compare sex distribution and the distribution of subcategories within each radiographic feature (eg, border type and buccolingual expansion pattern) between AM and OKC. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and Levene’s test was applied to examine homogeneity of variances.25 In cases where the data met both normal distribution and homogeneity of variance assumptions, independent t-tests were used for two-group comparisons, and one-way ANOVA was used for multiple-group comparisons. Otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were applied as their respective nonparametric alternatives. Effect sizes were determined using eta-squared (η²), which was interpreted according to standard thresholds, with 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 indicating small, medium, and large effects, respectively.26,27 Pairwise multiple comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni method where applicable. Data visualization was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Model performance

Out of 160 lesions in batch one, 32 samples (14 AM and 18 OKC) were used as a test set to validate the model performance, with a mean DSC score of 0.95 and mIoU score of 0.91. Meanwhile, the paired t-test results showed no statistically significant difference (all P > .05) between the morphological parameters of lesions obtained from the manual annotations and automatic segmentations (Supplementary Table 1).

A total of 151 cases diagnosed as AM and 164 cases diagnosed as OKC were included in this study, with their clinical and radiographic findings summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Figure 2. Comparison of the radiographic and morphological characteristics between AM and OKC.

Additionally, the chi-squared test revealed significant differences in all evaluated radiographic characteristics between AMs and OKCs (all P ≤ .05), except for the lesion-tooth relationship. Most AMs (91.4%) and OKCs (78.7%) were located in the mandible, with the molar and retromolar areas being the most common sites (Supplementary Figure 2). Scalloped borders were observed in 108 AM lesions (71.5%) and 91 OKC lesions (55.5%). In addition, 86 AM cases (57.0%) and 115 OKC cases (70.1%) showed a unilocular shape, respectively.

Of the 151 AM patients, only 11 cases (7.3%) did not show buccolingual expansion, and buccal expansion was the most common expansion pattern, occurring in 66 cases (43.7%). However, in OKC, 64 cases (39.0%) did not show buccolingual expansion, while among those with expansion, the buccolingual type was the most common pattern (24.4%). Additionally, cortical bone destruction was present in 88.1% of AMs and 79.3% of OKCs. Moreover, 77 AM lesions (51.0%) and 23 OKC lesions (14.0%) exhibited root resorption, while 55 AM cases (36.4%) and 39 OKC cases (23.8%) demonstrated tooth displacement.

Morphological characteristics of AM and OKC

In both AM and OKC, the 0 to 15 cm³ volume range was the most common category, comprising 49.34% and 60.74% of cases, respectively. Meanwhile, the length, width, and height of AM and OKC were typically between 26 and 50 mm (Supplementary Figure 2). The mean volume of AM lesions was 19.93 ± 15.42 cm³, which was notably larger than that of OKC lesions with a mean volume of 15.36 ± 13.74 cm³ (P = .002).

Additionally, the average length of AM lesions (39.35 ± 13.25 mm) was notably greater than that of OKC lesions (36.86 ± 15.13 mm, P = .009), while there were no significant differences in average width and height between AM and OKC. Moreover, the ratio of length-to-width (1.01 ± 0.03) and length-to-height (1.02 ± 0.03) in AM lesions were markedly greater than those (0.93 ± 0.03, P = .014; 0.95 ± 0.04, P = .022) in OKC lesions, respectively. However, no significant difference was found in the width-to-height ratio between AM and OKC.

Discussion

The radiographic and morphological characteristics of AM and OKC on CBCT are of great importance for their preoperative differential diagnosis. However, the required manual annotation is time-consuming and inefficient, particularly in large-scale datasets. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to adopt a fully automated approach, OCL-Net, for 3D segmentation and morphological analysis of a large number of jaw lesions, and to identify associations between morphological and radiographic features in AM and OKC. Our findings may contribute to a deeper understanding of AM and OKC for their preoperative differentiation in clinical practice.

Consistent with previous studies, we observed significant differences in most evaluated radiographic features between AM and OKC. Buccal (labial) expansion was the most common pattern in AMs (43.7%), in line with previous reports noting its predominance over lingual expansion.16 In contrast, the absence of buccal or lingual expansion was most frequently seen in OKCs (39%), reflecting their characteristic anteroposterior growth along the bone.28,29 These observations reinforce prior evidence that bone expansion patterns aid in differentiating these lesions.30 Additionally, root resorption and tooth displacement occurred far more frequently in AMs (51.0% and 36.4%) than in OKCs (14.0% and 23.8%), consistent with previous literature.15,17 Overall, AMs exhibit more aggressive radiographic characteristics than OKCs, which may assist clinicians in differential diagnosis and treatment planning.

In addition, significant morphological distinctions between AM and OKC were also revealed. The mean volume of AM cases was significantly larger than that of OKC cases (P = .002). This finding is consistent with the biological nature of AM as a tumour exhibiting more aggressive growth than OKC, a cystic lesion. However, Mohamed et al31 found similar lesion volumes in 31 unicystic ameloblastomas (UA) and 29 OKCs by using Mimics software for segmentation (P = .36). Similarly, Song et al32 reported that the difference in average volume between 17 OKCs and 10 UAs was not statistically significant (P = .121). These discrepancies may be attributed to small sample sizes, limitations in segmentation techniques, or variations among AM subtypes, as UA are generally less aggressive and smaller than solid or multicystic variants.33 Aside from volume, AMs also exhibited significantly greater length than OKCs, while no significant differences were found in width or height. This indicates that the volumetric difference is largely attributable to lesion length, with AMs showing significantly greater extension along the frontal axis than OKCs. Taken together, these quantitative morphological findings further provide evidence that AM exhibits more aggressive growth patterns compared to OKC.

As expected, buccolingual expansion patterns showed the largest effect sizes (η² = 0.224 and 0.284) for their associations with lesion volume in both AM and OKC, respectively. In contrast, although lesion location, border, and root resorption were statistically associated with lesion volume in both lesions, their effect sizes were relatively small to moderate, indicating that these features only explained a smaller proportion of volume variation. Notably, lesion shape showed moderate effect sizes in both AM and OKC, suggesting that shape was also closely associated with lesion volume in these two lesions.

In addition, several clinical and radiographic features were associated with the morphological parameters in only one of the two lesions, which may aid in differential diagnosis. A negative correlation between lesion volume and patient age was observed in OKC, with a similar but nonsignificant trend in AM. While no prior studies examined this association in AM, Lentzen et al34 reported that recurrent OKCs were larger in younger patients (6.32 ± 5.15 cm³) than in older patients (2.64 ± 2.03 cm³), supporting our findings. This may be attributed to age-related declines in jawbone vascularity and cellular activity, though the exact mechanism warrants further investigation. Additionally, OKC with cortical bone destruction were notably larger than those with continuous bone; while this feature was not associated with lesion size in AM, as most cases showed cortical disruption regardless of volume. This finding may reflect the difference in growth behaviour and biological aggressiveness previously discussed between the two lesions. Moreover, AM lesions containing impacted teeth were notably larger than those without, while there was a similar but nonsignificant trend in OKC. This may reflect the odontogenic nature of both AM and OKC, suggesting that the presence of the impacted tooth may create a favourable microenvironment for cyst growth.35,36 Additionally, mechanical interference from larger lesions may hinder tooth eruption, thereby contributing to this association.37 Notably, the lesion-tooth relationship showed a large effect size in AM (η² = 0.151) but not in OKC (η² = 0.028), supporting its potential value in differentiating these two lesions.

More interestingly, although lesion volume is related to the location in both AM and OKC, the AM lesions in the maxilla were greatly smaller than those in the mandible, whereas the opposite trend was observed for OKC. However, this did not fully align with previous reports that maxillary AMs are more invasive due to thinner cortical bone and looser cancellous structure.38 Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, it may be attributable to the limited number of maxillary AMs in our cohort. Second, although the maxilla has thinner bone, its anatomical complexity, particularly its proximity to the orbit and nasal cavity, may constrain lesion expansion and therefore lead to smaller volumes. Third, the previous conclusion was largely based on anatomical assumptions, whereas our study provides direct volumetric evidence through 3D segmentation, offering additional data-driven support.

However, this study had several limitations. First, as in previous studies,16,17 we did not restrict the pathological subtypes of AM in the inclusion criteria. However, due to the uneven distribution of samples, we did not perform a comparative analysis of radiographic and morphological features across different AM subtypes, such as UA and conventional AM. In our cohort, 76.8% of AM cases were classified as conventional AM, while the remaining were diagnosed as UA, consistent with previous studies.39 Future studies with more balanced subtype distributions are needed to refine these findings. Another limitation is that the sample source was homogeneous, lacking data from multiple centres. In addition, although the segmentation performance was evaluated using the DSC and mIoU, additional metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, precision, and recall were not included. These metrics should be included in future studies for a more comprehensive evaluation of the model. Furthermore, important clinical variables such as lesion duration and prior interventions were not included in this study, which may influence lesion size and radiographic characteristics, and therefore act as potential confounders in the analysis. Future studies incorporating more comprehensive clinical data are warranted to further validate our findings. In the future, we will incorporate multicentre and more comprehensive clinical data, further analyse the associations between radiographic characteristics and lesion volume across different AM subtypes, and ultimately develop a fully automated CBCT-based diagnostic system for the preoperative differentiation of AM and OKC.

Conclusion

A fully autosegmentation algorithm, OCL-Net, was proposed to facilitate comparisons of radiographic and morphological features between AMs and OKCs and to identify the associations between these features. Generally, OKCs show less aggressive radiographic features, whereas AMs exhibit larger lesion volumes and more extensive invasion along the frontal axis on CBCT. In addition, OKCs typically do not destroy surrounding tissues when the lesions are small, whereas AMs of smaller size rarely involve an impacted tooth. Overall, these findings may aid clinicians in the preoperative differentiation between AM and OKC.
