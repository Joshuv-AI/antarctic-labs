1.
Introduction

In extremal combinatorics, Turán type problems ask for determining the maximum possible size of a combinatorial object that does not contain a fixed forbidden object. An object is
rainbow
with respect to a coloring if all its elements receive distinct colors. Rainbow Turán problems (introduced for graphs by Keevash, Mubayi, Sudakov, and Verstraete
[
15
]
) ask for determining the maximum possible size of an object that does not admit rainbow forbidden objects with respect to any ’proper’ coloring. Another variant of rainbow problems, anti-Ramsey problems (introduced for graphs by Erdős, Simonovits, and Sós
[
7
]
), ask to maximize the number of colors used when coloring a big object under the condition that no rainbow forbidden small object appears.

We will be interested in the latter type of problems in the area of forbidden subposets. A family
{\mathcal{G}}
of sets is a
weak copy
of a poset
(P,\leqslant)
, if there exists a bijection
f:P\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}
such that
p\leqslant q
implies
f(p)\subseteq f(q)
, and
{\mathcal{G}}
is a strong copy of
(P,\leqslant)
, if there exists a bijection
f:P\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}
such that
p\leqslant q
if and only if
f(p)\subseteq f(q)
. With some abuse of notation, we will write
P
instead of
(P,\leqslant)
and say that a family
{\mathcal{F}}
is
weak (strong)
P
-free
if it does not contain weak (strong) copies of
P
. The
forbidden subposet problem
of determining
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,P)

(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,P))
, the maximum size of a weak (strong)
P
-free family
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq 2^{[n]}:=\{S:S\subseteq\{1,2\dots,n\}\}
. was introduced by Katona and Tarján
[
14
]
in the early 80s. For results and open problems, see the survey
[
9
]
and Chapter 7 of
[
8
]
.

Rainbow Turán forbidden problems were introduced and studied in
[
19
]
and another rainbow variant, Gallai-Ramsey numbers of poset pairs in
[
13
]
. In this paper, we start investigating the anti-Ramsey version of the forbidden subposet problem. Let
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar}(n,P)
(
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,P)
) denote the maximum number of colors used in a coloring of
2^{[n]}
that does not admit a rainbow weak (strong) copy of
P
. By definition, we immediately obtain the inequalities

(1)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar}(n,P)\leq\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,P)\hskip 28.45274pt\text{and}\hskip 28.45274pt\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,P)\leq\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,P),

as if a coloring of
2^{[n]}
uses more than
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,P)
(
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,P)
) colors, then the family we get by picking an arbitrary set from each color class contains a weak (strong) copy of
P
which is rainbow. The first inequality of (
1
) and an old result of Erdős
[
6
]
imply
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar}(n,P)=O(\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})
and the second inequality of (
1
) together with a result of Methuku and Pálvölgyi
[
17
]
imply
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,P)=O(\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})

Let us make further quick observations that improve the inequalities of (
1
) and that give lower bounds on
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar}(n,P)
and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,P)
and still connect the anti-Ramsey number to the original forbidden subposet problem. We need two concepts.
A family
{\mathcal{F}}
is said to be
convex
if
F\subseteq G\subseteq F^{\prime}
and
F,F^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}
imply
G\in{\mathcal{F}}
. Let
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La_{con}}(n,P)
(
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}_{con}}(n,P)
) denote the maximum size of a weak (strong)
P
-free convex family
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
.

For any poset
P
, we write
{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P)
to denote the set
\{P\setminus\{m\}:m~\text{is maximal or minimal in}\ P\}
. Also, for a set
{\mathcal{P}}
of posets, we say that the family
{\mathcal{F}}
is weak (strong)
{\mathcal{P}}
-free if it is weak (strong)
P
-free for all
P\in{\mathcal{P}}
.
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,{\mathcal{P}})
(
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,{\mathcal{P}})
denotes the maximum size of a weak (strong)
{\mathcal{P}}
-free family
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
.

Proposition 1.1
.

For any poset
P
, we have

(1)

1+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La_{con}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P))\leq\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar}(n,P)
,

(2)

1+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}_{con}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P))\leq\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,P)
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
be a convex weak (strong)
{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P)
-free family of size
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La_{con}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P)
(
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}_{con}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P)
). Color each
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
with distinct colors and use the same new color for all sets
2^{[n]}\setminus{\mathcal{F}}
. By the convexity of
{\mathcal{F}}
, sets of the new color can only be maximal or minimal elements in a weak (strong) rainbow copy of
P
, so the remaining sets of the copy should form a weak (strong) copy of a poset in
{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P)
within
{\mathcal{F}}
.
∎

As a corollary, we immediately obtain the following. Let
C_{k}
denote the chain (totally ordered set) on
k
elements. Then if
P
contains a weak copy of
2C_{2}
(two unrelated copies of
C_{2}
) or a copy of
C_{3}
, then
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,P),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar}(n,P)=\Theta(\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})
. Indeed, then any
P^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P)
contains a pair of elements in relation, so both
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}_{con}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P))
and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La_{con}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P))
are at least
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
as shown by the convex family of the middle layer of
2^{[n]}
.

Proposition 1.2
.

(1)

For any poset
P
, we have
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar}(n,P)\leq 2+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P))
.

(2)

If a poset
P
contains a smallest or largest element
m
, then
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,P)\leq 1+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,P\setminus\{m\})
.

Proof.

For any coloring
c
of
2^{[n]}
without a rainbow weak copy of
P
, pick one set from every color class of
c
except for the color classes of
\emptyset
and
[n]
. The family obtained must be
{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P)
-free, as such a copy is rainbow by definition, and can be extended by
\emptyset
or by
[n]
to obtain a rainbow weak copy of
P
. This proves (1).

If
m
is the largest element of
P
, then for any coloring
c
of
2^{[n]}
without a rainbow string copy of
P
, pick one set from every color class of
c
except for the color class of
[n]
. The family obtained must be
(P\setminus\{m\})
-free, as such a copy can be extended by
[n]
to obtain a rainbow strong copy of
P
. This proves (2).
∎

As a corollary, we obtain that if
P
does not contain a weak copy of
2C_{2}
nor a copy of
C_{3}
, then
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar}(n,P)=|P|-1
. Indeed, then
{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P)
contains the antichain
A_{k}
on
k=|P|-1
elements, and any
k
sets form a
weak
copy of
A_{k}
, so
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,A_{k})=k-1
.

All weak
\{2C_{2},C_{3}\}
-free posets
P
are of the form
P^{\prime}+A_{k}
, where
+
denotes the union of an uncomparable pair of copies of
P^{\prime}
and
A_{k}
with
P^{\prime}
being either a fork
\vee_{s}
(
a<b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{s}
) or a broom
\wedge_{s}
(
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}<d
).

Proposition 1.3
.

(1)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,\vee_{s})=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,\wedge_{s})=(s-1)(n-1)+2
.

(2)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,A_{k})=3+(k-2)(n-1)
if
n\geq 2k
.

(3)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,\wedge_{s}+A_{k})=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,\vee_{s}+A_{k})=O_{s,k}(n^{2})
.

For weak copies of
P
, the lower bound of Proposition
1.1
and the upper bound of Proposition
1.2
differ only by 1 and the possible difference of
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La_{con}}(n,P)
and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,P)
. In most cases, these are negligeable. For example, if
P=\Diamond
is the diamond poset on four elements
a,b,c,d
with
a<b,c<d
being all relations, then
{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(\Diamond)=\{\vee,\wedge\}
. A result of Katona and Tarján
[

]
states that
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,\{\vee,\wedge\})=2\binom{n-1}{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}
and an extremal family is
\binom{[n-1]}{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}\cup\{F\cup\{n\}:F\in\binom{[n-1]}{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}\}
, which is convex. (Here and throughout the paper,
\binom{S}{k}
denotes the family of all
k
-subsets of
S
, and
\binom{[n]}{k}
is often referred to as the
k
th layer of
2^{[n]}
.) So,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(\Diamond))=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La_{con}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(\Diamond))
. Finally, if in a coloring
c
of
2^{[n]}
the empty set and
[n]
receives distinct colors, then to avoid a rainbow weak copy of
\Diamond
, one can only use at most one further colors. Therefore,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar}(n,\Diamond)=1+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(\Diamond))=1+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La_{con}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(\Diamond))=1+2\binom{n-1}{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}
.

How far apart can
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P))
and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La_{con}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P))
be? It was conjectured
[
3
,
11
]
that for any set
{\mathcal{P}}
of posets,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,{\mathcal{P}})
(
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,{\mathcal{P}})
) is asymptotically the size of most number of middle layers of
2^{[n]}
that one can have without containing of weak (strong) copy of a member of
{\mathcal{P}}
. Note that the union of consecutive layers is always a convex family. The conjecture was disproved by Ellis, Ivan, and Leader
[
5
]
, who showed that
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,B_{d}),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,B_{d})\geq(d+\varepsilon-o(1))\binom{n}{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}
for

, where
B_{d}
is the Boolean poset on
2^{[d]}
ordered by inclusion. Although the construction of
[
5
]
for
B_{d}
can be easily turned into a construction for
B_{d}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
or for
B_{d}\setminus\{[d]\}
, we do not see how it could be turned into a construction that avoids both of them, i.e. a construction that is
{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(B_{d})
-free.

Problem 1.4
.

Is there a poset
P
and a positive real
\varepsilon
such that
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P))\geq(1+\varepsilon)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La_{con}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(P))

1.1.
Main results

We will focus on forbidding strong copies of
P
and on cases not covered by Proposition
1.2
(2), i.e. on posets having neither largest nor smallest elements.

The oriented Hasse diagram
\overrightarrow{H}(P)
of a poset
P
is an oriented graph with vertex set
P
where
\overrightarrow{pq}
is an arc if
q
covers
p
, i.e.
p\leqslant_{P}q
and there is no
z\neq p,q
with
p\leqslant_{P}z\leqslant_{P}q
. We obtain the (unoriented) Hasse diagram
H(P)
of
P
from
\overrightarrow{H}(P)
by dropping the orientation of all arcs. A poset is a
tree poset
if

is a tree.

Bukh proved
[
3
]

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,T)=(h(T)-1+o(1))\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
for any tree poset
T
, where
h(T)
denotes the
height of
T
, the length of the longest chain in
T
. This was strengthened by Boehnlein and Jiang
[
2
]
to
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,T)=(h(T)-1+o(1))\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
. As the construction showing the lower bound in these results, consists of the middle
h(T)-1
layers, which is a convex family, we obtain
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La_{con}}(n,T)=(1+o(1))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La}(n,T)=(1+o(1))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}_{con}}(n,T)=(1+o(1))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,T)
for all tree posets. Our first theorem determines
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,T)
asymptotically for all tree posets.

Note that if for all maximal or minimal elements of
T
we have
h(T\setminus\{t\})=h(T)
, then the result follows from
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,T)\leq\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,T)
of (
1
) and

of Proposition
1.1
(2). Also, if
T
has a largest or a smallest element, then the theorem follows from Proposition
1.1
(2) and Proposition
1.2
(2). So the hard part of the result is about tree posets
T
containing a minimal or maximal element
m
that is contained in all longest chains of
T
and yet
m
is neither smallest nor largest element of
T
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For any tree poset
T
, we have
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{ar^{*}}(n,T)=(1+o(1))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,{\mathcal{P}}^{-}(T))
.

The

[
4
]
. As

is a strong subposet of the tree poset
X

a_{1},a_{2}<c<b_{1},b_{2}

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{La^{*}}(n,\bowtie)=(2+o(1))\binom{n}{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}
. For even

, and the same result for odd

]
. The value of

As

The
Lubell-mass
of a family

is defined as

\lambda_{n}({\mathcal{F}})=\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{|F|}}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathbf{C}_{n}}|{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{F}}|,

the average number of sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
that a maximal chain contains chosen uniformly at random from

, the set of all maximal chains in

. We will consider the max-partition of

according to

, that is we write
{\mathbf{C}}_{F}=\{{\mathcal{C}}\in{\mathbf{C}}_{n}:F~\text{ is largest in}\ {\mathcal{F}}\cap{\mathcal{C}}\}
. Then
\frac{1}{|{\mathbf{C}}_{F}|}\sum_{{\mathcal{C}}\in{\mathbf{C}}_{F}}|{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{F}}|=\lambda_{|F|}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(F))
and
\lambda_{n}({\mathcal{F}})
is a weighted average of
\lambda_{|F|}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(F))
and so we have the following.

Observation 2.3
.

If
\lambda_{|F|}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(F))\leq B
for all

, then
\lambda_{n}({\mathcal{F}})\leq B
holds.

Lemma 2.4

(Griggs, Li, Lu, Lemma 3.2 in
[
10
]
)
.

For any family

, we have
|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\lambda_{n}({\mathcal{F}})\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
.

We will need the following folklore observation on minimum and average degree of graphs.

If

contains a subgraph

In the proof of Theorem

Definition 2.6

Let

We move some of the calculations of the proof of Theorem

Lemma 2.7
.

For

100k\leq j\leq 4\sqrt{n\ln n}
and

large enough, we have

(1)

\frac{\binom{n+2\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j}}{\binom{n-2\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j}}\leq n^{20}

(2)

\sum_{i=j/k}^{j}\binom{kn^{1/3}j}{i}\binom{n/2+2\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j-i}=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\ln n}}\binom{n/2-2n\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j-22}\right)

(3)

\binom{n^{2/3}+kn^{1/3}{j}}{j}=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\ln n}}\binom{n/2-2n\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j-22}\right)

Proof.

Using
1+x\leq e^{x}
, we obtain

\frac{\binom{n+2\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j}}{\binom{n-2\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j}}\leq\left(\frac{n-2\sqrt{n\ln n}}{n-6\sqrt{n\ln n}}\right)^{4\sqrt{n\ln n}}\leq e^{\frac{5\sqrt{n\ln n}}{n}\cdot 4\sqrt{n\ln n}}\leq n^{20}

which proves (1).

Using (1),
(\frac{n}{k})^{k}\leq\binom{n}{k}
,
\frac{\binom{n}{k}}{\binom{n}{k^{\prime}}}\leq(\frac{k^{\prime}}{n})^{k^{\prime}-k}
for
k<k^{\prime}
and
i\geq j/k\geq 1000
,
j\leq\sqrt{n\ln n}
, we obtain

\frac{\binom{kn^{1/3}j}{i}\binom{n/2+2\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j-i}}{\binom{n/2-2n\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j-22}}\leq\frac{n^{20}\binom{kn^{1/3}j}{i}\binom{n/2+2\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j-i}}{\binom{n/2+2n\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j-22}}\leq\frac{n^{20}(k^{2}n^{1/3})^{i}j^{i-22}}{n^{i-22}}\leq k^{2}n^{42}\left(\frac{j}{n^{2/3}}\right)^{i}=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

As there are at most
\sqrt{n\ln n}
terms in its left hand side, we obtain (2).

Using
(\frac{n}{k})^{k}\leq\binom{n}{k}\leq(\frac{en}{k})^{k}
, we have
\binom{n^{2/3}+kn^{1/3}{j}}{j}\leq(n^{2/3})^{j}
and
\binom{n/2-2n\sqrt{n\ln n}}{j-22}\geq(\frac{n}{10\sqrt{n\ln n}})^{j-22}
. As

and (3) follows.
