 Executive Summary:

 Robocasting and Oak Ridge National Laboratories (ORNL) proposed to reduce
 manufacturing costs of silicon carbide-silicon carbide (SiC/SiC) CMCs by increasing the
 efficiency of two significant steps in the fabrication process: 1) CMC preform layup and
 2) number of PIP cycles. The project advanced two manufacturing technologies that
 facilitate SiC/SiC CMC fabrication, reducing manufacturing costs by more than the
 required 20%. The first technology automated the CMC preform fabrication process via
 Additive Manufacturing (AM) with fiber tow placement by coextrusion of a
 polycarbosilane-resin-based matrix and fiber tows, and, coextrusion of water-based
 matrix and fiber tows. The automation of the fiber tow preforms with extrusion
 accelerated the preforming process, increased the uniformity of fiber placement, and
 enabled preforms with increased complexity as compared to State of the Art (SOTA)
 weaving and hand layups. The second technology improved the PIP densification
 process by increasing the yield of the polymer resin. The desired density was achieved in
 fewer PIP cycles. This provided a cost reduction by reducing furnace time and material
 capital. The combination of these two technologies provided a significant cost reduction
 in fabrication of SiC/SiC CMCs. The following table summarizes the manufacturing cost
 savings.

 Description SOTA Actual Reduction

            Time by >20%
 Reduce Densification 36 cycles 8 cycles 78%

 The series of advancements which build on each other throughout the project allowed for
 very good results and a unique method of Additively Manufacturing CMCs that has not
 previously been realized. Having a 3-axis machine print CMC’s layer-by-layer in
 complex shapes allows for many options for commercialization. As stated above, this
 manufacturing methods addresses AMTO’s goals of energy reduction for manufacturing
 by reducing the time/energy costs for manufacturing by 66% and the densification
 time/energy by 78%. This second (densification) benefit is very impressive as energy is
 constantly being used during the densification process in the form of heat.

 The most-relevant opportunity for commercialization is for use in nuclear shielding
 applications where cylindrical CMCs are used to shield components from a nuclear
 source. The fiber reinforcement allows for toughness. The matrix material can be
 modified with radiation-absorbing materials such as small amounts of boron nitride. The
 current status is “on hold” as this next step in the commercialization process requires
 another phase of funding.

1. Introduction

 Robocasting uses extrusion deposition of pastes to create parts layer-by-layer. Those
 parts are then dried and sintered to density. Oak Ridge has developed extensive
 chemistry knowledge towards improving the polymer infiltration and pyrolysis (PIP)
 method of densification. This method essentially densifies a ceramic part that cannot be
 further densified by conventional means by infiltration a liquid that turns into a ceramics
 upon heating, filling in any void space within the part. The advent of AM allows for
 complex shapes to be machine created using a variety of materials. To accomplish the
 goals shown above, the current SOTA for AM and PIP was advanced through a careful
 study and progression of smaller advancements. Those advancements include:
 1. Matrix Advancement
 a. Determining a matrix material (liquid constituent) that had a > 1-day shelf
 life, and could be modified to adjust purity and yields.
 b. Create a low-shrinkage, high solids matrix that is printable.
 c. Create a matrix that does not crack or warp upon curing.
 d. Create a matrix material that densifies to >90% density with PIP.
 2. PIP Advancement
 a. Chemically modify existing, low-cost preceramic polymer resins to have
 competitive yields with high-cost resins.
 3. Machine Advancement
 a. Modify an existing AM machine to coextrude a fiber tow and be able to cut
 that fiber tow in process.
 b. Modify existing software to easily create complex quasi-isotropic toolpaths on
 planar surfaces.
 4. Additively Manufacture simple SiC/SiC CMCs
 a. Coextrude matrix and tows as thin-walled structures.
 b. Densify CMCs to >90% density
 5. Improve PIP process
 a. Create a method to reduce PIP cycles by 20%.
 5

 6. Additively Manufacture complex SiC/SiC CMCs
 a. Print, cure, and densify small blocks on planar and non-planar surfaces.
 b. Print, cure, and densify large blocks on planar and non-planar surfaces.

2. Background

 CMCs are typically manufactured by running fiber tows through a coating process, pre-
 impregnation (prepreg) of polymer and particle slurry, weaving into 2D tapes or
 laminates, forming the 2D laminates into a layup, shaping and forming in molds and
 autoclaves, then densifying by either reactive melt infiltration (RMI), polymer
 impregnation and pyrolysis (PIP), chemical vapor infiltration (CVI), or a combination of
 these densification methods. Compared to the traditional methods of fiber tow
 prepregging, weaving into clothes, and shaping of layups; the proposed research is to
 combine some of these steps into an AM process for coated fiber tows, which increases
 the automation of fiber preforming and increases structural uniformity. Specifically, raw
 fibers are prepregged in situ by coextruding matrix and fiber tows through the printing
 nozzle. The fiber and matrix are coextruded onto the build platform to ‘print’ a net-
 shaped part. Matrix material is based on thermosetting resins (such as preceramic
 polymers) and is forced into the fiber tows while in the deposition head during the
 printing process. Previously-coated fiber tows can also be coextruded through the nozzle
 if desired. In-situ prepregging slurries will be developed to assist with both the adhesion
 and printing of fiber tows as well as high yield for less PIP cycles to full density. The
 final densification will be done with a PIP formulation that focuses on high yield and
 proper stoichiometry while reducing the number of PIP cycles from ~14 to ~3. These
 two technologies will reduce lead times for the entire CMC manufacturing process, and
 significantly reduce materials, labor, and energy costs.

 Robocasting has used additive manufacturing (AM) to produce ceramic parts for
 aerospace and other industries since 2007. Over 100 different ceramic, metal, and
 polymeric systems have been printed by the patented extrusion AM process. In addition
 to the final-part-design flexibility allowed by AM, the process has high throughput
 making it cost-competitive, and often less expensive than traditionally-produced
 ceramics. Robocasting has been expanding capabilities over the past several years with
 several multi-year projects sponsored by the DOD and DOE. These recent projects are
 utilizing high-temperature oxides (alumina, zirconia, mullite) and high-thermal-
 conductivity, high-strength carbides (silicon carbide, boron carbide). The projects have
 centered on AM of SiC mirrors, heat exchangers, and ceramic armor. Most relevantly to
 this proposal, Robocasting has proven the capability to produce continuous fiber, oxide-
 oxide CMCs with this AM technology, pictures are shown in the Feasibility section
 below. Robocasting is prepared to add these products to its current production lines and
 is excited to partner with ORNL on another production opportunity for the production of
 continuous-fiber CMCs.

 ORNL is home to the Manufacturing Demonstration Facility (MDF), focusing on
 research, development, and application of advanced manufacturing technologies. MDF
 has expertise in a wide variety of additive manufacturing (AM) processes and has
 pioneered AM technologies like BAAM (Big Area Additive Manufacturing). ORNL has
 6

 a long history and unique domain expertise in ceramic science, ceramic vapor synthesis
 and fabrication of CMC’s. The DOE CFCC program (1992-2002) was a 10-year
 approximately $100 million collaborative effort among industry, national laboratories,
 academia and government, and it played a key role in developing the materials and
 architecture of the current state of the art CMCs. The goals of the program were to
 advance processing methods, establish an infrastructure of supporting technologies,
 assess user needs, and achieve suitable properties. ORNL managed the CFCC program
 for DOE, providing it with a pivotal role, experience and knowledge with regards to the
 development of CMCs. ORNL also led R&D activity in several key areas, including
 chemical vapor infiltration (CVI) of ceramic matrices, development of CVI fiber
 interface coatings, understanding of interface microstructures, composite system
 properties and mechanics, as well as oxidation testing of protective coatings in water
 vapor.

images of the monolith’s fracture surface. One noticeable feature was a crack that propagated

diminishing the mechanical properties of a sample.

4. Key Achievements

 - A novel method of AM method for SiC/SiC was developed whereby a ceramic fiber tow
 is coextruded with a paste-like matrix material. To our knowledge, this has never been
 done before. There are methods for carbon fibers and resins but nothing with true high-
 temperature ceramics.
 - In developing the matrix material, particle size distribution was manipulated to achieve
 70 vol% solids, which can only usually be accomplished with spherical, perfectly-sized
 particles. The particles utilized in this study are typical SiC which are jagged and oddly-
 shaped. This allows for ~1% shrinkage from the printed/green state to the sintering state.
 If the matrix were to shrink, cracking would be induced during densification as the matrix
 shrinks but the fiber tows do not.
 - High-yield preceramic polymer was developed using COTS PCP. This material is <10X
 the cost of high-yield PCPs which is very significant.
 - A patent has been filed covering the COTS PCP modification invention.
 - Process energy reduction was accomplished. Target goals were a reduction of 20% for
 manufacturing and 20% for densification. Reduction in manufacturing was 66% (3X more
 than target), and reduction in densification was 78% (almost 4X more than the target).
 These are significant in terms of production speed and energy.
 - Several presentations and papers have been presented throughout the ceramics
 manufacturing community.
 - Both ORNL and Robocasting have provided significant advancements to the scientific
 community for developing these materials. However, the short-term commercialization
 opportunities have not shown themselves. This is a fairly revolutionary technology and it
 will take time to gain acceptance in the CMC community as the current technologies have
 been in place for decades and are well established. When looking for the next funding
 sources (hypersonics, nuclear, high-temperature applications, etc.) the typical starting point
 for most prime subcontractors and government entities looking to advance their application
 starts with these well-established technologies. This is the major challenge at the moment.

5. Benefits Assessment

 As previously stated, there is significant savings in manufacturing and densification costs
 (66% and 78% respectively). The most significant impact on the future will most-likely
 be the patented PIP modification. It will be difficult to get manufacturers to adopt an AM
 technique, but a simple substitute of PIP material which is 10X less-expensive, gives

 47

 similar yields, and improves densification times is certainly attractive to all those
 attempting to densify SiC.

6. Lessons Learned

 This was a very difficult project from many aspects with a multitude of basic building
 blocks needing to be determined before the final parts could be attempted. It was a
 year to develop the matrix material. A year development was thought to be
 conservative at the time but in retrospect, was needed. Having this foundation allowed
 for solid advancements.
 Presenting at research/technical conferences was good for spreading information about
 the new technology. However, with technology this interesting, yet immature,
 presenting at some of the more established application conferences might have yielded
 more interest in the technology allowing for more opportunity.

7. Conclusions

 This program made major steps into fabrication and densification of SiC/SiC CMCs. The
 manufacturing time benefits and densification time benefits are illustrated below.

 Description SOTA Actual Reduction
 Reduce Manufacturing 6 days 2 days 66%
     Time by >20%
 Reduce Densification 36 cycles 8 cycles 78%
     Time by >20%

 The series of advancements which build on each other throughout the project allowed for
 very good results and a unique method of Additively Manufacturing CMCs that has not
 previously been realized. Having a 3-axis machine print CMC’s layer-by-layer in
 complex shapes allows for many options for commercialization. As stated above, this
 manufacturing methods addresses AMTO’s goals of energy reduction for manufacturing
 by reducing the time/energy costs for manufacturing by 66% and the densification
 time/energy by 78%. This second (densification) benefit is very impressive as energy is
 constantly being used during the densification process in the form of heat.

8. Future Work/Recommendations

 Future work continues to be searching for follow-on development funding. The
 immaturity of the technology needs to be incrementally tested in smaller applications
 before it can go into the originally-proposed aerospace turbine engine shroud.
 Opportunities like fiber-reinforced crack-filling material to seal windows or joints in
 hypersonic vehicles for example. Currently, Robocasting is utilizing short fibers in high-
 temperature applications for DoD-related projects with prime subcontractors.
