Background

Advancements in genome engineering have revolutionized our ability to model human disease, interrogate gene function, and develop precision therapeutics. Among the most promising tools is prime editing—a CRISPR-derived technology capable of introducing precise insertions, deletions, and base substitutions without requiring double-strand breaks or donor DNA templates [1]. Despite its precision, prime editing remains technically challenging in certain cell types, particularly human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), which are notoriously resistant to genome editing due to their hypersensitivity to DNA damage and robust mismatch repair mechanisms [2, 3]. To address these challenges, we present a detailed protocol leveraging the PINE-TREE system (Prime-Induced Nucleotide Engineering using a Transient Reporter for Editing Enrichment), a fluorescence-based enrichment strategy previously developed in Frisch et al. 2023, that significantly improves the efficiency and recovery of prime-edited clones in hPSCs [4].

Briefly, this PINE-TREE-based method utilizes a transient, episomal fluorescent reporter to identify and isolate cells via flow cytometry that have undergone precise single-nucleotide edits at specific genomic loci (Fig. 1). PINE-TREE couples prime editing activity with a fluorescent reporter that directly reflects editing at a co-transfected episomal construct. In this system, a blue fluorescent protein (BFP) reporter is engineered to convert to green fluorescent protein (GFP) upon successful prime editing, such that only cells in which the prime editor and pegRNA are functionally active display GFP signal. These reporter-positive cells can be isolated by flow cytometry, and because reporter activation directly reflects prime editing activity within the same cell, the sorted GFP-positive population exhibits significantly higher frequencies of on-target genomic edits compared with unsorted populations or enrichment strategies based on conventional reporters of transfection. In contrast to approaches that rely on fluorescently labeled editor or reporter plasmids which confirm plasmid delivery or expression but do not distinguish cells with active or productive editing, PINE-TREE functions as a direct reporter of genome editing, enabling selective enrichment of cells in which the editing machinery is functionally engaged. This distinction reduces the inclusion of cells that have taken up plasmids but failed to undergo editing, thereby improving enrichment efficiency and downstream clonal recovery. As a result, PINE-TREE enables efficient isolation of cells harboring desired substitutions, insertions, and deletions at genomic target loci and facilitates generation of clonal isogenic lines, particularly in challenging cell types such as human pluripotent stem cells, where recently reported strategies achieving similar efficiencies have required stable integration of a prime editor or multiple rounds of transfection [5, 6]. This protocol provides a detailed methodology for utilizing a fluorescence-based prime editing enrichment system to enhance genome modifications in hPSCs. The outlined procedure includes target-site pegRNA cloning, hPSC culture and expansion, transfection, and downstream validation (Fig. 2).

Off-target effects and indel formation, although generally reduced in prime editing compared to conventional CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing, remain an important consideration—particularly in therapeutic or stem cell applications where genomic integrity is critical. Unlike Cas9 nucleases, which introduce double-stranded breaks (DSBs) and can lead to undesired editing, prime editors operate through a nickase-based mechanism and a reverse transcriptase, resulting in fewer unintended insertions, deletions, or rearrangements [1]. Likewise, we have previously shown that PINE-TREE does not increase the presence of indels in edited cell populations [4]. However, the pegRNA and associated nicking sgRNA can still mediate off-target activity, especially in genomic regions with high sequence homology or open chromatin [7, 8]. In PINE-TREE enriched populations, we report minimal off-target effects, but further investigation is warranted as additional prime editing modalities are employed and utilized across diverse genomic loci [4]. To that end, several in silico tools such as CCTop [9], CRISPOR [10], and ge-CRISPR [11] can help predict and validate potential off-target loci. More recently, tools use as PINE-CONE [12], DeepPrime [8], PE-Designer [13], and pegFinder [14], have been developed specifically for prime-editing applications to predict pegRNA efficiency as well as minimize off-target editing for pegRNA and nicking sgRNA. In this protocol, we outline how PINE-CONE can be used in conjunction with PINE-TREE to facilitate the design of pegRNAs and corresponding nicking sgRNAs. PINE-CONE is the recommended system as it generates validated pegRNA and nick sgRNA sequences with the correct overhangs ready for cloning.

The PINE-TREE method currently utilizes PE2 and PE3b systems—both validated for use in hPSCs—the higher editing efficiency of PE3, which employs an additional nicking sgRNA, makes it particularly advantageous [1]. Recent advancements in prime editing, including PEmax, PE4, PE5, PE6, and PE7, can be incorporated into the PINE-TREE workflow to potentially improve editing performance [7, 15, 16]. Additionally, optimizing pegRNA design and screening multiple constructs in bulk sorts may further increase editing success rates in clonal hPSC populations.

PINE-TREE offers broad adaptability across various prime editing technologies and cell types. Although originally developed and validated in hPSCs, the PINE-TREE approach holds promise for broader application across mammalian cell types, including immortalized lines and primary cells. Furthermore, the system may be adaptable for use in differentiated progeny derived from pluripotent cells—such as neurons, cardiomyocytes, and hepatocytes—enabling modeling of tissue-specific variants and phenotypes. Overall, by enabling efficient generation of precisely edited clonal cell population, PINE-TREE facilitates the generation of isogenic cell lines for use in developmental biology, disease modeling, and regenerative medicine. Integration with next-generation editing systems, improved delivery methods, and broader cell-type applications will continue to expand its utility in basic biology as well as translational research.

Procedure

Overview of protocol

Generating isogenic hPSC lines using the PINE-TREE system consists of four main stages: (i) design and cloning of pegRNAs using PINE-CONE software (Steps 1–13), (ii) multiplexing pegRNA and nsgRNA into the target reporter vector (Steps 14–25), (iii) transfection and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for single-cell isolation (Steps 26–72), and (iv) clonal expansion, genotyping validation, and characterization of edited cell lines (Steps 73–99). This protocol enables efficient prime editing enrichment using a transient reporter system and includes troubleshooting strategies for optimizing editing efficiency.

Design of target site pegRNAs using PINE-CONE (steps 1–4)

There are multiple strategies for designing the target site pegRNA for use with the PINE-TREE system, supported by a variety of publicly available software tools [8, 12–14]. Key design parameters—including the length of the primer binding site (PBS), the reverse transcriptase template (RTT), and the proximity and orientation of the protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM)—can significantly influence pegRNA efficiency [1]. To streamline this process, we describe an automated approach using PINE-CONE, a publicly available software tool that enables the design of multiple pegRNA variants based on user-defined inputs [12]. PINE-CONE is compatible with a range of prime editing systems, including PE2, PE3, and PE3b, and generates validated pegRNA and nicking sgRNA(nsgRNA) sequences ready for cloning, making it the recommended design method for use with PINE-TREE. Alternatively, pegRNAs may be designed manually, though this requires careful annotation of the target region and identification of suitable PAM sites surrounding the intended edit.

The ability to utilize a PE3b or PE5b system depends on the relative positioning of the PAM to the desired edit site. These systems are generally preferred over conventional PE3 and PE5 approaches because they direct nicking of the non-edited DNA strand only after the reverse-transcribed strand has been successfully edited, thereby reducing the frequency of indel byproducts and improving overall editing efficiency. To maximize success in generating clonal isogenic cell lines, we recommend screening multiple pegRNA designs, comparing different prime editing strategies, and considering the introduction of silent mutations to disrupt PAM sites or improve pegRNA performance. Cloning of pegRNA and nsgRNA intermediates (steps 5–14)

Construction of the final dual-targeting vector involves an intermediate cloning step to insert the same U6 small nuclear RNA promoter upstream of both the pegRNA and nsgRNA sequences (Fig. 4). This step introduces a U6 promoter upstream of both the pegRNA(TS) and nsgRNA(TS), enabling their subsequent multiplex insertion into the PE-pegRNA(BG)-nsgRNA(BG) vector to generate the final plasmid construct. A two-step cloning strategy enables modular assembly of pegRNA and nsgRNA components while also improving cloning efficiency by separating guide construction from the final experssion vector that contains multiple U6 promoters. Sequence-validated intermediate guides can also be readily available to test in different combinations for the highest editing efficiency.

Cloning strategy for generating pegRNA(TS) and nsgRNA(TS) intermediates. This figure outlines the stepwise construction of pegRNA(TS) and nsgRNA(TS) intermediates, which are subcloned into the pHSG1C3 vector downstream of a U6 promoter. The process consists of five stages: (1) duplexing of the oligonucleotides corresponding to the guide, scaffold, and 3′ extension sequences, (2) preparation of the pHSG1C3 vector via restriction digest using BbsI and/or PstI restriction sites and dephosphorylation, (3) ligation of duplexed oligos into the prepared vector, (4) transformation of ligation products into competent E. coli, and (5) colony screening followed by plasmid purification for sequence verification

Once designed, oligonucleotides for the target site pegRNA(TS)—including the guide, scaffold, and 3′ extension—as well as the corresponding nsgRNA(TS) can be synthesized as paired oligonucleotides and duplexed into the pHSG1C3 vector, which contains a U6 promoter (Fig. 4). It is recommended that cloning of the pegRNA(TS) and nsgRNA(TS) intermediates be performed in parallel to streamline the workflow and facilitate efficient assembly of the complete editing vector.

Assembly of the final PE-pegRNA(DT)-nsgRNA(DT) vector. This figure outlines the assembly of the final PE-pegRNA(TS)-nsgRNA(TS) expression plasmid. General notes and troubleshooting

A commonly encountered challenge during cloning for the PINE-TREE system is the absence of colonies following transformation, which may reflect a failure in one or more upstream molecular cloning steps. Incomplete digestion of the target vector, inadequate dephosphorylation of digested ends, and incompatible or improperly designed oligonucleotides can each contribute to this outcome. To minimize these issues, it is recommended that researchers perform multiplexed in situ reactions prior to large-scale cloning to ensure compatibility of pegRNA/nsgRNA inserts with vector overhangs. In silico cloning tools, such as SnapGene or Benchling, can be used to visualize digestion sites and simulate ligation outcomes, greatly reducing the risk of incompatible overhangs or vector re-ligation. In particular, cloning reactions that rely on SapI and HindIII require precise design of sticky ends, and failure to include appropriate overhangs in synthetic oligonucleotides can lead to reduced ligation efficiency.

While the PINE-TREE protocol has been optimized for Lipofectamine™ Stem transfection, which offers an efficient chemical transfection method for a wide range of hPSC lines, this approach has some limitations. Lipofectamine-based delivery is known to suffer from batch variability, poor DNA complex stability, and cytotoxicity in certain sensitive cell types [19]. Although lipofectamine was selected for its compatibility with various culture media and high delivery efficiency in hPSCs, alternative methods such as nucleofection, electroporation, or viral delivery might be beneficial in contexts requiring lower toxicity or higher throughput [19]. In particular, recent developments in viral-based prime editor delivery systems for in vivo applications offer potential alternative approaches to in vitro chemical transfection [20]. Each of these alternative delivery strategies would require protocol-specific optimization, including titration of ratio of editing components and validation of efficiency.

Another issue that may arise is low or inconsistent transfection efficiency. If BFP-positive cells are not observed 24 hours after transfection, this may indicate poor plasmid quality, inefficient complexation, or transcriptional silencing of the episomal reporter. Plasmid integrity should be verified via restriction digest and Sanger sequencing prior to use. Additionally, ensuring that endotoxin-free plasmid preparations are used is essential for transfecting sensitive cells like hPSCs. If BFP-positive cells are observed with no GFP expression, this suggests that transfection occurred but prime editing did not take place. In this case, it may be necessary to assess pegRNA efficacy, ensure correct stoichiometry between the prime editor and guide constructs, and confirm the presence of a compatible PAM at the target site. Transfection toxicity—evidenced by widespread cell death and reduced colony integrity—may result from excessive lipid-to-DNA ratios or poor cell health at the time of transfection. This can often be mitigated by plating cells at higher densities, using ROCK inhibitor to support survival post-transfection, or reducing the total amount of DNA used in the reaction.

The rate of successful prime editing is influenced by multiple factors including pegRNA architecture, length and sequence of the RTT and PBS, the GC content of the target region, transcriptional activity at the target locus, chromatin accessibility, and the characteristics of the hPSC line itself [15, 21]. Furthermore, hPSCs have inherently reduced DNA repair activity in some pathways, limiting the efficiency of homology-based repair mechanisms including those utilized during prime editing. To that end, it is recommended that researchers perform bulk editing and flow cytometry-based sorting prior to isolating clones to determine whether a given pegRNA construct is functional. The use of engineered pegRNA (epegRNA) designs, which incorporate structured 3′ stabilizing RNA motifs, has been shown to improve prime editing efficiencies by reducing degradation and enhancing editing complex stability [15]. Moreover, use of the PEmax architecture, which includes codon-optimized reverse transcriptase domains and improved nuclear localization signals, has been reported to significantly increase the yield of intended edits while minimizing indels [15]. When combined with epegRNAs and a PE3 system employing a nicking sgRNA in the context of PINE-TREE, editing success can be improved, even at genomic loci previously considered refractory to editing.

Expected results

The PINE-TREE system has been demonstrated to facilitate efficient prime editing in both HEK293T cells and hPSCs across multiple genomic loci, enabling the generation of isogenic cell lines with single-nucleotide changes, small insertions, or deletions [4]. The PINE-TREE system has been successfully employed to generate multiple isogenic cell lines from both hPSCs and HEK293T cells across a variety of genomic loci [4]. Upon successful transfection of hPSCs, distinct populations of BFP+, GFP+, and BFP+/GFP+ cells are typically observed by fluorescence microscopy within 24–48 hours. These populations represent unedited transfected cells (BFP+), prime-edited cells (GFP+), and cells in which both the episomal reporter and prime editing machinery are active (BFP+/GFP+). The frequency of GFP+ or BFP+/GFP+ cells varies depending on several factors, including the editing architecture used (e.g., PE2, PE3, or PE3b), the specific genomic target site, the sequence and stability of the pegRNA, and the parental cell line. PE3b-based strategies typically yield higher frequencies of edited cells compared to PE2 or PE3, likely due to the strand-specific nicking mechanism that enhances reverse transcription completion and reduces competition with endogenous DNA repair pathways. For example, in hPSCs edited using PINE-TREE with PE3b, up to 5–10% of cells may be BFP+/GFP+ by 72 hours post-transfection. Importantly, among the GFP-positive population enriched by FACS, editing efficiencies can reach 80% in bulk-sorted cell populations, as validated by Sanger sequencing or next-generation sequencing of the genomic target site

Following FACS-based isolation of dual-positive cells and their plating into 96-well plates for clonal expansion, the expression of both BFP and GFP reporters is transient. The PINE-TREE reporter system is based on non-integrating episomal plasmids, which are gradually lost over successive cell divisions. Typically, by 16 to 20 days post-transfection, edited clones no longer exhibit detectable fluorescence by microscopy or flow cytometry, consistent with plasmid loss and the establishment of stable, transgene-free edited lines. Following single-cell cloning, editing efficiency in isolated clonal cell populations is typically consistent with that observed in bulk-sorted GFP+ cells, with most clones harboring the intended edit in either a heterozygous or homozygous state. Depending on the cell line, transfection efficiency, and plating conditions, outgrowth can be expected from approximately 10–40 wells per 96-well plate, yielding sufficient clones for downstream validation and expansion.

Edited hPSC clones generated using PINE-TREE retain hallmark properties of pluripotency. Specifically, clones should retain characteristic hPSC morphology and remain positive for key pluripotency markers such as OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG. Additionally, clonal lines should be capable of differentiating into all three germ layers (i.e. ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm) under spontaneous differentiation protocols. Cytogenetic analysis of successfully edited clones should reveal a normal euploid karyotype, consistent with the preservation of genomic integrity during the editing and expansion process. Lastly, with any CRISPR-based genome editing protocol, it is strongly recommended that predicted off-target loci—identified via tools such as CCTop—be empirically assessed in each clonal line using targeted sequencing prior to downstream functional assays or differentiation protocols.
