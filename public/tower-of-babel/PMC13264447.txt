Good things come in threes: evaluating clinical utility of machine learning-derived clusters
Authors: Daniil Lisik, Jip W T M De Kok, Nicolás Bermúdez Barón, Lowie E G W Vanfleteren, Bright I Nwaru, Rani Basna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMachine learning (ML)-based cluster analysis is a common method for subtyping medical conditions and presentations of disease states. Recent advancements in algorithms and increasing access to vast healthcare data have further increased the use of such ML models. However, practical implementation is lacking, largely due to methodological limitations and insufficient reporting and clinical contextualization in the extant literature, with no existing guidelines for this purpose.ObjectiveTo propose a general framework to assess and ensure clinical utility of ML-derived clusters.DiscussionThe proposed framework encompasses three domains, focusing on clinical relevance, stability and generalizability, and ease of identification, enabling researchers and readers to ensure clinical utility of ML-generated subgroups.

Artificial intelligence (AI), specifically machine learning (ML), has become widely used to derive clinical subtypes. Such grouping (clustering) may be produced either through single (cross-sectional) or repeated (longitudinal) assessments (trajectory analysis).1–4 One of the main strengths of ML in this context is the ability to “learn” non-apparent (latent) patterns in complex data without explicit labeling or instructions.5 This enables a data-driven approach for discovering distinct subgroups, contrasting with conventional methods based on clinical experience/experiments and manual labeling, which are constrained by the limited number of characteristics that can be considered simultaneously.6,7 However, subjective clinical judgment remains necessary at each step of ML-based clustering pipelines to ensure clinical relevance, particularly when selecting between models that are similar in terms of quantitative validation measures.

The clinical impact of cluster analyses has thus far been modest due to methodological limitations, insufficient data (quality), lack of clinical contextualization (not least assessment of association to meaningful outcomes), and incomplete reporting. Although there are numerous guidelines for cluster analyses from a technical standpoint, there is no guideline on how to evaluate clinical utility of ML-generated clusters. In this perspective, we propose a framework to aid readers in assessing clinical utility of ML-based clustering papers. Likewise, this framework may guide researchers to ensure clinical utility of their clustering studies, with guidance on both technical aspects such as model choices and evaluation as well as approaches for clinical contextualization. An overview of this framework is presented in Figure 1 and a checklist to be used for practically assessing clinical utility can be found in Table 1. A short description of technical terms used in this paper is provided in Table 2.

1. Clinical relevance

From a broader perspective, the very purpose of the study needs to be considered and clearly described, as this greatly affects all downstream steps and the output. For example, a cluster analysis may be performed to stratify cancer patients by survival, or to produce distinct phenotypic subgroups of asthma. The very nature of these two analyses renders the derived subgroups to be interpreted and used in very different ways.

The clinical context in which an ML model is applied has implications for generalizability and which types of subgroups may be identified. Therefore, it is essential to select an appropriate study sample. For example, cluster analyses focusing specifically on asthma patients enable the algorithm to discern finer nuances in the input data and subsequently facilitate identification of detailed subtypes of the disease, useful for precision medicine.8–10

The clinical context (and hence the model output) also depends on the research question and input data. For example, asthma subgroups may be derived based on data collected through multiple follow-ups, e.g., lung function progression11 or data collected at one time point, e.g., a single assessment of comorbidities.10 These cluster analyses will identify different asthma subgroups within the same population due to the different input variables and data structures. More importantly, however, the clustering should differentiate individuals by clinically relevant outcomes or characteristics, e.g., treatment response, disease events, or death.12

Preferably, clinically relevant clusters should also provide actionable insights (e.g., differences in influential lifestyle factors). Assessment of clinical relevance may be done through tabulation/plotting of cluster characteristics and association analyses (e.g., regression models) using subgroup labels (preferably with a covariate indicating the degree of certainty in label assignment) as independent variable.13 Decision curve analysis (DCA) may then be used to gain insight into net clinical benefit across various decision thresholds. Other means of assessing clinical utility can be to compare treatment response between derived subgroups, model cost/resource utilization projections, etc. Beyond understanding whether a subgroup provides meaningful information for preventing or managing disease, assessing clinical relevance may aid in unearthing potential disease mechanisms to be used for development of preventive measures or treatment strategies. Involvement of clinical domain experts is critical for this process.

2. Stability and generalizability

No ML algorithm is immune to overfitting, which occurs when the model does not generalize/perform well on unseen data (i.e., other populations),14 e.g., due to a limited sample size or over-partitioning (too many subgroups).15 Overfitting may also occur prior to the actual clustering, for example when training a neural network to reduce dimensionality of the input data. Stability and generalizability both indicate that identified subgroups are not artifacts of a specific clustering model but rather can be reproduced despite sampling variation in variables or study populations. The difference between these two complementary aspects lies in that stability measures whether (essentially) the same clusters can be recovered with slightly changing the data, whereas generalizability tells if similar clusters appear in other cohorts/populations. The most clinically informative assessment of generalizability is external validation–testing the model or an equivalent one using an independent population with comparable data definitions but somewhat different demographic/clinical characteristics–to confirm subgroup robustness in real-world settings. However, often such data are not available. In these cases, at least stability should be assessed, e.g., by resampling the data and measuring the Jaccard coefficient across clusters (cluster-wise stability).16 It is also beneficial to calculate participant-wise stability, by measuring how often participants end up in the same cluster across resampled datasets, which can provide a more easily interpretable estimate of stability.17 As stability naturally weakens with increasing number of subgroups, alternative approaches, e.g., quantification of prediction strength (accuracy of cluster assignment using cluster centers between splits of the data), with the highest number of subgroups above a specified threshold, may also be used.18

The selection of input variables and algorithmic weight given to each of them are also highly influential in regard to stability and generalizability. For example, including correlated variables may “bias” the algorithm toward the distribution of these features and result in noise therein to be mistaken for relevant subgroup structure (and thereby lower robustness). Over-influence of variables may also reduce generalizability if they reflect context-specific exposures which are rare elsewhere, emphasizing a small proportion of individuals, etc. Nevertheless, such variables should not necessarily be avoided, especially if they capture relevant nuance. Instead, dimensionality reduction techniques, such as embedding methods, can be used to retain essential data structures and relationships. Additionally, applying weights to variable groups can adjust their relative influence, although caution is needed to avoid confirmation bias. These strategies, if chosen appropriately for the context, can help ensure that the most clinically relevant and distinguishable subgroups are identified.

3. Ease of identification

A limitation of ML-based methods for deriving subgroups is that they will generally always produce an output, even if the data does not contain sensible subgroups.19,20 Further, cluster analyses do not necessarily generate the most clinically relevant subgroups if the only basis for selecting the optimal number of subgroups is a quantifiable validation measure. For example, in data with natural overlap, certain metrics may be unreliable.21 In general, each internal validation metric weighs characteristics of subgroups differently, e.g., size, compactness, separation, and noise.21 Therefore, relying on a single measure may produce biased results. Instead, multiple relevant validation metrics should be used to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of different statistical aspects. Although validation/quality metrics may provide valuable information, they should merely constitute part of the assessment, together with evaluation from a clinical (“subjective”) perspective. For example, it is important in clinical contexts to be able to reliably and easily classify individual patients into fitting subgroups. If clusters are generalizable and clinically relevant, the importance of cluster separation (internal validity) is lessened. However, in such cases, soft clustering techniques (e.g., fuzzy c-means)22 might be preferable, given the absence of clear cluster boundaries.

Ease of identification can be assessed from an algorithmic perspective, corroborating clinical identifiability. Probabilistic (e.g., finite mixture models)23 and soft clustering algorithms provide such information intrinsically by measures of precision/certainty in cluster label assignments, but similar measures may also be derived post-hoc, indirectly, with supervised ML models such as naïve Bayes classifiers,24 using cluster labels as target variable. A measure of algorithmic certainty can provide further insight into cluster separation and whether there are participants whose characteristics are in-between several subgroups. Supervised ML models can additionally be used to gain insight into how the clusters were formed. So-called “white-box” ML algorithms, of which the decision-making process is understandable to humans, e.g., decision trees,25 can be used directly by training on prediction of cluster membership. Thereafter, assessment of the supervised model decision criteria may unearth likely influential variables and combinations of variable values for the clustering algorithm. A more in-depth evaluation of the influence (and direction) of individual variables may be attained with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).26 SHAP can be coupled to any supervised learning algorithm, including more advanced black-box models, which may increase accuracy. Further, SHAP may be used to assess likely reasons for subgroup assignment at participant-level. Specific variable subsets (e.g., the n most influential variables as determined by a supervised algorithm, such as random forest)27 may also be evaluated to assess how well they can predict cluster labels for (unseen/“new”) participants, which may facilitate use by those without access to the original clustering model.

Clinically assessing which variables are most influential for differentiating each subgroup–and not least the degree of separation–provides further information about the distinctiveness of the subgroups. For example, if two clusters share a large proportion of important variables, this may indicate important contrasting groups, reflected by diametral distributions of sex, biomarkers, etc. However, it may also be found with less clinically relevant (subtle) differences.28

Overall homogeneity of subgroup-defining variables within clusters (and difference in-between clusters) can be evaluated with e.g., heatmaps for categorical variables and density/violin/box plots for continuous variables. Tables of characteristics and simple statistical tests are also useful for this. Clusters should preferably differ by subgroup-defining variables in clinically meaningful ways from two perspectives: (1) the difference in variable distributions should be sufficient for separation of individuals by these factors to make clinical sense (e.g., if the average height in two clusters is 170 cm and 175 cm, the difference may be statistically different but clinically irrelevant); (2) the differences explaining the clustering should be possible to boil down to simple-enough sets of variables such that clinicians–without overly burdensome examination procedures or time-consuming assessments–can classify individuals to likely subgroups. For example, if a cluster analysis is utilizing computed tomography (CT) scans, blood work, and a set of questionnaire responses, and the latter variables, together with individual blood markers, suffice to accurately assign individuals, the clinical utility of these phenotypes is strengthened, given the radiation exposure and cost associated with performing CT scans. This assessment of clinically meaningful differences may be streamlined by first identifying the top n influential variables as per above.

Although ease of identification has clear benefits from a clinical implementation standpoint, it has also been argued that it is ethically justifiable to use an ML model which is not (fully) explainable, but which outperforms clinicians and which can be fully validated.29,30 Similarly, it has been reported that people from the general population, in healthcare-related contexts (e.g., stroke diagnosis), prefer accuracy over explainability.31 In contexts where data privacy laws/regulations allow for making such models available to end-users and where associated outcomes are of substantial importance, it may be sensible to down-prioritize ease of identification,32 although generally, it is likely most useful to aim at a balance between simplicity of “manually” identifying phenotypes on one hand, and clinical utility of subgroup-defining variables as well as overall context/benefit for patients on the other. Generally, explainability should only be sacrificed for significant performance gain. A closely related concept, interpretability, i.e., understanding of inner mechanisms of an ML model, is useful for e.g., debugging, but in the context of clinically oriented research, high-level explainability and empirical validation is usually sufficient.32

Transparency and co-operation between clinicians and methodologists

A key component for all the abovementioned points is transparency in reporting methodological choices. Well-established reporting guidelines and checklists for many other ML-based studies, such as TRIPOD+AI for prediction models.33 This, however, is not the case for cluster analysis studies, although a dedicated guideline and checklist is currently under development34 and a reporting guideline (GRoLTS) exists for longitudinal ML-based phenotyping studies.35 In the meantime, general guidelines on reporting ML-based studies may be used36,37 and fine-tuned as needed. Describing the analysis pipeline, from data preprocessing to cluster description and post-hoc analyses, as well as recording/providing logs of re-runs, provide a high-level understanding of the model used, and therethrough explainability. This can be furthered with so-called model cards, which are short documentations of the context, usage, and evaluation in varied conditions (e.g., across different populations).

Similarly, each of the three domains described above are constituted of both clinical and technical elements. In fact, the complete pipeline, from start to finish, of studies incorporating cluster analyses, necessitates close co-operation between clinicians and methodologists. Choice of sample/variables, preprocessing of input data, selection and fine-tuning/evaluation of models, and assessment of derived subgroups all require a deep understanding of appropriate methodological approaches (and thus the inclusion of methodologists experienced in ML). However, throughout the entire pipeline, the involvement of clinical domain experts is equally critical to ensure clinical validity of methods, results, and interpretations. In this regard, interactive computing environments, such as Jupyter Notebooks, may be used to facilitate communication, enabling methodologists to demonstrate output in a digestible fashion while allowing for rapid iterations and revisions. Such tools may also be used to enhance transparency and reproducibility.

Concluding remarks

There is an exponential increase in studies using ML-based clustering techniques to derive clinical subgroups. Such approaches are often easy to implement, and rich characterization data are abundant. At the same time, it remains challenging to discern whether ML-derived phenotypes are clinically relevant. Therefore, we propose a minimal framework for assessing the clinical utility of ML-derived subgroups and underscore the importance of close collaboration between clinicians and methodologists. Continued efforts are needed to confirm the comprehensiveness and appropriateness of this framework; nevertheless, our work constitutes a broadly generalizable foundation and important first step in this endeavor.
