1.
Introduction

Discrete differential geometry is based on the idea that a discrete object should not merely approximate a smooth one, but should preserve its essential geometric structure. From this point of view, polygonal models are most useful when the fundamental constructions of smooth differential geometry admit natural, intrinsic, and computable discrete counterparts. This philosophy is particularly fruitful in affine differential geometry, where many classical objects are defined not by Euclidean metric quantities, but by affine-invariant constructions such as parallel tangents, chords, envelopes, and oriented areas (see
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
7
,
26
]
, and the literature therein).

The aim of the present paper is to develop this point of view for affine
\lambda
-equidistants of convex polygons with parallel opposite sides. These polygons, introduced in
[
7
]
, form a natural discrete analogue of smooth convex curves. Indeed, they may be obtained by sampling tangent lines to a convex curve in pairs of parallel directions, and many affine constructions known in the smooth case have direct polygonal counterparts. In this sense,
\mathrm{CPPOS}
es are not only combinatorial objects, but also a discrete affine-differential model of convex ovals.

For a smooth closed planar curve, affine
\lambda
-equidistants are defined by taking affine combinations

\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b

of pairs of points whose tangent lines are parallel. The special case
\lambda=0.5
is the Wigner caustic, also known in some contexts as the area evolute and as the middle hedgehog. Closely related to it is the centre symmetry set, which may be regarded as an affine analogue of the centre of symmetry: for a centrally symmetric oval, this set degenerates to a single point, while in general it becomes a singular curve measuring the failure of central symmetry. These objects have been studied from several complementary perspectives, including singularity theory, affine geometry, convex geometry, and isoperimetric inequalities; see for instance

The polygonal case is interesting for at least two reasons. First, it provides a discrete model in which the constructions are elementary and explicitly computable: affine equidistants, the Wigner caustic, and the centre symmetry set are again polygonal objects determined by the combinatorics and affine geometry of the original polygon. Secondly, the discrete theory reflects the smooth one surprisingly well. Thus, one can prove certain results directly in the polygonal setting and then interpret them as discrete analogues, or even as approximating versions, of the corresponding theorems for smooth convex curves. This gives a useful bridge between affine differential geometry and discrete geometry.

In this paper we study this bridge in detail. We first recall and develop the basic geometry of
\mathrm{CPPOS}
es and their affine
\lambda
-equidistants. We show that the construction of polygonal approximations by tangent lines is compatible with taking affine equidistants: under natural assumptions, taking the
\lambda
-equidistant of a polygonal approximation gives the same polygonal object as approximating the
\lambda
-equidistant of the original oval. We also prove a Hausdorff convergence result, showing that, as the set of chosen directions becomes finer, the corresponding polygonal chains converge to the smooth affine equidistant.

We then analyze the singularities of polygonal equidistants. In the discrete setting, cusps are described by a simple condition involving consecutive edges, or equivalently, by the way the affine parameters of consecutive great diagonals cross the level
\lambda
. This leads to a parity result: for a generic
\lambda\neq 0.5
, the number of cusps of
{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{P})
is even, whereas the Wigner caustic
{\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P})
has an odd number of cusps. This mirrors the behaviour known from the smooth theory and emphasizes that
\mathrm{CPPOS}
es retain the essential affine geometry of parallel pairs.

Another part of the paper is devoted to reconstruction. We investigate when a
\mathrm{CPPOS}
can be recovered from its Wigner caustic and centre symmetry set. Since these two singular sets encode information about the great diagonals and about the midpoints of opposite vertices, they carry a substantial amount of data about the original polygon. We present a reconstruction procedure and formulate conditions under which the reconstruction is possible. This shows that, in the polygonal affine setting, the Wigner caustic and the centre symmetry set are not merely auxiliary objects, but can be used as geometric fingerprints of the original polygon.

Finally, we prove isoperimetric-type results. Our main inequality is a discrete version of the improved isoperimetric inequality involving the oriented area of the Wigner caustic:

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{P})^{2}\geqslant 8n\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)\cdot\big(A(\mathcal{P})+2\left|A^{\ast}\left(E_{0.5}(\mathcal{P})\right)\right|\big),

where
\mathcal{P}
is a
\mathrm{CPPOS}
with
2n
vertices. Equality holds precisely for equiangular
\mathrm{CPPOS}
es of constant width. We also obtain sharp estimates for the oriented areas of the Wigner caustic and the centre symmetry set in terms of the area enclosed by the initial polygon. These estimates provide discrete counterparts of known smooth phenomena and show that the singular affine sets associated with a
\mathrm{CPPOS}
control a significant part of its global geometry.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce
\mathrm{CPPOS}
es, their
\lambda
-equidistants, including the Wigner caustic, and the centre symmetry set, and we discuss the relation between the discrete and smooth constructions. Section 3 is devoted to reconstruction from the Wigner caustic and the centre symmetry set. In Section 4 we prove the discrete improved isoperimetric inequality and the sharp oriented area estimates.

2.
Convex Polygons with Parallel Opposite Sides

We begin by introducing the class of convex polygons that constitutes the main object of this paper. In this section, we fix the notation, present the basic definitions, and describe the geometric structure of convex polygons with parallel opposite sides. These polygons provide a discrete counterpart of smooth convex curves and form the natural setting for the study of affine equidistants, the Wigner caustic, and the centre symmetry set. We also describe a construction of such polygons from a prescribed family of directions.

Throughout this paper, the vertices and edges of a polygon will be indexed periodically. More precisely, we write

P_{i+2n}=P_{i}
and

e_{i+2n}=e_{i}

for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. Unless stated otherwise, all definitions, conditions, and identities involving the index
i
are understood to hold for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a convex planar
2n
-gon for some integer
n\geqslant 3
. Denote its vertices by
P_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, and its edges as
e_{i}:=P_{i+1}-P_{i}
.
If
\mathcal{P}
satisfies the conditions:

(1)

e_{i}\|e_{i+n}
,

(2)

\left[e_{i},e_{i+1}\right]>0
,

(3)

\left<e_{i},e_{i+1}\right>>0

for all
i
, where
[\cdot,\cdot],\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
denote determinant product and scalar product, respectively, then we will call
\mathcal{P}
a
convex polygon with parallel opposite sides
, abbreviated as a
\mathrm{CPPOS}
.

Remark 2.2
.

The definition used in
[
7
]
also permits
\mathrm{CPPOS}
es with acute interior angles. In the present paper, we restrict our attention to polygons whose interior angles are obtuse. Equivalently, the angle between each pair of consecutive oriented edge vectors

is acute, as expressed by condition (3) in Definition
2.1
.

This convention allows cusp singularities to be interpreted geometrically exactly as in Definition
2.15

2.16

[
7

Let

d_{i}:=P_{i+n}-P_{i}

denote the vector of the
i
-th great diagonal of
\mathcal{P}
. Let
D_{i}
be the point of intersection of the two great diagonals
P_{i}P_{i+n}
and
P_{i+1}P_{i+n+1}
, and let
\lambda_{i}
be the signed affine parameter defined by

D_{i}-P_{i}=\lambda_{i}d_{i}.

Thus,
\lambda_{i}
measures the signed fraction of the great diagonal from the vertex
P_{i}
to the intersection point
D_{i}
. By the intercept theorem,
\lambda_{i}
also satisfies

D_{i}-P_{i+1}=\lambda_{i}d_{i+1}.

Clearly,
D_{i+n}=D_{i}
, and consequently
\lambda_{i+n}=1-\lambda_{i}
. Moreover, the homothety with center
D_{i}
which maps the side
e_{i}
to the opposite side
e_{i+n}
gives

(2.1)

e_{i+n}=-\frac{\lambda_{i+n}}{\lambda_{i}}e_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{i}-1}{\lambda_{i}}e_{i}.

The reader may notice that the authors of

]

e(i+\frac{1}{2})

D(i+\frac{1}{2})
, and
\lambda(i+\frac{1}{2})
. Although this convention may be more intuitive, keeping it here would make some of our formulae rather long and difficult to read. Therefore, we replace it by the shorter notation
e_{i}
,
D_{i}
, and
\lambda_{i}
, respectively.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a
\mathrm{CPPOS}

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

denote the polygon whose vertices, in cyclic order, are

P_{i}(\lambda):=P_{i}+\lambda d_{i}.

The polygon

\lambda

\mathcal{P}

\lambda
-equidistant of
\mathcal{P}

Its edges will be denoted by

In particular,
{\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P})

Let
\mathcal{P}

Definition 2.7
.

Let

Remark 2.8
.

A value
\lambda

\lambda:(1-\lambda)
. In this situation, the corresponding edge of the
\lambda
-equidistant degenerates to their point of intersection. Equivalently,

\lambda=\lambda_{i}

\lambda_{i+n}=1-\lambda_{i}

One possible construction of a
2n

\mathrm{CPPOS}
proceeds as follows. We begin with a set
\nu\subset[0,\pi)
consisting of
n

\pi

2n

\nu

\nu
and of the corresponding lines does not necessarily yield a
\mathrm{CPPOS}
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let

\nu=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\}\subset[0,\pi)
, where

m\geqslant 3

and

0\leqslant\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{m}<\pi.

We say that
\nu
is an

if

\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i-1}\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

for

i=2,\ldots,m,

and

\alpha_{1}+\pi-\alpha_{m}\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right).

Definition 2.10
.

Let
M
be a closed curve whose only singularities, if any, are cusp singularities.
By a
cusp

t\mapsto(t^{2},t^{3})

at

has no inflection points and that its rotation number is equal to either

\frac{1}{2}

has only cusp singularities, there exists a continuous normal vector field along

Let

If the rotation number of

We shall call a closed planar curve an
oval
if it is smooth, strictly convex, and has no self-intersections.

The following theorem is a key observation: constructing a polygonal chain from an affine equidistant of an oval is compatible with taking the discrete equidistant of the polygonal chain constructed from the original oval – see Figure

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
M
be an oval, let
\nu
be an allowable set of directions, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, such that the only singularities of
{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)
are ordinary cusps. Then

\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)\right)={\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}\left(\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu}(M)\right).

Proof.

Let
a,b\in M
be a parallel pair such that the tangent lines to
M
at
a
and
b
have a direction belonging to
\nu
. By Theorem 2.6 in
[
18
]
, the line tangent to
{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)
at the point

\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b

is parallel to the tangent lines to
M
at
a
and
b
.

This tangent line contains one side of the polygonal chain

\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)\right).

On the other hand, the tangent lines to
M
at
a
and
b
contain two corresponding sides of
\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu}(M)
. Hence, by the definition of the discrete equidistant, the corresponding side of

{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}\left(\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu}(M)\right)

is parallel to them as well.

Moreover, by the intercept theorem, this side also passes through the point

(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b
.
Thus, the two corresponding side-lines of

\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}\left(\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu}(M)\right)

are parallel and pass through the same point. Therefore, they coincide.

Since this argument applies to every direction in
\nu
, the corresponding side-lines of both polygonal chains coincide. Consequently, their consecutive intersection points coincide as well. Hence,

\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)\right)={\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}\left(\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu}(M)\right),

which ends the proof.

Definition 2.13
.

For a finite set of directions

\nu=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\}\subset[0,\pi),\qquad 0\leqslant\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{m}<\pi,

we define its
angular mesh
by

\operatorname{mesh}(\nu):=\max\left\{\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}-\alpha_{m-1},\alpha_{1}+\pi-\alpha_{m}\right\}.

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
M
be a generic oval, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be generic. If
(\nu_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of allowable sets of directions for the affine equidistant
{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)
such that

\operatorname{mesh}(\nu_{k})\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad k\to\infty,

then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu_{k}}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)\right)={\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)

in the Hausdorff metric.

Proof.

Since
M
is generic, it is known that, for a generic value of
\lambda
, the affine equidistant
{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)
is a piecewise smooth curve whose only singularities are ordinary cusps – for details and for the precise genericity assumptions, see
[
18
]
and the literature therein.

Remove from
{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)
all its cusp points. The remaining curve is the union of finitely many open smooth arcs. These arcs can be further divided into finitely many overlapping arcs which, in suitable local coordinate systems, are graphs of
C^{1}
functions whose first derivatives are uniformly bounded by some constant
L>0
. On each compact regular subarc, the tangent direction is a local coordinate whose inverse is uniformly continuous. Hence, as
\operatorname{mesh}(\nu)\to 0
, the distance between consecutive points of tangency tends uniformly to zero.

Let us focus on one such arc. It is represented as the graph of a function

g_{i}\colon(a_{i},b_{i})\to\mathbb{R}.

For a sufficiently small mesh, the corresponding part of the polygonal chain

\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu_{k}}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)\right)

is represented by a piecewise linear function

h_{i}\colon(a_{i},b_{i})\to\mathbb{R}

whose segments are tangent to the graph of
g_{i}
. The first and the last segment may fail to be tangent at points belonging to
(a_{i},b_{i})
; however, by slightly shrinking the interval, we may assume that all these segments are tangent at points of the considered arc. This can be done in such a way that, for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
, all points of
{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)
and of the polygonal chain which are not represented by some
g_{i}
or
h_{i}
lie in an
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of the cusp points. This follows from the local normal form of an ordinary cusp and the continuous dependence of the intersections of nearby tangent lines on their points of tangency.

Fix a set of directions
\nu
satisfying the above assumptions and put

\mathcal{P}:=\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)\right).

Recall that

d_{H}\left(\mathcal{P},{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)\right)=\max\left\{\sup_{y\in{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)}\inf_{z\in\mathcal{P}}\|y-z\|,\ \sup_{z\in\mathcal{P}}\inf_{y\in{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)}\|y-z\|\right\},

where
d_{H}
is the Hausdorff metric.

We split
{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)
and
\mathcal{P}
into two parts:

M_{1}=\{y\in{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M):y=(x,g_{i}(x))\text{ for some }i\text{ and some }x\},\ \ M_{2}={\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)\setminus M_{1},

and

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{z\in\mathcal{P}:z=(x,h_{i}(x))\text{ for some }i\text{ and some }x\},\quad\mathcal{P}_{2}=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{1}.

Let

, say

Assume that

Hence, by the mean value theorem,

\displaystyle\inf_{z\in\mathcal{P}}\|y-z\|

\displaystyle\leqslant|g_{i}(x)-h_{i}(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|g_{i}(x)-g_{i}(x_{0})|+|g_{i}^{\prime}(x_{0})|\,|x-x_{0}|

\displaystyle=\bigl(|g_{i}^{\prime}(c)|+|g_{i}^{\prime}(x_{0})|\bigr)|x-x_{0}|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2L|x-x_{0}|

for some

between
x
and
x_{0}
. Since the mesh of
\nu
tends to
0
, the maximal distance between

and the corresponding point of tangency

tends to
0
. Therefore,

\sup_{y\in M_{1}}\inf_{z\in\mathcal{P}}\|y-z\|\to 0.

Now let
y\in M_{2}
. By construction,
y
lies in an
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of a cusp point. Hence, there exists

such that

\|y-y_{1}\|\leqslant 2\varepsilon.

Using the previous estimate, we get

\inf_{z\in\mathcal{P}}\|y-z\|\leqslant\|y-y_{1}\|+\inf_{z\in\mathcal{P}}\|y_{1}-z\|\leqslant 2\varepsilon+\inf_{z\in\mathcal{P}}\|y_{1}-z\|.

Thus,

\sup_{y\in{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)}\inf_{z\in\mathcal{P}}\|y-z\|

can be made arbitrarily small by first choosing

sufficiently small and then taking the mesh of
\nu
sufficiently small.

The reverse estimate is analogous. Indeed, if

, say
z=(x,h_{i}(x))
, then choosing

gives

\inf_{y\in{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)}\|z-y\|\leqslant|h_{i}(x)-g_{i}(x)|,

which is estimated exactly as above. If

, then

lies in an
\varepsilon

Consequently,

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{P}}\inf_{y\in{\mathrm{E}}_{\lambda}(M)}\|z-y\|

also tends to

as
\operatorname{mesh}(\nu)\to 0
. Therefore,

which proves the theorem.
∎

Proof.

corresponding to the pair

\displaystyle d_{H}\bigl({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathrm{PPOS}_{\nu_{k}}(M)),{\mathrm{CSS}}(M)\bigr)\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad k\to\infty.

4.
Isoperimetric-Type Inequalities

We begin this section by recalling the classical isoperimetric inequality for polygons. If
\mathcal{P}
is an
m
-sided convex polygon, then

(4.1)

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{P})^{2}\geqslant 4m\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{m}\right)A(\mathcal{P}),

where
L(\mathcal{P})
denotes the perimeter of
\mathcal{P}
and
A(\mathcal{P})
denotes the area of the region enclosed by
\mathcal{P}
. Moreover, equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is a regular polygon. Passing to the limit
m\to\infty
, one recovers the classical isoperimetric inequality for smooth planar curves.

Isoperimetric inequalities for planar objects have attracted considerable attention in recent years, leading to a wide range of new results for smooth curves, convex bodies, and various generalized geometric structures (see
[
3
,
8
,
23
,
28
,
29
,
31
,
32
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
). In this context, discrete models provide more than merely finite-dimensional analogues of smooth problems. They may reveal additional combinatorial and geometric features that are difficult to detect in the continuous setting, while at the same time offering effective tools for numerical experiments and for testing possible conjectures. Thus, the discretization considered here may contribute not only to a better understanding of the classical smooth theory, but also to the discovery of new properties and inequalities that may subsequently prove useful in the study of planar geometric objects.

In what follows, we prove a refinement of this inequality for convex polygons with parallel opposite sides. The additional term, which appears in the estimate, is expressed in terms of the oriented area of the Wigner caustic. Thus, in the class of
\mathrm{CPPOS}
es, the isoperimetric deficit is controlled not only by the area enclosed by the polygon, but also by the affine geometry encoded in its equidistant at level
0.5
. This gives a discrete analogue of the improved isoperimetric inequality known for smooth ovals and shows that the Wigner caustic naturally measures part of the deviation from the extremal case.

The proofs of Theorem
4.4
and Theorem
4.7
make use of another natural construction associated with a convex body
K
, which is its central symmetral:
\frac{1}{2}\bigl(K+(-K)\bigr)
,
while the unnormalized body
K+(-K)=K-K
is usually called the difference body of
K
. It is also an example of a secant caustic (see
[
15
]
). The central symmetral is an origin-symmetric convex body whose support function is equal to one half of the width function of
K
. Consequently, it has the same width function as
K
, and, in the planar case, the same perimeter. On the other hand, the Brunn–Minkowski and Rogers–Shephard inequalities imply

(4.2)

\displaystyle A(K)\leqslant A\left(\frac{K-K}{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{3}{2}A(K).

Thus, Minkowski symmetrization preserves the perimeter while increasing the enclosed area, which makes it particularly natural in the study of isoperimetric inequalities and geometric measures of asymmetry (see, for instance,
[
23
,
33
,
40
]
).

Definition 4.2

Let

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}

\mathrm{CPPOS}

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is a
\mathrm{CPPOS}
of constant width and has equally spaced side directions.

Proof.

Let

W_{i}:=P_{i}(0.5)=\frac{P_{i}+P_{i+n}}{2}

be the vertices of the Wigner caustic. We also define an auxiliary polygon
\mathcal{Q}
by the vertices

. Thus,

is the boundary of the central symmetral
\frac{1}{2}(K-K)
of the convex body
K
bounded by
\mathcal{P}
.
Then

P_{i}=W_{i}+Q_{i}
.

Denote the edges of
\mathcal{Q}
by

q_{i}:=Q_{i+1}-Q_{i}
.
Then

(4.4)

q_{i}=\frac{P_{i+1}-P_{i+n+1}-P_{i}+P_{i+n}}{2}=\frac{e_{i}-e_{i+n}}{2}.

Since
\mathcal{P}
is a
\mathrm{CPPOS}
, the opposite edges
e_{i}
and

are parallel and oppositely oriented. Hence, there exists
\alpha_{i}>0

e_{i+n}=-\alpha_{i}e_{i}.

Therefore, by (

(4.5)

q_{i}=\frac{1+\alpha_{i}}{2}e_{i}.

Thus, every edge of
\mathcal{Q}
is a positive multiple of the corresponding edge of
\mathcal{P}
. Since
\mathcal{P}
is convex, it follows that
\mathcal{Q}
is convex as well.

Moreover, from (
4.5
) we get

|q_{i}|=\frac{|e_{i}|+|e_{i+n}|}{2}.

Hence,

(4.6)

L(\mathcal{Q})=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}|q_{i}|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}|e_{i}|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}|e_{i+n}|=L(\mathcal{P}).

P_{i}=W_{i}+Q_{i}

\displaystyle A^{*}(\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}[W_{i}+Q_{i},W_{i+1}+Q_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\bigl([W_{i},W_{i+1}]+[W_{i},Q_{i+1}]\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\bigl([Q_{i},W_{i+1}]+[Q_{i},Q_{i+1}]\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}[W_{i},W_{i+1}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}[Q_{i},Q_{i+1}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\bigl([W_{i},Q_{i+1}]+[Q_{i},W_{i+1}]\bigr).

i+n

Since

Using also

Then

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))\right|<\frac{1}{4}A(\mathcal{P})\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leqslant\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))\right|\leqslant\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))\right|<A(\mathcal{P}).

Moreover, the equality cases in the above non-strict inequalities hold if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is centrally symmetric. Furthermore, strict inequalities are sharp – more precisely,

\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\frac{\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))\right|}{A(\mathcal{P})}=\frac{1}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\frac{\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))\right|}{A(\mathcal{P})}=1,

where the supremum is taken over all
\mathrm{CPPOS}
es with
2n
vertices.

Proof.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the convex body bounded by
\mathcal{P}
. We use the previously established identity (see equation (
4.8
)):

A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))=\frac{1}{2}\left(A(\mathcal{P})-A\left(\frac{K-K}{2}\right)\right)\leqslant 0.

By the Rogers–Shephard inequality in the plane,

(4.10)

\displaystyle A\left(\frac{K-K}{2}\right)\leqslant\frac{3}{2}A(\mathcal{P}),

with the equality case only for simplices. Therefore, in our case, the inequality in (
4.10
) is strict.
Using the identity (
4.8
), we get

\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))\right|=\frac{1}{2}\left(A\left(\frac{K-K}{2}\right)-A(\mathcal{P})\right)<\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3}{2}A(\mathcal{P})-A(\mathcal{P})\right)=\frac{1}{4}A(\mathcal{P}),

which ends the first part of the proof of the theorem.

Let us recall that

D_{i}-P_{i}=\lambda_{i}d_{i},

where
\lambda_{i}\in(0,1)
. Since

d_{i}=P_{i+n}-P_{i}=-2Q_{i},

we have

D_{i}=P_{i}+\lambda_{i}d_{i}=W_{i}+Q_{i}-2\lambda_{i}Q_{i}=W_{i}+(1-2\lambda_{i})Q_{i}.

For simplicity put

\eta_{i}=1-2\lambda_{i}.

Then

D_{i}=W_{i}+\eta_{i}Q_{i}
.
Consequently,

D_{i}=P_{i+1}+\lambda_{i}d_{i+1}.

Using

P_{i+1}=W_{i+1}+Q_{i+1}
,

d_{i+1}=-2Q_{i+1}
,
we also get

D_{i}=W_{i+1}+\eta_{i}Q_{i+1}.

Therefore,

(4.11)

\displaystyle D_{i}=W_{i}+\eta_{i}Q_{i}=W_{i+1}+\eta_{i}Q_{i+1}.

In particular,

(4.12)

\displaystyle W_{i+1}-W_{i}=-\eta_{i}(Q_{i+1}-Q_{i}).

We now compute the difference between the oriented area of the centre symmetry set and the oriented area of the Wigner caustic.

First,

A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[W_{i},W_{i+1}]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[W_{i},W_{i+1}-W_{i}].

Using (
4.12
),
we get

(4.13)

\displaystyle A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}[W_{i},Q_{i+1}-Q_{i}].

Now consider the centre symmetry set. We have

A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[D_{i},D_{i+1}].

Using
(
4.11
), we get

D_{i+1}=W_{i+1}+\eta_{i+1}Q_{i+1}

and

D_{i+1}-D_{i}=(\eta_{i+1}-\eta_{i})Q_{i+1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle[D_{i},D_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=[W_{i+1}+\eta_{i}Q_{i+1},(\eta_{i+1}-\eta_{i})Q_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=(\eta_{i+1}-\eta_{i})[W_{i+1},Q_{i+1}]+\eta_{i}(\eta_{i+1}-\eta_{i})[Q_{i+1},Q_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=(\eta_{i+1}-\eta_{i})[W_{i+1},Q_{i+1}].

Thus,

\displaystyle A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\eta_{i+1}-\eta_{i})[W_{i+1},Q_{i+1}]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\eta_{i}-\eta_{i-1})[W_{i},Q_{i}].

We now use summation by parts. Since the indices are cyclic,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\eta_{i}-\eta_{i-1})[W_{i},Q_{i}]=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\left([W_{i+1},Q_{i+1}]-[W_{i},Q_{i}]\right).

Therefore,

(4.14)

\displaystyle A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\left([W_{i+1},Q_{i+1}]-[W_{i},Q_{i}]\right).

Let us compute the expression in parentheses. From (
4.12
)
we get

\displaystyle[W_{i+1},Q_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=[W_{i}-\eta_{i}(Q_{i+1}-Q_{i}),Q_{i+1}]=[W_{i},Q_{i+1}]-\eta_{i}[Q_{i+1}-Q_{i},Q_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=[W_{i},Q_{i+1}]+\eta_{i}[Q_{i},Q_{i+1}].

Thus,

[W_{i+1},Q_{i+1}]-[W_{i},Q_{i}]=[W_{i},Q_{i+1}-Q_{i}]+\eta_{i}[Q_{i},Q_{i+1}].

Substituting this into the formula for
A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))
(see (
4.14
)), we obtain

A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}[W_{i},Q_{i+1}-Q_{i}]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}^{2}[Q_{i},Q_{i+1}].

By the formula for the oriented area of the Wigner caustic (see equation (
4.13
)) we get that

A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))=A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}^{2}[Q_{i},Q_{i+1}].

Since

\eta_{i}=1-2\lambda_{i}
,
we finally obtain

\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))\right|-\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))\right|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-2\lambda_{i})^{2}[Q_{i},Q_{i+1}]\geqslant 0,

since the auxiliary polygon with vertices
Q_{i}
is positively oriented. Note that the equality in the above inequality holds if and only if
\lambda_{i}=0.5
for all
i
– it means that
\mathcal{P}
is centrally symmetric.

Finally, let us compare the absolute value of the oriented area of the centre symmetry set with the area of the original polygon.

Since
0<\lambda_{i}<1
, we have

\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))\right|-\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[Q_{i},Q_{i+1}]=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}[Q_{i},Q_{i+1}]=\frac{1}{2}A(\mathcal{Q}),

where
\mathcal{Q}=\partial\left(\frac{K-K}{2}\right)
.

On the other hand, from the area identity for the Wigner caustic (see equation (
4.8
)),

A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}))=\frac{1}{2}\left(A(\mathcal{P})-A(\mathcal{Q})\right),

we obtain

\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\left(A(\mathcal{Q})-A(\mathcal{P})\right)+\frac{1}{2}A(\mathcal{Q})=A(\mathcal{Q})-\frac{1}{2}A(\mathcal{P}).

By the Rogers–Shephard inequality in the plane (see inequality (
4.10
)), we get

\displaystyle\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}))\right|<A(\mathcal{P}).

It remains to show that the constant
\frac{1}{4}
and
1
are sharp in the inequalities.

First, consider the case
n=3
. Let
T
be the following equilateral triangle of side length
\sqrt{3}
with center in
(0,0)
:

T=\operatorname{conv}\left\{\left(0,1\right),\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},-\frac{1}{2}\right),\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right\}.

For
0<\varepsilon<\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
, define the hexagon
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
by the vertices:

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+\varepsilon,-\frac{1}{2}\right),

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}-\varepsilon,-\frac{1}{2}\right),

\displaystyle P_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{3}\varepsilon}{2}\right),

\displaystyle P_{4}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2},1-\frac{\sqrt{3}\varepsilon}{2}\right),

\displaystyle P_{5}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},1-\frac{\sqrt{3}\varepsilon}{2}\right),

\displaystyle P_{6}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{3}\varepsilon}{2}\right).

Figure 18
.
Polygon
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}

This is triangle
T
with its three vertices cut off by segments parallel to the opposite sides (see Figure
18
). Then, by direct calculations, one gets the following results for the Wigner caustic and the centre symmetry set of
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
:

\frac{\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}))\right|}{A(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon})}=\frac{\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{16}(1-\sqrt{3}\varepsilon)^{2}}{\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{4}(1-\varepsilon^{2})}=\frac{(1-\sqrt{3}\varepsilon)^{2}}{4(1-\varepsilon^{2})}\text{ and }\frac{\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}))\right|}{A(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon})}=\frac{(1-\sqrt{3}\varepsilon)^{2}}{1-\varepsilon^{2}}.

Thus, the constants
\frac{1}{4}
and
1
are sharp for the Wigner caustic and the centre symmetry set in the case
n=3
, respectively.

Now assume that
n>3
and put

r=n-3
.
We shall replace the two
opposite sides
P_{2}P_{3}
and
P_{5}P_{6}
by two polygonal arcs, each
consisting of
r+1
edges.

Since the sides
P_{2}P_{3}
and
P_{5}P_{6}
are parallel and oppositely
oriented, there exists a unique number
\mu_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

P_{6}-P_{5}=-\mu_{\varepsilon}(P_{3}-P_{2})
.

Define the negative homothety by the following equation

H_{\varepsilon}(x)=P_{5}-\mu_{\varepsilon}(x-P_{2}).

Then

H_{\varepsilon}(P_{2})=P_{5}
,
and

H_{\varepsilon}(P_{3})=P_{6}
.

For sufficiently small
\rho>0
, let
\gamma_{\rho}
be a strictly
convex circular arc joining
P_{2}
to
P_{3}
, lying on the exterior side
of the segment
P_{2}P_{3}
, and contained in the
\rho
-neighbourhood of

P_{2}P_{3}
. Choose points

Q_{0}^{\rho},Q_{1}^{\rho},\ldots,Q_{r+1}^{\rho}

on
\gamma_{\rho}
, in their cyclic order, such that

Q_{0}^{\rho}=P_{2}
,

Q_{r+1}^{\rho}=P_{3}
(see Figure
19
),
and, as
\rho\to 0
,

Q_{j}^{\rho}\to Q_{j}^{0}:=\left(1-\frac{j}{r+1}\right)P_{2}+\frac{j}{r+1}P_{3},\qquad j=0,\ldots,r+1.

Figure 19
.
A circular arc through
P_{2}
and
P_{3}
in
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}

Define the corresponding points on the opposite arc by

S_{j}^{\rho}=H_{\varepsilon}(Q_{j}^{\rho})
for

j=0,\ldots,r+1
.
Then

S_{0}^{\rho}=P_{5}
,

S_{r+1}^{\rho}=P_{6}
,
and

S_{j+1}^{\rho}-S_{j}^{\rho}=-\mu_{\varepsilon}\bigl(Q_{j+1}^{\rho}-Q_{j}^{\rho}\bigr)
for

j=0,\ldots,r
.
Therefore,

Q_{j}^{\rho}Q_{j+1}^{\rho}\parallel S_{j}^{\rho}S_{j+1}^{\rho},\qquad j=0,\ldots,r.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon,\rho}^{(n)}
be the polygon whose vertices,
in cyclic order, are

P_{1},\,Q_{0}^{\rho},Q_{1}^{\rho},\ldots,Q_{r+1}^{\rho},\,P_{4},\,S_{0}^{\rho},S_{1}^{\rho},\ldots,S_{r+1}^{\rho}.

It has

1+(r+2)+1+(r+2)=2r+6=2n

vertices and by the construction, its opposite sides are parallel.
Moreover, for sufficiently small
\rho>0
, the directions of the
successive sides occur in the same cyclic order as the directions of
the sides of
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
. Hence,

\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon,\rho}^{(n)}
is convex and therefore is a

\mathrm{CPPOS}
. Furthermore, the two polygonal arcs converge to the original sides
P_{2}P_{3}

and
P_{5}P_{6}
, respectively. Consequently,

A\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon,\rho}^{(n)}\bigr)\to A(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon})
,

A^{*}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon,\rho}^{(n)}\bigr)\right)\to A^{*}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon})\right)
,
and

A^{*}\left({\mathrm{CSS}}\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon,\rho}^{(n)}\bigr)\right)\to A^{*}\left({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon})\right)

as
\rho\to 0
.

Finally, let
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
. For each
k
, choose
\rho_{k}>0

sufficiently small so that the two ratios corresponding to

\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon_{k},\rho_{k}}^{(n)}
differ from the corresponding
ratios for
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon_{k}}
by less than
1/k
. Since

\frac{\left|A^{*}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon_{k}})\right)\right|}{A(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon_{k}})}\to\frac{1}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\left|A^{*}\left({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon_{k}})\right)\right|}{A(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon_{k}})}\to 1,

we conclude that

\frac{\left|A^{*}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon_{k},\rho_{k}}^{(n)}\bigr)\right)\right|}{A\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon_{k},\rho_{k}}^{(n)}\bigr)}\to\frac{1}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\left|A^{*}\left({\mathrm{CSS}}\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon_{k},\rho_{k}}^{(n)}\bigr)\right)\right|}{A\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon_{k},\rho_{k}}^{(n)}\bigr)}\to 1.

Hence, the constants
\frac{1}{4}
and
1
are sharp for every
n\geqslant 3
.
∎

As a corollary of Theorem
4.7
, we obtain the analogous result for smooth ovals and their Wigner caustics by approximating an oval by the converging sequence of
\mathrm{CPPOS}
es.

Corollary 4.8

(
[
40
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be an oval. Then

\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{O}))\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{4}A(\mathcal{O})\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leqslant\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{E}}_{0.5}(\mathcal{O}))\right|\leqslant\left|A^{*}({\mathrm{CSS}}(\mathcal{O}))\right|\leqslant A(\mathcal{O}).
