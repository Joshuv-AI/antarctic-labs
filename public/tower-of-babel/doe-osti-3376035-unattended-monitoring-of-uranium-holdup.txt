 ABSTRACT
 This work evaluated the use of scintillating fiber (SciFi) radiation sensors as a
cost-effective, unattended monitoring technique for uranium holdup accumulation
in reprocessing equipment. With ruggedized packaging and an outer diameter of
less than 1 cm, scintillating fibers offer deployment flexibility. Additionally,
coupling these sensors with standard optical fibers provides an affordable option
to monitor locations up to kilometers away from a localized control room or data
acquisition (DAQ) system. These combinations of characteristics along with their
relatively low manufacturing costs make scintillating fibers ideal for installation at
key measurement points such as pipes, valves, and filtration systems. Evaluation
efforts culminated in a successful test of the SciFi sensor system’s ability to detect
the movement of nuclear materials in such a nuclear facility at Idaho National
Laboratory.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 The primary goal of this project was to develop a low-cost solution for continuous unattended
monitoring of uranium holdup that is versatile and flexible enough for a wide range of deployment
configurations and scenarios while still allowing for timely identification of anomalies. These project goals
align with the approach for this evaluation involved developing a prototype sensor system, performing
extended and comprehensive testing and optimization of the system in a laboratory setting, and finally
deploying it at the Material Recovery Pilot Plant (MRPP) for field testing in an operational reprocessing
facility. This research aligns with the International Atomic Energy Agency’s (IAEA) Department of
Safeguards’ Development and Implementation Support (D&IS) Plan SGTS-011, Unattended
Measurements Techniques, of its 2024–2025 Program for Nuclear Verification. Specifically, it supports
this plan’s Outcome #4: Improved Unattended Monitoring System (UMS) reliability, modularity, and
security [1]. The effort culminated in a working prototype suitable for deployment in a nuclear fuel cycle
facility where accountable materials are routinely transferred between process stages, such as a gas
centrifuge enrichment plant.
 Idaho National Laboratory (INL) brings over 15 years of experience with SciFi arrays to this project.
The lab has successfully tested long sensors, ranging from 30 to 50 meters, under various environmental
conditions [2]. Hence, the bulk of development efforts focused on optimizing the SciFi sensors and data
acquisition (DAQ) for the intended application. Significant advancements were made in methodologies for
SciFi array production and testing. The production of 0.1- and 1.0-m long sensor segments was refined
using a newly developed automated DAQ system for streamlined performance testing. This included
comprehensive assessments of long-term drift, noise, and temperature sensitivity, ensuring the reliability
and accuracy of the sensors for their intended monitoring applications. Benchtop measurements were also
performed to optimize coupling with 20-m lengths of optical fiber in a nuclear processing facility
environment.

 2. SENSOR FABRICATION
 Scintillating fibers were coupled to subminiature assembly (SMA) optical connectors using a mixture
of 4-parts epoxy and 1-part cement from Eljen Technology. This mixture was carefully applied and left to
cure overnight to ensure a secure connection and optimal bonding. Photographs of this process are provided
in Figure 1. The coupling process was followed without deviation to produce sets of 1-m and 10 -cm long
sensors to ensure performance consistency and reliability of each unit. These sets were produced using
both BCF-10 and BCF-12 scintillating fiber varieties as each has variety has operational strengths and
weaknesses and having both will ensure the system will be able to address the span of geometric,
environmental, and source strength parameters found in a typical nuclear facility [2].

Figure 1. Photographs of the optical coupling process.

 Following SMA coupling, any excess fiber was carefully trimmed and sanded. The sanding process
involved progressively using finer grits of sandpaper, starting with 600-grit, followed by 800-grit, and
finally 1000-grit paper to ensure a smooth finish. Figure 2 contains photographs of the sanding process.
Once the sanding was complete, the fibers were meticulously polished to achieve a clear and smooth
surface. Each fiber was then inspected and recorded using a microscope and camera to validate the
terminal’s finish quality. A small handful of units were identified during inspection with low levels of
surface pitting. A photograph example of this is shown in Figure 3 and compared to a unit with a smooth
surface. After further testing it was determined that small amounts of pitting did not have a significant
impact on sensor performance. A risk of sanding off the beveled edge of each sensor was also identified
during the inspection process. Each anomaly, whether significant or not, was thoroughly documented so
that it could be quickly addressed if performance problems manifested later during testing.

 The photomultiplier tube (PMT) models used in this project were Hamamatsu models H10722-110 and
H10722-210. An example model is pictured in Figure 6. The use of the two different PMT was required
due to the manufacturer availability. Both models had a similar spectral response over the range of 230 to
700 nm. The only difference was that the H10722-110 model used a “super” bialkali photocathode while
the H10722-210 had an “ultra” bialkali photocathode. Sensitivity and performance discrepancies between
the two models were normalized during testing. Bayonet Neill-Concelman (BNC) connectors were added
to the signal wires for bulkhead connections and fast system assembly.

Figure 6. Photograph of a photomultiplier used in this effort.

 A single Hamamatsu model C10709 power source, pictured in Figure 7, was used to provide high
voltage to the PMTs. The gain control for the PMT on channel 1 was managed directly through this power
source, while gain control for the remaining sensors was achieved by installing individual potentiometers
for each sensor. This configuration allowed for sensitivity and performance matching between individual
detector assemblies.

 Operation in a nuclear facility requires that DAQ and electronic components be collocated in an area
that limits interference with ongoing processes. Often this will be at some distance from individual sensors
assemblies. This was accomplished by coupling each sensor assembly to a 20-meter-long single-fiber optic
reel manufactured by Thorlabs. All reels underwent rigorous performance testing prior to integration.
 A LabVIEW graphical user interface was developed for real-time monitoring, hardware control, and
data acquisition of measurements. The software featured two main charts for data visualization. A top chart
displayed the count rate per channel and provided real-time tracking of each detector’s performance. The
bottom chart displayed time-averaged count rates to provide information on the system’s performance and
trends over time. A screenshot of the software is shown in Figure 8.

 3. SYSTEM TESTING

 A detector assembly evaluation station, photographed in Figure 9, was configured in the laboratory for
bias plateau optimization and response normalization to ensure performance uniformity across all detectors.
Full detector assemblies (SciFi sensor, fiber optic transmission line, and PMT) were tested to assess
performance of the system as near to a state of deployment as possible. Additionally, the power supply and
DAQ components intended for deployment at MRPP were also used during this testing.

Figure 9. Photographs of the sensor evaluation station in the laboratory.

 To minimize light pollution and ensure optimal performance, two separate enclosures were designed:
one for the power sources and another for the PMTs. This separation helps to reduce any potential
interference from external light contamination by reducing the probability for ambient exposure should
adjustments to the power supply be necessary. The PMT models required a significant amount of time to
recover following exposure, producing counts significantly above background levels for tens of minutes.
Isolating the tubes and limiting exposure allowed for quick setup and configuration. Additionally, these
PMTs are sensitive to small temperature swings and therefore a thermocouple was added to the PMT
enclosure to monitor the local temperature. This allowed for temperature compensation in post processing.
The enclosures were both mounted on a rack along with the potentiometer array used for gain calibrations.
The rack is pictured in Figure 10, while the potentiometers are pictured in Figure 11.

housing the PMTs. A 10-day long measurement was performed to monitoring period to develop

 Further testing of a subset of sensors was performed using an environmental chamber. Photographs
from these tests are shown in Figure 15. Several isotopic sources with gamma-ray emissions that ranged
from ~100 to 1300 keV were used to measure performance under a controlled change in temperature. The
chamber gradually heated from 12°C to 60°C in 10°C increments, allowing for a prolonged measurement
dwell at each step. After reaching 60°C, the chamber was then cooled back down to room temperature. This
controlled heating and cooling cycle was designed to test the detectors' resilience and accuracy across a
broad range of temperatures. The sensor performance remained relatively stable throughout the test. The
results of count rate versus temperature are plotted in Figure 16.

 4. FIELD TESTING
 The Material Recovery Pilot Plant at INL performs downblending of highly enriched uranium (HEU)
from unirradiated fuel into high-assay low enriched uranium (HALEU) for use in next generation reactor
fuels. Down blending at MRPP occurs at in the early decladding stage of fuel reprocessing with the intent
of reducing the nonproliferation and security risks associated with uranium recovery during the later stages
of most reprocessing methods. The ZIRCEX process used at MRPP for cladding removal and material
recovery involves several key steps. First the cladding is volatilized using high temperature HCl gas to
produce metal chloride gases. These gases interact with steam and oxygen to form stable oxide solids that
are collected in a filter. The remaining decladded fuel undergoes voloxidation with NO2 and O2 gases to
create U3O8. These uranium oxides are then filtered and transported by vacuum for final collection. The
MRPP was chosen for field testing the developed SciFi system because the ZIRCEX process is
representative of commercial reprocessing methods, allowing the system performance to be assessed for
real-world uranium movement and holdup in real-world environmental conditions.

 4.1. Installation
 One-meter long SciFi assemblies were wrapped around various pipes and collection vessels at
designated measurement points in the facility. These fibers were securely fastened using metal tape shown
in photographs in Figure 17. All sensor signals were transported via the single core fiber optic transmission
lines to the DAQ located several meters from the reprocessing equipment. Figures 18-20 provide diagrams
of the three primary steps in the ZIRCEX process. The SciFi sensors were strategically reinstalled at each
stage of these three stages to demonstrate and assess reconfigurability of the system. In the decladding
process, shown in Figure 18, detectors were stationed along the path of the metal chloride gases. Figure 19
shows how the detectors were reconfigured to follow the path of the uranium oxides in the oxidation
process. Finally, for the elutriation processes shown in Figure 20, several detectors were positioned around
the final collection vessel.

 4.2. Temperature Effects

 5. CONCLUSION
 A uranium holdup detection system was developed using scintillating fiber sensors for gamma-ray
detection. Quality control assessments and component testing were performed at each stage of the
development process. Fiber optical transmission cables were incorporated into the system to minimize
system impact on ongoing processes at potential facilities and limit environmental effects from these
processes on the systems electronics. Following laboratory testing of the system, it was deployed to INL’s
Material Recovery Pilot Plant for a measurement campaign to track uranium movement throughout the
ZIRCEX fuel reprocessing methods performed at this facility. The system performed well, clearly detecting
the accumulation of uranium in various components of the reprocessing system. The system was also able
to perform in the extreme facility environment, demonstrating this type of system’s suitability for
unattended uranium holdup monitoring in a real-world nuclear facility.
