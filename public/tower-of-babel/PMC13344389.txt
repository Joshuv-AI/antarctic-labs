Expert Consensus on Vascular Assessment and Treatment Strategies for Adult Femoral Neck Fractures
Authors: Dewei Zhao, Stuart Goodman, Quanjun (Trey) Cui, Ling Qin, William C. Lineaweaver, Jiake Xu, Nobuhiko Sugano, Nicolas Reina, Ke Li, Baoyi Liu, Liangliang Cheng, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article presents an expert consensus on vascular assessment and management strategies for adult femoral neck fractures, based on a narrative review of current evidence and the collective clinical experience of a multidisciplinary panel of orthopaedic and reconstructive surgeons. To assist clinicians in accurately evaluating the vascular status of the femoral head and formulating individualized treatment plans, based on anatomic understanding of its blood supply, current vascular assessment techniques, and the establishment of a novel classification system. The ultimate goal is to reduce postoperative complications and improve patient outcomes. Femoral neck fractures in adults are associated with high rates of avascular necrosis (AVN) and nonunion, largely due to disruption of the femoral head’s vascular supply. This expert consensus contains an updated vascular-based classification system and corresponding treatment strategies to improve outcomes in femoral neck fracture management. It integrates anatomic insights into femoral head blood supply, mechanisms of vascular injury, and advanced imaging assessments—especially digital subtraction angiography (DSA)—which may be used to evaluate perfusion status. A novel vascular classification system incorporating CT and DSA findings is proposed to stratify fractures into 4 types based on vascular involvement. Treatment recommendations are made accordingly, ranging from conservative management to internal fixation using bidirectional porous tantalum screws, femoral neck system, or hip arthroplasty. Emphasis is placed on preserving the epiphyseal arterial network by minimizing invasive maneuvers and controlling screw depth. For patients with complete vascular disruption, open reduction combined with microvascular reconstruction and vascularized bone grafting may be considered as an advanced option based on expert consensus. In addition, a scoring system based on patient age, fracture displacement, and vascular injury is proposed to predict complications. This consensus supports a shift from mechanically focused treatment toward a combined biomechanical-biological approach, highlighting the central role of vascular preservation in femoral head-saving strategies.

Femoral neck fractures are common in clinical practice and are associated with high rates of disability and complications. In patients undergoing hip-preserving treatments—such as fracture reduction and internal fixation—the incidence of postoperative femoral head necrosis and nonunion remains alarmingly high. Among adult patients, due to variations in fracture severity and/or surgical techniques, the reported incidence of avascular necrosis (AVN) ranges from 12% to 86%, while nonunion rates range from 7% to 33%.1–7 In recent years, vascular injury following fracture has been increasingly recognized as a key contributing factor to these postoperative complications.8–11

This work is presented as an Expert Consensus, the recommendations were developed by a multidisciplinary panel of orthopedic trauma surgeons and reconstructive microsurgeons with extensive clinical experience in the management of femoral neck fractures. A comprehensive narrative review of the current literature was conducted, including anatomic studies, clinical trials, and observational studies, to inform the consensus statements. The panel held iterative discussions to review and refine the recommendations based on available evidence and clinical experience. No formal Delphi voting process or evidence grading system (eg, GRADE) was used; all statements represent collective expert agreement on best clinical practice. It aims to assist clinicians in accurately evaluating the vascular status of the femoral head and formulating individualized treatment plans, based on an anatomic understanding of its blood supply, current vascular assessment techniques, and the establishment of a novel classification system. The ultimate goal is to reduce postoperative complications and improve patient outcomes.

ANATOMIC BASIS AND MECHANISMS OF VASCULAR INJURY IN THE FEMORAL HEAD AND NECK

Primary Vascular Supply to the Femoral Head

The blood supply to the femoral head is primarily derived from the superior, inferior, and anterior retinacular arteries, the artery of the ligamentum teres, and the intraosseous nutrient arteries of the femoral neck.12–14 Among these, the superior and inferior retinacular arteries are branches of the medial femoral circumflex artery, while the anterior retinacular artery arises from the lateral femoral circumflex artery. These 3 groups of retinacular arteries, together with the artery of the ligamentum teres, form the most extensive intraosseous vascular network within the femoral head (Fig. 1).

Vascular distribution of the retinacular arteries in the femoral head and neck. The superior retinacular artery (SRA), inferior retinacular artery (IRA), and anterior retinacular artery (ARA) are located in the posterosuperior, posteroinferior, and anterior regions of the femoral neck, respectively.

Critical Intramedullary Vascular Structure of the Femoral Head

The Epiphyseal Arterial Network

The 3 groups of retinacular arteries are interconnected through 3 major vascular networks, the epiphyseal arterial network within the femoral head, the metaphyseal arterial network, and the vascular ring located at the superior aspect of the femoral neck (Fig. 2).13 The superiorly positioned epiphyseal arterial network serves as a direct conduit linking the retinacular arterial systems with the artery of the ligamentum teres. These vessels converge near the epiphyseal plate to form the epiphyseal arterial network, from which terminal arcuate arteries branch off, forming the core blood supply of the femoral head. Once this network is disrupted, the collateral capacity among the 3 arterial systems becomes severely compromised, significantly increasing the risk of ischemic complications such as avascular necrosis.

Course of the retinacular arteries and the epiphyseal arterial network within the femoral head. The superior, inferior, and anterior retinacular arteries anastomose with branches of the epiphyseal vessels, forming the epiphyseal arterial network.

Mechanisms of Vascular Injury

Subcapital fractures are most likely to directly disrupt all retinacular arteries and the vascular ring at the superior aspect of the femoral neck. Transcervical fractures carry a moderate risk of vascular injury, whereas basal fractures typically cause the least compromise to femoral head perfusion. The degree of fracture displacement also correlates with the extent of vascular damage. Previous studies have demonstrated that the superior retinacular arterial system, as the primary source of blood supply to the femoral head, constitutes the largest and most critical vascular component.15–17

In 2017, our research revealed that the inferior retinacular artery has a luminal diameter comparable to that of the superior system. Its characteristic “high preservation rate” may be attributed to 2 key compensatory pathways: (1) the inferior retinacular artery → inferior epiphyseal artery → epiphyseal arterial network, and (2) the inferior retinacular artery → metaphyseal arterial network → epiphyseal arterial network.13 These pathways potentially support collateral circulation following femoral neck fracture.

Moreover, secondary displacement caused by muscle contraction—triggered by movement or pain—may further compromise retinacular vessels. Intraoperatively, repeated manipulation for reduction, drilling, and screw placement may also unavoidably injure the intraosseous vascular system, particularly the epiphyseal arterial network, thereby affecting the internal perfusion of the femoral head.

ASSESSMENT TECHNIQUES

Direct Imaging Techniques

Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA)

DSA is currently the most direct and dynamic method for evaluating vascular patency and may be used for preoperative assessment of femoral head perfusion. On the basis of institutional experience, DSA may be considered for patients who have minimal comorbidities, a low American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, and maintain independent mobility, daily functioning, and intact cognitive status before injury. By intra-arterial injection of iodinated contrast agents, superselective imaging of the retinacular arteries can be performed to evaluate their patency and the extent of blood supply.

Limitations of DSA and Considerations for Clinical Feasibility

Invasiveness

DSA is a minimally invasive procedure requiring arterial puncture (typically femoral artery) and intra-arterial contrast agent injection, carrying a small but non-negligible risk of complications, including puncture-site hematoma, vascular dissection, contrast-induced nephropathy, and arterial thrombosis—especially in elderly patients with comorbidities such as peripheral vascular disease or chronic kidney disease.

Cost and Availability

DSA is a resource-intensive modality that requires specialized angiographic equipment, trained interventional radiologists/orthopedic surgeons, and dedicated operating room time. It is widely available in tertiary academic medical centers and large regional hospitals but is often unavailable in community hospitals, rural medical facilities, or resource-limited settings due to high capital and operational costs.

Radiation Exposure

DSA involves ionizing radiation exposure for both the patient and the operating team; cumulative radiation dose is higher than that of conventional CT or plain radiography, which is a consideration for repeated postoperative assessments or in younger patients.

Feasibility

Preoperative DSA requires careful patient selection to balance diagnostic benefit with procedural risk, and postoperative DSA is rarely feasible in patients with poor postoperative functional status or multiple comorbidities.

Conventional Perfusion Imaging (Spalteholz Technique)

The Spalteholz technique provides a general overview of the vascular architecture within the femoral head but typically reveals only fragmented evidence of anastomoses between different vascular regions.11,17–20 Gadolinium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been used to confirm the presence of such anastomoses;21 however, due to the dense and overlapping capillary networks, these imaging modalities cannot accurately delineate the exact distribution of the anastomotic branches, nor can they reliably assess the severity of potential vascular injury.

Alternative Imaging Strategies for Vascular Assessment

Contrast-Enhanced Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) is a widely available noninvasive modality that provides 3-dimensional visualization of the retinacular arteries and femoral head perfusion. It avoids arterial puncture, has lower procedural risk than DSA, and is more cost-effective and accessible in secondary and some primary care settings. While CTA has lower temporal resolution for dynamic perfusion assessment than DSA, it can reliably identify complete retinacular artery disruption and is sufficient for guiding routine treatment decisions (eg, internal fixation vs. arthroplasty) in the absence of DSA. Radiation exposure is comparable to conventional CT, and contrast load is lower than that of DSA.

Gadolinium-enhanced magnetic resonance angiography (MRA)+Perfusion MRIContrast-enhanced MRA provides noninvasive visualization of the medial/lateral femoral circumflex arteries and their retinacular branches, with no ionizing radiation exposure—making it ideal for younger patients or those requiring repeated assessments. Perfusion MRI (including dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI) can quantitatively evaluate femoral head marrow perfusion and identify early ischemic changes, with high sensitivity for detecting avascular necrosis (AVN) and hypoperfusion. While MRA is less widely available than CTA in resource-limited settings, it is a preferred alternative in centers with MRI capability and is recommended for patients with contrast-induced nephropathy (when using gadolinium-based contrast agents with low nephrogenic systemic fibrosis risk).

Conventional MRI (T1/T2/STIR sequences)+metal artifact reduction sequence (MARS) MRINon-contrast conventional MRI is a standard, widely available modality for evaluating femoral head vascularity by detecting bone marrow edema, hemorrhage, and early ischemic changes (eg, low T1 signal intensity in the femoral head). MARS MRI is particularly valuable for postoperative vascular assessment in patients with internal fixation, as it eliminates metal artifact and allows for accurate detection of early AVN or persistent hypoperfusion. This modality is the first-line postoperative vascular assessment tool in all clinical settings, including resource-limited ones, and serves as a valid alternative to postoperative DSA.

VASCULAR CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM

The Garden classification system is based on anteroposterior (AP) radiographs, which do not provide stereoscopic evaluation and may be subject to interobserver variability, limiting its reliability. In contrast, CT imaging allows for 3-dimensional assessment of fracture displacement. Given that all retinacular arteries are attached to the surface of the femoral neck, the extent of fracture displacement can be used to infer potential damage to these vessels. With the increasing application and accessibility of digital subtraction angiography (DSA), the relationship between fracture displacement and the vascular status of the retinacular arteries has become clearer and more visualized. Thus, establishing a vascular-based classification system for femoral neck fractures may be helpful in clinical practice.

The vascular evaluation classification system, proposed in our 2022 single-center study,24 has undergone internal clinical validation in our institutional cohort of femoral neck fracture patients who underwent both CT and DSA assessment. This internal validation demonstrated that the system could effectively stratify patients by the extent of retinacular artery injury and predict the risk of postoperative AVN and nonunion in our clinical practice. However, this classification system is currently undergoing external multicenter validation, formal interobserver reliability testing, and head-to-head comparative performance assessment against established classification systems (including the Garden and Pauwels classifications). To date, the results of these ongoing investigations have not been published; thus, the clinical application of this classification system remains subject to certain limitations. Future research is therefore warranted to address these knowledge gaps through multicenter collaborative studies, standardized imaging assessment protocols, and comparative effectiveness research with traditional fracture classification systems. TREATMENT RECOMMENDATIONS

According to the American Academy of Orthopedic Surgeons (AAOS) Clinical Practice Guideline on Management of Hip Fractures in the Elderly (2015 edition)25 and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) Guideline on Hip Fracture Management (2011 edition),26 surgical intervention is the first-line treatment for femoral neck fractures. The specific surgical approach should be determined by factors such as the patient’s age, prefracture functional status, fracture type, and degree of displacement. On the basis of recent studies on femoral head and neck vascularity and postfracture vascular changes, we recommend incorporating vascular injury status into the clinical decision-making and evaluation process13,27—with imaging modalities selected based on institutional resource availability and clear delineation of essential versus optional use of advanced imaging such as DSA.

The role of reconstructive/microsurgical techniques (vascular anastomosis, vascularized bone grafting) in femoral neck fracture management is clearly delineated as a specialized femoral head-preserving strategy for selected high-risk patients; these techniques are distinct from standard orthopedic management (conservative treatment, conventional internal fixation, and arthroplasty) and require dedicated microsurgical expertise.

Conservative Treatment

Conservative management may be considered only for patients meeting all of the following objective criteria (fracture, vascular, and patient-related), to ensure clinical reproducibility and minimize the risk of secondary displacement or ischemic complications.

Fracture Displacement and Stability Criteria (Objective Imaging-Based)

Anatomically undisplaced femoral neck fracture (Garden type I, vascular classification type I) on anteroposterior/pelvic lateral X-ray and multidetector CT, with no cortical disruption, comminution, or rotational deformity of the femoral neck; fracture stability confirmed by the absence of displacement on dynamic imaging if clinically indicated.

Judged by CT findings and vascular classification system or confirmed by DSA, all superior, inferior and anterior retinacular arteries remain patent. No signs of femoral head hypoperfusion or intraosseous vascular injury are observed on perfusion CT or MRI.

Patient-Related Criteria

Ability to maintain strict nonweight-bearing status (partial or touch-down weight-bearing only) for a minimum of 12 weeks with compliant follow-up; no severe osteoporosis [T-score ≥ −2.5 on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scanning] or pathologic fracture etiology; no neurological or musculoskeletal comorbidities precluding adherence to immobilization protocols; low American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification (ASA I/II) with no medical comorbidities increasing surgical risk.

Even for patients meeting all above criteria, conservative management carries a relatively high risk of secondary fracture displacement during the treatment course,28,29 thus, close clinical and imaging follow-up (serial X-rays every 1 to 2 wk for the first 6 wk) is mandatory to monitor for displacement or early ischemic changes.

Closed Reduction and Internal Fixation

For patients under 65 years of age with nondisplaced, stable fractures, internal fixation remains a standard option.25,26,28 If DSA shows that at least 2 groups of retinacular arteries are patent, we recommend fixation using bidirectional compression porous tantalum screws. A single or double cannulated screw placed centrally may be used, with the screw depth not exceeding the physeal scar to help minimize damage to the epiphyseal basal arterial network. Finite element analysis has demonstrated that a single screw provides fixation strength and fracture fragment displacement comparable to 2 screws.30

In patients under 65 years of age with displaced fractures, where DSA indicates at least one group of retinacular vessels remains intact and fracture fragment stability is moderate or poor, we recommend fixation using bidirectional compression porous tantalum screws combined with an angular stable plate or medial support plate, femoral neck system (FNS), or dynamic hip screw (DHS) may be considered.31–36 The screw depth should not exceed the physeal scar whenever possible.

Hip Arthroplasty

For patients over 65 years of age with significantly displaced fractures and DSA findings indicating complete disruption of retinacular arterial blood supply, total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a well-established option, particularly in those who were independently mobile before injury and have no significant comorbidities. In elderly patients with limited life expectancy, low functional demands, multiple comorbidities, and no pre-existing arthritis, hemiarthroplasty may be considered as an appropriate alternative.25,26

Open Reduction and Vascularized Bone Graft/Microvascular Anastomosis

This microsurgical/reconstructive approach is a specialized femoral head-preserving strategy that requires formal microsurgical expertise (plastic, reconstructive, or orthopedic microsurgery training) and is only indicated for a highly selected patient cohort in specialized centers with microsurgical resources. Specifically, this approach may be considered for young patients (65 y of age or younger) with Garden type IV/vascular classification type IV femoral neck fractures who have confirmed complete retinacular artery disruption on DSA and are not suitable for arthroplasty.37 The procedure combines open anatomic reduction and rigid internal fixation with microsurgical retinacular artery anastomosis (to restore femoral head perfusion) and vascularized bone grafting (typically iliac crest or fibular grafts with pedicled vascular supply, to enhance osteogenesis and revascularization), with the dual goals of reducing nonunion and post-traumatic avascular necrosis risk. For fractures with high shear forces, such as Pauwels type II or III, the use of an angular stable device or medial buttress plate may be beneficial.30

Minimally Invasive Fixation

For elderly patients or those in poor general condition with limited tolerance for surgery, closed reduction and internal fixation under local anesthesia may be considered, even in the presence of fracture displacement. A single bidirectional compression porous tantalum screw or a cannulated screw can be used for fixation. This approach minimizes surgical trauma, alleviates pain, facilitates nursing care, and may help avoid complications associated with prolonged bed rest.31,32,38

In conclusion, when managing femoral neck fractures, it is essential to provide sufficient mechanical stability to the fracture site while preserving the epiphyseal basal arterial network within the femoral head as much as possible. In patients with femoral neck fractures, priority should be given to evaluating whether femoral head preservation is feasible. If preservation is possible, the chosen treatment strategy should aim to achieve stable fixation while safeguarding the vascular supply to the femoral head. If preservation is not feasible, the most appropriate arthroplasty option should be selected based on factors such as the patient’s age, functional demands, and comorbidities (Table 1).

KEY TECHNIQUES FOR VASCULAR PRESERVATION

Preoperative Skin Traction

Preoperative skin traction may be considered routinely to maintain the stability of the fracture fragments and prevent rotational displacement or impaction. This may help avoid torsion or compression of the surrounding retinacular vessels, thereby reducing the risk of secondary vascular injury.39

Minimizing Closed Reduction Attempts

Internal fixation under closed reduction is preferred whenever possible to avoid damaging the vascular ring at the superior aspect of the femoral neck, which could reduce blood flow to the femoral head. If closed reduction fails after 3 attempts, open reduction may be needed, as repeated manipulation during closed reduction may cause torsion or tearing of the retinacular vessels.40–43

Kirschner Wire/Screw Depth

Kirschner wires are commonly used for positioning before implant placement. However, if the Kirschner wire penetrates beyond the physeal scar, it may damage the epiphyseal basal arterial network within the femoral head and compromise its internal blood supply.13,24

Considerations for Screw Placement

Repeated or multiple screw insertions and adjustments should be avoided. Provided that sufficient fracture stability can be achieved, the number of screws should be minimized. A central fixation strategy is preferred, with the screw depth not exceeding the physeal scar. These measures may help to minimize damage to the retinacular vascular network near the physeal scar.13,27,31

Timing of Vascularized Bone Graft Reconstruction

In young patients with Garden type III or IV fractures, where preoperative vascular classification suggests poor retinacular perfusion, we recommend intraoperative exposure of the fracture site, creation of a cortical window at the femoral head-neck junction, and implantation of a vascularized bone graft. This approach can significantly reduce the risk of nonunion and post-traumatic avascular necrosis.37

Vascularized Bone Graft/Anastomosis

For young patients with significantly displaced fractures and DSA findings confirming disruption of the retinacular arteries and loss of blood supply, microvascular anastomosis of the retinacular arteries can be considered to restore perfusion and improve long-term outcomes.44

COMPLICATIONS AND SCORING SYSTEM

Preoperative Prediction Model

A comprehensive femoral neck fracture scoring system is proposed based on 3 major risk factors: patient age, degree/stability of fracture displacement, and extent of vascular injury. The system has a total score of 10 points: age (2 points), degree/stability of displacement (3 points), and vascular assessment (5 points). Scores of 1 to 3 indicate low risk, 4 to 6 indicate moderate risk, and 7 or above indicate high risk. Higher scores are associated with poorer fracture healing outcomes and a greater likelihood of post-traumatic avascular necrosis (Table 2).

Femoral Neck Fracture Scoring System

This femoral neck fracture scoring system is developed based on our institutional clinical experience13,31 and retrospective analysis of risk factors for postoperative complications, with internal validation in our single-center cohort of 312 patients showing moderate predictive value for postoperative AVN and nonunion (AUC=0.78 for AVN, 0.75 for nonunion). Similar to the vascular classification system, this scoring model lacks external multicenter validation, and its clinical utility awaits further refinement and verification in subsequent research.

Postoperative DSA and MRI

Routine postoperative DSA is not recommended for all patients with femoral neck fractures, given its invasiveness, cost, radiation exposure, and limited practicality in routine clinical practice. Postoperative DSA is used only for highly selected patients in tertiary/microsurgical centers based on explicit clinical and imaging indications, with the primary goal of assessing retinacular artery perfusion recovery and guiding targeted management of persistent hypoperfusion. For the diagnosis of postoperative AVN and routine vascular monitoring in all clinical settings, MRI is the first-line noninvasive diagnostic tool and is superior to conventional radiography. The use of metal artifact reduction sequence (MARS) MRI effectively eliminates artifacts from internal fixation devices, improves the detection rate of femoral head AVN following internal fixation, and provides prognostic information—patients with normal MARS-MRI findings generally have better outcomes.45–47 Contrast-enhanced MRI can identify early abnormalities in bone marrow perfusion, and gadopentetate dimeglumine administration can differentiate between well-perfused and ischemic regions of the femoral head,48–50 serving as a valid, noninvasive alternative to postoperative DSA for routine vascular assessment in the majority of patients.

CONCLUSION

Early surgical intervention is generally supported for femoral neck fractures, with internal fixation aimed at femoral head preservation as the preferred treatment approach. In selecting fixation methods, both mechanical stability and vascular preservation and reconstruction should be considered, integrating biomechanical and biological factors. This expert opinion promotes a shift from mechanically focused treatment toward a combined biomechanical-biological approach for femoral neck fracture management, with vascular assessment at the core of precise femoral head-saving strategies. For cases where femoral head preservation is not feasible, arthroplasty remains the definitive treatment.

Future efforts should focus on comprehensive evaluation strategies, incorporation of microsurgical techniques, innovations in bone implant materials, and multicenter clinical validation to further reduce complication rates and improve long-term patient quality of life.52
