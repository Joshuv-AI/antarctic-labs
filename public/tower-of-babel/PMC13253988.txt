The Prevalence of Substance Use Among Students and Associated Factors: A Systematic Review With Narrative Synthesis
Authors: Nikta Bazleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSubstance use is a major public health and social problem that affects individuals of all ages, particularly adolescents and young adults, across every society. School as an educational environment can be suitable for education and orientation towards substances.MethodsOnline databases, including Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase, and domestic databases, including SID, Magiran, and RICeST, were systematically searched from 2003 to now using Persian and English languages. Through narrative synthesis of prevalence data from 35 studies, we investigated the patterns of substance use among Iranian school students by province, gender, and substance type.ResultsNarrative synthesis of 35 cross‐sectional studies (total n ≈ 45,000) showed wide variation in lifetime prevalence: any substance use 1.4–55.9% (median ≈ 20%), cigarette smoking 0.4–32.7% (median ≈ 15%), alcohol 6.8–37.7% (median ≈ 13%), hookah up to 59%. Rates were consistently higher among boys than girls and in southeastern provinces like Kerman and Zahedan, located near the Afghanistan border, a major source and transit route for opiates.ConclusionThe findings indicate the necessity of implementing and developing comprehensive primary prevention strategies and policies based on different cultures, regions, geographies, ages, and ethnicities. Also, the educational content of textbooks on planning life success skills in the educational system can be effective in solving this problem.

Introduction

Undoubtedly, substance use is one of the disasters that threatens all people, particularly young people. The triangle of poverty, ignorance, and addiction takes dynamism, growth, activity, and creativity from some young people and makes them sick and come across as lacking individuality. In addition, addiction is associated with profound mental, physical, and social consequences and is ranked among the most serious challenges facing Iranian society after economic problems. The field of drugs includes theoretical foundations such as sociological theories, psychological theories, social psychological theories, and biological theories. According to research, addiction is related to individual factors and external and environmental factors (Figure 1) [1, 2]. The problem of addiction in Iranian society ranks third among thirteen problems after economic problems, and the majority of people believe that this problem is more severe than average. Studies in recent years have shown that the topic of addiction has entered research more seriously [3, 4]. In recent years, extensive national studies have shown that opium and heroin are known as common addictive substances in Iran. The use of substances such as ecstasy and stimulants has also increased significantly [5]. According to a global report, 2.29% to 3.11% of life years are lost each year due to disability‐adjusted life years in men, which is approximately 2.6 times higher than in Iranian women [6].

According to the findings, adolescence can be seen as a pivotal period of every individual's life and a unique process [7]. This period is a critical period of growth and development that causes profound physical and mental changes in the person and disrupts the physical and mental order of the teenager. Adolescents in this period are emotionally immature, limited in experience, and socially very fragile and impressionable. The formation of attitudes resulting from information and knowledge, beliefs and emotions, as well as the amount of value they attach to substances, shapes their attitude and motivation towards substances [8]. Studies have shown that the first experience of substance abuse often begins at a young age, and on the other hand, drug use mainly begins with the use of cigarettes and alcohol. Therefore, it seems that the initial study of the prevalence of drug abuse, including smoking and alcohol, could be the first step in the prevention and control of drug use. According to the statistics on the prevalence of drug use among teenagers in Iran and most of the countries involved with this problem, schools as an educational place can play an essential role in the addiction of teenagers because teenagers spend half of their day in school. Culture is both a product and a process. School culture is a set of common principles and solutions for the outside world and internal cohesion [9]. In the present systematic review with narrative synthesis, an attempt has been made to review the statistics of addiction in Iranian schools and the ways to deal with it by collecting essential articles and reports from the past years until today.

Methods

The search strategy was conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 statement using observational Studies in Epidemiology [10] and PRISMA guidelines [11] based on studies conducted among school students from different cities in Iran. The study was systematically conducted in online databases, including PubMed, Institute of Scientific Information (ISI), Web of Science, Scopus, and domestic databases such as SID (Scientific Information database, http://sid.ir/), Magiran (http://www.magiran.com/), IRANDOC (http://irandoc.ac.ir/), and Regional Information Center for Science and Technology (RICeST) (https://ricest.ac.ir/). Search strategy was done in both Persian and English languages from January 2003 to to April 2026 with ([“Substance‐Related Disorders” OR “Drug Abuse” OR “Street Drugs” OR “Tobacco Use Disorder” OR “Alcohol Drinking” OR “Smoking” OR “substance use” OR “drug abuse” OR “addiction” OR “smoking” OR “cigarette” OR “tobacco use” OR “hookah” OR “water pipe” OR “alcohol” OR “heroin” OR “hashish” OR “cannabis” OR “opium” OR “methamphetamine” OR “tramadol” OR “cocaine” OR “marijuana” OR “morphine” OR “ecstasy” OR “stimulants”] AND [“Prevalence” OR “Risk Factors” OR “Epidemiologic Factors” OR “prevalence” OR “incidence” OR “epidemiology” OR “associated factors” OR “risk factors” OR “determinants” OR “coping strategies”] AND [“Adolescent*” OR “Student*” OR “teen*” OR “youth” OR “school*” OR “high school” OR “secondary school”] AND [“Iran” OR “Iranian” OR “Persia*”]). The year 2003 was chosen as the lower limit because it corresponds to the earliest electronically indexed studies on high‐school substance use in the searched databases that met the inclusion criteria. Persian keywords and their English equivalents were used to search for internal sources. Relevant publication references were also manually studied, and authors of selected articles were contacted via email to submit any additional studies for analysis. The systematic review included observational studies (cross‐sectional, prevalence, case‐control) conducted in Iran, focusing on high school students. Only English or Persian articles were considered. Exclusions comprised qualitative studies, reviews, editorials, commentaries, and non‐primary data sources. Duplicate studies from the same population were excluded, with only the most recent or comprehensive version retained. The search identified 412 records. After removing 98 duplicates, 314 unique records remained. After title/abstract screening, 193 were excluded, leaving 121 for full‐text review. After full‐text assessment, 85 were excluded (10 not relevant, 12 reviews, 8 no prevalence data, 15 irrelevant population, 6 low quality, 4 duplicate data source, and 30 for other reasons such as not being primary studies or lacking full‐text access), resulting in 36 studies included in the narrative synthesis (see Figure 2). Of the 36 included studies, 15 were published in Persian‐language journals. Extracted information and characteristics of all studies included: first author names, publication date, the year the study was conducted, study population (school students), study design, total sample size, and geographic location. Prevalence estimates were synthesized narratively because of substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity (different substances assessed, lifetime vs. current use, and variable reporting formats across studies). Qualitative analysis was not performed. Patterns by gender, geographic region, and substance type were described, and ranges and approximate medians were calculated where data allowed. According to the reported P‐value, there is a publication bias. A P‐value less than 0.05 indicates a bias in the publication.

Narrative synthesis of the 36 cross‐sectional studies (total n ≈ 54,402, including the large recent nationwide study by Hadianfard et al. with n = 9402 [12]) showed wide variation in lifetime prevalence of substance use among Iranian high school students. Gender Differences

Substance use prevalence was consistently higher among boys than girls across all studies that reported gender‐stratified data (Table 1). Boys showed markedly elevated rates for any substance use, cigarette smoking, alcohol, hookah, and illicit drugs.

Regional Variations

Nationwide data indicated a lifetime any‐substance‐use prevalence of 39.2% [12]. Higher prevalence was observed in southeastern provinces (e.g., Kerman and Zahedan) located near the Afghanistan border and major opiate trafficking routes. Lifetime any‐substance‐use rates reached as high as 55.9% in some southeastern samples, compared with substantially lower rates in northern and northwestern provinces (Table 1).

Substance‐Specific Trends

Summary ranges and approximate medians (derived as the middle value of the ordered prevalence estimates) are presented in Table 2. Lifetime prevalence of any substance use (12 studies) ranged from 1.4% to 55.9% (median ≈ 20%). Cigarette smoking (18 studies) ranged from 0.4% to 32.7% (median ≈ 15%). Alcohol use (15 studies) ranged from 6.8% to 37.7% (median ≈ 13%). Hookah use (8 studies) reached up to 59% (median ≈ 25%). These ranges are broadly consistent with a previous national meta‐analysis of 58 studies conducted between 2000 and 2014.

Detailed Findings From Individual Studies

In 2020, researchers in Jahrom (Fars province) assessed the predictive role of self‐efficacy on substance‐use behaviors among 405 high school students selected by multi‐stage cluster sampling. Data were analyzed with SPSS. A strong negative correlation emerged between self‐efficacy and drug‐use tendency (r = −0.598, p < 0.001). Academic grade, parental occupation and education, and smoking history among friends and parents were also significantly associated with both self‐efficacy and tendency to use drugs [48]. A cross‐sectional survey of 420 high school students used multi‐stage random sampling. Validated scales measured addiction tendency, family communication patterns, and school culture [49]. In Semnan, lifetime drug use was reported by 7.8% of 400 high school students (26 boys, 5 girls) [50]. A qualitative study in the suburbs of Birjand explored factors influencing substance‐use behavior using the COM‐B model [51]. In disadvantaged areas of Qom, loneliness significantly mediated the associations between perceived support, psychological distress, and readiness for addiction [52]. A 2015 study in Isfahan province (3223 students) identified parental divorce and seeking pleasure as the strongest correlates of drug‐use propensity [53].

The prevalence of drug use in schools based on various parameters in the researchers’ studies is shown in Table 1. In Zahedan, regular smoking rates were 0.4% for girls and 2.3% for boys [54]. Among students in Kerman, the mean age of first use was 14.0 years for cigarettes and 13.9 years for hookah [55]. A 2017–2018 survey of 2000 students in Kerman found tobacco (12.9%), alcohol (16.4%), and sedatives (5.1%) as the most prevalent substances, with lower rates among girls [56]. In Kerman province, lifetime substance use was 55.9% in boys and 36.6% in girls, with hookah (41.8%) and alcohol (16.9%) most common [15]. In Guilan province, approximately 12% had smoked at least once and lifetime prevalences were cigarettes 20%, alcohol 10.5%, opium 2.4%, ecstasy 1.2%, hashish 2%, and heroin 0.3% [57]. In Karaj, lifetime use of any drug was 56% in boys and 44% in girls [58]. In Nazarabad, lifetime substance use (including cigarettes) was 24.5% [59]. In Hamadan, 18% of 800 students reported lifetime use of at least one substance [60]. In East Azerbaijan province, latent class analysis identified low‐risk (86%), tobacco experimenters (9.5%), and high‐risk (4.6%) groups [61]. In Qazvin, hookah use was 59%, cigarette smoking 32.7%, alcohol 16.7%, and other drugs 7.3% [62]. In Ilam, lifetime smoking was 11.4% and other substance prevalences were alcohol 11.1%, opium 2.8%, tramadol 7.6%, hashish 3.3%, ecstasy 2.7%, and methamphetamine 2.1% [63]. In Shiraz, current smoking was 14%, lifetime alcohol use 13.5%, and psychotropic‐drug use 1% [22]. The characteristics of the 35 included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Studies 11–35 (nationwide and from Hamadan, Tabriz, Tehran, Yazd, Semnan, and other provinces) further confirmed the same wide geographic and gender gradients described above (Table 1) [64]. The summary ranges are presented in Table 2.

Associated Factors and Coping Strategies

Intervention studies and associated‐factor analyses further examined coping strategies. In Rasht, negative academic emotions and academic alienation showed positive associations with substance‐use tendency, while general self‐efficacy showed a negative association; the three variables explained 62% of the variance [65]. A quasi‐experimental study in Malayer found that life‐skills training produced a statistically significant increase in resilience among high‐risk students [66]. In Bojnord, a positive‐youth‐development programme significantly improved self‐efficacy and reduced negative identity among students at risk of addiction [67]. Among 375 high school students in Tabriz, conformity orientation in family communication was positively correlated with addiction tendency, whereas dialogue orientation and all measured school‐culture components were negatively correlated [68]. A survey of 750 high school students in Rasht reported a moderate inverse correlation between social‐skills score and positive attitude toward substance abuse (r = –0.442, p < 0.001) [69]. In Naqadeh, self‐control training significantly lowered addiction‐readiness scores compared with the control group [70].

Discussion

Coping Strategies

The protective role of self‐efficacy, positive family communication patterns, and school culture identified in the Results section aligns with international evidence on resilience‐building and social‐development models [6, 7]. School‐based life‐skills training, positive‐youth‐development programmes, and self‐control interventions appear promising for reducing addiction tendency and increasing resilience among Iranian adolescents. However, most of these programmers have been evaluated in small‐scale or quasi‐experimental studies; their long‐term effectiveness and scalability require evaluation in larger randomized controlled trials. These findings reinforce the need for culturally tailored, multi‐level prevention strategies that simultaneously address individual (e.g., self‐efficacy) and environmental (e.g., family and school) risk and protective factors.

Heterogeneity

Substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity was observed across studies, arising from differences in substance definitions (lifetime vs. current use), sampling frames, data‐collection tools, and geographic coverage. This heterogeneity precluded quantitative meta‐analysis and necessitated narrative synthesis.

Comparison With Existing Literature

The reported ranges are broadly consistent with a previous national meta‐analysis of 58 studies (2000–2014) that found pooled lifetime prevalences of 16.8% for cigarette smoking, 14.7% for alcohol, and 23.1% for hookah. Higher rates in southeastern provinces are well documented and are attributed to geographic proximity to Afghanistan, the world's largest opium producer, and major drug‐trafficking routes.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the high heterogeneity prevented meta‐analysis. Second, many included studies did not report 95% confidence intervals or conduct quality assessments. Third, coverage of all Iranian provinces remains incomplete. Finally, publication bias cannot be formally assessed because of the narrative approach. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

Addiction as an individual and social problem has negative consequences for the person and society, especially in the high‐risk age group of youth. The outcomes of this study showed that, in general, the prevalence of substance use among Iranian students, particularly cigarettes and alcohol, is high, which can be reduced with preventive methods. On the other hand, students in the southeastern regions of Iran, such as Zahedan and Kerman, are more exposed to the use of opioids, which can be attributed to the proximity of these areas to the border of Afghanistan, which is a significant producer of opioids such as opium, heroin, and methamphetamine in the world, as well as the transit routes of the trafficking of Afghan opiates. According to the study, it is recommended that life skills training centers and family counseling centers be established to prevent this global concern. Considering that group education on the effects of drug abuse can create a negative attitude towards addiction in students, it is necessary to pay attention to such educational programs in schools. In general, education should make more efforts to provide educational content in textbooks and plan life success skills to help solve this problem.
