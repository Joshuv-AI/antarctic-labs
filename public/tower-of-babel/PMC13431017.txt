Evaluating the association between potentially morally injurious events (PMIEs) and types of psychological distress. A systematic review
Authors: Andreana Farrell Kakouris, Dominic Murphy, Neil Greenberg, Rebecca McClintock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Potentially morally injurious events (PMIEs) are experiences that violate deeply held moral values through one’s own transgressive actions (commission), failing to prevent or witnessing wrongdoing (omission), or betrayal by a trusted person. PMIE exposure has been linked to psychological distress, but it is unclear whether different PMIE subtypes confer distinct patterns of risk. This systematic review examined associations between specific PMIE subtypes and key psychological outcomes.Methods: Multiple databases were searched for quantitative studies that assessed PMIE subtypes in relation to PTSD or complex PTSD, depression, anxiety, alcohol misuse, and suicidal ideation or self-injurious behaviour. Fifteen studies published in the last seven years (total N ≈ 21,700) in military, healthcare, veteran, and civilian samples met inclusion criteria. A Population–Exposure–Outcome framework guided data extraction, and findings were synthesised narratively by outcome and subtype.Results: Betrayal-type PMIEs were frequently associated with PTSD, depression, and anxiety across included studies. Commission-type events were associated with several outcomes, including PTSD, depression, and suicidality, although findings varied across samples and analytic approaches. Omission-type exposures showed more variable associations overall, although several studies reported significant links with PTSD, depression, anxiety, or suicidality.Conclusions: PMIE subtypes showed overlapping but potentially different patterns of associations with psychological distress. Across the available literature, betrayal-related PMIEs emerge as the most prominent and consistent correlate of PTSD, depression, and anxiety, while commission-related events were also linked with distress, particularly suicidality, and omission-related findings were less consistent. These findings support considering PMIE subtypes separately in assessment and future research.KEYWORDS: Moral injury, potentially morally injurious events, psychological distress, PTSD, depression, substance misuse, review

Introduction

Potentially morally injurious events (PMIEs) involve perpetrating or witnessing acts that violate deeply held moral beliefs (Litz et al., 2009) or experiencing betrayal by a leader or other trusted person or authority (Shay, 1994). Such events can disrupt emotional, psychological, behavioural, social, and spiritual functioning, and may culminate in moral injury. The concept of moral injury is a relatively young and still-evolving construct whose conceptual boundaries remain under discussion, particularly regarding the relative emphasis placed on event exposure, moral emotions, identity disruption, and relational or spiritual harm. In this review, moral injury is conceptualised as a multisystemic set of consequences characterised by intense moral emotions (e.g. shame, guilt, self-loathing, and anger), disruptions to identity and social trust, and functional impairment in relationships and role functioning (Jinkerson, 2016; Litz & Walker, 2025). Moral injury overlaps with but is not reducible to established psychiatric diagnoses. Whilst many studies quantify sequelae using PTSD, depression and anxiety measures, moral injury is better conceived as a moral and relational wound that may cut across diagnostic categories. Conceptual work therefore emphasises both symptom-level consequences and broader moral, existential and social processes that are not yet fully captured by standard diagnostic frameworks (Currier et al., 2020; Litz & Walker, 2025). In the face of a PMIE, individuals may appraise the violation as reflecting global, internal and stable deficits, fostering profound moral dissonance that, if unresolved, can precipitate important clinical outcomes including depression, PTSD and suicidality (Levi-Belz & Zerach, 2018).

PMIE exposure is broadly associated with PTSD, depression, anxiety, substance misuse and suicidality (Griffin et al., 2019), yet most reviews treat PMIEs as unitary, obscuring whether specific subtypes confer distinct risks. Emerging work suggests meaningful differentiation across military and civilian settings. In military samples, self-transgressions (commission) predict elevated PTSD and substance misuse (Maguen et al., 2010), failures to prevent harm (omission) are linked to PTSD and major depressive disorder (Jinkerson, 2016), and betrayal by comrades or leaders is strongly associated with suicide attempts (Maguen et al., 2022). Beyond the military, PMIE exposure predicts PTSD, depression and suicidal ideation in diverse professions, and during the COVID-19 pandemic frontline healthcare staff scoring above cut-off on the Moral Injury Events Scale (MIES) were more likely to meet criteria for PTSD, anxiety, depression and burnout (Williamson et al., 2018, 2023). Together, these findings indicate that commission, omission and betrayal exposures may each confer distinct vulnerabilities to specific forms of psychological distress.

No review has yet systematically mapped how PMIE domains relate to specific patterns of psychological distress. This review synthesises 15 quantitative studies (N ≈ 21,700) across military, civilian and occupational cohorts. Guided by a Population–Exposure–Outcome framework, it addresses two aims: (1) to examine whether different types of PMIEs are related to different patterns of psychological distress; and (2) to identify which patterns of distress are associated with specific PMIE subtypes (for example betrayal, commission and omission).

This was done by examining whether PMIE subtypes show differential associations with six outcomes: PTSD or complex PTSD, depression, combined distress, anxiety, alcohol misuse and suicidal ideation or self-injurious thoughts and behaviours. By delineating these domain-specific links, the review seeks to refine theoretical models of moral injury and inform more targeted clinical assessment and intervention.

Methods

Search strategy

Peer-reviewed journal articles published in English between 2019 and 27 May 2026 were searched in EMBASE, MEDLINE, PsycINFO and Web of Science. Keywords and MeSH terms relating to moral injury, potentially morally injurious events (PMIEs) and psychological outcomes were combined (full strategy in Supplementary File 1). Terms for PMIEs and moral injury were specific to aid identification, and outcome terms were broad to capture a wide range of impacts.

Search results were imported into EndNote, duplicates were removed, and the remaining records were screened at title, abstract and full text stages. No meta-analysis was planned because of heterogeneity in study designs, PMIE subtypes and outcome measures. A structured narrative synthesis was therefore undertaken in line with Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guidance.

Study selection and data extraction

Inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarised in Table 1. Cross-sectional studies published in English and in peer-reviewed journals were included in the review. Qualitative studies, systematic review/meta-analyses, studies using secondary data, opinion articles, grey literature and non-English publications were excluded.

Records were imported into Rayyan for screening. Titles and abstracts were screened against eligibility criteria, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant articles. To check reliability, a second researcher (RM) independently screened 20% of records at each stage; discrepancies were resolved through discussion. For included studies, we extracted: authors, year and country, aims, sample size and demographics, inclusion/exclusion criteria, data collection procedures, measures, analytic approach, PMIE prevalence, and psychological outcomes.

Quality appraisal

Methodological quality was assessed using the Standard Quality Assessment Criteria for Evaluating Primary Research Papers (Kmet et al., 2004), selected for its applicability to diverse quantitative designs (Supplementary File 3). Each item was rated ‘yes’ = 2, ‘partial’ = 1, or ‘no’ = 0; items not applicable to a given design were coded ‘n/a’ and omitted from the denominator. A summary score (0–1) was calculated by dividing the obtained score by the maximum possible score. Higher scores indicate stronger methodological quality. Quality ratings were not used to exclude studies but informed interpretation of findings in the narrative synthesis. All studies were assessed independently by two assessors (AK and RM), who met to discuss and review any discrepancies in scoring.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis approach (Popay et al., 2006) was used to integrate findings across heterogeneous studies. This was appropriate given variation in populations, outcome measures, and conceptualisation and measurement of PMIEs, as well as limited availability of validated PMIE measures outside military contexts, which precluded statistical pooling.

Guided by a preliminary theoretical framework derived from the moral injury literature, we first summarised each study in terms of PMIE subtype(s), mental health outcomes, and population. We then examined patterns of association between specific PMIE subtypes (e.g. betrayal, self-perpetrated, omissions) and forms of psychological distress (e.g. PTSD, depression, suicidality), taking account of study quality, measurement variation, and generalisability across settings.

The synthesis focused on five core psychological outcomes: PTSD, depression, anxiety, suicidality, and alcohol misuse. These domains were the most consistently assessed across studies using standardised, validated measures, allowing meaningful comparison despite heterogeneity in samples and designs. Initial scoping identified additional outcomes (e.g. burnout, religious or spiritual struggles, shame, guilt), but these were measured infrequently or inconsistently and therefore could not be synthesised systematically. Although relevant to the broader moral injury construct, they were excluded to preserve methodological coherence.

Results

Study selection

The database search yielded 1,525 records. After removing 634 duplicates, 891 titles/abstracts were screened, of which 798 were excluded. Ninety-three full-text articles were assessed and 15 studies met inclusion criteria (Figure 1). Screening was intentionally broad at title/abstract stage, with more stringent criteria at full text. Studies whose primary outcomes did not correspond to clinically recognised distress (PTSD, depression, anxiety, suicidality, or substance use) were excluded, leading to the removal of 14 papers focused on related but non-clinical constructs.

Study characteristics

Fifteen cross-sectional studies published between 2019 and 2025 were included (Table 2). Studies were conducted in Israel, the United Kingdom, and the United States, and sampled combat and military veterans, active-duty soldiers, health and social care workers, veterinarians, teachers, protestors, and civilians. Sample sizes ranged from 50 to 12,965 participants, with mean ages spanning approximately 25–54 years. All studies examined patterns of psychological distress following PMIE exposure. Two publications (Bravo et al., 2020; Kelley et al., 2019) drew on the same parent dataset/sample. These were treated as linked reports of a single underlying study for study-level characteristics, while outcome-specific findings were extracted separately.

PMIE exposure distributions

Most studies reported descriptive statistics for the three core PMIE domains. Nine provided means and standard deviations for self-transgression (commission), eight for other-related transgression (omission), and seven for betrayal. Some combined domains into a single ‘perpetration’ score or reported only subgroup contrasts rather than overall means. Scoring approaches also varied, with most using summed subscale scores and one averaging item-level ratings to derive mean domain scores.

Nine studies reported prevalence of endorsement (at least ‘slightly agree’) for each domain. Across studies, self-transgressions were least frequently endorsed (6–56.7%), whereas other-transgressions (15–92.7%) and betrayal (22–68.9%) tended to be more common. Overall, the distributions indicate substantial exposure to PMIEs in both military and civilian cohorts, with witnessing and betrayal events generally more prevalent than self-transgressive acts.

Measure of PMIEs, moral injury and psychological distress

Thirteen studies assessed PMIE exposure using variants of the Moral Injury Events Scale (MIES; Nash et al., 2013). Nine applied the original or lightly modified MIES, three used the civilian adaptation (MIES-C; Morriss & Berle, 2023), one supplemented the MIES with the Moral Injury Symptom Scale–Health Professional (MISS-HP; Mantri et al., 2020), and one with the Moral Injury Questionnaire–Military version (MIQ-M; Currier et al., 2015). Two additional linked reports from the same data sample (Bravo et al., 2020; Kelley et al., 2019) used the 17-item Expression of Moral Injury Scale – Military Version (EMIS-M; Currier et al., 2018).

Nine studies evaluated PTSD or complex PTSD symptoms, using the PCL-5 (n = 4; Weathers et al., 2013), the civilian PCL-6 (n = 1; Goldberg & Hillier, 1979), and the ICD-11 International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ; Cloitre et al., 2018; n = 4). Depression symptoms were assessed in eleven studies. Of these, nine used measures from the Patient Health Questionnaire series – PHQ-2 (n = 2), PHQ-4 for common distress (n = 1), PHQ-8 (n = 2), and PHQ-9 (n = 4) (Kroenke et al., 2009) while one study used the Mood and Anxiety Symptom Questionnaire (MASQ; Clark & Watson, 1991) and another used the DSM-5 Self-Rated Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure-Adult (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Anxiety symptoms were assessed in five studies: three used the GAD-7 (Kroenke et al., 2001), one used an adapted GAD-2 (Plummer et al., 2016), and one used the DSM-5 Self-Rated Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure-Adult (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Suicidal ideation and behaviours were assessed in six studies: four used the Suicidal Behaviours Questionnaire–Revised (SBQ-R; Osman et al., 2001), one used adapted items from the Youth Risk Behaviour Surveillance System (CDC, 2012) and one used the six-item suicidality subscale of the Inventory of Depression and Anxiety Symptoms Suicide Scale (Watson et al., 2007). Alcohol misuse appeared in three studies using the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT; Saunders et al., 1993).

Definitions and scoring of PMIEs

Across the fifteen included reports, PMIE exposure was operationalised using five instruments. Ten studies used the original 9-item Moral Injury Events Scale (MIES) generally administered on a 1–6 Likert scale and scored by summing items into continuous domain or total scores, although one study averaged item ratings and another modified the item structure to separate potentially morally injurious acts from their effects. Three studies in civilian samples used the 10-item MIES-Civilian (MIES-C) on the same response scale. One healthcare study used the 10-item MISS-HP with a 10-point Likert format, and one military study applied the MIQ-M, focusing on 14 ‘cause’ items rated on a 1–4 scale. Two linked reports from the same data sample used the 17-item Expressions of Moral Injury Scale – Military Version (EMIS-M, Currier et al., 2018).

PMIE subtypes

Eight studies assessed all three core PMIE domains: self-transgression (commission), other-transgression (omission), and betrayal; often reporting both total and subscale scores. One study combined self- and other-transgressions into a single ‘perpetration’ category analysed alongside betrayal (Zerach & Levi-Belz, 2019). Two studies focused on a single subtype: self-related PMIEs in combat veterans (Schwartz et al., 2022) and betrayal events in civilians exposed to terror (Levi-Belz et al., 2024), and three further studies (including two linked-reports) assessed commission and omission domains (Bravo et al., 2020; Kelley et al., 2019; Williams & Berenbaum, 2019).

Associations with psychological outcomes

PTSD and C-PTSD

Nine studies examined PTSD or related symptomatology, with one also assessing complex PTSD. Betrayal showed the most consistent association with PTSD across samples, particularly in large healthcare cohorts where betrayal and commission PMIEs substantially increased the odds of probable PTSD/C-PTSD, and omission also showed significant effects. Veteran studies also indicated associations between PTSD and transgression-based PMIEs, particularly omission, although findings for commission were less consistent across studies. Taken together, the evidence suggests that betrayal-related PMIEs were most consistently associated with PTSD across studies, while evidence for omission and commission was more mixed, and formal comparisons between subtype associations were not consistently available.

Depression and anxiety

Nine studies investigated depressive symptoms. Across diverse samples including healthcare workers, civilians, protestors, teachers and veterans, betrayal generally emerged as the most consistent correlate of depression. In large healthcare and civilian cohorts, betrayal was associated with markedly elevated odds of clinically significant depressive symptoms, although in one large healthcare-worker study commission PMIEs showed the largest association, with betrayal and omission also significant. When self- and other-transgressions were combined into a single perpetration score, this composite was modestly associated with depression, whereas betrayal alone was not always significant, suggesting that perpetration-related PMIEs, including self-directed violations, may also be relevant to depressive symptoms in some contexts. Overall, betrayal showed the most consistent links to depression across studies, with commission effects appearing more sample-dependent and omission typically weaker.

Five studies examined anxiety, producing more mixed findings. In a small healthcare sample, PMIE domains showed minimal associations with anxiety, whereas in a large healthcare cohort, all three domains predicted elevated anxiety symptoms, with the largest estimates observed for commission and betrayal. In an active-duty soldier sample, betrayal alone was significantly associated with anxiety. Additional evidence from combat-wounded veterans and teachers also supported positive associations between PMIEs and anxiety, with betrayal showing the strongest association in the teacher sample. A separate study using a brief combined anxiety-depression distress measure found no significant associations between any PMIE domain and this composite outcome. These results suggest that PMIE–anxiety relationships may be sensitive to population and context, with betrayal emerging as the most consistent correlate across studies.

Suicidal ideation and behaviour

Six studies explored suicidal ideation and self-injurious thoughts and behaviours. Across these, self-transgressive PMIEs showed the clearest associations with suicidality, predicting higher suicidal ideation scores and increased incidence rates even after adjusting for depression and other risk factors in some studies. There was also evidence for associations with omission-based PMIEs, although this was less uniform and included findings grom a small veteran sample, whilst betrayal findings were mixed across studies. Overall, self-directed moral violations appeared most consistently linked with suicidality in the available studies, with a more variable pattern for omission- and betrayal-related events.

Alcohol misuse

Three studies investigated alcohol-related outcomes. In a small veterinarian sample, none of the PMIE domains were significantly associated with hazardous drinking. Similarly, in a small veteran sample, neither commission- nor omission-related PMIEs were significantly associated with alcohol misuse. In contrast, in a large healthcare cohort, commission PMIEs most strongly predicted risky alcohol use, with omission also significant and betrayal non-significant. Overall, the evidence for PMIE-alcohol misuse associations was limited and mixed, although commission-related PMIEs appeared most relevant in the largest available sample (Table 3).

Discussion

Summary of key findings

Across fifteen included studies in military, healthcare, civilian and veteran populations, betrayal-related PMIEs showed the most prominent and consistent associations with psychological distress, particularly PTSD, depression and anxiety. Commission events were also related to these outcomes and to alcohol misuse, although effects were more variable and often weaker in smaller samples. Omission events generally showed weaker and less consistent associations but were linked with several outcomes, including PTSD, depression and suicidality in both large cohort and smaller veteran samples.

Posttraumatic stress symptoms

Nine studies (n ≈ 50–12,965) assessed PTSD or complex PTSD in relation to PMIE exposure. Betrayal-related PMIEs showed the most consistent associations with PTSD symptoms. In diverse samples, betrayal exposure was associated with approximately 1.3- to 2.8-fold higher odds of meeting PTSD criteria. Commission-based PMIEs (such as killing or perpetrating harm) were also associated with elevated PTSD risk in several studies, aligning with classic combat research showing that perpetrating violence may be linked to more severe post-traumatic distress than witnessing trauma alone. Omission-type PMIEs also emerged as significant predictors in some analyses, although these associations were generally less consistent across studies. Notably, Nichter et al. (2020) found that PMIEs involving others’ transgressions were strongly associated with PTSD symptoms, suggesting that certain contexts such as intense combat exposure or ‘battle aftermath’ may amplify the impact of witnessing moral violations.

An important insight from this review is that betrayal-related PMIEs may be particularly relevant to PTSD, including in contexts where perpetration-based moral injury has traditionally received greater attention. Although perpetration, especially killing, has long been linked to PTSD, this pattern aligns with moral injury theories that highlight betrayal by trusted authorities or peers as especially shattering of core beliefs. Shay (1994) argued that betrayal of ‘what is right’ by leaders can unleash severe and lasting combat-related trauma symptoms. Whilst service members and other professionals may be psychologically prepared to face occupational threat, they often do so on the basis of trust in those around them and in the systems within which they work. When these bonds of trust fracture through betrayal, such as a leader’s abdication of responsibility or a colleague’s immoral actions, the resulting disillusionment and loss of faith may precipitate or magnify PTSD symptoms like hypervigilance, intrusion and emotional numbness. Evidence beyond the reviewed studies also supports the relevance of betrayal to PTSD severity in both military and civilian populations (Levi-Belz et al., 2024; Wisco et al., 2016), supporting the broader relevance of betrayal-related experiences across contexts. Collectively, these findings suggest that betrayal-related experiences should be considered alongside self-perpetrated transgressions when understanding PTSD presentations linked to moral injury. Clinically, this highlights the importance of addressing shattered trust, anger toward betrayers, and feelings of abandonment, alongside guilt-focused work more commonly associated with commission-related PMIEs.

Depression

Across nine studies (n ≈ 50–12,965), betrayal-type PMIEs were frequently associated with depressive symptoms, with several studies reporting moderate associations (standardised β ≈ .24–.45) or elevated odds of clinically significant depression. Commission-based PMIEs were also associated with depression in some contexts, including healthcare samples, although these findings were not uniform across studies. Omission-related PMIEs were also associated with depressive symptoms across several studies, although these were generally less prominent than those observed for betrayal. As with PTSD, apparent differences in the magnitude of these associations should be interpreted cautiously because formal between-subtype comparisons were not available.

These findings align with theoretical models emphasising the role of attribution and self- blame in moral injury. Kidwell and Kerig’s (2023) developmental model of moral injury posits that betrayal events encourage global, internal, and stable attributions of failure (e.g. ‘the world is unjust and it’s my fate to be betrayed’), fuelling chronic shame and hopelessness that can manifest as depression. By contrast, self-perpetrated transgressions may evoke intense guilt that is more directly linked to PTSD symptoms or substance-based coping rather than purely depressive withdrawal (Litz & Kerig, 2019). Empirical work supports this distinction: guilt about one’s own wrongdoing tends to correlate with re-experiencing and arousal, whereas shame arising from betrayal or moral failure is more strongly linked to low mood and diminished self-worth (C. J. Bryan et al., 2013; Norman et al., 2018). Frankfurt and Frazier (2016) similarly describe how committing immoral acts can ‘contaminate’ one’s identity as a good person, generating shame and self-disgust that contribute to social withdrawal, loss of meaning, and depressive affect.

A latent class analysis by Zerach et al. (2021) identified a veteran subgroup characterised by betrayal-only PMIE exposure who showed particularly elevated depression and PTSD, offering additional support for the salience of betrayal-only events. The strong association between betrayal and depression may reflect the interpersonal and existential crisis betrayal can trigger. Being betrayed can undermine fundamental trust in others and in one’s own judgment, which may resonate with depressive schemas. Taken together, the reviewed literature suggests that betrayal-related PMIEs are particularly relevant to depression across contexts, whereas commission-related PMIEs, although important, may require more specific conditions such as clear and unmitigated personal responsibility to translate into substantial depressive symptoms.

Anxiety symptoms

Evidence for anxiety symptoms was mixed and appeared sensitive to population and context. Five studies used the GAD-7. In a small sample of frontline healthcare workers, betrayal showed no unique association with anxiety and self-transgressions were negatively related to anxiety, which Levi-Belz and Zerach (2022) interpreted as possible desensitisation or defensive denial. By contrast, Williamson et al. (2023) found in a large UK healthcare cohort that all PMIE subtypes predicted higher anxiety, with the largest estimates observed for commission and betrayal. In an active-duty soldier sample, only betrayal was significantly associated with anxiety (Wetzler et al., 2024). Additional studies of combat-wounded veterans (Bravo et al., 2020) and teachers (Kaplan et al., 2025) also found positive associations between PMIEs and anxiety, with betrayal showing the strongest association in the teacher sample, followed by omission-related PMIEs.

Differences in baseline stress, timing and measurement likely contribute to these discrepancies. During acute crises such as the early COVID-19 pandemic, uniformly high anxiety may obscure PMIE-related variance, and some anxiety phenomena may be captured within PTSD hyperarousal. When power is adequate, commission and betrayal appear capable of increasing persistent worry and tension. Theoretically, betrayal may heighten anxiety through loss of a secure base and moral hypervigilance, including mistrust and cognitive preoccupation (Haidt, 2013; Keltner & Gross, 1999). Overall, associations between PMIE subtypes and anxiety varied across studies. This variation may reflect contextual, occupational, cultural or measurement-related differences, but the current evidence base is too limited to determine which of these factors are most influential. Betrayal-related PMIEs showed notable associations in some studies, though not uniformly.

Suicidal ideation and behaviour

Six studies linked PMIEs with suicidal ideation and self-injurious thoughts or behaviours (n = 50–669). Across these, self-transgressive PMIEs were repeatedly associated with suicidality, although the small number of studies and variation in measurement limit stronger comparative conclusions. Wetzler et al. (2024) found that exposure to one’s own immoral actions substantially increased the incidence of suicidal ideation in soldiers, whilst betrayal and other-related PMIEs were not significant in adjusted models. Levi-Belz et al. (2023b) similarly reported that commission PMIEs were uniquely associated with suicidal ideation in veterans and that emotional disclosure attenuated this relationship, implicating shame from one’s own transgressions as a key driver. However, newer evidence also suggested associations for other-related transgressions, whereas betrayal findings were more mixed across studies.

These findings echo meta-analytic work showing that killing in combat has a small but significant association with suicidal ideation and attempts beyond general combat exposure (Maguen et al., 2011). The mechanism linking self-transgressive PMIEs to suicidality likely involves moral emotions of profound shame, guilt and hopelessness, prompting individuals to see themselves as irredeemably bad or unworthy of life (Bryan et al., 2014; Nichter et al., 2020). Betrayal-related PMIEs may also contribute to suicidality through anger and perceived entrapment, and several studies link betrayal to suicide attempts (Bryan et al., 2018; Wisco et al., 2016), but the evidence is less consistent than for commission events. Longitudinal work indicates that PMIE exposure of any type predicts subsequent suicide attempts after accounting for prior mental health and combat exposure (Levi-Belz et al., 2023b; Maguen et al., 2022). These findings support the importance of assessing morally injurious experiences, including self-transgressive events, within suicide risk formulations. However, the evidence remains preliminary and does not yet establish stable subtype-specific risk profiles.

Alcohol misuse

Three studies examined alcohol outcomes. In a large healthcare sample, commission-based PMIEs significantly predicted hazardous alcohol use whilst omission events showed a smaller but still significant effect. Notably, betrayal was not related to drinking (Williamson et al., 2023). In contrast, smaller studies in veterinarian and military veteran samples found no significant associations between PMIE subtypes and alcohol misuse, likely due to limited power and the use of a brief screening measure (Williams & Berenbaum, 2019; Williamson et al., 2022).

These findings are consistent with military research showing that killing in combat and involvement in atrocities are associated with increased post-deployment alcohol problems (Killgore et al., 2008; Maguen et al., 2011) and substance use (Currier et al., 2014; Yager, 1984). A common explanation is a self-medication pattern in which individuals use alcohol to dampen guilt, shame and moral pain. The present review suggests that commission-related events may be relevant to alcohol-based coping in some contexts, although this evidence currently rests on a very small number of studies. The absence of a robust betrayal–alcohol link suggests that guilt may be a stronger driver of substance-based coping than betrayal-related anger or sorrow, which may instead manifest as social withdrawal or outward aggression. Integrated interventions that address PTSD, depression and maladaptive coping, including drinking, are likely to be important in morally injured populations.

Conceptual contributions to understanding moral injury and PMIEs

This review contributes to the conceptualisation of moral injury by suggesting that PMIE exposure is not best understood as a unitary construct. Instead, different PMIE domains appeared to show partially distinct patterns of association with psychological distress supporting the view that moral injury may involve multiple pathways rather than a single syndrome-like process. Betrayal-related PMIEs were most consistently linked with PTSD, depression and anxiety, whereas commission-related PMIEs appeared particularly relevant to suicidality and, to a lesser extent, alcohol misuse. Omission-type PMIEs showed a less uniform but still meaningful pattern. However, conceptual progress remains limited by inconsistent subtype operationalisation across studies. These findings therefore support more differentiated models of moral injury, whilst also highlighting the need for greater conceptual and measurement clarity.

Strengths, limitations and future directions

This review has several strengths. A Population–Exposure–Outcome framework allowed systematic mapping of how commission, omission and betrayal relate to distinct psychological outcomes, addressing a gap in earlier work that treated PMIEs as a single construct. Fifteen studies across military, healthcare, civilian and protestor populations support the transdiagnostic relevance of moral injury. Extraction of both adjusted and zero-order estimates enabled examination of unique predictive effects, and a structured quality appraisal (Kmet et al., 2004) enhanced transparency. By integrating evidence across PTSD and C-PTSD, depression, anxiety, suicidality and alcohol misuse, the review clarifies domain specific risks and highlights overarching patterns, notably the consistent salience of betrayal.

However, several limitations warrant caution. First, all included studies were cross-sectional, which precludes causal inference and means that psychological distress may influence how morally injurious experiences are appraised, recalled or reported. The studies also did not consistently test whether associations differed significantly between PMIE subtypes, which limits confidence in claims about their relative strength. Second, the evidence base relied heavily on the Moral Injury Events Scale (MIES), raising construct validity concerns because its ‘troubled by’ phrasing may blur exposure with distress, meaning that apparent subtype differences could partly reflect measurement structure rather than underlying differences in PMIE impact. Third, although the review prioritised comparability, measures and analytic approaches still varied across studies, and the exclusion of longitudinal, network, latent class and other non-comparable designs may have omitted informative evidence about subtype patterning and pathways. Finally, the literature was geographically concentrated in Israel, the UK or the USA, with many studies originating from a relatively small number of overlapping research groups. This limits cultural generalisability and reduces the degree of independence typically assumed in evidence synthesis, although it likely also reflects the current state of an emerging evidence base.

Future research should use longitudinal designs, apply clearer and more conceptually grounded definitions of PMIE subtypes, and distinguish exposure measures from measures of distress, impairment and moral injury outcomes. The present synthesis tentatively suggests that betrayal-related PMIEs may be especially relevant where trust, dependency, authority and belonging are disrupted, whereas commission-related PMIEs may be especially relevant where guilt, responsibility and self-condemnation are central. These propositions should be tested directly rather than assumed. Work examining moderators such as culture, institutional context, leadership and social support may help explain why subtype-outcome associations vary across settings.

There is also a need to study PMIEs in more diverse groups, including humanitarian workers, police, first responders and communities exposed to moral harm outside combat and healthcare. Clinically, the review suggests that morally injurious experiences should not automatically be treated as interchangeable. Asking not only whether a morally distressing event has occurred, but also what type of PMIE was experienced, may be crucial for risk assessment and formulation. However, subtype-informed intervention approaches remain provisional and require stronger empirical support.
