Model Order Reduction for Parametric Hermitian Eigenvalue Problems: Local Acceleration with Taylor-Reduced Basis Method
Benjamin Stamm † and Zhuoyao Zeng †

Abstract
. This paper is concerned with the Taylor-reduced basis method (Taylor-RBM) for the efficient approximation of eigenspaces of large scale parametric Hermitian matrices. The Taylor-RBM is a local model order reduction method, which constructs an approximation space by capturing derivatives information of the spectral projector at a reference point in the parameter domain. We perform a concise error analysis to justify the Taylor-RBM for eigenvalue problems, and we present a computationally efficient procedure to assemble the Taylor-reduced basis space. Since this method is tightly connected to the classical multivariate analytic perturbation theory, we also provide a detailed analysis of the spectral approximation using the truncated power series of the eigenprojector, and compare this with the approximation obtained from the Taylor-RBM.



Keywords:

Parametric eigenvalue problem,
spectral approximation,
model order reduction,
reduced basis method,
Rayleigh-Ritz method,
perturbation theory.

1.
Introduction

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\gg 1
and
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open parameter domain.
Consider a family of
N\times N
Hermitian matrices
\{\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\}_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}}
,
i.e., for each
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}
, the matrix
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
satisfies
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})^{*}
.
Throughout this work,
we assume that the parameter dependency of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
on
\bm{\mu}
is
(real-)analytic
,
in the sense specified later in (
3.1
).

For practical relevance and for simplicity,
this work is presented in the finite dimensional matrix setting.
There are no fundamental obstacles
when formulating the results to analytic families of unbounded self-adjoint linear operators after clarifications of technical details
(type of operators, type of spectrum, free from spectral pollution, matrix representations, etc.).

Suppose at a reference parameter
\bm{\mu}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
,
we have computed the smallest eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\}_{i=1}^{K}
counting
without
multiplicities,
i.e.,
\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mu}_{0})<\lambda_{2}(\bm{\mu}_{0})<\cdots<\lambda_{K}(\bm{\mu}_{0})<\lambda_{K+1}(\bm{\mu}_{0})<\cdots
,
and their corresponding
total eigenprojector

\{\mathbf{P}_{\!i}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\}_{i=1}^{K}
,
each with multiplicity
m_{i}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\coloneqq\mathrm{rank}\big(\mathbf{P}_{\!i}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big)
,
i.e,

\ker\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})-\lambda_{i}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\mathbf{I}\big)\,=\,\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{P}_{\!i}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\qquad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,K.

Let us denote
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbf{P}_{\!i}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
and
M\coloneqq\mathrm{rank}\big(\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big)
.
Note that

M=m_{1}(\bm{\mu}_{0})+\cdots+m_{K}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}(\bm{\mu}_{0})m_{i}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathrm{tr}\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big).

We are interested in approximating the eigenvalue sum and the total eigenprojectors at
\bm{\mu}
in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
Equivalently by the variational characterization of the sum of the smallest eigenvalues
[
21
]
,
we are interested in approximating the orthogonal projector
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
and its
Ritz-value sum

\mathrm{tr}\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\big)
,
where

\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\quad\text{ solves }\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{2}=\mathbf{M}^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\\
\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{M}=M\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{M}\big)\quad\text{for }\bm{\mu}\text{ near }\bm{\mu}_{0}.

Since the mapping
\bm{\mu}\mapsto\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
is assumed to be analytic over
\mathcal{P}
,
it is natural to expect that the total eigenprojector
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
also depends analytically on
\bm{\mu}
.
Locally, this is indeed the case as established in the classical works
[
34
,
5
]
.
Exploiting this analyticity,
we shall use the
Taylor reduced basis method
(Taylor-RBM) to approximate the total eigenprojector
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
and the Ritz-value sum
\mathrm{tr}\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\big)
for
\bm{\mu}
in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
The main idea is to construct a reduced basis (RB) space
\cal{V}
with
r\coloneqq\dim\cal{V}\ll N
and
r\geq M
,
where
\cal{V}
is spanned by the ranges of the Taylor coefficients of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
at
\bm{\mu}_{0}
up to a certain order.
Then, we perform a Galerkin projection of the original problem onto
\cal{V}
,
i.e., for a matrix
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times r}
whose columns form an orthonormal basis (ONB) of
\cal{V}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\!\!\mathbf{V}}\!(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{V}^{*}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{V}
,
we consider the reduced problem

(1.1)

\mathbf{Q}^{\!\mathbf{V}}\!(\bm{\mu})\ \text{ solves}\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{2}=\mathbf{M}^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}\\
\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{M}=M\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}\big(\mathbf{A}^{\!\!\mathbf{V}}\!(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{M}\big),

and we view
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\approx\mathbf{Q}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Q}^{\!\mathbf{V}}\!(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{V}^{*}
for
\bm{\mu}_{0}
near
\bm{\mu}
.

The motivation of our research is twofold.
For one thing, Taylor-RBM is well-studied in RBM theory for parametric linear source problems in model order reduction (MOR).
For another, the Taylor-RBM in case of univariate parameter space emerges independently in physics.
Our goal is to provide a concise mathematical understanding of this method that bridges the two communities and to extend the method to the multivariate parameter case.
We believe this method has great potential for applications involving parametric eigenvalue problems.
Our focus is on quantum-mechanical applications in computational physics and chemistry,
although engineering applications are also within its scope.
This work provides a solid theoretical foundation for its future development.
Before stating our main contributions,
we summarize related existing works.

1.1.
Literature review and state-of-the-art

MOR is a vast research area with numerous applications in science and engineering
[
8
,
3
,
50
]
.
The general idea of MOR for parametric systems is that given a large parametric full-order model (FOM) that is computationally expensive to solve,
one construct in the
offline phase
a reliable and efficient reduced order model (ROM) that approximates the FOM well in a certain sense,
and then use the ROM in the
online phase
for rapid repeated evaluations
[
10
]
.
Among the various MOR techniques, we focus on
projection-based MOR
,
which can be divided into two categories:

System-theoretic
MOR addresses more on control problems, see
[
7
]
for a detailed survey;

reduced basis method
(RBM) focuses on reducing the dimensionality of the state space through Galerkin-projection
[
9
]
.

RBM is a class of projection-based MOR techniques,
proposed originally for solving parametric source problems,
i.e., parametric partial differential equations (PDEs)
[
42
,
45
,
49
]
.
The key idea is to use information of the computed true solutions at selected parameter values,
also called
snapshot solutions
,
to construct a low-dimensional RB space, such that the Galerkin projection onto this space provides a good approximation of the solution set.
Following
[
28
]
,
there are two main ideas of constructing the RB space:

•

Lagrange-RBM
constructs the RB space by collecting snapshot solutions at selected parameters.
There are two common strategies:
The
proper orthogonal decomposition
(POD) refers to computing snapshot solutions of many preselected parameters and then performing a singular value decomposition (SVD) of the resulting large snapshot matrix.
Alternatively, one can use a
greedy method
to iteratively select the most “representative” snapshots from a training set of parameters based on a-posteriori error estimators.
In both cases, solid mathematical framework and convergence analysis have been established for linear, coercive continuous and affinely decomposable problems
[
47
,
30
,
16
]
.

•

Taylor-RBM
constructs the RB space by collecting the Taylor coefficients of the parametric solution at a given parameter up to a certain order,
provided that the parametric solution is differentiable w.r.t.​ the parameter.
Convergence analysis of Taylor-RBM for parametric source problems has been conducted in
[
22
]
.

Parametric eigenvalue problems (pEVPs) arises naturally in numerical linear algebra
[
36
,
53
,
33
,
40
,
46
]
,
in infinite dimensional setting (especially within finite element approximations of parametric PDE EVP)
[
37
,
2
,
1
,
24
,
31
]
,
in uncertainty quantification
[
17
,
52
,
19
,
6
]
,
as well as in quantum physics
[
15
,
29
,
23
,
41
,
20
]
.
Compared to parametric source problems,
pEVPs demonstrate a different nature.
The main challenges include
(i) the lack of Galerkin orthogonality,
(ii) the absence of a reliable residual based error estimator,
(iii) the complicated regularity behavior of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors w.r.t.​ the parameter, especially when eigenvalue crossings occur.

The first two challenges are intrinsic problem of subspace methods for eigenvalue problems
[
13
,
26
,
4
,
57
,
44
,
35
]
.
These challenges obstruct direct extensions of the existing RBM theory for parametric source problems to pEVPs.
Nonetheless, certified greedy approximation procedures for the smallest eigenvalue and its corresponding eigenspace have been proposed in
[
53
,
38
]
.

In case
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, challenge (iii) is well-addressed by the classical analytical perturbation theory (PT) of linear operators
[
48
,
34
]
,
which states that the
individual unordered
eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors are analytic functions of the parameter.
Exploiting this analyticity,
the quantum physics community has proposed the
eigenvector continuation
(EC) method,
which constructs an RB space by collecting derivatives of the eigenvectors
[
23
,
20
]
.
An involved mathematical convergence analysis of this univariate case has been recently provided in
[
25
]
.

The regularity of eigenvalues and eigenvectors in the multivariate parameter case is more complicated,
essentially because
individual
eigenvalues are generally only continuous and the
individual
eigenvectors may even be discontinuous.
This phenomenon is well-illustrated by the classical example of the
2\times 2
matrix family

(1.2)

\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}\\
\mu_{2}&-\mu_{1}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{for}\quad(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{\!\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

from
[
34
, Exp. 5.12 in Chp. 2]
,
which has eigenvalues
\pm{(\mu_{1}^{2}+\mu_{2}^{2})^{1/2}}
that are continuous but not differentiable at the origin,
and the corresponding eigenvectors
cannot
be defined continuously at the origin.
The classical works
[
34
,
5
]
and recent works
[
2
,
27
]
suggest to consider the total eigenprojector associated to a cluster of eigenvalues instead of individual eigenvalues and eigenvectors,
which remains analytic,
provided that the cluster is separated from the rest of the spectrum by a non-vanishing gap.
To the best of our knowledge, there has not been any existing work on Taylor-RBM for pEVPs in the multivariate parameter case,
let alone on its efficient assembly.

\mathbb{N}
denotes natural numbers without
0
, and
\mathbb{N}_{0}\coloneqq\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
.
Bold letters, such as
\bm{\mu}
,
\mathbf{y}
or
\mathbf{M}
, indicate quantities that are in general vectors or matrices.

\mathbf{I}_{n}
denotes the identity matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
and for convenience, we denote
\mathbf{I}\coloneqq\mathbf{I}_{N}
.
The
j
-th canonical unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which has
1
in the
j
-th position and
0
elsewhere, is denoted by
\mathbf{e}_{j}
.

For vectors
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote its Euclidean norm by
|\bm{\mu}|\coloneqq\big({\mu_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\mu_{d}^{2}}\big)^{1/2}
.
For multi-indices
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,

.
This slight abuse of notation shall not cause confusion from the context.

The spectral norm (or induced Euclidean norm) of a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is denoted by
\|\mathbf{M}\|
,
and the spectrum of
\mathbf{M}
is denoted by
\sigma(\mathbf{M})
.
Given an orthogonal projector
\bm{\Pi}={\bm{\Pi}}^{2}={\bm{\Pi}}^{*}
,
we denote by

We denote by
\mathbf{M}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\bm{\Pi}}:\mathrm{ran}{\bm{\Pi}}\to\mathrm{ran}{\bm{\Pi}},\mathbf{y}\mapsto{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{y}
the restriction of
{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{M}
to
\mathrm{ran}{\bm{\Pi}}
,
which is also known as the

of
\mathbf{M}
to
\mathrm{ran}{\bm{\Pi}}
.

To avoid clumsy brackets in formulae,
we will mostly neglect brackets when taking traces, ranks, kernels and ranges of matrix sums or products from now on,
e.g., we mostly write
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{M}_{1}\mathbf{M}_{2}
instead of

, or
\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{M}_{1}-\mathbf{M}_{2}
instead of
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{M}_{1}-\mathbf{M}_{2})
.

2.
Selected aspects of the subspace method for eigenspace approximation

Throughout this section, we consider a (non-parametric) Hermitian matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
.
Let
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be an orthogonal projector of rank
M
,
which commutes with
\mathbf{A}
.
In other words,
\mathbf{P}
is a sum of spectral projectors asscociated to
\mathbf{A}
.
Note that
\mathbf{P}
being a sum of spectral projectors implies that no eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{P}}
and of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{P}^{\perp}}
coincide.

Given a subspace
\cal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{N}
with associated orthogonal projector
{\bm{\Pi}}
and
r\coloneqq\dim\cal{V}=\mathrm{rank}{\bm{\Pi}}\ll N
as well as
r\geq M
,
the
subspace method
(or
Rayleigh–Ritz method
) for spectral approximations
refers to approximating the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of
\mathbf{A}
by those of its compression
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\bm{\Pi}}
.
In this context,
the eigenspaces of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\bm{\Pi}}
are viewed as subspaces of
\cal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
and so its eigenprojectors can be naturally compared with
\mathbf{P}
.
Let
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be an orthogonal projector of rank
M
,
such that
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{Q}\subseteq\cal{V}
and
\mathbf{Q}
commutes with
{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}
,
i.e.,
\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{Q}
is an invariant subspace of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\bm{\Pi}}
.
We are interested in how well
\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{Q}
approximates
\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{P}
,
or equivalently, how well
\mathbf{Q}
approximates
\mathbf{P}
.

The following example illustrates some issues of the subspace method in approximating eigenspaces.

Example 2.1

(Pathological case of subspace method for eigenspace approximation)
.

Consider
\mathbf{A}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(1,2,2,3)
and
\cal{V}\coloneqq\mathrm{ran}{\bm{\Pi}}\coloneqq\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{4},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3}\}
,
where
\mathbf{e}_{j}
denotes the
j
-th standard basis vector.
Then, we have
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\bm{\Pi}}=\mathrm{diag}(2,2,2)
.
Let us discuss two cases of
\mathbf{P}
:

(i)

If
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{*}
, i.e.
M=1
,
the choice of
\mathbf{Q}
is not unique,
as any 1-dimensional subspace of
\cal{V}
is an invariant subspace of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\bm{\Pi}}
,
and all approximations are bad,
since
\|{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|=2^{-1/2}
.

(ii)

If
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{e}_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{*}+\mathbf{e}_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}^{*}
, i.e.
M=2
,
the choice of
\mathbf{Q}
is also not unique,
as any 2-dimensional subspace of
\cal{V}
is an invariant subspace of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\bm{\Pi}}
.
We also see that any choice of
\mathbf{Q}
satisfies
\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{AQ}=\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{AP}=4
, because the the basis vector
\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{4}
of
\cal{V}
has Ritz-value
2
and is orthogonal to other basis vectors.
In other words, the subspace
\cal{V}
introduces additional "spurious" eigenvalues which coincide with the target eigenvalues.
However, we observe
\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{P}\subseteq\cal{V}
, so the best choice of
\mathbf{Q}
is certainly
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{P}
.

For our problem of interest, we shall comment on the case where
\mathbf{P}
is characterized variationally.

Remark 2.2

(Variational approximation)
.

From a practical point of view as discussed at the beginning of introduction,
we are often more interested in the invariant subspace associated to the smallest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
,
i.e.,
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
satisfy

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\,=\,\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{2}=\mathbf{M}^{*}\\
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{M}=M\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{M}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{Q}\,\in\,\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{2}=\mathbf{M}^{*}\\
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{M}=M\\
\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{M}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}{\bm{\Pi}}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{M},

respectively.
Note that the left hand side of (
2.1
) is uniquely solvable, since
\mathbf{P}
is the sum of spectral projectors,
while
\mathbf{Q}
may not be, see
Example

2.1

(i)
.
Also note that the right hand side of (
2.1
) is an abstract formulation of (
1.1
).

The variational characterisation of
\mathbf{P}
is called the
Ky-Fan’s minimum principle

[
21
]
,
which is the clustered version of the classical min–max principle for (individual) extremal eigenvalues
[
12
, Thm. 1 in Sec. 10.2]
.
We also refer to
[
55
, Prp. 1.3.4]
for a more modern formulation.

Besides practical relevance,
this variational characterization has further benefits.
For one thing,
it is more natural to think of and to specify
\mathbf{Q}
as an approximation of
\mathbf{P}
under this setting.
For another, we automatically have
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{P}
,
provided that
\mathbf{P}
is variationally characterized and
\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{P}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}{\bm{\Pi}}
.
This simple observation has further implications for the case when
\mathbf{P}
is slightly perturbed,
and it will be leveraged later in
Section

4
.

We also remark that the analysis of the subspace approximation method
is not restricted to this variational setting.
Hence, we do not impose (
2.1
) in this section.

It is natural to measure the approximation quality of
\mathbf{Q}
to
\mathbf{P}
by
\|\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P}\|
.
The following result provides a flexibility in measuring this approximation error under a rank assumption.

Lemma 2.3

(Equivalence of subspace approximation error)
.

Let
\mathbf{P}
,
\mathbf{Q}
be two orthogonal projectors of the same rank.
Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P}\|\;=\;\|\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|\;=\;\|\mathbf{P}^{\perp}\mathbf{Q}\|.

Note that in general
\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P}\neq\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\neq\mathbf{P}^{\perp}\mathbf{Q}
even though their norms are equal.

An operator theoretic proof can be found in
[
34
, Thm. 6.34 in Chp. 1]
;
a more geometric treatment linking to the principal angles between subspaces can be found in
[
56
, Sec. 2]
.

Next, we relate the approximation error
\|\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|

and the projection error
\|{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|
.
The following theorem has a similar form to the best approximation property of the Petrov-Galerkin solution in finite element methods
[
14
, Lem. 3.7]
,
and is adapted from
[
43
, Thm. 6]
.
Our formulation emphasizes the deviation of the entire subspace being an invariant subspace,
and avoids individual eigenvectors.
This is more suitable for the parametric setting, as will be discussed later in
Remark

3.6
.

Theorem 2.4

(Bounding eigenspace approximation error by eigenspace projection error)
.

Let us continue with the notation of this section.
Suppose that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{P}}
and
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}}
are separated by a gap

\displaystyle\gamma\coloneqq\min\Big\{|\tilde{\lambda}-\hat{\lambda}|:\tilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{P}}),\hat{\lambda}\in\sigma(\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}})\Big\}>0.

Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|^{2}\;\leq\;\ignorespaces\Big(1+\frac{\mathrm{Res}^{2}({\mathbf{A},\bm{\Pi}})}{\gamma^{2}}\Big)\|{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|^{2},

where
\mathrm{Res}^{2}(\mathbf{A},\bm{\Pi})\coloneqq\|{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\|^{2}
is the squared residual of the entire subspace
\cal{V}=\mathrm{ran}{\bm{\Pi}}
w.r.t.​
\mathbf{A}
.

This theorem states that the error of the eigenspace approximation by the subspace method,
in case of a meaningful approximation in the sense of
\gamma>0
,
can be controlled by how well the target eigenspace is approximated by the subspace
\cal{V}
,
up to a factor depending on the spectral gap and on how well
\cal{V}
is close to an invariant subspace of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

Firstly, we note that
\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}
is also an orthogonal projector satisfying
{\bm{\Pi}}=\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}
by
[
12
, Thm. 3 in Sec. 2.8]
.
Since
\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{Q}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}{\bm{\Pi}}
, we have
\mathrm{ran}{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}
, and so
\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}={\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}
.
Using these facts and
\mathbf{I}={\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}+{\bm{\Pi}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}({\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}+{\bm{\Pi}})\mathbf{P}={\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}.

Since
{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}
have orthogonal ranges,
we deduce

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|^{2}=\|{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|^{2}+\|\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}\|^{2}.

To bound
\|\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}\|
,
we rewrite it as the solution of a Sylvester-equation.
Multiplying
\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}
on the left to both sides of
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}
and using again
\mathbf{I}={\bm{\Pi}}+{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}
, we get

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\,=\,\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}\,=\,\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}({\bm{\Pi}}+{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp})\mathbf{P}\,=\,\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}.

Since
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}^{2}
commutes with
\mathbf{A}
,
the left most term in (
2.3
) can be rewritten as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\,=\,\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}^{2}\mathbf{A}\,=\,\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}\,=\,\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{P}}.

By construction,
\mathbf{Q}
commutes with
{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}
.
Since
{\bm{\Pi}}=\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}
,
we deduce that
\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}=(\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}})^{2}
also commutes with
{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}
.
Using this fact and
\mathbf{Q}^{\perp},{\bm{\Pi}}
being projectors,
we rewrite the first term on the right most side of (
2.3
) as

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}\,=\,(\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}})^{2}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}^{2}\mathbf{P}\,=\,\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}\,=\,\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}}\,\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}.

Replacing (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) into the left and right most sides of (
2.3
) and rearranging the terms,
we arrive at the Sylvester-equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}}\,\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{P}}\,=\,-\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}.

Note that viewing
\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}
as a map from
\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{P}
to
\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}
doesn’t change its norm.
By the assumption on the spectral gap
\gamma>0
,
we deduce with
[
11
, Sec. 10]
that

\displaystyle\ignorespaces\|\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{P}\|\;\leq\;\frac{\|\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|}{\gamma}\;\leq\;\frac{\|{\bm{\Pi}}\mathbf{A}{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\|\,\|{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|}{\gamma},

where the second step follows from the sub-multiplicativity of the spectral norm combined with the facts that
\|\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}\|=1
and
{\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp}=({\bm{\Pi}}^{\perp})^{2}
.
Inserting this bound into (
2.2
) completes the proof.
∎

The last issue in this section concerns the relation between the approximation error of the eigenspaces
and the difference of the corresponding Ritz-value sums.
It is commonly known that the Ritz-values approximate the eigenvalues with a
squared
order compared to the approximation error of the eigenspaces.
However, the formulation of this squaring effect in the literature usually relies on
\|\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}\|
and involves the spread of
\mathbf{A}
or some auxiliary operators,
see e.g.
[
54
, Thm. 3.2]
or
[
25
, Lem. 8.2]
.
Here, we present a concise trace identity, which reveals the squaring effect directly.

Lemma 2.5

(Squaring effect of Ritz-value difference)
.

Let us continue with the setting of this section.
For
{{\bm{\Delta}}}\coloneqq\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P}
,
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}{{\bm{\Delta}}}\;=\;\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}^{\perp}-\mathbf{P}){{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\!2}\;=\;\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{P}){{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\!2}.

Note that
\mathbf{P}^{\perp}-\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{P}
is the generalized Householder reflector,
which reflects across
\mathrm{ran}\mathbf{P}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}^{\perp}
, and
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{P}^{\perp}
are orthogonal projectors commuting with
\mathbf{A}
,
we know
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{\perp}=\mathbf{P}^{\perp}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{0}
.
Using these facts and the linearity as well as cyclicity of the trace, we obtain

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}{{\bm{\Delta}}}\,=\,\mathrm{tr}\,(\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}^{\perp})\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}^{\!\perp}){{\bm{\Delta}}}\,=\,\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}{{\bm{\Delta}}}\,\mathbf{P}+\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{\perp}{{\bm{\Delta}}}\,\mathbf{P}^{\perp}.

By the definition of
{{\bm{\Delta}}}
, we have
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{P}+{{\bm{\Delta}}}
.
Since
\mathbf{Q}^{2}=\mathbf{Q}
, we deduce
(\mathbf{P}+{{\bm{\Delta}}})^{2}=\mathbf{P}+{{\bm{\Delta}}}
.
Expanding
(\mathbf{P}+{{\bm{\Delta}}})^{2}
,
using the idempotent property of
\mathbf{P}
, multiplying both sides by
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{P}^{\perp}
from the left and right, respectively,
we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}{{\bm{\Delta}}}\,\mathbf{P}=\;-\mathbf{P}{{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\!2}\,\mathbf{P}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{P}^{\perp}{{\bm{\Delta}}}\,\mathbf{P}^{\perp}=\;\mathbf{P}^{\perp}{{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\!2}\,\mathbf{P}^{\perp}.

Applying these two relations to (
2.6
),
we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}{{\bm{\Delta}}}\,=\,-\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}{{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\!2}\,\mathbf{P}+\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{\perp}{{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\!2}\,\mathbf{P}^{\perp}\,=\,-\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}{{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\!2}+\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{P}^{\perp}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{\perp}{{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\!2}\,=\,\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}^{\perp}-\mathbf{P}){{\bm{\Delta}}}^{\!2},

where the second step follows from the cyclicity of the trace
and the third step relies on the fact that
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{P}^{\perp}
are orthogonal projectors commuting with
\mathbf{A}
.
∎

3.
The total projector as an analytic function and its finite truncation

In this section, we study the total eigenprojector,
i.e., the eigenprojector onto the invariant subspace associated with isolated eigenvalues of an analytic parameter-dependent Hermitian matrix,
following
[
34
, Chp. 2]
.
The goal of the first three subsections is to present the analyticity results for the total eigenprojector in the multivariate setting providing explicit formulae.
Afterwards, we analyze the truncated power series of the total eigenprojector,
which serves as a motivation for and a good comparison with the Taylor-RBM.

We begin with clarifying the analyticity of the matrix-valued function
\bm{\mu}\mapsto\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
.
Recall that the
length
of a
multi-index

\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}

is given by
|\bm{\beta}|\coloneqq\beta_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{d}
,
and we denote

\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\beta}}\coloneqq\mu_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\mu_{d}^{\beta_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{for }\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then, the mapping
\bm{\mu}\mapsto\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
is called
analytic
over
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
iff for each
\bm{\mu}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
, there exists
\delta>0
,
such that
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
can be represented by its convergent power series expansion

(3.1)

\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})=\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}\quad\text{for all }\bm{\mu}\text{ with }|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|<\delta,

where the coefficient matrices
\{\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}\}_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}
are given by the derivatives of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
at
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
i.e.,

\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\beta_{1}!\cdots\beta_{d}!}\frac{\partial^{|\bm{\beta}|}\mathbf{A}}{\partial\mu_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\partial\mu_{d}^{\beta_{d}}}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\quad\text{for all }\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}.

3.1.
Useful properties of the unperturbed case

Consider an arbitrary
\bm{\mu}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
and denote for brevity
\mathbf{A}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
.
Let us recall from
[
34
, Chp. 1 §5]
some useful results regarding
\mathbf{A}_{0}
and its resolvent
\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\coloneqq(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\zeta\,\mathbf{I})^{-1}

which is defined for
\zeta\notin\sigma(\mathbf{A}_{0})
.

Let
\lambda_{0}\coloneqq\lambda(\mathbf{A}_{0})
be an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
with multiplicity
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and corresponding orthogonal eigenprojector
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\coloneqq\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
,
i.e.,
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{2}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{*}
,
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{P}_{\!0})=m_{0}
and
\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}=\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{A}_{0}=m_{0}\lambda_{0}
.

Expanding the meromorphic function
\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)
in a Laurent-series around its isolated singularity
\lambda_{0}
,
we get

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=-1}^{\infty}(\zeta-\lambda_{0})^{t}\,\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(t+1)},

where the coefficient matrices
\{\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(t)}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
are obtained via the contour integral

(3.3)

\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}(\zeta-\lambda_{0})^{-t-1}\,\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta

with
\Gamma
being a closed contour in the complex plane encircling only the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
.
Note that the Laurent-series of
\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)
terminates at
t=-1
,
since
\mathbf{A}_{0}
is Hermitian
[
34
, Chp. 1 §5.4]
.
Besides, it is shown in
[
34
, Chp. 1 §5.3]
that the coefficient matrices are
given by
\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(0)}\coloneqq-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
,
and
\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(t)}\coloneqq\mathbf{S}_{0}^{t}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
with
\mathbf{S}_{0}
being the
reduced resolvent
of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
at
\lambda_{0}
,
which is characterized by

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{0}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\,\mathbf{S}_{0}=\mathbf{0},

(3.5)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\,\mathbf{I})\,\mathbf{S}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}_{0}\,(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\,\mathbf{I})=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}.

For detailed derivations and proofs of the above facts,
we refer the reader to
[
34
, Chp. 1 §5]
.

3.2.
Analyticity of the parametric total eigenprojector

We continue with the setting of the last subsection.
By the analyticity assumption of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
in the sense of (
3.1
),
we can write for
\bm{\mu}
in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
that

(3.6)

\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})+\sum_{|\bm{\beta}|\geq 1}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}\eqqcolon\mathbf{A}_{0}+\mathbf{N}_{0}(\bm{\mu}).

For
\zeta\notin\sigma\big(\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\big)
,
the resolvent of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
is denoted by
\mathbf{R}(\zeta;\bm{\mu})\coloneqq(\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})-\zeta\,\mathbf{I})^{-1}
.
Note from
Section

3.1
that
\mathbf{R}(\zeta;\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)=(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\zeta\,\mathbf{I})^{-1}
.
Using the elementary identity

\mathbf{R}(\zeta;\bm{\mu})=(\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})-\zeta\,\mathbf{I})^{-1}=\Big(\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{N}_{0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\big)(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\zeta\,\mathbf{I})\Big)^{-1}\!=\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\Big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{N}_{0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\Big)^{-1}

and the Neumann-series expansion
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{M})^{-1}\!=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{M}^{p}
for any matrix
\mathbf{M}
with
\|\mathbf{M}\|<1
,
we obtain for
\bm{\mu},\zeta
satisfying
\|\mathbf{N}_{0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\|<1
that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}(\zeta;\bm{\mu})&=\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\big(-\mathbf{N}_{0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\big)^{p}=\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{p}\,\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\Big(\sum_{|\bm{\beta}|\geq 1}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\Big)^{p}.\end{aligned}

Note that the condition
\|\mathbf{N}_{0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\|<1
can be ensured by choosing
\bm{\mu}
sufficiently close to
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
In this case,
the power series expansion in (
3.7
) converges absolutely in operator norm.
Hence, we can exchange the order of summation and regroup the terms according to the powers of
(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})
,
which yields the power series expansion

(3.8)

\mathbf{R}(\zeta;\bm{\mu})=\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\,\mathbf{R}_{0}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}(\zeta)\quad\text{for }\bm{\mu}\text{ in a small neighborhood of }\bm{\mu}_{0},

where the coefficient matrices
\{\mathbf{R}_{0}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}(\zeta)\}_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}
are given by
\mathbf{R}_{0}^{(\mathbf{0})}(\zeta)\coloneqq\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)
and

(3.9)

\mathbf{R}_{0}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}(\zeta)\;\coloneqq\;\sum_{p=1}^{|\bm{\beta}|}(-1)^{p}\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{p}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{\nu}_{p}=\bm{\beta}\end{subarray}}\!\!\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\cdots\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{p})}\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)\quad\text{for }|\bm{\beta}|\geq 1.

In this way, it is shown for
\zeta\notin\sigma(\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}))
that the resolvent map
\mathbf{R}(\zeta;\bm{\mu})
is analytic with respect to
\bm{\mu}
in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.

Next, we consider the total projector
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
around
\bm{\mu}_{0}
, which satisfies
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
.
This is obtained via the contour integral of the resolvent
\mathbf{R}(\zeta;\bm{\mu})
around the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
,
e.g., along the contour
\Gamma
used in (
3.3
).
Using the power series expansion of
\mathbf{R}(\zeta;\bm{\mu})
in (
3.8
) and exchanging the order of summation and integration due to absolute convergence,
we obtain for
\bm{\mu}
in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
that

(3.10)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\frac{-1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}\mathbf{R}(\zeta;\bm{\mu})\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,=\,\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\,\frac{-1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{R}_{0}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\eqqcolon\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}.\end{aligned}

The coefficient matrices
\{\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\}_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}
are calculated by putting (
3.2
) into (
3.9
) and observing that only terms with the power
(\zeta-\lambda_{0})^{-1}
contribute to the contour integral.
Consequently,
we obtain
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}=-\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(\mathbf{0})}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
as well as

(3.11)

\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\;\coloneqq\;-\sum_{p=1}^{|\bm{\beta}|}(-1)^{p}\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{p}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{\nu}_{p}=\bm{\beta}\\
k_{1},\ldots,k_{p+1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\\
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{p+1}=p\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{1})}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{2})}\cdots\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{p})}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{p})}\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{p+1})}\quad\text{for }|\bm{\beta}|\geq 1.

With that, the power series expansion of the total eigenprojector
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
for
\bm{\mu}
close to
\bm{\mu}_{0}
is explicitly determined.

Remark 3.1

(Connection to classical perturbation results)
.

In the univariate case
d=1
, the formulae from (
3.11
) reduce to the classical one-dimensional perturbation results,
cf.
[
34
, Chp. 2 §2.2]
.
The key of the generalization from univariate to multivariate cases lies in the natural extension of the univariate polynomial convolution formula "
(AB)_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}A_{n}B_{n-k}
" to the multivariate one "
(AB)_{\bm{\beta}}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}}A_{\bm{\nu}_{1}}B_{\bm{\nu}_{2}}
".

Since
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
and
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
are both analytic in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
the Ritz-value sum
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
is also analytic in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.

3.3.
Properties of the total projector series and its coefficients

Since
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
is a projector,
it satisfies the idempotent property
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})^{2}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
.
Inserting the power series expansion (
3.10
) into this relation and comparing the coefficients of the same powers of
(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})
,
we obtain

(3.12)

\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\ =\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\end{subarray}}\!\!\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}.

This provides a recursive relation among the coefficient matrices
\{\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\}_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}
,
and the total number of terms on the right-hand side is given by
\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\beta_{j}+1)
.
Moreover, by applying (
3.12
) repeatedly,
we can express each
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
as a sum of
\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\beta_{j}+1)
terms,
where each summand contains at least one factor of
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}
.
For instance, we can use

\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}

to derive

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(2\mathbf{e}_{j})}=\,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(2\mathbf{e}_{j})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(2\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(2\mathbf{e}_{j})}+\big(\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\big)\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(2\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\big(\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(2\mathbf{e}_{j})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}\big)+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(2\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}

and similarly

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{i})}=\,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{i})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{i})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{i})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{i})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\big(\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{i})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{i})}\big)+\big(\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{i})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{i})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}\big)\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{i})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{j})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{e}_{i})}.

Formally, this assertion on the number of terms can be proven by induction on the length
|\bm{\beta}|
,
similar as presented in the proof of
[
34
, Thm. 2.12 in Chp. 2]
.
We leave the details here for brevity.

The more important observation is that
\mathrm{rank}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\mathbf{0})}=\mathrm{rank}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}=m_{0}
.
Combining this fact with the repeated applications of (
3.12
),
we obtain the following upper bound for the rank of
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
.

Lemma 3.2

(Upper bound on the rank of the coefficient matrices)
.

For each
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
it holds for
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
from (
3.11
) that
\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\leq m_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\beta_{j}+1)
.

We now look at the involved explicit expression of the coefficient matrices
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
given in (
3.11
).

Lemma 3.3

(Hermitian property of the coefficient matrices)
.

For each
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
the matrix
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
from (
3.11
) satisfies
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}=\big(\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\big)^{*}
.

Proof.

Observe that both
\mathbf{S}_{0}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
are Hermitian as they are contour integrals of the Hermitian resolvent
\mathbf{R}_{0}(\zeta)
.
Besides, each
\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}
is also Hermitian,
since taking (real) partial derivatives preserves the Hermitian property.
Hence, for each summand
\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{1})}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{2})}\cdots\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{p})}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{p})}\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{p+1})}
in (
3.11
),
its adjoint is given by
\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{p+1})}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{p})}\cdots\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{2})}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(k_{1})}
and this also appears in the sum.
∎

If one is interested in constructing the coefficient matrices
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
,
the explicit formula (
3.11
) would be computationally too expensive to apply.
Instead, we should use the following recursive relation,
which is generalized from the univariate density matrix perturbation theory
[
39
]
.

Proposition 3.4

(Multivariate recursive formula for the projector coefficients)
.

The coefficients of the projector series (
3.10
) satisfy the recursive relation

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,=\,-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{Z}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{Z}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}-\mathbf{S}_{0}\mathbf{W}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}-(\mathbf{S}_{0}\mathbf{W}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0})^{\ast},

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,\coloneqq\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2}\neq\mathbf{0}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\end{subarray}}\!\!\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{W}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,\coloneqq\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}\neq\mathbf{0},\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\end{subarray}}\!\!\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{1})}

depend only on the projector coefficients
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu})}
with
|\bm{\nu}|<|\bm{\beta}|
.

The key aspect of the generalisation from the univariate formulae to the multivariate ones
lies again in the replacement of the univariate polynomial convolution formula by a multivariate one, as stated in
Remark

3.1
.
Although the univariate case is considered somewhat standard in mathematical physics,
the multivariate version has not been explicitly stated in the literature to the best of our knowledge.
Thus, we state it above and provide a complete proof in the following.

Proof.

The overall idea is to decompose the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
into four parts according to the orthogonal decomposition
\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}
,
i.e.,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,=\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp},

and derive recursive expressions for each of these four parts separately.

For the first two parts,
we recall from (
3.12
) that we can insert the series development into the idempotent property
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})^{2}=\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
to deduce that

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,=\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\end{subarray}}\!\!\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}\,=\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}+\mathbf{Z}^{(\bm{\beta})}.

Projecting (
3.15
) onto
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
and onto
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}
from both sides, respectively,
and rearranging the resulting expressions,
we obtain the expression for the first two parts in (
3.14
) as

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\,=\,-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{Z}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\,=\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{Z}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}.

To derive expressions for the last two parts in (
3.14
),
let us, for the brevity of notations,
introduce the commutation relation
[\mathbf{M}_{1},\mathbf{M}_{2}]\coloneqq\mathbf{M}_{1}\mathbf{M}_{2}-\mathbf{M}_{2}\mathbf{M}_{1}
for any matrices
\mathbf{M}_{1},\mathbf{M}_{2}
.
With this, we rewrite the commutation relation between the system matrix and the eigenprojector as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad[\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}),\,\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})]\,=\,\mathbf{0}.

Plugging in the series developments and comparing the coefficients of order
\bm{\beta}
, we obtain

(3.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\end{subarray}}\!\![\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{1})},\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}]\,=\,\mathbf{0}\quad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad-\mathbf{W}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,=\,[\mathbf{A}_{0},\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}]\,=\,\mathbf{A}_{0}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,\mathbf{A}_{0}.

Projecting
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}
on the left and
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
on the right of (
3.17
),
we obtain for the third part in (
3.14
) that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\,=\,-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{W}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}.

Simplifying this equality with the relations
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{A}_{\!0}=\lambda_{0}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
as well as
\mathbf{A}_{\!0}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{A}_{\!0}
,
and rearranging the resulting expression, we arrive at

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\mathbf{I})\,\big(\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\big)\,=\,-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{W}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}.

Using the property (
3.5
) of
\mathbf{S}_{0}
, we thus obtain the expression for the fourth part in (
3.14
).
We observe that the fourth part is the adjoint of the third by
Lemma

3.3
.

Collecting all four parts from (
3.16
) and the above derivations,
we arrive at the desired relation (
3.13
).
This finishes the proof of
Proposition

3.4
.
∎

Let us summarize the previous content in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5

(Analyticity of the total eigenprojector of one isolated eigenvalue)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{A}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be a Hermitian matrix-valued function that is real-analytic in an open neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
in the sense of (
3.1
).
Assume that
\lambda_{0}
is an isolated eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
with multiplicity
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
and let
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
be the total eigenprojector onto the eigenspace associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
.

Then, for
\bm{\mu}
in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
there exists an analytic orthogonal projector-valued function
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
commuting with
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
such that
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
.
The coefficient matrices of its power series expansion w.r.t.
\bm{\mu}_{0}
is given by (
3.11
).
For each
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
is Hermitian,
has rank at most
m_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\beta_{j}+1)
and satisfies the relations given in
Proposition

3.4
.
The Ritz-value sum
\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
is also analytic in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.

So far we have focused on the total eigenprojector and the Ritz-value sum.
The following remark concerns the behavior of individual eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Remark 3.6

(Individual eigenvalues and eigenvectors)
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

3.5
,
we know that
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
has rank
m_{0}
for
\bm{\mu}
sufficiently close to
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
Modifying the arguments from
Section

3.2
as in
[
34
, Sec. 5.1 & 5.7]
,
we can show that the
m_{0}
individual eigenvalues are
continuous
functions in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
and they all converge to
\lambda_{0}
as
\bm{\mu}\to\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
However, we cannot guarantee that individual eigenvectors are continuous functions in
\bm{\mu}
in general.
A good demonstration of this phenomenon has been given in (
1.2
).
Hence, it is more natural in the multivariate parametric setting to consider the total eigenprojector and the Ritz-value sum.

We conclude the section with a remark on the case of multiple isolated eigenvalues.

Remark 3.7

(Analyticity of the total eigenprojector of multiple eigenvalues)
.

For multiple isolated eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
,
the above results can be applied to each eigenvalue separately,
as eigenspaces w.r.t. different eigenvalues are orthogonal.
Hence, we obtain
the analyticity of the total eigenprojectors onto the corresponding eigenspaces
and of the Ritz-value sums associated with these eigenspaces in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
by summing up the respective power series expansions.
We omit the concrete formulation here for brevity,
and refer to
[
27
, Thm. 1]
for a more general result w.r.t.​ clusters of eigenvalues with a slightly different proof strategy.

3.4.
Truncation of the total eigenprojector series

Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{K}
be some
distinct
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
with respective multiplicities
m_{1},\dots,m_{K}\in\mathbb{N}
,
and let
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
be the total eigenprojector onto the eigenspaces associated with these eigenvalues,
i.e.,

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0}),\quad\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{k}\,\lambda_{k},\quad\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{k}=M.

From the last section, we know that there exists an analytic projector-valued function
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
such that
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{P}
.
Let us now consider power series of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
and its
n
-th order truncation
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
at point
\bm{\mu}_{0}
, i.e.,

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})=\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\,\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\sum_{|\bm{\beta}|\leq n}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\,\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}

for
\bm{\mu}
sufficiently close to
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
We also define a power series

(3.20)

\displaystyle P_{n+1}(x)\,\coloneqq\,\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}x^{p}\Big(\sum_{|\bm{\beta}|=p+n+1}\|\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\|\Big),

which is obtained by applying the triangle inequality to
\|\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})\|
, regrouping the terms
and dividing the resulting expression by
x^{n+1}
with
x\coloneqq|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|
.
Due to the analyticity of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
,

P_{n+1}(x)
is well-defined and convergent for
x
sufficiently small.

In general, the truncated projector
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
is not an orthogonal projector.
Nonetheless, a part of its range should be close to
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
for
\bm{\mu}
close to
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
This intuition is made precise as follows.

Lemma 3.8

(Total eigenprojector error concerning the truncated projector series)
.

Within the above setting,
there exist constants
\varepsilon>0
,

\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}\in(2,4]
and
an analytic orthogonal projector-valued function
{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}}(\bm{\mu})
,
satisfying for all
|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|\leq\varepsilon
that
\mathrm{ran}\,{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}}(\bm{\mu})=\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
and

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|\;\leq\;\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|^{n+1}P_{n+1}(|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|)

with
P_{n+1}(x)
defined in (
3.20
).
In particular, we have the asymptotic estimate

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|\;=\;\cal{O}\big(|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|^{n+1}\big)\quad\text{as }\bm{\mu}\to\bm{\mu}_{0},

and the coefficient of the leading error term is given by
\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}P_{n+1}(0)=\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}\sum_{|\bm{\beta}|=n+1}\|\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\|
.

Proof.

The total projector
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
has eigenvalues equal to
1
with multiplicity
M
and eigenvalues equal to
0
with multiplicity
N-M
for all
\bm{\mu}
in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
The truncated series
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
can be viewed as a small perturbation of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
for
\bm{\mu}\to\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
Applying the perturbation machinery from previous subsections,
we know that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
are continous and close to those of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
,
with
M
of them clustering around
1
and the rest around
0
.

Let us choose
\varepsilon>0
,
such that
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
enables the power series expansion (
3.19
),
and that the closed annulus
\overline{R_{1/4,3/4}(1)}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:1/4\leq|z-1|\leq 3/4\}
contains no eigenvalue of
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
for all
|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|\leq\varepsilon
.
In particular, the contour
\Gamma_{\!1}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z-1|=1/2\}
encircles exactly
M
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
for all
|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|\leq\varepsilon
.
We define
{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}}(\bm{\mu})
as the orthogonal projector onto the eigenspace of
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
associated with these
M
eigenvalues via the contour integral

(3.21)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}}(\bm{\mu})\,\coloneqq\,\frac{-1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma_{\!1}}\big(\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})-\zeta\,\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

Due to results from the previous subsections,
{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}}(\bm{\mu})
is analytic in the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
with
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
and
\mathrm{rank}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})=M
by construction.
For the error estimate, we firstly note that

\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})=\frac{-1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma_{\!1}}\big(\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})-\zeta\,\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta,

since
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
is an orthogonal projector.
Besides, applying the resolvent identity

(\mathbf{A}-\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}-(\mathbf{B}-\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}=(\mathbf{A}-\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})(\mathbf{A}-\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\quad\text{for }\zeta\notin\sigma(\mathbf{A})\cup\sigma(\mathbf{B})

to the two integral representations yield

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})=\,

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma_{\!1}}\big(\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})-\zeta\,\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\big)\big(\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})-\zeta\,\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

By taking the spectral norm of both sides,
using the classical contour integral estimates and the sub-multiplicativity of the spectral norm,
we obtain

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|\,\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{length}(\Gamma_{\!1})}{2\pi}\max_{\zeta\in\Gamma_{\!1}}\|\big(\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})-\zeta\,\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\|\,\|\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|\,\max_{\zeta\in\Gamma_{\!1}}\|\big(\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})-\zeta\,\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\|.

Clearly
\mathrm{length}(\Gamma_{\!1})=\pi
.
Since
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
has only eigenvalues
0
and
1
regardless of
\bm{\mu}
,
we see that

\max_{\zeta\in\Gamma_{\!1}}\|\big(\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})-\zeta\,\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\|\;=\max_{\ignorespaces\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}))\\
\zeta\in\Gamma_{\!1}\end{subarray}}\ignorespaces\frac{1}{|\lambda-\zeta|}\;=\;2\qquad\text{for all }|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|\leq\varepsilon.

By the choice of
\varepsilon
, eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
are not in
\overline{R_{1/4,3/4}(1)}
for any
|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|\leq\varepsilon
, and so

\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}\coloneqq\sup_{|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|\leq\varepsilon}\max_{\zeta\in\Gamma_{\!1}}\|\big(\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})-\zeta\,\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\|\;=\sup_{|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|\leq\varepsilon}\max_{\ignorespaces\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\in\Gamma_{\!1}\\
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu}))\end{subarray}}\ignorespaces\frac{1}{|\lambda-\zeta|}\in(2,4].

Collecting the previous facts and using again the absolute convergence of the power series of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
,
we deduce for all
|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|\leq\varepsilon
that

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|\;\leq\;\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}\|\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|.

The desired estimate and the assertion on the leading order term follows from the definition of
P_{n+1}(x)
in (
3.20
).
∎

Next, we investigate the difference between the total Ritz-value sums associated with
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
and
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})
, respectively.
Using
Lemma

3.8
and
Lemma

2.5
,
we can now prove that the corresponding Ritz-value difference is of squared order to the projector error with not much effort.
However,
to establish a clearer leading-order term in the error expansion,
we need additional preparations.

The first preparation concerns the power series
P_{n+1}(x)
defined in (
3.20
).
Let us extend
P_{n+1}(x)
as a complex function to
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq\varepsilon\}
,
where
\varepsilon>0
comes from
Lemma

3.8
.
Then, we define

(3.22)

\displaystyle\|P_{n+1}\|_{\infty,\varepsilon}\,\coloneqq\,\max_{|z|=\varepsilon}|P_{n+1}(z)|,

which can be interpreted as a measure of size of the tail of the projector series beyond order
n
.
Note that
\|P_{n+1}\|_{\infty,\varepsilon}\geq P_{n+1}(0)=\sum_{|\bm{\beta}|=n+1}\|\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\|
due to the maximum modulus principle.

Moreover, we know that
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})
is analytic in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
by
Lemma

3.8
.
Hence, we may write its power series expansion as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})\,\eqqcolon\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\,\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{pt}}

with coefficient matrices given by the multivariate Cauchy integral formula

(3.23)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{pt}}\,=\,\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{d}}\oint_{|\zeta_{1}|=\varepsilon}\!\!\cdots\oint_{|\zeta_{d}|=\varepsilon}\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu}_{0}+\zeta_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+\zeta_{d}\mathbf{e}_{d})}{\zeta_{1}^{\beta_{1}+1}\cdots\zeta_{d}^{\beta_{d}+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\zeta_{d}

for each
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
.
By a similar argument as in
Lemma

3.2
,
we deduce for an aribtary
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
that
the rank of
\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}
and of
\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}
is upper bounded by
M\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\beta_{j}+1)
.
Then, let us set

(3.24)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{pt}}(n)\;\coloneqq\;\max_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\,|\bm{\beta}|=n}\mathrm{rank}\big(\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{pt}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\big)\quad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

In addition, we use the coefficients of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})
and
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
to define

(3.25)

\displaystyle A^{(n)}_{\mathrm{pt}}\,\coloneqq\max_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\,|\bm{\beta}|=n}\|\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big(\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big)\big(\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{pt}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\big)\|\quad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

which can be roughly interpreted as the "energy leakage" at order
n+1
between the two projector series w.r.t. the unperturbed operator.

Finally, note that the number of multi-indices
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
|\bm{\beta}|=n
is
\binom{n+d-1}{d-1}\coloneqq\frac{(n+d-1)!}{n!(d-1)!}
.

We are now ready to present the result on the Ritz-value difference.

Theorem 3.9

(Total Ritz-value error w.r.t. the truncated projector)
.

Unter the setting of
Lemma

3.8
,
the Ritz-value difference
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\big(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\big)
is an analytic function in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}

and its Taylor expansion at point
\bm{\mu}_{0}
has vanishing coefficients up to order
2n+1
.
In particular, we have the asymptotic estimate

\displaystyle|\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\big(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\big)|\,\leq\,\cal{O}\big(|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|^{2n+2}\big)\quad\text{as }\bm{\mu}\to\bm{\mu}_{0},

and the coefficient of the leading error term is bounded from above by

(3.26)

\displaystyle 2\,\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}^{2}\,{\|P_{n+1}\|_{\infty,\varepsilon}^{2}}\,R_{\mathrm{pt}}(n+1)\;A^{(n+1)}_{\mathrm{pt}}\,\binom{n+d-1}{d-1}^{\!\!2}

with
\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}\in(2,4]
from
Lemma

3.8
, and
\|P_{n+1}\|_{\infty,\varepsilon}
,
R_{\mathrm{pt}}(n+1)
and
A^{(n+1)}_{\mathrm{pt}}
defined in (
3.22
), (
3.24
) and (
3.25
), respectively.

This upper bound for the error coefficient suggests that the dominating contribution to the Ritz-value difference comes from
the "energy leakage" at order
n+1
between the two projector series w.r.t. the unperturbed operator,
scaled at most by
(i) the maximal rank of the differences of the coefficient matrices at order
n+1
,
(ii) the size of the tail of the projector series beyond order
n
as a holomorphic function, and
(iii) the number of multi-indices at order
n+1
.

Proof.

Set
{\bm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
.
Since
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})
is analytic in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
by
Lemma

3.8
,
the Ritz-value difference
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}){\bm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})=\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\big(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\big)
is also analytic.

Also observe from the asymptotic order
\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|\in\cal{O}(|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|^{n+1})

for
\bm{\mu}\to\bm{\mu}_{0}
in
Lemma

3.8
that
\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{pt}}=\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}
for all
|\bm{\beta}|\leq n
.
Hence,
{\bm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
has the power series

\displaystyle{\bm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\sum_{|\bm{\beta}|\geq n+1}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\,\big(\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{pt}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\big).

Applying
Lemma

2.5
yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}){\bm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\big(\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\big){\bm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})^{\!2}.

Hence, the Taylor expansion of
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}){\bm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})
at point
\bm{\mu}_{0}
has vanishing coefficients up to order
2n+1
,
and its leading order terms are given by

\displaystyle T\;\coloneqq\sum_{|\bm{\nu}_{1}|=|\bm{\nu}_{2}|=n+1}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}}\,\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big(\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big)\big(\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}_{\mathrm{pt}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\big)\big(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}\big).

Since it holds by triangle inequality that

\displaystyle|T|\;\leq\;|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|^{2n+2}\!\!\!\!\sum_{|\bm{\nu}_{1}|=|\bm{\nu}_{2}|=n+1}|\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big(\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big)\big(\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}_{\mathrm{pt}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\big)\big(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}\big)|,

and there are
\binom{n+d-1}{d-1}^{2}
such terms in the summation,
it remains to show that

\displaystyle|\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big(\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big)\big(\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}_{\mathrm{pt}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\big)\big(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}\big)|\,\leq\,2\,\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}^{2}R_{\mathrm{pt}}(n+1)\,A^{(n+1)}_{\mathrm{pt}}\,{\|P_{n+1}\|_{\infty,\varepsilon}^{2}}

for arbitrary multi-indices
\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2}
with
|\bm{\nu}_{1}|=|\bm{\nu}_{2}|=n+1
.

To this end, note that

(3.27)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{M}_{1}\mathbf{M}_{2}\mathbf{M}_{3}|\,\leq\,(\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{M}_{1}\mathbf{M}_{2}\mathbf{M}_{3})\,\|\mathbf{M}_{1}\|\,\|\mathbf{M}_{2}\|\,\|\mathbf{M}_{3}\|\quad\text{for any matrices }\mathbf{M}_{1},\mathbf{M}_{2},\mathbf{M}_{3}.

By setting
\mathbf{W}
to be the orthogonal projector onto the range of
\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}_{\mathrm{pt}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}
,
we apply (
3.27
) to

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{1}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big(\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big)\mathbf{W},\quad\mathbf{M}_{2}\coloneqq\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}_{\mathrm{pt}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})},\quad\mathbf{M}_{3}\coloneqq\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{pt}}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}.

Also note that
\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{M}_{1}\mathbf{M}_{2}\mathbf{M}_{3}\leq\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{M}_{2}\leq 2R_{\mathrm{pt}}(n+1)

and
\|\mathbf{M}_{1}\|\leq A^{(n+1)}_{\mathrm{pt}}
by the definitions in (
3.24
) and in (
3.25
), respectively.
For the norms of
\mathbf{M}_{2}
and
\mathbf{M}_{3}
,
we apply the multivariate Cauchy integral formula to
{\bm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})
and obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{pt}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\|\,=\,\left\|\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{d}}\oint_{|\zeta_{1}|=\varepsilon}\!\!\cdots\oint_{|\zeta_{d}|=\varepsilon}\frac{{\bm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu}_{0}+\zeta_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+\zeta_{d}\mathbf{e}_{d})}{(\zeta_{1}-\mu_{0,1})^{\beta_{1}+1}\cdots(\zeta_{d}-\mu_{0,d})^{\beta_{d}+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\zeta_{d}\right\|

for
\bm{\mu}_{0}=(\mu_{0,1},\dots,\mu_{0,d})
and
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})
with
|\bm{\beta}|=n+1
.
By taking the spectral norm inside the integral,
observing
\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{\mathrm{pt}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|\leq\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}\|\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|
as in the proof of
Lemma

3.8

and using the definition of
\|P_{n+1}\|_{\infty,\varepsilon}
in (
3.22
),
we arrive at

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{pt}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\|\;\leq\;\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{2\pi\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\beta_{1}+1}}\cdots\frac{2\pi\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\beta_{d}+1}}\!\!\sup_{\ignorespaces\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\\
|\tilde{\bm{\mu}}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|=\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}\|\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\tilde{\bm{\mu}})-\mathbf{P}(\tilde{\bm{\mu}})\|\;\leq\;\delta_{\mathrm{pt}}{\|P_{n+1}\|_{\infty,\varepsilon}}.

Collecting the previous estimates completes the proof.
∎

To summarize,
Section

3.4
suggests that it is a good idea
to perform spectral approximations
within the range of the truncated projector series.
Hence, it would be beneficial to reduce the approximation problem into a lower-dimensional subspace containing the range of
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
.
This motivates the Taylor-RBM from another perspective.

4.
Taylor-Reduced Basis Method

In this section, we finally present and analyze the Taylor-reduced basis method for approximating the invariant subspace and the associated Ritz-value sum of a parameter-dependent matrix
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
.
We also provide some implementation considerations for the method.

Before that, let us clarify and recall the notations and the problem setting.

For a
general
orthogonal projector
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}^{2}=\mathbf{Q}^{*}
,
we denote by
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{Q}}\!\!(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{Q}}
the restriction of
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
onto the range of
\mathbf{Q}
.
We will also use the notation
\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}(\bm{\mu})
for the orthogonal projector onto the orthogonal complement of
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{Q}(\bm{\mu})
.

Given a fixed parameter value
\bm{\mu}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
,
let us denote by

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mu}_{0})<\dots<\lambda_{K}(\bm{\mu}_{0})<\lambda_{K+1}(\bm{\mu}_{0})<\dots

the
distinct
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
arranged in increasing order,
each with multiplicity
m_{1},m_{2},\dots
, respectively.
Denote by
M\coloneqq m_{1}+\dots+m_{K}
the total multiplicity of the first
K
distinct eigenvalues.
Let
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
be the total projector onto the invariant subspace associated with the first
K
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\coloneqq\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mu}_{0}),\dots,\lambda_{K}\coloneqq\lambda_{K}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
,
i.e.,
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
satisfies (
3.18
).
We are interested in approximating the invariant subspace
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
as well as the Ritz-value sum
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
for
\bm{\mu}
in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
using subspace methods.

4.1.
The Taylor-RBM and its convergence analysis

From
Section

3
,
we know that
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
admits a convergent power series expansion around
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
Together with
Theorem

2.4
and
Lemma

3.8
,
we are motivated to perform the approximation within a finite dimensional space containing reasonable information of the Taylor coefficients of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
.

Definition 4.1

(Taylor reduced basis method)
.

Consider the setting from the beginning of
Section

4
.
For a given order
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
let
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
be the
n
-th order Taylor approximation of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
around
\bm{\mu}_{0}
as in (
3.19
).

(i)

The
Taylor reduced basis space (Taylor-RB space)
w.r.t.
\bm{\mu}_{0}
of order
n
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\!n}\,\coloneqq\,\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\,\coloneqq\,\mathrm{span}\big\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\big\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{N}.

The corresponding orthogonal projector onto
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
is denoted by
{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}
or
{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
.

(ii)

Let
r\coloneqq\dim\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
be the dimension of the Taylor-RB space
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
,
and
\mathbf{V}_{\mkern-5.0mun}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times r}
a matrix whose columns form an ONB of
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
.
The matrix

\mathbf{A}^{\!\!\mathbf{V}_{\mkern-5.0mun}}(\bm{\mu})\,\coloneqq\,\mathbf{V}_{\mkern-5.0mun}^{*}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{V}_{\mkern-5.0mun}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}

is called the
reduced order model (ROM)
of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
w.r.t.​ the Taylor-RB space
\mathcal{V}_{n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
.

(iii)

For
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}
in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
let
\mathbf{Q}^{\mathbf{V}_{\mkern-5.0mun}}(\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
be a solution of the reduced problem

(4.1)

\mathbf{Q}^{\mathbf{V}_{\mkern-5.0mun}}(\bm{\mu})\ \text{ solves}\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{2}=\mathbf{M}^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}\\
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{M}=M\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}^{\!\!\mathbf{V}_{\mkern-5.0mun}}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{M},

and set
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{V}_{\mkern-5.0mun}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathbf{V}_{\mkern-5.0mun}}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{V}_{\mkern-5.0mun}^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
.
The matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
is called the
Taylor reduced basis approximation (Taylor-RB approximation)
of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
w.r.t.​
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
.

Note that (
4.1
) is a practical formulation,
and it is equivalent to

\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})\ \text{ solves}\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{2}=\mathbf{M}^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\\
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{M}=M\\
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{M}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\end{subarray}}\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{M}.

We should firstly clarify if
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
can be determined uniquely for
\bm{\mu}
near
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.

Lemma 4.2

(Well-posedness of the Taylor-RBM)
.

Under the setting from the beginning of
Section

4
and from
Definition

4.1
,
there exists a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
such that for all
\bm{\mu}
in this neighborhood,
the reduced problem defining
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
admits a unique solution.

Proof.

We should look at the compression
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}}\!\!(\bm{\mu})
,
and we firstly consider the case
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\mu}_{0}
.

Since
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}^{(\mathbf{0})}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
,
\lambda_{K}(\bm{\mu}_{0})<\lambda_{K+1}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
and we are in the variational setting,

\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
is uniquely determined with
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
.
This also implies that
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
and
{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
are spectral projectors of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}}\!\!\!(\bm{\mu}_{0})
.
In patricular,
the distance between the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu}_{0})}\!\!(\bm{\mu}_{0})
and those of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu}_{0})}\!\!(\bm{\mu}_{0})
is positive.

The previous considerations and the analyticity of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\!\upharpoonright_{{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}}\!\!(\bm{\mu})
in
\bm{\mu}
enable us to apply the perturbation results from
Section

3
.
Consequently,
the total eigenprojector associated with the first
M
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}}\!\!(\bm{\mu})
varies analytically with
\bm{\mu}
in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
and this is exactly
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
(after suitable embedding from
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
to
\mathbb{C}^{N}
).
Hence,
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
is uniquely determined for all
\bm{\mu}
in this neighborhood.
∎

Next, we analyze how well does
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
approximate
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
for
\bm{\mu}
in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
To this end, let us introduce some auxiliary quantities.
Firstly, we denote

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}^{2}\!(\bm{\mu},{\bm{\Pi}}_{n})\,\coloneqq\,\mathrm{Res}^{2}\!(\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}),{\bm{\Pi}}_{n})\,=\,\|{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}){\bm{\Pi}}_{n}^{\perp}\|^{2},

which was introduced in
Theorem

2.4
as the squared residual of the
entire
subspace
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
w.r.t.
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
.
Recall that this quantity measures how far is the
entire
Taylor-RB space
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
from an invariant subspace of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
.
Also observe that
\mathrm{Res}^{2}\!(\bm{\mu},{\bm{\Pi}}_{n})
is a non-negative continuous function in
\bm{\mu}
by
Remark

3.6
.
Moreover,
we know from
Lemma

4.2
that
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
is well-defined for
\bm{\mu}
in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
with
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
.
Since
\lambda_{K}(\bm{\mu}_{0})<\lambda_{K+1}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
and individual eigenvalues depend continuously on the parameter by
Remark

3.6
,
the quantity

(4.3)

\displaystyle\gamma(\bm{\mu})\,\coloneqq\,\min\Big\{|\lambda-\hat{\lambda}|:\lambda\in\sigma\big(\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})}\!\!(\bm{\mu})\big),\hat{\lambda}\in\sigma\big(\mathbf{A}\!\!\upharpoonright_{{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}\mathbf{Q}^{\perp}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})}\!\!(\bm{\mu})\big)\Big\}

is positive and continuous in a possibly smaller neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
Using the continuity of
\mathrm{Res}^{2}\!(\bm{\mu},{\bm{\Pi}}_{n})
and of
\gamma(\bm{\mu})
at
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
we also deduce that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+\ignorespaces\frac{\mathrm{Res}^{2}\!(\bm{\mu},{\bm{\Pi}}_{n})}{\gamma(\bm{\mu})^{2}}}\,=\,\sqrt{1+\ignorespaces\frac{\mathrm{Res}^{2}\!(\bm{\mu}_{0},{\bm{\Pi}}_{n})}{\gamma(\bm{\mu}_{0})^{2}}}+\raisebox{0.86108pt}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\mathcal{O}$}}(1)\qquad\text{as }\bm{\mu}\to\bm{\mu}_{0}.

With these preparations, we are ready to present the eigenprojector error bound.

Theorem 4.3

(A priori total eigenprojector error bound of the Taylor-RBM)
.

Under the notations from the beginning of
Section

4
and from
Definition

4.1
,
it holds for
\bm{\mu}
close to
\bm{\mu}_{0}
that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|\,\leq\,\sqrt{1+\ignorespaces\frac{\mathrm{Res}^{2}\!(\bm{\mu},{\bm{\Pi}}_{n})}{\gamma(\bm{\mu})^{2}}}\,\|{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|

with
\mathrm{Res}^{2}\!(\bm{\mu},{\bm{\Pi}}_{n})
and
\gamma(\bm{\mu})
defined in (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), respectively.
In particular, we have the asymptotic estimate

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|\;=\;\cal{O}\big(|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|^{n+1}\big)\qquad\text{as }\bm{\mu}\to\bm{\mu}_{0},

and the coefficient of the leading error term is given by

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+\frac{\mathrm{Res}^{2}\!(\bm{\mu}_{0},{\bm{\Pi}}_{n})}{\gamma(\bm{\mu}_{0})^{2}}}\;P_{n+1}(0),

where
P_{n+1}(0)=\sum_{|\bm{\beta}|=n+1}\|\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\|
is defined in (
3.20
).

This theorem tells us that within the setting of
Section

4
,
the approximation error of the Taylor-RBM is essentially controlled by projection error of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
on
{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}
,
which is by the construction of
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
at least in the magnitude of the truncation error of the Taylor series of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
.
The leading error term suggests that the quality of the approximation also depends on how well
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
approximates an invariant subspace of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
(measured by
\mathrm{Res}^{2}\!(\bm{\mu}_{0},{\bm{\Pi}}_{n})
)
as well as on "how bad is the problem" (measured by
\gamma(\bm{\mu}_{0})^{-1}
).

Proof.

As discussed right before this theorem,
\gamma(\bm{\mu})
is positive and continuous in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
Besides,
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
and
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
have the same rank in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
Applying
Lemma

2.3
and
Theorem

2.4
to
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
and
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
,
we obtain the desired inequality.

For the asymptotic estimate,
the definition of
{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}
in
Definition

4.1
yields

{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{0}
,
where
\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})
is the
n
-th truncation of the series expansion of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
at
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
cf. (
3.19
).
Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\Pi}}_{n}^{\perp}\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})\|\,\leq\,\|\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{P}^{[n]}(\bm{\mu})\|\,\leq\,|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|^{n+1}P_{n+1}(|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|)

for
P_{n+1}
defined in (
3.20
).
Combining this estimate with (
4.4
), we conclude the proof.
∎

We now compare the true Ritz-value sum
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
with its Taylor-RBM approximation
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
.
Proceeding similarly as in
Theorem

3.9
,
we need some preparations.

To begin with,
let us choose
\epsilon>0
,
such that
Lemma

4.2
and
Theorem

4.3
hold true for all
\bm{\mu}
with
|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|\leq\epsilon
.
Then, we extend
P_{n+1}(x)
as a complex function on
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq\epsilon\}

and define

(4.5)

\displaystyle\|P_{n+1}\|_{\infty,\epsilon}\,\coloneqq\,\max_{|z|=\epsilon}|P_{n+1}(z)|.

Note that
\|P_{n+1}\|_{\infty,\epsilon}\geq\max_{|z|\leq\epsilon}|P_{n+1}(z)|
due to the maximum modulus principle.

In addition,

\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
is analytic in
\bm{\mu}
for
|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0}|\leq\epsilon
by the proof of
Lemma

4.2
.
Thus, it admits a convergent power series expansion around
\bm{\mu}_{0}
that reads as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\,\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}^{(\bm{\beta})}.

Using the multivariate Cauchy integral formula, we obtain for
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{rb}}\,=\,\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{d}}\oint_{|\zeta_{1}|=\epsilon}\!\!\cdots\oint_{|\zeta_{d}|=\epsilon}\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu}_{0}+\zeta_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+\zeta_{d}\mathbf{e}_{d})}{\zeta_{1}^{\beta_{1}+1}\cdots\zeta_{d}^{\beta_{d}+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\zeta_{d}.

With
Lemma

3.2
, we also deduce that
\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}^{(\bm{\beta})}\leq M\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\beta_{j}+1)
.
Let us then define

(4.7)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{rb}}(n)\;\coloneqq\;\max_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\,|\bm{\beta}|=n}\mathrm{rank}\big(\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{rb}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\big)\quad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Moreover, we use the coefficients of
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{rb}}(\bm{\mu})
and of
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
to define

(4.8)

\displaystyle A^{(n)}_{\mathrm{rb}}\,\coloneqq\max_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\,|\bm{\beta}|=n}\|\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big(\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\big)\big(\mathbf{Q}^{(\bm{\beta})}_{\mathrm{rb}}-\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}\big)\|\quad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

which can be roughly understood as the "energy leakage" at order
n+1
between the two projector series w.r.t. the unperturbed operator.

Finally, we recall that the number of
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
satisfying
|\bm{\beta}|=n
is given by
\binom{n+d-1}{d-1}\coloneqq\frac{(n+d-1)!}{n!(d-1)!}
.

With all these preparations, we now present the Ritz-value error estimate of the Taylor-RBM.

Theorem 4.4

(A priori total Ritz-value error w.r.t. Taylor-RBM)
.

Unter the setting from the beginning of
Section

), (

), respectively.

.

4.2.
Practical assembly

We begin with the following remark concerning
Theorem

4.3
and
4.4
,
which are presented in the current form to align with the notion in
[
28
]
.

Remark 4.5

(Extended subspaces)
.

Theorem

4.3
and
4.4
also hold true for subspace methods
w.r.t. any subspace containing the Taylor-RB space
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
, and the proof is analogous.
Practically, this enables coupling the Taylor-RBM with other MOR techniques for parametric eigenvalue problems.
This will be addressed in future work.

To assemble a subspace containing
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}^{(\bm{\beta})}
for
|\bm{\beta}|\leq n
,
we only need to focus on finding
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
by
Remark

3.7
.
Here,
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
is a Taylor coefficient of the analytic total eigenprojector
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
around
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
where
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\eqqcolon\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
is the total eigenprojector associated to an isolated eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}\in\{\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mu}_{0}),\ldots,\lambda_{K}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\}
of multiplicity
m_{0}
.
In other words, it suffices to consider the setting of
Section

3.1
–
3.3
.
Let us also choose a matrix
\mathbf{U}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m_{0}}
,
whose columns form an ONB of
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
,
i.e.,
\mathbf{I}_{m_{0}}=\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{U}_{0}
,
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}=\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}
and
\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}=\lambda_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}
for
\mathbf{A}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
.

Our goal is to efficiently assemble a subspace containing
\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}
for a given
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
based on
\lambda_{0}
,
\mathbf{U}_{0}

and the partial derivatives
\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}
.
Efficiency here means threefold:
(i) avoiding the construction of dense
N\times N
matrices entirely,
(ii) minimizing the combinatorial complexity associated with the number of terms, and
(iii) ensuring the growth of the dimension of the constructed subspace as moderate as possible.

4.2.1.
Recursive formula for the assembly of
\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}

We shall not use the recursive formula for
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
in
Proposition

3.4
to obtain ranges of the projector coefficients,
as building and storing dense
N\times N
matrices is not practical for large-scale problems at all.

To efficiently construct the target space,
we need some preparations,
starting with the following lemma on the parametrization of subspaces to reduce the problem size.

Lemma 4.6

(Subspace parametrisation and problem reduction)
.

For
\bm{\mu}
in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
, let us consider

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m_{0}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{0}\times m_{0}}.

Then, the following assertions hold true for
\bm{\mu}
in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
:

(i)

\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})
is invertible and analytic.

(ii)

\mathbf{P}_{\!0}|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})}:\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\to\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}

and
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}}:\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\to\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})

are invertible.

(iii)

The ranges of
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
and
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})
coincide,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}_{0}.

(iv)

The orthogonal projector
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
can be expressed as

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}\,\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})^{*},

and it holds that

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}\,\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu}).

(v)

The matrix

(4.13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m_{0}}

is well-defined and analytic in
\bm{\mu}
.
It also holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}\,=\,\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})

and so

\displaystyle\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\mathrm{ran}\,\big(\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})\big).

(vi)

The matrix
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})\,\coloneqq\,\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}

is well-defined and analytic.
It also holds that

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}\,=\,\big(\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})\big)\,\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu}).

Remark 4.7

(Connection to many-body perturbation theory)
.

\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
is related to the
intermediate normalization
or the
correction operator
,
and
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})
to the
effective Hamiltonian
.
Moreover,
the identity
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}=\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
from
Lemma

4.6

(v)

can be seen as a numerical linear algebraic concretization of the
wave operator equation
[
32
,
18
]
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.6
.

Concerning
(i)
,
note that analyticity of
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})
follows from that of
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
.
Also observe that
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{U}_{0}=\mathbf{I}_{m_{0}}
.
Since
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})
is continuous in
\bm{\mu}
,

\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=m_{0}
and the matrix rank is invariant under small perturbations,

\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})
has full rank and is thus invertible.

We now show
(ii)
.
Recall that if
\mathbf{M}_{1},\mathbf{M}_{2}
are linear maps between finite-dimensional spaces of the same dimension,
and if the composition
\mathbf{M}_{1}\mathbf{M}_{2}
is invertible,
then both
\mathbf{M}_{1}
and
\mathbf{M}_{2}
are invertible.
Now in our setting,
observe that
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})^{2}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
.
Hence, we deduce from the invertibility of
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}_{0}

and from
\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})=m_{0}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu}_{0})

that
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})}=\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})}
is bijective as a map from
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
to
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
,
and also that
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}}
is bijective as a map from
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
to
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
.

(iii)
follows directly from the bijectivity of
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}}:\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\to\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
by
(ii)
and from the definition of
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})
.

To prove
(iv)
,
note that the orthogonal projector of the column space of a full-column-rank matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m_{0}}
is given by
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{M}^{*}\mathbf{M})^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{*}
.
Applying this to
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})
and using the definition of
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})
as well as
(iii)
,
we obtain (
4.11
).
The identity (
4.12
) follows from multiplying
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})
from the left to both sides of (
4.11
),
and reformulating the left hand side using
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
as well as
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})
.

Next, we show
(v)
.
Well-definedness and analyticity of
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
follow from those of
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})
and of
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}
.
The definition of
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}_{0}
and of
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})
yield
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}=\mathbf{U}_{0}
.
Hence, we use the definition of
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
to deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}\,=\,(\mathbf{P}_{\!0}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp})\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}\,=\,\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu}).

Again by the invertibility of
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})
,
we know that
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})=\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}
.
Combining this with
(iii)
and the above equality concludes the proof of
(v)
.

Finally, we prove
(vi)
.
By subsituting the definition of
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}
into (
4.12
)
using again
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}=\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
from
(v)
,
we arrive at (
4.14
).
∎

\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m_{0}}
is a thin analytic matrix function,
and
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{0}\times m_{0}}
is small analytic matrix function.
They are crucial for the efficient assembly of
\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}
.
We denote by

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})\,=\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\,\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})\,=\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\,\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\beta})}

their Taylor series expansion around
\bm{\mu}_{0}
with coefficients
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m_{0}}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\beta})}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{0}\times m_{0}}
.

Let us take a further look at
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
.
Note that
\mathbf{K}^{(\mathbf{0})}=\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{0}
by
Lemma

4.6

(v)
.
The same argument yields that
\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}=\mathbf{0}
for all
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
.
More importantly,
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
is related to
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
.

Theorem 4.8

(Connection between
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
and
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
)
.

Consider the analytic matrix function
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
defined in (
4.13
) with
\bm{\mu}
in a small neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
it holds that

(4.16)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}\,&=\,\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{U}_{0}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}\\
\,&=\,\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{U}_{0},\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}.\end{aligned}

We emphasize that constructing the desired subspace
\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}
by definition
concerns large and generally dense matrices
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}
.
However,
\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{U}_{0},\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}
only involves the thin matrices
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
of size
N\times m_{0}
with
m_{0}\ll N
,
which can be computed recursively as we will see in
Theorem

4.10
.

Remark 4.9

(Upper bound on the dimension of the constructed subspace)
.

As the number of multi-indices
\bm{\beta}
with
|\bm{\beta}|=n
is
\binom{n+d-1}{d-1}
,
and the thin matrices
\mathbf{U}_{0}
,
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
have rank at most
m_{0}
,
(
4.16
) together with the "hockey-stick identity"
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{k+d-1}{d-1}=\binom{n+d}{d}
implies that

\dim\,\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}\,\leq\,m_{0}\binom{n+d}{d}.

Proof of
Theorem

4.8
.

The two identities in (
4.16
) are proven separately.

\bullet\
Showing the first identity
\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}=\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{U}_{0}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}
.
The inclusion “
\supseteq
” is immediate to see.
To prove the other inclusion “
\subseteq
”,
we will proceed by showing for each fixed
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
|\bm{\beta}|\leq n
that

(4.17)

\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\subseteq\mathrm{span}\bigl\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}:|\bm{\nu}|\leq|\bm{\beta}|\bigr\}.

Taking the span over all
|\bm{\beta}|\leq n
then yields the desired inclusion in (
4.16
).

To prove (
4.17
),
Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
be arbitrary, and we define an analytic vector-valued map

\mathbf{f}(\bm{\mu})\coloneqq\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{u}\qquad\text{for }\bm{\mu}\ \text{near}\ \bm{\mu}_{0}.

By construction,
\mathbf{f}(\bm{\mu})\in\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
for
\bm{\mu}
in a neighborhood of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.

Let us fix an ONB
\{\mathbf{v}_{\!1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{\!m_{0}}\}
of
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
,
and we recall from
Lemma

4.6

(ii)
that the linear map

\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})|_{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}}:\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\to\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
is bijective. Hence,
\{\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{v}_{\!j}\}_{j=1}^{m_{0}}
forms a basis of
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
, and there exist unique coefficients
\alpha_{j}(\bm{\mu})
,
such that

(4.18)

\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{u}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{0}}\alpha_{j}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{v}_{\!j}.

Moreover, the coefficient vector
\bm{\alpha}(\bm{\mu})=\big(\alpha_{1}(\bm{\mu}),\dots,\alpha_{m_{0}}(\bm{\mu})\big)^{\!\top}
depends analytically on
\bm{\mu}
:
Applying
\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}
from the left to (
4.18
)
and using
\mathbf{v}_{\!i}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{v}_{\!i}\in\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
,
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}=\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}
as well as
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}_{0}
by definition as from
Lemma

4.6
,
we see that

\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{u}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{0}}\alpha_{j}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{\!j}=\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m_{0}}\alpha_{j}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{v}_{\!j}\Big).

Since
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})
is invertible and the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{\!j}
form an ONB of
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
,
multiplying
\mathbf{v}_{\!i}^{*}\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}
from the left yields

\alpha_{i}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{v}_{\!i}^{*}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{0}}\alpha_{j}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{v}_{\!j}=\mathbf{v}_{\!i}^{*}\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{u},

which is analytic since
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}
and
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\mu})
are analytic near
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.

Now we expand the analytic maps in multivariate Taylor-series about
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
Using the usual coefficient notation,
we write

\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})=\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}{(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha_{j}(\bm{\mu})=\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}{(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}}\alpha_{j}^{(\bm{\beta})}.

Substituting these into (
4.18
) and equating the coefficient of
(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}
for every
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
we obtain

(4.19)

\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{u}\,=\,\sum_{j=1}^{m_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{1}=\bm{\beta}\end{subarray}}\alpha_{j}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}\mathbf{v}_{\!j}.

Since
\mathbf{v}_{\!j}\in\mathrm{ran}(\mathbf{P}_{\!0})
,
we have
\mathbf{v}_{\!j}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{v}_{\!j}
,
and hence,

\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu})}\mathbf{v}_{\!j}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{v}_{\!j}\in\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
.
Therefore, (
4.19
) implies

\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{u}\in\mathrm{span}\bigl\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\nu})}\mathbf{P}_{\!0}:|\bm{\nu}|\leq|\bm{\beta}|\bigr\}.

Since
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is arbitrary, this proves (
4.17
), and hence, the first identity of (
4.16
).

\bullet\
Showing the second identity
\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{U}_{0}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}=\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{U}_{0},\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}
.
Recall from
Lemma

4.6

(v)
that

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})\mathbf{U}_{0}\,=\,\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\big(\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})\big)\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu}),

where each term is analytic in
\bm{\mu}
.
Also recall that
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu}_{0})=\mathbf{I}_{m_{0}}
,
and let us write its Taylor-series at
\bm{\mu}_{0}
as
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})\eqqcolon\mathbf{I}_{m_{0}}+\sum_{\bm{\beta}\neq\mathbf{0}}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}\mathbf{C}^{(\bm{\beta})}
.
Expanding (
4.20
) with the Taylor-series of
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}(\bm{\mu})
,
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
and
\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})
,
and equating the coefficient of
(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}
for each
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
we obtain

(4.21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{U}_{0}\,=\,\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}+\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}\neq\bm{\beta}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\,\mathbf{C}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}+\mathbf{U}_{0}\,\mathbf{C}^{(\bm{\beta})}.

This implies
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{U}_{0}\subseteq\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{U}_{0},\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}
.
Conversely, rearranging (
4.21
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,=\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{U}_{0}-\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}\neq\bm{\beta}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\,\mathbf{C}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\,\mathbf{C}^{(\bm{\beta})}.

This implies
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}\subseteq\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{U}_{0},\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})},\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu})}:|\bm{\nu}|<n\}
.
An induction argument over
n
concludes the proof.
∎

Next, we provide an iterative procedure for the calculation of
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
.

Let us use the convention
\sum_{\emptyset}\coloneqq\mathbf{0}
from now on.
With the wisdom of hindsight, we introduce

(4.22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,\coloneqq\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}\notin\{\mathbf{0},\bm{\beta}\}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{Y}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,\coloneqq\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\\
\bm{\nu}_{1}\notin\{\mathbf{0},\bm{\beta}\}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}\qquad\text{for }\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}.

Note that
\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(\bm{\beta})}
are thin matrices of size
N\times m_{0}

and that their computations involve only
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu})}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\nu})}
with
|\bm{\nu}|<|\bm{\beta}|
.

Theorem 4.10

(Recursive formula for
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
)
.

Consider the analytic matrix functions
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
,
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})
defined in
Lemma

4.6

and their Taylor-series expansions (
4.15
) at
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
For
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
it holds that

(4.23)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,=\,\mathbf{S}_{0}\big(-\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{U}_{0}-\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})}+\mathbf{Y}^{(\bm{\beta})}\big),

where
\mathbf{S}_{0}
is the reduced resolvent characterized by the property (
3.5
),
each
\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})},\mathbf{Y}^{(\bm{\beta})}
is defined in (
4.22
),

\mathbf{B}^{(\mathbf{0})}\coloneqq\lambda_{0}\mathbf{I}_{m_{0}}

and

(4.24)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,\coloneqq\,\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{0}\times m_{0}}\qquad\text{for }|\bm{\beta}|\in\mathbb{N}.

Observe that each matrix
\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\beta})}

relies only on
\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}
and on
\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})}
,
and the latter depends solely on the previously computed matrices
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu})}
with
|\bm{\nu}|<|\bm{\beta}|
.
We will exploit (
4.23
) and (
4.24
) for the practical assembly procedure of the Taylor-RBM in
Section

4.2.2
.

Proof.

For (
4.23
),
we firstly multiply both sides of (
4.14
) with
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}
from the left.
Using
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
by the definition of
\mathbf{U}_{0}

as well as
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
by (
4.13
),
we then obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\big(\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})\big)\,=\,\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu}).

Expanding this with the Taylor series of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
,
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
and
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})
,
and equating the coefficient of
(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}
for each
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\\
\bm{\nu}_{2}\neq\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\mathbf{A}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}\,=\,\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\\
\bm{\nu}_{2}\neq\bm{\beta}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}.

The only term involving
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
in the sum on the left-hand side is
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}=\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
,
where the equality follows from
the facts that
\mathbf{A}^{\!(\mathbf{0})}=\mathbf{A}_{0}
commutes with
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}=\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
by (
4.13
).
Besides, the only term involving
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
in the sum on the right-hand side is
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{B}^{(\mathbf{0})}=\lambda_{0}\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}
.
Consequently, rearranging the above equation yields

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\mathbf{I}\big)\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}\,=\,-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}\mathbf{U}_{0}-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\\
\bm{\nu}_{2}\notin\{\mathbf{0},\bm{\beta}\}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\mathbf{A}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}+\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}\\
\bm{\nu}_{2}\notin\{\mathbf{0},\bm{\beta}\}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\nu}_{2})}.

Observe that the right-hand side lies in
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}

— the last sum does so since each
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\nu}_{1})}
has its range in
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}
by (
4.13
).
Applying the reduced resolvent
\mathbf{S}_{0}
characterized in (
3.5
) to both sides and using the definition of
\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})}
and of
\mathbf{Y}^{(\bm{\beta})}
in (
4.22
) yields the desired formula (
4.23
).

Next, we show (
4.24
).
Using the definition of
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})
from
Lemma

4.6

(vi)

and the identity

\mathbf{U}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{C}(\bm{\mu})^{-1}=\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})

from
Lemma

4.6

(v)
,
we see that

(4.25)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu}),

Expanding (
4.25
) with the Taylor series of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
,
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mu})
and
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\mu})
,
equating the coefficient of
(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}_{0})^{\bm{\beta}}
for each
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
observing that
\mathbf{K}^{(\mathbf{0})}=\mathbf{0}
as well as
\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{A}^{\!(\mathbf{0})}\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}=\lambda_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}=\mathbf{0}
,
and using the definition of
\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})}
in (
4.22
),
we obtain exactly the formula of
\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\beta})}
in (
4.24
).
∎

Remark 4.11

(Simplification for univariate parameter dependence)
.

In the special case
d=1
,
a multi-index
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
reduces to a single non-negative integer
n=|\bm{\beta}|
.
With the convention
\sum_{1}^{0}\coloneqq\mathbf{0}
,
the formulae (
4.23
) and (
4.24
) are simplified to

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{(n)}\,=\,\mathbf{S}_{0}\Big(-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{A}^{\!(n)}\mathbf{U}_{0}-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{A}^{\!(n-j)}\mathbf{K}^{(j)}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{K}^{(j)}\mathbf{B}^{(n-j)}\Big)\qquad\text{for }n\geq 1,

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{(n)}\,=\,\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{A}^{\!(n)}\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{A}^{\!(n-j)}\mathbf{K}^{(j)}\qquad\text{for }n\geq 1,

respectively.
In this case,
the combinatorial complexity of decomposing multi-indices is reduced to the decomposition of a natural number into two.
This is frequently observed in mathematical physics,
see e.g.
[
34
,
39
]
.
A further simplification would be hardly possible even in this univariate case.
The combinatorial complexity in
Theorem

4.10
is intrinsic to the multivariate setting.

4.2.2.
Implementation considerations

Let us continue with the setting of
Section

4.2
.
From
Theorem

4.8
and
4.10
,
we know that
\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}
is equal to
\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{U}_{0},\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|\leq n\}
,
which in turn can be computed recursively via (
4.23
) and (
4.24
).
The formulae include the scaled partial derivatives
\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}
of the matrix function
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
,
the orthogonal projector
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}
,
as well as the reduced resolvent
\mathbf{S}_{0}
of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
w.r.t.​
\lambda_{0}
.
Let us address these three issues in the following individually.

Remark 4.12

(Affine decomposition and partial derivatives)
.

In usual RBM applications,
the matrix function
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
possesses an
affine decomposition structure
in terms of the parameters,
i.e., there exist parameter-independent Hermitian matrices
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{Q}

and real-analytic functions
\theta_{1}(\bm{\mu}),\ldots,\theta_{Q}(\bm{\mu})
with explicit expressions,
such that

(4.26)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\,=\,\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_{q}(\bm{\mu})\,\mathbf{A}_{q}.

In this case, the scaled partial derivative of
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})
w.r.t.
\bm{\beta}
at
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
namely
\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}
,
can be computed easily from (
4.26
) and from the derivatives of
\theta_{q}(\bm{\mu})
at
\bm{\mu}_{0}
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\!(\bm{\beta})}\,=\,\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_{q}^{(\bm{\beta})}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\,\mathbf{A}_{q}\quad\text{with}\quad\theta_{q}^{(\bm{\beta})}(\bm{\mu}_{0})\,\coloneqq\,\frac{1}{\beta_{1}!\cdots\beta_{d}!}\,\frac{\partial^{|\bm{\beta}|}\theta_{q}}{\partial\mu_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\partial\mu_{d}^{\beta_{d}}}(\bm{\mu}_{0}).

Remark 4.13

(Action of the orthogonal complement projector)
.

The orthogonal complement projector
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}
of
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
doesn’t need to be assembled explicitly.
Its action applied to any vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
can be obtained via

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{v}\,=\,\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{P}_{\!0}\mathbf{v}\,=\,\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{v}.

Remark 4.14

(Action of the reduced resolvent)
.

Recall from (
3.5
) that
the reduced resolvent
\mathbf{S}_{0}
of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
w.r.t.

\lambda_{0}
satisfies the equation
(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\mathbf{I})\,\mathbf{S}_{0}=\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}
.
Hence, for any vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
we obtain
\mathbf{S}_{0}\mathbf{v}
by solving the linear equation system (LES)

(4.27)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\,\mathbf{I})\,\mathbf{x}\,=\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{v}.

Note that the matrix
\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\,\mathbf{I}
is singular,
but the right-hand side
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\,\mathbf{v}
is always orthogonal to
\ker\,\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\,\mathbf{I}=\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
.
Hence, the LES (
4.27
) is still uniquely solvable on
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}
.

Practically,
we can add a regularization term
\alpha\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
with
\alpha>0
to the matrix on the left-hand side of (
4.27
)
and then apply a standard (matrix-free) solver on the modified regular LES

(4.28)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\,\mathbf{I}+\alpha\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0})\,\mathbf{x}\,=\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\mathbf{v},

such as
MinRes
,
GMRES
, or even
CG
if
\lambda_{0}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
.

By multiplying
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
to both sides of (
4.28
),
we deduce that the solution of (
4.28
) is orthogonal to
\mathrm{ran}\,\mathbf{P}_{\!0}
.
Thus, it is the desired norm-minimizing solution of (
4.27
).
Also note that the action of
\mathbf{P}_{\!0}^{\perp}\,\mathbf{v}
on the right-hand side is computed as in
Remark

4.13
.

In addition,
we also note
that for each multi-index
\bm{\beta}
with
|\bm{\beta}|\leq n
,
we can (and should) loop only once over all possible splittings
\bm{\nu}_{1}+\bm{\nu}_{2}=\bm{\beta}
with
\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2}\neq\mathbf{0}
to compute
\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(\bm{\beta})}
.
Besides,
the term
\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})}
defined in (
4.22
)
appears in both (
4.23
) and (
4.24
)
for each
\bm{\beta}
,
so we should store it and reuse it for the calculation of
\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\beta})}
.
Also note that
\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\beta})}
is only needed for
|\bm{\beta}|<n
.
To increase stability,
we can orthonormalize existing outputs after the computation of
\{\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}:|\bm{\beta}|=j\}
.

All in all, the implementation procedure of the Taylor-RBM is summarized in
Algorithm

1
.

0:

A real-analytic Hermitian matrix function
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
with series expansion at
\bm{\mu}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as in (
3.1
),
an isolated eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
of
\mathbf{A}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
of multiplicity
m_{0}
,
a matrix
\mathbf{U}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m_{0}}
whose columns form an ONB of the eigenspace
\ker(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\mathbf{I})
,
and a target approximation order
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

0:

A matrix
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times r_{\!n}}
of orthonormal columns spanning the Taylor-RB space
\mathcal{V}_{\!n}(\bm{\mu}_{0})
w.r.t.
\lambda_{0}
in the sense of
Definition

4.1
.

1:

Initialize
\mathbf{V}\leftarrow\mathbf{U}_{0}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(\mathbf{0})}\leftarrow\lambda_{0}\mathbf{I}_{m_{0}}
.

2:

for

j=0

to

n

do

3:

for all

\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
|\bm{\beta}|=j

do

4:

Compute
\mathbf{X}^{(\bm{\beta})}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m_{0}}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(\bm{\beta})}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m_{0}}
by (
4.22
).

5:

Compute
\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m_{0}}
by (
4.23
).

6:

Compute
\mathbf{B}^{(\bm{\beta})}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{0}\times m_{0}}
by (
4.24
) if
|\bm{\beta}|<n
.

7:

end

for

8:

Update
\mathbf{V}\leftarrow\mathrm{orthonormalize}\{\mathbf{V},\mathbf{K}^{(\bm{\beta})}:\,|\bm{\beta}|=j\}
.

9:

end

for

10:

return

\mathbf{V}
.

Efficient assembly of the Taylor-RB space w.r.t. an isolated eigenvalue of a parameter-dependent Hermitian matrix function.

5.
Numerical Experiments

This section aims to illustrate the performance of the Taylor-RBM.
Specifically, we will focus on
(i) studying the dimension growth of the Taylor-RB space in the derivative order,
(ii) validating the theoretical error estimates of the Taylor-RBM, and
(iii) comparing the approximations from Taylor-RBM with that from PT.

The numerical experiments are conducted on
Python
.
The code and the experiment data is available on GitHub (
https://github.com/zhuoyaozeng/taylor_rbm
).

Throughout the experiments,
full EVPs are solved with the
scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh
function,
and the reduced EVPs are solved with the
scipy.linalg.eigh
function,
both using machine precision as error tolerance.
The threshold for identifying eigenvalue clusters is
10^{-10}
.

5.1.
Model problem: xxz-chain quantum spin systems

For
L,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
j\leq L
and for any matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
,
we write

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{(L,j)}\vcentcolon=\mathbf{I}_{2^{j-1}}\otimes\mathbf{S}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{2^{L-j}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{L}\times 2^{L}},

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product of matrices.

We will test our method on the

xxz-chain model with open boundary conditions
,
which is in the affine decomposition form (
4.26
) and defined for
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{\!\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

(5.1)

\mathbf{A}(\bm{\mu})=\mathbf{A}_{1}+\mu_{1}\mathbf{A}_{2}-\mu_{2}\mathbf{A}_{3}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{L}\times 2^{L}},

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{1}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\!\Big(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{x}}^{(L,j)}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{x}}^{(L,j+1)}+\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{y}}^{(L,j)}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{y}}^{(L,j+1)}\Big),\quad\mathbf{A}_{2}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{z}}^{(L,j)}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{z}}^{(L,j+1)},\quad\mathbf{A}_{3}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{z}}^{(L,j)},\quad

with
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{x}},\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{y}},\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{z}}
denoting the
spin-1/2 matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{x}}\vcentcolon=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right],\qquad\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{y}}\vcentcolon=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&0\end{array}\right],\qquad\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{z}}\vcentcolon=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right].

This is a classical model in quantum many-body physics, which has been extensively studied in the physics literature
[
29
,
51
]
.
It exhibits diverse spectral behaviors at different parameter values,
providing good test cases for studying the Taylor-RBM.

The subspace dimension grows at the same rate as the theoretical upper bound,
implying that the ranges of the Taylor-derivatives are almost entirely linearly independent.
This suggests a complex local structure of the spectral projector,
where parameter variations explore a high-dimensional subspace in the Hilbert space without redundancy.

•

Moderate growth at
\bm{\mu}_{0}=(0,0)^{\!\top}
.

Although this point also possesses degenerate eigenvalues,
the dimension growth of the Taylor-RB space is much slower compared to the maximal growth seen at
(-1,0)^{\!\top}
.
This implies that a significant portion of vectors from ranges of the higher-order derivatives are linearly dependent,
allowing for a more compact representation.

•

Rapid growth at
\bm{\mu}_{0}=(1,1)^{\!\top}
despite a non-degenerate eigenvector.

At this point, the eigenvectors are simple (
m_{1}=m_{2}=1
),
yet the dimension of the Taylor-RB space grows rapidly and stays close to the theoretical limit.
This indicates that while the eigenstate is non-degenerate,
it is highly sensitive to the parameters
\bm{\mu}
.

•

Parameter invariance at
\bm{\mu}_{0}=(-1,1)^{\!\top}
.

For the smallest eigenvalue (
K=1
),
the dimension remains strictly constant (
r=1
).
This indicates that the corresponding eigenspace is locally invariant around
\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
In other words, the higher-order Taylor-derivatives vanish or their ranges reside in the span of the initial eigenvector,
making the expansion trivial.

We conclude that the comparison between the dimension of the Taylor-RB space and the combinatorial upper bound serves as a numerical measure of the local sensitivity or complexity of the total spectral projector.

•

The Taylor-RBM approximation error closely tracks the projection error,

Since the projection error is by definition the best approximation within the span of the ranges of the Taylor-derivatives,

as dictated by the variational principle.
All in all,
the Taylor-RBM is theoretically much less expensive to assemble for large-scale systems
and empirically tends to provide superior approximation quality both within and slightly beyond the analytical convergence radius,
so it is mathematically and computationally preferred over classical PT for eigenspace approximations.

6.
Conclusion and outlook

Taylor-reduced basis method (Taylor-RBM) for multivariate parametric eigenvalue problems (pEVPs) is a local model order reduction technique,
which constructs a subspace using the ranges of the Taylor series coefficients of the eigenprojector w.r.t.​ the reference parameter.
It is an intersection between the Rayleigh-Ritz method for spectral approximations,
the classical multivariate analytic perturbation theory,
and the reduced basis method.

In this work, we have firstly revisited subspace methods for spectral approximations in
Section

2
,
where we have in particular emphasized
Theorem

2.4
,
which relates the eigenspace approximation error by eigenspace projection error,
as well as
Lemma

2.5
,
which concisely shows the squaring effect of the Ritz-value sum approximation error w.r.t. the eigenspace approximation error.

Then in
Section

3
,
we have revisited the multivariate analytic perturbation theory and provided explicit formulae for directly or recursively computing the Taylor coefficients of the eigenprojector.
Besides, we have conducted a detailed analysis of the spectral approximation using the truncated power series of the eigenprojector in
Section

3.4
.

With all preparations, we have defined and analyzed the convergence properties of the Taylor-RBM for pEVPs in
Section

4
.
More importantly,
we have discussed and derived the computationally efficient algorithm for assembling the Taylor reduced basis space in
Section

4.2
.

Finally, we have presented some numerical experiments in
Section

5
using the xxz-chain model
to illustrate the Taylor-RBM and to compare it with the spectral approximation using the truncated power series of the eigenprojector.
Our numerical results have shown
that the Taylor-RBM can achieve a much better approximation than the truncated power series of the eigenprojector,
even at parameter regime beyond theoretical convergene guarantee.

Because of the observations in the numerical experiments,
we believe that the Taylor-RBM can be used to accelerate the assembly of an RB space for the spectral approximation of pEVPs over a large parameter domain,
or to generate a more expressive and more compact reduced basis.
This naturally hints a direction for future studies.
