Circula-based multivariate distributions on the flat torus, with applications in structural biology
Guillaume Carrière, Alix Lhéritier, Frédéric Cazals

Abstract
Modeling dependencies between random variables independently from their marginals is fundamental in applications ranging from finance to (structural) biology. In this work, we undertake this problem using circula to model data living on the d d -dimensional flat torus 𝕋 d \mathbb{T}^{d} , making two contributions. First, using a low rank covariance structure to define circulae based on a latent variable model, we design the first closed-form normalized distribution on the flat torus 𝕋 d \mathbb{T}^{d} –with covariance structure. Second, building on this framework, we propose the first models for joint distributions of torsion angles (backbone and side-chains) for neighboring amino-acids in proteins. In practice, we fit mixtures on flat torii from 𝕋 2 \mathbb{T}^{2} to 𝕋 14 \mathbb{T}^{14} , and show they are SOTA in terms of likelihood and sparsity. We anticipate that these models will prove fundamental to move from discrete structural studies like in AlphaFold2 , to thermodynamics and kinetics, which are the ultimate goals in theoretical biophysics.



1
Introduction

1.1
Previous work

Copulae and circulae.

Copulae are functions that allow us to model the dependence
structure between random variables independently of their marginal
distributions
[
1
]
. This makes them
especially useful for settings where complex dependencies matter,
e.g.
in
finance and risk management, hydrology, or (structural) biology. A
central result is Sklar’s theorem
[
2
]
, which
states that any multivariate joint distribution can be written as the
product of the marginals and a copula fully capturing their
dependence.
In practice, fitting copulae typically involves a two-step approach:
first estimating the marginal distributions, then selecting and
calibrating a copula family (e.g., Gaussian, t, Archimedean) using
methods such as maximum likelihood or inference functions for margins
(IFM)
[
3
]
. For elliptical copulae–which generalize multivariate normal distributions, an estimate of
the covariance or correlation matrix plays a central role, as it
directly parameterizes the dependence structure. In such cases, one
often fits the copula by estimating this matrix from
observations. When data are high-dimensional or noisy,
low-rank approximations of the covariance matrix can be used to
stabilize estimation, sometimes followed by a projection onto the
nearest valid correlation matrix using the Higham correction to ensure
positive semi-definiteness
[
4
]
.
Extensions like vine copulae (pair-copula
constructions)
[
5
]
allow flexible high-dimensional
modeling by decomposing complex dependence structures into cascades of
bivariate copulae.

Circulae are a class of copulae designed to capture dependence
structures with circular or periodic features arising in
directional data
[
6
]
. Circulae are
especially relevant to handle periodic data found meteorology,
oceanography, finance, or (structural) biology. Construction of
circulae typically involves adapting copula definitions to circular
domains or using transformations of linear copulae onto the unit
circle
[
7
]
.
In this work, we are concerned with
circulae defined on the
d
-dimensional flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Applications in structural biology.

Polypeptide chains, which are polymers of amino acids, are the
building blocks of proteins. There are 20 natural amino acids (a.a.) sharing the
same backbone (which involves the three atoms
N-C_{\alpha}-C
), and
differing by their so-called side-chains.
The intrinsic geometry of a protein is modeled by its internal
coordinates, namely bond lengths, valence angles, and torsion angles.
Of fundamental importance are the two torsion angles
(\phi,\psi)

found before and after the
C_{\alpha}
carbon, and those of the
side-chains – denoted
\chi_{i}
s. The angles
(\phi_{i},\psi_{i})

indeed define the Ramachandran diagram which encodes secondary
structures
[
8
]
, while the
\chi

angles define the so-called rotational isomers of side-chains, namely
the
preferred
low energy side-chain
conformations
[
9
,
10
]
.
These angles have been used for decades in structural
analysis
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
,
structure prediction
[
14
]
, protein
design
[
15
]
, generative
models
[
16
]
, loop closure
reconstructions
[
17
,
18
]
, etc.

More recently, torsion angles of individual a.a. played a pivotal role
in particular in
AlphaFold2

[
14
]
, the protein
structure prediction program for which the 2024 Nobel prize in
chemistry was co-awarded.
Indeed, the geometry inference in the structure module in
AlphaFold2
starts by pulling a tuple of torsion angles
\phi,\psi,\chi_{i}
s for
each a.a. from a high dimensional latent space. These tuples define the
individual a.a. geometries, and are iteratively adjusted by a
message passing
like algorithm also resorting to
invariant
point attention

[
14
]
.

2
Background for multivariate models on the flat torus

2.1
Copula models

We work on the
d
-dimensional flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
A random vector is denoted
\mathbb{T}^{d}:\Theta=({\Theta}_{1},\dots,{\Theta}_{d})
,
and a realization of this vector
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})
.
A dataset of
n
observations is written
{\bm{\theta}}^{(n)}=\{{\bm{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\}
.
Finally, we access coordinates as
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}=({\bm{\theta}}_{ij})
.
A copula
c(\cdot)
is a multivariate distribution whose marginals are uniform on
the interval
[0,1]
. Copulae are used to describe the dependence
structure of a multivariate distribution independently of its
marginals. Indeed, Sklar’s theorem
[
2
]
states that any multivariate
distribution
h
can be decomposed in terms of its marginals
f_{i}
and
a copula
c
modeling the coupling, namely

\displaystyle h(x_{1},...,x_{d})=f_{1}(x_{1})\cdot...\cdot f_{d}(x_{d})\cdot c(F_{1}(x_{1}),...,F_{d}(x_{d})),

with
f_{i}
(resp.
F_{i}
) are the marginal densities (resp. cumulative
distribution functions) and
c
is the copula density. Note that the
copula is applied to the observed vector
(F_{1}(x_{1}),...,F_{d}(x_{d}))
,
whose marginals are uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
as a result of
the probability integral transform
[
21
]
.

2.2
Circula models

Circulae.

Circulae are a circular analogue of copulae, namely distributions whose marginals
are uniform on
[0,2\pi)
.
We focus on a circula construction based on the latent variable
model from
[
7
]
(Fig.
2
), in
which each RV of interest is conditioned to a uniform random variable

\phi
on
[0,2\pi)
.
Formally, each uniform marginal of the circula is defined as
\Theta_{i}=(\Omega_{i}+q_{i}\phi)(\bmod 2\pi)
where
\phi
is circular
uniformly distributed,
\Omega_{i}
is a RV with density
g_{i}
, and
q_{i}=\pm 1
gives the sign of the dependency on
\phi
. It follows that

\Theta_{i}
is a uniform variable obtained by shifting
\phi
by an
angle
\omega_{i}\sim g_{i}
, in the following we refer to
g_{i}
as
binding densities
.
The complete-data joint density of the circula
c(\cdot)
is the product of the density on
\phi
and conditional densities on
\theta_{i}
given
\phi
:

c(\phi,\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\prod_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}(\theta_{i}-q_{i}\phi).

(1)

The circula is then obtained by integrating out the latent variable
\phi
:

c(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}(\theta_{i}-q_{i}\phi)d\phi.

(2)

We assume the binding densities
g_{i}
are centered with mean resultant length
\rho_{i}
.
(NB: the length of the
mean resultant vector
acts as concentration
parameter
[
6
]
). We also assume they are
symmetric about 0 – see Rmk
5
.
The mean resultant length of the
(k,l)
th marginal copula
density, which acts as dependence parameter for variables
(\theta_{k},\theta_{l})
, satisfies
[
7
]
:

\rho_{kl}=\rho_{k}\rho_{l}.

(3)

Circula-based multivariate distributions (CBMDs)

.
We focus on CBMDs, namely distributions of the form

h(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d})=(2\pi)^{d}\cdot f_{1}(\theta_{1})\cdot...\cdot f_{d}(\theta_{d})\cdot c(2\pi F_{1}(\theta_{1}),...,2\pi F_{D}(\theta_{d})),

(4)

with
f_{i}(\cdot)
(resp.
F_{i}(\cdot)
) are the marginal densities
(resp. marginal cumulative distribution functions), and
c(\cdot)
is
a circula.
Circulas’ uniform marginals on
[0,2\pi)
require the normalization term
(2\pi)^{d}
.

The CBMD construction can be used to generate various distributions by
selecting different distributions as marginals/circula. In the
following, we use the notation f-c to denote instances of CBMDs in
which f specifies the marginal distributions and c specifies the
circula.

[
7
]
.
Arrows
represent modeled correlations.

\rho
is the length of the mean resultant vector, which acts as a
concentration parameter
[
6
]
.
The product
\rho_{k}\rho_{l}
is the dependence value for the pair
(k,l)
.

3
Circula-based multivariate distributions: generic model

We first analyze the dependency structure of circula-based multivariate
distributions, and proceed with practical methods for sampling and
parameter estimation of CBMDs.

3.1
Dependency structure analysis

With
\mu_{i}
the circular mean of
\theta_{i}
, we characterize the dependency structure in CBMDs (Eq.
4
) using the so-called Jammalamadaka and
Sarma (JS) correlation coefficient
[
22
, Sec. 8.2]
:

\rho_{JS}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\sin(\theta_{1}-\mu_{1})\sin(\theta_{2}-\mu_{2})]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\sin^{2}(\theta_{1}-\mu_{1})]\mathbb{E}[\sin^{2}(\theta_{1}-\mu_{1})]}}

(5)

Since
E[sin(\theta-\mu)]=0
, the JS correlation corresponds to the
pearson correlation applied to variables
Z_{i}=\sin(\theta_{i}-\mu_{i})
.
In a multivariate setting one may
consequently be interested in the JS correlation matrix, that is the
matrix
R_{JS}
with typical element
[R_{JS}]_{i,j}=\rho_{JS}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})
.

Recall that the dependence parameter for the pair
(k,l)
is
the sign-less number
\rho_{kl}=\rho_{k}\rho_{l}
, with
\rho_{kl}\in[0,1]
– Eq.
3
.
Therefore, taking into account the sign of correlations as encoded by
the
q_{i}=\pm 1
values, the JS correlation matrix
R_{c}(\{q_{i},\rho_{i}\})
of populations of
such a circula reads as

R_{c}(\{q_{i},\rho_{i}\})=\begin{bmatrix}1&q_{1}\rho_{1}q_{2}\rho_{2}&\cdots&q_{1}\rho_{1}q_{d}\rho_{d}\\
q_{2}\rho_{2}q_{1}\rho_{1}&1&\cdots&q_{2}\rho_{2}q_{d}\rho_{d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
q_{d}\rho_{d}q_{1}\rho_{1}&q_{d}\rho_{d}q_{2}\rho_{2}&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}

(6)

Let
w
be a vector with element
w_{i}=q_{i}\rho_{i}
with
w_{i}\in[-1,1]
,
R_{c}
can also be
expressed as

R_{c}(\{q_{i},\rho_{i}\})=G(w):=w{w}^{\mathsf{T}}-\text{Diag}(w{w}^{\mathsf{T}})+{{\bf I}_{d}}.

(7)

The previous equation shows that
R_{c}
exhibits a
rank-one
off-diagonal
structure, namely a rank-one matrix with its diagonal
replaced by ones. This stems from the construction of the multivariate
circula as a latent variable model: the dependency structure is
determined by a single explanatory factor (the latent variable), with
only
d
degrees of freedom.

3.2
Marginal and conditional distributions

A useful property of CBMDs, arising from their construction as a
one-factor latent variable model, is that they are closed under
marginalization. That is, the marginal distribution of a CBMD over a
subset of marginal variables is itself a CBMD.
One has:

Theorem. 1

Let
h(\theta)
be a CBMD. and
u_{i}=2\pi F_{i}(\theta_{i})
. The
marginal distribution of
h(\theta)
with respect to the subset of
marginal variables
\theta_{A}
satisfies:

h(\theta_{A})=(2\pi)^{|\theta_{A}|-1}\prod_{i\in A}f_{i}(\theta_{i})\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{i\in A}g_{i}(u_{i}-q_{i}\phi)d\phi.

(8)

Let
\theta_{A}
(resp.
\theta_{B}
) denote conditioned
(resp. conditioning) variables, then the conditional distribution

h(\theta_{A}|\theta_{B})
is simply expressed as

h(\theta_{A}|\theta_{B})=\frac{h(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})}{h(\theta_{B})}=(2\pi)^{|\theta_{A}|}\prod_{i\in A}f_{i}(\theta_{i})\frac{c(u_{A},u_{B})}{c(u_{B})}

(9)

with
c(u_{B})
the marginal distribution of the circula
c(\cdot)
over variables
u_{B}
:

c(u_{B})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{i\in B}g_{i}(u_{i}-q_{i}\phi)d\phi

(10)

3.3
Sampling

The simplest method for generating values from a CBMD is to first sample the latent variable, and
then sample the remaining variables conditioned by the latent
variable, that is:
(i) Sample
\phi\sim U[0,2\pi)
;
(ii) For each
i=1,...,d
, sample
\omega_{i}\sim g_{i}
and compute
u_{i}=(\omega_{i}+q_{i}\phi)(\bmod 2\pi)
;
(iii) For each
i=1,...,d
, compute
\theta_{i}=F_{i}^{-1}(u_{i}/2\pi)
.
Sampling from the binding densities
g_{i}
and evaluating the
quantile functions
F_{i}^{-1}
depends on their analytical form.
See Sec.
S1.1

and Sec.
S1.2
for the von Mises and
wrapped Cauchy distributions.

3.4
Parameter estimation

CBMDs requires estimating two types of parameters: the
location and concentration parameters of the marginal and binding densities, and the
coupling indicator variables
q_{i}
in Eq.
2
.
For a dataset of
n
observations
{\bm{\theta}}^{(n)}
, the log likelihood of density (
4
) is equal to :

\displaystyle\log(h({\bm{\theta}}^{(n)})=nd\log(2\pi)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(f_{1}({\bm{\theta}}_{i1}))+\dots+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(f_{d}({\bm{\theta}}_{id}))+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(c(2\pi F_{1}({\bm{\theta}}_{i1}),...,2\pi F_{d}({\bm{\theta}}_{id})))

There is generally no closed form solution for the maximum likelihood
estimator (MLE) because the marginal summations are coupled to the circula
summation in terms of parameters
[
7
, Section 4.1]
.
Therefore, numerical methods such as L-BFGS-B are used to estimate
continuous parameters.
In contrast,
q_{i}=\pm 1
yields
2^{d}
possibilities for the
coupling indicators, whence
2^{d}
runs of L-BFGS-B.
This prohibitive complexity for large values of
d
is dealt with
in Alg.
1
using approximation techniques.

IFM method for continuous parameters inference.

Originating with the fit of bivariate von Mises
MLE
[
23
]
, the estimation of continuous
parameters can be be simplified by estimating the marginal parameters
independently, and then the circula parameters conditional on the
marginal parameter estimates.
This method is known as the Inference Function for Margins
(IFM)
[
3
]
for multivariate models on which
marginals are specified. IFM does not yield an exact
MLE but provides accurate estimates in practice.
Moreover, estimation of the individual marginal parameters
can often be performed using specialized efficient methods – for
example, von Mises parameters can be estimated as described in
appendix
S1.3
– rather than generic numerical methods.

1-factor approximation for discrete parameters inference.

To avoid estimating the aforementioned
2^{d}
models, we infer the best
dependency structure directly from the data using the sample
estimate of the correlation matrix
\hat{R}
(Eq.
6
).
Note that the one factor structure (Eq.
7
)
of correlation matrices
R_{c}
of CBMDs prevents representing all correlation matrices.
The classical one-factor approximation is usually undertaken using the
so-called Higham correction
[
24
,
4
,
25
]
.
We revisit this problem and provide a constructive algebraic
characterization for the rank-one case:

Theorem. 2

The best one factor approximation for the Frobenius norm of a
d\times d

correlation matrix can be found by solving an algebraic system
involving
d+1
equations of degree five in
d+1
variables.

Practically, this algebraic system can be solved
using tools from real algebraic geometry–
e.g.
Gröbner basis
and the real univariate representation.
In practice and as suggested by our analysis which exhibits a
functional to be optimized involving a Rayleigh-Ritz ratio, we simply
re-scale the top eigenvector of the
\hat{R}
(or
\hat{R}-{{\bf I}}
),
and clip its entries to
[-1,1]
.
See Sec.
S3
, Alg.
1
, Sec.
S4.1
.
This 1-factor approximation yields estimates for the discrete sign
parameters
q
, and provides initial estimates for correlation
magnitude parameters
\rho_{\text{init}}
, which are then refined numerically.

Circula-based multivariate distributions: instantiations

4.1
von Mises circula

The von Mises circula distribution is expressed as the
circula from Eq.
2
with centered von
Mises distributions (
\mu=0
) as binding densities
g_{i}
, namely:

\displaystyle c(u_{1},...,u_{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{vm}_{i}(\theta_{i}-q_{i}\phi)d\phi

The free parameters of this distribution are the concentrations
parameters
\kappa_{i}\in(0,\infty)
of the von Mises binding
densities, and the discrete indicators
q_{i}=\pm 1
for the sign
of
correlations with the latent variable. Let
I_{0}(\cdot)
denote
the modified Bessel function of the first kind and order
0
[
26
]
. Plugging the expression of

\mathrm{vm}(\cdot)
from Eq.
15
into the above formula
yields:

c(u_{1},...,u_{d})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\exp\{\kappa_{i}\cos(u_{i}-q_{i}\phi)\}}{I_{0}(\kappa_{i})}d\phi

(11)

Theorem. 3

The von Mises circula density of Eq.
11
admits the following closed form expression:

c(u_{1},...,u_{d})=\frac{I_{0}(R)}{(2\pi)^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{d}I_{0}(\kappa_{i})},\text{ with }R=\sqrt{(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}\cos(\theta_{i}))^{2}+(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}q_{i}\sin(\theta_{i}))^{2}}

(12)

4.2
Wrapped Cauchy circula

The wrapped Cauchy circula distribution is expressed as the circula from Eq.
2
with centered wrapped
Cauchy distributions (
\mu=0
) as binding densities
g_{i}
. This is
equal to

\displaystyle c(u_{1},...,u_{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{wc}_{i}(\theta_{i}-q_{i}\phi)d\phi.

The free parameters of this model are concentration parameters
\rho_{i}\in(0,1)
of the wrapped Cauchy binding densities, and again the
discrete indicators variables
q_{i}=\pm 1
. Plugging the expression of
\mathrm{wc}(\cdot)
from Eq.
18
into the above formula yields

c(u_{1},...,u_{d})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1-\rho_{i}^{2}}{1+\rho_{i}^{2}-2\rho_{i}\cos(u_{i}-q_{i}\phi)}d\phi

(13)

Theorem. 4

Under the condition
\rho_{k}\neq\rho_{l},(k\neq l)
, the wrapped Cauchy circula density of Eq.
13
admits the following closed form expression

c(u_{1},...,u_{d})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\rho_{j}e^{iq_{j}u_{j}})^{d-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{d}\frac{1-\rho_{k}^{2}}{(\rho_{j}e^{iq_{j}u_{j}}-\rho_{k}e^{iq_{k}u_{k}})(1-\rho_{j}\rho_{k}e^{i(q_{j}u_{j}-q_{k}u_{k})})}

(14)

4.3
Instantiations

The circula framework decouples the marginals and the circula itself.
We provide two instantiations of Eq. (
2
), namely the von Mises circula and the
wrapped Cauchy circula.
Using von Mises and wrapped Cauchy distributions as marginals yields the following four instantiations:

•

CBMDs with von Mises marginals:
vM-wC
and
vM-vM
;

•

CBMDs with wrapped Cauchy marginals:
wC-wC
and
wC-vM
.

A comparison of these instantiations is provided in Section
S5.2
.
In the sequel, we focus on
vM-wC
and
vM-vM
which use von
Mises marginals, due to their Gaussian-like behavior on the circle.

5
Mixture modeling with circulae

Mixture.

Given a component distribution
f_{k}
the density of a mixture model is expressed as

\displaystyle p(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}f_{k}(x),\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}=1.

Estimating a mixture model requires estimating the parameters of each
component, as well as the component weights
w_{k}
, a task usually
undertaken using the Expectation-Maximization (EM)
algorithm
[
27
]
.
Inference of CMMs via the EM algorithm is enabled by the
tractable likelihood maximization of each CBMD – Sec.
3.4
.

Fitting.

As detailed in Sec.
S4.2
, fitting a mixture consists of two steps:
(i) Initialization using
k-means++
on the flat torus;
(ii) Unsupervised mixture learning using the MML-EM algorithm
[
28
]
.
We note in passing that the learning method from

[
28
]
combines EM and the
Minimum
Message Length
(MML), instead of BIC or AIC to promote
sparsity. MML provides the
length of the shortest code (message) needed to describe both the model and the data, and
has the units of
bits
– see also Rmk
4
.
This is important in our mixture fitting setting for several
reasons:
(i) MML does not rely on parameter identifiability (permutating the
labels of components), as opposed to BIC and AIC; (ii) MML uses an
adaptive penalty, which is important to penalize components poorly
supported by data; and (iii) MML accounts for the precision with
which parameters are stated (instead of treating them as point
estimates as in BIC/AIC).

7
Discussion and outlook

Our work introduces a new type of normalized multivariate
distributions with covariance structure on the flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
This unlocks mixture modeling with
vM-wC
/
vM-vM
components, which is
not practical using classical toroidal distributions,
as most of them are not normalized. To the best of our knowledge, the
only exception is the multivariate wrapped Normal distribution, used
in mixture modeling in
[
31
]
. However, von Mises and
wrapped Cauchy distributions are generally preferred to wrapped Normal
distribution, as they are more tractable and use a more practical
cosine based measure of distance, instead of the geodesic distance.
An appealing further extension would be expand the circula model,
using multiple latent variables and/or Vine copulae.

Application-wise, we focus on the problem of modeling joint
distributions of torsion angles in proteins (backbone+side chains)
which live in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
to
\mathbb{T}^{14}
.
On the one hand and as recalled in Introduction, these angles are
ubiquitous in structural modeling. We show that our mixture models
are SOTA in two respect: they improve on previous models for single
amino acids, and for the first time, they enable modeling joint
distributions for pairs of amino acids.
On the other hand, these angles play a pivotal role in the recent Nobel prize winning
program
AlphaFold2
, which solely focuses on structure prediction, via a hybrid message passing
like architecture operating in latent and physical space.
Also combined with message passing like algorithms,
we anticipate that our mixtures will play a fundamental role to make a stride
towards the efficient prediction of thermodynamics and kinetics, the overarching goals
in theoretical biophysics.

S1
Background

S1.1
von Mises distribution

The
von Mises distribution

\mathrm{vm}(\mu,\kappa)
(sometimes referred to as the
Circular Normal distribution
) is the most commonly used analogue of the normal distribution on the unit circle. The von Mises distribution density function is

\mathrm{vm}(\theta;\mu,\kappa)=\frac{1}{2\pi I_{0}(\kappa)}\exp\left\{\kappa\cos(\theta-\mu)\right\}

(15)

where
\mu
is the mean parameter,
\kappa
is the concentration parameter and
I_{0}(\cdot)
stands for the modified Bessel function
of the first kind and order 0
[
26
]
.
The mean resultant length
\rho
of the von Mises distribution is then given by
\rho=A(\kappa)
where

A(\kappa)=I_{1}(\kappa)/I_{0}(\kappa)

(16)

As it is defined on the circle, the cumulative distribution function of a von Mises distribution can only be expressed analytically by integrating the pdf between
\theta
and a lower limit
\theta_{0}
, corresponding to our "frame of reference" on the circle.
As such, the cdf of a univariate von Mises marginal distribution is defined by
[
22
]
[2.2.4]:

\displaystyle\mathrm{VM}(\theta)=\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\theta}\mathrm{vm}(t)dt

with

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{vm}(t)dt=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\theta+\frac{2}{I_{0}(\kappa)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}I_{j}(\kappa)\frac{\sin[j(\theta-\mu)]}{j}\right),

where
I_{j}(\cdot)
is modified bessel function of the first king and order
j
.

Sampling

The von Mises distribution can be sampled using a standard algorithm by Best & Fisher
[
32
]
, summarized in
[
6
, Section 3.5.4]
. This algorithms implements an accept-reject scheme using a wrapped Cauchy distribution as envelope, with achieves a high acceptance ratio for most values of
\kappa
. Due to its simple implementation and good performance, this algorithm remains the dominant approach in for most scientific libraries (numpy, scipy, R package
CircStats
…)

Quantile function evaluation

The von Mises quantile function
\mathrm{VM}^{-1}(q)
does not admit a closed-form expression.
However, it can be evaluated efficiently using standard root-finding schemes – most commonly the Newton-Raphson method, whose iterative updates are of the form:

\displaystyle\theta_{i+1}=\theta_{i}-\frac{\mathrm{VM}(\theta_{i})-q}{\mathrm{vm}(\theta_{i})}

S1.2
Wrapped Cauchy distribution

The Cauchy distribution on the real line
C(\mu,\gamma)
has density

C(x;\mu,\gamma)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+(x-\mu)^{2}}

(17)

The corresponding
wrapped Cauchy distribution

\mathrm{wc}(\mu,\rho)
is obtained by wrapping a Cauchy distribution on the circle with the map
x_{w}=x\mod 2\pi
. Through its characteristic function
[
6
]
, it is one of the rare wrapped distributions that yields a closed form expression

\mathrm{wc}(\theta;\mu,\rho)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}C(\theta+2k\pi;\mu,\gamma)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos(\theta-\mu)}

(18)

where
\rho
is the mean resultant length, with
\rho=e^{-\gamma}
.

With the lower limit set at
\theta_{0}=\mu
, the cumulative distribution function is expressed with the following closed form
[
6
, Section 3.5.7]

\displaystyle WC(\theta)=\int_{\mu}^{\theta}\mathrm{wc}(t)dt=\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos\left(\frac{(1+\rho^{2})\cos(\theta-\mu)-2\rho}{1+\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos(\theta-\mu)}\right)

Sampling

Sampling can be performed by generating a linear Cauchy random variable and wrapping it onto the circle. If
u\sim U[0,1]
and as
\gamma=-\log(\rho)
, samples for the wrapped Cauchy distribution are then generated as

\displaystyle\theta=\left(\mu+-\log(\rho)\tan\big(\pi(u-\tfrac{1}{2})\big)\right)\mod 2\pi

Quantile function evaluation

The wrapped Cauchy quantile function is obtained by inverting the CDF, which yields

\displaystyle WC^{-1}(q)=\begin{cases}r(q),&\ 0\leq q\leq\frac{1}{2}\\
2\pi-r(1-q),&\ \frac{1}{2}\leq q\leq 1\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle r(q)=2\arctan\left(\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}\tan(\pi q\right)

S1.3
von Mises distribution MLE

The log likelihood of an individual von Mises marginal is equal to

\displaystyle\log(f(\mathbf{\theta}))

\displaystyle=n\log 2\pi+\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{n}\cos(\theta_{i}-\mu)-n\log I_{0}(\kappa)

\displaystyle=n\{\log 2\pi+\kappa\bar{R}\cos(\bar{\theta}-\mu)-\log I_{0}(\kappa)\}

with
\bar{\theta}
the mean direction and
\bar{R}
the mean resultant length.

\mu
estimator

Since
\cos(x)
is maximal at
x=0
the MLE of
\mu
is
\hat{\mu}=\bar{\theta}
.

\kappa
estimator

Differentiating the log likelihood with respect to
\kappa
results in

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\log(f(\mathbf{\theta}))=n\{\bar{R}\cos(\bar{\theta}-\mu)-\frac{I_{1}(\kappa)}{I_{0}(\kappa)}\}

Substituting
\hat{\mu}=\bar{\theta}
, the MLE of
\kappa
is the solution of

\displaystyle\frac{I_{1}(\kappa)}{I_{0}(\kappa)}-\bar{R}=0

This non linear equation is difficult to solve analytically but a good approximation can be obtained efficiently using the
Sra’s truncated newton approximation
method
[
33
]
, as summarized in
[
30
, Section 2.2]
.

\displaystyle=\int_{\theta_{B}}(2\pi)^{d}c(2\pi F_{1}(\theta_{1}),...,2\pi F_{d}(\theta_{d}))\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}(\theta_{i})\right)d\theta_{B}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{d}\prod_{i\in A}f_{i}(\theta_{i})\int_{\theta_{B}}c(2\pi F_{1}(\theta_{1}),...,2\pi F_{d}(\theta_{d}))\left(\prod_{i\in B}f_{i}(\theta_{i})\right)d\theta_{B}

Let
u_{i}=2\pi F(\theta_{i})
, such that
du_{i}=2\pi f_{i}(\theta_{i})d\theta_{i}
. Plugging the density of the circula from Eq.
2
yields

\displaystyle h(\theta_{A})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{d-|\theta_{B}|}\prod_{i\in A}f_{i}(\theta_{i})\int_{u_{B}}c(u_{1},...,u_{d})du_{B}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{|\theta_{A}|}\prod_{i\in A}f_{i}(\theta_{i})\int_{u_{B}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}(u_{i}-q_{i}\phi)d\phi du_{B}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{|\theta_{A}|-1}\prod_{i\in A}f_{i}(\theta_{i})\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\int_{u_{B}}\prod_{i\in B}g_{i}(u_{i}-q_{i}\phi)du_{B}\right)\prod_{i\in A}g_{i}(u_{i}-q_{i}\phi)d\phi

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{|\theta_{A}|-1}\prod_{i\in A}f_{i}(\theta_{i})\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\prod_{i\in B}\int_{0}^{2\pi}g_{i}(u_{i}-q_{i}\phi)du_{i}\right)\prod_{i\in A}g_{i}(u_{i}-q_{i}\phi)d\phi

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{|\theta_{A}|-1}\prod_{i\in A}f_{i}(\theta_{i})\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{i\in A}g_{i}(u_{i}-q_{i}\phi)d\phi

\Box

S2.2
von Mises circula: closed form expression

Proof.
[Thm.
3
]
Let the binding density
g
be a von Mises parameterized by concentration
\kappa
and mean direction
\mu=0
:

g(\theta)=\frac{\exp\{\kappa\cos(\theta)\}}{2\pi I_{0}(\kappa)}

Then the equation for the circula in integrate form is

\displaystyle c(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\exp\{\kappa_{i}\cos(\theta_{i}-q_{i}\phi)\}}{2\pi I_{0}(\kappa_{i})}d\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}I_{0}(\kappa_{i})}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}\cos(\theta_{i}-q_{i}\phi)\right\}d\phi

Focusing on the term in exponent we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}\cos(\theta_{i}-q_{i}\phi)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}\left(\cos(\theta_{i})\cos(q_{i}\phi)+\sin(\theta_{i})\sin(q_{i}\phi)\right)

Because
q_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
this is equal to

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}\cos(\theta_{i})\right)\cos(\phi)+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}q_{i}\sin(\theta_{i})\right)\sin(\phi)

Now recall the trigonometric identity

\displaystyle a\cos(x)+b\sin(x)=R\cos(x-\alpha)

where
R=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
and
\alpha=\arctan(b/a)

Set
a=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}\cos(\theta_{i})
and
b=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}q_{i}\sin(\theta_{i})
we have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}\cos(\theta_{i})\right)\cos(\phi)+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}q_{i}\sin(\theta_{i})\right)\sin(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\cos(\phi)+b\sin(\phi)=R\cos(\phi-\alpha)

we can now write the integrate form as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\kappa_{i}\cos(\theta_{i}-q_{i}\phi)\right\}d\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\exp\{R\cos(\phi-\alpha)\}d\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi I_{0}(R)

\Box

S2.3
wrapped Cauchy circula: closed form expression

The following proof of Thm.
4
was claimed in

[
7
]
and detailed in
[
34
]

Proof.
[Thm.

]
Let the binding density
g

\rho
and mean direction
\mu=0
:

g(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos\theta}

Then the equation for the circula in integrate form is

c(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1-\rho_{k}^{2}}{1+\rho_{k}^{2}-2\rho_{k}\cos(\theta_{k}-q_{k}\phi)}d\phi

Set
z=e^{i\phi}
and
\eta_{k}=\rho_{k}e^{iq_{k}\theta_{k}}
the integral part becomes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1-\rho_{k}^{2}}{1+\rho_{k}^{2}-2\rho_{k}\cos(\theta_{k}-q_{k}\phi)}d\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1-\rho_{k}^{2}}{1+\rho_{k}^{2}-2\rho_{k}\cos(\phi-q_{k}\theta_{k})}d\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{C}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1-|\eta_{k}|^{2}}{|z-\eta_{k}|^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{iz}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{C}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1-|\eta_{k}|^{2}}{(z-\eta_{k})(\overline{z-\eta_{k}})}\right)\frac{1}{iz}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i}\int_{C}z^{d-1}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1-|\eta_{k}|^{2}}{(z-\eta_{k})(1-\overline{\eta_{k}}z)}dz

where
C=\{z\in\mathbb{C};|z|=1\}
. This integral can be calculated with the residue theorem which states:

\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=2\pi i\sum\textrm{Res}(f,a_{k})

\gamma
is a closed curve on the complex plane,

are points on the interior of
\gamma
which are singularities of

and

is defined as :

The list of points

C
because

. The residue for
\eta_{j}

\displaystyle lim_{z\rightarrow\eta_{j}}(z-\eta_{j})z^{d-1}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1-|\eta_{k}|^{2}}{(z-\eta_{k})(1-\overline{\eta_{k}}z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle lim_{z\rightarrow\eta_{j}}\frac{1-|\eta_{j}|^{2}}{1-\overline{\eta_{j}}z}z^{d-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{d}\frac{1-|\eta_{k}|^{2}}{(z-\eta_{k})(1-\overline{\eta_{k}}z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{j}^{d-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{d}\frac{1-|\eta_{k}|^{2}}{(\eta_{j}-\eta_{k})(1-\overline{\eta_{k}}\eta_{j})}

such that, under the condition
\rho_{j}\neq\rho_{k}\quad(j\neq k)
the integral becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i}\int_{C}z^{d-1}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1-|\eta_{k}|^{2}}{(z-\eta_{k})(1-\overline{\eta_{k}}z)}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}^{d-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{d}\frac{1-|\eta_{k}|^{2}}{(\eta_{j}-\eta_{k})(1-\overline{\eta_{k}}\eta_{j})}

\displaystyle c(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}^{d-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{d}\frac{1-|\eta_{k}|^{2}}{(z-\eta_{k})(1-\overline{\eta_{k}}z)}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\rho_{j}e^{iq_{j}\theta_{j}})^{d-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\

\Box

S3
1-factor approximations for correlations matrices

S3.1
Previous work

Approximating a given covariance matrix is a classical topic in numerical analysis,
and no less than 15 methods are discussed in
[
35
]
.
The approximation is said to have a
k
factor structure
when the off diagonal terms
agree with that of a rank
k
matrix. Properties of such approximation
are studied in detail
[
24
,
4
,
25
]
,
with particular solvers based on
alternating projections

[
4
]
.

In the sequel, we derive a novel (to the best of our knowledge) characterization
of solutions of the 1-factor approximation problem, and use an insight
obtained along the calculation to initialize our circula fitting procedure.

S3.2
Exact 1-factor approximation of the correlation matrix
R

Let
R
be the correlation matrix. We want to approximate it using the following map – Eq.
7
:

G(w)=w{w}^{\mathsf{T}}-\text{Diag}(w{w}^{\mathsf{T}})+{{\bf I}_{d}}.

The following is, to the best of our knowledge, a novel algebraic
characterization of the 1-factor approximation. It will be used in
our circula estimation algorithm – Alg.
1
,
see Section
S4.1
.

Since we aim at providing a one factor approximating the matrix
R_{c}
,
it is tempting to invoke the Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem and using the
eigenvector associated to the leading singular value of
R_{c}
.

However, as the following analysis shows, extra terms appear and
require solving a more elaborate problem, which still admits the
algebraic solution claimed in the theorem.

Proof.
[Thm.
2
]

Step 1: expressing the functional.

We have

R-G(w)=R-{{\bf I}_{d}}-(w{w}^{\mathsf{T}}-\text{Diag}(w{w}^{\mathsf{T}})).

(19)

Define

A=R-{{\bf I}_{d}}.

(20)

We have

\displaystyle f(w)=\left\lVert{R-G(w)}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}(A_{ij}-w_{i}w_{j})^{2}

(21)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}A_{ij}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}w_{i}^{2}w_{j}^{2}-2\sum_{i\neq j}A_{ij}w_{i}w_{j}

(22)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-2{w}^{\mathsf{T}}Aw+\left\lVert{w}\right\rVert^{4}-\sum_{i}w_{i}^{4}

(23)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert{A-w{w}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-\sum_{i}w_{i}^{4}.

(24)

We use polar coordinates to decouple the magnitude and the direction of
w
,
writing
w=rv
with
v\in S^{d-1}
. We get

\displaystyle f(r,v)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-2r^{2}{v}^{\mathsf{T}}Av+r^{4}(1-\sum_{i}v_{i}^{4})

(25)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-2r^{2}\alpha(v)+r^{4}\beta(v),

(26)

with

\alpha(v)={v}^{\mathsf{T}}Av,\beta(v)=(1-\sum_{i}v_{i}^{4}).

(27)

Step 2: minimizing
f(r,v)
.

In seeking critical points of
f(r,v)
, let us start with the variable
r
.
The following is a necessary optimality condition:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}=-4r\alpha(v)+4r^{3}\beta(v)=4r(-\alpha(v)+r^{2}\beta(v))=0,

(28)

whence the conditions

\begin{cases}r&=0\\
r^{2}&=\nicefrac{{\alpha(v)}}{{\beta(v)}}.\end{cases}

(29)

Let us focus on the non trivial solution. Substituting

r^{2}=\nicefrac{{\alpha(v)}}{{\beta(v)}}
into the expression of
Eq.
25
yields

f(r,v)=\left\lVert{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-\frac{\alpha(v)^{2}}{\beta(v)}.

(30)

From which we get that minimizing
f(r,v)
is equivalent to maximizing

\Phi(v)=\frac{\alpha(v)^{2}}{\beta(v)},\left\lVert{v}\right\rVert=1.

(31)

One has
\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}=1
; and since
0\leq v_{i}^{2}\leq 1
, we get
v_{i}^{4}\leq v_{i}^{2}
. Summing
the latter yields
\sum_{i}v_{i}^{4}\leq\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}=1
. Therefore
\beta(v)\geq 0
.
The square
r^{2}
in the second expression of Eq.
29
implies
that
\alpha(v)\geq 0
.

Maximizing
\Phi(v)
is therefore equivalent to maximizing

\Phi_{1}(v)=\frac{\alpha(v)}{\sqrt{\beta(v)}},\left\lVert{v}\right\rVert=1.

(32)

The numerator is a Rayleigh-Ritz ratio, which requires taking a vector
in the eigenspace of the top eigenvalue. This observation motivates the
heuristic used in Algorithm Alg.
1
.

Note however, that the coupling with the
denominator makes the optimization non trivial.

Remark 2

In the subsequent calculations, we use the notation
\mathring{v}^{3}={(v_{1}^{3},\dots,v_{d}^{3})}^{\mathsf{T}}

for the cubic-wise coordinate vector associated with vector
v
.

Step 3: maximizing
\Phi
using Lagrange multipliers.

To solve this problem, we use Lagrange multipliers and introduce

{\mathcal{L}}=\Phi(v)-\lambda({v}^{\mathsf{T}}v-1).

(33)

Writing
\nabla_{v}{\mathcal{L}}=0
yields the following colinearity between the gradient of the objective function
and that of the constraint:

\nabla_{v}\Phi(v)=\nabla_{v}\frac{\alpha(v)^{2}}{\beta(v)}=2\lambda v.

(34)

Let us compute partial derivatives. For
\alpha(v)
, we get:

\alpha(v)={v}^{\mathsf{T}}Av\Rightarrow\nabla_{v}\alpha(v)=2Av.\\

(35)

For
\beta(b)
, we get:

\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}\sum_{j}v_{j}^{4}=4v_{i}^{3}\Rightarrow\nabla_{v}\beta(v)=-4\mathring{v}^{3}

(36)

Differentiating
\Phi(v)
yields

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}\Phi(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha\nabla_{v}\alpha(v)\ \beta(v)-\alpha(v)^{2}\beta(v)}{\beta(v)^{2}}

(37)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\alpha(v)}{\beta(v)^{2}}(\beta(v)Av+\alpha(v)\mathring{v}^{3}).

(38)

Plugging the previous expression into Eq.
34
:

4\alpha(v)\bigl[\beta(v)Av+\alpha(v)\mathring{v}^{3}\bigr]=2\lambda v\beta(v)^{2},

(39)

which expresses the fact that
\beta(v)Av+\alpha(v)\mathring{v}^{3}
is colinear to
v
.
Denoting
\mu
the proportionality coefficient, we get:

\begin{cases}\beta(v)Av+\alpha(v)\mathring{v}^{3}=\mu v\\
\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}=1\end{cases}

(40)

or in fully delineated:

\begin{cases}(1-\sum_{i}v_{i}^{4})Av+({v}^{\mathsf{T}}Av)\mathring{v}^{3}=\mu v\\
\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}=1\end{cases}

(41)

This is a polynomial system of
d+1
equation of degree five in the
d
coordinates
v_{i}
and
\mu
.

\Box

By Bezout’s theorem, the number of solution is bounded by the product
of the degrees of the individual equations, namely
2\times 5^{d}
.

Heuristics

Via Rayleigh-Ritz ratio.

As a heuristic, consider again the definition of the function
\Phi
to be maximized:

\Phi(v)=\frac{({v}^{\mathsf{T}}Av)^{2}}{(1-\sum_{i}v_{i}^{4})},\left\lVert{v}\right\rVert=1.

The numerator is a Rayleigh-Ritz ratio which is maximized for the eigenvector

of matrix
A
– Eq.

.
From Eq.

We finally get

While we have not minimized the denominator, unless

Via direct optimization upon choosing a unit vector.

The previous expression was obtained by maximizing

, given the

which is linear least squares problem in relation to

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}(A_{ij}^{2}-2A_{ij}r^{2}v_{i}v_{j}+r^{4}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2})

whence the final expression

S4.2.2
Unsupervised learning of mixture models

Selecting the correct amount of components when estimating a mixture model cannot rely entirely on maximizing likelihood. Indeed, increasing the number of components will always increase likelihood. However, such models are overly complex and overfit, capturing noise instead of the true underlying structure of the data, and unable to generalize. Instead, the appropriate amount of components balances between maximizing goodness of fit and minimizing model complexity.

In the general case, this trade-off is assessed using a variety of techniques, including the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)
[
42
]
, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)
[
43
]
, the Minimum Description Length (MDL) framework
[
44
]
, cross-validation methods
[
45
]
or likelihood ratio tests
[
46
]
amongst others. In the following we consider the Minimum Message Length (MML) information-theoretic framework
[
47
]
, which quantifies the total encoding cost of both model and data as a two-part message, with length expressed as :

\text{Length}(\theta,X)=\text{Length}(\theta)+\text{Length}(X|\theta)

(47)

For statistical models, this equates to

\text{Length}(\theta,X)=\frac{p}{2}\log q_{p}-\log\left(\frac{u(\theta)}{\sqrt{|{\mathcal{F}}(\theta)|}}\right)-{\mathcal{L}}(X|\theta)+\frac{p}{2}

(48)

with
p=\lvert\theta\rvert
the number of free parameters in the model,
q_{p}
the
p
-dimensional lattice quantization constant,
u(\theta)
the prior on the model’s parameters,
{\mathcal{F}}(\theta)
the Fisher information matrix and
{\mathcal{L}}(X|\theta)
the log-likelihood function.
In mixture models, the Fisher information matrix
F(\theta)
generally does not admit an analytical expression, such that an exact computation of the Message Length is intractable. However, an efficient approximation was proposed in Figueiredo and Jain
[
28
]
by replacing
F(\theta
) with the complete-data Fisher information matrix. Combined with an appropriate handling of empty components (
w_{k}=0
) and a careful choice of parameter prior, this approximation yields the following form :

\text{Length}(\theta,X)=\frac{N}{2}\sum_{k:w_{k}>0}\log(\frac{nw_{k}}{12})+\frac{K_{nz}}{2}\log(\frac{n}{12})+\frac{K_{nz}(N+1)}{2}-{\mathcal{L}}(X|\theta)

(49)

where
K_{nz}
denotes the number of components with non-zero weights,
n=|X|
is the sample size and
N
is the number of free parameters in a single component (
N=3d
for CBMD components from Eq.
4

1
1

1

The correlation sign and intensity vectors can be jointly represented as a continuous vector in
[-1,1]^{d}
. This remains consistent with the Figueiredo and Jain ML approximation, which depends only on the parameter dimension rather than the parameter space
).
Figueiredo and Jain
[
28
]
also introduce an estimation procedure based on EM, designed to minimize
\text{Length}(\theta,X)
over the component parameters
\theta
as well as the number of components
K
within a specified range
[k_{min},k_{max}]
. This is the approach adopted in this work, which we denote as MML-EM in the following.

48
corresponds to
[

\lvert X\rvert d\log(\epsilon)

\epsilon

In practice, it is taken as
0.0873

S5.2
Shape analysis

Modes and critical points.

Circula components and the CMM are real valued functions on the
flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. As such, they obey Morse
theory
[
48
]
. In particular, consider the critical
points
\{c_{i}\}
of such a function. Assuming the function is a Morse
function (isolated critical points and non degenerate Hessian at each
of them), the sum of the indices of critical points is equal to the
Euler characteristic of the flat torus
\chi(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. (The index of
a critical point is the number of negative eigenvalues of the
Hessian.) By the product property
[
49
]
, one has

\chi(\mathbb{T}^{d})=\prod_{i}\chi(S^{1})=0
. This has the following
implications:

•

In 2D: the number of local minima/saddle points/maxima, respectively denoted
a,b,c
,
satisfy
a-b+c=~0
.

•

In 3D: the number of local minima/saddle points of index
1/saddle points of index 2/maxima, respectively denoted
a,b,c,d
,
satisfy
a-b+c-d=0
.

•

This artifact a side effect of the circula construction, and is undesirable in the majority of practical applications. Because the correlation pattern induced by the circula is periodic, it correlates points in the circular uniform space that are distant in the original space. For the
vM-wC
and
