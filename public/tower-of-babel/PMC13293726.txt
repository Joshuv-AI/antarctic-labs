Bleeding without fracture: Unmasking a penile arterial leak as a cause of urethrorrhagia
Authors: Chadi Bourimi, Hicham El Kentaoui, Mohamed Khannous, Pierre-Adrien Leroux, Mathieu Kubiak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIsolated urethrorrhagia during erection is rare and may suggest penile fracture or urethral rupture. We report a 39-year-old man using exogenous testosterone who presented with recurrent urethral bleeding during intercourse, without external trauma or urinary symptoms. Flexible urethroscopy showed a small focal mucosal defect, and dynamic penile MRI demonstrated a 4 mm periurethral hematoma with early arterial extravasation from a perforator branch of the left centro-cavernosal artery, without tunical rupture or pseudoaneurysm. Given clinical stability and spontaneous cessation of bleeding, conservative treatment was chosen, with good functional recovery and complete healing at 3 months.Keywords: Isolated urethrorrhagia, Exogenous testosterone, Dynamic penile MRI, Centrocavernosal artery

Introduction

Urethral bleeding is a symptom that poses a substantial diagnostic challenge for clinicians. It usually arises from identifiable causes such as trauma, infection, iatrogenic injury, or urethral instrumentation, and can significantly impair sexual quality of life. When occurring during erection, it may suggest penile fracture with urethral rupture, among other etiologies.1 Isolated urethrorrhagia during intercourse, without external trauma and concomitant penile fracture, is rare and diagnostically challenging as it lacks the classic “snap,” deformity, or ecchymosis that usually suggest tunical rupture.1

Among the less common mechanisms, focal lesions of the cavernosal arterial system, such as pseudoaneurysms or small arterial breaches, can produce spontaneous or erection-induced hemorrhage via periurethral dissection, in the absence of overt priapism or obvious cavernosal deformation, resulting in an atypical and misleading presentation.2 In such cases, precise anatomical and functional characterization is crucial, and dynamic penile MRI is particularly valuable for confirming tunical integrity, localizing focal arterial leaks, and guiding the choice between conservative observation and selective embolization. The impact of exogenous testosterone administration on vascular fragility remains poorly defined.

We report a case of spontaneous urethrorrhagia during sexual intercourse, defined here as occurring without external trauma or obvious cause, in a testosterone user, secondary to a perforator branch breach of the left centro-cavernosal artery, successfully managed conservatively, underscoring both the diagnostic value of dynamic MRI and the outcomes of a non-interventional approach.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old man with no history of perineal trauma or endourologic instrumentation, but with ongoing self-administration of exogenous testosterone, presented to the emergency department with profuse urethral bleeding that occurred during penile erection in the setting of sexual intercourse. No traumatic event or foreign-body insertion was reported. The hemorrhage spontaneously resolved with detumescence but recurred during a subsequent morning reflex erection, accompanied by pain at the penile base, in the absence of dysuria or other urologic symptoms.

On physical examination the penis was of normal size and appearance, without ecchymosis, hematoma, or deviation during erection; the urethral meatus was intact and non-erythematous. Laboratory findings were unremarkable, including a hemoglobin level of 16 g/dL, a normal coagulation profile, and no elevation of inflammatory markers. The clinical course was marked by persistent discomfort at the penile base and subjective reduction in erectile rigidity.

Flexible urethroscopy revealed a globally erythematous urethral mucosa with a millimetric localized irregularity on the left urethral wall, corresponding to a focal mucosal defect with slightly edematous borders. Fine residual signs of recent bleeding appeared as small punctate reddish spots adjacent to this area, without active bleeding or false passage (Fig. 1). Placement of a transurethral vesical catheter was considered unnecessary in view of the small size of the defect.

Flexible urethroscopy of the penile urethra at first evaluation

Diffuse urethral mucosal erythema with a millimetric left-wall irregularity (arrow) representing a focal mucosal defect with mildly edematous margins. Adjacent punctate reddish spots indicated recent minor bleeding, without active hemorrhage or false passage.

Dynamic penile MRI, performed after intracavernous injection of prostaglandin E1 to induce pharmacologic erection, demonstrated normal morphology and signal characteristics of both corpora cavernosa and corpus spongiosum, with intact tunica albuginea. A focal T1-hypointense periurethral collection measuring 4 mm in diameter, in the mid-penile segment was consistent with a small hematoma. Dynamic contrast-enhanced sequences showed early arterial-phase contrast extravasation precisely localized to this periurethral region, strongly suggestive of a vascular breach involving a perforator branch of the left centrocavernosal artery (Fig. 2), with no arteriovenous fistulae, pseudoaneurysms, or structural abnormalities.

Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI of the penis (arterial phase, T1-weighted post-gadolinium sequences).

A. Axial view showing focal contrast extravasation (arrow) from a centro-cavernosal perforator artery leak into the corpus cavernosum, adjacent to a 4 mm diameter hematoma. B. Sagittal view confirming the self-limited arterial-phase leak (arrowhead) without pseudoaneurysm or tunical rupture.

Given cessation of bleeding episodes during detumescence, absence of hemodynamic instability or general repercussions, endovascular embolization was deemed unnecessary. Initial management included diazepam to maintain detumescence and prevent re-bleeding for 3 weeks, sexual abstinence for 6 weeks, followed by low-dose tadalafil (5 mg daily) to support erectile recovery. At 3-month Follow-up, the patient reported a normal erectile function. Repeat urethroscopy showed a well-calibrated penile urethra and near-complete re-epithelialization with only a small residual mucosal depression, consistent with ongoing cicatrization and no recurrence, stricture formation, or persistent vascular anomaly (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Urethral bleeding occurring during penile erection is an uncommon and heterogeneous clinical entity, encompassing traumatic, inflammatory, iatrogenic, vascular, and idiopathic causes. Although many episodes are readily attributable to obvious external trauma or urethral instrumentation, others arise in the absence of clear external injury and may reflect underlying urethral or corporal pathology, ranging from isolated mucosal injury to more complex vascular lesions involving the corpus spongiosum or cavernosal arteries 1, 2, 3. This spectrum of presentations renders accurate diagnosis and optimal management challenging, as such condition may occur with or without concomitant penile fracture, and that clinical findings can be subtle despite significant underlying pathology. Within this context, our observation can be interpreted in light of the existing literature on isolated urethral and spongiosal injuries and cavernosal arterial lesions.

Reports of isolated spongiosal and urethral lesions without cavernosal fracture remain rare and mostly occur in a context of external trauma. Ogunfowora et al. described an 18-year-old man with coital trauma who presented with penoscrotal swelling and delayed blood-stained urethral discharge, CT and MRI revealed an intact corpora cavernosa, a small bulbar urethral tear with extensive periurethral and scrotal abscess.1 Ettanji et al. reported a 36-year-old man with coital “misstep” who experienced a cracking sound and immediate detumescence but had only a small ventral hematoma of the penis; surgical exploration revealed a 1-cm corpus spongiosum tear and an associated linear urethral lesion with intact corpora cavernosa.3 Similarly, Yokoyama et al. reported an isolated corpus spongiosum rupture with urethral injury identified by pre-operative MRI, in the setting of typical fracture-like symptoms.4 Compared with these cases, our patient presented with abundant urethrorrhagia occurring at full erection during intercourse without obvious external trauma, resembling the unexplained clinical setting of nocturnal urethral bleeding reported by Arya et al..5

From a pathophysiological standpoint, the combination of a normal clinical examination in our patient, intact tunica albuginea on dynamic MRI, and a small periurethral hematoma with early arterial-phase contrast extravasation argue strongly against a classic cavernosal fracture and support a focal arterial mechanism. Several reports describe cavernosal or penile arterial pseudoaneurysm and pudendal artery rupture, in which selective angiography demonstrated a confined arterial leak causing urethral hemorrhage or high-flow priapism,2 Our findings fit within this spectrum but differ in that the lesion was radiologically small with early arterial extravasation on Dynamic MRI, without pseudoaneurysm or persistent high-flow priapism, consistent with a limited breach of a perforator branch of the left cavernosal artery dissecting toward the periurethral tissues. The focal urethral mucosal defect on urethroscopy, together with the periurethral hematoma and preserved corporal integrity, suggests that the arterial leak was channeled through the corpus spongiosum to a locally weakened urethral mucosa rather than through a macroscopic tear of the spongiosum. The history of self-administered exogenous testosterone, in light of reports of spontaneous arterial thrombosis and dissection in young users,6 supports the hypothesis that androgen-induced vascular fragility may have predisposed this patient to a focal penile arterial injury under high intracavernosal pressure during erection.

Other pathophysiological mechanisms must be considered when evaluating isolated urethrorrhagia during erection without obvious trauma. Congenital urethral vascular malformations, sometimes associated with Klippel–Trenaunay syndrome, can bleed under erectile hyperpressure; but typically show angiomatous changes on endoscopy and imaging.7 Limited micro-traumatic tears of the ventral corpus spongiosum without tunical cavernosal rupture have also been described after coital “missteps,” where surgical exploration or MRI demonstrates a focal spongiosal defect with urethral injury.1,4 In our case, dynamic MRI excluded any spongiosal disruption. Inflammatory or polypoid causes such as urethral hemangioma usually present with chronic or recurrent painless bleeding and a visible intraluminal mass, which were not observed.8 A direct effect of exogenous testosterone on urethral mucosal vascularity remains speculative but plausible in light of spontaneous large-artery thrombosis and dissection reported in testosterone users.6

Cross-sectional imaging has proven critical in characterizing atypical penile and urethral injuries. Our MRI findings indicated a focal arterial breach rather than spongiosal or cavernosal rupture. Similar use of cross-sectional imaging to delineate subtle urethral and peri-urethral pathology has been reported in isolated urethral and spongiosal injuries, where CT/MRI identified localized tears and surrounding collections while confirming preservation of the corpora cavernosa.1,4 This information is essential to guide a tailored management strategy—ranging from conservative measures to selective embolization—with the dual objective of controlling bleeding and preserving erectile and voiding function.

For cavernosal arterial lesions, color Doppler ultrasound and angiography have traditionally been used to diagnose pseudoaneurysms or high-flow fistula and to perform super selective embolization.2,9 In the present case, dynamic MRI alone was sufficient to demonstrate a small, well-localized, self-limiting lesion and no expanding pseudoaneurysm, which, together with the favorable clinical evolution, supported a conservative strategy rather than the embolization. Cavernosography may be used when corporal lesions are suspected but is now less frequently performed, particularly in complex penile trauma.10

Urethroscopy and retrograde urethrography (RUG) are key diagnostic tools for evaluating urethral involvement in penile trauma, particularly when urethrorrhagia, or voiding difficulty are present.11 RUG is the gold standard initial imaging to detect extravasation, partial/complete tears, or rare urethral-cavernosal communication, performed before catheterization to avoid exacerbating injury, and is indicated selectively rather than routinely.11 Urethroscopy can be an alternative to RUG by providing direct endoscopic visualization of clots or disruptions, may be used intraoperatively to guide repair.1,12

Most cavernosal or pudendal arterial lesions in the literature have been treated with selective embolization with preserved erectile and urinary function at two months.2,9 In contrast, isolated urethral and spongiosal injuries without major arterial pathology have been managed either surgically or conservatively, employing compression and anti-erectile measures with good functional outcomes.1,5

Therapeutic decision-making in small, self-limited cavernosal arterial leaks following penile trauma favors conservative management over immediate intervention, as demonstrated by our patient's complete resolution confirmed at 3-month cystoscopy with restored erectile function. Initial anti-erectile measures play a key role in management of cavernosal vascular injury—even without full high-flow priapism—by reducing intracavernosal pressure, limiting hematoma progression, and preventing re-bleeding from shear forces during recurrent erections.13 Low-dose daily tadalafil has been used for penile rehabilitation after penile fracture surgical repair to treat or prevent post-traumatic erectile dysfunction: its PDE5 inhibition enhances endothelial nitric oxide signaling, prevents hypoxia-induced fibrosis, maintains cavernosal oxygenation during healing, and supports early erectile recovery.14 In our case, Low-dose tadalafil (5 mg daily) was initiated after one month with good outcomes.

In our case of isolated self-limited cavernosal vascular leak—without priapism, urethral symptoms, or bleeding during detumescence—urethral catheterization was omitted, thereby avoiding risks of blind instrumentation. Patient education stressed strict sexual abstinence for 6 weeks and testosterone cessation to prevent androgen-driven tumescence during healing.

Taken together, the current literature and our observation - to the best of current knowledge, the first reported case of obvious external trauma free urethrorrhagia during erection associated with a focal perforator-type leak of the centro-cavernosal artery, in the absence of penile fracture or spongiosal rupture, successfully managed conservatively - supporta targeted, imaging-driven approach in atypical cases, guiding management strategy. This watch-and-wait approach is justified for hemodynamically stable patients with small leaks on dynamic imaging, reserving selective embolization for persistent high-flow priapism, abundant or recurrent bleeding.

Functional prognosis seems favorable with anti-erectile measures and rehabilitation, as our 3-month follow up confirmed full erectile recovery, aligning with success in small-leak observation cases.1,4 Limitations include the single-case design and omitted angiography (ethically unjustified given initial presentation and favorable evolution).

Conclusion

In conclusion, isolated urethrorrhagia during erection in young patients should evoke consideration of subclinical coital blunt trauma alongside the possibility of spontaneous lesions. Exogenous testosterone use may constitute an under-recognized risk factor for focal arterial injury, mirroring systemic vascular sequelae in anabolic steroid users and thus meriting systematic evaluation. Small fistulas herein warrant initial conservative management, reserving surgical intervention for refractory cases. Prospective registries and larger series of atypical urethrorrhagia are needed to delineate optimal imaging protocols, refine embolization versus observational indications, and rigorously compare medical and surgical efficacies.
