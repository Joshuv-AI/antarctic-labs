Rigorous Asymptotics for First-Order Algorithms Through the Dynamical Cavity Method
Yatin Dandi, David Gamarnik, Francisco Pernice, Lenka Zdeborová

Abstract
Dynamical Mean Field Theory (DMFT) provides an asymptotic description of the dynamics of macroscopic observables in certain disordered systems. Originally pioneered in the context of spin glasses by [ 19 ] , it has since been used to derive asymptotic dynamical equations for a wide range of models in physics, high-dimensional statistics and machine learning. One of the main tools used by physicists to obtain these equations is the dynamical cavity method , which has remained largely non-rigorous. In contrast, existing mathematical formalizations have relied on alternative approaches, including Gaussian conditioning, large deviations over paths, or Fourier analysis. In this work, we formalize the dynamical cavity method and use it to give a new proof of the DMFT equations for General First Order Methods, a broad class of dynamics encompassing algorithms such as Gradient Descent and Approximate Message Passing.



Keywords:
Dynamical Mean Field Theory, Spin Glass Dynamics, Exact Asymptotics.

1
Introduction

Dynamical Mean Field Theory (DMFT) is a framework for analyzing the high-dimensional behavior of dynamics in disordered systems, such as those coming from high-dimensional statistics and machine learning. Originating in the physics of spin glasses
[
19
,
11
]
, DMFT provides closed, finite-dimensional equations for the evolution of macroscopic observables —empirical and population risks, correlation with a planted signal, etc.— along trajectories that are themselves high-dimensional. In recent years, these ideas have been used in a wide variety of statistical and machine learning applications, where algorithms such as Gradient Descent, Approximate Message Passing (AMP) and Power Iteration are often described, in suitable asymptotic regimes, by DMFT-type equations.

Despite its success, the methodological approaches to DMFT of different communities remain disparate. In the physics literature, a major derivation technique is the dynamical cavity method, where one isolates a coordinate and does a perturbative expansion of the rest of the system with respect to the effect of this coordinate to calculate how the system “reacts” to its presence
[
18
]
. This approach is powerful and flexible but non-rigorous. By contrast, existing mathematical approaches have largely relied on different formalisms: Gaussian conditioning arguments
[
7
,
5
,
9
,
8
,
13
]
, pathwise large deviations
[
1
,
2
,
15
,
6
,
14
]
, or Fourier methods
[
4
,
17
]
. Notably,
[
3
,
16
]
follow an approach that is conceptually similar to the dynamical cavity method, but their proof works by first analyzing the dynamics of AMP and then reducing other DMFT equations to this case, an approach also used in
[
9
,
8
,
12
]
. The dynamical cavity method instead
treats both of these cases in a unified way: the only difference is that in the case of AMP, the perturbative expansion vanishes after the zeroth order term, while in the general case the first-order term also survives.

This paper gives a rigorous version of the dynamical cavity method and uses it to re-derive the DMFT equations for a broad algorithmic class known as General First Order Methods (GFOM)
[
9
]
. GFOM provides a unified template that captures a wide range of iterative procedures, including (variants of) Gradient Descent, AMP, Power Iteration, and related algorithms that alternate between multiplication by a random matrix and its transpose, and coordinate-wise nonlinearities and external randomness.

1.1
Gradient Descent for Generalized Linear Models Example

To introduce the model that we study, we begin by presenting the special case of Gradient Descent for a Generalized Linear Model (GLM), which will motivate the general definition.

GLM Setup.

The setting is the standard one for supervised learning: we have covariate-label pairs
(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,N,
where
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.
We assume that the pairs
(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})
are i.i.d. across
i
and that
y_{i}
depends on
\bm{x}_{i}
only through its inner product with a planted signal vector
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that there exists a measurable function
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
(on which we will later impose regularity conditions) and a noise vector
\bm{w}\sim N(0,I_{N})
such that

\displaystyle\bm{y}

\displaystyle=g(X\bm{\theta};\bm{w}).

Above,
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})
,
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
is the data matrix with rows
\bm{x}_{i}
, and it’s understood that
g
is applied coordinate-wise, i.e.,
g(X\bm{\theta};\bm{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
g(X\bm{\theta};\bm{w})_{i}=g(\left\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\right\rangle;w_{i}).

The goal is to find some
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the map
\bm{x}\mapsto\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{v}\right\rangle
is “close” to
\bm{x}\mapsto\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{\theta}\right\rangle.
For this purpose, given some (appropriately regular) loss function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},
we aim to minimize the empirical risk

\displaystyle R(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(\left\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{v}\right\rangle,y_{i}).

We do this by Gradient Descent (GD), starting from a random
\bm{v}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and iterating

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{v}^{t-1}-\eta\nabla R(\bm{v}^{t-1})

(1)

for some learning rate
\eta>0.
Note that the gradient of the risk can be written as

\displaystyle\nabla R(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{1}\ell(\left\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{v}\right\rangle,y_{i})\bm{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=X^{T}\partial_{1}\ell(X\bm{v},\bm{y}).

Above,
\partial_{1}\ell
denotes the partial derivative of
\ell
with respect to the first coordinate, which maps
\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},
and it’s being applied coordinate-wise to each pair
((X\bm{v})_{i},y_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,N
to obtain a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N}.
For the rest of the paper, we use this coordinate-wise notation without explicit mention.

What we Aim to Describe.

Under suitable assumptions (see
Section

1.4
), the DMFT equations describe the evolution of various macroscopic observables in the iteration (
1
). For example, they can be used to compute the limiting normalized empirical risk at step
t

\displaystyle r^{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}R(\bm{v}^{t}),

or the overlap between any two GD iterates

\displaystyle C_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\bm{v}^{s},\bm{v}^{t}\right\rangle

as well as many other quantities of interest along the GD trajectory. To do this, the key objects are joint empirical measures of the vectors
\bm{v}^{t},\bm{y}
and

\displaystyle\bm{b}^{t}

\displaystyle=X\bm{v}^{t-1}.

Indeed, note that
r^{t}
is a functional of the empirical measure
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(b^{t+1}_{i},y_{i})}
and
\{C_{s,t}\}_{1\leq s,t\leq T}
is a functional of the empirical measure
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\delta_{(v_{j}^{1},\dots,v_{j}^{T})}
. The DMFT equations will give exact asymptotic descriptions of these empirical measures, and hence of
r^{t},C_{s,t}
, and any other functional. However, in order for the equations to close, it’s convenient to work in a larger space of variables, describing a richer empirical measure object, and then obtaining these more specialized quantities as projections. This is what motivates the formulation of the general model that we analyze. We first write the iteration (
1
) in this language, and in the next section present the general model abstractly.

GFOM Formulation of Gradient Descent.

We introduce the additional vectors

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{t}

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}\ell(X\bm{v}^{t-1},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}\ell(\bm{b}^{t},\bm{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

and

\displaystyle\bm{h}^{t}

\displaystyle=X^{T}\bm{u}^{t}=\nabla R(\bm{v}^{t-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Now recall that we have
\bm{y}=g(X\bm{\theta},\bm{w}).
Defining
\bm{b}^{*}=X\bm{\theta}
and absorbing
\partial_{1}\ell
and
g
into one function, there exists a function
F:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{t}

\displaystyle=F(\bm{b}^{*},\bm{b}^{t};\bm{w}).

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{v}^{t-1}-\eta\bm{h}^{t},

so there exists a function
G_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{t}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{t}

\displaystyle=G_{t}(\bm{h}^{1},\dots,\bm{h}^{t};\bm{v}^{0}).

Overall, we can re-write the iteration (
1
) in the following form.

•

Initialize
\bm{\theta},\bm{v}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{b}^{*}=X\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

•

For
t=1,\dots,
let

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bm{b}^{t}=X\bm{v}^{t-1}&\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\\
\bm{u}^{t}=F(\bm{b}^{*},\bm{b}^{t};\bm{w})&\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\\
\bm{h}^{t}=X^{T}\bm{u}^{t}&\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
\bm{v}^{t}=G_{t}(\bm{h}^{1},\dots,\bm{h}^{t};\bm{v}^{0})&\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

(2)

To close the DMFT equations, it will be useful for us to describe the full joint empirical measures of
\bm{\theta},\{\bm{h}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{\bm{v}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
and
\bm{b}^{*},\{\bm{b}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{\bm{u}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},
respectively.

1.2
General First Order Methods

We now present the General First Order Methods (GFOM) model, first introduced in
[
9
]
, which is a generalization of the iteration (
2
) that also captures algorithms like Approximate Message Passing, Power Iteration, and many others.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d},\bm{\theta},\bm{v}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{b}^{*}=X\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.
For
t=1,\dots,
suppose
\bm{w}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\tilde{\bm{w}}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
F_{t},G_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{2t+1}\to\mathbb{R}
. The
GFOM orbit
is the iteration

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bm{b}^{t}=X\bm{v}^{t-1}&\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\\
\bm{u}^{t}=F_{t}(\bm{b}^{*},\bm{b}^{1},\dots,\bm{b}^{t};\bm{w}^{1},\dots,\bm{w}^{t})&\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\\
\bm{h}^{t}=X^{T}\bm{u}^{t}&\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
\bm{v}^{t}=G_{t}(\bm{h}^{1},\dots,\bm{h}^{t};\bm{v}^{0},\tilde{\bm{w}}^{1},\dots,\tilde{\bm{w}}^{t})&\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

(3)

In our notation, we will often write
\bm{u}^{t}=F_{t}(\bm{b}^{*},\bm{b}^{1},\dots,\bm{b}^{t})
and
\bm{v}^{t}=G_{t}(\bm{h}^{1},\dots,\bm{h}^{t}),
making the dependence on
\bm{w},\tilde{\bm{w}}
and
\bm{v}^{0}
implicit.

1.3
DMFT Equations

In this section we present the DMFT equations for the GFOM model of
Definition

1.1
. These equations will hold under some assumptions on the distribution of the data matrix
X
, the noise vectors
\bm{w},\tilde{\bm{w}},
the initializations
\bm{\theta},\bm{v}^{0},
and the functions
F_{t},G_{t}.
For now, we state the equations, mentioning these assumptions only as they are needed for the statement. We refer the reader to
Section

1.4
for a formal statement with all assumptions explicitly stated.

We assume throughout that
\alpha:=d/N
is a fixed constant. Given a time horizon
T\geq 0,
the DMFT equations will describe a pair of probability measures
\nu_{T}
and
\mu_{T}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2T+2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2T+1},
respectively. Under appropriate assumptions, we will prove that they are limits of the empirical measures

\displaystyle\nu_{T,N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\delta_{(\theta_{j},\{h^{t}_{j}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v^{t}_{j}\}_{t=0}^{T})}

(4)

\displaystyle\mu_{T,N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(b^{*}_{i},\{b^{t}_{i}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{u^{t}_{i}\}_{t=1}^{T})},

(5)

respectively (see
Section

1.4
). We will define
\nu_{T}
and
\mu_{T}
as the laws of explicit
effective processes

(\theta,\{h^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T})
and
(b^{*},\{b^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{u^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})
, respectively, which we will specify via sampling procedures. Moreover, these processes will be defined inductively in
T.

We will assume that the entries
(\theta_{j},v^{0}_{j})
are sampled i.i.d. across
j=1,\dots,d
from a fixed, known measure. Hence,
\nu_{0}
must be set equal to this measure for it to be a limit of (
4
). Then we set

\displaystyle b^{*}\sim N(0,\alpha\mathbb{E}_{(\theta,v^{0})\sim\nu_{0}}[\theta^{2}])

(6)

and
\mu_{0}=\mathrm{Law}(b^{*}).
This determines the base case
T=0.
For
T\geq 1
, to define
\mu_{T}
we assume
\nu_{T-1}
has already been defined. We sample a centered Gaussian vector
(b^{*},z^{1},\dots,z^{T})
with covariance

\displaystyle\mathsf{Cov}(b^{*},z^{1},\dots,z^{T})

\displaystyle=\alpha\cdot\mathsf{Cov}_{\nu_{T-1}}(\theta,v^{0},\dots,v^{T-1}),

(7)

where the right-hand side means the covariance of
(\theta,v^{0},\dots,v^{T-1})
when
(\theta,\{h^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1},\{v^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1})\sim\nu_{T-1}.
Independently of
(b^{*},z^{1},\dots,z^{T})
, we sample
(w^{1},\dots,w^{T})\sim N(0,I_{T})
, and for
t=1,\dots,T,
we set

\displaystyle\begin{cases}b^{t}=z^{t}+\alpha\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{T-1}}[\partial_{h^{s}}v^{t-1}]u^{s}\\
u^{t}=F_{t}(b^{*},b^{1},\dots,b^{t};w^{1},\dots,w^{t}).\end{cases}

(8)

Finally, we set
\mu_{T}=\mathrm{Law}(b^{*},\{b^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{u^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}).

To define
\nu_{T},
we assume that
\mu_{T}
has already been defined. Here we sample
(v^{0},\theta)\sim\nu_{0}
and, independently, a centered Gaussian vector
(g^{1},\dots,g^{T})
with covariance

\displaystyle\mathsf{Cov}(g^{1},\dots,g^{T})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Cov}_{\mu_{T}}(u^{1},\dots,u^{T}).

(9)

Then, independently of
v^{0},\theta,g^{1},\dots,g^{T},
we sample
(\tilde{w}^{1},\dots,\tilde{w}^{T})\sim N(0,I_{T}),
and for
t=1,\dots,T,
we set

\displaystyle\begin{cases}h^{t}=g^{t}+\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{T}}[\partial_{b^{*}}u^{t}]\theta+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{T}}[\partial_{b^{s}}u^{t}]v^{s-1}\\
v^{t}=G_{t}(h^{1},\dots,h^{t};v^{0},\tilde{w}^{1},\dots,\tilde{w}^{t}).\end{cases}

(10)

Finally, we set
\nu_{T}=\mathrm{Law}(\theta,\{h^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T})
.

Remark 1.2
.

In (
8
) and (
10
), the expressions
\partial_{h^{s}}v^{t-1},\partial_{b^{*}}u^{t}
and
\partial_{b^{s}}u^{t}
are defined recursively. For instance
\partial_{z^{s}}u^{t}
is recursively defined as follows:

\displaystyle\partial_{b^{s}}u^{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\partial_{k+1}F_{t}(b^{*},b^{1},\dots,b^{t})\partial_{b^{s}}b^{k}

\displaystyle\partial_{b^{s}}b^{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{s=k\}+\alpha\sum_{r=s}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{T-1}}[\partial_{h^{r}}v^{k-1}]\partial_{b^{s}}u^{r}

where
\partial_{k+1}
means derivative with respect to the
k+1
’th input variable to
F_{t}.
The expressions
\partial_{h^{s}}v^{t-1}
and
\partial_{b^{*}}u^{t}
are defined analogously.

1.4
Main Result

We begin by stating the assumptions needed for our main theorem. Below, we assume
\sigma>0
is a constant independent of
N.

(A1)

The entries of
X
are sampled i.i.d. from a
\sigma/\sqrt{N}
-subgaussian measure with mean zero and
variance
1/N
.

(A2)

The entries of
\bm{v}^{0},\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are i.i.d.
(\theta_{j},v^{0}_{j})\sim\nu_{0}
and independent of
X
, where
\nu_{0}
is
\sigma
-subgaussian.

(A3)

The functions
F_{t},G_{t}
are
L
-Lipschitz, where
L
is a constant independent of
N.

(A4)

The noise vectors
\bm{w}^{t},\tilde{\bm{w}}^{t}
are independent of each other and of everything else, with entries i.i.d.
N(0,1).

As it turns out, the last assumption is without loss of generality. Assuming the noise to be Gaussian is done for convenience. We can now state our main theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose assumptions (
A1
)-(
A4
) hold in
Definition

1.1
. Let
T\geq 0
, and fix
L
-Lipschitz functions
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{2T+2}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2T+1}\to\mathbb{R}.
Let
\nu_{T,N},\mu_{T,N}
be as in (
4
), (
5
), respectively and
\nu_{T},\mu_{T}
be defined by DMFT equations (
6
)-(
10
). Then as
N,d\to\infty
with
d/N=\alpha
fixed, we have

\displaystyle\int\phi d\nu_{N,T}

\displaystyle\to\int\phi d\nu_{T}

\displaystyle\int\psi d\mu_{N,T}

\displaystyle\to\int\psi d\mu_{T}

in probability.

Remark 1.4
.

Note that we are imposing only Lipschitzness on the non-linearities
F_{t},G_{t},
yet in equations (
8
), (
10
) there are expectations of derivatives of these functions. These derivatives can be understood in the weak (distributional) sense. Equivalently, one can consider a mollifier
\zeta_{t,\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{2t+1}\to\mathbb{R}
like the centered Gaussian density in
\mathbb{R}^{2t+1}
with covariance
\varepsilon I_{2t+1},
and replace the functions
F_{t},G_{t}
by
F_{t,\varepsilon}=F_{t}*\zeta_{t,\varepsilon},G_{t,\varepsilon}=G_{t}*\zeta_{t,\varepsilon},
respectively. The equations (
6
)-(
10
) can then be solved to obtain measures
\nu_{T,\varepsilon},\mu_{T,\varepsilon}.
Finally, the measures
\nu_{T},\mu_{T}
can be defined as (weak) limits of
\nu_{T,\varepsilon},\mu_{T,\varepsilon},
respectively, as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The fact that this is well-defined and doesn’t depend on the choice of mollifier is a standard exercise.

Proof Approach: Dynamical Cavity Method

In this section we describe our proof approach, which is the main technical novelty of the paper. Our proof can be viewed as a formalization of the dynamical cavity method, a powerful non-rigorous tool coming from physics that allows one to quickly derive DMFT equations like (

Smooth Approximation

To carry out the cavity derivation, we will need to assume that all functions we deal with are sufficiently regular. The following proposition allows us to assume this without loss of generality. Its proof, which follows

.

.

For the case of

in the appendix we show that the theorem below implies

1.3
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose assumptions (
A1
)-(
A4
) hold in

1.1
. Let

,

(12)

Dynamical Cavity Method Derivation of DMFT Equations

, respectively, and

.

Warmup: Gaussian Fields Ansatz.

To motivate the derivation, we begin by considering an (incorrect) ansatz that quickly leads to a wrong but instructive version of equations (
6
)-(

). The fixing of this ansatz will naturally lead us to the cavity method.

From
Definition

1.1
, we have

\displaystyle b^{t}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}v_{j}^{t-1}.

Consider the ansatz that the vector

\bm{\theta},\bm{v}^{0},\bm{v}^{1},\dots,\bm{v}^{T}.
If this is true, conditional on

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(v_{j}^{t-1})^{2}.

If we further assume that the entries of

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}(b_{i}^{t})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}(v_{j}^{t-1})^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}(v_{1}^{t-1})^{2},

where we have used exchangeability of the coordinates of

Now if we inductively (in

this yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}(b_{i}^{t})

\displaystyle\approx\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{T-1}}(v^{t-1})^{2}.

The same argument as above, applied to the whole vector
(b^{*}_{i},b^{1}_{i},\dots,b^{T}_{i}),
yields that this vector is approximately Gaussian with covariance

\displaystyle\mathsf{Cov}(b^{*}_{i},b^{1}_{i},\dots,b^{T}_{i})

\displaystyle\approx\alpha\mathsf{Cov}_{\nu_{T-1}}(\theta,v^{0},\dots,v^{T-1}).

Note that this is the same as (

. What’s missing in this derivation is the non-Gaussian terms that appear in

in (

These must arise due to dependence between

and
\bm{\theta},\bm{v}^{0},\dots,\bm{v}^{T}.

Dynamical Cavity Method Proof Sketch.

How do the entries
v_{j}^{t-1}
depend on
\{X_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{d}
?. Recall that
v_{j}^{t-1}
depends on

through the fields

for

, and from the definition of

we have

\begin{split}h_{j}^{s}&=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{N}X_{i^{\prime}j}u^{s}_{i^{\prime}}\\
&=X_{ij}u^{s}_{i}+\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}X_{i^{\prime}j}u^{s}_{i^{\prime}}.\end{split}

(15)

In other words,

has an
explicit

; it also depends implicitly on
\{X_{ij^{\prime}}\}_{j^{\prime}\in[d]\setminus\{j\}}
through the variables
\bm{u}^{s}.
The idea of our formal version of the dynamical cavity method is to do a Taylor expansion of the dynamical variable
v_{j}^{t-1}
with respect to the disorder variable
X_{ij}
which is driving this explicit dependence. In a nutshell, the zeroth order term in the expansion (which no longer has this explicit dependence) will give rise to the Gaussian terms

, the first order term will yield the non-Gaussian terms of (
8
), and the higher order terms will vanish as

This essentially follows the physicist’s dynamical cavity method in a rigorous way. The key technical ingredient in making this rigorous will be to prove sharp bounds on certain derivatives of the variables
v_{j},h_{j},u_{i},b_{i};
see
Section

2.3
.

Concretely, making the dependence of

on the disorder explicit in the notation, defining

to be equal to

on all entries except for
X^{ij}_{ij}=0
, and letting

we have

\displaystyle b_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}v_{j}^{t-1}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})+\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}^{2}\partial_{X_{ij}}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}d\eta(1-\eta)\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}^{3}\partial^{2}_{X_{ij}}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij}(\eta)).

We will show that the third term is

and can therefore be ignored. For the second term, we note that the iteration of
Definition

1.1
up to time

depends on

only through the variables

and

\displaystyle\partial_{X_{ij}}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})

\displaystyle=\left(\theta_{j}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}v_{j}^{s-1}\partial_{b_{i}^{s}}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})\right)+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}u_{i}^{s}\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij}).

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}^{2}\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})\right)u_{i}^{s}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}^{2}\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})

\displaystyle\approx\alpha\mathbb{E}\partial_{h_{1}^{s}}v_{1}^{t-1}

\displaystyle\approx\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{T-1}}\partial_{h_{1}^{s}}v_{1}^{t-1},

where the last two steps can be justified by derivative bounds and an induction argument, respectively. Second, we must show that the first term in (

To conclude this proof sketch, we give some intuition for why the term

behaves approximately like the Gaussian terms

For
1\leq s,t\leq T,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}v_{j}^{s-1}(X^{ij})\right)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}X_{ij}^{2}v_{j}^{s-1}(X^{ij})v_{j}^{t-1}(X^{ij})+\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}X_{ij}X_{ij^{\prime}}v_{j}^{s-1}(X^{ij})v_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1}(X^{ij^{\prime}})

where the last two steps can be justified by derivative bounds and an induction argument, respectively. What remains is to show that the second term above vanishes. For this, again through derivative bounds, we can use the approximate Gaussian integration by parts, which yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}X_{ij}X_{ij^{\prime}}v_{j}^{s-1}(X^{ij})v_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1}(X^{ij^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\partial_{X_{ij}}\partial_{X_{ij^{\prime}}}(v_{j}^{s-1}(X^{ij})v_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1}(X^{ij^{\prime}})).

\left\lVert\bm{u}^{t,\varepsilon}-\bm{u}^{t}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{N}O_{t,L}(1)

\left\lVert\bm{h}^{t,\varepsilon}-\bm{h}^{t}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{N}O_{t,L}(1)
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{h}^{t,\varepsilon}-\bm{h}^{t}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert X^{T}\bm{u}^{t,\varepsilon}-X^{T}\bm{u}^{t}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert X^{T}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\bm{u}^{t,\varepsilon}-\bm{u}^{t}\right\rVert_{2}.

But we have
\left\lVert X^{T}\right\rVert_{2}\leq O(1)
with high probability (see e.g.
[
20
]
). This proves that the inductive step
\bm{u}^{t}\to\bm{h}^{t}.

Finally, let
\phi_{\varepsilon},\psi_{\varepsilon}
be obtained from
\phi,\psi
in exactly the same way that we obtained
F_{t,\varepsilon},G_{t,\varepsilon}
from
F_{t},G_{t}.
To conclude the proof of the proposition, it suffices to show that we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\phi(\theta_{j},\{h_{j}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v_{j}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T})-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{j},\{h_{j}^{t,\varepsilon}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v_{j}^{t,\varepsilon}\}_{t=0}^{T})|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon O_{T,L}(1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\psi(b_{i}^{*},\{b_{i}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{u_{i}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T})-\psi_{\varepsilon}(b_{i}^{*},\{b_{i}^{t,\varepsilon}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{u_{i}^{t,\varepsilon}\}_{t=1}^{T})|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon O_{T,L}(1).

Since the proofs are analogous, we prove the first one. By the same reason as in (
19
) above, we have the same uniform bound for
\phi-\phi_{\varepsilon}.
Hence we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\phi(\theta_{j},\{h_{j}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v_{j}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T})-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{j},\{h_{j}^{t,\varepsilon}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v_{j}^{t,\varepsilon}\}_{t=0}^{T})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\phi(\theta_{j},\{h_{j}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v_{j}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T})-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{j},\{h_{j}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v_{j}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}))|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+|\phi_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{j},\{h_{j}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v_{j}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}))-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{j},\{h_{j}^{t,\varepsilon}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{v_{j}^{t,\varepsilon}\}_{t=0}^{T})|

\displaystyle\leq L\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left\lVert\phi_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta},\{\bm{h}^{t}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{\bm{v}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}))-\phi_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta},\{\bm{h}^{t,\varepsilon}\}_{t=1}^{T},\{\bm{v}^{t,\varepsilon}\}_{t=0}^{T})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq L\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}O_{T,L}(1)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\lVert\bm{h}^{t}-\bm{h}^{t,\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\bm{v}^{t}-\bm{v}^{t,\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon O_{t,L}(1),

as desired.
∎

A.2
Derivative Bounds: Proof of
Theorem

2.7

The proof will be by induction in the same order in which the different variables are defined in
Definition

1.1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle(\bm{v}^{0},\bm{\theta},\bm{b}^{*})\to\bm{b}^{1}\to\bm{u}^{1}\to\bm{h}^{1}\to\bm{v}^{1}\to\bm{b}^{2}\to\cdots.

(20)

At each step of the induction, our aim will be to verify the bounds of
Theorem

2.7
for the current variable in the chain (
20
), assuming the validity of the bounds for all prior variables in the chain and all values of
p,m.

The inductive cases for
\bm{b}^{t}
and
\bm{u}^{t}
are completely analogous to those of
\bm{h}^{t}
and
\bm{v}^{t}
, respectively. Hence, for brevity we only explicitly carry out the arguments for
\bm{b}^{t}
and
\bm{u}^{t}
. We will also use throughout various standard bounds on subgaussian and subexponential random variables which are stated in
Appendix

B
.

Base case.

As a base case, we begin by checking the bounds of
Theorem

2.7
for
\bm{v}^{0},\bm{\theta},\bm{b}^{*}.
Let
P
be a multi-set of variables as in
Definition

2.3
such that
|P|=m.
It’s clear that, for
\bm{v}^{0},\bm{\theta},
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{P}v^{0}_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{|P|=0\}v^{0}_{j}+\mathbbm{1}\{P=\{v^{0}_{j}\}\}

\displaystyle\partial_{P}\theta_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{|P|=0\}\theta_{j}+\mathbbm{1}\{P=\{\theta_{j}\}\}.

The right-hand sides have all moments
O(1)
by
Lemma

B.3
. Moreover, suppose
j
were not all-reaching in
P
. This in particular would mean that
|P|>0,P\neq\{v^{0}_{j}\}
and
P\neq\{\theta_{j}\}
, so the right-hand sides above would be identically zero. Hence, their moments are trivially
O(1/\sqrt{N}).

Finally we check the base case of
b_{i}^{*}.
We have

\displaystyle\partial_{P}b_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{P=\{b_{i}^{*}\}\}+\partial_{P}\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}\theta_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{P=\{b_{i}^{*}\}\}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{ij}\in P\}\partial_{P\setminus X_{ij}}\theta_{j}+X_{ij}\partial_{P}\theta_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{P=\{b_{i}^{*}\}\}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}\{P=\{X_{ij}\}\}\theta_{j}+\mathbbm{1}\{|P|=0\}\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}\theta_{j}

so

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{P}b_{i}^{*}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{1}\{P=\{b_{i}^{*}\}\}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}\{P=\{X_{ij}\}\}\left\lVert\theta_{j}\right\rVert_{p}+\mathbbm{1}\{|P|=0\}\left\lVert b_{i}^{*}\right\rVert_{p}.

The middle sum has at most one non-zero term, and hence is always
O(1)
by
Lemma

B.3
. For the last term, note that since
X_{ij}
and
\theta_{j}
are
\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}
-subgaussian and
\sigma
-subgaussian, respectively, their product is
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{N}}
-subexponetial by
Lemma

B.2
. But then by
Lemma

B.4
, their sum across
j
is
\sqrt{C}\sigma^{2}
-subexponential, and by
Lemma

B.3
, we get
\left\lVert b_{i}^{*}\right\rVert_{p}\leq C^{3/2}\sigma^{2}p=O(1).
Hence we conclude

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{P}b_{i}^{*}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq O(1)

always, checking part (1) of
Theorem

2.7
. For part (2), note that if
i
is not all-reaching in
P,
then we can neither have
P=\{b_{i}^{*}\}
nor
|P|=0
nor
P=\{X_{ij}\}
for any
j
. Hence
\partial_{P}b_{i}^{*}
is again equal to zero, and the bound is trivially satisfied.

Inductive case,
\bm{b}^{t}
.

We assume that the bounds of

2.7
are true for all variables prior to
\bm{b}^{t}
in the chain (
20
) and aim to verify them for

for some

Let

be a multi-set of variables as in

Using that

we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{P}b_{i}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbbm{1}\{P=\{b_{i}^{t}\}\}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[d]:X_{ij}\in P\end{subarray}}\partial_{P\setminus X_{ij}}v^{t-1}_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}\partial_{P}v_{j}^{t-1}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{1}\{P=\{b_{i}^{t}\}\}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[d]:X_{ij}\in P\end{subarray}}\left\lVert\partial_{P\setminus X_{ij}}v^{t-1}_{j}\right\rVert_{p}+\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}\partial_{P}v_{j}^{t-1}\right\rVert_{p}

Moreover, suppose that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}\partial_{P}v_{j}^{t-1}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\max\left\{\left\lVert u_{i}^{t}-\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}\right\rVert_{p},\left\lVert\partial_{b_{i}^{s}}u_{i}^{t}-\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{s}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}\right\rVert_{p},\left\lVert\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i}^{t}-\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}^{*}_{i}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}\right\}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}

and aim to prove the same for
s<t=T
(since in the case

, all partial derivatves are equal to 1 so the statement is trivial). Note that the base case

is trivially true. Since all inductive cases are analogous, we elaborate only the cases of
\bm{h}
and
\bm{v}.
For
\bm{h}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert h_{j}^{t}-\tilde{h}_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{ij}^{2}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{j}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{*}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\theta_{j}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\tilde{v}_{j}^{s-1}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{s}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-v_{j/ij}^{s-1}\partial_{b_{i}^{s}}u_{i/ij}^{t})\right)\right\rVert_{p}+\left\lVert\Delta_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\left\lVert\tilde{\theta}_{j}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{*}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\theta_{j}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{2p}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left\lVert\tilde{v}_{j}^{s-1}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{s}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-v_{j/ij}^{s-1}\partial_{b_{i}^{s}}u_{i/ij}^{t})\right\rVert_{2p}\right)+\left\lVert\Delta_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}.

We can bound each term individually. For
\left\lVert\Delta_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}
, using
Theorem

2.7
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Delta_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert u^{s}_{i/ij}X_{ij}^{2}\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}d\eta(1-\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert X_{ij}^{3}\partial^{2}_{X_{ij}}u_{i}^{t}(X^{ij}(\eta))\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{3p}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}d\eta(1-\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\left\lVert\partial^{2}_{X_{ij}}u_{i}^{t}(X^{ij}(\eta))\right\rVert_{2p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}d\eta(1-\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}.

Similarly, for
\left\lVert\tilde{\theta}_{j}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{*}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\theta_{j}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{2p}
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{\theta}_{j}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{*}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\theta_{j}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{2p}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\theta_{j}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{*}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\theta_{j}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{2p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{*}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{4p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{*}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i}^{t}\right\rVert_{4p}+\left\lVert\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i}^{t}-\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{4p}.

The first term is
O(1/\sqrt{N})
by inductive hypothesis. For the second term, we can write

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i}^{t}-\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{4p}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}d\eta\left\lVert X_{ij}\partial_{X_{ij}}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i}^{t}(X^{ij}(\eta)\right\rVert_{4p}

(40)

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}

again by
Theorem

2.7
. A similar proof to the above shows that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{v}_{j}^{s-1}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{s}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-v_{j/ij}^{s-1}\partial_{b_{i}^{s}}u_{i/ij}^{t})\right\rVert_{2p}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}

as well.

Next we show that we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}h_{j}^{t}-\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{h}_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}.

Here we can write

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}h_{j}^{t}-\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{h}_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}g_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}+\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{ij}^{2}\left(\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{\theta}_{j}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{*}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}\theta_{j}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{s-1}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{s}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}v_{j/ij}^{s-1}\partial_{b_{i}^{s}}u_{i/ij}^{t})\right)\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{ij}^{2}\left(\theta_{j}\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}v_{j/ij}^{s-1}\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}\partial_{b_{i}^{s}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right)\right\rVert_{p}+\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}\Delta_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}g_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\left\lVert\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{\theta}_{j}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{*}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}\theta_{j}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{2p}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left\lVert\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{s-1}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{b}_{i}^{s}}\tilde{u}_{i}^{t}-\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}v_{j/ij}^{s-1}\partial_{b_{i}^{s}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{2p}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\left\lVert\theta_{j}\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{2p}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left\lVert v_{j/ij}^{s-1}\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}\partial_{b_{i}^{s}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{2p}\right)+\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}\Delta_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

(41)

The second and last terms above can be bounded in a completely analogous way to how we bounded the corresponding terms in
\left\lVert h_{j}^{t}-\tilde{h}_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}
. The summands in the third term above can similarly be bounded since e.g.

\displaystyle\left\lVert\theta_{j}\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{2p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}\partial_{b_{i}^{*}}u_{i/ij}^{t}\right\rVert_{4p}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}

by
Theorem

2.7
. What remains is to bound the first term
\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}g_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}.
To bound this term, we use the same Taylor expansion as in (
25
). In this case we set

\displaystyle V_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}(X)

\displaystyle:=(\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}u_{i_{1}/i_{1}j}^{t-1})\cdots(\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}u_{i_{p}/i_{p}j}^{t-1}).

(42)

Following the same argument as in the proof of
Theorem

2.7
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}g_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{q=0}^{p}\int_{[0,1]^{q}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{q}d\eta_{\ell}\frac{1}{N^{(p+q)/2}}\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{p})\in\mathcal{A}_{q}}\left\lVert\partial_{X_{i^{\prime}_{1}j}}\dots\partial_{X_{i^{\prime}_{q}j}}V_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}(X^{\{i^{\prime}_{1},\dots,i^{\prime}_{q}\},j}(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{q}))\right\rVert_{p+1}.

We claim that, for each of the terms in the sum above, we have

\left\lVert\partial_{X_{i^{\prime}_{1}j}}\dots\partial_{X_{i^{\prime}_{q}j}}V_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}(X^{\{i^{\prime}_{1},\dots,i^{\prime}_{q}\},j}(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{q}))\right\rVert_{p+1}\lesssim(1/\sqrt{N})^{p}.

To prove this, it suffices to show that, for any way to distribute the partial derivatives
\partial_{X_{i^{\prime}_{1}j}},\dots,\partial_{X_{i^{\prime}_{q}j}}
among the factors in
(\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}u_{i_{1}/i_{1}j}^{t-1}),\cdots,(\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}u_{i_{p}/i_{p}j}^{t-1})
, every factor must have moments that are of order
1/\sqrt{N}
. Note first that, since
X_{i^{\prime}_{k},j}
has been set to zero in
\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}u_{i_{k}^{\prime}/i_{k}^{\prime}j}^{t-1},
we can assume that no derivative
\partial_{X_{i^{\prime}_{k},j}}
gets distributed to
\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}u_{i_{k}^{\prime}/i_{k}^{\prime}j}^{t-1},
since otherwise the corresponding term will be equal to zero. But recall that the
i^{\prime}_{1},\dots,i^{\prime}_{k}
are unique among
i_{1},\dots,i_{p}.
Hence, any factors that get any derivatives at all distributed to them must have moments that are
\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}
by
Theorem

2.7
. Morever, any term that doesn’t get any derivatives distributed to it is still being acted on by
\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}
, and hence again has moments that are
O(1/\sqrt{N})
by
Theorem

2.7
. This proves that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}g_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{q=0}^{p}\frac{1}{N^{(p+q)/2}}\cdot|\mathcal{A}_{q}|\cdot\frac{1}{N^{p/2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{N^{p/2}}.

Plugging this back into (
41
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}h_{j}^{t}-\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{h}_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N},

as desired.

To conclude the proof of

, we elaborate the inductive case for
\bm{v}.
By Lipschitzness of

, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert v_{j}^{t}-\tilde{v}_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left\lVert h_{j}^{s}-\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}

by inductive hypothesis. Similarly, by chain rule we have we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}v_{j}^{t}-\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\left\lVert\partial_{k}G_{t}(h_{j}^{1},\dots,h_{j}^{t};v_{j}^{0},\tilde{w}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j}^{t})\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}h_{j}^{k}-\partial_{k}G_{t}(\tilde{h}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{h}_{j}^{t};v_{j}^{0},\tilde{w}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j}^{t})\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{h}_{j}^{k}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{t}\left\lVert\partial_{k}G_{t}(h_{j}^{1},\dots,h_{j}^{t};v_{j}^{0},\tilde{w}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j}^{t})\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}h_{j}^{k}-\partial_{k}G_{t}(\tilde{h}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{h}_{j}^{t};v_{j}^{0},\tilde{w}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j}^{t})\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}h_{j}^{k}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\left\lVert\partial_{k}G_{t}(\tilde{h}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{h}_{j}^{t};v_{j}^{0},\tilde{w}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j}^{t})\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}h_{j}^{k}-\partial_{k}G_{t}(\tilde{h}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{h}_{j}^{t};v_{j}^{0},\tilde{w}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j}^{t})\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{h}_{j}^{k}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{t}\left\lVert\partial_{k}G_{t}(h_{j}^{1},\dots,h_{j}^{t};v_{j}^{0},\tilde{w}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j}^{t})-\partial_{k}G_{t}(\tilde{h}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{h}_{j}^{t};v_{j}^{0},\tilde{w}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{w}_{j}^{t})\right\rVert_{2p}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}h_{j}^{k}-\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{h}_{j}^{k}\right\rVert_{2p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{t}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{t}\left\lVert h_{j}^{k^{\prime}}-\tilde{h}_{j}^{k^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{2p}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\left\lVert\partial_{h_{j}^{s}}h_{j}^{k}-\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{h}_{j}^{k}\right\rVert_{2p}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}

by
Theorem

2.7
and the inductive hypothesis. This concludes the proof of
A.10
.

Claim A.9
.

Recall we are assuming all links in the chain of

A.3.3
are valid up to and not including (

), (
31
) at step

The following hold for every

and

and

\displaystyle\lim\left\lVert\tilde{b}_{i}^{t}-\overline{b}_{i}^{t}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\lim\left\lVert\tilde{h}_{j}^{t}-\overline{h}_{j}^{t}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=0.

Proof.

The proof is again by induction in
t=1,\dots,T.
At each step, we assume that, for all times
s<t,
we have

\displaystyle\lim\left\lVert\tilde{b}_{i}^{s}-\overline{b}_{i}^{s}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\lim\left\lVert\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}-\overline{h}_{j}^{s}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=0,

and we aim to prove this at time

, as long as

Since the proofs of both of the statements above are analogous, we carry out explicitly the proof of the first claim only.

As a first step, note that by construction of the variables with tilde above, for
s\geq 1
and
i\in[N]
, the quantities
\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1}

can be written as (recursively-defined)
C^{\infty}_{1b}
functions of the fields
\{\tilde{h}_{j}^{r}\}_{r=1}^{s}.
Call these functions
\phi_{t}
, such that
\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1}=\phi_{t}(\tilde{h}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{h}_{j}^{t-1}).
Note that for
j\neq j^{\prime}\in[d]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{Cov}(\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1},\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j^{\prime}}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Cov}(\phi_{t}(\tilde{h}_{j}^{1},\dots,\tilde{h}_{j}^{t-1}),\phi_{t}(\tilde{h}_{j^{\prime}}^{1},\dots,\tilde{h}_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{Cov}(\phi_{t}(h_{j}^{1},\dots,h_{j}^{t-1}),\phi_{t}(h_{j^{\prime}}^{1},\dots,h_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1}))+O(1/\sqrt{N})

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}

where in the first inequality we used
A.10
and in the second one we used (
31
) at step
t-1
.

Now we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{N}+N^{2}\max_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\mathsf{Cov}(X_{ij}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1},X_{ij^{\prime}}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j^{\prime}}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1})

and, for

\displaystyle N^{2}

\displaystyle\mathsf{Cov}(X_{ij}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1},X_{ij^{\prime}}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j^{\prime}}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1})\leq N^{2}|\mathsf{Cov}(X_{ij}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1},\frac{1}{N}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1})|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+|\mathsf{Cov}(\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1},\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j^{\prime}}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1})|+N^{2}|\mathsf{Cov}(\frac{1}{N}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j^{\prime}}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1},X_{ij^{\prime}}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j^{\prime}}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1})|.

The middle term vanishes as stated above, so we need to control the first and third term, which are analogous, so we focus on the first term. We have

\displaystyle N^{2}|\mathsf{Cov}(X_{ij}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1},\frac{1}{N}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1})|

\displaystyle=1/\sqrt{N}+|\mathsf{Cov}({\partial}_{{h}_{j}^{s}}{v}_{j/ij}^{t-1},{\partial}_{{h}_{j}^{s}}{v}_{j/ij}^{t-1})|

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}+|\mathsf{Cov}({\partial}_{{h}_{j}^{s}}{v}_{j}^{t-1},{\partial}_{{h}_{j}^{s}}{v}_{j}^{t-1})|

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}+|\mathsf{Cov}(\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1},\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1})|

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/\sqrt{N}+|\mathsf{Cov}(\phi_{t}(h_{j}^{1},\dots,h_{j}^{t-1}),\phi_{t}(h_{j^{\prime}}^{1},\dots,h_{j^{\prime}}^{t-1}))|

\displaystyle\to 0,

where we have again used
A.10
and the inductive hypothesis (

). This shows that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}_{j}^{s}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1}\right)\to 0.

(43)

Moreover, by inductive hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}^{2}\tilde{\partial}_{\tilde{h}^{s}_{j}}\tilde{v}_{j}^{t-1}-\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{T-1}}{\partial}_{{h}^{s}}{v}^{t-1}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{ij}^{2}{\partial}_{{h}_{j}}{v}_{j}^{t-1}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}{\partial}_{{h}_{j}}{v}_{j}^{t-1}\right|+1/\sqrt{N}

\displaystyle=1/\sqrt{N}

by inductive hypothesis, we conclude

Appendix B
Subgaussian and subexponential random variables

Here we state a few standard facts about subgaussian and subexpoential random variables that we use throughout our proofs. All proofs can be found in standard texts, e.g.
[
20
]
. In the lemmas below, for simplicity and without loss of generality, we take all the absolute constants to be the same, denoted by
C.

Definition B.1
.

Let
X
be a random variable in
\mathbb{R}.
We call the quantities

\displaystyle\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t>0:\mathbb{E}e^{X/t}\leq 2\}

\displaystyle\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t>0:\mathbb{E}e^{X^{2}/t^{2}}\leq 2\}

in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
the subexpotential and subgaussian norms of
X
, respectively.

Lemma B.2
.

For every pair of random variables
X,Y
in
\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert XY\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}.

Lemma B.3
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds. For every random variable
X
in
\mathbb{R}
and
p\geq 1,
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}p

\displaystyle\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\sqrt{p}.

Lemma B.4
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds. Suppose
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are independent, centered random variables. Then we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}^{2}.
