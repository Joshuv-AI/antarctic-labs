Reproducing Valgus Angle in Uncemented Humeral Hemiarthroplasty
Authors: Jefferson Li, John M Ibrahim, Sophie S Darwiche, Alexis L Fowler, Mark E Baratz, Robert A Kaufmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHemiarthroplasty may be considered for treatment of elbow arthritis with successful implantation requiring reproduction of native anatomy. This study identifies whether an uncemented hemiarthroplasty implant that achieves fixation with intramedullary (IM) screws can restore the valgus alignment of the distal humerus flexion-extension axis of rotation when assessed through radiographic parameters.MethodsWe stripped six cadaveric specimens of soft tissues and obtained orthogonal radiographs to characterize native distal humerus anatomy. We implanted hemiarthroplasty components using IM screw fixation and obtained postimplantation radiographs. We compared the coronal plane angle between the IM canal and the flexion-extension axis of the native humerus with the angle between the IM canal and the implant’s centerline of rotation. We identified the respective angulation between the IM canal of the humerus and the IM screw and body component.ResultsSix cadavers (five men and one woman) underwent implantation. The average age of the cadavers was 62.7 years (range, 53−74). The mean difference in articular spool valgus was 3.64° (1.86−5.84). The angulation between the IM canal of the humerus and the IM screw was between 1° and 2.3°and angulation between the IM canal of the humerus and the body component was <3.5°.ConclusionsThis study identified radiographic recreation of distal humerus valgus angulation within 4°with an uncemented hemiarthroplasty implant. Angulation that occurs between the humeral body and the IM screw as well as different screw lengths and thread diameters likely contribute to the implant’s ability to accommodate for different anatomic features.Clinical relevanceThese results suggest that uncemented hemiarthroplasty can reproduce valgus angulation of the centerline of rotation based on radiographic parameters.Key words: Distal humerus fracture, Elbow arthritis, Elbow hemiarthroplasty, Intramedullary screw, Uncemented hemiarthroplasty

The elbow joint exhibits a valgus carrying angle whereby the supinated forearm is deviated in valgus relative to the long axis of the humerus when the elbow is in extension, a feature that allows the forearm to clear the hips when swinging the arms during ambulation1,2 (Fig. 1). Maximum valgus is achieved in elbow extension and then lost as the elbow is flexed, which allows for the forearm and humerus to become mostly colinear and facilitates the hand reaching the mouth.3,4

Anatomical features influence the carrying angle of the left elbow.

Elbow carrying angles vary between 10° and 25° with this angle being typically bisected by the centerline of rotation, suggesting 5° to 12.5° of valgus originating from the humeral side.5, 6, 7 Although the centerline of rotation does exhibit laxity, the elbow can be modeled as a hinge joint with its axis of rotation both internally rotated and in valgus relative to the humeral shaft.8,9 The oblique orientation of this axis in the coronal plane can be visualized radiographically on an AP view as the angle between the centerline of ulnohumeral rotation (flexion-extension axis [F-E axis]) and the long axis of the humeral intramedullary (IM) canal.3,5,10

Hemiarthroplasty may be considered when injury, fracture, or arthritis is isolated to the distal humerus, and the proximal ulna is relatively preserved. Although not cleared by the food and drug administration, hemiarthroplasty is gaining increased international acceptance.11,12 In the United States, it can only be implanted in an off-label manner using the humeral component of the cemented Latitude total elbow arthroplasty (Stryker).13,14

To reproduce elbow biomechanics, a hemiarthroplasty should recreate native bone anatomy, including the alignment of the F-E axis.15, 16, 17 This reproduction may prevent excessive stress on the bone and reduce the incidence of implant loosening or failure.10,18 The humeral component of the KTE (Arrch Orthopaedics) implant system aims to reproduce native anatomy and embodies a 5.9° valgus alignment between the long axis of the IM canal and the F-E axis.19,20 It has the ability to accommodate for varying anatomy through 3.5° of laxity between the IM screw and the humeral body component. This laxity is in distinction to commercially available implants that employ a fixed stem design. The Latitude arthroplasty (Stryker) is the only commercially available implant that aims to reproduce native anatomy and can be used as a hemiarthroplasty in an off-label manner in the United States.13,14 It includes a fixed valgus alignment between the humeral stem and the F-E axis, and, unfortunately, the conventional stem needs to be shortened in an effort to reproduce valgus angulation.17

In this study, we investigated whether an uncemented hemiarthroplasty (KTE, Arrch Orthopaedics) can reproduce native valgus anatomy. This system references the IM axis of the humerus and the F-E axis for placing its cutting block (Fig. 2). After completion of cuts, a burr and sequential broaches are used to create a cavity for the humeral component that is available in three different sizes (small, medium, and large). Intramedullary fixation occurs by choosing from five screw sizes that are supplied in three different lengths.21,22 This modular hemiarthroplasty system has 45 implant fixation combinations with the goal of reproducing native articular features and normal kinematics of the elbow.19,23,24

The following steps are followed for the implantation of the hemiarthroplasty. A The IM canal of the humeral shaft and the centerline of rotation are identified. B Custom drill bits with nonaggressive leading edge aim to not penetrate IM canal. C Cutting block connects to the IM drill bit and is rotationally aligned with the centerline of joint rotation. D Sagittal saw cuts the anterior distal humerus. E Cutting block dimensions mimic the humeral body component. F Sagittal saw cuts the bone within the confines of the cutting block. G Distal cuts are made with the sagittal saw. H Linear broaches remove bone from the lateral and medial portions of the distal humerus. I IM canal is tapped with custom taps that correspond to the IM drill bit that was used. J The hemiarthroplasty components are secured with two bolts and two washers. K The implant is advanced by tightening the IM screw. L The hemiarthroplasty is secured with two finger tightness to the distal humerus.

Given natural variation in the position and orientation of the native F-E axis, our goal was to identify whether this implant system was able to reproduce this axis in six cadaver specimens.17,25,26 We would also identify the angulations between (1) the IM canal of the humerus and the IM screw and between (2) the IM canal and the body component.

Materials and Methods

All procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008.

Six cadaver arms were stripped of all soft tissues. Radiographs were obtained of the distal humerus that included the shaft to capture the longest length of IM screw fixation. Cadavers were excluded if they had prior surgery or injury that would have altered the anatomy of the region of interest including prior humeral fracture, retained ballistic fragments or other radiodense material, or internal fixation hardware.

An attending hand surgeon (RAK), a hand surgery fellow (JMI), and an attending radiologist (SSD) performed the measurements. These measurements were then repeated four times in order to assess inter- and intraobserver reliability. Prior results were not viewed at the time of repeat measurements and all steps were repeated.

Hemiarthroplasty implantation

The IM canal and F-E axis were identified first, which then determined the placement of the cutting block. Appropriate saw cuts were made upon which broaches and drills were employed to create a cavity that allowed the implant to fit securely within the distal humerus. Six hemiarthroplasties were implanted after selecting the best matched size to the native distal humerus anatomy. There exist three different implant sizes. The humeral component was implanted using a saw, broaches, and a burr. When drilling the IM canal, the smallest drill bit was used first to identify the IM canal. Drill bits of sequentially increasing diameter were then used until proprioceptive resistance was met. Each drill bit size corresponds to a custom tap, which was then advanced using standard technique. The length of tap advancement was measured and the longest screw, which was still shorter than this advancement depth, was chosen.

Postimplantation

The implant size (eg, small, medium, and large) and the different lengths of screws was recorded.

The absolute difference in measurements of Offset and ApDev and APScBodDev of the pre- and postimplantation parameters were calculated to compare native anatomy with implant anatomy. An intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) value was calculated to determine the inter-rater reliability of these measurements by having three of the authors take separate measurements for ValC, ValH, and APScBodDev on each of the six cadavers.

Results

Six cadaveric elbows were implanted with four medium screws, one small screw, and one large screw (Fig. 4; Supplement 1, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org). There were no large spools used. On average, the component implanted had −3.6° of valgus relative to the native anatomy (Table 1). The average offset seen on the AP radiographs was −3.3 mm for six cadavers.

Discussion

The goal of our investigation was to implant an uncemented hemiarthroplasty design into cadaveric humerus specimens and identify an ability to reproduce the valgus alignment of the centerline axis of rotation. We also aimed to characterize (1) collinearity between the IM screw and the humeral shaft and (2) the angulation between the IM screw and implant body. The match between native anatomy and the hemiarthroplasty with regards to trochlear width 3.39 mm (range, 2.37−4.7 mm), capitellum center 5.28 mm (range, 3.92−7.29 mm), and coronal plane offset of centerlines 0.35 mm (range, 0.08−0.87 mm) has been studied.20

Appropriate implantation of this implant required two body sizes, three screw lengths, and two screw diameters. The need for different humeral stem components supports this implant’s modular capability, which is in distinction to fixed stem constructs that only employ variable spool sizes and stem lengths (Latitude, Stryker).

The goal was to match valgus angulation by placing the cutting block over the IM drill bit and referencing two K-wires that represent the native F-E axis. This method is similar to other surgical techniques that employ visual cues and jigs.7,10,27 The F-E axes of the implants were deviated within 3.64° (range, 1.86°−5.84°) of the native valgus angle, which was 97.3° (range, 95.6°−99.4°).

The results are similar to a Latitude (Stryker) implantation series that used navigation and identified the least variation when using a stem truncated at the level of the anterior flange. Implantation error was 2.5° in the varus-valgus plane with approximately 1/3rd of their samples exhibiting <2°, 1/3rd 2°−4°, 1/3rd 4°−6°. Given that a truncated stem would result in compromised structural integrity, the authors advocate employing three fixed angle stems with 0°, 4°, and 8° valgus stem alignments and recommend modularity within future implant designs.15 Our results were similar whereby we had 1/6 < 2°, 2/6 between 2° and 4°, and 3/6 between 4° and 6°. We were able to match the navigated coronal accuracy while using a manually directed cutting guide that recreated the visualization experienced in vivo by the surgeon.

The offset (Off) between the centerline of rotation of the native humerus and the implant was 3.3 mm (1.0–8.7 mm). The implanted F-E axis was always distal to the native F-E axis, which is likely due to the nature of the broaching efforts that are required to seat the implant within the inner cortex of the distal humerus.

The angle that is recorded between the IM long axis of the native elbow and the long axis of the IM screw (ApDev) averaged less than 1° and, yet, in one implant the angulation was 2.3°. These results suggest that the IM screw will mostly be collinear with the long axis of the IM canal of the humerus in the coronal plane. The method of screw fixation, whereby increasingly larger drills are used until proprioceptive chatter is recognized and then the canal is tapped, explains this collinearity. The one outlier suggests that the IM screw was either not engaging the cortical bone fully or the screw was better secured in the sagittal plane, which can occur when greater apex posterior curvature affects the cylindrical nature of the distal humerus.28 Substantial fixation strength was identified in two studies even though the collinearity between screw and humeral shaft was not measured.21,22 Future studies are needed to shed light into the relationship between collinearity and pullout strength.

The angle between the IM screw and the humeral body component (APScBodDev) is a measure of the angulation of the IM screw relative to the humeral body component. Our angulation measurements were within the 3.5° that the implant allows and reflect the need for screw to body angulation during implantation.

We recognize that this study has substantial weaknesses. One limitation is that human error was introduced by manually placing and then measuring the IM canal centerline and the F-E axis in the cadaver and hemiarthroplasty radiographs.25,29 We attempted to quantify measurement reproducibility and our ICC was good for ValH (0.73) and moderate for ValC (0.48) and APScBodDev (0.54).30 The higher ValH ICC is likely due to easier quantification of the implant centerline based on its cylindrical spool geometry.

We aimed to limit out of plane rotation by using standardized radiography and removing the muscles. The manual placement of the IM canal centerline, identification of the centerline of rotation as well as angulation measurement are vulnerable to human error.29 Our study referenced the anterior-inferior aspect of the medial epicondyle and the center of the capitellum, which is comparable to identifying the geometric centers of the trochlea and the capitellum.7,27,29,31,32 Three-dimensional CT-based imaging would have provided more accurate characterization of these measurements, yet metallic implants can affect accurate computed tomography characterization. Standard two-dimensional radiography is widely available in the clinical setting. Additional concerns relate to the specimens that were selected from deceased donors with no history of elbow injury or disease. Patients seeking hemiarthroplasty will likely present with elbow abnormalities that are probably not represented by these specimens. A further weakness is that we only used six cadaver arms, which is fewer than two similar studies.17,25

This study demonstrates an ability to reasonably match native F-E axis anatomy when implanting a distal humerus hemiarthroplasty that employs modular IM fixation of the distal humerus. Angular laxity between the humeral body component and modular screw fixation reproduces coronal alignment similar to that of computer navigated implantation.17 An ability to accommodate morphologic variations in native anatomy may optimize hemiarthroplasty alignment.
