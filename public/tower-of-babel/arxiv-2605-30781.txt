The NF-operator and the NF-Numbers of Simplicial Complexes
Bilal Ahmad Rather

Abstract
Let △ \displaystyle\bigtriangleup be a simplicial complex and let δ 𝒩 ​ ℱ \displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}} denote the NF-operator. The NF-complex δ 𝒩 ​ ℱ ​ ( △ ) \displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup) is defined as the Stanley–Reisner complex of the facet ideal of △ \displaystyle\bigtriangleup . Iterating δ 𝒩 ​ ℱ \displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}} gives a periodic orbit (up to isomorphism), and the smallest positive integer t \displaystyle t for which δ 𝒩 ​ ℱ t ( △ ) ≅ △ \displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{\,t}(\bigtriangleup)\cong\bigtriangleup is called the NF-number of △ \displaystyle\bigtriangleup (Habi and Mahmood, Algebra Colloquium, 2022). In this work, we provide various results and determine explicit formulas for the NF-number for several families of graphs. In particular, we compute the NF-number for dumbbell graphs. We also prove that the NF-number of the complete split graph S n , m \displaystyle S_{n,m} equals m + n + 2 \displaystyle m+n+2 , and that the NF-number of the double star D p + q \displaystyle D_{p+q} equals p + q + 4 \displaystyle p+q+4 . We conclude with remarks, open problems, and conjectures to guide future research.



Keywords:
Stanley–Reisner complex, facet ideal, simplicial complex, NF-Number, vertex cover.

Introduction

The
Stanley–Reisner ideal

\displaystyle I_{\bigtriangleup_{\mathcal{N}}(\bigtriangleup)}
(encoding nonfaces) and the
facet ideal

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup)
(encoding facets as squarefree monomials) are two classical monomial ideals attached to a simplicial complex
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
on a finite set, see
[
15
,
12
,
13
]
.
Faridi stressed facet ideals as a suitable complement to Stanley-Reisner theory
[
3
]
. Hibi and Mahmood
[
7
]
discovered that passing from
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
to a new complex
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup)
transforms the facet ideal of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
into the Stanley–Reisner ideal of
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup)
. Iterating over this operator results in a periodic dynamical system on the finite set of complexes on
\displaystyle[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
vertices. The associated period is the
NF-number
.
This point of view places
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}
between combinatorial commutative algebra and finite dynamical systems. On the algebraic side, it uses the two standard ways of encoding a simplicial complex by squarefree monomial ideals: the Stanley–Reisner ideal and the facet ideal. On the combinatorial side, the operation can be described in terms of minimal vertex covers, or equivalently minimal transversals, of the facet hypergraph of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
. This makes the NF-operator closely related to classical duality phenomena for squarefree monomial ideals, including Alexander duality and the study of edge ideals, cover ideals, and facet ideals; see, for instance,
[
19
,
10
,
17
,
4
]
. The Stanley–Reisner correspondence has been one of the central tools in combinatorial commutative algebra since the work of Stanley and Reisner. It translates topological and combinatorial properties of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
into algebraic properties of the quotient ring
\displaystyle S/I_{\bigtriangleup}
, such as Cohen–Macaulayness, Betti numbers, and Hilbert series; see
[
12
,
13
,
8
,
14
,
11
]
. Facet ideals, introduced and systematically studied by Faridi, provide a complementary construction: instead of recording the nonfaces of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
, they record the maximal faces themselves
[
3
]
. The NF-complex connects these two constructions by applying the Stanley–Reisner construction to the facet ideal.
The NF-number can be defined for any simplicial complex, but graphs provide a concrete example. A finite simple graph
\displaystyle G
transforms into a one-dimensional simplicial complex with edges as facets. For disjoint unions of two cliques
\displaystyle K_{n}\cup K_{m}
, Hibi and Mahmood calculated the NF-number and obtained a clear formula
[
7
]
. The NF-number of two complete graphs joined by a common vertex can be seen in
[
1
]
. The NF-number of disjoint union of finite copies of complete graph are given in
[
2
]
.
The problem of determining NF-numbers is therefore naturally related to the problem of understanding how minimal vertex covers evolve under repeated complementation. Even for graphs, this is a delicate question because the first NF-iterate of a graph need not remain one-dimensional. Thus, graph-theoretic data may produce higher-dimensional simplicial complexes after only one or two applications of
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}
. This phenomenon is one of the reasons explicit NF-number computations are nontrivial beyond complete graphs and their close relatives. In the present work, we continue this line of investigation for several familiar graph families. We first analyze dumbbell graphs, obtained by joining two complete graphs by a bridge edge. We then treat complete split graphs and double stars. These families are sufficiently structured to allow explicit descriptions of their minimal vertex covers, but they are rich enough to show how quickly NF-iteration leaves the category of graphs and enters the broader category of simplicial complexes. Throughout the paper, graphs are regarded as one-dimensional simplicial complexes whose facets are their edges.

Vertex covers and a working formula for
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}

At first, a question comes in mind, weather such period
\displaystyle q
exist, and
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{(q)}(\bigtriangleup)\cong\bigtriangleup
. The following lemma shows the existence of period
\displaystyle q

[
7
]
, and hence, the NF-number of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
is well-defined.

Lemma 2.1
.

For each simplicial complex
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
on a finite vertex set, there exists
\displaystyle q>0
such that
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{(q)}(\bigtriangleup)=\bigtriangleup
.

Proof.

The sequence
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup,\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup),\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{(2)}(\bigtriangleup),\dots
must ultimately recur as there are only finitely many simplicial complexes on a fixed finite vertex set.
Hibi and Mahmood
[
7
, Lemma 1.4]
prove a stronger injectivity property that: if
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup^{\prime})=\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup^{\prime\prime})
, then
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup^{\prime}=\bigtriangleup^{\prime\prime}
. As a result, the first repetition must return to the beginning point, yielding a valid period.
∎

A key computational tool is the characterization of facets of
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup)
in terms of minimal vertex covers.
For a simplicial complex
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
on
\displaystyle V
with facets
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bigtriangleup)
, a
vertex cover
of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
is a subset
\displaystyle C\subseteq V
, if
\displaystyle C\cap F\neq\varnothing
, for every
\displaystyle F\in\mathcal{F}(\bigtriangleup)
. If it has no proper subset that is also a vertex cover, then it is
minimal vertex cover
.
The set of minimal vertex covers is denoted by
\displaystyle\mathrm{MinCov}(\bigtriangleup)
.

The following result shows that the facets of
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup)
are complements of minimal covers.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
be a simplicial complex on vertex set
\displaystyle V
. Then

\mathcal{F}(\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup))=\{\,V\setminus C:C\in\mathrm{MinCov}(\bigtriangleup)\,\}.

Proof.

In facet-ideal theory, it is well-known that
\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup)
has a (irreducible) decomposition
[
6
, p. 12]

I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup)=\bigcap_{C\in\mathrm{MinCov}(\bigtriangleup)}(x_{v}:v\in C).

A set
\displaystyle F\subseteq V
is in
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup_{\mathcal{N}}(I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup))
if and only if
\displaystyle\prod_{v\in F}x_{v}\notin I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup)
, meaning it does not belong to at least one prime
\displaystyle(x_{v}:v\in C)
in the intersection. This happens precisely when
\displaystyle F\cap C=\varnothing
for some
\displaystyle C\in\mathrm{MinCov}(\bigtriangleup)
.
Maximal such
\displaystyle F
are the exact complements of minimal covers.
∎

Also, if
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup\cong\varGamma
then
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{(k)}(\bigtriangleup)\cong\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{(k)}(\varGamma)
for all
\displaystyle k
, hence
\displaystyle\mathcal{NF}(\bigtriangleup)=\mathcal{NF}(\varGamma)
. An isomorphism renames variables in
\displaystyle S
and maps facet ideals and Stanley-Reisner complexes to each other.

The following result gives the dimension of
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup)
via minimum cover size.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\vartheta(\bigtriangleup)=\min\{|C|:C\in\mathrm{MinCov}(\bigtriangleup)\}
be the vertex cover number of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
.
Then
\displaystyle\dim\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup)=|V|-\vartheta(\bigtriangleup)-1.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
, facets of
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(\bigtriangleup)
have sizes
\displaystyle|V|-|C|
, for
\displaystyle C\in\mathrm{MinCov}(\bigtriangleup)
.
The largest facet size is
\displaystyle|V|-\vartheta(\bigtriangleup)
, so the dimension is one less.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle G=B_{n,m}
with its vertex set written as

\displaystyle A=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
,
\displaystyle B=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\}
, and the unique bridge edge is
\displaystyle x_{1}y_{1}

Then a subset
\displaystyle C\subseteq V
is a minimal vertex cover of
\displaystyle G
if and only if

\displaystyle C=(A\setminus\{x_{i}\})\cup(B\setminus\{y_{j}\})
for some pair
\displaystyle(i,j)\neq(1,1).

Proof.
Assume
\displaystyle C
is a minimal vertex cover of
\displaystyle G
. We consider the following cases.

Claim 1.

\displaystyle|C\cap A|\geq n-1
and
\displaystyle|C\cap B|\geq m-1
.
Now, to prove it, suppose that
\displaystyle|C\cap A|\leq n-2
. Then there exist two distinct vertices
\displaystyle x_{r},x_{s}\in A\setminus C
.
Since
\displaystyle A
is a clique, the edge
\displaystyle x_{r}x_{s}\in E(G)
, but neither endpoint lies in
\displaystyle C
. So, the edge
\displaystyle x_{r}x_{s}
is not covered, which contradicts the claim that
\displaystyle C
is a vertex cover. Thus, we must have
\displaystyle|C\cap A|\geq n-1
. A similar reason is valid for
\displaystyle B

Claim 2.
There exist indices
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\displaystyle j\in\{1,\dots,m\}

\displaystyle C\cap A=A\setminus\{x_{i}\}
and
\displaystyle C\cap B=B\setminus\{y_{j}\}.
For proof, we note by Claim 1,
\displaystyle C\cap A
contains at least
\displaystyle n-1
vertices of
\displaystyle A
. Hence, either
\displaystyle C\cap A=A
or
\displaystyle C\cap A=A\setminus\{x_{i}\}
for a unique
\displaystyle i
.
First, we show that
\displaystyle C\cap A\neq A
. Suppose, for contradiction, that
\displaystyle C\cap A=A
. Since
\displaystyle n\geq 2
, choose a vertex
\displaystyle x_{t}\in A
with
\displaystyle t\neq 1
, and set
\displaystyle C^{\prime}=C\setminus\{x_{t}\}
. We claim that
\displaystyle C^{\prime}
is still a vertex cover of
\displaystyle G
.
Indeed, every edge inside the clique on
\displaystyle A
that is incident to
\displaystyle x_{t}
has its other endpoint in
\displaystyle A\setminus\{x_{t}\}\subseteq C^{\prime}
. All other edges inside
\displaystyle A
are unaffected. The bridge edge
\displaystyle x_{1}y_{1}
is also unaffected, since
\displaystyle x_{t}\neq x_{1}
. Edges inside the clique on
\displaystyle B
are unchanged as well. Hence, every edge of
\displaystyle G
remains covered by
\displaystyle C^{\prime}
, contradicting the minimality of
\displaystyle C
. Therefore
\displaystyle C\cap A\neq A

\displaystyle C\cap A=A\setminus\{x_{i}\}
for some
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\dots,n\}

Similarly, by Claim 1, either
\displaystyle C\cap B=B

\displaystyle C\cap B=B\setminus\{y_{j}\}
for a unique
\displaystyle j
. If
\displaystyle C\cap B=B

\displaystyle m\geq 2
, choose
\displaystyle y_{s}\in B

\displaystyle s\neq 1

\displaystyle C^{\prime\prime}=C\setminus\{y_{s}\}

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle y_{s}
remains covered by its other endpoint, and the bridge edge
\displaystyle x_{1}y_{1}
is unaffected because
\displaystyle y_{s}\neq y_{1}
. All other edges are unchanged. Thus,
\displaystyle C^{\prime\prime}
is still a vertex cover, contradicting the minimality of
\displaystyle C
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle C\cap B=B\setminus\{y_{j}\}

for some
\displaystyle j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.

Claim 3.

\displaystyle C_{i,j}
is a vertex cover. Every edge of
\displaystyle G
is either:
(i) an edge inside the clique on
\displaystyle A
,
(ii) an edge inside the clique on
\displaystyle B

(iii) the bridge edge
\displaystyle x_{1}y_{1}

For (i), any edge inside the clique on
\displaystyle A
has endpoints
\displaystyle x_{r},x_{s}

\displaystyle C_{i,j}
omits only
\displaystyle x_{i}
from
\displaystyle A
,
at least one of
\displaystyle x_{r},x_{s}

\displaystyle A\setminus\{x_{i}\}\subseteq C_{i,j}

Similarly, all edges of type (ii) are covered.
For (iii), as
\displaystyle(i,j)\neq(1,1)

\displaystyle i\neq 1
or
\displaystyle j\neq 1
holds, hence
\displaystyle x_{1}\in C_{i,j}
or
\displaystyle y_{1}\in C_{i,j}

\displaystyle C_{i,j}

Claim 4.

\displaystyle C_{i,j}
is minimal. Let
\displaystyle v\in C_{i,j}

\displaystyle C_{i,j}\setminus\{v\}
is not a vertex cover.
If
\displaystyle v\in A\setminus\{x_{i}\}

\displaystyle v
is adjacent to
\displaystyle x_{i}
, since
\displaystyle A

Moreover, by construction
\displaystyle x_{i}\notin C_{i,j}

\displaystyle vx_{i}
has neither endpoint in
\displaystyle C_{i,j}\setminus\{v\}
,
so it becomes uncovered. Thus ,removing

\displaystyle vy_{j}

\displaystyle y_{j}\notin C_{i,j}

\displaystyle C_{i,j}

\displaystyle n,m\geq 2
.
Let
\displaystyle H
be the bipartite graph on
\displaystyle A\cup B
whose edge set is

E(H)=\bigl\{x_{i}y_{j}:1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m,(i,j)\neq(1,1)\bigr\}=E(K_{n,m})\setminus\{x_{1}y_{1}\}.

The minimal vertex covers of
\displaystyle H
are precisely the following three sets
\displaystyle A,B,
and
\displaystyle(A\setminus\{x_{1}\})\cup(B\setminus\{y_{1}\}).

Proof.

We verify that each of the three sets stated in assertion is a minimal vertex cover, and then show that no more minimal vertex covers exist. We have the following steps to consider.

1.

\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
are minimal vertex covers. Every edge of
\displaystyle H
has one endpoint in
\displaystyle A
and one endpoint in
\displaystyle B

\displaystyle A
meets every edge, resulting in a vertex cover. Similarly,
\displaystyle B
represents a vertex cover.
To demonstrate that
\displaystyle A
is minimum, consider
\displaystyle A^{\prime}=A\setminus\{x_{i}\}
.
Since
\displaystyle m\geq 2
, select an index
\displaystyle j\in\{1,\dots,m\}

\displaystyle j\neq 1
(for instance,
\displaystyle j=2

Then
\displaystyle(i,j)\neq(1,1)

\displaystyle x_{i}y_{j}\in E(H)

\displaystyle x_{i}\notin A^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle A^{\prime}
has no
\displaystyle y

\displaystyle A^{\prime}
does not intersect the edge
\displaystyle x_{i}y_{j}
. Consequently,
\displaystyle A
is minimum since
\displaystyle A^{\prime}
is not a vertex cover.
The same reasoning (with
\displaystyle n\geq 2
) demonstrates that
\displaystyle B
is minimal, since eliminating any
\displaystyle y_{j}
leaves uncovered an edge
\displaystyle x_{2}y_{j}
.

2.

\displaystyle C_{0}:=(A\setminus\{x_{1}\})\cup(B\setminus\{y_{1}\})
is a minimal vertex cover. For cover property, let
\displaystyle e=x_{i}y_{j}
be any arbitrary edge in
\displaystyle H
. Then
\displaystyle(i,j)\neq(1,1)

\displaystyle i\neq 1

\displaystyle j\neq 1

If
\displaystyle i\neq 1

\displaystyle x_{i}\in A\setminus\{x_{1}\}\subseteq C_{0}

\displaystyle e

If
\displaystyle i=1
, then necessarily
\displaystyle j\neq 1
(since
\displaystyle(i,j)\neq(1,1)
), and thus
\displaystyle y_{j}\in B\setminus\{y_{1}\}\subseteq C_{0}
,
so again
\displaystyle e
is covered. Hence
\displaystyle C_{0}
is a vertex cover.

For minimality, we demonstrate how deleting any vertex from
\displaystyle C_{0}
breaks the cover property.
If
\displaystyle v=x_{i}
with
\displaystyle i\geq 2
, then consider the edge
\displaystyle x_{i}y_{1}

\displaystyle i\geq 2
, we have
\displaystyle(i,1)\neq(1,1)

\displaystyle x_{i}y_{1}\in E(H)

However,
\displaystyle y_{1}\notin C_{0}

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle x_{i}y_{1}
lies in
\displaystyle C_{0}\setminus\{x_{i}\}

Similarly, if
\displaystyle v=y_{j}

\displaystyle j\geq 2

\displaystyle x_{1}y_{j}

\displaystyle E(H)

\displaystyle(1,j)\neq(1,1)

\displaystyle x_{1}\notin C_{0}

\displaystyle y_{j}

\displaystyle x_{1}y_{j}

\displaystyle C_{0}

3.
No other minimal vertex covers exist. Let
\displaystyle C
be a minimal vertex cover of
\displaystyle H.
If
\displaystyle C=A

\displaystyle C=B

\displaystyle C\neq A
and
\displaystyle C\neq B
.
Then
\displaystyle C
omits at least one vertex from
\displaystyle A
and at least one vertex from
\displaystyle B

\displaystyle x_{i}\in A\setminus C
and
\displaystyle y_{j}\in B\setminus C

\displaystyle i=1
and
\displaystyle j=1
.
If
\displaystyle i\neq 1

\displaystyle x_{i}
is adjacent in
\displaystyle H
to every vertex of
\displaystyle B

\displaystyle x_{1}y_{1}
.
Since
\displaystyle x_{i}\notin C
and
\displaystyle C
must meet every edge incident to
\displaystyle x_{i}

every

\displaystyle y\in B

\displaystyle C

Hence
\displaystyle B\subseteq C

\displaystyle C=B

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle C\neq B

\displaystyle i=1

\displaystyle x_{1}\notin C

\displaystyle j\neq 1

\displaystyle y_{j}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle C=A

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle C_{0}

Now, we have the following result, which presents the second NF-iterate of the dumbbell.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\displaystyle G=B_{n,m}
on
\displaystyle V=A\cup B
. Then
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{(2)}(G)
has exactly three facets

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\!\left(\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{(2)}(G)\right)=\{A,B,\{x_{1},y_{1}\}\}.

Proof.

As
\displaystyle G

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\bigtriangleup(G)

\displaystyle H\cong K_{n,m}\setminus\{x_{1}y_{1}\},

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle V=A\cup B

\displaystyle E(H)=\{x_{i}y_{j}:1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m,(i,j)\neq(1,1)\}.

Lemma

\mathrm{MinCov}(H)=\Bigl\{A,B,(A\setminus\{x_{1}\})\cup(B\setminus\{y_{1}\})\Bigr\}.

By Lemma

, for any simplicial complex (hence in particular for the graph complex
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup(H)

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(H)=\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{(2)}(G)

\displaystyle C=A

\displaystyle V\setminus C=(A\cup B)\setminus A=B

\displaystyle C=B

\displaystyle B

\mathcal{F}\!\left(\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{(2)}(G)\right)=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(H))=\{B,A,\{x_{1},y_{1}\}\}=\{A,B,\{x_{1},y_{1}\}\}.

∎

4
NF-complexes and NF-numbers of the complete split graph

The complete split graph with clique number
\displaystyle n\geq 2
and independence number
\displaystyle m\geq 1
is defined as
\displaystyle S_{n,m}:=K_{n}\vee\overline{K}_{m}
. Its vertex set is a disjoint union
\displaystyle V=A\cup B,
where
\displaystyle A=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},
and
\displaystyle B=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\}
. We consider
\displaystyle S_{n,m}
to be a one-dimensional simplicial complex on the ground set
\displaystyle V
. We begin with a detailed discussion of minimal vertex covers in the split graph
\displaystyle S_{n,m}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle G=S_{n,m}
on
\displaystyle V=A\cup B
as above.
Then the minimal vertex covers of
\displaystyle G
are precisely
\displaystyle A,
and
\displaystyle(A\setminus\{x_{i}\})\cup B
for
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,n.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle C\subseteq V
be a vertex cover of
\displaystyle G
. We consider the following cases.

(1) Covering the clique on
\displaystyle A
: Since
\displaystyle G[A]\cong K_{n}
, the set
\displaystyle C\cap A
must contain at least
\displaystyle n-1
vertices of
\displaystyle A
. If
\displaystyle C
omits two distinct vertices
\displaystyle x_{i},x_{j}\in A
, then the clique edge
\displaystyle x_{i}x_{j}
would have both endpoints outside
\displaystyle C
, contradicting that
\displaystyle C
covers all edges. Thus,
\displaystyle|C\cap A|\geq n-1
.

(2) If
\displaystyle C\cap A=A
, then
\displaystyle C=A
is minimal: If
\displaystyle A\subseteq C
, then every edge of
\displaystyle G
is incident to some vertex of
\displaystyle A
, so
\displaystyle A
itself is a vertex cover. Moreover
\displaystyle A
is minimal, as for any
\displaystyle x_{i}\in A
, if we remove
\displaystyle x_{i}
from
\displaystyle A
, then the edges
\displaystyle x_{i}y_{j}

(for any
\displaystyle j
) are no longer covered because
\displaystyle y_{j}\notin A
and
\displaystyle x_{i}
has been removed.
Hence, no proper subset of
\displaystyle A
is a vertex cover, so
\displaystyle A
is a minimal vertex cover.

(3) If
\displaystyle C\cap A=A\setminus\{x_{i}\}
, then
\displaystyle B\subseteq C
: Now, assume that
\displaystyle|C\cap A|=n-1
, so
\displaystyle C\cap A=A\setminus\{x_{i}\}
for a unique
\displaystyle i
.
If any
\displaystyle y_{j}\in B
, then the edge
\displaystyle x_{i}y_{j}
exists (all
\displaystyle A
–
\displaystyle B
edges exist).
Since
\displaystyle x_{i}\notin C
, to cover
\displaystyle x_{i}y_{j}
we must have
\displaystyle y_{j}\in C
.
As
\displaystyle j
was arbitrary, so
\displaystyle B\subseteq C
follows.

(4) Minimality forces no extra vertices: By (3), any vertex cover with
\displaystyle|C\cap A|=n-1
must contain

\displaystyle(A\setminus\{x_{i}\})\cup B
.
This set is itself a vertex cover as clique edges in
\displaystyle A
are covered since
all except one vertex of
\displaystyle A
are present, and every edge incident to
\displaystyle B
is covered because all vertices of
\displaystyle B
are included.
It is minimal by inclusion, as removing any
\displaystyle y_{j}
exposes the uncovered edge
\displaystyle x_{i}y_{j}
(since
\displaystyle x_{i}\notin C
),
and removing any
\displaystyle x_{k}\in A\setminus\{x_{i}\}
exposes the clique edge
\displaystyle x_{k}x_{i}
. Therefore, the only minimal covers of this type are exactly

\displaystyle(A\setminus\{x_{i}\})\cup B
. Now, combining (2) and (4) gives the given list.
∎

Applying Lemma
4.1
, we have the following result, which presents the facets of
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup)
, which
are the complements of minimal vertex covers of simplicial complex
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
on ground set
\displaystyle V
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup(G)
be the graph complex of
\displaystyle G=S_{n,m}
.
Then
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup(G))
has facets

\mathcal{F}\bigl(\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup(G))\bigr)=\{\,B\,\}\cup\bigl\{\{x_{1}\},\dots,\{x_{n}\}\bigr\}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
, the minimal vertex covers are

\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle(A\setminus\{x_{i}\})\cup B
.
Taking complements in
\displaystyle V=A\cup B
, we get
\displaystyle V\setminus A=B,
and
\displaystyle V\setminus\bigl((A\setminus\{x_{i}\})\cup B\bigr)=\{x_{i}\}.
These complements are inclusion-maximal among themselves, so they are precisely
the facets of
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup(G))
.
∎

A second iterate is described in the following lemma.

Proposition 4.3
.

For
\displaystyle G=S_{n,m}
, the complex
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}^{(2)}(\bigtriangleup(G))
has facets

\mathcal{F}\bigl(\delta_{NF}^{(2)}(\bigtriangleup(G))\bigr)=\bigl\{\,B\setminus\{y_{j}\}:j=1,\dots,m\,\bigr\}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup_{1}=\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup(G))
.
By Proposition
4.2
, the facets of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup_{1}
are
\displaystyle B
and the singletons

\displaystyle\{x_{i}\}
.
Thus, a subset
\displaystyle C\subseteq V
is a vertex cover of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup_{1}
if and only if it intersects
\displaystyle B

and also intersects every singleton facet
\displaystyle\{x_{i}\}
, that is, if and only if

\displaystyle\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subseteq C
and
\displaystyle C\cap B\neq\emptyset.
Hence, the minimal vertex covers of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup_{1}
are exactly
\displaystyle A\cup\{y_{j}\}
, for
\displaystyle j=1,\dots,m.

With complements in
\displaystyle V
, we obtain
\displaystyle V\setminus(A\cup\{y_{j}\})=B\setminus\{y_{j}\},
and these are maximal under inclusion among the obtained complements. Therefore, they are the facets of
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup_{1})=\delta_{NF}^{(2)}(\bigtriangleup(G))
.
∎

Let
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup(G)
denote the edge complex of
\displaystyle G=S_{n,m}
. Write
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{A}
for the full symmetric group on the set
\displaystyle A
(all permutations of the
\displaystyle x_{i}
’s), and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{B}
for the full symmetric group on
\displaystyle B
(all permutations of the
\displaystyle y_{j}
’s). The direct product
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{A}\times\mathfrak{S}_{B}

acts on
\displaystyle V
by permuting vertices inside
\displaystyle A
and inside
\displaystyle B
independently.
This action preserves adjacency in
\displaystyle S_{n,m}
(it preserves clique edges in
\displaystyle A
, preserves
the fact that there are no edges inside
\displaystyle B
, and preserves all cross edges), hence it is a subgroup
of the automorphism group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aut}(G)
. Consequently, it acts on
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup(G)
and on every
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}^{(k)}(\bigtriangleup(G))

by relabeling vertices.

The following lemma gives the equivariance of
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}
under relabeling.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\displaystyle\pi
be a permutation of
\displaystyle V
and let
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
be a simplicial complex on
\displaystyle V
.
Then
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}(\pi(\bigtriangleup))=\pi(\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup))
. In particular, if
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
is invariant
under a group of permutations
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
, then so is
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup)
and every iterate

\displaystyle\delta_{NF}^{(k)}(\bigtriangleup)
.

Proof.

By definition,
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup)=\bigtriangleup_{\mathcal{N}}(I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup))
, where
\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup)
is the facet ideal
and
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup_{\mathcal{N}}(\cdot)
is the Stanley–Reisner complex. Relabeling vertices by
\displaystyle\pi
sends each facet

\displaystyle F
to
\displaystyle\pi(F)
and hence sends each generator
\displaystyle\prod_{v\in F}v
of
\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup)
to the generator

\displaystyle\prod_{v\in\pi(F)}v
of
\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{F}}(\pi(\bigtriangleup))
. Thus,
\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{F}}(\pi(\bigtriangleup))=\pi(I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup))
, and taking
Stanley–Reisner complexes commutes with relabeling, so we have

\delta_{NF}(\pi(\bigtriangleup))=\bigtriangleup_{N}(I_{\mathcal{F}}(\pi(\bigtriangleup)))=\bigtriangleup_{N}(\pi(I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup)))=\pi(\bigtriangleup_{N}(I_{\mathcal{F}}(\bigtriangleup)))=\pi(\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup)).

∎

For any subset
\displaystyle F\subseteq V

by

\mathrm{type}(F):=(|F\cap A|,|F\cap B|)\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}\times\{0,1,\dots,m\}.

For integers
\displaystyle 0\leq i\leq n

\displaystyle 0\leq j\leq m

Note that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{A}\times\mathfrak{S}_{B}
acts transitively on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(i,j)

\displaystyle i
vertices in
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle j

\displaystyle B

The following lemma shows that invariant complexes have facet sets that are unions of type-classes.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
be a simplicial complex on
\displaystyle V=A\cup B
that is invariant under

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{A}\times\mathfrak{S}_{B}
. If
\displaystyle F
is a facet of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
of type
\displaystyle(i,j)
,
then
every
set in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(i,j)
is a facet of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
. Hence there exists
a set
\displaystyle\alpha\subseteq[0,n]\times[0,m]
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bigtriangleup)=\bigcup_{(i,j)\in\alpha}\mathcal{M}(i,j).

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle F
be a facet of type
\displaystyle(i,j)
. For any
\displaystyle F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(i,j)
, transitivity gives a

\displaystyle\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{A}\times\mathfrak{S}_{B}
with
\displaystyle\pi(F)=F^{\prime}
. Since
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
is invariant,

\displaystyle\pi
maps facets to facets, so
\displaystyle F^{\prime}
is a facet. Therefore, all of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(i,j)
lies in

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bigtriangleup)
. Taking
\displaystyle\alpha
to be the set of types appearing among facets yields the

∎

Put the product (componentwise) partial order on
\displaystyle[0,n]\times[0,m]

\displaystyle(i,j)\leq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
if and only if
\displaystyle i\leq i^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle j\leq j^{\prime}.

A subset
\displaystyle\alpha\subseteq[0,n]\times[0,m]

\displaystyle\alpha

are comparable under this order. The following lemma shows that facet-type sets are antichains.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
be invariant under
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{A}\times\mathfrak{S}_{B}
and write

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bigtriangleup)=\bigcup_{(i,j)\in\alpha}\mathcal{M}(i,j)
as in Lemma
4.5
.
Then
\displaystyle\alpha
is an antichain.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that
\displaystyle(i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\alpha
with
\displaystyle(i,j)<(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
componentwise, so

\displaystyle i\leq i^{\prime}
,
\displaystyle j\leq j^{\prime}
, and at least one inequality is strict. Choose any facet
\displaystyle F\in\mathcal{M}(i,j)
and any set
\displaystyle F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
. Then as
\displaystyle i\leq i^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle j\leq j^{\prime}
, we can extend
\displaystyle F
to some set
\displaystyle\widetilde{F}
of type
\displaystyle(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})

by adding
\displaystyle(i^{\prime}-i)
vertices from
\displaystyle A\setminus F
and
\displaystyle(j^{\prime}-j)
vertices from
\displaystyle B\setminus F
.
Thus,
\displaystyle F\subsetneq\widetilde{F}
and
\displaystyle\widetilde{F}\in\mathcal{M}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
. By Lemma
4.5
, every set in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
is a facet, so in particular

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}
is a facet. But then
\displaystyle F
cannot be a facet (it is properly contained in the face

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}
), contradicting that
\displaystyle F
was a facet. Hence, no such comparable pair exists and

\displaystyle\alpha
is an antichain.

Covers in terms of types and rowmotion.

Let
\displaystyle P:=[0,n]\times[0,m]
with the product order.
Define the involution
\displaystyle\varsigma:P\to P

\displaystyle\varsigma(a,b):=(n-a,m-b).

For an antichain
\displaystyle\psi\subseteq P

\displaystyle I(\psi)\subseteq P

Define
rowmotion
on antichains by
\displaystyle\rho(\psi):=\min\bigl(P\setminus I(\psi)\bigr),

the set of minimal elements of the complement of the ideal. Next, we have as result showing that the vertex-cover types are complements of an ideal.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
be invariant under
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{A}\times\mathfrak{S}_{B}
and suppose

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bigtriangleup)=\bigcup_{(i,j)\in\alpha}\mathcal{M}(i,j)
with
\displaystyle\alpha
an antichain.
Let
\displaystyle C\subseteq V
and set
\displaystyle\mathrm{type}(C)=(a,b)
. Then
\displaystyle C
is a vertex cover of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
if and only if
\displaystyle(a,b)\notin I(\varsigma(\alpha)).

Moreover, the set of
minimal
vertex cover types is exactly
\displaystyle\beta(\bigtriangleup)=\rho(\varsigma(\alpha)).

Proof.

For a fixed
\displaystyle C
of type
\displaystyle(a,b)
. The set
\displaystyle C
fails to be a vertex cover if and only if there exists a facet

\displaystyle F
disjoint from
\displaystyle C
. Since facets run over all sets in each type-class, such an
\displaystyle F
exists
with
\displaystyle\mathrm{type}(F)=(i,j)\in\alpha
if and only if we can choose
\displaystyle i
vertices of
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle j
vertices of
\displaystyle B

entirely from
\displaystyle V\setminus C
, that is, if and only if
\displaystyle i\leq|A\setminus C|=n-a,
and
\displaystyle j\leq|B\setminus C|=m-b.
Equivalently,
\displaystyle(a,b)\leq(n-i,m-j)=\varsigma(i,j)
. Thus, we have
\displaystyle C
is not a vertex cover if and only if thee exists
\displaystyle(i,j)\in\alpha
with
\displaystyle(a,b)\leq\varsigma(i,j)
if and only if
\displaystyle(a,b)\in I(\varsigma(\alpha)).

This proves the first claim. For minimality, among all cover types, the minimal ones are precisely the minimal elements of

\displaystyle P\setminus I(\varsigma(\alpha))
(if
\displaystyle(a,b)
is not minimal there is a strictly smaller cover type,
hence some cover
\displaystyle C^{\prime}
properly contained in
\displaystyle C
exists). Thus, by definition of rowmotion, we have

\min(P\setminus I(\varsigma(\alpha)))=\rho(\varsigma(\alpha)).

So, the minimal vertex cover types form
\displaystyle\beta(\bigtriangleup)=\rho(\varsigma(\alpha))
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

With hypotheses as above, if
\displaystyle\alpha(\bigtriangleup)
is the facet-type antichain of
\displaystyle\bigtriangleup
, then
the facet-type antichain of
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup)

\displaystyle\alpha(\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup))=\varsigma(\rho(\varsigma(\alpha(\bigtriangleup)))).

As the facets of
\displaystyle\delta_{NF}(\bigtriangleup)
are complements of minimal vertex covers.
At the level of types, complementation sends
\displaystyle(a,b)
to
\displaystyle\varsigma(a,b)
. So, by Lemma

,

\displaystyle\rho(\varsigma(\alpha(\bigtriangleup)))
. Taking complements yields the required formula.
∎

In
\displaystyle P=[0,n]\times[0,m]

\displaystyle(0,m+1)

\displaystyle S=(0,-1)

\displaystyle(m+1)
letters
\displaystyle S

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\displaystyle\psi
be an antichain in
\displaystyle P
and set
\displaystyle J=I(\psi)
.
Then
\displaystyle I(\rho(\psi))
has boundary word obtained from
\displaystyle w(J)
by cyclic rotation by one letter
\displaystyle w(I(\rho(\psi)))=\mathrm{rot}(w(J)),
where
\displaystyle\mathrm{rot}
moves the first letter of the word to the end.

Proof.

By the above boundary-corner description, the minimal elements of
\displaystyle P\setminus J
are precisely the
inner corners
of the Ferrers boundary of
\displaystyle J
. A point
\displaystyle(i,j)\notin J
is minimal outside
\displaystyle J
if and only if
\displaystyle(i-1,j)\in J
and
\displaystyle(i,j-1)\in J
(when defined),
which is exactly the local pattern of an
\displaystyle ES
corner on the boundary word.

Rowmotion replaces the generating antichain of
\displaystyle J
by those inner corners, that is, it shifts the cut point on the cyclic boundary word forward by one boundary step. Concretely, if one reads the boundary as a cyclic word,
choosing a different starting point changes
\displaystyle w(J)
by rotation. The update
\displaystyle J\mapsto I(\rho(\psi))
advances

\displaystyle\mathrm{rot}
. ∎

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle D_{p,q}
be the double star. Then the family of minimal vertex covers of
\displaystyle D_{p,q}
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{MinCov}(D_{p,q})=\bigl\{\,\{u,v\},\{u\}\cup B,\{v\}\cup A\,\bigr\}.

Proof.

Recall that a set
\displaystyle C\subseteq V
is a vertex cover if every edge of
\displaystyle D_{p,q}
has at least one endpoint in
\displaystyle C
.
We prove two that each of the sets
\displaystyle\bigl\{\,\{u,v\},\{u\}\cup B,\{v\}\cup A\,\bigr\}
is a minimal vertex cover, and every minimal vertex cover is one of these three sets. Indeed
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
is a vertex cover, since every edge of
\displaystyle D_{p,q}
is incident to either
\displaystyle u
or
\displaystyle v
. The edges
\displaystyle\{u,a_{i}\}
are incident to
\displaystyle u
, the edges
\displaystyle\{v,b_{j}\}
are incident to
\displaystyle v
, and the edge
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
is incident to both. Hence
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
meets every edge. The set
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
is minimal, as if we remove
\displaystyle u
, then the edge
\displaystyle\{u,a_{1}\}
(or any
\displaystyle\{u,a_{i}\}
) is uncovered because neither endpoint lies in
\displaystyle\{v\}
. If we remove
\displaystyle v
, then the edge
\displaystyle\{v,b_{1}\}
is uncovered since neither endpoint lies in
\displaystyle\{u\}
. Thus, no proper subset of
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
is a vertex cover.

Another vertex cover set is
\displaystyle\{u\}\cup B
, as it covers the bridge edge
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
because it contains
\displaystyle u
.
It covers every edge
\displaystyle\{u,a_{i}\}
because it contains
\displaystyle u
. Finally, it covers every edge
\displaystyle\{v,b_{j}\}
since it contains
\displaystyle b_{j}\in B
(for each
\displaystyle j
). So,
\displaystyle\{u\}\cup B
is a vertex cover.
If we remove
\displaystyle u
, then
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
becomes uncovered as
\displaystyle v\notin\{u\}\cup B
. If we remove some
\displaystyle b_{j}\in B
, then the edge
\displaystyle\{v,b_{j}\}
becomes uncovered because
\displaystyle v\notin\{u\}\cup(B\setminus\{b_{j}\})
. Thus, no vertex can be removed while preserving the vertex cover property, so
\displaystyle\{u\}\cup B
is minimal. The same argument (swapping the roles of
\displaystyle u,A
with
\displaystyle v,B
) shows
\displaystyle\{v\}\cup A
is a minimal vertex cover.

Let
\displaystyle C\subseteq V
be a minimal vertex cover of
\displaystyle D_{p,q}
. Then we show that
\displaystyle C
is one of the above listed sets. We consider the following cases.

(1). If
\displaystyle u\notin C
, then for each
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, the edge
\displaystyle\{u,a_{i}\}
must be covered by
\displaystyle a_{i}
, so
\displaystyle a_{i}\in C
.
Hence
\displaystyle A\subseteq C
.
Also the edge
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
must be covered, since
\displaystyle u\notin C
, it follows that
\displaystyle v\in C
.
Therefore, we obtain
\displaystyle\{v\}\cup A\subseteq C.
But as proved above,
\displaystyle\{v\}\cup A
is itself a vertex cover. So, by minimality of
\displaystyle C
we must have equality
\displaystyle C=\{v\}\cup A.

(2). If
\displaystyle v\notin C
, then by the symmetric argument, each edge
\displaystyle\{v,b_{j}\}
forces
\displaystyle b_{j}\in C
, so
\displaystyle B\subseteq C
,
and the edge
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
forces
\displaystyle u\in C
. Thus,
\displaystyle\{u\}\cup B\subseteq C
, and minimality implies
\displaystyle C=\{u\}\cup B
.

(3). If
\displaystyle u\in C
and
\displaystyle v\in C
, then we show that minimality forces
\displaystyle C=\{u,v\}
. Assume for contradiction that
\displaystyle C
contains some additional vertex
\displaystyle z\in C\setminus\{u,v\}
. Then
\displaystyle z\in A\cup B
. Now, consider
\displaystyle C^{\prime}:=C\setminus\{z\}
.
We claim
\displaystyle C^{\prime}
is still a vertex cover, and thereby we get contradiction to the minimality of
\displaystyle C
. Indeed, the only edges incident to
\displaystyle z
are: if
\displaystyle z=a_{i}\in A
, then the only incident edge is
\displaystyle\{u,a_{i}\}
, which is still covered by
\displaystyle u\in C^{\prime}
; and if
\displaystyle z=b_{j}\in B
, then the only incident edge is
\displaystyle\{v,b_{j}\}
, which is still covered by
\displaystyle v\in C^{\prime}
. All other edges are unchanged by removing
\displaystyle z
. Hence
\displaystyle C^{\prime}
remains a vertex cover, a contradiction.
Therefore, no such
\displaystyle z
exists and
\displaystyle C=\{u,v\}
. Thus, every minimal vertex cover
\displaystyle C
is exactly one of

\displaystyle\{u,v\}
,
\displaystyle\{u\}\cup B
,
\displaystyle\{v\}\cup A
, completing the proof.
∎

6
Conclusion

We gave detailed, self-contained foundations for NF-complexes and NF-numbers, and proved explicit new formulas for

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}(B_{n,m})
and
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}^{(2)}(B_{n,m})
. These computations show that dumbbells map first to an almost complete bipartite graph, then to a rigid three-facet complex. This structure strongly suggests the sharp conjecture

\displaystyle\mathcal{NF}(B_{n,m})=n+m+2
(outside the smallest exceptional case), and we verified it computationally for
\displaystyle 2\leq n,m\leq 5
.
A complete proof appears to require a full parameterization of the orbit, analogous to the orbit analysis for
\displaystyle K_{n}\cup K_{m}
. Theorem
4.10
gives the NF-number of complete split graph, and Theorem
5.7
gives the NF-number of double star. However, NF-number of general graphs remains yet to be discussed, and other homological invariants of NF-complexes can be investigated in future. It would be valuable to complete the validation of the dumbbell conjecture. Second, one may ask which graph families admit a rowmotion model similar to the one used here for complete split graphs and double stars. Natural candidates may include threshold graphs, complete multipartite graphs, block graphs, chordal graphs, and trees of small diameter. Such a classification would help explain when NF-periods are governed by simple rotation phenomena. Third, the NF-number of general trees remains largely open. Double stars provide the first nontrivial step beyond paths and stars, but more complicated trees may have orbits with richer intermediate complexes. A systematic study of how branching affects the NF-orbit could lead to new formulas or useful bounds. Fourth, the labelled and unlabelled versions of the NF-number deserve further attention. In small examples, especially those related to
\displaystyle P_{4}
, the first return may depend on whether one works on a fixed labelled vertex set or identifies complexes up to isomorphism at each stage. Clarifying this distinction may help reconcile different conventions in the literature. Fifth, it would be interesting to study algebraic and topological invariants along the NF-orbit. For example, one may ask how dimension,
\displaystyle f
-vectors, Betti numbers, shellability, Cohen–Macaulayness, and homotopy type change under repeated application of
\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{NF}}
. Since the NF-operator is defined through the facet ideal and the Stanley–Reisner complex, these questions naturally connect combinatorial dynamics with combinatorial commutative algebra. Finally, there is room for computational development. An efficient implementation of NF-iteration, based on minimal transversals and vertex-cover enumeration, could be used to build a database of NF-numbers for small graphs and simplicial complexes. Such data may reveal new patterns, suggest additional conjectures, and provide test cases for future theoretical work. Overall, the results of this paper show that even for familiar graph families, the NF-operator produces rich and structured periodic behaviour. The interaction between vertex covers, simplicial complexes, and rowmotion appears to be a promising framework for further study.
