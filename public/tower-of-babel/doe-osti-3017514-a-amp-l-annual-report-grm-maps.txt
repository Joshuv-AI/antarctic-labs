1 Introduction
Exploding Bridge Wire (EBW) is an important design for initiating detonators relevant to several LLNL
systems. In such designs, a metal bridge wire is in direct contact with a porous secondary high explosive
(HE), typically pressed to around 50% of its crystalline density. Initiation is believed to occur through shock
waves generated when the bridge wire is made to explode via the rapid passing of a strong current pulse.
Permeametry is an important characterization tool for the porous powdered HE (PETN, HMX, etc.)
component in such devices. Such powders are well known to coarsen with age, with an accompanying
deterioration in performance, which includes increased function time and sensitivity. 1,2,3,4 Among various
material characteristics, one that has been shown to correlate the most with such performance
parameters is the flow-permeable surface area (FSSA), measured using air-permeametry. Commercially
available permeametry apparatuses, such as that from Fisher Scientific or a more modern version from
Micromeritics suffer from a few drawbacks, most notably as related to monitoring age-related changes in
FSSA. This is because it requires re-compaction of the aged powder into the permeametry tube, which in
most cases leads to an unwanted increase in FSSA. To address this and a few other shortcomings, we have
been developing a modern version of the permeameter, Gee-Reinstein-Maiti Modern Air-Permeametry
System (GRM-MAPS). In this report, we summarize progress made in FY25 toward perfecting and
calibrating the instrument, which we aim at deploying within surveillance in FY26.

where ε, η, Q, ∆p, are, respectively, the average powder porosity, dynamic viscosity of air, volume flow
rate through the sample, and pressure drop across the sample bed, while L, A are the length and cross-
section of the sample (packed in a cylindrical holder), and k is a constant (known as the ‘aspect factor’)

commercial Fisher instrument manual, GRM-MAPS employs an aspect factor of k = 5.0.
According to Eq. (1), the measurement accuracy depends on the reliability of determination of the flow
conductance Q/∆p across the powder sample. To this end, we made significant progress over the last
couple of years, including: (1) a customizable sample holder (that could be non-cylindrical, e.g.,
hemispherical in shape, and work with as low as tens of milligrams of sample powder) that enables in-situ
evaluation; (2) three separate flow meters, each meter having the capacity to accurately resolve flow rates
in the range 0-4 SCCM, 0-10 SCCM, and 0-50 SCCM; (3) an LFPR (low flow pressure regulator) that is used
to precisely control the air flow-rate; (4) a pressure transducer (that accurately measures pressure drop
across the sample); (5) an easy-to-use GUI (run by LabView software) where the user can enter all
additional input (e.g., material name, sample ID, crystal density, powder porosity, etc.). With this system
inlet pressures, flows, and pressure drop readings across the sample are simultaneously monitored,
recorded, and averaged during operation. The data from these instruments are routed through an Analog
output device to a DAQ controller, which uses the Carman-Kozeny equation to determine the particle size
dCK. In the process we satisfactorily meet L2 milestone 8651, GC#3, i.e., “Complete a working assembly of
the modified flow specific surface area (GRM-MAPS) instrument.”
In FY24, we systematically calibrated all flow meters and pressure differential transducers. This was meant
to ensure that the collected data is measured against a NIST traceable standard to provide confidence in
the test data. Extensive validations of GRM-MAPS (vs. commercial Fisher instrument) were carried out on
cylindrical samples of diameter 6.35 mm and length 3.71 mm that can hold ~ 110 mg of PETN powder at
~ 47% porosity, 8 and excellent agreement was obtained (see Fig. 1 below).

2.2 Progress in FY25
Prior to deployment of GRM-MAPS into surveillance, the extension of its validation on two more systems
was desired in order to enhance its impact on several LLNL systems, i.e., (1) a hemispherical sample-holder
for PETN (containing ~ 110 mg of powder), and (2) a much smaller cylindrical sample-holder for HMX
(containing ~ 35 mg of powder). Both these projects began earlier but were primarily carried out in FY25.
For the hemispherical (HEMI) system we have explored various designs for input and output inlets (e.g.,
different diameter holes, multiple holes on the outlet side, etc.). From extensive experimentation (as
documented through periodic EIA reports throughout FY25) we have settled on the design displayed
below (Fig. 2). This designhas enabled much more uniform packing of the needle-like PETN powder in the
hemispherical enclosure, and prevented unwanted density gradients. The last piece of the puzzle was an
appropriate calibration of the aspect factor (k) and/or the cross-sectional area (A). We decided to maintain
k = 5.0 (as for cylindrical geometry) and determine an ‘effective’ A, so that the particle size dCK (according
to Eq. (1)) agrees with that obtained for the same powder lot when measured in the cylindrical enclosures.
