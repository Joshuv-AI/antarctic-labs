LLaTiSA : Towards Difficulty-Stratified Time Series Reasoning from Visual Perception to Semantics
Yueyang Ding, HaoPeng Zhang, Rui Dai, Yi Wang

Abstract
Comprehensive understanding of time series remains a significant challenge for Large Language Models (LLMs). Current research is hindered by fragmented task definitions and benchmarks with inherent ambiguities, precluding rigorous evaluation and the development of unified Time Series Reasoning Models (TSRMs). To bridge this gap, we formalize Time Series Reasoning (TSR) via a four-level taxonomy of increasing cognitive complexity. We introduce HiTSR , a hi erarchical t ime s eries r easoning dataset comprising 83k samples with diverse task combinations and verified Chain-of-Thought (CoT) trajectories. Leveraging HiTSR , we propose LLaTiSA , a strong TSRM that integrates visualized patterns with precision-calibrated numerical tables to enhance the temporal perception of Vision-Language Models (VLMs). Through a multi-stage curriculum fine-tuning strategy, LLaTiSA achieves superior performance and exhibits robust out-of-distribution generalization across diverse TSR tasks and real-world scenarios. Our code is available at https://github.com/RainingNovember/LLaTiSA .

2
Related Works

2.1
Time Series Reasoning Datasets

Recently released multimodal time series datasets adopt various types of TSR tasks. For general question-answering, early efforts like TSQA
Kong et al. 2025
and TRQA
Jing et al. 2025
explore LLMs’ potential in question-answering and traditional time series analysis tasks. SciTS
Wu et al. 2025
introduces a benchmark specifically designed for scientific time series analysis across a wide range of scientific domains. RATs40k
Yang et al. 2025b
transforms classical time series anomaly detection from discriminative into a reasoning-intensive task and annotates samples with explanatory reasoning. TimeMMD
Liu et al. 2024a
is tailored for the context-aware time series forecasting task. MMTS-Bench
Yin et al. 2026
provides a multi-dimensional task classification taxonomy, yet it overlooks evaluating models’ basic numerical read-out and combining diverse semantics of real scenarios. In addition to insufficient context, current benchmarks are further hampered by semantic ambiguities in label definitions and low-fidelity question-answer pairs
Guan et al. 2025
. Moreover, most of the datasets focus on either pattern description or complex reasoning scenarios such as forecasting, and overlook evaluating models’ basic ability of read-out numerical values. Our findings suggest that current models even struggle with finding point-level numerical evidence, suffering from overthinking or hallucinations with longer time horizons.

2.2
Time Series Reasoning Models

Understanding time series via language models generally mostly follows three encoding paradigms: (i) direct serialization into text tokens, (ii) visual rendering as plots for VLMs, and (iii) the integration of dedicated time-series encoders to construct Time-Series Multimodal Large Language Models (TS-MLLMs). TimeOmni-1
Guan et al. 2025
and Time-R1
Luo et al. 2025
employ textual inputs for context-aware forecasting. ChatTS
Xie et al. 2025b
and ITFormer
Wang et al. 2025b
incorporate an MLP-based and a Q-former
Li et al. 2023
style time series encoder to perform multivariate TSR tasks, respectively. TimeMaster
Zhang et al. 2025a
enables VLMs to perform interpretable classification based on visualized time series. While recent studies point out that TSRMs struggle with basic TSR tasks like description differentiation
Sen et al. 2025
, VLMs have demonstrated superior robustness compared to their LLM and TS-MLLM counterparts. This observation motivates us to further unlock the potential of VLMs for general-purpose TSR scenarios.

3
Method

3.1
Time Series Reasoning Taxonomy

We argue that reliable TSR is inherently compositional, necessitating a hierarchy that can: (i) faithfully ground observations to timestamps, (ii) abstract salient temporal patterns beyond point-level evidence, and (iii) integrate with contextual knowledge to support semantic judgment and prediction.
In contrast, prior TSR benchmarks are typically organized around isolated tasks and adopt heterogeneous definitions of what constitutes “reasoning”, often over-emphasizing a narrow subset of problem types (e.g., coarse pattern discrimination).
Such fragmentation leads to two limitations. First, cross-benchmark comparability is compromised because mismatched capability sets and inconsistent task difficulties prevent a unified assessment of progress. Second, current TSRMs often attempt to perform high-level reasoning (e.g., forecasting) on an unsteady foundation: models still struggle with elementary grounding and verification, which undermines the credibility and interpretability of more complex reasoning.

To address these limitations, we introduce a difficulty-stratified taxonomy that organizes TSR into progressively increasing levels of complexity. This framework serves as a unified schema for categorizing diverse TSR tasks and provides a diagnostic lens to pinpoint the specific cognitive boundaries of current models. Concretely, we decompose TSR into four hierarchical levels:

•

L1:

Numerical Read-out
. Establish time-aware indexing and point-level numerical retrieval.

•

L2:

Pattern Perception
. Identify and differentiate multi-scale temporal patterns using quantitative evidence.

•

L3:

Semantic Reasoning
. Integrate time series observations with contextual knowledge to perform domain-specific reasoning.

•

L4:

Predictive Inference
. Generate high-fidelity time-series predictions.

Based on such a taxonomy, we summarize current benchmarks into different levels in
Section

A.2
, posing a general standard to task definition and evaluation of TSRMs’ capabilities.

3.2
HiTSR
Dataset

Building on our four-level TSR taxonomy, we introduce
HiTSR
, a unified dataset for both training and evaluation of TSR capabilities across levels L1-L3. Unlike prior benchmarks that may contain ambiguous queries or underspecified context,
HiTSR
dataset is constructed with rigorous validity checks to ensure exact problem statements and sufficient contextual information. Overall,
HiTSR
comprises approximately 83k samples spanning synthetic and real-world time series, annotated and verified via a multi-stage process.

Task Formulation.

Following the proposed taxonomy, we instantiate a set of subtasks within each level to cover complementary facets of the TSR as shown in
Section

C.1
. L1 tasks are formulated as short-answer questions, given that the ground truth can be generated via rule-based methods. Tasks within L2-L3 are structured as multiple-choice (MC) questions to ensure objective and reproducible evaluation. To maintain a strict instruction-following orientation and minimize biases from formatting artifacts, each supervision target is crafted as a complete natural-language statement rather than a concise label. Detailed task definitions and additional examples are provided in
Appendix

C
.
Additionally, we adopt different answer-construction protocols tailored to the degree of output determinacy. For L1 and the
Numerical Perception
subtask in L2, the correct option can be derived by explicit rules from the underlying series; we therefore generate options programmatically under fixed templates. For the two
Pattern Differentiation
subtasks in L2 and the L3
Semantic Understanding
task, the outputs are descriptive and require higher-level interpretation. We construct these items with an LLM-assisted pipeline and subsequently verify by humans to ensure correctness and remove ambiguous cases.

Multi-Stage Verified CoT Annotation.

We adopt distinct annotation and verification pipelines for L2 and L3 tasks. For L2
Pattern Differentiation
, we first prompt GPT-5 with each time series’ visual plot and key statistics (e.g., extreme value-index pairs, spike locations with precise values, and notable local events) to produce numerically aware descriptions of local and global patterns. We then construct multiple-choice questions by sampling distractor options from descriptions of other series.
To ensure reliability, we employ GPT-5 to cross-validate each question, removing ambiguous descriptions and flawed items (e.g., those with non-unique correct options).
Finally, we hold out 10% of the validated pool for evaluation. This subset undergoes a comprehensive human auditing, and only items that pass this rigorous review are retained, yielding a unambiguous test set.
For L3
Semantic Understanding
, we leverage LLMs to generate scenario-aware multiple-choice questions by prompting them with real-world time series and their associated metadata. Each scenario description integrates pattern recognition and numerical grounding, effectively encapsulating the TSRMs’ capabilities across L1-L3.
Each item is subsequently dual-checked by GPT-5 and then reviewed by humans to comprehensively verify that the correct answer is supported by consistent numerical, pattern, and semantic evidence.

3.3
LLaTiSA

Time series can be presented to language models in various formats
Sen et al. 2025
. While purely visual plots effectively convey global shapes and local morphological cues, they often lack the fine-grained numerical precision required for grounded reasoning. Consequently, many TSR methods typically represent time series as textualized numerical sequences
Guan et al. 2025
;
Kong et al. 2025
or integrate LLMs with dedicated time series encoders to develop TS-MLLMs
Xie et al. 2025b
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
. Recent studies, however, indicate that TS-MLLMs frequently underperform relative to general-purpose LLMs or VLMs on L2 TSR tasks, while VLMs are stronger on pattern-centric tasks (e.g., local pattern differentiation)
Sen et al. 2025
, underscoring the advantages of visual representations for L2 reasoning.

To empower VLMs with precise numerical grounding, we propose
LLaTiSA
(
Figure

2
.b), a dual-view input framework that pairs standard time series visualizations with a secondary image rendering the data as a structured index-value table. This design facilitates a progressive interpretation paradigm: the first plot enables macroscopic inspection for overall perception, the image of the structured table enables point-accurate verification of specific indices or intervals. By synthesizing these dual modalities alongside textual context, the model produces semantically grounded reasoning. Empirically, compared to processing series as textual arrays,
LLaTiSA
provides extra index information, thereby significantly mitigating numerical hallucinations and improving performance on numerical-sensitive tasks.

Leveraging
HiTSR
,
LLaTiSA
is trained via a three-stage curriculum designed to progressively acquire the prerequisite capabilities defined in our taxonomy.
During the initial two stages, we perform sequential Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on
HiTSR-L1
and
HiTSR-L2
to consolidate the model’s numerical read-out precision and pattern perception capabilities.
In the third stage,
LLaTiSA
could be either trained on
HiTSR-L3
or general-purpose domain-specific TSR benchmarks. Our experiments further show that existing TSRMs often fail even on basic L1-L2 TSR, and struggle to combine LLM priors with series-level evidence for reliable L3 semantic inference. In contrast, curriculum-trained
LLaTiSA
across L1-L2 could be easily transferred to domain-specific L3 tasks (e.g., ECG interpretation).

4.5
Ablation Study

Effectiveness of CoT.

In the
HiTSR
dataset, we incorporate LLM-annotated CoT data, verified by humans, to enhance
LLaTiSA
’s performance and generalization, particularly on the more complex tasks at levels L2-L3. To isolate the impact of CoT, we perform SFT on the same dataset using only the correct options as supervision (“w/o CoT”). As shown in
Table

4
, while ID performance does not degrade significantly, the OOD performance deteriorates markedly, with accuracy decreasing by 4.31% and 17.91% on L2 and L3, respectively.
Furthermore, training solely on correct options significantly undermines the model’s original instruction-following capabilities. We validate the necessity of a three-stage curriculum for SFT, as opposed to a single-stage regime (i.e., “joint training”) in which all tasks are pooled and shuffled. As illustrated in

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a difficulty-stratified view of TSR and formalize a four-level taxonomy (L1-L4) that characterizes progressive TSR capabilities. We introduce
HiTSR
, a large-scale hierarchical dataset with over 83k high-fidelity TSR questions spanning L1-L3, constructed with unambiguous labels and verifiable reasoning chains to support both reliable training and evaluation. Building on
HiTSR
, we present
LLaTiSA
, a VLM-based TSR model that pairs time series plots with an auxiliary index-value table to bridge qualitative perception and precise numerical evidence, and is trained via a three-stage curriculum aligned with L1-L3. Extensive experiments on diverse OOD benchmarks and real-world scenarios show that
LLaTiSA
consistently outperforms strong baselines and different modeling strategies, and transfers effectively to domain-specific semantic reasoning, suggesting a practical path toward more robust unified TSRMs.

Limitations

Our study primarily focuses on systematic dataset construction and supervised curriculum fine-tuning, leaving the exploration of Reinforcement Learning Fine-Tuning (RFT) on
HiTSR
as a future direction. Effectively implementing RFT in this domain remains challenging due to the complexity of reward design, which must simultaneously supervise low-level numerical precision and high-level semantic logic across our L1-L4 taxonomy. Future work includes: (i) integrating RL into the hierarchical curriculum to further refine the model’s policy; (ii) investigating robust initialization strategies to mitigate cold-start issues.

Ethics Statement

All datasets and language models used in this work are publicly available. The
HiTSR
dataset was constructed from well-established, publicly accessible time series benchmarks and synthetic data generation pipelines, and
LLaTiSA
is built on the open-sourced model.

C.2
Data Verification

L1: Numerical Read-out.

The question-answer (QA) pairs utilized in this stage are completely constructed by strict rule-based annotations based on each time series’ attribution pool.

L2: Pattern Perception.

For numerical perception, it is similar to L1, which generated the complete QA pairs through an explicit rule-based pipeline. The distractors are created by slightly perturbing the numerical evidence.

For local pattern differentiation, distractors are synthesized using rule-based perturbations, such as numerical manipulation or random attribute sampling from heterogeneous series. To ensure label uniqueness, we perform cross-verification by benchmarking each candidate option against the ground-truth attributes of the series. Subsequently, we use GPT-5 with both the metadata and visual plots of the given time series to annotate the correct answers with CoT reasoning. For quality assurance, the training set only includes samples correctly resolved by GPT-5, while the test set undergoes rigorous human verification to ensure high fidelity.

Regarding global pattern differentiation, we first prompt GPT-5 to generate comprehensive descriptions of the series. Distractors are then constructed by incorporating annotations from disparate sequences. Similarly, the original dataset is processed by GPT-5 to produce reasoning-enhanced ground truth, ensuring the depth and accuracy of the labels.

L3: Semantic Understanding.

The raw QA pairs are initially synthesized by GPT-5 in a single pass. To ensure data integrity, we implement a cross-validation stage where GPT-5 evaluates the generated pairs against a set of rigorous criteria. This evaluation focuses on two critical dimensions: (i) verifying the ground truth, ensuring that numerical evidence, pattern characterization, and semantic logic are technically sound; and (ii) auditing distractors, where each incorrect option is re-examined to eliminate potential overlaps or counterexamples that might inadvertently align with the time series. Finally, a manual dual-check is performed on the validated candidates to finalize a high-fidelity, real-world TSR dataset.

Appendix I
Theoretical Underpinning of the Difficulty-Stratified Taxonomy

Our four-level decomposition of TSR is not arbitrary but is grounded in two well-established theoretical frameworks: Bloom’s Taxonomy
Bloom et al. 1956
(cognitive psychology) and Bertin’s Levels of Reading
Monmonier 1985
(visual analytic).

•

Cognitive Reasoning (Bloom’s Taxonomy):
Our hierarchy mirrors the progression from Low-Order Thinking Skills (LOTS) to High-Order Thinking Skills (HOTS). Specifically: (i) L1 (Numerical Read-out) maps to Remembering/Retrieving (discrete fact extraction); (ii) L2 (Pattern Perception) maps to Understanding (identifying structural relationships and trends); (iii) L3 (Semantic Reasoning) maps to Applying/Analyzing (contextualizing patterns within domain-specific knowledge); and (iv) L4 (Predictive Inference) maps to Creating (generating future states or logical relationships based on historical temporal data). Recent studies
Huber and Niklaus 2025
have formally advocated for using Bloom’s Taxonomy to evaluate the reasoning capabilities of LLMs.

•

Visual Perception (Bertin’s Levels of Reading):
From a data visualization perspective, Jacques Bertin’s classic framework justifies our structural necessity: (i) Elementary Level (L1) focuses on a single data element; (ii) Intermediate/Global Level (L2) focuses on relationships and macroscopic trends across multiple elements; and (iii) Extrapolative Level (L3/L4) involves deducing hidden semantics or future trends beyond the given data.

By aligning with these frameworks,
HiTSR
provides a principled road-map to evaluate whether a TSRM’s failure stems from a perceptual bottleneck (L1-L2) or a reasoning deficit (L3-L4). This structured decomposition is necessary for diagnosing the precise cognitive boundaries of TSRMs.

Appendix J
Position of Our Work

We emphasize that our paper intentionally focuses on TSR-Understanding (L1-L3) rather than TSR-Generation (L4). We argue that these are two fundamentally different paradigms.

•

Paradigm Distinction.
In the broader multimodal field, understanding models (e.g., Qwen3-VL) and generative model (e.g., the diffusion model) are typically treated as distinct research directions. Integrating L4 (especially numerical forecasting) would necessitate a transition from a Reasoning Model to a Unified Understanding-and-Generation Model, which presents distinct technical challenges (e.g., specialized generative losses) and constitutes a separate research frontier.

•

Diagnostic Purity.
Our primary goal is to provide a diagnostic lens to pinpoint the cognitive boundaries of current TSRMs. By focusing on L1-L3, we ensure a rigorous evaluation of the model’s analytical capabilities without the confounding factors of generative precision.

K.2
Dataset Construction

We extended our synthetic generation pipeline to ensure that L4 tasks are both challenging and unambiguous. To prevent “shared ground truth” (where multiple options could be plausible), we implemented a multi-stage filtering protocol for negative candidates:

•

Statistical Uniqueness.
We filter out candidates with high Pearson Correlation (
\geq
0.8) or small Euclidean Distance to the ground truth to ensure distinctiveness.

•

Strategic Truncation.
To avoid introducing excessive ambiguity, we ensure the split point between
x
and
y
does not occur during high-variance events, such as mid-spike or at sharp turning points. Splitting at such critical junctures may cause multiple plausible answers; by selecting structurally stable partition points, we ensure the ground truth is the only logically consistent successor.

•

Trend Smoothness.
We use a weighted continuation score, which incorporates gradient consistency and tail-to-head difference to verify that the positive sample is the most semantically and numerically plausible successor.

•

Mutual Exclusion.
Negative candidates are cross-checked to ensure low mutual correlation, preventing redundant options within a single question.

K.3
Transition to Vision Modalities

To align with the dual-view strategy of
LLaTiSA
, we transform the L4 predictive task into a dual-image input format. Specifically, the visualized patterns are rendered as a
2\times 2
grid of subplots, each representing one of the four options. Within each subplot, the lookback context and the candidate prediction patch are visualized in blue and red, respectively, allowing the model to perform visual comparative analysis of temporal continuity. Complementing this, a precision-calibrated numerical table provides exact values for the lookback window. This dual-view setup ensures the model can leverage global trend intuition from the plots while maintaining the fine-grained numerical grounding necessary for rigorous successor identification.

Appendix M
Data Quality Assurance

M.1
Human Verification

To ensure the data quality, we employed a hybrid verification pipeline combining deterministic scripts and human expert audits:

•

L1.
Numerical Read-out
: 3,000/30,000 (10%). Given the objective nature of read-out tasks, we primarily utilized deterministic scripts for data verification.

•

L2.
Pattern Perception
: 5,100/50,703 (10%). This subset includes 1,600 Local, 2,500 Global verified by human experts, and 1,000 Numerical Perception samples verified by deterministic scripts.

•

L3.
Semantic Reasoning
: 3,121/3,121 (100%). Due to the high cognitive complexity of L3, every single sample underwent manual human verification to ensure reasoning integrity.

Our experts followed a three-fold protocol: (1) Numerical Faithfulness to the source series, (2) Logical Consistency of the CoT trajectories, and (3) Ground-truth Correctness. To evaluate the reliability of our protocol, two independent annotators re-verified a random subset of 500 samples from L2 and L3.
We achieved a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.853, signifying “almost perfect agreement.”
This score demonstrates that our verification criteria are objective and consistent across different human experts.

M.2
Cross-Model Validation

To ensure the annotations are objective and free from model-specific biases, we conducted a cross-model validation using Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct (Qwen3) on the subset of
HiTSR
which contains verifiable CoT trajectories. The following table reports the successfully validated instance counts (number of passed samples relative to the total evaluated) and the inter-annotator agreement scores (Cohen’s Kappa calculated between GPT-5 and Qwen3).
All subsets across L2-L3 achieve “almost perfect agreement.” with an average score of 0.848.
Combined with the full human verification on L3, such cross-model evidence demonstrates that advanced language models converge on a consistent standard of validity, effectively serving as a robust, model-agnostic filter to maximize the efficiency and reliability of subsequent human verification.
