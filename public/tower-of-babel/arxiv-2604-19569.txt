Lyapunov-Certified Direct Switching Theory for Q-Learning
Donghwan Lee

Abstract
Q-learning is a fundamental algorithmic primitive in reinforcement learning. This paper develops a new framework for analyzing Q-learning from a switching-system viewpoint. In particular, we derive a direct stochastic switching-system representation of the Q-learning error. The key observation is that the Bellman maximization error can be expressed exactly as an average of action-wise Q-errors under a suitable stochastic policy. The resulting recursion has a switched linear conditional-mean drift and martingale-difference noise. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first convergence-rate analysis of standard Q-learning whose leading exponential rate is expressed through the joint spectral radius (JSR) of a direct switching family. Since the JSR is the exact worst-case exponential rate of the associated switched linear drift, the resulting rate is among the tightest drift-based rates that can be certified for this Q-learning representation. Building on this representation, we prove finite-time bounds based on a product-defined JSR-induced Lyapunov function and also give an optional common quadratic Lyapunov certificate. The quadratic certificate is only a sufficient condition and hence applies only to instances for which the certificate is feasible, whereas the JSR-induced Lyapunov construction applies to the full direct switching family whenever its JSR is below one. When feasible, the quadratic certificate replaces product-based verification by a computable matrix inequality and gives a simpler stochastic bound. We further extend the framework to Markovian observation models.



1
Introduction

Q-learning
[
29
]
is a foundational algorithm in reinforcement
learning (RL)
[
24
]
for solving discounted Markov
decision processes (MDPs) with unknown transition kernels. Its convergence has
been studied extensively over the past several decades. Classical analyses
primarily establish asymptotic convergence
[
27
,
9
,
3
,
13
]
. While these results are fundamental,
asymptotic convergence alone does not quantify how rapidly the iterates
approach the solution, and therefore does not fully characterize the efficiency
of the algorithm. This limitation has motivated a growing body of finite-time
convergence analyses, which provide explicit bounds on the progress of the
iterates toward the optimal Q-function. Recent advances in finite-time analysis
include
[
25
,
11
,
6
,
1
,
28
,
21
,
16
,
5
]
. Most existing results view Q-learning as a
nonlinear stochastic approximation scheme
[
12
]
and
rely on the contraction property of the Bellman optimality operator.

A useful alternative viewpoint is to regard Q-learning as a discrete-time
stochastic switching system
[
19
,
17
]
.
This perspective was developed in
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
18
]
and was used to prove a final-iterate bound for constant-step-size
Q-learning. In that formulation, the error dynamics are affine rather than
linear, because the greedy policy selected by the current iterate may differ
from an optimal policy. The resulting affine term is controlled through upper
and lower comparison systems. More specifically, the upper comparison system
remains a switching system, whereas the lower comparison system is linear.
Although this comparison-system approach is useful for deriving valid
finite-time bounds, it has an inherent limitation: it controls the Q-learning
error through auxiliary systems rather than directly exploiting the switching
structure of the original error recursion. Consequently, intrinsic quantities
from switching-system theory, such as the joint spectral radius
(JSR)
[
22
,
26
,
10
]
, are
difficult to apply directly to the original Q-learning dynamics.

To overcome this limitation, this paper develops a direct switching-system
representation of the Q-learning error. The key observation is that the Bellman
maximization error can be represented exactly as an average of action-wise
Q-errors under a suitably chosen stochastic policy. Substituting this identity
into the Q-learning recursion yields a switched linear conditional-mean error
system, while the stochastic component remains a martingale difference. The
corresponding deterministic convergence rate is characterized by the JSR of the
resulting direct switching family. This is the central distinction from existing
Bellman-contraction and comparison-system analyses: the rate is attached to the
exact direct switched drift rather than to an auxiliary contraction or row-sum
upper bound. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first convergence-rate
framework for standard Q-learning whose leading exponential rate is formulated
in terms of such a direct JSR. Since the JSR is the exact worst-case exponential
rate of the switched linear family, the resulting transient bound is among the
tightest drift-based bounds that can be certified for Q-learning under this
direct representation.

We first develop the framework under an i.i.d. observation model with a
constant step size, where the direct switching structure is most transparent.
We then extend both the representation and the corresponding finite-time
analysis to Markovian observation models. To certify convergence rates, we use
two complementary Lyapunov certificates. The first is a product-defined
JSR-induced Lyapunov function
[
7
,
8
]
for the direct switching family. This certificate is intrinsic to the JSR analysis and, because the direct family has

\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}<1
under the standing assumptions, it is
available for every Q-learning instance considered here. The second is a
common quadratic Lyapunov certificate. This quadratic certificate is only a
sufficient condition and therefore applies only to the special instances for
which the associated matrix inequalities are feasible. Its advantage is
computational and algebraic: it can be checked by finite linear matrix
inequalities and yields a shorter stochastic proof with more explicit
condition-number constants.

Finally, we emphasize that the goal of this paper is to offer a new perspective
and a methodological framework for understanding the convergence of Q-learning.
The proposed framework is not intended to replace existing analyses based on
Bellman contractions or stochastic approximation, nor does it claim uniformly
improved sample complexity across all problem instances. Rather, the
direct-switching viewpoint complements existing analyses by identifying the
intrinsic switched drift and by exposing when the direct JSR rate is sharper
than comparison-based rates.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

The set of real numbers is denoted by
\mathbb{R}
;
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the
m

\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}

m\times r

A
,
A^{\top}
denotes its transpose. For symmetric matrices,
A\succ 0
and
A\succeq 0
denote positive definiteness and positive semidefiniteness, and
A\preceq B
means
B-A\succeq 0
. The identity matrix is denoted by
I
. For vectors,
e_{i}
is the
i
th standard basis vector, with dimension clear from context, and
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. The quantities
\lambda_{\min}(A)
and
\lambda_{\max}(A)
denote the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix

, respectively. For a finite set
\mathcal{S}
,
|\mathcal{S}|
denotes its cardinality. We write
\Delta_{m}:=\left\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:q_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}=1\right\}
for the probability simplex in

. For a finite matrix family
\mathcal{H}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{N}\}
, the notation
\co(\mathcal{H})
denotes the convex hull
\co(\mathcal{H}):=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}A_{i}:\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}
.

2.2
Switching System

Let us consider the discrete-time stochastic switched affine system
[
17
,
19
,
23
]

x_{k+1}=A_{\sigma_{k}}x_{k}+b_{\sigma_{k}}+\xi_{k},

where
\sigma_{k}\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
is a switching signal,
A_{\sigma_{k}}
is selected from a prescribed family of matrices
{\cal H}:=\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{M}\}
, which is called a switching family,
b_{\sigma_{k}}
is a mode-dependent affine term, and
\xi_{k}
is a disturbance or martingale noise. When
b_{\sigma_{k}}=0
, the deterministic part reduces to a switched linear system,

x_{k+1}=A_{\sigma_{k}}x_{k}.

The worst-case exponential rate of the switched linear family is characterized by the joint spectral radius (JSR)
[
26
,
22
,
2
,
10
]
, defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Joint spectral radius)
.

For a bounded set of matrices
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, its joint spectral radius is

\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{H}):=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{H}}\|A_{k}\cdots A_{1}\|^{1/k},

where the value is independent of the chosen submultiplicative norm
[
22
,
10
]
. When
\mathcal{H}
is finite, the supremum for each fixed product length is a maximum over products generated by matrices in
\mathcal{H}
. For a finite family
\mathcal{H}
, the notation
\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\co(\mathcal{H}))
means the JSR computed when each factor in a product is allowed to be any convex combination of matrices in
\mathcal{H}
.

For Q-learning
[
24
]
, this perspective is natural because the greedy policy induced by the current iterate plays the role of the switching signal.
In the final-iterate analysis of
[
15
]
, constant-step-size Q-learning under an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observation model is represented as a stochastic affine switching system. The affine term appears because the greedy policy associated with
Q_{k}
need not be optimal. We keep the switching-system viewpoint, but replace the affine representation with a direct switched linear representation obtained from a stochastic-policy linearization of the Bellman maximization error.

2.3
Discounted Markov Decision Processes and Bellman Operator

Let us consider a finite discounted Markov decision process (MDP)
[
20
]
with state space
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{S}|\}
, action space
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{A}|\}
, transition probability
P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
for
(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
, one-step reward
r(s,a,s^{\prime})
for
(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
, expected reward
R(s,a):=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)r(s,a,s^{\prime}),(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, and discount factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Let
\Theta
denote the set of deterministic stationary policies
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{A}
. A Q-function is viewed as a vector
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}
using a fixed ordering of state-action pairs. We use the compact notation

P:=\begin{bmatrix}P_{1}\\
\vdots\\
P_{|\mathcal{A}|}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|)\times|\mathcal{S}|},\qquad R:=\begin{bmatrix}R(\cdot,1)\\
\vdots\\
R(\cdot,|\mathcal{A}|)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|},

where
P_{a}=P(\cdot\mid\cdot,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{S}|},a\in\mathcal{A}
.
For any stochastic policy
\mu:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
, define

\Pi^{\mu}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mu(1)^{\top}\otimes e_{1}^{\top}\\
\mu(2)^{\top}\otimes e_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mu(|\mathcal{S}|)^{\top}\otimes e_{|\mathcal{S}|}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times(|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|)}.

Then
P\Pi^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{(|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|)\times(|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|)}
is the state-action transition matrix under
\mu
. For a deterministic policy
\pi\in\Theta
, we use the same notation
\Pi^{\pi}
by identifying
\pi(s)
with its one-hot encoding for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
. For
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}
, let us define

V_{Q}(s):=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q(s,a),\qquad s\in\mathcal{S},

and
V_{Q}:=(V_{Q}(1),\ldots,V_{Q}(|\mathcal{S}|))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
.
The Bellman optimality operator is
F(Q):=R+\gamma PV_{Q}
. The optimal Q-function
Q^{*}
is the unique fixed point of
F
, i.e.,
Q^{*}=F(Q^{*})
, and
V^{*}:=V_{Q^{*}}
.

Definition 2

(Uniform reward bound)
.

Since
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are finite, let us define

R_{\max}:=\max_{(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}}|r(s,a,s^{\prime})|.

It is well-known that the optimal Q-function then satisfies
\|Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}
.

2.4
Q-Learning Setting

We consider the standard asynchronous Q-learning recursion
[
24
]
with a constant step size
\alpha
under an i.i.d. observation model. At each time
k
, a state-action pair
(s_{k},a_{k})
is sampled independently with
\mathbb{P}(s_{k}=s,a_{k}=a)=d(s,a):=p(s)b(a\mid s),(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, where
p
is a state-sampling distribution and
b
is a behavior policy. Then
s^{\prime}_{k}\sim P(\cdot\mid s_{k},a_{k})
and
r_{k+1}:=r(s_{k},a_{k},s^{\prime}_{k})
are sampled. The update is

Q_{k+1}(s_{k},a_{k})=Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})+\alpha\left(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u)-Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})\right),

while all coordinates
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
with
(s,a)\neq(s_{k},a_{k})
remain unchanged. Let
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the natural filtration of the Q-learning process

\mathcal{F}_{0}:=\sigma(Q_{0}),\qquad\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma\!\left(Q_{0},\{(s_{t},a_{t},s^{\prime}_{t},r_{t+1}):0\leq t\leq k-1\}\right),\quad k\geq 1.

Then,
Q_{k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable. Let
D:=\diag(d(s,a))_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|)\times(|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|)}
.
For the sampled coordinate, define the random vector

\zeta_{k}:=(e_{a_{k}}\otimes e_{s_{k}})\left(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u)-Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})\right).

Then, we have
\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=D(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k})
. Accordingly, the martingale-difference noise is defined by

w_{k}:=(e_{a_{k}}\otimes e_{s_{k}})\left(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u)-Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})\right)-D(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k}).

(1)

Therefore, the vector form of Q-learning is

\displaystyle Q_{k+1}=Q_{k}+\alpha\{D(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k})+w_{k}\},

where
w_{k}
is defined in
Equation

1
and satisfies
\mathbb{E}[w_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
with respect to the natural filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
defined above.

2.5
Affine switching-system representation of Q-learning

We next recall the affine switching representation used in
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. For each
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}
, let
\pi_{Q}
be a deterministic greedy policy satisfying
\pi_{Q}(s)\in\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q(s,a),s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Then

V_{Q}=\Pi^{\pi_{Q}}Q,\qquad F(Q)=R+\gamma P\Pi^{\pi_{Q}}Q.

Let
\pi_{k}:=\pi_{Q_{k}}
, and let
\pi^{*}
be an optimal deterministic policy so that
Q^{*}=R+\gamma P\Pi^{\pi^{*}}Q^{*}
. With
e_{k}:=Q_{k}-Q^{*}
, subtracting the fixed-point equation from the Q-learning recursion gives the stochastic affine switching system

e_{k+1}=\bigl(I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\Pi^{\pi_{k}}\bigr)e_{k}+\alpha\gamma DP(\Pi^{\pi_{k}}-\Pi^{\pi^{*}})Q^{*}+\alpha w_{k},

where

w_{k}:=(e_{a_{k}}\otimes e_{s_{k}})\left(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u)-Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})\right)-D(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k}).

Equivalently, it can be written as

e_{k+1}=A_{\pi_{k}}e_{k}+b_{\pi_{k}}+\alpha w_{k},

where
A_{\pi}:=I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\Pi^{\pi}
and
b_{\pi}:=\alpha\gamma DP(\Pi^{\pi}-\Pi^{\pi^{*}})Q^{*}
. The representation is affine because
b_{\pi_{k}}
is generally nonzero whenever the greedy policy
\pi_{k}
is not optimal. The next section shows that this offset is an artifact of the chosen parametrization:
V_{Q_{k}}-V^{*}
can instead be represented directly as the action-wise error averaged under a suitable stochastic policy.

Assumption 1
.

The following conditions hold throughout the paper.

(i)

d(s,a)>0
for every
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
.

(ii)

The step size satisfies
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

(iii)

The initial Q-table
Q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}
is deterministic.

Define

d_{\min}:=\min_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}d(s,a),

so that, since
d
is a probability distribution on

|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|
coordinates,

0<d_{\min}\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}.

The standard row-sum rate introduced in
[
14
,
15
]
is

\rho_{\mathrm{row}}:=1-\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma).

Under
1
,
\rho_{\mathrm{row}}\in(0,1)
.

3
Direct Stochastic-Policy Switching Representation

We now remove the affine offset from the error recursion. The only ingredient needed for this step is the state-wise convexity fact stated and proved in Appendix
A.2
,
Lemma

3
: the Bellman maximization error can be written exactly as an average of action-wise Q-errors under a suitable stochastic policy. In particular, let

e_{k}:=Q_{k}-Q^{*}.

For any stochastic policy
\mu
, let us define

M_{\mu}:=I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\Pi^{\mu}.

Substituting this linearization into the Q-learning recursion removes the affine term exactly.

Theorem 1

(Exact direct switching representation)
.

Under
1
, let
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the natural filtration defined in
Section

2.4
. Then, for each
k\geq 0
, there exists an
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable stochastic policy
\mu_{k}
such that

e_{k+1}=M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha w_{k},

where

w_{k}:=(e_{a_{k}}\otimes e_{s_{k}})\left(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u)-Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})\right)-D(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k})

and
\mathbb{E}[w_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
. Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[e_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}.

(2)

The proof is given in Appendix
A.3
. The recursion above singles out the deterministic modes that govern the drift.

3.1
Direct JSR rate

Define the deterministic switching family

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}:=\left\{M_{\pi}:=I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\Pi^{\pi}:\pi\in\Theta\right\}.

Throughout the paper, the corresponding direct JSR rate is defined as

\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}:=\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}).

The policies in
Theorem

1
are stochastic, whereas the JSR will be defined through deterministic extreme modes. The next lemma connects the two descriptions.

Lemma 1

(Convex-hull property and JSR convex-hull invariance; cf.
[
10
]
)
.

For every stochastic policy
\mu
,
M_{\mu}\in\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
. Moreover, when the JSR of
\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
is interpreted as the JSR over the compact set of all convex combinations of matrices in
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
, we have

\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}))=\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})=\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}.

The proof is given in Appendix
A.4
.
Therefore, a certificate for the deterministic extreme family also controls every stochastic-policy matrix that can appear along the Q-learning trajectory.

Proposition 1

(Direct drift rate and row-sum comparison)
.

The conditional-mean drift in
Equation

2
has exponential switching rate
\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}
. More precisely, for every
\varepsilon>0
such that
\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}+\varepsilon<1
, there exists a norm
p_{\varepsilon}
on
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}
such that

p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\mu}x)\leq(\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}+\varepsilon)p_{\varepsilon}(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|},

for every stochastic policy
\mu
. In addition,

\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}\leq\rho_{\mathrm{row}}:=1-\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma).

Here
\rho_{\mathrm{row}}
is the standard row-sum rate used in the prior comparison-system analyses of
[
14
,
15
]
.

The proof is given in Appendix
A.5
. The row-sum rate is only an upper bound on the direct switching family and can be strictly larger than the direct JSR. A concrete two-state single-action example demonstrating this strict gap is given in Appendix
F
(
Example

1
).

4
Finite-Time Error Bound via a JSR Lyapunov Function

The direct linear drift makes it possible to analyze finite-time behavior directly through the switching family itself. We begin with the most intrinsic certificate, a Lyapunov function induced by products of the direct family
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}

[
7
,
8
]
, which gives a final-iterate bound at any rate strictly larger than the direct JSR. Throughout this section, let

e_{k}:=Q_{k}-Q^{*}

and let
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the natural filtration defined in
Section

2.4
. Under
1
, the boundedness result in Appendix
A.1
,
Lemma

2
, gives
\mathbb{E}[w_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0,\mathbb{E}[\|w_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq W_{\max}
, where

w_{k}:=(e_{a_{k}}\otimes e_{s_{k}})\left(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u)-Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})\right)-D(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k}),

and the deterministic noise constant
W_{\max}
is defined in Appendix
A.1
,
Equation

10
. Moreover, by
Theorem

1
, we have

e_{k+1}=M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha w_{k}

for an
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable stochastic policy
\mu_{k}
.

4.1
Construction of the JSR Lyapunov function

The Lyapunov function is built from all finite products generated by the direct family. The auxiliary finite-family construction is collected in Appendix
B
,
Lemma

4
, while its direct Q-learning specialization and deterministic drift inequality are stated in
Lemma

5
. We recall here the part of the auxiliary statement that is used in the main finite-time argument.

Summary of
Lemma

4
in Appendix
B
.

Let

\mathcal{H}:=\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{M}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},\qquad\rho:=\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{H}),

and fix any
\varepsilon>0
such that

\beta_{\varepsilon}:=\rho+\varepsilon\in(0,1).

For a sequence of switching modes
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}
, write

A_{\sigma}:=A_{\sigma_{k}}\cdots A_{\sigma_{1}},

and for
k=0
interpret
\{1,\ldots,M\}^{0}
as the singleton empty word and
A_{\sigma}=I
. For each integer
t\geq 0
, let us define the piecewise quadratic function
[
7
,
8
]

V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2k}\max_{\sigma\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}^{k}}\|A_{\sigma}x\|_{2}^{2},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Then, the following statements in
Lemma

4
in Appendix
B
hold:

(1)

For every
t\geq 0
,

V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x)\geq\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(A_{i}x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(2)

For every
t\geq 0
, every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(\lambda x)=|\lambda|^{2}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)
, and
V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)\leq V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x)
.

(3)

There exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|x\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(4)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the limit
V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x):=\lim_{t\to\infty}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)
exists and is finite. Moreover,

\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|x\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(5)

The function
p_{\varepsilon}(x):=\sqrt{V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)}
is a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

(6)

The function
V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}
satisfies the stronger Lyapunov inequality

V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(A_{i}x)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)-\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}.

Equivalently,

p_{\varepsilon}(A_{i}x)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}p_{\varepsilon}(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}.

Statements 1-4 ensure that the finite product-based functions converge to a well-defined Lyapunov function uniformly comparable to the Euclidean norm. Statement 5 shows that its square root is a genuine norm, which allows the stochastic recursion to be handled by triangle-inequality arguments. Statement 6 provides the deterministic contraction inequality that is later applied to the direct Q-learning modes; the convex-hull extension then transfers this contraction from deterministic policies to the stochastic policies appearing in the exact direct representation.

We now apply this generic construction to the direct Q-learning family

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}=\{M_{\pi}:\pi\in\Theta\},\qquad M_{\pi}=I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\Pi^{\pi}.

Since

4.2
Finite-time final-iterate bound

Combining this drift inequality with the martingale-noise estimate gives the finite-time bound.

Theorem 2

(Finite-time bound via

)
.

Let
1

The standard upper/lower comparison analysis in

5
Common Quadratic Lyapunov Certificate

The JSR-induced Lyapunov function in
Section

4
gives an intrinsic direct-switching certificate. It is defined through products of the full switching family, but it is available for every instance in the present Q-learning setting because

. Appendix

presents a more computable but more restrictive alternative: the common quadratic certificate
[

is stated in

Theorem

.

Specifically, suppose that there exist

and

such that

M_{\pi}^{\top}HM_{\pi}\preceq\beta^{2}H,\qquad\forall\pi\in\Theta.

This condition is not automatic. It is only a sufficient certificate for the direct switching family
[
19
]
, and hence, it applies only to systems for which a common quadratic Lyapunov function exists. If the quadratic inequalities are infeasible, the product-based JSR Lyapunov construction in
Section

4
remains the general certificate for the direct family.

Since every stochastic-policy matrix
M_{\mu}
lies in
\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
, the same quadratic inequality extends from deterministic policies to all stochastic policies by the matrix-convexity argument in Appendix

,

. Therefore the exact direct recursion

can be analyzed with the quadratic Lyapunov function

,

\mathbb{E}[e_{k}^{\top}He_{k}]\leq\beta^{2k}e_{0}^{\top}He_{0}+\frac{\alpha^{2}\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}}{1-\beta^{2}}\left(1-\beta^{2k}\right),

and hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H)}}\,\beta^{k}\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{2}+\alpha\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}(H)(1-\beta^{2})}}.

Therefore, the quadratic certificate gives the same type of direct-switching transient-and-noise-floor decomposition as the JSR Lyapunov bound when the certificate exists. Its main advantage is that a single matrix inequality replaces the product-defined Lyapunov construction, and the martingale cross term vanishes exactly in the quadratic expansion; its limitation is that it is a sufficient linear matrix inequality type certificate

rather than a general consequence of JSR stability.

Markovian Observation Model

For constant step sizes under single-trajectory Markovian coordinate sampling, the direct representation becomes a time-varying Markovian switching linear system. Writing

\displaystyle e_{k+1}=M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\alpha\xi_{k+1},\qquad M_{\mu_{k}}=I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\Pi^{\mu_{k}},

(9)

where

The next theorem summarizes how the stationary averaged JSR certificate converts this representation into a practical finite-time estimate. It is a main-text summary of Appendix

,
Theorems

5
and
6
. The first estimate is stated in the JSR Lyapunov metric, and the following displays translate it into the usual sup-norm error bound.

Theorem 3
.

Let the single-trajectory Markovian Q-learning recursion

\alpha\in(0,1)
, and let
1
hold with

equal to the stationary distribution of the behavior-induced chain

\beta_{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}+\varepsilon<1,

and let

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\,C_{\varepsilon}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)\beta_{\varepsilon}^{k}+\alpha C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}.

Therefore, for a fixed Lyapunov certificate, the Markovian case has the same formal JSR-type transient-and-residual structure as the i.i.d. case: the transient is proportional to

with the enlarged disturbance constant below. No uniform control of

7
Conclusion

We developed a direct switching-system finite-time analysis of Q-learning. The central step is the exact stochastic-policy representation of the Bellman maximization error, which yields a switched linear conditional-mean error recursion whose intrinsic deterministic rate is the direct JSR
\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}=\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
. The standard row-sum rate remains a simple upper bound, but it can be conservative.
The resulting bounds use a JSR-induced certificate for the direct drift. The framework also extends to Markovian observations. Overall, the direct-switching viewpoint complements Bellman-contraction and comparison-system analyses by exposing instance-dependent drift rates and removing comparison-induced transient terms when sharper certificates are available.

Boundedness and noise variance

Lemma 2

(Boundedness and noise variance; cf.
[
27
,
6
,
28
]
)
.

Under
1
, let
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the natural filtration defined in
Section

2.4
. In particular,
Q_{0}
is deterministic. The Q-learning iterates satisfy

\|Q_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\},\qquad\forall k\geq 0.

Moreover, for the noise

w_{k}:=(e_{a_{k}}\otimes e_{s_{k}})\left(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u)-Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})\right)-D(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k}),

we have

\mathbb{E}[\|w_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq W_{\max},\qquad\forall k\geq 0,

where
W_{\max}
is defined as

W_{\max}:=\left(R_{\max}+(1+\gamma)\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}\right)^{2}.

(10)

Proof.

We prove the two claims separately.
First, we prove boundedness by induction. At time
k=0
,

\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}=\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\},

so the claim is true initially. Suppose now that

\|Q_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}

for some
k
. The random target used in the sampled coordinate is

Y_{k}:=r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u).

The induction hypothesis gives
|Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u)|\leq\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}
for every
u
, and hence

|Y_{k}|\leq R_{\max}+\gamma\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}.

Because
\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}\geq R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
, we have

\displaystyle R_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\gamma)\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\},

\displaystyle R_{\max}+\gamma\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}.

Therefore, the target
Y_{k}
always lies in the interval

Y_{k}\in\left[-B_{0},\,B_{0}\right],\qquad B_{0}:=\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}.

(11)

Only the coordinate
(s_{k},a_{k})
is changed. For that coordinate,

Q_{k+1}(s_{k},a_{k})=(1-\alpha)Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})+\alpha Y_{k}.

Since
0<\alpha<1
, this is a convex combination of two numbers in the interval in
Equation

11
, and therefore it also belongs to the same interval in
Equation

11
. Every other coordinate is unchanged and therefore remains in the same interval. This proves
\|Q_{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}
. By induction, we have

\|Q_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\},\qquad\forall k\geq 0,

which proves the first statement. We now prove the conditional second-moment bound for the noise. Let

\zeta_{k}:=(e_{a_{k}}\otimes e_{s_{k}})\left(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u)-Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})\right).

By definition,
w_{k}=\zeta_{k}-\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
. For any square-integrable random vector
Z
and any
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}
,

\mathbb{E}[\|Z-\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{F}]\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}]=\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}]-\|\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{F}]\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}].

Applying this identity with
Z=\zeta_{k}
gives

\mathbb{E}[\|w_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\zeta_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}].

The vector
\zeta_{k}
has at most one nonzero coordinate. Therefore, its Euclidean norm is exactly the absolute value of the scalar temporal-difference term. By the boundedness just proved, one can derive

\displaystyle\left|r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s^{\prime}_{k},u)-Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})\right|

\displaystyle\leq R_{\max}+\gamma\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}

\displaystyle+\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}

\displaystyle=R_{\max}+(1+\gamma)\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}.

Consequently, we have

\|\zeta_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(R_{\max}+(1+\gamma)\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}\right)^{2}=W_{\max}.

Taking conditional expectation on both sides of the above inequality gives the desired bound.
∎

Note that since
Q_{0}
is deterministic by

is a deterministic finite constant.
Since
\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)\right\}\geq R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
, the crude but useful bound on

W_{\max}\leq 4\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}^{2}

\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)

W_{\max}\leq\frac{4R_{\max}^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}.

A.2
Stochastic-policy linearization of the Bellman max

Lemma 3

(Stochastic-policy linearization of the Bellman max)
.

For every
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}
, there exists a stochastic policy
\mu_{Q}:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
such that

V_{Q}-V^{*}=\Pi^{\mu_{Q}}(Q-Q^{*}).

Moreover,
\mu_{Q}
can be chosen as a measurable function of
Q
.

Proof.

Fix a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and write

e:=Q-Q^{*}.

We first show that the scalar number

y_{s}:=V_{Q}(s)-V^{*}(s)

lies between the smallest and largest action-wise errors at state
s
.
Since

V_{Q}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\{Q^{*}(s,a)+e(s,a)\},\qquad V^{*}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{*}(s,a),

we have the upper bound

\displaystyle V_{Q}(s)

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\{Q^{*}(s,a)+e(s,a)\}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{*}(s,a)+\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e(s,a)=V^{*}(s)+\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e(s,a),

which leads to

y_{s}\leq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e(s,a).

For the lower bound, let
a^{*}(s)
be a maximizer of
Q^{*}(s,\cdot)
. Then

\displaystyle V_{Q}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\{Q^{*}(s,a)+e(s,a)\}\geq Q^{*}(s,a^{*}(s))+e(s,a^{*}(s))\geq V^{*}(s)+\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e(s,a).

Hence, it follows that

\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e(s,a)\leq y_{s}\leq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e(s,a).

In one dimension, the convex hull of the finite set

\{e(s,a):a\in\mathcal{A}\}
is exactly the interval between its minimum and maximum. Therefore
y_{s}
can be written as a convex combination of the action-wise errors.
We now choose one such convex combination explicitly. Break ties lexicographically and define

a_{\min}(s)\in\argmin_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e(s,a),\qquad a_{\max}(s)\in\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e(s,a).

If

e(s,a_{\min}(s))=e(s,a_{\max}(s)),

then all action-wise errors at state
s
are equal. In this case
y_{s}
must equal that common value, so we may set

\mu_{Q}(a_{\min}(s)\mid s)=1.

If the two values are different, define

\lambda_{s}:=\frac{y_{s}-e(s,a_{\min}(s))}{e(s,a_{\max}(s))-e(s,a_{\min}(s))}.

The bounds above guarantee
0\leq\lambda_{s}\leq 1
. Set

\mu_{Q}(a_{\max}(s)\mid s)=\lambda_{s},\qquad\mu_{Q}(a_{\min}(s)\mid s)=1-\lambda_{s},

and set all other action probabilities to zero. Then

\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{Q}(a\mid s)e(s,a)=(1-\lambda_{s})e(s,a_{\min}(s))+\lambda_{s}e(s,a_{\max}(s))=y_{s}.

This proves the desired identity at the fixed state
s
.
Repeating the same construction for every state and stacking the resulting scalar identities gives the exact vector–matrix identity

\displaystyle V_{Q}-V^{*}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}V_{Q}(1)-V^{*}(1)\\
\vdots\\
V_{Q}(|\mathcal{S}|)-V^{*}(|\mathcal{S}|)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{Q}(a\mid 1)e(1,a)\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{Q}(a\mid|\mathcal{S}|)e(|\mathcal{S}|,a)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{Q}(1)^{\top}\otimes e_{1}^{\top}\\
\mu_{Q}(2)^{\top}\otimes e_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{Q}(|\mathcal{S}|)^{\top}\otimes e_{|\mathcal{S}|}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}(Q-Q^{*})

\displaystyle=\Pi^{\mu_{Q}}(Q-Q^{*}).

Finally, the above rule can be made deterministic and unique as a function of
Q
. Because the action set is finite, lexicographic tie-breaking selects exactly one minimizer and exactly one maximizer at each state. Once these two actions are fixed, the displayed formula determines the coefficient
\lambda_{s}
uniquely; in the equal-error case, the rule deterministically assigns all mass to the selected action. Thus, for each input
Q
, the construction returns one well-defined policy rather than a set of possible choices. Since this deterministic rule is implemented by finitely many comparisons of continuous functions of
Q
together with the explicit formula for
\lambda_{s}
, the resulting policy is measurable as a function of
Q
.
∎

A.4
Proof of Lemma

Statement of Lemma
1
(Convex-hull property and JSR convex-hull invariance; cf.
[

).

For every stochastic policy
\mu
,
M_{\mu}\in\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
. Moreover, when the JSR of
\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
is interpreted as the JSR over the compact set of all convex combinations of matrices in
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}

\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}))=\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})=\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}.

Proof.

First fix a stochastic policy
\mu
. We show that its mode is a convex combination of deterministic-policy modes. A deterministic policy
\pi\in\Theta
chooses one action
\pi(s)
at every state. Define

c_{\pi}(\mu):=\prod_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(\pi(s)\mid s).

Each factor is nonnegative, so
c_{\pi}(\mu)\geq 0

\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)=\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}\prod_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(\pi(s)\mid s)=\prod_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(a\mid s)=1.

Therefore, the numbers
c_{\pi}(\mu)
are convex weights.

We next check that these weights reproduce the stochastic policy state by state. For fixed
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\Theta:\,\pi(s)=a}c_{\pi}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Theta:\,\pi(s)=a}\mu(a\mid s)\prod_{r\neq s}\mu(\pi(r)\mid r)

\displaystyle=\mu(a\mid s)\prod_{r\neq s}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(b\mid r)

\displaystyle=\mu(a\mid s).

This identity is exactly the statement that

\Pi^{\mu}=\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)\Pi^{\pi}.

Using the linear dependence of
M_{\mu}
on
\Pi^{\mu}
, we obtain

\displaystyle M_{\mu}

\displaystyle=I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\Pi^{\mu}

\displaystyle=I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)\Pi^{\pi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)\left(I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\Pi^{\pi}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)M_{\pi}.

Hence
M_{\mu}\in\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
.

It remains to prove that passing to the convex hull does not change the JSR. Fix a product length
k
. Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
. For each
j
, write

A_{j}=\sum_{i}c_{j,i}M_{j,i},\qquad c_{j,i}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{i}c_{j,i}=1,

where each
M_{j,i}\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
. By repeatedly distributing products over sums,

A_{k}\cdots A_{1}=\sum_{i_{k},\ldots,i_{1}}c_{k,i_{k}}\cdots c_{1,i_{1}}M_{k,i_{k}}\cdots M_{1,i_{1}}.

The coefficients in this expansion are nonnegative and sum to one. Thus the product of the convex-combination factors is itself a convex combination of products of extreme factors. By convexity of any norm,

\|A_{k}\cdots A_{1}\|\leq\max_{M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}}\|M_{k}\cdots M_{1}\|.

Taking the supremum over all
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
, then taking
k
th roots, and finally sending
k\to\infty
, gives

\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}))\leq\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}).

The opposite inequality is immediate from the set inclusion

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\subset\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
. Therefore the two JSRs are equal.
∎

Statement of Proposition
1
(Direct drift rate and row-sum comparison).

The conditional-mean drift in
Equation

2
has exponential switching rate
\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}
. More precisely, for every
\varepsilon>0
such that
\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}+\varepsilon<1
, there exists a norm
p_{\varepsilon}
on
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}
such that

p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\mu}x)\leq(\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}+\varepsilon)p_{\varepsilon}(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|},

for every stochastic policy
\mu
. In addition,

\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}\leq\rho_{\mathrm{row}}:=1-\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma).

Proof.

Let

\beta_{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}+\varepsilon.

By assumption,
\beta_{\varepsilon}<1
. A standard extremal-norm consequence of the JSR theory says that, for every number strictly larger than the JSR of a finite matrix family, there exists a norm that contracts all matrices in the family by that number
[
22
,
10
]
. Applying this result to the finite family
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
gives a norm
p_{\varepsilon}
satisfying

p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\pi}x)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}p_{\varepsilon}(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|},\forall\pi\in\Theta.

Now take an arbitrary stochastic policy
\mu
. By
Lemma

1
,

M_{\mu}=\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)M_{\pi},\qquad c_{\pi}(\mu)\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)=1.

Using the triangle inequality and positive homogeneity of the norm,

\displaystyle p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\mu}x)=p_{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)M_{\pi}x\right)\leq\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\pi}x)\leq\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)\beta_{\varepsilon}p_{\varepsilon}(x)=\beta_{\varepsilon}p_{\varepsilon}(x).

As a result, the same norm controls every stochastic-policy mode.

We next prove the row-sum comparison. The matrices
D
,
P
, and
\Pi^{\mu}
are nonnegative. Also
1-\alpha d(s,a)\geq 0
because
0<\alpha<1
and
d(s,a)\leq 1
. Hence every entry of
M_{\mu}
is nonnegative. The row of
P\Pi^{\mu}
associated with
(s,a)
is a probability vector, because after taking action
a
in state
s
, the next state is sampled according to
P(\cdot\mid s,a)
and the next action is sampled according to
\mu(\cdot\mid s^{\prime})
. Therefore that row sums to one. The corresponding row of
M_{\mu}
sums to

1-\alpha d(s,a)+\alpha\gamma d(s,a)=1-\alpha d(s,a)(1-\gamma)\leq 1-\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)=\rho_{\mathrm{row}}.

For a nonnegative matrix, the induced infinity norm is the maximum row sum. Hence

\|M_{\mu}\|_{\infty}\leq\rho_{\mathrm{row}}

for every stochastic policy
\mu
, and in particular for every deterministic policy. Since the JSR of a family is bounded above by any common induced-norm bound on that family,

\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}=\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})\leq\rho_{\mathrm{row}}.

∎

Appendix B
Proof of the JSR Lyapunov Bound

Lemma 4

(Common Lyapunov construction)
.

Let

\mathcal{H}:=\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{M}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},\qquad\rho:=\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{H}),

and fix any
\varepsilon>0
such that

\beta_{\varepsilon}:=\rho+\varepsilon\in(0,1).

For a sequence of switching modes
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}
, write

A_{\sigma}:=A_{\sigma_{k}}\cdots A_{\sigma_{1}},

and for
k=0
interpret
\{1,\ldots,M\}^{0}
as the singleton empty word and
A_{\sigma}=I
. For each integer
t\geq 0
, define the piecewise quadratic function

V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2k}\max_{\sigma\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}^{k}}\|A_{\sigma}x\|_{2}^{2},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Then the following statements hold.

1.

For every
t\geq 0
,

V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x)\geq\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(A_{i}x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(13)

2.

For every
t\geq 0
, every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(\lambda x)=|\lambda|^{2}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x),

and

V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)\leq V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x).

3.

There exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|x\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(14)

4.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the limit

V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x):=\lim_{t\to\infty}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)

exists and is finite. Moreover,

\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|x\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(15)

5.

The function

p_{\varepsilon}(x):=\sqrt{V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)}

is a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

6.

The function
V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}
satisfies the stronger Lyapunov inequality

V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(A_{i}x)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)-\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}.

Equivalently,

p_{\varepsilon}(A_{i}x)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}p_{\varepsilon}(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}.

7.

Consequently,

\|A_{i}\|_{p_{\varepsilon}}\leq\beta_{\varepsilon},\qquad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,M\},

where
\|A_{i}\|_{p_{\varepsilon}}
is the induced matrix norm generated by
p_{\varepsilon}
,

\|A_{i}\|_{p_{\varepsilon}}:=\sup_{x\neq 0}\frac{p_{\varepsilon}(A_{i}x)}{p_{\varepsilon}(x)}.

Therefore, the induced-norm bound yields

\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}.

Proof.

We prove the statements one by one.

Proof of 1).

By definition,

V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{t+1}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2k}\max_{\sigma\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}}\|A_{\sigma}x\|_{2}^{2}.

The
k=0
term is
\|x\|_{2}^{2}
. For
k\geq 1
, write
k=j+1
. A word of length
j+1
may be described by its first applied index
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
and the remaining word
\tau\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{j}
. Therefore, decomposing by the first applied index gives

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x)

\displaystyle=\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{t}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2(j+1)}\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}}\max_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{j}}\|A_{\tau}A_{i}x\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2j}\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}}\max_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{j}}\|A_{\tau}A_{i}x\|_{2}^{2}.

Since the summands are nonnegative, the sum of the maxima is at least the maximum of the sums. Hence

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2j}\max_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{j}}\|A_{\tau}A_{i}x\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(A_{i}x).

This proves
Equation

13
.

Proof of 2).

Absolute homogeneity follows from the homogeneity of the Euclidean norm:

V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(\lambda x)=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2k}\max_{\sigma}\|A_{\sigma}(\lambda x)\|_{2}^{2}=|\lambda|^{2}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x).

The monotonicity
V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)\leq V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x)
holds because
V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}
contains all terms of
V_{\varepsilon}^{t}
plus one additional nonnegative term.

Proof of 3).

The lower bound is immediate from the
k=0
term:

V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)\geq\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

For the upper bound, choose
\eta
such that

\rho<\eta<\beta_{\varepsilon}.

By the definition of the JSR, there exists an integer
K\geq 1
such that

\max_{\sigma\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}}\|A_{\sigma}\|_{2}^{1/k}\leq\eta,\qquad\forall k\geq K.

Thus, for all
k\geq K
,

\max_{\sigma\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}}\|A_{\sigma}\|_{2}\leq\eta^{k}.

Now define

C_{0}:=\max\left\{1,\,\max_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\eta^{-k}\max_{\sigma\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}}\|A_{\sigma}\|_{2}\right\}.

Then, for every
k\geq 0
,

\max_{\sigma\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}}\|A_{\sigma}\|_{2}\leq C_{0}\eta^{k}.

Therefore, the product bound gives the estimate

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2k}\max_{\sigma\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}}\|A_{\sigma}x\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{t}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2k}\left(\max_{\sigma\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}}\|A_{\sigma}\|_{2}\right)^{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{t}\left(\frac{\eta}{\beta_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2k}\|x\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\eta}{\beta_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2k}\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\eta/\beta_{\varepsilon}<1
, the geometric series converges. Setting

C_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{C_{0}^{2}}{1-(\eta/\beta_{\varepsilon})^{2}}

gives
Equation

14
.

Proof of 4).

By 2), the sequence
V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)
is nondecreasing in
t
. By 3), it is bounded above by
C_{\varepsilon}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
. Hence

V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x):=\lim_{t\to\infty}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)

exists and is finite. Passing to the limit in the bounds of 3) proves
Equation

15
.

Proof of 5).

For each
k\geq 1
, define

\nu_{k}(x):=\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-k}\max_{\sigma\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}}\|A_{\sigma}x\|_{2},

and define
\nu_{0}(x):=\|x\|_{2}
. Each
\nu_{k}
is a seminorm because it is the pointwise maximum of seminorms, and
\nu_{0}
is a norm. For finite
t
,

V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\nu_{k}(x)^{2},\qquad p_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x):=\sqrt{V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{t}\nu_{k}(x)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The function
p_{\varepsilon}^{t}
is a norm. Positivity follows from the
\nu_{0}
term, absolute homogeneity follows from the homogeneity of each
\nu_{k}
, and the triangle inequality follows from Minkowski’s inequality applied to the vector

(\nu_{0}(x),\nu_{1}(x),\ldots,\nu_{t}(x)).

Now

p_{\varepsilon}(x)=\sqrt{V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)}=\lim_{t\to\infty}p_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x),

and the limit is finite by 4). Since

p_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x+y)\leq p_{\varepsilon}^{t}(x)+p_{\varepsilon}^{t}(y)

for every
t
, letting
t\to\infty
gives

p_{\varepsilon}(x+y)\leq p_{\varepsilon}(x)+p_{\varepsilon}(y).

Absolute homogeneity is inherited from 2), and positive definiteness follows from

p_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}=V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)\geq\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus
p_{\varepsilon}
is a norm.

Proof of 6).

Using 1),

V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x)\geq\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\beta_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\max_{i}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(A_{i}x).

Therefore, rearranging the finite-horizon inequality gives

\max_{i}V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(A_{i}x)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x)-\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Fixing
i
, we have

V_{\varepsilon}^{t}(A_{i}x)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(V_{\varepsilon}^{t+1}(x)-\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Letting
t\to\infty
and using monotone convergence of both sides gives

V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(A_{i}x)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)-\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x).

Taking square roots yields

p_{\varepsilon}(A_{i}x)\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}p_{\varepsilon}(x).

Proof of 7).

From 6),

\|A_{i}\|_{p_{\varepsilon}}=\sup_{x\neq 0}\frac{p_{\varepsilon}(A_{i}x)}{p_{\varepsilon}(x)}\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}.

Hence, for any word
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
,

\|A_{\sigma}\|_{p_{\varepsilon}}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}\|A_{\sigma_{j}}\|_{p_{\varepsilon}}\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{k}.

Taking the maximum over all words of length
k
, then the
k
th root, and finally the limit as
k\to\infty
, gives

\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Because

\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable. By

\displaystyle p_{\varepsilon}(e_{k+1})=p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha w_{k})\leq p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k})+\alpha p_{\varepsilon}(w_{k}).

(27)

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2j}=\frac{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}},

(28)

combining
Equation

27
and
Equation

28
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})]\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{0})+\frac{\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}^{2}W_{\max}}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\left(1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}\right),

(29)

which is
Equation

6
.

We now convert this Lyapunov bound into the desired sup-norm error bound. The lower bound in
Equation

5
implies

\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\|e_{k}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})}.

By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}[\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})}]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})]}.

Combining
Equation

29
with the above inequality and

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{0})\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|e_{0}\|_{2}^{2},

we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[{\left\|{{e_{k}}}\right\|_{\infty}}]\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}({e_{k}})]}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}({e_{0}})+\frac{{{\alpha^{2}}C_{\varepsilon}^{2}{W_{\max}}}}{{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}(1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{{C_{\varepsilon}}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}\left\|{{e_{0}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{{{\alpha^{2}}C_{\varepsilon}^{2}{W_{\max}}}}{{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}}.

Finally, applying
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
proves
Equation

7
. The explicit version follows from

\displaystyle\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\|Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right).

This completes the overall proof.
∎

Appendix C
Sample-Complexity for the JSR Lyapunov Bound

For the appendix sample-complexity statements, we use the deterministic envelope

W_{\max}\leq 4\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)^{2}.

The sample-complexity statements below are certificate-dependent. The constant
C_{\varepsilon}
is the norm-equivalence constant of the chosen product-defined JSR Lyapunov certificate; it generally depends on
\varepsilon
, and hence may depend on
\alpha
or
\delta
when the slack is chosen as a function of them. No uniform bound on
C_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
or
\alpha\downarrow 0
is claimed.

For a fixed certificate, or in regimes where

is controlled, the bound has the familiar formal

noise-floor structure under linear-in-
\alpha
spectral-gap scaling.

Corollary 1

(Sample-complexity form for the JSR Lyapunov bound)
.

Suppose that the assumptions of
Theorem

2
hold. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\,C_{\varepsilon}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2})k}{2}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle+\alpha C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{W_{\max}}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}.

Consequently, a sufficient condition for

\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]\leq\delta
is

\alpha\leq\frac{\delta\sqrt{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}{2C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{W_{\max}}}

(31)

and

k\geq\frac{2}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{2\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\,C_{\varepsilon}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)}{\delta}\right).

(32)

Thus, suppressing logarithmic factors, the iteration complexity conditional on the chosen certificate and its constant
C_{\varepsilon}
is

k=\widetilde{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\right),

where
W_{\max}\leq 4\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)^{2}
may be used in the admissible step-size condition. If

\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
, then

Equation

12
gives the sufficient step-size condition

\alpha\leq\frac{(1-\gamma)\delta\sqrt{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}{4C_{\varepsilon}R_{\max}}.

Proof.

Because
0<\beta_{\varepsilon}<1
, we have

\beta_{\varepsilon}^{k}=(\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2})^{k/2}=\left(1-(1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2})\right)^{k/2}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2})k}{2}\right),

where we used
1-x\leq e^{-x}
for
x\in[0,1]
. Substituting this estimate into

Equation

8
gives
Equation

30
.

If
Equation

31
holds, then the noise-floor term in

Equation

30
satisfies

\alpha C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{W_{\max}}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

If
Equation

32
also holds, then

\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\,C_{\varepsilon}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2})k}{2}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

Thus the transient term and the noise-floor term are each at most
\delta/2
,
and their sum is at most
\delta
. The displayed
\widetilde{O}
-form follows
directly from
Equation

32
. The normalized step-size condition
follows by substituting

W_{\max}\leq 4R_{\max}^{2}/(1-\gamma)^{2}
into

Equation

31
.
∎

Let the assumptions of

Appendix D
Quadratic Lyapunov Function Certificate

This appendix gives a computable quadratic-certificate version
[
19
]
of the direct switching bound. The certificate is sufficient but not necessary; its value is that it can be checked through linear matrix inequalities and it yields a cleaner stochastic proof because the martingale cross term vanishes exactly in a quadratic expansion.

Assumption 2

(Common quadratic Lyapunov function certificate
[
19
]
)
.

There exist a matrix
H=H^{\top}\succ 0
and a scalar
\beta\in(0,1)
such that

M_{\pi}^{\top}HM_{\pi}\preceq\beta^{2}H,\qquad\forall\pi\in\Theta.

Remark 1

(Certificate, not an automatic consequence)
.

2
is a sufficient certificate, not a necessary consequence of

\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}<1
. The row-sum bound implies

\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}\leq 1-\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)<1,

and hence guarantees exponential stability of the direct switching family under arbitrary switching. General switched-system theory guarantees a common Lyapunov norm, but not necessarily a common quadratic Lyapunov function. Therefore,
2
should be understood as a computable linear matrix inequality type certificate
[
4
]
. If it is infeasible, one may instead use the JSR-induced Lyapunov function in Appendix
B
.

First, we note that a quadratic certificate for deterministic policies automatically extends to all stochastic policies.

Lemma 7

(Extension of the quadratic certificate to stochastic policies)
.

If
2
holds, then

M_{\mu}^{\top}HM_{\mu}\preceq\beta^{2}H

for every stochastic policy
\mu
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

1
, for every stochastic policy
\mu
there exist convex weights
c_{\pi}(\mu)
such that

M_{\mu}=\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)M_{\pi},\qquad c_{\pi}(\mu)\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)=1.

Here, matrix convexity means convexity in the Loewner order: for any matrices
A_{i}
and convex weights
c_{i}
,

\left(\sum_{i}c_{i}A_{i}\right)^{\top}H\left(\sum_{i}c_{i}A_{i}\right)\preceq\sum_{i}c_{i}A_{i}^{\top}HA_{i},\qquad H\succeq 0.

Indeed, with
\bar{A}:=\sum_{i}c_{i}A_{i}
,

\sum_{i}c_{i}A_{i}^{\top}HA_{i}-\bar{A}^{\top}H\bar{A}=\sum_{i}c_{i}(A_{i}-\bar{A})^{\top}H(A_{i}-\bar{A})\succeq 0.

Applying this identity to
A_{i}=M_{\pi}
gives

M_{\mu}^{\top}HM_{\mu}\preceq\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)M_{\pi}^{\top}HM_{\pi}\preceq\sum_{\pi\in\Theta}c_{\pi}(\mu)\beta^{2}H=\beta^{2}H.

This proves the claim.
∎

With this extension, the stochastic recursion can be analyzed by a standard quadratic Lyapunov argument.

Theorem 4

(Direct-switching finite-time bound under a quadratic certificate)
.

Let
1
and
2
hold. Then, for every
k\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}[e_{k}^{\top}He_{k}]\leq\beta^{2k}e_{0}^{\top}He_{0}+\frac{\alpha^{2}\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}}{1-\beta^{2}}\left(1-\beta^{2k}\right).

(34)

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]\leq\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H)}}\,\beta^{k}\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{2}+\alpha\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}(H)(1-\beta^{2})}}.

(35)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\lambda_{\max}(H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H)}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)\beta^{k}+\alpha\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}(H)(1-\beta^{2})}}.

(36)

Proof.

Let

V_{H}(e):=e^{\top}He.

By
Theorem

1
, the error system is given as

e_{k+1}=M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha w_{k},

where
\mu_{k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable and
\mathbb{E}[w_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
. Taking conditional expectation gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{H}(e_{k+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha w_{k}\right)^{\top}H\left(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha w_{k}\right)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=e_{k}^{\top}M_{\mu_{k}}^{\top}HM_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+2\alpha e_{k}^{\top}M_{\mu_{k}}^{\top}H\mathbb{E}[w_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]+\alpha^{2}\mathbb{E}[w_{k}^{\top}Hw_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}].

The cross term vanishes because
\mathbb{E}[w_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
. By
Lemma

7
and
Lemma

2
, it follows from the last inequality that

\mathbb{E}[V_{H}(e_{k+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]\leq\beta^{2}V_{H}(e_{k})+\alpha^{2}\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}.

Taking total expectation and iterating this scalar recursion yields
Equation

34
.
Next, since

\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\|e_{k}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(H)}}\sqrt{V_{H}(e_{k})}.

by Jensen’s inequality and
Equation

34
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(H)}\mathbb{E}[V_{H}(e_{k})]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H)}\beta^{2k}\|e_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}(H)(1-\beta^{2})}\left(1-\beta^{2k}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H)}}\,\beta^{k}\|e_{0}\|_{2}+\alpha\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}(H)(1-\beta^{2})}},

where the last step uses
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
. This proves
Equation

35
.
Finally,

\displaystyle\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\,\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\|Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right),

which proves
Equation

36
.
∎

The comparison-system analysis in
[
15
]
yields a final-iterate bound with the row-sum rate

\rho_{\mathrm{row}}=1-\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma).

The direct-switching quadratic-certificate bound has the form

\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]\leq O\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}}\right)+O(\beta^{k}).

When
\beta^{2}=1-\Theta(\alpha)
, the noise floor is
O(\sqrt{\alpha})
, as usual for constant-step-size stochastic approximation. Here,
g(\alpha)=\Theta(\alpha)
means that there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
, independent of sufficiently small
\alpha
, such that
c_{1}\alpha\leq g(\alpha)\leq c_{2}\alpha
. The advantage is that the exponential transient is governed by the direct switching certificate rate
\beta
, which may be strictly smaller than the row-sum rate
\rho_{\mathrm{row}}
, and the additional comparison-induced transient term is absent.
The preceding theorem also admits a sample-complexity form.

Corollary 3

(Sample-complexity form under a quadratic certificate)
.

Suppose that
1
and
2
hold and, in addition,

\beta^{2}\leq 1-\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2}.

(37)

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\lambda_{\max}(H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H)}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)k}{4}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}(H)d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}}.

Consequently, a sufficient condition for
\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]\leq\delta
is

\alpha\leq\frac{d_{\min}(1-\gamma)\lambda_{\min}(H)\delta^{2}}{8\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}}

(39)

and

k\geq\frac{4}{d_{\min}(1-\gamma)\alpha}\ln\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\lambda_{\max}(H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H)}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)}{\delta}\right).

(40)

Thus, choosing
\alpha
at the order of the upper bound in
Equation

39
gives

k=O\!\left(\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)W_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}(H)d_{\min}^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}\delta^{2}}\ln\frac{\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\lambda_{\max}(H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H)}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)\right)}{\delta}\right).

Proof.

By
Equation

37
,

\beta^{k}=(\beta^{2})^{k/2}\leq\left(1-\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2}\right)^{k/2}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)k}{4}\right),

and

\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}}.

Substituting these two inequalities into
Equation

36
gives
Equation

38
. The choices in
Equations

39
and
40
make the noise-floor term and the exponential transient term each no larger than
\delta/2
.

Appendix E
Extensions to Markovian observations

We next consider constant step sizes under a single-trajectory observation model. A behavior policy
b(a\mid s)
is fixed. Starting from an initial state-action pair, the trajectory evolves according to

s_{k+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid s_{k},a_{k}),\qquad r_{k+1}=r(s_{k},a_{k},s_{k+1}),\qquad a_{k+1}\sim b(\cdot\mid s_{k+1}).

Thus

X_{k}:=(s_{k},a_{k})

is a Markov chain on the state-action space with behavior-induced transition kernel

P^{b}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}\mid s,a):=P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)b(a^{\prime}\mid s^{\prime}).

Assume this behavior-induced chain is irreducible and aperiodic with stationary distribution
d
, and let

D=\diag(d(s,a)).

Let
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the filtration generated by
Q_{0}
, the initial state-action pair, and the trajectory samples up to the current pair
X_{k}
. Thus
Q_{k}
and
X_{k}
are
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}
-measurable, while
s_{k+1}
,
r_{k+1}
, and
a_{k+1}
are generated after conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}
.

The single-trajectory Q-learning update is

Q_{k+1}(s_{k},a_{k})=Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})+\alpha\left(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s_{k+1},u)-Q_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})\right),

(41)

while all other coordinates remain unchanged. Then
Equation

41
can be written as

Q_{k+1}=Q_{k}+\alpha e_{X_{k}}e_{X_{k}}^{\top}(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k})+\alpha\xi_{k+1},

(42)

where

\displaystyle\xi_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=e_{X_{k}}\Bigl(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s_{k+1},u)-Q_{k}(X_{k})-(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k})_{X_{k}}\Bigr).

By construction,

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]=0.

Applying
Lemma

3
to
Q_{k}
, there exists a
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}
-measurable stochastic policy
\mu_{k}
such that

F(Q_{k})-Q_{k}=\gamma P\Pi^{\mu_{k}}e_{k}-e_{k}.

Hence the exact sample-path error recursion is

e_{k+1}=\widehat{M}_{X_{k},\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha\xi_{k+1},\qquad\widehat{M}_{i,\mu}:=I-\alpha e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}+\alpha\gamma e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}P\Pi^{\mu}.

(43)

This is a Markovian switching system driven by the single trajectory
X_{k}
, while the policy mode
\mu_{k}
is generated endogenously by the current Q-table.

For comparison with the i.i.d. direct family, add and subtract the stationary average
D
. This gives

e_{k+1}=M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\alpha\xi_{k+1},

(44)

where

M_{\mu_{k}}=I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\Pi^{\mu_{k}}

is the stationary averaged direct mode and

b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}:=(e_{X_{k}}e_{X_{k}}^{\top}-D)(\gamma P\Pi^{\mu_{k}}-I)e_{k}

(45)

is the single-trajectory coordinate-sampling bias. Under i.i.d. stationary sampling this term disappears after conditioning. Along a Markovian trajectory, it is correlated with the past, and the bounded-disturbance estimate below treats this correlation conservatively.

The Markovian bias term in
Equation

45
prevents the exact i.i.d. martingale-difference identity used in
Theorem

2
. Nevertheless, an i.i.d.-style finite-time bound can be obtained without imposing a separate averaged-drift perturbation assumption. The price is that the single-trajectory coordinate-sampling bias is treated as an additional bounded disturbance, so the result is conservative and does not exploit temporal-correlation cancellations.

We will keep the Markovian disturbance level explicit. Define

B_{Q}:=\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right\}.

The combined disturbance will be bounded by

W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}.

Theorem 5

(Single-trajectory Markovian bounded-bias bound with a JSR Lyapunov certificate)
.

Let the single-trajectory Markovian Q-learning recursion
Equation

42
be run with a constant step size
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and let
1
hold with
d
equal to the stationary distribution of the behavior-induced chain
X_{k}=(s_{k},a_{k})
. Fix any
\varepsilon>0
such that

\beta_{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}+\varepsilon<1,

and let
V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}
be the JSR-induced Lyapunov function for the stationary averaged direct family
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
. Let
C_{\varepsilon}\geq 1
be the norm-equivalence constant in
Equation

5
. Then, for all
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})]

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{0})+\frac{\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\left(1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}\right).

(46)

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]\leq\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}}\,\beta_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{2}+\alpha C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}.

(47)

Moreover, using

\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right),

we have the explicit form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\,C_{\varepsilon}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)\beta_{\varepsilon}^{k}

(48)

\displaystyle+\alpha C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}.

No averaged-drift Markovian perturbation assumption is used in this theorem. The estimate is the direct analogue of the i.i.d. JSR Lyapunov bound, with
W_{\max}
replaced by the enlarged disturbance constant
\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)
.

Proof.

We write the proof in small steps. Start from the exact averaged recursion

e_{k+1}=M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\alpha\xi_{k+1},

where

M_{\mu_{k}}=I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP\Pi^{\mu_{k}}\in\co(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})

and

b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}=(e_{X_{k}}e_{X_{k}}^{\top}-D)(\gamma P\Pi^{\mu_{k}}-I)e_{k}.

The boundedness proof used for
Lemma

2
does not use the
independence of the sampled coordinates. It only uses the facts that rewards
are bounded and that
0<\alpha<1
. Therefore, along the single trajectory, we obtain

\displaystyle\|Q_{k}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq B_{Q},\quad\|Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\leq B_{Q},

\displaystyle\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\|Q_{k}\|_{\infty}+\|Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq 2B_{Q}.

For the vector multiplied by
e_{X_{k}}e_{X_{k}}^{\top}-D
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|(\gamma P\Pi^{\mu_{k}}-I)e_{k}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|P\Pi^{\mu_{k}}e_{k}\|_{\infty}+\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq(1+\gamma)\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq 2(1+\gamma)B_{Q}.

Moreover, for any vector
h
, we have

\|e_{X_{k}}e_{X_{k}}^{\top}h\|_{2}=|h_{X_{k}}|\leq\|h\|_{\infty},

and, because
D=\diag(d)
with
d_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}d_{i}=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|Dh\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}d_{i}^{2}h_{i}^{2}\leq\|h\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{i}d_{i}^{2}\leq\|h\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\|b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|(e_{X_{k}}e_{X_{k}}^{\top}-D)(\gamma P\Pi^{\mu_{k}}-I)e_{k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|e_{X_{k}}e_{X_{k}}^{\top}(\gamma P\Pi^{\mu_{k}}-I)e_{k}\|_{2}+\|D(\gamma P\Pi^{\mu_{k}}-I)e_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|(\gamma P\Pi^{\mu_{k}}-I)e_{k}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}.

Next we bound the transition-reward noise. Put

Y_{k+1}:=r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s_{k+1},u)-Q_{k}(X_{k}).

Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}
, the quantity

(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k})_{X_{k}}
is exactly the conditional mean of
Y_{k+1}
. Hence

\displaystyle\xi_{k+1}:=

\displaystyle e_{X_{k}}\Bigl(r_{k+1}+\gamma\max_{u\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(s_{k+1},u)-Q_{k}(X_{k})-(F(Q_{k})-Q_{k})_{X_{k}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{X_{k}}\left(Y_{k+1}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]\right).

Since
\|e_{X_{k}}z\|_{2}=|z|
for a scalar
z
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\xi_{k+1}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Y_{k+1}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]\right)^{2}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y_{k+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]-\left(\mathbb{E}[Y_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[Y_{k+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}].

Moreover, since

\displaystyle|Y_{k+1}|\leq R_{\max}+\gamma\|Q_{k}\|_{\infty}+\|Q_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq R_{\max}+(1+\gamma)B_{Q}.

By the definition of
W_{\max}
, this gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\xi_{k+1}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]

\displaystyle\leq\left(R_{\max}+(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}=W_{\max}.

Since
b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}
-measurable and

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]=0
, the cross term vanishes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[2\langle b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}},\xi_{k+1}\rangle\middle|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}\right]=2\left\langle b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}},\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]\right\rangle=0.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]

\displaystyle=\|b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}\|_{2}^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\|\xi_{k+1}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]

\displaystyle\leq W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}.

Combining this disturbance estimate with the averaged recursion gives

\displaystyle e_{k+1}=M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha(b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1}),\qquad\mathbb{E}[\|b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]\leq W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}.

(49)

This is the same Lyapunov recursion as in the i.i.d. case, except that the martingale noise is replaced by the combined Markovian disturbance.
Let

p_{\varepsilon}(x):=\sqrt{V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)}.

By
Lemma

6
, we have

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k})\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})-\|e_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right),

(50)

and hence

\displaystyle p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})-\|e_{k}\|_{2}^{2}}.

(51)

The norm comparison
V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|x\|_{2}^{2}

and the lower bound
V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)\geq\|x\|_{2}^{2}
imply

\displaystyle 0\leq V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})-\|e_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\leq(C_{\varepsilon}-1)\|e_{k}\|_{2}^{2}.

Combining the above inequality with (
51
) yields

\displaystyle p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k})\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}-1}\,\|e_{k}\|_{2}.

(52)

Using the triangle inequality for
p_{\varepsilon}
and the averaged recursion,

\displaystyle p_{\varepsilon}(e_{k+1})

\displaystyle=p_{\varepsilon}\left(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k}+\alpha(b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1})\right)\leq p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k})+\alpha p_{\varepsilon}(b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1}).

Squaring both sides of the above inequality gives

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k})+2\alpha p_{\varepsilon}(M_{\mu_{k}}e_{k})p_{\varepsilon}(b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1})+\alpha^{2}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1}).

(53)

Conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}
, we estimate the two terms involving

b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1}
. First, using Jensen’s inequality,
V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
, and the bound in
Equation

49
, we get the chain of inequalities

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[p_{\varepsilon}(b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[p_{\varepsilon}(b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1})^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[\|b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)}.

(54)

Second, using
V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
and the bound in
Equation

49
leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[\|b_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}+\xi_{k+1}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right).

(55)

Combining the previous bounds in
Equations

50
,
52
,
55
and
54
with
Equation

53
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\|e_{k}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\alpha\beta_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}(C_{\varepsilon}-1)\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)}\,\|e_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right).

(56)

Use
2ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}
with

a=\beta_{\varepsilon}\|e_{k}\|_{2},\qquad b=\alpha\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}(C_{\varepsilon}-1)\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)}.

to obtain

\displaystyle 2\alpha\beta_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}(C_{\varepsilon}-1)\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)}\,\|e_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\|e_{k}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}(C_{\varepsilon}-1)\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right).

The term
\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\|e_{k}\|_{2}^{2}
cancels the negative term

-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\|e_{k}\|_{2}^{2}
. Hence, it follows from
Equation

56
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{M}}]

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})+\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}(C_{\varepsilon}-1)\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})+\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right).

Taking total expectation and setting

a_{k}:=\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})]

gives the scalar recursion

a_{k+1}\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}a_{k}+\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right).

Iterating it gives

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}a_{0}+\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2j}

\displaystyle=\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{0})+\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)\frac{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

(57)

This proves
Equation

46
.

It remains to convert the Lyapunov estimate into the stated norm estimate.
By
Lemma

4
, since

\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\|e_{k}\|_{2}\leq p_{\varepsilon}(e_{k})=\sqrt{V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})},

Jensen’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})}]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})]}.

Using
Lemma

4
, the bound on
\mathbb{E}[V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{k})]
in
Equation

57
, and using

V_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(e_{0})\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|e_{0}\|_{2}^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|e_{k}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2k}\|e_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}C_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}}\,\beta_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|e_{0}\|_{2}+\alpha C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}.

This proves

Equation

47
. The explicit version follows from

\displaystyle\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\,\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\|Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right),

which proves
Equation

48
.
∎

Theorem 6

(Row-sum slack specialization for the single-trajectory Markovian bound)
.

Assume the conditions of
Theorem

5
. Choose the row-sum slack as

\varepsilon=\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2}.

Then we have

\beta_{\varepsilon}\leq 1-\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2},\qquad 1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\geq\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\,C_{\varepsilon}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)k}{2}\right)

(58)

\displaystyle+C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)}{d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}}.

Proof.

With
\varepsilon=\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2}
and by
Proposition

1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}+\varepsilon\leq 1-\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)+\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2}=1-\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2}.

Therefore, it follows that

\beta_{\varepsilon}^{k}\leq\left(1-\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2}\right)^{k}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)k}{2}\right),

where we used
1-x\leq e^{-x}
for
x\in[0,1]
.
Moreover,
1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}
is lower bounded as

\displaystyle 1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left(1-\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)-\frac{\alpha^{2}d_{\min}^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)}{2},

because
0<\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)<1
. Substituting these two estimates
into
Equation

48
gives

Equation

58
.
∎

Corollary 4

(Sample complexity for the single-trajectory Markovian bounded-bias bound)
.

Assume the conditions of
Theorem

5
. A
sufficient condition for
\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]\leq\delta
is

\alpha\leq\frac{\delta\sqrt{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}{2C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\left(W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}\right)}}

(59)

and

k\geq\frac{2}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{2\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}}\,\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)}{\delta}\right).

(60)

Thus, for the fixed certificate,

\displaystyle k=O\!\left(\frac{1}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\ln\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|\,C_{\varepsilon}}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)\right)}{\delta}\right).

Equivalently, suppressing logarithmic factors and fixed certificate constants,

k=\widetilde{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\right).

As above, this statement is certificate-dependent and does not claim a uniform
bound on
C_{\varepsilon}
as the slack varies.

Proof.

Using the deterministic bound

\|Q_{0}-Q^{*}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)

in
Equation

47
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{k}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}}\,\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|\,|\mathcal{A}|}\left(\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)\beta_{\varepsilon}^{k}+\alpha C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{W_{\max}+\left(4(1+\gamma)B_{Q}\right)^{2}}{1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}.

Since

\beta_{\varepsilon}^{k}=(\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2})^{k/2}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\beta_{\varepsilon}^{2})k}{2}\right),

the step-size condition
Equation

59
makes the
residual term at most
\delta/2
, and

Equation

60
makes the transient term at most

\delta/2
. This proves the sufficient condition. The displayed iteration
complexity follows from the lower bound on
k
.
∎

Appendix F
Examples

Example 1

(Why the direct rate can be faster)
.

The rate
\rho_{\mathrm{row}}=1-\alpha d_{\min}(1-\gamma)
is only a row-sum upper bound on the direct switching family. It is not generally equal to
\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}
. Consider the single-action two-state case with

\mathcal{S}=\{1,2\},\qquad|\mathcal{A}|=1,\qquad\gamma=0.9,\qquad\alpha=0.9,

D=\diag(0.1,0.9),\qquad P=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&0.5\\
0.5&0.5\end{bmatrix}.

Then the switching family has one matrix

M=I-\alpha D+\alpha\gamma DP=\begin{bmatrix}0.9505&0.0405\\
0.3645&0.5545\end{bmatrix}.

Its spectral radius
\rho(M)
is approximately
0.9848
, while the row-sum bound gives

\rho_{\mathrm{row}}=1-0.9\cdot 0.1\cdot(1-0.9)=0.991.

Thus the direct drift rate is strictly faster than the comparison row-sum rate in this instance. In multi-action problems, the same effect is measured by the JSR
\rho_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dir}}=\rho_{\mathrm{jsr}}(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})
and by feasible certificate rates such as a JSR-induced rate
\beta_{\varepsilon}
.
