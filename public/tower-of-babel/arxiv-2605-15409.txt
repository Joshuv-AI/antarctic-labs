A Model of a Buoyancy-Driven Heat Exchanger, with Implications for Optimal Design
Sylvie Bronsard and Charles S. Peskin

Abstract




Abstract

In this paper, we introduce a model for a buoyancy-driven, air-to-air heat exchanger. This model, derived from first principles, features a conservative boundary condition at inflow based on the compressible Bernoulli equation, and a dissipative boundary condition at outflow based on pressure continuity. We solve for the steady-state behavior numerically and asymptotically, with excellent agreement between the two, and we study the tradeoff between the efficiency and air flow predicted by the model.

1
Introduction

It takes significant energy expenditure to maintain comfortable indoor conditions in a well ventilated building
[
11
]
. In cold climates, fresh air from outdoors must be heated to indoor temperature; the energy required to do this can be lessened by using a device, called a heat exchanger, which uses the exhaust air to warm the incoming air.

Heat exchangers are used both in industry and in nature. For example, they improve fuel efficiency in
airplanes by using heat from the engine to preheat the fuel. They are also used in the making
of wine and beer, for pasteurizing dairy products, and more generally in the pharmaceutical,
food, and drink industries
[
18
]
. In nature, for example, flamingoes and other wading birds use
heat exchangers to keep their bodies warm while standing in cool water.

In buildings, heat exchangers can be active, using a fan or pump to force fluid flow; or passive. Passive heat exchangers make use of the fact that warm air is lighter than cold air. A simple example is a device consisting of a tube with a partition down the middle, allowing warm air to rise through one side of the heat exchanger, and cold air to
sink down the other. As the warm air rises, some of its heat is transferred to the incoming colder air across the partition, thus decreasing heating costs. Although active heat exchangers are more common, passive exchangers do
not require input energy (so they continue to run during a blackout),
are more quiet, require less maintenance, and have a longer operating
life than active heat exchangers
[
13
]
.

Buoyancy driven ventilation has been installed in schools
[
7
]
, with the additional benefit of being particularly effective at expelling small droplets and airborne particles from the indoor air, thus circulating fresh air in such a way as to reduce the spread of infectious disease
[
1
]
. Since a human being’s internal temperature is warmer than the air temperature, the air we exhale naturally rises, carrying with it the particles and pathogens contained within. Buoyancy driven ventilation takes advantage of this natural stratification, and replaces the stale air without forced mixing.

Since heat exchangers are in use in many industrial applications, they have naturally been extensively studied by engineers. This existing literature may be broadly divided into two categories: the first and largest category contains articles and review papers that use experimental methods and simple models based on empirical laws. In these models, the pressure difference determines the fluid velocity, which in turn determines the heat transfer between the fluids, without differential equations describing the fluid flow within the device. These works ask interesting empirical questions about the effect of turbulent versus laminar flow on the fluid velocity and heat transfer between fluids. They study the effects of modifying the shape of the heat exchanger (for example, by putting nails through the partition), and build experimental setups to test their models
[
15
,
5
,
8
]
. Most, but not all, of the existing literature focuses on driven heat exchangers rather than passive ones.

There has also been recent work using detailed three-dimensional
computational fluid dynamic (CFD) models to simulate fluid flow in driven, aluminum plate-fin heat exchangers, studying fully developed flow conditions and taking into account entrance effects on the fluid flow
[
4
]
.

The present paper introduces a framework that is intermediate in
complexity between the two types of models described above. Our
framework is based on one-dimensional compressible steady-state fluid dynamics within a pair of tubes, with heat conduction across a
partition that separates the two tubes. We neglect fluid viscosity
and heat conduction within each tube. At the entrance to each tube we use the compressible Bernoulli equation, which is energy
conserving. At the exit of each tube, we use a continuous-pressure
boundary condition that dissipates kinetic energy, and this dissipation is essential for the existence of a steady state in our
model (see Appendix). Our model is passive, with flow driven by gravity as a result of the difference in density between warm air and
cold air. The equations of the model are solved
numerically and an asymptotic analysis is also done, with excellent agreement between the numerical and asymptotic results. By both
methods, we determine the efficiency of the heat exchanger, suitably
defined, and the relative mass flux, also suitably defined, of fresh air brought in by the heat exchanger. We observe that the objective of maximizing efficiency is in conflict with the objective
of maximizing the relative mass flux, but we are able to maximize the minimum of these two objective functions and in that sense to
optimize the design of a passive heat exchanger.

2
Mathematical Formulation

We model a buoyancy-driven heat exchanger as a pair of one-dimensional vertical tubes in contact along their length, allowing warm air to rise through one side of the heat exchanger, and cold air to sink down the other. We ignore the effects of viscosity and thermal conductivity within the fluid, so that heat is only transferred across the partition between the two tubes. We focus on the steady-state behavior of the heat exchanger.

In other words, we want to study steady gas dynamics in a vertical tube, under the effect of gravity, and with a heat source or sink along the length of the tube. The partition between the two tubes is assumed to be a rectangle of width
W
and height
H
.

A diatomic gas at ordinary temperatures satisfies the ideal gas law,

p=\rho\left(\frac{RT}{m}\right),

(1)

and has internal energy (per mole)

e=\frac{5}{2}\left(\frac{RT}{m}\right),

(2)

where
p
is the pressure,
\rho
the density,
T
the temperature,
m
the mass per mole, and where R is the molar gas constant.

2.1
Boundary Conditions

We now turn to the boundary conditions. At the inflow to each tube, we picture steady flow from a reservoir; the interior of the house for tube 1, the exterior air for tube 2. We assume adiabatic equilibrium between the air at the tube entrance and the air in the reservoir, which we take to be at the same height as the tube entrance. We assume that the air flow from the reservoir to the entrance of the corresponding tube is governed by the 3D compressible Euler equation,

\rho\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla u\right)+\nabla p=0.

(11)

with the adiabatic equation of state

2
2

2

since we are assuming that there is negligible heat transfer
(as well as negligible viscosity) between adjacent parcels of fluid
within the flow from each reservoir into the corresponding tube.

p/\rho^{\gamma}=\text{constant,}

(12)

where
\gamma=\frac{7}{5}
is the ratio of the specific heat at constant pressure to the specific heat at constant volume for a diatomic gas.

Since all parcels of fluid come from the same place, namely the reservoir, the constant in equation (
12
) is independent of position throughout either one of the two entrance flows. The constants may be different, however, for the two entrance flows. In particular, we can write

p/\rho^{\gamma}=p_{\text{res}}/\rho_{\text{res}}^{\gamma},

(13)

where
p_{\text{res}}
and
\rho_{\text{res}}
are, respectively, the pressure and density in the reservoir.

Armed with equations (
11
) and (
13
), we are ready for action. We first divide (
11
) by
\rho
to obtain

\frac{\partial{u}}{\partial{t}}+u\cdot\nabla u+\frac{\nabla p}{\rho}=0.

(14)

We can rewrite
\frac{\nabla p}{\rho}
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla p}{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{\text{res}}}{\rho_{\text{res}}}\cdot\frac{p_{\text{res}}}{p_{\text{res}}}\cdot\frac{\nabla p}{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\text{res}}}{\rho_{\text{res}}}\cdot\frac{\rho_{\text{res}}}{\rho}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{p}{p_{\text{res}}}\right).

(15)

Here we note that
\frac{p_{\text{res}}}{\rho_{\text{res}}}=\frac{RT_{\text{res}}}{m}
, and that (
13
) is equivalent to
\frac{\rho_{\text{res}}}{\rho}=\left(\frac{p_{\text{res}}}{p}\right)^{1/\gamma}
, so

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla p}{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{RT_{\text{res}}}{m}\left(\frac{p}{p_{\text{res}}}\right)^{-1/\gamma}\nabla\left(\frac{p}{p_{\text{res}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{RT_{\text{res}}}{m}\left(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\nabla\left(\left(\frac{p}{p_{\text{res}}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right)\right).

(16)

Using (
13
) once again, we further note:

\frac{p}{\rho}=\frac{p}{\rho_{\text{res}}\left(\frac{p}{p_{\text{res}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}=\frac{p_{\text{res}}}{\rho_{\text{res}}}\left(\frac{p}{p_{\text{res}}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}=\frac{RT_{\text{res}}}{m}\left(\frac{p}{p_{\text{res}}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}},

(17)

so
\frac{\nabla p}{\rho}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\nabla\left(\frac{p}{\rho}\right)
. We can therefore write

\frac{\partial{u}}{\partial{t}}+u\cdot\nabla u+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\nabla\left(\frac{p}{\rho}\right)=0.

(18)

In steady flow
\frac{\partial{u}}{\partial{t}}=0
, and for a diatomic ideal gas
\gamma=\frac{7}{5}
, therefore

\displaystyle u\cdot\nabla u+\frac{7}{2}\nabla\left(\frac{p}{\rho}\right)=0

(19)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle u\cdot\left(u\cdot\nabla u+\frac{7}{2}\nabla\left(\frac{p}{\rho}\right)\right)=0

(20)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle u\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}+\frac{7}{2}\left(\frac{p}{\rho}\right)\right)=0,

(21)

since
u\cdot\left(u\cdot\nabla u\right)=u\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}
.
We conclude that the quantity
\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}+\frac{7}{2}\left(\frac{p}{\rho}\right)
is constant along streamlines. This is known as the compressible Bernoulli condition
[
2
, §3.11]
. Since
u=0
in the reservoir, at each tube entrance we then have
\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}+\frac{7}{2}\left(\frac{p}{\rho}\right)=\frac{7}{2}\left(\frac{p_{\text{res}}}{\rho_{\text{res}}}\right)
. The entrance to tube 1 is at
z=0
, and the entrance to tube 2 is at
z=H
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u_{1}^{2}(0)+\frac{7}{2}\frac{RT_{1}(0)}{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{2}\frac{RT_{{\operatorname{int}}}}{m}

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u_{2}^{2}(H)+\frac{7}{2}\frac{RT_{2}(H)}{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{2}\frac{RT_{{\operatorname{ext}}}}{m}

(23)

and

\displaystyle\frac{p_{1}(0)}{\rho_{1}(0)^{\frac{5}{7}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{{\operatorname{int}}}}{\rho_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\frac{5}{7}}}

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{2}(H)}{\rho_{2}(H)^{\frac{5}{7}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{{\operatorname{ext}}}}{\rho_{{\operatorname{ext}}}^{\frac{5}{7}}}.

(25)

Although we expect a smooth, energy-preserving flow at the entrance of each tube, as the fluid leaves the tube it collides with the still air outside the tube, and so its kinetic energy is lost. As a result, there is not the pressure recovery that would be predicted by the Bernoulli equation; instead, the pressure in the tube at the exit matches the pressure of the external air:

\displaystyle p_{1}(H)=p_{0}

(26)

\displaystyle p_{2}(0)=p_{{\operatorname{int}}}.

(27)

Here,
p_{0}
is the atmospheric pressure and
p_{{\operatorname{int}}}
is the pressure in the house. Although we regard
p_{0}
as given,
p_{{\operatorname{int}}}
is an unknown which is determined by the condition of mass conservation. Since we are assuming no flow through the walls of the house, the net flow of mass through the heat exchanger as a whole must be zero, and
p_{{\operatorname{int}}}
will have to adjust to achieve this condition.

Equations
\eqref{outbc1}-\eqref{outbc2}
imply energy dissipation at the outflow of each tube, since kinetic energy is dissipated. This will be shown in the appendix, and it is an important feature of our model, needed to achieve a steady state since there is no other dissipative mechanism in the model.

2.2
Power and Efficiency

We are interested in the energy efficiency as well as the mass flux of fresh air predicted by the above model. In this section we define the power
P
required to maintain the internal temperature of the house at
T_{{\operatorname{int}}}
:

P=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(Au_{i}p_{i}+Au_{i}\left(e_{i}\rho_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{i}u_{i}^{2}\right)\right).

(28)

Note that
P
is independent of
z
. This follows by summing over
i=1,2
in equation (
5
) and noting that the sum of the right-hand sides is zero, since
\rho_{i}u_{i}
has the same magnitude and opposite sign in
the two tubes. Thus
P
is the flux of energy from below across any
plane
z=
constant. The term in
P
involving the pressure is the net rate at which work is being done by the pressure from below the plane

z=
constant on the air above it, and the remaining terms in
P

describe the net rate of upward transport by convection, across the
plane
z=
constant, of the internal energy and the kinetic energy of
the air.

The following formula relates the power requirement
P
to the important
overall variables:
T_{{\operatorname{int}}}
,
T_{{\operatorname{ext}}}
,
\Phi
, and
Q=\frac{W}{A}\int_{0}^{H}q{\,\rm{d}}z
.

We claim that

P=A\Phi\left(\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}\left(T_{{\operatorname{int}}}-T_{{\operatorname{ext}}}\right)-gH-\frac{Q}{\Phi}\right).

(29)

Proof.

Since
\Phi_{i}=\rho_{i}u_{i}
and
\Phi_{1}=-\Phi_{2}=\Phi
, we can write

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}A\Phi_{i}\left(\frac{7}{2}\frac{RT_{i}}{m}+\frac{1}{2}u_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=A\Phi\left(\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}\left(T_{1}-T_{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}\right)\right).

(30)

But we also know, by integrating (
10
) from
z=0
to
z=H
, that

\displaystyle\left.\frac{7}{2}\frac{RT_{i}}{m}\right|_{0}^{H}+\left.\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\right|_{0}^{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{H}-\frac{q}{\Phi}\frac{W}{A}-g{\,\rm{d}}z

\displaystyle=-\frac{W}{\Phi A}\int_{0}^{H}q{\,\rm{d}}z-gH.

(31)

Using our boundary condition (
22
), we now have that

\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}T_{1}(H)+\frac{1}{2}u_{1}^{2}(H)=\frac{7}{2}\frac{RT_{{\operatorname{int}}}}{m}-gH-\frac{Q}{\Phi}.

(32)

Subtracting from this the boundary condition (
23
) gives us

\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}T_{1}(H)+\frac{1}{2}u_{1}^{2}(H)-\left(\frac{7}{2}\frac{RT_{2}(H)}{m}+\frac{1}{2}u_{2}^{2}(H)\right)=\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}(T_{{\operatorname{int}}}-T_{{\operatorname{ext}}})-gH-\frac{Q}{\Phi},

(33)

or, written in a more evocative form,

\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}\left(T_{1}(H)-T_{2}(H)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{1}^{2}(H)-u_{2}^{2}(H)\right)=\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}(T_{{\operatorname{int}}}-T_{{\operatorname{ext}}})-gH-\frac{Q}{\Phi}.

(34)

We now turn back to our expression for the power requirement of a heat exchanger, given by equation (
30
). Since the right hand side of (
30
) does not depend on
z
, we can evaluate it at any height, in particular at
z=H
:

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=A\Phi\left(\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}\left(T_{1}(H)-T_{2}(H)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{1}^{2}(H)-u_{2}^{2}(H)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=A\Phi\left(\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}(T_{{\operatorname{int}}}-T_{{\operatorname{ext}}})-gH-\frac{Q}{\Phi}\right),

(35)

which is the same as (
29
).

∎

The purpose of a heat exchanger is to bring in fresh air in an energy-efficient manner, so we are interested in the energy cost per unit mass of fresh air that is brought in; that is, in the quantity

\frac{P}{A\Phi}=\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}(T_{{\operatorname{int}}}-T_{{\operatorname{ext}}})-gH-\frac{Q}{\Phi}.

(36)

We can define the efficiency of a heat exchanger as the relative difference in this quantity in comparison to the situation in which there is no heat exchange, i.e. when
Q=0
.

\displaystyle E:

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{P}{A\Phi}\right)_{Q=0}-\left(\frac{P}{A\Phi}\right)}{\left(\frac{P}{A\Phi}\right)_{Q=0}}

(37)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q/\Phi}{\frac{7}{2}\frac{R}{m}\left(T_{{\operatorname{int}}}-T_{{\operatorname{ext}}}\right)-gH}.

(38)

In equation (
36
) it is reasonable to assume that
P
,
Q
, and
\Phi
are all positive. For
P
, this is because the gravity-driven heat exchanger is a passive device, the power requirement of which cannot be negative. For
Q
, this is because heat flows passively from the warmer air (tube 1) to the cooler air (tube 2). For
\Phi
it is because the warm air will
naturally be rising and the cool air descending. With
P
,
Q
, and
\Phi
positive, it follows from (
36
) that the denominator in (
38
) is positive and also that the numerator in (
38
) is both positive and less than the
denominator. Thus,
E\in(0,1)
, as it should be.

Positivity of the denominator in equation (
38
) deserves further comment. This is a restriction on the given parameters for our model of the heat exchanger to make sense. What presumably happens when this condition fails is that symmetry breaking does not occur, and the air remains at rest in both tubes. There is then no heat loss, and no fresh air entering
the house.

2.3
Dimensionless formulation

To make the system easier to work with, we nondimensionalize. We start from the conservation equations in the form (
7
) and (
10
). To make the equations dimensionless, we choose units of temperature, length, mass and time. We choose the height of the tube

as the unit length,

as the unit temperature,

as the unit pressure, and

as the unit density. It follows that:

\displaystyle p_{{\operatorname{ext}}}H^{3}=\text{ unit energy;}

(39)

\displaystyle\rho_{{\operatorname{ext}}}H^{3}=\text{ unit mass;}

(40)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{p_{{\operatorname{ext}}}}{\rho_{{\operatorname{ext}}}}}=\sqrt{\frac{RT_{{\operatorname{ext}}}}{m}}=\text{ unit velocity};

We now introduce dimensionless variables,

Substituting these into the ideal gas law, we have:

\displaystyle p_{{\operatorname{ext}}}p^{\prime}

Since

We will also need the following:

\displaystyle\sigma

We also need to rewrite the boundary conditions in dimensionless form. Substituting and cancelling all dimensional factors, as we have done above with the momentum and energy conservation equations, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}T_{1}^{\prime}(0)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\Phi^{\prime})^{2}}{(\rho_{1}^{\prime}(0))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{2}T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}

(56)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{1}^{\prime}(0)}{(\rho_{1}^{\prime}(0))^{\frac{7}{5}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}}{(\rho_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime})^{\frac{7}{5}}}

(57)

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}T_{2}^{\prime}(1)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\Phi^{\prime})^{2}}{\rho_{2}^{\prime}(1))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{2}

(58)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{2}^{\prime}(1)}{(\rho_{2}^{\prime}(1))^{\frac{7}{5}}}

\displaystyle=1

(59)

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\prime}(1)

\displaystyle=1

(60)

\displaystyle p_{2}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime},

(61)

where
T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}=T_{{\operatorname{int}}}/T_{{\operatorname{ext}}}
is given; and
p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}=p_{{\operatorname{int}}}/p_{{\operatorname{ext}}}
and
\rho_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}=\rho_{{\operatorname{int}}}/\rho_{{\operatorname{ext}}}
are unknown but related through
T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}
and the ideal gas law (
46
).

In summary, the behavior of the heat exchanger is governed by the following three dimensionless parameters:

\displaystyle T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}=\frac{T_{{\operatorname{int}}}}{T_{{\operatorname{ext}}}}

(62)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}=\frac{gH}{\left(\frac{RT_{{\operatorname{ext}}}}{m}\right)}

(63)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime\prime}=\left(\frac{WH}{A}\right)\frac{T_{{\operatorname{ext}}}}{p_{{\operatorname{ext}}}\sqrt{\frac{RT_{{\operatorname{ext}}}}{m}}}\sigma.

(64)

If we choose reasonable physical parameters (such as setting the indoor air to room temperature, the external air to freezing, the height and width of the heat exchanger to 1m, and so on), the dimensionless parameters will look like
T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}=1.0732
,
g^{\prime}=1.2471\times 10^{-4}
, and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}=0.0018
. We show plots of what the system looks like with those parameters in Section
4
.

We also note that the dimensionless parameter
g^{\prime}
has an interesting physical interpretation: in the adiabatic atmosphere approximation, the thickness of the atmosphere is given by
\frac{RT_{{\operatorname{ext}}}}{mg}

[
14
]
. So the dimensionless parameter
g^{\prime}
is the ratio of the height of the heat exchanger to the thickness of the atmosphere.

Lastly, we should also write the efficiency (
38
) in terms of the dimensionless parameters. Substituting the dimensionless parameters as done above, we find that

E^{\prime}=\frac{Q^{\prime}/\Phi^{\prime}}{\frac{7}{2}\left(T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}-1\right)-g^{\prime}},

(65)

where

Q^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{\prime\prime}\left(T_{1}^{\prime}-T_{2}^{\prime}\right){\,\rm{d}}z^{\prime}.

(66)

From now on, we will always be working with dimensionless parameters, and so will drop the prime notation on the dimensionless variables, but retain them on the parameters. We will also drop one prime on the
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
and write
\sigma^{\prime}
instead, so that it matches the notation for the other dimensionless parameters.

3
Numerical Method

In this section, we describe our numerical solver for the above system. Let
\Delta z=\frac{H}{n-1}
, where
n
is the number of gridpoints we use for our discretization. We discretize our derivatives using a second-order upwind scheme. Discretizing our conservation of momentum equation gives the following system of equations, in the interiors of tubes 1 and 2 respectively:

\frac{3p_{1,j}-4p_{1,j-1}+p_{1,j-2}}{2\Delta z}-\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{1,j}^{2}}\left(\frac{3\rho_{1,j}-4\rho_{1,j-1}+\rho_{1,j-2}}{2\Delta z}\right)-g^{\prime}\rho_{1,j}=0,

(67)

for
j=3,...n
, and

\frac{-p_{2,j+2}+4p_{2,j+1}-3p_{2,j}}{2\Delta z}-\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{2,j}^{2}}\left(\frac{-\rho_{2,j+2}+4\rho_{2,j+1}-3\rho_{2,j}}{2\Delta z}\right)-g^{\prime}\rho_{2,j}=0,

(68)

j=1,...n-2
.

For the gridpoint directly downwind of the boundary, we must do something different, as we do not have two upwind points to draw from. We instead use a first order upwind scheme:

\displaystyle\frac{p_{1,2}-p_{1,1}}{\Delta z}-\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{1,2}^{2}}\left(\frac{\rho_{1,2}-\rho_{1,1}}{\Delta z}\right)-g^{\prime}\rho_{1,2}=0

(69)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{2,n}-p_{2,n-1}}{\Delta z}-\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{2,n-1}^{2}}\left(\frac{\rho_{2,n}-\rho_{2,n-1}}{\Delta z}\right)-g^{\prime}\rho_{2,n-1}=0,

(70)

This does not affect the overall order of the scheme, which remains second order in the
L^{\infty}
norm (as shown in the following section, see Fig
3
).

We do the same for our conservation of energy equation:

\frac{7}{2}\left(\frac{3T_{1,j}-4T_{1,j-1}+T_{1,j-2}}{2\Delta z}\right)-\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{1,j}^{3}}\left(\frac{3\rho_{1,j}-4\rho_{1,j-1}+\rho_{1,j-2}}{2\Delta z}\right)+\frac{\sigma^{\prime}}{\Phi}\left(T_{1,j}-T_{2,j}\right)+g^{\prime}=0,

(71)

for
j=3,...n
, and

\frac{7}{2}\left(\frac{-T_{2,j+2}+4T_{2,j+1}-3T_{2,j}}{2\Delta z}\right)-\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{2,j}^{3}}\left(\frac{-\rho_{2,j+2}+4\rho_{2,j+1}-3\rho_{2,j}}{2\Delta z}\right)+\frac{\sigma^{\prime}}{\Phi}\left(T_{1,j}-T_{2,j}\right)+g^{\prime}=0,

(72)

for
j=1,...n-2
.

And similarly, on the gridpoint directly upwind from the boundary, in each tube:

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}\left(\frac{T_{1,2}-T_{1,1}}{\Delta z}\right)-\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{1,2}^{3}}\left(\frac{\rho_{1,2}-\rho_{1,1}}{\Delta z}\right)+\frac{\sigma^{\prime}}{\Phi}\left(T_{1,2}-T_{2,2}\right)+g^{\prime}=0,

(73)

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}\left(\frac{T_{2,n}-T_{2,n-1}}{\Delta z}\right)-\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{2,n-1}^{3}}\left(\frac{\rho_{2,n}-\rho_{2,n-1}}{\Delta z}\right)+\frac{\sigma^{\prime}}{\Phi}\left(T_{1,n-1}-T_{2,n-1}\right)+g^{\prime}=0.

(74)

Lastly, to complete the system, we have the boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}T_{1,1}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{1,1}^{2}}-\frac{7}{2}T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}=0,

(75)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{1,1}}{\left(\rho_{1,1}\right)^{7/5}}-1=0,

(76)

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}T_{2,n}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{2,n}^{2}}-\frac{7}{2}=0,

(77)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{2,n}}{\left(\rho_{2,n}\right)^{\frac{7}{5}}}-1=0,

(78)

\displaystyle p_{1,n}-1=0,

(79)

\displaystyle p_{2,1}-p_{{\operatorname{int}}}=0.

(80)

Equations (
69
) through (
80
), together with the ideal gas law at every grid point, define a nonlinear system that we solve for the unknowns
\rho_{i,j},T_{i,j}
and
p_{i,j}
for
i=1,2
and
j=1,...,n
, and also
\Phi
and
p_{{\operatorname{int}}}
. Thus, after using the ideal gas law to remove
p_{i,j}
from the list of unknowns we must solve for, the number of unknowns is

2(2n)+2
.
After eliminating pressure from these equations, we are left with two equations connecting
that grid point to its upwind neighborhood at each grid point. This count is applicable to the grid points at the entrance to each tube, provided that we consider the “upwind neighborhood” of such a
grid point to be its upstream reservoir, to which it is coupled by the compressible Bernoulli equation and also the adiabatic equation of state. So far we have accounted for two equations per grid point, or
2(2n)
overall. The two additional equations that we need are provided by pressure continuity
at the exit of each tube with the pressure in the air that is
external to the tube at its exit. We solve these equations using Newton’s method, as detailed below.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be the vector of unknowns
3
3

3

We only store
\rho_{i,j}
and
T_{i,j}
, since
p_{i,j}=\rho_{i,j}T_{i,j}
.
,

\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{1,1}\ldots\rho_{1,n}&\rho_{2,1}\ldots\rho_{2,n}&T_{1,1}\ldots T_{1,n}&T_{2,1}\ldots T_{2,n}&\Phi&p_{{\operatorname{int}}}\end{bmatrix},

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X})
be the function that evaluates the left hand sides of equations (
67
) through (
80
), and

be the Jacobian of
\mathbf{F}
with respect to
\mathbf{X}
. At each step of Newton’s method, we compute the
k
th guess for
\mathbf{X}^{(k)}
by solving the following linear system, using Gaussian elimination:

J\left(\mathbf{X}^{(k-1)}\right)\left(\mathbf{X}^{(k)}-\mathbf{X}^{(k)}\right)=-F\left(\mathbf{X}^{(k-1)}\right).

We stop iterating when
\left\|F\left(\mathbf{X}^{(k)}\right)\right\|
or
\left\|\mathbf{X}^{(k)}-\mathbf{X}^{(k)}\right\|
are less than
10^{-12}
.

As an initial guess for the Newton iteration, we use the following:

\displaystyle\rho_{1,j}=1/T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime},\qquad\rho_{2,j}=1,

\displaystyle T_{1,j}=T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime},\qquad T_{2,j}=1,

\displaystyle p_{{\operatorname{int}}}=1,\qquad\Phi=1/2.

(81)

5
Asymptotic Predictions

We expect the dimensionless parameter
g^{\prime}
to be small; indeed, even for a heat exchanger as tall as
O(10)
meters and an outdoor temperature as cold as freezing,
273.15K
,
g^{\prime}
only gets as big as
O(0.001)
.

5.1
Ansatz

We assume the following expansions in the small parameter
g^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}=\sqrt{g^{\prime}}\sigma^{\prime(0)}

(83)

\displaystyle T_{i}(z)=T_{i}^{(0)}(z)+g^{\prime}T_{i}^{(1)}(z)

(84)

\displaystyle p_{i}(z)=p_{i}^{(0)}(z)+g^{\prime}p_{i}^{(1)}(z)

(85)

\displaystyle\rho_{i}(z)=\rho_{i}^{(0)}(z)+g^{\prime}\rho_{i}^{(1)}(z)

(86)

\displaystyle p_{{\operatorname{int}}}=p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(0)}+g^{\prime}p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(1)}

(87)

\displaystyle\Phi=\sqrt{g^{\prime}}\left(\Phi^{(0)}+g^{\prime}\Phi^{(1)}\right).

(88)

We want expressions for
T_{i}^{(0)}(z),p_{i}^{(0)}(z),\rho_{i}^{(0)}(z),p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(0)},
and
\Phi^{(0)}
.

5.2
Conservation of Momentum, and Energy Dissipating Boundary Conditions

Our conservation of momentum equation,

\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(p_{i}+\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{i}}\right)=-\rho_{i}g^{\prime},

(89)

gives us the following information:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(g^{\prime 0}):\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(p_{i}^{(0)}\right)=0

(90)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(g^{\prime}):\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(p_{i}^{(1)}+\frac{(\Phi^{(0)})^{2}}{\rho_{i}^{(0)}}\right)=-\rho_{i}^{(0)}.

(91)

So we have that, to lowest order (or, when
g^{\prime}=0
), the pressure in each tube is constant. We can then use the following boundary conditions to get the constants:

p_{1}(1)=1,\quad p_{2}(0)=p_{{\operatorname{int}}}.

(92)

This gives us that
p_{1}^{(0)}\equiv 1
and
p_{1}^{(1)}(1)=0
in tube 1, as well as
p_{2}^{(0)}\equiv p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(0)}
and
p_{2}^{(1)}(0)=p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(1)}
in tube 2.

Or, in other words:

\displaystyle p_{1}(z)=1+g^{\prime}p_{1}^{(1)}(z),\quad\text{with }p_{1}^{(1)}(1)=0,

(93)

\displaystyle p_{2}(z)=p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(0)}+g^{\prime}p_{2}^{(1)}(z),\quad\text{with }p_{2}^{(1)}(0)=p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(1)}.

(94)

5.3
Conservation of Energy, and Compressible Bernoulli Boundary Conditions

First, let’s recall the conservation of energy equation:

\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{7}{2}T_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Phi^{2}}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\right)=-\frac{\sigma^{\prime}}{\Phi}(T_{1}-T_{2})-g^{\prime}.

(95)

Substituting in to our expansion in
g^{\prime}
, we find out that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(g^{\prime 0}):\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{7}{2}T_{i}^{(0}\right)=-\frac{\sigma^{\prime(0)}}{\Phi^{(0)}}T_{D}^{(0)}

(96)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(g^{\prime}):\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{7}{2}T_{i}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\Phi^{(0)}}{\rho_{i}^{(0)}}\right)^{2}\right)=-\frac{\sigma^{\prime(0)}}{\Phi^{(0)}}\left(T_{D}^{(1)}-\frac{\Phi^{(1)}}{\Phi^{(0)}}T_{D}^{(0)}\right)-1,

(97)

where
T_{D}=T_{1}-T_{2}
.

We can use the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}T_{1}(0)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\Phi}{\rho_{1}(0)}\right)^{2}=\frac{7}{2}T_{{\operatorname{int}}}

(98)

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}T_{2}(1)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\Phi}{\rho_{2}(1)}\right)^{2}=\frac{7}{2},

(99)

to get an expression for the temperatures at 0th order:

\displaystyle T_{1}^{(0)}(z)=-\frac{2}{7}\frac{\sigma^{\prime(0)}}{\Phi^{(0)}}\cdot T_{D}^{(0)}\cdot z+T_{{\operatorname{int}}}

(100)

\displaystyle T_{2}^{(0)}(z)=-\frac{2}{7}\frac{\sigma^{\prime(0)}}{\Phi^{(0)}}\cdot T_{D}^{(0)}\cdot(z-1)+1,

(101)

where
T_{D}=\frac{T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}-1}{1+\frac{2}{7}\frac{\sigma^{\prime(0)}}{\Phi^{(0)}}}
.

Remaining Boundary Conditions

We have one remaining boundary condition in each tube, and have yet to determine

and

\frac{p_{1}(0)}{\rho_{1}(0)^{7/5}}=\frac{p_{\operatorname{int}}}{\rho_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{7/5}}

To 0th order, this gives us that

.

Next, we have that

\frac{p_{2}(1)}{\rho_{2}(1)^{7/5}}=1.

(103)

This does not give us anything new, so we need to find something else to determine

5.5
Using the Order

Let’s revisit our boundary conditions, starting with the order

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}T_{1}^{(1)}(0)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\Phi^{(0)}T_{{\operatorname{int}}}\right)^{2}=0

\displaystyle\frac{7}{2}T_{2}^{(1)}(1)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\Phi^{(0)}\right)^{2}=0.

Next, the order

\displaystyle\frac{7}{5}\frac{T_{1}^{(1)}(0)}{T_{{\operatorname{int}}}}-\frac{2}{5}p_{1}^{(1)}(0)=-\frac{2}{5}p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\frac{7}{5}T_{2}^{(1)}(1)-\frac{2}{5}p_{2}^{(1)}(1)=0.

If we take twice (

\left(\Phi^{(0)}T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}\right)^{2}+2T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}p_{1}^{(1)}(0)=2T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(1)}.

Now we take twice (

\left(\Phi^{(0)}\right)^{2}+2p_{2}^{(1)}(1)=0.

Lastly, we look at the order

We have looked at all the boundary conditions, and now we use the order

term of the momentum conservation equation (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(p_{i}^{(1)}+(\Phi^{(0)})^{2}\frac{T_{i}^{(0)}}{p_{i}^{(0)}}\right)=-\rho_{i}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(p_{i}^{(1)}\right)=-(\Phi^{(0)})^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(T_{i}^{(0)}\right)-\rho_{i}^{(0)},

since

\displaystyle 0-\left(p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(1)}-\frac{T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}}{2}(\Phi^{(0)})^{2}\right)=-(\Phi^{(0)})^{2}\left(-\frac{2}{7}\frac{(\sigma^{\prime})^{(0)}}{\Phi^{(0)}}T_{D}^{(0)}\right)-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{T_{1}^{(0)}(z)}dz

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(1)}=(\Phi^{(0)})^{2}\left(\frac{T_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{\prime}}{2}-\frac{2}{7}\frac{(\sigma^{\prime})^{(0)}}{\Phi^{(0)}}T_{D}^{(0)}\right)+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{-\frac{2}{7}\frac{\sigma^{\prime(0)}}{\Phi^{(0)}}T_{D}^{(0)}z+T_{{\operatorname{int}}}}dz.

(115)

Similarly, in tube 2:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\Phi^{(0)})^{2}-p_{{\operatorname{int}}}^{(1)}=-(\Phi^{(0)})^{2}\left(-\frac{2}{7}\frac{(\sigma^{\prime})^{(0)}}{\Phi^{(0)}}T_{D}^{(0)}\right)-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{T_{2}^{(0)}(z)}dz

6
Summary and Conclusions

In this paper, we introduce a complete model of an air-to-air, buoyancy driven heat exchanger, based on one-dimensional gas dynamics. We consider only the steady state equations, and devise a second-order accurate numerical method to explore the consequences of these equations. An asymptotic method is also introduced, based on a very small parameter, the ratio of the height of the heat exchanger to the thickness of the atmosphere in the adiabatic atmospheric model. The asymptotic and numerical results are in excellent agreement.

Making use of these methods, we are able to evaluate the efficiency of the heat exchanger. We define efficiency by considering the energy required to heat each unit mass of fresh air that is brought into the house. In the absence of heat exchange, this is simply the energy cost of raising the temperature of the unit mass of air from the external to the internal temperature. The relative reduction in this power requirement in the presence of heat exchange is our definition of efficiency (see equation (
38
)).

We find that there is a tradeoff in the design of a buoyancy driven heat exchanger between minimizing heat loss and maximizing fresh air. As the thermal conductivity of the partition between the inflow and outflow increases, heat loss is reduced, but air flow is reduced as well. If we normalize the flow of fresh air by the largest amount that could flow through the heat exchanger, that is, by the amount of fresh air that would flow through it in the absence of heat exchange, then the optimal tradeoff is achieved when the normalized air flow is equal to the efficiency of the heat exchanger. At this point, with reasonable choices of parameters, the efficiency of the heat exchanger is approximately 60%.
