A polymicrobial opportunistic infection leading to osteolytic destruction and acute respiratory failure: a case report
Authors: Yanmei Huang, Fei Zheng, Wenjuan Cao, Ye Qiu, Mianluan Pan, Hairong Lin, Haiqiong Yu, Jianquan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolymicrobial infections involving opportunistic pathogens pose major diagnostic challenges, as clinical symptoms and imaging findings are often nonspecific. Comprehensive pathogen identification is essential for effective management.Case presentationThis report describes a case of polymicrobial infection caused by Cryptococcus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Aspergillus, and Mycobacterium abscessus in a diabetic patient. A 67-year-old male presented with cough, sputum production, and bone pain. Chest computed tomography revealed a cavitary lesion in the posterior segment of the left lower lobe. Histopathology of lung biopsy confirmed pulmonary cryptococcosis. Respiratory symptoms initially improved with antifungal therapy, but bone pain persisted. After treatment discontinuation, symptoms recurred and worsened, accompanied by fever and respiratory failure. Sputum culture grew Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and next-generation sequencing of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid revealed concurrent infections with Mycobacterium abscessus and Aspergillus fumigatus. Ultimately, the patient recovered and was discharged after targeted therapy.ConclusionsThis case underscores the inadequacy of single-pathogen frameworks in complex polymicrobial infections and highlights the clinical value of repeated assessment and a multimodal diagnostic approach. Early recognition of polymicrobial opportunistic infections may help prevent disease progression and improve clinical outcomes in susceptible patients.Keywords: Acute respiratory failure, Case report, Next-generation sequencing, Osteolytic destruction, Polymicrobial opportunistic infection

Background

Polymicrobial opportunistic infections pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Cryptococcus, Aspergillus, and non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are well-recognized opportunistic pathogens associated with pulmonary and disseminated infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals or patients with underlying diseases [1–4]. Infections caused by these organisms, whether occurring singly or in combination, can result in similar clinical symptoms, pulmonary imaging findings, and pathological features. Without timely and effective treatment, the prognosis is often poor. These pathogens exhibit distinct biological characteristics, and traditional diagnostic methods may not adequately identify all relevant pathogens. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has emerged as an important complementary diagnostic tool because of its ability to simultaneously detect bacteria, fungi, viruses, and mycobacteria without prior assumptions. In this case, NGS enabled identification of previously unrecognized pathogens and guided treatment. Therefore, integrating traditional diagnostic methods with modern techniques, such as NGS, along with repeated assessments, can provide a comprehensive understanding of the pathogen composition in infections. Thorough investigation of such complex cases may enhance clinicians’ awareness of polymicrobial opportunistic infections, ultimately optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old male patient was admitted on April 16, 2019, due to a cough and sputum production for 3 months. The patient presented with a cough productive of yellowish-white sputum in January 2019, without identifiable precipitating factors. In March 2019, he reported the onset of dyspnea, which exacerbated with physical exertion. Additionally, he experienced intermittent episodes of chest tightness and chest pain, along with palpitations and discomfort in the right wrist and lumbar region. The chest computed tomography (CT) conducted at a local hospital revealed a cavitary lesion in the posterior segment of the left lower lobe. Laboratory results indicated a white blood cell count of 7.11 × 109/L, hemoglobin of 125.0 g/L, and a neutrophil percentage of 88.1%. The patient was discharged after improvement following antibiotic treatment (details unspecified). After discharge, he continued to experience cough, sputum production, dyspnea on exertion, and joint pain, and was referred to our tertiary hospital. The patient had a 3-year history of hypertension. He reported no history of smoking, alcohol use, or immunosuppressive therapy. He maintained adequate nutrition and lived in a standard rural household, with no known high-risk exposures or travel to endemic areas.

Physical examination demonstrated a temperature of 36.5 ℃, respiratory rate of 20 breaths/min, heart rate of 97 beats/min, and blood pressure of 151/76 mmHg. The patient was in stable condition, alert and oriented. No thoracic deformities were noted; breath sounds were diminished in the left lung, with no wheezing or crackles auscultated. Upon admission to our hospital, routine blood examination revealed a white blood cell count of 5.37 × 109/L, with neutrophils comprising 79.1%. Hemoglobin level was recorded at 94.1 g/L. The C-reactive protein (CRP) level was elevated at 69.0 mg/L. The serum galactomannan antigen test (GM test) yielded a value of 0.763 (cutoff value for positivity: ≥0.45) µg/L, and the cryptococcal latex agglutination test (LAT) was positive with a dilution of 1:20 (reference value: negative). Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was 10.2%, fasting blood glucose was 8.8 mmol/L, and postprandial blood glucose at 2 h was 12.19 mmol/L. Serum immunoglobulin levels were within normal limits, with IgG 6.68 g/L, IgA 1.62 g/L, and IgM 0.57 g/L. The results of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antibody test, autoimmune antibody tests [1, 3], -β-D-glucan test (G test, normal range < 100 pg/ml), interferon-gamma autoantibody testing, lymphocyte subset analysis, and pulmonary-related tumor marker tests were all negative. Chest CT revealed an oval thin-walled cavitary lesion measuring approximately 4 × 3 × 4 cm in the left lower lobe, associated with exudative changes (Fig. 1). Emission computed tomography (ECT) demonstrated focal areas of increased radiotracer uptake in the L2 and L3 vertebrae as well as the right wrist joint (Fig. 2). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) revealed a significant quantity of fungal spores, and the cryptococcal latex agglutination test was positive. Pathological examination of the bronchoscopy lung biopsy showed granulomatous inflammation in the left lower lobe, associated with chronic suppuration, multinucleated giant cells, and caseous necrosis, with negative acid-fast staining (Fig. 3A). Pathological analysis of the percutaneous lung biopsy demonstrated inflammatory exudates and fibroblasts filling the alveoli, along with fibrous thickening of the alveolar walls and varying degrees of mononuclear and lymphocytic infiltration, suggesting organizing pneumonia (Fig. 3B). The Gomori’s methenamine silver stain yielded a positive result, with morphological findings consistent with Cryptococcus (Fig. 3C). The final diagnoses were (1) cryptococcal pneumonia and (2) diabetes mellitus (3) hypertension. The patient was treated with hypoglycemic agents and intravenous voriconazole (loading dose 6 mg/kg every 12 h on the first day, followed by 4 mg/kg every 12 h) for 7 days. Due to financial constraints, he requested discharge and was switched to the more affordable oral itraconazole (200 mg daily). After one month of antifungal therapy, his respiratory symptoms, including cough, sputum production, dyspnea, chest tightness, and chest pain, improved. Chest CT revealed a reduction in the size of the cavitary lesion in the left lower lobe, with thinning of the cavity wall (Fig. 4). However, he continued to experience joint and lower back pain.

In June 2019, the patient independently discontinued itraconazole for two weeks, leading to a recurrence of respiratory symptoms, exacerbation of joint and lower back pain, and the emergence of new symptoms including fever and weight loss. A complete blood count performed at a local hospital revealed a white blood cell count of 12.80 × 109/L, with neutrophils constituting 72.00%, and a hemoglobin level of 99.00 g/L. Sputum culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed. The culture yielded Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Susceptibility testing demonstrated sensitivity to ceftazidime and piperacillin-tazobactam, and resistance to levofloxacin. The organism was identified using a fully automated microbial identification mass spectrometry system (AutoMS1000, Autobio Diagnostics Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, China). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using a fully automated microbial identification and susceptibility testing system (AutoMIC-i600, Autobio Diagnostics Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, China) employing the broth microdilution method according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Guided by antimicrobial susceptibility testing, the patient was treated with intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam (4.5 g every 8 h) for 3 days, followed by intravenous ceftazidime (2 g every 8 h) for 4 days. However, no significant clinical improvement was observed, and he was subsequently readmitted to our hospital. Physical examination showed a temperature of 37.9℃, a respiratory rate of 24 breaths/min, a pulse rate of 132 beats/min, and a blood pressure of 130/82 mmHg. Laboratory results indicated a white blood cell count of 6.69 × 109/L, with neutrophils accounting for 95.30%; hemoglobin was 97.90 g/L. CRP was elevated at 147.72 mg/L. The GM yielded a value of 0.825 µg/L, and the LAT was positive at a dilution of 1:20. Arterial blood gas analysis confirmed type I respiratory failure (pH 7.49, PaO2 42.7 mmHg, PaCO2 21.2 mmHg, SaO2 78%, FiO2 21%, base excess − 5.6 mmol/L). The chest CT conducted on June 28, 2019, revealed bilateral pulmonary infection, with a slightly enlarged thin-walled cavitary lesion measuring approximately 4.3 cm×2.6 cm×3.8 cm in the posterior segment of the left lower lobe, along with bilateral pleural effusion (Fig. 5). The patient was diagnosed with severe pneumonia, respiratory failure, and diabetes mellitus. The patient showed no clinical improvement despite treatment with broad-spectrum antibiotics (meropenem and teicoplanin), antifungal therapy with voriconazole, and non-invasive ventilation. On July 3, the patient remained febrile, and follow-up CT revealed diffuse consolidation and ground-glass opacities in both lungs, with increased pericavitary exudation and worsening bilateral pleural effusion (Fig. 6). A bronchoscopy and bronchoalveolar lavage were repeated, and NGS detected the presence of Mycobacterium abscessus and Aspergillus fumigatus in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. The final diagnosis was a polymicrobial pulmonary infection caused by Cryptococcus, Mycobacterium abscessus, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In response to these findings, treatment was escalated to include oral ethambutol (750 mg once daily) and intravenous moxifloxacin (400 mg once daily), which resulted in significant improvement in fever, respiratory symptoms, and bone pain. Chest CT on July 16 showed significant absorption of pulmonary lesions, with a reduction in the size of the cavitary lesion in the posterior segment of the left lower lobe and a slight decrease in bilateral pleural effusion. The patient was discharged on July 22. Following discharge, he continued a regimen of oral moxifloxacin (400 mg once daily), clarithromycin (500 mg twice daily), ethambutol (750 mg once daily), and voriconazole (200 mg twice daily). The patient received the aforementioned oral medications and underwent follow-up at a local hospital. One month post-discharge, his bone pain resolved, and follow-up chest CT showed significant absorption of the bilateral pulmonary lesions. After one year of continued treatment, the pulmonary lesions were completely absorbed. Five years of telephone follow-up indicated no recurrence of infection.

Discussion

This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of polymicrobial opportunistic infections and the inadequacy of a single-pathogen diagnostic framework. Although pulmonary cryptococcosis was confirmed microbiologically and pathologically at initial presentation, several atypical features were present, including persistent bone pain, osteolytic lesions, and incomplete consistency between clinical manifestations and imaging findings. These findings were not fully explained by isolated cryptococcal infection and should have raised suspicion for additional pathogens.

Upon readmission, the patient exhibited respiratory failure, with Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection identified as a significant contributing factor to the exacerbation of the clinical condition. Subsequent bronchoscopy and bronchoalveolar lavage with NGS ultimately supported the diagnosis by identifying Aspergillus. In addition, Mycobacterium abscessus was identified, although its role as a true pathogen versus colonizer initially remained uncertain. Based on the overall clinical course, cryptococcosis was considered the primary infection, Pseudomonas aeruginosa was regarded as a secondary bacterial infection, and Aspergillus and NTM were considered likely coexisting pathogens.

A key unresolved question is whether the patient initially had a polymicrobial infection or subsequently developed secondary infections during disease progression, an issue that warrants careful consideration. While the diagnosis of cryptococcosis can be confirmed through cryptococcal latex agglutination tests and pathology, several clinical issues were overlooked: (1) The pathology of cryptococcosis is predominantly characterized by granulomatous tissue formation, with purulent changes and caseous necrosis being infrequent [2]. In contrast, the latter two pathological features are more commonly observed in infections caused by Aspergillus, Mucorales, NTM, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis [4–6]. (2) Notably, a positive GM test is rare in cases of cryptococcosis. (3) The patient presented with a prominent complaint of bone pain, and bone ECT revealed osteolytic destruction. However, this finding was initially underestimated, as we attributed the damage to cryptococcosis. Literature reports [7, 8] suggest that osteolytic destruction attributed to cryptococcosis is uncommon, with only sporadic cases documented in immunocompromised patients. In clinical practice, osteolytic destruction is more commonly associated with infections caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, NTM, Aspergillus species, Mucorales, Nocardia, and Actinomyces. Initially, no further pathogen investigation was performed. Although respiratory symptoms showed improvement following antifungal treatment, the bone pain remained unrelieved. (4) CT manifestations of pulmonary cryptococcosis typically present as solitary or multiple pulmonary nodules or masses, often situated near the pleura. Cavitary lesions in the lungs may also be observed, with an incidence ranging from 11.0% to 34.6% [9]. CT manifestations of pulmonary aspergillosis may include dense, well-defined lesions, with or without the halo sign; the air crescent sign; single or multiple cavities containing irregular contents; as well as wedge-shaped, segmental, or lobar consolidations [3]. Upon meticulous review of the patient’s lung CT, in addition to the thin-walled cavitary lesion in the left lower lung, irregular lesions were observed that align more closely with the imaging characteristics of aspergillosis. Surrounding these irregularities were small nodular lesions, while the mediastinal window revealed a granulomatous dense opacity, which is more consistent with changes associated with cryptococcosis. After the discontinuation of antifungal therapy, the respiratory symptoms recurred, accompanied by fever. A follow-up CT scan demonstrated marked thickening of the walls of the cavitary pulmonary lesions, along with an increased number of surrounding exudative lesions. Both the clinical progression and imaging findings were consistent with the advancement of pulmonary aspergillosis.

A retrospective review of the clinical course provided further clues regarding the role of NTM infection. Although pulmonary manifestations improved following antifungal therapy, the patient’s bone pain persisted. Early imaging demonstrated osteolytic lesions and increased metabolic activity in the involved joints, and lung pathology revealed chronic suppurative inflammation—findings that are more commonly associated with NTM infection. Increasing evidence suggests that NTM, including Mycobacterium abscessus, Mycobacterium avium complex, Mycobacterium kansasii, Mycobacterium fortuitum, Mycobacterium intracellulare, Mycobacterium chelonae, and Mycobacterium intermedium, can cause osteolytic bone and joint infections [10, 11]. Notably, the patient’s bone pain improved rapidly after initiation of combination anti-NTM therapy, including meropenem, moxifloxacin, clarithromycin, and ethambutol. Taken together, these findings suggest that the osteolytic destruction was likely attributable to NTM infection.

In addition to Aspergillus and NTM, other regional opportunistic pathogens, such as Talaromyces marneffei and Nocardia, were also considered in the differential diagnosis. However, these infections were considered less likely given the absence of characteristic radiologic features, the lack of supportive microbiological evidence, and no history of relevant epidemiologic exposure.

At the onset of the disease, the patient presented with hyperglycemia, as indicated by a glycated hemoglobin level exceeding 10%. This finding suggests pre-existing diabetes rather than stress hyperglycemia. Diabetes is a well-known risk factor for tuberculosis, aspergillosis, and cryptococcosis, but its role in NTM infection is less clear. Coinfections involving fungi (including cryptococci and Aspergillus) and NTM are commonly observed in patients with congenital or acquired immunodeficiency (such as those with positive γ-interferon antibodies, HIV-positive individuals, organ transplant recipients, those on immunosuppressive therapy, or with malignancies) or underlying pulmonary diseases (for instance, post-tuberculosis, cystic fibrosis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) [1]. Diabetes alone may not fully explain host susceptibility for these opportunistic coinfections. There may also be unknown cellular or extracellular immune abnormalities, warranting further immunological investigation.

This 2019 case was selected because it documents the complete diagnostic evolution of a polymicrobial opportunistic infection during a period when NGS was not yet routinely accepted in clinical practice. Subsequent clinical experience has confirmed the reliability of NGS in identifying pathogens such as Aspergillus and Mycobacterium abscessus, allowing the retrospective interpretation of this case with greater diagnostic clarity. The findings illustrate how the integration of emerging molecular diagnostics with established microbiological techniques can enhance pathogen detection and refine clinical decision-making. This case further supports a diagnostic strategy based on repeated evaluation and multimodal microbiological investigations, particularly in patients with complex or refractory infections, and underscores the growing role of combined diagnostic approaches in contemporary infectious disease practice.

Conclusions

In summary, this case underscores the diagnostic complexity of simultaneous polymicrobial opportunistic infections in a diabetic patient. Early focus on cryptococcal infection delayed the identification of Aspergillus, Mycobacterium abscessus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, contributing to disease progression. A more comprehensive diagnosis was achieved through the combined application of culture and NGS, highlighting the critical role of repeated, multimodal microbiological evaluation. Clinicians should remain alert to potential underlying immune deficiencies and implement comprehensive diagnostic strategies in patients with refractory or complicated pulmonary infections.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the direct cause of the patient’s bone pain and osteolytic lesions was not confirmed by biopsy or culture; attribution to NTM infection was inferred from treatment response, a common challenge in retrospective case reports. Second, follow-up bone imaging at the end of therapy was not performed, limiting direct assessment of post-treatment changes. Nevertheless, symptom resolution and over five years of follow-up without recurrence suggest healing and effectively rule out malignancy. Third, the initial treatment of cryptococcal infection was not fully consistent with guideline recommendations, largely due to the patient’s financial limitations. Early discharge and transition to oral itraconazole resulted in an insufficient treatment course and disease relapse, while continued intravenous voriconazole during readmission was also ineffective, indicating additional contributing factors. Fourth, as a single retrospective case, some clinical information from external hospitals, including early antibiotic regimens, could not be fully retrieved. Finally, interpretation of NGS results requires caution: although NGS offers high sensitivity and simultaneous detection of multiple pathogens, it may also identify colonizing microorganisms or environmental contaminants. Differentiating true pathogens from background signals requires careful correlation with clinical manifestations, imaging findings, conventional microbiology, and treatment response. In this case, the pathogenic roles of Mycobacterium abscessus and Aspergillus fumigatus were supported by clinical deterioration, radiologic progression, and response to targeted therapy, reinforcing the clinical relevance of the NGS findings.
