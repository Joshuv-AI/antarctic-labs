Methotrexate Toxicity in a Patient With Renal Failure Despite a Normal Methotrexate Concentration: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Fatemeh Afra, Mahshad Ghoddusi, Mahsa Mottahedi, Maryam Albaji, Samira Kafan, Roya Saedpanah, Amir Shokri, Elham Nazar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMethotrexate (MTX) is a folate antagonist widely used as a disease‐modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), as well as in several malignancies and autoimmune disorders. Although low‐dose MTX (5–20 mg/week) is generally considered safe, it has a narrow therapeutic index, and toxicity can occur even at therapeutic doses, particularly in high‐risk patients. We describe a 58 year‐old man with RA and chronic kidney disease (CKD) who presented with gastrointestinal symptoms, neurologic impairment, and a papulopustular rash. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia and acute kidney injury (AKI) superimposed on CKD. Despite a normal serum MTX level, clinical findings strongly suggested MTX toxicity. The patient received emergent hemodialysis, leucovorin rescue therapy, hematologic support, and intensive care management. Extensive investigations excluded alternative hematologic, infectious, and systemic causes. His clinical course was complicated by acute pancreatitis, and he ultimately died. This case emphasizes that serum MTX levels may not reliably predict toxicity in high‐risk patients and underscores the importance of recognizing clinical signs and symptoms, promptly identifying early manifestations, discontinuing MTX, and initiating leucovorin rescue therapy.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, drug toxicity, methotrexate, pancytopenia, renal insufficiency, rheumatoid arthritis

Introduction

Methotrexate (MTX) is a folate antagonist that exerts its therapeutic effects primarily through inhibition of dihydrofolate reductase, resulting in impaired synthesis of purine and pyrimidine nucleotides and subsequent disruption of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) replication [1, 2]. By limiting DNA replication and cellular proliferation, this mechanism underpins the wide clinical applicability of MTX across diverse disease entities, ranging from malignancies such as leukemia to chronic inflammatory and autoimmune disorders, most notably rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and psoriasis [1, 3, 4].

In RA, MTX is widely regarded as the anchor disease‐modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) and remains the preferred first‐line therapy owing to its well‐established efficacy and sustained long‐term benefits [5]. Although low‐dose MTX is generally perceived as safe and is routinely prescribed in outpatient settings, the drug is characterized by a narrow therapeutic index, rendering patients vulnerable to toxicity in the setting of accidental dosing errors, altered pharmacokinetics, or insufficient monitoring [1, 6].

MTX toxicity has traditionally been associated with high‐dose regimens (e.g., in the MAP regimen for osteosarcoma, which is 12 g/m2), especially in chemotherapy protocols [7]. Nevertheless, growing evidence from case reports and observational studies suggests that clinically significant toxicity may also occur with low‐dose MTX, conventionally defined as 5–20 mg per week [4, 5, 8, 9, 10]. The clinical manifestations of MTX toxicity are heterogeneous and may involve mucocutaneous lesions, gastrointestinal injury, bone marrow suppression with consequent myelosuppression, as well as hepatic, renal, pulmonary, and neurologic involvement, some of which can be severe or life‐threatening [11, 12].

Low‐dose MTX toxicity appears to occur more frequently in patients with pre‐existing comorbidities or identifiable risk factors. Advanced age, impaired renal clearance, chronic kidney disease (CKD), concomitant use of medications such as nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and dosing errors or irregular adherence have all been associated with an increased risk of toxicity [1, 3, 13, 14].

In this case report, we present a 58 year‐old man with RA and CKD secondary to recurrent nephrolithiasis who developed MTX toxicity despite a reported serum MTX level within the expected therapeutic range. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of low‐dose MTX toxicity and underscores the importance of clinical vigilance in high‐risk populations.

Case History/Examination

A 58 year‐old man with a past medical history of RA, CKD (the patient's baseline creatinine during previous hospitalizations was 3.03 mg/dL) with prior episodes of dialysis, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), and recurrent nephrolithiasis presented to the emergency department with oliguria, hematuria, and progressive alteration in mental status.

Approximately 20 days prior to admission, he had undergone replacement of a double‐J ureteral stent due to bilateral ureteral calculi. He had been prescribed MTX at a weekly dose of 7.5 mg (three 2.5 mg tablets), although adherence was reported to be inconsistent. Notably, during the four consecutive days immediately preceding admission, he had taken 2.5 mg of MTX daily instead of the prescribed weekly regimen. He also reported intermittent NSAID use for pain control prior to presentation.

In the days leading up to admission, the patient developed progressive anorexia, nausea, vomiting, lethargy, intermittent delirium, and generalized weakness. Two to 3 days prior to presentation, a papulopustular rash appeared over the abdomen and trunk.

On arrival (day 0), vital signs were as follows: blood pressure (BP) 125/45 mmHg, pulse rate (PR) 88 beats per minute, respiratory rate (RR) 29 breaths per minute, temperature 37°C, and oxygen saturation (SPO2) 94% on ambient air.

Dermatological examination revealed scattered, non‐patterned papulopustular eruptions over the hypogastric region, anterior thighs, posterior thorax, and right side of the trunk. Some lesions demonstrated vesiculobullous morphology with clear fluid on an erythematous base, without tenderness or increased warmth, and were associated with mild pruritus (Figure 1). No petechiae, purpura, rheumatoid nodules, or neutrophilic dermatoses were observed, and nail pitting was absent.

The patient's cutaneous lesions.

Oral examination revealed a vesicular lesion with a dark erythematous border on the left buccal mucosa and a linear erythematous ulcer on the lower lip. The patient reported a similar episode of cutaneous lesions approximately 1 year earlier.

Neurologically, the patient was lethargic and responded to verbal stimuli by opening his eyes. According to family members, he had exhibited delirium prior to presentation.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

On admission (day 0), initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated pancytopenia and acute kidney injury (AKI) superimposed on CKD (Table 1). Given clinical evidence of uremia and hemodynamic instability, emergent hemodialysis was initiated via Shaldon catheter placement, and two sessions were performed on consecutive days (days 0–1). The patient was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close monitoring.

Given the high clinical suspicion for MTX toxicity based on the patient's mucocutaneous lesions and laboratory abnormalities, empirical treatment with leucovorin (100 mg every 6 h) and filgrastim (300 μg daily) was initiated early during hospitalization, prior to diagnostic confirmation. In addition, the patient received 2 units of packed red blood cells and 2 units of platelet transfusion.

On hospital day 3, serum MTX level was measured using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and was reported as 0.06 μmol/L; notably, this measurement was obtained after two sessions of hemodialysis.

A dermatology consultation was obtained during hospitalization, and the lesions were determined to be drug‐induced. Although histopathological evaluation was not feasible, alternative diagnoses, including Stevens‐Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), uremic rash, and neutrophilic dermatoses, were excluded based on clinical and morphological features.

Concurrently, an extensive evaluation for systemic causes of pancytopenia and AKI was undertaken. Investigations for leukemia, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) included peripheral blood smear, flow cytometry, and bone marrow examination. Flow cytometric immunophenotyping demonstrated a marked reduction in granulocytic lineage with relative lymphoid predominance (granulocytes 26%, erythroid lineage 10%, monocytes 2%, plasma cells 10%, lymphocytes 52%), including 11% CD19+ B‐lymphocytes and 30% CD3+ T‐lymphocytes (see Supporting Information).

Bone marrow aspiration and trephine biopsy revealed a mildly hypocellular marrow with myeloid hypoplasia and megaloblastic changes in both erythroid and myeloid lineages. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis showed scattered CD3‐positive T‐lymphocytes and few CD20‐positive B‐lymphocytes, while CD34 expression was < 1%, indicating no significant increase in blasts (Figure 2). These findings were consistent with a reactive marrow pattern and effectively excluded acute leukemia and lymphoproliferative disorders. In the absence of malignant infiltration and with otherwise preserved marrow architecture, hematologic malignancies were ruled out as the cause of pancytopenia.

Common viral etiologies were excluded by negative CMV PCR/antigen, HIV antigen/antibody, HCV antibody, and HBc antibody testing (Table 1). Additionally, ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor levels were within normal limits, effectively ruling out thrombotic microangiopathy, including acquired TTP.

Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated normal liver and spleen size and echogenicity, with no evidence of hepatosplenomegaly.

Taken together, the overall clinical, laboratory, and bone marrow findings were consistent with methotrexate MTX‐induced myelosuppressive toxicity, which was considered the most likely diagnosis.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Unfortunately, the patient subsequently developed severe abdominal pain, and acute pancreatitis was diagnosed during hospitalization. Imaging and clinical findings were consistent with non‐biliary pancreatitis, and no clear drug‐induced or obstructive etiology was identified. Unfortunately, the patient subsequently developed severe abdominal pain, and abdominal ultrasonography findings were suggestive of acute pancreatitis during hospitalization. Imaging and clinical findings were consistent with non‐biliary pancreatitis, and no clear drug‐induced or obstructive etiology was identified. Despite supportive management, the patient's condition deteriorated with the development of bloodstream infection in the setting of neutropenia, with blood cultures growing Klebsiella pneumoniae . Sepsis was considered the most likely cause of death, and the patient ultimately succumbed to septic complications during hospitalization.

Search Strategy for Literature Review

A narrative literature review was conducted to identify previously reported cases and studies related to low‐dose MTX toxicity, particularly in patients with renal impairment.

A structured search was performed in PubMed on January 2026 using the following strategy: (“Methotrexate” [Title/Abstract]) AND (toxicity [Title/Abstract] OR adverse effects [Title/Abstract]) AND (low‐dose [Title/Abstract] OR therapeutic [Title/Abstract] OR normal [Title/Abstract]) AND (“acute kidney injury” [Title/Abstract] OR renal insufficiency [Title/Abstract] OR kidney [Title/Abstract]) as follows: Discussion. Additional keywords included “methotrexate AND renal toxicity” and “methotrexate toxicity at normal serum levels.”

Studies and case reports were included if they reported MTX toxicity associated with low‐dose or therapeutic regimens and described renal impairment or renal dysfunction as a risk factor.

Studies were excluded if they reported high‐dose MTX used in oncologic protocols and did not provide sufficient clinical or laboratory details.

All eligible articles were screened based on title and abstract, followed by full‐text review. Reference lists of included articles were also manually screened to identify additional relevant reports (Table 2).

Discussion

We report a 58 year‐old man with a history of RA and CKD who developed severe low‐dose MTX toxicity despite a reported MTX concentration within the expected therapeutic range (the reported level was 0.06 μmol/L) on the third day of his hospitalization and two subsequent emergency dialysis sessions due to his uremic state. The presentation was characterized by pancytopenia, mucocutaneous injury, neurologic impairment, and acute kidney injury superimposed on pre‐existing renal dysfunction. Together, these findings highlight the complex relationship between methotrexate pharmacokinetics, tissue toxicity, and clinical vulnerability in high‐risk patients.

MTX is a folate antagonist with a molecular weight of 454.4 Da. After oral administration, it is rapidly absorbed and, subsequently, predominantly bound to albumin. Elimination occurs mainly via the kidneys, with approximately 80%–90% excreted as intact drug, and to a lesser extent through hepatic pathways. A reduction in renal function and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) leads to decreased renal clearance of MTX and consequently increases the risk of toxicity. Moreover, MTX itself, even at low doses, may adversely affect GFR and tubular secretion, creating a vicious cycle that further impairs its elimination. For this reason, periodic monitoring of renal function is recommended in patients with RA receiving MTX therapy [2, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

In addition, the concomitant use of certain medications, including NSAIDs, can increase free MTX concentrations through various mechanisms such as reduction of glomerular filtration and alteration of protein binding, thereby enhancing toxicity [20]. Conversely, folate supplementation (folic acid or calcium folinate), through a mechanism opposing MTX action, may reduce MTX‐related toxicity. Our patient did not receive folic acid as part of the RA treatment regimen due to non‐adherence to the prescribed regimen, and the concurrent use of NSAIDs along with a history of CKD likely contributed synergistically to MTX toxicity in this case [3, 17]. Furthermore, experimental animal data have suggested that hydroxychloroquine may alter MTX pharmacokinetics; however, the clinical relevance of this interaction in humans remains uncertain [21].

Multiple studies have demonstrated that MTX toxicity does not strongly correlate with serum drug levels [15, 17, 22], and that interindividual variability in pharmacokinetics and dose–response is substantial [17, 23]. In patients with RA, serum MTX levels measured 24 h after administration typically fall within a low range regardless of age, and therefore lack a reliable relationship with clinical toxicity, particularly in patients with impaired renal clearance such as in our case. Accordingly, routine monitoring of serum MTX concentrations in low‐dose regimens has limited clinical utility [17, 24, 25, 26].

For instance, in the study by Dhillon et al., despite receiving 140 mg of MTX weekly for 4 weeks, the reported serum MTX level was less than 0.04 μmol/L [4]. Similarly, in the study by Khuwaja et al., despite administration of 15 mg MTX weekly for 3 weeks, the serum MTX concentration was reported as 0.05 μmol/L [3]. Comparable to our case, the serum MTX level was reported to be less than 0.06 μmol/L, yet the patient developed oral and cutaneous lesions as well as pancytopenia, which represent the predominant clinical manifestations of MTX toxicity.

Studies have shown that mild renal impairment (stage 2 CKD) is not associated with increased MTX toxicity [27]. However, in patients with moderate renal disease (stage 3 CKD), a significant reduction in MTX clearance is observed; nevertheless, due to wide interindividual variability in pharmacokinetics, prediction of clearance on an individual basis is not feasible [28]. This variability renders MTX therapy in patients with stage 4 and stage 5 CKD clinically challenging and high‐risk.

In end‐stage renal disease (ESRD), no formal studies have evaluated the pharmacokinetics and clearance of MTX. MTX clearance via peritoneal dialysis and routine dialysis has been reported to be poor, likely due to the molecular weight of the drug [29]. In contrast, studies have demonstrated that the use of high‐flux hemodialysis membranes can significantly enhance effective MTX clearance and may therefore represent a reasonable adjunctive therapeutic option in MTX toxicity [30].

The mechanisms of MTX toxicity can be categorized into three groups. The first group includes direct gastrointestinal and bone marrow toxicity, which are dose‐dependent effects and represent the most common adverse reactions. These effects are mediated through folate antagonism [31]. Furthermore, the C677T polymorphism of the methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) gene, an enzyme involved in the folate reduction pathway, is present in approximately 30%–40% of Caucasian individuals and results in nearly a 50% reduction in MTHFR activity, thereby increasing susceptibility to toxicity. The second pathway encompasses idiosyncratic or hypersensitivity reactions, including pneumonitis; however, methotrexate‐induced pneumonitis is a very rare side effect. The third category comprises long‐term complications, such as hepatic and cardiovascular disorders, which are largely attributed to hyper‐homocysteinemia [31]. Thus, in patients with CKD, reduced renal clearance of MTX leads to drug accumulation, making dose‐dependent toxicities of the first category more likely to occur.

Leucovorin (folinic acid) represents a key component in the treatment of MTX toxicity. As a reduced form of folate, it circumvents dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) inhibition and restores intracellular folate availability, which in turn supports DNA synthesis in rapidly proliferating tissues such as the bone marrow and mucosal lining. Prompt initiation of leucovorin therapy is important in limiting the extent of hematologic and mucocutaneous adverse effects [2, 4, 13, 15, 32].

Regarding mucocutaneous manifestations of MTX, it can produce a broad spectrum of lesions, ranging from early oral mucositis and painful ulcerations to papulopustular, vesiculobullous, erosive, or even necrotic eruptions that may clinically mimic SJS or TEN [33, 34]. These findings may result from folate antagonism and impaired DNA synthesis in rapidly dividing epidermal and mucosal cells, a process exacerbated in patients with renal dysfunction due to reduced drug clearance and intracellular accumulation [17, 35, 36, 37].

In our patient, scattered papulopustular and vesiculobullous lesions on an erythematous base, accompanied by oral mucosal involvement, emerged in close temporal association with MTX exposure and AKI on CKD. The morphology, distribution, and timing of the eruption were highly suggestive of MTX‐induced cutaneous toxicity [33, 34].

Because of the patient's rapid clinical decline, pancytopenia, particularly severe thrombocytopenia, and prior bone marrow biopsy, a skin biopsy was deferred to avoid additional bleeding risk. Nevertheless, the diagnosis of MTX‐related cutaneous toxicity was supported by several converging clinical factors, including AKI on CKD, concomitant mucosal involvement, and evidence of multisystem toxicity affecting renal and neurologic function. Furthermore, alternative differential diagnoses were carefully considered and excluded based on clinical and laboratory evaluations. The lesions lacked features suggestive of infectious exanthems, vasculitis, neutrophilic dermatoses, immune‐mediated blistering disorders, and did not demonstrate the hallmark findings of SJS or TEN, such as widespread epidermal detachment, targetoid lesions, or severe mucosal sloughing [38, 39, 40].

In our review of the literature regarding individual case studies related to low dose Methotrexate use causing toxicity, MTX was prescribed for RA in four patients, for psoriatic arthritis in two patients, and for psoriasis in two patients. Although the administered doses and duration of therapy varied, all cases fell within the low‐dose MTX category. All patients fully recovered except for one patient who expired, similar to our case. The main treatment strategies, alongside other supportive measures as in our patient, included leucovorin rescue and granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) [2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 13, 14, 15].

Across these case reports, serum MTX levels were consistently below the toxic range described for high‐dose MTX, and no clear correlation between serum drug levels and severity of adverse effects was observed. Most patients had comorbidities such as CKD [2, 10, 14]. In four cases, elevations in serum creatinine were observed [5, 10, 13, 14]. Pancytopenia and mucosal lesions were also among the most commonly reported adverse effects, consistent with our findings [2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 13, 14, 15].

The case most closely resembling ours was reported by Surapaneni et al., in which the patient presented with fever, generalized weakness, ulcers of the mouth, fingers, and limbs, bilateral limb swelling, and pancytopenia despite receiving a lower weekly dose of MTX for 10 years. However, serum MTX levels were not measured in that case [2]. Similarly, in the study by Boey et al., an ESRD patient who had received two doses of 5 mg MTX presented with clinical features resembling our patient, including nausea and oral lesions, with a reported serum MTX level of 0.03 mmol/L, which was even lower than the MTX level observed in our patient. That patient also developed pancytopenia and hepatotoxicity, similarly to ours [15].

Furthermore, in the studies by Surapaneni and Riquelme‐Mc Loughlin, recovery from pancytopenia began approximately during the second week of treatment [2, 14], suggesting that hematologic recovery in patients with renal insufficiency occurs over a longer period compared with patients without renal involvement [41].

In conclusion, as also observed in the reviewed studies, this case demonstrates that clinically significant low‐dose MTX toxicity can occur despite apparently normal or very low serum MTX concentrations, particularly in patients with CKD or ESRD and additional risk factors such as advanced age or co‐administration of certain drugs such as NSAIDs. Our patient developed multi‐organ toxicity, including mucocutaneous lesions, pancytopenia, and AKI on CKD, despite a serum MTX level within the expected therapeutic range. The temporal association between renal impairment and mucosal involvement was strongly suggestive of MTX‐related toxicity. Serum MTX concentrations may be misleading and do not consistently reflect tissue toxicity, particularly in patients with impaired renal function; therefore, the diagnosis of MTX toxicity remains primarily clinical. Early recognition, prompt discontinuation of MTX, timely initiation of leucovorin rescue, and close clinical monitoring are essential. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for MTX toxicity in high‐risk patients, even in the presence of apparently reassuring laboratory results.

Accordingly, MTX toxicity should always be considered in patients with renal impairment or other predisposing factors, even when serum MTX levels fall within the expected therapeutic range.

This case report has some limitations. First, histopathological confirmation of cutaneous lesions was not feasible due to the patient's critical condition and thrombocytopenia; therefore, the diagnosis of MTX‐induced toxicity was based on clinical and morphological assessment, as well as flow cytometry and IHC of the patient's PBS. Second, the measured serum MTX level was obtained after two sessions of hemodialysis, which may have affected the reported concentration and limited the accurate interpretation of peak exposure, although MTX, due to its molecular weight, is not effectively removed by conventional dialysis. Nonetheless, this case adds to the growing body of literature highlighting that low‐dose MTX toxicity can be severe and potentially fatal in patients with renal impairment, even in the absence of elevated serum MTX levels.

Conclusion

This case highlights that low‐dose MTX toxicity may occur even in the presence of normal serum drug levels, particularly in patients with renal impairment and other predisposing factors. Clinical recognition remains essential, as laboratory values may not reliably reflect toxicity severity. Early discontinuation of MTX and prompt initiation of leucovorin rescue therapy are critical to improving outcomes.
