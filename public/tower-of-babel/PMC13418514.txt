From cyclohexyl isocyanide and diaminomaleonitrile to functional 5-(alkylamino)-6-substituted pyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitriles: synthesis, X-ray structural elucidation, antimicrobial testing and OLED analysis
Authors: Elizabeth John, Sushanta Lenka, Anil Kumar, Jwo-Huei Jou, Amal Al-Azmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
New derivatives of 5-(alkylamino)-6-aryl/alkylpyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitriles were successfully synthesized with good to moderate yields through the reaction of alkyl isocyanides with aryl/alkyl carbonyl chlorides, followed by the incorporation of diaminomaleonitrile. The fluorescence quantum yields (ΦF) were calculated and the fluorescence behavior of the produced compounds was examined. The cyclic voltammograms of compound 12a and compounds 14–17 with pyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitrile fragments were measured and the compounds were used in the fabrication of OLEDs. The compounds were dispersed in a commercial 4,4′-bis(N-carbazolyl)-1,10-biphenyl host material and used for the formation of the emissive layer of OLEDs in concentration-dependent experiments. The OLED with 15 wt% of the emitting derivative 6,6′-(1,4-phenylene)bis(5-(cyclohexylamino)pyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitrile) exhibited superior performance. The antibacterial and antifungal properties of the newly synthesized pyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitriles were evaluated.

Introduction

In the realm of heterocyclic chemistry, diaminomaleonitrile (DAMN) 1 has consistently held a prominent position among precursors due to its versatility, cost-effectiveness, and ease of acquisition. It is utilized to synthesize a vast array of heterocyclic compounds, including imidazoles, purines, triazoles, pyrimidines, pyrazoles, pyrroles, and numerous others, all of which have demonstrated various applications in pharmaceuticals, dyes and pigments and electronics.1,2 Due to its weakly basic properties, DAMN 1 is a widely recognized reactant in multicomponent reactions (MCRs), particularly when combined with isocyanides and carbonyl compounds.3–5

Isocyanides 3 are conventionally linked to their reaction with aldehydes and ketones, as revealed in the Ugi and Passerini reactions.6 On the other hand, α-keto imidoyl chloride known as Nef adduct 4 is usually obtained from the combination acid chlorides 2 and isocyanides 3 under mild conditions, and it was first reported by John Ulric Nef in 1892.7 This intermediate 4 can be trapped to yield several cyclic compounds Scheme 1.

Reaction of acid chloride 2 and isocyanide 3 to produce Nef adduct 4.

A successful combination of Nef adduct 6 and DAMN 1 was reported by Yavari in 2012. He documented the one-pot method for synthesizing 3-(alkylamino)-5,6-dicyanopyrazines 7 and alkyl 3-(alkylamino)quinoxaline-2-carboxylates 8 when Nef adduct 6 was treated with DAMN 1 and o-phenylenediamine 5, respectively Scheme 2.8

Reactions of Nef adduct 6 with DAMN 1 and o-phenylenediamine 5.

In the last two decades, our team has concentrated on exploring various pathways to innovative nitrogen heterocyclic compounds derived from DAMN 1 as a rich source of a library of heterocycles that exhibit a wide spectrum of applications such as OLED or as biological active substances.9–13

We previously detailed the synthesis of 6-arylpyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitriles through the reaction of DAMN 1 with substituted carbonyl chlorides and isocyanides.14 The interaction between isocyanides and aryl carbonyl chlorides yielded reactive Nef adducts 4 formed in situ, which have been used in the production of a diverse range of pyrazines. Generally, pyrazines exhibit a variety of notable biological activities and are recognized as vital components in many approved pharmaceuticals. Pyrazine moiety is found in vitamin B2, and folic acid; additionally. They are prevalent in a variety of well-established pharmaceuticals, including anticancer agents and antibacterial drugs, in the treatment of urinary tract infections (UTI) and chronic bronchitis, as well as for anti-diabetic, antitubercular, and diuretic applications.15,16

A paper described the synthesis of pyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitriles 9, which were obtained from the condensation DAMN 1 with α-diketones.17 These compounds were found to be crucial in the domains of food, agriculture, dyes, pigments, and pharmaceuticals due to their fascinating chemical properties.

In recent decades, there has been remarkable and rapid progress in organic light emitting diode (OLED) technology, evolving it into a multi-billion-dollar industry.18–21 Its applications have broadened across numerous fields, including high-contrast flat-panel displays, smartwatches, smartphones, and large television screens. Furthermore, solid-state lighting is garnering increasing attention in both industrial and scientific sectors. OLED devices provide exceptional attributes such as high color fidelity, lighter weight, decreased power usage, quicker response times, and flexibility, exceeding the capabilities of current technologies.22,23 The evolution of blue OLED materials has spanned three generations: fluorescent, phosphorescent, and TADF materials. Up until now, the dominant commercial OLED devices have relied on phosphorescent materials that include noble metals such as platinum and iridium. The integration of noble metal atoms into the phosphorescent material structures creates a significant challenge, not only regarding the future production costs of these devices but also raises environmental issues. Furthermore, there is an observable trend towards an increase in the nonradiative transition rate of the d-orbitals in phosphorescent metal complexes as the emission peaks shift towards the blue region, which complicates the attainment of both high efficiency and stability in blue phosphorescent OLEDs.24–26 To address these challenges, there is a renewed emphasis on the creation of small-molecule fluorescent materials, mainly due to their high color purity and cost-effectiveness. In this research paper, we present the synthesis of a novel class of pyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitrile derivatives, along with results pertaining to their antimicrobial, OLED and cyclic voltammetry studies.

Results and discussion

In our current study, the imidoyl chlorides (Nef adducts) 10 and 11, produced in situ from the reaction between alkyl isocyanide (R = Cy) 3 and terephthaloyl carbonyl chloride 9, was reacted with DAMN 1 at ambient temperature, then 6,6′-(1,4-phenylene)bis(5-(cyclohexylamino)pyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitrile) 12a is produced with a yield of 51% Scheme 3.

Reaction of Nef adducts 10 and 11 with DAMN 1.

In a similar manner, a combination of alkyl isocyanide (R= tert-butyl) 3 and terephthaloyl carbonyl chloride 9 was agitated at 50 °C and subsequently reacted with DAMN 1 at ambient temperature, resulting in the formation of 6,6′-(1,4-phenylene)bis(5-(t-butylamino)pyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitrile) 12b in 39% yield Scheme 3.

The composition of compounds 12a was validated through NMR and IR spectral data. For instance, the 1H NMR spectrum displayed five multiplets ranging from δ = 1.06 to 1.86, along with a multiplet at δ = 3.96 attributed to the protons of the cyclohexyl ring, and multiplets at δ = 7.86 and a singlet at 7.96 Hz corresponding to the aromatic hydrogens. The decoupled 13C-NMR spectrum of 12a revealed 12 distinct resonances, consistent with the proposed structure. The mass spectra indicated a peak at 528, and HRMS showed 528.2483, both of which matched the mass calculated for the product formula C30H28N12, which is 528.2493. Ultimately, the structure of compound 12a was definitively confirmed through X-ray crystallographic analysis Fig. 2. The structures of compounds 12b were similarly validated by analytical methods as well as NMR and IR spectral data. The 1H-NMR spectrum of 12b presented a singlet at δ = 1.43 due to the protons of the tert-butyl ring, and singlets at δ = 7.11 and δ = 7.92 Hz for the aromatic hydrogens. The 13C-NMR spectrum of 12b exhibited 15 distinct resonances, aligning with the proposed structure, with the tert-butyl carbons resonating at 28.29, 28.31, 54.57, and 54.69 ppm. The mass spectra revealed a peak at 476, consistent with the mass calculated for the product formula C26H24N12. The cyano groups in structures 12a and 12b were readily identified by observing the signals associated with the CN group at 2222 and 2232 in the IR spectra. Several other 5-(cyclohexylamino)-6-arylpyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitriles 13a–d were synthesized in moderate yields by reacting substituted carbonyl chlorides 2 with alkyl isocyanides 3Scheme 4, Table 1. The products were isolated by easily washing the isolated precipitate with petroleum ether and characterized by the IR, 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR, and GCMS analysis Scheme 4 and Fig. 1.

Fluorescence properties and quantum yield analysis

The fluorescence behaviour of compounds 12a, 12b, 13a, 13b, 13c, and 13d was investigated in dichloromethane solution (5 × 10−6 M) using an excitation wavelength of 305 nm. Fluorescence quantum yields (ΦF) were determined by the relative method using 2,3-diamino-5,6-dicyanopyrazine as the reference standard measured in acetonitrile (ΦF = 0.34), with refractive index corrections applied to account for solvent-dependent optical effects. Both the emission spectra and quantum yields demonstrated a pronounced dependence on structural modification and the nature of the appended heteroaromatic substituents (Fig. 7 and Table 2). Compounds 12a, 12b, and 13a exhibited strong fluorescence emission with relatively high quantum yields ranging from 0.49 to 0.60. The comparatively high fluorescence quantum yields observed for compounds 12a, 12b, and 13a suggest that these structures possess favourable molecular rigidity and effective π-conjugation, which suppress non-radiative relaxation pathways and promote efficient radiative decay. Compound 12a exhibited emission maxima at 474 nm and a quantum yield of 0.51, likely arising from the rigid cyclic amine substituents that restricts intramolecular rotation and enhances structural rigidity. Fig. 6. Similarly, compound 12b displayed strong fluorescence efficiency (Φ = 0.49; emission maxima at 465 nm), indicating that the tert-butylamino substituent in this structure effectively preserves conjugation while limiting excited-state deactivation.

Among all the synthesized derivatives, compound 13a exhibited the highest fluorescence efficiency (ΦF = 0.60) and showed a partially resolved double-humped emission band at 459 and 486 nm. This structured emission pattern is indicative of vibronic coupling within the excited state and is consistent with the rigid, highly conjugated benzofuran-containing framework of the molecule. The fused benzofuran moiety enhances molecular planarity and extends π-electron delocalization, thereby stabilizing the excited state and restricting conformational flexibility. Such structural rigidity suppresses nonradiative relaxation pathways, including vibrational and conformational deactivation processes, leading to more efficient radiative decay and consequently stronger fluorescence emission with partially resolved vibronic features. In contrast, compounds 13b and 13c exhibited extremely weak fluorescence with quantum yields of only 0.002. The dramatic quenching observed in these compounds is likely associated with the presence of additional nitrogen-containing heteroaromatic substituents/aza-aromatic substituents capable of promoting non-radiative decay pathways. These substituents may facilitate intramolecular charge-transfer processes, excited-state energy dissipation, or enhanced intersystem crossing, resulting in severe fluorescence quenching. Furthermore, increased conformational flexibility and possible rotational relaxation within these heteroaromatic systems may additionally reduce fluorescence efficiency.

Compound 13d displayed weak but measurable fluorescence with an emission maximum at 458 nm and a low quantum yield of 0.03, (Φ = 0.03), higher than 13b and 13c but significantly lower than the strongly emissive derivatives. Although the benzofuran unit contributes conjugation, the tert-butylamino substituent may introduce increased conformational freedom and less efficient orbital overlap in the excited state, thereby facilitating non-radiative relaxation processes. The reduced fluorescence compared with 13a suggests that the nature of the amino substituent plays an important role in controlling excited-state rigidity and emission efficiency.

Overall, the fluorescence emission results demonstrate that the photophysical behaviour of these compounds is strongly influenced by the balance between π-conjugation, molecular rigidity, and conformational flexibility.

Electroluminescence studies

The pyrazine ring, characterized by its pronounced electron-deficient aromatic nature, serves as a highly promising scaffold for the electron-withdrawing component in π-conjugated push–pull systems utilized in optoelectronic applications. In this study, we present the investigation of newly synthesized compound 12a and compounds 14–17 14 with pyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitrile fragments Fig. 8. The key requirement for the emissive material for OLED is a high efficiency emission process, either fluorescence or phosphorescence; therefore, compounds 12a and 14–17 were used in the fabrication of OLEDs. The EL spectra of all tested materials exhibit emissions in the blue region, with peak wavelengths ranging from approximately 450 nm to 520 nm, corresponding to the characteristic emissions of materials 12a and 14–17. Notably, no significant color shift was observed with varying emitter concentrations. The presence of a single emission peak confirms efficient energy transfer between the emitter and host, indicating that these emitters are well-suited for CBP-based systems. However, the external quantum efficiencies of all tested materials remain relatively low.

EL characteristics of the OLEDs contain emitting materials 12a, 14–17

Fig. 9 visually represents the EL characteristics of devices employing the most efficient emitter 12a. It illustrates EL spectra of the devices, current density–voltage–luminance and power efficacy–luminance–current efficacy characteristics. The same characteristics of OLEDs using other emitters 14–17 are depicted in Fig. S27 in the SI of this article.

OLEDs with material 12a exhibited turn-on voltages between 4.1 V and 5.0 V, depending on dopant concentration. The 15 wt% doped device showed strong performance, achieving 1.1 cd A−1 current efficiency, 0.8 lm W−1 power efficiency, and a peak luminance of 1820 cd m−2. However, the 15 wt% doped OLED performed slightly better, with 1.1 cd A−1 current efficiency, 0.7 lm W−1 power efficiency, and a lower turn-on voltage of 4.1 V, enhancing efficiency and longevity. These results indicate that 15 wt% material 12a is an optimal doping level for high-performance OLEDs, suitable for optoelectronic applications. The EQE versus luminance curve (Fig. 9) reveals that the highest efficiency is achieved at a 20 wt% doping level; beyond this threshold, performance begins to deteriorate due to increased non-radiative losses and imbalance in carrier distribution. This optimized device reaches a maximum EQE of 0.2%, a current efficacy of 0.9 cd A−1, a power efficacy of 0.6 lm W−1, and operates at a low voltage of 4.1 V (Table 3), producing bluish green emission. These results underscore the significance of finely tuned dopant concentrations in maximizing OLED performance and affirm the suitability of the 20 wt% material D-doped CBP host system for achieving efficient exciton management and light emission under low voltage operation. Although increasing the dopant concentration can improve the efficiency of energy transfer from host to guest, it also raises the risk of molecular aggregation, which can negatively impact performance by promoting exciton quenching.

Cyclic voltammetry analysis

The cyclic voltammograms of compounds 12a, 14–17 show a single well-defined redox couple in the negative potential region (ca. −1.0 to −1.4 V), assigned to a ligand-centered L (0/−1) process strongly influenced by the pyrazine moiety's π-accepting character. The electrochemical data show a systematic shift in the formal potentials (E1/2), following the order D (−1.090 V) > A (−1.118 V) > C (−1.167 V) > B (−1.282 V) > E (−1.304 V). This trend indicates progressive stabilization of the reduced species as the substituents on the pyrazine framework become more electron-withdrawing. Fig. 10.

Cyclic voltammograms of compounds 12a, 14–17 recorded in DCM containing 0.10 M tetra-n-butylammonium hexafluorophosphate (TBAHFP) as the supporting electrolyte at a glassy carbon working electrode, with a platinum auxiliary electrode and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. Scan rate: 100 mV s−1. All potentials are referenced to Ag/AgCl.

Compounds 15 and 17, which display the most negative E1/2 values, are therefore inferred to have more electron-deficient electronic environments, consistent with the presence of strongly electron-withdrawing substituents. In contrast, compound 12a exhibits the most positive E1/2 value, suggesting a relatively electron-rich environment in the compound framework. All pyrazines demonstrate quasi-reversible electrochemical behavior, as evidenced by peak-to-peak separations (ΔEp) of 0.16–0.21 V, which significantly exceed the theoretical value of 59 mV expected for a reversible one-electron transfer. This deviation, together with the observed peak broadening and non-ideal current responses, indicates kinetically hindered electron transfer, likely associated with structural reorganization and/or follow-up chemical processes upon reduction. Overall, these results highlight the pronounced sensitivity of the redox properties to the substituent electronic effects, which play a crucial role in modulating the redox accessibility and electrochemical behavior of these compounds Table 4.

Antimicrobial activity of compounds 12a,b and 13a–d

The emergence of antimicrobial resistance poses a significant threat to public health, particularly in fungal and bacterial infections.28,29 This study aimed to evaluate the antimicrobial potential of compounds 12a,b and 13a–d against a panel of microorganisms including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, as well as opportunistic fungi.

All organisms were cultured in appropriate media under optimal growth conditions: bacteria were cultured on Nutrient Agar (NA), fungi were cultured on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA), incubation: 24–48 h at 37 °C (bacteria) or 30 °C (fungi). The controls included: positive controls: cycloheximide (for Saccharomyces), fluconazole (for Candida), amoxycillin, penicillin, and kanamycin (for bacteria). Negative control: DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide). All test samples were dissolved in sterile DMSO. Concentrations of 1.0 mg mL−1 were used for each compound. The agar well diffusion assay was employed to assess antimicrobial activity. Each test organism was standardized to 0.5 McFarland turbidity.30–33 Wells (7 mm diameter) were bored into pre-inoculated agar plates. 70 µL of each test compound or control was added to wells. Plates were incubated as per organism-specific requirements. Zones of inhibition (in mm) were measured after 24–48 hours. For data analysis measurements were taken in triplicate. Mean zone of inhibition and standard deviation were calculated for each treatment. No statistically significant zones were observed for inactive compounds (0 mm across all replicates).

Standard controls showed expected antimicrobial activity validating assay conditions. Among test compounds 13b and 13d demonstrated consistent inhibitory activity against fluconazole-resistant Candida spp., with average zones of inhibition of 12.83 ± 0.38 mm and 12.11 ± 0.58 mm, respectively. No activity was observed against bacterial strains or fungal strains under the assay conditions. These effects were consistent across the triplicate, demonstrating reproducibility. All other tested compounds including 12a, 12b, 13a and 13c did not exhibit any observable zones of inhibition against any of the tested microorganisms.

Conclusion

In summary, the reaction of imidoyl chlorides with DAMN provides a facile and efficient route for synthesis of 5-(cyclohexylamino)-6-biphenylpyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitrile and 5-(tert-butylamino)-6-biphenylpyrazine-2,3-dicarbonitrile. Other derivatives were also synthesized. The advantages of this method are simply available starting materials, neutral and easy reaction conditions, and very simple purification methods. The OLED results indicate that 15 wt% material 12a is an optimal doping level for high-performance OLEDs, suitable for optoelectronic applications. Further, the fluorescence emission results demonstrate that the photophysical behaviour of these compounds is strongly influenced by the balance between π-conjugation, molecular rigidity, and conformational flexibility. This study identifies 13b and 13d as promising antifungal candidates with activity against fluconazole-resistant Candida spp. Their observed inhibitory zones indicate potential utility in alternative antifungal development.

Experimental

All the reactions were conducted under a nitrogen atmosphere unless otherwise noted. All the analyses were conducted in the Research Sector Projects Unit (RSPU) at Kuwait. TLC was performed using Polygram Sil G/UV 254 TLC plates, and visualization was conducted by ultraviolet lights at 254 and 350 nm. Column chromatography was performed using Merck silica gel 60 of mesh size 0.040–0.063 mm. 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded using Bruker NEO 400 MHz and Bruker Avance II 600 MHz superconducting NMR spectrometers. Mass spectra were recorded with a GCMS-DFS-Thermo, high-resolution spectrometer. Fluorescence measurements were conducted with a Jobin Yvon-FluoroMax-4, spectrofluorometer using a 1 cm path length cuvette at room temperature. The single-crystal X-ray analysis was conducted on Rigaku Rapid II and Bruker X8 prospector diffractometers. Melting points were determined via differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analyses on Shimadzu DSC-50.

Single crystals of the compounds reported in this study were grown by slow solvent evaporation method. The single crystal data collection was made on Bruker X8 prospector diffractometer by Cu-Kα radiation at room temperature. The reflection frames were integrated with the Bruker SAINT software package using a narrow-frame algorithm. Finally, the structure was solved using the Bruker SHELXTL software package and refined using SHELXL-2017/1. The details of the X-ray diffraction analysis are given in the Crystallographic Information File (cif) file of 12a, 13a and 13d.34

Electrochemical measurements were performed using a standard three-electrode setup connected to a NOVA 2.11 workstation. A glassy carbon electrode (GCE) served as the working electrode, a platinum wire as the auxiliary (counter) electrode, and a silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) electrode as the reference electrode. All reported potential are referenced to the Ag/AgCl electrode. Cyclic voltammetry was conducted in DCM (CH2Cl2) with 0.10 M tetrabutylammonium hexafluorophosphate (TBAHFP) as the supporting electrolyte. Before each measurement, the glassy carbon electrode was polished with alumina slurry, rinsed thoroughly with distilled water and solvent, and dried under ambient conditions. The electrolyte solutions were purged with an inert gas (argon or nitrogen) for several minutes to remove dissolved oxygen, and an inert atmosphere was maintained during measurements. All experiments were conducted at room temperature. The scan rate was typically 100 mV s−1 unless otherwise stated. Electrochemical responses were recorded and processed using the NOVA 2.11 software package.

General procedure for the synthesis of compounds 12a,b and 13a–d

A dry 50 mL round-bottom flask was charged with 1.0 mmol of substituted carbonyl chloride derivatives and cyclohexyl isocyanide or tert-butyl isocyanide (2.1 mmol for 12 and 1.1 mmol for 13) and heated at 50 °C for 1/2 h. A mixture of dry tetrahydrofuran (12 mL) and DAMN 1 (2.1 mmol for 12 and 1.1 mmol for 13) was added to the reaction mixture, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 h. The solid was then filtered and further purified by crystallization from different solvents to furnish compounds 12a,b and 13a–d.
