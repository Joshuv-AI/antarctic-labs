Ultrasound-programmable adhesive hydrogel platform for sequential treatment of infected wounds
Authors: Chenhui He, Linrong Shi, Jiahao Shen, Hengyu Liu, Jiayue Li, Yang Liu, Liang Bai, Daocheng Wu, Mingxi Wan, Yi Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic infected wounds remain a clinical challenge to treat due to persistent bacterial colonization, heterogeneous healing microenvironments, and antibiotic resistance. Here, we developed an ultrasound-programmable adhesive hydrogel platform that sequentially coordinates tissue adhesion, transdermal nanoparticle delivery, sonodynamic activation, and tissue regeneration within an integrated therapeutic cascade. Amino-functionalized N-doped graphene quantum dots (aN-GQDs)–modified ZIF-8 nanocomposites (ZIF-8@aN-GQDs) were embedded within a polyacrylamide/alginate hydrogel (PA-ZA). Programmed kHz- and MHz-frequency ultrasound enables non-chemical tissue adhesion followed by transdermal delivery of ZIF-8@aN-GQDs with sonodynamic ROS generation for antibacterial activity. PA-ZA exhibited favorable biocompatibility and promoted cell migration and angiogenesis in vitro. In a full-thickness infected skin-defect model, ultrasound-activated PA-ZA reduced bacterial burden, accelerated inflammatory resolution, and induced dynamic regulation of angiogenesis, thereby expediting wound repair. Interestingly, a similar wound-closure benefit was maintained in a subcutaneous infection model, supporting potential use in non-exposed and anatomically constrained deep infections. Overall, this work establishes a programmable therapeutic strategy in which ultrasound governs tissue adhesion and on-demand treatment of complex infected wounds, with potential relevance to infected wounds and other anatomically constrained superficial infections.

Introduction

Chronic wound infections pose a significant global clinical and economic burden, driven by persistent bacterial colonization, excessive inflammation, and severely impaired tissue regeneration [1,2]. In 2019 alone, bacterial infections caused an estimated 13.7 million deaths worldwide, with Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) being among the most prevalent pathogens [3,4]. An ideal therapeutic strategy should not only achieve efficient bacterial eradication but also be dynamically coordinated with the phase-specific processes of inflammation resolution and tissue regeneration throughout the wound-healing process [5,6]. Despite their widespread clinical use, conventional dressings and systemic antibiotics rarely achieve durable healing due to poor adhesion on moist tissues, uncontrolled nanoparticle release, rising antibiotic resistance, and the risk of systemic toxicity [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Collectively, these challenges underscore the need for bioactive therapeutic strategies that extend beyond single-mode antibacterial action and instead support the coordinated resolution of infection and promotion of tissue repair.

Hydrogels are widely employed in the treatment of infected wounds owing to their excellent biocompatibility, three-dimensional structures that mimics the extracellular matrix, and strong capacity to absorb exudate while maintaining a moist wound environment [11,12]. They also facilitate localized delivery of therapeutic agents, thereby reducing the risk of systemic side effects [13]. However, most hydrogels exhibit weak and unreliable interfacial adhesion on moist and irregular tissue surfaces, which can lead to premature detachment and localized treatment failure. To enhance adhesion, chemical strategies such as catechol-mediated interactions or covalent coupling with tissue functional groups have been extensively explored to strengthen material–tissue interfaces [14,15]. Nevertheless, these approaches often involve complex synthesis, offer limited dynamic tunability, and irreversible covalent crosslinking may raise biosafety concerns [16]. In contrast, physical adhesion strategies better preserve native tissue chemistry and biocompatibility but typically rely on non-covalent interactions, resulting in insufficient adhesion strength when applied alone [16,17]. Moreover, nanoparticle release from hydrogels is primarily governed by passive parameters including network porosity, crosslink density, and degradation kinetics, leading to predefined and static release profiles that fail to adapt to the dynamic therapeutic demands encountered during the wound-healing process [18,19].

To achieve therapeutic control that matches the changing needs of wound repair, there is a clear need for an externally programmable, non-invasive strategy that integrates tissue adhesion, nanoparticle delivery, and therapeutic activation. In this context, stimulus-responsive systems have garnered growing interest [20]. Among these, sonodynamic therapy (SDT), which activates sonosensitizers via ultrasound and generates antimicrobial reactive oxygen species (ROS), has emerged as a promising approach for treating infected wounds and has motivated the development of diverse sonosensitizing nanomaterials because it enables deep tissue penetration, remains effective in moist environments, and is non-invasive [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. However, despite these advantages, most existing SDT-based studies conceptualize ultrasound primarily as a binary “on/off” trigger for ROS generation, thereby framing SDT as an isolated antimicrobial stimulus [27]. Such simplification overlooks the broader regulatory potential of ultrasound as a programmable biophysical signal capable of coordinating multiple therapeutic processes across an integrated wound-healing system.

Notably, ultrasound elicits parameter-dependent biological effects (e.g., frequency, intensity, and exposure duration), allowing it to function not only as a therapeutic activation trigger but also as an active regulator of biointerface interactions and mass transport [28,29]. Emerging evidence indicates that kHz-frequency ultrasound can induce cavitation-mediated interpenetration between polymer networks and soft tissues, thereby enabling non-chemical, reversible tissue adhesion [30,31]. In contrast, MHz-frequency ultrasound activates sonodynamic processes [32,33] while facilitating transdermal nanoparticle delivery and on-demand release [31,34]. Collectively, these effects render ultrasound a programmable biophysical signal capable of sequentially coordinating tissue adhesion, nanoparticle delivery, antibacterial activation, and tissue regeneration during different stages of wound treatment. Accordingly, integrating this sequential coordination capability with hydrogel platforms offers a promising strategy to overcome the inherent limitations of conventional wound dressings (i.e., static adhesion and passive release) by enabling dynamic adaptation to wound environments under ultrasound control and coordinated therapeutic outputs.

Here, we report an ultrasound-programmable adhesive hydrogel platform that enables a sequential therapeutic strategy by using ultrasound not only as a trigger for sonodynamic therapy but also as a means to regulate tissue adhesion and nanoparticle delivery. Specifically, this platform integrates tissue adhesion, transdermal nanoparticle delivery, sonodynamic activation, and subsequent tissue repair within a unified wound-treatment system. To implement this strategy, we developed a polyacrylamide/sodium alginate (PA) hydrogel incorporating aN-GQDs–modified ZIF-8 nanocomposite (ZIF-8@ aN-GQDs) (Scheme 1). Within it, ZIF-8 provides sonodynamic antimicrobial activity and structural stability, while aN-GQDs enhance tissue regeneration and synergistically augment the sonodynamic antimicrobial efficacy. Programmed kHz- and MHz-frequency ultrasound sequentially coordinates the adhesion–delivery–activation process, thereby coupling antibacterial treatment with subsequent tissue repair and improving wound healing outcomes in vitro and in vivo. Overall, this work provides a design strategy for ultrasound-programmable adhesive hydrogel platforms for complex infected wounds and potentially other anatomically constrained infections.

Results

Fabrication and structural basis of the PA-ZA hydrogel platform

To enable efficient sonodynamic activation while maintaining structural stability, we selected metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) as the sonodynamic component because of their high porosity and surface area, tunable composition, and facile functionalization [35]. Various sonosensitizers, including organic sensitizers, semiconductor or metal oxide nanoparticles, carbon-based nanomaterials, and MOF-based systems, have been explored for SDT-based antibacterial applications [21,22,24,27]. However, many conventional sonosensitizers primarily provide ultrasound-triggered ROS generation, whereas infected wound treatment further requires stable local retention, facile integration into hydrogel dressings, favorable biocompatibility, and regenerative support [12,18,36]. Among MOF-based candidates, ZIF-8 was chosen because of its structural stability under physiological conditions, favorable biocompatibility, and reported ultrasound-responsive ROS generation [[36], [37], [38]]. In parallel, to address the regenerative demands of infected wound healing, aN-GQDs were incorporated to provide pro-regenerative bioactivity, including enhanced angiogenesis, cell migration, and extracellular matrix remodeling [[37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. Accordingly, we prepared an aN-GQDs-modified ZIF-8 nanocomposite (ZIF-8@aN-GQDs), providing a stable, multifunctional foundation for subsequent ultrasound-programmed antibacterial and regenerative functions.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal that ZIF-8 retains its structural integrity after aN-GQDs modification (Fig. 1A and B). Consistently, dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed an increase in the average hydrodynamic diameter from 74.22 ± 12.58 nm for pristine ZIF-8 to 89.46 ± 24.48 nm after aN-GQDs modification (Fig. 1C), supporting the successful loading of aN-GQDs onto ZIF-8. Elemental mapping further confirmed homogeneous C, N, and Zn distributions within individual nanocrystals (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns indicate that the characteristic crystalline structure of ZIF-8 is retained after hybridization (Fig. 1E). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results further confirm that the prepared quantum dots exhibit amino-functionalized, N-doped features and have been successfully incorporated into the ZIF-8 system (Fig. 1F). Compared with pristine ZIF-8, both the Zn 2p peak and the N 1s component corresponding to Zn–N in ZIF-8@aN-GQDs exhibited slight shifts toward lower binding energies, while the amino-N peak originating from aN-GQDs showed a slight shift toward a higher binding energy. These results indicate that the composite of ZIF-8 and aN-GQDs involves interfacial interactions accompanied by local electronic redistribution, rather than representing a simple physical mixture. Importantly, ZIF-8@aN-GQDs preserved the characteristic photoluminescence emission of aN-GQDs (λ_em = 463 nm), thereby further confirming effective integration without compromising intrinsic photophysical properties (Fig. S2).

Next, we incorporated ZIF-8@aN-GQDs within a polyacrylamide/alginate dual-network hydrogel (PA-ZA) to enable local nanoparticle retention and ultrasound-responsive behavior in vivo (Fig. S3A and B). The PAAm and alginate matrix offers a porous network for nanoparticle retention, while combined ionic and covalent crosslinking maintains mechanical stability under wound conditions. SEM imaging revealed a well-defined three-dimensional porous network (Fig. 1G), indicating a permeable hydrogel microstructure suitable for nanoparticle incorporation and wound dressing applications. Rheological analysis showed that the storage modulus (G′) consistently surpassed the loss modulus (G″) across all tested frequencies (Fig. 1H), indicating a solid-like, elastic behavior suitable for soft-tissue applications. Importantly, incorporation of ZIF-8@aN-GQDs did not measurably alter the hydrogel's mechanical performance, as stiffness (Fig. 1I), swelling ratio (Fig. 1J), and viscoelastic properties remained comparable to those of the pristine PA hydrogel. Release kinetics of ZIF-8@aN-GQDs from PA-ZA hydrogels were further evaluated under US− and US + conditions using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) following nitric acid digestion of the collected supernatants. Therefore, the detected signal represents the total Zn derived from released Zn-containing components rather than from free Zn2+ ions alone. Under US- conditions, PA-ZA showed gradual and limited passive release, reaching approximately 36% cumulative Zn release at 60 h. In contrast, ultrasound stimulation significantly accelerated the release of Zn-containing components. A single ultrasound treatment resulted in a release of approximately 47%, while the cumulative release reached approximately 90% after three rounds of ultrasound stimulation (Fig. 1K). These results suggest that the porous, crosslinked PAAm/Alg network restricts rapid passive leakage through physical confinement and tortuous diffusion pathways, whereas ultrasound enhances local mass transport and facilitates the release or migration of pore-accessible or surface-localized ZIF-8@aN-GQDs without disrupting the bulk PAAm/Alg network.

Ultrasound-programmed tissue adhesion and transdermal transport

Effective therapy for infected wounds requires hydrogel dressings that maintain stable adhesion on tissue surfaces to preserve a moist microenvironment, prevent secondary contamination, and ensure localized retention of therapeutic agents [43,44]. Building on evidence that acoustic cavitation can drive polymeric chains into the tissues and that subsequent hydrogel application induces gelation of the anchoring agent, thereby enhancing tissue adhesion in a non-chemical and reversible manner [30], we employed an ultrasound-assisted anchoring strategy. Briefly, a chitosan primer solution was applied to the wound surface and then driven into the tissue by 28 kHz-frequency ultrasound. The 28 kHz condition was adopted as a practical low-frequency parameter for chitosan-primer-assisted anchoring based on the ultrasound-mediated bioadhesion mechanism reported by Ma et al. [30]. In contrast, 1 MHz ultrasound was subsequently used for nanocomposite delivery and sonodynamic activation because it provides a more controllable therapeutic acoustic field for transdermal transport and ROS generation. This process generated an interpenetrating interfacial network that coupled effectively with the PA-ZA hydrogel. Mechanical testing confirmed that ultrasound markedly enhanced hydrogel-tissue adhesion, consistent with the formation of an interpenetrating interfacial network: US-treated hydrogels remained firmly attached to ex vivo pig skin (Fig. 2A). Lap-shear testing demonstrated an approximately five-fold increase in adhesion energy compared with non-ultrasound controls (Fig. 2B).

Application of 28 kHz-frequency ultrasound enabled clear transdermal infiltration of FITC-labeled chitosan (Chi-FITC), whereas 1 MHz-frequency ultrasound substantially increased the penetration depth of ZIF-8 (ZIF-8-FITC) released from the hydrogel matrix (Fig. 2C and D), highlighting frequency-dependent transport across the skin barrier. By contrast, in the absence of ultrasound, Chi-FITC and ZIF-8-FITC remained confined to the epidermis. Safety assessments confirmed that this process introduced no thermal or histological damage: infrared thermography showed only a mild and transient temperature rise (to ∼40°C) that quickly normalized (Fig. 2E, S4A, B), and H&E staining revealed intact epidermal and dermal architecture without evidence of inflammation or injury (Fig. 2F). Importantly, the applied ultrasound parameters fall within commonly used therapeutic ultrasound parameter ranges, further supporting the translational feasibility of this ultrasound-assisted adhesion and activation strategy. To further assess the local compatibility of repeated ultrasound exposure, skin tissues were collected after prolonged and repeated ultrasound treatment and examined by H&E staining. The epidermal and dermal structures remained intact, with no obvious tissue necrosis, inflammatory infiltration, or structural disruption (Fig. S4C). These results further confirm that the applied ultrasound parameters did not induce detectable local tissue injury, thus supporting the safety of the ultrasound-assisted adhesion and delivery procedure.

These findings identify ultrasound as an effective and biophysically safe programmable regulator that strengthens hydrogel-tissue adhesion while actively promoting transdermal transport of nanocomposites. Building on this anchoring mechanism, we further incorporated MHz-frequency ultrasound to activate the PA-ZA hydrogel, enabling controlled ZIF-8@aN-GQDs release. In this sequential strategy, kHz-frequency ultrasound drives chitosan into tissues to form an interfacial anchoring layer, subsequent hydrogel application establishes robust mechanical coupling, and MHz-frequency ultrasound enhances transdermal transport of hydrogel-released nanocomposites, thereby integrating programmed adhesion and controlled delivery within an integrated platform (Fig. 2G).

Ultrasound-triggered sonodynamic antibacterial activity mediated by PA-ZA hydrogels in vitro

Having established that ultrasound enables stable tissue adhesion and effective transdermal delivery of the PA-ZA hydrogel, we then examined whether the ultrasound-delivered nanocomposite could be activated to exert antibacterial activity. Under non-ultrasonic conditions (US−), none of the hydrogel formulations inhibited the growth of either Gram-negative (E. coli) or Gram-positive (S. aureus) bacteria (Fig. 3A and B), demonstrating that antibacterial activity is strictly gated by ultrasonic activation rather than intrinsic material toxicity. In contrast, upon ultrasonic activation (US+), PA-ZIF-8 exhibited moderate antibacterial activity, whereas PA-ZA induced a pronounced reduction in bacterial colonies (>99%), indicating a marked enhancement of sonodynamic efficacy enabled by incorporating ZIF-8 with aN-GQDs. Fluorescence live/dead staining further supported these results: bacteria remained largely viable without ultrasound, while US irradiation induced extensive bacterial death only in the PA-ZA group for both E. coli and S. aureus (Fig. 3C and D), confirming a broad-spectrum and ultrasound-dependent sonodynamic antibacterial effect.

Notably, the XPS results in Fig. 1F revealed subtle but coordinated binding-energy shifts after hybridization, suggesting the formation of a reconfigured interfacial electronic structure between ZIF-8 and aN-GQDs rather than simple physical mixing. Such interfacial electronic redistribution may facilitate charge separation and interfacial electron transfer under ultrasound activation, thereby reducing charge recombination and promoting ROS generation, which is consistent with the mechanisms reported for MOF-assisted, carbon-dot-based, and heterojunction-based sonosensitizers [[45], [46], [47]]. To elucidate the antibacterial mechanism, we quantified ultrasound-triggered ROS generation using TA (•OH), DHR123 (O2-•), and SOSG (1O2) probes (Fig. 3E, S5). Upon ultrasound exposure, the PA-ZA hydrogel produced markedly higher levels of all three ROS species than all control groups, confirming that its bactericidal activity originated primarily from ultrasound-activated ROS generation. Consistent with the thermal safety profile observed during ultrasound-assisted adhesion (Fig. 2E), infrared thermal imaging during SDT activation revealed only a brief and minimal temperature rise (< 32°C, Fig. 3F), thereby excluding thermal contributions to bacterial killing and confirming that the applied acoustic parameters are safe for skin tissues. Taken together, these results demonstrate that PA-ZA functions as an effective, ultrasound-programmable sonodynamic therapy system capable of generating high levels of ROS in a controllable manner, thereby enabling localized and broad-spectrum antibacterial activity in vitro.

In Vitro biocompatibility and regenerative bioactivity

The in vitro biocompatibility of PA-ZA was first assessed. Hemolysis assays demonstrated that all formulations exhibited hemolysis ratios well below the 5% safety threshold (Fig. 4A), indicating excellent hemocompatibility [48]. Consistently, CCK-8 assays showed that ZIF-8, aN-GQDs, or ZIF-8@aN-GQDs did not measurably affect the viability of NIH/3T3 cells or HUVECs, with cell viability remaining above 95% across all groups (Fig. 4B and C). Live/dead staining corroborated the low cytotoxicity, showing predominantly viable cells in all groups (Fig. 4D and E). These results indicate that PA-ZA exhibits good hemocompatibility and cytocompatibility.

Beyond eliminating bacteria, effective wound treatment also requires preserving cellular functions that are critical to tissue repair. While aN-GQDs have demonstrated pro-regenerative activity, it remains unclear whether this bioactivity is maintained after nanocomposite formation and ultrasonic irradiation. To address this question, we employed an in vitro co-culture model [49], supernatants collected after ultrasound stimulation were applied to NIH/3T3 fibroblasts and HUVECs. Both PA-aN-GQDs and PA-ZA significantly enhanced cell migration in Transwell assays (Fig. 4F and G), confirming the retention of pro-migratory activity. Tube-formation assays further demonstrated a pro-angiogenic effect, as cells treated with PA-aN-GQDs or PA-ZA formed more extensive and interconnected capillary-like networks (Fig. 4H). These observations indicate that ZIF-8@aN-GQDs incorporation into the hydrogel matrix preserves their intrinsic regenerative bioactivity while maintaining overall compatibility with the composite system. Overall, PA-ZA hydrogels combine favorable biocompatibility with robust pro-migratory and pro-angiogenic bioactivities, supporting their use as an ultrasound-responsive platform that couples antibacterial therapy with regenerative processes.

Ultrasound-programmed therapeutic efficacy in infected wounds

A full-thickness wound model infected with S. aureus was established in mice to evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the PA-ZA hydrogel upon ultrasound activation. In the PA-ZA + US group, kHz- and MHz-frequency ultrasound were applied sequentially in the same animals according to the timeline shown in Fig. 5A. Briefly, 28 kHz ultrasound was first used for chitosan-primer-assisted peri-wound anchoring, followed by PA-ZA hydrogel placement; 1 MHz ultrasound was then applied immediately and repeated every 2 days for nanocomposite delivery and sonodynamic activation. Among all treatment groups, PA-ZA + US promoted the most rapid wound closure, achieving substantial contraction by day 7 and near-complete re-epithelialization by day 14 (Fig. 5B). In contrast, untreated, PA-ZA, and PA + US groups exhibited persistent inflammation, exudation, and delayed epithelial recovery, indicating that neither ultrasound activation nor nanocomposite delivery alone was sufficient to overcome infection-impaired healing. Quantitative analysis confirmed that PA-ZA + US achieved the greatest wound-closure rate and smallest residual wound area at all time points (Fig. 5C).

To further evaluate antibacterial performance, representative agar plates were used to assess bacterial growth recovered from the wounds. Dense bacterial colonies persisted in the control and PA + US groups even at day 14, PA-ZA + US markedly reduced colony formation on agar plates by day 7 and showed minimal residual colonies by day 14 (Fig. 5D and E), demonstrating timely and sustained antibacterial control throughout the healing process. Giemsa staining (Fig. 5F) corroborated these observations, revealing only minimal bacterial residues in the PA-ZA + US group.

Histological analysis of wound tissues revealed distinct differences in the quality of tissue regeneration (Fig. 5G). Control wounds displayed severe inflammatory infiltration, disrupted dermal architecture, and a discontinuous epithelium. The PA + US and PA-ZA groups showed partial epithelialization yet continued inflammatory cell accumulation and insufficient dermal remodeling. By contrast, PA-ZA + US resulted in continuous epithelial coverage, restored dermal organization, and markedly reduced inflammation, consistent with accelerated healing under ultrasound activation.

To further evaluate systemic tolerability following treatment of infected wounds, major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were harvested from mice in the wound treatment group and examined by H&E staining (Fig. S6). No significant pathological abnormalities, inflammatory lesions, tissue necrosis, or organ damage were observed in any group, supporting the in vivo biosafety of PA-ZA + US.

Interestingly, PA-ZA + US also demonstrated notable efficacy in a subcutaneous S. aureus infection model. Compared with the untreated control group, PA-ZA + US reduced the lesion area by day 7 (Fig. S7A), and colony-forming assays revealed a >95% decrease in viable S. aureus (Fig. S7B). These results indicate that ultrasound-activated PA-ZA can remain effective through intact skin, supporting its potential utility for non-exposed and anatomically constrained infections.

Overall, these in vivo findings indicate that ultrasound activation functions as a key programmable regulator that unlocks the therapeutic potential of the PA-ZA hydrogel. By integrating sonodynamic antibacterial clearance with enhanced epithelial regeneration and improved tissue remodeling, the ultrasound-programmable adhesive PA-ZA platform enables effective, temporally coordinated management of both exposed and non-exposed infectious injuries.

Ultrasound-programmed tissue regeneration during infected wound healing

Histological evaluation further revealed that PA-ZA + US markedly accelerated wound repair. Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 6A and B) showed pronounced differences in collagen remodeling across groups. At both days 7 and 14, PA-ZA + US exhibited the most extensive and well-organized collagen deposition. In contrast, the PA-ZA group showed moderate collagen accumulation and partial fiber organization, which, while improved over control and PA + US groups, was markedly inferior to the PA-ZA + US group.

Immunohistochemical analyses further revealed that PA-ZA + US orchestrated a stage-resolved healing response. CD68 staining for macrophages (Fig. 6C and D) revealed sustained and abundant infiltration in control and PA + US wounds, reflecting persistent inflammation. In contrast, PA-ZA + US showed markedly fewer CD68+ macrophages by day 7 and minimal residual infiltration by day 14, demonstrating rapid inflammatory resolution enabled by ultrasound-activated antibacterial clearance. Analysis of cellular proliferation revealed a coordinated healing progression. Ki67-positive cells peaked at day 7 in the PA-ZA plus ultrasound group and declined by day 14, consistent with a timely transition from the proliferative phase to the remodeling phase of wound healing (Fig. 6E and F).

Angiogenesis analyses further supported these observations. CD31 immunostaining showed that ultrasound-activated PA-ZA induced a dense and well-organized microvascular network by day 7, whereas control wounds displayed sparse, poorly developed vessels (Fig. 6G and H). By day 14, CD31 signals decreased in the PA-ZA + US group, consistent with transition into the remodeling phase and normalization of vascular density after wound closure, rather than continued neovascular expansion. In contrast, control wounds retained relatively high CD31 signals, suggesting that vascular reconstruction remained ongoing, consistent with delayed repair. PA-ZA treatment alone produced a moderate improvement, but it remained inferior to the vascularization achieved with ultrasound-activated treatment [50]. 4-HNE immunohistochemical staining was further performed on day 7 wound tissues to evaluate ROS-associated lipid peroxidation. PA-ZA + US showed the weakest 4-HNE staining among the infected-wound groups (Fig. S8), indicating that ultrasound-triggered ROS did not aggravate oxidative tissue damage under the applied treatment conditions.

To elucidate the molecular programs underlying the ultrasound-programmed healing response, RNA sequencing was performed on wound tissues collected on day 7. Global transcriptomic profiling revealed a distinct and coherent transcriptional response to PA-ZA + US treatment. Despite substantial overlap in expressed genes, PA-ZA + US induced a distinct expression signature. This was evidenced by principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson correlation, which confirmed clear intergroup separation and low intragroup variability (Fig. 7A and B and S9A). Differential expression analysis identified 5059 genes significantly altered by PA-ZA + US treatment, indicating extensive molecular reprogramming of the wound microenvironment (Fig. 7C).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of upregulated genes revealed activation of a coordinated repair program, characterized by modulation of inflammatory signaling, activation of pro-angiogenic pathways, and promotion of ECM remodeling (Fig. 7D). Significantly enriched pathways encompassed cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine and JAK–STAT signaling, as well as ECM–receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton. Several pro-angiogenic pathways, including MAPK, Rap1, Apelin, and cGMP–PKG signaling, were also significantly enriched, consistent with the enhanced neovascularization observed histologically. In contrast, downregulated genes were predominantly associated with metabolic and proliferative programs linked to chronic inflammation, suggesting a transition from a persistent inflammatory state toward a pro-resolution phenotype (Fig. S9B).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further corroborated these findings. GO-BP (Biological Process) terms (Fig. 7E) were significantly enriched for inflammation regulation, angiogenesis, and wound repair, reinforcing the KEGG-based conclusion that PA-ZA + US suppresses pro-inflammatory signaling while promoting regenerative processes. Likewise, GO cellular component and molecular function analysis (Fig. S9C and D) showed significant enrichment in ECM- and adhesion-associated structures and activities. These results align with histological and transcriptomic observations of enhanced ECM remodeling and activated angiogenesis.

To validate the activation of specific biological programs, we performed heatmap analysis of key gene sets involved in anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and pro-healing processes in the PA-ZA + US and control groups. The results revealed robust upregulation of key anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., Il10, Il1rn, Socs3, Nfkba, Tnfaip3), hallmarks of macrophage-mediated immune resolution (Fig. 7F). Notably, Arg1, a marker of M2 macrophages, was significantly upregulated, indicating a shift toward a reparative phenotype [51]. Similarly, key angiogenesis-related genes (e.g., Pdgfb, Vegfa, Angpt1, Flt1, Nrp1) were markedly upregulated (Fig. 7G), consistent with the observed increase in CD31+ vessels. The upregulation of Pecam1 (encoding CD31) provided further support [52]. Likewise, genes associated with ECM remodeling and wound healing (e.g., Fn1, Vcan, Itga5, Tnc, Has2, Col3a1, Mmp14, Lox, Timp2) were strongly upregulated (Fig. 7H), reflecting a pattern of organized ECM assembly and controlled remodeling, rather than dysregulated fibrosis.

These results indicate that ultrasound-activated PA-ZA does more than accelerate wound closure. It dynamically regulates cell proliferation and angiogenesis, with inflammation resolving more rapidly, proliferative activity increasing transiently, neovascularization emerging in a timely manner and subsequently stabilizing, and tissue remodeling becoming more organized. These tissue-level changes (Fig. 6), supported by the transcriptomic profile (Fig. 7), provide a mechanistic basis for the accelerated, improved-quality repair of infected wounds observed in vivo.

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents an ultrasound-programmable adhesive hydrogel platform that enables sequential coordination of tissue adhesion, transdermal therapeutic delivery, antibacterial activation, and tissue regeneration for infected wound treatment. Using kHz- and MHz-frequency ultrasound as external control signals, the PA-ZA hydrogel achieves stable tissue adhesion, ultrasound-triggered antibacterial activity, and pro-regenerative bioactivity, thereby accelerating wound healing in vivo. These therapeutic benefits are supported by macroscopic and histopathological evidence as well as transcriptomic profiling, indicating reduced inflammation, dynamically regulated angiogenesis, and improved tissue remodeling. Interestingly, a similar wound-closure benefit was observed in the subcutaneous infection model, supporting the potential applicability of this strategy in anatomically constrained infections. Overall, this work demonstrates that ultrasound can serve not only as a therapeutic trigger but also as a programmable control signal that coordinates infection control with tissue regeneration after establishing stable tissue adhesion. Together, these findings provide a useful design framework for adhesive hydrogel-based wound therapies tailored to complex infected microenvironments.

Experimental section

Materials

2-Methylimidazole, zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O), citric acid, acrylamide, sodium alginate, N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide (MBAA), N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED), ammonium persulfate (APS), calcium chloride (CaCl2) and chitosan were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (China). FITC was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). The LIVE/DEAD Bacterial Staining Kit containing DMAO and PI, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), and Calcein-AM/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit were obtained from Beyotime (China). High-glucose DMEM, Matrigel, and 24-well Transwell chambers (8-μm pore size) were purchased from Corning. CD68, Ki67, and CD31 antibodies were purchased from Abcam. Unless otherwise specified, all other chemicals were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. and used as received.

Mouse fibroblast (NIH/3T3) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained from the School of Life Science and Technology, Xi'an Jiaotong University. Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC 8739) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, ATCC 14458) were purchased from Beijing Aoboxing Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Male ICR mice (6-8 weeks old, 18-22 g) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Xi'an Jiaotong University. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xi'an Jiaotong University (Xi'an, China).

Synthesis of ZIF-8 nanoparticles

ZIF-8 nanoparticles were prepared in an aqueous phase at room temperature according to a previously reported method [53]. Briefly, 11.35 g of 2-methylimidazole was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water, while 0.585 g of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O was dissolved in 5 mL of deionized water. The Zn(NO3)2·6H2O solution was added to the 2-methylimidazole solution under vigorous stirring and the mixture was stirred for 15 min at room temperature. The product was collected by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 10 min), washed three times with deionized water, and dried at 37°C for 24 h to obtain ZIF-8 nanoparticles.

Synthesis of aN-GQDs nanoparticles

aN-GQDs was synthesized via a two-step procedure: direct pyrolysis of citric acid [54] followed by hydrothermal amination. Briefly, 4 g of citric acid was heated at 200°C until melting and maintained at this temperature for approximately 20 min, during which the melt gradually turned orange, yielding GQDs. The obtained GQDs was slowly added dropwise to 100 mL of NaOH solution (10 mg/mL) under vigorous stirring. After complete dissolution, the pH of the mixture was adjusted to 7.0, followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane and dialysis against ultrapure water for 3 days to obtain purified GQDs.

For aN-GQDs, 1 mL of ammonia solution was added to 10 mL of the purified GQDs dispersion. The mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 100°C for 6 h for hydrothermal amination. After cooling to room temperature, the product was dialyzed against ultrapure water for 3 days and lyophilized to yield purified aN-GQDs.

Preparation of ZIF-8@aN-GQDs

ZIF-8@aN-GQDs was prepared by anchoring aN-GQDs onto ZIF-8 nanoparticles. Briefly, 200 mg of ZIF-8 was dispersed in 10 mL of ultrapure water via sonication (20 min). Subsequently, 100 mg of aN-GQDs powder was added to the dispersion, and the mixture was magnetically stirred at room temperature for 12 h to allow surface anchoring of aN-GQDs. The composite was collected by centrifugation, washed three times with ultrapure water to remove unbound aN-GQDs, and dried at 37°C for 24 h. A bright yellow powder of ZIF-8@aN-GQDs was thus obtained.

Characterization of ZIF-8@aN-GQDs

The morphology and microstructure of ZIF-8@aN-GQDs were examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, GeminiSEM 500, Zeiss, Germany) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Elemental distribution was analyzed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) equipped with the TEM. The hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution of the nanoparticles were measured via dynamic light scattering (DLS, RESUN-G03 Nano Coulter G, China). Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded on a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FluoroMax-4, HORIBA Jobin Yvon, Japan) to assess the optical properties of aN-GQDs after hybridization. The crystalline structure was evaluated by X-ray diffraction (XRD, SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan). Surface chemical composition and bonding states were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-Alpha, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Preparation of PA-ZA hydrogels

Acrylamide (2% w/w) and sodium alginate (12% w/w) were dissolved in deionized water and magnetically stirred at room temperature until completely dissolved. Subsequently, 7 mL of the precursor solution was mixed sequentially with ZIF-8@aN-GQDs (final concentration: 500 μg/mL), 25.2 μL of MBAA crosslinker, 11.2 μL of TEMED, 158.2 μL of APS (66 mg/mL), and 87.5 μL of CaCl2 solution (15% w/w). The mixture (200 μL per slab) was immediately transferred into sealed polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) molds and allowed to polymerize overnight, yielding ZIF-8@aN-GQDs-loaded PAAm-Alg hydrogels, hereafter referred to as PA-ZA.

For preparation of the chitosan anchoring primer, 0.2 g of chitosan powder was dissolved in 10 mL of 1 mM HCl under magnetic stirring at room temperature until fully solubilized. The solution pH was then adjusted to 5.0, and the resulting formulation was used as the acoustic anchoring agent.

Characterization of PA-ZA hydrogels

The microstructural of PA and PA-ZA hydrogels was examined by SEM to determine the effect of ZIF-8@aN-GQDs incorporation on the internal porous network. The viscoelastic properties of the hydrogels were characterized using a rotational rheometer (MCR 302, Anton Paar, Austria) under oscillatory shear mode.

Swelling behavior was evaluated by weighing the dried hydrogels (W0), immersing them in 3 mL PBS (pH 7.4) at 37°C for 72 h, blotting surface water, and re-weighing (W1). The swelling ratio was calculated as follows: Swelling ratio (%) = [(W1 - W0) / W0] × 100%.

The in vitro release kinetics of components derived from ZIF-8@aN-GQDs within PA-ZA hydrogels were evaluated under both ultrasound-treated (US+) and non-ultrasound (US-) conditions. PA-ZA hydrogels were immersed in PBS at room temperature, and supernatants were collected at 5 min, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, 48 h, and 60 h. Specifically, in the US + group, ultrasound irradiation was applied for 5 min immediately prior to sampling at 5 min, 24 h, and 48 h. The collected supernatants were digested with nitric acid, and the total Zn content was subsequently determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; Agilent 5110, USA). PBS and supernatants from PA hydrogels served as background controls. The cumulative release was calculated based on the background-corrected Zn content in the supernatants and expressed as the cumulative release percentage of ZIF-8@aN-GQDs-derived Zn-containing components.

The hemocompatibility of the hydrogels was assessed using a standard hemolysis assay [51]. Whole blood obtained from mice was centrifuged to isolate red blood cells (RBCs). The RBCs were washed five times with physiological saline and resuspended to a 5% (v/v). Sterile hydrogel samples (PA, PA-ZIF-8, PA-aN-GQDs, PA-ZA) were placed in a 48-well plate and incubated with 1 mL of the RBC suspension at 37°C for 1 h. After incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatants at 540 nm was measured to determine hemoglobin release. Physiological saline and 0.1% Triton X-100 were used as the negative and positive controls, respectively. The hemolysis ratio was calculated using: Hemolysis ratio (%) = [(A_sample-A_negative)/(A_positive−A_negative)] × 100%, where A represents the absorbance at 540 nm.

Ultrasound-mediated tissue adhesion of PA-ZA hydrogels

A fresh porcine skin sample was coated with 200 μL of the chitosan anchoring solution and then exposed to low-frequency ultrasound (28 kHz, 1 min) using a transducer (WOODPECKER D7LED, China). Immediately afterward, PA-ZA hydrogel patches were applied to the treated surface. Samples without ultrasound served as controls. Adhesion was qualitatively evaluated by manual peel tests, and adhesion energy was quantified using a tensile testing machine (BLD-1028A, China).

Transdermal delivery of Chi-FITC and ZIF-8-FITC

To prepare FITC-labeled chitosan (Chi-FITC), 1 g of chitosan was dissolved in 100 mL of 0.1 M acetic acid under magnetic stirring, followed by the addition of an equal volume of anhydrous methanol. FITC (17.5 mg in 17.5 mL methanol) was added dropwise and the reaction proceeded for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. The pH was then adjusted to 10.0 to precipitate Chi-FITC. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 10 min), washed five times with deionized water, redissolved in 0.1 M acetic acid, dialyzed for 3 days, and freeze-dried. For the transdermal permeation study, 200 μL of a 2% (w/v) Chi-FITC solution was applied to excised pig skin and irradiated with low-frequency ultrasound (28 kHz, 1 min), whereas non-ultrasound samples served as US− controls. A PA-ZA hydrogel patch was then placed on the skin and incubated at 37°C for 1 h, after which the tissues were frozen at -80°C, cryosectioned, and imaged by confocal fluorescence microscopy to quantify Chi-FITC penetration depth.

To prepare ZIF-8-FITC, FITC solution (1 mg/mL in methanol, protected from light) was added dropwise to an aqueous dispersion of ZIF-8 nanoparticles at a FITC:ZIF-8 mass ratio of 1:30. The mixture was stirred for 4-6 h under light-protected conditions, after which the labeled nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 10–15 min) and washed three times with deionized water. To assess transdermal permeation, a PAAm/Alg hydrogel patch was anchored to pig skin using low-frequency ultrasound. The samples were then exposed to mid-frequency ultrasound (1 MHz, 0.94 W/cm2, 37.5% duty cycle, 10 min), with non-ultrasound samples serving as US− controls. After incubation at 37°C for 1 h, tissues were frozen at -80°C, cryosectioned, and imaged using confocal fluorescence microscopy to evaluate ZIF-8-FITC penetration depth.

ROS detection In Vitro

ROS generated under ultrasound were quantified using specific fluorescent probes for superoxide anion (DHR123), singlet oxygen (SOSG), and hydroxyl radicals (terephthalic acid, TA) [55]. PA-ZA hydrogels were immersed in probe-containing PBS and treated with or without ultrasound under the same SDT parameters as those used in the biological experiments. Probe-containing PBS with or without ultrasound served as the control groups, and PBS without probes was used as the negative control. Fluorescence spectra were recorded immediately after treatment. ROS generation was quantified based on the characteristic emission peak intensity of each probe.

Evaluation of ultrasound safety In Vitro

The biological safety of the ultrasonic parameters used for tissue anchoring and transdermal activation was systematically evaluated. Briefly, a chitosan-based anchoring primer was applied to the skin of porcine skin or ICR mice, after which low-frequency ultrasound was administered for 60 s. Skin-surface temperature was recorded in real time using an infrared thermal imaging camera to monitor potential thermal effects. Immediately following irradiation, treated skin tissues were harvested, fixed, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess tissue integrity.

To further evaluate local tissue compatibility following repeated ultrasound irradiation, the PA-ZA hydrogel was first attached to murine skin using low-frequency ultrasound-assisted anchoring. Subsequently, medium-frequency ultrasound was applied every other day using the same parameters used for sonodynamic activation. A total of five ultrasound treatments were performed. After the final ultrasound treatment, the treated skin tissue was collected and subjected to H&E staining. Potential ultrasound-induced tissue damage, including epidermal disruption, dermal structural damage, inflammatory infiltration, and tissue necrosis, was assessed by histological examination.

To further evaluate safety during therapeutic activation, PA-ZA hydrogels were first applied after ultrasound-assisted anchoring. Mid-frequency ultrasound was then delivered using the same parameters as in transdermal delivery experiments. Infrared thermal imaging was employed to continuously monitor changes in skin temperature during irradiation.

In Vitro antimicrobial activity assay

The in vitro antibacterial activity of the hydrogels was evaluated by quantifying viable bacterial colonies. E. coli or S. aureus suspensions were adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL in LB medium. Sterile hydrogels (PA, PA-ZIF-8, PA-aN-GQDs, PA-ZA) were mixed with 1 mL of bacterial suspension and irradiated with ultrasound (US + group; 1 MHz, 0.94 W/cm2, 10 min). Hydrogels without ultrasound served as the US− control. Following a 1 h incubation at 37°C, the bacterial suspensions were serially diluted and spread onto LB agar plates. After overnight incubation, plates were imaged and colony-forming units (CFUs) were enumerated.

Bacterial viability was visualized using a LIVE/DEAD Bacterial Staining Kit. Following incubation with hydrogels under US + or US− conditions, bacterial suspensions were stained with DMAO (live bacteria, green) and PI (dead bacteria, red) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence signals were acquired using a laser-scanning confocal microscope.

In Vitro biocompatibility

Biocompatibility was evaluated using CCK-8 assays and live/dead staining. Sterile hydrogels from each formulation were incubated in culture medium and subjected to mid-frequency ultrasound, after which the conditioned supernatant was collected and applied to NIH/3T3 cells and HUVECs. Cell viability was quantified using the CCK-8, and cytotoxicity was assessed using the Calcein-AM/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Cell migration and angiogenesis assays

Cell migration was assessed using Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size, Corning) in 24-well plates. NIH/3T3 or HUVECs (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into the upper chamber, while hydrogel-conditioned medium obtained following ultrasound treatment was added to the lower chamber. After a 24 h incubation at 37°C, cells remaining on the upper membrane surface were removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Images were acquired with a microscope, and migration was quantified using ImageJ software.

Tube formation assays were performed in Matrigel-coated 24-well plates. Matrigel was thawed on ice, dispensed into wells, and allowed to polymerize at 37°C for 30 min. HUVECs (1 × 105 cells/well) were then seeded onto the polymerized Matrigel layer and cultured in hydrogel-conditioned medium. After 6 h at 37°C, capillary-like network structures were imaged with an inverted microscope and quantified using Image J.

In Vivo infected wound and subcutaneous infection models

A full-thickness S. aureus–infected wound model was established in male ICR mice to evaluate the therapeutic effects of different hydrogel formulations. Following isoflurane anesthesia, dorsal hair was removed and the skin was disinfected with iodophor. Two circular full-thickness wounds (8 mm diameter) were created on the dorsum using a biopsy punch, after which a S. aureus suspension (10 μL, 1 × 108 CFU/mL) was inoculated into each wound. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): untreated control, PA + US, PA-ZA, and PA-ZA + US. In the PA-ZA + US group, 28 kHz ultrasound for chitosan-primer-assisted peri-wound anchoring and 1 MHz ultrasound for nanocomposite delivery/sonodynamic activation were applied sequentially in the same animals according to the timeline in Fig. 5A. Wound images were recorded on days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14, and wound area was quantified using ImageJ software. Residual bacterial burden was assessed by collecting wound swabs on days 1, 7, and 14. Swabs were immersed in PBS, vortexed, and the resulting suspension was plated on agar for colony formation after 12 h of incubation.

To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of PA-ZA under ultrasound stimulation in non-exposed infections, a subcutaneous S. aureus infection model was established. Mice were anesthetized, shaved, disinfected, and injected subcutaneously with S. aureus suspension (10 μL, 1 × 108 CFU/mL). Animals received either PA-ZA + US treatment or no treatment (control). Lesion appearance was documented on days 0 and 7, and bacterial loads on day 7 were determined by CFU assays.

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses

Wound tissues were harvested on days 7 and 14 for histological evaluation. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Sections were stained with Giemsa, H&E, or Masson's trichrome according to standard protocols to assess bacterial burden, general wound morphology, and collagen deposition, respectively.

To assess systemic tolerability, major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were harvested from mice in the different infected wound treatment groups on day 14 and subjected to H&E staining. Potential systemic toxicity was evaluated by histological examination.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for CD68, Ki67, and CD31 was performed on wound tissues collected on days 7 and 14 to evaluate inflammatory response, cellular proliferation, and neovascularization, respectively. In addition, 4-HNE staining was performed on wound tissues collected on day 7 to assess lipid peroxidation and ROS-associated oxidative tissue damage. Briefly, sections were dewaxed, subjected to antigen retrieval, quenched for endogenous peroxidase activity, and blocked with serum. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Quantitative image analysis was performed using ImageJ.

Transcriptomic analysis

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying PA-ZA + US-mediated wound repair, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on day 7 wound tissues from the control and PA-ZA + US groups. Total RNA was extracted, qualified, and sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (Novogene) following standard protocols. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with ∣log2 (fold change)∣ ≥ 1 and adjusted P value ≤ 0.05.

Downstream analyses—including Pearson correlation, principal component analysis (PCA), volcano plot visualization, and Venn diagram analysis—were conducted using R packages such as ggplot2, VennDiagram, and DESeq2. Functional enrichment of DEGs was performed with clusterProfiler based on the KEGG and GO databases. All transcriptomic visualizations were generated using the NovoMagic (Novogene) and OmicStudio (LC-Bio) cloud platforms.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least triplicate (n ≥ 3), and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
