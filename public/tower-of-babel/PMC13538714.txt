Multimodal semantic learning for early fault diagnosis in warp knitting machines via masked fault modeling
Authors: Wei Gao, Han Shi, Dazhou Li, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Warp knitting machines operate under high-speed and continuous working conditions, where minor abnormalities in yarn tension or vibration may quickly evolve into severe defects. Conventional vision-based inspection methods usually detect faults only after visible defects appear, resulting in substantial latency and limited capability for early warning and predictive maintenance. To address this issue, we propose KnitLLM, a multimodal semantic learning framework for early fault diagnosis in warp knitting machines, which integrates sensor sequences and fabric images for proactive anomaly detection. KnitLLM first employs a vector-quantized variational autoencoder to map heterogeneous temporal signals and image features into a unified discrete semantic space, enabling aligned fault primitive representation across modalities. A Transformer-based masked fault modeling strategy is then introduced to learn long-range dependencies and structural priors of the knitting process by reconstructing masked process states. In addition, a diffusion model is used to augment rare fault samples, alleviating class imbalance and improving recognition performance for minority fault categories. Theoretical analysis from the perspectives of manifold representation, quantization error, and attention structure further supports the semantic abstraction and information preservation capabilities of the proposed framework. Experiments on a real-world industrial dataset containing 10,000 h of warp knitting production data demonstrate that KnitLLM consistently outperforms competitive baselines across multiple metrics, achieving an F1-score of 0.961 and a mean detection latency of 124 ms. The results indicate that the proposed method can identify early faults before visible defects emerge, offering a practical solution for intelligent maintenance and fault warning in high-speed textile manufacturing.

Introduction

With the rapid development of intelligent manufacturing, fault diagnosis for industrial equipment is shifting from post-event inspection toward predictive maintenance1,2. In the textile industry, warp knitting machines are high-speed precision devices that typically operate at rotational speeds of 2000 to 2500 revolutions per minute. Under such conditions, even minor mechanical abnormalities may rapidly evolve into serious defects such as yarn breakage, bars, or fabric damage, leading to substantial and often irreversible production losses3,4. Conventional fault detection methods rely mainly on manual inspection or offline quality control. Such passive, result-oriented approaches are inherently delayed, as alarms are usually triggered only after visible defects appear on the fabric surface, by which point losses have already occurred5,6. In recent years, the adoption of Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) technologies has enabled warp knitting machines to be equipped with high-frequency sensors7,8, such as tension sensors, speed encoders, and vibration accelerometers, together with machine vision systems, thereby providing the data foundation for early fault warning9. However, effectively integrating temporal dynamic features from sensor signals with spatial texture information from fabric images to achieve end-to-end fault diagnosis from physical causes to visual manifestations remains a major challenge10,11.

Existing studies on fault diagnosis for warp knitting machines mainly follow two technical routes. The first focuses on fabric defect recognition based on machine vision12,13. These methods generally use convolutional neural networks to extract texture features from fabric surfaces and perform automatic classification of visually evident defects such as holes, oil stains, and needle marks14,15. However, vision-based methods suffer from inherent latency: image sensors can only capture defects after they become visible on the fabric surface and are therefore unable to perceive the dynamic causes that induce them16,17. For example, when yarn tension fluctuates abnormally, such disturbances may take hundreds of milliseconds or even several seconds to develop into visible bars or density irregularities, meaning that the optimal detection window has already passed. The second route focuses on temporal anomaly detection using sensor signals, where deep learning models monitor physical variables such as tension, rotational speed, and current18,19. Although these sensor-based methods offer better timeliness, they face two important limitations. First, high-frequency industrial signals often contain strong non-stationary noise and periodic vibrations, making threshold-based decisions on raw signals prone to false alarms20. Second, information from a single modality is limited, making it difficult to reliably distinguish transient disturbances under normal operation from early signs of real faults21.

From a broader perspective, multimodal learning and temporal modeling have shown clear advantages in industrial fault diagnosis22,23. By integrating information from different physical domains, multimodal methods can provide a more comprehensive description of equipment conditions24,25. Meanwhile, Transformer-based architectures have made significant progress in temporal anomaly detection26,27, owing to their ability to capture long-range dependencies through self-attention28,29. For instance, Anomaly Transformer achieves unsupervised anomaly detection through association discrepancy modeling27. Variable Temporal Transformer considers both temporal dependencies and inter-variable relationships30, improving performance on multivariate time series31,32. TimesNet transforms time series into two-dimensional tensors and applies convolution to extract periodic patterns33, achieving state-of-the-art results on several benchmark datasets. PatchTST improves the modeling of local patterns through patch-based strategies34, while Informer reduces the computational cost of long-sequence modeling by using sparse attention mechanisms35. Nevertheless, these approaches are mainly designed for single-modality temporal tasks and do not provide semantic alignment mechanisms for heterogeneous multimodal data such as sensor signals and images36.

In addition, recent research has explored intelligent optimization and reasoning methods to improve model performance across different applications. For example, particle swarm optimization has been used for automatic prompt design in large language model-based medical information extraction37, demonstrating the value of optimization-driven prompt engineering. Relation-based node quality evaluation has also been incorporated into multi-hop reasoning for sparse medical knowledge graph38, improving reasoning reliability under limited connectivity. Neural architecture search based on the Brain Storm Optimization Algorithm has likewise shown promise in designing efficient face detection models39. However, when directly applied to warp knitting fault diagnosis, two fundamental difficulties remain. First, sensor signals are high-frequency one-dimensional streams, whereas fabric images are discrete two-dimensional snapshots, and the difference in their temporal and spatial scales makes precise modality alignment difficult. Second, industrial fault data are often highly imbalanced, and the scarcity of rare fault samples prevents models from learning effective decision boundaries40,41.

To address these issues, this study proposes KnitLLM, a multimodal semantic learning framework for early fault diagnosis in warp knitting machines. In we, LLM refers to a language-model-inspired masked semantic reasoning architecture operating over discrete multimodal fault primitives, rather than a conventional text-only large language model. Unlike approaches that simply concatenate multimodal inputs, KnitLLM is designed to achieve semantic alignment across heterogeneous data sources. The core idea is to abstract continuous mechanical signals into discrete fault primitives and build a Transformer backbone on this basis to learn the long-range structural priors of the weaving process. Specifically, a Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE) is used as a context-aware tokenizer to jointly encode sensor time series and fabric images into a unified discrete semantic space42, enabling cross-modal alignment at the semantic level. Through masked modeling, the framework is trained to infer masked mechanical states from context, thereby learning the underlying structural rules of warp knitting operation. In addition, diffusion models are introduced to augment rare fault samples by synthesizing boundary cases in latent space43, alleviating the class imbalance problem. The proposed framework is not a simple combination of existing modules; rather, each component serves a distinct function within a unified semantic early fault diagnosis pipeline: VQ-VAE performs fault primitive discretization, masked modeling learns process grammar, and diffusion augmentation improves rare-event coverage.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the main contributions of we are threefold. First, at the methodological level, we propose a VQ-VAE-based multimodal semantic alignment framework that maps heterogeneous sensor signals and fabric images into a shared discrete fault primitive space, offering a new direction for industrial multimodal learning. Second, at the theoretical level, we analyze how masked fault modeling captures long-range structural priors in warp knitting operations through gradient flow analysis, and we establish the convergence of quantization error using manifold coverage theory, providing a mathematical basis for semantic fault detection. Finally, at the application level, we construct a real-world warp knitting dataset containing multiple fault types and conduct systematic experiments to validate the effectiveness of each module, offering empirical support for applying this approach in intelligent textile equipment.Fig. 1KnitLLM framework for semantic fault detection in warp knitting machines.

The main innovation lies in a semantic fault representation paradigm for industrial multimodal early fault diagnosis. Unlike existing multimodal fault diagnosis methods that rely on feature concatenation or decision-level fusion, KnitLLM first abstracts heterogeneous sensor signals and images into shared discrete fault primitives, and then uses masked fault modeling to infer latent process states and detect early deviations before visible defects emerge. Diffusion-based augmentation is used as a complementary strategy to improve the diversity of rare fault samples. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 describes the framework in detail, Sect. 3 gives the theoretical analysis, Sect. 4 presents the experimental results, Sect. 5 concludes the paper.

Manifold modeling in warp knitting machine

The operating process of a warp knitting machine is governed by periodic mechanical motions, yarn tension variation, and coupled electromechanical responses. Results and analysis

Experimental environment and dataset description

To train the Transformer backbone and support the diffusion-based feature generation module, we used a high-performance computing cluster. The hardware and software environments were selected to ensure stable optimization and efficient processing of the large-scale dataset. At the hardware level, the system was equipped with one NVIDIA A100 GPU and an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6348 CPU running at 2.60 GHz. At the software level, experiments were conducted on Ubuntu 22.04.3 LTS with PyTorch 2.1.0 (https://pytorch.org) and CUDA 12.1. The vector-quantized variational autoencoder was built upon the vector-quantize-pytorch library 0.14.0 (https://github.com/lucidrains/vector-quantize-pytorch). The diffusion model component was developed using the Hugging Face diffusers library 0.21.0 (https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers). Data preprocessing and analysis were implemented using Pandas 2.1.1, NumPy 1.24.3 (https://numpy.org), and Scikit-learn 1.3.0 (https://scikit-learn.org). The Transformer backbone was configured with an embedding dimension of 256, 4 encoder layers, and 8 attention heads in each layer. The hidden dimension of the feed-forward network was set to 1024. Model optimization was performed using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e-4, a weight decay of 1e-2, and a batch size of 32. The model was trained for 50 epochs, and the learning rate followed a cosine decay schedule. A dropout rate of 0.1 was applied to reduce overfitting. Early stopping was adopted according to validation performance, and all hyperparameters were determined empirically on the validation set.

To prevent data leakage, we adopted a machine-level split strategy, in which all samples from the same machine or production run were assigned to the same subset, namely training, validation, or testing. In addition, the dataset was divided chronologically rather than randomly to preserve temporal independence. Within each subset, samples were constructed using temporally ordered sliding windows. The first 70% of the time-ordered samples were used for training, the next 10% for validation, and the remaining 20% for testing. To further reduce dependency between adjacent samples, overlapping windows were not shared across different splits, and a temporal buffer was inserted between the training and testing segments. Normalization statistics were computed only from the training set and then applied consistently to the validation and test sets.

In the experimental design, we follow the task consistency principle. Regardless of whether the input is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\boldsymbol{S}}$$\end{document} (sensor), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\boldsymbol{I}}$$\end{document} (image), or a combination of both, all models ultimately aim to predict \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y \in \left\{ {0,1} \right\}$$\end{document}. To ensure a fair comparison, image-based models such as YOLO were allowed to use only fabric images, whereas KnitLLM was given access to both sensor signals and images. This setting was used to examine whether visual information alone is sufficient for identifying early dynamic abnormalities, or whether it needs to be combined with sensor-based process information. Evaluation metrics

To quantify the performance of KnitLLM in subsequent experiments and conduct horizontal comparison with traditional models, seven commonly used metrics for fault detection were adopted. These metrics not only assess classification accuracy but also the reliability of fault judgment. The following metrics are calculated based on true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN). Precision (P) measures the proportion of actual faults among samples judged as faults:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P = \frac{TP}{{TP + FP}}$$\end{document}

Recall (R) measures the proportion of all actual faults successfully detected during production, serving as the core indicator for assessing the risk of missed alarms for warp breakage shutdowns:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R = \frac{TP}{{TP + FN}}$$\end{document}

To balance precision and recall and prevent models from improving recall through blind alarming, the harmonic mean of P and R, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{1}$$\end{document}-Score, is introduced:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{1} = 2 \cdot \frac{P \cdot R}{{P + R}}$$\end{document}

False Alarm Rate (FAR) is defined as the frequency of erroneous alarms issued by the model under normal operating conditions, corresponding to unnecessary shutdown costs in industrial scenarios:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FAR = \frac{FP}{{FP + TN}}$$\end{document}

Missed Alarm Rate (MAR) is defined as the frequency of fault samples being identified as normal:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MAR = 1 - R = \frac{FN}{{TP + FN}}$$\end{document}

Area Under the Curve (AUC) integrates true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) across different discrimination thresholds, measuring the model’s global discriminative capability under complex manifolds:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AUC = \mathop \smallint \nolimits_{0}^{1} TPRd\left( {FPR} \right)$$\end{document}

Mean Time to Detection (MTTD) measures the time difference between the physical fault occurrence moment and the model alarm generation moment:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MTTD = \frac{1}{{N_{fault} }}\mathop \sum \limits_{i = 1}^{{N_{fault} }} (\hat{t}_{detect}^{\left( i \right)} - t_{start}^{\left( i \right)} )$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{t}_{detect}$$\end{document} depends on the moment when the model output first exceeds the decision threshold. This metric directly reflects the real-time response capability of KnitLLM.

Physical validity validation of diffusion-generated samples

To address concerns about the physical plausibility of samples generated by the diffusion module, we further validate the synthesized signals through a dedicated experimental procedure. This validation is intended to confirm that the latent vectors correspond to physically meaningful process states rather than being limited to statistical regularities. Specifically, the augmented embeddings are first decoded back into the original sensor domain. The validation is conducted from two complementary perspectives. First, a physical simulation test is performed by feeding the reconstructed trajectories of yarn tension and main shaft rotational speed into a warp-knitting dynamics model, where their consistency with momentum conservation and the viscoelastic behavior of yarns is examined. Second, a blind expert assessment is carried out in which three senior textile machinery engineers independently evaluate the process rationality of the reconstructed signals on a five-point scale without knowing whether the samples are real or synthesized.

The results indicate that the samples produced by KnitLLM exhibit stronger physical consistency than those generated by the conventional GAN-based augmentation method. As reported in Table 7, KnitLLM achieves a kinetic residual of 0.042, which is much lower than the 0.185 obtained by the GAN baseline, suggesting better agreement with the underlying physical dynamics. In addition, the mean expert score of KnitLLM reaches 4.72 out of 5, indicating that the reconstructed signals are highly consistent with actual production behavior. Frequency-domain analysis based on the FFT also shows that the spectral energy distribution of the generated signals closely matches that of real industrial fault samples, with a deviation of only 0.021. These results suggest that the diffusion head, conditioned on Transformer hidden states, is able to generate signals that preserve the periodic structure of the weaving process. For example, in simulated yarn-breakage cases, the reconstructed tension signal shows a sudden drop that remains synchronized with the main-shaft phase, rather than exhibiting random numerical drift. Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

The proposed KnitLLM framework delivers clear gains in warp knitting machine fault detection. As shown in Table 3, it achieves a precision of 0.968, recall of 0.954, and F1-score of 0.961, outperforming the strongest baseline, TimesNet, which obtains 0.938, 0.925, and 0.931, respectively. These results suggest that cross-modal attention is effective in capturing complementary relationships between sensor signals and fabric images. In particular, the VQ-VAE-based tokenizer appears to help the model extract more useful information from both temporal dynamics and spatial texture, which improves robustness under industrial operating conditions. Compared with previous studies, the main difference of our approach is the use of a semantic fault-primitive representation that adapts to the physical properties of the input data, rather than relying on fixed handcrafted or static feature extraction methods that may become less effective when the data distribution changes.

Ablation experiments further confirm the contribution of each module. Removing the image modality reduces the F1-score from 0.961 to 0.892, while removing the sensor modality lowers it to 0.843, indicating that both modalities are useful, with sensor signals playing a particularly important role in early fault diagnosis. The sensor-only setting still achieves a recall of 0.905, only slightly lower than the full model, which suggests that temporal information is more informative than static visual appearance in this task. The results on Mean Time to Detection (MTTD) show a similar trend: image-only models reach 248 ms, whereas the full KnitLLM system requires only 124 ms, demonstrating the advantage of incorporating sensor-based process information for early warning.

Compared with recent representative methods, KnitLLM also shows advantages in efficiency and robustness. Although the MFM module introduces additional computation, the use of discrete semantic abstraction keeps the overall complexity manageable. The model is slower than PatchTST in inference, but the difference is acceptable given the improvement in detection performance. In terms of reliability, KnitLLM achieves a FAR of 0.012, which is much lower than D-CNN’s 0.054. This improvement is likely due to the semantic primitive representation, which reduces sensitivity to electromagnetic noise and high-frequency vibration while preserving fault-related information. Even so, the model still struggles in extremely rare fault cases, such as main shaft bearing overheating with very few samples. This is consistent with the limitations of data-driven models in low-data settings and indicates that the diffusion-based augmentation strategy remains important.

Conclusion

This study proposes KnitLLM, a multimodal semantic learning framework for early fault diagnosis in warp knitting machines. By combining sensor signals and fabric images through a VQ-VAE tokenizer, masked fault modeling, and diffusion-based augmentation, the model can detect faults earlier and more accurately than conventional baselines. Experiments on a real industrial dataset show that KnitLLM achieves strong performance in precision, recall, F1-score, and detection latency, while also maintaining a low false alarm rate.

Despite these results, several limitations remain. First, the experiments were conducted mainly on a single machine type, so further validation is needed across different warp knitting machines and production environments. Second, the model still requires considerable computational resources, which makes deployment on edge devices difficult. Third, although attention visualization provides some interpretability, the decision process is not yet fully transparent.

Future work will focus on three directions. One is improving cross-device generalization through transfer learning or meta-learning. Another is developing lighter model architectures, for example by distilling the Transformer into smaller networks or using sparse attention mechanisms. The third direction is improving interpretability by incorporating causal analysis so that the relationship between sensor changes and fault decisions can be explained more clearly. In addition, building larger and more challenging benchmark datasets with device variation, missing modalities, and long-term degradation will help evaluate the method more thoroughly.
