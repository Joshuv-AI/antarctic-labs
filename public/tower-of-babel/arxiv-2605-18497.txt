1.
Introduction

1.1.
Measure quantization via MMD with power kernel and the main result

Measure quantization
is a central problem in approximation theory, numerical integration, and modern machine learning. Given a target probability measure
\omega
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an integer
N\geq 1
, one seeks an empirical measure

\mu_{N}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}},\qquad x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

that approximates
\omega
as accurately as possible with respect to a chosen discrepancy. Different choices of discrepancy lead to different quantization theories. The classical quantization theory, going back to Zador
[
42
]
adopts the
p
-Wasserstein distance, see also
[
19
]
for a throughoug review. In the last two decades the machine-learning, statistics, and image-processing communities
[
20
,
31
,
28
]
have increasingly turned to a different family of discrepancies, namely the
Maximum Mean Discrepancy
(MMD). Given a symmetric kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, the squared MMD between two probability measures
\mu,\omega\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is

(1.1)

\mathrm{MMD}_{K}^{2}(\mu,\omega)\;=\;\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y)\,d(\mu-\omega)(x)\,d(\mu-\omega)(y),

which is non-negative whenever
K
is positive (or conditionally negative) definite. A particularly important and analytically rich subfamily of MMD, and the one we shall focus on in this paper, is obtained by choosing the
power kernel

K_{q}(x,y)\;=\;-|x-y|^{q},\qquad q\in(0,2),

which, by a classical result of Schoenberg
[
29
,
30
]
, is conditionally negative definite on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and therefore yields a non-negative discrepancy on probability measures. The resulting quantity, classically known as the
energy distance
, is

(1.2)

\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu,\omega)\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{q}\,d(\mu-\omega)(x)\,d(\mu-\omega)(y),\qquad 0<q<2,

defined as soon as
\mu,\omega
have finite
q
-moments, and coincides up to a factor of
1/2
with
\mathrm{MMD}_{K_{q}}^{2}(\mu,\omega)
. The choice
q=1
recovers the original electrostatic halftoning energy of Schmaltz et al.
[
28
]
and lies at the heart of the energy statistics introduced by Székely
[
33
,
35
,
36
]
; we refer to Section
1.2
below for a detailed historical review. As we shall discuss in Section
1.3
, the use of
\mathcal{E}_{q}
in lieu of the Wasserstein distance offers decisive computational and statistical advantages in high dimensions, while still inducing a comparable topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, see Proposition
1.2
for a precise statement.

The natural quantitative question for this quantization problem is then:

How fast does the quantization error
\inf_{\mu_{N}\in\mathcal{P}^{N}}\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mu_{N},\omega)
decay as
N\to\infty
?

where
\mathcal{P}^{N}
denotes the class of
N
-point empirical measures. Fornasier and Hütter
[
15
]
established that minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{q}(\cdot,\omega)
on
\mathcal{P}^{N}
converge narrowly to
\omega
, but no quantitative rate of convergence was provided. The main contribution of this paper is to fill this gap by establishing
sharp two-sided bounds
on this infimum, under the natural assumption that
\omega
is Ahlfors regular of exponent
\beta\in(0,d]
. Our main result can be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume
\omega\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\textup{supp}\;\omega
is connected, and that there exist
C_{\omega}>1,R_{0}>0
and
\beta\in(0,d]
such that

(1.3)

C^{-1}_{\omega}r^{\beta}\leq\omega(B_{r}(x))\leq C_{\omega}r^{\beta}

for every
x\in\textup{supp}\;\omega
and every
r\in(0,R_{0})
. Fix
q\in(0,2)
and the constant
\kappa_{d,q}=2\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(1-q/2)/(2^{q}\Gamma((d+q)/2))>0
. Then the following two statements hold:

(i)

there exist constants
c_{L}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
N\geq N_{0}
and every empirical measure
\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
,

(1.4)

\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mu_{N},\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}_{d,q}}\|\mu_{N}-\omega\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}\ \geq\ c_{L}\,N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{q}{\beta})};

(ii)

there exist constants
C_{L}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
N\geq N_{0}
there exist
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\textup{supp}\;\omega
such that

(1.5)

\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mu_{N},\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}_{d,q}}\|\mu_{N}-\omega\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}\leq\ C_{L}\,N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{q}{\beta})},

where
{\mu_{N}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
.

As an immediate consequence we obtain the sharp asymptotics

\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{N}}\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mu,\omega)\asymp N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{q}{\beta})},\quad N\to\infty.

Before proceeding, we comment on two notable features encoded in the exponent
-\frac{1}{2}(1+q/\beta)
. First, the rate depends on the
intrinsic dimension

\beta
of
\mathrm{supp}\,\omega
and not on the ambient dimension
d
: when
\omega
is supported on a low-dimensional set (e.g. a smooth submanifold or a self-similar fractal), the convergence is genuinely faster than the worst-case dimension would suggest. Second, the rate
N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+q/\beta)}
is strictly faster than the i.i.d. rate
N^{-1/2}
obtained by sampling
N
points from
\omega
(see equation (
1.7
) below and the discussion therein): optimal quantization beats Monte Carlo, and quantitatively so.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
rests on two main ingredients. A key one is a revisitation of Schoenberg’s embedding theory (see Theorem
2.10
below): we establish that

(1.6)

\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu,\omega)\;=\;\kappa_{d,q}^{-1}\|\mu-\omega\|^{2}_{{H}^{-s}_{0}}\;=\;\kappa_{d,q}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\hat{\mu}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,d\xi,\qquad s=\tfrac{d+q}{2},

where
{H}^{-s}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the homogeneous Sobolev spaces of negative exponent
-s

[
3
,
15
,
36
]
,
identifying the squared energy distance with the squared homogeneous Sobolev norm of negative exponent of the signed measure
\mu-\omega
. Armed with this identification, the lower bound (
1.4
) is proved by a duality argument in the pre-Hilbert space
(\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|\cdot\|_{\dot{H}^{-s}_{0}})
, using carefully constructed bump functions adapted to the geometry of
\omega
. The upper bound (
1.5
) is proved probabilistically by constructing an equimass partition of
\mathrm{supp}\,\omega
and sampling one point per cell, exploiting the Ahlfors regularity to control the diameter of the cells. As a by-product, we obtain the sharp
stochastic
asymptotics

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}^{2}_{q}(\mu_{N},\omega)]\asymp N^{-(1+\frac{q}{\beta})},\quad N\to\infty,

for the random empirical measure obtained by sampling one point per cell of an equimass partition (see formula (
3.17
) and Theorem
3.10
), thus connecting the statistical convergence rate of
\mathcal{E}_{q}
with classical numerical integration / quantization theory
[
19
]
.

We now proceed to a detailed historical account of the energy distance, situating our contribution within several converging streams of research.

1.2.
Energy distances and their historical development

The energy distance (
1.2
) has roots in multiple distinct mathematical traditions of the twentieth century, which gradually converged over the decades.

The mathematical starting point is the classical
Riesz energy theory
of the
1920s–30s. The
\alpha
-Riesz capacity of a compact set is built around the notion of the

\alpha
-energy of a measure
\mu
, defined as

E_{\alpha}(\mu)=\iint|x-y|^{-\alpha}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)
for
\alpha>0
.
This is the classical
repulsive
(singular) case, opposite in sign to the
kernel
|x-y|^{q}
with
q>0
.
In modern language, the Riesz energy with external field
V
is

\mathcal{E}^{V}_{s}(\mu)\;=\;\iint\!\bigl(K_{s}(x-y)+V(x)+V(y)\bigr)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y),

where, for
-2<s<d
, the kernel is
K_{s}=s^{-1}|\cdot|^{-s}

(with the convention
K_{0}=-\log|\cdot|
).
The special case
s=d-2
recovers the Newton–Coulomb kernel.
When
s<0
, i.e. when the exponent
q=-s>0
, the kernel becomes

attractive
rather than repulsive, and one arrives precisely at the
kernel
|x-y|^{q}
appearing in
\mathcal{E}_{q}
.

The decisive abstract step was taken by Schoenberg in two landmark
papers published in 1938
[
29
,
30
]
.
Schoenberg’s theorem asserts that
e^{-t\rho(\cdot,\cdot)}
is a positive-definite kernel for
every
t>0
if and only if
\rho(\cdot,\cdot)
is a
conditionally
negative-definite
kernel.
He also established that a metric space embeds isometrically into a Hilbert space
if and only if its distance function is conditionally negative definite.
The key example, proved in those same papers, is that
|x-y|^{q}
for
0<q<2

is conditionally negative definite on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.

\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{q}\;\leq\;0\qquad\text{whenever }\sum_{i}c_{i}=0.

This is precisely the algebraic condition that guarantees
\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mu,\omega)\geq 0

whenever
\mu
and
\omega
are probability measures (or more generally signed measures
with equal total mass).
For
q\geq 2
the kernel
|x-y|^{q}
ceases to be conditionally negative definite,
and the functional
\mathcal{E}_{q}
can take negative values; in the limiting case

q=2
one finds a degenerate expression related to the variance, while for
q>2

no metric structure on the space of probability measures is obtained in general.

In the 1950s–60s, Jacques Deny (1950) and subsequently Bent Fuglede
(1960) developed the functional-analytic theory of energy spaces for Radon measures.
Deny introduced the pre-Hilbert space of signed measures with finite Riesz energy,
and Fuglede proved its completeness properties.
A central result in this line of work is that the pre-Hilbert space of Radon measures
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with finite weak
\alpha
-Riesz energy embeds isometrically into its
completion, which is a Hilbert space of tempered distributions with energy defined via
the Fourier transform.
This Fourier-analytic perspective reveals that
\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mu,\omega)
is, up to
a positive constant, the squared norm of
\mu-\omega
in a reproducing-kernel
Hilbert space, with reproducing kernel
k(x,y)=-|x-y|^{q}

(which is positive definite on the subspace of measures with zero total mass
precisely because
|x-y|^{q}
is conditionally negative definite).

The connection to mathematical statistics was forged independently by Gábor J. Székely in the mid-1980s.
During a series of lectures delivered in 1984–85 at the Hungarian Academy of Sciences
in Budapest and at MIT, Yale, and Columbia, Székely introduced the class of

energy statistics
(E-statistics), motivated by an analogy with Newton’s
gravitational potential energy: statistical observations are thought of as heavenly bodies
governed by a potential energy that equals zero if and only if a given statistical null
hypothesis is true.
The squared energy distance between two probability distributions
F
and
G

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with independent random vectors
X,X^{\prime}\sim F
and
Y,Y^{\prime}\sim G
, is

D^{2}(F,G)\;=\;2\,\mathbb{E}\|X-Y\|^{q}\,-\,\mathbb{E}\|X-X^{\prime}\|^{q}\,-\,\mathbb{E}\|Y-Y^{\prime}\|^{q},\qquad q\in(0,2).

Writing this in measure-theoretic terms immediately gives

D^{2}(\mu,\omega)=-\iint|x-y|^{q}\,d(\mu-\omega)(x)\,d(\mu-\omega)(y)
,
which is exactly
2\,\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu,\omega)
.
The energy distance satisfies all the axioms of a metric on the space of probability
measures: it is non-negative, symmetric, satisfies the triangle inequality, and equals
zero if and only if
F=G
.
The formal results were published and developed in subsequent work, including the
monograph
[
36
]
and the influential paper
[
35
]
and the paper
[
33
]
, which introduced
distance covariance
and

distance correlation
as independence measures derived from the same framework.

A parallel and independently developed line of work arose in the machine-learning
community under the name of
Maximum Mean Discrepancy
(MMD)
[
20
,
21
]
.
In this setting one defines, for a characteristic kernel
k
,

\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(\mu,\omega)\;=\;\iint K(x,y)\,d(\mu-\omega)(x)\,d(\mu-\omega)(y).

The choice
K(x,y)=-|x-y|^{q}
(restricted to zero-mean signed measures) yields

\mathrm{MMD}^{2}=2\,\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}
, so the energy distance is a special case of the
MMD with a Riesz-type kernel.
The equivalence between distance-based and kernel-based methods for hypothesis testing
was clarified by several authors in the 2000s and 2010s
[
31
,
25
,
32
]
, firmly unifying the statistical and machine-learning perspectives.

In summary, the functional
\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mu,\omega)
sits at the intersection of
classical potential theory (Riesz, 1920s), the theory of conditionally negative-definite
kernels and Hilbert-space embeddings (Schoenberg, 1938), the functional analysis of
energy spaces (Deny–Fuglede, 1950–60s), and the modern theory of energy statistics
(Székely, 1984–present) and kernel methods (MMD, 2000s).
The thread connecting all of these is the conditional negative definiteness of

|x-y|^{q}
for
0<q<2
, a property discovered in abstract form by Schoenberg and
subsequently re-interpreted from completely independent starting points by
statisticians and the machine-learning community alike.

After 2010, seemingly without full knowledge of this previous history, the energy distance (
1.2
) (for
q=1
) was independently re-discovered in the image processing community as a tool to provide image halftoning (or image quantization).

Schmaltz et al.
[
28
]
introduced electrostatic halftoning
as a practical image processing method: given a grayscale image representing a probability
measure
\omega
, they place
N
point masses by minimizing an energy that balances pairwise
repulsion between the points and attraction toward the image’s intensity.
Their discrete functional is exactly

\mathcal{E}_{N}^{2}(x)\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int|x_{i}-y|\,d\omega(y)\;-\;\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}|x_{i}-x_{j}|,

which is a particle discretization of the continuous energy above with
q=1
.
The paper is entirely computational and heuristic: it works well visually, but
no convergence or consistency theory is provided.

Teuber et al.
[
39
]
then gave this functional a rigorous
variational foundation. They recast the halftoning problem as the minimization of a
difference-of-convex-functions (DC) functional, proved analytical properties such as
coercivity, characterized one-dimensional minimizers explicitly, and proposed a
forward-backward splitting algorithm accelerated by the non-equispaced fast Fourier
transform (NFFT). The Fourier acceleration is significant: evaluating
|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{q}

for
N
particles naively costs
O(N^{2})
, but the NFFT reduces this to
O(N\log N)
.
However, Teuber et al. still do not address whether the discrete minimizers actually
converge to
\omega
as
N\to\infty
, i.e. whether the method is
consistent
.

This issue was the starting point of the work by Fornasier and Hütter
[
15
]
. Their main contribution is a proof that the empirical measures

\mu_{N}^{*}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
, supported on the minimizers of

\mathcal{E}_{N}^{2}
, converge narrowly to
\omega
as
N\to\infty
.
The key mathematical tool is
\Gamma
-convergence of the discrete functionals to a
continuous target functional. The central difficulty is that the continuous energy is
not lower semi-continuous with respect to the narrow topology and is not even
well-defined on all probability measures. They resolve both issues via a suitable
Fourier representation: for
\psi=|\cdot|^{q}
with
1\leq q<2
, the energy
equals (up to a constant)

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}[\mu]\;=\;\kappa_{d,q}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl|\hat{\mu}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\,|\xi|^{-d-q}\,d\xi,

which is manifestly non-negative, lower semi-continuous, and zero if and only if

\mu=\omega
.

This Fourier formula is precisely the energy distance (or MMD with a
Riesz-type kernel
K(x,y)=-|x-y|^{q}
) in its spectral form, as discussed in the
energy distance literature: the squared energy distance between
\mu
and
\omega

can always be written as a weighted
L^{2}
distance between their Fourier transforms,
with weight
|\xi|^{-d-q}

[
35
]
. The connection to the theory
of conditionally negative-definite kernels
[
29
,
30
]

is explicit in the paper, which relies on the fact that
|\cdot|^{q}
for
0<q<2

is a conditionally negative-definite kernel, the property first identified by
Schoenberg
[
29
,
30
]
and later exploited
systematically by Székely and Rizzo
[
33
,
35
]

in the statistical theory of energy distances.

The related paper
[
6
]
addresses the following general problem:
given a probability measure
\omega
on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, find its best approximation
in a set
\mathcal{M}_{N}
of Radon measures, where the discrepancy is measured by

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{N}}\bigl\|h*(\mu-\omega)\bigr\|_{2}^{2},

with
h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
a fixed convolution kernel.

The choice of the kernel
h
is crucial.
When
h
is taken to be the kernel associated with the energy distance
|\cdot|^{q/2}
,
the functional reduces exactly to the energy distance between
\mu
and
\omega
,
directly connecting this work to the framework of
Fornasier–Hütter
[
15
]
and to the broader literature
on energy statistics
[
35
,
31
]
.
The central theoretical contribution is a necessary and sufficient condition on the
sequence
(\mathcal{M}_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
that guarantees weak convergence of the
projections
\mu^{*}_{N}\to\omega
as
N\to\infty
.
This condition is of approximation type: the sequence of sets must be rich enough
to approximate, in the weak sense, any probability measure.
The proof relies on tools from functional analysis and measure theory, in particular
on the weak-
*
compactness of the space of Radon measures.

The authors show that this abstract projection problem encompasses, as special cases,
two concrete image rendering tasks.
The first is stippling, i.e. representing an image with
N
point masses
(Dirac deltas), where
\mathcal{M}_{N}
is the set of empirical measures supported on

N
atoms: in this case the problem reduces exactly to the electrostatic halftoning
of Schmaltz et al.
[
28
]
and to the measure quantization
framework of Fornasier–Hütter
[
15
]
.
The second is continuous line drawing, where the image is represented by a
single continuous curve of bounded length: here
\mathcal{M}_{N}
is the set of measures
that are absolutely continuous with respect to arc-length measure on curves of length

\leq N
, a much richer and geometrically structured family.

On the computational side, the authors propose a forward-backward splitting algorithm
to solve the discretized version of the problem.
As in Teuber et al.
[
39
]
, the non-equispaced fast Fourier
transform (NFFT) is used to reduce the computational cost from
O(N^{2})
to

O(N\log N)
. Numerical results include the automatic synthesis of artistic drawings
(single-stroke and stippled reproductions of images), demonstrating the versatility
of the framework.

In the broader picture, this work can be seen as a unifying generalization of the
preceding contributions: whereas Schmaltz et al. and Teuber et al. focus on sets
of discrete measures (stippling), and Fornasier–Hütter prove consistency for that
specific case, Chauffert et al. extend the theory to arbitrary structured sets of
measures and provide abstract convergence conditions. The connection with the energy
distance is explicit: by Parseval’s theorem, the functional
\|h*(\mu-\omega)\|_{2}^{2}

is a weighted
L^{2}
distance between the Fourier transforms of
\mu
and
\omega
,

\bigl\|h*(\mu-\omega)\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;(2\omega)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\hat{h}(\xi)|^{2}\,\bigl|\hat{\mu}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\,d\xi,

which is precisely the structure of the spectral representation

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}[\mu]\;=\;\kappa_{d,q}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{-d-q}\,\bigl|\hat{\mu}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)\bigr|^{2}\,d\xi

derived by Fornasier–Hütter
[
15
]

(corresponding to the choice
|\hat{h}(\xi)|^{2}\propto|\xi|^{-d-q}
),
and identified as the energy distance / MMD with a Riesz kernel by
Sejdinovic et al.
[
31
]
and
Székely–Rizzo
[
35
]
.

To complement this strand of work, the papers
[
14
,
12
]
addressed the Wasserstein gradient flow of MMD and energy distances for
\omega
fixed. In particular
[
14
]
did consider the gradient flow of MMD with sufficiently smooth kernels, while
[
12
]
did address the one of energy distances on measures on the one dimensional space. These results have been recently greatly generalized on the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
by the paper
[
8
]
, which studied the well-posedness and long-time
behaviour of the Wasserstein gradient flow of the Kernel Mean Discrepancy (KMD,
also known as MMD) functional. The model case considered is the squared homogeneous Sobolev distance

\mathcal{E}_{s}^{2}(\mu,\omega)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,\|\mu-\omega\|_{{H}_{0}^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2},\qquad s\geq 1,

with
\omega
a fixed target probability measure.
This functional corresponds to a pairwise Riesz kernel interaction with exponent

q=2s-d
, and its Wasserstein gradient flow is the continuity equation

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}\;+\;\nabla\cdot\bigl(\mu_{t}\,v_{t}\bigr)=0,\qquad v_{t}=-\nabla\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}_{s}^{2}(\cdot,\omega)}{\delta\mu}[\mu_{t}],

where the velocity field
v_{t}
is determined by the first variation of

\mathcal{E}_{s}^{2}(\cdot,\omega)
.

The paper establishes three main results.
First, inspired by Yudovich’s theory for the two-dimensional Euler equation,
the authors prove global existence and uniqueness of the flow in natural weak
regularity classes, without assuming absolute continuity of the initial measure.
Second, in the critical case
s=1
(corresponding to the Coulomb or Newtonian
kernel), they show that the flow converges
globally
to
\omega
at an

exponential
rate under minimal assumptions on the data.
Third, for
s>1
, they establish
local
convergence at explicit

polynomial
rates of the form
O(t^{-\alpha})
, where the exponent
\alpha

depends explicitly on
s
and on the Sobolev regularity of both
\mu_{0}
and
\nu
.
These rates hold both at the energy level and in higher Sobolev regularity classes,
and are shown to be sharp when
\nu
is the uniform measure.
Crucially, except for the special case
s=1
, even non-quantitative convergence
had been an open question in all these settings prior to this work.

As a notable application, the authors consider the gradient flow of the population
loss for shallow neural networks with ReLU activation in the infinite-width and
continuous-time limit. This dynamics can be recast as a Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao
gradient flow on the space of nonnegative measures on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
,
and exploiting a correspondence with the Sobolev energy case with
s=(d+3)/2
,
the authors derive an explicit polynomial local convergence rate for this training
dynamics.

The paper also includes numerical experiments in dimension
d=1
using both PDE
(finite-volume) and particle discretizations, which illustrate and validate the
theoretical convergence analysis.

In the broader context of the energy distance literature, this work can be seen as
the most quantitative and general convergence result available for the Wasserstein
gradient flow of the energy

\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu,\omega)\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}}|x-y|^{q}\,d(\mu-\omega)(x)\,d(\mu-\omega)(y),

whose Fourier representation
\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu,\omega)
is precisely

\|\mu-\omega\|_{H_{0}^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}
for
q=2s-d
. The gradient flow of this
functional governs both the long-time asymptotics of interacting particle systems
used in image halftoning
[
28
,
39
]
and
the training dynamics of overparameterized neural networks, thus unifying several
streams of research through a single analytical framework.

We conclude this historical account with the recent paper
[
3
]
, which is a comparative review to trace three families of divergences, namely Wasserstein distances, Fourier-based metrics, and energy distances, back to their origins in the kinetic theory of rarefied gases (Boltzmann equation, convergence to Maxwellian equilibrium). It argues that the same analytical tools developed to measure how fast a gas relaxes to equilibrium naturally give rise to the divergences most used in machine learning, and it establishes their mutual equivalence in the high-dimensional setting.

1.3.
Comparison of energy distances and Wasserstein distances in terms of computational cost

Given two empirical measures
\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
and

\omega_{M}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\delta_{y_{j}}
, computing the exact
p
-Wasserstein
distance
W_{p}(\mu_{N},\omega_{M})
requires solving a linear program with
N\times M

variables and
N+M
equality constraints. The exact cost is
O(N^{3}\log N)
using
the Hungarian or auction algorithm for the balanced case
N=M
, and more generally

O(N^{2}M+NM^{2})
for the unbalanced case. This is prohibitively expensive for
large
N
.

When it comes to statistical convergence, the dimension
d
enters in a subtle but
devastating way. For a fixed measure
\omega
with enough moments bounded, the expected Wasserstein error between the empirical
measure
\mu_{N}
built from
N
i.i.d. samples from
\omega
is well-known to be

\mathbb{E}[W_{p}^{p}(\mu_{N},\omega)]\;\asymp\;N^{-p/d},\qquad d>2p,

see
Dereich-Scheutzow-Schottstedt
[
11
]
and
Fournier-Guillin
[
16
]
for a precise statement. This means that to achieve a fixed approximation error
\varepsilon
one
needs
N\asymp\varepsilon^{-d}
samples, an exponential dependence on
d
.
In one dimension the Wasserstein distance admits the closed-form representation given by the Dall’Aglio formula
[
10
]

W_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu)\;=\;\int_{0}^{1}\bigl|F_{\mu}^{-1}(t)-F_{\nu}^{-1}(t)\bigr|^{p}\,dt,

where
F_{\mu}^{-1}
and
F_{\nu}^{-1}
are the quantile functions, and the empirical
version can be computed in
O(N\log N)
by sorting. In higher dimensions no such
closed form exists.
Several approximations reduce the cost.
The
Sinkhorn algorithm

[
9
,
27
]
regularizes the transport problem with
an entropic penalty
\varepsilon H(\mu)
, reducing the cost to
O(N^{2})
per
iteration with typically
O(N^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
overall complexity, but the approximation error and numerical stability degrade as

\varepsilon\to 0
. The
sliced Wasserstein distance
approximates
W_{2}
by
averaging one-dimensional Wasserstein distances over
L
random projections, at
cost
O(LN\log N)
, but introduces a bias that grows with
d
. Multiscale and
hierarchical methods can achieve near-linear complexity in favorable geometric
situations, but their guarantees remain dimension-dependent.
For general probability measures
\mu
and
\omega
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, computing

analytically is possible only in special cases: one-dimensional distributions (via
quantile functions), Gaussian measures in any dimension (via the Bures–Wasserstein
metric). In general, the computation requires solving
an infinite-dimensional linear program (Kantorovich’s formulation), which has no
closed-form solution and must be approximated numerically by discretizing the measures
and solving the resulting finite linear program, thus reducing to the empirical case
with all its associated costs.

Now, given two empirical measures
\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
and

\omega_{M}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\delta_{y_{j}}

\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu_{N},\omega_{M})\;=\;\frac{1}{NM}\sum_{i,j}|x_{i}-y_{j}|^{q}\;-\;\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{q}\;-\;\frac{1}{2M^{2}}\sum_{i,j}|y_{i}-y_{j}|^{q}.

The naive cost of evaluating each double sum is
O(N^{2})
(for

Moreover, since the kernel

The relationship between Wasserstein distances and Fourier-based discrepancies has

The comparison proved in Section

work. It is tailored specifically to the power-kernel MMD considered here and shows

The fundamental conclusion is that energy distances are computationally and
statistically far more tractable than Wasserstein distances in high dimensions,
at the cost of being a slightly weaker metric.
The topological relationships between

and Wasserstein distances

In Section

Since

a strictly stronger bound than the one implicit in

Proposition 1.2
.

Assume
q\in[1,2)
,
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
\omega\in\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The following statements hold:

(i)

\lim_{n}\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mu_{n},\omega)=0
implies
w-\lim_{n}\mu_{n}=\omega
and
\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{r}

d\mu_{n}(x)

=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{r}d\omega(x)
for any
r\in(0,q/2)
;

(ii)

w-\lim_{n}\mu_{n}=\omega
and
\sup_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{q}d\mu_{n}(x)<\infty
implies
\lim_{n}\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mu_{n},\omega)=0
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mu_{n},\omega)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then by the Fourier equivalence (
1.6
) we have that
\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)
converges to
\hat{\omega}(\xi)
, for a.e.
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The equicontinuity of
\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)
implies convergence for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence, by Levy continuity theorem
[
17
, Theorems 14.15 and 18.21]
we obtain
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
. Moreover
\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu_{n},\omega)\to 0
implies
\sup_{n}\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu_{n},\omega)\leq M<\infty
and by
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
there exists
M^{\prime}<\infty
such that
\sup_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{r}d\mu_{n}(x)\leq M^{\prime}
for any
r\in(0,q/2)
. Hence,
[
2
, Lemma 5.1.7]
yields
\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{r}d\mu_{n}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{r}d\omega(x)
for any
r\in(0,q/2)
. In particular,
\lim_{n}W_{r}(\mu_{n},\omega)=0
for any
r\in(0,q/2)
.

Now, assume that
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\omega
and
\sup_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{q}d\mu_{n}(x)\leq M<\infty
. We would like to show that
\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu_{n},\omega)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. We use again (
1.6
) and split the integration as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{|\xi|\leq R}|\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,d\xi}_{A_{n}(R)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{|\xi|>R}|\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,d\xi}_{B_{n}(R)}.

For the second integral we proceed as follows: first of all notice that

|\hat{\mu}(\xi)-1|^{2}=|\hat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}-2\,\mathfrak{Re}\,\hat{\mu}(\xi)+1
and from
|\hat{\mu}(\xi)|\leq 1
we obtain

|\hat{\mu}(\xi)-1|^{2}\leq 2(1-\mathfrak{Re}(\hat{\mu}(\xi)))
. Hence,

\displaystyle|\hat{\mu}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle|\hat{\mu}(\xi)-1|^{2}+|\hat{\omega}(\xi)-1|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2((1-\mathfrak{Re}(\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)))+(1-\mathfrak{Re}(\hat{\omega}(\xi)))).

Using the real part of the complex exponential in the Fourier transforms we obtain

\displaystyle B_{n}(R)\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{|\xi|>R}\frac{1-\cos(|\xi||x|)}{|\xi|^{d+q}}d\xi d\mu_{n}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{|\xi|>R}\frac{1-\cos(|\xi||x|)}{|\xi|^{d+q}}d\xi d\omega(x).

We estimate both these integrals in the same manner and we do it exemplarily on first one, by using
1-\cos(t)\leq\min(2,t^{2}/2)
and
\min(a,b)\leq a^{\theta}b^{1-\theta}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{|\xi|>R}\frac{1-\cos(|\xi||x|)}{|\xi|^{d+q}}d\xi d\mu_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{|\xi|>R}\frac{\min(2,|\xi|^{2}|x|^{2}/2)}{|\xi|^{d+q}}d\xi d\mu_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{|\xi|>R}\frac{2^{1-2\theta}|\xi|^{2\theta}|x|^{2\theta}}{|\xi|^{d+q}}d\xi d\mu_{n}(x)

If we choose now
\theta=q/2-\delta
for
\delta>0
small and we use spherical coordinates we can further bound the integral as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{|\xi|>R}\frac{1-\cos(|\xi||x|)}{|\xi|^{d+q}}d\xi d\mu_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{r>R}\frac{r^{q-2\delta}|x|^{q-2\delta}}{r^{d+q}}r^{d-1}drd\mu_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r>R}r^{q-2\delta}r^{-(d+q)}r^{d-1}dr\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{q-2\delta}d\mu_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{r>R}r^{-1-2\delta}dr\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{q}d\mu_{n}(x)\right)^{(q-2\delta)/q}=\frac{R^{-2\delta}}{2\delta}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{q}d\mu_{n}(x)\right)^{(q-2\delta)/q}\lesssim R^{-2\delta}.

Hence, by choosing
R>0
large enough we can ensure that
B_{n}(R)\leq\varepsilon/2
, uniformly with respect to
n
.

We turn now to the integral
A_{n}(R)
, which we split into two terms, one term around
0
and another in an annulus:

\displaystyle A_{n}(R)=\int_{\delta<|\xi|\leq R}|\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,d\xi+\int_{|\xi|\leq\delta}|\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,d\xi.

In the first integral the term
|\xi|^{-(d+q)}
is bounded on the annulus. As we assumed
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\omega
, we have
\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)\to\hat{\omega}(\xi)
for a.e.
\xi
and, by dominated convergence, the first integral on the annulus vanishes for
n\to\infty
. For the second integral near the origin, the bound
|e^{it}-1|\leq|t|
gives

|\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)|\leq|\xi|\bigl(\int|x|\,d\mu_{n}+\int|x|\,d\omega\bigr)
,
so
|\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\lesssim|\xi|^{2-(d+q)}
, which is integrable near
0

since
2-(d+q)>-d
if and only if
q<2
. This implies

\int_{|\xi|\leq\delta}|\hat{\mu}_{n}(\xi)-\hat{\omega}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,d\xi\lesssim\int_{r\leq\delta}r^{2-(d+q)}r^{d-1}dr=O(\delta^{2-q}),

which is made smaller than
\varepsilon/4
by choosing
\delta
small.
Hence
A_{n}(R)<\varepsilon/2
for all
n
large enough. We conclude that
\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu_{n},\omega)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Let us also discuss the relation with the closely related work

[
5
]
. There, the authors develop a
general theory of discrepancy and numerical integration on metric measure spaces
using stratified random sampling over equal-measure partitions. The probabilistic
idea behind our upper bound is similar in spirit: after decomposing the space into
cells of equal mass, one samples independently one point in each cell and estimates
the resulting error in terms of the cell diameters.

However, our result is not a direct application of that framework. In

[
5
]
, the functional setting is
intrinsic to the underlying metric measure space: the quadrature error is tested
against potential spaces or Hajłasz–Besov type spaces built from the metric,
the measure, and a suitable kernel. In the present paper, instead, the discrepancy is
fixed a priori by the ambient Euclidean power kernel
-|x-y|^{q}
, and is identified,
via Schoenberg’s theorem, with an ambient negative Sobolev norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Thus the function space naturally associated with the dual formulation is not
defined from
\omega
or from the intrinsic geometry of
\operatorname{supp}\omega
.
Consequently, even though restrictions of smooth ambient functions to

\operatorname{supp}\omega
have intrinsic regularity, applying the quadrature
results of
[
5
]
would require a
nontrivial trace or identification theorem between the ambient Sobolev structure and
an intrinsic potential Besov space on
\textup{supp};\omega
. For arbitrary
Ahlfors regular supports, possibly fractal or highly irregular, this is not an
automatic matter.

The proof methods are also different. The estimates in

[
5
]
rely essentially on the
Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality, together with intrinsic kernel estimates. Our
upper bound is more direct in this specific MMD setting: we use the Hilbertian
structure provided by the Schoenberg embedding, so that independence and centering
make the cross terms vanish and the variance is controlled directly by the diameter
of each cell. The lower bound is even further from the quadrature approach. Their
lower estimates provide averaged sharpness results for the stratified quadrature
strategy, namely lower bounds on the mean, over the random choice of one point in
each cell, of the corresponding worst-case integration error. Our lower bound is
deterministic with respect to the empirical configuration: it holds for every choice
of
N
points and follows from a duality argument in the ambient Sobolev norm,
using Ahlfors-adapted bump functions supported in empty balls. This yields the
matching rate for the MMD quantization problem.

2.1.
Fourier Transform

Given a signed measure
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we define its Fourier transform by

(2.1)

\widehat{\nu}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i\xi\cdot x}\,\textup{d}\nu(x),\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Then:

1.

\widehat{\nu}
is bounded and
\|\widehat{\nu}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
;

2.

\widehat{\nu}
is uniformly continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

3.

\widehat{\nu}(0)=\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

4.

if
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
then

|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|\leq|\xi|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|\,\textup{d}|\nu|(x).

Proof.

1. For any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i\xi\cdot x}\,\textup{d}\nu(x)\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|e^{-i\xi\cdot x}|\,\textup{d}|\nu|(x)=|\nu|(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\|\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

2. Fix
\xi,h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\widehat{\nu}(\xi+h)-\widehat{\nu}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(e^{-i(\xi+h)\cdot x}-e^{-i\xi\cdot x}\bigr)\,\textup{d}\nu(x),

hence, by
|e^{-i\xi\cdot x}|=1
, we obtain

|\widehat{\nu}(\xi+h)-\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|e^{-i(\xi+h)\cdot x}-e^{-i\xi\cdot x}|\,\textup{d}|\nu|(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|e^{-ih\cdot x}-1|\,\textup{d}|\nu|(x),

where the last integral does not depend on
\xi
. Testing on sequence
h_{n}\to 0
and using dominated convergence, we obtain
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|e^{-ih\cdot x}-1|\,d|\nu|(x)\to 0
as
h\to 0
, deducing the claim.

3. Direct consequence of the definition (
2.1
) with
\xi=0
.

4. Since
\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d})=0
we have
\widehat{\nu}(\xi)=\int(e^{-i\xi\cdot x}-1)\,\textup{d}\nu(x)
.
Using the bound
|e^{-it}-1|\leq|t|
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain

|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|\leq\int|e^{-i\xi\cdot x}-1|\,\textup{d}|\nu|(x)\leq\int|\xi\cdot x|\,\textup{d}|\nu|(x)\leq|\xi|\int|x|\,\textup{d}|\nu|(x).

∎

We denote by
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
the
Schwartz space
of
rapidly decreasing smooth functions, that is

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}):=\Bigl\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})\,:\,\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x^{\alpha}\partial^{\gamma}f(x)|<\infty\text{ for all multi-indices }\alpha,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\Bigr\}.

Equipped with the family of seminorms
\|f\|_{\alpha,\gamma}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x^{\alpha}\partial^{\gamma}f(x)|
,

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
is a Fréchet space whose continuous dual

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
is the space of tempered distributions.
The Fourier transform is an automorphism of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
,
and Fourier inversion holds pointwise for every

f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
:

(2.2)

f(x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\xi\cdot x}\,\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\textup{d}\xi,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We shall systematically exploit this
to rewrite pairings
\langle f,\nu\rangle
between Schwartz functions and
Radon measures as integrals in frequency space, which will allow us to
introduce a natural pre-Hilbert structure on
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
via the
homogeneous Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}
,
see Lemma
2.2
and Section
2.2
below.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and let
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
.
Then

(2.3)

\langle f,\nu\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\,\textup{d}\nu(x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{\nu}(\xi)}\,\textup{d}\xi.

Proof.

Integrate both sides of (
2.2
) against
\nu
.
Since
\widehat{f}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
, in particular

\widehat{f}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so by taking the absolute values and by the finiteness
of
|\nu|
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl|e^{i\xi\cdot x}\widehat{f}(\xi)\bigr|\,\textup{d}\xi\,\textup{d}|\nu|(x)=\|\widehat{f}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\|\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}<\infty.

Fubini’s theorem thus applies, yielding

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\,\textup{d}\nu(x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\xi)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\xi\cdot x}\,\textup{d}\nu(x)\right)\textup{d}\xi.

Since
\nu
is real-valued,

\int e^{i\xi\cdot x}\,\textup{d}\nu(x)=\overline{\widehat{\nu}(\xi)}
,
which proves (
2.3
).
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. If
\widehat{\nu}(\xi)=0
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\nu=0
.

Proof.

Assume
\widehat{\nu}(\xi)=0
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For every

f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
, Lemma
2.2
gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\,\textup{d}\nu(x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{\nu}(\xi)}\,\textup{d}\xi=0.

Since
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
is dense in

C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to the supremum norm, the above implies

\int f\,\textup{d}\nu=0
for all
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and the conclusion

\nu=0
follows from the Riesz representation theorem.
∎

2.2.
Hilbert Structure on
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

The Fourier-analytic perspective on
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
developed in the
previous subsection allows us to equip this space with a natural
pre-Hilbert structure. The key observation is that for
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

the Fourier transform
\widehat{\nu}
vanishes at the origin (since

\widehat{\nu}(0)=\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d})=0
), which makes the weight
|\xi|^{-2s}

integrable near zero against
|\widehat{\nu}|^{2}
, as we verify in
Proposition
2.4
below. This motivates us to introduce the following negative Sobolev norm.
For every
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})

we define the following

(2.4)

\|\nu\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\xi|^{-2s}\,\textup{d}\xi\quad\textup{and}\quad\|f\|_{H_{0}^{s}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\xi|^{2s}\,\textup{d}\xi.

Note that
0<\|f\|_{H_{0}^{s}}<\infty
for every non-zero

f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
, since Schwartz functions decay
faster than any polynomial and their Fourier transforms do likewise,
so the weight
|\xi|^{2s}
does not affect finiteness of the integral.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

1.

if
\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d})\neq 0
then
\|\nu\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}=\infty
;

2.

if
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
then
\|\nu\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}<\infty
.

Proof.

1. By Lemma
2.1
we have that
\widehat{\nu}(0)=\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d})\neq 0
and
\widehat{\nu}
is continuous. Therefore there exist
\delta>0
and
c_{0}>0
such that

|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|\geq c_{0}
for all
|\xi|\leq\delta
. In particular

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,\textup{d}\xi

\displaystyle\geq\ \int_{|\xi|\leq\delta}c_{0}^{2}\,|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,\textup{d}\xi

\displaystyle=c_{0}^{2}\int_{0}^{\delta}r^{-(d+q)}\,r^{d-1}\,\textup{d}r=c_{0}^{2}\int_{0}^{\delta}r^{-1-q}\,\textup{d}r=+\infty.

2. We split the integral:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,\textup{d}\xi=\int_{|\xi|\leq 1}|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,\textup{d}\xi+\int_{|\xi|\geq 1}|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,\textup{d}\xi.

By Lemma
2.1
item 1.,
|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|\leq\|\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
. Hence

\int_{|\xi|\geq 1}|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,\textup{d}\xi\leq\|\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\int_{|\xi|\geq 1}|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,\textup{d}\xi=\|\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-1-q}\,\textup{d}r<\infty.

Since
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, Lemma
2.1
item 4. yields

|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|\leq C_{\nu}|\xi|
for all
\xi
, where
C_{\nu}=\int_{\Omega}|x|\,d|\nu|(x)<\infty
, because
\nu
has compact support.
Therefore

\int_{|\xi|\leq 1}|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{-(d+q)}\,\textup{d}\xi\leq C_{\nu}^{2}\int_{|\xi|\leq 1}|\xi|^{2-(d+q)}\,\textup{d}\xi=C^{2}_{\nu}\int_{0}^{1}r^{1-q}\,\textup{d}r<\infty.

Combining the two parts proves finiteness.
∎

Proposition
2.4
shows that
\|\cdot\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}

is a well-defined finite quantity on
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. To upgrade this
to a genuine pre-Hilbert structure, we introduce the following inner
product on
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
: for every
\nu,\eta\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
define

(2.5)

\langle\nu,\eta\rangle_{H_{0}^{-s}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathfrak{Re}\big(\frac{\widehat{\nu}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{\eta}(\xi)}+\overline{\widehat{\nu}(\xi)}\,{\widehat{\eta}(\xi)}}{2}\big)\,|\xi|^{-2s}\,\textup{d}\xi.

Proposition 2.5
.

(\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{-s}_{0}})
is a real Pre-Hilbert space.

Proof.

For every
\nu,\eta\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have that (
2.5
) is well-defined, indeed

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg|\mathfrak{Re}\bigg(\frac{\widehat{\nu}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{\eta}(\xi)}+\overline{\widehat{\nu}(\xi)}\,{\widehat{\eta}(\xi)}}{2}\bigg)\bigg|\,|\xi|^{-2s}\,\textup{d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{\eta}(\xi)}|\,|\xi|^{-2s}\,\textup{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)||\xi|^{-s}\,|{\widehat{\eta}(\xi)}|\,|\xi|^{-s}\,\textup{d}\xi

and by Proposition
2.4
item 2. we have that
|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)||\xi|^{-s},|\widehat{\eta}(\xi)||\xi|^{-s}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, therefore we can use Cauchy-Schwartz inequality in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to infer

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\mathfrak{Re}\big(\frac{\widehat{\nu}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{\eta}(\xi)}+\overline{\widehat{\nu}(\xi)}\,{\widehat{\eta}(\xi)}}{2}\big)\big|\,|\xi|^{-2s}\,\textup{d}\xi\leq\|\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}\|\eta\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}<\infty.

The
\mathbb{R}
-bilinearity and the symmetry of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{-s}_{0}}
are clear from definition (
2.5
), from which we can also deduce that
\langle\nu,\nu\rangle_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\|\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}
for every
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

To conclude the proof we have to show that
\|\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}=0
if and only if
\nu=0
. An implication is clear. Assume now
\|\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}=0
, therefore we have
\widehat{\nu}(\xi)=0
for
\mathcal{L}^{d}
-a.e.
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and, being
\widehat{\nu}
continuous by Lemma
2.1
item 2., we obtain that
\widehat{\nu}(\xi)=0
for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The conclusion follows from Corollary
2.3
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
be the completion of
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to the metric induced by the norm

\|\cdot\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}
and consider the
\mathbb{R}
-linear isometry
T:\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
given by

T(\nu)(\xi):=\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|\xi|^{-s}.

In particular, being
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
separable, we have that
(\overline{\mathcal{M}_{0}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{-s}_{0}})
is a separable Hilbert space.

The following density result will be used to identify the dual norm
on
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in Proposition
2.8
below,
which is the key tool in the proof of the lower bound in
Section
3.1
.

Lemma 2.7
.

The set
\mathcal{D}_{s}:=\bigl\{\,|\xi|^{s}\widehat{f}(\xi):f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,\bigr\}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Since the Fourier transform is an automorphism of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
, the following equality holds
\mathcal{D}_{s}=\{\,|\xi|^{s}g(\xi):g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})\,\}.
We claim that
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})\subset\mathcal{D}_{s}
.
Indeed, if
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\},\mathbb{C})
then
g(\xi):=|\xi|^{-s}\phi(\xi)
is smooth with compact support
away from
0
, hence
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
and
\phi=|\xi|^{s}g\in\mathcal{D}_{s}
.

Finally,
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\},\mathbb{C})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
:
given
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
, first approximate
h
by a compactly supported smooth function
\tilde{h}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
,
then multiply
\tilde{h}
by a smooth cutoff that vanishes on
B(0,\varepsilon)
and equals
1
outside
B(0,2\varepsilon)
.
Letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
gives convergence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
and hence
\overline{\mathcal{D}_{s}}^{\,L^{2}}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\nu\in{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

(2.6)

\sup\Bigl\{\,|\langle f,\nu\rangle|:\ f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}),\ \|f\|_{H_{0}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq 1\,\Bigr\}=(2\pi)^{-d}\,\|\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}.

where
\langle f,\nu\rangle
is defined in (
2.3
).

Proof.

From Lemma
2.2
we have that

(2.7)

\langle f,\nu\rangle=(2\pi)^{-d}\,\langle\,\widehat{\nu}|\xi|^{-s},\widehat{f}|\xi|^{s}\,\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})},

therefore by Cauchy–Schwarz in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
we have

(2.8)

|\langle f,\nu\rangle|\leq(2\pi)^{-d}\,\|f\|_{H_{0}^{s}}\,\|\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}.

Taking the supremum over
\|f\|_{H_{0}^{s}}\leq 1
gives

\sup_{\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq 1}|\langle f,\nu\rangle|\leq(2\pi)^{-d}\,\|\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}.

In case
\nu=0
then the claim is obvious, otherwise let
h(\xi)={\|\nu\|^{-1}_{H^{-s}_{0}}}\widehat{\nu}(\xi)|\xi|^{-s}
and from Lemma
2.7
, we can choose
f_{k}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
such that

|\xi|^{s}\widehat{f}_{k}\to h
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
as
k\to\infty
.
Then
\|f_{k}\|_{H_{0}^{s}}=\||\xi|^{s}\widehat{f}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})}\to\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})}=1
.
Define
g_{k}:=f_{k}/\|f_{k}\|_{H_{0}^{s}}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
, so that
\|g_{k}\|_{H_{0}^{s}}=1
and

|\xi|^{s}\widehat{g}_{k}\to h
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
.
Using (
2.7
) we get

\displaystyle|\langle g_{k},\nu\rangle|

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}\|\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}\,\bigl|\langle h,\,|\xi|^{s}\widehat{g}_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})}\bigr|

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\ (2\pi)^{-d}\|\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}\,|\langle h,h\rangle_{L^{2}}|=(2\pi)^{-d}\|\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}.

∎

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
\nu\in\overline{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
and assume

\langle f,\nu\rangle=0\qquad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}),

then
\nu=0
.

Proof.

It is a direct consequence of (
2.7
), Lemma
2.7
and Riesz’s Theorem in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
.
∎

We conclude this section with the central equivalence result that
underpins all that follows: the
{H}^{-s}_{0}
-norm on
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

coincides, up to a explicit constant, with the energy distance

\mathcal{E}_{q}
, identifying the pre-Hilbert structure just constructed
with the MMD with power kernel introduced in Section
1.1
.

Theorem 2.10
.

For every
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the following holds

(2.9)

\|\nu\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}^{2}=-\,\frac{\kappa_{d,q}}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{q}\,d\nu(x)\,d\nu(y),

where
\kappa_{d,q}
is the constant appears in
1.1
. In particular the map
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
given by
\Phi(x)=\delta_{x}-\delta_{0}
, satisfies the following

(2.10)

\|\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}\|^{2}_{H^{-s}_{0}}=\|\Phi(x)-\Phi(y)\|^{2}_{H^{-s}_{0}}=\kappa_{d,q}|x-y|^{q}

for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

The equivalence (
2.9
) between the energy distance
and its Fourier representation first appeared informally in
Székely’s 1989 lecture notes
[
37
]
. The first explicit proof
(for
d=1
) is in the 2002 BGSU technical report
[
38
]
; the
first peer-reviewed proof for arbitrary
d
and
q=1
is in
[
34
]
.
The full result for
0<q<2
and any
d\geq 1
, together with the explicit
constant
\kappa_{d,q}
, appears as Proposition 1 of
[
35
]
.
This result has been redescovered independently by Fornasier-Hütter in
[
15
, Theorem 3.6]
using the theory of conditionally positive semidefinite functions and the generalized Fourier transform following the book
[
40
]
. One more proof of this result can be found in the paper
[
3
, Theorem 1]
.
∎

3.
Quantitative Rate of Convergence of Empirical Measures

We fix now
\omega\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that there exist
C_{\omega}>1,R_{0}>0
and
\beta\in(0,d]
with

(Ahl)

C^{-1}_{\omega}r^{\beta}\leq\omega(B_{r}(x))\leq C_{\omega}r^{\beta}

for every
x\in\textup{supp}\;\omega
and every
r\in(0,R_{0})
.
Measures satisfying (
Ahl
) are termed Ahlfors regular
in the literature. In particular, standard density estimates (cf.
[
1
, Theorem 2.56]
) imply that for every Borel set
B
in

\textup{supp}\;\omega

C^{-1}_{\omega}\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(B)\leq\omega(B)\leq 2^{\beta}C_{\omega}\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(B)\,,

where
C_{\omega}
is the constant appearing in (
Ahl
).
If
\beta\in\mathbb{N}\cap(0,d]
, then
\mathcal{H}^{\beta}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits E
obeys (
Ahl
) provided that
E
is
\beta
-rectifiable.
For general exponents
\beta
as above, self-similar sets satisfying the open set condition according to
[
24
]
, such as the Cantor set in
\mathbb{R}
or the Sierpinski gasket, support Ahlfors regular measures.

Remark 3.1
.

Property (
Ahl
) implies that
\textup{supp}\,\omega
must be compact. Indeed, if the support were not totally bounded, we could find a radius
\rho\in(0,R_{0})
and an infinite sequence of points
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\textup{supp}\,\omega
satisfying

\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\geq 2\rho\quad\text{for all }i\neq j.

This would imply that the open balls
\{B_{\rho}(x_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are pairwise disjoint. As a result, we would obtain

1=\omega(\mathbb{R}^{d})\geq\omega\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}B_{\rho}(x_{j})\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\omega(B_{\rho}(x_{j}))\geq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c\rho^{\beta}=\infty,

leading to a contradiction.

3.1.
Lower bound

The aim of this section is to show the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
. We stress out that no connected assumption on
\textup{supp}\;\omega
is needed here. We argue by duality of
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
(cf. Proposition
2.8
): we test
\mu_{N}-\omega
with a sum of bumps functions that vanishes on
\mu_{N}
, keeping the
H^{s}_{0}
-norm controlled by scaling and separation.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume condition (
Ahl
). Then there exist constants
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}

(depending only on
n,C,R_{0}
) with the following property: for every
N\geq N_{0}
and every sequence of points
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists a sequence of points
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
in

\textup{supp}\;\omega

such that the balls
\{B_{\rho_{N}}(z_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N}
are pairwise disjoint and

(3.1)

B_{\rho_{N}}(z_{j})\cap\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}=\emptyset\qquad\forall j=1,\dots,m,

where
\rho_{N}:=\alpha N^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}
.

Proof.

Fix
N
large enough so that

(3.2)

r_{N}:=(2C_{\omega}N)^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}\leq\frac{R_{0}}{2}.

This is possible for all
N\geq N_{0}
with
N_{0}
depending only on
C_{\omega},R_{0}
. Let
Z=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{M}\}\subset\textup{supp}\;\omega
be a maximal
r
-separated set, i.e.

|z_{j}-z_{k}|\geq r_{N}
for
j\neq k
, and maximal under inclusion. We notice that
Z
must be finite because of assumption (
Ahl
),
\omega\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the balls
\{B_{\frac{r_{N}}{2}}(z_{j})\}_{j=1}^{M}
are pairwise disjoint.

Maximality condition implies the covering
\textup{supp}\;\omega\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{M}B_{r_{N}}(z_{j})
:
indeed, if there were
x\in\textup{supp}\;\omega
with
|x-z_{j}|\geq r
for all
j
, then
Z\cup\{x\}
would still be
r
-separated,
contradicting maximality.

Using this cover and assumption (
Ahl
), we obtain

1=\omega(\textup{supp}\;\omega)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}\omega(B_{r_{N}}(z_{j}))\leq M\,C_{\omega}r_{N}^{\beta},

thanks to (
3.2
).
Thus
M\geq 2N
.

Consider the disjoint balls
\{B_{\frac{r_{N}}{2}}(z_{j})\}_{j=1}^{M}
.
Since they are disjoint, each point
x_{j}
can belong to at most one such ball,
hence at most
N
of these balls contain at least one sample point
x_{j}
.
Because
M\geq 2N
, at least
M-N\geq N
balls are empty of sample points.
Relabel
N
of these centers as
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}
, and upon setting

\rho_{N}:=\frac{r_{N}}{2}

we obtain (
3.1
) with
\alpha:=\frac{1}{2}(2C_{\omega})^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}
.
∎

Fix a function
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))
such that

(3.3)

0\leq\psi\leq 1,\qquad\psi=1\ \text{on }B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0).

For
r>0
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
define the rescaled bump

(3.4)

\psi_{r,z}(x):=r^{\,s-\frac{d}{2}}\,\psi\!\left(\frac{x-z}{r}\right)=r^{\frac{q}{2}}\,\psi\!\left(\frac{x-z}{r}\right),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
r>0
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
one has

\|\psi_{r,z}\|_{H_{0}^{s}}=\|\psi\|_{H_{0}^{s}}.

Proof.

We compute the Fourier transform of
\psi_{r,z}
.
Using the change of variables
y=(x-z)/r
, we have

(3.5)

\widehat{\psi_{r,z}}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i\xi\cdot x}\,r^{\,s-\frac{d}{2}}\psi\left(\frac{x-z}{r}\right)\,\textup{d}x=r^{\,s-\frac{d}{2}}r^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i\xi\cdot(z+ry)}\,\psi(y)\,\textup{d}y=r^{\,s+\frac{d}{2}}\,e^{-i\xi\cdot z}\,\widehat{\psi}(r\xi).

Hence

\|\psi_{r,z}\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{2s}\,|\widehat{\psi_{r,z}}(\xi)|^{2}\,\textup{d}\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{2s}\,r^{2s+d}\,|\widehat{\psi}(r\xi)|^{2}\,\textup{d}\xi.

Change variables
\eta=r\xi
, so
d\xi=r^{-d}d\eta
and
|\xi|^{2s}=r^{-2s}|\eta|^{2s}
:

\|\psi_{r,z}\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}r^{-2s}|\eta|^{2s}\,r^{2s+d}\,|\widehat{\psi}(\eta)|^{2}\,r^{-d}\,\textup{d}\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\eta|^{2s}|\widehat{\psi}(\eta)|^{2}\,\textup{d}\eta=\|\psi\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}.

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\delta\in(0,4]
and let
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
\delta
-separated, i.e.
|y-y^{\prime}|\geq\delta\text{ for all distinct }y,y^{\prime}\in Y
.
Then for every
R>0
,

\#(Y\cap B_{R}(0))\leq 2^{d}\left(\frac{R}{\delta}+1\right)^{d}.

Moreover, there exists a constant
C_{d}>0
, depending only on
d
, such that for every integer
m\geq 3
,

\#\bigl(Y\cap\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:m\leq|y|<m+1\}\bigr)\leq C_{d}\,\delta^{-d}\,m^{d-1}.

Proof.

Consider the family of open balls
\{B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(y)\}_{y\in Y}
. Since
Y
is
\delta
-separated, these balls are pairwise disjoint. Now letting
R>0
we have that
B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(y)\subset B_{R+\delta/2}(0)
and therefore

\#(Y\cap B_{R}(0))\,\mathcal{L}^{d}(B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(0))\leq\mathcal{L}^{d}(B_{R+\frac{\delta}{2}}(0)).

Setting
\omega_{d}:=\mathcal{L}^{d}(B_{1}(0))
, we conclude

\#(Y\cap B_{R}(0))\leq\frac{\omega_{d}(R+\delta/2)^{d}}{\omega_{d}(\delta/2)^{d}}=\left(\frac{2R}{\delta}+1\right)^{d}\leq 2^{d}\left(\frac{R}{\delta}+1\right)^{d}.

Fix now an integer
m\geq 3
and set

A_{m}:=Y\cap\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:m\leq|y|<m+1\}.

Let
y\in A_{n}
and
x\in B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(y)
. Since
|y|<m+1
, we have

|x|\leq|x-y|+|y|<\delta/2+m+1,

while since
|y|\geq m
, we also have

|x|\geq|y|-|x-y|>m-\delta/2.

Therefore

B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(y)\subseteq B_{m+1+\delta/2}(0)\setminus B_{m-\delta/2}(0).

Since the balls
\{B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(y)\}_{y\in A_{m}}
are pairwise disjoint, we obtain

\#(A_{m})\,\mathcal{L}^{d}(B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(0))\leq\mathcal{L}^{d}\bigl(B_{m+1+\delta/2}(0)\setminus B_{m-\delta/2}(0)\bigr).

Hence

\#(A_{m})\leq\frac{\omega_{d}\bigl((m+1+\delta/2)^{d}-(m-\delta/2)^{d}\bigr)}{\omega_{d}(\delta/2)^{d}}=\frac{(m+1+\delta/2)^{d}-(m-\delta/2)^{d}}{(\delta/2)^{d}}.

By the mean value theorem, there exists

\xi_{m}\in(\,m-\delta/2,\;m+1+\delta/2\,)

such that

(m+1+\delta/2)^{d}-(m-\delta/2)^{d}=d\,\xi_{m}^{\,d-1}(1+\delta)\leq 5d\,\xi_{m}^{\,d-1}.

Since
m\geq 3
, we have

\xi_{m}\leq m+1+\delta/2\leq 2\,m

and therefore

\#(A_{m})\leq C_{d}\,\delta^{-d}\,m^{d-1},

where
C_{d}:=5d\cdot 4^{d}
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists a constant
\widehat{C}
, depending only on
\psi
,
d
and
s
, such that for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
r>0
, and
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy the separation condition

(3.6)

|z_{j}-z_{k}|\geq 4r\qquad\text{for all }j\neq k,

we have that

(3.7)

\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{r,z_{j}}\right\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}\leq\widehat{C}\,N.

Proof.

By polarization,

(3.8)

\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{r,z_{j}}\right\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\psi_{r,z_{j}}\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}+2\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\langle\psi_{r,z_{j}},\psi_{r,z_{k}}\rangle_{H_{0}^{s}},

where

\langle g,f\rangle_{H_{0}^{s}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{2s}\,\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{g}(\xi)}\,\textup{d}\xi.

By Lemma
3.3
we have
\|\psi_{r,z_{j}}\|_{H_{0}^{s}}=\|\psi\|_{H_{0}^{s}}
for every
j=1,\dots,N
and hence

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\psi_{r,z_{j}}\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}=N\,\|\psi\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}.

From (
3.5
) we have

\widehat{\psi_{r,z}}(\xi)=r^{s+\frac{d}{2}}e^{-i\xi\cdot z}\widehat{\psi}(r\xi),

and therefore for
j\neq k
we obtain:

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{r,z_{j}},\psi_{r,z_{k}}\rangle_{H_{0}^{s}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{2s}\,r^{2s+d}\,e^{-i\xi\cdot(z_{j}-z_{k})}|\widehat{\psi}(r\xi)|^{2}\,\textup{d}\xi.

Changing variables
\eta=r\xi
, we get

\langle\psi_{r,z_{j}},\psi_{r,z_{k}}\rangle_{H_{0}^{s}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\eta|^{2s}\,e^{-i\eta\cdot\frac{z_{j}-z_{k}}{r}}|\widehat{\psi}(\eta)|^{2}\,\textup{d}\eta.

Setting

(3.9)

\Gamma(y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\eta|^{2s}|\widehat{\psi}(\eta)|^{2}e^{-i\eta\cdot y}\,\textup{d}\eta,\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

we get

(3.10)

\langle\psi_{r,z_{j}},\psi_{r,z_{k}}\rangle_{H_{0}^{s}}=\Gamma\left(\frac{z_{j}-z_{k}}{r}\right).

Since
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
\eta\mapsto|\eta|^{2s}|\widehat{\psi}(\eta)|^{2}
is in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
and in particular also
\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
. Therefore, for every
M\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a constant
C_{M}<\infty
such that

(3.11)

|\Gamma(y)|\leq C_{M}(1+|y|)^{-M}\qquad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Fix now
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and define

Y_{j}:=\left\{\frac{z_{j}-z_{k}}{r}:k\neq j\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We claim that
Y_{j}
is
4
-separated. Indeed, if
k\neq k^{\prime}
and both are different from
j
, then

\left|\frac{z_{j}-z_{k}}{r}-\frac{z_{j}-z_{k^{\prime}}}{r}\right|=\frac{|z_{k}-z_{k^{\prime}}|}{r}\geq 4

by (
3.6
).

Choose
M>d
. Then by (
3.11
) and Lemma
3.4
(applied with
\delta=4
),

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq j}\left|\Gamma\left(\frac{z_{j}-z_{k}}{r}\right)\right|=\sum_{y\in Y_{j}}|\Gamma(y)|=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{y\in Y_{j}\cap\{m\leq|y|<m+1\}}|\Gamma(y)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}\#\bigl(Y_{j}\cap B_{3}(0)\bigr)+C_{M}\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}\#\bigl(Y_{j}\cap\{m\leq|y|<m+1\}\bigr)(1+m)^{-M}

\displaystyle\leq 4^{d}+C_{M}C_{d}\sum_{m=3}\frac{m^{d-1}}{(1+m)^{M}}<\infty.

Hence there exists a constant
K
, that depens only on
\psi
,
d
and
s
, such that for every
j=1,\dots,N

\sum_{k\neq j}^{N}\left|\langle\psi_{r,z_{j}},\psi_{r,z_{k}}\rangle_{H_{0}^{s}}\right|\leq K.

In particular

\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\left|\langle\psi_{r,z_{j}},\psi_{r,z_{k}}\rangle_{H_{0}^{s}}\right|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq j}\left|\langle\psi_{r,z_{j}},\psi_{r,z_{k}}\rangle_{H_{0}^{s}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\,N\,K.

Combining this with (
3.8
) we obtain

\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{r,z_{j}}\right\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}\leq N\|\psi\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}+2\cdot\frac{1}{2}\,N\,K=N\bigl(\|\psi\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}+K\bigr).

Therefore (
3.7
) holds with

\widehat{C}:=\|\psi\|_{H_{0}^{s}}^{2}+K.

∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Assume (
Ahl
). Then there exist constants
c_{L}>0
and
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
N\geq N_{1}
and every empirical measure
\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
,

(3.12)

\|\mu_{N}-\omega\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}\ \geq\ c_{L}\,N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{q}{\beta})}.

Proof.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
, to be chosen large enough along the way, and an arbitrary configuration
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\Omega
.
Let
\nu:=\mu_{N}-\omega\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Apply Lemma
3.2
to obtain
N
pairwise disjoint balls

B_{\rho_{N}}(z_{1}),\dots,B_{\rho_{N}}(z_{N})
empty of sample points, thus setting

(3.13)

r_{N}:=\frac{\rho_{N}}{4}=\frac{\alpha}{4}N^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}.

the family
\{B_{r_{N}}(z_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N}
is empty of sample points as well.

Define

(3.14)

f(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{r_{N},z_{j}}(x),

with
\psi_{r,z}
as in (
3.4
).
Since
\textup{supp}\;\psi_{r_{N},z_{j}}\subseteq B_{r_{N}}(z_{j})
, we have
f(x_{i})=0
for every
i
, hence

(3.15)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\textup{d}\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x_{i})=0.

Using (
3.3
) and the definition (
3.4
),
for
x\in B_{\frac{r_{N}}{2}}(z_{j})
we have
(x-z_{j})/r_{N}\in B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0)
and thus
\psi((x-z_{j})/r_{N})=1
.
Hence

\psi_{r_{N},z_{j}}(x)=r_{N}^{\,s-\frac{d}{2}}\qquad\text{for }x\in B_{\frac{r_{N}}{2}}(z_{j}).

Therefore, being
\omega
a positive measure

\int f\,\textup{d}\omega=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int\psi_{r_{N},z_{j}}\,\textup{d}\omega\geq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{B_{\frac{r_{N}}{2}}(z_{j})}r_{N}^{\,s-\frac{d}{2}}\,\textup{d}\omega=r_{N}^{\,s-\frac{d}{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\omega(B_{\frac{r_{N}}{2}}(z_{j})).

By Ahlfors regularity (
Ahl
) and
z_{j}\in\textup{supp}\;\omega
(cf. Lemma
3.2
), for
N
large enough so that
r_{N}\leq R_{0}
, we have

\omega(B_{\frac{r_{N}}{2}}(z_{j}))\geq C^{-1}_{\omega}\left(\frac{r_{N}}{2}\right)^{\beta}.

Thus

\int f\,\textup{d}\omega\geq r_{N}^{\,s-\frac{d}{2}}\,N\,C^{-1}_{\omega}(r_{N}/2)^{\beta}=C^{-1}_{\omega}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{\beta}N^{-\frac{1}{\beta}(\,s-\frac{d}{2})}=C^{-1}_{\omega}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{\beta}N^{-\frac{q}{2\beta}}.

By Lemma
3.5
, since
|z_{j}-z_{i}|\geq 4r_{N}
holds for every
j\neq i
,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H_{0}^{s}}=\big\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{r_{N},z_{j}}\big\|_{H_{0}^{s}}\leq(\widehat{C}N)^{\frac{1}{2}}

Define the normalized test function
g:=f\|f\|^{-1}_{H^{s}_{0}}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})

then

(3.16)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g\,\textup{d}\omega=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\textup{d}\omega}{\|f\|_{H_{0}^{s}}}\geq\frac{C^{-1}_{\omega}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{\beta}N^{-\frac{q}{2\beta}}}{(\widehat{C}N)^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Now apply the dual formula in Proposition
2.8
for
\nu=\mu_{N}-\omega
,

\displaystyle\|\mu_{N}-\omega\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}

\displaystyle\geq(2\pi)^{d}\left|\int g\,d(\mu_{N}-\omega)\right|

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{d}\left|\int g\,d\mu_{N}-\int g\,d\omega\right|=(2\pi)^{d}\int g\,d\omega\geq c_{L}\,N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{q}{\beta})},

with
c_{L}:=C^{-1}_{\omega}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{\beta}(2\pi)^{d}\,\widehat{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.
∎

3.2.
Upper bound

We now turn to the proof of the upper bound. The argument for the main theorem
1.1

will use, in addition to the Ahlfors regularity assumption (
Ahl
), the
following connectedness hypothesis

(Conn)

\textup{supp}\;\omega
is connected.

Its role is to invoke the
[
18
, Theorem 2]
, which provides equimass cells with a
uniform diameter bound. After completing the connected-support upper bound, we
will explain what remains true for arbitrary Ahlfors regular supports, where one can
still construct equimass partitions but, in general, only with control of the sum of the

q
-powers of the diameters (cf. Lemma
3.12
).

In this section
(\Omega,\mathcal{N},\mathbb{P})
will be a probability space, large enough to guarantees the independence of some random objects. Moreover in addition to assumption (
Ahl
) on
\omega
, we require that

Lemma 3.7
.

Assume that
\omega\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies (
Ahl
) and (
Conn
).
Then there exist three constants
c_{3},C_{4}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, depending only on
d
and
\omega
, such that for every
N\geq N_{0}
there is a family
\{A_{1},\dots,A_{N}\}
of mutually disjoint Borel sets such that
\cup_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}=\textup{supp}\;\omega

\omega(A_{i})=\frac{1}{N}
and
\textup{diam}(A_{i})\leq C_{4}N^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}
for every

and exist
\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}
such that

and
B_{c_{3}N^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}}}(z_{i})\cap\textup{supp}\;\omega\subseteq A_{i}
for every

.

Proof.

Direct consequence of
[
18
, Theorem 2]
applied to the metric measure space
(\textup{supp}\;\omega,\textup{d},\omega)
where

is the Euclidean metric.
∎

Given
N\geq N_{2}
and
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
independent random variables such that
X_{i}\sim N\omega\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits A_{i}
for every

(cf. Lemma

), then we can consider the random empirical measure
\tilde{\mu}_{N}:\Omega\to\overline{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
given by

(3.17)

\tilde{\mu}_{N}(\eta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\delta_{X_{i}(\eta)}-\delta_{0})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi(X_{i}(\eta)),

where

is the function in (

).

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
(\mathcal{K},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{K}})
be a separable real Hilbert space and
Z,Z^{\prime}:\Omega\to\mathcal{K}
be two square-integrable i.i.d
\mathcal{K}
-valued random variables. Then

(3.18)

\mathbb{E}[\|Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]\|_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}]=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\|Z-Z^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}].

Proof.

Since
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
are i.i.d., expanding the square gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Z-Z^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]\|_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|Z^{\prime}-\mathbb{E}[Z^{\prime}]\|_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}]=2\,\mathbb{E}[\|Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]\|_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}],

where we used that the cross term

\mathbb{E}[\langle Z-\mathbb{E}[Z],\,Z^{\prime}-\mathbb{E}[Z^{\prime}]\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}]=0

vanishes by independence and zero mean of the centered variables.
Dividing by
2
gives (
3.18
).
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
(\mathcal{K},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{K}})
be a separable real Hilbert space and
Z,Z^{\prime}:\Omega\to\mathcal{K}
be two square-integrable i.i.d
\mathcal{K}
-valued random variables
such that
\mathbb{E}[Z]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[Z^{\prime}]=0
, then

\mathbb{E}[\langle Z,Z^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}]=0.

Proof.

Since
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
are independent and both have zero mean,

\mathbb{E}[\langle Z,Z^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}]=\langle\mathbb{E}[Z],\,\mathbb{E}[Z^{\prime}]\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}=\langle 0,0\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}=0,

where we used that for independent square-integrable
\mathcal{K}
-valued
random variables the expectation of the inner product factorises as the
inner product of the expectations.
∎

Theorem 3.10
.

Assume that
\omega\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies (
Ahl
) and (
Conn
). Then there exist
C_{L}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every

(3.19)

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\tilde{\mu}_{N}-\tilde{\omega}\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}^{2}\bigr]\ \leq\ C_{L}\,N^{-(1+\frac{q}{\beta})},

where
\tilde{\mu}_{N}
is given in (
3.17
) and
\tilde{\omega}:=\omega-\delta_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Fix
N\geq N_{0}
, with
N_{0}
given in Lemma
3.7
, and let
\Phi
and
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
as in (
3.17
). We first observe that
\Phi(X_{i})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\overline{\mathcal{M}_{0}})
for every

, indeed, from Theorem

, for every
\eta\in\Omega
we have

\|\Phi(X_{i}(\eta))\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}=\|\delta_{X_{i}(\eta)}-\delta_{0}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}=\kappa_{d,q}|X_{i}(\eta)|^{q}\leq\kappa_{d,q}\textup{dist}(\textup{supp}\;\omega,0)^{q}<\infty,

because
\omega
has compact support. Setting now, for every

,
\zeta_{i}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\overline{\mathcal{M}_{0}})

\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{N}:\Omega\to\overline{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
are independent
\overline{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
-random variables such that
\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}]=0
for every

.

Next, we claim that

(3.20)

\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mu}_{N}-\tilde{\omega}\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}^{2}]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\|\zeta_{i}\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}^{2}]\,.

To this aim, let
m_{i}:=N\omega\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits A_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every

,
as

(3.21)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\textup{d}m_{i}=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{i})]\qquad\textup{for every $f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})$}.

Note that

(3.22)

\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X_{i})]=m_{i}-\delta_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Indeed, fix

and by continuity-linearity and (

(3.23)

\displaystyle\langle f,\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X_{i})]\rangle=\mathbb{E}[\langle f,\Phi(X_{i})\rangle]=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{i})-f(0)]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(X_{i})]-f(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\;\textup{d}(m_{i}-\delta_{0})=\langle f,m_{i}-\delta_{0}\rangle.

Therefore, by Corollary
2.9
we conclude.

From (
3.22
), for every

\zeta_{i}=\Phi(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X_{i})]=(\delta_{X_{i}}-\delta_{0})-(m_{i}-\delta_{0})=\delta_{X_{i}}-m_{i}.

Since
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(m_{i}-\delta_{0})=(\omega-\delta_{0})=\tilde{\omega}
, we obtain:

\tilde{\mu}_{N}-\tilde{\omega}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\delta_{X_{i}}-\delta_{0})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(m_{i}-\delta_{0})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\delta_{X_{i}}-m_{i})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i}.

In particular, we have

\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mu}_{N}-\tilde{\omega}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{2}]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i}\big\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{2}\big].

Expanding the square and using Lemma
3.9
yields

\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i}\big\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{2}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\|\zeta_{i}\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}^{2}],

hence (
3.20
) follows.

Fix now
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. Let
Z=\Phi(X_{i})
and
X_{i}^{\prime}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an independent copy of
X_{i}
, then set
Z^{\prime}:=\Phi(X_{i}^{\prime})
.
Lemma
3.8
and (
2.10
) give

(3.24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\zeta_{i}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{2}]=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\|Z-Z^{\prime}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\Phi(X_{i})-\Phi(X_{i}^{\prime})\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{2}]=\frac{\kappa_{d,q}}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|^{q}].

Since
X_{i},X_{i}^{\prime}\in A_{i}
a.s.,
|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|\leq\textup{diam}(A_{i})
a.s., hence

\mathbb{E}[\|\zeta_{i}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{2}]\leq\frac{\kappa_{d,q}}{2}\,\textup{diam}(A_{i})^{q}.

Use (
3.24
) and insert into (
3.20
):

\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mu}_{N}-\tilde{\omega}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\kappa_{d,q}}{2}\,\textup{diam}(A_{i})^{q}\leq\frac{\kappa_{d,q}}{2N}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\textup{diam}(A_{i})\right)^{q}.

Finally, by Lemma
3.7
,
\textup{diam}(A_{i})\leq C_{4}N^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}
, so

\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mu}_{N}-\tilde{\omega}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{2}]\leq\frac{\kappa_{d,q}}{2N}C_{4}^{q}\,N^{-\frac{q}{\beta}}=C_{L}\,N^{-(1+\frac{q}{\beta})},

where
C_{L}:=\frac{\kappa_{d,q}}{2}C_{4}^{q}
.
∎

Corollary 3.11
.

Assume that
\omega\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies (
Ahl
) and (
Conn
), then there exists
C_{L}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
N\geq N_{0}
there exist
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\textup{supp}\;\omega
with

(3.25)

\|\mu_{N}-\omega\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}\leq\ C_{L}\,N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{q}{\beta})},

where
{\mu_{N}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
.

We now pass to consider the general Ahlfors regular case,
where no connectedness assumption is imposed on
\textup{supp}\;\omega
. The
price to pay is that the Gigante–Leopardi uniform estimate

\textup{diam}(A_{i})\lesssim N^{-1/\beta}
is no longer available in
general. However, the probabilistic proof only requires control of

\sum_{i}\operatorname{diam}(A_{i})^{q}
. The next lemma provides precisely this weaker
global estimate.

Lemma 3.12
.

Assume that
\omega\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies (
Ahl
). Then, there exist a constant
\hat{C}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every integer
N\geq N_{0}
, one can find a Borel partition
\textup{supp}\;\omega=A_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup A_{N}
satisfying

\omega(A_{i})=\frac{1}{N}\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,N,

and

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{diam}(A_{i})^{q}\leq\hat{C}\begin{cases}N^{1-\frac{q}{\beta}},&\text{if }q<\beta,\\[5.69054pt]
\log N,&\text{if }q=\beta,\\[5.69054pt]
1,&\text{if }q>\beta.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
K:=\textup{supp}\;\omega
. From Remark
3.1
we have that
K
is compact and thus we can embed it into a half-open cube
Q^{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of side length
\ell_{0}>0
. For each integer
k\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{D}_{k}
denote the grid of half-open dyadic subcubes of
Q^{0}
with side length
\ell_{k}:=2^{-k}\ell_{0}
. This induces a natural partition of
K
at each generation
k
, given by
K=\bigsqcup_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}K_{Q}
, where
K_{Q}:=K\cap Q
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{k}:=\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}:K_{Q}\neq\varnothing\}
denote the family of active cubes at generation
k
. We claim that for
k
sufficiently large,

(3.26)

\#\mathcal{A}_{k}\leq C_{1}\ell_{k}^{-\beta},

for some
C_{1}>0
. To see this, extract a maximal
\ell_{k}
-separated set
Z_{k}\subset K
. The maximality ensures
K\subset\bigcup_{z\in Z_{k}}B_{\ell_{k}}(z)
, while the separation implies that the balls
\{B_{\ell_{k}/2}(z)\}_{z\in Z_{k}}
are pairwise disjoint. For
k
large enough such that
\ell_{k}/2<R_{0}
, the lower Ahlfors bound yields
\omega(B_{\ell_{k}/2}(z))\geq C^{-1}_{\omega}(\ell_{k}/2)^{\beta}
. Since
\omega
is a probability measure, it follows that
\#Z_{k}\leq\frac{2^{\beta}}{c}\ell_{k}^{-\beta}
. The maximality ensures
K\subset\bigcup_{z\in Z_{k}}B_{\ell_{k}}(z)
, therefore, since each ball
B_{\ell_{k}}(z)
intersects at most
C_{d}
dyadic cubes of side length
\ell_{k}
, the bound on
\#\mathcal{A}_{k}
immediately follows.

Given
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
J=J(N)\in\mathbb{N}
as the smallest positive integer such that
C(\sqrt{d}\,\ell_{J})^{\beta}=C\ell_{0}^{\beta}(\sqrt{d}\,2^{-J})^{\beta}\leq\frac{1}{N}
, hence if
N
is large enough we get
\sqrt{d}\,\ell_{J}<R_{0}
and
J(N)>1
. In particular, by the minimality of
J
, we have that

(3.27)

C\ell_{0}^{\beta}(\sqrt{d}\,2^{-J+1})^{\beta}>\frac{1}{N}

therefore there exists a constant
\overline{C}
, independent of
N
, such that

(3.28)

J=J(N)\in(\overline{C}^{-1}\log N,\overline{C}\log N).

Consequently, for any cube
Q\in\mathcal{A}_{J}
, picking any
x_{Q}\in K_{Q}
gives
K_{Q}\subset B_{\sqrt{d}\,\ell_{J}}(x_{Q})
, which by the upper Ahlfors bound guarantees
\omega(K_{Q})\leq\frac{1}{N}
.

Fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we construct the partition inductively, moving from the terminal generation
J(N)
upward to the root
Q^{0}
. For each active cube
Q\in\mathcal{A}_{J}
, we initialize its residue as
R_{Q}:=K_{Q}
, and from the previous argument we have
\omega(R_{Q})\leq\frac{1}{N}
. We proceed by backward induction on
k<J
. At generation
k
, for each
Q\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
, assume that for all active sub-cubes
Q^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Sub}(Q)\subset\mathcal{A}_{k+1}
we have already defined valid residues
R_{Q^{\prime}}\subset K_{Q^{\prime}}
such that
\omega(R_{Q^{\prime}})\leq\frac{1}{N}
. Then we define the pooled residue as

S_{Q}:=\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Sub}(Q)}R_{Q^{\prime}}.

Since a dyadic cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has exactly
2^{d}
direct sub-cubes,
\omega(S_{Q})\leq 2^{d}/N
.

Let
m_{Q}:=\lfloor N\omega(S_{Q})\rfloor
. Now, by the Ahlfors regularity (
Ahl
) we have that
\omega
is non-atomic, and therefore we can extract exactly
m_{Q}
pairwise disjoint Borel sets
A_{Q,1},\dots,A_{Q,m_{Q}}\subset S_{Q}
, each of mass exactly
\frac{1}{N}
(see
[
13
, Corollary 1.21]
). The updated residue for
Q
is then defined as the remainder:

R_{Q}:=S_{Q}\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{m_{Q}}A_{Q,j}.

By the definition of
m_{Q}
we have that
\omega(R_{Q})=\omega(S_{Q})-\frac{1}{N}m_{Q}<\frac{1}{N}
. Furthermore, since each set
A_{Q,j}
is contained in
Q
, we have
\operatorname{diam}(A_{Q,j})\leq\operatorname{diam}(Q)=\sqrt{d}\,\ell_{k}
.

This process propagates up to the root
Q^{0}
at
k=0
. Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the global collection of all the pairwise disjoint sets
A_{Q,j}
extracted during the entire process across all scales. Let
M
be the total number of sets in
\mathcal{F}
. The construction terminates with a final root residue
R_{Q^{0}}
, which satisfies
\omega(R_{Q^{0}})<\frac{1}{N}
.

By mass conservation, since the original support
K
is partitioned exactly into the
M
sets of
\mathcal{F}
and the final residue
R_{Q^{0}}
, we can write:

1=\omega(K)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{F}}\omega(A)+\omega(R_{Q^{0}})=\frac{M}{N}+\omega(R_{Q^{0}}).

Rearranging this identity yields:

\omega(R_{Q^{0}})=\frac{N-M}{N}.

Because the inductive process ensures
\omega(R_{Q^{0}})<\frac{1}{N}
, we deduce that
(N-M)/N<\frac{1}{N}
, in turn implying so that
N-M<1
, and finally
M=N
since both
N
and
M
are non-negative integers.
Consequently,
\omega(R_{Q^{0}})=0
, proving that we have extracted exactly
N
sets. Adding the null residue
R_{Q^{0}}
to the first set
A_{1}
produces the desired exact Borel partition
K=A_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup A_{N}
, with
\omega(A_{i})=\frac{1}{N}
for all
i
.

During the construction, at each step
k
, each cube
Q\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
generates at most
2^{d}
sets, each bounded in diameter by
\sqrt{d}\,\ell_{k}
. Summing over all scales, thanks to (
3.26
), there exists a constant
\tilde{C}
, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\textup{diam}(A_{i})^{q}\leq\textup{diam}(K)^{q}+\sum_{i=2}^{N}\textup{diam}(A_{i})^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}+\tilde{C}\sum_{k=1}^{J(N)-1}(\#\mathcal{A}_{k})\,\ell_{k}^{q}\leq\tilde{C}\sum_{k=0}^{J(N)-1}\ell_{k}^{q-\beta}=\tilde{C}\ell_{0}^{q-\beta}\sum_{k=0}^{J(N)-1}2^{k(\beta-q)}.

In particular, if
q<\beta
, from (
3.28
) we get

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\textup{diam}(A_{i})^{q}\leq\frac{\tilde{C}\ell_{0}^{q-\beta}}{2^{\beta-q}-1}2^{J(N)(\beta-q)}\leq\hat{C}N^{\frac{\beta-q}{\beta}}=\hat{C}N^{1-\frac{q}{\beta}}.

Instead, if
q=\beta
, again by (
3.28
) we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\textup{diam}(A_{i})^{q}\leq\tilde{C}J(N)\leq\hat{C}\log(N).

Eventually, in case
q>\beta
, the series
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k(\beta-q)}
is convergent. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.13
.

Assume that
\omega\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies (
Ahl
), then for every
N\geq N_{0}
there exists
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\textup{supp}\;\omega
such that

(3.29)

\|\mu_{N}-\omega\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}\leq\ C_{L}\begin{cases}N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{q}{\beta})},&\text{if }q<\beta,\\[5.69054pt]
N^{-1}(\log N)^{\frac{1}{2}},&\text{if }q=\beta,\\[5.69054pt]
N^{-1},&\text{if }q>\beta.\end{cases}

where
{\mu_{N}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
.

Proof.

The proof is entirely analogous to the proof of Theorem
3.10
. Indeed,
one repeats the same probabilistic construction by sampling independently one point
in each element of an equimass partition of
\operatorname{supp}\omega
. The only
difference is that, instead of using the uniform diameter estimate provided by
[
18
, Theorem 2]
(cf. Lemma
3.7
), we use the partition constructed in
Lemma
3.12
. Thus the argument of Theorem
3.10

gives

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\mu_{N}-\omega\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{\kappa_{d,q}}{2N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\textup{diam}(A_{i})^{q}.

Applying Lemma
3.12
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\mu_{N}-\omega\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}^{2}\Big]\leq C\begin{cases}N^{-(1+\frac{q}{\beta})},&\text{if }q<\beta,\\[2.84526pt]
N^{-2}\log N,&\text{if }q=\beta,\\[2.84526pt]
N^{-2},&\text{if }q>\beta.\end{cases}

Therefore there exists at least one realization of the random variables for which the
same bound holds, giving (
3.29
) after taking the square root.
∎

The previous corollary shows that, without the connectedness assumption, the upper
bound may deteriorate in the critical and supercritical regimes. We now give a
simple one-dimensional example which illustrates that this loss is not merely a technical point of the proof. The obstruction is already present for a support with just two
connected components.

Remark 3.14
.

Let
d=1
,
q\in(0,2)
and consider
\omega:=\mathcal{L}^{1}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits K
where
K=[1,2]\cup[6,7]
, observing that
\omega
satisfies (
Ahl
) with
\beta=1
. We claim that exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
odd natural number and every sequence
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
we have

\|\mu_{N}-\omega\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}\geq\frac{C}{N},

where
\mu_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
.

We fix two bump functions
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
such that
0\leq\varphi_{i}\leq 1
for
i=1,2
and

\begin{cases}\textup{supp}\;\varphi_{1}\subseteq[-1,1]\textup{ and }\varphi_{1}\equiv 1\textup{ on }[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}],\\
\textup{supp}\;\varphi_{2}\subseteq[-5,5]\textup{ and }\varphi_{2}\equiv 1\textup{ on }[-4,4].\end{cases}

For every
i=1,2
and every
x\in\mathbb{R}
we set
\varphi_{i,x}(t):=\varphi_{i}(t-x)
and we notice that
\|\varphi_{i,x}\|_{H^{s}_{0}}=\|\varphi_{i}\|_{H^{s}_{0}}
, therefore there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|\varphi_{i,x}\|^{-1}_{H^{s}_{0}}\geq 2C,

for
i=1,2
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Fix now
N\in\mathbb{N}
odd natural number and a sequence
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
. We will use Proposition
2.8
to estimate
\|\mu_{N}-\omega\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}
. In case there exists a point
x_{j}\not\in[0,8]
we have that

(3.30)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varphi_{1,x_{j}}}{\|\varphi_{1,x_{j}}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}}\textup{d}(\mu_{N}-\omega)=\frac{1}{N\|\varphi_{1,x_{j}}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\varphi_{1,x_{j}}(x_{k})\geq\frac{2C}{N},

because
\textup{supp}\;\varphi_{i,x_{j}}\cap\textup{supp}\;\omega=\emptyset
.
Otherwise
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}\subseteq[0,8]
and we consider two mutually exclusive cases:

1.
there exists
x_{j}\in[3,5]
and as in the previous case we obtain (
3.30
);

2.

\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}\subseteq[0,8]\setminus[3,5]
. We set

A:=\{k\in\{1,\dots,N\}\;:\;0\leq x_{k}<3\}

and
m:=\#A
. Therefore

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varphi_{2,0}}{\|\varphi_{2,0}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}}\textup{d}(\mu_{N}-\omega)\right|=\|\varphi_{2,0}\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}^{-1}\Big|\frac{m}{N}-\frac{1}{2}\Big|\geq 2C\Big|\frac{2m-N}{2N}\Big|\geq\frac{C}{N},

where the last inequality holds because
N
is odd.

This example shows precisely where the connected-support rate breaks down. Indeed,
if
q\in(1,2)
, then the rate obtained under the connectedness assumption would be
N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+q)}
, which is strictly smaller than
N^{-1}
as
N\to+\infty
. The estimate above rules
out such a rate for this disconnected support, at least along odd values of
N
and
for every possible empirical configuration. On the other hand, the general
disconnected upper bound of Corollary
3.13
gives exactly an
order
N^{-1}
estimate in the regime
q>\beta
. Thus, in this case, the disconnected-support rate is the correct one, whereas
the connected-support rate is no longer available.

4.
Sharp comparison between
\mathcal{E}_{q}

and the Wasserstein distance

The energy distance
\mathcal{E}_{q}
and the Wasserstein distances
W_{p}

induce comparable (weak) topologies on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, as established in
Proposition
1.2
. Related quantitative comparisons between
Wasserstein distances and Fourier-based probability metrics have been studied in
the kinetic and imaging literature; see, for instance,

[
4
]
, where equivalence estimates
with
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are proved in a discrete setting, and the broader discussion
in
[
3
]
.

In this section we sharpen this qualitative
comparison to a quantitative inequality, showing that
\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq W_{1}^{q}(\mu,\nu)
, with equality characterized exactly. This estimate is sharp – equality holds precisely for pairs of Dirac masses – and implies in particular that
\mathcal{E}_{q}^{2}\leq W_{1}^{q}\leq W_{q}^{q}
. To the best of our knowledge, this estimate has previously appeared only in the case
q=1

[
23
, Theorem 2]
,
[
22
, Lemma 5]
. The extension to general
q\in(0,2)
and the characterization of equality cases appear to be new.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with compact support then

(4.1)

\|\mu-\nu\|^{2}_{H_{0}^{-s}}\leq\kappa_{d,q}\;W^{q}_{1}(\mu,\nu),

where
W_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the
1
-Wasserstein distance and
\kappa_{d,q}
is the constant given in Theorem
1.1
.
Furthermore the equality in (
4.1
) holds if and only if either
\mu=\nu
or
\mu=\delta_{x_{0}}
and
\nu=\delta_{y_{0}}
for some
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
be an optimal coupling for
W_{1}
, and let
X,Y:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two random variables such that
(X,Y)\sim\pi
, and thus

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\mathbb{E}[|X-Y|].

Using the same argument in (
3.23
) we have that
\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X)]=\mu-\delta_{0}
and
\mathbb{E}[\Phi(Y)]=\nu-\delta_{0}
, therefore

(4.2)

\displaystyle\|\mu-\nu\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}=\big\|\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\Phi(Y)]\big\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\big\|\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)]\big\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}].

Furthermore, from (
2.10
), we have

\mathbb{E}[\|\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}]=\kappa_{d,q}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[|X-Y|^{q/2}].

Since
q/2\in(0,1)
, the function
t\mapsto t^{q/2}
is concave on
[0,\infty)
, hence Jensen’s inequality gives

(4.3)

\mathbb{E}[|X-Y|^{q/2}]\ \leq\ (\mathbb{E}[|X-Y|])^{q/2}=W^{q/2}_{1}(\mu,\nu).

Combining the last three equations proves (
4.1
).

We now characterize the equality cases. If
\mu=\nu
, then

\|\mu-\nu\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=0,

so equality holds. If instead
\mu=\delta_{x}
and
\nu=\delta_{y}
, then

W_{1}(\delta_{x},\delta_{y})=|x-y|,

and therefore by (
2.10
) we have

\|\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}^{2}=\kappa_{d,q}|x-y|^{q}=\kappa_{d,q}W_{1}(\delta_{x},\delta_{y})^{q}.

Thus equality also holds in this case. It remains to prove the converse. Suppose that

(4.4)

\|\mu-\nu\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}^{2}=\kappa_{d,q}W_{1}(\mu,\nu)^{q}.

Set
m:=W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\mathbb{E}[|X-Y|]
. If
m=0
then, since
W_{1}
is a distance, we have
\mu=\nu
. Assume now that
m>0
. From (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), all
intermediate inequalities must be equalities.
Hence

(4.5)

\|\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)]\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}=\mathbb{E}[\|\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}],

and

(4.6)

\mathbb{E}[|X-Y|^{\frac{q}{2}}]=(\mathbb{E}[|X-Y|])^{\frac{q}{2}}.

Thanks to the strictly concavity of
t\mapsto t^{\frac{q}{2}}
, equality (
4.6
) in Jensen’s inequality is
possible only if
|X-Y|
is almost surely constant. Consequently

(4.7)

|X(\omega)-Y(\omega)|=m\quad

for
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
. From (
4.2
), (
4.4
), (
4.5
), (
4.7
) and (
2.10
) we have

\|\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)]\|_{H^{-s}_{0}}=\kappa_{d,q}^{1/2}m^{\frac{q}{2}}>0.

Then setting

w:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)]}{\|\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)]\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}},

we have

\displaystyle 0=\mathbb{E}[\|\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}]-\|\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)]\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}]-\left\langle\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)],w\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{-s}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}-\left\langle\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y),w\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{-s}}],

where

\|\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}-\left\langle\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y),w\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{-s}}\geq 0,

by Cauchy-Schwartz inequality. Since the expectation is zero, it must vanish almost surely.
Therefore

\left\langle\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y),w\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{-s}}=\|\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}

and consequently

\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)=\|\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}w

almost surely.
Since
\|\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)\|_{H_{0}^{-s}}
is almost surely constant, from (
4.7
) and (
2.10
), thus
\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)
itself is
almost surely constant. In particular there exists an element
h\in\overline{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
such that

\Phi(X)-\Phi(Y)=\delta_{X}-\delta_{Y}=h\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Define the closed set

S:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}=h,\quad|x-y|=m\right\}.

From the previous argument,

\pi(S)=1.

In particular
S
is non-empty, but from it’s definition we have that
S=\{(x_{0},y_{0})\}
for some
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
because for every
x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have that
\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}=\delta_{x^{\prime}}-\delta_{y^{\prime}}
implies
x=x^{\prime}
and
y=y^{\prime}
. Therefore
\pi=\delta_{(x_{0},y_{0})}
and
\mu=\delta_{x_{0}}
,
\nu=\delta_{y_{0}}
, concluding the proof.
