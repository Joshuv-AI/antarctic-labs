Pannexin 1 attenuates hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance via AMPK-autophagy axis activation
Authors: Lijing Zhu, Jiaying Yang, Zhijian Zhang, Xiaohui Wei, Yue Liu, Liping Gu, Guiling Liang, Yongde Peng, Yufan Wang, Xiaoying Ding, Yuhang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD) affects over 25% of the global population, yet effective therapies remain limited. While Pannexin 1 (Panx1) has been implicated in metabolic regulation, its role in hepatic lipid metabolism remains unexplored. We hypothesized that Panx1 regulates the AMPK-autophagy axis to control hepatic lipid accumulation and insulin sensitivity.MethodsWe employed a comprehensive experimental strategy combining genetic manipulation in mice and cultured hepatocytes with multiple complementary analytical approaches. In vivo studies utilized both Panx1 knockout (KO) mice and wild-type littermates subjected to high-fat diet (HFD) feeding to induce MASLD. In vitro experiments employed Hepa1-6 hepatocytes treated with palmitic acid (PA) and oleic acid (OA) to simulate lipid overload, with Panx1 expression modulated through siRNA-mediated knockdown or plasmid-mediated overexpression. We integrated histological analyses (Oil Red O staining, PAS staining), molecular techniques (Western blotting, quantitative RT-PCR), flow cytometry for glucose uptake assessment, transmission electron microscopy for autophagosome visualization, and dual-fluorescence LC3 assays for autophagic flux monitoring. To establish causality, we performed rescue experiments using the autophagy inhibitors chloroquine (CQ) and bafilomycin A1 (BafA1), as well as the autophagy activator rapamycin (Rapa).ResultsOur investigations revealed that Panx1 expression is significantly downregulated in both human MASLD patients and experimental MASLD models, establishing its clinical relevance. Through loss-of-function studies, we demonstrated that Panx1 knockout dramatically exacerbated HFD-induced metabolic dysfunction, manifesting as markedly increased body weight gain, severe glucose intolerance, pronounced insulin resistance, elevated serum triglycerides and cholesterol, and massive hepatic lipid accumulation accompanied by upregulation of key lipogenic genes (SREBP1c, FASN, ACC1, SCD1, DGAT1). Conversely, gain-of-function experiments using Panx1 overexpression yielded striking protective effects: significantly reduced hepatic steatosis, improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, decreased serum lipid profiles, and suppressed expression of lipogenic genes in HFD-fed mice. Mechanistically, we uncovered that Panx1 functions as a critical activator of the AMPK-autophagy signaling cascade. Panx1 overexpression robustly activated AMPK phosphorylation, enhanced LC3-II accumulation, accelerated P62 degradation, and increased autophagosome formation as visualized by transmission electron microscopy and dual-fluorescence LC3 assays. These molecular changes translated into enhanced autophagic flux and lipophagy, effectively clearing accumulated lipids. Importantly, our rescue experiments definitively established autophagy as the essential mediator of Panx1’s metabolic effects: pharmacological autophagy inhibition with CQ or BafA1 completely abolished the beneficial effects of Panx1 overexpression on lipid accumulation and insulin signaling, while rapamycin-induced autophagy activation successfully rescued the metabolic defects caused by Panx1 deficiency. Furthermore, we demonstrated that Panx1’s effects on insulin sensitivity are autophagy-dependent, as evidenced by restored GLUT4 expression, IRS phosphorylation, and AKT activation following autophagy modulation.ConclusionThis study identifies Panx1 as a novel regulator of hepatic lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity via the AMPK-autophagy pathway. Given its downregulation in MASLD patients and the lack of FDA-approved treatments, Panx1 represents a promising therapeutic target for this prevalent metabolic disorder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06305-y.Keywords: MASLD, Panx1, AMPK, Autophagy, Insulin

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver condition worldwide, affecting approximately 25% of the adult population. This condition demonstrates strong associations with obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and metabolic syndrome [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of MASLD is complex, involving multiple pathological processes including hepatic lipid metabolic imbalance, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation [3, 4]. Central to MASLD progression is the dysregulation of hepatic lipid synthesis and breakdown [5].

Autophagy functions as a critical intracellular mechanism for maintaining metabolic balance and exhibits a dual regulatory role in hepatic lipid homeostasis [6, 7]. Through lipophagy, autophagy directly degrades lipid droplets, thereby reducing hepatic lipid accumulation [8, 9]. Additionally, it eliminates damaged organelles, particularly mitochondria, to preserve normal metabolic functions [10]. Recent investigations have demonstrated that autophagic activity is significantly diminished in liver tissues from MASLD patients, with impaired autophagy strongly correlating with hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance [11, 12].

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) functions as a pivotal cellular energy sensor and serves as a central metabolic hub that coordinates hepatic lipid homeostasis by integrating lipid metabolism and autophagy pathways [13, 14]. Upon activation, AMPK orchestrates comprehensive metabolic reprogramming through simultaneous targeting of multiple pathways to maintain cellular energy balance. Regarding lipid metabolism, AMPK suppresses lipogenesis by inhibiting key transcription factors such as sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP1c) and downregulating essential lipogenic enzymes, including fatty acid synthase (FASN) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1) [15]. Concurrently, AMPK promotes lipid catabolism through phosphorylation of ULK1, the primary initiator of autophagy, thereby activating lipophagy—a selective autophagic process that specifically targets lipid droplets for degradation [16, 17]. This coordinated dual mechanism creates a metabolic switch that not only reduces de novo lipid synthesis but also enhances autophagic clearance of accumulated lipids, effectively preventing hepatic steatosis. Moreover, AMPK-mediated autophagy activation extends beyond lipid clearance to encompass the removal of damaged organelles and maintenance of cellular homeostasis, thereby preserving insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism. This interconnected AMPK-autophagy-lipid metabolism axis constitutes a fundamental regulatory network in hepatic metabolic homeostasis, where disruption of any component can precipitate the metabolic dysfunction characteristic of MASLD.

Pannexin1 (Panx1), a member of the pannexin family, is a six-transmembrane glycoprotein that forms large-pore membrane channels [18, 19]. With widespread tissue distribution, Panx1 has been implicated in diverse physiological processes, including intercellular communication, inflammatory responses, and metabolic regulation [20]. Accumulating evidence points to a significant role for Panx1 in metabolic diseases, where it modulates adipocyte differentiation and insulin sensitivity within adipose tissue [21, 22] and regulates glucose uptake and metabolic processes in skeletal muscle [23]. Despite these advances, the specific function and underlying molecular mechanisms of Panx1 in hepatic lipid metabolism and MASLD pathogenesis remain largely unexplored.

Recent investigations have begun to uncover potential connections between Panx1 and metabolic regulation through its modulation of AMPK signaling pathways [24]. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests a functional relationship between Panx1 and autophagy [25]. Considering the pivotal roles that AMPK and autophagy play in maintaining hepatic lipid homeostasis, we hypothesize that Panx1 may serve as a key regulator of hepatic lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity through modulation of the AMPK/autophagy signaling axis.

To address this hypothesis, we designed and conducted comprehensive in vivo and in vitro experimental approaches to systematically investigate Panx1’s role in MASLD development and to delineate the molecular mechanisms governing its regulation of the AMPK/autophagy pathway. Our investigations reveal a previously uncharacterized Panx1–AMPK–autophagy regulatory network that plays a crucial role in hepatic metabolic homeostasis, positioning Panx1 as a promising therapeutic target for MASLD intervention.

Materials and methods

Animal housing and care

Panx1 global knockout (KO) mice, Panx1-overexpressing (OE) mice, and their wild-type (WT) littermate controls were all of C57BL/6J genetic background, male, SPF grade, and were 6 weeks of age at the start of the experiment. All mice were housed at the SPF-grade Laboratory Animal Center of Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, under controlled conditions of constant temperature (22 ± 1 °C), constant humidity (50 ± 5%), and a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. After one week of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups (n = 10 per group). Animals were randomly allocated to experimental groups using a complete randomization procedure, matching mice by age and body weight at the time of assignment. All histological scoring, morphological analyses, and Oil Red O quantification were performed in a single-blind manner by two independent pathologists who were unaware of the experimental group assignments.Panx1 knockout (KO) and wild-type (WT) mice were divided into four experimental groups: WT control, KO control, WT + HFD (WT mice fed a high-fat diet), and KO + HFD (KO mice fed a high-fat diet). Panx1-overexpressing (OE) and control (Ctrl) mice were divided into four experimental groups: Ctrl group (chow diet), OE group (Panx1 OE mice on chow diet), Ctrl + HFD group (Ctrl mice on high-fat diet), and OE + HFD group (Panx1 OE mice on high-fat diet). HFD-assigned groups were fed a 60% kcal high-fat diet (Research Diets, D12492) for 16 consecutive weeks, while control groups received a standard chow diet (10% kcal fat; Research Diets, D12450B). This dietary regimen has been validated in multiple prior studies as a reliable protocol for inducing MASLD in mice. For molecular biology analyses including Western blot and qPCR, n = 6 animals per group were used. At experimental endpoints, mice were deeply anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation and subsequently euthanized by cervical dislocation, followed by rapid collection of liver tissues and blood samples. Hepa1-6 hepatocyte cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were passaged every 2–3 days. All cell-based experiments were performed with n = 3 independent biological replicates. All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and institutional animal welfare and ethics standards, and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval No.: 2025AWS264).

Histological and pathological analysis

To investigate Panx1 expression in MASLD, liver tissues from control (Ctrl) and MASLD mice were collected and processed for Oil Red O staining. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed with PBS, and then dehydrated using 60% isopropanol. After staining with Oil Red O working solution for 10 min, the sections were differentiated in 60% isopropanol and washed with distilled water. Lipid droplets were visualized as orange-red staining under a light microscope at 200× and 400× magnification. For in vitro studies, Hepa1-6 cells were cultured and treated with a mixture of palmitic acid (PA, 0.25 mM) and oleic acid (OA, 0.5 mM) conjugated with BSA for 24 h to induce lipid accumulation. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, washed with PBS, and stained with Oil Red O.For histological analysis, liver sections were stained with Oil Red O to assess lipid accumulation and PAS staining to evaluate glycogen storage. Lipid droplets were observed under a light microscope at 200× and 400× magnification, and glycogen content was assessed by PAS staining.

Western blot analysis

Liver tissues or cells were lysed using RIPA buffer and subjected to SDS-PAGE. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA method. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies for Panx1 (Abcam, ab124969, 1:1000), LC3-I/II, P62, AMPK, p-AMPK, p-ACC1, ACC1, SREBP1c, FASN, p-IRS, IRS, p-AKT, AKT, and GLUT4. GAPDH was used as a loading control (1:5000 dilution). Membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized using chemiluminescent substrate. Protein expression levels were analyzed by densitometry using ImageJ software, and target protein expression was normalized to GAPDH. For Western blot analyses in which a wide dynamic range of protein expression was encountered across experimental groups, exposure times were optimized to ensure accurate detection and quantification of low-abundance phosphorylated proteins. As a result, high-abundance total protein bands (including total AMPK, ACC1, AKT, and GAPDH) may appear saturated in certain figures. Saturation was limited exclusively to loading normalization proteins; all functional target proteins central to the conclusions of this study were confirmed to be within the linear quantification range.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Liver tissues were collected from the experimental groups, and total RNA was extracted using a commercial RNA extraction kit (e.g., TRIzol reagent). cDNA was synthesized through reverse transcription, and mRNA expression levels of lipogenesis-related genes, including SREBP1c, FASN, ACC1, SCD1, and DGAT1, were quantified using RT-qPCR. The primers used for amplification were as follows: m-SREBP1c-F: GCAGCCACCATCTAGCCTG, m-SREBP1c-R: CAGCAGTGAGTCTGCCTTGAT, m-Fasn-F: GGAGGTGGTGATAGCCGGTAT, m-Fasn-R: TGGGTAATCCATAGAGCCCAG, m-Acc1-F: GATGAACCATCTCCGTTGGC, m-Acc1-R: GACCCAATTATGAATCGGGAGTG, m-Scd1-F: TTCTTGCGATACACTCTGGTGC, m-Scd1-R: CGGGATTGAATGTTCTTGTCGT, m-DGAT1-F: GTGCCATCGTCTGCAAGATTC, m-DGAT1-R: GCATCACCACACACCAATTCAG, m-GAPDH-F: TGGCCTTCCGTGTTCCTAC, and m-GAPDH-R: GAGTTGCTGTTGAAGTCGCA.

For cell culture experiments, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, reverse-transcribed to cDNA, and subjected to real-time quantitative PCR with SYBR Green to detect mRNA expression of SREBP1c, FASN, ACC1, SCD1, and DGAT1, using GAPDH as the internal control and calculating relative expression.

GEO database analysis

(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) was accessed to retrieve publicly available transcriptomic datasets. Two independent datasets were analyzed: GSE126848, which contains liver tissue samples from MASLD patients and healthy controls, and GSE193066, which includes hepatocyte samples from various metabolic conditions. Panx1 expression in MASLD was assessed and its correlation with lipogenic genes such as SREBF1 and ACACA was analyzed.

Cell culture and transfection experiments

Hepa1-6 hepatocyte cells were cultured in appropriate medium and divided into experimental groups as needed. For Panx1 manipulation experiments, cells were transfected with either control siRNA (siNC), Panx1-specific siRNA, empty vector control, or Panx1 overexpression plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following transfection, cells were treated with a mixture of palmitic acid (0.25 mM) and oleic acid (0.5 mM) for 24 h to induce lipid accumulation.

Lipid and metabolite measurements

Intracellular triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) levels were measured using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Results were normalized to total protein concentration determined by the BCA method. ATP levels were determined with an ATP assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai) based on luminescence detection.

Intracellular calcium measurement

Intracellular calcium concentration was assessed using the Fluo-4 AM fluorescent probe. Cells were loaded with the probe according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Glucose uptake assay

For glucose uptake, cells were stimulated with PBS or 100 nM insulin, followed by incubation with 2-NBDG at 37 °C, fluorescence was analyzed by flow cytometry. Glucose uptake was evaluated in Hepa1-6 cells treated with insulin using a 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) fluorescence assay kit. To assess insulin sensitivity, we performed flow cytometry analysis of glucose uptake under insulin stimulation. Glucose uptake assays were performed to assess insulin sensitivity. After treatment, cells were incubated with or without insulin (100 nM) for 30 min, followed by glucose uptake measurement using a fluorescent glucose analog according to the kit instructions.

Transmission electron microscopy

For ultrastructural observation of autophagosomes, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed. Cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated through graded ethanol, embedded in epoxy resin, sectioned, and examined under an electron microscope to visualize autophagic vacuole formation.

Dual-fluorescence LC3 assay

To monitor autophagic flux, cells were transfected with a tandem fluorescent-tagged LC3 construct (RFP-GFP-LC3). After treatment, cells were fixed and stained with DAPI to visualize nuclei. Fluorescence microscopy was used to detect red and green puncta representing autophagosomes and autolysosomes, respectively. Merged images were captured to evaluate the distribution and accumulation of LC3 puncta, allowing assessment of autophagic flux.

Autophagy inhibition experiments

Hepa1-6 cells were treated with palmitic acid (PA) and oleic acid (OA) to induce lipid accumulation. Experimental groups included: (1) PA + Vector control, (2) PA + Panx1 overexpression (Panx1 OE), (3) PA + Panx1 OE + chloroquine (CQ, 10 µM, autophagy inhibitor), and (4) PA + Panx1 OE + Bafilomycin A1 (BafA1, 100 nM, autophagy inhibitor). Autophagy inhibitors were added 6 h prior to analysis.

Autophagy activation experiments

Cells were pretreated with PA and OA, then divided into four groups: (1) PA + siNC (control siRNA), (2) PA + Panx1 siRNA (Panx1 knockdown), (3) PA + siNC + Rapamycin (100 nM, autophagy activator), and (4) PA + Panx1 siRNA + Rapamycin. Rapamycin was used to pharmacologically activate autophagy and rescue the effects of Panx1 deficiency.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple group comparisons. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Panx1 expression is dysregulated in MASLD

To investigate the potential role of Panx1 in MASLD pathogenesis, we first examined Panx1 expression patterns in both in vivo MASLD models and in vitro lipid-induced hepatocyte models. Analysis of the GEO database confirmed that Panx1 was significantly downregulated in MASLD liver samples and exhibited negative correlations with lipogenic genes including SREBF1 and ACACA (Fig. 1A). Oil Red O staining of liver tissues demonstrated prominent lipid droplet accumulation in the MASLD group, confirming the presence of hepatic steatosis (Fig. 1B). Western blot analysis revealed that Panx1 protein expression was significantly reduced in the livers of MASLD mice compared to controls (Fig. 1C). In contrast, in vitro PA + OA treatment induced substantial lipid accumulation in Hepa1-6 cells (Fig. 1D) while paradoxically upregulating Panx1 protein levels (Fig. 1E). These findings collectively suggest that Panx1 expression is dynamically regulated in response to lipid accumulation, and its dysregulation—particularly the downregulation observed in chronic MASLD—may contribute critically to the initiation and progression of hepatic steatosis.

Panx1 knockout exacerbates hepatic steatosis in MASLD

To determine the functional significance of Panx1 downregulation in MASLD, we generated Panx1 knockout mice and subjected them to high-fat diet (HFD) feeding. Panx1 knockout (KO) mice fed with HFD exhibited significantly greater body weight gain, hepatic lipid accumulation, and elevated serum triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) levels compared to wild-type (WT) mice under the same dietary conditions. Additionally, these mice demonstrated upregulated expression of lipogenic proteins and impaired glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity (Fig. 2A-C). Daily food intake did not differ significantly between genotypes during the HFD feeding period (Supplementary Figure R2), excluding differential caloric intake as a confounding variable. Furthermore, serum ALT and AST levels were significantly elevated in KO + HFD mice compared with WT + HFD controls (Supplementary Figure R3), confirming that Panx1 deficiency exacerbates HFD-induced hepatocellular injury. Oil Red O staining demonstrated a progressive increase in lipid deposition: mild accumulation in the KO group, moderate deposition in the WT + HFD group, and severe accumulation with densely distributed red lipid droplets in the KO + HFD group compared to controls (Fig. 2D). Quantification of Oil Red O-positive area further confirmed significantly greater lipid accumulation in KO + HFD livers compared with WT + HFD controls (Supplementary Figure R4). Western blot analysis revealed decreased phospho-AMPK (pAMPK) and phospho-ACC (pACC) levels, increased lipogenic proteins (SREBP1c, FASN, ACC1), suppressed autophagy (reduced LC3-II, elevated P62), and diminished insulin signaling (reduced p-IRS, p-AKT, GLUT4) in KO mice, with these effects being particularly pronounced under HFD conditions (Fig. 2E-F, Supplementary Fig. 2). These results indicate that Panx1 knockout exacerbates HFD-induced hepatic lipid accumulation and accelerates MASLD progression. Panx1 gene deletion aggravates HFD-induced metabolic dysregulation by disrupting glucose and lipid metabolism, insulin signaling pathways, and autophagic processes, suggesting that Panx1 plays a critical protective role in maintaining hepatic metabolic homeostasis.

Panx1 overexpression ameliorates MASLD phenotypes in vivo

To investigate the therapeutic potential of Panx1 in MASLD, we generated Panx1-overexpressing (OE) mice and subjected them to HFD feeding. Our objective was to determine whether Panx1 overexpression could alleviate HFD-induced hepatic steatosis and improve metabolic dysfunction, including insulin resistance and dyslipidemia. Panx1-overexpressing mice subjected to HFD demonstrated reduced body weight gain, attenuated hepatic steatosis, and improved glucose and insulin tolerance compared to HFD-fed control mice (Fig. 3A). Serum insulin, TG, and TC levels were significantly decreased in the OE + HFD group (Fig. 3B). Consistently, serum ALT and AST levels were significantly reduced in OE + HFD mice compared with Ctrl + HFD controls (Supplementary Figure R3), confirming that Panx1 overexpression ameliorates HFD-induced hepatocellular injury, accompanied by downregulated lipogenic gene expression (Fig. 3C). Oil Red O staining demonstrated that the OE + HFD group exhibited significantly reduced lipid droplets compared to controls, while PAS staining revealed improved glycogen accumulation (Fig. 3D). Quantification of Oil Red O-positive area confirmed significantly reduced lipid content in OE + HFD livers compared with Ctrl + HFD controls (Supplementary Figure R4). Furthermore, Panx1 overexpression promoted AMPK activation, enhanced autophagic flux (increased LC3-II, decreased P62), and suppressed expression of lipogenic markers (SREBP1c, FASN, ACC1) (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Fig. 3). Insulin signaling proteins (p-IRS, p-AKT, GLUT4) were also upregulated (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings demonstrate that Panx1 overexpression significantly ameliorates HFD-induced MASLD through multiple mechanisms: reduced body weight, enhanced glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity, downregulated lipogenic gene and protein expression, enhanced autophagy, activated AMPK signaling, improved insulin signal transduction, and decreased hepatic lipid accumulation.

Panx1 regulates lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity in hepatocytes

To further elucidate the cellular mechanisms by which Panx1 modulates lipid metabolism and insulin responsiveness, we conducted in vitro experiments using Hepa1-6 hepatocyte models. Specifically, we manipulated Panx1 expression through siRNA-mediated knockdown or plasmid-based overexpression (OE), and exposed the cells to PA + OA to induce steatosis. Oil Red O staining revealed that Panx1 siRNA promoted lipid droplet accumulation, while Panx1 overexpression reduced lipid content under PA + OA treatment (Fig. 4A). Biochemical assays confirmed that Panx1 overexpression lowered intracellular TG and TC levels and enhanced glucose uptake (Fig. 4B). Flow cytometry analysis of insulin-stimulated glucose uptake demonstrated that Panx1 overexpression markedly increased insulin responsiveness, whereas Panx1 knockdown significantly impaired this process (Fig. 4C–D). At the transcriptional level, RT-qPCR revealed that Panx1 overexpression downregulated lipogenic gene expression, while knockdown produced opposite effects (Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis corroborated these findings: Panx1 overexpression suppressed SREBP1c and FASN, increased pACC, and enhanced insulin signaling through upregulation of p-IRS, p-AKT, and GLUT4 (Fig. 4F–G, Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings demonstrate that Panx1 directly regulates hepatic lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity at the cellular level by modulating both lipogenic gene expression and insulin signaling pathways.

Panx1 promotes autophagy via AMPK activation

To determine whether Panx1 regulates autophagy and to clarify its mechanistic relationship with AMPK signaling, we evaluated autophagy markers and AMPK activation status in hepatocytes under different Panx1 expression conditions. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed increased autophagosome formation in Panx1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 5A). Western blot analysis showed that Panx1 overexpression increased AMPK phosphorylation and autophagy markers (upregulated LC3-II, enhanced P62 degradation), while Panx1 siRNA produced opposite effects (Fig. 5B), and fluorescent LC3 reporter assays demonstrated enhanced autophagic flux in the Panx1 overexpression group compared to vector controls (Fig. 5C). These results demonstrate that Panx1 promotes autophagy in hepatocytes through AMPK activation, identifying autophagic induction as a key mechanism by which Panx1 regulates hepatic lipid metabolism. It should be noted that while changes in the LC3-II/I ratio and p62 levels reflect overall autophagic flux, the concurrent reduction in lipid droplet content observed alongside autophagic flux activation is consistent with lipophagic clearance. However, we cannot formally exclude contributions from general macroautophagy, as lipophagy-specific readouts were not employed in the current study.

Inhibition of autophagy abolishes the protective effects of Panx1

To verify whether the beneficial metabolic effects of Panx1 depend on autophagy, we pharmacologically inhibited autophagy using chloroquine (CQ) and bafilomycin A1 (BafA1) in Hepa1-6 cells overexpressing Panx1. These interventions block autophagosome-lysosome fusion, thereby suppressing autophagic flux and allowing assessment of autophagy’s requirement for Panx1-mediated metabolic regulation. Treatment with CQ and BafA1 reversed the Panx1 overexpression-induced reduction in lipid accumulation and improvements in glucose metabolism (Fig. 6A-B). Lipogenic gene expression was restored upon autophagy inhibition (Fig. 6C), and Western blot analysis showed reduced LC3-II, increased P62, suppressed pAMPK, and restored lipogenesis (SREBP1c, FASN, ACC1) following CQ/BafA1 treatment (Fig. 6D-F, Supplementary Fig. 6). These findings demonstrate that the metabolic benefits conferred by Panx1 overexpression—including reduced lipid accumulation and improved insulin signaling—are largely dependent on functional autophagy.

Autophagy activation rescues Panx1-deficiency-induced metabolic disorder

To further confirm that autophagy serves as a key downstream effector mediating Panx1’s metabolic regulatory role, we investigated whether pharmacological autophagy activation could rescue the lipid and glucose metabolic impairments induced by Panx1 knockdown. We treated Panx1-deficient (siRNA-transfected) Hepa1-6 cells with rapamycin (Rapa), an established autophagy activator, under PA + OA stimulation. Following rapamycin treatment, Oil Red O staining revealed markedly reduced lipid droplet accumulation in Panx1-silenced cells, suggesting partial restoration of lipid clearance capacity (Fig. 7A). Biochemical assays showed significantly decreased intracellular TG and TC levels upon rapamycin administration compared to untreated Panx1 knockdown cells (Fig. 7B), accompanied by suppressed lipogenic gene expression (Fig. 7C). Western blot analysis demonstrated restored AMPK activation, increased LC3-II, and decreased P62 in the rapamycin-treated group (Fig. 7D Supplementary Fig. 7). Importantly, insulin signaling was reactivated, with enhanced expression of p-IRS, p-AKT, and GLUT4 (Fig. 7E, Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings confirm that autophagy serves as a critical mediator of Panx1’s protective effects on hepatic lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity, and that restoring autophagic function can rescue the metabolic defects induced by Panx1 deficiency.

Discussion

In this comprehensive investigation, we have identified Panx1 as a novel and critical regulator of hepatic lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity, establishing for the first time that Panx1 functions through the AMPK-autophagy signaling axis to protect against MASLD. Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized metabolic regulatory network wherein Panx1 serves as an upstream activator that orchestrates a coordinated response to hepatic lipid overload by simultaneously suppressing lipogenesis and enhancing autophagic lipid clearance. This discovery not only fills a critical knowledge gap regarding Panx1’s hepatic metabolic functions but also identifies a promising therapeutic target for MASLD, a condition affecting over 25% of the global population yet lacking effective pharmacological interventions [26, 27].

A pivotal finding of our study is the significant downregulation of Panx1 expression in both human MASLD samples and experimental models, establishing the clinical relevance of this pathway. Through GEO dataset analysis, we identified strong negative correlations between PANX1 expression and key lipogenic genes (SREBF1, ACACA), suggesting that Panx1 deficiency directly contributes to the dysregulated lipid metabolism characteristic of MASLD. This downregulation appears to create a pathological feedback loop: lipid accumulation suppresses Panx1 expression, which in turn impairs AMPK-autophagy signaling, further exacerbating lipid deposition and metabolic dysfunction. While previous studies have established Panx1’s roles in regulating glucose metabolism in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [21, 23], and suggested broader functions in metabolic homeostasis [28, 29], our work represents the first systematic investigation of Panx1’s function in hepatic lipid metabolism and MASLD pathogenesis. The paradoxical acute upregulation of Panx1 in PA + OA-treated hepatocytes, contrasting with its chronic downregulation in MASLD, likely reflects an initial compensatory response to lipotoxic stress that ultimately proves insufficient to prevent disease progression, underscoring the importance of sustained Panx1 function in maintaining hepatic metabolic homeostasis.

Our loss-of-function studies using Panx1 knockout mice provided definitive evidence for Panx1’s protective role in MASLD. Panx1 deficiency dramatically sensitized mice to HFD-induced metabolic dysfunction, manifesting as severe hepatic steatosis, pronounced insulin resistance, and marked glucose intolerance. The molecular signature of Panx1-deficient livers was characterized by upregulation of lipogenic transcription factors and enzymes (SREBP1c, FASN, ACC1, SCD1, DGAT1), impaired AMPK activation, and defective autophagy evidenced by decreased LC3-II/I ratio and P62 accumulation. Critically, the accumulation of P62, a selective autophagy substrate, indicates not merely reduced autophagosome formation but impaired autophagic flux—a distinction we validated through dual-fluorescence LC3 assays and transmission electron microscopy. These findings align with mounting evidence identifying autophagy dysfunction as a key pathogenic mechanism in MASLD development [30], and demonstrate that Panx1 deficiency disrupts the coordinated regulation of both lipid synthesis and degradation pathways.

Conversely, our gain-of-function experiments demonstrated that Panx1 overexpression confers robust protection against MASLD, even in the context of continued HFD-induced metabolic stress. Panx1-overexpressing mice exhibited striking improvements across all metabolic parameters: significantly reduced hepatic lipid accumulation, markedly improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, decreased circulating triglycerides and cholesterol, and suppressed lipogenic gene expression. These phenotypic improvements were underpinned by enhanced AMPK phosphorylation and activated autophagy, as demonstrated by increased LC3-II levels, accelerated P62 degradation, and augmented autophagosome formation visualized by electron microscopy. The restoration of hepatic glycogen storage, revealed by PAS staining, provided additional evidence of improved insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism. The magnitude of these protective effects—achieved despite ongoing metabolic challenge—highlights Panx1’s potency as a metabolic regulator and suggests that therapeutic Panx1 activation could effectively counteract environmental and genetic risk factors for MASLD.

A central mechanistic insight from our study is the identification of the AMPK-autophagy axis as the essential mediator of Panx1’s metabolic effects. AMPK, functioning as the master cellular energy sensor and metabolic hub [31, 32], serves as the critical node linking Panx1 to downstream metabolic processes. Our data demonstrate that Panx1 overexpression robustly activates AMPK phosphorylation, triggering a coordinated cascade of metabolic responses: (1) suppression of lipogenesis through downregulation of SREBP1c, FASN, and ACC1; (2) activation of autophagy via enhanced LC3-II conversion and P62 degradation; and (3) improvement of insulin signaling through upregulation of p-IRS, p-AKT, and GLUT4. This integrated regulatory program effectively creates a metabolic switch that simultaneously reduces de novo lipid synthesis and enhances autophagic lipid clearance, while preserving insulin sensitivity—a coordinated response ideally suited to prevent hepatic steatosis.

The causal role of autophagy in mediating Panx1’s effects is definitively established by our pharmacological rescue experiments, which represent the most rigorous evidence for mechanism in our study. Autophagy inhibitors (chloroquine and bafilomycin A1) completely abolished all beneficial effects of Panx1 overexpression—lipid reduction, autophagic flux enhancement, and insulin signaling improvement—demonstrating that autophagy is not merely associated with but absolutely required for Panx1’s metabolic actions. Conversely, and equally importantly, rapamycin-mediated autophagy activation fully rescued the metabolic defects induced by Panx1 deficiency, restoring AMPK phosphorylation, normalizing lipid clearance, reactivating autophagic flux, and reinstating insulin signaling. These bidirectional rescue experiments unequivocally establish autophagy as the essential mechanism through which Panx1 regulates hepatic metabolism, and validate the AMPK-autophagy pathway as the primary signaling axis underlying Panx1’s protective effects against MASLD.

Beyond its effects on lipid homeostasis, Panx1 profoundly improved insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism, addressing a critical component of MASLD pathophysiology. The enhancement of insulin signaling proteins (p-IRS, p-AKT, GLUT4) in Panx1-overexpressing models suggests that Panx1’s metabolic benefits extend well beyond simple lipid clearance. This finding is consistent with the well-established reciprocal relationship between hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance, wherein lipid accumulation disrupts insulin signaling cascades [33]. The mechanism likely operates through multiple complementary pathways: autophagy-mediated removal of damaged mitochondria reduces oxidative stress and preserves insulin signaling capacity; lipophagy-dependent reduction of intracellular lipids decreases lipotoxic intermediates such as diacylglycerols and ceramides that interfere with insulin receptor substrate phosphorylation; and AMPK activation directly enhances glucose uptake and metabolism. The systemic metabolic improvements observed in Panx1-overexpressing mice—improved glucose tolerance, enhanced insulin sensitivity, and restored hepatic glycogen storage despite continued HFD feeding—suggest that Panx1 activation addresses multiple components of metabolic syndrome simultaneously [34], positioning it as a promising therapeutic approach for the broader spectrum of metabolic dysfunction beyond isolated hepatic steatosis.

The therapeutic implications of our findings are substantial and timely. Current treatment options for MASLD remain severely limited, with lifestyle modification representing the primary intervention [26, 27], and no FDA-approved pharmacological therapies specifically targeting this condition despite its enormous global burden. Our identification of Panx1 as a druggable target that simultaneously modulates multiple pathological processes—lipogenesis, autophagy, and insulin signaling—opens several promising therapeutic avenues. First, small-molecule activators of Panx1 channel function or enhancers of Panx1 protein stability could provide direct therapeutic benefit. Second, gene therapy approaches to restore hepatic Panx1 expression in deficient individuals represent a potential precision medicine strategy. Third, indirect pharmacological activation of the AMPK-autophagy pathway—using established agents such as metformin (an AMPK activator) or rapamycin analogs (autophagy inducers)—offers immediately translatable approaches, particularly given promising results from recent clinical trials investigating autophagy modulators for metabolic diseases [35]. However, our findings suggest that such downstream interventions may prove most effective in patients with preserved Panx1 expression, highlighting the potential value of Panx1 as a predictive biomarker for treatment stratification. Fourth, combination therapies targeting multiple nodes of the Panx1-AMPK-autophagy axis may achieve synergistic effects superior to single-agent approaches.

Several important questions warrant further investigation to fully translate these findings into clinical applications. First, while we establish that Panx1 activates AMPK, the precise molecular mechanisms linking Panx1 channel activity to AMPK phosphorylation require elucidation. Panx1’s known function in facilitating ATP release [29] suggests potential mechanisms involving altered cellular AMP: ATP ratios or purinergic receptor signaling, but direct experimental validation is needed. Second, our study focused primarily on hepatocytes, yet MASLD pathogenesis involves complex interactions among multiple liver cell types including Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells. Cell-type-specific conditional knockout models would clarify Panx1’s cell-autonomous versus non-autonomous functions. Third, the long-term safety and efficacy of sustained Panx1 activation require careful evaluation, particularly given that pannexins have been implicated in inflammatory processes [36], raising concerns about potential unintended consequences of chronic activation. Fourth, our human data derive from bioinformatic analyses of existing datasets; prospective clinical studies with detailed phenotyping and longitudinal follow-up are essential to validate Panx1 as a clinical biomarker and therapeutic target. Fifth, investigating Panx1’s role in MASLD-associated complications such as fibrosis progression and hepatocellular carcinoma development would provide crucial insights into disease evolution and identify additional therapeutic opportunities.

From a translational safety perspective, it is important to note that Panx1 channel activity has been linked to ATP release and NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated inflammatory signaling in immune and endothelial cells. In the context of our study, Panx1 overexpression consistently ameliorated hepatic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction; however, the net effect of systemic Panx1 upregulation on inflammatory pathways in other tissues — particularly under conditions of infection, injury, or immune challenge — warrants careful evaluation before any Panx1-based therapeutic strategy is pursued clinically. Targeted delivery approaches, such as hepatocyte-specific AAV-mediated Panx1 overexpression, may provide a safer therapeutic modality that preserves the hepatoprotective benefits while minimizing potential off-target inflammatory risks.

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. First, while our data demonstrate that Panx1 promotes autophagic flux and reduces lipid droplet content, the specific contribution of lipophagy—as distinct from general macroautophagy—was not directly confirmed, as lipophagy-specific assays such as LC3-lipid droplet co-immunoprecipitation, PLIN2/LC3 co-localization, or lysosomal lipid degradation assays were not performed. Future studies employing these approaches are necessary to definitively establish whether lipophagy, rather than general autophagy, is the primary mechanism underlying Panx1-mediated lipid reduction. This study was conducted exclusively in male mice, and the extent to which the role of Panx1 in MASLD is subject to sexual dimorphism remains to be investigated in future studies incorporating both male and female cohorts. Additionally, comprehensive metabolic assessments, including indirect calorimetry, oxygen consumption, and physical activity monitoring, were not performed in the current study, which limits our ability to fully characterize the systemic metabolic effects of Panx1 modulation. Future studies employing metabolic cage analyses will be necessary to comprehensively assess the impact of Panx1 on whole-body energy homeostasis. Although our bioinformatic analysis of multiple independent GEO datasets consistently demonstrates Panx1 downregulation in human MASLD, direct validation of Panx1 protein expression by immunohistochemistry and its correlation with histological disease severity (NAS score, fibrosis stage) in human liver biopsy specimens was not performed in this study due to sample availability constraints. This represents an important limitation and a priority direction for future translational validation. A notable limitation of the current study is the use of global Panx1 knockout and overexpression models rather than liver-specific or hepatocyte-specific genetic manipulation. Panx1 is expressed in multiple extra-hepatic tissues, including the central nervous system, immune cells, and adipose tissue, and systemic Panx1 modulation may contribute to the observed metabolic phenotypes through pathways that are independent of direct hepatocellular effects. Future studies employing hepatocyte-specific Cre-lox systems (e.g., Alb-Cre; Panx1^fl/fl) will be necessary to definitively establish the liver-autonomous contribution of Panx1 to MASLD pathophysiology.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study establishes Panx1 as a novel and critical regulator of hepatic lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity. Through activation of the AMPK-autophagy signaling pathway, Panx1 orchestrates a coordinated metabolic program that prevents hepatic steatosis and preserves insulin sensitivity. The downregulation of Panx1 in MASLD creates a pathogenic cycle of impaired autophagy and progressive lipid accumulation, while Panx1 restoration or AMPK-autophagy activation can break this cycle and restore metabolic homeostasis. These findings not only advance our fundamental understanding of hepatic metabolic regulation but also identify a promising therapeutic target for MASLD. Given the global burden of MASLD and the lack of approved pharmacological treatments, therapeutic strategies targeting the Panx1-AMPK-autophagy axis warrant urgent clinical investigation. Future studies should focus on developing Panx1-targeting therapeutics and identifying optimal patient populations for such interventions, with the ultimate goal of translating these mechanistic insights into effective treatments for this increasingly prevalent metabolic disorder.
